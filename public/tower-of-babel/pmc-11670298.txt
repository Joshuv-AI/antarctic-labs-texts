Population genetics of the freshwater red alga Batrachospermum gelatinosum (Rhodophyta) I: Frequent intragametophytic selfing in a monoicous, haploid–diploid species
Authors: Sarah J. Shainker‐Connelly, Roseanna M. Crowell, Solenn Stoeckel, Morgan L. Vis, Stacy A. Krueger‐Hadfield
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractLife cycles with a prolonged haploid phase are thought to be correlated with greater rates of selfing and asexual reproduction. In red algae, recent population genetic studies have aimed to test this prediction but have mostly focused on marine species with separate sexes. We characterized the reproductive system of the obligately monoicous (i.e., hermaphroditic) freshwater red alga Batrachospermum gelatinosum and predicted that we would find genetic signatures of uniparental reproduction because of its haploid‐diploid life cycle. We sampled 18 sites and genotyped 311 gametophytes with 10 polymorphic microsatellite loci to describe the reproductive system. Genotypic richness was low (<0.5) and pareto β values (describing clonal membership) were <0.7 for most sites. In taxa with separate sexes, these patterns are typically indicative of asexual reproduction. However, the genetic consequences of selfing in monoicous gametophytes are indistinguishable from those caused by asexual processes. Since we sampled gametophytes and have not yet genotyped the chantransia (i.e., the diploid phase), we interpreted low diversity as a signature of intragametophytic selfing. Additionally, to understand the factors that drive selfing, we tested latitude and several other environmental variables, but none was significantly correlated with the genetic variation we observed. Nevertheless, future studies should genotype the chantransia to measure observed heterozygosity among other summary statistics to disentangle the effects of selfing versus asexual reproduction. Together, these data, coupled with further characterization of abiotic factors that influence population genetic patterns, will allow us to test potential drivers of reproductive system evolution.

INTRODUCTION

The reproductive system describes the traits of life history that determine the relative rates of (i) sexual versus asexual reproduction and (ii) selfing versus outcrossing (Barrett, 2011). The prevailing reproductive mode governs the transmission of genes between generations, partitioning genetic diversity within and among populations (Hamrick & Godt, 1996) and shaping evolutionary responses to environmental change (e.g., Eckert et al., 2010; Orive et al., 2017). Moreover, the reproductive system has been predicted to be correlated with the type of life cycle (Otto & Marks, 1996). For example, life cycles dominated by the diploid phase, such as in animals and angiosperms, are expected to be associated with outcrossing, whereas life cycles with longer haploid phases are expected to be associated with uniparental reproduction, as deleterious mutations will be exposed and more easily purged (Otto & Marks, 1996). Yet, data on the reproductive system are largely available for angiosperms (e.g., Whitehead et al., 2018) and animals (e.g., Leonard, 2006), with fewer data available for other taxa.

Macroalgae exhibit considerable variation within life cycles (Bell, 1994) and reproductive systems (see Krueger‐Hadfield, 2024; Krueger‐Hadfield et al., 2021; Olsen et al., 2020). However, population genetic data are somewhat taxonomically restricted. Both brown algae (see Heesch et al., 2021) and green algae (Otto & Marks, 1996) have been proposed for testing the influence of diverse types of life cycle on reproductive and sexual system (i.e., monoicous and dioicous) variation. Unlike greens and browns, marine red algae are mostly dioicous, limiting the apparent scope of testing this question. Yet, even within the dioicous, intertidal marine red algae, there is considerable variation. For example, Chondrus crispus has exhibited high levels of intergametophytic selfing (Krueger‐Hadfield et al., 2015; Krueger‐Hadfield, Roze, et al., 2013) whereas Gracilaria gracilis has been an obligate outcrosser (Engel et al., 2004). Furthermore, freshwater red algae have largely been overlooked despite incredible variation in their sexual system within and among species (i.e., monoicy, dioicy, and trioicy; see definitions in Krueger‐Hadfield et al., 2024). Studying this group has the potential to expand our understanding of the variation within reproductive systems beyond previous work in marine reds, greens, and browns because the environment (e.g., freshwater streams versus the intertidal zone) and taxon‐specific evolutionary history and traits are expected to shape this variation.

Freshwater red algae in the order Batrachospermales have a unique life cycle. Unlike other red algae that alternate between a diploid tetrasporophyte and haploid gametophyte phase, Batrachospermalean algae alternate between a microscopic, perennial diploid phase—called the “chantransia”—and a macroscopic, ephemeral haploid gametophyte. The gametophyte remains physically attached to the chantransia. This life cycle results in unique eco‐evolutionary consequences distinct from those in animals and angiosperms (Krueger‐Hadfield, 2020) and marine reds (Krueger‐Hadfield et al., 2024). First, intragametophytic selfing describes fertilization between a genetically identical spermatium and a carpogonium produced by the same monoicous gametophyte. Unlike selfing in diploid‐dominant or dioicous organisms, intragametophytic selfing results in instantaneous, genome‐wide homozygosity (Klekowski, 1969; Figure 1a). Intragametophytic selfing can only occur in monoicous gametophytes and is, therefore, uncommon in marine red algae, which are mostly dioicous (but see Fujio et al., 1985; Lindstrom, 1993). Second, unlike marine red macroalgae, the single haploid cell produced following vegetative meiosis does not disperse, and three of the four products of meiosis are lost (Sheath, 1984). Moreover, the freshwater red algal gametophyte and chantransia are physically attached, unlike the morphologically independent gametophytes and tetrasporophytes in the marine red algae. It is possible that multiple gametophytes may be produced from different filaments of the same chantransia. These gametophytes would be in close proximity, and this proximity could facilitate selfing among gametophytes that share the same parental chantransia (i.e., intergametophytic selfing). Although selfing between gametophytes sharing the same sporophytic parent also occurs in dioicous marine red algae, the physical proximity of gametophytes with their parental chantransia may promote higher rates of this type of selfing in freshwater reds. Intergametophytic selfing is expected to gradually erode genetic diversity through the loss of heterozygosity, similarly to selfing or inbreeding in diploid‐dominant organisms (Klekowski, 1969; Figure 1b). Conversely, outcrossing, or intergametophytic crossing, describes fertilization between gametophytes that do not share a chantransia parent (Klekowski, 1969; Figure 1c). Intergametophytic crossing is expected to generate genotypic diversity and maintain heterozygosity in natural populations (see Goodwillie et al., 2010; Figure 1c).

(b) Intergametophytic selfing occurs between gametes produced by two gametophytes that originated from the same chantransia. In this example, the parental chantransia is heterozygous. Successive generations of intergametophytic selfing are expected to gradually erode genetic diversity but, unlike intragametophytic selfing, will not result in instantaneous genome‐wide homozygosity from a single fertilization event. (c) Intergametophytic crossing, or outcrossing, occurs when two gametophytes originating from genetically distinct chantransia cross‐fertilize. Genetic diversity will be maintained through successive generations of intergametophytic crossing. (d) The chantransia can reproduce asexually via monospore production, bypassing the gametophyte stage. The resultant chantransia will have the same genotype, barring mutation. Through time, somatic mutations will accumulate, resulting in increased heterozygosity (see Halkett et al., 2005).

Similarly to marine red algae, Batrachospermalean freshwater reds can reproduce asexually through the production of monospores by chantransia (Sheath, 1984; Figure 1d). Yet, the frequency of a single chantransia producing multiple gametophytes or monospores in natural populations is unknown. Additionally, we have observed very small gametophytes (~1 cm) bearing many carposporophytes (Shainker‐Connelly, Crowell, Vis, and Krueger‐Hadfield, personal observations). These observations suggest fertilization is efficient despite non‐motile spermatia and stream currents that transport propagules downstream but not upstream (Hambrook & Sheath, 1991). We do not know if there are multiple chantransia sharing the same genotype (i.e., repeated chantransia genotypes) throughout a stream reach due to the recruitment of individual carpospores after mitotic amplification of the zygote in the same carposporophyte (i.e., cystocarpic reproduction), which would be indistinguishable from asexual reproduction via monospores by a chantransia (Figure 1d). However, two studies in marine reds did not find evidence of repeated tetrasporophytes (see Engel et al., 2004; Krueger‐Hadfield et al., 2015), and it would be necessary to genotype chantransia to determine whether there are many repeated diploid genotypes throughout a stream reach.

Here, we focus on Batrachospermum gelatinosum. This species is widespread within the northern hemisphere, and all populations are monoicous to our knowledge (Vis & Necchi, 2021). We predicted that the unique batrachospermalean life cycle and monoicy would result in genetic signatures of selfing, asexual reproduction, or both. We aimed to characterize this variation across 18 sites in which B. gelatinosum was observed using 10 recently developed microsatellite loci (Crowell, Shainker‐Connelly, Vis, & Krueger‐Hadfield, 2024). We hypothesized that B. gelatinosum would exhibit (i) high levels of uniparental reproduction due to the haploid‐diploid life cycle (Otto & Marks, 1996) and short, unidirectional dispersal of gametes and spores (Hambrook & Sheath, 1991) and (ii) partial clonality due to the haploid‐diploid life cycle (Otto & Marks, 1996) and the capacity for both sexual and asexual reproduction (Hambrook & Sheath, 1991; Sheath & Vis, 2014).

MATERIALS AND METHODS

The current paper and Crowell, Shainker‐Connelly, Krueger‐Hadfield, and Vis (2024) used the same samples; therefore, the sections on sample collection, DNA extraction, and microsatellite amplification are similar between the two papers. Data analyses differed due to the distinct foci of each study.

Sample collection

We collected Batrachospermum gelatinosum samples from 18 sites throughout the eastern United States in 2021 and 2022 (Figure 2, Table 1; Table S1 in the Supporting Information). Since chantransia are microscopic and typically we cannot see them in the field, we collected gametophytic thalli (henceforth referred to as “gametophytes”), which are macroscopic. For each site, we noted GPS coordinates with Google Maps or the iPhone app GPSCoordinates ver. 5.18 (Neal, 2018). We chose our sampling area based on stream access and the location of gametophytes. We measured stream width and length of the sampled stream segment with a transect tape and measured physiochemical parameters close to the middle of the sampling area. We estimated stream bed composition, water color, and water clarity. At sites 1, 2, and 18 (see Table 1), we used an Oakton PCTSTestr 50 Pocket Tester to measure pH, water temperature, and specific conductivity. At other sites, we used an Oakton pHTestr 5 to measure pH and water temperature and an Oakton ECTestr low to measure specific conductivity. At sites 1, 2, and 18 (see Table 1), we used a flow probe (Global Water Instruments, Model FP111) to measure current velocity and stream depth. At other sites, we used another flow probe (General Oceanics, Mechanical Flow Meter) and measured stream depth with a ruler. At sites 1, 2, and 18 (see Table 1), we used a spherical densiometer (Forest Densiometer, Model A) to calculate percent canopy cover following Lemmon (1956, 1957), but with one reading instead of four. At other sites, canopy cover was estimated by eye.

We observed each gametophyte in the microscope for the presence of carposporophytes. We ensured that we preserved a single gametophyte by physically separating thalli if entangled with one another, and we removed the lower portion of the thallus to ensure that any remnants of the chantransia and other detritus from the biofilm were removed. We preserved each gametophyte in silica desiccant, and when possible, remaining tissue was mounted on herbarium paper (University of California‐type Herbarium Mounting Paper, Herbarium Supply, Bozeman, MT). A representative voucher was deposited in the Floyd Bartley Herbarium at Ohio University (Table 1).

DNA extraction and PCR amplification of 10 microsatellite loci

We extracted total genomic DNA using the Machery‐Nagel Nucleospin® Plant II kit (Macherey‐Nagel) following the manufacturer's protocol except in the lysis step, in which the lysate was incubated at room temperature for 1 h and DNA was eluted in a 100 μL volume of molecular grade water (see also Krueger‐Hadfield, Roze, et al., 2013).

We used the 10 microsatellite loci described in Crowell, Shainker‐Connelly, Vis, and Krueger‐Hadfield (2024) and described in Table S2 in the Supporting Information. We amplified most loci for most gametophytes using multiplex polymerase chain reactions (PCRs) with a final volume of 15 μL: 2 μL of DNA, forward and reverse primers (see Table S2), 1× Promega GoTaq® Flexi Buffer (Promega, Madison, WI, USA; Cat #M890A), 2 mM of MgCl2 (Promega, Madison, WI, USA; Cat #A351H), 250 μM of each dNTP (Promega, Madison, WI, USA; Cat #R0192), 1 mg · mL−1 of BSA, and 1.0 U of Promega GoTaq® Flexi DNA Polymerase (Promega, Madison, WI, USA; Cat #M829B). For the locus Bgel_056 and for reruns of any individual locus that did not amplify in the first attempt in a multiplex PCR, we used simplex PCRs with a final volume of 15 μL: 2 μL of DNA, 150 nM of the forward labeled primer, 100 nM of the forward unlabelled primer, 250 nM of the unlabelled reverse primer, 1× buffer (Promega, Madison, WI, USA; Cat #M890A), 2 mM of MgCl2, 250 μM of each dNTP (Promega, Madison, WI; Cat #R0192), 1 mg · mL−1 of BSA, and 1 U of Promega GoTaq® Flexi DNA Polymerase (Promega, Madison, WI, USA; Cat #M829B). We used the following PCR program: 95°C for 2 min, followed by 35 cycles of 95°C for 30 s, 59°C for 30 s, and 72°C for 30 s, with a final extension stage of 72°C for 5 min.

We diluted 1.5 μL PCR product in 9.7 μL HiDi formamide (Applied Biosystems, Cat #4311320) and 0.30 μL GS 500 LIZ (Applied Biosystems, Cat #4322682). We scored alleles using Geneious Prime v.2022.2.2 (https://www.geneious.com). We used TANDEM (Matschiner & Salzburger, 2009) to bin alleles and then manually checked each bin (Table S3 in the Supporting Information). The null allele frequency was directly estimated by calculating the percent of gametophytes that did not amplify at each locus after several PCR attempts and after discounting technical errors (see also Krueger‐Hadfield et al., 2011). After several PCR attempts, locus Bgel_071 did not amplify for most gametophytes sampled at sites KY‐CDK, OH‐BOG, and OH‐MCC, suggesting a geographic pattern (see companion paper by Crowell, Shainker‐Connelly, Krueger‐Hadfield, & Vis, 2024). For these three sites, Bgel_071 was removed, so a total of nine loci were used to calculate population genetic summary statistics.

Data analyses

Gametophytes that had any loci with no amplification after several PCR attempts were excluded from subsequent analyses. We then calculated the following summary statistics to describe the reproductive system of sites with n ≥ 6 gametophytes, following the recommendations and methods implemented in Krueger‐Hadfield et al. (2021) and Stoeckel et al. (2021). Sites CT‐BLR and CT‐IRM had fewer than six gametophytes and were thus excluded from the following analyses. We calculated the probability of identity between sibs (pid) to assess whether loci were of high enough resolution to distinguish among individuals (Table 2; Jacquard, 2012; Waits et al., 2001). Next, we computed genotypic richness (R), which provides information on the relative proportion of repeated multilocus genotypes (MLGs), as R=G−1N−1, where G is the number of distinct genotypes (i.e., genets) and N is the number of genotyped gametophytes (Table 2; Figure 3; Dorken & Eckert, 2001). We also calculated genotypic evenness, which provides information about the relative abundance of each MLG in a site (D*; see box 3 in Arnaud‐Haond et al., 2007; Table 2, Figure 3). This value is expected to increase with increasing R if populations are maintained by sexual reproduction (Baums et al., 2006; Krueger‐Hadfield et al., 2021). We plotted genotypic evenness (D*) versus genotypic richness (R) to visualize reproductive system variation. We fit an asymptotic regression to the plot y=0.9131+e0.0038x−1.796 (Figure 4). Finally, to describe spatial patterns of repeated MLGs, we sketched maps of the relative location of gametophytes as they were collected at all sites except OH‐MCC, OH‐TRM, and OH‐TRB. After genotyping, we color‐coded each MLG at a site. Finally, we calculated expected heterozygosity (H E) following Stoeckel et al. (2021).

We calculated pareto β, which describes the distribution of clonal membership. As Batrachospermum gelatinosum is monoicous, intragametophytic selfing results in similar patterns of population genetics to clonality, so pareto β cannot be used to disentangle the effects of clonality and intragametophytic selfing. Krueger‐Hadfield et al. (2021) suggested that pareto β > 2 was associated with low rates of clonality, 0.7 < pareto β < 2 was associated with intermediate clonal rates, and pareto β < 0.7 was associated with high clonal rates (Stoeckel et al., 2021). In our study, we used these ranges but considered the rate of uniparental reproduction to include intragametophytic selfing in addition to asexual reproduction. We calculated pairwise genetic distances for all gametophytes using GenAPoPop (Stoeckel et al., 2024) adapted for haploid data. The maximum possible number of diverging alleles between a pair of gametophytes was 10 for all sites except KY‐CDK, OH‐BOG, and OH‐MCC, as we had 10 loci for those sites. For KY‐CDK, OH‐BOG, and OH‐MCC, the maximum possible number of diverging alleles between a pair of gametophytes was nine because nine loci were used.

For each site, we calculated linkage disequilibrium (r¯d) following Agapow and Burt (2001). To calculate pairwise linkage disequilibrium (LD) between loci within each site, we used Genepop ver.1.1.7 (Rousset, 2008) in R ver. 2022.07.2 (R Core Team, 2022). In partially clonal taxa, both clonality and selfing can lead to an increase in r¯d and LD values, and variance of these values, within a species (Krueger‐Hadfield et al., 2021).

We conducted linear regressions in base R to test if latitude significantly predicted pareto β, r¯d, R, and H E. As predicted for geographic parthenogenesis, we expected that higher latitudes would predict genetic markers of uniparental reproduction (i.e., predicted lower pareto β, higher r¯d, lower R, and lower H E). We then used multiple linear regressions, also in R, to test if current velocity, stream width, and sampling length, as other metrics of “marginality,” significantly predicted pareto β, r¯d, R, and H E. (Stream depth was excluded due to multicollinearity with other predictor variables.) We predicted that higher current velocity, greater stream width, and greater sampling length would promote dispersal, resulting in higher levels of outcrossing (i.e., higher pareto β, lower r¯d, higher R, and higher H E). Pareto β was log‐transformed to fit the assumptions of normality for both analyses.

RESULTS

Genotyping and null alleles

We genotyped a total of 311 gametophytes from 18 sites. Among the gametophyte that did not amplify were one at locus Bgel_021, one at Bgel_052, 13 at Bgel_067, one at Bgel_070, two at Bgel_059, one at Bgel_073, five at Bgel_057, and one at Bgel_056. Fourteen gametophytes did not amplify at one locus; four did not amplify at two loci; and one did not amplify at three loci. Therefore, a total of 19 gametophytes were removed due to missing data. All gametophytes amplified at locus Bgel_053. Locus Bgel_071 displayed a geographic pattern of non‐amplification in which most gametophytes from sites KY‐CDK, OH‐BOG, and OH‐MCC (n = 72) had what we presumed were one or more null alleles, possibly due to an insertion or deletion in the sequences between the primers (see Crowell, Shainker‐Connelly, Krueger‐Hadfield, & Vis, 2024 for more detailed discussion on this pattern). At Bgel_071, null allele frequency reached 22.6% when KY‐CDK, OH‐BOG, and OH‐MCC were included, but 11 gametophytes from other sites amplified at this locus (Table S4 in the Supporting Information). Excluding Bgel_071, null allele frequencies were low overall (0.0%–4.1%; Table S4). For population genetic analyses, the 25 gametophytes with missing data, which we presumed were null alleles, at any locus besides Bgel‐071 were removed. Locus Bgel_071 was excluded from analyses of sites KY‐CDK, OH‐BOG, and OH‐MCC but included in analyses of other sites. This conservative approach was taken since missing data, such as these putative null alleles, would render analyses of genotypic diversity with existing methods challenging.

Genotypic and genetic diversity

The probability of identity (pid) was 0.001 over the 215 samples genotyped with 10 loci (sites AL‐CRC, AL‐YEC, CT‐BLR, CT‐FLB, CT‐IRM, MD‐HOU, MI‐CLI, MI‐CUT, MI‐HLK, MI‐KCL, MI‐TRB, MI‐TRM, OH‐BBC, PA‐COT, RI‐CPR) and 0.942 over the 77 samples genotyped with nine loci (sites KY‐CDK, OH‐BOG, and OH‐MCC). Among sites, pid varied from 1.47 × 10−5 at MI‐CUT (n = 8) to 1 at OH‐BBC (n = 6).

Genotypic richness (R) spanned the entire potential range from 0 to 1. At MI‐CUT, all gametophytes (n = 8) were unique MLGs, whereas at OH‐BBC (n = 6), all genotyped gametophytes belonged to the same MLG (Table 2). The other sites varied in genotypic richness (Table 2, Figure 3). Repeated MLGs were always distributed within and not among sites. The 16 out of 18 sites for which we noted the relative location of gametophytes within a reach, could be broadly categorized into four spatial patterns of repeated MLGs (Figure S1 in the Supporting Information). For example, at OH‐BBC and OH‐BOG, most or all gametophytes shared the same MLG. Though a map of gametophytes was not drawn for OH‐MCC, most gametophytes at this site also shared the same MLG. At AL‐CRC, CT‐FLB, MD‐HOU, MI‐CLI, MI‐KCL, PA‐COT, RI‐CPR, and KY‐CDK, there were clusters of repeated MLGs distributed throughout the sampled reach. Repeated MLGs were evenly distributed throughout the sampled reaches at AL‐YEC and MI‐HLK. At MI‐TRM and MI‐TRB, there were several repeated MLGs, but spatial patterns could not be determined because maps were not drawn during sampling. At MI‐CUT, CT‐BLR, and CT‐IRM, most or all gametophytes had unique MLGs (Figure S1d).

Genotypic evenness (D*) varied from 0 to 1, with most values in the upper half of this range (Table 2). Sites were distributed along the asymptotic relationship of D* versus R and were divided into three categories: high intragametophytic selfing (R < 0.30; includes sites AL‐YEC, MD‐HOU, OH‐BBC, OH‐BOG, OH‐MCC, MI‐CLI, MI‐KCL, MI‐TRM), intermediate intragametophytic selfing (0.30 < R < 0.75, includes sites AL‐CRC, KY‐CDK, PA‐COT, CT‐FLB, RI‐CPR, MI‐HLK, MI‐TRB), and low intragametophytic selfing (R > 0.75, includes site MI‐CUT; Figure 4). Expected heterozygosity (H E) ranged from 0 (OH‐BBC) to 0.417 (MI‐HLK).

Pareto β values ranged from 0.01 (OH‐MCC) to 2.17 (MI‐CUT), with most values less than 0.7 (Table 2). Of the 16 sites for which summary statistics could be calculated, 12 had pareto β < 0.7. Three sites had 0.7 < pareto β < 2, and one site (MI‐CUT) had pareto β > 2 (Table 2, Figure 3). Linkage disequilibrium (r¯d) values ranged from −0.13 (MI‐CLI) to 1 (OH‐BBC, OH‐BOG, OH‐MCC, and MI‐TRM; Table 2, Figure 3). Most sites had low values (<0.50). Pairwise LD between loci could not be tested for most locus pairs because within sites there was little to no variation in the alleles at a locus (Table S5 in the Supporting Information). The maximum number of diverging alleles observed between any pair of individuals was eight (MI‐HLK, MI‐TRB), and the minimum was zero. Most sites had a maximum of four diverging alleles: OH‐BBC, MI‐KCL, OH‐BOG, MI‐CLI, PA‐COT, MD‐HOU, MI‐TRM, CT‐BLR OH‐MCC, CT‐FLB, AL‐CRC, AL‐YEC. The remaining sites had a wider distribution in the number of diverging alleles (Figure 5).

The y‐axis (“count”) represents the number of pairs of gametophytes with the given number of diverging alleles. Site numbers and abbreviations are shown in Table 1.

Patterns of reproductive mode variation, habitat, and latitude

For linear regressions of latitude and log(pareto β), r¯d, R, and H E, the slopes were not significantly different from zero, suggesting that latitude does not predict genetic signatures of selfing or that our sampling resolution did not allow for the detection of a statistically significant relationship (Figure S2 in the Supporting Information). For multiple linear regressions of current velocity, stream width, and sampling length on log(pareto β), r¯d, R, and H E, the slopes were also not significantly different from zero, suggesting that these environmental parameters do not significantly influence the reproductive system or that our sampling resolution did not allow for the detection of a statistically significant relationship (Table S6 in the Supporting Information).

DISCUSSION

In Batrachospermum gelatinosum, we observed genetic signatures of intragametophytic selfing in the sites we sampled across a wide swath of its eastern North American distribution. These findings support the hypothesis of Otto and Marks (1996) that life cycles with longer haploid phases should be correlated with selfing and/or asexual reproduction. Intragametophytic selfing in particular is facilitated by short, unidirectional dispersal of gametes and spores and high fertilization rates (Hambrook & Sheath, 1991). We discuss these results and their consequences for B. gelatinosum and potentially for other monoicous freshwater red algae.

Intraspecific variation in the prevailing reproductive mode

We observed wide intraspecific variations in R and D* values for Batrachospermum gelatinosum, comparable to those observed in the marine red algae Gracilaria chilensis and G. vermiculophylla (as Agarophyton chilense and A. vermiculophyllum; see figure 3 in Krueger‐Hadfield et al., 2021). If we compared these three taxa, the observed patterns in the relationship between D* and R would likely be generated by different reproductive processes. The latter two species, and indeed all the red algae that have been studied to date using population genetic tools, are dioicous, so intragametophytic selfing is not possible, as both a male and a female gametophyte are required for fertilization. Moreover, these two Gracilaria spp. are characterized by a propensity to fragment in farmed (Guillemin et al., 2008) and non‐native sites (Krueger‐Hadfield et al., 2016). Therefore, the variation in reproductive mode indicated by the relationships between R and D* in these taxa is likely driven by variable rates of asexual reproduction via fragmentation. In contrast, asexual reproduction in B. gelatinosum occurs via monospore production in the chantransia, which bypasses the gametophyte stage to produce an offspring chantransia. Additionally, B. gelatinosum is monoicous and can reproduce via intragametophytic selfing, which produces chantransia that are homozygous across the entire genome (Klekowski, 1973; Figure 1a). As a result, we cannot use typical markers for population genetics, such as microsatellites, to distinguish between (i) monospore production in the chantransia or (ii) intragametophytic selfing from gametes produced by the same gametophyte. Yet, we suggest that intragametophytic selfing is common in B. gelatinosum and that intragametophytic selfing rather than monospore production drives variation in R and D* values for this taxon. Gametophytes are not produced through monospore production, only through meiosis in the chantransia, yet we collected gametophytes from all of our sampling sites, including those where only one or two MLGs were present (e.g., OH‐BBC, OH‐BOG, OH‐MCC). Additionally, though the dispersal of red algal spermatia is likely limited (Hambrook & Sheath, 1991; Santelices, 1990; Searles, 1980), we observed even very small B. gelatinosum gametophytes (~1 cm) bearing many carposporophytes, suggesting high fertilization rates (which have also been observed in other species in the Batrachospermales; Hambrook & Sheath, 1991; Necchi & Vis, 2005). For example, at OH‐BBC, we observed only a small number of gametophytes (n = 6), all of which shared the same MLG and bore carposporophytes despite their small sizes. We suggest that these carposporophytes were the result of intragametophytic selfing. The gametophytes at this site were all collected from the same rock. Thus, it is possible that they could be full siblings, further eroding genetic diversity following any cross fertilization. Alternatively, low genetic diversity at this site or other sites we sampled could be the result of a bottleneck following local extinction and recolonization, for example. At OH‐BBC, we observed few gametophytes and hypothesize this is due to sampling early in the season rather than a recent bottleneck event. However, future work with more in‐depth genotyping of both phases of the life cycle should assess the possibility of bottlenecks further eroding genetic diversity.

The prevalence of outcrossing and intergametophytic selfing likely varies as well. The effects of these forms of reproduction will depend on standing levels of genetic diversity and both current and past levels of intragametophytic selfing. For example, if all gametophytes at a site share the same MLG due to historically high levels of intragametophytic selfing, it will be impossible to distinguish among intragametophytic selfing, intergametophytic selfing, and outcrossing—this may be the case at site OH‐BBC. At a site with a higher standing level of genetic diversity, intergametophytic selfing would be expected to gradually rather than immediately erode genetic diversity, though this may only be possible to detect through temporal genotyping. Likewise, outcrossing between gametophytes that share the same MLG would be indistinguishable from intragametophytic selfing but would be expected to maintain genetic diversity in populations with higher standing genetic diversity (e.g., MI‐CUT, MI‐HLK; Table 2, Figure 3).

Aside from intragametophytic selfing, the detection of repeated MLGs could have been due to the resolution of our microsatellite loci. The probability of identity (pid) can be used to determine whether loci provide sufficient resolution to distinguish among individual genotypes, in which lower pid values indicate higher resolution (Waits et al., 2001) and to rank populations based on clonal rates (Stoeckel et al., 2021; Villate et al., 2010). For the sites that were genotyped with 10 loci, the pid value was adequate (pid = 0.001). For sites genotyped with nine loci (KY‐CDK, OH‐BOG, and OH‐MCC), the pid was high (pid = 0.942), indicating a higher probability that poor marker resolution resulted in the detection of repeated MLGs. However, among the sites genotyped with nine loci, 29 out of 30 gametophytes at OH‐BOG shared the same MLG, and 27 out of 28 gametophytes at OH‐MCC shared the same MLG. Based on our qualitative observations of population size, we think it is unlikely that this high proportion of gametophytes shared the same MLG by chance. Additionally, at KY‐CDK, there was one repeated MLG shared by 10 out of 19 gametophytes. The 10 gametophytes that shared the same MLG were dispersed through the stream reach. However, a subset of these samples were collected from the same rock or from adjacent rocks and, consequently, have a high probability of originating from the same parental chantransia. Therefore, if intragametophytic selfing is as common as our data suggest, high pid values may be a result of the instantenous erosion of diversity rather than poor marker resolution. Future work will need to genotype the diploid chantransia to measure heterozygosity and perform temporal analyses to assess selfing versus clonal rates more accurately (e.g., Becheler et al., 2017).

Repeated MLGs were often observed within sites, but no MLGs were repeated among sites. This finding was consistent with that of a previous study that showed that each stream reach was genetically distinct (Hall & Vis, 2002) for the freshwater red algal species Virescentia viride‐americana (as Batrachospermum helminthosum), which can be monoicous, dioicous, or trioicous (Vis & Necchi, 2021; see terms defined in Krueger‐Hadfield et al., 2024). It is possible that the patterns of repeated MLGs within sites are due to mitotic amplification of the zygote within the same carposporophyte (hypothesized as a mechanism to compensate for low fertilization rates, Searles, 1980; referred to as cystocarpic reproduction or amplification in marine reds, Krueger‐Hadfield, Roze, et al., 2013; Krueger‐Hadfield et al., 2015), but studies of dioicous marine red macroalgae have not observed evidence of repeated tetrasporophytic genotypes (Engel et al., 2004; Krueger‐Hadfield, Roze, et al., 2013). Mitotic amplification of the zygote would generate repeated chantransia genotypes, so it will be necessary to genotype chantransia in future studies. Studies of other monoicous haploid‐diploid taxa, including mosses and the red alga Porphyra sp., have also shown high differentiation among sites with little variation within sites (Eppley et al., 2007; Fujio et al., 1985; Lindstrom, 1993—but studies of Porphyra sp. used isozymes, which are less informative than microsatellite loci). This pattern should be further explored across other monoicous, haploid‐diploid taxa to better understand the effect of monoicy on patterns of population structure.

Spatial patterns of repeated MLGs

Visualizing spatial patterns of repeated MLGs can be useful for discerning how different types of reproduction and patterns of dispersal may generate, maintain, or erode genotypic diversity (e.g., Ardehed et al., 2015; Krueger‐Hadfield, Roze, et al., 2013). At most sites, there were several repeated MLGs distributed throughout the stream reach. At some sites, the MLGs formed clusters (i.e., multiple gametophytes with the same MLG fixed to the same rock or to adjacent rocks) observed in multiple locations throughout the stream (e.g., Cripple Creek, Figure S1b). At other sites, MLGs did not cluster but were distributed evenly throughout the sampling area (e.g., Yellow Creek, Figure S1c). This difference in the spatial distribution of repeated MLGs is likely driven by in‐stream dispersal patterns rather than differences in reproductive mode. Limited dispersal would generate clusters of repeated MLGs if carpospores resulting from intragametophytic selfing settled near their genetically identical gametophytic parent. If water flow and carpospore viability allowed for further dispersal, then repeated MLGs would be distributed throughout the sampling area rather than forming a cluster. Additionally, carposporophytes themselves may act as dispersal agents by detaching and drifting downstream (Hambrook & Sheath, 1991). There were a few sites at which all or most gametophytes shared the same MLG (e.g., OH‐MCC and OH‐BOG, Figure S1a). There were also several sites where most or all gametophytes had unique MLGs (e.g., MI‐CUT, Figure S1d). The roles of asexual versus sexual reproduction and stream flow dispersal dynamics remain to be tested.

In freshwater red algae, asexual reproduction occurs most commonly via monospore production by chantransia, although the frequency of monospore production is unknown. Monospores “recycle” the chantransia and bypass the gametophyte stage. We only sampled sites where gametophytes were present. In other words, meiosis had already generated new gametophytes. Moreover, many gametophytes bore carposporophytes, indicating that gametogenesis and fertilization had taken place. Monospore production could produce repeated MLGs, but genotyping of chantransia is needed to determine heterozygosity levels and the spatial distribution of chantransia genotypes. Additionally, genotyping chantransia would provide the necessary data to determine if there is genetic differentiation between haploid and diploid sub‐populations, another signature of clonality (e.g., Sosa et al., 1998; but see Stoeckel et al., 2021 because partial clonality is expected to act as a homogenizing force between haploid and diploid sub‐populations). With these data, we can begin to determine the relative rates of sexual versus asexual processes generating the patterns we observed as well as testing our hypothesis of the prevalence of intragametophytic selfing in Batrachospermum gelatinosum.

Potential drivers of reproductive system variation

According to Baker's law, uniparental reproduction (i.e., selfing, asexual reproduction, or both) is expected to be advantageous in marginal environments, such as during the colonization of new habitats (Baker, 1955; Pannell et al., 2015). Many studies have used latitude as a proxy for marginality, as the edges of species' ranges are often at high latitudes where there may be less dense populations and shorter growing seasons (Bierzychudek, 1985; Peck et al., 1998), although most data are northern hemisphere biased (Tilquin & Kokko, 2016). We observed no evidence of higher levels of intragametophytic selfing at high latitudes in Batrachospermum gelatinosum (Figure S2). It may be that we did not detect differences because we did not sample the entire latitudinal range. We sampled sites from Alabama to the upper peninsula of Michigan (Figure 2), but the species range extends northward into the boreal forests and subarctic North America (Vis & Necchi, 2021). Additionally, it is possible that B. gelatinosum undergoes higher rates of asexual reproduction than intragametophytic selfing at higher latitudes. Future studies would need to sample the chantransia to determine whether solely asexual populations are located at higher latitudes or under other marginal environmental conditions.

It may be possible that high latitude habitats are not marginal for this species. Instead, variation in the reproductive system may be influenced more by habitat divergences caused by local and small‐scale environmental patterns rather than by larger biogeographic patterns (e.g., tidal height, Fierst et al., 2010; Krueger‐Hadfield, Kübler, & Dudgeon, 2013; wetland stability, Yakimowski & Barrett, 2014; heavy metal contamination, Antonovics, 1970; soil conditions, Rajakaruna, 2004; the presence of herbicides, Kuester et al., 2017; and differences in microhabitat, Johnson & Shaw, 2015). We considered this possibility and tested for the effects of the physical stream environment for which we had measurements, including current velocity, stream depth, stream width, and sampling length, on population genetic parameters and did not detect any significant relationships. However, we had a small sample size with which to conduct these analyses (n = 7 sites) because not all environmental parameters were measured at all sites. Additionally, our measurements may not have accounted for the complexity of the physical factors influencing the marginality of a habitat. In fact, Hall and Vis (2002) have suggested that patterns of gene flow are more complex than unidirectional movement from upstream to downstream. Fertilization is likely facilitated by turbulent eddies, which may allow male gametes to pass by female gametophytes multiple times (Hambrook & Sheath, 1991), and by the entanglement of gametophytes in close physical proximity of one another (Necchi, 1997). More in‐depth hydrological measurements paired with measurements of gametophyte density within a stream may be required to detect differences among sites and to assess “marginality” for Batrachospermum gelatinosum. Additionally, it may be important to account for temporal variance in physical stream parameters. Physical conditions vary throughout seasons and from year to year (Dettinger & Diaz, 2000). Variance may play an important role in shaping the marginality of a given site, with greater variance making a habitat more marginal (see Yakimowski & Barrett, 2014—uniparental reproduction more frequently occurred in ephemeral compared to stable wetlands). Environmental fluctuations, in turn, are assumed to drive the evolution of reproductive modes within populations and species (Pierre et al., 2022). It is also possible that the physical stream conditions at the time of propagule release (e.g., male gametes, carpospores, or monospores) have greater influence in shaping dispersal, fertilization rates, and propagule settlement than the conditions at the time of our sampling (see Johnson & Shaw, 2015—the physical microhabitat shaped fertilization rates of Sphagnum mosses, driving inter‐population variation in the reproductive system).

The evolutionary costs and benefits of selfing

Inbreeding depression is one of the most important factors driving the evolution of reproductive systems (Charlesworth & Charlesworth, 1979; Schemske & Lande, 1985). Intragametophytic selfing combined with a prolonged haploid stage that undergoes somatic development and is thus exposed to selection, may facilitate the efficient purging of deleterious mutations such that inbreeding depression would be rare (Otto & Marks, 1996; Taylor et al., 2007). When deleterious mutations are purged from a population, selfing can be stable and even advantageous (Fisher, 1949; Lande & Schemske, 1985). It makes sense that intragametophytic selfing would be widespread in Batrachospermum gelatinosum as a monoicous taxon because as selfing rates decrease, inbreeding depression becomes more severe (Lande & Schemske, 1985), and dioicy would likely be selected for as a mechanism to prevent inbreeding depression (Eppley et al., 2007). If selfing is common enough to purge deleterious mutations from a population, then it may become advantageous, and monoicy may evolve to promote this strategy (Fisher, 1949; Lande & Schemske, 1985). In ferns, the capacity for intragametophytic selfing is widespread but is rarely the sole reproductive strategy. Intragametophytic selfing is more often observed with outcrossing, intergametophytic selfing, or both, suggesting that intragametophytic selfing is likely not adaptive on its own long‐term (Sessa et al., 2016). This finding corresponds with ours—the contribution of intragametophytic selfing varies among B. gelatinosum sites, suggesting that intergametophytic selfing and/or outcrossing may also occur at varying rates. In other words, B. gelatinosum likely has a stable mixed mating strategy. No dioicous or trioicous B. gelatinosum sites have been observed to date (Vis & Necchi Jr, 2021; see definitions in Krueger‐Hadfield et al., 2024). Future studies should investigate reproductive system variation in species with monoicous, dioicous, and/or trioicous populations to test these hypotheses.

Population bottlenecks also increase the probability of purging deleterious mutations (Glémin, 2003; Kirkpatrick & Jarne, 2000). Bottlenecks are associated with monoicous taxa that are likely to undergo frequent colonization and recolonization events because only one monoicous individual is needed to start a new population (Freckleton & Watkinson, 2002; Hanski & Gaggiotti, 2004). More phenological and life history trait studies are required to pair with population genetic data to understand the dynamics of colonization, recolonization, and bottlenecks in freshwater red algae.

Our results suggest that intragametophytic selfing is common in the monoicous, haploid‐diploid species Batrachospermum gelatinosum. Although intragametophytic selfing likely occurs in all populations to some extent, its prevalence varies among sites, and the drivers of this variation have yet to be elucidated. Future population genetic studies on freshwater red algal taxa with varying sexual systems, combined with finer scale environmental data taken at multiple time points, would expand our knowledge of the conditions that drive the evolution of different strategies. Together, these data will improve our understanding of the variations in reproductive systems in eukaryotes and the evolutionary ecology of sex.
