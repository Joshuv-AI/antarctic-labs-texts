Comparative analysis of heterologous lactate dehydrogenase expression in Ogataea polymorpha for enhanced L-Lactic acid production
Authors: Aksyniia Tsaruk, Justyna Ruchala, Roksolana Vasylyshyn, Kostyantyn Dmytruk, Andriy Sibirny
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Lactic acid (LA) is a key platform chemical with broad industrial applications, including biodegradable plastics and pharmaceuticals. While microbial fermentation enables the sustainable production of optically pure L-lactic acid, challenges persist in substrate flexibility and process optimisation. Thermotolerant methylotrophic yeast Ogataea polymorpha has been tested as an alternative biocatalyst for sustainable production of lactic acid, a key platform compound for bioplastics and green manufacturing. However, the influence of lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) origin on metabolic flux redirection in this chassis has not been systematically investigated. This study presents the first comparative evaluation of heterologous LDHs from fungal, bacterial, protistan and mammalian sources expressed in O. polymorpha and assessed across four carbon substrates (glucose, xylose, glycerol, and methanol). The recombinant strains were evaluated for lactic acid production on glucose, xylose, and methanol under various aeration and pH-controlled conditions. Among the tested constructs, the LDH_Bt strain expressing bovine LDH exhibited the highest lactic acid titres from glucose (up to 72.6 g/L), particularly under neutralised conditions with CaCO₃, while LDH_Ro achieved the highest titre on xylose under high agitation with a neutraliser (9.59 g/L). Under methanol-based conditions, LDH_Pf produced the highest titre among the strains under pH-controlled, low-agitation conditions (1.12 g/L). However, the highest absolute methanol-derived titre was observed for LDH_Lp at 220 rpm without CaCO₃ supplementation (1.27 g/L). Despite having an intact ethanol biosynthesis pathway, the obtained transformants produced very low amounts of ethanol (typically below 0.3 g/L, with occasional higher values depending on fermentation conditions). Lactic acid formation from xylose, glycerol, and methanol was notably lower compared to glucose, indicating substrate-dependent redox and compartmentalisation constraints. A weak-to-moderate association between LDH transcript level and lactic acid titre was observed, suggesting that expression contributes to flux control but does not solely determine pathway efficiency. These findings demonstrate that enzyme origin and substrate context jointly define metabolic output and highlight O. polymorpha as a promising platform for thermotolerant lactic acid bioproduction.

Introduction

Lactic acid (LA) is an organic compound commonly occurring in nature and broadly used in various industries, from food and chemical production to pharmaceutical and medical applications as a preservative, pH regulator, solvent, and antioxidant [1]. Recently, the product of lactic acid polymerisation, polylactic acid (PLA), is gaining popularity as an alternative to plastics derived from byproducts of fossil fuel processing due to its environmental benefits (biodegradability) and physical properties [2]. PLA is utilised for a variety of applications, including packaging materials and biodegradable textiles manufacture, and in medicine for drug delivery, tissue engineering, and in implants [3]. As a consequence, the global lactic acid market size was assessed at USD 3.37 billion in 2023 and is predicted to grow at a compound annual growth rate of 8.0% from 2024 to 2030 [4].

Chemical synthesis of lactic acid requires high energy input and petrochemical materials derived from fossil fuels [5]. Moreover, it produces a racemic mixture of both L-lactic acid and D-lactic acid isomers, which makes the purification stage more complex and costly. It is not favourable for lactic acid polymerisation since the enantiomeric constitution heavily impacts the physical properties of the polymer [6]. On the contrary, microbial lactic acid production allows obtaining an optically pure product from more sustainable substrates using less energy [7]. The most common way to produce lactic acid through fermentation is using lactic acid bacteria; however, other microorganisms, such as fungi, yeasts, and algae, might be implemented for this purpose [1, 8]. Metabolic engineering allows the use of microorganisms incapable of natural lactic acid production through heterologous expression of lactate dehydrogenase genes and alteration of metabolic pathways of bioproducts competing for the metabolic flux, such as ethanol and glycerol. While lactic acid bacteria remain the most efficient natural producers of optically pure lactic acid, they lack tolerance to high temperature and low pH, and require expensive nutrient supplementation [9]. Yeast hosts such as Saccharomyces cerevisiae or Kluyveromyces marxianus have been successfully engineered for lactic acid production but still exhibit limitations in redox balance and substrate range [10–12]. Ogataea polymorpha offers several advantages as a non-conventional chassis: high thermotolerance, methylotrophy, low nutrient demand, and a robust genetic toolbox [13–15]. These features motivate its evaluation as an alternative cell factory for organic acid biosynthesis.

The most common substrates for microbial lactic acid production are first-generation feedstock [9]. Food-grade sugars and starchy materials are easier to process; however, their use contributes to the high price of the end product and raises environmental and ethical concerns. Alternative carbon sources, such as methanol, lignocellulose, industrial, agricultural, and municipal waste, present an abundant and sustainable replacement without creating competition with food crops [7]. Considering this issue, using O. polymorpha for lactic acid production has another advantage – the ability to metabolise a vast range of low-cost and renewable substrates, such as xylose, methanol, and glycerol [14].

O. polymorpha has been previously used as a host for the production of recombinant proteins (enzymes, vaccines, pharmaceuticals), biofuels, high-value chemicals (glutathione, hyaluronic acid, terpenoids, free fatty acid, 3-hydroxypropionate, malic acid, acetone) [13, 15]. Lactic acid production from methanol by engineered O. polymorpha was reported by Wefelmeier [16]. In this study, we explored lactic acid production on various substrates by engineered O. polymorpha strains with heterologous expression of lactate dehydrogenase genes of different (fungal, mammal, protistan, and bacterial) origins.

The objective of this study was to systematically evaluate the impact of heterologous LDH gene expression from taxonomically diverse organisms on lactic acid production in the engineered O. polymorpha strains grown on renewable carbon sources, including glucose, xylose, methanol, and glycerol under varying fermentation conditions. However, systematic comparisons of LDHs from taxonomically distinct sources in this thermotolerant methylotrophic yeast are lacking, leaving unclear how enzyme origin influences pyruvate redirection and redox balance.

Shake flask fermentations

Yeast cells were cultivated in a rich YP medium (10 g/L yeast extract, 20 g/L peptone) with 20 g/L glucose, xylose, methanol, or glycerol as a sole carbon source to obtain biomass for fermentation inoculation. Cells were harvested by centrifugation at 4000 rpm for 5 min. Flask fermentations were performed in 100 mL flasks with a total volume of YNB medium of 40 mL containing 100 g/L glucose, 100 g/L xylose, 100 g/L glycerol, or 20 g/L methanol, respectively, at 45 °C and with shaking at 140 rpm or 220 rpm. Medium was additionally supplemented with 0.4 g/L leucine. CaCO3 was used for medium neutralisation at a concentration of 45 g/L. The initial biomass concentration for fermentation experiments was 2 g/L (dry weight). Fermentations were repeated at least three times in independent experiments.

Quantitative analysis

Biomass was determined by measuring the absorbance at 590 nm in a 10 mm cuvette. For biomass measurement in medium supplemented with CaCO3, 0.2 M H2SO4 was used to prepare dilutions of samples instead of water. L-lactic acid and ethanol concentrations in fermentation broth were measured using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC; Shimadzu Prominence-i LC-2030 C 3D Plus) with an Agilent MetaCarb 87 H column. Lactate was analyzed using refractive index detector (RID) A 7.5 mM H2SO4 was used as the mobile phase under isocratic conditions, and the flow rate was set to 0.6 mL/min at 35 °C. 1 mL of the fermentation sample was centrifuged at 5000 rpm for 5 min, and the supernatant was filtered through a syringe filter (pore size 0.2 μm).

RNA isolation and RT-PCR

Total RNA was extracted from O. polymorpha transformants expressing LDH genes from different sources using GeneMATRIX Universal RNA Purification Kit with DNAseI (EURx Ltd., Gdansk, Poland). qRT-PCR involved the 7500 Fast Real-Time PCR System (The Applied Biosystems, USA), with SG OneStep qRT-PCR kit (EURx Ltd., Gdansk, Poland). The pairs of primers used for qRT-PCR are listed in Table 3. Gene expression was quantified using a comparative Ct approach based on the 2⁻ΔΔCt calculation. The native O. polymorpha strain NCYC495 served as the calibrator, and ACT1 (actin gene) was used as the internal reference gene. As the wild-type NCYC495 strain does not possess any endogenous gene encoding lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), no specific amplification was detected with LDH-targeting primers. For the purpose of relative quantification, the LDH Ct value of the parental strain was therefore set to the assay’s limit of detection, and its relative expression (RQ) was defined as 1. Consequently, the reported fold changes in LDH transcript levels for the transformants represent expression above this background threshold rather than comparison to a physiologically expressed native LDH gene.

Enzyme activity assay

LDH and ADH activity were measured in cell-free extracts of respective strains cultivated at 37 °C at 220 rpm on YNB + 2% glucose (20 mL) for 24 h. Cells were harvested by centrifugation at 4000 rpm for 5 min. The cell pellets were resuspended in disintegration buffer (100 mM Tris-HCl, 1mM PMSF, 1mM DTT) and disrupted mechanically. One unit of L-LDH activity was defined as the amount of enzyme oxidising 1 µmol of NADH to NAD⁺ per minute at room temperature. One unit of ADH activity was defined as the amount of enzyme releasing 1 µmol of NADH per minute at room temperature.

Statistical analysis

The experimental data were collected from at least three independent experiments to ensure reproducibility of the observed trends. Error bars indicate the standard deviation (SD) from the mean values.

Results

Expression of LDHs of different origins in the wild-type strain of O. polymorpha

LDH catalyses the conversion of pyruvate to lactate and is encoded by the LDH gene. Metabolic flux at the pyruvate branch point was shown to be strongly influenced by LDH characteristics in lactic acid-producing S. cerevisiae and Candida sonorensis strains expressing LDHs of different origins, which demonstrated variability in lactic acid and ethanol yields, as well as biomass accumulation [10, 21]. To find the best-suited LDH gene for heterologous expression in the O. polymorpha NCYC495 strain, it was transformed with plasmids containing LDHs from different organisms. The expression of those LDHs has already been reported in other yeast species. LDHs from the fungus R. oryzae, the mammal B. taurus, the protist P. falciparum, and the bacterium L. plantarum were selected for this purpose. Codon-optimised LDH genes were placed under the control of the strong constitutive promoter of the GAP1 gene. The obtained integrative plasmids were used to transform the NCYC495 strain. It resulted in engineering LDH_Ro, LDH_Bt, LDH_Pf, and LDH_Lp strains.

L-lactic acid production by the obtained O. polymorpha transformants expressing heterologous LDH genes was studied in shake flask fermentation on minimal YNB medium containing either 10% glucose or 10% xylose to determine the impact each carbon source has on lactic acid production. Figure 1 demonstrates lactic acid production and biomass growth by transformant strains. The LDH_Bt strain produced the highest titer of lactic acid (5.25 g/L) on xylose, while LDH_Pf showed superior performance on glucose (3.27 g/L) among the other strains. This difference likely reflects enzyme-specific kinetic properties and NADH affinity - the bovine LDH is known to perform efficiently under microaerobic conditions, whereas the P. falciparum LDH exhibits high catalytic turnover at elevated NADH/NAD⁺ ratios, potentially favoured in glucose-grown cultures [10, 22]. The LDH_Ro, LDH_Bt, and LDH_Lp strains produced a maximum of 1.46, 1.73, and 2.13 g/L of lactic acid on glucose, respectively. When xylose was used as a sole carbon source, the LDH_Ro, LDH_Pf, and LDH_Lp strains produced a maximum of 2.24, 3.51, and 1.55 g/L of lactic acid, respectively.

Ethanol accumulation was minimal (< 0.1 g/L), indicating that LDH activity successfully redirected a portion of the pyruvate flux from alcoholic fermentation toward lactate formation. This effect suggests that even without the deletion of PDC or ADH, the heterologous LDH can effectively compete for cytosolic pyruvate under oxygen-limited conditions [10, 23, 24].

The initial cell concentration used for flask fermentation was 2 g/L. For the LDH_Pf strain, biomass increased to 2.21 g/L after 120 h of cultivation on glucose medium. The biomass of the LDH_Lp didn’t change drastically, and the biomass of the LDH_Ro and LDH_Bt strains gradually decreased throughout fermentation on glucose medium. The reduced growth of the transformant strains was likely influenced by the low pH of the medium due to the presence of lactic acid. Additionally, harsh fermentation conditions also affect biomass growth, especially on culture media based on carbon sources that are not as easily metabolised as glucose.

The effect of increased agitation on lactic acid production

To study the effect of cultural medium aeration on lactic acid production, fermentation was also performed with shaking at 220 rpm. Enhanced agitation (220 rpm) increased lactic acid formation in LDH_Bt, suggesting that improved oxygen transfer facilitated NADH reoxidation and sustained glycolytic flux (Fig. 2). In contrast, other strains showed reduced titres under high agitation on glucose-based medium, consistent with a metabolic shift toward respiration rather than fermentative lactate synthesis. However, lactic acid production from xylose wasn’t significantly affected by increased agitation. Biomass formation remained relatively unchanged across agitation regimes, implying that oxygen availability mainly affected the redox balance and metabolite partitioning rather than overall growth kinetics.

Lactic acid production in CaCO3-supplemented medium.

Weak acid stress caused by the presence of organic acids, including lactic acid, can negatively affect yeast metabolism [25]. Commonly, neutralising agents are used to maintain pH values optimal for the fermentation process. Firstly, different concentrations of СaСO3 were tested from 10 to 45 g/L, and supplementation with 45 g/L demonstrated the biggest increase in lactic acid production (it corresponds to higher lactic acid production). This finding is supported by other research describing the dependence of lactic acid yield on added CaCO3 concentrations [21]. Thus, in our study, to prevent medium acidification, the culture medium was supplemented with 45 g/L CaCO3 to neutralise lactic acid and maintain a stable pH in the medium. NaHCO3 was also tested as a medium neutraliser in different concentrations; however, it exhibited some drawbacks compared to CaCO3, which makes the latter the best suited for application in shake flask fermentations (data not shown). Nonetheless, the implementation of CaCO3 has been reported to yield the best results for lactic acid production by metabolically engineered yeast compared to other neutralisers, such as KOH and NaOH [26].

As shown in Fig. 3, neutralising the medium with CaCO₃ notably improved lactic acid titres across all strains, confirming that acidification was a key inhibitory factor. The maintenance of near-neutral pH prevented intracellular acid stress, sustaining metabolic activity and LDH performance. The LDH_Bt strain again achieved the highest lactic acid concentration (69.27 g/L), reinforcing that mammalian LDH performs optimally under the physicochemical conditions of the yeast cytosol and benefits most from pH stabilisation. This titre is comparable to those reported for optimised S. cerevisiae and K. phaffii systems expressing heterologous LDH (typically 60–80 g/L under fed-batch or pH-controlled conditions [10, 27, 28]), underscoring that O. polymorpha can reach competitive yields even without multi-copy integration or extensive pathway deletions. The lowest increase in lactic acid production under these conditions was observed for the LDH_Ro strain, which produced 9.94 g/L of lactic acid after 120 h of cultivation. All strains exhibited the same lactic acid production kinetics, characterised by a gradual increase in lactic acid throughout fermentation, reaching a peak after 120 h of incubation. On the contrary, lactic acid production from xylose under these conditions increased only for the LDH_Ro strain, reaching merely 4.51 g/L. Other transformant strains demonstrated an opposite trend of decrease in lactic acid production, with the most notable decline for the LDH_Bt strain to 0.82 g/L from 5.25 g/L under conditions without pH control.

No significant difference in biomass was observed among the strains grown on both carbon sources, except for the LDH_Pf strain, which sustained improved biomass accumulation on xylose-containing medium.

Lactic acid production from methanol

Methanol is an alternative substrate that has gained a lot of attention in recent years, particularly for microbial production using methylotrophic microorganisms to obtain organic acids and fatty acids [29]. The ability of O. polymorpha to utilise methanol makes it a potential cell factory for the conversion of methanol to lactic acid.

The LDH_Pf strain outperformed other variants on methanol, though titres remained low (< 1.2 g/L) (Fig. 5). This suggests that the P. falciparum enzyme can operate under the high NADH/NAD⁺ ratios generated by methanol oxidation, but overall flux to cytosolic pyruvate remains limited by peroxisomal compartmentalisation and redox imbalance. Lactic acid reached 0.67 g/L after 48 h of cultivation, but then gradually decreased. Medium neutralisation with CaCO3 further enhanced lactic acid production by this strain, reaching 1.12 g/L after 120 h of cultivation. Supplementation of culture medium with a neutraliser also increased lactic acid production for other transformant strains. In methanol-based fermentations, CaCO₃ supplementation led to a decrease in biomass for the LDH_Ro and LDH_Pf strains, whereas biomass remained largely unchanged for the LDH_Bt and LDH_Lp strains under comparable conditions. This indicates that the impact of pH neutralisation on growth during methanol utilisation is strain-specific rather than universal.

For the LDH_Ro strain, lactic acid production from methanol was the same under oxygen-limited conditions and with shaking at 220 rpm (Fig. 6). For the LDH_Bt strain, lactic acid production increased with increased agitation both with and without medium neutralisation. For the LDH_Pf strains, lactic acid production from methanol without medium neutralisation was completely abolished after 24 h of cultivation. With CaCO3 supplementation, lactic acid production remained consistent throughout the fermentation but didn’t exceed the titer of lactic acid produced under oxygen-limited conditions. For the LDH_Lp strain, lactic acid production reached its peak of 1.27 g/L with shaking at 220 rpm and no medium neutralisation. The addition of CaCO3 didn’t increase lactic acid production by this strain from methanol.

The LDH_Ro strain demonstrated an increase in biomass during fermentation on methanol under oxygen-limited conditions without medium neutralisation. With medium neutralisation and under oxygen-limited conditions, a notable decrease in cell biomass was observed. For the LDH_Bt and LDH_Lp strains, no significant difference in biomass was observed under oxygen-limited conditions, regardless of the presence of medium neutralisation. The LDH_Pf strain showed a similar increase in biomass to the LDH_Ro strain when shaken at 140 rpm without medium neutralisation. However, unlike for the LDH_Ro, the biomass of the LDH_Pf strain remained consistent throughout the cultivation in CaCO3-supplemented medium. We conclude that methanol is a poor substrate for lactic acid synthesis by the recombinant strains studied, and its further improvements will require additional strain engineering.

Lactic acid production from glycerol

Glycerol is an abundant and inexpensive waste product of biofuel manufacturing, which presents 77an alternative to feedstock sugars for the production of high-value chemicals [30]. Successful microbial biosynthesis of lactic acid from glycerol by yeast has been reported in the literature [27, 28]. The microbial conversion of glycerol to lactic acid has the potential to become an environmentally and economically sustainable solution for both biodiesel and lactic acid industries. This approach requires advances in strain engineering and process optimisation.

The ability of the obtained O. polymorpha transformants to assimilate glycerol for lactic acid production was studied under different agitation and neutralisation conditions. Under conditions with slower agitation at 140 rpm, the LDH_Ro and LDH_Pf strains showed modest lactate synthesis (≈ 0.8 g/L) from glycerol, indicating that the native metabolic network of O. polymorpha channels most carbon toward respiration rather than fermentative pathways (Fig. 7). Glycerol catabolism likely generates excess NADH, which may not be efficiently reoxidised by heterologous LDH under these conditions. Notably, the LDH_Ro and LDH_Lp strains also produced ethanol, 0.4 g/L and 0.15 g/L, respectively. Supplementation of the medium with CaCO3 led to a decrease in lactic acid production. Biomass accumulation also decreased in neutralised medium for the LDH_Ro and LDH_Pf strains, contrary to the LDH_Bt and LDH_Lp strains. However, in the medium with CaCO3, a drop in lactic acid titres towards the end of fermentation, like the one during cultivation without a neutraliser, wasn’t observed.

The lowest relative expression of LDH in the LDH_Ro strain corresponded to the lowest LDH activity among the studied transformant strains (Table 6). Although LDH_Bt displayed the strongest transcriptional upregulation (~ 45-fold vs. control), its enzymatic activity was not the highest among variants. This discrepancy implies potential post-translational constraints, such as suboptimal folding, codon bias, or differences in cofactor affinity, which decouple mRNA levels from catalytic performance. The P. falciparum LDH exhibited the highest measured specific activity, consistent with its naturally high catalytic turnover. This result correlates with the strain’s better performance on methanol, where high NADH levels favour its function. These results suggest that high gene expression doesn’t correspond to high enzyme activity, and some other factors might affect it. This requires additional data on enzyme activity under different conditions to draw meaningful conclusions. A weak-to-moderate positive association was observed between LDH transcript abundance (RQ) and maximal lactic acid titre across the four recombinant strains grown on glucose. Linear regression analysis yielded R² = 0.25 (r = 0.50), indicating that transcription level contributes to flux redirection toward lactate, although additional factors such as enzyme kinetics, redox balance, temperature stability, and lactate export also influence overall performance.

Discussion

This study provides a comparative evaluation of heterologous LDH enzymes from taxonomically diverse sources expressed in O. polymorpha, highlighting how enzyme origin determines the efficiency of pyruvate flux redirection towards L-lactate. To our knowledge, this is the first systematic comparison of fungal, mammalian, protistan, and bacterial LDHs in this thermotolerant methylotrophic yeast. The recombinant O. polymorpha strains expressing LDHs from R. oryzae, B. taurus, P. falciparum, and L. plantarum (LDH_Ro, LDH_Bt, LDH_Pf, and LDH_Lp, respectively) exhibited marked differences in lactate titres and biomass accumulation, confirming that LDH origin substantially affects metabolic flux partitioning and redox balance. The results obtained from performing fermentations using various carbon sources, including glucose, xylose, methanol, and glycerol, suggest that the activity of particular LDHs is dependent on substrate and the host’s metabolism and is potentially affected by factors such as enzyme kinetics and expression levels.

On xylose, the highest lactic acid titre was obtained by the LDH_Ro strain under high-agitation, CaCO₃-neutralised conditions (9.59 g/L), while LDH_Pf and LDH_Bt produced lower titres under the same parameters. This demonstrates that the optimal LDH variant on xylose differs from that observed for glucose and depends strongly on both substrate metabolism and fermentation conditions. Slight differences in cofactor affinity (Kₘ, NADH) or turnover (kcat) between LDHs can therefore shift the redox equilibrium. This indicates a partial redirection of pyruvate flux from ethanol to lactate formation despite the presence of active PDC and ADH pathways, aligning with previous findings [16]. However, usually yeast strains with an intact ethanol pathway and heterologous expression of LDH genes produce both ethanol and lactic acid in detectable amounts [10, 11, 23, 24, 31, 32]. These results may reflect differential coupling between glycolytic flux, NADH regeneration, and ATP yield. Since LDH competes with pyruvate decarboxylase and alcohol dehydrogenase for both pyruvate and cytosolic NADH, subtle variations in LDH cofactor preference and turnover can substantially alter the intracellular redox balance, determining whether carbon flux proceeds towards lactate or ethanol. These results collectively support a model in which LDH performance in O. polymorpha is governed by NADH availability, competition with Pdc1/Adh, enzyme-specific kcat/Km ratios, and lactate export capacity.

The influence of aeration on lactic acid synthesis appeared to be enzyme- and substrate-specific. In general, higher agitation decreased lactic acid production, consistent with reports that oxygen availability favours ethanol and biomass formation in LDH-expressing yeasts with intact PDC and ADH pathways [24, 27]. On the contrary, for engineered strains with defective genes involved in ethanol biosynthesis, increased aeration leads to higher lactic acid production from glucose, but not from xylose [12, 24, 33]. Only the LDH_Bt strain showed improved titres at 220 rpm on media with glucose and xylose without CaCO3 supplementation, suggesting that its activity may be less sensitive to oxidative NADH depletion.

Oxygen was the primary constraint on medium with glycerol, with CaCO₃ either reducing titres or providing additional benefit, suggesting subtle differences in enzyme stability or flux partitioning between different LDHs. Several studies on K. phaffii with heterologous expression of bovine LDH using glycerol as a substrate for lactic acid production showed that homologous overexpression of putative lactic acid transporter and disruption of PDC (pyruvate decarboxylase) and ArDH (arabitol dehydrogenase) increased titre and yield, which can be implemented for O. polymorpha to improve the efficiency of glycerol to lactic acid conversion [27, 28]. In contrast to glucose, glycerol metabolism in yeasts is predominantly respiratory and characterised by efficient mitochondrial NADH reoxidation, which may limit cytosolic NADH availability required for LDH-catalysed lactate formation [34, 35]. Under non-neutralised conditions, mild acid stress may partially restrict respiratory activity and promote redox balancing via fermentative pathways. The addition of CaCO₃ stabilises pH and alleviates acid stress, favouring respiratory metabolism and biomass formation rather than NADH consumption through LDH. This shift in metabolic balance may reduce flux toward lactate production in some transformants, as observed for the LDH_Ro strain (Fig. 8). Similar relationships between environmental conditions, redox balance, and organic acid production have been reported in engineered yeasts. Additionally, Ca²⁺ ions may influence intracellular pH homeostasis and membrane transport processes, further contributing to strain-dependent differences in lactate production efficiency [36]. The maintained high lactate production observed for the LDH_Lp strain suggests that enzyme-specific kinetic properties, including cofactor affinity and stability at near-neutral pH, may determine sensitivity to environmental conditions.

A significant improvement in obtained lactic acid titres was achieved due to the introduction of CaCO3 to the cultural medium as a neutralising agent. Lactic acid titres obtained in the neutralised medium were increased at higher agitation, where oxygen supported greater substrate uptake but otherwise shifted metabolism toward respiration. Neutralisation likely alleviated the inhibitory effects of low pH during fermentation, as the result, maintaining cellular activity and LDH function. Although fermentations performed at 140 rpm showed that medium neutralisation with CaCO3 had a varying outcome on lactic acid production, dependent on the substrate and the strain: positive effects were consistently observed only in glucose cultures, while responses on xylose, glycerol and methanol were variable, likely due to redox perturbations in the pentose phosphate pathway.

When methanol was tested as a substrate for lactate production, the LDH_Pf strain demonstrated the highest lactic acid titer, particularly under oxygen-limited and pH-neutralised conditions. Despite the metabolic potential of O. polymorpha as a methylotrophic host, lactic acid titres obtained from methanol were substantially lower than those achieved with glucose or xylose, which likely results from several metabolic limitations of methanol oxidation. Peroxisomal localisation of alcohol oxidase (MOX) and dihydroxyacetone synthase (DAS) limits the efficient channelling of carbon into cytosolic pyruvate and consequently to the LDH-catalysed conversion to lactate [16]. This also results in a redox imbalance since NADH production in peroxisomes is separated from cytosolic LDH activity. Additionally, methanol oxidation produces toxic intermediates such as formaldehyde and hydrogen peroxide, which inhibit enzymatic activity and cell viability. Recent studies have demonstrated lactic acid production from methanol by yeast. The highest L-lactic acid production from methanol by O. polymorpha was reported by Wefelmeier et al. (3.8 g/L) after adaptive laboratory evolution and optimisation of the cultural medium [16]. K. phaffii with a modified cofactor specificity of LDH, deleted CYB2, and mitochondrial localisation of LDH, was reported to produce 4.2 g/L of L-lactic acid from methanol [37]. Another K. phaffii was engineered by Yamada et al. to produce D-lactic acid from methanol through transformation with a multicopy integrative plasmid, achieving a maximum titer of 3.48 g/L [38]. These studies demonstrated that lactic acid production from methanol requires further improvements for its industrial application. To address limitations in lactic acid titer, productivity, and yield, future research should explore peroxisome-targeted LDH expression or the addition of cytosolic-to-peroxisomal pyruvate transporters to improve substrate flux, the introduction of NADH oxidases or other redox-balancing enzymes to enhance cytosolic NAD⁺ regeneration, and ALE to strengthen tolerance to methanol and its byproducts.

Quantitative RT-PCR revealed a weak-to-moderate positive association between LDH transcript abundance and lactate production on glucose (R² = 0.25), indicating that expression contributes to flux redirection but is not the primary determinant of pathway efficiency. The LDH_Bt strain exhibited the highest relative expression, consistent with its superior performance, whereas LDH_Ro showed the lowest transcription and titre. These patterns underscore that both gene expression and enzyme kinetics constrain pathway flux in heterologous systems. Furthermore, differences in thermal stability among LDHs at 45 °C and limitations in lactate export likely contribute to divergence between transcription level and final product accumulation. Additionally, kinetic properties of the R. oryzae LDH enzyme, such as relatively low affinity for NADH and suboptimal catalytic efficiency under cytosolic conditions, may contribute to reduced lactic acid synthesis [22]. Reported kinetic constants further support this interpretation: the Kₘ for NADH of R. oryzae LDH (~ 120 µM) is substantially higher than that of bovine LDH-A (~ 30 µM) [22], indicating less efficient NADH utilisation under cytosolic conditions of O. polymorpha.

The superior performance of the LDH_Bt strain likely results from the biochemical features of bovine LDH-A, which is optimised for cytosolic pH (7.0–7.2) and moderate redox potential, aligning with the conditions during glucose and xylose fermentation. In contrast, LDH from P. falciparum exhibits exceptionally high turnover at elevated NADH/NAD⁺ ratios, consistent with enhanced activity under methanol metabolism, where high NADH accumulation occurs. These findings suggest that cofactor specificity is a critical factor in LDH selection for yeast hosts. Across all substrates, the dominant bottleneck transitions from pH inhibition (glucose) to redox imbalance (xylose), to compartmentalisation (methanol), to respiratory preference (glycerol).

Despite promising titres, further improvements are required to achieve industrially relevant productivities. Key limitations include the lack of peroxisomal NADH utilisation, incomplete elimination of ethanol formation, and potential bottlenecks in lactate export. Future engineering should focus on (i) targeting LDH to peroxisomes or introducing redox shuttles, (ii) deleting or repressing PDC1 and ADH genes to eliminate ethanol byproduct formation, and (iii) overexpressing monocarboxylate transporters (JEN1, ADY2 homologs) to enhance secretion. Adaptive laboratory evolution (ALE) could further increase tolerance to acid stress and methanol. These strategies, combined with pH control and improved cofactor balance, could enable O. polymorpha to match the performance of bacterial lactic acid producers.

Conclusions

This study demonstrated L-lactic production from various carbon sources by O. polymorpha yeast with heterologous expression of LDH genes originating from different organisms. Optimisation of fermentation conditions via medium neutralisation, agitation rate, and substrate allowed obtaining high titres of lactic acid. This makes it a potential metabolic engineering target for obtaining a platform system for lactic acid production with industrial applications. Further alterations of competing metabolic pathways might improve lactic acid titres produced by the obtained transformant strains and provide a more profound understanding of lactic acid metabolism in yeast. Further metabolic engineering targeting NADH balance, pyruvate partitioning, and lactate export may enable O. polymorpha to serve as a robust, thermotolerant biocatalyst for sustainable L-lactic acid production from renewable feedstocks.
