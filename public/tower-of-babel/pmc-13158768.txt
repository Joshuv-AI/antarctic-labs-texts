Health, economic, and equity impact of a COVID-19 mobile vaccine clinic program in US: a cost-effectiveness analysis
Authors: Khanh NC Duong, Damon JA Toth, Danielle T Nguyen, Yue Zhang, Richard E Nelson, Andrew T Pavia, Barbara E Jones, Cindy Wynette, Makoto M Jones, Carl Asche, Daniel C Malone, Matthew H Samore, Nathorn Chaiyakunapruk
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundMobile vaccine clinic (MVC) programs represent a significant public health investment, rigorous data on their health, economic, and equity impacts are needed to guide policy for future immunization efforts. We estimated the health and economic outcomes of the State of Utah's 1-year COVID-19 MVC program, stratified by Hispanic and non-Hispanic populations.MethodsWe used a decision-analytic model combining a susceptible-infected-removed (SIR) model with a decision tree to simulate 3.23 million Utah residents over one year (April 1, 2021, to March 31, 2022). Research in context

Evidence before this study

We searched PubMed for studies that evaluated the economic outcomes of COVID-19 mobile vaccine clinic (MVC) programs, published from January 1, 2020, to December 31, 2025. We used terms such as “mobile vaccine clinic”, “mobile unit”, “COVID-19”, “cost”, “cost-effectiveness”, “economic evaluation”, and “return on investment”, with no language restrictions. Previous studies suggested that MVC programs were cost-effective in Africa and North Macedonia, these analyses are limited to simple cost-per-dose calculations. Such simplifications underestimate the full value of MVC programs by omitting the downstream benefits of disease transmission prevention, failing to capture the opportunity costs of forgoing implementation (e.g., costs of treating preventable disease), and neglecting return on investment metrics that are crucial for policy decision-making. Furthermore, no previous study has quantified the distributional health and economic impacts of MVC programs across equity-related factors (such as race and ethnicity), making the equity impact of these programs unclear. Given the high upfront costs of investing in MVC programs, the lack of robust evidence on the value of MVC programs hinders policymakers from prioritizing these programs in future vaccination campaigns.

Added value of this study

To our knowledge, this is the first study to use a decision-analytic model combining a dynamic transmission model (susceptible-infected-removed [SIR]) with a decision tree to evaluate the health, economic, and equity impact of a one-year statewide MVC program. By simulating outcomes for 3.23 million residents in Utah, we demonstrated that the MVC program achieved a “triple win”: it was effective at saving lives (preventing 253 deaths), highly cost-saving (generating $9.70 in societal savings for every $1 invested), and equity-enhancing (preventing 3 to 6 times more adverse outcomes per 100,000 persons in the Hispanic population compared to the non-Hispanic population).

Implications of all the available evidence

Our findings provide evidence in support of COVID-19 MVC programs and suggest the potential of investing and scaling this strategy for future vaccination campaigns.

Introduction

During COVID-19 vaccination rollout in the United States (US), limited access to healthcare facilities was a primary barrier to vaccine uptake, particularly in underserved populations.1, 2, 3, 4 Mobile vaccine clinic (MVC) programs were widely implemented to address these access barriers, increase vaccine uptake, and reduce existing disparities.5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13 While previous studies have consistently demonstrated the effectiveness of MVC programs in increasing vaccine uptake,5, 6, 7, 8, 9 these programs also entail substantial and highly variable operational costs.14 For example, one US study reported median daily operational costs per clinic of $10,314, with staffing comprising over 80% of expenses.14 This creates a significant resource-allocation trade-off for public health officials. Rigorous data on the cost-effectiveness and equity impacts of MVC programs have been lacking to justify these substantial costs. Such evidence is essential not only to evaluate the COVID-19 response but also to guide policy on future investment in MVC programs for vaccination campaigns.

To address these knowledge gaps, we conducted an economic evaluation of Utah's statewide COVID-19 MVC program. Utah provides a compelling case study due to its geographic and demographic characteristics, including large rural and frontier areas (77% of state's land area)15 and significant racial and ethnic minority populations (23% of state's population).16 These factors posed significant challenges for vaccination outreach in the state, allowing for an analysis of the MVC program's impact on both efficiency and equity.

The objectives of this study were 1) to estimate the total and distributional health and economic outcomes of Utah's MVC program, and 2) to calculate the program's return on investment (ROI).

Methods

Study design

We developed a hybrid decision-analytic model, combining a susceptible-infected-removed (SIR) differential equation model with a decision tree, to evaluate the cost-effectiveness of a 1-year MVC program. The SIR model simulates the spread of infection across two population groups (non-Hispanic and Hispanic). Subsequently, we used a decision tree to estimate the downstream clinical consequences (hospitalization, intensive care unit (ICU) admission, and death) for infected individuals. We compared the MVC program against a no-MVC counterfactual scenario, estimating health and economic outcomes for both.

Outcomes were stratified by non-Hispanic and Hispanic populations to assess health equity impacts. These populations were selected based on three key factors: first, the specific context of Utah, where the Hispanic population represents the largest minority group in Utah (14.5% of the total population in 2021) while other racial and ethnic groups represent significantly smaller proportions (Asian: 2.5%; Black/African American: 1.2%; Native Hawaiian/Pacific Islander: 1.0%; and American Indian/Alaska Native: 0.9%)17; second, the documented COVID-19 vaccination disparities, noting that as of March 31, 2021, only 15.6% for Hispanic residents had received at least one dose compared to 30.3% of non-Hispanic populations18; and finally, the availability of robust, granular data for these specific cohorts.

The time horizon was one year (April 1, 2021, to March 31, 2022), discounts were not applied. All costs were in 2021 US dollars. This study did not involve human subjects and was conducted using deidentified, retrospective, aggregate data. Therefore, institutional review board approval and informed consent were not required. We followed the Consolidated Health Economic Evaluation Reporting Standards 2022 (CHEERS 2022) checklist in reporting this work.19

Intervention and comparator

The intervention was the 1-year MVC program implemented in Utah from April 1, 2021, to March 31, 2022. This period was selected in consultation with local health department officers, as it was when the implementation of MVC programs was intensive across the state. The comparator was the counterfactual scenario without the MVC program in Utah during the same period.

Stakeholder engagement

We engaged with public health officials from the Utah Department of Health and Human Services throughout the study. This engagement informed the selection of evaluated strategies, the definition of the study period, and the equity impact assessment, ensuring the study was aligned with local policy priorities and operational realities.

Model structure

The SIR model simulated the spread of infected cases within two scenarios (with and without MVC) in two group populations (non-Hispanic and Hispanic populations). This model consisted of three compartments: susceptible (S), infected (I), and removed (R). Each main compartment was split into two sub compartments for the two groups. According to this model, a susceptible individual (in compartment S) who becomes infected immediately transitions to the infected (I) compartment. An infected individual subsequently moves to the removed (R) compartment upon recovery or death. The model incorporated differences in infection transmission rates between two groups through differences in contact rates. This approach is preferred to evaluating disparities in COVID-19 exposure and transmission between groups with different risks of infection and social contact rates. Specifically, we used a transmission matrix to capture both within-group and between-group interactions. This structural modeling framework was adopted from Ma et al.'s study.20 The parameters used in the model included the basic reproduction number (R0), contact rate ratio (ratio of contact rate in Hispanic versus non-Hispanic individuals), recovery rate, the proportion of exclusive contacts within groups, and the susceptibility to infection ratio (Hispanic versus non-Hispanic). Details of the model, which follows a similar structure to prior work,20 are presented in Appendix S1 in Supplementary Materials.

Vaccine was modeled an imperfect, “all-or-nothing” vaccine with an assumed effectiveness of 60%.21 Accordingly, 60% of vaccinated individuals were considered to have developed complete immunity and were transitioned directly to the R compartment (“removed” means either recovered from infection, died, or vaccine-immunized), the 40% remained fully susceptible (remaining in the S compartment). The model assumed one-dose vaccine with no waning immunity, no reinfection, and no population turnover.

To estimate the downstream consequences (including hospitalization, ICU admission, and death) of infected individuals (those in the I compartment and those who have moved to the R compartment after infection), we used a decision tree. Infected individuals may be hospitalized or managed as outpatients. If hospitalized, they could be admitted to the ICU or a non-ICU. Once admitted to the ICU or non-ICU, they could either die from the disease or recover. Similarly, individuals who were not hospitalized could either die from the disease or recover. Estimating vaccinated cases

The number of vaccinated cases in non-Hispanic and Hispanic groups in both the MVC scenario and the counterfactual scenario without the MVC was derived from a longitudinal analysis of MVC program and vaccination uptake in Utah (see Appendix S2 in Supplementary Materials).22 This underlying study used a mixed-effects zero-inflated beta regression model to quantify the effect of MVC deployment on COVID-19 vaccination rates in Utah from April 1, 2021, to March 31, 2022. The exposure variables were the weekly number of MVC days within ZIP codes and nearby ZIP codes; the outcome variable was the weekly first dose vaccination rate. The model was adjusted for confounding variables, including the Health Accessibility Barriers Index (HABI), the Resource-Constrained Health System Index (RCHS), the Social Vulnerability Index (SVI), vaccine hesitancy, and prior vaccination rates. From this analysis, we extracted the marginal effect of MVC on host ZIP codes, the spillover effects on nearby ZIP codes, and the ratio of MVC-administered vaccines distributed to Hispanic and non-Hispanic populations (see Appendix S2 in Supplementary Materials).

Perspectives

We used two perspectives recommended by the US Second Panel on Cost-effectiveness in Health and Medicine: healthcare sector and societal.23 The healthcare sector perspective included direct medical costs (vaccination and treatment). The societal perspective added productivity costs, estimated using the human capital approach.

Model calibration

We calibrated the modeled cumulative infected cases for both populations against reported cumulative infected cases from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) COVID-19 Case Surveillance Public Use Data24 at baseline (March 31, 2021) and the end of the study period (March 31, 2022). Calibration parameters included R0, contact rate ratio, recovery rate, and the proportion of exclusive contacts within groups. Appendix S3 in the Supplementary Materials presents details of the model calibration.

Model input parameters

Transition probabilities

The probabilities of hospitalization, death of infected patients, the probability of ICU admission, death of hospitalized patients, and the probability of death of ICU patients were obtained from the literature.25, 26, 27, 28, 29, 30, 31, 32 We applied higher hospitalization probabilities of infected patients for Hispanic groups, consistent with literature that reported higher risk of hospitalization in racial and ethnic minority COVID-19 infected patients than their counterparts.27 Subsequent outcome probabilities (e.g., ICU admission, in-hospital death) were identical for both populations supported by literature.27,29,30

Vaccination costs

The vaccination cost per patient at non-MVC sites was obtained from the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS) payment rate.33 The vaccination cost per patient at MVC sites was estimated from a previous study which calculated the start-up (office equipment, tables, chairs) and operational costs (staffing, supplies, facilities, and services) of COVID-19 vaccination clinics run by public health departments in the US.14 A vaccine wastage rate of 0.04 percent for Utah in 2021 was included.34,35

Healthcare treatment costs

We assumed that asymptomatic patients would not receive treatment, outpatient costs were estimated only for infected patients presenting with symptoms. Outpatient costs were derived from the median allowed amount for COVID-19 outpatient treatment from FAIR Health, 2021 for Utah.36 Given limited data on COVID-19 outpatient costs stratified by race and ethnicity, we adopted a conservative assumption of equal outpatient costs for Hispanic and non-Hispanic populations which also aligned with the ethical aspect in health equity research.37 Inpatient costs, including ICU treatment and non-ICU treatment, were derived from allowed amounts for COVID-19 complex and non-complex inpatients from FAIR Health, 2021, for Utah.36 These assumptions were supported by literature reporting that while raw costs might vary, no statistically significant disparities in COVID-19 hospital costs between Hispanic and White populations after adjusting for clinical characteristics and neighborhood factors.29,38

Productivity costs

Productivity costs included workday losses from vaccination (clinic visits for no-MVC sites and adverse events) and from COVID-19 treatment (outpatient and inpatient).29,39 The mean daily wage for Utah in 2021 was extracted from the US Bureau of Labor Statistics, 2021.40 To avoid the ethical concern of devaluing health interventions that target lower-income groups, we applied a uniform population average wage to both Hispanic and non-Hispanic groups, rather than using stratified wage rates.37

Vaccination rates

Baseline (April 1, 2021) and end-of-period (March 31, 2022) cumulative vaccination rates for non-Hispanic and Hispanic populations were derived from the Utah Statewide Immunization Information System (USIIS).18

Data analysis

We estimated total and distributional outcomes across both scenarios (with and without MVC) for non-Hispanic, Hispanic, and total populations. The outcomes included: vaccinations, infections, hospitalizations, ICU admissions, deaths, direct costs, productivity costs, and total costs. The incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER), expressed as costs per death averted, was also reported.

Return on investment

We estimated the ROI from both healthcare sector and societal perspectives using the formula: ROI = (Benefits of Investment−Costs of Investment)/(Costs of Investment).34,45 The “Benefits of Investment” were the difference in costs associated with health outcomes (e.g., averted direct treatment costs) between the MVC and no-MVC scenarios, expressed in monetary value. The “Costs of Investment” referred to the additional vaccination program costs incurred in the MVC scenario compared to the no-MVC scenario.

Inequity measurement

We measured disparities in health outcomes between non-Hispanic and Hispanic populations using absolute and relative disparity metrics. Absolute disparity was defined as the absolute difference in the percentage of a specific outcome between the two groups. For example, an absolute disparity in death of 5% means that the percentage of deaths in Hispanic population was 5 percentage points higher than in non-Hispanic population. Relative disparity was defined as the ratio of the outcome percentage in the Hispanic group to the non-Hispanic group. For instance, a relative disparity in death of 5 means that the percentage of death in Hispanic population was 5 times higher than in the non-Hispanic population. We estimated these metrics at baseline (March 31, 2021) and post-intervention (March 31, 2022) for both scenarios (Baseline outcomes were estimated using the same model with pre-intervention parameters, see Appendix S3 in Supplementary Materials).

Sensitivity analyses

We performed one-way and probabilistic sensitivity analyses (PSAs) to assess the impact of parameter uncertainty on the results. In one-way sensitivity analyses, parameters were varied one at a time in the plausible ranges (from literature, or ± 25% of the base-case values if unavailable) and the effect of each variation on outcomes was estimated. Results are presented in Tornado diagrams. For the PSAs, we ran 1000 Monte Carlo simulations, drawing all parameters simultaneously from predefined distributions. Specifically, a beta distribution was assumed for probability parameters, a log-normal distribution was assumed for effectiveness parameters, and a gamma distribution was assumed for costs parameters.46,47 PSA results are presented on cost-effectiveness planes.

All analyses were performed in R 4.4.2. The R code is available on GitHub (https://github.com/KhanhNCDuong/DCEA_MVC).

Role of the funding source

The funders had no role in the conduct of this study collection, management, analysis, and interpretation of the data.

Results

Model calibration

The cumulative reported COVID-19 cases in Utah, as of March 31, 2021, and March 31, 2022, for non-Hispanic and Hispanic populations were 261,888, 128,418, 657,668, and 275,596, respectively. Our model estimated 261,905, 128,457, 657,667, and 275,592 cases for these same time points, demonstrating a close fit to the reported data (Appendix S3 in Supplementary Materials).

Health and economic outcomes

In the base-case analysis, the MVC scenario resulted in 1,324,277 vaccinations, 542,897 infections, 11,436 hospitalizations, 2973 ICU admissions, and 3158 deaths. In comparison, the counterfactual no-MVC program resulted in 1,294,857 vaccinations, 584,400 infections, 12,359 hospitalizations, 3213 ICU admissions, and 3411 deaths. Consequently, MVC scenario vaccinated an additional 29,420 persons over one year compared to the no-MVC scenario. This prevented 41,503 infections, 923 hospitalizations, 240 ICU admissions, and 253 deaths (Table 2).

Disaggregated by Hispanic and non-Hispanic groups, the MVC program prevented 3101.5 infections, 99.4 hospitalizations, 25.8 ICU admissions, and 26.6 deaths per 100,000 persons in the Hispanic population. These numbers were 3.2, 6.0, 5.9, and 5.7 times higher, respectively, than those in the non-Hispanic population (which were 978.3 infections, 16.6 hospitalizations, 4.3 ICU admissions, and 4.7 deaths per 100,000 persons) (Appendix S5 in Supplementary Materials).

Compared to the no-MVC scenario, the MVC scenario incurred higher direct vaccination costs ($1,324,000) but saved $53 million in healthcare treatment costs, yielding a net saving of $51.7 million. From the healthcare sector perspective, the MVC scenario was cost-saving (i.e., prevented more deaths with lower direct costs, resulting in a negative ICER) (Table 2). From the societal perspective, the MVC program incurred an incremental vaccination cost of $7.3 million but saved $71.3 million in averted healthcare treatment and productivity costs. Therefore, the MVC program was also a cost-saving option from the societal perspective (Table 2). Cost savings per person attributed to MVC investment were $16 and $22 from the healthcare sector and societal perspectives, respectively (Appendix S5 in Supplementary Materials).

Return on investment

From the healthcare sector perspective, every $1 invested in the MVC program saved $39 in direct healthcare costs. From the societal perspective, every $1 invested in the MVC program saved $9.70 in direct healthcare and productivity costs (see Table 2).

Equity measurement

Compared to the no-MVC program, the MVC program reduced the absolute and relative disparities between Hispanic and non-Hispanic populations across all outcomes. Particularly, the relative disparity in vaccination was 0.92 in the MVC program (the percentage of Hispanic individuals vaccinated was 0.92 times that of non-Hispanic individuals), compared to 0.86 in the no-MVC scenario. Absolute and relative disparities for infections, hospitalizations, ICU admissions, and deaths were all lower in the MVC scenario (see Table 3).

Sensitivity analysis

One-way sensitivity analyses demonstrated that the MVC program remained cost-saving and produced a positive ROI across all tested parameter ranges. The parameters with the most significant influence on deaths averted, direct costs, and total costs were the effectiveness of 1-day MVC in nearby ZIP code, the probability of death given infection, and the probability of hospitalization given infection (Appendix S6 in Supplementary Materials). For ROIs, the vaccination cost per patient at MVC sites had the most significant impact on ROIs (Appendix S6 in Supplementary Materials). In the PSAs, the MVC program was cost-saving in 99.8% and 99.7% of simulations from both healthcare sector and societal perspectives, respectively (Fig. 2).

Discussion

This study demonstrated that the MVC program was a cost-saving intervention with a high ROI from both healthcare sector and societal perspectives. Beyond its efficiency, the MVC program also narrowed health disparities between Hispanic and non-Hispanic populations.

Our findings aligned with previous studies on the economic value of MVC programs. A study in Sierra Leone reported that the implementation cost of US$33 per person vaccinated at MVC was considered cost-effective compared to other vaccination campaigns.5 Similarly, an MVC program in North Macedonia reported an average cost of US$58 per person vaccinated, suggesting it as a cost-effective strategy.8 In the US, one study estimated that investing $25,880 in a single COVID-19 pop-up vaccination center in New York could avert between $1.9 million and $42 million in healthcare costs.48 Similarly, a one-year COVID-19 vaccination campaign in New York City that included vaccine outreach, education, and MVCs estimated $10 savings for every $1 invested.39

Our study reinforced this economic value of MVCs. We estimated a $9.70 societal saving for every $1 invested, a high ROI for a state-level public health intervention. Literature suggests ROIs for national public health interventions can be even higher (a median of 27.2 versus 4.1 for local interventions).49 While our study was limited to one state, a national-scale MVC program might produce an even higher ROI, warranting further investigation.

Our study also demonstrated that MVCs narrowed disparities in vaccination and subsequent health outcomes. This disparity reduction is likely attributable to the program's design. The Hispanic population began the study period with pre-existing disparities in vaccination, infection risk, and severe outcomes (e.g., hospitalization or death). MVCs, by design, targeted populations with healthcare access barriers and lower vaccination uptake, which disproportionately reached and vaccinated individuals within the Hispanic community. This, in turn, reduced downstream health outcomes in the Hispanic population, narrowing health disparities. Preventing these costly outcomes (hospitalizations, ICU admissions) also drove the program's overall cost-savings. Therefore, the MVC program was an efficient and equitable intervention.

However, practical and sustainable implementation of MVCs needs careful consideration of several factors. First, operational performance of MVCs varies substantially.5,11 One study showed a 2-fold difference in vaccines administered per team5 and another study also reported a wide range (from 46 to 135) in weekly vaccinations.11 This variability highlights an opportunity for optimization and standardization of the best practices for MVCs. Second, costs at MVC sites vary considerably,5,8,14 which influences ROI, as our sensitivity analyses confirmed. Cost drivers vary by setting (e.g., travel costs in Africa,5 advertising and outreach costs in North Macedonia,8 or staff costs in the US14) and operational characteristics (e.g., urban/rural, days open).14 Understanding these context-specific cost drivers is crucial for identifying strategies to reduce expenses. Third, fostering close collaboration with community leaders is essential to maximize MVCs' impact on equity, enhancing their ability to reach target communities.50 Fourth, most MVC programs are run by local health departments with limited staff and resources, or rely on state or federal support; securing adequate resources and consistent funding is also essential for program sustainability. Finally, enhancing data collection is needed to facilitate more accurate allocation and assess this implementation strategy.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, we acknowledge that our model aims to provide a parsimonious population-level estimate rather than a detailed epidemiological reconstruction of the pandemic's behavior in Utah. Consequently, we employed a structural simplification (a hybrid SIR and decision tree model), and made several simplified assumptions, such as a closed population cohort and a single-dose vaccine without waning or immunity reinfection. Furthermore, applying uniform model parameters throughout the study period was also a simplification of a complex reality, where the parameters, such as R0, contact rates ratio, and vaccine effectiveness, could change over time or vary across subpopulations. To enhance the model validity, we conducted expert face validation and calibrated the model against empirical Utah data (particularly infected cases and vaccination uptake) to ensure our estimates reflect real-world numbers as accurately as possible. However, it should be noted that this validation was partial, and the model's robustness relies on calibration to aggregate data points and sensitivity analyses, rather than on a comprehensive external validation of all health outcomes (i.e., vaccinations, infections, hospitalizations, ICU admissions, and deaths).

Second, regarding the estimation of vaccination uptake in the counterfactual no-MVC scenario, we assumed uniform odds ratios of MVC effect and a constant ratio of attributable vaccinations between the Hispanic and non-Hispanic populations across all ZIP codes and weeks in Utah. Although the MVC effectiveness parameters were derived from a previous longitudinal analysis and the ratio of attributable vaccinations was based on the empirical data,22 this approach may not fully capture all local variations or granular shifts in vaccine uptake.

Third, while age is an important factor for COVID-19 health outcomes, our model used ethnicity-stratified parameters rather than age-and-ethnicity-stratified parameters due to the limited availability of granular data with the specific context of Utah. While this captures the broader disparities between the study populations, future research incorporating age-specific parameters could provide further granular insights. Fourth, we only assessed disparities by ethnicity (Hispanic versus non-Hispanic) and did not explore other dimensions of equity (e.g., race, rural/urban, income, or socioeconomic factors). Additionally, sex- and/or gender-based disaggregation was not incorporated into the compartmental SIR model, as the focus remained on population-level ethnicity-stratified dynamics, and thus sex- and/or gender-specific equity impacts were not assessed. Consequently, the full equity impact of the MVC program may not have been captured; future studies should explore these additional equity dimensions to provide a more comprehensive evaluation.

Fifth, our model assumed a closed population cohort for Utah, whereas in reality, transmission and vaccination could involve out-of-state individuals. However, we assumed that the out-of-state contact and cross-border movement were minimal during the study period (2021 to early 2022) due to widespread travel restriction and reduced mobility associated with the COVID-19 pandemic. Sixth, we did not incorporate long COVID-19 sequelae or health-related quality of life metrics (such as QALYs). Given these factors were excluded, our findings likely represent a conservative estimate of the program's full health and economic benefits. Seventh, the study's one-year time horizon captured the immediate impact of the MVC investment but did not account for long-term health or avoided chronic care costs. Eighth, as a case study of Utah, this study used data specifically from Utah, our findings' generalizability may be limited.

Lastly, regarding reporting limitations according to CHEERS 2022, while a formal health economic analysis plan was developed internally prior to the analysis, it was not prospectively registered in a public repository. Furthermore, while health officials provided vital input, direct patient or public engagement was not conducted, which may limit the broader societal perspective of our engagement process.

Conclusions

This study demonstrated that the MVC program was both an efficient and equitable intervention, with a high return on investment. These findings support public health investment in the COVID-19 MVC program and suggest future investment in this strategy for other vaccinations.
