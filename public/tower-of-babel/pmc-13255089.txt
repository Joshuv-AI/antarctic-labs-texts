Sotatercept treatment in a patient with end-stage bronchiolitis obliterans syndrome after lung transplantation
Authors: Hooman D. Poor, Jimmy Zhang, Simone Hannah-Clark, Shyla Saini, Jing Wang, Elliot Eisenberg, Saarwaani Vallabhajosyula, Chang Li, Charles Powell, Scott Scheinin, Harish Seethamraju
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Bronchiolitis obliterans syndrome (BOS), the most common form of chronic lung allograft dysfunction after lung transplantation, is characterized by progressive small airway disease (SAD) and is associated with poor outcomes and limited therapeutic options. Sotatercept, an activin-signaling inhibitor approved for pulmonary arterial hypertension, has been associated with improvements in physiologic markers of SAD in patients with pulmonary hypertension. In this report, we describe a 55-year-old woman with primary ciliary dyskinesia status post bilateral lung transplantation who developed stage 3 BOS complicated by hypercapnic respiratory failure requiring mechanical ventilation. Following initiation of off-label sotatercept, she experienced rapid clinical and radiologic improvement, permitting transient liberation from mechanical ventilation before succumbing to progressive respiratory failure. Further study is required to assess the potential role for activin pathway modulation in advanced BOS.

Background

Bronchiolitis obliterans syndrome (BOS) is the most common form of chronic lung allograft dysfunction (CLAD) and the leading cause of late graft failure after the first year following lung transplantation.1 The principal pathological correlate of BOS is obliterative bronchiolitis, a form of small airways disease (SAD) characterized by progressive narrowing and eventual fibrosis of terminal bronchioles.2 Outcomes in advanced BOS are poor, and currently available therapies provide limited benefit. Sotatercept, an activin-signaling inhibitor approved for the treatment of pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH), has been associated with improvements in physiologic markers of SAD.3, 4 We report a patient with end-stage BOS and hypercapnic respiratory failure who experienced clinical and radiographic improvement following off-label treatment with sotatercept, permitting transient liberation from mechanical ventilation before succumbing to progressive respiratory failure.

Case report

A 55-year-old woman with primary ciliary dyskinesia complicated by bronchiectasis underwent uncomplicated bilateral lung transplantation. One year after transplantation, she achieved peak post-transplant lung function: FEV₁ 2.13 L (89% predicted), FVC 2.47 L (83% predicted), FEV₁/FVC 86%, and FEF25–75% 3.25 L/s (141% predicted). Six-minute walk distance was 427 m without oxygen desaturation on room air. Maintenance immunosuppression consisted of tacrolimus and prednisone; azathioprine had been discontinued due to leukopenia. Her subsequent course was complicated by Pseudomonas pneumonia 4 months later, followed 2 months thereafter by infections with Aspergillus fumigatus and Nocardia species.

After recovery from these infections, she developed progressive dyspnea on exertion accompanied by marked deterioration in pulmonary function: FVC 1.13 L (38% predicted), FEV₁ 0.76 L (32% predicted), FEV₁/FVC 67%, and FEF25–75% 0.35 L/sec (16% predicted). Six-minute walk distance declined to 152 m, and she required 4 L/min supplemental oxygen via nasal cannula. Chest computed tomography (CT) demonstrated resolution of prior infiltrates but revealed diffuse mosaic attenuation, concerning for CLAD with a small airways phenotype (BOS stage 3). She received a 5-day course of intravenous immunoglobulin and was initiated on extracorporeal photopheresis and azithromycin. Two days later, she developed worsening hypercapnic respiratory failure requiring high-flow nasal cannula and was transferred to the intensive care unit. Despite these measures, 2 weeks later she required mechanical ventilation. Repeat chest CT at the time demonstrated multifocal ground-glass opacities and left basilar atelectasis (Figure 1). Over the ensuing 2 weeks, she failed 2 extubation attempts due to recurrent hypercapnic respiratory failure. Given advanced CLAD and the patient’s preference not to pursue re-transplantation, the family considered comfort-focused care but elected to initiate off-label sotatercept in light of emerging data suggesting potential benefit in SAD. Other salvage therapies for BOS, including mTOR inhibition, were not pursued, as they were felt unlikely to provide meaningful clinical benefit in this advanced stage of disease.

A tracheostomy was performed, and sotatercept was initiated at 0.3 mg/kg subcutaneously 3 days later. Within 1 week of sotatercept initiation, she was successfully liberated from mechanical ventilation. Eight weeks after sotatercept initiation, the tracheostomy was downsized and capped. Repeat chest CT demonstrated substantial improvement in ground-glass opacities and left basilar atelectasis (Figure 1). Twelve weeks after initiation, she was decannulated and able to ambulate 200 feet with physical therapy, limited primarily by lower extremity weakness.

After the initial sotatercept dose of 0.3 mg/kg, sotatercept was increased to 0.7 mg/kg every 21 days. During this treatment period, she consistently reported worsening dyspnea in the days preceding each scheduled dose—often requiring nocturnal noninvasive ventilation—with subjective improvement beginning within 48 hours of injection. Despite this interval stabilization, 3 months after initiation of sotatercept, she developed recurrent hypercapnic respiratory failure requiring intubation and subsequently died from progressive respiratory failure.

Discussion

We describe a patient with end-stage BOS and hypercapnic respiratory failure requiring mechanical ventilation who, following initiation of sotatercept, experienced transient clinical improvement to permit liberation from mechanical ventilation before succumbing to progressive respiratory failure. Radiologic improvement, specifically reduction in ground-glass opacities and basilar atelectasis, accompanied her clinical course. Although spontaneous recovery or delayed response to concurrent therapies (extracorporeal photopheresis, intravenous immunoglobulin, azithromycin) cannot be excluded, the natural history of BOS stage 3 typically does not include reversal of ventilator dependance. Furthermore, the consistent temporal association between symptom improvement and sotatercept administration—followed by waning benefit before subsequent dosing—supports a possible pharmacologic effect.

Sotatercept is believed to exert its therapeutic effect in PAH chiefly through blockade of activin signaling, thereby reducing cellular proliferation and aberrant differentiation within the pulmonary vasculature.5 Given that vascular remodeling is typically a chronic process, 1 might expect sotatercept to exhibit a delayed hemodynamic response. However, case series have demonstrated rapid reductions in pulmonary artery pressures—occurring within hours of sotatercept administration—suggesting the presence of additional, more acute mechanisms of action.6, 7 Beyond vascular disease, activins are important mediators of innate immune activation, promoting macrophage recruitment, proinflammatory cytokine production, and tissue infiltration.8 In experimental PAH, a sotatercept analog reduced pulmonary macrophage infiltration and reversed proinflammatory gene expression.9 Accumulating data implicate activin signaling in airway pathology. Activin A expression is increased in airway epithelium, airway smooth muscle, and alveolar macrophages in chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, and cigarette smoke exposure upregulates its expression.10 In asthma models, activin A promotes airway smooth muscle proliferation and epithelial remodeling, contributing to hyperreactivity.11 Activin signaling may also be relevant in lung transplantation. Patients with primary graft dysfunction (an independent risk factor for BOS) demonstrate higher activin A/follistatin ratios in the early post-transplant period compared with lung transplant patients demonstrating normal initial graft function.12 In stable lung transplant patients, higher serum activin A concentrations are associated with increased risk of subsequent CLAD, particularly BOS.1

We hypothesize that sotatercept exerted anti-inflammatory and/or anti-remodeling effects within the small airways in this patient, resulting in temporary improvement in ventilation, air trapping, and atelectasis. There is substantial physiologic interplay between the pulmonary vasculature and the small airways. Pulmonary hypertension is frequently observed in BOS and may reflect shared remodeling pathways. In 1 series of lung transplant patients, PH was present in 72% of patients with BOS but absent in all patients without BOS.2 Among patients with sarcoidosis-associated PH and other forms of precapillary PH treated with sotatercept, we have observed improvements in physiologic markers of SAD.3, 4 The cyclical pattern of the patient’s respiratory status in relation to sotatercept administration—characterized by improvement within 48 hours of dosing and clinical worsening prior to the next dose—may reflect a diminishing effect of therapy on her airways over the dosing interval. Whether alternative dosing strategies or earlier initiation in less advanced BOS might produce more durable benefits remains unknown.

While this case does not prove activity of sotatercept in patients with BOS, it is nonetheless hypothesis-generating. Prospective investigation is warranted to evaluate the role of activin pathway modulation in CLAD, particularly BOS, where therapeutic options remain limited and outcomes poor.
