1.
Introduction

A well-studied subject in number theory is the distribution of roots of polynomials. This tradition includes, for example, Descartes’ Rule of Signs, the Gauss-Lucas Theorem, and the Schinzel-Zassenhaus Conjecture. Here, we study the relationship between the separation of a polynomial (the minimal distance between its roots) and the Mahler measure of that same polynomial. To be precise, we define these terms here.

Definition 1
.

Given a polynomial
f(x)\in\mathbb{C}[x]
with roots
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n}\in\mathbb{C}
, the
separation
of
f(x)
is the quantity

\operatorname{sep}(f):=\min_{\alpha_{i}\neq\alpha_{j}}|\alpha_{i}-\alpha_{j}|.

Since, for any nonzero
b\in\mathbb{C}
,
\operatorname{sep}(f)=\operatorname{sep}(bf)
, we assume in the remainder of the paper that all polynomials are monic. Our results can be easily adapted otherwise.

Definition 2

Given a monic polynomial
f(x)\in\mathbb{C}[x]
with roots
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n}\in\mathbb{C}
, the
Mahler measure
of
f(x)
is the quantity

M(f):=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\max\{1,|\alpha_{i}|\}.

These two quantities are useful for rather different reasons. Knowledge about root separation is helpful for writing algorithms that compute the number of real roots of a polynomial (see Koiran’s work in
[
13

[
10

[

Meanwhile, Mahler measure is a versatile tool for measuring the complexity of a polynomial. On the one hand, Mahler measure contains information about the roots of a polynomial via its definition. On the other hand, Mahler measure contains information about the coefficients of the polynomial: for any polynomial

These facts together make Mahler measure valuable for translating information about the roots of a polynomial into information about its coefficients, and vice versa. While lower bounds on separation have been well-studied, the authors are not aware of any attempt to provide upper bounds on separation. On the face of it, an upper bound on separation provides less intuitive information about a polynomial’s roots than does a lower bound. However, upper bounds on separation can provide us with useful information for Lehmer’s conjecture, which we will describe after our main theorem.

One can easily obtain a trivial upper bound on separation using the triangle inequality:
\operatorname{sep}(f)=\min_{\alpha_{i}\neq\alpha_{j}}|\alpha_{i}-\alpha_{j}|\leqslant 2M(f).

2.
The Optimal upper bound up to constant factor

The main goal of this section is to prove Theorem
1.2
.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

Write
f(x)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}(x-\alpha_{i})
where
|\alpha_{1}|\leqslant|\alpha_{2}|\leqslant\cdots\leqslant|\alpha_{n}|
.

We first prove that
\operatorname{sep}(f)\leqslant 2M(f)^{1/(n-1)}
. Note that for any
j\geqslant 2
, we have
0<|\alpha_{j}-\alpha_{1}|\leqslant 2|\alpha_{j}|
. As a consequence,

\displaystyle\operatorname{sep}(f)^{n-1}\leqslant|\alpha_{2}-\alpha_{1}||\alpha_{3}-\alpha_{1}|\cdots|\alpha_{n}-\alpha_{1}|\leqslant\prod_{j=2}^{n}2|\alpha_{j}|\leqslant 2^{n-1}M(f)

and the claim follows.

Next, we show that if
f(x)\in\mathbb{R}[x]
and if there exists
\alpha_{j}\notin\mathbb{R}
with
|\alpha_{j}|=|\alpha_{1}|
, then actually
\operatorname{sep}(f)\leqslant 2M(f)^{1/n}
. The argument is very similar, except we may now assume that
\alpha_{1}
and
\alpha_{2}
are complex conjugates. In this case,

\displaystyle\operatorname{sep}(f)^{n}\leqslant|\alpha_{2}-\alpha_{1}|^{2}|\alpha_{3}-\alpha_{1}|\cdots|\alpha_{n}-\alpha_{1}|\leqslant\prod_{j=1}^{n}2|\alpha_{j}|\leqslant 2^{n}M(f)

and the claim again follows.

Now we return to the situation where we only assume
f(x)\in\mathbb{C}[x]
is separable of degree
n
, and we aim to show that

\operatorname{sep}(f)\leqslant\frac{34}{\sqrt{n}}\cdot M(f)^{1/(n-1)}.

Since we have
\frac{34}{\sqrt{n}}\geqslant 2
for
n\leqslant 289
, we assume that
n\geqslant 290
in the following discussion.

Let
r=\operatorname{sep}(f)/2
and for any
R\geqslant 0
, let

N(R):=\#\{1\leqslant i\leqslant n:|\alpha_{i}|<R\}.

Additionally, for any
z\in\mathbb{C}
, let
B_{R}(z)
denote the open ball of radius
R
centered at
z
. We may safely assume that
r\geqslant\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}
, as the theorem easily follows otherwise.

Next, observe that for any
i\neq j
,
B_{r}(\alpha_{i})
and
B_{r}(\alpha_{j})
are disjoint. Additionally, for any
R>0
,

B_{R+r}(0)\supsetneq\bigcup_{|\alpha_{i}|\leqslant R}B_{r}(\alpha_{i}).

As a consequence,

\pi(R+r)^{2}=\operatorname{vol}(B_{R+r}(0))>\sum_{|\alpha_{i}|\leqslant R}\operatorname{vol}(B_{r}(\alpha_{i}))=N(R)\cdot\pi r^{2},

implying that

(1)

\displaystyle N(R)<\left(\frac{R}{r}+1\right)^{2}.

Now, for each positive integer
j\leqslant\lceil\log_{2}(n)\rceil-1=:L
(here we use the assumption that
n\geqslant 3
to obtain
L\geqslant 1
), define
R_{j}=r(\sqrt{n/2^{j}}-1)
. Note that
R_{j}>0
for
j\leqslant L
. For each
j
, we aim to bound from below the number of roots
\alpha_{i}
which satisfy
R_{j}<|\alpha_{i}|
.

Observe that
N(R_{j})<n/2^{j}
by inequality (
1
), and hence, for each
1\leqslant j\leqslant L
, there must be at least
n(2^{j}-1)/2^{j}
roots
\alpha_{i}
which satisfy
|\alpha_{i}|\geqslant R_{j}
. Therefore,

\displaystyle M(f)

\displaystyle\geqslant\prod_{|\alpha_{i}|\geqslant R_{L}}|\alpha_{i}|=(r\sqrt{n})^{n-N(R_{L})}\prod_{|\alpha_{i}|\geqslant R_{L}}\frac{|\alpha_{i}|}{r\sqrt{n}}

(2)

\displaystyle\geqslant(r\sqrt{n})^{n-N(R_{L})}\left(\frac{R_{1}}{r\sqrt{n}}\right)^{n/2}\left(\frac{R_{2}}{r\sqrt{n}}\right)^{n/4}\cdots\left(\frac{R_{L}}{r\sqrt{n}}\right)^{n/2^{L}}.

The remainder of this proof will be dedicated to showing that

\left(\frac{R_{1}}{r\sqrt{n}}\right)^{n/2}\left(\frac{R_{2}}{r\sqrt{n}}\right)^{n/4}\cdots\left(\frac{R_{L}}{r\sqrt{n}}\right)^{n/2^{L}}\geqslant\frac{1}{4n^{2}\cdot 16^{n}}.

First, note that for any
j\leqslant L
,

\frac{R_{j}}{r\sqrt{n}}=2^{-j/2}-n^{-1/2}=\frac{2^{-j}-n^{-1}}{2^{-j/2}+n^{-1/2}}=\frac{n-2^{j}}{n\cdot 2^{j}(2^{-j/2}+n^{-1/2})}.

Now, since
j\leqslant L
, we have that
n^{-1/2}\leqslant 2^{-j/2}
, implying that
2^{-j/2}+n^{-1/2}\leqslant 2^{-j/2+1}
. Hence,

\frac{R_{j}}{r\sqrt{n}}\geqslant\frac{n-2^{j}}{n\cdot 2^{j/2+1}}.

We now make slightly different estimates for
j=L
and for
j<L
. If
j=L
, we have

\frac{R_{L}}{r\sqrt{n}}\geqslant\frac{1}{n\cdot 2^{L/2+1}}.

If
j<L
, we use the fact that
2^{L}<n\leqslant 2^{L+1}
to find that

\frac{R_{j}}{r\sqrt{n}}\geqslant\frac{n-2^{j}}{n\cdot 2^{j/2+1}}\geqslant\frac{2^{L}-2^{j}}{2^{L+j/2+2}}=\frac{1-2^{j-L}}{2^{j/2+2}}\geqslant\frac{1}{2^{j/2+3}}.

Consequently, we can write

\displaystyle\prod_{j=1}^{L}\left(\frac{R_{j}}{r\sqrt{n}}\right)^{n/2^{j}}

\displaystyle\geqslant\left[\left(\frac{1}{n\cdot 2^{L/2+1}}\right)^{2^{-L}}\cdot\prod_{j=1}^{L-1}2^{(-j/2-3)/2^{j}}\right]^{n}

\displaystyle=\left[\frac{1}{(2n)^{2^{-L}}}\cdot 2^{-\sum_{j=1}^{L}j/2^{j+1}}\cdot 8^{-\sum_{j=1}^{L-1}2^{-j}}\right]^{n}

\displaystyle\geqslant\left[\frac{1}{(2n)^{2^{-L}}}\cdot 2^{-\sum_{j\geqslant 1}j/2^{j+1}}\cdot 8^{-\sum_{j\geqslant 1}2^{-j}}\right]^{n}.

The series in the above exponents converge, with

\sum_{j\geqslant 1}2^{-j}=1\quad\text{and}\quad\sum_{j\geqslant 1}j\cdot 2^{-(j+1)}=1.

Hence, since
n\leqslant 2^{L+1}
,

\prod_{j=1}^{L}\left(\frac{R_{j}}{r\sqrt{n}}\right)^{n/2^{j}}\geqslant\frac{1}{(2n)^{n/2^{L}}}\cdot(2^{-1}\cdot 8^{-1})^{n}\geqslant\frac{1}{4n^{2}\cdot 16^{n}}.

Finally, we can return to inequality (
2

(3)

M(f)\geqslant\frac{1}{4n^{2}\cdot 16^{n}}\cdot(r\sqrt{n})^{n-N(R_{L})}.

Since
N(R_{L})<n/2^{L}\leqslant 2
and since

is an integer, we actually have
N(R_{L})\leqslant 1
. Since we have assumed that

, inequality (

) implies

M(f)\geqslant\frac{1}{4n^{2}\cdot 16^{n}}\cdot(r\sqrt{n})^{n-1}

and we get

\operatorname{sep}(f)=2r\leqslant\frac{2\cdot(4n^{2})^{1/(n-1)}\cdot 16^{1+1/(n-1)}}{\sqrt{n}}\cdot M(f)^{1/(n-1)}\leqslant\frac{34}{\sqrt{n}}\cdot M(f)^{1/(n-1)},

where we use the fact that

in the final inequality.

Note that the additional, stronger statement for

also follows. If
f(x)
has a nonreal root among its roots of minimum absolute value, then we cannot have

For the remaining examples, we take

We first prove a lemma on bounding binomial coefficients.

Lemma 3.4
.

For any positive integer

,

\binom{n}{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}\leqslant\frac{2^{n+1}}{\sqrt{\pi(2n+1)}}.

Proof.

It is known that for any positive integer

\binom{2\ell}{\ell}\leqslant\frac{2^{2\ell}}{\sqrt{\pi(\ell+1/4)}},

see for example
[

. Now, if

\binom{n}{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}\leqslant\frac{2^{n}}{\sqrt{\pi(n/2+1/4)}}=\frac{2^{n+1}}{\sqrt{\pi(2n+1)}}.

If

\displaystyle\binom{n}{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}=\binom{2k+1}{k}=\frac{2k+1}{k+1}\binom{2k}{k}\leqslant\frac{2k+1}{k+1}\cdot\frac{2^{2k}}{\sqrt{\pi(k+1/4)}}=\frac{n}{n+1}\cdot\frac{2^{n+1}}{\sqrt{\pi(2n-1)}}

to complete the proof.

(else, we may apply the same proof to
g(-x)
). We now have

(5)

\displaystyle M(g)

\displaystyle\geqslant\prod_{i=1}^{T}|\beta+ri|\cdot\prod_{j=1}^{S}|\beta-rj|\geqslant\prod_{i=1}^{T}\left(ri-\frac{r}{2}\right)\cdot\prod_{j=1}^{S}\left(rj+\frac{r}{2}\right)=r^{n-1}\cdot\prod_{i=1}^{T}\left(i-\frac{1}{2}\right)\cdot\prod_{j=1}^{S}\left(j+\frac{1}{2}\right).

Getting a handle on the lower bound given in inequality (
5
) will require some use of the gamma function. We use the fact that
\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)=\sqrt{\pi}
together with the usual fact that
\Gamma(z)=(z-1)\Gamma(z-1)
to note that

\prod_{i=1}^{T}\left(i-\frac{1}{2}\right)=\frac{\Gamma\left(T+\frac{1}{2}\right)}{\sqrt{\pi}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\prod_{j=1}^{S}\left(j+\frac{1}{2}\right)=\frac{2\Gamma\left(S+\frac{3}{2}\right)}{\sqrt{\pi}}.

By Wendel’s inequality
[
19
]
, if
m
is a nonnegative integer, we have

\Gamma\bigg(m+\frac{1}{2}\bigg)>\frac{m\Gamma(m)}{\sqrt{m+\frac{1}{2}}}=\frac{m!}{\sqrt{m+\frac{1}{2}}}.

Thus,

\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{T}\left(i-\frac{1}{2}\right)=\frac{\Gamma\left(T+\frac{1}{2}\right)}{\sqrt{\pi}}>\frac{T!}{\sqrt{\pi(T+\frac{1}{2})}},\quad\quad\prod_{j=1}^{S}\left(j+\frac{1}{2}\right)=\frac{2\Gamma\left(S+\frac{3}{2}\right)}{\sqrt{\pi}}>\frac{2(S+1)!}{\sqrt{\pi(S+\frac{3}{2})}}.

Now we have

(6)

\displaystyle M(g)\geqslant r^{n-1}\cdot\frac{T!}{\sqrt{\pi(T+\frac{1}{2})}}\cdot\frac{2(S+1)!}{\sqrt{\pi(S+\frac{3}{2})}}

from inequality (
5
) and we aim to estimate the right-hand side of this inequality from below in terms of
n
. We have the restrictions
0\leqslant S,T\leqslant n
and
S+T+1=n
, so we can replace
S+1
in equation (
6
) by
n-T
to find

\displaystyle\frac{T!}{\sqrt{\pi(T+\frac{1}{2})}}\cdot\frac{2(S+1)!}{\sqrt{\pi(S+\frac{3}{2})}}

\displaystyle=\frac{T!}{\sqrt{\pi(T+\frac{1}{2})}}\cdot\frac{2(n-T)!}{\sqrt{\pi(n-T+\frac{1}{2})}}

(7)

\displaystyle=\frac{2n!}{\pi\binom{n}{T}\sqrt{(T+\frac{1}{2})(n-T+\frac{1}{2})}}\geqslant\frac{4n!}{\pi\binom{n}{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}(n+1)}

Now, using Lemma
3.4
and Robbins’ bound
n!>\sqrt{2\pi n}(n/e)^{n}e^{1/(12n+1)}

[
16
]
gives

\displaystyle\frac{T!}{\sqrt{\pi(T+\frac{1}{2})}}\cdot\frac{2(S+1)!}{\sqrt{\pi(S+\frac{3}{2})}}

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{4n!}{\pi\binom{n}{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}(n+1)}\geqslant\frac{n!\cdot\sqrt{2n+1}}{\sqrt{\pi}2^{n-1}(n+1)}

\displaystyle>\frac{\sqrt{2n}(n/e)^{n}e^{\frac{1}{12n+1}}\sqrt{2n+1}}{2^{n-1}(n+1)}=\left(\frac{n}{2e}\right)^{n}\cdot\frac{2\sqrt{2n(2n+1)}e^{\frac{1}{12n+1}}}{n+1}>3.46\left(\frac{n}{2e}\right)^{n}

by the fact that
n\geqslant 4
.

Finally, we return to inequality (
6
) to find that that

\displaystyle M(g)

\displaystyle\geqslant 3.46\operatorname{sep}(g)^{n-1}\left(\frac{n}{2e}\right)^{n},

and we can conclude that

\operatorname{sep}(g)\leqslant\left(\frac{M(g)}{3.46}\right)^{\frac{1}{n-1}}\left(\frac{2e}{n}\right)^{\frac{n}{n-1}}\leqslant\frac{2e(2e/3.46)^{1/3}}{n^{\frac{n}{n-1}}}M(g)^{\frac{1}{n-1}}\leqslant\frac{6.33}{n}M(g)^{\frac{1}{n-1}}.

Asymptotically, using Stirling’s approximation
n!\sim\sqrt{2\pi n}(n/e)^{n}
, we can combine inequality (
6
) and inequality (
7
) to deduce that

M(g)\geqslant\operatorname{sep}(g)^{n-1}\frac{4n!}{\pi\binom{n}{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}(n+1)}\geqslant(4-o(1))\operatorname{sep}(g)^{n-1}\left(\frac{n}{2e}\right)^{n},

as
n\to\infty
. It follows that
\operatorname{sep}(g)\leqslant\frac{2e+o(1)}{n}M(g)^{\frac{1}{n-1}}
as
n\to\infty
.

Finally, we show that the constant
2e
is asymptotically sharp in inequality (
4
). Let
r>1
be fixed. For each integer
n\geqslant 4
, set
f_{n}(x)=\prod_{j=-m}^{m}(x-jr)
if
n=2m+1
is odd, and
f_{n}(x)=\prod_{j=-m}^{m+1}(x-jr)
if
n=2m+2
is even. Stirling’s approximation then yields
\operatorname{sep}(f_{n})=\frac{2e+o(1)}{n}M(f_{n})^{\frac{1}{n-1}}
, as
n\to\infty
.
∎

Next, we consider cubic polynomials which have only one real root.

Proof of Proposition
3.2
.

Suppose that the real root of
f(X)
is
\alpha
and without loss of generality, we may assume that
\alpha\geqslant 0
. Let
\beta
denote the complex root of
f(X)
with positive imaginary part. Write
\beta=x+iy
for
x,y\in\mathbb{R}
.

We claim that we may assume that
x\leqslant 0
. If not, then set
\beta^{\prime}=-x+iy
and
g(X)=(X-\alpha)(X-\beta^{\prime})(X-\overline{\beta^{\prime}})
. Since
\operatorname{sep}(g)\geqslant\operatorname{sep}(f)
and
M(g)=M(f)
, proving the proposition for
g(X)
will prove it for
f(X)
. Hence, we only need to prove the proposition under the assumption that
x\leqslant 0
.

Let
R=|\beta|
. Note that if
y\leqslant\frac{\sqrt{3}R}{2}
, then

\operatorname{sep}(f)\leqslant|\beta-\overline{\beta}|=2y\leqslant\sqrt{3}R\leqslant\sqrt{3}M(f)^{1/2}

and we are done. Hence, for the rest of the proof, assume that
y>\frac{\sqrt{3}R}{2}
. In particular, this implies that
y>-x\sqrt{3}
.

Our next goal is to reduce to the case where
\alpha
is “small.” Let
t
be the unique value in
\mathbb{R}
for which the points
t,\beta,\overline{\beta}
form an equilateral triangle. One can check that
t=\sqrt{3}y+x
. Note that
t>-x\sqrt{3}+x=-x(\sqrt{3}-1)\geqslant 0
. Now, if
\alpha\geqslant t
, set
h(X)=(X-t)(X-\beta)(X-\overline{\beta})
; we have
\operatorname{sep}(f)=2y=\operatorname{sep}(h)
and
M(f)\geqslant M(h)
, so it suffices to prove the proposition for
h
. Hence, we may assume that
0\leqslant\alpha<t
.

Note that
M(f)=\max\{1,\alpha\}\cdot\max\{1,x^{2}+y^{2}\}
and
\operatorname{sep}(f)=|\alpha-\beta|=\sqrt{(\alpha-x)^{2}+y^{2}}
. Our goal is to show
M(f)/\operatorname{sep}(f)^{2}\geqslant\frac{1}{3}
. We divide our proof into two cases:

Case 1:

x^{2}+y^{2}\geqslant 1
.

Within this case, we have two subcases. First, assume that
\alpha<1
. Then we have

\frac{M(f)}{\operatorname{sep}(f)^{2}}=\frac{x^{2}+y^{2}}{(\alpha-x)^{2}+y^{2}}\geqslant\frac{x^{2}+y^{2}}{(1-x)^{2}+y^{2}}.

We aim to show that

\frac{x^{2}+y^{2}}{(1-x)^{2}+y^{2}}\geqslant\frac{1}{3}

or, equivalently, that
2(x^{2}+y^{2})\geqslant 1-2x.
If
-1/2\leqslant x\leqslant 0
, then this follows immediately from the fact that
x^{2}+y^{2}\geqslant 1
. If
x<-1/2
, then we can use the fact that
y>-x\sqrt{3}
to deduce that
2(x^{2}+y^{2})\geqslant 8x^{2}>1-2x.

Next, assume that
1\leqslant\alpha\leqslant t
. Here, we have

\frac{M(f)}{\operatorname{sep}(f)^{2}}=\frac{\alpha(x^{2}+y^{2})}{(\alpha-x)^{2}+y^{2}}=\frac{x^{2}+y^{2}}{\alpha-2x+\frac{x^{2}+y^{2}}{\alpha}}.

Observe that the denominator, as a function of
\alpha
, will be maximized at either
\alpha=1
or
\alpha=t
. In the case of
\alpha=1
, we have already shown that

\frac{x^{2}+y^{2}}{(1-x)^{2}+y^{2}}\geqslant\frac{1}{3}.

So it remains to check that

\frac{t(x^{2}+y^{2})}{(t-x)^{2}+y^{2}}\geqslant\frac{1}{3}.

First, observe that since
t=x+y\sqrt{3}
, we have

\frac{t(x^{2}+y^{2})}{(t-x)^{2}+y^{2}}=\frac{t((t-y\sqrt{3})^{2}+y^{2})}{4y^{2}}=\frac{t^{3}-2\sqrt{3}t^{2}y+4ty^{2}}{4y^{2}}.

Next, observe that
(t^{3}-2\sqrt{3}t^{2}y+4ty^{2})/(4y^{2})
is a nondecreasing function of
t
since its partial derivative with respect to
t
is
3(t-2y/\sqrt{3})^{2}/(4y^{2})
. Note additionally that since
y\geqslant-x\sqrt{3}
, we have

t=\sqrt{3}y+x\geqslant\left(\sqrt{3}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\right)y=\frac{2}{\sqrt{3}}y.

Since
(t^{3}-2\sqrt{3}t^{2}y+4ty^{2})/(4y^{2})
is nondecreasing in
t
and since
t\geqslant\frac{2}{\sqrt{3}}y
, we must have

\frac{t^{3}-2\sqrt{3}t^{2}y+4ty^{2}}{4y^{2}}\geqslant\frac{2}{3\sqrt{3}}y.

But the fact that
x^{2}+y^{2}\geqslant 1
together with the assumption that
y\geqslant-x\sqrt{3}
implies that
y\geqslant\sqrt{3}/2
, and hence, we can finally conclude that

\frac{M(f)}{\operatorname{sep}(f)^{2}}=\frac{t(x^{2}+y^{2})}{(t-x)^{2}+y^{2}}=\frac{t^{3}-2\sqrt{3}t^{2}y+4ty^{2}}{4y^{2}}\geqslant\frac{2}{3\sqrt{3}}y\geqslant\frac{1}{3}.

Case 2:

x^{2}+y^{2}<1
.

Note now that since
x^{2}+y^{2}<1
and
y\geqslant-x\sqrt{3}
, we must have
x\geqslant-1/2
.

Again, we have two subcases. First, if
\alpha<1
, then

\frac{M(f)}{\operatorname{sep}(f)^{2}}=\frac{1}{(\alpha-x)^{2}+y^{2}}\geqslant\frac{1}{(1-x)^{2}+y^{2}}=\frac{1}{1-2x+x^{2}+y^{2}}\geqslant\frac{1}{2-2x}\geqslant\frac{1}{3}

and we are done.

Now assume that
1\leqslant\alpha\leqslant t
. The fact that
t\geqslant 1
implies that
y=(t-x)/\sqrt{3}\geqslant 1/\sqrt{3}
. In this case, we have

\frac{M(f)}{\operatorname{sep}(f)^{2}}=\frac{\alpha}{(\alpha-x)^{2}+y^{2}}=\frac{1}{\alpha-2x+\frac{x^{2}+y^{2}}{\alpha}},

which is again minimized when
\alpha=1
or
\alpha=t
. We have already shown that

\frac{1}{(1-x)^{2}+y^{2}}\geqslant\frac{1}{3},

so it remains to show that

\frac{t}{4y^{2}}=\frac{t}{(t-x)^{2}+y^{2}}\geqslant\frac{1}{3}.

If
y\leqslant\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}
, then we have

\frac{t}{4y^{2}}\geqslant\frac{t}{3}\geqslant\frac{1}{3}
. If
y\geqslant\sqrt{3}/2
, then we use the fact that
x\geqslant-\sqrt{1-y^{2}}
to get

\frac{t}{4y^{2}}=\frac{x+y\sqrt{3}}{4y^{2}}\geqslant\frac{y\sqrt{3}-\sqrt{1-y^{2}}}{4y^{2}}\geqslant\frac{1}{3}.

We can see that the constant
\sqrt{3}
is optimal due to the polynomial
f(x)=x^{3}-1
.

∎

Finally, we turn our attention to the case where
f(x)
is quartic with no real roots.

Proof of Proposition
3.3
.

Suppose that the roots of
f(x)
are
\alpha,\bar{\alpha},\beta,\bar{\beta}
where
\Im[\alpha],\Im[\beta]>0
and
|\alpha|=r\leqslant|\beta|=R
. We first note that
\operatorname{sep}(f)=\min\{2\Im[\alpha],2\Im[\beta],|\alpha-\beta|\}).
We have the following two cases:

Case 1:

2r\leqslant R

In this case, we note that

\operatorname{sep}(f)\leqslant 2\Im[\alpha]\leqslant 2r\leqslant 2r^{1/2}\left(\frac{R}{2}\right)^{1/2}=\sqrt{2}r^{1/2}R^{1/2}\leqslant\sqrt{2}M(f)^{1/4}.

Case 2:

r\leqslant R<2r

In this case, we first observe that if
\Im[\alpha]<\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}r^{1/2}R^{1/2}
or if
\Im[\beta]<\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}r^{1/2}R^{1/2}
, then we are done because

\operatorname{sep}(f)\leqslant\min\{2\Im[\alpha],2\Im[\beta]\}\leqslant\sqrt{2}r^{1/2}R^{1/2}\leqslant\sqrt{2}M(f)^{1/4}.

Hence,

\alpha\in\left\{z\in\mathbb{C}:|z|=r\text{ and }\Im[z]\geqslant\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}r^{1/2}R^{1/2}\right\}=:S

and

\beta\in\left\{z\in\mathbb{C}:|z|=R\text{ and }\Im[z]\geqslant\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}r^{1/2}R^{1/2}\right\}=:T.

As a result, we have

\displaystyle\operatorname{sep}(f)

\displaystyle\leqslant|\alpha-\beta|\leqslant\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}z_{1}\in S\\
z_{2}\in T\end{subarray}}|z_{1}-z_{2}|

\displaystyle=\left|\left(\sqrt{r^{2}-\frac{rR}{2}}+i\cdot\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}r^{1/2}R^{1/2}\right)-\left(-\sqrt{R^{2}-\frac{rR}{2}}+i\cdot\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}r^{1/2}R^{1/2}\right)\right|

\displaystyle=\sqrt{r^{2}-\frac{rR}{2}}+\sqrt{R^{2}-\frac{rR}{2}}.

We claim that

\sqrt{r^{2}-\frac{rR}{2}}+\sqrt{R^{2}-\frac{rR}{2}}\leqslant\sqrt{2}r^{1/2}R^{1/2}.

To see this, divide both sides of the inequality by
r^{1/2}R^{1/2}
to obtain the equivalent inequality

\sqrt{\frac{r}{R}-\frac{1}{2}}+\sqrt{\frac{R}{r}-\frac{1}{2}}\leqslant\sqrt{2}.

Observe that this new inequality only depends on the ratio
x=\frac{R}{r}
, which we have bounded by
1\leqslant x<2.
It is now a simple calculus problem to show that

\sqrt{\frac{1}{x}-\frac{1}{2}}+\sqrt{x-\frac{1}{2}}\leqslant\sqrt{2}

for all
1\leqslant x<2
and the proof that
\operatorname{sep}(f)\leqslant\sqrt{2}M(f)^{1/4}
is complete.

To see that the bound is sharp, consider the family of polynomials

f_{t}(x)=(x-t(1+i))(x-t(1-i))(x-t(-1+i))(x-t(-1-i))

for
t\in\mathbb{R}
with
t\geqslant 1/\sqrt{2}
. Every polynomial in this family has
\operatorname{sep}(f_{t})=\sqrt{2}M(f_{t})^{1/4}.

∎
