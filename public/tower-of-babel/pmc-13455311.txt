Using an asset mapping approach to evaluate the reach of community-level health equity interventions
Authors: Kara Hollins, Sharon Fitzgerald Wolff, Daniel J. Parente, Edward F. Ellerbeck, Christina M. Pacheco, Antonio Miras Neira, Kevin J. Sykes, Elizabeth Ablah, Sarah Finocchario-Kessler
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAsset Mapping Score Analysis (AMSA) is a standardized approach to organizing data on community assets. This study uses AMSA in two ways to quantify health equity efforts of the Communities Organizing to Promote Equity (COPE) project: (1) quantify community assets identified by COPE; and (2) apply AMSA methods in a novel way to evaluate the reach and depth of COPE interventions.MethodsCOPE aimed to strengthen community-driven public health by establishing community coalitions, called Local Health Equity Action Teams, in 20 Kansas counties. LHEATs were tasked with: (1) identifying local organizations addressing health and social needs; (2) addressing needs through community-level interventions; and (3) addressing the needs of individual community members (clients). Using the AMSA framework, we assigned an asset score to organizational resources in each community and client-level interventions. We examined the relationship between asset scores and the level of area deprivation at the census block level.ResultsOf 1,786 organizations, 355 community-level interventions, and 3,241 individual clients supported, 63.4% (n = 1,133), 64.9% (n = 231), and 69.6% (n = 2,256) were in areas of high deprivation (Area Deprivation Index ≥ 7), respectively. Compared to lower ADI block groups, mean asset scores were significantly higher in block groups characterized by higher ADI for all three measures (organizations: 4.2 ± 9.06 vs 1.5 ± 4.85, p < .001; community activities: 0.7 ± 2.61 vs 0.2 ± 0.88, p < .001; individual client support: 55.7 ± 67.52 vs 26.7 ± 25.15, p < .001).ConclusionsIn this study, community coalitions prioritized areas of greatest need. GIS-based asset mapping provides a valuable lens for assessing the distribution and intensity of public health efforts beyond traditional evaluation methods.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13690-026-01978-0.

Introduction

Disparities exposed by the COVID-19 pandemic reinforced the urgent need to meaningfully engage community members and leverage existing infrastructure in public health planning, not only to strengthen public health responses to emergencies but also to better address upstream determinants of health and mitigate the downstream consequences of social inequities [1–3]. Community-driven interventions employing local resources identified by community members can support relevant and sustainable interventions addressing the diverse contexts and drivers of health inequities [3, 4].

Asset mapping geospatially depicts the strengths, resources, and capacities that exist within a community [5]. Asset Mapping Score Analysis (AMSA) is a standardized approach that helps communities identify service-dense and service-scarce areas by mapping local organizations and institutions that provide material support, as well as social and health services [6].

Organizations and individuals utilize asset maps to collaboratively identify and mobilize their strengths and inform decisions on distributing available resources [5, 6]. Several past studies have demonstrated the utility of asset mapping in identifying and addressing health-related community needs; for example, by revealing discrepancies between community-perceived and researcher-identified assets, highlighting inequitable resource distribution in areas with high social vulnerability, and illustrating geographic and population-level disparities in program availability [7–10]. However, many studies utilize asset mapping efforts related to a particular program or specific resource, such as organizations, programs, or community-developed interventions, to assess capacity-building efforts in high-need areas [11, 12].

Thus, while asset mapping is widely used in public health to depict community resources and identify gaps, it has yet to be applied to evaluate the depth and reach of community-level interventions [8]. Beyond quantifying community assets, AMSA methods could be applied to health equity interventions to evaluate their depth and reach, providing a visual representation of the strengths and opportunities for improvement of community-driven research.

Additionally, asset data can be overlaid with measures of neighborhood vulnerability, such as the Area Deprivation Index (ADI) to determine if assets and interventions are occurring in the areas of greatest social need to inform further targeted health interventions, resource allocation, policy development, and/or community planning [13, 14]. The purpose of this study is to evaluate the reach of the Communities Organizing to Promote Equity (COPE) project in 20 counties across Kansas; a project developed to address disparities exacerbated by COVID-19. Applying AMSA, we quantify three components of COPE: (1) organizational assets, (2) community-level activities, and (3) individual-level CHW client support and evaluate these measures in relation to area-level deprivation to visually depict the reach of COPE interventions in the context of geography and prioritized populations. Specifically, we evaluate three components of COPE: (1) organizational assets, (2) community-level activities, and (3) individual-level CHW client support. Further, we evaluate AMSA scores by area-level deprivation. By applying this approach we aim to demonstrate how identifying assets and building relationships can effectively reach highly vulnerable individuals and improve health outcomes.

Methods

Study overview and setting

Building upon the RADxUp Kansas model, Kansas developed the COPE project; a community-academic partnership implemented across 20 Kansas counties in June 2021 in collaboration with the Kansas Department of Health and Environment [15]. COPE counties (16 rural, 4 urban) were selected using multiple criteria, including Kansas Public Health rankings, Social Vulnerability Index and Area Deprivation Index data, rates of COVID-19 infection, testing and vaccination, and existing/emerging relationships with local leaders and organizations. This selection process focused on rural and under-resourced urban communities with higher socioeconomic deprivation, emerging needs, and more limited public health capacity compared to the state overall [16].

Grounded in Human-Centered Design (HCD), which offers strategies for partnering with community stakeholders, understanding their experiences, and co-designing interventions, COPE established Local Health Equity Action Teams (LHEATs) in each of the 20 counties, comprising community members, service providers, and community health workers (CHWs) [17]. Using a HCD approach, COPE addresses complex, context-specific health disparities by centering the needs and priorities of populations experiencing inequities, with a focus on capacity building and co-creating equity-informed strategies [18].

The COPE project invested in communities to build capacity for local community-driven responses to public health emergencies, leveraging community engagement to prevent disproportionate impact on historically resilient populations. Each LHEAT worked with 2–3 CHWs and received a $50,000 budget to design, support, and implement pragmatic and locally relevant strategies to address community-identified needs aligned with community-identified priority domains. COPE CHWs were trusted members of the communities they served, tasked with two primary functions: implementing LHEAT activities and fostering cross-sectional partnerships to facilitate access to resources for individuals and families in their community with unmet social needs. This study was approved by the institutional review board at the affiliated institution. Informed consents were obtained from all individuals who participated.

Measures and asset mapping scoring

LHEAT members, primarily CHWs, utilized a custom database created by and for COPE CHWs. This secure, HIPAA-compliant electronic application enabled real-time tracking of community assets, activities, and CHW support for community members (referred to as clients in this work) [19]. Deidentified data used for this study were collected between June 1, 2021, and December 31, 2023.

Adapting the framework described by Healy, Ramirez [6], we applied AMSA to quantify the geographic reach and depth of the COPE project. The original AMSA point system scored organizations based on their engagement across combinations of functional areas (e.g., education, direct services) and content areas (e.g., breastfeeding, mental health), allowing researchers to assess organizational strengths and capacity within specific public health domains. We applied this framework in two key ways to evaluate the efforts of the COPE project. First, we used AMSA to quantify community assets identified by COPE CHWs. Second, we applied the AMSA method in a novel way to evaluate the reach and depth of COPE health equity interventions, which included both community-level interventions (referred to in this work as LHEAT activities) and individual-level interventions (referred to here as client support). For each asset, organization, LHEAT activity, or client support, we awarded one point for every functional area addressed; the points were then summed to generate an individual score for each asset. These scores were then aggregated by census block group to create area-level scores representing the distribution and intensity of COPE engagement.

Organizational assets

As part of the COPE project, CHWs identified organizations in each county available to provide services and/or material support to community members and documented them in the COPE database. We extracted each organization’s address as well as fields reflecting if the organization participated in the following functional areas: (1) bi-directional referrals; (2) LHEAT partnership; (3) relationship building; and (4) other. The definitions of these functional areas were refined based on the predefined objectives documented for each partner in the database.

Bi-directional referrals were defined as organizations involved in referral networks, accepting referrals from or providing referrals to COPE CHWs for individual support. Organizations were considered an LHEAT partner if they worked with the LHEATs on health equity priorities, providing time, material resources, and/or financial contributions to expand resources beyond the initial seed funding provided to the LHEATs. Relationship building was characterized as organizations collaborating solely for ongoing relationship-building, maintaining project updates, fostering dissemination of innovation, and exploring future partnerships. Organizations that were considered to have another type of relationship with COPE were given one point for other (e.g., free publicity via Facebook posts or news articles, providing an access point location where a patient or client might have access to a CHW).

To capture the depth of relationships between COPE and community organizations, each organization was given an asset score (range 0–4) with one point for each of the functional areas, described above, in which organizations participated. Individual organizations’ scores were summarized by census block group (range 0–75), with higher scores indicating a stronger depth of relationships between COPE and partners in that area.

LHEAT activities

CHWs and LHEAT leaders logged comprehensive details on community-level LHEAT-initiated activities, which included implementation methods and components, locations, estimated reach, intended impact, and achieved outcomes. Functional areas for LHEAT activities were established based on predetermined target areas and defined in accordance with priority needs collectively identified by the community. The description of each activity was reviewed and assigned one point for each of the following functional areas it encompassed (range 0–5): (1) education; (2) direct services; (3) community needs assessments; (4) capacity building; and (5) link to services/service enhancement.

Education was defined as providing knowledge, tools, or resources to inform the community about a particular issue. This included efforts that offered immediate practical knowledge or provided short-term solutions to address an issue (e.g., handouts, campaigns, and newspaper publications). Activities were categorized as direct services if they involved the dissemination of tangible resources intended to meet immediate needs. Examples of direct services included providing food, clothing, vaccinations, or fuel for transportation. The community needs assessments category involved collecting information from residents to identify service and resource gaps in the community to better address residents’ needs. Activities meeting this criterion include conducting community needs assessments, town hall events, or surveys to inform planning. Capacity building referred to activities that improved skills and knowledge to strengthen the abilities of individuals, groups, or organizations to identify and solve their own issues improving long-term resilience and sustainability. Examples include community trainings for naloxone use, grant writing training, and financial literacy workshops. Lastly, link to services/service enhancement was characterized as activities that involved improving infrastructure or providing resources that improve the ability to offer services. Examples include wheelchair ramps to make a building ADA accessible, buying freezers for food pantries, or using events to connect with clients and linking clients to external services outside of the event.

Each activity was given a score (range 0–5) with one point allocated for each of the functional areas addressed by the activity. Individual activity scores were summarized by census block group (range 0–46), with higher scores indicating higher levels of reach within that block group.

Individual client support

COPE CHW clients were identified at community events or through referrals from local organizations. CHWs conducted interviews with all clients to identify what needs they had and strategies to address them. CHWs tracked engagement with clients in the COPE database [19]. We calculated each client’s engagement asset score by assigning one point for each identified need that was successfully addressed (range 0–65). To create the block group score, we summarized individual client scores by census block group (range 0–424), with higher scores indicating higher levels of reach to that block group.

Geocoding and spatial data processing

Organization, activity, and residential locations

Address data for organizational assets, LHEAT activities, and COPE clients were geocoded via the US Census Bureau batch address processing, which provided the latitude and longitude values for each address [20]. These coordinates were plotted in ArcGIS Pro 3.1 (Environmental Systems Research Institute, Redlands, CA, USA) and spatially joined to the census block group [21]. When available, exact addresses were used. If addresses were missing but the zip code was known, the latitude and longitude values for the centroid of the zip code were used. Organizational assets were excluded from analyses if: (1) the organization had no physical address; (2) addresses were unknown or intentionally undisclosed (e.g. a shelter for domestic abuse victims); (3) addresses were located outside the catchment area specific to COPE project (e.g. an organizational asset located in a neighboring state or county providing support to a COPE county); or (4) addresses fell in a block group with a null ADI value. Activities were excluded from analysis if: (1) the address was unknown or missing from the database; (2) the activity was considered county-wide (e.g., Facebook campaigns, online courses); or (3) the address fell within a block group with a null ADI value. Similarly, clients were excluded from analyses for the following reasons: (1) their address was missing or not disclosed; (2) they had no physical address or could not be geocoded (e.g., P.O. boxes, incomplete addresses, unhoused or living in transient housing); (3) the residential address was not within a county participating in COPE; or (4) their address fell in a block group with a null ADI value.

Area deprivation index

The 2021 ADI was downloaded from the University of Wisconsin’s Center for Health Disparities Research Neighborhood Atlas. The ADI estimates a neighborhood’s socioeconomic disadvantage through a score, comprised of factors related to income, education, employment, and housing quality [14]. State rank scores range from 1 (least deprived) to 10 (most deprived). We matched block groups within each COPE county to the state rank score and classified each block group as areas of high (ADI ≥ 7) versus lower (ADI < 7) deprivation.

Block-group characteristics

Characteristics of block groups in COPE counties were extracted from 2020 census data and American Community Survey 5-year estimates (2019–2023) from National Historical Geographic Information System (IPUMS NHGIS) [22]. These included block-group-level population, rurality, race, ethnicity, education, poverty, median household income, public assistance, and insurance.

Statistical and asset mapping analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize characteristics of block groups in COPE counties. We generated block-group-level choropleth maps by applying quantile classification of asset scores across each of the three measures (organizational assets, LHEAT activities, and client support) [23]. We overlaid block group scores with block-group ADI values to geographically illustrate the most vulnerable neighborhoods and the distribution of organizational assets, community-based efforts, and individual client support in relation to those areas. Associations between ADI and each asset score (organization, community, and client) were evaluated using generalized estimating equations (GEE) to account for clustering within block groups. Results

In 2020, the 20 COPE counties in Kansas included 1,522 block groups. The mean population per block group was 1,247 residents (SD 557), with a mean of 14.2% of residents residing in rural areas. Of the 1,522 block groups, 533 (35%) were characterized as areas of high deprivation and 800 (53%) as areas of low deprivation. Higher ADI areas had higher percentages of the population identifying as racial or ethnic minorities, having less than a high school diploma (or equivalent), receiving public assistance, and being uninsured. Household income in low ADI areas was more than double the household income in high ADI areas. Organizational assets

CHWs and LHEATs logged 2,435 organizational partnerships into the COPE database. A total of 649 organizational assets were excluded from analyses due to having a physical address outside of COPE counties (n = 513), having no physical address (e.g., P.O. boxes, mobile or virtual services) or being intentionally undisclosed (e.g., safehouses) (n = 40), or falling in a block group with null ADI values (n = 96).

Organizational partners included in the geocoding analysis (n = 1,786) included social service providers (n = 347, 19.4%), healthcare organizations (n = 332, 18.6%), corporations (n = 347, 19.4%), governmental agencies (n = 200, 11.2%), civic/community-based organizations (n = 223, 12.5%), faith-based organizations (n = 166, 9.3%), K-12 schools (n = 91, 5.1%), correctional facilities/law enforcement (n = 59, 3.3%), and academic institutions (n = 21, 1.2%). Of the 1,786 geocoded partners, 1,377 (77.1%) accepted bi-directional referrals, 845 (47.3%) were LHEAT partners, 1,267 (70.9%) engaged in relationship building, and 118 (6.6%) partnered with the LHEAT for another purpose. Asset scores for each organization ranged from 0 to 4, with a mean score of 2.0 (SD = 0.81) per organization. Nearly 2/3 (n = 1,133, 63.4%) of organizations were located in areas characterized by high levels of deprivation. A comparison of organizational asset scores by inclusion status is provided in Supplemental Table 1.

The mean block-group level score was 2.5 (range 0–75). About 68% (n = 999) of block groups had an asset score of 0, indicating that no organizations were present in those areas. The quantile ranking classified organizational asset scores into the following five groups based on block group composite asset scores: (1) zero; (2) one to two; (3) three to four; (4) five to 10; and (5) 11 to 75.

The mean asset score in block groups characterized by higher levels of area deprivation was 4.2 (SD 9.06) compared to the mean asset score of 1.5 (SD 4.85) for block groups with low ADI scores (p < 0.001). There was also a difference in the percentage of block groups with an asset score of 0 by level of ADI, with 78% of low ADI block groups having a score of 0 compared to 51% of high ADI block groups. LHEAT activities

LHEATs documented a total of 404 community outreach activities implemented across unique times and locations. A total of 355 activities were included in analyses after excluding 26 that were missing address data, eight that were considered county-wide (e.g., Facebook campaigns, online courses), and 15 that fell within block groups with null ADI values. Of the 355 geocoded activities, 144 (40.6%) involved educational components, 230 (64.8%) provided direct services, 19 (5.4%) received one point for community needs assessment functional area, 64 (18%) involved capacity building strategies, and 90 (25.4%) provided linkages to services or service enhancement. Asset scores for each activity ranged from 0 to 5 with a mean of 1.6 (SD 0.79) per activity. Of the 355 geocoded LHEAT activities, 231 (65.0%) were conducted within areas of high deprivation.

The mean block-group level score for activities was 0.4 (range 0–46). About 88% (n = 1,288) of the block groups had a score of 0, indicating no LHEAT activities were conducted there. The quantile ranking classified LHEAT activity asset scores into the following five groups: (1) zero; (2) one; (3) two; (4) 3; and (5) four to 46.

Block groups characterized by higher levels of area deprivation had higher activity asset scores than lower ADI groups (0.7 (SD 2.61) vs. 0.2 (SD 0.88), p < 0.001). There were fewer block groups with a score of 0 in high ADI areas (79%) compared to lower ADI areas (92%). Figure 3 displays this information for COPE counties in the Southeast region of Kansas. Individual client support

One county opted out of using the COPE database to track individual client support. Across 19 counties tracking client data in the COPE database, CHWs identified 5,447 individuals seeking support during the study time frame, of whom 3,241 are included in these analyses after excluding those with missing address data (n = 1,858), those with no physical addresses or locations that could not be geocoded (e.g., P.O. boxes, incomplete addresses, unhoused or living in transient housing) (n = 237), those whose coordinates were not in a COPE county (n = 322), or residential address falling within a null ADI area (n = 26).

CHWs helped the 3,241 clients successfully address a total of 7,849 reported needs during the study time frame (mean 2.3 (SD 3.58 per client). Nearly 70% (n = 2,256/3,241, 69.6%) of clients reached resided in areas of high deprivation.

The total number of needs successfully addressed per block group ranged from 0 to 424 and fell into the following five quantiles: (1) zero; (2) one to two; (3) three to seven; (4) eight to 21; and (5) 22–424. The mean client engagement asset score across block groups was 46.3 (SD 59.1). The mean client score in areas characterized by higher levels of area deprivation was significantly higher than the score among areas of lower ADI (mean: 55.7 (SD 67.52) versus 26.7 (SD 25.15), p < 0.001). Among block groups, 36% in low ADI areas had a score of 0, compared to 51% in high ADI areas. Figure 4 displays this information for COPE counties in the Southeast region of Kansas. Discussion

This study found that LHEATs, community coalitions intentionally composed of community residents, health and social service providers, and CHWs, were able to identify organizations and conduct community- and individual-level interventions that prioritized areas characterized by high levels of deprivation. Specifically, we found higher asset scores for organizational assets, community-level interventions, and individual-level support in more deprived areas compared to less deprived areas. This suggests that by distributing power equally among these three groups within LHEATs, COPE counties were positioned to identify and prioritize areas of greatest local need, effectively directing seed funding to where resources were most needed. However, given the selection criteria for COPE counties, these findings may have limited generalizability and may require adaptation across different settings. This reinforces the importance of HCD in relationship-building and empowering communities to determine their own priorities, which can strengthen local capacity and foster sustainable partnerships across organizational networks serving populations facing social barriers to health.

We found that applying the AMSA approach within this HCD project to be particularly useful for quantifying the reach of not only community assets, but also interventions, as LHEAT activities and relationships with organizational partnerships were often multi-faceted, making it difficult to capture the nuance and reach of assets using traditional quantitative methods. Further, there were varying levels of individual support provided by CHWs in the COPE database, with some clients requesting only one resource or service, and others requesting more than 60. The heterogeneity of the individual support also calls for more nuanced analyses to evaluate reach. Overall, our results suggest that integrating HCD principles with quantitative tools such as AMSA and GIS can enhance translational research by improving the identification of high-need areas, informing resource allocation, and strengthening the relevance and sustainability of interventions.

Evolving GIS techniques provide evidence of geographic differences in health-related outcomes and the role of place in equitably addressing health disparities [5]. While most studies focused on a particular program, they found that applying similar methods improved the planning, prioritization, allocation, and utilization of public health resources and services concurrently and in future initiatives, increasing the likelihood of sustainability [7, 11, 12]. Similar findings from past studies found that layering community input and multiple data sources was fundamental in bridging the gap of real versus perceived needs when informing intervention development or evaluation, capacity building efforts, feasible solutions, geographic reach, prioritization of vulnerable populations, and proper resource allocation [6, 9].

Past studies have indicated difficulties in obtaining community buy-in, which in turn hindered collaboration in adequately inventorying up-to-date community resources [5, 24]. COPE CHWs received ongoing training related to documenting organizations, activities, and client support, which enabled real-time updates and an inventory of community assets. Further, maintaining strong relationships with current and former COPE CHWs helped ensure that outdated information could be identified and updated. Documentation of client addresses, when available, and event zip code in which the asset was involved helps locate where the asset is being used on a map, despite it not having a physical location.

Some limitations must be noted. First, many organizations, activities, and client support could not be geocoded and matched to a block group. The reasons for this were multi-faceted. Despite ongoing training, data entry errors occurred. Further, some organizations and events did not have a physical address. Finally, some clients chose not to disclose their address or reported being unhoused. The COPE academic team was able to identify some data entry errors through asking CHWs for clarification when possible and/or conducting online searches for accurate information. For events and organizations in which we were able to identify a zip code, but not full address, we used the centroid of the zip code as the location. The use of zip code centroids, along with the exclusion of some organizations, activities, and client records due to insufficient data, may have introduced bias and influenced the observed distribution of assets.

Second, the functional areas for organizational assets were limited to data collection categories that were determined by CHWs based on importance when designing the database at the beginning of the COPE project implementation. Future research could collect more information from the organizations’ contacts to ask about the specific services provided, similar to the methods employed by Healy, Ramirez [6].

Finally, the current asset score density analysis giving one-point per client need successfully addressed provides a sufficient starting point for quantifying and illustrating CHWs’ efforts; however, it does not fully discern nuances between goals that were more readily achievable (like providing a list of food pantries in the area), and others that were more difficult, requiring more effort from CHWs (like connecting a client to resources for intimate partner violence). When combined with variation in CHW staffing across counties, due to differences in local hiring capacity and organizational resources, these limitations may have influenced distributions of both service delivery and the volume of recorded activities. Future work could assign a weighted scoring system to assign more value to those needs that are more complex to address.

Despite these limitations, this work demonstrates the feasibility of applying AMSA methods to quantify the reach of community- and individual-level interventions. Assigning asset scores to resources and interventions allows us to see beyond binary measures of reach (e.g., presence of the organization, activity, or client support) and see a more detailed picture of how resources are being allocated. Further, stratifying this by level of area deprivation helps to evaluate if these resources are being allocated to areas of greater need. Visualizations like those presented in this study can help communities and researchers identify areas of high need, represented by high ADI scores, that also have low asset scores; such areas may benefit from targeted support or asset-building efforts.

These findings have practical implications for organizations implementing and supporting community-based initiatives, including the COPE project. Integrating asset mapping with AMSA scoring can help identify strong partnerships with effective reach, as well as gaps in engagement across counties or regions. More broadly, these analyses lay the groundwork for future community-based research efforts to integrate community documentation and GIS technology. Not only is this a valuable method to evaluate the reach of work but can enable planners and other stakeholders to more accurately recommend, plan, and implement solutions to improve public health infrastructure networks, resource allocation and accessibility, and health outcomes.
