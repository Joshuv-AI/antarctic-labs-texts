Exploring free radiomics software tools: a multiparametric evaluation for cancer classification
Authors: Eduardo Almeda-Luna, José María Luna, Sebastián Ventura
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCancer remains the leading cause of mortality worldwide, accounting for over 10 million deaths annually. Despite recent advances in early detection and screening, traditional diagnostic procedures, such as biopsies, remain invasive and time consuming. Radiomics is a trending method that transforms visual information from medical images into numerical features and offers non-invasive, quantitative analysis of medical images to support clinical decision-making. Numerous radiomics software tools exist in the literature, each offering different features as they include a variety of transformations, calculations, and configurations. Selecting the most suitable tool poses a challenge that requires a comparative analysis due to the lack of standardisation. However, no prior study has systematically compared multiple free radiomics software tools across multiple cancer types while evaluating information fusion strategies, which represents the specific research gap addressed here. This paper aims to evaluate the performance of multiple free and open-access radiomics software tools, analyse the variability in the extracted features and demonstrate that combining different radiomics tools is more appropriate than choosing one individual.MethodsThis study conducts a comparative analysis of several free and open-access GUI radiomics software tools applied to cancer classification tasks using multiple datasets. The evaluation is carried out considering optimal machine learning pipelines that integrate feature selection and classification components using the radiomic features extracted from the radiomic tools. Information fusion methods are also proposed to demonstrate their high performance against single radiomics tools. Additionally, the radiomic features extracted from each tool were analysed, identifying those most relevant in the classification process.ResultsExperiments conducted on 10 datasets and 80 pipeline combinations (10 preprocessing pipelines and 8 classification models) revealed statistically significant differences in F1-score, AUROC and AUPRC a depending on the radiomics tool employed. Importantly, feature fusion consistently outperformed individual tools in classification tasks, reaching improvement of 3% on all the metrics. These results highlight the substantial impact of tool selection on model performance and the enhanced effectiveness of combining features from multiple sources. The study also identified the most relevant features contributing to model performance and the best classification pipeline.ConclusionThis study guides radiologists and researchers in selecting effective radiomics tools and pipelines for cancer classification. The findings underscore the importance of careful radiomics tool selection, as performance varies considerably across software. It demonstrates that fusion-based approaches that integrate features from multiple tools provide more robust and accurate predictions than any single tool.Keywords: Radiomics, Machine learning, Information fusion, Cancer classification

Introduction

Cancer is the leading cause of mortality worldwide, with more than 10 million deaths attributed to this disease every year, equating to approximately one in six deaths [1]. However, there has been a downward trend in cancer mortality in recent years, due to advances in early detection, screening, and other factors]. However, there has been a downward trend in cancer mortality in recent years, due to advances in early detection, screening, and other factors [2–4]. Although medical imaging serves as a valuable tool for detecting and initially evaluating tumours, biopsy remains indispensable for accurate diagnosis and guiding the appropriate treatment despite its drawbacks, including invasiveness and risk of infection. Any delay in identifying and treating cancer patients can lead to avoidable deaths.

In oncology, there was a noticeable decrease in cancer diagnoses in 2020 [5, 6], requiring alternative approaches to speed up the analysis process, reduce the need for biopsies and achieve the goal of personalised medicine [7]. Deep learning has shown great potential in a wide range of medical imaging applications [8–10]. These approaches have demonstrated strong predictive power, but often require large annotated datasets, considerable computational resources, and may lack interpretability, which can hinder their adoption in clinical practice. In this regard, radiomics [11] is an emerging field of extreme interest that includes methods/tools that extract numerous quantitative features, generally hardly perceptible to the human eye, from medical images. This quantitative evaluation is responsible for the growing importance of radiomics in oncology and radiology, improving clinical decision-making and augmenting the discriminatory and predictive potential of medical images.

Radiomics features can be categorised into four groups [12]: shape features to describe morphological characteristics; first-order (intensity) features to denote the brightness or signal strength; texture features to describe heterogeneity; and transform-based features, which are extracted after applying filters to identify specific image characteristics such as edges and repetitive patterns. Combining these features with clinical data and using advanced statistical methods and machine learning may support clinical decision-making [13].

Radiomics libraries and tools have recently been used in cancer detection through machine learning, generating valuable insights. Li et al. [14] showed that combining lesion and parenchyma features in mammography improves malignancy classification. Lu et al. [15] compared radiomics software to predict EGFR mutation status in lung cancer, while Chianca et al. [16] classified spine bone tumours using features extracted from radiomics software tools. Pespane et al. [17] highlighted current radiomics limitations and challenges for clinical integration to achieve more personalised breast cancer management.

Despite the growing interest in radiomics applications, a significant challenge persists in standardization and reproducibility [18]. Many radiomics programming libraries and commercial and open-source software tools have been proposed so far. The radiomics graphical user interface (GUI) software tools are crucial and assist radiologists who may lack the programming skills required to utilize the radiomics libraries. Among these GUI tools we can list 3D Slicer [19], LIFEx [20], MaZda [21], IBEX [22], CERR [23], TriDFusion [24], Radiomix [25] (Oncoradiomics, Liege, Belgium), and Syngo.via [25] (Siemens Healthcare, Erlangen, Germany). However, each software tool operates differently and generates distinct feature sets due to variations in transformations, calculations, and configurations. This lack of standardization leads to discrepancies in the computation of features across different implementations. These variations encompass diverse feature categories and employ dissimilar terminology, parameter configurations, and image processing techniques, yielding disparate feature values. Moreover, the exact replication of ROIs and parameter settings across different tools is required to ensure stability of features and to facilitate the study reproducibility. Consequently, specialists often face uncertainty when choosing which radiomics tool, machine learning models, or preprocessing strategies are most appropriate for their studies [26].

This paper aims to support radiologists in selecting the most suitable radiomic tool and machine learning pipeline for cancer classification. A comprehensive comparison of various free, open-access GUI radiomics tools across multiple cancer types is conducted to achieve this goal. Only free and open-access GUI software tools are considered to secure democratised access among specialists, so that access to tools is not dependent on economic considerations. The experimental analysis serves a quadruple purpose: (1) to evaluate the performance of multiple radiomics tools using optimized machine learning pipelines that include feature selection and classification methods, all of them widely used in radiomics [27–34], (2) to improve the final performance using information fusion methods and to be able to demonstrate that the use of different radiomics tools is more appropriate than choosing one individually, (3) to identify the most frequently selected and relevant feature types per tool, and (4) to determine the best overall machine learning pipeline regardless the methodology used.

The final goal of the experimental analysis is to provide the end user (generally radiologists who want to use machine learning approaches on radiomics) with the best free GUI software tool (or combination of them through information fusion methods) and pipeline. Critical to this aim is the statistical analysis performed through the StaTDS library [35] (statistical tests for data science) to demonstrate that choosing the appropriate radiomics software tool is not trivial and that different results may produce subsequent machine learning-based analyses. These differences come from features extracted by each software tool, so their analysis will be key to identifying those features with the greatest predictive potential.

Section 4 presents the results. Section 5 describes the limitations of the study and future work and Sect. 6 presents the main conclusions.

Related work

This section describes some existing works related to the use of radiomics for cancer analysis, as well as different radiomics software tools.

Radiomics in cancer analysis

Radiomics [11] has been the subject of numerous publications in recent years, but most studies focus on a single lesion type and feature-extraction method. For instance, authors in [15] compared multiple radiomics tools to predict EGFR mutation status in 228 non–small cell lung cancer patients, extracting 1539 features with IBEX, 1319 with PyRadiomics [36]and 1160 with their in-house tool CIFE. Using k-nearest neighbours (KNN), support vector machine (SVM), random forest (RF) and bagging they reported AUCs of 0.54, 0.56 and 0.64 for IBEX, PyRadiomics and CIFE, respectively.

In [37], the authors evaluated the ability of different radiomics software packages to classify radiation pneumonitis and examined variability across tools. Using data from 105 esophageal cancer patients treated with radiotherapy, they compared an in-house tool (A1), IBEX, and PyRadiomics. They extracted four first-order histogram features and four second-order grey-level co-occurrence matrix (GLCM) features. Of the eight radiomic features, five were significantly correlated with radiation pneumonitis across all tools, and seven agreed among the packages in whether the AUC value was considerably greater than 0.5.

Some other authors [25] compared the features extracted from two commercial software tools (Radiomix and Syngo.via) and a free software tool (LIFEx). This paper aimed to investigate the reproducibility of the extracted features between radiomics applications. For this purpose, they compared these three tools with each other and with the Image Biomarker Standardisation Initiative (IBSI) standard, using a digital phantom, a set of 27 computed tomography of colorectal liver metastases, and 39 magnetic resonance (MR) scans of breast cancer. The paper concluded that even with IBSI compliance, feature reproducibility across applications is not guaranteed.

In [16], the authors classified spine bone tumours from T1 and T2-weighted MR using radiomics features extracted from PyRadiomics (1,548 features) and a module from 3D Slicer called heterogeneityCAD (90 features). The classification was performed with 100 patients for training and two test cohorts (46 internal patients, 35 external) under 2-label (benign vs. malignant) and 3-label (benign, metastasis, primary malignant) settings. For the 2-label classification, heterogeneityCAD data obtained 94% accuracy in the internal test cohort and 86% in the external one. For the 3-label classification, PyRadiomics achieved 80% (internal) and 69% (external) accuracy.

Some research studies were performed [38] to evaluate the reliability and quality of radiomic features in glioblastoma multiforme from MR images of 45 patients. Stability, normalized dynamic range, and redundancy were used as quality evaluators. Semi-automatic segmentations from 3D Slicer and TumorPrism3D (in-house software) were compared. Using both segmentations, the feature extraction was performed using MATLAB software, obtaining 180 radiomic features. The study concluded that most features were sufficiently stable, but certain aspects of feature quality, including normalized dynamic range and redundancy, need to be assessed. No statistical comparison of both tools was provided.

In [39], a comparison among three software tools (BrainVoyager, 3D Slicer and ITK-SNAP) for image semi-automatic segmentation was performed on 18 glioblastoma MR images. Two experts manually segmented the tumours, and the study measured segmentation time and inter-expert/software similarity. The authors reported high agreement within and between tools, with small intra-rater, inter-rater, and between-software differences, as well as high Dice similarity coefficients. Segmentation was time-consuming for all tools, particularly BrainVoyager, which may limit their use in daily clinical practice. No radiomics feature analysis was performed, as the tools were limited to segmentation.

Table 1 summarizes the key characteristics, modalities, tasks, and main findings of these studies. Overall, the reviewed studies consistently highlight the use of multiple software tools applied mainly to CT and MRI images for tasks such as tumour classification, mutation prediction, feature reproducibility assessment, and segmentation evaluation. Common findings indicate that while some features and trends are consistent across tools, there is substantial variability in feature values and model performance, and reproducibility is not always guaranteed. Most studies rely on single, relatively small datasets and focus on one image modality, limiting generalizability. Additionally, few studies integrate multiple software tools or assess clinical usability comprehensively. These limitations suggest opportunities for multi-center, multi-modal studies, robust statistical comparisons across tools, feature standardization beyond IBSI guidelines, and evaluation of tool integration and efficiency in clinical workflows.

Radiomics software tools

In radiomics, the need for standardisation is a significant challenge, as it hinders the exportation/importation of ROIs between different tools. Consequently, this research requires identical ROIs to perform an unbiased assessment between different radiomics software tools. Even slight variations in the ROI can result in disparate values of the characteristics, underlining the importance of ensuring equality in this context. Criteria for selecting a software tool for this comparative analysis include the ability to load medical images in DICOM format, import ROIs in a standardised format, and be freely available. The following is a list of well-known radiomics software tools and their characteristics.

3D Slicer [19] is a free, open-source software for visualization, processing, segmentation, registration, and analysis of medical, biomedical, and other 3D images and meshes. It supports several different medical imaging formats, including DICOM, NRRD, MetaImage (mha, mhd), Analyze (hdr, img), TIFF, JPG, PNG and BMP among others. It provides various tools for manual, automatic and semi-automatic segmentation. It includes a radiomics module based on PyRadiomics [36].

LIFEx [20] is a free software written in Java for automatic measurement of a large number of features characterizing tissue types from medical images and assessing tumour heterogeneity. This software does not rely on any commercial libraries. LIFEx can process Positron Emission Tomography (PET), CT, MR, US and Mammography, supporting DICOM, NIfTI, JPG, TIFF, PNG and BMP. It includes manual and semi-automatic segmentation tools and a texture feature extraction module.

MaZda [21] is a computer program for calculating texture parameters (features) in digitized images. The program code has been written in C++ and Delphi™. It loads Windows Bitmaps, selected DICOM formats or unformatted grey-scale image files with pixel intensity encoded with 8 or 16 bits. Segmentations can be imported in their own format or bitmap format. In the case of 3D images, importing segmentations in BMP format must be done on a slice-by-slice basis.

TriDFusion [24] is a free and open-source, extensible medical viewing platform programmed in MATLAB. This software supports different types of medical images: PET, gamma camera, nuclear medicine, CT, digital radiography, digital angiography, magnetic resonance (MR), Secondary Pictures and Scanned Images, mammography and ultrasonography. The following formats are valid to import/export: DICOM, Raw data from nuclear imaging devices (raw), DICOM-RT structure (contours), CERR planC, dose volumes and constraints, CSV, STL, AVI, MP4, GIF, DICOM, JPG, BMP, PNG, NIfTI and NRRD. This software has both manual and automatic segmentation. The PyRadiomics library must be installed to use the radiomics feature extraction module, as the software radiomics module functions as an interface to select PyRadiomics options.

IBEX [22] is a radiomics software tool based on MATLAB and C/C++ which support the radiomics workflow. It allows the importation of medical images (PET, CT, MRI) in DICOM format. Unfortunately, the software is not capable of importing mammographies. Although the program can theoretically load ROIs in standard formats (DICOM, NifTi, TIFF, etc.), in practice it has not been possible to import the segmentations from other tools. As for the segmentation tools, it only includes manual options.

CERR [23] is a MATLAB-based radiomics platform that supports the import of medical images from DICOM and other formats such as MHA and RTOG. Various DICOM modalities that can be imported include CT, MR, PET, US, SPECT, RTSTRUCT, RTPLAN, RTDOSE, and GSPS. This software requires a license from MATLAB to use the source code or compile it and generate the GUI executable. Segmentation can be done manually, and it also includes a deep learning model for automatic segmentation, but, as in IBEX, it has not been possible to import segmentations.

Radiomix [25] Research Toolbox is a commercial software implemented in MATLAB. It supports CT, MR, PET and US in DICOM format. However, it does not have segmentation tools, RT-STRUCT format segmentation is required.

Last but not least, Syngo.via Frontier [25] is a commercial software programmed in Python. It supports DICOM medical images and DICOM SEG and NIfTi segmentations. The radiomics feature extraction module is based on PyRadiomics, which is free and open source software, so it is our understanding that it is not reasonable to pay for a licence when there are free alternatives.

Data and methods

This paper aims to alleviate the arduous process radiologists face when selecting the most suitable tool and machine learning pipeline for cancer classification. This section describes the methodology used to carry out a comprehensive comparative analysis of various free radiomics software tools across different types of cancer. The ultimate goal is to provide the end user (generally radiologists who want to use machine learning approaches in radiomics) with the best GUI software tool and pipeline and to demonstrate that using a single radiomics tool is not recommended. This paper also aims to obtain the best characteristics (and types) provided by each radiomics tool.

Free radiomics software tools

In order to perform a fair comparison among radiomics tools and to guarantee reproducibility, it is essential to meet the following requirements: 1) the ability to import images in DICOM format, 2) the possibility of importing segmentations from one software to another in a standard format, 3) the software should be freely available. Due to the European Union’s Open Science policy, which promotes unrestricted access, reuse, and transparency of research outputs under initiatives such as Horizon Europe, the use of commercial software is discouraged because it limits reproducibility, accessibility, and compliance with open-access requirements. Based on these requirements, the following are the chosen software tools:

3D Slicer [19] v0.5.8.1 (http://www.slicer.org). It includes a module based on PyRadiomic to extract a total of 874 features, including the wavelet-based. Some of the features extracted from this tool have been removed as they have no statistical relevance, such as identifiers, software versions, and formatting features, among others.

Datasets

In this research work, real and widely used data in radiomics were considered, most of them were extracted from the Cancer Imaging Archive [40]. In this sense, publicly available datasets were selected to ensure transparency and reproducibility, prioritizing diversity in imaging modalities and cancer types, high-quality annotations for classification, and, whenever possible, segmentation of lesions.

Adrenal-ACC-Ki67-Seg [41] (A-ACC). This dataset includes contrast-enhanced CT imaging studies of 53 confirmed patients with adrenocortical carcinoma and segmentation of the lesion. The TNM stage has classified the lesions, an international standard for classifying the malignancy of a tumour based on a list of factors, considering lower or equal 2 (23 patients), and higher than 2 (30 patients).

Segmentation

Some datasets include the segmentations of the lesions, but in some specific cases, they are incomplete or incorrect, so correcting them by hand with the 3D Slicer segmentation tool has been necessary. In the case of DMID, some segmentations had internal gaps due to the way the segmentation was done (brush tool), so it was corrected by applying a hole-removal operation. As for CMMD-CC, CMMD-MLO, and MDB datasets, the segmentation process was performed manually using 3D Slicer, which took advantage of its semi-automatic tool to speed up the segmentation process. Given the sharpness of the edges, a smoothing filter was applied using a median function with a kernel size between 0.5 and 0.7 mm, depending on the image. The filter was applied iteratively, when necessary, until satisfactory smoothing was achieved for the segmented regions. In cases where the semi-automatic tool was inaccurate, ROIs were drawn manually. Subsequently, all segmentations were exported to NIfTi format (used by LIFEx and TriDFusion) and to bitmap (compatible with MaZda). This process ensures a fair comparison among the software tools. As explained in previous sections, even the slightest variation in the ROI produces different results, underlining the need to maintain identical ROIs across all software tools (Fig. 1 illustrates we are working on the same ROI).

Data preprocessing and algorithms

After completing the segmentation, each radiomics software tool extracts a set of features, including elements such as identifiers, software versions and format features, some of which are considered irrelevant. Some features are useless from a machine learning point of view, either because they contain a single value or are missing altogether. Data preprocessing is done on the extracted features, removing the previously mentioned useless features and scaling the remaining ones to get to standardized data. Additionally, Nearest Neighbour Imputer [50] was used to impute missing values. Finally, diverse feature selection approaches will allow us to determine their impact on results. This analysis encompasses three distinct feature selection techniques, considered as widely used in the literature and showed good results working with radiomics features [51]: Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) [52], ANalysis Of VAriance (ANOVA) [53] and minimum Redundancy - Maximum Relevance (mRMR) [54]. The number of selected features was varied, considering 20, 30, and 40 features. This choice was motivated by several factors. First, the smallest dataset contains only 51 samples, so selecting more features than samples could lead to unstable models. Second, preliminary experiments indicated that models already achieved meaningful learning with approximately 15 features, suggesting that these numbers are sufficient without being excessive. Third, using a fixed number of features across datasets and software tools facilitates direct comparisons, avoiding variability that would arise if the number of selected features were optimized separately for each case. In other words, the goal of this study was not to maximize absolute model performance, but rather to evaluate the relative consistency of the tools under comparable conditions. Correlation between features was not pre-filtered prior to feature selection. Instead, each feature selection method was allowed to handle correlations according to its own mechanism. This approach was chosen to evaluate the relative effectiveness of each method in managing correlated features, as part of the comparison across feature selection methods.

Eight distinct machine learning algorithms, sourced from the scikit-learn library, were incorporated into the experiments, alongside the previously mentioned data preprocessing techniques. These algorithms are: Logistic regression [55] (LR), decision tree [56] (DT), RF [57], KNN [58], eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) [59], Multilayer perceptron [60] (MLP), Naive Bayes [61] (NB), and SVM [62]. Including various algorithms allows the exploration and evaluation of different modelling approaches to take the most effective one. This variety includes interpretable and non-interpretable algorithms, ensemble models, and classic algorithms. All these algorithms have been tested on radiomics, showing promising results [27–34], so it is fair to choose all of them for this analysis.

Procedure

The aim of the experimental procedure is fourfold. First, it evaluates the performance of the selected radiomics software tools in isolation to show which one provides the best performance. The aim is to demonstrate that no single radiomics software tool outperforms the rest. Second, it improves the final performance using information fusion methods and demonstrates that using different radiomics tools is more appropriate than choosing one individually. Third, it analyses the differences among the features extracted by different tools, denoting important features provided by specific tools. Fourth, it analyses the machine learning pipelines to statistically show the one that produces the best results regardless the methodology used (information fusion methods or machine learning on single radiomics tools).

Starting from evaluating the performance of radiomics software tools, this paper performs a series of experiments considering all possible combinations of the selected machine learning algorithms and preprocessing models. This analysis uses the F1 score measure to quantify the accuracy of the models, as this measure is robust against unbalanced data. In addition, AUROC and AUPRC are reported to evaluate the overall ranking performance and the ability to discriminate between classes, with AUPRC being particularly informative in highly imbalanced datasets. For all metrics, the mean performance and 95% confidence intervals (CI) are estimated using bootstrapping, providing a robust assessment of variability and statistical reliability. These experiments cover different cancer types and employ a 5-fold stratified cross-validation, grouped by patient when multiple lesions per patient exist. The cross-validation is nested, ensuring that optimal hyperparameters are determined for each classification algorithm within each preprocessing pipeline. Preprocessing steps, including scaling and KNN imputation, were fit only on the training data, first within the inner folds to select hyperparameters, and then on the outer training folds before evaluating the held-out data, thereby preventing any information leakage. In addition, non-parametric statistical tests will determine whether there are significant variations in overall performance. The Friedman test [63] will show general differences, while the Nemenyi post-hoc test [64] will be employed to perform all pairwise comparisons. All these statistical tests are applied using the StaTDS library [35]. As a summary, Fig. 2 shows the proposed procedure, which starts gathering medical images and performing a segmentation of them. Radiomics features are then extracted with each of the selected software tools, giving rise to a dataset including all the features extracted. A subsequent preprocessing step is done on this dataset using the different feature selection techniques, and then the selected classification algorithms are applied.

In the A-ACC dataset (the smallest in our study), SVM achieved the highest AUROC and AUPRC values across all radiomics software tools. However, this behaviour was inconsistent with the corresponding confusion matrices, which showed poor discrimination performance. This mismatch indicates that the probabilistic outputs from SVM were unreliable for this dataset. This is because SVMs do not natively produce probabilistic outputs. These are typically obtained via post-hoc calibration such as Platt scaling. However, such calibration is known to be highly unstable in small datasets and can lead to overfitting, producing misleading probability estimates and artificially inflated AUROC/AUPRC values despite poor actual discriminative performance [65, 66]. To avoid biasing the comparative analysis and drawing misleading conclusions, SVM results for A-ACC were excluded. For this dataset, the next best-performing models were reported instead for the AUROC and AUPRC metrics.

Information fusion models comparison

This second study aims to demonstrate that using various tools is more appropriate than using one in isolation. The characteristics obtained by each radiomics software tool can be used together to improve the classification results. Two different information fusion models are considered, one based on a democratic model or ensemble, and the other one based on a data fusion approach (see Figs. 3 and 4, respectively) that combines all features in a single dataset, and it applies feature removal techniques to retain the most relevant features from each software tool while reducing dimensionality and correlation.

Based on these results, it is important to evaluate whether the choice of voting method affects the final decision. Table 3 reports the agreement percentage between the three voting methods. It can be observed that, using the obtained weights, there is no difference between the unweighted method and the normalization method. Differences arise only with the softmax method, and only in multiclass problems. These differences occur when two software tools (3D Slicer and TriDFusion) agree on a class, while the other two (MaZda and LIFEx) vote for different classes. For example, if 3D Slicer and TriDFusion vote for class 0, MaZda votes for class 1, and LIFEx votes for class 2, the unweighted or normalization methods would select class 0 as the final decision. This behaviour occurs because the softmax transformation emphasizes extreme weights, which can change the outcome in cases of conflicting votes.

Feature analysis

This section aims to obtain the radiomics features obtained by the each software tool and the importance of them in the machine learning pipeline. As we have described along this research article, radiomics features can be classified as shape, first-order (intesity), texture or transform-based. Table 9 summarizes the number of features per category that have been extracted by each software tool for 2D and 3D images. According to this analysis, LIFEx and MaZda extract more texture features than any other category in both 2D and 3D images. In contrast, 3D Slicer and TriDFusion obtain most of the features generally based on transformations in both 2D and 3D images. Considering LIFEx, 3D Slicer and TriDFusion, the differences in the shape features are based on adding the Z dimension of the 3D images. Focusing on LIFEx, there are some first-order features that in 2D images cannot be extracted and missing values are returned. In TriDFusion, the big difference in the transform-based features is due to the wavelet transforms, as 2D images were forced to extract 2D features. In MaZda, the feature set is quite different in 3D, and the only shape feature is the ROI volume. Additionally, the number of texture calculations is reduced, and wavelet transformations are eliminated.

Some similarity in terms of common features by different software tools also appear and it is key to analyse it. In 2D images, all the tools get the area and the X and Y coordinates of the centre of the ROI together with the volume of the ROI for 3D images. Considering 3D Slicer and TriDFusion, both based on the same library (PyRadiomics), they get the same features except for transform-based features, where TriDFusion extracts more features than 3D Slicer. At this point, it is key to determine the most important features (and their types) when building a classification model. To this end, histograms were generated for each software tool, displaying the most frequently selected features. Due to space restrictions, only the top-10 most frequent features are shown.

For LIFEx (Fig. 8(a)), the most frequently selected features are primarily of shape or texture type. Particularly noteworthy are the coarseness of the neighbouring gray-tone difference matrix, reflecting texture uniformity, and compactness2, indicating shape regularity, with higher values corresponding to more spherical lesions. Several shape features that capture the position of the ROI centre are also consistently selected.

Most frequently selected features in LIFEx and MaZda

For MaZda (Fig. 8(b)), the most frequent features are predominantly related to the geometry of the ROI, although several texture features are also among the most common. The most relevant feature is GeoW15, which quantifies how efficiently the object fills its bounding ellipse defined by its Feret diameters; it is calculated as the ratio of four times the object’s area to the product of π, the minimal Feret diameter, and the maximal Feret diameter. Other notable features include GeoRf, which is similar to elongation, and GeoW2, which measures similarity to a perfect circle.

Focusing on 3D Slicer (Fig. 9(a)), no first-order or texture features appear among the most frequently selected. Instead, shape descriptors dominate. In particular, original_shape_Maximum2DDiameterRow stands out, measuring the maximum lesion extent along the horizontal (row) axis in a 2D slice, with higher values associated with more elongated or larger lesions. Other commonly selected shape features include elongation and sphericity.

Most frequently selected features in 3D Slicer and TriDFusion

For TriDFusion (Fig. 9(b)), the results follow a similar pattern: shape features are again the most frequently selected, largely overlapping with those identified by 3D Slicer. However, differences arise in the transform-based features, as TriDFusion applies a broader range of image filters, leading to a distinct set of selected texture descriptors.

It is notable that the most informative features across tools are predominantly shape-based. In general, these descriptors quantify whether a lesion is round and compact versus elongated or irregular, which is an observation consistent with established radiological principles. In oncologic imaging, lesion morphology is a well-known surrogate of malignancy. This is particularly evident in breast cancer, where spiculated, ill-defined, or irregular masses are strongly associated with malignant behaviour, whereas round or oval lesions are more often benign. For instance, invasive carcinomas tend to exhibit irregular or spiculated contours with heterogeneous internal structure, whereas calcifications are frequently highly regular, well-circumscribed, and homogeneous. Texture descriptors, such as coarseness, which reflects the uniformity of local grey-level variation, help quantify this internal consistency.

Regarding transform-based features, 3D Slicer primarily relies on wavelet-derived features, whereas TriDFusion incorporates a broader spectrum of filter families (e.g., square, exponential), yielding a more diverse set of high-level texture representations. This contributes to the differences observed in the most frequently selected transform-based features between both tools.

Finally, it is noteworthy that, in all software tools, at least one coordinate-related descriptor (e.g., centroid X or Y) appears among the most frequently selected features. Although this may seem counter-intuitive at first, it aligns with clinical reasoning: some tumour types demonstrate location-dependent prevalence patterns. For instance, in breast imaging, tumours arise more frequently in specific quadrants, especially the upper-outer quadrant. Thus, spatial location may indirectly contribute clinically relevant information and become predictive within a radiomics-based model.

Feature selection techniques

This first study aims to determine the best feature selection technique for radiomics. The list of feature selection techniques was described in Sect. 3.4, including widely used techniques in the radiomics literature (LASSO [52], ANOVA [53], and mRMR [54]) with multiple values. This analysis also includes the option of not using any feature selection technique. Every combination (feature selection technique and parameter value) was compared considering all the datasets (5-fold nested cross validation for each dataset), all the methodologies/software tools considered in this research work (3D Slicer, LIFEx, MaZda, TriDFusion, Democracy and Fusion), and all the machine learning algorithms (LR, DT, RF, KNN, XGBoost, MLP, NB and SVM), except the SVM results on the A-ACC dataset. More than 2,000 combinations were considered for this study (the results are available at http://www.uco.es/kdis/efrst-amecc/). The Friedman test applied to this set of results detected that there were general statistical differences between the features selection options at a significance level of α = 0.05, rejecting the null hypothesis with a p-value of for F1-score, for AUROC and for AUPRC. The Nemenyi test yielded, at a significance level of α = 0.05, a critical difference value of 0.6183 on F1-score and 0.6222 for AUROC and AUPRC (see Fig. 10(a), 10(b), and 10(c)). As shown, mRMR with 40 features appears as the best feature selection technique, showing statistical differences with LASSO and ANOVA. Furthermore, it can be observed that the greater the number of characteristics, the higher the position in the ranking.

Nemenyi’s on feature selection techniques comparison

To compare the computational complexity of the feature-selection pipelines, we measured their preprocessing time. Due to space constraints, only configurations extracting 40 features and the no–feature-selection baseline are reported (see Table 11). Classification models

This second study aims to determine the best machine learning technique for radiomics. The list of classification algorithms was described in Sect. 3.4, including widely used methods in the radiomics literature (LR, DT, RF, KNN, XGBoost, MLP, NB and SVM). All these algorithms have been tested on radiomics, showing promising results [27–34], so it is fair to choose all of them for this analysis. All these algorithms were compared considering all the datasetsexcept A-ACC, to avoid misleading conclusion due to SVMs (5-fold nested cross validation for each dataset), all the methodologies/software tools considered in this research work (3D Slicer, LIFEx, MaZda, TriDFusion, Democracy and Fusion), and all feature selection combinations and values presented in the previous subsection (LASSO, ANOVA, mRMR, and none). Around 3,000 combinations were considered for this study (the results are available at http://www.uco.es/kdis/efrst-amecc/). The Friedman test applied to this set of results detected general statistical differences between the classification models used. The Friedman test detected that there were general statistical differences in the eight classification algorithms at a significance level of α = 0.05, rejecting the null hypothesis with a p-value for F1-score, and values below for AUROC and AUPRC. The Nemenyi post-hoc test was then performed (see Fig. 11(a), 11(b) and 11(c)), obtaining that, at a significance level of α = 0.05, the best performing models are LR and XGBoost for F1, RF and LR for AUROC, and RF with statistical difference among the others for AUPRC.

Confusion matrix analysis and class imbalance

To better understand model behaviour beyond global accuracy, the confusion matrices of the best results from Table 10 are shown on Fig. 12. Overall, most models demonstrated acceptable discrimination between positive and negative outcomes, as it can be noticed in CMMD-CC, CMMD-MLO or USG-B. However, several datasets exhibited substantial class imbalance, which strongly influenced predictive performance.

Confusion matrixes from results of Table 10

The DMID and NSCLC-stages datasets showed pronounced skew toward a dominant class. In DMID, the model achieved strong recognition of the majority class, with only minor confusion toward the minority class. In NSCLC-stages, the classifier showed markedly higher sensitivity to the predominant class, whereas minority-class samples were frequently misassigned, indicating limited generalization to underrepresented stages.

Multiclass tasks accentuated imbalance effects. In NSCLC-histo, class 1 (squamous cell carcinomas, 152 samples) was consistently identified, while class 2 (adenocarcinoma, 51 samples) suffered from extremely low recall and substantial confusion with the other classes. This suggests that the model may be learning predominantly from the dominant class distribution without sufficiently capturing discriminatory features of the imbalanced class. Similarly, USG-H, a four-class problem, exhibited highly uneven class representation. The model concentrated predictions on the most frequent class (invasive carcinoma, 87 samples), with minimal correct assignments for the rarest class (carcinoma in situ, 11 samples), underscoring the difficulty of achieving balanced recognition in sparse label conditions. In addition, class similarity further compounds these challenges. Lesions from different classes may exhibit highly similar textural and shape characteristics, making it difficult for models to discriminate them based only on radiomic features.

These findings demonstrate that predictive performance is closely tied to label distribution, with minority classes particularly vulnerable to misclassification. In strongly imbalanced datasets, models tend to bias toward majority classes.

Notably, the confusion matrices of models optimized for F1 (see Fig. 12(a)) generally display a clearer concentration of correct predictions along the main diagonal compared with those selected based on AUROC or AUPRC (see Fig. 12(b) and 12(c)). This is expected because F1 emphasizes precision and recall under a fixed decision threshold, whereas AUROC and AUPRC capture ranking quality and may not translate into more reliable thresholded predictions.

Discussion

The experimental results demonstrate that MaZda consistently outperforms 3D Slicer and the other evaluated tools, achieving statistically significant gains in F1-score, AUROC, and AUPRC. A plausible explanation for this superior performance is that MaZda does not adhere to the IBSI standard, which may allow it to exploit its own formulations of texture and shape descriptors that better capture subtle lesion differences. This represents a double-edged sword: IBSI compliance promotes reproducibility and cross-study comparability, but it may restrict the descriptive richness of the features and thereby reduce predictive performance. In contrast, MaZda provides a broader set of shape and texture features, which likely increases the probability of capturing highly discriminative patterns, contributing to its improved classification results. Interestingly, despite TriDFusion offering an even larger feature space through additional transformation filters, results degraded compared with 3D Slicer, likely because the enlarged feature set introduces redundancy and noise that hinder generalization rather than helping it.

The combination of multiple radiomic software tools through information fusion methods was shown to further enhance predictive performance. Among these, the Democracy method achieved the best results, indicating that ensemble approaches leveraging complementary feature sets from different tools are superior to relying on a single software. The ablation study confirmed that MaZda is the component that contributes most strongly to the performance of the Democracy model. This aligns with the feature analysis, where MaZda features, particularly those based on shape, appear with high frequency among the top-ranked features. These findings reinforce that MaZda captures discriminative morphological and textural properties that are not fully recovered by the other tools, and therefore provide complementary value rather than redundant information.

The results have demonstrated that some pipelines are more appropriate than others. Regarding feature selection, mRMR achieved the best performance with statistically significant differences over ANOVA and LASSO. Although no significant differences were observed between configurations using 20 (only on F1), 30, or 40 selected features, the ranking shows a consistent trend in which methods score higher when more features are retained. In particular, mRMR with 40 features consistently ranked first. This can be explained because the combination of mRMR and a larger feature budget ensures that models are trained on descriptors that are both discriminative and minimally redundant, while reducing the risk of discarding potentially relevant information. As for classification algorithms, LR, XGBoost and RF have produced the best average results. These algorithms combine robustness to overfitting with the ability to capture relevant patterns in high-dimensional radiomic feature spaces. LR provides a stable baseline with interpretable coefficients, while RF and XGBoost leverage ensemble strategies to model complex non-linear relationships and interactions between features.

The observed variability between radiomics software tools highlights that different implementations capture complementary aspects of lesion morphology and texture. Radiologists and researchers should be aware that absolute values of radiomic features may differ across tools, and therefore clinical decisions or research conclusions should not rely on a single software output in isolation. Ensemble or hybrid approaches, which combine features from multiple tools, provide a more robust basis for decision-making, reducing the impact of tool-specific biases and enhancing generalizability.

Regarding the potential influence of software versions on the reported results, we note that for 3D Slicer and TriDFusion, as feature extraction is based on PyRadiomics, the features definitions remained stable across releases. MaZda is no longer under active development, so no version drift is expected. In contrast, LIFEx recently underwent a major update (from version 7 to version 25), which introduced changes to its feature library, adding a few non-IBSI-compliant features. However, none of these new features were retained as relevant in our experiments, indicating that versions do not affect the conclusions presented. Nevertheless, we acknowledge that future software revisions or updates to the IBSI guidelines may potentially influence radiomics feature values, and ongoing validation will be essential to ensure reproducibility.

Open challenges

Building upon the findings of this study, several avenues for future research can be envisioned. First, MaZda tends to extract features that capture similar properties to those provided by PyRadiomics, yet it consistently yields higher predictive performance. A detailed investigation into the underlying calculations, particularly given the absence of IBSI standardization, is necessary to understand the source of this advantage. Once the relevant MaZda features are thoroughly analysed, it would be valuable to integrate them with features from PyRadiomics and LIFEx into a unified extraction pipeline. This approach could significantly reduce computational overhead by avoiding separate extractions for each tool while maintaining the benefits of democratic and fusion-based models.

Cross-dataset transfer could not be applied to ensure the validity of our results because radiomic descriptors are highly sensitive to acquisition-dependent variability. When images are obtained using different scanners, reconstruction kernels, voxel spacing, or intensity scales, feature distributions shift substantially, often leading to severe performance degradation and unreliable conclusions. Therefore, a transfer experiment would not be methodologically meaningful unless strong harmonisation and acquisition standardisation are guaranteed, even when both correspond to the same modality and anatomical site. The only potentially compatible pair within our collection is CMMD-CC and CMMD-MLO, but these datasets contain lesions from the same patients acquired under different mammographic projections. Using them in cross-dataset transfer would introduce data leakage, preventing a valid evaluation. For this purpose, investigating harmonization strategies (e.g., ComBat, voxel resampling, intensity normalization) may enable model generalization across different scanners, imaging protocols, or institutions.

We acknowledge that the approach used to select the number of features for the feature selection methods is not optimal. In principle, each dataset could have a distinct subset of radiomic features that maximises performance. Identifying this optimal subset, however, would require an exhaustive and computationally intensive search across all possible feature combinations (), which is not feasible in practice. To overcome this limitation, future work could explore metaheuristic optimization techniques, such as genetic algorithms, to identify near-optimal feature subsets without resorting to exhaustive search, balancing performance and computational feasibility.

Conclusions

Radiomics libraries/tools have been widely considered for cancer detection tasks using machine learning approaches in recent years. Despite the growing interest in radiomics applications, a significant challenge persists in standardization and reproducibility. Each radiomics software tool operates differently and generates distinct feature sets due to variations in transformations, calculations, and configurations. This lack of standardization leads to discrepancies in the computation of features across different implementations. These variations encompass diverse feature categories and employ dissimilar terminology, parameter configurations, and image processing techniques, yielding disparate feature values. Specialists often need clarification when selecting a radiomics software tool for their studies and determining the most suitable machine learning models and data pre-processing approaches for radiomics.

This paper aimed to alleviate the arduous process radiologists face when selecting the most suitable tool and machine learning pipeline for cancer classification. A comprehensive comparative analysis of various radiomics software tools, and information fusion methods, across different cancer types have performed. This experimental stage has demonstrated a combination of radiomics software tools produce really good results in F1, AUROC, and AUPRC, outperforming the use of radiomics software tools in isolation. Additionally, the experiments have demonstrated that it is hard to find the best machine learning pipeline, and it depends on the dataset and the software tools/methods to be considered.

Overall, these findings provide practical guidance for radiologists and researchers when selecting radiomics software and machine-learning pipelines. Importantly, hybrid approaches that integrate multiple feature sources can maximize predictive performance and robustness across heterogeneous datasets, supporting the development of more reliable and generalizable radiomics workflows.
