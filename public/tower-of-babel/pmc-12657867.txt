FDA-authorized software as a medical device in mental health: a perspective on evidence, device lineage, and regulatory challenges
Authors: Julian Herpertz, Ariel D Stern, Nils Opel, Ulrich Reininghaus, John Torous
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractFDA approval is widely regarded as a benchmark of quality for medical devices. However, concerns persist regarding its regulatory framework for digital mental health devices. This perspective article examined FDA-authorized Software as a Medical Device (SaMD) in mental health, tracing the devices’ regulatory lineage through the De Novo and 510(k)-clearance pathways while assessing the quality of evidence that led to their authorization. Many 510(k)-cleared devices lacked direct evidence of effectiveness, relying solely on equivalence to predicate devices. Furthermore, we identified four FDA-authorized SaMD whose pivotal randomized controlled trials tested prototypes delivered on different digital platforms than those of the final marketed products. Strengthening regulatory standards requires randomized controlled trials evaluating the final marketed product on its intended platform and the use of context-appropriate control conditions. Sham placebo controls may be considered feasible; however, evidence supporting fully inert and fully blinding sham controls for digital interventions remains limited at present. This should occur alongside consistent application of the FDA’s discretionary authority to require new 510(k) submissions when substantial product changes occur.Subject terms: Human behaviour, Technology

Introduction

Digital mental health interventions are rapidly growing in popularity and accessibility. With over 10,000 mental health apps available on the Apple App Store and Google Play Store1, and more than 6.5 billion people owning smartphones2, these technologies hold significant potential to bridge the gap between the onset of a mental health disorder and access to professional care3. Moreover, in clinical trials related to the development of digital therapies, mental health represents the largest area of R&D in recent years4. However, the quality of mental health apps varies greatly. Only a small number have demonstrated effectiveness through evidence from randomized controlled trials5, while others may even pose potential risks to users6. Many digital mental health applications are marketed as wellness products without making explicit therapeutic claims, which allows them to avoid FDA oversight that applies to software intended to diagnose or treat medical conditions7,8. Those apps that seek regulatory approval are classified as software as a medical device (SaMD9) and constitute the focus of this study. Given the rapidly increasing number of wellness-oriented mental health apps of widely varying quality, and the growing reliance on FDA-authorized SaMD, it is particularly important at this time to critically evaluate whether current regulatory processes sufficiently ensure consistent product quality, safety, and effectiveness.

The U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) plays a vital role in regulating the marketing of medical devices and ensuring the monitoring of safety signals in the United States10. Mobile medical apps that wish to market themselves as SaMD must obtain FDA authorization. The FDA categorizes all medical devices, including SaMD, seeking regulatory authorization (i.e., “approval” for high-risk devices or “clearance” for moderate-risk devices) into three classes based on their perceived risk to patients: Class I (low risk), Class II (moderate risk), and Class III (high risk). Digital mental health devices typically fall under the Class II category11, which are subject to special controls, requiring clinical data to ensure their safety and effectiveness12. After a SaMD has been evaluated in a clinical study (e.g., a randomized controlled trial), the data and formal requests for authorization are submitted through either the so-called De Novo pathway13 or the 510(k)-clearance pathway (510(k))14. While the De Novo pathway is used to classify a novel device whose safety and effectiveness must be established (classification)15, the 510(k) pathway requires demonstrating that new devices are similar to devices initially classified through the De Novo or the 510(k) pathway (clearance)16,17.

The regulatory process for 510(k) clearances allows new entrants to gain regulatory clearance without providing direct evidence of their product’s effectiveness and safety18. Instead, manufacturers are only required to demonstrate “substantial equivalence” to an already-cleared product, referred to as a predicate19. It is therefore valuable to trace newly-cleared devices back to their predecessors to assess whether the original studies provided convincing and reliable evidence.

The FDA maintains separate databases for products regulated by the De Novo and 510(k)-clearance pathway. To date, no comprehensive list of FDA-regulated SaMD for the treatment of psychiatric disorders has been published. In this perspective article, we analyze these databases to identify all FDA-regulated SaMD in mental health and trace each 510(k)-cleared product back to its corresponding device classified through the De Novo pathway. Additionally, we investigate the studies that supported the clearance of these devices, assessing and comparing the quality of the evidence presented. Our goal is to enhance understanding of the FDA’s regulatory process and evaluate whether the authorized devices are backed by strong evidence of their effectiveness and safety.

Methods

Identification of FDA-regulated digital mental health devices

The first and last authors systematically searched the De Novo database to identify all initial predicates among digital mental health devices. Subsequently, we examined the 510(k) database to trace the subsequent derivatives of app devices linked to these predicates. Neither database features a dedicated decision panel for innovations in the field of behavioral or psychiatric disorders. Instead, devices for psychiatric disorders are categorized under neurological devices11. Since 1998 (when the 510(k) database begins) and as of the date of writing this manuscript, 42 devices were added to the neurology panel in the FDA’s De Novo database20. Here, we identified the app reSET®, developed by Pear Therapeutic, as the first computerized therapy device in psychiatry that was classified by the FDA in September 201721. Since 2017, 1,478 medical devices were added to the 510(k) database under the neurology panel. Between January 6, 2025, and January 10, 2025, we reviewed each of these entries to identify all SaMD in mental health that received clearance by the FDA. As the De Novo pathway was established in 199720, we could not identify predecessor devices for those with predecessors dating back earlier than this year.

Our analysis was not limited to products operating on Android or iOS platforms; instead, we aimed to provide a comprehensive overview of SaMD by also including those designed for tablet-like devices and virtual reality glasses. Our inclusion criteria focused on devices intended primarily for standalone mental health conditions and delivered through interfaces. Devices targeting mental health symptoms as secondary to somatic diseases, as well as those relying predominantly on wearable-only platforms, were excluded to ensure a consistent focus on screen-based SaMD for primary mental health indications. Devices utilizing neurostimulation were excluded from this study. We believe our screening captures the complete set of FDA-authorized SaMD that meet these inclusion criteria at the time of our systematic database search.

The FDA publishes decision summaries for all authorized devices, including information on the predicate devices referenced by 510(k)-cleared products. By analyzing these relationships, we constructed lineages of mental health devices.

Evaluating studies that led to FDA authorization

We reviewed the individual studies submitted to the FDA for the classification and clearance of each SaMD, comparing their quality by examining the following criteria: type of conducted study, the treatment period with the device, the number of participants, the platforms that devices run on and those used for the studies, primary outcome measures, the presence and nature of any comparator or control condition and the study outcomes. This analysis focused solely on interventional devices, excluding mental health assessment devices from further analysis.

Results

Identification of FDA-regulated digital mental health devices

We identified five SaMD that met inclusion criteria in the FDA’s De Novo database. The earliest classified device in the field of mental health was reSET® by Pear Therapeutics, which was granted clearance for marketing in 2017. Since then, 14 SaMD in mental health were able to present substantial equivalence to another U.S. marketed device and received 510(k)-clearance (Fig. 1).

The identified SaMD can be classified into six categories based on their FDA regulation names: Computer-Based Cognitive Behavioral Therapy Devices (CBT devices), Virtual Reality Behavioral Therapy Devices (VR devices) for Pain Relief, Digital Therapeutic Software for ADHD, Biofeedback Devices, Pediatric Autism Spectrum Disorder Diagnosis Aids, and Attention Task Performance Recorders (Fig. 2). CBT devices are prescription-based apps that have been authorized for a variety of mental and behavioral health disorders, including depression, substance abuse, insomnia, and anxiety (Table 1). VR devices leverage virtual reality technology to deliver CBT for managing lower back pain. Digital therapeutic software for ADHD is implemented through video game experiences that incorporate reward cycles. Biofeedback devices guide users through breathing exercises designed to reduce stress. Lastly, Pediatric Autism Spectrum Disorder Diagnosis Aids use eye-tracking software to aid in the diagnosis of autism spectrum disorder, while Attention Task Performance Recorders support the assessment of attention deficits, including ADHD. Biofeedback devices and Attention Task Performance Recorders have predicate devices that received 510(k)-clearance in 1986 and 2000 respectively. As the De Novo pathway was introduced in 1997, we could not identify the predecessor device classified through the De Novo pathway. Evaluating studies that led to FDA authorization

All SaMD regulated by the De Novo pathway conducted randomized controlled trials to obtain FDA clearance. For example, reSET® offers web and app-based support for patients with substance use disorder. The clinical trial that led to its classification evaluated 12-week use as an adjunct to standard outpatient therapy in 255 participants, compared to 252 receiving treatment as usual, demonstrating improved treatment retention and increased abstinence rates. However, it did not evaluate the app product that was later commercialized and marketed to clinicians, but instead focused on a desktop-based version with equivalent content, which the majority of study participants accessed at the treating clinic21. Additionally, patients in the intervention arm received financial incentives, while the control group did not21. Participants in the reSET arm could earn vouchers worth up to $100, based on negative drug screens and module completion (Table 1). Akili Interactive Labs’ Endeavor® is another SaMD marketed as a treatment aid for ADHD22. It underwent a multicenter, double-blind randomized controlled trial to obtain classification through the De Novo pathway in 2020. The study used a sham digital control condition that mimicked digital reward and engagement mechanisms, showing that Endeavor significantly outperformed the control in improving in Test of Variables of Attention (T.O.V.A)- and Attention Performance Index scores in children aged eight to twelve with a diagnosis of ADHD. EaseVRx®, developed by AppliedVR, Inc., is a virtual reality-based solution designed to alleviate lower back pain23. The trial that led to its approval compared EaseVRx® to a sham digital control condition consisting of 2-D nature imagery designed to match the time, device use, and sensory engagement of the intervention while omitting the targeted pain management components. Over the 8-week treatment period, participants assigned to EaseVRx® reported greater reductions in self-reported pain than those in the sham control condition.

Additional randomized controlled trials were conducted for CBT devices to demonstrate substantial equivalence to previous devices and obtain 510(k)-clearance. reSET-O®24, Somryst®25 and Rejoyn®26 were investigated to establish substantial equivalence to reSET®. reSET-O® was cleared to aid in the treatment of opioid use disorder. Yet the trial leading to reSET-O’s clearance failed to show superiority over treatment as usual in improving abstinence from opioids and cocaine, demonstrating superiority only in retention (time to dropout). As in the trial that led to reSET’s classification, a desktop-based version was investigated, instead of the later commercialized app. Rejoyn® is an app designed for the treatment of depression. In its clinical trial, it outperformed a sham digital control condition only when analyzing all participants recruited for the study (i.e., based on an intention-to-treat analysis), but not when focusing on those who actively participated (modified intention-to-treat analysis; Table 1)27. The sham control consisted of a shapes memory task matched to Rejoyn for time and attention, designed to control for general engagement and expectancy effects while excluding therapeutic content.

There are FDA-cleared devices whose clinical trials did not reveal significant deficiencies in study design or evidence of efficacy. Somryst® targets insomnia and was investigated in combination with treatment as usual and compared to a lifestyle web program as the control. The web program was designed as an attention control, providing general health information and weekly surveys to match time and interaction levels with the treatment group. It was shown to result in significantly greater improvements in the Insomnia Severity Index (ISI) than the control. Like Somryst®, Sleepio® was developed to assist in the treatment of insomnia and was proven to be superior to online sleep education28. The control was designed to match time, attention, and expectancy of benefit while excluding active cognitive behavioral therapy components. Big Health’s Daylight® targets anxiety and its use resulted in a significant effect on self-reported improvements in generalized anxiety disorder compared to online psychoeducation29. The psychoeducation was designed to match Daylight on key non-specific factors including human contact, self-pacing, immediate access to content, and participants’ perceptions of credibility and expected benefit. Lastly, MamaLift Plus® provides support for postpartum depression and was shown to be more effective in alleviating symptoms when compared to treatment as usual with a sham digital control condition that was similar in format and appearance to MamaLift but excluded cognitive behavioral therapy content30.

Discussion

We analyzed the FDA’s De Novo and 510(k) databases to collect and evaluate the evidence and lineage of FDA-regulated digital mental health devices. The result of this exercise was the generation of a novel, comprehensive list of FDA-regulated digital mental health devices, combined with comprehensive data associated with the type of clinical evidence that was submitted to the FDA in support of their regulatory authorization.

Our analysis indicates that several products cleared through the 510(k) process exhibit limitations, including pivotal trials that fail to demonstrate superiority over selected comparator conditions, evaluations conducted using prototype devices that differ from the final marketed products, and instances in which devices diverge markedly from their predicate devices by targeting entirely different clinical indications. We identified cases where devices were cleared despite failing to demonstrate superiority over their comparator conditions; specifically, reSET-O did not show superiority over treatment as usual on its primary endpoint, and Rejoyn failed to demonstrate superiority over its sham digital control condition when analyses were limited to participants who completed the intervention, rather than the intention-to-treat population. Additionally, some trials for devices regulated by the De Novo pathway used treatment as usual as the comparator, rather than a control condition designed to match the digital intervention in format or engagement. Even when sham digital control conditions are used, they often differ from the investigated device in visual presentation, functionality, or user experience, which may limit their ability to fully control for expectancy and engagement effects. For example, websites with educational content frequently lack the interactive features of the tested app or device, potentially influencing user engagement and perceived efficacy31. These challenges highlight the unique nature of digital therapeutics compared to pharmacological treatments: because digital interventions rely on user interaction, design features, and engagement, developing sham placebo control conditions that are both credible and therapeutically inert remains difficult, and full blinding is rarely achievable in practice. While the standard of care may serve as a comparator in some contexts, sham digital control conditions that match the intervention in format and engagement can be informative when feasible. However, designing such controls remains challenging, and their ability to isolate specific treatment effects is not yet well-established.

Some studies seeking FDA regulation investigated prototypes rather than the final commercialized product. For example, both reSET and reSET-O evaluated web-based prototypes instead of the mobile apps brought to market. Recent evidence suggests that modifying the design or delivery platform of an intervention can significantly affect how users engage with and respond to its content, which may in turn influence its effectiveness32. Therefore, we believe that the platform evaluated for efficacy and safety in clinical trials should match the version later offered to patients.

All CBT devices demonstrated equivalence to either the De Novo classified reSET® or the 510(k) cleared Somryst® through randomized controlled trials. Against this backdrop, it is noteworthy that reSET® itself was not utilized in the study leading to its clearance; instead, a desktop-based module with similar content was used21. Additionally, contingency management incentives were handled differently between the intervention and control groups: participants in the reSET arm could earn vouchers worth up to $100 based on negative drug screens and module completion, whereas participants in the control arm did not receive any financial incentives21. Providing financial compensation exclusively to the intervention arm may introduce selection bias (e.g., attracting participants with lower income), differential engagement, and attrition bias, potentially inflating efficacy estimates33,34. Because the intervention modules were delivered on a computer in the clinic rather than via a smartphone app, and only the intervention arm received compensation while the control arm did not, reSET®’s successor, reSET-O®, faced criticism from the Institute for Clinical and Economic Review (ICER). ICER assigned reSET-O a grade of “C + ,”35 indicating that the device provides only a “comparable or small net health benefit“36. After receiving 510(k) clearance in March 2024, Rejoyn® became the first FDA-cleared smartphone application for depression. While the authorization introduces innovation in the treatment of depression37, Rejoyn®’s clinical trial results have been met with similar skepticism: no significant difference was found between Rejoyn® and the sham digital control used in the trial when analyzing participants who completed at least one assessment on either app (modified intention to treat). Results indicated that the sham control produced a similar improvement in symptoms38.

While EndeavorRx® in the “Digital Therapeutic Software for ADHD” category has been shown to improve attention in children who have been diagnosed with ADHD, it has yet to demonstrate measurable improvements in academic performance39, arguably a far more relevant endpoint for individuals with ADHD and their families. Akili’s game received FDA clearance based on participants’ improvements in the research-focused metric TOVA, rather than clinical improvements in ADHD symptoms.

The study design that leads to FDA classification or clearance affects not only consumers’ trust and adoption of these devices, but also the perceptions and decisions of healthcare stakeholders. Early last year, United Healthcare announced that its pharmacy benefit manager, OptumRx, would deny coverage for all computer-based treatments for CBT devices40. In the statement, it noted that their approach “is considered unproven until additional studies are available“ and that applications are “not medically necessary.” Such decisions are highly regrettable. With product costs reaching up to $100035, it is unlikely that users will cover the expense out of pocket, limiting accessibility of many digital tools primarily to privileged populations. Insurance companies are not the first to express skepticism toward the FDA’s regulatory framework. Safety failures in the fields of trauma surgery and gynecology have sparked criticism41,42. Key concerns cited include inadequate requirements for premarket clinical testing, limited postmarket surveillance, and a lack of regulatory transparency17. Safety issues in the field of digital mental health are more subtle, as negative effects are often difficult to trace directly to a mental health device. However, devices without proven effectiveness pose a clear safety risk by potentially delaying access to more effective treatments such as psychotherapy or psychopharmaceutical treatment16,43. Therefore, to expand the range of evidence-based options for managing mental health, a more robust framework for FDA regulation of SaMD in the mental health field is needed16,44.

The FDA acknowledges that its current regulatory framework for medical devices is not well-suited for SaMD, primarily due to the rapid pace of innovation in this field. The Software Precertification Program was introduced to regulate these technologies more individually—but only in pilot form45. The program ultimately could not progress due to the lack of legislative mandate. The FDA has concluded that legislative changes are necessary to regulate software as a medical device more effectively46. Indeed, countries such as Germany that have successfully introduced new regulatory frameworks for digital health interventions have done so through formal policymaking47,48. While the FDA currently possesses discretionary authority to require new 510(k) submissions when substantial changes are made to a device’s design, intended use, or technological characteristics49, this authority could be leveraged more proactively. In particular, when a device’s indication changes to target a different disorder, we believe the FDA should require new randomized controlled trials, since a new clinical indication can introduce distinct safety concerns and may impact the device’s effectiveness. Building on these considerations, and informed by both U.S. regulatory challenges and international best practices, we propose five legislative changes to strengthen the oversight of digital mental health devices:

The “substantially equivalent” approach to using the 510(k) pathway is appropriate for minor changes and product updates such as app extensions, however, when devices utilize entirely different software and target different disorders, obtaining 510(k)-clearance should be made more stringent and the De Novo pathway should be used more frequently - in particular when the mechanism of action of a digital therapy has not been studied yet or as such.

Devices that fail to demonstrate evidence of superiority over a sham placebo control or active comparator should not be cleared for marketing by the FDA, regardless of whether they are regulated by the De Novo or 510(k) pathway. The regulatory standard for new medical devices is “safety and effectiveness” and many safe-to-use devices may nevertheless fail to demonstrate meaningful effectiveness.

We believe that these innovations would enhance trust in FDA-regulated digital mental health devices among consumers, companies, and researchers.

We remain optimistic that innovations stemming from legislative changes could inspire companies to pursue more innovative and effective products in the field of digital mental health. Ultimately, such advancements have the potential to enhance trust in both the regulatory process and the devices themselves, creating a virtuous cycle for evidence-based digital mental health product development.
