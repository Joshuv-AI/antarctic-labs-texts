A novel shifted Vieta–Lucas spectral collocation approach for multidimensional generalized Benjamin–Bona–Mahony–Burgers equations
Authors: Ramy M. Hafez, Hany Mostafa Ahmed, Alhanouf Alburaikan, Moodi Abdulrhman Abdullah Alrajeh, Hamiden Abd El-Wahed Khalifa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Here, we put forward a numerical method for solving the (1+1)- and (2+1)-dimensional nonlinear generalized Benjamin–Bona–Mahony–Burgers (GBBMB) equation using a collocation approach based on shifted Vieta–Lucas polynomials (SVLPs). The proposed technique involves expressing the approximate solution as a finite series expansion in terms of SVLPs and enforcing the governing equation at specific collocation points. This process leads to a nonlinear algebraic system amenable to efficient solution. The method offers high accuracy and fast convergence. An error analysis and convergence proof of the proposed algorithm are established, thereby ensuring its theoretical soundness. Several numerical experiments were conducted to validate the effectiveness of the approach, and the results demonstrate its superiority over existing methods in terms of accuracy and simplicity. This makes the proposed SVLP-based collocation method a versatile and stable technique for tackling nonlinear partial differential equations in applied sciences and engineering.

Introduction

Nonlinear partial differential equations (PDEs) are crucial tools for modeling real-world phenomena involving wave propagation, diffusion, convection, and dissipation. In particular, the Benjamin–Bona–Mahony–Burgers (BBMB) equation and its generalized forms are commonly employed to model unidirectional, long waves in nonlinear dispersive media with dissipation effects1–5. The (1+1)- and (2+1)-dimensional forms of GBBMB equation are especially relevant in modeling shallow water waves6,7, fluid flow8, nonlinear acoustics9, and plasma transport10.

In recent years, the investigation of soliton solutions for nonlinear partial differential equations (NLPDEs) has gained significant momentum due to their role in modeling complex physical phenomena. For instance, recent studies have explored new analytical and numerical solutions for the extended (2+1)-dimensional Boussinesq equation using fractional derivative approaches11. Similarly, the dynamical landscape of higher-dimensional models, such as the negative-order (3+1)-dimensional Calogero–Bogoyavlenskii–Schiff equation12 and the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta$$\end{document}-fractional-dimensional generalized KP model13, has been investigated to reveal complex wave structures and bifurcation behaviors. Furthermore, the search for dynamical soliton solutions has extended to the paraxial wave and Fokas wave equations using efficient techniques like the truncated M-fractional derivative14. In the realm of nonlinear optics, researchers have explored optical solitons, modulation instability, and chaotic behavior in the Schrödinger–Hirota equation15, as well as new complex hyperbolic structures for Schrödinger dynamical models16. Within this broader context of evolving nonlinear dynamics, the present study on the (2+1)-dimensional GBBMB equation provides a robust numerical alternative for capturing dispersive-dissipative wave behaviors through a spectral collocation framework.

The GBBMB equation incorporates both nonlinear and dispersive terms alongside viscosity components17–19, making it a rich model for analyzing physical systems exhibiting complex interactions between convection, diffusion, and dispersion. Its generalized version allows for a more realistic representation of physical processes, yet solving it analytically becomes infeasible in most scenarios due to its nonlinearity20 and the presence of mixed high-order derivatives21,22.

To address such challenges, various numerical techniques have been developed over the years. Wang et al.18 proposed a high-order finite difference scheme for solving GBBMB equations efficiently; Fardi et al.17 developed an improved finite difference approach with enhanced stability; Han and Ram23 studied error estimates for high-order schemes, while Han et al.24,25 extended these schemes to multidimensional problems. Galerkin and Petrov-Galerkin finite element methods were explored by Devi et al.26 for higher-order accuracy, Dehghan27 focused on stability and convergence, and Han et al.28 applied these methods to generalized BBMB equations. Spline-based techniques have been applied by Arora et al.29 and Joshi et al.30 to improve smoothness and approximation, while Han et al.31–33 investigated their convergence properties. Meshless methods using radial basis functions were developed by Dehghan34 and Hajiketabi35, showing flexibility in irregular domains. Spectral methods based on orthogonal polynomials were studied by Gheorghiu36 and Han et al.37,38, demonstrating exponential convergence for smooth solutions. Among these, spectral collocation methods have shown superior performance due to their rapid convergence, as reported by Hafez et al.39, Abd Elhameed40, Ahmed41, and Han et al.42–44. Recently, several studies have also focused on developing efficient analytical and semi-analytical techniques for solving nonlinear fractional differential equations. For instance, modified decomposition approaches have been successfully applied to nonlinear fractional models45. In a related direction, semi-analytic frameworks have been employed to treat fractional Fokker–Planck and Fornberg–Whitham equations46. Moreover, numerical and theoretical investigations have been conducted for fractional dynamical systems arising in applied sciences47. Additionally, other semi-analytical treatments for fractional nonlinear evolution equations have demonstrated promising accuracy and efficiency48. Additional, Randomized fractional SEIR frameworks have been utilized for epidemiological forecasting49, while peridynamic differential operators50 and discrete least squares meshless methods51 have been developed for multi-term fractional equations. Furthermore, meshless approaches have been applied to reaction–convection–diffusion models in fuel cell technology52. The investigation of classical integer-order PDEs, such as the GBBMB equation, remains a vital prerequisite for developing these advanced computational frameworks. By establishing a high-precision SVLP-based collocation method for the (1+1)- and (2+1)-dimensional integer-order models, this study provides a validated numerical baseline. This foundation is essential for the future extension of the VLCM to fractional-order dispersive-dissipative systems, where the interplay of nonlinearity and non-locality requires a highly stable underlying spectral basis.

In recent years53–57, approximation methods based on Vieta–Lucas polynomials (VLPs) have emerged as a viable and powerful solution for different types of DEs. Among these, the use of VLPs has shown promise due to their computational efficiency and flexibility in approximating periodic functions over a finite closed interval [a, b]. VLPs possess properties that allow for rapid convergence and high accuracy in representing solutions over specified domains.

Furthermore, unlike the existing Vieta–Lucas spectral methods53–55, the proposed Vieta–Lucas collocation method (VLCM) introduces a novel collocation framework that systematically transforms the (1+1)- and (2+1)-dimensional GBBMB equations into solvable nonlinear algebraic systems. This approach enhances both accuracy and computational efficiency by reducing truncation errors and accelerating convergence. Moreover, VLCM allows flexible handling of boundary and initial conditions, which provides improved stability and robustness compared to previous Vieta–Lucas-based spectral schemes39,40.

This paper proposes a novel numerical collocation algorithm to numerically solve (1+1)-dimensional and (2+1)-dimensional nonlinear GBBMB equations using the SVLPs as basis functions. This approach will be denoted as VLCM, which involves expressing the approximate solution as a finite series expansion in terms of SVLPs, allowing for a systematic transformation of the given GBBMB equations into systems of nonlinear algebraic equations. These systems can be solved efficiently using appropriate numerical techniques, leading to accurate and reliable solutions. To validate the effectiveness of VLCM, we conducted several numerical experiments, demonstrating its superiority over existing methods in terms of accuracy and simplicity. The results indicate that VLCM not only provides high precision but also exhibits rapid convergence, making it a valuable tool for solving nonlinear PDEs in various fields.

It is worth noting that the proposed VLCM not only achieves high numerical accuracy but also preserves key physical characteristics of the GBBMB model, such as wave propagation, dissipation, and nonlinear interactions. This ensures that the computed solutions are consistent with the underlying physical phenomena6,8,9.

Conditioning and numerical stability

The effectiveness of the proposed SVLP-based collocation method depends not only on accuracy but also on the conditioning of the resulting algebraic system and its numerical stability. The Vandermonde-like matrices generated by SVLPs generally exhibit moderate condition numbers, which helps to prevent amplification of round-off errors during computations62,63. Furthermore, the orthogonality of SVLPs ensures well-behaved derivatives and contributes to stable convergence across different spatial resolutions.

For larger values of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\aleph$$\end{document}, the conditioning of the collocation system may deteriorate due to the increase in the polynomial degree, a behavior commonly observed in spectral methods64. However, the use of SVLPs mitigates this effect to a certain extent because of their orthogonality and bounded behavior over the computational domain. In practice, although the condition number increases with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\aleph$$\end{document}, the proposed VLCM remains numerically stable for moderate expansion orders, especially when appropriate numerical techniques such as scaling and stable solvers are employed.

These observations indicate that the proposed method achieves a reliable balance between accuracy and stability, making it suitable for solving nonlinear GBBMB equations.

Computational complexity and efficiency

The computational cost of the proposed VLCM algorithm mainly arises from two sources: evaluating the shifted Vieta–Lucas polynomial (SVLP) basis functions and solving the resulting nonlinear algebraic system. Evaluating the SVLP expansion over a grid of size \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\aleph + 1$$\end{document} requires approximately \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$O((\aleph + 1)^2)$$\end{document} operations, due to the pairwise calculation of polynomial terms37,65. Solving the transformed system of nonlinear algebraic equations, typically dense, incurs a computational cost of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$O((\aleph + 1)^3)$$\end{document}, which corresponds to standard matrix inversion or iterative solution procedures62,64.

These computational considerations highlight that VLCM achieves a balance between efficiency and accuracy. Remark 3.1

In the discrete system (3.17), the nonlinear term \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\partial _{\zeta }R(\mathbb {Z})$$\end{document} is evaluated directly at the collocation points. While this can theoretically introduce spectral aliasing error64, the factorial decay of the SVLP coefficients for the analytic solutions considered here ensures that such errors remain below the truncation threshold. For problems involving stronger nonlinearities, a dealiasing strategy, such as the 3/2-rule, could be integrated into the VLCM framework to maintain numerical stability.

Discussion of results

The numerical experiments across the three test cases (1D and 2D) demonstrate several key features of the proposed VLCM. First, the method exhibits exponential convergence as the number of collocation points \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\aleph$$\end{document} increases, which is a hallmark of spectral-type methods. Second, the integration of Newton’s scheme allows for the efficient handling of various nonlinearities (quadratic and trigonometric) without compromising stability. As shown in Tables 1, 2 and 3, the VLCM consistently achieves lower error norms compared to classical finite difference and meshless schemes, often by several orders of magnitude. Furthermore, the inclusion of CPU times reveals that the high precision (up to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$10^{-11}$$\end{document}) is obtained within a reasonable computational timeframe, even for (2+1)-dimensional systems. The stability of the iterative process and the lack of failure scenarios underscore the robustness of the chosen SVLPs basis in approximating complex nonlinear wave phenomena.

Conclusions

In this work, a collocation method based on SVLPs was developed for solving the (1+1)- and (2+1)-dimensional nonlinear GBBMB equations. The proposed technique demonstrated high accuracy and rapid convergence without the need for operational matrices. Numerical experiments confirmed the method’s superiority over several classical schemes. Due to its simplicity, flexibility, and accuracy, the approach can be extended to more complex models. Future studies will focus on extending this framework to fractional and coupled systems. Moreover, to handle higher-dimensional nonlinear PDEs without suffering from the curse of dimensionality, the integration of sparse spectral approximations or low-rank tensor decompositions into the VLCM framework will be explored to maintain computational efficiency.
