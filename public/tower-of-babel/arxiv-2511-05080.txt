An Architectural Advantage of The Instruction-Tuned LLM in Containing The Readability-Accuracy Tension in Text Simplification
P. Bilha Githinji, Aikaterini Melliou, Zeming Liang, Lian Zhang, Peiwu Qin

Abstract
The increasing health-seeking behavior and digital consumption of biomedical information by the general public necessitate scalable solutions for automatically adapting complex scientific and technical documents into plain language. Automatic text simplification solutions, including advanced large language models (LLMs), however, continue to face challenges in reliably arbitrating the tension between optimizing readability performance and ensuring preservation of discourse fidelity. This report empirically assesses two major classes of general-purpose LLMs, demonstrating how they navigate the readability-accuracy tension compared to a human benchmark. Using a comparative analysis of the instruction-tuned Mistral-Small 3 24B and the reasoning-augmented Qwen2.5 32B, we identify an architectural advantage in the instruction-tuned LLM. Mistral exhibits a tempered lexical simplification strategy that enhances readability across a suite of metrics while preserving human-level discourse with a BERTScore of 0.91 0.91 . QWen also attains enhanced readability performance and a reasonable BERTScore of 0.89 0.89 , but its operational strategy shows a disconnect in balancing between readability and accuracy. Additionally, a comprehensive correlation analysis of a suite of 21 metrics spanning readability, discourse fidelity, content safety, and underlying distributional measures for mechanistic insights, confirms strong functional redundancies, and informs metric selection and domain adaptation for text simplification.

Background

Adapting scientific or technical text into plain text or lay text, also referred to as simplification, fundamentally involves reducing the use of specialized vocabulary and complex sentence structures, and managing the mapping of esoteric details. It is often framed as a specific instance of machine translation, where the source domain (technical lexicon and syntax) is computationally mapped onto equivalent targets that are suitable for a general audience
10
.
Effective plain-text adaptation requires optimization of not only surface linguistic features but also the strict maintenance of semantic equivalence between the technical source and the simplified output.
It is crucial to distinguish text simplification from lay summarization, where the latter also involves a content distillation step that reduces the length of the original text and necessitates navigating an additional trade-off between conciseness and semantic equivalence
13
.
In this study, we isolate and rigorously assess the core linguistic simplification capacity independent of the content distillation function.
We employ biomedical abstracts as the experimental document class, which are suited for this purpose as they are inherently compressed summaries of larger findings and exhibit concentrated technical jargon, complex sentence structures and high informational density.

Empirical evidence consistently demonstrates a persistent and critical performance trade-off between readability gains and content accuracy, with solutions, including LLM-based methods, often achieving high readability at the cost of factual inaccuracies, semantic drift and undesirable omissions

Results

3.1
Overview of the experimental setup

We execute a quantitative audit that compares two distinct classes of general-purpose LLMs for the linguistic style transfer task of plain-text adaptation of biomedical text. The Mistral-Small 3 24B represents the instruction-tuned class, while Qwen2.5 32B represents the reasoning-augmented LLMs. The models are subjected to a controlled evaluation using a purposively crafted zero-shot prompt that instructs the models on what the simplification process entails, and requires a self-reported rationale for post-hoc audit. Additionally, architectural robustness is investigated by exploring the performance of the models under two generative temperatures,
T=0.2
and
T=0.4
, which are identified by the suffixes
strict
and
flexi

Furthermore, we systematically quantify how the models navigate the tension between optimizing for readability and ensuring discourse fidelity using a suite of 21 metrics and a human benchmark dataset. Our evaluation datasets comprise a benchmarking collection of biomedical abstracts with corresponding human-simplified reference texts, the source documents of the benchmarking dataset as a control set that spans 75 biomedical topics, and a custom dataset of randomly sampled abstracts on the topic of Traditional Chinese Medicine in Oncology.
Subsequently, evaluation involves a statistical comparison of the mean performance of the simplification processes, and an in-depth correlation analysis to map the functional associations and trade-offs between readability and discourse fidelity.
All comparative statistical results are reported at a significance level of
\alpha=0.05

4
Discussion

Overall performance.

The empirical results in this report demonstrate that the evaluated general-purpose LLMs exhibit foundational readiness for automated text simplification of biomedical text.
To elaborate, the evaluated architectural classes demonstrate improved proficiency in simplification compared to previous systems, as indicated by SARI scores of 42.46 (95% CI: 41.86 - 43.05) for Mistral-Small 3 24B and 38.38 (95% CI: 38.28 - 38.47) for Qwen2.5 32B.
This performance exceeds the best-reported baseline of 34 for encoder-decoder models T5 and BART
27
, which are optimised for machine translation tasks. Additionally, Mistral has competitive performance compared to GPT-4.1-mini (with best of five runs SARI score of 43.83), a more recent, proprietary, highly-optimised, general-purpose LLM
8
. We do note, though, as suggested by the regression results, the tactics employed by the LLMs may not reflect the multifaceted process of human experts that considers readability and accuracy jointly.

Furthermore, the models demonstrate statistically superior readability performance while maintaining reasonable discourse fidelity.
The LLMs outperform the human benchmark across four readability indices (SMOG Index, FKGL, ARI, and Flesch reading ease) and attain BERTScore accuracies of
0.91
and
0.89
for Mistral-Small 3 24B and Qwen2.5 32B respectively. Mistral’s BERTScore is equivalent to the human benchmark.
Despite this quantitative majority, the Dale-Chall and Gunning Fog indices indicate statistically inferior results compared to the human benchmark. This observed discrepancy is traced to formulaic differences concerning the inherent definition and weighting of lexical importance. Overall, readability performance and correlation results suggest a mastery of syntactic simplification and that the crux of the problem for the LLMs is lexical control.

Architectural characteristics.

Previous works highlight the ongoing challenge in simultaneously optimizing for readability and discourse fidelity using various domain adaptation strategies, including parameter-altering techniques such as fine-tuning
27
,
6
,
8
,
9

This study demonstrates that with a purposively designed zero-shot prompt, the instruction-tuned Mistral model successfully simplifies the biomedical abstracts, effectively balancing readability and discourse fidelity objectives. The model does so consistently under different temperature configurations and appears to have a conservative lexical substitution/retention strategy.

The comparative investigation into the instruction-tuned Mistral and reasoning-augmented QWen reveals distinct operational choices and a potential architectural advantage in the instruction-tuned LLM class that enables effective navigation of the readability-accuracy trade-off.
The Mistral model attains readability performance while preserving discourse integrity at a level that is statistically comparable to human experts, as per the semantic metric BERTScore and the thematic-oriented LDA-topics score. Results from correlation analysis and the model’s self-reported rationale reveal Mistral’s conservative treatment of lexical simplification, and that this correlates with its higher accuracy scores.
Conversely, while QWen’s BERTScore is numerically reasonable, it is statistically lower than the human benchmark.
QWen appears to engage more in conceptual elaboration that risks discourse fidelity as observed in its self-reported rationale and in the correlation analysis results. This exploration may also explain the observed lower task-completion rates.
For a linguistic-first task such as automated simplification, however, the nature of this exploration could benefit from further investigation given the growing capabilities of LLMs, the increasing inclusion of domain-specific training data, and the conceptually larger lexical search space of LLMs.

Furthermore, while research recommends text expansion (in effect, verbosity) as a strategy for enhancing the readability of complex sentences
28
,
27
, a tactic human experts adopt in the benchmarking dataset, we find that the LLMs appear to primarily employ verbosity as a means for containing discourse integrity and not for the readability objective. The models attain higher readability scores with more concise output compared to human experts.

Implications for practice and further research.

The successful performance of the general-purpose LLMs in this study, with only prompting, suggests a readiness of the models for simplification of complex text without the need for costly parameter-altering customizations whose consistent efficacy is conflicting.
Relatedly, our investigation into the underlying mechanisms suggests that the primary performance obstacle is lexical control. As a result, targeted domain adaptation strategies may benefit from prioritizing integration of specialized lexical support, as opposed to generalized expansion of the domain knowledge.
In addition, the results offer an empirical justification for prioritizing the instruction-tuned Mistral-Small 3 24B for automatic text simplification. Ascertaining the observed architectural advantage of the instruction-tuned class over a wider range of models, however, requires further investigation.

Furthermore, the analysis of metric associations provides empirical heuristics for metric selection. We identify redundancies within readability metrics, noting that four of the six formulas (SMOG index, FKGL, ARI and Flesch reading ease) are strongly correlated with coefficient magnitudes
\geq 0.8
. While the Gunning Fog index also exhibits strong correlations with the other formulas (magnitudes
\geq 0.7
), it may produce a different outcome linked to lexical complexity. The Dale-Chall score, on the other hand, is distinctively different with coefficient magnitudes in the range
[0.4-0.6]
and comes across as more stringent on lexical simplification.

Limitations.

While we systematically assess two architectural classes using capable representatives, further evaluation across a wider range of models is required to unequivocally characterize the architectural advantage of instruction-tuned LLMs for plain-text adaptation.
In addition, despite employing established and transferable plain-text adaptation tactics, which should offer directional evidence or an informed starting point for broader application areas, the study explicitly addresses only one domain.
Methodologically, the inherent limitations of conventional readability formulas must be recognized, and quantitative metrics should be integrated with human validation strategies as recommended by existing research
29
.

5
Materials and Methods

Here, we detail the experimental procedures used to compare the two LLM architectures, the instruction-tuned and reasoning-augmented models, and the various readability-accuracy metrics.

5.1
Data

We draft three data cohorts for the evaluation.
The primary benchmarking dataset is a publicly accessible collection of
750
biomedical abstracts and their matched human-simplified texts
27
.
We refer to the human simplified documents as the benchmark dataset and the process of adaptation by human experts as the human model.
Additionally, the original scientific documents of this corpus constitute a curated control cohort that captures 75 biomedical topics.
We refer to this cohort as the control set.
Lastly, an uncurated custom dataset is derived from a random sample of domain-specific abstracts covering Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) and Oncology, offering generalizability and robustness.
The selection of TCM and Oncology is partly motivated by the density of unique terminology in this subdomain and partly by the translational potential given the growing public interest in the application of TCM in the management of side effects from cancer therapeutics
30
,
31
.

5.2
LLM-based simplification systems

The Mistral-Small 3 24B model represents the instruction-tuned architectures, which are optimized for reliable fidelity in executing specified commands.
The Qwen2.5 32B model embodies reasoning-augmented architectures, designed to leverage complex multi-step problem-solving capabilities.
In addition, we assess for robustness by configuring each LLM under two generative temperature settings, with the thinking that successful incorporation of core linguistic functions for lay simplification should be robust to changes in model parameters. The first configuration (suffix
strict
) sets the temperature to
T=0.2
and is a state of low stochasticity while still allowing for some creative leeway. The second temperature setting (suffix
flexi
for flexible) is
T=0.4
and offers increased variability in model output.
The four resulting LLM simplification processes and the human-expert simplification process define the five measured plain-text adaptation processes in our experiments.

5.3
Simplification process and prompt design

We specify the simplification process to the LLMs via a standardised prompt that is empirically developed to ensure consistent reception of the task across the models.
This prompt defines the task as a direct sentence-by-sentence adaptation of the input text, strictly prohibits summarisation, and provides the source document in one shot.
Additionally, the prompt addresses both linguistic complexity (e.g. jargon replacement, splitting complex sentences) and discourse complexity (e.g. adding explanations, abstracting information).
This articulation of possible transforms is informed by established principles for plain language communication
28
,
27
, aligns with the human-benchmark simplification design, and is relevant for comparability.
The prompt does not specify the type of document being simplified, suggest transforms that are specific to the sub-domain of our custom dataset, or offer any exemplars (is zero-shot).
Furthermore, we incorporate a self-reporting mechanism where LLMs tag their outputs, sentence-by-sentence, with a rationale and labels identifying the applied simplification transform.
This systematic design results in a controlled instrument that allows us to empirically observe and quantify performance and navigation of the two fundamentally opposing objectives of enhanced readability and preservation of factual integrity. The prompt is presented in
Section

A.2
.

5.4
Evaluation metrics

We collate a suite of commonly employed metrics for readability, discourse fidelity and content safety. Additionally, foundational distributional metrics such as average sentence length and proportion of difficult words, inherent in readability formulas, are also tracked.
This enables objective measurement of linguistic accessibility and content integrity as well as fine-grained visibility into potential drivers of metric score variations.
The 21 metrics and their operating properties are summarised in
Section

A.1
.

Readability and coherence metrics.

This functional group of metrics comprises established readability formulas.
The metrics primarily quantify and integrate lexical and syntactic complexity, differing in the non-linear weighting assigned to the two, and in their methodological determination of word difficulty.
Lexical complexity is typically determined by polysyllabic counts, with notable exceptions being the Automated Readability Index (ARI), which relies on character count, and the Dale-Chall formula, which employs a predefined lexicon of 3000 words easily read by a U.S. fourth-grader.
Syntactic complexity is consistently measured as the average number of words per sentence (sentence length).
Standard indices like Dale-Chall score, Gunning Fog Index, Flesch-Kincaid Grade Level (FKGL), SMOG Index and ARI, report performance as an estimated U.S. school grade level and a lower score value indicates improved simplification.
Furthermore, this functional group of metrics includes SARI, a metric specifically formulated for the evaluation of automated text simplification processes. SARI assesses the overall goodness of a simplification system, quantifying the effectiveness of addition, deletion, and retention of terms against a reference human-simplified output.

Content and discourse preservation metrics.

This group of metrics focuses on retention of semantic integrity, completeness and subject matter relevance.
Traditional lexical alignment metrics like ROUGE and SacreBLEU, while not ideal for text simplification due to their reliance on lexical retention, serve as foundational content preservation measures for internal consistency and cross-study comparability.
Robust meaning preservation is evaluated using metrics based on semantic congruence that include BERTScore and document-level semantic similarity using LLM embeddings.
Additionally, we capture thematic consistency via Latent Dirichlet Allocation (LDA) topic modeling and utilize vocabulary matching for mechanistic insights into the handling of specialized jargon.
While LDA topic modeling is not ideal for short documents, abstracts have high information density and reasonable length for meaningful word co-occurrence. Moreover, our evaluation is a comparative analysis.

Content safety metrics.

Although source biomedical abstracts inherently lack toxic content, we recognize that the generative nature of LLMs can inadvertently introduce biases even from benign input.
Consequently, we intentionally incorporate safety checks into the overall evaluation framework and hope to foster continuous monitoring and active engagement necessary for the responsible integration of AI into health systems.
We utilize an established toxicity classifier and note that this is a general-purpose toxicity model and that some aspects of simplification, such as oversimplification and misplaced emphasis, may have unintended health safety consequences, which may be out of scope for the model.

5.5
Evaluation analysis

Performance estimators are calculated as the document-level means for each of the metrics. Welch’s t-test, which robustly accommodates potential heterogeneity in sample sizes and variances, is then utilized for statistical analysis.
Furthermore, pairwise correlation matrices and regression by principal component analysis (PCA) formally investigate the congruence within and across functional groups of the evaluation metrics.
All comparative statistical results are reported at a significance level of
\alpha=0.05
.

6
Conclusion

This study demonstrates that, with only a zero-shot purposive prompt, general-purpose LLMs are capable of plain-text adaptation without domain-specific tuning. The models appear to have mastery over syntactic simplification and the primary hurdle is lexical control.
The instruction-tuned Mistral-Small 3 24Bexhibits superior operational robustness, attains discourse fidelity comparable to human experts, and displays a conservative algorithmic strategy that selectively balances lexical transformation and semantic preservation.
In contrast, the characteristic conceptual expansion behavior of reasoning-augmented Qwen2.5 32B risks semantic degradation but requires further investigation.
Moreover, the within- and cross-functional evaluation of various metrics provides a basis for objective comparison and heuristics for metric selection.
