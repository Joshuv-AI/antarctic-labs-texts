Evaluating the Pattern of Kava (Piper methysticum) Consumption in Australia by Wastewater‐Based Epidemiology
Authors: Lorato Makanye, Emma L Keller, Cobus Gerber, Bradley S Simpson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionKava ( Piper methysticum ) is a plant native to the Pacific Islands, used in the preparation of a beverage consumed during ceremonies. The plant has gained popularity in other countries as a supplement for the treatment of anxiety and insomnia. Nevertheless, products containing P. methysticum were associated with liver damage, which led to the kava ban in several jurisdictions across the world, including Australia. However, in 2019, the Australian government started relaxing import restrictions as part of the kava pilot. It is therefore of interest to establish a baseline of kava consumption on a national population scale, serving as a reference point for understanding the long‐term effect of this policy change.MethodsWastewater analysis of kavalactones and metabolites was employed to establish a baseline measurement of use in Australia. Influent samples were collected from 54 wastewater treatment plants from December 2023 to December 2024. Liquid–liquid extraction was used for sample preparation, followed by liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry analysis.ResultsEight chemical markers were detected in wastewater collected from all participating treatment plants. The highest excreted loads were measured in regional Western Australia and the capital cities of New South Wales and the Northern Territory.Discussion and ConclusionsThe use of kava and P. methysticum containing products was on average higher in regional areas compared to capital cities, and elevated at the weekend, suggesting recreational use. Wastewater analysis can provide specific insights into the use of kava and P. methysticum containing products and is implemented as part of routine drug epidemiology programs.Keywords: anxiety, epidemiology, insomnia, liver damage, prevalence of use, wastewater‐based epidemiology

Introduction

Kava ( Piper methysticum ) is a perennial plant native to the Pacific Islands [1, 2]. Traditionally, beverage kava is prepared by soaking and straining the powdered or crushed roots of the plant through cold water [3]. The water‐based extracts have been used in the Pacific Islands for over 2000 years in traditional healing practices and are consumed ceremonially and recreationally [3, 4, 5]. With respect to this study, kava refers to the traditional water extract of crushed or powdered roots of the plant. This is distinct from other products such as tablets, capsules, shots and gummies that are derived from or contain the P. methysticum plant. In other parts of the world, products containing P. methysticum are made by concentrating the active components, kavalactones, using methods such as supercritical carbon dioxide and extracting using organic solvents like ethanol [4, 6, 7, 8]. These P. methysticum containing pop‐culture beverages, dietary and herbal supplements are used for the treatment of anxiety, stress and insomnia and have become popular due to the calming effects of the kavalactones [1, 7, 9, 10, 11]. In some cases, these P. methysticum containing products are also mixed with other substances to create alternative food items and beverages [3].

Despite the potential therapeutic benefits of kavalactones, the use of products containing P. methysticum was reportedly linked to several cases of liver damage between 1990 and 2002 [7, 8, 12]. Heavy and prolonged use of P. methysticum‐containing products has also been linked to pulmonary hypertension, renal dysfunction and seizures [3, 13, 14]. Other effects of P. methysticum‐containing products include gastrointestinal distress, malnutrition, loss of appetite, weight loss, conjunctivitis, raised cholesterol levels, loss of sexual drive and low motivation [15, 16]. Acute traditional kava use can result in relaxation, a calming effect, drowsiness and muscle weakness [13]. Long‐term and heavy use of traditional kava has been associated with dermopathy [10]. The current literature on the effects of traditional kava on cognitive function and operating a motor vehicle is mixed. Some studies suggest that kava impairs driving‐related cognitive performance, while some report minimal or no significant impairment [9, 17]. The hepatotoxicity associated with P. methysticum‐containing products, not kava itself, led to the ban of kava in Europe and Canada in the early 2000s, and warning alerts were issued in the United States of America (USA) and Australia [7, 18, 19, 20]. Some authors argue that kava bans are mistakenly attributed to Western herbal medicine(s), often marketed as products such as ‘Kava‐kava’, that triggered the European kava ban [21]. A 2016 World Health Organization report acknowledges that the harms due to the traditional use of kava are of an acceptably low level [22].

In Australia, P. methysticum containing medicines were voluntarily recalled in 2003 following increased international reports of liver damage as well as the death of a woman in Australia from liver failure related to these medicines [23]. Subsequently, kava was the subject of strict import restrictions in Australia due to growing concerns of its harm potential and significant undesirable health effects among Indigenous communities in the Northern Territory, where it had been introduced in the 1980s as an alternative to alcohol [24, 25, 26].

Australia has a long‐standing socioeconomic relationship with the Pacific Islands. In December 2019, the Australian government commenced a phase‐wise lifting of the kava importation restrictions. Phase 1 of the kava pilot was focused on personal use, which resulted in the increase of the quantities of kava (powder) allowed from 2 to 4 kg [27]. The second phase of the pilot was implemented in December 2021 and allowed the commercial importation of kava across the country [27, 28]. The forms of kava that are permitted are dried or raw root in the form of chips, root powder, whole root or extracted using cold water only [29]. However, kava remains illegal in the Northern Territory of Australia due to historical instances related to this substance in the jurisdiction [30, 31].

Kava meets the definition of a food in Australia and is available from major grocery stores in the form of a dried root powder. P. methysticum containing products are also readily available over the counter in pharmacies in different forms such as capsules, beverages and liquid drops. In the USA, products containing P. methysticum are classified as dietary supplements and sold in different forms such as capsules/tablets, gummies, tea bags, and so on [32]. Conversely, traditionally prepared kava is referred to as a conventional food and not regulated as a dietary supplement [33].

In Australia, data collection relating to the use of kava and P. methysticum containing products has been conducted through surveys. The National Drug Strategy Household Survey (NDSHS) included kava use questions for the first time in 2022–2023 [34]. The NDSHS is conducted every 2–3 years and generally covers less than 1% of the Australian population. Other surveys that have included questions on kava use include the National Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Social Survey as well as studies conducted in Arnhem Land in the Northern Territory [35, 36]. The Illicit Drug Reporting System included a kava question in the 2024 survey, while in the Ecstasy and Related Reporting System, kava was listed as one of the options as far back as 2004, even though in some of the reports data relating to kava are not shown due to few responses. The 2022 Australian Secondary School Students Alcohol and Drug Survey also listed kava as one of several options under the ethno‐botanicals that the students could have used in the last 12 months prior to the survey. Therefore, the results obtained did not indicate whether the students used kava specifically or other ethno‐botanicals that were listed as options.

Surveys provide valuable information on the use of kava and P. methysticum containing products, such as demographics and the frequency of use. However, they have limited population coverage and as a result, they cannot be extrapolated to a widespread population. The information obtained from surveys pertaining to the use of kava and P. methysticum containing products is challenging to interpret as the use of the substance is mainly limited to specific demographics and cultural groups. Surveys may also be insufficiently powered to detect any statistically significant changes in spatiotemporal trends pertaining to kava use (where use is rare) between different groups or geographical locations. Therefore, complementary methods are needed for population‐level monitoring to further understand its use across the country.

Wastewater‐based epidemiology (WBE) is a non‐invasive, scalable approach that can be used to monitor chemical markers of substances that are consumed by large populations in near real time. WBE is a well‐established and recognised tool in Australia that has been used to measure different psychoactive substances, including alcohol and tobacco, at a national scale [37]. The detection of one kavalactone (kavain) in Australian wastewater has only been reported at a 5‐day, annual musical festival in 2021 and 2022 in Queensland [38]. However, the excretion of bioactive components of kava has not been studied in wastewater nationwide in Australia over time.

The kava root or the kava beverage that is obtained by aqueous extraction and consumed in line with traditional practices does not pose significant risk to public health [15]. However, the consumption of P. methysticum containing products that are not consistent with Standard 2.6.3 and the intent of P1057 Urgent Proposal of the Food Standards Australia New Zealand Code are considered a risk food with potential medium or high risk to public health [15]. P. methysticum containing products are in some cases mixed with substances or drugs of concern such as kratom which can have unpredictable effects. In the USA, overdoses (fatal or nonfatal) involving both P. methysticum containing products and kratom were reported between January 2024 and August 2025 [39]. More recently, the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention documented 233 kratom‐related deaths [40], many associated with what have been described as ‘kava‐kratom’ products, as well as a 1200% increase in poisoning reports over the past decade [41]. Therefore, the recurrent misuse of P. methysticum containing products could lead to undesirable or unpredictable health effects and warrants the need for an effective and rapid consumption surveillance approach for public health purposes, especially in high‐risk communities.

This 13‐month wastewater study aimed to determine suitable and quantifiable chemical markers specific to kava to establish the latest and most comprehensive understanding of the use of kava and P. methysticum containing products in Australia.

Methods

Materials

A mixed reference standard containing kavalactones (dihydrokavain, dihydromethysticin, desmethoxyyangonin, kavain, yangonin and methysticin) and flavokavains (flavokavain A, flavokavain B and flavokavain C) was purchased from NovaChem Pty Ltd. (Melbourne, Australia) in the form of a standard solution. The reference standards for the metabolites (p‐OH‐kavain and p‐OH‐7,8‐dihydrokavain) and the deuterated internal standards (rac‐kavain‐d3 and yangonin‐d3) were purchased as powders from PM Separations Pty Ltd. (Capalaba, Australia). A surrogate was used for analytes lacking a matched internal standards. Sodium metabisulfite, HPLC grade ethyl acetate, sodium chloride, hydrochloric acid, ammonium formate, analytical grade methanol, acetonitrile and formic acid were all purchased from ChemSupply Pty Ltd. (Gillman, Australia).

Sample Collection

Preparation of Kava Retail Products

A 50 g instant kava mixed root powder and P. methysticum derived/containing capsules from two different brands were purchased from retail outlets in Adelaide, South Australia, in July 2024. From each of the three purchased items, 25 mg of the powdered material was weighed and mixed with 25 mL of tap water. The mixtures were then placed on an orbital shaker at high speed for 90 min before centrifuging at 4000 rpm for 5 min. The samples were then filtered using glass‐fibre syringe filters (25 mm diameter, 1.0 μm) from PM Separations Pty Ltd. Aliquots of the mixtures were then diluted 1:20,000 using ultrapure water. Aliquots of the diluted mixture were then transferred into liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry vials and spiked with internal standards to give a final concentration of 571 ng/L before analysis.

Wastewater Sampling

Twenty‐four‐hour composite influent wastewater samples were collected for seven consecutive days bimonthly in capital cities and every 4 months in regional areas across Australia. In Tasmania, samples were not collected on weekends. The sampling period was from December 2023 to December 2024. A total of 54 wastewater treatment plants (WWTP) took part, covering ~56% of the Australian population. Of the 54 WWTPs, 20 were in capital cities while the remainder were in regional areas. The sampling mode used was time proportional (every 15 min) or flow proportional (340–500 kL), depending on the WWTP. Sodium metabisulfite was added to the wastewater samples during collection and samples were frozen before being transported to the laboratory for processing. The addition of sodium metabisulphite (0.5 g/L) to wastewater samples, in addition to freezing the samples, extended the stability of the analytes in wastewater.

Sample Preparation and Analysis

The collected wastewater samples were brought to room temperature upon arrival at the laboratory, after which they were filtered under vacuum using Whatman glass microfiber filters GF/C. The filtered samples were extracted using liquid–liquid extraction with ethyl acetate. All the wastewater and commercial kava product samples were analysed using liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry [42]. The detailed liquid–liquid extraction protocol and liquid chromatography‐tandem mass spectrometry method are all included in the Supporting Information, with the compound‐specific data listed in Table S1.

Calculation and Quantification

The matrix‐matched calibration curve was prepared using pooled wastewater by combining equal amounts from each sample within a batch. The obtained analyte concentrations were used to back‐calculate the target analyte excretion for a given population using Equation (1).

Daily excreted load represents the amount of the drug target residues introduced into the WWTP (mg/day). Flow rate is the daily flow rate of influent wastewater (ML/day) over 24 h reported by the WWTP, while the population is the number of people served by the WWTP, derived from Census 2021 estimates [37]. At the time of this study, there were no known/reliable human excretion data for the target analytes. As a result, back‐calculation to consumed amounts was not performed.

Spatial Pattern Analysis

Population‐weighted excreted mass loads were calculated within a state/territory and capital and regional areas were separated. The population mass loads for capital or regional areas in a state from each collection month were all combined to give the total mass loads for the entire sampling period. The sum was then divided by the total number of sampling months, that is, four for regional areas and seven for capital cities. The states/territories were labelled as follows: Australian Capital Territory (ACT), New South Wales (NSW), Northern Territory (NT), South Australia (SA), Tasmania (TAS), Victoria (VIC), Queensland (QLD) and Western Australia (WA). A one‐way analysis of variance test and multiple comparisons were performed at a 95% confidence interval to determine if there was a statistically significant difference in the excretion of kava markers between capital and regional (intra‐state/territory), capital versus capital or regional versus regional (interstate/territory).

Temporal Pattern Analysis

Monthly patterns of excretion of each target analyte were reported as the sum of the population‐weighted average mass loads for each sampling month. The total sum for capital cities was combined separately from that of regional areas. A two‐way analysis of variance and multiple comparisons tests were performed at a 95% confidence interval to find the statistically significant difference in the excretion of kava markers during the different sampling months.

Weekly Pattern Analysis

For weekly excretion patterns, population‐weighted daily averaged mass loads were combined for a specific day of the week. The sum for each specific day was then divided by the total number of sampling months, that is, four for regional areas and seven for capital cities. A Welch's t‐test, assuming unequal variance, was performed to find the statistical significance between weekdays (Monday–Friday) and weekend (Saturday–Sunday) excretion of kava markers for capital cities and regional areas separately.

Results

Analyte Selection

Kava ( P. methysticum ) has more than 200 cultivars with varying concentrations of kavalactones [43, 44]. The kavalactones are the active compounds of the plant and are highly concentrated in the roots and rhizome. The six major kavalactones that are mainly responsible for the pharmacological effects of kava are dihydrokavain, dihydromethysticin, desmethoxyyangonin, kavain, yangonin and methysticin [45, 46]. The composition of kavalactones in kava or P. methysticum ‐containing products can vary depending on the age and cultivar of the plant used [45]. A commercially available instant kava root powder (Figure 1A) as well as P. methysticum‐containing products (Figure 1B,C) were analysed, with the relative composition of kavalactones in each item shown in Figure 1. Dihydrokavain and kavain were the two most abundant kavalactones in each item. Dihydrokavain and kavain accounted for a substantial portion of the total kavalactones in the items analysed and serve as reliable markers for kava use monitoring, as they can be excreted unchanged. These markers have previously been monitored in different matrices such as roots, human urine and plasma [47].

Composition of kavalactones in (A) instant kava mixed root powder, (B) Piper methysticum derived/containing capsules from brand 1 and (C) P. methysticum derived/containing capsules from brand 2.

Analytical methods generally target the six major kavalactones as they are responsible for the pharmacological effects of kava. Urinary metabolites of kava, such as p‐OH‐kavain and p‐OH‐7,8‐dihydrokavain, have also been reported in serum and urine [48, 49]. For WBE, it is important to target both parent compounds and metabolites, as it gives a more comprehensive means of estimating excretion or consumption and it permits the distinction between direct/industrial disposal from actual consumption. As a result, the excretion of kava chemical markers was monitored through the previously mentioned six kavalactones and two major urinary metabolites during this study.

All eight target analytes were detected in wastewater samples collected during the study period. The combined population‐weighted excreted loads of all target analytes from December 2023 to December 2024 are shown in Figure 2. The measured concentrations of dihydrokavain and kavain were above the limit of quantification (LOQ) in 34% and 27% of the analysed wastewater samples, respectively. For dihydromethysticin, methysticin, yangonin and desmethoxyyangonin, the measured concentrations above the LOQ were in 24%, 15%, 11% and 5% of the analysed samples, respectively. The measured concentrations for the metabolites that were above the LOQ were 37% for p‐OH‐kavain and 94% for p‐OH‐7,8‐dihydrokavain.

The box and whisker plots in Figure 2 were used to visualise the daily excreted mass loads of each analyte during the entire sampling period in both capital cities and regional areas. The mean excretion (represented by a + sign) was higher for dihydrokavain, kavain, p‐OH‐kavain and p‐OH‐7,8‐dihydrokavain compared to the other analytes. Desmethoxyyangonin had the lowest recorded mean in both capital cities and regional areas of 3.4 mg/d/1000 people and 3.6 mg/d/1000 people, respectively. The metabolite, p‐OH‐7,8‐dihydrokavain, was the most abundant analyte with mean values of 45 mg/d/1000 people and 65 mg/d/1000 people for capital cities and regional areas, respectively. There was a statistically significant difference in the mean of the p‐OH‐7,8‐dihydrokavain compared to all the other analytes for both capital cities and regional areas (p < 0.0001). p‐OH‐7,8‐dihydrokavain is a major urinary metabolite for both kavain and dihydrokavain. When p‐OH‐7,8‐dihydrokavain was excluded from the statistical test due to very high excreted mass loads, there was a statistically significant difference between the means of other analytes as indicated by an asterisk in Figure 2. Accordingly, kavain, dihydrokavain and the two urinary metabolites were used for primary spatiotemporal analysis. This selection was based on the four analytes having the highest mean for daily excreted mass loads, frequency of detection in samples and their concentrations were above the LOQ, compared to the other analytes in most samples. In the three commercially available items that were analysed, dihydrokavain and kavain amounted to a substantial portion of kavalactones. The results for the other four kavalactones that were also targeted are included in the accompanying Supporting Information.

Spatial Patterns

The spatial comparison of excreted mass loads for kava chemical markers was performed at the level of intrastate/territory and interstate/territory, separating capital cities from regional areas. The intra‐territory comparison was not applied to ACT as the territory does not include regional centres. Within states, regional SA and WA recorded higher population‐normalised excreted loads compared to their capital cities for the four analytes shown in Figure 3 and this difference was statistically significant (p < 0.0020). Other locations also recorded statistically significant differences between capital and regional areas for some analytes. At the interstate/territory level, NSW and NT recorded the highest excreted mass loads for capital cities, while WA loads were lower for most analytes in Figure 3. In regional areas, WA had the highest excreted mass loads, followed by NT and SA. An interesting finding relates to the presence and quantity of kava chemical markers detected in the NT, where kava remains illegal, with excreted amounts being significantly higher than all other states/territories except for NSW (capital), WA (regional) and SA (regional). The kava restrictions in the NT preclude kava from being carried into the jurisdiction [30]. There is a complex historical interplay of factors that led to the prohibition. Some authors have attributed the ban to adverse health, social and economic effects within Aboriginal communities [50, 51]. However, a contrary view is that the ban was not solely due to health concerns and that the research failed to establish a clear connection between kava drinking and the reported adverse health effects [52]. The legislation around kava in Australia has been complex, consisting of periods of no regulations, regulations, deregulations and licensing from the 1980's to 2007 [53]. This was followed by over a decade of import restrictions from 2007 to 2019 with an exemption for medical and scientific purposes [54]. The significant use of kava and or P. methysticum containing products in the NT could also be a result of online purchases, smuggling and potential illicit drug market sales, which have been reported very recently to be on the rise in the territory [55, 56]. Kava and P. methysticum containing products obtained from the illicit drug market may affect people who use it in different ways, such that the products sold may be more potent or have varying and unpredictable effects due to a lack of regulated production [43].

The difference in the excretion of kava chemical markers across and within states could be due to the consumption of different kava cultivars, different P. methysticum containing products, ease of access to products, lifestyle, as well as cultural practices. From 2007, the import restrictions came into effect allowing incoming travellers to bring up to 2 kg of kava with them [28]. However, in 2019, the Australian Government introduced the kava pilot. Phase 1 of the kava pilot allowed incoming passengers to bring up to 4 kg of kava (powder) from December 2019 and phase 2 allowed the commercial importation from December 2021 [27]. Since the easing of importation restrictions, kava and P. methysticum containing products have become more readily available in most parts of Australia [56]. This is also reflected in the wastewater data reported here, with kava chemical markers being detected in all Australian states and territories.

Temporal Patterns

The excretion of kavalactones in the capital cities fluctuated across the sampling period. There was an increase in the excretion of all kavalactones (dihydrokavain, dihydromethysticin, desmethoxyyangonin, kavain, yangonin and methysticin) at the mid‐point of the study in June 2024 compared to the other months (Figure 4 and Figure S2). This difference was statistically significant for both dihydrokavain (p = 0.0005) and kavain (p = 0.013). This increase in excreted loads in June 2024 was due to the high concentrations of kavalactones detected from one of the wastewater treatment plants in the capital, New South Wales, compared to other months. In contrast, the measured mass loads of the two metabolites in June 2024 were not usually high compared to the other months during the collection period. The increase in the excreted mass loads for the parent compounds (kavalactones) with no subsequent increase in their metabolites suggests that there was a potential disposal of kava or P. methysticum ‐containing products in the sewer lines. In regional areas, excreted mass loads for the kavalactones also fluctuated, and the metabolites showed a steady increase during the sampling period (Figure 4B,D). There was no statistically significant difference in the excreted mass loads during the 4 months of sampling in regional areas.

Weekly Patterns

To gain insights into the consumption behaviour (i.e., habitual vs. recreational use) of kava and P. methysticum containing products across Australia, consolidated daily capital and regional data are presented in Figure 5. The excretion of dihydrokavain and kavain in capital cities fluctuated during the week. The metabolites p‐OH‐kavain and p‐OH‐7,8‐dihydrokavain (Figure 5B,D) showed a gradual increase during the weekdays into the weekend, peaking on Sunday and abating thereafter. These observed excretion patterns of the chemical markers during the study period suggest that on a national level, the consumption of kava and P. methysticum containing products is most consistent with recreational rather than habitual/supplementary use but does not rule out the latter. The difference in weekday versus weekend excretion was statistically significant for dihydrokavain in regional areas (p = 0.009). The results for the other kavalactones are in Figure S3.

Discussion

Lifestyles in capital and regional Australia differ greatly in terms of the cost of living, entertainment, access to amenities and services, as well as employment opportunities. It has been found that the type and frequency of use of psychoactive substances also differ between capital cities and regional cities in Australia [37, 57, 58]. From the results reported here, the use of kava and P. methysticum containing products varies from one geographical location to another, both within and across borders. In contrast, data from the 2022 to 2023 NDSHS, indicates the prevalence of kava and P. methysticum containing products use is consistent regardless of socioeconomics and remoteness [50]. However, the relative standard error for inner and outer remote areas in the NDSHS data set is large. Therefore, comparison between the two should be interpreted with caution. One of the major companies trading in kava and P. methysticum containing products showed that the 2024 and 2025 financial year sales in Oceania increased by 350% and 107% respectively, compared to the previous year, with Australia accounting for more than 70% of the sales [59, 60]. Data derived from wastewater analysis appear concomitant with sales figures.

For many years, the use of kava has extended beyond the borders of the Pacific Islands, gaining popularity as a food or herbal supplement. However, at a global scale, the patterns of kava use are underexplored, with very few to no studies conducted in most parts [25, 32]. The results from this study bridge the gap in knowledge regarding the spatiotemporal trends in the use of kava and P. methysticum containing products across Australia. Long‐term monitoring could therefore be beneficial for public health, as new and emerging trends of use can be obtained which could inform intervention strategies, especially in vulnerable communities. These data are also very relevant for changing policies as they provide insights to evaluate the implemented changes. The monitoring of kava chemical markers demonstrated through this study gave insights into patterns of use across Australia, offering comparative data to other countries where kava and P. methysticum containing products are also used or have been on the rise. Limitations of the current study need to be acknowledged. Kavalactones were targeted for analysis. These markers cannot be used to distinguish between traditional kava extracts and new popular commercial blends. Therefore, it would be inappropriate to suggest the uptake of traditional, herbal kava is on the increase or that its use is recreational. WBE cannot determine whether kava roots powder or P. methysticum containing products were consumed. Through WBE, we measured excretion of chemical markers which are very specific to kava and found in the natural kava root as well as in the products that are derived from it.

Conclusion

This study pioneered the first‐ever application of WBE to monitor eight different specific kava chemical markers, providing insights into kava consumption across Australia. Spatially, populations in capital New South Wales, the Northern Territory (both capital and regional), regional Western Australia and regional South Australia consumed the most kava and P. methysticum containing products. The temporal patterns showed fluctuations of use across different months. A weekend peak in excretion of the metabolites suggests kava and P. methysticum containing products are favoured more recreationally than habitually. The trends established from this study provide an important baseline for understanding the consumption of kava and P. methysticum containing products and warrant longer‐term monitoring for determining new and emerging consumption patterns. This study further underpins the importance of WBE to obtain information near real‐time at a population level. These data provide a useful baseline for policymakers, which complements existing sales and survey datasets to better inform the development and evaluation of policy relevant to kava consumption in Australia. Further research is required to better understand which P. methysticum containing products are driving spatial and temporal changes in consumption across Australia.
