CY0‐to‐CY1 Conversion in Gastric Cancer Peritoneal Lavage Cytology: Clinical Significance and Improved Cell Retention by Et + PA Fixation
Authors: Akihiko Fukagawa, Nao Yoshizawa, Shun Iwai, Kosei Nakamura, Kota Itamoto, Ryo Wakabayashi, Shinji Hamakawa, Hirokazu Yamaguchi, Taichiro Yoshimoto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPeritoneal lavage cytology (PLC) is important for staging gastric cancer, but intraoperative assessment may miss malignant cells because only a limited portion of the specimen can be examined. We retrospectively analyzed 287 patients who underwent gastrectomy with intraoperative PLC between 2014 and 2022. Cytologic status was classified as CY−, CY0‐to‐CY1 conversion, or CY+ based on intraoperative and permanent cytologic findings. Of 287 patients, 251 were CY−, 10 showed CY0‐to‐CY1 conversion, and 26 were CY+ on intraoperative assessment, resulting in 36 final CY1 cases. Patients with CY0‐to‐CY1 conversion had significantly worse overall survival than CY− patients and outcomes comparable to those of CY+ patients. Serosal invasion/pT4 disease was strongly associated with final CY1 status and was frequent among conversion cases. In a separate technical evaluation, brief ethanol fixation followed by polyethylene glycol and acetone–methanol fixation (Et + PA fixation) significantly improved total cell retention and preservation of tumor‐cell clusters compared with conventional ethanol fixation in 11 paired cases. CY0‐to‐CY1 conversion represents clinically meaningful cytology‐positive disease. Et + PA fixation improves cellular retention during cytologic preparation and may improve the quality of intraoperative PLC preparations.Keywords: CY0‐to‐CY1 conversion, fixation, gastric cancer, peritoneal lavage cytology

Introduction

Gastric cancer (GC) remains one of the most common malignancies and a leading cause of cancer‐related mortality worldwide [1]. Peritoneal metastasis is frequently observed in advanced GC and is associated with poor prognosis [2, 3]. Peritoneal lavage cytology (PLC), which directly detects free tumor cells in the peritoneal cavity, is an important diagnostic tool for staging and treatment planning [4, 5]. Positive peritoneal cytology (CY1) is classified as metastatic disease in major staging systems, including the AJCC TNM classification and the Japanese Classification of Gastric Carcinoma [6, 7, 8]. Patients with positive peritoneal cytology, even in the absence of macroscopic peritoneal dissemination, have been reported to show poor outcomes comparable to those of patients with overt peritoneal metastasis [2, 3, 9, 10, 11].

PLC can be performed as a rapid intraoperative assessment or as a permanent cytologic evaluation. Intraoperative PLC is particularly useful because it can provide real‐time information for surgical decision‐making [4]. However, only a limited portion of the lavage specimen can usually be examined during surgery because of time and resource constraints. In addition, rapid processing and fixation conditions may affect cellular yield, tumor‐cell cluster preservation, and cytologic interpretation [12, 13]. These pre‐analytic factors may reduce the sensitivity of intraoperative PLC compared with permanent preparations, in which the residual specimen can be more extensively evaluated.

The discrepancy between intraoperative and permanent PLC is clinically important. The recently revised Japanese Classification of Gastric Carcinoma provides a standardized rule for handling discrepancies between intraoperative and permanent cytology, in which cases diagnosed as CY0 intraoperatively but CY1 on permanent cytologic evaluation are recorded as CY0 [8]. Nevertheless, when malignant cells are detected on permanent preparations after an intraoperative CY0 diagnosis, clinicians may face substantial difficulty in postoperative counseling and treatment planning, because CY1 status may alter stage classification and therapeutic strategy [3, 6, 14]. Therefore, the clinicopathologic and prognostic significance of CY0‐to‐CY1 conversion remains an important unresolved issue. In particular, it remains uncertain whether patients with CY0‐to‐CY1 conversion should be regarded as having cytology‐negative disease or managed similarly to patients with intraoperative CY1.

In this retrospective study, we analyzed 287 patients with GC who underwent gastrectomy with intraoperative PLC between January 2014 and December 2022. By evaluating the residual lavage specimens on permanent preparations, we identified patients who converted from intraoperative CY0 to permanent CY1. We then assessed the clinicopathologic and prognostic significance of CY0‐to‐CY1 conversion. Finally, we compared conventional ethanol fixation with Et + PA fixation, a fixation method incorporating polyethylene glycol and acetone‐methanol after brief ethanol fixation, to determine whether optimized fixation could improve cell and tumor‐cell cluster retention during intraoperative PLC.

Materials and Methods

Patients and Cytological Specimens

We retrospectively identified consecutive patients with histologically confirmed GC who underwent gastrectomy with intraoperative PLC at our institution between January 2014 and December 2022 (Table S1). Of 321 patients initially evaluated, 17 received upfront chemotherapy owing to disease stage or patient preference (Table S2), and 17 had unresectable disease (Table S3). During the study period, intraoperative CY1 status alone was not considered a contraindication to gastrectomy at our institution. These patients were excluded from the main analysis. The analytic cohort therefore comprised 287 patients who underwent gastrectomy. Clinicopathologic data were extracted from medical records, including age, sex, tumor size, tumor location, histologic type classified according to the Japanese Classification of Gastric Carcinoma, and pathological stage determined according to the AJCC eighth edition [7, 8].

Cytology

Peritoneal lavage was performed immediately after laparotomy or laparoscopy by instilling 50 mL of warm saline into the Douglas pouch and subphrenic spaces, followed by gentle aspiration of the fluid. Intraoperative preparations were fixed in ethanol according to the routine protocol and stained using rapid Papanicolaou staining. For permanent cytologic evaluation, cytospin preparations were routinely generated by processing the residual lavage specimen and stained using the Papanicolaou method. When sufficient material remained after cytospin preparation, an additional cell block was prepared, and immunocytochemistry was performed when diagnostically indicated. No carcinoma was detected for the first time by immunocytochemistry performed on a cell‐block preparation.

Cytologic results were classified as CY0 or CY1. CY1 was defined as the presence of suspicious or unequivocal malignant epithelial cells, corresponding to Class IV or V cytology, as determined by two cytologists and at least one cytopathologist. Cases without suspicious or unequivocal malignant epithelial cells were classified as CY0. Patients were categorized according to the combination of intraoperative and permanent cytologic findings: CY− was defined as CY0 on both intraoperative and permanent assessments, CY0‐to‐CY1 conversion as CY0 on intraoperative assessment but CY1 on permanent assessment, and CY+ as CY1 on intraoperative assessment. For survival analyses, permanent CY1 included both CY0‐to‐CY1 conversion and CY+ cases, because an intraoperative CY1 diagnosis was regarded as definitive and was not downgraded on the basis of subsequent permanent evaluation. Discrepant cases were adjudicated by consensus.

Statistical Analysis

Cytology groups were defined as CY− (intraoperative CY0 → permanent CY0), CY0‐to‐CY1 conversion, and CY+ (intraoperative CY1). Group comparisons used χ 2 or Fisher's exact test for categorical variables and the Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables. Factors associated with CY0‐to‐CY1 conversion (vs. CY−) and with permanent CY1 (CY0‐to‐CY1 conversion plus CY+ vs. CY−) were evaluated using logistic regression; variables with p < 0.10 in univariable analyses were entered into multivariable models. Overall survival (OS) was calculated from the date of gastrectomy to death from any cause or last follow‐up and estimated by the Kaplan–Meier method with log‐rank tests. Cox proportional hazards models were used to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Two‐sided p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. As a sensitivity analysis, survival analyses were repeated using a more stringent definition of CY1 restricted to Class V cytology. Analyses were performed with R 4.4.1.

Comparison of Fixation Methods

For comparison of fixation methods, paired cytology slides were prepared using conventional ethanol fixation and polyethylene glycol‐containing coating fixation followed by acetone–methanol fixation, hereafter referred to as Et + PA fixation. Eleven cases were selected based on the availability of sufficient residual lavage specimens for preparation of paired slides. In the conventional ethanol fixation protocol, slides were fixed in 95% ethanol for 60 s. In the Et + PA fixation protocol, slides were first immersed in 95% ethanol for 10 s, followed by immersion in a polyethylene glycol‐containing coating fixative for 10 s and an acetone–methanol mixture for 60 s. The polyethylene glycol‐containing coating fixative was Rapid Spray GF (Muto Pure Chemicals, Tokyo, Japan), which is originally intended for spray coating of smeared cytology slides; in this study, it was used as an immersion fixative by placing the solution in a staining dish. The acetone–methanol mixture was prepared at a ratio of 3:7.

Air‐dried Giemsa‐stained preparations were used as the reference for cellularity, and their cellularity was defined as a cellularity index of 100. Cell‐retention rates were then calculated for each fixation method. Total cells and tumor‐cell clusters were counted manually. A tumor‐cell cluster was defined as a cohesive group of three or more tumor cells. Cytological evaluation was not blinded, and interobserver reproducibility was not formally assessed. Statistical comparisons between paired fixation methods were performed using the Wilcoxon signed‐rank test.

Results

Cytological Diagnosis Among Resected Patients

Among these 287 patients, 251 (87.5%) were negative on both intraoperative cytology and permanent review (CY−) (Figure 1). Ten patients (3.5%) were classified as CY0 intraoperatively but were upgraded to CY1 on permanent review of the residual lavage specimen (conversion group, CY0‐to‐CY1 conversion). Twenty‐six patients (9.1%) were classified as CY+ because they were CY1 on intraoperative cytology. Overall, 36 patients (12.5%) were ultimately classified as CY1, including 10 patients (27.8%) who had been diagnosed as CY0 during intraoperative assessment.

Clinicopathologic Features of CY0‐to‐CY1 Conversion Cases

We further examined the clinicopathologic features of the 10 patients with CY0‐to‐CY1 conversion (Table 1). All 10 patients had pathologically advanced GC without other distant metastasis at the time of surgery. Eight patients had pT4a disease, and the remaining two patients had pT3 disease. Nodal metastasis was present in all cases, including pN1 in one case, pN2 in six cases, pN3a in one case, and pN3b in two cases. According to the pathological stage, seven patients were classified as stage IIIA, one as stage IIIB, and two as stage IIIC. Histologically, the tumors showed variable differentiation, including mucinous adenocarcinoma, tubular adenocarcinoma, papillary adenocarcinoma, poorly differentiated adenocarcinoma, and signet‐ring cell carcinoma components. Poorly differentiated adenocarcinoma and/or signet‐ring cell carcinoma components were observed in 6 cases. During follow‐up, 8 of the 10 patients died, whereas two patients were alive at the last follow‐up. These findings suggest that CY0‐to‐CY1 conversion occurred mainly in patients with advanced locoregional disease, particularly those with serosal invasion and nodal metastasis, even in the absence of other distant metastasis.

Permanent CY1 Is an Independent Adverse Prognostic Factor

We first evaluated whether permanent CY1 status was associated with OS. Patients with permanent CY1 had significantly shorter OS than those with permanent CY0 (log‐rank p < 0.001) (Figure 2A). In univariable Cox proportional hazards analysis, permanent CY1 was significantly associated with worse OS compared with permanent CY0 (HR = 6.30, 95% CI 3.88–10.25, p = 9.42 × 10−12) (Table S4). To determine whether this association was independent of established clinicopathological factors, we performed multivariable Cox proportional hazards analysis including permanent cytological status and relevant prognostic variables. Permanent CY1 remained significantly associated with an increased risk of death after adjustment for these factors (HR = 4.91, 95% CI 2.68–9.00, p = 2.49 × 10−7), indicating that cytological positivity on permanent review is an independent adverse prognostic factor (Figure 2B and Table S5).

Clinical Outcomes of CY0‐to‐CY1 Conversion Versus CY− and CY+

To determine whether patients who converted from negative to positive cytology on permanent review (CY0‐to‐CY1 conversion) had clinical outcomes closer to CY− or CY+, we compared OS across cytology groups. OS in the CY0‐to‐CY1 conversion group was significantly shorter than in patients who remained cytology‐negative (CY−) (median OS: 30.0 months vs. not reached; log‐rank p < 0.001). In Cox proportional hazards analysis using CY0‐to‐CY1 conversion as the reference group, CY− was associated with a significantly lower risk of death (HR = 0.19, 95% CI 0.09–0.40, p < 0.001). In contrast, OS did not differ significantly between CY0‐to‐CY1 conversion and patients with positive intraoperative cytology (CY+) (median OS: 30.0 vs. 21.0 months; log‐rank p = 0.552; HR = 1.29, 95% CI 0.56–2.97) (Figure 2C). Patients with CY0‐to‐CY1 conversion were generally managed as CY1 after the permanent cytologic diagnosis, and details of postoperative therapy and recurrence patterns are provided in Table S6.

We next examined outcomes according to pathological stage. After excluding the 10 patients with CY0‐to‐CY1 conversion, 144 patients with pStage IIIA–IV disease were categorized according to pathological stage. The pStage IV group included patients with CY1 disease. Patients in the CY0‐to‐CY1 conversion group had significantly shorter OS than those with pStage IIIA or IIIB disease (log‐rank p = 0.0027 vs. pStage IIIA; p = 0.0039 vs. pStage IIIB), whereas no significant difference in OS was observed between the CY0‐to‐CY1 conversion group and patients with pStage IIIC or IV disease (p = 0.077 vs. pStage IIIC; p = 0.439 vs. pStage IV) (Figure 2D and Table S7). In a sensitivity analysis restricted to Class V cytology, the OS pattern was similar to that observed in the primary analysis (Figure S1).

Clinicopathologic Correlates of CY0‐to‐CY1 Conversion and CY Status

To identify factors associated with conversion from intraoperative CY0 to permanent CY1 (CY0‐to‐CY1 conversion), we performed univariable analyses followed by multivariable modeling. On univariable analysis, pT4 was significantly associated with CY0‐to‐CY1 conversion compared with CY− status (OR, 10.55; 95% CI, 2.19–50.92; p = 0.0034) (Table 2). pN2–3 was also significantly associated with CY0‐to‐CY1 conversion on univariable analysis (OR, 12.52; 95% CI, 1.56–100.34; p = 0.017). In multivariable analysis including pT4 and pN2–3, pT4 showed a trend toward association with CY0‐to‐CY1 conversion, although this did not reach statistical significance (adjusted OR, 5.30; 95% CI, 0.98–28.48; p = 0.052), whereas pN2–3 was not significant (adjusted OR, 5.73; 95% CI, 0.62–52.91; p = 0.123).

Cytology in the CY0‐to‐CY1 Conversion Group

We re‐reviewed paired intraoperative and permanent cytology preparations from all 10 CY0‐to‐CY1 conversion cases. No unequivocal malignant cells were identified on the intraoperative slides. Comparison of Fixation Methods for Cell Retention

We compared conventional ethanol fixation with a fixation method incorporating polyethylene glycol and acetone–methanol after brief ethanol fixation (Et + PA fixation). Using air‐dried Giemsa‐stained preparations as the reference standard, we compared the percentage of retained cells and the percentage of retained tumor‐cell clusters between ethanol fixation and Et + PA fixation in 11 cases (Figure 4A,B). Et + PA fixation yielded significantly higher percentages of retained cells and tumor‐cell clusters than ethanol fixation (both p = 9.8 × 10−4). These findings indicate that Et + PA fixation increased both cell retention and tumor‐cell cluster retention in this experimental setting.

Discussion

In this single‐institution cohort of patients with resected GC, we identified a small but clinically important subset of patients whose intraoperative PLC was negative but whose permanent cytologic evaluation was positive. CY0‐to‐CY1 conversion was associated with adverse clinical outcomes, while comparison of fixation methods demonstrated improved cell and tumor‐cell cluster retention with Et + PA fixation. These findings suggest that CY0‐to‐CY1 conversion is not merely an incidental discrepancy and warrants clinical attention.

A change in cytologic diagnosis from intraoperative CY0 to permanent CY1 has important clinical implications. Intraoperative cytology is often used to guide surgical decision‐making and to provide an initial explanation to patients and their families. Therefore, when a patient is regarded as CY0 during surgery but is later found to be CY1 on permanent preparations, clinicians may face substantial difficulty in postoperative counseling and treatment planning. The present study directly addresses this problem. The survival of patients with CY0‐to‐CY1 conversion was significantly worse than that of CY− patients and was similar to that of CY+ patients. This prognostic pattern was also maintained in a sensitivity analysis restricted to Class V cytology, supporting the robustness of the main findings. Although the number of CY0‐to‐CY1 conversion cases was limited, these results suggest that malignant cells detected only on permanent cytologic preparations may have clinical significance. This finding is particularly relevant in light of the recently revised Japanese Classification of Gastric Carcinoma, which records cases diagnosed as CY0 intraoperatively but CY1 on permanent cytologic evaluation as CY0 [8]. Our data do not directly challenge the practical need for standardized cytology recording; rather, they suggest that CY0‐to‐CY1 conversion may identify a biologically and prognostically relevant subset that warrants careful clinicopathologic attention.

To our knowledge, the prognostic significance of conversion from negative intraoperative cytology to positive permanent cytology has not been specifically evaluated as a distinct clinical entity. Previous studies, however, have demonstrated that the results of PLC may be influenced by sampling and methodological conditions. Ki et al. reported substantial variation in cytology‐positive rates according to the surgeon performing the lavage [15], and a recent multicenter survey of Japanese high‐volume centers demonstrated considerable interinstitutional variability in sampling, specimen preparation, fixation, and staining methods [16]. These observations suggest that the frequency of CY0‐to‐CY1 conversion may vary according to institutional methodology and may not be directly generalizable across institutions. In addition, because intraoperative CY1 may influence surgical decision‐making in some institutions, direct prognostic comparison between CY0‐to‐CY1 conversion and intraoperative CY1 can be difficult. In our cohort, CY1 status itself was not used as a criterion to avoid gastrectomy, allowing these groups to be evaluated within the same surgical cohort. Despite such methodological and clinical variability, our findings indicate that malignant cells detected only on permanent preparations retain prognostic significance, with CY0‐to‐CY1 conversion showing clinical behavior more similar to intraoperative CY1 than to persistently CY0 disease.

The clinical behavior of CY0‐to‐CY1 conversion cases is consistent with the established adverse prognostic significance of positive peritoneal cytology in GC. Patients with cytology‐positive disease, even in the absence of macroscopic peritoneal dissemination, are generally considered to have metastatic disease in major staging systems [2, 3, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10]. In this context, the clinically relevant issue may be the presence of malignant cells in the peritoneal cavity rather than the timing of their detection. Our findings therefore support careful clinical consideration of malignant cells detected only on permanent preparations.

The discordance between intraoperative and permanent cytology may be influenced by several factors during rapid intraoperative slide preparation. In the CY0‐to‐CY1 conversion cases, no unequivocal malignant cells were identified on intraoperative slides, whereas permanent preparations revealed cohesive tumor‐cell clusters and/or singly dispersed malignant cells. This observation indicates that malignant cells present in the residual lavage specimens were not detected on the intraoperative preparations. Because both the number of malignant cells and the preservation of cohesive clusters may influence cytologic detection, optimization of specimen preparation may be important for improving the sensitivity of intraoperative PLC.

Et + PA fixation yielded higher cell retention and better preservation of tumor‐cell clusters than ethanol fixation in the evaluated cases. These findings suggest that Et + PA fixation may reduce preparation‐related cell loss, consistent with the importance of fixation and preservation conditions in cytology preparations [12, 13]. Importantly, Et + PA fixation is a practical modification of specimen handling and does not require specialized molecular assays or expensive equipment. Therefore, it may be readily applicable to routine cytology workflows, particularly in institutions where intraoperative PLC is used to guide surgical and postoperative decision‐making. However, whether this improved cellular preservation directly reduces false‐negative intraoperative diagnoses requires prospective validation.

This study has several limitations. First, it was a retrospective study conducted at a single institution. Second, the survival analysis was restricted to patients who underwent gastrectomy, which may have introduced selection bias; however, CY1 status itself was not used as a criterion to avoid surgery during the study period. Third, the number of CY0‐to‐CY1 conversion cases was small, which limits the statistical power of subgroup analyses and precludes definitive conclusions regarding independent prognostic impact. Fourth, the comparison of fixation methods was performed in a limited number of cases and did not directly assess whether fixation‐related cell loss accounted for CY0‐to‐CY1 conversion; these findings should therefore be validated prospectively in a larger cohort. Nevertheless, the present study provides clinically relevant evidence that CY0‐to‐CY1 conversion identifies a prognostically important subset of GC patients. The fixation study further demonstrates that Et + PA fixation improves cell retention during cytological preparation.

In conclusion, conversion from intraoperative CY0 to permanent CY1 is a clinically meaningful finding in GC. Patients with CY0‐to‐CY1 conversion showed poor outcomes comparable to those of patients with intraoperative CY1, suggesting that malignant cells detected only on permanent preparations should not be regarded as clinically negligible. Permanent evaluation of peritoneal lavage specimens is important for detecting missed CY1 cases. Et + PA fixation may represent a practical approach to improving cellular retention during intraoperative PLC preparation.
