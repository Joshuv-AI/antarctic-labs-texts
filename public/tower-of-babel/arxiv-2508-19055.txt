Abstract
. Quantum databases open an exciting new frontier in data management by offering privacy guarantees that classical systems cannot match. Traditional engines tackle user privacy , which hides the records being queried, or data privacy , which prevents a user from learning more than she has queried. We propose a quantum database that protects both by leveraging quantum mechanics: when the user measures her chosen basis, the superposition collapses and the unqueried rows become physically inaccessible. We encode relational tables as a sequence of Quantum Random Access Codes (QRACs) over mutually unbiased bases (MUBs), transmit a bounded number of quantum states, and let a single, destructive measurement reconstruct only the selected tuple. This allows us to preserve data privacy and user privacy at once without trusted hardware or heavyweight cryptography. Moreover, we envision a novel hybrid quantum–classical architecture ready for early deployment, which ensures compatibility with the limitations of today’s Noisy Intermediate-Scale Quantum devices.



1.
Introduction

Quantum computing is transitioning from laboratory curiosity to a commercially funded technology stack. Yet for data management practitioners, the fundamental question remains unanswered:

When could a quantum database provide measurable benefit over a mature classical database?
Addressing this question requires more than algorithmic speedups in theory; instead, it demands application
-
driven analysis and realistic system design.

We define a
quantum database
as a data storage and query engine that employs quantum phenomena such as superposition, entanglement, and measurement to realize functionality that is provably inefficient or impossible for classical databases. Existing studies indicate three broad motivations. The first is
database search.
Grover’s algorithm
(
Grover 1996
)
can search unstructured data with complexity of
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{N})
, where
N
is the size of the domain. However, recent work indicates that
Noisy Intermediate
-
Scale Quantum (NISQ) devices cannot achieve the promised quadratic speedup
(
Chen et al. 2023
)
. Moreover, classical databases have more mature implementations, such as B
-
trees or hash indexes. Thus, the opportunity window for search acceleration is, for now, narrow.
The second possibility is using quantum database to
store native quantum data.
Scientific instruments such as quantum sensors emit quantum states directly. While compelling, these workloads are niche and do not motivate a general
-
purpose quantum database.

In this paper, we explore another promising possibility yet understudied, that is, employ quantum databases for
privacy
. Quantum cryptographic primitives such as quantum private queries
(
Giovannetti et al. 2008
)
, quantum key distribution
(
Bennett and Brassard 1984
)
, and quantum bit commitment
(
Mayers 1997
;
Lo and Chau 1997
)
show potential in highly regulated sectors such as banking audits and privacy-constrained healthcare,
where a verifier retrieves only the qualifying data tuples while concealing both the query and the remaining data. Emerging privacy legislation including GDPR
(
Voigt and Von dem Bussche 2017
)
, HIPAA
(
hip 1996
)
, IPA
(
Inf [n.d.]
)
, and PIPL
(
Per [n.d.]
)
, which magnifies both the urgency and the business value of this capability. We therefore argue that privacy
constitutes the most immediate and impactful opportunity for quantum databases.

Problem Statement.
We target a
private quantum database
that enforces both
user privacy
(the server learns nothing about the query) and
data privacy
(the client learns only the requested tuples). The following example illustrates the setting.

Example 1.

An auditor requires data about a customer from a bank’s databases while satisfying two privacy constraints: first, the auditor’s query (which customer is being audited) must remain oblivious from the bank; second, the auditor must learn nothing beyond that customer’s records. A quantum database, used alongside the classical database, facilitates privacy-preserving audits without exposing either the auditor’s intent or other clients’ data.

Achieving both user privacy and data privacy in the NISQ era is challenging, as today’s quantum processors operate at the hundreds to thousands of qubits scale, which are noisy.
For instance, one of the most advanced quantum processors from IBM is Heron
1
1

1

https://docs.quantum.ibm.com/guides/processor-types
, which has 156 qubits and not yet equipped with full error correction. The IBM System Two, currently under development, connects multiple Heron processors and aims to scale to 4,000 qubits by 2025 and 100,000 qubits in the 2030s.
Therefore, in this work we are interested in designing a quantum database that brings meaningful practical applications under the limitation of NISQ hardware, possibly through the help of existing classical database management systems (DBMSs).

Contributions.
We introduce a
private quantum database
centered on a restricted quantum database query protocol that guarantees both user and data privacy. The design rests on fundamental quantum principles: measurement is
destructive
, so observing one property collapses the state and irreversibly erases all others; and the
no-cloning theorem

(
Wootters and Zurek 1982
)
, which forbids duplicating the state to bypass that collapse. We also propose a hybrid quantum–classical architecture that embeds the quantum database within existing DBMS engines. This early prototype delivers a principled framework for reasoning about quantum advantage in designing quantum-native database systems and offers a concrete path toward application
-
driven adoption of quantum hardware in future database systems.

2.2.
Quantum DBs Based on Grover’s Algorithm

A
database
is a persistent, electronically stored collection of data
(
Abiteboul et al. 1995
;
Ramakrishnan and Gehrke 2003
)
.
In the quantum community, the term
database
usually denotes a conceptual framework for processing and searching data using quantum algorithms.
Grover’s algorithm searches an unsorted set of
N
items in
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{N})
iterations instead of the classical
\mathcal{O}(N)
bound
(
Grover 1996
)
.
During the 1990s-2010s, a series of works extended the basic idea of Grover’s algorithm to searching databases
(
Terhal and Smolin 1998
;
Boyer et al. 1998
;
Patel 2001
;
Tsai et al. 2002
;
Imre and Balázs 2004
;
Ju et al. 2007
)
.
Moreover, several attempts have been made to build full relational “quantum DBMSs” in which SQL
-
like statements are realized by quantum circuits, supporting relational operations such as joins and set operations (intersection, union, difference)
(
Cockshott 1997
;
Gueddana et al. 2010
;
Salman and Baram 2012
;
Pang et al. 2013
;
Gueddana et al. 2014
;
Jóczik and Kiss 2020
)
, and data manipulation operations (insert, update, delete)
(
Gueddana et al. 2010
;
Younes 2013
;
Gueddana et al. 2014
)
.

However, these approaches do not translate into practice in terms of algorithmic cost and in physical implementability.
First, each query contains a quantum measurement, which destroys the qubit state, so the qubit state must be rebuilt for the next query, resulting in
\mathcal{O}(N)
time, canceling Grover’s
\sqrt{N}
speed
-
up. Second, NISQ quantum computers are noisy and limited in qubits.
The circuit depths and number of qubits required in the above proposals,
even for small relational tables, are beyond capabilities of NISQ hardware.
Thus, in this work, we do
not
pursue Grover
-
style quantum databases.
Instead, our design targets quantum advantages in
privacy
, while explicitly considering the NISQ hardware’s constraints in circuit depth, qubit count, and noise.

2.3.
Private Databases

Data privacy
addresses how a DBMS executes queries without revealing unnecessary information, typically by encrypting the data.
Hacigümüs
et al.
tackle the challenge of maintaining data confidentiality on untrusted cloud providers and propose processing SQL queries over encrypted cloud databases
(
Hacigümüs et al. 2002
)
.
MONOMI
(
Tu et al. 2013
)
splits analytical queries between an encrypted cloud server and an unencrypted client engine for higher efficiency.
Performance can be further improved with secure hardware, such as IBM cryptographic coprocessors in TrustedDB
(
Bajaj and Sion 2011
)
and FPGAs in Microsoft Cipherbase
(
Arasu et al. 2013
)
.
Instead of relying solely on cryptographic primitives, Enclave
(
Sun et al. 2021
)
leverages Intel SGX.
More recent works employ differential privacy for private SQL processing
(
Kotsogiannis et al. 2019
)
, or formalize leakage-safe design rules across heterogeneous cryptographic primitives
(
Zhang et al. 2024
)
.
These systems hide data values, yet the access pattern, namely, which tuples a user reads, often remains visible.

User privacy
, also known as
Private Information Retrieval (PIR)
, tackles a complementary problem: a user retrieves a record without revealing her query to the server.
In a PIR setting, the server treats its dataset as an indexed array, and the client is assumed to possess the index of the item that it intends to retrieve.
One database application is location-based services
(
Ghinita et al. 2008
)
.
Pantheon
(
Ahmad et al. 2022
)
cuts single-round, server-side latency in key-value stores to two million item lookups in about one second.
The following tutorial surveys both theoretical developments and practical engines for PIR over public datasets
(
Ahmad et al. 2023
)
.

S^{2}

(
Sharma et al. 2023
)
extends multi-server, information-theoretic PIR and supports keyword search and retrieval of rows obliviously over relational tables.
Femur
(
Zhang et al. 2025
)
lets clients trade bounded privacy loss for better performance in public key-value stores.
These contributions focus on protecting the query (user privacy), and the user is still free to infer the rest of the data beyond her query results.

Symmetric Private Information Retrieval (SPIR)
provides
two-sided
privacy, both data and user privacy, preventing either party from learning more than necessary
(
Gertner et al. 1998
)
.
The aforementioned systems in this section do not deliver end-to-end SPIR. In the classical, information-theoretic setting, SPIR is achievable only with multiple servers that share common randomness (a shared random string) unknown to the user
(
Gertner et al. 1998
)
.
Recently, practical SPIR has become feasible via homomorphic encryption
(
Lin et al. 2022
)
. By contrast, quantum protocols can eliminate the need for shared randomness in the multi-server model
(
Kerenidis and De Wolf 2004
)
. Moreover, quantum private queries (QPQ)
(
Giovannetti et al. 2008
)
offer cheat-sensitive privacy and have been demonstrated experimentally using quantum key distribution (QKD) systems
(
Jakobi et al. 2011
)
.

In this work, we propose a novel quantum database design
paired with standard RDBMSs. Unlike cryptographic SPIR (homomorphic encryption) and network-centric quantum key distribution/quantum private queries, we realize SPIR as a database primitive:
We encode the data and query as a Quantum Random-Access Code; a client-side measurement retrieves only a selected tuple and irreversibly destroys residual information, achieving SPIR and bringing two-sided privacy to database systems.

3.2.
Quantum Measurement and Mutually-Unbiased Bases

In quantum mechanics, an
n
-qubit measurement basis
V=\bigl\{\,\ket{v_{i}}\;\big|\;i=0,1,\dots,2^{n}-1\bigr\}
is a complete set of
2^{n}
orthonormal quantum states of
n
qubits, with
\langle v_{i}|v_{j}\rangle=\delta_{ij}
, where
\delta_{ij}=1
if
i=j
and
0
otherwise. This basis defines a type of projective (strong) measurement
\mathcal{O}_{V}
with distinct eigenvalues
\lambda_{i}
for respective eigenvectors
\ket{v_{i}}
. This projective measurement collapses
n
-qubit quantum states
\ket{\psi}
into
\ket{v_{i}}
with probability
p_{i}=|\braket{v_{i}|\psi}|^{2}
, with the measurement apparatus registering which collapse happened. A strong measurement
\mathcal{O}_{V}
can be also defined by a collection of
weak
measurements
\{\mathcal{O}_{j}\}_{j}
which individually collapse quantum systems into Hilbert subspaces instead of sharp quantum states, but collectively define a sharp collapse
(
Wiseman and Milburn 2009
)
.

Two quantum measurement bases
V=\{\ket{v_{i}}\}
and
W=\{\ket{w_{j}}\}\ (i,j=0,1,\dots,2^{n}-1)
are
Mutually Unbiased Bases (MUBs)
if the probability distribution of measuring any
v_{i}
in the
W
basis is uniform. That is,
|\langle v_{i}\mid w_{j}\rangle|^{2}=\penalty\ \frac{1}{2^{n}},\ \forall i,j
. Additionally, if
V
and
W
are MUBs, they should have no intersection when expressed as sets of observables. In this manner, MUBs define quantum measurements with disjoint informational content. Systems of
n
qubits have up to
2^{n}+1
MUBs. More generally,
d
-level systems with prime-power
d
have up to
d+1
MUBs (
n
-qubit systems have
2^{n}
levels)
(
Wootters and Fields 1989
)
.

3.3.
Quantum Random-Access Codes

An
m\rightarrow n
Quantum Random-Access Code (QRAC) is a protocol which encodes
m
bits into an
n
-qubit quantum system, with
m>n
. QRACs maximize the success probability of accessing any of the
m
bits via measurements on the quantum system. High-dimensional QRACs typically make use of the
2^{n}+1
available MUBs, by constructing a nearby and equidistant quantum state to basis-states of all MUBs. This allows to maximize the probability of collapsing into a desired state of each MUB
(
Ambainis et al. 2009
)
.

For instance, consider a
3\rightarrow 1
QRAC, where
3
bits are encoded into a single-qubit. The MUBs of a single-qubit system are given by the eigenvectors of the Pauli Observables
X
,
Y
and
Z
. Encoding bits ‘000’ means finding an intermediate state between the first eigenvector of
X
, the first eigenvector of
Y
and the firsy eigenvector of
Z
, that is, an intermediate state between
\ket{+}=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\ket{0}+\ket{1})
,
\ket{L}=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\ket{0}+i\ket{1})
and
\ket{0}
. This can be visualized as one corner of a cube embedded within the Bloch Sphere, showcasing a bias towards
\ket{+}
when measured in the
X
-basis, towards
\ket{L}
when measured in the
Y
basis, and towards
\ket{0}
when measured in the
Z
basis (see Fig.
2
). Specifically, these collapses occur with probability
0.789
, the
success probability
of the
3\rightarrow 1
QRAC.

3.4.
Private and Restricted Database Queries

We propose a private and restricted database query protocol based on the destructive nature of quantum measurement.
A high-level illustration of our proposed approach is depicted in Fig.
3
. We present an overview of its steps in the following.

(1)

Alice has a database of size
R\times C
, where
R
is the number of rows (keys) and
C
(binary-column) is the binary size of the database rows. Bob only knows the database keys. Alice and Bob agree beforehand on how to assign the database keys to a list of
n
-qubit Mutually Unbiased Bases (MUBs), such that each basis corresponds to one row of the database.
In relational databases, redundancy is often minimized through normalization. Here, our choice of MUBs serves a similar purpose; it maps the classical row to its quantum basis only once, which reduces the redundancy of encoding classical data.
The number of rows must satisfy
R\leq 2^{n}+1
.

(2)

Alice encodes bits
0
through
n-1
of each row
r
into a corresponding MUB-state
\ket{\psi_{r}^{0}}
(
r=0,1,...,R-1
), and computes a pure equidistant state
\ket{\psi^{0}}
to all of them, storing it mathematically (not physically). The same process is repeated for bits
n
through
2n-1
, and so on until the database has been fully encoded into states
\ket{\psi^{i}}
with
i=0,1,...,\left\lceil C/n\right\rceil
.

(3)

Bob requests a restricted and private query of the database. Alice agrees and sets Bob’s query limit to
k
copies of each state
\ket{\psi^{i}}
.

(4)

Alice prepares and sends to Bob
k
copies of each quantum state
\ket{\psi^{i}}
, classically communicating their indexes
i
. To do this, Alice must make use of an appropriate quantum hardware and quantum circuit setup.

(5)

Bob selects
M
MUBs to measure, splitting the
k
copies of each kind of quantum state among those measurements (it is convenient for
k/M
to be an exact odd number to maximize retrieval fidelity). He notes down his measurement results.

(6)

Using majority rules between his multiple measurements, Bob reconstructs a
M\times C
fragment of the original database from his measurement outcomes, with fidelity inversely dependent on
M
: the number of database keys he attempted to query.

Data privacy is preserved, as Bob performed a query restricted by the number of state copies
k
sent by Alice. If he tries to query additional database rows, the fidelity of the retrieved records drops, and potentially becomes unusable.
Meanwhile, user privacy is also preserved, as Alice cannot determine which data fragment was retrieved by Bob. Because this information is never shared between them, not even in an encrypted manner.

3.5.
Quantum States in RDBMS

We leverage relational databases to store metadata such as circuit parameters for preparing qubit states in our proposed quantum protocol. Alternatively, one can precompute qubit states and store their classical representations directly in the relational databases. The most commonly used classical representations of quantum states relevant to our work include the state vector, tensor networks, decision diagrams, and the stabilizer formalism. 3.6.
Hybrid Approach

Fig.
3
sketches our hybrid architecture. Most classical data remains in conventional RDBMSs, which handles updates, schema evolution, and transactions. Because quantum resources in NISQ era are still costly, only a
hot subset
of chosen tuples is off-loaded to the quantum back-end. This split preserves ACID guarantees while exploiting the quantum advantage brought by our quantum protocol.

The DBMS streams tuples to the quantum server, which executes a three-stage pipeline (Sec.
3.4
): key–basis mapping deterministically assigns each primary key to a mutually unbiased basis;
the quantum encoder packs selected bits into
n
-qubit QRACs; and qubit preparation instantiates the states on hardware and sends
k
copies to the client. Qubit states and protocol parameters are checkpointed in the relational databases (Sec.
3.5
), enabling job resumption without full re-preparation and paving the way for cost-based optimization across classical and quantum workloads.
The client executes Bob’s restricted query by choosing at most
M
measurement bases.
Measurement outcomes are post-processed locally through majority voting and then reconstructed into an
M\times C
fragment of pure classical bits. The fragment is surfaced to the application as an ordinary table-valued function, making the quantum round-trip transparent to user-level SQL. The classical channel sends metadata (e.g., the copy count
k
), and quantum channel sends the database as qubit states; thus, the server learns nothing about which rows were actually requested.

The quantum states sent by Alice in our database query protocol can be generated with the general quantum circuit in Fig.

Superconducting circuits via cloud quantum computing are an appropriate hardware to test the quantum-state generation and measurement in the protocol, but photonic systems are better suited to implement a working proof of principle where Alice and Bob are in different locations. For this end, we can either employ two-photon systems, encoding each of the two qubits of the protocol in their polarization degree of freedom, or we can alternatively employ single-photon systems, encoding one qubit in the polarization degree of freedom and the other in the spatial-mode degree of freedom, as done in

5.
Conclusion

One of quantum computing’s most immediate benefits to data management is privacy. We propose a quantum database design that achieves both user and data privacy by encoding relational tuples as Quantum Random Access Codes over Mutually Unbiased Bases. Beyond this protocol, we outline a system-level blueprint that embeds quantum privacy primitives into existing DBMS engines, paving the way for practical, application-driven quantum database systems.
