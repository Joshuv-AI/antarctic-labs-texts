Machine-learning-based cardiovascular mortality prediction using a cumulative PM2.5 exposure metric in high risk groups: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Agata M. Wijata, Gregory Y. H. Lip, Anna Kurasz, Michał Święczkowski, Piotr Jemielita, Paweł Kralisz, Sławomir Dobrzycki, Jakub Nalepa, Łukasz Kuźma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Classical risk factors for cardiovascular disease (CVD) are well established. Although the association between air pollution (AP) and CVD is also well recognized, its integration into cardiovascular mortality risk prediction models remains limited, particularly in the context of machine learning approaches. We conducted a study within the prospective EP-PARTICLES cohort to quantify the improvement in CVD mortality risk prediction achieved by integrating environmental factors into prediction models using the ‘ePM-years Index’ which reflects the proportion of cumulative long-term PM2.5 exposure exceeding a reference threshold relative to total exposure and advanced machine learning (ML) techniques. The study included 6935 patients (mean age 64.5 ± 10.1 years; 46% women) with a median observation period of 3140 days (IQR 2377–4169 days). We analysed 21 classical risk factors with CV death as the primary endpoint. The ‘ePM-years Index’ was calculated for each patient. The χ2 test and Monte Carlo strategy selected the most discriminating predictors. ML techniques were applied to improve the accuracy of mortality risk predictions and to identify key risk factors. Among the patients, 2646 (38.2%) had prevalent CVD, and 927 (13.4%) died from CVD. Feature selection identified 14/21 (66.7%) most discriminative predictors. The best ML model without the ePM-years Index achieved the Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC) of 0.281 ± 0.021, correctly classifying 72.59% of patients. Adding the ‘ePM-years Index’ improved the ML model’s performance to an MCC of 0.657 ± 0.027, correctly classifying 92.75% of patients. The Area Under the Curve for the ML model exploiting the ‘ePM-years Index’ ranged from 0.88 to 0.91. Long-term exposure to PM2.5 was associated with higher CVD mortality risk in high risk groups. ML incorporating PM2.5 demonstrated greater predictive abilities and clinical utility. Including environmental factors into CVD mortality risk scales improves risk stratification, and ML tools can help build simpler, more clinically useful models.Trial registration The study was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT05198492).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-54080-4.

Introduction

Cardiovascular diseases (CVD) have persistently been the leading cause of death worldwide1. Despite major advancements in various branches of medicine in recent years, encompassing both therapeutic methods and initiatives aimed at enhancing the effectiveness of primary and secondary prevention strategies, the healthcare burden of CVD remains immense, with a major impact on populations globally. Data from 2021 indicates that in the European Union, CVDs were responsible for 1.7 million deaths, resulting in a loss of 1.3 million working years2. The total cost of CVD in the EU in 2021 was €282 billion, with an estimated 7.5 billion hours of unpaid care provided by relatives of CVD patients2.

The risk factors for the development of CVD and mitigation strategies have been the focus of research for many years. Classical risk factors include hypertension, hyperlipidemia, diabetes, smoking, and chronic kidney disease; however, increasing attention is being paid to non-conventional CVD risk factors, such as air pollution (AP), whose detrimental effects are being confirmed by a growing body of research3–9. In 2019, for example, AP was linked to 20% of deaths caused by CVD, surpassing traditional risk factors such as high Low-Density Lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol levels and kidney dysfunction10.

Incorporating environmental exposure, particularly AP, into risk assessment may improve preventive strategies and cardiovascular risk prediction models. However, widely used clinical models consider individual clinical risk factors but do not account for significant environmental factors, such as AP.

There is growing interest in developing indicators that reflect the impact of environmental exposures on human health3,8,11,12. In the context of CVD, identifying precise indicators of exposure to these factors is crucial for understanding and predicting risk. We recently proposed the concept of the ‘ePM-years Index’13, which represents the proportion of cumulative long-term PM2.5 (particulate matter with an aerodynamic diameter ≤ 2.5 μm) exposure that exceeds a reference level, given recent studies showing how the integration of environmental factors into CVD risk scales improved risk prediction3,8,11. Particulate matter (PM) is a heterogeneous mixture of solid and liquid particles suspended in the air and represents the key component of AP due to its widespread monitoring and environmental relevance. Its fine fraction—PM2.5, shows strong correlations with other environmental indicators and consists of sulfates, nitrates, organic aerosols, and trace metals capable of penetrating deep into the alveoli, leading to significant cardiopulmonary effects14. Our ‘ePM-years Index’ is one such indicator of long-term exposure to PM2.5, providing valuable information on the relationship between the environment and CV health.

Machine learning (ML) is a branch of artificial intelligence that employs statistical techniques to enable computers to learn from data without explicit programming for specific tasks. In this study, we applied a range of predictive models to assess CVD mortality, utilizing both traditional statistical approaches and advanced machine learning (ML) ensemble techniques15. These ensemble methods, such as Random Under-Sampling Boosting and Gentle Adaptive Boosting16, were specifically selected for their ability to handle imbalanced clinical datasets. To ensure the clinical interpretability of these models, the Shapley additive explanations (SHAP) method was employed to quantify feature importance17. A detailed description of these computational techniques is provided in the “Materials and methods” Section.

Various ML methods have found increasing applications in medicine, such as assessing stroke risk in non-anticoagulated patients with and without atrial fibrillation18, predicting cardiovascular risk based on easy-to-acquire demographic and clinical features19–22, and on modelling of irregularly repeated electronic health records24, as well as developing various risk calculators23. These methods, however, did not exploit any environmental factors which directly influence patients’ health. Evidence combining ML with environmental exposures in cardiovascular research is still limited, although some initial studies have begun to explore this area. A recent ML study based on National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey data incorporated environmental exposure to volatile organic compounds, assessed using urinary metabolites, to predict CVD risk in a cross-sectional population-based sample. However, this approach relies on biomonitoring data and cross-sectional analysis, limiting its scalability and applicability for population-level or longitudinal mortality risk prediction25. More recently, ML models incorporating environmental data have also been applied to predict mortality in acute coronary syndrome patients using short-term AP exposure26. However, these approaches rely on short-term exposure metrics and event-based prediction, limiting their applicability for long-term population-level cardiovascular mortality risk assessment. Within the ML-PARTICLES cohort, we aimed to investigate the utility of integrating one of the most harmful environmental factor—PM2.5, into CVD mortality risk scales using the ‘ePM-years Index’ and advanced ML techniques.

Materials and methods

Study design

The ML-PARTICLES cohort consists of consecutive patients without previously established chronic coronary syndrome (CCS), who were recruited at the Medical University of Bialystok, Poland, from those scheduled for planned coronary angiography due to exacerbated angina without a confirmed diagnosis at admission (recurrent chest pain, classical stable angina, long history of chest pain/angina, or other symptoms such as dyspnea) between 1 January 2007 and 31 December 2016. We excluded patients with: (1) acute coronary syndromes, (2) Takotsubo cardiomyopathy, (3) a history of CCS, (4) referral for coronary angiography prior to heart valve surgery, or (5) any previous cardiosurgical procedures. As this was a retrospective analysis of consecutive clinical admissions, no formal response rate applies. Details of the primary cohort have been described in detail in previously published work27.

The extracted variables included demographic data, medical history, physical examination, laboratory tests, resting electrocardiogram, routine transthoracic echocardiogram, and coronary angiogram. All patients were assigned to PM2.5 exposure levels based on their residential addresses (zip code). The follow-up process was two-tiered: in the first stage, coronary angiography results and treatments were assessed; in the second stage, cardiovascular mortality was evaluated as of December 31, 2022. The final cohort consisted of 6935 patients (for details see Figs. 1 and S1).

The study protocol conformed to the ethical guidelines of the 1975 Declaration of Helsinki and the STROBE guidelines (checklist available as Table S1) to ensure transparency and reproducibility of the results. Additionally, it was approved by the Bioethics Committee of the Medical University of Bialystok (approval number APK.002.81.2022). Informed consent was waived by the Bioethics Committee of the Medical University of Bialystok due to the retrospective, observational nature of the study, which was based on anonymized documentation. The study was financed from the funds of the National Science Center granted under contract number UMO-2021/41/B/NZ7/03716 and Medical University of Bialystok contract numbers: UMB-B.SUB.23.509, UMB-B.SUB.23.290, UMB- B.SUB.23.102, and registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT05198492). Conceptual framework for predictor selection: The selection of predictors in this study followed a modular conceptual framework known as the EP-SCORE strategy. This approach utilizes the established clinical predictors from the European Society of Cardiology SCORE2 and SCORE2-OP frameworks as its foundation, ensuring that the model accounts for well-documented biological pathways of cardiovascular mortality, including demographics, metabolic markers, and pre-existing conditions. Within this framework, the ‘ePM-years Index’ was integrated as an environmental risk modifier, reflecting the cumulative burden of long-term PM2.5 exposure. This is conceptually analogous to ‘pack-years’ in smoking research, capturing the time-dimension of exposure history that traditional markers often overlook. From an initial set of 21 clinically-justified variables, we applied a Monte Carlo-based feature selection strategy to identify the 14 most discriminative predictors, balancing theoretical relevance with computational efficiency and model stability.

Definitions of diseases and conditions

Diagnosis of CCS and indication for Percutaneous Coronary Interventions (PCI) were performed according to the current European guidelines. Significant stenosis of the coronary vessel was defined as more than 50% in the main stem of the left coronary artery and 70% in the other vessels. Left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) was assessed in transthoracic echocardiography using the modified biplane Simpson’s method and was derived following the current recommendations of the European Society of Echocardiography. Heart failure (HF) was defined according to the then-current guidelines as LVEF ≤ 40%, corresponding to HF with reduced Ejection Fraction (HFrEF). Atrial fibrillation (AF) was defined as the presence of AF on an electrocardiogram during the index hospitalization and/or as indicated by a diagnosis found in medical records, the hospital inpatient database, or outpatient databases. Biochemical tests were performed fasting as routine during hospitalisation. Smoking status was determined based on the interview and the patient’s declaration at the time of discharge from the hospital. Concomitant diseases were defined via diagnosis codes in medical history.

The primary outcome was cardiovascular mortality, assessed up to 31 December 2022, defined using pre-specified broad cause-of-death groupings. Specifically, this included ischemic heart disease (I20–I25), HF-related diagnoses (I50, I11.0, I13.0, I13.2, I42–I43), cerebrovascular disease (I60–I69), as well as R96.0–R96.1, consistent with established broad cardiovascular outcome definitions.

Environmental exposure assessment

Initial data were collected from local environmental stations for the entire cohort. Subsequently, in collaboration with the Institute of Environmental Protection – National Research Institute, we used the GEM-AQ (Global Environmental Multiscale – Air Quality) model for further calculations, in order to complement ground monitoring data28. The GEM-AQ model is an advanced numerical tool for air quality modelling across various scales – from local to global. PM2.5 concentrations were computed using community resolution grids, with hourly outputs from the surface layer aggregated to annual averages. Exposure assignment was based on a harmonised framework combining ground monitoring data with the GEM-AQ model, rather than relying solely on the nearest monitoring station, in order to reduce distance-related exposure misclassification and ensure spatial consistency across the study area. This modelling approach was selected to address heterogeneous monitoring coverage and to provide complete exposure estimates for all participants. A single, internally consistent exposure framework was prioritised over combining multiple data sources, thereby minimising potential bias and preserving the full analytical cohort.

Due to the lack of reliable historical data, exposure assessments were limited to the period from 2001 onwards. The postal code of the patient’s residence was used to link the individual PM2.5 exposure at the time of study inclusion. Our quantification of long-term exposure assessment was defined as the ‘ePM-years Index’, which is estimated by multiplying the number of years spent in a given environment by the average annual PM2.5 concentration.

‘ePM-years Index’ captures the cumulative burden of PM2.5 above a reference concentration over the observation period. For each participant and each calendar year y, we first derived the annual mean PM2.5 concentration cy. The index therefore represents the percentage of an individual’s cumulative PM2.5 exposure that occurred above this reference level. Unlike direct annual PM2.5 measurements, which describe exposure in a single year, the ‘ePM-years Index’ condenses the entire multi-year exposure history into a unitless measure that emphasizes years with concentrations exceeding the health-based benchmark (index = 0% if all years are ≤ C ref, and approaches 100% when virtually all exposure occurs above cref. In the primary specification we set cref. Retrospectively, this value corresponds to the former WHO annual guideline for PM2.5 (2005 Air Quality Guidelines) and, after the 2021 update, to the most stringent interim target (IT-4) in the revised WHO guidance. This choice provides a consistent reference level over the whole 2001–2022 period despite the change in guideline values. The reference threshold used in the ePM-years Index does not represent a biologically safe level of exposure; rather, the WHO guideline value was selected as a pragmatic benchmark to ensure methodological consistency and to enable a standardised, clinically interpretable quantification of cumulative excess PM2.5 burden. Statistical analysis

The assessment of variable distribution was conducted through the Shapiro-Wilk test. For continuous variables, the presentation was in the form of mean values with standard deviation (SD) and median (Me), while categorical variables were presented as counts (N) and proportions (%). Since the variables did not follow a normal distribution, the two-tailed Mann-Whitney U-test (non-parametric test) was used for the comparative analysis of continuous data. For the categorical variables’ comparison, the chi-square (χ2) test was applied. The two-tailed p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Machine learning techniques

The most discriminating predictors across all available features with differentiated outcomes were selected using the χ2 test (for the categorical predictors) and two-tailed Mann-Whitney U-tests (for continuous predictors, as they do not follow the normal distribution), following the Monte Carlo strategy. The stability of these features was ensured by repeating the simulation 1000 times. In each experiment, 80% of all patients were randomly drawn, for which the most discriminating predictors were selected. The final set of features included the features that were identified as important in all independent Monte Carlo repetitions.

An array of ML models was used to classify patients according to the CVD death outcomes. These models included LR, decision trees, and random forests (RFs), as well as the ensemble approaches intrinsically tackling the training data imbalance issues. The latter encompassed the Random Under-Sampling Boosting classifier (RUSBoost), which benefits from under-sampling the majority class and the Gentle Adaptive Boosting classifier (GentleBoost)29,30. GentleBoost is a variant of a widely-adopted AdaBoost classifier – notably, it exploits weighted least-squared regression to minimise the error at each step, instead of a log-ratio approach to significantly improve the optimisation31–33. To explain the contribution of the individual features to a prediction for each patient; therefore, to effectively quantify their importance, we used the Shapley additive explanations (SHAP) built upon the Shapley values which are rooted in the coalition game theory34,35. This method has been proven consistent and adaptable to a variety of ML models36.

The entire dataset of patients enrolled into this study was split into two non-overlapping subsets, Dataset A and Dataset B, with the former including the patients without any missing data (NA = 4993, 72% of all patients enrolled into the study), whereas Dataset B containing those patients, for whom at least one parameter was missing (NB = 1942 patients, 28%). To verify the generalizability of ML models, we followed a five-fold cross-validation strategy with stratification within Dataset A, in which NA = 4993 patients were split into five non-overlapping folds stratified according to the primary endpoint distribution. In this strategy, the ML models are independently trained five times, and their generalisation is validated over a test fold, and each fold acts as the test fold exactly once. Of note, Dataset B was kept aside this procedure, and it acts as an independent hold-out test set providing the estimate of the performance of the ML model operating on the predictors that are never missing in Dataset B. Importantly, in both validation scenarios (cross-validation over Dataset A and the final validation of ML models over Dataset B), we never used data imputation, to ensure that the insights are learned from the real clinical data, and they are not affected by synthesised predictors (even following the same distribution as the original data).

The classification abilities of ML algorithms are quantified using sensitivity, specificity, the percentage of correctly classified patients, and the Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC), with the latter metric being the most reliable for imbalanced classification38. Additionally, the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve was determined and the area under this curve (AUC) along with a 95% Confidence Interval (95%CI) was calculated for each test fold. To visualize the feature importance quantified using the Shapley values, we used the summary plots, combining the feature importance with its effect. Finally, the clinical utility of the models was investigated using decision curve analysis (DCA).

All analyses were performed using MS Excel (Microsoft, 2023, version 16.78.3, Redmond, WA, USA), Stata Statistical Software, (StataCorp, 2023, version 18, TX, USA) and MATLAB (The MathWorks, Inc., 2024, version R2024a, Natick, MA, USA). The list of functions that were used for implementing ML models is as follows: fitctree, fitclinear, fitcensemble, templateTree, and shapley.

Results

We studied 6935 patients (46% women) with a mean age of 64.5 ± SD 10.1 years (median 65 years, min-max: 57–72 years). The data were collected in 2007–2016, with a median patient observation window of 3140 days (min-max: 2377–4170 days). The flowchart of patient recruitment is presented in Fig. 1. The ‘ePM-years Index’ had a mean value of 17.2, SD of 2.1, ranging from a minimum value of 11.3 to a maximum value of 51.5 (Table S2 and Fig. S1). Overall, 2646/6935 (38.2%) suffered from prevalent CVD, and 927/6935 (13.4%) died from CVD-related reasons. Full ML models

The traditional LR model, trained on the 13 clinical and demographic predictors, optimized for overall accuracy by defaulting to predicting all patients as alive. This resulted in zero sensitivity and a non-informative specificity, a common outcome for standard LR in the context of class imbalance (13.4% deaths) when no specific reweighting or threshold adjustments are applied. This baseline performance highlights the challenge of predicting infrequent clinical events and justifies the use of ensemble techniques like RUSBoost and GentleBoost, which are specifically designed to tackle such data imbalances. Among the ML models restricted to the same 13 predictors, the best-performing approach was RUSBoost. This model established the baseline performance, achieving a mean MCC of 0.281 ± 0.021 (median: 0.289, IQR: 0.272–0.298) and correctly classifying, on average, 72.59% of patients as either high- or low-risk.

Once the ‘ePM-years Index’ was included in the feature set, the MCC of the best classifier (GentleBoost) increased markedly to 0.657 ± 0.027 (median: 0.674, IQR: 0.630–0.674). In this configuration, in which the ML models operated on 14 predictors, 92.75% of patients were correctly classified on average, with the median exceeding 93%. This model consistently outperformed the remaining classifiers, as quantified by ROC analysis.

Depending on the test fold, its AUC ranged from 0.88 (95% CI 0.86–0.90) in Fold 3 to 0.91 (95% CI 0.89–0.92) and 0.91 (95% CI 0.89–0.93) in Fold 2 and Fold 5, respectively (Fig. 2a). To isolate the predictive contribution of the environmental factor, we performed an internal comparison that demonstrated that the inclusion of the ‘ePM-years Index’ provided a substantial incremental improvement, evidenced by the increase in MCC from 0.281 to 0.657 and the gain in AUC from ~ 0.76 to > 0.88. This suggests that long-term cumulative exposure history contains prognostic information not captured by traditional clinical predictors alone. In contrast, the best ML model based on the 13 predictors (RUSBoost) achieved AUC values ranging from 0.74 (95% CI 0.72–0.77) in Fold 5 to 0.78 (95% CI 0.75–0.80) in Fold 2 (Fig. 2b).

Simpler ML models

The ten most influential features for the best-performing model (GentleBoost working with 14 predictors) are gathered in the SHAP summary plot in Fig. 3. The ‘ePM-years Index’ was consistently indicated as the predictor with the largest effect (i.e., the largest spread of the Shapley values) on the ML model’s predictions (the left panel for each fold). In this plot, each point corresponds to the Shapley value for the predictor and a given (classified) patient, and the colour of this point reflects the value of the feature (from low to high, according to the colour scale). Therefore, the younger patients with lower values of the ‘ePM-years Index’ were commonly less likely to die due to CVD.

Building upon the insights learned from the SHAP summary plot, a set of GentleBoost classifiers operating over only the two most discriminative features (‘ePM-years Index’ and the age at admission) were trained for all training folds. The results presented in Table 2 indicate that the classification performance of such ML models was decreased when compared to their counterparts utilizing 14 predictors, they still obtain high-quality classification, with the MCC of 0.609 ± 0.020 (median: 0.612, IQR 0.601–0.628), being the second largest average MCC over all ML models.

For GentleBoost classifiers operating on 2 discriminators, their AUC ranged from 0.82 (95%CI 0.78–0.85) for Fold 5, up to 0.85 (95%CI 0.82–0.89) for Fold 2 and Fold 3, significantly outperforming all ML models built upon 13 features excluding the ‘ePM-years Index’.

Dataset B

The generalisation of GentleBoost models operating on two predictors was further verified over the hold-out Dataset B. The classifiers trained over the training folds extracted from Dataset A proved their ability to accurately identify high-risk patients by obtaining the MCC score ranging from 0.712 (AUC 0.82, 95%CI 0.78–0.85, Fig. 4a) to 0.738 (AUC 0.83, 95%CI 0.79–0.86, Fig. 4a) over all Dataset B patients (NB=1942) (Table 3). The best classification performance was obtained by a GentleBoost ML model trained over all Dataset A patients, with MCC of 0.745, and 93.87% of patients correctly assigned to the high- and low-risk groups (AUC 0.86, 95%CI 0.84–0.89, Fig. 4b). Discussion

The key findings of our study are as follows: (i) ML models demonstrate superiority over traditional risk scales used in clinical practice; (ii) Including environmental factors, such as ‘ePM-years Index’ in the feature set significantly enhances their predictive abilities; and (iii) ML was useful in building much simpler models that operate on a smaller number of features, which may be easier to obtain, significantly increasing their clinical utility.

In recent years, the concept of the exposome, understood as the cumulative set of all external and internal environmental exposures and their interactions with the human organism across the entire lifetime, along with ML techniques, has begun to emerge in research. For example, one study focused on cardiometabolic risk estimation revealed that an exposome-based ML model outperformed the Framingham risk score in the external validations, although AP was not taken into account during the analysis39.

Similarly, our ML models without including PM2.5 concentrations significantly outperformed widely used scales in cardiology. The Systematic Coronary Risk Evaluation (SCORE), first introduced in 2003, assesses the probability of a fatal cardiovascular event in the population aged 40–65, was one of the pioneering predictive models, and at the time its AUC ranged from 0.71 (95%CI 0.65–0.78) to 0.84 (95%CI 0.79–0.88)40. In 2021, its successors were introduced, SCORE2 and SCORE2-OP, with changes in the included parameters (non-HDL cholesterol instead of the previous total or LDL-cholesterol), extended age ranges to 40–69 years (SCORE2) and 70–89 years (SCORE2-OP) and evaluating risk of fatal and non-fatal cardiovascular events41,42. All three abovementioned predictive models were validated on the Clinical Practice Research Datalink dataset, achieving AUCs of 0.706 (95%CI 0.702–0.709) for SCORE, 0.717 (95%CI 0.714, 0.721) for SCORE2, and 0.646 (95%CI 0.642–0.650) for SCORE2-OP41,42.

The Framingham Risk Score, expanding the range of variables to include hypertension treatment status and diabetes prevalence, demonstrated good calibration and discrimination with c-indices for the risk function ranging from 0.763 (95%CI 0.746–0.780) in men to 0.793 (95%CI 0.772–0.814) in women43. In contrast to the other predictive models, SMART Risk Score considered patients with preexisting CVD and included a broader range of variables, but its validation gave a modest c-index of only 0.675 (95%CI 0.642–0.708)44. The analysis concerning the importance of features and their contribution to the model outcomes revealed a strong influence of PM2.5 quantified by the ‘ePM-years Index’ on the developed predictions. AP poses a particular threat and challenge to public health, as it is the fourth leading cause of disease and death worldwide, with 50% of these deaths being linked to CVD45.

Previous attempts to develop tools for assessing the risk associated with exposure to AP have led to the creation of tools like the Air Quality Index (AQI) and the Air Quality Health Index (AQHI)46. The widely used AQHI in Canada helps assess the risk associated with exposure to AP on a given day, aiding individuals, particularly those vulnerable, in making decisions about outdoor activities; however, there remains a lack of a scale that allows for long-term, individual assessment of the risk associated with this environmental risk factor. In contrast, the ‘ePM-years Index’ allows for the assessment of overall exposure during patients’ lifetime to the most significant environmental risk factor for CVD, i.e., AP (specifically, PM2.5), which has been shown in our previous studies to be its most dangerous component8,47.

To our knowledge, the ‘ePM-years Index’ is one of the first approaches to integrate long-term PM2.5 exposure into cardiovascular mortality risk prediction models using machine learning. The clear benefits of the ‘ePM-years Index’ include its simplicity, as it requires only two variables: postal code and patients’ age. Furthermore, it is up to date by using the latest guidelines from the World Health Organization. Also, it is an independent predictor of mortality associated with HF48. Currently, existing CVD risk prediction models, such as the widely used SCORE2 and SCORE2-OP, do not consider for the impact of AP, only dividing Europe into four risk zones41,42. However, analyses show that variation in AP levels even between neighbouring counties within a single country can be significant8. We believe that the ‘ePM-years Index’ should be incorporated into future CVD mortality risk prediction models to improve risk prediction and did decision-making for primary and secondary prevention. We further emphasize that the ‘ePM-years Index’ is designed as a complementary metric rather than a replacement for conventional exposure indicators, capturing the cumulative and temporally sustained nature of PM2.5 burden. By integrating duration and excess exposure into a single value, it may facilitate both longitudinal risk assessment and clearer communication in clinical and public health settings. Nevertheless, as a single-pollutant construct, it should be interpreted within the broader context of multifactorial environmental risk.

Our findings should be interpreted as proof-of-concept rather than an immediately deployable intervention tool, demonstrating that incorporation of long-term environmental exposure metrics can meaningfully improve CVD risk discrimination. Conceptually, the ‘ePM-years Index’ may function as a modular extension of established risk prediction frameworks, such as SCORE2, forming an integrated EP-SCORE approach (ePM-years + SCORE2). This strategy aligns with current trends in cardiovascular risk modelling, which favour incremental, clinically interpretable add-on enhancements of validated tools rather than complete reconstruction of existing risk algorithms, thereby facilitating translational applicability and future implementation49–52.

The SHAP analysis also allowed the development of ML models operating on only two predictors (‘ePM-years Index’ and age at admission). Such models proved to offer high-quality classification. Since these two predictors are trivial to obtain in everyday practice, this allows for the effective use of ML models without the need for synthetic data imputation. Some of the previously developed methods for cardiovascular risk assessment utilize a small number of predictors while still achieving adequate classification ability, for example, risk assessments related to percutaneous coronary interventions53, post-myocardial infarction risk54, and the Duke Treadmill Score, which uses an exercise test55. In our model, using only two predictors, the fewest among all the mentioned scales, includes the assessment of exposure to PM2.5, and allows for achieving high-quality results in a simple and accessible manner. These findings should be interpreted with caution, as other environmental co-exposures, such as temperature, which may act as confounders or effect modifiers, were not included in the model and could have influenced the observed associations. Importantly, in our previous studies, we have examined the role of other environmental factors, such as temperature, confirming their relevance in cardiovascular risk5,7,8,12,47,56. However, such analyses require a different modelling framework and were beyond the scope of the present study.

Limitations

First, it is a single-centre study, although patients originated from different regions of Poland (Fig. S1); changes in residence were not captured and may have contributed to exposure misclassification. Second, our patient cohort was limited to a single country with specific levels and characteristics of AP. Importantly, PM2.5 is a heterogeneous mixture whose chemical composition varies across geographic regions and over time, depending on emission sources and environmental conditions. Therefore, the health effects associated with a given PM2.5 concentration may differ between settings. This variability may limit the external validity and reproducibility of the ‘ePM-years Index’ when applied to populations with different pollution profiles. Further validation in diverse geographic regions is warranted. Third, reliable data on AP concentrations that allow for an accurate assessment of individual exposure has only existed for the 25 years. Fourth, the study did not examine the impact of various sociodemographic factors, such as income and education, therefore, part of the observed association may reflect residual confounding by unmeasured sociodemographic characteristics. Unmeasured sociodemographic factors may have influenced both exposure and outcomes, potentially biasing the observed associations in either direction. We also did not account for temperature or other environmental co-exposures, which may act as confounders or effect modifiers and could have influenced the observed associations, this should be addressed in future studies. Importantly, participants were recruited among patients undergoing coronary angiography, which represents a clinically selected, higher-risk population. This may have led to an overestimation of risk prediction performance, as models trained in higher-risk populations often achieve better discrimination than in general population settings. This introduces a potential selection/referral bias and limits external validity, as the observed associations may not fully translate to community-based or lower-risk groups. Further studies in more diverse and population-based cohorts are needed to confirm these findings and evaluate their applicability beyond angiography-referred patients. We acknowledge that we did not perform a direct head-to-head comparison with established clinical scores on our specific cohort, nor did we test alternative exposure metrics, as the ePM-years Index was intended as a pragmatic first step to capture cumulative PM2.5 burden. Fifth, exposure was assigned based on baseline residence and that, although residential mobility appears minimal, some degree of exposure misclassification cannot be completely excluded. This misclassification is likely non-differential and may have attenuated the observed associations, potentially underestimating the true effect of long-term PM2.5 exposure. Moreover, our exposure estimates relied on GEM-AQ model fields supplemented by monitoring data, and while such high-resolution inputs are not universally available, this reflects a general limitation of exposure assessment rather than of the ePM-years Index itself. We did not perform sensitivity analyses using alternative exposure datasets, as our aim was to apply a single, internally consistent exposure framework combining monitoring data and GEM-AQ modelling.

Another limitation is that follow-up assessments were conducted only at the end of the study, without intermediate checkpoints. This approach, while focusing exclusively on cardiovascular mortality, may overlook other critical clinical events and disease progression indicators. This could be, however, a very interesting and clinically-relevant research direction, to predict whether the event happens in short, medium, or in a long term. We are also aware of certain methodological limitations of this study. Despite conducting a rigorous multi-fold cross-validation procedure to thoroughly verify the generalisation capabilities of ML models, our results could be further strengthened through large-scale validation on a cohort of patients from different geographic regions. Additionally, we did not perform data imputation in this study. Although we focused on analysing real (non-synthesised) clinical data to ensure that the insights derived from this analysis are not biased by synthetic data injections, this may be considered a weakness of the entire process, as it may be more challenging to apply it to patients with missing data. This issue can be addressed by incorporating data imputation into our modular processing chain, thereby enhancing the clinical utility of the tool.

Conclusion

Long-term exposure to PM2.5, was associated with higher CVD mortality risk in high risk groups. ML incorporating ‘ePM-years Index’ demonstrated greater predictive abilities and clinical utility. Including this environmental factor into CVD mortality risk scales may potentially improve risk stratification, and incorporation of ML tools could help build simpler, more clinically useful models; however, further validation in population-based cohorts is required before broader clinical application.
