Viral Dynamic Model‐Informed Dose Selection of Mosnodenvir Prophylaxis against DENV‐3 in a Controlled Human Infection Model
Authors: Nele Goeyvaerts, Xavier Woot de Trixhe, Martine Neyens, Thomas N. Kakuda, Liesbeth Van Wesenbeeck, Annemie Buelens, Freya Rasschaert, Anna Durbin, Oliver Ackaert
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Dengue virus (DENV) infection is a significant global health threat. Mosnodenvir (JNJ‐64281802) is a pan‐serotype small molecule antiviral blocking DENV replication. A Phase IIa study (NCT05048875) evaluated the antiviral activity, safety, and pharmacokinetics of mosnodenvir as prophylaxis against DENV‐3 in a controlled human infection model (CHIM). A combined population pharmacokinetic (PopPK) and viral dynamic model (VDM) was developed to estimate the prophylactic effect of mosnodenvir and to inform dose selection. The PopPK model is a two‐compartment model based on plasma pharmacokinetic data from three Phase I studies and Cohort 1 of the Phase IIa study. Mosnodenvir exposure metrics were estimated for participants receiving high, medium, or low doses. A VDM with an immune response compartment adequately described DENV‐3 RNA profiles for placebo participants and was subsequently extended by including a sigmoid E max relationship with average mosnodenvir concentration at steady state (C avg,ss) to capture the antiviral effect on virion production rate. Simulations supported daily and weekly mosnodenvir dosing regimens to further characterize the pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic relationship in Cohort 2. Based on the complete Phase IIa study data, the basic reproductive number for DENV‐3 in this CHIM was estimated at 21.7, and the typical C avg,ss required to achieve complete virus eradication was estimated to be 1,410 ng/mL. In conclusion, the combined PopPK and VDM approach effectively informed dose selection and characterized the pharmacokinetic‐antiviral activity relationship, supporting the development of mosnodenvir as a prophylactic treatment for dengue infection.

Dengue is an increasing global health threat with more than half of the world's population at risk of contracting dengue infection. 1 The disease is caused by any of the four antigenically distinct DENV serotypes (DENV‐1, DENV‐2, DENV‐3, and DENV‐4) and transmitted to humans through the bite of an infected Aedes mosquito. 2 Currently, antiviral treatment or a prophylactic drug is unavailable. Preventive vaccines such as Butantan‐DV, CYD‐TDV (for individuals with confirmed prior dengue infection), and TAK‐003 are approved for use in select countries, 1 , 3 , 4 but face weak global market demand. 5 Another vaccine, V181, is under investigation for preventing dengue disease. 6 , 7 , 8

Mosnodenvir (formerly JNJ‐64281802) is an anti‐DENV small molecule targeting the interaction between DENV nonstructural proteins NS3‐NS4B, with potent antiviral activity against all 4 DENV serotypes in nonclinical studies. Mosnodenvir prevents the interaction between NS3 and NS4B, but does not disrupt already established NS3–NS4B complexes. 9 , 10 In a first‐in‐human Phase I study, mosnodenvir was generally safe and well tolerated. 11 Recently, the antiviral activity of mosnodenvir against DENV‐3 infection was evaluated in a Phase IIa (64281802DNG2002) controlled human infection model (CHIM). 12 , 13 In this Phase IIa study, healthy adult participants were inoculated with a recombinant DENV‐3 strain, rDEN3Δ30, 5 days (Cohort 1, C1) or 2 days (Cohort 2, C2) after initiation of mosnodenvir at different dose levels or placebo, administered as pre‐exposure prophylaxis. 12 , 13 The aim of the CHIM study was to assess the antiviral efficacy of mosnodenvir in a prophylactic context, and to evaluate its safety, tolerability, pharmacokinetics, and the relationship between pharmacokinetics and antiviral activity of repeated oral dosing, compared with placebo. C1 established proof‐of‐concept for the superior antiviral activity of the high dose (C1 high dose) vs. placebo and collected data for the characterization of the pharmacokinetic‐antiviral activity relationship for daily dosing based on DENV‐3 RNA. 12 C2 further characterized this relationship using different dosing regimens: C2 daily dose, C2 weekly low dose, C2 weekly high dose, or placebo. 13

Mathematical modeling has served as a valuable approach to decipher and quantify within‐host viral infection dynamics and immune response as well as to guide treatment strategies. 14 , 15 Initial models to describe viral kinetics were target cell‐limited models, where infection dynamics are driven by the availability of uninfected target cells. 16 , 17 These simpler models were then expanded to account for a delay in virus production 16 or to include uninfected target cell production and death as well as an explicit host immune response. 18 Viral dynamic models (VDMs) were applied on pre‐clinical data to estimate the prophylactic inhibitory effect of mosnodenvir in vitro and in vivo. In vitro, the data comprised intracellular and extracellular viral RNA profiles from infection experiments in Vero cells. 19 In vivo, the data consisted of individual‐level virology, serology, and pharmacokinetic results from pre‐clinical DENV‐2 challenge studies in AG129 mice and rhesus macaques. 20

Here, we describe how a combined population pharmacokinetic (PopPK) and VDM based on DENV‐3 RNA was used to estimate the prophylactic effect of mosnodenvir against DENV‐3 in the Phase IIa study, and how model‐based simulations with an interim VDM based on emerging data were used to inform dose selection for C2.

METHODS

Population pharmacokinetic model

Study data

A PopPK model for mosnodenvir was developed based on plasma pharmacokinetic data from three Phase I studies 11 , 21 and C1 of the Phase IIa study 12 (Table S1 ), including single‐ and multiple‐doses administered daily or (twice) weekly. The dataset included 8,352 plasma pharmacokinetic samples from 246 healthy participants. Plasma samples were analyzed to determine concentrations of mosnodenvir using a validated or fully validated liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) method by or under the supervision of Johnson & Johnson. 11 , 12 , 13 , 21 Plasma concentrations of mosnodenvir were determined using a lower limit of quantification (LLOQ) ranging between 0.5 and 5.0 ng/mL.

Model structure

Mosnodenvir pharmacokinetics was adequately described by a two‐compartment PopPK model with a food‐, dose‐, and formulation‐dependent delayed first‐order absorption and first‐order elimination (Figure S1 ). A covariate effect of body mass index (BMI) was included in the relative bioavailability, first‐order absorption rate constant, and apparent clearance. Based on the PopPK model, individual mosnodenvir exposure metrics were estimated for C1. After a predictive check of the PopPK model on C2 (Figure S2 ), a maximum a posteriori estimation using the PopPK model as prior information was used to estimate the individual PopPK parameters for participants included in C2 (MAXEVAL = 0 option in nonlinear mixed effects modeling (NONMEM) software). More details on the PopPK model can be found in the supplementary information.

Viral dynamic model

Study data

Data were taken from the Phase IIa CHIM study, in which healthy participants received multiple oral doses of mosnodenvir as oral capsules administered under fasted conditions in C1 and as tablets administered under fed conditions in C2. Tablets in fed conditions were considered in C2 to maximize the bioavailability and resulting mosnodenvir exposures. In the PopPK model, the relative bioavailability for capsule formulation in fasted conditions was estimated as 0.683 relative to tablet formulation in fed conditions (Table S2 ). The study consisted of two cohorts conducted in a staggered manner: C1: high dose 600 mg daily (QD) loading dose (LD)/200 mg QD maintenance dose (MD) (N = 10) and placebo (N = 6); medium dose 200 mg QD LD/50 mg QD MD (N = 6) and low dose 40 mg QD LD/10 mg QD MD (N = 6) and matching placebo (N = 2) 12 ; C2: one daily‐dose regimen, mosnodenvir 800 mg twice daily (BID) LD/250 mg QD MD (N = 6), and two weekly (Q7D) dose regimens, weekly high‐dose mosnodenvir 450 mg BID LD/1200 mg Q7D MD (N = 6) and weekly low‐dose mosnodenvir 250 mg BID LD/500 mg Q7D MD (N = 6), each vs. matching placebo (N = 2). 13 C1 participants received a QD LD from Day (D)‐5 to D‐1, followed by a QD MD from D1 to D21. C2 participants received a BID LD from D‐2 to D‐1, followed by a QD MD from D1 to D21 or Q7D MD on D1, D8 and D15. Participants were subcutaneously inoculated with 3 log10 plaque‐forming units of the under‐attenuated DENV‐3 strain rDEN3Δ30 22 on D1 (i.e., administered as pre‐exposure prophylaxis).

Blood samples for DENV‐3 RNA assessments were taken at baseline (before challenge on D1) and every 2 or 3 days after challenge with rDEN3Δ30 on D1, up to D21 (last day of dosing for the QD regimens), and less frequently thereafter up to D85. 12 , 13 DENV‐3 RNA serum levels were assessed using a validated quantitative DENV RT‐PCR assay. The upper limit of quantification (ULOQ) of the assay was 109 copies/mL, the LLOQ was 1,000 copies/mL and the limit of detection (LOD) was 36 copies/mL.

Model development

Generally, in a VDM, the within‐host infectious disease system is characterized by the amount of uninfected target cells T, infected cells I and free virus V. Baccam et al. used a target cell‐limited model to describe influenza viral kinetics, assuming that infection is limited by the availability of uninfected target cells. 16 This model is expressed by the following set of ordinary differential equations, dTdt=−βVT dIdt=βVT−kII (1) dVdt=pII−kVV assuming uninfected target cells T can become infected at a rate β proportional to the free virus V, which is produced by infected cells I at a rate pI, and that infected cells I and free virus V decay at first‐order elimination rates kI and kV, respectively (Figure 1 a ).

Schematic of the viral dynamic models (a) target cell‐limited model and (b) immune response model. A, immune response; α, infected cell removal rate; β, infection rate; bT zero‐order formation rate of uninfected target cells; I, infected cells; T, uninfected target cells; pI, production rate of virions; φ, immune cell proliferation rate; V, free virus; kI, infected cell death rate; kT, target cell death rate; kV, virion clearance rate.

To describe DENV RNA data from a clinical trial, including hospitalized patients with primary or secondary DENV‐1 infection, Clapham et al. (2014) 18 extended the target cell‐limited model to an immune response model by including uninfected target cell production and death, as well as an explicit host immune response with a negligible decline over the period under consideration: dTdt=bT−βVT−kTT dIdt=βVT−kII−αAI dVdt=pII−kVV (2) dAdt=φIA assuming that uninfected target cells T are generated at a zero‐order rate bT and eliminated at a first‐order rate constant kT, and that the immune response A proliferates at a rate φ proportional to the number of infected cells I, and clears the infected cells at a rate α (Figure 1 b ).

Based on these models, the basic reproductive number (R0) reflecting the replication potential of a virus within a host, can be derived. Mosnodenvir prevents the interaction between NS3 and NS4B and consequently inhibits the production of virus, which is captured in the VDM as follows: pI↦pI*=1−εppI, with εp∈0,1 the inhibitory drug effect on pI, the production rate of virions. The relationship between the inhibitory drug effect (εp) and effective mosnodenvir concentration conc may be described using a sigmoid Emax model: (3) εpconc=Imax·conchIC50h+conch where IC50 is the effective mosnodenvir concentration at which half of the maximal inhibitory effect Imax is achieved, and h denotes the Hill coefficient, quantifying the steepness of the exposure–response curve. Based on the εp, the effective reproductive number (Re) reflecting the effect of the drug on R0 and the reproduction minimum inhibitory concentration (RMIC), can be derived. For more detailed descriptions of the reproductive numbers and RMIC, please see the supplementary information.

The target cell‐limited model (Figure 1 a ) and the immune response model (Figure 1 b ) were considered as base models to describe the observed DENV‐3 RNA levels in this CHIM. A stepwise approach was used by first building a VDM for participants on placebo and subsequently extending the VDM with the PopPK model to capture the antiviral effect of mosnodenvir. This stepwise approach was applied using data from C1, resulting in an interim VDM (used for simulations to support C2 dose selection) and thereafter the model was updated using data from C1 and C2 combined.

When modeling the natural log DENV‐3 RNA data, the likelihood‐based method (M3 method or Tobit model) was used for samples with undetectable DENV‐3 RNA, assuming these were left‐censored below the LOD, while samples <LLOQ were imputed by 500 copies/mL (LLOQ/2), according to pre‐specified data handling rules. 23 , 24 Model fit was assessed using standard goodness‐of‐fit (GOF) plots and individual fit as a function of time plots.

Simulation

The aim of C2 was to further characterize mosnodenvir pharmacokinetics/pharmacodynamics, that is, the relationship between mosnodenvir exposure and antiviral activity in the DENV‐3 CHIM. The dose selection for C2 was guided by simulations based on the mosnodenvir PopPK model and the interim VDM for C1. These simulations involved 500 study replicates with six participants per dose regimen, exploring different dose levels and maintenance dose frequencies (tablet dosed in fed conditions, 2 days BID LD followed by QD or Q7D MD). In the simulations, the inter‐individual variability (IIV) in the mosnodenvir PopPK model and IIV in the VDM, except for IIV on baseline viral load, were included, while the inter‐occasion variability in the PopPK model, the residual unexplained variability and parameter uncertainty were ignored in the simulations. BMI was considered as a covariate in the mosnodenvir PopPK model, with values randomly sampled from the pooled pharmacokinetics source data file. Simulated mosnodenvir exposure metrics (maximum mosnodenvir concentration (Cmax) and area under the curve during 24 hour (AUC24h)) along with antiviral activity metrics, that is, the number of participants with undetectable DENV‐3 RNA up to D29, AUC under the DENV‐3 RNA viral load curve from immediately before inoculation (baseline on Day 1) until Day 29 (AUCD1‐D29 (VL)) and time to onset of detectable DENV‐3 RNA, were compared across dose regimens. DENV‐3 RNA was simulated up to D85 and for those participants with undetectable DENV‐3 RNA, time to onset of detectable DENV‐3 RNA was right‐censored at D85.

Software

NONMEM software (Version 7.4 and higher, ICON plc, Hanover, Maryland, USA) and the first‐order conditional estimation with interaction (FOCE‐I) estimation option was used for the PopPK analysis, while the Laplace method was used for the VDM analysis. 25 Perl‐speaks‐NONMEM (PsN) version 4.7.0 and higher was used. The NONMEM analysis was performed in a validated environment, based on good automated manufacturing practice and in accordance with 21 CFR Part 11 and good clinical practice regulations. The exploratory analysis and post‐processing of NONMEM analysis results as well as PopPK model simulations were carried out in R version 3.6.2 or higher. 26

RESULTS

Baseline characteristics

In total, 1,047 serum samples (603 in C1, 444 in C2) from 54 participants (29 in C1, 25 in C2) were included in the complete (C1 + C2) analysis. From these serum samples, 69 (6.6%) samples had detectable DENV‐3 RNA ≥LLOQ and ≤ULOQ, 71 (6.8%) samples had detectable DENV‐3 RNA < LLOQ, and 907 (86.6%) samples had undetectable DENV‐3 RNA.

No major difference in demographics was observed between the two study cohorts; only minor differences were observed in terms of race distribution (Table 1 ). The majority was black, of African heritage or African American (48.3%) in C1, while the majority was white, not Hispanic or Latino (72.0%) in C2.

Viral dynamic modeling

Two models are detailed here, an interim model based on C1 data only and used for dose selection of C2, and a model describing the complete C1 and C2 data. The DENV‐3 viral load profile in participants receiving placebo in C1 was adequately described by an immune response model similar to Clapham et al. (Figure 1 b ). 18 To obtain a stable model, some model parameters were fixed (Table S3 ). Parameters T0, kT, kI, and α were fixed based on Clapham et al.'s values. A baseline target cell density T0 was set at 107 cells/mL, assuming a mean lifespan of 7 days for target and infected cells, similar to activated macrophages. 18 The clearance rate of infected cells by the immune response α was assumed to be 0.001/(immune cell * day). 18 The production rate of virions (i.e. free virus) pI was assumed to be 102 copies/mL/(infected cell * day), which is smaller than the value of 104 used by Clapham et al. and consistent with the lower viral load peaks observed for DENV‐3 in this CHIM. The baseline immune response A0 was fixed at 1. While keeping these parameters fixed, the following fixed effect parameters were estimated by fitting the model to the placebo data: V0, β, φ (all log10 scale), and kV. In the placebo model, IIV was quantified additively on the baseline viral load (log10V0) and immune cell proliferation rate (log10φ), (Table S3 ).

When quantifying the antiviral activity of mosnodenvir by fitting the VDM to the complete C1 pharmacokinetics/pharmacodynamics analysis dataset, the VDM parameters were fixed to the parameter estimates obtained for the placebo model. In the VDM for C1 (Figure 1 b ), the mosnodenvir antiviral effect was estimated assuming a sigmoid Emax relationship with the average mosnodenvir concentration at steady state (C avg,SS) (formula (3), Method section). Individual predictions of C avg,SS were derived from the mosnodenvir PopPK model. IC50 and the Hill coefficient h were estimated (Table S3 ; Table 2 ), while Imax was fixed at 1 and IIV was quantified additively on log10IC50.

The interim VDM for C1 showed adequate precision in parameter estimates, with correlations below 0.91 and shrinkage between 20% and 39% (Table 2 ). GOF plots (Figure S3 ) and individual fit plots (Figure S4 ) indicated that the VDM adequately described the DENV‐3 RNA viral load time course for C1, although viral load peaks for medium‐ and low‐dose groups were slightly underpredicted. R0 was estimated to be 28.7 and Re decreased with increasing mosnodenvir C avg,SS (Figure 2 a ). The individual estimates of R e were at or below 1 for those participants with undetectable DENV‐3 RNA. The typical RMIC or critical concentration to achieve complete virus eradication 27 was calculated at 2,180 ng/mL, marking the threshold where R e reaches 1 (Figure 2 a ).

For the C1 and C2 model, the model parameters were re‐estimated in a 2‐step approach by combining data from both cohorts. In a first step the parameters of the placebo VDM were updated based on all participants receiving placebo in 64281802DNG2002. In a second step, the VDM parameters were updated based on all participants in 64281802DNG2002 while fixing VDM parameters from the placebo model from Step 1.

The VDM adequately described the DENV‐3 RNA viral load time course for both cohorts as demonstrated by the GOF plots and individual fit plots (Figure S5 and Figure 3 ). A negative bias was observed in scatterplots of normalized prediction distribution errors vs. population predictions and vs. time since first dose (Figure S5 ) due to left‐censored values (undetectable DENV‐3 RNA samples). However, visual predictive checks confirmed that the VDM could adequately capture the proportion of undetectable DENV‐3 RNA over time (Figure S6 ).

Changes in population parameter estimates when compared to the VDM for C1 included a decrease in R0 from 28.7 to 21.7, a reduction in mosnodenvir IC50 from 263 ng/mL to 200 ng/mL (Table 2 ) as well as a decrease in the RMIC from 2,180 ng/mL to 1,410 ng/mL (Figure 2 b ). Relatively high shrinkage on the IIV random effects was observed, ranging from 41% to 47% (Table 2 ).

Dose selection C2

Simulations based on the PopPK model for mosnodenvir and the interim VDM for C1 supported one daily‐dose regimen and two weekly‐dose regimens (low and high dose) for C2, aiming to further characterize the pharmacokinetics/pharmacodynamics relationship of mosnodenvir.

Simulations of plasma concentration‐time profiles showed that predicted mean maintenance concentrations for C2 daily dose were higher than those of C1 high dose (Figure 4 ), complementing C1 daily‐dose regimens to characterize the complete exposure–response range of mosnodenvir. The C2 weekly high‐dose regimen had predicted mean concentrations fluctuating around those of C1 high dose, with higher peak concentrations, and the concentrations were larger than the estimated typical RMIC up to D21. The C2 weekly low‐dose regimen had concentrations between C1 medium‐ and high‐dose groups, further characterizing the pharmacokinetics/pharmacodynamics relationship following weekly dosing. All C2 dose regimens had predicted mean Cmax and mean AUC24h below the pre‐clinical no‐observed‐adverse‐effect level (NOAEL) mean safety thresholds of 11,800 ng/mL and 177,000 ng·h/mL, respectively (Table S4 ).

DISCUSSION

A semi‐mechanistic VDM assuming a sigmoid E max relationship with C avg,SS, adequately described the exposure–response relationship between the pharmacokinetics of mosnodenvir and its antiviral effect observed on DENV‐3 RNA profiles under placebo and different daily‐dose regimens in the CHIM Phase IIa study. Simulations based on an interim VDM for C1 supported the selection of one daily‐dose regimen and two weekly‐dose regimens that were tested in C2 of the CHIM study. The observed number of participants with undetectable DENV‐3 RNA up to D29 in C2 (daily dose: 4/6, weekly high dose: 5/6, weekly low dose: 5/6, and placebo: 0/6) was captured by the simulation intervals for all the dose groups (Table 3 ). 13

The VDM based on all DENV‐3 RNA data from both cohorts entailed a typical RMIC estimate of 1,410 ng/mL, that is the typical mosnodenvir C avg,SS required to bring the effective reproductive number Re below 1 for rDEN3Δ30 in this CHIM. In in vitro experimental infection studies with mosnodenvir in Vero cells, median RMIC estimates of 2.02 nM and 2.43 nM were obtained for the DENV‐2/RL and DENV‐2/16681 strains, corresponding to protein‐binding adjusted RMIC of 48.3 and 58.1 ng/mL, respectively. 19 Median estimates of mosnodenvir RMIC in experimental challenge studies in AG129 mice (immunocompromised model, 28 , 29 DENV‐2/RL strain) and rhesus macaques (naturally susceptible to dengue infection, 9 , 30 DENV‐2/16681 strain) were 537 ng/mL and 236 ng/mL, respectively. Since the RMIC relies on R0, which may vary between DENV serotypes and strains, it is difficult to compare estimates from the current study directly with those from pre‐clinical experiments. Nevertheless, the VDM framework would allow to investigate translatability of antiviral effect across different host species if the same DENV strain would be tested in all species, ideally repeating these experiments for different DENV serotypes. How the mosnodenvir antiviral activity established for rDEN3Δ30 in this CHIM would translate into the field is yet another question, which we aimed to address in a Phase II field study, exploring the prophylactic effect of mosnodenvir on the number of (symptomatic) DENV infections in regions where DENV is endemic. 31

Our estimate of human within‐host R0 (21.7) for rDEN3Δ30 was in a similar range as the estimates of R0 described for the dengue modeling study by Clapham et al. (within‐host R0 estimate was 35 (95% CI: 29, 40) for primary DENV‐1 infection) and for other acute infections, such as SARS‐COV‐2 (within‐host R0 estimates ranged 13.7–14.3). 18 , 32

One of the limitations of this model is that it focuses on serum DENV‐3 RNA while not considering other markers related to DENV‐3 infection. This approach facilitated a timely model‐informed dose recommendation for C2 during a pre‐planned interim analysis. The VDM may be extended in the future by including other markers, such as DENV viremia to inform on infectious virus or DENV immunoglobulin (Ig)M and IgG data to inform the immune response component. 20 , 33

Emerging genetic variations in NS4B were detected in all (18/18) participants with NS4B sequencing data available in the mosnodenvir arms, as compared to 2/12 of the participants in the placebo arms. 12 , 13 Why, at similar mosnodenvir concentrations, some participants had breakthrough infections with DENV‐3 and other participants did not is unknown, but could be related to individual variation in cell physiology or immune response. In the VDM, this heterogeneity was partly captured by the IIV on mosnodenvir IC50. Given that sequencing data are only available in participants with detectable DENV‐3 RNA and emerging genetic variations in NS4B were detected in all participants on mosnodenvir with detectable DENV‐3 RNA, these data were not considered to further inform the VDM. The IIV random effect on IC50 appeared to increase with increasing dose (Figure S7 ). An alternative model using a 2‐component mixture for the inhibitory drug effect with a dose‐dependent mixture probability could alleviate this trend but did not entail a stable model and was therefore not explored further.

In the VDM, mosnodenvir C avg,ss could adequately capture the antiviral effect on the virion production rate across different dosing frequencies. Alternatively, mosnodenvir concentration over time could be considered as pharmacokinetic driver, although this has the limitation of predicting viral breakthrough after treatment stop, because in the VDM free virus V will never be equal to zero. Typically, the latter issue is mitigated by the implementation of a viral eradication threshold. 34 , 35

The CHIM also comes with a couple of limitations, such as the use of an under‐attenuated DENV‐3 challenge strain and the subcutaneous injection of healthy, seronegative participants. To generalize the current observations, the study findings must be confirmed in larger efficacy studies under field conditions where multiple DENV serotypes may co‐circulate, the virus is transmitted by the Aedes mosquito, and participants may be seropositive due to prior infection. 36 , 37 , 38

In conclusion, the combined PopPK and VDM framework proved useful to inform the development of mosnodenvir as dengue prophylaxis by supporting adequate dose selection for a subsequent cohort, quantifying the pharmacokinetics/pharmacodynamics relationship, and deriving important metrics such as R0 and Re as well as critical concentration (RMIC). This modeling framework could also be used to investigate translatability of antiviral effect across different host species and infection conditions (viral challenge study vs. natural infection).
