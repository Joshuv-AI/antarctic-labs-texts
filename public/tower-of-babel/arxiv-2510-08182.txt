Frictional martingale optimal transport and robust hedging
Pratik Rai

Abstract
We study the martingale optimal transport problem with state-dependent trading frictions and develop a geometric and duality framework extending from the one time–step to the multi–marginal setting. Building on the left–monotone structure of frictionless MOT (Beiglböck and Juillet, Ann. Probab., 2016; Henry-Labordère and Touzi, Finance Stoch., 2016; Beiglböck et al., Ann. Probab., 2017), we introduce a convex frictional cost combining proportional bid–ask spreads and quadratic liquidity impacts. The framework extends the martingale Spence–Mirrlees condition to nonlinear frictions and establishes a frictional monotonicity principle. At each time step, the joint distribution between consecutive asset prices exhibits a bi–atomic, monotone geometry: conditional on the current price, the next price lies on one of two monotone branches representing upward and downward rebalancing. A no–transaction region, or trade band, arises where maintaining the position is optimal, while outside the band, transitions follow two monotone graphs whose endpoints satisfy an equal–slope condition balancing continuation value and marginal trading cost. The framework extends dynamically via a recursive identity, ensuring stability and convergence to the frictionless left–curtain limit, and applies to model-independent pricing and robust hedging of path-dependent derivatives.

Introduction

In robust hedging one seeks strategies and price bounds that do not rely on a single probabilistic model but are consistent with primitive market information such as the one–dimensional marginals of the underlying at a finite set of dates. From the viewpoint of an electronic market maker or a high–frequency trading desk, the central operational object is the inventory, i.e., the number of shares (or contracts) held between decision times
t
and
t+1
, and rebalancing consists of executing an order of size
\Delta_{t}
to move the position from
t
to
t+1
. These trades are discrete and incur frictions: a spread–like linear cost proportional to
\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\Delta_{t}\right\rvert}
and a quadratic or transient impact cost proportional to
\Delta^{2}_{t}
. Such costs directly shape turnover and the geometry of optimal rebalancing, echoing classical execution and market–making models in which proportional spreads and quadratic penalties proxy liquidity and impact
[
1
,
3
,
37
,
27
]
. In this setting, superhedging becomes especially relevant because attainable bounds depend not only on the payoff and the marginal constraints but also on the cost of transporting probability mass across states: the frictional penalty induces explicit inaction thresholds (no–trade bands) that identify when the inventory should be left unchanged, and—once these thresholds are crossed—determines target adjustments and order sizes for the next rebalance. Concretely, the linear component governs the width of the no–trade region (where small moves are optimally ignored), while the quadratic component calibrates the magnitude of the optimal jump in holdings when a trade is undertaken. As a result, the pricing problem yields operational prescriptions both for when not to trade and for how far to rebalance when one does.

Mathematically, the robust superhedging problem can be formulated as a stochastic control problem with a martingale state process subject to pathwise constraints. The objective is to maximize the expected payoff
\Phi
of a contingent claim over all martingale measures
\pi
whose marginals
(\mu_{0},\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{N})
coincide with the market-implied distributions of the underlying asset at discrete trading dates. Each marginal
\mu_{t}
represents the
risk-neutral law
of the underlying price at time
t
, recoverable in practice from observed call prices via the Breeden–Litzenberger identity
[
11
]
. In this setting, the superhedging price is the smallest initial capital required to dominate the payoff under all admissible martingale measures—equivalently, the largest value achievable by optimizing
\Phi
over all such models consistent with the observed marginals. This reformulation casts the problem as a martingale optimal transport (MOT) problem, where one seeks a joint law on
\mathbb{R}^{N+1}
with prescribed one-dimensional marginals that maximizes an expected payoff subject to the martingale constraint
\mathbb{E}[S_{t+1}\mid S_{t}]=S_{t}

The dual formulation of this problem admits a natural financial interpretation. It describes the construction of semi–static hedging portfolios combining predictable dynamic strategies in the underlying with static holdings in vanilla options, ensuring that the portfolio’s terminal value dominates the claim across all consistent models. The dual variables—typically a pair of functions

Convex order emerges as the feasibility condition guaranteeing the existence of such martingale measures, while the dual problem expresses the sharpest model-independent price bounds attainable given the marginal information. In continuous time, this construction links directly to the Skorokhod embedding problem, which represents admissible martingale measures as embeddings of Brownian motion with a prescribed terminal distribution

The present work builds on this discrete-time MOT foundation and extends it to incorporate state-dependent frictions, thereby enriching the geometry of optimal couplings. When transaction costs or execution frictions are present, the stochastic control problem is modified by an additional penalty on the displacement of the underlying across periods i.e. a penalty that captures the cost of transporting probability mass in state space. The resulting dual variables not only determine the hedging instruments and trading rates but also encode optimal rebalancing regions and no-trade zones, where marginal costs of adjustment exceed marginal benefits. This extension links robust hedging to modern models of optimal execution and liquidity provision, where the geometry of the optimal coupling translates directly into practical trading prescriptions for when to remain inactive, when to rebalance, and how the scale of the transaction should adjust to the frictional regime.

In this work we focus on the discrete–time setting, where the MOT formulation admits a particularly transparent structure on the real line. At the level of a single time–step, optimizers exhibit a left–monotone support and a bi–atomic disintegration on the active set where transport is required

Linear–quadratic specifications, motivated by spread–dominated costs and transient impact, are canonical in the execution literature

1.1
Contributions

The main contributions of this paper are threefold.

1.

Frictional one time–step geometry and trade bands.

We introduce a frictional extension of the martingale optimal transport problem in discrete time, where transaction costs depend on both state and displacement through a convex function
f^{(a,b)}_{t}(x,v)
.
Under a frictional martingale Spence–Mirrlees (MSM) condition, we establish a
frictional monotonicity principle
showing that any optimizer admits a bi–atomic disintegration supported on two monotone graphs
T_{d}^{(t)}
and
T_{u}^{(t)}
, together with an identity band
B_{t}=\{x\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}h_{t}(x)\in\partial_{v}f^{(a,b)}_{t}(x,0)\}
on which the no–trade condition holds.
The band boundaries are characterized by an equal–slope system balancing continuation values and marginal trading costs, thereby connecting convex analysis, geometry, and trading behavior.

2.

Stability and vanishing–friction limit.

We prove that the family of optimal couplings and endpoints is stable under joint perturbations of marginals and friction parameters.
In particular, when the linear and quadratic friction coefficients
(\alpha_{n},\beta_{n})
vanish, the frictional optimizer converges in law and
L^{1}
to the frictionless left–curtain coupling of
[
7
,
30
]
.
This provides a quantitative bridge between frictional and frictionless martingale geometries and clarifies how transaction costs regularize the support of optimal transport.

3.

Multi–marginal extension and strong duality.

Using dynamic programming and measurable selection, we extend the one–step analysis to a multi–marginal setting, showing that the bi–atomic structure and trade–band geometry propagate across time.
We then establish strong duality between the primal martingale optimal transport problem with frictions and its dual formulation involving semi–static portfolios.

Beyond their theoretical interest, these results provide a rigorous foundation for understanding the interaction between liquidity costs, hedging geometry, and robust pricing. The framework developed here unifies concepts from optimal transport, stochastic control, and execution theory, and opens the way to practical algorithms for frictional calibration and stability analysis in financial markets.

Setup and Problem Formulation

2.1
Discrete-time market model and marginal structure

We consider a discrete-time financial market model defined over a finite time horizon
T>0
, subdivided into
N\in\mathbb{N}
periods. The canonical path space is given by

S_{t}(\omega)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\omega_{t},\hskip 9.24994pt\text{for }t=0,\dots,N,

where each element
\omega=(\omega_{0},\dots,\omega_{N})\in\mathbb{R}^{N+1}
describes a discrete trajectory of the price process. The coordinate process
(S_{t})_{t=0}^{N}
represents the underlying asset price at each time step, and is endowed with the natural filtration
\mathcal{F}_{t}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\sigma(S_{0},\dots,S_{t})
, capturing the available information up to time
t
. We denote by
\mathcal{F}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\mathcal{F}_{N}
the terminal sigma-algebra.

The pricing framework is model-independent in the sense that we do not specify a single probability measure to govern the underlying asset dynamics. Instead, we are only given a sequence of one-dimensional marginal distributions
(\mu_{t})_{t=0}^{N}\subset\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R})
, prescribing the law of
S_{t}
at each time
t
.

Here we recall the assumptions that we place on the marginals:

Assumption 2.1

(Convex order and peacock)
.

A1.

For every
t=0,\dots,N-1
, we assume that

\mu_{t}\preceq_{\mathrm{cx}}\mu_{t+1},

i.e., the sequence
(\mu_{t})
is increasing in convex order. Moreover, there exists
p\geq 2
such that

\int_{\mathbb{R}}|x|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{t}(x)<\infty\hskip 9.24994pt\text{for all }t=0,\dots,N.

A2.

In particular, all
\mu_{t}
have the same mean,
\int x\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{t}(x)=s_{0}
.

The convex ordering assumption ensures the existence of martingale couplings, while the moment condition guarantees sufficient integrability for evaluating the cost function.

Let

\mathcal{M}(\mu_{0},\dots,\mu_{N})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\Big\{\pi\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}(S_{t})_{t=0}^{N}\text{ is a }\pi\text{-martingale and }\mathcal{L}_{\pi}(S_{t})=\mu_{t}\ \forall t\Big\}

(1)

define the set of coupling measures. By Kellerer’s theorem
[
34
]
,
\mathcal{M}(\mu_{0},\dots,\mu_{N})\neq\varnothing
under Assumption
2.1
. This construction essentially reflects the scenario of model uncertainty where only the marginal distributions of the asset price are known, and the hedger seeks robustness over all consistent models.

2.2
Admissible Payoff

The terminal payoff of a derivative contract is modeled as a measurable function
\Phi\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\Omega\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}

To ensure the well-posedness of the robust pricing problem and the validity of the duality arguments, we impose the following regularity and integrability conditions on
\Phi

Definition 2.1

(Admissible Payoff)
.

A function
\Phi\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\Omega\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
is called admissible if:

(i)

For every
\pi\in\mathcal{M}(\mu_{0},\dots,\mu_{N})
, the negative part
\Phi^{-}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\max\{-\Phi,0\}
is integrable, i.e.,

\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[\Phi^{-}]<\infty;

(ii)

\Phi
is upper semicontinuous on
\Omega
and has at most

-order growth:

\exists C>0\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\hskip 9.24994pt\Phi(\omega)\leq C\Big(1+\sum_{t=0}^{N}|\omega_{t}|^{p}\Big)\hskip 18.49988pt\text{for all }\omega\in\Omega.

2.3

2.4
Portfolio Value and Frictional Superhedging Price

In an incomplete market with transaction costs, an investor may hedge a payoff
\Phi
using a combination of static and dynamic trading strategies. The static part consists of positions in a set of European options written on the terminal asset price
S_{N}
, while the dynamic part comprises of predictable rebalancing strategies based on the observed path of the underlying up to each trading date.

Let
\lambda\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be a measurable function and
\lambda(S_{N})
be the European claim, then the initial cost of acquiring this static position is given by

\nu(\lambda)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\lambda(x)\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{N}(x).

(5)

In addition to the static hedge, the investor deploys a dynamic trading strategy
\Delta=(\Delta_{0},\dots,\Delta_{N-1})
as described in Section
2.3
. The initial capital
Y_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
is allocated between financing the static hedge and covering the dynamic trading path. The cumulative value of the strategy at terminal time

Y_{N}^{\mathrm{fric}}=Y_{0}+\sum_{t=0}^{N-1}\Delta_{t}(S_{0},\dots,S_{t})(S_{t+1}-S_{t})+\lambda(S_{N})-\nu(\lambda)-\sum_{t=0}^{N-1}f^{(a,b)*}_{t}~\big(S_{t},\Delta_{t}(S_{0},\dots,S_{t})\big).

Definition 2.2

(Frictional Superhedging Price)
.

Let
\Phi
be the payoff function that satisfies
definition

. The frictional superhedging price of
\Phi
is defined as

\mathsf{SH}(\Phi)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\inf\left\{Y_{0}\in\mathbb{R}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\exists\,\lambda,\,\Delta\text{ s.t. }Y_{N}^{\mathrm{fric}}\geq\Phi\hskip 9.24994pt\text{$\pi$-a.s. for all }\pi\in\mathcal{M}(\mu_{0},\dots,\mu_{N})\right\}.

(13)

Therefore, (

Y_{N}^{\mathrm{fric}}
required to hedge the claim

Here the inclusion of frictions ensures that the replication cost reflects real-world trading constraints and precludes over-optimistic hedging strategies that rely on idealized, costless markets.

2.5
Primal problem

The superhedging problem introduced in Definition
2.2
admits an elegant reformulation as a penalized martingale optimal transport (MOT) problem.

Let
\pi\in\mathcal{M}(\mu_{0},\dots,\mu_{N})
denote a discrete-time martingale measure on
\mathbb{R}^{N+1}
, and let
\pi_{t}
, defined in (
7
), be the marginal law of
(S_{t},S_{t+1})
under
\pi
. The total trading cost associated with a given transport plan
\pi
is defined by
\mathcal{C}(\pi)
as in (
8
). Consequently, in (
8
), each term
f^{(a,b)}_{t}(x,y-x)
encodes the cost incurred when trading from a position at
x
to
y
, and the integrals over
\pi_{t}
aggregate these costs across all possible transitions allowed by the transport plan.

The primal formulation of the robust pricing problem then becomes

\mathbf{V}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\sup_{\pi\in\mathcal{M}(\mu_{0},\dots,\mu_{N})}\left\{\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[\Phi]-\mathcal{C}(\pi)\right\}.

(14)

This variational representation expresses the superhedging value as the maximal expected payoff over all martingale couplings, penalized by the cumulative cost of trading. The term
\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[\Phi]
reflects the potential gain from a successful hedge under the worst-case path scenario, while
\mathcal{C}(\pi)
imposes a penalty for incurring liquidity or execution costs.

Compared to the classical MOT framework studied in
[
5
,
24
]
which assumes frictionless markets, the inclusion of
\mathcal{C}(\pi)
introduces a regularization that penalizes rapid or volatile movements in the price path. This distinguishes our approach from the frictionless setting and aligns it more closely with the real-world trading environment.

2.6
Dual Formulation: From Portfolio Inequality to Strong Duality

We now derive the dual formulation of the superhedging problem under frictions. Recall the expression for the terminal wealth in (
6
) and the superhedging condition

Y_{N}^{\mathrm{fric}}(\omega)\;\geq\;\Phi(\omega)\hskip 18.49988pt\text{for all }\omega\in\Omega.

(15)

Using the Fenchel–Young inequality, we note that for each friction function
f^{(a,b)}_{t}
and its Legendre–Fenchel conjugate
f^{(a,b)*}_{t}
, it holds

f^{(a,b)}_{t}(x,\delta)+f^{(a,b)*}_{t}(x,y)\;\geq\;y\delta,

(16)

with equality if and only if
y\in\partial_{\delta}f^{(a,b)}_{t}(x,\delta)
. Applying this inequality pathwise with
\delta=S_{t+1}-S_{t}
, we obtain:

\Delta_{t}(S_{t+1}-S_{t})-f^{(a,b)*}_{t}(S_{t},\Delta_{t})\ \leq\ f^{(a,b)}_{t}\big(S_{t},S_{t+1}-S_{t}\big).

(17)

We now encode the dynamic trading part via the frictional cost and the static position via a potential. Set
u_{N}=\lambda
and
u_{t}\equiv 0
for
t<N
, and define the pathwise dual functional

\Psi_{u,\Delta}(\omega)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\sum_{t=0}^{N}u_{t}\big(S_{t}(\omega)\big)+\sum_{t=0}^{N-1}\Big[\Delta_{t}(\omega)\,(S_{t+1}-S_{t})(\omega)-f^{(a,b)*}_{t}\big(S_{t}(\omega),\Delta_{t}(\omega)\big)\Big].

(18)

Then the superhedging constraint becomes equivalent to

\Phi(\omega)\;\leq\;\Psi_{u,\Delta}(\omega)+Y_{0}-\nu(\lambda),\hskip 18.49988pt\text{for all }\omega\in\Omega.

(19)

Minimizing the initial capital
Y_{0}
subject to this inequality corresponds to minimizing
\sum_{t=0}^{N}\int u_{t}\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{t}
over all admissible dual variables.

We thus define the dual domain:

\mathcal{D}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\Big\{(u,\Delta)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\ u_{t}\in L^{1}(\mu_{t}),\ \Delta\ \text{predictable},\ \Phi\leq\Psi_{u,\Delta}\ \text{pointwise on }\Omega\Big\}

(20)

and the dual value is given by:

\mathbf{D}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\inf_{(u,\Delta)\in\mathcal{D}}\sum_{t=0}^{N}\int u_{t}\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{t}.

(21)

Theorem 2.1

(Strong Duality with State-Dependent Frictions)
.

Suppose that the marginal constraints satisfy the convex ordering condition of Assumption
2.1
, the claim
\Phi
is admissible in the sense of Definition
2.1
, and the friction cost functions
f^{(a,b)}_{t}
satisfy the convexity and superlinearity assumptions. Then the primal and dual problems satisfy strong duality:

\mathbf{V}=\sup_{\pi\in\mathcal{M}(\mu_{0},\dots,\mu_{N})}\left\{\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[\Phi]-\sum_{t=0}^{N-1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}f^{(a,b)}_{t}(x,y-x)\,\mathrm{d}\pi_{t}(x,y)\right\}=\mathbf{D}.

(22)

Moreover, both extrema are attained.

See section
6
for the proof.

Remark 2.1

(Subhedging with frictions)
.

In parallel with the superhedging problem, one may consider the
subhedging
value of a contingent claim
\Phi
, i.e. the maximal guaranteed initial capital that can be raised by selling
\Phi
while trading dynamically under frictions. Formally, the subhedging problem is

\mathbf{V}_{\mathrm{sub}}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\inf_{\pi\in\mathcal{M}(\mu_{0},\dots,\mu_{N})}\left\{\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[\Phi]+\sum_{t=0}^{N-1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}f^{(a,b)}_{t}(x,y-x)\,\mathrm{d}\pi_{t}(x,y)\right\}.

(23)

This is the natural counterpart to (
14
), with the frictional penalty added rather than subtracted. For the dual problem, the subhedging problem can be expressed as

\mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{sub}}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\sup_{(u,\Delta)}\ \sum_{t=0}^{N}\int u_{t}\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{t},

(24)

where the supremum is taken over pairs
(u,\Delta)
such that

\Phi\;\geq\;\Psi_{u,\Delta}\hskip 9.24994pt\text{pointwise on }\Omega,

with
\Psi_{u,\Delta}
defined in (
18
). As in the superhedging case, convexity and superlinearity of the cost functions
f^{(a,b)}_{t}
ensure strong duality
\mathbf{V}_{\mathrm{sub}}=\mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{sub}}
, and both extrema are attained.

The one time–step geometry under friction

Here we will develop the structure of the optimal martingale coupling for the marginals at time

and

in the presence of frictions
[
6
,
7
,
30
]
. The central tool is an

cost function that combines the continuation value of the payoff with the trading friction.

Fix
t\in\{0,\dots,N-1\}
and abbreviate
\mu\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\mu_{t}
,
\eta\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\mu_{t+1}
. Denote by
X(x,y)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=x
and
Y(x,y)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=y
the coordinate projections and set

\Pi^{M}(\mu,\eta)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\Bigl\{\pi\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\ \pi\circ X^{-1}=\mu,\ \pi\circ Y^{-1}=\eta,\ \mathbb{E}_{\pi}[Y\mid X]=X\Bigr\}.

Under
2.1
, the continuation value
\mathsf{V}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
at time
t{+}1
is defined as

\mathsf{V}(y)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\sup_{\pi^{\prime}\in\mathcal{M}(\mu_{t+1},\dots,\mu_{N})}\mathbb{E}_{\pi^{\prime}}\left[\Phi(S_{0},\dots,S_{N})\ \Big|\ S_{t+1}=y\right],\hskip 18.49988pty\in\mathbb{R},

(25)

which depends only on the next marginal
\mu_{t+1}
and the future marginals

(\mu_{t+2},\dots,\mu_{N})

The adjusted one time–step cost function is defined as

\widetilde{c}_{t}(x,y)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\mathsf{V}(y)-\mathsf{V}(x)-f^{(a,b)}_{t}\big(x,\,y-x\big),\hskip 18.49988pt(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2},

(26)

and consider

\sup_{\pi\in\Pi^{M}(\mu,\eta)}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\widetilde{c}_{t}\,d\pi\;=\;\eta(\mathsf{V})-\mu(\mathsf{V})\;-\;\inf_{\pi\in\Pi^{M}(\mu,\eta)}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}f^{(a,b)}_{t}\big(x,\,y-x\big)\,d\pi.

(27)

Thus the term
\mathsf{V}(y)-\mathsf{V}(x)
contributes an additive constant

\eta(\mathsf{V})-\mu(\mathsf{V})
independent of

\pi\mapsto\int f^{(a,b)}_{t}(x,y-x)\,d\pi
.

3.1
Martingale Spence–Mirrlees (MSM) condition

The one time–step problem relies on a variational comparison over local martingale
competitors and a curvature condition on
\widetilde{c}_{t}
.
This is the one–dimensional monotonicity mechanism of

[
30
,
6
]
, adapted to the frictional setting, where the monotonicity is obtained by testing optimality against elementary rectangle
perturbations among martingale competitors.

Definition 3.1

(Martingale competitors)
.

Let
\alpha
be a finite Borel measure on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
with finite first moments and

x
–marginal
\mu_{\alpha}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\alpha\circ X^{-1}
.
A finite Borel measure
\alpha^{\prime}
on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
is a martingale competitor of
\alpha
if

\alpha^{\prime}\circ X^{-1}=\mu_{\alpha},\hskip 18.49988pt\alpha^{\prime}\circ Y^{-1}=\alpha\circ Y^{-1},

and, for
\mu_{\alpha}
–a.e.
x
,

\int y\,\alpha(\mathrm{d}y\mid x)=\int y\,\alpha^{\prime}(\mathrm{d}y\mid x).

Equivalently,
\mathbb{E}_{\alpha^{\prime}}[Y\mid X]=\mathbb{E}_{\alpha}[Y\mid X]
holds
\mu_{\alpha}
–a.e.

Definition 3.2

(Rectangle MSM inequality)
.

We say that
\widetilde{c}_{t}
satisfies the martingale Spence–Mirrlees condition if,
for all
x<x^{\prime}
and
y^{-}<y^{+}

\Delta^{\square}\widetilde{c}_{t}\big((x,x^{\prime});(y^{-},y^{+})\big)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\widetilde{c}_{t}(x,y^{-})+\widetilde{c}_{t}(x^{\prime},y^{+})-\widetilde{c}_{t}(x,y^{+})-\widetilde{c}_{t}(x^{\prime},y^{-})\ >\ 0.

(28)

When
\widetilde{c}_{t}\in C^{3}
, a sufficient pointwise criterion is

\partial_{xyy}\widetilde{c}_{t}(x,y)\ \geq\ \kappa_{t}\ >\ 0\hskip 18.49988pt\text{for all }(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2},

(29)

see
[
30
, Sec. 3]
,
[
7
]
and
[
8
, Thm. 5.2]
.

Recall that

\widetilde{c}_{t}(x,y)=\mathsf{V}_{t+1}(y)-\mathsf{V}_{t+1}(x)-f^{(a,b)}_{t}(x,y-x),

and write
v\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=y-x
. The separable part
\mathsf{V}_{t+1}(y)-\mathsf{V}_{t+1}(x)
has zero rectangle increment, and differentiating yields

\partial_{xyy}\widetilde{c}_{t}(x,y)=\underbrace{0}_{\text{from }\mathsf{V}_{t+1}(y)-\mathsf{V}_{t+1}(x)}\;+\;(f^{(a,b)}_{t})_{vvv}(x,v)\;-\;(f^{(a,b)}_{t})_{xvv}(x,v).

Thus the frictional MSM requirement ((
28
) or (
29
)) amounts to a positivity condition on the
v
–curvature of
f^{(a,b)}_{t}
relative to its
x
–dependence. In the
x
–independent linear–quadratic model
f_{\alpha,\beta}(v)=\alpha|v|+\beta v^{2}
with
\beta>0
, the smooth curvature vanishes away from
v=0
, yet the rectangle inequality still enforces left–monotonicity; uniqueness may fail when the linear part is active (see the forthcoming
remark

3.1
).

To encode the marginal and martingale constraints at time
t
we use one–dimensional quantile functions which are defined as

F_{\mu}(k)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\mu((-\infty,k]),\hskip 18.49988ptF_{\eta}(k)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\eta((-\infty,k]),\hskip 18.49988ptk\in\mathbb{R},

(30)

and the call–type potential of a measure
\tau
by

\mathcal{C}_{\tau}(k)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}(x-k)^{+}\,\mathrm{d}\tau(x),\hskip 18.49988ptk\in\mathbb{R}.

(31)

If
\mu\preceq_{\mathrm{cx}}\eta
, then
\int x\,\mathrm{d}\mu=\int y\,\mathrm{d}\eta
and
\mathcal{C}_{\eta}\geq\mathcal{C}_{\mu}
. Set

\Delta F_{t}(k)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\mathcal{C}_{\eta}(k)-\mathcal{C}_{\mu}(k)\ (\geq 0),

(32)

which is convex with
\lim_{k\to\pm\infty}\Delta F_{t}(k)=0

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}k}\Delta F_{t}(k)=F_{\eta}(k)-F_{\mu}(k),\hskip 18.49988pt\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}}{\mathrm{d}k^{2}}\Delta F_{t}=\mathrm{d}\big(F_{\eta}-F_{\mu}\big).

(33)

Definition 3.3

(Irreducible components of
\Delta F_{t}
)
.

Let
\mu\preceq_{\mathrm{cx}}\eta
and
\Delta F_{t}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\mathcal{C}_{\eta}-\mathcal{C}_{\mu}
as in (

An irreducible component of

is a maximal open interval

such that

\Delta F_{t}(k)>0\hskip 18.49988pt\text{for all }k\in I.

Equivalently, the irreducible components are the connected components of the open set

\{k\in\mathbb{R}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\Delta F_{t}(k)>0\}
.

Since
\Delta F_{t}
is convex, the set

is open and decomposes uniquely as a countable disjoint union of such intervals. On each component

one has

\Delta F_{t}(a)=\Delta F_{t}(b)=0,

with one–sided limits understood at finite endpoints. Transport between
\mu
and
\eta

the identity coupling is feasible, see

Remark 3.1

(Degenerate MSM and trade bands)
.

Assume the frictional MSM curvature is non–strict

\partial_{xyy}\,\widetilde{c}_{t}\equiv 0\hskip 9.24994pt\text{(for instance, }f^{(a,b)}_{t}(x,v)=\alpha|v|+\beta v^{2}\text{ with no $x$\textendash dependence and }(f^{(a,b)}_{t})_{vvv}\equiv 0\text{).}

Then:

(a) Monotonicity principle
[
30
, §3]
and
[
7
]
.

The variational competitor argument (
41
) still implies that any optimizer is supported on a

\widetilde{c}_{t}
–monotone set. In one dimension,

\widetilde{c}_{t}
–monotonicity rules out crossings. On irreducible components with

\mu
atomless, a left–monotone optimizer exists and admits a bi–atomic disintegration
as in Theorem
3.1
. However, without strict curvature one cannot
exclude additional optimal couplings as some optimizers need not be (globally) left–monotone.

(b) Possible nonuniqueness.

Loss of strict curvature creates flat directions, so uniqueness of the optimizer is not
guaranteed. In particular, the endpoint system consisting of the mass balance

F_{\eta}\bigl(T_{u}^{(t)}(x)\bigr)=F_{\mu}(x)+\Delta F_{t}\bigl(T_{d}^{(t)}(x)\bigr),

together with the equal–slope condition

\partial_{y}\mathsf{V}_{t+1}\bigl(T_{d}^{(t)}(x)\bigr)-\partial_{v}f^{(a,b)}_{t}\bigl(x,T_{d}^{(t)}(x)-x\bigr)=\partial_{y}\mathsf{V}_{t+1}\bigl(T_{u}^{(t)}(x)\bigr)-\partial_{v}f^{(a,b)}_{t}\bigl(x,T_{u}^{(t)}(x)-x\bigr),

may admit multiple solutions compatible with the monotonicity of
T_{d}^{(t)}
and

T_{u}^{(t)}
. Uniqueness can still be recovered under additional conditions, for instance strict monotonicity of

y\mapsto\partial_{y}\mathsf{V}_{t+1}(y)-\partial_{v}f^{(a,b)}_{t}(x,y-x)
on the active range.

(c) Trade bands with proportional costs.

If

has a linear part, then
0\in\partial_{v}f^{(a,b)}_{t}(x,0)
.
The KKT equal–slope condition in subgradient form (
69
) is satisfied by

T_{d}^{(t)}(x)=T_{u}^{(t)}(x)=x
whenever the dual slope
h_{t}(x)\in\partial_{v}f^{(a,b)}_{t}(x,0)
.
In the linear–quadratic case

this yields a no-trade region which is defined by the band

B_{t}=\{\,x\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\ |h_{t}(x)|\leq\alpha\,\},

on which the optimizer keeps mass on the diagonal. More details will follow in
section

4
Explicit transport maps and trade bands

In the presence of trading frictions, optimal martingale couplings develop
no–trade regions
(or
trade bands
) on which the identity coupling is optimal and all mass remains on the diagonal. The mechanism is convex–analytic. Writing the friction in displacement form
v\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=y-x
as
f^{(a,b)}_{t}(x,v)
and denoting by
h_{t}(x)
the dual Lagrange multiplier for the martingale constraint, the Fenchel–Young/KKT optimality conditions (see (
68
)–(
69
)) give

v=0\hskip 9.24994pt\Longleftrightarrow\hskip 9.24994pth_{t}(x)\in\partial_{v}f^{(a,b)}_{t}(x,0).

This motivates the trade band

B_{t}=\bigl\{x\in\mathbb{R}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\ h_{t}(x)\in\partial_{v}f^{(a,b)}_{t}(x,0)\bigr\}.

Inside
B_{t}
the conditional law equals
\delta_{x}
. Outside
B_{t}
, movement occurs and the martingale constraint enforces a bi–atomic kernel supported on two monotone graphs (see
theorem

3.1
); the endpoints are then selected by the equal–slope system (
38
)–(
39
) together with the mass relations (
40
)–(
37
). In the frictionless case
f\equiv 0
, there is no subdifferential dead zone at
v=0
and thus no band. The optimizer reduces to the frictionless left–curtain geometry on each irreducible component
[
7
,
30
]
.

When
f^{(a,b)}_{t}
depends on
x
, the band is state–dependent through the set–valued map
x\mapsto\partial_{v}f^{(a,b)}_{t}(x,0)
. For instance, if
f^{(a,b)}_{t}(x,v)=\alpha_{t}(x)\,|v|+\beta_{t}(x)\,v^{2}
with
\alpha_{t},\beta_{t}\geq 0
, then
\partial_{v}f^{(a,b)}_{t}(x,0)=[-\alpha_{t}(x),\alpha_{t}(x)]
and

B_{t}=\{\,x\in\mathbb{R}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\ |h_{t}(x)|\leq\alpha_{t}(x)\,\}.

The width of the band therefore adjusts with liquidity/impact heterogeneity across
x
. In the
x
–independent linear–quadratic model
f_{\alpha,\beta}(v)=\alpha|v|+\beta v^{2}
, the subdifferential is
[-\alpha,\alpha]
, yielding
B_{t}=\{\,|h_{t}|\leq\alpha\,\}
; off the band, (
69
) gives the explicit displacement rule (
70
).

Band boundaries are free boundaries determined by the contact (equal–slope) system as their location governs where transport is active and where the coupling is the identity, thereby shaping both pricing functionals and hedging policies. Band geometry yields sharp comparative statics in the liquidity parameters: increasing a linear cost widens
B_{t}
, while increasing a quadratic penalty shrinks off–band displacements (see (
69
) and (
39
)). Regularity and monotonicity of the band further underpin numerical stability and convergence, including the vanishing–friction regime of Corollary
3.1
.

From a purely financial viewpoint, trade bands formalize threshold behaviour typical of market makers and high–frequency traders. Small price moves are not traded because expected gains do not offset costs, whereas sufficiently large moves trigger immediate rebalancing along the optimal up/down maps. This has direct implications for robust pricing of path–dependent claims. For lookbacks, wider bands dampen tail–pushing; for barriers, bands near the barrier reduce effective crossing under worst–case transport; for Asians, bands suppress frequent small rebalances and reduce short–horizon dispersion. The remainder of this section derives the dual/KKT system, specializes it to linear–quadratic frictions to obtain explicit off–band maps and band boundaries, and discusses how these features propagate to the pricing of such path–dependent contracts.

.

On

Proof.

Suppose

Proposition 4.1

(Structure of
B_{t}
and off–band maps)
.

Let

\{\Delta F_{t}>0\}

is a Borel subset of

In the linear–quadratic case

which is the specialization of the KKT relation (
70

Proof.

By Lemma
4.2
,

on

For the second item, the complement of a relatively closed subset of an open interval is a countable union of open intervals. Fix an open

Proposition 4.2

(Comparative statics in
(\alpha,\beta)
in the linear–quadratic case)
.

Assume
f_{\alpha,\beta}(v)=\alpha|v|+\beta v^{2}
with
\beta>0
. Fix an irreducible component
I
of
\{\Delta F_{t}>0\}
. Suppose that:

1.

The measures
\mu
and
\eta
are absolutely continuous on the relevant ranges with continuous densities

that are strictly positive there.

2.

On each open interval

, the selection system consisting of the mass identity and the equal–slope condition admits a unique solution
(T_{d}^{(t)},T_{u}^{(t)},\theta_{t})
which depends continuously on
(\alpha,\beta)
, and

is continuous and bounded on the images

and

3.

(Sign and curvature conditions) Along

\Delta F_{t}^{\prime}(T_{d}^{(t)}(x))<0\hskip 9.24994pt\text{and}\hskip 9.24994ptf_{\eta}\bigl(T_{u}^{(t)}(x)\bigr)>0\hskip 18.49988pt\text{for a.e.\ }x\in J,

Then:

1.

The band expands weakly in each parameter: if
\alpha_{2}\geq\alpha_{1}
, then
B_{t}(\alpha_{1},\beta)\cap I\subseteq B_{t}(\alpha_{2},\beta)\cap I
, and if

2.

\frac{\partial}{\partial\alpha}\,|T_{u/d}^{(t)}(x)-x|\ \leq\ 0,\hskip 18.49988pt\frac{\partial}{\partial\beta}\,|T_{u/d}^{(t)}(x)-x|\ \leq\ 0\hskip 9.24994pt\text{for a.e.\ }x\in J.

Proof.

Work on a fixed open
J\subset I\setminus B_{t}
and write
T_{d/u}
for

. By Theorem
3.1
and the potential formalism,

F_{\eta}\bigl(T_{u}(x)\bigr)-F_{\mu}(x)=\Delta F_{t}\bigl(T_{d}(x)\bigr),

(74)

and the linear-quadratic equal–slope condition

Set

and define

\partial_{T_{d}}H_{1}=-\Delta F_{t}^{\prime}(T_{d})=f_{\mu}(T_{d})-f_{\eta}(T_{d})=\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}a_{d}>0,\hskip 18.49988pt\partial_{T_{u}}H_{1}=f_{\eta}(T_{u})=\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}a_{u}>0,

J(x)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\frac{\partial(H_{1},H_{2})}{\partial(T_{d},T_{u})}=\begin{pmatrix}a_{d}&a_{u}\\[2.0pt]
2\beta-\mathsf{V}_{t+1}^{\prime\prime}(T_{d})&\mathsf{V}_{t+1}^{\prime\prime}(T_{u})-2\beta\end{pmatrix}

\partial_{\alpha}T_{u}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
2\end{pmatrix}.

Cramer’s rule gives

\partial_{\alpha}T_{d}=\frac{-\,2\,a_{u}}{\det J}\ \geq\ 0,\hskip 18.49988pt\partial_{\alpha}T_{u}=\frac{2\,a_{d}}{\det J}\ \leq\ 0,

so both displacements

and

are weakly decreasing in
\alpha

\beta

J(x)\begin{pmatrix}\partial_{\beta}T_{d}\\
\partial_{\beta}T_{u}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
2\,(T_{u}-T_{d})\end{pmatrix}\hskip 9.24994pt\Longrightarrow\hskip 9.24994pt\partial_{\beta}T_{d}=\frac{-\,2\,(T_{u}-T_{d})\,a_{u}}{\det J}\ \geq\ 0,\hskip 18.49988pt\partial_{\beta}T_{u}=\frac{2\,(T_{u}-T_{d})\,a_{d}}{\det J}\ \leq\ 0,

and the same monotonicity in

follows. Finally,
B_{t}\cap I=\{x\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\ T_{u}(x)=x\}=\{x\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\ T_{d}(x)=x\}

4.3
Impact on high-frequency trading

We next quantify the operational impact that are directly relevant to high-frequency execution and market-making.

Proposition 4.3

(Turnover representation and bounds)
.

Let
I
be an irreducible component of
\{\Delta F_{t}>0\}
, and let
\pi^{\star}_{t}
be the frictional left–monotone optimizer with binomial kernel (
34
). The one time–step (absolute) turnover is

\mathcal{T}_{t}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\Big[\mathbb{E}\big[\,|Y-X|\,\big|\,X\,\big]\Big]=\int_{I\setminus B_{t}}\Big(\theta_{t}(x)\,\big|T_{u}^{(t)}(x)-x\big|+\big(1-\theta_{t}(x)\big)\,\big|x-T_{d}^{(t)}(x)\big|\Big)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x).

Moreover, on
I\setminus B_{t}
,

\mathbb{E}\big[\,|Y-X|\,\big|\,X=x\,\big]=2\,\theta_{t}(x)\big(1-\theta_{t}(x)\big)\,\Big(T_{u}^{(t)}(x)-T_{d}^{(t)}(x)\Big)\ \leq\ \frac{1}{2}\,\Big(T_{u}^{(t)}(x)-T_{d}^{(t)}(x)\Big),

(76)

and consequently

\mathcal{T}_{t}\ \leq\ \frac{1}{2}\int_{I\setminus B_{t}}\Big(T_{u}^{(t)}(x)-T_{d}^{(t)}(x)\Big)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x).

(77)

In the linear–quadratic case with
\beta>0
and
f_{\alpha,\beta}(v)=\alpha|v|+\beta v^{2}
,

\big|T_{u/d}^{(t)}(x)-x\big|=\frac{\big(|h_{t}(x)|-\alpha\big)_{+}}{2\beta},

hence

\mathbb{E}\big[\,|Y-X|\,\big|\,X=x\,\big]\ \leq\ \frac{\big(|h_{t}(x)|-\alpha\big)_{+}}{2\beta},\hskip 18.49988pt\mathcal{T}_{t}\ \leq\ \frac{1}{2\beta}\int_{I}\big(|h_{t}(x)|-\alpha\big)_{+}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x),

(78)

where
(\cdot)_{+}=\max\{\cdot,0\}
.

Proof.

Using (
34
), conditional on
X=x
,

\mathbb{E}\big[\,|Y-X|\,\big|\,X=x\,\big]=\theta_{t}(x)\,\big|T_{u}^{(t)}(x)-x\big|+\big(1-\theta_{t}(x)\big)\,\big|x-T_{d}^{(t)}(x)\big|.

On
B_{t}
we have
T_{d}^{(t)}(x)=T_{u}^{(t)}(x)=x
, so the conditional mean vanishes. On
I\setminus B_{t}
,
T_{d}^{(t)}(x)\leq x\leq T_{u}^{(t)}(x)
and the martingale constraint gives

\theta_{t}(x)\big(T_{u}^{(t)}(x)-x\big)=(1-\theta_{t}(x))\big(x-T_{d}^{(t)}(x)\big)=\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathfrak{m}(x).

Hence the conditional mean equals

, and using

T_{u}^{(t)}(x)-x=(1-\theta_{t}(x))\big(T_{u}^{(t)}(x)-T_{d}^{(t)}(x)\big)
and

x-T_{d}^{(t)}(x)=\theta_{t}(x)\big(T_{u}^{(t)}(x)-T_{d}^{(t)}(x)\big)
yields (
76
). Since
\theta_{t}(1-\theta_{t})\leq 1/4
, the inequality in (
76
) follows. Integrating over

gives (
77
). In the linear-quadratic case, the displacement identity is (
70

78
).
∎

.

4.4

In path–dependent problems the continuation value at

Lemma .1

(Fatou’s lemma)
.

Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mu)
be a measure space and
(f_{n})_{n\geq 1}
a sequence of nonnegative measurable functions. Then

\int_{\Omega}\liminf_{n\to\infty}f_{n}\,\mathrm{d}\mu\;\leq\;\liminf_{n\to\infty}\int_{\Omega}f_{n}\,\mathrm{d}\mu.

If
(f_{n})
is uniformly integrable and
f_{n}\to f
in measure, then

\int f_{n}\,\mathrm{d}\mu\to\int f\,\mathrm{d}\mu
.

Theorem .1

(Fenchel–Rockafellar duality)
.

Let
X
be a Banach space,
X^{\ast}
its dual, and

proper, convex, lower semicontinuous.
If

, then

\inf_{x\in X}\{F(x)+G(x)\}\;=\;\sup_{x^{\ast}\in X^{\ast}}\{-F^{\ast}(-x^{\ast})-G^{\ast}(x^{\ast})\},

Theorem .2

(Kuratowski–Ryll-Nardzewski measurable selection theorem)

Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F})
be a measurable space and
K\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\Omega\rightrightarrows X
a set-valued map into a Polish space
X
such that for every open
U\subset X
,

\{\omega\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}K(\omega)\cap U\neq\emptyset\}\in\mathcal{F}
.
Then there exists a measurable selector
s\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\Omega\to X
with
s(\omega)\in K(\omega)
for all
\omega\in\Omega
.

Theorem .3

(Rokhlin disintegration theorem)
.

Let
(\Omega_{1},\mathcal{F}_{1})
and
(\Omega_{2},\mathcal{F}_{2})
be standard Borel spaces,
and let
\pi\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega_{1}\times\Omega_{2})
with first marginal
\mu\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\pi\circ\mathrm{pr}_{1}^{-1}
.
Then there exists a measurable family of probability measures

\{\pi(\cdot\mid x)\}_{x\in\Omega_{1}}\subset\mathcal{P}(\Omega_{2})

such that for all bounded measurable
f\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\Omega_{1}\times\Omega_{2}\to\mathbb{R}
,

\int f(x,y)\,\pi(\mathrm{d}x,\mathrm{d}y)=\int_{\Omega_{1}}\left(\int_{\Omega_{2}}f(x,y)\,\pi(\mathrm{d}y\mid x)\right)\mu(\mathrm{d}x).

Moreover, the conditional law
x\mapsto\pi(\cdot\mid x)
is measurable in the sense of weak convergence.

Theorem .4

(Ionescu–Tulcea extension theorem)
.

Let
(E_{t},\mathcal{E}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be measurable spaces and
K_{t}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}(E_{0}\times\cdots\times E_{t})\to\mathcal{P}(E_{t+1})
transition kernels.
Given an initial measure
\mu_{0}\in\mathcal{P}(E_{0})
, there exists a unique probability measure

\pi\in\mathcal{P}\big(\prod_{t\geq 0}E_{t}\big)
such that for every measurable
A_{t+1}\subset E_{t+1}
,

\pi(S_{t+1}\in A_{t+1}\mid S_{0},\dots,S_{t})=K_{t}(S_{0},\dots,S_{t};A_{t+1})\hskip 9.24994pt\text{a.s.}

The marginals are defined recursively by

\mu_{t+1}(A)=\int K_{t}(x_{0},\dots,x_{t};A)\,\mu_{t}(\mathrm{d}x_{0},\dots,\mathrm{d}x_{t})
.

Theorem .5

(Prokhorov compactness theorem)
.

A family
\mathcal{P}_{0}\subset\mathcal{P}(E)
of probability measures on a Polish space
E

is relatively compact under the topology of weak convergence if and only if it is tight,
i.e. for every
\varepsilon>0
there exists a compact
K_{\varepsilon}\subset E
such that

\inf_{\pi\in\mathcal{P}_{0}}\pi(K_{\varepsilon})\geq 1-\varepsilon
.
Every tight sequence admits a weakly convergent subsequence.

Lemma .2

(de la Vallée–Poussin criterion for uniform integrability)
.

A family
\mathcal{F}\subset L^{1}(\mu)
is uniformly integrable if and only if
there exists a convex, increasing function
\Phi\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
with
\Phi(x)/x\to\infty
as
x\to\infty

such that

\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\int\Phi(|f|)\,\mathrm{d}\mu<\infty.

Lemma .3

(Komlós subsequence theorem)
.

Let
(X_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be a sequence bounded in
L^{1}(\mu)
on a probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mu)
.
Then there exists a subsequence
(X_{n_{k}})_{k\geq 1}
and
X\in L^{1}(\mu)
such that the Cesàro means converge a.s.:

\frac{1}{m}\sum_{k=1}^{m}X_{n_{k}}\ \longrightarrow\ X\hskip 9.24994pt\text{a.s. as }m\to\infty.

In particular,
X_{n_{k}}
converges to
X
weakly in
L^{1}(\mu)
.
