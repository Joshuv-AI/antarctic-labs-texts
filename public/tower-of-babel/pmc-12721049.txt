Fragmentation patterns of pathogen-derived cell-free DNA as a promising non-invasive biomarker for bloodstream infection diagnosis
Authors: Qichuan Fu, Jie Wang, Liya Yue, Tianyu Lu, Wenyu Shi, Cuidan Li, Xiaoyuan Jiang, Peihan Wang, Fei Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBloodstream infections (BSIs) caused by bacteria, viruses, and parasites pose a global health challenge, with high mortality rates. Traditional blood cultures are considered the gold standard but are limited by long turnaround times, low sensitivity, and reliance on culturable pathogens. Cell-free DNA (cfDNA) has emerged as a promising non-invasive biomarker for rapid pathogen detection.Materials and methodsIn this study, we analyzed plasma cfDNA from 102 BSI patients (42 bacterial, 34 viral, and 23 parasitic infections) using next-generation sequencing to examine the differences in cfDNA fragmentation patterns across pathogen types.ResultsPathogen-derived cfDNA fragments were shorter than human-derived cfDNA (median: 166 bp), with bacterial cfDNA averaging 126 bp and viral cfDNA 140 bp. Bacterial cfDNA fragments were typically shorter than viral ones, revealing distinctive patterns that could differentiate bacterial from viral infections. Fragment lengths varied among bacterial and viral species, suggesting the potential for pathogen-specific detection. EBV-derived cfDNA, at 163 bp, resembled human cfDNA possibly due to its nucleosome-bound form, while parasite-derived cfDNA had a broader distribution (median: 165 bp), indicating limitations in using cfDNA length for detecting parasitic infections.ConclusionsOur findings demonstrate that pathogen-derived cfDNA exhibits distinct fragmentation patterns, providing a potential non-invasive tool to complement traditional diagnostic methods, particularly for hard-to-culture pathogens. However, further studies with larger sample sizes are needed to refine pathogen-specific fragment length ranges and validate its clinical applicability.

Introduction

Bloodstream infection (BSI) is a severe condition caused by various pathogenic microorganisms, including bacteria, viruses, and parasites. In China, BSIs account for approximately 20 % of ICU admissions, with a 90-day mortality rate of 35.5 % [1]. Timely diagnosis and early initiation of pathogen-specific therapy are crucial for improving patient outcomes [2]. Currently, blood culture remains the gold standard for pathogen identification in BSI. However, this method has several limitations, including restriction to culturable pathogens, long culture times (typically 48–72 h), and high false-negative rates, particularly for slow-growing pathogens or patients who have received prior antimicrobial therapy [3,4]. Culture-independent molecular methods have shown promise for rapid pathogen identification. Techniques like polymerase chain reaction (PCR), fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH), and DNA microarrays enable faster diagnosis directly from blood specimens [5,6]. However, these approaches are limited by factors such as reduced sensitivity in low-burden infections, narrow pathogen detection ranges, and the complexity of mixed infections [5,7].

Circulating cell-free DNA (cfDNA) are small DNA fragments found in the blood's cell-free compartment and has emerged as a promising non-invasive biomarker. It is widely used in molecular diagnostics for conditions like fetal abnormalities [8,9], transplanted organ rejection [10,11] and malignant tumors [12,13]. Recently, pathogen-derived genomic DNA fragments have been identified in plasma cfDNA from infected patients, highlighting its potential for non-invasive detection of infectious agents using NGS-based assays [5]. Advances in this field have demonstrated the utility of microbial cfDNA in diagnosing invasive fungal diseases, tuberculosis, and other infections in immunocompromised patients [6,7,14]. By analyzing pathogen-derived cfDNA, such as its abundance and composition, diagnostic accuracy and clinical predictions can be significantly enhanced. For instance, recent studies have shown that elevated cfDNA concentrations in septic patients support its potential as a biomarker for bloodstream infections (BSI) [2,15]. Additionally, combining host and pathogen cfDNA improved diagnostic accuracy (AUC = 0.992 vs. 0.917) and enhanced 28-day mortality prediction (AUC = 0.802 vs. 0.750) compared to using host cfDNA alone [14].

Despite the promising potential of cfDNA for pathogen detection, several challenges hinder its widespread clinical use. A key issue is the difficulty in distinguishing microbial cfDNA from the much larger volume of host-derived cfDNA. The low abundance of microbial cfDNA, combined with potential contamination from blood cells or environmental sources, can lead to false positives in next-generation sequencing (NGS) assays [8,16]. Additionally, the presence of human commensal microorganisms complicates data interpretation, making pathogen identification more challenging [17]. While microbial and human cfDNA may differ in fragmentation patterns, these differences have not been thoroughly explored. Understanding these patterns could improve the diagnostic accuracy of NGS-based cfDNA assays, making them a more reliable tool for rapid, comprehensive pathogen detection.

This study aims to investigate the fragmentation patterns of pathogen-derived cfDNA in bloodstream infection (BSI) patients and compare them with human-derived cfDNA. By analyzing these differences, we seek to improve the diagnostic performance of NGS-based cfDNA assays, enhancing their ability to rapidly and non-invasively identify a wide range of infectious pathogens.

Materials and methods

Study design

We retrospectively reviewed 99 subjects with bacterial, viral, or parasitic infections. These individuals were suspected of having bloodstream infections based on clinical symptoms, including high fever, chills, cough, and septic shock, as well as their treatment response. However, the specific pathogens had not been identified at the time. Plasma samples were collected at the BGI Clinical Laboratory Center from April 2018 to January 2020. Negative controls consisted of plasma samples from three healthy volunteers processed in the same batch as patient samples. Any pathogen reads detected in these controls were used to establish a background threshold, which was then applied to patient samples to avoid false-positive calls. These specimens underwent NGS-based cfDNA analysis, with some verified by Sanger sequencing due to insufficient sample volumes. Samples were classified into three groups based on NGS results: bacterial infection (BI), viral infection (VI), and parasitic infection (PI). The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the BGI Institutional Review Board (IRB), and plasma was collected under a waiver of informed consent as permitted by the IRB.

Sample processing and cfDNA extraction

Blood samples (5–10 mL) were drawn from the 102 subjects, placed in EDTA tubes, and stored at 4 °C until processing. Plasma was separated within 8 h by centrifugation at 1600 g for 10 min at 4 °C. Plasma was transferred to 2 mL sterile tubes and shipped in dry ice containers. cfDNA was extracted from 300 μL of plasma using the TIANamp Micro DNA Kit (DP316, TIANGEN Biotech, Beijing, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions.

NGS methodology

DNA libraries were constructed through an end-repair method. The extracted DNA underwent the following procedures: end-repair, adapter ligation, and PCR amplification. Quality control of the DNA libraries were performed by using Agilent 2100 (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). Quality qualified DNA libraries were then sequenced by the MGISEQ-2000 platform (BGI-Tianjin, Tianjin, China).

Data processing and analysis

High-quality sequencing data were obtained by trimming adapter sequences and removing low-quality reads (Phred score <20) and short reads (length <35 bp). Paired-end reads were aligned to human (hg19) and pathogen reference genomes using BWA. The pathogen reference genomes used for alignment were sourced from our in-house constructed database, which includes 5050 viral genomes, 10,989 bacterial genomes or scaffolds, 1179 fungal genomes, and 282 parasite genomes. Their reference genomes were downloaded from NCBI (ftp://ftp.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/genomes/). Reads with low mapping quality scores (<30) for both human and pathogen reference genomes were excluded. Reads with high mapping quality scores but a difference in mapping quality <20 were also removed to reduce ambiguity. Only reads that were clearly mapped to either the human or pathogen genome were retained. Fragment length was defined as the distance between the outermost bases of each forward and reverse read. Pathogen-specific reads were identified as those uniquely mapping to a pathogen without matching other pathogens with equal or higher mapping scores. Pathogen presence was confirmed if at least 10 specific reads were detected, alongside high genome coverage compared to negative controls. Pathogen identification was cross-validated with the patient's clinical manifestations.

Confirmed by sanger sequencing

Plasma-extracted DNA was used as a template for PCR amplification under the following conditions: 94 °C for 5 min, followed by 40 cycles of 94 °C for 30 s, 55 °C for 50 s, and 72 °C for 30 s, with a final extension at 72 °C for 5 min and cooling to 4 °C. PCR products were visualized by agarose gel electrophoresis, purified using a gel extraction kit (DC3511-02, Biomiga Inc., San Diego, CA), and sequenced using an ABI 3730 Genetic Analyzer. Sequencing results were aligned to the NCBI NT database using BLAST (available at: http://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov) for pathogen identification.

Characteristics of human-derived cfDNA

We first analyzed the fragment length distribution of human-derived cfDNA in BSI patients. As shown in Fig. S1, the peak fragment length of human-derived cfDNA in infected patients was approximately 166 bp (black solid line), with a median fragment length of around 165 bp (red dotted line). These findings align with previous studies, which reported that the predominant cfDNA fragment length is around 166 bp—corresponding to the length of DNA wrapped around a histone octamer [[18], [19], [20]]. Our results suggest that the length distribution of human-derived cfDNA is not affected by the presence of different pathogens.

Comparison of fragment length distribution between human-derived and pathogen-derived cfDNA

We next compared the peak and median fragment lengths of pathogen-derived cfDNA with those of human-derived cfDNA. The peak fragment lengths of cfDNA from human, bacteria, viruses, and parasites were 166 bp, 104 bp, 165 bp, and 135 bp, respectively, indicating that pathogen-derived cfDNA tends to have smaller fragment sizes than human-derived cfDNA (Fig. 2a). Specifically, the median fragment lengths of bacteria-derived cfDNA (126 bp) and virus-derived cfDNA (140 bp) were significantly shorter than that of human-derived cfDNA (166 bp; p = 3 × 10−15 and p = 1.2 × 10−14, respectively), with bacteria-derived cfDNA being shorter than virus-derived cfDNA. These differences suggest that cfDNA fragment length could serve as a useful biomarker for distinguishing between bacterial and viral causes of bloodstream infections. Fragment length profiles of bacterial-derived cfDNA in the BI group

In the BI group, we analyzed the fragment length distributions of cfDNA from five bacterial species, including two Gram-positive bacteria (Staphylococcus aureus and Enterococcus faecium) and three Gram-negative bacteria (Acinetobacter baumannii, Klebsiella pneumoniae, and Escherichia coli) (Fig. 3). The size profiles for each bacterial species are shown in Fig. S2–S6. The median fragment lengths for the Gram-positive bacteria were 96 bp for Staphylococcus aureus and 130 bp for Enterococcus faecium. For the Gram-negative bacteria, the median fragment lengths were 125 bp for Acinetobacter baumannii, 131 bp for Klebsiella pneumoniae, and 122 bp for Escherichia coli (Fig. 3b). These results showed that bacterial-derived cfDNA fragments were consistently 35–60 bp shorter than human-derived cfDNA. Notably, statistical analysis revealed that Staphylococcus aureus cfDNA had significantly shorter fragment lengths compared to other bacterial species. However, there was no significant correlation between fragment length and whether the bacteria were Gram-positive or Gram-negative. Fragment length profiles of viral-derived cfDNA in the VI group

In the VI group, we analyzed the fragment length distributions of cfDNA from five viral species: CMV, BKV, EBV, HSV1, and VZV. As shown in Fig. 4, the median fragment lengths of cfDNA derived from these viruses were 126 bp for CMV, 149 bp for BKV, 163 bp for EBV, 114 bp for HSV1, and 101 bp for VZV. The median length of cfDNA from all viruses was significantly shorter than human-derived cfDNA, except for EBV-derived cfDNA, which was nearly identical to human-derived cfDNA, differing by only 3 bp. Further statistical comparisons of fragment lengths revealed that BKV-derived cfDNA had longer fragments than CMV and HSV1-derived cfDNA, ranking second in length. Discussion

Currently, diagnosing bacteremia primarily relies on culturing live organisms from blood samples [21,22]. However, this method has several limitations, including restriction to culturable pathogens, long culture times, complications in detecting mixed infections, and high false-negative rates [21]. cfDNA offers a promising alternative for diagnosing bloodstream infections (BSI) [22]. In this study, we investigated the fragmentation patterns of both human- and pathogen-derived cfDNA in BSI patients. Our results showed that both bacteria-derived cfDNA (126 bp) and virus-derived cfDNA (140 bp) had significantly shorter median lengths compared to human cfDNA, which had a median length of 166 bp. Statistical analysis further revealed significant differences in cfDNA fragment lengths across various pathogens, emphasizing the potential of pathogen-derived cfDNA as a biomarker for differentiating microbial pathogens in bloodstream infections.

While most existing studies have focused on a single infection type (e.g., bacterial or viral) [23], this study is the first to systematically examine the differences in pathogen-derived cfDNA lengths in BSI patients with bacterial, viral, and parasitic infections. This provides new insights into diagnosing and distinguishing between different infection types using cfDNA. We also show for the first time that the length of human-derived cfDNA remains unaffected by infection, consistently peaking at 166 bp, which is consistent with histone octamer-associated DNA [24]. This finding supports the potential of cfDNA fragmentation analysis as a tool for infection detection.

Our comparison of pathogen-derived cfDNA further confirmed that, as in previous studies, bacterial (126 bp) and viral (140 bp) cfDNA fragments are shorter than human cfDNA (166 bp) [25]. The shorter lengths are likely due to two factors: the lack of protective chromatin structures in bacterial and viral genomes, and the rapid degradation of pathogen cfDNA during immune lysis or intracellular destruction [[26], [27], [28]].

Interestingly, the median length of EBV-derived cfDNA was 163 bp, just 3 bp shorter than human-derived cfDNA. This similarity suggests that EBV may integrate into host chromosomes in a nucleosome-bound form, resembling human DNA [29,30]. Additionally, bacterial cfDNA fragments were shorter than viral ones, likely due to the faster degradation of bacterial cfDNA from antibiotic treatment or immune responses [27,28]. These differences in cfDNA fragmentation mechanisms could help differentiate between bacterial and viral infections.

Additionally, parasite-derived cfDNA exhibited fragment lengths similar to those of human cfDNA, with no significant differences observed. This may be due to the fact that both parasites and humans are eukaryotes, sharing similar cellular structures [24]. This suggests that cfDNA length may have limitations in detecting parasitic infections.

Further statistical analysis revealed variability in cfDNA fragment lengths among different bacterial and viral species, suggesting that cfDNA not only distinguishes between pathogen types but may also help identify specific pathogens. For instance, Staphylococcus aureus cfDNA fragments were significantly shorter than those from other bacteria, and HSV1 cfDNA fragments were notably shorter than those from other viruses [31,32]. Traditional cfDNA detection methods, such as metagenomic sequencing, often rely on sequence alignment [33], but their sensitivity can be limited by low cfDNA abundance or sequencing depth [34]. Our results suggest that cfDNA length is non-sequence-specific, making this approach a convenient method for detecting bloodstream microbial pathogens, particularly when sequence abundance is low or in cases of mixed infections. However, the wide range of cfDNA fragment lengths observed warrants further research to refine pathogen-specific length ranges.

In summary, our study demonstrates that pathogen-derived cfDNA in BSI patients exhibits distinct fragmentation patterns, facilitating developing more accurate and sensitive diagnostic tools. These non-invasive methods could complement traditional blood cultures and aid diagnosis, reducing diagnostic times and enabling timely and precise treatment, especially for slow-growing or difficult-to-culture pathogens.

However, there are some limitations to our study. First, the sample size was limited, and the findings need validation in larger samples to confirm their generalizability. Second, while we compared cfDNA fragment lengths between different pathogens, further research is needed to explore the clinical relevance of these differences in terms of diagnostic sensitivity and specificity. Lastly, contamination from host-derived cfDNA or external sources remains a challenge, particularly when using next-generation sequencing (NGS) for clinical diagnostics. Addressing these limitations will be important for advancing this technology and translating it into clinical practice.
