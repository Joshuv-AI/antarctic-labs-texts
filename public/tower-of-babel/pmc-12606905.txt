The effect of 3D printing integrated with problem-based learning on AGP training for thoracolumbar fractures: a randomised controlled trial
Authors: Dingyuan Jiang, Kui Duan, Zhigang Li, Xueming Chen, Nani Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThoracolumbar fractures are common and complex spinal injuries, critical to the training of assistant general practitioners (AGP). Traditional lecture-based teaching struggles to effectively convey the intricate three-dimensional anatomy of these fractures. While both Problem-Based Learning (PBL) and 3D printing (3DP) have demonstrated individual benefits in medical education, their combined effect has not been rigorously evaluated for AGP training on thoracolumbar fractures. This randomized controlled trial (RCT) aimed to compare the effectiveness of a 3D printing-integrated PBL approach with conventional lecture-based teaching in improving AGP trainees’ knowledge and competencies regarding thoracolumbar fractures.MethodsA randomized controlled trial was conducted at Zhuzhou 331 Hospital from June 2022 to September 2023. Eighty AGP trainees were randomly assigned to the 3DP group (n = 40) using 3D-printed fracture models within a PBL curriculum, or the control group (n = 40) receiving traditional lecture-based instruction. Randomization was performed with computer-generated sequences, and allocation concealment was maintained. The primary outcome was the score on a post-course knowledge test, with secondary outcomes including a delayed test at 4 weeks and self-reported evaluations. Outcomes were analysed with Mann–Whitney U tests and reported as medians (IQR) with effect size r and 95% CI.ResultsEighty learners were randomised (3DP n = 40; control n = 40) and completed follow-up. The 3DP group achieved significantly higher scores than the control group on both the immediate post-course test (80.0 vs. 70.0, U = 523.50, r = 0.301 (95% CI 0.087–0.488), p = 0.007) and the delayed post-course test (4 weeks) (82.5 vs. 70.0, U = 255.00, r = 0.593 (95% CI 0.429–0.719), p < 0.001). Learner-reported outcomes favoured 3DP in 9/10 domains, including clinical thinking, initiative, self-study, anatomy recognition, teamwork and satisfaction (all p < 0.001), with no difference in mastery of basic knowledge (p = 0.640).ConclusionIntegrating 3D printing with PBL significantly improved AGP trainees’ knowledge of thoracolumbar fractures and enhanced their clinical reasoning, engagement, and satisfaction. This combined approach offers a promising educational strategy for AGP training.Trial registrationNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-025-08152-0.

Introduction

Over the past decade, China has implemented an Assistant General Practitioner (AGP) training program to improve the quality of primary healthcare services [1–3]. As a key component of the “General Practitioner Training Program,” the program adopts a “3 + 2” model, adding two years of standardized clinical training after three years of college education, dedicated to providing professional development opportunities for medical students with a background of three years of college education [1–3].

Traditional lecture-based teaching has long been dominant in the fields of medical education and training, with significant advantages and efficiency in conveying core knowledge and concepts to a wide audience [4–6]. However, studies have found that traditional lecture-based teaching is lacking in higher education, especially professional medical training. This is because traditional teaching methods are usually teacher-centered, with teachers dominating the process of knowledge transmission, while students often play a passive role in receiving knowledge [7].

In recent years, significant progress has been made in the field of medical education in terms of teaching methods and many innovative and practical teaching methods have emerged. Problem-Based Learning (PBL) is a student-centered teaching model that is problem-oriented learning. The PBL teaching method encourages students to discuss and analyze specific cases in group settings, promoting problem-solving through independent inquiry and collaborative communication within a cooperative environment, thereby advancing the learning process. This emphasizes active student participation and self-directed learning. Under this model, the teacher’s role shifts to that of a facilitator and supporter, guiding students towards deep thinking and effective learning [8–11]. Compared with traditional learning methods, the PBL teaching method can better cultivate students’ ability to independently solve problems, stimulate students’ awareness of self-education, cultivate the ability for sustainable learning, and lay a solid foundation for students’ future development [12]. A substantial body of empirical evidence supports the effectiveness of Problem-Based Learning (PBL) in enhancing clinical reasoning, fostering knowledge integration, and improving long-term knowledge retention among medical students [13–16].

Spinal fractures are numerous and complex, mainly because the spine is composed of various structures such as vertebrae, intervertebral discs, and ligaments. When these structures are subjected to external forces, they may suffer various forms of injury that can lead to fractures, with or without spinal cord and nerve damage [17]. Thoracolumbar fractures had the highest incidence rate of spinal fractures [18]. This topic is one of the core contents that must be mastered in assistant practitioner qualifications and standardized training graduation examinations. The main teaching content covered anatomical structure and physiological function, etiology and pathogenesis, classification of fractures, clinical manifestations, diagnostic methods, treatment strategies, rehabilitation process of thoracolumbar fractures, and first-aid and handling techniques for patients with thoracolumbar fractures. For AGP who are just beginning their clinical practice, it is crucial to fully master knowledge related to thoracolumbar spine fractures. However, for students lacking practical clinical experience, mastering content related to thoracolumbar fractures is undoubtedly a challenge, and they may encounter difficulties in understanding these concepts.

3D printing technology, with its highly realistic three-dimensional models, provides medical students with a visual and tangible learning aid that can effectively deepen their understanding and mastery of medical knowledge, such as anatomy and pathology [19–22]. A growing body of research specifically supports the use of 3D-printed models in anatomy education and trauma simulation, showing improved spatial understanding, fracture pattern recognition, and preoperative planning capabilities [23–26]. It is particularly noteworthy that this technology demonstrates distinct advantages in teaching complex anatomical structures, pathological changes, and traumatic mechanisms [26, 27]. Published evidence confirms that, compared to conventional two-dimensional imagery or textbook illustrations, learning based on 3D-printed models significantly enhances students’ accuracy and efficiency in spatial understanding, structural identification, and pathological recognition [28]. These benefits are especially pronounced in specific disciplines such as orthopaedic trauma [17]. More specifically, with the help of 3D-printed fracture models, students can observe the fracture site, direction of the fracture line, and damage to surrounding structures more clearly, thereby better understanding the characteristics of fractures and treatment methods [29, 30]. Furthermore, 3D printing technology plays a pivotal role in various aspects, such as surgical simulation, personalized teaching, interdisciplinary education, and injecting new vitality into medical education [19, 22, 31–36].

PBL teaching methods and 3D printing technology have unique advantages in the field of medical education. PBL teaching methods, through problem-based learning, guide students to actively explore and solve problems and foster their clinical thinking and practical abilities [16, 37, 38]. 3D printing technology, with its intuitive three-dimensional models, helps students better understand complex medical knowledge and improve learning outcomes. These two innovative teaching methods have been widely applied in various clinical teaching and training scenarios, including undergraduate internships and standardized training for resident physicians, and have achieved significant results [35, 39, 40]. However, despite the respective efficacy of problem-based learning (PBL) and 3D printing individually within medical education contexts, their combined application has remained under-investigated. No randomized controlled trial has evaluated integrating 3D printing within a PBL framework for AGP training on thoracolumbar fractures. We conducted a parallel-group RCT to test whether 3DP improves knowledge (primary) and knowledge retention and learner-reported outcomes (secondary) versus lecture-based instruction.

Methods

Design and participants

This investigation employed a prospective, parallel-group, randomized controlled trial (RCT) design with a balanced 1:1 allocation ratio. The study underwent formal review and received approval from the Ethics Committee of Zhuzhou 331 Hospital, as evidenced by the Approval Number: ZZS331YYLL-202,202-JX1-J1. Study participants were recruited from the AGP training program at Zhuzhou 331 Hospital between June 1, 2022, and September 1, 2023. All participating students had completed a standardized foundational training session on the PBL method prior to the commencement of the trial, ensuring a basic and consistent understanding of the PBL process. Inclusion criteria required participants to be: (1) trainees of the AGP program; and (2) voluntarily and actively participating in the course throughout, with no absences or truancies. Exclusion criteria comprised an anticipated inability to complete the full course content. Elimination criteria included: (1) failure to complete the pre- and post-course knowledge tests and questionnaires in a timely manner, and (2) provision of missing key answers or obviously illogical responses in the study questionnaires, which rendered the data unusable for effective analysis. We have clarified that the assessment for all exclusion criteria was conducted prior to randomization, while the evaluation of data elimination criteria was performed following randomization but before data analysis.

Interventions

3DP group

First, before the course, the teacher selected clinical cases related to the topic from the hospital’s spinal surgery department database. All patient data were anonymized prior to use, and their application for educational purposes was conducted in accordance with institutional ethical and data protection guidelines. Three-dimensional CT data were extracted from the cases and saved in the DICOM format. Subsequently, three-dimensional reconstruction was performed using RadiAnt DICOM Viewer (version 2023, Medixant company, Poznań, Poland), and the results were ultimately exported in STL format. Next, CHITUBOX (V1.9.5, Chuangyi Technology Co., Ltd., China) was used for 3D printing slicing, and a 1:1 scale thoracolumbar fracture model was printed using a 3D printer (Chuangxiang, Shenzhen, China) (Fig. 1).

In addition to 3D printed models, which served as the key “trigger material” to stimulate problem identification and initiate the inquiry process during the PBL sessions [41], teachers provided typical clinical materials for thoracolumbar fractures, including imaging data such as X-rays, CT, and MRI images, teaching videos on emergency transportation for thoracolumbar fractures, as well as relevant treatment guidelines and reference literature. Educators required students to engage in independent studies using these materials during their discretionary time.

Before the experiment, all the students in this group had completed a 4-hour basic standardized training course on PBL (Problem-Based Learning), which systematically covered the basic principles and implementation process of PBL. At the beginning of the course, the teacher first introduced the learning objectives and expected outcomes to ensure that the students had a clear understanding of the class goals. Subsequently, through the presentation of case studies, students were guided to identify and define problems while using modeling software and 3D models to delve into the case study, encouraging them to raise their own questions. During the group discussion session, students developed strategies to address core issues, and under the guidance of the teacher, they integrated interdisciplinary knowledge through the internet and library resources. Each group’s representatives reported on the discussion outcomes, reviewed key points, and shared their group’s insights on the issue, while also discussing unresolved questions. At the end of the course, students engaged in self-assessment and peer assessment; the teacher provided feedback and summary, clarified complex issues, and provided further learning resources and guidance.

Control group

Before the course began, the control group received traditional lecture-based instruction without any 3D-printed models. These students acquired the same content through traditional lecture-based pedagogical methods; specifically, instructors provided detailed explanations of the theoretical knowledge within the official curriculum. In essence, teacher-centered instruction constitutes the primary educational approach [42, 43].

Outcome measures

The primary outcome of this trial was the score on the post-course knowledge test, administered immediately after the teaching intervention. The secondary outcomes included: (1) the score on the delayed post-course test, administered four weeks after the course to assess knowledge retention; and (2) the students’ self-evaluation and satisfaction scores across ten dimensions, as measured by the post-course questionnaire.

Evaluation tool

The evaluation tools comprised knowledge tests and a self-report questionnaire, all detailed in Supplementary Materials 1 to 4. Referring to previous studies [42–44], before and after classroom activities, we required each group of students to complete pre- and post-course test, as well as tests four weeks later. The pre-course test mainly covered basic knowledge of thoracolumbar spine fractures, including the anatomical structure and imaging of the spine, and the general principles of spinal fractures. The post-course test and the test after four weeks included the pathogenesis, classification, clinical manifestations, imaging characteristics, diagnostic methods, selection of treatment plans, and rehabilitation process of thoracolumbar fractures, as well as the first aid and handling of patients with thoracolumbar fractures. Each examination set comprised 20 multiple-choice questions, with each question valued at five points, yielding a total of 100 points. All studies were designed according to Bloom’s taxonomy [45, 46]. To ensure the objectivity of outcome assessment, all personnel responsible for scoring the knowledge tests and questionnaires were blinded to group allocation. During the scoring process, all responses were anonymized with group identifiers removed and presented only by coded identification.

Additionally, the students were required to complete a questionnaire regarding their self-evaluation and satisfaction with the conclusion of the instructional course. The questionnaire, which referenced the studies by Oderinu [44], Zhao [42], and Yan [43], was modified. The questionnaire includes ten dimensions of self-evaluation: clinical thinking skills, learning enthusiasm, self-study ability, mastery of basic knowledge, ability to analyze and solve problems, interest in learning, recognition of anatomical knowledge, understanding of knowledge, teamwork skills, and satisfaction with teaching methods. Students rated these aspects using a five-point Likert scale, with each item ranging from 1 to 5 points [42–44].

Sample size calculation

The sample size was calculated a priori using G*Power software (version 3.1.9.7, Universität Düsseldorf, Germany) based on data obtained from our pre-experiment, which comprised 20 participants evenly divided into two groups of 10. In the pre-experiment, the 3DP group exhibited a mean score of 80.53 with a standard deviation (SD) of 10.25, while the control group showed a mean of 73.85 with an SD of 9.58. The parameters for the calculation were set as follows: a t-test for the difference between two independent means (two groups) was selected under an a priori type of power analysis with two tails; the effect size d was 0.68 (calculated from the pre-experiment means and pooled standard deviation); the α error probability was set at 0.05; the desired power (1-β err prob) was 0.80; and the allocation ratio (N2/N1) was 1. The computation indicated that a minimum of 36 subjects per group would be required to detect a statistically significant difference. To account for an anticipated dropout rate of approximately 10%, the sample size was inflated to 40 per group. Consequently, a final target sample size of 80 participants was determined.

Randomisation and allocation concealment

We used simple individual randomisation (1:1). An independent statistician—uninvolved in recruitment or intervention delivery—generated the allocation sequence in R (version 4.3.1; set.seed(2022)). Group assignment was mapped by the parity of the computer-generated integer (odd = 3DP; even = control) [42, 43]; no blocking or stratification was used. Allocation was concealed with sequentially numbered, identical, opaque, tamper-evident sealed envelopes (SNOSE) prepared off-site by the statistician. After written informed consent and completion of baseline assessments, the enrolling researcher opened the next envelope in numerical order to reveal the assignment. The master sequence was stored in a password-protected file accessible only to the statistician; personnel involved in enrolment or intervention delivery had no access.

Masking

This was an open-label trial; participants and instructors were not masked. Knowledge tests were anonymised and independently marked by assessors masked to group allocation, with masking maintained until database lock; discrepancies were adjudicated by a second masked assessor when required. Questionnaires were self-administered, and masking was not applicable.

Statistical methods

Baseline characteristics were summarised descriptively; no hypothesis testing was performed at baseline in accordance with CONSORT. For the primary and secondary outcomes, we used two-sided Mann–Whitney U tests and reported median (IQR) and U, Z, p. Results

In this study, a total of 80 eligible participants were recruited and randomly assigned to either the 3DP group or the control group, where they received the designated teaching intervention. All participants completed all tests and surveys. A participant flow diagram is shown in Fig. 2.

Baseline characteristics

Baseline characteristics are summarized in Table 1. Of the 80 randomized trainees, 40 were allocated to the 3DP group and 40 to the control group. The two groups were descriptively similar in sex distribution, urban/rural residence, age, education, and prior clinical experience. Most trainees were female (3DP: 23/40 [57.5%]; control: 26/40 [65.0%]) and from urban areas (3DP: 23/40 [57.5%]; control: 28/40 [70.0%]). Median age was 22 years in both groups (3DP: IQR 22–23; control: IQR 22–22). All participants held an associate degree and had no previous clinical experience. Pre-course knowledge scores were 60.0 (IQR 55.0–71.3) in the 3DP group and 65.0 (IQR 57.5–75.0) in the control group.

Discussion

This study investigated the effect of a combined educational strategy integrating 3D printing technology as an auxiliary tool within a problem-based learning (PBL) framework for training AGP in thoracolumbar spine fractures. This study compared the combined strategy of “3D printing integrated with PBL” against conventional teaching alone. Both immediate and 4-week delayed post-course scores were significantly better than those of the control group, providing strong evidence for the effectiveness of this combined instructional approach.

There was no significant difference in the pre-course test scores between the two groups, which may be attributed to the fact that the tests covered basic knowledge of thoracolumbar fractures, and all assistant medical students had received three years of basic medical education during their time at school, equipping them with solid learning abilities. They demonstrated a profound understanding of basic knowledge through traditional classroom teaching methods [43] improvement in post-course test scores for the 3DP group demonstrated the significant advantages of the combined strategy in knowledge acquisition and the cultivation of application abilities. There are numerous types of thoracolumbar fractures in the diagnostic and treatment processes, and each case possesses its own uniqueness [47, 48]. Within the PBL framework of this study, the introduction of 3D printing technology allowed for the creation of personalised models for each PBL case, enabling students to understand complex thoracolumbar anatomical relationships more intuitively through tactile and visual models. More importantly, the PBL pedagogy served as the core instructional design, using real-case discussions and collaboration to promote the integration of prior and new knowledge among students. This vivid PBL learning experience, enhanced by the tangibility of 3D printing, greatly increased student engagement and initiative, helping students absorb and apply knowledge more deeply, thereby improving learning outcomes [20, 40, 49].

This study tested content based on the requirements of the AGP qualification examination and the AGP graduation examination syllabus, including anatomy and physiological functions, etiology and pathogenesis, fracture classification, clinical manifestations, diagnosis, treatment, rehabilitation, as well as the first aid and handling of patients with thoracolumbar fractures. The content design was tailored to the clinical practice. At the same time, the design of this test paper follows Bloom’s taxonomy, a method that has become a key tool for evaluating teaching effectiveness owing to its systematic, operational, scientific, and widely recognized nature. It can comprehensively and deeply assess students’ mastery of knowledge [45, 46]. To some extent, the longer the interval between study and test time, the more accurately exam results reflect students’ mastery of knowledge [50, 51]. In this study, considering that the training time for AGP in orthopedics (including spinal surgery) was four weeks, the departmental test was conducted after four weeks. The 3DP group’s outstanding performance in theoretical scores further proves the effectiveness of this teaching model in cultivating clinical abilities. This result differs from that of previous studies. In the study by Yan et al. [43], the test time was set at 2 weeks later, and the test results showed no difference compared to the control group. The results of this study confirm that by personalizing case simulations to mimic real clinical scenarios, students, with the aid of 3D models, have their understanding and memory of complex knowledge reinforced by rich visual materials, master the relevant knowledge proficiently, and enhance their ability to solve real clinical problems.

The 3DP group significantly outperformed the control group in multiple key indicators, including clinical thinking skills, learning initiative, self-learning ability, analytical and problem-solving skills, interest in thoracolumbar fractures, anatomical understanding, knowledge mastery of thoracolumbar fractures, teamwork skills, and satisfaction with the teaching method. These results suggest that the educational strategy combining 3D printing with PBL excels not only in knowledge transmission but also in stimulating learning interest, fostering higher-order thinking skills, and promoting teamwork. This indicates that the strategy effectively facilitates the transition from theory to practice and enhances students’ ability to solve real-world problems. Furthermore, the high scores of the 3DP group on learning initiative and self-study ability reflect the positive role of this PBL-centric teaching method, augmented by 3D models, in stimulating students’ intrinsic motivation and autonomous learning capability, which are significantly important for their future career development and lifelong learning. Simultaneously, high satisfaction ratings also indicate that this combined approach can better meet students’ learning needs and enhance their learning experience. In summary, integrating 3D printing technology into the PBL framework demonstrates superiority across multiple dimensions, provides a new perspective and practical approach for medical education, and is worth promoting and applying in broader educational contexts.

This single-centre design may limit generalisability. Although allocation concealment was implemented, the randomisation approach had theoretical predictability. We did not include 3D printing-only or PBL-only arms; thus, the observed benefits reflect the combined strategy. This education RCT, which assessed non-health outcomes, was not prospectively registered; future multicentre, factorial, preregistered trials with objective skills assessments are warranted.

Conclusion

In this single-centre randomized trial, integrating 3D-printed models within a PBL framework improved immediate and 4-week knowledge scores and several learner-reported outcomes versus lecture-based instruction. These findings support 3DP as a promising approach for AGP training on thoracolumbar fractures and warrant multicentre, factorial studies with objective skills assessments.
