From gut microbiota metabolism to microvascular injury: Exploring the role and mechanisms of gut microbiota in obesity-induced coronary microcirculation dysfunction
Authors: Yuhang Liu, Yong Wang, Haodong Teng, Wenyang Nie, Xinyu Qiao, Yingying Shen, Zhen Wang, Xiaodong Yao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObesity, a global epidemic, drives coronary microvascular dysfunction (CMD), a precursor to cardiovascular disease, via gut microbiota dysbiosis. Clinical evidence shows a 47.7% reduction in coronary flow reserve among obese patients, highlighting obesity’s pivotal role in CMD pathogenesis. This review elucidates molecular mechanisms linking obesity-induced dysbiosis to CMD, focusing on microbial metabolites: TMAO promotes lipid deposition and inflammation; reduced SCFAs impair endothelial function; bile acids modulate vascular tone via FXR/TGR5; LPS induces metabolic endotoxemia; BCAAs trigger insulin resistance; endogenous ethanol fosters oxidative stress; indole metabolites exert dual vascular effects; ImP and PAGln accelerate atherosclerosis and thrombosis; and H2S deficiency and elevated succinate exacerbate remodeling. The gut-heart axis amplifies CMD risk beyond traditional factors. Future multi-omics studies should identify biomarkers and develop targeted therapies, such as metabolite inhibitors and precision nutrition, to mitigate obesity-related CMD and improve outcomes. Introduction

Obesity, as a global epidemic, has seen an exponential increase in prevalence over the past few decades, posing a serious threat to public health [1]. According to projections for 2025, by 2050, approximately 3.8 billion adults worldwide (over 50% of the total adult population at that time) may face overweight or obesity issues, with Asia and Africa expected to be the fastest-growing regions [2]. Obesity, typically defined as a Body Mass Index (BMI) ≥30 kg/m2, is closely associated with cardiovascular diseases (Cardiovascular Disease, CVD) [3]. Excessive fat accumulation not only accelerates the process of atherosclerosis but also promotes abnormal changes in cardiac function through its harmful effects on the myocardium and blood vessels, as well as through obesity-related comorbidities such as hypertension, dyslipidemia and type 2 diabetes [4]. Multiple studies have shown that obesity affects not only the large coronary arteries but also significantly impacts the coronary microcirculation system, leading to microvascular endothelial dysfunction and inadequate myocardial perfusion, which can induce myocardial ischemia even in the absence of significant large vessel obstruction [5,6]. Microvascular disease is a common complication of obesity, often occurring prior to hypertension and type 2 diabetes, and can predict severe cardiovascular events [7]. Obesity, as a major risk factor for cardiovascular diseases, is not merely an accumulation of fat but rather a complex metabolic disease state. It can induce various pathological changes, such as hypertension, hyperglycemia, dyslipidemia, and gut microbiota imbalance, all of which collectively increase the risk of Major Adverse Cardiovascular Events (MACE). Its impact even surpasses that of traditional risk factors, as it can independently lead to an increase in cardiovascular diseases and related mortality [1,8]. Therefore, understanding how obesity affects cardiovascular health through complex molecular pathways, particularly in the microcirculation system, is crucial for developing more effective prevention and treatment strategies.

Research indicates that obesity leads to coronary microcirculatory dysfunction [9]. However, the diagnosis and treatment of Coronary Microvascular Dysfunction (CMD) have long faced significant challenges, as its fundamental pathophysiological mechanisms have not yet been fully elucidated. Therefore, a deeper exploration of how obesity leads to CMD, and whether there exists a key link mediating both, has become the focus of current cardiovascular research. Traditional risk factors for cardiovascular disease, such as obesity, hypertension, dyslipidemia, and diabetes, have been widely confirmed to be associated with CMD [10]. However, after strict control of these traditional risk factors, a considerable number of patients still face persistent and unexplained residual cardiovascular risk [11]. This has prompted researchers to turn their attention to deeper pathological mechanisms in search of new diagnostic and therapeutic breakthroughs. In recent years, with the rapid development of cutting-edge technologies such as metagenomics and metabolomics, the gut microbiota, as a “microbial organ” within the human body, has gradually revealed its core role in host health and disease. Increasing evidence suggests that dysbiosis of the gut microbiota is closely related to the occurrence and development of various metabolic diseases, including cardiovascular diseases, leading to the formation of the so-called “gut-heart axis” concept [12]. Gut microbiota dysbiosis triggers excessive systemic inflammation and vascular endothelial dysfunction by increasing intestinal permeability, lipopolysaccharide (LPS) translocation, activating the nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB) pathway, and reducing nitric oxide bioavailability,etc., thereby exhibiting a strong and significant association with cardiovascular diseases. Recent studies further support therapeutic modulation of the microbiome in this context [13–16]. The gut microbiota not only directly influences host physiology through its own metabolic products but also indirectly affects distant organs by regulating the immune and nervous systems [17]. This complex pathological network tightly links diet, lifestyle, obesity, and cardiovascular health, indicating that dysbiosis of the gut microbiota is not an isolated factor but is situated within a “pathological core hub” woven from various adverse elements. It integrates and amplifies the detrimental effects of lifestyle factors, such as unhealthy diets, on the cardiovascular system, providing a fresh perspective for explaining the residual risk that traditional risk factors cannot fully account for. This review aims to investigate the pivotal role and mechanisms of the gut microbiome in obesity-induced coronary microcirculatory dysfunction. By analyzing how key metabolites such as TMAO and SCFAs mediate systemic inflammation, oxidative stress, and endothelial dysfunction, it provides a theoretical foundation for developing novel preventive and therapeutic strategies targeting the gut-heart axis.

Obesity-induced coronary microcirculatory dysfunction

Coronary microvascular dysfunction (CMD), as a subclinical manifestation of myocardial ischemia, is prevalent in many patients with high-risk factors but normal coronary angiography. Its essence lies in the structural and functional remodeling of the coronary microcirculation, leading to impaired coronary blood flow autoregulation and myocardial ischemia [18]. The coronary microcirculation plays a crucial role in regulating coronary blood flow to meet the dynamically changing metabolic demands of the myocardium and is involved in the pathogenesis of both obstructive and non-obstructive coronary syndromes as well as myocardial diseases [19]. Its core physiological function is to achieve a tight coupling between local blood supply and tissue metabolic demands [20]. The primary pathological mechanisms involve chronic low-grade inflammation induced by cardiovascular risk factors, which initiates endothelial dysfunction, resulting in an imbalance of vasodilatory factors, accompanied by microvascular structural remodeling (such as rarefaction and fibrosis) and autonomic nervous regulation abnormalities. Together, these factors lead to impaired coronary blood flow autoregulation and myocardial ischemia [21,22]. A considerable body of research has revealed the complex molecular mechanisms through which obesity can induce CMD. Chronic low-grade inflammation and metabolic disorders induced by obesity significantly affect the coronary microvasculature, with key molecular pathways including pro-inflammatory cytokines (such as tumor necrosis factor-alpha and interleukin-6), adipokines such as leptin and resistin, and hormonal dysregulation. These factors lead to oxidative stress, systemic inflammation, and endothelial dysfunction [23–25]. Clinical studies have also provided strong evidence for the association between obesity and CMD, further elucidating multiple facets of this complex disease mechanism. A meta-analysis that included 1,399 subjects (among whom 456 were obese) demonstrated that [26] the coronary flow reserve (CFR) in the obese population was significantly reduced, with a decrease of 47.7% (sample size n = 422; 95% confidence interval −80.2% to −15.2%). Additionally, under pharmacologically induced stress conditions, myocardial blood flow (MBF) in obese patients was also significantly reduced by 47.8% (n = 409; 95% CI −73.7% to −21.8%), while no statistically significant difference was observed in resting MBF (15%, 95% CI −24% to 53%). These pooled findings of approximately 48% reduction in both CFR and stress-induced MBF are highly consistent with a large-scale longitudinal study involving 827 patients with normal myocardial perfusion (median follow-up of 5.6 years) [27], which reported a strong negative correlation between CFR and BMI (p < 0.0001) and showed that each 1-unit decrement in CFR nearly doubled the risk of cardiovascular events (HR 1.95, 95% CI 1.41–2.69). Moreover, elevated levels of pro-inflammatory adipokines such as leptin and C-reactive protein were closely associated with the decline in CFR and the reduction in stress MBF. These findings support the notion that obesity-related inflammation may play a crucial role in the dysfunction of coronary microvascular function, although the specific molecular mechanisms warrant further exploration.

The interactive relationship between obesity and gut microbiota

The human gut harbors approximately 10^14 microorganisms, including bacteria, fungi, viruses, and archaea, which constitute a complex gut microbiota [28]. In healthy individuals, the gut microbiota is primarily composed of Firmicutes, Bacteroidetes, Actinobacteria, and Proteobacteria, with Firmicutes and Bacteroidetes accounting for over 90% [29]. These microorganisms participate in various physiological functions through a symbiotic relationship with the host, including food digestion, nutrient absorption, vitamin synthesis, immune regulation, and maintenance of intestinal barrier integrity [30]. The gut microbiota produces a variety of bioactive metabolites through metabolic activities, including short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), trimethylamine (TMA) and its oxide TMAO, bile acids, vitamins, and tryptophan metabolites; these metabolites circulate through the bloodstream to reach various tissues and organs, including the cardiovascular system, and are involved in regulating the host’s metabolic and immune functions [31].

Dysbiosis of the gut microbiota refers to an imbalance in the composition and function of the gut microbiota, typically manifested as reduced microbial diversity or abnormal proportions of specific microbial communities. This imbalance may lead to an increase in microbial byproducts, potentially overwhelming the body’s waste clearance mechanisms [32]. Dysbiosis has been associated with intestinal diseases such as inflammatory bowel disease and irritable bowel syndrome, as well as a wide range of extraintestinal diseases including metabolic syndrome, cardiovascular diseases, and obesity. The primary pathological mechanisms involve impaired intestinal mucosal barrier function, inflammation activation, immune dysregulation, and metabolic abnormalities [33,34]. Studies have shown that there are significant differences in the composition of gut microbiota between obese and lean individuals, typically characterized by an increase in Firmicutes and a decrease in Bacteroidetes, although this pattern varies due to dietary and genetic factors [35,36]. High-fat diets have been shown to induce dysbiosis of the gut microbiota, leading to metabolic disorders and systemic inflammation, laying the groundwork for the occurrence of cardiovascular diseases [37]. The reduction of gut microbiota diversity in obesity indicates a compromised metabolic and immune buffering capacity of the host. High diversity is associated with stable and resilient ecosystems capable of performing diverse metabolic functions and resisting pathogen colonization. Therefore, a reduction in diversity implies a decrease in microbial species, which may lead to a narrowed metabolic capacity and a diminished ability to respond effectively to dietary or environmental changes. This functional impairment may indicate a reduced efficiency in the production of beneficial metabolic products and/or an increased susceptibility to the proliferation of harmful bacteria, thereby exacerbating the metabolic and inflammatory aspects of obesity.

The relationship between obesity and gut microbiota is not a simple linear association but rather a complex bidirectional interaction system [38]. Individuals with obesity typically exhibit a decrease in gut microbiota diversity [39], which is a commonly observed phenomenon of ecological dysbiosis. In terms of microbiota composition, relevant studies have also found a reduction in beneficial bacteria, specifically Bifidobacterium, in the intestines of obese children, alongside an increase in opportunistic pathogens such as Escherichia coli [40]. This imbalance in microbiota structure is a typical manifestation of the intestinal microecology in obesity. The dysbiosis associated with obesity can trigger a series of cascading effects, including energy metabolism imbalance and chronic systemic inflammation [38]. Specifically, the imbalanced gut microbiota significantly enhances the host’s efficiency in extracting energy from food, disrupting the balance between energy intake and expenditure, thereby creating conditions for abnormal fat accumulation. On the other hand, it also interferes with the normal secretion of hormones and signaling molecules by intestinal cells. This disruption in secretion can directly trigger chronic low-grade inflammation in the body and sustain this inflammatory state, further leading to or exacerbating obesity [35].

Early studies have widely reported an increased ratio of Firmicutes to Bacteroidetes (F/B ratio) in obese populations [35,41]. This finding has been considered an important marker linking obesity to dysbiosis. However, the gut microbiome is an extremely complex and dynamic ecosystem, and this reductionist approach may overlook important nuances. Subsequent research has failed to consistently confirm this association. From experimental evidence, the clustering features of gut microbiota structure depend more on the specific study itself, with differences primarily driven by variations in experimental conditions such as DNA extraction methods, primer selection, sequencing platforms, and analytical workflows. In addition, the majority of studies commonly suffer from insufficient statistical power, because even detecting only a weak difference in the F/B ratio would require enrolling thousands of samples per group to meet statistical requirements. These experimental-level limitations indicate that the F/B ratio may not be the sole or key determinant of human obesity [39]. The inconsistency of these research conclusions reveals a fundamental challenge in this field: the composition of gut microbiota is highly individualized and influenced by a multitude of factors, including diet, genetics, and environment [39]. In contrast to the controversy surrounding the F/B ratio, the role of certain specific bacteria is clearer. For instance, Akkermansia muciniphila, a Gram-negative anaerobic mucin-degrading bacterium, has an abundance that is negatively correlated with obesity, diabetes, and inflammation [42]. This bacterium is believed to improve metabolic health by restoring intestinal barrier integrity, producing beneficial short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), and regulating inflammation [43]. Other species are also associated with metabolic status. For example, Eubacterium rectale and Clostridium coccoides are related to efficient energy extraction [44], while increased abundance of species such as Citrobacter and Klebsiella is associated with inflammation and obesity [45]. Therefore, any single, universal characteristic of the microbiome (such as the F/B ratio) may be inaccurate. Future research should not focus solely on taxonomic lists or ratios of species but should instead shift toward a deeper understanding of microbiome functions and metabolic pathways, which is the true direction for understanding its pathogenic mechanisms.

A high-fat diet is a direct “catalyst” linking obesity to gut microbiota dysbiosis [46]. Its mechanism of action goes beyond merely “excessive energy intake;” it profoundly impacts the balance of the gut ecosystem. Research indicates that a high-fat diet can directly disrupt the mitochondrial function of intestinal epithelial cells. Once mitochondrial function is impaired, intestinal epithelial cells generate more oxygen and nitrate, and this alteration in the gut microenvironment further stimulates the excessive proliferation of harmful microorganisms from the Enterobacteriaceae family (e.g. Escherichia coli). Notably, during their proliferation, these harmful bacteria produce a metabolic byproduct known as trimethylamine (TMA) [47]. This finding extends the traditional “diet-obesity” pathway to a molecular mechanism level of “diet-gut microenvironment-dysbiosis-harmful bacterial metabolites (TMA),” providing a more comprehensive explanation for the pathological association between obesity and gut microbiota dysbiosis.

From obesity to CMD: key mechanisms of gut microbiota dysbiosis

In the intricate interplay between obesity and cardiovascular health, the gut microbiota emerges as a critical mediator, influencing systemic inflammation, metabolic homeostasis, and vascular integrity through its diverse array of metabolites and byproducts. Disruptions in gut microbial composition, or dysbiosis, commonly observed in obese individuals, lead to altered production of bioactive compounds that can either exacerbate or mitigate coronary microcirculatory dysfunction. Key among these are trimethylamine N-oxide (TMAO), short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), bile acids (BAs), and lipopolysaccharides (LPS) resulting from increased intestinal permeability, each contributing uniquely to the pathogenesis of obesity-related CMD through molecular pathways involving lipid metabolism, inflammation, oxidative stress, and endothelial function. These metabolites are absorbed into the circulation and can be detected in various host tissues [48].

Trimethylamine N-oxide (TMAO)

Trimethylamine N-oxide (TMAO) is a metabolite derived from gut microbiota, produced from dietary precursors such as phosphatidylcholine, L-carnitine, and choline, which are metabolized by intestinal bacteria into trimethylamine and subsequently oxidized to TMAO in the liver [49]. Notably, obesity is often accompanied by dysbiosis of the gut microbiota, which may exacerbate the excessive production of TMAO; moreover, elevated levels of TMAO have been confirmed to be closely associated with the risk of various cardiovascular diseases, including atherosclerosis and heart failure, playing a significant role in obesity-related cardiovascular complications [50]. Recent studies have confirmed for the first time through invasive examinations that the TMAO levels in the CMD group are significantly higher than in the non-CMD group (5.28 µM vs. 2.41 µM, p < 0.001), indicating a close association with CMD [51]. This finding is consistent with a previous study related to inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), which found that intestinal TMAO concentrations were negatively correlated with flow-mediated vasodilation and coronary flow reserve and jointly influenced microvascular function with systemic inflammatory markers [52]. Furthermore, chronic low-grade inflammation and metabolic disorders in the obese state may further amplify this effect. However, the specific mechanism by which TMAO causes coronary microcirculatory dysfunction remains unclear. It is speculated that TMAO may disrupt microvascular endothelial function and impair blood flow homeostasis regulation, thereby becoming a key driver of CMD development in obese individuals. The following discussion explores, at the molecular mechanism level, how TMAO synergizes with obesity to exacerbate coronary microcirculatory dysfunction, aiming to provide new insights into the specific mechanisms underlying TMAO-induced CMD.

Under obese conditions, TMAO first lays the foundation for lipid deposition in the microcirculation primarily by interfering with cholesterol metabolism.Obesity is frequently accompanied by lipid metabolism disorders, and TMAO further inhibits the reverse cholesterol transport (RCT) pathway. Key mechanisms include reducing hepatic expression of cholesterol 7α-hydroxylase (CYP7A1) and cholesterol 27-hydroxylase (CYP27A1) expression in the liver, ultimately leading to cholesterol accumulation in macrophages. It also downregulates the intestinal cholesterol absorption transporter (NPC1L1) and cholesterol efflux transporter (ABCG5/8), reducing net intestinal cholesterol absorption [53]. simultaneously upregulates expression of the macrophage scavenger receptors CD36 and SR-A1, enhancing cholesterol uptake and ultimately promoting macrophage transformation into foam cells – an early hallmark event in atherosclerosis [54]. Secondly, TMAO amplifies obesity-related chronic inflammation’s damage to microcirculation by activating inflammatory signaling pathways. Obesity itself induces systemic low-grade inflammation, and TMAO further activates the nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB) pathway, increasing expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-6) and intercellular adhesion molecule-1 (ICAM-1) while suppressing the anti-inflammatory factor IL-10, thereby exacerbating inflammatory infiltration in coronary microcirculation [55,56]. Furthermore, TMAO disrupts the already compromised microvascular endothelial function in obesity by inducing oxidative stress. Obesity is frequently accompanied by heightened oxidative stress, and TMAO further inhibits the SIRT3-SOD2-mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (ROS) signaling pathway, leading to excessive ROS accumulation [57]. simultaneously impairing endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS) activity and reducing nitric oxide (NO) bioavailability – NO being a key molecule for maintaining microcirculatory vasodilation, whose deficiency directly contributes to obesity-related coronary microvascular vasodilation impairment [58,59]. Building upon this, TMAO further exacerbates endothelial dysfunction through multiple direct pathways:First, it activates the inflammatory mediator high mobility group box 1 (HMGB1), which disrupts tight junction protein expression in endothelial cells by stimulating TLR4-ERK1/2 and NF-κB signaling pathways. This increases endothelial permeability (particularly under obesity-induced elevated vascular wall pressure), facilitating LDL entry into the intima and subsequent oxidation [60–62]. Second, activation via the PKC/NF-κB pathway induces endothelial dysfunction and upregulates expression of vascular cell adhesion molecule-1 (VCAM-1) and intercellular adhesion molecule-1 (ICAM-1) in human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs), enhancing monocyte adhesion to the endothelium. This exacerbates monocyte infiltration, which is already increased in obesity. This process further promotes macrophage differentiation and accelerates endothelial dysfunction [63,64]. Additionally, TMAO inhibits endothelial cell proliferation, impairing the endothelium’s self-repair capacity [65]. Concurrently, TMAO enhances platelet reactivity, exacerbating obesity-associated microcirculatory thrombosis risks. Obesity is frequently accompanied by platelet hyperreactivity [66]. TMAO promotes platelet hyperreactivity and aggregation by increasing platelet sensitivity to adenosine diphosphate (ADP) and thrombin, thereby facilitating the release of intracellular calcium ions (Ca2 +) from stores (primarily via activation of the IP3 signaling pathway). This ultimately elevates the risk of thrombosis in vivo [67]. which may induce microthrombus formation in coronary microcirculation, exacerbating blood flow obstruction. Finally, TMAO can affect the heart, leading to fibrosis and maladaptive remodeling. It activates the ROS/TXNIP/NLRP3 inflammasome and TGF-β1/Smad3 signaling pathways, triggering fibrosis and maladaptive cardiac remodeling [50], thereby indirectly compressing coronary microvessels and exacerbating microcirculatory perfusion impairment. In summary, within the obesity context, TMAO mounts a “multi-target attack” on coronary microcirculation through synergistic mechanisms: disrupting cholesterol metabolism, amplifying inflammation, intensifying oxidative stress, compromising endothelial integrity, promoting thrombosis, and inducing myocardial remodeling. This synergistic effect positions TMAO as a key driver of obesity-associated coronary microcirculatory dysfunction. Short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs)

Short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), primarily comprising acetate, propionate, and butyrate, are major microbial metabolites produced by gut microbiota through the fermentation of dietary complex car bohydrates such as dietary fiber [68,69]. They confer extensive health benefits spanning anti-inflammatory, immunomodulatory, anti-obesity, anti-diabetic, and cardiovascular protective activities [70]. In obesity, dysbiosis typically leads to a specific reduction in butyrate-producing bacteria and a decrease in total SCFA production [71]. This reduction inhibits GPCR pathways and increases expression of inflammatory factors like INF-γ, resulting in lipid metabolism disorders, heightened inflammation, vascular remodeling, and accelerated arterial thrombosis [72]. Similarly, in myocardial infarction (MI)-associated dysbiosis, reduced SCFA levels impede recovery [50].

Direct effects of SCFAs

Indeed, SCFAs exert positive effects on vascular endothelial function and inflammatory responses through multiple molecular mechanisms. Among these, their direct protective action on microvascular endothelial cells stands as one of the most compelling findings in current SCFA-cardiovascular disease research. Existing research has clearly demonstrated how propionate directly counteracts obesity-related pathological factors damaging coronary microvasculature at the molecular level: Oxidized low-density lipoprotein (ox-LDL), a key pathological product of obesity and dyslipidemia, has been shown to impair endothelial cell function [73]. Further research has found that oxidized low-density lipoprotein (ox-LDL) can induce the expression of the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress chaperone protein GRP78 in endothelial cells and activate downstream signaling pathways such as PERK/eIF2α, IRE1/JNK, and ATF6, leading to coronary microvascular dysfunction [74]. Meanwhile, propionate can significantly improve the dysfunction of human cardiac microvascular endothelial cells (HCMECs) induced by ox-LDL by directly inhibiting the ER stress activated by ox-LDL and its downstream signaling pathways, thereby reversing the decrease in eNOS phosphorylation levels and restoring normal NO production. This mechanism provides direct experimental evidence for the protective role of short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) on coronary microvessels [74]. Additionally, animal studies have further elucidated these effects. In angiotensin II-induced hypertensive mice (a model simulating obesity-related hypertension), oral administration of propionate effectively reduced cardiac hypertrophy, myocardial fibrosis, and vascular dysfunction in the mice [75].

Intravenous infusion of acetic acid significantly increases coronary blood flow and cardiac output, a mechanism associated with vasodilation induced by adenosine release. Studies demonstrate acetic acid’s regulatory effect on systemic microvascular networks via the circulatory system, providing early evidence for systemic vascular regulation by SCFAs [76]. Butyrate inhibits angiotensin II (AngII)-induced downregulation of GPR41 and GPR43, with AngII being a known inducer of endothelial dysfunction [77]. Concurrently, butyrate and propionate specifically regulate endothelial function by inhibiting histone deacetylases (HDACs), thereby exerting anti-inflammatory effects [78,79]. Furthermore, butyrate and acetate reduce NADPH oxidase and mitochondrial ROS production. This decrease in ROS levels helps maintain NO bioavailability, further supporting the conclusion that SCFAs protect endothelial function through the aforementioned mechanisms [77]. However, it is noteworthy that the effects of acetic acid are not always unidirectionally beneficial; its effects may exhibit paradoxes that are dose-dependent or context-dependent. In a study on hemodialysis, high concentrations of acetic acid (38 mmol/L) not only increased nitric oxide synthase (NOS) activity, leading to excessive vasodilation and hemodynamic instability, but also enhanced the synthesis of tumor necrosis factor (TNF-α) [80]. This contradicts the anti-inflammatory properties of acetic acid observed in most studies [81]. This finding suggests that at physiological or therapeutic concentrations, acetic acid primarily exerts its effects through beneficial signaling pathways; however, at pathological or supraphysiological concentrations, it may produce negative effects by aberrantly activating certain pathways. This complexity indicates that strict control over the dosing of acetic acid must be maintained when designing future clinical interventions, taking into account its different metabolic pathways and effects under various pathological conditions.

Indirect, gut-barrier – mediated effects of SCFAs

Beyond these direct effects, SCFAs (particularly butyrate) are crucial for maintaining the integrity of the intestinal epithelial barrier. They reduce intestinal permeability by activating the AMPK signaling pathway and promoting the dynamic assembly of tight junction proteins, thereby significantly increasing trans-epithelial resistance (TER) and decreasing paracellular permeability [82,83]. This effectively prevents the translocation of bacterial pro-inflammatory molecules, such as LPS, into the circulatory system.

Short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) serve as systemic regulators of microvascular health, with effects that extend far beyond the gut. While SCFAs are produced in the intestine and help maintain intestinal integrity, studies have shown that they are absorbed into the circulation and directly regulate the functions of the coronary arteries, kidneys, heart, and brain [72]. They directly reduce endothelial cell activation, increase nitric oxide (NO) production, and decrease reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels. This indicates that SCFAs have a direct systemic effect on the microvascular system, rather than merely functioning indirectly through the improvement of gut health.This means SCFAs are not merely metabolites confined to the gut, but powerful systemic signaling molecules.

Bile acids (BAs)

Bile acids (BAs) are amphipathic molecules synthesized from cholesterol in the liver, primarily categorized into two classes: primary bile acids, such as cholic acid and chenodeoxycholic acid; and secondary bile acids, such as lithocholic acid (LCA) and deoxycholic acid (DCA) [84]. Their core physiological function is to participate in the digestive process within the intestine, playing a crucial role in the absorption of dietary fats. Beyond this traditional lipid digestion function, bile acids also act as important signaling molecules by binding to various receptors, with the farnesoid X receptor (FXR) and the G protein-coupled bile acid receptor 1 (TGR5/GPBAR1) being their primary targets [85].

In the state of obesity, the homeostasis of bile acids undergoes significant disruption. Previous studies have reported an increase in hepatic bile acid production in obese individuals, accompanied by elevated levels of bile acids in the systemic circulation [86,87]. Under normal physiological conditions, bile acids maintain dynamic equilibrium through the enterohepatic circulation; however, obesity leads to dysbiosis of the gut microbiome: on one hand, it enhances the activity of 7α-hydroxylase in the intestine, promoting an increase in secondary bile acid levels; on the other hand, it suppresses the expression of the bile acid synthesis enzyme CYP7A1 in the liver, ultimately resulting in cholesterol accumulation in the circulatory system [88,89]. Further research indicates that the changes in gut microbiome composition induced by obesity are associated with alterations in the conversion rate from primary to secondary bile acids, which may interfere with bile acid-mediated FXR and TGR5 signaling [90].

Specifically regarding vascular effects, FXR is expressed in both the heart and coronary arteries [91,92]. In cultured endothelial cells, chenodeoxycholic acid (CDCA) and FXR agonists can promote eNOS expression [93]; however, in contrast, chronic FXR stimulation may impair NO-dependent vasodilation [94]. This dual effect of “acute promotion of eNOS expression and chronic impairment of NO function” suggests a complex dose- and time-dependent role of FXR.

TGR5 expression has also been confirmed in hepatic sinusoidal endothelial cells and cardiomyocytes [95,96]. Under physiological conditions, activation of TGR5 by bile acids in the intestine promotes the release of glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1), which itself possesses well-established vasoprotective effects [97]. Furthermore, studies indicate that certain bile acids (e.g. taurocholate (DCT)) induce concentration-dependent relaxation in the aorta. This process can be blocked by endothelial cell detachment or nitric oxide synthase (NOS) inhibitors, suggesting a mechanism mediated by M3 muscarinic receptors (M3R) and nitric oxide (NO) [98].

Beyond receptor-dependent mechanisms, bile acids influence vascular tone via non-receptor pathways: they interact with large-conductance Ca2 + -activated potassium (BKCa) channels on vascular smooth muscle cells, inducing membrane hyperpolarization and vasodilation [99]. These receptor-dependent pathways involving FXR/TGR5/muscarinic receptors, alongside non-receptor-mediated mechanisms through potassium channels, collectively constitute the complex pathways by which bile acids influence vascular tone. This reflects their direct, multidimensional regulation of microcirculation – a regulation that extends beyond simple systemic metabolic effects to the specific cellular and molecular interactions within the vascular wall. Furthermore, bile acids have been demonstrated to play a pivotal role in mediating oxidative stress, reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, mitochondrial dysfunction, and cellular damage, and are closely associated with CVD and metabolic disorders, thereby contributing to the maintenance of cardiovascular function and health [98,100]. At the pathophysiological level, bile acids can induce hepatocytes to produce pro-inflammatory cytokines [85]. Conversely, TGR5 activation has been shown to inhibit NLRP3 inflammasome activation, exerting anti-inflammatory effects [101]. In summary, bile acids participate in cardiovascular regulation through multiple mechanisms. The complexity of their actions – such as the dual effects of FXR – provides specific targets for pharmacological interventions in coronary microcirculation. This raises questions about the “optimal bile acid concentration or specific types required to achieve therapeutic benefits,” offering new perspectives for deepening our understanding of cardiovascular-metabolic cross-regulation and the treatment of related diseases.

Increased intestinal permeability and lipopolysaccharide (LPS) translocation

A key consequence of gut microbiota dysbiosis in obesity is the disruption of intestinal mucosal barrier function, leading to increased intestinal permeability, commonly referred to as “leaky gut” [34]. This compromised barrier allows bacterial components, particularly lipopolysaccharides (LPS) from Gram-negative bacteria, as well as other pro-inflammatory molecules, to translocate from the intestinal lumen into the bloodstream [38], resulting in a condition known as “metabolic endotoxemia,” characterized by elevated levels of LPS in the blood [102]. As a central gateway for systemic pathology, leaky gut serves as a prerequisite for the entry of LPS and other bacterial components into the systemic circulation, with the translocation of LPS subsequently directly triggering “metabolic endotoxemia” and “chronic low-grade inflammation” [103], making it a crucial node in regulating systemic inflammatory burden.

Upon entering the bloodstream, LPS activates signaling through Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4), triggering cytokine release that induces systemic inflammation, oxidative stress, and fibrosis, potentially leading to arrhythmias and cardiac injury [104]. Concurrently, elevated LPS levels impair endothelial function by promoting vascular inflammation [105]. More critically, this process forms a self-reinforcing vicious cycle: gut dysbiosis initially increases intestinal permeability (leaky gut), allowing LPS translocation and TLR4 activation; increased TLR4 expression further upregulates protein kinase C, leading to dephosphorylation of the tight junction protein occludin, enlarging the interstitial space, and in turn exacerbating intestinal permeability (i.e. allowing more endotoxin into the circulation) [106]. This cycle – “dysbiosis → intestinal permeability → LPS translocation → TLR4 activation → pro-inflammatory response → epithelial damage and chronic inflammation → worsening intestinal permeability”— [107]—highlights the complex feedback between gut microbiota, barrier function, and inflammation: initial dysbiosis disrupts the barrier, and the resulting systemic inflammation further exacerbates intestinal barrier dysfunction, creating a pathological cascade amplification effect. The existence of this vicious cycle offers multiple therapeutic targets for disease intervention: restoring symbiotic balance in the gut microbiota, strengthening tight junctions to repair the barrier, or suppressing systemic inflammation – interrupting the cycle at any point may yield cascading benefits for microvascular health. Furthermore, studies have revealed that LPS activates the DNA damage response (DDR), subsequently activating the key enzyme DNA-PKcs. This process promotes mitochondrial fission in cardiomyocytes and disrupts the actin cytoskeleton, ultimately leading to cardiac microvascular dysfunction and sepsis-induced cardiomyopathy. Intervention with the DNA-PKcs inhibitor NU7441 or the mitochondrial fission inhibitor Mdivi-1 significantly improves LPS-induced pathological manifestations such as cardiac systolic/diastolic dysfunction and microvascular injury [108]. Therefore, restoring intestinal barrier integrity through dietary interventions, probiotic or prebiotic supplementation, and administering relevant inhibitors may fundamentally block the systemic inflammatory cascade triggered by metabolic endotoxemia, thereby preventing obesity-induced microvascular dysfunction. Potential role of other gut microbiota metabolites in obesity-induced CMD

Branched-chain amino acids (BCAA)

Branched-chain amino acids (BCAAs), comprising leucine, isoleucine, and valine, are essential amino acids for the human body [109]. In the field of metabolic disease research, epidemiological evidence has established a clear consensus: plasma BCAA levels are significantly elevated in individuals with obesity, insulin resistance, and type 2 diabetes [109]. More clinically significant, elevated BCAA levels serve as early predictive markers for cardiovascular and metabolic diseases. Research indicates they can effectively forecast disease risk even over a decade before clinical symptoms of type 2 diabetes emerge [110]. The mechanism linking BCAAs to metabolic diseases is complex, with the core logic revolving around the “chain of metabolic dysregulation in obesity:” In an obese state, the activity of branched-chain α-ketoacid dehydrogenase (BCKD), a key enzyme in BCAA catabolism, is inhibited. This blockage directly leads to the accumulation of BCAAs and their metabolites in the body. Accumulated BCAAs further activate the mTORC1 signaling pathway. Abnormal activation of this pathway inhibits normal insulin signaling, ultimately triggering insulin resistance [111]. Specifically in CMD, BCAAs may induce endothelial cell stress by activating the mTOR pathway, leading to microvascular remodeling and reduced blood flow reserve. Furthermore, excessive BCAAs alter mitochondrial function, disrupt lipid metabolism, and potentially induce lipotoxicity [111–113]. These mechanisms are highly correlated with the pathophysiological basis of obesity-induced CMD – endothelial dysfunction and inflammation.

However, the relationship between BCAAs and cardiovascular diseases is not a simple linear association. On one hand, high plasma levels of BCAAs are associated with increased disease risk; on the other hand, animal studies have shown that dietary BCAA supplementation can improve hyperlipidemia and inflammation in ApoE−/− mice, thereby alleviating atherosclerosis [114]. Additionally, other research has found that a low BCAA diet can effectively reduce circulating BCAA levels in mice and improve cardiac structure and function following myocardial infarction [115]. This contradiction suggests that the relationship between BCAAs and obesity-induced cardiovascular metabolic disorders is a complex paradox of “pathological states and nutritional signals.” The core issue lies in whether high BCAA levels are a result of metabolic dysfunction caused by pathological states or whether they are a pathogenic factor in themselves. Both scenarios may coexist. Future research needs to shift from merely focusing on the levels of BCAAs to exploring their metabolic pathways and regulatory mechanisms under specific pathological conditions in order to clarify their roles in different contexts.

Endogenous ethanol

Endogenous ethanol is a metabolite produced by the fermentation of carbohydrates by the gut microbiota, typically present at trace levels [116]. In metabolic disorders such as obesity, abnormal proliferation of certain ethanol-producing bacterial species (e.g. Klebsiella pneumoniae) can lead to significantly elevated endogenous ethanol levels, triggering a rare condition known as auto-brewery syndrome (ABS) [117]. Although no direct studies currently demonstrate the direct impact of “endogenous ethanol” on “coronary microcirculation,” a highly plausible indirect causal chain can be established. Obesity has been proven to cause gut microbiota dysbiosis. Concurrently, substantial evidence links high ethanol levels to cardiovascular dysfunction through mechanisms including: ethanol metabolism generating ROS and toxic acetaldehyde, triggering oxidative stress and cellular damage; inducing gut dysbiosis and disrupting the intestinal barrier to cause endotoxemia; activating TLR/NLR-mediated excessive innate and adaptive immune responses, disrupting hepatic and adipose tissue homeostasis, altering secondary bile acid metabolism, reducing short-chain fatty acid production, potentially elevating TMAO levels, and activating formyl peptide receptor 1 (FPR-1). These multi-pathway synergistic effects collectively contribute to vascular dysfunction, atherosclerosis, hypertension, and heart failure [118]. Furthermore, gut microbiota and their metabolites are considered potential contributors to ethanol-induced cardiovascular dysfunction [118]. Thus, it can be inferred that obesity-induced gut dysbiosis may trigger systemic inflammation and oxidative stress by increasing endogenous ethanol production, ultimately leading to endothelial dysfunction in coronary microcirculation. This pathophysiological axis – gut-ethanol-microcirculation – though not directly studied, is highly plausible and offers a novel perspective for understanding the complex pathophysiology of obesity-induced coronary microcirculatory dysfunction.

Indole metabolites

Indole metabolites, such as indole-3-propionic acid (IPA), are products of gut microbiota metabolism of the dietary essential amino acid tryptophan and serve as a classic example of the “gut-heart axis.” Research indicates that IPA plays a central role in blood pressure regulation, anti-inflammation, metabolic balance, and atherosclerosis [119,120].

IPA exerts dual effects on the cardiovascular system. On one hand, animal studies demonstrate that IPA increases blood pressure and cardiac contractility in rats while inducing vasoconstriction [121]. This pressor and vasoconstrictive effect aligns with pathophysiological alterations in CMD (e.g. endothelial dysfunction and vasoconstriction), potentially directly exacerbating microcirculatory dysfunction. Conversely, indole compounds themselves possess anti-inflammatory potential [122]. This “double-edged sword” effect likely depends on specific metabolites, concentrations, host status, and balance with other signaling pathways. This suggests that in obesity, certain mechanisms (e.g. microbiota composition, enzyme activity) may increase production of harmful indole metabolites or decrease production of beneficial ones. Future research should focus on specific dysbalances in gut microbiota-host metabolic interactions, rather than the effects of any single metabolite.

Imidazolepropionic acid (ImP)

Imidazolepropionic acid (ImP) is a metabolite derived from histidine metabolism by gut microbiota [123]. Related studies indicate that circulating ImP levels are significantly associated with the risk of type 2 diabetes, obesity, and heart failure [124]. In HIV-infected individuals, elevated plasma ImP levels correlate with obstructive coronary artery disease [125].

Research on ImP provides a robust chain of evidence from correlation to causation. Mechanistic studies reveal that ImP impairs glucose tolerance and insulin signaling [124]. More critically, the gut microbial metabolite imidazolepropionic acid (ImP) has been demonstrated to activate the ImP-imidazoline-1-receptor (I1R)-mTOR pathway in myeloid cells. This pathway induces systemic and local vascular inflammation, compromises vascular endothelial function, directly promotes atherosclerosis formation in animal models, and has been observed in human cohorts. I1R-mTOR pathway to induce systemic and local vascular inflammation, impair vascular endothelial function, and directly promote atherosclerosis formation in animal models. It is significantly associated with subclinical atherosclerosis (particularly metabolically active atherosclerosis) in human cohorts. Furthermore, blocking the ImP-I1R axis inhibits atherosclerosis progression, ImP also holds potential as an early diagnostic biomarker for atherosclerosis [126]. This finding indicates that ImP is not merely a biomarker of metabolic dysfunction but itself a direct pathogenic factor. Its mechanism of action – through impairment of insulin signaling and endothelial function – highly aligns with the core pathology of obesity-induced CMD, making it a highly promising therapeutic target.

Phenylacetylglutamine (PAGln)

Phenylacetylglutamine (PAGln) is a product of intestinal microbial metabolism of dietary phenylalanine [127]. Studies indicate that elevated plasma PAGln levels are strongly associated with heart failure, atherosclerotic heart disease, and major adverse cardiovascular events (MACEs) [128].

The mechanism of action of PAGln is unique and clinically significant. It modulates cellular events by binding to G protein-coupled adrenergic receptors, including α2A, α2B, and β2-adrenergic receptors [127]. This interaction induces a hyperreactive state in platelets, thereby increasing thrombotic risk. Notably, studies reveal that a commonly used β-blocker – carvedilol – can block PAGln’s prothrombotic effects [127]. This finding links the gut microbiota-metabolite axis to classical neuroendocrine regulatory systems and offers a novel perspective on understanding some beneficial effects of beta-blockers. In summary, PAGln may offer a novel explanation for the pathophysiology of coronary microcirculatory dysfunction by influencing both the neuroendocrine system and platelet activity. It not only accounts for why certain patients are prone to microthrombus formation but also provides a pharmacological rationale for the role of beta-blockers in improving microcirculation. Obesity is frequently accompanied by sympathetic nervous system hyperactivation [129]. PAGln exerts its effects by binding to adrenergic receptors such as α2A, α2B, and β2 receptors, suggesting it may serve as a critical link between sympathetic overactivity and increased cardiovascular disease risk in obese individuals. Thus, PAGln may serve as a crucial potential mediator linking obesity-induced neuroendocrine imbalances to consequences such as microvascular dysfunction and cardiovascular disease.

Hydrogen sulfide (H2S)

Hydrogen sulfide (H2S), as a gas-signaling molecule derived from gut microbiota, exerts vasoprotective effects, including reducing ROS stress and inhibiting the NF-κB pathway [130]. Studies indicate that under obesity conditions, H2S bioavailability in blood vessels decreases, leading to microvascular endothelial dysfunction. This further confirms that H2S depletion is a key driver of obesity-mediated microvascular remodeling [7].

The pathophysiological mechanism of H2S deficiency is highly specific. Obesity induces macrophage accumulation in perivascular adipose tissue (PVAT), where these activated macrophages reduce vascular H2S bioavailability through their inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) activity [131]. Moreover, chronic obesity (e.g. high-fat diet-induced) significantly downregulates expression of the H2S synthase cystathionine gamma lyase (CSE) in skeletal muscle, leading to reduced H2S levels in plasma and muscle tissue. Concurrently, the high-sugar, palmitic acid environment associated with obesity further inhibits CSE activity, exacerbating H2S deficiency [132]. This “PVAT-macrophage-H2S” axis presents a precise, targetable intervention point. It refines the macro-concept of obesity-induced vascular damage to specific tissue, cellular, and molecular levels, suggesting that restoring H2S bioavailability by suppressing PVAT inflammation may offer a pathway to improve microcirculatory function.

Succinate

Succinate is a metabolite with a dual role in the pathophysiology of cardiovascular disease. It serves both as a crucial intermediate in the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle and as a signaling molecule acting through the G protein-coupled receptor GPR91 [133,134]. Significantly elevated circulating succinate levels in obese individuals correlate with metabolic abnormalities. This is closely linked to metabolic imbalances and increased intestinal permeability resulting from increased succinate-producing bacteria (e.g. Prevotellaceae, Veillonellaceae) and reduced catabolic bacteria (e.g. Odoribacteraceae, Clostridiaceae) within the gut microbiota. Dietary weight loss interventions can reduce circulating succinate levels by modulating these gut microbiota. Furthermore, the impact of spontaneous microbiota evolution on succinate levels is independent of weight changes and correlates with glucose homeostasis, suggesting circulating succinate may serve as a potential biomarker for obesity-related cardiovascular disease risk [135].

Research indicates [136] that the succinate-GPR91 axis serves as a critical pathway linking metabolic dysregulation to cardiovascular injury in metabolic diseases (obesity, hypertension, diabetes, etc.). In these diseases, abnormal activation of this axis triggers pathological changes through multiple mechanisms: On one hand, GPR91-activated succinate drives macrophage polarization toward a pro-inflammatory phenotype, promotes inflammatory cytokine release to exacerbate chronic inflammation, and activates signaling pathways such as ERK1/2 and CaMKIIδ to accelerate myocardial hypertrophy. On the other hand, it induces mitochondrial fragmentation by activating the PKCδ/ERK1/2 pathway, which, combined with mitochondrial reverse electron transport, generates excessive ROS, leading to mitochondrial dysfunction. These processes collectively contribute to cardiovascular damage. Furthermore, studies confirm that the succinate-GPR91 axis indirectly influences cardiac remodeling and contractile/relaxation function by regulating intracellular Ca2 + levels and energy metabolism-related signaling in cardiomyocytes, further highlighting its pivotal role in metabolic disease-associated cardiovascular pathologies. These findings suggest that two seemingly distinct sources of metabolic stress – systemic metabolic disorders and gut microbiota dysbiosis – can converge on the same signaling pathway (the succinate-GPR91 axis) to jointly drive cardiovascular disease. Succinate is thus regarded as a “hub molecule” connecting host cell metabolism with gut microbial metabolism. Conclusion

Coronary microcirculatory dysfunction, as a key subclinical lesion in obesity-related cardiovascular injury, exhibits a close association with gut microbiota dysbiosis. This provides a novel perspective for understanding obesity-induced cardiac pathological processes and their underlying mechanisms. For obesity – a global epidemic affecting over half of the world’s adult population – the in-depth analysis of its pathological cascade involving gut microbiota imbalance and CMD further strengthens the evidence chain linking the “gut-heart axis” to obesity-related cardiac pathogenesis. Gut dysbiosis exacerbates CMD through harmful metabolites such as TMAO and LPS, ultimately becoming a major driver of adverse cardiovascular events in obese populations. This review systematically examines the molecular mechanisms through which dysbiosis mediates the association between obesity and coronary microcirculatory dysfunction via key metabolites (e.g. TMAO, SCFAs, bile acids, ImP, PAGln). It reveals the core regulatory networks of the gut-heart axis in microvascular structural remodeling and functional impairment. simultaneously providing theoretical support for developing early warning biomarkers, preventive strategies, and targeted therapies for obesity-related CMD by targeting gut microbiota and their metabolic pathways. It also lays the groundwork for further elucidating interactions among microbial metabolites and clinical translation pathways for personalized interventions.

However, despite significant progress in studying the role of gut dysbiosis in obesity-induced coronary microcirculatory dysfunction, several bottlenecks and gaps remain: The fundamental pathophysiological mechanisms of CMD are not fully elucidated; the precise causal relationship and specific molecular mechanisms linking microbial metabolites to CMD require exploration; and single indicators like the F/B ratio cannot comprehensively reflect the complex functions of the gut microbiota. Notably, Professor Zhao Liping’s team at Shanghai Jiao Tong University proposed the “Keystone Functional Group” model [137], categorizing gut microbiota into health-associated “Keystone Functional Groups” and disease-aggravating “Pathogenic Functional Groups,” which maintain a seesaw-like equilibrium. This model shifts the assessment of gut health from “quantity” to “function,” providing a theoretical foundation for personalized interventions. Moreover, since dysregulation of the keystone functional group precedes the emergence of disease symptoms, it opens new pathways for disease prevention, treatment, and health management and is expected to become a new standard for future gut health assessment.

Based on the above, future research should: Conduct multi-omics integration studies to overcome limitations of single indicators, shifting focus to exploring microbial functions and metabolic pathways; Advance clinical translational research to identify CMD risk biomarkers and validate their value; Develop novel targeted intervention strategies for the “gut-heart axis,” such as specific metabolite inhibitors, signaling pathway blockers, and precision dietary interventions based on individual microbial profiles. In the future, deepening exploration of gut microbiota regulation in obesity and CMD associations not only promises to bridge these knowledge gaps but may also establish a novel diagnostic and therapeutic paradigm for obesity-related CMD – spanning risk prediction to precision interventions – ultimately improving cardiovascular outcomes in obese populations.
