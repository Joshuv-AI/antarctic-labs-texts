Nesterov-Accelerated Robust Federated Learning Over Byzantine Adversaries
Lihan Xu 1 , Yanjie Dong 1 , Gang Wang 2 , Runhao Zeng 1 , Xiaoyi Fan 1 , and Xiping Hu 1

Abstract
We investigate robust federated learning, where a group of workers collaboratively train a shared model under the orchestration of a central server in the presence of Byzantine adversaries capable of arbitrary and potentially malicious behaviors. To simultaneously enhance communication efficiency and robustness against such adversaries, we propose a Byzantine-resilient Nesterov-Accelerated Federated Learning (Byrd-NAFL) algorithm. Byrd-NAFL seamlessly integrates Nesterov’s momentum into the federated learning process alongside Byzantine-resilient aggregation rules to achieve fast and safeguarding convergence against gradient corruption. We establish a finite-time convergence guarantee for Byrd-NAFL under non-convex and smooth loss functions with relaxed assumption on the aggregated gradients. Extensive numerical experiments validate the effectiveness of Byrd-NAFL and demonstrate the superiority over existing benchmarks in terms of convergence speed, accuracy, and resilience to diverse Byzantine attack strategies.

Problem Description and Preliminaries

II-A

Vanilla Federated Learning

We consider an FL system where
N
workers collaboratively train a global model without uploading the local raw data
\{\mathcal{D}_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}
to the central server.
The primary objective of FL is to minimize the global loss
f(x)
that is defined as the average local loss
f_{n}(x)
over the local dataset
{\cal D}_{n}
as

\tilde{x}_{opt}=\argmin_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(x):=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}f_{n}(x)

(1)

where

is the
d

f_{n}(x):=\mathbb{E}_{\xi_{n}\sim\mathcal{D}_{n}}[\ell(x;\xi_{n})]
with
\ell(x;\xi_{n})
is the loss evaluated at model
x
on a mini-batch
\xi_{n}
sampled from the local dataset
\mathcal{D}_{n}
,
n=1,\ldots,N
.

Per each communication round, the server broadcasts the current model
x_{k}
to all workers, and each worker
n
locally can compute the stochastic gradient
g_{n,k}=\nabla\ell(x_{k};\zeta_{n,k})
that is an unbiased estimator to
\nabla f_{n}(x_{k})
, i.e.,
\nabla f_{n}(x_{k})=\mathds{E}[g_{n,k}]
with mini-batch of data uniformly sampled from
\mathcal{D}_{n}
,
n=1,2,\ldots,N
.
The server requires different information for aggregation.
For instance, the aggregation policy of FedGrad
[
1
]
per each iteration
k
is

{\mbox{AGG}([g_{n,k}]_{n=1}^{N})}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}g_{n,k}

(2)

where
g_{n,k}
denotes the local gradient per worker
n
.

II-B

Byzantine-Resilient Aggregation

An illustration of Byzantine-resilient aggregation.

When a subset of the
N
workers are Byzantine, the aggregation rule in (

Without loss of generality, we assume that the first
H

Byzantine-resilient aggregation requires the number of Byzantine adversaries to satisfy
H>N/2

[

The Byzantine-resilient FL aims at minimizing the population loss of the honest workers as

x_{opt}=\argmin_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(x):=\frac{1}{H}\sum_{n=1}^{H}f_{n}(x).

(3)

To solve (

\tilde{g}_{n,k}=\begin{cases}g_{n,k}&n=1,\ldots,H\\
g^{*}_{n,k}&n=H+1,\ldots,N.\end{cases}

Therefore, it must adopt a Byzantine-resilient aggregation rule to mitigate the impact of adversarial inputs.
Based on (

\nabla_{k}={\mbox{BR-AGG}([\tilde{g}_{n,k}]_{n=1}^{N})}.

(5)

To enable Byzantine-resilient FL algorithms, the aggregation rule in (

16

\displaystyle\langle\mathds{E}[\nabla_{k}],\nabla f(x_{k})\rangle

\displaystyle\mathds{E}[\|\nabla_{k}\|^{2}]

\displaystyle\leq c_{1}\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|^{2}+c_{2}

(7)

where

Compared to the conventional

Moreover, Assumption

First proposed by Yurii Nesterov, the Nesterov’s momentum can leverage the historical gradient information to accelerate and stablized the convergence by using mini-batch of samples per recursion

]

The standard Nesterov’s momentum recursion can be cast as

\displaystyle y_{k}

\displaystyle=x_{k}-\eta\nabla f(x_{k})

\displaystyle=y_{k}+\beta(y_{k}-y_{k-1})

Substituting (

\displaystyle=x_{k}-x_{k+1}-\eta\nabla f(x_{k}).

\displaystyle z_{k+1}

\displaystyle=\beta z_{k}+\nabla f(x_{k})

(10a)

\displaystyle y_{k}

\displaystyle=\beta z_{k+1}+\nabla f(x_{k})

(10b)

\displaystyle x_{k+1}

\displaystyle=x_{k}-\eta y_{k}.

Based on (

\frac{1}{\eta}(x_{k}\!-\!x_{k+1})=(1+\beta)\nabla f(x_{k})\!+\!\sum_{t=0}^{k-1}\beta^{k+1-t}\nabla f(x_{t}).

Remark 1

The right-hand side (RHS) of (

III-A

Byrd-NAFL Algorithm

In the previous section, we demonstrated that Nesterov’s momentum accelerates and stabilizes convergence by incorporating historical gradient information.
Motivated by such insight, we propose to leverage historical Byzantine-resilient gradients to develop Byrd-NAFL to operate robustly in the presence of Byzantine adversaries.
The detailed procedures of the Byrd-NAFL are summarized in Algorithm
1
.

•

On the worker side, each worker
n
maintains a local copy of the global model
x_{k}
.
At each iteration
k
, each honest worker
n
uniformly samples a mini-batch
\zeta_{n,k}
from its local dataset
\mathcal{D}_{n}
, and computes the corresponding local stochastic gradient
g_{n,k}
.
At the step
5
of the Byrd-NAFL algorithm, all workers upload their local information to the server, where honest workers upload the local stochastic gradients
[g_{n,k}]_{n=1}^{H}
, while Byzantine adversaries may upload arbitrary misleading information.

•

The server is responsible for the Byzantine-resilient aggregation and model updates per recursion
k
.
After receiving local information from both honest workers and Byzantine adversaries, the server performs the aggregation to collect a Byzantine-resilient gradient
\nabla_{k}
at step
6
of the Byrd-NAFL algorithm.
The Byzantine-resilient gradient
\nabla_{k}
is then used to update the global model at step
7
of the Byrd-NAFL algorithm.

Overall, each worker computes a local stochastic gradient and uploads it to the server.
Honest workers send true gradients, while Byzantine adversaries may send arbitrary misleading.
The server applies a Byzantine-resilient aggregation to obtain a robust gradient, which is then used to update the global model via the Nesterov’s momentum. Such process repeats each iteration to ensure reliable convergence.
In the subsequent subsection, we establish the theoretical convergence guarantees of the proposed Byrd-NAFL algorithm for smooth and non-convex loss functions.

\displaystyle\frac{\eta(1-\sin\gamma)}{1-\beta}\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq f(v_{0})-f(v_{K})+\Big[\frac{\eta^{2}L^{2}\beta^{4}}{2(1-\beta)^{4}}+\frac{\eta^{2}L}{(1-\beta)^{2}}\Big]\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\|\nabla_{k}\|^{2}.

(29)

Recalling the fact in Assumption 1, we obtain the upper bound of (
III-B
) as

\displaystyle\frac{\eta(1-\sin\gamma)}{1-\beta}\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\Big[\frac{\eta^{2}L^{2}\beta^{4}}{2(1-\beta)^{4}}+\frac{\eta^{2}L}{(1-\beta)^{2}}\Big]c_{1}\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|^{2}

\displaystyle+f(v_{0})-f(v_{K})+\Big[\frac{\eta^{2}L^{2}\beta^{4}}{2(1-\beta)^{4}}+\frac{\eta^{2}L}{(1-\beta)^{2}}\Big]c_{2}K.

(30)

By setting
\eta\leq\nicefrac{{(1-\sin\gamma)(1-\beta)^{3}}}{{c_{1}L[L\beta^{4}+(1-\beta)^{2}]}}
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2(1-\beta)}{\eta K(1-\sin\gamma)}[f(v_{0})-f(v_{K})]

\displaystyle+\frac{\eta Lc_{2}}{1-\sin\gamma}\Big[\frac{L\beta^{4}}{(1-\beta)^{3}}+\frac{2}{1-\beta}\Big].

(31)

Based on Theorem
1
, we observe that the proposed Byrd-NAFL algorithm converges to an
{\cal O}\left(\nicefrac{{\eta}}{{(1-\sin\gamma)(1-\beta)}}\right)
-neighborhood of a local optimum (i.e., the error floor), with a convergence rate of
{\cal O}\left(\nicefrac{{(1-\beta)}}{{\eta K(1-\sin\gamma)}}\right)
under smooth non-convex loss functions. From (
17
), two key insights emerge: 1) the presence of Byzantine adversaries degrades the effectiveness of the classical Nesterov momentum by a multiplicative factor of
1-\sin\gamma
, where
\gamma
quantifies the worst-case Byzantine perturbation; and 2) the error-floor term diminishes with a smaller stepsize
\eta
, albeit at the cost of a slower convergence rate.
When the momentum parameter
\beta
is set to zero, the Byrd-NAFL algorithm reduces to the Byrd-SGD algorithm. Moreover, the allowable range of the stepsize
\eta\leq\nicefrac{{(1-\sin\gamma)(1-\beta)^{3}}}{{c_{1}L[L\beta^{4}+(1-\beta)^{2}]}}
implies that the feasible learning rate shrinks as the worst-case Byzantine perturbation
\gamma
increases.
By choosing the maximal allowable stepsize
\eta\equiv\nicefrac{{(1-\sin\gamma)(1-\beta)^{3}}}{{c_{1}L[L\beta^{4}+(1-\beta)^{2}]}}
, the error floor is further bounded by
{\cal O}\left(\nicefrac{{c_{2}[L\beta^{4}+2(1-\beta)^{2}]}}{{c_{1}[L\beta^{4}+(1-\beta)^{2}]}}\right)
.
This reveals that, by slowing down the convergence rate, a larger momentum factor
\beta
is beneficial in reducing the error floor under the maximum learning rate configuration.

IV-A

Byzantine Attacks

We verify the Byzantine-resilience of our proposed Byrd-NAFL against three major attacks, i.e., Random-noise, Sign-flipping, and Zero-gradient attacks.
In a Random-noise attack, Byzantine adversaries upload gradients sampled from a random noise distribution as

g^{*}_{n,k}\sim\mathcal{N}\Big(\frac{1}{H}\sum_{h=1}^{H}{g}_{h,k},\mu I\Big),n=H+1,\ldots,N

(32)

where the attack strength

In a Sign-flipping attack, Byzantine adversaries flip the global update as

g^{*}_{n,k}=\frac{\mu}{H}\sum_{h=1}^{H}{g}_{h,k},n=H+1,\ldots,N

(33)

where the attack strength
\mu
is set as
\mu=-10

In a Zero-gradient attack, Byzantine adversaries upload crafted vectors that cancel out the mean update at the server as

g^{*}_{n,k}=-\frac{1}{N-H}\sum_{h=1}^{H}{g}_{h,k},\quad n=H+1,\ldots,N.

(34)

We verify the performance of our proposed Byrd-NAFL algorithm by using a Byzantine-resilient aggregation and the Nesterov’s momentum. The detailed setups are as follows.

Federated objective and server-side update

The server minimizes the average honest worker loss

x_{\mathrm{opt}}=\arg\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(x)\triangleq\frac{1}{H}\sum_{n=1}^{H}f_{n}(x)

(35)

where the local loss is
f_{n}(x)=\mathbb{E}_{\xi_{n}\sim\mathcal{D}_{n}}\left[\ell(x;\xi_{n})\right]
.

At iteration
k
, honest worker
n
draws a mini-batch
\zeta_{n,k}\subset

\mathcal{D}_{n}
and computes
g_{n,k}=\nabla\ell(x_{k};\zeta_{n,k})
.
At iteration
k
, the server receives

\tilde{g}_{n,k}=\begin{cases}g_{n,k}&n=1,\ldots,H\\
g^{*}_{n,k}&n=H+1,\ldots,N.\end{cases}

(36)

Based on the received information (
36
), the aggregated gradient is obtained via a Byzantine-resilient rule as

\nabla_{k}={\mbox{BR-AGG}([\tilde{g}_{n,k}]_{n=1}^{N})}.

(37)

Byrd-NAFL performs the Nesterov recursion on the server

\displaystyle z_{k+1}

\displaystyle=\beta z_{k}+\nabla_{k}

(38a)

\displaystyle y_{k}

\displaystyle=\beta z_{k+1}+\nabla_{k}

(38b)

\displaystyle x_{k+1}

\displaystyle=x_{k}-\eta\,y_{k}

(38c)

where
z_{0}=0
,
y_{0}=0
, and
x_{0}
is initialized from the Kaiming normal distribution.

Binary classification tasks (COVTYPE dataset)

Each sample is
(\phi,y)
with
\phi\in\mathbb{R}^{54}
and
y\in\{-1,+1\}
. We use
\ell_{2}
-regularized logistic regression with parameter vector
x\in\mathbb{R}^{54}
and regularization coefficient
\rho=0.01

\ell(x;(\phi,y))=\log\!\bigl(1+\exp(-y\,\phi^{\top}x)\bigr)+\frac{\rho}{2}\|x\|_{2}^{2}.

Each image is flattened to
u\in\mathbb{R}^{784}
with label

.

32

(W_{1},b_{1},W_{2},b_{2})

W_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{32\times 784}

\displaystyle h

\displaystyle p(x;u)

(41)

V
Choice of Different Aggregation Rules

In order to enhance comprehension of the function of aggregation in the context of federated optimization under Byzantine threats, we investigate the performance of the following aggregation rules – Mean, CwMed,GeoMed and Krum – when employed in conjunction with Nesterov acceleration. Whilst preceding sections have emphasized the superior performance of Byrd-NAFL (Krum + Nesterov), this subsection provides a more comprehensive analysis of the interaction between different aggregation rules and Nesterov momentum.
From Figures
2
and
3
, we have the following key observations and insights.

Although Mean aggregation performs competitively under the No-attack setting, it is extremely vulnerable to adversarial manipulation, particularly under Sign-flipping and Zero-gradient attacks. In both the COVTYPE and MNIST datasets, the combination of Mean + Nesterov leads to a significant deterioration in performance, with accuracy dropping to 56.15% and 10.73%, respectively, and failing to recover during the training process.
This finding indicates that Nesterov’s acceleration may not be sufficient to counterbalance the deficiency in Mean aggregation, and it could potentially amplify instability when malicious gradients prevail.

In Table
I
and
III
, CwMed enhances the Mean aggregation by incorporating per-dimension robustness, demonstrating moderate resilience to attacks. Nevertheless, the system continues to demonstrate performance degradation in the presence of more sophisticated adversarial scenarios. To illustrate this point, consider the MNIST dataset. When subjected to Sign-flipping, the CwMed + Nesterov attains a mere 83.34%, a significant deviation from the 89.75% achieved by Byrd-NAFL (Krum + Nesterov).
The finding suggests that CwMed’s local filtering approach is deficient in its lack of a global perspective, which is necessary to ensure the complete exclusion of coordinated adversarial patterns.

It is evident that GeoMed demonstrates superior robustness in comparison to CwMed and Mean, particularly when employed in conjunction with Nesterov. The algorithm demonstrates near-competitive accuracy to Byrd-NAFL under No-attack and Random-noise conditions, with results of 79.49% (COVTYPE, No-attack) and 89.78% (MNIST, Random-noise), respectively. However, under targeted attacks such as Sign-flipping and Zero-gradient, GeoMed’s performance becomes less stable, highlighting GeoMed’s sensitivity to outliers in high-dimensional gradient space.

Krum, employed in Byrd-NAFL, selects the gradient closest to the majority, excelling in adversarial settings. On the COVTYPE dataset, Krum attains 79.62% (Sign-flipping) and 79.36% (Zero-gradient), consistently outperforming GeoMed (77.36%, 77.07%) and CwMed (75.70%, 75.72%). As demonstrated in Figure
2
and
3
, Byrd-NAFL exhibits a stable and highly accurate trajectory, signifying Krum’s capacity to filter out malicious updates, a capability that is augmented by Nesterov’s accelerated convergence. It is evident that Krum is the most robust aggregation strategy, particularly when employed in conjunction with Nesterov. The efficacy of Krum approach is predicated on the selection of a single well-behaved gradient vector based on proximity, which enables the filtration of extreme or adversarial values. When combined with Nesterov’s momentum, Krum enables both robustness and fast convergence, leading to state-of-the-art performance across all attack settings.

In summary, the results clearly indicate that both Krum and Nesterov consistently outperform other methods across various performance metrics.
Notably, they achieve the highest level of top-1 accuracy when subjected to various attack scenarios, especially during Sign-flipping and Zero-gradient attacks, across both datasets examined.
Moreover, the convergence curves observed in the experiments reveal a smooth and stable progression throughout the training process. Even when faced with significant adversarial interference, suggesting that they are resilient to perturbations.
Overall, the findings underscore the effectiveness of Krum and Nesterov in achieving robust performance, marked by high accuracy, stable convergence, and resilience to adversarial attacks, making them strong candidates for use in security-sensitive applications.

VI
Concluding Remarks

In this work, we proposed Byrd-NAFL, a Byzantine-resilient federated optimization algorithm that integrates Krum aggregation with Nesterov momentum.
Our objective was to jointly enhance robustness against Byzantine adversaries through Byzantine-resilient aggregation and improve convergence rate in FL systems with Nesterov’s momentum.
We have theoretically established the
{\cal O}\left(\nicefrac{{(1-\beta)}}{{\eta K(1-\sin\gamma)}}\right)
-convergence rate of our proposed Byrd-NAFL algorithm and quantitatively revealed the impacts of Byzantine adversaries, Nesterov’s momentum, and stochastic gradient noise on the learning error.
Through extensive experiments on both the COVTYPE and MNIST datasets, we demonstrate that Byrd-NAFL consistently outperforms existing baselines across a wide range of Byzantine attacks, e.g., Sign-flipping, and Zero-gradient perturbations, and Random-noise.
