Network pharmacology guided development and optimization of curcumin and sesame oil nanostructured lipid carriers for psoriasis
Authors: Maedeh Tahvilian, Masomeh Mehrabi, Shima Dalvand, Fatemeh Mahmoudi, Zhila Izadi, Soroor Sadegh Malvajerd
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Psoriasis is a chronic inflammatory skin disease that requires long-term management. Natural compounds such as curcumin and sesame oil have shown promising antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties; in contrast, their practical application is hampered by poor solubility, low stability, and limited skin permeability. This study aimed to design, optimize, and characterize a nanostructured lipid carrier (NLC) co-loaded with curcumin and sesame oil for potential dermal application in psoriasis, integrating a network pharmacology approach to validate therapeutic relevance. The NLC formulation was optimized using a response surface methodology (RSM) approach with two independent variables liquid-to-solid lipid ratio (0.25–0.40) and homogenization time (5–10 min) to minimize particle size (PS) and polydispersity index (PDI). The optimized formulation achieved a PS of approximately 132.420 nm, PDI of 0.208, and a desirability score of 0.903. Physicochemical characterizations including DLS, SEM, and XRD confirmed the nanoscale, spherical, and amorphous nature of the particles. In vitro release studies demonstrated a sustained release profile of curcumin over 24 h. MTT assay on human fibroblasts indicated good biocompatibility, while DPPH assay confirmed strong antioxidant activity comparable to free curcumin and sesame oil. The co-loaded curcumin–sesame oil NLC demonstrated desirable physicochemical properties, providing a promising platform for future preclinical investigations in inflammatory skin disorders such as psoriasis. Network pharmacology analysis identified 74 overlapping targets of curcumin, sesame oil, and psoriasis, with key hub genes (e.g., TNF, IL1B, IL10, CASP3) involved in inflammation, oxidative stress, and apoptosis. Gene ontology (GO) and Kyoto encyclopedia of genes and genomes (KEGG) enrichment revealed modulation of IL-17, TNF, and oxidative stress pathways, underscoring the multi-target therapeutic potential of curcumin and sesame oil in psoriasis. Collectively, this study establishes a dual-evidence strategy combining in silico validation and formulation science to support the translational development of curcumin and sesame oil-based nanotherapeutics for inflammatory skin diseases like psoriasis.

Introduction

Psoriasis is a chronic autoimmune skin disease characterized by inflammation, redness, thickening of the skin, and scaling. With a high prevalence worldwide, psoriasis significantly affects patients’ quality of life1,2. Conventional treatments, including corticosteroids, immunosuppressants, and biologics, often face limitations such as adverse side effects, high costs, and the development of drug resistance2. Consequently, there is growing interest in exploring alternative therapeutic strategies based on naturally derived compounds with anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties3. Curcumin, a hydrophobic polyphenol derived from the rhizomes of curcuma longa, has been widely studied for its ability to modulate multiple inflammatory and oxidative stress pathways4. Sesame oil (Sesamum indicum L.), another plant-derived agent, possesses unique skin-penetration-enhancing properties and exhibits intrinsic antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects5,6. Several studies suggest that curcumin and sesame oil may contribute to therapeutic benefits in skin disorders characterized by inflammation, including psoriasis7–9. To support the scientific rationale for this combination, we conducted a network pharmacology analysis, which revealed shared molecular targets and signaling pathways between curcumin, sesame oil, and psoriasis-associated genes. This systems-level insight offers a conceptual basis for the rational design of combination-based dermal formulations, representing an alternative to the traditional single-target drug development model10. Introduced by Hopkins in 2007, network pharmacology integrates systems biology, bioinformatics, and pharmacological strategies to enable a more comprehensive, multitarget, and multipathway therapeutic intervention. This approach not only accelerates drug discovery but also facilitates the translation of research findings into clinical applications11,12. In recent years, nano-formulation approaches have been widely studied for their potential to optimize the delivery and therapeutic effects of natural compound. Curcumin has been extensively reformulated using variouse nanoemulsions. For instance, Sadegh et al. (2019) showed that curcumin-loaded solid lipid nanoparticles (SLNs) and NLCs improved its transport across the blood–brain barrier compared to free curcumin13. Similarly, Iriventi et al. (2020) formulated a dual-drug NLC system containing curcumin and caffeine, resulting in nanoparticles around 103 nm in size, with EE% of 61.5%. Their in vivo imiquimod-induced psoriasis model in mice showed notable improvement in psoriatic symptoms and histopathological outcomes14. On the other hand, various studies have been conducted on sesame oil nanoformulation. Khaled et al. (2022) investigated the use of NLCs to enhance the performance of sesame oil and improve the antifungal efficacy of miconazole against oral candidiasis15. Javed et al. (2024) formulated sesame oil-loaded NLCs that demonstrated enhanced dermal retention and therapeutic potential for psoriasis and eczema16. Dalal et al. (2023) emphasized nanoencapsulation strategies such as SLNs and NLCs to overcome sesame oil’s instability and boost skin delivery17. Burlec et al. (2024) and Ashraf et al. (2023) both reported that sesame oil can serve not only as a skin permeation enhancer but also as a therapeutic agent when included in NLCs or botanical nanoformulations for psoriasis18,19. Despite the individual promise of curcumin and sesame oil nanoformulations, no previous study has integrated both agents into a unified NLC platform for psoriasis therapy. The current study aims to contribute to addressing this gap by developing and optimizing a dual-loaded NLC formulation that may assist in modulating both anti-inflammatory and barrier-repair mechanisms in psoriatic skin. NLCs have been studied in the context of dermal drug delivery, with attention to their biocompatibility and ability to load drugs20,21. In this study, we further employed experimental design and response surface methodology (RSM) to refine key formulation variables specifically, the liquid-to-solid lipid ratio and homogenization time in order to achieve minimal particle size (PS) and polydispersity index (PDI), which are critical for formulation stability and skin absorption22,23. These nanostructures may improve the efficacy of poorly soluble drugs by increasing the drug-skin contact area, controlling drug release, and enhancing skin absorption. Finally, the optimized nanoformulation was systematically characterized for its physicochemical properties and evaluated for its biological activities through in vitro assays, including cytotoxicity assessment (MTT assay) and antioxidant activity (DPPH assay).

Materials and methods

Materials

Curcumin, cetyl palmitate, 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH), and (3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) were purchased from Sigma–Aldrich. Tween 80 was supplied by kimyagaran emrooz (Iran). Fetal bovine serum (FBS) was purchased from Life Technologies (USA). Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM)-high glucose was from Invitrogen. All other chemicals and reagents used were of high purity suitable for analytical purposes.

Protein–protein interaction (PPI) network construction and hub gene identification

A PPI network was constructed to explore the functional associations among the target proteins, including coexpression, neighborhood, gene fusion, and co-occurrence relationships. The list of potential target genes was re-evaluated using the STRING v11.5 database (https://cn.string-db.org/), selecting “Homo sapiens” as the organism of interest and applying a confidence score threshold of > 0.4, representing medium-confidence interactions24,25. In the resulting network, genes were represented as nodes, and their interactions were depicted as edges. To investigate the interactions between differentially expressed genes (DEGs) across various tissues, we used STRING to generate the network. Subsequently, hub genes those with the highest degree of connectivity were identified using the CytoHubba plugin in Cytoscape. The final PPI network was visualized using Gephi (version 0.10.1) to better interpret the topological features and central regulators within the network.

GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analysis

Gene ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of genes and genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis were carried out to explicate the functional roles and systemic impacts of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) shared among curcumin, sesame oil, and psoriasis26–28. The GO analysis focused on three domains: cellular components (CC), molecular functions (MF), and biological processes (BP). KEGG pathway analysis was performed using the SRplot online tool (https://www.bioinformatics.com.cn/en), with a significance threshold set at p < 0.05. This analysis identified ten critical signaling pathways through which curcumin and sesame oil exert their therapeutic effects on psoriasis.

Preparation of NLCs

Curcumin and sesame oil -loaded NLCs (CS-NLCs) were prepared using the melt-emulsification and solvent evaporation method29. Many studies have shown that cetyl palmitate is a suitable choice as a solid lipid in structures due to its ability to produce the smallest PS, optimal dispersion index (PI), and most stable lipid nanocarriers30–32. Cetyl palmitate was heated to 5 °C above the melting temperature. After adding sesame oil as the liquid lipid, curcumin was dispersed into the melted mixture. Ethanol was used as the solvent to completely dissolve the curcumin. An aqueous solution containing tween 80 was introduced to the melted mixture, which was magnetically stirred under isothermal conditions. The mixture was then homogenized using a IK (Germany) homogenizer.

Experimental design and optimization

In our team’s previous study, the main focus was on optimizing the concentration of curcumin in the formulation of NLCs, which, by using appropriate experimental design, achieved the optimal conditions to achieve the desired physicochemical properties and stability33. In the present study, the focus is on optimizing the ratio of liquid lipid to solid lipid and the homogenization time process as two key process variables, in order to improve the final properties of nanoparticles including PS and dispersion index PI. The experimental design and statistical analysis were executed using Design-Expert software (Version 7.0.2, State-Ease Minneapolis, MN, USA). To optimize the formulation, the design of experiments was carried out based on the RSM using the Central Composite Design. The ratio of liquid lipid to solid lipid (in the range of 0.25 to 0.40) and the homogenization time (in the range of 5 to 10 min) were selected on the basis of previous studies. Physicochemical characterization of CS-NLCs

Estimate of mean particle size and nanoparticles morphology

PS and PI of the nanodispersion were established using photon correlation spectroscopy (PCS) through a dynamic light scattering (DLS) instrument (Malvern Zetasizer, UK). To ensure accurate measurements, samples were diluted with deionized water and analyzed at 25 °C with a medium refractive index of 1.33 and viscosity of 0.8872 cP, measured at a 90° angle. The morphology of the optimized nanoparticles was examined using a scanning electron microscope (SEM, HITACHI S-4160, Netherlands). Tiny drops of the sample were embedded on an aluminum plate and allowed to dry at ambient temperature. Afterward, SEM images were captured at 20 kV following gold sputter coating.

X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis

The crystalline state of curcumin and its integration within the lipid matrix of the NLCs were evaluated using XRD with an X’Pert PRO MPD diffractometer (PANalytical, Netherlands) equipped with a Cu Kα radiation source (λ = 1.5406 Å). The analysis was employed at 40 kV and 40 mA over a 2θ range of 3–80°, with a step size of 0.026° and a scan time of 37.995 s per step. Changes in diffraction patterns, particularly the reduction or absence of characteristic crystalline peaks of curcumin, indicated its partial or complete amorphization upon incorporation into the lipid matrix34.

Encapsulation efficiency (EE%) and loading capacity (LC%)

To determine curcumin EE% and LC%, the amount of unloaded curcumin was calculated. Freshly prepared nanodispersions )1 ml) were initially centrifuged at 3000 × g for 5 min to separate the crystaline curcumin. The resulting supernatant was then further centrifuged at 15000 × g for 20 min using a membrane filter (Amicon® Ultra-4 tubes, 10 kDa cut-off) to separate the free curcumin in the aqueous solution. The concentrations of crystals and free curcumin were analyzed via UV–visible spectrophotometer method. In vitro evaluation of curcumin release kinetics from CS- NLCs using the dialysis bag diffusion method.

In vitro release profiling of NLCs was conducted using the dialysis bag diffusion method under physiological temperature conditions (37 °C). To ensure sink conditions critical for accurately characterizing release kinetics tween 80 was (1% w/v) incorporated into phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) as the receptor medium, thereby enhancing curcumin solubility and diffusion potential. A precise volume of 0.5 mL of the NLC formulation was loaded into pre-conditioned dialysis membranes (molecular weight cut-off: 12 kDa) and immersed in the receptor phase. The system was maintained under continuous agitation over a 24 h duration, allowing for the sustained release behavior of curcumin to be quantitatively assessed. This methodological approach facilitates robust evaluation of release dynamics and offers insight into the potential in vivo performance of the NLCs system33.

Quantitative analysis of curcumin content

The quantification of curcumin in the formulations was performed using a high-sensitivity UV–visible spectrophotometric method. Measurements were carried out utilizing a PerkinElmer Lambda Series spectrophotometer. Prior to analysis, appropriate dilutions of the curcumin-containing samples were prepared using a validated solvent system to ensure linearity within the Beer–Lambert range. The absorbance was recorded at 420 nm, and quantification was achieved based on a pre-established calibration curve constructed from standard curcumin solutions of known concentrations35. All measurements were performed in triplicate to ensure reproducibility.

In vitro cytotoxicity assessment via MTT assay

The cytotoxic potential of CS-NLCs was evaluated using the MTT assay on mouse fetal fibroblast (MFF) cells at passage 2. Given the known antiproliferative and cytotoxic effects of curcumin on various cancer cell lines, non-malignant fibroblastic cells were selected to assess the biocompatibility of the developed nanoformulation under non-pathological conditions33. MFF cells were cultured in DMEM-high glucose, supplemented with 10% FBS, and maintained under standard conditions (37 °C, 5% CO₂, and 95% relative humidity). For cytotoxicity testing, cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 5 × 105 cells per well in 100 µL of complete medium and incubated overnight to allow cell attachment. After 24 h, cells were incubated with varying concentrations (1–20 µg/mL) of CS-NLCs, NLC, and free curcumin dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO). At predetermined time intervals, the culture media were aspirated, and cells were gently washed with PBS. Fresh culture medium containing 10 µL of MTT reagent (5 mg/mL) was then added to each well, followed by a 4-h incubation at 37 °C to allow for formazan crystal formation. Subsequently, the supernatant was discarded, and 100 µL of DMSO was added to solubilize the formazan product. Wells containing only DMSO-treated cells were considered as the negative control with 100% viability, while MTT-treated wells without cells served as blanks. Absorbance was measured using a dual-wavelength microplate spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific Multiskan Spectrum, USA) at 570 nm (test) and 630 nm (reference). Evaluation of antioxidant activity via DPPH radical scavenging assay

To assess the influence of the CS-NLCs formulation process on the antioxidant efficacy of curcumin, the DPPH radical scavenging assay was utilized, as previously described in standardized protocols with some modifications36. Statistical analysis

All quantitative results are expressed as mean ± SD based on data obtained from at least three independent replicates (n = 3). Statistical comparisons between multiple groups were performed using one-way ANOVA followed by appropriate post hoc tests to determine intergroup significance. SPSS software (Version 16.0; SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) was used to analyse the data. A p-value of less than 0.05 (p < 0.05) was assumed statistically significant, while a p-value less 0.01 (p < 0.01) was interpreted as highly significant.

Results and discussion

Construction PPI network and identification of candidate targets against psoriasis

In this analysis, a total of 3214 targets associated with psoriasis were retrieved from the GC database. For curcumin (both keto and enol form) and sesame oil-related targets, data were collected from two databases: GC and STP. After eliminating duplicates, 950 targets were associated with curcumin and 188 with sesame oil. Subsequently, our analysis identified 74 potential candidate targets for the treatment of psoriasis, which were filtered using Venny 2.1.0 (Fig. 1B).

The STRING database was used to construct a PPI network from the overlapping genes, resulting in a network of 74 nodes and 671 edges (Fig. 2A). Cytoscape was then used to identify candidate hub genes, which were visualized using STRING and Gephi (Fig. 2B). The top 10 hub genes based on degree score were selected, including TNF, BCL2, IL1B, ESR1, IL4, IFNG, IL10, CASP3, CXCL8, and FOS. Many of these genes (e.g., TNF, IL1B, IL4, IFNG, IL10, CXCL8) are cytokines or cytokine-related, central to inflammatory and immune responses37,38. Genes like BCL2 and CASP3 are involved in apoptosis and cellular stress responses, which are often activated by oxidative stress and are relevant in psoriasis pathogenesis39,40. Experimental design and D-optimal design

In this study, the effect of two independent variables, including the ratio of liquid to solid lipid (0.25–0.40) and homogenization time (5–10 min), on the physical properties of nanocarriers containing curcumin and sesame oil was investigated with the aim of improving the treatment of psoriasis. Design expert software and RSM were used to design the experiments. Parameters, experimental design matrix and ANOVA for D-optimal design are listed in (Table 1). The results of the experimental design showed that the quadratic model was significant for both PS and PDI responses (Table 2). The results of the ANOVA for the PI parameter show that the model is generally significant (F-value = 43.65, p-value < 0. 0.0001), indicating a very low probability of this value being due to noise. Among the model parameters, A (liquid to solid lipid ratio) with p-value < 0. 0.0001 and an F-value of 110.58, B (homogenization rate) with a p-value < 0. 0.0001 and an F-value of 85.57, and A2 (the squared effect of lipid ratio) with a p-value < 0. 0.0001 have a significant effect. However, the interaction effect of AB and the squared effect of B2 are not significant, as their p-values are 0.2208 and 0.3645, respectively. The lack of fit of the model is also not significant (F-value = 1.95 and p-value = 0.2638), indicating that the model describes the data well and the observed differences are due to noise. Overall, the model has a high ability to predict PI and provides favorable results. The results of the ANOVA for the PDI show that the overall model is significant, as the F-value of the model is 21.05 and the p-value < 0.0004, indicating a very low probability of such values occurring due to noise. Among the model parameters, A2 (the squared effect of the liquid to solid lipid ratio) with a p-value of 0.0020 and B2 (the squared effect of the homogenization rate) with a p-value = 0.0002 have a significant effect. However, the linear effects of A (liquid to solid lipid ratio) and B (homogenization rate) as well as the interaction effect of AB are not significant, as their p-values are 0.0745, 0.4916 and 0.3780, respectively. The lack of fit of the model is also not significant (F-value = 2.15 and p-value = 0.2369), indicating that the model explains the data well and the observed differences are due to noise. Overall, the model has a high ability to predict PDI and provides acceptable results. The coefficients of determination R2 for PS and PDI were 0.96 and 0.93, respectively, indicating a good fit of the model. Also, the Predicted R2 value for both responses was within the desired range and in reasonable agreement with the Adjusted R2. All the statistical data from were summerised in (Table 3). The graphs presented from 2-D contour plots and the 3D response surface plots (Fig. 4) for the two responses (PS, and PDI) and desirability, show the interaction effect of two main variables, namely the ratio of liquid to solid lipid and the homogenization rate, on the design results. This analysis helps to characterize the system behavior and suggests the optimal points by displaying the contour lines and color changes. In the desirability 2-D contour plots (Fig. 4A), the contour lines indicate the overall desirability level. Blue colors indicate low desirability and red and orange colors indicate high desirability. The optimal desirability value is 0.903, which is in the region of liquid to solid lipid ratio around 0.35–0.4 and homogenization rate between 7 and 8 min. This means that these combined conditions have led to the best overall results for the system. This observation was strongly supported by the 3D surface plot (Fig. 4D), which showed a well-defined peak in the same region, confirming the presence of a unique optimal condition for maximizing desirability. According to the contour plot for PS (Fig. 4B), the contour lines show the variation in PS in different regions. Green and yellow colors represent smaller PS (more desirable) and dark blue and purple colors represent larger PS (less desirable). The predicted optimum PS is 132.427 nm. This value is achieved in a region with a liquid-to-solid lipid ratio of about 0.39 and a homogenization time of about 8.3 min. The denser the contour lines, the faster the PS response changes. This trend was consistently demonstrated in the 3D surface diagram (Fig. 4E), in which a valley-shaped region confirmed the minimized PS at those specific experimental conditions. In PDI 2-D contour plot (Fig. 4C), the contour lines show the homogeneity of the particle dispersion. Blue colors represent lower PDI values (more uniform and desirable dispersion) and green and yellow colors represent higher PDI values (less desirable). The optimum PDI value is 0.208, which is obtained in a similar region to the PS plot (around lipid ratio 0.35 and homogenization time 7–8 min). This indicates that these settings have resulted in a homogeneous dispersion in the system. The corresponding 3D surface plot (Fig. 4F) revealed a clear depression in the same region, reaffirming that both a moderate lipid ratio and homogenization time contribute significantly to obtaining a more uniform nanoparticle dispersion.The contour plots and 3D surface plot well illustrate the interactions between the two main variables and identify the optimal points where all three responses achieve the best conditions. The colors and contour lines are a powerful tool for detecting trends; in general, areas close to the orange and red areas in overall fitness, green areas in PS, and blue areas in PDI reflect ideal conditions. This analysis confirms that the system design is well tuned and the responses are in line with each other. The optimal conditions obtained from the analysis included a liquid-to-solid lipid ratio of 0.336 and a homogenization time of 8 min. Under these conditions, the predicted value for the PS was determined to be 132.420 nm and the PDI was determined to be 0.208, which the desirability value was calculated to be about 0.903. This value indicates a high compliance of the optimal conditions with the design criteria. The results showed that the use of sesame oil as a liquid lipid, along with sufficient homogenization time, plays an effective role in reducing PS and increasing PDI. These optimal values may contribute to improved skin absorption and controlled release of curcumin, which could be beneficial for topical applications in psoriasis42,43. The validation experiments were conducted in three separate runs under the optimal conditions and the results were in perfect agreement with the predicted values of the model, indicating the accuracy of the modeling and the reproducibility of the optimal formulation (Table 4). These results indicate that increasing the homogenization time, due to higher mechanical energy, helps to reduce the PS and uniformity of distribution44,45. Also, increasing the liquid-to-solid lipid ratio to a certain extent improves the particle dispersion, but higher values may lead to system instability46,47. This study also highlights the importance of using modeling tools and contour plots to identify and optimize experimental conditions, as this method can help researchers better understand the relationships between variables and responses. DLS analysis was employed to determine the average PS and PDI of the optimized CS-NLCs. The results revealed a mean PS of 132.17 ± 2.4 nm and a narrow size distribution, as shown by a PDI value of 0.200 ± 0.026, suggesting good homogeneity of the nanoparticle suspension (Fig. 5A). SEM micrographs (Fig. 5B) confirmed that the nanoparticles exhibited a uniform, spherical morphology with smooth surfaces and minimal aggregation. The particle dimensions observed by SEM were consistent with the DLS measurements, supporting the reliability of the formulation’s nanoscale features and dispersion quality. The formulation exhibited a high EE% of 93% and LC% of 4.5%, suggesting successful incorporation of curcumin into the lipid carrier system. These results suggest the formulation help maintain drug integrity and support sustained release behavior, both of which are advantageous for dermal delivery. Crystallinity and amorphous state evaluation using XRD analysis

The XRD spectra presented in Fig. 5C show structural and crystallinity changes in the formulation of lipid nanostructures containing curcumin and sesame oil. The sharp and distinct peaks corresponding to curcumin indicate its highly crystalline nature, while similar peaks in the spectrum of cetyl palmitate also indicate the high crystallinity of this solid lipid. In the CS-NLC spectrum, a decrease in the intensity and clarity of the peaks is observed, indicating a decrease in the crystallinity of curcumin after loading into the lipid matrix. This structural change is due to the incorporation of curcumin into the lipid nanostructure and the creation of an amorphous or semi-crystalline state, which can help improve its solubility and bioavailability. In contrast, the NLC spectrum lacks prominent peaks, indicating the predominantly amorphous nature of these lipid structures. These results suggest that lipid nanostructure formulation may improve curcumin’s physicochemical by reducing crystallinity and contributing to a more stable system.

In vitro drug release study

The in vitro release behavior of curcumin from CS-NLCs was evaluated in tween 80 was (1% w/v) incorporated into PBS, pH = 7.4, under sink conditions, and the release profiles are depicted in (Fig. 6A). Comparative analysis revealed that free curcumin exhibited a rapid dissolution pattern, with nearly 80% release occurring within the initial hours of the study (8 h), consistent with its poor aqueous solubility and lack of a controlled matrix. In contrast, the CS-NLC formulation demonstrated a biphasic release pattern characteristic of lipid-based nanocarriers. An initial burst release phase was observed during the first few hours, which is commonly attributed to the presence of unencapsulated curcumin. This phenomenon has been widely reported in literature on nanolipid systems48,49, and may play a beneficial role in achieving an early therapeutic plasma concentration. Following the initial burst phase, a second sustained-release phase was observed, indicating the controlled diffusion of curcumin from the lipid matrix into the surrounding medium. This slow and extended release phase reflects the successful entrapment of curcumin within the inner core of the lipid matrix and the structural integrity of the NLC system. Over the course of the release study, approximately 90% of the encapsulated curcumin was gradually released, indicating high release efficiency of the NLCs and suggesting their potential as a delivery platform for sustained therapeutic applications. A standard calibration curve for curcumin was established by a UV–visible spectrophotometer (Shimadzu UV-1800, Japan). MTT assay results: comparative cytotoxicity of Cur-NLCs and free curcumin

The cytotoxic potential of CS-NLCs in comparison with free curcumin was assessed using the MTT assay on mouse fetal fibroblast (MFF) cells. To ensure accuracy in cell viability quantification, the absorbance value of 0.19, obtained from MTT-treated wells lacking cells (blank), was subtracted from all recorded optical densities prior to calculation. The relative cell viability (%) was then computed according to the standard formula. As illustrated in Fig. 6B, treatment with CS-NLCs and NLC at lower concentrations (1 and 10 µgmL-1) resulted in cell viabilities exceeding 85%, indicating a favorable biocompatibility. At higher concentrations (e.g., 20 µg-mL−1), a modest decrease in cell viability was observed, which may be attributed to the cumulative effect of curcumin release and potential cellular uptake saturation. Nevertheless, even at elevated doses, the cytotoxic effect remained limited. In contrast, free curcumin exhibited a slightly more pronounced reduction in viability at comparable concentrations, likely due to its rapid cellular internalization and poor solubility profile, leading to higher local concentrations and potential oxidative stress. The NLC formulation appears to mitigate these effects by enabling controlled release and improved dispersibility in the culture medium. Overall, the results suggest that CS-NLCs exhibit a favorable cytocompatibility profile and do not induce toxicity in normal fibroblast cells in vitro, supporting their potential for further exploration as a safe therapeutic delivery of curcumin.

Assessment of antioxidant activity via DPPH assay

The antioxidant activity of curcumin, sesame oil, their combination in CS-NLC, and Vit C (positive control) was evaluated using the DPPH radical scavenging assay, and the results are presented in (Fig. 6C). Free curcumin exhibited an acceptable antioxidant activity of approximately 90.42%, while sesame oil also showed notable activity at around 87.49%, consistent with previous reports highlighting their radical scavenging and lipid peroxidation inhibitory propertiers 50,51. Interestingly, the CS-NLC formulation preserved the antioxidant potential of both components, showing a comparable scavenging effect without significant loss due to the nanoformulation process. This suggests that the encapsulation procedure including heating, homogenization, and emulsification did not impair the intrinsic antioxidant functionality of curcumin or sesame oil. As expected, ascorbic acid demonstrated nearly complete scavenging (close to 100%) and served as a benchmark for comparison. This finding was documented for other antioxidant products in their free and encapsulated forms in lipid-based nanoparticles52,53. These findings suggest that CS-NLC preserves the bioactivity of its loaded compounds and may serves as a stable antioxidant delivery system suitable for further evaluation in oxidative stress-related skin disorders such as psoriasis. To statistically validate these findings, one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s HSD post hoc test was performed. ANOVA indicated no statistically significant difference was observed between free curcumin vs. sesame oil, free curcumin vs. CS-NLCs, or sesame oil vs. CS-NLCs (p > 0.05).

Conclusion

In this study, network pharmacology analysis suggested that curcumin and sesame oil may act on key genes related to inflammation, oxidative stress, and apoptosis in psoriasis. Enrichment analysis indicated their possible involvement in IL-17, TNF, and oxidative stress pathways, supporting their multi-target therapeutic potential for psoriasis management. NLCs containing curcumin and sesame oil were successfully prepared and optimized to improve delivery properties. The optimal conditions (0.36 liquid to solid lipid and 8 min of homogenization, desirability ≈0.903) demonstrated a controlled release profile, preserved antioxidant activity, and limited cytotoxicity in vitro. However, our findings are limited to in vitro experiments due to resource constraints; therefore, future studies should include in vivo and clinical evaluations to confirm the therapeutic potential. Also, although network pharmacology offered insights into possible mechanisms, these remain predictive and need experimental validation in further research. In conclusion, these findings provide a promising basis for future preclinical research into the use of combination of herbal nanoformulations in chronic inflammatory skin diseases such as psoriasis.
