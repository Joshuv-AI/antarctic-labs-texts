Artificial intelligence-based personalized clinical decision-making for patients with localized prostate cancer: surgery versus radiotherapy
Authors: Yuwei Liu, Litao Zhao, Jiangang Liu, Liang Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSurgery and radiotherapy are primary nonconservative treatments for prostate cancer (PCa). However, personalizing treatment options between these treatment modalities is challenging due to unclear criteria. We developed an artificial intelligence (AI)-based model that can identify patients with localized PCa who would benefit more from either radiotherapy or surgery, thereby providing personalized clinical decision-making.Material and MethodsData from consecutive patients with localized PCa who received radiotherapy or surgery with complete records of clinicopathological variables and follow-up results in 12 registries of the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results database were analyzed. Patients from 7 registries were randomly assigned to training (TD) and internal validation datasets (IVD) at a 9:1 ratio. The remaining 5 registries constituted the external validation dataset (EVD). TD was divided into training-radiotherapy (TRD) and training-surgery (TSD) datasets, and IVD was divided into internal-radiotherapy (IRD) and internal-surgery (ISD) datasets. Six models for radiotherapy and surgery were trained using TRD and TSD to predict radiotherapy survival probability (RSP) and surgery survival probability (SSP), respectively. The models with the highest concordance index (C-index) on IRD and ISD were chosen to form the final treatment recommendation model (FTR). FTR recommendations were based on the higher value between RSP and SSP. Kaplan-Meier curves were generated for patients receiving recommended (consistent group) and nonrecommended treatments (inconsistent group), which were compared using the log-rank test.ResultsThe study included 118 236 patients, categorized into TD (TRD: 44 621; TSD: 41 500), IVD (IRD: 4949; ISD: 4621), and EVD (22 545). Both radiotherapy and surgery models accurately predicted RSP and SSP (C-index: 0.735-0.787 and 0.769-0.797, respectively). The consistent group exhibited higher survival rates than the inconsistent group, particularly among patients not suitable for active surveillance (P < .001).ConclusionFTR accurately identifies patients with localized PCa who would benefit more from either radiotherapy or surgery, offering clinicians an effective AI tool to make informed choices between these 2 treatments.

Implications for Practice

We developed an artificial intelligence-based model, namely final treatment recommendation model (FTR), that can effectively identify patients with localized prostate cancer (PCa) who will derive greater survival benefit from either radiotherapy or surgery, particularly those unsuitable for active surveillance (AS). Moreover, for patients with localized PCa deemed suitable for AS, FTR could be used when these patients are reclassified to a higher-risk category during regular monitoring and require definitive treatment. Given the ease of assessing the variables used in FTR in clinical settings, FTR holds considerable potential for aiding in clinical decision-making for localized PCa.

Introduction

Patients newly diagnosed with localized prostate cancer (PCa) are confronted with a critical decision regarding their initial treatment options.1-3 The available primary nonconservative treatments, namely surgery and radiotherapy, have been in practice for over a century and have proven effective.4-6 However, existing studies assessing survival following these treatments for localized PCa do not conclusively address the relative superiority of one over the other.4,5,7 Therefore, deciding between these 2 options remains a significant challenge in clinical practice.1-3

In clinical practice, treatment decisions for localized PCa are guided by risk stratification according to established guidelines,8 including those of the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN),9 European Association of Urology,10 and European Society for Medical Oncology.11 These guidelines consider factors such as prostate-specific antigen (PSA), clinical tumor stage (cT stage), biopsy Gleason grade group (GGG), and the percentage of positive biopsy cores (PPC). For patients classified as very low (VLR), low (LR), and favorable intermediate (FIR) risks, active surveillance (AS) is recommended to mitigate the risk of overtreatment.9 By contrast, patients classified as unfavorable intermediate (UFIR), high, and very high (VHR) risk often require definitive treatments, such as radiotherapy or surgery, to prevent further disease progression and the development of metastatic disease.9 However, the criteria for choosing between radiotherapy and surgery for patients requiring definitive treatment are not well-defined.8-12 Therefore, optimal treatment decision-making is challenging in this context.

Machine learning (ML) models have demonstrated advantages in managing high-dimensional data and assessing the relationship between covariates and survival outcomes.13 Several studies have developed ML models to predict cancer-specific survival (CSS) or overall survival (OS) for patients undergoing various treatments, showing promising results.14-17 Despite the excellent performance of ML models in survival prediction, there has been limited exploration of ML-based methodologies for treatment decision-making in the context of PCa.18-21

This study aimed to address this gap by developing a recommendation system based on ML models. The proposed system is designed to identify patients with localized PCa who would benefit more from either radiotherapy or surgery, providing personalized treatment recommendations.

Methods

Data source and eligibility criteria

This retrospective study used data from the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) research plus database, focusing on patients with PCa diagnosed between 2000 and 2019.22 The SEER database, renowned for its comprehensive collection of patient demographics, tumor characteristics, treatments, and survival outcomes,22 served as the primary data source. We included patients who had undergone either surgery or radiotherapy and excluded those who received chemotherapy, because the chemotherapy is not a primary treatment for localized PCa.9 Patients who received both surgery and radiotherapy were excluded because of the inability to determine the sequence of these treatments from the SEER data, which could introduce confounding factors. Patients with missing data for any clinicopathological variables were also excluded. Additionally, patients diagnosed in 2019 were excluded due to the lack of follow-up data. The study’s inclusion and exclusion criteria, along with dataset splitting, are depicted in Figure 1, which also summarizes the number of patients who received surgery or radiotherapy in each dataset. This study adheres to the STROBE reporting guidelines (https://www.equator-network.org/reporting-guidelines/strobe/).

Data splitting

According to the inclusion and exclusion criteria, 118 236 cases from 12 registries between January 2010 and December 2018 were selected (Figure 1). Among them, 95 691 cases from 7 registry areas (ie, Alaska, California, Georgia, Kentucky, Louisiana, New Jersey, and Utah) were randomly divided into a training dataset (TD) and an internal validation dataset (IVD) at a 9:1 ratio. The remaining 22 545 cases from 5 other registry areas (ie, Connecticut, Hawaii, Iowa, New Mexico, and Seattle) comprised the external validation dataset (EVD).

As depicted in Figure 1, TD was categorized based on the treatment received: patients who underwent radiotherapy were assigned to the training-radiotherapy dataset (TRD) and those who underwent surgery were assigned to the training-surgery dataset (TSD). Similarly, IVD was divided into the internal-radiotherapy dataset (IRD) and internal-surgery dataset (ISD).

Study variables and data preprocessing

In this study, we collected clinicopathological variables and follow-up results for each included patient from the SEER database. The clinicopathological variables comprised age, race, cT stage, PSA, surgery, radiotherapy, number of positive biopsy cores (NPC), number of total biopsy cores (NTC), and biopsy GGG. The follow-up results included survival status, cause of death, and follow-up time (defined as the duration between the first treatment and either the patient’s death or their last follow-up). CSS for localized PCa was defined as deaths exclusively caused by PCa. Patient races included 4 categories: White, Black, Asian/Pacific Islander, and American Indian/AK Native. PPC represented the ratio of NPC to NTC. Continuous variables (ie, age, PSA, and PPC) were normalized using Z-score normalization, and categorical variables (ie, race, cT stage, and GGG) were dummy-coded. These adjusted variables were used as the basis for developing the ML models.

Model development and evaluation

The study used 6 ML models: RSF,23 FastsurvivalSVM,24 CoxLASSO,25 Survivaltree,26 Extrasurvivaltree,26 and CoxPH.27 These models were constructed using 6 clinicopathological variables (ie, age, race, PSA, cT stage, PPC, and GGG) and implemented in Python 3.7 (Python Software Foundation) using the PySurvival (https://pypi.org/project/pysurvival/) and scikit-survival packages (https://pypi.org/project/scikit-survival/).

Six radiotherapy models and 6 surgery models were trained using TRD and TSD to predict radiotherapy survival probability (RSP) and surgery survival probability (SSP), respectively. Optimal hyperparameters for the models were identified using GridsearchCV (https://scikit-learn.org/stable/modules/generated/sklearn.model_selection.GridSearchCV.html#sklearn.model_selection.GridSearchCV) with a 5-fold cross-validation. Details of the optimal hyperparameters for each model were summarized in Supplementary Table S1. Harrell’s concordance index (C-index) was used to assess the models’ performance. The radiotherapy and surgery models that showed the highest C-index on IRD and ISD, respectively, were selected to form the final treatment recommendation model (FTR) (Figure 2a). FTR compares RSP with SSP. If RSP is higher, FTR recommends radiotherapy; otherwise, it recommends surgery.

Effectiveness of FTR’s treatment recommendations

The effectiveness of FTR for treatment recommendations was externally evaluated on EVD regardless of the actual treatment received by the patients (Figure 2b). For all patients in EVD, if the treatments patients received were consistent with FTR’s recommendations, they were assigned to a consistent group. Otherwise, they were assigned to an inconsistent group. Kaplan-Meier (KM) survival curves for these groups were plotted and compared using the log-rank test.

Definition of risk stratification

According to the risk stratification of the NCCN guidelines,9 patients with PCa are classified into 5 groups: VLR, LR, intermediate risk (IR), high risk, and VHR. However, owing to the absence of PSA density records in the SEER database, we were unable to categorize patients into the VLR group. Thus, we categorized patients into 4 risk groups: LR, IR, high risk, and VHR. Specifically, LR PCa was identified by the following criteria: a cT stage of cT1-cT2a, GGG of 1, and PSA level < 10 ng/mL. IR PCa was considered as having no high-risk features, no VHR features, and one or more intermediate risk factors (IRFs), which include cT2b-cT2c, GGG 2 or 3, and PSA 10-20 ng/mL. IR PCa was further classified into favorable intermediate risk (FIR) and unfavorable intermediate risk (UFIR). FIR refers to a patient with PCa having the following factors: one IRF, GGG 1 or 2, and <50% positive biopsy cores (PBCs). UFIR refers to a patient with PCa having one or more of the following factors: 2 or 3 IRFs, GGG 3, and ≥50% PBCs. High-risk PCa is defined as having only one high-risk feature (ie, cT3a, GGG of 4 or 5, and PSA > 20 ng/mL), without any VHR features (ie, cT3b-cT4, primary Gleason pattern of 5, 2 or 3 high-risk features, and >4 cores with a GGG of 4 or 5). Finally, VHR PCa is defined as having at least one of the VHR features.

Subgroup analysis

To further validate the performance of FTR’s treatment recommendations, 3 types of subgroup analyses were conducted on EVD. First, patients in EVD were categorized into 2 subgroups based on FTR’s recommendations: those recommended for radiotherapy (radiotherapy-recommended candidates) and those recommended for surgery (surgery-recommended candidates). The first type of subgroup analysis was conducted in these 2 subgroups. Subsequently, according to the NCCN guidelines,9 patients with localized PCa were stratified into VLR, LR, and FIR categories, which are suitable for AS. However, patients stratified into UFIR, high risk, and VHR categories were required to undergo definitive treatment and, as a result, were considered as non-AS candidates. The second type of analysis was conducted within these AS and non-AS candidate subgroups. Finally, non-AS candidates were further divided into radiotherapy-recommended and surgery-recommended candidates. The third type of subgroup analysis was conducted in these 2 subgroups.

For each subgroup analysis, KM curves were plotted for the consistent and inconsistent groups and compared using the log-rank test. Hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% sCIs are reported.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using RStudio (v.4.0.3; https://posit.co/download/rstudio-desktop/), with a significance level of P < .05. The patient baseline clinicopathological variables between the 2 groups (those who received radiotherapy or surgery) within TD, IVD, and EVD were compared using the R package CBCgrps.28 This package assesses both categorical and continuous variables, determining the appropriate distribution for continuous variables and employing suitable descriptors. Categorical variables (ie, race, cT stage, and GGG) were represented using frequencies and percentages and compared using the Chi-square test. Continuous variables (ie, age, PSA, and PPC) were represented using either the mean and SD or the median and interquartile range (IQR), depending on their distribution. Comparisons were made using the 2-sample t-test or Mann-Whitney U test, as appropriate. KM curves were used to visually represent CSS analyses, and differences between the curves were assessed using the log-rank test. This analysis spanned from January 2023 to June 2023.

Performance of developed candidate models

The C-index of the radiotherapy and surgery models is summarized in Supplementary Table S2. These models were trained using TRD and TSD, and internally validated using IRD and ISD, respectively. Based on the validation of IRD and ISD, CoxPH-radiotherapy (C-index of IRD = 0.787) and CoxLASSO-surgery (C-index of ISD = 0.797) emerged as the best-performing models among the radiotherapy and surgery models, respectively. Consequently, these 2 models were selected to construct FTR. The development (selection procession of CoxPH-radiotherapy and CoxLASSO-surgery) and decision-making process of FTR are illustrated in Figure 2.

Performance of FTR

FTR was externally tested using EVD. Figure 3a displays the KM curves for the consistent and inconsistent groups. The consistent group exhibited higher survival rates than the inconsistent group (P < .001, HR = 0.288 [95% CI: 0.228–0.365]).

Subgroup analysis

Analysis of subgroups with radiotherapy-recommended and surgery-recommended candidates

In both subgroups, the consistent group demonstrated significantly higher survival rates than the inconsistent group (radiotherapy-recommended candidates: HR = 0.556 [95% CI: 0.404-0.765], P = .0003; surgery-recommended candidates: HR = 0.118 [95% CI: 0.080-0.174], P < .0001; Figure 3b).

Analysis of subgroups with AS and non-AS candidates

In the subgroup of AS candidates (26.3% [5929/22 545] of EVD, as shown in Supplementary Table S3), no significant differences in CSS were observed between the consistent and inconsistent groups (P = .061, HR = 0.437 [95% CI: 0.179-1.065]). However, in the subgroup of non-AS candidates (73.7% [16 616/22 545] of EVD, as shown in Supplementary Table S3), the consistent group exhibited significantly higher survival rates than the inconsistent group (P < .0001, HR = 0.314 [95% CI: 0.246-0.402]; Figure 3c).

Analysis of subgroups with radiotherapy-recommended and surgery-recommended candidates among non-AS candidates

Expanding on the previous subgroup analysis, the non-AS candidates were further divided into radiotherapy- and surgery-recommended candidates. In these subgroups, the consistent group showed significantly higher survival rates than the inconsistent group for both radiotherapy-recommended (P = .013, HR = 0.659 [95% CI: 0.474-0.917]) and surgery-recommended (P < .0001, HR = 0.143 [95% CI: 0.096-0.212]) candidates (Figure 3d).

Discussion

Our study illustrated that FTR can accurately predict the survival probability of patients with localized PCa, irrespective of the treatment received (surgery or radiotherapy). Notably, FTR’s treatment recommendations were linked to significantly better survival rates in the consistent group compared to the inconsistent group. This pattern persisted across each subgroup of radiotherapy- and surgery-recommended candidates, indicating that both radiotherapy and surgery have distinct populations for whom they are suitable. Consequently, FTR emerges as an effective tool for identifying appropriate candidates for radiotherapy or surgery based on individualized clinicopathological variables.

In clinical practice, patients with localized PCa are typically stratified into different risk categories to determine the necessity of definitive treatment.9-12 A key aspect of this risk stratification is distinguishing between AS and non-AS candidates. Specifically, patients classified as VLR, LR, and FIR are suitable for AS, while those categorized as UFIR, high risk, and VHR are non-AS candidates requiring definitive treatment (ie, radiotherapy or surgery) to prevent disease progression.7 Therefore, selecting between radiotherapy and surgery is critical for non-AS candidates. Our study demonstrated that FTR showed promising performance in recommending treatments for non-AS candidates, including its subgroups (ie, radiotherapy-recommended and surgery-recommended candidates). Notably, non-AS candidates represented a significant portion, 73.7%, of EVD, underscoring FTR’s potential to provide personalized treatment recommendations for the majority of patients with localized PCa. By contrast, for AS candidates, who constituted 26.3% of EVD, no significant survival differences were observed between the consistent and inconsistent groups, regardless of the radiotherapy and surgery recommendations. This finding suggests that FTR may not be suitable for patients with PCa with relatively low risks. In clinical settings, AS candidates typically do not require immediate, definitive treatments such as radiotherapy or surgery and thus do not benefit from a treatment recommendation. However, if these AS candidates are reclassified into a higher-risk category during regular monitoring and subsequently require definitive treatment,9-12 FTR can then provide valuable guidance on whether radiotherapy or surgery would be more beneficial.

In summary, as shown in Figure 4, patients with localized PCa can initially be categorized into AS and non-AS groups based on risk stratification. Non-AS patients, as well as AS patients who are reclassified as non-AS during regular monitoring, may benefit from the treatment recommendations provided by the proposed FTR. Given the straightforward assessment of the 6 variables (ie, age, PSA, PPC, race, GGG, and cT stage) used in FTR in clinical settings, this model holds considerable potential for aiding clinical decision-making in localized PCa.

To our knowledge, this study is the first to investigate artificial intelligence-based personalized treatment recommendations for patients with localized PCa. The choice of treatment is crucial, significantly affecting patients’ quality of life and survival time.5,29 Previous studies have highlighted that patients with PCa often face challenges of undertreatment and overtreatment,30 with some experiencing regret over their treatment choices.29 However, our model offers a promising solution to mitigate these issues. FTR’s recommendations are based on predicting and comparing survival probabilities, offering physicians valuable insights into the survival outcomes associated with different treatments. This approach aids in identifying patients who would benefit more from either surgery or radiotherapy. Therefore, FTR has the potential to serve as a decision-support tool, guiding clinicians toward the most suitable treatment for their patients.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the SEER database lacks certain important clinical variables, such as smoking history, radiotherapy dose, radiotherapy type (ie, intensity-modulated radiotherapy [IMRT], image-guided, and brachytherapy), surgery type (eg, robotic laparoscopic, open surgery, and whether node dissections were done), records of androgen deprivation therapy (ie, none, short course, and long course), detailed histology reports (eg, cribriform pattern), performance status (eg, quality of life), and expected 10-year survival. Previous studies reported that these clinical variables,31,32 particularly histology reports (eg, cribriform pattern),33-36 performance status (eg, quality of life),37,38 and expected 10-year survival,39 were closely related to the prognosis of patients with PCa. Thus, including these variables could potentially improve the predictive accuracy of the model, and thereby produce a more generally applicable model. Second, due to the wide time span of the included patients from the SEER database and the gradual maturation of treatment technology with the progress of the times, it is unclear whether patients diagnosed early have similar outcomes to those diagnosed later. The addition of such information may further improve the performance of the proposed model in making treatment recommendations. The future research could address this issue by collecting more detailed prospective data that includes these treatment details. Finally, although the proposed model was validated on an external dataset, our study is retrospective in nature. Future research should aim to validate and expand upon our findings using prospective data.

Conclusion

The FTR model offers personalized treatment recommendations for patients with localized PCa, integrating 2 ML models predicting RSP and SSP. Using easily accessible clinicopathological variables, FTR effectively identifies patients benefiting more from either radiotherapy or surgery, particularly among non-AS candidates. This establishes a relationship between common clinicopathological variables and survival probabilities, providing a basis for treatment decisions and aiding clinicians in choosing the most suitable option between radiotherapy and surgery for patients with localized PCa.
