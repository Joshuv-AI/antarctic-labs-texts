Predicting blastocyst viability: the role of post-vitrification re-expansion speed and its correlation with artificial collapse and preimplantation genetic testing
Authors: Adva Aizer, Shay Kaplan, Meirav Noach-Hirsh, Olga Dratviman-Storobinsky, Roni Hoshen, Aliza Segev-Zahav, Jigal Haas, Raoul Orvieto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPost-thaw blastocyst behavior is clinically relevant and closely linked to implantation and pregnancy success after frozen embryo transfer. Re-expansion of the blastocyst after warming is recognized as an important indicator of embryo viability and has been associated with clinical outcomes, although it is not routinely evaluated in a dynamic manner. In addition, blastocysts undergo different laboratory procedures before vitrification, such as trophectoderm biopsy for genetic testing and artificial collapse, which may influence post-thaw re-expansion dynamics. Therefore, this study aimed to dynamically assess post-thaw blastocyst re-expansion and to evaluate its association with pregnancy outcomes, as well as to examine differences in re-expansion dynamics across different laboratory procedures.ResultsA total of 463 frozen embryo transfer cycles were analyzed, including 170 cycles selected for detailed morphokinetic evaluation. Blastocysts were categorized according to pre-vitrification procedures into a Control group, a biopsy group, and an artificial collapse group. After thawing, the initial re-expansion ratio (T1) was slightly lower in the artificial collapse group compared with the Control and biopsy groups, although this difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.096). The artificial collapse group demonstrated a significantly higher maximal re-expansion ratio (p = 0.031) and a longer time to reach this maximum (p = 0.015). Re-expansion speed was highest in the artificial collapse group, followed by the biopsy group and the Control group, and showed a strong positive correlation with pregnancy outcomes (p = 0.011). Blastocysts in the highest Average Re-expansion Speed Rate (ARSR) quartile (≥ 5.1%) were more than three times more likely to result in pregnancy compared with those in the lowest quartile. The highest ongoing pregnancy rate was observed in the artificial collapse group (52.6%), followed by the biopsy group (41.7%) and the Control group (35.9%) (p = 0.033).ConclusionA dynamic assessment of post-thaw blastocyst re-expansion is strongly associated with pregnancy outcomes after frozen embryo transfer. Differences in re-expansion dynamics were observed between blastocysts subjected to different laboratory procedures, with artificial collapse before vitrification showing the most favorable profile. This approach may provide a useful tool for improving embryo evaluation and supporting clinical decision-making in IVF.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13048-026-02199-3.Keywords: Blastocyst re-expansion, Artificial collapse, Time-lapse, Trophectoderm biopsy, Average re-expansion speed

Introduction

Optimizing vitrification is pivotal for enhancing IVF success rates. Vitrification, now a cornerstone of ART, has eclipsed slow freezing in survival rates and developmental competence, particularly in preserving surplus embryos for subsequent transfers and raising cumulative pregnancy rates [1]. The “freeze-all” strategy [2] mitigates OHSS risks [3, 4] and complements PGT [5]. Moreover, advancements in extended culture systems now enable the development of embryos to the blastocyst stage, enhancing the selection of embryos with the highest potential for transfer, biopsy, or vitrification [6, 7]. Together, efficient vitrification and improved culture systems have significantly advanced ART outcomes, driving continuous progress in reproductive success.

During the early blastocyst formation at the morula stage, trophoblast cells establish tight junctions, making the structure impermeable. Concurrently, active sodium pumps induce fluid influx, creating the blastocoel—a fluid-filled cavity [8, 9]. Although vitrification prevents ice crystal formation by transitioning water molecules directly from liquid to a vitreous state, the blastocoelic fluid volume poses a risk of cellular damage, including reduced permeability to cryoprotectants, ice crystal formation, and cryo-damage [10–12]. This damage often manifests as cell lysis during thawing. Consequently, studies suggest that removing blastocoel fluid before vitrification using a laser pulse significantly improves blastocyst survival, clinical pregnancy, and implantation rates [13–20].

During warming, blastocysts often collapse. Shu et al. (2009) defined good blastocyst survival as having more than 50% of cells intact post-thaw and re-expansion within 2–4 h [21]. Re-expansion of a frozen-thawed blastocyst is a key viability indicator [22–24]. Methods for assessing re-expanded blastocysts include recording frames immediately and two hours post-thaw [25, 26], with the degree of re-expansion graded according to the percentage of the original volume recovered, providing a reliable method for evaluating blastocyst re-expansion post-thawing. More recently, early post-warming re-expansion dynamics have been shown to be associated with pregnancy outcomes, suggesting that quantitative assessment shortly after warming may provide clinically relevant information [27].

Cimadomo et al. investigated the association between trophectoderm biopsy for PGT-A and artificial pre-vitrification shrinkage with post-warming blastocyst behavior and implantation potential [28]. They identified blastocyst morphology and artificial shrinkage as key predictors of post-warming performance and implantation potential, though no correlation was found with trophectoderm biopsy.

Recognizing that collapsed embryos are asymmetrical, we applied an area-based approach for continuous evaluation of blastocyst re-expansion dynamics. This method involves manually tracking the embryo boundaries to create a closed polygon chain, divided by the inner space inside the zona pellucida, where the re-expanding embryo is expected to occupy over time. This analysis is facilitated by continuous time-lapse monitoring of warmed blastocysts, offering a dynamic assessment of embryo re-expansion.

By integrating time-lapse system recordings with a precise method for calculating two-dimensional embryo areas, we define re-expansion speed, reflecting the blastocyst’s post-warming performance. Our primary objective was a comprehensive analysis of blastocyst behavior post-thaw, correlating initial size, growth rate, and maximum size with clinical outcomes after embryo transfer. The secondary goal was to compare re-expansion speed with and without artificial collapse, including a PGT-M population to assess the combined effect of artificial collapse and trophectoderm biopsy on blastocyst morphokinetics and implantation potential.

Materials and methods

Study population and evaluation

We conducted a retrospective analysis of 463 FET cycles between September 2022 and May 2024 at Sheba Medical Center, selecting 170 cycles for detailed morphokinetics analysis. The full cohort was used to compare clinical outcomes across pre-vitrification laboratory procedures, whereas the 170-cycle subset was used for detailed time-lapse-based assessment of post-warming re-expansion dynamics. The cycles were divided into three groups: Control (no pre-vitrification manipulation), PGT (trophectoderm biopsy 30 min pre-vitrification), and Artificial Collapse (AC, laser pulse 10 min pre-vitrification) (Fig. 1). The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board at Sheba Medical Center (0362-23-SMC).

Flowchart of group allocation according to pre-vitrified laboratory practice

Embryo culture, assessment and vitrification

Embryos were assessed on day 5 or 6 (120 h or 144 ± 1 h post-ICSI) using Gardner method scoring [29, 30]. Only type A and B blastocysts were selected for vitrification. Artificial collapse (AC) was implemented as a laboratory-wide protocol during a predefined validation period. During this phase, AC was applied uniformly to all eligible blastocysts scheduled for vitrification, independent of embryo quality or clinical characteristics, in order to evaluate its effect on post-warming behavior. Following completion of this validation period, the laboratory reverted to standard vitrification practice. The decision to subsequently adopt AC as a routine procedure was made only after retrospective evaluation of post-warming morphokinetics and clinical outcomes. Only blastocysts that had reached an expanded stage at the time of vitrification (Gardner grade 3–4) were technically eligible for AC, as physical collapse cannot be reliably induced in non-expanded embryos. All vitrified embryos included in the study were of good morphological quality (Gardner A–B). Laser-assisted artificial collapse was performed using the ZILOS-tk laser system (Hamilton Thorne, Beverly, MA, USA). A single laser pulse was applied at the junction between two trophectoderm cells, away from the inner cell mass, using the minimal laser setting routinely applied in our laboratory protocol.

Following laser application, all blastocysts in the AC group were incubated for 10 min before vitrification. Collapse was operationally defined as visible contraction of the blastocyst accompanied by a reduction in the blastocoel cavity. The extent of collapse was assessed qualitatively but was not quantitatively measured or categorized as partial versus complete; complete disappearance of the blastocoel cavity was not required. Further contraction may occur during subsequent exposure to the equilibration solution. No blastocysts were excluded from the AC group or reassigned to another study group on the basis of the extent of collapse.

For PGT-M blastocysts, performed for clinical monogenic disease testing and, with exclusion of concurrent PGT-A analysis, zona pellucida dissection was performed on day 3. Upon reaching the appropriate stage, 3–7 herniated trophectoderm cells were biopsied and vitrified 30 min later, according to the routine laboratory workflow and biopsy-to-vitrification protocol used in our IVF laboratory during the study period. During this interval, partial blastocoel re-expansion was commonly observed prior to vitrification.

Vitrification was performed using a vitrification kit (Sage, Cooper Surgical, Denmark) with Cryotop™ or VitriFit™ devices, following manufacturer guidelines. The warming protocol utilized the Kitazato warming kit, involving sequential transfers through sucrose solutions and washing in WS solution before culturing in blastocyst media (Sage, Cooper Surgical, Denmark) under mineral oil [31].

Time-lapse monitoring of warmed blastocyst morphokinetics

The subset of 170 cycles selected for detailed morphokinetic analysis was determined by the availability of complete time-lapse recordings and image quality suitable for area-based measurements, and was independent of clinical outcome. Blastocysts were incubated in the EmbryoScope™ system (Unisense FertiliTech, Vitrolife Denmark) from warming until transfer. Images were recorded automatically at seven focal planes every 17 min and saved for evaluation. The sharpest plane was manually selected for final measurements.

Measurements of blastocyst diameter and re-expansion speed

Images were exported at 4 to 20 time points and analyzed using GeoGebra graphic calculator software (BYJU’S, India). The polygon maker tool was used to trace the margins of re-expanded blastocysts, and areas were calculated (Fig. 2A). This method was applied to the outer periphery of the blastocyst, excluding the zona pellucida. The ratio between these measurements was calculated. This process was repeated for all frames to determine the expansion rate over time (Fig. 2B). Throughout post-warming time-lapse monitoring, the zona pellucida retained a stable morphology, allowing its inner contour to be used as a fixed reference for normalized re-expansion measurements. Collected data underwent statistical analysis.

Embryo transfer and clinical pregnancy

Endometrial preparation methods, natural or hormone replacement treatment (HRT), were chosen by the physician [32]. A single warmed blastocyst was transferred under ultrasound guidance 5 days post-ovulation or with progesterone supplementation. β-hCG tests confirmed pregnancy 12 days post-transfer, and implantation was verified through ultrasound.

Calculations and statistical analysis

The three groups were compared using the following variables and calculations: (a) T1, representing the initial re-expansion ratio immediately after thawing. (b) MaxR, the maximal re-expansion ratio during culture. (c) MaxR–T1/time intervals, calculated as the difference between the maximal re-expansion ratio (MaxR) and T1, divided by the number of time intervals between these measurements (. (d) ARSR, the average re-expansion speed rate, calculated by dividing the change in re-expansion ratio across all time points by the number of time intervals ( (e) Time to Max refers to the duration required to achieve the maximal re-expansion ratio during culture.

Continuous variables were tested for normal distribution before applying parametric statistics, including One-way ANOVA with post hoc Bonferroni and Kruskal-Wallis tests. Categorical variables were analyzed using the chi-square test. Significance was accepted at P < 0.05. A multivariate logistic regression model was used to evaluate the association between pregnancy outcome and ARSR, while adjusting for relevant post-thaw morphokinetic parameters (T1, time to maximal re-expansion) and study group.

For comparisons of clinical outcome rates between study groups (positive β-hCG, clinical pregnancy, and ongoing pregnancy), overall group differences were assessed using omnibus testing. Pairwise post-hoc comparisons between individual groups were not performed, as these analyses were considered exploratory.

Statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS v.29 (IBM Corporation Inc, Chicago, Illinois, USA).

In examining the re-expansion ratios over time in pregnant versus non-pregnant patients, as shown in Fig. 4, several key trends emerged. Initially, both groups displayed similar re-expansion ratios, with 67% for non-pregnant and 68% for pregnant patients. Mean ARSR was significantly higher in embryos resulting in pregnancy compared with non-pregnant embryos (4.3% vs. 3.0%, P = 0.033) (Fig. 4). To illustrate the gradient effect of re-expansion speed on blastocyst viability and implantation potential, we categorized ARSR into four quartiles (Table 4). For blastocysts with an ARSR of ≤ 0.9%, 69.8% were non-pregnant, while 30.2% achieved pregnancy. As ARSR increased to 1%-3%, the non-pregnancy rate decreased to 52.4%, with the pregnancy rate rising to 47.6%. This trend continued in the 3.1%-5% ARSR category, where 59.5% of blastocysts were non-pregnant, and 40.5% resulted in pregnancy. Notably, in the highest ARSR group (≥ 5.1%), the non-pregnancy rate dropped significantly to 41.9%, while the pregnancy rate increased to 58% (Table 4). Within the pregnant group, the 4th quartile (≥ 5.1%) showed almost 30% more pregnancies than the first quartile (≤ 0.9%), P = 0.021, highlighting the positive correlation between higher ARSR and successful pregnancy rates. A significant increasing trend in ongoing pregnancy rates was observed across increasing ARSR quartiles (P for trend = 0.021).

Multivariate logistic regression analysis of predictors for pregnancy outcomes

Multivariate logistic regression analysis (Supplemental Table 1), adjusted for T1, time to maximal re-expansion, and study group, demonstrated that embryos in the highest ARSR quartile (≥ 5.1%) had significantly higher odds of achieving pregnancy compared with the lowest quartile, after adjustment for T1 and time to maximal re-expansion (OR = 3.42, 95% CI: 1.32–8.81; p = 0.011). In contrast, T1 and time to maximal re-expansion were not independently associated with pregnancy outcome in the adjusted model.

Discussion

Re-expansion of frozen-thawed blastocysts is a significant indicator of their viability and implantation potential [22–24]. While existing methods evaluate re-expanded blastocysts [25, 26], the present approach enabled continuous evaluation of post-thaw blastocyst re-expansion dynamics including accurate measurement of the collapsed embryo’s area and the ability to track re-expansion over time, capturing the blastocyst’s dynamics and recovery/re-expansion speed rate. To ensure precision, we used a method that traces the margins of the collapsed blastocyst to form a closed polygon. Although this approach is time-consuming, it currently provides the most precise re-expansion rate calculation. Future work should focus on developing an automatic annotation model to streamline these calculations. It is important to note that this method assesses the two-dimensional area of the blastocyst and does not consider the full Z-axis measurement of the warmed blastocyst.

Recent studies have emphasized the significance of pre-vitrification and post-warming embryo characteristics in predicting successful implantation and clinical pregnancy outcomes. Gabrielsen et al. found that while pre-vitrification parameters like trophectoderm grading and post-warming blastocyst size correlate significantly with clinical pregnancy, factors such as natural collapses and maximal collapse size before vitrification do not [33]. Similarly, Ito et al. showed that inadequate post-warming re-expansion of blastocysts negatively impacts ART outcomes, highlighting the importance of evaluating blastocyst shrinkage both before vitrification and after warming [34]. Elkhatib et al. further supported the relevance of post-warming re-expansion, identifying the re-expansion rate one hour post-biopsy as an independent predictor of live birth [35].

Although previous studies have focused on discrete post-warming time points, our findings suggest that such snapshot assessments may not fully capture blastocyst behavior. For example, Zhu et al. demonstrated that assessment of blastocyst re-expansion at a defined time point after warming was associated with implantation and pregnancy outcomes, supporting the clinical relevance of post-thaw re-expansion evaluation [36]. Differences in re-expansion between embryos that resulted in pregnancy and those that did not were modest at early time points, but became more apparent when re-expansion was followed over time. In particular, non-pregnant embryos frequently showed interrupted or unstable re-expansion patterns, including secondary collapse, whereas embryos leading to pregnancy demonstrated more sustained and consistent growth. Recent work by Conversa et al. has further demonstrated that post-warming blastocyst behavior is highly dynamic and may include both expansion and contraction patterns, with sustained and stable re-expansion being associated with improved implantation and live birth outcomes, whereas recurrent or pronounced collapses may reflect reduced developmental competence [37]. These observations emphasize the importance of evaluating post-thaw blastocyst dynamics as a continuous process rather than relying on isolated measurements.

In our clinical study, we introduced the Average Re-Expansion Speed Rate (ARSR), a novel parameter that tracks all changes in embryo size post-warming to predict pregnancy outcomes. Our findings reveal a clear correlation between higher re-expansion rates and successful pregnancies. The pregnant group showed faster and more consistent re-expansion, emphasizing that both speed and consistency are critical for predicting blastocyst viability and implantation potential. While some embryos with low ARSRs did result in pregnancy, our logistic regression analysis showed that embryos in the highest ARSR quartile (≥ 5.1) were over three times more likely to result in pregnancy compared to the first quartile (OR = 3.419, p = 0.011). This underscores ARSR as a potentially clinically relevant morphokinetic parameter in assessing embryo viability and optimizing IVF outcomes.

Several studies indicate that removing blastocoel fluid from blastocysts before vitrification using a laser pulse significantly improves survival, clinical pregnancy, and implantation rates [13–20, 38]. Six out of nine studies showed significantly higher survival rates [14–16, 18, 19, 38], and five out of seven reported increased implantation or clinical pregnancy rates [15–17, 19, 20]. A meta-analysis by Boyard et al. found a non-significant increase in live birth rates following artificial collapse [39]. Accordingly, the PGT-M group served as a procedural comparator to assess the impact of different laboratory manipulations on post-thaw blastocyst dynamics. In our cohort, higher ARSR values were associated with pregnancy, and both the AC and PGT-M groups demonstrated higher ARSR compared with the Control group (4.81% and 4.33% vs. 3.5%). Ongoing pregnancy rates also differed across groups (52.6% for AC, 41.7% for PGT-M, and 35.9% for Control). Consistent with the mechanism of fluid reduction before vitrification, initial post-thaw re-expansion (T1) was slightly lower in the AC group than in the Control and PGT-M groups, although this difference was not statistically significant (63.9% vs. 70.2% and 65.4%; P = 0.096), likely reflecting pre-vitrification shrinkage induced by laser pulses. Notably, the AC group achieved a significantly higher maximal re-expansion ratio (MaxR) than the Control and PGT-M groups (93.8% vs. 88.6% and 85.2%; P = 0.031), and a significantly longer interval between T1 and maximal re-expansion (MaxR–T1/time intervals) (3.98% vs. 2.51% and 3.39%; P = 0.015). Taken together, these findings align with Boyard et al. [39] and support the clinical utility of artificial collapse before vitrification, as it was associated with improved post-thaw re-expansion dynamics and favorable ongoing pregnancy outcomes in our dataset. Notably, despite the higher mean maternal age observed in the AC group, this group demonstrated the most favorable post-thaw re-expansion dynamics and the highest ongoing pregnancy rate, suggesting that the observed effects were not driven by younger patient age.

Although blastocyst survival after warming often leads to transfer, post-thaw development represents an additional biological phase that may provide clinically relevant information beyond pre-vitrification morphology. Allowing several hours of culture after warming enables assessment of re-expansion dynamics and recovery stability, which may assist embryo prioritization when multiple embryos are available or support decision-making in scenarios where each transfer is critical, such as surrogacy. Despite the recognized importance of pre-vitrified embryo quality, post-thaw behavior remains underexplored and is not routinely integrated into clinical evaluation.

A notable limitation of this study is the relatively small sample size. Additionally, re-expansion ratio calculations relied on manual area measurements due to the lack of an automated system. Pre-vitrification absolute blastocyst diameter was not assessed, as the study focused on relative post-thaw re-expansion dynamics over time. Nevertheless, baseline blastocyst size may have influenced post-warming morphokinetic behavior and therefore represents a potential source of variability that should be considered when interpreting the findings. In addition, the extent of blastocyst collapse following laser application was not quantitatively measured or categorized as partial versus complete. Consequently, the potential influence of both baseline blastocyst size and the degree of pre-vitrification contraction on subsequent post-warming morphokinetic behavior could not be independently evaluated.

Future research should aim to develop automated systems for more precise and efficient analysis, expand the sample size to validate these findings, and investigate the long-term effects of AC on embryo development and clinical outcomes to refine this practice.

Given the demonstrated importance of morphokinetic parameters, time-lapse imaging techniques may provide clinically relevant information regarding blastocyst behavior following embryo thawing. In current clinical practice, post-warming observation is commonly performed within a 2–4-hour interval, allowing dynamic assessment of blastocyst behavior while avoiding unnecessary prolongation of embryo culture prior to transfer. Future research should focus on comprehensively analyzing these parameters by studying a large cohort of embryos. By leveraging machine learning and artificial intelligence, it is necessary to develop predictive models that accurately forecast implantation probabilities, thereby optimizing clinical outcomes and advancing the field of assisted reproductive technology.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that dynamic post-thaw blastocyst behavior is strongly associated with pregnancy outcomes following frozen embryo transfer. The findings highlight that blastocyst response varies across laboratory procedures, with artificial collapse prior to vitrification significantly enhancing embryo viability. This approach offers a valuable tool for optimizing embryo selection and improving in vitro fertilization outcomes. Further research should focus on developing automated measurement systems and predictive models by machine learning and artificial intelligence to refine this approach for clinical application.
