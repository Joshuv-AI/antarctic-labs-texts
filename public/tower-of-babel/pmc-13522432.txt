Reply to ensuring trustworthy AI assisted guideline development for clinical practice
Authors: Dubai Li, Nan Jiang, Yu Tian, Xinping Tian, Jingsong Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In this Reply, we respond to the Matters Arising by Zhang and Fu regarding the trustworthiness of AI-assisted clinical guideline development, using Quicker as a case study. We clarify which safeguards for transparency, traceability, and uncertainty handling are already embedded, to a substantial extent, in Quicker’s design, and outline areas of alignment on validation, governance, and modular evaluation. We further discuss broader challenges related to trust, safeguards, and responsible deployment of large language model–based systems in evidence-based medicine. We emphasize that advancing such systems requires not only technical acceleration, but also systematic human oversight, rigorous evaluation frameworks, and community-wide governance to ensure safe and trustworthy clinical adoption.

We thank Zhang and Fu for their thoughtful Matters Arising entitled “Ensuring trustworthy AI-assisted guideline development for clinical practice”, and for their careful engagement with our work introducing Quicker, an LLM-driven system for streamlining evidence-based clinical recommendation development1. We appreciate their emphasis on trust, governance, and responsible deployment, and we broadly agree that speed alone is not sufficient for clinical translation. Their discussion provides an important opportunity to clarify which safeguards are already embedded in Quicker’s design, which align closely with our intended use and future directions, and how the community might more systematically address the remaining gaps between technical feasibility and trustworthy clinical adoption.

Implemented safeguards for transparency, traceability, and uncertainty handling

We strongly agree with Zhang and Fu’s proposal of delivering AI-assisted recommendations in the form of verifiable evidence bundles. In practice, Quicker already implements this principle to a substantial extent. Throughout execution, Quicker persistently stores and indexes structured intermediate outputs—including decomposed clinical questions, retrieved records, screening decisions, extracted evidence profiles, and assessment results—to enable continuous human inspection and intervention. These design choices reflect our core assumption that transparency and controllability are prerequisites for any LLM-assisted system operating in evidence-based medicine. The corresponding code implementation and illustrative examples are publicly available in our open-source repository, allowing users to examine, reproduce, and audit the full workflow.

Zhang and Fu further suggest that each recommendation should be explicitly linked to the underlying source literature. We consider this recommendation highly practical and valuable. However, it is important to clarify that in Quicker, final recommendations are not generated by directly paraphrasing or summarizing individual source texts. Instead, they are derived from structured evidence profiles through quantitative aggregation, scoring, and statistical synthesis. As a result, there is no one-to-one textual correspondence between a recommendation statement and a specific paragraph in the original literature. Nevertheless, we fully agree that richer explanatory layers—such as expanded rationales grounded in the contributing evidence profiles—can and should be provided to facilitate user understanding, verification, and critique. We view this as an important direction for improving interpretability without compromising methodological rigor.

The Matters Arising highlights the risk of overconfidence in LLM-based systems, including those employing retrieval augmentation, and calls for uncertainty-aware outputs and calibrated deferral. We fully concur with this assessment. A central motivation behind Quicker’s design is precisely to support users in critically interpreting AI-assisted outputs rather than accepting them as authoritative conclusions.

To this end, Quicker explicitly incorporates the GRADE framework—one of the most widely accepted tools in evidence-based medicine—for evaluating evidence quality and communicating recommendation confidence2. By structuring the workflow around established expert-driven criteria, Quicker aligns its outputs with the epistemic standards already familiar to guideline developers and clinical researchers. This design choice not only improves interpretability but also facilitates systematic checking and debate over evidence certainty, limitations, and trade-offs.

We further agree with Zhang and Fu’s proposals to expose sources of uncertainty more explicitly and to support calibrated deferral when confidence is low or evidence is inconsistent. Such mechanisms would complement existing human methodologies by making uncertainty actionable rather than implicit. In our view, these features are not in tension with Quicker’s current framework; rather, they represent a natural extension that broadens the evaluative lens through which LLM-assisted systems can be assessed and responsibly used.

Shared perspectives on validation, governance, and modular evaluation

Beyond safeguards already implemented, we find substantial alignment with several additional recommendations raised by Zhang and Fu.

First, we agree that the human-in-the-loop component should be formalized through lightweight but standardized validation procedures, such as short checklists for guideline panels or designated rapid reviewers. Given that errors in eligibility decisions, outcome extraction, or risk-of-bias assessment can disproportionately affect downstream conclusions, targeted human verification at these high-impact points is both efficient and necessary.

Second, we concur that broader validation is required to establish generalizability and detect bias prior to wide deployment, a need that is also acknowledged in our original paper. Prospective pilots across multiple specialties, populations, and institutional settings—with pre-specified evaluation endpoints such as screening sensitivity, extraction error rates for key outcomes, stability of recommendations across model versions, and subgroup coverage—are essential.

Finally, we view Quicker as a modular evidence-synthesis pipeline rather than a monolithic generator, with explicit stages for retrieval, study selection, evidence assessment, and recommendation formulation. This decomposition enables stage-specific evaluation, targeted quality control, and systematic error tracing, which are essential for trustworthy deployment and continuous refinement. Within this pipeline-based perspective, governance should be addressed primarily at the deployment level, including clear identification of AI-assisted drafts and model versions, preservation of structured execution records for auditability, and explicit constraints on the downstream reuse of unreviewed AI-generated content. Taken together, these measures complement technical system design by ensuring that AI-assisted outputs remain traceable, reviewable, and clearly distinguished from validated clinical evidence.

Broader discussion: trust, safeguards, and responsible deployment

We deeply share Zhang and Fu’s perspective that trust is the central challenge facing LLM-based systems in clinical guideline development. On one hand, improving user confidence requires more transparent, interpretable, and methodologically grounded system designs. On the other hand, robust safeguards are equally necessary to prevent misuse, overreliance, or premature deployment of systems whose apparent fluency may exceed their epistemic reliability.

Guided by this dual concern, Quicker was deliberately not released as a polished, public-facing product with a visual interface designed for broad consumption. We intentionally avoided presenting Quicker in a form that could be misconstrued as a ready-to-trust clinical product. Instead, Quicker is positioned as a technical instantiation of a research concept—one that demonstrates how LLMs can be systematically embedded within evidence-based medicine workflows and evaluated under controlled, transparent conditions.

Despite encouraging empirical results, we believe that substantial gaps remain between current LLM capabilities and truly reliable, deployable clinical systems. Many of these gaps stem from limitations intrinsic to LLMs themselves, including sensitivity to prompt design, instability across model versions, and challenges in representing uncertainty and normative judgment3. Bridging these gaps will require not only improved system designs but also shared community efforts to establish comprehensive usage guidelines, evaluation frameworks, and governance standards.

We therefore advocate collective engagement from the research community. Advancing LLM-based clinical systems responsibly will depend on coordinated progress in methodology, benchmarking, transparency, and policy—so that gains in efficiency do not come at the expense of scientific rigor or patient safety.
