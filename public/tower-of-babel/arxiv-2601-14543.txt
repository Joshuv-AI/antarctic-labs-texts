Shapley Value on Uncertain Data
Zhuofan Jia, Jian Pei

Abstract
The Shapley value provides a principled framework for fairly distributing rewards among participants according to their individual contributions. While prior work has applied this concept to data valuation in machine learning, existing formulations overwhelmingly assume that each participant contributes a fixed, deterministic dataset. In practice, however, data owners often provide samples drawn from underlying probabilistic distributions, introducing stochasticity into their marginal contributions and rendering the Shapley value itself a random variable. This work addresses this gap by proposing a framework for the Shapley value of probabilistic data distributions that quantifies both the expected contribution and the variance of each participant, thereby capturing uncertainty induced by random sampling. We develop theoretical and empirical methodologies for estimating these quantities: on the theoretical side, we derive unbiased estimators for the expectation and variance of the probabilistic Shapley value and analyze their statistical properties; on the empirical side, we introduce three Monte Carlo–based estimation algorithms—a baseline estimator using independent samples, a pooled estimator that improves efficiency through sample reuse, and a stratified pooled estimator that adaptively allocates sampling budget based on player-specific variability. Experiments on synthetic and real datasets demonstrate that these methods achieve strong accuracy–efficiency trade-offs, with the stratified pooled approach attaining substantial variance reduction at minimal additional cost. By extending Shapley value analysis from deterministic datasets to probabilistic data distributions, this work provides both theoretical rigor and practical tools for fair and reliable data valuation in modern stochastic data-sharing environments.

As another example, data markets and commercial data-sharing platforms frequently employ metered or pay-per-query access to data. Data buyers do not receive full datasets; rather, they obtain a budgeted number of randomized samples or query responses from each data provider

These examples underscore that sampling-based contribution is not a theoretical abstraction but a practical and desirable mechanism in many real-world systems. This stochasticity gives rise to a fundamental challenge. The Shapley value becomes a

This work fills this gap by introducing a general framework for the Shapley value of

We formally define the Shapley value as a random quantity induced by sampling from players’ distributions and derive expressions for its expectation and variance whenever closed-form solutions exist. Our formulation captures not only the average contribution of each distribution but also the variability caused by stochastic sampling, thereby extending deterministic Shapley valuation to a realistic probabilistic setting.

This study makes the following key contributions:

•

A probabilistic formulation of data Shapley.

We define the Shapley value when each participant contributes data by sampling from an underlying distribution, making the Shapley value itself a random variable. We show how its expectation and variance characterize average contribution and contribution stability, respectively.

•

Closed-form characterization of Shapley expectation and variance.

We derive analytical expressions for the expectation and variance of the probabilistic Shapley value whenever the underlying marginal contributions admit tractable forms, establishing a theoretical foundation for uncertainty-aware data valuation.

•

Unbiased empirical estimators.

We prove that natural Monte Carlo estimators for the expectation and variance of the probabilistic Shapley value are unbiased and characterize their statistical properties.

•

Three scalable Monte Carlo estimation algorithms.

We develop:

(ii) a

(iii) a

•

Comprehensive empirical evaluation.

Through experiments on synthetic and real datasets, we demonstrate that the proposed estimators achieve favorable accuracy–efficiency trade-offs, with the stratified pooled method yielding substantial variance reductions and strong robustness across settings.

Our contributions produce a unified framework for data valuation under uncertainty. By quantifying both the expectation and variance of probabilistic Shapley values, this work enhances the interpretability, robustness, and practical viability of Shapley-based mechanisms in data markets and collaborative learning.

II
Problem Formulation

In this section, we formalize the problem of Shapley value estimation when participants contribute data by sampling from underlying probabilistic distributions. This setting departs fundamentally from the classical deterministic formulation: the utility of a coalition and the marginal contribution of each player now depend on random draws, making the Shapley value itself a random variable. We begin by reviewing the standard Shapley value definition and its computational interpretation, then extend these concepts to the probabilistic regime. This formulation provides the mathematical foundations necessary for defining, analyzing, and ultimately estimating the expectation and variance of Shapley values under stochastic data generation.

II-A

Shapley Value and Computation

Consider a cooperative game with a set of players
N=\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
and a utility function
v:2^{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, where
v(S)
denotes the total value or payoff achieved by coalition
S\subseteq N
. The goal is to allocate
v(N)
fairly among all players according to their marginal contributions.

Definition II.1

(Shapley value
[
45
]
)
.

The Shapley value of player
i\in N
is defined as

\phi_{i}=\sum_{S\subseteq N\setminus\{i\}}\frac{|S|!\,(n-|S|-1)!}{n!}\big(v(S\cup\{i\})-v(S)\big),

(1)

where
v(S\cup\{i\})-v(S)
represents the marginal contribution of player
i
to coalition
S
.

The Shapley value satisfies four desirable properties
[
45
,
46
,
49
,
44
,
8
]
.
First,
efficiency
ensures that the total value is fully distributed among all players, that is,
\sum_{i\in N}\phi_{i}(v)=v(N)
.
Second,
symmetry
requires that players who make identical marginal contributions across all coalitions receive the same payoff.
Third, the
null player
property states that a player who contributes nothing to any coalition obtains zero value.
Finally,
additivity
guarantees that for two games with utility functions
v
and
w
, the Shapley value in the combined game equals the sum of the Shapley values in the individual games, i.e.,
\phi_{i}(v+w)=\phi_{i}(v)+\phi_{i}(w)
.

An equivalent expression of the Shapley value is derived through permutations of the players
[
45
]
. Let
\Pi(N)
denote the set of all
n!
permutations of
N
. For a permutation
\pi\in\Pi(N)
, let
P_{i}^{\pi}
denote the set of players that precede
i
in
\pi
. The Shapley value can then be expressed as

\phi_{i}=\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\pi\in\Pi(N)}\big(v(P_{i}^{\pi}\cup\{i\})-v(P_{i}^{\pi})\big).

(2)

Equation (
2
) shows that the Shapley value can be viewed as the average marginal contribution of player
i
across all possible player orderings. However, enumerating all permutations is computationally prohibitive for large
n
. In fact, exact computation of the Shapley value is #P-hard for general cooperative games
[
11
]
, and remains intractable for several compact game representations such as MC-nets
[
13
]
.

To approximate this, the Monte Carlo method can be used to sample a subset of
k
random permutations
\{\pi_{1},\allowbreak\pi_{2},\allowbreak\ldots,\allowbreak\pi_{k}\}\allowbreak\subseteq\allowbreak\Pi(N)
. The approximate Shapley value is then given by

\widehat{\phi_{i}(v)}=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\big(v(P_{i}^{\pi_{j}}\cup\{i\})-v(P_{i}^{\pi_{j}})\big).

(3)

In a collaborative machine learning context, each player
i
owns a dataset or data source, and the utility function
v(S)
corresponds to the model performance (e.g., accuracy or inverse loss) achieved when training on the data from coalition
S
. The Shapley value thus measures the expected contribution of each data source to the collective model performance.

II-B

Shapley Value of Probabilistic Distributions

We extend the classical formulation by considering the case where each player
i
owns a probabilistic data distribution
P_{i}
rather than a fixed dataset. Each player contributes random samples drawn from
P_{i}
, and the model utility depends on these realizations. The game outcome therefore becomes stochastic, making each player’s Shapley value a random variable.

The objective is to estimate both the expected Shapley value
\mathbb{E}[\phi_{i}(v)]
and its variance
\mathrm{Var}(\phi_{i}(v))
for each player
i
. The expectation quantifies the average contribution of player
i
, while the variance measures the uncertainty in that contribution due to sampling variability. Accurately estimating both quantities enables practitioners to assess the stability and reliability of data valuation in probabilistic environments. For clarity, we summarize the main notation used in this paper in Table
I
.

II-C

The Baseline Method

We propose a Monte Carlo estimation framework for computing the Shapley value of probabilistic distributions. The baseline method, summarized in Algorithm
1
, performs repeated sampling to obtain unbiased estimates of both the expectation and variance of the Shapley value.

For each of
n_{\text{games}}
independent games, we draw fresh samples from each player’s distribution. Specifically, for game
g\in\{1,\ldots,n_{\text{games}}\}
, we generate

D_{i}^{(g)}=\{(x_{j},y_{j})\sim P_{i}\}_{j=1}^{n_{\text{sample}}},\quad i=1,\ldots,n.

(4)

These samples remain fixed within the game but differ across games. Within each game, we perform
n_{\text{iterations}}
random permutations of players. For each permutation
\pi
, we sequentially form coalitions and compute the marginal contribution of each player:

\Delta_{i}^{(g,t)}=v(S\cup\{i\}\mid D_{S\cup\{i\}}^{(g)})-v(S\mid D_{S}^{(g)}),

(5)

where
D_{S}^{(g)}=\bigcup_{j\in S}D_{j}^{(g)}
aggregates samples from players in
S
.

The Shapley value estimate for player
i
is obtained by averaging over all games and permutations:

\widehat{\mathbb{E}[\phi_{i}]}=\frac{1}{n_{\text{games}}\,n_{\text{iterations}}}\sum_{g=1}^{n_{\text{games}}}\sum_{t=1}^{n_{\text{iterations}}}\Delta_{i}^{(g,t)}.

(6)

Treating each game as an independent realization, the variance across games provides an empirical estimate of
\mathrm{Var}(\phi_{i}(v))
:

\widehat{\mathrm{Var}(\phi_{i})}=\frac{1}{n_{\text{games}}-1}\sum_{g=1}^{n_{\text{games}}}\left(\bar{\phi}_{i}^{(g)}-\widehat{\mathbb{E}[\phi_{i}]}\right)^{2},

(7)

where
\bar{\phi}_{i}^{(g)}=\frac{1}{n_{\text{iterations}}}\sum_{t=1}^{n_{\text{iterations}}}\Delta_{i}^{(g,t)}
.

Although unbiased, the baseline method requires
O(n\cdot n_{\text{sample}}\cdot n_{\text{games}}\cdot n_{\text{iterations}})
total samples, making it computationally demanding when access to distributions is expensive. This motivates the development of the pooled and stratified pooled methods, which reduce sampling overhead by reusing samples across games while maintaining accuracy.

III
Closed Form Solutions

In this section, we develop closed-form solutions for the Shapley value in the probabilistic setting, focusing in particular on its expectation and variance. When both coalitions and player data are stochastic, the utility function and the resulting marginal contributions become random variables. Our objective is to characterize the expectation and variance of
\phi_{i}
under this stochastic framework, providing analytical foundations for understanding Shapley values on uncertain data.

III-A

Expectation of Shapley Value

Denote by
\phi_{i}
the random Shapley value of player
i
, computed from a single realization of samples drawn from all players’ distributions. The corresponding ground-truth quantity of interest is its expectation,
\mathbb{E}[\phi_{i}]
.

Proposition III.1
.

If the expected marginal contribution
\mathbb{E}[u(S\cup\{i\})-u(S)]
admits a closed-form expression for all
S\subseteq N\setminus\{i\}
, then the expected Shapley value
\mathbb{E}[\phi_{i}]
also admits a closed-form expression.

Proof.

Taking the expectation of Eq. (
1
) and using linearity of expectation,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\phi_{i}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{S\subseteq N\setminus\{i\}}\frac{|S|!(n-|S|-1)!}{n!}\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[u(S\cup\{i\})-u(S)\right].

Each term is a constant weight multiplied by the expected marginal gain, so the entire expression admits a closed form whenever each
\mathbb{E}[u(S\cup\{i\})-u(S)]
does.
∎

Example III.1
.

Suppose each player
i
is associated with a Gaussian variable
X_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{i},\sigma_{i}^{2})
and
u(S)=\sum_{i\in S}X_{i}
. The marginal contribution of player
i
is
X_{i}
, since
u(S\cup\{i\})-u(S)=\sum_{j\in S\cup\{i\}}X_{j}-\sum_{j\in S}X_{j}=X_{i}
.
Consequently,
\mathbb{E}[u(S\cup\{i\})-u(S)]=\mu_{i}
. Substituting into Eq. (
1
) and using the fact that the weights sum to
1
yields

\mathbb{E}[\phi_{i}]=\mu_{i}.

As another example, if
u(S)
follows a finite mixture of Gaussian distributions,

u(S)\sim\sum_{k=1}^{|S|}w_{k}\,\mathcal{N}(\mu_{k},\sigma_{k}^{2}),

then the expected marginal gain is
\mathbb{E}[u(S\cup\{i\})-u(S)]=w_{i}\mu_{i}
. Therefore,

\mathbb{E}[\phi_{i}]=w_{i}\mu_{i}.

Even when
\mathbb{E}[u(S)]
lacks a simple form, a closed-form
\mathbb{E}[\phi_{i}]
exists as long as the expected marginal gains are tractable.
∎

III-B

Variance of Shapley Value

The variance of
\phi_{i}
, denoted
\mathrm{Var}(\phi_{i})
, captures the uncertainty of player
i
’s contribution across stochastic realizations.

Proposition III.2
.

If the variance and covariance of the marginal contributions
u(S\cup\{i\})-u(S)
admit closed-form expressions for all
S,T\subseteq N\setminus\{i\}
, then the variance of the Shapley value
\mathrm{Var}(\phi_{i})
also admits a closed-form expression.

Proof.

From Eq. (
1
),

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}(\phi_{i})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Var}\!\left(\sum_{S}c_{S}\,(u(S\cup\{i\})-u(S))\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{S}c_{S}^{2}\,\mathrm{Var}(u(S\cup\{i\})-u(S))

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{S\neq T}c_{S}c_{T}\,\mathrm{Cov}(u(S\cup\{i\})-u(S),

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad u(T\cup\{i\})-u(T)).

where
c_{S}=\frac{|S|!(n-|S|-1)!}{n!}
and
c_{T}=\frac{|T|!(n-|T|-1)!}{n!}
. If all variances and covariances of marginal gains are available in closed form, so is
\mathrm{Var}(\phi_{i})
.
∎

Example III.2
.

Let
u(S)
follow the same Gaussian mixture as in Example
III.1
:

u(S)\sim\sum_{k=1}^{|S|}w_{k}\,\mathcal{N}(\mu_{k},\sigma_{k}^{2}).

Then,
u(S\cup\{i\})-u(S)\sim w_{i}\,\mathcal{N}(\mu_{i},\sigma_{i}^{2})
, giving

\mathrm{Var}(u(S\cup\{i\})-u(S))=w_{i}^{2}\sigma_{i}^{2}.

Since these marginal gains are independent of the coalition
S
, the covariance between any two marginal contributions equals
w_{i}^{2}\sigma_{i}^{2}
. Consequently,
\mathrm{Var}(\phi_{i})
is a weighted finite sum of these quantities and has a closed-form expression.
∎

III-C

Estimated Expectation of Shapley Value

We now analyze the empirical estimator of the expected Shapley value. Let
m
denote the number of independently sampled games. For each game
j
, let
\phi_{i}^{(j)}
be the Shapley value of player
i
. The estimator is

\widehat{\mathbb{E}[\phi_{i}]}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\phi_{i}^{(j)}.

(8)

Proposition III.3
.

The estimator in Eq. (
8
) is unbiased, i.e.,

\mathbb{E}[\widehat{\mathbb{E}\allowbreak[\phi_{i}]}]\allowbreak=\mathbb{E}\allowbreak[\phi_{i}]
.

Proof.

Since each
\phi_{i}^{(j)}
is an i.i.d. realization of
\phi_{i}
,

\mathbb{E}[\widehat{\mathbb{E}[\phi_{i}]}]=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\mathbb{E}[\phi_{i}^{(j)}]=\mathbb{E}[\phi_{i}].

∎

Proposition III.4
.

If
\;\mathrm{Var}(\phi_{i})
admits a closed-form expression, then the variance of the estimator
\widehat{\mathbb{E}[\phi_{i}]}
is

\mathrm{Var}\!\left(\widehat{\mathbb{E}[\phi_{i}]}\right)=\frac{1}{m}\,\mathrm{Var}(\phi_{i}).

(9)

Proof.

Using independence of
\phi_{i}^{(j)}
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}\!\left(\widehat{\mathbb{E}[\phi_{i}]}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Var}\!\left(\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\phi_{i}^{(j)}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\mathrm{Var}(\phi_{i})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m}\,\mathrm{Var}(\phi_{i}).

∎

III-D

Estimated Variance of Shapley Value

The sample variance across
m
realizations is

\widehat{\mathrm{Var}(\phi_{i})}=\frac{1}{m-1}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\left(\phi_{i}^{(j)}-\widehat{\mathbb{E}[\phi_{i}]}\right)^{2}.

(10)

Proposition III.5
.

The estimator in Eq. (
10
) is unbiased, i.e.,

\mathbb{E}[\widehat{\mathrm{Var}(\phi_{i})}]=\mathrm{Var}(\phi_{i})
.

Proof.

Expanding the expectation of Eq. (
10
) and applying the variance decomposition identity yield

\mathbb{E}[\widehat{\mathrm{Var}(\phi_{i})}]=\frac{1}{m-1}\left(m\,\mathrm{Var}(\phi_{i})-m\,\mathrm{Var}\!\left(\widehat{\mathbb{E}[\phi_{i}]}\right)\right).

Substituting Eq. (
9
) gives

\mathbb{E}[\widehat{\mathrm{Var}(\phi_{i})}]=\frac{1}{m-1}\left(m\,\mathrm{Var}(\phi_{i})-\mathrm{Var}(\phi_{i})\right)=\mathrm{Var}(\phi_{i}).

∎

Finally, the variance of this sample variance estimator
[
39
]
is given by

\mathrm{Var}\!\left(\widehat{\mathrm{Var}(\phi_{i})}\right)=\frac{1}{m}\left(\mu_{4}-\frac{m-3}{m-1}\sigma^{4}\right),

(11)

where
\mu_{4}=\mathbb{E}[(\phi_{i}-\mathbb{E}[\phi_{i}])^{4}]
is the fourth central moment and
\sigma^{4}=(\mathrm{Var}(\phi_{i}))^{2}
. In general,
\mu_{4}
depends on high-order joint moments of the utility function and lacks an analytic form, making
\mathrm{Var}(\widehat{\mathrm{Var}(\phi_{i})})
intractable in closed form. Nevertheless, Eq. (
11
) provides the exact finite-sample relationship for evaluating the uncertainty of the variance estimator.

IV
Empirical Estimation Approaches

Closed-form solutions are attainable only when the expected marginal contributions themselves admit closed-form expressions. In practice, however, the data distributions contributed by players may not yield such analytic forms, making closed-form computation of the expected marginal contribution infeasible. This section therefore presents empirical estimation methods designed to handle these general and more realistic scenarios.

IV-A

Empirical Setup and Data Generation

To evaluate the proposed estimation methods for probabilistic Shapley values, we establish a comprehensive empirical setup that specifies the data sources, utility functions, and evaluation metrics used throughout our study. This framework combines both synthetic and real datasets to assess estimation accuracy, robustness, and computational efficiency under controlled and realistic conditions. The following subsections describe the dataset construction procedures, the definition of coalition utility, and the metrics employed to quantify estimation reliability.

IV-A
1
Dataset Preparation

We evaluate the proposed Shapley value estimation methods using both synthetic and real datasets to analyze estimation accuracy, robustness, and computational efficiency. The synthetic dataset enables controlled experiments with known ground truth, while the real dataset demonstrates the practicality of our framework under realistic data heterogeneity.

Synthetic Dataset:

The synthetic dataset is generated from a composite function

f(x)=p(x)+s(x)
,
where
p(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{K}c_{k}x^{k}
represents a polynomial component with coefficients
c_{0}=0.0,c_{1}=0.3,c_{2}=-0.02
,
and
s(x)=A\sin(2\pi\nu x+\phi)+b
adds a periodic term with

A=2.0
,
\nu=0.5
,
\phi=\pi/4
, and
b=3.0
.
We create
N=10
players, each owning a Gaussian sampling distribution

\mathcal{N}(\mu_{i},\sigma_{i}^{2})

with centers
\mu_{i}
uniformly spaced over
[-7,7]

and standard deviations
\sigma_{i}
uniformly drawn from
[0.5,2.5]
.
Each player samples
x
values from its local distribution, clipped to
[-10,10]
,
and obtains labels
y=f(x)
.
A validation set of 1000 uniformly spaced points from the same function is used to evaluate model accuracy.
This design ensures that each player observes a different region of the global function, capturing distributional diversity among participants.

Real Dataset:

We use the Wine Quality dataset
[
10
]
, which contains 4898 white-wine samples with 11 physicochemical attributes and quality scores from 3 to 9.
To simulate heterogeneous players, we partition the training data by the
alcohol
feature, a key determinant of wine quality that varies naturally across regions.
After shuffling with seed 42, we split the data into 70% training (3429 samples) and 30% validation (1469 samples).
Let
\{q_{0},q_{1},\ldots,q_{10}\}
be the deciles of the alcohol values in the training set.
Player
i
receives all samples whose alcohol content lies in
[q_{i-1},q_{i})
.
This yields ten players with partition sizes between 227 and 450.
During each game, we draw subsamples from each player’s partition to introduce sampling variability while preserving their distinct data distributions.
This construction mirrors federated learning scenarios where clients collect data from different subpopulations.

IV-A
2
Utility Function

The utility function quantifies model performance for a coalition’s sampled data rather than the entire dataset.
For both datasets, we define the utility as the inverse of the mean-squared error (MSE) on a fixed validation set.

Synthetic Data Utility:

For each game, player
i
provides a bootstrap sample

\tilde{\mathcal{D}}_{i}=\{(\tilde{x}_{i}^{(k)},\tilde{y}_{i}^{(k)})\}_{k=1}^{m}

drawn from its pool.
Given a coalition
S\subseteq\{1,\ldots,N\}
, the aggregated sample set is

\tilde{\mathcal{D}}_{S}=\bigcup_{i\in S}\tilde{\mathcal{D}}_{i}.

Predictions for a validation point
(x_{\text{val}},y_{\text{val}})
are obtained using nearest-neighbor interpolation:

\hat{y}_{\text{val}}=y,\quad\text{where}\quad(x,y)=\arg\min_{(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\in\tilde{\mathcal{D}}_{S}}|x^{\prime}-x_{\text{val}}|.

The coalition utility is then

v(S;\tilde{\mathcal{D}}_{S})=\frac{1}{1+\mathrm{MSE}(S)}

and

\mathrm{MSE}(S)=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{V}|}\sum_{(x_{\text{val}},y_{\text{val}})\in\mathcal{V}}(\hat{y}_{\text{val}}-y_{\text{val}})^{2}.

This definition ensures that different bootstrap samples of the same coalition yield distinct utilities, capturing sampling uncertainty explicitly.

Real Data Utility:

For the Wine Quality data, each player
j
holds a subset of indices
\mathcal{I}_{j}
from the training set.
In each game, we draw a subsample

\tilde{\mathcal{I}}_{j}\subset\mathcal{I}_{j}

without replacement to form

\tilde{\mathcal{D}}_{S}=\{(\mathbf{X}_{i},y_{i}):i\in\bigcup_{j\in S}\tilde{\mathcal{I}}_{j}\}.

A linear regression model is trained on
\tilde{\mathcal{D}}_{S}

and evaluated on the validation set
\mathcal{V}
:

v(S;\tilde{\mathcal{D}}_{S})=\frac{1}{1+\mathrm{MSE}(S)}

and

\mathrm{MSE}(S)=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{V}|}\sum_{t=1}^{|\mathcal{V}|}(\hat{y}_{\text{val}}^{(t)}-y_{\text{val}}^{(t)})^{2},

where

\hat{y}_{\text{val}}^{(t)}=\boldsymbol{\beta}_{S}^{T}\mathbf{X}_{\text{val}}^{(t)}

and
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{S}
is obtained via ordinary least squares on
\tilde{\mathcal{D}}_{S}
.
Coalitions with fewer than two samples are assigned zero utility.
This sample-dependent formulation directly reflects the probabilistic nature of Shapley value estimation, as different draws yield different coalition performances.

IV-A
3
Evaluation Metrics

To evaluate estimation quality, we run
R
independent replications, each containing
G
games with independently sampled data.
For player
i
, replication
r
produces a sequence

\{\phi_{i}^{(r,g)}\}_{g=1}^{G}
.
Within each replication, we compute

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{r}[\phi_{i}]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{G}\sum_{g=1}^{G}\phi_{i}^{(r,g)},

(12)

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathrm{Var}}_{r}(\phi_{i})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{G-1}\sum_{g=1}^{G}\left(\phi_{i}^{(r,g)}-\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{r}[\phi_{i}]\right)^{2}.

(13)

Across all replications, we summarize estimation reliability by

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}(\widehat{\mathbb{E}}[\phi_{i}])

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{R-1}\sum_{r=1}^{R}\left(\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{r}[\phi_{i}]-\frac{1}{R}\sum_{r^{\prime}}\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{r^{\prime}}[\phi_{i}]\right)^{2},

(14)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}(\widehat{\mathrm{Var}}(\phi_{i}))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{R-1}\sum_{r=1}^{R}\left(\widehat{\mathrm{Var}}_{r}(\phi_{i})-\frac{1}{R}\sum_{r^{\prime}}\widehat{\mathrm{Var}}_{r^{\prime}}(\phi_{i})\right)^{2}.

(15)

Averaging over all
N=10
players yields

\displaystyle\overline{\mathrm{Var}(\widehat{\mathbb{E}}[\phi])}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathrm{Var}(\widehat{\mathbb{E}}[\phi_{i}]),

(16)

\displaystyle\overline{\mathrm{Var}(\widehat{\mathrm{Var}}(\phi))}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathrm{Var}(\widehat{\mathrm{Var}}(\phi_{i})).

(17)

The first metric captures the stability of expected Shapley value estimates, while the second measures reliability in variance estimation.
Lower values of both indicate more consistent and trustworthy estimation under sampling uncertainty.

IV-B

Baseline Method: Sources of Uncertainty

We begin by analyzing the baseline method to understand how different sources of randomness affect Shapley value estimation under probabilistic data distributions. Two major uncertainty sources are considered:

1.

sampling uncertainty, arising from stochastic data draws across games, and

2.

computation uncertainty, introduced by the finite number of Monte Carlo permutations used to approximate coalition values.

We quantify their individual and combined effects by systematically varying the number of games
n_{\text{games}}
and the number of iterations
n_{\text{iter}}
per game.

IV-B

Impact of Computation Uncertainty

Increasing the number of Monte Carlo iterations also reduces variance, particularly at higher game counts. For
\overline{\mathrm{Var}(\widehat{\mathbb{E}}[\phi])}
, increasing
n_{\text{iter}}

to

IV-B
3
Summary

Both sampling and computation uncertainty significantly influence estimation quality, though their effects differ in magnitude. Sampling uncertainty primarily affects the variance of mean estimates and is mitigated most effectively by increasing
n_{\text{games}}
, leading to 94–96% reductions. Computation uncertainty has a stronger influence on the variance-of-variance metric and benefits from larger
n_{\text{iter}}
, achieving 69–99% reductions. When computational resources are limited, allocating the budget to increase the number of games improves the stability of the estimated mean Shapley values. In contrast, obtaining reliable confidence intervals or uncertainty estimates requires sufficient iterations to stabilize the variance estimates. These findings motivate the development of sampling-efficient strategies discussed in later sections.

IV-C

Pooled Method: Efficient Sampling Through Reuse

The baseline method requires fresh samples from each player’s distribution in every game, which becomes costly when data access is costly or restricted. To reduce this cost, we propose the
pooled method
, which separates data access from Monte Carlo estimation. The key idea is that each player first generates a fixed sample pool that represents its distribution. Subsequent games then draw bootstrap samples from these pools, avoiding repeated access to the original distributions.

IV-C
1
Method Description

The pooled method consists of two stages. In the pool construction stage, each player
i
draws
n_{\text{pool}}
samples from its distribution
P_{i}
to form a reusable pool
\mathcal{S}_{i}=\{(x_{j},y_{j})\}_{j=1}^{n_{\text{pool}}}
. In the bootstrap stage, for each game
g
, player
i
draws
n_{\text{boot}}
samples with replacement from
\mathcal{S}_{i}
to form a temporary dataset
\mathcal{B}_{i}^{(g)}
. These bootstrap datasets are then used in all iterations of that game.

For coalition
S\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}
, the pooled data are

\mathcal{B}_{S}^{(g)}=\bigcup_{i\in S}\mathcal{B}_{i}^{(g)},

and the marginal contribution for player
i
in permutation
t
of game
g
is

\Delta_{i}^{(g,t)}=v(S\cup\{i\}\mid\mathcal{B}_{S\cup\{i\}}^{(g)})-v(S\mid\mathcal{B}_{S}^{(g)}).

The method reduces total sampling cost from
O(n_{\text{games}}\cdot n_{\text{boot}}\cdot n)
in the baseline method to
O(n_{\text{pool}}\cdot n)
since the pools are constructed only once.

IV-D

Stratified Pooled Method: Variance-Adaptive Allocation

The pooled method achieves substantial computational efficiency through sample reuse. However, it allocates the same bootstrap size to all players, regardless of the variability of their data distributions. In practice, players contribute different amounts of uncertainty to Shapley value estimation. High-variance players introduce greater sampling noise into coalition evaluations, while low-variance players yield more stable contributions. This heterogeneity suggests that uniform sampling is inefficient. We can improve estimation efficiency by allocating more sampling effort to high-variance players and less to low-variance ones.

We propose the
stratified pooled method
, which extends the pooled approach by computing player-specific bootstrap sizes based on their empirical variance. Inspired by Neyman allocation in stratified sampling
[
40
]
, this method redistributes the same total sampling budget in proportion to player variance. It lowers the overall estimation variance without increasing computational cost. The approach is particularly relevant in data valuation scenarios where players correspond to diverse data sources with different coverage and quality.

IV-D
1
Method Description

The stratified pooled method adds a variance analysis step between pool construction and bootstrap sampling. It proceeds in three phases.

Pool construction:
Each player
i
draws
n_{\text{pool}}
samples from its distribution
P_{i}
to form a reusable pool
\mathcal{S}_{i}=\{(x_{j}^{(i)},y_{j}^{(i)})\}_{j=1}^{n_{\text{pool}}}
, identical to the pooled method.

Variance analysis:
We compute a standardized total variance for each player’s pool to measure its uncertainty. For a pool
\mathcal{S}_{i}
with
d
features, we define

\sigma_{i}^{2}=\sum_{k=1}^{d}\mathrm{Var}\left(\frac{\mathcal{S}_{i}^{(k)}-\mu_{i}^{(k)}}{\hat{\sigma}_{i}^{(k)}}\right),

where
\mathcal{S}_{i}^{(k)}
is the
k
-th feature column, and
\mu_{i}^{(k)}
,
\hat{\sigma}_{i}^{(k)}
are its empirical mean and standard deviation. Standardization ensures scale invariance across heterogeneous features. We normalize variances to
[0,1]
using min–max scaling:

\tilde{\sigma}_{i}^{2}=\frac{\sigma_{i}^{2}-\min_{j}\sigma_{j}^{2}}{\max_{j}\sigma_{j}^{2}-\min_{j}\sigma_{j}^{2}},

which ranks players by relative variability, independent of absolute magnitude.

Stratified bootstrap:
We then assign each player a specific bootstrap size

n_{\text{boot}}^{(i)}=n_{\text{min}}+\tilde{\sigma}_{i}^{2}(n_{\text{max}}-n_{\text{min}}),

where
n_{\text{min}}=\max(1,\lfloor n_{\text{boot}}/2\rfloor)
and
n_{\text{max}}=\max(n_{\text{min}},\lfloor\alpha n_{\text{pool}}\rfloor)
. The parameter
\alpha\in[0.5,1.0]
controls the upper bound of allocation. For each game
g
, we draw
n_{\text{boot}}^{(i)}
samples with replacement from
\mathcal{S}_{i}
to form
\mathcal{B}_{i}^{(g)}
.

Coalition evaluation and Shapley aggregation then proceed as in the pooled method. For each Monte Carlo iteration
t
within game
g
,

\Delta_{i}^{(g,t)}=v(S\cup\{i\}\mid\mathcal{B}_{S\cup\{i\}}^{(g)})-v(S\mid\mathcal{B}_{S}^{(g)}),

where
\mathcal{B}_{S}^{(g)}=\bigcup_{j\in S}\mathcal{B}_{j}^{(g)}
. The overall sampling cost remains approximately the same, since the total bootstrap size satisfies
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\allowbreak n_{\text{boot}}^{(i)}\approx n\cdot n_{\text{boot}}
. The stratified allocation redistributes samples instead of increasing them.

Summary

The stratified pooled method combines the efficiency of pooling with adaptive precision. It reallocates a fixed sampling budget toward players with higher variance, reducing overall estimation variance without added cost. Across datasets, it achieves 70–95% lower variance than the pooled method and over 70% lower than the baseline, confirming that adaptive allocation provides a robust and cost-effective improvement for Shapley value estimation.

V
Related Work

The Shapley value was first introduced by Shapley
[
45
]
as a foundational concept in cooperative game theory, providing a unique characterization of fair value allocation among players. It was later applied to broader domains such as economics and political science
[
46
,
49
]
. More recently, the concept has gained renewed attention in the context of machine learning, particularly as a principled tool for data valuation in collaborative and distributed learning systems
[
16
,
43
,
28
]
. This section reviews related work in two key areas: models of data Shapley and computational methods for estimating Shapley values.

V-A

Models of Data Shapley

Ghorbani and Zou
[
16
]
introduced the concept of
data Shapley
, which quantifies the contribution of each individual training sample to a model’s performance. Their framework treats the dataset as a deterministic collection of points and computes the Shapley value by measuring the marginal impact of each sample on the overall learning objective. This formulation has since become a canonical model for data valuation and has inspired a large body of follow-up work
[
21
,
43
]
. However, it fundamentally assumes a fixed and fully observed dataset, where data contributions are deterministic.

Building on this line of work, Ghorbani
et al.

[
15
]
proposed a
distributional Shapley
framework that extends data Shapley to the level of data distributions, quantifying the contribution of a population of data rather than individual samples. Kwon
et al.

[
25
]
further studied efficient computation and analysis of distributional Shapley values by exploiting the statistical structure of distributions to reduce computational complexity. While these works take an important step toward incorporating uncertainty, they primarily focus on estimating the expectation of the Shapley value and do not characterize variability induced by stochastic data sampling.

Recent work has explored alternative modeling assumptions for data valuation. Liu
et al.

[
31
]
proposed 2D-Shapley, a framework for valuating fragmented data sources that contain partial samples and features. Wang
et al.

[
48
]
introduced in-run data Shapley, which enables targeted attribution for a specific model obtained from a single training run with negligible training overhead. Garrido Lucero
et al.

[
14
]
proposed DU-Shapley, a proxy estimator that leverages structural properties of dataset valuation to improve efficiency. Despite these advances, existing formulations still associate each participant with a single deterministic contribution score, even when data access is inherently stochastic.

More recently, Wu
et al.

[
51
]
studied statistical uncertainty in data Shapley estimation via infinite-order U-statistics, deriving confidence intervals under data distribution shifts. Their analysis treats the Shapley value as fixed and focuses on estimation uncertainty rather than stochastic data contribution. Relatedly, Shapley values under uncertainty have been studied in settings where the value function itself is stochastic, such as Shapley values with uncertain value functions
[
19
]
and stochastic Shapley values for Gaussian process models
[
9
]
. However, these works focus on uncertainty induced by model or utility randomness rather than stochastic data ownership.

V-B

Data Shapley Computation

Computing the exact Shapley value is combinatorially expensive, as it requires evaluating all possible coalitions of players, a problem known to be #P-hard in general
[
11
]
. Consequently, a wide range of approximation methods have been developed, most of which rely on Monte Carlo sampling over permutations or coalitions
[
7
,
36
,
38
,
20
]
. These methods provide unbiased or asymptotically consistent estimates but can suffer from high variance and slow convergence.

Several studies aim to improve the efficiency and statistical reliability of sampling-based Shapley estimation. Ghorbani and Zou
[
16
]
proposed
Truncated Monte Carlo Shapley
and
Gradient Shapley
, which approximate data Shapley values by truncating coalition evaluations or leveraging gradient-based estimators. Jia
et al.

[
21
]
developed KNN-based Shapley estimators that reduce the cost of utility evaluation by exploiting local data geometry. Maleki
et al.

[
36
]
provided non-asymptotic error bounds for sampling-based Shapley approximation, and Illés and Kerényi
[
20
]
proposed ergodic sampling schemes to reduce estimator variance. More recent work formulates Shapley estimation as a stratified sampling problem and derives principled variance reduction techniques
[
53
,
50
]
. Pang
et al.

[
42
]
proposed estimating Shapley values indirectly via a differential matrix of pairwise differences, further reducing estimation variance through least-squares reconstruction.

Other approaches exploit structural properties of the underlying utility function. Luo
et al.

[
33
]
studied data assemblage tasks with binary utilities and derived efficient Shapley computation by decomposing the induced cooperative simple game. For dynamic settings in which data points are added or removed, Zhang
et al.

[
54
]
and Xia
et al.

[
52
]
proposed algorithms that update Shapley values incrementally without recomputing from scratch. A comprehensive overview of Shapley value applications and computation techniques in databases and machine learning is provided by Luo and Pei
[
34
]
.

While these methods significantly improve the computational tractability of Shapley value estimation, they share a common assumption that each participant contributes a fixed dataset. In contrast, our work departs from this deterministic setting by modeling each participant as owning a
probabilistic data distribution
. This shift fundamentally changes the problem formulation: a participant’s contribution becomes a random variable whose distribution depends on the stochastic sampling process. Our framework explicitly estimates both the expectation and variance of these probabilistic Shapley values, enabling uncertainty-aware data valuation in settings where data access is inherently stochastic.

VI
Conclusion

This paper investigates Shapley value estimation in settings where participants contribute probabilistic data distributions rather than fixed datasets. We formalized this stochastic environment and derived unbiased estimators for both the expectation and variance of each player’s Shapley value, providing a principled framework for quantifying not only average contribution but also contribution uncertainty. This probabilistic formulation extends classical data Shapley theory to realistic collaborative environments in which data are sampled dynamically and determinism cannot be assumed.

To operationalize this framework, we developed three Monte Carlo estimation methods that balance accuracy and computational efficiency. The baseline estimator offers an unbiased reference based on fully independent sampling. The pooled estimator achieves comparable accuracy while reusing cached samples, reducing data access by over 97%. Building on these insights, the stratified pooled estimator introduces variance-adaptive allocation, directing larger sampling budgets toward high-variance players and achieving up to 95% reduction in estimation variance without increasing overall sampling cost. Together, these methods constitute a scalable and statistically rigorous toolkit for Shapley value estimation under stochastic data generation.

Our empirical results show that explicitly modeling uncertainty in data contributions yields more stable and interpretable Shapley values, benefiting stakeholders in data markets, privacy-constrained systems, and federated learning. Beyond efficiency gains, our analysis clarifies how sampling and computational uncertainty jointly shape the reliability of Shapley-based valuation, offering guidance for designing robust data-sharing mechanisms.

Future work will extend this framework to federated learning environments with randomized client participation. In such lottery-style systems, only a subset of parties is selected in each round to contribute updates, introducing an additional layer of stochasticity in contribution evaluation. Characterizing Shapley values under this participation uncertainty is a critical next step toward fair and reliable data valuation in large-scale, decentralized learning systems.
