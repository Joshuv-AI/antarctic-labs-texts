Whole‐Exome Sequencing Identifies Novel CPAMD8 Variants in Congenital Cataract and Candidate Variants in Pathologic Myopia
Authors: Qiu‐ling Xie, Qiao Wang, Yanan Liu, Teng Huang, Jin Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPurposeTo report CPAMD8 variants identified by whole‐exome sequencing (WES) in two Chinese families presenting with congenital cataract or pathologic myopia (PM) and to evaluate the evidence supporting their genotype–phenotype relationships.MethodsWES was performed in the probands, followed by Sanger sequencing for variant validation and segregation analysis when DNA samples from family members were available. Candidate variants were interpreted according to ACMG/AMP guidelines.ResultsIn Family 1, the proband with congenital cataract carried compound heterozygous CPAMD8 variants, including a likely pathogenic frameshift variant (c.4570delA: p.M1524Cfs*32) and a missense variant of uncertain significance (c.1717G>A: p.D573N). Sanger sequencing confirmed that the two variants were inherited from unaffected parents and were located in trans. In Family 2, the proband with PM, bilateral cataract, and posterior staphyloma carried a heterozygous splice‐site variant (c.487‐2A>T), classified as likely pathogenic, and an additional missense variant of uncertain significance (c.5342C>G: p.P1781R). However, segregation analysis in other family members and functional validation of the predicted splicing effect were not available.ConclusionsThese findings support a CPAMD8‐related congenital cataract phenotype in Family 1 and may inform genetic counseling for affected families. The CPAMD8 variants identified in Family 2 should be interpreted as candidate findings, as their causal relevance to PM remains unconfirmed in the absence of segregation and functional evidence.

Introduction

Cataract is a leading cause of blindness worldwide and results from loss of transparency of the crystalline lens. Congenital cataract, which is usually detected at birth or during infancy, is an important cause of visual impairment in children. Approximately 20,000–40,000 children are born with congenital cataract each year worldwide, and more than 18% of cases have a positive family history (Wirth et al. 2002; Foster et al. 1997). It is estimated that 200,000 children worldwide are blind from cataract (Chan et al. 2012). Clarifying the genetic basis of congenital cataract may improve molecular diagnosis, genetic counseling, and clinical management.

Pathologic myopia (PM) is another major cause of visual impairment worldwide (Wong et al. 2014). It is distinct from high myopia (HM), which primarily refers to a high degree of refractive error without necessarily involving structural fundus abnormalities (Ohno‐Matsui 2025). According to the International Myopia Institute (IMI), PM is defined by characteristic posterior segment complications, such as posterior staphyloma or myopic maculopathy at least at the level of diffuse choroidal atrophy (Ohno‐Matsui et al. 2021). The global prevalence of PM is estimated to be up to 3%, with marked ethnic variation: approximately 1%–3% of Asians and about 1% of Caucasians are affected. In Asia, PM is a leading cause of irreversible blindness in regions such as Taiwan, Japan, and China (Hsu et al. 2004; Iwase et al. 2006; Xu et al. 2006). In parallel with environmental and lifestyle changes, the prevalence of myopia and high myopia has increased rapidly, and the burden of high myopia closely follows the overall prevalence of myopia (Raval et al. 2022).

The human CPAMD8 gene, complement C3 and PZP‐like alpha‐2‐macroglobulin domain‐containing 8 (OMIM 608841), encodes a member of the A2M/C3 protein family involved in immune regulation and tissue homeostasis (Li et al. 2004). Previous studies have associated CPAMD8 variants with anterior segment dysgenesis, ectopia lentis, Morgagnian cataract, and glaucoma‐related phenotypes, including childhood and juvenile open‐angle glaucoma (Wiggs 2020; Apam‐Garduño et al. 2026; Hollmann et al. 2017). These findings suggest that CPAMD8 is involved in ocular development and may contribute to a range of anterior segment and lens‐related phenotypes.

In this study, we performed clinical and genetic evaluations in one family with congenital cataract and one family with PM. Whole‐exome sequencing (WES), Sanger sequencing, and bioinformatic analyses were used to identify and interpret CPAMD8 variants. We aimed to characterize the genotype–phenotype findings in these two families, support the role of CPAMD8 in congenital cataract, and report candidate CPAMD8 variants in a proband with PM.

Materials and Methods

Subjects and Sample Collection

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Eye Hospital of Wenzhou Medical University (approval No. 2021‐239‐k‐209) and conducted in accordance with the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. All patients and available family members underwent comprehensive ophthalmic and general physical examinations performed by experienced ophthalmologists. Peripheral blood or oral mucosal samples were collected from probands and their family members for subsequent genetic analyses. Genomic DNA was extracted using the MagMAX DNA Multi‐Sample Ultra 2.0 Kit (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Norway) or the QIAGEN Blood DNA Extraction Kit (QIAGEN, Germany), according to the manufacturers' instructions.

WES and Bioinformatic Analysis

WES was performed in the probands only, while available family members were subsequently examined by Sanger sequencing for variant validation and segregation analysis when DNA samples were available. Comprehensive WES was performed with the Twist Human Core Exome Kit (Twist Bioscience, USA), followed by sequencing on the NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). Detailed protocols for next‐generation sequencing and data processing, including variant annotation, pathogenicity assessment, in silico prediction, and population frequency filtering, have been described previously (Lin et al. 2022; Huang et al. 2018).

Briefly, short‐read sequencing data were aligned to the human reference genome hg19 using the Burrows–Wheeler Aligner (BWA) (Li and Durbin 2010). Variant calling was performed using the Genome Analysis Toolkit (GATK) according to GATK best‐practices recommendations (DePristo et al. 2011). ANNOVAR (Wang et al. 2010) was used for variant annotation. Population frequencies of the identified variants were assessed using the Genome Aggregation Database (gnomAD v4.1.0) (Gudmundsson et al. 2022), the Exome Aggregation Consortium (ExAC) (Karczewski et al. 2017), the MAGIC cohort of PSI Genomics Co. (Su et al. 2023), and the China Metabolic Analytics Project (ChinaMAP) database (Cao et al. 2020). Candidate variants were prioritized based on allele frequency, predicted functional consequence, inheritance pattern, and consistency with the ocular phenotype. Candidate variants were classified according to the 2015 American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics/Association for Molecular Pathology (ACMG/AMP) guidelines and relevant ClinGen Sequence Variant Interpretation recommendations (Richards et al. 2015; Abou Tayoun et al. 2018; Biesecker and Harrison 2018; Ghosh et al. 2018).

Sanger Sequencing

Candidate variants identified by WES were confirmed by Sanger sequencing when validation samples were available. Primer pairs targeting the regions of interest were designed, and polymerase chain reaction (PCR) was performed to amplify the corresponding DNA fragments. Sanger sequencing was performed using an ABI 3730xl DNA Analyzer (Applied Biosystems, USA). The resulting sequences were aligned with the reference sequence using MutationMapper software (Vohra and Biggin 2013). When DNA samples from family members were available, Sanger sequencing was also used for familial segregation analysis.

Results

Clinical Evaluation

Family 1

The proband (II:2) was a 4‐year‐old boy who presented to the Eye Hospital of Wenzhou Medical University with bilateral lens opacities. His father (I:1), mother (I:2), and older brother (II:1) had no ocular abnormalities (Figure 1A). He was born at term by uncomplicated vaginal delivery, with normal birth weight and length. There was no parental consanguinity. His mother reported no history of infection, medication use, or radiation exposure during pregnancy or the perinatal period.

At 4 years of age, the proband was diagnosed with congenital cataract, bilateral lower eyelid entropion, and trichiasis. Anterior segment slit‐lamp examination demonstrated bilateral posterior polar lens opacities. Fundus photography showed optic disc cupping, with a cup‐to‐disc ratio of approximately 0.7 (Figure 2A). Retinal OCT showed no significant retinal abnormalities (Figure 2B). Axial lengths were 25.66 mm in the right eye (OD) and 24.95 mm in the left eye (OS). Anterior chamber depths were 4.45 mm OD and 4.49 mm OS, and lens thicknesses were 3.69 mm OD and 3.77 mm OS. Mean keratometry values were 42.1 D OD and 41.6 D OS. Posterior segment B‐scan ultrasonography showed vitreous opacities and abnormal optic disc cupping in both eyes (Figure 2C). Intraocular pressure was within normal limits, and no obvious iris abnormalities were observed. The proband underwent bilateral cataract surgery at our hospital at 4 years of age. On postoperative day 1, best‐corrected visual acuity was 0.5 OD and 0.25 OS in decimal notation.

Family 2

Family 2 was from Zhejiang Province in southeastern China. In this pedigree, the proband had PM, and her son had early‐onset high myopia (Figure 1B). The proband (II:1) was a 57‐year‐old woman who reported painless, progressive bilateral visual loss. Corneal topography showed maximum anterior corneal curvatures of 45.8 D in the right eye and 46.4 D in the left eye. Central corneal thicknesses were 521 μm OD and 527 μm OS. Axial lengths were 31.84 mm OD and 32.97 mm OS. The pupils were displaced nasally (Figure 3A), and CASIA2 anterior segment OCT demonstrated posterior bowing of the iris in both eyes, without transillumination defects (Figure 3C). B‐scan ultrasonography showed vitreous opacities in both eyes (Figure 3B). Fundus examination revealed marked posterior staphyloma, tessellated fundus, attenuated retinal vessels, mottled atrophy, and severe peripapillary atrophy (Figure 3D). Her son (III:1) had been diagnosed with high myopia in early childhood, with a spherical equivalent of approximately −6.0 D in both eyes at around 3 years of age; however, neither detailed ophthalmic examination nor CPAMD8 Sanger testing was available for him.

Genetic Analysis

In Family 1, WES identified compound heterozygous CPAMD8 variants in the proband with congenital cataract: c.4570delA (p.M1524Cfs*32) in exon 35 and c.1717G>A (p.D573N) in exon 15 (Figure 1C, Table 1). The frameshift variant c.4570delA was classified as likely pathogenic according to ACMG/AMP criteria (Richards et al. 2015) based on PVS1 and PM2_Supporting. The missense variant c.1717G>A was classified as a variant of uncertain significance based on PM2_Supporting, PM3, and PP3. Sanger sequencing confirmed that c.4570delA was inherited from the mother and c.1717G>A from the father, indicating that the two variants were located in trans. No clinically relevant mitochondrial genome variants, copy‐number variants, structural variants, or short tandem repeat expansions were reported.

In Family 2, WES of the proband identified two heterozygous CPAMD8 variants: a splice‐site variant, c.487‐2A>T, located in intron 5, and a missense variant, c.5342C>G (p.P1781R), located in exon 41 (Figure 1C, Table 1). The c.487‐2A>T variant was classified as likely pathogenic according to ACMG/AMP criteria based on PVS1 and PM2 and was absent from the Shenzhou Genome Database, ExAC, the 1000 Genomes Project, and gnomAD. The c.5342C>G (p.P1781R) variant was classified as a variant of uncertain significance, with reported allele frequencies of 0.00941993 in the Shenzhou Genome Database, 0.00051778 in ExAC, 0.00159744 in the 1000 Genomes Project, and 0.0098646 in gnomAD. No segregation analysis was available for other family members, and the phase of the two heterozygous variants could not be determined. Therefore, the variants identified in Family 2 were interpreted as candidate findings with unconfirmed causal relevance to PM.

Discussion

In this study, we describe two Chinese families with ocular phenotypes involving congenital cataract or PM and report the CPAMD8 variants identified by WES. In Family 1, the proband with congenital cataract carried compound heterozygous CPAMD8 variants: a likely pathogenic frameshift variant and a rare missense variant of uncertain significance. The two variants were inherited in trans from unaffected parents, supporting a CPAMD8‐related congenital cataract phenotype in this family. In Family 2, two heterozygous CPAMD8 variants were identified in a proband with PM, bilateral cataract, and posterior staphyloma. Because segregation analysis and functional validation were not available, these variants should be considered candidate variants, and causality cannot be established from the available evidence.

The segregation pattern in Family 1 was consistent with autosomal recessive CPAMD8‐related ocular disease. The proband inherited the c.4570delA variant from his mother and the c.1717G>A variant from his father. The frameshift variant c.4570delA was classified as likely pathogenic because it is predicted to cause loss of function. Although c.1717G>A was classified as a variant of uncertain significance, it was rare in population databases, predicted to be deleterious by in silico tools, and detected in trans with a likely pathogenic variant. The absence of ocular abnormalities in heterozygous relatives further supports recessive inheritance. Together, these results suggest that the compound heterozygous CPAMD8 variants are likely associated with the congenital cataract phenotype in the proband (Table 1).

The genetic evidence in Family 2 was less conclusive. Although the splice‐site variant c.487‐2A>T was classified as likely pathogenic according to ACMG/AMP criteria, segregation testing could not be performed for the proband's son, who had early‐onset high myopia, and the phase of the two heterozygous CPAMD8 variants could not be determined. Moreover, the predicted splicing effect was not confirmed by transcript analysis or a minigene assay. The additional missense variant c.5342C>G (p.P1781R) was classified as a variant of uncertain significance and had a relatively high reported allele frequency in population databases. Therefore, the CPAMD8 variants identified in Family 2 should be interpreted as candidate variants without sufficient evidence to establish a causal relationship with PM.

The CPAMD8 gene has been implicated in ocular developmental disorders, particularly anterior segment dysgenesis and glaucoma (Siggs et al. 2020). Previous studies have reported biallelic CPAMD8 variants in childhood glaucoma (Li et al. 2022) and other ocular phenotypes, including ectopia lentis and Morgagnian cataract (Cheong et al. 2016). The Family 1 findings are consistent with the established role of CPAMD8 in congenital cataract and related ocular developmental phenotypes.

The CPAMD8 gene is located on chromosome 19p13.11 and encodes a protein belonging to the A2M/C3 protein family (Hollmann et al. 2017). Members of this family participate in immune regulation and interact with cytokines and growth factors (Pan et al. 2025). Growth factor signaling has also been implicated in ocular development and myopia progression (Ku et al. 2024). Nevertheless, the mechanistic link between CPAMD8 variants and PM remains speculative. Further experimental evidence is needed to determine whether altered CPAMD8 function affects scleral remodeling, extracellular matrix homeostasis, or anterior segment development during myopic eye growth.

These findings support considering the inclusion of CPAMD8 in gene panels for congenital cataract and related ocular developmental disorders. Future studies are needed to clarify how altered CPAMD8 function may affect lens homeostasis and whether growth factor‐related pathways contribute to cataract development or progression.

This study has several limitations. First, the study was limited by the inclusion of only two families. Second, segregation analysis could not be completed in Family 2 because the proband and other family members were unavailable for further follow‐up. Third, the splice‐altering effect of c.487‐2A>T was predicted but not functionally validated by transcript analysis or a minigene assay. Fourth, the inheritance model in Family 2 remains uncertain because the phase of the two heterozygous variants could not be determined. Finally, protein structure modeling was not used for variant interpretation because the structural consequences of the frameshift and splice‐site variants could not be reliably inferred without experimental or transcript‐level validation. Accordingly, the potential relationship between CPAMD8 and PM should be interpreted cautiously.

In conclusion, this study identified compound heterozygous CPAMD8 variants in a child with congenital cataract and additional candidate CPAMD8 variants in a proband with PM, bilateral cataract, and posterior staphyloma. The findings support a CPAMD8‐related congenital cataract phenotype in Family 1 and may inform genetic counseling for affected families. In contrast, the Family 2 findings should be interpreted cautiously, as their causal relevance to PM remains unconfirmed in the absence of segregation and functional evidence.
