DREAMS: Density Functional Theory Based Research Engine for Agentic Materials Simulation
Ziqi Wang, Hongshuo Huang, Hancheng Zhao, Changwen Xu, Shang Zhu, Jan Janssen, Venkatasubramanian Viswanathan

Abstract
Materials discovery relies on high-throughput, high-fidelity simulation techniques such as Density Functional Theory (DFT), which require years of training, extensive parameter fine-tuning and systematic error handling. To address these challenges, we introduce the DFT-based Research Engine for Agentic Materials Screening (DREAMS), a hierarchical, multi-agent framework for DFT simulation that combines a central Large Language Model (LLM) planner agent with domain-specific LLM agents for atomistic structure generation, systematic DFT convergence testing, High-Performance Computing (HPC) scheduling, and error handling. In addition, a shared canvas helps the LLM agents to structure their discussions, preserve context and prevent hallucination. We validate DREAMS capabilities on the Sol27LC lattice-constant benchmark, achieving average errors below 1% compared to the results of human DFT experts. Furthermore, we apply DREAMS to the long-standing CO/Pt(111) adsorption puzzle, demonstrating its long-term and complex problem-solving capabilities. The framework again reproduces expert-level literature adsorption-energy differences. Finally, DREAMS is employed to quantify functional-driven uncertainties with Bayesian ensemble sampling, confirming the Face Centered Cubic (FCC)-site preference at the Generalized Gradient Approximation (GGA) DFT level. In conclusion, DREAMS approaches L3-level automation - autonomous exploration of a defined design space - and significantly reduces the reliance on human expertise and intervention, offering a scalable path toward democratized, high-throughput, high-fidelity computational materials discovery.

Introduction

Materials discovery is a complex, multidimensional challenge that requires significant time, expertise, training, and computational resources. Traditional trial-and-error experiments are resource-intensive and inherently limited in throughput, exploring only a small fraction of candidates
Pant et al. 2023
. In contrast, computational screening methods—ranging from physics-based simulations to data-driven methods—offer a powerful alternative to efficiently screening large materials libraries. In this context, physics-based simulations provide fundamental insights into materials behavior through computational modeling. Density Functional Theory (DFT) has become the workhorse of computational materials science, enabling accurate predictions of electronic, structural, and thermodynamic properties at affordable computational cost
Menon et al. 2024
.
DFT-based computational screening has successfully identified promising candidates for energy materials, catalysts, and structural applications
Ertekin and Schiller 2020
;
Jain et al. 2013
. However, setting up a DFT-based high-fidelity, high-throughput workflow typically requires substantial human expertise and intervention, which slows down the discovery process
Kavalsky et al. 2023
and introduces cognitive biases that limit the exploration of the design space
Santiago et al. 2023
. To address these limitations, there is growing interest in autonomous materials screening systems that can minimize the need for human intervention
Santiago et al. 2023
.
In analogy to self-driving cars and automated chemical design
On-Road Automated Driving Committee(2021) ORAD
;
Goldman et al. 2022
, we define six different levels of automation in computational materials discovery as shown in

Current automation efforts in materials science focus primarily on developing computational pipelines, a pre-defined execution of specific tasks. These pipelines are commonly constructed and executed with workflow management systems (WfMS)
Curtarolo et al. 2012
;

;

;
Kavalsky et al. 2024
.
The WfMSs enable the rapid prototyping and up-scaling of reproducible computational workflows, still they are typically hard-coded for specific tasks, limiting them to level 1 automation. The predefined nature of their workflows means they cannot effectively handle unexpected errors, without extensive prior preparation or adaptation to screen for novel material properties.

Large language model (LLM) agents provide a potential solution to this challenge, as LLM agents combine the reasoning capabilities of LLMs with specialized tools to perform complex domain-specific tasks
Zimmermann et al. 2025
. With their ability to reason about problems and dynamically respond to exceptions and adjustments in the research question, LLM agents can achieve L2+ level automation. ReseachAgent
Baek et al. 2024
and SciAgent
Ghafarollahi and Buehler 2024
show the effectiveness of high-quality research ideas and hypothesis generation with LLM agents. Furthermore, Coscientist
Song et al. 2025
, El Agente
Zou et al. 2025
, ChemCrow
M. Bran et al. 2024
, and MDCrow
Campbell et al. 2025
showcase the potential of LLM agents in accelerating research across diverse domains. Finally, the LangSim project
Zimmermann et al. 2025
shows that LLM agents can perform complex challenges including the design of inverse materials by mapping desired properties to material configurations. However, LLM agents face significant challenges in scientific applications, particularly the fidelity of their results, their tendency to hallucinate, and their ability to handle complex scientific challenges: (i)
Most current work is focused on low-fidelity models, leaving high-fidelity calculations largely unexplored. Density Functional Theory (DFT) is widely used as a high-fidelity model in materials science, but few LLM agent frameworks incorporate DFT capabilities. Even when DFT is included, calculation parameters are rarely optimized, potentially leading to inaccurate results
M. Bran et al. 2024
;
Ghafarollahi and Buehler 2025
;
Campbell et al. 2025
.
(ii) LLMs are prone to hallucination, providing false positive answers or accepting approximately correct results without proper validation. This decreases result accuracy and is particularly problematic in scientific applications, where correctness and reliability are paramount and small errors can cascade into significant inaccuracies. This limitation also prevents the LLM agents from successfully executing long-horizon or complex tasks
Wang et al. 2023
;
Kandpal et al. 2023
.
(iii) Although LLMs provide some level of automation, their error handling capabilities are typically limited to the tools available to them. Without carefully designed error-handling protocols, LLM agents can only address trivial errors, such as file-not-found exceptions or type errors. Finally, complex convergence errors, which frequently challenge DFT practitioners, remain beyond the capabilities of existing frameworks
Kudin and Scuseria 2007
;
Janssen et al. 2024
.

Herein, we introduce the DFT-based Research Engine for Agentic Materials Screening (DREAMS) to address these challenges.
As shown in
Fig.

1
, it is implemented as a hierarchical, multi-level LLM agent team with dynamic planning capabilities. This architecture reduces the cognitive load of the LLM and helps mitigate hallucination by distributing tasks among the specialized LLM agents. These planning capabilities enable complex task handling and adaptation to unexpected situations and changing research questions. Furthermore, structure creation tools ensure calculations are performed on physically valid systems and a shared information dashboard, canvas, maintains key information accessible to all LLM agents, reducing communication-related hallucinations and enabling complex multi-step workflows. These tools, accessible to all involved LLM agents, are carefully designed to reduce hallucination and provide guidance on error handling. Finally, a dedicated convergence error handling LLM agent further boosts the system’s robustness to computational challenges encountered in DFT calculations.

Figure 1
:
DFT based Research Engine for Agentic Materials Screening (DREAMS) combines: (a) Planning supervisor: generates and updates task plans based on research objectives and real-time progress, and assigns tasks to the appropriate worker agents. (b) DFT Agent: processes the computation pipeline, including structure generation, optimal DFT parameters determination, calculation script generation, resolving convergence issues, and other related tasks. (c) HPC Agent: allocates computational resources, submits simulations to HPC, monitors their execution, and obtains the corresponding output files. (d) Convergence Agent: a child agent of the DFT agent, responsible for giving suggestions on how to fix convergence issues of a certain job based on its input, output, and error file. (e) HPC cluster, where the HPC agent can schedule, monitor, and retrieve simulations. (f) Canvas: a shared memory system across agents, tools, and users for structured and time-invariant data sharing.

To highlight the general applicability of this approach it is tested on three DFT benchmarks. (1) The capabilities of DREAMS are demonstrated in the Sol27LC dataset
Wellendorff et al. 2012
of bulk material systems, which contains 27 elemental crystals with varying crystal structures. Here, DREAMS achieves human expert level accuracy in lattice constant calculation. (2) We challenge DREAMS on the catalyst absorption problem, the famous “CO/Pt(111) puzzle”
Feibelman et al. 2001
. It is chosen due to its wide industrial interests
Allian et al. 2011
and yet computationally difficult and heavily debated
Feibelman et al. 2001
;
Grinberg et al. 2002
;
Janthon et al. 2017
due to its sensitivity to calculation settings. DREAMS manifests human-expert level ability in this puzzle and concludes the same preference site compared to human experts in the literature
Feibelman et al. 2001
;
K. G et al. 2019
;
Janthon et al. 2017
. (3) DREAMS analyzes the uncertainty caused by the choice of exchange-correlation functional using Bayesian statistics
Wellendorff et al. 2012
. We confirmed the validity of the results calculated by DREAMS by performing bond length and charge transfer analysis.

The results of our three benchmarks demonstrate that DREAMS can handle high-level objectives, technical errors and different simulation objectives, approaching L3-level automation—a significant step towards automated materials discovery that accelerates the identification of novel materials for energy storage, catalysis, and other applications.

Problem Statement

The current challenges in applying LLM agents in scientific research are (1) lack of access to high-fidelity experiments and simulation
M. Bran et al. 2024
;
Ghafarollahi and Buehler 2025
;
Campbell et al. 2025
, (2) superficial error-handling without in-depth analysis due to either lack of tools or limited reasoning capability
Zhang et al. 2024
;
Ren et al. 2025
, and (3) the tendency of hallucination and loss of context
Dhuliawala et al. 2023
;
Ji et al. 2023
;
Ge et al. 2025
.
To overcome these challenges,
we designed DREAMS: DFT based Research Engine for Agentic Materials Screening, and the detailed explanation on DREAMS’ architecture can be found in the Method section. We demonstrate the effectiveness of DREAMS in scientific problem-solving by presenting results from three distinct challenges: the lattice constant prediction challenge (Sol27LC benchmark) , the CO adsorption on Pt(111) surfaces challenge (CO/Pt(111)) and finally the quantification of the uncertainty caused by the choice of exchange-correlation functional. Together, these challenges showcase DREAMS’s ability to autonomously navigate both routine computational tasks and complex research challenges, i.e., with limited to no human intervention being required.

Before the submission of all production calculations, DREAMS realizes that the calculation script for the isolated CO molecule is missing. The supervisor LLM agent modifies the plan again to create extra steps for the CO calculation script generation. After the missing script is generated, the HPC LLM agent again allocates computing resources and submits the production calculations to the HPC cluster. However, seven out of eight jobs failed.
Since the production stage requires fully converged results, the planning supervisor revises the plan and prompts the DFT LLM agent to analyze the failures. By calling the convergence LLM agent, the DFT LLM agent obtains suggestions for parameter adjustments and updates the scripts accordingly.

Exchange Correlation Functional Uncertainty

Upon demonstrating DREAMS’s capability of reproducing human expert level high-fideltity DFT simulation results on the CO/Pt(111) puzzle problem, the scope of the challenge was extended. A natural third challenge from these results is the sensitivity of the adsorption site preference to the specific choice of exchange correlation functional at the same level of theory (GGA). To quantify this uncertainty, DREAMS was asked to perform Bayesian ensemble sampling with van der Waals correction (BEEF-vdW)
Wellendorff et al. 2012
and the distribution of possible
\Delta BE

Fig.

5

\Delta BE
, indicating that, even when considering uncertainty, the FCC site remains more energetically favorable than the ontop site at the GGA level. These results are consistent with our earlier findings obtained using the PBE and LDA functionals, as well as with previous literature
Feibelman et al. 2001
. We therefore conclude that the CO adsorption behavior on the Pt(111) surface is unlikely to fully match experimental observations under the current GGA-level approximations.

As for the discrepancies, some possible factors are identified. First, the convergence threshold in scf calculations used by DREAMS was higher (1
\times
10
-6
Ry) than that used by the human expert (1
\times
10
-7
Ry), despite the lower threshold being recommended in Quantum ESPRESSO for structure relaxation. Second, although both DREAMS and the human expert employed the same smearing method, DREAMS set a higher smearing width of 0.02 Ry compared to the human expert’s setting of 0.01 Ry. This larger smearing width introduces further deviations in the computed adsorption energies. Together, these differences could explain the minor shift observed in DREAMS results relative to the human expert calculations.

With the adsorbed configurations explored by DREAMS, we can provide some insight for the CO/Pt(111) puzzle. We perform Bader charge analysis and bond length analysis, and the results are shown in
Table

5
. Recall that DREAMS predicts that FCC site is more energetically favorable than ontop sites under all three functionals used in this work. We observe that for all adsorbed CO, the C-O bond length, i.e.
d_{C-O}
, increases compared to the pristine CO molecule. The reason for CO bond length increase lies in the electron transfer from the Pt surface to CO molecule. Upon adsorption, free electrons in Pt metal partially transfer to C-O bond antibonding
\pi^{*}
orbitals, effectively decreasing the bond order of the C-O bond, leading to a bond length increase. In all 3 functionals, the FCC site probes 0.15-0.18
e^{-}
more electron transfer than the ontop site (charge density plots are available in Supplementary Information S-6), and consequently the C-O bond length is always longer on the FCC site. The larger degree of electron transfer indicates enhanced CO-Pt interaction via electron sharing, which explains why CO adsorption is more favored on the FCC site
K. G et al. 2019
.
We would like to point out that the electron transfer from transition metal surface to CO has actually been studied as early as 1964
Blyholder 1964
. The mechanism observed in this work, namely the electron donation from Pt to CO, has been proposed as the back donation mechanism
Blyholder 1964

Discussion

DREAMS showcases the potential of large language model (LLM) agents in addressing scientific challenges in computational materials science. Built upon a hierarchical multi-agent architecture with structured planning, tool integration, and a shared memory system, DREAMS is capable of conducting high-fidelity DFT simulations at an automation level nearing L3, while overcoming key limitations of existing LLM frameworks, such as hallucination, limited context retention, and insufficient error handling.

The validation of our approach using the Sol27LC benchmark dataset confirms the reliability and accuracy of DREAMS for high-fidelity DFT calculations. When applied to the CO/Pt(111) system, it successfully performed comprehensive adsorption energy calculations across multiple configurations, demonstrating the potential of LLM agents to fit in the high-fidelity material discovery loop. On the exchange-correlation functional uncertainty benchmark, DREAMS employs Bayesian ensemble sampling with BEEF-vdW to quantify functional-driven uncertainty, confirming FCC-site preference remains robust across the ensemble, providing insights into why FCC sites are preferred, and reflecting the impact of DFT parameters on calculation results.

Key innovations in our approach include: (1) carefully designed tools that constrain the LLM agents behavior without restricting its capabilities, (2) a sophisticated planning supervisor LLM agent that decomposes complex research challenges into manageable tasks, (3) a shared canvas structure that supports robust multi-agent communication, and (4) systematic convergence testing procedures that ensure computational accuracy.
This work not only contributes to resolving a specific puzzle in surface chemistry but also establishes a generalizable framework for applying LLM agents in computational materials science and chemistry. Future work could extend this approach to other materials science challenges and incorporate additional specialized LLM agents with different expertise. We find immense potential of agentic workflows in accelerating scientific discovery by providing mechanistic insights and identifying overlooked physical factors in computational modeling, opening new avenues for AI-assisted automated research.

Methods

DREAMS (DFT based Research Engine for Agentic Materials Screening) is implemented as a hierarchical multi-agent system using the Claude 3.7 Sonnet (20250219)
Anthropic 2025
API to provide large language model (LLM) capabilities, constructing a hierarchical agentic workflow facilitated by the LangGraph
LangChain Inc. 2024
framework and applying it to multiple DFT challenges. For the initial release, the system features three major agents: a planning supervisor LLM agent and two worker LLM agents, namely a DFT LLM agent and an HPC LLM agent. Each of the worker LLM agents are built as ReAct Agent
Yao et al. 2022
and incorporate various tools for execution. These worker LLM agents have reasoning ability to dynamically adapt to changing task requirements as well as handle exceptions, while actions allow them to interface with built-in functions as tools. Their system prompts are formatted into four sections:
\left<Role\right>
,
\left<Objective\right>
,
\left<Instruction\right>
, and
\left<Requirement\right>
. An example is provided in the Supplementary Information S-7 to S-8. Based on a given prompt, these LLM agents can complete specific tasks by using the tools available to them. DREAMS’s tools are designed to balance scientific flexibility with deterministic behavior, minimizing opportunities for hallucinated outputs. Unlike traditional hard-coded functions or workflows, each of DREAMS tools is implemented as an agentic tool with a built-in LLM interface, enabling contextual understanding while enforcing strict scientific constraints. The LLM tools incorporate rigorous validation checks and produce informative error messages to facilitate LLM agent self-correction. Finally, a canvas is provided and shared among all worker LLM agents, tools, and users to mitigate the hallucination challenge. This hierarchical approach was chosen for its flexibility and ability to manage complex interactions among multiple LLM agents, as well as its extendability.

Dynamic Planning

The planning LLM supervisor agent plays a central role in orchestrating the overall workflow. Unlike simple task schedulers, the supervisor LLM agent decomposes complex research objectives into manageable subtasks tailored to each worker LLM agent’s capabilities. It operates on a dictionary containing five fields:
input
,
plan
,
past steps
,
response
, and
next
. The
input
field stores the user-provided objective. The
plan
holds the current plan of actions. The
past steps
field records summaries of completed steps. The
response
contains the final answer generated by the planning supervisor LLM agent, and
next
specifies the next LLM agent to be called. At the beginning, only the
input
field is populated with the user’s objective. The planning supervisor LLM agent then breaks down the objective into smaller, well-defined tasks based on the capabilities and constraints of available worker LLM agents. It writes these tasks into the
plan
, selects the next LLM agent to act, and updates the
next
field accordingly. Once a worker LLM agent completes a task, its response is summarized and recorded in the
past steps
field. The planning supervisor LLM agent then revises the
plan
based on three components: (1) the original research objective, (2) the current state of the plan, and (3) the history of completed steps. This process is repeated iteratively. Once the objective is fully addressed, the supervisor LLM agent writes the final result to the
response
field, terminates the workflow, and returns the result to the user. This hierarchical planning strategy reduces the cognitive load on individual LLM agents and mitigates context window overflow by narrowing the active context to only the relevant task history. As a result, LLM agents are less likely to hallucinate during long-running workflows, and the example of which can be found under the result section.

DFT LLM Agent

The DFT LLM Agent functions as a specialized worker LLM agent with computational chemistry expertise focused on high-fidelity density functional theory (DFT) calculations. This LLM agent is responsible for managing the scientific aspects of electronic structure calculations, including the construction of atomistic models, optimization of computational parameters, and analysis of calculation results. The DFT LLM agent’s core functions include: (1) generating atomistic structures such as bulk crystals, surfaces, and adsorbate configurations; (2) determining suitable DFT convergence parameters through systematic convergence testing; (3) preparing input files for quantum chemistry software; and (4) analyzing outputs and extract physical properties, including lattice constants and adsorption energies. To perform these tasks, the DFT LLM agent is equipped with a suite of tools, each designed for a specific purpose. The LLM agent selects tools based on their documented capabilities. Further details of these tools are provided in the Supplementary Information S-12 to S-18.

The tools of the DFT LLM agent are interfaced through established frameworks such as the Atomic Simulation Environment (ASE)
Larsen et al. 2017
, preventing unsafe operations like direct file manipulation, which can otherwise lead to invalid structures or runtime failures.
Each tool maintains explicit input-output mappings and implements comprehensive error handling. For example, the adsorption energy tool requires exactly three DFT output files (clean slab, isolated adsorbate, and adsorbate-on-slab) and applies standard thermodynamic formulas to compute results. This architecture prevents the use of hallucinated parameters or unphysical equations, ensuring scientific correctness.
To further reduce complexity and redundancy, we developed a batch modification tool for convergence testing, enabling the rapid generation of Quantum ESPRESSO (QE)
Giannozzi et al. 2009
scripts based on a template. This optimization reduces the complexity of script generation from
O(N)
to
O(1)
by modifying only the specified convergence parameters while preserving the remainder of the input, significantly lowering the risk of inconsistencies or human error.

Atomistic Structure Generation

In the benchmarking experiment on the CO/Pt(111) system, we utilize the AutoCat library
Kavalsky et al. 2024
as a computational framework for generating and analyzing adsorption structures. Compared to simply letting the LLM decide the atomistic structures, using AutoCat to control the structural degrees of freedom ensures that the structures to be simulated are physically meaningful. For example, DREAMS can create structures with different adsorbate sites, surface slab sizes, vacuum spacing, adsorbate orientations, coverage patterns, and adsorption heights, and we are confident that the structures are physically meaningful, as illustrated in
Fig.

5
(a).

Implementing domain knowledge through AutoCat also avoids redundant configurations. For example, for Pt with FCC crystal structure, the (111) surface has only 4 distinct adsorption sites, namely the ontop, bridge, FCC and HCP sites. However, if such symmetry constraints are not known in advance, a grid-search with a 0.1Å spacing would result in 121 possible adsorption sites, which is almost 30 times the computation cost.

In conclusion, AutoCat abstracts these low-level configurational operations behind a structured API, allowing LLM agents to invoke high-level actions such as “generate FCC adsorption structures with all relevant CO orientations” without having to reason about individual atomic coordinates or manually define geometry constraints. The integration of such carefully designed tool thus enables reliable and efficient exploration of a chemically rich configuration space that would otherwise be prone to failure or hallucination if handled entirely at the LLM level.

Convergence LLM Agent

The convergence LLM agent is implemented as a large language model (LLM) embedded within a tool. To use this tool, the DFT LLM agent must first specify which job has encountered a convergence issue. The tool then locates the relevant files for that job and, for each file, prompts the LLM with the following query:

Here is the content of the file <content>, please give me suggestions on how to fix the convergence issue.

The LLM is instructed to provide a structured and concise response to the formatted question. In this setup, most operational tasks, such as parameter parsing and preprocessing, are handled deterministically by code. This allows the LLM to focus exclusively on decision-making. Additionally, by decoupling the LLM from the broader LLM agent communication graph, the LLM agent can concentrate on context-specific questions without the complexity of managing inter-agent interactions. This design enhances both the robustness and success rate of convergence handling. The convergence LLM agent is able to provide suggestions in parameter setting and script generation, and the reasoning process, and enables the DFT LLM agent to self-correct and conduct updated calculations. HPC LLM Agent

The HPC LLM Agent acts as a specialized High-Performance Computing (HPC) resource manager. Its core responsibilities include: (1) optimizing computational resource allocation based on the complexity of the calculation; (2) scheduling and submitting DFT calculations to appropriate HPC clusters; (3) monitoring calculation status and progress; and (4) retrieving and organizing output files upon completion. To ensure efficient resource usage, the HPC LLM agent dynamically selects computational parameters—such as node count, processor allocation, partition, and walltime—based on calculation-specific information, including system size, calculation type, and settings.

The LLM agent is equipped with a suite of tools designed to support these tasks. It selects tools based on their documented capabilities. Further details of these tools are provided in the Supplementary Information S-12 to S-18.
For calculation submission, the LLM agent employs the Python Simple Queuing System Adapter (pysqa)
Janssen et al. 2019
, which provides seamless integration with SLURM-based
Jette and Wickberg 2023
HPC environments. This abstraction layer decouples the scientific logic from infrastructure-specific details, enabling the framework to remain focused on computational chemistry tasks while making effective use of available computational resources.

Canvas

Canvas is a shared information dashboard that enables lossless and time-invariant data sharing among LLM agents, tools, and human users. It stores all variables in their native format, reducing errors and preventing hallucinations that may arise from converting data to and from text. Before executing any task, LLM agents inspect the canvas to retrieve relevant information and record important results back into canvas when appropriate.
Fig.

6
shows an example snapshot from a CO/Pt(111) workflow. In this example, when the LLM agent encounters a convergence issue for the second time, it first reviews the previous parameter settings stored under
convergence_modifications
. It then uses this information to request updated DFT setting suggestions. Next, it retrieves the corresponding structure file from
Pt111_CO_upright_path
, verifies and loads the correct pseudopotentials from canvas, and finally generates a new set of calculations, avoid repetitively generating structures and searching pseudopotentials. This process demonstrates how canvas helps maintain data consistency in long-running workflows and prevents redundant operations. Full snapshot of canvas from Sol27LC and CO/Pt(111) challenge can be found in Supplementary Information S-2 and Supplementary Information S-9 to S-11.

All information in the canvas are stored in a centralized dictionary object. In addition, the canvas provides several functions to support core operations. The three main functions—inspection, reading, and writing—are exposed to LLM agents through specialized tools, allowing them to interact with the canvas in a structured and consistent manner. While LLM agents access these functions through dedicated tools, other tools that require access to canvas data can directly interact with the underlying dictionary using native read and write operations.
For inspection, no arguments are required; the canvas returns a list of all available keys. For reading, a valid key must be provided, and the canvas returns the corresponding value. If the key is invalid, the canvas suggests performing an inspection to locate the correct key before attempting to read again. For writing, LLM agents must supply a descriptive key along with the object to be stored. If the key already exists, canvas prompts the LLM agent for confirmation before overwriting, thereby preventing accidental data loss.
Keys can be marked with additional constraints such as read-only, protected, or format-restricted. Protected keys can only be modified under predefined conditions, while format-restricted keys accept only specific types of input (e.g., lists of valid filenames). If an LLM agent attempts to violate these constraints, the canvas returns an informative warning to guide corrective action.
All updates to canvas are logged and made visible to human users for transparency and to support post-processing. Additionally, a serialized object (a pickle file) is created to capture the current state of canvas, enabling session resumption and ensuring data availability for downstream analysis.

DFT Benchmark

Density functional theory (DFT) is one of the most widely employed first-principles simulation methods for atomistic systems. A number of softwares packages have been developed to carry out these calculations, including VASP
Kresse and Hafner 1993
, Gaussian
Frisch et al. 2016
, Quantum Espresso (QE)
Giannozzi et al. 2009
, etc.

In this work, we use QE v7.2 for DFT calculations, which uses the plane wave (PW) basis set for describing electron wavefunctions, and uses pseudopotentials (PP) to describe core electrons. We also investigate the impact of exchange correlation functional (XC) choices on calculated structural properties, including the lattice constant and adsorption energies. These include functionals under the Local Density Approximation (LDA) and the generalized gradient approximation (GGA), namely Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE) functional and the PW91 functional. All the calculations are carried out using the GBRV pseudopotentials.

The lattice constant values are obtained from fitting an equation of state (EOS) curve between DFT-calculated potential energy and volume, and finding the lowest-energy point. These values are benchmarked using the Sol27LC dataset, which was collected in Ref.
Wellendorff et al. 2012
and includes lattice constants of 27 elemental crystals in BCC, FCC, and diamond lattices. The elements in this dataset covers both metals and non-metals, which makes it ideal for testing DREMAS’ ability across the periodic table. The adsorption energies are calculated as:
