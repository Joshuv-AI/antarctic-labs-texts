Introduction

Beef-production systems play a key role in the intersection between food production, biodiversity conservation, and preservation of ecosystem functions (Gerber et al., 2015). As global interest in the concept of “Nature Positive” increases, in parallel to an increasing world population and demand for protein sources, it will be necessary to ensure that beef-producing landscapes contribute to the Kunming-Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework (GBF) mission of “halting and reversing” global biodiversity loss (Convention on Biological Diversity, 2022). Achieving this outcome will require efforts to avoid and minimize negative impacts on biodiversity wherever possible and compensating for unavoidable impacts with better-than-equivalent biodiversity gains, facilitated via ecosystem restoration (Maron et al., 2024). In particular, Target 10 of the GBF focuses on enhancing biodiversity and sustainability in agriculture and other production systems. As such, being able to measure, monitor and report on the state of biodiversity and ecosystem functions across beef-producing landscapes that can vary immensely in production systems, environments, climates and governance is essential to underpin the GBF mission and support long-term resilience of production lands.

Engagement in sustainability initiatives that seek to improve net environmental outcomes alongside production can offer opportunities for producers, governments and the private sector alike. Governments are taking steps to measure the economic value provided by ecosystems, and their role in national security (Australian Bureau of Statistics, 2026; Department of Environment, Food and Rural Affairs, 2026a). Processors, manufacturers, retailers, and brands are also increasingly interested in the ecological integrity of beef-production systems, as they recognize their business dependencies on ecosystems and look to safeguard future supply, reduce volatility and risks within their supply chains, and support storytelling and communications about partnership within farming communities (Consumer Goods Forum, 2025). As a result, producers with demonstrable environmental credentials may be able to maintain wider market access (e.g., deforestation-free supply) or premium pricing on their products (Meyer and Miller, 2015; Audubon, 2026). Further, there are opportunities for producers to engage with both government- and private-sector-led voluntary schemes aiming to improve environmental outcomes, including agri-environmental stewardship schemes and carbon and biodiversity markets (Whish et al., 2016).

The beef industry will be key to achieving positive outcomes for biodiversity given the substantial land area it occupies (Gilbert et al., 2018), particularly in landscapes where ecosystems and species of conservation concern are concentrated. To provide assurance around the environmental benefits of beef sustainability initiatives, we need robust, high-integrity measurements of the state of biodiversity and ecosystem function. Ideally, such measures would provide a streamlined, consistent approach to measuring biodiversity across different beef-production regions that has relevance both to producers at the local scale and processors, manufacturers, retailers, and brands at broader regional and global scales.

In early 2026, the Global Roundtable for Sustainable Beef (GRSB) published a report aiming to highlight metrics that are commonly used in global ecosystem accounting and biodiversity monitoring frameworks (GRSB, 2026). This report was developed with the intention of improving clarity within the beef industry regarding what metrics may be required or desired to demonstrate nature-positive outcomes within beef-producing regions. The report highlighted that measurements of ecosystem extent and ecosystem condition, alongside measures of priority species, would provide information relevant to downstream supply chain actors in alignment with global reporting frameworks while also being relevant to farm and landscape-level projects.

However, to leverage the full value of ecosystem extent and ecosystem condition metrics for reporting and decision-making, two key challenges remain. First, access to this information by downstream actors is often restricted by a lack of comprehensive traceability systems in many beef-producing regions. It is often where downstream actors have the poorest visibility in their supply chains (due to indirect supply) that challenges to ecosystem condition or extent (like deforestation) can occur (zu Ermgassen et al., 2022). Without this visibility, it can be difficult for downstream actors to implement actions that help address these challenges, like the incentivization of science-based land management practices. Second, actions to improve ecosystem extent and condition are implemented and often measured at the farm-level but must be scaled to broader resolutions to be most useful for global supply chains and government applications.

In response to these challenges, landscape and jurisdictional approaches (LJAs) are increasingly being used in key commodity production regions (SourceUp, 2026). Landscape approaches are multi-stakeholder initiatives that aim to advance shared sustainability goals and build resilience within a defined geographic area (ISEAL, 2026). Jurisdictional initiatives associate the geographic area with political boundaries with the purpose of using strong government representation to ensure public policy is aligned with the goals of the landscape approach. Landscape initiatives can refer to coordinated efforts within either political boundaries or environmental delineations (such as within watersheds). Since many of the drivers of loss of biodiversity and ecosystem functioning often occur on a landscape scale, like habitat loss and fragmentation (Bennett et al., 2006), landscape initiatives have the potential to address these threatening processes on the same scale at which they occur. They also provide important geographic focal points for processors, manufacturers, retailers, and brands to provide a way to prioritize action and investment at a geographic scale where traceability is incomplete (zu Ermgassen et al., 2022).

At a global level, there is significant interest from the private sector to increase the number of hectares under LJAs, including in beef-producing landscapes. For instance, the Consumer Goods Forum’s Forest Positive Coalition—made up of retailers and manufacturers from around the world—prioritize beef as one of its core commodities, with one of their overarching ambitions to have “landscapes that implement and monitor sustainable land use policies that enable responsible commodity production, protect forests and natural ecosystems, respect human rights of local communities and workers, and strengthen their livelihoods” (Consumer Goods Forum, 2023, p. 18). However, LJAs that focus on a single dimension of a landscape (e.g., beef production or conservation outcomes) may not achieve this goal as effectively as those that seek to integrate more sectors and stakeholders operating within the landscape and across different scales of governance (Carmenta et al., 2020). With growing interest in the Nature-Positive concept, understanding how nature-related metrics may be applied within LJAs to demonstrate outcomes and foster stakeholder alignment around landscape-level targets and actions is increasingly important.

To date, the literature surrounding LJAs has not examined the potential benefits of adopting a landscape-level approach to improving biodiversity and ecosystem functions in beef-producing landscapes or how ecosystem accounting metrics can be applied within LJAs in alignment with Nature-Positive principles. In this feature article, we aim to address these knowledge gaps by (1) outlining possible benefits of LJAs in beef-producing systems beyond those of exclusively farm-scale initiatives to secure improved environmental outcomes, (2) outline a process by which ecological metrics can be applied within LJAs to facilitate effective target-setting and stakeholder engagement and (3) highlight important implementation and governance considerations in the context of LJAs.

The Value of Landscape and Jurisdictional Approaches to Improve Biodiversity and Ecosystem Functions in Beef-Production Systems

Landscape-level processes affect biodiversity and ecosystem function

Ecological functions and the persistence of biodiversity in beef-production systems emerge from processes that operate across entire landscapes and are driven by the sum of management outcomes of individual farms and areas outside of specific property boundaries, like within protected areas (e.g., national parks and nature reserves). Grazing intensity, land conversion, water management, and ground cover loss on individual farms can generate cumulative effects that propagate through hydrological networks and ecosystems, influencing ecosystem functions far beyond the site of management (Waterhouse et al., 2012). Evidence from integrated landscape research shows that these outcomes are strongly influenced by interactions among land uses, landscape stakeholders, and ecological processes across spatial scales, highlighting the limitations of isolated farm-level interventions (Carmenta et al., 2020). Therefore, coordinated land management changes across most farms in a priority landscape can be one of the best ways to instigate positive change in ecological functions.

Water quality in beef-production landscapes provides a clear example of the importance of coordinated management changes across priority landscapes. Empirical evidence from grazing catchments draining into the Great Barrier Reef demonstrates that cumulative grazing pressure and associated losses of ground cover increase soil erosion and the export of sediments and nutrients into downstream aquatic ecosystems (Waterhouse et al., 2012). Pollutant loads and ecological risk are spatially aggregated across entire catchments, indicating that water quality outcomes are governed by landscape-scale patterns of land condition, which can be impacted by stocking rate and grazing management decisions at the property scale (Bartley et al., 2023). In response, catchment-specific priorities and action plans were defined to help beef producers address water quality alongside other landscape stakeholders, like sugar cane growers (Queensland Government, 2018). Landscape- or regional-scale feedback loops have also been documented in other grazing systems. For example, deforestation in Brazil’s Amazon biome has been linked to reductions in regional precipitation estimated to cost US$4.8 billion annually, in a country in which 85% of agriculture is rainfed (Baker et al., 2026). This reduced productivity could trigger a theoretical positive feedback loop, whereby further deforestation is done in compensation for reduced production, which in turn further reduces precipitation (Figure 1). In addition, the persistence of species at a site is affected not only by site-level factors, but also by the extent, condition, and configuration of habitat within the wider landscape. For example, when the amount of habitat in a landscape drops below a particular threshold, the rate of localized extinctions can increase due to insufficient habitat resources and fragmentation effects (Banks-Leite et al., 2014). Where these ecological thresholds have been identified, LJAs could aim to restore the amount of habitat above the ecological threshold at the landscape-scale. Otherwise, improvements to habitat amount at a farm-level may fail to translate to population-level benefits if overall habitat amounts at the landscape scale remain low.

Mitigation of leakage and rebound risks

Actions that may improve on-farm biodiversity, but that reduce yields, can increase the risk of leakage, where production is displaced to another part of the landscape (or within a completely different region), potentially creating net biodiversity loss (Balmford et al., 2025; Erm et al., 2026). For example, where improvements in ecosystem condition can cause negative trade-offs with beef production (Mastrangelo and Gavin, 2012), efforts to increase on-farm ecosystem condition could potentially generate negative leakage effects, where production is simply shifted elsewhere in the landscape. False conclusions regarding the effectiveness of management interventions may therefore be made if biodiversity monitoring occurs in isolation from measurements of yield and only considers biodiversity persistence inside farm boundaries and not within the broader landscape.

LJAs can help to identify leakage risks and can promote production practices that result in the best net outcome for biodiversity and ecosystem functions across a landscape. In some production regions, ecological intensification, where ecological processes are managed to increase beneficial ecosystem service provisioning, can support yield increases and business resilience while also improving ecosystem condition (Ruggia et al., 2021). Other work indicates that in many agri-ecological systems, higher-yielding agriculture that limits the amount of land under production in a landscape can deliver the lowest overall biodiversity loss (Phalan et al., 2016). This trend has been observed in beef-production systems (Williams et al., 2017), and has contributed to the rationale for initiatives seeking to improve yields through sustainable intensification while also supporting compliance with landscape forest retention targets (Zu Ermgassen et al., 2018). However, yield improvements can potentially result in rebound effects. Rebound effects occur where improvements in yields are achieved, encouraging expansion into additional areas at detriment to biodiversity and ecosystem functions (Phalan et al., 2016). It is therefore important to pair initiatives seeking to improve yields and production efficiencies with efforts to improve habitat protection and management.

LJAs can help reduce rebound effects by explicitly linking improvements in yield to habitat protection and management via multi-stakeholder platforms that bring together agricultural producers, private sector, non-governmental organizations (NGOs), conservationists, agricultural scientists, and policymakers. Several mechanisms have been proposed to make this link; these include land use zoning (in which government participation is essential), improved management and monitoring of protected areas, and the delineation and protection of areas of High Conservation Value and/or irreplaceability within landscapes (Phalan et al., 2016). Incorporating these elements within LJAs is important to avoid potential perverse outcomes and essential for achieving net biodiversity gain in beef-producing landscapes.

Overcoming traceability barriers

Many efforts to improve biodiversity and ecosystem function outcomes in commodity-producing landscapes have focused on farm-level certification schemes (zu Ermgassen et al., 2022; Thomas et al., 2023; Audubon, 2026). In the case of beef production, these schemes involve beef producers implementing or maintaining management actions on their property that conserve and/or restore biodiversity and ecosystem functions (Thomas et al., 2023). These schemes can help producers access premium markets and create incentives for biodiversity-friendly land management. For example, Audubon’s Conservation Ranching program certifies beef and bison producers who manage their grasslands to improve habitat value and environmental sustainability, allowing them to retail their products at a premium price (Audubon, 2026). However, they rely on a clear understanding of traceability within the supply chain; certified beef must have come from a certified property. In beef-production regions where there is poor data available to beef buyers, these requirements can be hard to meet (zu Ermgassen et al., 2022). For example, indirect supply is common in many beef supply chains, whereby cattle move across different farms throughout their lifecycle (Skidmore et al., 2024). This indirect supply creates challenges for verifying biodiversity and ecosystem function outcomes, especially with regard to deforestation and conversion-free commitments. Further, it creates challenges for direct downstream private sector investment in encouraging adoption of science-based land management practices at the farm-level.

LJAs offer the potential to help better engage with these producers that are considered indirect suppliers through jurisdictional and landscape sourcing (LJS), which can help to overcome some of the limitations of direct supply-chain efforts. LJS involves companies preferentially procuring from jurisdictions or landscapes that meet important criteria, like being deforestation-free (Meyer and Miller, 2015; Boshoven et al., 2021). When the whole jurisdiction or landscape is certified as deforestation-free, direct farm-level traceability potentially becomes less important, as both direct and indirect suppliers within the landscape would be covered. Preferential sourcing from certified jurisdictions/landscapes could then be used to reward progress, with pilots currently underway for palm oil production regions (Seymour et al., 2020). However, implementing LJS could be difficult in beef-producing regions where cattle are shifted between landscapes and/or jurisdictions during different life stages. Nevertheless, LJS could provide a pathway for overcoming indirect supply challenges in some beef-producing regions.

Biodiversity Metrics and Measurement in LJAs to Help Focus Action

Successful LJAs working toward nature-positive outcomes will need fit-for-purpose, scalable metrics that reliably indicate the state of biodiversity. The information provided by these metrics can help with the prioritization of actions to improve biodiversity outcomes within the landscape while also highlighting existing good management practices. Since biodiversity incorporates many different measurable elements, selecting metrics that represent the broader “state of nature” has become a priority (Nature Positive Initiative, 2025). The GRSB reviewed these frameworks and found that ecosystem extent, ecosystem condition, and some species-level indicators are the most used metrics throughout various frameworks (GRSB, 2026). In this section, we provide an overview of these metrics (except species-level indicators, as they are highly context-dependent and beyond the scope of this article), how they can be measured, and how they can be used in the baselining and target setting phases of an LJA. We also consider their value as proxies for species responses, as iconic and/or threatened species are often important foci of efforts to improve biodiversity outcomes.

Ecosystem extent and condition as foundational metrics of biodiversity

Ecosystem extent refers to the size and spatial distribution of specific ecosystem types classified using a consistent typology, whereas ecosystem condition is the quality or integrity of an ecosystem, measured in terms of its abiotic and biotic characteristics (United Nations, 2021). These two metrics play a critical role in governing the persistence of biodiversity within landscapes; as the extent of an ecosystem declines within a landscape, habitat loss often occurs, while declining ecosystem condition often causes habitat degradation for many species (Heinrichs et al., 2016).

Different management strategies of beef-production systems can positively or negatively affect ecosystem extent and/or condition. For example, adaptive grazing (where stocking rates and grazing periods are managed flexibly in response to factors including rainfall, land type and land condition) can help to improve the ecosystem condition of native grasslands, thereby increasing habitat quality for species of conservation concern (Davis et al., 2020). Deforestation of native forests for the establishment of pastures reduces the extent of these ecosystems, leading to negative biodiversity outcomes (Maxwell et al., 2016), and can also induce reduced condition in the forest that remains (Laurance et al., 2002). Establishing measurement systems that can track these gains and losses in ecosystem extent and condition within a beef-producing landscape is essential for nature-positive outcomes and broader industry sustainability claims (Figure 2).

Despite the correlation between ecosystem extent and condition, and species richness, the effectiveness of these measures as proxies for individual species is often limited. Species vary in their response to different ecosystem attributes, and the composite metrics typically used to indicate ecosystem condition are limited in their ability to predict particular species-level outcomes (Bracey et al., 2026). Fauna in particular can be susceptible to pressures not captured in metrics based on ecosystem structure, such as hunting, invasive predators, or disease (Redford, 1992). As such, landscape- or regionally-tailored approaches to track threatened and iconic species for which ecosystem condition metrics are poor indicators are likely to be necessary adjuncts.

Measuring ecosystem extent and condition

Ecosystem extent and condition can be measured with varying levels of granularity and precision. Remote sensing imagery may be used to assess the extent of ecosystems by leveraging ecosystem typologies to classify land cover types into different ecosystem classes. This can be applied both within a farm boundary or across a broader landscape and/or jurisdiction. For example, the International Union for Conservation of Nature (IUCN)’s Global Ecosystem Typology provides a consistent classification of ecosystems and can be used to describe and help map the extent of ecosystems at local or regional scales in a way relevant to broader international goals and commitments (Keith et al., 2022).

The measurement of ecosystem condition can be more challenging than measuring ecosystem extent. This is because within each example of each ecosystem type, there may be multiple “condition states” that reflect a scale of integrity, from heavily degraded to “intact” condition (Figure 3). While there are some remotely sensed products indicating ecosystem condition (Paruelo et al., 2025), many rely on field-work based assessments that consider key habitat quality variables. These assessments are often defined in a way specific to the region and/or ecosystem type. For example, the Ecosystem Integrity Index developed for temperate grassland ecosystems of South America provides a numerical measure of ecosystem condition based on key characteristics like vegetation structure, plant species diversity, soil erosion, and riparian zone health (Blumetto et al., 2019). State-and-transition models are also increasingly being applied in agricultural landscapes to measure ecosystem condition (Rainsford et al., 2024). These models define ecosystem “states” and describe the drivers that cause changes between ecosystem states (e.g., restoration, deforestation, and wildfire) and can be used to establish ecosystem accounts.

What are baselines and what is their role?

In the context of ecosystem accounts, a baseline is the score of ecosystem extent and condition at the beginning of a project or accounting period, essentially the “opening balance.” A baseline provides the point of reference from which losses and gains in ecosystem extent and condition may be measured. Since the key concept of Nature Positive is a net gain in biodiversity from a 2020 baseline (Nature Positive Initiative, 2025), an LJA seeking to achieve a nature positive-aligned outcome must demonstrate an absolute net gain above this baseline, which could be measured using historical satellite imagery (Figure 4). Alternatively, an absolute net gain outcome could still be demonstrated from baselines post-2020.

The target setting process

Once a baseline has been established, it is necessary to establish targets for relevant biodiversity—including ecosystem extent and condition—within the LJA. This process is heavily dependent on the region in which the LJA is embedded; in some places, where ecosystem outcomes are already considered satisfactory to the multistakeholder group, maintenance may be applicable, while in others, substantial gains may be required to prevent ecosystem collapse and restore biodiversity and ecosystem functions. It is likely that private sector investment in LJAs occurs in locations that are considered highly relevant in terms of supply chain sourcing sheds, and in areas that do not need maintenance regarding ecosystem outcomes but instead are identified with the possibility of significant ecological gains.

Determining the amount of gain above the baseline required to secure enhanced biodiversity and ecosystem functions within a landscape relies on available local or regional scientific knowledge. As detailed earlier, many landscapes have evidence regarding thresholds of extent and condition below which important ecosystem functions and the persistence of some species are heavily compromised (Banks-Leite et al., 2014). By evaluating the relationships between these thresholds and indicators of farm resilience, profitability and productivity, it is possible to determine targets that can feasibly be achieved with beef-producing landscapes.

Once targets are determined, it is then important to determine what management actions need to be implemented within the landscape to achieve them, and to understand the costs and benefits of implementing each management action. Some management actions may deliver substantial public goods, while potentially reducing farm profitability; in these cases, it may be important to prioritize the delivery of agri-environmental stewardship schemes and/or environmental markets within the LJA, while also ensuring leakage risks are considered. However, the implementation of management actions that completely rely on continued stewardship payments to remain profitable could compromise the long-term adoption of the actions with potential changes in public and private sector priorities. For example, England’s Sustainable Farming Incentive scheme recently removed payments for the creation and maintenance of species-rich grasslands, potentially making this management action far less attractive for English beef producers (Department for Environment, Food and Rural Affairs, 2026b). Making progress toward targets by first prioritizing management actions that improve biodiversity alongside production outcomes is therefore important. Targets that can be only achieved through management actions that result in genuine trade-offs (e.g., biodiversity gain at a productivity cost) will need further examination to reduce leakage risks; this may involve developing spatial plans that prioritize the use of innovations to increase production efficiencies in some parts of the landscape, while other areas are dedicated to achieving biodiversity gain.

Funding the maintenance of ecosystems already in good condition remains a particular challenge, as private investment may flow toward landscapes where measurable gains can be demonstrated and rewarded, rather than toward retaining intact assets. Because the private mechanisms most readily available within LJAs tend to reward measurable gain, the ongoing maintenance of intact, high-value assets is likely to depend on public or blended finance, including agri-environmental payments that explicitly reward retention. Legislative frameworks may also need to be included to enforce the maintenance of ecosystem extent and condition.

During the target setting process, it is also critically important to delineate areas within the landscape that, if degraded or lost, would make the achievement of Nature-Positive outcomes impossible. This is because some ecosystem assets are “irreplaceable” on ecologically meaningful time scales; for example, the loss of an intact old growth forest cannot be compensated for by the retention of a secondary regrowth forest (Maron et al., In Press). Within LJAs, these assets must be mapped, and pathways put in place for their protection. In some jurisdictions, legal frameworks may still facilitate the loss of these irreplaceable assets. For example, private properties in Brazil that have native vegetation surpluses above what is required in the Forest Code can legally clear this vegetation (Vieira et al., 2018). These private properties have been recognized as having immense biodiversity and ecosystem function value; a Nature Positive LJA would need to outline mechanisms like financial or market access incentives to protect this surplus forest.

Target setting should also consider factors outside the control of stakeholders involved in the LJA. These include large-scale pressures like climate change, the potential impacts of which can be included in feasibility assessments of management actions and whether they contribute to climate change adaptation and/or mitigation (Harvey et al., 2014). However, short-term disturbance events, like wildfire, could trigger potential net loss of nature, affecting the ability to make Nature-Positive claims within the LJA. In these scenarios where the loss has been beyond the control of stakeholders who were aiming at delivering net positive outcomes (e.g., beef producers), it should not form the basis of any penalty, and initiatives to build resilience should continue.

Implementing LJAs

Implementing LJAs, and progressing toward set targets, requires the effective engagement of stakeholders operating within the defined landscape boundaries, including beef producers, governments, industry-representative bodies, environmental non-governmental organizations and slaughterhouses, processors, manufacturers, retailers, and brands who source commodities directly or indirectly from the landscape. Governance of multistakeholder platforms can be challenging, especially as there are likely competing interests, and has been highlighted as a determining factor in LJA success (Sayer et al., 2015). LJAs may be led by businesses, NGOs or government, and will involve power disparities that must receive careful consideration; for example, local people may require government agencies or NGO activists to represent their concerns (Sayer et al., 2015). The most successful LJAs typically include a broad range of stakeholders that attempt to engage with the structural changes required to make lasting impact, like providing training and seeking legislative change (Carmenta et al., 2020). While beef may be the primary commodity produced in many landscapes, failure to adequately engage stakeholders beyond those involved in the beef supply chain will limit the effectiveness of LJAs, and facilitators must consider other interest groups that should be involved.

The implementation of LJAs, the type of management interventions, and the establishment of these multi-stakeholder groups will also vary depending on the type of beef-production system of focus. For example, production systems that rely on significant inputs from external sources (like feedlot systems) would likely need to engage with landscapes and stakeholders from which they source feed inputs, which may be geographically distinct from the feedlot itself.

Robust producer technical assistance and advisor networks are critical for realizing the potential positive outcomes of an LJA. These advisors help producers to measure metrics on their properties, facilitate trust by producers in the aggregation of farm-level information to the landscape level, and support communication in potential management actions that could lead to Nature-Positive outcomes, while also reporting back the goals and concerns of producers to the multi-stakeholder platform.

Since the actions to achieve Nature-Positive outcomes within LJAs must largely be implemented by agricultural producers on their properties, it is critically important to determine a clear value proposition for producers to participate. This value proposition may take several forms; management actions that improve biodiversity may be shown to improve farm resilience and productivity (Davis et al., 2020; Ruggia et al., 2021), adoption of these management actions may be used to facilitate participation within environmental markets so producers can receive financial incentives, and adoption of management actions could be linked to market-access requirements, private sector recognition platforms, and government regulatory compliance.

Conclusions and Future Directions

The application of LJAs in beef-producing landscapes provides several advantages, including the ability to positively leverage the impact of landscape-scale processes on biodiversity persistence and ecosystem functions; creating a structure to mitigate leakage risks and reduce potential rebound effects; and overcoming traceability barriers that can limit the ability to direct private-sector funding toward the adoption of science-based land management practices. Applying measures of ecosystem extent and ecosystem condition within these LJAs, establishing a baseline and setting clear time-bound targets supported by stakeholders will be essential for evaluating the impact of the LJA. Multistakeholder platforms should be established as a central component of an LJA, with adequate technical support provided to producers.

The ability of LJAs to drive positive change in the triple bottom line across beef-producing regions could be constrained by some limitations. Long-distance transport of cattle between different landscapes depending on life stage and forage availability could mean important parts of the supply chain are missed by LJAs; for example, an LJA focused on a “backgrounding” or fattening region could fail to engage with important cow-calf breeder regions. Achieving consensus on landscape-level targets among multiple stakeholders within an LJA may also prove challenging, especially in regions where biodiversity gain may come at a higher cost to producers. Careful consideration of leakage risk and public good provisioning will be required in these regions as part of the LJA process.

An important area for future work will be research that aims to identify potential beneficial synergies between ecosystem extent and condition, long-term landscape and farm business resilience, and reliable commodity production. This information could be used within spatial planning frameworks in landscapes to prioritize particular areas for conservation actions and/or improvements in production outcomes. Also, measuring yield and productivity alongside measures of ecosystem extent and condition will be important in helping to recognize leakage risks.

Moving forward, in partnership with regional stakeholders, the GRSB plans to pilot the approach of monitoring ecosystem extent and ecosystem condition within two distinct LJAs in beef-producing regions, to identify elements of success. There is also an opportunity to investigate methods to encourage the establishment and maintenance of LJAs, like preferential sourcing agreements that have been trailed in palm oil production landscapes.

These collaborative approaches to the application of LJAs offer opportunities to generate more investment in geographies of common interest, and in the sharing of learnings and outcomes, something that will be important in ensuring beef-production landscapes can make positive contributions to the preservation of biodiversity and ecosystem functions, while still providing important nutrition.
