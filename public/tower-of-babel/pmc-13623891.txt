Characterization of the antibacterial and biofilm inhibitory activities of Arctium lappa L. (greater burdock) against representative members of the ESKAPE group
Authors: Danae Kala Rodriguez Bardaji, Gabriella Fedus, Samantha Tran, Dinindu de Silva, Lily Fagan, Michael A. Savka, Dawn Carter, Nagela Bernadelli Sousa Silva, André O. Hudson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Antimicrobial resistance creates an urgent need for new antibacterial strategies, and medicinal plants remain an important source of chemically diverse bioactive metabolites. This study investigated the antibacterial potential of Arctium lappa L. (greater burdock) naturalized in the United States using a genome-guided approach integrated with microbiological and mechanistic assays. Genome mining identified 35 putative biosynthetic gene clusters associated with diverse specialized metabolite classes. An 80% aqueous ethanol leaf extract was evaluated against six ESKAPE-like bacterial strains: Enterococcus faecium RIT857 and Staphylococcus aureus ATCC 25923 (Gram-positive), and Klebsiella aerogenes RIT3131, Acinetobacter sp. RIT587, Pseudomonas aeruginosa ATCC 27853, and Enterobacter sp. RIT637 (Gram-negative). MIC and MBC values ranged from 64 to 512 and 128–512 μg/mL, respectively, while MBIC50 values ranged from 64 to 256 μg/mL. Time-kill assays demonstrated bactericidal activity, with Gram-negative strains reaching a ≥3-log10 reduction within 6 h and Gram-positive strains showing slower killing kinetics. LIVE/DEAD staining, scanning electron microscopy, and atomic force microscopy showed changes consistent with compromised membrane integrity, while H2DCFDA assays showed increased intracellular ROS in S. aureus (181% ± 11%) and P. aeruginosa (214% ± 13%) relative to untreated controls. A modified disk diffusion assay also showed increased inhibition zones for selected extract–antibiotic combinations. The individual metabolites responsible for these effects were not chemically identified, which limits direct linkage between predicted biosynthetic pathways and observed activity. Overall, the findings demonstrate antibacterial and biofilm-inhibitory activity of A. lappa extract and support further bioassay-guided fractionation and metabolomic characterization.

Introduction

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) is one of the greatest challenges facing modern medicine, threatening the effective treatment of bacterial infections and increasing morbidity, mortality, and healthcare costs worldwide (Antimicrobial Resistance Collaborators, 2022; Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2019; World Health Organization, 2024a). Among the pathogens of greatest concern are the ESKAPE group, which includes Enterococcus faecium, Staphylococcus aureus, Klebsiella aerogenes, Acinetobacter spp., Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and Enterobacter spp. These organisms are responsible for a large proportion of healthcare-associated infections and possess diverse mechanisms of resistance, including reduced membrane permeability, active efflux systems, enzymatic antibiotic inactivation, and biofilm formation (Pendleton et al., 2013; Rice, 2008; Ciofu and Tolker-Nielsen, 2019). The continued emergence of multidrug-resistant strains, together with the limited development of new antibacterial agents, underscores the urgent need for alternative strategies for antibiotic discovery (Antimicrobial Resistance Collaborators, 2022; World Health Organization, 2024b).

Natural products have historically provided the structural foundation for many clinically important antibiotics, and they continue to represent one of the richest sources of chemically diverse bioactive molecules (Newman and Cragg, 2020; Atanasov et al., 2021). Although microorganisms have been the primary focus of natural product discovery efforts, medicinal plants synthesize a wide variety of specialized metabolites that contribute to defense against environmental stressors and microbial pathogens (Atanasov et al., 2021; Dias et al., 2012). However, despite their long history of use in traditional medicine, many medicinal plants remain poorly characterized at the genomic level, limiting our understanding of their biosynthetic potential and their value as sources of novel antibacterial metabolites.

Arctium lappa L. (greater burdock), a member of the Asteraceae family, has been widely used in traditional medicine and has been reported to contain lignans, terpenoids, phenolic acids, and other classes of secondary metabolites with diverse biological activities (Navarro-Muñoz et al., 2020; Bardaji et al., 2025b). Nevertheless, the genomic basis underlying the biosynthesis of these metabolites and their potential contribution to antibacterial activity remain largely unexplored. Recent advances in whole-genome sequencing and biosynthetic gene cluster prediction provide an opportunity to integrate genomic information with functional studies to better understand the antimicrobial potential of medicinal plants (Bahramian et al., 2023; Perez-Delgado et al., 2021).

The objective of this study was to investigate the antibacterial potential of A. lappa naturalized in the United States using a genome-guided approach. By integrating genomic analysis with microbiological and mechanistic characterization, this work aims to provide new insights into the biosynthetic capacity and antibacterial properties of this medicinal species and to establish a framework for future natural product discovery.

Materials and methods

Plant material preparation

Leaves of A. lappa L. (Asteraceae) were collected in Rochester, New York, United States on the campus of the Rochester Institute of Technology (43.0847°N latitude and 77.6761°W longitude) Species identity was confirmed by genomic sequencing of both nuclear and chloroplast genomes (Bardaji et al., 2025a, Bardaji et al., 2026). In addition, morphological characteristics of the collected specimens, including features of the leaves and fruits, were consistent with those described for A. lappa (Figure 1), providing independent phenotypic support for the molecular identification. No voucher specimen was deposited; however, species identity was independently assessed through both genomic and morphological evidence. The accepted botanical name and taxonomic status of A. lappa L. were verified using the Medicinal Plant Names Services (MPNS) of the Royal Botanic Gardens, Kew, in accordance with recommendations for reporting plant materials.

Arctium lappa L. (greater burdock) used in this study. (A) Representative whole plant showing the characteristic large, broad leaves. (B) Close-up of the upper leaf surface showing its characteristic morphology and prominent venation. (C) Abaxial (lower) leaf surface showing the dense pubescence and prominent venation. (D) Mature burrs showing the characteristic hooked involucral bracts associated with seed dispersal.

Leaves of A. lappa were selected for extraction based on their traditional medicinal use and phytochemical potential. Freshly harvested leaves were rinsed with tap water, freeze-dried using a Harvest Right Home Freeze Dryer (United States), and ground into a fine powder using an electric blender. The resulting homogeneous plant powder was subjected to solvent extraction.

Extraction was performed via maceration using solvents of varying polarity: methanol, ethanol, acetone, hexane, ethyl acetate, aqueous methanol (80:20 v/v), and aqueous ethanol (80:20 v/v). For each condition, the plant powder was mixed with solvent at a ratio of 1:10 (w/v) and incubated in an orbital shaker at 80 rpm for 48 h at 20 °C. The resulting mixtures were filtered through Whatman No. 1 filter paper to remove solid residues. The filtrates were concentrated using a rotary evaporator (BUCHI, United States) to obtain dried crude extracts, which were stored at 4 °C until further use. The extraction procedure was based on a conventional maceration method for medicinal plant materials (Abubakar and Haque, 2020). Initial antimicrobial screening by disk diffusion against ESKAPE-like bacterial strains (Section 2.3) revealed that the aqueous ethanol extract exhibited the most promising activity. Therefore, subsequent experiments were carried out exclusively with this extract. Extraction yields and preliminary screening results for all solvent extracts are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Genome mining and biosynthetic gene cluster analysis

The genome of A. lappa was analyzed to investigate its biosynthetic potential using the draft genome sequence generated and described in our previously published dataset (Bardaji et al., 2025a). The genome sequencing data are publicly available in the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) under BioProject accession number PRJNA1167729 and BioSample accession number SAMN44012260. The draft genome was submitted to the PlantiSMASH v1.1 web server for automated identification and annotation of biosynthetic gene clusters (BGCs).

The analysis was performed using default parameters and focused on the detection of clusters containing three or more distinct biosynthetic enzyme subclasses, including terpene synthases (TPS), cytochrome P450 enzymes, type III polyketide synthases, methyltransferases, and glycosyltransferases. Each cluster was classified according to its predicted metabolic class (e.g., terpene, alkaloid, polyketide, flavonoid, or hybrid) and further analyzed based on the presence of core scaffold-forming enzymes and tailoring components.

Cluster architecture and gene content were visualized using PlantiSMASH’s interactive tools and manually curated to identify candidate BGCs with potential relevance to the antimicrobial activity of A. lappa.

Bacterial strains

Six ESKAPE-like bacterial strains were used to evaluate the antibacterial activity of the A. lappa extract. These included both reference strains and resistant clinical/environmental isolates from the RIT culture collection. These strains were selected for their phylogenetic similarity to the core ESKAPE group, their known antibiotic resistance profiles, and their relevance to nosocomial infections. The bacterial panel included E. faecium RIT857, S. aureus ATCC 25923, K. aerogenes RIT 3131, Acinetobacter sp. RIT 587, P. aeruginosa ATCC 27853, and Enterobacter sp. RIT637.

Determination of the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) and minimum bactericidal concentration (MBC)

The antibacterial activity of the 80% aqueous ethanol leaf extract of A. lappa was evaluated by determining the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) using the broth microdilution method according to the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) guidelines (CLSI, 2024). The extract was dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) and serially diluted two-fold in Mueller–Hinton broth (MHB) in sterile 96-well microplates to obtain final concentrations ranging from 1 to 512 μg/mL. The final concentration of DMSO in the assay did not exceed 1% (v/v). Bacterial inoculum were prepared from overnight cultures and adjusted to approximately 5 × 105 CFU/mL in each well.

Microplates were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. Following incubation, 30 μL of 0.01% (w/v) aqueous resazurin solution was added to each well and the plates were incubated for an additional 30 min (Sarker et al., 2007). Wells that remained blue were considered indicative of growth inhibition, whereas wells that changed from blue to pink indicated metabolically active bacterial cells. Gentamicin was used as a positive control. Wells containing Mueller–Hinton broth only, bacterial inoculum without extract, and the highest concentration of DMSO used in the assay served as sterility, growth, and solvent controls, respectively. All experiments were performed in triplicate.

The minimum bactericidal concentration (MBC) was determined from wells showing no visible bacterial growth. Prior to the addition of resazurin, 10 μL aliquots from these wells were plated onto Mueller–Hinton agar and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. The MBC was defined as the lowest concentration at which no bacterial colonies were observed. The antibacterial effect was classified as bactericidal when the MIC and MBC values were identical and bacteriostatic when the MBC exceeded the MIC. All assays were performed in triplicate. For interpretation of crude-extract antibacterial potency, MIC values < 100 μg/mL were considered significant, values > 100 to ≤625 μg/mL moderate, and values > 625 μg/mL weak, following the criteria summarized by Kuete (2010).

Time-kill kinetics assay

Time-kill assays were performed to evaluate the bactericidal activity of the 80% aqueous ethanol extract of A. lappa against ESKAPE-like bacterial strains following the general methodology described by Balouiri et al. (2016). Overnight cultures were adjusted to approximately 105–106 CFU/mL in MHB and incubated at 37 °C with shaking in the presence or absence of the extract. The extract was tested at the previously determined MBC for each strain. Untreated cultures served as growth controls, while gentamicin (for Gram-negative bacteria) or vancomycin (for Gram-positive bacteria), each tested at their respective MBCs, served as positive antibiotic controls.Aliquots were collected at 0, 2, 4, 6, 12, and 24 h, serially diluted in sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and plated onto Mueller–Hinton agar. Following incubation at 37 °C for 24 h, colonies were enumerated and expressed as log10 CFU/mL. Time-kill curves were generated by plotting viable bacterial counts against time. A reduction of ≥3 log10 CFU/mL relative to the initial inoculum was considered indicative of bactericidal activity. All experiments were performed in triplicate.

Biofilm inhibition assay

The biofilm inhibitory activity of the 80% aqueous ethanol extract of A. lappa was evaluated using a crystal violet biofilm quantification assay against six ESKAPE-like bacterial strains following O’Toole (2011) with modifications. Overnight cultures were diluted in MHB to an optical density at 600 nm (OD600) of 0.1. Aliquots were dispensed into sterile 96-well polystyrene microplates containing two-fold serial dilutions of the extract (1–512 μg/mL). Plates were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h under static conditions to allow biofilm formation.

Following incubation, planktonic cells were carefully removed, and wells were gently washed three times with sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to remove non-adherent bacteria. The attached biofilms were fixed, stained with 0.1% (w/v) crystal violet for 15 min, and washed thoroughly with distilled water to remove excess stain. The bound crystal violet was subsequently solubilized with ethanol, and the absorbance was measured at 600 nm using a microplate reader. Biofilm biomass was quantified as the absorbance of the solubilized crystal violet, with lower absorbance values indicating greater inhibition of biofilm formation. Untreated bacterial cultures served as the biofilm growth control. No separate reference antibiofilm compound was included in this assay; therefore, inhibition was quantified relative to the untreated control.

The minimum concentration required to inhibit 50% of biofilm biomass (MBIC50) was determined as the lowest extract concentration that reduced the measured biofilm biomass by approximately 50% relative to the untreated control. All experiments were performed in triplicate.

To investigate the mechanism of antibacterial activity, one representative Gram-positive (S. aureus ATCC 25923) and one representative Gram-negative (P. aeruginosa ATCC 27853) strain were selected from the initial screening panel. These strains were chosen because they displayed clear susceptibility to the A. lappa extract and are clinically relevant ESKAPE pathogens. Therefore, all subsequent mechanistic studies (SEM, AFM, LIVE/DEAD fluorescence staining, and ROS assays) were conducted using these representative organisms.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM)

The effects of the 80% aqueous ethanol extract of A. lappa on bacterial cell morphology were evaluated by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) following the sample preparation protocol described by Parthasarathy (2019), with minor modifications. Pseudomonas aeruginosa ATCC 27853 and Staphylococcus aureus ATCC 25923 cultures were treated with the A. lappa extract at 4× MIC for 2 h. Untreated cultures served as controls. Following incubation, 10 μL of each culture was processed for SEM analysis. Untreated cultures served as morphological controls. A separate membrane-damaging positive control was not included because the SEM experiment was designed as a qualitative comparison between extract-treated and untreated cells.

Briefly, bacterial cells were fixed in 2% glutaraldehyde prepared in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; pH 7.4) for 45 min at room temperature. Samples were washed three times for 5 min each with PBS and subsequently dehydrated through a graded ethanol series (50%, 60%, 70%, and 80%) for 10 min at each concentration, followed by two 5-min washes in 95% ethanol and three 15-min washes in fresh 100% ethanol. Residual ethanol was removed by pipetting, and samples were allowed to dry overnight at room temperature.

Prior to imaging, samples were sputter-coated with gold–palladium for 2 min using an SPI sputter coater. Micrographs were acquired at an accelerating voltage of 5 kV using a Vega3 Tescan field-emission scanning electron microscope housed in the Chemistry and Materials Science Facility at the Rochester Institute of Technology.

Atomic force microscopy (AFM) analysis

Atomic force microscopy (AFM) was used to evaluate morphological changes in P. aeruginosa ATCC 27853 and S. aureus ATCC 25923 following treatment with the 80% aqueous ethanol extract of A. lappa. Sample preparation was carried out according to the protocol described by Bolshakova et al. (2004), with minor modifications. Overnight cultures were diluted to approximately 1 × 107 CFU/mL in Mueller–Hinton broth and exposed to the extract at their respective minimum bactericidal concentrations (MBCs) for 2 h at 37 °C under static conditions. Untreated cultures incubated under identical conditions served as controls. Untreated cultures served as morphological controls. A separate membrane-damaging positive control was not included in the AFM experiment.

Following treatment, bacterial cells were harvested by centrifugation (5,000 × g, 5 min), washed twice with sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde in PBS for 1 h at room temperature. Fixed cells were deposited onto freshly cleaved mica discs and allowed to adhere for 30 min. Samples were gently rinsed with deionized water and air-dried overnight at room temperature.

AFM imaging was performed in tapping mode using a Multimode 8 atomic force microscope (Bruker, Billerica, MA, United States) equipped with silicon cantilevers (spring constant ∼40 N/m). Images were acquired under ambient conditions over scan areas of 5 × 5 μm and 20 × 20 μm at a scan rate of 1.0 Hz.

Cell morphology, surface topography, and membrane integrity were qualitatively compared between untreated and treated cells. Representative three-dimensional topographical images were acquired at scan areas of 5 × 5 μm and 20 × 20 μm. Structural alterations, including increased surface roughness, irregular surface topology, membrane deformation, and cell collapse, were considered indicative of extract-induced damage.

Cell viability and membrane integrity assessment by LIVE/DEAD fluorescence staining

The effects of the 80% aqueous ethanol extract of A. lappa on bacterial viability and membrane integrity were evaluated using the LIVE/DEAD™ BacLight™ Bacterial Viability Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, United States), following the methodology recommended by the manufacturer and the methodology described by Berney et al. (2007). Staphylococcus aureus ATCC 25923 and P. aeruginosa ATCC 27853 were grown in Mueller–Hinton broth to early exponential phase and adjusted to an OD600 of 0.2–0.3. Bacterial suspensions were exposed to the extract at 4× MIC for 2 h at 37 °C. A concentration of 4× MIC was selected for the mechanistic assays to ensure a rapid and reproducible antibacterial effect, thereby facilitating the detection of membrane damage and oxidative stress within the experimental incubation period. Final treatment concentrations were 512 μg/mL for S. aureus and 256 μg/mL for P. aeruginosa. Untreated and vehicle-treated cultures served as controls.

Following treatment, cells were collected by centrifugation, washed twice with sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and stained with SYTO 9 and propidium iodide (PI) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Samples were incubated in the dark for 15 min at room temperature and transferred to 6-well plates for imaging.

Fluorescence images were acquired using an Olympus IX81 inverted fluorescence microscope equipped with a ×40 objective (Olympus Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) and appropriate filter sets for SYTO 9 and PI detection. SYTO 9 stains all bacterial cells, whereas PI penetrates only cells with compromised membranes. Cells exhibiting green fluorescence were considered viable, while cells exhibiting red fluorescence were considered membrane-damaged or nonviable. Representative images were acquired using identical microscope settings for untreated and treated samples to enable direct qualitative comparison. All experiments were performed in triplicate.

Reactive oxygen species (ROS) detection assay

Intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) production following treatment with the 80% aqueous ethanol extract of A. lappa was evaluated using 2′,7′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (H2DCFDA; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, United States) following the method described by Wang and Joseph (1999), with minor modifications. Staphylococcus aureus ATCC 25923 and P. aeruginosa ATCC 27853 were grown overnight in MHB, subcultured to the early exponential phase, and adjusted to an optical density at 600 nm (OD600) of 0.2–0.3.

A 10 mM H2DCFDA stock solution was prepared in anhydrous dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), aliquoted, and stored at −20 °C protected from light. Immediately before each experiment, a fresh 10 μM working solution was prepared in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Bacterial suspensions were incubated with H2DCFDA (10 μM) for 20 min at room temperature in the dark.

Following staining with H2DCFDA, 100 μL aliquots of the bacterial suspensions were transferred to sterile 96-well microplates and treated with the A. lappa extract at 4× MIC (512 μg/mL for S. aureus and 256 μg/mL for P. aeruginosa). Untreated cells and vehicle-control wells containing the same final concentration of DMSO (1%, v/v) as the extract-treated samples served as negative controls. Plates were incubated at 37 °C for 2 h. No exogenous ROS-inducing positive control was included; ROS changes were therefore interpreted relative to untreated and vehicle controls.

Fluorescence emission spectra were recorded using a Synergy H1 Hybrid Multi-Mode Microplate Reader (BioTek Instruments, Winooski, VT, United States) with an excitation wavelength of 485 nm and an emission scan from 500 to 600 nm. Fluorescence values at the emission maximum (535 nm) were normalized by dividing the relative fluorescence units (RFU) by the corresponding OD600 value for each well. The normalized fluorescence values were then expressed as a percentage relative to the untreated control, which was assigned a value of 100%. All experiments were performed in triplicate.

Antibiotic interaction assay using antibiotic disks and Arctium lappa extract

Potential interactions between the 80% aqueous ethanol extract of A lappa and selected antibiotics were evaluated using a modified disk diffusion assay based on the Kirby–Bauer method (Bauer et al., 1966), with minor modifications. Overnight bacterial cultures were adjusted to a 0.5 McFarland standard and incorporated into molten Mueller–Hinton agar at a ratio of 1:100 (200 μL bacterial suspension in 20 mL agar). The inoculated agar was poured into Petri dishes and allowed to solidify.

Commercial antibiotic disks were placed onto the agar surface under two experimental conditions: (1) antibiotic disk alone and (2) antibiotic disk supplemented with 10 μL of A. lappa extract (512 μg/mL) applied directly onto the disk. Plates were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h.

Zones of inhibition were measured in millimeters using ImageJ software following calibration with the 6-mm antibiotic disk diameter. Potential interactions were inferred when the combination of extract and antibiotic produced a larger inhibition zone than the corresponding antibiotic alone. Assays were performed in triplicate, and the mean zone diameter was used for analysis.

Statistical analysis

Graphs were generated using GraphPad Prism 8 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, United States). Quantitative data are presented as the mean ± SD of three independent experiments.

Results

Genome mining and biosynthetic gene cluster analysis

Genome mining of A. lappa identified 35 putative biosynthetic gene clusters (BGCs) distributed throughout the genome, indicating substantial biosynthetic potential for the production of specialized metabolites. The predicted clusters encompassed diverse biosynthetic classes, including terpene, alkaloid-like, polyketide, phenylpropanoid/flavonoid, saccharide, cyclopeptide, fatty acid–saccharide, saccharide–alkaloid, hybrid, and unclassified pathways (Table 1). This diversity of predicted BGCs demonstrates the extensive repertoire of specialized metabolic pathways encoded within the A. lappa genome.

In addition to terpene-associated BGCs, several clusters displayed domain architectures consistent with alkaloid-, polyketide-, and phenylpropanoid/flavonoid-related biosynthesis. These clusters encoded enzymes such as type III polyketide synthases, methyltransferases, oxidoreductases, glycosyltransferases, and other biosynthetic proteins commonly associated with plant specialized metabolism. Furthermore, multiple hybrid or unclassified BGCs containing genes from different biosynthetic pathway classes were identified, suggesting the potential production of structurally diverse specialized metabolites.

Overall, the broad diversity of predicted biosynthetic gene clusters identified in the A. lappa genome provides a genomic resource for future functional and metabolomic investigations aimed at linking specific biosynthetic pathways with the antibacterial, biofilm inhibitory, and antibiotic-potentiating activities observed for the aqueous ethanol extract.

Antibacterial activity and bactericidal kinetics

The antibacterial activity of the extract was evaluated against six ESKAPE-like bacterial strains by determining the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) and minimum bactericidal concentration (MBC) (Table 2). The extract exhibited broad-spectrum antibacterial activity, with MIC values ranging from 64 to 512 μg/mL and MBC values ranging from 128 to 512 μg/mL. Using the above crude-extract cutoffs, the MIC of 64 μg/mL against P. aeruginosa falls within the significant-activity range, whereas MICs of 128–512 μg/mL against the remaining strains fall within the moderate-activity range.

Among the tested strains, P. aeruginosa ATCC 27853 exhibited the greatest susceptibility, with the lowest MIC value (64 μg/mL), although a four-fold higher concentration (256 μg/mL) was required to achieve bactericidal activity. Acinetobacter sp. RIT587 and S. aureus ATCC 25923 displayed MIC values of 128 μg/mL; however, only Acinetobacter sp. exhibited identical MIC and MBC values (128 μg/mL). To further characterize the bactericidal activity of the extract, time-kill assays were performed using the previously determined MBC values for each strain. The extract exhibited rapid and sustained antibacterial activity against all tested organisms. Gram-negative bacteria, including P. aeruginosa, Acinetobacter sp., Enterobacter sp., and K. aerogenes, exhibited a ≥3-log10 reduction in viable cell counts within 6 h of treatment. In contrast, the Gram-positive organisms E. faecium and S. aureus displayed slower killing kinetics, reaching bactericidal thresholds after 12 h and 12–24 h, respectively (Figure 3).

Biofilm inhibition and morphological alterations induced by Arctium lappa extract

The ability of the 80% aqueous ethanol extract of A. lappa to inhibit biofilm formation was evaluated against six ESKAPE-like bacterial strains using a crystal violet biofilm assay. Biofilm biomass decreased in a concentration-dependent manner for all tested strains (Figure 4). The concentration required to inhibit 50% of biofilm formation (MBIC50) varied among the bacterial species (Table 2). Acinetobacter sp. RIT587 was the most susceptible strain, exhibiting a MBIC50 of 64 μg/mL, whereas S. aureus ATCC 25923 exhibited the lowest susceptibility, requiring 256 μg/mL to achieve equivalent inhibition. Intermediate susceptibility was observed for P. aeruginosa ATCC 27853, Enterobacter sp. RIT637, E. faecium RIT857, and K. aerogenes RIT 3131, all of which exhibited MBIC50 values of 128 μg/mL. A substantial reduction in biofilm biomass was observed at concentrations ≥128 μg/mL for most strains, with the greatest inhibition occurring at the highest concentrations tested.

Biofilm biomass was determined by measuring absorbance at OD600 after crystal violet staining and ethanol solubilization. Data are presented as mean ± SD from three independent experiments.

To further investigate the effects of the extract on bacterial structure, scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was performed on treated and untreated cells. Untreated bacteria exhibited normal cellular morphology, characterized by smooth surfaces, intact cell envelopes, and well-defined cellular architecture. In contrast, extract-treated cells displayed marked morphological alterations, including surface irregularities, membrane deformation, cellular collapse, and disruption of biofilm architecture (Figure 5). These structural changes were observed in both P. aeruginosa and S. aureus, supporting the biofilm inhibitory activity observed in the crystal violet assays.

Atomic force microscopy (AFM) analysis further characterized the surface morphological changes induced by the A. lappa extract (Figure 6). Untreated P. aeruginosa ATCC 27853 and S. aureus ATCC 25923 cells exhibited relatively smooth surface topography and well-defined cellular morphology. Following treatment with the A. lappa extract, both species displayed increased surface roughness, irregular surface contours, and pronounced topographical alterations. In P. aeruginosa, treated cells exhibited prominent grooves, depressions, and disruption of the normally uniform surface architecture compared with untreated controls. Similarly, treated S. aureus cells displayed irregular surface morphology and deformation of the cell envelope, resulting in a less organized surface topography. These alterations were consistently observed in treated cells and were absent in untreated controls.

Membrane damage and oxidative stress induced by Arctium lappa extract

To investigate the effects of the extract on bacterial membrane integrity and intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, LIVE/DEAD fluorescence staining and H2DCFDA-based ROS assays were performed using P. aeruginosa ATCC 27853 and S. aureus ATCC 25923.

Membrane integrity was assessed using LIVE/DEAD fluorescence staining with SYTO 9 and propidium iodide (PI) (Figure 7). Untreated control cells exhibited predominantly green fluorescence, indicating intact cell membranes and high cell viability. In contrast, cells treated with the A. lappa extract at 4× MIC for 2 h exhibited a marked increase in red fluorescence, indicating increased PI uptake and reduced membrane integrity. This pattern was observed in both bacterial species, with a greater proportion of red-fluorescent cells observed following treatment compared with untreated controls.

Discussion

Genome-guided natural product discovery has emerged as a powerful strategy for identifying novel antimicrobial agents in response to the increasing global burden of antimicrobial resistance (Newman and Cragg, 2020; Navarro-Muñoz et al., 2020). Medicinal plants have emerged as promising sources of antibacterial metabolites against clinically important and multidrug-resistant pathogens (Majeed et al., 2022; Hasan, 2025). Although medicinal plants have long been investigated as sources of bioactive metabolites, most studies have relied primarily on phenotypic screening without considering the genomic basis underlying specialized metabolite biosynthesis (Atanasov et al., 2021; Dias et al., 2012; Newman and Cragg, 2020). Recent advances in whole-genome sequencing and biosynthetic gene cluster prediction have enabled the integration of genomic information with functional analyses, providing a powerful framework for prioritizing candidate biosynthetic pathways and accelerating natural product discovery (Navarro-Muñoz et al., 2020; Atanasov et al., 2021; Newman and Cragg, 2020).

In the present study, genome mining of A. lappa identified 35 putative biosynthetic gene clusters (BGCs), including terpene-, polyketide-, phenylpropanoid/flavonoid-, alkaloid-like-, saccharide-, and hybrid-associated pathways. The diversity of these predicted clusters indicates substantial biosynthetic potential and provides a genomic framework supporting the production of structurally diverse specialized metabolites with antimicrobial activity. In particular, the identification of multiple terpene-associated BGCs encoding terpene synthases together with cytochrome P450 monooxygenases, glycosyltransferases, methyltransferases, and other tailoring enzymes suggests an extensive capacity for terpenoid diversification, a feature commonly associated with bioactive plant secondary metabolites (Huang et al., 2022).

The first chromosome-level genome assembly of A. lappa was reported by Yang et al. (2022), who generated a high-quality reference genome for a cultivated Chinese accession and primarily focused on genome assembly, comparative genomics, and genes involved in lignan biosynthesis and plant development. Their work identified more than 32,000 protein-coding genes and established an important genomic resource for future studies of A. lappa (Yang et al., 2022). More recently, Feng et al. (2025) generated a second chromosome-level genome assembly from the Chinese cultivar ‘Peixian yellow’, improving genomic resources for the species and investigating carotenoid biosynthesis. Comparative analyses between Chinese accessions revealed extensive genomic variation, including millions of single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs), hundreds of thousands of insertion/deletion polymorphisms (INDELs), and thousands of structural variants, demonstrating considerable genetic diversity within A. lappa (Feng et al., 2025).

Unlike these previous studies, which primarily emphasized genome assembly quality, comparative genomics, and metabolic pathways related to plant development or phytochemical accumulation, the present study focused on the biosynthetic potential associated with antimicrobial metabolites. Furthermore, the A. lappa investigated here represents a population naturalized in the United States rather than a cultivated Chinese accession. Geographic separation and adaptation to distinct environmental conditions may contribute to genomic and metabolic diversification, potentially influencing the composition and regulation of specialized metabolite biosynthetic pathways (Feng et al., 2025). Although comparative genomic analyses between geographically distinct populations were beyond the scope of the present study, the genome reported here expands the available genomic resources for A. lappa and provides a valuable foundation for future population genomic, metabolomic, and evolutionary investigations.

An important strength of the present study is the integration of genome mining with comprehensive biological validation. Rather than relying solely on genomic predictions, the predicted biosynthetic potential was evaluated alongside antibacterial susceptibility testing, bactericidal kinetics, biofilm inhibition, membrane integrity assays, intracellular ROS production, scanning electron microscopy, atomic force microscopy, and antibiotic interaction analyses. This integrated strategy enables comparison between predicted biosynthetic capacity and experimentally observed antibacterial phenotypes, facilitating the prioritization of candidate metabolite classes for future isolation and characterization. Among the predicted biosynthetic pathways, terpene-associated clusters are of particular interest because plant terpenoids have been widely reported to possess antibacterial, biofilm inhibitory, membrane-disruptive, and antibiotic-potentiating activities (Huang et al., 2022). Similarly, polyketides, phenylpropanoids, flavonoids, and alkaloids constitute important classes of antimicrobial natural products capable of affecting bacterial membranes, quorum sensing, oxidative stress, and multiple intracellular targets (Atanasov et al., 2021; Newman and Cragg, 2020). Therefore, the broad diversity of biosynthetic pathways identified in the A. lappa genome is consistent with the broad-spectrum antibacterial and mechanistic activities observed for the aqueous ethanol extract in the present study. Collectively, these findings demonstrate the value of integrating genome mining with functional microbiological analyses to facilitate the discovery of plant-derived antimicrobial metabolites and provide a foundation for future isolation and characterization of the bioactive metabolites responsible for the observed antibacterial activity.

The aqueous ethanol extract of A. lappa exhibited broad-spectrum antibacterial activity against all six ESKAPE-like bacterial strains tested, with MIC values ranging from 64 to 512 μg/mL. Similar antibacterial activity has previously been reported for A. lappa extracts against clinically relevant bacterial pathogens, supporting the antimicrobial potential of this species (Bahramian et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2019). Among the organisms evaluated, P. aeruginosa exhibited the greatest susceptibility, whereas E. faecium was the least susceptible. Time-kill analyses further demonstrated rapid bactericidal activity, particularly against the Gram-negative pathogens, which exhibited a ≥3-log10 reduction in viable cell counts within 6 h of treatment. This susceptibility pattern differs from that commonly reported for many plant-derived extracts, in which Gram-positive bacteria are often more susceptible because the outer membrane of Gram-negative bacteria restricts the diffusion of hydrophobic phytochemicals (Guimarães et al., 2019).

Biofilm formation is a major contributor to antimicrobial tolerance and persistence among ESKAPE pathogens. The aqueous ethanol extract markedlyinhibited biofilm formation in all strains tested, with Acinetobacter sp. exhibiting the greatest susceptibility. Similar biofilm inhibitory activity has previously been reported for A. lappa leaf fractions against S. aureus (Tang et al., 2014). The broad-spectrum biofilm inhibitory activity observed in this study may be associated with metabolites predicted by genome mining, including terpenoids, flavonoids, and polyketide-derived metabolites. However, these metabolites were not experimentally identified in the extract. These metabolite classes have been widely reported to interfere with quorum sensing, bacterial adhesion, extracellular polymeric substance production, and biofilm maturation (El et al., 2023). The agreement between genomic predictions and the observed phenotypic activity further supports the value of genome-guided approaches for prioritizing medicinal plants with antibacterial and biofilm inhibitory potential.

Mechanistic analyses provided additional insight into the antibacterial mode of action of the extract. SEM and AFM consistently demonstrated increased surface roughness, irregular surface topology, membrane deformation, and partial collapse of bacterial cells following treatment, suggesting that membrane disruption may contribute to the antibacterial activity of the extract. These observations were further supported by LIVE/DEAD fluorescence staining, which demonstrated a marked increase in propidium iodide-positive cells following treatment, indicating compromised membrane integrity. Similar membrane-associated damage has been reported for terpene-rich plant extracts and essential oil constituents, which interact directly with bacterial envelopes and increase membrane permeability (Guimarães et al., 2019; Yap et al., 2014). These effects were particularly evident in P. aeruginosa, suggesting that components of the extract may partially overcome the permeability barrier characteristic of Gram-negative bacteria.

In addition to membrane disruption, treatment with the extract induced a pronounced increase in intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) production in both P. aeruginosa and S. aureus. Oxidative stress has increasingly been recognized as an important contributor to bacterial killing by natural antimicrobial metabolites and may act synergistically with membrane damage to accelerate cell death (Gupta et al., 2021). The simultaneous observation of increased ROS production, membrane permeabilization, and extensive structural damage suggests that multiple cellular processes may contribute to the antibacterial activity of the extract rather than indicating a single mechanism of action. Although the SEM, AFM, LIVE/DEAD fluorescence staining, and ROS assays consistently support membrane damage and oxidative stress following treatment, these observations do not establish definitive molecular mechanisms of action. Additional molecular and biochemical studies will be required to confirm the pathways involved. Such multimodal mechanisms are particularly attractive because they may reduce the likelihood of resistance development while enhancing antibacterial efficacy.

The enhanced activity observed against Gram-negative bacteria in the present study may reflect the combined action of multiple classes of specialized metabolites extracted with 80% aqueous ethanol, including terpenoids, phenylpropanoids, flavonoids, and other polar and moderately lipophilic classes of specialized metabolites predicted through genome mining. Such combinations may facilitate membrane permeabilization, oxidative stress induction, and disruption of bacterial physiology, consistent with the membrane damage, increased ROS production, and rapid bactericidal activity observed in this study. Similar membrane-disruptive and anti-virulence effects have recently been reported for arctigenin, a lignan isolated from A. lappa, against P. aeruginosa, further supporting the ability of Arctium-derived metabolites to target Gram-negative pathogens (Koshak et al., 2024). However, additional phytochemical characterization will be required to identify the specific metabolites responsible for the antibacterial activity of the crude extract.

An additional finding of this study was the ability of the extract to potentiate the activity of selected conventional antibiotics. The largest increase in inhibition zone diameter was observed for rifampin against Acinetobacter sp., while chloramphenicol, tetracycline, and streptomycin also exhibited enhanced activity against E. faecium. Similar antibiotic-potentiating effects have previously been reported for A. lappa extracts and isolated burdock-derived metabolites (Guedes et al., 2024; Lewis et al., 2024). Although checkerboard assays and fractional inhibitory concentration index (FICI) analyses were not performed, the observed increases in inhibition zone diameter suggest that components of the extract may facilitate antibiotic activity. Increased membrane permeability, disruption of bacterial defense mechanisms, and enhanced intracellular antibiotic accumulation have all been proposed as mechanisms through which plant-derived terpenes potentiate antibiotic efficacy (Bardaji et al., 2025b; Guimarães et al., 2019).

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the antibacterial activity was evaluated using a crude aqueous ethanol extract; therefore, the individual metabolites responsible for the observed biological effects remain unidentified. Secondly, although genome mining identified 35 putative biosynthetic gene clusters, neither their expression nor the production of their corresponding metabolites was experimentally validated. Consequently, direct associations between specific biosynthetic pathways and the observed antibacterial phenotypes cannot yet be established. Finally, all experiments were conducted in vitro, and additional studies are required to evaluate efficacy, toxicity, pharmacological properties, and therapeutic potential in biologically relevant in vivo models. No GC-MS, LC-MS, HPLC, NMR, or other metabolite-fingerprinting analysis was performed on the tested extract; accordingly, the study does not assign the observed antibacterial or biofilm-inhibitory effects to specific chemical constituents.

Future studies should therefore focus on bioassay-guided fractionation, metabolomic characterization, and molecular networking approaches to identify the metabolites responsible for the antibacterial activity of A. lappa. Integrating metabolomic analyses with genome mining will facilitate direct associations between biosynthetic gene clusters and their corresponding bioactive metabolites, providing a powerful platform for natural product discovery. In addition, transcriptomic analyses, checkerboard assays, resistance-development studies, and in vivo infection models will further clarify the mechanisms of action, therapeutic potential, and clinical applicability of A. lappa-derived metabolites as novel antimicrobial agents or antibiotic adjuvants.

Overall, the convergence of genome mining, antibacterial susceptibility testing, time-kill kinetics, biofilm inhibition assays, microscopy, ROS detection, and antibiotic interaction testing demonstrates that the aqueous ethanol extract of A. lappa exerts antibacterial and biofilm-inhibitory effects associated with membrane damage and increased oxidative stress. Because the extract was not chemically profiled, these findings cannot be attributed to specific metabolites or directly linked to individual predicted biosynthetic gene clusters. The results provide a basis for future bioassay-guided fractionation and metabolomic characterization.

Conclusion

The present study demonstrates antibacterial and biofilm-inhibitory activity of an aqueous ethanol extract of A. lappa against a panel of ESKAPE-like bacteria. Genome mining identified 35 putative biosynthetic gene clusters, while microbiological and cell-based assays showed bactericidal activity, altered membrane integrity, increased intracellular ROS, and changes in the activity of selected antibiotics. These observations support further investigation of A. lappa as a source of antibacterial natural products; however, chemical profiling and bioassay-guided isolation are required before the active metabolites, and their molecular mechanisms can be established.
