Robust Geometrical Dimensions in Uniform Directional Couplers Preprint: APS/123-QED
Jonatan Piasetzky †∗1, Khen Cohen §∗1, Yehonatan Drori 2, Amit Rotem 2, Yuval Warshavsky 12, Yaron Oz 12, Haim Suchowski 12

Abstract
Integrated photonics, a scalable platform for optical networks, sensors, and circuits, has experienced exponential growth. Over the past decade, these platforms have been increasingly harnessed for the emerging field of quantum integrated photonics. However, fabrication tolerances, such as waveguide width, wafer height variations, and sensitivity to temperature changes, significantly impact their robustness and performance. In this work, we identify and analyze stationary geometrical dimensions of directional couplers that enhance tolerance to such variations. Through theoretical predictions and experimental validation, we investigate the behavior of these robust dimensions under changes in refractive index, waveguide height, wavelength, and inter-waveguide distance. We experimentally demonstrate the efficiency of these stationary geometrical dimensions in a single-coupler design and subsequently utilize them to fabricate a quantum two-photon controlled-NOT (CNOT) gate. The CNOT gate achieves an improved mean fidelity, as compared to a CNOT gate fabricated at a different arbitrary design point. This discovery highlights the potential of stationary geometrical dimensions for robust, high-performance photonic circuits and paves the way for new, resilient configurations in quantum technologies.

Introduction

Photonic-integrated circuits have garnered significant attention in recent decades due to their versatility and potential in a wide range of applications. These include optical switches for communication systems

;
Fang et al. 2011
, interconnects

, sensing technologies

, and optical neural networks

. More recently, integrated optics has emerged as a promising platform for advancing quantum technologies, including quantum information processing
Politi et al. 2008
;
Silverstone et al. 2014
;
Matthews et al. 2009
;
Fu 2003
, quantum cryptography, and quantum sensing
Wang et al. 2020
;
Rudolph 2017
;
Qiang et al. 2018
. Three key factors contribute to the attractiveness of photonic-integrated circuit technology. First, it enables straightforward manipulation of light through passive elements, such as directional couplers, and active elements, including thermal tuning and nonlinear effects
Chrostowski and Hochberg 2015
;
Dong et al. 2006
. Second, it is highly power-efficient, making it particularly appealing for applications such as optical neural networks
Fu et al. 2024
. Finally, its scalability is a significant advantage, as it leverages well-established fabrication processes and existing industrial infrastructure
Rudolph 2017
.

In the quantum regime, performing multi-qubit operations presents significant challenges due to the intrinsic nature of photons, which inherently very weakly interact with one another
Knill et al. 2001
. Additionally, while classical optical circuits can tolerate moderate error levels, in quantum applications, the sensitivity to errors is usually much larger. Also, correcting those errors is still a big challenge, according to the well-known threshold theorem, fault-tolerant quantum computing requires the physical error rate to be lower than some threshold
Aharonov and Ben-Or 2008
. Furthermore according to this theorem, the lower the physical error rate is, the fewer resources will be required for error correction
Aharonov and Ben-Or 2008
. The threshold value differs from code to code but previous work in state-of-the-art surface codes, for example, exhibit thresholds in the order of
1\%
physical per operation error
Fowler et al. 2012
;
Raussendorf et al. 2007
;
Wang et al. 2003
. Thus, achieving a low error rate is essential for constructing reliable quantum circuits
Nielsen and Chuang 2010
. One of the fundamental building blocks of an optical integrated circuit, in both classical and quantum regimes, is the directional coupler
Chrostowski and Hochberg 2015
;
Yariv et al. 2007
;
Lifante 2003
. A directional coupler divides light between two waveguides
Horst et al. 2013
or functions as a combiner
Bogaerts et al. 2012
. For quantum information applications in the dual rail representation, it is the basic building block allowing for quantum gates
Knill et al. 2001
;
Kok et al. 2007
.
However, the presence of errors in the directional coupler, arising from factors such as fabrication imperfections, temperature fluctuations, or the spectral width of the light field
Chrostowski and Hochberg 2015
;
Henry and Kazarinov 1996
, alters the behavior of quantum gates. These errors ultimately reduce their fidelity and degrade the overall performance of the quantum circuit.

Despite significant advancements in fabrication technology and the development of various error mitigation techniques
Katzman et al. 2022
;
Kyoseva et al. 2019
;
Kaplan et al. 2022
;
Lu et al. 2015
;
Yun et al. 2018
, achieving high circuit quantum fidelity remains a significant challenge, particularly when extremely low error thresholds are required
Nielsen and Chuang 2010
. Moreover, the error mitigation approaches that have been proposed
Katzman et al. 2022
, often depend on assumptions about internal error correlations and involve complex manufacturing processes, which are frequently accompanied by power losses
Cohen et al. 2024
.

Here, we introduce a novel approach for designing robust directional couplers that can withstand various types of errors. Our method is based on the discovery that a stationary geometrical point, where first-order error effects are inherently canceled, consistently exists. This approach is both straightforward to implement and passive, as it does not add complexity to the design or manufacturing process. We present the theoretical framework supporting the existence of such a stationary point and analyze its behavior under different error models. Additionally, we demonstrate its practical benefits through numerical simulations and experimental validation in the fabrication of directional couplers. Finally, we showcase its advantages for quantum photonics circuits by simulating and experimentally implementing a two-photon controlled-NOT (CNOT) quantum gate, highlighting its potential for enhanced performance in quantum technologies.

III
Robust Uniform Directional Couplers

Utilizing the fundamental interaction relation between the waveguide couplers, we first theoretically show that in any integrated platform, there always exists a robust point as a function of the geometrical parameters. We start by a variation of the width values. Then we show that it applies to other geometrical parameters.

Eq.
2
provides a formal expression for the coupling coefficient. To better understand the relationship between the coupling coefficient and the waveguide width, we must take a look at the electric field distribution of the propagating modes
\vec{E}_{\perp}^{(i)}
. These modes arise from propagating solutions of Maxwell’s equations in fixed cross-section three-dimensional dielectric structures. Given the finite refractive index contrast, much like particles in finite potential well, parts of the modes are confined in the waveguide, while also exhibiting evanescent field distribution outside of the waveguide. Now, we consider two extreme cases, as illustrated in Fig.
1
.

Figure 1:
(a) A perspective schematic view of a directional coupler. (b-d) Overlap integral determining the coupling coefficient as described by the Coupled Mode Theory. Solid lines show the optical modes of each waveguide in 1D, the light gray boxes are the waveguides and the dark gray areas show the integrand. From top to bottom, the coupling coefficient transitions from high to low and back to high. This variation corresponds to the narrowing of the waveguides, highlighting the interplay between the reduced integration area, which decreases the coupling coefficient, and the larger evanescent mode, which increases it. Insets show matching modes as simulated using Lumerical software

In the first case, shown in Fig.
1
b, increasing the width of the two waveguides enlarges the first argument of the integrand, effectively expanding the integration region. This occurs because the evanescent wave from one waveguide exhibits a greater overlap with the cross-sectional area of the second waveguide. In this region, the modes are very confined but increasing the widths decreases the wall-to-wall gap and increases the integration area. In the second case, shown in Fig.
1
d, when the width is reduced to very small dimensions, the diffraction principle predicts exponential divergence of light from the waveguide. In this region, the modes become much less confined, and larger parts of the modes propagate outside the waveguide. As a result, the amplitude of the evanescent electric field increases, enhancing the second argument of the integral. This behavior has also been observed experimentally
Almeida et al. 2003
. These two extreme cases demonstrate an increase in the coupling coefficient. Given the continuous dependence of the coupling coefficient on waveguide width, we can infer the existence of at least one configuration where
\frac{\partial\kappa}{\partial W}=0
, which is shown in Fig.
1
c. In both the experimental measurements and our numerical simulations, we observe the existence of a single robust point, where the variation of the width of the waveguide has a dramatically low effect on the coupling coefficient.

We fabricated several directional couplers with varying widths to experimentally validate our simulations, using Applied NanoTools’ Multi-Project Wafer (MPW) run
noa
. For each design width, several couplers were designed, with varying interaction lengths. The splitting ratio spectrum of each coupler was measured, in a robust measurement scheme
Piasetzky et al. 2024
, using light from a broadband source and an optical spectrum analyzer, coupled to the device via a grating coupler
Chrostowski and Hochberg 2015
. From these measurements, the coupling coefficient for each design width was extracted by fitting the splitting ratio dependence on interaction length, as shown in Eq.
1
. Appendix
B
and Ref.
Piasetzky et al. 2024
provides a more detailed description of the experimental setup and measurement methods.

Our results, presented in Fig.

, clearly demonstrate the presence of a stationary minimum point, consistent with our simulations. This design point inherently offers robustness to width variations, as the first-order derivative of the coupling coefficient is nullified at this point.

IV
Robust CNOT Gate

We further implement the use of such a robust stationary gate in in more complex quantum circuits. We evaluate the design of a quantum CNOT gate as proposed in Ref.
Ralph et al. 2002
, and experimentally demonstrated in bulk optics
O’Brien et al. 2003
and integrated optics
Politi et al. 2008
. Following the analysis in Ref.
Brańczyk 2017
and extending it to an arbitrary frequency-dependent photonic circuit
U(\omega)
, the following output state is received for a two-photon input from a continuous wave pump Spontaneous Parametric Down Conversion (SPDC) source:

\ket{\psi}_{out}=\int g(\omega)U(\bar{\omega}+\omega)a^{\dagger}_{\omega}U(\bar{\omega}-\omega)b^{\dagger}_{-\omega}\ket{0}d\omega

(3)

where
\bar{\omega}=\omega_{pump}/2
is half the pump wavelength,
a^{\dagger}_{\omega}
is the creation operator of a photon in mode a with frequency
\bar{\omega}+\omega
, and
g(\omega)=\Phi(\bar{\omega}-\omega,\bar{\omega}+\omega)
is the Joint Spectral Amplitude (JSA) of the bi-photon wavefunction evaluated across the secondary diagonal, imposed by the narrow pump and energy conservation. The JSA is affected by the SPDC process’s phase-matching condition and the spectral response of the grating couplers used for the optical interface to and from the circuit. We assumed
g(\omega)
to have a Gaussian shape centered around zero, and we evaluated its width to be 12 nm using a Hong Ou Mandel interference scan, which also matches nicely the classical measurements of the grating coupler’s spectral response (see Fig.
4
(a)).
The input modes
a
and
b
are selected for each input state according to the dual rail representation
Politi et al. 2008
. Our CNOT circuit
U(\omega)
can be calculated by applying the transformation shown in Eq.
1
to the two modes participating in each coupler in the circuit in the order of appearance. Lastly, our setup’s Hong-Ou-Mandel visibility is not perfect, but measured to be 94% (see Fig.
4
(b)). This can be attributed to some distinguishability between the two photons arising from other degrees of freedom (e.g., Polarization might not be exactly aligned). To account for that, a partial trace needs to be performed. The output state is best described as a density matrix with some probability of being in the indistinguishable state and some probability of being in the distinguishable state where the two-photon interference disappears.

Next, we performed a Monte Carlo simulation of CNOT circuits using the measured coupling ratio spectrums (see Sec.
II
). We sampled 1000 circuits with width errors from a normal distribution with a 10 nm standard deviation for each design width. The gate is made from five directional couplers, as can be seen in Fig.
4
(e), and we assume that for each coupler both waveguides are completely correlated (suffer the same width error), which is fairly reasonable due to their physical proximity and fabrication process. We explored both ends of the spectrum of the correlation in width errors between the different couplers, completely correlated or completely uncorrelated with small differences in the outcome. We then calculated the mean error probability, which is defined as the probability that the measured output quantum state will be different than the expected state, by evaluating the integral in Eq.
3
and averaging over all the separable basis input states and all the iterations. The results are shown in
4
(b). It can be noticed that a stationary point also exists in the design of a circuit-level scale, and improved fidelity can be achieved.

To experimentally validate our circuit-level performance, we fabricate two CNOT circuits, one designed around the stationary point of 508 nm, and the second designed with a different arbitrary width of 450 nm. We then measure the output coincidence rate for all logical outputs while varying the input ports. After post-selecting only logical outputs, which are within the computation basis, we extracted the probability to measure each output state according to the input state. These probabilities are shown in Fig.
4
(c-d). The circuit designed around the stationary point has a measured mean fidelity of
93.30(11)\%
, while the circuit not designed around the stationary point was measured to have a mean fidelity of
91.93(17)\%
. The improvement in the mean error of using the stationary point was measured to be even larger than expected by the simulation. We believe it is caused due to the bigger mode mismatch, resulting in more distinguishability
Politi et al. 2008
.

V
Conclusion

In this study, we discover and examine the robust configuration of the coupling coefficient in a pair of identical coupled optical waveguides. We offer a theoretical explanation, alongside simulation results and experimental data, to substantiate the presence of this robust configuration. Moreover, we investigate how this robust configuration responds to various changing conditions, including refractive index, waveguide
height
, wavelength, and
gap
.

Tackling errors in integrated photonics, particularly within the realm of quantum information processing, presents considerable challenges. Whereas existing approaches often rely on active error-correction strategies, our findings identify a resilient point capable of passively increasing the robustness of quantum gates of the form
X^{\alpha}
, as well as quantum circuits composed of multiple such couplers, as shown in sec.
IV
. Further research is required to extend these results to asymmetric couplers as well, crucial for various quantum gates, such as Hadamard. We anticipate that this discovery will encourage further examination of designs in proximity to this resilient configuration and explore the potential extension to other quantum integrated technologies, such as quantum key distribution, quantum sensing, and boson sampling, as well as to other integrated platforms such as Silicon Nitride and Lithium Niobate.
