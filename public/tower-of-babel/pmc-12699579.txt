Rapid establishment of public sector COVID-19 test-and-treatment programmes across seven low- and middle-income countries: implementation strategies and program monitoring results
Authors: Caroline E Boeke, Nervine Hamza, Chukwuemeka Agwuocha, Okechukwu Amako, Khamsay Detleuxay, Michelle Gao, Bridget C Griffith, Yamikani Gumulira, Elina Urli Hodges, Jessica Joseph, Norman Lufesi, Emily Macharia, Nyuma Mbewe, Elizabeth McCarthy, Faustina O Mintah, Moses Mukiibi, Mwaba Mulenga, Alida Ngwije, Lawrence Ofori-Boadu, Ijeoma Uzondu Okoli, Sompasong Phongphila, Christian Ramers, Sean Regan, Evarist Twinomujuni, Edison Rwagasore, Jessica Tebor, Krishna Udayakumar, Krishna Udayakumar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe COVID-19 Treatment QuickStart Consortium worked with governments in seven low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), Ghana, Laos, Malawi, Nigeria, Rwanda, Uganda and Zambia, to implement COVID-19 test-and-treat programmes at 776 health facilities, including training over 5000 staff and facilitating a donation of 11 300 courses of the oral antiviral nirmatrelvir/ritonavir for treatment. This paper describes the process of implementing COVID-19 test-and-treat programmes in each country, provides aggregate programme monitoring data on numbers tested and treated and analyzes programme enablers and challenges. Between country-level programme initiation (Ghana, May 2023; Laos and Malawi, July 2023; Nigeria, June 2023; Rwanda, March 2023; Uganda, September 2023; Zambia, December 2022) and June 2024, a total of 731 970 SARS-CoV-2 tests were conducted. Of 6724 positive tests, a subset were documented to meet eligibility criteria for nirmatrelvir/ritonavir initiation, and 3041 patients were prescribed nirmatrelvir/ritonavir. The largest number of prescriptions was in Zambia. Programme enablers included decentralisation of services; task-shifting from higher to lower health worker cadres; increased access to point of care antigen tests, including self-tests; and the integration of COVID-19 with other health services. Challenges included COVID-19 de-prioritisation at the time of programme rollout, test commodity stockouts and expiries, and dwindling national surveillance efforts. Learnings from rapid initiation and scale-up of COVID-19 test-and-treat programmes in these seven countries can be used to inform future pandemic preparedness strategies in LMICs.

Introduction

The COVID-19 pandemic has led to over 9 million deaths globally as of June 2024.1 Effective vaccines2 and treatments3 have helped prevent a higher death toll. However, over the course of the pandemic, low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) experienced delayed and more limited access to diagnostics, vaccines and treatment.

In particular, nirmatrelvir/ritonavir, an oral antiviral demonstrated to reduce risk of hospitalisation and death due to COVID-19,3 was made available in high income countries after US Food and Drug Administration Emergency Use Authorization in December 2021, but the first reported delivery to LMIC by global buyers did not take place until March 2023.4 5 Nirmatrelvir/ritonavir is intended for use through a test-and-treat model, in which individuals who test positive for COVID-19, have a mild/moderate infection and are defined as high-risk (including older individuals or those with co-morbidities such as HIV, hypertension, cardiac disease, diabetes) are initiated on treatment within 5 days of symptom onset. Treatment with this 5-day regimen in this group of individuals reduces the risk of progression to severe/critical COVID-19, mitigating hospitalisations and death.69 Our literature review did not identify any published evaluations in LMICs contexts of real-world COVID-19 test-and-treat programme implementation and utilisation of nirmatrelvir/ritonavir.

The COVID-19 Treatment QuickStart Consortium worked with governments in seven LMICs (Ghana, Laos, Malawi, Nigeria, Rwanda, Uganda and Zambia) to develop and roll out COVID-19 test-and-treat programmes from 2022 to 2024.10 Support included facilitating a nirmatrelvir/ritonavir donation as well as providing technical assistance on programme implementation and monitoring and evaluation (M&E). An evaluation was conducted across the programme countries; details on the evaluation methodology have been previously described.11 This manuscript aims to describe programme implementation approaches across the seven countries, present aggregate M&E results across the seven countries, highlight key programme enablers and challenges and facilitate sustainability planning for COVID-19 testing and treatment programmes.

Analysis methodology

This manuscript presents descriptive data about programme implementation gathered from government documents, programme implementation documentation, meeting minutes and conversations with government stakeholders. Data about programme implementation at individual test-and-treat facilities were gathered through standardised facility information forms developed for this evaluation and administered at facilities or through an online portal.11 Aggregate data on COVID-19 testing and treatment volumes at QuickStart programme sites through June 2024 were gathered through routine national data reporting systems between July and November 2024. Effective programme implementation was defined holistically across a number of established implementation outcomes: acceptability, adoption, appropriateness, feasibility, fidelity, implementation cost, penetration and sustainability.12 Outcomes were not all assessed individually given their interrelated nature, but rather key patterns were assessed across adoption, feasibility and penetration; specific outcomes included rapid programme rollout, scale-up to a large number of decentralised facilities, high testing volumes across sites, high linkage to treatment among eligible individuals and programme consistency over time. Key enablers of effective programme implementation and challenges across countries were identified based on inductive analysis of programme data and information gathered through communications with key stakeholders.

Program implementation approach

The QuickStart Consortium

The COVID-19 Treatment QuickStart Consortium, a partnership between Duke University, Americares, the Clinton Health Access Initiative and the COVID Collaborative, received funding from Open Society Foundations, Pfizer and the Conrad N. Hilton Foundation to introduce donated nirmatrelvir/ritonavir in 10 LMICs and provide technical assistance to governments in implementing COVID-19 test-and-treat programmes. As of July 2024, when programme implementation data were analysed, eight of the initial 10 countries had moved forward with approving the importation of the drug and implementing programmes, and seven had M&E data available.

Ethics statement

The data presented in this manuscript came from aggregate sources rather than patient-level data; authors could not identify individual participants. Nonetheless, this study was approved by the Duke University Institutional Review Board (Pro00111388). Consent was not obtained due to participant anonymity.

238 facilities with QuickStart COVID-19 test-and-treat programmes had facility information data available (in Rwanda, most health centres did not submit facility data). 66.1% of these facilities were hospitals; the remainder were health centres or clinics. 72.0% were located in urban or semi-urban areas. 33.5% were supported by partner organisations in addition to the government. 72.4% were open for COVID-19 testing and treatment 7 days per week. At the time of data collection, 35.6% offered PCR testing, 65.7% offered POC-Ag testing and 8.9% offered self-testing kits for personal use. COVID-19 screening was most commonly offered in outpatient departments (70.3% of facilities), inpatient departments (61.4%), the emergency department (60.2%), tuberculosis (TB) clinics (53.9%), HIV clinics (51.3%) and non-communicable disease clinics (51.3%). COVID-19 oral antiviral treatment was most commonly offered in outpatient departments (76.3%) followed by inpatient departments (55.9%).

Testing and treatment volumes

Table 3 provides aggregate M&E data from each country from programme initiation through June 2024. Across the seven countries, 731 970 people were tested for SARS-CoV-2 at activated test-and-treat facilities, with the highest volumes of testing occurring in Rwanda (442 228), Nigeria (193 486) and Zambia (38 426). There were 6724 positive tests across countries, with the highest numbers of positives in Zambia (3314), Rwanda (1516) and Malawi (691). The percentage of people who tested positive varied dramatically across countries depending on testing strategy (targeted vs mass screening campaigns) and volume. 1965 people were recorded by test-and-treat programme staff as eligible for treatment. Ultimately, 3041 people were prescribed nirmatrelvir/ritonavir, with the largest numbers of prescriptions in Zambia (1461), Rwanda (786) and Malawi (282).

Program enablers

Several key enablers—ranging from decentralisation of services to identification of champions—were critical to effective programme rollout (table 4). First, decentralisation of services to lower-level facilities and rapid task shifting to lower health worker cadres allowed for expanded access to services. This approach was more effective to facilitate rapid scale-up with high penetration in comparison with limiting the provision of services to a small number of specialists. Second, access to POC-Ag tests, including self-testing in some country contexts, was another critical factor to facilitate rapid programme scale-up. The investments made into scaling rapid diagnostic tests before the QuickStart programme launch were significant to facilitate effective rollout of treatment. For example, the Ghana programme leveraged previous experience activating COVID-19 antigen testing facilities across all regions of the country—prior to QuickStart and led by Ghana Health Service with support from the Clinton Health Access Initiative—to expedite test-and-treat programme roll-out. Facilities were equipped with human resources and commodities to conduct testing, allowing training to extensively focus on clinical management. Third, integrated services delivery was critical to rapid implementation. Implementation worked best in facilities where there was a high degree of coordination between the clinical services, diagnostics and supply chain departments to ensure effective implementation. Integrated service delivery was less resource intensive and leveraged existing human resources and programme structures, ultimately strengthening these structures. For example, Zambia leveraged TB and HIV infrastructure to set up the programme leading to establishment of bi-directional screening of COVID-19 and TB/HIV, which ultimately facilitated better patient care across disease areas.

In addition to these core factors, facility selection played a role in the ability to quickly launch the programme in some contexts. For example, in Malawi, all health facility levels were considered; selection was based on regional disease burden, each facility’s ability to conduct testing, presence of doctors on site and capacity to manage and store stock of drugs and tests, which ultimately led to the initial programme rolling out effectively at facilities best equipped to handle it. However, as noted above, decentralisation of services to lower-level facilities is a critical longer-term goal. Identification of champions to support with introduction and implementation of test and treat was another enabler. For example, in Zambia, there was strong MOH leadership through the case management specialist who championed the programme and continued engagement on the part of the government, which sustained momentum. Finally, in some contexts such as Zambia, community engagement was viewed as an important enabler for programme success.

Challenges to program rollout

The greatest challenge to effective programme implementation highlighted by countries (table 4) was COVID-19 fatigue by both communities and healthcare workers, as well as the WHO declaration ending the Public Health Emergency of International Concern designation in May 2023. With the reduced number of COVID-19 cases at the time of treatment scale-up, many did not see the importance of test and treat given the timing and other competing priorities, challenging programme sustainability. In particular, competing public health emergencies, including cholera in Malawi and Zambia, redirected attention from COVID-19. Relatedly, test commodity availability was a challenge across all countries. Once facilities stocked out of tests or the stock expired, it was challenging to revive testing efforts. For example, in 2022, with Global Fund funding, Ghana procured and supplied COVID-19 POC-Ag testing to all testing facilities across the country. The tests’ expiration dates occurred during implementation of the QuickStart programme, causing significant stockouts across QuickStart supported facilities for a period of over 2 months (February to April 2024), which led to gaps in testing and treatment initiation. In Nigeria, there were also test stockouts from May to June 2024. Finally, national surveillance efforts dwindled during the period of programme implementation, limiting availability to assess programme progress. Without surveillance and testing, this reinforced the sentiment that COVID-19 was no longer a significant problem. Despite these challenges, these seven countries did successfully roll out COVID-19 test-and-treat programmes within a period of 12 months of donor support.

Two additional countries supported by QuickStart did not roll out the programme within this timeframe, due to the impossibility to bring donated nirmatrelvir/ritonavir in country. In one country, South Africa, the drug was deemed not to be a cost-effective treatment for the public sector. In the other, Zimbabwe, delays in obtaining clearing documents in time to bring in drugs with adequate shelf-life led to an inability to advance the programme. Finally, another country, Kenya, experienced a delayed rollout of the programme, meaning that treatment data were not available as of June 2024.

Learnings from these challenges will be important to incorporate into planning for future pandemic preparedness response.

Discussion

In summary, over a period of 19 months, seven LMICs (Ghana, Laos, Malawi, Nigeria, Rwanda, Uganda and Zambia) were able to implement COVID-19 test-and-treat programmes at 776 health facilities. This included training over 5000 staff, facilitating a donation of 11 300 courses of nirmatrelvir/ritonavir for treatment, conducting 731 970 SARS-CoV-2 tests and prescribing nirmatrelvir/ritonavir to 3041 patients. Despite some challenges around de-prioritisation of COVID-19 as a public health emergency, countries were able to leverage existing POC-Ag testing networks and approaches including decentralisation, task shifting and integration with existing services to rapidly roll out and scale up test-and-treat programmes.

COVID-19 was not the first, nor will it be the last global public health emergency. The last four decades alone have witnessed the rise of SARS, Influenza A virus subtype H1N1, HIV/AIDS, Middle East Respiratory Syndrome (MERS) and Ebola, along with worsening outbreaks of well-known epidemic-prone infectious diseases including cholera and dengue. The public health and economic consequences of COVID-19 underscore the need for all governments to improve their ability to detect and respond to infectious disease threats before they become national or global concerns. This is reinforced by the impact of climate change, which is increasing event-driven health emergencies and outbreaks, and even pandemic risk. Frameworks such as the WHO Health Emergency Preparedness, Response and Resilience,13 the International Health Regulations Joint External Evaluation process,14 and the Global Health Security Index15 outline the core public health capacities that countries require to achieve these goals, such as real-time surveillance reporting and early warning systems, data accessibility, emergency preparedness and response planning, medical countermeasure and personnel deployment.

We demonstrated that it was feasible to implement a test-and-treat programme utilising newly developed products with high quality of delivery during the later stages of a public health emergency, while caseloads were declining but the potential for future surges remained. However, this programme was implemented without the benefit of strong pandemic preparedness response/emergency response infrastructure in QuickStart programme countries, and unfortunately, given this and other challenges, the COVID-19 test-and-treat programmes included here did not ultimately develop into more established, resourced and sustainable pandemic preparedness response programmes over time. Ultimately, building pandemic preparedness response capacity at the country level is paramount and will facilitate a faster and more effective response to future outbreaks/epidemics/pandemics.

Limitations of this analysis include that testing and treatment data were gathered through country-level data systems and were aggregated; therefore, there may be inaccuracies and incomplete data in some contexts, and data cannot be linked to track individual patients across the cascade of care. Eligibility criteria were not consistently documented in the data across all countries, and in some circumstances, providers used clinical judgement to make prescription decisions, or eligibility was not initially documented; this explains why in some countries, such as Zambia, the number of prescriptions is greater than the number recorded in the registers as eligible for nirmatrelvir/ritonavir. In addition, this analysis does not include patient outcome data; this is covered in a subset of facilities/countries in a separate manuscript.

LMICs are now dealing with a new health crisis due to global aid pullbacks. This abrupt decline in available resources is forcing governments to dramatically recalibrate their short-term health priorities, how they provide services and find adequate resources to fill in gaps in service provision moving forward. The extent to which governments prioritise strengthening surveillance systems, developing surge preparedness plans and putting mechanisms in place for expedited access to emergency medical countermeasures within this new funding reality remains to be seen but is highly uncertain given competing priorities for funding. Learning from experiences rapidly rolling out COVID-19 test-and-treat programmes will be critical to prepare for the next pandemic and should remain a priority for LMICs despite the overwhelming number of challenges to address in the near future.
