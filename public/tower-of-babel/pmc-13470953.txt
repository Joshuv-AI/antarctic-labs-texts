Validation of the Spanish Sexual Risk Behaviour Scale among adolescents, youth, and future health and educational professionals
Authors: Laura Alonso Martínez, María Consuelo Sáiz Manzanares, Isadora Gabriella Silva Palmieri, Gabriela Tavares Magnabosco, Emanuele Fino, Jesús Puente Alcaraz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study seeks to adapt and validate the Sexual Risk Behaviour Scale (SRBS) for Spanish-speaking adolescents, young adults and future health and educational professionals. The research sought to assess the scale’s psychometric properties. To establish known-groups validity, differences across age, professional, and sociodemographic groups were analysed.MethodsThe SRBS, a 6-item scale with 5 response options, was validated by psychologists and healthcare professionals in collaboration with educational institutions, targeting secondary school and university students in health and education fields. A total of 478 students aged 13 to 48 aged (M = 19.26, SD = 6.13) participated. Of the participants, 327 identified as women, 148 as men, 20 as gay, 392 as heterosexual, 54 as bisexual, 9 as asexual, and 3 indicated another gender and sexual orientation. Among the adults, 275 were single, 11 married, 2 divorced and 1 widowed. Evidence based on internal structure was gathered through exploratory and confirmatory factor analyses of the scale scores. Sociodemographic factors, including gender, age, education level, marital status, and use of dating apps, along with six standardized sexuality scales, were examined to assess the known-groups validity of the Spanish SRBS scores.ResultsThe scores of the Spanish SRBS yielded evidence of acceptable reliability (α = 0.78) and an average score of 10.08 (SD = 4.52, Min = 6, Max = 24). Factor analyses confirmed its unifactorial structure. The findings of the Spanish SRBS scores revealed significant patterns of risky behaviours influenced by developmental and social factors, such as gender, age, education, and attitudes toward safe practices. Furthermore, sexual risk behaviours (SRBS) were linked to social media (ARS), pornography consumption (PPC-6) and attitudes supportive of rape (RAPE).ConclusionThe Spanish SRBS is a reliable and valid tool for assessing sexual risk behaviours among adolescents and young adults. The findings offer valuable insights for clinicians, educators, and researchers working to evaluate safer sexual practices addressing public health challenges.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-026-04971-y.Keywords: Underage, Youth, Sexual health, Sexual risk behaviour, Scale validation

Background

Adolescents and young adults in Spain face growing sexual health challenges that mirror global patterns of increasing sexually transmitted infections (STIs) and engagement in risky sexual behaviours [1, 2]. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), more than one million individuals aged 15 to 49 contract STIs daily [3], underscoring the pressing need for effective prevention strategies targeting this population for the present study. The global population living with Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV) increased from 36.7 million in 2015 to 39.9 million by 2023 [4]. However, other major STIs, including chlamydia, gonorrhoea, trichomoniasis, HPV, and genital herpes, remain far more prevalent worldwide [2]. Risky sexual behaviours, defined as activities increasing the likelihood of physical or emotional harm in sexual contexts, include inconsistent use of contraceptives, early sexual debut, and unintended pregnancies [5]. These behaviours are particularly prevalent among youth aged 15 to 25, with data from the Centres for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) revealing that only 9% to 45% consistently use protective measures, indicating major gaps in condom use and preventive practices [6]. Social and cultural factors increased these risks, including inadequate sexual education, substance use, and pervasive exposure to online sexual content [7–9]. Such factors contribute to the persistence of misconceptions about sexuality and hinder consistent protective behaviours like condom use [10].

Despite progress in sexual education since the 1990s, Spain still lacks a comprehensive, mandatory sex education program that has implemented such curricula [2]. According to the Spanish Youth Institute [11], adolescents and young adults in Spain are heavily impacted by STIs, with nearly 50% of recorded cases occurring in individuals under the age of 24. Additionally, young adults face considerable barriers to accessing essential sexual health services, including serological testing and HIV pre- and post-exposure prophylaxis [12]. These barriers are particularly pronounced in this population, which often lacks sufficient knowledge on how to seek help or whom to contact, and many hesitate to approach healthcare professionals due to the stigma surrounding STIs [1, 5]. Consequently, this leads to delays in seeking care [2].

The essential role of healthcare and education professionals in promoting sexual health cannot be overstated [13–18]. By adopting a holistic approach, these professionals can effectively address the sexual health needs of young people, facilitating education, managing healthcare systems, and fostering communication and collaboration among students, families, educators, and healthcare providers [14, 15]. Recent research underscores the intricate relationship between sociocultural factors and risky sexual behaviours. Ticona et al. [9] highlight how adherence to traditional gender role attitudes is strongly associated with an increased likelihood of engaging in risky sexual behaviours, such as pornographic consume, substance or drug use during sexual activity. Similarly, Pengpid et al. [19] report that one in four adolescents engage in risky sexual behaviours, including early sexual initiation, inconsistent contraceptives use, and maintaining multiple sexual partners. These behaviours are compounded by external factors such as psychological distress, bullying, and substance use, creating a multifaceted public health challenge [2]. The findings stress the importance of comprehensive and inclusive sexual health education, focusing on equipping adolescents with the knowledge and developing scales to make responsible decisions about their sexual health [20, 21]. To achieve this, validated assessment tools play a critical role. By identifying and assessing the prevalence of sexual risk behaviours, such tools contribute to the development of targeted interventions and educational strategies [5]. However, while there are many tools available, there is a lack of universal standardization for brief instruments specifically designed to assess risky sexual behaviour and STI prevention, tailored to the current educational and social contexts of young people [2, 20]. Many existing instruments focus on specific aspects such as condom use, self-efficacy or communication and are outdated of today’s adolescents, limiting their applicability in comprehensive risk assessment [2, 5, 12].

Given the high prevalence of risky sexual behaviours among youth globally and within Spain, there is an urgent need for reliable and culturally sensitive assessment tools to identify these behaviours and mitigate their associated risks [2]. The Sexual Risk Behaviour Scale (SRBS) fills this gap by providing a concise yet comprehensive tool to assess the frequency of risky sexual behaviours among young adults [5]. Unlike other instruments, the SRBS extends beyond inconsistent condom use, incorporating behaviours such as casual sexual encounters and substance use before sexual activity, which are strongly associated with increased risk [5, 20].

The development of the SRBS was conducted through a three-phase process. Phase 1, Scale Development, entailed a review of recent research on sexual risk behaviours among college students to construct the initial scale. This phase resulted in the generation of 13 items, which measured the frequency of specific risky sexual behaviours over the past month, utilizing a 5-point Likert scale (0 = Never to 4 = Very Often). Phase 2, Content Validation, involved an expert evaluation by three specialists in psychology and human sexuality, who assessed the items for relevance, clarity, and appropriateness. This process refined the scale to six items, excluding those that assessed perceptions rather than actual risk behaviours. Phase 3, Psychometric Testing, involved the empirical validation of the SRBS with a sample of 547 undergraduate students from two UK universities. Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA) and Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) provided evidence of validity based on internal structure, supporting a unidimensional model with scores that demonstrated adequate reliability (α = 0.76). One of the six original items (How often have you had vaginal sex without a condom? ) exhibited lower psychometric performance than the five-item model and was subsequently eliminated, resulting in the final five-item version. The SRBS assesses the frequency of risky sexual behaviours linked to unprotected vaginal, anal, and oral sex, and sexual activity under alcohol or drug influence. The scale scores demonstrated acceptable reliability, with α = 0.76 [5].

Since its development, the SRBS has been employed across various populations. In the United States, Childs et al. [22] utilised it in a study involving 210 juvenile probationers aged 12–18 years with substance use disorders. However, it was administered as a dichotomous instrument, which precluded comparison with the original scale’s scores that span a range of five responses. In Honduras, Landa-Blanco et al. [23] validated the SRBS in a cohort of 452 adults, yielding a mean score indicative of lower engagement in risky sexual behaviours compared to the UK sample. These findings underscore the SRBS’s adaptability to diverse sociocultural contexts, thereby reinforcing its potential for cross-cultural applications.

Despite its demonstrated utility, the SRBS has not yet been adapted for Spanish-speaking populations. Validation of the SRBS in Spanish would enhance its cultural relevance and provide a valuable tool for public health initiatives, enabling targeted interventions and educational programs aimed at reducing STI transmission and improving sexual health outcomes. This would allow for the identification of specific areas where education and awareness need to be improved [2, 24]. Furthermore, adapting the scale ensures that its psychometric properties are maintained across linguistic and cultural boundaries, offering practical solutions tailored to cultural differences and facilitating international research and comparative studies on risky sexual behaviours effectively across diverse populations [2]. Its comprehensive approach is especially relevant given the rising prevalence of risky sexual behaviours, which aligns with increasing trends highlighted in previous research [23, 25].

Adapting the SRBS to this Spanish context is also particularly important given its relevance to future professionals in education and health-related fields such as psychologist, sociologist, educator, physician and nurse [13]. These disciplines play vital roles in sexual health prevention, diagnosis, and counselling across diverse settings, from clinical consultations to community outreach and school programs. A culturally adapted SRBS will enable these professionals to identify risky behaviours more effectively and implement timely interventions, fostering better health outcomes among underage and young populations [15, 21]. Evaluating future health and education professionals, rather than those already practising, is supported by their closer age proximity to adolescents and young adults, which may offer a clearer understanding of the evolution of sexual attitudes and behaviours over time [2, 13, 15, 21]. Moreover, as the next generation of educators and health professionals, these future professionals are likely to be responsible for delivering sex education in the years to come [2]. Identifying gaps in the assessment of sexual behaviour among these young people is crucial for understanding their current sexual health status and conducting future interventions [2, 5].

In recognition of this need, the Spanish version of the SRBS was created through a collaborative process involving health and educational professionals who work directly with adolescents within the healthcare and educational systems. This participatory approach ensured the tool’s relevance to the target population and its ability to accurately assess behaviours related to sexual risk and STI prevention, analysing the impact on sexual decision-making. The objectives of this study are to adapt the SRBS to this Spanish context and to gather evidence of reliability and validity for the scores obtained from a sample of students aged 13 to 17 and young adults. We hypothesized that the scale would exhibit known-groups validity, showing significant differences in sexual risk scores based on age groups and professional backgrounds.

Methods

Design

This study employed a quantitative validation design using a questionnaire that included six standardized scales to validate the SRBS scores [5]. This study is based on an intervention, the results of which will be presented in detail in a separate study [26].

Participants

The population of this study was composed of 478 students aged between 13 and 48 years (M = 19.26, SD = 6.13). Participants included students enrolled in Secondary Education, Sixth Form, and university programs specializing in health and education-related fields. Participants were recruited through a convenience sampling method. High school and university students were recruited from a city in northern Spain. University students were drawn from the city’s only public university, with data collected during lectures. High school participants were recruited from the city’s largest high school, where data collection took place during mandatory tutoring hours after obtaining consent from the school, parents, and students. Data collection took place in person over a period of six months during the 2023-24 academic year, using a Microsoft Forms survey. To facilitate participation and ensure data protection, the questionnaire link was uploaded to course platforms, distributed via institutional email, and displayed in the classroom using a QR code. Participation was voluntary, and no compensation was provided. Refer to Table 1 for a detailed analysis of the sociodemographic data included in the study.

Instruments

The study questionnaire was developed using Microsoft Form hosted on the university’s Office 365 platform to ensure data protection, as mandated by the ethics committee. This questionnaire was divided into three sections: section A contains eight self-developed questions on sociodemographic data; section B contains the SRBS, and the five standardized scales mentioned above; and finally, section C contains a question about comments and suggestions. Each respondent was informed that their participation would be anonymous, through a participant information sheet, a consent form, and a participant report.

The SRBS, validated in this study, evaluated the frequency of risky sexual behaviours concerning condom use in anal, oral, and vaginal intercourse, as well as the consumption of alcohol, drugs, and other substances during sexual activity [5]. The scale showed acceptable internal consistency, with a coefficient α = 0.76. A 5-point Likert scale with 6 items is used, and the scores ranged between 6 and 30. Lower scores reflected lower frequency of risky sexual behaviours. The SRBS was initially designed as a six-item scale. However, despite being described as a six-item scale in the original validation study by Fino et al. [5], their results and discussion indicated that a five-item model, excluding the first item, exhibited superior fit indices and greater reliability of the scores. Because the scale’s structure remains unsolidified, the authors recommended further exploration of the six-item version. Accordingly, the present study re-evaluates the inclusion of the sixth item via factor analysis to determine its relevance and contribution to the overall scale structure, in line with the original authors’ suggested research directions.

To assess the convergent and discriminant validity of the Spanish SRBS, the instrument was administered alongside other measures of related constructs described below.

The Brief Body Dissatisfaction Evaluation Scale (EBEIC) assessed satisfaction with physical appearance, body weight, body image, and its connection to eating habits [27]. The scale has shown acceptable internal consistency, with a reported Cronbach’s alpha α of 0.74. It consisted of a concise 3-item Likert scale with 3- and 4-point response options, yielding scores ranging from 0 to 11. Lower scores indicated greater body satisfaction. In this study, the EBEIC scores demonstrated acceptable reliability, with an α of 0.76.

The Health Protective Sexual Communication Scale (HPSC) measured how often participants discuss sexual health with a new partner [2]. The internal consistency of the scale scores demonstrated high reliability with a α of 0.84. Its 8 items covered topics such as sexual history, health protection concerns, communication about safe sex and the use of contraceptives. The answers were collected on a 5-point Likert scale, with scores ranging from 8 to 40. Higher scores reflected a greater concern for conversations about health protection issues. The reliability of the HPSC scores (α = 0.85) obtained in this study is high.

The Brief Scale of Problematic Pornography Use (PPCS-6) measured the importance, emotional impact, conflict, tolerance, relapse, and abstinence related to pornography use [7, 28]. The scale scores demonstrated high internal consistency, with a reliability of α = 0.94. Responses were collected using a 5-point Likert scale, with 6 items in total, and scores ranged from 1 to 30, with higher scores reflecting greater pornography use. In this study, the reliability of PPCS-6 scores was acceptable, with a α = 0.85.

The Brief Rape Support Attitudes Scale (RAPE) measured the behaviours and attitudes that favour rape culture [29, 30]. The scale demonstrated high internal consistency, with a coefficient α = 0.91. A 5-point Likert scale with 12 items is used, and scores ranged from 12 to 60. Lower scores reflected less favourable attitudes and behaviours toward rape. In this study, the reliability of the RAPE scores (α = 0.85) was considered acceptable.

The Social Network Addiction Questionnaire (ARS) assessed obsession, lack of personal control and excessive use of social networks [31]. The internal consistency of the scale scores showed high reliability with α = 0.92. Responses were given on a six-item 5-point Likert scale, and scores ranged from 6 to 30. Lower scores indicated lower addiction to social networks and the reliability of the ARS-6 scores was α = 0.76.

The Sexuality Knowledge Assessment Questionnaire (SEKAQ) assessed participants’ knowledge of sexuality content [32]. This questionnaire examined a variety of sexuality-related issues, including exposure to risky sexual behaviour, knowledge about sexually transmitted infections (STIs), respect for sexual diversity, gender stereotypes, violence, body dissatisfaction and social pressure to lose the “virginity”. The SEKAQ consisted of 11 items, each of which had responses ranging from 3 to 5 options. Scores on the questionnaire ranged from 0 to 11 points, with lower scores indicating lower knowledge about sexuality. The SEKAQ scores had a high α of 0.84.

Procedure

Prior to the validation of the SRBS scale, the questionnaires were approved by the University’s Ethics Committee. Participants required approximately 60 min to complete the questionnaire. The development and validation of the SRBS psychometric scale were conducted in three distinct phases: adaptation of the scale, content validation, and psychometric evaluation.

The Phase 1 (Scale Development and adaptation) focused on adapting the SRBS from English to Spanish. Bilingual translators with six professional expertise in English and Spanish and backgrounds in education, psychology, health, sociology and politics undertook the translation. The process followed the European Social Survey Translation Guidelines, which enhanced construct validity by ensuring rigorous translation protocols that promoted face and content validity.

The Phase 2 (Content Validation) involved a panel of six experts in sexuality and education reviewed each item for relevance, clarity, and appropriateness for the target demographic. Their feedback refined the translation and ensured the retention of all six SRBS items, maintaining alignment with the original constructs. Following the initial translation and back-translation process, both experts and university students assessed contextual and linguistic suitability. This group included 11 students, seven of whom were students pursuing a Master’s in Sex Education (primarily trained in psychology, education, biology and communication), one nursing student, and three high school students. The panel of six experts, specializing in sexuality, consisted of psychologists, nurses, sociologists, and teachers of social and natural sciences at both primary and secondary school levels. While the original items were preserved, the Spanish version incorporated inclusive language tailored to the local context. In our study, we adhered to the same methodology employed by the original authors, translating the six items initially proposed by the authors of the scale.

Phase 3 (Psychometric Evaluation) involved gathering evidence of reliability and validity for the scores. The evidence based on test content obtained in Phase 2 was complemented by evidence based on internal structure, which was examined through exploratory and confirmatory factor analyses. While the original authors conducted the CFA using the six-item scale, they also performed the analysis with a five-item scale, as Item 1 exhibited lower statistical values. Nevertheless, they recommended that future validation studies reassess the six-item scale. Ultimately, they retained only five items in the CFA due to superior psychometric properties [5]. Accordingly, our experts suggested that incorporating all six items in the validation process would be beneficial, using both EFA and CFA to provide greater clarity. They further recommended comparing the fit of the five-item and six-item models through CFA to provide a more comprehensive assessment. Evidence of validity based on relations to other variables was gathered by examining the associations between SRBS scores and other relevant constructs. Specifically, convergent evidence was supported by positive correlations with the HPSC, RAPE, PPCS-6, and SEKAQ. Conversely, divergent evidence (discriminant) was supported by non-significant correlations with unrelated measures, such as the EBEIC and ARS. Evidence based on relations to criteria was established by comparing SRBS scores with participants’ professional training and educational background, while evidence based on differences between groups was examined across demographic subgroups. Regarding reliability, Cronbach’s alpha coefficients were calculated to evaluate the internal consistency of the scores. Retaining the six items, as opposed to the original five-item version, was justified to strengthen the evidence of validity and the consistency of the scores. Moreover, in subsequent phases of analysis, the six-item version demonstrated superior psychometric properties compared to the latter five-item version proposed by the original authors. The finalized version of the Spanish SRBS is presented in supplementary materials.

Data analysis

Data were analysed using SPSS v.29 and AMOS v.28 software. Preliminary analyses were conducted to ensure there were no violations of normality, linearity, or multicollinearity. To validate the scale, both EFA and CFA were performed. The study sample was divided into two subsamples: the exploratory subsample (314 participants) was used to explore the underlying factor structure of the items, while the confirmatory subsample (164 participants) was used to validate the factor structure identified in the exploratory analysis. The sociodemographic characteristics of the subsamples were similar to those of the full sample.

To assess the reliability of the scale scores, Cronbach’s alpha values greater than 0.70 are considered acceptable, with a recommended threshold of at least 0.72 [33–35]. Prior to conducting factor analysis, the Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) measure of sampling adequacy and Bartlett’s test of sphericity were performed to assess the suitability of the data [31–33]. Such analyses are well-suited for identifying differences in structure when underlying scales exist [34, 35]. The KMO value for the five-item model was 0.68, falling below the recommended threshold of 0.75 [31], although Bartlett’s test remained significant, χ² = 630.27, df = 10, p < .001. This indicates that the five-item model is not suitable for factor analysis [33, 35]. In contrast, the KMO for the six-item models value obtained was 0.78, exceeding the recommended threshold of 0.75 [35], and Bartlett’s test was significant, χ² = 987.47, df = 15, p < .001. These results in the entire sample confirm that factor analysis is appropriate for the dataset in the context of the six-item model [33, 35, 36]. Similarly, in both the EFA and CFA subsamples, the KMO value for the five-item model did not meet the recommended threshold, whereas the six-item model exceeded it. However, Bartlett’s test remained consistently significant in both models. This confirms that factor analysis is appropriate for the six-item model [33, 35, 36]. For this reason, the EFA was conducted exclusively on the six-item model.

The EFA enables the examination of latent variables or common factors that explain responses to the items in the scale. This approach also facilitates the identification of composite dimensions formed by the summarized information inherent in the original variables. In the analysis, a promax oblique rotation was applied to the six-item model, facilitating the possibility of correlations among the extracted factors [37]. This rotation technique is particularly suitable when the factors are anticipated to be interrelated, thereby enabling a more realistic and interpretable factor structure (38). Parameter estimation was conducted using the maximum likelihood (ML) method [33]. The ML method estimates the parameters of statistical models by maximizing the likelihood of the observed data given the model. This approach ensures that the parameter estimates align with the data in a way that renders the observed outcomes most probable under the specified model [38, 39].

To confirm the factor structure of the EFA [37, 38], the CFA was conducted after verifying the absence of missing values, as all respondents had completed the questionnaire. The CFA was performed using AMOS v.28 statistical software. Standardised estimates, as well as total, direct, and indirect effects and covariance estimates, were requested. The use of maximum likelihood estimation ensured the robustness of standardized, and covariance estimates in both models [36, 38]. According to existing literature, CFA facilitates the comparison of models through the chi-square (χ²) fit index, which reflects both the differences between models and the covariance of the data [33, 35–38]. In these studies, model fit is evaluated using the Adjusted Goodness of Fit Index (AGFI), the Goodness of Fit Index (GFI), Comparative Fit Index (CFI), Incremental Fit Index (IFI), and Tucker-Lewis Index (TLI), all of which should also exceed 0.85 [39–41]. Additionally, the Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA) should be below 0.1, and the Minimum Discrepancy per Degree of Freedom (CMIN/DF) should be under 5, in line with established guidelines [42]. Invariance criteria were also considered adequate if the model differences in ΔCFI are ≤ 0.01, ΔRMSEA are ≤ 0.015, and ΔSRMR are ≤ 0.015 [33, 36–38, 41].

It is important to highlight the adequacy of the sample size for this analysis, as the number of participants was 20 times greater than the number of items [33]. To provide evidence of validity based on relations to other variables, we examined the associations between sociodemographic characteristics and the scale scores (known-groups validity). The statistical analyses employed for this psychometric purpose included Pearson correlation, Student’s t-test, and ANOVA. To evaluate the relationships following the ANOVA, the Bonferroni test was applied. To represent the results, we used small subscript letters (x̅a, x̅a, b, x̅b, x̅c, .) to indicate which subgroups exhibited statistically similar relations to the SRBS scores, where shared distinct letters indicate significant variations in score. This procedure facilitates the interpretation of validity evidence across diverse sociodemographic variables (34). A p-value of less than 0.050 was considered statistically significant. For psychometric interpretation, correlation strength was categorized as small, medium, or large, with r values of 0.10, 0.30, and 0.50, respectively. Regarding effect sizes to substantiate score differences, Cohen’s d was interpreted as small, medium, or large for d values of 0.20, 0.50, and 0.80, respectively. In the case of the ANOVA test, the partial eta squared statistic (ηp2) was used, with interpretations of ηp2 = 0.01 (small), ηp2 = 0.06 (medium), and ηp2 = 0.14 (large) (35).

Results

The SRBS scores reported by the respondents indicated that 46.7% of the students (212 participants) exhibited a low frequency of sexual risk behaviours, with mean scores ranging from 6 to 7. A moderate level of risky sexual behaviours was observed in 32.82% of participants (149 individuals), with mean scores between 8 and 14. Additionally, 18.94% (86 participants) displayed a high level of risky sexual behaviours, with scores ranging from 15 to 20, while 1.54% (7 participants) reported a very high level, with scores between 21 and 30.

Validation of the psychometric properties of the SRBS

Reliability analysis of SRBS scores

The reliability of the SRBS scores for the five-item models was slightly questionable, with an α of 0.69 and an average inter-item correlation of 0.38. The mean scale score was 8.14 (SD = 3.54, Min = 5, and Max = 20). The reliability of the SRBS scores for the six-item models was acceptable, with an α of 0.78 and an average inter-item correlation of 0.40. The mean scale score was 10.08 (SD = 4.52, Min = 6, and Max = 24). Table 2 presents the internal consistency metrics for the six-item model across the entire sample. As indicated, the correlations between each individual item and the overall scale scores consistently exceeded 0.30. In accordance with the guidelines proposed by Boateng et al. [36], no items were removed, as all demonstrated significant correlations with the scale.

Confirmatory factor analysis

Although the KMO values were not adequate for conducting factor analysis on the five-item model, the analysis was nevertheless applied to both the five-item and six-item models, given the precedent set by the original study, which ultimately validated the five-item structure (5). Regarding the 5-Item Factor, the scores yielded a questionable reliability, with an α value of 0.63. In contrast, the scores for the 6-item factor demonstrated a satisfactory reliability, with an α value of 0.75.

The fit indices for both the five-item and six-item factors exhibited acceptable performance, meeting all eight goodness-of-fit criteria. However, in both models, the RMSEA exceeded its optimal upper threshold. Additionally, the 5-Item Factor loadings were both statistically significant and within an acceptable range, spanning from 0.24 to 0.91 and the 6-Item Factor loadings were both statistically significant and within an acceptable range, spanning from 0.33 to 0.79. CFA of SRBS-6 in the confirmatory sample

In this case, measurement invariance refers to the consistency of SRBS-6 scores across gender groups. First, a configural invariance model was tested, yielding CFI = 0.83, RMSEA = 0.17, and SRMR = 0.07. Next, a metric invariance model was examined by constraining factor loadings to be equal across groups, showing CFI = 0.84, RMSEA = 0.15, and SRMR = 0.07. Scalar invariance, which constrains item intercepts to be equal across groups, produced CFI = 0.83, RMSEA = 0.14, and SRMR = 0.08. The strict invariance model, which additionally constrains residual variances, resulted in CFI = 0.83, RMSEA = 0.13, and SRMR = 0.09. Differences between models were considered acceptable based on established criteria [33, 36–38, 41]. Given the partial fit of the baseline models, the evidence for invariance should be interpreted with caution. Instead of claiming full invariance, the SRBS-6 findings are presented as exploratory, indicating only approximate equivalence between men and women responses.

Analyses revealed significant differences in scale scores, with higher scores observed among female participants, single individuals, active dating app users, and university students. Evidence of convergent and discriminant validity across sociodemographic variables

The SRBS-6 showed weak correlations with the HPSC, PPCS, and EBEIC. Specifically, higher scores on the SRBS-6 were statistically associated with increased communication about sexual risk, higher pornography addiction scores, and lower body satisfaction scores. Regarding discriminant evidence, a weak correlation with the EBEIC was anticipated, as it represents a conceptually distinct construct. Conversely, regarding convergent evidence, stronger correlations with the HPSC and PPCS were expected due to their conceptual proximity to the SRBS-6 domain; however, these emerged as weak. Furthermore, the SRBS-6 scores showed a small correlation with the ARS, moderate correlations with the RAPE scale, and strong correlations with the SEKAQ. These findings indicate that higher SRBS scores align with more supportive attitudes toward rape victims and lower levels of sex education knowledge within this sample. Additionally, a significant positive correlation emerged between SRBS-6 scores and age, suggesting that older individuals in this sample may engage in riskier sexual behaviours. Discussion

Aligned with the objectives of this study, we adapted and validated the Sexual Risk Behaviour Scale (SRBS) [5] for use in this Spanish context. The Spanish adaptation of the SRBS showed acceptable psychometric properties consistent with the original scale by Fino et al. [5]. The estimates of reliability and the findings from the EFA and CFA closely aligned with those reported in the original study, providing further evidence of score consistency. Notably, during the initial validation, the original authors encountered similar challenges with the RMSEA, further reinforcing the comparability of the Spanish adaptation. Both EFA and CFA supported a unifactorial structure, with comparable results between the original British and this Spanish samples. This process included an assessment of its psychometric properties comprising underage students and young adults.

When adapting the SRBS to Spanish, the six original items proposed by Fino et al. [5] were retained, despite one item having been excluded in the original study due to a poorer model fit in the CFA. This decision was supported by experts and the original author of the English scale to maintain consistency and encompass a broader range of behaviours. Given that the original scale was initially developed for validation with all six items, reassessing their relevance in subsequent studies was deemed necessary [5]. Consequently, this study incorporated and validated the previously excluded item in a separate sample by comparing the five-item factor, validated in the final stage of the CFA in the English study, with the six-item factor, which was theoretically recommended for validation in the original study [5]. The analyses conducted indicated that the six-item factor in the Spanish version exhibited superior psychometric properties compared to the final five-item factor from the original English study, highlighting the importance of reassessing item selection in cross-cultural adaptations. The additional item may capture specific cultural nuances relevant to Spanish-speaking populations, who might be more accustomed to discussing and reporting their sexual behaviours than those in Anglo-Saxon contexts [2, 24]. This finding underscores the importance of an iterative approach to adapting measurement instruments across diverse cultural settings while preserving the theoretical integrity established in the instrument’s original construction [38, 39].

As evidence of construct validity based on known-groups differences, the SRBS-6 scores observed in this study among 478 students from high schools and higher education institutions in the fields of health and education are consistent with existing theories suggesting that higher presence of sexual risk behaviours are influenced by a combination of developmental, social, and individual factors [10, 12, 20, 22–24]. Moreover, comparison with previous studies using the SRBS suggests a growing prevalence of risky sexual behaviours. For instance, in a study by Landa-Blanco et al. [23] with a sample size of 452 adults aged 18 to 68 years (M = 29.96, SD = 8.98) in Honduras, the SRBS revealed a mean score of 8.07 (SD = 3.49), indicating fewer instances of risky sexual behaviours compared to the current study.

The significant patterns observed in this study provide further evidence of construct validity based on known-groups differences. Specifically, higher SRBS scores among adults, individuals in relationships, and dating app users are consistent with previous research indicating that sexual experience and opportunities for risky sexual behaviour tend to increase with age and relationship status [5, 19, 24, 28]. The higher SRBS scores in the control and pretest groups may indicate that those with less exposure to sexual health education or interventions are more likely to engage in unsafe sexual practices. This finding supports the importance of educational programs and interventions in reducing sexual risk, as those with less knowledge may lack the necessary tools to make informed decisions about their sexual health [3, 8, 11]. Additionally, the link obtained in this study between high SRBS scores and attitudes supportive of rape is concerning and may reflect deeper societal issues related to gender norms and the normalization of sexual violence [43]. The SRBS scores highlight that even among university students and health professionals, who are typically presumed to have a higher level of understanding, there are still significant levels of risky sexual behaviour [2]. The results indicate that individuals with formal education and knowledge may not always apply this knowledge in practice [2, 15], which underscores the importance of validating tools like the SRBS in this population. This is particularly concerning since the behaviours and attitudes formed in youth often carry over into adulthood [19]. By ensuring that both students and professionals are well-educated and properly equipped with the tools to assess sexual health, we can prevent the reinforcement of harmful behaviours and better support the sexual health of future generations [2, 15, 17].

Further evidence of construct validity based on known-groups differences was observed in relation to gender. Contrary to findings reported in previous studies [2, 9], women in this sample exhibited higher levels of sexual risk behaviours. These differences may be explained by societal gender norms and expectations that influence sexual behaviour [20]. Previous research suggests that women, especially young women, often face greater societal pressure regarding sexual engagement, which can increase their susceptibility to risky behaviours and unhealthy relationship dynamics [9]. Additionally, gender disparities in sexual health communication may play a role, as women may encounter more challenges in negotiating safe sexual practices due to societal constraints and expectations surrounding their sexual autonomy [2].

As evidence of convergent validity, the weak correlations between the SRBS and both the HPSC and PPCS were unexpected, given their established theoretical associations with sexual risk behaviours. Protective sexual communication is typically associated with safer practices and barriers like discomfort, fear of conflict, or inadequate negotiation skills may hinder the effectiveness of protective communication, as supported by prior studies [2, 36]. This finding could be related to the disparity in results between young adults who have had sexual intercourse and adolescents who have not. However, this does not imply that they lack knowledge of sexual health, nor does it predict future behaviour of this adolescent in their sexual life. Similarly, the weak correlation with PPCS was not expected, as pornography consumption is often linked to risky sexual attitudes and behaviours due to the normalization of unprotected or high-risk acts portrayed in media [28, 44]. The association between sexual risk behaviours and pornography consumption is well-documented in the literature, as frequent exposure to pornography often influences individuals’ attitudes toward sex, potentially normalizing unsafe behaviours and leading to unrealistic expectations [7]. This unexpected result could stem from individual differences in how pornography influences behaviours, with factors like cultural norms, media literacy, or personal values moderating the relationship. The content type may also play a role, with certain pornography potentially promoting safer sexual practices [28]. These findings underscore the complexity of these constructs and the need for further research to explore mediating and moderating factors. Investigating these nuances will enhance theoretical models and inform interventions that address communication barriers and media influences to reduce sexual risk behaviours.

As evidence of discriminant validity, no correlations were initially expected between the ARS and EBEIC scales, however, higher levels of risky sexual behaviour (SRBS) were also associated with greater social network addiction and increased body dissatisfaction. These results highlight the complex interaction between developmental, social, and media-related factors in shaping sexual risk behaviours. They emphasize the need for more comprehensive sexual education programs in high schools, as well as enhanced university training for education and health professionals [15, 17, 45].

The strengths of this study lie in the adaptation and validation of the Sexual Risk Behaviour Scale (SRBS) for this Spanish context, effectively addressing a pressing need in psychological education and clinical practice. Our research not only addresses the limitations identified in the original study but also follows the suggested future directions. By examining score interpretations in a different cultural and linguistic context, this study expands the psychometric evidence of reliability across a broader age range, including both minors and young adults. However, given the use of a convenience sample drawn from a single geographic region and primarily comprising students from specific educational contexts, these findings must be interpreted as context-specific. While these data offer valuable preliminary insights, further studies with more diverse and representative sampling methods are required to establish broader generalization. A major strength lies in the collaboration between schools, universities, and health services to adapt and validate the SRBS. This cooperative approach facilitates a rigorous evaluation of the tool while aiming to keep it aligned with practical, real-world needs within this specific framework.

Validating the SRBS for underage students is valuable because adolescents frequently experience unique developmental, social, and environmental factors that can influence their sexual behaviours and associated risks [12, 17, 45]. Since previous research [5] has not explored the application of SRBS in this age group, it is important to evaluate whether the scale can capture the specific risks faced by underage individuals. Adolescents often face pressures, peer dynamics, and access to information that differ from those of adults, suggesting that a targeted psychometric tool could better reflect behaviours in this age group [9, 12, 17]. Validating the SRBS within this sample of underage students could contribute to a better understanding of sexual risk behaviours and support the future development of tailored interventions, educational programs, and policies. Ultimately, this exploratory step aims to provide more refined data to assist ongoing efforts in adolescent sexual health and well-being.

The Spanish adaptation of the SRBS provides a concise instrument for assessing sexual risk behaviours within the specific social landscape of this sample. Its Spanish adaptation retained the psychometric rigor of the original version while adapting its cultural relevance for the studied context, through the inclusion of all six original items, which allowed for an exploratory evaluation of nuanced risk behaviours within this specific context.

Despite the strengths of the Spanish adaptation of the SRBS, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the sample for this study was predominantly composed of high school and university students from a city of Spain, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other Spanish-speaking populations. Another limitation of this study is the reliance on self-reported data, which can be influenced by social desirability bias and recall inaccuracies. In addition, the RMSEA value for the 6-item model was above the traditionally recommended threshold. This result should be interpreted with caution, however, as in models with low degrees of freedom and a small number of items, the RMSEA is known to over-reject well-specified models [46]. Future studies could incorporate behavioural observations to supplement self-report data and provide a more comprehensive understanding of sexual risk behaviours. Furthermore, while the scores obtained in this study provided acceptable evidence of reliability and validity within this Spanish context, the generalizability of these findings to other languages and cultures requires further investigation to support the intended interpretation of scores in those settings. A critical next step in the validation process is extending the SRBS to other languages, particularly Portuguese, to assess its cross-cultural relevance. This process contributes to the validity evidence regarding the interpretations of scores across different linguistic and cultural groups, ultimately supporting the generalizability and reliability of the instrument for global sexual health assessments. Future research should investigate additional mediating variables such as family dynamics. Researchers could also explore the role of gender in shaping these behaviours in more depth, given the contradictory findings related to gender differences observed in this study. Understanding the complex interplay of these factors could lead to more tailored interventions aimed at reducing sexual risk behaviours.

Implications for practice in sexual health

The findings of this study offer potential implications for the practice, particularly in the psychology fields of sexual health, education, and counselling. First, the validation of the SRBS in this Spanish context provides clinicians, educators, and researchers with a preliminary reliable tool for assessing sexual risk behaviours. This tool can be used in clinical settings to identify individuals at higher risk for unsafe sexual practices and guide interventions aimed at promoting healthier sexual behaviours. In educational settings, the SRBS can inform the development of targeted sexual health programs that address the specific needs and risk factors of adolescents and young adults. Given the observed patterns of risky sexual behaviours within this sample, exploring the integration of the SRBS into local school and university frameworks could offer additional tools to support informed decision-making among students. Finally, the adaptation and validation of the SRBS in different languages and cultural contexts will further expand its utility in diverse populations, allowing health and education professionals to better support sexual health initiatives across various cultural settings.

Conclusion

The cross-cultural adaptation of the SRBS into this Spanish context provides initial evidence of validity and reliability for assessing sexual risk behaviours among adolescents and young adults. The SRBS scores exhibited acceptable internal consistency, with an α of 0.78. To support the psychometric validation framework, the secondary comparative analyses across age, professional, and sociodemographic groups were utilized specifically to establish known-groups validity, showing the instrument’s capacity to differentiate between theoretically distinct subgroups. It offers valuable insights into specific sexual risk behaviours within the investigated sample, helping to identify areas requiring educational attention. By establishing a reliable measurement framework, the SRBS provides a psychometric foundation for future interventions. Ultimately, this study underscores the utility of culturally adapted instruments in sexual health research, offering a validated baseline to guide future behavioural assessments in comparable student populations.
