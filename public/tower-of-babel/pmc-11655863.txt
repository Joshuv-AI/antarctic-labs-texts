Chromosome-level genome assembly of Phortica okadai, a vector of Thelazia callipaeda
Authors: Lingjun Wang, Hongsong Yu, Bo Luo, Rong Yan, Juan Zhou, Hui Liu, Ze Zhang, Wei Sun
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractPhortica okadai, known as the vector of Thelazia callipaeda in Asia, is of concern to public health in the past few decades. We generated a high-quality chromosome-level genome assembly of P. okadai with a size of 263.82 Mb and a contig N50 of 15 Mb, scaffold N50 of 47.45 Mb, combining PacBio HiFi, Illumina short-reads, and Hi-C sequencing data. Approximately 98.5% of 100 contigs could be properly ordered and oriented into 6 pseudo-chromosomes and a total of 15,052 protein-coding genes were obtained from the P. okadai genome, of which 91.76% were functionally annotated. BUSCO analysis revealed that 96.20% of 1315 predicted genes were clearly covered the conserved invertebrate genes within the database. Additionally, 302 rRNAs, 377 tRNAs, 52 miRNAs, 40 snRNAs and 21 snoRNAs were detected. In conclusion, this high-quality chromosome level reference genome will be a valuable resource for furthering the fields of ecology, genetics, evolution and functional genomics of P. okadai.Subject terms: Evolutionary developmental biology, Animal behaviour

Background & Summary

Drosophilidae is a diverse family of flies with wide variations in morphology, behavior and ecology. The family is classified into two subfamilies, Drosophilinae and Steganinae. Most Drosophilidae species are well known as ecological generalists that feed on decaying fruit and other decaying vegetation. Interestingly, as the sister to Drosophilinae, some of Steganinae species, such as Phortica okadai and Phortica variegata have evolved a clear zoophilic tendency to feed on tears or eye liquid around the eyes of humans and carnivores1,2. Animals could obtain energy, essential nutrients or salts from tears which may be beneficial for the oogenesis of female3. Moreover, the habitat of P. okadai is close to that of the human community, and the tear-feeding behavior make it could act as a vector of the zoonotic nematode Thelazia callipaeda (Spirurida, Thelaziidae) that can infect a wide range of host species, including humans and domestic mammals, causing thelaziasis4.

Previous studies have demonstrated that the olfactory system plays an important role in behaviors such as host seeking, mating, and oviposition. In mosquitoes, olfactory receptor 2 (OR2) is highly attuned to major components of human and animal odors, such as benzaldehyde and indole5. Glossina morsitans, a vector of deadly trypanosomes, exhibits chemotaxis toward 1-hexanol-3-ol, an odorant found in human emanations, through activation of GmmOr356. SfruOR47 and SlituOR9 have been demonstrated to play key roles in regulating oviposition site preference in two Spodoptera species (Spodoptera frugiperda and Spodoptera litura)7. However, there is still no evidence to explain how Steganinae species have evolved the ability to respond to mammalian tears. One possible reason is that no chromosome-level genome has been published for any species of Steganinae thus far, limiting in-depth studies on chemosensory perception.

In this study, we present a high-quality chromosome-level genome assembly for P. okadai through the combined approaches of Illumina short-read sequencing, PacBio long-read sequencing, and Hi-C technology-based analyses. The final genome size was 263.82 Mb with a contig N50 length of 15.0 Mb and a scaffold N50 length of 47.45 Mb. A total of 15,052 protein-coding genes, 302 rRNAs, 377 tRNAs, 52 miRNAs, 40 snRNAs and 21 snoRNAs were predicted in the genome of P. okadai. This high-quality P. okadai genome provides a valuable resource for further research on the biological and ecological characteristics of P. okadai holds immense importance in the development of sustainable ecological prevention and control strategies.

Methods

Sample collection and used in different sequencing strategies

The studied sample of P. okadai line was collected in 2017 from Zunyi (27 13′ 15″ N, 106 17′ 22″ E), a city in southwest of China, and then cultured in the Pathogen Biology Laboratory, Zunyi Medical University, Guizhou, for over 4 years. This line was reared with fermenting fruit (pear or apple fermented for three days) and distilled water, within a well-sealed cage (22 × 22 × 27 cm) at 28 ± 2 °C, 75% ± 10% relative humidity, and 12:12 hr light/darkness. This inbred strain was used for all genomic sequencing experiments in this study. A total of 10 newly emerged unfed and gut-removed female adults were used for genome sequencing, of which 5 adults were used for genome survey sequencing and assembly and 5 whole adults were used for Hi-C library construction and sequencing. To assist in genome annotation, individuals comprising 5 adult females and 5 adult males were selected for transcriptome sequencing. All samples were first washed three times with distilled water and anesthetized by freezing on ice for 5 minutes, followed by rapid freezing with liquid N2 and storage at −80 °C.

Library construction and sequencing

For the genome survey, genomic DNA (gDNA) was extracted by the SDS method8. The quantity of extracted DNA was measured using a Qubit 3.0 fluorometer (Invitrogen, USA) and a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and the integrity of the extracted DNA was estimated on a 0.5% agarose gel to check for any degradation. Finally, 350 bp paired-end libraries were constructed according to the standard protocol provided by Illumina and sequenced with an Illumina NovaSeq6000 with a 2 × 150 bp paired-end configuration. a total of 10.31 Gb of clean data (~43.71 × depth) were obtained (Table 1).

For long-read sequencing, genomic DNA (~8 µg) was purified and concentrated after being sheared by a G-tube (Covaris) with 20 kb settings for sequencing. Libraries were constructed as previously described for SMRT sequencing9 and sequenced with the PacBio Sequel sequencing platform. Finally, a total of 11.79 Gb of filtered PacBio sequencing data (~44.68 × depth) with an average length of 15.124 kb were used to assemble the P. okadai genome (Table 1).

Hi-C sequencing technology was used to improve draft genome assemblies and create chromosome-length scaffolds. The libraries were created from whole insects, as described in a previous study10, and sequenced with an Illumina NovaSeq6000 with a 2 × 150 bp paired-end configuration11. The construction of a Hi-C library included cross-linking of formaldehyde, restriction enzyme digestion, end repair of fragments and other steps. Finally, a total of 27.92 Gb of clean data (~94.06 × depth) were generated and used to anchor contigs in the initial assembly onto a chromosomal-scale assembly (Table 1).

For transcriptome sequencing, total RNA from the sample described above was extracted using TRIzol reagent according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Invitrogen). RNA purity was checked using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The RNA concentration was measured using a Qubit RNA Assay Kit with a Qubit 3.0 fluorometer (Invitrogen, USA). Then, the cDNA libraries were subsequently sequenced with an Illumina NovaSeq6000 according to the manufacturer’s protocols to improve contiguity and ensure the accuracy of the assembly results. All sequencing services were provided by Biomarker Technologies Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). A total of 8.84 Gb of clean data were generated and used to anchor contigs in the initial assembly onto a chromosomal-scale assembly (Table 1).

Genome size estimation

The genome size, heterozygosity rate and number of repeat sequences were estimated via 21 k-mer depth frequency distribution analysis with Jellyfish (version 2.2.6)12, and the results were interpreted via Genome Scope (version 2.0)13. Finally, the 21 k-mer analysis predicted that the genome size of P. okadai was approximately 234.57–235.83 Mb, with moderate levels of heterozygosity ranging from 0.90% to 0.98%, and approximately 39.07% of the genome was predicted to contain repetitive sequences (Table 2, Fig. 1).

Hi-C analysis and chromosome-level genome assembly

The long PacBio reads were corrected using the PacBio circular consensus sequencing (pbccs) pipeline with default parameters (https://github.com/PacificBiosciences/ccs) to assemble the draft genome scaffolds. The resulting CCS reads were subjected to hifiasm (version 0.12)14 for de novo assembly. The primary contigs were corrected using Pilon software (version 1.18)15. BWA (version 0.7.10-r789) and SAMtools (version 1.9)16 were used for read alignment and SAM/BAM format conversion. Finally, the initial genome with 309.16 Mb including 148 contigs with an N50 size of 16.20 Mb was obtained.

Chromosome-level assembly of P. okadai was conducted using Hi-C technology. The trimmed reads were truncated at the putative Hi-C junctions and then mapped to the draft assembly with BWA software (version 0.7.10-r789)17, and unique read pairs were selected for further analysis using HiC-Pro software (version 2.10.0)18. The genome contigs were first split into 50 kb segments, and then the long fragments combined with uniquely matched Hi-C data were clustered, ordered, and directed onto pseudochromosomes using LACHESIS11 with the following parameters: CLUSTER_MIN_RE_SITES = 19; CLSTER_MAX_LINK_DENSITY = 2; ORDER_MIN_N_RES_IN_TRUNK = 14; and ORDER_MIN_N_RES_IN_SHREDS = 18. Finally, Juicebox (https://github.com/aidenlab/Juicebox)19 was employed to manually adjusting error joints and construct an interaction map among chromosomes with 100 contigs. Chromosomes, gene density, GC content, and synteny blocks across the genome were drawn using Circos (version 0.69–9)20. After correction, a chromosome-level genome assembly of P. okadai was generated with a total length of 263.82 Mb containing 100 contigs (Fig. 2, Table 3). Among them, 98.5% of the contig sequences could be properly ordered and oriented into 6 pseudochromosomes (Table 4, Fig. 3). The contig N50 and scaffold N50 were 15.00 Mb and 47.45 Mb, respectively.

Gene prediction, and gene functional annotation

The protein-coding genes were detected by the combination of the following three approaches: ab initio gene prediction, homology-based prediction, and transcriptome-based prediction. For ab initio analysis-based gene prediction, Augustus (version 2.4)21 and SNAP (version 2006-07-28)22 was used. For the homology-based approach, official gene sets of four related species, Drosophila melanogaster23, Drosophila busckii24, Leucophenga varia25 and P. variegata26, were downloaded from GenBank. GeMoMa software (version 1.7)27 was used to align the protein sequences of the P. okadai genome to those of the four other species. For the transcriptome-based method, RNA-seq data from 5 female and 5 male adults were assembled against reference transcripts using HISAT (version 2.0.4)28, StringTie (version 1.2.3)29, GeneMarkS-T (version 5.1)30, Trinity (version 2.11)31, and PASA (version 2.4.1)32. All hint files identified by the three approaches were combined in the EVM pipeline (version 1.1.1)33 and polished using PASA (version 2.4.1)32. Finally, a total of 15,052 protein-coding genes (PCGs) were obtained from the P. okadai genome. Among all the predicted genes, a total of 11,370 (75.54%) were supported by transcriptome evidence, and 14,368 (95.46%) were detected as homologs in related species (Table 5 and Fig. 4).

Repeat elements and non-coding RNA predictions

The repeat sequences were identified by both homology-based and de novo prediction methods. First, for de novo prediction, the RepeatModeler2 pipeline (version 2.0.1) (https://github.com/DFAM-CONTORIUS/RepeatModeler)34 was used to analyze the repeat sequences of the genome by two algorithms, RECON (version 1.0.8)35 and RepeatScout (version 1.0.6)36. RepeatClassifier was then used to identify and cluster repetitive elements with the Dfam database (version 3.3) (http://www.dfam.org/)37. The LTR_retriever (version 2.8)38, LTRharvest (version 1.5.9)39, and LTRFINDER (version 1.1)40 software packages were then used to classify the unknown-type transposon sequences (TEs). Finally, RepeatMasker (version 4.1.0) (http://repeatmasker.org/cgi-bin/WEBRepeatMasker)41 was used to predict the TEs in the genome with the default commands according to the constructed repeat sequence database. The microsatellite identification tool MISA (version 2.1)42 and the Tandem Repeat Finder version 409 (TRF) 2 7 7 80 10 50 500 -d -hpackage43 were used to identify tandem repeat sequences in the genome. A total of 86.64 Mb of transposable elements were identified constituting 32.84% of the genome of P. okadai, which Class I Retroelement accounts for 13.32% with LTR/Gypsy accounted for the largest number (4.86%) and ClassII DNA transposon accounts for 19.52% with unclassified accounted for the largest number (11.35%). in addition, A total of 66.63 M of tandem repeats were identified constituting 25.25% of the genome, which satellite accounted for largest number (23.09%) (Table 7).

Noncoding RNAs are usually divided into several groups, including miRNAs, rRNAs, tRNAs, snoRNAs and snRNAs. tRNAscan-SE (version 1.3.1)44 was used to predict tRNAs with eukaryotic parameters. Barrnap (version 0.9)45 was used to identify rRNA genes. MiRNAs were identified by searching the miRBase database (version 21)46. The snoRNA and snRNA genes were predicted using Infernal (version 1.1)47 against the Rfam database (version 14.5)48. Finally, noncoding RNA prediction identified 302 rRNAs, 377 tRNAs, 52 miRNAs, 40 snRNAs and 21 snoRNAs.

Phylogenetic tree construction and divergence time estimation

OrthoFinder (version 2.5.1) with default parameters was used to identify orthologs among the genomes of P. okadai and the other 8 species49. The obtained gene families were annotated with the Pfam database (version 33.1)50. GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were performed using the unique gene families of P. okadai via clusterProfiler (version 3.14.0)51. The protein sequences of the single-copy orthologous genes were aligned by the MAFFT (version 7.407)52 program with the parameters -localpair -maxiterate 1000. Poorly aligned regions were removed using Gblocks (version 0.91b, −b5 = h)53. All of the remaining gene family sequences were combined to obtain a supergene. ModelFinder54, as implemented in IQTREE (version 1.6.11)55, was used to estimate the best substitution models. Finally, RAxML was used with the best-fit substitution model and 1000 bootstrap replicates to infer the maximum-likelihood tree. Aedes aegypti and Anopheles gambiae served as the outgroup for obtaining a rooted evolutionary tree. Divergence times were calculated using the MCMCTree program in Phylogenetic Analysis by Maximum Likelihood software (PAML, version 4.9i)56 with four secondary calibration points for D. melanogaster, D. busckii, Hermetia illucens, and Musca domestica obtained from the TimeTree database (http://www.timetree.org/). The graphical phylogenetic tree was constructed using MCMCTreeR (version 1.1)57. A total of 14,384 (95.56%) genes were clustered into 12,216 orthogroups using OrthoFinder software and a total of 2,965 single-copy orthologs were identified for phylogenetic inference (Table 8). The phylogenetic analyses results indicated that P. okadai is closely related to P. variegata and L. varia with P. okadai-P. variegata emerged 10.31 (2.22–22.47) million years ago (Mya), and P. okadai-L. varia emerged 68.28 (44.86–91.7) Mya. In addition, the phylogenetic tree also showed that Steganinae (containing P. okadai, P. variegata and L. varia) separated from Drosophilinae approximately 89.19 (66.07–110.65) Mya (Fig. 5).

Synteny analysis

Gene sequences from P. okadai, D. melanogaster23 and D. busckii24 were compared, and collinear blocks were determined using Diamond (version 0929130)58 with an E value < 1 × 10−5 and a C score > 0.5. The C score was filtered by JCVI software (https://github.com/tanghaibao/jcvi). MCScanX59 and the gff3 file for the genome assembly were used to determine whether similar gene pairs were adjacent on the chromosome. The maximum gap between the anchor genes was set to 15 to obtain all syntenic gene blocks, and then, the syntenic blocks were drawn with JCVI software. Clearly, P. okada as a diploid number of 12 chromosomes60, had revealed poor collinearity in general to D. busckii and D. melanogaster (Fig. 6, Table 9). The chromosome PoChr 1 was syntenic to the DmChr X and DbChr X. The PoChr 3 and PoChr 4 was syntenic to a small fragment of DmChr 2 (R, L) and DbChr 2 (R, L), PoChr 2 and PoChr 5 exhibit identical characteristics to DmChr 3 (R, L) and DbChr 3(R, L) as well.

Technical Validation

Quality assessment of the genomic material

The quality and concentration of extracted DNA/RNA were assessed using a Qubit 3.0 fluorometer (Invitrogen, USA) and a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), and their integrity was further evaluated on 0.5% agarose gel to check for any degradation.

Genome assembly assessment

The accuracy and completeness of the assembled P. okadai genome were validated using paired-end clean Illumina reads mapped to the assembly, resulting in a mapping rate of 98.22% and a mapping coverage of 93.25% (Table 10). Furthermore, the clean PacBio subreads were also mapped to the assembly, resulting in a mapping rate of 98.86%. Based on ORTHODB version 4 of Insecta, BUSCO analysis showed that 97.59% of the conserved genes belonged to complete and single-copy, complete and duplicated, fragmented, and missing categories, which accounted for 1,323 (96.78%), 11 (0.80%), 2 (0.15%), and 31 (2.27%) of the total genes, respectively (Table 10). Compared to the scaffold-level assembly, the genome size was reduced and became more approximated to the estimated genome size. All these results indicated that the well-organized chromosome-level assembly of P. okadai was of high quality in this study.
