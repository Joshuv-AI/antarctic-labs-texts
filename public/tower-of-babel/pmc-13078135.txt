Racial Disparities in Weather‐Related Mortality in Virginia
Authors: Pamela B. DeGuzman, Wendy M. Novicoff, Gabriel Ramos, Melanie M. Pane, Murphy C. Johnson, Patrick C. Roney, Hannah V. Leigh, William Basener, Amber L. Curran, Bryan DeMarcy, Jungyun Jang, Christian Schroeder, Robert E. Davis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTo examine racial disparities in weather‐related mortality in Virginia from 2005 to 2020. An ecological descriptive study using daily mortality data from the Virginia Department of Health and weather data from the National Climatic Data Center. Generalized additive models and distributed lag nonlinear models were used to estimate the relative risk of mortality as the primary endpoint associated with temperature extremes over a 21‐day lag period, stratified by race. Black residents of the state had a higher risk of dying at both high and low temperatures compared to white residents; however, the risk was more profound with low temperatures. On the coldest days, the mortality risk for the Black population was more than three times that of the white population. Notably, the impact of cold on the Black population extended through lag day 15, while for white people, the impact only lasted through lag day 5. Heat‐related mortality risk for Black individuals also exceeded that for white individuals, but only when the minimum temperature exceeded 20°C. Racial disparities exist in weather‐related mortality in Virginia, with the Black population experiencing a disproportionately higher risk of death as well as poorer health outcomes, especially during extreme cold weather events. Policymakers should consider developing and evaluating policies that protect vulnerable communities when they are subject to weather extremes.

Introduction

Weather‐related mortality in the United States is an increasing public health concern, with about 2000 deaths annually (Berko et al., 2006). The National Safety Council (2025) reports that over the last 5 years, weather‐related deaths in the U.S. have risen by 20% from 2019 levels, and the threat of weather‐related mortality is increasing as climate change alters weather patterns globally. However, not all weather‐related deaths occur equally across racial groups: Black Americans are disproportionally impacted, dying from heat or cold exposure at approximately twice the rate of white Americans (Berko et al., 2006; Sharpe & Wolkin, 2022).

The circumstances in which people live (i.e., location, poverty, housing quality) drive differential exposure to extremes in both hot and cold weather. For example, 75% of Black Americans live in an urban or suburban community, placing them at increased risk of the urban heat‐island mortality effect (Hsu et al., 2021). Black Americans are also overrepresented in urban homeless populations, with Black people accounting for nearly 40% of all unhoused individuals (National Alliance to End Homelessness, 2023). A confluence of historic events and racist policies dating back to the 19th century led to the segregation of Black Americans in U.S. urban centers. In response to the Great Migration—a large movement of Black Americans from the rural South to northern U.S. cities—white residents and policy makers implemented restrictive housing policies to limit Black families' access to predominantly white neighborhoods (Logan et al., 2015). The quality of housing in these neighborhoods today — including access to efficient air conditioning— significantly impacts risk of weather exposure. Between 1960 and 2004, a U.S. study found a 75% reduction in heat‐related mortality associated with increased access to residential air conditioning (Barreca et al., 2016). In addition, Black Americans are more likely to live in multigenerational housing, (Cohn & Passel, 2020). While multigenerational living itself can be a source of economic and social support, when protective mechanisms such as reliable air conditioning or efficient heating systems are absent, this arrangement can result in a greater number of people exposed to unsafe conditions. Although many individuals may have adequate protective measures in place within their homes, Black and other historically marginalized individuals are more likely to have to work outside of the home, with fewer than 20% of employed Black individuals across the United States working from home (Gould & Sheirholz, 2020), thus also increasing their exposure to outdoor conditions.

Differences in housing, energy, and poverty‐related policies, as well as variation in weather extremes across the U.S., translate to weather‐related mortality that varies regionally. As such, area‐level policies that mediate risk can be aided through an understanding of regional and state‐level data. Virginia is an ideal location to study the impacts of cold and heat waves on racial disparities in weather‐related mortality, as it is characterized by a wide variation in temperature and a racially and socioeconomically diverse population. Located in the mid‐Atlantic region, Virginia has over 8.6 million residents, making it the 12th most populous state. Virginia has a higher percentage of Black/African Americans (20.0%) than the U.S. overall (13.6%; US Census Bureau, 2021). Black Virginians experience poverty at rates that are nearly two times that of their white counterparts, with 21.3% of Black Virginians living at 125% of the poverty level or below, compared with just 10.7% of white Virginians (U.S. Census Bureau, 2021). Furthermore, Virginia has a history of redlining. These discriminatory housing practices were responsible for Black residents persistently living in areas that are currently more vulnerable to environmental impacts (Li & Yuan, 2022).

In this research, we present a comprehensive examination of the risk of heat‐ and cold‐related mortality in the Commonwealth of Virginia from 2005 to 2020, stratified by race. To our knowledge, there has been no prior statewide study of racial disparities in weather‐related mortality in Virginia based on a data set that was this extensive over both time and space. By analyzing detailed multi‐year individual‐level mortality with regional climate data, this study provides a comprehensive examination of climate‐related racial disparities in mortality in Virginia and may offer insight into how structural factors have influenced health inequities in the state.

Methods

A descriptive ecological study design was used to evaluate mortality risk by race in Virginia. Daily mortality data were acquired from the death certificate records compiled from the Virginia Department of Health Interjurisdictional Exchange Mortality Files from 1 January 2005, through 31 December 2020. Individual records include the decedent's date of death, place of residence and death (by county or independent city), primary and underlying cause(s) of death (reported as ICD 10 diagnosis codes), and demographic information, including race (determined by what was presented on the death certificate). Because of low numbers of non‐white and non‐Black people in Virginia, we classify all other races as “other race” in the sample description and exclude them from further analysis. Across the state, there are an average of 5 non‐white/non‐Black deaths daily over our period of record. Non‐Virginia residents were also excluded, as were those with no recorded place of residence. We mapped the primary cause of death as listed on the death certificate to Major Diagnostic Categories using the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services ICD‐10‐CM/PCS MS‐DRG v 41.0 Definitions Manual (Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services, 2015).

Virginia was divided into 12 climatically homogeneous regions (i.e., to ensure similar weather conditions in each area), and each decedent was assigned to a region based on their county or city of residence (Figure 1). Weather data were acquired from archived files maintained by the National Climatic Data Center, with one weather station associated with each region (Figure 1). After an extensive, iterative variable fitting procedure, the primary weather variable selected for this study was the daily minimum temperature (See Table S1 and S2 and Figures S1 and S2 in Supporting Information S1).

Virginia was divided into 12 regions, each of which consists of multiple counties and independent cities, with one first‐order weather station (as indicated by the 3‐digit station code) selected to represent the climate of that region. To scale local relationships up to the statewide level, we computed a mortality‐weighted average value of minimum temperature for each day. In this way, the temperatures from those areas of the state with higher mortality on a given day have more influence on the daily average than temperatures from regions with lower death counts (Figure 1). We initially intended to examine spatial variability in mortality‐temperature relationships, but the small daily sample sizes in many regions made the models unstable and the fitting process (described in the subsequent text) unreliable. This problem was exacerbated by the relative lack of observations at the low and high portions of the minimum temperature distribution, where most of the mortality impacts were observed. We therefore decided to examine the more robust daily samples in a statewide analysis in which we biased the temperature observations toward those locations with higher mortality counts.

We associated weather on the day of death and the prior 21 days using generalized additive models (GAMs). In these models, the daily temporal variation of mortality is associated with a series of potential predictor variables, much like in multiple regression. The dependent variable (mortality) is linked to the predictors via a (natural) log link function, and a trend term is modeled using a natural cubic spline with a fixed number of degrees of freedom per year to account for the long‐term mortality trend and seasonal variability. As count data are often Poisson distributed, the mortality data are thus normalized via a log transformation. The residual variance can be modeled by a combination of continuous and nominal variables.

Specifically, we fit the following model: Yt=a+S(trend,16∗3)+bMinT(3)+cDOWt where Y is the daily mortality count on day t, a is the y‐intercept, S is a natural cubic spline fit with 3 degrees of freedom for each of the 16 years, MinT is the daily minimum temperature, DOW is a nominal variable for day‐of‐week, and b and c are fitted coefficient vectors. Mortality, the dependent variable, is associated with the predictor variables via a quasi‐Poisson link function, which is used to address the overdispersion commonly found with count data. To select the annual degrees of freedom, we ran a simple GAM with the trend term as the lone predictor and sequentially increased the degrees of freedom. Our selection of 3° of freedom per year provided the simplest fitting of the seasonality and trend without overfitting (adding degrees of freedom that had little impact on the model fit). We selected this model after extensive consideration of different climate predictors (See Table S1 and S2 and Figures S1 and S2 in Supporting Information S1). The primary result of this model is a plot of the relative risk of mortality as a function of minimum temperature over the 22‐day period up to and including the date of death.

Typically, some time period elapses between the triggering weather event and the subsequent mortality. This time lag will vary based on the initial health of the decedent, the latency period and nature of the specific disease, and many other factors. To examine lag effects specifically, we use the same approach in a distributed lag nonlinear model (DLNM), where we can now explore the relative risks of mortality as a function of both temperature and lag. In these models, the minimum temperature is fitted with quadratic splines with two equally spaced knots, and in the lag dimension, natural cubic splines are fitted with two equally spaced knots in natural log space. The result of this model is a heat map of relative risk as a function of temperature and lag.

DLNMs are among the most commonly used statistical tools in modeling the associations between climate and human health (Bao et al., 2016; Gasparrini et al., 2015; Yang et al., 2012). We performed extensive model testing in which we varied the lag period, number and placement of knots, type of splines, degrees of freedom, etc. With respect to the lag, we ultimately selected a 21‐day lag period to capture the longest likely lag that might be expected between infection and death from an infectious disease (like influenza) in the cold season, when lags tend to be longer (Bao et al., 2016; Gasparrini et al., 2015). All analyses were conducted in R Studio using the “mgcv” and “dlnm” libraries (RStudio Team, 2023). For details of these methods, please refer to Gasparrini (2011). The code is available on Github (De Guzman et al., 2025).

This work was conducted with approval from the University of Virginia Institutional Review Board for Health Sciences Research.

Results

Over our 16‐year period of record, we documented 985,072 mortalities, or 169 per day, of which 130 were white deaths and 34 were Black deaths. The demographics of the sample are presented in Table 1. Deaths during this period were evenly split by gender, with 55% of the mortality consisting of individuals 75 years and older. The most common cause of death was related to circulatory system disease (26%), followed by respiratory system disease (17%). Black men were slightly more likely to die during this time period than Black women (51% compared to 49%), and Black individuals were more likely than white individuals to die at younger ages. For example, 23% of Black individuals died at age 50–64 compared with just 16% of white individuals. Of note, respiratory‐related deaths were more common in white than Black individuals (18% vs. 14%).

Figure 2 presents the relative risk of mortality in Virginia as a function of mortality‐weighted minimum temperature over a 21‐day lag period by race. In general, mortality increases for both races when minimum temperatures are below the median. Although the shape of the response curve for Black and white Virginians is similar, the magnitudes differ. When the minimum temperature was freezing (0°C), the relative risk was similar for both groups (1.03 for Black people and 1.04 for white people) relative to a minimum temperature of 5°C. However, as days got colder, there was a more significant net mortality impact on the Black population. For example, at −10°C, the relative risk of mortality for white people is comparable to the risk at freezing (1.02), whereas the relative risk of death for Black people has increased to 1.13. As minimum temperatures decline, the difference in mortality risk between the white and Black populations tends to increase. This difference in risk approaches 12% for minimum temperatures at or below −16°C, although it is important to note that these events are rare.

Relative risk of mortality in Virginia as a function of mortality‐weighted minimum temperature (°C) over a 21‐day lag period (date of death and subsequent 21 days) for (a) white and (b) Black populations. The plot is centered at the median value of minimum temperature. 95% confidence intervals are presented in gray shading.

The mortality impact of high minimum temperatures is muted compared to that of low temperatures. In fact, for the white population in Virginia, high minimum temperatures are protective, with a decline in mortality risk of 7% compared to the median (at 25°C, RR = 0.93 [0.87, 1.00]). Conversely, there is no evidence of a similar protective effect for Black residents, and the relative risk of death was reasonably constant when minimum temperatures exceeded the median value.

These mortality differences by race are more evident when examined as a function of lag days (Figure 3). At short lags (0–2 days), minimum temperatures in the 0°C to −10°C range were slightly protective (e.g., at lag 0 and –7°C, RR = 0.98 [1.00, 0.96] and RR = 0.98 [0.99, 0.97] for Blacks and whites, respectively). But at lag days 3–6 for that same temperature range, the peak mortality risk for Black people exceeded that for white people. For example, at −10°C and lag 4, the relative risk of mortality for Black people is double that of white people (1.02 vs. 1.01). This difference accounts for the higher relative risk for Black people over the entire 3‐week lag period shown in Figure 2. The highest cold‐related mortality for both groups occurs when weighted average minimum temperatures are below −12°C. On these rare days, the maximum relative risk is 1.03 for white people and 1.10 for Black people. Therefore, on the coldest days in Virginia, the risk of mortality is three times higher in the Black population than in the white population.

Relative risk of mortality as a function of mortality‐weighted minimum temperature (°C) and lag (days) over a 21‐day lag period for (a) white and (b) Black populations in Virginia. Mortality risks also differ on warm days but are more muted by comparison. At short lags, the heat‐related risk at 20°C is 1.02 (1.01, 1.03) for white people and 1.04 (1.01, 1.06) for Black people at lag 0. Both populations exhibit a comparable 2% decline in risk (as compared to the median temperature) about 3–7 days after a warm event.

Discussion

We found that although the overall response patterns to temperatures were similar between the Black and white populations in Virginia from 2005 to 2020, the magnitude of the mortality risk was greater for the Black population. On the coldest days, the overall relative risk of Black mortality was 25% higher than the risk for a minimum temperature of 5°C, whereas the comparable maximum risk for white people was 10%. Furthermore, over a 21‐day lag period, heat proved to be slightly protective for the white population (i.e., relative risk is slightly lower than 1.0). In contrast, no protective effect was evident for the Black population.

On the coldest days, the mortality risk for the Black population was higher than that of the white population in Virginia, and the impact on the Black population extended through lag day 15. In contrast, for white people the impact only lasted through lag day 5. However, even when minimum temperatures were below freezing but not exceptionally low (through approximately −10°C), mortality risk peaked 3–6 days later, and the mortality risk for the Black population was higher than that of the white population. Given the rarity of these extremely cold days, these relative risks are not statistically significant.

Heat impacts were found to be acute for both populations (i.e., lags of up to 2 days). There was a reduction in mortality from about 2 to 10 days after heat events that reflects mortality displacement, or the forward advancement in the death date of vulnerable individuals from heat‐related stress, leaving behind a less‐susceptible population for a short period (several days) following the heat event. The mortality displacement effect was comparable for both populations (relative risk = 0.98), but heat‐related mortality risk for Black individuals exceeded that for white individuals when the minimum temperature exceeded 20°C.

Our heat‐mortality findings are similar to a prior study conducted by Lee and Dessler (2023) in the Southeastern U.S., which indicated a higher risk of mortality to extreme heat for Black individuals. This may be due to the infrequency of cold days across much of the Southeastern U.S. In Lee and Dessler's study, there was no higher risk of cold‐related mortality for Black people, which reflects findings from other prior research. Keivabu et al. (2024) did not find a difference among racial groups with respect to mortality on cold days; however, their analysis only evaluated the top percentile of coldest days in a region. Our analysis of the daily minimal temperatures allowed us to analyze data across a broader spectrum. Furthermore, our population‐based analysis of one state's data allowed us to focus on state‐level effects, unlike studies that may blur these findings by combining data over a larger geographic area.

Implications for Public Health Policy

Virginia policymakers should be aware that Black people die at a higher rate than white people at both high and low temperature extremes and enact policies that address these disparities. Given the differences in socioeconomic factors between Black and white Virginia residents, as well as some of its cities' history of redlining, this disparity may stem from factors that limit access to protective measures, such as air conditioning, quality housing, and adequate heating systems (see Introduction). Housing in historically redlined areas of Virginia is older and in greater disrepair than in other comparable Virginia neighborhoods (Virginia Housing Alliance, 2023; Virginia Housing & Virginia Department of Housing and Community Development, 2022). Targeting existing state resources that assist low‐income residents with weatherization and HVAC modernization toward those previously redlined communities may help reduce existing disparities in weather‐related morality.

It is important to note that mortality overall is higher during extreme cold weather than extreme heat, a phenomenon not unique to Virginia (Lee & Dessler, 2023); however, policymakers may be unaware of the higher mortality risk during cold extremes. In recent years, occurrences of extreme heat have received far more media coverage than extreme cold, which may alter perceptions of the disproportionate impacts on mortality risk (Pianta & Sisco, 2020). Heat extremes are also more studied due to their direct and immediate impact (Tong et al., 2021). In fact, given the recent increase in the frequency of extreme cold nights across much of the Southeastern United States, including Virginia (Fall et al., 2021), disparities in cold‐related mortalities should be noted by policymakers, who may want to consider mechanisms for providing protective effects for vulnerable populations, such as weather alerts and assistance for energy costs during extreme temperatures.

The mortality impacts of policies that impact housing, environmental, and energy equity can be evaluated using an annual re‐evaluation of mortality data. A recent change to Virginia law prevents utilities from shutting off residents' power for non‐payment when temperatures are at/below 32°F or at/above 92°F (Srirahman, 2024). The results of this analysis should be compared to post‐2024 data to understand policy impact on human health and health disparities.

Limitations

As with many large data set studies, we were limited in our ability to evaluate differences in causative factors driving these disparities. Our analysis of causes of death during the hottest and coldest days did not show any differences by race. The database contained no socioeconomic variables, and only county or city of residence was included, limiting our ability to reliably estimate socioeconomic drivers of disparities in this highly diverse state.

We initially intended to examine spatial variability in mortality disparities across Virginia. However, given the low daily mortality totals in many regions of the state, the statistical models were found to be unstable. Thus, we do not explicitly examine spatial variations in either mortality or climate in this study. As data are available to us at the county level, we used a single weather station to represent each of 12 multi‐county regions. Although this is a source of some error, other studies have shown that this approach provides reasonable risk estimates compared to spatially explicit models in locations where temperature variability is not extreme (Guo et al., 2013). In Virginia, the most likely source of error would be in the less populated mountainous regions in the western parts of the state.

Conclusion

Over a 16‐year period in Virginia, Black residents have a higher risk of dying at both high and low temperatures compared to white residents. Notably, the relative risk to Black Virginians increased as days got colder while extreme heat was protective to white residents. Given the historic events that impacted Black residents' access to equitable housing, policymakers should consider prioritizing, developing and evaluating housing and utility policies that help protect communities at risk.
