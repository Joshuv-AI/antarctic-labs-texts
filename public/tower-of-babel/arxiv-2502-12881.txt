Separation of time scales in weakly interacting diffusions
Zachary P. Adams, Maximilian Engel, Rishabh S. Gvalani

Abstract
. We study metastable behaviour in systems of weakly interacting Brownian particles with localised, attractive potentials which are smooth and globally bounded. In this particular setting, numerical evidence suggests that the particles converge on a short time scale to a “droplet state” which is metastable , i.e. persists on a much longer time scale than the time scale of convergence, before eventually diffusing to 0 0 . In this article, we provide rigorous evidence and a quantitative characterisation of this separation of time scales. Working at the level of the empirical measure, we show that (after quotienting out the motion of the centre of mass) the rate of convergence to the quasi-stationary distribution, which corresponds with the droplet state, is O ⁡ ( 1 ) O(1) as the inverse temperature β → ∞ \beta\to\infty . Meanwhile the rate of leakage away from its centre of mass is O ⁡ ( e − β ) O(e^{-\beta}) . Futhermore, the quasi-stationary distribution is localised on a length scale of order O ⁡ ( β − 1 2 ) O(\beta^{-\frac{1}{2}}) . We thus provide a partial answer to a question posed by Carrillo, Craig, and Yao (see [ CCY19 , Section 3.2.2] ) in the microscopic setting.



1.
Introduction

Consider the following system of weakly interacting diffusions

(1.1)

\mathrm{d}X_{t}^{i}=-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\nabla W(X_{t}^{i}-X_{t}^{j})\,\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2\beta^{-1}}\mathrm{d}B_{t}^{i}\,,

where
(X_{t}^{i})_{i=1}^{N}
,
N\in\mathbb{N}
, represent the positions of
N
exchangeable particles (or agents or spins), taking values in some smooth manifold
\Omega
,
W
is a smooth even interaction potential with well-behaved growth properties, and
(B_{t}^{i})_{i=1}^{N}
are
N
independent Brownian motions.

Depending on the choice of the interaction potential
W
, the system in (
1.1
) can exhibit a wide variety of interesting dynamical phenomena. We are particularly interested in the case in which
W
is attractive (i.e.
\nabla W(x)\cdot x\geq 0
), globally bounded, and has smooth and globally bounded derivatives of all orders. In this situation the pairwise attraction mediated by
W
competes with the diffusive behaviour produced by the independent Brownian motions. This competition is most easily observed at the level of the thermodynamic limit
N\to\infty
when
\Omega
is bounded. Consider the empirical measure
\mu^{N}_{t}
of the system (
1.1
), which is defined as

(1.2)

\displaystyle\mu^{N}_{t}\coloneqq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{X_{t}^{i}}\,.

Then, assuming
W
is sufficiently well-behaved, it is well-known (see, for example,
[
Szn91
]
) that we have the estimate

(1.3)

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\mathbb{E}\left[d_{2}^{2}(\mu_{t},\mu_{t}^{N})\right]\lesssim\frac{1}{N}\,,

for any
T<\infty
, where
d_{2}(\cdot,\cdot)
is the
2
-Wasserstein distance and
\mu_{t}
is the unique distributional solution of the nonlocal parabolic PDE

(1.4)

\partial_{t}\mu_{t}=\beta^{-1}\Delta\mu_{t}+\nabla\cdot(\mu_{t}\nabla W*\mu_{t})\,,

often referred to as the McKean–Vlasov equation. The above PDE can exhibit the phenomenon of phase transitions (see
[
CGPS20
,
CP10
]
): for
\beta
sufficiently small, equation (
1.4
) has a unique steady state (in the space of probability measures) while for
\beta
sufficiently large, equation (
1.4
) possesses multiple steady states. This switch between one and multiple steady states for (
1.4
) is caused exactly by the aforementioned competition between the diffusive behaviour produced by the Laplacian, which dominates in the
\beta\ll 1
regime and the tendency to aggregate due to the nonlocal drift, which prevails in the
\beta\gg 1
regime.

The setting: bounded potential on unbounded domain

In this article, we are interested in the setting of
\Omega=\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Since
W

1.4

[

.

Even though (
1.4

[

, wherein solutions of a dissipative dynamical system converges on a fast time scale to a submanifold of its state space, along which the time evolution is slow.

Absence of invariant probability measure for the particle system

We are studying this separation of time scales as it shows up in the corresponding particle system. In analogy to the absence of steady states for the thermodynamic limit, the particle system (
1.1
) does not have an invariant probability measure under our assumptions. To see this, consider the Fokker–Planck equation associated to the law
\rho_{t}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x
of (
1.1
),

(1.5)

\partial_{t}\rho_{t}=\beta^{-1}\Delta\rho_{t}+\nabla\cdot(\rho_{t}\nabla H_{N})\,,

where the Hamiltonian
H_{N}:(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{N}\to\mathbb{R}
is given by

(1.6)

H_{N}(x)\coloneqq\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}W(x_{i}-x_{j})\,.

We define
f_{t}\coloneqq\rho_{t}e^{\beta H_{N}}
and observe that it solves the following equation

(1.7)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}f_{t}=

\displaystyle\,\beta^{-1}\Delta f_{t}-\nabla H_{N}\cdot\nabla f_{t}

(1.8)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,\beta^{-1}e^{\beta H_{N}}\nabla\cdot(e^{-\beta H_{N}}\nabla f_{t})\,.

Multiplying by
f_{t}
and integrating by parts against the measure
e^{-\beta H_{N}(x)}\,\mathrm{d}x
, we obtain the estimate

(1.9)

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{N}}|f_{t}|^{2}e^{-\beta H_{N}(x)}\,\mathrm{d}x=-2\beta^{-1}\int_{(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{N}}|\nabla f_{t}|^{2}e^{-\beta H_{N}(x)}\,\mathrm{d}x\,.

We now use the fact that
W
is globally bounded by some constant
K<\infty
along with the Nash inequality
[
Nas58
]
to assert that there exists a constant
C=C(N,d,K,\beta)
such that

(1.10)

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{N}}|f_{t}|^{2}e^{-\beta H_{N}(x)}\,\mathrm{d}x\leq-C\left[\int_{(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{N}}|f_{t}|^{2}e^{-\beta H_{N}(x)}\,\mathrm{d}x\right]^{\frac{Nd+2}{Nd}}\,.

Applying Grönwall’s inequality, rewriting in terms of
\rho_{t}
, and using the global boundedness of
W
again, we see that

(1.11)

\int_{(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{N}}|\rho_{t}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\lesssim(1+t)^{-\frac{Nd}{2}}\,,

which contradicts the existence of an invariant probability measure.

Phenomenology of the particle system

In the absence of an invariant probability measure, one observes a similar dynamical picture as that seen for the thermodynamic limit: the particles almost instantaneously converge to a droplet state in which they are all close to their collective centre of mass; a configuration which is analogous to the droplet state observed in the thermodynamic limit. This configuration persists for a long time before eventually dissipating, similar to the slow motion regime observed in the
N\to\infty
limit. We refer the reader to
Fig.

2
where we have demonstrated this phenomenon for a particular choice of
W
in dimension
d=2
.

.

The simulations are performed with
W=1-e^{-|x|^{2}}
and
\beta=0.3
.

A key difference between the particle system and the limit is that the centre of mass of the particles is not conserved while that of the limit
\mu_{t}
is, i.e.
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}x\mu_{t}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}x\mu_{0}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x
for all times
t\geq 0
. In contrast, one can check that the centre of mass of the particles satisfies

(1.12)

\displaystyle\bar{X}_{t}^{N}\coloneqq

\displaystyle\,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}x\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{t}^{N}

(1.13)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}X_{t}^{i}=\frac{\sqrt{2\beta^{-1}}}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}B_{t}^{i}\,,

where we have used the fact that
W
is even. Thus,
\bar{X}_{t}^{N}
performs Brownian motion with variance of order
N^{-1}
, and this motion vanishes in the limit. However, since we are only interested in the distance of the particles from their centre of mass and not the motion of the centre of mass itself, we quotient out this random drift. This quotienting procedure is explained in more detail in
Section

2
.

Based on these observations, we study the separation of time scales for the particle system (
1.1
) instead of the PDE (
1.4
).
The reason for this is two-fold.
Firstly, our approach to the problem involves studying the spectrum of the generator of the interacting particle system (
1.1
).
As this generator is linear, it can be tackled using standard tools from spectral theory (as opposed to the limit (
1.4
), which is nonlinear).
Secondly, if one views the interacting particle system (
1.1
) as representing the “ground truth” and the limit (
1.4
) only as an approximation, studying the time scale separation on this level is more appropriate.

Main result

We now present a heuristic version of the main result of this paper. Let
\mu_{t}^{N}
denote the empirical measure of the system of SDEs (
1.1
) (after quotienting out the centre of mass
\bar{X}_{t}^{N}
), and let
\bar{\mu}_{N}
denote the empirical measure of the droplet configuration described above (also after quotienting out
\bar{X}^{N}_{t}
).
Assume that
W
satisfies the previous boundedness assumptions and is attractive.
Furthermore, assume that
\beta\gg 1
, and that the particles are sufficiently localised (in a sense that we make more precise later on).

Theorem 1.1
.

For any
N\in\mathbb{N}
and large
\beta\gg 1
, there exist constants
\lambda_{1,\beta,N}>0
and
\lambda_{2,\beta,N}>0
such that
\mu_{t}^{N}
converges to the random droplet configuration
\bar{\mu}^{N}
on a time scale of order
\lambda_{2,\beta,N}^{-1}
,
while the leakage from the droplet state occurs on a time scale of order
\lambda_{1,\beta,N}^{-1}
, with

(1.14)

\displaystyle\lim_{\beta\to+\infty}\lambda_{1,\beta,N}=0,\qquad\lim_{\beta\to+\infty}\lambda_{2,\beta,N}=\lambda_{2,N}>0\,.

Moreover, the limit
\lambda_{2,N}
is bounded away from
0
uniformly in
N
.
Finally,
\bar{\mu}^{N}
is localised on a length scale of order
\beta^{-\frac{1}{2}}
, i.e.

(1.15)

\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(x-\int\bar{x}\,\mathrm{d}\bar{\mu}^{N}(\bar{x}))^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\bar{\mu}^{N}(x)\right]\leq\beta^{-1}\,.

The mathematically precise version of the above result can be found in
Section

2.7
. The proof relies on studying the system (
1.1
) on a bounded domain with killing and using the theory of quasi-stationary distributions (QSDs) (introductions to which can be found in
[
CMSM13
]
or
[
CV16
,
CV23
]
). The quantities
\lambda_{1,\beta},\lambda_{2,\beta}
then correspond to the top two eigenvalues of the generator of the killed process. Furthermore, the droplet configuration mentioned in
Eq.

1.15
is exactly the empirical measure of points which are sampled from the quasi-stationary distribution of the system.

Structure of the paper

Section 2 gives an overview of the notation, techniques, and results of this paper. In Section 2.1, we specify the assumptions on the interaction potential
W
and, in Section 2.2, we introduce the setting of the projected dynamics after quotienting out the motion of the centre of mass.
After introducing a more precise definition of the droplet state
\bar{\mu}^{N}
in Section 2.3, we introduce the (sub-)Markovian semigroups and their generators used in our setting in Section 2.4. The sub-Markovian part concerns the situation where trajectories hitting the boundary of the domain of interest are disregarded, and conditioning on survival within this domain is considered. This leads to the notion of a quasi-stationary distribution in Section 2.5 that helps to describe the droplet state and convergence to it.
In Section 2.6 our main results are stated: Theorem 2.2 describes the rate of escape from the droplet state as exponentially small in the inverse temperature
\beta
as
\beta\to\infty
, while Theorem 2.5 yields a precise description of the
\beta\to\infty
-convergence for higher order eigenvalues; in particular, this yields an
\mathcal{O}(1)
rate for the convergence to the QSD and the associated droplet state.
Theorem 2.6 then translates these spectral results into a precise characterisation of the droplet state and convergence to it, along with leakage from the droplet state, occurring on different time scales. In the following, Section 3 contains the proof of Theorem 2.2, Section 4 gives the proof of Theorem 2.5 and Section 5 is dedicated to proving Theorem 2.6.

2.1.
Assumptions on the potential
W

It is clear from the discussion in the introduction that a result of the form we are searching for cannot possibly be true for all forms of
W
. The potential
W
should be locally attractive at a minimum, to allow the formation of a droplet, but at the same time its gradient should decay near infinity, to allow particles to escape the droplet. The assumption below captures these restrictions on
W
.

Assumption 2.1
.

The interaction potential
W
is radial, with
W(x)\coloneqq w(|x|)
for some
w:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
which is bounded, even and smooth with
w^{\prime}
being a Schwartz function. Finally, we assume that
w(0)=w^{\prime}(0)=0
,
w^{\prime\prime}(0)>-\min_{\mathbb{R}}w^{\prime\prime}>0
, and
w^{\prime}(x)>0
for
x>0
.

It follows from the above assumption that
W
is
\lambda
-convex, i.e.

(2.5)

W((1-t)x+ty)\leq(1-t)W(x)+tW(y)-\frac{\lambda}{2}t(1-t)|x-y|^{2}

for all
x,y,\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
t\in[0,1]
, with

(2.6)

\lambda=\inf_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\inf\left\{\kappa:\kappa\in\spec((D^{2}W)(x))\right\}\,,

where
\spec(A)
denotes the spectrum of the matrix
A
. Note that we necessarily have
\lambda<0
, since if
\lambda\geq 0
, we would have that
W
is a bounded and convex function and thereby constant, which it is not. The following form of
\lambda
-convexity is needed for our arguments

(2.7)

W\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}x_{i}\right)\leq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}W\left(x_{i}\right)+\frac{\lambda(1-N)}{2N^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}|x_{i}|^{2}\,,

for any collection of points
(x_{i})_{i=1,\dots,N}\subset\Omega
. The above inequality is a straightforward consequence of (
2.5
).

2.2.
Projected dynamics

We now describe the quotienting procedure alluded to in the introduction. In the following, we set
\Omega=\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
As discussed earlier, we are interested in the relative positions of the particles, and would like to disregard the motion of their centre of mass. To this end, we define the following linear subspaces

(2.8)

\displaystyle\Gamma_{N}\coloneqq

\displaystyle\,\left\{x\in\Omega^{N}:x_{i}=c\in\Omega,\,\forall i=1,\dots,N\right\},

(2.9)

\displaystyle\Gamma_{N}^{\perp}\coloneqq

\displaystyle\,\left\{x\in\Omega^{N}:\sum_{i=1}^{N}x_{i}=0\right\}\,.

Furthermore, we define
\Pi_{N}:\Omega^{N}\to\Gamma_{N}^{\perp}
to be the orthogonal projection onto
\Gamma_{N}^{\perp}
.
Let
X_{t}=(X_{t}^{1},\dots,X_{t}^{N})
denote the strong solution to the system of SDEs in (
1.1
). Then, we define

(2.10)

Y_{t}\coloneqq\Pi_{N}\,X_{t}\,.

Since
X_{t}
is a solution of the SDE

(2.11)

\mathrm{d}X_{t}=-\nabla H_{N}(X_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2\beta^{-1}}\mathrm{d}B_{t}\,,

where
B_{t}=(B_{t}^{1},\dots,B_{t}^{N})
, it follows that
Y_{t}
is a solution of

(2.12)

\mathrm{d}Y_{t}=-\Pi_{N}\,\nabla H_{N}(X_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2\beta^{-1}}\mathrm{d}(\Pi_{N}\,B_{t})\,.

We now note that, since
\Pi_{N}
is orthogonal,
\bar{B_{t}}\coloneqq\Pi_{N}\,B_{t}
is a standard
(N-1)d
-dimensional Brownian motion on
\Gamma_{N}^{\perp}
. Furthermore, since
(\nabla H_{N})(x)=(\nabla H_{N})(\Pi_{N}\,x)
we have that
\Pi_{N}\,\nabla H_{N}(X_{t})=\bar{\nabla}U_{N}(Y_{t})
, where
\bar{\nabla}
is the Euclidean gradient on
\Gamma_{N}^{\perp}
and
U_{N}
is the restriction of
H_{N}
to
\Gamma_{N}^{\perp}
. To see this, note that for any
y\in\Gamma_{N}^{\perp},v\in T_{y}\Gamma_{N}^{\perp}

(2.13)

\displaystyle\langle\bar{\nabla}U_{N}(y),v\rangle\,\ =

\displaystyle\,\left.\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}U_{N}(y+\varepsilon v)\right|_{\varepsilon=0}

(2.14)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,\left.\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}H_{N}(y+\varepsilon v)\right|_{\varepsilon=0}\,=\,\langle\nabla H_{N}(y),v\rangle\,,

where we have used the fact that the metric on
\Gamma_{N}^{\perp}
is the restriction of the metric on
\mathbb{R}^{Nd}
. Using the fact that projections are idempotent and self-adjoint, we have that

(2.15)

\langle\bar{\nabla}U_{N}(y),v\rangle=\langle\Pi_{N}\,\nabla H_{N}(y),v\rangle\,.

Thus, we have that
Y_{t}
solves the SDE

(2.16)

dY_{t}=-\bar{\nabla}U_{N}(Y_{t})\,dt+\sqrt{2\beta^{-1}}d\bar{B}_{t}\,,

implying that

is also a Markov process. Additionally, the positive measures
{\mu}_{N,X}\in\mathcal{M}_{+}(\Omega^{N})
and
{\mu}_{N,Y}\in\mathcal{M}_{+}(\Gamma_{N}^{\perp})
, given by

(2.17)

\mu_{N,X}(\mathrm{d}x)\coloneqq\exp(-\beta H_{N}(x))\,\mathrm{d}x\qquad\mu_{N,Y}(\mathrm{d}y)\coloneqq\exp(-\beta U_{N}(y))\,\mathrm{d}y\,,

are invariant for the processes
X_{t}
and
Y_{t}
, respectively. In the following sections, for the sake of notational convenience, we drop the bar on
\bar{\nabla}
(resp.
\bar{B}_{t}
) when referring to the gradient (resp. Brownian motion) on
\Gamma_{N}^{\perp}

\mu_{N,Y}

2.3.
The empirical measure

In this section, we provide a more precise mathematical formulation of what it means for the particles to droplet together. As mentioned previously, we are interested in the positions of particles relative to their centre of mass. Keeping this in mind, we arrive at the following natural notion of a droplet: we say the configuration of particles
x\in\Omega^{N}
lies in a droplet of size
\ell>0
if

(2.18)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(z-\bar{x}\right)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mu^{(N),x}\leq\ell^{2}\,,

where
\mu^{(N),x}
is the empirical measure associated to the configuration
x
, i.e.

(2.19)

\mu^{(N),x}\coloneqq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{x_{i}}\,

and

(2.20)

\bar{x}\coloneq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}z\,\mathrm{d}\mu^{(N),x}\,.

The above notion of droplet is agnostic to the choice of centre of mass,
\bar{x}
. Indeed, we can equivalently express condition (
2.18
) in terms of
\mu^{(N),y}\coloneqq\mu^{(N),\Pi_{N}\,x}
,
y=\Pi_{N}\,x
, as

(2.21)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|z|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mu^{(N),y}\leq\ell^{2}\,.

At the level of the configuration of particles, conditions (
2.18
) and (
2.21
) reduce to

(2.22)

\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}|x_{i}-x_{j}|^{2}\leq\ell^{2}

and, equivalently,

(2.23)

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}|y_{i}|^{2}\leq\ell^{2}\,,

for
y=\Pi_{N}\,x
. We note in particular that (
2.18
) (resp. (
2.21
)) implies that
W_{2}(\mu^{(N),x},\delta_{\bar{x}})=\ell
(resp.
W_{2}(\mu^{(N),y},\delta_{0})=\ell
).

and the surrounding discussion). Clearly this semigroup must agree with
S_{t}
on
L^{2}(\Gamma_{N}^{\perp};\mu_{N})\cap L^{\infty}(\Gamma_{N}^{\perp})
and so we continue to denote it by
S_{t}
. By interpolation (see
[
Bau14
, Theorem 4.31]
), we can extend
S_{t}
to act as a contraction Markov semigroup on
L^{p}(\Gamma_{N}^{\perp};\mu_{N})
for any
1\leq p\leq\infty
.

Given the notion of droplet introduced in
Section

2.3
, it makes sense to define the following stopping time for the process
Y_{t}
:

(2.26)

\tau_{\delta}\,\coloneqq\,\inf\left\{t\geq 0\,:\,Y_{t}\notin B_{\sqrt{N}\delta}\right\}\,,

where
B_{\sqrt{N}\delta}
is the open ball of radius
\sqrt{N}\delta
in
\Gamma_{N}^{\perp}
for some
\delta>0
. In what follows, we will choose
\delta>0
such that

(2.27)

w(\delta)+\frac{\lambda}{2}\delta^{2}\,>\,0\,,

where
w
is as given in
Assumption

2.1
. Note that this is always possible since
w^{\prime\prime}(0)
is assumed to be larger that
-\min_{\mathbb{R}}w^{\prime\prime}
, and we can choose
\lambda=\min_{\mathbb{R}}w^{\prime\prime}
. Indeed, by Taylor expanding we see that there exist constants
c_{w},\delta^{\prime}>0
such that for all
\delta<\delta^{\prime}

(2.28)

w(\delta)+\frac{\lambda}{2}\delta^{2}\,>c_{w}\delta^{2}\,.

The rationale for the condition (
2.27
) will become apparent in the proof of Lemma
3.4
.

We denote by
(P_{t})_{t\geq 0}
,
P_{t}:L^{\infty}(\bar{B}_{\sqrt{N}\delta})\to L^{\infty}(\bar{B}_{\sqrt{N}\delta})
, the sub-Markov semigroup associated to
Y_{t},\tau_{\delta}
,

(2.29)

(P_{t}f)(y)\,\coloneqq\,\mathbb{E}\left[f(Y_{t})\,\mathbf{1}_{t<\tau_{\delta}}|Y_{0}=y\right]\,,

where
\bar{B}_{\sqrt{N}\delta}
denotes the closed ball of radius
\sqrt{N}\delta
.

2.
Properties of the sub-Markov semigroup
P_{t}
.

It is straightforward to check that, for
f\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Gamma_{N}^{\perp})
with
\mathrm{supp\,}f
strictly contained in
\bar{B}_{\sqrt{N}\delta}
, the generator of
P_{t}
is exactly given by
L
.
Furthermore, we denote by
p_{N}\in\mathcal{P}(\bar{B}_{\sqrt{N}\delta})
the normalized version of
\mu_{N}
, i.e.

(2.30)

p_{N}\coloneqq\left(\mu_{N}(\bar{B}_{\sqrt{N}\delta})\right)^{-1}\mu_{N}\,.

Consider the set

(2.31)

C_{c}^{\infty}(\bar{B}_{\sqrt{N}\delta}):=\left\{f\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Gamma_{N}^{\perp}):\mathrm{supp\,}f\subseteq\bar{B}_{\sqrt{N}\delta}\right\}\,.

Then, it follows from
[
Bau14
, Proposition 4.50]
, that
L
is essentially self-adjoint on
C_{c}^{\infty}(\bar{B}_{\sqrt{N}\delta})
with respect to the inner product on
L^{2}(\bar{B}_{\sqrt{N}\delta};p_{N})
, and its minimal self-adjoint extension which we denote by
L_{D}
generates a strongly continuous sub-Markov semigroup
P_{t}^{D}
on
L^{2}(\bar{B}_{\sqrt{N}\delta};p_{N})
.
Note that for any
f\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\bar{B}_{\sqrt{N}\delta})
,
P_{t}^{D}f
is the unique solution to the Dirichlet initial value problem with initial data
f
. Applying Itô’s formula to
f(Y_{t})
, one can check that this is also the case for
P_{t}f
. It thus follows that
P_{t}f=P_{t}^{D}f
for all
f\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\bar{B}_{\sqrt{N}\delta})
. Additionally, from Jensen’s inequality and the invariance of
\mu_{N}
it follows
that

(2.32)

\lVert P_{t}f\rVert_{L^{2}(\bar{B}_{\sqrt{N}\delta};p_{N})}\leq\lVert f\rVert_{L^{2}(\bar{B}_{\sqrt{N}\delta};p_{N})}\,,

for all
f\in L^{\infty}(\bar{B}_{\sqrt{N}\delta})
. It therefore follows that
P_{t}f=P_{t}^{D}f
for all
f\in L^{\infty}(\bar{B}_{\sqrt{N}\delta})
and
P_{t}^{D}
is the unique uniformly continuous extension of
P_{t}
to
L^{2}(\bar{B}_{\sqrt{N}\delta};p_{N})
. We will drop the superscript
D
for the sake of notational convenience. Again, by interpolation (see
[
Bau14
, Theorem 4.31]
), we can extend
P_{t}
to act as a sub-Markov contraction semigroup on
L^{p}(\bar{B}_{\sqrt{N}\delta};p_{N})
for any
1\leq p\leq\infty
.

3.
Spectrum and heat kernel representation of
P_{t}
.

It now follows from
[
Bau14
, Theorem 4.52]
that
P_{t}
is a compact operator with a discrete spectrum on
L^{2}(\bar{B}_{\sqrt{N}\delta};p_{N})
. More precisely, there exist a complete orthonormal basis
(e_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
of the space
L^{2}(\bar{B}_{\sqrt{N}\delta};p_{N})
and real numbers
0<\lambda_{1}<\lambda_{2}\leq\lambda_{3}\leq\dots<\infty
, such that
-L_{D}
has discrete spectrum with eigenfunctions and eigenvalues
e_{k}
and
\lambda_{k}
, respectively. Note that
\lambda_{1}
is simple
[
Pin85
]
.
Hence, we can define, for any
K\geq 1
, the projection operator

(2.33)

\mathsf{Q}_{\geq K}:L^{2}(\bar{B}_{\sqrt{N}\delta};p_{N})\righttoleftarrow,\qquad\mathsf{Q}_{\geq K}f\coloneqq\sum_{k\geq K}\left(\int_{\bar{B}_{\sqrt{N}\delta}}fe_{k}\,\mathrm{d}p_{N}\right)e_{k}\,.

Furthermore,
P_{t}
enjoys the following heat kernel representation

(2.34)

(P_{t}f)(y)=\int_{\bar{B}_{\sqrt{N}\delta}}p_{t}(y,y^{\prime})f(y^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}p_{N}(y^{\prime})\,,

where
p_{t}\in C^{\infty}({B}_{\sqrt{N}\delta}\times{B}_{\sqrt{N}\delta})\cap C(\bar{B}_{\sqrt{N}\delta}\times\bar{B}_{\sqrt{N}\delta})
is defined as follows

(2.35)

p_{t}(y,y^{\prime}):=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda_{k}t}e_{k}(y)e_{k}(y^{\prime})\,,

with
p_{t}(y,y^{\prime})=0
if
y,y^{\prime}\in\partial{B}_{\sqrt{N}\delta}
. Furthermore,
P_{t}f\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\bar{B}_{\sqrt{N}\delta})
for all
f\in L^{2}(\bar{B}_{\sqrt{N}\delta};p_{N})
. We use the notation
\lambda_{k}=\lambda_{k,\beta,N}
when we want to emphasise the dependence on
\beta,N
.

2.5.
One point compactification

Consider the set
B_{\star,\sqrt{N}\delta}
, the one-point compactification of
B_{\sqrt{N}\delta}
with some point
\star
, which we refer to as the cemetery state.
Note that
B_{\star,\sqrt{N}\delta}
is a compact Hausdorff space (in fact, it is homeomorphic to the sphere
\mathbb{S}^{Nd}
). Furthermore, we can equip the space with the metric

(2.36)

\displaystyle d_{\star}(y_{1},y_{2})\coloneqq

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\min(|y_{1}-y_{2}|,h_{\star}(y_{1})+h_{\star}(y_{2})),&\,\text{ if }(y_{1},y_{2})\in B_{\sqrt{N}\delta},\\
h_{\star}(y_{1}),&\,\text{ if }y_{2}=\star,\end{cases}

(2.37)

\displaystyle h_{\star}(y)\coloneqq

\displaystyle\,\inf_{\hat{y}\in\partial\bar{B}_{\sqrt{N}\delta}}|y-\hat{y}|\,,

making it a compact metric space (and therefore Polish). In
Theorem

2.6
, we present our result in terms of the process
\hat{Y}_{t}
, defined as

(2.38)

\hat{Y}_{t}\coloneqq\begin{cases}Y_{t}&\,\text{ if }t<\tau_{\delta}\,,\\
\star&\,\text{ if }t\geq\tau_{\delta}\,.\end{cases}

For a given
f:\bar{B}_{\sqrt{N}\delta}\to\mathbb{R}
(resp.
f:\Gamma_{N}^{\perp}\to\mathbb{R}
), we will often abuse notation by using
f
to also refer to its extension by
0
to
\Gamma_{N}^{\perp}
(resp. restriction to
\bar{B}_{\sqrt{N}\delta}
). It will be clear from context which object we are referring to.

2.6.
The quasi-stationary distribution

As we will see in
Eq.

2.42
below, the bottom eigenvalue
\lambda_{1}
of
-L_{D}
is simple for large enough
\beta>0
. Consequently, the probability measure
q_{N}
, defined for measurable
A\subset\bar{B}_{\sqrt{N}\delta}
as

(2.39)

q_{N}(A)\,\coloneqq\,\left(\int_{\bar{B}_{\sqrt{N}\delta}}e_{1}(y)\,\mathrm{d}p_{N}(y)\right)^{-1}\,\int_{A}e_{1}(y)\,\mathrm{d}p_{N}(y)\,,

is the unique
quasi-stationary distribution
(QSD) of (
2.16
). That is,
q_{N}
is the only probability measure such that

(2.40)

\mathbb{E}_{q_{N}}\left[Y_{t}\in\,\cdot\,\rvert\,t<\tau_{\delta}\right]\,=\,q_{N}(\,\cdot\,)\qquad\text{ for }\,t\geq 0\,,

where
\mathbb{E}_{q_{N}}
denotes the expectation conditioned on the event of
Y_{0}
being distributed according to
q_{N}
.
In Theorem 2.6, below, we see that the probability measure
q_{N}
can be used to characterise the droplet state described in Section 1
at the level of the stochastic process
Y_{t}
.
Therefore, the probability measure
\bar{\mu}^{N}
referred to in Theorem 1.1 may be defined as the empirical measure of a stationary process with distribution
q_{N}
.

The fact that
q_{N}
is the unique QSD for
Y_{t}
on
B_{\sqrt{N}\delta}
can be proven using the arguments of
[
ZLS14
, Theorem 3.1]
.
The existence and uniqueness of QSDs has also been studied using spectral techniques in
[
CCL+09
,
DMV18
,
HK19
]
, as well as using modified Doeblin type conditions
[
CV16
,
CV23
]
, Lyapunov type conditions
[
CV21
]
, and Banach lattice theory
[
CLOMR24
]
.
The ideas underlying QSDs trace back to
[
Wri31
,
Yag47
]
, with the first use of the term “quasi-stationarity” being due to
[
Bar60
,
Bar57
]
.

QSDs have since been widely used to characterise metastable behaviour (see the reviews
[
MV12
,
vDP13
]
for more details). Here, we use the QSD
q_{N}
to characterise the metastable droplet state of (
2.16
). In particular, we characterise the length scale of this droplet state as the variance of
\mu^{(N),Y}
, where
\mu^{(N),Y}
is the empirical measure of some random variable
Y\sim q_{N}
.

2.7.
Statement of the main results

We are now prepared to rigorously and precisely state our main results on the convergence to, and leakage from, the metastable droplet state of (
1.1
).
Our first result characterises the large
\beta
asymptotics of the first and second eigenvalues of
-L_{D}
.
We find that the first eigenvalue of
-L_{D}
tends to zero at an asymptotically exponential rate as
\beta\rightarrow\infty
, with this rate being entirely determined by the behaviour of
w
at zero and the boundary of a carefully chosen neighbourhood of zero.

Theorem 2.2

(Low temperature asymptotics on the exponential scale)
.

Fix
N\in\mathbb{N}
, assume that
W
satisfies
Assumption

2.1
, and fix
\delta>0
such that (
2.27
) is satisfied. Let
-L_{D}
and
(\lambda_{i,\beta,N})_{i\geq 1}
be as defined in
Section

2.4
. Then,

(2.41)

\lim_{\beta\to\infty}\beta^{-1}\ln\lambda_{1,\beta,N}\leq-\frac{1}{2}\left(w(\delta)+\frac{\lambda}{2}\delta^{2}\right)\,.

Furthermore, for all
i\geq 2
, we have

(2.42)

\lim_{\beta\to\infty}\beta^{-1}\ln\lambda_{i,\beta,N}=0\,.

Remark 2.3
.

We remark that
Eq.

2.42
is true even if (
2.27
) does not hold, as will become clear from the proof in
Section

3
. However, in this case the bound (
2.41
) does not contain any useful information.

Example 2.4
.

As an example of a one-dimensional potential
W
which satisfies (
2.27
), consider
W(x)=w(x)=1-e^{-x^{2}}
. Note that this function is
\lambda
-convex with
\lambda
chosen to be
-4e^{-\frac{3}{2}}
. We thus have

(2.43)

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}\left(w(x)+\frac{\lambda}{2}x^{2}\right)=2xe^{-x^{2}}-4e^{-\frac{3}{2}}x\,.

The above expression is strictly greater than
0
for
0<x<\delta^{\prime}\coloneq\sqrt{3/2-\ln 2}
. Thus, (
2.27
) is true for all
\delta<\delta^{\prime}
.

Our next main result refines the asymptotic description of
\lambda_{2,\beta,N}
in (
4.8
).
This result implies a bound away from zero of each
\lim_{\beta\to\infty}\lambda_{2,\beta,N}

which is uniform in
N\in\mathbb{N}
.
As seen in Section
4
, we use the fact that
-L_{D}
is unitarily equivalent to a certain Schrödinger operator and study the spectrum of this operator borrowing ideas from
[
Sim83
]
.

Theorem 2.5

(Refined low temperature asymptotics)
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
2.42
, the following convergence holds:

(2.44)

\displaystyle\lim_{\beta\rightarrow\infty}\lambda_{1,\beta,N}=

\displaystyle\,0\,,

(2.45)

\displaystyle\lim_{\beta\rightarrow\infty}\lambda_{2,\beta,N}=

\displaystyle\,w^{\prime\prime}(0)\,.

Now that we have identified the large
\beta
asymptotics of the spectrum of
-L_{D}
, we are able to characterise the dynamical metastability of (
1.1
) under the assumptions used in this paper.
We see in particular that
\lambda_{2,\beta,N}
describes the rate of convergence of (
1.1
) to a droplet state, whereas
\lambda_{1,\beta,N}
describes the rate at which particles escape the droplet.
Additionally, the droplet state itself is characterised by the QSD
q_{N}
(or rather, the random empirical measure sampled from
q_{N}
), and we demonstrate that this droplet state has size (on average)
\beta^{-\frac{1}{2}}
.
Theorem

2.6
below provides a precise description of this phenomenon.
Corollary

2.7
, which translates
Theorem

2.6
to the language of time scales, is then almost immediate. Specifically, we find that for each large but fixed
\beta
, there is an interval of times determined by
\lambda_{1,\beta,N}
and
\lambda_{2,\beta,N}
over which the distribution of

is close to the QSD

(in either total variation or

-Wasserstein distance).

Theorem 2.6
.

Consider the process
\hat{Y_{t}}
as defined in (
2.38
) for some
\delta>0
.

(1)

For any
f\in C(B_{\star,\sqrt{N}\delta})
(see
Section

2.5
) and
\nu_{N}\in\mathcal{P}(\bar{B}_{\sqrt{N}\delta})
, the following identity holds true:

(2.46)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[f(\hat{Y}_{t})|Y_{0}\sim\nu_{N}\right]-\alpha_{\beta,N}e^{-\lambda_{1,\beta,N}t}\left(\int_{\bar{B}_{\sqrt{N}\delta}}f\,\mathrm{d}q_{N}\right)-(1-\alpha_{\beta,N}e^{-\lambda_{1,\beta,N}t})f(\star)

(2.47)

\displaystyle\qquad=\int_{\bar{B}_{\sqrt{N}\delta}}(P_{t}\mathsf{Q}_{\geq 2}(f-f(\star)))\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{N}\,,

where
\mathsf{Q}_{\geq 2}
is the projection defined in (
2.33
) and

(2.48)

\alpha_{\beta,N}\coloneqq\left(\int_{\bar{B}_{\sqrt{N}\delta}}e_{1}\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{N}\right)\left(\int_{\bar{B}_{\sqrt{N}\delta}}e_{1}\,\mathrm{d}p_{N}\right)\,.

(2)

Let
\rho_{t}^{\nu_{N}}:=\mathrm{Law}(\hat{Y}_{t}|Y_{0}\sim\nu_{N})
and recall
p_{N}
(
2.30
).
Then, given (
2.47
), we can derive the following estimates.

•

Control in

: Assume that

, then we have the bound

(2.49)

\lVert\rho_{t}^{\nu_{N}}-\alpha_{\beta,N}e^{-\lambda_{1,\beta,N}t}q_{N}-(1-\alpha_{\beta,N}e^{-\lambda_{1,\beta,N}t})\delta_{\star}\rVert_{\rm TV}\leq 2e^{-\lambda_{2,\beta,N}t}\left\lVert\frac{\mathrm{d}\nu_{N}}{\mathrm{d}p_{N}}\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\bar{B}_{\sqrt{N}\delta};p_{N})}\,.

•

Control in
W_{1}
: Assume again that
\nu_{N}\ll p_{N}
, then we have the bound

(2.50)

W_{1}\left(\rho_{t}^{\nu_{N}},\alpha_{\beta,N}e^{-\lambda_{1,\beta,N}t}q_{N}+(1-\alpha_{\beta,N}e^{-\lambda_{1,\beta,N}t})\delta_{\star}\right)\leq\sqrt{N}\delta e^{-\lambda_{2,\beta,N}t}\left\lVert\frac{\mathrm{d}\nu_{N}}{\mathrm{d}p_{N}}\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\bar{B}_{\sqrt{N}\delta};p_{N})}\,,

where
W_{1}
is defined with respect to the metric

defined in (

(3)

We have that

\lim_{\beta\to\infty}\alpha_{\beta,N}=1\,.

(4)

If

\limsup_{\beta\to\infty}\beta\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|x|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mu^{(N),Y}\right]\lesssim 1\,.

From Theorem

, we then have the following.

Taking

\displaystyle\left\|\rho_{t_{\beta}}^{\nu_{N}}-q_{N}\right\|_{\rm TV}

3.
Proof of
Eq.

2.42

The proof of
Eq.

2.42
relies on a result due to Mathieu (see
[
Mat95
, Theorem 1]
) which studies the asymptotics of operators which have the same form as
L_{D}
in the regime of small noise. Before we can recall this result, we need to introduce some additional notions.

Given a smooth potential
U_{N}:\bar{B}_{\sqrt{N}\delta}\to\mathbb{R}
, we say that a subset
A\subseteq\Gamma_{N}^{\perp}
with smooth boundary is an
r
-valley, for some
r>0
, if it is a connected component of the set
\{y\in\Gamma_{N}^{\perp}:U_{N}(y)<a\}\cap\bar{B}_{\sqrt{N}\delta}
for some
a\in\mathbb{R}
, and furthermore

(3.1)

\inf_{\partial A\setminus\partial{B}_{\sqrt{N}\delta}}U_{N}-\inf_{A}U_{N}=r\,.

We denote by
f(r)
the number of
r
-valleys associated to
U_{N}
and define for
i\geq 1

(3.2)

d_{i}=\inf\{r>0:f(r)<i\}\,.

The result of Mathieu then takes the following form in our setting.

Theorem 3.1

(
[
Mat95
, Theorem 1]
)
.

Let
-L_{D}
be as constructed in
Section

2.4
and
\lambda_{i,\beta,N},i\geq 1
be its eigenvalues. Then,

(3.3)

\lim_{\beta\to\infty}\beta^{-1}\ln\lambda_{i,\beta,N}=-\frac{d_{i}}{2}\,.

We proceed to show that the Hamiltonian
U_{N}
has a unique minimum and critical point at
0
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
W
satisfy
Assumption

2.1
. Then, the function
U_{N}
possesses a unique minimum and critical point at
0
with
U_{N}(0)=0
. Furthermore, for any
a\in\mathbb{R}
, the set

(3.4)

\bar{A}=\{y\in\Gamma_{N}^{\perp}:U_{N}(y)<a\}\cap\bar{B}_{\sqrt{N}\delta}

is path-connected and, thereby, connected.

Proof.

We start by noting that
H_{N}(x)=0
if
x\in\Gamma_{N}
, the orthogonal complement of
\Gamma_{N}^{\perp}
. Furthermore, if
x\notin\Gamma_{N}
, we can write
x=z+y
for some
z\in\Gamma_{N}
and some
0\neq y\in\Gamma_{N}^{\perp}
. This implies that there exists some
(i_{0},j_{0})\in\{1,\dots,N\}^{2}
such that
y_{i_{0}}\neq y_{j_{0}}
. Using
Assumption

2.1
, we then have

(3.5)

\displaystyle H_{N}(x)=

\displaystyle\,H_{N}(y)

(3.6)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\,\frac{1}{N}w(|y_{i_{0}}-y_{j_{0}}|)>0\,.

We recall that
U_{N}=\left.H_{N}\right|_{\Gamma_{N}^{\perp}}
. Hence, we have
U_{N}(0)=0
and, for any
0\neq y\in\Gamma_{N}^{\perp}
,

(3.7)

U_{N}(y)=H_{N}(y)>0\,.

This establishes the uniqueness of the minimum at
0
.

To argue that
0
is also the unique critical point,
we observe from the properties of
W
in
Assumption

2.1
that

(3.8)

\displaystyle\langle\nabla H_{N}(x),x\rangle=

\displaystyle\,\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}w^{\prime}(|x_{i}-x_{j}|)\frac{x_{i}-x_{j}}{|x_{i}-x_{j}|}\cdot x_{i}\,,

from which it follows by flipping the roles of
i
and
j
and summing that

(3.9)

\displaystyle\langle\nabla H_{N}(x),x\rangle=

\displaystyle\,\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}w^{\prime}(|x_{i}-x_{j}|)|x_{i}-x_{j}|>0\,,

if
x\notin\Gamma_{N}
. It follows from the definition of
U_{N}
that

(3.10)

\langle\nabla U_{N}(y),y\rangle>0\,,

for
y\neq 0
. This establishes the uniqueness of
0
as a critical point.

For path-connectedness, we argue that, for any
(y_{0},y_{1})\in\bar{A}
, the curve

(3.11)

\displaystyle y(t)=\begin{cases}(1-2t)y_{0},&\text{ if }t\in[0,\frac{1}{2}]\\
(2t-1)y_{1},&\text{ if }t\in(\frac{1}{2},1]\end{cases}\,,

lies in
\bar{A}
. This is clearly true for
\bar{B}_{\sqrt{N}\delta}
. For
y_{0}\in\{y\in\Gamma_{N}^{\perp}:U_{N}(y)<a\}
, we note that

(3.12)

U_{N}(y_{0})=U_{N}(\alpha y_{0})+\int_{\alpha}^{1}\langle\nabla U_{N}(\alpha^{\prime}y_{0}),y_{0}\rangle\,\mathrm{d}\alpha^{\prime}\geq U_{N}(\alpha y_{0})\,,

as long has
\alpha\leq 1
. From this it follows that
\alpha y_{0}\in\{y\in\Gamma_{N}^{\perp}:U_{N}(y)<a\}
for any
\alpha\in[0,1]
. The same holds for
y_{1}
such that path-connectedness follows.
∎

As an immediate corollary of the above lemma, we have the following result.

Corollary 3.3
.

Proof.

It remains to establish a lower bound on

Lemma 3.4

\displaystyle\inf_{y\in\partial B_{\sqrt{N}\delta}}U_{N}(y)\,\geq\,\,w(\delta)+\frac{\lambda}{2}\delta^{2}\,,

For the proof of (

(3.17)

\displaystyle U_{N}(x)=

\displaystyle\,H_{N}(x)

\displaystyle\geq

4.
Proof of
Theorem

2.5

In the following, for a Banach space
B
, if
x\in B
then
\left\|x\right\|_{B}
denotes the the norm of
x
, while if
A:B\to B
is an operator, then
\left\|A\right\|_{B}=\left\|A\right\|_{B\to B}
denotes the operator norm of
A
. To study the asymptotic behaviour of the higher eigenvalues of
-L_{D}
, we adopt the approach of
[
Sim83
]
. To this end, we introduce a Schrödinger operator
S_{\beta}
, which is unitarily equivalent to the sub-Markov generator
-L_{D}
,
given by

(4.1)

S_{\beta}=-\beta^{-1}\Delta+\frac{\beta}{4}|\nabla U_{N}|^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\Delta U_{N}\,=:-\beta^{-1}\Delta+V_{\beta},

with Dirichlet boundary conditions on
B_{\sqrt{N}\delta}
.
A Schrödinger operator of this form is sometimes referred to as a
Witten Laplacian

[
HN05
,
HN06
,
Wit82
]
.
Recall that
-L_{D}
is defined on
L^{2}(B_{\sqrt{N}\delta};p_{N})
, where
p_{N}
is as defined in (
2.17
) and (
2.30
), and let the density of
p_{N}
be denoted by
u_{N}
. One can check that

(4.2)

T:L^{2}(B_{\sqrt{N}\delta};p_{N})\,\rightarrow L^{2}(B_{\sqrt{N}\delta}),\qquad Tf\,\coloneqq\,u^{1/2}f\,,

is a unitary conjugacy between
-L_{D}
as an operator on
L^{2}(B_{\sqrt{N}\delta};p_{N})
and
S_{\beta}
as an operator on
L^{2}(B_{\sqrt{N}\delta})
:

(4.3)

-L_{D}f\,=\,T^{-1}\left(S_{\beta}(Tf)\right)=u_{N}^{-1/2}S_{\beta}\left(u_{N}^{1/2}f\right),\qquad f\in L^{2}(B_{\sqrt{N}\delta};p_{N})\,.

It thus follows that
S_{\beta}
has domain
u_{N}^{\frac{1}{2}}D(-L_{D})\subseteq L^{2}(B_{\sqrt{N}\delta})
, is self-adjoint, and
S_{\beta},-L_{D}
have the same spectrum.
The analysis of Schrödinger operators in
[
Sim83
]
is presented on the whole space. Thus, to apply the analysis in
[
Sim83
]
, we need to establish a connection between the spectra of operators on
\Gamma_{N}^{\perp}
and operators on
B_{\sqrt{N}\delta}
with Dirichlet boundary conditions. Moreover, we recall that we may identify
\Gamma_{N}^{\perp}
with
\mathbb{R}^{(N-1)d}
, and so consider all Schrödinger operators to be defined on (a subset of) Euclidean space.

4.1.
Approximating Schrödinger operators on the whole space

As mentioned earlier, to study the spectrum of
S_{\beta}
, we apply the results of
[
Sim83
]
, which are only stated for Schrödinger operators defined on
\mathbb{R}^{k}
,
k\in\mathbb{N}
. As
[
Sim83
]
employs scaling arguments to study the asymptotics of the spectra of Schrödinger operators, the results therein do not trivially transfer to Schrödinger operators defined on bounded domains.
To impose boundary conditions in a way that is compatible with
[
Sim83
]
, we approximate
S_{\beta}
by a sequence of operators over
\mathbb{R}^{(N-1)d}
; the spectra of these operators will be shown to converge to the spectrum of
S_{\beta}
.

The convergence of sequences of differential operators on varying spatial domains has been widely studied, for instance in
[
AD08
,
BD06
,
Dan08
]
. However, issues often arise in the case where the spatial domains tend to an unbounded set. To circumvent these issues, we use ideas found in
[
DPL10
]
, and excellently explained in the thesis
[
Big22
]
, where Schrödinger operators on bounded domains are approximated by Schrödinger operators on the whole space in a strong resolvent sense.
As we want to approximate the spectrum of
S_{\beta}
by the spectra of operators
\tilde{S}_{\beta}
, and it is known that strong resolvent convergence alone may allow for spectral pollution as
n\rightarrow\infty

[
Kat95
, Chapter IV.3.1]
, this result as such is insufficient.
Hence, in the following, we strengthen the results of
[
DPL10
]
to norm resolvent convergence.

In this section, for
f:B_{\sqrt{N}\delta}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
we denote by
\overline{f}
the extension of
f
to
\mathbb{R}^{(N-1)d}
by setting
\overline{f}=0
on
\bar{B}_{\sqrt{N}\delta}^{c}
. Similarly, for
f:\mathbb{R}^{(N-1)d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, we denote by
f\rvert
the restriction of
f
to
B_{\sqrt{N}\delta}
. Let
(B_{t})_{t\geq 0}
denote a standard Brownian motion on
\mathbb{R}^{(N-1)d}
. We denote by
\mathbb{P}_{x}
the Wiener measure corresponding to
(B_{t})_{t\geq 0}
with
B_{0}=x
, and by
\mathbb{E}_{y}
the corresponding expectation. By the Feynman–Kac formula, the semigroup generated by
S_{\beta}
with Dirichlet boundaries on
B_{\sqrt{N}\delta}
can be expressed as

(4.4)

(e^{-S_{\beta}t}f)(y)\,\coloneqq\,\mathbb{E}_{y}\left[f(B_{\beta^{-1}t})e^{-\beta\int_{0}^{\beta^{-1}t}V_{\beta}(B_{s})\,\mathrm{d}s}1_{\{\beta^{-1}t<\tau\}}\right]\,,

where
\tau
is the first exit time of
(B_{t})_{t\geq 0}
from
B_{\sqrt{N}\delta}

[
Wu98
, Lemma 2.2]
(note that the
\tau
introduced here is distinct from
\tau_{\delta}
, defined in (
2.26
)).
Let
V_{0}:\mathbb{R}^{(N-1)d}\rightarrow[0,1]
be a smooth function such that

(1)

V_{0}
is non-decreasing in
|y|
,

(2)

V_{0}(y)=0
for
y\in B_{\sqrt{N}\delta}
,
V_{0}(y)>0
for
y\in\bar{B}_{\sqrt{N}\delta}^{c}
, and

(3)

\lim_{|y|\to\infty}V_{0}(y)=+\infty
.

We then define
\tilde{S}_{\beta}
on
C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{(N-1)d})
as

(4.5)

\tilde{S}_{\beta}\,\coloneqq\,-\beta^{-1}\Delta+V_{\beta}+\beta V_{0}\,.

One can readily check that
\tilde{S}_{\beta}
is essentially self-adjoint on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{(N-1)d})
(see
[
RS75
, Theorem X.29]
) and we continue to denote by
\tilde{S}_{\beta}
its unique self-adjoint extension. One can also check that
\tilde{S}_{\beta}
is a non-negative operator. As before,
\tilde{S}_{\beta}
is the generator in the
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{(N-1)d})
-topology of the semigroup

(4.6)

e^{-\tilde{S}_{\beta}t}f(y)\,\coloneqq\,\mathbb{E}_{y}\left[f(B_{\beta^{-1}t})e^{-\beta\int_{0}^{{\beta^{-1}t}}V_{\beta}(B_{s})\,\mathrm{d}s}e^{-\beta^{2}\int_{0}^{\beta^{-1}t}V_{0}(B_{s})\,\mathrm{d}s}\right]\,.

As mentioned above, the goal here is to prove that
\tilde{S}_{\beta}
converges to
S_{\beta}
in norm resolvent as
\beta\to\infty
. To achieve this, we first prove the norm convergence of the semigroups
e^{-\tilde{S}_{\beta}t}
to
e^{-S_{\beta}t}
as
\beta\to\infty
for fixed
t>0
in
Lemma

4.1
. Then, in
Lemma

4.2
, we use the Laplace transform representation of the resolvent to prove norm convergence of the resolvents
R(\lambda,-\tilde{S}_{\beta})
to
R(\lambda,-S_{\beta})
for all
\lambda>0
where the resolvent operators are given by

(4.7)

R(\lambda,-S_{\beta})\,=\,(\lambda+S_{\beta})^{-1}\,,\qquad\lambda\in\mathbb{C}\,\backslash\spec(-S_{\beta})\,,

and similarly for
S_{\beta}
replaced with
\tilde{S}_{\beta}
.
Finally, we extend this norm resolvent convergence to every admissible
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
in
Proposition

4.3
using the Stone–Weierstrass theorem.
Note that in what follows, we will abuse notation by using
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{(N-1)d})
to denote the space of complex-valued square-integrable functions over
\mathbb{R}^{(N-1)d}
.

Lemma 4.1
.

For
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{(N-1)d})
and
t>0
, it holds that

(4.8)

\lim_{\beta\to\infty}\sup_{\left\|f\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{(N-1)d})}\leq 1}\left\|\overline{e^{-S_{\beta}t}(f\rvert)}-e^{-\tilde{S}_{\beta}t}f\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{(N-1)d})}\,=\,0\,.

Proof.

For an arbitrary
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{(N-1)d})
and
\gamma\in(0,1)

(4.9)

\displaystyle\left\|\overline{e^{-S_{\beta}t}(f\rvert)}-e^{-\tilde{S}_{\beta}t}f\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{(N-1)d})}^{2}

(4.10)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{(N-1)d}}\left(\mathbb{E}_{y}\left[f(B_{\beta^{-1}t})e^{-\beta\int_{0}^{\beta^{-1}t}V_{\beta}(B_{s})\,\mathrm{d}s}e^{-\beta^{2}\int_{0}^{\beta^{-1}t}V_{0}(B_{s})\,\mathrm{d}s}1_{\{\beta^{-1}t\geq\tau\}}\right]\right)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}y

(4.11)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{(N-1)d}}\mathbb{E}_{y}\left[|f(B_{\beta^{-1}t})|^{2}\right]\mathbb{E}_{y}\left[e^{-2\beta\int_{0}^{\beta^{-1}t}V_{\beta}(B_{s})\,\mathrm{d}s}e^{-2\beta^{2}\int_{0}^{\beta^{-1}t}V_{0}(B_{s})\,\mathrm{d}s}1_{\{\beta^{-1}t\geq\tau\}}\right]\,\mathrm{d}y

(4.12)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\,\int_{B_{\gamma\sqrt{N}\delta}}\mathbb{E}_{y}\left[|f(B_{\beta^{-1}t})|^{2}\right]\mathbb{E}_{y}\left[e^{-2\beta\int_{0}^{\beta^{-1}t}V_{\beta}(B_{s})\,\mathrm{d}s}e^{-2\beta^{2}\int_{0}^{\beta^{-1}t}V_{0}(B_{s})\,\mathrm{d}s}1_{\{\beta^{-1}t\geq\tau\}}\right]\,\mathrm{d}y

(4.13)

\displaystyle\,+\int_{B_{\gamma^{-1}\sqrt{N}\delta}\setminus B_{\gamma\sqrt{N}\delta}}\mathbb{E}_{y}\left[|f(B_{\beta^{-1}t})|^{2}\right]\mathbb{E}_{y}\left[e^{-2\beta\int_{0}^{\beta^{-1}t}V_{\beta}(B_{s})\,\mathrm{d}s}e^{-2\beta^{2}\int_{0}^{\beta^{-1}t}V_{0}(B_{s})\,\mathrm{d}s}1_{\{\beta^{-1}t\geq\tau\}}\right]\,\mathrm{d}y

(4.14)

\displaystyle\,+\int_{B_{\gamma^{-1}\sqrt{N}\delta}^{c}}\mathbb{E}_{y}\left[|f(B_{\beta^{-1}t})|^{2}\right]\mathbb{E}_{y}\left[e^{-2\beta\int_{0}^{\beta^{-1}t}V_{\beta}(B_{s})\,\mathrm{d}s}e^{-2\beta^{2}\int_{0}^{\beta^{-1}t}V_{0}(B_{s})\,\mathrm{d}s}1_{\{\beta^{-1}t\geq\tau\}}\right]\,\mathrm{d}y

(4.15)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\,e^{C_{\Delta}t}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{(N-1)d}}\mathbb{E}_{y}\left[|f(B_{\beta^{-1}t})|^{2}\right]\,\mathrm{d}x\right)\bigg(\esup\limits_{y\in B_{\gamma\sqrt{N}\delta}}\mathbb{P}_{y}\left[\tau\leq\beta^{-1}t\right]

(4.16)

\displaystyle\,+\esup_{y\in B_{\gamma^{-1}\sqrt{N}\delta}\setminus B_{\gamma\sqrt{N}\delta}}\mathbb{E}_{y}\left[e^{-\frac{\beta^{2}}{4}\int_{0}^{\beta^{-1}t}|\nabla U_{N}|^{2}(B_{s})\,\mathrm{d}s}\right]+\esup_{y\in B_{\gamma^{-1}\sqrt{N}\delta}^{c}}\mathbb{E}_{y}\left[e^{-\beta^{2}\int_{0}^{\beta^{-1}t}V_{0}(B_{s})\,\mathrm{d}s}\right]\bigg)

(4.17)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\,e^{C_{\Delta}t}\lVert f\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{(N-1)d})}^{2}\bigg(\esup\limits_{x\in B_{\gamma\sqrt{N}\delta}}\mathbb{P}_{y}\left[\tau\leq\beta^{-1}t\right]

(4.18)

\displaystyle\,+\esup_{y\in B_{\gamma^{-1}\sqrt{N}\delta}\setminus B_{\gamma\sqrt{N}\delta}}\mathbb{E}_{y}\left[e^{-\frac{\beta^{2}}{4}\int_{0}^{\beta^{-1}t}|\nabla U_{N}|^{2}(B_{s})\,\mathrm{d}s}\right]+\esup_{y\in B_{\gamma^{-1}\sqrt{N}\delta}^{c}}\mathbb{E}_{y}\left[e^{-\beta^{2}\int_{0}^{\beta^{-1}t}V_{0}(B_{s})\,\mathrm{d}s}\right]\bigg)\,,

where we have used the fact that the heat semigroup is non-expansive in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{(N-1)d})
,
|\Delta U_{N}|\leq C_{\Delta}<\infty
, and
V_{0}\geq 0
. We now need to control the three terms that show up in the bracket on the right hand side of (

(4.19)

\esup_{x\in B_{\gamma\sqrt{N}\delta}}\mathbb{P}_{y}\left[\tau\leq\beta^{-1}t\right]\,\leq\,\mathbb{P}_{\bar{y}}\left[\tau\leq\beta^{-1}t\right]

for some arbitrary
\bar{y}\in\partial B_{\gamma\sqrt{N}\delta}
(due to the rotational invariance of Brownian motion this probability is independent of the choice of
\bar{y}
).
The term on the right hand side goes to

as

since
\tau>0
almost surely for
\bar{y}\in\partial B_{\gamma\sqrt{N}\delta}
.
To see that (
4.19

\tau^{\prime}
denote the first exit time from
B_{\gamma\sqrt{N}\delta}
.
Then, we argue that, for
y\in B_{\gamma\sqrt{N}\delta}

(4.20)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{y}[\tau\leq t]=

\displaystyle\,\mathbb{P}_{y}\left[\sup_{0\leqslant s\leqslant t}|B_{s}|\geq\sqrt{N}\delta\right]

(4.21)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,\mathbb{P}_{y}\left[\sup_{\tau^{\prime}\leq s\leq t}|B_{s}|\geq\sqrt{N}\delta\right]

(4.22)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,\mathbb{P}_{y}\left[\sup_{0\leq s\leq t-\tau^{\prime}}|B_{s+\tau^{\prime}}|\geq\sqrt{N}\delta\right]

(4.23)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,\mathbb{P}_{y}\left[\sup_{0\leq s\leq t-\tau^{\prime}}|B_{s+\tau^{\prime}}-B_{\tau^{\prime}}+B_{\tau^{\prime}}|\geq\sqrt{N}\delta\right]\,.

Note now that by the strong Markov property

is a Brownian motion itself and is independent of
\mathcal{F}_{\tau^{\prime}}
.
As a consequence, we can rewrite the above expression as

(4.24)

\displaystyle\int_{\partial B_{\gamma\sqrt{N}\delta}\times[0,\infty)}\mathbb{P}_{0}\left[\sup_{0\leq s\leq t-r}|B_{s}+y|\geq\sqrt{N}\delta\right]\mathrm{d}\mu(y,r)

(4.25)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,\int_{[0,\infty)}\mathbb{P}_{0}\left[\sup_{0\leqslant s\leqslant t-r}|B_{s}+\bar{y}|\geq\sqrt{N}\delta\right]\mathrm{d}\tilde{{\mu}}(r)\leq\mathbb{P}_{\bar{y}}[\tau\leq t]\,,

where
\bar{y}\in\partial B_{\gamma\sqrt{N}\delta}
is arbitrary,
\mu
is the joint law of
\left(B_{\tau^{\prime}},\tau^{\prime}\right)
, and
\tilde{{\mu}}
is the law of
\tau^{\prime}
. The last inequality follows from the fact that
\{\tau\leq t-r\}\subseteq\{\tau\leq t\}
for all

.

We now control the second term in the bracket on the right hand side of (
4.18
). By
Lemma

3.2
, we know that

(4.26)

\inf_{y\in B_{2\gamma^{-1}\sqrt{N}\delta}\setminus B_{(\gamma/2)\sqrt{N}\delta}}|\nabla U_{N}|^{2}(y)\,\eqqcolon\,C_{\nabla}\,>\,0\,.

\tilde{\tau}_{1}
denote the first exit time from

. Then, we have that

(4.27)

\displaystyle\,\esup_{y\in B_{\gamma^{-1}\sqrt{N}\delta}\setminus B_{\gamma\sqrt{N}\delta}}\mathbb{E}_{y}\left[e^{-\frac{\beta^{2}}{4}\int_{0}^{\beta^{-1}t}|\nabla U_{N}|^{2}(B_{s})\,\mathrm{d}s}\right]

(4.28)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\,\esup_{y\in B_{\gamma^{-1}\sqrt{N}\delta}\setminus B_{\gamma\sqrt{N}\delta}}\mathbb{E}_{y}\left[e^{-\frac{\beta C_{\nabla}t}{8}}\right]

(4.29)

\displaystyle\,+\esup_{y\in B_{\gamma^{-1}\sqrt{N}\delta}\setminus B_{\gamma\sqrt{N}\delta}}\mathbb{E}_{y}\left[\mathbf{1}_{\tilde{\tau}\leq\beta^{-1}t/2}\right]

(4.30)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\,e^{-\frac{\beta C_{\nabla}t}{8}}+\esup_{y\in B_{\gamma^{-1}\sqrt{N}\delta}\setminus B_{\gamma\sqrt{N}\delta}}\mathbb{P}_{y}\left[\tilde{\tau}\leq\frac{\beta^{-1}t}{2}\right]

(4.31)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\,e^{-\frac{\beta C_{\nabla}t}{8}}+\esup_{y\in B_{\gamma^{-1}\sqrt{N}\delta}\setminus B_{\gamma\sqrt{N}\delta}}\mathbb{P}_{y}\left[\tilde{\tau}_{1}\leq\frac{\beta^{-1}t}{2}\right]

(4.32)

\displaystyle\,+\esup_{y\in B_{\gamma^{-1}\sqrt{N}\delta}\setminus B_{\gamma\sqrt{N}\delta}}\mathbb{P}_{y}\left[\tilde{\tau}_{2}\leq\frac{\beta^{-1}t}{2}\right]

(4.33)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\,e^{-\frac{\beta C_{\nabla}t}{8}}+\mathbb{P}_{\bar{y}_{1}}\left[\tilde{\tau}_{1}\leq\frac{\beta^{-1}t}{2}\right]

(4.34)

\displaystyle\,+\mathbb{P}_{\bar{y}_{2}}\left[\tilde{\tau}_{2}\leq\frac{\beta^{-1}t}{2}\right]\,,

where
\bar{y}_{1}\in\partial B_{\gamma^{-1}\sqrt{N}\delta}

We now treat the third term in the bracket on the right hand side of (

be the first hitting time of

for some

(4.35)

\displaystyle\,\esup_{y\in B_{\gamma^{-1}\sqrt{N}\delta}^{c}}\mathbb{E}_{y}\left[e^{-\beta^{2}\int_{0}^{\beta^{-1}t}V_{0}(B_{s})\,\mathrm{d}s}\right]

(4.36)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\,\esup_{y\in B_{\gamma^{-1}\sqrt{N}\delta}^{c}}\mathbb{E}_{y}\left[e^{-\frac{\beta C_{V_{0}}t}{2}}\right]+\esup_{y\in B_{\gamma^{-1}\sqrt{N}\delta}^{c}}\mathbb{P}_{y}\left[\hat{\tau}\leq\frac{\beta^{-1}t}{2}\right]\,,

where the first term converges to
0
as
\beta\to\infty
.
The second term also converges to
0
as
\beta\to\infty
using similar arguments as for the other two terms on the right hand side of (

).
This completes the proof.
∎

Lemma 4.2
.

For
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{(N-1)d})
and
\lambda>0
, it holds that

(4.37)

\lim_{\beta\to\infty}\sup_{\left\|f\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{(N-1)d})}\leq 1}\left\|\overline{R(\lambda,-S_{\beta})(f\rvert)}-R(\lambda,-\tilde{S}_{\beta})f\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{(N-1)d})}=0

Proof.

Fixing
\lambda>0
, by
[
EN00
, II.1.10]
, we write

(4.38)

R(\lambda,\tilde{S}_{\beta})f\,=\,\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda t}e^{-\tilde{S}_{\beta}t}f\,\mathrm{d}t,\qquad\forall f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{(N-1)d})\,,

and observe that

(4.39)

\overline{R(\lambda,-S_{\beta})(f\rvert)}\,=\,\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda t}\overline{e^{-S_{\beta}t}(f\rvert)}\,\mathrm{d}t,\qquad\forall f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{(N-1)d})\,.

Using
Lemma

4.1
, the fact that the heat semigroup is non-expansive in
L^{2}
, and the dominated convergence theorem, we have that

(4.40)

\displaystyle\lim_{\beta\to\infty}\sup_{\left\|f\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{(N-1)d})}\leq 1}\left\|\overline{R(\lambda,-S_{\beta})(f\rvert)}-R(\lambda,-\tilde{S}_{\beta})f\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{(N-1)d})}

\displaystyle\leq\,\lim_{\beta\to\infty}\sup_{\left\|f\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{(N-1)d})}\leq 1}\,\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda t}\left\|\overline{e^{-S_{\beta}t}(f\rvert)}-e^{-\tilde{S}_{\beta}t}f\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{(N-1)d})}\,\mathrm{d}t=0\,.

Note, to apply the dominated convergence theorem, we have used the fact that both the semigroups are non-expansive since
\tilde{S}_{\beta},S_{\beta}
are non-negative.
∎

We have therefore shown that
R(\lambda,-\tilde{S}_{\beta})
converges in a generalized norm sense to
R(\lambda,-S_{\beta})
for
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}

where in the last step we have applied the Cauchy–Schwartz inequality and have used the heat kernel representation in (
2.35
) along with the definition of the projection
\mathsf{Q}_{\geq 2}
in (
2.33
). If we now take the a supremum over all
f\in C(B_{\star,\sqrt{N}\delta})
with
|f|\leq 1
, we can apply the Riesz–Markov–Kakutani representation theorem (since
B_{\star,\sqrt{N}\delta}
is a compact Hausdorff space) to argue that (
2.49
) holds true.

If we now choose
f\in\mathrm{Lip}_{1}(B_{\star,\sqrt{N}\delta})
, we have

(5.14)

\displaystyle\lVert f-f(\star)\rVert_{L^{2}(\bar{B}_{\sqrt{N}\delta};p_{N})}\left\lVert\frac{\mathrm{d}\nu_{N}}{\mathrm{d}p_{N}}\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\bar{B}_{\sqrt{N}\delta};p_{N})}

(5.15)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\,e^{-\lambda_{2,\beta,N}t}\sup_{\bar{B}_{\sqrt{N}\delta}}d_{\star}(\cdot,\star)\left\lVert\frac{\mathrm{d}\nu_{N}}{\mathrm{d}p_{N}}\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\bar{B}_{\sqrt{N}\delta};p_{N})}

(5.16)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\,\sqrt{N}\delta e^{-\lambda_{2,\beta,N}t}\,,

where we have used (
2.35
) again and
d_{\star}
is as defined in (
2.36
). Using the dual formulation of the
W_{1}
distance (see (
2.3
)) we obtain (
2.50
).

(iii) We now move on to the proof of (
2.51
).
To this end, we employ
[
DF78
, Theorem 2.4]
, which tells us if the ODE

(5.17)

\dot{y}(t)=-\nabla U_{N}(y(t))\,,\quad y(0)\in B_{\sqrt{N}\delta}

only has solutions which converge to
0
, then

(5.18)

e_{1}\to 1\,,\textrm{uniformly on compact subsets of }B_{\sqrt{N}\delta}.

For any
0<\delta^{\prime}<\delta
, we then have

(5.19)

\displaystyle\,\left|\int_{\bar{B}_{\sqrt{N}\delta}}(e_{1}-1)\,\mathrm{d}p_{N}\right|

(5.20)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\,\left|\int_{\bar{B}_{\sqrt{N}\delta^{\prime}}}(e_{1}-1)\,\mathrm{d}p_{N}\right|+\left|\int_{\bar{B}_{\sqrt{N}\delta^{\prime}}\setminus\bar{B}_{\sqrt{N}\delta^{\prime}}}(e_{1}-1)\,\mathrm{d}p_{N}\right|

(5.21)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\,\lVert e_{1}-1\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\bar{B}_{\sqrt{N}\delta^{\prime}})}p_{N}(\bar{B}_{\sqrt{N}\delta^{\prime}})+\left(\lVert e_{1}\rVert_{L^{2}(\bar{B}_{\sqrt{N}\delta};p_{N})}+1\right)p_{N}^{\frac{1}{2}}(\bar{B}_{\sqrt{N}\delta}\setminus\bar{B}_{\sqrt{N}\delta^{\prime}})

(5.22)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,\lVert e_{1}-1\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\bar{B}_{\sqrt{N}\delta^{\prime}})}p_{N}(\bar{B}_{\sqrt{N}\delta^{\prime}})+2p_{N}^{\frac{1}{2}}(\bar{B}_{\sqrt{N}\delta}\setminus\bar{B}_{\sqrt{N}\delta^{\prime}})\,,

where for the penultimate identity we have applied the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality for the second term. As
\beta\to\infty
, the first term on the right hand side goes to
0
due to the convergence of
e_{1}
while the second term goes to
0
because
p_{N}
converges weakly to
\delta_{0}
due to the fact that
U_{N}
has a unique minimum at
0
(see
Lemma

3.2
).
A similar argument can be applied to the term
\int_{\bar{B}_{\sqrt{N}\delta}}(e_{1}-1)\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{N}
with the only difference being that we first choose
\delta^{\prime}
close to
\delta
so that
\nu_{N}(\bar{B}_{\sqrt{N}\delta}\setminus\bar{B}_{\sqrt{N}\delta^{\prime}})
is arbitrarily small (this is possible since
\nu_{N}\ll p_{N}\ll\mathrm{d}y
) and then carry out the same argument as above.

Now, as shown in
Lemma

3.2
(see (
3.10
)),
-\langle\nabla U_{N}(y),y\rangle<0
for all
y\neq 0
and
\nabla U_{N}(0)=0
. Thus, the ODE (
5.17
) only has solutions which converge to
0
. The result then follows.

iv) Finally, we prove that the empirical measure of the droplet state, characterised by the QSD
q_{N}
, has a second moment which scales as
\beta^{-1}
.
For
Y\sim q_{N}
, we compute

(5.23)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|x|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mu^{(N),Y}\right]=

\displaystyle\,\frac{1}{N}\int_{\bar{B}_{\sqrt{N}\delta}}|y|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}q_{N}

(5.24)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,\frac{1}{N\displaystyle\int_{\bar{B}_{\sqrt{N}\delta}}e_{1}\,\mathrm{d}p_{N}}\int_{\bar{B}_{\sqrt{N}\delta}}|y|^{2}e_{1}\,\mathrm{d}p_{N}

(5.25)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\,\frac{1}{N\displaystyle\int_{\bar{B}_{\sqrt{N}\delta}}e_{1}\,\mathrm{d}p_{N}}\left(\int_{\bar{B}_{\sqrt{N}\delta}}|y|^{4}\,\mathrm{d}p_{N}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\,,

where we have applied the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality. Let us now consider the integral in the numerator by itself:

(5.26)

\displaystyle\,\int_{\bar{B}_{\sqrt{N}\delta}}|y|^{4}\,\mathrm{d}p_{N}

(5.27)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,\frac{1}{\displaystyle\int_{\bar{B}_{\sqrt{N}\delta}}e^{-\beta U_{N}(y)}\,\mathrm{d}y}\int_{\bar{B}_{\sqrt{N}\delta}}|y|^{4}e^{-\beta U_{N}(y)}\,\mathrm{d}y

(5.28)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\,\frac{1}{\displaystyle\int_{\bar{B}_{\sqrt{N}\delta}}e^{-\beta U_{N}(y)}\,\mathrm{d}y}\int_{\bar{B}_{\sqrt{N}\delta}}|y|^{4}e^{-\beta c_{w}|y|^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}y\,,

where in the last step we have used the result of
Lemma

3.4
and (
2.28
). Rescaling using the map
\beta^{\frac{1}{2}}y\mapsto y
, we have

(5.29)

\displaystyle\,\int_{\bar{B}_{\sqrt{N}\delta}}|y|^{4}\,\mathrm{d}p_{N}\leq\frac{1}{\beta^{2+\frac{d}{2}}\displaystyle\int_{\bar{B}_{\sqrt{N}\delta}}e^{-\beta U_{N}(y)}\,\mathrm{d}y}\int_{\Gamma_{N}^{\perp}}|y|^{4}e^{-c_{w}|y|^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}y\,.

Putting the above estimate together with (
5.25
), we obtain

(5.30)

\frac{\beta}{N}\int_{\bar{B}_{\sqrt{N}\delta}}|y|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}q_{N}\leq\frac{1}{N\left(\displaystyle\int_{\bar{B}_{\sqrt{N}\delta}}e_{1}\,\mathrm{d}p_{N}\right)\left(\beta^{\frac{d}{2}}\displaystyle\int_{\bar{B}_{\sqrt{N}\delta}}e^{-\beta U_{N}(y)}\,\mathrm{d}y\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}}\,.

We have already shown that first integral in the denominator converges to
1
as
\beta\to\infty
. For the second integral, we note that
\lim_{\beta\to\infty}\beta^{\frac{d}{2}}\displaystyle\int_{\bar{B}_{\sqrt{N}\delta}}e^{-\beta U_{N}(y)}\,\mathrm{d}y>0
by a Laplace approximation argument and the fact that
U_{N}(0)=0
is the unique minimum of
U_{N}
in
\bar{B}_{\sqrt{N}\delta}
and
D^{2}U_{N}(0)>0
(see
2.1
and
3.2
). Taking the limit superior, (
2.52
) follows.
