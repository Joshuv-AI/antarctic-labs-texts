m6A-modified ATF4 regulates glucose/lipid metabolism via the Sestrin2/GSK3β axis to alleviate myocardial ischemia-reperfusion injury
Authors: Jie Wu, Jian Tong, Fen Zhang, Xingxing Li, Xizhi Liu, Yang Dong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMyocardial ischemia-reperfusion injury (MIRI) is a critical complication in the treatment of cardiovascular diseases, and its pathogenesis is closely associated with mitochondrial dysfunction and the imbalance of glucose/lipid metabolism. This study aims to investigate the molecular mechanisms underlying glucose/lipid metabolism in MIRI and identify potential therapeutic targets.MethodsKey genes and prognostic biomarkers related to MIRI were identified through bioinformatics analysis, and a hypoxia/reoxygenation (H/R) model using HL-1 cardiomyocytes was employed to simulate the pathological process of MIRI. RNA interference was used to knock down ATF4, and bioinformatics analysis identified key molecules involved in m6A methylation. The regulatory mechanism of ATF4 mRNA stability mediated by RBM15 was further explored. Flow cytometry, mitochondrial membrane potential, and ATP assays were conducted to evaluate cell apoptosis, glucose uptake, fatty acid oxidation, and mitochondrial function.ResultsBioinformatics analysis and the H/R model experiments in HL-1 cardiomyocytes revealed that ATF4 was highly expressed in MIRI. Knockdown of ATF4 exacerbated H/R-induced cell apoptosis and metabolic disturbances. m6A methyltransferase RBM15 modulated the stability and expression of ATF4 mRNA through methylation. Further investigation showed that ATF4 upregulates Sestrin2 to inhibit GSK3β activity, thereby maintaining mitochondrial membrane potential and ATP production, promoting glucose uptake, and enhancing fatty acid oxidation.ConclusionThis study is the first to reveal the molecular mechanism by which the m6A modified ATF4-Sestrin2/GSK3β signaling axis alleviates MIRI through dual regulation of glucose/lipid metabolism homeostasis and mitochondrial energy supply. It elucidates the bridging role of RBM15-mediated m6A epigenetic modification in this process. These findings provide a new strategy for targeting metabolic reprogramming in MIRI therapy and suggest that ATF4 may serve as an intervention target.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13019-026-04282-8.Keywords: Myocardial ischemia-reperfusion injury, m6A modification, ATF4, Sestrin2, GSK3β, Glucose/lipid metabolism

Introduction

Myocardial ischemia-reperfusion injury (MIRI), characterized by cardiomyocyte apoptosis, oxidative stress, and mitochondrial dysfunction, represents a major complication following revascularization in acute myocardial infarction (AMI) patients, ultimately leading to heart failure and poor prognosis [1]. The pathological and molecular bases of MIRI involve reactive oxygen species burst, calcium overload, inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, and microvascular damage, including microvascular obstruction and leakage [2]. Although reperfusion therapy effectively salvages ischemic myocardium, current clinical interventions (e.g., antioxidants and calcium channel blockers) have limited efficacy against MIRI. Moreover, the translation of cardioprotective strategies from preclinical models to clinical practice remains a major challenge [3–5]. Therefore, elucidating the molecular network underlying glucose and lipid metabolic imbalance in MIRI is crucial for developing novel therapeutic targets.

Cardiac energy metabolism relies predominantly on mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation, in which the coordinated utilization of glucose and fatty acids maintains myocardial homeostasis [6]. During ischemia, hypoxia forces cardiomyocytes to switch to anaerobic glycolysis, leading to lactate accumulation and ATP depletion. Upon reperfusion, mitochondrial electron transport chain dysfunction triggers a burst of reactive oxygen species (ROS), while compensatory fatty acid β-oxidation exacerbates glucose/lipid metabolic imbalance [7]. Such dysregulation not only impairs ATP synthesis but also activates endoplasmic reticulum stress (ERS) and promotes the opening of the mitochondrial permeability transition pore (mPTP), thereby driving programmed cell death [6]. Therefore, restoring metabolic equilibrium may serve as a breakthrough strategy for mitigating MIRI.

Activating transcription factor 4 (ATF4) serves as a central regulator of the integrated stress response (ISR), coordinating cellular adaptation to stress signals such as endoplasmic reticulum stress (ERS), hypoxia, and nutrient deprivation by modulating the expression of downstream target genes essential for cell survival [8, 9]. Recent studies have revealed a dynamic expression pattern of ATF4 in myocardial ischemic injury. During the early ischemic phase, ATF4 mitigates cellular damage by upregulating pro-survival genes such as CHOP and GADD34, whereas its overactivation during reperfusion may intensify oxidative stress and apoptosis [10, 11]. Notably, ATF4 directly binds to the promoter regions of genes involved in glucose metabolism (e.g., GLUT1 and PDK4) as well as lipid metabolism regulators such as CPT1A, highlighting its pivotal role in metabolic reprogramming [12–14].

N6-methyladenosine (m6A) is the most prevalent post-transcriptional modification in eukaryotic mRNA. RNA‑binding motif protein 15 (RBM15) is a key m6A “writer” that facilitates the assembly of the methyltransferase complex and promotes methylation of specific mRNA targets [15]. Notably, the ATF4 mRNA itself contains conserved m6A modification sites, and its translation efficiency is influenced by m6A-dependent regulatory mechanisms involving METTL16 [16] and FTO [17], among others. These findings suggest that m6A modification may regulate ATF4 expression, thereby modulating downstream metabolic pathways.

Sestrin2 is a stress-induced protein that modulates glucose and lipid metabolism primarily through activation of the AMPK/mTOR pathway, while also mitigating oxidative damage via an Nrf2-dependent mechanism [18–20]. In cardiomyocytes, overexpression of Sestrin2 inhibits GSK3β activity [21], a multifunctional kinase whose phosphorylation state regulates mitochondrial permeability transition pore (mPTP) opening, mitochondrial dynamics, and autophagic flux. Recent evidence suggests that ATF4 directly binds to the Sestrin2 promoter region to promote its transcription. Additionally, GSK3β inhibitors have been shown to enhance mitochondrial complex I activity, thereby improving myocardial energy metabolism [22–24]. Collectively, these findings highlight the potential of the ATF4/Sestrin2/GSK3β axis as a critical regulatory pathway for the control of metabolism and mitochondrial function.

Building on the research background outlined above, we propose the following hypothesis: In MIRI, ATF4 expression is upregulated through m6A modification. This upregulation enhances Sestrin2 expression and inhibits GSK3β activity, thereby preserving mitochondrial function, restoring the imbalance in glucose/lipid metabolism, and ultimately reducing cardiomyocyte damage. To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to integrate m6A epitranscriptomic regulation, the transcription factor ATF4, and downstream metabolic effectors into the MIRI research framework. Our findings not only offers novel insights into the molecular mechanisms of metabolic dysregulation but also provides a theoretical foundation for developing precision therapeutic strategies based on RNA epigenetic modifications.

Methods

Bioinformatics analysis

Transcriptomic data from MIRI patients and healthy controls were retrieved from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) databases. To evaluate the differential expression of ATF4 between MIRI and control groups, we analyzed the GSE67308 dataset, which consists of 8 cardiac tissue samples from mice subjected to ischemia-reperfusion (I/R) injury (MIRI group) and 8 cardiac tissue samples from sham-operated control mice without surgical intervention (normal group). The mRNA expression levels of ATF4 were compared between myocardial tissues from MIRI group and those from the control group. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) and Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analyses were utilized to identify metabolic pathways associated with ATF4.

Cell culture and model construction

HL-1 cells were purchased from the Cell Bank of the Shanghai Institute of Cell Biology, Chinese Academy of Sciences. And cultured in Claycomb supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 0.1 mM norepinephrine, and 2 mM L-glutamine under standard conditions of 5% CO2 at 37℃. A hypoxia/reoxygenation (H/R) model using HL-1 cardiomyocytes was employed to simulate the pathological process of MIRI. For hypoxia treatment, glucose-free DMEM (Gibco) was used, and the medium was nitrogen-saturated for 30 min to eliminate dissolved oxygen. The cells were then transferred to this glucose-free hypoxic medium and incubated in a hypoxic chamber with a gas mixture of 95% N₂ and 5% CO₂ at 37 °C for 12 h to simulate myocardial ischemia. For reoxygenation, after the hypoxic phase, the glucose-free hypoxic medium was discarded, and cells were washed 2–3 times with PBS. Fresh DMEM medium containing serum and glucose was added, and the cells were cultured in a standard incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO₂ for 24 h, completing the myocardial ischemia-reperfusion injury model.

Gene silencing and overexpression

Gene knockdown was performed using ATF4‑specific shRNA (sh‑ATF4, Santa Cruz) and negative control shRNA (sh‑NC, Santa Cruz). RBM15‑specific shRNA (sh‑RBM15, Santa Cruz was also used. For overexpression, the full‑length coding sequence of human ATF4 was cloned into the pcDNA3.1(+) vector (oe‑ATF4), and the full‑length coding sequence of Sestrin2 was inserted into the pLVX‑IRES‑ZsGreen1 lentiviral vector (oe‑Sestrin2). HL‑1 cells were transfected with 50 nM shRNA or 1 µg/mL plasmid using Lipofectamine 3000 transfection reagent (Invitrogen) for 48 h. The knockdown or overexpression efficiency was verified by western blot analysis.

CCK-8 assay for cell viability

Cell viability was assessed using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8, Dojindo). Logarithmic phase cells were seeded at a density of 4 × 10³ cells per well in a 96-well plate and treated according to the experimental conditions. After 48 h of incubation, 10 µL of CCK-8 reagent was added to each well, and the cells were incubated for an additional 2 h. Cell viability was assessed by measuring the optical density (OD) at 450 nm.

Flow cytometry for detection of cell apoptosis

Cell apoptosis was assessed using Annexin-V-FITC/PI (BD Biosciences) staining. Cells were digested with trypsin, and the resulting suspension was collected. The cells were then resuspended in pre-chilled PBS, followed by centrifugation to remove the supernatant. The cell pellet was incubated with 300 µL of 1× Binding Buffer and 5 µL of Annexin V-FITC for 10 min. Afterward, 5 µL of PI and 200 µL of 1× Binding Buffer were added, and the cells were incubated for an additional 5 min. Apoptotic cells were analyzed using flow cytometry.

RNA extraction

Total RNA was extracted from HL‑1 cells using Trizol reagent (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA concentration was determined by spectrophotometry.

RT-qPCR

Each sample was measured in triplicate. The following components were combined in a total volume of 10 µL in an 8-well reaction tube: 1 µL of primer (3 µM), 1 µL of template, 3 µL of DEPC-treated water, and 5 µL of SYBR Green. RT-qPCR data were analyzed using the 2−△△CT method, with the reference gene used as an internal control. The relative expression levels of the target gene were calculated based on the CT values.

Total protein extraction

Total protein was extracted using RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime) containing 1% phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF).

Western blot

Cells were lysed using RIPA buffer (Beyotime), and protein concentration was quantified using the BCA protein assay kit (Thermo Scientific). The proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred to a PVDF membrane (Millipore). After blocking with 5% skim milk for 1 h, the membrane was incubated overnight with the primary antibody. Following this, the membrane was incubated with an HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (CST) for 1 h at room temperature. Chemiluminescent substrate was applied, and the membrane was scanned to capture images, which were then saved using a chemiluminescence imaging system. Primary antibodies included: ATF4 (Cat#FNab10416) and RBM15 (Cat# FNab07164) purchased from Wuhan Fine Biotech Co., Ltd, Sestrin2 (Cat# 21346-1-AP) purchased from Proteintech. GSK3β (Cat#ab185141), p-GSK3β (Cat# ab68476), and β-actin (Cat#ab8226) purchased from Abcam and diluted according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

ATP content assay

Cells were collected and lysed using an extraction buffer to release ATP. The samples and reagents were added to a 96-well plate, with the substrate solution added first, followed by the enzyme solution and fluorescent dye. After mixing thoroughly, the plate was incubated under the conditions specified in the manufacturer’s instructions (Abcam). Fluorescent intensity was measured using a fluorescence microplate reader, with the excitation wavelength typically set between 499 and 502 nm and the emission wavelength between 521 and 530 nm. ATP concentrations in the samples were determined using a standard curve.

JC-1 staining for mitochondrial membrane potential

Reagents were prepared and diluted according to the instructions provided in the kit (Abcam). A suitable amount of the sample was transferred to a test tube, and 1 mL of the JC-1 staining working solution was added to the culture dish, ensuring thorough mixing with the cells. The cells were incubated at 37 °C for 20 min. After the incubation, the supernatant was discarded, and the cells were washed 2–3 times with JC-1 buffer. Subsequently, 2 mL of PBS was added, and mitochondrial membrane potential was assessed using a laser confocal microscope.

LDH activity assay

The assay was performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Jiancheng). To each well of a 96-well plate, the following reagents were added: 10 µL of sample, 140 µL of extract, 20 µL of NADH, 10 µL of pyruvate, and 20 µL of colorimetric reagent. After thorough mixing, the reaction was allowed to proceed at room temperature. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm immediately (A1) and again after 10 min (A2). LDH activity was calculated using the formula provided.

Glucose uptake assay

Cells were seeded in a 96-well plate and cultured to the desired density. 2-Deoxy-D-glucose (2-DG, Abcam, typically 1 mM) was added to the culture medium, and the cells were incubated for 10–30 min. The reaction was terminated by the addition of acidic lysis buffer, followed by neutralization with buffer and enzymatic reaction reagents (Abcam). Absorbance was measured at 570–610 nm using a microplate reader, and glucose uptake was calculated using a standard curve.

Lactate accumulation assay

Cell or tissue samples were weighed (g) and homogenized in 1:5 to 1:10 volumes of extraction buffer (Abcam). The homogenate was incubated in a 75 °C water bath for 20 min, followed by centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 10 min. The supernatant was collected and applied to a chromatographic column (Abcam). A 10 µL sample was injected, with the column maintained at 30 °C and a flow rate of 0.4 mL/min. Lactate concentration was determined by measuring absorbance at 210 nm, with retention time and peak area recorded for both standard and sample solutions.

Triglyceride content measurement

Reagents were prepared and diluted according to the kit instructions (Abcam). A suitable amount of sample was added to a test tube, followed by the addition of isopropanol, and mixed to dissolve the triglycerides. Gallic acid propyl ester solution and concentrated sulfuric acid were added, gently mixed, and incubated in a 37 °C water bath for 10–15 min to allow the triglycerides to react with the reagents and produce a colorimetric product. After cooling to room temperature, absorbance was measured at 540–570 nm using a spectrophotometer.

Cholesterol content measurement

Reagents were prepared and diluted according to the kit instructions (Abcam). A suitable amount of sample was added to a test tube, followed by the addition of anhydrous ethanol to extract cholesterol. The sample was centrifuged, and the supernatant was collected. O-phthalaldehyde reagent and concentrated sulfuric acid were added, and the mixture was incubated in a 60 °C water bath for 15–20 min to allow cholesterol to react with the reagents, forming a colorimetric product. After cooling to room temperature, absorbance was measured at 550–570 nm using a spectrophotometer.

MeRIP-qPCR

A/G immunomagnetic beads (Abcam) and m6A antibody (Abcam) were pre-mixed according to the standard procedure in the kit to prepare the antibody-bead mixture. The pre-mixed immunomagnetic beads and m6A antibody were then added to total RNA or mRNA, and m6A-immunomagnetic beads were enriched using a magnetic stand. The RNA-antibody complexes were digested with protease, and after digestion with m6A antibody, the remaining RNA was eluted. The eluted RNA and input RNA were reverse transcribed using random primers. Specific primers for MeRIP-qPCR were designed, and qPCR reactions were performed on RNA extracted from input, m6A-IP, and IgG-IP samples. The CT values were calculated for each sample.

Actinomycin D half-life determination

Cells were seeded into culture dishes and treated with actinomycin D (Abcam) at appropriate concentrations for specific time points (0, 1, 2, 4, 6, 12, 24 h). At each time point, at least one sample was collected. The concentration of actinomycin D in the samples was quantified using techniques such as HPLC (Agilent). The concentration-time relationship was plotted as a logarithmic curve, and the half-life (T1/2) was calculated using first-order elimination or nonlinear regression methods.

Data analysis and statistics

Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 9.0. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test was used for comparisons. Statistical significance was set at *P < 0.05 and **P < 0.01.

Results

Knockdown of ATF4 attenuates the progression of the hypoxia/reoxygenation (H/R) cell model

Bioinformatics analysis of transcriptomic data from MIRI patients in the GEO database revealed that the mRNA expression level of ATF4 in myocardial tissue from MIRI patients was significantly higher than that in healthy controls (Fig. 1A). Previous studies have also confirmed the abnormal expression of ATF4 in ventricular myocytes of failing heart [25]. Western blot analysis confirmed that ATF4 knockdown significantly reduced the expression of Sestrin2 protein, while ATF4 overexpression markedly increased Sestrin2 protein levels (Fig. 1B). Compared to the H/R group, the sh-ATF4 group exhibited a significant decrease in cell viability (Fig. 1C) and an increase in apoptosis rate to 9.21% (vs. 6.73% H/R group), whereas the oe-ATF4 group showed significantly enhanced cell viability and reduced apoptosis rate (Fig. 1D). Further analysis of mitochondrial function revealed that ATF4 knockdown led to a significant reduction in mitochondrial membrane potential (Fig. 1E), indicative of mitochondrial dysfunction.

(A) Analysis of GEO databases revealed significantly higher ATF4 mRNA expression levels in myocardial tissues of MIRI patients compared to healthy controls. (B) Western blot showed a significant reduction in Sestrin2 protein expression after ATF4 knockdown, whereas ATF4 overexpression markedly increased Sestrin2 protein levels. (C) CCK-8 assay revealed decreased cell viability in the sh-ATF4 group compared to the H/R group, while the oe-ATF4 group exhibited increased cell viability. (D) Flow cytometry demonstrated a marked increase in apoptosis rate in the sh-ATF4 group, whereas the oe-ATF4 group showed reduced apoptosis. (E) JC-1 staining indicated that ATF4 knockdown significantly reduced mitochondrial membrane potential, while the overexpression group displayed the opposite effect. Significance: *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01

As ATF4 is a key effector molecule of the integrated stress response and a core component of the endoplasmic reticulum stress signaling pathway, we further investigated whether endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress is involved in hypoxia/reoxygenation (H/R) injury and regulated by ATF4. Western blotting was performed to detect the protein expression levels of ER stress-related markers including p-PERK, PERK, p-eIF2α, eIF2α, Bip, CHOP, and ATF4. Compared with the control group, the protein ratios of p-PERK/PERK and p-eIF2α/eIF2α as well as the protein levels of Bip and CHOP were significantly elevated in the H/R group, confirming the activation of ER stress. Knockdown of ATF4 markedly reduced the protein levels of Bip, CHOP, and ATF4, but exerted no significant effects on the ratios of p-PERK/PERK and p-eIF2α/eIF2α. In contrast, overexpression of ATF4 significantly upregulated the levels of Bip, CHOP, and ATF4 (Fig. S1A-H). These results demonstrate that ATF4 positively regulates ER stress, and that H/R-induced ER stress activation is partially dependent on ATF4.

Modulation of ATF4 exerts bidirectional effects on H/R-induced metabolic dysregulation in cardiomyocytes

To further clarify the regulatory role of ATF4 in glucose and lipid metabolism during MIRI, we performed ATF4 knockdown and overexpression prior to establishing the H/R model in HL-1 cardiomyocytes (Fig. 1B). Further analysis of mitochondrial function revealed that ATF4 knockdown led to a significant reduction in ATP levels (Fig. 2A), confirming mitochondrial dysfunction. Since mitochondrial function is closely associated with the regulation of glucose and lipid metabolism [6], we next evaluated glucose metabolism. The sh-ATF4 group exhibited a significant increase in LDH activity (Fig. 2B) and lactate production (Fig. 2D), while glucose uptake was substantially reduced (Fig. 2C). Conversely, the oe-ATF4 group exhibited the opposite alterations. In lipid metabolism, free fatty acids (FFA) (Fig. 2E) and triglyceride (TG) levels (Fig. 2F) were markedly elevated, and CPT1A activity was significantly decreased (Fig. 2G). In contrast, ATF4 overexpression significantly decreased FFA and TG levels while increasing CPT1A activity. CPT1A is a key enzyme in lipid metabolism, playing a crucial role in fatty acid transport, β-oxidation, and the maintenance of lipid homeostasis [26]. Of note, prolonged ischemia may induce mitochondrial damage and accelerated protein degradation, ultimately resulting in reduced protein levels. These results indicate that ATF4 plays a critical role in regulating mitochondrial function and glucose/lipid metabolism in MIRI.

(D-I) Metabolic analysis: sh-ATF4 group exhibited elevated LDH activity (D), lactate levels (F), FFA (G) and TG (H), along with reduced glucose uptake (E) and CPT1A activity (I), whereas the oe-ATF4 group showed opposite metabolic profiles. To determine whether endoplasmic reticulum stress is required for ATF4-mediated metabolic regulation, this study employed the PERK-specific inhibitor GSK2606414 for intervention. HL-1 cells were pretreated with GSK2606414 (1 µM, 2 h) before hypoxia/reoxygenation (H/R) exposure, with groups established in the presence or absence of ATF4 overexpression. Western blot results showed that GSK2606414 treatment markedly reduced the protein levels of p-PERK and p-eIF2α, but had no obvious effect on total PERK and total eIF2α (Fig. S2A). Metabolic analysis revealed that, compared with the H/R group, PERK inhibition alone significantly exacerbated metabolic disorders: ATP levels and glucose uptake were markedly decreased, lactate accumulation was increased, free fatty acid and triglyceride levels were elevated, accompanied by reduced CPT1A activity (Fig. S2B-G). Importantly, combined overexpression of ATF4 significantly reversed the above metabolic effects. These results indicate that ATF4 plays a critical role in regulating glycolipid metabolism in cardiomyocytes under hypoxia/reoxygenation injury.

ATF4 expression in myocardial ischemia-reperfusion injury is regulated by RBM15-induced m6A modification

Previous studies have suggested that the regulation of ATF4 in ischemia-reperfusion injury is influenced by m6A modification [8]. To further elucidate the specific mechanism of ATF4’s epigenetic regulation in MIRI, we conducted bioinformatics analyses. The results revealed a significant correlation between ATF4 and m6A “writers” (Fig. 3A), with a particularly strong positive correlation with RBM15 (Fig. 3B). Subsequent validation in HL-1 cells demonstrated that modulating RBM15 (knockdown/overexpression) bidirectionally regulated ATF4 expression (Fig. 3C). Additionally, MeRIP-qPCR analysis showed a significant reduction in m6A modification level of ATF4 mRNA in the sh-RBM15 group, while the oe-RBM15 group exhibited significantly elevated m6A levels (Fig. 3D). To assess the role of m6A in regulating ATF4 stability, we blocked new RNA synthesis using actinomycin D. This approach revealed markedly decreased ATF4 mRNA stability in the sh-RBM15 group compared to control (Fig. 3E). Collectively, these results indicate that RBM15 enhances ATF4 mRNA stability through m6A modification.

(C) Regulating RBM15 significantly affects the expression level of ATF4. (D) MeRIP-qPCR showed that regulating RBM15 significantly affects the m6A modification level of ATF4. (E) Actinomycin D assay revealed that the half-life of ATF4 mRNA in the sh-RBM15 group was shortened, indicate that RBM15 enhances ATF4 mRNA stability via m6A modification. Significance: *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01

ATF4 modulates mitochondrial function and glucose/lipid metabolic homeostasis via the Sestrin2/GSK3β axis

We further explored the molecular mechanisms by which ATF4 regulates glucose and lipid metabolism in MIRI. Knockdown of ATF4 resulted in significantly reduced Sestrin2 protein expression alongside markedly increased GSK3β phosphorylation. Overexpression of Sestrin2 (sh-ATF4 + oe-Sestrin2 group) reversed these effects (Fig. 4A). ATF4 knockdown significantly reduced cell viability, which was restored by Sestrin2 overexpression (Fig. 4B). Similarly, flow cytometry analysis showed that overexpression of Sestrin2 reversed the increased apoptosis observed in the ATF4 knockdown group (Fig. 4C). Moreover, Sestrin2 restoration recovered mitochondrial membrane potential and ATP production (Fig. 4D, E). In glucose metabolism, overexpression of Sestrin2 reversed the effects of ATF4 knockdown on LDH activity (Fig. 4F), glucose uptake (Fig. 4G), and lactate production (Fig. 4H). In lipid metabolism, Sestrin2 overexpression also reversed the impact of ATF4 knockdown on free fatty acid (FFA) levels (Fig. 4I), triglyceride (TG) levels (Fig. 4J), and CPT1A activity (Fig. 4K). Taken together, these results suggest that ATF4 regulates mitochondrial function and glucose/lipid metabolism in MIRI via the Sestrin2/GSK3β axis.

(A) Western blot showed that ATF4 knockdown decreased Sestrin2 protein and increased GSK3β phosphorylation which were reversed by Sestrin2 overexpression. (B-C) Overexpression of Sestrin2 restored cell viability (B) and reduced apoptosis (C) in the sh-ATF4 group. (D-E) ATP assay (D) and JC-1 staining (E) confirmed mitochondrial functional recovery. (F-K) Overexpression of Sestrin2 reversed sh-ATF4-induced alterations in LDH activity (F), glucose uptake (G), lactate (H), FFA (I), TG (J), and CPT1A activity (K). Given that GSK3β is known to regulate mitochondrial dynamics and mitochondrial permeability transition pore (mPTP) opening, the present study examined these downstream events. The protein expression levels of mitochondrial fusion markers (MFN1, MFN2, OPA1) and the mitochondrial fission marker DRP1 were detected by Western blotting. Hypoxia/reoxygenation (H/R) injury significantly decreased the expression of MFN1, MFN2, and OPA1, while markedly increasing DRP1 expression. Knockdown of ATF4 aggravated these changes, whereas overexpression of ATF4 reversed them (Figures S3A-E). Importantly, overexpression of Sestrin2 in ATF4-knockdown cells significantly rescued the imbalance in mitochondrial dynamics: compared with the ATF4 knockdown alone group, the expression of MFN1, MFN2, and OPA1 was increased, while DRP1 expression was decreased (Figures S4A-E), suggesting that Sestrin2 mediates the regulatory effect of ATF4 on mitochondrial dynamics.

Subsequently, mPTP opening was evaluated by measuring calcium retention capacity (CRC), where a lower CRC indicates a higher degree of mPTP opening. Compared with the control group, H/R injury reduced CRC; ATF4 knockdown further decreased CRC, while ATF4 overexpression elevated CRC (Figure S3F). Co-overexpression of Sestrin2 reversed the effects induced by ATF4 knockdown (Figure S4F), confirming that ATF4 inhibits mPTP opening via the Sestrin2/GSK3β axis.

Discussion

This study uncovers the dual role of ATF4 in MIRI, in which its expression is regulated by RBM15-mediated m6A modification, and ATF4 improves mitochondrial function and metabolic homeostasis through the Sestrin2/GSK3β axis. Knockdown of ATF4 exacerbates H/R-induced apoptosis and metabolic dysregulation in glucose and lipid pathways, while m6A modification stabilizes ATF4 mRNA, thereby maintaining its expression levels. Further mechanistic investigation reveals that ATF4 upregulates Sestrin2, which inhibits GSK3β activity, restores mitochondrial membrane potential, promotes ATP production, and corrects imbalances in glucose uptake and fatty acid oxidation.

ATF4, as a central regulator in the cellular stress response, plays a crucial role in MIRI through its dynamic expression and functional regulation. Recent studies have highlighted the pivotal role of ATF4 in ischemia-reperfusion injury, where it regulates various signaling pathways involved in the initiation and progression of myocardial ischemia-reperfusion injury [8–11]. In the context of glucose metabolism, ATF4 has been shown to regulate key enzymes involved in glucose metabolism, thereby promoting energy balance in cardiomyocytes [13, 14]. Moreover, previous research has demonstrated that ATF4 enhances glucose uptake and metabolism by upregulating glucose transporters (GLUT4) and glycolytic enzymes, thus providing an essential energy source during ischemic conditions [12]. In lipid metabolism, ATF4 also plays a critical role. It regulates both fatty acid synthesis and degradation and is involved in controlling fatty acid oxidation pathways [27, 28]. Research has shown that ATF4 modulates fatty acid metabolism by regulating the expression of enzymes such as FAS and ACSL, which are involved in fatty acid synthesis [29, 30]. These fatty acid metabolites not only serve as crucial energy sources for myocardial metabolism but also directly influence oxidative stress within the cell, playing a significant role in MIRI. In the present study, we further established that ATF4 positively regulates ER stress downstream of PERK‑eIF2α, and that the PERK‑ATF4 axis is essential for maintaining glucose and lipid homeostasis, as PERK inhibition exacerbated metabolic dysfunction while ATF4 overexpression reversed these effects (Figs. S1 and S2). These findings integrate ER stress, ATF4 activation, and metabolic regulation into a coherent pathway in MIRI.

Regarding the mechanistic of ATF4 regulation, m6A modification was identified as a key regulatory mechanism. Previous research has demonstrated that m6A modification of ATF4 affects its mRNA stability and translation efficiency, thereby regulating its expression in myocardial ischemia-reperfusion injury (MIRI). Wang et al. [8] found that in MIRI, WTAP regulates ATF4 expression by influencing the m6A methylation of ATF4 mRNA, and the inhibitory effects of WTAP on endoplasmic reticulum stress and apoptosis are dependent on ATF4. Furthermore, ATF4’s m6A modification has been shown to have a significant impact on its function in autoimmune thyroid diseases [31] and intraocular inflammatory diseases [17].

The Sestrin2/GSK3β axis has been confirmed as a key downstream effector pathway of ATF4 in this study. Sestrin2 preserves mitochondrial function by inhibiting GSK3β activity, which is consistent with previous findings [32, 33]. However, this study further elucidates that ATF4 mediates this process by directly regulating Sestrin2 expression. Notably, excessive activation of GSK3β can induce mPTP opening and intensify apoptosis [21, 34, 35]. In our study, we observed that ATF4 deficiency resulted in increased dephosphorylation of GSK3β, suggesting that ATF4 may amplify the damage through a negative feedback loop. This mechanism complements the ATF4-m6A regulatory network proposed by Wang et al. [8], thereby highlighting the interaction between metabolism and epigenetics.

While this study provides validation of the ATF4-m6A-Sestrin2/GSK3β axis in a cell model, several limitations must be addressed. First, the study predominantly used the HL-1 cell line, and we fully acknowledge the lack of in vivo experiments. The present study mainly elucidates the molecular mechanism in a well‑controlled cellular system; therefore, further validation in animal models and clinical samples is required to confirm the generalizability of these findings. In our follow‑up research, we plan to perform in vivo validation using a mouse myocardial ischemia‑reperfusion model combined with AAV‑mediated ATF4 modulation. Second, m6A modification likely involves other “writers” or “readers” (e.g., METTL3/YTHDF1), and their potential synergistic effects warrant further investigation. Lastly, the specific effector molecules downstream of GSK3β, such as mPTP-related proteins, have yet to be fully defined and should be explored using omics technologies.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that ATF4 regulates the Sestrin2/GSK3β axis via an m6A-dependent mechanism, thereby improving mitochondrial function and metabolic balance in MIRI. This discovery provides new insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying MIRI and lays the groundwork for the development of therapeutic strategies based on RNA modifications or metabolic reprogramming. Future research should focus on identifying specific m6A regulators or GSK3β inhibitors to facilitate clinical application.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that RBM15-mediated m⁶A modification upregulates ATF4, which in turn activates the Sestrin2/GSK3β axis to improve mitochondrial function, correct glucose/lipid metabolic imbalance, and preserve mitochondrial dynamics in myocardial ischemia-reperfusion injury. These findings identify ATF4 as a potential therapeutic target for metabolic reprogramming in MIRI.
