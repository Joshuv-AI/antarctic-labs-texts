Mitochondrial quality control mechanisms as molecular targets for impaired lung development: from fetuses to neonates
Authors: Ziwei Zhu, Liang Zhang, Jianhua Fu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractLung development is a highly programmed and energy-dependent dynamic process, with the fetal and neonatal periods representing critical developmental windows. Emerging evidence indicates that mitochondrial quality control (MQC) mechanisms—including mitophagy, mitochondrial dynamics (fusion/fission), and mitochondrial biogenesis—play a central regulatory role in pulmonary development and injury responses during these periods. While, under stressors in the maternal-fetal environment or hyperoxia therapy, transiently upregulate MQC may serve as protective compensatory response, MQC dysregulation can lead to the accumulation of dysfunctional mitochondria, exacerbated oxidative stress, aberrant cellular metabolism, and amplified inflammatory responses. These disruptions contribute to pathological sequelae such as impaired alveolar epithelial differentiation, aberrant pulmonary vascular remodeling, diminished antioxidant capacity, and failed tissue repair. In this review, we summarize recent mechanistic advances in MQC-mediated regulation of lung development across fetal, preterm, and term infant models. Therapeutic strategies targeting MQC have shown promise in preclinical studies, with stem cell therapy and precision-targeted antioxidant delivery emerging as potential approaches to restore mitochondrial homeostasis and promote alveologenesis. We advocate for timely, mitochondria-centered interventions during fetal and neonatal periods to improve pulmonary outcomes and sustain lifelong respiratory health.Keywords: Mitochondrial quality control, Lung development, Bronchopulmonary dysplasia, Mitochondrial homeostasis

Introduction

Human lung development constitutes an exquisitely regulated and highly complex dynamic process, classically divided into five distinct stages: embryonic, pseudoglandular, canalicular, saccular, and alveolar [1]. This intricate developmental program is coordinately controlled by multifaceted regulatory networks involving genetic determinants, nuclear transcription factors, hormonal signals, growth factors, and environmental modulators [2]. Initiated during the fourth week of embryogenesis, pulmonary development achieves functional competency by 37 weeks of gestation at term delivery. However, alveolar maturation continues postnatally through a process of secondary septation, with both alveolar number and volume progressively increasing until approximately 8 years of age [3]. The transition from intrauterine to extrauterine existence represents a profound physiological challenge, wherein the lungs - as the primary interface with the external environment - require meticulous developmental regulation. Preterm birth disrupts this precisely orchestrated developmental continuum, and lung developmental impairments resulting from preterm delivery, which occurs at a global prevalence exceeding 10%, are associated with an increased susceptibility to a spectrum of chronic pulmonary diseases [4, 5]. Notably, suboptimal pulmonary health in adulthood can be etiologically traced to compromised developmental trajectories during critical growth periods [6] Consequently, targeted interventions addressing pulmonary developmental disturbances hold substantial promise for improving both comprehensive neonatal management and long-term clinical outcomes in preterm populations [7].

Mitochondria, as essential intracellular organelles, play a pivotal role in cellular energy metabolism, signal transduction, and the maintenance of cellular homeostasis [8]. Due to their susceptibility to damage from diverse endogenous and exogenous stressors, mitochondria undergo continuous renewal to preserve their functional integrity. Mitochondrial quality control (MQC) constitutes an integrated surveillance system encompassing three fundamental processes: (1) mitophagy for selective degradation of damaged mitochondria, (2) mitochondrial dynamics (fusion and fission) regulating organelle morphology and distribution, and (3) mitochondrial biogenesis for generation of new mitochondrial components. Through coordinated regulation of mitochondrial quality, morphology, spatial organization, and functional capacity, the MQC system orchestrates organelle-level homeostasis. This sophisticated regulatory network enables adaptive responses to fluctuating environmental demands, thereby ensuring cellular viability and maintaining systemic physiological equilibrium [9–11].

In recent years, MQC has emerged as a pivotal research focus in diverse diseases, providing critical insights into early disease prevention and intervention [12]. Cutting-edge research underscores the determinative role of MQC in ensuring spatiotemporal precision during embryonic and organ development, with mechanistic dysregulation potentially leading to developmental defects in embryos or multiple vital organs. Notably, impaired mitochondrial dynamics have been implicated in female reproductive health, adversely affecting oocyte quality, fertilization competence, and embryonic development, thereby contributing to infertility or early pregnancy loss [13]. Emerging evidence further posits mitochondrial dysfunction as a pathogenic driver in preterm birth [14, 15]. Given the indispensable role of mitochondria in cardiac development and homeostasis, disrupted MQC may precipitate prenatal cardiomyopathy; conversely, enhanced mitochondrial biogenesis has been shown to improve cardiomyocyte viability and mitigate pathological cardiac remodeling [16, 17]. Neuronal homeostasis exhibits stringent dependence on mitochondrial activity and MQC efficiency. Deficits in mitophagy have been mechanistically linked to neurodevelopmental disorders, including autism spectrum disorders [18]. Systemic metabolic disturbances may arise from aberrant mitochondrial dynamics, manifesting as skeletal muscle atrophy [19]. Of particular clinical relevance, MQC impairment in pulmonary systems disrupts alveolar epithelial cell proliferation, induces apoptosis of alveolar type II (ATII) cells, and elevates susceptibility to bronchopulmonary disorders—including bronchitis, asthma, pulmonary fibrosis, and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease [20–25].This review provides an in-depth discussion on recent pathological research advances regarding mitochondrial quality control-related fetal and neonatal impaired lung development, as well as their potential as next-generation therapeutic targets.

Overview of mitochondrial quality control

Mitophagy

Autophagy is an evolutionarily conserved lysosome-dependent process through which cells degrade and recycle intracellular components. Mitophagy represents a specialized form of organelle-selective autophagy that specifically targets damaged or superfluous mitochondria for elimination, thereby maintaining mitochondrial quality control and regulating mitochondrial abundance [26]. During mitophagic flux, damaged mitochondria exhibiting loss of mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP) are selectively engulfed by autophagosomal membranes to form mitophagosomes. These vesicular structures subsequently undergo lysosomal fusion and degradation, with the resulting catabolites being recycled back into the cytoplasm for biosynthetic reuse [27].

Mitophagy mechanisms are classified into ubiquitin-dependent and receptor-dependent pathways. The PTEN-induced putative kinase 1(PINK1)- Parkin RBR E3 ubiquitin-protein ligase pathway (Parkin) represents the most extensively studied ubiquitin-dependent mechanism [28]. PINK1, a serine/threonine kinase, accumulates on the outer mitochondrial membrane following mitochondrial damage, promoting Parkin translocation and activation. This converts Parkin into an active E3 ubiquitin ligase that mediates ubiquitination of mitochondrial proteins, leading to autophagic receptors such as p62/SQSTM1 aggregation and LC3 specific binding, which facilitates autophagosome assembly and damaged mitochondrial clearance. PINK1 can also directly recruit autophagy receptors OPTN and NDP52 through ubiquitin phosphorylation. Other E3 ubiquitin ligases involved in mitophagy include Smad Ubiquitination Regulatory Factor 1 (SMURF1), Mitochondrial E3 Ubiquitin Protein Ligase 1 (MUL1), and Glycoprotein 78 (Gp78). The receptor-dependent pathway involves mitochondrial outer membrane mitophagy receptors containing LC3-interacting regions that directly bind LC3/GABARAP proteins without requiring ubiquitination [29]. Major mitophagy receptors include Nip3-like protein X/ BCL2 Interacting Protein 3 Like (NIX also known as BNIP3L), BCL2 Interacting Protein 3 (BNIP3), and FUN14 Domain Containing 1 (FUNDC1) [30].

Mitophagy selectively eliminates irreversibly damaged mitochondria to maintain cellular homeostasis, serving as a central executor of MQC networks while playing pivotal roles in cellular metabolic reprogramming. This selective autophagic process can be triggered by diverse physiological and pathological stimuli, including nutrient or energy deprivation, oxidative stress, hypoxia, cellular senescence, and differentiation signals [31, 32].

Mitochondrial dynamics

Mitochondrial dynamics comprises two fundamental processes: mitochondrial fusion and fission. Mitochondrial fusion involves the sequential merging of outer membranes followed by inner membranes between adjacent mitochondria. This process is mediated by mitofusin 1/2 (Mfn1/2) located on the outer mitochondrial membrane and optic atrophy 1 (OPA1) which facilitates inner membrane fusion and maintains cristae structure [33]. Through fusion, mitochondria achieve complementation by exchanging mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA), proteins, and lipids, thereby enhancing organellar tolerance to mtDNA mutations and restoring functional homogeneity. Mitochondrial fission divides mitochondria into smaller organelles, facilitating intracellular transport, turnover, and mitophagy while determining subcellular distribution [34]. The primary mediator of fission is dynamin-related protein 1 (Drp1), a cytosolic GTPase. Drp1 recruits receptors such as mitochondrial fission factor (Mff), fission 1 protein (Fis1), mitochondrial dynamics protein of 49 kDa (MiD49), and mitochondrial dynamics protein of 51 kDa (MiD51) to the outer mitochondrial membrane, where it binds to initiate membrane constriction [35]. Mitochondrial fission is regulated by numerous factors, including the endoplasmic reticulum(ER), lysosomes, Golgi-derived vesicles, as well as actin filaments and actin-binding regulators. The specific site of fission influences the ultimate fate of resulting fragments, determining whether they undergo degradation or participate in biogenesis [36].

Mitochondrial dynamics are critically implicated in the intracellular redistribution of mitochondria, equitable mitochondrial inheritance during cell division, and are intimately associated with mitochondrial function, cellular metabolic states, stress responses, and disease pathogenesis. Genetic knockout studies demonstrate that isolated deficiencies in either fusion or fission exert severe detrimental effects on mitochondrial function. Remarkably, concomitant loss of both fission and fusion capacity can rebalance mitochondrial dynamics and restore robust mitochondrial functionality [37]. These findings indicate that reduced levels of both fusion and fission activities remain compatible with optimal cellular and tissue function, suggesting that the appropriate equilibrium between these opposing processes is of greater physiological significance than their absolute activity levels.

Mitochondrial biogenesis

Mitochondrial biogenesis refers to the continuous and dynamically coordinated process of synthesizing new mitochondria from pre-existing mitochondria [38]. Mitochondria are semi-autonomous organelles whose functionality is dually regulated by both nuclear and mitochondrial genomes. Notably, only 13 proteins are encoded by mtDNA, while approximately 99% of mitochondrial proteins are nuclear-encoded, which are synthesized on cytoplasmic ribosomes and subsequently transported into the mitochondrial matrix [39]. Mitochondrial biogenesis encompasses three fundamental processes: (1) mtDNA replication and transcription, (2) synthesis of mitochondrial-encoded proteins, and (3) production and import of nuclear-encoded mitochondrial proteins. This integrated biogenetic program is modulated by diverse exogenous and endogenous factors, including physical activity (exercise), metabolic interventions (caloric restriction), environmental stressors (hypothermia, oxidative stress), and cellular processes (division, renewal, and differentiation) [40]. The AMP/ATP ratio, calcium ions(Ca²⁺), the NAD⁺/NADH ratio, reactive oxygen species (ROS), and nitric oxide (NO) serve as potent stimuli for mitochondrial biogenesis. This process is physiologically upregulated during growth and development, stress, and periods of heightened metabolic demand [41].

The expression of nuclear genome-encoded mitochondrial proteins is regulated through the coordinated actions of transcription factors and transcriptional coactivators. The principal transcriptional coactivators associated with mitochondrial biogenesis belong to the peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor γ coactivator-1 (PGC-1) family. In mammalian systems, PGC-1α serves as the master regulator orchestrating mitochondrial biogenesis [42]. AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) and sirtuin 1 (Sirt1) serve as upstream regulators of PGC-1α, activating this transcriptional coactivator through phosphorylation and deacetylation modifications, respectively. PGC-1α initiates a transcriptional cascade governing mitochondrial biogenesis through coordinated activation of downstream transcription factors, including nuclear respiratory factor-1 (NRF1), nuclear respiratory factor-2 (NRF2), peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor γ (PPARγ), and estrogen-related receptor-α (ERR-α). This regulatory network ultimately upregulates mitochondrial transcription factor A (TFAM) expression [43, 44]. TFAM serves as the exclusive mtDNA promoter, initiating mitochondrial genome replication, transcription, and translation. PGC-1α interacts with nuclear respiratory factors (NRFs) and TFAM to orchestrate the expression of a broad repertoire of nuclear-encoded mitochondrial proteins, thereby enhancing the mitochondrial antioxidant defense system [45].

Mitochondrial biogenesis enhances mitochondrial mass, though quantitative changes in mitochondrial populations are not exclusively attributable to de novo synthesis. Standard biomarkers of mitochondrial biogenesis include: mtDNA copy number, elevated mtDNA/nuclear DNA ratio, mitochondrial gene expression levels, and expansion of mitochondrial phospholipid membranes.

MQC network

Mitophagy, mitochondrial dynamics, and mitochondrial biogenesis operate not as isolated processes, but rather as an integrated, multi-layered cellular network interconnected through molecular hubs. This highly dynamic system establishes a continuous ‘damage-repair-clearance-regeneration’ cycle that serves as a critical gatekeeper in disease pathogenesis [46, 47].

Emerging evidence has elucidated the pivotal role of the PGC-1α-NRF1-FUNDC1 axis in orchestrating the crosstalk between mitochondrial biogenesis and mitophagy. Mechanistic studies reveal that NRF1 directly binds to the FUNDC1 promoter region, while PGC-1α enhances this transcriptional activation as a coactivator. Notably, FUNDC1 deficiency not only impairs mitophagic flux but also destabilizes PGC-1α protein, thereby suppressing biogenesis - this bidirectional regulatory imbalance manifests in multiple pathological contexts [48]. In experimental chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) models, pathologically augmented mitophagy leads to excessive depletion of functional mitochondria, consequently secondarily suppressing mitochondrial biogenesis [49]. In lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced acute lung injury (ALI) rat models, PGC-1α overexpression (both in vivo and in vitro) enhances mitophagy through upregulation of key mitophagy-related proteins including transcription factor EB (TFEB), lysosome-associated membrane protein 1 (LAMP1), microtubule-associated protein 1 light chain 3B (LC3B), and Beclin-1, which ameliorates pulmonary inflammation and attenuates alveolar edema [50].

Mitochondrial dynamics function as a “quality control hub” that couples with mitophagy and biogenesis through multiple mechanisms. Under hypoxic stress, FUNDC1 recruits DRP1 to drive mitochondrial fission, thereby promoting LC3B lipidation and Parkin-PINK1-mediated mitophagy [51]. This coupling is pathologically hyperactivated in COPD, resulting in excessive mitochondrial fragmentation and autophagy [46]. Recent studies demonstrate that in hyperoxia-induced ALI models, mitophagy increases ATII cell apoptosis via MFN1 modulation [52]. Similarly, Liu et al. reported that in pulmonary hypertension (PH) models, downregulation of PGC-1α reduces MFN2 expression [53]. During developmental organogenesis, such fine-tuned regulation of mitochondrial dynamics is particularly critical. In the perinatal heart, Mfn1/2 double knockout downregulates expression of mitochondrial biogenesis and mitophagy-related genes, ultimately precipitating lethal cardiomyopathy. This demonstrates the central role of mitochondrial dynamics in programmed mitochondrial turnover [48].

MQC imbalance in lung developmental impairment

Prenatal lung developmental impairment

The strict maternal inheritance of mitochondria implies that embryonic mitochondrial abnormalities can become distributed throughout fetal tissues. Previous studies demonstrate that pregnancy is associated with increased physiological oxidative stress, and maternal exposure to particulate matter (PM) air pollution may exacerbate mitochondrial oxidative damage, thereby impairing lung development. Alveolar epithelial cells exposed to PM2.5 exhibit impaired mitophagic flux coupled with diminished mitochondrial fusion and fission activity [54]. Epidemiological analysis of late-gestation Mexican women (35–40 weeks) revealed a significant inverse correlation between maternal PM2.5 exposure levels and umbilical cord blood mtDNA content at delivery [55]. Murine models demonstrate that high-dose intrauterine PM exposure induces developmental impairments in offspring, including somatic growth retardation and compromised pulmonary development and remodeling. Gestational exposure to elevated levels of air pollution reduces placental growth factor (PlGF) expression, elevates umbilical cord blood concentrations of IgE and interleukin-33 (IL-33), and leads to mtDNA damage [56]. Early-life exposure to PM air pollution is positively associated with biomarkers of maternal and fetal mitochondrial DNA oxidative damage, specifically mitochondrial 8-hydroxy-2’-deoxyguanosine (8-OHdG) [57]. Acute tobacco smoke exposure increases the abundance of autophagy and receptor-mediated mitophagy molecules in human primary bronchial epithelial cells [58]. Longitudinal analysis of Dominican and African-American pediatric cohorts revealed a persistent positive association between blood mtDNA copy number throughout childhood and maternal prenatal tobacco smoke exposure [59]. MtDNA copy number serves as a robust biomarker of mitochondrial biogenesis, suggesting the potential activation of compensatory mechanisms in response to mitochondrial dysfunction. Maternal cigarette smoke exposure in rats induces mitochondrial ultrastructural alterations, swelling, decreased ATP production, loss of MMP, and increased mtDNA copy number in the parabrachial respiratory group, a key brainstem nucleus for respiratory control, thereby disrupting its normal development [60]. Acute ozone exposure in rat models exacerbates pulmonary oxidative stress and upregulates the Bnip3 receptor-mediated mitophagy pathway. Notably, maternal high-fat diet during the perinatal period potentiates these pathological responses specifically in female offspring [61].

Mitochondrial dysfunction impairs pulmonary development through disruption of alveolar epithelial cell differentiation and repair processes. In idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) patients, SFTPC gene mutations induce pathological alterations including decreased MMP and compromised mitophagy in ATII cells. ATII cells in PINK1-deficient murine models exhibit characteristic mitochondrial ultrastructural abnormalities and impaired mitophagic flux, predisposing to accelerated apoptosis and progressive pulmonary fibrogenesis. Disrupted mitochondrial dynamics promote the systemic release of mtDNA, which serves not only as a diagnostic biomarker for IPF but also correlates with adverse clinical outcomes [62]. Furthermore, Mfn1/2 regulate phospholipid and cholesterol biosynthesis pathways in alveolar epithelial cells in IPF [63].

Mitochondrial activity and subcellular distribution play pivotal roles in conferring specialized cellular functions to both alveolar epithelial cells and mesenchymal myofibroblasts during secondary septation formation in murine lung development. Secretion of platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF) ligands by alveolar epithelial cells and the migratory capacity of myofibroblasts are dependent on the regulated activity and spatial distribution of mitochondria. Mitochondrial activity and spatial distribution in mesenchymal cells play a pivotal role in alveolar morphogenesis through mechanistic target of rapamycin complex 1 (mTORC1) pathway-mediated regulation [64–66].

Owing to the spatiotemporal specificity of pulmonary development, mitochondrial density continues to increase postnatally under physiological conditions to support growth and normal lung maturation. In parallel, the maturation of adult intestinal epithelial cells is critically dependent on the mitochondrial transcription factor TFAM. Embryos carrying TFAM mutations exhibit pulmonary developmental defects and demonstrate postnatal lethality [67]. Furthermore, conditional ablation of the electron transport chain Complex I subunit Ndufs2 in murine pulmonary epithelial cells during gestation results in neonatal mortality due to impaired alveolar development [68].

Postnatal lung developmental impairment

In neonatal intensive care units (NICUs), oxygen therapy remains critical for reducing mortality and complication risks associated with respiratory disorders. However, uncontrolled oxidative stress may precipitate diffuse lung injury and pulmonary fibrosis. Mitochondrial electron transport chain (ETC) components and mtDNA exhibit particular vulnerability to oxidative damage from reactive oxygen species (ROS). Compromised mitochondria generate excessive ROS, establishing a vicious cycle wherein oxidative stress induces mtDNA damage/mutations, promotes deleterious oxidative modifications of mitochondrial proteins and lipids, and ultimately triggers cell death.

Hyperoxia-Associated lung developmental impairment

Bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD) represents one of the most prevalent complications in preterm infants, exhibiting particularly high incidence among very low birth weight (VLBW) neonates [69]. While the etiology of BPD is multifactorial and its pathogenesis remains under active investigation [64, 70], diverse risk factors ultimately converge on a characteristic histopathological manifestation: arrested alveolar development. This suggests the existence of a common pathological pathway that simultaneously disrupts alveolar and pulmonary vascular maturation while promoting fibrotic remodeling. Emerging evidence demonstrates significant disruption of both mitochondrial density and function in the lungs of infants with BPD [71]. Ratner et al. established that in hyperoxia-induced or mechanical ventilation-induced murine BPD models, suppression of mitochondrial respiratory chain activity correlates strongly with arrested alveolar development. Inhibition of oxidative phosphorylation directly induces alveolar growth arrest, phenocopying all hallmark features of ‘new BPD’ pathology [72]. Hyperoxia-induced elevation of ROS production in pulmonary capillary endothelial cells (ECs) is abolished by inhibition of the mitochondrial ETC, demonstrating that Mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (mtROS) mediate pulmonary injury [73]. Furthermore, hyperoxia compromises mitochondrial antioxidant defenses in ECs, disrupts pulmonary endothelial tight junctions, and ultimately contributes to arrested lung development [74]. In murine models of BPD, both hyperoxia and chronic oxygen exposure elevate pulmonary mtROS levels and induce mtDNA damage [75]. BEAS-2B cells exposed to hyperoxia exhibit pathological mitochondrial alterations, including vacuolization, cristae fragmentation/destruction, and loss of MMP. These abnormalities are significantly attenuated by vitamin D pretreatment through MMP preservation and ultrastructural restoration [76].

Transcriptomic profiling of neonatal mouse models under hyperoxia exposure revealed significant dysregulation of pulmonary genes. Hyperoxia upregulated BCL2 binding component 3 (BBC3), whose sustained expression induces mitochondrial dysfunction and activates apoptotic cascades, suggesting a shift from mitophagic repair toward apoptotic clearance. Concurrently, hyperoxia downregulated organogenesis-promoting gene like platelet-derived growth factor receptor alpha (PDGFRA), the neurodevelopmental regulator like neurotrophic receptor tyrosine kinase 2 (NTRK2), and cell proliferation modulator like cyclin-dependent kinase 5 regulatory subunit 1 (CDK5R1). Hyperoxia also activated AMPK while reducing levels of key lung developmental proteins such as platelet-derived growth factor receptor β (PDGFRβ) and snail family transcriptional repressor 2 (SLUG) [77]. Therapeutic strategies aimed at normalizing these dysregulated genes and proteins may help preserve mitochondrial functional integrity, attenuate apoptosis and abnormal pulmonary innervation, normalize pulmonary and airway vascular contractility, and ultimately promote optimal lung repair and development under hyperoxic conditions. Prenatal exposure to environmental stressors - including tobacco smoke, metallic particles, and suboptimal maternal nutrition - may induce pathogenic mtDNA methylation patterns that elevate BPD susceptibility [20]. Comparative in vitro studies demonstrate that human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) isolated from BPD-affected neonates exhibit exacerbated hyperoxia-induced mitochondrial dysfunction relative to non-BPD controls, manifesting as elevated mtROS production, impaired oxidative phosphorylation capacity, reduced oxygen consumption rates, and increased mtDNA damage [78]. Mitochondrially targeted fluorescent protein-based tracing assay revealed markedly augmented mitochondrial biogenesis within pulmonary vascular ECs during hyperoxia-induced lung injury [79]. Comparative transcriptomic analysis demonstrated significant downregulation of multiple mitochondrial-associated genes in blood cells from oxygen-treated neonates (both BPD and non-BPD) versus untreated controls, encompassing mtDNA-encoded genes (MT-ND5, MT-CYB, MT-CO2, MT-ATP6) and nuclear genes regulating mitochondrial morphology and permeability (BAX, HTT, IGF1R, OGDH, TGFB1) [14]. Following oxidative stress, human cells exhibit more extensive and persistent damage in mtDNA compared to nuclear DNA. Hyperoxia-induced mtDNA lesions have been experimentally demonstrated to inhibit branching morphogenesis in rat lung explants, resulting in abnormal alveolar maturation [80]. Furthermore, mitochondrial functional variations induced by mtDNA haplogroup polymorphisms can independently influence neonatal alveolar development and susceptibility to BPD [20]. Kam et al. demonstrated that mitochondrial bioenergetic and redox dysfunction, coupled with mtDNA damage, are mechanistically linked to both hyperoxic lung injury in neonatal mice and increased BPD risk in extremely low birth weight (ELBW) infants. These findings suggest that impaired mitochondrial biogenesis may underlie delayed alveolarization in BPD pathogenesis [80]. Furthermore, in hyperoxia-exposed neonatal murine lungs, insufficient AMPK activation and overactive mTORC1 signaling pathway are associated with compromised pulmonary development [81].

Approximately 17% of BPD patients develop comorbid PH, elevating mortality rates in this population [82, 83]. In hyperoxia-exposed neonatal murine models, administration of the mitochondria-targeted antioxidant mitoTEMPO attenuates BPD-associated PH pathophysiology through amelioration of mitochondrial dysfunction [84].

Long-Term pulmonary dysfunction following BPD

The persistent pulmonary function impairments associated with BPD may endure into later life and demonstrate significant correlation with respiratory morbidity. Furthermore, the incidence of abnormal lung function in adolescent and adult survivors of BPD correlates positively with the severity of neonatal disease, underscoring the critical need for early intervention to improve long-term outcomes [85–87]. The cumulative pulmonary impairment in preterm individuals often results from recurrent respiratory infections, hyperoxia exposure, and arrested lung development during neonatal and childhood periods [88]. BPD patients frequently exhibit airway hyperresponsiveness, increasing susceptibility to asthma [89, 90]. The predominant functional abnormality in BPD is airflow obstruction, sharing pathophysiological features with COPD [85]. Longitudinal studies demonstrate progressive deterioration of flow-based parameters such as forced expiratory volume in the first second (FEV1) in VLBW infants with BPD history, who face elevated COPD risk in adulthood even without smoking exposure [91, 92]. Imaging studies reveal persistent structural abnormalities including emphysematous regions and bronchial wall thickening in adults with BPD, establishing BPD as a significant precursor to chronic respiratory conditions [93]. Epidemiological evidence emphasizes the necessity for lifelong monitoring to prevent and manage sequelae of BPD [94]. The long-term respiratory dysfunction in BPD stems from alveolar simplification, aberrant vascular development, and sustained inflammation [88, 95]. Animal models provide crucial insights for these pathogenic mechanisms. Previous studies have demonstrated that hyperoxia exposure induces alveolar epithelial cell apoptosis and septal thickening, thereby disrupting alveolarization [96]. Furthermore, hyperoxia suppresses pulmonary vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) expression, leading to reduced vascular density and PH [97]. Animal studies confirm that neutrophil infiltration and release of pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-6, TNF-α) exacerbate lung injury and impair repair [98]. In hyperoxia-induced BPD mouse models, researchers observed reduced lung compliance and airway hyperreactivity in adulthood [99]. For end-stage BPD, lung transplantation emerges as ultimate therapeutic option, with registry data indicating median transplant age of 21, severely reduced FEV1 (17% predicted), and 23% requiring mechanical ventilation [100].

Pulmonary vascular developmental impairment

Mitochondria serve as critical biosynthetic and signaling hubs that regulate EC migration, proliferation, and angiogenic processes. MtROS and associated proteins such as aptamer protein p66Shc and ubiquinol-cytochrome c reductase binding protein (UQCRB), mitochondrial Ca²⁺ regulators including voltage-dependent anion channel 1(VDAC1)、Cyclophilin D (CypD) and FAM3A, as well as hydrogen sulfide (H₂S)-generating enzymes like cystathionine beta-synthase (CBS), cystathionine-gamma-lyase(CSE),3-mercaptopyruvate sulfurtransferase (3-MST), collectively fine-tune EC function and angiogenesis through distinct signaling pathways [101]. PINK1 knockout models exhibit reduced cardiac capillary density, while Parkin inhibits tumor angiogenesis by ubiquitinating HIF-1α at Lys477 [102, 103]. FUNDC1 deficiency disrupts mitochondria-associated endoplasmic reticulum membranes (MAMs) formation and angiogenesis by impairing SRF-VEGFR2 interaction [104]. HIF-1-induced VEGF and BNIP3 coordinate the balance between cell survival and apoptosis [105]. Mfn2 suppression elevates mtROS and disrupts ER-mitochondria contacts, whereas Mfn1 knockdown attenuates VEGF-stimulated Akt–eNOS signaling in HUVECs [106, 107]. Drp1 silencing impairs caffeine-induced lamellipodia formation and migration, while its overexpression enhances angiogenic function and attenuates apoptosis through a mitochondrial Ca²⁺-dependent pathway [108–110]. Furthermore, sulfenylation and phosphorylation at Cys644 of Drp1 regulate endothelial cellular senescence. Fis1 promotes cell proliferation, capillary density, and angiogenesis, and reverses the senescent phenotype in aging endothelial progenitor cells (EPCs) [111]. Mitochondrial dysfunction contributes to impaired pulmonary vascular development. A deeper understanding of the mechanisms by which mitochondria regulate EC metabolism, function, and angiogenesis provides novel perspectives and promising therapeutic targets for restoring endothelial function and promoting alveolar–vascular coordination during lung development.

Inflammation-Mediated pulmonary developmental disruption

Preterm complicated by severe chorioamnionitis is associated with significantly increased risks of childhood pulmonary disorders and early life morbidity, indicating that fetal inflammatory exposure substantially alters lung developmental programming [112]. These developmental abnormalities are mechanistically linked to impaired mitophagy, which fails to eliminate dysfunctional mitochondria, resulting in excessive mtROS production and mtDNA release. Circulating mtDNA exhibits potent immunogenicity, and systemic administration has been shown to induce robust inflammatory responses in both pulmonary and hepatic tissues [113]. The synergistic action of mtROS and oxidized mtDNA potently stimulates NLRP3 inflammasome assembly and activation, triggering caspase-1-mediated processing and secretion of interleukin-1β (IL-1β) and interleukin-18 (IL-18) [114]. Notably, IL-1β drives pulmonary epithelial stem cells to exit their self-renewing state and undergo differentiation into basal cells during fetal lung development [115]. Even in the absence of inflammatory stressors, targeted overexpression of IL-1β during the saccular stage of lung development disrupts pulmonary morphogenesis [116]. IL-1β and IL-18 activate transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β) signaling pathways, promoting fibroblast-to-myofibroblast differentiation and thereby contributing to BPD progression [64, 117]. Furthermore, mtDNA serves as a potent ligand for the DNA sensor cyclic GMP-AMP synthase (cGAS), triggering stimulator of interferon genes (STING) activation and subsequent type I interferon (IFN-I) production, which amplifies inflammatory cascades [118, 119]. This enhanced inflammatory response directly exacerbates mitochondrial dysfunction, while concurrent inflammasome activation impairs mitophagy, establishing a pathological positive feedback loop. The anti-inflammatory cytokine interleukin-10 (IL-10) enhances mitophagic flux, and its endogenous deficiency may lead to dysregulated, persistent inflammatory responses associated with increased BPD risk in neonates [120–123]. Itaconate, a macrophage-derived metabolite, mitigates hyperoxia-induced alveolar simplification in neonatal rats by promoting TFEB nuclear translocation, enhancing mitophagy, and suppressing apoptosis of ATII cells in both in vivo and in vitro models [124]. These findings suggest that functional mitophagy exerts anti-inflammatory effects, and that pathological accumulation of mitochondria may be a pivotal contributor to BPD.

In ALI, mitochondrial damage is conspicuous yet cell death remains modest, suggesting the engagement of protective stress responses. Studies have demonstrated concurrent activation of both mitochondrial biogenesis and mitophagy within individual ATII cell during the first 24 h of Staphylococcus aureus exposure [125]. These findings indicate that in inflammatory injury, the activation of mitophagy is interdependent with increased mitochondrial biogenesis. Damaged organelles eliminated by mitophagy must be replaced through induced biogenesis, and failure to maintain adequate mitochondrial quantity and energy supply will ultimately lead to impaired lung development.

Alveolar macrophages (AMs), as the primary immune sentinels in pulmonary tissue, play pivotal roles in lung development, inflammatory regulation, and tissue repair [126]. Within inflammatory microenvironments, macrophages demonstrate remarkable plasticity in polarizing toward pro-inflammatory (M1-like) or anti-inflammatory (M2-like) phenotypes. M1-polarized macrophages release abundant pro-inflammatory mediators and chemokines that recruit neutrophils and monocytes, whereas M2-polarized macrophages facilitate inflammation resolution and tissue regeneration [127].In sepsis-induced ALI, AMs exhibit decreased MFN1/2 and OPA1 expression alongside increased Drp1 phosphorylation at Ser616 [128]. During Streptococcus pneumoniae infection, AMs utilize Mfn1-dependent stress-induced mitochondrial hyperfusion (SIMH) to counteract ROS-mediated damage; conversely, Mfn1 deficiency enhances SOD activity, reduces AMs and mtROS production, and mitigates lipid peroxidation and inflammation [129]. The polarization state of AMs is critically regulated by mitochondrial dynamics. SIRT3 promotes fusion equilibrium and reverses pro-inflammatory polarization in AMs by deacetylating OPA1 at lysine 792, thereby suppressing its proteolytic cleavage. Specific inhibition of SIRT3 enhances OPA1 acetylation, exacerbates mitochondrial dynamic imbalance, and reinforces M1 polarization. Enhanced mitochondrial fusion favors an M2 polarization state, whereas excessive fission drives M1 polarization [130]. In bleomycin-induced pulmonary fibrosis mouse models, FGF19 signaling through the FGFR4/AMPKα-p38/MAPK axis upregulates MFN1/2 expression, suppresses Drp1 activation, restores fusion/fission balance, and promotes M2 polarization to ameliorate fibrosis [131]. Inhibition of excessive Drp1 activation reduces mitochondrial fragmentation and pro-inflammatory cytokine release in sepsis-associated ALI [132].In ozone-induced ALI mouse models, AMs exhibit elevated mitochondrial content coupled with NLRP3 inflammasome-mediated disruption of mitochondrial dynamics (increased DRP1, decreased OPA1) and suppression of biogenesis (reduced NRF1 and mtDNA) [133]. The Drp1 inhibitor Mdivi-1 attenuates ALI by blocking NLRP3-driven M1 polarization, underscoring the therapeutic potential of modulating mitochondrial dynamics to reprogram AM phenotype [134].Hyperoxia exposure activates AMs, promotes M1 polarization, and impairs ATII cell survival, recapitulating severe neonatal chronic lung disease. Selective blockade of macrophage-derived IL-6 trans-signaling preserves ATII viability and sustains lung growth post-hyperoxia [135]. These collective findings position mitochondrial dynamics as a central regulator of AM immunometabolism and phenotype, revealing a promising therapeutic strategy for BPD. However, the specific relationship between AM mitochondrial dynamics and BPD pathogenesis remains underexplored and warrants further investigation to elucidate underlying mechanisms and translational opportunities (Fig. 1).

Regulatory mechanisms of MQC-Mediated lung developmental impairment

PINK1-Parkin-Mediated mitophagy

Both deficiency and overexpression of PINK1-Parkin indicate aberrant pulmonary mitophagy. Studies demonstrate that maternal PM2.5 exposure elevates Drp1 and Parkin levels while inducing mtROS accumulation, resulting in offspring mitochondrial dysfunction that impairs fetal alveolar development and lung function. Importantly, cessation of exposure prior to pregnancy fails to fully prevent these deficits, suggesting that mitochondrial damage may occur during early embryogenesis and exert persistent effects. In vitro studies show that the mitochondria-targeted antioxidant MitoQ can significantly rescue such mitochondrial dysfunction [56]. Recently, the PINK1-Parkin pathway has been established as a major contributing factor in the pathogenesis of BPD. Preterm infants exhibit diminished mitochondrial activity and significantly downregulated PINK1-PRKN expression in upper airway epithelial progenitor cells, indicating impaired mechanisms regulating mitochondrial function and cellular integrity. Under allostatic conditions, these progenitor cells demonstrate heightened sensitivity to environmental stressors compared to term counterparts [136]. Zhang et al. demonstrated that PINK1 deficiency exacerbates hyperoxia-induced lung injury [137], while PINK1-Parkin-mediated mitophagy activation is mechanistically linked to MMP dissipation [114]. In hyperoxia-exposed neonatal rats, ATII cells show reduced MMP concomitant with elevated pulmonary expression of PINK1, Parkin, and NIX, thus augmented mitophagy [138]. In hyperoxia-induced murine lung injury, the pulmonary protective effects of triiodothyronine (T3) are mediated through intact expression of the mitophagy marker PINK1 [139]. Wu et al. demonstrated that lipoxin A4 (LXA4) attenuates airway inflammation and reduces lung injury severity in hyperoxia-induced BPD rat models by downregulating the expression of PINK1, Parkin, BNIP3L/Nix, and LC3B [140]. In vitro studies reveal that reduced O-linked N-acetylglucosamine (O-GlcNAc) levels in RLE-6TN cells under hyperoxic conditions disrupt mitochondrial homeostasis by impairing Parkin-dependent mitophagy, suggesting that either diminished O-GlcNAc levels or enhanced O-GlcNAcylation of Parkin contributes to hyperoxia-induced ATII cell injury [141]. In oxidant- or hyperoxia-exposed rat pulmonary arterial ECs, overexpression of the mitochondria-targeted fusion protein Ogg1 upregulates mtDNA repair mechanisms, preserves MMP, reduces apoptosis, and protects endothelial barrier function. Conversely, even under normoxic conditions, genetic ablation of mitochondrial uncoupling protein 2 (UCP2) in murine pulmonary ECs elevates PINK1 and Parkin levels, resulting in enhanced mitophagy and spontaneous PH development [142]. Zhang et al. observed reduced PINK1 and Parkin protein expression alongside impaired AMPK activation, aberrant p62 expression and blocked autophagic flux in a baboon model of BPD [81]. This finding suggests that under prolonged hyperoxia and sustained stress, the PINK1-Parkin pathway may undergo a time-dependent shift from adaptive activation to functional exhaustion, leading to the accumulation of damaged mitochondria and a reduction in autophagic flux. This further underscores the coordinated interplay between mitophagy and autophagy in BPD animal models.

The PINK1-Parkin pathway is also intimately linked to inflammatory processes. Genetic knockout of either Parkin or PINK1 in murine models results in impaired mitophagy and subsequent mtDNA release, triggering robust inflammatory activation [143, 144]. FBXO7 (F-box protein 7) exacerbates mitochondrial damage and inflammation in experimental pneumonia by mediating PINK1 ubiquitination and degradation [145]. Previous studies utilizing both murine and preterm baboon BPD models, as well as tracheal aspirates from human BPD infants, have identified activated NLRP3 inflammasomes as pivotal drivers of BPD pathogenesis. Notably, Zhang et al. demonstrated that elevated PINK1 expression in hyperoxia-exposed murine pulmonary ECs and tissues mediates the protective effects of NLRP3 inflammasome deficiency against lung injury [146].

FUNDC1-Mediated mitophagy

Intrauterine growth restriction (IUGR), a prevalent pregnancy complication, is associated with delayed pulmonary development during the fetal and neonatal periods, elevating perinatal mortality risks [147]. Exposure to the atmospheric pollutant 1-nitropyrene induces ULK1-FUNDC1-mediated mitophagy in human trophoblasts, leading to suppressed progesterone synthesis and subsequent IUGR [148]. Cadmium exposure upregulates the FUNDC1 pathway in ovine pulmonary tissue, promoting excessive mitophagy and mitochondrial vacuolization while attenuating pulmonary antioxidant capacity [149]. FUNDC1-mediated mitophagy is critical for maintaining pulmonary vascular endothelial homeostasis. Its disruption increases secretion of insulin-like growth factor-binding protein 2 (IGFBP2), driving pulmonary arterial remodeling and precipitating pulmonary hypertension [150]. FUNDC1-deficient mice exposed to LPS exhibit significantly reduced pulmonary mitophagy, elevated ROS and pro-inflammatory cytokine expression, and exacerbated lung injury [151]. In LPS-induced ALI murine models, accumulated mitochondrial citrate directly binds FUNDC1, promoting its interaction with DRP1 to enhance mitochondrial fission. This triggers excessive alveolar epithelial mitophagy and initiates necroptosis, thereby accelerating ALI progression [51].

Mitochondrial dynamics mediated by Mfn1/2 and Drp1

Studies using hyperoxia-exposed RLE-6TN cells revealed an imbalance in mitochondrial fusion and fission, characterized by significantly reduced expression of Mfn1 and Mfn2 alongside increased Drp1 expression [152]. T3 modulates this equilibrium by upregulating Mfn2 while suppressing Drp1, thereby enhancing the fusion/fission ratio and altering the fate of depolarized mitochondria to mitigate hyperoxic lung injury in mice [139]. MLE-12 cell experiments demonstrated that hyperoxia exposure for just 4 h induces marked alterations in mitochondrial morphology and distribution, including elevated transcription of fusion proteins Mfn1 and Opa1, leading to the formation of extensively elongated, interconnected mitochondrial networks. Upon re-establishment of normoxic conditions, transcript levels of fission-related proteins (Drp1, Fis1, Mtp18) were downregulated during recovery. Prolonged hyperoxia (6 h) resulted in mitochondrial fragmentation, while neonatal mice exposed to hyperoxia for 12 h exhibited alveolar simplification. These findings suggest that transient mitochondrial fusion under acute hyperoxia represents a protective adaptive response to mitochondrial dysfunction [153].

Excessive activation of mitochondrial fission under hyperoxic conditions is a critical contributor to pulmonary vascular injury. In hyperoxia-induced BPD models using neonatal rats, elevated Drp1 expression was observed in lung tissues. The mitochondrial fission inhibitor Mdivi-1 effectively improved pulmonary microvascular development by suppressing Drp1, suggesting that inhibiting mitochondrial fission may represent a viable therapeutic strategy for hyperoxia-associated lung diseases [154]. During the critical alveolar formation phase in neonatal rat BPD models, hyperoxia increased both Drp1 expression and phosphorylation in lung tissues. Treatment with Mdivi-1 not only suppressed Drp1 but also enhanced small pulmonary artery development and attenuated pulmonary hypertension [154]. Furthermore, hyperoxia reduced ATP production in ATII cells of neonatal rats, while activation of glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor (GLP-1R) signaling promoted ATP generation and downregulated glycolysis by modulating DRP1-mediated mitochondrial fission, thereby supporting lung development [155]. This study highlights the interplay between mitochondrial fission and glucose metabolism, though numerous nutrient metabolism-related mechanisms remain to be explored.

Imbalanced mitochondrial dynamics are frequently characterized by mitochondrial fragmentation, yet excessive mitochondrial fusion can also mediate apoptotic processes. In a rat model, miR-20b attenuates hyperoxia-induced ALI by suppressing mitochondrial fusion-mediated apoptosis through downregulation of Mfn1 and Mfn2 expression [118].

PGC-1α-Mediated mitochondrial biogenesis

During pulmonary development, increased mitochondrial density occurs in distal lung cells, including ATII cells, small vascular smooth muscle cells, and inflammatory cells within alveolar regions. ATII cells activate mitochondrial biogenesis in response to hyperoxic lung injury, ALI, and pneumonia. Upregulation of PGC-1α is beneficial for promoting mitochondrial biogenesis and improving lung development. In animal models of LPS-induced ALI, the PGC-1α-HSF1 axis mitigates lung injury by suppressing inflammatory responses and oxidative stress [156]. Additionally, upregulation of the PGC-1α-TFEB axis alleviates pulmonary inflammation and injury by enhancing mitophagy [50, 157].

Animal models of IUGR exhibit impaired mitochondrial biogenesis across multiple tissues—including the liver, skeletal muscle, and jejunal cells—characterized by downregulated expression of PGC-1α, NRF1, ERRα, and TFAM. However, the mechanisms linking these perturbations to pulmonary developmental outcomes remain to be elucidated [158, 159].

Lung development relies on intricate epithelial-mesenchymal interactions and signaling networks. Zhang et al. discovered that in liver cancer and mammary epithelial cells, SIRT1 influences the epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) by modulating the mitochondrial biogenesis pathway. Additionally, SIRT1 can regulate the EMT in lung cancer cells through the SIRT1-PGC-1α axis [160].

Studies have demonstrated that resveratrol upregulates the expression of SIRT1, PGC-1α, NRF1, NRF2, and TFAM in hyperoxia-exposed alveolar epithelial cells of neonatal rats, thereby enhancing mitochondrial biogenesis, alleviating mitochondrial dysfunction, and reducing alveolar damage [161]. Similarly, Zhu et al. reported that resveratrol upregulates SIRT1, PGC-1α, NRF1, and TFAM expression in hyperoxia-treated human alveolar epithelial cells, reducing mtROS production and apoptosis [162]. Yadav et al. demonstrated that in newborn rats exposed to hyperoxia during the saccular stage of lung development, metformin supplementation enhances AMPK activity, maintains PGC-1α levels, regulates mitochondrial function and energy homeostasis, and restores alveolar and vascular growth [163]. Yang et al. found that Nesfatin-1 significantly reverses hyperoxia-induced cellular damage in ATII cells derived from newborn rats in vitro. Further in vivo experiments in hyperoxia-exposed neonatal mice revealed that Nesfatin-1 activates the SIRT1-PGC-1α signaling pathway, promoting mitochondrial biogenesis and attenuating lung injury [164]. The non-essential amino acid L-citrulline (L-CIT) alleviates pulmonary inflammation and oxidative stress in developing lungs of newborn rats by upregulating the expression of PGC-1α, NRF1, and TFAM in lung tissue and pulmonary artery smooth muscle cells, while inducing SIRT1, SIRT3, and superoxide dismutase protein expression, thereby mitigating the progression of BPD [165]. Bianca M et al. demonstrated that in hyperoxia-exposed neonatal mice, T3 ameliorates mitochondrial dysfunction in ATII cells and lung injury by upregulating PGC-1α expression and its key transcriptional regulators, including myocyte enhancer factor 2 A (MEF2A) and forkhead box protein O1 (FOXO1) [166]. In a murine model of hyperoxia-induced ALI, T3 pretreatment resulted in elevated pulmonary expression of the mitochondrial anti-ROS protein stanniocalcin-1 (STC1) and uncoupling protein 2 (UCP2), enhanced PGC-1α induction, thus stimulated mitochondrial biogenesis [139].

In murine models, genetic ablation of PPAR-γ results in spontaneous development of PH with concomitant right ventricular hypertrophy [22]. In hyperoxia-exposed rat models of PH during early postnatal development, cardiomyocytes exhibit transient adaptive upregulation of PGC-1α, NRF1, NRF2, and TFAM to maintain mitochondrial function and provide compensatory reduction of pulmonary arterial pressure. However, this compensatory mechanism is gradually compromised by increasing mtDNA mutation rates and diminished mitochondrial biogenesis, which persist into adulthood [167, 168]. Inhaled NO represents a conventional therapeutic approach for PH, including bronchopulmonary dysplasia-associated pulmonary hypertension (BPD-PH). Studies in hyperoxia-exposed neonatal lamb models of PH demonstrate significant reductions in pulmonary expression of p-AMPKα, SIRT1, PGC-1α protein, and PGC-1α mRNA levels. NO inhalation therapy can restore mitochondrial function by markedly upregulating these critical markers of mitochondrial biogenesis [142] (Fig. 2).

Diverse pathological insults disrupt MQC in pulmonary cells. Inflammation, hyperoxia, air pollution, smoke exposure, and genetic defects compromise mitochondrial homeostasis in a cell-type-specific manner within the lung. Under infectious conditions, pulmonary immune cells release pro-inflammatory cytokines, triggering an inflammatory cascade that disrupts MQC homeostasis. Under air pollution or smoke exposure, ATII cells exhibit mitochondrial swelling, disrupted mitochondrial dynamics and mitophagy, while placental trophoblasts exhibit enhanced FUNDC1-mediated mitophagy, contributing to IUGR. Concurrently, bronchial epithelial cells show increased FUNDC1-dependent mitophagy. Genetic deficiencies in mitophagy-related genes within ATII and pulmonary ECs lead to defective mitophagy. Endothelial FUNDC1 deficiency leads to PH. Therapeutic prospects

Promoting MQC rebalancing

Mitophagy represents a double-edged sword that requires precise calibration to maintain optimal levels, avoiding either insufficient clearance or excessive degradation of damaged mitochondria, with particular emphasis on the PINK1-Parkin and FUNDC1-BNIP3-NIX pathways. Studies have revealed that when mitochondrial fission is disrupted, cellular compensatory feedback mechanisms can partially rebalance mitochondrial dynamics and restore mitochondrial function [37]. Consequently, the rebalancing of mitochondrial dynamics may offer therapeutic strategies for diseases associated with aberrant mitochondrial fusion or fission. However, the safety profile of pharmacological agents that restore mitochondrial dynamic balance, such as Mdivi-1 and SAMβA, remains to be further validated by clinical data, particularly in vulnerable populations like fetuses and neonates. Overexpression of PGC-1α enhances mitochondrial biogenesis and promotes pulmonary development, suggesting that drugs targeting PGC-1α-associated signaling pathways may confer therapeutic benefits. A more comprehensive understanding of the molecular pathways and regulatory mechanisms underlying MQC will enable more systematic and targeted approaches for predicting and intervening in impaired pulmonary development.

Stabilizing mesenchymal stem cells

Mitochondrial dysfunction impairs the ability of mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) to repair distal lung epithelium. Transcriptomic analysis of upper airway basal cells from preterm infants revealed downregulation of PINK1 and PRKN genes, leading to impaired mitophagy. Furthermore, mitochondrial respiratory function assays demonstrated reduced maximal oxygen consumption and diminished capacity to respond to metabolic challenges, which may compromise their differentiation and functional maintenance [136]. Hazra et al. found that umbilical cord-derived MSCs (UC-MSCs) from infants who died or developed moderate-to-severe BPD exhibited suppressed PINK1 expression under hyperoxic conditions, indicating defective mitophagy. This suggests that impaired mitophagy in endogenous MSCs from ELBW infants is associated with reduced proliferation and survival of lung-resident MSCs, as well as an increased risk of severe BPD in ELBW neonates [169]. Supplementation with T3 during hyperoxia exposure helps maintain MMP stability in both neonatal mouse lung fibroblasts (NMLFs) and UC-MSCs, alleviating mitochondrial dysfunction and ultimately mitigating lung injury [166]. Additionally, MSCs can restore mitochondrial function in damaged lung cells through the direct donation of healthy mitochondria. Maintaining mitochondrial homeostasis may thus be a key mechanism by which MSCs promote lung development and regeneration. Current research has explored the therapeutic potential of MSCs in BPD, particularly their role in enhancing alveolarization and pulmonary vascular growth [64].

Precisely modulating antioxidant defense

The dual nature of ROS, coupled with their molecular instability and spatiotemporal specificity, renders them a challenging therapeutic target. Contemporary antioxidant strategies have consequently evolved from conventional “global scavenging” approaches to more sophisticated “precision modulation” paradigms. Mitochondria-targeted antioxidants such as MitoQ and MitoTEMPO have demonstrated efficacy in preserving mitochondrial function during cigarette smoke exposure [170]. Experimental evidence demonstrates that MitoTEMPO effectively scavenges mtROS, attenuates hyperoxia-induced mitochondrial dysfunction and modulates EMT in human airway epithelial cells. Early administration of mitoTEMPO under hyperoxic conditions in neonatal mice activates NRF2, reduces mtROS production, ameliorates mitochondrial dysfunction, prevents impaired alveolarization, and mitigates BPD [75, 84]. Chronic fetal hypoxia promotes oxidative stress, whereas the application of MitoQ in healthy or hypoxic pregnancies upregulates the expression of key mitochondrial proteins involved in pulmonary surfactant maturation, lung fluid absorption, and ATP synthesis in fetal sheep lungs, thereby promoting fetal lung maturation [171].

Further studies have demonstrated that targeted modulation of mitochondrial function-related proteins may yield greater therapeutic benefits compared to conventional antioxidant treatments. Key mitochondrial regulatory factors, such as A-kinase anchoring protein 1 (AKAP1) and B cell-specific Moloney murine leukemia virus integration site 1 (BMI1), may serve as novel targets for regulating lung development. Under hyperoxic conditions, AKAP1-deficient mice exhibited upregulated expression of PINK1, Parkin, and p62 in lung tissues, along with a significant increase in morphologically disorganized mitochondria in ATII cells, enhanced mitophagy, and aggravated ALI [172]. In hyperoxia-exposed human pulmonary epithelial cells, silencing BMI1 expression promoted PINK1 and Parkin expression while suppressing OPA1, exacerbating mitochondrial dysfunction [173].

The integration of nanomolecular technology with mitochondrial-targeting strategies enables the specific delivery of therapeutics to mitochondria, allowing for precise administration of antioxidants, agents that ameliorate mitochondrial dysfunction, and other personalized therapeutics, thereby significantly enhancing the clinical translation and efficacy of mitochondrial medicine [174].

Targeted mitochondrial repair

With the continuous advancement of mitochondrial repair technologies, novel therapeutic approaches have emerged, including mitochondrial regeneration and transplantation to replace damaged mitochondria [175]. Although the efficacy of these methods has been validated, their widespread clinical application remains constrained by high treatment costs and challenges in technological translation. Furthermore, innovative strategies targeting specific mitochondrial metabolic pathways, such as mitochondrial reprogramming techniques, have demonstrated considerable therapeutic potential [176]. For instance, interventions employing DNA methylation inhibitors or SOD mimetics (e.g., MnTBAP) to enhance SOD2 activity and improve mtDNA repair mechanisms (e.g., via hOgg1) have shown promising results in animal models, suggesting potential future applications in human disease [177–179] (Fig. 3).

Future therapeutic strategies will integrate systems biology, nanotechnology, and personalized medicine to develop placenta-fetus targeted delivery systems that enhance treatment efficacy, thereby providing tailored therapeutic regimens for high-risk infants susceptible to impaired pulmonary development. The implementation of high-throughput assessment of mitochondrial biological characteristics, coupled with breakthroughs in emerging technologies, may pioneer novel approaches to reduce the incidence of pulmonary developmental impairments in fetuses and neonates while optimizing interventional outcomes.
