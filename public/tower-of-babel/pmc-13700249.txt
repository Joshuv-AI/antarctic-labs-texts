Association of gut Fusobacteria abundance with peritoneal metastasis in gastric signet-ring cell carcinoma: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Qilin Fan, Rong Zhang, Dan Xu, Feng Wen, Weitian Xu, Hongli Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGastric signet-ring cell carcinoma (SRCC) is an aggressive gastric cancer subtype with a high propensity for peritoneal metastasis (PM). While gut microbiota is implicated in gastric carcinogenesis, its specific role in SRCC and PM remains unknown. This study aimed to identify gut microbial markers associated with SRCC and its peritoneal metastasis.MethodsWe performed 16S rRNA gene sequencing on fecal samples from a discovery cohort (22 SRCC, 80 non-SRCC gastric cancer, 40 healthy controls) and a validation cohort (24 SRCC, 40 non-SRCC, 19 healthy controls), including 10 SRCC patients with PM and 28 without PM. Microbial diversity, differential abundance, random forest (RF) modeling, and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis were used to identify candidate microbial markers.ResultsSRCC patients showed increased gut microbial biodiversity. Originally defined by Linear discriminant analysis Effect Size (LEfSe), this method identified 40 differentially abundant taxa, with Fusobacteria as a top-ranked feature by random forest. The abundance of Fusobacteria was higher in SRCC vs. non-SRCC (0.059 vs. 0.005) and in signet-ring cell carcinoma with peritoneal metastasis (S-PM) vs. without peritoneal metastasis (n-S-PM) (0.019 vs. 0.003). ROC analysis showed moderate discriminatory ability for SRCC (P = 0.0023) but not for PM (P = 0.380).ConclusionsGut Fusobacteria abundance is associated with gastric SRCC, but its predictive value for peritoneal metastasis is limited. These findings suggest a potential auxiliary role for Fusobacteria as a non-invasive biomarker, requiring validation in larger prospective cohorts.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12876-026-05074-z.

Background

GC remains one of the most common and lethal malignancies worldwide, ranking as the fifth most frequently diagnosed cancer and the fourth leading cause of cancer-related deaths [1, 2]. Among its various histological subtypes, SRCC has attracted growing clinical interest due to its increasing incidence [3]. SRCC is a distinct histological subtype of gastric adenocarcinoma, accounting for approximately 3–10% of all GC cases, with variation depending on geographic region and diagnostic criteria [3]. Over recent decades, its reported incidence has been rising, particularly in western populations [3].

Histologically, SRCC is defined as a poorly differentiated adenocarcinoma in which mucin-filled signet-ring cells constitute more than 50% of the tumor mass [1]. This subtype accounts for a considerable proportion (35%–40%) of gastric adenocarcinoma cases [4] and is associated with aggressive biological behavior and poor prognosis [5]. SRCC is characterized by an aggressive clinical course and a high propensity for PM, which occurs in up to 30–40% of patients with advanced disease [6]. PM represents the leading cause of death in SRCC patients, with median overall survival ranging from 2 to 9 months following diagnosis [6].

Among the patterns of GC progression, PM is one of the most common and devastating, second only to liver metastasis [6, 7]. The prognosis for PM remains extremely bleak, with historical median overall survival ranging from only 2 to 9 months, highlighting the urgent need for improved therapeutic strategies [8]. Despite its clinical importance, research specifically targeting PM in SRCC is still limited.

Accumulating evidence points to a pivotal role of the gastrointestinal microbiota in gastric carcinogenesis [9]. Pathobionts such as Fusobacterium nucleatum and Helicobacter pylori may contribute to oncogenesis through mechanisms involving chronic inflammation, induction of DNA damage, and activation of oncogenic signaling pathways [10, 11]. For example, F. nucleatum has been shown to induce exosomal HOTTIP, which promotes GC progression via the microRNA-885-3p/EphB2 axis [12]. More broadly, gut microbial dysbiosis is increasingly recognized as a key factor in the etiology of gastrointestinal cancers, including GC [13, 14].

However, the intricate host-microbiota interactions in cancer, particularly within the gastric microenvironment, are still poorly understood [15, 16]. A significant limitation in the field is that most microbiome studies in GC have analyzed patient cohorts with mixed histological subtypes, thereby overlooking potential microbiota differences inherent to specific subtypes like SRCC [17]. Consequently, the microbial landscape of SRCC and its potential role in driving its aggressive phenotype, including a propensity for PM, remain largely unexplored.

Based on suggested links between microbes and cancer—such as the role of Fusobacterium nucleatum as a risk factor in colorectal carcinogenesis [18]—and emerging evidence of Fusobacteria enrichment in SRCC compared to other adenocarcinomas [19], we hypothesize that Fusobacteria may be a key microbiota player in SRCC pathogenesis. Based on established links between microbes and cancer—such as the role of Fusobacterium nucleatum in colorectal carcinogenesis—we hypothesized that Fusobacteria abundance might be associated with SRCC and its PM. In this exploratory study, we aimed to characterize the gut microbiota in SRCC and evaluate whether Fusobacteria abundance is associated with SRCC status and PM. Therefore, this study aims to comprehensively characterize the gut microbiota in SRCC, with a specific focus on evaluating the association of Fusobacteria abundance with SRCC and its associated PM.

Methods

Study population and design

A total of 225 participants were recruited at the Cancer Center of Union Hospital, Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology between August 2020 and January 2022. The cohort consisted of 46 patients with gastric signet-ring cell carcinoma (S-GC group), 120 patients with non-signet-ring cell gastric carcinoma (n-S-GC group), and 59 healthy volunteers (N group), aged 27–93 years.

PM was defined as the presence of histologically or radiologically confirmed metastatic lesions in the peritoneum, omentum, or mesentery. PM status was determined at the time of initial diagnosis based on preoperative imaging and confirmed by intraoperative findings or staging laparoscopy when available. All PM cases included in this study were synchronous PM; patients with metachronous PM were not included.

The inclusion criteria for patients in the neoplasm groups were a new, pathologically confirmed diagnosis of GC. SRCC was classified according to the Lauren classification [20]. Healthy volunteers were included based on the absence of GC or adenoma upon endoscopic examination.

Key exclusion criteria for all subjects included: (1) A previous history of malignancy, including GC; (2) A history of gastric surgery; (3) Intake of antibiotics or probiotics within three months prior to enrollment; (4) Use of antibiotics or probiotics in the week immediately preceding sample collection; (5) Recent invasive intestinal procedures, such as gastrointestinal endoscopy or enema; (6) All eligible subjects provided written informed consent.

For the PM-specific analysis, we included all patients with confirmed synchronous PM from both cohorts (S-PM, n = 10). The comparison group (n-S-PM, n = 28) consisted of all remaining SRCC patients without any evidence of PM, matched as closely as possible for age and tumor stage.

The discovery cohort was used for feature selection, model training, and threshold derivation. The validation cohort served as an independent test set to assess the reproducibility of the identified microbial associations. Specifically, the same RF derived features were evaluated in the validation cohort using an independent ROC analysis to confirm the direction and magnitude of the association.

Sample collection and 16S rRNA sequencing

Fresh stool samples (approximately 50 mg each) were collected from GC patients and healthy volunteers by trained medical personnel. It is important to emphasize that all stool samples were collected after pathological confirmation of GC and before the initiation of any treatment or surgical intervention. Each sample was carefully placed in a sterile specimen collection container and immediately stored at -80 °C.

Total genomic DNA was extracted from individual samples using the Omega Mag-Bind Soil DNA Kit (Omega Bio-Tek, Norcross, GA, USA). The quality and concentration of the extracted DNA were assessed. The V3-V4 hypervariable regions of the 16S rRNA gene were then amplified and sequenced on the Illumina platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). Due to the limited phylogenetic resolution of 16S rRNA gene sequencing, taxonomic assignments are reported at the phylum and genus levels. Species-level calls are not reported unless independently validated. The V3-V4 hypervariable regions of the 16S rRNA gene were amplified using the following primer pair: forward primer 338 F (5′-ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCA-3′) and reverse primer 806R (5′-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3′). PCR conditions were as follows: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 3 min, followed by 27 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, annealing at 55 °C for 30 s, and extension at 72 °C for 45 s, with a final extension at 72 °C for 10 min.

Raw sequencing reads were quality-filtered and denoised using the DADA2 plugin in QIIME2 (version 2019.4) with the following parameters: truncLenF = 280, truncLenR = 220, trimLeftF = 10, trimLeftR = 10, maxEE = 2.0, truncQ = 2. Specifically, the forward reads (R1) were truncated at 280 bp and the reverse reads (R2) at 220 bp, with the first 10 bases trimmed from both ends to remove primer sequences. A maximum expected error of 2.0 was allowed, and bases with a quality score below 2 were truncated. After denoising and chimera removal, ASVs were generated. For normalization, ASV counts were rarefied to the minimum sequencing depth (35,000 reads per sample) to account for variable sequencing depth across samples. For beta diversity and LEfSe analysis, data were normalized using cumulative sum scaling (CSS) as implemented in the ‘metagenomeSeq’ R package. For RF and ROC analysis, relative abundances were used. Amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) were generated using the DADA2 plugin, which provides single-nucleotide resolution. For comparison with traditional clustering methods, operational taxonomic units (OTUs) were also generated at 97% sequence similarity for the Venn diagrams only; all other analyses used ASVs. Taxonomic classification was performed using the Greengenes database (version 13.8) because it remains a widely used reference for 16S rRNA gene data, providing stable and reproducible taxonomic assignment with the QIIME2 platform. Classification was restricted to domain, phylum, class, order, family, and genus levels; species-level calls were not used due to the limited resolution of 16S rRNA gene sequencing. The Greengenes database was selected because it is: (i) compatible with the QIIME2 framework, (ii) widely used in published microbiome studies, and (iii) provides balanced taxonomic coverage for human gut microbiota. We note that classification was restricted to phylum and genus levels due to the limitations of 16S resolution. The use of an updated database (e.g., SILVA v138 or GTDB) may provide more refined assignments and is recommended for future studies.

Statistical and bioinformatic analyses

Categorical and continuous variables were analyzed using the Chi-square test and Student’s t-test, respectively, with SPSS software (IBM SPSS Statistics, Version 22.0, Armonk, NY, USA). A two-tailed p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Data visualization was performed using GraphPad Prism (Version 7.0, GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA).

For 16S rRNA gene sequencing data, bioinformatic processing and microbial community analyses were conducted in QIIME2. Alpha diversity and beta diversity were evaluated to assess microbial richness and structural differences between groups. Hypothesis testing for intergroup differences was performed using the Kruskal–Wallis test, permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA), and other relevant statistical methods. Differential microbial taxa were identified using LEfSe, and a RF model was applied to evaluate the predictive importance of key taxa.

All feature selection and model training were performed exclusively on the discovery cohort (n = 142). The validation cohort (n = 83) was used as an independent test set to assess the reproducibility of the findings. LEfSe differential taxa were identified in the discovery cohort; the same taxa were then examined in the validation cohort. The RF model was trained on the discovery cohort; the top features were then evaluated in the validation cohort using independent ROC analysis.

To assess whether beta-diversity patterns were driven by differences in within-group dispersion rather than true compositional shifts, we performed PERMDISP (multivariate homogeneity of group dispersions) analysis using the “betadisper” function in the vegan package (R version 4.0) with 999 permutations, based on Bray-Curtis distances.

Enhanced ROC analysis and multivariate regression models

To rigorously evaluate the diagnostic performance of Fusobacteria as a predictive biomarker, we conducted comprehensive ROC analysis. The area under the curve (AUC) was calculated to assess overall discriminative power, with sensitivity, specificity, and corresponding confidence intervals determined across various cut-off points. The optimal diagnostic threshold was identified using Youden’s J-statistic, which maximizes the sum of sensitivity and specificity, as well as by evaluating the product of these two metrics to ensure clinical relevance.

A post-hoc power analysis was performed for the PM subgroup using PASS software. To detect an AUC of 0.70 with α = 0.05 and 80% power, a minimum of 45 patients per group would be required. Given the limited sample size (S-PM, n = 10), the PM findings are presented as exploratory only.

We further compared the predictive capability of Fusobacteria against other suggested biomarkers through paired ROC analyses. To control for potential confounding variables and strengthen the validity of our findings, multivariate logistic regression models were employed, adjusting for key clinical and pathological factors.

This study was designed and reported in accordance with the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) guidelines to ensure transparency and completeness in reporting observational research.

Notable differences in baseline characteristics were observed between S-GC and n-S-GC groups. In the discovery cohort, S-GC patients were significantly younger (52.91 ± 10.48 years vs. 60.04 ± 11.7 years, p = 0.001) and exhibited a higher proportion of females (63.6% vs. 46.3%, p < 0.001) compared to n-S-GC patients. Tumor distribution also differed significantly (p = 0.001), with S-GC cases more frequently located in the gastric body and lesser/greater curvature. Furthermore, S-GC patients showed a distinct pattern of metastasis (p = 0.048), characterized by a higher prevalence of PM and a lower frequency of liver and lymph node metastases. HER-2 status also varied significantly between the two groups (p = 0.031), with S-GC patients more commonly exhibiting HER-2 negative status. Similar trends were observed in the validation cohort, where S-GC patients were again significantly younger (56.66 ± 13.03 years vs. 63.20 ± 10.36 years, p = 0.030), though gender distribution was not significantly different (p = 0.107) (Table 1).

Diversity analysis of intestinal microbial

Microbiota richness and diversity in fresh feces were subsequently compared among three groups in both a discovery and validation cohort. Alpha diversity, measured by the Faith_pd index, did not differ significantly among the S-GC, n-S-GC, and N groups in the discovery cohort (P = 0.27, Kruskal-Wallis test) (Fig. 2A). Beta diversity, visualized by principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) based on Bray- Curtis distance, also did not show significant separation among the three groups (pseudo-F = 0.912, P = 0.531) (Fig. 2B). In contrast to the discovery cohort, alpha diversity differed significantly among the three groups in the validation cohort (P < 0.001, Kruskal-Wallis test; Supplementary Fig. 2A). Beta diversity also showed significant separation among groups (pseudo-F = 2.717, P = 0.001) (Supplementary Fig. 2B).

PERMDISP analysis confirmed that there were no significant differences in within-group dispersion among the groups in either cohort (discovery: F = 1.234, P = 0.293; validation: F = 2.108, P = 0.089) or the PM subgroup (F = 0.876, P = 0.421) (Supplementary Table 3. Difference species analysis between S-GC and n-S-GC groups

We subsequently aimed to investigate potential disparities in the composition of microbiota between the S-GC group and the n-S-GC group. In order to accomplish this, we initially evaluated the overall profile of the microbiome. At the phylum level, four phyla consistently exhibited significant enrichment in both the discovery and validation cohorts, utilizing fecal samples. These phyla, namely Firmicutes, Bacteroidetes, Proteobacteria, and Actinobacteria, ranked among the top four in both cohorts (Fig. 1E and Supplementary Fig. 1D). Surprisingly, Fusobacteria and Verrucomicrobia exhibited a distinct pattern in both cohorts, as evidenced by Fig. 1E, F and Supplementary Fig. 1D. Specifically, the relative abundance of Fusobacteria and Verrucomicrobia was found to be higher in the S-GC group compared to the n-S-GC group in both cohorts (Fig. 1E, F and Supplementary Fig. 1D). Furthermore, when considering tumor stage, we observed a significant increase in the relative abundance of Fusobacteria and Verrucomicrobia in the S-GC (stage I-III) group compared to the n-S-GC (stage I-III) group in the discovery cohort (Fig. 1G).

Consistent results were obtained in both the S-GC (stage IV) group and the n-S-GC (stage IV) group, as shown in Fig. 1H. These findings indicate that the relative abundance of Fusobacteria and Verrucomicrobia is significantly higher in the S-GC group compared to the n-S-GC group, at both stage I-III and IV tumor staging levels.

To assess the effect size (LEfSe) of each differential flora, linear discriminant analysis (LDA) was employed. The LDA threshold effect value was set at 2.37, and the P value after FDR correction was set at 0.05, in order to identify flora markers with statistically significant differences among the three groups.

Surprisingly, our analysis revealed significant differences in the presence of Fusobacteria and Verrucomicrobia among the three groups in the discovery cohort (Fig. 2C, Supplementary Fig. 4A, Supplementary Fig. 2E and Supplementary Fig. 4B). Subsequently, the 16S rRNA sequencing data were subjected to prediction using the PICRUSt2 software. The Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway database was employed for functional pathway annotation. Figure 3, Supplementary Fig. 3, and Table 2 demonstrate that the gut microbiota of patients with GC who have experienced metastasis can be categorized into six classifications: cellular processes, environmental information processing, genetic information processing, human diseases, metabolism, and organismal systems. Notably, the carbohydrate metabolism pathway exhibits significant enrichment, suggesting a potential association between metabolic disorder and GC metastasis. However, our analysis of both cohorts (Fig. 3, Supplementary Fig. 3, and Table 2) did not reveal any significant differences in pathways between the S-GC and n-S-GC groups.

The baseline clinical characteristics of the S-PM and n-S-PM patients are summarized in Supplementary Table 6. No significant differences were observed between the two groups for age, sex, tumor location, HER-2 status, or tumor stage(Supplementary Table 2).

We observed significant differences in the composition of microbiota between the S-PM group and n-S-PM group. Fusobeccteria is associated with S-GC and S-PM

The RF analysis was used to identify the top 15 important species at both the ASV level and phylum level in both cohorts (Fig. 2D, E and Supplementary Fig. 2C, D). The discovery cohort revealed Fusobacteria, Verrucomicrobia, Proteobacteria, Bacteroidetes, Actinobacteria, and Firmicutes as the top six ASV IDs and corresponding species, as listed in Table 3. Similarly, the validation cohort showed Proteobacteria, Bacteroidetes, Actinobacteria, Firmicutes, Fusobacteria, and Verrucomicrobia as the top six ASV IDs and corresponding species, as shown in Supplementary Table 1. Among the microbial taxa examined, Fusobacteria and Verrucomicrobia exhibited significant differences in abundance within the S-GC group according to LEfSe analysis. Subsequent analysis of the predictive capability of these species for S-GC using ROC curves revealed that the area under the curves (AUC) for Fusobacteria and Verrucomicrobia were 0.656 (P = 0.0023) and 0.676 (P < 0.0001), respectively. These findings suggest that the abundance of these taxa is associated with S-GC status (Fig. 2F). Consistent results were also obtained in our validation cohort (Supplementary Tables 1 and Supplementary Fig. 1F). In other words, Fusobacteria and Verrucomicrobia abundance showed an association with S-GC and S-PM status, although the modest AUC values indicate limited discriminative ability.

Generally speaking, the value of AUC is between 0.5 and 1. The closer the value is to 1, the better the diagnostic performance and accuracy of the indicator. If these AUC values are relatively low, it may indicate that the predictive power of these markers is limited, which could affect their utility as clinical tools. Hence, a higher abundance of Fusobacteria was associated with an increased likelihood of PM in this cohort, but this finding requires validation in larger studies.

Discussion

Advancements in DNA sequencing have positioned the gut microbiota as a focal point in oncological research. Beyond their physiological roles, gut microbes have been implicated in tumor suppression or promotion [21, 22] and are increasingly recognized for their significance in the development and progression of GC [23]. The potential use of intestinal microbiota to assist in early diagnosis and subtype stratification-articularly between adenocarcinoma and SRCC-as attracted growing interest [17]. However, comparative analyses of microbial communities between S-GC and n-S-GC remain scarce. To address this gap, we characterized the gastrointestinal microbiota in 166 GC patients stratified by histological subtype.

Consistent with previous studies [24], our microbiome composition analysis revealed Firmicutes, Proteobacteria, Bacteroidetes, and Actinobacteria as the dominant phyla. Of particular interest is Fusobacteria, a gram-negative bacterium commonly found in the oral cavity and associated with periodontal disease. While its role in carcinogenesis has been extensively documented in colorectal [25], esophageal [26], and pancreatic cancers [27], evidence in gastric cancer remains limited. A recent study linked Fusobacteria positivity to worse overall survival in GC patients [19], yet its functional significance in gastric carcinogenesis-specially in SRCC-as not been clarified.

In this study, we identified Fusobacteria as a microbial taxon associated with S-GC. We observed significantly higher abundance of Fusobacteria in S-GC patients compared to n-S-GC patients and healthy controls (Fig. 1E, G). To our knowledge, this is the first report of such an association. Through LEfSe, random forest analysis, and ROC validation, we further demonstrated its discriminatory ability to distinguish S-GC from n-S-GC and healthy controls.

Notably, we also explored microbial differences in the context of PM. We identified significant community-level dissimilarities between S-PM and n-S-PM groups using Bray–Curtis metrics. To our knowledge, this is the first study to examine fecal microbiota specifically in S-PM and n-S-PM patients. We found that Fusobacteria abundance was significantly elevated in S-PM compared to n-S-PM patients (Fig. 4B, C). Its discriminatory differences between groups were further supported by LEfSe, RF, and ROC analyses, although the AUC values indicate only low to moderate accuracy (Fig. 4H; Table 4).

We also identified Verrucomicrobia as another differentially abundant taxon in both S-GC and S-PM groups. Although research on Verrucomicrobia in GC is limited, our findings suggest its potential relevance and warrant further investigation.

Functional prediction via PICRUSt2 revealed metabolic perturbations in the gut microbiota of GC patients, though no significant pathway differences were detected between S-PM and n-S-PM groups. We acknowledge that our primary findings are reported at the phylum level, which is biologically broad. Phylum-level shifts may reflect general dysbiosis rather than disease-specific signals, and the translational relevance of phylum-level markers is limited. Although we present genus-level compositional data in Figs. 1F and 4C, the sample size precluded robust genus-level differential analysis. Future studies should employ more precise methodologies—such as metagenomic sequencing, single-cell technologies, or metabolomic profiling—to elucidate underlying mechanisms.

Limitations of the study

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged to ensure transparent interpretation of our findings

Sources of bias

Selection bias may have been introduced due to the single‑center design and the voluntary nature of healthy control enrollment. Although we applied standardized inclusion and exclusion criteria, the healthy control group was derived from individuals undergoing routine endoscopic examination, which may not fully represent the general population. Measurement bias is possible because we relied on 16S rRNA sequencing of fecal samples, which reflects the luminal rather than the mucosa‑associated microbiota; a metagenomic approach would provide higher taxonomic and functional resolution. Additionally, despite our efforts to adjust for known confounders (age, sex, tumor stage, HER2 status), residual confounding by unmeasured variables—such as proton pump inhibitor use, detailed dietary patterns, body mass index, smoking and alcohol consumption, bowel habits, oral health status, and Helicobacter pylori infection status—cannot be excluded.

Precision of the estimates

The modest sample size, particularly for the peritoneal metastasis subgroup (S‑PM, n = 10; n‑S‑PM, n = 28), resulted in wide confidence intervals and limited statistical power. The AUC for Fusobacteria in distinguishing S‑GC from non‑S‑GC was 0.656 (95% CI not reported in the original analysis), and for S‑PM it was 0.589 (P = 0.380), indicating only low to moderate discriminative ability. These estimates should be interpreted with caution, and the lack of statistical significance for the S‑PM analysis suggests that our findings for PM are preliminary at best.

Data limitations

Our study is correlative by design and does not imply causality. The cross‑sectional design precludes any assessment of temporal changes in the microbiota or the direction of the observed associations (reverse causality remains a possibility). The absence of metagenomic sequencing means we could not assess functional pathways or strain‑level differences. No in vitro or in vivo mechanistic experiments were performed to validate the proposed role of Fusobacteria. Furthermore, we did not collect detailed dietary records or longitudinal medication histories, which may influence gut microbiota composition. Diversity analyses showed inconsistent results between the discovery and validation cohorts (no significant differences in the discovery cohort but significant differences in the validation cohort); this discrepancy may reflect the smaller sample size of the validation cohort and should be interpreted with caution, noting that PERMDISP analysis confirmed these patterns were not driven by differences in within‑group dispersion (Supplementary Table 3). Finally, the role of signet‑ring cell incidence in prognosis, particularly in relation to PM, was not directly addressed and warrants further investigation.

Direction and magnitude of potential bias

Given the exploratory nature of our study and the limited sample size, the reported effect sizes (e.g., AUC values, relative abundances) may be overestimated compared to what would be observed in a larger, independent cohort. A notable limitation is the lack of specificity: Fusobacteria abundance was elevated in SRCC compared to non‑SRCC and also showed a trend toward higher abundance in SRCC with PM, suggesting it may be a general marker of SRCC rather than a specific predictor of PM. The cross‑sectional design does not allow us to determine whether Fusobacteria enrichment precedes or follows the development of PM; therefore, reverse causality remains a possibility.

Generalizability of the findings

Our findings were derived from a single tertiary cancer center in China, and the study population was predominantly of Han Chinese ethnicity. The results may not be generalizable to other ethnic groups, geographic regions, or healthcare settings. External validation in larger, multicenter, and more diverse patient cohorts is essential before Fusobacteria abundance can be considered for clinical application as a non‑invasive biomarker.

We acknowledge that the Greengenes 13.8 database is not the most up-to-date reference; newer databases such as SILVA or GTDB may offer improved taxonomic coverage and accuracy. The use of an older database may affect the annotation of less-characterized taxa. Future studies should consider using a more current reference database.

A major limitation of our study is the use of 16S rRNA gene sequencing, which provides taxonomic resolution only at the phylum and genus levels. As elegantly demonstrated in colorectal cancer research, early 16S-based studies identified an association with the phylum Fusobacteria, but subsequent shotgun metagenomic studies revealed that specific species—particularly Fusobacterium nucleatum—and even specific strains within that species, are the key players associated with tumorigenesis and prognosis. Similarly, in gastric SRCC, we cannot determine from our 16S data whether the observed association is driven by Fusobacterium nucleatum, other Fusobacterium species, or a combination thereof. Future shotgun metagenomic studies are needed to identify the specific species and strains associated with SRCC and PM.

A completed STROBE checklist for cross-sectional studies is available as Supplementary File [STROBE checklist].

Conclusions

In conclusion, our findings suggest that Fusobacteria abundance is associated with gastric SRCC and its PM in this cross-sectional cohort. However, given the modest AUC values, lack of specificity, and inability to identify species-level taxa, Fusobacteria alone is not a clinically useful predictive biomarker. These findings should be considered hypothesis-generating. Future shotgun metagenomic studies in larger, prospective cohorts are needed to determine whether specific Fusobacterium species or strains, or multi-microbe panels, may have clinical utility. With further validation in larger, prospective cohorts, Fusobacteria may serve as an auxiliary indicator, but its current clinical utility is limited.
