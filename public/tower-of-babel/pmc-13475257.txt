Introduction

Vaccines are among the most cost-effective public health interventions, saving 3.5–5 million lives annually by preventing over 30 infectious diseases.1 Vaccination programmes substantially reduce morbidity and mortality, contribute to herd immunity, and strengthen disease prevention and outbreak control efforts.2 3 They also improve life expectancy and population health outcomes, promote health equity, and lessen the burden on healthcare systems and economies.3 4

Evidence generated from vaccine clinical trials informs decisions related to vaccine development, regulatory approval, policy recommendations and large-scale implementation.1 5 6 For such evidence to be robust and generalisable, vaccine clinical trials should adequately represent the populations and settings in which vaccines are ultimately deployed.6

Low-income and middle-income countries (LMICs) bear a disproportionately higher burden of vaccine-preventable diseases and are often the primary targets of national and global immunisation programmes.6–8 Variations in epidemiology, host genetics, nutritional status, comorbidities, environmental exposures and health system capacity may influence vaccine performance and safety profiles.7 Consequently, the geographical distribution of vaccine clinical trials has important implications for the external validity and generalisability of trial findings, as well as for public health decision-making in LMIC settings.6

Despite these considerations, long-standing concerns remain regarding inequities in the global conduct of clinical research.8 Vaccine trials, like many other forms of biomedical research, have historically been concentrated in high-income countries, even when the anticipated benefits are greatest in LMICs.6 7 Although recent global health initiatives have emphasised inclusiveness, local capacity strengthening and equitable research partnerships, it remains unclear to what extent these commitments have translated into improved geographical representation in vaccine clinical trials.6 7 Existing literature on global research inequities has largely focused on ethical considerations, access to post-trial benefits or vaccine availability following licensure.7 Fewer studies have systematically quantified where vaccine clinical trials are conducted or examined how trial characteristics such as phase, sponsor type, funding source and vaccine platform, are associated with LMIC participation.6 7 Moreover, analyses restricted to published studies may underestimate LMIC involvement because many trials conducted in LMIC settings remain unpublished or are reported only in clinical trial registries.6 7

Meta-epidemiological methods provide a systematic way to explore trends in research practices across different trial groups, rather than focusing solely on the effects of interventions.9 By integrating trial-level data from published studies and trial registries, these analyses can uncover underlying patterns in global research activities and reveal areas lacking representation and transparency.9

To date, no comprehensive systematic review has evaluated the representation of LMICs in vaccine clinical trials across diseases, vaccine platforms and trial phases using a meta-epidemiological framework. This systematic review therefore aims to quantify the extent of LMIC representation in vaccine clinical trials and to examine how representation varies according to trial characteristics, sponsorship, funding source and time.

Included

We will include vaccine clinical trials conducted on human participants irrespective of age, sex, pregnancy status or underlying health conditions. Eligible trials will include studies reported in clinical trial registries and/or peer-reviewed publications conducted in any geographical setting worldwide.

Excluded

Animal, preclinical and in vitro studies will be excluded.

Study selection

The selection process will be conducted in accordance with the PRISMA guidelines. All records retrieved from bibliographical databases and clinical trial registries will be imported into reference management software, and duplicate records will be removed prior to screening.

Potential duplicate records identified across multiple registries or publications will be assessed using trial identifiers, study titles, sponsor information, sample size, intervention characteristics and study dates to ensure that each trial is included only once.

Two reviewers will independently screen titles and abstracts against the predefined eligibility criteria. Records considered potentially eligible by either reviewer will proceed to full-text or full-record assessment. Full-text publications and registry records will subsequently be assessed independently by the same reviewers to determine final inclusion.

Any disagreements during screening will be resolved through discussion and consensus. Where consensus cannot be achieved, a third reviewer will be consulted. Reasons for exclusion at the full-text stage will be documented.

The study selection process will be summarised using a PRISMA flow diagram.

Registry records and publications will be linked primarily using trial registration identifiers (eg, National Clinical Trial (NCT) numbers, International Standard Randomised Controlled Trial Number (ISRCTN) identifiers or registry-specific IDs). Where identifiers are unavailable, linkage will be performed manually using study title, sponsor information, sample size, intervention characteristics and study location.

For trials with both registry records and corresponding publications, data will be crosschecked to ensure consistency and avoid duplicate counting. Discrepancies identified during data extraction will be resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer.

Data management

All extracted data will be compiled into a structured database for analysis. Data cleaning procedures will include verification of key variables, consistency checks and duplicate assessment.

The extent and patterns of missing data will be assessed descriptively. Analyses will be conducted using available data, and variables with missing or unclear geographical information will be reported transparently.

Planned analyses and statistical methods

Descriptive statistics will be used to summarise trial characteristics and geographical representation. Categorical variables will be presented as frequencies and proportions, while continuous variables will be summarised using means with SD or medians with IQRs, as appropriate.

The primary unit of analysis will be the individual clinical trial. Trials will be classified according to whether they include at least one LMIC study site.

For multicountry trials, each trial will be counted once in the primary analysis. Multicountry trials involving at least one LMIC site will be classified as LMIC-including trials. Secondary analyses will additionally examine country-level participation and regional representation.

Comparative analyses will assess associations between LMIC participation and trial characteristics, including trial phase, sponsor type, funding source, vaccine platform, study design and publication status. χ² tests or Fisher’s exact tests will be used for categorical comparisons, where appropriate.

If sufficient data are available, multivariable logistic regression analyses will be conducted to identify trial-level factors independently associated with LMIC participation. Variables considered for inclusion in regression models will include trial phase, sponsor type, funding source, vaccine platform, trial size, study design and registration year.

Temporal analyses will be conducted according to trial registration year using predefined calendar periods, including 5-year intervals, where appropriate, while accounting for changes in international trial registration practices over time.

Sensitivity analyses will be undertaken, where feasible, including analyses restricted to completed trials, published trials and registry-only records.

Formal risk-of-bias and certainty-of-evidence assessments using the GRADE methodology will not be undertaken because the review is meta-epidemiological in nature and focuses on trial characteristics and patterns of research conduct rather than on intervention effects or clinical outcomes. Instead, completeness and consistency of reporting between registry entries and corresponding publications will be assessed descriptively.

All analyses will be conducted using Stata statistical software V.19.

Discussion

This systematic review will provide a comprehensive meta-analysis of the geographical representation of LMICs in global vaccine clinical trials and evaluate how representation varies across diverse vaccine platforms, trial phases, sponsors and funding structures.

By integrating empirical evidence from both published studies and international clinical trial registries, the review aims to evaluate structural patterns in global vaccine research and identify critical gaps in geographic inclusiveness.

The findings may inform future policies and strategies to enhance equitable participation in vaccine research and improve the generalisability of evidence on vaccine efficacy and safety for LMIC populations. Additionally, this study may contribute to ongoing discourse on transparency, accountability and best practices in trial registration and reporting in global vaccine research.
