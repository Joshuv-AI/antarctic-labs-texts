"I just needed help": a qualitative study of patients' experiences with a single responder ambulance
Authors: Elisabeth Larsen Engholm, Camilla Brændstrup Laursen, Penille Horsbøl Kirkegaard Jensen, Tine Bennedsen Gehrt, Ulla Væggemose
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn response to the growing demand for prehospital care in the Central Denmark Region, a single responder ambulance, the Prehospital Visitation Unit (PVU), was introduced in early 2022. Staffed by a single paramedic, the PVU focuses on completing patient treatment on-site and/or facilitating access to appropriate healthcare services. Despite the increasing implementation of non-conveyance initiatives, little is known about patients’ experiences of receiving emergency medical care in such settings. This qualitative study aimed to explore patients’ expectations when calling for emergency help and their experiences with encountering the PVU in response to their need for help.MethodsThe study was conducted in two stages of fieldwork: preliminary and main. Participant observation and semi-structured interviews were utilized throughout. The preliminary fieldwork took place between 1 June and 31 August 2022, and the main fieldwork between 1 February and 30 March 2023. In total, the researchers observed 12 PVU shifts, documenting patient-paramedic encounters through fieldnotes. Additionally, 22 patients completed a follow-up telephone interview. Interview transcripts and fieldnotes were analyzed using a two-cycle coding approach.ResultsOverall, patients expressed satisfaction with the PVU and appreciated receiving treatment on-site rather than being transported to the hospital. When asked about their expectations after calling the Emergency Medical Services (EMS), patients consistently emphasized that they “just needed help”. They were unconcerned about which type of EMS resource was dispatched or how many staff arrived. The PVU was perceived as less dramatic due to its smaller vehicle and single-paramedic operation. The paramedic’s calm attitude, clear communication, and use of eye contact contributed to a reassuring atmosphere that put patients at ease.ConclusionsThis study highlights patient satisfaction with the PVU as an EMS response, emphasizing their need for help and the benefits of on-site treatment. Background

The demand for emergency medical services (EMS) has increased considerably over the last decades in Denmark as well as in other Western countries [1–3]. This has become evident with more emergency medical calls [4] along with an increasing use of ambulances and other EMS resources [2, 3, 5]. This growing demand for EMS not only challenges prehospital care but also contributes to increased pressure on hospitals [3]. Alongside this development, the organization of EMS has changed over the last years. EMS services have shifted from primarily focusing on patient transportation to placing greater emphasis on providing advanced assessment and treatment both on-site and during transport to the hospital [2, 6, 7].

As EMS faces increasing demands and new opportunities arise, various initiatives have been introduced to optimize emergency medical care. An emerging theme among these initiatives is the treatment and release of patients, known as non-conveyance, which is used when hospital transport is deemed unnecessary [2, 8, 9]. While this practice has already become more common in ambulances [2, 5, 10–14], several countries have developed and implemented non-conveyance initiatives aimed at providing on-site assessment and treatment, as well as referring patients to the appropriate level of care outside the hospitals [15–22]. Non-conveyance as a prehospital approach has shown to reduce unnecessary hospital transports and visits to emergency departments [15, 16, 22–24], decrease patient waiting times [15], enhance cost-effectiveness, and maintain both patient satisfaction and safety [17, 18, 22, 24].

The existing literature on patients' experiences with single responder ambulances and non-conveyance reveals a notable gap in qualitative research, as most studies primarily rely on questionnaires or surveys [13, 25–27] with only a few based on interviews [12, 28, 29]. Together, the existing studies suggest that patients are generally satisfied with the lack of transport as well as with the option of being treated on-site or at home. Paramedics appear to play a crucial role in shaping patients' experiences with non-conveyance [12, 13, 25–27, 29], with the initial patient-paramedic interaction being particularly critical in the prehospital context [30]. However, further research into patients' experiences with single responder ambulances and non-conveyance is particularly valuable given the ongoing development of EMS, with an increasing focus on non-conveyance, on-site treatment, and reducing unnecessary hospital admissions.

This qualitative study aimed to explore what patients expect when calling for emergency help, and how they experience encountering a single responder ambulance, the Prehospital Visitation Unit (PVU), in response to their need for help in the Central Denmark Region.

Methods

The data for this study stem from an ethnographic exploration of interactions between patients and the PVU. The data were collected through preliminary and main fieldwork conducted in the Central Denmark Region, Denmark, where the PVU was introduced. The preliminary fieldwork, 1 June-31 August 2022, applied an inductive approach, focusing on gaining initial insights into non-conveyance situations. The main fieldwork, 1 February-30 March 2023, continued this approach, building on insights from the preliminary stage. This two-stage approach was appropriate because non-conveyance was a new research area within the organization. Insights from the preliminary fieldwork informed methodological adjustments and improvements to the main fieldwork. All data were collected by ELE and PHKJ.

Setting

This study took place in Aarhus in the Central Denmark Region, one of the five Danish regions responsible for organizing EMS in Denmark. The region is home to 1.3 million inhabitants, including about 370.000 residing in Aarhus, its largest city.

When citizens in Denmark need emergency medical help, they can call the national emergency number 112. These calls are managed by a regional Emergency Medical Coordination Center, where medical dispatchers triage the calls and allocate care based on urgency, assessed using a criteria-based decision tool. Responses are divided into five levels of urgency (A-E): A responses are dispatched to potentially life-threatening situations, while E responses apply to non-emergency cases, such as providing advice or directing patients to other health services [31].

The prehospital resources available include ambulances, mobile emergency care units, non-emergency medical transport, and the national helicopter EMS. While the emergency units are staffed by paramedics, paramedics with special competencies, and/or anesthesiologists, non-emergency medical transports are operated by trained transport personnel [32].

The prehospital visitation unit

The Prehospital Visitation Unit (PVU) is a single responder ambulance introduced in March 2022 in the Central Denmark Region as a supplement to the existing EMS vehicles. The PVU is smaller than an ambulance and does not have the capacity to transport patients. If transport is deemed necessary, an ambulance will be dispatched as an additional vehicle to the scene.

The PVU is staffed by an experienced paramedic with specialized competencies such as operating additional point-of-care diagnostics (e.g. measurements of CRP and lactate) [18]. These competencies allow the paramedic to perform more precise on-site assessments, diagnostics, and treatments, sometimes in consultation with an EMS physician.

The primary tasks of the PVU are to examine, assess, and treat patients on-site, with the aim of optimizing patient evaluations and reducing unnecessary hospital admissions by providing treatment on-site or involving alternative healthcare services.

The PVU is primarily assigned to possible non-conveyance urgency level B responses, involving urgent but non-life-threatening conditions such as ‘impaired consciousness’ or ‘stomach or back pain’ [31]. The PVU may also be assigned to acute situations assessed as potentially life-threatening, in situations where it is the closest vehicle to an emergency and can begin patient treatment before the arrival of other EMS units, or when additional resources are needed on-site, such as during a large traffic accident. At the time of the fieldwork, one PVU was operating in the Central Denmark Region, responding to emergency calls in the proximity of Aarhus.

Participants

The participants of this study are patients who contacted the EMS and had the PVU dispatched to them. Consequently, the researchers were not acquainted with them prior to their inclusion in the study.

Patients were recruited in person either in their own homes or in public places, sometimes with relatives or bystanders present. The researchers provided the patients with information regarding the study and included them either during breaks in their interactions with the paramedics or after the conclusion of the encounter with the PVU. This depended on the situation and the urgency of the paramedics’ work.

Participant observation

Participant observation was used to gain insight into the work of the paramedics operating the PVU, as well as the patients’ experiences and reactions when encountering the PVU [33]. The researchers, ELE and PHKJ, independently accompanied the paramedics operating the PVU during their routine 12-h shifts, observing four shifts in the preliminary fieldwork and eight shifts in the main fieldwork. Six paramedics, all men, participated in this study by facilitating data collection. All paramedics had several years of experience within a prehospital organization, as well as specialist skills that qualified them to carry out the work of the PVU. The fieldwork was planned in accordance with the paramedics’ work schedules to ensure a diverse picture of care provided in the PVU setting and to minimize the influence of individual paramedics on the findings.

Besides observing interactions during the shifts, the researchers assisted the paramedics with simple tasks on-site, such as carrying bags or finding a chair for the patient to sit on. The level of participation varied throughout the fieldwork [33]. In some encounters, the situation was calm, allowing participation; in others, such as during a traffic accident or a suspected stroke, the urgency necessitated a primarily observational role as the situation required the paramedic to focus and act quickly to secure patient safety.

Despite efforts to minimize the researcher’s influence on the patient-paramedic interaction by keeping a primarily observing role while the paramedic led, the researcher’s presence may still have shaped how the paramedics and patients acted. For example, the paramedics may have followed guidelines more strictly, and the patients may have presented their concerns differently.

In both stages of the fieldwork, fieldnotes were written to ensure descriptions of the encounters between the PVU and the patients [34]. In the preliminary fieldwork, the fieldnotes were explorative, and they shortly described the essence of what happened in the situations. For the main fieldwork, the researchers refined their methods based on insights from the preliminary fieldwork. A key outcome was development of an observation guide (see Additional file 1) [34]. The guide focused on three questions:

During the participant observation, the researchers also engaged in informal conversations with patients, relatives, paramedics, and other personnel, which were later on supplemented by formal semi-structured interviews with patients.

Semi-structured interviews

In both stages of fieldwork, patients’ perspectives on receiving help from the PVU were explored through semi-structured interviews conducted once and in Danish. The interviews were designed as semi-structured to ensure coverage of predetermined topics while allowing space for unexpected insights and perspectives to emerge during the interviews [35]. The interviews were conducted over the phone due to the practical, cost- and time-saving advantages of this method, as well as the opportunity to carry out interviews promptly after the patients’ encounter with the PVU [36].

Prior to the interviews, an interview guide was developed. The guide underwent revision between the two stages, with revisions being informed by insights derived from the preliminary fieldwork. The initial guide focused on patient experiences when encountering the PVU, particularly on factors influencing their feeling of safety and security, which was the primary concern of the EMS organization (see Additional file 2). The revised interview guide was structured in two parts (see Additional file 3). In the first part, patients were asked to recount the day they (or someone on their behalf) called the EMS and encountered the PVU, providing narrative accounts of their experiences with receiving emergency medical help and highlighting memorable aspects of their encounter [37]. The second part consisted of three themes identified from the preliminary data: expectations, safety, and preventive potential.

In the preliminary fieldwork of the study, patients were interviewed by the researcher they had previously encountered on-site. In the main fieldwork, it was decided that interviews would be conducted by the researcher who was unfamiliar to the patient. This decision was made to avoid overly brief or less detailed interviews due to the shared understanding between the patient and the researcher both having been present during the encounter with the PVU. This change led patients to share more detailed accounts.

Data collection was concluded upon reaching data saturation, which was evaluated and discussed by all the researchers based on the number of included patients and the extent of new themes identified during participant observation and interviews.

Analysis

We applied methodological triangulation by drawing on data from both interviews and participant observation. All semi-structured interviews were audio recorded. In the preliminary fieldwork, interviews were purposefully transcribed, and in the main fieldwork they were transcribed verbatim. Preliminary fieldwork data were analyzed collaboratively by ELE and PHKJ using manual coding, while main fieldwork data were analyzed by ELE using NVivo 14. All interviews and fieldnotes were coded twice. We followed the analytical approach described by Miles, Huberman, and Saldaña [38], which defines coding as both a method of condensing data and a means of discovery. This approach consists of two coding cycles. The first cycle of coding consisted of an open and inductive approach, with codes emerging progressively throughout the coding process. This process led to the second coding cycle resulting in a sample of pattern codes which were used in the coding in the main fieldwork (see Table 1 for examples of coding). These pattern codes were subsequently synthesized into the two overarching analytical themes presented in the findings.

Ethical considerations

Conducting prehospital research requires careful planning and ethical considerations, due to its often unpredictable and urgent nature [40, 41]. A key challenge is conducting research without compromising patient safety or interfering with care. Therefore, we took four precautions in the study’s design and execution.

First, we clearly distinguished the researchers’ roles from those of prehospital staff by explicitly introducing them as researchers during fieldwork. Along with the occasional mention of their academic background, this was the only information patients received about the researchers. This ensured that the researchers did not become the focus of the interaction.

Second, we planned our strategy for obtaining informed consent, recognizing that patients in distress in the prehospital setting may complicate this process [41]. Paramedics gave verbal consent to participate in the study in a facilitating role both before the study as well as before each shift. On-site, patients were verbally informed about the study and had the option to choose not to have the researcher observing. They were also verbally informed about the option of participating in a follow-up interview. To ensure the validity of the consent, we prepared a sheet with information about the study and the researchers' contact information. This was done to make it easy for the patients to contact the team with questions or to withdraw their consent. We also reiterated the study’s purpose and the participants’ rights when we called them for interviews. If a patient was unable to consent, the researcher remained in the background in an observing role and did not take field notes.

Third, in the urgent prehospital setting, obtaining consent from the patients immediately was not always possible [40, 41]. We therefore took notes of our observations before obtaining consent to ensure fieldnotes describing the encounter from the beginning [34]. If consent was not obtained, the completed observation guide was maculated when the PVU returned to base. No personal information was recorded before consent was obtained.

Fourth, if the researchers were unsure about including a patient, they consulted with the paramedic, who was informed of the exclusion criteria, and/or the patient’s relatives.

Reflexivity and researcher positioning

At the time of data collection, the two primary researchers (ELE and PHKJ, both female) were early-career researchers with academic backgrounds in midwifery, anthropology, political science, and psychology. Furthermore, neither had prior professional relationships with the ambulance service or prehospital care systems. Their status as outsiders was a contributing factor to their curious and open stance during fieldwork, while their disciplinary lenses informed their attention to both structural and interpersonal aspects of the data.

In order to facilitate reflexivity and ensure methodological rigor, the research team convened for regular meetings throughout the study period. These meetings, which included supervision by experienced qualitative researchers (CBL, TBG and UV, all female), provided space to discuss methodological decisions and analytical reflections. These conversations created a forum to consider how the researchers' backgrounds might have influenced their perspectives, thereby contributing to the development of analytical themes. The team's interdisciplinary composition, encompassing anthropology, psychology, political science, cultural sociology and pharmacy, fostered a comprehensive interpretive framework and encouraged continuous reflexive awareness.

Findings

The patients in this study were generally satisfied with their experience of the PVU and with receiving care on-site rather than being transported to the hospital. Two overarching analytical themes were identified: just needing help and no drama. These themes help reveal factors underlying patients' satisfaction with the PVU as a prehospital response to medical emergency calls.

Theme 1: Just needing help

This theme unfolds through four subthemes, providing nuanced insight into how patients perceive and articulate the experience of needing help.

The only option

A recurring theme was that the patients called EMS simply because they needed help and felt they had no alternative. For instance, Mary, an elderly woman with chronic pulmonary disease who experienced breathing difficulties on the day she called for help, stated:

“We simply needed to get some help (…) It wasn’t about taking the easiest option; it was simply the only option…”

Several patients had tried other approaches to getting help such as contacting their general practitioner. When that failed, these patients called the EMS for immediate help as they faced worsening symptoms and incomprehensible consequences. Miriam, a middle-aged woman with acute chest pain, explained:

“So, I called my husband, and he said, "You really need to call 1-1-2 [emergency number] or the doctor [general practitioner]." But I thought, "No, it can't be serious enough for 1-1-2," so I tried to call the doctor, but I couldn't get through. Then I tried to call their emergency number, but I couldn't get through there either... So, I finally had to call 1-1-2, and I got a nurse. I had a video conference with her first, and she said, "I'm sending a vehicle now."

Miriam sought help from various sources, including her general practitioner and her husband, before ultimately calling the EMS, illustrating that for many patients, the primary concern was simply obtaining assistance rather than the specific type of help received.

Expectations to help

When asked about their expectations to the EMS, patients universally expressed a need for help. However, when elaborating about this, it became evident that patients had expectations and assumptions about the nature of that help. Several patients expressed that the help they expected was not tied to a specific type of EMS response or vehicle. This included Alex, a young man seeking assistance for a head injury:

"I don't care if it is one or the other or the third car that comes, as long as help comes!"

Neither Alex nor the other patients requested a certain vehicle, number of paramedics, or specific treatment; they simply needed help to arrive. Instead, the help was described as being tied to someone who could do something. Aida, who had called the EMS due to anxiety and persisting chest pain, elaborated on this:

“I just expected to get help! I mean, I just didn’t want to be alone in that situation where I didn’t know what could be happening to me (…) I didn’t know… I didn’t know when I would wake up or when the pain would go away, so I just tried to call 1-1-2, because… to get help and to not be alone, at least.”

For Aida, help meant not facing her symptoms, such as anxiety, alone. It also meant having someone clarify what was happening to her. This was a common theme among the patients who were uncertain about their symptoms.

Paramedic competences in meeting patients’ needs

All patients spontaneously mentioned the paramedics’ interpersonal skills when describing help. They highlighted that the paramedics were professional and capable of providing competent help. For instance, Thomas, a young man experiencing sudden chest pain, emphasized the paramedic's attitude:

“Um, he was incredibly present, getting down to eye level with lots of eye contact and speaking in a calm tone, and so on. And he constantly explained what he was doing and what the next step would be.”

For Thomas, help was associated with feeling calm and safe. This was echoed by other patients who further described the paramedics as kind, attentive, and accommodating, which reassured them and helped them understand what was happening and what was going to happen next.

In addition to interpersonal qualities, patients had specific expectations about care. They expected paramedics to relieve pain, treat injuries, and arrange transportation to the hospital if necessary. These needs were largely met during the fieldwork.

When expectations are not met

In this EMS context, help meant the reassuring presence of someone who could take charge, explain the situation, and provide care. Upon arrival of the PVU, patients were generally observed to appear passive and expectant, quietly allowing the paramedics to assess them and determine the course of action. Few patients described their symptoms spontaneously or asked questions, and only one expressed dissatisfaction with the care received.

Julia, a young woman with persistent symptoms including headache, vomiting, and sensory disturbances, was the only patient in the study who was dissatisfied with the PVU. Despite seeking assistance multiple times from her general practitioner and other services, Julia had not received any explanations or treatment that had helped her. Julia felt her concerns were dismissed, leaving her feeling lost in the healthcare system:

"I have tried to make contact, you know? Umm, or maybe through my general practitioner regarding what else could be done. I feel like I'm a bit lost in the system."

Unable to get help from less acute services, Julia felt compelled to call the EMS for persistent symptoms that had now become an acute concern. When Julia called the EMS, she hoped that someone would take her concerns seriously and provide a solution. However, the paramedic assessed her condition as non-acute, leaving her without the help she was expecting:

"But I mean, what am I supposed to do... Insist? I didn't know what else to do. Should I have said no [to staying at home], but you must take me to the hospital?"

Julia's experience illustrates that despite the strengths of the PVU, there are still patients who seek help from the EMS but ultimately do not receive it, as the assistance they require falls outside the scope of the EMS.

Overall, patients consistently expressed that they “just needed help” when calling EMS. In this context, help was embodied by someone who could provide professional, competent care, take control of the situation, and bring clarity and reassurance to an uncertain experience.

Theme 2: no drama

This theme is presented through two subthemes that illustrate how the PVU is perceived to contribute to a reduction in on-site drama.

Avoid attracting attention

All patients needed help from the EMS, and they needed it to come directly to them. At the same time, several patients expressed that they disliked attracting the attention that EMS vehicles normally draw with their sirens, flashing blue lights, and their striking yellow color. One such patient was Peter, who suddenly developed severe abdominal pain while at work:

“Being the center of attention. That’s why I… I couldn’t handle all of that, if suddenly seventeen emergency vehicles showed up. That wasn’t what I called for.”

Peter, like other patients, found himself in what can be seen as a paradox where he needed instant emergency assistance, but not to be a burden or attract too much attention. Here, the PVU was described positively as a less attention-attracting vehicle compared to full emergency responses including bigger vehicles, sirens, lights, and stretchers. David, who received EMS assistance for sudden dizziness and fainting, elaborated on this positive perception of the PVU by stating the following:

“But there was no drama. No drama if I can put it that way. It was calm and steady, with a sort of flow... Where I became more and more, you could say, awake, right? And I also relaxed a lot more...”

The PVU seemed less dramatic than an ambulance to David and other patients because it was a smaller vehicle, it arrived without lights and sirens, and there was only one paramedic.

Calm atmosphere

The PVU was not only seen as a less dramatic EMS response on arrival. In general, the patients described the PVU as contributing to a calm atmosphere, even in the midst of the acute situations they were facing. By the end of the encounters, many patients as well as relatives or bystanders were observed to appear physically calmer – their shoulders were visibly relaxed, their previously tense expressions were often replaced by gentle smiles and a softer, calmer look in their eyes. In several situations, bystanders were also seen leaving the scene as soon as the paramedic initiated the assessment and treatment of the patient. Furthermore, patients began to engage in more casual small talk with both paramedics and researchers towards the end of the interaction, indicating an increased sense of calm and/or enhanced sense of well-being following the encounter with the PVU.

The less dramatic atmosphere was primarily due to two characteristics of the PVU. The first; it treats patients on-site, in their home or at a public place. Patients were neutral or positive about being assessed and/or treated on-site. Those neutral about the location explained that they were focused on getting help and did not think much about where it took place. Most patients were very satisfied with the option of being treated at home or in the community instead of at a hospital. They described feeling safer at home and appreciating the involvement of their relatives. One patient, Thomas, felt reassured not to have to leave his family when he experienced sudden chest pain:

“I had actually expected a regular ambulance to arrive and that we would go out to it, and so on. But it was very calm and reassuring that we could sit on the couch and take things slowly. It also meant that my wife could listen in without having to go outside with our newborn.”

The second; the PVU allows paramedics to spend more time on-site with patients, thoroughly assessing their condition and providing the necessary treatment. This was, for instance, explained by Kim, who had suffered a head wound while at work:

"There wasn't an atmosphere that you [the paramedic and the researcher] were in a hurry to move on. What had to be done was done, and when that was in place, then you left."

The lack of haste and minimizing the drama enhanced the positive experience of encountering the PVU. The same was true of the paramedics’ attitudes towards patients, as David explained:

“In the calm atmosphere they created by explaining what they were doing. "You just need to put these on. Just lift your arm”, or what's it called, “roll up your sleeve”, so they could take a sample from my arm, right?"

Overall, a key point about the PVU and how it differs from an ambulance, which would have been the alternative response for these patients in this setting, was that the PVU was perceived and experienced as a less dramatic EMS response:

"It all helps to keep the heart rate down (…) At least it doesn't activate more alertness. On the contrary…" (David).

Discussion

In response to the increasing demand for EMS over the past decades, a single responder ambulance, the PVU, was introduced in the Central Denmark Region. This qualitative study, based on ethnographic methods, aimed to explore patients’ expectations when calling for emergency help and experiences with encountering the PVU as a response to their need for help. The study showed how the PVU has proven to be an interesting focal point for exploring contemporary EMS and how patients experienced a system that has shifted from primarily focusing on transport to providing treatment and care on-site and/or during transport. Patients did not have specific expectations about the type of response they wanted from the EMS. Help was perceived in a less concrete way, as the patients "just needed help" from someone who could take charge, clarify the situation, and initiate appropriate care and treatment. In addition, the PVU was perceived as a less dramatic option compared to other EMS responses, which was positively emphasized by patients as they benefitted from a response that promoted a calm atmosphere. The PVU-patients were initially not assessed to be in life-threatening danger, but most of them still experienced fear. Despite this, and despite the fact that they needed emergency help, they also preferred not to draw unnecessary attention to the situation, which a more subtle response helped to achieve.

Patient safety and sense of safety

The patients in this study contacted the EMS because they needed assistance and feared for the situation they were in. Studies have pointed to how patients feel distress, fear, and/or anxiety when contacting the EMS for help [28, 30]. This was also the case for the patients in this study as many, due to their various symptoms, described fearing for their lives and seeking confirmation that their symptoms did not indicate a life-threatening emergency.

Investigating safety in relation to non-conveyance as well as other prehospital initiatives is common, and several studies suggest that non-conveyance, under the right circumstances, is a safe prehospital choice [18, 22]. However, these studies are primarily based on investigating patient safety building on data illustrating factors of morbidity and mortality rather than patients' experienced sense of safety. Exploring this aspect is an important addition to investigating safety in relation to EMS initiatives, as it may influence patients' level of satisfaction, trust in the healthcare system, and their future help-seeking behavior [25, 42]. In addition to potentially shaping the immediate encounter, these factors may also impact future situations requiring medical assistance.

Patients contacting the EMS have been described as “surrendering in dependence of another” [43] and handing over responsibility for their bodies to professionals [44], meaning that patients were in a position where they could no longer take care of themselves and therefore, they surrendered to the health care professionals. Arriving on-site with the paramedics in this study, patients’ reactions were characterized by minimal verbal engagement, few or no questions, and a tense, expressionless facial expression, emphasizing a surrendering behavior towards the paramedics operating the PVU. Furthermore, they expressed fear or worry, having furrowed brows, shrugged shoulders, and some were crying. This adds to the literature describing the initial state of emotion for patients contacting the EMS [12, 28].

From the initial encounters in this study, patients placed trust in the hands of the paramedics underscoring the impact of the paramedics on the patients’ experiences, including their sense of safety [30]. This dynamic between the patient and the paramedic creates an uneven relationship, where the paramedic holds a significant responsibility for the situation as experienced by the patient. This includes the responsibly of whether the patient feels safe or not. Here, the PVU serves as a case for illustrating how paramedics can manage this responsibility and contribute to a sense of safety for patients, despite several potentially fear-inducing factors: symptoms like pain or discomfort, the on-site setting, decision on non-conveyance, and a single paramedic. Hence, paramedics must have a person-centered approach while also taking charge of the situation and contribute to a calm atmosphere. Previous studies have illustrated how feeling safe arises from especially level of information and communicative skills in combination with the paramedics’ soft skills like having a compassionate approach [12, 13, 27–29].

The relationship between expectations and experience

In this study, the patients who reported good experiences and who were satisfied with the PVU were the same patients whose expectations were met. As they expected, these patients received help in the form of someone who did something that either resolved their problem or referred them to someone responsible for doing so. On the other hand, one patient was dissatisfied because she did not receive the help she had hoped for. In this setting, expectations appear to have a potentially large impact on patient satisfaction, alongside other factors such as previous encounters with EMS, trust, communication, and the context of the encounter [45], which this study does not include.

As we continue to study patients’ perspectives through experiences, this study shows the importance of keeping patients’ expectations in mind, as they can impact outcomes. Previous studies also demonstrate a link between expectations and satisfaction, which is consistent with this study [46, 47]. They describe how there sometimes is a mismatch between clinicians’ perceptions of what patients expected and what patients actually reported that they expected [48] which underscores the value in exploring expectations from the patients’ own point of view. Although several studies describe a connection between expectations and the outcome of experiences, the relationship remains unclear, highlighting the need for further research in this area. This knowledge would support the further development of EMS initiatives and enhance communication about EMS capabilities when reaching out for medical assistance.

Strengths and limitations

This qualitative study builds on ethnographic methods that complements the existing, primarily quantitative, studies examining patients’ experiences with non-conveyance within EMS [12, 13, 25–29]. The present study provides in-depth insight into patients’ expectations of EMS care and their experiences receiving assistance from a single responder ambulance by observing interactions between patients and the PVU and including patients’ reflections afterwards. The study was methodologically refined throughout the field data collection, as insights from the preliminary fieldwork led to adjustments in the main fieldwork. This contributed to a more comprehensive understanding of the patients’ expectations and experiences.

The inclusion of patients was based on those needing assistance on the days the researchers accompanied the PVU. This ensured diversity in age, gender, and medical conditions, strengthening the study’s relevance across different demographics. Of the 49 eligible patients, 19 were excluded, primarily due to age or being under the influence of alcohol or drugs. While this limits the representativeness of the findings, the study still captures the experiences of the majority of the patient group. However, a subgroup frequently encountered by the PVU, patients affected by substance abuse and complex psychosocial conditions, is not represented in this sample and should be addressed in future studies.

Despite these strengths, certain limitations should be acknowledged. The study’s geographical scope was limited to Aarhus, a larger city with a different demographic than the rest of the region. Given the service requirement to reach patients within 30 min, the PVU cannot be expected to operate similarly across the rest of the region due to the greater geographical distances.

Furthermore, both gratitude bias and social desirability bias are relevant to consider, as patients received help in moments of acute need. However, the interview data, particularly the account of one patient, Julia, demonstrate that critical perspectives were also present. To minimize these potential bias, the researchers emphasized their interest in both positive and negative experiences and explicitly included a question in the interview guide asking what patients wished had been different during their encounter with the PVU (see Additional Files 2 and 3).

In addition, the study did not explore patients’ expectations prior to seeking emergency care from the PVU, which could have provided insights into pre-existing perceptions of EMS care and the meaning of help in an emergency context. Instead, the expectations shown in this study may be influenced by the encounters between patients and the PVU, including the outcome of the situation. A deeper understanding of how expectations evolve throughout a care process could contribute to a broader perspective on patient expectations and experiences with EMS.

Conclusion

Most patients described being satisfied with the PVU as an EMS vehicle responding to emergency calls, expressing that they "just needed help" and recognizing the benefits of being treated on-site. This study contributes to the limited existing literature that explores patient experiences with single responder ambulances and non-conveyance in an EMS setting, adding how the PVU meets patient expectations, how the PVU adds to a calm atmosphere, and how it fosters a sense of safety for patients being assessed and treated on-site.
