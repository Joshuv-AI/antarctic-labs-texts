Decoding lactylation heterogeneity in glioblastoma: machine learning identifies G6PC3 as a prognostic target
Authors: Yushu Liu, Hui Deng, Ping Song, Qingya Qiu, Jiarui Bu, Mengxian Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGlioblastoma (GBM) is the most common malignant glioma in adults. It has an extremely poor prognosis, highlighting an urgent need for new therapeutic strategies to improve patient survival. Lactylation is a novel post-translational modification (PTM) that modulates tumor progression through multiple mechanisms. Yet, research on lactylation in GBM remains limited and fragmented.MethodThis study integrated GBM single-cell sequencing data with bulk data from TCGA-GBM, CGGA325, and CGGA693. We first constructed a single-cell lactylation-associated gene expression score (LAGES), and performed subpopulation analysis and functional enrichment on the high and low LAGES groups. Correlation analysis was used to clarify the association between the LAGES and malignant phenotypes, while cell communication analysis was conducted to explore key interaction pathways. We employed 10 machine learning algorithms to build the model, from which we identified the key genes and subsequently constructed a Cox regression analysis model. Meanwhile, the function of the core gene was validated by combining clinical features, functional enrichment, drug sensitivity analysis, and in vitro experiments.ResultsSingle-cell LAGES effectively stratified GBM cells into high- and low-expression subgroups with distinct functional profiles. High-LAGES tumor cells were enriched in pro-invasive, pro-angiogenic, and immune-suppressive pathways. Low-LAGES cells showed active T cell activation and metabolic homeostasis. Correlation analysis confirmed LAGES was positively associated with GBM malignant phenotypes, including invasion, DNA repair, and epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT). Cell communication analysis identified the SPP1-CD44 axis as a key interaction pathway between macrophages and tumor cells. This axis may potentially amplify malignancy in high-LAGES populations, though this inference is exploratory and requires further functional validation. Among the ten machine learning models, the StepCox [backward] + Random Survival Forest (RSF) model exhibited preliminary optimal prognostic performance in the studied cohorts. G6PC3 was identified as the top-ranked core gene closely associated with lactylation. G6PC3 expression increased with glioma grade, which was validated by tissue microarrays. Clinically, high G6PC3 expression correlated with unfavorable features. It also served as an independent poor prognostic factor for IDH-wildtype GBM in the current datasets. Functional enrichment linked G6PC3 to G protein-coupled receptor (GPCR) signaling, calcium ion transport, and exocytosis. Drug sensitivity analysis identified six candidate inhibitors for high-G6PC3 GBM, which are preliminary candidates requiring further preclinical validation. Notably, high G6PC3 expression may tentatively correlate with a potential, unconfirmed higher response to PD-1 inhibitors—a strictly hypothesis-generating finding with no direct clinical predictive value, pending rigorous validation in large, well-characterized ICB-treated cohorts. In vitro experiments confirmed G6PC3 knockdown inhibited GBM cell proliferation, migration, and invasion, while promoting apoptosis. This validated its potential pro-tumorigenic role in vitro, with no in vivo or clinical validation completed.ConclusionWe constructed LAGES to decipher lactylation heterogeneity in GBM and identified G6PC3 as a key gene closely associated with lactylation. G6PC3 may act as a preliminary prognostic biomarker and a strictly hypothesis-generating candidate for exploring potential immunotherapy response in GBM, with no definitive clinical utility implied. Its targeted inhibitors provide a preliminary, exploratory new direction for precision therapy research. Deepening the preliminary understanding of lactylation’s potential role in GBM lays a foundational, exploratory basis for subsequent translational research, rather than implying immediate translational applicability.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-026-16225-6.Keywords: Glioblastoma (GBM), Lactylation, Machine Learning, Prognostic model, G6PC3

Introduction

Malignant central nervous system tumors are one of the major challenges in the field of modern medicine, for which there are currently no effective treatments [1, 2]. A large proportion of these are malignant gliomas. Glioblastoma (GBM) is the most common malignant glioma in adults, characterized by high invasiveness, high recurrence rate, and extremely poor prognosis, with its incidence increasing with age [3]. Despite certain progress in current treatment strategies, recurrence of GBM is almost inevitable. The poor response of GBM to conventional therapies is attributed to its high heterogeneity, dense structure, blood-brain barrier (BBB) obstruction, and immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME) [4]. Therefore, there is an urgent need to develop new therapeutic approaches to prolong the survival of GBM patients, reduce recurrence, and improve their quality of life.

Post-translational modification of proteins is a process that alters protein structure and function through the covalent attachment of chemical groups [5]. Among these, lactylation modification is a novel type of post-translational modification. It involves the addition of lactic acid molecules to specific proteins, thereby altering the structure and function of the proteins. In the tumor microenvironment, lactic acid accumulates in large quantities to promote tumorigenesis; local high lactic acid levels can induce an acidic microenvironment, enhance immunosuppression, and facilitate tumor invasion and metastasis [6]. Abnormal lactylation occurs in various types of tumors, including breast cancer [7], colorectal cancer [8], and liver cancer [9]. However, to date, research on lactylation in GBM remains limited. Single-cell sequencing technology has recently facilitated more in-depth exploration of tumors [10, 11]. Given the scarcity of single-cell proteomic data on protein lactylation in GBM, transcriptomic-based scores have been widely used as surrogate indicators to investigate lactylation-related biological processes in tumor heterogeneity studies. Therefore, we constructed lactylation-associated gene expression score (LAGES) based on a carefully curated set of lactylation-related gene(LRGs) sets from a published study, aiming to characterize the activity of lactylation-related transcriptional programs. Building on this, the study conducted in-depth analysis of LRGs, performed subpopulation analysis, phenotypic correlation analysis, and cell communication analysis, and constructed an optimal prognostic model based on machine learning to identify G6PC3 as a key prognostic gene for GBM. Meanwhile, the important function of G6PC3 in GBM was verified through both bioinformatics analysis and experimental validation, laying a foundation for subsequent functional characterization and prognostic research.

Materials and methods

Data download

We downloaded the single-cell sequencing data of GBM (GSE84465, including 3589 cells) [12] from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/gds/). GBM RNA-seq data and LGG RNA-seq data was downloaded from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database (https://www.cancer.gov/ccg/research/genome-sequencing/tcga), and glioma RNA-seq data (CGGA325 includes 137 GBM patients and CGGA693 includes 237 GBM patients) was downloaded from the Chinese Glioma Genome Atlas (CGGA) database (https://www.cgga.org.cn/). Additionally, we obtained 330 LRGs from the paper published by Cheng Z et al. [13]. The 330 LRGs are composed of two categories: (1)Lactylation regulatory enzymes: Including 1 lactylase (EP300) and 6 delactylases (HDAC1, HDAC2, HDAC3, SIRT1, SIRT2, SIRT3), which were selected based on previously published studies and cover the core regulatory steps of lactylation modification; (2)Lactylated target protein-coding genes: These genes correspond to 327 lactylated proteins using a novel ion-based diagnostic strategy in human cell lines. Finally, the 7 regulatory enzymes and 327 target protein-coding genes were merged without redundancy, resulting in a total of 330 LRGs. Furthermore, we obtained the gene sets HALLMARK_HYPOXIA, HALLMARK_GLYCOLYSIS, GOBP_LACTATE_METABOLIC_PROCESS, GOBP_LACTATE_TRANSPORT, and GOMF_LACTATE_TRANSMEMBRANE_TRANSPORTER_ACTIVITY from the MSigDB database(https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/msigdb/human/genesets.jsp).

Analysis of single-cell sequencing data

We used the Seurat R package [14] to perform normalization and scaling on the raw count expression profile of GSE84465. Genes expressed in no more than three cells were removed. The LAGES was constructed using the AddModuleScore function based on lactylation modification-related genes. It is important to note that LAGES captures the co-expression pattern of LRGs rather than direct lactylation PTM levels, serving as a transcriptomic surrogate to reflect the transcriptional activity of lactylation-associated pathways. Subsequently, cells were grouped into high and low LAGES groups based on the median of the LAGES. We applied the top 500 highly variable features of the dataset for principal component analysis (PCA) to reduce data dimensionality, and the first 20 principal components were used for downstream analysis. Cell subpopulations were identified using the “FindClusters” function and visualized by t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE). To identify marker genes for each subpopulation, the “FindMarkers” function was used for each LAGES subpopulation.We further subdivided the subpopulations of the high and low LAGES groups using the clustree package, respectively. To determine the optimal number of subgroups for the high and low LAGES groups, we evaluated cluster stability across resolutions ranging from 0.1 to 0.9 using the “clustree” method. This approach uses node size to reflect the number of cells in each cluster and arrows to indicate cluster splits as resolution increases. Based on the branching characteristics, a resolution of 0.5 was selected for the high LAGES group, resulting in 4 stably clustered subgroups with significant functional heterogeneity. For the low LAGES group, a resolution of 0.1 was chosen, which divided the group into 3 subgroups. These subgroups showed clear separation in the t-SNE plot and significant differences in LAGES. Increasing the resolution of the low LAGES group led to more clusters; however, these fine-grained subgroups contained fewer cells, with negligible differences in LAGES, and thus lacked biological significance. We visualized subgroup distribution using Sankey diagrams via the networkD3 package and displayed LAGES differences among different subgroups using violin plots.

Differential expression analysis of genes between different cell populations was conducted using the FindMarkers function. For differential expression analysis, the Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used by default to assess statistical significance, and p-values were adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg method. The results were finally visualized by volcano plots. In addition, for the upregulated differential genes of each LAGES subgroup, Gene Ontology (GO) analysis was conducted using the clusterProfiler R package (p < 0.05, FDR < 0.05). The results were visualized using the ggplot2 package.Phenotypic signature genes were downloaded from the CancerSEA database (http://biocc.hrbmu.edu.cn/CancerSEA/), and the phenotypic score of each cell was calculated based on these signature genes using the AddModuleScore function. Subsequently, Pearson correlation analysis was performed between this phenotypic score and the LAGES constructed earlier.

Crosstalk between tumor cells and macrophages in different LAGES groups was analyzed using the CellChat package [15]. The gene expression data of each cell were derived from the Seurat-normalized expression matrix, and overexpressed ligands or receptors in cell types were identified.

Construction of prognostic models

Based on 10 machine learning algorithms (random survival forest (RSF), Lasso, generalized boosted regression modeling (GBM), CoxBoost, stepwise Cox, partial least squares regression for Cox (plsRcox), supervised principal components (SuperPC), Ridge, elastic network (Enet), and survival support vector machine (survival-SVM)), 101 combined algorithms were used to construct predictive models for the training set (TCGA-GBM) and testing sets (CGGA325 and CGGA693), which were evaluated using a 10-fold nested cross-validation (NCV) framework to ensure the robustness of model performance and avoid overfitting and data leakage. Specifically, the outer 10-fold cross-validation loop was applied to assess the overall model generalization ability, while the inner cross-validation loop was utilized for hyperparameter tuning and feature selection optimization. Considering the effectiveness and wide applicability of the C-index in evaluating the prognostic prediction accuracy of models, we calculated and compared the average C-index of the constructed prognostic models to assess their value. Finally, we performed Cox regression analysis based on the key genes to construct a prognostic model. Patients were divided into high-risk and low-risk groups according to the median risk score, and Kaplan-Meier (K-M) curves were plotted. Additionally, the timeROC package was used to generate Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curves and we also performed calibration analysis of the model.

Bioinformatics analysis of single genes

Differential analysis and prognostic analysis were conducted based on the self-downloaded TCGA-GBM and CGGA data, with visualization via violin plots. K-M curves were used to visualize prognostic differences between high and low G6PC3 expression groups. Additionally, univariate and multivariate COX proportional hazards regression analyses were performed to evaluate the prognostic value of G6PC3 in IDH-wildtype gliomas. Nine clinical features were included in the univariate analysis; variables with P < 0.05 from the univariate analysis were further incorporated into the multivariate model to verify whether G6PC3 serves as an independent prognostic factor. GSEA was used to identify enriched signaling pathways among differentially expressed genes between high and low G6PC3 groups. It is important to note that GSEA results reflect statistical enrichment of pathway-related genes and do not directly confirm functional or molecular interactions between G6PC3 and the identified pathways. Subsequent experimental validation is required to verify causal relationships. In the CancerSEA database, correlation analysis was performed between G6PC3 expression and phenotypes in various cancers. Moreover, we used the TICA database(https://tcia.at) to evaluate the potential immunotherapeutic efficacy of immunotherapy in subgroups with different G6PC3 expression levels, and the cellMiner package was applied for drug sensitivity analysis. The immunophenotype score (IPS) was used to infer potential responses to anti-CTLA-4 and anti-PD-1 antibodies. It is important to note that IPS is a transcriptome-derived surrogate index that reflects the presence of immune-related features associated with immunotherapy response, rather than a direct measure of clinical treatment outcomes. Thus, the inference based on IPS should be interpreted as an indirect indication of potential immunotherapy sensitivity.

Experimental validation of the single gene G6PC3

Cell culture

The U87 and U251 glioma cell lines were obtained from Shanghai Genechem Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Cells were cultured in Corning basic medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Ausbian) and 100 U/mL penicillin-streptomycin mixture (GibCo BRL, Grand Island, NY, USA) at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂. The culture medium was replaced every 2–3 days. When cell confluency reached approximately 80%, cells were passaged using 0.25% trypsin and seeded at appropriate densities for subsequent experiments.

Cell transfection

The lentiviral shRNA constructs targeting the human G6PC3 (sh-G6PC3-1: P25D2421, CCCTCAGATCAGCCTCTTCTA; sh-G6PC3-2: P25D2422, CCTCATCTATTGGACCCTCTT; sh-G6PC3-3: P25D2423, GCTGGGAAATGGCCAGAAGAT) were cloned into GV493 plasmid. The viral vector were transfacted into 293T cells using Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc.) together with two helper plasmid psPAX2 and pMD2.G. Viruses were collected at 72 h after transfection and then used to infect cells with Polybrene. Stable transfectants were generated by limiting dilution after selection with Puromycin (MedChemExpress) for 2 weeks.

Western Blot (WB)

Total protein was extracted from stably transfected U87 and U251 cells using Western and IP cell lysis buffer (Beyotime) containing 1 mM PMSF (Beyotime), followed by sonication and centrifugation. Protein concentrations were determined using a BCA Protein Assay Kit (Beyotime). Equal amounts of protein samples (adjusted to 2 µg/µL) were mixed with 6× loading buffer, boiled, separated by 5% stacking gel and 8–15% SDS-PAGE (formulated according to G6PC3 molecular weight), and transferred onto PVDF membranes (Millipore) via wet transfer at 4 °C with a constant current of 200 mA for 120 min. Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk in TBST for 1 h at room temperature or overnight at 4 °C, then incubated with primary antibodies (diluted in blocking buffer) overnight at 4 °C. The primary antibodies are as follows: G6PC3 (1:1000, ER1909-20, HUABIO) and β-actin (1:8000, sc-69879, Santa Cruz). After 4 washes with TBST (8 min each), membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1.5 h at room temperature, followed by another 4 TBST washes. The secondary antibodies are as follows: Anti-rabbit IgG (1:10000, #7074, CST) and Anti-mouse IgG (1:10000, #7076, CST). Chemiluminescent signals were detected using an ECL-PLUS Kit (Cell Signaling Technology) and a Bio-rad ChemiDox high-sensitivity chemiluminescence system.

Real-time quantitative reverse transcription Polymerase Chain Reaction (qRT-PCR)

All RNA samples were reverse transcribed to cDNA using M-MLV reverse transcriptase (Promega) according to the manufacturer’s protocol, with the reaction system containing 5× RT buffer, 10 mM dNTPs (Promega), Oligo dT (Sangon Biotech), and RNase Inhibitor (Promega). SYBR-based real-time quantitative PCR was performed on a Roche LightCycler 480 II qPCR instrument (Roche). PCR products for G6PC3 were detected using gene-specific primers (Shanghai Genechem). The PCR reaction was carried out in a 12 µL system per well using 1× SYBR Master Mixture (TAKARA), with cDNA as the template. Two-step PCR was performed with melting curve analysis: the reaction procedure followed standard two-step amplification parameters, and melting curve analysis was conducted to verify amplification specificity. Fold-change (FC) in gene expression levels were calculated using the 2-ΔΔCt method.

The experiment of Cell Counting Kit-8 assay

Cell proliferation was assessed using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay (Dojindo Laboratories, Japan) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Stably transfected U87 and U251 cells in logarithmic growth phase were digested with trypsin (GIBCO), resuspended in complete culture medium (Corning basic medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, Ausbian), and counted using a Nexcelom Cellometer Auto1000 cell counter. Cells were seeded in 96-well plates (Corning) at a density of 2000 cells per well in 100 µL of complete culture medium, with 3 replicate wells per group. After cells settled completely, cell density was observed under a microscope; if uneven, the cell amount was adjusted for re-seeding. The plates were then incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO₂. Starting from the second day after seeding, 10 µL of CCK-8 solution was added to each well 2–4 h before the end of culture, without changing the medium. After incubation, the 96-well plates were placed on an oscillator and shaken for 2–5 min. The absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader (Tecan infinite) to evaluate cell viability. All experiments were performed in triplicate.

Transwell migration assay

Transwell inserts (Corning) were placed in a new 24-well plate. The upper chamber was added with 100 µL serum-free medium (Corning basic medium) and incubated at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂incubator (SANYO) for 1 h. Stably transfected U87 and U251 cells in logarithmic growth phase were digested with trypsin (GIBCO), resuspended in serum-free medium, and counted. A 100 µL cell suspension (adjusted to 1 × 10⁵ cells per well based on pre-experiment) was added into the upper chamber, whereas 600 µL culture medium containing 30% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Ausbian) was added into the bottom chamber; the cells were incubated at 37 °C for an appropriate time determined by pre-experiment. After incubation, the inserts were inverted on absorbent paper to remove medium, and non-migrated cells in the upper chamber were gently wiped off with cotton swabs. The inserts were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd.) for 30 min, then stained with 0.5% crystal violet aqueous solution (Shanghai Yuanye Bio-Technology Co., Ltd.) for 1–3 min. After rinsing and air-drying, migrated cells were visualized and counted using an inverted microscope (Shanghai Caikon Optical Instruments Co., Ltd.). Nine 200× fields were randomly selected for counting and statistical analysis.

Apoptotic rate assessed through flow cytometric analysis

Apoptosis in stably transfected U87 and U251 cells was assessed using an Annexin V-APC & DAPI Apoptosis Detection Kit (eBioscience) and flow cytometry. Briefly, cells were cultured in 6-well plates until the confluency reached approximately 70% (drug induction was performed if required by the experimental design). After treatment, adherent cells were harvested: supernatant cells were collected first, then the remaining cells were digested with trypsin, resuspended in complete medium, and combined with the supernatant cells into the same 5 mL centrifuge tube. Each group was set with three replicate wells, ensuring the cell number was ≥ 5 × 10⁵ per treatment. Suspended cells were collected directly. The harvested cells were centrifuged at 1300 rpm for 5 min, and the supernatant was discarded. Cell pellets were washed with 4 °C pre-cooled D-Hanks (pH = 7.2 ~ 7.4), then washed once with 1×binding buffer, followed by centrifugation at 1300 rpm for 3 min to collect cells. Cells were resuspended in 200 µL 1×binding buffer for double staining: 10 µL Annexin V-APC was added for incubation at room temperature for 10–15 min, then centrifuged to remove supernatant, and cells were resuspended in 200 µL 1×binding buffer; 1 µL DAPI was added, and 400–800 µL 1×binding buffer was supplemented according to the cell amount. The cells were incubated in the dark and analyzed using a flow cytometer (Beckman) within 15 min. The Annexin V-positive/DAPI-negative population was considered early apoptotic cells, while Annexin V-positive/DAPI-positive population included mid-late apoptotic and necrotic cells. The experiment was performed in triplicate, and apoptosis rates were calculated as the percentage of apoptotic cells (early and mid-late apoptosis) relative to the total cell population.

Cell scratch assays

Cell migration ability was evaluated using Celigo scratch assay. Stably transfected U87 and U251 cells in logarithmic growth phase were digested with trypsin (GIBCO), resuspended in complete medium (Corning basic medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, Ausbian), and counted. Cells were seeded into 96-well plates (Corning) at a density of 50,000 cells/well with 100 µL culture system per well, and incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO₂ (SANYO) until reaching over 90% confluence the next day. Each group was set with 3 replicate wells. A dedicated scratch tool was used to create scratches by pushing upward at the central upper part of the 96-well plate. The wells were gently rinsed 2–3 times with PBS (Corning) to remove floating cells, then 1% FBS-containing medium was added, and Celigo (Nexcelom) was used for plate scanning at 0 h. Cells were continued to culture, and plate scanning was performed at appropriate time points based on wound healing status, with a total of three time points collected. Scratch areas were analyzed using Celigo software. Cell migration rate was calculated by the formula: Migration rate = [(S3 + S4) - (S1 + S2)] / [1 - (S1 + S2)], where (S1 + S2) represents the cell area at 0 h and (S3 + S4) represents the cell area at x h.

Tissue microarray and immunohistochemistry

Tissue microarrays with clinical information were obtained from Outdo Biotech (Supplementary Table 1). The antibody used was G6PC3 (1:5000, Proteintech, 23967-1-AP). The EnVision detection system (Dako) was used according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Immunostained microarrays were scored by multiplying the staining intensity (0–3) and extent (0-100) of each tissue spot, as previously described by Bollag et al. [16].

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.4.1) or GraphPad Prism 10. The Kruskal-Wallis test was used for analyzing all multiple group comparisons. For normally distributed data, Student’s t-test was applied for comparisons between groups; otherwise, the Mann-Whitney test was used. All correlation analyses were conducted using Spearman’s correlation analysis. A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Establishment of the LAGES

Establishment of the LAGES

The flowchart of the entire study is shown in Fig. 1. First, we downloaded 330 LRGs. To verify the rationality of the LRGs, we analyzed the overlap between this gene set and HALLMARK_HYPOXIA and HALLMARK_GLYCOLYSIS gene sets. As shown in the supplementary Fig. 1A, there are 5 genes (ALDOA, ALDOB, ENO1, PFKP, and PGK1) that are the same in all three gene sets and these five genes all encode the core rate-limiting enzymes in the glycolysis pathway. This partial overlap reflects the inherent physiological interaction between lactylation and glycolysis/hypoxia response. It is noteworthy that over 96% of the genes are specific to the LRG set, indicating that the gene sets used in this study are not merely a simple repetition of the characteristics of glycolysis or hypoxia genes. Then, we downloaded 330 LRGs and established the LAGES based on these genes. LAGES captures the co-expression pattern of LRGs, serving as a transcriptomic surrogate to reflect the transcriptional activity of lactylation-associated pathways. In the GBM dataset GSE84465, we identified 7 major cell types (Immune cells, Neoplastic cells, Oligodendrocyte Precursor Cells(OPCs), Neurons, Oligodendrocytes, Astrocytes, Vascular cells) (Fig. 2A) and calculated the LAGES (Fig. 2B). Based on the median of the LAGES, cells were divided into high LAGES groups and low LAGES groups (Fig. 2C), and the proportions of high and low LAGES groups in each cell type and different regions were calculated. What’s more, we found that most tumor cells and cells in the core region belonged to the high LAGES group, and the main components of the high LAGES group were tumor cells and immune cells (supplementary Fig. 1B). Consistent with our hypothesis, the regulation by lactylation-associated transcriptional programs plays a crucial role in GBM cells.

Exploring the heterogeneity of tumor cells in high and low LAGES groups

To explore the heterogeneity of tumor cells, we obtained differential genes of tumor cells between high and low LAGES groups (Supplementary Fig. 1C). Subsequently, we extracted tumor cells from the high LAGES group and low LAGES group separately and performed subtyping again (Supplementary Fig. 2). The high LAGES group was divided into 4 subgroups (H1, H2, H3, H4) (Fig. 2D), among which the H1 subgroup had the highest LAGES, the H4 subgroup had the lowest, and the H2 and H3 subgroups were similar (Fig. 2E). The low LAGES group was divided into 3 subgroups (L1, L2, L3) (Fig. 2G), among which the L3 subgroup had the lowest LAGES, and the L1 and L2 subgroups were similar (Fig. 2H).

Intragroup analysis of high and low LAGES groups

To facilitate the exploration of characteristics of each LAGES subgroup, we analyzed the up-regulated and down-regulated genes in each group separately. Subsequently, we performed functional analysis on the up-regulated genes in each subgroup (Fig. 2F and I & Supplementary Fig. 3). We found that the H1 subgroup with the highest LAGES was significantly enriched in pathways such as angiogenesis, hypoxic response, positive regulation of canonical NF-κB signal transduction, positive regulation of cell migration, and positive regulation of DNA-templated transcription(Fig. 2F). This suggests that the highly activated state of the lactylation-associated transcriptional program in the H1 subgroup may enable tumor cells to adapt to harsh microenvironments and enhance their invasive capacity by regulating the aforementioned pathways. In contrast, the L3 subgroup with the lowest LAGES (Fig. 2I) was significantly enriched in pathways including glycolytic process via glucose-6-phosphate, positive regulation of T cell activation, positive regulation of T cell proliferation, positive regulation of inflammatory response, and T cell receptor signaling pathway. This indicates that the lowly activated state of the lactylation-associated transcriptional program in the L3 subgroup may reduce metabolic inhibition, provide sufficient metabolic and signaling support for T cell activation, proliferation, and anti-tumor effects, and create favorable conditions for the effective exertion of GBM immune responses.

Based on the functional discrepancies, it can be inferred that LAGES may serve as an indirect and exploratory indicator of the dual status of malignancy and immunity in GBM. The high-LAGES group correlates with a phenotype of high malignancy and immunosuppression: tumors augment their malignant traits by activating pro-invasive and pro-angiogenic pathways, while the hyperactivation of lactylation-associated transcriptional programs may contribute to an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment. These observations raise the hypothesis that such GBM may potentially exhibit poor responses to traditional chemoradiotherapy and a higher propensity for resistance to immunotherapy, although these inferences remain indirect and require experimental validation. In contrast, the low-LAGES group corresponds to a phenotype of low malignant potential and immune activation: tumor metabolism is closer to homeostasis, and T cell activation pathways are robustly activated. This pattern supports the exploratory hypothesis that low-LAGES GBM may be associated with potential sensitivity to immune checkpoint inhibitors and adoptive T cell therapy, a proposition that is highly speculative at present and strictly requires further validation in prospective clinical cohorts.

To confirm that our results are robust to gene set selection, we constructed two alternative gene sets and repeated core analyses (Supplementary Figs. 4 & 5): Set 1 (Non-overlapping Genes): Comprising 317 genes, which was derived by excluding 13 genes overlapping with glycolysis/hypoxia signatures from the original 330-gene set. Set 2 (Lactate-related Genes): Including 28 genes integrated from three lactate-associated gene sets (GOBP_LACTATE_METABOLIC_PROCESS, GOBP_LACTATE_TRANSPORT, and GOMF_LACTATE_TRANSMEMBRANE_TRANSPORTER_ACTIVITY) retrieved from the MSigDB database. Subsequently, for these two alternative gene sets, we performed score construction, functional analysis, and correlation analysis following the identical procedures applied for LAGES. Analysis results based on two alternative gene sets indicated that the core conclusion of this study—that LAGES could characterize lactylation-related metabolic reprogramming and immune-suppressive phenotypes of GBM—was highly robust and independent of the selection of specific LRGs sets.

Correlation between the LAGES and phenotypes

We downloaded phenotypic signature genes for each phenotype from the CancerSEA database and used AddModuleScore to calculate phenotypic scores based on these signature genes. We found that the previously constructed LAGES was positively correlated with the scores of invasion (p = 2.0e-42, r = 0.40) (Fig. 3A), DNA repair (p = 1.5e-29, r = 0.34) (Fig. 3B), and DNA damage (p = 5.0e-23, r = 0.30) (Fig. 3C). Single-cell sequencing also showed that the distribution of phenotypic scores was generally consistent with that of LAGES (Fig. 3D − 3 F). Furthermore, we observed significant differences in the invasion, DNA repair, and DNA damage scores of tumor cells between the high and low LAGES groups, which was consistent with the previous results (Fig. 4G − 4I). Based on this, we speculate that the lactylation-associated transcriptional program is a potential metabolic correlate of enhanced invasiveness in GBM, potentially associated with the malignant progression of tumors.

Cell communication

We extracted immune cells and manually annotated them using canonical markers (Fig. 4A and B). Since macrophages are the most abundant immune cells in the tumor microenvironment and can regulate tumor progression through immunosuppression, angiogenesis, and metabolism, we next focused on cell communication between macrophages and tumor cells. Subsequently, we divided macrophages into high-LAGES macrophages (Macrophages_H) and low-LAGES macrophages (Macrophages_L). By modeling ligand-receptor interactions to investigate intercellular communication between macrophages and tumor cells, we identified 155 interaction pairs and 42 important signaling pathways (Supplementary Tables 2 & 3). We found that compared with the low LAGES group, the number of cell communication pathways in malignant cells of the high LAGES group was significantly increased (Fig. 4C).

By calculating the interaction strength of selected ligand-receptor pairs in different cell types, we inferred a cell state-specific ligand-receptor interaction network (Fig. 4D). It was observed that the SPP1-CD44 interaction had a high interaction score between macrophages and tumor cells. By calculating multiple network-centric metrics for each cell type, it indicated that SPP1 signals were mainly sent by Macrophages_H and received by Neoplastic_H (Fig. 4E and F). We also observed that the SPP1 gene was primarily expressed in immune cells, while CD44 was mainly expressed in tumor cells (Fig. 4G and H).

Most previous studies have shown that increased SPP1 expression induces GBM-associated macrophage infiltration and is associated with poor prognosis in GBM patients. Our results suggest a potential role for the SPP1-CD44 interaction in inducing a more malignant phenotype in high-LAGES tumor cells, though this is an exploratory inference based on cell communication analysis and requires further functional validation to confirm causality (Supplementary Fig. 6).

Construction of lactylation-related prognostic models

To explore LRGs that play a key role in the prognosis of GBM patients, we screened genes meeting the following criteria: highly expressed in TCGA GBM compared with the normal group; highly expressed in tumor cells compared with other cell types; highly expressed in the high LAGES group compared with the low LAGES group; and highly expressed in high LAGES cancer cells compared with low LAGES cancer cells. A total of 95 genes were obtained (Fig. 5A). We integrated 10 machine learning algorithms to generate 101 combined algorithms, which were evaluated using a 10-fold nested cross-validation (NCV) framework to ensure the robustness of model performance and avoid overfitting and data leakage. TCGA-GBM was used as the training set, and CGGA325 and CGGA693 as the independent testing sets, with the testing sets strictly excluded from any iterative model tuning or selection process. The concordance index (C-index) results of the machine learning models are shown in Fig. 5B and C presents the mean C-index of each model in the dataset. Among all the models, the StepCox (backward) + RSF model achieved the highest C-index in the training set, with the model selection performed strictly within the training set under the nested CV framework. Specifically, 45 genes were screened via StepCox (backward) elimination, and these 45 genes were further subjected to RSF analysis, which finally yielded 10 optimally fitted genes. Figure 5D shows the ranking of gene importance for the optimally fitted genes in the StepCox (backward) + RSF model, with G6PC3 having the highest importance.

Establishment of the prognostic model. A The Venn diagram for screening core genes. B A total of 101 kinds of prognostic models. C The C-index of each model across all datasets. D Variable importance in StepCox [backward] + RSF. E Univariate prognostic analysis of factors in TCGA-GBM cohort samples

Finally, Cox regression analysis was employed to weight the expression levels of these 10 genes based on their respective regression coefficients, and the risk score for each patient was calculated accordingly (Fig. 5E); 7 genes were included in the final model. All these 7 genes were significantly associated with survival, among which G6PC3 presented the highest risk (HR = 1.35, P = 0.00094), followed by IGFBP2 (HR = 1.28, P = 0.0024), RCN1 (HR = 1.28, P = 0.0064), TNFRSF12A (HR = 1.23, P = 0.021), NUDT1 (HR = 1.21, P = 0.036), RFC2 (HR = 1.20, P = 0.045) and LGALS3 (HR = 1.18, P = 0.049).

To further quantify the discriminatory ability of the final prognostic risk model, we calculated the C-index with 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs) across the training and independent validation cohorts. The results demonstrated that the model achieved a C-index of 0.603 (95% CI: 0.547–0.659) in the TCGA-GBM training cohort, indicating a moderate but preliminary discriminatory capacity to distinguish between high- and low-risk patients. In the CGGA325 validation cohort (n = 137), the C-index was 0.527 (95% CI: 0.491–0.563), and in the CGGA693 validation cohort (n = 237), the C-index was 0.549 (95% CI: 0.428–0.67). Both CGGA subsets showed modest but acceptable prognostic stratification ability, confirming the model’s preliminary consistent performance across distinct independent external cohorts with similar data characteristics.

Subsequently, we performed Kaplan-Meier (KM) survival analysis and time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (time-ROC) analysis to further validate the prognostic performance of the risk score model across all three cohorts. For the KM survival analysis, patients were stratified into high- and low-risk groups based on the median risk score. The results demonstrated significant differences in overall survival between the high- and low-risk groups in all cohorts (Figs. 6A–C), confirming that the risk score can effectively stratify patients into distinct prognostic subgroups in the current cohorts. Time-ROC analysis was conducted to evaluate the time-dependent predictive accuracy of the model. In the TCGA-GBM training cohort (Fig. 6D), the area under the curve (AUC) values for 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival were 0.65, 0.72, and 0.94, respectively, indicating a trend toward increasingly accurate long-term prognostic prediction in the training set. Furthermore, we validated this model in CGGA325(Fig. 6E) and CGGA693(Fig. 6F). To assess the calibration accuracy of the model, we generated 3-year survival calibration curves using the Kaplan-Meier method (Fig. 6G-I). The curves for all three cohorts closely fitted the ideal diagonal line (slope = 1), indicating that the predicted 3-year survival probabilities were highly consistent with the actual observed survival rates. Specifically, the TCGA-GBM training cohort exhibited the best calibration, while the CGGA325 and CGGA693 validation cohorts also showed satisfactory alignment between predicted and observed probabilities. These findings collectively confirm that the risk score model not only effectively stratifies patients in the current cohorts but also provides preliminary reliable survival probability predictions within the studied datasets. Importantly, given the relatively modest C-index values in the external validation cohorts, the model’s generalizability and potential clinical applicability remain preliminary and require further validation in larger, multi-center, and prospectively collected cohorts before any clinical consideration.

Single-gene analysis of G6PC3

Differential, prognostic, and functional analyses

Given that G6PC3 had the highest importance in the model, we subsequently conducted more in-depth studies on this gene. First, differential expression analysis revealed that G6PC3 expression levels progressively increased with glioma grade (WHO II, III, IV), showing significant differences across grades (Fig. 7A). Additionally, G6PC3 expression was significantly higher in IDH-wildtype gliomas compared to IDH-mutant counterparts (Fig. 7B). Furthermore, based on the 2021 WHO glioma classification (defined by IDH mutation and 1p/19q co-deletion status), we analyzed G6PC3 expression across three key subtypes: G6PC3 was lowest in IDH-mutant, 1p/19q non-codeleted astrocytomas (with significant differences from the other two subtypes), highest in IDH-wildtype glioblastomas, and showed no statistical difference between glioblastomas and IDH-mutant, 1p/19q codeleted oligodendrogliomas (P = 0.552) (Fig. 7C).

A The differences in the expression levels of G6PC3 in different grades of gliomas. B The differences in the expression levels of G6PC3 in different IDH status of gliomas. C The differences in the expression levels of G6PC3 in different 2021 WHO glioma subtypes. D Prognostic analysis of G6PC3 in GBM. E Prognostic analysis of G6PC3 in IDH-mutant gliomas. F Functional enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes between the high and low G6PC3 expression groups. G Univariate prognostic analysis of factors in IDH-wildtype gliomas. H Multivariate analysis of of factors in IDH-wildtype gliomas

Prognostic analyses consistently demonstrated the clinical relevance of G6PC3: in GBM specifically, high G6PC3 expression was associated with poor patient prognosis (Fig. 7D); similarly, in IDH-mutant gliomas, elevated G6PC3 expression correlated with unfavorable outcomes (Fig. 7E). To further validate G6PC3’s prognostic value in IDH-wildtype gliomas (a clinically critical subgroup), we performed univariate and multivariate COX proportional hazards regression analyses. Univariate analysis included nine clinical features (G6PC3, PRS_type, Grade, Gender, Age, Radio_status, Chemo_status, 1p19q_codeletion_status, MGMTp_methylation_status), and results showed that G6PC3 was significantly associated with prognosis (Fig. 7G). We then included variables with P < 0.05 from the univariate analysis in the multivariate model, which confirmed that G6PC3 remained an independent prognostic factor for IDH-wildtype gliomas after adjusting for confounding factors (Fig. 7H).

Functional enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes between high- and low-G6PC3 groups (Fig. 7F) revealed key enriched pathways and biological processes. Notably, high-G6PC3 samples were significantly enriched in G protein-coupled receptor (GPCR) signaling pathways (including phospholipase C-activating GPCR signaling), calcium ion transport, and exocytosis. These statistically supported enrichment results (derived from differential gene expression data) suggest potential associations between G6PC3 and interrelated molecular/cellular processes that may contribute to glioma malignant progression. Specifically, G6PC3 may engage in potential crosstalk with GPCR signaling networks. This crosstalk, along with its links to calcium ion transport and exocytosis, merits further molecular and functional validation. As a hypothesis-generating inference from the statistical enrichment data, calcium ion transport and exocytosis—processes closely linked to GPCR-mediated signaling cascades—may synergistically promote tumor cell invasiveness and pro-tumorigenic factor secretion. This aligns with the observed prognostic and phenotypic correlations of G6PC3.

To further verify the prognostic value of G6PC3 in IDH wild-type gliomas, univariate and multivariate COX proportional hazards regression analyses were conducted. Univariate analysis included 9 clinical characteristics (G6PC3 expression, PRS type, grade, gender, age, radiotherapy status, chemotherapy status, 1p19q co-deletion status, MGMT promoter methylation status). The results showed that G6PC3 was significantly associated with prognosis (Fig. 7G). Subsequently, the variables with P < 0.05 in the univariate analysis were included in the multivariate model(Fig. 7H). The results confirmed that after adjusting for confounding factors, G6PC3 remained an independent prognostic factor for IDH wild-type gliomas.

Phenotypic, immunotherapeutic, and drug sensitivity analyses

We performed correlation analysis between G6PC3 expression and phenotypes in various cancers using the CancerSEA database (Fig. 8A). It was found that G6PC3 expression in GBM was significantly positively correlated with invasion, metastasis, DNA repair, and EMT. This observation supports a potential, preliminary role of G6PC3 in promoting GBM tumorigenesis and progression.

Subsequently, patients were stratified into high- and low-G6PC3 groups. We calculated the Immunophenotype Score (IPS) (Fig. 8B). This score serves as a surrogate indicator for predicting responses to anti-CTLA-4 and anti-PD-1 antibodies. Higher IPS generally indicates a potentially higher response rate. Differences were observed between the IPS-CTLA4(-)/PD-1(+) and IPS-CTLA4(+)/PD-1(+) subgroups. In both subgroups, the high-G6PC3 group exhibited higher IPS related to anti-PD-1 response. This merely points to an exploratory, hypothesis-generating association between high G6PC3 and a potential anti-PD-1 response advantage, with no direct clinical predictive value implied. The correlation, however, remains highly speculative and requires rigorous validation using clinical outcome data from immune checkpoint blockade (ICB)-treated GBM cohorts.

Furthermore, drug sensitivity analysis identified the top 6 drugs with the highest inhibitory effect on G6PC3 expression, namely 5-fluoro deoxy uridine 10mer, XL-147, Bleomycin, Mitomycin, Floxuridine, and Gemcitabine (Fig. 8C & Supplementary Fig. 7).

Immunohistochemistry of G6PC3 expression in gliomas

To verify the expression of the hub gene G6PC3 in gliomas, we performed immunohistochemical analysis of glioma tissue microarrays. The results showed that the expression of G6PC3 gradually increased in Grade 1–4 gliomas (Fig. 9A and B), which was consistent with our previous bioinformatics analysis results. This finding suggests that G6PC3 may play a vital role in the tumorigenesis and progression of gliomas. To validate the prognostic value of G6PC3 in gliomas, we conducted Kaplan-Meier analysis and time-ROC curves based on the glioma tissue microarrays. In 132 glioma samples, a high percentage of G6PC3 + cells was associated with poorer overall survival (OS) (P < 0.001) (Fig. 9C). In the glioma tissue microarrays, the percentage of G6PC3 + cells also accurately predicted glioma OS at 1 month (AUC = 0.63), 2 months (AUC = 0.59), and 3 months (AUC = 0.63) (Fig. 9D).

Functional analysis of G6PC3 in glioma cell lines

To investigate the biological functions of G6PC3 in glioma cells, we established stable knockdown of G6PC3 in U87 and U251 cell lines. Western blot analysis (Supplementary Fig. 10A & 10B) and RT-qPCR (Supplementary Fig. 10C & 10D) confirmed successful knockdown in both cell lines. Full-length, unprocessed original western blot analysis was in Supplementary Figs. 8 & 9 .CCK-8 assay showed that cell viability in the G6PC3 knockdown group was significantly decreased compared with the control group (Fig. 10A and B). We also performed Transwell assay to evaluate the migratory potential of glioma cells, and representative images demonstrated that G6PC3 silencing resulted in impaired migration (Fig. 10C). Quantification of the migration assay revealed that the migratory capacity of U87 and U251 glioma cells was significantly reduced upon G6PC3 knockdown, supporting the notion that G6PC3 is crucial for glioma cell migration (Fig. 10D and E). Taken together, these data demonstrate that knockdown of G6PC3 inhibits glioma cell proliferation and migration, and enhances cell apoptosis, highlighting its potential role as a regulator of glioma cell survival and progression.

Discussion

GBM is a highly malignant primary brain tumor with an extremely poor prognosis, and its treatment options and efficacy remain limited. This therapeutic dilemma underscores the urgent need to unravel GBM’s biological mechanisms and identify key regulators of its malignant behaviors. PTMs are well-recognized as core drivers of tumor progression. As a novel PTM, lactylation has been implicated in the prognosis and treatment of various tumors [7–9], but research on lactylation in GBM remains fragmented—most studies focus on low-grade gliomas or tissue-level analyses, failing to capture lactylation-related cellular heterogeneity in GBM or clarify potential downstream targets or functional correlates of lactylation. To address this gap, the present study explores lactylation-mediated GBM progression at the single-cell level.

Previous lactylation studies in gliomas, while foundational, have notable limitations. Tang et al. focused on hypoxia-lactylation related genes in low-grade gliomas and constructed a prognostic model centered on FABP5 [16], but their conclusions cannot be extrapolated to GBM due to significant differences in molecular and metabolic characteristics. Lu et al. and Tang et al. developed lactylation-related prognostic models (including LAGES) and identified subtypes [17, 18], but none established a direct link between lactylation and the functional regulation of proposed targets.

In contrast, our study addresses these limitations by focusing on GBM with single-cell resolution. We first constructed a single-cell LAGES to quantify individual cell lactylation levels. This enabled accurate analysis of lactylation dynamics across GBM cell subpopulations. Subtyping high and low LAGES groups revealed distinct functional profiles. High LAGES tumor cells exhibit enrichment of pro-invasive/angiogenic pathways, which may potentially be associated with enhanced malignancy, though this inference is based on functional enrichment analysis and requires further validation. Lactic acid accumulation is associated with inhibited immune cell function, an observation that is indirect and does not directly imply clinical implications for immunotherapy. Meanwhile, low LAGES tumors exhibit metabolic homeostasis and active T cell activation. This is a strictly hypothesis-generating observation with no direct predictive value for immunotherapy response, which warrants further validation in large, well-characterized immunotherapy-treated cohorts. Correlation analysis confirmed LAGES is positively associated with GBM malignant phenotypes. This supports lactylation as a potential, preliminary regulatory factor, rather than a confirmed functional regulator. Cell communication analysis identified high interaction scores for the SPP1-CD44 axis between macrophages and tumor cells. This suggests a putative, exploratory association with malignant phenotypes in high-lactylation cells, with no confirmed causal link established. The pattern may be linked to M2 macrophage polarization or enhanced tumor cell adhesion/invasion, though this remains speculative and requires functional experiments to verify. It is a pattern not previously reported in GBM lactylation studies, providing a novel exploratory basis for future mechanistic investigations.

To explore potential research targets rather than directly translate these findings into clinical applications, we developed a prognostic model using ten machine learning techniques, with StepCox [backward] + RSF identified as optimal. G6PC3 emerged as the top-ranked key gene, representing a putative novel association with lactylation in GBM—an unreported relationship in the current literature, despite G6PC3’s known roles in other cancers [19, 20] and GBM glucose metabolism [21, 22]. We further confirmed that G6PC3 expression progressively increases with glioma grade (validated by tissue microarrays), consistent with lactylation-associated transcriptional activity escalating with malignancy [23]. High G6PC3 expression correlates with poor prognosis, unfavorable clinical features (advanced age, IDH wild-type, recurrence), and serves as an independent prognostic factor for IDH-wildtype GBM. Functional enrichment analysis identified potential associations between G6PC3 and GPCR signaling, phospholipase C-activating pathways, exocytosis, and calcium ion transport. These are statistically supported enrichment observations derived from bioinformatics analysis; no direct functional validation of their interaction with G6PC3 was performed in this study. Based on enrichment analysis and prior literature, G6PC3 encodes the endoplasmic reticulum (ER)-localized glucose-6-phosphatase catalytic subunit, which hydrolyzes glucose-6-phosphate into glucose and inorganic phosphate (Pi). As a signaling molecule, Pi inhibits sarcoplasmic/ER calcium ATPase (SERCA), elevating cytoplasmic calcium [24]—a key trigger for exocytosis (via calcium sensor proteins and SNARE complex regulation) [25]. Exocytosis is critical for secreting extracellular matrix-degrading enzymes (e.g., MMPs) [26], and calcium signaling modulates cytoskeletal reorganization associated with tumor migration/invasion—processes aligned with G6PC3’s correlation with GBM invasiveness. Strictly as a hypothesis-generating model derived from combined statistical enrichment data and literature support (not experimentally validated in this study), we propose that high G6PC3 expression in GBM enhances G6P hydrolysis, increases Pi production, inhibits SERCA, elevates cytoplasmic calcium, and promotes exocytosis/tumor-related factor secretion, ultimately augmenting invasiveness—consistent with its association with poor prognosis. Phenotypic correlation analysis confirmed G6PC3 is positively linked to invasion, metastasis, DNA repair, and EMT.

Clinically, high G6PC3 expression may tentatively be associated with a potential, unconfirmed higher response rate to PD-1 inhibitors—a strictly hypothesis-generating observation with no direct clinical predictive utility, which could be linked to glucose metabolism regulation reducing TME lactic acid accumulation [27] or modulating PD-L1 expression, but this proposed mechanism also remains speculative and unvalidated. Notably, this potential predictive value for immunotherapy response remains highly exploratory, indirect, and non-definitive, and requires rigorous validation in large, well-characterized ICB-treated GBM cohorts before any clinical application can be considered. This could serve solely as a hypothesis-generating candidate predictive biomarker for GBM immunotherapy, with no current clinical relevance and strictly pending robust validation in ICB-treated GBM cohorts. Drug sensitivity analysis identified six candidate inhibitors for high G6PC3 GBM. In vitro experiments confirmed G6PC3 knockdown inhibits proliferation/migration and promotes apoptosis. This validates it as a potentially feasible therapeutic target in preclinical settings, providing preliminary preclinical evidence supporting G6PC3 as a feasible therapeutic target—but further in vivo validation (e.g., orthotopic xenograft models) and clinical translation studies are essential, and no clinical therapeutic implications can be inferred at this stage.

This study has limitations. First and foremost, LAGES is an indirect transcriptomic surrogate derived from the co-expression patterns of LRGs, rather than a direct measure of lactylation PTM levels. As such, its biological specificity for lactylation-driven processes in GBM remains inferential—it reflects transcriptional activity associated with lactylation, but not the actual lactylation status of proteins or the direct functional consequences of lactylation modification. This inherent limitation means LAGES cannot fully capture the complexity of lactylation biology, including tissue-specific lactylation dynamics or the functional heterogeneity of lactylated substrates. Second, LAGES is solely based on transcriptomic data, lacking proteomic or metabolomic validation of lactylation activity; this further limits the reliability of inferences regarding lactylation-mediated regulatory mechanisms in GBM. Third, the single-cell sequencing sample size is limited, which may restrict the comprehensiveness of capturing lactylation-related cellular heterogeneity in GBM. Additionally, the prognostic model, despite exhibiting consistent performance across two distinct CGGA subsets (CGGA325 and CGGA693), still faces limitations in generalizability. Potential inter-cohort heterogeneity—such as differences in sample collection protocols, sequencing platforms, or patient demographic and clinical characteristics across independent databases—may constrain its applicability to diverse GBM populations. Future validation in larger, multi-center cohorts with standardized data collection and comprehensive clinical follow-up is required to further enhance the model’s robustness and translational potential. Moreover, the prognostic model lacks validation in patients treated with chemoradiotherapy, which hinders the assessment of its utility in clinically relevant treatment contexts. Fourth, inferences regarding immunotherapy sensitivity in low-LAGES subgroups rely solely on IPS analysis, a surrogate indicator of immunogenicity, rather than direct clinical immunotherapy data or in vitro/in vivo experimental validation. This weakens the reliability of claims about LAGES as a predictive biomarker for immunotherapy response. Finally, direct experimental evidence demonstrating that G6PC3 is a lactylation substrate or a downstream effector of lactylation remains lacking. The potential crosstalk among the SPP1-CD44 axis, G6PC3, and lactylation, as well as G6PC3’s putative link to GPCR pathways, requires further molecular and functional validation to confirm causal relationships.

Despite these limitations, our study provides preliminary, exploratory new insights into GBM immunotherapy and targeted therapy, with no direct clinical implications inferred at this stage. Future research will address these gaps through the following directions: (1) Integrate lactylation proteomics, lactylation-specific mass spectrometry, and site-directed mutagenesis to validate LAGES, identify key lactylated substrates. Assess whether G6PC3 is a direct lactylation target and clarify potential regulatory effects of lactylation on G6PC3; (2) Combine ChIP-seq/ATAC-seq and in vitro/in vivo experiments to explore lactylation-mediated G6PC3 regulation via transcription factor modification and unravel the potential SPP1-CD44-G6PC3-lactylation regulatory network; (3) Integrate clinical datasets of immune checkpoint blockade (ICB)- or chemoradiotherapy-treated GBM patients to explore and tentatively validate LAGES/G6PC3’s prognostic value and hypothetical predictive potential for ICB response, rather than confirm definitive clinical predictive utility; (4) Validate G6PC3’s functional link to calcium transport/exocytosis via calcium imaging, vesicle fusion assays, and MMP secretion detection. The potential mechanistic link between G6PC3 and the GPCR pathway will be clarified through the following experiments: reporter gene assays to detect G6PC3-mediated regulation of transcriptional activity of key GPCR pathway targets; rescue experiments to verify whether GPCR signal activation/inhibition can reverse GBM cell phenotypic changes induced by G6PC3 knockdown/overexpression; co-immunoprecipitation and co-immunofluorescence localization to explore interactions between core components of G6PC3 and the GPCR pathway; and phosphoproteomics analysis to investigate the phosphorylation levels of key proteins in the GPCR signal cascade regulated by G6PC3; (5) Use G6PC3 knockout/overexpression orthotopic xenografts (with lactylation inhibitors) to verify the preclinical efficacy of “lactylation inhibition + G6PC3 targeting” and explore lactylation’s potential role in GBM cell-microenvironment interactions and hypothetical ICB sensitivity, with no definitive conclusions about immunotherapeutic implications pending clinical validation.

Conclusion

In this study, we first constructed a single-cell LAGES to analyze the lactylation heterogeneity among different cell subpopulations in GBM. Using ten machine learning techniques, we developed key gene-based prognostic models. We identified G6PC3 as a key gene associated with lactylation in GBM. This finding suggests a putative, preliminary link between lactylation and metabolic signaling pathways, with no confirmed causal relationship established. Additionally, this study provides preliminary, hypothesis-generating potential biomarkers and candidate drugs for the prognostic evaluation and treatment of GBM, which require further preclinical and clinical validation before any practical application can be considered. These findings not only deepen the preliminary understanding of the potential role of lactylation modification in GBM, but also lay a foundational, exploratory basis for subsequent translational research, rather than implying immediate translational applicability.
