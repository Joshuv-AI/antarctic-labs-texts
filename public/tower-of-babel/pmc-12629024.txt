Uncovering oral and maxillofacial clues in congenital insensitivity to pain with anhidrosis: what can sibling cases teach us?
Authors: Katibe Tugce Temur
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCongenital Insensitivity to Pain with Anhidrosis (CIPA) is an extremely rare congenital disorder characterized by severe clinical and oral manifestations. The aim of this case report is to present the dental and systemic findings in two adult siblings diagnosed with CIPA, highlighting their shared genetic condition yet differing oral manifestations. This report is notable for the patients’ advanced age and provides comparative insight into variations in dentition, periodontal status, and cooperation during dental care. These features highlight the importance of long-term follow-up in CIPA patients by shedding light on the oral and systemic changes that may emerge with advancing age.Case presentationA 20-year-old male and his 22-year-old sister, both diagnosed with CIPA, presented to the Faculty of Dentistry at Niğde Ömer Halisdemir University for dental examination. The male patient showed plaque and calculus buildup, moderate gingival inflammation with bleeding, widespread staining from past iron use, and a tongue scar likely due to self-injury. He had a dolichofacial profile and missing mandibular central incisor (#31). Despite no previous dental treatment, he cooperated well and full-mouth scaling was completed. The female patient had more severe oral issues, including heavy plaque and calculus, generalized gingival recession, poor gum health, and several missing teeth. She had a brachycephalic facial pattern and restricted mouth opening due to scarring. Radiographs showed missing upper and lower central incisors (#21 and #31). Due to limited cooperation, only anterior scaling was done, and further treatment was advised under sedation or general anesthesia. Systemically, both siblings had typical CIPA findings: insensitivity to pain and temperature, recurrent high fever due to lack of sweating, joint deformities, and fingertip amputations. They also had moderate-to-severe intellectual disability, were unable to walk independently, and needed full-time caregiver support.ConclusionsThis case emphasizes the significance of oral and systemic alterations that can manifest in CIPA patients during adulthood. The presence of notable dental anomalies in the absence of multiple carious lesions, oral ulcerations, or missing permanent teeth distinguishes these adult cases from most CIPA reports typically documented in childhood. Additionally, a certain level of treatment compliance particularly in the male patient further emphasizes the uniqueness of this report. The findings underscore the necessity for long-term monitoring and highlight the importance of individualized dental care and follow-up strategies in managing CIPA patients.

Introduction

Congenital Insensitivity to Pain with Anhidrosis (CIPA) is a rare disorder first described by Dearborn in 1932 [2]. Also known as Hereditary Sensory and Autonomic Neuropathy Type IV (HSAN Type IV), CIPA is an extremely rare congenital condition characterized by the absence of pain perception and anhidrosis, inherited in an autosomal recessive pattern [1, 2]. Clinically, it is defined by three major features: insensitivity to pain, anhidrosis, and intellectual disability. It is estimated to occur in approximately one in 125 million live births [3–5]. Mutations in the Neurotrophic Tyrosine Kinase Receptor Type 1 (NTRK1) gene are implicated in its pathogenesis. The NTRK1 gene encodes a receptor critical for the survival of nerve cells by interacting with the Nerve Growth Factor beta (NGFβ) protein. Mutations in this gene result in a nonfunctional receptor protein, leading to disrupted signal transduction. The absence of pain perception is attributed to the lack of functional afferent neurons, whereas anhidrosis results from dysfunction of eccrine sweat glands innervated by sympathetic neurons [6].

The lack of pain sensation in CIPA patients can lead to various orthopedic complications such as recurrent fractures, osteomyelitis, and neuropathic joint deformities. In particular, Charcot joints represent a severe form of joint deformity secondary to nerve damage [7, 8]. Cutaneous manifestations are also common and are typically associated with dehydration, repetitive trauma, and self-mutilating behaviors. Clinically, patients may present with xerosis, palmoplantar hyperkeratosis, skin infections, ulcerations, scarring, and fissures [4, 8].

Oral manifestations constitute an important aspect of the disease. Due to increased dryness around the mouth, scarring of the lips may develop, potentially leading to restricted mouth opening [8, 9]. Other reported complications include mandibular osteolysis [10], changes in facial morphology [11], osteomyelitis, soft tissue trauma, missing teeth, and dental caries [12].

However, information regarding the characteristics of oral findings and the dental management of patients with CIPA remains limited and is largely based on pediatric cases. This case report aims to identify the dental treatment needs of a patient diagnosed with CIPA and to discuss potential oral findings in light of the current literature.

Case presentation and comparative analysis of two siblings diagnosed with CIPA

Ethical considerations and informed consent

Two siblings (a 20-year-old male and a 22-year-old female) with CIPA and cognitive impairment were referred to the Department of Oral and Maxillofacial Radiology, Niğde Ömer Halisdemir University, Faculty of Dentistry. Owing to their limited decision-making capacity, they were unable to provide informed consent independently. Due to cognitive limitations and the absence of literacy skills, the patients were unable to directly participate in the study; therefore, written informed consent for clinical procedures, study participation, and the publication of anonymized data and images was obtained from their legal guardians. The study was conducted in compliance with institutional guidelines and the Declaration of Helsinki.

Patient history

Both patients were diagnosed with CIPA in infancy at the early age of 2 months for the male patient and 9 months for the female. According to family report, genetic testing had been performed during infancy, supporting the diagnosis, although the detailed results were not available in the medical records. Both patients also exhibited intellectual disability, a common comorbidity of CIPA, and no additional systemic diseases were identified.

Family history

The patients’ family history revealed a fourth-degree consanguineous marriage between their parents. Among four siblings, these two were diagnosed with CIPA. While the male patient had no prior history of dental treatment, the female had previously undergone restorative and extraction procedures under general anesthesia during orthopedic surgery for a hip fracture. Both patients are unable to maintain independent oral hygiene due to their intellectual and physical limitations; however, their caregivers actively assist with daily oral care.

Systemic findings

Both siblings exhibited hallmark features of CIPA, including a profound insensitivity to pain and temperature, which became apparent during early childhood through self-injurious behavior. Recurrent episodes of unexplained hyperthermia were reported in both patients due to absence of sweating. Physical examination revealed similar musculoskeletal findings, including joint deformities in the knees, feet, and hands, hyperkeratotic skin lesions, and fingertip amputations. Both individuals had lost their ability to walk independently and required full-time assistance for mobility. These findings are illustrated in Fig. 1.

Both patients had received oral iron supplementation in childhood due to iron deficiency anemia, and the resulting extrinsic staining was more notable in the male patient. At the time of presentation, both were taking only cetirizine (Cedrina® 50 mg) once daily. No additional systemic medications were reported.

Cognitive status

Both patients exhibited moderate to severe intellectual disability, as indicated by family reports and clinical observations. Family members stated that neither sibling acquired literacy skills despite several years of formal education. In the absence of standardized neurocognitive or developmental assessments in the medical records, their cognitive status was characterized through family history and clinical impressions, which revealed marked difficulties in communication, cooperation during dental procedures, and oral hygiene maintenance.

Orofacial findings

Both patients were diagnosed with CIPA in infancy at 2 months for the male patient and 9 months for the female. According to family report, some dental interventions, such as restorations and extractions under general anesthesia, had been performed at earlier ages; however, the exact timing of these procedures could not be confirmed.

Intraoral examination for the present case report was conducted when the patients attended our clinic at 20 and 22 years of age, respectively. This timing is relevant because oral and periodontal complications in CIPA generally accumulate with disease progression, impaired pain perception, and difficulties in maintaining adequate oral hygiene.

The male patient exhibited gingival bleeding, dental plaque and calculus accumulation, and extrinsic dental staining. Scar tissue was observed on the lateral border of the tongue, likely resulting from repeated self-inflicted trauma. He presented with a dolichofacial pattern.

The female patient showed more advanced oral findings, including poor gum health, gingival recession, and multiple missing teeth. Her facial morphology was brachycephalic, characterized by a short and wide face. No ulcerative lesions were detected in the oral mucosa of either patient. Although both had poor oral hygiene, the periodontal status of the female patient was markedly more severe, consistent with the age-related progression of the condition. Radiological findings

Panoramic radiography in both patients revealed multiple anomalies. In the male patient, hypodontia was observed with congenital absence of the mandibular central incisor (#31). The female patient exhibited a more pronounced form of hypodontia, with congenital absence of both the maxillary and mandibular central incisors (#21 and #31). In addition, restorations were observed in teeth #16 and #24, and only the root of tooth #36 was retained.

Both patients showed malpositioned permanent teeth consistent with clinical malocclusion. No impacted teeth were noted. The maxillofacial skeletal structures appeared within normal anatomical limits, and no pathological lesions were detected in either the maxilla or mandible. In the female patient, lip constriction and limited mouth opening due to scar tissue formation were also observed and correlated with clinical and radiographic findings. Clinical referral and treatment planning

The male patient’s primary complaint was malocclusion, for which he was referred to the Department of Orthodontics. However, due to limited cooperation and poor hand-foot coordination related to his neurological condition, he was deemed unsuitable for orthodontic treatment. Clinical examination revealed extensive plaque accumulation, widespread extrinsic staining, and generalized gingival bleeding. A professional dental scaling (detertrage) was planned and successfully completed. Despite no prior dental treatment experience, the male patient cooperated well during the procedure. In addition to scaling, preventive strategies such as regular topical fluoride application, the use of desensitizing agents, and behavior management approaches were recommended to support long-term oral health. He was advised to maintain daily oral hygiene with caregiver assistance and scheduled for a follow-up two weeks later. The post-treatment intraoral view is presented in Fig. 6.

The female patient also presented with malocclusion. Clinical findings included generalized plaque and calculus accumulation, gingival bleeding, marked gingival recession, and multiple missing teeth. Radiographic assessment confirmed the malocclusion. According to her family, she had previously undergone dental treatments under general anesthesia. During the current appointment, however, her cooperation was severely limited. As a result, only a short session was possible, and the dental scaling was restricted to the anterior region. Given her advanced periodontal condition and poor cooperation, it was recommended that future dental treatments be performed under sedation or general anesthesia to ensure safe and effective care. Preventive measures, including fluoride therapy and caregiver-assisted oral hygiene, were emphasized to reduce further disease progression. Caregivers were counseled on the importance of balancing potential risks, such as trauma resulting from insensitivity to pain, with the benefits of interventions like orthodontic or periodontal treatment. Finally, a multidisciplinary management plan involving dental professionals, neurologists, orthopedic specialists, and psychologists was proposed to address both the oral and systemic challenges of patients with CIPA comprehensively.

Discussion

CIPA is a rare hereditary sensory and autonomic neuropathy that usually manifests during infancy or early childhood and leads to lifelong complications, including self-injurious behaviors, recurrent hyperthermia, and progressive musculoskeletal deformities. Considering the case reports and case series presented in the existing literature, the reported age range of patients diagnosed with CIPA varies between 16 months and 19 years, and cases have been described in both male and female pediatric patients [13, 15, 17–19]. The present report is distinctive because it describes two adult siblings (20-year-old male and 22-year-old female) diagnosed during infancy, both exhibiting intellectual disability and severe systemic and oral complications. Such prolonged follow-up into adulthood has rarely been documented in the literature [22], and long-term survival with such extensive systemic sequelae is rarely detailed in published case series.

CIPA is typically diagnosed during infancy or early childhood, coinciding with the onset of the first symptoms. The absence of pain response, recurrent hyperthermic episodes, self-mutilating behaviors, and genetic test results play critical roles in establishing the diagnosis [13–19]. In both cases, the family reported that the diagnosis was made during early childhood. In both cases, the family reported that the diagnosis was made during early childhood, consistent with previous studies.

The genetic background of CIPA is well established. Guven et al. reported two Turkish siblings with the same mutation, emphasizing the autosomal recessive inheritance and the importance of genetic counseling in consanguineous families [16]. Similarly, both of our patients were born to consanguineous parents, and one sibling was affected, further supporting this inheritance mechanism. Comparable findings of familial clustering have been documented in other cohorts [20].

CIPA is a multisystemic disorder, with the hallmark feature being a complete absence of pain sensation [13, 14, 17, 18]. Sweating abnormalities such as anhidrosis or hypohidrosis are also common, leading to recurrent hyperthermic episodes [13, 14, 17–19]. Many patients show delayed cognitive development and intellectual disability, which complicates compliance with medical and dental treatments [13, 14, 18, 19]. The absence of pain perception leads to persistent self-mutilating behaviors, particularly involving the lips, tongue, cheeks, and fingers, resulting in soft tissue loss and scarring [13–15, 17–19]. Unnoticed trauma may progress to osteomyelitis and pathological fractures [19]. Ocular complications such as corneal ulceration and keratitis, secondary to absent corneal reflexes, have also been reported [19]. Neves et al. reported extensive traumatic lesions in pediatric patients with CIPA, showing that repeated unnoticed trauma could result in severe oral and soft tissue destruction [21]. In contrast, although both of our cases showed oral soft tissue damage, the extent of mutilation was less severe than that observed in Neves’ pediatric cohort, which may suggest age-related adaptation or protective behaviors acquired over time. Similarly, Pérez-López et al. emphasized that recurrent trauma and thermoregulatory dysfunction may cause progressive musculoskeletal disability and even wheelchair dependence [22]. This observation aligns with our male patient, who lost ambulatory function, whereas the female sibling remained partially mobile, highlighting phenotypic variability even within the same family.

Oral manifestations are among the most consistent findings in CIPA. Traumatic ulcers, scar tissue, and soft tissue losses involving the tongue, lips, buccal mucosa, and cheeks are frequently described [13, 14, 17–19]. Oral features include tongue tip loss, frictional keratosis, atrophic glossitis, and decubital ulcers [14, 15, 17, 19]. Chronic biting of the tongue or cheeks has been associated with spontaneous tooth loss, devitalized pulps, advanced mobility, and periapical pathology [18]. Paduano et al. described additional orthodontic challenges in CIPA patients, such as limited mouth opening and compromised compliance, making orthodontic treatment difficult [23]. This corresponds to our findings, as both patients were referred to orthodontics for malocclusion but treatment was deferred due to poor neuromotor coordination. In our patients, scar tissue on the tongue (male) and gingival problems (female) were consistent with previous reports. Interestingly, neither patient exhibited dental caries or tooth wear, which differs from many studies reporting hypomineralization and dentin defects predisposing to wear and decay [12]. This highlights the phenotypic variability of oral involvement in CIPA.

Radiographically, common findings in CIPA patients include tooth agenesis, premature tooth loss, alveolar bone thinning, impacted teeth, and abnormal root morphologies [15, 18]. These changes are typically attributed to repeated trauma, chronic inflammation secondary to unnoticed injuries, or developmental anomalies linked to the underlying genetic defect. In our cases, panoramic radiography revealed hypodontia and malpositioned permanent teeth, but no impacted teeth or pathological lesions were identified. Moreover, the maxillofacial bone structures appeared within normal anatomical limits. This relative preservation differs from prior reports in which progressive alveolar bone resorption and condylar alterations were observed [15, 18, 22]. The absence of such severe bone pathology in our patients may be explained by reduced masticatory activity or protective behaviors, rather than an absence of disease activity. The relative preservation of maxillofacial structures in the present siblings may suggest a degree of phenotypic variability, where some patients develop significant skeletal alterations while others maintain near-normal anatomy despite severe systemic manifestations. It is also possible that the absence of extensive bone pathology in these patients reflects reduced masticatory load due to early tooth loss or self-protective behavioral adaptations, as suggested in previous case series [18, 22]. Taken together, these observations indicate that radiological findings in CIPA are heterogeneous, and skeletal involvement may not always parallel the severity of systemic and oral soft tissue complications.

The management of patients with CIPA poses significant challenges for dental practitioners due to cognitive impairment, poor cooperation, and the constant risk of trauma. Treatment approaches must be carefully tailored, taking into account the patient’s level of cooperation, mental status, and behavioral patterns. Preventive and restorative strategies may include fluoride applications, composite restorations, stainless steel crowns, extractions when necessary, and in more severe cases, comprehensive dental rehabilitation under general anesthesia [13, 14, 18]. Custom-made protective oral appliances have been employed to prevent self-inflicted traumatic injuries, although compliance is often limited and influenced by age and cognitive status [19]. In the present cases, the male patient exhibited widespread dental staining, significant calculus accumulation, and poor oral hygiene but no carious lesions, while the female patient also demonstrated poor oral hygiene with gingival recession and bleeding, along with several restored teeth. Interestingly, despite the predisposition of many CIPA patients to caries and tooth wear due to enamel and dentin alterations, neither sibling exhibited such findings, highlighting the phenotypic variability of oral manifestations in this syndrome. Professional scaling was successfully performed in the male patient; however, the procedure could not be carried out for the female due to limited cooperation. Both siblings were referred to orthodontics because of malocclusion, but treatment was not considered appropriate owing to impaired neuromotor coordination. These findings underline the importance of individualized treatment planning and reinforce prior evidence suggesting that caregiver education, preventive protocols, and, when needed, sedation or general anesthesia are essential to achieve safe outcomes in this patient group. A multidisciplinary approach involving dentistry, neurology, psychiatry, and physical therapy remains critical to optimizing long-term outcomes. It should be noted that these patients were not referred to our clinic during childhood but presented only at the ages of 20 and 22. Consequently, the dental examinations reflect the cumulative oral and maxillofacial manifestations of CIPA in early adulthood rather than during childhood, which represents a limitation of the present report.

Conclusion

This case highlights the importance of recognizing oral and systemic changes that may emerge in CIPA patients during adulthood. The documentation of two siblings in the permanent dentition phase, with preserved dentition and the absence of expected complications such as dental caries and severe oral ulcerations despite significant anomalies, distinguishes this report from most previous studies that predominantly focus on pediatric patients. These findings underscore the need for close, long-term monitoring of individuals with CIPA and emphasize that management strategies must be individualized according to the wide clinical heterogeneity of the disease. Furthermore, this report contributes to the limited body of evidence on adult CIPA patients, underlining the necessity of multidisciplinary approaches and tailored preventive care to improve quality of life and long-term outcomes.
