SegFormer-based segmentation approach for landscape planning and overhead remote sensing image analysis
Authors: Sha Liao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study developed a semantic segmentation framework for landscape element analysis. The objective was to achieve high precision, structural stability, and computational efficiency in spatial information extraction. The framework was built on a Transformer architecture and adopted SegFormer as the core modeling paradigm. A Mix Transformer (MiT) encoder generated multi-scale spatial feature representations, while a Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) decoder performed cross-layer semantic fusion. At the architectural level, an Edge-aware Auxiliary Supervision mechanism was introduced to strengthen structural learning. Gradient information served as an explicit constraint, improving discrimination of boundary-sensitive elements such as roads and water bodies. At the training level, a Unified Label Mapping strategy and a Boundary Jitter Augmentation strategy were implemented. These strategies enhanced generalization stability across multi-source landscape datasets. To eliminate performance bias arising from distributional differences and inconsistent training settings, a Two-track Fair Comparison Protocol was designed. This protocol enforced unified training conditions while maintaining independent evaluation. Experiments were conducted on the Land-Cover in High-Resolution Visual Dataset (LoveDA) and a self-constructed landscape dataset. Under the unified LoveDA setting, the model achieved a Mean Intersection over Union (mIoU) of 76.5% across five key landscape categories. This result represents a 5.3% improvement over DeepLabV3+. For the Boundary F1-score, structurally sensitive categories showed clear advantages, confirming the effectiveness of structural supervision. During the inference stage, the edge auxiliary branch used in training was removed. Under a unified setting of a 512 × 512 input and a batch size of 1, the deployed model achieved 42.1 Frames Per Second (FPS), indicating that the structural gains were not obtained at the cost of a significant loss in inference efficiency. Overall, the performance improvements were primarily driven by the interaction between the structure-adaptive workflow and planning-oriented evaluation metrics. Network scaling alone did not account for the observed gains. The proposed framework offers a reproducible and engineering-feasible solution for structured spatial representation in landscape planning, remote sensing interpretation, and geospatial information analysis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-54946-7.

Introduction

With the continuous advancement of urban expansion and spatial renewal, the demand for fine-grained identification, structural representation, and rapid assessment of landscape spaces has increased accordingly1,2. Urban green spaces, waterfront areas, road networks, and ecological corridors not only shape spatial morphology but also influence ecological connectivity and functional configuration. Traditional landscape planning has primarily relied on manual surveys, static maps, and fragmented spatial interpretation, which face limitations in processing efficiency and representational accuracy under the rapid growth of multi-scale and multi-source spatial information3. Remote sensing imagery and UAV data have provided a more stable data foundation for landscape pattern recognition, green space extraction, and spatial structure monitoring4,5. With the integration of deep learning methods into remote sensing interpretation, pixel-level classification performance in complex scenes has been significantly improved; however, challenges such as blurred boundaries, difficulty in recognizing small-scale objects, and insufficient cross-regional structural consistency still persist6,7. In landscape scenarios that simultaneously involve natural and artificial elements, these issues are further exacerbated, directly affecting subsequent connectivity analysis, interface identification, and the quality of spatial interpretation8.

To address the growing demand for fine-grained spatial understanding in landscape planning, this study does not aim to introduce an entirely new segmentation architecture. Instead, it adopts a task-driven architectural adaptation strategy that systematically integrates the structural characteristics of SegFormer with the domain-specific requirements of landscape scenes. Specifically, this study focuses on three critical issues that remain insufficiently addressed in existing semantic segmentation research for landscape planning. These include preserving boundary continuity among complex landscape elements such as roads, water bodies, and green spaces; maintaining spatial connectivity and topological consistency between landscape patches; and improving the interpretability and practical usability of segmentation results for downstream planning analysis.

By combining multi-scale Transformer representations with edge-aware auxiliary supervision and unified label normalization, this study develops a SegFormer-based framework tailored to landscape semantic segmentation tasks. Experimental results on public remote sensing datasets and a custom urban landscape dataset demonstrate that the proposed adaptation strategy improves segmentation reliability in boundary-sensitive and structurally complex scenarios. These improvements support practical applications such as planning map generation, green space analysis, and landscape visualization.

To resolve the mismatch between generic semantic segmentation objectives and the specific needs of landscape planning, this study developed a landscape-oriented framework for structural adaptation and evaluation. The focus was not on designing a new network architecture, but on aligning segmentation optimization with planning-oriented structural requirements. The novelty of this study lies not in architectural innovation, but in incorporating landscape structural logic as an explicit adaptation constraint. Literature review

With the rapid growth of high-resolution remote sensing imagery and urban geospatial data, landscape planning has become increasingly dependent on automated land-cover recognition, spatial structure representation, and visual analysis. Wang et al. (2021) introduced the LoveDA dataset to advance cross-domain land-cover semantic segmentation in urban and rural settings. The dataset covers multiple cities and diverse scenes, and it presents imbalanced class distributions. These characteristics enable systematic evaluation of model robustness across multi-scale targets and complex backgrounds9.

In the evolution of semantic segmentation models, Chen et al. (2018) proposed DeepLabv3+, which combined atrous convolution with an encoder–decoder structure to enhance multi-scale contextual modeling. It has become a strong baseline in remote sensing segmentation. The model performs well in maintaining semantic consistency over large regions. However, it often produces discontinuities or over-smoothing in slender structures and boundary-ambiguous areas10. Sun et al. (2019) introduced HRNet, which employs parallel high-resolution feature streams throughout the network. This design improves boundary detail preservation and small-object localization, making it suitable for fine-contour extraction tasks11. Dosovitskiy et al. (2020) proposed Vision Transformer (ViT), which incorporated self-attention into visual representation learning. This mechanism captures long-range dependencies and provides a natural structural basis for cross-regional semantic consistency in remote sensing imagery12.

Within Transformer-based segmentation, Cheng et al. (2022) proposed Mask2Former as a unified segmentation framework. It adopts a masked-attention mechanism to enable cross-task modeling. In semantic segmentation scenarios, it serves as a representative contemporary Transformer baseline and strengthens the relevance and persuasiveness of comparative experiments13.

Recent research has increasingly focused on prediction uncertainty and boundary ambiguity in remote sensing segmentation. To address boundary confusion caused by complex backgrounds and texture variability, He et al. (2023) proposed UANet, an uncertainty-aware network for building extraction. The model explicitly learns pixel-level uncertainty and enhances boundary stability, thereby reducing misclassification and omission in complex environments14. Building on this idea, PUGNet introduces hierarchical uncertainty modeling. It separately represents background, foreground, and contextual uncertainty. This design improves robustness in binary and fine-structure segmentation tasks on high-resolution imagery15. Uncertainty-centered approaches highlight the inherent stability limits of segmentation models when handling ambiguous boundaries and fine-grained targets. They also provide theoretical guidance for improving structural coherence and boundary integrity.

Although remote sensing semantic segmentation has matured in land-cover mapping and single-object extraction, a persistent mismatch remains between evaluation metrics and landscape planning objectives. Many studies emphasize pixel-level accuracy but lack explicit modeling of planning-sensitive structural goals, such as boundary completeness, road–green space relationships, and connectivity preservation. Meanwhile, research on boundary ambiguity and structural uncertainty continues to advance. These studies emphasize uncertainty modeling to enhance boundary reliability and offer new directions for risk-sensitive analysis in landscape planning. Such perspectives warrant further integration and discussion in subsequent research and comparative evaluation.

Research model

SegFormer model architecture

SegFormer addresses scene segmentation by combining a hierarchical Mix Transformer (MiT) encoder with a lightweight MLP-based decoder to achieve efficient multi-scale feature representation. The MiT encoder consists of four stages and incorporates overlapping convolutional embeddings together with a spatial reduction attention (SRA) mechanism. This design preserves spatial continuity while controlling computational complexity, which is particularly beneficial for irregular scene objects and ambiguous boundaries16. The decoder replaces conventional convolutional upsampling with linear projection, resolution alignment, and MLP-based prediction, enabling multi-level feature fusion with low computational overhead17. SegFormer further removes explicit positional encoding and instead relies on convolutional embeddings to capture spatial information. Bilinear upsampling with edge smoothing is applied to align predictions with ground-truth labels, thereby supporting subsequent patch-based spatial analysis18.

Feature modeling requirements in landscape planning tasks

Landscape planning tasks involve structural constraints that differ fundamentally from those of conventional segmentation problems. Unlike generic vision datasets, landscape scenes are dominated by linear structures, boundary transitions, and spatial continuity relationships. Pixel-level optimization alone cannot adequately capture these characteristics. Even minor boundary deviations may produce disproportionate planning errors.

To address this structural sensitivity, the proposed framework shifts the learning objective from region-based classification to structure-preserving segmentation. This shift leads to three core design principles: boundary awareness implemented through edge-aware auxiliary supervision; scale consistency achieved via scale-aware feature fusion weighting; and semantic stability ensured through unified label mapping.

In landscape planning, semantic segmentation extends beyond a simple classification task and serves as a means of structurally interpreting spatial patterns19,20. Unlike general scene understanding, landscape elements such as roads, green spaces, and water bodies are defined by spatial continuity, boundary regularity, and topological relationships21,22. Consequently, the primary modeling objective is not only to assign correct semantic labels at the pixel level but also to preserve structural integrity across a broader spatial context.

From a planning perspective, three structural properties are particularly important. The first is boundary continuity between adjacent landscape elements, such as the interfaces between roads and green spaces or between water bodies and land23. The second is the spatial connectivity of linear or corridor-like features. The third is scale consistency among different landscape patches. Together, these properties determine the functional usability of segmentation results in downstream planning analyses, including connectivity assessment, buffer delineation, and visual corridor evaluation.

Landscape scenes encompass a broad semantic range, commonly involving categories such as buildings, vegetation, water bodies, roads, and farmland. SegFormer adaptation for landscape semantic segmentation tasks

The structure-adaptive strategy is grounded in landscape spatial logic rather than architectural novelty. Landscape elements such as roads, waterfronts, and green corridors exhibit elongated geometries and high boundary-to-area ratios. These characteristics make them particularly vulnerable to segmentation fragmentation.

Accordingly, the decoder optimization scheme incorporates three targeted measures. Edge-aware supervision is applied to stabilize boundary gradients. Scale-aware weighting is introduced to mitigate suppression of small targets. Boundary jitter augmentation is implemented to enhance structural robustness. This design explicitly aligns network optimization with the intrinsic structural properties of landscape elements.

Experimental design and performance evaluation

Datasets collection

This study employed two datasets for model training and evaluation: the public remote sensing semantic segmentation dataset LoveDA and a self-constructed urban landscape dataset. The two datasets were complementary in terms of spatial scale, structural complexity of land-cover elements, and semantic focus. This complementarity provided an experimental basis for evaluating the structural adaptation capability of the proposed model in landscape planning scenarios.

The LoveDA dataset, released by Wuhan University, contained 5,983 high-resolution remote sensing images (1,024 × 1,024 pixels), covering diverse scenarios including urban, suburban, and rural environments. The dataset provided pixel-level semantic annotations for multiple land-cover categories, such as buildings, roads, and water bodies. To ensure experimental consistency, all images were uniformly resampled to 512 × 512 resolution and normalized to match the model input specifications.

The self-constructed urban landscape dataset was designed to capture fine-grained spatial structures commonly encountered in landscape planning contexts. It comprised 1,248 RGB images, of which 72% were acquired via UAV aerial photography and 28% from high-resolution optical remote sensing platforms. The ground sampling distance (GSD) ranged from 0.08 m/px to 0.5 m/px, enabling simultaneous representation of fine-scale urban elements and broader spatial patterns. All images underwent orthorectification and were converted to a unified RGB color space before being cropped into 512 × 512 image patches.

A random partitioning strategy was adopted, resulting in 874, 187, and 187 images for the training, validation, and test sets, respectively. No spatial overlap existed among subsets to prevent potential data leakage. Semantic annotation was conducted using the Labelme tool under a pixel-level fine-grained labeling protocol. Each image was independently annotated by two trained annotators. Discrepancies were resolved through cross-review and arbitration procedures to ensure labeling consistency. The category system comprised eight landscape semantic classes. A category mapping strategy was implemented to maintain compatibility with the semantic taxonomy of LoveDA.

Compared with LoveDA, which emphasized cross-domain land-cover representation, the self-constructed dataset placed greater focus on urban landscape structural expression and boundary complexity. The integration of these two datasets facilitated a systematic evaluation of model performance in both general remote sensing scenarios and planning-oriented structural contexts.

The experiments were conducted on a system running Ubuntu 22.04, equipped with an Nvidia RTX 4090 GPU (24 GB VRAM), an Intel i9 multi-core processor, and 128 GB of RAM. The deep learning framework was based on PyTorch 2.1 with CUDA 12.1, supporting efficient multi-GPU training for SegFormer. A customized version of MMsegmentation (OpenMMLab 1.x) was used, incorporating label mapping, auxiliary edge supervision, and multi-scale fusion enhancements.

Parameters setting

All training images were resized to 512 × 512 pixels to match SegFormer’s multi-scale feature extraction architecture, ensuring a balance between GPU memory efficiency and spatial context retention. The model was trained using the AdamW optimizer with a base learning rate of 6 × 10⁻⁵, weight decay of 0.01, and a batch size of 8. Training was conducted over 160 epochs, with the first 5 epochs employing a linear warm-up strategy to reduce gradient instability during the initial phase.

The construction of the overall loss function follows the principles described in Sect. 3.2 and 3.3. It combines the primary segmentation loss (cross-entropy loss plus Dice loss) with an auxiliary edge-aware loss. The weight of the primary loss is set to 0.8, while the edge loss is assigned a weight of 0.2. To improve training stability, only the primary loss is applied during the first 20 epochs, after which the edge loss is gradually introduced. In addition, DropPath regularization is employed with a ratio of 0.1. Early stopping is triggered if the validation mIoU shows no improvement for 20 consecutive epochs.

For all comparison models in this study, ImageNet-pretrained weights are used for initialization when official pretrained models are available. No additional domain-specific pretraining or dataset-specific fine-tuning is performed prior to the main training phase. This design aims to avoid performance bias introduced by pretraining and to ensure that the observed differences primarily reflect architectural characteristics and task adaptability. Across all comparative experiments, the input resolution, batch size, learning rate schedule, and data augmentation strategies are kept consistent. For baseline methods, no model-specific tuning is applied beyond the configurations recommended by their original implementations. This standardized setup minimizes confounding factors introduced by training strategies and ensures that performance differences mainly arise from model architecture rather than optimization advantages.

Performance evaluation

To eliminate comparison bias arising from discrepancies in training data distribution, input resolution, and pretraining sources, a Fair Comparison Protocol was established based on a Two-track Evaluation framework. Track A (LoveDA-only): All models were trained and evaluated on the identical LoveDA training/validation/test split. The input resolution was uniformly set to 512 × 512. All models were initialized with ImageNet-1 K pretrained weights. The optimizer configuration, learning rate scheduler, number of training epochs, and data augmentation strategies were strictly standardized across models. This track ensured a controlled, in-distribution comparison in which architectural differences constituted the sole experimental variable. Track B (Custom-only): All models were independently trained and evaluated on the same partition of the self-constructed dataset. The training configuration remained fully consistent with Track A. This track was designed to assess cross-distribution generalization performance under planning-oriented landscape scenarios while maintaining experimental parity. Unless otherwise specified, all comparative conclusions were drawn strictly within the same evaluation track to prevent the misattribution of performance gains derived from additional training data to architectural improvements. The primary performance claims reported in the main text were exclusively based on Track A (LoveDA-only) in-distribution comparisons.

In addition to conventional CNN-based baselines (UNet, DeepLabV3+, and HRNet), two Transformer-oriented baseline models are incorporated to reflect current state-of-the-art trends. First, Mask2Former is included as a general-purpose Transformer-based segmentation framework that integrates mask attention mechanisms with multi-scale feature decoding. Second, multiple variants of SegFormer (B1, B2, and B3) are compared to analyze the sensitivity of model performance to architectural scale. This comparison supports a controlled assessment of whether the observed performance gains stem from task-driven adaptation strategies or merely from increased model capacity. The detailed results are reported in Tables 2 and 3.

To further strengthen the competitiveness and contemporaneity of the comparative analysis, the evaluation framework incorporated SegNeXt as an additional state-of-the-art baseline. SegNeXt represents recent advances in convolutional segmentation architectures, emphasizing multi-scale feature aggregation and efficient spatial modeling. Unlike earlier CNN-based designs, it adopts modern architectural principles that enhance representational capacity while maintaining computational efficiency.

The inclusion of SegNeXt served two methodological purposes. First, it provided a strong, contemporary CNN-oriented reference, ensuring that performance comparisons were not restricted to earlier convolutional baselines. Second, it enabled clearer attribution of performance gains—distinguishing improvements derived from the proposed structural adaptation strategy from those attributable solely to architectural modernization.

Consistent with the Fair Comparison Protocol defined in Step 1, SegNeXt was trained and evaluated under strictly identical experimental conditions, including dataset split, input resolution (512 × 512), ImageNet-1 K pretrained initialization, optimizer configuration, learning rate schedule, and data augmentation pipeline.

Analysis of boundary recognition and the effectiveness of edge supervision

In landscape semantic segmentation, categories such as roads and water bodies often feature complex, ambiguous boundaries that are prone to misclassification. To improve boundary delineation, an edge-aware auxiliary supervision module was integrated into the SegFormer decoder. This module leverages image gradients to guide a parallel edge prediction branch, which is jointly optimized through a multi-loss strategy. To assess its effectiveness, two model variants were compared: SegFormer-base (the original backbone) and SegFormer-edge (enhanced with edge supervision). Similarly, edge-aware versions of Swin-Unet and HRNet—named Swin-edge and HRNet-edge—were also developed. The comparative results are presented in Fig. 4.

As shown in Table 9, evaluating models using landscape-oriented structural metrics reveals pronounced differences. Conventional segmentation metrics primarily measure pixel-level overlap, whereas structural metrics capture variations in spatial organization that mIoU alone cannot fully reflect. The proposed SegFormer-Adapt model achieved the highest Boundary F1 scores for both road–green space and water–land interfaces, indicating superior boundary localization and continuity. This improvement demonstrates that the edge-aware supervision mechanism effectively enhances the model’s sensitivity to spatial transitions, particularly in sparse or irregular structures. In contrast, CNN-based models (e.g., UNet and DeepLabV3+) exhibited lower boundary consistency, reflecting their limited ability to model long-range contextual dependencies. Significant differences were also observed in the FI. SegFormer-Adapt attained the lowest FI values, indicating that predicted landscape elements tended to form more coherent and spatially clustered regions. Lower FI values imply reduced over-segmentation and fewer artificial discontinuities, which is crucial for connectivity analysis and spatial pattern interpretation in landscape planning. Models with higher FI values sometimes achieved competitive mIoU scores, but their predictions were more fragmented, potentially distorting downstream spatial assessments. The Connectivity Preservation Score (CPS) exhibited a similar trend. Higher CPS values for SegFormer-Adapt indicate better preservation of structural integrity for linear and patch-based landscape elements. This finding highlights a key advantage of Transformer-based global context modeling, namely, its ability to represent extended spatial structures consistently, such as road networks and waterfront boundaries. By comparison, models relying primarily on local convolution operations are more prone to structural discontinuities.

Discussion

The experimental results and ablation analyses indicate that the performance advantage of SegFormer-Adapt is not limited to overall mIoU improvements; it is primarily reflected in structure-sensitive categories and boundary stability metrics. Compared with traditional convolutional models, the Transformer architecture’s strength in landscape semantic segmentation stems largely from its global context modeling capability. Landscape elements often exhibit pronounced spatial structures, such as linear roads, strip-like green spaces, and continuous water networks. Semantic discrimination of these targets depends on cross-region structural consistency rather than solely on local textures. The multi-scale self-attention mechanism enables the model to capture long-range dependencies at different spatial resolutions, effectively mitigating structural discontinuities caused by the fixed receptive field of convolutional models. Class-level results show that this advantage is particularly pronounced for spatially extended elements like roads and water bodies. Ablation studies further reveal the sources of performance gains. Edge-aware supervision contributed most directly to Boundary F1 improvements. By explicitly incorporating gradient-based structural information into the supervision signal, the model learns boundary localization constraints alongside semantic prediction. This indicates that in planning-oriented scenarios, relying solely on region-overlap metrics is insufficient; boundary consistency and structural coherence are critical for reliable downstream spatial analysis. Scale-aware fusion and Boundary jitter augmentation primarily stabilize fine-structured categories. Their effect is not merely numerical improvement but a reduction in the risk of structural discontinuities. The Unified Label Mapping strategy demonstrated consistent generalization across cross-dataset experiments, highlighting the fundamental importance of semantic label hierarchy consistency for multi-source landscape data. Model performance still varies across different landscape types. For categories with clear structure or strong spatial continuity, the combination of Transformer architecture and structural supervision produces synergistic gains. However, in highly fragmented or semantically transitional regions, misclassifications persist. This does not indicate model failure but rather reflects the inherent semantic uncertainty in complex landscape scenes. These findings suggest that future research could incorporate uncertainty modeling or more refined scale constraints to improve robustness for very small targets and complex transition zones. Overall, the results support a methodologically significant conclusion: the advantage of SegFormer-Adapt arises not from a single architectural modification but from the synergy between structure-driven model adaptation and planning-oriented evaluation protocols. This perspective implies that performance optimization in landscape semantic segmentation should extend beyond network design to integrate model architecture, supervision strategies, and evaluation metrics within a unified structural logic. Unlike uncertainty-aware segmentation approaches, this study does not explicitly model predictive uncertainty. Instead, boundary instability is indirectly reduced through structural supervision and scale constraints. The two approaches are complementary, and future work could combine structural constraints with uncertainty estimation to further enhance robustness in complex transitional regions.

Conclusion

Research Contribution

This study re-examines semantic segmentation from the perspective of landscape structure. It did not introduce a new architecture, but demonstrated that the segmentation performance in the planning scenario fundamentally depends on the structural fidelity, including boundary continuity and connectivity maintenance. The proposed two-level evaluation protocol extends the traditional visual indicators by embedding spatial structure semantics into quantitative evaluation.

A reproducible structural adaptive process is established to match the segmentation optimization with the spatial characteristics of a specific landscape. The framework provides a systematic mechanism for integrating boundary-aware supervision and scale-sensitive modeling without changing the backbone architecture.

The study results illustrate how to evaluate the segmentation model and apply it to landscape planning. By identifying structural sensitive categories as the main source of performance differences, this study provides operational guidance for model selection and deployment in planning workflow.

Future work and research limitations

Despite the promising performance of SegFormer in landscape semantic segmentation, several limitations and opportunities for enhancement remain. First, the current label system depends on predefined category mappings, which restricts adaptability to heterogeneous regional taxonomies or non-standard classification schemes. Future research could explore the incorporation of learnable semantic clustering techniques to improve model generalizability across diverse label sets. Second, although the edge-aware auxiliary module strengthens structural integrity, it shows instability when detecting very small targets such as urban furniture or fragmented water bodies. The integration of super-resolution guidance or structural prior modules may enhance fine-grained feature recognition in these cases. Third, the present study primarily focuses on medium-resolution remote sensing imagery and lacks mechanisms for continuous spatial modeling of ultra-high-resolution scenes. Subsequent work might leverage sliding-window inference or image stitching methodologies to scale the model’s applicability to city-scale landscape planning maps. Furthermore, practical deployment will necessitate the fusion of geographic coordinate mapping, three-dimensional terrain data, and semantic layers reflecting human activities—critical advancements toward achieving comprehensive spatial intelligence.
