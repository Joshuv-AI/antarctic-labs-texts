Partition Trees: Conditional Density Estimation over General Outcome Spaces
Felipe Angelim, Alessandro Leite

Abstract
We propose Partition Trees , a tree-based framework for conditional density estimation over general outcome spaces, supporting both continuous and categorical variables within a unified formulation. Our approach models conditional distributions as piecewise-constant densities on data-adaptive partitions and learns trees by directly minimizing conditional negative log-likelihood. This yields a scalable, nonparametric alternative to existing probabilistic trees that does not make parametric assumptions about the target distribution. We further introduce Partition Forests , an ensemble extension obtained by averaging conditional densities. Empirically, we demonstrate improved probabilistic prediction over CART-style trees and competitive or superior performance compared to state-of-the-art probabilistic tree methods and Random Forests, along with robustness to redundant features and heteroscedastic noise.

Introduction

Decision trees are among the earliest and most widely used learning algorithms, valued for their computational efficiency, interpretability, and ability to capture nonlinear interactions through recursive partitioning
(
25
;
21
;
3
;
23
)
. Early methods such as Automatic Interaction Detection (AID)
(
25
)
and CHAID
(
21
)
introduced the idea of fitting piecewise-constant models on data-adaptive partitions of the input space. The CART framework later systematized tree construction for both classification and regression and popularized pruning via cost-complexity regularization
(
3
)
.

Although classical trees focus on point prediction or class probabilities, there has been growing interest in extending tree-based methods to probabilistic prediction and conditional density estimation. Recent approaches include CADET
(
9
)
, which employs parametric conditional densities at the leaves and selects splits using a cross-entropy criterion, and CDTree
(
34
)
, which jointly optimizes splits and leaf-wise histograms via a minimal description length objective. Although these methods demonstrate strong empirical performance, CADET’s leaf models impose parametric assumptions, whereas CDTree’s joint split-and-histogram optimization can be computationally demanding, limiting scalability on large datasets.

In this paper, we introduce
Partition Trees
, a tree-based framework for conditional density estimation over general outcome spaces that supports both continuous and categorical variables. Our approach models conditional distributions as piecewise-constant densities defined on data-adaptive partitions of the joint covariate-outcome space. Formally, we adopt a measure-theoretic perspective in which conditional densities are defined via probability measures and Radon–Nikodym derivatives with respect to these partitions. Within this unified framework, both classification and regression emerge as cases of conditional density estimation. Trees are constructed greedily by maximizing an empirical log-loss objective, which is equivalent to minimizing the conditional negative log-likelihood of the resulting density estimator. This objective naturally accommodates heteroscedastic noise and leads to empirical robustness to redundant features.

We further extend
Partition Trees
to ensembles, termed
Partition Forests
, by averaging the predicted conditional densities across multiple trees. Experiments on a diverse set of classification and regression benchmarks demonstrate that
Partition Forests
consistently improve probabilistic prediction compared to CART-style trees. On classification tasks, they achieve lower log-loss on several datasets, while
Partition Forests
outperform Random Forests on the majority of benchmarks. For regression,
Partition Forests
provide competitive point predictions and provide strong probabilistic performance, outperforming CADET on larger datasets without relying on parametric assumptions. Finally, semi-synthetic experiments highlight robustness to both homoscedastic and heteroscedastic noise, and to redundant features. We provide an implementation of
Partition Trees
and
Partition Forests
at
github.com/felipeangelimvieira/partition_tree
.

2.1
Setting

Let
\displaystyle(\Omega,\mathcal{A},\mathbb{P})
be a probability space. Let
\displaystyle X\in\mathcal{X}
denote the covariates and

\displaystyle Y\in\mathcal{Y}
is the outcome. We observe a dataset

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}=\{(x_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}
of i.i.d. samples drawn from the joint distribution

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{XY}
, the push-forward of
\displaystyle\mathbb{P}
on the product space

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}:=\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
.

A measurable cell
\displaystyle A\subseteq\mathcal{Z}
is written as a product set

\displaystyle A=A_{X}\times A_{Y}
, where
\displaystyle A_{X}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
and
\displaystyle A_{Y}\subseteq\mathcal{Y}
denote its covariate
and outcome components, respectively. We use subscripts to indicate projections of a cell onto a specific coordinate space, and omit subscripts when referring to
subsets of the joint space. Let
\displaystyle\mathrm{pr}_{X}:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\to\mathcal{X}

\displaystyle\mathrm{pr}_{Y}:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\to\mathcal{Y}

Accordingly, for any
\displaystyle A\subseteq\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}

\displaystyle A_{X}:=\mathrm{pr}_{X}(A)
and
\displaystyle A_{Y}:=\mathrm{pr}_{Y}(A)
.
Let

\displaystyle\displaystyle n_{XY}(A)

\displaystyle\displaystyle:=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{1}\{(x_{i},y_{i})\in A\}=N\;\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{XY}(A)

(1)

\displaystyle\displaystyle n_{X}(A)

\displaystyle\displaystyle:=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{1}\{x_{i}\in A_{X}\}=N\;\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{X}(A_{X})

(2)

denote the number of samples on a cell and its
\displaystyle\mathcal{X}
and
\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}
components. We use
\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{X}(A):=\mathbb{P}_{X}(A_{X})
,
\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{Y}(A):=\mathbb{P}_{Y}(A_{Y})
,
\displaystyle\mu_{Y}(A):=\mu_{Y}(A_{Y})
for simplicity. In addition, let
\displaystyle\pi_{N}(\{z_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N})
,
\displaystyle z_{i}\in\mathcal{Z}
, be a partitioning rule that maps datasets of size
\displaystyle N
to measurable partitions
\displaystyle\pi_{N}(\{z_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N})=\{A_{i}\}_{i=1}^{K}
. We overload the usage of
\displaystyle\pi_{N}
to also represent a partition generated by
\displaystyle\pi_{N}(\{z_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N})
when the dataset is fixed or irrelevant to the context. In particular,
\displaystyle\pi_{N}(\mathcal{D})
implies that
\displaystyle|\mathcal{D}|=N
. For any
\displaystyle z\in\mathcal{Z}

\displaystyle\pi_{N}[z]

\displaystyle A\in\pi_{N}
such that

\displaystyle z\in A
.

2.2
Mixed-Type Covariates and Outcomes

We allow
\displaystyle\mathcal{X}
and
\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}
to contain a mixture of coordinate types.

\displaystyle\displaystyle X

\displaystyle\displaystyle=(X^{(c)},X^{(k)})\in\mathcal{X}^{(c)}\times\mathcal{X}^{(k)}=:\mathcal{X}

\displaystyle\displaystyle Y

\displaystyle\displaystyle=(Y^{(c)},Y^{(k)})\in\mathcal{Y}^{(c)}\times\mathcal{Y}^{(k)}=:\mathcal{Y}

where,

(a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}^{(c)}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d_{x,c}}
and
\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}^{(c)}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d_{y,c}}
are continuous coordinates;

(b)

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}^{(k)}=\prod_{j=1}^{d_{x,k}}\Sigma_{x,j}
and
\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}^{(k)}=\prod_{j=1}^{d_{y,k}}\Sigma_{y,j}
are categorical coordinates with alphabets
\displaystyle\Sigma_{x,j},\Sigma_{y,j}
(assumed finite).

Cells are factorized coordinate-wise, with internal splits for continuous coordinates, and subset splits for categorical coordinates.

We introduce some notions that will be important in the consistency theorem. We first define a distance metric for continuous coordinates. To handle unbounded continuous domains, we use the transformation
\displaystyle\phi(u)=u/(1+u)
on the Euclidean distance.

Define
\displaystyle d_{cont}(z,z^{\prime})=\phi(\big\|(x^{(c)},y^{(c)})-(x^{\prime(c)},y^{\prime(c)})\big\|_{2})
and
\displaystyle\mathrm{diam}_{cont}(A)=\sup_{z,z^{\prime}\in A}d_{cont}(z,z^{\prime})
. Also, define the diameter:

\mathrm{diam}(A)=\mathrm{diam}_{cont}(A)+\sum_{k}\frac{|S_{k}(A)|-1}{|\Sigma_{k}|-1}.

where
\displaystyle S_{k}(A)\subseteq\Sigma_{k}
is the set of categories present in leaf
\displaystyle A
along categorical coordinate
\displaystyle k
.

2.3
Conditional Density Estimation via Radon–Nikodym Derivatives

Let
\displaystyle(X,Y)
be a random pair taking values in
\displaystyle\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
, where
\displaystyle\mathcal{X}
and
\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}
are measurable spaces. Let
\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{XY}
and
\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{X}
denote the joint and marginal distributions, respectively. We fix a
\displaystyle\sigma
-finite reference measure
\displaystyle\mu_{Y}
on
\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}
, chosen as the counting measure for discrete outcomes and Lebesgue measure for continuous ones.

Existence of a conditional density.

We assume throughout that the joint distribution admits a conditional density of
\displaystyle Y
given
\displaystyle X
with respect to
\displaystyle\mu_{Y}
.

Assumption 2.1

(Existence of conditional density)
.

The joint distribution
\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{XY}
is absolutely continuous with respect to

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{X}\otimes\mu_{Y}
, i.e.,

\mathbb{P}_{XY}\ll\mathbb{P}_{X}\otimes\mu_{Y}

Under
2.1
, the Radon–Nikodym derivative

f:=\frac{d\mathbb{P}_{XY}}{d(\mathbb{P}_{X}\otimes\mu_{Y})}

exists and defines a version of the conditional density of
\displaystyle Y
given
\displaystyle X
with respect to
\displaystyle\mu_{Y}
, in the sense that for
\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{X}
-almost every
\displaystyle x
and all measurable
\displaystyle B\subseteq\mathcal{Y}
,

\mathbb{P}(Y\in B\mid X=x)=\int_{B}f(x,y)\,\mu_{Y}(dy).

2.3.1
Piecewise-constant approximation on a partition

Define
\displaystyle\nu:=\mathbb{P}_{X}\otimes\mu_{Y}
and let
\displaystyle\bar{\mathcal{Z}}\subseteq\mathcal{Z}
be a measurable subset with
\displaystyle\nu(\bar{\mathcal{Z}})<\infty
.
When
\displaystyle\nu(\mathcal{Z})<\infty
, one may take
\displaystyle\bar{\mathcal{Z}}=\mathcal{Z}
. Let
\displaystyle\pi
be a measurable
partition of
\displaystyle\bar{\mathcal{Z}}
into cells
\displaystyle A=A_{X}\times A_{Y}
.

On a cell
\displaystyle A
with
\displaystyle 0<\nu(A)<\infty
, the best constant approximation of
\displaystyle f
in
the sense of matching the
\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{XY}
-mass of the cell is obtained by choosing

\displaystyle c_{A}\geq 0
such that

\int_{A}c_{A}\,\nu(dz)=\mathbb{P}_{XY}(A).

Solving gives the cell-average value

c_{A}=\frac{\mathbb{P}_{XY}(A)}{\nu(A)}.

(3)

This yields the piecewise-constant estimator

f_{\pi}(z)=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{\mathbb{P}_{XY}(\pi[z])}{\nu(\pi[z])},&z\in\bar{\mathcal{Z}},\\[6.00006pt]
0,&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

(4)

Equivalently,
\displaystyle f_{\pi}=\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[f\mid\sigma(\pi)]

\displaystyle\nu
-a.e. on
\displaystyle\bar{\mathcal{Z}}
, i.e.,
the conditional expectation of
\displaystyle f
given the
\displaystyle\sigma
-algebra generated by the
partition.

Because
\displaystyle\nu=\mathbb{P}_{X}\otimes\mu_{Y}
factorizes over
\displaystyle X
and
\displaystyle Y
, a product cell

\displaystyle A=A_{X}\times A_{Y}
satisfies

\nu(A)=\mathbb{P}_{X}(A_{X})\,\mu_{Y}(A_{Y}),

so (
3
) can be written more explicitly as

c_{A}=\frac{\mathbb{P}_{XY}(A)}{\mathbb{P}_{X}(A_{X})\,\mu_{Y}(A_{Y})}.

2.3.2
Empirical estimator from counts

We estimate
\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{XY}(A)
and
\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{X}(A_{X})
from the sample

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}=\{(x_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}
. Recall the counts defined in Equations (
1
) and (
2
). Then
\displaystyle\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{XY}(A)=n_{XY}(A)/N
and
\displaystyle\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{X}(A_{X})=n_{X}(A)/N
, and the
plug-in estimate of
\displaystyle c_{A}
becomes

\hat{c}_{A}=\frac{\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{XY}(A)}{\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{X}(A_{X})\,\mu_{Y}(A_{Y})}=\frac{n_{XY}(A)}{n_{X}(A)\,\mu_{Y}(A_{Y})},

(5)

defined whenever
\displaystyle n_{X}(A)>0
and
\displaystyle\mu_{Y}(A_{Y})>0
. Substituting (
5
)
into (
4
) yields a fully explicit estimator once a
partition
\displaystyle\pi_{N}
is fixed.

2.3.3
Truncation of unbounded outcome spaces

If
\displaystyle\mu_{Y}(\mathcal{Y})=\infty
(e.g.,
\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}
contains an unbounded continuous component), then
\displaystyle\nu(A)=\mathbb{P}_{X}(A_{X})\mu_{Y}(A_{Y})
may be infinite unless
\displaystyle A_{Y}
has finite
\displaystyle\mu_{Y}
-measure. To ensure well-defined denominators while still covering most of the probability mass, we restrict the estimator to a data-dependent truncated outcome domain
\displaystyle\bar{\mathcal{Y}}_{N}
with
\displaystyle\mu_{Y}(\bar{\mathcal{Y}}_{N})<\infty
, and define

\displaystyle\bar{\mathcal{Z}}_{N}:=\mathcal{X}\times\bar{\mathcal{Y}}_{N}
.

For
\displaystyle N
samples
\displaystyle\{y_{i}^{(c)}\}_{i=1}^{N}
in the continuous part
\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}^{(c)}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d_{y,c}}
, define the axis-aligned box

\displaystyle\displaystyle\bar{\mathcal{Y}}_{N}^{(c)}:=\prod_{j=1}^{d_{y,c}}\big[\,\underline{y}_{N,j}-\delta_{N},\ \overline{y}_{N,j}+\delta_{N}\,\big],

(6)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\underline{y}_{N,j}:=\min_{1\leq i\leq N}y_{i,j},\ \ \overline{y}_{N,j}:=\max_{1\leq i\leq N}y_{i,j},

and set
\displaystyle\bar{\mathcal{Y}}_{N}:=\bar{\mathcal{Y}}_{N}^{(c)}\times\mathcal{Y}^{(k)}
for the mixed case. The
padding
\displaystyle\delta_{N}>0
(typically
\displaystyle\delta_{N}\downarrow 0
) avoids boundary effects
and ensures
\displaystyle\mu_{Y}(\bar{\mathcal{Y}}_{N})<\infty
.

Given a data-dependent partition
\displaystyle\pi_{N}
of
\displaystyle\bar{\mathcal{Z}}_{N}
, our estimator is

\hat{f}_{\pi_{N}}(z)=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{n_{XY}(\pi_{N}[z])}{n_{X}(\pi_{N}[z])\,\mu_{Y}(\pi_{N}[z]_{Y})},&z\in\bar{\mathcal{Z}}_{N},\\[8.99994pt]
0,&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

(7)

We assume that
\displaystyle\delta_{N}\downarrow 0
and that the truncation captures asymptotically all the probability mass, i.e.,

\mathbb{P}_{XY}(Y\notin\bar{\mathcal{Y}}_{N})\longrightarrow 0\quad\text{almost surely as }N\to\infty.

We assume a deterministic boundary convention (e.g., half-open intervals) so that partition cells from a disjoint cover up to
\displaystyle\mu_{Y}
-null cells.

For a fixed
\displaystyle x
, the map
\displaystyle y\mapsto\hat{f}_{\pi_{N}}(x,y)
is piecewise constant over the sets

\displaystyle\{A_{Y}:A\in\pi_{N},\ x\in A_{X}\}
, and its normalizing factor depends on the
corresponding
\displaystyle X
-projections
\displaystyle A_{X}
. Unless these sets form a partition of

\displaystyle\bar{\mathcal{Y}}_{N}
with a common denominator,
\displaystyle\hat{f}_{\pi_{N}}(x,\cdot)
need not integrate to one.
When a proper conditional density on
\displaystyle\bar{\mathcal{Y}}_{N}
is required, we therefore use the normalized version

\bar{f}_{\pi_{N}}(x,y)=\frac{\hat{f}_{\pi_{N}}(x,y)}{\int_{\bar{\mathcal{Y}}_{N}}\hat{f}_{\pi_{N}}(x,y^{\prime})\,d\mu_{Y}(y^{\prime})},

(8)

whenever the denominator is positive.

The next section describes how to efficiently choose
\displaystyle\pi_{N}
to optimize a conditional log-loss objective.

2.4.1
Candidate splits and feasibility

A candidate split acts on a single coordinate of
\displaystyle Z=(X,Y)
and preserves the
product structure of the leaves:

\displaystyle\displaystyle A\;\longrightarrow\;\{A_{l},A_{r}\},\qquad A=A_{l}\uplus A_{r}

\displaystyle\displaystyle A_{l}=A_{l,X}\times A_{l,Y},\;\;A_{r}=A_{r,X}\times A_{r,Y}.

If we split along an
\displaystyle X
-coordinate, then
\displaystyle A_{l,Y}=A_{r,Y}=A_{Y}
and
\displaystyle A_{X}
is
partitioned into
\displaystyle A_{l,X}\uplus A_{r,X}
. If we split along a
\displaystyle Y
-coordinate,
then
\displaystyle A_{l,X}=A_{r,X}=A_{X}
and
\displaystyle A_{Y}
is partitioned into
\displaystyle A_{l,Y}\uplus A_{r,Y}
.

For continuous coordinates
\displaystyle z_{\ell}\in\mathbb{R}
, we use threshold splits

\displaystyle z_{\ell}\leq t
vs.
\displaystyle z_{\ell}>t
. For categorical coordinates
\displaystyle z_{\ell}\in\Sigma
, we
use subset tests
\displaystyle z_{\ell}\in S
vs.
\displaystyle z_{\ell}\notin S
for

\displaystyle\emptyset\subsetneq S\subsetneq\Sigma
. We only accept splits that keep both
children well-defined for the density estimator, i.e.,

\displaystyle\displaystyle n_{X}(A_{l})>0,\quad n_{X}(A_{r})>0,

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mu_{Y}(A_{l,Y})>0,\quad\mu_{Y}(A_{r,Y})>0.

Clearly, when splitting on a
\displaystyle Y
-coordinate,
\displaystyle n_{X}(A_{l})=n_{X}(A_{r})=n_{X}(A)
.

Candidate splits producing a child with
\displaystyle n_{X}(A)=0
or
\displaystyle\mu_{Y}(A_{Y})=0
are discarded; if no admissible split exists for a leaf, it is declared terminal.

2.4.2
Log-loss objective and split gain

Given a partition
\displaystyle\pi
, recall that the piecewise-constant conditional density
estimate is
\displaystyle f_{\pi}(z)=\mathbb{P}_{XY}(\pi[z])/\nu(\pi[z])
with
\displaystyle\nu=\mathbb{P}_{X}\otimes\mu_{Y}
.
We score a partition using the conditional negative log-likelihood

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\pi)

\displaystyle\displaystyle:=-\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{XY}}\big[\log f_{\pi}(Z)\big]

(9)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=-\sum_{A\in\pi}\mathbb{P}_{XY}(A)\log\!\left(\frac{\mathbb{P}_{XY}(A)}{\mathbb{P}_{X}(A_{X})\,\mu_{Y}(A_{Y})}\right),

with conventions
\displaystyle 0\log 0:=0
.

Consider splitting a leaf
\displaystyle A\in\pi
into
\displaystyle A_{l},A_{r}
and let

\displaystyle\pi^{\prime}:=(\pi\setminus\{A\})\cup\{A_{l},A_{r}\}
. The population gain is the log-loss
reduction

G(\pi^{\prime},\pi):=\mathcal{L}(\pi)-\mathcal{L}(\pi^{\prime})\;\;\geq 0,

and can be written as a Jensen gap (Appendix
A
).

In practice, we maximize the empirical gain obtained by replacing
\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{XY}
and
\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{X}

with their empirical counterparts.

\displaystyle\displaystyle\widehat{G}(\pi^{\prime},\pi)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\frac{n_{XY}(A_{l})}{N}\log\!\left(\frac{n_{XY}(A_{l})}{n_{X}(A_{l})\,\mu_{Y}(A_{l,Y})}\right)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\quad+\frac{n_{XY}(A_{r})}{N}\log\!\left(\frac{n_{XY}(A_{r})}{n_{X}(A_{r})\,\mu_{Y}(A_{r,Y})}\right)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\quad-\frac{n_{XY}(A)}{N}\log\!\left(\frac{n_{XY}(A)}{n_{X}(A)\,\mu_{Y}(A_{Y})}\right),

(10)

where any term with
\displaystyle n_{XY}(\cdot)=0
contributes
\displaystyle 0
.

2.4.3
Best-first growth with exploitation and optional exploration budgets

The tree is grown by repeatedly selecting a leaf and splitting it, under a global split budget
\displaystyle k_{N}
. Our default procedure is
gain-based
(exploitation), which greedily chooses the split that maximizes the empirical log-loss gain (
2.4.2
). For worst-case
\displaystyle L^{1}(\nu)
-consistency guarantees (
Section

3
), we also analyze a
hybrid
variant that injects a small number of geometry-driven splits (exploration) to enforce leaf shrinkage.

Exploitation (gain-based) step.

For each current leaf
\displaystyle A
, we search over admissible one-coordinate splits and
record the best achievable empirical gain. We then split the leaf–split pair
with the largest gain among all leaves. Operationally, we maintain a priority queue of leaves by their current best gain; after splitting a leaf, only that leaf’s entry needs to be recomputed.

Optional exploration (diameter-based) step.

Pure gain maximization can, in principle, stall on coarse partitions when all one-step gains are
zero, even though
\displaystyle f
varies within the leaf (for a standard greedy-tree failure mode, see
(
30
)
). To obtain a worst-case leaf-shrinkage condition (used in
Section

3
), the hybrid variant allocates an exploration budget
\displaystyle k_{N,e}
and repeatedly splits the
populated
leaf (i.e., with
\displaystyle n_{XY}(A)>0
) of the largest diameter. The split coordinate is chosen as the one with the largest side length inside that leaf. For bounded continuous sides, we split at the geometric mean value within the leaf; for categorical coordinates, we split off a (random) singleton category. If the selected leaf is unbounded along the chosen continuous coordinate, we instead split at an empirical threshold so that all samples in the leaf fall into a bounded child (e.g., at
\displaystyle\max\{z_{i}:\ z_{i}\in A\}
, creating one bounded populated child and one empty unbounded remainder).
We design the exploration splits so that each such split satisfies a uniform diameter contraction

\max\big(\mathrm{diam}(A_{l}),\mathrm{diam}(A_{r})\big)\leq\rho\,\mathrm{diam}(A)

for some
\displaystyle\rho\in[1/2,1)
, as required by the consistency analysis in
Section

3
.
One simple schedule is to perform the
\displaystyle k_{N,e}
exploration splits first and then use the remaining
\displaystyle k_{N}-k_{N,e}
steps for gain-based refinement; interleaving the two phases is also possible. The asymptotic scaling conditions on
\displaystyle k_{N}
and
\displaystyle k_{N,e}
required for consistency are explicitly stated in
Section

3
.

2.4.4
Efficient split search and complexity

A computational advantage of (
2.4.2
) is that it depends only on simple count statistics for candidate children. For a continuous coordinate
\displaystyle z_{\ell}
and a leaf
\displaystyle A
, candidate thresholds are the midpoints between consecutive distinct values observed in the leaf. After sorting the relevant values within each leaf, we scan thresholds in increasing order and update the required counts using prefix sums, enabling evaluation of all thresholds on that coordinate in linear time, plus sorting.
Algorithm

1
in
Section

B.1
gives one concrete implementation using two sorted index lists: one for the covariate samples in
\displaystyle A_{X}
(to update
\displaystyle n_{X}
when splitting on
\displaystyle X
), and another for the joint samples in
\displaystyle A
(to update
\displaystyle n_{XY}
and to handle splits on either
\displaystyle X
or
\displaystyle Y
).

For categorical coordinates with alphabet
\displaystyle\Sigma
, we avoid enumerating all
\displaystyle 2^{|\Sigma|}-2
subset tests by computing per-category count statistics inside the leaf, sorting categories by the score, and scanning the
\displaystyle|\Sigma|-1
prefix
thresholds.
Section

B.2
shows that this scan attains the best
subset split for a leaf and coordinate.

Let
\displaystyle d_{Z}
be the number of splittable coordinates in
\displaystyle Z=(X,Y)
. With per-leaf sorting, evaluating all candidate splits at a leaf with
\displaystyle n_{X}(A)
covariate points costs
\displaystyle O(d_{Z}\;n_{X}(A)\log n_{X}(A))
in a straightforward implementation.

For categorical coordinates, since alphabet sizes are fixed, the sort-and-scan subset search adds only
\displaystyle O(n_{X}(A))
work per leaf, which is smaller than the split search cost over continuous coordinates. Summed over the best-first growth, this leads to an overall time complexity on the order of
\displaystyle O(d_{Z}\,N\log N)
with global presorting and maintained sorted indices, or
\displaystyle O(d_{Z}\,N\log^{2}N)
when sorting within each node for balanced trees, matching the scaling observed in practice for CART trees.

2.5
Partition Forest (bagging ensemble)

To improve predictive stability and log-loss, we also consider an ensemble variant,
Partition Forest
, obtained by bagging
Partition Trees

\displaystyle B
trees independently, where tree
\displaystyle b
is trained on a bootstrap sample
\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{(b)}
(and optionally using random feature subsampling at each split, as in Random Forests
(
4

\displaystyle\{\hat{f}_{\pi_{N}}^{(b)}\}_{b=1}^{B}
, the forest predictor is the averaged density

\hat{f}_{\pi_{N}}^{\mathrm{F}}(x,y):=\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\hat{f}_{\pi_{N}}^{(b)}(x,y),

which is then normalized as in

\displaystyle\bar{f}_{\pi_{N}}^{\mathrm{F}}(x,y)
.

3

To evaluate robustness to correlated and redundant features, we augment the feature matrix
\displaystyle X\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times n_{x}}

\displaystyle k

\displaystyle X_{c_{i}}

\displaystyle\epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{i}^{2})

\displaystyle\sigma_{i}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j}|X_{j,c_{i}}|

We evaluated robustness using the Concrete Compressive Strength dataset using five-fold cross-validation. The results are summarized in

5
Conclusion

We introduced
Partition Trees
, a decision-tree framework for conditional density estimation over general outcome spaces, including continuous, categorical, and mixed-type targets. The method is grounded in a measure-theoretic formulation in which conditional densities are defined as a Radon-Nikodym derivative with respect to the dominating measure
\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{X}\otimes\mu_{Y}
. This yields a unified piecewise-constant estimator that naturally covers both classification and regression within a single framework.

Partition Trees
are learned via a greedy, best-first procedure that directly optimizes a conditional log-loss objective on data-adaptive partitions of the joint covariate-outcome space. We further analyzed a hybrid variant that interleaves a vanishing fraction of diameter-reducing exploration splits, and showed that it admits an
\displaystyle L^{1}(\nu)
-consistency guarantee under standard partition growth and shrinkage conditions. An ensemble extension,
Partition Forests
, averages conditional densities across trees and improves predictive stability and log-loss performance.

Empirically, the proposed models demonstrate competitive probabilistic performance across a diverse set of classification and regression benchmarks.
Partition Forests
typically achieve lower log-loss than CART-based trees and often improve over Random Forests, while providing accurate predictive densities and competitive point predictions in regression tasks. Experiments also highlight robustness to heteroscedastic noise and redundant features.

Several directions for future work remain open. From an algorithmic perspective, integrating
Partition Forests
into boosting frameworks and developing pruning strategies tailored to conditional density objectives may further improve efficiency performance and efficiency. More broadly,
Partition Trees
’ ability to handle heterogeneous and mixed-type outcomes makes them a natural candidate for integration into causal inference pipelines, where flexible conditional density estimation plays a central role.

Properties of the Log-loss Gain

We first show that the population gain of a candidate split is a Jensen gap, related to child averages. Define
\displaystyle\varphi(u)=u\log u
. For a leaf
\displaystyle A\in\pi
and split
\displaystyle A\rightarrow\{A_{l},A_{r}\}
. Let
\displaystyle\pi^{\prime}
be the partition after splitting
\displaystyle A
. The population loss of a partition
\displaystyle\pi
can be expressed as:

\mathcal{L}(\pi)=-\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{XY}}[\log f_{\pi}]=-\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[f_{\pi}\,\log f_{\pi}]

so the population gain can also be written:

\displaystyle\displaystyle G(\pi^{\prime},\pi)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=G(\{A_{l},A_{r}\},\{A\})

(12)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\nu(A_{l})\varphi(c_{A_{l}})+\nu(A_{r})\varphi(c_{A_{r}})-\nu(A)\varphi(c_{A})

(13)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\nu(A_{l})}{\nu(A)}\varphi(c_{A_{l}})+\frac{\nu(A_{r})}{\nu(A)}\varphi(c_{A_{r}})-\varphi(c_{A})\right)\,\nu(A)

(14)

where
\displaystyle c_{A}=\frac{1}{\nu(A)}\int_{A}fd\nu
.

From this identity, we derive the following proposition: the gain is a Jensen Gap.

Proposition A.1

(Population gain is a Jensen Gap)
.

Consider a cell
\displaystyle A
and two children
\displaystyle A_{l}
and
\displaystyle A_{r}
,
\displaystyle A=A_{l}\cup A_{r}
. Let
\displaystyle\nu_{A}=\nu(\cdot\cap A)/\nu(A)
be a normalized restriction of the measure
\displaystyle\nu
to
\displaystyle A
and let
\displaystyle\mathcal{G}:=\sigma(\{A_{l},A_{r}\})
be the
\displaystyle\sigma
-algebra generated by the split. Assume
\displaystyle\nu(A),\nu(A_{l}),\nu(A_{r})>0
. The gain can be expressed by the following Jensen gap times the measure
\displaystyle\nu(A)
:

G(\pi^{\prime},\pi)=\left(\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{A}}[\varphi(X)]-\varphi(\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{A}}[X])\right)\cdot\nu(A)

with
\displaystyle X(z)=\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{A}}[f\mid\mathcal{G}](z)
,
\displaystyle z\sim\nu_{A}
.

Proof.

Define the normalized restriction
\displaystyle\nu_{A}(B):=\nu(B\cap A)/\nu(A)
for measurable
\displaystyle B
.
Then

c_{A}=\frac{1}{\nu(A)}\int_{A}f\,d\nu=\int_{A}f\,d\nu_{A}=\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{A}}[f].

Let
\displaystyle\mathcal{G}:=\sigma(\{A_{l},A_{r}\})
. Since
\displaystyle\mathcal{G}
has atoms
\displaystyle A_{l}
and
\displaystyle A_{r}
, the conditional expectation
\displaystyle X(z):=\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{A}}[f\mid\mathcal{G}](z)
is
\displaystyle\mathcal{G}
-measurable, hence constant on each child:
\displaystyle X=u_{l}\mathbf{1}_{A_{l}}+u_{r}\mathbf{1}_{A_{r}}
. By the defining property of conditional expectation, for
\displaystyle B\in\{A_{l},A_{r}\}
,

\int_{B}X\,d\nu_{A}=\int_{B}f\,d\nu_{A}.

Thus
\displaystyle u_{l}=\frac{1}{\nu_{A}(A_{l})}\int_{A_{l}}f\,d\nu_{A}=\frac{1}{\nu(A_{l})}\int_{A_{l}}f\,d\nu=c_{A_{l}}

and similarly
\displaystyle u_{r}=c_{A_{r}}
. Therefore,
\displaystyle\nu_{A}
-a.e.,

X(z)=\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{A}}[f\mid\mathcal{G}](z)=\begin{cases}c_{A_{l}},&z\in A_{l},\\
c_{A_{r}},&z\in A_{r}.\end{cases}

Let
\displaystyle w:=\nu_{A}(A_{l})=\nu(A_{l})/\nu(A)
. Then

\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{A}}[\varphi(X)]=w\,\varphi(c_{A_{l}})+(1-w)\,\varphi(c_{A_{r}})=\frac{\nu(A_{l})}{\nu(A)}\varphi(c_{A_{l}})+\frac{\nu(A_{r})}{\nu(A)}\varphi(c_{A_{r}}).

Also, by the tower property,

\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{A}}[X]=\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{A}}\big[\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{A}}[f\mid\mathcal{G}]\big]=\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{A}}[f]=c_{A}.

Plugging these identities into (
12
) yields

G(\pi^{\prime},\pi)=\nu(A)\Big(\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{A}}[\varphi(X)]-\varphi(\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{A}}[X])\Big),

as claimed.
∎

Since the gain is a Jensen Gap, it is immediate that the gain is always greater than or equal to zero.

Corollary A.2

(Non-negativity of the gain)
.

For any split
\displaystyle\{A\}\to\{A_{l},A_{r}\}
, if
\displaystyle\nu(A),\nu(A_{l}),\nu(A_{r})>0
, then
\displaystyle G(\{A_{l},A_{r}\},\{A\})\geq 0
.

Proof.

Use the non-negativity of
\displaystyle\nu(A)
and the non-negativity of the Jensen Gap in
Proposition

A.1
to see that
\displaystyle G(\pi^{\prime},\pi)\geq 0
.

Proposition B.1

(Optimal subset split is a sorted prefix)
.

Let

and let
\displaystyle D(u,v)

\displaystyle S^{\star}

\displaystyle G(S)

\displaystyle r_{c}

\displaystyle\{r_{c}\}
once and scanning the
\displaystyle K-1
prefix thresholds using prefix sums of
\displaystyle\{a_{c},b_{c}\}

Proof.

Using
\displaystyle\sum_{c\in S}b_{c}(r_{c}-r_{S})=0
and the definition of
\displaystyle D

\displaystyle S^{c}

\displaystyle X

\displaystyle S

\displaystyle S

\displaystyle u:=r_{S}
and
\displaystyle v:=r_{S^{c}}

\displaystyle u\leq v

\displaystyle x

C.4
Hybrid Tree Algorithm Satisfies Conditions for Consistency

In this section, we show that the proposed algorithm satisfies the conditions for consistency.

Lemma C.4
.

Consider a tree algorithm that partitions
\displaystyle\bar{\mathcal{Z}}_{N}
with a maximum budget of
\displaystyle k_{N}
splits. If
\displaystyle k_{N}=o(\sqrt{\frac{N}{\log N}})
,
\displaystyle k_{N}\to\infty
, then conditions 1., 2. and 4. of Theorem
3.1
are met.

Proof.

To check condition 1, note that with
\displaystyle k
splits, there are
\displaystyle k+1
leaves and and
\displaystyle m(\mathcal{A}_{N})=\sup\limits_{\pi\in\mathcal{A}_{N}}|\pi|=k+1
. Then
\displaystyle N^{-1}m(\mathcal{A}_{N})\to 0
as
\displaystyle N\to\infty
whenever
\displaystyle k_{N}=o(N)
, which is satisfied by
\displaystyle o(\sqrt{N/\log(N)})
.

Now, we verify condition 2. Each split partitions the current sample indices by one coordinate test.
For a continuous coordinate, the number of distinct dichotomies on
\displaystyle N
points is at most
\displaystyle N

(threshold between two consecutive sorted values).
For a categorical coordinate with finite alphabet size
\displaystyle q
, the number of distinct subset splits is at most
\displaystyle 2^{q}-2
,
which is a constant independent of
\displaystyle N
.

Let
\displaystyle d_{\mathrm{thr}}
be the number of coordinates eligible for threshold splits,
and let
\displaystyle C_{\mathrm{cat}}:=\sum_{j=1}^{d_{\mathrm{cat}}}(2^{q_{j}}-2)
be the total number of categorical subset tests
across categorical coordinates (finite alphabets).
Then the number of distinct 2-way splits available at any node is at most

S_{N}\ \leq\ N^{d_{\mathrm{thr}}}\cdot(1+C_{\mathrm{cat}}),

hence a crude upper bound for the growth function of
\displaystyle k
-split trees is

\Delta^{*}_{N}(\mathcal{A}_{N})\ \leq\ (S_{N})^{k+1}.

Therefore,

\frac{1}{N}\log\Delta^{*}_{N}(\mathcal{A}_{N})\ \leq\ \frac{k+1}{N}\Big(d_{\mathrm{thr}}\log N+\log(1+C_{\mathrm{cat}})\Big),

The RHS goes to zero if
\displaystyle k=o(N/\log N)
, which is also satisfied by
\displaystyle k=o(\sqrt{N/\log N})
, and we have checked condition 2.

With respect to condition 4, under axis-aligned threshold splits on (
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{d_{c}}
) and subset-membership splits on categorical coordinates with finite alphabets, each leaf (X)-region is a product of an axis-aligned rectangle and a categorical cylinder set. Hence the induced class (
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{X}
) has finite VC dimension. Also, we have that
\displaystyle m_{X}(\mathcal{A}_{N})\leq m(\mathcal{A}_{N})
, then
\displaystyle k_{N}=o(\sqrt{\frac{N}{\log N}})\implies m_{X}(\mathcal{A}_{N})\sqrt{\frac{\log N}{N}}\to 0
.
∎

Lemma
C.4
showed that the tree algorithm can satisfy conditions 1,2, and 4. We now show that under some conditions, the tree algorithm also satisfies condition 3.

For condition 3, we need a splitting strategy that guarantees the shrinkage of cell sizes. The negative log-likelihood gain might not achieve this, since there might be distributions where one-step gain is zero, but there is a non-negligible oscillation of the conditional density.

Exploration contraction assumption.

\max(\mathrm{diam}(A_{1}),\mathrm{diam}(A_{r}))\leq\rho\,\mathrm{diam}(A)\quad\text{for some }\rho\in[1/2,1).

C.5
Normalization preserves
\displaystyle L^{1}(\nu)
-consistency

In practice, we may normalize a nonnegative estimator
\displaystyle h_{N}(x,\cdot)
to integrate to one
with respect to
\displaystyle\mu_{Y}
. The following lemma shows that this operation does not affect

\displaystyle L^{1}(\nu)
consistency, where
\displaystyle\nu=\mathbb{P}_{X}\otimes\mu_{Y}
.

Lemma C.6

(Normalization preserves
\displaystyle L^{1}(\nu)
-consistency)
.

Let
\displaystyle\mu_{Y}
be a
\displaystyle\sigma
-finite measure on
\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}
and let
\displaystyle\nu:=\mathbb{P}_{X}\otimes\mu_{Y}
.
Assume
\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{XY}\ll\nu
and let

f:=\frac{d\mathbb{P}_{XY}}{d(\mathbb{P}_{X}\otimes\mu_{Y})}.

Let
\displaystyle h_{N}:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\to[0,\infty)
be measurable and define

s_{N}(x):=\int_{\mathcal{Y}}h_{N}(x,y)\,\mu_{Y}(dy),\qquad\bar{h}_{N}(x,y):=\begin{cases}h_{N}(x,y)/s_{N}(x),&s_{N}(x)>0,\\
0,&s_{N}(x)=0.\end{cases}

If
\displaystyle\|h_{N}-f\|_{L^{1}(\nu)}\to 0
, then
\displaystyle\|\bar{h}_{N}-f\|_{L^{1}(\nu)}\to 0
. Moreover, for all
\displaystyle N
,

\|\bar{h}_{N}-f\|_{L^{1}(\nu)}\;\leq\;6\,\|h_{N}-f\|_{L^{1}(\nu)}.

Proof.

Define the pointwise
\displaystyle L^{1}(\mu_{Y})
error

\xi_{N}(x):=\int_{\mathcal{Y}}|h_{N}(x,y)-f(x,y)|\,\mu_{Y}(dy),

so that
\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{X}}[\xi_{N}]=\|h_{N}-f\|_{L^{1}(\nu)}
by Tonelli’s theorem. Next define the
“good” set

E_{N}:=\{x\in\mathcal{X}:\ \xi_{N}(x)\leq 1/2\}.

Since
\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{Y}}f(x,y)\,\mu_{Y}(dy)=1
for
\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{X}
-a.e.
\displaystyle x
, we have for such
\displaystyle x
,

|1-s_{N}(x)|=\left|\int_{\mathcal{Y}}\big(f(x,y)-h_{N}(x,y)\big)\,\mu_{Y}(dy)\right|\leq\int_{\mathcal{Y}}|f(x,y)-h_{N}(x,y)|\,\mu_{Y}(dy)=\xi_{N}(x).

Hence, on
\displaystyle E_{N}
we have
\displaystyle s_{N}(x)\geq 1-|1-s_{N}(x)|\geq 1-\xi_{N}(x)\geq 1/2
, so
\displaystyle\bar{h}_{N}
is well-defined there.

We decompose the
\displaystyle L^{1}(\nu)
error into contributions over
\displaystyle E_{N}
and its complement:

\|\bar{h}_{N}-f\|_{L^{1}(\nu)}=\int_{E_{N}}\int_{\mathcal{Y}}|\bar{h}_{N}-f|\,\mu_{Y}(dy)\,\mathbb{P}_{X}(dx)+\int_{E_{N}^{c}}\int_{\mathcal{Y}}|\bar{h}_{N}-f|\,\mu_{Y}(dy)\,\mathbb{P}_{X}(dx).

(29)

Step 1: bound on
\displaystyle E_{N}
.

Fix
\displaystyle x\in E_{N}
. Using the triangle inequality,

\int_{\mathcal{Y}}|\bar{h}_{N}-f|\,\mu_{Y}(dy)\leq\int_{\mathcal{Y}}|\bar{h}_{N}-h_{N}|\,\mu_{Y}(dy)+\int_{\mathcal{Y}}|h_{N}-f|\,\mu_{Y}(dy)=\int_{\mathcal{Y}}|\bar{h}_{N}-h_{N}|\,\mu_{Y}(dy)+\xi_{N}(x).

Moreover, for
\displaystyle x\in E_{N}
(so
\displaystyle s_{N}(x)>0
),

\int_{\mathcal{Y}}|\bar{h}_{N}-h_{N}|\,\mu_{Y}(dy)=\int_{\mathcal{Y}}h_{N}(x,y)\Big|\frac{1}{s_{N}(x)}-1\Big|\,\mu_{Y}(dy)=\Big|\frac{1}{s_{N}(x)}-1\Big|\int_{\mathcal{Y}}h_{N}(x,y)\,\mu_{Y}(dy)=|1-s_{N}(x)|.

Combining with
\displaystyle|1-s_{N}(x)|\leq\xi_{N}(x)
yields

\int_{\mathcal{Y}}|\bar{h}_{N}-f|\,\mu_{Y}(dy)\leq 2\,\xi_{N}(x)\qquad\text{for all }x\in E_{N}.

Integrating over
\displaystyle E_{N}
gives

\int_{E_{N}}\int_{\mathcal{Y}}|\bar{h}_{N}-f|\,\mu_{Y}(dy)\,\mathbb{P}_{X}(dx)\leq 2\int_{E_{N}}\xi_{N}(x)\,\mathbb{P}_{X}(dx)\leq 2\,\|h_{N}-f\|_{L^{1}(\nu)}.

(30)

Step 2: bound on
\displaystyle E_{N}^{c}
.

For any
\displaystyle x
, both
\displaystyle f(x,\cdot)
and
\displaystyle\bar{h}_{N}(x,\cdot)
are nonnegative and satisfy

\int_{\mathcal{Y}}f(x,y)\,\mu_{Y}(dy)=1\quad\text{and}\quad\int_{\mathcal{Y}}\bar{h}_{N}(x,y)\,\mu_{Y}(dy)\leq 1,

where the second inequality follows from the definition of
\displaystyle\bar{h}_{N}
(it equals
\displaystyle 1

when
\displaystyle s_{N}(x)>0
and equals
\displaystyle 0
otherwise). Therefore, for all
\displaystyle x
,

\int_{\mathcal{Y}}|\bar{h}_{N}-f|\,\mu_{Y}(dy)\leq\int_{\mathcal{Y}}\bar{h}_{N}\,\mu_{Y}(dy)+\int_{\mathcal{Y}}f\,\mu_{Y}(dy)\leq 2.

Hence

\int_{E_{N}^{c}}\int_{\mathcal{Y}}|\bar{h}_{N}-f|\,\mu_{Y}(dy)\,\mathbb{P}_{X}(dx)\leq 2\,\mathbb{P}_{X}(E_{N}^{c}).

(31)

It remains to bound
\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{X}(E_{N}^{c})
. By Markov’s inequality,

\mathbb{P}_{X}(E_{N}^{c})=\mathbb{P}_{X}\big(\xi_{N}>1/2\big)\leq 2\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{X}}[\xi_{N}]=2\,\|h_{N}-f\|_{L^{1}(\nu)}.

Substituting into (
31
) gives

\int_{E_{N}^{c}}\int_{\mathcal{Y}}|\bar{h}_{N}-f|\,\mu_{Y}(dy)\,\mathbb{P}_{X}(dx)\leq 4\,\|h_{N}-f\|_{L^{1}(\nu)}.

(32)

Step 3: conclude.

Combining (
29
), (
30
), and (
32
)
yields

\|\bar{h}_{N}-f\|_{L^{1}(\nu)}\leq 6\,\|h_{N}-f\|_{L^{1}(\nu)}.

In particular, if
\displaystyle\|h_{N}-f\|_{L^{1}(\nu)}\to 0
, then
\displaystyle\|\bar{h}_{N}-f\|_{L^{1}(\nu)}\to 0
.
∎

and Lemma

D.2
Hyperparameter Tuning

All methods were tuned using Optuna with 200 trials and a 20-minute timeout per model.
Cross-validation employed an 80/20 train-test split within each fold. For classification,
the scoring metric was negative log-loss, while for regression, log-loss from the skpro
library was used to optimize probabilistic predictions.

D.2.1
Classification Methods

Partition Tree
was tuned over two regularization parameters: the minimum number of
samples required at a leaf node (
min_samples_leaf

\displaystyle\in[1,100]
) and the minimum
number of samples in the feature space at each leaf (
min_samples_leaf_x

\displaystyle\in[1,400]
).

Partition Forest
, the ensemble extension of Partition Tree, included additional
hyperparameters controlling the subsampling strategy: the fraction of features to consider
at each split (
max_features

\displaystyle\in[0.7,1.0]
) and the fraction of samples to draw
for each tree (
max_samples

\displaystyle\in[0.7,1.0]
), along with the same leaf constraints
as Partition Tree.

XGBoost
was tuned over five hyperparameters: the number of boosting rounds
(
n_estimators

\displaystyle\in[1,200]
), tree depth (
max_depth

\displaystyle\in[1,3]
),
learning rate (
learning_rate

\displaystyle\in[0.01,0.3]
, log-scale), minimum loss reduction
for splits (
min_split_loss

\displaystyle\in[0.0,5.0]
), and minimum child weight

\displaystyle\in[1,100]

Random Forest

max_depth

\displaystyle\in[3,50]

\displaystyle\in[2,150]

All models were configured without hyperparameter tuning, using permissive settings (a large maximum depth/iterations and a minimum samples per leaf of 1) to ensure the runtime reflects the inherent algorithmic complexity rather than early stopping behavior.

Figure

presents the training time as a function of sample size on a log-log scale. Partition Tree consistently achieves the fastest training times across all sample sizes, being approximately
\displaystyle 5\times
faster than CADET and
\displaystyle 25\times

. The shaded regions indicate the range between the minimum and maximum observed runtimes across runs.

Appendix F
Gain-based Feature Importance

Partition Trees
retain the interpretability advantages of classical decision trees: each internal node applies a single-coordinate test, and the learned model is a piecewise-constant conditional density on the induced leaves.

To summarize which
covariates
drive the conditional density estimate, we use a gain-based feature importance that is aligned with the training objective.
Recall that every accepted split is chosen to maximize the empirical log-loss gain
\displaystyle\widehat{G}
(
Section

2.4.2
). For a fitted tree
\displaystyle T
and a covariate coordinate
\displaystyle j\in\{1,\dots,d_{x}\}
, define the unnormalized importance

\widetilde{I}_{j}(T):=\sum_{s\in\mathrm{Int}(T)}\widehat{G}_{s}\;\mathbf{1}\{\,\text{split }s\text{ uses }X_{j}\,\},

where
\displaystyle\mathrm{Int}(T)
is the set of internal nodes and
\displaystyle\widehat{G}_{s}
is the
gain realized at node
\displaystyle s
. We then report the normalized importance

I_{j}(T):=\frac{\widetilde{I}_{j}(T)}{\sum_{s\in\mathrm{Int}(T)}\widehat{G}_{s}},\qquad\sum_{j=1}^{d_{x}}I_{j}(T)=1.

This definition matches the standard “impurity decrease” importances used for
CART, with
\displaystyle\widehat{G}
replacing the classification or regression impurity.
Because Partition Trees also allow
\displaystyle Y
-splits to refine the outcome partition,
we restrict
\displaystyle I_{j}
to
\displaystyle X
-splits only
so that importances reflect how the
model adapts to the covariates (rather than how it discretizes the outcome).

F.1
Comparison with CADET importances

CADET selects splits using a cross-entropy criterion, so its gain has the same
log-loss structure as ours. Empirically, the resulting importances are closely
aligned across regression benchmarks: Figure
5

plots CADET importances against Partition Tree importances (each point is one
feature in one dataset), and most points concentrate near the equality line,
indicating broad agreement in which covariates matter and at what scale.

.
