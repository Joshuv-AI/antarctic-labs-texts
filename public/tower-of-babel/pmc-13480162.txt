Beyond the Physical Symptoms: An Exploratory Single‑Case Psychological Profile of an Adult with HSAN‑II
Authors: Arash Jelodari, Laleh Hosseini
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AimHereditary Sensory and Autonomic Neuropathy Type II (HSAN‑II) is an extremely rare autosomal recessive disorder characterized by progressive sensory loss, recurrent ulcers, and frequent amputations. Despite its severe physical consequences, the psychological impact of HSAN‑II remains largely unexplored. This exploratory single‑case report presents the psychological profile of a 31‑year‑old Iranian man diagnosed with HSAN‑II.MethodsA semi‑structured clinical interview and a battery of standardized psychological instruments were administered, including the Interpersonal Communication Skills Questionnaire, Rosenberg Self‑Esteem Scale, Differentiation of Self Inventory, Ryff’s Psychological Well‑Being Scale (18‑item version), and Body Image Concern Inventory.FindingsResults revealed profound impairments across multiple domains: low interpersonal communication skills (total score = 121), low self-esteem (score = 18), poor self-differentiation (total score = 145), markedly reduced psychological well-being (total score = 49), and clinically significant body image concerns (total score = 76). Qualitative findings indicated that prolonged hospitalizations, social isolation during critical developmental periods, and visible physical deformities (including multiple amputations) were major contributing factors to these psychological difficulties.ConclusionThis preliminary single‑case evidence highlights the substantial psychosocial burden associated with HSAN‑II and underscores the need for routine psychological screening and multidisciplinary care in patients with rare genetic neuropathies. Although findings are not generalizable, they point to the potential value of early psychosocial interventions targeting body image, self‑esteem, and social skills.

Introduction

Hereditary Sensory and Autonomic Neuropathy Type II (HSAN-II), also known as HSAN2, is a rare autosomal recessive disorder characterized by early-onset, progressive sensory loss primarily affecting the distal limbs in a “glove and stocking” distribution. The condition typically manifests in infancy or early childhood with reduced or absent sensation to pain, temperature, and touch, leading to recurrent painless ulcers, chronic infections, osteomyelitis, and progressive mutilating deformities, often necessitating multiple amputations of fingers and toes.1,2

Although the exact global prevalence remains unknown, HSAN-II is considered extremely rare, with only a few hundred cases reported worldwide.1,3 Due to the loss of protective sensation, patients frequently experience unnoticed injuries that progress to severe complications, resulting in repeated and prolonged hospitalizations, restricted mobility, and significant lifestyle limitations from an early age.

From a clinical perspective, HSAN‑II represents a complex, lifelong condition in which physical symptoms interact continuously with developmental, social, and psychological processes. The combination of early-onset disability, visible bodily deformities, and chronic medical interventions places individuals at heightened risk for psychosocial distress.4,5 Research on comparable rare neuropathies and congenital sensory disorders shows that early physical impairment often disrupts identity formation, peer bonding, autonomy development, and body image consolidation, core developmental tasks that shape long-term mental health outcomes.6,7

The chronic and debilitating physical consequences of HSAN-II, including visible disfigurements, functional impairments, and social isolation, are likely to impose a substantial psychological burden. Patients often face prolonged periods of hospitalization during critical developmental stages, limited peer interactions, and challenges in forming intimate relationships, all of which may adversely affect self-esteem, body image, psychological well-being, and interpersonal functioning. Despite these plausible psychosocial impacts, the psychological aspects of HSAN-II have been almost entirely neglected in the scientific literature. To date, no systematic psychological studies or detailed case reports focusing on the mental health profile of individuals with this condition have been published.2

From a psychosocial standpoint, single-case studies in rare diseases play a crucial role in expanding clinical understanding. Because large-scale studies are often impossible, detailed psychological case reports provide foundational evidence that helps clinicians recognize patterns of distress, anticipate psychosocial needs, and design early interventions.8 This case therefore contributes not only to HSAN‑II literature but also to broader disability psychology by illustrating how chronic sensory loss, repeated amputations, and long-term medical dependency shape emotional functioning and interpersonal development.

The present case report aims to address this gap by providing a comprehensive psychological profile of a 31-year-old Iranian man diagnosed with HSAN-II. Using a combination of semi-structured clinical interview and standardized psychometric instruments, this study examines key psychological domains including interpersonal communication skills, self-esteem, differentiation of self, psychological well-being, and body image.

Method

Case Presentation

The participant was a 31-year-old single Iranian man with a master’s degree in computer science, working as a self-employed information technology (IT) specialist. He is the eldest child of three siblings (one older sister and one younger brother). He was diagnosed with Hereditary Sensory and Autonomic Neuropathy Type II (HSAN-II) in early childhood based on clinical manifestations and genetic testing. The patient has a history of multiple hospitalizations due to recurrent ulcers and infections, resulting in several amputations of toes and fingers as well as visible deformities in the hands and feet.

Ethical Considerations

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The research protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of Islamic Azad University, Ahvaz Branch (Approval ID: IR.IAU.AHVAZ.REC.1400.030). The study took place at the researcher’s private psychology clinic in Ahvaz, Iran. Written informed consent was obtained from the participant prior to the interview and questionnaire administration. The participant was fully informed about the purpose of the study, voluntary participation, the right to withdraw at any time without any consequences, and the confidentiality of all personal information. Institutional approval covered both the conduct of the study and the publication of the case details. No additional institutional permission was required for dissemination of the anonymized case information. To protect anonymity, all potentially identifying details have been removed or generalized in this report.

Consent Statement

The authors confirm that written informed consent for both participation in the psychological assessment and publication of all case‑related information was obtained from the patient. The patient received a full explanation of the study purpose, assessment procedures, potential risks, confidentiality safeguards, and the nature and scope of the information to be published. Written consent was obtained prior to data collection and manuscript preparation.

Procedure

Data collection was carried out in 2019 during three sessions at the researcher’s private clinic. In the first session, a semi-structured clinical interview was conducted by the first author, a licensed clinical psychologist with over 10 years of experience. The interview lasted approximately 90 minutes and was audio-recorded with the participant’s permission. Following the interview, the participant completed a battery of standardized self-report questionnaires in the subsequent sessions.

Semi-Structured Interview Guide

The interview was designed to explore the patient’s lived experiences across key psychological domains. Main questions included: Can you describe your experience of living with HSAN-II since childhood?How has the disease affected your daily life, education, and occupation?What are your experiences regarding social relationships and friendships?How do you perceive your body and physical appearance? Have the amputations and deformities affected your self-image?How would you describe your relationship with your family members and the level of your independence?What challenges have you faced in hospitals and during long periods of isolation?How do you evaluate your overall quality of life and future outlook?Have you experienced any significant emotional difficulties or psychological challenges related to your condition?

Data Analysis

Qualitative data from the interview were analyzed using thematic analysis.9 Quantitative data were scored according to the standard scoring protocols of each instrument. No statistical tests were performed due to the single-case design.

Items are rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (never) to 5 (very much). Total scores range from 54 to 270, with higher scores indicating stronger interpersonal communication skills. Psychometric evaluations in Iranian samples have demonstrated satisfactory reliability, with Cronbach’s α reported as 0.8413 and 0.75 in later studies.14 Convergent validity has been supported through significant associations with related constructs, including a negative correlation with the Death Obsession Scale (r = −0.34, p <0.01).13Differentiation of Self Inventory (DSI): The Differentiation of Self Inventory (DSI)15 assesses emotional and cognitive autonomy within interpersonal relationships. Findings

The results of the current case study are presented in two sections: qualitative findings derived from the semi-structured interview and quantitative findings from the standardized questionnaires.

Qualitative Findings

Thematic analysis of the semi-structured interview revealed several overarching themes that illustrate the profound psychological impact of HSAN-II on the patient’s life.

Chronic Hospitalization and Social Isolation

The patient reported spending significant portions of his childhood and adolescence in hospitals, with 2 to 4 admissions per year, each lasting at least 30 days. He described these prolonged isolations as severely limiting normal social development: “I missed out on playing with friends, going to parties, and even regular school life”. As an adult, he continued to experience restricted social participation, with limited attendance at university events or recreational activities due to fear of injury and infection.

Body Image Distress and Visible Deformities

A dominant theme was intense dissatisfaction with physical appearance. The patient has undergone multiple amputations of toes and fingers and lives with noticeable deformities in his hands and feet. He expressed deep shame and embarrassment: “I avoid looking at my hands and feet. I feel deformed and unattractive”. This distress significantly affected his willingness to engage in social and romantic relationships, particularly with the opposite sex. He stated that his physical condition was the primary barrier to forming intimate relationships.

Low Self-Esteem and Negative Self-Perception

The patient described a pervasive sense of inferiority compared to peers. He recounted experiences of bullying and humiliation by classmates and even some teachers during adolescence due to his visible disabilities and frequent absences. These experiences led to chronic self-criticism and feelings of worthlessness. He reported constant comparison with others, resulting in a sharply diminished sense of self-worth.

Emotional Dependence and Low Differentiation of Self

Strong emotional and practical dependence on his family was evident. The patient explained that due to safety concerns, his parents adopted an overprotective (“helicopter”) parenting style. Even as an adult, he feels highly dependent: “I still rely on them for many things… I feel incomplete without their guidance”. He demonstrated difficulty in expressing emotions and maintaining an autonomous “I-position” in relationships.

Diminished Psychological Well-Being and Future Outlook

The patient expressed a pessimistic view of his future, with limited aspirations in personal, occupational, and social domains. He described his life as constrained by constant medical vigilance, leading to reduced purpose, autonomy, and personal growth. Positive relations with others outside the immediate family were notably scarce.

These qualitative themes were highly consistent with the quantitative results presented below.

Discussion

The present case report provides one of the first comprehensive psychological profiles of an adult living with Hereditary Sensory and Autonomic Neuropathy Type II (HSAN-II), a rare genetic disorder whose psychosocial consequences have been largely overlooked in the literature. The integration of qualitative interview data with standardized psychological assessments revealed a coherent pattern of significant impairments across interpersonal communication, self-esteem, differentiation of self, psychological well-being, and body image. These findings illustrate how the chronic medical complications of HSAN-II, particularly recurrent infections, prolonged hospitalizations, and visible deformities, shape long-term psychological functioning.

These findings are also consistent with broader disability and rehabilitation psychology literature, which emphasizes that early-onset physical impairment and chronic medical dependency can disrupt normative developmental trajectories and increase vulnerability to long-term psychosocial distress. Studies on rare genetic disorders and chronic pediatric conditions transitioning into adulthood show that individuals often experience reduced autonomy, limited social participation, and persistent identity challenges.25,26 Research in rehabilitation psychology further indicates that repeated medical interventions and functional limitations can contribute to diminished self-efficacy, restricted life goals, and heightened emotional burden.27 Integrating these perspectives suggests that the patient’s psychological profile reflects not only the direct consequences of HSAN‑II but also the cumulative developmental impact of living with a rare, disabling condition from childhood into adulthood.

A central theme emerging from the patient’s narrative was the profound impact of chronic hospitalization and social isolation throughout childhood and adolescence. The patient reported 2–4 hospitalizations per year, each lasting at least one month, which substantially disrupted normative developmental processes such as peer bonding, social learning, and identity formation. These experiences align with evidence showing that early and repeated medical isolation increases vulnerability to social withdrawal, reduced social competence, and internalizing symptoms.28 The patient’s low scores on the Interpersonal Communication Skills Questionnaire are consistent with this developmental trajectory, reflecting limited opportunities to practice and internalize effective communication behaviors.

Body image distress emerged as one of the most salient psychological challenges. Multiple amputations and visible deformities of the hands and feet contributed to pervasive shame, avoidance of social situations, and reluctance to pursue romantic relationships. The patient’s BICI score fell within the clinical range, indicating clinically significant body image concerns. These findings are consistent with research demonstrating that individuals with visible physical differences or limb loss often experience heightened self-consciousness, reduced self-esteem, and impaired relational functioning.29–31 In this case, body image dissatisfaction appeared to function as a core mechanism linking physical disability to broader psychosocial difficulties.

Recent research on body image disturbance following limb loss further supports this interpretation. Post‑amputation studies show that visible differences, functional limitations, and social stigma contribute to heightened self-consciousness, avoidance behaviors, and reduced relational confidence.32,33 These findings align closely with the patient’s experiences of shame, social withdrawal, and reluctance to pursue intimate relationships, suggesting that his body image concerns may represent a central mechanism through which physical disability influences broader emotional and interpersonal functioning.

The patient also demonstrated low differentiation of self, characterized by emotional dependence on family members and difficulty maintaining an autonomous identity. His parents’ long-term overprotective behavior motivated by legitimate medical concerns likely contributed to restricted autonomy and limited development of emotional regulation skills. This pattern is consistent with literature showing that chronic childhood illness often leads to parental overprotection, which in turn hinders autonomy development and increases emotional vulnerability.34 The patient’s low scores on the Differentiation of Self Inventory further support this interpretation.

Although the patient’s psychological difficulties appear closely tied to his medical history, alternative explanations should also be considered. Personality traits such as introversion, harm avoidance, or dependency may have contributed to reduced social engagement or emotional vulnerability. Family dynamics, including parental overprotection, enmeshment, or limited emotional expressiveness, may also have shaped the patient’s interpersonal style and self-concept. Cultural factors, particularly norms surrounding disability, physical appearance, and family interdependence in Iranian contexts, may further influence self-esteem and autonomy development.35 Socioeconomic constraints, including limited access to rehabilitation services or adaptive equipment, may also exacerbate functional limitations and reduce opportunities for social participation. These alternative explanations highlight the importance of interpreting the findings within a broader biopsychosocial framework.

Across all domains of psychological well-being, the patient scored in the low range, particularly in autonomy, self-acceptance, and positive relations with others. These findings reflect the cumulative burden of lifelong medical complications, restricted mobility, and limited social participation. Research on individuals with rare chronic conditions similarly indicates reduced life satisfaction, diminished sense of purpose, and limited opportunities for personal growth.36 The patient’s pessimistic future outlook and restricted aspirations appear to be shaped by both the unpredictability of his medical condition and the absence of supportive social experiences that typically foster resilience.

Taken together, the findings highlight the multidimensional psychological burden associated with HSAN-II. Although the neurological and physical manifestations of HSAN-II are well documented, its psychosocial consequences remain underexplored. This case demonstrates that psychological difficulties are not secondary or incidental but represent integral components of the lived experience of individuals with HSAN-II. The convergence of qualitative and quantitative data underscores the need for systematic psychological assessment and tailored interventions for this population.

It is also important to interpret the questionnaire scores in light of the study’s methodological limitations. Some low scores, particularly in autonomy, environmental mastery, and interpersonal functioning, may partially reflect physical restrictions rather than purely psychological dysfunction. The reliance on self-report measures may have amplified distress or minimized strengths due to emotional state or limited insight. Additionally, the absence of disease-specific psychological tools and clinician-rated assessments restricts the ability to distinguish between disability-related limitations and underlying psychological processes. These considerations underscore the need for cautious interpretation and highlight the value of future studies incorporating multimethod assessment and disability-sensitive instruments.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged when interpreting the findings of this case report. First, the study is based on a single participant, which inherently restricts the generalizability of the results. Although the depth of qualitative and quantitative data provides valuable insight into the psychological profile of an individual with HSAN‑II, the experiences of this patient may not fully represent the broader population of individuals with this rare condition. Future research involving multiple cases or cross‑case comparisons is needed to determine whether the patterns observed here are consistent across patients.

Second, the study relied primarily on self-report measures, which may be influenced by response biases such as social desirability, emotional distress, or limited insight. Although standardized instruments were used, the absence of clinician-rated or behavioral assessments limits the ability to triangulate findings across multiple data sources. Previous literature has noted that brain scintigraphic imaging for HSAN-IV provides a clearer view of changes in activity across different brain regions;37 therefore, scheduling brain imaging should be a future direction for assessment.

Third, the psychological instruments employed, while validated in Iranian samples, were not specifically designed for populations with rare neuropathies or severe physical disabilities. As a result, some constructs (eg, autonomy, interpersonal functioning) may be influenced by physical limitations rather than purely psychological factors, complicating interpretation.

Fourth, the data were collected in 2019, and the patient’s psychological functioning may have changed since then due to disease progression, life events, or psychosocial adaptation. Longitudinal follow-up would provide a more dynamic understanding of psychological trajectories in HSAN‑II.

Finally, the study did not include genetic subtype analysis, family functioning assessments, or medical severity indices, which could have enriched the interpretation of psychological outcomes. Integrating multidisciplinary data (neurological, genetic, social, and psychological) would allow for a more comprehensive understanding of the lived experience of HSAN‑II.

Clinical Implications

Despite these limitations, the findings of this case report offer several important clinical implications for the assessment and treatment of individuals with HSAN-II and similar rare neuropathies.

First, the results underscore the need for routine psychological screening as part of standard medical care. The patient exhibited clinically significant impairments across multiple domains suggesting that psychological difficulties are not secondary but central components of the HSAN-II experience. Early identification of these challenges may prevent long-term psychosocial complications.

Second, the prominence of body image distress highlights the importance of integrating interventions that specifically target appearance-related concerns. Evidence-based approaches such as cognitive-behavioral therapy for body image, compassion-focused therapy, and acceptance-based interventions may help patients reduce shame, challenge maladaptive beliefs, and increase social engagement.

Third, the patient’s low differentiation of self and emotional dependence on family members suggest that family-based interventions may be beneficial. Psychoeducation for parents regarding autonomy-supportive caregiving, as well as structured family therapy, may help reduce overprotective patterns and promote healthier independence.

Fourth, the patient’s limited social experiences and low interpersonal communication skills indicate a need for social skills training, peer support groups, and structured opportunities for safe social participation. Programs designed for individuals with chronic illnesses or disabilities may help reduce isolation and foster a sense of belonging.

Fifth, the patient’s pessimistic future orientation and reduced sense of purpose point to the value of meaning-centered and resilience-based interventions, which can help individuals reconstruct a coherent life narrative, identify personal strengths, and cultivate long-term goals despite physical limitations.

Finally, the findings highlight the importance of multidisciplinary care models that integrate neurology, rehabilitation, psychology, and social work. Given the complex interplay between physical disability, social isolation, and emotional functioning, a coordinated approach is essential to improving quality of life for individuals with HSAN-II.

Conclusion

This exploratory single‑case report offers preliminary insight into the potential psychological challenges experienced by individuals living with HSAN‑II. In this case, the patient exhibited clinically significant difficulties in communication skills, self‑esteem, differentiation of self, psychological well‑being, and body image—patterns that appeared closely linked to lifelong medical complications, repeated hospitalizations, and restricted social participation. While these findings are informative, they reflect the experiences of a single individual and therefore cannot be generalized to all patients with HSAN‑II.

The observations from this case suggest that individuals with rare neuropathies may benefit from multidisciplinary care models that include routine psychosocial assessment and supportive interventions. Approaches targeting body image concerns, self‑esteem, autonomy development, and social skills could be helpful, particularly when introduced early in life. However, these implications should be interpreted cautiously, as broader evidence is needed to determine whether similar psychological profiles are common among others with HSAN‑II.

Given the scarcity of psychological research on HSAN‑II, larger multi‑case studies and longitudinal investigations are essential to clarify the psychosocial trajectories of affected individuals and to inform the development of evidence‑based interventions tailored to this rare population.
