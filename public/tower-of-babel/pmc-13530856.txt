Internal Root Resorption in a Previously Endodontically Treated Maxillary Molar: Nonsurgical Retreatment With Cold Ceramic
Authors: Ali Sharbatdar, Maryam Gharechahi, Paria Molaei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTInternal root resorption in a previously endodontically treated maxillary molar is an exceptionally rare finding. This case demonstrates that CBCT is essential for accurate lesion characterization, and obturation with Cold Ceramic yielded a favorable short‐term radiographic stabilization at 15‐month follow‐up in the nonsurgical retreatment of IRR; longer‐term follow‐up is required.

Introduction

Root resorption involves the destruction of dentin and cementum through either physiologic or pathologic mechanisms, with the underlying process driven by osteoclastic activity at the root surface in response to a variety of triggering stimuli [1]. Broadly, root resorption is categorized as either internal or external depending on its anatomical origin relative to the root surface. Internal root resorption (IRR) is a pathological process initiated along the root canal wall, causing progressive deterioration of radicular dentin within the canal system. It is classified into two distinct subtypes: inflammatory IRR, in which the pulp chamber or root canal enlarges into an ovoid or fusiform shape, spreading apically and laterally, and replacement IRR, an uncommon variant characterized by uneven canal enlargement with metaplastic changes, heterogeneous radiopacities and radiolucencies, and potential obliteration of the canal space by cancellous‐like bone [2, 3].

The precise pathogenic mechanism of IRR remains incompletely understood. It is widely accepted that initiation depends on damage to the odontoblastic layer and unmineralized predentin, exposing underlying mineralized dentin to odontoclasts. Implicated etiological factors include dental trauma, periodontal infections, caries‐related pulpitis, excessive procedural heat, orthodontic treatment, calcium hydroxide pulp capping, cracked teeth, and idiopathic dystrophic changes [2]. These stimuli activate pulp tissue, triggering inflammatory reactions that convert undifferentiated pulp cells into osteoclasts or macrophages via the OPG/RANK/RANKL signaling pathway, sustaining progressive dentin destruction [4].

A large‐scale CBCT‐based study identified root resorption in 15.7% of 1086 consecutive patients, with IRR accounting for 9.6% of all resorption types and demonstrating the highest prevalence in anterior teeth (15.6%), followed by premolars (8.3%) and molars (4.0%) [1]. Importantly, teeth without prior endodontic treatment showed significantly higher frequencies of IRR compared to previously treated teeth, whereas endodontically treated teeth were disproportionately affected by infection‐induced apical resorption [1]. The present case report describes the diagnosis and nonsurgical retreatment of a maxillary molar with extensive IRR confined to the palatal root in a tooth previously endodontically treated with Cold Ceramic.

Case Presentation

A 20‐year‐old male was referred to the Endodontics Department of Mashhad University of Medical Sciences with the chief complaint of chronic pain. Medical history was not contributory. He reported no history of dental trauma and no history of orthodontic treatment. The chief complaint had been present for several months, although the patient could not specify the exact date of onset. Root canal treatment of the maxillary left first molar had been performed several years previously at another clinic; no records of that treatment were available and the patient was unable to recall the precise interval.

The tooth was not responsive to electric pulp test, cold test, or heat test. Furthermore, the patient reported no discomfort on palpation, although the tooth was sensitive to percussion. Periapical radiography revealed an oval‐shaped radiolucent enlargement within the palatal root, and IRR was suspected (Figure 1). The root fillings of the remaining canals were assessed as inadequate. On both the periapical radiograph and CBCT, the fillings demonstrated voids and poor adaptation to the canal walls.

Preoperative clinical and radiographic assessment. The periapical radiograph demonstrated an oval‐shaped radiolucent enlargement within the palatal root, characteristic of internal root resorption (IRR). Inadequate previous endodontic treatment and poor adaptation of the filling material within the root canals are evident across all three canals.

CBCT was acquired to determine the true nature and extent of the resorptive lesion and to exclude perforation. In the palatal root, an ovoid resorptive defect confluent with the root canal lumen was identified, extending approximately 6–7 mm coronally from the apical foramen on coronal reconstructions and thus involving the apical and middle thirds of the root (Figure 2A,B). Its maximum buccolingual dimension measured approximately 2.5 mm at the widest coronal and sagittal levels (Figure 2A,B). Consequently, the dentinal walls of the palatal root were markedly thinned, the residual thickness at the narrowest point measuring 0.67 mm in the apical third and 0.81–0.95 mm in the middle third on axial sections (Figure 3). Serial axial sections through the entire coronoapical extent of the lesion demonstrated a continuous layer of dentin circumscribing the defect at all levels, with an uninterrupted external root surface and no communication between the resorptive lacuna and the periodontal ligament space. Perforation of the palatal root was therefore excluded. All three roots exhibited apical infection‐induced resorption, with blunting of the root apices and enlargement of the apical foramina (Figure 2). No MB2 canal was identified. The tooth was diagnosed as a previously treated first maxillary molar with symptomatic apical periodontitis. Therefore, it was decided to initiate retreatment of the root canals. Written informed consent was obtained from the patient.

Differential Diagnosis, Investigation, and Treatment

Following the administration of infiltration local anesthesia using 2% lidocaine with 1:100,000 epinephrine (Daroupakhsh Pharmaceutical Co., Tehran, Iran), the tooth was isolated with a rubber dam and the previous coronal restoration was removed. A conventional endodontic access cavity was prepared, which revealed profuse bleeding and granulation tissue, consistent with active internal inflammatory resorption. Removal of the existing root canal filling material was accomplished using Gates Glidden drills, Hedström files (Mani Inc., Tochigi, Japan), and the Ultra X ultrasonic activator (Eighteeth Medical, Changzhou Sifary Medical Technology Co. Ltd., China). The granulation tissue was subsequently extirpated using ultrasonic instrumentation in conjunction with copious irrigation using 5.25% sodium hypochlorite (NaOCl), with a total irrigant volume of approximately 40 mL. A ledge was identified in the mesiobuccal canal, which was successfully negotiated and bypassed using a size #10 stainless steel hand file (Mani Inc., Tochigi, Japan). Mechanical instrumentation was completed in the mesiobuccal and distobuccal canals to a final apical size of #30 with a 4% taper, and the palatal canal to #40 with a 4% taper, with particular emphasis placed on thorough chemical debridement using sodium hypochlorite and 17% ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA; Morvabon, Tehran, Iran). At the end of the first appointment, calcium hydroxide paste was placed as an intracanal medicament, and the access cavity was sealed with Cavit temporary restorative material (3 M ESPE, Seefeld, Germany). The patient was reviewed after 1 week.

At the second appointment, the temporary restoration was removed and the calcium hydroxide dressing was thoroughly irrigated from the canal system. Given the compromised dentinal wall thickness of the palatal root, the presence of extensive IRR, and associated apical root resorption, a decision was made to obturate the palatal canal up to the coronal extent of the resorptive defect (approximately 6–7 mm from the apical foramen, corresponding to cut 10 in Figure 2A) with Cold Ceramic bioceramic cement (SJM Co., Tehran, Iran). The Cold Ceramic powder and liquid were proportioned and mixed according to the manufacturer's instructions, and the material was delivered into the palatal canal using the MAP System (Produits Dentaires SA, Vevey, Switzerland). Compaction was achieved using finger and hand pluggers to ensure adequate adaptation of the material to the irregular resorptive defect walls. In view of the large canal volume and to facilitate the setting reaction of the bioceramic material through moisture provision, a sterile paper cone (#80) was pre‐moistened with sterile water and placed passively within the canal in direct contact with the Cold Ceramic, without further manipulation, to minimize the risk of displacing or contaminating the material. The remaining canals, which also demonstrated varying degrees of apical resorption, were obturated with Cold Ceramic to the full working length (approximately 17–18 mm). A sterilized moistened cotton pellet was placed in the pulp chamber, a temporary restoration was placed, and the patient was re‐appointed after 48 h. In the presence of moisture, Cold Ceramic sets initially within 15 min and reaches complete set within 24 h [5]. Since the patient was unable to return for the 24‐h appointment, the next session was scheduled at 48 h.

At the third appointment, the temporary restoration and the paper cone were removed and it was checked that the Cold Ceramic in the palatal canal was hardened. Then, the remaining coronal portion of the canal was filled using injectable thermoplasticized gutta‐percha in conjunction with CC Sealer (SJM Co., Tehran, Iran), a calcium silicate‐based root canal sealer from the same manufacturer. A periapical radiograph was obtained immediately following obturation to verify the density and adequacy of the root canal filling (Figure 4A). The patient was subsequently referred for definitive coronal restoration.

Conclusion and Results (Outcome and Follow‐Up)

At the 15‐month follow‐up appointment, the tooth remained asymptomatic and functional. Periapical radiograph and CBCT indicated an early‐stage stabilization of the IRR defect and the apical resorption lesions (Figure 4B–D).

Discussion

The present case report describes the diagnosis and nonsurgical endodontic retreatment of a maxillary first molar presenting with extensive IRR affecting the palatal root and varying degrees of apical resorption in the remaining canals, in a tooth with a history of prior root canal treatment.

Several case reports have documented the endodontic management of molars with IRR due to chronic pulp inflammation or hyperparathyroidism [6, 7, 8, 9, 10]. However, IRR in previously endodontically treated posterior teeth is rare and poorly documented [1]. According to Dao et al. [1], teeth with prior endodontic treatment were significantly more likely to be affected by apical infection‐induced resorption (43.1%) than teeth without prior endodontic treatment (3.7%). Data specific to previously treated teeth remain scarce. In a CBCT survey of 609 root‐filled teeth, root resorption of any type was identified in 6.57%, of which 18.92% were internal, and the affected teeth were mostly molars (83.78%) [11].

In the present case, several recognized precipitants of IRR could be excluded on the basis of the history. The patient reported neither dental trauma nor orthodontic treatment, and the medical history was non‐contributory. IRR is dependent on the presence of vital pulp tissue capable of nourishing the clastic cell population, and unlike external resorption, it is generally considered to cease once the pulp becomes wholly necrotic or is extirpated during endodontic treatment [12]. The persistence of an actively resorbing lesion in a tooth treated several years previously may therefore imply that vital tissue had survived the original procedure. The finding of profuse hemorrhage and granulation tissue on access indicates that vital, chronically inflamed connective tissue was present within the resorptive lacuna, implying that pulpal tissue capable of sustaining a clastic response had persisted since the original procedure. Persistent low‐grade inflammation in such retained tissue may provide a sustained stimulus for the recruitment and activation of odontoclasts on a predentin surface denuded by chronic irritation. This interpretation is consistent with the observation of Nistor et al. [11], who reported that the internal resorptive defects identified in their series of root‐filled teeth occurred in incompletely obturated or missed canals and had not been addressed by the previous endodontic treatment. A comparable mechanism has been described in two contralateral mandibular first molars in which apical IRR persisted after root canal treatment characterized by short, underfilled root fillings and in which mild bleeding was again encountered from the resorptive defects on re‐entry [12]. In the present case, the technically inadequate previous root filling would have provided both the residual vital tissue and the continuous microbial stimulus required for the resorptive process to remain active.

IRR is typically asymptomatic and detected incidentally on routine radiographic examination; however, symptoms of acute or chronic pulpitis, apical periodontitis, percussion tenderness, or abscess formation may arise depending on pulp vitality, the extent of resorption, and its location [13]. In the present case, the patient presented with chronic pain and tenderness to percussion, a clinical presentation that, in the context of a previously endodontically treated tooth, initially suggested persistent or secondary apical periodontitis rather than active IRR. This underscores the diagnostic complexity of IRR in teeth with prior root canal treatment, where the expected absence of pulp vitality may mask the classical symptomatic picture.

In the present case, CBCT was obtained to assess the extent of the lesion and to identify the MB2 canal. According to the CBCT findings, the tooth lacked an MB2 canal and showed no evidence of root canal wall perforation. Furthermore, varying degrees of apical root resorption were evident in the mesiobuccal and distobuccal canals. High‐resolution CBCT is recommended to investigate the exact nature of IRR lesions, as it can accurately confirm the extent of the defect and the presence or absence of root canal wall perforation. Several studies have confirmed that CBCT improves the diagnosis and treatment planning of root resorption when compared to conventional radiographs [2].

The primary therapeutic objective was the thorough debridement of resorptive tissue, effective disinfection of the root canal system, and sealing of the resorptive defect to prevent reinfection. The shape of the resorption defect usually renders it inaccessible to direct mechanical instrumentation; therefore, emphasis should be placed on chemical debridement. NaOCl serves as the primary irrigant due to its tissue‐dissolving properties on necrotic debris, biofilms, and organic components in IRR defects, which are often inaccessible to direct instrumentation. It excels at breaking down vital and necrotic pulp tissue but cannot remove the inorganic smear layer alone. Ultrasonic activation enhances NaOCl by generating acoustic streaming and cavitation, improving irrigant penetration into irregular IRR defects and complex canal areas [8, 14, 15]. Calcium hydroxide was also employed as an intracanal medicament owing to its potent antimicrobial activity and remineralizing potential [16].

Finally, a proper root‐filling material was selected to obturate the canal system. Because of intracanal irregularity, IRR defects can be challenging to obturate [17]. For obturation of IRR lesions without perforation, thermoplasticized techniques are recommended, with warm vertical compaction demonstrating superiority over lateral condensation and carrier‐based systems [2]. Where perforation has occurred, bioactive hydraulic silicate cements are indicated for perforation repair, and a hybrid technique combining apical gutta‐percha with a bioactive silicate cement at the resorptive defect is recommended [2]. However, in the present case, given the presence of apical root resorption in all three canals and the inherent risk of material extrusion with thermoplasticized techniques in the absence of an intact apical stop, calcium silicate cement was selected as the primary obturation material throughout, exploiting its dimensional stability and bioactivity. Several materials are available for this purpose, including mineral trioxide aggregate (MTA) and Biodentine [18]. Cold Ceramic is nontoxic and biocompatible with host tissues, and exhibits good handling characteristics and consistency after mixing [5]. According to published data, Cold Ceramic's sealing ability is comparable to MTA in dry and saliva‐contaminated situations and superior to MTA in blood‐contaminated conditions. The setting time of Cold Ceramic is shorter than that of MTA, reported to be approximately 15 and 165 min, respectively. Additionally, no significant difference between MTA and Cold Ceramic regarding tooth discoloration has been reported [5]. Asgari and Hajihassani [19] reported the successful management of a perforating IRR in a maxillary dens invaginatus lateral incisor in a 13‐year‐old patient.

At the 15‐month follow‐up, the tooth remained asymptomatic and fully functional, with periapical radiography demonstrating an early‐stage stabilization of the resorptive process. This outcome is consistent with reports in the literature demonstrating a favorable short‐term prognosis following nonsurgical retreatment of IRR when thorough debridement and adequate obturation are achieved [13, 20]. Longer observation is nonetheless required before healing can be regarded as established: in the comparable series of previously treated molars with apical IRR, symptoms resolved within 1 week but complete radiographic resolution of the periapical lesions was documented at the 5‐year recall [12]. Nevertheless, a 24‐ to 48‐month observation period is warranted.

This report has several limitations inherent to the retrospective assessment of a referred case. Records of the original endodontic treatment were unavailable, and the patient could not specify either the interval since that treatment or the precise onset of symptoms; consequently, the rate of progression of the resorptive lesion and its temporal relationship to the initial procedure cannot be established. The etiological interpretation advanced here is therefore inferential rather than demonstrated.
