HAIC-Based Combination Therapy for Advanced Hepatocellular Carcinoma with Vp4 Portal Vein Tumor Thrombus and Child-Pugh B Liver Function: A Case Report
Authors: Pan Xiao, Ke-Xin Chen, Li Zhang, Lian-Dong Shi, You-Fu Bin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAdvanced hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) with Vp4/main portal vein tumor thrombus (PVTT) and Child-Pugh B liver function remains difficult to manage because patients with impaired hepatic reserve are vulnerable to hepatic decompensation and are underrepresented in pivotal systemic therapy trials. We report a 57-year-old man with hepatitis B virus-related cirrhosis, advanced HCC, Vp4 PVTT, BCLC stage C/CNLC stage IIIa disease, Child-Pugh B7 liver function, and ECOG performance status 1. Baseline contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) showed multifocal confluent intrahepatic tumors forming a measurable target mass of 91 mm × 71 mm. After multidisciplinary evaluation, the patient received modified FOLFOX-based hepatic arterial infusion chemotherapy (HAIC) every 3 weeks for four cycles, combined with lenvatinib (8 mg once daily), camrelizumab (200 mg every 3 weeks), and entecavir. After four cycles, the confluent target lesion decreased to 46 mm × 45 mm, alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) decreased from 656 ng/mL to 3.58 ng/mL (normal range, 0–7 ng/mL), and PVTT burden decreased radiologically. Ascites present at baseline resolved during treatment. At 34 months, the residual lesion measured 41 mm × 28 mm, AFP remained within the normal range, no new intrahepatic lesions were detected, and PVTT remained controlled, although a small amount of ascites was again detected. A retrospective RECIST version 1.1 assessment classified the best overall response as partial response, which was sustained at the 34-month follow-up. The Child-Pugh score was B7 at baseline, improved to A5 after ascites resolution, and was A6 at the 34-month follow-up. No grade 3 or higher treatment-related adverse events were documented. This case does not establish treatment efficacy but illustrates a hypothesis-generating approach to patient selection, non-embolization-based locoregional therapy, antiviral treatment, and longitudinal safety monitoring in a high-risk HCC subgroup for whom prospective evidence is limited.

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) with main portal vein tumor thrombus (PVTT) represents advanced-stage disease characterized by major vascular invasion, high intrahepatic tumor burden, and limited treatment tolerance.1,2 The therapeutic challenge is particularly substantial in patients with impaired liver reserve.

Locoregional-systemic strategies have been explored for unresectable HCC with macrovascular invasion, but the evidence base remains sparse for patients with impaired hepatic reserve. Immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI)-based combinations have substantially broadened the therapeutic landscape for advanced HCC.3 HAIC-based combination therapy may provide early intrahepatic cytoreduction while targeted therapy and ICI may contribute to systemic and vascular-thrombus control in selected patients. However, most prospective evidence has focused on Child-Pugh A patients, and the tolerability of intensive combination therapy in patients with both Child-Pugh B liver function and Vp4 PVTT remains insufficiently defined.

We report a patient with hepatitis B virus (HBV)-related cirrhosis, BCLC stage C/CNLC stage IIIa HCC, Vp4 PVTT, and Child-Pugh B7 liver function who had sustained disease control for 34 months after modified FOLFOX-based HAIC combined with lenvatinib, camrelizumab, and entecavir. The case is reported in accordance with the CARE guideline.

Case Presentation

Clinical History and Diagnostic Assessment

A 57-year-old man was admitted after a hepatic mass was detected during evaluation for upper abdominal discomfort. He had no prior history of major illness, smoking, or alcohol consumption; his family history was unremarkable. Physical examination showed mild upper abdominal tenderness; the liver edge was palpable 2 cm below the right costal margin.

Baseline laboratory tests showed markedly elevated alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) at 656 ng/mL (reference range, 0–7 ng/mL). Hepatitis B surface antigen was positive (70.56 IU/mL) and HBV DNA was detectable at 130.4 IU/mL (reference, <100 IU/mL). Hepatitis C serology was negative. Albumin was mildly decreased at 34.4 g/L, gamma-glutamyl transferase was elevated at 242 U/L, total bilirubin (TBIL) was 23.7 μmol/L, and prothrombin time (PT) was 13.9 s (Table 1). The Child-Pugh score was 7 (class B): TBIL 1 point, albumin 2 points, PT 1 point, mild ascites 2 points, and no hepatic encephalopathy 1 point. In the context of HBV-related cirrhosis and markedly elevated AFP, a diagnosis of HCC with PVTT was made based on clinical and radiological criteria.2 The tumor thrombus involved the main portal vein and its branches (Vp4). The disease was staged as BCLC stage C and CNLC stage IIIa.1,2 Intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma and combined hepatocellular-cholangiocarcinoma were considered in the differential diagnosis, but the HBV background, elevated AFP, and typical imaging findings favored HCC. The portal vein filling defects were continuous with the intrahepatic tumor and were interpreted as tumor thrombus rather than bland thrombosis. According to Chinese guideline diagnostic criteria, the patient could be clinically diagnosed without biopsy.2 Mild ascites, impaired hepatic reserve, and portal hypertension increased the procedural risk of biopsy. The absence of histological confirmation precludes assessment of tumor grade, molecular subtypes, or PD-L1 expression.

Therapeutic Intervention

In July 2022, after multidisciplinary evaluation, the patient received HAIC combined with lenvatinib, camrelizumab, and antiviral therapy. The selection of this regimen was based on the patient’s Vp4 PVTT, predominant intrahepatic tumor burden, impaired hepatic reserve, and the limited applicability of pivotal trial evidence to patients with Child-Pugh B liver function. The 2022 Chinese guidelines for primary liver cancer recommend atezolizumab plus bevacizumab and sintilimab plus a bevacizumab biosimilar (ORIENT-32) as first-line systemic options for advanced HCC, alongside lenvatinib and sorafenib as first-line TKIs.2 However, patients with Vp4 PVTT and Child-Pugh B liver function were not represented in the pivotal trials supporting these recommendations. IMbrave150, ORIENT-32, HIMALAYA, and LEAP-002 all enrolled exclusively or predominantly Child-Pugh A patients.4–7

Among the available systemic options, atezolizumab plus bevacizumab was considered but was not preferred after multidisciplinary assessment. The patient had HBV-related cirrhosis, mild ascites, Child-Pugh B7 liver function, predominant intrahepatic tumor burden, and Vp4/main portal vein tumor thrombus, indicating limited hepatic reserve and a high-risk clinical profile. Because the pivotal evidence for current first-line immune-based combinations was derived primarily from patients with Child-Pugh A liver function, these data could not be directly extrapolated to this patient.4–7 The treatment decision was therefore individualized, taking into account hepatic reserve, potential bleeding and hepatic-tolerance concerns, major vascular invasion, and the need for rapid intrahepatic tumor control. Lenvatinib was selected as the TKI backbone based on its higher objective response rate than sorafenib in REFLECT,8 and camrelizumab was added based on institutional experience, local availability, and individualized multidisciplinary assessment. HAIC was incorporated to provide rapid locoregional cytoreduction for the confluent intrahepatic tumor burden and Vp4 PVTT. Camrelizumab has demonstrated antitumor activity as monotherapy in previously treated HCC9 and in combination with rivoceranib in first-line HCC;10 however, neither study directly validates the lenvatinib–camrelizumab combination used in this case. This regimen represents an individualized, non-standard combination rather than a guideline-established treatment strategy.

Transarterial chemoembolization (TACE) was considered less suitable because the patient had Vp4 PVTT and impaired hepatic reserve, in which embolization may increase the risk of hepatic ischemia and liver-function deterioration. HAIC allows regional chemotherapy delivery without intentional arterial embolization, which may be advantageous when portal venous flow is compromised.11 The HAIC regimen consisted of oxaliplatin 85 mg/m2 intra-arterially on day 1, leucovorin 400 mg/m2 intra-arterially on day 1, fluorouracil 400 mg/m2 intra-arterial bolus on day 1, followed by continuous intra-arterial fluorouracil 2400 mg/m2 over 46 hours, repeated every 3 weeks. For each cycle, femoral arterial puncture and selective catheterization were performed; the catheter was removed at the end of each 46-hour infusion and was not left indwelling between cycles. No infusion port was implanted. The infusion targeted selective tumor-feeding branches. No major procedure-related complication was documented.

Lenvatinib was started at 8 mg orally once daily because the patient weighed 52.5 kg (<60 kg) and had Child-Pugh B liver function requiring cautious safety monitoring. During induction and maintenance therapy, lenvatinib was continued without dose reduction. Camrelizumab was administered at 200 mg intravenously every 3 weeks. Available follow-up records documented continued administration of lenvatinib and camrelizumab through May 2025. However, uninterrupted exposure, exact administration counts, temporary treatment interruptions, and cumulative doses could not be reliably reconstructed from the retrospective medical records. Treatment was intended to continue until disease progression or unacceptable toxicity. Entecavir was given continuously throughout the treatment and follow-up period to suppress HBV replication and help preserve hepatic reserve during intensive combination therapy.12

Follow-Up and Outcomes

Laboratory tests were performed before each treatment cycle, and contrast-enhanced CT was performed at baseline, after HAIC cycles, and during scheduled follow-up. Tumor size was measured on comparable contrast-enhanced CT phases and planes whenever possible. Adverse events were monitored at each visit and graded according to CTCAE version 5.0. At 34 months after treatment initiation, the residual lesion measured 41 mm × 28 mm (approximately 54.9% reduction from baseline; Figure 3C and D), AFP was 5.35 ng/mL, no new intrahepatic lesions were detected, and PVTT remained controlled. A small amount of ascites was detected again at this time point.

A retrospective response assessment was performed according to RECIST version 1.1.13 The multifocal intrahepatic tumors formed a confluent mass that could not be reliably separated into individual measurable lesions; therefore, the confluent mass was designated as the target lesion. Its longest diameter decreased from 91 mm at baseline to 46 mm after four treatment cycles, representing a 49.5% reduction. At the 34-month follow-up, the target lesion measured 41 mm, representing a 54.9% reduction from baseline. In the absence of new lesions or unequivocal progression of non-target disease, the best overall response was retrospectively classified as partial response according to RECIST 1.1. PVTT was evaluated as non-target disease and showed radiological regression followed by sustained control. A formal mRECIST assessment was not performed because standardized measurements of the viable arterially enhancing tumor component were unavailable across all imaging time points.

Liver function did not deteriorate during treatment. TBIL decreased from 23.7 μmol/L at baseline to 17.1 μmol/L after four cycles and 12.17 μmol/L at 34-month follow-up. Albumin increased from 34.4 g/L at baseline to 42.9 g/L after four cycles and was 36.9 g/L at 34-month follow-up. PT showed transient prolongation during treatment (13.9 s at baseline, 15.7 s after cycle 2, 15.2 s after cycle 4), but the cause could not be definitively determined. PT returned to 13.0 s at the 34-month follow-up, and no clinical bleeding was documented. Serum creatinine increased from 54.6 μmol/L at baseline to 96 μmol/L at the 34-month follow-up. No clinical renal complication was documented, but continued renal-function monitoring was considered necessary.

The Child-Pugh score was B7 at baseline, improved after resolution of ascites during treatment (A5 after cycles 2–4), and was A6 at the 34-month follow-up because a small amount of ascites was again detected (Table 1). Prothrombin time prolongation was calculated using an institutional upper limit of normal of 12.6 s; PT prolongation was less than 4 s at all time points and was scored as 1 point.

Treatment-related rash and fever were grade 1–2 (CTCAE v5.0). Platelet counts decreased during treatment, with a nadir of 64 × 109/L after cycle 3, corresponding to grade 2 thrombocytopenia according to CTCAE version 5.0. No bleeding event or grade 3 or higher hematologic toxicity was documented. No other grade 3 or higher treatment-related adverse events were documented.

Discussion

Clinical Significance

The main clinical value of this case is that it illustrates a carefully monitored management scenario in a patient with advanced HCC, Vp4 PVTT, and Child-Pugh B7 liver function—a population for whom prospective evidence is scarce. After four cycles, the confluent intrahepatic target lesion decreased from 91 mm × 71 mm to 46 mm × 45 mm, AFP normalized, PVTT regressed radiologically, and ascites resolved. At 34 months, the residual lesion was stable at 41 mm × 28 mm, AFP remained normal, and PVTT was controlled. The best overall response was retrospectively classified as partial response according to RECIST version 1.1, and the Child-Pugh score improved from B7 to A5 during HAIC induction and was A6 at the 34-month follow-up because small-volume ascites had reappeared. These findings are clinically notable, but this observation must be interpreted with extreme caution: it reflects a single selected patient, and the durable disease control may partly reflect favorable tumor biology, preserved performance status, effective antiviral therapy, or selection bias rather than the treatment regimen alone.

Child-Pugh B HCC: The Evidence Gap

The core value of this report lies in the patient’s Child-Pugh B liver function. Pivotal trials defining current first-line standards—IMbrave150, ORIENT-32, HIMALAYA, REFLECT, and LEAP-002—enrolled almost exclusively Child-Pugh A patients.4–8 ASCO guidelines recommend a cautious individualized approach for Child-Pugh B patients, given the absence of high-level evidence.14

The present case provides a real-world observation that intensive HAIC-based combination therapy was tolerated without sustained deterioration in liver function in one carefully selected Child-Pugh B7 patient with Vp4 PVTT. Liver function improved, with the Child-Pugh score decreasing from B7 to A5 during treatment. This is consistent with the hypothesis that effective tumor control may alleviate tumor-related hepatic impairment, but it cannot be generalized without validation in larger cohorts.

Recent real-world studies have explored HAIC-based combination strategies in HCC patients with PVTT. Chang et al reported outcomes of HAIC combined with lenvatinib plus a PD-1 inhibitor in high-risk advanced HCC,15 Li et al evaluated HAIC combined with anti-PD-1 immunotherapy in HCC patients with PVTT,16 and Wang et al examined HAIC combined with TKIs and PD-1 inhibitors in patients with Vp4 PVTT.17 Liu et al described TACE-HAIC plus targeted therapy and immunotherapy for HCC with tumor thrombus and arteriovenous fistula.18 These studies reinforce the clinical relevance of locoregional-systemic strategies for vascular-invasion-predominant HCC, although most data remain retrospective and do not specifically address Child-Pugh B patients.

Mechanistic Considerations and Safety

HAIC may have provided early intrahepatic tumor debulking by delivering chemotherapy directly through the hepatic arterial supply, potentially contributing to AFP decrease and PVTT regression.11,19 Lenvatinib may have maintained disease control by inhibiting angiogenic and proliferative signaling. Camrelizumab may have enhanced antitumor immune activity, potentially facilitated by chemotherapy-induced immunogenic cell death.3 Continuous entecavir therapy may have reduced the risk of HBV reactivation.12 This interpretation is biologically plausible but remains hypothesis-generating.

Treatment-related adverse events were limited to grade 1–2 rash and fever and grade 2 thrombocytopenia according to CTCAE version 5.0. Transient PT prolongation and gradual creatinine elevation were observed. No clinical bleeding or renal complication was documented. This safety profile suggests that in carefully selected patients with Child-Pugh B7 liver function and preserved performance status, close monitoring may permit safe administration of this combination. However, this is based on a single patient and should not be interpreted as evidence of safety in the broader Child-Pugh B population.

Patient Selection and Limitations

Based on this experience, we suggest the following criteria for considering HAIC-based combination therapy in patients with Vp4 PVTT and Child-Pugh B liver function: (1) Child-Pugh score of 7 (not 8 or 9); (2) ECOG performance status 0–1; (3) predominantly intrahepatic disease burden with Vp4 PVTT rather than extensive extrahepatic metastases; (4) absence of severe uncontrolled ascites, hepatic encephalopathy, or recent variceal bleeding; (5) adequate renal function; (6) no severe cardiopulmonary comorbidity; and (7) feasibility of close follow-up with serial imaging, laboratory monitoring, and multidisciplinary reassessment. These criteria are empirically derived from a single case and require prospective validation.

Several limitations must be acknowledged. This is a single case, subject to selection and survivorship bias. Pathological confirmation was not obtained, precluding assessment of tumor grade, molecular subtypes, or PD-L1 expression. Without a control arm, the relative contribution of each treatment component cannot be determined. A formal mRECIST assessment was not performed because standardized measurements of the viable arterially enhancing tumor component were unavailable across all imaging time points. Quantitative PVTT thrombus length was not measured across all imaging time points. Cumulative doses and detailed maintenance strategies for lenvatinib and camrelizumab could not be reconstructed from retrospective records. Because Child-Pugh components, including ascites status, were reconstructed retrospectively from available clinical and imaging records, some potential for retrospective misclassification remains. Future studies with standardized imaging, formal response evaluation, and detailed safety monitoring are needed to define which patients with main PVTT and impaired hepatic reserve may benefit from this strategy.

Conclusions

This case illustrates that in a selected patient with Vp4 PVTT and Child-Pugh B7 liver function, intensive HAIC-based combination therapy with lenvatinib, camrelizumab, and entecavir was associated with a sustained partial response according to retrospective RECIST version 1.1 assessment and radiological and biochemical disease control for 34 months, without documented grade ≥3 treatment-related adverse events or sustained deterioration in liver function. The Child-Pugh score improved from B7 to A5 during HAIC induction and was A6 at the 34-month follow-up because small-volume ascites had reappeared. The observation is hypothesis-generating and does not establish treatment efficacy. Under conditions of careful patient selection—including Child-Pugh score of 7, preserved performance status, predominantly intrahepatic disease, absence of severe hepatic decompensation, and feasibility of close monitoring—this combination may be feasible, but this requires validation in larger prospective cohorts with standardized response and safety assessment.
