Abstract
We propose a physics-informed neural networks (PINNs) framework to solve the infinite-horizon optimal control problem of nonlinear systems. In particular, since PINNs are generally able to solve a class of partial differential equations (PDEs), they can be employed to learn the value function of the infinite-horizon optimal control problem via solving the associated steady-state Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman (HJB) equation. However, an issue here is that the steady-state HJB equation generally yields multiple solutions; hence if PINNs are directly employed to it, they may end up approximating a solution that is different from the optimal value function of the problem. We tackle this by instead applying PINNs to a finite-horizon variant of the steady-state HJB that has a unique solution, and which uniformly approximates the optimal value function as the horizon increases. An algorithm to verify if the chosen horizon is large enough is also given, as well as a method to extend it – with reduced computations and robustness to approximation errors – in case it is not. Unlike many existing methods, the proposed technique works well with non-polynomial basis functions, does not require prior knowledge of a stabilizing controller, and does not perform iterative policy evaluations. Simulations are performed, which verify and clarify theoretical findings.

Introduction

The infinite-horizon optimal control problem
[
1
]
, also known as the optimal stabilization problem
[
2
]
, has been a long-standing issue in control theory. Its main purpose is to find the control policy that stabilizes a dynamical system to the origin, while achieving a desired balance between the rate of convergence and the control effort expended in the closed loop. It yields a closed-form solution when the system dynamics are linear and the desired balance expressed by a quadratic function
[
1
]
, however, no such closed-form solution exists when the system dynamics are nonlinear or if the cost function is non-quadratic.

At the heart of nonlinear/non-quadratic optimal stabilization is the so-called steady-state HJB equation. The importance of this equation lies in that it is solved by the optimal value function of the problem, and this function is crucial in obtaining the optimal control policy for the system. Nevertheless, the steady-state HJB is a nonlinear PDE that is difficult – and almost always impossible – to solve analytically. For this reason, a lot of the literature focuses on trying to solve it approximately, typically using actor-critic methods
[
3
,
4
,
5
,
6
,
7
,
8
]
, many of which have also been extended in the context of multi-agent systems and data-driven control
[
9
,
10
,
11
,
12
,
13
]
.

One of the main methods for approximately solving the steady-state HJB equation for the optimal value function is what is known as successive approximations, or policy iteration (PI)
[
14
,
3
]
. This procedure begins with a stabilizing control policy for the system, then proceeds to iteratively evaluate and improve it till convergence. However, the requirement of knowing a policy that asymptotically stabilizes the system to the origin can be restrictive, especially for a nonlinear system. Moreover, the unavoidable need to perform an iterative policy evaluation procedure can lead to an increased computational burden. Finally, polynomial basis functions are often required in the PI procedure
[
14
]
, which can lead to severe numerical issues when higher-order monomials are used in the basis.

A different, emerging approach for solving a general class of PDEs, is that of PINNs
[
15
]
. The main idea is that the function to be solved for in the underlying PDE can be uniformly approximated by a neural network (NN), the weights of which are trained to force the flow and boundary equations of the PDE to hold as closely as possible over some data. Interestingly, this idea has already been used in the literature to approximate optimal value functions through their associated steady-state HJB equations
[
16
]
, however, a critical issue is that the steady-state HJB equation generally yields more than one solution. This can be problematic in practice, as it can cause the PINN to converge to a solution of the HJB that is unrelated to the optimal value function, or to stall very easily during training. Interestingly, the reference
[
15
]
also points out that PINNs generally work well only when applied to PDEs with unique solutions.

Contributions:
Motivated by this discussion, we propose a PINNs framework for solving the infinite-horizon optimal control problem, which does not suffer from the issue of possibly converging to unwanted solutions of the steady-state HJB equation. To avoid these solutions, instead of directly applying PINNs to the steady-state HJB, we apply them to its finite-horizon variant that has a unique solution, and rigorously prove that this solution uniformly approximates the optimal value function when the horizon length is large enough. We also provide: a) a method to evaluate whether the horizon length is indeed large enough; b) a method to increase it (in case it is not) with reduced computations; and c) a proof that
approximation errors do not necessarily accumulate when if the horizon length is increased multiple times. Unlike PI, the proposed technique does not require knowledge of an initial stabilizing control policy, or computationally expensive iterative policy evaluations.

A preliminary version of this paper appeared in
[
17
]
. However,
[
17
]
did not include proofs of the main results, did not study robustness to approximation errors, and was restricted to finite-horizon approximations of the steady-state HJB with terminal costs equal to zero.

Structure:
The rest of the paper is structured as follows. Section
2
formulates the problem of infinite-horizon optimal control with PINNs, and a solution is proposed in Section
3
by applying them to the finite-horizon HJB. In Section
4
, it is proved that the solution of the finite-horizon HJB uniformly approximates the infinite horizon value function when the horizon is large enough. In Section
5

6
, and the paper is concluded in Section
7
.

2
Problem Formulation

Consider a continuous-time affine nonlinear system of the form:

\dot{x}(t)=f(x(t))+g(x(t))u(t),~x(0)=x_{0},~t\geq 0,

(1)

where
x(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
denotes the state with initial value
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
u(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
is the control input, and
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
g:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
are the system’s drift and input dynamics functions, respectively. We assume that
f,~g
are locally Lipschitz, so that existence and uniqueness of solutions to (
1
) can be established. Moreover, we assume that
f(0)=0
, so that the origin is an equilibrium point of the uncontrolled version of (
1
).

The infinite horizon optimal control problem regards finding the feedback control policy that stabilizes system (
1
) to the origin, while having some form of optimality. By optimality, we mean that it minimizes the cost functional

J(\mu;~x_{0})=\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(Q(x)+\mu^{\textrm{T}}(x)R\mu(x)\right)\textrm{d}\tau,

(2)

with respect to
\mu:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m}
, where
Q:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
is a positive definite function penalizing the state trajectories,
R\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
is a positive definite matrix penalizing the control inputs, and the integration is over the trajectories of (
1
) under
u=\mu
. The integral (
2
) is well-defined in any compact set
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
as long as
\mu
is
admissible
in that set.

Definition 2.1
.

A control policy
\mu:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m}
is defined to be admissible on a set
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and denoted as
\mu\in\Psi(\Omega)
, if it is continuous with
\mu(0)=0
, and if
u=\mu
asymptotically stabilizes (
2
) to the origin for any
x_{0}\in\Omega
while
J(\mu;~x_{0})
is finite.

Denote the optimal control policy on a compact set
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
as

\mu^{\star}(x)=\arg\min_{\mu\in\Psi(\Omega)}J(\mu;~x),

and the corresponding optimal value function as
V^{\star}(\cdot)=J(\mu^{\star};~\cdot)
.
Then, by defining the Hamiltonian

H(x,\mu(x),\nabla_{x}V(x))=\nabla_{x}V^{\textrm{T}}(x)(f(x)+g(x)\mu(x))+Q(x)+\mu^{\textrm{T}}(x)R\mu(x),

and using the stationarity condition
\frac{\partial H}{\partial\mu}=0
, the optimal control policy can be shown to satisfy
[
1
]
:

\mu^{\star}(x)=-\frac{1}{2}R^{-1}g^{\textrm{T}}(x)\nabla_{x}V^{\star}(x).

(3)

In addition, the optimal value function can be shown to satisfy the following PDE, also known as the steady-state HJB equation
[
1
]
:

\nabla_{x}V^{\star\textrm{T}}(x)f(x)+Q(x)-\frac{1}{4}\nabla_{x}V^{\star\textrm{T}}(x)g(x)R^{-1}g^{\textrm{T}}(x)\nabla_{x}V^{\star}(x)=0,~V^{\star}(0)=0.

(4)

Clearly, if one can solve (
4
) for the optimal value function
V^{\star}
, then it is straightforward to obtain the optimal control policy from the formula (
3
). Nevertheless, obtaining
V^{\star}
from (
4
) is not an easy task. This is because the PDE (
4
) is nonlinear, yields multiple solutions even in the linear-quadratic case
[
18
]
, and is thus very difficult to solve analytically. For this reason, the majority of the literature focuses on solving this equation approximately
[
5
,
4
,
9
]
. However, most of the existing approximate solution concepts either rely on prior knowledge of an admissible control input for (
1
), on the use of polynomial basis functions for approximation, or on the execution of computationally expensive iterations, all of which can be restrictive requirements in practice.

Motivated by the aforementioned facts, we seek an alternative method to approximately solve the PDE (
4
) for
V^{\star}
. Towards this end, we draw inspiration from the method of the so-called physics-informed neural networks, which have been extensively used in the literature to solve a general class of PDEs
[
15
]
. Using these networks, we can exploit the structural aspect of the Hamilton-Jacobi Bellman equation, which is informed about the physics of the system through the system dynamics functions
f,g
, in order to obtain the optimal value function and controller.

Remark 2.2
.

While we could allow
\Omega
to be any arbitrary set, within this work we restrict our attention to
\Omega
being compact. This is because we will employ a neural network to approximate
V^{\star}
and
\mu^{\star}
, and the uniform approximation properties of neural networks are valid only on compact sets.

3
A Finite-Horizon Approximation with PINNs

In this section, we propose a procedure for approximating the infinite-horizon optimal value function
V^{\star}
via the use of PINNs. We note that one of the main difficulties in this direction is the fact that the steady-state HJB (
4
) is not in the standard form for which PINNs have been developed
[
15
]
. In particular, the time-derivative term in the steady-state HJB equation (
4
) is missing, and such a term is critical because it ensures uniqueness of solutions. Without such a term, solving (
4
) is akin to an equilibrium search problem in ordinary differential equations, which, while having unique solutions given certain regularity assumptions, can admit multiple equilibrium points.
Accordingly, applying PINNs to directly solve (
4
) could cause the training procedure of PINNs to either stall or converge to a solution completely unrelated to the value function
V^{\star}
.

To deal with these two issues, note that (
4
) is the steady-state version of another, time-varying HJB equation that admits a unique solution. In particular, it is the steady-state version of the PDE:

0=\nabla_{t}V_{T}(x,t)+\nabla_{x}V_{T}^{\textrm{T}}(x,t)f(x)+Q(x)-\frac{1}{4}\nabla_{x}V_{T}^{\textrm{T}}(x,t)g(x)R^{-1}g^{\textrm{T}}(x)\nabla_{x}V_{T}(x,t),\quad V_{T}(x,T)=\phi(x),

(5)

where
T>0
is a positive horizon, and
\phi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
is a positive semidefinite function. The unique solution
V_{T}
of (
5
) is the optimal value function of the
finite-horizon
approximation of (
2
), namely of:

J_{T}(\mu;~x_{0})=\int_{0}^{T}\Big(Q(x(\tau))+r\left(\mu(x(\tau),\tau)\right)\Big)\textrm{d}\tau+\phi(x(T)),

(6)

where we note that the policy
\mu
here is allowed to be time-varying. Hence, as
T
increases, it is expected that the finite-horizon value function
V_{T}(\cdot,0)
will pointwise approximate the infinite-horizon value function
V^{\star}
. In addition, it is expected that the control policy
\mu_{T}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times[0,T]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m}
that minimizes (
6
), and is given by:

\mu_{T}(x,t)=-\frac{1}{2}R^{-1}g^{\textrm{T}}(x)\nabla_{x}{V_{T}}(x,t),

(7)

will approach
\mu^{\star}
as
T
increases, i.e. that
\mu_{T}(x,0)
will converge pointwise to
\mu^{\star}(x)
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Hence, one can proceed to approximate
\mu^{\star}
and
V^{\star}
by solving (
5
) via the method of PINNs for a sufficiently large horizon
T>0
. In the following sections, we will mathematically show that this approximation is valid, i.e., that
V_{T}(\cdot,0)
indeed uniformly approximates
V^{\star}
over
\Omega
as
T
increases, and a similar result holds for the optimal control.

Towards approximating the solution
V_{T}
of the PDE (
5
) using PINNs, we follow
[
15
]
and consider the flow residual function on a compact set
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
for any
v_{T}:\Omega\times[0,T]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
:

F_{e}(x,t;v_{T})=\nabla_{t}v_{T}(x,t)+\nabla_{x}v_{T}^{\textrm{T}}(x,t)f(x)+Q(x)-\frac{1}{4}\nabla_{x}v_{T}^{\textrm{T}}(x,t)g(x)R^{-1}g^{\textrm{T}}(x)\nabla_{x}v_{T}(x,t),~x\in\Omega,t\in[0,T].

This residual informs us about how close
v_{T}
is to satisfying the time-dependent HJB equation (
5
), and is identically zero if and only if
v_{T}
is a solution to (
5
) for any boundary condition. Similarly, we consider the following boundary residual function on
\Omega
:

F_{b}(x;v_{T})=v_{T}(x,T)-\phi(x),~x\in\Omega,

which is equal to zero if and only if
v_{T}
satisfies the boundary condition of (
5
). Hence, it follows that
v_{T}
is a solution to (
5
) if and only if
F_{e}(\cdot,\cdot;v_{T})
and
F_{b}(\cdot;v_{T})
are identically zero. Finally, one can define the following problem-specific residual:

F_{in}(t;v_{T})=v_{T}(0,t),~t\in[0,T],

which, for any value function associated with the optimal stabilization problem, should be equal to zero for all
t\in[0,T]
.

To find a solution to (
5
), a neural network
\hat{v}_{T}(\cdot,\cdot;w):\Omega\times[0,T]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is constructed, where
w\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
denote the network’s parameters, and
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a compact set where approximation will take place. Such a neural network can have either one or multiple layers, and activation functions that could be sigmoids, RELUs, or radial-basis functions.
The parameters
w
are trained by attempting to force the residuals
F_{e}(x,t;\hat{v}_{T})
,
F_{b}(x;\hat{v}_{T})
,
F_{in}(t;\hat{v}_{T})
to be zero across a data set
(x^{i}_{e},t^{i}_{e})\in\Omega\times[0,T]
,
i=1,\ldots,N_{e}
,
x^{i}_{b}\in\Omega
,
i=1,\ldots,N_{b}
, and
t^{i}_{in}\in[0,T]
,
i=1,\ldots,N_{in}
. This is done by defining the following mean square error (MSE)

\textrm{MSE}=\textrm{MSE}_{e}+\textrm{MSE}_{b}+\textrm{MSE}_{in},

(8)

where

\displaystyle\textrm{MSE}_{e}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N_{e}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{e}}F_{e}(x^{i}_{e},t^{i}_{e};\hat{v}_{T})^{2},

\displaystyle\textrm{MSE}_{b}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N_{b}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{b}}F_{b}(x^{i}_{b};\hat{v}_{T})^{2},

\displaystyle\textrm{MSE}_{in}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N_{in}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{in}}F_{in}(t^{i}_{in};\hat{v}_{T})^{2}

and training
w
so that the MSE given by (
8
) is minimized. This training can take place using off-the-shelf optimization algorithms, such as Adam
[
19
,
15
]
.
The name
physics informed neural network
is usually used for the function
F_{e}(\cdot,\cdot,\hat{v}_{T})
, because it is essentially a neural network with the same parameters
w
as
\hat{v}_{T}
, but whose activation functions have been “informed” about the physics of the system through the underlying HJB equation. Although strict theoretical guarantees of convergence do not exist for the method of PINNs, they have been empirically shown to perform well when the solution to the underlying PDE is unique and the neural network architecture is expressive enough
[
15
]
. We describe the overall physics-informed learning procedure in Algorithm
3
.

{algorithm}

[!t]

Physics-Informed Learning for Optimal Control

{algorithmic}
[1]

\Procedure

\State
Choose sufficiently large horizon length
T>0
, area of approximation
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and number of grid points
N_{e},N_{b},N_{in}>0
.

\State
Sample points
(x^{i}_{e},t^{i}_{e})\in\Omega\times[0,T]
,
i=1,\ldots,N_{e}
,
x^{i}_{b}\in\Omega
,
i=1,\ldots,N_{b}
, and
t^{i}_{in}\in[0,T]
,
i=1,\ldots,N_{in}
.

\State
Choose the number of layers and basis functions for the neural network and construct
\hat{v}(\cdot,\cdot;~w)
.

\State
Train
w
to minimize MSE in (
8
) using Adam
[
19
]
.

\State
Compute approximate optimal controller as
\hat{\mu}_{T}(x,t)=-\frac{1}{2}R^{-1}g^{\textrm{T}}(x)\nabla_{x}{\hat{v}_{T}}(x,t;w).

\EndProcedure

Apparently, in the context of PINNs, dealing with the HJB equation (
5
) is much more convenient than handling the HJB (
4
). Unlike (
4
), the HJB equation (
5
) is exactly the form for which PINNs have been developed
[
15
]
. In addition, given that
V_{T}
is continuously differentiable, then it is certain that (
5
) admits only one solution, equal to
V_{T}
; hence, the possibility of the PINN approximating a function entirely different than
V_{T}
is excluded. Nevertheless, simply substituting the infinite-horizon HJB with a finite-horizon one with a large horizon, and anticipating that the solution
V_{T}(\cdot,0)
of the latter will be close to the solution
V^{\star}
of the former, could possibly prove to be a careless action. In that respect, a couple of important questions must be answered to validate such an approach:

1.

As the horizon
T
increases, do the functions
V_{T}(\cdot,0)
and
\mu_{T}(\cdot,0)
provide
uniform
approximations of the optimal value function
V^{\star}(\cdot)
and control
\mu^{\star}(\cdot)
?

2.

How can one evaluate, for a fixed
T
, whether the derived control policy
\mu_{T}(\cdot,0)
is close enough to the infinite-horizon optimal control policy
\mu^{\star}(\cdot)
? In addition, if it is concluded that
\mu_{T}(\cdot,0)
and
V_{T}(\cdot,0)
are not close enough to
\mu^{\star}(\cdot)
and
V^{\star}(\cdot)
, how can these functions be used to obtain better ones without completely restarting the training process of the PINN?

The remainder of this paper is focused on answering these significant questions.

Remark 3.1
.

While numerical methods could be used to approximately solve (
5
), those have much higher computational complexity as they rely on very fine partitioning of the state space in order to avoid stability issues
[
15
]
. At the same time, they cannot generalize outside the compact set
\Omega
the same way that neural networks do.

4
Uniform Approximation of
V^{\star}
and
\mu^{\star}
using the Finite-Horizon HJB

The purpose of this section is to investigate whether
V_{T}(\cdot,0)
and
\mu_{T}(\cdot,0)
provide uniform approximations of the optimal value function
V^{\star}(\cdot)
and control
\mu^{\star}(\cdot)
as
T
increases. Towards this end, notice that for any positive semidefinite function
\psi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, the following inequality is true:

V_{T}(\cdot,0;0)\leq V_{T}(\cdot,0;\psi)\leq V_{T}(\cdot,0;\psi+V^{\star}),

(9)

where, by abusing notation, the argument after the semicolon denotes the value of the terminal cost in (
6
). That is,
V_{T}(\cdot,0;0)
is the optimal value of the cost (
6
) with
\phi\equiv 0
,
V_{T}(\cdot,0;\psi)
is the optimal value for
\phi\equiv\psi
, and
V_{T}(\cdot,0;\psi+V^{\star})
is the optimal value for
\phi\equiv\psi+V^{\star}
. Hence, if one can prove that
V_{T}(\cdot,0;0)
and
V_{T}(\cdot,0;\psi+V^{\star})
converge uniformly to
V^{\star}
, the same will also hold for
V_{T}(\cdot,0;\psi)
, i.e., for
V_{T}(\cdot,0)
for any arbitrary terminal cost. The rest of this section is focused on proving these facts.

Before proceeding, and whenever necessary to increase clarity, we will denote as
x^{\star}
the trajectories of (
1
) generated by the control
\mu^{\star}
, and as
x_{T}
the trajectories of (
1
) generated by the control
\mu_{T}
. The notation
x_{0}
will still be reserved for the initial condition.

4.1
Uniform Approximation with
\phi\equiv 0

In this subsection, we prove that for
\phi\equiv 0
,
V_{T}(\cdot,0)
converges uniformly to
V^{\star}
on
\Omega
. Towards this end, we will first need a few intermediate results about the behavior of
V_{T}
(for
\phi\equiv 0)
with respect to
T
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Assume that
\phi\equiv 0
. Then, for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the sequence
V_{T}(x,0)
is increasing with respect to
T
and upper bounded by
V^{\star}(x)
, i.e., for every real
T_{2}\geq T_{1}>0
, it holds that:

V_{T_{1}}(x,0)\leq V_{T_{2}}(x,0)\leq V^{\star}(x),~\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

In addition,
\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}V_{T}(x,0)=L(x)
for some Lebesgue measurable function
L:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
.

Proof 4.2
.

Let
0<T_{1}<T_{2}<\infty
. For every
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we have:

\displaystyle\hskip-5.69054ptV_{T_{2}}(x_{0},0)=J_{T_{2}}(\mu_{T_{2}};~x_{0})

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T_{2}}\Big(Q(x_{T_{2}}(\tau))+r\left(\mu_{T_{2}}(x_{T_{2}}(\tau),\tau)\right)\Big)\textrm{d}\tau

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T_{1}}\Big(Q(x_{T_{2}}(\tau))+r\left(\mu_{T_{2}}(x_{T_{2}}(\tau),\tau)\right)\Big)\textrm{d}\tau+\int_{T_{1}}^{T_{2}}\Big(Q(x_{T_{2}}(\tau))+r\left(\mu_{T_{2}}(x_{T_{2}}(\tau),\tau)\right)\Big)\textrm{d}\tau

\displaystyle\geq\int_{0}^{T_{1}}\Big(Q(x_{T_{2}}(\tau))+r\left(\mu_{T_{2}}(x_{T_{2}}(\tau),\tau)\right)\Big)\textrm{d}\tau.

(10)

However, since
\mu_{T_{1}}
minimizes the cost
J_{T_{1}}(\cdot;~x_{0})
:

\int_{0}^{T_{1}}\Big(Q(x_{T_{2}}(\tau))+r\left(\mu_{T_{2}}(x_{T_{2}}(\tau),\tau)\right)\Big)\textrm{d}\tau=J_{T_{1}}(\mu_{T_{2}};~x)\geq J_{T_{1}}(\mu_{T_{1}};~x)=V_{T_{1}}(x,0).

(11)

Hence, combining (
10
)-(
11
), we obtain that
V_{T_{2}}(x_{0},0)\geq V_{T_{1}}(x_{0},0)
, for all
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The fact that
V_{T}(x_{0},0)\leq V^{\star}(x_{0})
for all
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
T>0
can be proved using an identical procedure, by setting
T_{1}=T
while taking the limit
T_{2}\rightarrow\infty
.

To conclude, note that for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the sequence
V_{T}(x,0)
is increasing with respect to
T
and bounded above by
V^{\star}(x)
. Therefore,
\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}V_{T}(x,0)=L(x)
for some Lebesgue measurable function
L:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
,
\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Lemma 4.3
.

Assume that
\phi\equiv 0
. Then, it holds that
\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}x_{T}(T)=0
.

Proof 4.4
.

For each
T>0
, we have:

\displaystyle V_{T}(x_{0},0)=J_{T}(x_{0},\mu_{T})

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\Big(Q(x_{T}(\tau))+r\left(\mu_{T}(x_{T}(\tau),\tau)\right)\Big)\textrm{d}\tau

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\frac{T}{2}}\Big(Q(x_{T}(\tau))+r\left(\mu_{T}(x_{T}(\tau),\tau)\right)\Big)\textrm{d}\tau+\int_{\frac{T}{2}}^{T}\Big(Q(x_{T}(\tau))+r\left(\mu_{T}(x_{T}(\tau),\tau)\right)\Big)\textrm{d}\tau.

Hence

\displaystyle V_{T}(x_{0},0)

\displaystyle=J_{\frac{T}{2}}(x_{0},\mu_{T})+\int_{\frac{T}{2}}^{T}\Big(Q(x_{T}(\tau))+r\left(\mu_{T}(x_{T}(\tau),\tau)\right)\Big)\textrm{d}\tau

\displaystyle\geq J_{\frac{T}{2}}(x_{0},\mu_{\frac{T}{2}})+\int_{\frac{T}{2}}^{T}\Big(Q(x_{T}(\tau))+r\left(\mu_{T}(x_{T}(\tau),\tau)\right)\Big)\textrm{d}\tau

\displaystyle=V_{\frac{T}{2}}(x_{0},0)+\int_{\frac{T}{2}}^{T}\Big(Q(x_{T}(\tau))+r\left(\mu_{T}(x_{T}(\tau),\tau)\right)\Big)\textrm{d}\tau.

(12)

Note that
\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}V_{\frac{T}{2}}(x_{0},0)=\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}V_{T}(x_{0},0)=L(x_{0})
according to Lemma
4.1
. Applying this to (
12
) yields:

\displaystyle\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}\int_{\frac{T}{2}}^{T}\Big(Q(x_{T}(\tau))+r\left(\mu_{T}(x_{T}(\tau),\tau)\right)\Big)\textrm{d}\tau\leq 0\Longrightarrow\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}\int_{\frac{T}{2}}^{T}\Big(Q(x_{T}(\tau))+r\left(\mu_{T}(x_{T}(\tau))\right)\Big)\textrm{d}\tau=0.

Since
Q(\cdot)
is positive definite and
R\succ 0
, the energy of the state and the control converge to zero in the steady state as
T\rightarrow\infty
, which may hold only if
\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}x_{T}(T)=0
.

The two results above can be exploited to prove that, when
\phi\equiv 0
,
V_{T}(\cdot,0)

pointwise
approximates the optimal value function
V^{\star}(\cdot)
as
T
increases.

Lemma 4.5
.

Assume that
\phi\equiv 0
. Then, for each fixed
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
:

\displaystyle\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}V_{T}(x,0)=V^{\star}(x).

Proof 4.6
.

Taking the time derivative of
V^{\star}
over the trajectories generated by the control input
\mu_{T}
, we have

\begin{split}\dot{V}^{\star}(x)=&\nabla_{x}V^{\star\textrm{T}}(x)(f(x)+g(x)\mu_{T}(x,t))\\
=&\nabla_{x}V^{\star\textrm{T}}(f(x)+g(x)\mu^{\star}(x))+\nabla_{x}V^{\star\textrm{T}}(x)g(x)(\mu_{T}(x,t)-\mu^{\star}(x)).\end{split}

(13)

From (
3
)-(
4
), we obtain the following relations:

\displaystyle\nabla_{x}V^{\star\textrm{T}}(f(x)+g(x)\mu^{\star}(x))=-Q(x)-\mu^{\star\textrm{T}}(x)R\mu^{\star}(x),

\displaystyle\nabla_{x}V^{\star\textrm{T}}(x)g(x)=-2\mu^{\star\textrm{T}}(x)R.

Combining these with (
13
) yields

\dot{V}^{\star}(x)=-Q(x)-\mu^{\star\textrm{T}}(x)R\mu^{\star}(x)-2\mu^{\star\textrm{T}}(x)R(\mu_{T}(x,t)-\mu^{\star}(x)),

or equivalently

\displaystyle\dot{V}^{\star}(x)=

\displaystyle-Q(x)-\mu_{T}^{\textrm{T}}(x,t)R\mu_{T}(x,t)+(\mu^{\star}(x)-\mu_{T}(x,t))^{\textrm{T}}R(\mu^{\star}(x)-\mu_{T}(x,t)).

Integrating this equation over
t\in[0,T]
, and recalling that the trajectories of
x
are generated by the control
\mu_{T}
, we get:

\displaystyle V^{\star}(x_{T}(T))-V^{\star}(x_{0})=-\int_{0}^{T}(Q(x_{T})+\mu_{T}^{\textrm{T}}(x_{T},t)R\mu_{T}(x_{T},t))\textrm{d}t+\int_{0}^{T}(\mu^{\star}(x_{T})-\mu_{T}(x_{T},t))^{\textrm{T}}R(\mu^{\star}(x_{T})-\mu_{T}(x_{T},t))\textrm{d}t.

(14)

Recalling again that the integrals are taken over the trajectories generated by
\mu_{T}
, and since
\phi\equiv 0
, we have by (
6
) and (
14
) that:

V^{\star}(x_{T}(T))-V^{\star}(x_{0})=-V_{T}(x_{0},0)+\int_{0}^{T}(\mu^{\star}(x_{T})-\mu_{T}(x_{T},t))^{\textrm{T}}R(\mu^{\star}(x_{T})-\mu_{T}(x_{T},t))\textrm{d}t

or equivalently

\displaystyle V_{T}(x_{0},0)=V^{\star}(x_{0})-V^{\star}(x_{T}(T))+\int_{0}^{T}(\mu^{\star}(x_{T})-\mu_{T}(x_{T},t))^{\textrm{T}}R(\mu^{\star}(x_{T})-\mu_{T}(x_{T},t))\textrm{d}t.

(15)

Since
R\succ 0
, this implies that
V_{T}(x_{0},0)\geq V^{\star}(x_{0})-V^{\star}(x_{T}(T))
. In addition, from Lemma
4.1
we have
V_{T}(x_{0},0)\leq V^{\star}(x_{0})
, hence overall:

V^{\star}(x_{0})-V^{\star}(x_{T}(T))\leq V_{T}(x_{0},0)\leq V^{\star}(x_{0}).

(16)

It holds by continuity and Lemma
4.3
that
\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}V^{\star}(x_{T}(T))=V^{\star}(\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}x_{T}(T))=V^{\star}(0)=0
. Therefore,
taking the limit as
T\rightarrow\infty
in (
16
), we obtain:

V^{\star}(x_{0})\leq\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}V_{T}(x_{0},0)\leq V^{\star}(x_{0}),

i.e.
\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}V_{T}(x_{0},0)=V^{\star}(x_{0}).

Finally, the uniform convergence of
V_{T}(\cdot,0)
to
V^{\star}
under
\phi=0
follows by combining Lemmas
4.1
and
4.5
.

Lemma 4.7
.

Assume that
\phi\equiv 0
. Then,
V_{T}(\cdot,0)
converges uniformly to
V^{\star}
on
\Omega
.

Proof 4.8
.

From Lemmas
4.1
and
4.5
, the sequence of continuous functions
V_{T}(\cdot,0)
is increasing and converges pointwise everywhere on
\Omega
to the continuous function
V^{\star}
. Since
\Omega
is compact, it follows from Dini’s theorem (Theorem 7.13 in
[
20
]
) that the mode of convergence is uniform.

4.2
Uniform Approximation with
\phi\equiv\psi+V^{\star}

In this subsection, we prove a more straightforward result: that for
\phi\equiv\psi+V^{\star}
, where
\psi
is positive semidefinite,
V_{T}(\cdot,0)
once again converges uniformly to
V^{\star}
on
\Omega
.

Lemma 4.9
.

Assume that
\phi\equiv\psi+V^{\star}
for some positive semidefinite, continuous function
\psi
. Then,
V_{T}(\cdot,0)
converges uniformly to
V^{\star}
on
\Omega
.

Proof 4.10
.

Under this terminal cost, we have from (
6
):

V_{T}(x_{0},0)\leq J_{T}(\mu^{\star};~x_{0})=\int_{0}^{T}\Big(Q(x^{\star}(\tau))+r\left(\mu^{\star}(x^{\star}(\tau))\right)\Big)\textrm{d}\tau+V^{\star}(x^{\star}(T))+\psi(x^{\star}(T))=V^{\star}(x_{0})+\psi(x^{\star}(T)).

(17)

On the other hand:

J_{T}(\mu;~x_{0})=\int_{0}^{T}\Big(Q(x(\tau))+r\left(\mu(x(\tau),\tau)\right)\Big)\textrm{d}\tau+\psi(x(T))+V^{\star}(x(T))\geq\int_{0}^{T}\Big(Q(x(\tau))+r\left(\mu(x(\tau),\tau)\right)\Big)\textrm{d}\tau+V^{\star}(x(T)).

By the principle of optimality, the right-hand side is minimized when
\mu=\mu^{\star}
. Hence:

J_{T}(\mu;~x_{0})\geq\int_{0}^{T}\Big(Q(x^{\star}(\tau))+r\left(\mu^{\star}(x^{\star}(\tau))\right)\Big)\textrm{d}\tau+V^{\star}(x^{\star}(T))=V^{\star}(x_{0}).

(18)

Finally, setting
\mu=\mu_{T}
, one has
J_{T}(\mu_{T};~x_{0})=V_{T}(x_{0},0)
, hence (
18
) gives

V_{T}(x_{0},0)\geq V^{\star}(x_{0}).

(19)

Combining (
17
) with (
19
), we obtain:

V^{\star}(x_{0})\leq V_{T}(x_{0},0)\leq V^{\star}(x_{0})+\psi(x^{\star}(T))\Longrightarrow|V_{T}(x_{0},0)-V^{\star}(x_{0})|\leq\psi(x^{\star}(T))\\
\Longrightarrow\max_{x_{0}\in\Omega}|V_{T}(x_{0},0)-V^{\star}(x_{0})|\leq\max_{x_{0}\in\Omega}\psi(x^{\star}(T)).

(20)

But
\mu^{\star}
is asymptotically stabilizing on
\Omega
, hence
\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}\max_{x_{0}\in\Omega}\psi(x^{\star}(T))=0
. Owing to (
20
), this implies
\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}\max_{x_{0}\in\Omega}|V_{T}(x_{0},0)-V^{\star}(x_{0})|=0
, i.e.,
V_{T}(\cdot,0)
converges uniformly to
V^{\star}
on
\Omega
.

4.3
Uniform Approximation with Arbitrary Terminal Cost

To conclude with the main result of this section, recall that (
9
) implies that if
V_{T}(\cdot,0)
converges uniformly to
V^{\star}
as
T\rightarrow\infty
for
\phi\equiv 0
and
\phi\equiv\psi+V^{\star}
, then the same result is generalizable for any arbitrary positive semi-definite terminal cost
\phi
. Hence, we get the following theorem.

Theorem 4.11
.

Let
\phi
be an arbitrary, positive semi-definite terminal cost for (
6
). Then,

V_{T}(\cdot,0)\rightarrow V^{\star}
and
\mu_{T}(\cdot,0)\rightarrow\mu^{\star}
uniformly on
\Omega
.

Proof 4.12
.

The fact that
V_{T}(\cdot,0)\rightarrow V^{\star}
uniformly on
\Omega
follows directly from (
9
) and Lemmas
4.7
-
4.9
. Moreover, following the same arguments as in
[
4
]
, the Lipschitz continuity of (
1
) as well as the continuity of the controllers
\mu_{T}
imply that, as
T\rightarrow\infty
, the trajectories of (
1
) under
u=\mu_{T}
converge uniformly
\forall x_{0}\in\Omega
, hence
\mu_{T}(\cdot,0)\rightarrow\mu^{\star}
uniformly on
\Omega
since
V_{T}(\cdot,0)\rightarrow V^{\star}
uniformly.

Remark 4.13
.

Combining Theorem
4.11
with the universal approximation property of neural networks, we conclude that approximating the finite-horizon value function
V_{T}
with PINNs for a large horizon
T
is a valid procedure towards estimating the infinite horizon value function
V^{\star}
over a compact set.

Note that (
9
) implies
V_{T}(\cdot,0;0)-V^{\star}(\cdot)\leq V_{T}(\cdot,0;\psi)-V^{\star}(\cdot)\leq V_{T}(\cdot,0;\psi+V^{\star})-V^{\star}(\cdot)
. Combining this with (
16
) and (
20
), it follows that

\max_{x_{0}\in\Omega}|V_{T}(x_{0},0)-V^{\star}(x_{0})|\leq\max\{\max_{x_{0}\in\Omega}\psi(x^{\star}(T)),~\max_{x_{0}\in\Omega}V^{\star}(x_{T}(T;~0))\},

where
x_{T}(\cdot;~0)
denotes the trajectory of
x_{T}
when
\phi\equiv 0
.
The expression above gives us some information regarding the quality of the approximation of
V^{\star}(\cdot)
by
V_{T}(\cdot,0)
with respect to
T
. Specifically, it tells us that the approximation quality depends on how quickly the optimal trajectories
x^{\star}
and
x_{T}
at time
T
converge to zero as
T
increases, which is natural as the convergence rates of
x^{\star}
and
x_{T}
dictate how quickly the tail of the integral (
2
) converges to 0.

5
Effect of the Horizon Length
T

5.1
Evaluation of the Horizon Length

So far, it has been proven that the finite-horizon value function
V_{T}(\cdot,0)
indeed uniformly approximates the infinite-horizon one, namely
V^{\star}
, as
T\rightarrow\infty
. Therefore, using PINNs to get an estimate of
V_{T}(\cdot,0)
is, in principle, a valid procedure in the task of obtaining an estimate of
V^{\star}
, provided that
T
is large enough. This brings forward another question: how large should
T
be chosen? Or, in other words, how can we evaluate whether the chosen horizon
T
is large enough, so that
\mu_{T}(\cdot,0)
,
V_{T}(\cdot,0)
are close to
\mu^{\star}
,
V^{\star}
?

One way to verify whether
V_{T}(\cdot,0)
is close enough to
V^{\star}
in
\Omega
, is by checking whether it satisfies the infinite-horizon HJB (
4
), since
V^{\star}
is in fact a solution to that equation. Towards this end, one can define the following flow residual:

E_{e}(x;V_{T}(\cdot,0))=\nabla_{x}V_{T}^{\textrm{T}}(x,0)f(x)+Q(x)-\frac{1}{4}\nabla_{x}V_{T}^{\textrm{T}}(x,0)g(x)R^{-1}g^{\textrm{T}}(x)\nabla_{x}V_{T}(x,0).

(21)

This is essentially an error indicating how far
V_{T}(\cdot,0)
is from satisfying the infinite-horizon HJB (
4
) at the point
x
; it is zero if and only if
V_{T}(\cdot,0)
solves (
4
) at this specific point. Accordingly, one can define the boundary residual:

E_{b}(V_{T}(\cdot,0))=V_{T}(0,0),

which is zero if and only if
V_{T}(0,0)=0
, i.e. if and only if
V_{T}(\cdot,0)
satisfies the boundary condition of the infinite-horizon HJB (
4
). Therefore, by aggregating the residuals into a single error term:

E=\frac{1}{N_{c}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{c}}E_{e}(x^{i}_{c};V_{T}(\cdot,0))^{2}+E_{b}(V_{T}(\cdot,0))^{2},

(22)

where
x^{i}_{c}\in\Omega
,
i=1,\ldots,N_{c}
, are data points, a procedure to check whether
V_{T}(\cdot,0)
provides a good approximation to
V^{\star}
would be to check how close
E
is to zero.

Remark 5.1
.

Since PINNs are used to approximate
V_{T}
, only an approximation
\hat{V}_{T}
of
V_{T}
is available. Therefore, in practice, one would only be able to compute the residuals
E=\frac{1}{N_{c}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{c}}E_{e}(x^{i}_{e};\hat{V}_{T}(\cdot,0))^{2}+E_{b}(\hat{V}_{T}(\cdot,0))^{2}
, and check whether those are close to zero or not. Since
\hat{V}_{T}
would only approximate
V_{T}
, a nonzero residual may not necessarily imply that
T
is not large enough, but it could mean that the underlying neural network architecture is not expressive enough to sufficiently approximate
V_{T}
.

5.2
Extension of the Horizon Length

In case that the horizon
T
is deemed to be small, by means of the residual error
E
being large, then one can increase this horizon to get a better approximation of
V^{\star}
. In that respect, one could recompute
V_{T^{\prime}}
from scratch for some larger horizon
T^{\prime}>T
by reemploying the PINNs method of Section
3
over the larger domain
\Omega\times[0,T^{\prime}]\supset\Omega\times[0,T]
. However, this can be a tedious procedure, and in fact unnecessary; instead, the value function
V_{T}
that has already been computed for a smaller horizon can be used as an aid to find
V_{T^{\prime}}
,
T^{\prime}>T
, without resolving the whole problem from scratch over
[0,T^{\prime}]
, but rather by solving another smaller problem only over
[0,T^{\prime}-T]
. To see this, consider the finite-horizon cost functional, which is similar to (
6
) but augmented with a different terminal cost, dependent on
V_{T}
:

J^{\prime}(\mu;x_{0})=\int_{0}^{T^{\prime}-T}\Big(Q(x(\tau))+r\left(\mu(x(\tau),\tau)\right)\Big)\textrm{d}\tau+V_{T}(x(T^{\prime}-T),0).

(23)

The optimal value function
V^{\prime}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times[0,T^{\prime}-T]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and control
\mu^{\prime}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times[0,T^{\prime}-T]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m}
of this problem satisfy:

\displaystyle\nabla_{t}V^{{}^{\prime}}(x,t)+\nabla_{x}V^{{}^{\prime}\textrm{T}}(x,t)f(x)+Q(x)-\frac{1}{4}\nabla_{x}V^{{}^{\prime}\textrm{T}}(x,t)g(x)R^{-1}g^{\textrm{T}}(x)\nabla_{x}V^{\prime}(x,t)=0,

(24)

\displaystyle V^{\prime}(x,T^{\prime}-T)=V_{T}(x,0),

and

\mu^{\prime}(x,t)=-\frac{1}{2}R^{-1}g^{\textrm{T}}(x)\nabla_{x}{V^{\prime}}(x,t).

(25)

Notice that these two functions are defined only over
t\in[0,T^{\prime}-T]
and not over
t\in[0,T^{\prime}]
, and the same holds for the corresponding PDE (
24
). In what follows, we show that, in fact,
V^{\prime}(\cdot,0)=V_{T^{\prime}}(\cdot,0)
and
\mu^{\prime}(\cdot,0)=\mu_{T^{\prime}}(\cdot,0)
.

Theorem 5.2
.

It holds that
V^{\prime}(\cdot,0)=V_{T^{\prime}}(\cdot,0)
, and
\mu^{\prime}(\cdot,0)=\mu_{T^{\prime}}(\cdot,0)
.

Proof 5.3
.

The value function
V_{T^{\prime}}(x_{0},0)
, by definition, is equal to the optimal value of the following cost functional for any
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
:

\displaystyle J_{T^{\prime}}(\mu;~x_{0})=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T^{\prime}}\Big(Q(x(\tau))+r\left(\mu(x(\tau),\tau)\right)\Big)\textrm{d}\tau+\phi(x(T^{\prime}))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T^{\prime}-T}\Big(Q(x(\tau))+r\left(\mu(x(\tau),\tau)\right)\Big)\textrm{d}\tau+\int_{T^{\prime}-T}^{T^{\prime}}\Big(Q(x(\tau))+r\left(\mu(x(\tau),\tau)\right)\Big)\textrm{d}\tau+\phi(x(T^{\prime})).

However, by the principle of optimality, we have:

\displaystyle V_{T^{\prime}}(x_{0},0)

\displaystyle=J_{T^{\prime}}(\mu_{T^{\prime}};~x_{0})=\min_{\mu}J_{T^{\prime}}(\mu;~x_{0})

\displaystyle=\min_{\mu}\Big(\int_{0}^{T^{\prime}-T}\Big(Q(x(\tau))+r\left(\mu(x(\tau),\tau)\right)\Big)\textrm{d}\tau+\int_{T^{\prime}-T}^{T^{\prime}}\Big(Q(x(\tau))+r\left(\mu(x(\tau),\tau)\right)\Big)\textrm{d}\tau+\phi(x(T^{\prime}))\Big)

\displaystyle=\min_{\mu}\Big(\int_{0}^{T^{\prime}-T}\Big(Q(x(\tau))+r\left(\mu(x(\tau),\tau)\right)\Big)\textrm{d}\tau+\min_{\mu^{\prime}}\Big(\int_{T^{\prime}-T}^{T^{\prime}}\Big(Q(x(\tau))+r\left(\mu^{\prime}(x(\tau),\tau)\right)\Big)\textrm{d}\tau+\phi(x(T^{\prime}))\Big)\Big)

\displaystyle=\min_{\mu}\Big(\int_{0}^{T^{\prime}-T}\Big(Q(x(\tau))+r\left(\mu(x(\tau),\tau)\right)\Big)\textrm{d}\tau+V_{T}(x(T^{\prime}-T),0)\Big)

\displaystyle=\min_{\mu}J^{\prime}(\mu;~x_{0})=V^{\prime}(x_{0},0),

which is the required result. The fact that
\mu^{\prime}(\cdot,0)=\mu_{T^{\prime}}(\cdot,0)
then follows from (
7
) and (
25
) and the fact that
V_{T^{\prime}}(x_{0},0)=V^{\prime}(x_{0},0)
for all
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Based on Theorem
5.2
, if one knows the value function
V_{T}(\cdot,0)
for some horizon length
T>0
, then they can compute the value function
V_{T^{\prime}}(\cdot,0)
over a larger horizon
T^{\prime}>T
by solving the PDE (
24
). This PDE is defined only over a time length of
T^{\prime}-T
, so it is significantly less tedious to deal with than resolving the PDE (
5
) from scratch. In that respect, if one needs to increase the horizon length
T
so that
V_{T}(\cdot,0)
better approximates
V^{\star}
, it would be less tedious to apply the PINN method on (
24
), instead applying it from scratch on (
5
) for a larger horizon.

Remark 5.4
.

The PDE (
24
) can be approximately solved similarly to (
5
), by sampling data points on the flow and the boundary condition and defining a loss function of the form (
8
). The specific details are straightforward and omitted to avoid repetition.

With regard to the computational complexity of the horizon extension mechanism, there is a tradeoff between i) choosing a new horizon length
T^{\prime}
that is large enough and ii) using a neural network structure that is relatively small and thus faster to train. On the one hand, choosing
T^{\prime}
to be much larger than
T
allows one to achieve a desired tolerance one-shot, without having to re-extend the horizon length multiple times. On the other hand, choosing
T^{\prime}
to be only slightly larger than
T
means that the horizon-extension problem (
23
) can be solved with a smaller neural network, because the time domain of the extension lies on
[T,T^{\prime}]
. Similarly, choosing an initial horizon length
T
that is large enough can enable one-shot learning of the infinite-horizon optimal controller without resorting to any iterations. Nevertheless, a small horizon
T
combined with iterative extensions can enable the use of physics-informed neural networks that are smaller in size and thus faster to train.

5.3
Effect of Approximation Errors on Horizon Length Extension

Typically, the finite-horizon value function
V_{T}(\cdot,0)
cannot be computed exactly owing to the finite approximation capabilities of PINNs. This is not an issue if
T
is chosen to be sufficiently large from the very beginning, however, problems may arise if
T
is initially small and then extended using the method of Subsection
5.2
. In this case, errors in the estimation of
V_{T}(\cdot,0)
owed to the finite approximation capabilities of the PINNs may be carried over to the estimation of
V_{T^{\prime}}(\cdot,0)
, and such errors can accumulate if the horizon is extended multiple times.

To study the effect of approximation errors on the extension of the horizon length
T
, suppose that instead of
V_{T}(\cdot,0)
, the estimated finite-horizon value function given by PINNs is
\hat{V}_{T}(\cdot,0)
, and denote as
e_{T}(x)=\hat{V}_{T}(x,0)-V_{T}(x,0)
the approximation error for any
x\in\Omega
. In this case, if we apply the method of Subsection
5.2
to extend the horizon length to
T^{\prime}>T
, instead of
V_{T^{\prime}}(\cdot,0)
we will obtain a value function estimate
\hat{V}_{T^{\prime}}(x,0)
that is the minimum value of the cost:

\hat{J}^{\prime}(\mu;x_{0})=\int_{0}^{T^{\prime}-T}\Big(Q(x(\tau))+r\left(\mu(x(\tau),\tau)\right)\Big)\textrm{d}\tau+\hat{V}_{T}(x(T^{\prime}-T),0).

(26)

Note that here, unlike (
23
),
\hat{V}_{T}
is used as a terminal cost because the exact
V_{T}
is unknown owing to approximation errors. Accordingly, instead of
\mu_{T^{\prime}}(\cdot,0)
, we will obtain an optimal control estimate
\hat{\mu}_{T^{\prime}}(x,0)
that is the minimizer of (
26
) at
t=0
.

In the following theorem, we provide some intuition on how close
\hat{V}_{T^{\prime}}(\cdot,0)
will be to
{V}_{T^{\prime}}(\cdot,0)
, based on the approximation error
e_{T}=\hat{V}_{T}(x,0)-V_{T}(x,0)
.

Theorem 5.5
.

Denote as
\hat{x}_{T^{\prime}}
the trajectories of (
1
) under
u=\hat{\mu}_{T^{\prime}}
. Then, for all
x_{0}\in\Omega
:

|\hat{V}_{T^{\prime}}(x_{0},0)-V_{T^{\prime}}(x_{0},0)|\leq\max\{|e_{T}(x_{T^{\prime}}(T^{\prime}-T))|,|e_{T}(\hat{x}_{T^{\prime}}(T^{\prime}-T))|\}.

(27)

Proof 5.6
.

We have:

\begin{split}\hat{V}_{T^{\prime}}(x_{0},0)&=\min_{\mu}\hat{J}^{\prime}(\mu;~x_{0})\leq\hat{J}^{\prime}(\mu_{T^{\prime}};~x_{0})\\
&=\int_{0}^{T^{\prime}-T}\Big(Q(x_{T^{\prime}}(\tau))+r\left(\mu_{T^{\prime}}(x_{T^{\prime}}(\tau),\tau)\right)\Big)\textrm{d}\tau+\hat{V}_{T}(x_{T^{\prime}}(T^{\prime}-T),0)\\
&=\int_{0}^{T^{\prime}-T}\Big(Q(x_{T^{\prime}}(\tau))+r\left(\mu_{T^{\prime}}(x_{T^{\prime}}(\tau),\tau)\right)\Big)\textrm{d}\tau+{V}_{T}(x_{T^{\prime}}(T^{\prime}-T),0)+e_{T}(x_{T^{\prime}}(T^{\prime}-T))\\
&={V}_{T^{\prime}}(x_{0},0)+e_{T}(x_{T^{\prime}}(T^{\prime}-T)).\end{split}

(28)

Moreover:

\begin{split}{V}_{T^{\prime}}(x_{0},0)&=\min_{\mu}{J}^{\prime}(\mu;~x_{0})\leq{J}^{\prime}(\hat{\mu}_{T^{\prime}};~x_{0})\\
&=\int_{0}^{T^{\prime}-T}\Big(Q(\hat{x}_{T^{\prime}}(\tau))+r\left(\hat{\mu}_{T^{\prime}}(\hat{x}_{T^{\prime}}(\tau),\tau)\right)\Big)\textrm{d}\tau+{V}_{T}(\hat{x}_{T^{\prime}}(T^{\prime}-T),0)\\
&=\int_{0}^{T^{\prime}-T}\Big(Q(\hat{x}_{T^{\prime}}(\tau))+r\left(\hat{\mu}_{T^{\prime}}(\hat{x}_{T^{\prime}}(\tau),\tau)\right)\Big)\textrm{d}\tau+\hat{V}_{T}(\hat{x}_{T^{\prime}}(T^{\prime}-T),0)-e_{T}(\hat{x}_{T^{\prime}}(T^{\prime}-T))\\
&=\hat{V}_{T^{\prime}}(x_{0},0)-e_{T}(\hat{x}_{T^{\prime}}(T^{\prime}-T)).\end{split}

(29)

Combining (
28
)-(
29
), we obtain

e_{T}(\hat{x}_{T^{\prime}}(T^{\prime}-T))\leq\hat{V}_{T^{\prime}}(x_{0},0)-V_{T^{\prime}}(x_{0},0)\leq e_{T}(x_{T^{\prime}}(T^{\prime}-T)).

This yields (
27
).

Theorem
5.5
provides some intuition on how approximation errors in the estimation of the value function
V_{T}
affect the horizon-extending procedure of Subsection
5.2
. In particular, if the approximation error
e_{T}
is bounded by a constant
\epsilon>0
, then Theorem
5.5
implies the following error bound for the extended-horizon value function estimate
\hat{V}_{T^{\prime}}
:

|\hat{V}_{T^{\prime}}(x_{0},0)-V_{T^{\prime}}(x_{0},0)|\leq\epsilon.

(30)

Yet, the result of the Theorem is much stricter than (
30
). In particular, note that by Lemma
4.3
, one has
\lim_{T^{\prime}\rightarrow\infty}x_{T^{\prime}}(T^{\prime}-T)=0
, therefore the continuity of the approximation error implies
\lim_{T^{\prime}\rightarrow\infty}e_{T}(x_{T^{\prime}}(T^{\prime}-T))=e_{T}(0)
. Hence, if the basis functions of the PINN are chosen so that
\hat{V}_{T}(0,0)=0
, or if this condition is enforced through the residual
F_{in}
, then
e_{T}(0)=\hat{V}_{T}(0,0)-{V}_{T}(0,0)=0
, and thus
\lim_{T^{\prime}\rightarrow\infty}e_{T}(x_{T^{\prime}}(T^{\prime}-T))=e_{T}(0)=0
.
Accordingly, if the extended horizon length
T^{\prime}
is large enough, then this implies that
|e_{T}(x_{T^{\prime}}(T^{\prime}-T))|
will be small. Moreover, as the approximation capabilities of the neural network increase,
\hat{x}_{T^{\prime}}
converges uniformly to
x_{T^{\prime}}
, thus
e_{T}(\hat{x}_{T^{\prime}}(T^{\prime}-T))
also becomes smaller as the extended horizon length
T^{\prime}
increases. Combining these two observations and looking at equation (
27
), we conclude a much stronger result than (
30
): that a large enough horizon extension rules out the phenomenon of “accumulating” approximation errors when the method of Subsection
5.2
is applied multiple times.

Conclusion

We proposed a physics-informed machine learning framework to solve the infinite-horizon optimal control problem of nonlinear systems. Since the steady-state HJB equation has many solutions, we applied PINNs on a finite-horizon HJB that has a unique solution, and which provably uniformly approximates the infinite-horizon value function when the horizon length is large. A method to evaluate whether the horizon length is indeed large was also provided, as well as to extend it (in case it is not) with reduced computations. The robustness of the horizon-extending procedure to approximation errors was also studied, and simulations were performed to verify and clarify the theoretical results.

Future work includes extending this work to solve differential games defined over nonlinear dynamics.
