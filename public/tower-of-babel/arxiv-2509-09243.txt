A classification of Prüfer domains of integer-valued polynomials on algebras
Giulio Peruginelli, Nicholas J. Werner

Abstract
Let D D be an integrally closed domain with quotient field K K and A A a torsion-free D D -algebra that is finitely generated as a D D -module and such that A ∩ K = D A\cap K=D . We give a complete classification of those D D and A A for which the ring Int K ​ ( A ) = { f ∈ K ⁡ [ X ] ∣ f ⁡ ( A ) ⊆ A } \textnormal{Int}_{K}(A)=\{f\in K[X]\mid f(A)\subseteq A\} is a Prüfer domain. If D D is a semiprimitive domain, then we prove that Int K ​ ( A ) \textnormal{Int}_{K}(A) is Prüfer if and only if A A is commutative and isomorphic to a finite direct product of almost Dedekind domains with finite residue fields, each of them satisfying a double-boundedness condition on its ramification indices and residue field degrees.



Keywords: integer-valued polynomial, Prüfer domain, algebra, integrally closed domain, almost Dedekind domain.

Introduction

Let
D
be an integrally closed domain with quotient field
K
. We will always assume that
D
is properly contained in
K
. Let
A
be a
D
-algebra which is a torsion-free and finitely generated
D
-module. Let
B=A\otimes_{D}K
be the extended
K
-algebra; note that
B
is equal to the localization
(D\setminus\{0\})^{-1}A
. We can embed
K
and
A
inside
B
via the natural mappings
k\mapsto 1\otimes k
and
a\mapsto a\otimes 1
, and we will use this fact throughout this paper. For a subset
S
of
A
we define the ring of integer-valued polynomials over
S
as:

\textnormal{Int}_{K}(S,A)=\{f\in K[X]\mid f(S)\subseteq A\}.

When
S=A
, we set
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(A,A)=\textnormal{Int}_{K}(A)
. We will also assume throughout that
A\cap K=D
which amounts to say that
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(A)\subseteq\textnormal{Int}_{K}(D)=\textnormal{Int}(D)
, the ring of integer-valued polynomials over
D
.

Integer-valued polynomials have a long history in commutative ring theory. One problem that attracted significant interest
[
4
,
13
,
5
,
17
]
was to classify the domains
D
such that
\textnormal{Int}(D)
is a Prüfer domain. Necessary and sufficient conditions on
D
for this to occur were determined in
[
18
]
. Since the late 2000s, attention has turned to rings of the form
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(A)
and
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(S,A)

[
33
,
19
,
7
,
9
,
10
,
22
,
8
,
11
,
14
,
20
,
21
]
. Here, as in the traditional case where
A=D
, researchers have studied situations in which these rings are or are not Prüfer. For instance, when
D=\mathbb{Z}
and
A
is a ring of algebraic integers, it is known that
\textnormal{Int}_{\mathbb{Q}}(A)
is Prüfer
[
19
, Theorem 3.7]
. By contrast, if
D
is a Dedekind domain with finite residue fields and
A
is the matrix algebra
A=M_{n}(D)
with
n\geq 2
, then
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(A)
is never integrally closed
[
28
, Corollary 3.4]
, hence cannot be Prüfer.

In this paper we give a complete answer to
[
3
, Problem 28]
, namely, establishing exactly when
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(A)
is a Prüfer domain. Since
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(A)\subseteq\textnormal{Int}(D)
and overrings of Prüfer domains are Prüfer, in order for
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(A)
to be Prüfer it is necessary that
\textnormal{Int}(D)
be Prüfer. Recall that
D
is an almost Dedekind domain if
D_{M}
is a discrete valuation ring for each maximal ideal
M
of
D
. Note that a Noetherian almost Dedekind domain is a Dedekind domain. In order for
\textnormal{Int}(D)
to be Prüfer, it is necessary (but not sufficient) for
D
to be an almost Dedekind domain with finite residue fields
[
4
, Proposition 6.3]
. For such a domain, we adopt the following notations and vocabulary.

Notation 1.1
.

Let
D
be an almost Dedekind domain with finite residue fields.

•

The domain
D
contains a principal ideal domain
D_{0}
, the structure of which depends on the characteristic of
K
. If
\text{char}(K)=0
, then
D_{0}\cong\mathbb{Z}
. If
\text{char}(K)=p
, for some integral prime
p
, then
D_{0}\cong\mathbb{F}_{p}[t]
, the univariate polynomial ring over the finite field
\mathbb{F}_{p}
.

•

Let
\mathfrak{p}
denote a nonzero prime element of
D_{0}
. Explicitly, if
D_{0}\cong\mathbb{Z}
, then
\mathfrak{p}
is an integral prime; otherwise,
\mathfrak{p}
is a polynomial irreducible over
\mathbb{F}_{p}
.

•

For each prime
P
of
D
, let
v_{P}
be the normalized
P
-adic valuation on
K
.

•

For each
\mathfrak{p}
and each
P
over
\mathfrak{p}
, define

\quad e_{P,\mathfrak{p}}=v_{P}(\mathfrak{p})\quad\text{ and }\quad f_{P,\mathfrak{p}}=[(D/P):(D_{0}/\mathfrak{p}D_{0})].

•

For each
\mathfrak{p}
, define

E_{\mathfrak{p}}:=\{e_{P,\mathfrak{p}}\mid P|\mathfrak{p}D_{0}\}\quad\text{ and }\quad F_{\mathfrak{p}}:=\{f_{P,\mathfrak{p}}\mid P|\mathfrak{p}D_{0}\}.

•

We say that
D
satisfies the
double-boundedness condition
if, for each
\mathfrak{p}
, both
E_{\mathfrak{p}}
and
F_{\mathfrak{p}}
are bounded.

Definition 1.2
.

A domain
D
will be called an ADDB-domain (short for “almost Dedekind, doubly bounded”) if
D
is an almost Dedekind domain with finite residue fields that satisfies the double-boundedness condition from Notation
1.1
.

By the following theorem, ADDB-domains are exactly those for which
\textnormal{Int}(D)
is Prüfer.

Theorem 1.3
.

(
[
18
, Theorem 2.5]
)
Let
D
be an integral domain that is not a field. Then,
\textnormal{Int}(D)
is Prüfer if and only if
D
is an ADDB-domain.

Thus, in order for
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(A)
to be Prüfer, it is necessary that
D
be an ADDB-domain. Since Prüfer domains are integrally closed, we can obtain further information on when
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(A)
is Prüfer by studying the integral closure of
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(A)
. For this, the elements of
A
that are integral over
D
are prominent. The fact that
A
is finitely generated as a
D
-module implies that
B=A\otimes_{D}K
is finitely generated as a
K
-module. Hence, each element of
B
is algebraic over
K
.

Definition 1.4
.

We denote by
\overline{D}
the absolute integral closure of
D
in a fixed algebraic closure
\overline{K}
of
K
. For an element
b\in B
, we let
\mu_{b}\in K[X]
be the minimal monic polynomial of
b
over
K
and let
\Omega_{b}
be the set of roots of
\mu_{b}
inside
\overline{K}
. When
S\subseteq B
, we set
\Omega_{S}=\bigcup_{b\in S}\Omega_{b}
. We say that
b
is
integral over
D
if
b
solves a monic polynomial over
D
, we let
A^{\prime}:=\{b\in B\mid b\text{ is integral over }D\}
, and we say that
A
is
integrally closed
if
A=A^{\prime}
.

Our assumptions on
A
imply that
A\subseteq A^{\prime}
. However, there is no guarantee that
A
is equal to
A^{\prime}
, and in fact
A^{\prime}
may fail to be a ring because
A
is allowed to be noncommutative (this behavior is similar to that of
D
-orders in
K
-algebras, cf.
[
30
, Section 8]
). Nevertheless, in some cases the set
A^{\prime}
affords a description of the integral closure of
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(A)
.

Definition 1.5
.

Let
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(A,A^{\prime}):=\{f\in K[X]\mid f(A)\subseteq A^{\prime}\}
. For each
n\geq 1
, let

\Lambda_{n}=\{\alpha\in\overline{K}\mid\alpha\text{ solves a monic polynomial in $D[X]$ of degree $n$}\}.

For
\mathscr{E}\subseteq\Lambda_{n}
, let
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(\mathscr{E},\Lambda_{n}):=\{f\in K[X]\mid f(\mathscr{E})\subseteq\Lambda_{n}\}
.

Theorem 1.6
.

(
[
27
, Theorem 10 & 13]
)
Let
D
be an integrally closed domain. Let
n
be the vector space dimension of
B
over
K
. Then,
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(A,A^{\prime})
is an integrally closed ring, and
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(A,A^{\prime})=\textnormal{Int}_{K}(\Omega_{A},\Lambda_{n})
. Moreover, if all residue rings (by nonzero ideals) of
D
are finite, then
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(A,A^{\prime})
is equal to the integral closure of
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(A)
.

Note that if
D
is a Dedekind domain with finite residue fields, then Theorem
1.6
applies and
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(A,A^{\prime})
is the integral closure of
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(A)
. It is not known whether the same is true when
D
is a general ADDB-domain (i.e. if
D
is not Noetherian).

We now state our main result, which says in part that
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(A)
is Prüfer if and only if
A=A^{\prime}
. The theorem also provides structural descriptions of
A
and
B
that are necessary and sufficient for
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(A)
to be Prüfer. Recall that for a
D
-module
M
,
M_{P}
denotes the localization of
M
at
D\setminus P
. Also, recall that a ring
R
is semiprimitive when the Jacobson radical of
R
is
\{0\}
.

Theorem 1.7
.

Let
D
be an ADDB-domain. Let
A
be a
D
-algebra that is torsion-free and finitely generated as a
D
-module, and such that
A\cap K=D
. Let
B=A\otimes_{D}K
.

(1)

The following are equivalent.

(i)

\textnormal{Int}_{K}(A)
is Prüfer.

(ii)

\textnormal{Int}_{K}(A)=\textnormal{Int}_{K}(A,A^{\prime})
.

(iii)

A=A^{\prime}
.

(iv)

For all
a\in A
,
A\cap K[a]
is integrally closed in
K[a]
.

(v)

For some
t\geq 1
,
B\cong\prod_{i=1}^{t}\mathcal{D}_{i}
, where each
\mathcal{D}_{i}
is a division ring over
K
, and
A\cong\prod_{i=1}^{t}A_{i}
, where each
A_{i}
is an integrally closed domain (not necessarily commutative) in
\mathcal{D}_{i}
.

(vi)

For each prime ideal
P
of
D
,
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(A)_{P}
is Prüfer.

(vii)

For each prime ideal
P
of
D
,
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(A_{P})
is Prüfer.

(viii)

For each prime ideal
P
of
D
,
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(A_{P})
is integrally closed.

(2)

Assume
D
is semiprimitive. Then,
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(A)
is Prüfer if and only if
A\cong\prod_{i=1}^{t}A_{i}
, where
A_{i}
is the integral closure of
D
in a finite field extension
F_{i}/K
. In this case, each
A_{i}
is an ADDB-domain, and
A
is a commutative ring.

Each condition in Theorem
1.7
(1) implies that
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(A)
is integrally closed. In analogy with condition (viii), we suspect that
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(A)
is Prüfer if and only if
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(A)
is integrally closed; however, we have only been able to prove this with additional assumptions on
D
or
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(A)
.

Conjecture 1.8
.

Let
D
and
A
be as in the statement of Theorem
1.7
. If
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(A)
is integrally closed, then
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(A)
is Prüfer.

The full resolution of Conjecture
1.8
remains an open problem. We can show that the conjecture is true if
D
is an ADDB-domain that is also a Dedekind domain or, more generally, if
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(A)
is well-behaved with respect to localization at primes of
D
:

Theorem 1.9
.

Let
D
and
A
be as in the statement of Theorem
1.7
. If
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(A)_{P}=\textnormal{Int}_{K}(A_{P})
for all primes
P\subset D
, then Conjecture
1.8
is true.

In particular, Theorem
1.9
holds when
D
is Dedekind, because in that case
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(A)_{P}=\textnormal{Int}_{K}(A_{P})
by
[
29
, Lemma 3.2]
.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we classify the finite subsets
S\subseteq A
such that
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(S,A)
is Prüfer. This gives us some control on the overrings of
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(A)
, and these are used in Section
3
where we derive a number of necessary conditions on

in order for
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(A)
to be Prüfer. In Theorem
3.4
, we prove that if
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(A)
is Prüfer, then
A
and
B
have the structure specified in condition (v) of Theorem
1.7
(1). Moreover, we prove Theorem
1.7
(2) (see Corollary
3.13
), which shows that if
D
is semiprimitive, then
A
must be commutative. If
D
is not semiprimitive, then it is possible that
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(A)
is Prüfer and
A
is noncommutative; an example of such an algebra

is given in Section
5
at the end of the paper. We complete the proof of Theorem
1.7
in Section
4
by constructing a family of Prüfer domains contained in

. We show that
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(A,A^{\prime})
must contain one of these Prüfer domains, and hence is itself Prüfer (Corollary

). Under any of conditions (iii) – (viii) in Theorem
1.7
(1), we demonstrate that
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(A)=\textnormal{Int}_{K}(A,A^{\prime})
, and conclude
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(A)
is Prüfer. Section
4
closes with a discussion of Conjecture
1.8
and Theorem
1.9
.

2
Finite subsets of
A

Our first goal is to determine when
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(S,A)
is Prüfer for a finite subset
S\subseteq A
. Since Prüfer domains are integrally closed, we begin by studying the integral closure of
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(S,A)
. In
[
23
]
, subsets of
\overline{D}
were used to describe the integral closure of
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(S,A)
. Recall from Definition
1.4
that for
b\in B
and
S\subseteq B
, we let
\Omega_{b}\subseteq\overline{K}
be the set of roots of
\mu_{b}
in
\overline{K}
, and
\Omega_{S}=\bigcup_{b\in S}\Omega_{b}
.

Theorem 2.1
.

(1)

(
[
23
, Proposition 4.2]
) Let
\Omega
be a finite subset of
\overline{D}
. Then,
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(\Omega,\overline{D})
is Prüfer if and only if
D
is Prüfer.

(2)

(
[
23
, Corollary 1.5]
) Let
S
be a finite subset of
A
. Then, the integral closure of
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(S,A)
is equal to
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(\Omega_{S},\overline{D})=\{f\in K[X]\mid f(\Omega_{S})\subseteq\overline{D}\}
. Thus,
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(S,A)
is Prüfer if and only if
D
is Prüfer and
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(S,A)
is integrally closed.

It remains to determine conditions on
S
and
A
under which
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(S,A)
is integrally closed. We begin by considering the case where
S=\{a\}
for some
a\in A
. Even when
S
is a singleton set, it is not always apparent whether or not
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(S,A)
will be integrally closed. Consider the following two examples involving
2\times 2
matrices over
\mathbb{Z}
.

Example 2.2
.

Let
D=\mathbb{Z}
,
A=M_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
, and
\mu(X)=X^{2}-2X-4
, with roots
1\pm\sqrt{5}
. Let
\theta=\tfrac{1+\sqrt{5}}{2}
. Let
a=[\begin{smallmatrix}0&4\\
1&2\end{smallmatrix}]
and
b=[\begin{smallmatrix}0&2\\
2&2\end{smallmatrix}]
, both of which are matrices in
A
with minimal polynomial
\mu
. Since
a
and
b
share the same minimal polynomial, they are conjugate over
GL(2,\mathbb{Q})
, although one may check that they are not conjugate over
GL(2,\mathbb{Z})
. We will show that
\textnormal{Int}_{\mathbb{Q}}(\{a\},A)
is not integrally closed, but
\textnormal{Int}_{\mathbb{Q}}(\{b\},A)
is integrally closed.

Let
\mathcal{R}=\textnormal{Int}_{\mathbb{Q}}(\{1\pm\sqrt{5}\},\mathbb{Z}[\theta])
. Then,
\mathcal{R}
is the integral closure of both
\textnormal{Int}_{\mathbb{Q}}(\{a\},A)
and
\textnormal{Int}_{\mathbb{Q}}(\{b\},A)
by Theorem
2.1
. To see that
\textnormal{Int}_{\mathbb{Q}}(\{a\},A)
is not integrally closed, note that
\tfrac{X}{2}\in\mathcal{R}
, but
\tfrac{a}{2}\notin A
. Hence,
\textnormal{Int}_{\mathbb{Q}}(\{a\},A)\subsetneqq\mathcal{R}
.

Next, we show that
\textnormal{Int}_{\mathbb{Q}}(\{b\},A)=\mathcal{R}
. We have
\textnormal{Int}_{\mathbb{Q}}(\{b\},A)\subseteq\mathcal{R}
by Theorem
2.1
. For the reverse containment, let
f\in\mathcal{R}
. Then,
f(1+\sqrt{5})\in\mathbb{Z}[\theta]
, so there exist
c_{0},c_{1}\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
f(1+\sqrt{5})=c_{0}+c_{1}\theta
. Note that
\mathbb{Q}(b)\cong\mathbb{Q}[X]/(\mu(X))\cong\mathbb{Q}(1+\sqrt{5})
, so there exists a ring isomorphism
\phi:\mathbb{Q}(1+\sqrt{5})\to\mathbb{Q}(b)
such that
\phi(1+\sqrt{5})=b
. As
\phi(\theta)=\tfrac{b}{2}=[\begin{smallmatrix}0&1\\
1&1\end{smallmatrix}]
and polynomials in
\mathbb{Q}[X]
commute with
\phi
, we have

f(b)=f(\phi(1+\sqrt{5}))=\phi(f(1+\sqrt{5}))=c_{0}+c_{1}[\begin{smallmatrix}0&1\\
1&1\end{smallmatrix}]\in A.

This shows that
f\in\textnormal{Int}_{\mathbb{Q}}(\{b\},A)
. Thus,
\textnormal{Int}_{\mathbb{Q}}(\{b\},A)=\mathcal{R}
is integrally closed.

Example 2.3
.

Again let
D=\mathbb{Z}
and
A=M_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
. Let
k\in\mathbb{Z}
,
k>0
. Let
a=[\begin{smallmatrix}0&k\\
k&0\end{smallmatrix}]
and
b=[\begin{smallmatrix}k&0\\
0&-k\end{smallmatrix}]
. Then,
\mu(X)=X^{2}-k^{2}
is the minimal polynomial of both
a
and
b
. As in the previous example,
a
and
b
are conjugate over
GL(2,\mathbb{Q})
, but are not conjugate over
GL(2,\mathbb{Z})
. We demonstrate that
\textnormal{Int}_{\mathbb{Q}}(\{a\},A)
is not integrally closed, but
\textnormal{Int}_{\mathbb{Q}}(\{b\},A)
is integrally closed.

This time, the integral closure of both
\textnormal{Int}_{\mathbb{Q}}(\{a\},A)
and
\textnormal{Int}_{\mathbb{Q}}(\{b\},A)
is
\mathcal{R}=\textnormal{Int}_{\mathbb{Q}}(\{\pm k\},\mathbb{Z})
. We have
\tfrac{X-k}{2k}\in\mathcal{R}\setminus\textnormal{Int}_{\mathbb{Q}}(\{a\},A)
, because

\tfrac{1}{2k}(a-k)=\begin{bmatrix}-k/2&k/2\\
k/2&-k/2\end{bmatrix}\notin A.

Thus,
\textnormal{Int}_{\mathbb{Q}}(\{a\},A)
is not integrally closed. However, since
b
is a diagonal matrix, for each
f\in\mathcal{R}
we have
f(b)=[\begin{smallmatrix}f(k)&0\\
0&f(-k)\end{smallmatrix}]
, and this is in
A
because
f(\pm k)\in\mathbb{Z}
. Hence,
\textnormal{Int}_{\mathbb{Q}}(\{b\},A)=\mathcal{R}
.

One distinguishing feature between
a
and
b
in these examples is the structure of the algebras
A\cap K[a]
and
A\cap K[b]
. For each
a\in A
,
K[a]
is a commutative
K
-subalgebra of
B
and the intersection
A\cap K[a]
is a
D
-subalgebra of
K[a]
. Recall that a commutative ring
R
(possibly with zero divisors) is said to be integrally closed if it is integrally closed in its total ring of fractions
T
(i.e. every element of
T
which satisfies a monic polynomial over
R
belongs to
R
).

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
a\in A
, let
R=A\cap K[a]
, and let
T
be the total ring of fractions of
R
.

(1)

T=K[a]
.

(2)

R
is integrally closed if and only if
R
is equal to the integral closure of
D
in
K[a]
.

Proof.

(1) Recall that
\mu_{a}\in D[X]
is the minimal polynomial of
a
over
D
. Factor
\mu_{a}
over
K
as
\mu_{a}(X)=\prod_{i=1}^{t}p_{i}(X)^{e_{i}}
, where each
p_{i}\in K[x]
is irreducible and each
e_{i}\geq 1
. Then,

K[a]\cong K[x]/(\mu_{a}(X))\cong\prod_{i=1}^{t}K[x]/(p_{i}(X)^{e_{i}}).

Each ring
K[x]/(p_{i}(X)^{e_{i}})
is local with maximal ideal generated by
p_{i}(X)
. In each such ring, the set of zero divisors is equal to the maximal ideal, and all other elements are units. Hence, each of these rings is equal to its own total ring of fractions. It follows that
K[a]
is also equal to its own total ring of fractions. Now,
D[a]\subseteq R\subseteq K[a]
, and considering the total rings of fractions of these rings, we see that
K[a]\subseteq T\subseteq K[a]
. Thus,
T=K[a]
.

(2) Let
R^{\prime}
be the integral closure of
R
in
T
, and let
D^{\prime}
be the integral closure of
D
in
K[a]
. We have
D\subseteq R
, and
T=K[a]
by (1), so
D^{\prime}\subseteq R^{\prime}
. Conversely, let
b\in R^{\prime}\subseteq K[a]
. Then, there exists a monic
f\in R[X]\subseteq A[X]
such that
f(b)=0
. Since each coefficient of
f
is in
A
and
A
is integral over
D
,
b
is integral over
D
. Hence,
b\in D^{\prime}
and
R^{\prime}\subseteq D^{\prime}
.
∎

In both Example
2.2
and Example
2.3
, the ring
A\cap K[a]
is not integrally closed. In the former example,
a/2\in K[A]\setminus A
is integral over
D
, and in the latter
(a-k)/2k\in K[a]\setminus A
is integral over
D
. As we now proceed to show,
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(\{a\},A)
is integrally closed if and only if
A\cap K[a]
is integrally closed.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
b\in B
.

(1)

b
is integral over
D
if and only if
\Omega_{b}\subseteq\overline{D}
.

(2)

For each
f\in K[X]
, we have
\Omega_{f(b)}=f(\Omega_{b})
.

Proof.

Let
n
be the vector space dimension of
B
over
K
. Then,
B
embeds into the matrix ring
M_{n}(K)
, and under this embedding
K
corresponds to the field of scalar matrices in
M_{n}(K)
. Thus, each
b\in B
corresponds to a matrix
M_{b}\in M_{n}(K)
with minimal polynomial
\mu_{b}\in K[X]
. The eigenvalues of
M_{b}
are exactly the roots of
\mu_{b}
, and it is shown in
[
27
, Lemma 2]
that
\mu_{b}\in D[X]
if and only if each eigenvalue of
M_{b}
is in
\overline{D}
. This proves (1). For (2), we use the fact that for a matrix
C\in M_{n}(K)
, a scalar
\lambda\in K
, and a polynomial
f\in K[X]
,
\lambda
is an eigenvalue of
C
if and only if
f(\lambda)
is an eigenvalue of
f(C)
.
∎

Proposition 2.6
.

Let
a\in A
. The following are equivalent.

(1)

\textnormal{Int}_{K}(\{a\},A)
is integrally closed.

(2)

\textnormal{Int}_{K}(\{a\},A)=\textnormal{Int}_{K}(\Omega_{a},\overline{D})
.

(3)

A\cap K[a]
is integrally closed.

Proof.

(1)
\Leftrightarrow
(2) This follows from Theorem
2.1
, because
\{a\}
is finite.

(3)
\Rightarrow
(2) Assume that
A\cap K[a]
is integrally closed. From Theorem
2.1
,
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(\Omega_{a},\overline{D})
is the integral closure of
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(\{a\},A)
, and so it is always true that
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(\{a\},A)\subseteq\textnormal{Int}_{K}(\Omega_{a},\overline{D})
. Let
f\in\textnormal{Int}_{K}(\Omega_{a},\overline{D})
. Then,
f(a)\in K[a]
, because
f\in K[X]
, and
f(a)
is integral over
D
by Lemma
2.5
because
\Omega_{f(a)}=f(\Omega_{a})\subseteq\overline{D}
by Lemma
2.5
. By Lemma
2.4
,
A\cap K[a]
is the integral closure of
D
in
K[a]
. Thus,
f(a)\in A\cap K[a]\subseteq A
,
f\in\textnormal{Int}_{K}(\{a\},A)
, and
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(\{a\},A)=\textnormal{Int}_{K}(\Omega_{a},\overline{D})
.

(1)
\Rightarrow
(3) Assume that
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(\{a\},A)
is integrally closed. Let
b\in K[a]
be integral over
D
. Then,
b=f(a)
for some
f\in K[X]
, and there exists a monic
g\in D[X]
such that
g(b)=0
. So,
g\circ f\in\textnormal{Int}_{K}(\{a\},A)
. Since
g\in D[X]\subseteq\textnormal{Int}_{K}(\{a\},A)
, this implies that
f
is integral over
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(\{a\},A)
, and hence
f\in\textnormal{Int}_{K}(\{a\},A)
. Thus,
b=f(a)\in A\cap K[a]
. This shows that
A\cap K[a]
is the integral closure of
D
in
K[a]
, so
A\cap K[a]
is integrally closed by Lemma
2.4
.
∎

We now have necessary and sufficient conditions on
A
and
D
to describe when
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(\{a\},A)
is Prüfer. The conditions extend naturally to the case where
S\subseteq A
is finite.

Theorem 2.7
.

Let
a\in A
. The following are equivalent.

(1)

\textnormal{Int}_{K}(\{a\},A)
is Prüfer.

(2)

D
is Prüfer and
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(\{a\},A)
is integrally closed.

(3)

D
is Prüfer and
A\cap K[a]
is integrally closed.

Proof.

Apply Theorem
2.1
and Proposition
2.6
.
∎

Corollary 2.8
.

Let
D
be a Prüfer domain and let
S\subset A
be a finite subset. Then
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(S,A)
is Prüfer if and only if for each
a\in S
,
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(\{a\},A)
is Prüfer.

Proof.

We have just to observe that

\textnormal{Int}_{K}(S,A)=\bigcap_{a\in S}\textnormal{Int}_{K}(\{a\},A).

If
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(S,A)
is Prüfer then each of its overrings
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(\{a\},A)
is Prüfer as well. Conversely, if
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(\{a\},A)
is Prüfer, then by Theorem
2.1
,
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(\{a\},A)=\textnormal{Int}_{K}(\Omega_{a},\overline{D})
. The intersection of these rings over all
a\in S
is equal to
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(\Omega_{S},\overline{D})
which is Prüfer because
\Omega_{S}=\bigcup_{a\in S}\Omega_{a}
is finite.
∎

3
Necessary conditions

In this section, we will establish necessary conditions for
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(A)
to be Prüfer. Since one of our standard assumptions on
A
is that
A\cap K=D
, we have
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(A)\subseteq\textnormal{Int}(D)
. Thus, if
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(A)
is Prüfer, then so is
\textnormal{Int}(D)
. If the reader wishes, for the remainder of this section they may consider
D
to be an ADDB-domain (i.e.
D
is such that
\textnormal{Int}(D)
is Prüfer), since this is the assumption we will make in our major theorems. However, we state our intermediary results using only the properties of the integrally closed domain
D
that are needed to reach the desired conclusion.

Recall that an element
b\in B
is integral over
D
if
b
solves a monic polynomial in
D[X]
, and that
A
is integrally closed if
A=A^{\prime}=\{b\in B\mid b\text{ is integral over }D\}
. While
A^{\prime}
may not be a ring,
A\cap K[a]
is a commutative subring of
K[a]
for each
a\in A
. So, the integral closure of
A\cap K[a]
is a commutative ring, and these rings allow us to describe when
A=A^{\prime}
.

Lemma 3.1
.

The ring
A
is integrally closed if and only if, for each
a\in A
,
A\cap K[a]
is integrally closed.

Proof.

Clearly, if
A=A^{\prime}
and
a\in A
, then every element
a^{\prime}
of
K[a]
that is integral over
A\cap K[a]
is also integral over
D
. Hence,
a^{\prime}\in A
and
A\cap K[a]
is integrally closed. Conversely, suppose that
A\cap K[a]
is integrally closed for each
a\in A
and let
a^{\prime}\in A^{\prime}
. Since
a^{\prime}\in B=(D\setminus\{0\})^{-1}A
, there exist
a\in A
and
d\in D
such that
a^{\prime}=\frac{a}{d}
. So,
a^{\prime}\in K[a]
and is integral over
D\subset D[a]\subseteq A\cap K[a]
. It follows that
a^{\prime}\in A\cap K[a]\subset A
.
∎

Recall that a ring
R
is reduced if it has no nonzero nilpotent elements. One obstruction to
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(A)
being Prüfer is the presence of nonzero nilpotent elements of
A
. In the next two lemmas, we show that if
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(A)
is Prüfer, then
A
must be reduced. We thank Hwankoo Kim for pointing out Lemma
3.2
.

Lemma 3.2
.

The
D
-topology on
A
is Hausdorff. That is,
\bigcap_{d\in D\setminus\{0\}}dA=\{0\}
.

Proof.

Let
I=\bigcap_{d\in D\setminus\{0\}}dA
. Then,
I
is a divisible
D
-submodule of
A
because
dI=I
for all nonzero
d\in D
. Hence,
I
is injective because
A
is torsion-free
[
15
, Theorem 2.4.5(3)]
, and so
I
is a direct summand of
A
. Thus,
I
is a finitely generated
D
-submodule of
A
. Let
d\in D
such that
d
is not a unit of
D
, which exists because
D
is not a field. By
[
1
, Corollary 2.5]
, there exists
c\in D
such that
(1+cd)I=\{0\}
. Since
d
is not a unit of
D
,
1+cd\neq 0
. We conclude that
I=\{0\}
.
∎

Lemma 3.3
.

(1)

Let
a\in A
. If
A\cap K[a]
is integrally closed, then
K[a]
is reduced.

(2)

If
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(A)
is Prüfer, then both
A
and
B
are reduced.

Proof.

(1) We prove the contrapositive. Suppose that
K[a]
is not reduced. Then, there exists a nonzero
b\in K[a]
and
n\geq 2
such that
b^{n}=0
. Let
f\in K[X]
be such that
b=f(a)
. Write
f(X)=g(X)/d
, where
g\in D[X]
and
d\in D
. Since
f(a)\neq 0
,
g(a)
is also nonzero. So, there exists a nonzero
c\in D
such that
g(a)\notin cA
by Lemma
3.2
. Then, the polynomial
g(x)/c\notin\textnormal{Int}_{K}(\{a\},A)
. However,
f(a)^{n}=0
, so

\Big(\dfrac{g(X)}{c}\Big)^{n}=\Big(\dfrac{df(X)}{c}\Big)^{n}\in\textnormal{Int}_{K}(\{a\},A).

Thus,
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(\{a\},A)
is not integrally closed. By Proposition
2.6
,
A\cap K[a]
is not integrally closed.

(2) Suppose that
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(A)
is Prüfer, but
A
is not reduced. Let
a\in A
be nonzero and nilpotent. Since
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(A)
is Prüfer, its overring
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(\{a\},A)
is also Prüfer, and hence is integrally closed. But,
K[a]
is not reduced, so by (1),
A\cap K[a]
is not integrally closed. By Proposition
2.6
,
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(\{a\},A)
is not integrally closed, which is a contradiction. Thus,
A
must be reduced, and it follows that
B
is also reduced.
∎

Theorem 3.4
.

Suppose that
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(A)
is a Prüfer domain. Then,
A=A^{\prime}
, and
B
and
A
have the following structure:

•

For some
t\geq 1
,
B\cong\prod_{i=1}^{t}\mathcal{D}_{i}
, where
\mathcal{D}_{i}
is a division ring over
K
for each
i=1,\ldots,t
, and

•

A\cong\prod_{i=1}^{t}A_{i}
, where
A_{i}\subset\mathcal{D}_{i}
is an integrally closed domain (but is not necessarily commutative) for each
i=1,\ldots,t
.

Proof.

Since
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(A)
is Prüfer, each overring of the form
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(\{a\},A)
, where
a\in A
, is also Prüfer. By Theorem
2.7
,
A\cap K[a]
is integrally closed for each
a\in A
. So,
A=A^{\prime}
by Lemma
3.1
. Also, both
A
and
B
are reduced by Lemma
3.3
.

Now, since
B
has finite dimension over
K
it is artinian (see e.g.
[
6
, §23, p. 158]
), and
B
contains no nonzero nilpotent ideal because it is reduced. By Wedderburn’s theorems
[
30
, Theorem 7.1, Theorem 7.4]
,
B
is semisimple, thus isomorphic to a finite direct product of matrix rings
M_{n_{i}}(\mathcal{D}_{i})
, where
\mathcal{D}_{i}
is a division ring over (an isomorphic copy of)
K
for each
i
. If any
n_{i}\geq 2
, then
M_{n_{i}}(\mathcal{D}_{i})
—and hence
B
—contains nonzero nilpotent elements, which is impossible because
B
is reduced. Hence,
B\cong\prod_{i=1}^{t}\mathcal{D}_{i}
for some
t\geq 1
.

For the structure of
A
, let
e_{i}=1_{\mathcal{D}_{i}}
for each
1\leq i\leq t
. Then,
\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{t}\}
is the set of orthogonal idempotents related to the above decomposition of
B
. Each
e_{i}
is a root of
X^{2}-X\in D[X]
, and so is in
A
because
A=A^{\prime}
. Thus,
A
decomposes as
\prod_{i=1}^{t}A_{i}
, where
A_{i}=Ae_{i}\subset Be_{i}=\mathcal{D}_{i}
; in particular,
A_{i}
is a (possibly noncommutative) domain for each
i
. Finally, assume
b_{i}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{D}_{i}
is integral over
De_{i}\cong D
with minimal polynomial
\mu_{i}(X)\in D[X]
. Then, the element
b=(b_{1},\ldots,b_{t})\in B
with
b_{i}=b_{i}^{\prime}
and
b_{j}=0
for
j\neq i
is a root of
X\mu_{i}(X)
, and hence is integral over
D
. Since
A=A^{\prime}
, it follows that
b_{i}^{\prime}\in A_{i}
, and so
A_{i}=A_{i}^{\prime}
.
∎

For any ring
R
, let
\mathscr{J}(R)
be the Jacobson radical of
R
. We recall that
R
is
semiprimitive
if
\mathscr{J}(R)=\{0\}
. If
D
is semiprimitive (which occurs, for instance, when
D=\mathbb{Z}
), we can impose even stronger necessary conditions on
A
in order for
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(A)
to be Prüfer. To do this, we first show that in some cases the existence of a nonzero nilpotent element in a simple residue ring of
A
is enough to force
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(A)
to not be Prüfer.

Lemma 3.5
.

Assume that
D
and
A
satisfy both conditions (i) and (ii) below:

(i)

D
contains a principal ideal
\pi D
of finite index.

(ii)

A/\pi A
has a simple residue ring isomorphic to
M_{n}(F)
, where
n\geq 2
and
F
is a finite field.

Then, the following hold.

(1)

There exists
a\in A
such that
a^{2}\in\pi^{2}A
, but
a\notin\pi A
.

(2)

\textnormal{Int}_{K}(A)
is not Prüfer.

Proof.

(1) Let
R_{1}=A/\pi A
and
R_{2}=A/\pi^{2}A
, which have Jacobson radicals
J_{1}:=\mathscr{J}(R_{1})
and
J_{2}:=\mathscr{J}(R_{2})
. By (i),
D/\pi D
is finite, and
A
is finitely generated as a
D
-module, so
R_{1}
is finite. So,
R_{2}/J_{2}\cong R_{1}/J_{1}
is a finite semisimple ring. By (ii),
R_{2}/J_{2}
contains a direct summand
S\cong M_{n}(F)
.

Write
1_{S}=\overline{e_{1}}+\cdots+\overline{e_{n}}
for primitive orthogonal idempotents
\overline{e_{1}},\ldots,\overline{e_{n}}\in S
. Then,
\overline{e_{1}}S\overline{e_{2}}\neq 0
in
R_{2}/J_{2}
, and we may lift
\overline{e_{1}}
and
\overline{e_{2}}
to
R_{2}
(see e.g.
[
16
, Prop. 21.25]
). Thus,
R_{2}
contains orthogonal idempotents
e_{1}
and
e_{2}
such that
e_{1}R_{2}e_{2}\not\subseteq J_{2}
. Hence, there exists
b\in e_{1}R_{2}e_{2}
such that
b\notin\pi R_{2}\subseteq J_{2}
. However,
b^{2}=0
because
(e_{1}R_{2}e_{2})(e_{1}R_{2}e_{2})=\{0\}
. It follows that the desired element
a
exists in
A
.

(2) Let
a\in A
satisfy the conclusion of (1). Then,
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(\{a\},A)
is not integrally closed, because
X^{2}/\pi^{2}\in\textnormal{Int}_{K}(\{a\},A)
, but
X/\pi\notin\textnormal{Int}_{K}(\{a\},A)
. Since
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(A)\subseteq\textnormal{Int}_{K}(\{a\},A)
, this implies that
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(A)
is not Prüfer.
∎

Theorem 3.6
.

Let
D
be a Prüfer domain and assume that
A
contains a maximal ideal
\mathfrak{m}
such that
A/\mathfrak{m}\cong M_{n}(F)
, where
n\geq 2
and
F
is a finite field. Then,
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(A)
is not Prüfer.

Proof.

Let
P=D\cap\mathfrak{m}
. We first verify that
P
is a prime ideal of
D
. Certainly,
P
is a proper ideal of
D
because
1\notin P
. Let
cd\in P
. Then,
cd\in\mathfrak{m}
, and since both
c
and
d
are central in
A
, we have
cAd\subseteq\mathfrak{m}
. The ring
A
is noncommutative, but maximal ideals in noncommutative rings are still prime
[
16
, p. 165]
, so the condition
cAd\subseteq\mathfrak{m}
implies that either
c\in\mathfrak{m}
or
d\in\mathfrak{m}

[
16
, Prop. 10.2]
. Thus, either
c
or
d
is in
P
.

Next, recall that
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(A)\subseteq\textnormal{Int}(D)
. If
P
has infinite index in
D
, then by
[
2
, Proposition I.3.4]
we have
\textnormal{Int}(D)\subseteq D_{P}[X]
and so
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(A)
cannot be Prüfer. So, assume
[D:P]<\infty
and consider the
D_{P}
-algebra
A_{P}=(D\setminus P)^{-1}A
. Since
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(A)\subseteq\textnormal{Int}_{K}(A_{P})
, it suffices to show that
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(A_{P})
is not Prüfer. Because
D
is Prüfer,
D_{P}
is a valuation domain. If
PD_{P}
is not principal, then by
[
2
, Proposition I.3.16]
we have that
\textnormal{Int}(D_{P})=D_{P}[X]
, so the subring
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(A_{P})
cannot be Prüfer. Suppose now that
PD_{P}
is principal and let
\pi\in D_{P}
be a generator of
PD_{P}
Then,
A_{P}/\pi A_{P}
has
M_{n}(F)
as a residue ring and
[D_{P}:\pi D_{P}]=[D:P]
is finite. By Lemma
3.5
,
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(A_{P})
is not Prüfer, and we are done.
∎

Corollary 3.7
.

If
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(A)
is Prüfer, then every simple residue ring of
A
is a finite field.

Proof.

Assume that
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(A)
is Prüfer. Then,
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(A)\subseteq\textnormal{Int}(D)
because
A\cap K=D
, and so
\textnormal{Int}(D)
is Prüfer. By
[
4
, Prop. 6.3]
,
D
must be an almost Dedekind domain with finite residue fields, hence
D
is a Prüfer domain. Since
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(A)
is Prüfer, the condition of Theorem
3.6
cannot hold. Therefore, every simple residue ring of
A
must be a finite field.
∎

When
D
is semiprimitive, combining Theorem
3.4
with Corollary
3.7
allows us to show that
A
is a commutative ring whenever
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(A)
is Prüfer. In that case, the structure of
A
is further restricted by Theorem
1.3
. Towards this end, we now study how
D
being semiprimitive affects
A
.

In what follows, for any ring
R
, let
\textnormal{Max}(R)
be the set of maximal ideals of
R
, called the maximal spectrum of
R
.

Lemma 3.8
.

Assume that
D
is one-dimensional.

(1)

Let
\mathfrak{m}\in\textnormal{Max}(A)
. Then,
\mathfrak{m}\cap D\in\textnormal{Max}(D)
.

(2)

Let
M\in\textnormal{Max}(D)
. Then, there exists a maximal ideal of
A
containing
MA
.

Proof.

(1) Let
M=\mathfrak{m}\cap D
. Working as in the proof of Theorem
3.6
, we see that
M
is a prime ideal of
D
. It remains to show that
M\neq\{0\}
. If this were the case, then
D
would be isomorphic to a subring of
A/\mathfrak{m}
, because

D=D/(\mathfrak{m}\cap D)\cong(D+\mathfrak{m})/\mathfrak{m}\subseteq A/\mathfrak{m}.

However,
A/\mathfrak{m}
is a simple ring and
D
is central in
A
, so this would force
D
to be a field, which is a contradiction. Thus,
M
is a nonzero prime ideal of the one-dimensional domain
D
, and hence
M
is maximal.

(2) Consider the localization
D_{M}
and the
D_{M}
-algebra
A_{M}
. The ring
A_{M}
cannot be simple, since then
dA_{M}=A_{M}
for each
d\in D\setminus\{0\}
. This implies that
K=A_{M}\cap K
, and consequently
D\subsetneqq A\cap K
. So,
A_{M}
contains a nonzero maximal ideal
\mathfrak{M}
. The ideal
\mathfrak{M}\cap D_{M}
is maximal in
D_{M}
, so we have
MD_{M}=\mathfrak{M}\cap D_{M}
. It follows that the contraction of
\mathfrak{M}
to
A
is a proper ideal of
A
that contains
M
.
∎

Proposition 3.9
.

Assume that
B
is a division ring.

(1)

If
\mathfrak{I}
is a two-sided ideal of
A
such that
\mathfrak{I}\cap D=\{0\}
, then
\mathfrak{I}=\{0\}
.

(2)

If
D
is one-dimensional and semiprimitive, then the intersection of all the maximal ideals of
A
is
\{0\}
. Consequently,
A
is semiprimitive.

Proof.

(1) Assume that
\mathfrak{I}
is a two-sided ideal of
A
with
\mathfrak{I}\cap D=\{0\}
. Let
U=D\setminus\{0\}
. Since
B=U^{-1}A
is a division ring, the extended ideal
U^{-1}\mathfrak{I}
of
B
is equal to either
\{0\}
or
B
. If
U^{-1}\mathfrak{I}=B
, then
1=d^{-1}d
for some nonzero
d\in\mathfrak{I}\cap D
, which is a contradiction. Thus,
U^{-1}\mathfrak{I}=\{0\}
, and this implies that
\mathfrak{I}=\{0\}
.

(2) Assume that
D
is semiprimitive and let
\mathfrak{I}=\bigcap_{\mathfrak{m}\in\textnormal{Max}(A)}\mathfrak{m}
. Let
d\in\mathfrak{I}\cap D
. Using Lemma
3.8
and the fact that
D
is commutative and semiprimitive,

d\in\bigcap_{\mathfrak{m}\in\textnormal{Max}(A)}(\mathfrak{m}\cap D)=\bigcap_{M\in\textnormal{Max}(D)}M=\{0\}.

Thus,
\mathfrak{I}\cap D=\{0\}
, and so
\mathfrak{I}=\{0\}
by (1). Finally,
A
is semiprimitive because
\mathscr{J}(A)\subseteq\mathfrak{I}
.
∎

Proposition 3.10
.

Assume that
B=\prod_{i=1}^{t}\mathcal{D}_{i}
, where each
\mathcal{D}_{i}
is a division ring. For each
1\leq i\leq t
, let
e_{i}=1_{\mathcal{D}_{i}}
and let
D_{i}=De_{i}\cong D
. Assume also that
A=\prod_{i=1}^{t}A_{i}
, where each
A_{i}
is a
D_{i}
-subalgebra of
\mathcal{D}_{i}
. If
D
is one-dimensional and semiprimitive, then the intersection of all the maximal ideals of
A
is
\{0\}
. Consequently,
A
is semiprimitive.

Proof.

Let
\mathfrak{I}=\bigcap_{\mathfrak{m}\in\textnormal{Max}(A)}\mathfrak{m}
. Since
A
is a direct product of rings, we have
\mathfrak{I}=\prod_{i=1}^{t}\mathfrak{I}_{i}
, where each
\mathfrak{I}_{i}
is a two-sided ideal of
A_{i}
. We claim that

\mathfrak{I}_{i}=\bigcap_{\mathfrak{m}_{i}\in\textnormal{Max}(A_{i})}\mathfrak{m}_{i}

for each
i
. To see this, note that each maximal ideal
\mathfrak{m}\subseteq A
has the form
\mathfrak{m}=\prod_{i=1}^{t}\mathfrak{a}_{i}
, where
\mathfrak{a}_{j}
is a maximal ideal of
A_{j}
for exactly one
j\in\{1,\ldots,t\}
, and
\mathfrak{a}_{i}=A_{i}
for
i\neq j
. Intersecting all of the maximal ideals of
A
yields the desired decomposition of
\mathfrak{I}
.

Now, if
D
is semiprimitive, then so is each
D_{i}
. Hence,
\mathfrak{I}_{i}=\{0\}
for each
1\leq i\leq t
by Lemma
3.9
(2), and therefore
\mathfrak{I}=\{0\}
. As in Proposition
3.9
,
A
is semiprimitive because
\mathscr{J}(A)\subseteq\mathfrak{I}
.
∎

Theorem 3.11
.

If
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(A)
is Prüfer and
D
is semiprimitive, then
A\cong\prod_{i=1}^{t}A_{i}
, where
A_{i}
is the integral closure of
D
in a finite field extension
F_{i}/K
. In particular, each
A_{i}
is an ADDB-domain and
A
is commutative.

Proof.

Assume
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(A)
is Prüfer. First, we prove that
A
is a commutative ring. Let
\mathfrak{I}
be the intersection of all the maximal ideals of
A
. Since
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(A)
is Prüfer and
D
is semiprimitive, by Theorem
3.4
and Proposition
3.10
,
\mathfrak{I}=\{0\}
. Moreover, by Corollary
3.7
,
A/\mathfrak{m}
is a finite field for each
\mathfrak{m}\in\textnormal{Max}(A)
. So, for all
a,b\in A
and all
\mathfrak{m}\in\textnormal{Max}(A)
, we have
ab-ba\in\mathfrak{m}
. Hence,
ab-ba\in\mathfrak{I}
, and therefore
ab=ba
.

Next, apply Theorem
3.4
and express
B
and
A
as
B\cong\prod_{i=1}^{t}\mathcal{D}_{i}
and
A\cong\prod_{i=1}^{t}A_{i}
, where each
\mathcal{D}_{i}
is a division ring and each
A_{i}
is an integrally closed domain. For each
1\leq i\leq t
, let
e_{i}=1_{\mathcal{D}_{i}}
,
D_{i}=De_{i}\cong D
, and
K_{i}=Ke_{i}\cong K
. Then,
\mathcal{D}_{i}=(D_{i}\setminus\{0\})^{-1}A_{i}
for each
i
. Since
A
is commutative, so is each
A_{i}
. Thus, each
\mathcal{D}_{i}
is a field
F_{i}
, and
[F_{i}:K_{i}]
is finite because
\dim_{K}(B)
is finite.

Since
\textnormal{Int}(D)
is Prüfer, by Theorem
1.3
,
D
—and hence each
D_{i}
—is an ADDB-domain. From Theorem
3.4
, we know that
A=A^{\prime}
and
A_{i}=A_{i}^{\prime}
for each
i
. This means that
A_{i}
is the integral closure of
D_{i}
in
F_{i}
, and is itself an ADDB-domain by
[
12
, Theorem 36.1(a)]
.
∎

If
D
is not semiprimitive, then there exist examples of noncommutative rings
A
such that
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(A)
is Prüfer; see Theorem
5.4
. However, the following partial converse of Theorem
3.11
is true.

Theorem 3.12
.

Let
D
be an ADDB-domain. Let
A\cong\prod_{i=1}^{t}A_{i}
, where
A_{i}
is the integral closure of
D
in a finite field extension
F_{i}/K
. Then
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(A)
is Prüfer.

Proof.

Note that each
A_{i}
is an ADDB-domain
[
12
, Theorem 36.1(a)]
. Let
F
be a finite Galois extension of
K
containing
F_{i}
for
i=1,\ldots,t
and let
D_{F}
be the integral closure of
D
in
F
. Note that
D_{F}
is also an ADDB-domain. So,
\textnormal{Int}(D_{F})
is Prüfer by Theorem
1.3
.

Let
G=\text{Gal}(F/K)
, let
i\in\{1,\ldots,t\}
, and let
G(A_{i})=\bigcup_{\sigma\in G}\sigma(A_{i})
. It is easy to show that
G(A_{i})=\Omega_{A_{i}}
and
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(A_{i})=\textnormal{Int}_{K}(G(A_{i}),\overline{D})=\textnormal{Int}_{K}(G(A_{i}),D_{F})
, and this last ring is integrally closed by Theorem
1.6
. Hence, we have

\textnormal{Int}_{K}(A)=\bigcap_{i=1}^{n}\textnormal{Int}_{K}(A_{i})=\bigcap_{i=1}^{n}\textnormal{Int}_{K}(G(A_{i}),D_{F})=\textnormal{Int}_{K}(\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}G(A_{i}),D_{F}).

Now, by
[
26
, Lemma 2.20]
,
\textnormal{Int}_{F}(\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}G(A_{i}),D_{F})
is the integral closure of
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}G(A_{i}),D_{F})
in
F(X)
. Since
\textnormal{Int}(D_{F})\subseteq\textnormal{Int}_{F}(\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}G(A_{i}),D_{F})
and the former ring is a Prüfer domain, it follows that
\textnormal{Int}_{F}(\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}G(A_{i}),D_{F})
is also Prüfer. By
[
12
, Theorem 22.4]
,
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}G(A_{i}),D_{F})
is Prüfer, as we wanted to prove.
∎

Corollary 3.13
.

Let
D
be a semiprimitive ADDB-domain. Then,
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(A)
is Prüfer if and only if
A\cong\prod_{i=1}^{t}A_{i}
, where
A_{i}
is the integral closure of
D
in a finite field extension
F_{i}/K
.

4
Sufficient conditions

In this section, we complete the proof of Theorem
1.7
by showing that if
D
is an ADDB-domain and
A=A^{\prime}
, then
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(A)
is Prüfer. This is accomplished by constructing a family of Prüfer domains within
K[X]
, one of which must be a subring of
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(A,A^{\prime}):=\{f\in K[X]\mid f(A)\subseteq A^{\prime}\}
(which is equal to
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(A)
if
A=A^{\prime}
). To construct these Prüfer domains, we generalize
[
19
, Section 2]
, which dealt with a similar problem for
D=\mathbb{Z}
.

Recall from the introduction that
\overline{K}
denotes a fixed algebraic closure of
K
, and from Definition
1.5
that for each
n\geq 1
,
\Lambda_{n}
is the set of elements of
\overline{K}
that are integral over
D
of degree at most
n
. For
\mathscr{E}\subseteq\Lambda_{n}
, define
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(\mathscr{E},\Lambda_{n})=\{f\in K[X]\mid f(\mathscr{E})\subseteq\Lambda_{n}\}
. As usual, we let
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(\Lambda_{n}):=\textnormal{Int}_{K}(\Lambda_{n},\Lambda_{n})
.

It is shown in
[
27
, Prop. 7]
that for any
\mathscr{E}\subseteq\Lambda_{n}
,
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(\mathscr{E},\Lambda_{n})
is an integrally closed subring of
K[X]
. We wish to prove that
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(\Lambda_{n})
is a Prüfer domain for each
n\geq 1
. To do this, we provide a broad construction that produces a Prüfer domain within
K[X]
.

4.1
Building a Prüfer domain inside
K[X]

Construction 4.1
.

Let
D
be an almost Dedekind domain with finite residue fields. As mentioned in Notation
1.1
,
D
contains a principal ideal domain
D_{0}
and we use
\mathfrak{p}
to denote a nonzero prime element of
D_{0}
.

For each nonzero prime
P
of
D
, let
I_{P}
be an index set and let
\{V_{P,i}\mid i\in I_{P}\}
be a collection of valuation overrings of
D[X]
such that for each
i\in I_{P}
, the valuation
v_{P,i}
of
K(X)
extends
v_{P}
, and
V_{P,i}
has maximal ideal
M_{P,i}
. Let
\mathfrak{p}
be the prime of
D_{0}
below
P
. Assume that the following two conditions hold:

(i)

there exists
e_{\mathfrak{p}}\geq 1
such that for all
P|\mathfrak{p}D_{0}
and all
i\in I_{P}
,
M_{P,i}^{e_{\mathfrak{p}}}\subseteq\mathfrak{p}V_{P,i}
, and

(ii)

there exists
f_{\mathfrak{p}}\geq 1
such that for all
P|\mathfrak{p}D_{0}
and all
i\in I_{P}
,
[(V_{P,i}/M_{P,i}):(D_{0}/\mathfrak{p}D_{0})]\leq f_{\mathfrak{p}}
.

Let
\text{Spec}(D)
and
\textnormal{Max}(D)
be, respectively, the sets of prime and maximal ideals of
D
. We then form the following subring of
K[X]
:

\mathscr{D}:=\bigcap_{P\in\textnormal{Max}(D)}\Big(\;\bigcap_{i\in I_{P}}V_{P,i}\Big)\cap K[X].

Remark 4.2
.

Since
D
is assumed to be an almost Dedekind domain, each valuation
v_{P}
is discrete. The existence of
e_{\mathfrak{p}}
implies that for each
P|\mathfrak{p}D_{0}
and each
i\in I_{P}
, there exists a positive integer
\lambda_{P,i}\leq e_{\mathfrak{p}}
such that
M_{P,i}^{\lambda_{P,i}}=\mathfrak{p}V_{P,i}
. Indeed, the containments
M_{P,i}^{e_{\mathfrak{p}}}\subseteq\mathfrak{p}V_{P,i}\subseteq M_{P,i}
imply that
\mathfrak{p}V_{P,i}
is
M_{P,i}
-primary, so by
[
12
, Theorem 17.3(b)]

\mathfrak{p}V_{P,i}
is equal to a power
M_{P,i}^{\lambda_{P,i}}
of the maximal ideal
M_{P,i}
and necessarily
\lambda_{P,i}\leq e_{\mathfrak{p}}
.

For the remainder of this section, assume that
\mathscr{D}
is a domain formed via Construction
4.1
. We will prove that
\mathscr{D}_{\mathfrak{M}}
is a valuation domain for each maximal ideal
\mathfrak{M}
of
\mathscr{D}
, and hence that
\mathscr{D}
is a Prüfer domain. The ideals of
\mathscr{D}
fall into two types. An ideal
\mathfrak{I}\subseteq\mathscr{D}
is
unitary
if
\mathfrak{I}\cap D\neq\{0\}
, and is
non-unitary
otherwise. The non-unitary maximal ideals of
\mathscr{D}
can be dealt with quite quickly.

Lemma 4.3
.

If
\mathfrak{M}
is a non-unitary maximal ideal of
\mathscr{D}
, then
\mathscr{D}_{\mathfrak{M}}
is a valuation domain.

Proof.

When
\mathfrak{M}
is non-unitary and maximal, we have
K[X]\subseteq\mathscr{D}_{\mathfrak{M}}
. Thus,
\mathscr{D}_{\mathfrak{M}}
is a local overring of the Prüfer domain
K[X]
, and hence is a valuation domain.
∎

Working with the unitary maximal ideals of
\mathscr{D}
is more complicated. When
\mathfrak{M}
is unitary, its contraction to
D
is a nonzero prime
P\subseteq D
, and
P
itself lies above a prime element
\mathfrak{p}
of
D_{0}
.

Notation 4.4
.

Recall the definitions of the positive integers
e_{\mathfrak{p}}
and
f_{\mathfrak{p}}
from Construction
4.1
. For each
\mathfrak{p}
, let
s_{\mathfrak{p}}=e_{\mathfrak{p}}!
and
r_{\mathfrak{p}}=|D_{0}/\mathfrak{p}D_{0}|^{f_{\mathfrak{p}}!}
. Note that
r_{\mathfrak{p}}\geq 2
.

Proposition 4.5
.

(1)

For each
\mathfrak{p}
and each
f\in\mathscr{D}
,
\dfrac{(f^{r_{\mathfrak{p}}}-f)^{s_{\mathfrak{p}}}}{\mathfrak{p}}\in\mathscr{D}
.

(2)

Let
\mathfrak{M}
be a unitary prime ideal of
\mathscr{D}
above
\mathfrak{p}
. Then,
f^{r_{\mathfrak{p}}}-f\in\mathfrak{M}
for all
f\in\mathscr{D}
.

Proof.

For readability, let
r=r_{\mathfrak{p}}
and
s=s_{\mathfrak{p}}
.

(1) First, we show that
(f^{r}-f)^{s}\in\mathfrak{p}V_{P,i}
for each
P|\mathfrak{p}D_{0}
and each
i\in I_{P}
. By construction,
[(V_{P,i}/M_{P,i}):(D_{0}/\mathfrak{p}D_{0})]
divides
f_{\mathfrak{p}}!
and
e_{\mathfrak{p}}\leq s
. So,
f^{r}-f\in M_{P,i}
, and then
(f^{r}-f)^{s}\in M_{P,i}^{s}\subseteq\mathfrak{p}V_{P,i}
. Thus,
(f^{r}-f)^{s}/\mathfrak{p}\in V_{P,i}
.

Next, if
P^{\prime}
is a nonzero prime of
D
that is not above
\mathfrak{p}
, then
\mathfrak{p}
is invertible in each valuation overring
V^{\prime}
of
D
associated to
P^{\prime}
. In this case,
(f^{r}-f)^{s}/\mathfrak{p}\in V^{\prime}
. We conclude that
(f^{r}-f)^{s}/\mathfrak{p}\in\mathscr{D}
.

(2) By part (1),
(f^{r}-f)^{s}\in\mathfrak{p}\mathscr{D}\subseteq\mathfrak{M}
for each
f\in\mathscr{D}
. Consequently,
f^{r}-f\in\mathfrak{M}
.
∎

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
f\in\mathscr{D}
. For every
\mathfrak{p}
, every
P|\mathfrak{p}D_{0}
, and each
i\in I_{P}
, one of the following two conditions holds. Either

(i)

v_{P,i}\Big(\dfrac{f^{s_{\mathfrak{p}}}}{\mathfrak{p}^{t}}\Big)=0
for some
t\geq 0
, or

(ii)

v_{P,i}\Big(\dfrac{f^{s_{\mathfrak{p}}}}{\mathfrak{p}^{t}}\Big)>0
for all
t\geq 0
.

Proof.

Fix
\mathfrak{p}
,
P
, and
i
. Let
V=V_{P,i}
,
M=M_{P,i}
,
v=v_{P,i}
, and
s=s_{\mathfrak{p}}
. Since
f\in\mathscr{D}
,
v(f)\geq 0
. If
v(f)=0
, then condition (i) holds with
t=0
. So, assume that
v(f)>0
. This means that
f\in M
. If condition (ii) holds, then we are done. So, assume not. Then, there exists some
n\geq 1
such that
\mathfrak{p}^{n}V\subseteq f^{s}V
. By the same argument of Remark
4.2
,
fV=M^{a}
, for some
a\geq 1
, so
f^{s}V=M^{sa}
. Recall from Remark
4.2
that
\mathfrak{p}V=M^{\lambda}
for some integer
\lambda\geq 1
. Note that
\lambda
divides
s
because
\lambda\leq e_{\mathfrak{p}}
. Hence, there exists some
t\geq 1
such that
sa=\lambda t
, so that
f^{s}V=M^{\lambda t}=\mathfrak{p}^{t}V
. Hence,
v(f^{s})=v(\mathfrak{p}^{t})
, which is (i).
∎

Lemma 4.7
.

Let
f\in\mathscr{D}
. Assume that for some
\mathfrak{p}
, every
P|\mathfrak{p}D_{0}
, and every
i\in I_{P}
,
f
satisfies one of the following two conditions. Either

(i)

v_{P,i}\Big(\dfrac{f}{\mathfrak{p}^{t}}\Big)=0
for some
t\geq 0
, or

(ii)

v_{P,i}\Big(\dfrac{f}{\mathfrak{p}^{t}}\Big)>0
for all
t\geq 0
.

Let
g=\dfrac{f(f^{r_{\mathfrak{p}}-1}-1)^{s_{\mathfrak{p}}}}{\mathfrak{p}}\in K[X]
. Then,
g
also satisfies (i) or (ii), and
g\in\mathscr{D}
.

Proof.

As before, let
r=r_{\mathfrak{p}}
and
s=s_{\mathfrak{p}}
. First we show that
g
satisfies either (i) or (ii) for each
P|\mathfrak{p}D_{0}
and each
i\in I_{P}
. Fix
P
and
i\in I_{P}
, let
V=V_{P,i}
,
M=M_{P,i}
, and
v=v_{P,i}
. By Proposition
4.5
(2),
f(f^{r-1}-1)=f^{r}-f\in\mathfrak{M}
for each unitary maximal ideal of
\mathscr{D}
above
\mathfrak{p}
. So, either
v(f)>0
or
v(f^{r-1}-1)>0
. Note that these two conditions cannot occur simultaneously.

Assume first that
v(f)>0
. Then,
v(g)=v(\tfrac{f}{\mathfrak{p}})
. If (i) holds for
f
, then
v(f)=tv(\mathfrak{p})
for some integer
t\geq 1
. So,
v(g)=(t-1)v(\mathfrak{p})
and (i) holds for
g
. On the other hand, if
f
satisfies (ii), then so does
g
, because for all
t\geq 0
we have
v(\tfrac{g}{\mathfrak{p}^{t}})=v(\tfrac{f}{\mathfrak{p}^{t+1}})>0
.

Next, assume that
v(f^{r-1}-1)>0
. Then,
f^{r-1}-1\in M
,
(f^{r-1}-1)^{s}\in\mathfrak{p}V
and
v(f)=0
(so, in this case, condition (i) is true for
f
with
t=0
). By Lemma
4.6
, either condition (i) or condition (ii) holds for
(f^{r-1}-1)^{s}
. Moreover, if (i) is true for
(f^{r-1}-1)^{s}
, then the fact that
(f^{r-1}-1)^{s}\in\mathfrak{p}V
means
v((f^{r-1}-1)^{s}/\mathfrak{p}^{t})=0
for some
t\geq 1
. We have
v(g)=v((f^{r-1}-1)^{s}/\mathfrak{p})
, so either (i) or (ii) holds for
g
.

Finally, the fact that
g
satisfies either (i) or (ii) implies that
g\in V
, and this is true for every
P|\mathfrak{p}D_{0}
and every
i\in I_{P}
. This is enough to conclude that
g\in\mathscr{D}
, because if
P^{\prime}
is a nonzero prime of
D
that is not over
\mathfrak{p}
, then
\mathfrak{p}
is a unit in each valuation overring associated to
P^{\prime}
.
∎

Lemma 4.8
.

Let
\mathfrak{M}
be a unitary prime ideal of
\mathscr{D}
above
\mathfrak{p}
and let
f\in\mathscr{D}\setminus\{0\}
. Then, either
f^{s_{\mathfrak{p}}}/\mathfrak{p}^{t}\in\mathfrak{M}\mathscr{D}_{\mathfrak{M}}
for all integers
t\geq 1
, or
f^{s_{\mathfrak{p}}}/\mathfrak{p}^{t}
is a unit of
\mathscr{D}_{\mathfrak{M}}
for some integer
t\geq 0
.

Proof.

Once again, let
r=r_{\mathfrak{p}}
and
s=s_{\mathfrak{p}}
. There is nothing to prove if
f\notin\mathfrak{M}
, so assume that
f\in\mathfrak{M}
. Let
f_{0}=f^{s}
, and for each
k\geq 1
, let

f_{k}=\dfrac{f_{k-1}(f_{k-1}^{r-1}-1)^{s}}{\mathfrak{p}}.

By Lemma
4.6
,
f_{0}
meets the conditions of Lemma
4.7
. Thus,
f_{1}\in\mathscr{D}
, and by induction
f_{k}\in\mathscr{D}
for all
k\geq 0
.

For each
k\geq 1
, let
h_{k}=\prod_{i=0}^{k-1}(f_{i}^{r-1}-1)^{s}
. Then,
f_{k}=(f^{s}h_{k})/\mathfrak{p}^{k}
for all
k\geq 1
. Notice that if
f_{i}\in\mathfrak{M}
for all
0\leq i\leq k-1
, then
h_{k}\notin\mathfrak{M}
. Hence, if there exists a smallest positive integer
t
such that
f_{t}\notin\mathfrak{M}
, then
f^{s}/\mathfrak{p}^{t}=f_{t}/h_{t}
is a unit in
\mathscr{D}_{\mathfrak{M}}
. Otherwise,
f_{t}\in\mathfrak{M}
for every
t\geq 1
, each
h_{t}\notin\mathfrak{M}
, and
f^{s}/\mathfrak{p}^{t}=f_{t}/h_{t}\in\mathfrak{M}\mathscr{D}_{\mathfrak{M}}
for all
t\geq 1
.
∎

Theorem 4.9
.

Let
\mathscr{D}
be a domain formed via Construction
4.1
. Then,
\mathscr{D}
is Prüfer.

Proof.

Let
\mathfrak{M}
be a maximal ideal of
\mathscr{D}
. If
\mathfrak{M}
is non-unitary, then
\mathscr{D}_{\mathfrak{M}}
is a valuation domain by Lemma
4.3
. So, assume that
\mathfrak{M}
is a unitary maximal ideal above
\mathfrak{p}
. Let
f,g\in\mathscr{D}
be nonzero and coprime in
K[X]
. We will show that either
f/g
or
g/f
is in
\mathscr{D}_{\mathfrak{M}}
. This is clear if either
f\notin\mathfrak{M}
or
g\notin\mathfrak{M}
. So, assume that both
f
and
g
are in
\mathfrak{M}
.

Let
s=s_{\mathfrak{p}}
. Since
f
and
g
are coprime in
K[X]
, there exist
h_{1},h_{2}\in K[X]
such that
1=h_{1}f^{s}+h_{2}g^{s}
. Choose
d\in D\setminus\{0\}
such that
dh_{1},dh_{2}\in D[X]\subseteq\mathscr{D}
. If both
f^{s}/\mathfrak{p}^{t}
and
g^{s}/\mathfrak{p}^{t}
are in
\mathfrak{M}\mathscr{D}_{\mathfrak{M}}
for every positive integer
t
, then

\dfrac{d}{\mathfrak{p}^{t}}=\dfrac{dh_{1}f^{s}+dh_{2}g^{s}}{\mathfrak{p}^{t}}\in\mathfrak{M}\mathscr{D}_{\mathfrak{M}}

for all
t\geq 1
. This implies that
d/\mathfrak{p}^{t}\in P=\mathfrak{M}\cap D
for all
t\geq 1
, which is impossible. By Lemma
4.8
, we can find
t\geq 1
such that both
f^{s}/\mathfrak{p}^{t}
and
g^{s}/\mathfrak{p}^{t}
are in
\mathscr{D}_{\mathfrak{M}}
, and either
f^{s}/\mathfrak{p}^{t}
or
g^{s}/\mathfrak{p}^{t}
is a unit of
\mathscr{D}_{\mathfrak{M}}
. Without loss of generality, assume that
f^{s}/\mathfrak{p}^{t}
is a unit of
\mathscr{D}_{\mathfrak{M}}
. Then,

(g/f)^{s}=g^{s}/f^{s}=(g^{s}/\mathfrak{p}^{t})/(f^{s}/\mathfrak{p}^{t})\in\mathscr{D}_{\mathfrak{M}}.

(4.10)

Since
\mathscr{D}
is an intersection of valuation domains, it is integrally closed. So,
\mathscr{D}_{\mathfrak{M}}
is also integrally closed. Thus,
g/f\in\mathscr{D}_{\mathfrak{M}}
,
\mathscr{D}_{\mathfrak{M}}
is a valuation domain, and
\mathscr{D}
is Prüfer.
∎

4.2
Proving that
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(\Lambda_{n})
is Prufer

Next, we show how to express
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(\Lambda_{n})
as the intersection of a collection of valuation domains like those of Construction
4.1
. It then follows from Theorem
4.9
that
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(\Lambda_{n})
is Prüfer, and this will be enough to complete the proof of Theorem
1.7
(1).

For each
P\in\textnormal{Max}(D)
, we let
\widehat{D_{P}}
and
\widehat{K}_{P}
be the
P
-adic completions of
D
and
K
, respectively. Let
\overline{\widehat{K}_{P}}
be a fixed algebraic closure of
\widehat{K}_{P}
. Since
D_{P}
is a rank one valuation domain, it follows that
v_{P}
extends uniquely to
\overline{\widehat{K}_{P}}
and the valuation domain of this unique extension is equal to
\overline{\widehat{D_{P}}}
, the absolute integral closure of
\widehat{D_{P}}
. We still denote this unique extension by
v_{P}
. For
n\geq 1
, we set
\widehat{\Lambda}_{P,n}:=\{\alpha\in\overline{\widehat{D_{P}}}\mid[\widehat{K}_{P}(\alpha):\widehat{K}_{P}]\leq n\}
. Finally, for
\alpha\in\overline{\widehat{D_{P}}}
, we define
V_{P,\alpha}:=\{\phi\in K(X)\mid\phi(\alpha)\in\overline{\widehat{D_{P}}}\}
, which is a valuation domain of
K(X)
lying above
D_{P}
by
[

, Proposition 2.2]
.

Theorem 4.11
.

Let
n\geq 1
. Then,

\textnormal{Int}_{K}(\Lambda_{n})=\bigcap_{P\in\textnormal{Max}(D)}\bigcap_{\alpha\in\widehat{\Lambda}_{P,n}}V_{P,\alpha}\cap K[X].

Proof.

Let
f\in\textnormal{Int}_{K}(\Lambda_{n})
and write
f(X)=\frac{g(X)}{d}
for some
g\in D[X]
and
d\in D\setminus\{0\}.
For each
P\in\textnormal{Max}(D)
such that
d\notin P
, we have
f\in D_{P}[X]
, so
f\in V_{P,\alpha}
for each
\alpha\in\widehat{\Lambda}_{P,n}
. Next, let
P\in\textnormal{Max}(D)
be such that
d\in P
and fix
\alpha\in\widehat{\Lambda}_{P,n}
. Let

. Let

be the minimal polynomial of
\alpha
.

Extend the valuation
v_{P}
to
\widehat{K}_{P}[X]
by taking
v_{P}(h)
to be the minimum of the values of the coefficients of
h\in\widehat{K}_{P}[X]
. Since
D
is dense in
\widehat{D_{P}}
, for any
\delta\in\mathbb{Z}
we can find a monic
q\in D[X]
such that
\deg(q)=\deg(p_{\alpha})
and
v_{P}(p_{\alpha}-q)>\delta
. Let
\varepsilon\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
\varepsilon>\max\{k,v_{P}(\alpha_{i}-\alpha_{j})\mid\alpha_{i}\not=\alpha_{j}\in\Omega_{\alpha}\}
. Then, by
[
31
, Chapter 1, (P)]
, there exists
q\in D[X]
monic of degree
\deg(p_{\alpha})
and a root
\gamma
of
q
in
\overline{\widehat{D_{P}}}
such that

.

Next, for each root
\lambda\in\Omega_{q}
of
q
in
\overline{K}
, let
D_{\lambda}^{\prime}
be the integral closure of
D
in
K(\lambda)
. By
[
31
, Chapter 4, (L)]
, for each
\lambda
and for each prime
Q
of
D_{\lambda}^{\prime}
above
P
, there exists a
K
-embedding
\sigma_{\lambda,Q}:K(\lambda)\to\overline{\widehat{K}_{P}}
such that the extension
v_{Q}
of
v_{P}
to
K(\lambda)
is given by
v_{Q}=v_{P}\circ\sigma_{\lambda,Q}
. Choose
\beta\in\Omega_{q}
with
K
-embedding
\sigma:K(\beta)\to\overline{\widehat{K}_{P}}
such that
\sigma(\beta)
is equal to the root
\gamma
of
q
in
\overline{\widehat{D_{P}}}
chosen as above. Then, by the previous paragraph,
v_{P}(\alpha-\sigma(\beta))>k
. Since
g
has coefficients in
D
,
\alpha-\sigma(\beta)
divides
g(\alpha)-g(\sigma(\beta))
in
\overline{\widehat{D_{P}}}
. Thus,
v_{P}(g(\alpha)-g(\sigma(\beta)))>k
. Furthermore,
v_{P}(g(\sigma(\beta)))=v_{P}(\sigma(g(\beta)))=v_{Q}(g(\beta))
, and

because

and

. It follows that

and

.

Conversely, let
f\in K[X]
be in each of the valuation domains
V_{P,\alpha}
, for

and
\alpha\in\widehat{\Lambda}_{P,n}
. Let
\beta\in\Lambda_{n}
and suppose that
f(X)=\frac{g(X)}{d}
for some
g\in D[X]
and
d\in D\setminus\{0\}.
We set
F=K(\beta)
and let
D^{\prime}
be the integral closure of
D
in

. Let
P\in\textnormal{Max}(D)
. If

, then

. In this case,
f(\beta)\in(D^{\prime})_{Q}
for each prime ideal

of
D^{\prime}
lying above
P
, so
f(\beta)\in(D^{\prime})_{P}

D_{P}
in

in

lying above
P
. By
[
31
, Chapter 4, (L)]
, there is an embedding
\sigma_{Q}:F\to\overline{\widehat{K}_{P}}
such that the extension

to

\alpha=\sigma_{Q}(\beta)

, we have

. So,
v_{Q}(f(\beta))\geq 0
. This holds for each

above

, so
f(\beta)\in(D^{\prime})_{P}
. We have shown that
f(\beta)
is in
(D^{\prime})_{P}
for each

∎

Corollary 4.12
.

Let
D
be an ADDB-domain.

(1)

For each
n\geq 1
,
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(\Lambda_{n})
is Prüfer.

(2)

Let
A
be a
D
-algebra that is torsion-free and finitely generated as a
D
-module, and such that
A\cap K=D
. Then,
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(A,A^{\prime})
is Prüfer.

Proof.

(1) Let
n\geq 1
and express
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(\Lambda_{n})
as in Theorem
4.11
. Let
\mathfrak{p}
be a nonzero prime element of
D_{0}
. Since
D
is an ADDB-domain, we know that the integers
E_{\mathfrak{p}}
and
F_{\mathfrak{p}}
from Notation
1.1
are bounded above, say by some integer
d_{\mathfrak{p}}
. Let
P
be a prime of
D
above
\mathfrak{p}
. By the results of
[
24
, Proposition 2.2]
, we have
M_{P,\alpha}^{n}=PV_{P,\alpha}
and
[V_{P,\alpha}/M_{P,\alpha}:D/P]\leq n
for each
\alpha\in\widehat{\Lambda}_{P,n}
. Therefore,
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(\Lambda_{n})
is a domain formed by Construction
4.1
, because we can take
e_{\mathfrak{p}}=f_{\mathfrak{p}}=nd_{\mathfrak{p}}
. Hence, by Theorem
4.9
,
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(\Lambda_{n})
is a Prüfer domain.

(2) Let

. Then,
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(A,A^{\prime})=\textnormal{Int}_{K}(\Omega_{A},\Lambda_{n})
by Theorem
1.6
. Since
\Omega_{A}\subseteq\Lambda_{n}
, we have
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(\Lambda_{n})\subseteq\textnormal{Int}_{K}(\Omega_{A},\Lambda_{n})
. By part (1),
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(\Lambda_{n})

\textnormal{Int}_{K}(A,A^{\prime})
is also Prüfer.
∎

4.3
The proof of Theorem
1.7

To finish the proof of Theorem
1.7
, it remains to consider the conditions that involve localizations at primes of
D
. For a prime
P\subseteq D
, let
(A^{\prime})_{P}=(D\setminus P)^{-1}A^{\prime}
and
(A_{P})^{\prime}=\{b\in B\mid b\text{ is integral over }D_{P}\}
. It is straightforward to check that
(A^{\prime})_{P}=(A_{P})^{\prime}
, so we may take
A_{P}^{\prime}:=(A^{\prime})_{P}=(A_{P})^{\prime}
and use the notation
A_{P}^{\prime}
without ambiguity.

Lemma 4.13
.

Let
P\in\text{Spec}(D)
. Then,
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(A)_{P}\subseteq\textnormal{Int}_{K}(A_{P})
and
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(A,A^{\prime})_{P}\subseteq\textnormal{Int}_{K}(A_{P},A_{P}^{\prime})
.

Proof.

We prove that
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(A,A^{\prime})_{P}\subseteq\textnormal{Int}_{K}(A_{P},A_{P}^{\prime})
by employing an induction technique due to Rush
[
32
, Proposition 1.4]
. The proof that
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(A)_{P}\subseteq\textnormal{Int}_{K}(A_{P})
is similar. Let
f\in\textnormal{Int}_{K}(A,A^{\prime})_{P}
have degree
m
and let
a/d\in A_{P}
, where
a\in A
and
d\in D\setminus P
. We use induction on
m
. If
m=0
, then
f\in D_{P}\subseteq A_{P}^{\prime}
and we are done. Assume that
m\geq 1
and that any polynomial in
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(A,A^{\prime})_{P}
of degree at most
m-1
is an element of
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(A_{P},A_{P}^{\prime})
. Let
g(x)=d^{m}f(x)-f(dx)
. Then, both
f(dx)
and
g
are in
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(A,A^{\prime})_{P}
, and
\deg g\leq m-1
. By induction,
g\in\textnormal{Int}_{K}(A_{P},A_{P}^{\prime})
.

Now, let
R=A_{P}^{\prime}\cap K[a]
. It may the case that
A_{P}^{\prime}
is not a ring; however,
R
is equal to the integral closure of
D_{P}
in
K[a]
, and hence is a ring. Both
g(a/d)
and
f(a)
are in
R
, and
d
is invertible in
R
, so
f(a/d)=d^{-m}(g(a/d)+f(a))\in R\subseteq A_{P}^{\prime}
. This is true for all
a/d\in A_{P}
, so
f\in\textnormal{Int}_{K}(A_{P},A_{P}^{\prime})
.
∎

Lemma 4.14
.

Let
D
be an ADDB-domain. The following are equivalent.

(i)

\textnormal{Int}_{K}(A)
is Prüfer.

(ii)

For each

,
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(A)_{P}
is Prüfer.

(iii)

For each
P\in\text{Spec}(D)
,
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(A_{P})
is Prüfer.

(iv)

For each
P\in\text{Spec}(D)
,
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(A_{P})
is integrally closed.

(v)

\textnormal{Int}_{K}(A)=\textnormal{Int}_{K}(A,A^{\prime})
.

Proof.

(i)
\Rightarrow
(ii)
\Rightarrow
(iii) Certainly,
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(A)\subseteq\textnormal{Int}_{K}(A)_{P}
for all
P
, and
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(A)_{P}\subseteq\textnormal{Int}_{K}(A_{P})
by Lemma
4.13
.

(iii)
\Rightarrow
(iv) This is clear, because Prüfer domains are integrally closed.

(iv)
\Rightarrow
(v) Assume that
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(A_{P})
is integrally closed for every
P\in\text{Spec}(D)
. On the one hand, we always have
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(A)\subseteq\textnormal{Int}_{K}(A,A^{\prime})
. On the other hand,

\textnormal{Int}_{K}(A,A^{\prime})\subseteq\bigcap_{P\in\text{Spec}(D)}\textnormal{Int}_{K}(A,A^{\prime})_{P}\subseteq\bigcap_{P\in\text{Spec}(D)}\textnormal{Int}_{K}(A_{P},A_{P}^{\prime}),

where the second containment is true by Lemma

. By assumption, each
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(A_{P})
is integrally closed. Since

is an ADDB-domain,

is a DVR with finite residue field. So,
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(A_{P})=\textnormal{Int}_{K}(A_{P},A_{P}^{\prime})
by Theorem

. This gives

\textnormal{Int}_{K}(A,A^{\prime})\subseteq\bigcap_{P\in\text{Spec}(D)}\textnormal{Int}_{K}(A_{P},A_{P}^{\prime})=\bigcap_{P\in\text{Spec}(D)}\textnormal{Int}_{K}(A_{P})\subseteq\textnormal{Int}_{K}(A).

Thus,
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(A)=\textnormal{Int}_{K}(A,A^{\prime})
.

(v)
\Rightarrow
(i) Apply Corollary
4.12
(2).
∎

We now have all the pieces necessary to complete the proof of Theorem

.

We now consider Conjecture

Assume that

5
A noncommutative example

Here, we give an example of a noncommutative
D
-algebra
A
such that
\textnormal{Int}_{K}(A)
is Prüfer. By Theorem
3.11
,
D
cannot be semiprimitive. Our example uses quaternion algebras over the local domain
\mathbb{Z}_{(2)}
.

Notation 5.1
.

Let
D
be an overring of
\mathbb{Z}
in
\mathbb{Q}
. The ring of Hurwitz quaternions over
D
is

D_{\mathbf{H}}=\{a_{0}+a_{1}\mathbf{i}+a_{2}\mathbf{j}+a_{3}\mathbf{k}\mid\text{either }a_{i}\in D\text{ for each $i$, or }a_{i}\in D+\tfrac{1}{2}\text{ for each $i$}\}.

Here,
\mathbf{i}
,
\mathbf{j}
, and
\mathbf{k}
satisfy
\mathbf{i}^{2}=\mathbf{j}^{2}=-1
and
\mathbf{i}\mathbf{j}=\mathbf{k}=-\mathbf{j}\mathbf{i}
. The ring
D_{\mathbf{H}}
also contains the Hurwitz unit
\mathbf{h}=(1+\mathbf{i}+\mathbf{j}+\mathbf{k})/2
, which is a root of
X^{2}-X+1
. The extended algebra
B=D_{\mathbf{H}}\otimes_{D}\mathbb{Q}
of
D_{\mathbf{H}}
is the division ring of rational quaternions

B=\{a_{0}+a_{1}\mathbf{i}+a_{2}\mathbf{j}+a_{3}\mathbf{k}\mid a_{i}\in\mathbb{Q}\text{ for all $i$}\}.

It is well known that
\alpha=a_{0}+a_{1}\mathbf{i}+a_{2}\mathbf{j}+a_{3}\mathbf{k}\in B
solves the polynomial
X^{2}-2a_{0}X+N(\alpha)\in\mathbb{Q}[X]
, where
N(\alpha):=a_{0}^{2}+a_{1}^{2}+a_{2}^{2}+a_{3}^{2}
is the norm of
\alpha
.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
a,b,c,d,n\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
n\geq 2
. Assume that
a^{2}+b^{2}+c^{2}+d^{2}\equiv 0\mod{4^{n}}
. Then,
a,b,c
, and
d
are all even.

Proof.

By assumption,
a^{2}+b^{2}+c^{2}+d^{2}\equiv 0\mod{4}
, which implies that
a,b,c
, and
d
are either all odd or all even. If all four integers are odd, then
a^{2}+b^{2}+c^{2}+d^{2}\equiv 4\mod{8}
, which is a contradiction. So,
a,b,c
, and
d
are all even.
∎

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
D
be an overring of
\mathbb{Z}
in
\mathbb{Q}
.

(1)

If
\alpha\in D_{\mathbf{H}}
, then
N(\alpha)\in D
. Thus,
\alpha
is integral over
D
.

(2)

Let
D=\mathbb{Z}_{(2)}
and let
a_{0},a_{1},a_{2},a_{3},e\in\mathbb{Z}
be odd. Then,
(a_{0}+a_{1}\mathbf{i}+a_{2}\mathbf{j}+a_{3}\mathbf{k})/(2e)\in D_{\mathbf{H}}
.

Proof.

(1) Let
\alpha\in D_{\mathbf{H}}
. If
\alpha=a_{0}+a_{1}\mathbf{i}+a_{2}\mathbf{j}+a_{3}\mathbf{k}
with each
a_{i}\in D
, then the result is clear. If not, then each
a_{i}\in D+\tfrac{1}{2}
, which means that
a_{i}=(1+2b_{i})/2
for some
b_{i}\in D
. Then, for each
i
,
(1+2b_{i})^{2}\in 1+4D
. So,

(1+2b_{0})^{2}+(1+2b_{1})^{2}+(1+2b_{2})^{2}+(1+2b_{3})^{2}\in 4D,

and thus

N(\alpha)=\tfrac{1}{4}\big((1+2b_{0})^{2}+(1+2b_{1})^{2}+(1+2b_{2})^{2}+(1+2b_{3})^{2}\big)\in D,

as required.

(2) For each
0\leq i\leq 3
, let
b_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}
be such that
a_{i}=2b_{i}+1
. Then,
e
is a unit of
\mathbb{Z}_{(2)}
, so

\dfrac{a_{0}+a_{1}\mathbf{i}+a_{2}\mathbf{j}+a_{3}\mathbf{k}}{2e}=e^{-1}(\mathbf{h}+b_{0}+b_{1}\mathbf{i}+b_{2}\mathbf{j}+b_{3}\mathbf{k}),

which is in
D_{\mathbf{H}}
.
∎

Theorem 5.4
.

Let
D=\mathbb{Z}_{(2)}
and
A=D_{\mathbf{H}}
. Then,
\textnormal{Int}_{\mathbb{Q}}(A)
is a Prüfer domain.

Proof.

We show that
A=A^{\prime}
. Let
\alpha\in B
be integral over
D
. We may write

\alpha=\dfrac{a_{0}+a_{1}\mathbf{i}+a_{2}\mathbf{j}+a_{3}\mathbf{k}}{2^{n}e},

where
a_{0},a_{1},a_{2},a_{3},n,e\in\mathbb{Z}
,
n\geq 0
, and
e
is odd. If
n=0
, then
\alpha\in A
. So, assume that
n\geq 1
. Then, we may further assume that at least one
a_{i}
is odd. Since
\alpha
is integral over
D
,
N(\alpha)\in D
. Thus, there exist
c,d\in\mathbb{Z}
with
d
odd such that

N(\alpha)=\dfrac{a_{0}^{2}+a_{1}^{2}+a_{2}^{2}+a_{3}^{2}}{2^{2n}e^{2}}=\dfrac{c}{d},

which is equivalent to

(a_{0}^{2}+a_{1}^{2}+a_{2}^{2}+a_{3}^{2})d=4^{n}e^{2}c.

Since
d
is odd, this means that
a_{0}^{2}+a_{1}^{2}+a_{2}^{2}+a_{3}^{2}\equiv 0\mod{4^{n}}
. If
n\geq 2
, then each
a_{i}
is even by Lemma
5.2
, which is a contradiction. So,
n=1
. Since at least one
a_{i}
is odd and
a_{0}^{2}+a_{1}^{2}+a_{2}^{2}+a_{3}^{2}\equiv 0\mod{4}
, each
a_{i}
must be odd. Thus,
\alpha=(a_{0}+a_{1}\mathbf{i}+a_{2}\mathbf{j}+a_{3}\mathbf{k})/(2e)\in A
, because
e
and all the
a_{i}
are odd. Therefore,
A=A^{\prime}
, and
\textnormal{Int}_{\mathbb{Q}}(A)
is Prüfer by Theorem
1.7
.

∎
