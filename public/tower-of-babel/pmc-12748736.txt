Nickel-catalyzed highly diastereo- and enantioselective hydroaminocarbonylation and hydrocarboxylation of cyclopropenes
Authors: Jianing Wu, Bangkui Yu, Bao Gao, Hanmin Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The asymmetric hydrocarbonylation of olefins represents a promising and effective strategy for the synthesis of chiral carboxylic acids and their derivatives. However, achieving the synthesis of chiral cyclopropyl primary amides and cyclopropyl carboxylic acids via asymmetric hydroaminocarbonylation and hydrocarboxylation of cyclopropenes remains a significant challenge. Here, we present an efficient catalytic system that enables the utilization of cost-effective NH4OAc as a practical alternative to gaseous ammonia and simple organic amines in nickel-catalyzed asymmetric hydroaminocarbonylation of cyclopropenes under 1 atm of CO, which provides rapid access to structurally diverse primary amides, secondary amides, and tertiary amides. Notably, the Ni-catalyzed asymmetric hydrocarboxylation reaction is also established with H2O as a nucleophile to give the corresponding cyclopropyl carboxylic acids in good yields with excellent stereoselectivities. These reactions feature mild reaction conditions, exceptional levels of enantioselectivities and diastereoselectivities while also exhibiting remarkable tolerance towards diverse functional groups. Moreover, our methodology proves to be highly efficient for the synthesis of pharmaceutical compounds such as Levomilnacipran and Bicifadine.

Introduction

The distinctive structural characteristics and physicochemical properties of cyclopropane play a pivotal role in the rational design of small-molecule drugs, thereby facilitating their development as therapeutics with enhanced efficacy, reduced off-target effects, and improved metabolic stability1,2. As such, over the past few decades, the cyclopropane derivatives have been increasingly utilized as molecular building blocks in drug discovery and development3–12. On the other hand, amide and carboxylic acid moieties also play a prominent role in medicinal chemistry and are widely found in biologically active small molecules, encompassing both naturally occurring and synthetic compounds13–16. Given the significance of cyclopropanes, amides and carboxylic acids in chemistry and biology, the enantioselective installation of amide into the cyclopropane framework is considered to be the frontier in innovative pharmaceuticals or agrochemicals. Transition-metal-catalyzed hydrocarbonylation of alkenes, especially hydroaminocarbonylation, has attracted increasing interest in recent years as one of the most atom-economic and straightforward approaches to access amides and their derivatives22–27. Notable examples, including the palladium- and copper-catalyzed alkene hydroaminocarbonylations, have been extensively studied28–35. Despite impressive advancements have been achieved, the enantioselective synthesis of chiral amides via catalytic asymmetric hydroaminocarbonylation remained a formidable challenge in organic chemistry36–39. Only isolated examples have been demonstrated on the enantioselective variant. Recently, Guan and coworkers accomplished an asymmetric hydroaminocarbonylation of styrenes with arylamines realized by palladium catalysis. However, the limited substrate scope and the requirement of high-pressure CO may limit further synthetic applications36. In 2023, Wu and colleagues established a Cu-catalyzed asymmetric hydroaminocarbonylation of unactivated olefins37. However, the reaction necessitated 10 atm of CO, a super stoichiometric amount of silane, and protected hydroxylamines, which significantly resulted in lower atom economy. It is therefore imperative to develop a mild reaction condition, enhancing the atom economy, and expanding the substrate scope for asymmetric hydroaminocarbonylation. Moreover, to the best of our knowledge, there have been no reports on the construction of chiral primary amides under 1 atm of CO through transition-metal-catalyzed hydroaminocarbonylation of alkenes (Fig. 1B).

As one of the first-row transition metals, nickel possesses appealing attributes and exhibits distinctive characteristics as well as prominent catalytic properties in alkene-carbonylation chemistry40–49. Despite the susceptibility of nickel to carbon monoxide (CO) poisoning under high-pressure conditions, nickel exhibits remarkable catalytic activity for carbonylation reactions at ambient pressure50, thereby establishing a robust research foundation for achieving mild and asymmetric hydroaminocarbonylation. In our previous study, we have successfully demonstrated that the synthesis of primary amides from olefins could be realized by Pd-catalyzed hydroaminocarbonylation with ammonium salts as nitrogen sources51, in which the utilization of ammonium salt as an alternative to ammonia can effectively mitigate the excessive formation of unreactive Werner-type amine-palladium complex, thereby circumventing catalyst deactivation52. Inspired by this successful strategy, we reasoned that the inorganic ammonium salts might also be utilized as an ammonia surrogate for the Ni-catalyzed asymmetric hydroaminocarbonylation to provide chiral primary amides. Herein, we report a nickel-catalyzed asymmetric hydroaminocarbonylation of cyclopropenes with simple ammonium salt and organic amines under mild reaction conditions, offering rapid access and high productivity for cyclopropanecarboxamides and their derived drugs. Moreover, the asymmetric hydrocarboxylation reaction could also proceed smoothly to form cyclopropyl carboxylic acids with H2O as a nucleophile under the same Ni-catalysis (Fig. 1C).

Results

Reaction development

We started the investigation with (1-methylcycloprop-2-en-1-yl)benzene 1a as the model substrate and NH4OAc as the amine source (Table 1). The desired reaction was initially carried out at 60 °C for 24 h in the presence of 1 atm of CO in THF with 5 mol% of Ni(COD)2 as the catalyst precursor. Firstly, the effect of chiral ligands on the model reaction was evaluated. When Synphos (L1) was employed as a chiral ligand, only 5% yield of the racemic product 3 was obtained. Another commonly utilized diphosphine ligand Duphos (L2), was also tested, and only a trace amount of the desired product was detected. Similarly, the pyridine-containing oxazoline ligand (Pybox) (L3) also proved to be ineffective to the desired hydroaminocarbonylation. Further screening of the commercially available ligands led us to find that phox-type P,N-ligand L4 could afford the desired product in 21% yield with 73% ee. Verifying the substituent on the chiral oxazoline fragment of the P,N-ligand revealed that the incorporation of the isopropyl group could increase the reactivity (59% yield), but there was almost no improvement in enantioselectivity (75% ee). Next, a series of phosphinoferrocenyloxazolines derived from ferrocene were evaluated. The L7 with isopropyl group incorporated into the oxazoline-moiety exhibited high reactivity and enantioselectivity to furnish the desired primary amide 3 in 71% isolated yield with 93% ee and complete diastereoselectivity. After identifying L7 as the best chiral ligand, additional investigations were carried out to maximize the efficiency of the reaction and the desired product 3 could be obtained in 85% yield with 99% ee (entries 1–4). Given that the amine source could significantly influence the reactivity of the carbonylation, a series of ammonium salts, such as revealed NH4F, NH4Cl, NH4Br, and NH4I, were tested in all cases (entries 5–8). These results shown in Table 1 disclosed that only NH4OAc was effective for the desired reaction. Furthermore, we examined the impact of CO pressure on the reaction system and no desired product was obtained when the CO pressure was increased to 10 atm (entry 9). Substrate scope evaluation

With the optimal reaction condition in hand, the substrate scope of the cyclopropenes was then explored with NH4OAc as amine source. As shown in Fig. 2, the absolute structure of the product 3 was determined as (1S, 2S) by X-ray analysis of the single crystal of product 3, in which the newly installed amide group is trans to the sterically hindered benzene ring. The absolute structures of other products are deduced from this product. Remarkably, this reaction demonstrated exceptional versatility by accommodating various substituted groups on the benzene ring of the phenyl-containing cyclopropenes. Consequently, the corresponding products (3–13) could be obtained with good yields ranging from 71% to 86% while simultaneously maintaining remarkable enantioselectivities (94–99% ee) and >20:1 diastereoselectivities. The substrates bearing substituents on the ortho-position exhibited relatively lower reactivity to give the desired products (5 and 13) with lower yields and enantioselectivities (94–95% ee). The salient feature of this reaction lies in its exceptional compatibility with iodobenzene-derived alkene, facilitating the attainment of the target product 12 in 72% yield with 96% ee. Notably, the corresponding chiral amides (14–15) were also obtained in good yields and high enantioselectivities (90–95% ee) with retention of the hydroxyl functional groups when cycloproprenes containing hydroxyl group at benzene ring were used as substrates. In addition to the presence of a methyl substituent (R1 = Me) on the cyclopropene skeleton, various alkyl substituents were also suitable for the reaction (16–17). Furthermore, the reaction exhibited compatibility with esters, acids, and other functional groups, leading to high yields and enantiomeric excesses of the corresponding products (18–21). Besides phenyl substituents (R2 = Ph), cyclopropenes containing heterocycles such as furan, thiophene and pyridine, could be smoothly converted to the corresponding primary amides (22–24) in decent yields (65–70% yields) with excellent enantioselectivities (97–99% ee) and completely diastereoselectivities. For the dialkyl-substituted cyclopropene, the hydroaminocarbonylation could also proceed successfully to form the desired product 25 in 60% yield with 95% ee. Furthermore, the spiro-cyclopropenes bearing different tension rings also worked smoothly to afford the corresponding primary amides (26–28) in 47–77% yields with 92–99% ee. Subsequently, we investigated the substrate scope of the carbonylation reaction between substrate 1a and various aromatic and aliphatic amines under slightly modified reaction conditions by using 10 mol% of acetic acid as co-catalyst (Fig. 3, top). It is worth emphasizing that these reactions exhibited remarkable diastereoselectivities and enantioselectivities. A series of aromatic primary amines containing different substituents on the phenyl ring could be converted to the corresponding secondary amides (30–37) in high yields and excellent enantioselectivities (96–99% ee). There was no essential correlation between reactivity and the electronic properties of substituents on the phenyl ring of aromatic amines, as both electron-donating (Me and NMe2) and electron-withdrawing (F and Br) groups were compatible well. For aniline bearing hydroxyl group at benzene ring, the desired hydroaminocarbonylation reaction still proceeded smoothly, delivering the desired amides (36–37) in good yields and high enantioselectivities. Besides, this catalytic protocol also tolerated primary aliphatic amines, furnishing the desired products (38–39) in good yields with 97–98% ee. Except for the asymmetric synthesis of secondary amides, the tertiary amides (40–46) could also be prepared in good yields with high enantioselectivities (95–99% ee) by using secondary amines as coupling partners. Both linear alkylamines (40–42) and cyclic amines (43–45) were tolerated well, affording the desired tertiary amides with excellent enantioselectivities (98–99% ee), albeit at slightly reduced yields. The high compatibility of amine encouraged us to extend this synthetic approach to the late-stage modification of pharmaceutically relevant amines. As expected, desloratadine could also be successfully converted to the corresponding amide 46 in 76% yield and 98% ee. The chiral carboxylic acids serve not only as vital building blocks but also as ubiquitous structural motifs in natural products and bioactive molecules. Transition metal-catalyzed hydrocarboxylation of alkenes with H2O as the nucleophile represents one of most straightforward strategies to construct synthetically useful carboxylic acids. The success of the above hydroaminocarbonylation reaction motivated us to further evaluate its scope for carboxylic acid synthesis with H2O as a nucleophile (Fig. 3, bottom). To our delight, a series of cyclopropenes proved to be compatible with this asymmetric hydrocarbonylation reaction, affording the corresponding chiral carboxylic acids (47–53) in good yields (54–73% yields) and excellent enantioselectivities (91–99% ee). Both electron-donating (48) and electron-withdrawing (49–50) groups were well compatible. Beyond phenyl substituents, cyclopropenes substituted with heterocycle or ester could be smoothly converted to the corresponding cyclopropyl carboxylic acids (52–53) in good yields with 99% ee. Synthetic applications

After investigating a range of substrates, we conducted several synthetic transformations to demonstrate the practicality of this asymmetric reaction (Fig. 4). Firstly, the total synthesis of levomilnacipran 59 was undertaken (Fig. 4a). Under the standard reaction conditions, the catalytic reaction on a 12 mmol scale delivered 1.8 g of the desired product ent-20 in 70% yield with 98% ee using ent-L7 as the chiral ligand instead. Treatment of ent-20 with K2CO3 in toluene afforded the cyclization intermediate 55, which was further selectively reduced by BH3 to afford the corresponding lactam 56 in 60% yield. Following the hydrolysis of lactam into the amino acid and a protection of NH group with Boc2O under basic conditions provided 57 in 80% yield. After the amino acid 57 was coupled with diethylamine, TFA-mediated Boc deprotection of 58 afforded levomilnacipran 59 in 82% yield and 98% ee. It is worth mentioning that this asymmetric synthesis of levomilnacipran from methyl phenylacetate with 98% ee is the most enantioselective route at present53. By utilizing this catalytic reaction as the key step, the preparation of bicifadine54 61 could be achieved through continuous cyclization and reduction processes in 62% yield and 99% ee (Fig. 4b). Mechanistic investigations

To gain insights into the catalytic process of this reaction, several control experiments were conducted (Fig. 5). First, when BHT, a radical scavenger, was introduced to the reaction under standard conditions, the desired product 3 was obtained in 78% yield and no BHT adduct was detected (entry 1). For the carbonylation of NH4Cl and PhNH2, a catalytic amount of acetic acid was proven to be very necessary for the formation of amides. Notably, diphenylsilane, a reagent used to produce metal-hydride species56, also exhibits catalytic activity similar to that of acetic acid (entries 2–7). The above results indicate that this hydroaminocarbonylation reaction does not involve radical intermediates, and the generation of nickel-hydride species is the key to the catalytic reaction, which was further confirmed by NMR experiments (for more details see SI). Moreover, the key alkylnickel intermediate C and acylnickel intermediate D were also detected by HRMS, respectively (see SI). Finally, the catalytic reaction was conducted with deuterated cyclopropene 1a-d as the starting material and the deuterated amide product 3-d was isolated in 80% yield. The syn-addition of the Ni−H bond across the alkene double bond was confirmed by comparing the relative configurations of H and Me in the deuterated product 3-d and the absolute configuration of the product 3 (see SI).Fig. 5Mechanistic studies and catalytic cycle.a Control experiments. b Proposed catalytic cycle. On the basis of the results presented above and our previous work51,57, a possible mechanism of this hydroaminocarbonylation reaction was proposed (Fig. 5b). First, the key nickel-hydride species B was generated via oxidative addition of NH4OAc to Ni(0) A. Coordination and insertion of cyclopropene into the Ni-H species B yielded the alkyl nickel intermediate C, which underwent CO insertion to form acylnickel intermediate D. Finally, SN2-type reductive elimination took place between the acylnickel intermediate D and NH3 to release the desired amide 3 with the regeneration of the catalytic active species A.

Discussion

In summary, we have developed a nickel-catalyzed highly diastereo- and enantioselective hydrocarbonylation of cyclopropenes under 1 atm of CO. Diverse cyclopropenes and simple inorganic ammonium salt, organic amines as well as water are compatible with these conditions, making this strategy more attractive for step economical and atom economical synthesis. The employment of chiral Ni-catalyst composed with a chiral bidentate phosphine-oxazoline ligand bearing a ferrocene backbone is vital for the reactivity and enantioselective control of the reaction. The synthetic potential of this method has been demonstrated by the efficient synthesis of medicinally relevant molecules. We believe this work not only provides a rapid approach to chiral amides, but also inspires further advances in Ni-catalyzed carbonylation chemistry.

Methods

General procedure for asymmetric hydroaminocarbonylation reactions

In a N2-filled glove box, 1 (0.2 mmol), 2 (0.25 mmol), Ni(COD)2 (2.75 mg, 0.01 mmol), L7 (7.1 mg, 0.015 mmol) and THF (1.5 mL) were added to a dry glass vessel, which was placed in an autoclave. Then, the autoclave was taken out of the glove box to purge and charge with CO (1 atm). The reaction mixture was stirred at 60 °C for 24 h. After the reaction finished, the autoclave was cooled to room temperature and the pressure was carefully released in the fume hood. The d.r. were measured by 1H NMR analysis of the crude reaction mixture. Then the corresponding reaction mixture was purified by flash column chromatography on a silica gel column (petroleum ether/ethyl acetate = 30/1–5/1) to give the desired product. The ee value of product was determined by HPLC analysis with chiral column.
