Latent profiles of grit, self-control, belonging, and college motives: differential effects of self-control on academic performance among Chilean university students
Authors: Bernardita Tornero, Marcela Cárcamo, Manuel Rengifo, Jorge Tricio, Cristian A. Rojas-Barahona, Jorge Gaete
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The challenge of improving academic performance, retention, and graduation rates has prompted researchers to examine the role of psychological factors in higher education success. Among these, grit, self-control, sense of belonging, and motives for attending college have emerged as key psychological variables that shape academic trajectories. The purpose of this study was to identify latent profiles of university students based on grit, self-control, sense of belonging, and motives for attending college, and to explore their associations with academic performance. The sample consisted of 1,651, mostly first-year students, from a private highly selective university in Santiago, Chile. Participants completed validated scales measuring each of the four psychological variables. Latent Profile Analysis was conducted to uncover distinct psychosocial configurations, followed by multinomial logistic regression to identify predictors of profile membership and associations with academic outcomes. Results supported a four-profile solution, with most students classified into a predominant group characterized by moderate grit and self-control but a strong sense of belonging. Profiles were primarily differentiated by self-control and motivational orientations rather than grit or belonging. Neither grit nor sense of belonging predicted academic performance; however, self-control subscales showed differential associations—interpersonal self-control was positively related to academic achievement, whereas work self-control showed a slight negative association. These findings suggest that while psychosocial profiles offer valuable insights into student diversity, their predictive power for academic performance may be limited in homogeneous, high-achieving university contexts. Nevertheless, the differential role of self-control highlights the importance of targeted interventions promoting adaptive self-regulation and emotional competence in higher education settings.

Introduction

Academic underperformance among university students represents a significant global challenge, as reflected in retention and graduation rates across higher education systems [53]. A substantial proportion of students fail to complete their degree programs, generating considerable personal and societal costs [1]. At the individual level, non-completion often leads to financial loss, reduced lifetime earnings, and fewer career opportunities. At the societal level, low graduation rates contribute to a less qualified workforce, thereby constraining innovation, productivity, and economic growth [9]. Moreover, high dropout rates place a burden on public resources, as governments invest heavily in students who ultimately do not enter the skilled labor market [2, 30]. These dynamics highlight the urgent need to better understand and address academic underachievement in order to promote both individual advancement and broader national development [36].

Within higher education, academic performance is widely considered a central indicator of student success, retention, and well-being [80]. Understanding the factors that influence academic achievement has therefore become a priority for researchers and policymakers. One integrative approach to explaining academic outcomes is provided by the Conceptual Utility Model for the Management of Stress and Psychological Wellbeing (CMMSPW™) [18]. This model organizes psychological and contextual variables into a structured system of predictive, mediating, and outcome factors. It builds on the Presage–Process–Product (3P) framework and integrates theoretical perspectives on self-regulation, external regulation of behavior, and competence development.

Within this framework, presage variables refer to personal and contextual factors that exist prior to the learning process and shape the conditions under which learning occurs. These include individual characteristics such as personality traits, self-regulation, and affect, as well as contextual factors related to the educational environment. Process variables, in contrast, correspond to mediating mechanisms that operate during learning, including cognitive, motivational, and emotional competencies. These competencies involve conceptual knowledge, procedural skills (e.g., learning strategies and metacognitive regulation), and attitudinal components such as motivation, achievement of emotions, and coping strategies. The interaction between presage and process variables ultimately influences product variables, which represent the outcomes of the learning process and include academic performance, satisfaction with learning, psychological well-being, and levels of academic stress.

In line with this perspective, the variables examined in the present study can be conceptually organized using the CMMSPW™ model. Presage variables include demographic characteristics (age and sex), family educational background, financial support for studies, and prior academic achievement indicators (e.g., high school GPA and standardized test scores). Process variables, in turn, encompass motivational and socio-cognitive constructs that influence how students engage with academic tasks. Among these, grit (i.e., perseverance toward long-term goals), self-control, sense of belonging, and motives for attending college have received increasing research attention [28, 40, 47, 55, 63, 67]. These constructs are particularly relevant because they not only help explain academic performance but also represent modifiable factors that can be targeted through educational and psychological interventions. Finally, product variables represent the outcomes of the learning process, with academic performance serving as the primary dependent variable in the present study. This conceptual organization allows the research to be situated within a comprehensive explanatory framework in which academic achievement emerges from the interaction between students’ personal and contextual antecedents and the motivational–regulatory processes that unfold during university learning.

Further details on the measurement of presage variables are provided in the Methods section. Process variables are introduced conceptually in the following sections, where their relationships with academic performance (the product variable) are also examined.

Grit

Duckworth et al. [23] conceptualized grit as perseverance and passion to achieve long-term goals. In other words, grit represents a sustained commitment to an overall goal, even in the face of difficulties, failures, or lack of progress [25, 49]. Numerous studies with high school and college students have shown that grit correlates positively with academic performance indicators, such as grade point average (GPA) [25, 49] and academic engagement [49, 68].

Among the two dimensions that comprise the construct (perseverance of effort and consistency of interest), it has been observed that perseverance of effort contributes more to explaining academic performance and student engagement, even after controlling variables such as conscientiousness, self-control, prior performance, and sociodemographic factors [43]. Also in longitudinal studies, grit has been shown to be a robust predictor of academic performance, even when controlling intelligence or prior grades. In university contexts, it provides insight into why some students persist despite facing personal or academic difficulties, maintaining a sustained effort towards their goals [24].

Furthermore, it has been shown that grit can be developed through specific interventions. Quasi-experimental studies have shown positive effects on improving grit, particularly when targeting goal commitment, growth mindset, and emotional self-regulation [28, 40]. For example, Tang et al. [68] show that fostering commitment to learning goals increases persistence in the face of obstacles [68]. Along these same lines, Durlak and Weissberg [27] and Singh and Jha [61] argue that social-emotional learning programs also contribute to the development of skills such as tenacity, which facilitate coping with challenges in the educational setting [28, 61].

Self-control

Self-control refers to the ability to regulate conflicting thoughts, emotions, and behaviors in accordance with long-term goals [26]. According to Duckworth and Gross [22], self-control predicts sustained achievement by enabling disciplined task execution, even in the absence of immediate motivation. Its role is particularly critical during the transition from secondary school to the greater autonomy of university life [22].

Grit and self-control are closely related in that both pertain to the pursuit of goals despite obstacles or distractions [10]. However, they are theoretically distinct along three key dimensions: the type of goals they support, the nature of the temptations they counter, and the time frame in which they operate. In terms of goal type, grit is oriented toward long-term, superordinate goals that often reflect a person’s core values or life ambitions. These goals typically require sustained effort over the years—such as becoming a scientist or building a successful business. Grit involves a deep-seated passion and commitment to such high-level objectives and is marked by a persistent focus even when progress is slow or setbacks occur. In contrast, self-control relates more to immediate or short-term goals that may support broader aspirations but are often narrower in scope. For instance, choosing to study for an exam instead of going out with friends reflects a momentary act of self-control that aligns with—but is distinct from—the broader goal of academic success.

The nature of temptation also differentiates between the two constructs. Grit helps individuals persevere through challenges like monotony, delayed gratification, and repeated failure. These are not fleeting urges but more enduring psychological hurdles that can erode long-term motivation. Grit requires an ability to maintain interest and commitment over time, even when enthusiasm wanes. Self-control, by contrast, is concerned with resisting immediate, often pleasurable distractions—such as social media, junk food, or procrastination—that threaten short-term goal execution. It operates in contexts where the individual must override an impulsive response in favor of a more rational, goal-consistent action.

Finally, grit and self-control differ in their time frames. Grit functions on a long-term horizon. It is about sticking with one’s goals over months, years, or even decades, and is measured by the consistency of interest and effort over time. Self-control, on the other hand, plays out in the short term. It is about making good choices in the moment—choices that are important both at the individual or at the collective level. In other words, self-regulation also includes the degree to which a person can regulate their thoughts, emotions, and behaviors specifically in social or relational contexts.

In summary, while grit and self-control are both essential for achieving success, they operate in distinct psychological domains. Grit provides the motivational stamina to pursue enduring goals across long time spans, while self-control enables individuals to manage impulses and distractions that could derail progress in the short term [22]. Concerning this similarity, Duckworth [21] stated that people who are gritty are more likely to be self-controlled and vice versa. From her point of view, grit and self-control are so connected that having both secures more success than having only one [21].

Despite the previous evidence indicating that both grit and self-control are positively associated with a range of academic outcomes such as retention [45, 65, 77], GPA [3, 7, 8, 37, 38, 45, 66], and self-reported grades [12, 17, 24], this conceptual distinction has been criticized based on empirical data that has shown that self-control has no incremental validity over grit [16, 49, 54].

However, more recent studies have used structural equation modeling to show that self‐control is a valuable mediator between grit and academic performance [56].

Just like with grit, there are different cognitive and behavioral interventions that have proven to be effective in encouraging self-controlled conduct [47, 55, 63, 67], which reinforces the conclusion that self-control is a malleable psychological trait.

Sense of belonging

Sense of belonging is defined as the personal experience of a strong connection to social groups, physical locations, and both individual and shared experiences [4–6]. Research has shown that almost all people wonder if they belong in a context that is new to them. Walton & Cohen [73] have called this phenomenon to be uncertain. The concern about belonging can be especially strong in those who face group disadvantages, because they become more attentive to relevant cues, such as the numerical underrepresentation of her group or physical elements that signal the dominance of other groups. Such signals can continually raise and re-raise questions of belonging. Therefore, concerns about belonging carry an important psychological consequence: they provide students with a lens through which they make sense of contextual events. When a student who is already concerned about belonging fails a course, has a conflict with a classmate, or has her academic work criticized, she may infer that she or people like her simply do not belong in the university at large [73].

Several studies have found positive relationships between sense of belonging and academic performance [29, 32, 62]. For example, Kelly et al. [41] have shown that a greater sense of belonging is associated with higher levels of intrinsic motivation, enjoyment, and academic persistence [41]. In turn, an environment that promotes belonging protects against thoughts of abandonment and fosters engagement with academic tasks [50].

In addition, higher social belonging levels have been promoted by specific interventions both in schools [76] and in higher education [11, 14, 42, 59, 72, 74]. Social belonging interventions have proven to be more beneficial for students who are usually marginalized [11, 42, 59, 74, 75]. Like the other variables, social belonging interventions have proven to be more beneficial for usually marginalized students [11, 42, 59, 74, 75].

Motives for going to college

Students with a stronger sense of purpose persist longer on a monotonous task rather than succumbing to a tempting distraction, and several months later, they were less likely to drop out of college. Motivation has also been identified as a mediator between factors such as locus of control, belonging, and performance [70]. Assessing the quality of motivation allows us to understand not only how much effort a student puts in, but also why they do so.

Fewer research has explored the relationship between the motives for going to college and the quality of involvement, persistence, and learning outcomes. Yeager et al., [79] developed an instrument to explore differences between self-transcendent (e.g., “I want to become an educated citizen that can contribute to society”), self-oriented (e.g., “I want to become an independent thinker”), and extrinsic motives (e.g., “I want to get a good job”) for going to college. Research has found that students who declare more self-transcendent purposes tend to show greater college persistence. In contrast, more self-focused motives—such as the desire for an interesting or enjoyable career—did not consistently produce these benefits on their own. Notably, these outcomes were not influenced by cognitive ability [79]. Other studies have found that it is possible to facilitate the development of self-transcendent purposes through brief psychological interventions. In turn, these purposes have promoted improvements in motivation and academic achievement [35].

Interrelation between variables

The relationship between grit, self-control, sense of belonging, and motives for attending college has been examined across multiple theoretical perspectives in educational and motivational psychology (e.g., [57, 58]). These perspectives converge in suggesting that students’ motivation, emotional connection to the academic environment, and regulatory capacities are interrelated and jointly influence engagement, persistence, and academic outcomes [34, 39].

In general terms, self-control can be understood as a key regulatory mechanism that shapes how students translate their motivations into sustained academic behavior [22, 81]. Higher levels of self-control support goal-directed actions, reduce the impact of distractions, and facilitate persistence over time [26]. At the same time, students’ motives for attending college and their sense of belonging influence the quality and direction of their motivation, which in turn affects their capacity to maintain effort and engagement [34, 73]. Grit, as a tendency to sustain long-term goals, reflects the cumulative expression of these motivational and self-regulatory processes [24, 49].

Taken together, these variables can be understood as complementary and dynamically related psychological factors. Changes in one domain-particularly in self-regulation-may influence motivational orientations and engagement, highlighting the importance of considering their combined effects when examining academic performance [39, 57].

Based on these relations, our general objective is to examine latent profiles in university students’ psychological characteristics-grit, self-control, sense of belonging, and motives for attending college-and analyzing their associations with academic performance. The specific objectives are: 1) To identify latent profiles of university students based on their levels of grit, self-control, sense of belonging, and motives for attending college. 2) To examine whether the identified profiles differ in academic performance one year later, assessing whether different configurations of psychological characteristics are associated with distinct academic outcomes. 3) To investigate whether background variables, including sociodemographic characteristics, prior academic achievement, and parental educational background, predict membership in the identified latent profiles. And finally, 4) to assess the individual associations between each psychological variable (grit, self-control, sense of belonging, and motives for attending college) and academic performance.

Methods

Participants

The sample selection was for convenience. Inclusion criteria were: (a) enrollment as a university student at the participating institution, and (b) provision of informed consent. No formal exclusion criteria were applied beyond incomplete or missing questionnaire responses.

We asked permission from the university authorities and faculty to implement the questionnaire during class. We invited all students from a private university in Santiago, Chile, to participate. Students came from different majors or careers, and their participation was voluntary. Most participants were first-year students (1,142; 69.17% of the sample). Individual responses were strictly confidential and only known to the research team. All 1,651 participants signed a written and informed consent form before answering the questionnaire. Instruments

Presage variables measures

Presage variables included demographic, socioeconomic, and prior academic achievement indicators that characterize students before entering the university learning process.

Demographic variables

Age was treated as a continuous variable, and sex was coded as a dichotomous variable.

School achievement history

This measure refers to several variables related to students’ academic performance during and at the end of high school. We considered the global Grade Point Average (gGPA) for high school, calculated as the average of the annual GPAs from 1 st to 4th year of high school (equivalent to 9th–12th grades). In Chile, students wishing to apply to higher education must take the Test of Access to Higher Education (PSU, for its acronym in Spanish). This test assesses competencies by focusing on both "knowledge" and "know-how," ensuring that the skills and knowledge necessary for success in university training are integrated. For this study, we combined the scores from two mandatory PSU sections—Reading Skills and Math Skills—into a single score ranging from 100 to 1,000 points.

We also included the Ranking of Grades; a selection factor used in the centralized university admissions system alongside PSU and gGPA scores. This ranking evaluates a student’s relative academic performance in high school by comparing their grades with those of students from the previous three graduating classes at the same school.

In addition, students’ gGPAs are converted into the standardized PSU scoring scale (gGPA score conversion). The high school graduation grade is determined by averaging the annual GPAs from 9 to 12th grade and rounding the result to two decimal places. This average is then transformed into a standardized score using a conversion table developed by the National Department of Educational Evaluation, Measurement and Registration (DEMRE), the institution responsible for overseeing higher education admissions in Chile [20].

Financing

We asked for sources of university financing, exploring these options: 1 = Family, 2 = Credit, 3 = Credit and family, 4 = Partial scholarship and family, 5 = Partial scholarship, family, and credit, 6 = Scholarship/Gratuity, 7 = Other. The reference group was 1 = Family.

Mother’s and father’s education

We collected data on the educational attainment of students' parents (both mother and father). We used a scale ranging from 1 to 11 to categorize the highest level of education each parent had completed: 1 = Incomplete Basic Education, 2 = Complete Basic Education, 3 = Incomplete Secondary Education, 4 = Complete Secondary Education, 5 = Incomplete Higher Technical Education, 6 = Complete Higher Technical Education, 7 = Incomplete University Education, 8 = Complete University Education, 9 = Incomplete Post-university Education, 10 = Complete Post-University Education, and 11 = Not applicable. The lowest level, Incomplete Basic Education, served as the baseline for comparison.

Process variables instruments

Given the use of previously validated instruments, we relied on established factor structures reported in prior studies. Internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha and omega) was assessed in the present sample.

Grit scale

This is a tool consisting of eight questions designed to evaluate a person's grit, which is understood as having both consistent determination and enthusiasm for achieving goals over a long period [23]. Research indicates that grit, as measured by this scale, can predict success in difficult areas, even when considering natural abilities or talent. Studies have generally found the scale to be reliable, with measures of internal consistency typically falling between 0.73 and 0.83 [25]. In our specific group, the internal consistency was 0.75 (Cronbach's alpha) and 0.64 (Omega).

Self-control scale

To measure self-control, defined as the capacity to manage one's focus, feelings, and actions even when facing distractions [81], we used an 8-question version of the Brief Self-Control Scale [69]. This scale assesses two aspects of self-control: Work self-control (for example, "I pay attention and resist distractions in class") and Interpersonal self-control (for example, "I can remain calm when criticized or otherwise provoked"). In our study group, the scale showed an internal consistency of 0.64 (Cronbach's alpha) and 0.65 (Omega).

Sense of social and academic fit (SSF)

Students completed the Sense of Academic Fit scale to assess how well they felt they belonged in their field of study. This 17-item survey measures students perceived social belonging within their academic department. Participants were asked to rate statements such as "People in [my school or academic] department like me" and "I belong in [my school or academic] department" on a scale ranging from strongly disagree to strongly agree. A previous study validated this instrument, reporting a reliability coefficient of 0.89. In addition, students evaluated their perceived social fit relative to other students using two items, which demonstrated a reliability of 0.67 [73]. In the analyses, this measure was operationalized as “sense of belonging” to ensure consistency with the conceptual framework and reporting across sections.

Motives for going to college

To understand the reasons behind attending college, we used a 7‑item scale from Yeager et al. [79], rated from 1 (“Not at all true”) to 5 (“Completely true”). It has two subscales: self-transcendent motives (e.g., “I want to learn things that will help me make a positive impact on the world”) and extrinsic self-oriented motives (e.g., “I want to earn more money”). In our sample, internal consistency was Cronbach’s α = 0.65 and Omega = 0.72 [79].

Product variable measure

Academic achievement

Academic achievement was operationalized as students’ overall academic performance during their first year at university. This variable was obtained from institutional academic records and reflects students’ cumulative Grade Point Average (GPA) at the end of the academic period. To facilitate interpretation and comparability across analyses, GPA was standardized (z-score), with higher values indicating better academic performance. This standardized measure was used as the primary outcome variable in all subsequent analyses examining associations with presage and process variables, as well as differences across latent profiles.

Procedure

This study followed a quantitative, cross-sectional design and was carried out during the first academic semester of 2016. Our goal was to explore how university students differ in key motivational and psychosocial aspects. To do this, we collected data at a single point in time using self-report questionnaires administered during scheduled class sessions. This approach allowed us to capture natural variation in student characteristics and to apply latent profile analysis to identify distinct patterns based on selected psychological variables.

Participation was voluntary, and students provided informed consent prior to completing the survey. The questionnaire included measures of grit, self-control, sense of belonging, and motives for attending college, as well as socio-demographic information and previous academic background.

The study was approved by a Scientific Ethics Committee before data collection began. Written and Informed consent was obtained from all subjects. All methods were performed in accordance with the declaration of Helsinki.

Data analysis

A descriptive analysis was conducted for all study variables. The normality of quantitative variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Continuous variables were summarized as means and standard deviations, while categorical variables were reported as absolute and relative frequencies. A latent profile analysis (LPA) was conducted using the previously described scales (grit, self-control, sense of belonging, and motives for going to college) to identify distinct subgroups of students based on their motivational and psychosocial characteristics. Model selection was based on multiple fit indices. The Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), and Bayesian Information Criterion adjusted for sample size (SABIC) were examined, with lower values indicating better model fit. Entropy was also considered as an indicator of classification quality, with values ≥ 0.60 interpreted as reflecting moderate to high classification accuracy. Final model selection is additionally considered model interpretability and theoretical plausibility. Models were also compared based on profile size (e.g., prevalence) and interpretability (including profiles with a low prevalence may be capitalizing on chance). Once the optimal number of profiles were determined, the probabilities of the indicator items were analyzed to examine the nature of the profile [15].

After identifying the optimal number of profiles, we explore associations between latent profiles and relevant outcome variables that were estimated using multinomial regression. The coefficient (e(β)), standard error and relative risk ratio (RRR), with their respective 95% confidence intervals were reported [46]. Several variables initially considered for the LPA were excluded due to moderate to high collinearity. Alternative model specifications were explored, and the final model retained variables that were theoretically relevant, statistically stable, and contributed to meaningful profile differentiation. This approach aims to ensure model parsimony, interpretability, and robustness. To explore the association between psychological variables and academic achievement, we used linear regression.

Sample size

Nylund-Gibson and Choi [52] suggest that it is desirable to have 300 or more cases for LCA and LTA analyses. A smaller sample size results in poor fitness, failure to converge, and the inability to identify classes with low membership [52].

Background predictors of latent profile membership

Multinomial regression analyses were conducted to examine predictors of latent profile membership, using Profile 1 as the reference group. For Profile 2, age was significantly associated with a higher likelihood of belonging to this profile (RRR = 1.18, 95% CI: 1.01–1.39, p = 0.037), while PSU Mathematics score showed a marginal association (p = 0.051). College year and sex were not significantly associated with Profile 2 membership.

For Profile 3, sex was significantly associated with a higher likelihood of belonging to this profile (RRR = 2.36, 95% CI: 1.67–3.34, p < 0.001). College year showed a marginal association (p = 0.090), whereas PSU Mathematics score and age were not significantly associated.

For Profile 4, sex remained significantly associated with profile membership (RRR = 3.17, 95% CI: 1.26–8.01, p = 0.015). No significant associations were observed for PSU Mathematics score, college year, or age.

Associations between psychological variables and academic achievement

To assess whether individual psychological variables were associated with academic performance, supplementary analyses were conducted. Results indicated significant associations between academic achievement and specific self-control dimensions.

Work self-control showed a small but significant negative association with academic achievement (β = − 0.091, p = 0.021), suggesting that higher levels of task-focused self-regulation were linked to slightly lower performance. In contrast, interpersonal self-control demonstrated a positive association with academic achievement (β = 0.127, p = 0.002), indicating that students with better emotional and social regulation tended to achieve higher academic outcomes.

No significant associations were observed for grit, sense of belonging, or motivational variables.

Discussion

This is the first study exploring the profile analyses of four psychological variables—grit, self-control, sense of belonging, and motives for going to college—and their relationship to academic achievement among undergraduates in Latin America. The analysis showed four distinct profiles, mainly differentiated by two out of the four variables, namely motives for attending college (self-transcendent and extrinsic subscales) and self-control (work and interpersonal subscales). In this sense, neither grit nor sense of belonging were relevant for the profiles’ configuration. These results contrast with a substantial body of literature positioning these constructs as central predictors of academic success [24, 31, 33]. However, this finding is consistent with more recent meta-analytic and critical perspectives indicating that grit may have limited incremental validity when considered alongside other self-regulatory and motivational variables [16, 49].

Turning to the association between these profiles and academic performance, the absence of significant differences across profiles further suggests that, within relatively homogeneous and high-achieving samples, psychosocial configurations may not translate into differentiated academic outcomes. Students at this private, highly selective university may share similar levels of perseverance and belonging due to their comparable previous academic achievement, educational backgrounds, and social contexts. Similarly, a previous study found that medical students at a single, highly selective university in China shared similar academic backgrounds and admission standards, resulting in a relatively homogeneous cohort where no “low-achieving” profile emerged because all participants had already demonstrated comparable academic success [78]. As a result, this pattern can be interpreted in light of range restriction effects commonly observed in selective educational contexts, where reduced variability in both cognitive and non-cognitive characteristics attenuates observed associations [57].

Beyond profile-level differences, the findings also provide insight into the role of background variables in shaping these configurations. Demographic variables—particularly age and sex—emerged as meaningful predictors of profile membership, aligning with prior research demonstrating systematic variation in motivational and self-regulatory patterns across developmental stages and gender groups [44, 60]. These findings underscore the importance of considering broader developmental and sociodemographic factors when examining the organization of psychological characteristics in higher education populations.

When examining the variables at the individual level, self-control emerged as the only construct significantly associated with academic performance, although its dimensions showed divergent effects. Interpersonal self-control was positively related to achievement, supporting theoretical perspectives that conceptualize self-regulation—particularly in its emotional and social dimensions—as a proximal determinant of academic functioning [22, 82]. This result is consistent with evidence highlighting the role of socio-emotional regulation and executive functioning in facilitating persistence, engagement, and adaptive responses to academic challenges [13]. In contrast, the negative association observed for work self-control diverges from the predominantly positive relationships reported in prior research [26, 38, 77]. One possible interpretation is that high levels of task-focused regulation may, in some contexts, reflect compensatory effort, rigidity, or reduced flexibility in learning strategies, which could hinder performance under demanding academic conditions. Such an interpretation aligns with emerging critiques suggesting that self-control may not uniformly yield positive outcomes and that its effects may depend on context and the specific regulatory processes involved [48, 71]. This pattern is consistent with theoretical perspectives that emphasize the central role of self-regulatory capacities in translating motivational intentions into effective academic behavior. Within the CMMSPW framework, self-control can be understood as a regulatory competency that enables students to manage cognitive, emotional, and behavioral demands during the learning process. More broadly, the CMMSPW™ model provides an integrative framework for understanding how presage, process, and product variables interact in shaping academic outcomes. From this perspective, self-control operates as a core regulatory mechanism within the process domain, enabling students to manage cognitive, emotional, and behavioral demands, while motivational and contextual factors contribute to the configuration of profiles and their potential impact on performance.

In contrast, grit, sense of belonging, and motives for attending college did not show direct associations with academic achievement in this study. While these constructs have been widely linked to persistence, engagement, and performance [34, 41, 79], their lack of predictive power in the present sample may reflect their more distal role within the broader system of academic functioning. From an integrative perspective, these variables may contribute to shaping students’ motivational orientations and engagement processes, but their effects on performance may be mediated by more proximal self-regulatory capacities, such as self-control. This interpretation is consistent with models of self-regulated learning that position regulatory skills as key mechanisms translating motivation into effective academic behavior [18, 19].

Taken together, these findings suggest that the relationship between non-cognitive variables and academic achievement is more nuanced than often assumed. Rather than exerting uniform or direct effects, psychological constructs appear to operate at different levels of proximity to academic outcomes, with self-regulatory processes playing a particularly central role. This highlights the need for more integrative approaches that consider not only the presence of specific psychological traits but also their functional interplay and contextual relevance within higher education settings. Furthermore, the results highlight the importance of examining the combined influence of personal and contextual factors in explaining academic outcomes. Although the present study focused primarily on students’ psychological characteristics, contextual variables—such as institutional support, teaching practices, or family educational background—may interact with personal attributes to shape students’ academic trajectories.

Looking ahead, future research could benefit from integrating both personal and contextual dimensions within broader analytical frameworks of self- and externally regulated learning to better understand how different educational environments interact with student characteristics to influence academic performance.

Limitations

Despite its contributions, the present study should be interpreted in light of several important considerations. First, the research was conducted in a single private and highly selective university, resulting in a large but relatively homogeneous sample in terms of prior academic achievement and socioeconomic background. While the large sample provides a valuable opportunity to examine psychosocial variation within a high-performing student population, it may also have reduced the variability of certain constructs—particularly grit and sense of belonging—potentially limiting their ability to differentiate students across latent profiles. Range restriction and potential ceiling effects are common methodological challenges in studies conducted within selective educational contexts [57] and may attenuate the strength of observed associations between psychosocial variables and academic outcomes [16, 49]. For example, previous research conducted in highly selective university settings has shown that when students share similar academic backgrounds and performance levels, variability in non-cognitive traits such as grit is reduced, thereby limiting their predictive power for academic outcomes [78].

Second, the relative homogeneity of the sample may also have influenced the distribution and interpretability of the latent profiles identified in the analysis. In the present study, one profile encompassed a large proportion of participants, whereas the others represented comparatively small subgroups of students. Although latent profile analysis can provide useful insights into patterns of co-occurring characteristics even in relatively homogeneous samples [46, 64], it relies on sufficient variability to detect well-separated latent subpopulations. In this context, the identified profiles should be interpreted cautiously, as they may reflect gradations within a largely homogeneous population rather than clearly distinct psychological types. Future research using larger and more diverse samples could help examine the variability, stability, and generalizability of these configurations.

Third, the psychological variables were assessed at a single time point during the academic year. Although academic performance was examined at the end of the same period, this design limits the possibility of drawing strong causal conclusions about the relationships between the variables [19, 57]. Longer longitudinal approaches would provide a valuable avenue for future research by allowing the examination of how motivational and self-regulatory profiles evolve over time and how these dynamics relate to students’ academic trajectories [51, 52].

Finally, the negative association observed between work self-control and academic performance should be interpreted with caution. Although a theoretical explanation related to overcontrol and reduced behavioral flexibility was considered, this interpretation remains speculative. Alternative explanations include multicollinearity among predictors, restricted variance due to sample homogeneity, or measurement limitations, such as the moderate internal consistency of the work self-control subscale. In addition, the focus on individual psychological characteristics, while informative, does not fully capture the broader contextual influences on academic performance. Future research could benefit from incorporating additional contextual dimensions—such as instructional practices, institutional support systems, and classroom climate—to better understand the complex interaction between presage and process variables.
