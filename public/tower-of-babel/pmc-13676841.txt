Symmetry‐Guided Optical Control of Dynamic 1D Protein Self‐Assembly and Precise In Vitro Initiation of Biomolecular Condensation
Authors: Jangwon Bae, Yongwon Jung
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTDynamic control over protein self‐assembly into defined architectures remains an important objective in bionanotechnology. Here, we report a fully genetically encoded, photoreversible one‐dimensional (1D) protein self‐assembly system. By exploiting symmetry matching between D2‐symmetric StrepTactin and the photoswitchable tetrameric fluorescent protein Dronpa145N, we engineer a hierarchical 1D protein array whose assembly and disassembly can be reversibly toggled using specific wavelengths of light. Optical regulation of Dronpa145N oligomerization enables dynamic modulation of protein connectivity and effective valency without chemical cross‐linkers or exogenous building blocks, providing a robust and modular platform for stimuli‐responsive protein assembly. As an application of this controllable architecture, we demonstrate that light‐induced array formation amplifies multivalency and can trigger liquid‐liquid phase separation (LLPS) in vitro, whereas optical disassembly suppresses condensate formation. This system further enables precise temporal and spatial initiation of phase separation under well‐defined conditions. Overall, this symmetry‐guided, photoreversible assembly offers a versatile framework for engineering dynamic protein materials and serves as a phase‐separating platform for dissecting the physicochemical principles underlying biomolecular condensate formation.

Introduction

The accurate and controllable assembly of biomolecules into well‐defined architecture remains a key challenge in the field of bionanotechnology. Proteins serve as versatile building blocks with diverse biological functions and have been extensively harnessed to construct ordered nanostructures using a wide range of biotechnological and chemical strategies. In general, protein assembly strategies can be broadly classified into two categories: those driven by intrinsic protein‐protein interactions (PPIs) and those mediated by chemical linkers [1]. Among these strategies, symmetry‐guided assembly is of particular interest, as symmetry underlies many natural protein‐protein interactions and macromolecular complexes. Because oligomeric proteins often possess intrinsic point symmetries (e.g., C2, C3, and D2), rational coupling of compatible symmetric interfaces enables the construction of highly ordered 1D nanowires, 2D lattices, and 3D polyhedral architectures [2]. Indeed, prior studies have demonstrated that combining proteins with matching rotational symmetries can yield artificially designed linear or planar self‐assembled arrays [3]. Such protein assemblies have been explored for a broad range of applications, including biosensing, biocatalysis, drug delivery, diagnostics, and therapeutics [2, 3].

Conventional static protein scaffolds, which are designed to adopt fixed architectures, have reached a relatively high level of methodological maturity. In contrast, stimuli‐responsive protein assemblies provide opportunities for precise spatiotemporal control over molecular structure and function. Although engineering assemblies with reversible, stimuli‐coupled energy landscapes remains challenging and limits the widespread adoption of such systems, their potential as smart biomaterials continues to motivate active investigation. Notable examples include chaperonins cross‐linked with photoswitchable small molecules, which reversibly open and close their cage structures [4]. Additional responsive systems involve merocyanine‐modified proteins that form 1D assemblies in the presence of metal ions and disassemble upon ATP addition [5], as well as photosensitive protein micelles capable of light‐triggered degradation [6]. Strategies have evolved from simple chemical cross‐linking toward increasingly sophisticated genetic engineering approaches, such as incorporating stimuli‐responsive domains to create controllable protein cages [7] or computationally designing hinge proteins that reversibly toggle conformations upon peptide binding [8]. Most recently, de novo protein design has been combined with genetic code expansion to incorporate light‐switchable non‐canonical amino acids, enabling reversible control over protein assembly [9]. However, many existing strategies rely on the use of exogenous building blocks or synthetic chemical modifications. This dependence often limits their compatibility with native biological environments and hinders the genetic encodability required for seamless integration into living systems. Achieving the dynamic organization of proteins into higher‐order, well‐defined architectures remains a significant challenge, as most responsive systems lack the structural precision to form extended assemblies such as 1D nanowires or 2D lattices. Therefore, a strategy that enables fully dynamic, reversible protein assembly using exclusively natural amino acids while maintaining high structural fidelity remains an important unmet need.

Light represents a particularly suitable external stimulus for dynamic protein assembly, offering precise spatiotemporal control, low cytotoxicity, and rapid response times. Among various optogenetic tools [10, 11], the fluorescent protein Dronpa [12] stands out as an ideal candidate for reversible assembly due to its symmetric oligomerization and bidirectional photoswitching. This dual‐light control circumvents the limitations of spontaneous thermal reversion of the photoactivated state, enabling more precise and repeatable modulation of protein‐protein interactions. Specifically, the Dronpa145N mutant (Lys145→Asn) predominantly exists as a tetramer exhibiting D2 point symmetry [13, 14]. Upon irradiation with cyan light (∼500 nm), the tetramer dissociates into monomers, concomitantly abolishing its D2 symmetry (Figure 1A). In contrast, violet light (∼400 nm) induces reversible oligomerization into the tetrameric, symmetry‐restored state. Leveraging this photoswitchable oligomerization behavior, we hypothesized that coupling tetrameric Dronpa145N to another D2‐symmetric protein, such as Streptavidin, could generate a dynamic 1D array. In this design, cyan light induces dissociation of Dronpa145N, leading to array disassembly, whereas violet light restores tetrameric symmetry, thereby driving reassembly (Figure 1B). Here, we demonstrate the construction of a photoreversible 1D protein array governed by symmetry matching and optical stimulation.

Schematic of the light‐controlled, symmetry‐driven 1D protein self‐assembly. (A) Reversible photo‐switching of the Dronpa145N. (B) D2‐symmetric Dronpa145N‐StreptagII and StrepTactin form a 1D assembly through symmetry matching. 505 nm light triggers disassembly by dissociating Dronpa145N tetramers, while 405 nm light induces reversible reassembly.

This photoreversible self‐assembly provides a versatile method for controlling linear protein valency, a key determinant of multivalent interactions. One important consequence of multivalent interactions is liquid–liquid phase separation (LLPS), the process by which biomolecules demix to form condensed phases [15, 16]. LLPS‐driven condensates play central roles in the organization of cellular physiology, including signaling cascades and gene expression. Because increased effective valency is known to promote biomolecular condensation, light‐tunable assembly systems enable optical regulation of LLPS. While intracellular optogenetic LLPS strategies have been established [17, 18, 19], the complexity of the cellular environment complicates quantitative mechanistic dissection. As an alternative, a controllable in vitro system provides a minimal, well‐defined platform for examining the physicochemical factors that influence phase behavior [19]. However, robust in vitro protein LLPS platforms remain scarce. Current methods often rely on coarse environmental triggers (e.g., temperature shifts or salt titration), which are insufficient for analyzing condensate dynamics under controlled conditions. Furthermore, existing light‐responsive tools often result in irreversible in vitro aggregation or lack the structural stability required for reliable control. In this study, we demonstrate that our symmetry‐guided protein assembly enables optical induction of LLPS via controlled valency amplification. This creates a powerful in vitro platform for accurate control and systematic analysis of biomolecular condensate formation.

Results and Discussion

Tetrameric Dronpa145N‐StreptagII and StrepTactin Self‐Assemble Into 1D Protein Arrays

To construct a 1D protein array using tetrameric Dronpa145N, we built upon a symmetry‐matching strategy. This design was inspired by a previous study demonstrating the formation of a one‐dimensional array by linking two D2‐symmetric proteins, DsRed and Streptavidin, via Streptavidin–Streptag interactions [3]. Recognizing that tetrameric Dronpa145N shares D2 symmetry and substantial structural homology with DsRed, we adopted a similar architectural framework. Additionally, to enhance array robustness at lower protein concentrations, we replaced the original components with StrepTactin (an engineered Streptavidin variant) and StreptagII. This binding pair exhibits a significantly higher affinity (KD ≈ 1 µM) [20] than the conventional Streptavidin–Streptag interaction (KD ≈ 37 µM) [21], thereby facilitating more stable assembly.

Accordingly, StrepTactin and Dronpa145N‐StreptagII were prepared as the constituent building blocks, and assembly was initiated by mixing them at equimolar concentrations (Figure 1B). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was then used to characterize the resulting assemblies. To prevent excessive adsorption of arrays onto the TEM grid during imaging, samples prepared by mixing 20 µM of each component (based on the Dronpa monomer concentration) were subsequently diluted to 4 µM before TEM analysis. Under these conditions, TEM images revealed linear, chain‐like assemblies with heterogeneous lengths, including structures extending from 100 nm to 300 nm (Figure 2A,B). Notably, the repeating unit in the array was measured to be 10–12 nm, consistent with the predicted unit length of ∼11 nm derived from the protein component structures (Figure S1) [3]. Based on these measurements, the length distributions correspond to arrays comprising up to approximately 25 repeating units, which equates to the assembly of 50 individual protein components.

To verify the formation of high‐molecular‐weight assemblies, we employed native polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE) (Figure 2C). Dronpa145N alone exhibited two distinct bands corresponding to its tetrameric and monomeric forms, consistent with its known oligomerization equilibrium. Analysis of the equimolar mixture revealed that the assembled species were retained mainly in the loading well and did not migrate into the resolving gel, while the band corresponding to the Dronpa tetramer was almost entirely depleted. As an orthogonal approach to assess hydrodynamic size, size‐exclusion chromatography (SEC) was performed (Figure 2D). The chromatogram of Dronpa145N‐StreptagII alone displayed two peaks corresponding to its tetrameric (early elution) and monomeric (late elution) forms, whereas StrepTactin eluted as a single peak at an intermediate retention volume. In contrast, the mixture exhibited a pronounced shift, with the peaks corresponding to the individual components nearly eliminated and replaced by a high‐molecular‐weight species eluting at a substantially lower retention volume. These results confirm that the two proteins spontaneously and efficiently assemble into large 1D arrays upon mixing. Interestingly, a secondary effect was consistently observed in both analyses: signals corresponding to the Dronpa monomer were significantly reduced in the mixture compared with Dronpa‐only controls (Figure 2C,E). Given that Dronpa145N exists in a concentration (proximity) dependent oligomerization equilibrium [13, 22], this observation suggests that incorporation into the 1D assembly increases the local concentration of Dronpa via multivalent anchoring, thereby shifting the equilibrium toward the stabilized tetrameric state.

We also performed multi‐angle light scattering (MALS) to determine the absolute molecular weight of the Dronpa145N‐StreptagII/StrepTactin system. In MALS coupled with SEC (SEC‐MALS) using the same column as in the SEC experiments, the molar masses of individually eluted Dronpa145N‐StreptagII and StrepTactin matched their sequence‐based theoretical masses (Figure S2A,B). This confirms the identity of the SEC peaks as the expected protein species. When the two proteins were mixed, SEC‐MALS resolved a species with a molar mass of 463.5 kDa, corresponding to an assembly of approximately five linked protein components rather than the fully assembled species (Figure S2C), suggesting that the assemblies were disrupted during passage through the SEC column. To circumvent this issue, we conducted flow‐injection MALS without a size‐exclusion column, which revealed a substantially larger assembly with a molar mass of 3.85 MDa (Figure 2F,G). Assuming a 1:1 stoichiometry between Dronpa145N‐StreptagII and StrepTactin, this mass corresponds to 42.5 linked components (21 repeating units), in good agreement with the maximum chain length observed by TEM.

Optical Control of Reversible 1D Protein Self‐Assembly

We next sought to demonstrate that the assembly state of the 1D arrays could be reversibly modulated by light. While TEM images of samples prepared at higher protein concentrations confirmed light‐induced disassembly (Figure S3), dense protein adsorption on the TEM grid obscured the details of individual arrays. To resolve individual array structures, we optimized sample preparation by reducing each protein's concentration to 0.4 µM. We added 10% (w/v) PEG8000 as a molecular crowding agent to maintain Dronpa145N in its assembly‐competent tetrameric state under these dilute conditions. Under these optimized conditions, discrete one‐dimensional arrays were clearly resolved (Figure 3A, top panel), with lengths typically remaining below 100 nm, which is shorter than those formed at higher protein concentrations. Using this platform, we next tested photo‐reversibility. Irradiation with 505 nm (cyan) LED light for 30 min caused complete disassembly into dispersed species (Figure 3A, middle panel). As designed, subsequent exposure to 405 nm (violet) LED light for 3 min induced rapid reassembly, restoring the chain‐like arrays (Figure 3A, bottom panel). These results provide direct visual evidence that the assembly and disassembly of our protein arrays can be reversibly toggled by specific wavelengths of light.

Top: Initial state; Middle: Disassembled state (505 nm); Bottom: Reassembled state (405 nm). Scale bars, 20 nm. (B) Native PAGE analysis demonstrating reversible assembly and disassembly upon alternating light irradiation. (C) Native PAGE control experiment showing that monomeric GFP‐StreptagII does not bind to StrepTactin. (D) Dynamic light scattering (DLS) profiles confirming bulk‐phase reversibility of the assembly process. (E) Average hydrodynamic diameters extracted from the DLS data shown in (D).

This reversible switching was further verified by native PAGE analysis (Figure 3B). In the initial non‐irradiated state, a high‐molecular‐weight band was retained in the loading well, characteristic of the assembled 1D array. Upon illumination at 505 nm, this assembly band disappeared, concomitant with the emergence of a prominent band corresponding to the Dronpa monomer. This process was fully reversible; subsequent illumination at 405 nm resulted in the diminution of the monomer band and the recovery of the array band in the loading well. Additionally, the appearance of a distinct Dronpa monomer band upon disassembly provides direct mechanistic insight, revealing that photo‐induced dissociation leads to the release of Dronpa monomers from StrepTactin. This observation supports our proposed disassembly model (Figure 1B), which posits that the monomeric StreptagII‐fused protein exhibits negligible affinity for StrepTactin. This model is substantiated by a control experiment showing that monomeric GFP‐StreptagII fails to bind to StrepTactin (Figure 3C). Given its light‐driven nature, we next asked whether the system could be repeatedly cycled without substantial loss of assembly efficiency. Native PAGE analysis over 12 successive assembly/disassembly cycles showed that the assembled‐state band was stably maintained, retaining >60% of its third‐cycle fluorescence intensity by the 12th cycle (Figure S4). This decline is likely attributable, at least in part, to the reported cumulative photobleaching of Dronpa145N under repeated illumination [13]. These results indicate that the 1D assembly preserves a considerable degree of photoreversibility following repeated modulation.

Lastly, dynamic light scattering (DLS) was employed to obtain quantitative, bulk‐level confirmation of the assembly process. The initial non‐irradiated mixture exhibited particles with an average hydrodynamic diameter of 16.24 nm (Figure 3D,E). Because DLS estimates hydrodynamic size based on a spherical model, this value does not reflect the full physical length of linear arrays. Upon illumination with 505 nm light for 30 min, the average particle size decreased to 5.38 nm. Although the DLS resolution precludes the separation of individual component peaks, this value is consistent with a mixed population of disassembled species. As an independent control, we monomerized Dronpa145N‐StreptagII alone under the same 505 nm irradiation condition, which yielded a smaller average diameter of 4.27 ± 1.43 nm (Figure S5). Given the theoretical 4:1 molar ratio of released Dronpa monomers to StrepTactin, the dominance of the smaller monomeric species accounts for the pronounced reduction in the average particle size, consistent with effective dissociation. Subsequent irradiation with 405 nm light for 3 min restored the assembled state, resulting in a recovered average size of 15.55 nm, nearly identical to the initial value. Collectively, these DLS data corroborate the TEM and native PAGE results, demonstrating robust, light‐mediated, and reversible control over the assembly state of the protein arrays. In contrast to most light‐responsive protein systems that rely on passive dark‐state relaxation, our approach offers prompt, bidirectional optical reversibility.

Light‐Controlled 1D Self‐Assembly Enables Optical Induction of Biomolecular Condensation

We hypothesized that these light‐controlled 1D arrays could serve as a platform for modulating protein multivalency and thereby controlling LLPS. Specifically, fusing phase‐separating domains to StrepTactin enables the formation of 1D arrays that increase effective valency, thereby driving phase separation. To test this hypothesis, StrepTactin was fused to two well‐established LLPS‐inducing proteins that represent distinct physical interaction motifs (Figure 4A). The PRM‐SH3 system was chosen as a model for structured domain‐ligand interactions [23, 24], whereas the intrinsically disordered RGG domain of LAF (expressed as a SUMO fusion for enhanced solubility) was used to represent phase separation driven by disordered regions [25, 26, 27]. As predicted, mixing either StrepTactin‐PRM‐SH3 or StrepTactin‐SUMO‐LAF with Dronpa145N‐StreptagII in its tetrameric state resulted in protein condensation (Figure 4B and Figure S6). This behavior was confirmed by the appearance of spherical droplets in confocal microscopy, together with quantitative turbidity measurements (Figure 4B,C). When assessed by fluorescence recovery after photobleaching (FRAP), the two systems exhibited markedly different diffusivities. After 30 min, the LAF condensates recovered nearly 80% of their fluorescence, whereas the PRM‐SH3 condensates showed only ∼20% recovery, indicating distinct material properties (Figure S7). Phase separation further exhibited a dependence on stoichiometry. Turbidity measurements revealed that LLPS was maximal when the two proteins were present at or near an equimolar ratio. This observation underscores that the alternating incorporation of the two components, which generates extended, high valency 1D arrays, is critical for inducing LLPS (Figure 4C). Conversely, when one component was present in significant excess (e.g., >2:1 ratio), array growth was inhibited by chain termination, failing to reach the required valency threshold and consequently suppressing droplet formation (Figure S6). Using separately expressed StrepTactin‐PRM and GST‐SH3 constructs, bio‐layer interferometry via anti‐GST biosensors further confirmed that 1D array formation with Dronpa145N‐StreptagII increases the avidity of the PRM‐SH3 interaction (Figure S8), consistent with the proposed increase in multivalency that underlies array‐driven LLPS.

(B) Phase diagrams and representative confocal Dronpa/DIC images of liquid droplets for PRM‐SH3 (left) and LAF (right) systems. Scale bars, 50 µm. (C) Turbidity measurements at 350 nm. The absorption increase represents the change in turbidity upon the addition of Dronpa145N‐StreptagII to solutions containing fixed concentrations of StrepTactin‐fused proteins, showing concentration‐dependent condensate formation for PRM‐SH3 (left) and LAF (right) systems.

Next, we demonstrated the optical induction of phase separation. We first pre‐irradiated Dronpa145N‐StreptagII with 505 nm light to dissociate it into its monomeric form and then prepared an LLPS‐suppressed sample by mixing it with StrepTactin‐PRM‐SH3. Under these conditions, simple mixing of the two proteins resulted in little to no condensate formation, indicating effective suppression of phase separation (Figure 5A). This effective suppression was independently confirmed by turbidity measurements (Figure S9). In contrast, when the photoswitchable Dronpa145N was replaced with non‐photoswitchable DsRed2, irradiation at 505 nm failed to suppress condensate formation (Figure S10A), indicating that optical suppression requires the photo‐responsive dissociation of Dronpa. Subsequent irradiation of the suppressed sample with 405 nm light for 3 min rapidly triggered LLPS (Figure 5A), yielding condensate formation rates comparable to those of the positive control (Figure S10B). This result demonstrates that the system affords precise temporal control over the initiation of phase separation.

Intriguingly, the system exhibited pronounced resistance to dissolution: once condensates were formed, subsequent illumination at 505 nm failed to dissolve them. This apparent “phase‐locked” behavior can be attributed to the exceptionally high Dronpa concentration within the dense phase, which we experimentally determined to exceed 1 mM (Figure S11A), far surpassing the 7.7 µM concentration where monomeric and tetrameric Dronpa145N exist in equal proportions, based on a previously reported association coefficients [13]. At such extreme concentrations, the proximity of Dronpa proteins is likely to stabilize the assembly‐competent tetrameric state, overriding the light‐driven dissociation. Consistent with this notion, sedimented condensates also remained intact following a four‐fold dilution of the dilute phase (Figure S11B). This resistance to dissolution may also reflect a property of biomolecular condensates, whereby the dense, viscoelastic environment of the condensed phase imposes kinetic barriers to component rearrangement, such that the system does not necessarily re‐equilibrate immediately upon an environmental perturbation [28, 29]. This behavior underscores the distinct thermodynamic environments of dilute solutions and highly concentrated condensates.

The StrepTactin‐SUMO‐LAF system exhibited similar optical inducibility (Figure S12). However, owing to the LAF domain's intrinsic propensity for self‐association, minor background phase separation was observed even under suppression conditions (505 nm illumination), particularly at high LAF concentrations. Nevertheless, at appropriate protein concentrations, continuous illumination with a 505 nm LED remained effective at suppressing phase separation in both systems.

Finally, we examined the spatial control of LLPS using localized optical activation. In bulk solution, localized photoactivation successfully initiated condensate nucleation within the irradiated region; however, the resulting drop lets rapidly diffused into non‐irradiated areas, limiting sustained spatial confinement (Figure S13). To overcome this limitation, LLPS was induced within a polyacrylamide gel matrix, which restricts macromolecular diffusion. Under these conditions, photo‐induced condensate formation remained spatially confined to the irradiated region and its immediate vicinity (Figure 5B and Figure S14A). Quantification of droplet density as a function of distance from the activation area confirmed a decline beyond the activation boundary, with a slight extension into the non‐irradiated region (Figure 5C). This extension likely reflects residual mobility of protein components within the porous gel network or laser scattering. In support of a diffusion‐based origin, we found that activated Dronpa145N‐StreptagII progressively diffused beyond the irradiated area over time when StrepTactin‐PRM‐SH3 was omitted (Figure S14B). A control experiment using 640 nm illumination, which does not activate Dronpa145N, showed no condensate formation, ruling out photothermal effects (Figure 5D). These results demonstrate that combining optical triggering with a diffusion‐restricting environment enables spatially resolved induction of in vitro LLPS, providing a valuable platform for studying localized phase separation and condensate dynamics.

Conclusion

In summary, we report a photoreversible 1D protein array composed solely of proteinaceous components. By coupling the light‐switchable protein Dronpa145N to StrepTactin through symmetry matching, we establish a fully genetically encoded system for dynamic, reversible self‐assembly that overcomes the constraints of irreversible or chemically cross‐linked models. A key application demonstrated here is the precise spatiotemporal control of biomolecular condensates, enabling optical initiation and suppression of LLPS as well as on‐demand recruitment of client proteins (Figure S15). Mechanistically, the light‐induced array is proposed to drive phase separation by increasing the effective valency of scaffold domains, providing a pre‐organized multivalent platform that could lower the nucleation barrier for condensate formation [15, 30, 31]. This functional versatility allows the system to serve as a controllable in vitro artificial condensate or a cell‐free microreactor, in which localized biochemical processes can be orchestrated with high precision, free from the confounding complexity of the intracellular milieu. Consequently, this modular platform allows systematic modulation of environmental variables, including pH, temperature, and ionic strength, to quantitatively assess their effects on condensate formation kinetics and material properties. Such capabilities are essential for bridging the gap between theoretical models and complex cellular behaviors, ultimately advancing our understanding of phase separation‐associated pathologies [32, 33, 34]. Looking forward, the symmetry‐matching strategy presented here offers substantial potential for further expansion. Integration of photoswitchable proteins with partners exhibiting higher‐order symmetries (e.g., D4) may enable the construction of stimuli‐responsive two‐dimensional lattices or three‐dimensional polyhedral protein architectures.

Experimental Section

Plasmid Construction

DNA sequences encoding the proteins used in this study, except for that of StrepTactin, were synthesized by Bioneer (Daejeon, Republic of Korea). The StrepTactin gene was engineered from a wild‐type Streptavidin template through site‐directed mutagenesis. All target sequences were amplified via PCR using specific oligonucleotide primers and subsequently ligated into the pET‐21a(+) expression vector (Novagen). Detailed amino acid sequences for all constructs are provided in Table S1.

Protein Expression and Purification of StrepTactin Variants

Plasmids encoding StrepTactin or its LLPS‐prone fusions (StrepTactin‐PRM‐SH3 and StrepTactin‐SUMO‐LAF) were transformed into E. coli BL21(DE3) cells, which were subsequently cultured in LB medium supplemented with ampicillin at 37°C. Upon reaching an OD600 of 0.8–1.0, protein expression was induced with 1 mM isopropyl β‐D‐1‐thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG), and cultures were incubated for 5–6 h at 37°C. The cells were harvested by centrifugation at 6,000 rpm (6,400 × g), and the pellet was resuspended in PBS (pH 7.3, 160 mM NaCl) containing 0.1% (v/v) Triton X‐100 (lysis buffer) and lysed by sonication. StrepTactin variants were expressed as inclusion bodies (IBs) after centrifugation at 12,000 rpm (16,000 × g) for 15 min. This IB pellet was washed twice by resuspending in lysis buffer, then centrifuging at 12,000 rpm (16,000 × g, 15 min). The washed IB pellet was solubilized and denatured in 6 M guanidine hydrochloride (GuHCl) supplemented with 50 mM Tris‐HCl (pH 8.0). The solution was incubated overnight at 4°C to ensure complete denaturation. The denatured protein was refolded by rapid dilution: the solution was added dropwise via syringe into a large volume of rapidly stirring PBS. The refolded solution was clarified by centrifugation at 6 000 rpm (6,400 × g) for 30 min to remove aggregated protein. The clarified supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 µm membrane filter (Stericup Quick Release, Millipore). The solution was then adjusted to 250 mM NaCl and 50 mM Tris‐HCl (pH 8.0) and loaded onto a Ni‐IDA affinity column (Takara). The bound protein was washed with wash buffer (50 mM Tris‐HCl, 500 mM NaCl, 50 mM imidazole, pH 8.0) and eluted with elution buffer (50 mM Tris‐HCl, 500 mM NaCl, 500 mM imidazole, pH 8.0). Most eluted proteins were dialyzed against a final storage buffer (PBS) at 4°C. However, StrepTactin‐SUMO‐LAF, which is prone to cold precipitation, was dialyzed against PBS supplemented with 10% (v/v) glycerol at 28°C, flash‐frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80°C. Frozen samples were thawed at 37°C for 30 min before use.

Expression and Purification of Soluble Proteins

Plasmids encoding Dronpa145N‐StreptagII (or related variants like GFP‐StreptagII and DsRed2‐StreptagII) were transformed into E. coli BL21(DE3) cells. Cells were cultured in LB medium containing ampicillin. When the OD600 reached 0.6–0.8, protein expression was induced with 1 mM IPTG, and the cultures were incubated overnight (16–18 h) at 20°C. Cells were harvested by centrifugation, and the cell pellet was resuspended in Ni‐IDA binding buffer (50 mM Tris‐HCl, 500 mM NaCl, pH 8.0 and lysed by sonication. The cell lysate was clarified by centrifugation at 12,000 rpm (16,000 × g) for 15 min, and the supernatant was collected. This supernatant, containing the His‐tagged target protein, was purified using a Ni‐IDA affinity column (Takara) as described in the previous sub‐section. The eluted proteins were dialyzed against PBS.

Protein Concentration

Protein concentrations were determined by measuring absorbance at 280 nm (A280) using a NanoPhotometer N60 (Implen) and their respective calculated extinction coefficients. All protein concentrations in this study are reported as monomer equivalents, based on the avidin fragment monomer of StrepTactin and the monomeric forms of Dronpa and DsRed2.

Size‐Exclusion Chromatography (SEC)

Superdex 200 Increase column (10/300 GL, GE Healthcare) was mounted on a fast protein liquid chromatography (FPLC) system (ÄKTA pure, GE Healthcare). Protein solutions were injected, and SEC was performed at 0.2 mL/min with a filtered PBS buffer. Protein elution was monitored by 280 nm absorption. Data processing was performed using Unicorn 6.3 and Excel.

LED Irradiation

Sample aliquots (10–50 µL) were transferred into 0.2 mL thin‐wall PCR tubes (QSP), or 50–100 µL of samples were transferred into a 96‐well black/clear bottom plate (Thermo Fisher). Samples were illuminated using either a cyan LED (505/37 nm, 220 mW, Thorlabs) or a UV LED (405/20 nm, 665 mW, Thorlabs). Illumination was directed from the side for PCR tubes and from the top for 96‐well plates for the indicated times.

Negative‐Stain Transmission Electron Microscopy

To confirm 1D protein assembly, Dronpa145N‐StreptagII and StrepTactin solutions were mixed to a final concentration of 20 µM each and allowed to assemble for several hours. The resulting mixture was then diluted to 4 µM in PBS. To observe optical control of self‐assembly, samples were prepared by mixing the protein solutions to a final monomer concentration of 10 µM each and incubating for several hours. This mixture was subsequently diluted to 0.4 µM in reaction buffer supplemented with 10% (w/v) PEG8000. Three types of samples were prepared: (i) a dark‐state control (no light exposure), (ii) a sample exposed to a 505 nm LED for 30 min, and (iii) a sample exposed to a 505 nm LED for 30 min followed by a 405 nm LED for 3 min. For imaging, each prepared sample was adsorbed onto a glow‐discharged carbon‐coated grid (200 mesh, Ted Pella Inc.) for 30 s. The grids were then washed with filtered PBS and stained with 0.8% (w/v) uranyl formate. Finally, the grids were imaged using a 300 kV transmission electron microscope (Tecnai G2 F30 S‐Twin, FEI).

Native Polyacrylamide Gel Electrophoresis (PAGE)

Protein solutions were mixed to a final monomer concentration of 20 µM each and incubated for 30 min to allow assembly. For repetitive assembly, protein solutions were sequentially exposed to a 505 nm LED for 30 min and a 405 nm LED for 3 min, as described. The 405 nm LED‐exposed sample was incubated for an additional 30 min to allow the 1D array to reassemble. Protein solutions were diluted to 4 µM each and analyzed by 12% native PAGE. The gels were imaged using a ChemiDoc system (Bio‐Rad).

Size‐Exclusion Chromatography Coupled With Multi‐Angle Light Scattering (SEC‐MALS)

SEC‐MALS was performed at the Korea Basic Science Institute (KBSI) using a Shimadzu HPLC system equipped with UV, light‐scattering, and refractive‐index detectors in series. Protein samples at 20 µM were loaded onto a Superdex 200 Increase column (10/300 GL, GE Healthcare), identical to that used for SEC analysis, equilibrated in 1× PBS, and run at a flow rate of 0.5 mL/min with an injection volume of 100 µL at 25°C. Light scattering and refractive index were measured with a DAWN Heleos‐II (18‐angle) multi‐angle light scattering detector and an Optilab T‐rEX refractive index detector (Wyatt Technology), respectively. Molar mass was determined from the combined light scattering, UV, and refractive index signals using a dn/dc value of 0.185 mL/g in ASTRA 6 software (Wyatt Technology).

Flow Injection Multi‐Angle Light Scattering (FI‐MALS)

The molecular weight of the 1D assembly formed by Dronpa145N‐StreptagII and StrepTactin was determined using FI‐MALS. All buffers and protein solutions were degassed by sonication prior to use. Individual protein components in 1× PBS were filtered through a 0.22 µm syringe filter (Advantec) prior to mixing. A mixture containing 50 µM Dronpa145N‐StreptagII and 50 µM StrepTactin was prepared and injected directly without prior size‐exclusion chromatography, with a PEEK inline filter holder during injection. Each sample (500 µL) was injected at a flow rate of 0.25 mL/min at 25°C into a DAWN Heleos‐II light scattering detector (Wyatt Technology) equipped with a 662 nm laser. Detector normalization was performed using 50 µM StrepTactin. Protein concentration was determined by UV absorbance using extinction coefficients calculated from each protein's amino acid sequence. A dn/dc value of 0.185 mL/g, based on literature values for proteins, was used for molecular weight calculations. The fluorescence background contributed by Dronpa145N at each detector angle was calculated based on a concentration‐dependent fitting to the known tetramer molecular weight of Dronpa145N, and this background was subtracted from the measured light scattering signal. Raw data were processed using ASTRA software (Wyatt Technology), followed by post‐processing and first‐order Zimm fitting in Excel.

Dynamic Light Scattering

Protein solutions were filtered through a 0.22 µm syringe filter (Advantec), diluted to 8 µM concentration, and mixed in equal volumes (final 4 µM). Three mixture samples were prepared: (i) no light exposure, (ii) exposure to a 505 nm LED for 30 min, and (iii) exposure to a 505 nm LED for 30 min followed by a 405 nm LED for 3 min. The 405 nm LED‐exposed sample was incubated for an additional hour. 50 µL of the solutions in disposable solvent‐resistant micro cuvettes (ZEN0040, Malvern Instruments) were analyzed (173° backscattered) with a Zetasizer Nano ZS DLS system (Malvern Instruments) at 25°C. Each measurement consisted of ten runs (10 s per run). Data processing was performed using Zetasizer software.

Protein Phase Separation

StrepTactin‐PRM‐SH3 or StrepTactin‐SUMO‐LAF solutions were mixed with Dronpa145N‐StreptagII at the concentrations indicated. 100 µL of protein solutions were analyzed in 96‐well black/clear bottom plates that were previously passivated with 2 mg/mL bovine serum albumin (BSA; Fraction V, Thermo Fisher, dissolved in PBS) for 2 h at 25°C. Phase separation diagrams were obtained by measuring the turbidity of the protein solution (absorbance at 350 nm) using a spectral scanning multimode reader (VarioSkan Flash, Thermo Fisher) 15 min after mixing. Proteins were considered phase‐separated when the A350 increased by > 0.03 relative to a clear StrepTactin protein solution without Dronpa145N‐StreptagII. Samples were imaged using a confocal laser scanning microscope (A1R HD25 with Eclipse Ti2, Nikon) equipped with a 60×/1.40 numerical aperture (NA) apochromatic oil‐immersion objective lens. A 488 nm laser was used for Dronpa protein imaging, and a 561 nm laser was used for DsRed2, mCherry, and Cy3‐labeled protein imaging. To confirm optical LLPS inhibition and induction, the final monomer concentration of each protein was set to 5 µM, and samples were exposed to LED light for the indicated times. For the combination of StrepTactin‐PRM‐SH3 and DsRed2‐StreptagII, the StrepTactin‐PRM‐SH3 concentration was set to 20 µM to prevent droplet gelation, which can occur at a 1:1 stoichiometric ratio due to excessive multivalency. For the client recruitment assay, client protein (1 µM) was added to the 5 µM condensate system, and droplet partition coefficients were calculated as previously reported [24].

Fluorescence Recovery After Photobleaching (FRAP)

FRAP experiments were performed on the A1R HD25 confocal microscope equipped with a 60×/1.40 NA oil‐immersion objective lens. Condensates were prepared in BSA‐passivated 96‐well black/clear‐bottom plates as described previously. A circular region of interest (ROI) with a 5 µm diameter, positioned near the center of the condensate, was photobleached using a single, high‐intensity pulse of the 488 nm laser for 10 s. Fluorescence recovery within the ROI was immediately monitored by time‐lapse imaging at low laser power (images captured every 10 s). For data analysis, the average fluorescence intensity of the bleached ROI was measured over time. The recovery curves were normalized by defining the intensity of the bleached ROI immediately post‐bleaching (t = 0) as 0% and the average intensity of the entire condensate as 100% (n = 3 condensates measured).

Bio‐Layer Interferometry (BLI)

Binding kinetics were measured using an Octet R2 (Sartorius) in 96‐well black polypropylene plates (Sartorius). Assays were performed in 1× PBS supplemented with 1× Kinetics Buffer (Sartorius) at 30°C with 1000 rpm shaking throughout. Anti‐GST biosensors were conditioned using three cycles of exposure to Regeneration Buffer (10 mM glycine, pH 1.7; Sartorius) followed by neutralization. Biosensors were loaded with GST‐SH3 (3 µM, 103 µg/mL) for 300 s. For comparison (Figure S8), StrepTactin‐PRM was used alone or mixed with an equimolar concentration of Dronpa145N‐StreptagII to form a 1D assembly. For assembly samples, an additional blocking step with biocytin (15 µg/mL) was performed to minimize nonspecific interactions between the StreptagII moiety and the biosensor surface; this blocking step was omitted for the StrepTactin‐PRM‐alone samples, for which the nonspecific signal was confirmed to be below 3% using a 10 µM StrepTactin control. For assembly samples, residual nonspecific binding (<10% of the total signal) was removed by reference subtraction using a control sample in which StrepTactin‐PRM was replaced with an equimolar concentration of StrepTactin lacking the PRM. Data were processed using Octet Analysis Studio software (Sartorius). Steady‐state response values (Req, average response over the last 1 min of the association step) were plotted against analyte concentration and fitted to the binding equation: Req = (Rmax × [Concentration]) / (KD + [Concentration]) to obtain the apparent equilibrium dissociation constant (KD). Detailed BLI steps and durations are provided in Table S2.

Spatial Control of Phase Separation

Spatial control of protein phase separation was characterized using two confocal laser scanning microscopes: a ZEISS LSM880 (KAIST Analysis Center for Research Advancement, KARA) and a Nikon A1R HD25 with Eclipse Ti2. For bulk solution assays, samples containing 10 µM StrepTactin‐PRM‐SH3 and 5 µM Dronpa145N‐StreptagII (pre‐monomerized via 505 nm LED irradiation) in 1% (w/v) Dextran T500 (Pharmacosmos) were imaged on the LSM880 using a 10× objective lens. For gel‐based experiments, 8 µM StrepTactin‐PRM‐SH3 and 5 µM pre‐monomerized Dronpa145N‐StreptagII were embedded along with 1% Dextran within a 6% polyacrylamide gel (29:1 acrylamide:bis‐acrylamide) prepared between glass slides separated by double‐sided tape (0.2 mm thick, Scotch tissue tape, 3 M). The gel‐based experiment shown in Figure 5B was performed on the LSM880, while the quantitative spatial confinement analysis (Figure 5C and Figure S14) was performed on the A1R HD25. On the LSM880, localized photoactivation was performed with a 405 nm laser for 1.5 min over a 249 × 249 µm2 region (300 × 300 pixels) using a 10× objective, and high‐resolution magnified images of the resulting condensates were acquired using a 63× oil‐immersion objective lens. On the A1R HD25, localized photoactivation was performed with a 405 nm laser for 10 s over a 372 × 372 µm2 region (300 × 300 pixels) using a 10× objective lens, and magnified images were acquired using a 60× oil‐immersion objective lens. For droplet quantification, images were processed in ImageJ by applying a uniform threshold and using the particle analysis function to count droplets with an area ≥ 3 pixels (0.03 µm2).
