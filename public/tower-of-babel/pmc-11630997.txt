Exploring the relationship between gut microbiota, immune characteristics, and female genital tract polyps using genetic evidence
Authors: Ran Jia, Yuhang Fu, Miaomiao Xue, Cheng Zhou, Jie Jin
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The study investigates the causal relationship between gut microbes and female genital tract polyps, exploring the potential mediating role of immune cells via Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis. Our MR study was designed following the STROBE-MR guidelines. We combined data from a large-scale GWAS meta-analysis, including 731 immune profiles and female genital tract polyps, with gut microbiology data sourced from the MiBioGen consortium. Univariate Mendelian randomization was employed to identify gut microbes and immune profiles significantly associated with female genital tract polyps causally. A 2-step MR analysis was utilized to investigate the potential mediating role of immune cells. Furthermore, we utilized the multivariable MR approach based on Bayesian model averaging (MR-BMA) to further assess the prioritization of gut microbiota vs immune characteristics in the development of female genital tract polyps. Through univariate MR analysis, we identified a significant causal link between 12 gut microbiota, 31 immune features, and female genital tract polyps. Four causal pathways involving gut microbiota, immune cells, and polyps were identified among them. MR-BMA analysis indicated marginal inclusion probability (MIP) values exceeding 0.1 for 5 gut microbiota groups: Victivallaceae (model-averaged causal estimate [MACE] = 0.060, MIP = 0.581, P = .0089), Ruminococcus gautreuii (MACE = 0.052, MIP = 0.346, P = .0640), Lachnoclostrium (MACE = 0.0380, MIP = 0.225, P = .1875), Alphaprobacter (MACE = 0.0186, MIP = 0.140, P = .3934), and Fusicatenibacter (MACE = 0.013, MIP = 0.110, P = .5818). Six immune features exhibit high priority, with MIP values exceeding 0.5, including HLA DR on CD33+ HLA DR+ CD14dim (MACE = −0.015, MIP = 0.753, P = .0853), HVEM on naive CD4+ T cell (MACE = 0.024, MIP = 0.737, P = .0053), CD80 on CD62L+ plastic cytoplasmic dendritic cell (MACE = 0.024, MIP = 0.721, P = .0228), CD28 on activated and secret CD4 regulatory T cell (MACE = 0.0054, MIP = 0.706, P = .3245), HLA DR on CD14+ CD16 monocyte (MACE = −0.0003, MIP = 0.520, P = .7927), HLA DR on CD14+ monocyte (MACE = −0.0029, MIP = 0.509, P = .5576). Our research indicates that gut microbiota exerts an independent causal influence on female genital tract polyps, potentially impacting them via various immune cells.

1. Introduction

Female genital tract polyps are abnormal growths resulting from local tissue proliferation, encompassing endometrial polyps, cervical polyps, vaginal, and external genital polyps. Endometrial polyps are the most prevalent, followed by cervical polyps, with vaginal polyps having the lowest occurrence rate.[1] Due to advancements in medical technology, the diagnostic rate is continuously increasing. The majority of female genital tract polyps present no overt clinical symptoms. In cases where symptoms manifest, infertility and abnormal uterine bleeding are the most prevalent complaints.[2] The etiology of this condition remains unclear and may be associated with factors like age, hormone levels, immune dysregulation, and metabolism. Importantly, gut microbiota serves as one of the risk factors for this condition.[3,4]

Microorganisms establish intricate symbiotic relationships with human hosts, crucial for maintaining overall health. Microorganisms are ubiquitous in various microecological niches within the human body, spanning the gut, oral cavity, skin, genital tract (e.g., the vagina), and other regions.[5] The gut is recognized as both an endocrine organ and the largest reservoir of microbes in the human body. The internal microbiota of the gut influences the physiological and pathological conditions of the human body through engagement in critical processes like metabolism and immune regulation.[6] The gut houses a plethora of microorganisms alongside a rich population of immune cells that engage in intricate signaling pathways to uphold intestinal equilibrium.[7] Studies indicate that gut microbiota can modulate the immune system by influencing neutrophil migration and T cell differentiation.[8] Furthermore, diverse molecules generated by gut microbiota can impact immune system equilibrium. For instance, short-chain fatty acids regulate immune cell activity, bolster intestinal barrier function, and exhibit anti-inflammatory properties. Conversely, trimethylamine-N-oxide, lipopolysaccharides (LPS), cytotoxins, among others, might trigger or worsen inflammatory reactions.[9] Hence, gut microbiota could potentially influence the development of female genital tract polyps through immune system modulation.

The gut microbiota composition in female patients with genital tract polyps markedly differs from that of healthy individuals, offering a novel approach to discerning this condition.[10] Building on this, another study delved into the atypical relationship between endometrial polyps and the immune system. The research revealed a notable upward trend in monocyte activation levels among endometrial polyp patients, coupled with heightened secretion of inflammatory cytokines.[11] Notably, intestinal microbial products like LPS can additionally trigger the release of inflammatory cytokines. Consequently, our focus will center on investigating the possible causal link between gut microbiota and female genital tract polyps, as well as delving into the potential mediating role of the immune system in this context.

In clinical settings, the majority of polyps are benign; however, there exists a risk of malignant progression. Presently, no preventive measures are in place. Hysteroscopic resection is deemed the “gold standard” for managing female reproductive tract polyps, although the recurrence rate remains uncertain and not universally embraced by patients.[12] Consequently, the quest for biomarkers linked to female reproductive tract polyps can offer crucial support in preventing, screening, treating, and formulating preventive strategies against this condition.

Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis employs genetic variation as an instrumental variable (IV) to examine potential causal links between exposure and outcomes. Since genetic variations are determined at conception and randomly allocated, MR methods can reduce potential biases like confounding factors, reverse causality, and measurement errors.[13] Furthermore, mediation analysis can assess how exposure influences outcomes via intermediary variables.

The study employed a bidirectional and mediating Mendelian randomization design with 2 samples to assess the causal link between gut microbiota and female reproductive tract polyps, as well as investigate the potential mediating function of immune characteristics. Furthermore, MR approach based on Bayesian model averaging (MR-BMA) was employed for multivariate Mendelian randomization analysis to identify gut microbiota species or immune traits crucial to the interplay between gut microbiota and female genital tract polyps.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Research design

This study was designed according to STROBE-MR guidelines to ensure the integrity of this Mendelian randomization study.[14] This study contains 3 main parts: assessing the causal link between gut microbiota and reproductive tract polyps, investigating the causal association between immune cells and reproductive tract polyps, exploring the mediating function of immunity in the influence of gut microbiota on reproductive tract polyps, and utilizing MR-BMA to prioritize the significance of gut microbiota and immune cells. Single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) that meet the criteria are defined as IV. This study rigorously conforms to the fundamental tenets of Mendelian randomization: the IV is strongly linked to exposure; the IV is independent of confounding factors; the IV impacts the outcome solely through exposure (refer to Fig. 1 for an overview of the research).

Overview of the study. (A) Provides detailed data on exposure, mediation, and outcomes. (B) In Step 1, path 1A illustrates the causal relationship between gut microbiota and female genital tract polyp, while path 1B shows the reverse causal link. In Step 2, path 2A signifies the causal impact of immune features on genital tract polyps, and path 2B shows the opposite causal relationship. Step 3 involves analyzing how immune characteristics mediate the pathway from gut microbiota to genital tract polyps: path a shows how gut microbiota affect immune characteristics; path b represents the overall impact of gut microbiota on genital tract polyps; path c details the influence of immune features on genital tract polyps. (C) Univariate Mendelian randomization analysis comprises 3 elements: exposure to gut microbes and genital tract polyps as outcome; exposure to immune traits and genital tract polyps as outcome; exposure to gut microbiota and immune traits as outcome. Genetic instrumental variables are solely linked to exposure factors and do not directly or indirectly influence outcomes through confounding factors.

2.2. Data sources

The genetic data of gut microbiota comes from the latest GWAS summary data MiBioGen consortium (https://mibiogen.gcc.rug.nl). It includes 24 queues with a total of 18,340 participants and 211 taxa, including 131 genera, 35 families, 20 orders, 16 classes, and 9 phyla. The GWAS data for genital polyps (14,324 cases and 107,564 controls) were sourced from the FinnGen database (https://www.finngen.fi/fi). The data for 731 immune features came from GWAS of a cohort of 3757 Sardinians.[15] A sum of 731 immune features can be divided into 7 types of immune cells, including B cells (n = 190), dendritic cells (n = 64), maturation stages of T cells (n = 79), monocytes (n = 43), myeloid cells (n = 64), TBNK (n = 124), and Treg (n = 167).

2.3. Instrumental variables selection

Independent SNPs are crucial for the study’s completion. We selected SNPs highly correlated with gut microbiota (P < 1 × 10−5), aligning with prior findings.[16] To mitigate biased outcomes, SNPs with linkage disequilibrium (k > 500 kb and r2 > 0.1) were excluded. Additionally, we computed the F statistic for each SNP to mitigate bias. If F statistic below 10 may indicate a weak instrument bias. The F statistics for SNPs associated with gut microbiota ranged from 14.59 to 88.43 (Document S1, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/O125), while those for immune traits ranged from 19.55 to 2381.77 (Document S2, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/O125), exhibit strong correlations.[17] To ensure SNP effects on exposure align with those on outcomes, palindromic SNPs were excluded.[18] Lastly, a thorough literature search revealed no sample overlap between exposure and outcome GWAS datasets.

2.4. Univariate Mendelian randomization

We used the TwoSampleMR[19] package, MRPRESSO package, and MendelianRandomization package from R studio (version 4.3.2) for 2 sample Mendelian randomization analysis to evaluate the causal effects of gut microbiota and immune characteristics on reproductive tract polyps. If the number of SNPs is 1 during the process, use Wald ratio test to estimate the association between exposure and genital polyps. If the number of SNPs is >1, the 5 popular MR methods of inverse variance weighted (IVW) test,[20] weighted mode,[21] MR Egger regression,[22] and weighted median estimator[23] are used for analysis. IVW is the most robust analysis method for MR, and we use it as the main analysis method, with the other 4 as auxiliary methods. Meanwhile, we used Cochran’s Q test[24] to evaluate heterogeneity among instrumental variables, and MR Egger intercept[22] and MR-PRESSO method[25] to detect level pleiotropy.

3. Results

3.1. Univariate MR analysis and MR-BMA analysis of gut microbiota on female genital tract polyps

According to the IVW method, we detected a notable causal link between 12 types of gut microbiota and female genital tract polyps, all of which act as risk factors. These include 2 classes, 1 order, 4 families, and 5 genera. Ruminococcusgauvreauii (OR = 1.20, 95%CI: 1.06–1.36, P = .0038), Bifidobacterium (OR = 1.19, 95%CI: 1.04–1.36, P = .013), Fusicatenibacter (OR = 1.16, 95%CI: 1.03–1.30, P = .016), Actinobacteria (OR = 1.17, 95%CI: 1.03–1.33, P = .017), Bifidobacteriaceae (OR = 1.17, 95%CI: 1.02–1.33, P = .024), ClostridialesvadinBB60 (OR = 1.13, 95%CI: 1.02–1.25, P = .024), unknowngenus (OR = 1.02, 95%CI: 1.02–1.25, P = .024), unknownfamily (OR = 1.08, 95%CI: 1.01–1.16, P = .026), Bifidobacteriales (OR = 1.18, 95%CI: 1.02–1.36, P = .028), Lachnoclostridium (OR = 1.16, 95%CI: 1.01–1.33, P = .031), Alphaproteobacteria (OR = 1.16, 95%CI: 1.01–1.34, P = .036), Victivallaceae (OR = 1.08, 95%CI: 1.00–1.17, P = .041). The mr_steiger algorithm consistently indicates the directionality in Mendelian randomization analysis. Upon further analysis, we observed that Actinobacteria, Bifidobacteria, and Bifidobacteria demonstrate heterogeneity, while Bifidobacteria and Bifidobacteria display horizontal pleiotropy, potentially impacting the reliability of the causal relationship outcomes. Following adjustment for the false discovery rate, no significant microbial community was evident (Fig. 2).

3.2. Univariate MR analysis and MR-BMA analysis of immune traits on female genital tract polyps

According to the IVW method, we identified 31 significant causal relationships between immune features and genital tract polyps. These relationships encompass 8 types of B cells, 4 types of cDCs, 4 stages of T cell maturation, 2 types of monocytes, 3 types of myeloid cells, 7 types of TBNK cells, and 3 types of Tregs. The mr_steiger algorithm did not reveal any significant bidirectionality in the Mendelian randomization analysis. Out of these 31 immune features, 5 were excluded due to heterogeneity or horizontal pleiotropy. Further application of the false discovery rate method for correction did not yield significant immune features postscreening (Fig. 3).

4. Discussion

To our knowledge, we are the first to employ MR to explore the causal link between gut microbiota and female genital tract polyps, highlighting the intermediary function of immune characteristics. Our research pinpointed 12 gut microbiota and 31 immune features exhibiting a notable causal association with female genital tract polyps. Following assessments for heterogeneity and horizontal pleiotropy, 8 gut microbiota and 26 immune features were selected. Moreover, we performed MR-BMA analysis and prioritized the significance of these factors. Subsequent calculations revealed 4 causal pathways aligning with the impact of gut microbiota on polyps through the mediation. These pathways involve Alphasproteobacteria affecting CD33+ HLA DR+ CD14dim with a mediation effect of 2.07%; Clostridiales vadin BB60 influencing CD8 on natural killer T with a mediation effect of 0.99%; unknown genus (id.1000000073) mediating CD8 on natural killer T with a mediation effect of 0.99%; and Victivallaceae impacting CD62L− HLA DR++ monocyte absolute count with a mediation effect of 1.26%. Our MR analysis on the connection among gut microbiota, immune traits, and female genital tract polyps offers fresh insights into polyp pathogenesis, underscoring the impact of gut microbiota and immune status in polyp development. This finding aids in preventive measures, early detection, and the formulation of effective strategies to mitigate the risk of genital tract polyps.

Alphasproteobacteria, a gram-negative bacterium in the phylum Proteobacteria, encompasses a diverse range of species and possesses various trigger ligands like LPS and peptidoglycans.[26] These ligands are crucial for preserving intestinal homeostasis and host health.[27] Numerous studies have demonstrated the close association of Alphasproteobacteria with the body’s immune response and its ability to activate distinct signaling pathways, including MyD88-dependent or MyD88-independent pathways, via plasma membrane receptors like Toll-like receptor 4. Subsequent to pathway activation, a cascade of reactions is initiated, resulting in the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines like IL-1β, TNF-α, and IL-6, influencing the immune elements of host cells.[28] These discoveries underscore the dual function of Alphasproteobacteria in preserving intestinal well-being and modulating the body’s immune system.

Walsh et al[29] highlighted that the activated MyD88-independent signaling pathway plays a crucial role in the intestinal microenvironment, enhancing the turnover of intestinal epithelial cells through the upregulation of cell proliferation signals and the inhibition of cell apoptosis pathways. This process creates conducive conditions for the development of intestinal polyps. Conversely, in studies concerning Parkinson’s disease, evidence indicates that exposure of midbrain neuronal cells to Alphasproteobacteria elicits an immune response.[30] This cascade results in heightened release of pro-inflammatory cytokines, activation of the innate immune system, subsequent entry of alpha-synuclein into mitochondria, prompting their dysfunction, and ultimately fostering the development of Parkinson’s disease via mechanisms like oxidative stress and energy metabolism.[31] To the best of our knowledge, there is currently no research examining the association between Alphasproteobacteria and genital tract polyps. Our findings suggest a causal link between Alphasproteobacteria and genital tract polyps, representing a risk factor for their development. One potential mechanism involves elevating the risk of genital tract polyps by suppressing the protective factor HLA-DR on CD33+ HLA-DR+ CD14dim cells.

Clostridiales vadin BB60 is classified under the order Clostridium in the phylum Firmicutes. Clostridia encompass a diverse array of bacterial species with varied physiological functions, including the production of short-chain fatty acids like butyrate and propionate. These metabolites play a crucial role in preserving intestinal ecological balance, enhancing nutrient absorption, and contributing to body metabolism, immune response, and other physiological processes.[32] Multiple studies have demonstrated that the majority of clostridia are benign to humans and can even be beneficial in specific scenarios.[33,34] A study with 285 colorectal cancer patients and 290 healthy controls uncovered a correlation between clostridia levels and colorectal cancer. Studies have identified a significant decrease in clostridia levels among colorectal cancer patients, with the extent of reduction positively linked to disease advancement, indicating a potential pivotal role of clostridia in colorectal cancer. Subsequent mouse experiments corroborated this stance, with the experimental group receiving a blend of 4 clostridia types displaying a notable decrease in colorectal tumor count in contrast to the control group. Particularly noteworthy is the elimination of the positive impacts of a clostridial mix in mice deficient in mature T and B cells, suggesting that particular clostridia strains could potentially induce anti-tumor effects through immune cell infiltration in tumors.[35] Nevertheless, it is important to note that not all clostridia exert positive effects on the human body. For instance, Clostridioides difficile, present in mammalian intestinal tracts and belonging to the order Clostridium, can lead to severe infections in the body. In cases of gut microbiota imbalance, Clostridioides difficile undergoes rapid proliferation, generating toxins that result in a range of clinical manifestations including mild diarrhea, pseudomembranous colitis, toxic megacolon, and potentially fatal outcomes.[36]

Currently, our knowledge about Clostridiales vadin BB60 remains limited, with only a few studies investigating its implications for human health. One study, employing MR analysis, suggested that Clostridiales vadin BB60 could be a risk factor for hepatitis B cirrhosis.[37] Conversely, another MR analysis focused on the relationship between gut microbiota and hypertension demonstrated a negative association between Clostridiales vadin BB60 and hypertension, indicating a potential protective role against hypertension.[38] These contrasting findings regarding human health may stem from the intricate functionality of gut microbiota and its diverse effects across different disease contexts. In our research, we discovered that Clostridiales vadin BB60 acts as a risk factor for genital tract polyps. Specifically, it may heighten the susceptibility to genital tract polyps by modulating the risk factor CD8 on natural killer T cells. A study investigating the association between alterations in intestinal microbial composition and HIV-1-infected men who have sex with men indicates that among patients progressing to AIDS within 5 to 10 years post HIV-1 infection, Vivallaceae levels are notably elevated compared to those uninfected for an extended period without antiretroviral therapy.[39] Conversely, Zheng et al[40] demonstrated using MR analysis that Victivallaceae could elevate the susceptibility to allergic rhinitis. Nevertheless, a different study utilizing MR analysis indicates that Victivillaceae might confer a protective influence against diabetic nephropathy.[41] These apparently conflicting research outcomes reassert the intricate and diverse functionality of gut microbiota, susceptible to host physiological conditions, disease types, environmental variables, and exhibiting varied roles across distinct scenarios. Our research uncovers the potential adverse effect of Victivallaceae on female genital tract polyps: by suppressing the protective factor CD62L HLA DR++ monocyte absolute count, it heightens the risk of developing genital tract polyps.

CD62L, also known as L-selectin, acts as a homing receptor present on naive T cells’ surface, pivotal in transitioning naive T cells activated by antigens into mature T cells. During activation, CD62L expression diminishes on T cell surfaces, distinguishing between CD62L- memory T cells and CD62L+ naive T cells. CD62L− cells lack CD62L expression almost entirely, enabling swift responses and immune activation upon antigen re-encounter. Research suggests that CD62L− T cells can effectively infiltrate the tumor microenvironment, utilizing specific migration patterns and bolstered immune responses, potentially aiding in tumor cell elimination.[42] Additionally, Ebbens et al[43] highlighted an increased expression of L-selectin (or its ligands) in simple nasal polyps and cystic fibrosis nasal polyps, indicating a potential role in facilitating leukocyte migration into nasal polyp lesions. This sheds light on the potential involvement of CD62L− in nasal polyp formation and inflammation regulation.

HLA-DR, a type 2 human leukocyte antigen molecule, is crucial for antigen presentation. Studies demonstrate that co-culturing nasal polyp epithelial cells with lymphocytes leads to a significant reduction in HLA-DR expression on lymphocytes.[44] These findings indicate the pivotal role of HLA-DR as an immune regulatory factor in nasal polyp development, emphasizing the necessity for additional research to elucidate the specific molecular mechanisms and interactions.

This study offers several strengths. Firstly, we rigorously adhered to the 3 core assumptions of Mendelian randomization research throughout the analysis, employed various common sensitivity analysis techniques, and mitigated the influence of confounders and reverse causality to ensure the robustness of our findings. Secondly, our data stem from the most extensive genetic study conducted to date, enhancing the statistical power of our results and conclusions, thereby increasing their reliability. Moreover, our research findings are innovative, revealing that the gut microbiota impacts the onset and progression of female reproductive tract polyps through multiple immune pathways. While the immune aspect’s contribution may be modest, it opens avenues for investigating the targeted regulation of female reproductive tract polyps via specific gut microbiota. This breakthrough has the potential to advance disease prevention, screening, and the development of efficacious interventions and treatments.

Despite the numerous strengths of this study, several limitations exist. Firstly, due to the restricted genetic and metabolic data, we couldn’t stratify female genital tract polyps into various subgroups for detailed scrutiny. The female genital tract polyps originated from FinnGen databases, with efforts made to explore additional databases for subgroup information, albeit without success. Nonetheless, we acquired a substantial number of targeted samples that lent robust support for our conclusions. Secondly, the study participants exclusively represented European populations, necessitating caution when extrapolating the findings to other regions and demographics. Moreover, this study solely relies on data analysis and lacks validation through clinical trials or animal experiments, warranting future investigations. Nonetheless, leveraging the most extensive dataset available, encompassing a significant participant pool, and executing comprehensive sensitivity analyses ensured the results’ reliability and consistency. Lastly, beyond the 731 immune features studied, there might exist additional unexplored immune attributes that could play a role in the causal association between gut microbiota and female reproductive tract polyps.

5. Conclusion

Our study indicates that the gut microbiota exerts an autonomous causal impact on female genital tract polyps, influencing them via diverse immune cells. The identified biomarkers offer fresh perspectives into the underlying mechanisms of the condition, facilitating advancements in prevention, screening, diagnosis, treatment, and beyond.
