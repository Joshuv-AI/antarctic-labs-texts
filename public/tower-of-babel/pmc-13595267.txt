Obesity in Heart Failure: Mechanistic, Diagnostic and Therapeutic Considerations
Authors: Faraz Pathan, Kathryn Williams
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The prevalence of obesity among the general population has resulted in significant overlap and complex interactions with heart failure. This review aims to describe recent changes to the definition of obesity and explore direct and indirect mechanistic links between obesity and heart failure. Diagnostic challenges presented by obesity regarding echocardiography and B-type natriuretic peptide are also explored. Treatment options and the evidence base for addressing obesity in heart failure are examined across both heart failure with preserved ejection fraction and heart failure with reduced ejection fraction. Finally, the safety profile of new incretin-based therapies is examined.

Global prevalence of obesity and excess weight is projected to increase to more than half the projected world population (3.8 billion people) by 2050.1 Obesity is commonly defined by a BMI ≥30 kg/m2. Having a BMI ≥30 kg/m2 doubles the risk of developing heart failure (HF), with a 5–7% increase in the risk of developing HF for every 1 kg/m2 incremental increase in BMI.2 The prevalence of HF is 56.2 million cases globally.3 Almost half the patients with HF with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) have obesity (BMI ≥30 kg/m2).4 Obesity may directly contribute to HF. It also increases risk for other diseases that contribute to HF, including type 2 diabetes, hypertension, metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), AF, sleep-disordered breathing and ischaemic heart disease.5

Given the prevalence of obesity and its direct and indirect contributions to HF, there is a need to understand the link between these two conditions. This article aims to review updates in the definition of obesity and the association between obesity and HF, including important mechanistic links. We describe challenges in diagnosis of HF in the presence of obesity and review the current evidence base of therapies for the treatment of obesity in patients with HF.

Definitions and Clinical Associations

Definition Change for Obesity

A recent Lancet commission on obesity sought ‘to establish objective criteria for disease diagnosis, aiding clinical decision making and prioritisation of therapeutic interventions and public health strategies’.6 This is important because it recognises that ectopic adiposity can lead to disease and gives clinicians a greater understanding of when to use more advanced treatments to manage excess adiposity. The definitions proposed are summarised in Table 1.

These changes address known limitations of BMI, including the inability to accommodate the impact of fat-free mass by expanding the clinical diagnosis of obesity to include other measures of adiposity (including visceral adiposity), such as waist circumference, waist-to-hip ratio and body composition as shown by dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry.6

Mechanistic Links

Obesity can directly affect cardiac function via ectopic adipose tissue, particularly visceral adipose tissue, associated with inflammatory and profibrotic pathways and perturbations in myocardial energetics, circulating blood volume expansion and a high cardiac output state, and via obesity-related diseases, including type 2 diabetes, hypertension, MASLD, chronic kidney disease and severe sleep-disordered breathing and associated hypoxic burden.

Ectopic Adipose Tissue

Ectopic adipose tissue, particularly visceral adipose tissue, intramyocardial adipose tissue, epicardial adipose tissue and hepatic steatosis, may contribute to the development of HF via the inflammatory cascade. Intramuscular fat measured around the thigh was independently associated with the development of HF, even after adjusting for cardiometabolic risk factors and other measures of adiposity such as BMI and visceral fat.7

Visceral adipose tissue leads to the secretion of inflammatory cytokines, which lead to both myocardial inflammation and fibrosis.8 Epicardial fat is associated with and increased risk of myocardial fibrosis, AF and coronary artery disease, as well as possibly direct adipocyte infiltration into cardiac walls. 9–11

Inflammation and Fibrosis

Obesity is associated with a chronic low-grade inflammatory state, associated with greater secretion of inflammatory cytokines, including interleukin-6 (IL-6) and tumour necrosis factor α, leptin and IL-1β and a decrease in the secretion of adiponectin, which has anti-inflammatory, anti-atherogenic and insulin-sensitising effects.12 The consequence of the systemic and subsequent cardiac inflammation, plasma volume expansion and myocyte hypertrophy is fibrosis.13

Myocardial Energetics

Perturbation of myocardial energetics and substrate usage seen in obesity may also contribute to the development of HF.14 The hearts of patients with obesity exhibit inflexible substrate usage through mechanisms such as insulin resistance and relative insulin deficiency, with a shift towards fatty acid metabolism and consequent intracellular accumulation of long chain fatty acids and lipotoxicity.15–17

Volume Expansion

Obesity-associated volume and pressure overload (due to hypertension) cause concentric left ventricular remodelling. The volume and pressure load coupled with hyperglycaemia, insulin resistance and metabolic dysfunction, systemic inflammation and hypoxia may trigger cardiac fibrosis.18

Obesity also leads to the dysregulation of the renin–angiotensin– aldosterone system and increased activity of the renal sympathetic nerve via systemic, neuroendocrine and local mechanisms leading to volume expansion and hypertension.19 Finally, obesity contributes to 31% of cases of high output cardiac failure characterised by a high cardiac output state, elevated filling pressures and pulmonary hypertension.20

These findings can be observed in patients with obesity who have been otherwise deemed metabolically healthy, with cardiac MRI showing higher extracellular volume and native T2 relaxation times, which is a marker of inflammation, in participants who had obesity without metabolic derangement compared to healthy controls. This group also demonstrated early evidence of diastolic dysfunction based on strain analysis.21

The relationships with inflammation, epicardial fat and the dysregulation of cardioactive adipokines are not seen as clearly in patients with HF with reduced EF (HFrEF).8,13

Thus, the combination of neurohormonal changes and consequent volume expansion, the impact of ectopic and visceral fat on proinflammatory cytokines and suppression of anti-inflammatory cytokines, and the alteration of myocardial energetics and ultrastructural changes, such as ventricular hypertrophy and myocardial fibrosis, all contribute to the development of HF.

Association Between Obesity and HFpEF/HFrEF

BMI shows a dose-response relationship with the risk of developing HF with preserved EF (HFpEF).22 Compared to individuals with normal BMI (18.5–25 kg/m2), those with obesity class 1 (30–34.9 kg/m2) and obesity class 2–3 (BMI ≥35 kg/m2) have been shown to have a 1.56–1.74 and 2.72–3.78 times greater risk of developing HFpEF, respectively.

Evidence indicates there may be a specific obesity phenotype for HFpEF, characterised by increased plasma volume, greater concentric remodelling, right ventricular dysfunction, epicardial fat thickness and greater total heart volumes, despite low NT-B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-BNP) levels, compared to patients with HFpEF without obesity and healthy controls.23

Data from the PARAGON-HF study identified that half the population with HFpEF had obesity and 96% had elevated central adiposity as measured by a waist-to-hip circumference ratio of >0.5.4

Obesity is also associated with an increase in the risk of HFrEF, albeit the relationship is not as strong or dose-dependent as that seen for HFpEF.22 While all the above measures including expansion of plasma volume, neurohormonal activation, inflammation and fibrosis and altered substrate energetics can be seen in HFrEF, it is likely that they lay the foundation for the development of HFrEF with additional insult or risk required to causes the development of HF. The additional risk may be genetic as among a large population data set of patients with dilated cardiomyopathy it was found that obesity increased the odds of dilated cardiomyopathy by 1.82 compared to non-obese individuals. A similar effect was also seen for hypertrophic cardiomyopathy; however, it was only observed in men.24 A recent Mendelian randomisation study observed a similar effect size between obesity and a greater risk of developing non-ischaemic cardiomyopathy (OR 1.87; 95% CI [1.70–2.06]). Data from the UK Biobank have shown greater left ventricular mass, biventricular volume and reduced biventricular EF, arguing that obesity increases the risk of cardiomyopathy by directly affecting structure and function only partially mediated by hypertension.25

Ischaemia may also be an additional injury as there is an increased predisposition to the development of ischaemia in patients with obesity. In a German study of 88,247 patients admitted with MI, 21.3% had obesity, and despite a higher prevalence of cardiovascular risk factors in this cohort, they had lower short-term complications and mortality but higher rates of HF on follow-up.26 Obesity-associated inflammation and genetic vulnerability have been proposed as a risk factor for dilated cardiomyopathy.27

Unlike the direct dose-response relationship between BMI and HFpEF, the relationship between obesity and HFrEF is more nuanced, with the mechanistic links described above contributing to the risk of developing HF and additional vulnerabilities such as genetic or subsequent MI or myocarditis acting as drivers leading to the development of HFrEF.

Challenges in the Diagnosis of HF in Patients with Obesity

Given the shared pathophysiological mechanisms and clinical associations between obesity and HF, it is imperative that early cardiovascular abnormalities in patients deemed to have obesity are recognised.

Symptom Overlap between Obesity and Heart Failure

Dyspnoea in obesity is typically multifactorial. Confounders include poor conditioning and respiratory-attributable dyspnoea due to reduced lung volumes, respiratory muscle endurance and severe sleep-disordered breathing, and psychological factors, such as anxiety.28

Use of simple clinical scores, such as the H2FPEF score (BMI ≥30 = 2 points, hypertension = 1 point, AF = 3 points, pulmonary hypertension [echocardiography pulmonary artery systolic pressure >35 mmHg] = 1 point, age >60 years = 1 point and filling pressure E/e’ >9 = 1 point), has been proposed to discriminate HFpEF from non-cardiac causes of dyspnoea.29 However, in a study on adults with obesity and dyspnoea who underwent invasive cardiopulmonary exercise testing, one-third of the 78 participants had unrecognised HFpEF, which was not picked up by echocardiography, H2PEF score and clinical biomarkers.30

Non-invasive cardiovascular imaging, particularly echocardiography, and NT-BNP levels are both used routinely in the diagnosis and evaluation of HF; however, both of these tests are less reliable in the setting of obesity.31,32

Cardiovascular Imaging in Obesity

There are significant challenges to accurate cardiovascular imaging, with respect to the diagnosis and evaluation of HF in patients with obesity.33

Image Quality

Obesity is a significant predictor of both non-diagnostic echocardiograms and those which require ultrasound enhancing agents for accurate assessment of wall motion and ejection fraction (Figure 1).34

In patients with BMI ≥40 kg/m2 compared to those with BMI <25 kg/m2, there is poor agreement between echocardiographic and cardiac MRI-measured left ventricular ejection fraction (72% versus 80% agreement; kappa 0.53 versus 0.70) and right ventricular ejection fraction (58% versus 72% agreement, kappa 0.18 versus 0.34).

Severe obesity had a limited impact on image quality when using cardiac MRI, and it can significantly degrade echocardiographic image quality and agreement with cardiac MRI when assessing ventricular function.35

Cardiac MRI also has the advantage of a lower interobserver error and the potential of using multi-parametric mapping and late gadolinium enhancement imaging to identify inflammation and fibrosis.21

Indexation

Indexed left atrial volume is a key measure of the chronicity and severity of elevated left atrial pressures and is incorporated into diastolic dysfunction guidelines.36,37

Indexation to body surface area (BSA) is necessary to adjust for body size, however in patients with obesity, there is over-correction for the BSA and consequent underestimation of pathological left atrial dilatation.38 Although alternative methods have been proposed, a recent large study exploring alternative indexation of multiple cardiac chamber strategies in an all-comers population failed to show incremental predictive value over indexation for BSA.39

Assessing Elevated Filling Pressures and Congestion in Patients with Obesity

The non-invasive measurement of filling pressures relies on E/e’ as a surrogate for left atrial filling pressure. It is concerning that there can be an underestimation of pulmonary capillary wedge pressure (PCWP) when using E/e’ in patients with obesity due to the uncoupling between intravascular and distending pressures, as shown in Figure 2. A similar upward shift is seen for right atrial pressures. Thus, for patients with obesity, both E/e’ and indexed left atrial volume may both underestimate congestion and left atrial/PCWP, thereby under-recognising and/or misclassifying echocardiographic evidence of HFpEF.40

B-type Natriuretic Peptide in Obesity

Despite its value in HF, NT-BNP shows an inverse relationship to BMI and current cut-off values are not sufficient to rule out HF in a patient with obesity and dyspnoea.41,42 Obokata and colleagues demonstrated a similar relationship for NT-BNP as was seen for E/e’ in estimating congestion in patients with obesity.40 Alternative and lower cut-off values have been proposed to address this issue both in patients with preclinical (stage B HF) and those with acute HF.43,44 As yet, no single cut-off value has been recommended in clinical guidelines for the diagnosis of HF in obesity.

In summary, the clinical assessment of dyspnoea and potential HFpEF or HFrEF is challenging in patients with obesity. Diagnostic tools such as NT-BNP and echocardiography have limitations related to image quality, indexation and decoupling between E/e’ and wedge pressure.

Enhancing agents should be considered, specifically in cases of suboptimal image quality on echocardiography-ultrasound, with cardiac MRI being the gold standard for volumetric and functional analysis and enabling deeper phenotyping of patients with HFpEF.45,46 Diastolic stress echocardiography can be helpful in cases of indeterminate diastolic function, given limitations of E/e’ values and indexation of left atrial volume.47 Finally, in cases where the cause of dyspnoea is uncertain, cardiopulmonary exercise testing can help differentiate between respiratory, cardiac and deconditioning causes.48 Given the above limitations with NT-BNP and echocardiographic parameters in patients with obesity and unexplained dyspnoea, invasive haemodynamic assessment should be considered.49

Treatment of Obesity in Heart Failure

In the setting of clinical obesity occurring in the presence of HF, the goals of therapy should be to improve symptoms, quality of life and survival using evidence-based treatments aimed at fully regaining or improving function, rather than focusing on weight alone.

The management of obesity in HF comprises treatment of obesity as a key pillar of management – specifically in HFpEF and HF with mildly reduced EF (HFmrEF) – with obesity treated to achieve a specific goal, for example a target BMI to enable transplantation, and the treatment of obesity-associated illnesses which may cause or worsen HF.

Treatment of Obesity in Patients with HFpEF

There is an emerging body of evidence for the treatment of obesity as a pillar of therapy in HFpEF. The STEP-HFpEF trial examining the use of semaglutide demonstrated benefit in exercise capacity, symptom control and HF hospitalisations in patients with obesity without diabetes, while achieving 13.3% weight loss.50 Similar benefit was seen in patients with HFpEF, obesity and co-existent diabetes, independent of diabetes control at baseline.51 A pooled analysis offour trials, using semaglutide in patients with HFpEF/HFmrEF and obesity/diabetes, found a reduction in clinical HF events.52

The SUMMIT trial investigated the role of tirzepatide in patients with obesity and HFpEF. Tirzepatide achieved a mean 13.9% reduction in weight and demonstrated a reduction in the primary composite endpoint of death from cardiovascular causes or worsening HF events, driven predominantly by the latter and improved clinical status.53

There are no randomised trials evaluating the impact of bariatric surgery in patients with obesity and HFpEF.

Treatment of Obesity in Patients with HFrEF

The data on HFrEF is not as compelling, and the treatment of obesity in this population needs to be considered in certain contexts. Liraglutide did not improve left ventricular ejection fraction in patients with HFrEF (with or without diabetes) and actually increased heart rate and serious adverse cardiovascular events.54,55

Prespecified analysis of the SELECT trial showed reduced cardiovascular events in patients with atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease with a history of investigator-specified HF who were overweight or obese, irrespective of HF phenotype.56 Similarly, in a prespecified analysis of the FLOW trial, semaglutide showed benefit in patients with HF and chronic kidney disease, reducing risk of time to first HF event or cardiovascular death; HF subtype was HFpEF in 47.9%, HFrEF in 18.1% and unknown in 33.9% of cases.57 In the context ofsuch equipoise, there has been an appropriate call for adequately powered trials of incretin-based therapies in patients with HFrEF.58

In the absence of randomised data in HFrEF, cautious use may be considered to address obesity to improve the burden of obesity-associated conditions or as a bridge to transplant candidacy. A BMI >35 kg/m2 is a relative contraindication to transplantation, with guidelines recommending weight loss prior to transplantation as severe obesity, defined as a BMI >35 kg/m2, is associated with higher rates of infection, graft failure and post-transplant mortality.59,60

Despite the absence of randomised data, observational data suggest weight loss surgery can be used to improve symptom control and survival in patients with obesity and end-stage HFrEF.61

Treatment of obesity-associated illnesses, which may affect the disease course of HF, needs to be considered. There is randomised controlled evidence for the benefit of incretins in obstructive sleep apnoea, pre-diabetes and diabetes, MASLD and chronic kidney disease, all of which may also contribute to outcomes in patients with obesity and HF.62–65

Recent narrative reviews have synthesised the rapidly evolving evidence base for glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1 RAs) and dual GIP/GLP-1 receptor agonists in HF. Shchendrygina et al. examined their impact on cardiac structure and function, exercise capacity and quality of life across ejection-fraction phenotypes, while Bonfioli et al. provided an updated overview incorporating the latest outcome data.66,67 Together, these reviews underscore that although substantial weight loss remains the principal driver of benefit, pleiotropic effects, such as reductions in systemic and myocardial inflammation, epicardial adipose tissue and improvements in endothelial function, may confer additional advantages in obesity-related HFpEF. They also confirm that improvements in cardiac remodelling and systolic function have generally been modest or neutral, reinforcing the need for further dedicated, adequately powered outcome trials, particularly for HFrEF.

Safety of Incretin-based Therapies

Loss of lean muscle mass has been observed in clinical trials of incretin-based therapies. This ranges between 15–45.2% of total weight loss.68 These changes may be adaptive, nevertheless, care may need to be taken in patients with frailty.69 This loss of muscle mass can be offset by cardiac rehabilitation and resistance training.69,70

Liraglutide therapy alone has been shown to reduce bone mineral density; however, bone health was preserved when it was coupled with exercise.71 Other side effects include gastrointestinal issues, particularly nausea, and there have been reports of pancreatitis and ophthalmological issues.72 The side-effect profile can be managed by low-dose initiation and slow uptitration every 4 weeks.

Cessation of these medications in patients with obesity is followed by rapid weight regain and reversal of beneficial effects on cardiometabolic markers. These findings suggest caution over short-term use of these drugs without a more comprehensive approach to weight management.73

Conclusion

HF can be an obesity-associated disease via direct and indirect mechanisms related to inflammatory, fibrotic and neurohormonal pathways and as a complication of other obesity-associated diseases. There is a more pronounced relationship between obesity and HFpEF compared to HFrEF. HF presents unique diagnostic challenges which pertain to the impact of obesity on image quality in echocardiography, the impact of indexation to BSA and the uncoupling of echocardiographic/serum biomarkers of congestion. Novel GLP-RA and GIP-based therapies have shown significant benefit in patients with obesity and HFpEF and limited evidence in HFrEF. These drugs show a significant rebound effect and should be considered a long-term treatment.
