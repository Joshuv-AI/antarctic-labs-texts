Persistently Exciting Online Feedback Optimization Controller with Minimal Perturbations*
Tore Gude, Marta Anna Zagorowska, Lars Struen Imsland

Abstract
This paper develops a persistently exciting input generating Online Feedback Optimization (OFO) controller that estimates the sensitivity of a process ensuring minimal deviations from the descent direction while converging. This eliminates the need for random perturbations in feedback loop. The proposed controller is formulated as a bilevel optimization program, where a nonconvex full rank constraint is relaxed using linear constraints and penalization. The validation of the method is performed in a simulated scenario where multiple systems share a limited, costly resource for production optimization, simulating an oil and gas resource allocation problem. The method allows for less input perturbations while accurately estimating gradients, allowing faster convergence when the gradients are unknown. In the case study, the proposed method achieved the same profit compared to an OFO controller with random input perturbations, and 1.4 % 1.4\% higher profit compared to an OFO controller without input perturbations.



I
Introduction

Increasingly complex industrial processes must be optimally operated to be competitive and minimize environmental impacts. The availability of sensor data, computational power, and advances in machine learning motivate utilizing real-time measurements to find the optimal operating point of a process even in the presence of model-plant mismatch and external disturbances. To compensate for model-plant mismatch, the measurements must contain enough information about the system, formalized through persistency of excitation
[
1
]
.

In this work, we propose a method for generating persistently exciting inputs while minimally disturbing an Online Feedback Optimization (OFO) controller. Persistently exciting input signals are necessary for accurate model estimation, which is used by the OFO controller. The proposed controller is tested in a simulated scenario in which multiple systems utilize the same shared, limited, resource for production optimization, reflecting resource allocation challenges in the oil and gas industry.

An OFO controller optimizes a system in a feedback loop
[
1
]
. Using optimization in feedback loops has recently been investigated and has shown promising results for optimal power system operation
[
2
]
. OFO controllers that generate persistently exciting input have also been investigated in
[
1
]
and
[
3
]
. However, these approaches involve random perturbations and do not consider possible adverse effects of these perturbations.

An overview of other methods that combine optimization with feedback control and their relation to OFO is provided in
[
4
]
, including a comparison of real-time optimization methods such as extremum seeking and modifier adaptation. Furthermore, an overview of extremum seeking approaches including treatment of persistent excitations is given in
[
5
]
.

Our contribution is a persistently exciting input generating OFO controller with minimal perturbations while converging toward the optimal operating point. This approach eliminates the need for random perturbations, which an operator or production engineer may be reluctant to introduce into a critical feedback loop. For the controller to efficiently find a solution, a nonconvex full rank constraint is relaxed using linear constraints and penalization. While this looses the theoretical guarantee for generating persistently exciting inputs, it renders the problem efficient to solve and demonstrates effectiveness in the case study.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows: In Section
II
we present preliminary theory of OFO and default gradient estimation. In Section
III
, we present our persistently exciting input generating OFO controller, with case study results given in Section
IV
. Finally, the paper is concluded in Section
V
.

II
Preliminaries

II-A

Online Feedback Optimization

The recent research in optimization in closed loop is based on three main benefits, 1) optimize systems with inaccurate data and time-varying parameters, 2) obey constraints without an accurate model of the whole system and 3) eliminate the need for exogenous setpoints and reference signals
[
4
]
. OFO is a real-time optimization scheme that solves an optimization problem by iteratively driving a system closer to the optimal operating point. This is done utilizing steady-state measurements in a feedback loop instead of a model of the system.

Given a general control system with inputs
u\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{u}}
, outputs
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{y}}
and a steady-state mapping
y=h(u)
, OFO solves the following optimization problem,

\displaystyle\min_{u,y}

\displaystyle\quad\Phi\left(u,y\right)

(1a)

s.t.

\displaystyle u\in\mathcal{U}

(1b)

\displaystyle y\in\mathcal{Y}

(1c)

\displaystyle y=h\left(u\right),

(1d)

where
\Phi:\mathbb{R}^{n_{u}}\times\mathbb{R}^{n_{y}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a continuously differentiable objective function,
h:\mathbb{R}^{n_{u}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n_{y}}
is a continuously differentiable nonlinear input-output mapping and
\mathcal{U}
and
\mathcal{Y}
are the sets of input and output constraints, respectively. To solve the optimization problem (
1
), OFO typically actuates the real system in the direction of the negative descent of the objective function until a local minimum of (
1
) is found. To account for input and output constraints, in this work OFO uses projected gradient descent, which projects the gradient onto the closest direction in the tangent cone,
\mathcal{T}_{u}
,

w_{t}=\underset{w\in\mathcal{T}_{u}}{\mathrm{arg\,min}}\lVert w+\nabla_{u}\Phi^{\top}\left(u,y\right)\rVert^{2}_{2},

(2)

ensuring safe and feasible operation while converging
[
6
]
. Then the solution to (
2
),
w_{t}
, is applied as an input actuation

u_{t+1}=u_{t}+\alpha w_{t},

(3)

where
\alpha
is a constant stepsize and

\nabla_{u}\Phi^{\top}\left(u,y\right)=\frac{\partial\Phi^{\top}\left(u,y\right)}{\partial u}+\nabla_{u}h(u)^{\top}\frac{\partial\Phi^{\top}\left(u,y\right)}{\partial y}

(4)

is the gradient of the objective function with respect to
u
. An analysis of the impact of the step size
\alpha
in OFO was done by
[
6
]
. When the input-output mapping (
1d
) is explicitly known, the derivatives in (
4
) can be found analytically. To obtain the descent direction (
4
) given an unknown input-output mapping
y=h(u)
, the Jacobian,
\nabla_{u}h
needs to be estimated
[
1
]
. It can be shown that OFO will converge to an optimal operating point with the estimated
\nabla_{u}h
and with disturbances affecting the system
[
7
]
.

Gradient Estimation

For estimation of
\nabla_{u}h
, we use a similar approach as in
[
1
]
where its column-wise vector representation
\hat{h}_{t}
, is estimated through a recursive least-squares algorithm,

\displaystyle K_{t-1}=

\displaystyle\Sigma_{t-1}U^{\top}_{\Delta,{t-1}}\left(\Sigma_{m,t-1}+U_{\Delta,t-1}\Sigma_{t-1}U_{\Delta,t-1}^{\top}\right)^{-1}

(5a)

\displaystyle\hat{h}_{t}=

\displaystyle\hat{h}_{t-1}+K_{t-1}\left(\Delta y_{t-1}-U_{\Delta,t-1}\hat{h}_{t-1}\right)

(5b)

\displaystyle\Sigma_{t}=

\displaystyle(I_{n_{u}n_{y}}-K_{t-1}U_{\Delta,t-1})\Sigma_{t-1}(I_{n_{u}n_{y}}-K_{t-1}U_{\Delta,t-1})^{\top}

\displaystyle\quad+K_{t-1}\Sigma_{m,t-1}K_{t-1}^{T}+\Sigma_{p,t-1},

(5c)

where
\Delta y_{t-1}=y_{t}-y_{t-1}
,
\Delta u_{t-1}=u_{t}-u_{t-1}
,
U_{\Delta,t-1}=\Delta u_{t-1}\otimes I_{n_{y}}
, where
\otimes
is the Kronecker product,
I_{n_{y}}
is the identity matrix of dimension
n_{y}
,
\Sigma_{p,t}=\Sigma_{p1}+\Sigma_{p2}\lVert\Delta u_{t}\rVert_{2}^{2}
is the covariance matrix of the Gaussian process noise and
\Sigma_{m,t}=\Sigma_{m1}+\Sigma_{m2}\lVert\Delta u_{t}\rVert_{2}^{2}+\Sigma_{m3}\lVert\Delta u_{t}\rVert_{2}^{4}
is the covariance matrix of the Gaussian measurement noise.
The parts
\Sigma_{p2},\Sigma_{m2}
and
\Sigma_{m3}
of the covariance matrices are proportional to
\lVert\Delta u_{t}\rVert_{2}
, so that larger variations in input are associated with greater uncertainty. The covariance matrices also have parts independent of the input,
\Sigma_{p1}
and
\Sigma_{m1}
to account for uncontrolled random changes. In this paper,
\Sigma_{m,0}
and
\Sigma_{m,0}
are initialized as identity and
\hat{h}_{0}
as a vector of ones.

III-A

Random Perturbations

In the OFO formulation from
[
1
]
, the descent direction (
4
) is perturbed with Gaussian noise,
s_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(0,\sigma\right)
, to ensure persistency of excitation with high probability. Combining the OFO controller (
2
) from
[
6
]
with the perturbation from
[
1
]
the following formulation is an OFO controller that generates persistently exciting input signals with the same high probability as in
[
1
]
,

\displaystyle w_{t}=

\displaystyle\underset{w\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{u}}}{\mathrm{arg\,min}}\lVert w+\nabla_{u}\Phi^{\top}\left(u,y\right)+s_{t}\rVert^{2}_{2}

(6a)

s.t.

\displaystyle\quad u_{t}+\alpha w\in\mathcal{U}

(6b)

\displaystyle\quad y_{t}+\alpha\nabla_{u}h_{t}w\in\mathcal{Y},

(6c)

where
y_{t}+\alpha\nabla_{u}h_{t}w_{t}
is the estimated output
y_{t+1}
. Persistently exciting OFO controllers have also been investigated in
[
3
]
. Here, if the input actuation during an iteration of the OFO controller is not persistently exciting, it is arbitrarily perturbed in a direction giving persistently exciting input. This approach is similar to the OFO controller we present in the following subsections, where we also perturb the descent direction only when the candidate solution is not persistently exciting. However, the methods differ in how the perturbation is determined.

III-B

Persistently Exciting Input Design

The persistency of excitation condition
[
8
]
states that
\Delta u_{t}
is persistently exciting if there exists a
T>0
, such that for all
t>0
, the matrix formed by columns
\Delta u_{t-i},\forall i\in\{0,\ldots,T\}
has full rank, rank equal to the dimension of the input,
n_{u}
. This means that
\mathbb{R}^{T}
can be spanned by
\Delta u_{i},\forall i\in\{0,\ldots,T\}
in
T
steps when
\Delta u_{i},\forall i\in\{0,\ldots,T\}
is persistently exciting. To have a persistently exciting OFO controller, we want to achieve persistency of excitation with a minimal number of time steps,
T=n_{u}
, resulting in a square matrix. Ideally, full rank would be achieved following the gradient descent without any perturbation as in (
3
). Any nonzero perturbation that is nonparallel to the gradient descent in (
4
) would affect the projection (
6a
) which could lead to slower convergence. In particular, we want to avoid perturbing the gradient descent in a given direction if it already gives a persistently exciting input. We therefore reformulate the update step (
3
) to

u_{t+1}=u_{t}+\alpha w_{t}+s_{t}

(7)

and define the following condition for persistently exciting input with a minimal number of time steps,

\text{rank}\left(\left[\Delta u_{t-n_{u}},\ldots,\Delta u_{t-1},\alpha w_{t}+s_{t}\right]\right)=n_{u},

(8)

where
\Delta u_{t-n_{u}},\ldots,\Delta u_{t-1}
are the
n_{u}-1
previous input actuations and
\alpha w_{t}+s_{t}
is the current candidate solution for the next actuation. The idea here is that we first calculate the descent direction and then perturb the corresponding input if the corresponding input is not persistently exciting. When a perturbation is required for (
8
) to be satisfied, we want to minimize its effect on the descent direction, measured by its impact on the objective function, by solving:

\displaystyle s_{t}\left(w\right)=

\displaystyle\underset{s\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{u}}}{\mathrm{arg\,min}}\quad\frac{1}{2}\left(\nabla_{u}\Phi\left(u,y\right)s\right)^{2}

(9a)

s.t.

\displaystyle\text{rank}\left(\left[\Delta u_{t-n_{u}},\ldots,\Delta u_{t-1},\alpha w_{t}+s\right]\right)=n_{u}

(9b)

\displaystyle\underline{s}\leq s\leq\overline{s},

(9c)

where
\underline{s}
and
\overline{s}
are upper and lower limits on
s_{t}
, respectively. With the input actuation reformulation (
7
), the OFO controller (
6
) becomes

\displaystyle w_{t}=

\displaystyle\underset{w\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{u}}}{\mathrm{arg\,min}}\lVert w+\nabla_{u}\Phi^{\top}\left(u,y\right)\rVert^{2}_{2}

(10a)

s.t.

\displaystyle\quad u_{t}+\alpha w+s_{t}\left(w\right)\in\mathcal{U}

(10b)

\displaystyle\quad y_{t}+\alpha\nabla_{u}h_{t}w+\nabla_{u}h_{t}s_{t}\left(w\right)\in\mathcal{Y},

(10c)

where
y_{t}+\alpha\nabla_{u}h_{t}w_{t}+\nabla_{u}h_{t}s_{t}\left(w\right)
is our estimate of the next output and
s_{t}\left(w\right)
is the solution of (
9
) given
w_{t}
.

In general, the rank function in constraint (
9b
) is discontinuous and nonconvex, and general rank-constrained optimization problems are mostly classified as NP-hard
[
9
]
. An iterative rank minimization method is proposed in
[
9
]
, treating the entire matrix as the optimization variable. However, this approach is not suitable for our purposes, as our optimization variables are limited to the last column of the matrix. In
[
10
]
, a convex rank constraint is formulated but is limited to symmetric positive definite matrices, which in general does not hold for the matrix in (
9b
). In this paper, we propose a relaxation of constraint (
9b
) using linear constraints and penalization. The left nullspace vector of the
n_{u}-1
first columns of the matrix in (
9b
):

v_{\perp}=\text{null}\left(\left[\Delta u_{t-n_{u}},\ldots,\Delta u_{t-1}\right]^{\top}\right)

(11)

provides a vector orthogonal to the span of these columns
[
11
]
. For the new input actuation,
\alpha w_{t}+s_{t}
, to lie outside this span, it must extend in a direction not contained within this span, i.e. the dot product between
\alpha w_{t}+s_{t}
and
v_{\perp}
has to be nonzero:

v_{\perp}^{\top}\cdot\left(\alpha w_{t}+s_{t}\right)\neq 0.

(12)

To ensure robustness, this condition is reformulated by introducing a small positive margin parameter,
\epsilon
, resulting in the following inequality:

\lvert v_{\perp}^{\top}\cdot\left(\alpha w_{t}+s_{t}\right)\rvert\geq\epsilon.

(13)

To incorporate the nonconvex constraint (
13
) into a solver designed for linear constraints, we apply a standard lifting technique by introducing two variables
z^{+}_{t}
and
z^{-}_{t}
, to represent the absolute value in (
13
). For this reformulation to be valid, only one of the variables may be nonzero at a time
t
, satisfying the following conditions:

\displaystyle z^{+}_{t}+z^{-}_{t}

\displaystyle\geq\epsilon

(14a)

\displaystyle z^{+}_{t}

\displaystyle\geq 0

(14b)

\displaystyle z^{-}_{t}

\displaystyle\geq 0

(14c)

\displaystyle z^{+}_{t}-z^{-}_{t}

\displaystyle=v_{\perp}^{\top}\cdot\left(\alpha w_{t}+s_{t}\right)

(14d)

\displaystyle z^{+}_{t}z^{-}_{t}

\displaystyle=0.

(14e)

Constraints (
14a
) and (
14e
) together ensure that only one of
z_{t}^{+},z_{t}^{-}
is nonzero. To overcome the non-convexity introduced by (
14e
), we relax (
14e
) by introducing a penalization term
\gamma\left(z^{+}_{t}+z^{-}_{t}\right)
into the objective (
9a
). The relaxation allows formulating (
9
) as a convex optimization problem. As demonstrated in Section
IV
, the relaxation provides persistently exciting inputs even for moderate values of
\gamma
.

III-C

Bilevel Formulation

To solve (
9
) and (
10
), we propose a bilevel program with (
10
) as the upper-level problem and (
9
) as the lower-level problem. The rank equality constraint (
9b
) is addressed using the reformulation (
14
) with the penalty term added to the objective function (
9a
), relaxing the nonconvex constraint (
14e
):

Upper-level problem:

\displaystyle w_{t}=

\displaystyle\underset{w\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{u}}}{\mathrm{arg\,min}}\lVert w+\nabla_{u}\Phi^{\top}\left(u,y\right)\rVert^{2}_{2}

(15a)

s.t.

\displaystyle\quad u_{t}+\alpha w+s_{t}\in\mathcal{U}

(15b)

\displaystyle\quad y_{t}+\alpha\nabla_{u}h_{t}w+\nabla_{u}h_{t}s_{t}\in\mathcal{Y}.

(15c)

Lower-level problem:

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}s_{t}\\
z^{+}_{t}\\
z^{-}_{t}\end{bmatrix}=

\displaystyle\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}s\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{u}},\\
z^{+},z^{-}\in\mathbb{R}\end{subarray}}{\mathrm{arg\,min}}\frac{1}{2}\left(\nabla_{u}\Phi\left(u,y\right)s\right)^{2}+\gamma\left(z^{+}+z^{-}\right)

(16a)

s.t.

\displaystyle\quad\underline{s}\leq s\leq\overline{s}

(16b)

\displaystyle\quad z^{+}+z^{-}\geq\epsilon

(16c)

\displaystyle\quad z^{+}\geq 0

(16d)

\displaystyle\quad z^{-}\geq 0

(16e)

\displaystyle\quad z^{+}-z^{-}=v_{\perp}^{\top}\cdot\left(\alpha w_{t}+s\right).

(16f)

Bilevel optimization is a class of optimization problems involving two hierarchically nested optimization problems and is in general NP-hard to solve
[
12
]
. However, if the lower-level problem is convex, bilevel optimization problems can be uniquely solved by reformulating the lower-level problem with its KKT-conditions
[
12
]
. The objective function (
16a
) is quadratic with a positive semi-definite Hessian, and the constraints (
16b
)–(
16f
) are linear. As a result, the lower-level problem (
16
) is convex
[
13
]
. However, while the relaxation (
16
) of (
9
) does not guarantee the generation of persistently exciting inputs, the perturbations will in practice ensure persistently exciting inputs and in contrast to the controller (
6
), the perturbations are chosen in a minimal disturbing way. The proposed persistently exciting OFO controller (
15
)-(
16
), with the lower-level problem (
16
) written using its KKT solution, is:

\displaystyle w_{t}=

\displaystyle\quad\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}w\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{u}},s\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{u}},z^{+}\in\mathbb{R},\\
z^{-}\in\mathbb{R},\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{2n_{u}+3},\mu\in\mathbb{R}\end{subarray}}{\mathrm{arg\,min}}\lVert w+\nabla_{u}\Phi^{\top}\left(u,y\right)\rVert^{2}_{2}

(17a)

s.t.

\displaystyle\quad u_{t}+\alpha w+s\in\mathcal{U}

(17b)

\displaystyle\quad y_{t}+\alpha\nabla_{u}h_{t}w+\nabla_{u}h_{t}s\in\mathcal{Y}

(17c)

\displaystyle\quad\text{A}\begin{bmatrix}s^{\top}&z^{+}&z^{-}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}-\text{b}\leq 0

(17d)

\displaystyle\quad\lambda_{i}\left(\text{A}\begin{bmatrix}s^{\top}&z^{+}&z^{-}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}-\text{b}\right)_{i}=0,\quad i\in\mathcal{I}

(17e)

\displaystyle\quad z^{+}-z^{-}-v_{\perp}^{\top}\cdot\left(\alpha w+s\right)=0

(17f)

\displaystyle\quad\nabla_{\{s,z^{+},z^{-}\}}\mathcal{L}\left(w,s,z^{+},z^{-},\lambda,\mu\right)=0,

(17g)

where the Lagrangian,
\mathcal{L}
, is defined as

\begin{split}&\mathcal{L}(w,s,z^{+},z^{-},\lambda,\mu)=\frac{1}{2}\left(\nabla_{u}\Phi\left(u,y\right)s\right)^{2}\\
&+\gamma\left(z^{+}+z^{-}\right)+\lambda^{\top}\left(A\begin{bmatrix}s^{\top}&z^{+}&z^{-}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}-b\right)\\
&+\mu\left(z^{+}-z^{-}-v_{\perp}^{\top}\cdot\left(\alpha w+s\right)\right),\end{split}

(18)

with
\lambda_{i}\geq 0
as the Lagrange multipliers for the inequalities in
\mathcal{I}
, the subscript
i\in\mathcal{I}
denote the rows in
A
and
b
, and
\mu
is the Lagrange multiplier for the equality constraint (
17f
).

IV
Case Study

The case study is motivated by an oil and gas industrial setup. Figure

Gas lift enters a subsea hub connected to four oil wells, allocating gas lift to each pipeline and sending the produced fluid to the platform at sea level. Typically, there is a total limit of available gas lift and there might also be upper and lower limits of injected gas to each well. In the case study, we only consider input constraints,

Several approaches to real-time optimization of gas lifted oil wells are discussed in

V
Conclusion

In this paper we present a persistently exciting input generating Online Feedback Optimization (OFO) controller that perturb the input in a minimal disturbing manner. Our proposed persistently exciting input generating OFO controller has a similar performance to a more traditional OFO controller while we have eliminated the need for random perturbations in a feedback loop. Minimal deviations from the descent direction could also lead to faster convergence, which is especially useful in scenarios where the optimal operating point is time-varying.

In a physical system, noise would be present, which could contribute to having persistently exciting input-output samples without designing a controller to do so. However, depending on the signal-noise ratio in the system, and considering that not all operating ranges may not be sufficiently explored, our proposed method could be useful in physical systems with disturbances to ensure that all possible directions in the input space are sufficiently explored. Future work could include verifying the method on a physical system with disturbances.
