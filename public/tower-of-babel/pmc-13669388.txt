Reconfigurable acoustic tweezers based on a programmable droplet array
Authors: Zhen Wang, Menglei Zhao, Kangfu Chen, Yao Lu, Haopu Wang, Zongliang Guo, Zonghao Li, Meiyi Zhou, Huiliang Cao, Kaiqiang Cao, Xiaoyi Wang, Rongxin Fu, Hang Li, Zhichao Ma, Jiafang Li, Huikai Xie, Shuailong Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Conventional acoustic manipulation relies on either direct acoustic radiation force acting on particles or indirect methods using acoustic streaming generated by acoustic pressure fields to manipulate particles. While acoustic devices generating standing waves can create patterned arrangements of particles and cells, these methods are typically limited to producing regular arrays in static configurations. This study introduces a dynamic acoustic field modulation method to overcome these limitations, significantly enhancing manipulation flexibility. Crucially, the propagation of acoustic energy varies substantially at liquid-solid interfaces or gas-solid interfaces due to differences in acoustic impedance matching. To exploit this, we use a digital microfluidic chip (DMF) to manipulate droplets and generate a programmable droplet array via the electrowetting-on-dielectric effect. By utilizing the programmable droplet array, we dynamically alter the spatial distribution of the acoustic field from a bulk acoustic wave (BAW) device in real time and demonstrate this technique’s capability for capturing and manipulating particles on demand. We believe this is a unique method to create acoustic tweezers with great flexibility and reconfigurability, which holds promising potential for advanced applications in micromanipulation, micro-assembly, and beyond.

Introduction

High-precision, non-contact manipulation of microscale particles, particularly individual cells, is essential for studying cell–cell communication, disease modeling, and tissue engineering1,2. Applications, such as cell alignment, single-cell functional studies, and dynamic microenvironment regulation, require precise spatiotemporal manipulation3. For example, optical tweezers provide precise, label-free manipulation of micron-scale particles and single cells through tightly focused laser beams, enabling sub-micron spatial control and quantitative force measurements4–6. However, their trapping efficiency is limited by low photon momentum transfer, and the high optical intensities required for trapping can induce local heating and photodamage to cellular membranes. On the other hand, magnetic manipulation offers strong, long-range forces and enables parallel control of many particles with relatively simple instrumentation7–9. However, magnetic manipulation requires the object to exhibit a magnetic response, which places strict constraints on the materials from which it is made. Acoustic manipulation has therefore emerged as an attractive alternative because ultrasound can generate non-contact forces in liquid environments with good biocompatibility, broad material compatibility, and scalable operation10,11.

Acoustic manipulation is commonly mediated by two physical effects: acoustic radiation force and acoustic streaming. Acoustic radiation force arises from momentum transfer between the acoustic field and suspended objects, enabling particles or cells to migrate toward pressure nodes, antinodes, or other stable trapping positions depending on their acoustic contrast12,13. Acoustic streaming, by contrast, is a steady fluid motion generated by viscous attenuation of acoustic waves and can indirectly transport, concentrate, or mix particles through hydrodynamic drag14–16. In both mechanisms, the spatial distribution of the acoustic pressure and velocity fields ultimately determines the direction, magnitude, and selectivity of particle motion. As a result, controlling the acoustic field in space and time is central to the development of programmable acoustic tweezers.

Considerable progress has been made in engineering acoustic fields for particle and cell manipulation. Acoustic holography and acoustic metasurfaces can impose designed phase and amplitude profiles onto acoustic waves, producing complex pressure landscapes and three-dimensional potential wells for trapping, patterning, and assembling particles or cells17–25. For example, acoustic metasurfaces combined with bulk acoustic wave resonators have been used to generate holographic fields for three-dimensional particle and cell patterning17,26. Similarly, planar arrangements of interdigital transducers can generate surface-acoustic-wave interference patterns, enabling periodic particle alignment, cell patterning, and tissue-like assembly27–30. On the other hand, acoustic streaming enables particle manipulation through steady fluid motion generated by the viscous attenuation of sound waves15,31,32. Three-dimensional acoustic streaming systems have been shown to produce microscale vortices for aggregating nanoparticles down to tens of nanometers and continuously focusing and sorting sub-micron particles33,34. High-frequency (GHz) BAW-driven platforms can generate localized shear forces that transiently permeabilize cell membranes, allowing efficient delivery of nanovesicles directly into the cytoplasm while maintaining cell viability35.

Despite these advances, most existing acoustic systems still rely on static or preconfigured acoustic field distributions21,36,37. These fixed configurations limit the system’s ability to adapt to changing tasks and restrict precise temporal control, making real-time reconfiguration of acoustic fields challenging. To address these limitations, dynamic acoustic field modulation strategies have been explored38. One approach employs multi-channel transducer arrays with independent phase or frequency control to shift standing wave nodes in two- or three-dimensional space, thereby enabling controlled particle translation, reconfiguration, and pattern evolution39–41. Other strategies involve physically translating acoustic sources to achieve particle capture, droplet transport, fusion, splitting, and mixing20,42. Although these methods provide greater flexibility, they often require complex electroacoustic hardware and careful system calibration and remain limited by spatial resolution and system size.

In this work, we present a dynamic acoustic field modulation platform that integrates digital microfluidics (DMF) with BAW resonators. Using electrowetting-on-dielectric (EWOD) technology43, we dynamically reconfigure droplet arrays to modulate acoustic wave transmission paths and interference patterns. Unlike phased-array or multi-transducer systems that rely on complex electronic phase control, the proposed approach achieves real-time acoustic field reconfiguration using a single resonator and digitally controlled droplet motion. This strategy directly addresses several limitations of existing dynamic acoustic manipulation systems. By eliminating the need for multi-channel transducer arrays, it significantly reduces hardware complexity and calibration requirements. In this platform, the droplet layer serves as a tunable acoustic medium and programmable interface, enabling real-time acoustic field reconfiguration without mechanical movement or multi-channel excitation. This approach allows reversible pattern switching, adaptive particle trajectory control, and on-demand field reshaping, not only overcoming the limitations of static acoustic manipulation systems but also expanding the controllable parameter space, providing new methods for high-throughput, non-contact, and single-cell-level manipulation in biomedical applications.

Results and discussion

Integrated BAW-DMF platform

As illustrated in Fig. 1a, the integrated system comprises three key components: a BAW resonator, a DMF chip, and a fluid reservoir. The BAW resonator (0.96 MHz), positioned beneath the DMF chip, generates a large acoustic field. Leveraging EWOD principles, the DMF system enables precise microliter-scale droplet manipulation43. Figure 1b details the DMF structure: top/bottom glass plates with transparent ITO and patterned electrode arrays; both coated with hydrophobic layers44. By selectively applying voltage to the bottom electrodes, the local contact angle of the droplet is reduced through the electrowetting effect, creating a surface tension gradient that drives droplet movement across the electrode array45–47. The acoustic transducer is driven by an AC signal generated by a signal generator and amplified by a power amplifier. The resulting ultrasonic waves propagate through the BAW resonator and into the DMF chip. Because the DMF chip is positioned between the BAW resonator and the fluid reservoir, the droplets on the chip dynamically regulate how acoustic energy is transmitted into the reservoir. This configuration allows the droplet position to control the spatial distribution of the acoustic field above the chip. The underlying mechanism arises from the large difference in acoustic impedance between liquids and gases24,38. At solid–air interfaces, the strong impedance mismatch causes most acoustic energy to be reflected, significantly limiting upward propagation. In contrast, the acoustic impedance mismatch at the solid–liquid interface is much smaller, allowing efficient transmission of acoustic energy. Within the DMF chip, droplets are sandwiched between the top and bottom plates, while the surrounding regions are filled with air. By repositioning droplets on the DMF chip, the locations where acoustic energy enters the reservoir can be dynamically reconfigured. Each droplet, therefore, functions as a localized acoustic coupling element that focuses acoustic energy into the fluid above. The resulting acoustic radiation forces can trap, concentrate, and transport suspended particles, effectively forming programmable “acoustic tweezers”. Through coordinated control of droplet positioning and acoustic excitation, the system enables dynamic and reconfigurable manipulation of particles within the reservoir.

When a droplet is present, acoustic waves generated by the BAW resonator preferentially propagate through the droplet region due to the improved acoustic impedance matching at the solid–liquid interface. As shown in Fig. 2a, the transmitted acoustic waves become spatially concentrated after passing through the droplet, forming a localized high-pressure region above the droplet in the reservoir. Subsequent reflection at the water–air interface produces a standing-wave pattern confined to this region, indicating strong local acoustic energy accumulation. Quantitative vertical SPL profiles extracted along the droplet path and the surrounding air path further highlight the contrast between the two transmission paths (Fig. 2b). Along the droplet path, acoustic energy propagates efficiently with relatively small attenuation, maintaining a high SPL within the reservoir. In contrast, acoustic waves traveling through the air-filled regions experience severe attenuation due to the strong impedance mismatch at the solid–air interface, resulting in a rapid SPL drop and preventing effective energy transfer into the reservoir. This effect becomes more evident when compared with the droplet-free simulations shown in Fig. 2c, d. Without droplets, acoustic waves transmitted through the plates disperse broadly within the DMF chip and the fluid chamber, producing a relatively weak and spatially diffuse acoustic field. The corresponding vertical SPL profile (Fig. 2d) shows substantial attenuation as the waves propagate upward. Together, these results demonstrate that droplets on the DMF chip act as movable acoustic transmission channels that efficiently couple ultrasonic energy from the BAW resonator into the reservoir. The integration of DMF enables dynamic reconfiguration of the acoustic trapping location. By translating the droplet on the DMF chip using electrowetting actuation53–55, the position of the localized acoustic field shifts accordingly. As a result, particles captured within the acoustic trap can be transported collectively. Figure 4d documents this capability: droplet movement directly drives aggregated microsphere clusters (see supplementary Video S2). Particle cluster transport occurs at approximately 1 mm/s, with directional control governed arbitrarily by droplet trajectory.

Furthermore, taking advantage of the inherent high-throughput capability of digital microfluidics, the strategic arrangement of multiple droplets within the DMF chip enables simultaneous multi-locus acoustic field regulation. Consequently, spatially distinct particle aggregation zones can be established in the fluid reservoir, as demonstrated in Fig. 4e, f. Precisely positioned droplets within the DMF chip, upon acoustic field activation, induce simultaneous particle accumulation directly above each controlled droplet. Microspheres cluster densely above droplets while exhibiting striped distribution patterns in intermediate regions. This phenomenon arises from the spatial extension of the modulated standing wave field throughout the reservoir.

The strong particle aggregation observed above individual droplets originates from the locally enhanced acoustic field produced by efficient acoustic transmission through the droplet, as previously demonstrated in the simulations in Fig. 2. Concurrently, acoustic emissions from multiple droplet positions undergo spatial superposition in inter-droplet regions, establishing regular standing wave patterns that yield the observed striped particle distributions illustrated in Fig. 4f. Figure 4g shows a comparison without droplets. Under prolonged exposure to the acoustic field, the particles also exhibit a striped distribution, though not as pronounced as in Fig. 4f. As shown in the simulation results in Fig. 2, acoustic energy is also transmitted into the fluid reservoir in this state, albeit at a much weaker level. The reflection of sound waves at the edges of the rectangular fluid reservoir creates a standing wave field, which drives the particles to form a striped distribution observed in the experiments.

Overall, this droplet-mediated acoustic field modulation enables particle trapping and transport functionalities similar to those of conventional acoustic tweezers, but with a fundamentally different field-control strategy. Conventional acoustic tweezers often use phased transducer arrays, whereas the present system accomplishes the dynamic modulation of the acoustic field at a fixed frequency with a single BAW device. The acoustic trapping location is instead controlled by droplet positioning through the DMF system, allowing rapid field reconfiguration with minimal hardware complexity. The demonstrated capability for real-time aggregation and transport of PS microspheres suggests direct applicability to biological applications such as targeted cell positioning and scalable assembly of cellular architectures for tissue engineering.

Deposited particle patterning

The dynamic acoustic field modulation method we present can manipulate not only suspended microparticles but also particles deposited at the bottom of the fluid reservoir. We verified this capability by actuating carbon particles sedimented on the reservoir bottom, which were prepared by grinding a graphite rod. Detailed experimental procedures are provided in Section S1 of the Supplementary Information. As shown in Fig. 5, carbon particles appear visually as dark regions. In the previous analysis of the forces acting on suspended particles, we employed the method developed by Gor’kov, which expresses the acoustic radiation force as the gradient of a potential function. However, this analytical method fundamentally relies on the Rayleigh assumption, where the particle radius must be much smaller than the acoustic wavelength (a≪λ), and neglects thermoviscous effects. Consequently, it is not suitable for accurately predicting the dynamics of large particles with dimensions comparable to the acoustic wavelength. To investigate the dynamic behavior of 500 μm particles in the modulated acoustic field, we employed a simulation approach based on a direct fluid-structure interaction (FSI) transient numerical model. This model thoroughly accounts for fluid compressibility and thermoviscous effects by fully coupling the acoustic pressure field, structural stress, and moving mesh deformation (Fig. 6a). Specifically, Fig. 6a presents a partial schematic of the established FSI simulation model. Because the incident acoustic excitation is harmonic, the loads exerted on the particle exhibit high-frequency cyclic alternations. As illustrated by the instantaneous pressure field at 6.55 μs (Fig. 6b), the localized pressure gradient generates a negative force (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${F}_{{{\mathrm{neg}}}}$$\end{document}Fneg) that pushes the particle leftward. Conversely, half an acoustic cycle later at 7.076 μs (Fig. 6c), the pressure distribution inverts, subjecting the particle to a rightward positive driving force (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${F}_{{{\mathrm{pos}}}}$$\end{document}Fpos). Within a single acoustic period, this alternating acoustic pressure causes the particle to undergo high-frequency reciprocating oscillations.

Crucially, despite this periodic oscillation, spatial gradients within the modulated non-uniform acoustic field break the symmetry of the particle’s dynamics during the positive and negative half-cycles. This asymmetry originates from the spatially varying acoustic pressure distribution created by the droplet-controlled transmission pathway, which results in unequal momentum transfer during the positive and negative half-cycles. Biological applications

To verify the potential of the integrated BAW-DMF platform for dynamic acoustic field modulation in biomedical applications, we manipulated biological samples in both suspended and deposited forms. For suspended biological samples, we selected three cell types: HeLa, A549, and Saccharomyces cerevisiae; for deposited biological samples, we selected shrimp eggs (Fig. 7). HeLa and A549 cells were stained with Calcein-AM, and their aggregation processes were observed in a single-droplet modulated acoustic field under a fluorescence microscope (Fig. 7a, b). The aggregated cells remained stably trapped above the control droplet and maintained normal cellular morphology, with cells tightly packed within the microclusters. We also observed the acoustic-controlled aggregation of Saccharomyces cerevisiae under bright-field microscopy. By controlling the movement of the droplet via DMF, the acoustic field changed synchronously, and the aggregated Saccharomyces cerevisiae cell clusters followed the control droplet as it moved. Similarly, by controlling the droplet movement via DMF, deposited biological samples can also be manipulated in a patterned manner. Conclusion

In this work, we have developed a DMF-based platform capable of dynamic and programmable modulation of acoustic fields for diverse particle manipulation. By controlling the number and arrangement of droplets, acoustic waves can be selectively transmitted to specific locations, thereby generating tailored acoustic fields. The droplets act as reconfigurable acoustic field modulation units. Their movement is precisely controlled by a DMF system, enabling dynamic modulation of the field distribution of a single BAW resonator without the need for mechanical drives or complicated phased-array systems. Acoustic transmission paths can be programmatically altered in real-time, overcoming the limitation of static or preconfigured acoustic field patterns and enabling rapid field reconfiguration and particle trajectory programming. This mechanism offers high flexibility in both temporal and spatial domains.

The platform’s flexibility and multifunctionality are demonstrated through two experimental scenarios: controlled aggregation and directional transfer of suspended polystyrene microspheres and three types of cells, and acoustic propulsion of deposited carbon particles and shrimp eggs to “write” customizable patterns. Moreover, the use of multiple droplets as independent control units further expands the degrees of freedom in field modulation, paving the way for high-throughput, parallel, and programmable acoustic manipulation.

By addressing the limitations of static field configurations, this DMF–BAW integration provides a compact and fully programmable acoustic manipulation solution. Beyond fundamental acoustic field control, this approach has significant potential for applications such as dynamic assembly of multicellular constructs, spatiotemporal stimulation of living tissues, and programmable delivery of functional materials in next-generation lab-on-a-chip and biomedical systems.
