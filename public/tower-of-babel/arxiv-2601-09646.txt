K.L. Helmes, R.H. Stockbridge, C. Zhu

Abstract
This paper analyzes and explicitly solves a class of long-term average impulse control problems and a related class of singular control problems. The underlying process is a general one-dimensional diffusion with appropriate boundary behavior. The model is motivated by applications such as the optimal long-term management of renewable resources and financial portfolio management. A large class of admissible policies is identified over which the agent seeks to maximize her long-term average reward, consisting of a running reward and income from either discrete impulses or singular actions. The long-term expected total reward and its relation to overtaking optimality is also considered. Sensitivity analysis with regard to the parameters of the impulse control model are performed. Key connections between the impulse and singular control problems are displayed. Keywords: Impulse control; singular control; long-term average reward; renewal theory; overtaking optimality; probabilistic cell problem; sensitivity. AMS 2020 subject classifications: 93E20, 60H30, 60J60

Introduction

This paper considers and explicitly solves both a long-term average stochastic impulse control problem and a related singular control problem, with particular emphasis on impulse control. Our motivation stems from two sources. The first is the applications in natural resource management, specifically in the context of optimal and sustainable harvesting strategies. The second is mathematical in nature. It concerns (a) the important but subtle interplay between two revenue streams, the incomes from a running reward and from intervention decisions, (b) the exploration of a direct approach to analyze such control problems over a large class of admissible policies using renewal theory and the renewal reward theorem, and (c) ways in which the impulse and singular problems are related. An additional motivation arises from the connection of the notion of optimality for an impulse problem with accumulated rewards to the concept of overtaking policies and their relation to an optimal policy for the long-term average problem.

Let us now introduce the underlying uncontrolled process. In the absence of controls, the dynamics of the state process – which may describe the evolution of some renewable natural resource – is modeled by a one-dimensional diffusion process on an interval
{\mathcal{I}}\subset\mathbb{R}

dX_{0}(t)=\mu(X_{0}(t))\,dt+\sigma(X_{0}(t))\,dW(t),\ \ X_{0}(0)=x_{0},

(1.1)

where
x_{0}\in{\mathcal{I}}
is an arbitrary but fixed point throughout the paper,
W
is a one-dimensional standard Brownian motion, and the drift and diffusion are given by the functions
\mu
and
\sigma
, respectively. The diffusion process is assumed to have certain boundary behaviors, which are consistent with growth models arising from important applications; see Condition
2.1
for details.

For the impulse control problems under consideration, the decision maker wants to specify when and by how much the state of the process should be reduced to achieve economic benefits. Her strategy is modeled by a policy
R:=\{(\tau_{k},Y_{k}),k=1,2,\dots\}
such that for each
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
\tau_{k}
is the time of the
k
th intervention and
Y_{k}
is the size of the intervention. The resulting controlled process
X^{R}
satisfies

X^{R}(t)=x_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}\mu(X^{R}(s))\,ds+\int_{0}^{t}\sigma(X^{R}(s))\,dW(s)-\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}I_{\{\tau_{k}\leq t\}}Y_{k},\qquad t\geq 0,

(1.2)

where we set
\tau_{0}=0
and
X^{R}(0-)=x_{0}\in{\mathcal{I}}
. In addition, since this paper is concerned with long-term average problems, we restrict ourselves to policies with
\lim_{k\to\infty}\tau_{k}=\infty
a.s.
With a view towards models involving mean field interactions, a fundamental quantity associated with each policy
R
is the long-term average supply rate of product to the market given by

\kappa^{R}:=\limsup_{t\to\infty}t^{-1}\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}I_{\{\tau_{k}\leq t\}}Y_{k}\Bigg].

(1.3)

One condition on the allowable policies is that
\kappa^{R}<\infty
. Based on the results of this paper, the solutions of mean field game and control adaptations are presented in
Helmes et al., 2026
.

The manager continually receives a subsidy based on the state of the process as well as earning income at discrete times when the state is reduced for economic gain. Let
c
be a running reward function and
\gamma>0
a scaling parameter. The function
\gamma\,c>0
gives the net reward after the associated running costs are taken into account. Let
p_{1}>0
be the gross price received per unit when a reduction in the state process is initiated. We assume that the costs for instantaneous reductions consist of both a fixed amount
K>0
for each action and a proportional cost with parameter
q_{1}<p_{1}
. Set
p=p_{1}-q_{1}>0
to be the net price per unit of reduction due to an intervention. Thus the reward functional for a policy
R
is the expected long-term average profit:

J(R;p,K,\gamma):=J(R):=\liminf_{t\to\infty}t^{-1}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\int_{0}^{t}\gamma\,c(X^{R}(s))\,ds+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}I_{\{\tau_{k}\leq t\}}(pY_{k}-K)\bigg].

(1.4)

In the context of harvesting problems, the function
\gamma\,c
can represent the utility derived from maintaining desirable state values
X^{R}(s)
at time
s
, as well as the state’s contribution to the overall ecosystem’s stability. For example, the function
\gamma\,c
can be used to model running carbon credits for managing large tracts of forest.

The positive fixed cost for an intervention in (
1.4
) implies that the optimal policy involves discrete interventions rather than continuous adjustments, ensuring effective product management while maximizing the overall profit rate.

Due to the presence of the nonnegative running reward rate
c
, the interplay between
c
and the mean production/growth rate
\mu
is one of the essential and important features of
the model and requires careful analysis. Although the case of a negative function
c
is typical in applications such as inventory control and industrial animal husbandry, a negative running or holding cost term in fact simplifies both the analysis and the characterization of optimal controls near the right boundary of the state space. Specifically, a negative
c
prizes interventions that keep the controlled process away from the right boundary, thereby avoiding complications associated with boundary behavior of the underlying diffusion. By contrast, a nonnegative running reward function
c
may encourage the manager to allow the process to approach the right boundary.

Although the impulse control model considered here resembles those analyzed in our prior work (e.g.,
Helmes et al., 2018
,
Helmes et al., 2025
), a crucial difference exists: we incorporate a positive running reward rather than a negative holding cost. This distinction, particularly when combined with an attracting natural right boundary, necessitates a careful re-examination of several fundamental results. Specifically, we identify conditions on the model under which a strategy analogous to an
(s,S)
-type ordering policy in the context of inventory control is optimal and adopt further conditions that guarantee the uniqueness of such a policy. The details are in Propositions
4.5
and
4.9
. (In inventory theory,
s
is the threshold at which orders are placed to raise the inventory to
S
; in this paper,
S
is the threshold at which the state is lowered to the reset level
s
. We adopt the notation
(s,S)=(w,y)
for this type of policy, see Definition
3.1
.)

Turning to the singular control problem, as before, the underlying uncontrolled process
X_{0}
satisfies (
1.1
). A (singular) control
Z
is a nondecreasing and right continuous process, with the resulting state process
X^{Z}
satisfying

X^{Z}(t)=x_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}\mu(X^{Z}(s))\,ds+\int_{0}^{t}\sigma(X^{Z}(s))dW(s)-Z(t),\quad t\geq 0.

(1.5)

The long-term average reward associated with the singular control
Z
is

\widehat{J}(Z;p,\gamma):=\widehat{J}(Z):=\liminf_{t\to\infty}t^{-1}\mathbb{E}_{x_{0}}\bigg[\int_{0}^{t}\gamma\,c(X^{Z}(s))\,ds+p\,Z(t)\bigg].

(1.6)

Observe that for a given impulse policy
R=\{(\tau_{k},Y_{k}),k=1,2,\dots\}
, we may define the nondecreasing, right continuous process
Z
by

Z(t)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}I_{\{\tau_{k}\leq t\}}Y_{k},\qquad t\geq 0,

in which case the two equations (
1.2
) and (
1.5
) describe the same controlled process
X^{R}=X^{Z}
. Thus the collection of singular policies contains the impulse policies.

Now briefly compare the singular long-term average payoff rate (
1.6
) with the similar quantity (
1.4
) for the impulse control problem for a given impulse policy
R
. Both problems have the same running reward. For each
k\in\mathbb{N}
, when the nondecreasing process
Z
has a jump discontinuity at time
\tau_{k}
,
\Delta Z(\tau_{k}):=Z(\tau_{k})-Z(\tau_{k}-)=Y_{k}
so the corresponding revenue generated at time
\tau_{k}
by the singular problem is
p\,Y_{k}
whereas for the same intervention the impulse problem also incurs the positive fixed cost
K
so generates the smaller revenue
p\,Y_{k}-K
. Intuitively, one can regard the singular control problem (
1.6
) as a limiting case of the impulse control problem (
1.4
) when the fixed cost
K
approaches zero. This intuition will be made precise and rigorously established in Section
7
.

To avoid penury, the impulse control problem with a fixed cost
K>0
avoids policies with infinitely many interventions in any finite time interval. In contrast, with
K=0
, the singular control problem allows for continuous control such as a local time process that reflects the state to prevent it from growing too large. The term
pZ(t)
then includes the total revenue earned from these infinitesimal reductions (up to time
t
) which result in the reflection of
X^{Z}
.

), and dividend payment and capital injection (

2
gives the precise model formulations and collects the key conditions used in later sections. In particular, a large class of admissible policies is defined by requiring a weak transversality condition to be satisfied, based on the fundamental quantities of the underlying model.
Section

introduces the class of

-impulse policies. Using a renewal argument for the impulse control problem, we then express the corresponding payoff (

defined in (

) and derive the analytical form of the controlled process’s stationary density in (

). Of particular significance, Section

The long-term average optimal impulse control problem is addressed in Section

. Conditions are given for the existence and uniqueness of an optimal

-policy. Furthermore, it is shown that the “impulse reward potential” function

)) along with the maximal value

). This, in turn, establishes the optimality of the corresponding

. Section

2
Formulation

In this section, we establish the models under consideration and collect some key conditions that will be used in later sections of the paper.

Dynamics.

Let
{\mathcal{I}}:=(a,b)\subset\mathbb{R}
with
a>-\infty
and
b\leq\infty
. In the absence of interventions, the process
X_{0}
satisfies (
1.1
) and is a regular diffusion with state space
{\mathcal{I}}
. The measurable functions
\mu
and
\sigma
are assumed to be such that a unique non-explosive weak solution to (
1.1
) exists; we refer to Section 5.5 of
Karatzas and Shreve, 1991
for details. For simplicity, we assume
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\{\mathcal{F}_{t}\},\mathbb{P})
is a filtered probability space with an
\{\mathcal{F}_{t}\}
-adapted Brownian motion
W
and on which
X_{0}
is defined, as well as each controlled process. In addition, we assume that
\sigma^{2}(x)>0
for all
x\in{\mathcal{I}}
. We closely follow the notation and terminology on boundary classifications of one-dimensional diffusions in Chapter 15 of
Karlin and Taylor, 1981
. The following standing assumption is imposed on the model throughout the paper:

Condition 2.1
.

(a)

Both the speed measure
M
and the scale function
S
of the process
X_{0}
are absolutely continuous with respect to Lebesgue measure. The scale and speed densities, respectively, are given by

s(x):=\exp\bigg\{-\int_{x_{0}}^{x}\frac{2\mu(y)}{\sigma^{2}(y)}dy\bigg\},\quad m(x)=\frac{2}{\sigma^{2}(x)s(x)},\quad x\in(a,b),

(2.1)

where
x_{0}\in{\mathcal{I}}
is as in (
1.1
) and is an arbitrary point, which will be held fixed.

(b)

The left boundary
a>-\infty
is a non-attracting point and the right boundary
b\leq\infty
is a natural point.
Moreover,

M[a,y]<\infty\text{ for each }y\in{\mathcal{I}},

(2.2)

and the potential function
\xi
defined by

\displaystyle\xi(x):=\int_{x_{0}}^{x}M[a,v]dS(v),\quad x\in{\mathcal{I}}

(2.3)

satisfies

\lim_{x\to b}\xi^{\prime}(x)=\lim_{x\to b}s(x)M[a,x]=\infty.

(2.4)

(c)

The function
\mu
is continuous on
{\mathcal{I}}
and extends continuously to the boundary points with
|\mu(a)|<\infty
.

Condition
2.1
(a) places restrictions on the model (
1.1
) which are quite natural for harvesting problems and other applications, such as in mathematical finance.
The assumption that
a>-\infty
is a non-attracting point implies that it cannot be attained in finite time by the uncontrolled diffusion. For growth models with
a=0
, this condition excludes the possibility of extinction. Likewise,
b\leq\infty
being a natural boundary prevents the state from exploding to
b
in finite time. Note that
a
can be either an entrance point or a natural point; the state space for
X_{0}
is respectively
{\cal E}=[a,b)
or
{\cal E}=(a,b)
.

Condition
2.1
(b,c) imposes further limitations on the model. For instance, the assumption that
|\mu(a)|<\infty
excludes the consideration of Bessel processes. In addition, the finiteness condition (
2.2
) always holds when
a
is an entrance boundary, and implies that the expected hitting times from
w
to
y
are finite whenever
a<w<y<b
. This requirement eliminates some diffusions when
a
is natural; see Table 6.2 on p. 234 of
Karlin and Taylor, 1981
.

The next proposition highlights implications of Condition
2.1
and introduces the useful identity (
2.5
), which will be crucial for later calculations.
The proof of Proposition
2.2
is placed in Appendix
A.1
to preserve the flow of the presentation.

Proposition 2.2
.

Assume Condition
2.1
holds. Then

(i)

\mu(a)\geq 0
. Moreover, if
\mu(a)>0
then
a
is an entrance point;

(ii)

for each
x\in{\mathcal{I}}
,

\int_{a}^{x}\mu(u)m(u)\,du=\int_{a}^{x}\mu(u)dM(u)=\frac{1}{s(x)};

(2.5)

(iii)

if
M[a,b]<\infty
, then
\langle\mu,\pi\rangle=0
in which
\pi(du)=\frac{dM(u)}{M[a,b]}
and
\langle\mu,\pi\rangle=\int\mu(u)\,\pi(du)
;

(iv)

if
M[a,b]=\infty
,
then
\mu(b)=0
.

Proposition
2.2
(iii) and (iv) have an intuitive interpretation. In (iii),
\pi
is the stationary distribution for the uncontrolled process
X_{0}
so this result means that the mean drift rate under
\pi
is
0
, which accords with the notion of stationarity. Somewhat similarly, the “stationary” distribution in (iv) is a unit point mass at
b
and again the result indicates the (degenerate) mean drift is
0
.

We now specify the class of admissible policies for the impulse control problem which, apart from the transversality condition when
a
is a natural boundary, is quite standard.

Definition 2.3

(Impulse Admissibility)
.

We say that
R:=\{(\tau_{k},Y_{k}),k=1,2,\dots\}
is an
admissible impulse policy
if

(i)

\{\tau_{k}\}
is an increasing sequence of
\{\mathcal{F}_{t}\}
-stopping times with
\lim_{k\to\infty}\tau_{k}=\infty
,

(ii)

for each
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
Y_{k}
is
\mathcal{F}_{\tau_{k}}
-measurable with
0<Y_{k}\leq X^{R}(\tau_{k}-)-a
when
\tau_{k}<\infty
, where equality is only allowed when
a
is an entrance boundary;

(iii)

X^{R}
satisfies (
1.2
) and we set
\tau_{0}=0
and
X^{R}(0-)=x_{0}\in{\mathcal{I}}
; and

(iv)

if

a
is a natural boundary, either

(a)

there exists an
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\tau_{N}=\infty
, which implies
\tau_{k}=\infty
for all
k\geq N
and, to completely specify the policy, we set
Y_{k}=0
for all
k\geq N
; or

(b)

\tau_{k}<\infty
for each
k\in\mathbb{N}
and, for the function
\xi
defined in (
2.3
), it holds that

\lim_{t\to\infty}\limsup_{n\to\infty}t^{-1}\mathbb{E}[\xi^{-}(X^{R}(t\wedge\beta_{n}))]=0,

(2.6)

where
\xi^{-}
denotes the negative part of the function
\xi
, and for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
\beta_{n}:=\inf\{t\geq 0:X^{R}(t)\notin(a_{n},b_{n})\}
, in which
\{a_{n}\}\subset{\mathcal{I}}
is a decreasing sequence with
a_{n}\to a
and
\{b_{n}\}\subset{\mathcal{I}}
is an increasing sequence with
b_{n}\to b
.

We denote by
{\mathcal{A}}_{\rm Imp}
the set of admissible impulse strategies.

Remark 2.4
.

An admissible impulse policy
R
satisfying Definition
2.3
(i), (ii), (iii), and (iv)(a) has a finite number of interventions or no intervention (corresponding to the case when
\tau_{1}=\infty
); the latter is called a “do-nothing” policy and is denoted by
\mathfrak{R}
. For convenience of later presentation, we denote by
\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{F}}
the set of admissible policies with at most a finite number of interventions.

On the other hand, if
R\in{\mathcal{A}}_{\rm Imp}
satisfies Definition
2.3
(i), (ii), (iii), and (iv)(b), the number of interventions is infinite; the set of such policies is denoted by
\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{I}}
. We have

{\mathcal{A}}_{\rm Imp}=\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{F}}\cup\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{I}}\quad\text{ and }\quad\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{F}}\cap\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{I}}=\emptyset.

Note that (
2.6
) is a transversality condition
imposed only on diffusions for which
a
is a natural boundary.
It is satisfied by the
(w,y)
-policies defined in Definition
3.1
; see Lemma
3.2
for details. When
a
is an entrance boundary, (
2.6
) is automatically satisfied because
\xi
is bounded below; see the proof of Lemma
4.4
for details. Finally we point out that the
\{\beta_{n}\}
sequence in (
2.6
) satisfies
\lim_{n\to\infty}\beta_{n}=\infty
a.s. since
a
is non-attracting and
b
is natural thanks to Condition
2.1
.

We emphasize that the admissible impulse policies defined in Definition
2.3
are not required to be of any particular type, such as a
(w,y)
-policy or a stationary policy. For example, while the class of
(w,y)
-policies belongs to the admissible set
{\mathcal{A}}_{\rm Imp}
, nonstationary policies that alternate between a finite number of such policies are also admissible. Requiring transversality in Definition
2.3
(iv)(b) for models in which
a
is a natural boundary is a weak condition that allows for a large class of admissible policies.

We now turn to the formulation of the rewards.

Reward Structures.
A running reward is earned at rate
\gamma\,c
, in which
c
depends on the state of the process and
\gamma>0
is a scale parameter.

For the impulse control problem, the reward is proportional to the size of the intervention, with the unit price
p=p_{1}-q_{1}>0
being the net price as described in the introduction. Each intervention also incurs a fixed cost
K>0
. Consequently, given the parameters
p
,
K
and
\gamma
, the long-term average reward for the product manager who adopts the strategy
R\in{\mathcal{A}}
is given by (
1.4
).

We assume that
c
satisfies the following condition.

Condition 2.5
.

The function
c:{\mathcal{I}}\mapsto\mathbb{R}_{+}
is continuous, increasing, and extends continuously at the endpoints, with
0\leq c(a)<c(b)<\infty
.

We now introduce an essential technical condition that connects the mean growth rate
\mu
to the subsidy rate
c
.

Condition 2.6
.

There exists some
\widehat{x}_{\mu,c}\in{\mathcal{I}}
so that
\mu
is strictly increasing on
(a,\widehat{x}_{\mu,c})
and the functions
c
and
\mu
are concave on
(\widehat{x}_{\mu,c},b)
.

It is also worth pointing out that the long-term average reward for every
R\in\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{F}}
is equal to that of the do-nothing policy
\mathfrak{R}
, which will be shown to be equal to
\bar{c}(b)
in (
3.11
). In addition, we have
\kappa^{R}=0
for every
R\in\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{F}}
.

To have a meaningful impulse problem, we require that the parameters
p
,
K
and
\gamma
be such that it is always beneficial to intervene with an impulse as compared to the do-nothing policy; Condition
4.1
captures this requirement in a functional form.

Now define the operators
A
and
B
that will be used often in the sequel. The generator of the process

between jumps (corresponding to the uncontrolled diffusion process

) is

Af:=\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}f^{\prime\prime}+\mu f^{\prime}=\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dM}(\frac{df}{dS}),

(2.7)

where
f\in C^{2}({\mathcal{I}})
. Define the set
\mathcal{R}:=\{(w,y)\in{\cal E}\times{\cal E}:w<y\}
. For any function
f:{\cal E}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
and
(w,y)\in\mathcal{R}
,

Bf(w,y):=f(y)-f(w).

(2.8)

The jump operator

captures the effect of an instantaneous impulse.

\displaystyle g(x)

Finally, the following proposition provides probabilistic representations for the differences of the functions of

Proposition 2.7

Definition 3.1

(
(w,y)
-Policies)
.

Let

and set

and

(w,y)
-impulse policy

, such that for

The definition of

to allow for the first jump to occur at time 0 when

Under this policy, the impulse controlled process

immediately resets to the level
w
at the time it would reach (or initially exceed) the threshold
y
. For simplicity, this type of policy will be called a
(w,y)
-policy, with the fact that it consists of impulses being understood from the notation.

Observe that all interventions, except possibly the first, have a reduction of size
y-w
and an expected cycle length of
\xi(y)-\xi(w)
. Using a renewal argument as in
Helmes et al., 2018
,
Helmes et al., 2025

is

\lim_{t\to\infty}t^{-1}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}I_{\{\tau_{k}\leq t\}}\right]=\frac{1}{\xi(y)-\xi(w)}

and hence the long-term average supply rate is

\kappa^{R^{(w,y)}}=\lim_{t\to\infty}t^{-1}\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}I_{\{\tau_{k}\leq t\}}(X^{(w,y)}(\tau_{k}-)-X^{(w,y)}(\tau_{k}))\Bigg]=\frac{y-w}{\xi(y)-\xi(w)}=:{\mathfrak{z}}(w,y).

(3.2)

Furthermore, the renewal argument shows that the long-term average reward (
1.4
) is given by the ratio of the expected net rewards in a cycle to the expected cycle length. This means that when the parameters are
p
,
K
and
\gamma
, the value is
J(R^{(w,y)};p,K,\gamma)=F(w,y;p,K,\gamma)
, where

F(w,y;p,K,\gamma)=F(w,y):=\frac{p(y-w)-K+\gamma(g(y)-g(w))}{\xi(y)-\xi(w)},\quad(w,y)\in\mathcal{R}.

(3.3)

Notice that
F
is comprised of three terms. The first is the price
p
times the long-term average supply rate
\mathfrak{z}(w,y)
. The second term,
\frac{K}{\xi(y)-\xi(w)}
, is a reduction by the equivalent expected rate at which the fixed cost would be continually assessed during the cycle. The third term is the fraction
\frac{\gamma(g(y)-g(w))}{\xi(y)-\xi(w)}
, which is the expected running reward during a cycle divided by the expected cycle length.

Using the elegant renewal argument presented in Chapter 15, Section 8 of
Karlin and Taylor, 1981
, or the analytical argument in
Helmes et al., 2017

R^{(w,y)}
induces an invariant measure having density
\nu(x;w,y)
on
{\cal E}
, where

\nu(x;w,y)=\nu(x):=\begin{cases}\varrho\,m(x)\,S[w,y]&\text{ if }x\leq w,\\
\varrho\,m(x)\,S[x,y]&\text{ if }w<x\leq y,\\
0&\text{ if }x>y,\end{cases}

(3.4)

and
\varrho=(\int_{a}^{w}m(x)S[w,y]dx+\int_{w}^{y}m(x)S[x,y]dx)^{-1}
is the normalizing constant. In view of the second equality of (
2.13
), we have
\varrho=(\xi(y)-\xi(w))^{-1}
. Furthermore, using (
2.14
) and the definition of
\nu(x)
, we can write

g(y)-g(w)=\int_{w}^{y}c(u)S[u,y]\,dM(u)+S[w,y]\int_{a}^{w}c(u)\,dM(u)=\varrho^{-1}\int_{a}^{b}c(x)\nu(x)\,dx.

Similarly, with the aid of (
2.5
), straightforward computations leads to

\displaystyle y-w

\displaystyle=\int_{w}^{y}\bigg(\int_{a}^{x}\mu(u)m(u)du\bigg)s(x)dx=\varrho^{-1}\int_{a}^{b}\mu(u)\nu(u)du.

Consequently, the long-term average supply rate (
3.2
) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\mathfrak{z}(w,y)=\frac{y-w}{\xi(y)-\xi(w)}

\displaystyle=\int_{a}^{b}\mu(u)\nu(u)du,

and the long-term average running reward rate is

\displaystyle\frac{\gamma(g(y)-g(w))}{\xi(y)-\xi(w)}

\displaystyle=\int_{a}^{b}\gamma\,c(u)\,\nu(u)\,du.\rule{0.0pt}{22.0pt}

Hence the long-term average reward rate (

) of the policy
R^{(w,y)}
has the representation

F(w,y)=\frac{p(y-w)-K+\gamma(g(y)-g(w))}{\xi(y)-\xi(w)}=\int_{a}^{b}[r(x)-K\varrho]\nu(x)\,dx,

where

r(x;p,\gamma)=r(x):=\gamma c(x)+p\mu(x).

(3.5)

Note that the factor

in the integrand gives a combined rate of reward when the process is in state
x

gives the stationary profit rate. Notice also that the long-term average supply rate
{\mathfrak{z}}(w,y)
is the mean drift under the stationary distribution.

4.3
Probabilistic Cell Problem and Overtaking Optimality

We briefly digress from the long-term average problem (
1.4
) to consider other approaches to infinite-horizon impulse control. For notational simplicity, for any
R\in{\mathcal{A}}
, we define the total expected reward up to time
T

\displaystyle J_{T}(x;R):=\mathbb{E}_{x}\bigg[\int_{0}^{T}\gamma c(X(s))\,ds+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}I_{\{\tau_{k}\leq T\}}(p(X(\tau_{k}-)-X(\tau_{k}))-K)\bigg],

where
x\in{\mathcal{I}}
and
T>0
. Often one wishes to maximizes the performance functional over a large time horizon; i.e., to maximize
\liminf_{T\to\infty}J_{T}(x;R)
over all admissible policies
R
. However, as the running reward function
c
is nonnegative, such a problem may be ill-posed in the sense that
\liminf_{T\to\infty}J_{T}(x;R)=\infty
for many
R\in{\mathcal{A}}
. To address this issue, we consider a modified performance criterion in which a linear function of time is subtracted from
J_{T}(x;R)
. More specifically,
we consider the infinite-horizon optimization problem of finding an income rate
\lambda
and a finite-valued function
V
which satisfies

V(x):=\sup_{R\in{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathbb{T}}}\liminf_{T\to\infty}[J_{T}(x;R)-\lambda T],\qquad\forall x\in{\mathcal{I}},

(4.20)

where
{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathbb{T}}
is the class of all
(w,y)
-policies introduced in Definition
3.1
; a mnemonic for
\mathbb{T}
is that these are thresholds policies. With
\lambda T
serving as a benchmark, we can think of (
4.20
) as a maximization problem for the long-term accumulated relative reward under impulse control. Further, we seek an optimal policy
R\in{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathbb{T}}
which achieves the maximal values for every
x\in{\mathcal{I}}
.

Such a problem is motivated by the so-called probabilistic cell problem studied in the context of LQG problem; see, for example,
Bayraktar and Jian, 2025
,
Jian et al., 2024
. Similar to the LQG setting, in which the probabilistic cell problem is related to the ergodic Bellman equation, we demonstrate that for the impulse control model studied in this paper, the solution to (
4.20
) is closely connected to the function
G
defined in (
4.10
). In fact, recall that
(G,F^{*})
solves the QVI (
4.12
) and that
F^{*}
is the optimal value for the long-term average problem (
1.4
).
Proposition
4.10
shows that if
a
is an entrance boundary, then the value function
V
of (
4.20
) is given by the impulse reward potential
G
, adjusted by its mean under the invariant measure resulting from the optimal
(w^{*},y^{*})
-policy.

The rate
\lambda
in (
4.20
) cannot be chosen arbitrarily. To see this, assume the conditions of Proposition
4.9
hold, let
F^{*}=F(w^{*},y^{*})
be the resulting optimal value and
R^{*}=R^{(w^{*},y^{*})}
be the optimal policy. If
\lambda<F^{*}
, then

\displaystyle\liminf_{T\to\infty}[J_{T}(x,R^{*})-\lambda T]=\infty,\quad\forall x\in{\mathcal{I}}.

On the other hand, if
\lambda>F^{*}
, then for any admissible policy
R\in{\mathcal{A}}
, we have

\displaystyle\liminf_{T\to\infty}[J_{T}(x,R)-\lambda T]=-\infty.

Therefore, the only reasonable choice is
\lambda=F^{*}
.

Proposition 4.10
.

Assume Conditions
2.1
,
2.5
,
2.6
, and
4.1
hold and that
a
is an entrance boundary. Then the solution to problem (
4.20
) is the triplet
(F^{*},G(\cdot)-\langle G,\nu^{R^{*}}\rangle,R^{*})
, where
F^{*}=F(w^{*},y^{*})
in which
(w^{*},y^{*})
is the unique maximizing pair for the function
F
of (
3.3
),
R^{*}=R^{(w^{*},y^{*})}
,
\nu^{R^{*}}
is the invariant measure induced by
R^{*}
. Therefore the value function
V=G-\langle G,\nu^{R^{*}}\rangle
is the deviation of the impulse reward potential from its mean under
\nu^{R^{*}}
.

Proof.

We have observed
in Proposition
4.5
that the policy
R^{*}
is optimal for (
1.4
) with optimal payoff
F^{*}
. We now show that the value function
V(\cdot)
of (
4.20
) is given by
G(\cdot)-\langle G,\nu^{R^{*}}\rangle
and that
R^{*}
is also optimal for (
4.20
). In view of (
3.3
) and Proposition
4.9
, for any
R\in{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathbb{T}}
with
R^{(w,y)}\neq R^{*}
, we have

.
Therefore it follows that

\displaystyle\liminf_{T\to\infty}\big[J_{T}(x,R^{(w,y)})-F^{*}T\big]=-\infty.

(4.21)

On the other hand, for the
R^{*}
-policy, denoting by
X
the controlled process, using the same calculations as those in deriving (
4.1
), we obtain

\displaystyle J_{T}(x;R^{*})-F^{*}T=G(x)-\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}_{x}[G(X(T\wedge\beta_{n}))].

Since

is an entrance boundary, as observed in the proof of Lemma

, the function

is uniformly bounded from below. On the other hand, since the process

is bounded above by

, the random variables

are uniformly bounded. Hence

. Using this observation in the above displayed equation yields

\displaystyle\liminf_{T\to\infty}[J_{T}(x;R^{*})-F^{*}T]=G(x)-\langle G,\nu^{R^{*}}\rangle.

(4.22)

A combination of (

) and (

) yields

∎

Remark 4.11
.

Now consider problem (
4.20
) over a larger class of impulse control policies, namely,
{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathrm{S}}
, the class of all admissible policies
R
for which the controlled process
X^{R}
has a stationary distribution, then it is not clear whether the value function
V
and an optimal policy
R
remain the same as those over
{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathbb{T}}
.

Suppose
R\in{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathrm{S}}
is arbitrary and denote by
\nu^{R}
the invariant measure induced by
R
. If the long-term average reward of
R
is less than
F^{*}
, then using similar calculations as those preceding Proposition
4.10
, we have

\liminf_{T\to\infty}[J_{T}(x;R)-F^{*}T]=-\infty.
On the other hand, if the long-term average reward of
R
equals
F^{*}
, then for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
T>0
, with
\beta_{n}
similarly defined as in Definition
2.3
, using the same calculations as those in deriving (
4.1
), we have

\displaystyle\limsup_{T\to\infty}[J_{T}(x;R)-F^{*}T]

\displaystyle\leq G(x)-\liminf_{T\to\infty}\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}_{x}[G(X(T\wedge\beta_{n}))]

\displaystyle\leq G(x)-\liminf_{T\to\infty}\mathbb{E}_{x}[G(X(T))]

\displaystyle\leq G(x)-\langle G,\nu^{R}\rangle,

where the second inequality follows from Fatou’s lemma and the assumption that
a
is an entrance boundary, which ensures that
G
is bounded from below; which together with the continuity of
G
and the Portmanteau theorem imply the last inequality. Therefore it follows that

\displaystyle V(x)\leq G(x)-\inf_{R\in{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathrm{S}}:J(R)=F^{*}}\langle G,\nu^{R}\rangle.

(4.23)

Since it is not clear whether the above inequality is in fact an equality, we leave this issue as an interesting topic for future research.

We now turn to another perspective on impulse control over an infinite horizon: overtaking optimality. This important concept in infinite-horizon optimization was first introduced in
Ramsey, 1928
, with a weaker formulation later provided in
von Weizsäcker, 1965
and others. Following the terminology in
Huang et al., 2021
, we say that an admissible policy
\widehat{R}\in{\mathcal{A}}
is overtaking optimal starting from
x\in{\mathcal{I}}
if for each
R\in{\mathcal{A}}
, we have

\displaystyle\limsup_{T\to\infty}[J_{T}(x;R)-J_{T}(x,\widehat{R})]\leq 0.

Corollary 4.12
.

Assume Conditions
2.1
,
2.5
,
2.6
, and
4.1
hold. Then the

-policy is overtaking optimal in

starting from each

Proof.

The Singular Control Problem

This section is devoted to the singular control problem (

Definition 5.1

(Singular Admissibility)
.

A process
Z=\{Z(t),t\geq 0\}
is an
admissible singular control
if it is nondecreasing, right-continuous, and
\{\mathcal{F}_{t}\}
-adapted process with
Z(0-)=0
, the controlled state process

satisfies (

for all

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}\limsup_{n\to\infty}t^{-1}\mathbb{E}[\xi^{-}(X^{Z}(t\wedge\beta_{n}))]=0,

(5.1)

where
\beta_{n}:=\inf\{t\geq 0:X^{Z}(t)\notin(a_{n},b_{n})\}
, with
\{a_{n}\}
and
\{b_{n}\}
as in Definition
2.3
(iv)(b). Denote the set of admissible singular controls by
{\mathcal{A}}_{\rm Sing}
.

We introduce the important subclass of reflection policies.

Definition 5.2

(

-Reflection Policies)
.

For each
x\in{\mathcal{I}}
, let

denote the local time process at
x
of the uncontrolled process
X_{0}
. Define the

-reflection policy

by

Z_{x}(t)=(x_{0}-x)^{+}+L_{x}(t),\qquad t\geq 0.

The policy

keeps the controlled process

within
[a,x]
by reflection at
x
; if
x_{0}>x
, an initial jump to

occurs at time

.

Lemma 5.3
.

For each
x\in{\mathcal{I}}
, the singular policy
Z_{x}
is admissible in the sense of Definition
5.1
. Moreover, the following assertions are true:

(a)

the long-term average reward for the reflection policy

is

\widehat{J}(Z_{x})=\int_{a}^{b}r(u)\pi_{x}(u)\,du,

(5.2)

in which

is given by (

\displaystyle\pi_{x}(u)=\frac{m(u)}{M[a,x]}I_{[a,x]}(u);

(5.3)

(b)

\lim_{x\to a}\widehat{J}(Z_{x})=r(a)=\gamma c(a)+p\mu(a)
and

\lim_{x\to b}\widehat{J}(Z_{x})=\gamma\bar{c}(b)
.

Remark 5.4
.

The value of
\widehat{J}(Z_{x})
of (

given in (

for each

can be interpreted as the long-term average reward of the

-reflection policy for the singular control problem. In particular,

-reflection policy for the singular problem and also for the

-reflection policy when

Proof.

It is easy to see that
Z_{x}
is a nondecreasing, right-continuous, and
\{\mathcal{F}_{t}\}
-adapted process
with

. Referring to Section 5 of Chapter 15 in

on

.
Then, using the ergodicity for linear diffusions in Chapter II, Section 6 of

Using the invariant density

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}t^{-1}\mathbb{E}_{x_{0}}\bigg[\int_{0}^{t}\gamma c(X^{Z_{x}}(s))ds\bigg]=\int_{a}^{b}\gamma c(u)\pi_{x}(u)\,du.

\displaystyle 0

Proposition 5.7

Assume Conditions

,

The validity of Lemma

\displaystyle U(x)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}h(\widehat{y})\xi(x)-\gamma g(x),&\text{ if }x\in(a,\widehat{y}],\\

.

Now consider the singular control problem from an operational viewpoint. When there is no positive fixed cost, the optimal singular control policy is

is that we can approximate the optimal reflection policy

by an

8
Conclusions and Remarks

This paper presents a comprehensive analysis of a class of long-term average impulse control problems having two sources of income, together with their closely related singular control formulations. Working within a general one-dimensional diffusion framework with boundary conditions motivated by applications, we derive explicit solutions and characterized the optimal controls for both problems. Our findings rigorously establish an intrinsic connection between the two control formulations, with the singular control solution emerging as a natural limit of the impulse control problem as fixed costs vanish. It also identifies the penalty incurred on the optimal impulse value (relative to the optimal singular value) by the presence of the positive fixed cost
K
. This penalty includes a reduction due to the restriction to the subclass of impulse controls along with the long-term average reduction arising directly from the fixed costs.

The paper conducts a detailed sensitivity analysis of the impulse control solution with respect to key parameters, providing practical guidance for applications, for example by identifying parameters that require particularly accurate estimation. The related long-term total reward criterion and its connection to overtaking optimality are also explored. The explicit nature of the solutions makes the results directly applicable to canonical problems in areas such as renewable resource and portfolio management, where decision makers must balance continuous subsidies against the income from interventions.

The explicit solutions obtained here provide a solid foundation for the study of long-term average impulse control with mean field interactions in the companion paper
Helmes et al., 2026
.

Several avenues for future research remain. Some of the technical conditions and assumptions imposed in this paper may be relaxed. Moreover, the framework could be extended to more general state processes, including those with McKean-Vlasov dynamics, to risk-sensitive objective functions, or to an exploration of the connections between long-term average, discounted and finite-time horizon problems, homogenization theory, and turnpike properties in the context of impulse and singular controls.
