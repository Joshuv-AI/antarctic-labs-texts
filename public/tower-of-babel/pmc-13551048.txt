Elevated risk of ischemic stroke after invasive cancer diagnosis: A nationwide population‐based study
Authors: Carmit Libruder, David Tanne, Barbara G Silverman, Binyamin Binyaminy, Rita Dichtiar, Lital Keinan‐Boker
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCancer and stroke are major causes of morbidity and mortality, with an increasing shared burden. This study aimed to provide population‐based estimates of ischemic stroke (IS) risk after cancer diagnosis across multiple cancer sites and demographic subgroups.MethodsA retrospective cohort study was conducted linking the Israel National Cancer and Stroke Registries. Residents ≥40 years old diagnosed with first invasive cancer during 2014–2021 were followed for first‐ever IS within 1 year. IS risk was assessed using standardized incidence ratios (SIRs) and analyzed by cancer site, time since diagnosis, cancer stage, and demographic subgroups. One‐year cumulative incidence was estimated with death as a competing event.ResultsAmong 182,221 patients, the SIR for IS within 1 year after cancer diagnosis was 1.8 (95% confidence interval [CI], 1.7–1.9). Risk peaked during the first 3 months (SIR, 2.5; 95% CI, 2.2–2.7). The highest SIRs were observed for pancreatic (SIR, 5.9; 95% CI, 4.8–7.3) and lung (SIR, 3.9; 95% CI, 3.4–4.4) cancers. SIRs decreased with age from 3.6 (95% CI, 2.8–4.6) among adults 40–54 years old to 1.4 (95% CI, 1.3–1.6) among those ≥75 years old. SIRs increased with stage, from 1.2 (95% CI, 1.0–1.4) in localized disease to 2.8 (95% CI, 2.5–3.2) in metastatic disease. One‐year cumulative incidence of IS was 0.62%, ranging from 0.30% in breast cancer to 1.62% in pancreatic cancer.ConclusionsThe early period after cancer diagnosis represents a critical window of increased IS risk. Incorporating targeted cerebrovascular risk assessment into oncologic care may facilitate earlier identification of high‐risk subgroups and support tailored prevention strategies.Keywords: cancer, cancer‐associated stroke, cerebrovascular disease, cohort study, ischemic stroke, population‐based study

INTRODUCTION

Cancer and stroke are among the leading contributors to the global burden of chronic disease, each associated with high mortality and substantial disability‐adjusted life years (DALYs) lost due to premature death and long‐term disability. 1 , 2 , 3 Approximately 40% of individuals are expected to be diagnosed with a malignancy during their lifetime, 4 and nearly one in four will experience a stroke. 5 An estimated 10% of stroke cases occur among individuals with comorbid cancer. 6 With an aging population and improving cancer survival, 7 the number of individuals experiencing stroke in the context of active or recent cancer is expected to rise. 8

Systematic reviews and meta‐analyses have consistently demonstrated an increased risk of stroke following cancer diagnosis, particularly early after diagnosis. 9 , 10 , 11 Overall estimates suggest a 1.7‐ to 2.6‐fold increase compared with cancer‐free controls, 9 , 10 with substantial variation across cancer sites and follow‐up periods. 9 , 10 , 11 Large population‐based studies have corroborated this association. 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 A Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER)‐Medicare analysis of older adults reported an approximately 2‐fold higher risk of ischemic stroke (IS) within 6 months after newly diagnosed cancer. 13 A US prospective cohort study also reported a marked increase in stroke risk within 30 days after cancer diagnosis, although stroke was not restricted to ischemic events. 17 More recently, an Ontario population‐based matched cohort reported a 1.4‐fold higher risk of IS within 1.5 years after cancer diagnosis, restricted to individuals without prior IS. 18

Despite this growing evidence base, the site‐specific magnitude and timing of first‐ever IS risk after cancer diagnosis remains incompletely characterized. Prior studies have varied in age eligibility, health care setting, cancer‐site coverage, outcome definitions, and analytic approaches and have not consistently examined differences by stage or demographic characteristics. Moreover, site‐specific cumulative incidence estimates remain limited, restricting assessment of the clinical burden of IS after cancer diagnosis. These patterns are clinically relevant, as IS occurring in the context of cancer is often more severe, 19 with a higher likelihood of recurrence 20 and increased mortality. 21

In this nationwide registry‐based study, we used linked data from population‐based cancer and stroke registries within a universal health care system to estimate first‐ever IS risk after invasive cancer diagnosis. We estimated standardized incidence ratios (SIRs) relative to general population rates across a broad range of cancer sites, stratified by time since cancer diagnosis, stage at diagnosis, and demographic subgroups. We further calculated 1‐year cumulative incidence with death treated as a competing event. By integrating relative and absolute measures, this study provides a comprehensive assessment of early first‐ever IS risk after cancer diagnosis and helps identify cancer sites and patient subgroups at particularly high risk.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

We conducted a retrospective, population‐based cohort study using data from two national registries in Israel: the Israel National Cancer Registry (INCR) and the Israel National Stroke Registry (INSR).

The INCR, established in 1960 with mandatory reporting since 1982, provides nationwide coverage of incident malignant neoplasms (excluding nonmelanoma skin cancers), carcinoma in situ, and benign tumors of the brain and central nervous system. Tumors are coded according to the International Classification of Diseases for Oncology, Third Edition (ICD‐O‐3), and stage is assigned using the SEER Summary Staging Manual. 22 Registry completeness is high, with estimates of 96.8% for invasive solid tumors and 88.0% for hematopoietic and lymphoid neoplasms. 23

The INSR, established in 2014, captures all Israeli residents ≥18 years old hospitalized with acute stroke or transient ischemic attack. 24 Reporting to the registry is mandatory, and case identification is based on ICD‐9 discharge codes. Validation studies have demonstrated high positive predictive values for stroke identification (95%) and subtype classification (96.5%). 25

The study population included Israeli residents ≥40 years old with a first primary invasive cancer recorded in the INCR between January 1, 2014, and December 31, 2021. Analyses were restricted to individuals ≥40 years old because IS events were uncommon at younger ages, limiting statistical power. Individuals identified solely through death certificates were excluded, as were those with benign tumors, tumors of uncertain behavior, or carcinoma in situ; the latter is noninvasive and classified separately from localized invasive cancer. 22

We restricted the outcome to IS events to maintain clinical and etiologic homogeneity. IS events within 1 year of cancer diagnosis were identified by linking the INCR and INSR using encrypted personal identification numbers and were defined according to ICD‐9 discharge codes used by the INSR for IS (434.xx, 433.x1, 436, and 997.02). First‐ever IS was defined by excluding patients with any documented prior ischemic or hemorrhagic stroke before cohort entry. Prior stroke history was ascertained by the INSR using registry records from 2014 onward and linked national hospital discharge records for earlier events, based on registry‐defined ICD‐9 stroke diagnosis codes.

Statistical analysis

The risk of first‐ever IS following cancer diagnosis was assessed using SIRs, defined as the ratio of observed to expected IS cases. The observed cases included all first‐ever IS events occurring between 2014 and 2022 among individuals with a first primary invasive cancer identified between 2014 and 2021. Person‐time was accrued from cancer diagnosis until the earliest of first‐ever IS, death from any cause, or completion of 1 year of follow‐up.

Expected IS cases were estimated by applying general population incidence rates stratified by age (5‐year intervals), sex (male and female), population group (Jewish and Arab), and calendar year to the corresponding person‐time in the cancer cohort. Five‐year age intervals were used for standardization, whereas age‐stratified analyses used broader age groups (40–54, 55–74, and ≥75 years). Incidence rates were based on first‐ever IS cases from the INSR and population denominators from annual files of the Israel Central Bureau of Statistics. Ninety‐five percent confidence intervals (95% CIs) were calculated assuming a Poisson distribution.

SIRs were estimated overall and by cancer site. Unless otherwise specified, SIRs were standardized by age, sex, and population group. Stratified analyses were standardized by the remaining variables. Temporal variation was assessed using nonoverlapping time intervals since cancer diagnosis (0–3, 3–6, and 6–12 months). In an exploratory extended follow‐up analysis, SIRs beyond the first year and through 3 years after cancer diagnosis were estimated using successive nonoverlapping 6‐month intervals, with administrative censoring on December 31, 2022, the end of available IS follow‐up.

Stage‐specific analyses were conducted according to cancer stage at diagnosis (localized, regional, and metastatic). In an exploratory morphology analysis, cancers were grouped into major histologic morphology categories using ICD‐O‐3 morphology codes, and SIRs were estimated by morphology group and within selected cancer sites with clinically interpretable morphology subgroups.

Subgroup comparisons with more than 10 observed events used Poisson regression with a log link and the expected number of cases as an offset. For subgroups with sparse data, exact conditional binomial tests were used. Trends across ordered categories were assessed by modeling the ordinal variable in Poisson regression.

As a complementary analysis, we assessed the 1‐year cumulative incidence of first‐ever IS after cancer diagnosis to provide absolute risk estimates, overall and by cancer site, accounting for death as a competing event. For a general‐population reference, we estimated an expected cumulative incidence curve by applying national rates of first‐ever IS and all‐cause mortality, stratified by age, sex, population group, and calendar year, to the cancer cohort's person‐time.

All analyses were performed using SAS software, version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc, Cary, North Carolina, USA).

The cohort contributed 162,593.4 person‐years of follow‐up, during which 1125 first‐ever IS events occurred.

SIRs varied markedly by cancer site. The highest excess risk was observed for pancreatic and lung cancers, with nearly 6‐fold (SIR, 5.9; 95% CI, 4.8–7.3) and 4‐fold (SIR, 3.9; 95% CI, 3.4–4.4) elevations, respectively. Gallbladder and esophageal cancers also showed pronounced excess risk (SIRs of 3.6 and 4.1, respectively). However, these estimates were based on a limited number of events.

Two‐ to 3‐fold elevations were observed for bladder, stomach, myeloid leukemia, liver, and malignant brain and nervous system tumors. More modest but statistically significant elevations were observed for colorectal, corpus uteri, multiple myeloma, lymphoid leukemia, and kidney cancers. In contrast, no excess IS risk was observed for breast and prostate cancers (Table 2).

Exploratory morphology analyses showed elevated SIRs for several major morphology groups. Within selected cancer sites, comparisons were limited by the predominance of a single morphology group or by overlapping CIs (Tables S3a and S3b).

SIRs for first‐ever IS by time since cancer diagnosis

The overall SIR peaked during the first 3 months after cancer diagnosis (SIR, 2.5; 95% CI, 2.2–2.7) and declined thereafter (SIR, 1.6; 95% CI, 1.4–1.8 at 3–6 months and SIR, 1.5; 95% CI, 1.3–1.6 at 6–12 months). Across cancer sites, significant temporal gradients were observed for colorectal, lung, pancreatic, multiple myeloma, ovarian, and connective and soft tissue cancers (p for trend <.05), with borderline evidence for bladder, corpus uteri, and kidney cancers (p = .06). For several cancer sites, statistically significant SIR elevations were confined to the first 3 months after diagnosis (Table S4). Lung and pancreatic cancers had the highest early SIRs and remained elevated throughout the first year (Figure 2).

SIRs for first‐ever IS by demographic characteristics

Overall SIRs were similar across sex and population groups, with estimates of 1.8 (95% CI, 1.7–1.9) in males and 1.8 (95% CI, 1.6–1.9) in females, and 1.8 (95% CI, 1.7–1.9) in Jewish individuals compared with 1.9 (95% CI, 1.5–2.2) in Arab individuals. At the cancer‐site level, subgroup differences were uncommon. For a few sites, SIR elevations reached statistical significance in only one subgroup, but between‐group differences were not significant. Full sex‐stratified site‐specific estimates are shown in Table S6a. Power for site‐specific population‐group comparisons was limited by the small number of events among Arab individuals.

An inverse age gradient was observed, with overall SIRs declining from 3.6 (95% CI, 2.8–4.6) among individuals 40–54 years old to 1.4 (95% CI, 1.3–1.6) among those ≥75 years old (p for trend <.001) (Figure 3A). Similar patterns were observed for several cancer sites. For lung cancer, the SIR declined from 13.0 (95% CI, 7.3–21.4) in individuals 40–54 years old to 2.2 (95% CI, 1.7–2.8) in those ≥75 years old, and for pancreatic cancer, from 18.1 (95% CI, 6.6–39.4) to 4.0 (95% CI, 2.7–5.7). Full age‐stratified site‐specific estimates are provided in Table S6b.

SIRs for first‐ever IS by stage at cancer diagnosis

A stage‐related gradient in IS risk was observed. Overall SIRs increased with advancing stage, from 1.2 (95% CI, 1.0–1.4) in localized disease to 2.8 (95% CI, 2.5–3.2) in metastatic disease (p for trend <.001) (Figure 3B). Site‐specific analyses revealed similar patterns for selected cancer sites, although stage information was missing for nearly half of cases, limiting precision of site‐specific estimates. For lung cancer, SIRs increased from 1.2 (95% CI, 0.7–2.1) in localized disease to 7.5 (95% CI, 5.8–9.6) in metastatic disease. For pancreatic cancer, SIRs increased from 1.2 (95% CI, 0.1–4.4) to 10.4 (95% CI, 6.8–15.1). Full stage‐stratified site‐specific estimates are provided in Table S7.

Cumulative incidence of first‐ever IS overall and by cancer site

The 1‐year cumulative incidence of first‐ever IS was 0.62% (95% CI, 0.58–0.65), compared with an expected 0.40% (95% CI, 0.37–0.42) based on national rates, corresponding to approximately six versus four events per 1000 patients. Risk varied substantially across cancer sites, ranging from 0.30% in breast cancer to 1.62% in pancreatic cancer, corresponding to approximately 3 to 16 events per 1000 patients (Table 2). The cumulative incidence curves for both IS and death increased most steeply during the first 3 months, with IS remaining markedly lower than death throughout the first year (Figure 4).

DISCUSSION

In this nationwide population‐based study, patients with cancer had a nearly 2‐fold excess risk of first‐ever IS within the first year after diagnosis, compared with the general population. SIRs varied substantially by cancer site, were highest during the first 3 months, and attenuated thereafter. An inverse age gradient was observed, with markedly higher SIRs in younger compared with older adults. A stage‐related gradient was also evident, with SIRs increasing from localized to metastatic disease. Cumulative incidence analyses accounting for competing mortality showed broadly similar site‐specific and temporal patterns, supporting the robustness of the findings.

Previous large‐scale studies from the United States, 12 , 13 Denmark, 15 and Sweden 16 have similarly reported elevated relative measures of IS risk following cancer diagnosis, although estimates varied in magnitude and duration. One‐year cumulative incidence estimates of IS after cancer diagnosis have likewise varied substantially, from 0.42% in a retrospective single‐center study from Japan 26 to 1.3% in a systematic review 9 and 4.3% in a SEER‐Medicare cohort of older adults. 13 This variability likely reflects differences in cancer‐site composition, age eligibility, and analytic approach, supporting closer examination of site‐specific patterns.

In our study, lung and pancreatic cancers were associated with the highest IS risk, with nearly 4‐fold and 6‐fold increases respectively, within the first year after diagnosis. This markedly elevated risk may partly reflect shared vascular risk factors, particularly smoking, a well‐established etiologic factor for both IS 27 and these malignancies. 28 Nevertheless, the magnitude and early timing of the associations support a contribution from cancer‐related mechanisms beyond lifestyle confounding. Lung and pancreatic cancers have consistently been identified among the malignancies most strongly associated with cancer‐related stroke, 11 , 29 likely reflecting pronounced prothrombotic and inflammatory features. 30 Pancreatic cancer is highly thrombogenic, with hypercoagulability linked to tissue factor expression, cancer‐derived microvesicles, platelet activation, and procoagulant tumor microenvironment. 31 In lung cancer, hypercoagulability may involve tumor‐mediated coagulation activation, inflammation, endothelial injury, and treatment‐related factors. 32

Esophageal and gallbladder cancers were also associated with markedly elevated IS risk in our study, although estimates were based on small numbers of events. Both malignancies have been less frequently investigated in relation to stroke risk, and evidence remains limited. For esophageal cancer, prior population‐based studies have reported either a moderately elevated risk 16 or a nonsignificant association. 33 Consistent with our findings, a Korean national cohort reported a 2.5‐fold increase in IS risk within the first year after gallbladder cancer diagnosis. 34 Larger population‐based studies or meta‐analyses are needed to clarify stroke risk in these less commonly studied malignancies.

We further observed 2‐ to 3‐fold elevations in IS risk following gastric and bladder cancer diagnosis. Similar early elevations were reported in the SEER‐Medicare study, 13 whereas weaker associations for bladder cancer were observed in Sweden 16 and Denmark. 15 For gastric cancer, smaller but statistically significant increases were reported in Taiwan 35 and Korea, 34 but not in Denmark. 15

Additional malignancies, including liver cancer, malignant brain tumors, and myeloid leukemia, were also associated with moderate elevations in IS risk in our cohort, based on small numbers of events. Prior studies have reported moderately elevated IS risk for liver cancer 34 , 36 and malignant brain tumors, 37 while evidence for myeloid leukemia remains limited, as available data did not distinguish between leukemia subtypes. 16

Notably, breast and prostate cancers, two of the most common malignancies, were not associated with excess IS risk in our study. For breast cancer, our findings align with evidence from a large cohort of breast cancer survivors in Japan 38 and a Danish population‐based study, 15 indicating no excess IS risk within the first year after cancer diagnosis. Nonetheless, a few studies have reported a modest increase confined to the immediate post‐diagnosis period. 12 , 13 , 16 Similarly, SEER‐Medicare data have indicated a transient elevation in IS risk during the first month following prostate cancer diagnosis. 12 , 13 Breast and prostate cancers have well‐established early detection strategies, 39 , 40 which may result in earlier‐stage disease at diagnosis and lower tumor burden. Consistent with this, metastatic disease was less frequent in breast and prostate cancers than in sites with higher excess IS risk, such as lung and pancreatic cancers (Table S7). However, interpretation of stage‐specific estimates was limited by missing stage information.

The pronounced early elevation in IS risk, together with the marked heterogeneity observed across cancer sites, suggests a contribution from cancer‐related mechanisms beyond conventional vascular risk factors. A central mechanism is cancer‐associated hypercoagulability, driven by tumor‐related prothrombotic pathways 41 and systemic inflammation, including neutrophil extracellular trap formation. 42 Elevated D‐dimer levels in patients with cancer‐related stroke support this mechanism. 43 In addition, periprocedural interruption of anticoagulant therapy in cancer patients may increase short‐term IS risk.

Treatment‐related factors have also been implicated. Certain antineoplastic agents have been associated with an increased risk of IS. 44 Evidence regarding chemotherapy remains inconsistent and may reflect confounding by cancer stage, as studies lacking stage adjustment have reported an elevated risk of IS, 45 , 46 whereas a stage‐adjusted analysis showed no association. 47 In contrast, radiotherapy has well‐documented vascular sequelae. 48 Examination of treatment‐related mechanisms was beyond the scope of this study. Importantly, despite the recognized thrombotic potential of cancer, evidence guiding antithrombotic prevention for arterial events remains limited, 49 as existing oncology guidelines primarily address venous thromboembolism. 50

We observed an inverse age gradient, with the highest SIRs among younger patients despite lower absolute IS rates. This likely reflects the low background incidence of IS at younger ages, whereby even a modest post‐diagnosis excess can yield a high observed‐to‐expected ratio. Conversely, the greater burden of traditional vascular risk factors among older adults increases expected IS events and attenuates the relative excess associated with cancer. Similar patterns have been reported in prior population‐based studies. 35 , 51 Although stroke mechanisms could not be directly assessed, this gradient is compatible with a contribution from cancer‐related mechanisms beyond conventional age‐dependent vascular risk factors, highlighting younger cancer patients as a subgroup with markedly elevated relative IS risk.

We found no evidence of sex differences in IS risk. For a few cancer sites, SIR elevations reached statistical significance in one sex but not the other. However, given small numbers of events, these findings should be interpreted cautiously, as they may reflect chance variation or differences in disease characteristics or survival rather than true biological susceptibility. Confirmation in larger population‐based studies or pooled analyses is warranted.

Strengths and Limitations

The main strength of this study is its nationwide, population‐based design linking two comprehensive national registries, providing near‐complete ascertainment of incident cancer and hospitalized IS events in Israel. Conducted within a universal health care system, this design may enhance the generalizability of relative risk estimates to comparable health care settings, although absolute risks may vary across populations. An additional strength is the inclusion of stratified analyses by sex, population group, and age. Such analyses are less commonly reported in population‐based studies of cancer‐associated stroke and provide insight into demographic variations in risk.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. Despite the substantial cohort size, IS events were sparse for some cancer sites, limiting statistical precision, particularly in stratified analyses. Cancer stage at diagnosis was unknown for nearly half of cases, limiting interpretation of stage‐specific estimates. Information on comorbidities, anticancer treatments, and antithrombotic medication use was unavailable in the INCR, precluding adjustment for these potential confounders. Prior registry‐based evidence suggests that adjustment for comorbid conditions may have limited impact on relative risk estimates. 15 However, the absence of treatment and medication data limited our ability to distinguish the contribution of cancer itself from that of anticancer treatment or concomitant medication use.

Although both national registries have high completeness and validity, minor coding misclassification cannot be excluded. In advanced metastatic disease, a small proportion of events coded as IS may reflect unrecognized brain metastases, although this is likely uncommon. Despite ascertainment of prior stroke history using INSR records complemented by national hospital discharge records, undocumented prior strokes may have led to some misclassification of recurrent events as first‐ever IS. Furthermore, as the INSR captures only hospitalized strokes, nonhospitalized and pre‐admission fatal strokes were not recorded. Because most acute strokes in Israel result in hospitalization, under‐ascertainment is expected to be small, although missed pre‐admission fatal strokes could modestly underestimate incidence.

From a methodological standpoint, SIRs do not account for death as a competing risk, which may overstate early excess risk when mortality is high. To address this, cumulative incidence analyses accounting for competing mortality were performed as a complementary approach and yielded patterns broadly consistent with the SIR estimates, whereas the expected population‐based reference provided additional context for the observed absolute risk. Finally, as an observational study, causal inferences cannot be drawn.

In conclusion, the early period after cancer diagnosis appears to represent a critical window of heightened IS risk. For several cancer sites, excess risk extends beyond the initial phase, suggesting both short‐term and sustained elevations in IS incidence. Recognition of these temporal patterns may support timely preventive efforts, including careful assessment and management of vascular risk factors.

Our nationwide, population‐based findings highlight specific high‐risk cancer sites and younger age groups that may warrant particular clinical attention. The cumulative incidence estimates further place these findings in an absolute‐risk context that may be more directly interpretable in clinical practice. Taken together, these results support the integration of targeted cerebrovascular risk assessment into routine oncologic care to facilitate earlier risk identification and tailored prevention strategies within multidisciplinary cancer care.
