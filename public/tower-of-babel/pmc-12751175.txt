A qualitative exploration of gender-based violence throughout the migration journey among refugee women in Nairobi, Kenya
Authors: Lena Moraa Obara, Edward J Alessi, Chiara Sabina, Samantha C Winter, Rachel John, Aishworya Shrestha, Gi Un Shin, Victoria Banyard
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGender-based violence (GBV) is a serious public health issue and violation of human rights that disproportionately impacts refugee women. This qualitative study explores GBV among urban refugee women in Nairobi, Kenya, using the migration process framework (Zimmerman C, Kiss L, Hossain M, PLoS Med 8: e1001034-e1001034, 2011) to explore violence across the migration journey (i.e., in the country of origin, in transit, and while awaiting refugee status determination). To strengthen the analysis, we triangulate the data with the perspectives of service providers.MethodsData collection involved two integrated phases. In Phase I, we conducted in-depth interviews with 50 female refugees who survived GBV and represented diverse national backgrounds, all of whom were in Nairobi. In Phase II, we interviewed 20 service providers working with refugees in Nairobi to contextualize and triangulate the accounts of survivors. Thematic analysis was employed to identify patterns of GBV across the migration journey.ResultsThe findings revealed that refugee women experience multiple and intersecting forms of GBV at each stage of the migration journey. In their country of origin, participants reported that forms of GBV were often tied to war and displacement. During transit, women described exploitation, sexual assault, and violence. In Nairobi, survivors reported ongoing risks, including intimate partner violence, financial abuse, and technology-facilitated violence, compounded by poverty, legal precarity, and stigma.ConclusionThis study highlights the continuity of GBV across the migration trajectory, with some forms shifting and others remaining the same but also persisting across the three stages. The findings indicate the need for survivor-centered interventions that address cultural, structural, and systemic drivers of GBV while strengthening protection and services for urban refugees. By centering on refugee women’s voices, this study helps fill the evidence gap in understanding GBV among displaced populations in Sub-Saharan Africa.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-26021-9.Keywords: Gender-Based violence, Refugee women, Migration journey, Urban refugees, Kenya

Background

Gender-based violence (GBV) is a serious public health issue and a devastating violation of human rights, disproportionately affecting refugee women worldwide [1, 2]. It includes acts of violence rooted in gender inequities and strict gender norms that cause physical, sexual, or psychological harm [3, 4]. According to the Government of Kenya, Refugee Affairs Secretariat (2025), there were 849,625 registered refugees and asylum seekers in Kenya [5], with 87% living in camps and 13% in urban areas. Refugees are individuals forced to flee their countries due to persecution, conflict, or violence and have pursued protection in another country [6]. Estimates suggest that one in three refugee women has experienced GBV at least once in her lifetime [7]. A study conducted among refugees in the Dadaab camp in Kenya reported that 60.3% and 66.7% of women had experienced non-partner violence or intimate partner violence in their lifetime, respectively [8]. In complex humanitarian emergencies, approximately 21.4% of refugees and displaced people report experiencing such violence [9]. The impacts of GBV are severe and enduring and include physical injuries, sexually transmitted infections, unplanned pregnancies, and acute and long-term mental health issues [3, 10]. Refugee women are also vulnerable to experiencing multiple forms of GBV from various perpetrators throughout their migration journey: in their country of origin, during transit, or while awaiting refugee status in a host country.

Despite numerous studies on GBV in various contexts [1–3, 7, 10], there is still limited research focused on refugee women from Sub-Saharan Africa, especially those living in urban areas of the country where they are awaiting refugee status determination, rather than in designated refugee camps. In Kenya, for example, 49% of refugees are women, either in transit or waiting for placement in a third country through the UNHCR [5]. The purpose of this study is to explore, using the migration process framework [11], the experiences of GBV among refugee women from five African countries across three key stages of migration: (1) in their countries of origin (2), during transit, and (3) while living in Kenya pending refugee status determination. This study’s findings have the potential to inform the development of evidence-based interventions, policies, and advocacy initiatives that identify the distinct vulnerabilities and resiliencies of refugee women within metropolitan contexts. By exploring these dynamics, this research aims to contribute to a more nuanced understanding of the experiences of displaced populations and the factors that impact their safety and well-being in urban settings.

Gender-based violence among women in conflict situations

To put these experiences into context, it is crucial to examine the patterns of GBV against women in conflict-affected regions of Africa. Factors such as displacement, financial insecurity, and resource scarcity increase women’s vulnerability to abuse [12]. GBV often occurs during civil unrest, war, and conflict, which, in turn, can lead to migration and exacerbate its impact on both mental and physical health [13]. Notably, war and conflict are primary drivers of displacement among women, with GBV compounding the adversity they experience throughout the migration journey.

In the country of origin, women face heightened vulnerability to GBV, especially when men in their families leave to participate in the armed forces, leaving women responsible for protecting the family home and children [14]. In fact, sexual violence is considered a common tactic used during conflict [15–17]. Such atrocities have been observed in regions such as Bosnia, Liberia, Rwanda, and Sierra Leone [18]. Additionally, women frequently stay silent after experiencing GBV to avoid further stigma and ostracization from their families and communities, which prolongs their suffering [3, 18]. For example, during a decade-long conflict in Sierra Leone, 94% of displaced women reported experiencing sexual violence, including rape and torture perpetrated by both rebel forces and pro-government militias [19]. And in a study of refugee women who fled Uganda, those awaiting their status determination, 77.5% had experienced sexual violence at least once in their lives, and 76.2% had experienced physical violence at some point [20]. This violence occurred both in Uganda and during migration. Indeed, even after fleeing their country of origin, evidence shows refugee women experience non-partner violence, such as rape by armed actors/civilians, assault at checkpoints, borders, or reception points, and the key risk factors were unsafe routes; overcrowded shelters [19–22]. And while in detention or reception center areas, abuses, including harassment and assault by staff or residents, and exploitation during intake or document processing [21].

Migration process framework

The migration process framework by Zimmerman et al. [11] can be used to better contextualize and understand the different forms of GBV that refugee women experience in the country of origin, in-transit, and as they await their refugee status determination in Kenya, also helping to emphasize multiple opportunities for intervention. Encompassing the phases of predeparture, travel, destination, interception, and return [11], the framework can be applied to examine how GBV is experienced by refugee women at different stages, specifically in the country of origin, during transit, and while awaiting refugee status determination.

The application of the migration process framework may help highlight how GBV varies across different stages and how women rely on various coping strategies and support systems during the migration process. The migration process framework provides a lens through which to understand how refugee women experience and cope with GBV across different phases of displacement; however, it is equally important to examine the role of service providers who respond to these needs and shape the support systems available throughout the migration continuum.

Service provider perspectives

Service providers for refugees typically include humanitarian organizations, community-based groups, healthcare professionals, psychosocial counselors, legal aid workers, and case managers who deliver direct assistance to displaced populations [23]. Evidence from peer-reviewed studies highlights the importance of incorporating service providers’ perspectives into GBV research, as they provide valuable insights into both survivors lived realities and the structural barriers that hinder access to care [24]. Moreover, service providers can identify gaps between policy frameworks and on-the-ground implementation, shedding light on systemic challenges such as underfunding, staff shortages, and cultural stigmas that hinder effective prevention and response to GBV [25]. The inclusion of their voices in research thus enriches the understanding of refugee women’s experiences while strengthening the evidence base for designing holistic interventions that address GBV across the migration continuum.

Method

The current study was conducted in two integrated phases to enrich and triangulate the data to better understand the experiences of GBV during the three stages of migration: premigration, transit, and while awaiting refugee status determination in Nairobi, Kenya. In the first phase, we interviewed refugee women, and in the following phase, we interviewed refugee service providers to gain insight into their perceptions of GBV experiences among refugee women and their work with them. To ensure the safety and protection of all research participants, to assist with recruitment, and to ensure that all research processes were culturally informed, the first author formed a community advisory board (CAB) comprising seven members who were consulted throughout the research process. Five members were community leaders and refugees from Somalia, Burundi, the Democratic Republic of the Congo (DRC), Ethiopia, and South Sudan, while two were from local organizations that worked with refugees. All procedures were reviewed and approved by the Rutgers University Institutional Review Board (IRB), and research authorization was granted by the National Commission for Science, Technology and Innovation (NACOSTI).

Participants

Refugee women

This study involved 50 refugee women in Nairobi, Kenya, all of whom had experienced at least one form of GBV during migration and were awaiting UNHCR resettlement decisions. The participants were evenly distributed across five countries of origin: Burundi, DRC, Ethiopia, Somalia, and South Sudan. They ranged in age from 19 to 59 years, with the majority being between 19 and 39 years. Their time in Kenya varied from 1 to 33 years, with many having arrived as minors. All had basic literacy, but 60% had not completed secondary school; the remaining had finished secondary school (36%) or attended some college (4%). Their marital status was as follows: 50% were single, 32% were married, 10% were separated, and 8% were widowed. Additional demographic details are provided in Table 1 below.

Recruitment

Refugee women

With the assistance of the CAB, we used snowball sampling to identify refugee women living in Nairobi. CAB members distributed the initial recruitment flyers to invite an initial group of women from the community, who subsequently referred other potential participants. Because urban refugees mainly connect through informal social networks and community-based service providers [26, 27], snowball sampling enabled access to these networks. To ensure safety during recruitment, CAB members privately contacted potential participants whom they already knew and shared the recruitment flyers with them. To protect potential participants, CAB members did not disclose during initial recruitment conversations that the study focused on GBV. Instead, they presented it as a study that focused on the lived experiences of refugee women in general. Once the women contacted the first author and she verified that they were in a private setting, the first author provided more information about the study. The eligibility criteria were as follows: (1) living in Nairobi (2), feeling safe enough to complete the interview (3), providing informed consent, and (4) being able to communicate in English and/or Swahili. Once eligibility and willingness to participate were confirmed, survivors were scheduled for interviews.

Providers

We used purposive sampling to identify organizations that provide services to refugees in Nairobi. After consultation with the CAB, the first author contacted each organization to see if they would allow staff to participate in the study. Once it was established that staff could participate, the first author distributed the recruitment flyers. Those who expressed interest in participating contacted the first author directly. Eligibility criteria were as follows: (1) working at their current job for at least 6 months (i.e., they had passed the probation period for most organizations) (2), providing informed consent and following study procedures (3), working with refugees directly, and (4) being able to communicate in English or Swahili.

Data collection

Refugee women

The first author conducted interviews in Nairobi between August and October 2024. The interviews lasted approximately 40–90 min and, with participants’ permission, were recorded (see supplementary file: survivors interview guide). Given the sensitive nature of the topic and potential risks, interviews were conducted in three secure locations following consultation with the CAB. The first author followed the World Health Organization’s (2018) ethical and safety guidelines for studying GBV, which emphasize the importance of consent, confidentiality, and providing psychological support to participants who may experience distress during the research. Interviews were conducted in participants’ preferred language (i.e., English, Swahili, or a mix of both). When participants spoke Swahili, the interviews were first transcribed verbatim in Swahili and subsequently translated into English by trained bilingual transcribers. To ensure accuracy, translation occurred after transcription. The first author also compared the translated transcripts with the original audio to ensure fidelity.

As a gesture of appreciation, participants were provided with refreshments and snacks throughout the interviews. Each received an equivalent of $10 in local currency, a rate determined in consultation with the CAB to avoid coercion and align with the compensation practices of local refugee-serving organizations.

Providers

The first author conducted interviews either in person in Nairobi or via Zoom between August and October 2024. The interviews were conducted in English or Swahili and lasted approximately 40–90 min. For the interviews, each service provider was compensated with $30 in local currency, an amount consistent with reimbursement norms for NGO and service-sector staff in Nairobi, for the professional time they contributed to the study.

The questions and probes in the interview guide (supplementary file 2) focused on exploring service providers’ perceptions of the coping strategies and support systems used by female refugee survivors of GBV. Topics and areas of inquiry included questions about their daily lives; for example, please describe a typical day for you. The second set of questions focused on their perceptions of the experiences of GBV that refugee women encounter, as well as their coping strategies. The interviews with providers were recorded, transcribed, and translated.

Data analysis

The interview transcripts were analyzed using thematic analysis to explore the experiences of GBV among refugee women [28]. Thematic analysis comprises six steps: becoming familiar with the data, generating initial codes, searching for themes, reviewing themes, defining and naming themes, and producing the report [28]. The first author uploaded all the interview transcripts into NVivo 15 software [29]. She also implemented anonymization procedures immediately after transcription. All identifying information, including names, locations, and unique personal details, was removed and replaced with pseudonyms before coding began. Only de-identified transcripts were used in the analysis.

The first author developed separate codebooks for refugee women and providers. Two research assistants, trained in qualitative methods, independently coded a subset of the transcripts. Biweekly meetings were held to review findings and address any coding discrepancies [30]. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion until consensus was reached.

Several strategies were implemented to ensure analytical rigor. First, data from refugee participants were triangulated with those from provider participants. This involved comparing the women’s qualitative responses with interviews from service providers. Second, the first author generated an initial set of codes from the field notes, continuously comparing them with the interview transcripts. Third, member checking was conducted with 20% of the participants via phone calls. This subset was selected to balance the need for validation with considerations of feasibility, participant availability, and ethical concerns about re-contacting a population that may experience research fatigue. During these check-ins, the first author provided a summary of the overall themes and discussed various quotes with the participants. Member checking helped ensure that the data were accurately coded and allowed participants to clarify or elaborate on specific cultural nuances that may have been missed during the analysis. Finally, all authors reviewed the initial themes. They worked closely with the first author to ensure they captured the multifaceted experiences of refugee women in Nairobi in a nuanced manner.

Positionality

We are a multidisciplinary team of Kenyan and U.S.-based researchers experienced in working with vulnerable populations, including refugees and asylum seekers. We have expertise in trauma-informed practice, interpersonal violence, and mental health. While our background influences our interpretation of experiences of GBV among refugee women in Nairobi, we approached this work with reflexivity, including actively questioning assumptions around risk, disclosure, and experiences of violence. Additionally, the entire team shared a commitment to ethical, survivor-centered research.

Findings

Through an analysis of both participant and provider narratives, we identified experiences of GBV across three distinct phases of migration: in the country of origin, during transit, and while awaiting determination of refugee status. To protect the identities of participants, they are referred to by their study number.

Encountering GBV in the country of origin

GBV in the country of origin included child marriage, ethnic violence, emotional abuse, threats, sexual violence, and physical violence.

Living under constant threat

Many participants reported receiving threats in their country of origin (n = 22). For instance, they described situations in which they were coerced into marriages against their will. Attempts to escape could result in their families threatening or harming them. One participant shared the following after she fled her home:

My dad discovered this and sent me a message that read, ‘You think going to Bukavu makes you unreachable? Is it not in the Congo? I will kill you. This time round, when I get hold of you, I will kill you.’ (Survivor_ DRC − 5).

Another participant mentioned how her partner constantly threatened her with physical violence: ‘It became too much for me as he continued threatening that he would chop my hand and kill me’ (Survivor _B_8). Another participant talked about threats that she received from rebel groups.

They came and told us that if our person did not want to join them, they would kill him. So, they started threatening us. I left that house and fled to a neighbor, but the neighbor also got scared and told me they’d find me there and said that I should find another place to go to with my child. (Survivor_B_9)

Sexual violence as a form of trade

Most participants (n = 19) recounted their experiences with sexual violence. In such situations, participants were taken in by relatives who assumed full responsibility as caregivers and providers, sharing the challenges they faced after moving in with relatives because of their parents’ death, following the violence committed against them by state actors. As one participant stated:

Most people only knew that [my aunt] was running a food restaurant, but she was also doing her other business at the back. I went to the room; I found a big man. I served him food, but he started to touch me, so I pushed him. He asked why I was pushing him. I got out of the room and went to report it to my aunt. I told her, “Look at this man.” She slapped me and asked. “How could you push him, and you don’t know where the food you eat comes from!” I told her, “However, we work here.” She slapped me again and returned me to that room. That man started touching me. I pushed him again and tried to leave the room. My aunt was standing outside. She pushed me back inside and asked the man to lock the door. That man raped me that day for the first time. (Survivor_DRC_4)

Participants reported that they also experienced collateral damage during conflict and political strife: “I was still at my place waiting for two weeks until I delivered. Therefore, [strangers] came and broke the door. They took my husband, then three of them raped me” (Survivor_B_1). One service provider reported that such women may express a lack of familiarity with the individuals responsible for their victimization. “When there is war in the area or insecurity, these women and girls are being raped by unknown men” (Service Provider_Inf_5). The situation could be even more complex when the refugee women married individuals from rival tribes that had ongoing disputes. For example, one participant described a situation in which her spouse from the opposing tribe became dissatisfied, as issues of infertility arose. This resulted in acts of violence to demean her:

He used to get money from men that he brought to sleep with me. In addition, I was his wife. He told me that if I report, he was going to kill me. He said I could not give birth, so he threw me away. (Survivor_B_6)

Sexual violence sometimes occurred in the presence of family members, resulting in children being involuntarily exposed to traumatic experiences. In certain instances, the children were also forced to observe the violence as it unfolded:

They raped you in front of your children…I mean, it is a lot of things that we have gone through. Very many, very difficult things! Even difficult to talk about, but at times, you talk about them to get them off your chest. (Survivor_DRC_10)

A service provider also mentioned that through his work, he had heard from women in the community that they faced harrowing experiences of being raped in front of their children:

There are those on the government side, they come, and if they find a wife, they abuse her in front of the children, they are raped in front of the children, they don’t fear. Now, if the women see what has been done to a fellow woman, they just flee early. (Service Provider_Inf_9)

Beaten into obedience by spouses and family members

Most participants (n = 19) also reported experiencing physical violence in their country of origin, with some being victimized by their spouses or extended family members. In communities where polygamy is accepted, older wives faced pressure to remain silent about their husbands’ behavior and avoid seeking affection. If they insisted on this need, it could lead to severe consequences, including verbal reprimand or physical abuse. One participant shared:

My husband beat me because he has many wives. I wanted to be close to him so that the children could be happy, but he beat me. He wanted to be with his second and third wives. He did not want to divorce me; he wanted me to stay and take care of the children, and he was taking care of his other wives. (Survivor_SS_4)

There were times when participants found themselves normalizing the harsh realities of violence, often leading to a sense of helplessness in the face of these situations: “Beating is a must if you make a mistake. You know that you only have those people [her partner] in life.” (Survivor_SS_7).

A service provider noted that some women who are refugees face challenges in decision-making. Their partners often do not allow them the opportunity to express themselves, and many report feeling voiceless. When they attempt to question their partners, they risk facing physical abuse:

… They are not even given a chance to express themselves. You see now, for them, if she is married, she has no voice completely. They are treated like objects, making them the way they are. If you do not obey what their culture says, the belief that those men have even caused physical assault. (Service Provider_F_CBO_10)

Marriage as a strategy for survival and control

During wartime and conflict, forced child marriage (defined as a union where one party is underage based on the country’s definition for children and the other is an adult) became even more prevalent. Seven women reported being married young before attaining adulthood. Families facing extreme poverty, insecurity, and fear sometimes turned to early marriage as a means of protecting their daughters from sexual violence or securing their survival. For example, Survivor_S_3 mentioned: “I was 15 years old, and that is when my dad married me away instead of him seeing me being raped in front of him.” Unaware of the greater risk of GBV in child marriages, some participants’ parents felt that early marriage would give their children stability. Still, these marriages often subjected young girls to further harm, including abuse, exploitation, and trauma. For instance, a participant stated:

My parents had no clue. Therefore, the man brought me to a house, gave me a bed, and locked me in. He would give me food and just rape me, and I got pregnant. The first child even died in my arms (Survivor_E_2).

Displacement caused by war frequently dismantled support systems, leaving participants without safe alternatives. Some who were orphaned or separated from their parents were left under the care of elderly relatives who could not provide the support they needed; thus, these participants were forced into marriage when they were girls:

It got to a point where she got tired. Her age was advanced. Life became more challenging, and we lived that way. It became necessary for me to get married prematurely. I got into marriage while underage because of suffering, and the man beat me all the time. (Survivor_DRC_9)

Service providers echoed the participants’ sentiments. One provider mentioned hearing about child marriage as a financial transaction:

Early marriage is also GBV. Some parents can sell someone for money. Imagine a 9-year-old child just because the man has money. If he likes you and you have not reached the age for marriage, your parents decide for you because they want money (Service Provider_Inf_4).

Weaponizing culture and identity

In some instances, participants (n = 5) reported feeling violated when perpetrators exploited their cultural beliefs and traditions to justify or legitimize GBV against them. For example, women who are refugees from specific communities may avoid reporting incidents of violence because of culturally-based beliefs that doing so would disgrace their families. As one service provider explained:

When a woman is married, you respect that marriage, even if your husband beats you every day. You cannot tell anyone, and some women cannot even tell their parents that this is what my husband is doing to me…they just keep it to themselves. When the issue worsens, they can report it to the in-laws or the parents but not to the police. It is very rare. (Service Provider_Inf_5).

There were instances where perpetrators misused cultural norms, values, and practices to justify and normalize acts of GBV:

[My 1 st husband] used to beat me up because I did not give birth… You know, in [my country of origin], if you don’t give birth, they insult you. They say, ‘You are just here to fill up the toilet. You are of no use to this family. You are useless.’ Therefore, I had to leave. However, the problem was not with me but with him. When I had the baby, [my 1 st husband] came looking for me and my [2nd husband]. He came to my house, and I was not there. It’s the neighbors who told me that someone was looking for me and that he had a gun. (Survivor_ B7).

Devaluation and rejection by Kin

A few participants (n = 5) reported having experienced emotional abuse and rejection from their marital families in their countries of origin. These accounts illustrated how the loss of a spouse intensified women’s vulnerability within family structures that already disapproved of their marriages. In many rural settings, families lived within the same compound but in separate households. While this led to creating proximity, support could still be limited. After losing their partners to war or political conflict, women described being blamed, ostracized, or insulted by their marital relatives rather than receiving comfort. One participant shared how quickly hostility arose after her husband’s death, a time marked by profound social rejection when emotional support was most needed: “Leaving the funeral, there was no look[ing] back. You are chased like a dog. They tell you that you bring bad luck. You cannot even be allowed to change your clothes” (Survivor_B_1). Such experiences.

For participants who lived with extended families in one homestead after their husbands died from war or natural causes, ending up alone in a place where they were not liked or accepted left them vulnerable to violence from family members. For example, a participant whose husband died faced persecution from their in-laws, who accused her of being responsible for her husband’s death despite not being involved at all. As one participant said:

It was the hard and harsh life at my in-laws’ that made me leave my home after he had died. His family did not like me, and they insulted and physically abused me every time. They accused me of causing my husband’s death (Survivor_SS_10).

Encountering gender-based violence in-transit

Depending on their countries of origin, some participants entered Kenya through direct borders with neighboring nations such as Somalia, South Sudan, and Ethiopia. Participants indicated that they encountered various types of GBV in transit, including honor-based violence, emotional abuse, physical violence, and sexual violence. Collectively, these experiences demonstrate how displacement settings heighten vulnerability to various forms of violence, with physical force becoming a tool through which families, partners, and traffickers exert control over women’s bodies, choices, and mobility.

Physical and coercive violence as mechanisms of control during displacement

Several participants (n = 11) reported experiencing coercion and physical violence from their families during transit; these acts of violence served as mechanisms of control while participants were displaced. Even while living in camps or in second or third countries, families often pressured participants to marry. Many of these marriages were driven by poverty and the family’s urgent need for resources. Some of these participants still had not reached the legal marriage age in the host country or in the countries where they had fled. One participant recounted an incident that occurred while living in a camp while in transit to Nairobi, illustrating how displacement intensified familial coercion: “When I refused to get married, they used to beat me up; they used to bring older boys from the community to beat me up.” (survivor_SS_6). Some were compelled to keep running away due to ongoing abuse from their partners while they were in transit. As one participant stated: “I had conflicts with my husband daily; he would beat me and hold a knife to me. I ran away to Nairobi with the kids without him knowing” (Survivor_S_7).

Owing to the difficulties surrounding migration and the urgent need for safety, participants could also become vulnerable to traffickers. A service provider recounted the experience of one refugee woman who endured abuse from traffickers during her journey, resulting in the loss of her pregnancy, as she was forced to keep up with how fast the traffickers wanted her to move:

Someone said while they were being trafficked, that is, when she lost her pregnancy. This is because they reached a certain area, and she mentioned that they came through Uganda. They arrived somewhere in Uganda, and all of them were told to get out of the lorry they were in and start walking because she was expectant, keeping up with the speed of others was problematic, and because the people moving them were trying to make sure, they did not escape. Because it is like a business or whatever, they were beating her up, and sometimes she would fall (Service Provider_F_CBO_12).

The risk of sexual violence along every step of the journey

Participants remained vulnerable during transit, with 11 reporting experiences of sexual violence. Due to limited resources during migration, participants often stayed in unsafe environments, which increased their risk of sexual violence. One participant described a situation that she encountered:

When we got to Tanzania, my husband had just left. That day… (sigh) That day, three men raped me. All three of them raped me. They tore me down there. When I bore my other child here in Kenya at Pumwani Hospital, the doctors asked me what had happened to me, but I never talked about it with anyone. (sobbing) They cut me down there. I don’t know if they used a razor blade or something else.…. (crying). (Survivor_DRC_5)

Service providers highlighted the numerous challenges that participants encountered while in transit. In some cases, individuals who came to their rescue later became perpetrators. One service provider recounted an instance where a woman was victimized by someone who had initially rescued her:

“We also had a case of a beautiful lady, who was raped by a man who rescued her, and she thought the guy was a good person. However, that person raped her and ruined her completely. She has been on medication for a long time.” (SP_F_CBO_11).

Encountering GBV while awaiting refugee status determination

The participants reported ongoing risks and experiences of GBV in Nairobi, which were often exacerbated by the precarious and uncertain nature of their immigration status. Some women arrived in Nairobi with documentation from the Department of Refugee Services (DRS) and a mandate from the UNHCR, meaning that they were officially recognized as refugees by the UN. Others had left the two main refugee camps in Kenya, drawn to the city for various reasons, including lack of safety in the camps, the need for specialized medical care, increased economic opportunities, and the belief that the resettlement process would be quicker. However, challenges persisted as some participants continued to experience GBV in Nairobi, with their situation not unfolding as smoothly as they hoped. They described experiencing psychological abuse and reputational harm, the effects of the complex interplay of sexual violence and precarious immigration status, deception and exploitation, and technology-facilitated abuse.

Psychological abuse through verbal attacks and reputational harm

While waiting for their refugee status determination, participants (n = 18) reported experiencing psychological abuse through verbal insults and reputational attacks, with the sole purpose of demeaning and isolating them from their communities. This abuse occurred from pre-existing social connections they had in Nairobi. While these networks could sometimes be helpful, they also became sources of hostility, particularly for those whose marriages had ended or who were perceived to have violated social expectations. Several participants recounted being labeled, shamed, or publicly humiliated as a form of punishment and control. One participant shared, “They said I am a street prostitute; I have left my husband, and now I am pretending to sell coffee on the street, but I am a prostitute. You just accept it; they were even insulting my baby.” (Survivor E 5).

Effects of the complex interplay of sexual violence and precarious immigration status

The participants discussed the complexities of their situation as they attempted to make it in Nairobi with minimal resources. Ten participants experienced sexual violence, but they had precarious immigration status, marked by financial insecurity, which led to stress and anxiety. They feared deportation or being sent back to camps, which in turn discouraged some participants from reporting their experiences. One participant mentioned:

He raped me the first time, and he knelt, asking me to forgive him. I feared for my life in Nairobi. Living in Nairobi without a job, having to pay rent, with kids depending on you for food and everything else, all these, despite my ailing and using all the money I have on transport shuttling between hospitals, is a big burden. I therefore agreed to be in a relationship with him, but he lived in his house, and I also stayed in mine (Survivor_B_8).

Experiencing deception and exploitation: financial entrapment and technological abuse

Participants reported that abuse (n = 10) from partners could manifest as deception and exploitation, including financial entrapment. Some participants noted that intimate partners deceitfully referred potential business associates to them to gain control of economic opportunities, as the women were trying to build a foundation of survival for their families. For example: “Therefore, he sent someone who lied to me. I did not know he was working for that man. He said that we should contribute money together and buy a mini market. Therefore, we bought the mini market, then he took everything.” (Survivor_B_6).

Another woman shared how she chose casual work to support her children, but some people tried to take advantage of her:

I decided to rent my iron sheet (mabati) house at Ksh. 2,000 [$20]. I suffered there, in that house, because I couldn’t raise the rent. I would do casual jobs only for some to refuse to pay me. Some also refused to pay for the job I did because I refused to sleep with them. I was doing casual work for both Kenyans and Congolese. I opted to forfeit so much of my dues because I did not want to infect them with the disease, I knew I had. (Survivor B 9)

One service provider reported that some refugee participants reported unequal wage payments. Some employers in Kenya with businesses may refuse to compensate them just because they are refugees and give them less money than they would pay Kenyans.

When they go to workplaces, many people know that they don’t know the law; now, they are underpaid, and you know some rights you cannot champion. You find that most people want to employ them because they are the people who allow themselves to be underpaid compared with the laws of the country. (Service Provider_F_CBO_10)

Technological abuse

Participants reported that their partners engaged in technological abuse, using their phones to share private photos with others to exert control or tarnish their reputation (n = 2). One participant stated. “He has tried every way to ruin me. Just recently, I was baptized, and he took photos from the past with no clothes or videos of me and is now telling people who I am a prostitute.” (Survivor_B_8). Sharing such photos could significantly impact participants and, in some cases, may lead them to engage in substance use. During a discussion about her experience, one participant stated:

There was a time when I almost became a drug addict. There was a time I went to the club; he is now circulating my videos to other people from home and saying that I am a bad person. (Survivor_E_3).

Discussion

This study explored refugee women’s experiences of GBV across three phases of migration: the country of origin, in transit, and while awaiting refugee status determination in Nairobi. Findings revealed that GBV was pervasive throughout the migration journey, and that there were distinct forms of violence in each phase, which varied in intensity. Experiences of physical, sexual, psychological, economic, and technology-facilitated violence (TFV) were deeply shaped by intersecting vulnerabilities, including gender, ethnicity/culture/and migration status. These findings reinforce existing work on intersectionality, a term coined by Crenshaw [31] and discussed by Black feminists and other feminists of color [32], and examined by Bowleg [33] in public health. Intersectionality demonstrates how layered marginalizations compound risk among refugee women and restrict their access to resources and essential sources of protection [31, 34]. This study further supports the migration process framework by demonstrating how violence is interconnected and continuously embedded throughout migration rather than isolated incidents [11]. Thus, for the women in this study, GBV is not a single, time-limited event but a continuum of violence that develops across space and context.

While the general categories of psychological abuse, sexual violence, and physical abuse were present across migration phases, the context of the specific migration phase created different experiences according to participants. Furthermore, both survivors and service providers described many of the same forms of GBV and described nuanced and specific ways in which GBV was used within cultural norms and within the context of family survival. For example, violence was often justified as necessary for a family’s economic survival, enforcing cultural norms, or managing the perceived dangers linked to conflict and displacement. Understanding these layered vulnerabilities is essential for developing interventions that prioritize both cultural sensitivity and the protection of fundamental human rights. Findings continue to support the notion that GBV is both interpersonal and structural, embedded in broader systems of inequality that shape refugee women’s daily lives.

In their countries of origin, women reported facing sexual and physical violence, frequently in the hands of intimate partners, family members, extremist groups, or community leaders. Child marriage emerged as a particularly devastating form of GBV, with survivors describing rape, forced school dropout, and entrenched abuse within marital homes. Cultural violence also played a critical role: families often justified physical abuse through cultural beliefs, and women who could not conceive children reported being insulted or ostracized by their communities. Women who were widowed or whose marriages were not accepted by their in-laws were also devalued. In some cases, they were blamed for their husbands’ deaths, highlighting how kinship structures can perpetuate GBV and reinforce structural inequities [35]. These findings reveal how patriarchal cultural norms and armed conflict interact to normalize violence and silence survivors, a dynamic also observed in studies of refugee populations from patriarchal societies [36]. The implications are profound: when cultural norms frame violence as acceptable, survivors are less likely to recognize their experiences as abuse, and the stigma surrounding GBV discourages disclosure or help-seeking. As Crenshaw’s framework suggests, refugee women’s vulnerability at this stage is not simply a function of gender but rather of its intersection with social norms, political instability, and limited access to protective systems.

During transit, women remain highly vulnerable to GBV. While movement to new communities might offer the chance for new resources and escape from the cultural norms described in relation to GBV in countries of origin, many challenges remained for the women in this study. For example, the presence of co-nationals in transit camps often reinforced harmful community norms that silence survivors. These findings emphasize that vulnerability to GBV is not limited to the country of origin but can occur throughout migration, especially when patriarchal norms are reproduced in new contexts [23, 37]. Prior research similarly shows that social networks while in transit may replicate or maintain community norms around gender, honor, or silence, thereby limiting survivors’ ability to disclose abuse or seek help [23, 37].Thus, even though reporting resources might exist in transit facilities, fear of stigma and retaliation prevented many from reporting incidents, and participants reported remaining silent.

Furthermore, although some women migrated to or moved through countries perceived to have stronger legal protections, this study’s findings did not indicate that they were aware of or able to effectively access or use them. This is aligned with previous studies demonstrating that asylum-seeking and refugee women in transit or in displacement contexts often lack awareness of protections and mechanisms for accountability [38]compounding the silence and fear of retaliation reported in this study. However, as research shows, these efforts are often hindered by persistent patriarchal norms and the lack of accessible, trusted support systems [37, 38]. Being in transit exposed participants in this study to new risks while simultaneously limiting their agency [21]. This frequently left them torn between the perception of safety from stronger legal frameworks and the reality of ongoing obstacles to protection and justice.

In this study, for women awaiting refugee status determination in Nairobi, new forms of violence emerged alongside more familiar patterns of abuse. Technology-facilitated violence, such as the nonconsensual sharing of intimate photos or videos by partners, was described as a tool for control and community shaming, possibly because, in their country of origin and in transit, there was limited digital access compared with Nairobi. This technology-facilitated abuse amplified the reach and permanence of reputational harm, contributing to women’s emotional distress [39–41]. In this phase, women who were refugees and practitioners both discussed GBV coming from their new communities in the country of relocation.

Economic exploitation was also pervasive, as both local employers and fellow refugees took advantage of women’s undocumented status, withholding wages or demanding sexual favors in exchange for employment. These dynamics were closely linked to what participants described as “survival relationships,” in which women remained with or entered relationships with abusive partners to secure housing, food, or protection for themselves and their children as a result of legal precarity and social isolation [42]. Such relationships underscore that women’s choices were often constrained by structural conditions and the need to protect themselves and their children. These accounts are consistent with previous research, highlighting how urban refugee women’s visibility in Nairobi exposes them to heightened risks of exploitation [39]. The intersection of gender, displacement, and precarious immigration status thus creates compounded vulnerabilities: women are targeted because they are women, exploited because they are displaced, and silenced because they lack recognized protection [23]. These findings underscore the urgent need for stronger legal safeguards, labor protections, and community-based advocacy to address multiple layers of marginalization. They also reflect the need for more robust, accessible, and refugee-inclusive social services that can support women’s uptake of legal processes, provide confidential psychosocial care, and offer economic and housing assistance that reduces reliance on abusive partners or exploitative employers [43].

There were also themes related to GBV that cut across all migration phases (i.e., sexual violence, emotional abuse, and physical violence), even if some were more emphasized in one phase than in another. Across all migration stages, harmful cultural norms profoundly influenced women’s willingness and ability to seek support. The women described feelings of shame and fear of ostracization, which is consistent with findings from Lenderts et al. [44], suggesting that cultural stigma silences survivors and perpetuates violence [44]. While some humanitarian organizations have attempted to respond, service providers sometimes deferred to “cultural sensitivity,” choosing not to intervene in cases of domestic abuse [45]. This practice, while well-intended, risks privileging cultural relativism over women’s fundamental rights to safety and dignity. As other scholars have argued, service providers must strike a balance between respecting cultural practices and safeguarding survivors [27, 46, 47]. Failure to do so can exacerbate trauma, reinforce cycles of abuse, and perpetuate distrust in service systems. The implications for service utilization are clear: without interventions that acknowledge intersecting vulnerabilities and affirm women’s rights, refugee women may continue to avoid seeking help, out of fear, stigma, and disbelief that services will meet their needs. Prior research has identified several migration-related interventions aimed at reducing GBV risk and improving access to support, including legal rights education, culturally grounded psychosocial services, and community-based GBV prevention programs [23, 38]. Evidence suggests that while these interventions can improve awareness of rights and increase reporting in some settings, their effectiveness is often limited by inconsistent implementation, inadequate resources, and the persistence of harmful gender norms in transit or displacement contexts.

Finally, women’s experiences were further shaped by the broader contexts of conflict and persecution, including being targeted based on their ethnic or tribal identity. Displacement compounded these risks, forcing women into precarious positions where exploitation and violence became almost inevitable [30, 41, 42]. While some refugee women sought to resist or defend themselves, acts of resistance were fraught with danger, potentially provoking further violence or legal repercussions when perpetrators held greater social or political power [24]. Women’s narratives in this study highlight both resilience and constraint: they negotiated safety, cared for children, and sought ways to survive in hostile environments, yet their efforts to resist or exit violent situations were often undermined by structural barriers, legal precarity, and community surveillance [47]. These dynamics illustrate both the resilience and the vulnerability of refugee women, underscoring the need for service providers and policymakers to adopt an intersectional approach that recognizes how layered marginalizations constrain choices while shaping survival strategies.

Limitations

The study provides valuable insights into the experiences of GBV by survivors across different countries, but there were some limitations. One limitation is the absence of some quantitative measures that assess mental health and other quality of life outcomes, which could enrich the data and be used as another form of triangulation. Future research should incorporate such measures.

Furthermore, language and cultural barriers restricted participation to English- and Swahili-speaking refugees, excluding other voices. Future research would benefit from the inclusion of participants who communicate in additional languages such as French, Somali, and Arabic. Addressing these limitations is essential to capturing survivor perspectives that are both nuanced and genuinely representative of the broader refugee population.

Conclusion

This paper examined the experiences of GBV among urban refugee women across three stages of the migration journey: in the country of origin, during transit, and while awaiting refugee status determination in Nairobi. A common thread across these stages is the layered and complex victimization that women endure, with perpetrators ranging from intimate partners and family members to community actors, smugglers, and even host populations. The findings further demonstrate that GBV shifts in form as women move between contexts shaped by conflict, displacement, and legal precarity. Women’s narratives revealed persistent patterns of sexual violence, psychological abuse, physical violence, economic exploitation, and, in Nairobi, technology-facilitated violence, forms of harm that remain understudied in African refugee settings.

The study demonstrates how war, displacement, and migration are intertwined with multiple forms of violence, creating compounded vulnerabilities at every stage. Women’s ability to seek help or exit violent situations was severely constrained by cultural norms, fear of retaliation, limited legal protection, economic dependence, and the risk of deportation or loss of asylum claims. These constraints contributed to what participants described as “survival relationships,” where women remained with or entered relationships with abusive partners as a strategy to secure food, housing, safety, or protection for their children. Importantly, many of the formal and informal channels intended to address GBV often fail to provide meaningful protection and, in some cases, exacerbate survivors’ victimization. The findings also reveal that the impact of GBV extends beyond immediate physical and psychological harm. Women’s trauma is compounded by the loss of family members, precarious living conditions, and insecurities of displacement. For some, this includes the burden of raising children born of rape, further entrenching experiences of stigma and transgenerational trauma. The implications are profound: without targeted, culturally sensitive, and rights-based support, the effects of GBV not only undermine survivors’ health and well-being but also may reverberate across generations.

Addressing these layered experiences requires an intersectional approach that acknowledges how gender, displacement, ethnicity, and legal status intersect to shape refugee women’s vulnerabilities and limit their access to justice, safety, and healing [43]. The study underscores the urgent need for stronger legal safeguards, labor protections, accessible reporting mechanisms, and fair wage systems that explicitly include refugees; for trauma-informed and refugee-inclusive social and psychosocial services; and for protections against technology-facilitated violence. Without such interventions, refugee women remain exposed to cycles of violence and exploitation that extend across migration stages. Ultimately, a coordinated, rights-based, and culturally-informed response is essential to addressing the complex forms of GBV faced by refugee women and to ensuring their long-term well-being, dignity, and autonomy.
