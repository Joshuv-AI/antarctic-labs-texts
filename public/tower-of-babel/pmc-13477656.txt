Assessment of Inflammation Control in Allergic Rhinitis and Chronic Rhinosinusitis
Authors: Aleksandar Perić, Cveta Milutin Špadijer Mirković, Tijana Vidoje Vukadinović, Biserka Vukomanović Đurđević, Danilo Vojvodić
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAllergic rhinitis (AR) and chronic rhinosinusitis (CRS) in their non-polypous (CRS without nasal polyps - CRSsNP) and polypous (CRS with nasal polyps - CRSwNP) main phenotypes are chronic inflammatory diseases of the nasal mucosa and/or paranasal sinuses with a whole series of unknowns regarding etiology, pathogenesis, clinical manifestations, and therapeutic modalities. Although intranasal and oral antihistamines and intranasal corticosteroids are the main drugs in AR therapy, emerging evidence on the effects of herbal and biological drugs may add a new dimension to the treatment of these patients. CRS is a far more heterogeneous disease with numerous clinical phenotypes that require completely different approaches. In addition to endoscopic sinus surgery (ESS), which remains the mainstay of treatment, intranasal and systemic corticosteroids, macrolide antibiotics, and tetracyclines, as well as nasal irrigation with saline solutions, are the main modalities, especially in the period after surgical treatment. Recently, there has been increasing evidence for the beneficial effects of herbal medicines and biological therapy. However, to assess how well conservative treatment of AR and CRS keeps chronic inflammation under control, numerical parameters derived from cytological findings, biochemical analysis of nasal secretions by enzyme-linked immunoassay (ELISA), flow cytometry, and pathohistological and immunohistochemical analyses of tissue samples are necessary. This narrative review of the literature was done through a critical approach, which included our long-term experience in researching the effects of various drugs, as well as the results of other authors. The results of numerous studies have shown that the application of these drugs can significantly change the profile of inflammatory mediators (cytokines, chemokines, enzymes, growth factors) and thus direct the course of inflammation to a less intense form. Nevertheless, in spite of everything, the control of AR and CRS is still not at a satisfactory level, which directs us towards finding new modalities of conservative therapy, especially in the field of application of biological drugs and plant extracts.

Introduction

Allergic rhinitis (AR) is the most common chronic inflammatory disease of the upper respiratory tract, with prevalence in European countries and North America ranging from 10 to 30%.1 It occurs as a reaction of the nasal mucosa to allergens. According to the type of allergen, we distinguish seasonal AR, which occurs as a reaction to grass, weed, and tree pollen, perennial AR, due to a reaction to indoor allergens (mites, house dust, animal epithelium, mold, feathers), and occupational AR, due to a reaction to allergens in the workplace (spices, substances of plant and animal origin, etc).1–3 Recent progress in comprehending AR has revealed the significant role played by type 2 cytokines, specifically interleukin (IL)-4, IL-5, and IL-13. These cytokines are released by helper T cells 2 (Th2) and innate lymphoid cells (ILC2s).2 Apart from the inflammation that occurs as part of the immune response to allergens, AR is also characterized by a strong neurogenic inflammation that results from damage to the respiratory epithelium and exposure of the basement membrane, where the sensory nerve fiber endings of the trigeminal nerve are located.4–6 While seasonal AR is characterized by violent symptoms (sneezing, increased watery and mucous discharge from the nose, itchy nose, conjunctival itching and redness, increased lacrimation), the dominant symptoms of perennial AR are nasal obstruction and pain and pressure in the facial area.1,2 In all three forms of AR, the T helper 2 (Th2) type of immune response dominates.1,2,7 In seasonal AR, there is an increased activity of mast cells, with the release of histamine and leukotrienes, while in perennial AR, there is a more pronounced increase in the activity of eosinophils, which locally release toxic enzymes, causing tissue remodeling and hypertrophy of the nasal mucosa.7–9 Accordingly, therapy for AR includes oral, intranasal, and ocular antihistamines, intranasal corticosteroid sprays, combination intranasal antihistamine and intranasal corticosteroid spray, sodium cromoglycate, saline nasal irrigation, biological therapy, herbal remedies, and more.6 In situations where conservative treatment does not give the expected results, primarily in patients with perennial AR with a high level of nasal mucosa hypertrophy, surgical reduction of the mucous membrane of the inferior nasal turbinate is applied, with the use of radio waves, electrocautery, or laser.10 Sometimes, it is necessary to combine this procedure with the correction of nasal septum deformation.10 The disease is characterized by a high level of association with other diseases, primarily with asthma, chronic rhinosinusitis (CRS), and eosinophilic granulomatosis with polyangiitis (EGPA).7–9

CRS is an inflammation of the mucous membrane of the nasal cavity and paranasal sinuses that lasts longer than 12 weeks and, with its symptoms and signs, significantly impairs patients’ quality of life.11,12 In addition to nasal obstruction, increased nasal secretion with postnasal discharge, weakened or impaired sense of smell, pain, and pressure in the facial area, the disease is characterized by appropriate endoscopic findings, with or without the presence of inflammatory nasal polyps (NPs), as well as findings on computed tomography (CT) scans of the paranasal sinuses.11,12 There are multiple clinical phenotypes of CRS in which Th1, Th2, or Th17 immune responses predominate.11–14 With such a heterogeneous form of presentation, CRS poses a challenge for diagnosis and treatment worldwide.

The basic classification of CRS, which in Europe, North America, and Australia has a prevalence of approximately 10.9% (ranging from 6.9% to 27.1% depending on the region), is into CRS without nasal polyps (CRSsNP), in which the Th1 immune response predominates, and CRS with nasal polyps (CRSwNP), with predomination of the Th2 immune response.11,12 CRS is a multifactorial disease, the pathogenesis of which involves a large number of often combined factors, such as dysfunction of the respiratory epithelial membrane, immune reaction to the presence of staphylococcal enterotoxins A and B, mold and other allergens, the presence of bacterial biofilm, a disorder in the metabolism of arachidonic acid, anatomical variations of the lateral wall of the nasal cavity, deformations of the nasal septum, tobacco smoke, air pollution, various immunodeficiencies, etc.11,12 However, the main characteristic of this disease is an extremely high level of heterogeneity, expressed through the manifestation of very different clinical phenotypes of CRS: isolated CRS with and without NPs, CRS associated with AR, asthma, and CRS as a part of non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drug (NSAID)-exacerbated respiratory disease (N-ERD), Central Compartment Atopic Disease (CCAD), Woakes’ syndrome, allergic fungal rhinosinusitis (AFRS), CRS in cystic fibrosis, primary ciliary dyskinesia, systemic granulomatous diseases, different types of immunodeficiency, etc.11–16 Although the association between CRSwNP and tissue and serum eosinophilia predominates in more than 90% of the European, North American, and Australian population and in about 50% of the Far Eastern population, the association between CRS and AR is a matter of controversy.17–19 It is assumed that the same type of immunoglobulin E (IgE)-mediated chronic inflammation leads to both AR and eosinophilic form of CRS.17–19 Swelling of the nasal mucosa in AR, especially in patients with perennial AR, leads to impaired ventilation and drainage of the paranasal sinuses, which creates conditions for the retention of secretions in the sinus cavities.17–19 According to epidemiological records from the literature and pathophysiological mechanisms, this association between CRS and AR is most pronounced in patients with CCAD, which is characterized by the formation of NPs in the central compartment of the nasal cavity, primarily on the mucosa of the upper and middle nasal turbinates, and in patients with AFRS.13,19

Modern approaches in the therapy of CRS, including, in addition to endoscopic sinus surgery (ESS), various modalities of conservative treatment: intranasal and systemic corticosteroids, antibiotics, primarily from the macrolide and tetracycline groups, rinsing the nasal cavity with saline solutions with or without the addition of corticosteroids, desensitization with aspirin, biologics, application of probiotics, herbal medicines, etc.11,12,20–22 Accordingly, the importance of personalized medicine is increasingly coming to the fore, especially during the treatment of CRS with so many different clinical phenotypes.23

Pathohistological (PH) and primarily immunohistochemical (IHC) analysis of tissue samples of the nasal mucosa/NPs can provide us with valuable data on the dominant type of inflammation and the profile of inflammatory mediators.24–26 In this way, detailed analyses of tissue samples can indicate the clinical course of the disease and, eventually, provide guidelines for some of the conservative treatment modalities.24–26

The layer of secretions that lines the mucous membrane of the nose and paranasal sinuses represents the first line of defense against harmful external and internal factors.27 According to the European Position Paper on Diagnostic Tools in Rhinology, the biochemical composition of nasal secretions quite faithfully reflects the inflammatory status of the nasal mucosa and the evolution of diseases of the nasal cavity and paranasal sinuses.28 Also, monitoring the differences in the concentrations of inflammatory mediators in the nasal secretions can provide us with a lot of valuable data on the effects of the applied therapy in the treatment of AR and CRS.28

The purpose of this narrative review is to demonstrate the importance of cytological, PH, and IHC analysis of the nasal mucosa and paranasal sinus tissue, as well as the analysis of the biochemical composition of nasal secretions for obtaining new information about the profile of inflammatory mediators and the response to the applied therapy in patients with AR and CRS. Before citing the research results, an important note should be made. Given that symptoms, cytological findings, biochemical composition of nasal secretions, and histological characteristics of the nasal mucosa in patients with seasonal AR are highly dependent on the concentration of seasonal allergens in the air, the results of these studies should be viewed with some caution.6,28 This also applies to studies in which seasonal AR is associated with CRS. In fact, much more reliable results are obtained in patients with perennial AR and its comorbidities, due to the consistency in the intensity of symptoms, composition of nasal secretions, and histological characteristics, where a certain remodeling of the tissue of the nasal mucosa has already occurred.6,28 The article relies, for the most part, on the results of our research, with appropriate critical reviews, except in situations where we did not research the application of appropriate treatment types and their impact on inflammatory mediator concentrations.

Allergic Rhinitis

Assessment of the Inflammation Level and Effects of Therapy

Cytological Examination

Nasal smear eosinophilia is a simple, non-invasive procedure that measures the percentage of eosinophils among different inflammatory cells in nasal secretions.28 After taking a sample of nasal secretions with a swab, it is applied to a glass slide, fixed, and then the percentage of eosinophils is determined by cytological examination under a microscope.28 It is accepted that eosinophilia means a percentage of eosinophils >10%.28 With a positive skin prick test with inhalant allergens, positive serological tests for specific inhalant allergens, symptoms of chronic rhinitis, and appropriate local findings, the diagnosis of T2-driven allergic inflammation is practically guaranteed.28 While some authors claim that eosinophilia is higher in the nasal secretions of patients with seasonal AR,29 others have found it to be higher in patients with perennial AR.30 Also, while the results of some studies find a positive correlation between the degree of eosinophilia and the score of nasal symptoms,29 other studies refute this.31 The percentage of eosinophils in the nasal secretion can also be used to evaluate the therapeutic effects of intranasal corticosteroid therapy. A study by Pai et al32 shows that a one-month intranasal application of fluticasone furoate nasal spray (FFNS) leads to an improvement in perennial AR symptoms, an improvement in lung function, and a significant reduction in the number of eosinophils in nasal secretion samples (Figures 1 and 3). This paper emphasizes the importance of intranasal corticosteroid administration in the therapy of not only AR, but also the united airway.32 In another study, a three-week use of mometasone furoate nasal spray (MFNS) in combination with the oral antihistamine cetirizine significantly improved the nasal symptom score, local findings, and reduced the number of eosinophils, neutrophils, and desquamated goblet cells in nasal secretions in patients with seasonal AR (Figures 1 and 3).33

However, good effects are also achieved by intranasal application of the antihistamine azelastine hydrochloride, as well as by the combination of azelastine hydrochloride and fluticasone propionate in spray form.34 A cytological study by Trybus et al35 shows that a four-week application of azelastine hydrochloride spray, twice a day with one spray in each nostril, leads to a significant reduction in the number of eosinophils in the cytological smear, as well as signs of regeneration of the ciliary cells of the respiratory epithelium (Figures 1 and 3).

Parisi et al36 found that sublingual immunotherapy (SLIT) in children with mite-induced AR led to a significant reduction in the percentage of eosinophils in nasal secretions from 86% to 16%, simultaneously with a reduction in the percentage of neutrophils, already six months after the applied therapy (Figures 1 and 3). The results of other authors show that the percentage of eosinophils in nasal secretions can also be used to evaluate the effectiveness of subcutaneous immunotherapy (SCIT).37 They also found a significant correlation between a reduction in the percentage of eosinophils and an improvement in the nasal symptom score (Figures 1 and 3).37

The importance of the use of plant extracts in the treatment of AR has come to the fore, especially during the last 20 years. Zingiber cassumunar is a plant that grows in Thailand. The results of a randomized, placebo-controlled study involving patients with perennial AR showed that after four weeks of therapy with the oil extract of this plant in 200 mg capsules, the number of eosinophils in nasal secretions decreased by more than 3 times (Figures 1 and 3).38 Nigella sativa is an annual plant that grows in the Mediterranean countries and the Middle East. The results of the study showed that after 4 weeks of taking 200 mg capsules containing the oil extract of this plant, only 6.1% of patients with perennial AR had detectable eosinophils in nasal secretions, while 89% of patients did not detect specific IgE to Dermatophagoides pteronyssinus in nasal secretions (Figures 1 and 3).39 A study by Pagliuka et al40 showed that the average number of eosinophils in nasal secretions of patients with perennial AR decreased significantly (p < 0.001) after 30 days of nasal therapy with a combination of Glycyrrhiza glabra extract and mannitol in a nasal spray (Figures 1 and 3).

Biochemical Composition of Nasal Secretions

When we talk about the biochemical composition of nasal secretions, everything mentioned in the section on AR also applies to CRS. Nasal secretions represent the first line of defense of the nasal mucosa and paranasal sinuses, in which leukocytes with their products, together with mucociliary transport and biochemical protection of mucus, play their protective roles. They are a mixture of blood plasma transudate/exudate, cells, and mucus produced by goblet cells and seromucous glands.41 Normally, they consist of the so-called “gel” and “sol” phases. The “gel” phase is located on the surface. It is denser and tougher, and consists of mucopolysaccharides secreted by the glands, dissolved in blood plasma, and transudated in high concentration. The “sol” phase is located below. It is much thinner and consists of blood plasma transudate/exudate with significantly less mucopolysaccharides.41 Electrolytes and organic substances are dissolved in it at concentrations that allow mucociliary transport to proceed smoothly. Water makes up 95% of nasal secretions; organic substances (mucopolysaccharides, proteins, and fats) make up 3%; and electrolytes make up the remaining 2%. The largest part of the protein composition is made up of albumins and immunoglobulins, between 45% and 80%.41 While albumins originate from blood plasma transudates/exudates, immunoglobulins are produced by immunocompetent cells - plasma cells. The proteins of nasal secretion also include the so-called protective substances (lysozyme, lactoferrin, kallikrein), as well as mediators of the immune response and inflammatory reactions - cytokines and chemokines.41 It is also important to mention enzymes – indicators of granulocyte cell function, which include major basic protein (MBP) and eosinophil cationic protein (ECP), as the main indicators of eosinophil function, myeloperoxidase (MPO), as the main indicator of neutrophil function, and tryptase, as the main indicator of mast cell function.41 The recent research has shown that the composition of nasal secretions accurately reflects the state of inflammation of the nasal mucosa and paranasal sinuses and tracks the evolution of inflammatory diseases.28 Given that inflammatory mediators play major roles in complex pathophysiological mechanisms in the nasal mucosa, determining their concentrations in nasal secretions may allow us to more easily understand the complicated cascade processes that underlie inflammatory reactions, as well as the effects of treatment.28

There is currently no generally standardized technique for sampling nasal secretions.41,42 There are suction, dilution, and absorption techniques.41,42 In our research, we used a modified absorption technique.41 A wooden stick with cotton wool on the tip (the part with the cotton wool is about 10 mm long and about 4 mm wide), during anterior rhinoscopy, is placed as close as possible to the middle nasal passage and held there for 5 minutes, the amount of time estimated to be necessary for the piece of cotton wool to be soaked with nasal secretions.41 Then, a part of the stick with the cotton wool on the tip is broken off and inserted into an Eppendorf tube containing “transfer medium” with two antibiotics and one antifungal to avoid infection of the sample.41 The sample is left in the “transfer medium” for a minimum of 30 minutes, which is necessary for the diffusion of inflammatory mediators from the secretion sample into the “transfer medium”, which is hypoosmolar.41 The contents of the tube are then centrifuged for 10 minutes at 1000 g to separate the supernatant, in which the mediators have diffused, from the precipitate, in which parts of the cells, as well as large molecules of proteins, fats, and polysaccharides, are present. After separation of the supernatants, they are frozen at −70°С and stored until the detection of mediators.41 Mainly, two techniques are used today to determine the concentration of mediators of inflammation in nasal secretions: enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA), which measures inflammatory markers individually, and the flow cytometry method, in which we can measure up to 21 different mediators in one sample of nasal secretions.41,42

In addition to mediators that are products of the immune response, we can also determine substance P and other neurotransmitters in the nasal secretions of patients with AR, as markers of neurogenic inflammation, which occurs in parallel in the nasal mucosa. Shinoda et al43 found that azelastine hydrochloride in the form of an intranasal spray reduced the concentration of substance P in nasal secretions in patients with perennial AR (p ˂ 0.005). Also, there is a correlation between the decrease in the concentration of substance P and the improvement of symptoms in these patients. In a placebo-controlled study by Kortekaas Krohn et al,44 a combination of azelastine hydrochloride and fluticasone propionate, but not placebo, reduced nasal hyperreactivity (p < 00001 vs p = 0.21), levels of substance P (p = 0.026 vs p = 0.941), and β-hexosaminidase (p = 0.036 vs p = 0.632) in human nasal secretions (Figures 1 and 3).

In a study by Perić et al,45 the authors found significantly higher concentrations of non-selective chemokines, monocyte chemoattractant protein 1 (MCP-1) (p < 00001), MCP-3 (p = 0.018), and regulated on activation, normal T-cell expressed and secreted (RANTES) (p < 0.0001) in the nasal fluid of patients suffering from non-allergic rhinitis with eosinophilic syndrome (NARES) compared to patients with perennial AR (Figures 1 and 3). Chemokines are a form of cytokines with a chemotactic effect. They attract cells of the inflammatory response to the site of inflammation.45 We divide them into non-selective ones, which primarily attract eosinophils but also other cells, and selective ones, which attract only eosinophils to the site of chronic inflammation.45 In patients with perennial AR, the authors found a positive correlation between RANTES levels, nasal symptom score, and eosinophil counts in the nasal secretions.45 In patients with NARES, the concentrations of MCP-1 and RANTES were associated with nasal symptom score and percentage of eosinophils in nasal secretions.45

Clara cell protein 16 (CC16), which is a product of specialized secretory Clara cells present in the nasal and bronchoalveolar epithelium, is a very important ingredient of nasal secretions. CC16 has a protective role against oxidative stress and inflammation in the respiratory tract.46 CC16 is an inhibitor of the proinflammatory enzymes phospholipase A2 and transglutaminase, and an inhibitor of the release of arachidonic acid metabolites in allergic inflammation.46,47

CC16 production is significantly impaired in patients with seasonal and especially perennial AR, the eosinophilic form of CRS, in a state of increased air pollution, as well as in professional swimmers, whose nasal mucosa is exposed to the action of chlorinated water.47,48 Roth et al,49 in an experimental animal study, showed that after prolonged allergen stimulation, most specialized Clara cells transform into hypertrophic secretory (goblet) cells, leading to reduced CC16 production and increased mucus production in the tracheobronchial tree. A study by Špadijer Mirković et al50 showed that patients with perennial AR have higher ECP values and lower CC16 values in their nasal secretions than subjects with a healthy nasal mucosa (Figure 1). However, after only two weeks of FFNS administration, there is a significant drop in the concentration of ECP as a marker of eosinophilic inflammation, and an increase in the concentration of CC16 as a marker of functional recovery of the nasal mucosa (Figure 2).50 In another study by Perić et al,51 the level of CC16 in patients with perennial AR was significantly lower than in the healthy subjects (p = 0.023) (Figures 1 and 3). The eosinophil counts and nasal secretion concentrations of selective chemokine eotaxin-2 and non-selective chemokine RANTES were higher in patients with perennial AR in comparison with controls (p = 0.008, p = 0.001, p = 0.031, respectively) (Figures 1 and 2). The authors also found a negative correlation between CC16 and eotaxin-2 levels in nasal secretions from patients with perennial AR (r = −0.492, p = 0.023). After two weeks of FFNS therapy, the patients with perennial AR had lower nasal symptoms, eosinophil counts, eotaxin-2, and RANTES levels and higher levels of CC16 (p <0.001 for all parameters) (Figures 1 and 2). We can practically observe the “seesaw phenomenon” here, considering that CC16 and chemokines are in a very inverse correlation. The production of one suppresses the production of the other in the nasal mucosa and vice versa. Also, a one-month administration of the oral antihistamine fexofenadine hydrochloride leads to a significant increase in the concentration of CC16 in patients with perennial AR (Figures 1 and 3).52

It is interesting that biological therapy, except for omalizumab, has not been widely used in the treatment of AR and is mainly used in the treatment of different forms of CRS.53,54 Accordingly, no data were found in the literature on the direct effect of biological therapy on inflammatory mediators in AR, and the evaluations of effects refer only to clinical effects.53,54

In contrast to biological therapy, there are emerging studies on the effect of herbal medicines on mediators of inflammation in nasal secretions. A four-week application of oil capsules with an extract of the tropical plant Zingiber cassumunar leads to a reduction in the concentration of T2 cytokines IL-5 and IL-13 in nasal secretions (Figures 1 and 3).38 High mobility group box 1 (HMGB1) is an important protein originating from the cell nucleus, which, when released into the extracellular space, acts as an alarmin, a special form of cytokine that, acting on receptors in the cell membrane of eosinophils, increases their survival.55 Thus, activated eosinophils produce new amounts of HMGB1, so the presence of this mediator is significantly increased in patients with AR, NARES, and CRSwNP.55,56 Cavone et al56 found a significant reduction in the concentration of HMGB1 in the nasal secretions of patients with perennial AR as early as one week after starting the application of a nasal spray with an extract of the plant Glycyrrhiza glabra (Figures 1 and 3). The decrease in the concentration of this molecule was equivalent to that in patients treated with budesonide nasal spray.56

Pathohistological and Immunohistochemical (IHC) Examination

A review of the literature reveals a very small number of studies based on IHC examination evaluating the effects of applied therapy on the profiles of inflammatory mediators in the mucosal tissue of patients with AR. Probably, the reason for such a small number of studies should be sought in the semi-quantitative nature of IHC studies. Most refer to the use of intranasal corticosteroids, as well as herbal extracts. IHC staining is preceded by mucosal sampling, primarily from the lower nasal concha during turbinoplasty. After six weeks of budesonide nasal spray therapy at a dose of 200 µg per day, there is a decrease in expression for the receptors of IL-4 and IL-5, and an increase in the expression for the interferon gamma (IFN-γ) receptor (p < 0.05; p < 0.05; p < 0.05, respectively) (Figures 1 and 3).57 The following IHC study deals with the expression of two very important proteins in the pathogenesis of perennial AR. MBP is released from eosinophils in their most active phase of degranulation. This enzyme, as well as tryptase, released from the mast cells, leads to damage to the nasal mucosa, creating conditions for its remodeling and hypertrophy (Figure 1).58 Twelve-month administration of MFNS at a dose of 200 µg per day leads to a significant decrease in the expression of MBP and, to a lesser extent, tryptase in these patients (Figures 1 and 3).58 Trans-endothelial migration of cells to sites of inflammation is a hallmark of the allergic reaction. The adhesion cascade involves the initial expression of the adhesion molecule E-selectin on endothelial cells. This molecule is necessary for the trans-endothelial migration of eosinophils to the stroma and subsequently to the epithelium of the nasal mucosa, which is a prerequisite for its damage during perennial AR (Figure 1).59 Fluticasone propionate intranasal spray (FPNS), to a greater extent than prednisolone, leads to a decrease in expression of E-selectin in the mucosa (Figure 2).59 IL-25 is an important driver of Th2 inflammatory response and increased production of IL-4, IL-5, and IL-13 (Figure 1).60 Three-month administration of MFNS in patients with perennial AR leads to a significant reduction of IL-25 expression in as many as 87% of patients (Figure 2).60

An experimental IHC study on a rat model of perennial AR used oil prepared with the herb coriander (Centipeda minima), which was instilled in the nasal cavity of animals in a dose of 100 μL of a 0.1% solution of this herb.61 The results showed reduced immunoexpression of two very important molecules. Prostaglandin-endoperoxide synthase 2 (PTGS2) or cyclooxygenase 2 is an enzyme that directs the metabolism of arachidonic acid in the nasal mucosa towards the production of prostaglandin E2 (PGE2), which is one of the few pro-inflammatory prostaglandins.61 Mitogen-activated protein kinase 3 (MAPK3) is a protein that participates in the transmission of signals that regulate the proliferation, differentiation, survival, and apoptosis of eosinophils and mast cells.61 Both of these proteins have, after the coriander oil treatment, reduced immunoexpression in the nasal mucosa of rats after the induction of AR (Figure 2).61 Rosmarinic acid from Perilla frutescens leaf extract reduces immunoexpression of IL-4, IL-5, and eotaxin-1 in the nasal mucosa of perennial AR induced by Dermatophagoides farinae antigens in an experimental rat model (Figure 2).62

Chronic Rhinosinusitis

Assessment of the Inflammation Level and Effects of Therapy

Inflammatory Mediator Levels in Nasal Secretions

The abundance of secretions produced by the mucous membrane of the nasal cavity and paranasal sinuses due to increased activity of seromucinous glands as part of chronic inflammation provides great opportunities for evaluating the inflammatory status and therapeutic response. This primarily applies to patients with CRSwNP, but even there, there are significant variations depending on the clinical phenotype. Results of a study suggested that the production of heat shock protein 70 (HSP 70), ECP, and tryptase was significantly increased in patients with N-ERD and those patients with NPs sensitized to inhaled allergens.63,64 This potentially indicates a high activity of eosinophils and mast cells in those patients. On the other hand, CC16 production was significantly reduced in both groups of subjects compared to those with healthy nasal mucosa.63,64 This could be explained by finding that the release of HSP 70, ECP, and tryptase from inflammatory cells damages the nasal mucosa and reduces its capacity to produce anti-inflammatory proteins such as CC16. One of the important characteristics of patients with CRSwNP is neurogenic inflammation, which, as in AR, occurs in parallel with inflammation based on the immune response.65 Our previous investigation suggests that the production of substance P and bradykinin is very high in patients with N-ERD, and that the concentration of bradykinin correlates very well with the extent of the disease on CT scans of the paranasal sinuses (Lund Mackay score) (Figure 3).65

Also, our previous studies have shown that the cytokine profile in nasal secretions differs significantly depending on whether CRSwNP is associated with AR or not. Allergic patients with NPs have significantly higher concentrations of IL-4, IL-5, IL-6, and tumor necrosis factor beta (TNF-β) compared to those who were not sensitized to inhaled allergens (Figure 3).66 In addition, atopic patients with CRSwNP have significantly higher concentrations of the cytokine IL-5 and the enzyme ECP compared to patients with perennial AR.67 A key role appears to be played by IL-5, which activates eosinophils and stimulates their survival.68–71 Thus, activated eosinophils produce new amounts of IL-5, which expands and deepens the inflammatory process.68–71 For this reason, monitoring the concentration of IL-5 in nasal secretions can be useful not only for assessing the severity of Th2-inflammation, but also for evaluating the therapeutic response to the use of intranasal and systemic corticosteroids, and biological therapy.68–71 Just two weeks of FFNS administration led to a significant decrease in the concentration of the enzyme ECP, as well as the chemokines eotaxin-2 and RANTES in the nasal secretions of allergic and non-allergic patients with CRSwNP, as well as those suffering from perennial AR, but also to a decrease in the clinical parameters of the disease (Figures 2 and 4).50,72 Far better effects are obtained with long-term use of these drugs, given that these are chronic diseases with irreversible changes in tissue structure. These studies show that in most cases of eosinophilic inflammation of the nasal and sinus mucosa, intranasal corticosteroids in spray can successfully control the course of the disease, if continuously applied (Figures 2 and 4).50,72

Long-term low-dose administration of macrolide antibiotics is particularly interesting in the treatment of CRS, especially variants with NPs. Although the latest clinical study refutes the effectiveness of clarithromycin in relation to ESS,73 the results of authors from the Far East say otherwise (Figure 4).74 One of the explanations is the lower prevalence of polyps with eosinophilic infiltration among residents of the Far East (Figure 3).74 However, certain studies from Europe indicate that macrolide antibiotics, due to their immunomodulating effect, lead to a significant reduction in the size of NPs and postponement of the recurrence of the disease.75,76 One study examined whether the association of CRSwNP and AR influences the effects of preoperative clarithromycin therapy, followed by ESS.75 The results of this study suggested that preoperative 500 mg daily clarithromycin administration for 8 weeks postponed nasal polyp relapse after FESS (Figure 4).75 Patients were followed up repeatedly after ESS, with cut-off points at 6 and 12 months. A statistically smaller difference between cut-off points at 12 and 6 months was observed in the group preoperatively treated with clarithromycin compared to patients who were exclusively treated surgically (p = 0.006).75 Thus, the results suggest that preoperative administration of clarithromycin slows the growth of nasal polyps after ESS (Figure 4).75 This could be explained by the fact that macrolides have the potential to reduce the ability of the nasal and paranasal sinuses mucosa to produce inflammatory mediators and growth factors that stimulate the tissue remodeling process and the growth of nasal polyps. Also, these results suggest that allergies do not affect the clinical efficacy of clarithromycin therapy or the efficacy of ESS.75 According to research published by Varvyanskaya and Lopatin, long-term low-dose clarithromycin administered at a daily dose of 250 mg postoperatively well controls eosinophilic inflammation and prevents early relapse of polyps after ESS.76 A study from Thailand suggests that good responders to macrolide therapy in CRS are patients with low eosinophil counts and low IgE concentrations in nasal secretions.77 A study from Serbia and Norway suggests that an eight-week administration of clarithromycin 500 mg once a day leads to a decrease in the concentration of ECP in nasal secretions in the group of non-allergic patients and IL-6 in the group of allergic patients suffering from CRSwNP (Figure 4).78 The chemokine RANTES was decreased after clarithromycin therapy in both groups of subjects and correlated with a reduction in the size of NPs in both allergic and non-allergic patients (Figure 4).78 Another study with Th1 cytokines came to the result that the strong pro-inflammatory cytokine TNF-α in nasal secretions correlates with a decrease in the size of NPs in non-allergic patients, whereas IL-12 in nasal fluid correlates with the shrinkage of NPs in allergic patients.79

An old study from 1983 from Sweden showed a high potential of doxycycline for penetration and deposition in the tissue of NPs as early as 3 hours after the first administered dose, and that the therapeutic level does not change in the tissue during the next 24 hours.80 The study triggered several others that showed a potentially beneficial effect of this antibiotic in the treatment of patients with CRSwNP. A prospective, open-label study from Brazil and Belgium showed that patients with CRSwNP who, in addition to intranasal corticosteroids and nasal irrigation, also used doxycycline (200 mg on the first day, then 100 mg once a day) for 12 weeks had a better effect on quality of life, nasal symptom scores, and findings on CT scans of the sinuses (Figure 4).81 A negative correlation was observed between the improvement in quality of life and the presence of N-ERD and increased serum IgE concentration before therapy.81 In a double-blind, placebo-controlled study, Van Zele et al82 divided the patients with CRSwNP into three groups that were treated for 20 days: the first received oral methylprednisolone in a decreasing dose from 32 to 8 mg once daily; the second was treated with doxycycline at a dose of 200 mg on the first day, then 100 mg once a day, while the third received a placebo.82 Methylprednisolone and doxycycline significantly reduced the size of NPs compared to placebo patients (Figure 4). In addition, methylprednisolone significantly reduced the concentrations of ECP, IL-5, and IgE, while doxycycline reduced the levels of MPO, ECP, and matrix metalloproteinase 9 (MMP-9) in nasal secretions (Figure 4).82

Pathohistological and Immunohistochemical Analyses

Previous studies showed that allergic CRSwNP patients have higher eosinophil counts than non-allergic patients (Figure 3).66 Also, both non-allergic and allergic patients with NPs have a much higher number of eosinophils in the tissue than patients with perennial AR.67 However, for a more accurate assessment of eosinophilic activity in the mucosal tissue of patients with CRS, IHC analysis is required. The BMK-13 molecule is an antibody that specifically binds to the MBP enzyme precisely at the stage when eosinophils secrete it into the tissue of the nasal mucosa or the mucosa of the paranasal sinuses.83,84 Besides ECP, MBP is the most aggressive enzyme that damages the integrity of the mucosa and creates conditions for its remodeling. Vukadinović et al83 found in their IHC study that expression for BMK-13 in the mucosa of NPs removed endoscopically from the ethmoidal labyrinth was significantly higher compared to healthy nasal mucosa. In addition, the level of BMK-13 expression in NP tissue is positively correlated with impairment of quality of life and with the Lund Mackay score.83 Another study shows that the level of expression for BMK-13, but also for vascular proliferation markers CD31 and CD34, is significantly higher in patients with N-ERD compared to subjects with CRSwNP without NSAID hypersensitivity, and especially compared to patients with CRSsNP and those with non-inflamed nasal mucosa.84 Transforming growth factor beta 1 (TGF-β1) is a mediator of inflammation which, in addition to MMPs, has one of the key roles in the process of tissue remodeling in CRS. A 2025 systematic review of the literature shows that the concentration of TGF-β is higher in patients with CRSsNP compared to those with CRSwNP, which is closely related to the process of formation of fibrous tissue (Figure 3).85 Alpha smooth muscle actin (α-SMA) is an antibody and marker that binds well to myofibroblasts in the tissue of the nasal mucosa and paranasal sinuses. A cross-sectional study by Labus et al86 shows that the highest expression of this marker was found in the tissue of NPs of patients with N-ERD, while the lowest was in the tissue of the nasal mucosa of healthy subjects. Also, this marker correlates quite well with the estimated size of nasal polyps and the Lund Mackay score (Figure 3).86

PH and IHC analyses can also be used to evaluate the effects of the applied therapy. A histological study suggests that patients with more intense eosinophilic infiltration of the respiratory epithelium in NPs responded less well to long-term low-dose clarithromycin therapy (Figure 4).87 The study by Iino et al88 shows that in patients with NPs without atopic constitution, the number of CD80-positive macrophages is higher in patients treated with macrolide antibiotics, roxithromycin or clarithromycin compared to those who were not treated (Figure 4). The percentage of CD80-positive macrophages is negatively correlated with the number of eosinophils in NPs.88 The study shows that macrolides can modulate the immune response by influencing macrophage CD80 expression (Figure 4).88 De Borja Callejas et al89 in their IHC study found that treatment with oral prednisolone for 2 weeks, followed by intranasal budesonide for 12 weeks, modulated the process of NPs mucosal remodeling by stimulating respiratory epithelial recovery, reducing expression of stromal markers of tissue remodeling MMP-1, −2 and −9, increasing deposition of collagen fibers in the stroma, and reducing eosinophil infiltration of both the epithelium and the stroma (Figure 4).

Today, the fact emerges that there is a need to look for an alternative to the usual CRSwNP therapy, given the potential side effects of corticosteroids, macrolide antibiotics, doxycycline, and biological therapy. Also, some patients cannot undergo surgery for health reasons, and some refuse surgical treatment. A randomized controlled trial from Taiwan may offer a solution.90 Glycyrrhiza glabra is a traditional herbal remedy with a long-known anti-inflammatory and immunomodulating effect. The patients received the extract of this herbal drug in the form of a nasal spray, twice a day in each nostril for two months. The results of the study showed a significant improvement in the nasal symptom score and the quality of life questionnaire due to a significant reduction in the size of the NPs (Figure 4).90 The IHC results of the study showed a decrease in the expression of stromal mesenchymal markers (α-SMA, fibronectin, and vimentin) and an increase in the expression of epithelial markers (E-cadherin and epithelial cell adhesion molecule - EpCAM) (Figure 4). No side effects were recorded in any patient. These results suggest to us that Glycyrrhiza glabra may be an effective and safe therapeutic option in the treatment of CRSwNP.90

What After ESS?

In this review, we did not focus on evaluating the effects of ESS, considering that, apart from rougher subjective and objective parameters (nasal symptoms score, Lildholdt and Lund-Kennedy endoscopic score, Lund Mackay CT score), we do not have clear numerical parameters that could indicate the control of CRS after applied therapy, as is the case with conservative therapy. That is why it is more interesting to pay attention to the control of CRS in the period after the ESS. Rinsing the nasal cavity with saline solutions is necessary in the period after ESS, as it is important to start intranasal corticosteroid therapy as soon as possible.6,11 In modern literature, however, there is still no consensus on whether to observe an isotonic or hypertonic solution in the period after ESS. A real-life study by Perić et al91 shows that after ESS, there is a significantly better improvement in the nasal symptoms score and the endoscopic score in subjects who rinsed the nasal cavities with a hypertonic solution of concentration 2.3% with the addition of Undaria pinnatifida and Spirulina platensis algae extracts compared to those who rinsed the nose with an isotonic solution without additives. A statistically significant difference was also found in increasing the concentration of epidermal growth factor (EGF) and decreasing the concentration of transforming growth factor alpha (TGF-α) and IL-8 in the group of patients who, after ESS, rinsed the nasal cavity with a hypertonic 2.3% solution with algae extracts.91 Although the study was not placebo-controlled, it suggests that better control of epithelialization, tissue remodeling, and infection is achieved with nasal irrigation with a hypertonic solution with medicinal additives.91 Vandewalle et al92 in their randomized placebo-controlled study showed that the administration of doxycycline for 56 days after ESS significantly increased the quality of wound healing by preventing the local increase in the production of MPO, ECP, and MMP-9 compared to patients who received a placebo postoperatively. Despite being a revolutionary endeavor, the application of biological therapy continues to be a challenge and a certain unknown in the post-ESS period. The price of drugs, the necessity of observing comorbidities that exclude the use of some monoclonal antibodies, potential side effects, and the necessity of recognizing a specific clinical phenotype within CRS somewhat limit their use. So far, four biological drugs have been approved by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and the European Medicines Agency (EMA) for the treatment of CRSwNP: omalizumab (anti-IgE antibody), mepolizumab (anti-IL-5 antibody), dupilumab (anti-IL-4R and anti-IL-13 antibodies), and the latest, tezepelumab (anti-thymic stromal lymphopoietin (TSLP) and anti-IL-33 antibodies). The last of them works where it all starts, at the level of the respiratory epithelium, because by blocking the release of TSLP and IL-33, it indirectly blocks the T2 immune response.93–96 However, it is an interesting fact that apart from rough clinical parameters, subjective and objective, as well as basic laboratory parameters (total serum IgE, number of eosinophils in peripheral blood), we generally do not have detailed numerical parameters that would reflect disease control. Research in the near future may bring more information about this.

Conclusion

AR and CRS are diseases that are on the rise in the modern world. Therapeutic modalities are numerous, but for a fine assessment of the degree of control of chronic inflammation, it is necessary to have numerical data, such as cytological findings, biochemical analysis of nasal secretions and/or serum, PH, and IHC analysis. The results of numerous studies have shown that the application of intranasal and systemic corticosteroids, antihistamines, antibiotics, biological and herbal drugs can significantly change the profile of inflammatory mediators (cytokines, chemokines, enzymes, growth factors) and thus direct the course of inflammation to a less intense form. Nevertheless, in spite of everything, the control of AR and CRS is still not at a satisfactory level, which directs us towards finding new modalities of conservative therapy, especially in the field of application of biological drugs and plant extracts.
