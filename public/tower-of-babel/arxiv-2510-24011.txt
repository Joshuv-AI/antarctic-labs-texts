Abstract
. As AI writing support becomes ubiquitous, how disclosing its use affects reader perception remains a critical, underexplored question. We conducted a study with 261 participants to examine how revealing varying levels of AI involvement shifts author impressions across six distinct communicative acts. Our analysis of 990 responses shows that disclosure generally erodes perceptions of trustworthiness, caring, competence, and likability, with the sharpest declines in social and interpersonal writing. A thematic analysis of participants’ feedback links these negative shifts to a perceived loss of human sincerity, diminished author effort, and the contextual inappropriateness of AI. Conversely, we find that higher AI literacy mitigates these negative perceptions, leading to greater tolerance or even appreciation for AI use. Our results highlight the nuanced social dynamics of AI-mediated authorship and inform design implications for creating transparent, context-sensitive writing systems that better preserve trust and authenticity.



1.
Introduction

Artificial intelligence (AI) systems have reached a level of writing fluency comparable to human authors leading to their widespread adoption across professional, creative, and interpersonal contexts.
While such tools can improve clarity and expression, they also introduce new social challenges.
Readers often perceive AI-generated texts as less empathetic or authentic
(
Rubin et al. 2025
)
, and disclosing AI authorship can degrade perceptions of authenticity and trust
(
Jakesch et al. 2019
;
Liu et al. 2022
;
Schilke and Reimann 2025
)
.
This tension arises because writing is more than information transfer.
It can also be a social act that conveys an author’s intention, effort, and emotion to readers.
When an AI actively contributes to the writing process, this fundamental social meaning of authorship becomes ambiguous, complicating how a text and its author are perceived.

While previous work in AI-mediated communication has outlined its ethical and relational challenges
(
Hancock et al. 2020
)
, empirical studies have so far examined reader perceptions with limited granularity or control over AI authorship.
Much of the existing research compares purely manual writing to AI-assisted text in specific, narrow contexts, often treating writing as a monolithic activity
(
Jain et al. 2024
;
Liu et al. 2022
;
Schilke and Reimann 2025
)
.
This overlooks the nuanced nature of writing, which serves a wide spectrum of communicative goals.
As articulated in frameworks like Berge et al.’s
Wheel of Writing
, writing can be used to persuade, to interact socially, to reflect internally, or to imagine creatively
(
Berge et al. 2016
)
.
The social expectations for an apology letter, for example, are vastly different from those for a technical manual.
As AI writing support becomes deeply embedded in these varied contexts, the boundary between helpful assistance and core authorship blurs.
Furthermore, readers’ attitudes toward writing co-authored by AI may vary depending on their familiarity with the technology. Previous studies have indicated that individuals who are more knowledgeable about AI tend to view its use as a pragmatic choice rather than a lack of competence
(
Mahmud et al. 2024
)
.
This raises critical, unanswered questions: How do readers’ perceptions shift based on the
degree
of AI authorship across these different communication purposes? Furthermore, how do individual factors, such as readers’ AI literacy, moderate these perceptions?
Addressing these questions is essential for designing AI authoring tools that are not only effective but also socially acceptable and trustworthy.

To address this research gap, we conducted a user study with 261 participants to examine how readers’ perceptions of a fictitious author change upon the disclosure of AI authorship across 6 acts of writing
(
Berge et al. 2016
)
.
We found consistent negative shifts in perceived trustworthiness, caring, competence, and likability, especially in emotional and interpersonal writing.
We also found that participants with higher AI literacy exhibited greater tolerance toward and even appreciation of AI authorship.
Our results reveal the complex social dynamics of AI authorship and inform the design of transparent, context-sensitive writing tools that preserve authenticity and trust.
Our work positions AI writing assistance not as a purely technical problem of automation but as a relational design challenge.
We argue that future interfaces must balance AI capabilities with the need to communicate human authorship, social context, and the perceived effort invested by the author.

In summary, we make the following contributions to research on AI-mediated writing and human–AI interaction:

•

Granular empirical evidence on perception shifts after AI authorship disclosure
: We present findings from a controlled study (N=261) that systematically investigates how reader impressions of an author change after disclosing varied levels of AI authorship across six different acts of writing. Our results reveal consistent negative perception shifts in trust, caring, competence, and likability, with stronger effects observed in interpersonal contexts.

•

Insights into the social dynamics of AI-mediated writing
: Our quantitative and qualitative analyses show that perceptions are shaped not only by the degree of AI authorship but also by perceived human effort, emotional context, and readers’ AI literacy. These factors provide a foundation for designing more socially aware AI authoring systems.

•

Design implications for future AI-mediated writing
Based on our findings, we derive four design implications for future systems: 1) context-sensitive transparency that explains the purpose of AI authorship; 2) interfaces that preserve and communicate human effort and agency; 3) writing support adaptive to emotional and social contexts; and 4) reflective interfaces that foster AI literacy and calibrated trust.

2.
Related Work

We situate our work within two primary streams of research: the application of AI across the diverse functional purposes of writing, and the growing body of literature on how readers perceive and react to the disclosure of AI authorship in communication.

2.1.
AI-assisted Writing for Diverse Communicative Purposes

Writing serves a wide spectrum of goals, from the interpersonal to the informational.
Berge et al. 2016
outline six fundamental purposes: persuading audiences, interacting with others, reflecting on experiences, describing facts, exploring ideas, and creating imaginative worlds. Modern AI-assisted writing tools are increasingly used across all of these distinct cognitive and social functions.

Prior research has explored how these tools support a range of writing goals.
Mirowski et al. demonstrated that AI can be integrated into writers’ workflows to enhance creativity in screen- and playwriting
(
Mirowski et al. 2023
)
.
AI has also been leveraged in contexts where writing serves an interpersonal or social function.
For instance, systems that suggest, generate, or refine email responses can help users convey positivity
(
Mieczkowski et al. 2021
)
, politeness, and professionalism in workplace interactions
(
Miura et al. 2025
)
, while AI-assisted dating profiles can enhance self-presentation
(
Barkallah et al. 2025
)
.
These applications demonstrate that AI’s role extends far beyond grammatical correction to shaping a writer’s tone, style, and communicative intent, aiding in both persuasion and social engagement
(
Hancock et al. 2020
)
.

This expanding role, however, introduces social friction, particularly when AI’s contribution blurs the boundaries of authorship. Readers often question the authenticity and credibility of AI-generated content.
For instance,
Li et al. 2024
found that revealing AI’s role in writing essays lowered readers’ quality ratings
(
Li et al. 2024
)
.
Similarly, research on AI-mediated communication showed that online profiles perceived as AI-generated are less trusted
(
Jakesch et al. 2019
)
.
These concerns are particularly salient when writing serves a social purpose, such as persuasion or interaction, where impressions of the author are shaped by perceived authenticity. Recent studies on disclosure dynamics confirm this tension: while AI assistance can improve a text’s fluency, revealing its use can lead readers to judge the author as less sincere or capable
(
Hwang et al. 2025
;
Draxler et al. 2024
)
.

AI-assisted writing thus presents a fundamental trade-off. On one hand, it empowers individuals, especially those with language barriers or weaker writing skills, to communicate more effectively
(
Ningrum et al. 2023
;
Noy and Zhang 2023
)
. On the other, the social meaning of authorship complicates its adoption. When readers know or suspect AI involvement, they may perceive the writing—and by extension, the author—as less authentic or trustworthy
(
Jakesch et al. 2019
)
. Understanding how these perceptions vary across different communicative purposes is therefore critical for designing AI tools that successfully balance expressive power with the need to maintain credibility.

2.2.
Reader Perceptions of AI Disclosure

Research on AI disclosure highlights its paradoxical effect on interpersonal trust.
Several studies show that when readers learn AI contributed to a text, their trust in the human author declines even if the text’s quality is unchanged
(
Jakesch et al. 2019
;
Lermann Henestrosa and Kimmerle 2024
;
Liu et al. 2022
;
Proksch et al. 2024
;
Schilke and Reimann 2025
)
. This "transparency penalty" challenges the conventional assumption that openness builds trust
(
Sah et al. 2018
)
.
The magnitude of this trust erosion is not uniform and appears to be moderated by individual differences. For instance,
Schilke and Reimann 2025
found that people with more positive attitudes toward AI show a smaller decline in trust upon disclosure. Furthermore, a user’s AI literacy plays a significant role in shaping their perceptions
(
Mahmud et al. 2024
;
Schiavo et al. 2024
)
. AI-literate individuals tend to view its use as pragmatic, whereas less knowledgeable readers may see it as incompetence or laziness
(
Mahmud et al. 2024
;
Schiavo et al. 2024
)
.

Complicating this landscape is the fact that people are increasingly unable to distinguish AI-generated text from human writing without explicit disclosure
(
Májovskỳ et al. 2024
;
Weber-Wulff et al. 2023
)
, a finding that holds true even for those knowledgeable about LLMs
(
Dugan et al. 2023
;
Hakam et al. 2024
;
Porter and Machery 2024
)
. This creates a risk of mis-attribution, where readers may credit AI-generated text to a human, only to have their perception negatively altered if the AI’s role is later revealed. Despite the importance of this phenomenon, little research has systematically examined how varying
degrees
of AI involvement influence reader perception across different writing contexts.

Beyond trust, research also reveals mixed attitudes toward the social qualities of AI-generated text. Some studies find that AI is perceived as less caring, warm, or empathetic than humans, particularly in sensitive contexts like health communication
(
Rubin et al. 2025
;
Wang et al. 2025a
)
. Conversely, other studies show that AI-generated content can be rated as equal or even superior to human-authored text, especially when qualities like efficiency and accuracy are valued
(
Ayers et al. 2023
;
Ovsyannikova et al. 2025
)
.

One factor that may explain these divergent findings is people’s prior experience and familiarity with AI. Regular users may view AI authorship as beneficial
(
Mahmud et al. 2024
)
, while less experienced individuals might perceive it as cold, inauthentic or inadequate
(
Li et al. 2024
)
. Together, these findings suggest that AI disclosure has multifaceted effects, influencing not only trust in the author but also perceptions of warmth, competence, and empathy.

2.3.
Our Approach

Building on this body of work, our study introduces a more granular approach to understanding the effects of AI authorship disclosure. Unlike research focused on single domains or simple binary comparisons (human vs. AI-assisted), we systematically investigate these effects across six distinct communicative acts while also varying the disclosed
degree
of AI authorship. This multi-faceted design allows us to model how reader perceptions shift as AI’s role moves along a continuum from minor assistant to primary author, providing a more nuanced understanding of the social acceptability of human-AI co-authorship.

3.
Method

To investigate how the disclosure of AI assistance may influence reader perception, we conducted a repeated-measures online study. Participants first rated their impressions of a text and then rated it again after we disclosed that a certain percentage of the text was generated or edited by AI. In contrast to prior work, our primary goal was to examine how perceptions shifted across systematically varying
levels
of disclosed AI contribution. To achieve this, we used a deception-based design: while all texts were entirely AI-generated (with minor human proofreading), we informed participants that only specific portions of each text were created or altered by AI. This approach allowed us to precisely control the perceived degree of AI involvement.

Our study was designed to answer the following research questions:

RQ1

How does the communicative purpose (i.e., the act of writing) of a text shape reader perceptions upon the disclosure of AI assistance?

RQ2

How does a reader’s AI literacy moderate these perception shifts?

RQ3

What qualitative reasons and underlying themes explain the changes in perception following AI disclosure?

The following study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the first author’s university.

To manipulate the perceived level of AI involvement, we simulated various levels of AI authorship. For each text, a random proportion of sentences was labeled as having been generated or edited by AI (in reality, all texts were entirely AI-authored). The disclosed proportion varied from 0% to 100% in 10% increments across conditions, corresponding to approximately two sentences per increment (Figure
1
). This design allowed us to model situations where a reader discovers that some portions of a text, from minor local edits to the entire document, were created with AI assistance.

3.2.
Procedure

The experimental workflow consisted of three main parts for each text evaluated by participants.

3.2.1.
Pre-disclosure Evaluation

After providing informed consent, participants were each assigned four of the 18 texts, representing different acts of writing, and were asked to read them carefully. For each text, they answered a questionnaire assessing their initial impressions of the (fictitious) author (Figure
1(a)
).
The questionnaire assessed five perception dimensions using 7-point Likert scales adapted from established instruments (27 statements in total).

Trustworthiness
(6 statements),
Caring/Goodwill
(6 statements), and
Competence
(6 statements) were adapted from McCroskey and Teven
(
McCroskey and Teven 1999
)
, and responses ranged from -3 (very negative) to +3 (very positive).

Likability
(7 statements) and the participant’s
Desire for Future Interaction
(2 statements), were adapted from Reysen
(
Reysen 2005
)
and Sprecher
(
Sprecher 2021
)
.
These were rated from -3 (
Strongly disagree
) to 3 (
Strongly agree
).
All statements are included in Table
5
and
6
in the Appendix.

The same set of questions was used in both the pre- and post-disclosure phases. To ensure data quality, we included reverse-coded items and attention checks in both questionnaires.

3.2.2.
Simulated AI Authorship Disclosure and Post-disclosure Evaluation

After submitting their initial ratings for a text, participants were informed that “XX% of the text was generated or edited by AI”. The corresponding sentences were then highlighted in the document viewer, similar to how tools like HaLLMark visualize AI-produced content
(
Hoque et al. 2024
)
.

Following this disclosure, participants answered the same five questions to capture their revised impressions.
They were also asked to provide a free-form text response explaining how and why their ratings had changed (if that was the case).

3.2.3.
Post-experimental Questionnaire

After completing all four evaluations, participants filled out a final AI-literacy questionnaire to examine whether their familiarity and skills with AI tools influenced their perceptions.
To measure this competency, we adapted three relevant sub-constructs from the Meta AI Literacy Scale (MAILS)
(
Carolus et al. 2023
)
: “Apply AI”, “Detect AI”, and “AI Persuasion Literacy”.
“Apply AI” measures an individual’s ability to use AI concepts for problem-solving, reflecting their proficiency and comfort with AI tools
(
Ng et al. 2022
)
.
“Detect AI” assesses the ability to distinguish technologies that use AI from those that do not, a core component of understanding what AI is
(
Long and Magerko 2020
;
Wang et al. 2023
)
.
Finally, “AI Persuasion Literacy” refers to the ability to recognize and resist AI-driven influence on one’s decisions.

3.3.
Participants

We recruited 264 participants from a Japanese crowdsourcing platform
2
2

2

https://crowdworks.jp/
.
The task was described as “a survey to evaluate the quality and impression of written messages”, concealing the true purpose of the study.
After the initial pre-disclosure evaluation, the study’s real objective was revealed. Participants were then given the option to withdraw with full compensation (600 JPY).
Three participants withdrew, leaving 261 who gave additional consent for the post-disclosure evaluation.

Our experimental design included 6 acts of writing, 3 scenarios per act, and 11 levels of disclosed AI authorship (0% to 100% in 10% increments), resulting in 198 unique conditions. We aimed to collect at least 5 responses for each condition, so a total of
6~\text{acts}\times 3~\text{scenarios}\times 11~\text{AI levels}\times 5~\text{response samples}=990~\text{responses}
.

Each of the 261 participants evaluated four randomly selected combinations, yielding 1,044 collected responses. To ensure a balanced dataset across all conditions, we randomly down-sampled any condition with more than five responses to exactly five. This resulted in the final dataset of 990 responses used for our analysis.

3.4.
Data Analysis

We used a mixed-methods approach to analyze the data, combining quantitative modeling of rating shifts with qualitative analysis of participants’ free-form explanations.

3.4.1.
Quantitative Analysis

To analyze how the disclosure of AI authorship affected participants’ impressions, we fit a series of linear mixed-effects models.

The dependent variables in our models were the five metrics capturing the change in perception, calculated as the score differences (i.e., post-disclosure evaluation
-
pre-disclosure evaluation):
TrustworthinessShift
,
CaringShift
,
CompetenceShift
,
LikabilityShift
, and
FutureShift
.

The independent variables were:

•

Act
: The six acts of writing
Convince
,
Interact
,
Reflect
.
Describe
,
Explore
, and
Imagine
, with
Describe
set as the reference category, as it is arguably the most objective and least emotionally-valenced form.

•

AIRatio
: The disclosed percentage of simulated AI authorship in the text (0%–100%).

•

ApplyAI
,
DetectAI
,
PersuasionAI
: The participants’ scores on the three AI literacy sub-scales of the MAILS questionnaire.

Our models included interaction terms between
AIRatio
and all other independent variables. We integrated participant-level random effects to account for individual differences in rating behavior and used the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) for model selection.

3.4.2.
Qualitative Analysis

We analyzed 990 open-ended responses using a multi-stage process that combined computational topic discovery and manual thematic coding.

First, we pre-processed the data by filtering out 61 non-substantive comments (e.g., “none,”, “no change”), leaving 929 responses for analysis.
We then tokenized the text using fugashi
(
McCann 2020
)
and a custom stopword list to filter out low-information words.
We then used this cleaned data for an initial phase of inductive thematic discovery.
We calculated multilingual Sentence-BERT embeddings
(
Reimers and Gurevych 2020
)
and used BERTopic
(
Grootendorst 2022
)
for embedding-based clustering with c-TF-IDF to identify potential topic structures.
To determine the optimal number of topics k, we assessed topic coherence (
c_{v}
) for k values ranging from 3 to 30. We selected
k=14
as it yielded the highest score.

This automated step produced a preliminary thematic structure, but manual review revealed semantic overlaps and misclassified responses.
We therefore proceeded to a second phase of manual refinement.
We merged and redefined the initial 14 topics to create a more robust and unambiguous codebook consisting of nine final themes (see Table
4
).
With this finalized codebook, we obtained an initial categorization of all 929 responses using GPT-5. Two of the authors then independently reviewed this classification, and any disagreements were resolved through discussion to reach a consensus.

Finally, to characterize each of the nine themes, we extracted representative key phrases from the original Japanese responses using 2-grams and 3-grams.
For each theme, we concatenated all associated responses and identified the top n-grams based on their TF-IDF scores.
These n-grams were then reviewed and combined to create meaningful descriptors for each theme.

We conducted the analysis described above using Python libraries including
sentence-transformers
,
BERTopic
,
scikit-learn
(KMeans),
fugashi
,
pandas
, and
statsmodels
.

Theme N1: Loss of Human Touch and Sincerity
(71 responses)

Theme N2: Stylistic Awkwardness
(132 responses)

It felt more mechanical, which changed my impression of its warmth and friendliness”

5.
Discussion

Our findings provide a nuanced understanding of how readers’ perceptions of an author shift upon the disclosure of AI authorship. We distill our results through the lens of our three research questions, discussing the significance of how communicative purpose, AI literacy, and perceived authorial effort shape reader judgments.

5.1.
RQ1: Context is Critical: AI for Creation, Not Connection

Our results reveal a sharp distinction in how readers perceive AI authorship depending on the communicative purpose of the text. In acts of writing that are object-oriented, such as persuading (
Convince
), creating narratives (
Imagine
), or developing knowledge (
Explore
), AI involvement was viewed more favorably. The positive association with perceived competence in
Convince
and
Imagine
suggests that readers may appreciate AI as a tool that enhances the quality of argumentation or creative expression. This aligns with prior work suggesting that readers value AI’s contribution when it serves to consolidate or develop an author’s ideas
(
Hwang et al. 2025
)
.

In stark contrast, the use of AI in the person-oriented act of
Interact
was met with a strong negative reaction across nearly all perceptual measures. Our qualitative themes confirm that in such contexts, AI-authored text was perceived as “cold”, “impersonal” (Theme N1), and even “insincere” (Theme N4). This negative perception is consistent with prior work showing that AI disclosure can signal a lack of author engagement and thereby erode credibility
(
Jain et al. 2024
;
Jakesch et al. 2019
;
Schilke and Reimann 2025
)
. This finding highlights the importance of the "communicative relationship" in interpersonal communication
(
Burleson 2010
)
. Because AI is perceived as incapable of genuine empathetic engagement
(
Liu et al. 2022
;
Rubin et al. 2025
)
, its use in relational contexts can be interpreted as a violation of social expectations, making human attribution critical. Interestingly, in the
Convince
act, AI use boosted perceived competence without a corresponding penalty to likability or caring, suggesting that when AI’s role is to strengthen an argument, leveraging its perceived objectivity
(
Ovsyannikova et al. 2025
)
, its use can be seen as strategic rather than socially detrimental.

5.2.
RQ2: The Moderating Roles of AI Authorship Level and Reader Literacy

Two factors emerged as key determinants of the magnitude of perception shifts: the disclosed level of AI contribution and the reader’s own AI literacy. As confirmed by our regression analysis, a higher disclosed
AIRatio
consistently led to more negative perceptions of the author. Our qualitative themes provide the reasoning behind this trend: as AI’s role grew, participants were more likely to perceive a loss of human touch (Theme N1), question the author’s expertise (Theme N3), and infer a lack of effort or agency (Theme N5). This finding aligns with recent work suggesting that dominant AI authorship can lower an author’s perceived credibility and ownership
(
Draxler et al. 2024
;
Kirk and Givi 2025
)
.

Our results also confirm that a reader’s AI literacy can significantly mitigate these negative effects. Participants with higher self-reported AI literacy exhibited smaller negative perception shifts and, in some cases, expressed positive attitudes toward AI’s capabilities (Theme P8). This is consistent with prior studies indicating that greater familiarity with AI leads to a more pragmatic and less critical evaluation of its use
(
Li et al. 2024
;
Mahmud et al. 2024
)
. AI literacy appears to function as a calibration mechanism, allowing readers to more accurately assess the role and utility of AI in the writing process. However, the effect size of AI literacy was smaller than that of the writing act itself. This implies that while literacy can soften negative reactions, it does not erase the strong social norms governing contexts like interpersonal communication, where even AI-literate readers may expect human-authored content.

5.3.
RQ3: “Considerate” AI Authorship Can Be Socially Acceptable

While the overall trend in our study was toward negative perception shifts, a substantial portion of responses (38.6%) indicated neutral or even positive changes. These reactions were not random; they were most common when the disclosed AI contribution was low (under 50%) and occurred in object-oriented acts such as
Explore
,
Convince
, and
Imagine
, rather than person-oriented acts like
Interact
.

Our qualitative data reveal that this “considerate” AI authorship was perceived positively when it served a clear, assistive role that did not supplant the author’s voice. Participants noted that when AI was used for supplementary tasks, it could preserve the author’s core intention or even strengthen their argument (Theme P6). This aligns with findings from
Hwang et al. 2025
, who observed that writers themselves feel their work is authentic when AI is used to refine or develop their own pre-existing ideas, particularly in non-emotional writing. Therefore, our findings suggest that the social acceptability of AI authorship hinges on its perceived role: when positioned as a supportive tool that enhances human expression, it can be valued; when it is seen as a substitute for human effort and emotion, it is often rejected.

5.4.
Design Implications for AI-mediated Writing

Based on our findings, we formulate four design implications for future AI-mediated writing systems aiming to balance utility with the preservation of authenticity and trust.

5.4.1.
Design for Context-Sensitive Transparency

Our results show that a simple quantitative disclosure, such as stating the amount of AI contribution, is a blunt instrument that is often insufficient, particularly in relational contexts. Instead, transparency mechanisms should be context-sensitive and qualitative. Rather than focusing on
how much
AI was used, systems should help authors explain the
nature
and
purpose
of the AI’s involvement (e.g., AI assisted with grammatical revisions or AI generated the initial draft). This aligns with emerging best practices, such as the ACM’s policy on authorship, which calls for specifying the role AI played. Furthermore, the mode of disclosure should adapt to the communicative act. For descriptive or exploratory writing, explicitly showing the history of AI edits, such as with text highlighting
(
Hoque et al. 2024
)
, may help to increase the credibility of authors. For creative co-writing, disclosure could emphasize the collaborative nature of the process. In sensitive interpersonal contexts, transparency should focus on preserving the human author’s effort and emotional investment.

5.4.2.
Preserve and Communicate Human Effort

Our qualitative analysis reveals that negative reactions to AI disclosure are significantly mitigated when readers can perceive authentic human effort. This suggests that readers evaluate not just the extent of AI authorship, but the degree of perceptible human agency in the final text. Therefore, AI writing tools should be designed not only to disclose AI’s role but also to actively preserve and communicate the author’s contributions. Interfaces could integrate features that visualize human effort, such as edit histories, authorship heatmaps, or representations of the author’s strategic decisions and intentions
(
Peng et al. 2025
;
Wang et al. 2025b
)
. By foregrounding the author’s role in guiding, refining, and curating the text, systems can frame human-AI collaboration as a process of thoughtful engagement rather than delegation. This approach complements context-sensitive transparency: while the latter aims to minimize harm from disclosure, making human effort visible can actively restore authenticity and trust.

5.4.3.
Provide Adaptive Support in Emotional and Social Contexts

The strongest negative perception shifts occurred in writing with emotional or relational significance, where readers expect sincerity and personal investment. This finding underscores that the social function of writing fundamentally shapes how AI authorship is judged. AI-assisted writing systems should therefore be designed with social and emotional awareness. Interfaces could be developed to detect communicative intent, for example by recognizing linguistic markers of empathy, apology, or gratitude. In such contexts, the system could adapt its support, perhaps by limiting its suggestions to structural or stylistic refinements while leaving core affective expressions to the human author. It could also prompt writers to add a personal reflection or review the text to ensure it conveys their genuine feelings. Such adaptive designs would not only help preserve relational authenticity but also guide authors toward a more balanced and appropriate division of labor with AI.

5.4.4.
Foster AI Literacy for Both Authors and Readers

Our study confirms that higher AI literacy correlates with a greater tolerance for AI authorship. This suggests that perceptions are shaped not only by the act of writing but also by the user’s understanding of AI’s role. We therefore see enhancing AI literacy as an important step toward fostering calibrated trust. AI writing tools can be designed to actively cultivate this literacy. For authors, reflective interfaces could visualize their reliance patterns, such as the frequency with which they accept suggestions verbatim versus editing them, and prompt them to consider their authorship balance. For readers, provenance interfaces could provide contextual information about typical forms of AI assistance, helping them to calibrate their trust without overreacting to the mere fact of disclosure. Over time, such feedback loops could help establish shared social norms around what constitutes appropriate and considerate use of AI in writing.

5.5.
Limitations and Future Work

Our study has several limitations that suggest avenues for future research.

First, we manipulated the perceived level of AI authorship using a quantitative ratio of sentences, which, while systematic, does not account for the semantic importance of those sentences. A sentence containing the main thesis of an argument carries more weight than one providing a minor detail; consequently, the perceived influence of AI likely varied depending on which specific sentences were highlighted. Future work should explore how readers’ perceptions are affected when AI contributes to different functional parts of a text, such as core arguments versus stylistic refinements.

Second, our simulation of AI disclosure does not capture the full complexity of real-world human-AI writing collaborations. As suggested by prior work, a more nuanced representation would involve describing the nature of the collaboration, such as the types of prompts the author used, the kinds of assistance the AI provided, and how the author accepted, rejected, or modified the AI’s suggestions
(
Draxler et al. 2024
;
He et al. 2025
)
. Future experiments should include these contribution-level descriptions to better model the diverse patterns of human-AI co-authorship.

Finally, our study was conducted exclusively with Japanese participants. While our design was not language-specific, cultural norms surrounding communication, authenticity, and technology may influence how AI authorship is perceived. The generalizability of our findings to other linguistic and cultural contexts is therefore an open question. Cross-cultural comparative studies are needed to investigate how these nuanced social dynamics vary across different populations.

6.
Conclusion

We examined how the disclosure of AI authorship in writing influences readers’ perceptions across different acts of writing.
Our user study with 261 participants revealed that disclosure generally erodes trust, caring, competence, and likability, particularly in social and emotional contexts. However, we found that these negative effects are moderated by a reader’s AI literacy and by the extent to which human effort and agency remain perceptible. Our findings demonstrate that the social acceptance of AI-mediated writing depends not just on the content but on how human intentionality is communicated and perceived. Based on these insights, we offer design implications for AI writing tools that emphasize context-sensitive transparency, visibility of human effort, and literacy building to sustain authenticity. Our work calls for re-imagining AI writing assistance as a co-creative partnership and relational process where technology amplifies, rather than replaces, human expression and empathy. Future research should explore these dynamics across different cultures and over time as AI becomes more deeply integrated into our communicative practices.
