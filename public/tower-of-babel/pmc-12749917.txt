Unveiling faking in job interviews by examining facial thermal cues in deception detection
Authors: Petra Hypšová, Martin Seitl, Jan Kubíček, Tomáš Mimra
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractDetecting deceptive information in job interviews is a major challenge for improving personnel selection validity. Traditional interviewer-based methods for detecting applicant faking are limited in accuracy, highlighting the need for reliable techniques to enhance detection and minimize suboptimal hiring decisions. This study aimed to (a) investigate functional infrared thermal imaging (fITI), a non-invasive method for measuring facial temperature, to determine whether cognitive load and arousal during direct deception induce facial thermal changes indicative of faking; and to (b) develop an automated framework for processing infrared recordings, accounting for head movements and physiognomic differences. A within-person experimental design was conducted with 27 participants in simulated job interviews under five conditions: baseline, rehearsed truth, rehearsed lie, spontaneous truth, and spontaneous lie. A high-sensitivity infrared camera recorded thermal changes on the nose, forehead, and cheeks. Our dynamic feature extraction approach enabled robust temporal analysis despite naturalistic head movements. Linear mixed-effects models revealed no significant overall effect of faking; however, rehearsal and interaction effects were significant. Nasal temperature was higher in rehearsed than spontaneous responses and in spontaneous lies than spontaneous truths, while forehead and cheeks temperatures remained stable. Findings suggest that cognitive load from rehearsed and spontaneous questioning influences nasal thermal changes during faking.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-29072-5.Keywords: Applicants faking, Deception detection, Facial temperature analysis, Infrared thermal imaging, Job interviews, Personnel selectionSubject terms: Computer science, Software, Physiology, Psychology

Introduction

Job interviews are often used for personnel selection to predict the future job performance of applicants for a particular position1. However, they can be jeopardized by the appearance of applicants’ faking2,3. In the broadest sense, faking is a deliberate attempt to change answers to interview questions in order to achieve a higher score or make a favorable impression4. This dishonest behavior can affect the validity of the interview4, may lead to counterproductive work behavior5, lower work performance6,7 and overall negative work outcomes2,8. What is even more problematic is its high frequency9 and the low accuracy of its detection by interviewers10,11. In this vein, developing methods to increase accuracy of faking detection beyond interviewer observations is essential to improve the objectivity and reliability of interviews and to reduce the risk of suboptimal hiring decisions.

A possible solution lies in recent findings12–14 on the use of various technological possibilities for the non-contact detection of physiological characteristics that are invisible to the human eye but can serve as an important indicator of fraudulent behavior. Currently, scientific attention is focused on the non-contact measurement of facial temperature using functional infrared thermal imaging (hereafter fITI) for deception detection with accuracy of 70 to 90%15–20. However, the existing results are often generalized and derived from narrowly defined conditions. For instance, although entire experimental sequences are recorded, many studies analyze only a few selected frames, usually at the beginning and at the end of each video thermogram16,21. Partly, because the processing of continuous thermal recordings remains an extremely challenging task for which no fully functional and empirically validated methods have yet been established22. In addition, some studies analyze facial regions of interest (hereafter ROIs) without accounting for size differences (e.g., by applying the same number of pixels to the forehead and nose regions)21. This poses a major challenge, because for accurate thermal measurements, the size of the ROI should be adjusted to the proportions of each ROI23 and the entire recording should be considered to account for the different character of the interview22.

Furthermore, when aiming using non-invasive technologies to measure physiological changes to detect deception in job applicants, it is important to consider the unique characteristics associated with their deceptive behavior. Applicants often prepare part of their answers (truthful or deceptive) in advance to increase their chances of being hired24. While this can reduce cognitive load25–27, resulting in fewer signs of deception28, unexpected questions require more cognitive effort29 and increase arousal30, which may lead to more signs of deception, such as different facial temperatures31.

Although the importance of detecting faking in job applicants is widely recognized1,32,33 and although fITI has attracted much attention as a tool for detecting deception31, there is a notable gap in the literature regarding its application for detecting faking in job applicants and solving the above methodological issues.

To address these gaps, we aim to experimentally investigate whether the inclusion of cognitive load and arousal induced by rehearsed and spontaneous questions in job interview simulations elicits thermal differences in applicants’ faces during deception and thus serves as an indication of direct faking. Building on previous research, we aim to develop and apply a framework that analyses heat maps throughout recordings, adjusting ROIs to account for participants’ head movements and individual facial physiognomy structure. This study represents an initial step toward mapping thermal response patterns in simulated job interviews, targeting direct fake or honest responses, and provides a basis for future validation efforts.

Applicant faking in the interview

While a variety of definitions of the applicant faking have been suggested34, this paper will draw on Levashina and Campion4 who defined faking as "conscious distortions of answers to the interview questions in order to obtain a better score on the interview and/or otherwise create favorable perceptions". In examining faking during job interviews, it is necessary to distinguish between faking and overlapping constructs of impression management (hereafter IM) and socially desirable responding (hereafter SDR)34.

Faking vs. impression management. Even though these constructs are described interchangeably in psychodiagnostics, they are different terms in the context of job interviews34. In the interview, IM is described as a deliberate attempt to influence the interviewer’s opinion during the interaction35 and is defined as “conscious or unconscious, deceptive or authentic, goal directed behavior”36. The difference between faking and IM lies in the multidimensionality of IM. IM consists of different tactics and forms, some of which are honest and therefore less serious (e.g., slight image creation: stretching the truth to appear in a better light), while others are deceptive and more problematic (e.g., extensive image creation: outright lying about the candidate’s qualities)4,34. However, faking in job interviews can be perceived as one-dimensional and includes only the deceptive forms of IM34. In this article, we use the term faking, although the existing literature sometimes also refers to deceptive IM.

Faking vs. socially desirable responding. Paulhus37 has defined SDR as a “tendency to give answers that make the respondent look good.” Respondents may, for example, emphasize socially desirable characteristics by asserting their good qualities and denying their bad ones38. In contrast, faking involves inventing or feigning characteristics in order to deceive the interviewer4. SDR is usually considered a stable trait that remains consistent across different situations34, whereas faking is situation-dependent39 and varies depending on the specific requirements of the interview40.

Facial thermal cues to deception

From a physiological perspective, thermal imaging has become one of the most widely used techniques for detecting thermal radiation of physical objects, as it enables remote observations41. In line with research on applicant faking1, cognitive load induced by spontaneous questions can increase cues to deception42 and lead to facial thermal changes during lying20,21,43,44. These results suggest that both cognitive load and the structure of the interview questions play a crucial role in eliciting specific thermal facial responses that may indicate deceptive behavior. fITI is particularly useful for detecting such changes, as it allows the measurement of temperature fluctuations in key facial ROIs (mostly nose15,16,21,22,44, forehead15,20–22, and cheeks15,21,22,45) associated with the changes in blood flow due to the different activation of the autonomic nervous system (hereafter ANS)46.

Nasal temperature is the most accurate sign of distress and negative emotional arousal47. Since lying evokes high arousal and negatively valenced experiences48, decreased nasal temperature is considered the most important ROI for detecting deception16,21,49. In their review, Ioannou et al.23 stated that the drop in nasal temperature could be caused by stress, anxiety or guilt. Moliné et al.16 attributed this to sympathetic nervous system (hereafter SNS) activation triggered either by deception itself or the anxiety of lying, while Gálvez-García et al.21, linked this to higher subjective arousal.

Cheeks thermal responses to different emotional states are non-specific and vary from person to person50–52. However, it is considered a good indicator of deception due to its physiological features such as increased thermal capacitance and heightened thermal reactivity45. During deception, cheeks temperature usually rises45 which has been associated with the liars’ efforts to make a convincing impression21 or with increased deceptive anxiety15. In addition to lying itself, higher cheeks temperature has been associated with feelings of guilt, shame53 and feelings of embarrassment triggered by SNS activity related to blushing54–56.

Although the forehead is the area of the human face that is least sensitive to temperature changes and psychological manipulations21, increased cognitive load, experienced stress43 and negative affective states in general57 are associated with increased forehead temperature20,58. Since deception is associated with increased stress and cognitive load, a higher forehead temperature can serve as an additional indicator20.

The present experiment

Taken together, the previous findings show both the potential and the limitations of fITI in deception detection research. In the present study, we therefore used a within-person experimental design of job interview simulations, targeting on direct fake or honest answers by stressing the difference between faking and IM. The independent variables were interview conditions: baseline, rehearsed truth, rehearsed lie, spontaneous truth and spontaneous lie. The dependent variables were temperature changes of the nose, right cheek, left cheek and forehead. The main aim of the present study was to experimentally investigate whether the inclusion of cognitive load and arousal induced by rehearsed and spontaneous questions in job interview simulations elicits the thermal differences in applicants’ faces during deception and thus serves as an indicator of direct faking. In light of the above theoretical considerations, the present study analyzed the following three effects:

The effect of faking (the difference between faking and honest responding). From a physiological perspective, deception is associated with changes in ANS activity59,60. The reason lies in the assumption that deception can induce stress and increase mental workload, which subsequently affects blood flow in the subcutaneous vessels and leads to changes in facial temperature44. Based on previous research on thermal deception detection16,20,21,45, we hypothesize that during faking the forehead and cheeks temperature increases, while the nose temperature decreases compared to honest responses.

The effect of rehearsal (the difference between rehearsed and spontaneous responding). It is known that people tend to prepare themselves for anticipating job interviews to increase their chances of being hired24, which can significantly decrease their anxiety and cognitive load25,27. Based on physiological thermal research, the lower the anxiety and the lower the cognitive load, the lower the ANS activation, which leads to less pronounced thermal changes and vice versa61. We hypothesize that the temperature of the forehead and cheeks will be lower or remain stable during the rehearsed responding compared to the spontaneous responding. Conversely, we expect the temperature of the nose to be higher during rehearsed responding than during the spontaneous responding.

The interaction effect (the interaction between all conditions and ROIs’ temperature changes). Drawing from prior studies, we expect the following interactions:

Nasal temperature: Individuals who deceive under both rehearsed and spontaneous conditions show a decrease in nasal temperature compared to rehearsed and spontaneous truthful responses likely due to increased subjective anxiety and guilt16 or increased cognitive load21 associated with the deceptive behavior.

Nasal temperature: Individuals who deceive under both rehearsed and spontaneous conditions show a decrease in nasal temperature compared to rehearsed and spontaneous truthful responses likely due to increased subjective anxiety and guilt16 or increased cognitive load21 associated with the deceptive behavior.

Forehead temperature: In line with Zhu et al.20, we expect a significant increase in forehead temperature during spontaneous deceptive responses compared to spontaneous honest responses due to the increased experience of stress and mental effort. However, as cognitive load is expected to be less pronounced during rehearsed responses42, we expect forehead temperature to remain stable during rehearsed honest and faked responses.

Forehead temperature: In line with Zhu et al.20, we expect a significant increase in forehead temperature during spontaneous deceptive responses compared to spontaneous honest responses due to the increased experience of stress and mental effort. However, as cognitive load is expected to be less pronounced during rehearsed responses42, we expect forehead temperature to remain stable during rehearsed honest and faked responses.

Cheeks temperature: In both the rehearsed and spontaneous deceptive responses compared to the rehearsed and spontaneous truthful responses, we expect an increase in cheeks temperature due to the combined effects of guilt and the cognitive effort required to present a convincing lie15,21.

Furthermore, in thermographic research, one of the critical issues is the precise selection of the ROIs and their sizes, using methods ranging from manual delineation to fully automated procedures22,62. A major challenge when analyzing thermal facial videos is the movement of the person, which makes the direct extraction of temperature-related features across all video frames difficult. To cope with these difficulties, many studies limit their analyses to a few selected frames, often at the beginning and at the end of the recording, inevitably losing a considerable amount of critical data reflecting the dynamics of the interaction. In contrast to studies that rely on the selection of a limited number of thermal frames, usually at the beginning and at the end of each video thermogram16,21, continuous approach focuses on the analysis of entire thermal recordings and thus allows real-time monitoring of the transitions between all studied phases, such as baseline, truth, deception, etc.22. This methodology offers greater ecological validity, as it mirrors fluid and evolving nature of interview interactions. By allowing uninterrupted physiological monitoring, it enhances the reliability of deception detection under variable cognitive and emotional states. Collectively, these methodological advancements represent a substantial step toward bridging the gap between experimental thermography and its application in realistic personnel selection contexts.

Consequently and in addition to the above described effects, we aimed to address the critical issue in thermographic research: the accurate selection of ROIs using methods ranging from manual delineations to fully automated procedures22,62, and the analysis of the entire thermograms rather than a few selected frames. To achieve this, we have developed an automated system that processes thermal video sequences frame by frame (sampled at 20 Hz) and aggregates ROI-level temperature values for each experimental condition. This approach enables robust comparisons of average temperature shifts between different experimental manipulations. The primary objective of this framework was to facilitate the automated modeling of temperature-based features across entire thermal video sequences visualized through infrared heat maps. Following heat map generation, the framework systematically computes a range of descriptive statistical parameters, including mean, median, standard deviation, and coefficient of dispersion within each predefined ROI. Moreover, the framework allows thermal data to be stored and exported in a proprietary raw format so that researchers can use their own preferred metrics and statistical analyses. This quantitative approach enables a fine-grained, objective assessment of facial temperature dynamics and allows for the detection of subtle physiological differences across continuous subsegments of the recordings. As such, the framework offers a robust analytical tool for examining the thermal cues of veracity and deception in various contexts. Detailed technical specifications of the framework, including implementation and processing steps, are provided in the Supplementary Information.

We report the results in three parts. Firstly, the effect of rehearsal; secondly the effect of faking; and finally, the interaction between all conditions.

The interaction between rehearsal and region did not reach statistical significance, F(3, 398) = 2.35, p = 0.072, = 0.017. Pairwise comparisons as shown in Fig. 1 revealed that nasal temperature was significantly higher in the rehearsed condition than in the spontaneous condition, Mdiff = 0.198, SE = 0.048, p < 0.001. No significant differences were observed in the forehead (p = 0.423), left cheek (p = 0.228), or right cheek (p = 0.257) regions.

Nasal temperature. Post-hoc multiple comparisons showed that the baseline condition resulted in significantly higher nasal temperature compared to the spontaneous truth condition (Mdiff = 0.361, SE = 0.066, p < 0.001), indicating a significant decrease in nasal temperature during spontaneous honest responses compared to a baseline condition. Similarly, nasal temperature was significantly higher in the rehearsed lie condition than in the spontaneous truth condition (Mdiff = 0.272, SE = 0.066, p < 0.001), and significantly higher in the rehearsed truth condition than in the spontaneous truth condition (Mdiff = 0.318, SE = 0.066, p < 0.001), suggesting that spontaneous responses elicit greater physiological changes compared to rehearsed responses, regardless of truthfulness. In addition, nasal temperature was significantly higher in the spontaneous lie condition than in the spontaneous truth condition (Mdiff = 0.193, SE = 0.066, p < 0.05). This finding suggests that spontaneous truth telling is associated with greater physiological activation compared to spontaneous deception and that the nasal region is particularly sensitive to changes in conditions, especially between spontaneous and rehearsed responses.

Discussion

This study provides fundamental insights into thermal response patterns in simulated job interviews, targeting direct fake or honest responses, and represents a starting point for future validation research. The main aim was to experimentally investigate whether the inclusion of cognitive load and arousal induced by rehearsed and spontaneous questions in job interview simulations elicits thermal differences in applicants’ faces during deception and thus serves as an indicator of direct faking. In addition, we aimed to develop and apply a framework for extracting facial features from the entire infrared recordings that would allow continuous tracking of ROIs independent of participants’ head movements or individual physiognomic facial differences. Three main results were obtained.

First, we found no significant effect of faking on facial temperature. This result is of crucial importance for our later interaction findings. It shows that when using fITI to detect applicant direct faking, factors such as cognitive or mental workload, and not just the faking itself, need to be taken into account. Simply because in certain situations, such as job interviews and even their laboratory simulations, both faking and answering honestly trigger several psychological processes that alternately activate the ANS and lead to non-specific thermal changes in the face. This is in line with previous physiological studies (for a review see e.g., Synnott et al.64) showing that both liars and truth tellers can experience a variety of emotions, making physiological deception detection more difficult.

It is noteworthy that our non-significant result between direct faking and honest responding contrasts with previously published work16,17,21. This discrepancy could be due to different statistical analyses. Most of the cited studies used a repeated measures ANOVA, treating thermal changes as dependent variable and the truth/deception condition and facial ROIs as fixed factors. This approach is not inherently wrong and is common for repeated measures65,66, however when applied without taking individual variability, it can lead to exaggerated significance of physiological data. Traditional repeated measures ANOVA assumes uniform variance and covariance between subjects, which may not be true for complex physiological responses65. Therefore, we preferred GLMMs over repeated measures ANOVA, because: (a) it allows for the inclusion of both fixed and random effect, which is crucial when working with physiological data67, (b) it provides a more flexible framework for modeling complex interactions while still controlling for individual differences, and (c) it reduces the overall risk of errors68. For more information on GLMMs and their advantages for deception detection studies, see Zloteanu and Vuorre66. Our non-significant results are likely due to rigorous methods, controlling individual variability and applying Bonferroni corrections to minimize Type I errors. In future studies, we plan to compare not only the physiological responses but also different statistical analyses such as repeated measures ANOVA, linear mixed-effects models, non-linear models, or non-linear mixed-effects models to show how such models affect not only the results but also their interpretability in deception research.

Second, when we included cognitive load, we observed a significant effect of rehearsal on nasal temperature, which shows sensitivity to various emotional activations21,23,47. Our results showed that nasal temperature was significantly higher under rehearsed conditions than under spontaneous conditions. This could be explained by several factors, such as increased mental stress or increased arousal16,21,23, therefore the SNS activation16. Specifically, when the SNS is activated in response to a perceived threat, it triggers subcutaneous vasoconstriction, which reduces blood flow to certain areas of the skin, including glabrous skin such as the palms of the hands, soles of the feet and tip of the nose23,69,70, leading to a drop in local skin temperature61. Aligned with previous statements on forehead thermal stability21, we observed no significant differences between conditions. Similarly, in line with prior findings on the non-specific thermal responses of the cheeks to different emotional stimuli50–52, we found no temperature differences in the cheeks between the conditions. Future research should further investigate these non-specific thermal responses in relation to different forms of deception across various contexts.

Third, our results extend the current knowledge on nasal sensitivity to deception detection16,17,21,71, by showing thermal variability between honest and fake responses. Importantly, contrary to our previous expectations, nasal temperature decreased significantly with spontaneous honest responding compared to spontaneous faking, whereas no such difference was observed with rehearsed responding. We propose a new insight into the physiological detection of direct faking. This experiment was set up as a simulation of job interviews in which participants were informed that one of them would be hired in the fictitious company based on the assumption that the interviewer believes everything they say, which was replaced by the possibility of winning an amount of 158 euros. When faced with unexpected questions, increased mental stress and arousal resulted not from faking but from answering honestly, driven by the fear of false accusations and losing the chance of being “hired”. This heightened anxiety in honest responding would be consistent with polygraph results in which, particularly on the Control Question Test (CQT), innocent subjects show stronger physiological responses to relevant questions than to control questions72 which is inconsistent with its theoretical basis73. Although this may seem a priori, research on deception detection using thermography has not yielded such results yet, probably because most studies15–17,21,49 focus on the thermal patterns during deception compared to truth or compared to baseline and neglect the patterns during the different types of honest responses. Future research should not only compare the baseline, honest and dishonest responses, but also investigate thermal differences in the face between different truthful responses.

Last but not least, our research provides other researchers with a framework that focuses on extracting facial ROIs that reflect statistical variations related to truth and deception. The effectiveness of the algorithm is due to an integrated registration procedure that aligns infrared frames and ensures consistent geometric placement of facial features in all frames of an infrared video sequence. As a result, the algorithm calculates descriptive statistical parameters that characterize the temperature dynamics associated with individual activities. The proposed framework is based on a rigorous statistical analysis that allows a precise characterization of temperature distributions in different facial ROIs. Moreover, the problem of subject movement is addressed by robust image registration techniques so that the analysis of temperature-related features remains accurate and reliable even in dynamic interview situations. The combination of dynamic and aggregated statistical evaluations provides a multi-layered approach to understanding the thermal characteristics of human behavior and provides valuable insights for applications in areas such as security screening, forensic analysis and psychological assessment. In addition, the framework enables thermal data to be stored and exported in a proprietary raw format. This not only enables the reproduction of the aggregated analyses presented in this article, but also gives researchers the possibility to calculate any temperature-based parameters according to their own hypotheses or analytical needs. By maintaining complete thermal information at the pixel level throughout time, the software ensures analytical flexibility, methodological transparency and reproducibility.

While no significant main effect of deception was found, the presence of significant interaction effects suggests that thermal responses are context-dependent and are shaped by the interplay of spontaneity, rehearsal and cognitive load. This indicates that deception cannot be considered a uniform physiological event, but is rather characterized by situational factors. Furthermore, the modest effect sizes, although statistically significant, suggest that facial thermal changes are subtle and may require larger samples or more sensitive measurement systems, as described below, to be detected with greater reliability. Future studies with larger and more diverse samples are therefore needed to re-evaluate the potential of fITI for detecting applicant faking in personnel selection and to further advance the methodological framework presented in this study.

To our knowledge, this study represents one of the first experimental studies on the potential of fITI to detect applicants’ direct faking, as previous researchers have mostly focused on mock-crime scenarios15,20,45,49,74–76, alibi creation16,21, best-friend scenarios15,49 or a card-game paradigms44. Rajoub and Zwiggelaar19 and Hypšová et al.22 are, as far as we know, among the few researchers who have investigated fITI for deception detection in a more regular interview, also controlling for the rehearsed effect, however, Rajoub and Zwiggelaar19 only investigated the periorbital region. Although our results extend current knowledge on the use of fITI for faking detection, some limitations are worth mentioning.

The most important limitation, which applies to most deception detection studies, is the use of laboratory settings in which researchers have limited ability to increase risk as they would in real life77. Future studies are needed to use fITI in real-life settings, but with strict adherence to the necessary conditions required in human thermal research62,78.

In addition, this study did not take into account participants’ previous experience with job interviews, which could influence their faking strategies. Roulin and Bourdage39 emphasized that applicants may adapt their IM tactics across multiple interviews, suggesting that previous interview experience could influence their approach in this experiment. This study aimed to provide insights into physiological detection of faking using fITI, controlling for cognitive load and arousal and developing a framework for continuous tracking of thermal facial features rather than considering all potentially moderating variables. Although interview experience was not examined, we acknowledge its potential influence on faking behavior and plan to include it in future studies to further improve the understanding of faking variability and its physiological correlates.

Our developed framework does not facilitate the classification of deceptive and truthful responses in real time. An attention-based system that is able to identify segments of the video in which the subject is either telling the truth or lying would greatly increase the utility of this framework. Such system holds considerable promise for real-world applications, particularly in the context of automatic, non-contact detection deception in job interviews. The core functionality lies in the extraction of facial ROIs, currently manually annotated from registered infrared video frames. Despite careful alignment to anatomical facial landmarks, manual extraction introduces potential errors, including variability in the size and positioning of the bounding box. To mitigate these issues, future work should incorporate an analysis of both inter- and intra-observer variability, which would provide objective metrics on the reproducibility and repeatability of facial feature extraction. A key area for future research will involve the development of machine learning algorithms capable of performing automatic extraction of facial features from IR images. This would streamline the process, eliminate subjective bias, and potentially improve the precision of temperature readings across facial ROIs. One promising direction is the adaptation of established frameworks, such as the Viola–Jones object detection algorithm79, which has proven effective in identifying facial features in conventional visual images. Moreover, recent advances in facial expression recognition (hereafter FER) research provide valuable insights that can inform and extend the present findings on thermal based deception detection. Specifically, FER methods have progressed from handcrafted feature extraction techniques, such as Local Binary Patterns80 or Histogram of Oriented Gradients81, toward deep learning architectures, including convolutional82 and recurrent neural networks83 capable of modeling spatial–temporal dynamics of facial movements. These models commonly leverage facial landmark detection84 to normalize head pose, localize key ROIs, and enhance robustness against variations in illumination or occlusion, challenges that similarly affect infrared thermal imaging in realistic interview conditions. State of the art approaches increasingly integrate hybrid strategies, combining learned representations with physiological or geometric features to improve generalization across diverse populations and environments. In addition, temporal modeling through 3D convolutions or LSTM networks has proven effective in capturing subtle micro expressions and transient cues, which is particularly relevant for spontaneous versus rehearsed responses observed in our data. An emerging trend is the use of explainable AI methods, such as Grad CAM85 or Action Unit analysis86, which enable visualization of model decision processes and could enhance interpretability and trustworthiness in applied settings like personnel selection. Incorporating these methodological advancements, particularly multimodal integration of thermal and visual cues, robust landmark alignment, and dynamic sequence modeling, represents a promising avenue for future work aimed at improving both accuracy and ecological validity of automated deception detection systems. The contribution of FER progress to fITI is particularly beneficial when a measurement context suggests a correspondence between behavioral and psychophysiological variables, and multi-method assessment may thus allow cross-validation of conclusions.

Benchmarking our results against established methods in deception detection and FER is essential to contextualize the effectiveness of our framework. The current findings, summarized in Tables 1, 2 and 3, show distinct nasal temperature shifts across conditions, particularly between spontaneous and rehearsed responses, and minimal changes in forehead and cheek regions. These outcomes highlight the sensitivity of nasal cues to cognitive load and arousal but also underscore the need to compare our thermal based approach to alternative modalities, such as RGB based FER systems that utilize landmark detection and action unit analysis. Visual FER models, especially those trained on datasets like CK + 87, FER201388, and AffectNet89, report accuracies exceeding 90% in controlled conditions, but their robustness often declines in “in-the-wild” scenarios, a challenge where our thermal approach may offer advantages, particularly under variable lighting or partial occlusion.

Figures 1, 2 and 3 provide a dynamic depiction of thermal responses across truth, lie, rehearsed, and spontaneous conditions, offering rich temporal data that could be benchmarked against micro expression datasets frequently used in FER studies. Furthermore, Figs. 5, 6 and 7 illustrate the alignment and ROI extraction workflow, which parallels landmark-based preprocessing in visual FER pipelines, enabling future integration of multimodal features (thermal + RGB). To achieve robust benchmarking, future studies should systematically evaluate our thermal framework on standard FER metrics (accuracy, F1-score) and compare it to both unimodal and multimodal systems in deception detection tasks.

Building upon this, the future directions should be aimed on the following areas: (a) Cross modal Benchmarking: Conduct direct comparisons between fITI and state of the art FER models, including hybrid approaches that merge handcrafted thermal features with deep neural networks, (b) Temporal Sequence Modeling: Apply 3D CNNs or LSTM architectures to capture fine grained temporal dynamics (micro expressions, transient thermal cues) across entire interview sequences, and (c) Explainability and Real World Validation: Integrate explainable AI tools (e.g., Grad CAM85, Action Unit overlays86) to visualize decision pathways and validate the system in real interview environments with diverse participant demographics.

In conclusion, our research is among the first to use fITI for detecting applicants faking, and our software represents an important step towards automated analysis of facial temperature during an interview. By identifying baseline thermal response patterns in simulated job interviews, this study provides an important foundation that demonstrates methodological possibilities and provides initial insights into thermal responses during structured, interview-like interactions. In addition, these findings emphasize the need for validation in larger and more diverse samples, including benchmarking fITI against established methods for deception detection. In this vein, there are clear opportunities for future developments. Improving the reproducibility and accuracy of the current extraction process and developing robust machine learning solutions for the automatic extraction of facial features from thermal images will be crucial to overcoming the limitations identified in this study. These advances will not only improve the reliability of temperature-based faking detection but also extend the applications of the technique to other areas where thermal images are used to analyze physiological changes.

Materials and methods

Participants

The participants were selected from the general population using a non-probability convenience sampling method. Interested individuals signed up using a registration form that was distributed via social media platforms such as Instagram and LinkedIn, as well as via QR codes on printed posters in public places such as bus stations and university corridors. The data for this study was collected over a six-week period beginning on June 15, 2023 and ending on July 27, 2023. Our within-subject experiment included 33 participants from the general population, 66,67% (n = 22) were women aged from 18 to 33 years (M = 21.5 years, SD = 3.8 years), and 33,33% (n = 11) were men aged from 20 to 29 years (M = 23.9 years, SD = 2.9 years). Because skin temperature can be influenced by various factors besides deception62, each participant had to answer a series of biographical questions based on Moreira et al.'s78 skin temperature assessment checklist, which was used as an exclusion criterion. Three participants were excluded for not following the criteria (i.e., they had caffeine beverages less than 4 h prior to the experiment) and three participants were excluded because of the wrong quality of their thermal video. The final sample consisted of 27 participants: 26% men (n = 7, M = 23.7 years, SD = 3.2) and 74% women (n = 20, M = 21.4 years, SD = 3.9), aged from 18 to 33 years. To justify our sample size and ensure compatibility with the mixed effects framework, we conducted a Monte Carlo simulation-based power analysis using the simr package90 in R, which revealed that our study with 27 participants had a power of approximately 78% (α = 0.01, Bonferroni-corrected) to detect the observed partial eta squared interaction effect from our LMMs.

Ethics declarations

All participants have been properly instructed and have indicated that they consent to participate by signing the written informed consent form. The individual in this manuscript has given written informed consent to publish these case details. The experiment was conducted in accordance with the Ethical Principles for Psychologists and the Code of Conduct of the American Psychological Association91. The project was approved by the Palacký University Institutional Review Board (IRB): FF UP Ethics Panel for Research under the reference number 03/2023.

Procedure and settings

The experiment took place in a 35m2 laboratory with covered windows, in which the room temperature was set to 21.5 °C and which was shielded from all infrared radiation and airflow. The entire experiment was designed as a simulation of a job interview. After registering for the experiment, each participant received an email in the form of an invitation to a job interview for a specific position (each participant could imagine which position they would like to apply for). This email also stated that each candidate was to rehearse two questions about their work and study life before the actual interview, one of which had to be answered completely truthfully and the other completely deceptively (the decision of which of the questions to answer truthfully or deceptively was entirely up to the candidate). This served as a rehearsed condition. Each candidate marked on the prepared form which of the rehearsed questions they would answer truthfully and which they would answer deceptively. This form was used exclusively for the experimenter and served as the first step in determining the ground truth. Each participant was interviewed by one of two female interviewers of similar age (distributed approximately evenly across participants), both of whom were trained by a work psychologist with HR experience to follow a standardized script and maintain a neutral demeanor while wearing identical black T-shirts to ensure procedural consistency. The interviewer remained blind to all of the participants’ instructions.

In addition, the participants were told that one of them would be hired by the fictitious company based on their performance in the interview (i.e., if the interviewer believed everything the participants said). The hiring was replaced by the possibility of winning an amount of 158 euros. This encourages motivation to lie, as lying is arguably only more difficult than telling the truth when participants are motivated to be believed92.

On the day of the experiment, each participant had to stay in the laboratory for 20 minutes to acclimatize to the room temperature. This rest period was used each time, as it is a necessary condition for working with fITI78 and was not recorded. After the rest period, each participant was positioned in front of a thermal imaging camera. Once the camera was set to the participant, the experimenter turned on the camera, left the room and invited the interviewer, who simulated being an employee of HR department. The interviewer greeted the candidate and asked them to wait a few minutes before the actual interview began. This waiting period lasted 3 minutes and was used as a baseline condition. After the baseline condition, the interviewer began the interview. At the beginning of the interview, the interviewer asked two rehearsed questions. After this rehearsal period, the interviewer told the candidate that they had to answer two more questions requested by the manager of the fictitious company. To see if the answer had to be truthful or fake, the participant opened an envelope in front of them to see the instruction without showing it to the interviewer. The procedure was completely randomized, i.e., the experimenter randomly decided the order of answering spontaneous questions and whether to start with a lie or the truth (using the Research Randomizer; https://randomizer.org/), while the interviewer remained blind to the participant’s instructions. All questions were asked in a fixed order, with the rehearsed questions always being asked first and the spontaneous questions last. This structure, which aims to preserve the participant’s cognitive representation of the interview93, was also used in this study22.

After the interview, the participants completed a final questionnaire in which they explained their honest and faked responses. This served as a final validation of the information collected and allowed us to confirm that the responses were entirely genuine or falsified.

Equipment

The Fluke Ti450 PRO thermographic camera94 with increased sensitivity was used to visualize temperature differences. The thermal sensitivity (NETD) is ≤ 0.025 at 30 °C target temperature (25mK). The emissivity was set to a value of 0.98, which corresponds to the emissivity of human skin95. The autofocus function of the camera was activated for each observation. A resolution of 320 × 240 was used in the study. The sampling rate corresponds to 20 frames per second when autofocus is activated. The camera was positioned on a tripod at a distance of 110 cm from the participant’s face. The height was adjusted so that only the facial area of each participant was captured. To ensure a non-contact measurement, no chin rest or hand grip was used. SmartView Classic software and MATLAB 2022b were used to further analyze the thermal imaging data.

Data preparation

Extracting thermal features framework

The framework uses the intrinsic sensitivity of fITI to detect temperature variations that are thought to correlate with physiological changes triggered by psychological stimuli. By segmenting the facial regions into spatially significant areas, the framework isolates and records temperature variations during various activities. These activities include both controlled truth-telling and lying, allowing a comparison of thermal responses between these conditions. The proposed framework relies on infrared video recordings as input data, with each video represented as a sequence of individual frames in a text format. These frames form the basis for statistical feature extraction, making the pre-processing stage a critical component of the overall analysis. To enable accurate feature extraction, the raw data is converted and structured in a series of steps for further processing in MATLAB to ensure compatibility and efficiency. The entire scheme of facial image processing is summarized in Fig. 5. The detailed description of individual steps with the mathematical formulas and algorithms used can be found in Supplementary Information 2.

In the proposed framework, image registration for the infrared image alignment is applied based on the Demons Algorithm to align video frames and mitigate motion artifacts. To achieve a consistent extraction of thermal features, we chose as reference image an image from the baseline condition where the participant was looking directly into the camera. Based on this reference image, the algorithm adjusts all subsequent images to ensure that the defined facial regions remain consistent despite the movement of the participant. This approach enables reliable extraction of temperature data across the entire video, as these facial regions are treated as fixed reference points throughout the sequence, as shown in Fig. 6.

The alignment process begins with the user manually defining ROIs on the video frame, specifically targeting key facial areas such as the forehead, nose and both cheeks, as shown in Fig. 7. These regions are then mapped onto the remaining registered frames to ensure that facial features remain consistently aligned throughout the analysis, even if the person is moving.

In the final phase of the framework, the aligned ROIs are used to calculate several statistically significant features, including the average temperature value, median temperature, standard deviation, and dispersion within each ROI. This statistical analysis provides two different approaches to analyzing the data. First, the framework enables the tracking of dynamic trends in these statistical parameters across individual video frames, revealing time-dependent fluctuations that can be associated with changes in the subject’s psychological or emotional state. Secondly, the framework enables the calculation of aggregated statistical parameters across all frames for each activity, providing a comprehensive summary of temperature-related features during periods of truth-telling or lying. The complete software of the framework with all codes and explanations is attached in Supplementary Information 3. For researchers interested in testing the software provided, please see Supplementary Information 4 for a sample .txt data set from a consenting participant.

Statistical analyses

We used R Studio version 4.4.1 with the following packages lme496, lmerTest97, emmeans98, effectsize99, and car100. The main dependent variable in our study was thermal change across 5 conditions (baseline, rehearsed truth, rehearsed lie, spontaneous truth, and spontaneous lie) and 4 ROIs (forehead, left cheek, right cheek, and nose). Using a linear mixed-effect models (LMM), we set thermal change as the dependent variable, conditions and ROIs as fixed factors, and participants’ variability as a random effect. In total, we conducted three separate models to test all operationalized effects (effect of faking, rehearsal and interaction):

Model 1 examined the effect of rehearsal (Spontaneous vs. Rehearsed) and ROIs using the formula: Temperature ~ Rehearsal * Region + (1 | ID).

Model 1 examined the effect of rehearsal (Spontaneous vs. Rehearsed) and ROIs using the formula: Temperature ~ Rehearsal * Region + (1 | ID).

Model 2 focused on faking (Truth vs. Lie) across ROIs: Temperature ~ Faking * Region + (1 | ID).

Model 2 focused on faking (Truth vs. Lie) across ROIs: Temperature ~ Faking * Region + (1 | ID).

Model 3 integrated the full factorial design, testing the joint effects of veracity, rehearsal, and ROIs while maintaining baseline as fifth condition: Temperature ~ All conditions * Region + (1 | ID).

Model 3 integrated the full factorial design, testing the joint effects of veracity, rehearsal, and ROIs while maintaining baseline as fifth condition: Temperature ~ All conditions * Region + (1 | ID).

All models included participants’ ID as a random intercept. The thermal changes followed a continuous variable with approximately normal distribution and were therefore analyzed using the Gaussian linear mixed model (GLMM). All LMMs models met the required assumptions, including the absence of multicollinearity, normality of residuals, homoscedasticity and model convergence.
