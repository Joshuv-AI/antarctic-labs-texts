Rectus-based Stoma and Rectus Abdominis Myocutaneous Flap Laterality Planning for Perineal–Anal Defects After Hemipelvectomy
Authors: Hisataka Takeuchi, Shinnosuke Yamashita, Masataka Tanimoto, Takashi Honjo, Yoshihiro Tsukamoto, Yuki Segawa, Satoshi Ota, Eijiro Onishi, Tadashi Yasuda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Summary:Perineal–anal defects are difficult to manage because negative-pressure wound therapy (NPWT) commonly fails to achieve an airtight seal, and stool contamination hinders wound-bed preparation. Fecal diversion is often required, with a stoma typically created within the rectus abdominis muscle. When a rectus abdominis myocutaneous (RAM) flap is planned, the stoma and flap must be placed on opposite sides, making laterality planning essential. Assessment of the deep inferior epigastric artery (DIEA) patency is critical because vascular compromise may limit flap harvesting. Herein, we report a rare case of pelvic necrotizing soft-tissue infection (NSTI) requiring hemipelvectomy in which coordinated stoma planning, vascular evaluation, and RAM flap reconstruction enabled successful treatment. A 63-year-old woman presented with a fulminant NSTI extending from the lower limbs to the pelvis. Despite emergency hip disarticulation, the infection progressed, necessitating a hemipelvectomy. To allow complete NPWT coverage, fecal diversion was planned. Preoperative computed tomography angiography revealed right DIEA occlusion, precluding RAM flap harvesting on that side; therefore, a “left-sided” RAM flap was selected, with the stoma created within the “right” rectus abdominis muscle. After hemipelvectomy and repeat NPWT-assisted debridement, definitive reconstruction using a left RAM flap with skin grafting achieved stable wound coverage. Successful reconstruction after hemipelvectomy for NSTI requires reliable DIEA evaluation, precise laterality planning, and coordinated multidisciplinary management. Preoperative computed tomography angiography is critical to determine the appropriate side for stoma creation and preserve the RAM flap donor muscle. A structured sequence integrating fecal diversion, NPWT, and flap selection enables safe and definitive soft-tissue closure.

Perineal–anal soft-tissue defects are among the most challenging conditions in reconstructive surgery. Reliable wound-bed preparation is difficult, as negative-pressure wound therapy (NPWT) often fails to achieve an airtight seal in this region, and stool contamination further complicates local control. Consequently, fecal diversion is frequently required, typically using a rectus-based stoma created through the rectus abdominis muscle, which provides stable fixation and reduces the risk of parastomal hernia.1,2 When a rectus abdominis myocutaneous (RAM) flap is needed, the stoma and flap must be planned on opposite sides, making precise laterality planning essential.

Preoperative assessment of the deep inferior epigastric artery (DIEA) patency is critical, as vascular compromise may limit RAM flap availability. Failure to coordinate stoma placement with flap laterality can damage donor muscles and compromise reconstruction.

Necrotizing soft-tissue infections (NSTIs) extending into the pelvis can cause extensive defects requiring hemipelvectomy and careful planning. Herein, we report a rare case of pelvic NSTI requiring hemipelvectomy, which was successfully reconstructed through coordinated infection control, rectus-based stoma planning, vascular evaluation, and RAM flap sequencing.

CASE PRESENTATION

A 63-year-old woman with no significant medical history presented with a 3-day history of abdominal pain and diarrhea. On the day of presentation, she developed right lower limb weakness, progressive thigh and hip pain, and subsequent paralysis. She was transferred to our hospital.

On arrival, she was alert with a temperature of 35.2°C, blood pressure of 94 mm Hg systolic and 68 mm Hg diastolic, pulse rate of 127 beats per minute, and respiratory rate of 29 breaths per minute. The right thigh showed extensive purpura, bullae, and crepitus. Laboratory tests revealed the following values: white blood cell count, 10.7 × 103 per µL; hemoglobin, 11.7 g/dL; platelets, 8.9 × 104 per mL; creatine kinase, 3842 U/L; blood urea nitrogen, 41.0 mg/dL; creatinine, 2.18 mg/dL; and C-reactive protein (CRP), 37.1 mg/dL. Contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) showed massive subcutaneous emphysema from the lower leg to the buttocks and muscle liquefaction in the posterior thigh. (See Video [online], which details the clinical course, including serial imaging, operative findings, and postoperative results.) With shock requiring high-dose norepinephrine and vasopressin, NSTI was diagnosed, and emergency right hip disarticulation was performed.

By postoperative day 3, vasopressor and ventilatory support were discontinued, and the CRP level decreased to 3.7 mg/dL by day 6. However, the stump deteriorated (Fig. 1), and CT angiography showed recurrent gas in the buttocks and around the right iliopsoas muscle, requiring further debridement and hemipelvectomy.

Preoperative view on day 12. The gluteal region is covered with infected, unhealthy granulation tissue, whereas the perineal soft tissues show black necrosis, indicating persistent infection.

As hemipelvectomy was expected to create extensive perineal–anal defects, flap reconstruction was anticipated. NPWT was essential for wound preparation, but sealing was difficult due to stool contamination. Therefore, a diverting stoma was planned before hemipelvectomy to allow complete NPWT coverage.

By day 8, the creatinine level decreased to 0.48 mg/dL, allowing CT angiography, which revealed “right” DIEA and external iliac artery occlusion (see Video [online]), precluding RAM flap harvest on that side. The “left” rectus abdominis muscle was selected for the flap, and the gastrointestinal surgeons were instructed to create the colostomy within the right rectus abdominis muscle. On day 10, a right-sided ascending colostomy was performed laparoscopically, followed by hemipelvectomy on day 14. A colostomy rather than an ileostomy was selected to avoid a high-output stoma and dehydration in this critically ill patient and to provide more manageable stool consistency during prolonged peristomal wound care under NPWT. NPWT was applied at –80 mm Hg (continuous), with dressing changes performed twice weekly. Dressing changes were performed without general anesthesia. An airtight seal was achieved using gauze and occlusive film. (See figure, Supplemental Digital Content 1, which displays the following wide debridement and hemipelvectomy as the wound was managed with NPWT. The stoma pouch is indicated with an asterisk, https://links.lww.com/PRSGO/F195.) Particular attention was paid to sealing around the anus and intergluteal fold by carefully packing gauze into the cleft to eliminate uneven surfaces and dead space before applying the occlusive film. After 4 additional debridements, definitive reconstruction was performed on day 43 using a vertically oriented DIEA-based RAM flap (3.5 × 19 cm; Fig. 2; see Video [online]). The flap was rotated inferiorly toward the pelvic-perineal defect through a direct skin bridge between the donor and recipient sites, without subcutaneous tunneling, to avoid pedicle compression. The limited width of the vertical skin paddle allowed primary closure of the abdominal donor site without mesh reinforcement. The skin paddle was limited to the perineal portion requiring durable coverage, whereas the remaining defect was reconstructed with muscle and split-thickness skin grafting.

Empirical broad-spectrum antimicrobial therapy was initiated with meropenem and subsequently de-escalated to ampicillin/sulbactam and later piperacillin/tazobactam based on blood culture and intraoperative tissue culture results. Antimicrobial therapy was continued until adequate infection control and wound stabilization were achieved.

The patient was discharged on day 76. At 10-month follow-up, no abdominal wall hernia was observed, and she maintained independent wheelchair sitting. The infection was attributed to descending colon carcinoma with mucosal disruption. Treatment details are provided in Figure 3.

DISCUSSION

The NSTI involving the pelvis carries high mortality, and survival depends on prompt source control and stable soft-tissue management.3 When infection extends beyond the hip joint, hemipelvectomy may be required; however, it creates massive defects, and postoperative control of the perineal–anal region is critical. Difficulty managing these defects directly contributes to mortality, highlighting the need for a structured reconstructive strategy.3,4

NPWT is central to wound-bed preparation; however, achieving a seal in the perineal–anal region is challenging due to air leakage and stool contamination. Fecal diversion has been reported to facilitate reliable NPWT application,5,6 consistent with our case. Diverting stoma creation enabled complete NPWT coverage of the anus, allowing stable negative pressure and safe progression to reconstruction.

Reconstructive options include free flaps and pedicled flaps such as the anterolateral thigh flap, gracilis flap, tensor fasciae latae flap, latissimus dorsi flaps, and limb-bank free flaps. However, in the present case, emergent hip disarticulation precluded preservation of the amputated limb as a donor source. Nevertheless, the RAM flap remains a dependable option because it provides reliable vascularity, sufficient bulk, and a long arc of rotation for obliterating deep contaminated cavities, which cannot be achieved by skin grafts alone.3,6,7 Extended vertical RAM flaps, reported by Villa et al,8 increase flap reach and volume, enabling coverage of extensive pelvic defects that would otherwise require multiple or free flaps. Importantly, as a diverting stoma is typically created through the rectus abdominis muscle, the RAM flap must be harvested from the contralateral side, making laterality planning essential.

Preoperative CT angiography is critical for assessing DIEA patency. Pelvic and iliac procedures can cause arterial occlusion, compromising the DIEA.9,10 In this case, right DIEA and external iliac artery occlusions were identified, likely related to prior surgery. Accordingly, a left-sided RAM flap and right-sided stoma were planned. Surgeons must, therefore, confirm vascular status and coordinate stoma laterality preoperatively.

In our institution, orthopedic surgeons performed both pelvic resection and complex soft-tissue reconstruction, gastrointestinal surgeons created the stoma, and plastic surgeons assisted when necessary. This case demonstrates that successful reconstruction requires vascular evaluation, precise laterality planning, and multidisciplinary coordination. Establishing this sequence preoperatively is essential for durable soft-tissue coverage. A conceptual framework of this approach is illustrated in Figure 4.

Conceptual schema of the present case. A diverting stoma (A) was created within the rectus abdominis muscle on the side with the occluded DIEA (B), thereby enabling contamination-free NPWT (C) and healthy granulation near the perineum. Final coverage was achieved with a RAM flap (D) based on the patent contralateral artery (E). Because the stoma must be elevated before hemipelvectomy, the flap surgeon should confirm the patency of the DIEA preoperatively and communicate the appropriate side for stoma creation to the general surgeon.

CONCLUSIONS

Reconstruction after hemipelvectomy for NSTI requires precise laterality planning of rectus-based stoma creation and RAM flap harvest. Preoperative CT angiography is essential to confirm DIEA patency. Coordinated sequencing—including fecal diversion, NPWT, and contralateral RAM flap coverage—is critical to preserve the donor muscle and achieve stable soft-tissue closure.
