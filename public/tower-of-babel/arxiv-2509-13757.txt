On the geometry of WDVV equations and their Hamiltonian formalism in arbitrary dimension
S. Opanasenko INFN, sezione di Leccevia per Arnesano, 73100 Lecce, Italyand Institute of Mathematics of NAS of Ukraine3 Tereshchenkivska Str., 01024 Kyiv, Ukraine stanislav.opanasenko@le.infn.it, R. Vitolo Dipartimento di Matematica e Fisica “E. De Giorgi” Università del Salentovia per Arnesano, 73100 Lecce, Italy and INFN sezione di Lecce raffaele.vitolo@unisalento.it

Abstract
It is known that in low dimensions WDVV equations can be rewritten as commuting quasilinear bi-Hamiltonian systems. We extend some of these results to arbitrary dimension N N and arbitrary scalar product η \eta . In particular, we show that WDVV equations can be interpreted as a set of linear line congruences in suitable Plücker embeddings. This form leads to their representation as Hamiltonian systems of conservation laws. Moreover, we show that in low dimensions and for an arbitrary η \eta WDVV equations can be reduced to passive orthonomic form. This leads to the commutativity of the Hamiltonian systems of conservation laws. We conjecture that such a result holds in all dimensions. MSC 2020 classification : Primary 37K10 secondary 37K20, 37K25. Keywords: Hamiltonian formalism, WDVV equations, line congruence.

1.1
WDVV equations

Witten–Dijkgraaf–Verlinde–Verlinde (WDVV) equations arose within the
framework of topological field theory
[
16
,
67
]
.
Later, Dubrovin
[
21
]
found striking connections between them and the theory of
Integrable Systems, which led him to introduce Frobenius manifolds.
The
potential
of a Frobenius manifold is a solution of the WDVV
equations.
Nowadays, Frobenius Manifolds are a very active research subject among
mathematicians: see,
e.g.
, the recent works

[

,
7
,

,

,

,

,

,

,

,

,

,
68
]
.
Another remarkable achievement was the discovery
in
[
39
]
that a recursive formula for Gromov–Witten
invariants could be found by solving WDVV equations for
\mathbb{P}^{2}
.
WDVV equations are also actively studied within the community of Theoretical
Physicists, with ongoing research on supersymmetric quantum
mechanics
[
6
,
31
,
40
]
,
topological quantum field theory
[
33
]
,
string theory
[
10
,
58
]

and supersymmetric gauge theory
[
36
]
.

We introduce WDVV equations following the presentation
in
[
22
]

by defining the potential
F:\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}
such that

1.

\eta_{\alpha\beta}:=F_{1\alpha\beta}
is a constant symmetric
nondegenerate matrix, which we interpret as a metric
\eta

on
\mathbb{R}^{N}
;

2.

c^{\gamma}_{\alpha\beta}:=\eta^{\gamma\epsilon}F_{\epsilon\alpha\beta}

(here
(\eta^{\alpha\beta})=(\eta_{\alpha\beta})^{-1}
)
are the structure constants of an associative algebra;

3.

F
is quasihomogeneous:

F(c^{d_{1}}t^{1},\ldots,c^{d_{N}}t^{N})=c^{d_{F}}F(t^{1},\ldots,t^{N})
.

Here and below,

F_{\alpha\beta\gamma}:=\frac{\partial^{3}F}{\partial t^{\alpha}\partial t^{\beta}\partial t^{\gamma}}.

If
e_{1}
,…,
e_{N}
is the basis of
\mathbb{R}^{N}
then the algebra operation
is
e_{\alpha}\cdot e_{\beta}=c^{\gamma}_{\alpha\beta}(\mathbf{t})e_{\gamma}
with

e_{1}
being the unity.
The associativity condition for this operation takes the form of the
following system of PDEs:

S_{\alpha\beta\gamma\nu}:=\eta^{\lambda\mu}(F_{\lambda\alpha\beta}F_{\mu\gamma\nu}-F_{\lambda\alpha\nu}F_{\mu\gamma\beta})=0.

(1.1)

It can be reduced to a system with the unknown function

f=f(t^{2},\ldots,t^{N})
, as the above requirements completely specify the
functional dependence of
F
on
t^{1}

up to second degree polynomials
,

F(t^{1},\dots,t^{N})=\frac{1}{6}\eta_{11}(t^{1})^{3}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k>1}\eta_{1k}t^{k}(t^{1})^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k,s>1}\eta_{sk}t^{s}t^{k}t^{1}+f(t^{2},\ldots,t^{N}).

The WDVV equations are the overdetermined nonlinear system of PDEs (
1.1
)
in one unknown function
f
of
N-1
independent variables. Quasihomogeneity
in (
3
) can be added as a further requirement on the solutions of the
WDVV equations (sometimes people refer to the system (
1.1
) without this
condition as associativity equations).

Two choices determine WDVV equations: the dimension
N
and the metric
\eta
.
It is proved in
[
21
]
that, in the case of distinct quasihomogeneity
weights, unity-preserving linear transformations of
\mathbb{R}^{N}
bring the
metric
\eta
to the canonical forms
\eta^{(1)}=(\eta^{(1)}_{\alpha\beta})

when
\eta_{11}=0
, with

\eta^{(1)}=\begin{pmatrix}0&&1\\
&\iddots&\\
1&&0\end{pmatrix}

and
\eta^{(2)}=(\eta^{(2)}_{\alpha\beta})
when
\eta_{11}\neq 0
, with

\eta^{(2)}=\begin{pmatrix}\mu&&1\\
&\iddots&\\
1&&0\end{pmatrix}

where
\mu\neq 0
.

1.2
First problem: determining nontrivial
WDVV equations

The first nontrivial WDVV equation appears in dimension
N=3

[
21
]
:
it is a single equation on
f=f(x,t)
, where
x=t^{2}
,
t=t^{3}
.
In particular, for a canonical form
\eta=\eta^{(1)}
it is:

f_{ttt}=f_{xxt}^{2}-f_{xxx}f_{xtt}.

(1.2)

In dimension
N=4
, for a canonical form
\eta=\eta^{(1)}
,
after relabeling the independent variables as
x:=t^{2}
,
y:=t^{3}
,
z:=t^{4}
, we have the WDVV system of six equations
[
21
]

\begin{split}&-2f_{xyz}-f_{xyy}f_{xxy}+f_{yyy}f_{xxx}=0,\\
&-f_{xzz}-f_{xyy}f_{xxz}+f_{yyz}f_{xxx}=0,\\
&-2f_{xyz}f_{xxz}+f_{xzz}f_{xxy}+f_{yzz}f_{xxx}=0,\\
&f_{xxy}f_{yyz}-f_{xxz}f_{yyy}+f_{yzz}=0,\\
&f_{zzz}-(f_{xyz})^{2}+f_{xzz}f_{xyy}-f_{yyz}f_{xxz}+f_{yzz}f_{xxy}=0,\\
&f_{yyy}f_{xzz}-2f_{yyz}f_{xyz}+f_{yzz}f_{xyy}=0.\end{split}

(1.3)

We observe a feature of the above system that is implicitly present
in
[
26
]
. If we choose one independent variable, say
x

(without loss of generality), and introduce new field variables:

\displaystyle u^{1}=f_{xxx},\ u^{2}=f_{xxy},\ u^{3}=f_{xyy},\ u^{4}=f_{xxz},\ u^{5}=f_{xyz},\ u^{6}=f_{xzz},

(1.4)

then we can solve the WDVV system (
1.3
) with respect to the remaining variables
f_{yyy}
,
f_{yyz}
,

f_{yzz}
,
f_{zzz}
(
x
-free derivatives) in an explicit way.
More precisely, in the system (
1.3
) all equations are linear with respect to the
x
-free derivatives.
After solving a subsystem of any four equations, there are two remaining equations,
both of which are identically satisfied by means of the values of the
x
-free derivatives
obtained in terms of the
x
-derivative variables (
1.4
).
So, the WDVV system (
1.3
) is actually equivalent to the system

\begin{split}&f_{yyy}=\frac{2u^{5}+u^{2}u^{3}}{u^{1}},\quad f_{yyz}=\frac{u^{3}u^{4}+u^{6}}{u^{1}},\quad f_{yzz}=\frac{2u^{4}u^{5}-u^{2}u^{6}}{u^{1}},\\
&f_{zzz}=(u^{5})^{2}-u^{3}u^{6}+\frac{(u^{3}u^{4})^{2}+u^{4}u^{6}-2u^{2}u^{4}u^{5}+(u^{2})^{2}u^{6}}{u^{1}}.\end{split}

Problem:
can we generalize the above feature to WDVV equations for
generic metrics
\eta
and dimensions
N
? More precisely, in the general
case, having chosen an arbitrary independent variable
t^{p}
, is it possible to
reduce the WDVV system to a system that consists of equations for
t^{p}
-free
derivative variables in terms of
t^{p}
-derivative variables?
After having achieved the above result, what about compatibility of the
“reduced” WDVV system?

1.3
Second problem: finding a Hamiltonian formulation

The integrability of the WDVV equations was considered in
[
21
]
, where a
Lax pair for the WDVV equations was provided. The geometric nature of the WDVV
equations makes the Lax pair nature quite transparent: since the WDVV equations
are conditions of flatness of a Levi-Civita connection (see also

[
19
]
), the (linear) equation of flat
vectors/covectors can be interpreted as its Lax pair.

Moreover, it was shown in
[
24
]
that the simplest WDVV
equation (
1.2
) (
N=3
,
\eta=\eta^{(1)}
) is integrable also
in the sense that it is bi-Hamiltonian. Since the Hamiltonian formalism for
PDEs is developed mostly for evolutionary equations, the Authors used the idea
of Mokhov
[
48
]
to rewrite the
equation (
1.2
) as a first-order quasilinear system (or a
hydrodynamic-type system) of PDEs. More precisely, introducing the coordinates

u^{1}=f_{xxx}
,
u^{2}=f_{xxt}
,
u^{3}=f_{xtt}
,
u^{4}=f_{ttt}
we observe that
compatibility conditions for such coordinates are

u^{1}_{t}=u^{2}_{x},\quad u^{2}_{t}=u^{3}_{x},\quad u^{3}_{t}=u^{4}_{x}.

With the help of the equation (
1.2
), the above compatibility conditions
take the form of the following first-order quasilinear system of PDEs:

u^{1}_{t}=u^{2}_{x},\quad u^{2}_{t}=u^{3}_{x},\quad u^{3}_{t}=((u^{2})^{2}-u^{1}u^{3})_{x}.

(1.5)

We will call such a system a
first-order WDVV system
.
It is of the general conservative form

u^{i}_{t}=(V^{i}(\mathbf{u}))_{x}=\mathchoice{\frac{\partial V^{i}}{\partial u^{j}}}{\partial V^{i}/\partial u^{j}}{\partial V^{i}/\partial u^{j}}{\partial V^{i}/\partial u^{j}}u^{j}_{x},

(1.6)

where
u^{i}=u^{i}(t,x)
are field variables,
i=1
, …,
n
,
n
is the
number of
x
-derivative variables, and
\mathbf{u}=(u^{1},\dots,u^{n})
.
We observe that it is possible to
reconstruct a solution
F
(up to second-degree polynomials in the independent
variables) from a solution of the above system of conservation laws (see
Remark
3.3
). That is likely possible in dimensions
N\geq 4
(although it has not been investigated).

For such systems it is possible to look for a bi-Hamiltonian
formalism of the type

u_{t}^{i}=A_{1}^{ij}\mathchoice{\frac{\delta H_{2}}{\delta u^{j}}}{\delta H_{2}/\delta u^{j}}{\delta H_{2}/\delta u^{j}}{\delta H_{2}/\delta u^{j}}=A_{2}^{ij}\mathchoice{\frac{\delta H_{1}}{\delta u^{j}}}{\delta H_{1}/\delta u^{j}}{\delta H_{1}/\delta u^{j}}{\delta H_{1}/\delta u^{j}},

with respect to two
compatible
local Hamiltonian operators
A_{1}

and
A_{2}
. We recall that an operator
A
is called Hamiltonian if and only
if the operation

\{F,G\}_{A}=\int\mathchoice{\frac{\delta F}{\delta u^{i}}}{\delta F/\delta u^{i}}{\delta F/\delta u^{i}}{\delta F/\delta u^{i}}A^{ij}\mathchoice{\frac{\delta G}{\delta u^{j}}}{\delta G/\delta u^{j}}{\delta G/\delta u^{j}}{\delta G/\delta u^{j}}\,\mathrm{d}x

is a Poisson bracket, that is, it is skew-adjoint and satisfies the Jacobi
identity (see e.g.
[
11
,
17
,
32
,
41
,
45
,
51
]
). Compatibility of two Hamiltonian
operators
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
means that any linear combination

\lambda A_{1}+\mu A_{2}
yields another Poisson bracket.

More generally, it was proved in
[
61
]
that in dimension
N=3

the WDVV system admits a bi-Hamiltonian formalism for any choice of
\eta
.

In all cases, one of the Hamiltonian operators is a homogeneous first-order
operator of Ferapontov type
[
23
]

\displaystyle A_{1}^{ij}=g^{ij}\mathrm{D}_{x}{}+\Gamma^{ij}_{k}u^{k}_{x}+c^{\alpha\beta}w^{i}_{\alpha q}u^{q}_{x}\mathrm{D}_{x}^{-1}w^{j}_{\beta p}u^{p}_{x},

(1.7)

where
c^{\alpha\beta}
is a constant symmetric matrix and all other
coefficients are functions of
(u^{i})
(which correspond to the
t^{p}
-derivative
variables). The other Hamiltonian operator is a homogeneous third-order
operator of Dubrovin–Novikov type
[
20
]
in the canonical
Doyle–Potëmin form
[
8
,
18
,
54
,
55
]
,

A_{2}^{ij}=\mathrm{D}_{x}\circ(f^{ij}\mathrm{D}_{x}+c^{ij}_{k}u^{k}_{x})\circ\mathrm{D}_{x}.

(1.8)

Such a pair is not limited exclusively to WDVV equations. Indeed,
in
[
52
]
we considered such “WDVV-type” systems
and classified them in low dimensions.

When
N=4
and
\eta=\eta^{(1)}
it was proved
[
26
]
that
the compatibility conditions for the new variables (third-order derivatives
of
f
) can be written, using the WDVV equations, as a pair of
commuting

hydrodynamic type systems in a conservative form:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\begin{cases}u^{1}_{y}=u^{2}_{x},\\
u^{2}_{y}=u^{3}_{x},\\
u^{3}_{y}=u^{5}_{x},\\[4.30554pt]
u^{4}_{y}=\left(\dfrac{2u^{5}+u^{2}u^{3}}{u^{1}}\right)_{x}\\[12.91663pt]
u^{5}_{y}=\left(\dfrac{u^{3}u^{4}+u^{6}}{u^{1}}\right)_{x}\\[12.91663pt]
u^{6}_{y}=\left(\dfrac{2u^{4}u^{5}-u^{2}u^{6}}{u^{1}}\right)_{x}\end{cases}\end{aligned}\qquad\begin{aligned} &\begin{cases}u^{1}_{z}=u^{4}_{x},\\
u^{2}_{z}=u^{5}_{x},\\
u^{3}_{z}=u^{6}_{x},\\[4.30554pt]
u^{4}_{z}=\left(\dfrac{u^{3}u^{4}+u^{6}}{u^{1}}\right)_{x}\\[12.91663pt]
u^{5}_{z}=\left(\dfrac{2u^{4}u^{5}-u^{2}u^{6}}{u^{1}}\right)_{x}\\[12.91663pt]
u^{6}_{z}=\left((u^{5})^{2}-u^{3}u^{6}+\dfrac{u^{3}(u^{4})^{2}+u^{4}u^{6}-2u^{2}u^{4}u^{5}+(u^{2})^{2}u^{6}}{u^{1}}\right)_{x}\end{cases}\end{aligned}

The Authors of
[
26
]
also proved that the above systems
admit the same
local
(
c^{\alpha\beta}=0
) Ferapontov-type Hamiltonian
operator, of course, with two distinct Hamiltonian densities.
It was later found in
[
53
]
that the above two systems admit the same

compatible
with the above operator third-order operator of the type (
1.8
).

Hamiltonian pairs were also found in the cases
N=4
,

\eta=\eta^{(2)}
and
N=5
,
\eta=\eta^{(1)}
,
\eta=\eta^{(2)}

[
43
,
61
,
62
]
.

Problem:
is it always true that, by introducing new variables, we can
express WDVV equations as a finite number of
commuting
quasilinear
systems of first-order conservation laws?
If the above is true, is it always true that the commuting quasilinear systems
of first-order conservation laws will have
the same

Hamiltonian operators?

1.4
Main results

We have been able to prove the following results.

Hamiltonian formalism.

The WDVV equations contain subsystems that
can be rewritten, after a choice of a distinguished independent variable

t^{p}
, as
N-2
quasilinear first-order systems of conservation laws, each of
which is Hamiltonian with respect to the same homogeneous third-order
Hamiltonian operator in canonical form (
1.8
), see
Theorem
4.16
. The steps to achieve this result are

•

the Structure Theorem
4.8
, where we observe
that WDVV equations contain subsystems that can be identified with linear
line complexes in suitable Plücker embeddings;

•

the Metric Theorem
4.10
, where we complete the
proof of the existence and uniqueness of a Hamiltonian operator as above.

We observe that a Hamiltonian operator as above is uniquely determined by its
leading coefficient matrix, which is a Monge metric (see

[
27
,
28
]
). Such a metric determines a quadratic line complex, which
is a variety of lines in the Plücker embedding of the projective space
determined in coordinates by the field variables. In other words, the choice of
an independent variable
t^{p}
determines a quadratic line complex, which ‘sits’
in WDVV equations. It is not unlikely that such an object is more than just a
mathematical curiosity.

We could not prove that all first-order WDVV systems can be endowed with a
first-order Hamiltonian operator. However, we proved by direct computation,
using results from a forthcoming paper
[
43
]
, that
in the dimensions
N=4
and
N=5
the first-order WDVV systems associated with

\eta=\eta^{(2)}
, and obtained after the choice
t^{p}=t^{2}
, admit
a Ferapontov-type first-order nonlocal Hamiltonian operator of the
form (
1.7
). Such operator is compatible with the third-order
operator, hence first-order WDVV systems turn out to be bi-Hamiltonian of
WDVV-type. See the discussion in Section
6
.

It is natural to
conjecture
that all first-order WDVV systems admit a
first-order operator of Ferapontov type that is compatible with the
third-order operator found in this paper for all such systems.

Reducibility, compatibility and commutativity.

Compatibility of WDVV equations is still an open problem. We observed that such
conditions are at the heart of the commutativity of the above systems of
conservation laws. Indeed, we were able to prove that:

•

In dimensions
N=4
and
N=5
, and for arbitrary choice of the scalar
product
\eta
, the WDVV equations can be reduced as follows. Having chosen
an independent variable
t^{p}
, the WDVV equations can be solved for all

t^{p}
-free third-order derivatives, and are equivalent to a system of the
form

f_{ijk}=G^{ijk}(f_{plm}),\qquad i,j,k\in\{2,\ldots,N\}\setminus\{p\}

(Theorem
5.4
and Proposition
5.7
).

•

The above system is compatible, or more precisely, the system is in a
passive orthonomic form. This means that it does not give rise to any
hidden integrability conditions (Corollary
5.7
).

•

Compatibility implies the fact that the above Hamiltonian systems
determined by the choice of
t^{p}
commute (Theorem
5.8
).

•

In general, WDVV equations can be split into two subsystems, one of which
contains only linear equations with respect to
t^{p}
-free derivatives. We
have proved, that this subsystem can be solved with respect to
t^{p}
-free
derivatives, that is, it is in an orthonomic form. Based on the examples
above, we
conjecture
that the remaining equations vanish in view of
the expressions for
t^{p}
-free derivatives.

2
Preliminaries

2.1
The metric
\eta

and related constructions

We consider the metric
\eta
, which is the crucial data in the framework of WDVV equations,
as the nondegenerate symmetric bilinear form

\eta\colon\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}.

Let us denote by
\bar{\eta}
the inverse metric on
(\mathbb{R}^{N})^{*}
.
If
\{e_{i}\mid i=1,\ldots,N\}
is a basis of
\mathbb{R}^{N}
and
\{e^{i}\mid i=1,\ldots,N\}

is the dual basis of
(\mathbb{R}^{N})^{*}
, then we set
\eta_{ij}:=\eta(e_{i},e_{j})
.
The matrix
(\eta_{ij})
is invertible; its inverse is denoted by
(\eta^{ij})
,
and it is the matrix of
\bar{\eta}
.
We can regard the metric
\eta
as a nondegenerate, symmetric tensor

\eta\in(\mathbb{R}^{N})^{*}\otimes(\mathbb{R}^{N})^{*}
; its inverse is identified with the symmetric
nondegenerate tensor
\bar{\eta}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\otimes\mathbb{R}^{N}
.
The metric
\eta
defines a metric
\eta^{r}_{s}
on the space of
r
-contravariant
and
s
-covariant tensors

\otimes^{r}_{s}\mathbb{R}^{N}=\otimes^{r}\mathbb{R}^{N}\otimes\otimes^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{N})^{*}
as follows. On
decomposable elements

t=v_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes v_{r}\otimes\alpha^{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\alpha^{s},\quad u=w_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes w_{r}\otimes\beta^{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\beta^{s}

we define

\eta^{r}_{s}(t,u)=\left(\prod_{i=1}^{r}\eta(v_{i},w_{i})\right)\left(\prod_{j=1}^{s}\bar{\eta}(\alpha^{j},\beta^{j})\right),

and uniquely extend the definition to arbitrary tensors by
bilinearity. Non-degeneracy is again clear. Recalling that a basis of

\otimes^{r}_{s}\mathbb{R}^{N}
is given by all elements of the form

e_{i_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes e_{i_{r}}\otimes e^{a_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes e^{a_{s}}
, the coordinate expression of
\eta^{r}_{s}
is

\eta^{r}_{s}(e_{i_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes e_{i_{r}}\otimes e^{a_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes e^{a_{s}},e_{j_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes e_{j_{r}}\otimes e^{b_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes e^{b_{s}})=\eta_{i_{1}j_{1}}\cdots\eta_{i_{r}j_{r}}\eta^{a_{1}b_{1}}\cdots\eta^{a_{s}b_{s}}.

The above metric can be restricted to the exterior algebra of
r
-vectors

\wedge^{r}\mathbb{R}^{N}\subset\otimes^{r}_{0}\mathbb{R}^{N}=\otimes^{r}\mathbb{R}^{N}
. Recalling that a basis of

\wedge^{r}\mathbb{R}^{N}
is provided by

\{e_{i_{1}}\wedge\cdots\wedge e_{i_{r}}\mid 1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{r}\leq N\}
and that

e_{i_{1}}\wedge\cdots\wedge e_{i_{r}}=\frac{1}{r!}\sum_{\sigma\in S_{r}}|\sigma|e_{\sigma(i_{1})}\otimes\cdots\otimes e_{\sigma(i_{r})}

we have the coordinate expression

\eta^{r}_{0}(e_{i_{1}}\wedge\cdots\wedge e_{i_{r}},e_{j_{1}}\wedge\cdots\wedge e_{j_{r}})=\frac{1}{r!}\eta_{i_{1}\cdots i_{r},j_{1}\cdots j_{r}}

where by
\eta_{i_{1}\cdots i_{r},j_{1}\cdots j_{r}}
we indicate the
retainer

minor
[
63
, p. 18]
of
\eta=(\eta_{ij})
,
made by selecting the rows
i_{1}
, …,
i_{r}
and the columns
j_{1}
, …,
j_{r}

and then taking the determinant of the resulting square submatrix of order
r
.
In particular, when
r=2
we have

\eta^{2}_{0}(e_{i_{1}}\wedge e_{i_{2}},e_{j_{1}}\wedge e_{j_{2}})=\eta_{i_{1}i_{2},j_{1}j_{2}}:=\frac{1}{2}(\eta_{i_{1}j_{1}}\eta_{i_{2}j_{2}}-\eta_{i_{1}j_{2}}\eta_{i_{2}j_{1}}).

We can repeat the entire construction for
s
-forms

\wedge^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{N})^{*}\subset\otimes^{0}_{s}\mathbb{R}^{N}=\otimes^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{N})^{*}
, and obtain
the inverse metric
\eta^{0}_{s}
. In particular:

\eta^{0}_{2}(e^{i_{1}}\wedge e^{i_{2}},e^{j_{1}}\wedge e^{j_{2}})=\eta^{i_{1}i_{2},j_{1}j_{2}}=\frac{1}{2}(\eta^{i_{1}j_{1}}\eta^{i_{2}j_{2}}-\eta^{i_{1}j_{2}}\eta^{i_{2}j_{1}}).

We can regard a metric on
\wedge^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{N})^{*}
as a symmetric tensor

\phi\in S^{2}(\wedge^{2}\mathbb{R}^{N})\subset(\wedge^{2}\mathbb{R}^{N})\otimes(\wedge^{2}\mathbb{R}^{N}),

where we have denoted by
S^{2}(\wedge^{2}\mathbb{R}^{N})
the subspace of symmetric
tensors. We observe that there is a natural linear map

C\colon S^{2}(\wedge^{2}\mathbb{R}^{N})\to\wedge^{4}\mathbb{R}^{N},\quad X\otimes Y\mapsto X\wedge Y.

The coordinate expression of the above map
C
is obtained as follows.
Let
\phi\in S^{2}(\wedge^{2}\mathbb{R}^{N})
be of the form

\phi^{i_{1}j_{1},i_{2}j_{2}}e_{i_{1}}\wedge e_{j_{1}}\otimes e_{i_{2}}\wedge e_{j_{2}},

where the sum is extended to all indices fulfilling the conditions

i_{1}<j_{1}
and
i_{2}<j_{2}
. Then, the image of the map
C
is

\phi^{i_{1}j_{1},i_{2}j_{2}}e_{i_{1}}\wedge e_{j_{1}}\wedge e_{i_{2}}\wedge e_{j_{2}},

where

3
First-order WDVV systems

As we have mentioned in Introduction, the WDVV equations (

overdetermined nonlinear system of PDEs in the unknown function

. In low dimensions and for particular values of

Here, we would like to write down the transformation of WDVV equations into

\eta

arbitrary value of the dimension

Our framework is that of the Geometry of PDEs, which is based on jet spaces.

We recall some material from the theory of jet spaces. We will follow

The WDVV system (

The WDVV system in the orthonomic form (

∎

4
WDVV equations as Hamiltonian systems

In this Section we would like to prove the Structure Theorem, which is the
‘heart’ of the paper. Indeed, WDVV equations can be interpreted as families of
linear systems of equations in Plücker coordinates, with respect to the
Plücker embedding of a suitable projective space, discussed in
Subsection
2.2
.

Each linear systems of equations as above is of the type (studied
in
[
29
]
) that is associated with systems of conservation
laws (
1.6
) with a Hamiltonian structure of the third
order (
1.8
). Indeed, by means of the Metric Theorem we will prove
that such Hamiltonian structures exist and are unique.

4.1
Symmetries of WDVV equations and trivial cases

We begin our analysis by an enumeration of the most
evident symmetry properties of the equations. This leads us to preliminary
results on the identification of trivial WDVV equations.

Lemma 4.1
.

The WDVV equations fulfill the following symmetry properties:

\displaystyle S_{\alpha\beta\gamma\nu}=S_{\gamma\nu\alpha\beta},

(4.1a)

\displaystyle S_{\alpha\beta\gamma\nu}=S_{\beta\alpha\nu\gamma},

(4.1b)

\displaystyle S_{\alpha\beta\gamma\nu}=-S_{\alpha\nu\gamma\beta},

(4.1c)

\displaystyle S_{\alpha\beta\gamma\nu}=S_{\alpha\beta\nu\gamma}+S_{\alpha\gamma\beta\nu},

(4.1d)

where all indices run from
1
to
N
.

We remind that the above symmetry properties are inherited by the fact that the
WDVV conditions can be interpreted as a zero curvature condition (see

[
50
]
). In particular, the condition (
4.1d
) is equivalent to the
Bianchi identity.

It follows that
S_{\alpha\beta\alpha\nu}=0
and
S_{\alpha\beta\gamma\beta}=0

identically; it is then an easy consequence that any set of indices with at
least
3
repeated indices yields an identically vanishing WDVV equation.

Lemma 4.2
.

Any set of indices with exactly two repeated indices yields only one WDVV
equation (up to an overall sign).

Proof.

There are twelve distinct sets of indices constructed from the set

\{\alpha,\alpha,\beta,\gamma\}
; using (
4.1
) we easily realize that
the corresponding equations are the same up to a sign.
∎

So, nontrivial equations are of one of the following types:

1.

S_{\alpha\alpha\beta\gamma}=0
, where
\alpha
,
\beta
,
\gamma
are
distinct and
\beta\leq\gamma
, and

2.

S_{\alpha\beta\gamma\nu}=0
, where all indices are distinct.

Lemma 4.3
.

The subsystem of WDVV equations obtained by means of a set of mutually
distinct indices
\{\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\nu\}
is equivalent to two
independent equations:

S_{\alpha\beta\gamma\nu}=0,\quad\text{and}\quad S_{\alpha\beta\nu\gamma}=0,

where
\alpha<\beta<\gamma<\nu
.

Proof.

It is easy to realize that we can always move a specified index in

S_{\alpha\beta\gamma\nu}
to the first place (up to a sign):

S_{\alpha\beta\gamma\nu}=S_{\beta\alpha\nu\gamma}=S_{\gamma\nu\alpha\beta}=-S_{\nu\alpha\beta\gamma}.

Moving to the beginning the smaller index
\alpha
, we remain with
6
cases:

\displaystyle S_{\alpha\beta\gamma\nu}=0,\quad S_{\alpha\gamma\beta\nu}=0,\quad S_{\alpha\nu\beta\gamma}=0,

\displaystyle S_{\alpha\beta\nu\gamma}=0,\quad S_{\alpha\nu\gamma\beta}=0,\quad S_{\alpha\gamma\nu\beta}=0,

which can be further reduced to

S_{\alpha\beta\gamma\nu}=0,\quad S_{\alpha\beta\nu\gamma}=0,\quad S_{\alpha\gamma\beta\nu}=0.

using (
4.1c
). In view of (
4.1d
) we have the result.
∎

In order to further analyze the WDVV equations we
introduce Latin indices
i
,
j
,
k
,…running
from
2
to
N
. We have the expressions

\begin{split}&F_{1}=\frac{1}{2}\eta_{11}(t^{1})^{2}+\eta_{1k}t^{k}t^{1}+\frac{1}{2}\eta_{sk}t^{s}t^{k},\\
&F_{i}=\frac{1}{2}\eta_{1i}(t^{1})^{2}+\eta_{ik}t^{k}t^{1}+f_{i},\\
&F_{11}=\eta_{11}t^{1}+\eta_{1k}t^{k},\\
&F_{1i}=\eta_{1i}t^{1}+\eta_{ik}t^{k}.\end{split}

(4.2)

Lemma 4.4
.

The expression
S_{11ij}
vanishes identically.

Proof.

\displaystyle S_{11ij}=

\displaystyle\eta^{\lambda\mu}(F_{\lambda 11}F_{\mu ij}-F_{\lambda 1j}F_{\mu i1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\eta^{11}(F_{111}F_{1ij}-F_{11j}F_{1i1})+\eta^{1k}(F_{111}F_{kij}-F_{11j}F_{ki1})

\displaystyle+\eta^{k1}(F_{k11}F_{1ij}-F_{k1j}F_{1i1})+\eta^{hk}(F_{h11}F_{kij}-F_{h1j}F_{ki1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\eta^{11}(\eta_{11}\eta_{ij}-\eta_{1j}\eta_{1i})+\eta^{1k}(\eta_{11}f_{kij}-\eta_{1j}\eta_{ki})

\displaystyle+\eta^{k1}(\eta_{1k}\eta_{ij}-\eta_{kj}\eta_{1i})+\eta^{hk}(\eta_{1h}f_{kij}-\eta_{hj}\eta_{ki})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\eta^{11}\eta_{11}+\eta^{k1}\eta_{1k})\eta_{ij}-(\eta^{11}\eta_{1j}+\eta^{k1}\eta_{kj})\eta_{1i}

\displaystyle+(\eta^{1k}\eta_{11}+\eta^{hk}\eta_{1h})f_{kij}-(\eta^{1k}\eta_{1j}+\eta^{hk}\eta_{hj})\eta_{ki}

\displaystyle\eta_{ij}-\delta^{k}_{j}\eta_{ki}=0.

∎

Lemma 4.5
.

The expression
S_{1kij}
vanishes identically.

Proof.

\displaystyle S_{1kij}=

\displaystyle\eta^{\lambda\mu}(F_{\lambda 1k}F_{\mu ij}-F_{\lambda 1j}F_{\mu ik})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\eta^{11}(F_{11k}F_{1ij}-F_{11j}F_{1ik})+\eta^{1k}(F_{11k}F_{kij}-F_{11j}F_{kik})

\displaystyle+\eta^{k1}(F_{k1k}F_{1ij}-F_{k1j}F_{1ik})+\eta^{hk}(F_{h1k}F_{kij}-F_{h1j}F_{kik})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\eta^{11}(\eta_{1k}\eta_{ij}-\eta_{1j}\eta_{ik})+\eta^{1k}(\eta_{1k}f_{kij}-\eta_{1j}f_{kik})

\displaystyle+\eta^{k1}(\eta_{kk}\eta_{ij}-\eta_{kj}\eta_{ik})+\eta^{hk}(\eta_{hk}f_{kij}-\eta_{hj}f_{kik})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\eta^{11}\eta_{1k}+\eta^{k1}\eta_{kk})\eta_{ij}-(\eta^{11}\eta_{1j}+\eta^{k1}\eta_{kj})\eta_{ik}

\displaystyle+(\eta^{1k}\eta_{1k}+\eta^{hk}\eta_{hk})f_{kij}-(\eta^{1k}\eta_{1j}+\eta^{hk}\eta_{hj})f_{kik}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f_{kij}-f_{jik}=0.

4.6
The Hamiltonian Theorem

Let us choose two indices
\lambda
,
\mu
such that
2\leq\lambda<\nu\leq N
.
Then, according to the Structure Theorem, the subsystem of WDVV equations

S_{\lambda\beta\mu\nu}=0
can be written as

\psi^{\alpha}_{i}u^{i}_{\mu}=Z^{\alpha},

(4.12)

where
\psi^{\alpha}_{i}=\psi^{\alpha}_{ij}u^{j}_{\lambda}+{}_{\lambda}\omega^{\alpha}_{i}

and
Z^{\alpha}={}_{\mu}\omega^{\alpha}_{i}u^{i}_{\lambda}+{}_{\lambda\mu}\xi^{\alpha}
. We also know that there exists a scalar product

\phi
which fulfills the equations (
4.9
). We need to make a final step
before the Hamiltonian property can be proved: nondegeneracy of

\psi=(\psi^{\alpha}_{i})
. We will use the following simple Proposition.

Proposition 4.14
.

Let
\phi
be a nondegenerate symmetric bilinear form on a
k
-dimensional
real vector space
A
. Let
\{A^{\alpha}\mid\alpha=1,\ldots,n\}
be generators
of
A
. If the Gram matrix
(\phi(A^{\alpha},A^{\beta}))
is nondegenerate, then the generators
\{A^{\alpha}\}
are independent.

Proof.

The map
\cdot^{\flat}\colon A\to A^{*}
, defined by
\phi
, is a linear
isomorphism. If, say,
A^{1}=\sum_{\beta=2}^{n}k_{\beta}A^{\beta}
then

(A^{1})^{\flat}=\sum_{\beta=2}^{n}k_{\beta}(A^{\beta})^{\flat}.

Computing the above covector relation on the vectors in

\{A^{\alpha}\mid\alpha=1,\ldots,n\}
,
e.g.

(A^{1})^{\flat}(A^{\beta})=\phi(A^{1},A^{\beta})
and similarly on the right-hand
side, it turns out that the first row of the Gram matrix is a linear
combination of the other rows.
∎

Corollary 4.15
.

The matrix
\psi=(\psi^{\alpha}_{i})
is nondegenerate.

Proof.

It is a direct consequence of the Metric Theorem, which states that
\phi

coincides, in coordinates, with the (nondegenerate) metric induced naturally
by
\eta
on
\wedge^{2}\mathbb{R}^{N}
.
∎

Theorem 4.16

(Hamiltonian theorem)
.

Let
N\geq 3
, and choose an independent variable
t^{p}
,

. Then, we obtain

(u^{I}_{p})_{\mu}=(V^{I}_{\mu})_{p},\qquad I=1,\ldots,n,

(4.13)

where the fluxes are functions of
(u^{J}_{\lambda})

. Each of the above systems is Hamiltonian
with respect to one and the same third-order Hamiltonian operator

in
canonical form (

f_{ij}=\phi_{\alpha\beta}\psi^{\alpha}_{i}\psi^{\beta}_{j}
, where

is defined in the Structure Theorem

and

is defined in the Metric

Proof.

The fluxes of the systems are defined by:

(4.14)

where

. The conditions for the above

are, respectively, the third
and fourth equation of system (

and are a family of fluxes of
systems of conservation laws that are Hamiltonian with respect to
A_{2}
.

We already proved that, having chosen an independent variable
t^{p}
, all WDVV
first-order systems of conservation laws are Hamiltonian with respect to a
third-order Hamiltonian operator (Hamiltonian Theorem
4.16
).

Unfortunately, we do not have a similar result concerning a first-order
compatible operator. But, as we have many examples of bi-Hamiltonian WDVV
systems (see, for example,
[
61
]
) for particular values
of
\eta
, it is interesting to prove that such examples propagate to the orbit
of the action of the invariance group of the WDVV equations.

More precisely, it is known
[
22
]
that
the WDVV system admits as a group of symmetries the group of linear
transformations that preserve the unit vector field
\partial_{t^{1}}
:

\tilde{t}^{\alpha}=P^{\alpha}_{\beta}t^{\beta}+Q^{\alpha},\qquad\det(P^{\alpha}_{\beta})\neq 0,\quad P^{\alpha}_{1}=\delta^{\alpha}_{1},\quad\alpha,\beta=1,\dots,N.

This group obviously changes the value of the matrix
\eta
.

Proposition 6.2
.

A symmetry transformation of the WDVV
equations preserves the form of the bi-Hamiltonian pair in a bi-Hamiltonian
first-order WDVV system.

Proof.

The matrix
P=(P^{\alpha}_{\beta})
of the change of coordinates can be
factorized as

\displaystyle P=T_{1}\cdot T_{2},\qquad\text{where}

\displaystyle P=\begin{pmatrix}1&(P^{1}_{j})\\
0&(P^{i}_{j})\end{pmatrix},\quad T_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&(P^{i}_{j})\end{pmatrix},\quad T_{2}=\begin{pmatrix}1&P^{1}_{j}\\
0&I\end{pmatrix},

where
(P^{1}_{i})
is a row of length
N-1
,
P^{i}_{j}
is an invertible square
matrix of order
N-1
and
I
is the identity matrix.

The transformation
T_{2}
does not change third-order derivatives of
f
,
so we can restrict our attention to
T_{1}
.

It is already known
[
61
]
that invariance
transformations that involve only two independent variables preserve the form
of the Hamiltonian operators and that
T_{1}
maps first-order WDVV systems
into new first-order systems.

The proof is thus completed if we can decompose any invertible matrix into a
sequence of
2\times 2
blocks. And indeed, any matrix in

\mathrm{GL}(\mathbb{C}^{N-1})
can be generated by means of Gauss’
elementary matrices (up to permutations).
∎

Remark 6.3
.

We tacitly assumed that the pair of Hamiltonian operators remains compatible
after the transformation
T_{1}
. Indeed, the Schouten bracket has a geometric
definition, and we do expect that compatibility is preserved by
transformations. Computational experiments suggest that this is the case,
and the recent results in

[
44
,
43
]
(see also the above
Theorem
6.1
) seem to confirm it.

Remark 6.4
.

The transformation
T_{2}
does not bring any first-order WDVV system into
another first-order WDVV system in a direct way. Indeed, our way to obtain
families of commuting WDVV systems is attached to a given set of independent
coordinates and to the choice of one of them. We will analyze the
transformation properties of first-order WDVV systems under the symmetry
group of WDVV equations in the near future.

Since in all examples of first-order WDVV systems that we have been able to
deal with (by means of the computational resources and algorithms at our
disposal) we found a bi-Hamiltonian pair
A_{1}
,
A_{2}
of WDVV-type, we can
formulate a Conjecture.

In the general case we have
N-2
commuting flows
provided
we
can put the WDVV equations in passive orthonomic form: they are
given by the fluxes of the systems of the Hamiltonian
Theorem
4.16
. Moreover, the flow
\delta^{i}_{j}u^{j}_{x}\mathchoice{\frac{\partial}{\partial u^{i}}}{\partial/\partial u^{i}}{\partial/\partial u^{i}}{\partial/\partial u^{i}}

is trivially commuting with all others. So, the dimension of the matrix that
defines the nonlocal part of
A_{1}
must be taken as
N-1
. Let us define

\tilde{\eta}=(\eta^{ij})_{i,j\in\{2,\ldots,N\}}
.

Conjecture 2
.

Every first-order WDVV system admits a unique first-order Hamiltonian
operator
A_{1}
of Ferapontov type (
1.7
) that is compatible with
the third-order Hamiltonian operator from the Hamiltonian
Theorem
4.16
.

The constants
c^{\alpha\beta}
are defined by

(c^{\alpha\beta})_{\alpha,\beta=1}^{N-1}=\tilde{\eta}\det\tilde{\eta}
. In
particular,
A_{1}
is a local operator if and only if
\tilde{\eta}
is
degenerate.

We stress that we formulated the conjecture on the basis of several
computational experiments in dimensions up to
N=5
. More details will appear
in
[
43
]
.
