Comparing activated carbon and graphene-based electrodes using electrosorption process to quantify environmental impact associated with thorium extraction via LCA framework
Authors: Naseem Akhtar, Aznan Fazli Ismail, Marlia M. Hanafiah, Syazwani Binti Mohd Fadzil
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Thorium extraction from radioactive waste via various methods, including solvent extraction, ion exchange, adsorption, and electrosorption, raises significant concerns regarding radiological risks, human health, emissions, and other environmental impacts. To date, previous research estimated an optimal thorium recovery efficiency of 92% using electrosorption from a rare earth facility, though without a life cycle assessment (LCA) paradigm. To the best of our knowledge, this article quantified the environmental impact and emissions lifecycle of thorium extraction via the electrosorption process, employing activated carbon electrodes (ACE) and graphene-based electrodes (GBE) within a sustainable LCA framework. In this context, the inventory data for LCA were compiled from Ecoinvent database 3, sourcing input including raw material extraction, energy consumption, and chemical compounds. The comparative outcomes of midpoint analysis indicated that for each 1 kg of thorium extracted, the ACE system showed substantially higher environmental impacts than GBE, especially regarding human toxicity, freshwater ecotoxicity, and marine ecotoxicity, signifying a heightened release of toxins detrimental to ecosystems and human health. Also, the comparing results of endpoint indicators revealed that ACE showed a high impact over GBE in human health (0.0003–0.0001 DALY), ecosystems (7.14E-07–1.87E-07 species·yr), and resources (7.549–2.921 USD 2013), probably due to differences in chemical usage and emissions release during processing. In terms of output effectiveness and adverse environmental impacts, the GBE technique is more effective in removing thorium compared to ACE for the sustainable management of radioactive waste.

Introduction

Thorium, a naturally occurring element, is extracted from radioactive wastes associated with rare earth residues and used for numerous applications such as catalysis, high-temperature ceramics, high-quality optics, and nuclear medicine1. The systematic extraction of thorium from radioactive waste materials is crucial for the further advancement and application of thorium energy production2,3. Compared to uranium reactors, thorium-based systems have numerous advantages in the nuclear fuel sector, such as decreased operational expenses, limited radioactive waste production, and secure clean energy output4. However, thorium extraction processes provide considerable environmental issues owing to its chemical toxicity, radiotoxicity, and extended half-life, which caused a substantial risk to the natural environment and human well-being for millennia, especially in Malaysia1,5,6. The ongoing trend is expected to persist globally and Malaysia, necessitating careful consideration and requiring effective radioactive waste disposal systems or waste management technologies7.

Considering Malaysia is a significant hub for rare earth element (REEs), including the processing of minerals such as monazite, xenotime, and zircon, along with the presence of facilities like the Lynas Advanced Material Plant (LAMP) for rare-earth oxide extraction, a comprehensive environmental assessment of thorium extraction processes is essential8–10. The LAMP facility processes monazite concentrate sourced from Mount Weld, Australia, producing Water Leach Purification Residue (WLP) that contains thorium11,12. Conventional thorium extraction techniques, including cracking, leaching, ion exchange, adsorption, and solvent extraction, usually present concerns for human health and environmental consequences6,13–16. In Malaysia, electrosorption has emerged as a viable method for thorium extraction from radioactive waste and has focused on gaining notable interest based on the Activated Carbon Electrodes (ACE) and Graphene-Based Electrodes (GBE). Although the electrosorption technique is cost-effective, performs favorably at a low electrical potential, and provides high efficiency in thorium extraction17,18.

Previous research indicates that electrosorption utilizing GBE has achieved thorium recovery rates of 84–92%3,19, while ACE demonstrated recovery rates of up to 85% from rare earth industrial by-products20,21. Despite comparable recovery efficiency, the selection between GBE and ACE for thorium extraction is influenced by considerations including material synthesis complexity, operational energy requirements, and the overall environmental impact of each electrode type22. However, knowledge gaps remain in the rare earth nuclear industry; specifically, the significant environmental impacts associated with numerous chemical components, the energy used for laboratory testing, and water consumption during thorium extraction from radioactive waste remain mainly unknown23. Rectifying these drawbacks is essential; the environmental impacts of emissions from raw material consumption during the electrosorption process require great discourse due to their ecological significance. Previous investigations have demonstrated a high efficiency recovery of thorium using electrosorption at a rare earth factory; however, a life cycle assessment (LCA) has been lacked24,25.

In this regard, the LCA framework is a crucial tool for quantifying and evaluating the environmental impacts associated with emissions from raw materials, energy consumption, and instrumental analysis across the entire life cycle of the electrosorption process, considering its significant environmental concerns23,26–28. While the environmental impact of rare earth mineral production and processing is extensively researched29,30, a detailed LCA for thorium extraction using the electrosorption method has not been properly explored. Several LCA studies in analogous domains have examined the environmental repercussions of nuclear energy cycles, encompassing thorium-uranium-fueled systems31, as well as diverse rare earth production techniques, including separated rare earth oxides32, nuclear-derived hydrogen and ammonia production33, and monazite processing34. However, these investigations did not concentrate on the electrosorption approach utilizing ACE and GBE for thorium extraction from rare earth wastes, hence leaving a significant gap in our knowledge of its specific environmental profile.

This study addresses existing knowledge gaps by providing a comparative LCA of thorium extraction via electrosorption utilizing ACE and GBE. The study aims to measure the environmental impacts and emissions throughout the lifecycle, taking into account chemical compounds, raw material extraction, and energy consumption, in order to determine which type of electrode has a greater environmental footprint. This LCA methodology, incorporating input data from the Ecoinvent database and laboratory analyses, attempts to enhance thorium extraction methodologies and advance sustainable radioactive waste management and environmental conservation.

Methods and materials

The broader overview of thorium production in Malaysia is significant, considering the nation’s abundant mineral reserves, such as monazite, xenotime, zircon, and ilmenite, all classified as radioactive due to their thorium and uranium concentration. The LAMP is a large 100-hectare facility that serves as the largest REO extraction facility in Malaysia, which is crucial to rare earth oxide (REO) production in Pahang, Malaysia. LAMP produces monazite concentrate sourced from mining operations at Mount Weld, Western Australia, acknowledged as the largest rare earth processing facility globally35. The monazite mining process for thorium and rare earth oxides generally consists of three sequential stages: initial extraction and beneficiation of mineral sands, subsequent concentration of monazite minerals via flotation (extraction and separation of rare earth oxides), and finally, splitting, leaching, and purification. WLP and Neutralization Underflow (NUF) are significant by-products produced during REO production at LAMP, serving the nuclear energy industry19.

This study was conducted to collect data from laboratory testing at the Nuclear Energy Radiological Assessment (NERA), Faculty of Science and Technology (FST), Universiti Kebangsaan Malaysia (UKM), Malaysia2,3. In this regard, the electrosorption technique involves processing an aqueous solution within an electrosorption apparatus, thereafter undergoing annealing via a hot plate and hydrothermal treatment to produce thorium-containing compounds. The entire electrosorption phase is characterized by considerable energy consumption. Following the extraction, the amount of adsorbed thorium ion and its oxide, along with the concentration of thorium in the produced aqueous solution, are quantified using diverse analytical instruments. The techniques encompass X-ray Diffraction (XRD), Inductively Coupled Plasma Mass Spectrometry (ICP-MS), etc., each exhibiting distinct energy consumption during operation.

The data related to chemical constituents, equipment usage, water consumption, and electrical inputs directly linked to the electrosorption process was collected from previous research information. This data is essential for building the LCA database and measuring the environmental impacts. Further, publicly available data were widely employed for enhancing missing source data and to characterize background activity. Key secondary sources comprised the Ecoinvent database 3, statistics from the Malaysian Electricity Office36. The Schematic illustrations of thorium extraction process, encompassing the industrial chain from ore mining, transportation, rare earth facility (LAMP), and depicting location maps of NERA, FST, UKM, and Peninsular Malaysia (modified from Akhtar et al.37, were created using ARCGIS 10.3 and CorelDraw Graphics Suite X7 64-bit software, incorporating base imagery from Google Maps (maps.google.com), as well as a bottom section detailing electrosorption technique, showing setup for aqueous solution processing, annealing, hydrothermal treatment for compound synthesis, and subsequent instrument analysis (e.g., XRD, SEM-EDX,) for LCA database collection associated with energy consumption, as shown in Fig. 1.

Environmental impact quantified by life cycle assessment (LCA)

This study applied LCA to estimate the environmental implications associated with thorium extraction from radioactive waste in order to obtain the detailed understanding of a product’s emissions life cycle by assessing environmental impacts at every stage38,39. The LCA provides a standardized, organized method to evaluate the entire life cycle of resource extraction processes, accounting for inputs (such as raw materials, energy, water, and chemicals) and outputs (including emissions and waste) within a specified system boundary. The LCA methodology has established by the International Organization for Standardization (ISO)40,41.

Goal and scope

The initial stage of the LCA entails elucidating the goal and scope of the study, as described by defining the functional unit and establishing the system boundary. The main goal of this research was to evaluate the environmental impacts associated with thorium extraction, specifically through electrosorption processing from radioactive waste, and its subsequent management. The functional unit for this investigation has been defined as 1 kg (kg) of thorium produced from the electrosorption method, representing standardized quantity for comparative analysis. The system boundary started with the electrosorption extraction mechanism, encompassing the establishment of residue storage facilities for water-leached purification (WLP) at the nuclear facility’s backend, while neglecting the waste disposal phase. This research encompasses the analysis of direct and indirect inputs related to chemicals, energy infrastructure, water usage, equipment, and thorium production via the electrosorption extraction method based on CBE and GBE. The cradle-to-gate system boundary included raw materials, energy infrastructure, water use, and chemical substances (as depicted in Fig. 2).

Inventory analysis

Following to the defining of the goal and scope, the inventory analysis compiled input and output data for each process cycle. The accuracy and validity of LCA conclusions are largely contingent upon the quality of the supporting inventory42. The data collection carried out with ISO 14,044 standards, referred to as unit processes, waste and resource emissions43. The LCI offers foundational knowledge for improvement, evaluating the importance of contributions from a particular activity or phase in the life cycle and identifying areas with insufficient data11. In some cases, the discrepancy may eventually affect the inventory data collection, impact assessment results and cause uncertainty44. Primary inventory data for the thorium extraction process, encompassing amounts of GBE and ACE as well as energy consumption during laboratory testing, were aggregated from local facilities. Secondary data from the Ecoinvent 3 dataset has been combined with contextual information regarding chemical substances and energy use.

Impact assessment

LCIA were employed to transform hazardous chemicals, pollutants, and resource extractions into midpoint and endpoint indicators This study encompassed 18 midpoint impact categories, including global warming (GW), stratospheric ozone depletion (SOD), ionizing radiation (IR), ozone formation human health (OFHH), fine particulate matter formation (FPMF), ozone formation terrestrial ecosystems (OFTE), terrestrial acidification (TA), freshwater eutrophication (FE), marine eutrophication (ME), terrestrial ecotoxicity (TET), freshwater ecotoxicity (FET), marine ecotoxicity (MET), human carcinogenic toxicity (HCT), human non-carcinogenic toxicity (HNCT), land use (LU), mineral resource scarcity (MRS), fossil resource scarcity (FRS), and water consumption (WC).

Endpoint evaluation assessed findings across three distinct categories: human health (HH), ecological diversity (ED), and resource availability (RA). This phase assesses the impact of the system/process/activity on humans, ecosystems, and resources. Emissions released into several environments, such as air, water, and soil that can be inert, poisonous, or radioactive[48]. The 18 midpoint and 3 endpoint categories were analyzed using the ReCiPe 2016 Midpoint and Endpoint (H) V1.06 methodology in SimaPro 9.6.0 software.

Interpretation

The life cycle interpretation step encompassed the validation and assessment of the LCIA data to reduce uncertainty47. The interpretation process provides findings as essential information for the community, customers, and policymakers to help decision-making48. Results from the inventory analysis and impact assessment were integrated with the established goals and scope to develop conclusions and suggestions. Sensitivity analysis was conducted utilizing multiple electricity sources, with solar-driven energy serving as the baseline49. Uncertainty analysis, assessing several inventory data sources, utilized the Monte Carlo method to measure uncertainty. Hilali et al.50 noted that examining input and output selections in the LCA approach also heightened uncertainty. SimaPro software was employed to evaluate data quality and the impact of unclear data during the inventory process.

Results and discussions

This section assesses and analyzes the findings from the inventory evaluation and impact evaluation step comparing the thorium extraction phases and quantifying the impacts associated with raw material and energy consumption phases, as well as the harm to ecosystems, human health, and resources.

LCI analysis for electrosorption process using ACE and GBE systems

The LCA can facilitate a systematic assessment of environmental impacts, quantify emissions to air, water, and soil across each product or process cycle, evaluate the carbon footprint of compounds, and compare the health and ecological repercussions of a particular good or procedure to determine the most effective electrosorption strategy regarding ACE and GBE.

Contribution factors of electrosorption process to extract thorium (1 kg) at midpoint level

The present study examined the environmental impacts of thorium extraction processing, emphasizing the energy consumed by equipment and quantifying the chemical compounds that compose the products and materials involved in the electrosorption process at rare earth facility. During the ACE process, major factors were recognized as major environmental impact contributors of polyvinylidene fluoride (28%), followed by electricity infrastructure (27%), activated carbon powder (22.6%), N-dimethylacetamide (19.2%), and sulphuric acid (2.66%), as well as sodium hydroxide (0.117%), and deionized water (0.00029%), highlighting less potential environmental impact at the midpoint level (Fig. 3A). At the GBE system, electricity accounted for approximately 78% of the significant influence on the electrosorption process, primarily due to the hard coal electricity. The electricity consumption data indicates that the national grid mix depends mainly on hard coal for electricity generation. The significant reliance on fossil fuels is a crucial determinant affecting the environmental impact of energy-intensive processes in this research, particularly for elements derived from the Malaysian electricity grid mix.

The significantly elevated electricity contribution in GBE (78% compared to 27% in ACE) is directly associated with the synthesis of graphene-based materials and the energy requirements of the hydrothermal process utilized in GBE production, as compared with the more conventional and less energy-intensive production methods for activated carbon. The GBE process necessitates 30 kWh of electricity per kilogram of thorium produced (Table 1), whereas the ACE process requires 25 kWh (Table 2), indicating that the overall energy intensity for GBE, which includes material preparation and the electrosorption setup, was greater. Further materials showed the impacts, such as urea (10.5%), methanol (9.07%), sulfuric acid (2.13%), bis(2-ethylhexyl) phosphate (0.531%), graphitic carbon nitride (0.0897%), and deionized water (0.015%), as illustrated in Fig. 3B.

Contribution factors of electrosorption process to extract thorium (1 kg) at endpoint level

Major environmental impact categories were recognized at the endpoint level, and the main contributor was the electricity infrastructure at ACE processing, which accounts for 28% of the total. Following closely behind is activated carbon powder around 26%, polyvinylidene fluoride (18.1%), and sulphuric acid (13.5%), as well as N-dimethylacetamide (13.5%), sodium hydroxide (0.13%), and deionized water (0.00077%), highlighting the less potential environmental impact of the ACE process (Fig. 4A). In addition, the hydrothermal process using GBE system, revealing of each direct process emission from thorium extraction pathways such as electricity (78.5%), sulfuric acid (9.99%), urea (6.15%), and methanol (4.77%) were responsible for major environmental impact contributors, as well as bis(2-ethylhexyl) phosphate (0.544%), graphitic carbon nitride (0.0716%), and deionized water (0.015%) were showed less impact by the electrosorption process (Fig. 4B).

The key contributors from ACE and GBE at both the midpoint and endpoint levels can be related to the substantial materials and energy inputs necessary for the synthesis of chemical compounds such as polyvinylidene fluoride, activated carbon powder, N-dimethylacetamide, sulfuric acid, urea, and methanol, which result in major environmental emissions during thorium extraction processes. In this scenario, midpoint evaluation of electrosorption processing reveals that GBE has a greater impact on global warming than ACE due to the substantial energy demand in synthesizing graphene-based materials, subsequently leading to elevated carbon emissions. One study performed by Cossutta et al.51, a comparative analysis of LCA related to GBE and ACE for supercapacitor applications, indicating that GBE typically exhibits greater environmental footprints than ACE in terms of global warming, toxicity, and ozone depletion, attributable to its requiring energy operations. Uncertainty analysis

Uncertainty analysis reveals fluctuations in essential input parameters values that considerably affect the impact assessment results and pinpoints which factors notably change environmental effects. Decision-makers can enhance their understanding of the possible dangers and possibilities linked to different actions by evaluating data sources, enabling individuals to formulate robust decisions against different outcomes, including variables such as electricity consumption and chemical compounds56. Numerous studies concerning thorium-uranium extraction, REE production, REO processing, and other radioactive waste investigations neglected to include uncertainty analysis for endpoint indicators12,23,31,34,53,57. In the context, the findings provide an uncertainty analysis derived from input data of unit processes, indicating the probability distributions of the midpoint classifications based on ACE and GBE.

All uncertainties for background data were derived using the LCA program SimaPro 9. Monte Carlo simulations utilizing estimated uncertainty were performed to calculate the uncertainties of the assigned results. The model presumes that inventory data conforms to a log-normal distribution or parameters of the log-normal probability density functions (PDF) for each input data have been determined in this study. The uncertainty range for direct inputs was assessed using the pedigree matrix model defined for the Ecoinvent 3 database, incorporating mean, coefficient of variation (CV), median, and standard deviation (SD), as detailed in (Table 4). The CV is primarily used for presentation since it measures the comparative variability of the dataset and evaluates the consistency and stability of the effect categories across various units. The simulation results demonstrate that variance has been observed for all environmental indicators associated with CV values ranging from 19 to 30 for ACE and CV values between 15 and 32 for GBE.

Challenges, limitations, and future work

The LCA performed the environmental sustainability of two thorium extraction processes, concentrating on electrosorption techniques while excluding transportation, cracking, and leaching due to data limitations. This study follows the innovative instruction of Cossutta et al.51 for evaluating emerging technologies, promoting scenario planning to account for technological advancements and process evolution to assist with decisions over the long term. A background structure is impacted by decisions implemented in the foreground system yet remains beyond direct oversight. Consequently, the findings serve as comparative examinations in particular processes to ACE and GBE. A significant research challenge is the insufficient number of publications addressing the environmental impacts of electrosorption process utilizing ACE and GBE, particularly under hard coal energy at rare earth facilities. This research gap complicates environmental impact evaluation and comparisons for these processes.

The limitations of this research resulted from the lack of reliable and up-to-date inventory data, which subsequently affected the accuracy of the LCA. Challenges emerged from the absence of sufficient input data points in existing databases for several critical aspects of the process. This encompasses, but is not restricted to, detailed cradle-to-gate lifecycle inventories for specific new electrode components (e.g., particular graphene precursor synthesis steps), complicated energy consumption profiles for specific sub-processes within the electrosorption apparatus (beyond aggregated indicators), and highly localized environmental data pertinent to the manufacturing of specific chemical compounds in the Malaysian context. Consequently, for distinct processes involving particular products and geographic restrictions, global or proxy data from the Ecoinvent database needed to be employed, potentially introducing variability in the calculation results for Malaysia. Moreover, variations were noted in the electricity consumption data for the LCA inventory, caused by inconsistencies between published literature and laboratory experiments, especially regarding optimal extraction ratios and specific equipment performance metrics. Moreover, social and economic factors, crucial for a comprehensive sustainability assessment, were not analyzed in this article due to the lack of relevant information.

In evaluating the future of waste disposal and recycling through ACE and GBE systems, it is essential to focus on the main perspectives due to the crucial necessity to improve the recovery of radioactive waste51. This can be accomplished by refining current techniques or innovating novel strategies to balance raw material extraction with thorium recovery. Moreover, subsequent studies should concentrate on the environmental efficacy of electrosorption methods in conjunction with other processes such as leaching, cracking, and other solvent extraction techniques. Furthermore, energy efficiency is crucial for the viability of these technologies in supplying energy and for the environmental consequences linked to energy efficiency54. Despite numerous structures exhibiting substantial energy consumption, future studies should focus on methods to mitigate the heat and energy associated with this process. Furthermore, the electrosorption technology examined in this study requires additional improvement at the process level while concurrently minimizing or mitigating environmental consequences during its application in process manufacturing. Consequently, additional investigations must address the identified problems and constraints to close the knowledge gap in this domain.

Conclusion

The previous assessment lacked a thorough analysis of the LCA of the electrosorption method for thorium extraction, and the findings of this study have significantly enhanced our comprehension of the many environmental impacts linked to the rare earth ions facility. These findings allow the REE sector to formulate innovative and sustainable practices to address the increasing demand for rare earth production, and ultimately, a more comprehensive environmental impact assessment of Th extraction via electrosorption will be established. Given that most Th and REE production facilities are presently located in LAMP facilities, life cycle inventories employed in this study for the electrosorption process were derived from published NERA team articles with other published literature. This research explored two process ways using ACE and GBE systems for thorium extraction from radioactive waste at rare earth plants.
