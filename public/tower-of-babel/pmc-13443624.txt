Exploring the association between physical activity and life satisfaction among college students
Authors: Liwei Zhang, Ruitao Li, Jiabin Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPhysical activity contributes to improving the mental health and well-being of college students, but the mechanisms through which it influences life satisfaction remain unclear. Guided by the Stress-Coping Model, this study examines the independent and chain mediating roles of psychological capital and perceived stress in this relationship. Data were collected from 921 college students using standardized scales measuring physical activity, psychological capital, perceived stress, and life satisfaction. Subsequently, we proposed a chain mediation model and validated it. The findings indicate that physical activity positively predicts life satisfaction. Psychological capital and perceived stress function as independent mediators and also form a chain mediating pathway in this relationship. This study uncovers the psychological pathways linking physical activity and life satisfaction, suggesting that universities may enhance students’ life satisfaction through physical activity and psychological interventions.Keywords: Physical activity, Life satisfaction, Psychological capital, Perceived stress, College studentsSubject terms: Health care, Psychology, Psychology

Introduction

Life satisfaction (LS), proposed by American psychologist Diener et al.1, is considered a core component of subjective well-being and reflects an individual’s cognitive judgment of overall life conditions. For college students, LS is closely related to emotional health, learning motivation, interpersonal functioning, and adjustment, and thus serves as an important indicator of their developmental status. Existing studies suggest that psychological capital (PsyCap)2,3, physical activity (PA)4, and various aspects of perceived stress (PS)5 are each associated with LS. In addition, when understanding psychological and behavioral outcomes, it is also necessary to pay attention to the links among multi-stage emotional, cognitive, and resource-related processes6,7. This perspective suggests that overall life evaluations may be embedded in broader psychological processes, rather than only reflected in independent associations among variables. PA, PsyCap, and PS represent three important psychological dimensions: behavioral engagement, internal psychological resources, and stress experience. PA involves bodily participation and emotional regulation; PsyCap reflects internal resources that help sustain positive functioning; PS represents students’ subjective appraisal of environmental demands relative to their coping resources. Since LS is a global evaluative judgment, examining these dimensions together may help present more clearly the relationship between college students’ daily adjustment and life evaluations. In daily student contexts, PsyCap may co-occur with lower PS, and variations in PS are related to students’ evaluations of life.

Existing research has rarely examined PA, PsyCap, and PS within an integrated psychological framework. Moreover, few studies have considered these variables as interconnected psychological processes that unfold within the same model. Prior work also has not clarified whether PA is related to both increases in positive psychological resources and decreases in stress experiences. Although prior evidence links PA with LS, how PA aligns with sequential shifts in PsyCap and PS in relation to LS remains insufficiently clarified. Based on this gap, the present study proposes a chain mediation model that includes PsyCap and PS to examine how PA is associated with LS through sequential variations in internal resources and stress experiences. This integrative framework aims to provide a more systematic and coherent explanation of the psychological processes related to LS among college students.

Theories and hypotheses

Theoretical foundation

The Stress-Coping Model, proposed by Lazarus8, suggests that when individuals encounter stressors from the external environment, they engage in a cognitive appraisal process to evaluate both the potential threat of the event and their available resources to cope with it. This model has been widely applied in fields such as developmental psychology and health psychology9,10. And it is frequently used to analyze individuals’ emotional responses, coping strategies, and psychological adaptation outcomes in stressful contexts. Specifically, when internal resources are at a higher level, individuals tend to show more positive appraisals and coping patterns, which are associated with more favorable evaluations of life11.

PA is often accompanied by feelings of engagement, control, and positive emotion, and these experiences may function as external inputs that align with the positive functions reflected in PsyCap12. PsyCap is closely related to coping patterns and stress appraisal13, while PS reflects students’ subjective sense of burden when facing external demands14. LS can be viewed as the overall evaluative outcome of these psychological processes. This structure is consistent with the “resource–appraisal–outcome” pathway described in the Stress–Coping Model. However, although the model highlights a continuous psychological process linking resource states and stress experience, existing studies show limitations in the integration and ordering of these variables. Prior findings have not reached agreement on the directional relation between internal resources such as PsyCap and PS15,16. PA, PsyCap, PS, and LS have also rarely been examined within a single theoretical framework, leaving the continuity implied by the Stress–Coping Model insufficiently supported in university student samples. In addition, many studies use single mediators or parallel mediators, making it difficult to describe a potential sequence in which resource accumulation corresponds with changes in stress experience and is then associated with LS. Based on these gaps, the present study employs the Stress–Coping Model as the theoretical foundation and proposes that PA may relate to LS through successive associations with PsyCap and PS. A chain mediation structure is adopted to examine this process in a more coherent manner and to provide a more complete explanation of psychological adjustment among university students under stress-related conditions.

Hypotheses

Building on the Stress-Coping Model, the present study focuses on the theoretical implications this model offers for specific variable relationships rather than repeating conceptual descriptions. The model highlights the joint roles of resource states and stress appraisal in coping processes. This framework provides the logical foundation for the hypotheses in this study, allowing each hypothesis to be derived from theoretical reasoning rather than solely from empirical findings.

Physical activity (PA) and life satisfaction (LS)

PA is an important pathway to higher LS. Its benefits are not limited to improved physical health but also include the accumulation of psychological resources and the strengthening of social functioning. Empirical evidence indicates that higher PA levels are strongly linked to greater LS17–19. At the psychological level, Deng et al.17 and Cao et al.20 reported that exercise improves self-efficacy, resilience, and sleep quality, which directly enhance life evaluations. At the social level, Sui et al.18 found that PA facilitates peer interaction and teamwork, broadens social networks, and increases support resources, thereby improving LS. At the academic and adjustment level, Zhang et al.21 and Jeong-su19 demonstrated that moderate exercise relieves academic stress, enhances attention and cognitive functioning, and promotes learning efficiency and campus adjustment. However, existing evidence also indicates variability in these effects. Lee & Tashman22 and Wypych-Slusarska et al.23 noted that PA may sometimes lead to physical fatigue or heightened psychological stress, which can weaken its positive effects and result in a nonsignificant association with LS. Although existing studies vary to some extent, overall evidence shows that PA is a relatively stable source of positive experiences in students’ daily lives and shapes their subjective perceptions of life situations. Based on this reasoning, it is plausible that PA is positively associated with LS, which provides the theoretical basis for the first hypothesis.

Psychological capital (PsyCap) as a mediator

PsyCap refers to a positive psychological state and reserve of mental resources that enable individuals to cope with challenges and pursue goals in work, study, and life. It consists of self-efficacy, hope, optimism, and resilience24. PsyCap not only strengthens individuals’ coping and adaptation but is also regarded as a critical psychological basis for maintaining and enhancing LS25–27. On one hand, Huang et al.26 found that high levels of self-efficacy, optimism, and resilience are significantly associated with more positive overall evaluations of life. On the other hand, the accumulation of PsyCap enhances college students’ perceived support from family, friends, and teachers, which in turn shows a significant positive relationship with LS27. Research also indicates that PA contributes to the accumulation of PsyCap28–30. For example, Kim28 reported that PA in natural environments enhances self-control, prosocial behaviors, and environmental behavioral capacity, which improves psychological well-being. Moreover, although few studies have directly shown that PA may increase LS through improvements in PsyCap, existing findings already indicate that exercise-related experiences are closely linked to key dimensions of PsyCap, and these dimensions also show strong associations with LS17,29. In university settings, students’ evaluations of LS often rely more on stable internal resources rather than short-term emotional shifts. Based on this reasoning, PsyCap may act as a psychological pathway linking PA with LS. Positive experiences that occur during PA are more likely to correspond to resource accumulation, and such resource states tend to show more stable links with LS.

Perceived stress (PS) as a mediator

PS refers to an individual’s feelings or thoughts about the amount of stress they experience at a given point in time or over a specific period31. Among college students, PS is often associated with factors such as academic demands, social adjustment, and employment pressure. Previous studies have shown a significant negative relationship between PS and LS2,32,33. For example, Dong et al.32 revealed that PS among college students not only directly reduces LS but also lowers it indirectly by increasing the risk of smartphone addiction and weakening exercise commitment. Similarly, Hatun & Kurtça2 found that PS reduces students’ sense of hope, which further undermines LS. The association between PA and PS has also been supported in recent studies34–36. For instance, Ahmed et al.34 and Zhai et al.36 demonstrated that PA is often accompanied by shifts in attention, physical relaxation, and emotional recovery, and these experiences tend to appear alongside lower levels of PS. Although PA does not change academic or career demands directly, it can provide brief psychological relief in daily settings, which corresponds to reduced stress experiences. Based on this pattern, PS may function as a psychological link between PA and LS. Exercise-related experiences are associated with lower PS, and PS levels are closely related to LS. As a result, PS can serve as a mediator that reflects the role of PA in students’ everyday stress management and offers a context-sensitive explanation for the association between PA and LS.

Psychological capital (PsyCap) and Perceived stress (PS) as sequential mediators

Existing research shows that PA effectively enhances the positive psychological state and coping ability of college students, and it is significantly positively associated with PsyCap25–27. PS intensifies psychological tension and anxiety in academic, social, and employment domains, and it is significantly negatively associated with LS2,32,33. In addition, Ramírez-Pérez37 and Wang et al.38 further indicated that high levels of PsyCap strengthen students’ ability to cope with academic, social, and employment pressures. And also influences cognitive appraisals of stressful situations, reduces negative emotions, and lowers subjective stress. This evidence suggests that PsyCap may be significantly negatively related to PS39,40. In addition, Yan et al.41 and Yan et al.42 showed that the association between behavioral factors and related outcomes may involve specific psychological conditions, and may also include sequential associations among psychological constructs such as psychological resources and emotional distress. Building on this reasoning, the positive experiences associated with PA may relate first to increases in PsyCap, followed by changes in PS, and ultimately by students’ evaluations of LS. This pattern suggests that PsyCap and PS may form a continuous psychological pathway rather than two independent mediators. Using a chain mediation model makes it possible to examine the sequential links between resource accumulation and stress experiences in PA contexts and provides a more structured perspective for understanding how PA is associated with LS.

The present study

Building on previous research, the present study applies the Stress-Coping Model to examine the mechanisms linking physical activity (PA) and life satisfaction (LS) among college students. Psychological capital (PsyCap) and perceived stress (PS) are included as mediators, and their independent as well as sequential mediating roles are analyzed within this relationship. Following this approach, the study constructs the research model shown in Fig. 1 and tests the following hypotheses:

Hypothetical Model.

H1: PA is positively associated with LS among college students.

H2: PsyCap mediates the relationship between PA and LS.

H3: PS mediates the relationship between PA and LS.

H4: PsyCap and PS sequentially mediate the relationship between PA and LS.

Methods

Sample and data collection

Data were collected anonymously from May 6 to July 9, 2025, using the online platform Wenjuanxing (www.sojump.com). According to the formula proposed by Kline43(number of items × 10 + number of items × 10 × 20%), the required sample size was calculated as 624 participants. We randomly selected participants from multiple universities in China. First, the cooperating universities provided the research team with complete student rosters, from which we randomly selected eligible students as the study participants. Next, the course instructors briefly introduced the survey in class to give students a basic understanding of the questionnaire. After that, we sent the Wenjuanxing survey link or QR code to these randomly selected students through on-campus communication channels and directed WeChat messages. Completing the questionnaire was entirely voluntary, and participation or non-participation involved no rewards, penalties, or any actual consequences for the students. All participants provided informed consent, and confidentiality and data security were guaranteed. A total of 931 questionnaires were collected, and after excluding invalid or duplicate responses, 921 valid questionnaires remained. The descriptive statistics are presented in Table 1. Place of origin did not significantly affect LS among college students, while gender, grade level, and age showed significant effects. Further analysis showed that the mean LS score for males was 21.230, whereas the mean score for females was 18.820, indicating that males reported higher overall LS than females. In addition, students in their fourth year of study and those older than 21 reported higher levels of LS (P < 0.05).

Measurement

We measured PA with the Physical Activity Rating Scale (PARS-3), revised by Liang44. The scale contains three items and uses a 5-point Likert format, with higher scores indicating higher levels of PA. In China, the questionnaire has undergone validation and provides evidence of solid reliability and validity3. In the present study, the original wording and structure of the items were retained, and the Cronbach’s α was 0.783.

We measured PsyCap with the Positive Psychological Capital Questionnaire, which was developed by Zhang & Dong45 for Chinese college students. The scale includes 26 items across four dimensions: self-efficacy (7 items), hope (6 items), optimism (6 items), and resilience (7 items). Responses were rated on a 7-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree, 7 = strongly agree). Five items were reverse scored, and higher scores indicated higher levels of PsyCap. In China, the questionnaire has undergone validation and provides evidence of solid reliability and validity40. In the present study, the original wording and structure of the items were retained. The Cronbach’s α coefficients for the four dimensions were 0.887, 0.896, 0.877, and 0.897, respectively.

We measured PS with the Chinese version of the Perceived Stress Scale, originally developed by Sheldon Cohen46 and later translated and revised by Yang47. The scale contains 14 items across two dimensions: tension (7 items) and loss of control (7 items). Responses were rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree, 5 = strongly agree). Seven items were reverse scored, and higher scores indicated greater PS. In China, the questionnaire has undergone validation and provides evidence of solid reliability and validity48. In the present study, the original wording and structure of the items were retained. The Cronbach’s α coefficients for the two dimensions were 0.894 and 0.889, respectively.

We measured LS with the Satisfaction with Life Scale developed by Diener et al.1. The scale consists of five items rated on a 7-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree, 7 = strongly agree). Higher scores indicated greater LS. In China, the questionnaire has undergone validation and provides evidence of solid reliability and validity49. In the present study, the original wording and structure of the items were retained, the Cronbach’s α was 0.954.

Data analysis

The study used AMOS 29.0 to conduct the common method bias test and confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) to evaluate the model fit. Smart PLS 4 was employed to assess reliability and validity and to perform the structural model analysis.

Confirmatory factor analysis

The results of the confirmatory factor analysis indicated that the overall measurement model demonstrated a good fit. Specifically, χ²/df = 1.822 (less than 2), RMSEA = 0.030 (below 0.08), and the values of GFI = 0.924, NFI = 0.944, CFI = 0.974, IFI = 0.974, and TLI = 0.973 all exceeded 0.900, meeting the model fit criteria suggested by Hu & Bentler53.

Model testing

The latent method factor approach was applied to examine common method bias. This was done by comparing the baseline model with a method-factor model. The chi-square difference test showed that the difference between the two models was not significant (Δχ² = 3.812, df = 1, p > 0.05), indicating that adding the method factor did not substantially improve model fit. Therefore, common method bias was not a serious concern in this study54. At the same time, we used linear regression to test multicollinearity. The results showed that the maximum variance inflation factor (VIF) was 2.601, which was lower than 3 (Table 6). This indicated that multicollinearity did not exist in the study55.

Mediation effect analysis

Based on 5000 bootstrap resamples, the indirect effects were examined, and the results are presented in Table 9. PA showed a significant indirect association with LS through PsyCap (β = 0.434, P < 0.001, 95% CI [0.387, 0.482]), accounting for 60.6% of the total effect, indicating that PsyCap represents a major indirect pathway linking PA and LS. The indirect association from PA to LS through PS was also significant (β = 0.043, P < 0.001, 95% CI [0.025, 0.064]), accounting for 6.0% of the total effect, suggesting that the PA–LS association is partly related to variations in PS. In addition, the chain mediation pathway was significant as well (β = 0.047, P < 0.001, 95% CI [0.030, 0.069]), accounting for 6.6% of the total effect. This pattern indicates that the association between PA and LS is linked not only to the individual indirect paths involving PsyCap or PS but also to the sequential process in which higher PsyCap is accompanied by lower PS.

Discussion

This study found that PA was significantly and positively related to LS among college students, and this finding supported H1. This conclusion aligns with earlier empirical studies, which indicated that PA is associated with better physical health and higher LS among college students17–19. In the context of heavy academic demands and growing uncertainty about the future, PA offers students an activity that is structured, controllable, and feedback-oriented, which is often associated with emotional recovery and psychological ease21. Social interaction and teamwork during PA are linked to a stronger sense of social connection, which has been identified as an important factor related to more positive life evaluations18. PA also appears to provide experiences associated with competence and self-worth, and these experiences tend to co-occur with more stable emotions and more positive overall evaluations of daily life when students face academic or personal challenges21. From a theoretical perspective, PA is not only a behavioral factor related to LS, but may also be a behavioral resource in college students’ daily psychological adjustment. This understanding is consistent with evidence that PA has behavioral buffering significance between stressors and psychological outcomes56. This also highlights the value of understanding PA from a behavioral mechanism perspective. Overall, PA is closely related to more positive psychological states among college students.

The results of this study observed that PsyCap functioned as a mediator linking PA and LS among college students, and this finding supported H2. This conclusion was consistent with existing empirical research, which indicated that PA was indirectly associated with LS among college students through PsyCap17,29. During PA, college students often encounter challenges, engage in goal-directed activity, and receive continuous feedback. These experiences are commonly associated with positive self-perceptions, emotional states, and psychological strength. Thus, the association between PA and LS may appear together with the accumulation of these psychological resources17,26,29. In this study, the mediating effect of PsyCap appeared stronger, which may relate to the resource accumulating features of experiences associated with PA. Positive psychological states formed during PA are more likely to remain as stable internal resources, and college students often link their evaluations of LS to components such as self-efficacy, hope, optimism, and resilience rather than to short term emotional changes. Because these components show close associations with both PA experiences and LS, PsyCap presents a more prominent statistical contribution in the model. In addition, PsyCap is a multidimensional psychological resource, and its components usually occur together in daily situations, which may explain its more concentrated role in the overall pathway. This explanation is also consistent with recent research, which suggests that psychological resources are usually linked to emotional pathways and may be important antecedent resources for understanding stress experiences and well-being-related outcomes6,7. Overall, the findings highlight the importance of internal psychological resources in students’ evaluations of daily life.

The results of this study observed that PS functioned as a mediator linking PA and LS among college students, and this finding supported H3. This conclusion was consistent with previous empirical studies suggesting that PS may serve as an indirect pathway linking PA and LS among college students33,35,57. During periods of exam preparation or academic tasks, PA often offers students brief moments of attention shift and emotional relief, which is associated with reduced psychological strain from intensive study demands58. When PS is relatively low, students tend to form more positive evaluations of their academic life, daily routines, and social interactions. For this reason, PS shows a mediating role between PA and LS in previous findings2,32. In this study, the mediating effect of PS appeared smaller. This pattern may relate to the situational nature of PS among college students. PS is influenced by multiple factors, including academic load, time management, social interaction, and concerns about the future59. The relief associated with PA may be modest when compared with these broader sources of stress, which makes the link between PS and LS relatively weaker in the overall evaluation of daily life. In addition, students’ judgments of LS tend to align more closely with stable internal resources such as PsyCap, which may result in a milder contribution of PS within the indirect pathways.

The results of this study observed that PsyCap and PS sequentially functioned as a mediator linking PA and LS among college students, and this finding supported H4. Specifically, PA was associated with higher levels of positive psychological resources, such as confidence, hope, and resilience, which were further related to lower subjective perceptions of stress in academic and daily life and, in turn, to higher overall LS. College students often gain positive emotional experiences, a sense of bodily control, and a sense of goal attainment during participation in PA. These positive experiences help them maintain a relatively stable state of positive resources and are further associated with higher levels of PsyCap25. From the perspective of the Stress-Coping Model, when individuals face stressful events, they usually first conduct a cognitive appraisal and then form corresponding stress experiences. PsyCap may make college students more likely to understand these demands as manageable tasks and focus their attention on available resources and feasible solutions when facing academic, interpersonal, or future development pressures, thereby reducing their threat perception of stressful events and their subjective sense of PS40. At lower levels of PS, individuals’ evaluations of life are more likely to show a positive trend2. Therefore, PsyCap and PS form a continuous pathway of action. This result is consistent with the theoretical logic among resources, appraisal, and outcomes in the Stress-Coping Model, and it also provides a more detailed psychological mechanism perspective for understanding the relationship between PA and LS. Notably, the effect proportion of the chain mediation path in this study was 6.6%, indicating that this pathway should be understood as one possible mechanism rather than a complete explanation. This may be because college students’ psychological processes and stress experiences may involve multiple interconnected pathways56. In addition to academic tasks, they also involve employment pressure, interpersonal relationships, family expectations, and uncertainty about future development. In addition, as an overall evaluation of life, LS is also jointly related to factors such as social support, personality traits, and living environment. The significant effect of gender on LS in this study also illustrates this to a certain extent. Therefore, the chain path formed by PsyCap and PS is not the only mechanism linking PA and LS, but it provides a useful supplement for understanding the relationship between the two.

Implications and limitations

Implications

Within the framework of the Stress-Coping Model, this study revealed the mechanism by which PA influences LS through PsyCap and PS, providing new evidence for extending the model. The traditional Stress-Coping Model emphasizes the use of resources in the cognitive appraisal process. By incorporating PA, this study showed that it is not only an external resource but also promotes the development of internal resources such as PsyCap, thereby playing an indirect role in reducing PS and enhancing well-being. It is noteworthy that PsyCap occupied the most prominent position in the mediation pathway, which further confirmed the key function of positive psychological resources on the psychological adaptation of college students. These findings not only deepened the understanding of the relationship between PA and mental health but also provided a more contextualized explanation for the application of psychological theories to educational populations.

The findings of this study offer valuable insights for promoting mental health and educational practice among university students. First, universities should integrate PA into campus mental health intervention systems and establish a “PA and psychology” integration mechanism. For example, they can design course modules such as a “resilience fitness training camp” or an “emotion regulation exercise workshop,” incorporating psychological education elements like emotion recognition and stress management into physical education courses. Second, given the important role of PsyCap, universities should collaborate with the physical education department and counseling center to organize thematic activities such as a " PsyCap empowerment camp” or a “positive psychology and sports day.” By combining group exercise, mindfulness meditation, and psychological lectures, these programs can enhance students’ self-efficacy, optimism, and sense of hope. In addition, universities are advised to regularly organize fun sports events, outdoor hiking, and non-competitive community sports, while encouraging students to consistently engage in experiential and process-oriented activities such as aerobics, hiking, and jogging. Such initiatives can help avoid excessive competition driven by performance outcomes and foster a positive and inclusive exercise atmosphere. Finally, when universities carry out work related to sports and mental health promotion, they should pay attention to differences among students of different genders and improve the targeting of intervention measures. Supportive PA and psychological counseling services for female college students can be strengthened to help them accumulate positive psychological resources, regulate stress experiences, and improve LS. For male college students, regular participation in PA can be used to further consolidate their positive psychological state. Through these diverse measures, universities can strengthen students’ PsyCap, effectively relieve stress, and ultimately improve their overall LS.

Limitations and future research directions

Although this study verified the mechanisms among PA, PsyCap, PS, and LS, several limitations remain. First, the study adopted a cross-sectional design, which cannot fully reveal causal relationships. Future research may adopt longitudinal tracking or experimental intervention designs and combine chain mediation models with moderated mediation models to further examine the psychological processes between PA and LS and their boundary conditions. Second, the data were collected through self-report questionnaires. Although anonymity and statistical controls were applied to minimize potential bias, the results may still have been influenced by participants’ subjective perceptions and social desirability effects. Future studies may incorporate multi-source data and objective indicators, including the use of wearable technologies to monitor exercise duration and intensity, and physiological measures to assess stress levels, in order to reduce bias. Finally, this study did not further distinguish differences in exercise behaviors among participants, such as whether they maintained regular exercise habits, the frequency of activity, or the duration of exercise, which may influence the actual effects of PA. Future research could include exercise behavior characteristics in the model. For example, comparing individuals with and without regular exercise habits in terms of the pathways of PsyCap enhancement and stress reduction may further clarify the conditions and boundaries of the psychological mechanisms influenced by PA.

Conclusion

Drawing on the Stress-Coping Model, this study found a significant positive association between PA and LS among college students, with PsyCap and PS serving as both independent and sequential mediators in this relationship. Among these effects, PsyCap was the most prominent, accounting for 60.6% of the total effect. These results enriched the application of the Stress-Coping Model and offered practical implications for universities to use PA as a means to promote mental health and LS among college students.
