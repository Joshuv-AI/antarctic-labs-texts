Support vector regression model for the prediction of buildings’ maximum seismic response based on real monitoring data
Authors: Dongwang Tao, Shizhe Fang, Haixu Liu, Jianqi Lu, Jiang Wang, Qiang Ma
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractMaximum drift ratio (MDR), one of the engineering demand parameters (EDPs), provides fundamental physical value for predicting building damage. Existing machine learning based prediction models mainly rely on numerical simulation data or structural experiments and are not appropriate for prediction of seismic response of real structures. The New Earthquake Data (NDE1.0) is the most comprehensive publicly available dataset of actual structural seismic response observations. Currently the prediction models using NDE1.0 are mainly based on linear or log-linear regression. In this study, based on the NDE1.0 flatfile, we develop a full-feature support vector regression (SVR) based MDR prediction model (SVR-MDR), treating all the available 41 characteristic parameters including structural information as input feature. To improve the model’s efficiency and practical applicability, we also establish a reduced-feature SVR model (RSVR-MDR) by selecting 10 fundamental parameters based on SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) values and the accessibility of features. Our results demonstrate that SVR-MDR model outperform other machine learning models such as kernel ridge regression and decision tree models, and SVR-MDR and RSVR-MDR models outperform conventional loglinear regression and multinomial models, because SVR can map the complex nonlinear function of multiple variables and consider the available information of buildings especially the fundamental frequency. The proposed RSVR-MDR model have promising potential application for post-event seismic damage assessment and post-event emergency response in near real time.Keywords: Structural response, Engineering demand parameters, Maximum drift ratio, Support vector regression, Intensity measureSubject terms: Natural hazards, Civil engineering

Introduction

Earthquake is one of the most destructive natural hazards faced by modern society and human beings. The effects from earthquake include strong ground shaking, surface rupturing and ground failure, among which strong ground shaking is most destructive. The civil engineering structures under strong ground shaking are exerted on strong horizontal inertial force and may be damaged or destroyed. Meanwhile the destruction and collapse of building structures under strong shaking will directly lead to casualties, property damage, and overall functional loss. The direct average economic losses are up to 14.7 billion USD per year resulted from ground-shaking-related damage to building structures in the United States1.

Rapid post-earthquake assessment of buildings’ seismic damage is paramount to post-event emergency response and economic loss assessment2,3. The structural seismic responses and engineering demand parameters (EDPs) such as building’s maximum drift ratio (MDR) which is defined as the maximum relative displacement between the top and bottom floors normalized by building height, peak floor accelerations, maximum inter-story drift ratio, residual story drift are fundamental physical values for estimating building damage. For a big city, there are hundreds of thousands of buildings and most of the buildings have incomplete or missing design information. The key step for post-event damage assessment for urban building clusters is to rapidly predict the buildings’ maximum seismic responses using the ground motions and incomplete building information.

Currently the approaches for estimating structural seismic responses and EDPs are mainly divided into three categories: mechanics-based, purely data-driven and hybrid approaches4. Mechanics-based approach is the most reliable solution, requiring both the mathematical model such as finite-element model (FEM) or shear and flexural beam model or multiple-degree-of-freedom (MDOF) model for modeling building structure and the site-specific ground motion history recorded at the basement of the analyzed building4–7. Miranda and Reyes combined the Timoshenko beam theory and the spectral displacement of the fundamental period of the structure to estimate the amplification factor and the MDR5. Lu et al. proposed SimCenter Workflow for seismic damage simulation and loss prediction of urban buildings, which combined actual building inventories, MDOF models and nonlinear time history analysis6. Ghahari et al. compared Federal Emergency Management Agency (FEMA) P-588, American Society of Civil Engineers (ASCE) 7-169 simplified equations, and response spectrum method to predict peak floor acceleration and found that response spectrum method and gave accurate estimation result7. However the response spectrum method requires knowledge of the seismic loading and building modal properties. To balance computation efficiency and prediction accuracy, Scaini et al. used single-degree-of-freedom oscillator as a first order approximation of building typology and estimated the MDR rapidly for near-real-time damage estimation10. Shan et al. proposed a seismic performance prediction method based the equivalent SDOF hysteresis model of the structure, which is obtained through FEM11. However, it is noted that mechanics-based approach is time-consuming and requires a large amount of computational resources, which hinders the usage for rapid post-earthquake assessment. Purely data-driven approach uses linear regression or machine learning (ML) models to establish relationships between ground motion intensity measure (IM) or time history and the EDPs of building structure. Unlike mechanics-based approaches that do not require pre-existing data for training, ML approaches need amounts of high-quality data to train effectively. Shen et al. used deep neural network to establish relationships between pseudo-acceleration spectra and structural safety, and the training and test accuracy were above 93%12. However, this method needs over tens of thousands of samples to train the deep learning model. The other main problem of purely data-driven approach is the lack of interpretability. The hybrid approach is a combination of mechanics-based and purely data-driven approaches, combining the advantage of interpretability of mechanics-based solution and applicability of the purely data-driven solution4, and the approach has attracted the attention of researchers and has strong application prospect in rapid post-event emergency response. Guan et al. proposed ML based linear elastic MDOF with known yield strength model to predict peak story drift based on building information, modal parameters, spectral intensity parameters and nonlinear pushover analysis parameters, and the results showed that the proposed hybrid model had better performance than the purely data driven model4. Wen et al. used convolutional neural network to predict the MDR and peak floor acceleration by taking the ground motion time history and five easily available building parameters including story height, story number, fortification intensity, longitudinal column distance and transverse span as inputs13. Demertzis et al. conducted extensive comparative evaluation of 15 machine learning algorithms including light gradient boosting machine, gradient boosting regressor, random forest regression to predict 3D R/C buildings’ seismic response and found that light gradient boosting machine was a good choice for EDPs prediction according to generalizability and computation efficiency14. However, the data used in the former hybrid approach research were obtained by numerical simulation of a series of FEM models rather than real structure response under ground shaking. Real structural seismic response data in buildings have made fundamental contributions to the seismic design of buildings and provide basic data for establishing relationships between ground motions and EDPs. Nowadays there are two main structural seismic response databases: the US Centre for Engineering Strong Motion Data (CESMD) and Japanese Building Research Institute (BRI) strong motion network. Perrault and Guéguen used California earthquake records in CESMD to improve EDPs prediction models by using multiple logarithmic linear function and ground motion parameters including peak acceleration, cumulative absolute velocity, velocity response spectrum corresponding to the fundamental period and displacement response spectrum15. Abdelmalek‑Lee and Henry constructed a model combining kriging and XGBoost based on a database of responses of 207 buildings from 35 earthquake events using CESMD earthquake data16. Astorga et al. constructed the New Earthquake Data (NDE1.0) of instrumented buildings, which contained 118 buildings and 8520 recordings from 2737 events17. The NDE1.0 database provides excellent earthquake data for the prediction of structural seismic response and EDPs. We download the original xlsx file provided by Astorga et al. (2020) which included 147 buildings and 12,293 recordings from 3081 events. We use the original xlsx file dataset to establish MDR prediction models.

In this study, a full-feature support vector regression (SVR) model is used to predict MDR of building structures under ground motion based on the earthquake data from NDE1.0 flatfile. In the full-feature SVR-MDR model, 41 characteristic parameters including 6 structure information, 9 spectrum information, 16 duration information, and 10 other earthquake information parameters are chosen as input features. Considering the importance and availability of input features, the reduced-feature SVR for MDR prediction model (RSVR-MDR) model is also developed. Finally, the performance of SVR-MDR, RSVR-MDR are analyzed and compared to linear regression models.

Dataset and methodology

Seismic response database of building structures (NDE1.0)

The NDE1.0 is a database of building response records and corresponding earthquake information, which provides a shared and accessible data source for studies on seismic hazard, damage prediction and other fields. The information of NDE 1.0 is from the US CESMD and Japanese BRI, and provides post-processed ground motion IMs, EDPs, building details and earthquake characteristics17. In terms of building typology, the database includes 77 steel frame, 45 reinforced concrete (RC), 11 steel-reinforced concrete (SRC), 10 masonry and 4 wooden structures. Support vector regression

Support vector machine (SVM) is a supervised learning method that can be used for both classification and regression tasks. SVR is a powerful nonlinear regression method, and introduces nonlinear kernel to model nonlinear function of multiple variables and uses insensitive loss function to accept small deviation from objective function18. Training and test results of SVR-MDR model

In this section, earthquake response data of SRC buildings in NDE1.0 is used to train SVR-MDR model. The MDR is selected as target output of the model, and the 41 parameters including 6 structure information parameters, 9 spectrum information parameters, 16 duration information parameters, and 10 other earthquake information parameters are selected as input feature. The training and test datasets are split in a 9:1 ratio using the StandardScaler function of the scikit-learn, a free and open-source machine learning library for Python. Training and test results of RSVR-MDR model

There are 41 input features in the initial SVR-MDR model, and each feature exerts different degrees of influence on the SVR-MDR performance. To quantify the importance of each feature, the SHAP method proposed by Lundberg and Lee is employed20. SHAP is an explainability tool for ML aiming to evaluate the impact of each feature. It employs Shapley value as the evaluation index, which reflects the average change in the prediction outcome when a specific feature is added to all possible combinations of other features20. Due to the relatively high computational cost of applying SHAP for SVR-MDR, 604 sets of data, about 10% of total SRC earthquake response data, are randomly selected. The absolute SHAP values of each feature on these 604 samples are ranked as shown in Fig. 5.

SHAP values of 41 input features: (a) structural information; (b) other earthquake information; (c) spectrum information; (d) duration information.

The fundamental frequency f1 and co-seismic minimum frequency f2 contribute most to the prediction in the 6 structural information parameters. Magnitude, with the highest SHAP value of 85.71, contribute most to the prediction, indicating magnitude’s dominant influence on MDR prediction. In the spectral information parameters, the average of pseudo-spectral acceleration (Avg_Sa) between the fundamental f1 and co-seismic minimum frequency f2 contributes most to prediction. DSa2, the duration corresponding to (5–95)% of total energy associated with ground motion acceleration, contributes most to prediction in the 16 duration information parameters. It is noted that the selection of input feature should not only be based on the SHAP value rankings, but also on the practical availability of input feature. For example, although the fundamental frequency f1 has lower SHAP value than the co-seismic minimum frequency f2, f1 is more accessible than f2. Since the former can be easily obtained by empirical formula or modal analysis; on the other hand, f2 necessitates building response records under specific ground motion21. Consequently, co-seismic minimum frequency and dependent parameters, such as SA2, SV2, SD2, Avg_Sa, Avg_Sv, Avg_Sd, are not easily obtained after earthquake and are not considered in the reduced SVR model. A total of 10 input features are finally selected based on their significance and ease of acquisition, including 3 building information parameters structure height H, number of stories N, and fundamental frequency f1, 4 other earthquake information parameters PGV, PGD, epicentral distance Dep and magnitude M, and 3 spectrum information parameters SA1, SV1, SD1. The RSVR-MDR model is then trained with these 10 input features. The predictive performance of the RSVR-MDR model is shown in Fig. 6. The standard deviation of errors for training and test dataset are 0.192 and 0.213 respectively, almost the same as those in the SVR-MDR model. The calculated for the train and test datasets are 97.0% and 95.7% respectively, also almost the same as those in the SVR-MDR model. In this regards, the RSVR-MDR with 10 features (H, N, f1, PGV, PGD, Dep, M, SA1, SV1, SD1) has almost the same predictive performance as the SVR-MDR model with all the 41 features. It is noted that although duration information parameters DSa2, and DSv2 have higher SHAP value than building information parameters such as structural height H, number of stories N, the corresponding predictive performance with 10 features (DSa2, DSv2, f1, PGV, PGD, Dep, M, SA1, SV1, SD1) is worse than that using 10 features (H, N, f1, PGV, PGD, Dep, M, SA1, SV1, SD1), as shown in Fig. 7. In this regards, in the combination of multiple features, the building information parameters are more important than duration information parameters.

Conclusion

In this paper, first a full-feature SVR-MDR model is trained using data of SRC structures from the NDE1.0 database to predict the MDR of buildings. Then a reduced-feature RSVR-MDR model with much less input features that are selected based on the contribution and accessibility. The performance of SVR-MDR and RSVR-MDR is compared against conventional loglinear methods. Additionally, performance of models with data of other types of building is analyzed and discussed. We can find that (1) the SVR-MDR and RSVR-MDR models demonstrate high accuracy in predicting the MDR of buildings, significantly outperforming traditional loglinear regression methods; (2) the reduced-feature RSVR-MDR model offers nearly the same predictive performance as the full-feature model, while requiring fewer input parameters that are easily accessible; (3) models trained separately on specific building types provide better predictive accuracy than those trained using data from all building types combined. We believe the proposed RSVR-MDR model can give accurate and rapid MDR predictions and provide important data for near real-time seismic damage assessment and post-earthquake decision-making.

Although the proposed SVR models have better predictive performance than KRR and DT models, for a more comprehensive comparison, the SVR models need to be compared with other ML methods, especially ensemble learning methods such as gradient boosting machine, extreme gradient boosting. The authors believe that there will be more research using ML or deep learning methods to fully utilize NDE1.0 to predict structural responses in the future. Another limitation of SVR models is that we do not consider sufficient structural information, such as high-order frequencies of the structure, presence or absence of infill walls, building construction age, and seismic fortification intensity of the building. If the above structural information is considered, the SVR models may further reduce the prediction variance. However, due to the limited availability of these structural information, we can only use the basic information such as B_lat, B_long, H, N, f1 and f2 provided by NDE1.0 to establish the optimal model. For further research, we will use the original earthquake records and structural information from CESMD and BRI, conduct modal analysis to obtain high-order frequencies and high-order response spectra, and establish ML models that consider more high-order structural information as features.
