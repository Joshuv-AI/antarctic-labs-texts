High Ki-67 as an independent prognostic factor in extraskeletal osteosarcoma: a comparative cohort study
Authors: Hülya Odabaşı Bükün, Türkkan Evrensel, Erdem Çubukçu, Adem Deligönül, Ahmet Bilgehan Şahin, Ender Eren Özçelik, Ömer Can Kaya, Ülviye Yalçınkaya, Seyit Ali Volkan Polatkan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe objectives of this study are to compare clinicopathological features and outcomes of extraskeletal osteosarcoma (EOS) and conventional osteosarcoma (COS) and identify independent prognostic factors for disease-free survival (DFS) and overall survival (OS).MethodsThis study included 72 patients (Group EOS, n = 12; Group COS, n = 60) with a definitive diagnosis. Clinicopathological variables, SATB2 expression, and Ki-67 proliferation index were compared between groups. Outcomes were assessed using the Kaplan–Meier method and the log-rank test, and independent prognostic factors were identified by Cox regression analysis.ResultsGroup EOS had significantly higher median age (53.5 vs. 33.4 years, p < 0.001) and Ki-67 proliferation index than Group COS. Primary tumor location differed significantly (p < 0.001), with EOS cases affecting internal organs (kidneys, lungs) and COS cases mainly in long bones around the knee. Metastases were more common in Group EOS (83.3% vs. 13.6%, p < 0.001). Median OS and DFS for Group EOS were 19.3 months (vs. 71.6 months for COS, p = 0.007) and 2.2 months (vs. 21.6 months for COS, p < 0.001), respectively. Multivariate analysis identified age (OS: HR per year 1.04, 95% CI: 1.02–1.06, p < 0.001; DFS: HR 1.03, 95% CI: 1.02–1.05, p < 0.001) and Ki-67 proliferation index (OS: HR per %-point 1.02, 95% CI: 1.00–1.04, p = 0.038; DFS: HR 1.02, 95% CI: 1.01–1.04, p = 0.006) as independent prognostic factors.ConclusionsSurvival outcomes for patients with EOS were significantly worse than for those with COS. High Ki-67 proliferation index and advanced age were associated with a more aggressive clinical course of EOS. Therefore, more intensive follow-up and treatment are prudent for elderly EOS patients with a high Ki-67 proliferation index.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-026-16120-0.

Background

Extraskeletal osteosarcoma (EOS) and conventional osteosarcoma (COS) are malignant mesenchymal neoplasms that produce osteoid matrix but may represent distinct biological entities. EOS is a rare and aggressive malignancy of mesenchymal origin that can produce osteoid or chondroid matrix [1]. EOS primarily affects patients over 50 years of age compared to conventional osteosarcoma, and accounts for approximately 1% of all soft tissue sarcomas and 4% of all osteosarcomas. It usually affects the extremities, especially the thigh, but can also involve the retroperitoneum, chest, breast, and orbit [2–4].

Osteosarcoma is the most common primary mesenchymal tumor of bone. It is frequently seen at a young age, with its incidence peaking between the ages of 18 and 60. The most commonly affected anatomical region is the metaphysis of long bones, including the distal femur, proximal tibia, and proximal humerus, with more than half of cases developing around the knee joint [5]. Although the etiology of EOS, as with most tumors, remains unknown, several factors have been implicated, including advanced age, radiation exposure, intramuscular injections, and myositis ossificans. Unlike conventional osteosarcoma, no significant genetic abnormalities have been associated with EOS [3, 4]. The most common genetic alterations in osteosarcoma are mutations in the p53 tumor suppressor gene and loss of RB1, the retinoblastoma gene [6]. Osteosarcoma is a relatively radioresistant tumor, and the susceptibility of EOS to radiotherapy remains unclear [7].

In patients with localized EOS, the 5-year disease-free survival rate was similar between the surgical plus (neo)adjuvant chemotherapy and surgery alone groups [8]. There is no clear consensus on the optimal chemotherapy regimen. Patient age and tumor size are factors that affect the prognosis of EOS. Radiotherapy has not been shown to provide significant benefit on prognosis. In one study, multivariate Cox regression analysis demonstrated that tumors at visceral T2 or T4 sites were independently associated with worse overall survival, while tumors located in areas other than the breast and soft tissues were associated with better OS [9].

Given these considerations, we conducted this study to compare histopathological features, Ki-67 indices, and clinical characteristics of EOS and COS, and to identify independent factors influencing prognosis and survival.

Methods

Study design and setting

This retrospective observational cohort study was conducted at the Department of Medical Oncology, Uludağ University, Bursa, Turkey, a tertiary referral center, between January 2015 and December 2023. The study protocol was approved by the Health Research Ethics Committee of Uludağ University Faculty of Medicine (Decision No: 2025/5–27, Decision Date: March 5th, 2025). The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical considerations outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Due to the study’s retrospective nature, the requirement for informed consent from participants was waived.

Population and sample

This study’s population consisted of patients with EOS and COS. The World Health Organization (WHO) defines EOS as a malignant mesenchymal tumor arising in soft tissues without bone involvement that produces osteoid, bone, or chondroid matrix, and COS as a primary bone cancer.

After all cases were reviewed by an experienced pathologist, the sample comprised 12 EOS and 60 COS patients with confirmed diagnoses.

Data collection procedure

We obtained the study data from patients’ electronic records, pathology and radiology reports, and surgical notes. We recorded patients’ age at diagnosis, gender, tumor size, and tumour, node, metastasis (TNM) stage according to the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) 8th edition.

The primary tumor locations in EOS patients were lower extremity soft tissues in 4 (33.3%) patients, upper extremity soft tissues in 3 (25%), kidneys in 2 (16.7%), lungs in 2 (16.7%), and chest wall in 1 (8.3%) and in COS patients were lower extremity (knee region, femur, or tibia) in 32 (54.2%) patients, upper extremity (humerus) in 8 (13.6%), maxilla/mandible of the face in 12 (20.3%), the chest wall in 4 (6.8%), and the vertebrae in 3 (5.1%) patients.

We recorded patients’ histological tumor grade, Ki-67 proliferation index, tumor necrosis, mitotic count per 10 fields, treatment modalities (including neoadjuvant and adjuvant chemotherapy regimens), details of radiotherapy (purpose, site, duration), and any recurrence treatment(s) and second-line chemotherapy received.

As for the patients’ oncological outcomes, we assessed subsequent recurrence or metastasis status, metastasis site, survival status, OS (time from diagnosis to death from any cause), and DFS (time from achievement of complete remission to recurrence or death).

Imaging analysis

Upon diagnosis, all patients underwent standardized imaging protocols, including computed tomography (CT) scans featuring a slice thickness of 3–5 mm and/or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI).

Histopathological analysis

Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was performed for morphological assessment, using the same protocol for all patients. Tissue samples were fixed in 10% neutral-buffered formalin for 24 h. Sections were incubated in hematoxylin for 5 min and then in eosin for 2 min. The samples were analyzed under a light microscope. In EOS patients, we observed histopathological similarities in the lung (Fig. 1), kidney (Fig. 2), and lower extremity (Fig. 3). These included osteoid matrix uptake, Ki-67 expression, STAT6, and neoplastic cells with significant pleomorphism.

Immunohistochemical analysis

Formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded tissue Sect. (4 μm thick) were prepared from the most representative tumor blocks selected after reviewing H&E-stained slides. Immunohistochemical (IHC) staining was performed using the Leica Bond Max automated immunostainer (Leica Microsystems, Berlin, Germany). Heat-induced epitope retrieval was performed using citrate buffer (pH 6.0).

IHC staining was conducted for special AT-rich sequence-binding protein 2 (SATB2) (clone SATBA4B10, dilution 1:100, Cell Marque). SATB2 expression was evaluated either as positive (nuclear staining in ≥ 5% of tumor cells) or negative. Sections were incubated with a Ki-67 primary antibody (Ventana CONFIRM anti–Ki-67 [30 − 9] rabbit monoclonal primary antibody; Ventana Medical Systems, Inc., Tucson, AZ) for 30 min at room temperature. Immunoreactivity was visualized using the OptiView IHC DAB detection system on the BenchMark Ultra automated staining platform, followed by hematoxylin counterstaining. Lymph node tissue was used as a positive control.

Ki-67 proliferation index was assessed using the hotspot method at 400× magnification. A minimum of 100 tumor cells were counted in the area of highest nuclear immunoreactivity. Cells with nuclear staining of any intensity were considered positive, and results were recorded as the percentage of positive tumor cells.

All IHC evaluations were performed simultaneously by two experienced pathologists using a dual-headed microscope, with consensus reached for each case. Formal interobserver agreement analysis was not performed; however, this dual-review approach ensured consistency in Ki-67 scoring.

Follow-up procedures

Patients were followed up per the standardized institutional protocol, which includes clinical examinations, laboratory tests, and imaging studies (chest CT and local MRI) every 3 months for the first 2 years, every 6 months between years 3 and 5, and annually thereafter.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses of the collected data were performed using Python (lifelines package, version 0.27), Jamovi project 2.6.44 (Jamovi, version 2.6.44, 2025, retrieved from https://www.jamovi.org), and JASP 0.95.4 (Jeffreys’ Amazing Statistics Program, version 0.95.4, 2025, retrieved from https://jasp-stats.org) software packages. A two-sided p value ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

The results of the statistical analyses were expressed using descriptive statistics, i.e., median with interquartile ranges (IQRs) for non-normally distributed continuous variables, and frequencies (n) and percentages (%) for categorical variables. Numerical and categorical variables were compared between Groups EOS and COS using Fisher’s Exact test or the Fisher-Freeman-Halton test in cases where the expected cell counts were < 5 and the Mann-Whitney U test, respectively.

Missing data were handled using group-stratified median imputation. The Ki-67 proliferation index data, which were missing in 30 patients (41.7%; EOS: n = 2, COS: n = 28), were imputed using group-specific median values (EOS: 70%, COS: 30%). The tumor size data, which were missing in 7 (9.7%; all in Group COS) patients, were imputed using the median tumor size of the COS patients (7.0 cm). T-stage and clinical-stage data, which were missing in 3 patients (4.2%; all in Group COS), were imputed using the mode values. Missing data analysis revealed that the absence of Ki-67 data was associated with age (p = 0.002), suggesting a missing-at-random (MAR) pattern, which supports the use of conditional imputation methods. Survival curves were estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method and compared using the log-rank test.

Univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression analyses were performed to identify independent prognostic factors for OS and DFS. Variables with p < 0.20 in univariate analysis were further analyzed using the multivariate model. To address overfitting in relation to the limited sample size (n = 72, events: OS = 38, DFS = 50), three nested multivariate models were constructed with increasing complexity: Model 1 (four variables: age, clinical stage, patient group, Ki-67), Model 2 (five variables: Model 1 plus tumor size), and Model 3 (six variables: Model 2 plus T stage). T stage and clinical stage were dichotomized as T1-2 versus T3-4 and early (I-II) versus advanced (III-IV), respectively.

The proportional hazards assumption was evaluated using Schoenfeld residuals with the global test. Model fit was assessed using the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and Harrell’s concordance index (C-index).

To address potential overfitting in the multivariable Cox regression models, events-per-variable (EPV) ratios were calculated for each nested model and reported in Supplementary Table S1. Penalized Cox proportional hazards regression with L2 regularization was performed alongside standard Cox regression to evaluate the stability of hazard ratio estimates under regularization (Supplementary Table S2). The proportional hazards assumption was assessed for all models using Schoenfeld residuals and the global chi-squared test. For overall survival, the assumption was satisfied across all covariates and models (global test p > 0.05). For disease-free survival, tumor size showed a departure from proportionality (Schoenfeld p = 0.02); however, the primary variable of interest (Ki-67) satisfied the assumption across all models and both outcomes.

To assess the robustness of findings to the missing data handling approach for Ki-67, three complementary strategies were employed: (1) group-specific median imputation (EOS median: 70%; COS median: 30%), (2) complete-case analysis restricted to patients with available Ki-67 data, and (3) multiple imputation using chained equations (MICE) with predictive mean matching, generating 20 imputed datasets over 10 iterations. Auxiliary variables included patient group, age at diagnosis, tumor size, clinical stage, survival status, and overall survival time. Pooled estimates were obtained using Rubin’s rules, and the fraction of missing information was calculated for each parameter (Supplementary Table S3). For complete-case models with EPV below 10, penalized Cox regression was applied.

To establish a clinically relevant Ki-67 threshold, time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was performed using inverse probability of censoring weighting at 12-, 24-, and 36-month time points for overall survival and distant metastasis (Supplementary Table S4, Supplementary Figure S1). The optimal binary cut-off value was determined using Youden’s index. The discriminative contribution of Ki-67 to the Cox regression model was quantified using Harrell’s concordance index (C-statistic) and the likelihood ratio test comparing models with and without Ki-67 (Supplementary Table S5). Kaplan-Meier survival curves stratified by the ROC-derived Ki-67 cut-off (≥ 45%) and by the literature-based 30% cut-off were compared using the log-rank test (Supplementary Table S6, Supplementary Figure S2).

The median age of Group EOS was significantly higher than that of Group COS (53.5 [42.3–78.2] years vs. 33.4 [11.3–77.5] years; p = 0.001). Although males were in the majority in Group EOS (75.0%), there was no significant difference between the groups in terms of gender (p = 0.243).

The rate of patients with an advanced T stage (T3-4) tumor was significantly higher in Group EOS than in Group COS (58.3% vs. 26.3%, p = 0.043). Similarly, the rate of patients with advanced (III-IV) clinical stage was significantly higher in Group EOS than in Group COS (91.7% vs. 38.6%, p = 0.001). On the other hand, the rate of patients in the early clinical stage (I-II) was significantly higher in Group COS than in Group EOS (61.4% vs. 8.3%; p = 0.001).

The distribution of tumors’ histopathological characteristics and treatment modalities received by the patients by the study groups is shown in Table 2. Accordingly, it was found that high-grade tumors were predominant in both groups, while the median Ki-67 proliferation index was significantly higher in Group EOS than in Group COS (70% vs. 30%, p = 0.001). No significant differences were found in tumor necrosis, mitotic count, procedure type, or surgical margin positivity between the groups. Neoadjuvant chemotherapy was administered exclusively in Group COS (36.7% vs. 0%, p = 0.001). On the other hand, there was no significant difference between the groups in the rate of patients receiving adjuvant chemotherapy. The groups also differed significantly in the rates of patients receiving different chemotherapy regimens. While patients in Group COS predominantly received cisplatin+doxorubicin (97.4%), those in Group EOS received a more diverse range of regimens (p = 0.001).

Treatment outcomes

The distribution of disease recurrence and metastatic sites, and treatment responses following primary therapy by the study groups, is given in Table 3. Accordingly, the rate of patients with local recurrence was higher, albeit not significantly, in Group COS than in Group EOS (n = 20, 33.9% vs. n = 1, 8.3%, p = 0.094). On the other hand, the rate of patients with subsequent metastasis was significantly higher in Group EOS than in Group COS (n = 10, 83.3% vs. n = 8, 13.6%, p < 0.001). 9 (75%) and 16 (26.7%) patients had lung metastases in Groups EOS and COS, respectively (p = 0.002). While the rate of patients who received recurrence chemotherapy was the same in both groups (33.3%), the median duration of recurrence chemotherapy was significantly longer in Group EOS than in Group COS (6.5 vs. 1.8 months, p = 0.009). Lastly, the rate of patients who received second-line chemotherapy was significantly higher in Group EOS than in Group COS (n = 5, 41.7% vs. n = 5, 8.3%, p = 0.009).

The results of the univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses for OS are presented in Table 4. Accordingly, univariate analysis revealed sufficiently significant (p < 0.20) relationships between OS and all evaluated variables, including age (Hazards Ratio [HR]: 1.04, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.02–1.06, p < 0.001), tumor size (HR: 1.09, 95% CI: 1.01–1.18, p = 0.021), T stage T3-4 vs. T1-2 (HR: 1.85, 95% CI: 0.94–3.64, p = 0.076), advanced clinical stage (HR: 1.89, 95% CI: 0.99–3.60, p = 0.054), type of osteosarcoma, i.e., EOS vs. COS (HR: 2.92, 95% CI: 1.30–6.56, p = 0.010), and Ki-67 proliferation index (HR: 1.02, 95% CI: 1.01–1.04, p < 0.001).

Across all models, age and Ki-67 proliferation index consistently emerged as independent prognostic factors for OS (Model 1: age: HR: 1.04, p < 0.001; Ki-67: HR: 1.02, p = 0.038). In Model 1, the EPV was 8.0 for OS and 11.0 for DFS; in Models 2 and 3, the EPV ranged from 5.2 to 8.6. In a sensitivity analysis comparing three missing data approaches, Ki-67 remained a significant independent predictor of both OS and DFS under multiple imputation (OS: HR 1.029, 95% CI 1.014–1.045, p < 0.001; DFS: HR 1.035, 95% CI 1.020–1.050, p < 0.001) and complete-case analysis (DFS: HR 1.028, 95% CI 1.011–1.045, p = 0.001), consistent with the primary median imputation results (Supplementary Table S3). The fraction of missing information for Ki-67 ranged from 0.06 to 0.10, indicating that the imputation model adequately handled the missing data pattern. Adding Ki-67 to the Cox model significantly improved its discriminative ability, as reflected by an increase in Harrell’s C-statistic from 0.745 to 0.755 for OS (likelihood ratio test, p = 0.034) and from 0.719 to 0.745 for DFS (p = 0.011) (Supplementary Table S5).

Time-dependent ROC analysis revealed that the discriminative ability of Ki-67 for overall survival improved over time, with AUC values of 0.674 at 12 months, 0.821 at 24 months, and 0.907 at 36 months (Supplementary Table S4, Supplementary Figure S1A). For distant metastasis prediction, Ki-67 demonstrated consistently high performance, with AUC values ranging from 0.834 to 0.891 (Supplementary Figure S1B). Binary ROC analysis identified an optimal Ki-67 cut-off of ≥ 45% for overall survival (AUC 0.699, sensitivity 64.0%, specificity 76.5%, Youden’s J = 0.405) and ≥ 40% for distant metastasis (AUC 0.656, sensitivity 70.0%, specificity 59.1%). Ki-67 demonstrated limited discriminative ability for local recurrence (AUC 0.552). Kaplan-Meier analysis stratified by the ROC-derived ≥ 45% cut-off revealed significantly worse outcomes in the high Ki-67 group for both OS (median 19.4 vs. not reached months, log-rank χ²=12.41, p < 0.001) and DFS (median 2.5 vs. 25.6 months, χ²=18.12, p < 0.001) (Supplementary Table S6, Supplementary Figure S2A–B).

Discussion

This study features a comprehensive clinicopathological comparison of EOS and COS patients, highlighting their differences in demographic characteristics, tumor behavior, Ki-67 proliferation indices, treatment responses, and survival outcomes. Most notably, EOS patients had significantly worse survival outcomes, with significantly shorter OS (19.3 vs. 71.6 months) and DFS (2.2 vs. 21.6 months) compared to COS patients.

The distinct age distribution observed in our cohort, with EOS affecting predominantly older individuals (median 53.5 years) compared to COS (median 33.4 years), is consistent with previously published data [2, 3]. A study reported a median age of 53.7 years in their series of 40 EOS patients, which is remarkably similar to our findings [1]. While COS frequently affects adolescents and young adults during periods of rapid bone growth, EOS appears to develop through distinct mechanisms, possibly related to age-associated soft tissue changes or prior exposure to environmental factors [4].

Our finding regarding the unique anatomical distribution of EOS, including significant involvement of visceral organs—16.7% in the kidneys and 16.7% in the lungs—is particularly noteworthy, in view of the fact that COS predominantly affects the metaphyseal regions of long bones, particularly around the knee [5]. The predilection of EOS for extraosseous sites, including visceral organs, implies fundamental biological differences in tissue tropism between EOS and COS. In fact, Ahmad et al. documented atypical locations in their multi-institutional analysis of EOS, including retroperitoneum, abdominal cavity, and thorax [10].

SATB2 expression was positive in all EOS and COS patients in our cohort. SATB2, previously identified as a marker of osteosarcomatous differentiation, is a nuclear matrix protein involved in osteoblast differentiation [11]. Recent studies have highlighted SATB2 as a valuable diagnostic tool for evaluating bone and soft-tissue tumors with osteoblastic differentiation [12].

The significantly higher median Ki-67 proliferation index in EOS patients than in COS patients (70% vs. 30%) correlates with the more aggressive clinical course of EOS. This heightened proliferative activity likely contributes to increased metastatic potential and shorter survival. Multivariate analysis revealed the Ki-67 proliferation index as a significant independent prognostic factor for both DFS (HR: 1.03, 95% CI: 1.01–1.04) and OS (HR: 1.02, 95% CI: 1.01–1.04), suggesting its utility as a prognostic biomarker across osteosarcoma subtypes. Similarly, in a European Musculoskeletal Oncology Society study conducted with 266 EOS patients, Longhi et al. reported a significant correlation between higher Ki-67 proliferation indices and worse outcomes [13]. Ki-67 is an analogous marker indicating proliferation and tumor aggressiveness in breast cancer and many solid tumors. Niculescu et al. [14] found that higher expression of Ki-67, p53, and cyclin D1 was significantly correlated with the aggressive potential for progression in osteosarcoma. They noted that higher Ki-67 expression was significantly associated with the epithelioid subtype, high-grade tumors, and advanced tumor stage. Gu et al. [15] reported that lower Ki-67 expression was an independent predictor of greater chemotherapy sensitivity and a better long-term prognosis in osteosarcoma patients.

It has been thought that a lower degree of proliferative activity, as reflected by lower Ki-67 expression, was a critical determinant of therapeutic response and prognosis. Ki-67 has been speculated to reflect the intrinsic genomic instability and clonal heterogeneity of tumors, leading not only to high proliferative activity but also to a biologically aggressive, therapy-resistant tumor milieu [15, 16]. In a meta-analysis [17], Zeng et al. reported that positive Ki-67 expression was associated with Enneking stage, distant metastasis, and OS in osteosarcoma patients, suggesting that Ki-67 is a potential biomarker for predicting prognosis and guiding clinical therapy. Another systematic literature review found that Ki-67 overexpression in osteosarcoma was significantly associated with Enneking stage, distant metastasis, and lower survival [18]. There are also studies demonstrating the relationship between Ki-67 proliferation index and recurrence [19].

In line with the literature, we found that high Ki-67 levels were significantly associated with shorter survival and a more aggressive disease course [17, 18]. To provide a framework for clinical utility, we performed ROC analysis to identify an optimal Ki-67 threshold. The ROC-derived cut-off of ≥ 45% yielded the strongest prognostic stratification in our cohort, with a median OS of 19.4 months in the high Ki-67 group compared with not reached in the low Ki-67 group. Tanaka et al. [20] stratified the patients as Grade 3 if their Ki-67 level was ≥ 30. They thought that the Ki-67 grading system for soft tissue sarcomas might serve as a criterion for histological grading. Similarly, a Ki-67 cut-off value of ≥ 30 was used to define a high proliferation level for soft tissue sarcomas [21, 22]. In other studies, the median proliferative index, as determined by manual counting, was 24% [23, 24]. The variances in Ki-67 values in these cohorts, from 7% to 61% and 0–90% in our study, might be influenced by several clinical factors [23, 24]. Notably, all EOS patients with available Ki-67 data had values ≥ 30%, compared with only 53% of COS patients, further supporting the association between high proliferative activity and the aggressive clinical behavior of EOS. The literature-based 30% cut-off, commonly used in soft tissue sarcoma studies, also significantly stratified patients into groups with distinct survival outcomes (OS: χ²=9.83, p = 0.002; DFS: χ²=13.71, p < 0.001) (Supplementary Figure S2C) in our cohort, suggesting that this threshold may serve as a practical starting point for clinical risk assessment until validated in larger prospective studies. Taken together, these findings emphasize the importance of integrating Ki-67 proliferation index into the risk-stratification models of osteosarcoma patients to enhance prognostic precision and potentially guide future therapeutic stratification.

From a practical standpoint, Ki-67 may be incorporated alongside established clinicopathological parameters (e.g., stage, tumor size, and metastatic status) to refine risk stratification at diagnosis. Patients with Ki-67 ≥ 45% may constitute a high-risk subgroup warranting closer surveillance intervals, earlier imaging for metastasis detection, and consideration of intensified or investigational adjuvant strategies. In contrast, patients with lower Ki-67 values may be suitable for standard follow-up protocols. Importantly, given the variability in reported cut-offs across studies, the 30% threshold may serve as a pragmatic and generalizable benchmark in routine practice until disease-specific thresholds are prospectively validated. These findings support a model in which Ki-67 functions not as a standalone marker but as a complementary biomarker integrated into multimodal decision-making algorithms.

von Baer et al. [25] reported a case exhibiting features of dedifferentiated EOS. Heterogeneous patterns of differentiation might be critical in the transition from an extraskeletal low-grade osteosarcoma with features close to those of a parosteal osteosarcoma to a dedifferentiated extraskeletal high-grade osteosarcoma [26]. The authors speculated that tumor progression to a high-grade sarcoma may have stemmed from the changes at the genomic level. Although we could not evaluate dedifferentiation in our cohort, the significantly elevated Ki-67 indices—particularly in EOS—may reflect underlying biological events associated with such aggressive transitions, a hypothesis that needs to be tested in future prospective studies.

The metastatic behavior observed in our cohort further highlights the biological disparity between EOS and COS. The significantly higher rate of patients with metastatic disease in EOS patients than in COS patients (83.3% vs. 13.6%) and the fact that most (75%) of these EOS patients had pulmonary metastases underscore the aggressive nature of EOS. This high metastatic propensity may reflect both the intrinsic biological aggressiveness of EOS and the absence of anatomical barriers that might otherwise contain tumor spread, unlike COS, which arises within the bone [7]. In parallel, a high incidence of pulmonary metastases has been reported in EOS patients, noting that such metastatic patterns significantly impact survival outcomes [27].

While the rate of patients receiving adjuvant chemotherapy was comparable between Groups EOS and COS, the chemotherapy regimens differed significantly. The heterogeneity of regimens used in Group EOS (cisplatin+doxorubicin, ifosfamide+doxorubicin, and gemcitabine+docetaxel) compared to the relative homogeneity of regimens in Group COS (predominantly cisplatin+doxorubicin) reflects the current lack of consensus regarding optimal EOS management. Recent studies by Tsukamoto et al. have questioned the efficacy of adjuvant chemotherapy in localized EOS, suggesting that tumor biology rather than treatment approach may be the primary determinant of outcomes [28].

The longer duration of recurrence chemotherapy (6.5 vs. 1.8 months) and higher utilization of second-line chemotherapy (41.7% vs. 8.3%) in EOS patients compared to COS patients likely reflects the attempts to manage the more aggressive disease course rather than treatment efficacy. In fact, a study concluded that even though chemotherapy and radiotherapy are frequently used to treat EOS, their impact on OS remains unclear [29].

Multivariate analysis revealed a significant correlation between higher Ki-67 proliferation indices and a more aggressive clinical course of the disease, suggesting that the prognostic impact of tumor type may be partially mediated through proliferative activity. The most remarkable feature of the Ki-67 proliferation index is its consistent independent prognostic value for survival (OS, DFS). It is particularly important that the Ki-67 proliferation index maintains its prognostic value in multivariate analyses, independent of other factors such as tumor type (EOS or COS) and clinical stage, suggesting that it can be used in clinical decision-making.

Our study has several methodological limitations that warrant consideration when interpreting its results. The first limitation was the relatively small size of Group EOS (n = 12) compared to Group COS (n = 60), reflecting the inherent rarity of EOS. This limitation reduced the statistical power of certain comparisons, particularly subgroup analyses, and was especially relevant for multivariate Cox regression models, as the small Group EOS sample size limited the number of covariates that could be reliably included, increasing the risk of overfitting. Consequently, some variables that were found to significantly predict OS in univariate analyses—such as T stage and tumor size—may have failed to reach significance in multivariate models due to insufficient statistical power, raising the possibility of type II errors. Additionally, surgical margin status, although clinically relevant, was not included in the multivariate analyses due to inconsistent documentation across cases, partial collinearity with tumor size and clinical stage, and the need to preserve an adequate events-per-variable ratio in our limited sample.

The Ki-67 data were missing in 41.7% of patients (46.7% in the COS group), which may introduce bias despite the use of multiple imputation. Although the fraction of missing information was low (0.06–0.10), the possibility of a missing-not-at-random mechanism cannot be excluded. The ROC-derived Ki-67 cut-off of ≥ 45% was determined from the same dataset and requires external validation in independent cohorts before clinical application. Additionally, the proportional hazards assumption was violated for tumor size in the disease-free survival models (Schoenfeld p = 0.02), which should be considered when interpreting the adjusted hazard ratios for this variable, although this violation did not affect the primary variable of interest (Ki-67).

Therefore, the results of our multivariate analyses should be interpreted with caution and validated in future multicenter studies with larger cohorts. While our findings align with previous studies and provide valuable insights, our limited sample size may not have fully captured the heterogeneity within the EOS population and could potentially overemphasize certain clinicopathological features. Therefore, larger, prospective, multicenter studies with standardized data collection are essential to validate our observations, strengthen the statistical robustness of the associations identified, and better characterize the full spectrum of EOS features.

The retrospective study design introduces inherent methodological limitations, including selection bias and the potential for incomplete or variable data quality during the study period. Additionally, the varying follow-up durations impacted the assessment of long-term outcomes and recurrence patterns. These limitations may have limited the precision of survival estimates and the generalizability of our results.

Comprehensive genomic and transcriptomic characterization of EOS should be prioritized to elucidate its unique molecular drivers and potentially identify actionable alterations that could guide precision medicine approaches.

International collaborative networks focused on rare sarcomas should be leveraged to facilitate the accrual of larger EOS cohorts, enabling more robust statistical analyses and potentially identifying prognostic subgroups that may benefit from tailored therapeutic approaches.

Conclusions

We found that higher Ki-67 proliferation indices in EOS patients correlate with a more aggressive clinical course and worse outcomes, suggesting that Ki-67 may be an important prognostic factor. Multivariate analysis identified age and Ki-67 proliferation index as independent prognostic factors for both DFS and OS, providing clinically relevant parameters for risk stratification and treatment planning. The fact that each 1% increase in Ki-67 proliferation index is associated with approximately a 2–3% increase in risk suggests that this parameter can be used for risk stratification. These findings may guide clinicians in developing more personalized therapeutic strategies based on biological risk factors rather than historical treatment paradigms.

In conclusion, Ki-67 is not only a diagnostic marker but also a powerful prognostic factor with practical clinical value. Therefore, routinely assessing the Ki-67 proliferation index in osteosarcoma patients may be valuable for prognostic stratification and treatment planning. More aggressive follow-up and treatment protocols may be required, especially in patients with high Ki-67 proliferation indices.
