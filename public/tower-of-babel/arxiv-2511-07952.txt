Left and Right Bousfield Localization on Lattices
Andrés Carnero Bravo, Shuchita Goyal, Sofía Martínez Alberga, Cherry Ng, Constanze Roitzheim, Daniel Tolosa

Abstract
. The key information of a model category structure on a poset is encoded in a transfer system, which is a combinatorial gadget, originally introduced to investigate homotopy coherence structures in equivariant homotopy theory. We describe how a transfer system associated with in a model structure on a lattice is affected by left and right Bousfield localization and provide a minimal generating system of morphisms which are responsible for the change in model structure. This leads to new concrete insights into the behavior of model categories on posets in general.



Introduction

Transfer systems originally emerged in equivariant homotopy theory with the goal of understanding equivariant analogs of higher coherences.
Nonequivariantly, May
[
May72
]
showed that homotopy-coherent commutative and associative multiplicative structures were in direct and unique correspondence with iterated loop spaces.
These are now commonly known as
E_{\infty}
-structures.
Given a finite group, Blumberg and Hill
[
BH15
]
studied homotopy-coherent commutative multiplicative structures in equivariant settings and found that, unlike in the nonequivariant setting, these so-called “
N_{\infty}
-structures” were no longer unique up to a reasonable notion of equivalence.
In fact, the homotopy type of
N_{\infty}
-operads is completely described by
transfer systems
, which are combinatorial objects, see e.g.
[
Rub21
]
and
[
BBR21
]
.

In a more category-theoretic language, a transfer system on a poset
\mathcal{C}
is a wide subcategory of
\mathcal{C}
closed under pullbacks.
When
\mathcal{C}=Sub(G)
, the subgroup lattice of a finite group
G
, one recovers precisely the notion from
[
BBR21
]
.
Subsequent work has not only linked transfer systems to well-established combinatorial methods, but has also showed that transfer systems can be identified as the acyclic fibrations of model category structures
[
FOO+22
]

[
BOOR23
]
.
Since the data of a model structure can be entirely determined by its classes of weak equivalences
\mathsf{W}
and acyclic fibrations
\mathsf{AF}
, the model category information on a lattice is given by
\mathsf{W}
and a transfer system.

Creating one model structure from an existing one can be a rather difficult process, and
Bousfield localization
provides one way of doing just this.
It is a powerful tool in homotopy theory used to enlarge the weak equivalence class of a model category.
The aim of localization is to formally invert morphisms in its associated homotopy category.
In this paper, we show how the transfer system given by a model structure on a lattice changes under left and right Bousfield localization.
Furthermore, we describe an explicit set of morphisms, which we call
Golden Arrows

\Gamma_{f}
, that generates the new acyclic fibration set by adding
\Gamma_{f}
to the old
\mathsf{AF}
as a transfer system.
In other words, the new acyclic fibrations are given by
\left<\mathsf{AF}\cup\Gamma_{f}\right>
, where
\left<M\right>
denotes the smallest transfer system containing the set
M
.
More precisely, we prove the following statement, which appears as Theorem
2.16
.

Theorem
.

For any model structure
(\mathsf{W},\mathsf{AF})
on a lattice and an indecomposable morphism
f
, right Bousfield localization at
f
coincides with the model structure produced in the following way.

•

The new weak equivalences are given by iteratively closing
\mathsf{W}\cup\left<\{f\}\right>
under a two-out-of-three property and transfer system operations.

•

The new acyclic fibrations are given by the set
\left<\mathsf{AF}\cup\Gamma_{f}\right>
.

This further generalizes a theorem in
[
BOOR23
, Section 5]
, which characterized Bousfield localization on the finite total orders
[n]
.
Dually, we can give an analogous characterization of left Bousfield localization using the notion of co-transfer systems.
Although co-transfer systems have been less studied than their dual counterparts, this contributes to a more comprehensive description of Bousfield localization overall, which in turn contributes to studying their interactions with other structures.

Another topic that this article addresses is the effect of Bousfield localization on special kinds of transfer systems known as
saturated transfer systems
.
In recent work, saturated transfer systems have been strongly linked to equivariant linear isometries operads
[
Rub21
]
.
Furthermore, crucial constructions of commutative
G
-spectra rely on the saturated hull of a transfer system rather than the just transfer system itself
[
BH21
]

[
BHK+25
]
.
In our context, we investigate the following.
While for total orders
[n]
, every model category structure can be written as a sequence of left and right localizations of the trivial model
[
BOOR23
]
, one soon observes this to be false in general, and this article provides many counterexamples.
Yet, saturation helps us to better understand this observation.
Not only does localization preserve non-saturation, but we can say the following in Theorem
4.10
, which provides another initially unexpected bridge between equivariant homotopy theory and model categories.

Theorem
.

Let
(\mathsf{W},\mathsf{AF})
be a model structure on
[m]\times[n]
where
\mathsf{AF}
is a saturated transfer system. Then this model structure can be written as applying a sequence of left and right localizations to the trivial model structure.

We would like to note that while our work is presented for finite lattices, we expect many of the concepts to generalize to arbitrary lattices.

This paper is organized as follows.
We will gather the necessary terminology in Section
1
where we highlight some general properties of model category structures on posets as well as a summary of left and right Bousfield localization, before linking these to the language of transfer systems. With this in place, we move on to Section
2
, where we present our construction on how transfer systems change under right Bousfield localization by adding just a minimal set of arrows, before presenting the dual constructions involving left localization and co-transfer systems in Section
3
.
Finally, in Section
4
we introduce the concept of saturation and examine its interaction with localization.

1.2.
Model Structures on Lattices and Transfer Systems

Recall that a poset
\mathcal{P}=(P,\leq)
is a category where
\mathrm{Ob}(\mathcal{P})=P
and for any two objects
x,y
there
is at most one morphism, more precisely:

\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathcal{C}}(x,y)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}\{\ast\}&\mbox{if }x\leq y,\\
\emptyset&\mbox{else}.\end{array}\right.

In this category, given two objects
x,y
, their product, if it exists, is an object
z
such that
z\leq x,y
and for any other object

w\leq x,y
we have that
w\leq z
.
This means,
z
is the
meet
(the infimum)
x\wedge y
. In the same fashion, the coproduct is the

join
(the supremum)
x\vee y
. A poset is a
lattice
if for any two elements their join and their meet exist.

When we consider a model structure on a lattice
\mathcal{P}
, the axiom MC3 in Definition
1.3

In lattices there are no non-trivial retracts, as in a diagram

\textstyle{y\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{w\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{y,}

we have that

and

and therefore

and

.
On a lattice, we furthermore have a stronger axiom than that of MC2 in Definition

A

1.3.
Left and Right Bousfield Localization

When given a model structure on a category, it is generally of interest to create other model structures on the same category by means of the original model structure.
One strategy for doing this is to enlarge the class of weak equivalences.
Bousfield localizations provide a universal way of executing this strategy.

Starting with a model structure on a category, let us denote the
left Bousfield localization
of a model structure
(\mathsf{W},\mathsf{AF})
by

L_{f}(\mathsf{W},\mathsf{AF})=(L_{f}(\mathsf{W}),L_{f}(\mathsf{AF}))

as well as write
L_{f}(\mathsf{C}),L_{f}(\mathsf{F}),L_{f}(\mathsf{AC})
for the respective cofibrations, fibrations and acyclic fibrations after left localization.
The set
L_{f}(\mathsf{W})
is the smallest weak equivalence set containing
f
and
\mathsf{W}
in some universal sense, while
L_{f}(\mathsf{C}):=\mathsf{C}
.
Dually, the
right localization
of
(\mathsf{W},\mathsf{AF})
is denoted by

R_{f}(\mathsf{W},\mathsf{AF})=(R_{f}(\mathsf{W}),R_{f}(\mathsf{AF})),

and we have the sets
R_{f}(\mathsf{C}),R_{f}(\mathsf{F})
and
R_{f}(\mathsf{AC})
. Again,
R_{f}(\mathsf{W})
is the smallest weak equivalence set containing
\mathsf{W}
and
f
and satisfying a universal property, and
R_{f}(\mathsf{F}):=\mathsf{F}
.
For a detailed treatment of Bousfield localization, see
[
BR20
, Chapter 7.1]
, but we would like to remark that on a lattice, the usual set-theoretic hurdles for the existence of a Bousfield localization become trivial.

Of course, when altering one class of morphisms in a model structure, one is typically interested in how this alteration affects the other classes of maps, and whether one can control this alteration. A basic consequence of the definitions is the following.

•

L_{f}(\mathsf{W})\supseteq\mathsf{W}
,

•

L_{f}(\mathsf{C})=\mathsf{C}
and
L_{f}(\mathsf{AF})=\mathsf{AF}
,

•

L_{f}(\mathsf{AC})\supseteq\mathsf{AC}
,

•

L_{f}(\mathsf{F})\subseteq\mathsf{F}
.

For right Bousfield localization, dually, we have

•

R_{f}(\mathsf{W})\supseteq\mathsf{W}
,

•

R_{f}(\mathsf{F})=\mathsf{F}
and
R_{f}(\mathsf{AC})=\mathsf{AC}
,

•

R_{f}(\mathsf{AF})\supseteq\mathsf{AF}
,

•

R_{f}(\mathsf{C})\subseteq\mathsf{C}
.

As described above, left localization does not change acyclic fibrations, so
L_{f}(\mathsf{AF})=\mathsf{AF}
.
However,
R_{f}(\mathsf{AF})\supset\mathsf{AF}
.
Therefore, our question now becomes, how does right localization affect the transfer system
\mathsf{AF}
?
In the case where the lattice is the total order lattice,

[

, Proposition 5.12]
and we recall their result here.

.

where for

with the addition of the arrows

2.
An explicit characterization of Bousfield localization on finite lattices

We know that the data of a model structure is encoded entirely by its weak equivalences
\mathsf{W}
together with its acyclic fibrations
\mathsf{AF}
, the latter forming a transfer system.
Right Bousfield localization enlarges both
\mathsf{W}
and
\mathsf{AF}
, and the goal of this section is to provide explicit constructions to make this precise, which we state in Theorem
2.16
.
Moving forward, we will specifically focus on right localizing at
short edges
as in the following definition.

Definition 2.1
.

We say that a morphism
f
in a lattice is
short
if it is indecomposable, i.e. if
f=f_{2}\circ f_{1}
then either
f_{2}
or
f_{1}
is the identity.

By the universal property of Bousfield localization, every Bousfield localization can be written as a sequence of localizations along short arrows. Therefore, it will suffice for our purpose to understand those.

Definition 2.2
.

Given any collection of morphisms in a category,
M\subset\operatorname{Mor}(\mathcal{C}),

let the
transfer system closure
of
M
, denoted
\left<M\right>
, be the smallest transfer system on
\mathcal{C}
containing
M
.

In general,
\left<M\right>
is the smallest subcategory of
\mathcal{C}
containing
M
that is closed under pullbacks. In practice, the order of closing under composition and pullbacks can be fixed as in the construction below.

Construction 2.3
.

For poset categories
\mathcal{C}
there is an explicit procedure for constructing
\langle M\rangle
, i.e. the smallest transfer system containing the set
M
, see
[
MOR+25b
, Lemma 3.6]
. We give the procedure below. The construction involves expanding the collection
M
in two stages
M_{1},M_{2}
to build the smallest transfer system containing
M
.

•

Restriction:
M_{1}\coloneqq\{K\wedge L\to L\mid(K\to H)\in M,(L\to H)\in\mathcal{C}\}
,

•

Composition:
M_{2}\colon=\{f_{1}\circ f_{2}\circ\ldots\circ f_{n}\mid f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}\in M_{1}\}
.

The resulting collection
M_{2}
is the transfer system closure
\langle M\rangle
that we seek. In words,
M_{1}
expands
M
by addding all morphisms that are pullbacks of any morphisms in
M
along any map in the category. Finally,
M_{2}
expands
M_{1}
by addding any morphisms that are compositions of finitely many morphisms in
M_{1}
.

Now we describe the general construction for enlarging the acyclic fibrations of a model structure by a set
M
. The goal of this section is to produce a set
\Gamma_{f}
such that the right localization
R_{f}(\mathsf{W},\mathsf{AF})
of a model structure
(\mathsf{W},\mathsf{AF})
is given by enlarging
\mathsf{AF}
by
\Gamma_{f}
.

Definition 2.4
.

Given a model structure
(\mathsf{W},\mathsf{AF})
on a lattice and a set of morphisms
M
, we define the
model structure closure with respect to
M
as

M(\mathsf{W},\mathsf{AF})=(R_{M}(\mathsf{W}),\left<\mathsf{AF}\cup M\right>).

Proposition
2.5
will show that
M(\mathsf{W},\mathsf{AF})
is indeed a model structure, which justifies the name.

Proposition 2.5
.

Let
(\mathsf{W},\mathsf{AF})
be a model structure on a lattice
P
, then
M(\mathsf{W},\mathsf{AF})
is also a model structure on
P
.

Proof.

First, we observe that by definition,
\left<\mathsf{AF}\cup M\right>\subseteq R_{M}(\mathsf{W})
.
As
(\mathsf{W},\mathsf{AF})
is assumed to be a model structure, we know that
\mathsf{AF}
is a transfer system with

T_{min}\subseteq\mathsf{AF}\subseteq\mathsf{W},

see Theorem
1.11
. We therefore have to show that
T^{\prime}_{min}\subseteq\left<\mathsf{AF}\cup M\right>

Firstly, note that in any model structure, short edges have to satisfy the following properties in order for the factorization axiom MC5 to hold.

•

Every short edge that is

•

Every short edge that is a weak equivalence has to be a fibration
or

In addition to this, we recall that (acyclic) fibrations are closed under pullbacks, and (acyclic) cofibrations are closed under pushouts.

Using this information, we see that in a model category all short edges in

either must have all their pushouts or all their pullbacks contained in
\mathsf{W}
as well.
This is not sufficient to characterize whether a decomposable subcategory arises as the weak equivalences of a model structure on a finite lattice - one has to add a further condition.

\mathsf{W}

To see what arrows we have to add to

[

The data of a pre-model structure is equivalent to two pairs

({}^{\boxslash}T_{1},T_{1}),({}^{\boxslash}T_{2},T_{2})

are transfer systems and

Theorem 4.10

Let
(\mathsf{W},\mathsf{AF})
be a model structure on

where
\mathsf{AF}

Proof.

We prove the claim in the following steps.

(1)

Create a model structure on
[m]\times[n+1]
with our desired acyclic fibrations
\mathsf{AF}
, and weak equivalences contained in
\mathsf{W}
but not necessarily equal to
\mathsf{W}
. This is achieved in the following substeps:

(a)

Create a model structure
(T_{A},T_{A})
on a smaller grid
A\times[n]
,
A\subset[m]
, where
T_{A}
is the transfer system corresponding to
\mathsf{AF}
in Construction
4.6
.

(b)

Use
T_{A}
to build a model structure
(\mathsf{AF}_{\iota},\mathsf{AF}_{\iota})
, where
\mathsf{AF}_{\iota}
is exactly the transfer system resulting from applying Step (1) of Construction
4.6
to
T_{A}
.

(c)

Right localize
(\mathsf{AF}_{\iota},\mathsf{AF}_{\iota})
further, according to the remaining steps of Construction

, to obtain the model structure
(\mathsf{AF},\mathsf{AF})
.

(2)

Apply left localization to the previous step, which enlarges the weak equivalences to
\mathsf{W}
but leaves
\mathsf{AF}
intact.

So let us begin with creating a model structure with the desired acyclic fibrations and argue inductively using Construction
4.6

and saturated transfer system
T_{A}
such that applying the smaller-to-bigger part of Construction

creates the transfer system
\mathsf{AF}
.

By Lemma

is a model structure on

. By our induction hypothesis,

can be obtained from the trivial model structure by left and right localization, i.e. there are short arrows

(T_{A},T_{A})=(R_{g_{t}}\circ L_{g_{t-1}}\circ\cdots\circ R_{g_{2}}\circ L_{g_{1}})(Id,Id).

Note that for this presentation, we also allow some of the

to be the identity. But this is not the only simplification. Because
(T_{A},T_{A})
is a model structure with its weak equivalences equal to its acyclic fibrations, its only acyclic cofibrations are identity morphisms. By definition, left localization increases the set of acyclic cofibrations, which can only mean that there are no nontrivial left localizations in the above presentation. This means that we can in fact write

(T_{A},T_{A})=(R_{g_{k}}\circ R_{g_{k}-1}\circ\cdots\circ R_{g_{2}}\circ R_{g_{1}})(Id,Id).

Let
\iota:A\times[n+1]\longrightarrow[m]\times[n+1]

•

The arrows

By definition,

for all

•

By the model category axioms, every short arrow in

has to be either an acyclic cofibration or an acyclic fibration.

Since

was given by a sequence of right localizations of the trivial model structure, this means that no acyclic cofibrations were added, and therefore all short arrows in

This implies that

Next, we look at

Recall that Construction

Remark 4.11
.

In
[
KEMO+25
]
, Ormsby, Osorno and coauthors
provide an explicit description of saturated transfer systems on
P\times[n]
for any finite lattice
P
which indicates that the recursive construction on
[m]\times[n]
might be generalized.
Therefore, we also expect a more general version of our saturation theorem to hold.

We can summarize our findings as follows.

•

The left or right localization of a model structure with unsaturated acyclic fibrations will also have unsaturated acyclic fibrations.

•

If the acyclic fibrations are saturated, they might become unsaturated after certain right localizations.

•

On
[m]\times[n]
, if the acyclic fibrations are saturated, the model structure can be obtained as a sequence of left and right localizations from the trivial model.
