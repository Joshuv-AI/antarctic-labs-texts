B Cell Deficiency Protects Against the Development of c‐MET and Beta‐Catenin Driven Hepatocellular Carcinoma in Mice
Authors: Fang Wang, Eun Bi Lim, Rehan Mehta, Yee Ling Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTHepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), a lethal form of liver cancer, continues to show rising mortality rates despite advancements in immunotherapies. This highlights the need for more effective treatments. Recent studies linked the presence of B cells within human tumors, including HCC, to improved responses to immunotherapy and better prognoses. Specifically, tumor‐infiltrating B cells have been associated with reduced cancer cell proliferation, suggesting that B cell‐mediated responses may be harnessed as a potential therapeutic strategy against HCC. However, in advanced HCC, IL‐10‐producing regulatory B cells accumulate and promote tumor growth. These paradoxical findings on B cells in human HCC highlight the necessity for clarifying the specific functions of B cells in HCC development to develop B cell‐targeted therapies. To investigate the role of B cells in HCC, we used B cell‐deficient (muMT) mice and anti‐CD20 antibodies to deplete B cells in WT mice. In both systems, we observed a strong protection against liver tumor formation upon HCC induction, indicating the presence of a potent anti‐tumor effect in the absence of B cells before HCC induction. Additionally, depletion of B cells after tumor foci have formed in the liver at later stage of HCC also potently suppressed tumor growth. Functionally, depletion of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells partially restored susceptibility to tumor growth, suggesting that B cell depletion suppresses HCC development partly through enhanced T cell‐mediated immunity. These findings reveal an underappreciated tumor‐suppressing effect of B cells in HCC and suggest that targeting the mechanisms of B cell suppression may boost anti‐tumor responses in HCC.Keywords: B cells, CD4, CD8, hepatocellular carcinoma, innate immune cells

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is among the deadliest forms of cancer, ranking sixth in global cancer‐related mortality (Bray et al. 2018; Samant et al. 2021). According to the American Cancer Society, more than 800,000 people are diagnosed with liver cancer annually, resulting in over 700,000 deaths worldwide each year. Although immune checkpoint inhibitor therapies have modestly extended survival by 3–5 months, the overall mortality rate of HCC has continued to rise over the past three decades (Rumgay et al. 2022; Ryerson et al. 2016). This underscores the need for more effective and durable therapeutic strategies.

Recent studies have demonstrated that the presence of B cells is associated with improved prognosis and enhanced response to immunotherapy in multiple types of cancers (Chen et al. 2020; DiLillo et al. 2010; Fridman et al. 2022; Fridman et al. 2021; Inoue et al. 2006; Jacquelot and Belz 2021; Laumont et al. 2022; Meylan et al. 2022; Sautès‐Fridman et al. 2019; Wouters and Nelson 2018; Zhang et al. 2021). In human HCC, intra‐tumoral B cell infiltration correlates with decreased cancer cell proliferation (Garnelo et al. 2017; Nie et al. 2022), suggesting that B cells may mediate anti‐tumor immunity and could be harnessed to develop new therapeutic strategies. However, reports on advanced HCC have also shown that regulatory B cells can accumulate and promote tumor growth by secreting interleukin‐10 (IL‐10) to suppress anti‐tumor responses (Catalán et al. 2021; Neo et al. 2025; Petriv et al. 2024; Shao et al. 2014). These findings indicate that the effect of B cells during HCC progression is complex and not simply anti‐tumor or pro‐tumor.

To investigate the role of B cells in HCC development, we employed a c‐MET/β‐catenin‐driven HCC mouse model, which recapitulates the molecular basis of human HCC by overexpressing human oncogenes c‐MET and β‐catenin in mouse liver. Notably, co‐activation of c‐MET and β‐catenin accounts for more than 20% of human HCC cases (Tward et al. 2007). We induced HCC in muMT mice that lack peripheral B cells and in wild‐type (WT) C57BL/6 mice injected with anti‐CD20 antibodies to deplete B cells. In both models, liver tumor formation was significantly reduced in the absence of B cells compared to B cell‐proficient controls, indicating that the presence of B cells is necessary for tumor formation in this context. To elucidate the underlying mechanisms, we profiled immune cell populations in the livers of WT and muMT mice following c‐MET/β‐catenin overexpression, and detected changes in major immune cell types. To determine if these cell types are responsible for the anti‐tumor effect observed in the absence of B cells, we used depletion antibodies against these cell types and found that depletion of CD4+ T cells or CD8+ T cells in muMT mice partially restored tumor development. Our study demonstrates that B cell depletion protects against c‐MET/β‐catenin‐driven HCC development, in part through CD4+ and CD8+ T cells. Lastly, as a proof of concept for interventions, depletion of B cells after tumor foci have formed in the liver also substantially suppressed tumor progression. Collectively, these findings reveal the immunomodulatory role of B cells in supporting liver tumorigenesis and highlight the need to decipher the exact mechanisms underlying B cell suppression to unleash the robust anti‐tumor immunity against HCC.

Materials and Methods

Mice

C57BL/6 J (Strain No. 000664) and B‐cell deficient muMT (Strain No. 002288) mice (Kitamura et al. 1991) were purchased from The Jackson Laboratory. Mice were housed in micro‐isolator cages in a temperature‐controlled facility with a 12‐h light‐dark cycle and were provided with ad libitum access to water and chow.

C‐MET (MET)/Beta‐Catenin (CAT)‐Driven Model of HCC

Age‐ and sex‐matched C57BL/6 J and muMT mice (6–8 weeks old) were injected with oncogene‐expressing plasmids via hydrodynamic delivery, as previously described (Wang et al. 2020). Briefly, 50 μg of total plasmid DNA was diluted in 2 mL of 0.9% NaCl solution per 20 g of body weight and administered via tail vein injection within 7 s. The plasmid mixture contained the Sleeping Beauty transposase (5 μg, SB100, Cat. No. 34879, Addgene) and transposons encoding c‐MET (22.5 μg, pT3‐EF1a‐human c‐MET) and the constitutively active β‐catenin deleted of amino acids 1‐90 (DN90‐β‐CAT) (22.5 μg, pT3‐EF1a‐human DN90‐β‐catenin). Mice were euthanized at 6‐, 8‐, and 11‐weeks post‐injection for analyses. Tumor burden was assessed by calculating the liver‐to‐body weight ratio.

In Vivo Depletion of B Cells

C57BL/6 J mice were intravenously injected with 100 μg of anti‐CD20 antibodies (Cat. No. BE0356, BioXCell) or isotype control antibodies (Cat. No. BE0366, BioXCell) in 200 μL of PBS 1 week before HCC induction by MET/CAT injection. Antibody injections were repeated every 3 weeks to maintain B‐cell depletion.

In Vivo Depletion of Major Immune Cell Types in B‐Cell Deficient Mice

To deplete plasmacytoid dendritic cells, muMT mice were intravenously injected with 70 μg of anti‐Ly6C antibodies (Cat. No. BE0203, BioXCell; or isotype control antibodies, Cat. No. BE0089, BioXCell) 2 weeks prior to HCC induction. Depletion was maintained by bi‐weekly injections until endpoint. To deplete natural killer cells, muMT mice were intravenously injected with 50 μg of anti‐NK1.1 antibodies (Cat. No. BE0036, BioXCell; or isotype control antibodies, Cat. No. BE0085, BioXCell) 3 days prior to HCC induction and maintained by repeating injections weekly until endpoint. For depletion of T cell subsets, muMT mice were intravenously injected with anti‐CD4 antibodies (Cat. No. BE0003‐1, BioXCell; or isotype control antibodies, Cat. No. BE0090, BioXCell), or with anti‐CD8 antibodies (Cat. No. BE0223, BioXCell; or isotype control antibodies, Cat. No. BE0088, BioXCell), 1 week before HCC induction by MET/CAT injection. T cell‐depletion antibodies were injected at 100 μg in 200 μL PBS, and injections were repeated every 2 weeks to maintain depletion.

Isolation of Immune Cells from Mouse Livers

Liver immune cells were isolated following the protocol described by Molina‐Sánchez et al. (Molina‐Sánchez et al. 2020). Briefly, the liver was perfused with 2 mM EDTA in PBS. The tissue was minced with dissection scissors and enzymatically digested in Hank's Balanced Salt Solution (HBSS) (Cat. No. 21‐021‐CV, Corning) containing 1 mg/mL collagenase IV (Cat. No. C4‐BIOC, Sigma‐Aldrich) and 0.02 mg/mL DNase I (Cat. No. 0453628200, Roche) for 30 min at 37°C. The suspension was neutralized with 1xHBSS and filtered through a 70‐μm nylon cell strainer to remove clumps. The filtrate was centrifuged at 30 rcf for 3 min at room temperature to pellet down hepatocytes. The immune‐cell containing supernatant was collected and further centrifuged at 600 rcf for 5 min at 4°C. The resulting cell pellet was resuspended in 36% Percoll (Cat. No. 17089101 Cytiva) in HBSS and centrifuged at 800 rcf for 20 min at 4°C. To remove erythrocytes, the final cell pellet was incubated with ACK lysis buffer (150 mM NH4Cl, 10 mM KHCO3, and 0.1 mM EDTA) for 5 min at room temperature. The resulting single‐cell suspension was used for flow cytometry analysis.

Flow Cytometry

Cells were stained for flow cytometry following a standard protocol. First, cells were stained with Zombie NIR dye (Cat. No. 423105, BioLegend) to distinguish live from dead cells, followed by Fc receptor blocking using anti‐mouse Fc antibodies (clone 2.4G2, BioXCell). Next, cells were incubated with fluorophore‐conjugated antibodies targeting immune cell markers. High‐dimensional flow cytometry was performed using Cytek Aurora spectral flow cytometer (Cytek). Specific immune subsets (CD45+) were identified as follows: B cells (CD19+), T cells (CD3+, CD4+, CD8+, CD44+), NK cells (NK1.1+), macrophages (F4/80+), monocytes (CD14+), neutrophils (Ly6G+), and plasmacytoid dendritic cells (Ly6C+, Ly6D+). Details of antibodies are listed in Table S1.

Western Blotting

Western blotting was performed as previously described (Wang et al. 2018). Briefly, liver tissues were collected and lysed in SDS lysis buffer, followed by protein quantification using a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay kit (Cat. No. 23227, Thermo Fisher Scientific). 20 μg of total protein was loaded for SDS‐PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes. The membranes were incubated with primary antibodies against c‐MET (1:1000 dilution, Cat. No. 8198, Cell Signaling Technology), purified antibodies against β‐CAT (1:1000 dilution, Cat. No. 610153, BD Biosciences) and GAPDH (1:1000 dilution, Cat. No. MAB374, Sigma). Secondary antibodies used were goat anti‐rabbit IgG conjugated with HRP (1:3000 dilution, Cat. No. 7074, Cell Signaling Technology) and goat anti‐mouse IgG conjugated with HRP (1:3000 dilution, Cat. No. 405306, BioLegend). Blots were developed using HRP substrate (Cat. No. WBLUR0100, Millipore‐Sigma), and chemiluminescence signal was detected in the ProteinSimple FluorChem E imaging system.

Immunohistochemistry

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) of Ki67 in liver section was performed as previously described (Wang et al. 2018). Briefly, liver tissues were fixed in 10% formalin, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned. The sections were deparaffinized, subjected to antigen retrieval, and blocked with horse serum. Sections were incubated with a primary antibody against Ki67 (Cat. No. 12202, Cell Signaling Technology) followed by incubation with a secondary antibody (horse anti‐rabbit IgG‐HRP, Cat. No. MP‐7800, Vector Laboratories). Ki67 staining was developed using a DAB substrate kit (Cat. No. SK‐4103, Vector Laboratories). Images were captured using a light microscope (Olympus BX51). Ki67+ cells were quantified in at least five randomly selected fields (magnification ×200) and reported as means ± standard deviation (SD). Three mice per group were analyzed.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10.0 software. Statistical significance was determined using unpaired two‐tailed Student's t‐test for comparisons of means. The total numbers of animals analyzed are shown as individual dots in the bar graphs. A p‐value (adjusted for multiple comparisons) of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Error bars representing standard deviation (SD) are shown in the figures where applicable.

Results

B Cell‐Deficient Mice Are Protected Against the Development of MET/CAT‐Driven HCC

We employed the c‐MET/β‐catenin‐driven HCC model, as it closely resembles human HCC, in which over 20% of patients exhibit co‐activation of c‐MET and β‐catenin (Tward et al. 2007). To investigate the role of B cells in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), we used B cell‐deficient mice to assess their effect on tumor development. To induce HCC, we introduced plasmids with c‐MET and β‐catenin encoding transposons along with a transposase into the livers of wild‐type (WT) and B cell‐deficient (muMT) mice via hydrodynamic injection. Eight weeks after HCC induction, WT mice developed liver tumors, whereas muMT mice exhibited no tumor formation at all with liver morphology indistinguishable from normal liver (Figure 1A). Tumor burden was assessed using the liver‐to‐body weight ratio, which was significantly higher in WT mice compared to muMT mice with MET/CAT injection. The ratio in muMT mice with HCC induction was similar to that in WT mice without overexpression of c‐MET and β‐catenin (Figure 1B). To verify oncogene expression in mice, we analyzed c‐MET expression in liver tissues at different time points (1, 3‐, 5‐, 7‐, 8‐, and 15‐weeks post‐injection) by western blotting. In WT mice, c‐MET expression was detectable from 1 week post injection, and increased along with tumor growth from 5 to 8 weeks (Figure 1C, S1). By contrast, in muMT mice, c‐MET expression was initially detectable at 1 week post injection, but it rapidly declined to undetectable levels by week 3 (Figure 1C, S1). Similarly, the expression of the constitutively active β‐catenin mutant protein, DN90‐β‐CAT, was apparent early on in WT mice and increased at later time points, while expression in muMT mice was transient in the early stage (Figure S1). This lack of sustained oncogene expression in the liver of muMT mice suggests the rapid elimination of transformed hepatocytes and likely explains why muMT mice do not develop liver tumors.

To further evaluate tumor formation, we performed histological analysis of liver tissues at 8 weeks post‐HCC induction. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining revealed large tumor foci in WT mice but not in muMT mice (Figure 1D). Additionally, there were increased numbers of Ki67+ hepatocytes in WT mice compared to muMT mice (Figure 1E, F), indicating tumor cell proliferation in WT livers. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that B cell‐deficient mice are protected against MET/CAT‐driven HCC, suggesting that the presence of B cells is masking a robust anti‐tumor response against HCC.

Depletion of B Cells Before HCC Induction Prevents the Development of Tumor

To further validate the observations in muMT mice, we used anti‐CD20 antibodies to deplete B cells in WT mice. One week before inducing HCC, WT mice received intravenous injection of either anti‐CD20 antibodies or isotype control antibodies. Injection of anti‐CD20 antibodies was repeated every 3 weeks to maintain B cell depletion (Figure 2A). To verify the efficiency of B cell depletion, we analyzed blood samples by flow cytometry 1 week after the initial injection of antibodies. B cells were undetectable in the blood of anti‐CD20‐antibody treated mice (Figure S2). We then induced HCC by introducing MET/CAT‐expressing plasmids into both anti‐CD20‐antibody and isotype‐control treated groups. After 8 weeks, the isotype‐treated group developed large tumors, whereas the anti‐CD20‐treated group did not develop liver tumors (Figure 2B). The liver‐to‐body weight ratios mirrored the pattern observed in WT and muMT mice, with a significantly higher ratio in the isotype‐treated group compared to the anti‐CD20‐treated group (Figure 2C). We performed histological analysis of liver tissues at 8 weeks post‐injection. H&E staining revealed distinct tumor foci in all isotype‐treated mice, while there was no sign of tumor formation in the anti‐CD20‐treated group except one mouse that developed small tumor foci but had no increase in liver‐to‐body weight ratio (Figure 2D). Additionally, immunohistochemistry showed higher numbers of Ki67+ hepatocytes in the isotype‐treated group compared to the anti‐CD20‐treated group (Figure 2E, F). Overall, these findings indicate that depletion of B cells in WT mice, similar to the genetic deficiency of B cells in muMT mice, protects against MET/CAT‐driven HCC progression, further supporting the role of B cells in suppressing anti‐HCC responses.

Immunoprofiling Reveals Changes of Major Immune Cell Types in B‐Cell Deficient HCC Livers

The disappearance of MET and β‐catenin oncoproteins in muMT mice was between 2 and 3 weeks post HCC induction (Figure 1C, S1), a time point at which both innate and adaptive immune cell mediated elimination of transformed cells is possible. We hypothesized that B‐cell deficiency leads to changes in immune cells, potentially increases of immune cell types that eliminate tumor cells. Thus, we performed multi‐dimensional flow cytometry to profile major immune cell populations (Figure S3) in the livers of WT and muMT mice at steady state (without HCC induction) and at the experimental end point of HCC. Quantification of major innate immune cell types showed that at steady state both the frequencies and numbers of innate cells including natural killer (NK) cells (NK1.1+ ), macrophages (CD11b+F4/80+), monocytes (CD11b+CD14+), neutrophils (CD11b+Ly6G+), plasmacytoid dendritic cells (pDCs) (Ly6C+Ly6D+) were not different between WT and muMT mice (Figure S4A,B and S5). Although the absence of B cells naturally led to the increased proportion of total T cells (CD3+) within the hematopoietic compartment (CD45+), the frequencies of total CD4+, CD8+ T cells or effector T cells (CD44+) were not altered (Figure S4C). There were no changes in the cell numbers within the T cell compartment (Figure S4D).

At HCC end point (8 weeks post HCC induction), within the myeloid compartment, only NK cells appeared to be elevated, and the frequency of monocytes was decreased (Figure 3A,B) in the liver of muMT mice. There was no significant distortion in the proportion of CD4+ or CD8+ T cells (Figure 3C), although the total number of T cells (CD3+) might be trending up in muMT mice (Figure 3D). In support of these results observed in muMT mice, WT mice depleted of B cells with anti‐CD20 antibodies also exhibited a trend towards increased numbers of total and T cell subsets compared to the isotype‐treated group (Figure S6). Altogether, immunoprofiling of major immune cell types revealed that there were no major phenotypic differences (other than B cells) between the WT and muMT mouse livers at steady state but subtle distortions in NK cells, monocytes and potentially T cells at the HCC end point.

CD4+ and CD8+ T Cells Partially Contribute to the Anti‐Tumor Effect in B Cell‐Deficient Mice

The presence of B cells may suppress an immune cell population with anti‐tumor effects. Previously, pDCs and natural killer (NK) cells were reported to be increased in the lung of muMT mice, leading to a protection against lung metastasis of melanoma cells (Rawat et al. 2021). However, we found no difference in pDCs in the liver of B‐cell deficient mice at steady‐state or at HCC end point (Figure S5). At the HCC end point, we observed an increase in NK cells in muMT mice, suggesting an expansion or their recruitment to the tumor developing liver in the absence of B cells. However, depleting pDCs using anti‐Ly6C antibodies or NK cells using anti‐NK1.1 antibodies in muMT mice did not lead to liver tumor formation (Figure S7).

In aggregate, in the HCC liver, the T cell compartment appeared to be expanded. B cells interact with CD4+ T cells as antigen presenting cells (Hong et al. 2018), and this crosstalk bilaterally regulates the functions of both B and T cells (Ahrends et al. 2017). We next assessed whether CD4+ T cells contributed to the anti‐tumor response observed in B cell‐deficient mice by depleting CD4+ T cells using anti‐CD4 antibodies. muMT mice were treated with either anti‐CD4 antibodies or isotype control antibodies via intravenous injection every 2 weeks throughout the course of tumor development (Figure 4A). CD4+ T cell depletion was confirmed by flow cytometry analysis of peripheral blood 1 week after the initial antibody injection (Figure S2B). Following confirmation of depletion, MET/CAT expression plasmids were delivered into muMT mice by hydrodynamic injection. The absence of CD4+ T cells was also confirmed in the livers of muMT mice by flow cytometry (Figure S2B). Two out of four muMT mice treated with anti‐CD4 antibodies developed tumor nodules and exhibited elevated liver‐to‐body weight ratio at 11 weeks post‐MET/CAT injection (Figure 4B,C), compared to the typical tumor development in WT mice at 8‐week by which mice required euthanization due to substantial tumor burden (Figures 1 and 2). By contrast, no tumor growth was observed in muMT mice in the isotype‐treated group.

Effector CD4+ T cells can facilitate the activation and differentiation of CD8+ T cells (Ahrends et al. 2017). Therefore, similarly, we depleted CD8+ T cells using anti‐CD8 antibodies (Figure 4A) in muMT mice. CD8+ T cell depletion was confirmed in the blood via flow cytometry 1 week after the initial injection of depletion antibodies (Figure S2C). Subsequently, we induced HCC in these animals by hydrodynamic delivery of MET/CAT expression plasmids. The absence of CD8+ T cells was also confirmed in livers of muMT mice by flow cytometry (Figure S2C). muMT mice treated with anti‐CD8 antibodies developed liver tumor nodules at 11 weeks post‐MET/CAT injection (Figure 4D). Notably, tumors formed in multiple anti‐CD8‐treated muMT mice, whereas only one out of five mice in the isotype‐treated group showed tumor growth. The liver‐to‐body weight ratio was elevated in the anti‐CD8 group (Figure 4E). Altogether, these results suggest that both CD4+ and CD8+ T cells each partially mediate the anti‐tumor effects observed in B‐cell deficient mice.

Depletion of B Cells After the Onset of HCC Suppresses Tumor Growth

Genetic deficiency and depletion of B cells prior to HCC induction prevented the development of HCC. To test if B cell depletion can suppress tumor progression after the onset of HCC, we administered anti‐CD20 antibodies after tumor foci have formed in the liver. In our MET/CAT‐driven HCC model, tumor foci typically initiate at 4–5 weeks post‐induction, with mice reaching a tumor burden endpoint requiring euthanasia by 8 weeks. We thus administered anti‐CD20 antibodies at 6 weeks post‐MET/CAT induction and analyzed the animals at 8 weeks (Figure 5A). In anti‐CD20 treated mice, liver tumor foci were hardly observable (Figure 5B,C) and the liver‐to‐body weight ratio was significantly reduced (Figure 5D) compared to isotype control‐treated mice. These findings suggest that B cells not only play a tumor permissive role at the initiation stage before tumor formation but also at the progression stage, and depletion of B cells after the onset of HCC can effectively inhibit tumor progression.

Discussion

Tumor‐infiltrating B cells have been widely associated with better prognosis. However, regulatory B cells have also been linked to tumor promotion. These conflicting findings have inspired in‐depth functional studies on the role of B cells in different cancers. In this study, we evaluated the effect of B cells on the development of HCC in a relevant mouse model of human HCC. We found that both genetic B cell deficiency (muMT mice) and antibody‐mediated B cell depletion (anti‐CD20‐treated mice) were highly protective against tumor development in the c‐MET/β‐catenin‐driven HCC model. Additionally, depletion of B cells after tumor formation is effective in inhibiting tumor growth. These findings revealed a robust anti‐tumor effect masked by the presence of B cells.

To identify the potential immune cells responsible for the anti‐tumor effects suppressed by B cells, we examined the immune cell profiles in WT and B cell‐deficient (muMT) mice with and without HCC induction. We found that in B cell‐deficient mice, alteration of major immune cell types was minimal and only at the HCC end point. We observed minor increases in NK cells and T cells in the muMT mice, suggesting that B cells may suppress the proliferation and/or the infiltration of these cells into the tumor‐forming liver in WT mice. Importantly, depletion of either CD4+ T or CD8+ T cells (but not NK cells) in muMT mice partially restored tumor susceptibility, indicating that these T cell populations contribute to the anti‐tumor response induced by the loss of B cells.

B cells can modulate the functions of T cells via different mechanisms. Contact with activated B cells in vitro has been shown to induce hypoxia in interacting CD4+ T cells through metabolic competition, suppressing the proliferation and production of TNF and IFNγ by the interacting CD4+ T cells (Imahashi et al. 2022). Additionally, subsets of in vitro and in vivo activated B cells express the inhibitory molecule PD‐L1 (Ogishi et al. 2024), which inhibits the proliferation and survival of co‐cultured CD4+ (as well as CD8+) T cells (Wang et al. 2019). Recently, in non‐alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), it has been shown that IgA+ plasma cells expressing PD‐L1 and IL‐10 directly suppress cytotoxic CD8+ T cell function (Shalapour et al. 2017). Similarly, in our MET/CAT‐driven HCC model, we found a small proportion of liver B cells expressing PD‐L1 (Figure S8). Future experiments blocking PD‐1/PD‐L1 interaction or neutralizing IL‐10 in muMT mice with HCC induction will be highly illuminating on testing the involvement of these potential mechanisms. Besides immune modulating interactions, B cell‐derived extracellular vesicles have been found to be rich in ATP hydrolyzing enzymes CD39 and CD73, which convert ATP released from tumor cells (after chemotherapies) to adenosine, thereby suppressing the growth of CD8+ T cells and their differentiation into cytotoxic T cells (Zhang et al. 2019).

Another major mechanism by which B cells, particularly regulatory B cells (Bregs), suppress T cell functions is via the secretion of cytokines. Through the secretion of IL‐10, Bregs suppress the differentiation of CD4+ T cell subsets into TNF‐ and IFNγ‐producing effector cells that are critical components of anti‐tumor responses (Carter et al. 2012; M. Yang et al. 2012). Additionally, through the production of transformation growth factor beta (TGFβ), Bregs promote the generation of regulatory T cells (Tregs), which in turn suppress effector T cell functions, leading to tumor progression (Lee et al. 2014). We acknowledge that a major limitation of our immune profiling is that we did not distinguish IL‐10‐ or TGFβ‐producing B cells. Thus, we were not able to test the involvement of regulatory B cells directly. Nonetheless, the multifaceted functions of B cells warrant future in‐depth immunophenotyping and functional studies to delineate the exact mechanisms responsible for suppressing the anti‐tumor effect of T cells in HCC.

Although we found that depletion of CD8+ and CD4+ T cells partially rendered muMT mice susceptible to the development of HCC, these mice developed tumors at much later time points (i.e., at 11 weeks post‐HCC induction) compared to 8–9 weeks in B‐cell sufficient mice. The delayed tumor development suggests additional mechanisms contributing to the anti‐tumor immunity observed in the absence of B cells. Since our strategies using anti‐CD4 and anti‐CD8 antibodies were able to completely eliminate CD4+ and CD8+ T cells in the circulation, we do not think the incomplete restoration of tumor development was due to insufficient amount of depletion antibodies used. The synergistic effect of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells as well as contributions from other immune cells are more likely to be at play. Thus, future experiments involving simultaneous depletion of both CD4+ and CD8+ T cells in HCC‐induced muMT mice will be important to elucidate if there is any synergy between these two T cell subsets or additional effects from other immune cells.

In this study, we also profiled the innate immune cell compartment, including macrophages, monocytes, and neutrophils in the livers of WT and muMT mice with HCC induction. However, we observed no significant changes in the frequency or cell number of macrophages and neutrophils associated with B‐cell deficient mice (Figure 3 and S3). Thus, we did not prioritize functional investigations of these cells in the development of HCC. Although monocytes were trending towards a decrease in the liver of B‐cell deficient mice at HCC end point, whether this monocyte population includes any subpopulation that may suppress anti‐tumor response is unknown. pDCs and NK cells, previously reported to have anti‐tumor functions in other cancer models, were either increased or unchanged in the livers at HCC end point in muMT mice. We performed pilot experiments to deplete these cell types using anti‐Ly6C and anti‐NK1.1 antibodies, and found no sign of restoration of tumor development. In‐depth investigations on the phenotypic and functional changes of these innate cell types associated with B‐cell deficiency will be needed to determine their contributions in HCC development.

Lastly, B cells are increasingly recognized for their role in the tumor microenvironment including their direct effect on promoting tumor progression. It is possible that B cells in the HCC liver may also contribute to tumor progression through the secretion of tumor promoting cytokines. Interleukin‐6 (IL‐6), for example, can directly stimulate hepatocyte proliferation (Hatting et al. 2015; Mittenbühler et al. 2018) and thereby promote liver tumor progression. A recent single‐cell RNA‐seq study on tumor infiltrating B cells in multiple human tumors found that 3% of tumor‐infiltrating B cells expressed the transcripts for IL‐6 (Yang et al. 2024).

Collectively, our study demonstrated that B cell deficiency (both genetic deficiency and antibody‐mediated depletion) protects against the development of c‐MET/β‐catenin‐driven HCC, suggesting B cells may be suppressing a robust anti‐tumor immunity. Mechanistically, this anti‐tumor effect is in part from CD4+ and CD8+ T cells. Dissecting these B‐cell mediated immunomodulatory mechanisms will be critical for unleashing the anti‐tumor immune response, thus important for the development of novel immunotherapies to treat HCC and potentially other solid tumors.
