Isolated REM sleep without atonia and isolated REM sleep behaviour disorder: a study of clinical and prognostic differences
Authors: Cresta Asah, Rune Frandsen, Nikolaj Kjær Høier, Lone Baandrup, Steen Gregers Hasselbalch, Poul Jennum
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractStudy ObjectivesIsolated REM sleep without atonia (iRSWA) is increasingly detected on polysomnography (PSG), yet its clinical relevance remains unclear. We compared the demographic profile, sleep physiology, comorbidities, and longitudinal outcomes of people with iRSWA and isolated REM sleep behaviour disorder (iRBD).MethodsWe retrospectively reviewed PSG reports (2008–2022) from a tertiary sleep-medicine center, classifying people as iRSWA or iRBD based on self-reported dream-enactment behavior. Clinical data were extracted from charts, and PSG variables from sleep reports. Neurological and psychiatric diagnoses before the sleep examination were obtained from national registries. Comparisons used multivariable regression and Fine–Gray competing-risk models.ResultsThe cohort comprised 302 participants (iRSWA = 98 and iRBD = 204). Participants with iRSWA were more often female (38.8% vs 23%, p = .004), with a younger age distribution (35–49 years; 25.5% vs 13.7%, p = .015). They reported more daytime sleepiness/fatigue (odds ratio [OR] 2.84, p < .001) despite comparable objective sleep architecture. Objective neurodegenerative signs appeared only in the iRBD group. Pre-index neurological disorders were more common in iRSWA (31.6% vs 19.1%, p = .042), while neurodegenerative conversion during follow-up occurred in 4.1 per cent of iRSWA and 8.8 per cent of iRBD. In multivariable analysis, sleepiness/fatigue and pre-existing neurological disorder were associated with iRSWA; when spouse status was included in the model, spouse status became the strongest predictor, periodic limb movements in sleep of 15 or more reached significance, and the association with sex attenuated.ConclusionsiRSWA differs from iRBD in profile and pre-index comorbidity patterns. Our findings support the view that iRSWA may reflect mechanisms other than a uniform prodromal stage of α-synucleinopathy. Statement of SignificanceREM sleep without atonia (RSWA) is increasingly recognized during polysomnographic evaluation of sleep disturbances, yet its clinical significance remains uncertain. In a direct comparison of sleep-clinic cohorts with isolated RSWA (iRSWA) and isolated REM sleep behaviour disorder (iRBD), we found that participants with iRSWA were younger, more often female, and reported greater self-reported sleepiness and fatigue despite similar sleep macroarchitecture. In multivariable modeling, significant predictors of iRSWA were female sex, pre-existing nondegenerative neurological disorders, and sleepiness/fatigue. When spouse status was added, it became the strongest predictor, while PLMS equal to or greater than 15 reached significance and the association with sex attenuated, underscoring the risk of detection bias in DEB and the need to systematically document patients’ living arrangements in future studies. No objective neurodegenerative signs were observed in the iRSWA group, and only 4 per cent converted to a neurodegenerative disorder within 7 years of follow-up. These findings question whether iRSWA should be regarded as an early stage of neurodegeneration and suggest that iRSWA alone may have limited predictive value. Prospective multicentre studies incorporating serial video-polysomnography and comprehensive neuropsychiatric follow-up are warranted.

Introduction

Isolated REM sleep behaviour disorder (iRBD) is a well-established precursor to neurodegenerative diseases, particularly α-synucleinopathies, with Parkinson’s disease being the most common manifestation [1]. The diagnosis of RBD is defined by two core components: excessive muscle activity during REM sleep, referred to as “REM sleep without atonia” (RSWA), and a history of dream-enactment behavior (DEB). In recent years, several studies have investigated these components in isolation—specifically, whether either may independently serve as a prodromal marker of iRBD and, by extension, neurodegeneration [2–5].

RSWA is of particular interest, as it represents an objective and quantifiable biomarker of disrupted brainstem regulation during REM sleep [6–8]. It is measured via polysomnography (PSG) and has been associated with various conditions, including structural brain lesions, autoimmune disorders, narcolepsy, sleep apnea, psychiatric diseases, and the use of medications, particularly antidepressants [9–18]. When RSWA occurs in the absence of an identifiable cause, it is referred to as isolated RSWA (iRSWA). However, distinguishing isolated from secondary RSWA is challenging, as factors such as sleep apnea and antidepressant use may act as underlying causes of both RSWA and RBD while also frequently occurring as comorbidities in both conditions [14, 19–22]. While investigations suggest that iRSWA may represent a prodromal stage of iRBD and later neurodegeneration [3, 4], its clinical significance remains uncertain. Further phenotypic characterization is required before iRSWA can be reliably evaluated as a prodromal marker of iRBD and subsequent neurodegenerative disorders, as noted by other authors [23].

In this study, we aimed to determine whether iRSWA represents a prodromal stage of neurodegeneration or reflects heterogeneous underlying mechanisms with separate clinical implications. Specifically, we examined differences between people with iRBD and people with iRSWA regarding demographic characteristics, medication use, clinical symptoms, comorbidities, and long-term outcomes, including the risk of conversion to neurodegenerative disorders. This characterization is important for refining the conceptual framework surrounding iRSWA and improving risk stratification, ultimately informing clinical decision-making regarding its prognostic relevance, the need for follow-up, and potential therapeutic considerations.

Methods

Study population and design

Participants were identified through an internal database of sleep reports from January 1, 2008, to July 4, 2023. All the reports were written by trained sleep technicians following standardized scoring protocols. Only reports from 2008 onward were eligible, as relevant diagnostic variables were routinely included from that year forward. Reports were selected using keyword-based search terms for “REM sleep without atonia” (RSWA) or “REM sleep behaviour disorder” (RBD).

Polysomnography assessment

PSGs were conducted either as home-based studies or as video-PSGs during hospital admission. All the recordings were scored according to the version of the American Academy of Sleep Medicine (AASM) manual that was current at the time of scoring. Scoring was initially performed by a sleep technician and reviewed by a sleep medicine specialist. Discrepancies were resolved by consensus. A minimum of 20 minutes of REM sleep was required for inclusion in the study.

The information extracted from the PSG reports included measures of sleep architecture, apnea–hypopnea index (AHI), periodic limb movements in sleep (PLMSs), arousals, awakenings, sleep efficiency, total sleep time, and electrophysiological features suggestive of neurodegeneration.

Categorization of variables

In accordance with International Classification of Sleep Disorders (ICSD-3) research practice, clinically significant obstructive sleep apnea was defined as an AHI of 15 events/hour or more, and clinically significant PLMS were defined as a PLMS index of 15 movements/hour or greater in adults [24]. Sleep efficiency (SE) was dichotomized at 80 per cent (SE < 80% vs ≥ 80%). This pragmatic cutoff was chosen to accommodate our mixed clinical cohort, aligning with age-stratified normative PSG data [25] and values typically reported in clinical cohorts [26, 27]. Electrophysiological features encompassed markers that the interpreting sleep specialist had labeled as suggestive of neurodegenerative changes, based on attenuated or absent K-complexes and sleep spindles, undifferentiated sleep architecture, and low-amplitude EEG activity. This was coded as a binary variable indicating the presence or absence of suspected neurodegenerative EEG changes.

Clinical information

Clinical data were extracted from hospital records. All the participants were assessed by a medical professional, who documented symptoms and objective findings in the electronic health record. Neurodegenerative symptoms were classified as cognitive changes, motor symptoms, cranial-nerve deficits (including olfactory dysfunction), or autonomic symptoms. Clinical information was considered valid if recorded at least 6 months before or after the sleep examination, or if a stable condition was explicitly documented.

Categorization of variables

sleep complaints

For analytic purposes, and given the low frequency of individual symptoms, participant-reported sleep complaints were grouped into six categories, closely mirroring those used in a prior RSWA cohort study [16]: (1) sleepiness/tiredness (sleepiness, sleep attacks, tiredness); (2) noncomplex motor activity (tossing and turning, restless legs, leg cramps, myoclonic jerks); (3) disturbed sleep (fragmented sleep, nocturia, circadian misalignment, insomnia); (4) respiratory symptoms (snoring, pauses in breathing, dyspnea); (5) other symptoms (seizures, palpitations, headache, night sweats, pain, discomfort, enuresis, hyperhidrosis); and (6) REM instability (sleep paralysis, hypnagogic hallucinations).

medication use

For analytic purposes, medications were grouped into categories selected for their potential to influence PSG findings or clinical symptoms: (1) antidepressants; (2) antipsychotics; (3) hypnotics (benzodiazepines and melatonin); (4) dopaminergic agents; (5) other neurologically active drugs (central stimulants, anticonvulsants, lithium); and (6) other medications known to affect sleep (beta blockers, opioids, antihistamines, others).

Demographic variables

Demographic variables, including educational level, marriage status, and employment status, were obtained from Statistics Denmark (DST), a national registry that provides annually updated socioeconomic data for all residents of Denmark. Marital and cohabitation status (“spouse status”) was defined as two adults registered at the same address in a partner relationship, as classified by DST.

Categorization of variables

Age was categorized into 35–39, 40–49, 50–59, 60–69, 70–79, and 80 years or above. This scheme was chosen to avoid masking distributional differences by a single mean, to isolate the less than 50-year subgroup in which isolated RBD is uncommon [28], and to reflect the age-related increase in neurodegenerative diagnoses.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age 35 years or above, (2) PSG with 20 minutes of REM sleep or more, (3) presence of RSWA, and (4) clinical documentation within 6 months of the sleep examination. Participants were excluded if they had a diagnosis of narcolepsy with cataplexy, pseudo-RBD due to obstructive sleep apnea, insufficient clinical documentation, or index date in 2023. Group classification

Patients were categorized as iRSWA or iRBD based on the presence of DEB, defined as repeated episodes of sleep-related vocalization and/or complex motor activity, confirmed by video-PSG or clinical history—consistent with the ICSD-3 [29].

Comorbidities were identified using the Danish National Patient Register (DNPR), which captures all hospital-recorded diagnoses, and supplemented by prescription data from the National Prescription Register (NPR) to identify conditions managed in primary care. Neurodegenerative disorders were defined as Parkinson’s disease (PD), multiple system atrophy (MSA), progressive supranuclear palsy (PSP), Parkinson’s disease dementia, dementia with Lewy bodies (DLB), vascular dementia, frontotemporal dementia (FTD), Alzheimer’s disease (AD), and other or unspecified dementia. Both psychiatric and neurodegenerative disorders were defined using the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10) codes, supplemented by redemption of disease-specific prescriptions (e.g., antipsychotics and cholinesterase inhibitors), according to the predefined criteria detailed in Supplementary Table S1.

Because Danish health-register data for 2023 were still undergoing quality assurance, we excluded people whose index date fell in that calendar year; only examinations performed on or before 31 December 2022 were included. Pre-index neurological and psychiatric comorbidities were identified during the 10-year retrospective observation period preceding the index date. Individuals with a neurodegenerative disorder during this period were excluded from further analyses.

Prospective follow-up began on the index date and ended at the earliest of (1) first diagnosis of a neurodegenerative disorder, (2) death, (3) emigration (registry loss to follow-up), or (4) the administrative censoring date of 31 December 2022.

Statistical analysis

A two-sided significance level of p < .05 was used. Continuous variables were reported as median (IQR) or mean (±SD) and compared using t-tests or Mann–Whitney U tests, as appropriate. Categorical variables were summarized as n (%) using available cases and compared using chi-squared or Fisher’s exact tests. Exact sample sizes (Ns) for each analysis are reported in the tables. Missing data were quantified and reported for each variable; analyses used complete cases, excluding records with missing values for the variable under analysis.

Pre-index and index analyses

Logistic regression estimated odds of self-reported symptoms, objective findings, medication use, and pre-index comorbidities, comparing participants with iRSWA and those with iRBD. Logistic regression models of all the clinical variables, PLMS, AHI, and sleep efficiency had full coverage. For other PSG variables, 3 per cent of iRSWA and 1 per cent of iRBD data were missing; in these analyses, complete cases were used. Exact Ns are reported in Supplementary Tables S2 and S3. All the models were adjusted for age and sex to reduce confounding. Confounders were selected based on established sex differences in self-reported and objective sleep measures and the well-documented age-related deterioration in objective sleep measures. Logistic regression results are reported as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). For count outcomes, Poisson or negative-binomial regression was applied when skewness or overdispersion was present; the results are reported as incidence-rate ratios (IRRs) with 95% CIs.

Post-index analyses

Fine–Gray competing-risk models estimated subdistribution hazard ratios for neurodegenerative disorders, with death as a competing event. Models were adjusted for age and sex. Confounders were selected based on established sex differences in neurodegenerative disease types and the age-related increase in disease risk. All the variables had full coverage with no missing data.

A multivariable logistic regression model was constructed to identify clinical and demographic predictors of iRSWA versus iRBD. The main model included age, sex, AHI equal to or greater than 15, PLMS index equal to or greater than 15, self-reported sleepiness/tiredness, antidepressant therapy, and pre-index neurological disorders. Variables were selected because PLMS, AHI, and antidepressant therapy have been associated with the occurrence of RSWA; sex and age differences between iRSWA and iRBD have been documented; and in our sample, sleepiness/tiredness and pre-index neurological disorders also distinguished groups. A sensitivity analysis additionally included spouse status to assess potential detection bias. Pairwise correlations between predictors were evaluated using Pearson’s correlation coefficients. Model fit was assessed using the likelihood ratio test and Hosmer–Lemeshow test. All the variables in the multivariable logistic regression model had full coverage with no missing values.

Reporting follows the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) checklist for cohort studies.

Results

Sociodemographic characteristics

Patients with iRSWA were more often female than those with iRBD (38.8% vs 23.0%, p = .004). The mean age was 58.4 years (SD = 12.7) in the iRSWA group and 62.5 years (SD = 10.9) in the RBD group; this difference was statistically significant (p = .008, Mann–Whitney U test). A significant difference in age group distribution was observed between groups (p = .015), with the iRSWA group showing a higher proportion of younger individuals (35–49 years age range 25.5% vs 13.7%). Individuals with iRSWA were less often married or cohabitating (44.9% vs 74.5%, p < .001). No significant group differences were observed in educational level, employment status, smoking, or alcohol and drug abuse. See Table 1 for further details.

Sleep examination findings

Groups were comparable across most objective sleep parameters. The prevalence of clinically significant sleep apnea (AHI ≥ 15) did not differ between groups (iRSWA: 27.6% vs iRBD: 33.8%, p = .948). Likewise, the proportions of individuals with clinically significant periodic limb movements of sleep (PLMS index ≥ 15) were similar (iRSWA: 58.2% vs iRBD: 50.5%, p = .063). The iRSWA group had higher total sleep time (incidence rate ratio [IRR] 1.03, 95% CI = 1.02% to 1.04%, p < .001), while other indices of sleep continuity—including sleep efficiency greater than 80 per cent, arousal count, and number of awakenings—were similar (Figure 2). The relative amounts of rapid eye-movement (REM) and slow-wave (N3) sleep did not differ. In contrast, alterations in sleep architecture suggestive of prodromal neurodegeneration were only seen in the iRBD cohort (5.9% vs 0%).

Clinical findings

Patients with iRSWA more frequently reported excessive sleepiness or fatigue (76.5% vs 51.5 %, OR = 2.84, 95% CI = 1.64% to 4.94%, p < .001), but no other sleep complaint categories differed significantly between groups. Registered psychiatric symptoms at the time of the sleep examination were comparable between groups (iRSWA 4.1% vs iRBD 3.9%), while neurodegenerative symptoms were only seen in the iRBD group (4.4% vs 0%). Medication use

Patients in the iRSWA group were significantly more likely to be taking central stimulants, anticonvulsants, or lithium at the time of the sleep examination (22.4% vs 9.8%, OR = 2.1, 95% CI = 1.06% to 4.19%, p = .035). Use of antidepressants, antipsychotics, hypnotics, dopaminergic agents, and other medications known to affect sleep (e.g., beta blockers, opioids, antihistamines), did not differ significantly between groups (Figure 4).

Comorbidities prior to sleep examination

There was a higher prevalence of neurological disorders other than neurodegeneration in the iRSWA group compared to the RBD group (31.6% vs 19.1%, OR = 1.80, 95% CI = 1.02% to 3.17%, p = .042). Similarly, psychiatric disorders (63.3% vs 48.5%) were numerically more frequent in the iRSWA group, although these differences were not statistically significant (Table 2).

Comorbidities after sleep examination

The incidence of neurodegenerative disorders was lower in participants with iRSWA than in those with iRBD (n = 4, 4.1% vs n = 18, 8.8%; HR = 0.53, 95% CI = 0.17% to 1.64%, p = .270), but this difference was not statistically significant, and the number of incident cases was small in both groups. Parkinson’s disease was the most frequently observed neurodegenerative diagnosis in both groups. Median follow-up was 6 years and 10 months in the iRSWA group and 4 years and 5 months in the iRBD group. Mortality and censoring rates were similar between groups (Table 3).

Multivariable logistics regression model

In the main model, self-reported sleepiness/tiredness (OR 2.99, 95% CI = 1.68% to 5.34%, p < .001), female sex (OR 1.89, 95% CI = 1.06% to 3.33%, p = .030), and pre-existing neurological disorder (OR 2.01, 95% CI = 1.09% to 3.71%, p = .025) were significant predictors of iRSWA classification. Age, AHI equal to or greater than 15, PLMS equal to or greater than 15, and antidepressant therapy were not significant predictors. When adding spouse status in the sensitivity model, the association between sex and diagnosis was attenuated (OR = 0.70, 95% CI = 0.38% to 1.28%, p = .241), and PLMS equal to or greater than 15 emerged as a significant predictor of iRSWA (OR = 1.79, 95% CI = 1.03% to 3.11%, p = .037). Sleepiness/tiredness (OR = 3.13, 95% CI = 1.72% to 5.72%, p < .001) and pre-existing neurological disorders (OR 2.06, 95% CI = 1.09% to 3.87%, p = .026) remained strong predictors and not having a spouse was the strongest predictor of iRSWA (OR = 0.30, 95% CI = 0.17% to 0.54%, p < .001). These findings are summarized in Table 4. Pairwise Pearson correlations were calculated to screen for potential collinearity among predictors. All the absolute correlations were weak (|r| < 0.30). Spouse correlated positively with being male (r = 0.27, p < .001) and negatively with antidepressant therapy (r = −0.23, p < .001). Inclusion of spouse improved model fit (Akaike Information Criterion [AIC] 358.6–343.2) and discrimination (c = 0.715–0.753) but reduced calibration (Hosmer–Lemeshow p = .41 to .0049).

Discussion

In this retrospective study, we compared individuals with iRSWA to those with iRBD in a large sleep clinic cohort. Our findings highlight several clinical differences adding to the debate on whether iRSWA represents an early prodromal stage of neurodegeneration or a heterogeneous condition with multiple etiologies. We found that participants with iRSWA and those with iRBD differed in comorbidities in the years preceding their sleep examination, with participants with RSWA having more neurological disorders prior to index. The iRSWA group included significantly more females and was younger, with a higher proportion below the reported average age of participants with iRBD, where a diagnosis before the age of 50 years is uncommon [1, 30, 31]. They were also less often married/cohabiting. Participants with iRSWA reported higher levels of daytime sleepiness/fatigue despite objectively longer total sleep time, while other objective sleep parameters, including prevalence of sleep apnea and periodic limb movements, were comparable. Medication use profiles were similar between groups, except that participants with iRSWA more often used central stimulants, anticonvulsants, or lithium. No neurodegenerative changes were described in the PSG reports of the iRSWA group, and no neurodegenerative symptoms were recorded at the time of the sleep examination. During follow-up, participants with iRBD had twice the incidence of neurodegenerative disorders compared with participants with iRSWA, despite a shorter average follow-up duration. Taken together, these results suggest that, in clinical practice, iRSWA may include individuals on biological trajectories other than neurodegeneration.

Our data inform the ongoing debate on whether iRSWA is a prodromal phase of synucleinopathy or a heterogeneous phenomenon. On one hand, iRSWA is often regarded as a possible precursor to iRBD and Parkinsonian disorders [4, 23]. Longitudinal studies have shown that a subset of individuals with isolated RSWA eventually develop overt RBD or neurodegenerative disease, albeit at lower rates than those with established iRBD [3, 4]. However, the reported timelines vary widely: one prospective study reported that 26.8 per cent of individuals with iRSWA converted to iRBD over 2.6 years (SD = 2.2) of follow-up, and about 9 per cent developed a neurodegenerative syndrome (mostly PD) within a similar period [4], whereas a small follow-up study of 14 individuals with iRSWA found that after about 8 years, only one individual had progressed to clinical RBD and none to neurodegenerative disease, though over 70 per cent had at least one prodromal neurodegenerative marker [3]. These findings suggest that many individuals with iRSWA harbor underlying brain changes consistent with early neurodegeneration, supporting the view of iRSWA as an early or subclinical stage in the RBD spectrum.

On the other hand, other evidence points to iRSWA as a heterogeneous condition, where not all cases inevitably progress to iRBD or neurodegenerative disease [32]. iRBD typically occurs in older men and is strongly linked to α-synuclein pathology (up to ~90% phenoconvert to PD or a related disorder over 15 years) [1]. Our findings align with reports that iRSWA and iRBD can occur in midlife individuals who do not fit the typical iRBD demographic [16, 19]. One likely explanation is that many iRSWA cases may be identified incidentally during evaluation for other conditions, with REM atonia loss due to factors such as medication, PLMS, or PTSD rather than neurodegeneration [12, 16, 17]. The participants with iRSWA in our study reported higher levels of daytime sleepiness/fatigue, consistent with a recent study in which EDS was a common reason for referral of participants with iRSWA [16]. This may reflect different referral pathways: iRBD is often brought to medical attention by DEBs, whereas iRSWA may be detected through other sleep complaints. Daytime sleepiness and fatigue have inconsistently been associated with iRBD [33–36], but often linked to PD. [37–39] We found a small increase in total sleep time in the iRSWA group, but no other differences in conventional sleep-architecture variables. However, the self-reported sleepiness/fatigue reported by participants with iRSWA may be reflected in objective metrics that were not captured in our study. RSWA was classified according to the binary AASM criteria in place at the time, which, while widely used, reduce a complex physiological spectrum to a binary classification that may rest on a small number of REM epochs. Continuous quantification of RSWA provides more stable and discriminative measures and could have strengthened group comparisons in our study, as RSWA characteristics and REM density may differ across iRSWA, iRBD, and overt neurodegenerative disease [32, 40–47], thereby helping to delineate the physiological boundaries between these entities.

Neurological and psychiatric disorders were more prevalent in the iRSWA group prior to index, with pre-existing neurological disease reaching statistical significance. In our earlier registry study, a combined iRSWA/iRBD cohort showed excess morbidity across multiple disease groups both years leading up to and after diagnosis compared with controls [30]. This suggests potential value in prediagnostic phenotyping. Medication use was broadly similar, though participants with iRSWA were more likely to receive neuroactive medication of anticonvulsants, central stimulants, or lithium—possibly reflecting the difference in symptom profiles and comorbidities. Together, our findings suggest that iRSWA may cluster with a broader spectrum of comorbidities, although the small numbers warrant caution. Testing the hypothesis that RSWA represents an REM-sleep instability that may confer vulnerability to both neurological and affective disorders will require large, longitudinal cohort studies with repeated PSG assessments and comprehensive neurological and psychiatric evaluation.

Neurodegeneration-associated EEG changes (low-amplitude activity, blunted K-complexes/spindles, undifferentiated sleep architecture) were observed only in the iRBD group. No participant with iRSWA had neurodegenerative symptoms at the time of their sleep examination, whereas 4.4 per cent of participants with iRBD did. During follow-up, 4 participants with iRSWA (4.1%) and 18 with iRBD (8.8%) developed a neurodegenerative disorder; this difference was not statistically significant. Follow-up ranged from a few months to 14.5 years, with a median of 6.9 years in iRSWA and 4.5 years in iRBD. The phenoconversion rate in the iRBD group was lower than most published estimates, possibly reflecting earlier referral to specialist care in Denmark and under-ascertainment in registry-based surveillance, whereas the younger age distribution of the iRSWA cohort may have contributed to the lower absolute conversion rate in this group. While the conversion rate in iRSWA was 47 per cent lower than in iRBD despite longer follow-up, it remained above that of the general population, with α-synucleinopathies being the most frequent incident diagnoses. These findings suggest that iRSWA detected in a healthcare-seeking population includes cases on a neurodegenerative trajectory.

In multivariable analysis, being female was significantly associated with iRSWA classification; however, this association was attenuated and became nonsignificant when spouse status was included, suggesting that co-sleeping arrangements partly explain the observed sex difference. Women were more likely to live alone, potentially reducing the detection of DEB and increasing iRSWA classification. Age showed a nonsignificant trend toward younger age in iRSWA but was not an independent predictor. Pre-index neurological disease and sleepiness/fatigue were significant predictors in both models. PLMS of 15/hour or more reached significance only after inclusion of spouse status. This finding should be interpreted with caution: frequent PLMS are common in RBD and iRSWA, but they can also fragment REM sleep and complicate RSWA and DEB assessment, thereby increasing the risk of misclassification between groups [48].

Model fit assessed by the Hosmer–Lemeshow test indicated that the main model had good calibration and discrimination, reliably distinguishing between iRSWA and iRBD. Inclusion of spouse status further improved discrimination (c = 0.753) but reduced calibration, consistent with spouse being an important predictor of group status and a potential source of detection bias. Several associations remained significant after adjustment for spouse status, suggesting that between-group differences are not fully explained by detection bias. The co-occurrence of iRSWA with sleepiness/fatigue and pre-existing neurological disease raises the possibility of alternative underlying mechanisms distinct from the classic iRBD phenotype, potentially involving REM dysregulation secondary to neurological or medication-related factors rather than early neurodegeneration. These findings should be interpreted with caution. The strong association between spouse status and iRSWA reinforces that RBD detection in clinical practice is influenced by observation bias, as people who sleep alone are less likely to be classified as iRBD. This represents a key methodological limitation in iRSWA classification and underscores the importance of documenting participants’ living arrangements in studies based on questionnaires or self-reported reports.

Strengths and limitationsTo our knowledge, this is the largest direct clinical comparison of iRSWA and iRBD. The iRSWA cohort is also one of the largest reported to date, enhancing the reliability of our findings. Through detailed chart review, polysomnography, and linkage to nationwide health and prescription registries, we captured both self-reported complaints and objective sleep metrics at baseline, with up to 7 years of prospective diagnostic follow-up. Because participants with common comorbidities such as sleep apnea and antidepressant use were retained, the sample reflects routine sleep-clinic populations, enhancing external validity.

However, several important limitations should be acknowledged. First, we deliberately retained participants with comorbid sleep apnea or antidepressant use, both of which may influence REM atonia. This decision was made after careful consideration, as both conditions frequently co-occur with RBD and RSWA as comorbidities, rather than functioning as causal factors. In our center, it is standard practice to re-evaluate suspected pseudo-RBD cases after initiation of continuous positive airway pressure treatment, and patients whose symptoms resolved with treatment were excluded. Moreover, antidepressant use was equally common across groups, reducing the likelihood that group differences are attributable to medication effects. Consistently, when entered into the multivariable model, neither antidepressant use nor clinically significant sleep apnea emerged as an independent predictor of group classification.

Second, half of the cohort underwent video-PSG, while the remainder had home-based PSG without video. Although REM sleep behavior was assessed using bedpartner reports when available, a large proportion of participants with iRSWA lived alone. In such cases, DEB assessment relied solely on participant self-report, increasing the likelihood that subtle or unrecognized behaviours were missed and potentially contributing to diagnostic misclassification. Since cohabitation differed by approximately 30 percentage points between groups, the potential for detection bias is substantial and may partly account for observed differences. In our clinic, home-based PSG findings that raise suspicion of DEB generally prompt referral for video-PSG, unless the patient can confirm DEB through self-report. We assessed both living arrangements and whether participants primarily slept alone; however, the substantial proportion of missing responses precluded meaningful group comparisons and instead, we incorporated register-based marital and cohabitation status into our multivariable model. To our knowledge, no previous iRSWA study has systematically set out to document both living and sleeping arrangements. Our findings highlight an important methodological limitation that has not been systematically addressed in prior research and should be considered in future studies.

Third, although neurological history, examination, and technician evaluation of neurodegenerative changes are performed systematically at our center, the documentation of symptoms may vary between clinicians and be influenced by the clinical context. This inherent variability in routine clinical records may lead to underrepresentation of milder or less explicitly documented features.

Fourth, RSWA scoring followed the AASM criteria in place at the time, which rely on assessing 3-second mini-epochs within 30-second REM epochs but do not specify a minimum number of such epochs required for classification. Although we required a minimum REM duration of 20 minutes, the number of qualifying epochs likely varied, raising concerns about the stability of classification in cases with very limited REM sleep. Continuous RSWA metrics are increasingly recognized as more precise but were not feasible to apply retrospectively in this large clinical dataset. Given the lack of established criteria to distinguish physiological from pathological RSWA outside the RBD context, interpretation remains challenging, underscoring the need for a standardized scoring framework and evidence-based thresholds. Interscorer variability over the 15-year period may also have diluted group differences. Although the magnitude of this bias is likely small, given that all scorings were performed by trained technicians, it nonetheless underscores the potential value of automated approaches.

Fifth, conversion from isolated RSWA to overt RBD could not be tracked in the Danish national registries, as both conditions share the same ICD-10 code. Although the ability to distinguish between these entities would have significantly strengthened our study, our findings remain comparable to those of previous reports examining the progression from RSWA to neurodegeneration. However, while the Danish registries are known for their high overall validity, the use of registry data also inherently entails a risk of misclassification or underreporting of diagnoses. Such under-ascertainment is likely modest in magnitude, as severe cases are consistently captured, but some incident neurodegenerative cases may have been missed, thereby attenuating the observed conversion rates.

Sixth, all the participants were referred for sleep evaluation in a tertiary clinic, reflecting a healthcare-seeking population. This introduces potential referral bias and limits generalizability to asymptomatic individuals in the general population. Longitudinal, population-based studies are needed to determine whether similar risks apply outside the sleep clinic.

Taken together, our findings suggest that iRSWA may differ meaningfully from iRBD, but also underscore the diagnostic uncertainty within these phenotypes. To clarify the prognostic significance of iRSWA, both multicentre and population-based longitudinal studies incorporating multiple-night PSG recordings, uniform RSWA analyses, and comprehensive neurological and psychiatric phenotyping will be essential.
