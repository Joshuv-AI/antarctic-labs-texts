OncoPT: long-context transformer models for in hospital tumor phenotype extraction from pathology reports
Authors: Thanh Duong, Dung Le, Vonetta Williams, Sandra Stewart, Yayi Zhao, Muntasir Zitu, Issam El Naqa, Dana Rollison, Thanh Thieu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDespite recent advances in medical informatics, extracting tumor information from pathology reports remains a challenge in modern cancer registry and surveillance workflows. These documents often have an unstructured format, complex medical content, and a considerably lengthy context, creating significant challenges for automated phenotypic information extraction. Although some recent language models such as BERT, GatorTron, and GPT-4 have demonstrated efficacy in medical applications, they are either constrained by sequence length limitations or cloud-based computing that violates the handling of protected health information. We introduce two oncology pathology-optimized transformer models OncoPT, based on Longformer and BigBird architectures and trained on real-world pathology reports. OncoPT efficiently processes reports up to 4,096 tokens, making it suitable for hospitals’ onsite deployment with limited resources. We apply OncoPT to a common malignancy (exemplified by breast cancer) and a rare malignancy (exemplified by gastric cancer), across five key tumor phenotypes: Subsite, Histology, Grade, Stage, and Laterality. The results demonstrate that OncoPT achieves state-of-the-art weighted F-1 on a private pathology dataset and surpasses commercial chatbots (ChatGPT 4o and o1) on the public CORAL dataset (up to 30% improvement). These findings highlight the robustness of OncoPT models with the added benefit of preserving the privacy of patient health information.Subject terms: Cancer, Computational biology and bioinformatics, Oncology

Introduction

Over the past decade, the Health Information Technology for Economic and Clinical Health (HITECH) Act of 20091,2 has catalyzed the integration of electronic medical records (EMRs), enabling large-scale oncology resources such as SEER3, NCDB4, TCGA5, and HTAN6. However, a significant amount of tumor phenotype information remains buried in unstructured clinical free text, which continues to be the primary format for recording clinical narratives, provider assessments, and treatment plans7 (require costly manual abstraction). To address this challenge, natural language processing (NLP) has emerged as a critical tool for extracting structured insights from large volumes of clinical text with several NLP systems have been developed for analyzing pathology reports, including DeepPhe8, CLAMP9, and Amazon Comprehend Medical10. Although these tools show initial promise, their broader applicability in oncology is limited by fundamental constraints: DeepPhe’s rule-based design lacks generalizability, CLAMP’s Conditional Random Field (CRF)11 and dictionary-based methods struggle with contextual nuance, and Amazon Comprehend Medical raises privacy concerns due to cloud-based processing of patient data.

While early NLP systems laid important groundwork for automated pathology abstraction, recent transformer-based models have advanced clinical language understanding but continue to exhibit fundamental limitations. First, encoder-only architectures such as BERT12 and its oncology variants (CancerBERT13 and CancerBERT network14) capture rich contextual representations but remain constrained by limited context windows (512–1024 tokens), which are insufficient for full-length pathology reports that often exceed 2500 tokens. Next, sparse-attention extensions based on BigBird15 (BB-TEN16 and Path-BigBird17) attempt to address this limitation but rely on limited downstream adaptability by framing tumor phenotype extraction as a single sequence-classification task. Furthermore, although these models claim to generalize across multiple cancer types, their actual performance on individual common or rare cancers remains ambiguous (Path-BigBird reports only four subsites: C504, C508, C502, C509, completely ignoring the remaining five subsites of code C50).

Lastly, generative large language models (LLMs) such as GPT-418 and PaLM-219 demonstrate acceptable performance on general medical question-answering tasks but incur high inference costs and raise privacy and security concerns due to reliance on cloud-based deployment, limiting their suitability for processing protected patient data. While smaller open-source generative models (e.g., LLaMA20 or Qwen21) can be deployed in-house, existing evidence suggested that BERT architectures generally outperform generative models on structured natural language understanding tasks, particularly in pathology information abstraction, where bidirectional encoding more effectively captures dense, context-dependent diagnostic cues, whereas decoder-only models are primarily optimized for free-text generation and tend to underperform on fine-grained classification tasks22,23.

Given the critical need for secure and efficient extraction of tumor phenotypic information from pathology reports within hospital perimeter, we introduce a suite of two oncology pathology optimized transformer models (OncoPT) suitable for local deployment while maintaining cutting-edge performance. Our approach leveraged BigBird15 and Longformer24, which incorporate sliding window and global attention mechanisms that extend the maximum context length to 4,096 tokens. OncoPT models are initially pre-trained using a masked language modeling (MLM) objective on MIMIC-III25 clinical text and further pre-trained on over 2 million pathology-related reports to enhance their understanding of the pathology language (Fig. 1a–b). Next, we fine-tune OncoPT on breast cancer, one of the most commonly diagnosed malignancies, before utilizing transfer learning to adapt it for gastric cancer, a rare and detrimental malignancy. The high prevalence of breast cancer places an analytical burden on healthcare systems, highlighting the need for artificial intelligence (AI) solutions to expedite abstraction26. Moreover, by utilizing transfer learning, we were able to adapt knowledge from a common cancer to improve the analytical processing of the rare cancer. We evaluated OncoPT on five key tumor phenotypic information abstraction tasks (single tumor phenotype set for each case), including Subsites, Histology in conjunction with Behavior, Grade, Stage, and Laterality (Fig. 1c). The results demonstrate that OncoPT achieves state-of-the-art accuracy across all metrics on Moffitt’s real-world breast cancer dataset. Furthermore, when evaluated on the public CORAL27 dataset, our small-sized OncoPT outperformed the larger and powerful ChatGPT (4o, o1) models18(on the most three challenging task), highlighting the robustness and efficiency of domain-specialized language modeling.

Results

Common cancer phenotype identification: a case study of breast cancer

Breast cancer is a common malignancy having sufficiently large retrospective data to fine-tune specialized models. We experimented with two fine-tuning approaches to abstract tumor phenotypic information from breast cancer pathology reports: sequence classification (SC) and prompt-based fine-tuning (PT). In the first approach, we incorporated a classification head on top of transformer layers to predict the value of each tumor characteristic. As the pathology report moves through each transformer layer, the model computes self-attended token embeddings that capture contextualized semantic representations. The last few hidden layers are then grouped (Fig. 2a) as input for the classification head, which in turn selects the phenotypic value with the highest SoftMax probability as prediction output. In the second approach, we reformulated the information abstraction task as a masked language modeling problem and fine-tuned the models to decode predefined label words (“yes”/”no”) to indicate which phenotypic value is chosen in the prompt-based template (Fig. 2b). This approach did not add any additional neural layer to the foundation language models, thus requiring less compute and data to fine-tune28,29.

The model fills masked positions with categorical labels using structured prompts. c. Weighted F1 scores on the Moffitt breast cancer dataset for OncoPT variants LongformerSC, BigBirdSC, LongformerPT, BigBirdPT compared with baselines (DeepPhe, BERT, GatorTron, ModernBert). OncoPT achieved the largest relative gain in Subsite classification (0.52–0.56), substantially outperforming DeepPhe (0.02) and BERT (0.39). In Histology prediction, all OncoPT models reached 0.80–0.82, nearly doubling DeepPhe’s 0.44 and surpassing other baselines. For Grade, scores of 0.70–0.71 exceeded DeepPhe (0.48) and matched or improved upon BERT, GatorTron, and ModernBert. Stage prediction performance was consistent across OncoPT variants (0.80–0.81), clearly higher than DeepPhe (0.44) and competitive with strong baselines. Laterality achieved the highest absolute scores (0.91–0.93), showing both a large margin over DeepPhe (0.35) and incremental improvement over high-performing baselines (BERT 0.88, GatorTron 0.89, and ModernBert 0.89). These results demonstrate that the OncoPT fine-tuning strategy is robust across multiple phenotypic extraction tasks, with the largest benefits observed for subtasks requiring fine-grained contextual understanding.

Figure 2c compares OncoPT ‘s performance against established methods, including DeepPhe8, BERT12, GatorTron30, and Clinical ModernBERT31, across five tumor phenotype abstraction tasks: Subsite, Histology, Grade, Stage, and Laterality. Across all tasks, OncoPT models consistently achieved the highest weighted F-1 evaluation scores, the harmonic mean between precision and recall. Supplementary Table 1 reports weighted F-1 scores with 95% confidence intervals, while in Supplementary Table 2 presents paired bootstrap comparisons using OncoPTBigBirdSC as base line to compare with all other models (for all models and tasks, we use number of bootstraps of 100). Overall, OncoPT consistently outperformed both encoder-based models and large generative language models across all five tumor phenotype tasks, achieving higher weighted F-1 scores with tighter confidence intervals. While generative models demonstrated reasonable performance on more explicit tasks such as Laterality, they showed substantial degradation on Subsite and Histology, suggesting that prompt-based reasoning alone is insufficient for reliably aggregating dispersed and implicit pathological evidence. These results highlight the importance of oncology-specific pretraining and long-context encoding over large-scale generative modeling for accurate and stable phenotype abstraction in real-world pathology reports.

Our results also showed that the choice of long-context architecture, whether BigBird15 or Longformer24, did not substantially affect downstream performance, while the choice of fine-tuning method had a more notable impact. Across all tasks, OncoPTBigBird and OncoPTLongformer performed similarly with only slight differences in F-1 scores, while prompt-based fine-tuning (PT) consistently outperformed sequence classification (SC). Specifically, OncoPTBigBirdPT achieved the highest F-1 scores in Subsite (0.56), Grade (0.71), and Stage (0.81), while OncoPTLongformerPT exceled in Histology (0.82) and Laterality (0.93). Although PT method achieved the highest performance, SC fostered a more stable accuracy across different architectures, with OncoPTBigBirdSC and OncoPTLongformerSC exhibiting comparable performance. Prompt-based fine-tuning is a more effective domain-adaptation technique because it frames downstream tasks in a fashion similar to the foundation model’s masked-language pre-training objective 28,29. In complex tasks such as Subsite, Grade, and Stage abstraction, the strength of PT was amplified by BigBird’s emphasis on capturing long-range dependencies through mixed global-random attention. Conversely, in simpler tasks like Histology and Laterality identification, Longformer’s non-prefix global attention manifested better performance. We refer readers to Supplementary Table 3-10 for more details regarding model performance.

Lastly, in the Subsite identification task, OncoPT demonstrated the most substantial improvement, with OncoPTBigBirdSC achieving an F-1 score of 0.558, 95% CI: [0.541, 0.578], representing a 50% gain over DeepPhe8 and more than 15% over all other baseline models. While compared with all generative models (HuatuoGPT-o132, LLaMA-3.1,20, Woollie-medicine33, and Qwen-321), OncoPTBigBirdSC scored more than double in weighted F1 on the Subsite task (0.558 vs. ≤0.258), with all performance gaps being large and statistically significant (ΔF1 ≈ 0.33 to 0.40, p < 0.001), highlighting the clear advantage of a long-context encoder model for fine-grained clinical phenotype abstraction. This improvement is remarkable given Subsite information in pathology reports is often hidden amidst multiple tissue samples originating from the same or distinct anatomical regions34. The primary tumor location is typically embedded in dense narrative text, where the primary Subsite mentioned is often implicit, ambiguous, or intertwined with surgical, radiological, or procedural details35. Despite OncoPT achieving the best overall performance, the absolute weighted F1 score of 0.558 on the Subsite task remains modest, reflecting the intrinsic difficulty of subsite extraction even in a common cancer such as breast cancer, where several subsites are rare (C501, C503, C505) or extremely rare (C500, C506). Performance on these low-frequency categories is unstable and disproportionately influenced by dominant subsites (C504, C508), leading to fluctuations and class imbalance bias. We will examine this limitation in detail and outline targeted future directions to improve rare-subsite modeling in the “Discussion” section. For all confusion matrix, refer to Supplementary Data Figs. 1–4.

Rare cancer phenotype identification: a case study of gastric cancer

After validating OncoPT on a common cancer with sufficient training data, we next extend it to a rare cancer setting to evaluate how well it adapts to a substantially different data distribution and limited-sample regime. We employed parameter-efficient transfer learning to extend the capabilities of our models to gastric cancer, a rare malignancy with limited retrospective data. We experimented with three progressively more sophisticated approaches to facilitate the transition from a common cancer to a much less common one. We began with zero-shot inference to leverage the models previously trained on breast cancer to predict characteristics of gastric cancer without any additional fine-tuning. Second, we employed soft-prompt tuning, which fine-tunes a low-dimensional prompt embedding vector to facilitate domain adaptation while preserving the foundation model’s pre-trained parameters. Lastly, we implemented prompt-based fine-tuning that reformulates a masked language modeling problem into a low-dimensional token classification representing gastric tumor’s phenotypes.

Figure 3a presents evaluation of OncoPT models across four abstraction tasks: Subsite, Histology, Grade, and Stage. Laterality is irrelevant to gastric cancer. Without additional fine-tuning, zero-shot inference produced baseline results, struggling across all tasks with weighted F-1 scores at 0.03 and 0.18 for Subsite, 0.29 and 0.21 for Histology. In contrast, prompt-based fine-tuning demonstrated superior performance, with OncoPTLongformerPT achieved the highest F-1 scores of 0.72 in Histology, and OncoPTBigBirdPT achieved F-1 scores of 0.55 for both Subsite and Grade abstraction. This performance represented a 10% improvement compared to the next best method (soft-prompt tuning), highlighting the effectiveness of prompt-based optimization in transferring knowledge from a common cancer to a rare cancer. These results also aligned with respective model performance on breast cancer, where OncoPTLongformer demonstrated better performance in simpler tasks, while OncoPTBigBird exceled in more complex tasks, including Subsite and Grade classification. However, the performance on the Subsite and Grade tasks still showed some limitations, suggesting an extended investigation into each label class to understand the causes (Fig. 3b). We refer readers to Supplementary Table 11–13 for more detailed reporting of model performance.

These experiments demonstrate that, even when transferred from a common to a rare cancer setting, OncoPT maintains promising performance, suggesting a strong capacity for adaptation under limited-data conditions. Although absolute performance decreases due to substantial class distribution shifts and limited sample sizes, the results indicate that the learned representations retain pan-cancer pathological signals (textual and semantic patterns in pathology reports that are shared across cancer types) that generalize beyond the original training domain. Importantly, this behavior suggests that the proposed transfer learning is not restricted to a single cancer type but can be extended to other rare cancers with minimal architectural changes. Together, these findings highlight the potential of our approach as a scalable and practical solution for tumor phenotype abstraction across a broad spectrum of rare malignancies.

Performance against ChatGPT

The rapid emergence of large language models such as ChatGPT18 has greatly influenced medical informatics, warranting stricter evaluation of their performance in high-stakes domains. While these models have reported impressive capabilities in medical language understanding benchmarks36, their actual effectiveness in real-world medical tasks remains unverified. Because protected health information precludes the use of Moffitt’s private dataset on cloud-based LLMs such as ChatGPT, we resort to using CORAL27, an expert-manually curated dataset of 20 fully deidentified breast cancer pathology reports from the University of California, San Francisco (UCSF), to compare the performance of our OncoPT models against ChatGPT. This dataset facilitates a public evaluation bench for assessing LLMs on oncology-specialized problems. We collaborated with a certified tumor registrar (CTR) to abstract tumor phenotypes in each CORAL report. The CTR applied ICD-O-337 standardization for tumor Subsite and Histology while systematically organizing Grade, Stage, and Laterality according to recognized clinical terminology frameworks.

We selected OncoPTBigBirdSC, our best model overall, to perform the evaluation against ChatGPT(4o, o1)18 and used a customized prompt to abstract tumor phenotypic information (Fig. 4a). Figure 4b shows our model consistently outperformed ChatGPT across multiple tasks (Subsite, Histology, Stage). Remarkably, in the most challenging task (Subsite identification), OncoPTBigBirdSC achieved the highest F1-score of 0.51, substantially exceeding GPT-4o (0.07) and GPT-o1 (0.21). This demonstrated the robustness of specialized models in processing and extracting meaningful insights from complex medical text, surpassing general-purpose models. In contrast, for the Grade identification task, where the information is often explicitly stated in surface cues, our model achieved the same performance as GPT-4o (0.67) but was outperformed by GPT-o1 (0.94). This divergence highlights our models’ exclusive strengths in complex context understanding, while simpler surface-cue information can be better analyzed by large reasoning models. Other probable advantages for commercial LLMs like ChatGPT is that it possibly has already seen the dataset during its training, or clinical notes in the CORAL27 dataset could be chosen to maximize its original named entity recognition objective, thus potentially biasing the notes to consists of explicitly stated and easily discernible information38.

To assess the effectiveness of our methodology on abstracting surface-cue information, we trained OncoPTBigBirdSC-surface on a subset of simplified pathology reports that contain explicitly stated and easily discernible tumor phenotype information (including Subsite, Histology, and Grade, while excluding Stage and Laterality). To construct this subset, we leveraged the gold-standard labels and selected only those reports where the corresponding phenotypic terms (e.g., “adenocarcinoma” for Histology or “grade 2” for Grade) appeared as exact matches in the text. Tumor stage was excluded due to the high variation in ways to mention stage in pathology reports, making keyword matching infeasible. For instance, stage may be written as “Stage IIb”, “pT3N1M0”, or “advanced stage disease”, among many other notations. In contrast, tumor laterality was easy to identify while also consistently documented in pathology reports, enabling all models to already achieve perfect accuracy on CORAL27. We performed sample selection through exact label matching with training sample sizes shown in Fig. 4c. OncoPTBigBirdSC-surface demonstrated superior performance on CORAL, achieving F-1 scores of 0.55 and 0.78 on the Subsite and Histology tasks, respectively, compared to 0.51 and 0.74 of the OncoPTBigBirdSC general model. Furthermore, when benchmarked against ChatGPT (4o, o1)18, it exhibited a notable advantage, particularly in the Subsite task, where it significantly exceeded the performance of GPT-4o (0.07) and GPT-o1 (0.21). Additionally, OncoPTBigBirdSC-surface matched GPT-o1’s performance in identifying tumor grade (0.94), highlighting its effectiveness in abstracting surface-cue tumor phenotype information. However, when benchmarking on the full internal dataset, which reflects a more complex and realistic context, OncoPTBigBirdSC-surface showed significant trade-offs. Its performance declined across all tasks, with the Subsite dropping from 0.56 to 0.44, Histology from 0.81 to 0.71, and Grade from 0.70 to 0.39. These results highlight the limitations of training models solely on surface-cue information, which may offer advantages for identifying explicitly stated tumor phenotype information but can significantly hinder a model’s robustness and generalizability in real-world scenarios.

Evaluation of bias on breast cancer

We accessed OncoPT’s potential biases by analyzing its performance across multiple configurations and examining the characteristics of the breast cancer patient cohort. To examine fairness, we stratified performance by race, ethnicity, and age. Figure 5a presents the racial and ethnic composition of the dataset, indicating that “White” (n = 2308) and “Non-Spanish” (n = 2415) collectively represent approximately 90% of the cohort. Age distribution also reflected these imbalances, with patients under 30 and over 85 being aggregated into broader groups due to their small sample sizes, while the remaining are segmented into 5-year intervals to facilitate trend analysis. The largest age groups were 65–69 (n = 413) and 70–74 (n = 397), while those younger than 30 (n = 23) and older than 85 (n = 39) constituted a small fraction of the dataset. To simplify the comparison, we selected OncoPTBigBirdSC and weighted F-1 score for this analysis.

To improve interpretability and comparability, race and ethnicity were aggregated into broader categories (“White” vs. “Non-White” and “Spanish” vs. “Non-Spanish”). This aggregation was necessitated by pronounced sample size imbalance across individual demographic subgroups, many of which comprised fewer than 20 cases, rendering subgroup-level performance estimates statistically unstable. Aggregation was therefore used to mitigate the risk of spurious or misleading conclusions that can arise from evaluating model performance on extremely small subgroups. Under this analysis, OncoPTBigBirdSC model performance was comparable across race and ethnicity, with similar F-1 scores for ethnicity (Subsite: 0.56 vs. 0.57; Laterality: 0.92 vs. 0.94) and race (Subsite: 0.56 for both groups; Laterality: 0.92 vs. 0.95). These results indicate no detectable performance disparities at the aggregated level, though finer-grained equity assessments will require substantially larger and more balanced subgroup samples.

Lastly, across all age groups ranging from 30 to 85, the model maintains stable and reliable performance despite the differences in sample sizes. For example, in the Subsite classification, F-1 scores show minimal fluctuation, ranging from 0.50 (ages 45–49) to 0.63 (ages 80–84), indicating consistent accuracy across groups. Moreover, the performance in the Laterality task is particularly robust, with scores consistently exceeding 0.90. However, reduced performance is observed in the least represented age groups (under 30 and over 85), especially in more challenging tasks such as Subsite and Stage classification. Notably, patients under 30 exhibit the lowest Subsite F-1 score (0.48), while those over 85 show a decline in Stage prediction performance (F-1 score 0.63). These drops are consistent with instability in low-sample regimes, where limited training and evaluation examples increase variance and reduce the reliability of fine-grained predictions. Clinically, these age groups are less frequently represented in routine pathology workflows and often have more heterogeneous or incomplete documentation, which further weakens the textual signals required for accurate classification39,40. Together, these factors explain the observed degradation without indicating a systematic age-related bias in the model.

Error analysis

Figure 6 summarizes model’s performance across three categories: (i) surgery only vs surgery combined with biopsy reports, (ii) single vs multiple primary tumors, and (iii) metastatic status. First, having biopsy corpus added to surgical reports yields consistent improvement, especially on the two most challenging tasks (Subsite and Grade), indicating the significant benefit of richer, biopsy-inclusive data. However, in distinguishing single vs multiple primary tumors, the model’s performance declined notably in multiple-primary cases, primarily due to architectural constraints that limit predictions to only phenotypes of one tumor per report. In multiple-primary cases, reports frequently describe more than one tissue or tumor site, increasing textual complexity and leading to misclassification when distinct tumors are conflated into the most likely prediction. Lastly, for metastatic status, the model performed well overall, particularly for non-metastasized tumors, but experienced a substantial drop in performance for metastasized cases, with the Subsite F-1 score declining sharply from 0.59 to 0.30 and the Stage task falling from a peak of 0.85 to 0. This degradation is partly driven by the extreme class imbalance of metastatic cases (<1% of the dataset) and further exacerbated by the increased linguistic complexity of metastatic reports, which often reference multiple tissues and sites within a single document.

Next, working with certified cancer registrars, we systematically reviewed more than 100 misclassified cases across all tasks to identify recurrent error patterns and their primary causes. Among these tasks, Subsite classification is the most problematic, with performance score significantly lower than other categories (F-1 score of approximately 50% compared to others exceeding 70%). The complexity of Subsite task reflects ambiguity in clinical documentation, where tumor location is often described using varying terminologies and anatomical references41. A significant concern is the model’s predictive bias, which frequently misclassifies tumor location into the C504 and C508 categories (the two most prevalent classes in the dataset), diminishing overall classification accuracy. Moreover, a recurring error pattern is observed in tumor Laterality and positional reporting, revealing the model’s difficulty in differentiating between inner and outer Subsite classifications. This issue particularly appears when tumor locations vary between the left and right breast, as a tumor located at the 10:00 position on the right breast is often misclassified as outer, while the equivalent position on the left breast is categorized as inner. Discussion

While existing NLP methods show significant potential for extracting cancer phenotypes from pathology reports, they struggle to handle extensive and unstructured reports within the bounds of privacy regulations, limiting their current viability in real-world clinical settings. Some recent models, such as BB-TEN16 and Path-BigBird17, have shown some promising results; their reliance on biased validation and limited downstream approach raises concerns about their actual generalizability and effectiveness. Although both models claim to generalize across multiple cancer types, their actual performance on individual cancers remains ambiguous, with limited analysis of any cancer type. In contrast, our work focuses on a specific cancer type, first adapting to a common cancer (breast) before applying transfer learning to a rare cancer (gastric). By integrating long-dependency transformer models with multiple downstream approaches, our solution effectively addresses the key limitations of prior methods, making AI-driven pathology report analysis more practical and clinically relevant.

In this study, we demonstrate that foundation models trained on large-scale clinical and oncology text can effectively support diverse cancer phenotype abstraction tasks, while also revealing important trade-offs between specialization and real-world robustness. The strong performance of OncoPT on breast cancer phenotypes underscores the value of architectures that combine local and global contextual modeling with efficient fine-tuning, particularly for clinically challenging tasks such as Subsite extraction. Additionally, a comparison between our model and ChatGPT18 on a standardized dataset such as CORAL27 demonstrated the advantage of a domain-specific model over general large language models. Although ChatGPT outperformed only on the Grade classification task, this raised the question of whether ChatGPT’s performance reflects genuine pathological understanding or overfitting the curated language of the CORAL dataset. OncoPTBigBirdSC-surface addressed this concern by demonstrating superior performance across all tasks on the CORAL dataset, yet its performance declines on our internal dataset, highlighting the trade-offs when applied to the more complex and realistic pathology corpus. These findings highlighted the need to balance specialization and generalizability, with training on a curated dataset improving performance, but can reduce robustness on real-world data.

Our experiment on transferring knowledge from a common cancer (breast) to a rare cancer (gastric) represented a significant advancement in applying machine learning, illustrating how models trained on abundant common cancers can meaningfully improve performance in low-resource settings. As a rare and highly aggressive malignancy, gastric cancer remained challenging to study due to the limited data, which had obstructed the development of effective models42. Leveraging insights from breast cancer to improve model performance in gastric cancer constituted a substantial step forward, offering new opportunities for the application of machine learning in rare cancer analysis. To explore this potential, we employed three distinct transfer learning techniques: zero-shot learning, soft prompt tuning, and prompt-based tuning, each designed to assess the model’s adaptability in a data-limited setting. Our findings demonstrated promising performance, particularly with prompt-based tuning, which achieved strong results in key classification tasks despite the constraints imposed by data scarcity. This experiment represented a critical first step in assessing the potential of language models to address real-world challenges and establishing a foundation for future advancements in the study of rare cancers.

However, this study presents several notable limitations that require careful consideration in future research and practical applications. One key challenge was the significant class imbalance present in downstream tasks, particularly in subsite and tumor grade, where underrepresented classes may hinder model performance and lead to biased outcomes. Although OncoPT showed the highest performance in breast cancer, its ability to identify rare (C501, C503, C505) or extremely rare (C500, C506) codes remained limited, often biasing predictions toward more common classes such as C504 and C508. This limitation arises from extreme class imbalance, where rare subsites constitute less than 10% of the data and very rare less than 1%, leading the optimization process to favor dominant classes. Consequently, ambiguous signals associated with rare subsites are overwhelmed by frequent patterns, biasing predictions toward C504 and C508. To address this issue, in future research, we will be focusing on integrating data augmentation techniques, generating synthetic samples, and implementing hierarchical classification methods to improve rare-class detection and mitigate prediction bias.

Additionally, multiple-primary and metastatic cases remain challenging primarily due to the increased linguistic and clinical complexity of the underlying pathology reports. In multiple-primary tumors, a single report often describes several tissues, subsites, or distinct tumor entities, while our model is constrained to predict only one set of tumor phenotypes per report, leading to inevitable conflation and misclassification. Metastatic cases also have this issue, as reports frequently mention primary and secondary disease sites, historical context, and treatment-related findings, making phenotypic signals harder to be found. These cases are also extremely rare in the dataset (<1%), limiting the model’s ability to learn robust representations under severe class imbalance. In future research, these limitations can be addressed through a prepossessing model that first detects multiple primary tumors and metastatic disease, combined with rarity-aware data augmentation and LLM-generated synthetic samples to improve robustness in complex and underrepresented cases.

Another important limitation stems from the dataset’s institutional homogeneity, as all reports were sourced from a single cancer center, which may reduce generalizability across diverse healthcare settings. Although the study leverages over two million oncology reports, reliance on a single institution may limit external validity due to center-specific conventions in report formatting, terminology, and documentation practices. Such institutional styles can introduce systematic biases in how phenotypic information is expressed, potentially affecting downstream model performance when applied to reports from other healthcare systems, particularly for tasks that depend on fine-grained textual cues. It requires multi-institutional collaborations that capture variability in reporting styles and clinical practices, enabling more robust evaluation and improving the generalizability of pathology-focused models across healthcare systems. Addressing these limitations will be crucial for advancing the development of robust and generalizable AI-driven pathology analysis systems, ultimately contributing to more equitable, reliable, and clinically impactful methods in oncology.

Methods

Dataset curation

To train our OncoPT foundation models, we used two datasets of medical reports: the publicly available MIMIC-III25 dataset and a collection of oncology reports from Moffitt Cancer Center. The MIMIC-III dataset contains over 100,000 de-identified reports from more than 40,000 patients admitted to critical care units at Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center between 2001 and 2012. The oncology report dataset, sourced from Moffitt’s cancer registry, consists of 2,070,241 reports covering cases from 1980 to 2023. The curation of Moffitt dataset began with an original collection of over 8 million reports gathered over a span of 40 years and using FuzzyWuzzy library43, which applies Levenshtein distance for report name selection. The original dataset included a diverse range of report types, and many of them did not contain relevant pathology information that could mislead model training and negatively impact downstream performance. We collaborated with certified cancer registrars to curate a list of oncology-relevant pathology report types. Because report naming conventions changed over time, exact string matching was infeasible. Instead, we applied fuzzy string matching (FuzzyWuzzy) with a 50% similarity threshold to capture naming variations while excluding unrelated documents, reducing the dataset from over 8 million to approximately 2 million high-quality oncology reports. Report names are detailed in Supplementary Table 14.

For downstream tasks on breast and gastric cancer, we used datasets derived from the Cancer Registry, which include gold-standard annotations for key cancer characteristics (Subsite, Histology, Stage, Grade, and Laterality) along with additional patient and tumor attributes such as demographics, diagnosis timelines, metastatic status, and prior cancer history. Pathology reports were extracted from two systems, PathNet (2008–2022) and PowerPath (2022–2023), focusing on surgical, biopsy, and cytology reports most likely to contain relevant cancer characteristics. We linked text reports to Cancer Registry data by filtering based on definitive surgery dates, ensuring alignment between surgical and biopsy procedures and cancer diagnoses. Errors in definitive surgery dates were corrected by reviewing procedure descriptions and adjusting to the most relevant prior surgery date when necessary. In total, the dataset included 12,493 adult women diagnosed with breast cancer and 714 adult patients diagnosed with gastric cancer between 2009 and June 2023. Finally, stratified random sampling was employed to create an 80% training and 20% testing dataset for breast cancer, ensuring balanced representation across multiple clinical and procedural factors (Supplementary Table 15), while almost all stomach cancer cases are designated for testing.

This study was conducted under Moffitt Cancer Center protocol MCC# 22624. In accordance with the U.S. Department of Health and Human Services regulations (45 CFR 46.104(d)(4)), the Institutional Review Board (IRB) determined that the project qualifies as exempt from IRB oversight. The Advarra IRB granted a Full Waiver of HIPAA Authorization under 45 CFR 164.512(i)(2), and a waiver of informed consent was not required because the study involved retrospective chart review of data previously collected for clinical care, including records from patients who were deceased or lost to follow-up.

Foundation model training

We trained two OncoPT foundation models utilizing the BigBird15 and Longformer24 architectures, which were designed to efficiently process long input sequences. Both models had identical architectural configurations, including 12 transformer layers, 12 attention heads, a 768-dimensional embedding space, and a maximum context length of 4096 tokens. Their hybrid attention mechanism strategically combines sliding window and global attention to address computational challenges while maintaining effective information capture capabilities across extensive reports. The sliding window attention mechanism offers an efficient approach to reducing computational complexity in the network by constraining token interactions in a local neighborhood. By limiting each token’s focus to its immediate contextual neighbors, the mechanism transforms the traditionally quadratic computational complexity from O(n2) to a linear, efficient O(nw), where n represents the sequence length, and w denotes the window size. In our implementation, we set the window size to w = 3, allowing each token in the sequence X = [x1, x2, …, xn] to focus attention solely on its adjacent tokens xi-1, xi and xi+1. However, sliding window attention restricts information processing to localized regions, hindering the model’s ability to capture long-range dependencies across the entire report. To solve it, both architectures incorporate global attention, allowing selected tokens to attend to all positions in the sequence and enabling a more holistic understanding of context. For both OncoPT models, we selected the special [CLS] token as the global attention token, leveraging it to capture comprehensive representational information and enhance performance in downstream tasks. Additionally, in the OncoPTBigBird model, we incorporated three random attention blocks, following the implementation of BigBird15. For each OncoPT model, we first trained for three epochs on the MIMIC-III dataset, followed by an additional three epochs on our internal oncology report dataset. Training was conducted using four NVIDIA A100 40-GB GPUs with a local batch size of 2 per GPU, the AdamW optimizer, a learning rate of 5 × 10−5, and a fixed random seed of 42.

We first approached cancer phenotype abstraction task as a sequence classification task by adding a classification head to the pretrained OncoPT models and fine-tuning them to predict the report label. The multi-layered architecture of Transformers enables each layer to capture distinct levels of representation, forming a rich hierarchy of linguistic information, with deeper layers progressively focusing on more semantic features. Prompt based fine-tuning

Prompt-based fine-tuning is an optimization technique that reformulates traditional classification tasks into a masked language modeling problem, enabling language models to leverage their pre-trained knowledge more effectively. Unlike standard fine-tuning, which requires adding and training task-specific layers, prompt-based fine-tuning directly maps model outputs to classification labels through predefined label words. By reusing the model’s inherent understanding of language, prompt-based fine-tuning enhances both sample efficiency and generalization, often outperforming conventional fine-tuning in low-resource settings28,29.

To evaluate its effectiveness, we trained OncoPT models to classify five distinct cancer phenotypes using a breast cancer dataset with a larger number of samples. The trained models were then applied to a significantly smaller gastric cancer dataset and demonstrated the adaptability of prompt-based techniques in cross-domain transfer learning. Soft-prompt tuning

Soft prompt tuning is an efficient adaptation technique designed for scarce-resource scenarios, minimizing the number of learnable parameters while leveraging the full expressive power of large language models (LLMs). Unlike prompt-based fine-tuning, which relies on textual tokens, soft prompt tuning replaces these tokens with compact, learnable vectors that is optimized through backpropagation while keeping the pre-trained parameters of the foundation LLM frozen. This approach significantly reduces computational costs and memory requirements, making it particularly advantageous for medical NLP tasks with limited annotated data. By fine-tuning only a small subset of task-specific parameters, soft prompt tuning maintains the model’s generalization capabilities while improving adaptation efficiency across different datasets.
