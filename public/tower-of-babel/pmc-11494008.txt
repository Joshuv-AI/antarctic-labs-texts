Torque teno virus: a potential marker of immune reconstitution in youths with vertically acquired HIV
Authors: Laura Tarancon-Diez, Itziar Carrasco, Laura Montes, Iker Falces-Romero, Elena Vazquez-Alejo, Santiago Jiménez de Ory, Marta Dapena, Jose Antonio Iribarren, Cristina Díez, Luis Ramos-Ruperto, Elena Colino, Cristina Calvo, Mª Ángeles Muñoz-Fernandez, María Luisa Navarro, Talía Sainz
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Torque teno virus (TTV) viral load (VL), a component of the human virome, increases during immune suppression or dysregulation. This study aimed to explore TTV VL in youths living with vertically acquired HIV (YWVH) and its potential as an immunovirological marker. We performed an observational, retrospective study involving YWVH under antiretroviral treatment (ART) from the Spanish Cohort of HIV-infected children, adolescents, and vertically HIV-infected patients transferred to Adult Units (CoRISpe-FARO), compared to HIV-negative healthy donors (HD). Plasma TTV VL was assessed by qPCR. T-cell phenotype was analysed on cryopreserved peripheral blood mononuclear cells by flow cytometry. Correlations with baseline CD4 and CD8 and long-term virological evolution were examined. A total of 57 YWVH were compared with 23 HD. YWVH had a median CD4 T-cells of 736 cells/mm3 [IQR: 574–906], a median of 17 years [IQR: 14–20.5] since ART initiation, and 65 months [IQR: 39–116] under HIV-RNA virological control. TTV VL was higher among YWVH and in males compared with females (p < 0.05). Among YWVH, TTV VL correlated with CD4 and CD8 counts and the CD4/CD8 ratio (p = 0.002; r = − 0.39, p = 0.037; r = 0.277, p = 0.005; r = − 0.37 respectively). TTV VL correlated with activation expression markers (HLA-DR+/CD38+) on CD4 (p = 0.007, r = 0.39) and the soluble proinflammatory cytokine IL-6 (p = 0.006, r = 0.38).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-73870-2.

Introduction

Torque Teno Virus (TTV) is a small, non-enveloped, single-stranded DNA-virus, representing the most abundant component of the human virome. TTV prevalence worldwide is extremely high, peaking at over 95% according to metagenomic sequencing studies1. Although the TTV life cycle and pathogenic potential are still poorly understood, no evidence has linked TTV to any human disease1,2. However, an increase in TTV viral load has been observed during immune suppression or dysregulation. Studies on TTV in the context of sepsis, cancer, transplantation, or HIV infection suggest that TTV levels inversely correlate with immune competence3–6. Based on the hypothesis that T-cells control TTV replication7, measuring TTV viremia has been proposed as a marker of immune reconstitution in immunosuppressed individuals8,9. In solid organ transplant recipients, measuring TTV viral load might help optimize immunosuppressive drugs, reducing infection and rejection risks10.

In the context of HIV infection, early studies before the arrival of antiretroviral treatment (ART) described higher rates of TTV in people living with HIV11,12, showing an inverse relationship between TTV viral load and CD4 T-cell counts, and higher rates of progression to AIDS in individuals with higher TTV viral loads13. During treated HIV infection, data are contradictory concerning the association between TTV viral load and immune status or clinical outcomes. While some studies reported no relationship between TTV levels and immune status3,14, others reported strong inverse correlations between TTV levels in blood and CD4 T-cell counts, as well as decreased survival rates in patients with high TTV viral loads12,13,15,16. A recent study describes an association between TTV viral load in saliva and CD4 T-cell reconstitution15. None of these studies included people with perinatally acquired HIV, so the potential role of TTV as a marker of immune reconstitution in vertical transmission remains unknown. Youths living with vertically acquired HIV (YWVH) are a unique population exposed to HIV and ART since childhood. Immunological abnormalities despite ART have been described in this population, related to both the innate and adaptive immune systems17,18, as well as chronic inflammation17,19–21. These phenomena are thought to underline non-AIDS related morbidity and mortality22. Validating biomarkers for routine clinical practice is key to optimizing management of this population and improving the prognosis of high-risk patients.

Objectives

The objective of this study was to quantify plasma TTV viral load in YWVH compared to non-HIV controls and to analyse its potential role as an immunological marker by examining associations between TTV viral load and immunity.

Results

Characteristics of studied subjects

Epidemiological, analytical, and clinical characteristics of the studied group (YWVH, n = 57) at the time of sampling and of the group of HIV-negative donors (healthy donors, HD, n = 23) are shown in Table 1. YWVH were younger than HD (20 [IQR: 17–24] vs. 26 [IQR: 24–27] years, p < 0.001) and there were differences in %CD4 T-cells and CD4/CD8 ratio among groups. YWVH had a median of 17 years [14–20.5] since ART initiation, 65 months [IQR: 39.5–116] of HIV-RNA virological suppression, and a good immunological situation with a median CD4 T-cell count of 736 cells/mm3 [IQR: 574–906]. Higher TTV viral load and immunological situation

The prevalence of TTV was higher among YWVH compared to HD (100% (57/57) versus 78.3% (18/23)). TTV viral load was significantly increased in YWVH compared to HD (Fig. 1A) and among males (Fig. 1B). TTV viral load did not correlate with age, either in the overall analysis or when stratified by HIV status (data not shown). Interestingly, TTV viral load inversely correlated with the %CD4 T-cells and CD4/CD8 ratio and directly with %CD8 T-cells among YWVH, while no significant correlations were observed in HD (Fig. 1C). Regarding HIV-related variables, no significant correlations were found between TTV load and CD4 nadir or time since ART initiation (Fig. 2A,B), but there was a trend towards an inverse correlation between TTV load time under viral HIV suppression (Fig. 2C).

Data regarding soluble IL-6 and T-cell immunophenotyping were available in all HD and in a subgroup of YWVH. Soluble IL-6 levels were increased in YWVH compared to HD (p = 0.01) (Supplementary Table 1) and, including all participants, a direct correlation was found between TTV load and soluble IL-6 (Fig. 2D). Regarding the analysis of T-cell immunophenotyping, YWVH showed higher levels of activation, senescence, and exhaustion in both CD4 and CD8 T-cell subsets (Supplementary Table 2), as we have previously described in the cohort17. Co-expression of activation markers HLA-DR/CD38 in CD4 T-cells (Fig. 2E), the exhaustion marker TIM-3 in total (Fig. 2F) and CD8 T-cell memory subsets (r = 0.32 and p = 0.023; r = 0.35 and p = 0.01 for CM and EM subsets respectively), PD-1 in EM CD4 T-cells (Fig. 2G) and the senescence marker CD57 in EM CD8 T-cell (Fig. 2H) directly correlated with TTV load. After segregating by groups, only the correlation with soluble IL-6 remained statistically significant in YWVH (r = 0.38 and p = 0.04) (Supplementary Fig. 1).

TTV viral load and longitudinal evolution in YWVH

Data on long-term evolution were available for a median of 57 months [IQR: 41–77] for YWVH. Five patients (9%) experienced viral failure (VF) during the follow-up. When compared to the remaining participants, the baseline levels of TTV viral load were significantly higher in these patients (Fig. 3A). No significant correlation was observed between TTV viral load and time until VF (r = − 0.371 and p = 0.468). Eighteen patients (31.5%) developed blips during the follow-up, but no differences were found in baseline TTV load when compared to those YWVH that maintained HIV viral suppression. Among patients who experienced blips, baseline TTV load inversely correlated with the time until the first blip appearance (Fig. 3B-C), however, it was not statistically significant. There was no correlation with the blip magnitude (data not shown).

To evaluate the potential of TTV as predictor for immune recovery in YWVH, TTV levels were correlated to the CD4 T-cell count and CD4/CD8 ratio gain for each year of follow-up, and no significant correlation was observed between baseline TTV levels and changes in the immune variables (data not shown).

Discussion

In our series, we found significantly higher TTV load in YWVH compared to HD. TTV viral load inversely correlated with CD4 T-cells and the CD4/CD8 ratio, and directly with the proinflammatory cytokine IL-6. Correlations with activation, senescence, and exhaustion markers were mild, but the data suggest TTV may be used as a potential marker of immune reconstitution in this population.

The high prevalence of TTV observed in YWVH confirms previous findings in people living with HIV (PLWH)14,23. In our study, the median TTV load in YWVH (3.19 log10 [2.66–4.08]) was relatively lower compared to previous studies in adults living with HIV. This contrasts with the hypothesis that persistent viral infection is significantly related to higher TTV load, especially at a younger age12,16. The inclusion of patients on stable ART and suppressed HIV-RNA for at least 2 years may explain these differences.

Although host immune control of TTV replication is thought to decrease with age24, we found no associations between TTV viremia and age in this study. The narrow age range of participants (adolescents and youths but not children) may partially explain this. Previous studies in children suggest that TTV is acquired during early childhood25, and therefore no differences should be expected after this period. In our study, TTV load was higher in males. Although not many studies on TTV viremia focused on sex, this finding agrees with previous observations in non-HIV infected people, describing sex-specific host immune control of TTV replication26. Women are known to mount stronger immune responses, displaying a more effective adaptive immune response to viral pathogens27,28. The effect of steroid hormones on the immune system29 may also explain the decrease in host immune control of TTV replication. Estrogen increases IFN-γ cytokine promoter activity, implicated in cell-mediated responses to viral pathogens. In other viral scenarios, some authors suggest that the over-expression of toll-like receptor 9 (TLR9) in women, due to its location on chromosome X, might lead to a differential antiviral signalling and responsiveness30. In the context of HIV infection, it is well established that women living with HIV present preserved CD4 T-cell and a greater CD4/CD8 ratio recovery after ART initiation31,32. The inverse correlation between those variables and TTV levels aligns with lower TTV load in women.

Inverse correlations were found between TTV load, CD4 T-cells, and the CD4/CD8 ratio in YWVH. These findings align with previous observations in non-vertically acquired HIV infection15,16 and support the assumption that T-cells are the major site of TTV replication7. A persistently low CD4/CD8 ratio is associated with sustained immune activation in children and adults living with HIV17,19,33, as well as adverse clinical outcomes, including an increased risk of non-AIDS events and mortality in adults18,34. The strong association between TTV load and the CD4/CD8 ratio provides new insights into the potential utility of TTV as a marker of immunosuppression. Interestingly, we also found mild correlations between TTV viremia and activation, senescence, and exhaustion markers in T-cells. However, these differences did not remain significant after stratifying by HIV status, likely due to the reduced sample size. Unfortunately, the effect of CMV status, a well-known determinant of T-cell homeostasis in treated HIV infection, could not be analysed in this study, and therefore its potential effect on TTV remains to be elucidated. Previous studies on TTV load in PLWH with non-vertically acquired HIV have indicated higher TTV loads associated to an exhausted phenotype in B-cells3. In hematopoietic stem cell transplant recipients, an increase in TTV load directly correlated with the percentage of dysfunctional and senescent CD57+ CD8 T-cells in blood35. Thus, the combination of high TTV loads and a low CD4/CD8 ratio could be a useful marker of immune dysfunction to identify PLWH at higher risk of long-term complications.

The correlation between soluble IL-6 and TTV load also supports the usefulness of TTV as a prognostic marker, as IL-6 has been widely associated with morbidity and all-cause mortality in the context of HIV infection36. Some authors have suggested that TTV DNA might induce immune cells to produce and secrete proinflammatory cytokines, including IL-6, via continuous stimulation of TLR937. This receptor, expressed in various cell types, is involved in innate sensing of DNA viruses and triggers signal cascades that culminate in the secretion of proinflammatory cytokines and also intervenes in the induction of Th1 adaptive immune responses38. Larger longitudinal studies are needed to validate the prognostic value of TTV in the context of treated HIV infection, with promising associations found with abnormalities of immunity and inflammation.

Regarding the relationship between TTV and HIV viremia, previous studies showed that HIV viral suppression after ART is associated with a significant reduction in TTV load39. In our study, despite the small number of participants who developed VF, TTV load at baseline was high in these patients. We found a moderate inverse association between TTV viral load, time under HIV viral suppression and the time to blips appearance, suggesting that low-level HIV replication may facilitate the immune evasion of TTV, resulting in higher TTV replication rates. Therefore, TTV load may be further explored to identify adherence issues and patients at risk of blips or VF.

The limitations of this study include its retrospective and cross-sectional design, the relatively small sample size, and the availability of immunological data in only a subset of participants due to the multicenter nature of the study. The assumption of a 100% prevalence among healthy donors, based on data provided by massive sequencing studies suggesting that the measurement of TTV by PCR techniques could underestimate the real prevalence40, led us to include all patients in the analysis, assigning a value below the minimum detectable TTV viral load to undetectable subjects. This fact, and the analysis of TTV viral loads below the technique theoretical detection limit, can be controversial. In addition, the use of plasma instead of cells could have limited our ability to detect TTV. A study including plasma and total blood samples found that TTV was detected in whole blood in a 26% of patients with undetectable TTV in plasma41. Due to all these technical limitations, results interpretation needs to be cautious. However, our study is the first analysing TTV in vertically acquired HIV infection.

In conclusion, our study found higher TTV viral load in YWVH and correlations with the CD4/CD8 ratio and IL-6 levels, suggesting that TTV should be further explored as a marker of immune reconstitution, useful for the identification of high-risk patients within the unique population of people living with vertically acquired HIV.

Methods

Study design and participants

All the methods were carried out in accordance with relevant standards and regulations. An observational, retrospective study was performed within the Spanish Cohort of HIV-infected children and adolescents, and vertically HIV-infected patients transferred to Adult Units (CoRISpe-FARO)42. A convenience sample at the Spanish HIV Hospital Universitario Gregorio Marañón BioBank (HIV HUGM BioBank) was selected among participants on stable ART and virologically suppressed for HIV-RNA for at least 6 months (undetectable viral load < 20 copies/ml). Patients co-infected with hepatitis C or B viruses were excluded. Longitudinal data on the immunological status and viral suppression were collected up to September 2022. Patients were censored during follow-up upon treatment interruption or loss of viral suppression. A cohort of HIV-negative healthy donors (HD) was recruited from participating hospitals for comparison, with the inclusion criteria being a negative HIV serology and no known medical conditions. Virological failure (VF) was defined as the loss of viral suppression (two consecutive HIV-RNA determinations above 20 copies/mL at least six months apart) without treatment interruption or suspected adherence issues. Transient viral load values above detection limit but below 1,000 copies/ml, followed by a return to suppression, were defined as episodes of intermittent viremia or “blips”.

The study was approved by the ethic committee of Hospital Universitario La Paz in Madrid (PI-4636). Written informed consent was obtained from all HIV participants before inclusion in CoRISpe-FARO and from HD before inclusion in the study.

Sample processing

Soluble proinflammatory biomarkers and T-cell immunophenotyping were evaluated as part of an extended protocol, with the first results already published17. Briefly, plasma and peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) from YWVH were provided by the HIV HUGM BioBank according to sample availability (in future laboratory determinations, the number of patients will vary due to that limitation). A 30 mL sample of fresh whole blood was collected from HD volunteers in ethylene diamine tetra-acetic acid tubes. The percentage of CD4 and CD8 T-cells from HD was determined in fresh whole blood using Cytomics FC (Beckman-Coulter, Brea, CA, USA). Plasma and PBMCs were immediately isolated by Ficoll-Paque density gradient centrifugation and stored at − 80 °C and − 170 °C respectively, until subsequent analysis.

Laboratory determinations

As previously described17, PBMCs were thawed, washed with PBS containing 3% of Bovine Serum Albumin (BSA) (Millipore) and stained with LIVE/DEAD Fixable Aqua Cell Stain (Invitrogen) for cell viability and T-cell surface marker antibodies distributed across two different flow cytometry panels for 35 min at room temperature including, anti-CD19-CD14-CD56-BV510, anti-CD3-APC-Cy7 and anti-CD4-APCR700 for lineage, anti-CD45RA-ECD and anti-CD27-PerCP-Cy5.5 for maturation, anti-HLA-DR-APC, anti-CD38-FITC and anti-CD69-R700 for activation, anti-CD127-PeCy7 for IL-7 receptor, anti-CD57-FITC for senescence and anti-PD1-BV421, anti-LAG3-PeCy7, anti-TIGIT-AF647 and anti-TIM3-PE for exhaustion. Then, cells were washed and fixed for 20 min at 4 °C with 4% paraformaldehyde solution (PFA) (see Supplementary Material for protocol details). Isotype controls were included for TIM-3, LAG-3, TIGIT and PD-1. CD4 T-cells were identified as viable cells, negative for CD19-CD14-CD56 and positive for CD3 and CD4, while CD8 T-cells were identified as viable cells, negative for CD19-CD14-CD56, positive for CD3 and negative for CD4. The T-cell maturation subsets were defined based on the expression of CD45RA and CD27 as naïve (CD45RA + CD27+), central memory (CM; CD45RA−CD27+), effector memory (EM; CD45RA−CD27−) and terminally differentiated (TemRA; CD45RA+CD27−). Acquisition was carried out in a Gallios flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter). Schematic gating strategy can be found in Supplementary Fig. 2. Before acquisition, cells were fixed with 4% PFA. FlowJo 10.7.1 software (TreeStar) was used for data analysis. Soluble IL-6 was assessed in plasma (COBAS e411, Roche Molecular Systems, Basel, Switzerland).

Quantitative TTV PCR

The DNA extraction from plasma samples was performed using eMAG (bioMerieux) according to the manufacturer’s recommendations. The TTV-DNA was quantified by real-time PCR (qPCR) using TTV R-Gene® kit (bioMerieux) as previously described23. The determined TTV DNA plasma level presented a high range (from 250 to 109 copies/ml) in the analysed cohort.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were described as medians and interquartile ranges [IQR] and categorical variables as absolute numbers and percentages. Non-parametric tests were performed after Kolmogorov–Smirnov test for assessing non-normal distribution. For the analysis of TTV viral load, samples with undetectable TTV load were assigned a value immediately below the lowest viral load obtained (5 copies/mL, 0.69 log10 copies/mL)23, to allow for a log10 transformation. To compare two independent groups when the variables categorical or continuous chi-square test and two-tailed Mann Whitney U-test were used, respectively. Correlations were assessed using Spearman’s rank test. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The Statistical Package for the Social Sciences software (SPSS 20.0) was used for the statistical analysis. Graphs were generated using GraphPad Prism 9.0 (GraphPad Software).
