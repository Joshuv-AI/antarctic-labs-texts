Extreme Cold Events and Pediatric Asthma Outpatient Visits: The Modifying Role of Ambient Carbon Monoxide and Sulfur Dioxide
Authors: Xing Gong, Li Wei, Yunshu Xu, Changdi Xu, Yi Zhang, Fengxia Sun, Man Tian, Liya Wang, Hengxue Wang, Ming Ge, Feng Liu, Lilin Xiong, Wei Pan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeExtreme cold exposure may trigger respiratory morbidity in children, but whether concurrent ambient air pollution modifies cold-related risks of pediatric asthma remains unclear. This study evaluated associations between extreme cold events and pediatric asthma outpatient visits and assessed whether these associations differed according to ambient pollutant concentrations.Patients and methodsWe conducted a time-series study of 62,167 pediatric asthma outpatient visits among children diagnosed with asthma at a tertiary children’s hospital in Nanjing, China, from 2019 to 2024. Daily asthma visits were linked with meteorological variables and ambient concentrations of PM10, PM2.5, NO2, SO2, CO, and O3. Extreme cold events were defined as ≥2, ≥3, or ≥4 consecutive days with daily mean temperature ≤4.5°C (the 10th percentile of the study-period distribution). Quasi-Poisson distributed-lag non-linear models were used to estimate cumulative relative risks over lag 0–14 days. Pollutant-related effect modification was evaluated using stratified analyses and interaction measures.ResultsOverall, extreme cold events were not significantly associated with pediatric asthma outpatient visits. However, stronger associations were observed during periods with higher CO and SO2 concentrations. For lag 0–14 days, cumulative relative risks for ≥2-day cold events were 2.60 (95% CI: 1.13–5.95) in the higher-CO stratum and 3.41 (95% CI: 1.15–10.11) in the higher-SO2 stratum. Similar patterns were observed for longer cold-event definitions. Multiplicative interactions were identified for both CO and SO2, whereas additive interaction evidence was clearer for CO. Other pollutants showed less consistent modification patterns.ConclusionThe association between extreme cold events and pediatric asthma outpatient visits may depend on concurrent gaseous pollutant conditions. Relatively elevated CO and SO2 may help identify cold-event periods with greater pediatric asthma outpatient burden.Keywords: pediatric asthma outpatient visits, extreme cold events, ambient air pollutants, distributed-lag non-linear models, effect modification

Introduction

Climate change has become a defining public health challenge of the twenty-first century.1 The Sixth Assessment Report of the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) indicates that, despite continued global warming, short-term cold extremes will persist across many mid-latitude regions and may even intensify in some areas.2 A global modeling study covering 750 locations across 43 countries estimated that non-optimal temperatures were associated with 5.08 million deaths annually during 2000–2019, with cold accounting for most of this burden.3 Children are a particularly vulnerable subgroup in this context.4 This vulnerability may reflect age-related differences in thermoregulation, higher minute ventilation relative to body mass, smaller airway caliber, and the continuing development of respiratory and immune systems.5–7

Asthma is one of the most common and burdensome chronic respiratory diseases in childhood. Recent estimates from the Global Burden of Disease Study 2021 indicate that asthma continues to impose a substantial global burden, with approximately 260 million cases across all ages in 20218 In children and young people, clinical reviews have estimated that around 14% are affected by asthma.9 Beyond its high prevalence, childhood asthma also imposes considerable healthcare and societal burdens, contributing to outpatient visits, emergency department utilization, hospitalizations, medication use, and asthma-related school absenteeism10,11 Cold weather can aggravate respiratory symptoms in people with asthma, while airway cooling and drying provide a plausible pathway for bronchoconstriction in susceptible individuals.12,13 However, epidemiological evidence linking ambient cold exposure to pediatric asthma morbidity remains inconsistent. Positive associations have been reported in several Chinese studies, and estimated effects vary according to cold-spell definition, duration, lag window, and age group.14,15 In contrast, studies from other settings have suggested that temperature–asthma associations may be modified by local climate, seasonality, air pollution, and aeroallergen profiles16,17 with some pediatric emergency-department studies observing stronger associations with warmer rather than colder temperatures.18 This heterogeneity underscores the need for region-specific assessments of cold-related pediatric asthma risk.

Ambient air pollution is an important co-exposure that may modify cold-related risks for pediatric respiratory outcomes. Recent multi-pollutant analyses in Chinese cities have associated particulate matter (PM2.5, PM10), nitrogen dioxide (NO2), ozone (O3), sulfur dioxide (SO2), and carbon monoxide (CO) with pediatric asthma morbidity, including asthma incidence, outpatient visits, and hospitalizations.19–21 Pediatric time-series evidence has also begun to examine temperature–pollution joint effects. In Hefei, higher PM2.5 and PM10 concentrations strengthened the association between cold exposure and childhood asthma hospitalization, whereas a study in Jinan found that low apparent temperature modified the association between NO2 exposure and pediatric asthma visits.22,23 During cold-wave episodes, increased heating demand, combustion-related emissions, stagnant atmospheric conditions, and reduced pollutant dispersion may jointly alter urban air-pollution profiles, thereby increasing the likelihood of concurrent exposure to low temperature and elevated ambient pollutants.24–26 Cold and air pollution also converge on plausible pathways involving epithelial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and airway inflammation.27–30 However, pediatric asthma-specific joint effects across multiple pollutants remain insufficiently characterized, particularly for outpatient outcomes.31

We hypothesized that associations between cold events and pediatric asthma outpatient visits would differ according to concurrent ambient pollutant levels. To test this hypothesis, we conducted a six-year time-series study in Nanjing, China, using daily pediatric asthma outpatient counts linked with meteorological and ambient air-pollution data. We first estimated overall cold-event associations across three duration thresholds and then assessed whether six ambient pollutants modified these associations. Within the pollutant-stratified analyses, we further quantified multiplicative and additive interaction measures and model-based attributable outpatient burden.

Methods

Study Setting and Population

The present study was conducted in Nanjing, the capital of Jiangsu Province. Nanjing experiences a humid subtropical climate, characterized by hot summers and cool winters. The population of the city is consistently over 9 million. Anonymous electronic outpatient records of children diagnosed with asthma were obtained from a tertiary children’s hospital in Nanjing that serves the city and surrounding regions. The study spanned 2,192 consecutive days from January 1, 2019, to December 31, 2024. A total of 62,167 pediatric asthma outpatient visits were included in the analysis. Only records from Nanjing residents were included. All asthma diagnoses were coded according to the 10th revision of the International Classification of Diseases (ICD-10 code J45) and extracted by trained medical record reviewers. The daily counts of asthma outpatient visits were aggregated by date and further stratified by sex (boys and girls) and age group (<6 years or ≥6 years). Information on BMI, asthma severity, asthma control status, clinical phenotypes, and endotypes was unavailable; therefore, these characteristics could not be described or evaluated in subgroup analyses.

Environmental Exposure Data

The daily relative humidity and the 24-hour average temperature were obtained from the ERA5-Land reanalysis dataset (0.1° × 0.1°).32 The ChinaHighAirPollutants (CHAP) dataset (1 km × 1 km) was used to determine the daily average concentrations of ambient air pollutants, including PM10, PM2.5, NO2, SO2, CO, and maximum daily 8-hour average ozone (O3–8h).33–36 For each environmental variable, grid cells covering the administrative area of Nanjing were extracted and spatially averaged to generate a city-level daily exposure series. These city-level daily meteorological and air-pollution estimates were then matched to the daily aggregated pediatric asthma outpatient counts by calendar date. The environmental exposure series was complete for all 2,192 study days, and no exposure days were imputed. Daily measurements of aeroallergens, including pollen and mold, and information on indoor air pollution, household heating and ventilation, and personal exposure patterns were unavailable and therefore were not included in the statistical models.

Definition of Extreme Cold Events

Extreme cold events were defined using a relative temperature threshold to account for regional acclimatization. Specifically, cold events were identified as consecutive days during which daily mean temperature was at or below the 10th percentile (P10) of the study-period temperature distribution. In the six-year dataset, P10 corresponded to 4.5 °C. We then constructed three duration-based event indicators, requiring at least two, three, or four consecutive days at or below this threshold. This duration-graded definition is consistent with previous cold-spell studies in China and allowed us to examine whether associations differed by event duration.14

Statistical Analysis

The statistical analyses followed a sequence of related comparisons. We first examined whether daily temperature and cold events lasting at least 2, 3, or 4 consecutive days were associated with the number of pediatric asthma outpatient visits, while accounting for seasonal and long-term time patterns, relative humidity, day of the week, public holidays, and the COVID-19 period. We then compared cold-event associations on days with relatively higher and lower concentrations of each of the six ambient pollutants to assess whether air pollution changed the relationship between cold events and asthma visits. Formal interaction measures were estimated for each pollutant, whereas subgroup analyses focused on CO and SO2, for which the pollutant-conditioned patterns were most consistent. We also estimated the model-based number and proportion of outpatient visits associated with cold events within each pollutant stratum. Finally, we repeated the analyses using alternative model specifications, pollutant cutoffs, and exclusion of the COVID-19 period to assess the robustness of the findings. Detailed model specifications are provided below.

All analyses were performed in R version 4.6.0, using the dlnm and splines packages.37,38 Daily counts of asthma outpatient visits were modeled with a quasi-Poisson distribution to accommodate overdispersion.39 Statistical significance was assessed at the two-sided α = 0.05 level. The technical specifications are described below.

Overall Temperature and Cold-Event Associations

To model the temperature-asthma association with adequate flexibility, a distributed-lag non-linear model (DLNM) for daily mean temperature was constructed.40 The DLNM incorporated a natural cubic spline (three internal knots positioned at the 25th, 50th, and 75th percentiles of the temperature distribution) for the exposure-response dimension and a natural cubic spline (three internal knots placed at equally spaced log-scale lag values) for the lag dimension. Pollutant-Stratified Analyses

To assess the potential of ambient air pollutants to modify the association between cold events and asthma, we conducted pollutant-stratified DLNM analyses as an effect-modification screen. For each pollutant, daily concentrations were dichotomized at the 75th percentile (P75), defining days at or above P75 as the higher-concentration stratum and days below P75 as the lower-concentration stratum. The observed P75 values were approximately 72.43 μg/m3 for PM10, 38.80 μg/m3 for PM2.5, 38.14 μg/m3 for NO2, 7.77 μg/m3 for SO2, 0.81 mg/m3 for CO, and 136.62 μg/m3 for O3. The P75 cutoff was selected as a relative within-city contrast because WHO 2021 24-h guideline values41 were either frequently exceeded (for example, PM2.5) or never exceeded (CO and SO2) in the study data, making guideline thresholds unsuitable as common stratification cut points. The DLNM model structure described above was refitted within each pollutant stratum. The cold-event indicator (binary; coded 1 on event days and 0 otherwise) was incorporated as a linear-exposure cross-basis term to estimate cumulative relative risks (cumRRs) over lag windows of 0–1, 0–7, and 0–14 days. The lag 0–14 window was prespecified as the primary cumulative window. Shorter windows were reported for completeness.

Subgroup analyses were conducted by sex (boys and girls) and age group (<6 and ≥6 years). For CO and SO2, pollutant-stratified cold-event associations were estimated separately within each subgroup. These analyses focused on cumulative associations over lag 0–14 days and were considered exploratory.

Sensitivity Analyses

Several sensitivity analyses were conducted to evaluate the robustness of the primary higher-CO and higher-SO2 findings. First, the degrees of freedom for the relative humidity spline were varied from 3 df in the main model to 4, 5, and 6 df. Second, the number of log-spaced knots in the lag-response function was varied from the main-model specification of three knots to two, four, and five knots. Third, because the COVID-19 pandemic may have altered healthcare-seeking behavior, respiratory infection circulation, and exposure patterns, the higher-CO and higher-SO2 cold-event analyses were repeated after excluding the COVID-19 period. Finally, the pollutant-stratified analyses were repeated using P50 and P80 cutoffs to assess sensitivity to alternative concentration thresholds. P50 provided a median split with balanced strata, whereas P80 represented an upper-tail cutoff while retaining sufficient observations for model estimation. Sensitivity analyses focused on lag 0–14 cumulative RRs in the higher-pollutant strata.

Results

Descriptive Characteristics

A total of 62,167 pediatric asthma outpatient visits were recorded over 2,192 consecutive days from January 1, 2019, to December 31, 2024. The daily median number of visits was 27, with an interquartile range of 17 to 37. Boys accounted for 62.32% of visits, and children aged <6 years accounted for 50.93% of visits. During the study period, the daily mean temperature averaged 17.39 °C, and the 10th percentile of daily mean temperature was 4.5 °C. Based on this threshold, 210 days were classified as part of ≥2-day cold events, 182 days as part of ≥3-day events, and 167 days as part of ≥4-day events. The median daily concentrations of PM10, PM2.5, NO2, O3, SO2, and CO were 49.97 μg/m3, 26.23 μg/m3, 26.73 μg/m3, 100.43 μg/m3, 6.00 μg/m3, and 0.68 mg/m3, respectively. Notably, daily CO and SO2 concentrations remained below the WHO 2021 24-h guideline values throughout the study period. Descriptive characteristics are shown in Table 1.

Association Between Extreme Cold Events and Pediatric Asthma Outpatient Visits

We first evaluated the overall association between ambient temperature and pediatric asthma outpatient visits. The cumulative exposure-response curve over lag 0–14 days showed a J-shaped pattern, with the minimum morbidity temperature estimated at 6.36 °C (Figure 1A). Around the cold-event threshold of 4.5 °C, the cumulative relative risk was close to unity, suggesting no clear increase in pediatric asthma visits at this temperature threshold in the overall population. Consistently, the lag-response curves for ≥2-, ≥3-, and ≥4-day cold events showed only modest deviations from a relative risk of 1.0 across lag days 0 to 14 (Figure 1B). The lag 0–14 cumulative relative risks were 1.13 (95% CI: 0.94–1.35), 1.11 (95% CI: 0.94–1.32), and 1.06 (95% CI: 0.89–1.26) for ≥2-, ≥3-, and ≥4-day cold events, respectively. Modification of Cold-Event Effects by Ambient Air Pollutants

Stratified analyses indicated that cold-event associations differed according to ambient CO and SO2 concentrations (Figure 2). Using lag 0–14 days as the primary cumulative window, cold-event associations were consistently elevated in the higher-CO stratum across all three duration definitions, with cumulative RRs of 2.60 (95% CI, 1.13–5.95), 2.41 (95% CI, 1.08–5.38), and 2.36 (95% CI, 1.07–5.22) for ≥2-, ≥3-, and ≥4-day cold events, respectively. By contrast, none of the corresponding associations reached statistical significance in the lower-CO stratum. A similar pattern was observed for SO2. In the higher-SO2 stratum, the lag 0–14 cumulative RRs were 3.41 (95% CI, 1.15–10.11), 3.40 (95% CI, 1.06–10.97), and 3.06 (95% CI, 0.98–9.60) for ≥2, ≥3, and ≥4-day cold events, respectively, whereas none of the corresponding associations reached statistical significance in the lower-SO2 stratum. The other pollutants did not demonstrate a consistent lag 0–14 modification pattern. Full pollutant-stratified estimates across lag windows are provided in Supplementary Table S2.

Attributable Burden

To translate these relative risks into potential healthcare burden, we estimated model-based attributable outpatient visits for cold events within pollutant strata. Model-based attributable estimates suggested a potentially substantial outpatient burden during cold events in the higher-CO and higher-SO2 strata. In the higher-CO stratum at lag 0–14 days, 1007.57 (95% CI, 143.31–1,356.10) pediatric asthma outpatient visits during ≥2-day cold events were attributable to cold events within that stratum, corresponding to an attributable fraction of 61.47% (95% CI, 8.74%–82.74%). For ≥3-day cold events, the attributable case count was 797.64 (95% CI, 159.26–1,106.74), with an attributable fraction of 58.44% (95% CI, 11.67%–81.08%); for ≥4-day cold events, the attributable case count was 749.58 (95% CI, 67.69–1,049.16), with an attributable fraction of 57.62% (95% CI, 5.20%–80.64%). For SO2, attributable case counts were 786.38 (95% CI, 118.21–998.58), 654.03 (95% CI, 3.32–838.81), and 580.48 (95% CI, −1.36–773.51) for ≥2-, ≥3-, and ≥4-day events, respectively. Formal Interaction Analyses

Formal interaction analyses supported differences in cold-event associations according to CO and SO2 concentrations (Table 3). The IRRs for CO were 2.57 (95% CI, 1.08–6.10), 2.45 (95% CI, 1.06–5.67), and 2.55 (95% CI, 1.11–5.87) for ≥2-, ≥3-, and ≥4-day cold events, respectively. For SO2, the corresponding IRRs were 3.56 (95% CI, 1.17–10.78), 3.55 (95% CI, 1.08–11.67), and 3.32 (95% CI, 1.04–10.64). On the additive scale, RERI estimates indicated significant positive interaction for CO, with values of 0.80 (95% CI, 0.06–1.55), 0.77 (95% CI, 0.04–1.50), and 0.78 (95% CI, 0.06–1.50), respectively. For SO2, RERI estimates were positive but not statistically significant: 0.43 (95% CI, −0.46–1.32), 0.28 (95% CI, −0.53–1.10), and 0.25 (95% CI, −0.55–1.06), respectively. Interaction estimates for other pollutants are provided in Supplementary Table S4. Although statistically significant RERI estimates were observed for some other pollutant–cold-event combinations, these signals were not accompanied by statistically significant and consistently elevated lag 0–14 cold-event cumRRs in the corresponding higher-pollutant strata. Therefore, they were interpreted as less robust than the consistent CO- and SO2-conditioned patterns.

Subgroup and Sensitivity Analyses

After establishing the main CO- and SO2-conditioned patterns, we explored whether these associations differed by sex and age group. In subgroup analyses (Figure 3), CO- and SO2-conditioned cold-event associations varied by sex and age strata. For CO, lag 0–14 cumulative RRs in the higher stratum were elevated across all three cold-event durations in girls and in children aged <6 years, reaching statistical significance (Figure 3A); estimates in boys and in children aged ≥6 years were positive but did not reach statistical significance. For SO2, elevated higher-stratum risks were observed across subgroups, reaching statistical significance in boys and children aged ≥6 years, whereas estimates in girls and children aged <6 years were positive but had wider confidence intervals (Figure 3B). Corresponding lower-stratum estimates remained close to unity. Detailed subgroup estimates are provided in Supplementary Table S5.

Finally, we conducted sensitivity analyses to assess the robustness of the main findings (Supplementary Tables S6–S9). The higher-CO associations remained stable after changing the degrees of freedom for relative humidity and the number of lag knots. The higher-SO2 associations also remained positive, although some estimates, especially for longer cold-event definitions, became borderline. In the sensitivity analysis excluding study days during the COVID-19 period, the higher-CO and higher-SO2 estimates remained elevated, with most associations still statistically significant. These analyses support the robustness of the main model. Under the P50 and P80 cutoffs, the higher-CO and higher-SO2 strata generally showed positive cumulative RRs across all three cold-event durations, although statistical significance was not reached for some event definitions. Overall, these analyses supported the direction of the main findings.

Discussion

In this six-year time-series analysis, there was no clear association between extreme cold events and pediatric asthma outpatient visits in the overall study population. Stronger estimated associations were observed in the higher-CO and higher-SO2 strata, whereas no clear association was observed in the corresponding lower-concentration strata. Notably, the daily concentrations of CO and SO2 remained below the 2021 World Health Organization guideline values throughout the study period. These findings suggest that, within guideline-compliant ranges, elevated gaseous pollution may help identify cold-event periods with higher pediatric asthma risk.

The absence of a clear overall association between extreme cold events and pediatric asthma outpatient visits may reflect heterogeneity in exposure context rather than the absence of cold-related respiratory vulnerability. Previous pediatric asthma studies have reported inconsistent temperature-related associations, with some observing cold-related increases, others reporting stronger associations with warmer temperatures, and some finding weak or null associations.18,44,45 Moreover, cold events defined by ambient temperature alone may encompass heterogeneous exposure scenarios, including differences in humidity, wind conditions, and air-pollution mixtures,17,46 and may not fully capture children’s personal thermal exposure.47 Such heterogeneity could dilute the average population-level estimate. In this context, the emergence of significant cold-event associations under relatively high CO and SO2 concentrations is consistent with the interpretation that cold-related asthma risk may be conditional on concurrent pollution context.

Our results suggest that the overall null association masked a pollution-conditioned subset of cold days with elevated pediatric asthma risk. Among the six pollutants assessed, CO and SO2 showed the clearest alignment between the pollutant-stratified and multiplicative interaction analyses: significant lag 0–14 cold-event associations were observed in their higher-concentration strata, and the corresponding multiplicative interactions were significant. In contrast, PM10, PM2.5, and NO2 showed no significant lag 0–14 associations within either concentration stratum and no significant multiplicative interactions, although additive interactions were observed for PM2.5 across all three cold-event definitions and for PM10 and NO2 at selected durations. Interaction estimates for O3 were not estimable. A study from Shenyang reported that CO was positively associated with childhood asthma outpatient and emergency visits, with the largest cumulative CO effect observed for asthma visits in winter.48 In Beijing, SO2 was associated with childhood asthma admissions, and this association was stronger during the cold season.49 However, these studies primarily evaluated pollutant-specific associations and seasonal effect modification. By contrast, our analysis examined whether ambient pollutants modified the delayed asthma response to objectively defined extreme cold events in an observational time-series framework. The concentration of excess risk in the higher-CO and higher-SO2 strata, despite all daily concentrations remaining below WHO guideline values,41 indicates that elevated combustion-related gaseous pollutants may define cold-event conditions with greater respiratory relevance. This finding adds observational evidence by showing that pediatric asthma risk during cold events may emerge even under guideline-compliant, but contextually important, gaseous-pollution conditions.

One explanation is that the higher-CO and higher-SO2 strata may reflect a cold-season combustion-related pollution pattern. Cold periods are often accompanied by increased heating demand, greater fuel combustion, and more stable atmospheric conditions, all of which can raise or accumulate gaseous pollutants.50 CO is closely linked to incomplete combustion, while SO2 is mainly generated from sulfur-containing fuel combustion. Therefore, relatively high CO and SO2 during cold events may indicate a winter pollution context characterized by combustion-related emissions. This context provides a plausible environmental explanation for why cold-event risks were more apparent in the higher-CO and higher-SO2 strata.

This pattern is also biologically plausible. Recent pediatric evidence shows that cold dry air provocation is associated with airway hyperresponsiveness in children with asthma, suggesting that cold-air sensitivity may be an important feature of pediatric asthma phenotypes.51 Under such conditions, concurrent gaseous pollutants may impose additional respiratory stress. CO is a primary marker of incomplete combustion that may contribute through systemic and cellular pathways. By binding to hemoglobin and other heme-containing proteins, CO can reduce effective oxygen transport, interfere with mitochondrial respiration and cellular energy metabolism, and promote oxidative or redox imbalance.52,53 SO2 is highly water-soluble and can be rapidly absorbed into airway-surface fluid, where it forms sulfite and bisulfite derivatives. Recent experimental evidence indicates that SO2 and its derivatives may aggravate asthma-like airway injury through oxidative stress, TLR4/NF-κB-mediated inflammatory signaling, TRPV1-related pathways, and disruption of epithelial tight junctions.54–57 Under cold-event conditions, when respiratory and metabolic demands may be increased, these mechanisms provide a plausible basis for the stronger asthma associations observed in the higher-CO and higher-SO2 strata.

The age- and sex-stratified analyses provided a more nuanced view of the CO- and SO2-conditioned cold-event associations. In the higher-CO stratum, cumulative estimates over lag days 0–14 were more evident among girls and children aged <6 years, whereas estimates in boys and children aged ≥6 years were positive but did not reach statistical significance. This pattern is partly consistent with a recent time-series study in Chengdu, China, which reported that CO mainly affected children younger than 7 years and that air-pollution-related asthma hospitalizations were generally stronger among girls.58 However, in a Toronto case-crossover study of children aged 6–12 years, short-term CO exposure was significantly associated with asthma hospitalization, particularly among boys.59 For SO2, the higher-stratum cumulative estimates in our study were more apparent among boys and children aged ≥6 years, while estimates among girls and younger children were positive but more uncertain. Previous studies have also reported heterogeneous subgroup patterns. A pediatric non-allergic rhinitis study in Hefei, China found that SO2 was associated with increased outpatient visits, while stronger pollutant-related associations were observed among boys and school-aged children.60 By contrast, asthma-specific evidence from northern China further reported stronger SO2 associations among preschool children and no significant sex-specific differences.49 Taken together, these findings suggest that age- and sex-specific susceptibility to gaseous pollutants is likely context-dependent rather than fixed. Differences in study region, climate, pollutant mixture, healthcare-use pattern, age categorization, exposure contrast, and statistical modeling may explain the inconsistency across studies. Therefore, our subgroup findings should be interpreted as exploratory evidence of potential susceptibility rather than definitive proof of sex- or age-specific effects.

This study has several strengths. First, the study used six years of daily pediatric asthma outpatient data, which enabled the assessment of cold-event effects across multiple event durations and cumulative lag windows. Second, the analysis incorporated relative risks, interaction contrasts, and attributable cases, thereby ensuring that interpretation was not solely dependent on the statistical significance of a single regression estimate. Third, the use of outpatient data captured a broader spectrum of pediatric asthma healthcare demand in comparison to admission-only studies. This is important because cold-related asthma morbidity may increase outpatient visits even when it does not progress to hospitalization.

Four limitations should be acknowledged. First, this single-city study used outpatient records from a tertiary pediatric hospital, which may limit the generalizability of the effect and model-based attributable estimates to other climates and healthcare systems. However, the six-year time series and consistent outcome source reduced variation in outcome ascertainment. Second, modeled city-level exposure estimates may not fully represent children’s personal exposures, particularly during cold weather when time spent indoors, heating, ventilation, and indoor pollution sources vary. The absence of daily pollen, mold, and other aeroallergen measurements may also have resulted in exposure measurement error and residual confounding. Third, the aggregated daily visit counts did not distinguish incident asthma exacerbations from follow-up visits or repeated consultations and lacked information on BMI, asthma severity and control, clinical phenotypes, and endotypes, precluding analyses by these characteristics. Fourth, sensitivity analyses using the P50 and P80 pollutant cutoffs produced broadly similar directional patterns to the primary analysis, although estimate precision varied, particularly for SO2. Future studies should integrate multicity data, detailed clinical records, aeroallergen monitoring, and indoor or personal exposure measurements.

Conclusions

In summary, extreme cold events were not uniformly associated with increased pediatric asthma outpatient visits in Nanjing. Instead, the excess association was concentrated on days where levels of CO or SO2 were relatively high. This pattern suggests that the observed cold-event susceptibility was not simply a general air-pollution signal. Evidence for PM10, PM2.5, and NO2 was less consistent across stratified and interaction analyses, while interaction estimates for O3 were not estimable. These findings add to pediatric evidence on combined environmental exposures and may be relevant to other urban settings where cold weather, combustion-related emissions, and guideline-compliant but locally elevated gaseous pollutants co-occur. Moreover, future multicity studies incorporating source apportionment, indoor exposure indicators, or personal exposure measurements are required to assess whether the CO/SO2 pattern recurs across different heating practices and urban pollution profiles.
