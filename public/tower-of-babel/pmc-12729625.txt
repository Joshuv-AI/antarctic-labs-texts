Integrating pathophysiology and pharmacology in nursing education: student perceptions, learning outcomes, and implications for clinical competence
Authors: Ohood K. Almuzaini, Ashwaq Alanazi, Sumaiah Alrubiaan, Ashraf N. Abdalla
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundUnderstanding pathophysiology and pharmacology is essential for nursing students to ensure safe medication administration and clinical decision-making. In response, an integrated course was developed to help students translate theory into practice and build the competencies required for safe, effective care.ObjectiveThis main aim was to identify strengths, challenges, and the practical applicability of acquired knowledge to inform targeted instructional improvements.MethodsThis study used a cross-sectional survey among nurses’ student at Umm Al-Qura University (UQU) during the 2024/2025 academic year, to evaluate students’ perceptions of the integrated pathophysiology and pharmacology (IPP) course. Data was collected using a validated questionnaire covering four domains: course content and difficulty, general pharmacology, general pathophysiology, and medication management. Statistical analyses involved Fisher’s exact test, Wilcoxon rank-sum tests, and internal-consistency assessment with Cronbach’s alpha.ResultsOf 236 eligible students, 150 participated (63.6%). Most agreed the course was appropriate (86%), the content was manageable (81%), and delivery was satisfactory (89%). 72% felt confident applying pharmacology concepts in clinical scenarios. Notably, 62% agreed they could recall drug names, side effects, and contraindications, the weakest measurement relative to other areas, highlighting a memorization gap. Higher GPA and longer weekly study time were independently associated with better understanding across all of the domains (p < 0.05).ConclusionThe integrated course improved nursing students’ understanding and application of pharmacology, highlighting the need for strategies that reinforce knowledge retention strategies. Thus, this may reduce stress and resilience which remains a key factor of performance during clinical placements.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-025-08251-y.

Background

Nursing professionals have a key role in patient care, especially in the accurate administration of medications and the delivery of therapeutic interventions. An essential principle for effective medication administration is based on nurses’ understanding of pharmacology, which helps them to predict therapeutic effect, manage adverse reactions and drug interactions [27, 29]. In response to the growing demand for clinically applicable education, most nursing curriculums integrate pathophysiological and pharmacological (IPP) concepts together. IPP course is delivered for the 2nd year nursing students at the College of Pharmacy, Umm Al-Qura University (UQU) in Saudi Arabia, to introduce basic concepts which correlate the development of diseases (pathophysiology) with the actions and uses of drugs (pharmacology). The course highlights understanding how changes in organ function due to disease are connected to the therapeutic effects and mechanisms of medications used for treatment. This course is proposed to provide Nursing students with comprehensive principles in both pathophysiology and pharmacology including etiology and clinical manifestations, as well as a clear understanding of pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics [15]. The course was developed using several reliable sources, including Lippincott’s Illustrated Reviews [13], Pharmacology, Basic & Clinical Pharmacology [14], and reputable online databases such as the National Center for Biotechnology Information, and Lexicomp. It also provides students with a functional understanding of how disease interacts with pharmacological medications. The aim of the IPP course was to bridge the gap between theoretical and clinical practice, thereby promoting critical thinking and enhancing clinical decision-making skills.

The integrative learning models are becoming an interesting teaching tool in modern nursing education worldwide. These models are formed by constructive and experiential learning theories, which highlight the active role of learners through the connection of new information to previous knowledge [2]. Several studies have shown that students who attend integrated courses have greater comprehension and confidence when implementing their knowledge into practice [1, 3, 23]. However, student perception remains a crucial measurement. Learners’ self-assessments of their understanding, as well as their confidence in applying that understanding, are frequently used as indicators of curriculum effectiveness [12]. As a result, these perceptions can shape student engagement, motivation, and retain information over time.

Although research has been conducted in several countries on the benefits and challenges of integrated medical and nursing curriculums, there remains a lack of localized data or clear IPP course specifications, especially within the context of Saudi nursing education. The regional variability in educational and healthcare institutional settings emphasizes the need to assess students’ perceptions of the IPP course [10]. That will allow for the shaping of teaching strategies to appropriate enhancement of student needs and can inform academic institutions, healthcare settings, and professional training centers for future revisions of the curriculum. This is in particular for the undergraduate level, as perceptions formed early in training can have a sustainable impact on how students approach patient care and clinical problem-solving.

The IPP is a course which aligns with international guidance of nursing education, as it encourages students to observe the patient as a whole rather than as a set of single symptoms, conditions or medications [26, 27, 30]. Additionally, students understanding of how pathophysiological changes at the cellular, molecular or systemic level are affected by pharmacological interventions are more likely to make informed and accurate decisions in clinical practice [6, 9]. This approach also enhances clinical decisions by prompting students to consider the complex relationships between medications, diseases, and other therapies.

Several studies suggested that the combination of two subjects enhances the students’ clarity and ability to make clinical intervention. Several literatures indicated students struggle with the retention and implementation of pharmacological knowledge when the material is presented alone without a clear clinical context [9, 16, 20]. Additionally, pharmacology is considered one of the most challenging subjects among nursing students. Therefore, the integrative approaches have attempted to resolve this issue by providing a combined structure in which pharmacology is taught within the progression of diseases, all which make the information more understandable and easier to remember.

Nevertheless, some challenges remain unresolved like the amount of material or the complexity of pharmacodynamic and pharmacokinetic principles and the pressure of clinical practice. Also, the confidence levels of students vary significantly, with some feeling adequately prepared for clinical application, while others express uncertainty about their information [23]. This variability emphasizes the need for continuous evaluation of curriculum design and teaching methods for nursing students. Therefore, this study aims to investigate the perceptions of 2nd -year nursing students at UQU after completing the IPP course. Ultimately, this study seeks to enhance student understanding and confidence in pathophysiology and pharmacology, which can lead to better translation into clinical practice and improved patient safety.

Methods

Study design and participants

In this research, a cross-sectional quantitative study design was used to evaluate the perceptions of 2nd -year nursing students regarding their understanding of the IPP course, which is delivered through the College of Pharmacy at UQU, Makkah, Saudi Arabia. Data was collected using a self-developed questionnaire (see Supplementary File 1), which was designed based on a comprehensive literature review and refined through expert consultation to ensure content validity and relevance. In addition to assessing internal consistency using Cronbach’s alpha, the instrument underwent a pilot test with a small sample of students to evaluate clarity, structure, and feasibility. Feedback from the pilot informed minor wording and formatting adjustments.

Study sample

The target population of this research included all 2nd -year nursing students who were enrolled in the IPP course during the academic year 2024/2025. We clearly explained the importance of the study and questionnaire to the students before distributing it. The questionnaire was shared online with students during IPP classes, and all students were encouraged to fill it voluntarily; they were assured that their responses would remain anonymous. Out of a total of 236 students (male = 75: 24.7%, females = 161: 75.3%), 150 (63.56%) students participated in the questionnaire.

Data collection

Data were collected only from 2nd year nurses’ students during 2024/2025 academic year. The questionnaire consisted of two main parts: First part: student’s demographics and student’s academic data. Second part: course content and difficulty, general pharmacology, general pathophysiology, and medication management.

Scoring methods

All responses to the questionnaire sections, applied 5-point Likert scales (1 = Strongly disagree, to 5 = Strongly agree), except demographics. The questionnaire demonstrated high internal consistency across subscales (Cronbach’s alpha: 0.833 to 0.910). Domain scores were calculated by adding the item responses within each section, with higher scores representing more positive perceptions or greater understanding. All questionnaire items were fully completed by participants, and no missing data was present in the dataset.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize demographic characteristics, response distributions across Likert-scale items, and domain scores, presented as frequencies and percentages or median with interquartile range (IQR). To assess associations between demographic/academic variables and domain-specific understanding of IPP concepts, the Kruskal–Wallis and Wilcoxon rank sum tests were used for initial inferential analysis. All the demographic and academic characteristics were further analyzed using multivariable linear regression. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. All analyses were conducted using RStudio (version 2024.9.1.394, Boston, MA, USA using R version 4.4.2).

Students’ perceptions of course content and difficulty

Most students agreed or strongly agreed that the amount of material covered in the pharmacology part was manageable (84.0%) and that the integration of pathophysiology and pharmacology was helpful (88.7%). A majority also reported understanding the mechanisms of action of drugs effectively (84.0%) and feeling confident applying pharmacology concepts to clinical scenarios (72.0%). Notably, 62.0% of students agreed or strongly agreed that they could easily remember drug names, side effects, and contraindications, indicating this is the weakest area compared to other metrics. Students’ perceptions of general pharmacology

The majority of students agreed or strongly agreed that they understood the basic principles of pharmacokinetics (94.0%) and pharmacodynamics (86.7%). Approximately 80.6% felt confident explaining the mechanisms of action of common drug classes, while 85.4% understood the factors influencing drug efficacy and toxicity. A total of 88.0% expressed confidence in their general pharmacological knowledge, and 68.0% reported the ability to differentiate between similar drugs within the same class (Fig. 2).Fig. 2Students’ perceptions of general pharmacology

GPA and study hours were significantly associated with higher understanding of general pharmacology. Discussion

Optimizing instructional strategies in health sciences education requires development of creative courses such as integration of foundational biomedical sciences with applied clinical content. This study explored the perceptions of 2nd-year nursing following completion of an IPP course. The study identified both areas where integration succeeds and where targeted reinforcement is still required. Three essential results were found: (1) students report high satisfaction and perceive integration as educationally valuable, and helpful; (2) conceptual understanding in pharmacology, pathophysiology, and medication management is strong; and (3) rote memorization of drug facts (names, adverse effects, contraindications) remains challenging.

Out of 236 students registered on the IPP course, 150 students (63.6%) participated in the questionnaire. Most participants were aged 18 to 23 years (74%) and female (75.3%). Notably, 26% of students were older than 23 years, which represented a cohort of mature learners within the study. While age and gender diversity can impact on learning engagement and comprehension, particularly, in applied or clinically oriented subjects, this study found no significant association between these demographic factors and academic performance across any of the assessed domains. This finding aligns with the work of Peng and Kievit [22], who concluded that cognitive effort and academic performances are better indicators of achievement rather than demographic characteristics [22].

Although 49% of the students reported a moderate level of study engagement, about 51% of them required more than 4 h a week for studying IPP. These findings suggest that while short, concentrated sessions could work well for students with high GPAs, they might not be enough for others who are having learning difficulties and basic clinical fundamentals. Research consistently shows a strong correlation between GPA and perceived competence in pharmacological sciences [21]. However, prior research has shown that longer and sustained study correlates with better retention in complex content areas such as pharmacology and pathophysiology [5, 21]. These findings can be explained through Self-Regulated Learning Theory (SRL) and Cognitive Load Theory. Students with stronger academic foundations tend to plan, monitor, and regulate their learning more effectively, allowing them to benefit from integrative teaching with less external guidance. In contrast, lower-GPA students may have weaker SRL skills and are more prone to mental overload. For this group, focused academic support, such as clear study plans, structured practice sessions, strategy training (e.g., spaced review and frequent recall activities), which can help reduce unnecessary cognitive demands and strengthen learning efficiency. These findings align with Jouhari et al. [11], who identified multiple personal, social, and environmental factors influencing self-regulated learning in medical students, highlighting the importance of structured support to improve learning outcomes [11].

It is noteworthy that students reported varying learning style preferences, with visual learning being most favoured (71.3%), followed by reading/writing (64%), and auditory (60%), while a smaller group (34.7%) preferred kinesthetics’ method. These findings highlight the importance of multimodal instructional design, integrating diagrams, structured texts, audio explanations, and hands-on elements to address cognitive diversity within the classroom.

Given that pharmacology requires memory-intensive studies, effective learning requires more than conceptual understanding alone. Several evidence-based strategies can be implemented to support retention across different learning styles. These include spaced repetition systems, flashcards, mnemonics, dual coding techniques, and low-stakes retrieval practice to strengthen recall. For kinesthetics and experiential learners, simulation-based exercises, medication administration role-plays, and case-based activities can help connect abstract drug facts to meaningful clinical contexts, reducing cognitive load and improving long-term retention. This finding aligns with evidence supporting the use of case studies and flipped classroom strategies in pharmacology and pathophysiology education. A study conducted at Trent University in Canada [19] demonstrated that case-based discussions in a flipped format enhance conceptual understanding, critical reasoning, and application of pharmacological knowledge, moving students beyond basic recall toward clinical decision-making. Such active learning strategies provide meaningful context, reinforcing both understanding and retention of complex drug information [19].

Importantly, the students showed strong comprehension as 86–92% stated a strong confidence in general pharmacology, indicating a solid understanding of core concepts such as pharmacokinetics, pharmacodynamics, and drug mechanisms. While over 91% demonstrated the ability to connect pathophysiology with symptoms and therapeutic choices. Moreover, about 90% expressed confidence in safe medication administration, and 78% could recognize and prevent errors. Interestingly, older students (> 35 years) showed higher scores in this domain, potentially reflecting greater clinical exposure or professional background. This aligns with evidence that medication management benefits more from kinesthetic and experiential learning, such as simulation and clinical placement than from classic methods alone [4]. Furthermore, these results support Knowles’ andragogy principles, emphasizing experiential and self-directed learning for adult learners. Knapke et al. [18] similarly showed that applying andragogy enhances engagement and readiness to learn in biomedical education settings [18]. This observed confidence is particularly relevant to clinical preparation, where stress and resilience are key determinants of performance during clinical placements [28].

Overall, the majority of the students 86% found that combining pathophysiology and pharmacology was appropriate, and 81% found the course content manageable. Also, about 89% demonstrated a high satisfaction rate reflects overall acceptance of the course structure and delivery. Notably, three-quarters showed confidence in applying pharmacological concepts in clinical settings. However, 62.0% of students agreed they could recall drug names, side effects, and contraindication, the lowest score across domains indicating that memorization remains a relatively weak point and requires targeted support. This aligns with prior evidence that students face difficulty with rote memorization when content is not connected in clinical relevance [8]. This challenge reflects a broader issue in nursing education internationally, where limited pharmacology knowledge has been shown to undermine nurses’ confidence in drug administration, patient education, and prescribing, while increasing anxiety in clinical practice [17]. Moreover, this mirrors findings from Shahzeydi et al. [25], who reported that Iranian nurses and students often depend on experience-based rather than evidence-based pharmacological knowledge, highlighting the need for structured, research-informed teaching approaches [25]. Integration appears to mitigate this challenge by enhancing context, aligning with Harden’s SPICES model, which supports student-centered, clinically relevant learning [7, 24].

Limitations and future research

This study relied on self-reported data, which may introduce desirability bias. Additionally, instructional variability (e.g., different lecturers or teaching methods) was not controlled for this study. Because the study was conducted at a single institution, the generalizability of the findings may be limited. Future research should adopt longitudinal designs and mixed methods approaches to explore causal relationships between course design, engagement strategies, and academic outcomes. Evaluating the impact of specific teaching intervention, such as simulation or blended learning, on performance and clinical readiness will be essential for curriculum refinement.

Conclusion

This study highlights the generally positive perceptions of 2nd-year nursing students toward the IPP course at UQU. The integration of pathophysiological and pharmacological principles was perceived to improve conceptual understanding and clinical application. Key factors associated with better student outcomes included higher academic performance and increased study time. Despite these positive findings, challenges persist in areas such as memorization of drug details and clinical confidence among certain student groups. To address these gaps, curriculum refinement should incorporate evidence-based, diversified teaching strategies, including mnemonics, flashcards, spaced repetition, simulation-based learning, and supplementary structured study sessions for lower-GPA students. Continuous evaluation of instructional methods is essential to ensure equitable learning outcomes, strengthen knowledge retention, and improve long-term clinical competence.
