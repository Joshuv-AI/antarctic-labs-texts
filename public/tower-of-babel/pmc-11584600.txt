Tuberculous parotitis: A case series diagnosed on fine needle aspiration
Authors: Sana Ahuja, Shaivy Malik, Sufian Zaheer
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
IntroductionTuberculosis (TB) remains a global health concern with diverse clinical manifestations. Parotid tuberculosis, although rare, presents diagnostic challenges due to its atypical presentation. Cytological examination is pivotal in diagnosing parotid lesions.Case presentationWe present three cases of parotid tuberculosis diagnosed through cytology. A 31-year-old male presented with a painless left parotid swelling. FNAC revealed granulomatous inflammation with AFB positivity. The second case, a 14-year-old female had an enlarging mass in the left parotid. FNAC showed granulomas, confirmed by GeneXpert MTB/RIF assay. The third case, a 39-year-old female presented with a painless swelling in the left parotid. FNAC demonstrated granulomatous inflammation, confirmed by mycobacterial culture.ConclusionCytological examination plays a crucial role in diagnosing parotid tuberculosis. These cases underscore the importance of considering TB in parotid masses. Collaboration among clinicians, radiologists, and cytopathologists is essential for accurate diagnosis and prompt management. Further research is needed to optimize diagnostic algorithms for parotid tuberculosis.

Introduction

Tuberculosis (TB) remains a significant global health challenge, with diverse clinical manifestations that often necessitate meticulous diagnostic approaches. Parotid tuberculosis, albeit rare, presents unique diagnostic dilemmas due to its atypical presentation and resemblance to other benign and malignant conditions of the parotid gland. Salivary glands are thought to possess mechanisms that hinder the growth of tubercular bacilli. This resistance is attributed to the continuous flow of saliva, which prevents the lodging and proliferation of bacilli. Additionally, antibacterial properties are conferred by thiocyanate ions and various proteolytic enzymes. The exact route by which Mycobacterium reaches the parotid gland remains unclear. Involvement of the parotid gland and its lymph nodes can occur via two pathways. Firstly, a focus of mycobacterial infection in the oral cavity, such as infected sputum or tonsillar tissue, may release bacilli that ascend into the salivary gland through its duct or spread to its associated lymph nodes via lymphatic drainage. Alternatively, hematogenous or lymphatic dissemination from a distant primary lung focus may lead to parotid gland involvement. The cytological examination serves as a valuable tool in the armamentarium of diagnosing parotid lesions, offering rapid, minimally invasive, and cost-effective insights. [1], [2] In this case series, we present three intriguing cases of parotid tuberculosis diagnosed through cytological examination according to CARE guidelines, highlighting the utility and nuances of cytology in this context. [3].

Case presentation

The present study included three cases of tuberculous parotitis for which fine needle aspiration cytology was done in the last year. They were aged 14, 31 and 39 years respectively. Clinical symptoms varied from painless infra-auricular swelling and low grade fever and the duration of symptoms varied from 2 to 8 months.

Case 1

A 31 year old male patient presented with a painless swelling in the left infra-auricular region that had gradually increased in size over the past six months. He denied any history of fever, weight loss, or facial nerve involvement. Physical examination revealed a firm, non-tender mass measuring approximately 3 cm in diameter in the left parotid gland region. [Fig. 1a] There were no palpable cervical lymph nodes. No previous history of tuberculosis was present history of contact with a patient of tuberculosis.

Discussion

Cytological examination is a diagnostic technique involving microscopic analysis of cells obtained from a lesion or mass to identify specific pathological features. Fine-needle aspiration cytology is commonly employed in diagnosing parotid tuberculosis. This technique involves the percutaneous insertion of a fine-gauge needle into the parotid gland to aspirate cellular material. The sample is then prepared on slides, stained, and examined microscopically for characteristic cytological features, such as epithelioid histiocytes, multinucleated giant cells, lymphocytes, and, in some cases, caseous necrosis.

Primary tuberculosis of the parotid gland, although rare, presents unique challenges in diagnosis due to its uncommon presentation and similarity to other benign and malignant parotid lesions. Unlike secondary involvement, where the parotid gland becomes affected as a result of hematogenous spread or direct extension from nearby structures, primary parotid tuberculosis arises primarily within the gland itself. This distinction underscores the importance of considering tuberculosis in the differential diagnosis of parotid masses, particularly in regions with a high prevalence of tuberculosis. [1], [2], [4], [5].

Clinically, primary parotid tuberculosis often presents insidiously with nonspecific symptoms such as painless swelling, low-grade fever, and occasionally, facial nerve involvement. These symptoms can easily be misconstrued as manifestations of more common parotid pathologies like neoplasms or chronic sialadenitis. Furthermore, the lack of constitutional symptoms or pulmonary involvement may obscure the diagnosis, leading to delays in appropriate management. Heightened clinical suspicion is paramount, especially in individuals with risk factors for tuberculosis or in regions with endemic tuberculosis prevalence. [6].

Radiologically, primary parotid tuberculosis may present as a solid mass with variable enhancement patterns on contrast-enhanced imaging modalities such as computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). However, distinguishing tuberculosis from other parotid lesions based solely on radiological findings can be challenging, as these imaging features may overlap with those of benign and malignant conditions. Moreover, the absence of typical radiological signs of tuberculosis, such as calcifications or central caseation, further complicates the differentiation process. Thus, radiologists must be vigilant in recognizing subtle imaging clues suggestive of tuberculosis and correlating these findings with clinical and cytological data. [5], [6], [7], [8], [9].

Cytological examination, particularly fine-needle aspiration cytology (FNAC), plays a pivotal role in the diagnostic workup of primary parotid tuberculosis. This minimally invasive technique provides cellular material from the lesion, allowing pathologists to assess for the presence of specific cytological features. The cytological features typically include granulomatous inflammation characterized by the presence of epithelioid histiocytes, multinucleated giant cells, lymphocytes, and caseous necrosis in some instances. However, differentiating primary parotid tuberculosis from other granulomatous lesions based solely on cytology can be challenging, as similar findings may be observed in various infectious and non-infectious conditions. These findings, although non-specific, raised the suspicion of tuberculosis and prompted further confirmatory investigations such as acid-fast bacilli (AFB) staining and molecular tests like GeneXpert MTB/RIF assay, which ultimately confirmed the diagnosis. [5], [6], [7], [8], [9], [10], [11] Thus further confirmation with Ziehl Neelson stain, culture and polymerase chain reaction or cartridge-based nucleic acid amplification test (CBNAAT) is needed. Therefore, careful correlation with clinical and radiological data is essential to establish an accurate diagnosis.

FNA cytology of the parotid gland for tuberculosis diagnosis has shown moderate sensitivity, estimated to range between 60 % and 80 % in the literature. [6] In our present study, fine-needle aspiration cytology of the parotid masses revealed consistent findings across the three cases, including epithelioid cell granulomas, multinucleated giant cells, lymphocytes, and varying degrees of caseous necrosis. These cytological features are characteristic of necrotizing granulomatous inflammation, raising suspicion for tuberculosis.

Histological findings through FNA in confirmed cases of parotid tuberculosis commonly include granulomatous inflammation with varying degrees of caseous necrosis. Fine needle aspiration cytology when compared with the final histopathology, showed a sensitivity of 88.9 %, specificity of 97.9 %, positive predictive value of 93 %, negative predictive value of 96.7 % and diagnostic accuracy of 95.8 %. The overall concordance between the cytology specimen and the final histological diagnosis has been found to be 85 % in parotid gland. [21] Comparing our cytological findings with those reported in previous studies reveals several similarities and nuances. Studies such as those by Yang et al. and Kamal et al. also observed granulomatous inflammation with epithelioid cell granulomas and multinucleated giant cells in FNAC specimens of parotid tuberculosis cases. [4], [5] However, the degree of caseous necrosis and lymphocytic infiltration may vary between cases, as seen in our study. Furthermore, the absence of AFB staining positivity in some cases, as noted in our study and corroborated by others like Gupta et al., underscores the limitations of relying solely on AFB staining for diagnosis. [14] Positive AFB staining is seen in approximately 30 % to 50 % of parotid tuberculosis cases. This highlights the importance of incorporating additional confirmatory tests such as molecular assays like GeneXpert MTB/RIF and culture, as demonstrated in our study and supported by findings from Gupta et al. and Sethi et al. Mycobacterial culture, though time-consuming, is positive in around 70 % to 80 % of cases, providing a definitive diagnosis when other tests are inconclusive. [14], [19].

Overall, while cytological findings in primary parotid tuberculosis often exhibit consistent features such as granulomatous inflammation, the nuances in the extent of necrosis and lymphocytic infiltration, as well as the variability in AFB staining results, emphasize the importance of a comprehensive diagnostic approach integrating cytology with other confirmatory tests for accurate diagnosis and management.

Our approach involved thorough clinical evaluation, imaging studies, fine-needle aspiration cytology, and confirmatory tests such as Ziehl Neelson staining, culture, and molecular assays like GeneXpert MTB/RIF. Compared to previous reports, our study emphasizes the integration of advanced diagnostic modalities alongside traditional techniques, leading to improved accuracy in diagnosing tuberculous parotitis.

Conclusion

In conclusion, primary parotid tuberculosis poses diagnostic challenges due to its rarity and atypical presentation. A high index of suspicion is crucial for early recognition and prompt management, particularly in individuals with risk factors for tuberculosis. Collaboration among clinicians, radiologists, and cytopathologists is imperative for integrating clinical, radiological, and cytological findings to establish an accurate diagnosis and initiate timely anti-tubercular therapy. Further research is warranted to elucidate the diagnostic nuances of primary parotid tuberculosis and optimize diagnostic algorithms in clinical practice.
