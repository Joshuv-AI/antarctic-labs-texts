Hepatitis C virus-related hepatitis flare after immunochemotherapy in a patient with follicular lymphoma
Authors: Yotaro Motomura, Kota Yoshifuji, Keisuke Tanaka, Chizuko Sakashita, Yoshihiro Umezawa, Toshikage Nagao, Sayuri Nitta, Yasuhiro Asahina, Takehiko Mori, Masahide Yamamoto
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Reactivation of hepatitis viruses during chemotherapy can be problematic in the treatment of malignant lymphomas. However, studies on reactivation of chronic hepatitis C virus (HCV) infection are limited. A 43-year-old woman presented with generalized lymphadenopathy and multiple liver tumors, and she was diagnosed with follicular lymphoma (grade 3a; clinical stage IV). Chronic HCV infection was clinically diagnosed. Immunochemotherapy (ICT), including bendamustine and obinutuzumab, was initiated with close liver function monitoring without specific treatment for hepatitis C. However, liver dysfunction worsened 17 days after ICT initiation, and ICT was interrupted. HCV-RNA and transaminase levels continued to elevate. Liver biopsy results confirmed acute exacerbation of chronic hepatitis C. Direct active antiviral (DAA) therapy was started and effective. She has maintained a sustained virologic response since DAA therapy ended. With regard to lymphoma, complete metabolic response was maintained for 4 years without additional treatment. Physicians should be aware of HCV reactivation with hepatitis flare after ICT for lymphoma and consider the indication and timing of DAA therapy for hepatitis C in this setting.

INTRODUCTION

Reactivation of hepatitis B virus (HBV) is a common concern during chemotherapy for hematological malignancies, particularly in malignant lymphoma.1–3 Studies have extensively explored the risk factors for HBV reactivation, including the use of cytotoxic agents, high-dose steroids, and anti-CD20 antibodies.2,3 Although some studies suggest that the risk factors for hepatitis C virus (HCV) reactivation are similar to those for HBV reactivation,4 research on HCV reactivation is limited compared with HBV. Particularly, reports on HCV reactivation after immunochemotherapy (ICT) for indolent lymphoma are scarce,5 and reactivation after obinutuzumab plus bendamustine (GB) therapy, currently considered the standard treatment for follicular lymphoma (FL), has not been documented. In this study, we report a case of rapid HCV reactivation following GB therapy initiation.

CASE REPORT

A 43-year-old woman with a left buccal mass was referred to our hospital due to suspicion of a malignant tumor. Computed tomography (CT) revealed left cervical and abdominal lymphadenopathies, along with multiple liver tumors. A left cervical lymph node biopsy confirmed FL grade 3a. The lymphoma cells were positive for CD10, CD20, and Bcl-2 and negative for CD5. Immunohistochemistry indicated a Ki-67 index of 15%. Positron-emission tomography (PET)/CT revealed involvement on both sides of the diaphragm, including a bulky abdominal lesion and multiple intrahepatic lesions (Figure 1). Bone marrow aspiration and biopsy showed no lymphoma cell infiltration. Laboratory tests showed a white blood cell count of 3,300/μL, hemoglobin of 12.9 g/dL, platelet count of 135,000/μL, lactate dehydrogenase level of 226 U/L, aspartate aminotransferase (AST) level of 78 U/L, alanine aminotransferase (ALT) level of 77 U/L, total bilirubin of 0.4 mg/dL, beta-2 microglobulin of 4.14 mg/dL, and soluble interleukin-2 receptor level of 3,266.9 U/mL. She was diagnosed with Ann Arbor stage IV lymphoma with a high tumor burden, and she was considered high risk based on Follicular Lymphoma International Prognostic Index (FLIPI) and Primary RItuximab and MAintenance-Prognostic Index (PRIMA-PI).

AST and ALT levels were persistently elevated for >6 months, and HCV antibody was positive at the time of lymph node biopsy. She had a family history of HCV infection on the maternal side. Before starting treatment, we assessed her hepatic status. Blood tests showed negative results for HBs antigen, anti-HBs, and anti-HBc antibodies. Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) revealed an HCV-RNA level of 5.1 Log IU/mL, and the HCV genotype was 2a. Serum biomarkers for liver fibrosis were elevated, including hyaluronic acid (58 ng/mL), type IV collagen (230 ng/mL), and Mac-2 binding protein glycan isomer (2.98 C.O.I.). Therefore, we diagnosed the patient with chronic hepatitis C and consulted a hepatology specialist. We decided to initiate ICT with close liver function monitoring and planned to treat hepatitis C after completing ICT.

Initially, the patient received bendamustine (90 mg/m2, days 1 and 2), followed by obinutuzumab (1,000 mg, day 8) after the absence of signs of tumor lysis syndrome and hepatitis exacerbation was confirmed. However, on day 10, skin rash and systemic edema appeared, requiring the administration of prednisolone (20 mg/day for 6 days) and leading to the postponement of the scheduled obinutuzumab on day 15. After the skin rash resolved, AST and ALT levels gradually increased. Due to suspicion of drug-induced liver injury (DILI), possible drugs (i.e., lansoprazole, febuxostat, and trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole) were discontinued. However, the ALT level increased to > 400 U/L on day 29, and the HCV-RNA level increased to 6.2 Log IU/mL. Figure 2 shows the clinical course, including liver function tests and lymphocyte count. Regarding FL, she achieved partial response (PR) during treatment interruption (day 31) and remained in PR without additional treatment on day 74. However, liver dysfunction with HCV flare worsened, and we discontinued ICT and assessed the status of liver histology. Subsequently, liver biopsy results revealed acute exacerbation of chronic hepatitis with bridging fibrosis and moderate activity of necro-inflammatory reaction (Figure 3). Thus, the histological diagnosis of chronic hepatitis was F2A2 based on the New Inuyama Classification.

Based on the results, we diagnosed an HCV-related hepatitis flare and initiated direct-acting antiviral (DAA) therapy consisting of sofosbuvir and ledipasvir. ALT and HCV-RNA levels decreased immediately after therapy initiation. DAA therapy was administered for 3 months. At the end of DAA therapy, qRT-PCR did not detect HCV-RNA, and she maintained a sustained virologic response thereafter. Furthermore, she achieved a complete metabolic response after 1 year of ICT and has maintained the response at 4 years post-ICT.

DISCUSSION

Reactivation of the hepatitis virus after chemotherapy is well known in hepatitis B, and the use of cytotoxic agents, steroids, and anti-CD20 antibodies poses a risk.2,3 The Japanese guideline on hepatitis B describes an algorithm for monitoring during chemotherapy, identifying drugs, such as obinutuzumab and bendamustine, as high-risk factors for reactivation.6 In contrast, the latest Japanese guideline for hepatitis C recommends universal HCV screening in patients with hematological malignancy before chemotherapy. However, hepatitis C reactivation during chemotherapy has not been sufficiently discussed.7

Although FL lesions are rare in the liver, a report on primary hepatic FL indicated that many cases are positive for hepatitis viruses,8 suggesting that the liver involvement in this case may be related to HCV infection. Furthermore, the patient initially achieved only PR after GB therapy, but CR was attained following HCV elimination. As both HCV and lymphoma responses were sustained after achieving CR, HCV infection may be related with lymphoma development.

Next, hepatotoxicity often occurs in patients with HCV infection within the first month after chemotherapy initiation,9 and the possible causes are DILI and HCV reactivation. Our case showed worsened liver dysfunction based on elevated HCV-RNA levels even after drug discontinuation. Furthermore, liver function normalized, and HCV-RNA levels decreased after DAA therapy, indicating HCV reactivation as the cause but not DILI. The liver biopsy result was also consistent with acute exacerbation of chronic viral hepatitis, supporting this finding. Reports on HCV-RNA positive patients indicate that the rates of HCV reactivation and the incidence of HCV-related hepatitis flare (defined as an increase in ALT levels to three times the upper limit of normal) were 27% and 44%, respectively, in retrospective studies and 23% and 43%, respectively, in prospective studies.4,10 These findings indicate that the incidence of HCV-related hepatitis flare is not necessarily low. Recently, M2BPG has emerged as a valuable marker for detecting liver damage related to HCV reactivation.11 In our case, liver dysfunction worsened, and M2BPG levels increased in parallel with rising HCV-RNA levels, which subsequently declined following HCV elimination. These findings indicate that M2BPG is an effective marker for monitoring disease progression in HCV-related hepatitis. Notably, no cases of liver decompensation or HCV reactivation-related death were observed early after treatment.4,9–11

Conversely, some patients required treatment interruption due to hepatitis flare, and most deaths were attributed to the exacerbation of the underlying disease. Chemotherapy-associated HCV reactivation impacts the treatment of the underlying disease and has prognostic relevance. Therefore, understanding which drugs pose a high risk for reactivation is crucial. Similar to HBV reactivation, chemotherapy for hematological malignancies poses a risk for HCV reactivation. Notably, R-CHOP therapy in HCV antibody-positive patients is associated with severe hepatic toxicity in 6 of 131 patients (4.6%).12 Rituximab, purine analogs, and bendamustine are considered high-risk agents,4 and it is anticipated that obinutuzumab, as an anti-CD20 antibody similar to rituximab, may also be a high-risk agent. Both rituximab and obinutuzumab can potentially reactivate HBV,13 and while the impact of obinutuzumab on HCV reactivation is not well-documented, reactivation is observed in 56% of patients treated with rituximab.4 Although the difference in the extent of risk is unclear, since both drugs operate through similar mechanisms, obinutuzumab is also considered a high-risk agent and may have had an adverse effect in the present case. Regarding cytotoxic agents, CHOP therapy increases hepatic toxicity and HCV-RNA levels, as noted in a study by Ennishi et al.12 However, the effects of bendamustine are not well understood. According to reports on chemotherapy in HCV-RNA positive patients, reactivation occurred in 15%–45% of patients receiving cytotoxic agents included in CHOP therapy, whereas all five patients treated with bendamustine experienced reactivation. Moreover, the risk of viral infection appears to be higher with bendamustine than with other chemotherapy regimens, suggesting that it may have a greater impact on HCV reactivation than in regimens such as CHOP.4 These findings indicate that obinutuzumab combined with bendamustine, which was used in our case, poses high-risk of HCV reactivation.

The treatment algorithm for HCV infection during chemotherapy has not been established. However, Li et al. recently proposed an algorithm recommending DAA therapy for cancer patients with HCV infection.14 A prospective observational study has reported the effectiveness of DAA therapy.14,15 Because HCV eradication is effective against extrahepatic manifestations, including lymphoma, DAA therapy is also recommended before chemotherapy.14,16 Our case maintained a long-term complete response after only one course of GB therapy, indicating the positive effect of HCV eradication on lymphoma. Although the combination of chemotherapy with DAA therapy should be considered in aggressive lymphoma complicated with HCV infection,14 no definitive evidence currently supports this approach and an accumulation of such experiences is required.
