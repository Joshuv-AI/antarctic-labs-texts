In situ Synthesis of Amorphous Fluorinated Calcium Phosphate Nanoparticles via a Dual-Component Hydrogel for Biomimetic Tooth Remineralization
Authors: Zhixin Zhang, Zhe Wang, Jiangling Su, Qi Yang, Zihan Cui, Xiaoping Tang, Baiping Fu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo report the in situ synthesis of amorphous fluorinated calcium phosphate (AFCP) nanoparticles within a dual-component hydrogel and evaluate their efficacy for enamel remineralization and dentinal tubules (DTs) occlusion.Materials and MethodsThe AFCP hydrogel was constructed using hydroxypropyl methylcellulose (HPMC) and carboxymethyl chitosan (CMC) to stabilize calcium, phosphate, and fluoride ions in a metastable amorphous state. Physicochemical characterization was performed by FTIR, XRD, TEM, and SEM-EDX. Biocompatibility was assessed via CCK-8 assays and oral mucosal irritation test. Demineralized enamel and dentin specimens (n=8) were treated with deionized water, fluoride (NaF, 2000 ppm F), 10% CPP-ACP with 900 ppm F (GC Tooth Mousse Plus, Japan), or AFCP hydrogel, and immersed in artificial saliva for 7 days. Remineralization was evaluated by SEM, XRD, and nanoindentation, followed by in vivo validation in a rabbit model.ResultsThe hydrogel generated AFCP nanoparticles (30–80 nm) that remained amorphous in artificial saliva for over 2 hours before crystallizing into hydroxyapatite. The hydrogel exhibited negligible cytotoxicity and no oral mucosal irritation. After 7 days, the AFCP hydrogel induced dense mineral deposition that completely occluded exposed DTs and formed a ~32 µm remineralized layer on acid-etched enamel, substantially outperforming fluoride and CPP-ACP + NaF. XRD confirmed the newly formed mineral as hydroxyapatite-like. Treatment with the AFCP hydrogel restored the mechanical properties of enamel and dentin to near-physiological levels, achieving the greatest recovery among the four groups (P < 0.05). In vivo results further validated its remineralization and tubule-occluding efficacy.ConclusionThe AFCP hydrogel functions as an exogenous mineral reservoir, enabling sustained ion release or direct transformation into hydroxyapatite for in situ enamel remineralization and DTs occlusion. This non-invasive strategy offers a promising therapeutic approach for early caries and dentin hypersensitivity.Keywords: amorphous fluorinated calcium phosphate, enamel remineralization, dentinal tubule occlusion, mineralizing hydrogel

Introduction

Dental caries affects nearly 2.5 billion permanent and 573 million primary dentitions worldwide, representing a major global health burden.1 Beyond local tooth destruction, caries impairs quality of life and elevates the risk of systemic comorbidities.2,3 Untreated carious lesions can progress to severe pain, pulp infection, and eventual tooth loss, leading to irreversible masticatory dysfunction and psychosocial morbidity.2 Thus, strategies that arrest caries progression and enable effective prevention are of paramount clinical importance.

Enamel and dentin are the most highly mineralized tissues in the human body and consist predominantly of hydroxyapatite (HAp) crystallites.4,5 Their intricate hierarchical micro- and nano-architecture underpins exceptional mechanical resilience and a robust barrier function.4,6 However, cariogenic microorganisms ferment dietary carbohydrates into organic acids, which demineralize these hard tissues by dissolving calcium (Ca2+) and phosphate (PO43-) ions.5 Notably, enamel is an acellular tissue and thus intrinsically incapable of self-repair once damage.4 Furthermore, exposure of dentinal tubules (DTs) elicits hydrodynamic fluid shifts within the tubule lumen, activating intradental A-β and A-δ nerve fibers and triggering the symptoms of dentin hypersensitivity (DH).7 Consequently, occluding exposed DTs constitutes a principal therapeutic objective in the management of DH. Various commercial desensitizing agents and varnishes, including Gluma Desensitizer, Duraphat, and MI Varnish, are routinely employed in clinical practice to achieve tubule occlusion.8,9 Caries progression is a highly dynamic process characterized by cyclical episodes of demineralization and remineralization. As the principal inorganic phase of dental hard tissues, calcium phosphate underpins the antagonistic balance between demineralization and remineralization of enamel and dentin, driven by the dissolution and reprecipitation of Ca2+ and PO43- ions.5,10 Therefore, caries lesion arrest or reversal is achievable at any stage by enriching the local microenvironment with bioavailable Ca2+, PO43-, and F−.

Topical fluoride application has long been the cornerstone of caries prevention.11,12 However, fluoride-based systems primarily drive fluorapatite (FAp) formation and show limited capacity to replenish lost mineral volume.13–15 In the absence of sufficient bioavailable Ca2+ and PO43-, the rapid nucleation and growth of HAp crystallites on demineralized tooth surfaces are severely hindered. Moreover, fluoride agents typically penetrate poorly into deep carious lesions, compromising remineralization within the lesion body.16 Additionally, excessive systemic or topical fluoride exposure raises legitimate concerns regarding potential toxicity.17

Biomimetic remineralization, inspired by natural biomineralization, offers a promising route to functionally restore dental hard tissues.18,19 It typically involves the infiltration of demineralized regions with amorphous calcium phosphate (ACP) nanoprecursors, stabilized by non-collagenous proteins (NCPs) or synthetic polymers. Under saliva or simulated body fluid (SBF), these metastable precursors undergo directed transformation into organized mineral phases that recapitulate the hierarchical enamel and dentin architecture.19–21 This process mirrors the native matrix-mediated mineralization, where inorganic ions deposit in an ordered fashion under organic templates ranging from small molecules to macromolecular proteins. Building on these biological paradigms, a range of biomimetic materials analogous to natural NCPs—including poly(aspartic acid) (PASP), poly(acrylic acid) (PAA), casein phosphopeptide (CPP), and carboxymethyl chitosan (CMC)—have been exploited to stabilize ACP nanoprecursors and direct their crystallization into HAp.22–25 ACP, as the primary precursor for biological HAp deposition, features high bioactivity and sustained release of bioavailable Ca2+ and PO43- ions.22,26,27 This principle has been clinically implemented by incorporating ACP into toothpastes, dental creams, composite resins, and adhesive systems, with CPP-ACP paste as a prime example for enamel remineralization.28–32 As a liquid-phase mineralization precursor, ACP is intrinsically metastable with a strong thermodynamic driving force toward crystallization into more stable calcium phosphate phases.22 Preserving its amorphous state therefore requires sufficient nucleation inhibitors, posing formidable challenges for long-term shelf-life stability. Nevertheless, embedding dry ACP nanoprecursors within carriers such as adhesives or mesoporous silica severely restricts their translational mobility.30 Moreover, the bioactivity of solid-state ACP is often compromised by disruption of its intrinsic hydration layer, which is essential for facilitating ion release and mineral deposition.33,34 These limitations collectively underscore the critical need to develop alternative strategies that preserve both the amorphous character and the inherent mineralization potential of ACP precursors.

Hydrogels, with their three-dimensional crosslinked networks, high water content, and facile functionalizability, have emerged as ideal carriers for remineralization agents.35,36 They effectively recapitulate key features of the native extracellular matrix (ECM), providing a spatially confined microenvironment conducive to regulated mineral deposition. Moreover, the ordered arrangement of functional groups or the network architecture can direct crystallite orientation.35,37 For instance, carboxymethyl chitosan (CMC), a water-soluble chitosan derivative, possesses abundant carboxyl, hydroxyl, and amino groups that strongly bind Ca2+ and PO43- ions, effectively lowering the nucleation energy barrier and stabilizing the metastable ACP phase.38,39

Moreover, hydrogels function as diffusive barriers that markedly retard mineral ion transport to reaction sites, enabling controlled mineralization kinetics. In our previous work, we synthesized amorphous fluorinated calcium phosphate (AFCP) nanoparticles ex situ via a solution precipitation and incorporated them into a hydroxypropyl methylcellulose (HPMC) matrix to form a mineralizing film.40,41 Upon hydration, HPMC forms a cohesive gel network that sustains release of AFCP nanoparticles and their constituent ions (Ca2+, PO43-, and F−), achieving effective remineralization of enamel caries lesions. However, clinical translation of this film is hindered by three key challenges: (i) thermodynamic instability of AFCP nanoparticles, compromising long-term storage and sustained mineralization activity; (ii) suboptimal conformability of the film on tooth surfaces; and (iii) unproven occlusion of DTs.

To address these limitations, we present a paradigm shift from ex situ nanoparticles synthesis and loading to an in situ formation strategy. Specifically, we develop a dual-component hydrogel system consisting of an HPMC-calcium phase and a CMC-phosphate/fluoride phase, enabling direct in situ synthesis of AFCP nanoparticles within the hydrogel matrix. This biphasic design offers three key advantages over our previous approach: (1) Ca2+, PO43-, and F− ions are preserved in a non-crystalline, pre-nucleation state during storage, thereby ensuring long-term shelf stability; and (2) AFCP nanoparticles are generated in situ upon mixing of the two hydrogel components immediately, enabling true chairside applicability without reliance on pre-synthesized nanoparticles; and (3) the injectable, moldable hydrogel form conforms intimately to tooth topography, facilitating enamel remineralization and deep DTs occlusion in an operator-friendly procedure. We therefore hypothesize that this biphasic design maintains ionic precursors in a metastable state before use to prevent premature precipitation, and that upon mixing, in-situ generated AFCP nanoparticles will promote both enamel remineralization and DTs occlusion. This study aims to validate these hypotheses, thereby demonstrating that engineered HPMC-CMC polysaccharide interactions can stabilize the metastable ACP phase and offering an alternative therapeutic strategy for the clinical management of enamel caries and dentin hypersensitivity.

Materials and Methods

Materials

Calcium chloride (CaCl2) and 2-[4-(2-hydroxyethyl)-1-piperazinyl] ethanesulfonic acid (HEPES) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (Shanghai, China). Disodium hydrogen phosphate (Na2HPO4), sodium fluoride (NaF), sodium hydroxide (NaOH), dipotassium hydrogen phosphate (K2HPO4), potassium dihydrorogen phosphate (KH2PO4), chloramine T and hydroxypropyl methylcellulose (HPMC) were obtained from Aladdin (Shanghai, China). Potassium chloride (KCl) and poly-L-aspartic acid (PASP, Mw = 7000−8000 Da) were sourced from Macklin (Shanghai, China). Carboxymethyl chitosan (CMC) was acquired from Feiyu Biological Technology Co., Ltd. (Nantong, China). Deionized water was used throughout the study, and all solutions were filtered through 0.22-μm syringe filters (Millipore) prior to use.

Preparation and Characterization of the AFCP Nanoparticles

The dual-component hydrogel was prepared following the procedure described below. (i) For the preparation of Hydrogel A, 0.444 g of CaCl2 and 0.192 g of PASP were dissolved completely in 10 mL of deionized water. Subsequently, 0.3 g of HPMC powder was added into calcium-containing solution under magnetic stirring to allow full swelling. (ii) For the preparation of Hydrogel B, 0.341 g of Na2HPO4 and 20 mg of NaF were dissolved in 10 mL of deionized water, and the pH of the resulting phosphate solution was adjusted to 11.5 ± 0.5 using 5 M NaOH. Then, 0.5 g of CMC powder was introduced into the phosphate solution under magnetic stirring until fully swollen. Hydrogels A and B were subsequently mixed at a Ca/P molar ratio of 1.67, immediately generating an AFCP hydrogel rich in AFCP nanoparticles. The two precursor hydrogels can be stored separately at ambient temperature for future use. The as-synthesized AFCP nanoparticles were characterized by Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR, Nicolet iS10, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), X-ray diffraction (XRD, Bruker D8 Advance, Bruker, Germany), transmission electron microscopy (TEM, JEM-1230, JEOL, Tokyo, Japan), and scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Zeiss Gemini SEM 300, Oberkochen, Germany) equipped with energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX).

Phase Transition of AFCP Nanoparticles in Artificial Saliva

Artificial saliva (1.5 mM CaCl2, 0.9 mM K2HPO4, 130 mM KCl, 1 mM NaN3, and 20 mM HEPES buffer, pH 7.02) was prepared following a previously reported protocol.42 Freshly prepared AFCP hydrogel (2 mL) was incubated with 20 mL of artificial saliva at 37 °C for varying durations (0, 4, 8, and 24 h). Following incubation, a drop of the supernatant was applied to a gold mesh, blotted with filter paper, and rapidly vitrified in liquid ethane using a liquid nitrogen-cooled workstation. The morphological evolution of the AFCP nanoparticles was then examined using cryo-transmission electron microscopy (cryo-TEM, Talos F200C, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). For FTIR analysis, AFCP hydrogel samples incubated with artificial saliva at 37 °C for 0, 2, 4, 8, and 24 h were collected by centrifugation, dried at 70 °C, and subsequently analyzed. The phase transition of the AFCP nanoparticles in artificial saliva was evaluated using the splitting function (SF) proposed by Posner.43

Biosafety Evaluation

L929 cells and human gingival fibroblasts (HGFs) were commercially purchased from Wuhan Sunncell Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China). Cells were seeded into sterile 96-well plates at a density of 1×104 cells per well in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM, Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco, USA) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin for L929 cells, or with 15% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin for HGFs, and pre-cultured at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere for 24 h. After the pre-culture, the medium was removed and replaced with fresh medium containing sterilized AFCP hydrogel dispersed at various concentrations (0.5, 1, 2, 4, and 8 mg/mL). The medium for L929 cells contained 10% FBS, while that for HGFs contained 15% FBS; both were supplemented with 1% penicillin/streptomycin. The cells were then incubated for 1, 3, 5, and 7 days, with the medium (including the dispersed hydrogel) refreshed 2–3 days. At each time point, the culture medium was gently removed, and 100 µL of medium containing 10% CCK-8 solution (CCK-8, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) was added to each well, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 2 h. The absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (BioTek Eon, USA).

For oral mucous membrane irritation assay, six adult male golden hamsters (approval number: ZJU20240712) weighing 110–130 g were used. Under anesthesia, a cotton ball (approximately 5 mm in diameter) saturated with freshly prepared AFCP hydrogel was placed into one cheek pouch of each animal, while a cotton ball soaked in sterile 0.9% NaCl was placed into the contralateral pouch as a control. The cotton balls remained in contact with the pouch mucosa for 15 min. After removal of the cotton balls, any inflammatory responses on the pouch surface were assessed macroscopically. Tissue samples from representative areas of the cheek pouches were excised, sectioned, and stained with hematoxylin-eosin (HE) for histological examination. The microscopic irritant effects on the oral tissues were then evaluated.

Tooth Specimen Preparation

Ethical approval for this study was granted the Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Hospital of Stomatology, Zhejiang University (approval number: 2024–139), and the study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Thirty-two isolated human premolars were collected from patients aged 18–30 years for orthodontic reasons with informed consent. Only intact teeth free of caries, restorations, or structural defects were included. Enamel and dentin blocks (4 × 3×1.5 mm3) were prepared using a water-cooled low-speed Isomet diamond saw. Subsequent grinding and polishing were performed under running water using silicon carbide paper of progressively finer grit (600 to 3000). All surfaces except a defined window of 3×2 mm2 were coated with two layers of acid-resistant nail varnish. The prepared specimens were stored in 0.616% (w/v) chloramine-T solution at 4 °C until further use.

In vitro Enamel Remineralization and Dentinal Tubule (DT) Occlusion

To create artificial demineralization models, the prepared enamel and dentin specimens were first etched with 37% phosphoric acid for 30s, rinsed thoroughly with deionized water, and then ultrasonicated cleaned for 3 min to remove residual debris. The demineralized surfaces were randomly assigned to four groups (n = 8 per group) and uniformly coated using a microbrush with one of the following treatments: 100 μL of freshly prepared AFCP hydrogel; Tooth Mousse Plus (10% CPP-ACP, 900 ppm F; GC, Tokyo, Japan); a sodium fluoride solution (NaF, 2000 ppm F), or deionized water (blank control). All specimens were then incubated in 5 mL of artificial saliva at 37 °C. This coating-incubation cycle was repeated daily for 7 days, with the artificial saliva refreshed each day. After incubation, the specimens were repeatedly rinsed with deionized water and air-dried. Surface micromorphology was examined using SEM, and the crystalline phase of the newly formed minerals were analyzed by XRD. Nanomechanical properties, including elastic modulus (Er) and hardness (H), were evaluated using a Berkovich indenter (Hysitron TI980, Bruker, Germany) under a maximum load of 15 mN. Ten indentations were performed per specimen, and the results were recorded as load–displacement curves. All SEM and nanoindentation assessments were conducted by two independent investigators blinded to group allocation, who performed sample preparation, instrument operation, and data analysis. Sample size (n = 8 per group) was determined based on previous studies employing similar in vitro remineralization models, in which this number was sufficient to detect statistically significant differences in surface micromorphology and nanomechanical properties.40

Animal Experiments

All animal procedures were complied with the National Research Council’s Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals, and were approved by the Laboratory Animal Welfare and Ethics Review Committee of Zhejiang University (Approval No. ZJU20240712). Six healthy male New Zealand rabbits (12 weeks old, body weight 2.1–2.5 kg) were included in this study. Demineralized enamel and dentin surfaces were prepared as follows: First, general anesthesia was induced by intravenous injection of 30 g/L pentobarbital sodium at a dose of 0.2 mL/100 g body weight. Following anesthesia, silicone rubber impressions of the maxillary incisors were obtained, stone casts were prepared, and transparent polymeric retainers were fabricated via thermoforming. The incisor surfaces were then polished with a low-speed handpiece and demineralized by etching with 37% phosphoric acid, following the same protocol as for the in vitro specimens. The experiment comprised two groups: the experimental group, in which the demineralized enamel and dentin surfaces were coated with 100 µL of AFCP hydrogel, protected by retainers for 8 h daily, and then exposed to the natural oral cavity; and the control group, in which the demineralized surfaces were directly exposed to the oral cavity without any remineralizing agent. Throughout the study, the rabbits were fed a powdered diet to minimize mechanical abrasion. After 7 days, the rabbits were euthanized via intravenous pentobarbital overdose, and the central incisors were harvested for SEM analysis.

Statistical Analysis

All of the data were presented as the means ± standard deviations (SDs). Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 24.0 software (IBM, Armonk, NY). Normality of data distribution and homogeneity of variances were assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk and Levene’s test, respectively. A one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s HSD post hoc test was then conducted. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Results

Characterization of the AFCP Hydrogel

The Hydrogel A and Hydrogel B were stored separately and could be mixed at a Ca/P molar ratio of 1.67 to generate AFCP nanoparticles in situ (Figure 1a). FTIR spectra of the individual hydrogels and the resulting AFCP hydrogel are presented in Figure 1b. Hydrogel A exhibited characteristic peaks at 3354 cm−1 (O-H stretching vibration), 2930 cm−1 (C-H stretching), 1628 cm−1 (amide I band, C=O stretching of PASP carboxylates), 1420 cm−1 (symmetric C=O stretching vibration), and 1050 cm−1 (C-O stretching vibration).44 These peaks suggest that Ca2+ ions coordinate with the carboxylate groups of PASP to form crosslinking networks, while HPMC provides a supporting hydroxyl-rich network.45,46 Hydrogel B displayed bands at 3218 cm−1 (broad overlapping O-H and N-H stretching vibrations), 1654 cm−1 (amide I band, C=O stretching of CMC), 1420 cm−1 (symmetric C=O stretching), 1079 (overlapping C-O and P=O stretching), and 988 cm−1 (P-O-C stretching).47 The distinct peak at 988 cm−1 indicates that Na2HPO4 has undergone chemical crosslinking (phosphorylation) with CMC rather than merely being physically mixed. The AFCP hydrogel showed bands at 3447, 2930, 1601, 1420, 1321, 1069, and 563 cm−1. Compared with Hydrogel A (3354 cm−1) and Hydrogel B (3218 cm−1), the broad O-H/N-H envelope shifted to 3447 cm−1, and the amide I band shifted from 1628/1654 cm−1 to 1601 cm−1. XRD analysis of the AFCP hydrogel showed only diffuse halos centered at ~20°and ~30°, with no sharp diffraction peaks (Figure 1c). TEM images revealed spherical nanoparticles, and the corresponding SAED pattern exhibited no discrete spots or rings, confirming the amorphous nature (Figure 1d). SEM micrographs displayed uniformly distributed spherical aggregates with diameters of 30–80 nm (Figure 1e). Elemental mapping verified the presence of C, N, O, F, P, and Ca throughout the particles (Figure 1f). Quantitative EDS analysis revealed a weight percent (wt%) composition of 27.02% C, 2.15% N, 32.80% O, 1.43% F, 11.08% P, and 25.51% Ca, corresponding to a Ca/P atomic ratio of 1.78 (Table 1).

Phase Stability of AFCP Nanoparticles in Artificial Saliva

The morphological evolution of AFCP nanoparticles immersed in artificial saliva was followed by cryo-TEM. Initially, AFCP nanoparticles remained spherical and non-aggregated (Figure 2A(i)). After 4 h, particle size increased and adjacent particles began to aggregate (Figure 2A(ii)). At 8 h, most spherical particles had transformed into needle-like structures (Figure 2A(iii)), and by 24 h the transformation to acicular crystals was nearly complete (Figure 2A(iv)). FTIR spectra acquired during immersion showed a broad envelope between 560 and 600 cm−1 within the first 2 h (Figure 2B). After 4 h, this band split into a doublet, indicating the formation of crystalline HAp.48 After 24 h, the splitting function (SF) approached 1, signifying complete crystallization (Figure 2C and D).

Biosafety Evaluation of the AFCP Hydrogel

CCK-8 assays were performed on L929 cells and human gingival fibroblasts (HGFs) exposed to AFCP hydrogel extracts (0.5–8 mg/mL) for 1, 3, 5, and 7 days. No significant reduction in cell proliferation was observed for either cell type at any concentration or time point relative to the untreated control (Figure 3). In the oral mucosal irritation test conducted according to YY/T 0127.13–2018, histological examination of the epithelium and lamina propria revealed normal tissue architecture, with no signs of hyperplasia, edema, inflammatory infiltration, or necrosis.

In vitro Enamel Remineralization

Enamel surfaces etched with 37% phosphoric acid for 30s displayed a characteristic fish-scale morphology with exposed rod structures and widened intercrystalline spaces (Figure 4A and B). After 7 days of immersion in artificial saliva, both the blank control and the fluoride-treated groups showed minimal mineral deposition, and the rod structures remained markedly exposed (Figure 4C–F). The CPP-ACP + NaF group exhibited partial improvement: intercrystalline gaps were reduced and mineral deposits increased (Figure 4G and H). In contrast, the AFCP hydrogel-treated group featured a dense, continuous layer of newly formed minerals that completely covered the enamel rods (Figure 4I and J), with a remineralized layer thickness of approximately 32 µm (Figure 4K). XRD patterns of the remineralized enamel showed characteristic HAp peaks at 2θ = 25.8° (002), 31.8° (211), 34.1° (202), 49.5° (213), and 53.2° (004), matching those of sound enamel (Figure 4L). Nanoindentation test revealed that acid-etched enamel had a significantly greater indentation depth (~0.74 µm) than sound enamel (~0.34 µm), and the depth decreased sequentially in the order: blank control > fluoride > CPP-ACP + NaF > AFCP hydrogel (Figure 4M). The corresponding elastic modulus (Er) and hardness (H) values are summarized in Figure 4N. Sound enamel showed Er = 120.51 ± 11.03 GPa and H = 4.74 ± 0.51 GPa; acid etching reduced them to 50.96 ± 8.88 GPa and 0.78 ± 0.10 GPa. After 7 days of remineralization, the blank control gave Er = 68.01 ± 11.35 GPa and H = 0.98 ± 0.31 GPa. Fluoride treatment yielded Er = 69.98 ± 8.68 GPa and H = 1.39 ± 0.44 GPa, while CPP-ACP + NaF gave Er = 77.64 ± 13.00 GPa and H = 1.15 ± 0.12 GPa. The AFCP hydrogel group achieved Er = 92.22 ± 8.28 GPa and H = 2.10 ± 0.09 GPa, corresponding to ~77% of sound enamel Er and ~44% of sound enamel H. The AFCP hydrogel group exhibited the greatest recovery in mechanical properties among the four groups (P < 0.05).

In vitro Dentinal Tubules Occlusion

Dentin surfaces etched with 37% phosphoric acid for 30s showed completely exposed dentinal tubules (DTs) with a characteristic demineralized morphology (Figure 5A–D). After 7 days in artificial saliva, both the control and fluoride-treated groups exhibited minimal morphological change; DTs remained widely open with only minor non-occlusive mineral deposition (Figure 5E–H and 5I–L). The CPP-ACP + NaF group showed more substantial mineral deposition on the dentin surface and a slight reduction in tubule diameter, though DTs remained clearly visible (Figure 5M–P). In the AFCP hydrogel-treated group, DTs were almost completely occluded and the dentin surface was largely covered by newly formed minerals (Figure 5Q–T). Cross-sectional imaging confirmed dense intratubular mineralization extending to a depth exceeding 3 µm beneath the surface (Figure 5S and T). XRD patterns of native dentin exhibited HAp peaks at 2θ = 25.8° (002), 31.8° (211), 34.1° (202), 49.5° (213), and 53.5° (004). These peaks were absent in demineralized dentin surfaces, indicating damage to the mineral crystal structure following demineralization. Following AFCP hydrogel treatment, the XRD pattern regained peaks consistent with those of native dentin (Figure 5U). Nanoindentation analysis showed that indentation depth was minimal for native dentin and maximal for demineralized dentin, with the AFCP hydrogel group exhibiting a depth almost comparable to that of native tissue (Figure 5V). Quantitatively, native dentin had Er = 21.86 ± 0.89 GPa and H = 0.84 ± 0.03 GPa. Acid etching reduced these to 6.12 ± 0.53 GPa and 0.17 ± 0.03 GPa. After remineralization, blank control values were Er = 7.67 ± 1.26 GPa, H = 0.20 ± 0.06 GPa; fluoride group values were Er = 9.40 ± 1.15 GPa, H = 0.22 ± 0.05 GPa; CPP-ACP + NaF treatment gave Er = 10.77 ± 2.40 GPa, H = 0.36 ± 0.09 GPa. The AFCP hydrogel group achieved Er = 17.23 ± 2.36 GPa and H = 0.63 ± 0.12 GPa, significantly higher than all other treatment groups (P < 0.05), corresponding to ~80% of the Er and ~75% of the H of native dentin (Figure 5W).

In vivo Evaluation in a Rabbit Model

An in vivo study using a rabbit model was conducted to assess clinical translatability (Figure 6). The rabbits’ oral environment provides a clinically relevant milieu, containing ions and enzymes similar to those in humans. Following a protocol analogous to the in vitro experiments, the demineralized enamel and dentin surfaces were either left untreated (blank control) or treated with the AFCP hydrogel under a retainer for 8 h daily over 7 days. In the control group, demineralized enamel exhibited persistent coarse surfaces with clearly visible intercrystalline spaces (Figure 6I and J). AFCP hydrogel-treated enamel displayed a smooth, homogeneous surface with newly formed minerals densely filling the intercrystalline gaps (Figure 6M and N). These results indicate the hydrogel’s efficacy for clinical enamel repair. Similarly, control demineralized dentin showed patent DTs (Figure 6K and L), while AFCP hydrogel-treated dentin featured a distinct mineralized layer and effective tubular occlusion (Figure 6O and P).

Discussion

Biomimetic mineralization has seen notable advances, yet two fundamental challenges persist. First, true enamel repair requires recreating its highly organized prismatic architecture.49 Second, dentin regeneration demands mineralization within collagen fibrils.50 Existing clinical agents, such as fluoride, act solely on the collagen surface to occlude tubules externally, unable to access intrafibrillar compartments. ACP is a promising nanoprecursor, but its inherent thermodynamic instability drives premature crystallization into HAp.33 This early transition prevents ACP from infiltrating deep lesions or penetrating the intrafibrillar spaces of collagen. The field thus confronts a critical dilemma: how to stabilize ACP sufficiently to prolong its functional lifetime while maintaining dimensions small enough for nanoscale infiltration.

The two-component hydrogel system reported herein enables the in situ synthesis of AFCP nanoparticles within a hybrid polysaccharide network. As shown in Figure 1, FTIR spectroscopic analysis revealed that upon mixing Hydrogel A and Hydrogel B, the amide I band shifts markedly from 1628 cm−1 (PASP carboxylate–Ca2⁺) and 1654 cm−1 (CMC carboxylate) to 1601 cm−1, suggesting an altered coordination environment of carboxylate groups,51 while a broad O–H/N–H envelope emerges at higher wavenumber, implying a weakened hydrogen bonding network.52 These shifts indicated that Ca2+ ions may partially dissociate from the carboxylate groups of Hydrogel A and instead interact with the phosphate groups supplied by Hydrogel B, yielding an organic–inorganic hybrid scaffold in which carboxylate–Ca2⁺–phosphate coordination bridges the two polymer networks.45–47 The appearance of characteristic phosphate ʋ3 and ʋ4 bands at 1069 and 563 cm−1 confirmed the formation of AFCP nanoparticles.33 XRD and SAED patterns unequivocally establish the amorphous character of these nanoprecursors.53,54

A central finding of this study is that the AFCP hydrogel prolongs the amorphous phase lifetime in artificial saliva for at least 2 h, as evidenced by cryo-TEM and the delayed splitting of the phosphate ʋ4 band (Figure 2). Previous studies have indicated that the energy barrier for phase transformation of amorphous minerals in solution is associated with the degrees of freedom of anions or cations.55,56 In this study, both PASP and CMC, which contain abundant carboxyl groups, can interact with AFCP precursors and mediate their nucleation and growth. In Hydrogel A, Ca2⁺ ions preferentially bind to the negatively charged carboxyl groups on the PASP side chains via electrostatic attraction, forming PASP-Ca complexes uniformly distributed within the HPMC hydrogel matrix. In Hydrogel B, PO43− and F− ions are uniformly dispersed within the alkaline CMC hydrogel, where ionization of carboxyl group imparts a net negative charge to CMC. Upon mixing Hydrogel A and Hydrogel B, the negatively charged CMC electrostatically interact with Ca2⁺, acting synergistically with PASP to stabilize Ca2⁺ ions. Concurrently, PO43− and F− ions are attracted by Ca2⁺ and confined within the microenvironment of the three-dimensional polymeric network of the hydrogel. This confinement reduces the supersaturation of mineral ions within the hydrogel system and effectively retards the transformation of the amorphous mineral phase into its crystalline state. Nevertheless, over time, as the concentrations of Ca2⁺, PO43−, and F− reach a critical threshold, the AFCP nanoprecursors aggregate and transform into crystalline HAp, forming needle-like structures.57 This structural evolution endows the system with excellent mineralization activity. This design ensures that AFCP nanoprecursors remain in a highly active, penetrative amorphous state during the critical early period of application, while ultimately transforming into the thermodynamically stable HAp phase that integrates with tooth mineral.

In vitro, the AFCP hydrogel induced robust enamel remineralization and DTs occlusion, significantly outperforming both fluoride and CPP-ACP + NaF treatment (Figures 4 and 5). The limited availability of calcium and phosphate ions renders natural salivary remineralization inefficient,48 while fluoride relies critically on residual crystallites and the salivary ion supply.13,58 CPP-ACP + NaF (GC Tooth Mousse Plus) provides supplementary ion reservoirs and thus outperforms fluoride alone; however, its paste-like consistency restricts capillary-driven penetration into deep enamel lesions, and the uncontrolled burst release of ions lacks the spatiotemporal regulation required for sustained subsurface crystal growth. In contrast, the 30–80 nm AFCP nanoparticles infiltrate the widened intercrystalline spaces, and the hydrogel matrix suppresses premature phase transformation, enabling sustained ion release into subsurface voids. This facilitated intrarod crystalline growth, yielding a dense remineralized layer of approximately 32 µm in thickness, which restored ~77% of the elastic modulus and ~44% of the hardness of sound enamel—markedly outperforming fluoride (58% and 29%, respectively) and CPP‑ACP + NaF (64% and 24%, respectively). XRD analysis revealed that the newly formed mineral phase resembled that of natural enamel; however, XRD alone cannot unequivocally distinguish between hydroxyapatite (HAp) and fluorapatite (FAp). Therefore, complementary spectroscopic techniques would be valuable in future studies to definitively confirm fluorine incorporation into the apatite lattice. DH arises from open DTs that permit fluid movement and nerve stimulation, and treatment strategies therefore aim at tubular sealing.7,59 Analogously, the AFCP hydrogel achieves deep DTs occlusion: its nanoscale dimensions and the two-component system’s capacity to preserve amorphous precursors in situ retard phase transformation and drive intratubular mineralization. Notably, the depth and density of occlusion achieved here are difficult to realize with conventional fluoride or CPP-ACP pastes, which deposit only superficial mineral layers. Together, these findings establish that the AFCP hydrogel mediates genuine subsurface remineralization, restoring structural integrity rather than merely coating the surface. The in vivo rabbit study validates these findings under clinically relevant conditions, where salivary ions, enzymes, and mechanical forces are present (Figure 6). AFCP hydrogel treatment effectively repaired demineralized enamel and occluded DTs, producing smooth, mineralized surfaces. The AFCP hydrogel’s key advance lies in its two-component design and dual-stabilizer system (PASP and phosphorylated CMC), which enables in situ synthesis of AFCP nanoparticles while simultaneously extending amorphous precursor lifetime and preserving nanoscale mobility. This enables the amorphous precursors to infiltrate narrow inter-rod spaces and dentinal tubules prior to crystallization.

Taken together, these in vitro and in vivo findings establish the AFCP hydrogel as a promising non-invasive platform for early enamel caries and dentin hypersensitivity. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. The regenerated hydroxyapatite layer lacks the hierarchical organization of natural enamel. The challenge may be addressed by future integration of oriented templates or peptide-guided mineralization. Long-term durability under cyclic masticatory loading, pH fluctuations, and enzymatic activity remains unevaluated. Although the two-component design inherently prevents premature crystallization during storage, formal shelf-life and sterilization compatibility studies are pending. Moreover, validation in larger animal models and controlled clinical trials will be necessary to substantiate translational potential. Notably, the modular nature of the two-component system may offer unique therapeutic flexibility: future studies could investigate whether sequential application of Hydrogel A and Hydrogel B directly onto the tooth surface—allowing in situ mixing and mineralization to occur within the lesion micro-environment—further enhances penetration and spatial control of remineralization.

Conclusion

In summary, a stable two-component AFCP hydrogel was constructed using HPMC and CMC to stabilize calcium, phosphate, and fluoride ions in a metastable amorphous state. Both in vitro and in vivo results demonstrated that this hydrogel effectively promotes the remineralization of demineralized enamel and occlusion of DTs. Although the regenerated mineral layer does not fully recapitulate the hierarchical architecture of native enamel, its mechanical properties are significantly superior to those achieved with current clinical remineralizing agents, including fluoride and CPP-ACP + NaF. The AFCP hydrogel acts as an exogenous mineral reservoir capable of mediating in situ remineralization and DTs occlusion, likely through sustained ion release coupled with gradual phase transformation. However, long-term durability under mechanical and acidic challenges, together with validation in larger animal models and clinical trials, remains to be established to confirm translational promise.
