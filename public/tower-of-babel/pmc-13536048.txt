Integration of the patient, caregiver and community voice into paediatric care, education and research: the SickKids model of advisor-centred engagement
Authors: Brooke Allemang, Francine Buchanan, Priscilla Medeiros, Karen Kinnear, Tomasz L Czarny, Eyal Cohen, Padmaja Subbarao, Karen Haas, Julie Rose, Melissa Jones, Colin Macarthur
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractBackgroundPatient engagement within healthcare institutions is growing, with studies outlining its relevance and impact on clinical care, education and research. Literature exists on the engagement of patients in specific contexts; however, articles on engagement models, which centralise these practices at the institutional level, are limited. This article addresses that gap by describing the development and implementation of the SickKids Model of Advisor-Centred Engagement, an institution-wide approach to integrating the voices of patients, families and communities across clinical care, education and research at The Hospital for Sick Children (SickKids). Historically, engagement practices at SickKids were siloed and inconsistent, thus limiting the potential for meaningful partnerships. To address these challenges and drawing on existing frameworks and extensive stakeholder consultations, the SickKids Model of Advisor-Centred Engagement and a centralised Office of Patient, Family and Community Engagement were established.Main bodyThe SickKids Model prioritises advisor-centredness, efficiency, sustainability and scalability to foster meaningful collaborations across the institution. Core elements include the Engagement Incubator, a space for training, knowledge sharing and capacity building, along with centralised resources to streamline advisor onboarding, honourarium processes and engagement practices. During its first year of operations, the Office has supported numerous engagement activities, developed inclusive policies and offered training to staff and advisors. Metrics to assess implementation and impact are being developed, including measures of engagement reach, satisfaction, capacity building and methodological contributions to patient-oriented research practice. Learnings to date highlight the importance of institutional commitment, dedicated resources and cross-departmental collaboration in embedding engagement at a systemic level. Challenges remain, including managing increasing demands for engagement support and developing robust evaluation metrics.ConclusionThe SickKids Model of Advisor-Centred Engagement moves engagement beyond project-level approaches to the implementation of an institution-wide framework for coordinated, scalable and sustainable engagement across clinical care, education and research. Initial findings suggest that implementation of a centralised infrastructure can support cross-domain learning, reduce fragmentation and strengthen organisational capacity for meaningful engagement. This model may serve as a blueprint for healthcare institutions looking to embed engagement practices at the organisational level, thereby ensuring that lived experiences inform clinical care, education and research.Keywords: Patient Participation, PAEDIATRICS, Patient Preference

Background

Authentic and meaningful partnership between persons with lived experience of a health condition and clinicians, researchers, educators and policymakers has been used to improve healthcare, health education, health systems and promote community-driven change.1 2 Such partnerships have been termed ‘patient engagement’ or ‘patient and public involvement’, with ‘patient’ being an overarching term that includes persons with lived experience as well as caregivers, family and community members.1

Patient engagement at the institutional level to enhance clinical care delivery is a well-established area of practice, primarily driven by the implementation of patient, family and community advisory councils.3–12 A recent systematic review by Oldfield et al highlighted the key role of patient, family and community advisors in influencing direct care, quality improvement initiatives, organisational design and institutional policy.11 Partnering with persons with lived experience for health system improvement ensures that solutions are relevant, meaningful and important to those whom the system is intended to serve.1 2

In the educational context, patients have been engaged as partners in coproducing teaching, mentoring and educational curricula to enhance health professional training.13–16 Examples include cocreated medical education curricula for trainees caring for children with medical complexity and training to improve paediatric resident skills in rapport-building, family communication and cultural humility.17 18

National initiatives focused on patient engagement in health research have been established in the United Kingdom, the USA and Canada over the last several decades.16 19 20 Patient engagement in research (PEiR) is hypothesised to enhance research quality, relevance and feasibility.21–25 Patient partner engagement in research has also been shown to collectively benefit researchers, patient partners and community members through enhanced capacity building, skill development, knowledge sharing and relationship building.26–28 Last, partnering with patients and community members in the design and implementation of health research interventions is intended to ensure that interventions address important priorities for patient partners and communities.1 2 Challenges to authentic and meaningful engagement include tokenism, communication barriers, lack of clarity about patient partner roles and inadequate infrastructure or resources to efficiently engage patient partners.25

Community engagement is an emerging area of focus for hospitals to advance health equity.29 Several authors have suggested that social capital is a key driver of hospital-community collaborations and can extend institutional impact beyond clinical care to address social determinants of health and broader population health goals.30 31 Embedding community perspectives into the design and implementation of community initiatives acknowledges the importance of engaging voices beyond patients and families to improve relevance, equity and impact.32 33

While there is an extensive and growing body of literature on patient engagement conducted in specific contexts (eg, clinical, educational or research settings),22 34–36 there is a dearth of information on patient engagement in healthcare institutions across the continuum of clinical care, education and research. The published literature on engagement processes and impacts remains largely siloed by setting,22 34–36 with most studies focused on individual projects and limited attention to institutional-level factors or cross-domain knowledge sharing. Institutional models and infrastructure for patient, family and community engagement in research exist,36–38 however, to our knowledge, the integration of engagement activities across clinical care, education and research in the paediatric context has not been reported. At the Hospital for Sick Children (SickKids) in Toronto, Canada, it was apparent that patient and public engagement activities were siloed, engagement practices were inconsistent across settings, and resources to support patient engagement were limited and scattered. In this context, SickKids sought to develop an enterprise-wide model for patient, family and community engagement.

Objective

The objective of this commentary is to describe the SickKids Model of Advisor-Centred Engagement and the implementation of the model through a centralised ‘Office of Patient, Family, and Community Engagement’ (Office). The paper highlights the institutional context, describes the development of the SickKids Model of Advisor-Centred Engagement, its implementation through the Office of Patient, Family and Community Engagement and a summary of the first year of operations of the ‘Office.’

Institutional context

SickKids is a paediatric academic health sciences centre in Toronto, Canada, providing specialised care to children in the Greater Toronto Area and the province of Ontario (population approximately 16 million). Affiliated with the University of Toronto, SickKids is Canada’s most research-intensive paediatric hospital.39 The SickKids enterprise encompasses SickKids Hospital, the SickKids Research Institute and the SickKids Learning Institute, all committed to providing exemplary child-centred and family-centred care, innovative research and high-quality education to train the next generation of child health clinicians, educators and researchers. Annually, there are approximately 300 000 ambulatory visits to SickKids, including more than 65 000 emergency department visits.40 The SickKids Learning Institute delivers online and instructor-led courses, simulation-based learning, continuing education courses, workshops and an international learner programme, with more than 7000 participants annually.41 Last, more than 3000 researchers, trainees, and staff work in the SickKids Research Institute across the continuum of research (basic science to population health), with $270 million CAD in external research funding secured in 2022/2023.40 SickKids is consistently ranked among the top paediatric hospitals worldwide.42

History of engagement at SickKids

In 2013, SickKids’ Centre for Innovation and Excellence in Child and Family-Centred Care (Centre) codesigned a care model with patients and families. A key component was the role of the child and family to ‘inform the design, implementation and evaluation of clinical practices’.43–45 Formalised engagement with patients and families for clinical quality improvement initiatives, clinical care pathways and patient-facing resources occurred through a Lived Experience Network (formerly the Family Advisory Network) that includes approximately 80 family advisors. Two major initiatives supported by the Lived Experience Network are the Patient and Family Experience Advisory Committee and the Patient Advisory Council, a committee of young people age 10–18 years.

Within the Learning Institute, patients and family members have been engaged in an ad hoc manner to support the development of patient-facing educational resources shared on AboutKidsHealth, a health education website for children, youth and caregivers. For example, the ‘Family As Faculty’ programme offers opportunities for patient and family advisors to share their lived experiences at orientations, workshops and other learning events. Codeveloped with staff and Lived Experience Network members, the programme provides mentorship and skills training to help advisors communicate their stories to enhance staff learning.46

In the Research Institute, engagement has been driven by individual research teams, funding opportunities such as Canada’s Strategy for Patient Oriented Research (SPOR) and through provincial and national patient-oriented research networks.21 32 33 47–50 The PEiR programme, in the Research Institute, was established in 2018.51 Supported by the SPOR-funded Ontario Child Health SUPPORT Unit, the PEiR programme assisted research teams through consultation, training and matching researchers with patient and family advisors. A dedicated Research and Family Advisory Committee (RFAC), comprised of clinicians, researchers and family advisors, was also established to advise research teams on meaningful engagement.44 The Edwin S.H. Leong Centre for Healthy Children—an interdisciplinary research centre at SickKids in partnership with the University of Toronto—has built capacity in community-engaged research through training and education for researchers, staff, trainees, patients, families and community partners since 2022.52 53

Prior to the establishment of the Office, SickKids advanced patient engagement through institution-wide initiatives focused on capacity building. For example, the ACTION in Child Health Research Symposium, a patient-led, 1-day event, brought together 60 patients, caregivers, clinicians, researchers, trainees and decision-makers to assess strengths and barriers in patient-oriented research and to codevelop tools and system-level solutions. The Edwin S.H. Leong Centre for Healthy Children built engagement capacity through its Trainee Hub and Community Engagement Series, offering interdisciplinary workshops, panels and hands-on training in codesign, compensation, engagement planning and integrating lived experience into research. The PEiR programme embedded patient engagement training into onboarding for new clinical research staff. Last, structured learning was provided through the Patient-Oriented Research Curriculum in Child Health (PORCCH), an open-access, five-module online curriculum used by hospitals across Ontario and national research networks to onboard patient partners and researchers.16 54 55 Collectively, these initiatives laid the groundwork for a more coordinated, enterprise-level approach to patient and family engagement at SickKids.

From value to practice: challenges to scale and spread of engagement

A distributed model of engagement at SickKids, where clinical, teaching and research teams independently involved patients and community members in their work, diluted the enormous potential of patient involvement in the institution. The team-by-team approach to engagement led to lost opportunities and limited scale and spread. Teams faced similar challenges with patient engagement, but lacked the authority, time or resources to implement broader-based solutions. The main challenges identified were institutional barriers to engagement and a lack of accountability to ensure that patient engagement was authentic, meaningful and not tokenistic.

The lack of centralised coordination to oversee, support and track engagement efforts across the institution led to inconsistent and variable engagement practices and quality. For patients and families, this meant seeking out engagement opportunities independently, encountering unclear expectations and receiving inconsistent recognition for their time and contributions. For staff, the absence of an enterprise-wide strategy, tools and resources on engagement limited patient-oriented research. This led to duplication of efforts, inconsistency in engagement practices and missed opportunities for meaningful, high-quality engagement. The pervasive barriers to engagement also resulted in tokenistic or ‘easy engagements’. In addition, staff often turned to a small, familiar group of advisors, repeatedly seeking their input. This limited the diversity of perspectives informing hospital initiatives, in terms of lived experience, socioeconomic, cultural and ethnic backgrounds.

Developing the model of advisor-centred engagement at SickKids

The SickKids Model of Advisor-Centred Engagement was developed over the course of a year, beginning in late 2022. A core team (CM, FB, PM) led the process, which included reviewing the literature on engagement, consulting with interest holders (including youth and family advisors) and analysing existing engagement drivers, funding structures, incentives and resources at SickKids.

Review of the literature

The core team drew on several foundational frameworks. For example, the ‘Ottawa Model for Patient Engagement in Research’ outlined the importance of collaboration along with infrastructure, resources and evaluation to build capacity and achieve meaningful impact.37 Abelson et al described a capability framework for patient, family and caregiver engagement in Ontario Health Teams and highlighted the need for engagement-capable environments to support sustainable, high-quality engagement at the organisational level. The ‘Ecology of Engagement’ approach framed health systems as interconnected ecosystems, encouraging system-level thinking and the integration of engagement efforts across domains such as self-care, education and policy.56 57 Last, recommendations by Lee-Foon et al on how to embed engagement in learning health systems were also considered.58

Institutional support

The importance of institutional support in operationalising patient engagement within organisations has been highlighted by several authors.7 37 59 60 Key facilitators to ensure systemic integration of engagement into healthcare institutions include trained and dedicated staff, a shared vision for engagement and the allocation of resources and funding.7 59–61 At SickKids, there was significant institutional commitment to patient engagement and inclusion. For example, the hospital strategic plan included a specific aim of ‘Partner(ing) with patients and families in decision-making for care, research and education’.62 In addition, the SickKids Patient and Family Experience Strategy focused on ‘an enterprise-wide approach to engagement that centres child, youth, family and community knowledge and lived experiences in designing the future of SickKids’.63 Development of the strategy was informed by a large, multimodal engagement process that gathered perspectives from patients, families, staff and the broader community. This process included 134 engagements with patients and families, 70 focus groups, 536 staff survey responses and 325 public survey responses collected in English, French and several other languages commonly spoken in the Greater Toronto Area.63 These inputs identified priorities for strengthening engagement practices across the organisation and informed the strategic objectives.

In summary, the constellation of institutional strategic commitment, clinical, educational and research initiatives in patient engagement, and strong support from patients and advisors created the ideal environmental conditions to establish an enterprise-wide SickKids Model of Advisor-Centred Engagement.

Advisor-centredness reflects an institutional commitment to viewing advisors as a strategic resource and integral to institutional learning, rather than individual contributors to isolated projects, and whose collective insights strengthen the organisation.

Core elements of the model

The primary goal of the SickKids Model of Advisor-Centred Engagement is the scale and spread of high-quality engagement practices that promote, support, enable and build capacity among healthcare professionals, educators, researchers, patients, families and communities to partner in care, education and research. To this end, the model provides guidance, support and infrastructure to optimise engagement and meaningful partnerships with patients, families and communities. The model shifts the focus of engagement from individual projects and teams to a shared goal of enhancing meaningful engagement across the institution, which distinguishes it from existing frameworks.22–37

Central to the model is the Engagement Incubator (see figure 1)—a distinctive space where individuals come to learn, support one another and build relationships. It acts as both an entry point and launchpad, strengthening both capacity and community through training, knowledge sharing, community-building and methods development. As participants exit, they apply what they have learnt to future projects, regardless of setting, creating a ripple effect that extends engagement across the institution. The process is intentionally cyclical, with individuals returning to refine skills, continue learning and build long-term partnerships.

Model of engagement.

The SickKids Model of Advisor-Centred Engagement aims to support a coordinated approach to patient, family and community engagement across an entire organisation. A unique aspect of this model is its focus on the patient voice at the institutional level, while taking into account hospital strategic objectives, departmental mandates and a broad range of clinical, educational and research initiatives. By prioritising advisor-centredness, patients, families and community members are positioned as partners who can shape priorities and inform processes and outcomes across clinical care, education and research. Initiatives to reduce barriers to involvement, to broaden and diversify the advisor community and to ensure that onboarding procedures and training are inclusive of key commitments.

Implementing the model of advisor-centred engagement at SickKids

Implementation of the model was through a centralised Office of Patient, Family, and Community Engagement (‘Office’). Staff include a Senior Manager lead, a Program Manager, Family Advisory; a Child and Youth Engagement Specialist; an Engagement in Research Specialist; a Clinical Engagement Specialist; and an Engagement Methods Researcher. The ‘Office’ is supported through a combination of fixed operational and external grant funding. Continuity of core staff is key to ensure that ‘institutional’ engagement knowledge is retained, and long-term relationships are built. A cross-departmental steering/advisory committee—consisting of leads of three institution-wide strategic initiatives, a research executive, a clinical operations department lead and the vice-president of clinical operations—has been struck. The role of this committee is to provide guidance to the ‘Office’ on its operations, governance structures, reporting metrics and accountability.

During the first year of operations, the ‘Office’ has focused on unifying engagement support services, reducing institutional barriers and providing services, tools and resources to support consistent engagement activities across the institution. A team-based approach has ensured that the responsibility and accountability for engagement at SickKids fall to a purpose-built infrastructure to facilitate engagement across the organisation. Streamlining engagement-related queries to a single point of contact has also reduced duplication of effort and improved efficiency. From a capacity-building perspective, the ‘Office’ has supported 118 engagements and delivered 19 training sessions involving 1166 participants.

The ‘Office’ has addressed long-standing institutional barriers to engagement, including the development of institution-wide policies and standardised processes for advisor onboarding, advisor honoraria, reducing administrative delays in initiating engagements and ensuring equitable compensation for patient, family and community partners. The ‘Office’ also developed and launched a SickKids Engagement Framework,64 providing clear expectations, shared language and practical guidance to support consistent, high-quality engagement across programmes. During the first year, engagement practices at SickKids were mapped to existing institutional policies and procedures, with Human Resources, Legal and Finance Departments involved in the development of relevant policies and practices. On the research side, the ‘Office’ raised awareness of engagement funding opportunities, reduced procedural bottlenecks and developed standard, plain-language agreement templates that outline expectations for advisors and researchers. These concrete system improvements, along with direct engagement support and training, demonstrate early, measurable success of the ‘Office’ to strengthen the quality, consistency and strategic alignment of engagement across the institution.

To evaluate the implementation success of the ‘Office’, a comprehensive set of performance and impact metrics is under development, in consultation with SickKids leadership, family and youth advisors, and staff. Evidence-based measures of engagement quality and impact will be selected based on review of the literature. The metrics selected will assess the ‘quality’ of engagement, eg, is the engagement inclusive, meaningful, and is there reciprocity within partnerships. Potential metrics include number and diversity of studies involving patient, family and community partners; quality of engagement as assessed through validated tools; satisfaction with engagement supports provided by the ‘Office’; alignment with institutional priorities, for example, strategic goals related to equity, patient experience, research excellence and impact; multidirectional capacity building, for example, staff, trainee and advisor participation in training and knowledge-sharing initiatives; and institutional culture measures, for example, advisor and staff perceptions of SickKids as a patient-engaged and community-engaged research environment, and the extent to which engagement is embedded in decision-making. These metrics will enable SickKids to measure the reach, effectiveness and strategic contribution of the ‘Office’.

Current challenges include the ever-increasing demands for engagement support, enhancing existing advisor relationships, and how to select, refine and implement engagement evaluation metrics to capture the frequency, quality and impact of engagement at SickKids. To ensure that the ‘Office’ remains advisor-centred, it is important to have advisors provide regular feedback on the performance of the ‘Office’ and how it might improve. In addition, given that family advisors often share deeply personal experiences throughout advisory work, embedding peer support and caregiver wellness into engagement practices is essential to acknowledge and mitigate the emotional vulnerability that can accompany this role. Other priorities include expanding the role of youth advisors and formally evaluating the SickKids Model.

Conclusion

This commentary outlines the development of the SickKids Model of Advisor-Centred Engagement, and its implementation through an Office of Patient, Family and Community Engagement to provide a coordinated, institutional approach to embedding authentic and meaningful engagement across clinical care, education and research. Development of the SickKids Model drew on existing engagement models and frameworks that focused on building engagement-capable environments and ecosystems in which partnerships could be fostered within and across healthcare institutions.37 56 65 66 The ‘Office’ provides a practical approach for engagement across clinical care, education and research, while addressing system-level factors, and has secured support from institutional leadership, ensuring cross-departmental collaboration and accountability. The SickKids Model is scalable in nature and has already supported those that are new to engagement, to teams with established programmes and services.16 61

The generalisability of the model to smaller institutions or those beginning the engagement journey is dependent on implementation of the core principles, ie, advisor-centredness, meaningful partnership and equitable engagement. Contextual factors such as institutional size, available resources and existing engagement infrastructure are also important. While the operational components of the model are intentionally adaptable, all elements can be implemented at smaller scale by embedding them within a single dedicated role and integrating engagement into existing institutional decision-making structures. In our view, developing an incubation engagement space connected to organisational strategy and leadership is critical. Such a space that advances meaningful engagement ensures visibility, legitimacy and sustainability.

Embedding engagement at the institutional level through the ‘Office’ has brought lived experience into aspects of care, teaching and research at SickKids. Addressing engagement gaps across different domains, and promoting the value of patient, family and community engagement has required a culture shift; one that has been reflected in the hospital strategic plan. The ultimate value-add of the SickKids Model of Advisor-Centred Engagement, however, is the potential to improve clinical outcomes, bridge the research-implementation gap, enhance educational initiatives and ensure that patient and family perspectives inform practice, teaching, research and policy at SickKids and beyond.
