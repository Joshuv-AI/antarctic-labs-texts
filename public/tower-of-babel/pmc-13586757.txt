Video-rate label-free molecular mapping in living plant tissue with a deployable optical encoder
Authors: Alexander Ingold, Richard G Baird, Dasmeet Kaur, Nidhi Dwivedi, Reed Sorenson, Leslie Sieburth, Chang-Jun Liu, Rajesh Menon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLiving tissues contain dynamic biochemical information that is difficult to capture with conventional hyperspectral microscopes because sequential spectral acquisition is poorly matched to in vivo molecular processes that evolve during measurement. Here, we introduce a task-specific optical encoding framework for video-rate molecular inference in living plant tissue. The system integrates a passive spectral encoder, implemented here as a low-angle scattering low-density polyethylene (LDPE) layer, into a 22-mm miniaturized probe and learns a supervised mapping from ultraviolet-excited autofluorescence measurements to biomolecular abundance maps. Unlike conventional pipelines that first reconstruct hyperspectral datacubes and then perform spectral unmixing, the deployed system directly estimates endogenous molecular contrast associated primarily with lignin and chlorophyll in poplar tissue, with additional suberin-associated contrast evaluated in suberin-rich tissue. This reframing makes the measurement task biomolecular inference rather than spectral reconstruction, enabling biochemical mapping under low-photon autofluorescence conditions while reducing data burden and computational latency. In living poplar stems, the platform captures autofluorescence-derived videos of embolism propagation and wound-induced biochemical remodeling, dynamic processes for which sequential spectral acquisition can introduce temporal mixing because the molecular contrast evolves during the scan itself. The system also resolves genotype-dependent reductions in lignin-associated autofluorescence in engineered poplar lines. Direct molecular inference improves biomolecular estimation relative to a reconstruction-based pipeline, while probabilistic decoding provides uncertainty estimates. These results show that compact passive spectral encoding, when optimized for biological inference rather than datacube recovery, enables deployable, label-free molecular videography of living plant tissue dynamics after task-specific calibration.

Living tissues contain biochemical signals that change over seconds to hours, but conventional molecular imaging often requires labels, destructive sampling, or slow spectral scanning. We developed a compact, deployable imaging platform that converts single autofluorescence snapshots from living plant tissue directly into maps of molecularly associated contrast. This enables video-rate, label-free visualization of processes such as embolism propagation, wound-induced remodeling, and genotype-dependent lignin changes in intact poplar stems. The work shifts hyperspectral imaging from reconstructing complete spectral datacubes toward measuring the molecular information needed for a biological task, providing a practical route to studying dynamic biochemical processes in living systems.

Introduction

Mapping molecular organization in living tissue is essential for understanding physiological dynamics, stress responses, and genotype-dependent biochemical remodeling. Hyperspectral imaging provides one route to this capability by measuring a spectral signature at each spatial pixel, enabling identification of biomolecular spectral components and reconstruction of spatial abundance maps within complex biological systems (1–3). However, conventional hyperspectral microscopes generally acquire spectral information sequentially, either by scanning wavelength, space, or angle. This acquisition strategy is powerful for static specimens, but it is poorly matched to living systems in which molecular contrast changes during the measurement itself. In such settings, sequential spectral acquisition trades molecular specificity against temporal fidelity, making fast biochemical processes difficult to capture without temporal aliasing.

Snapshot hyperspectral imaging addresses this limitation by encoding spatial and spectral information into a single exposure. Existing snapshot architectures broadly fall into two classes. The first extends color filter arrays into multispectral mosaics (4). These sensors are compact and compatible with standard imaging hardware, but spatial sampling is partitioned among spectral channels, limiting the number of recoverable bands and reducing spatial resolution or photon efficiency. The second class uses dispersive or coded optical elements to multiplex spectral information into spatially overlapping measurements that are subsequently demixed computationally (5–7). Fourier-plane dispersive systems can provide high spectral resolution but often require relay optics and longer optical paths (8), whereas coded-aperture systems can incur photon losses that are problematic under low-light conditions (5).

More compact computational imaging systems place thin diffractive, scattering, or spectral encoding layers near the sensor plane, enabling snapshot spectral measurements in smaller form factors (9–12). Across these systems, the dominant objective has typically been reconstruction of a hyperspectral datacube with high spatial and spectral fidelity. This objective is appropriate when the desired output is the datacube itself. However, many biological measurements do not require recovery of every spectral channel with maximal fidelity; instead, they require reliable estimation of specific molecular or physiological quantities. This distinction motivates a different design principle: the optical measurement should be optimized for the biological inference task rather than for reconstruction of an intermediate hyperspectral representation.

This task-specific view is particularly important for ultraviolet (UV)-excited, label-free autofluorescence imaging in intact living tissue (13–16). In this regime, endogenous signals are weak, emission spectra from different biomolecular species overlap, and tissue motion, scattering, and physiological variability complicate quantitative interpretation. At the same time, many of the relevant biological events occur on time scales ranging from fractions of a second to hours and must be observed without labels, destructive sampling, or bulky laboratory instrumentation. Conventional hyperspectral microscopes are therefore poorly suited to deployable molecular videography: scanning-based instruments can provide spectral information, but they cannot faithfully capture molecular dynamics that evolve during the scan; benchtop snapshot systems can improve temporal resolution, but they are often not designed for direct mounting on living tissue or for low-photon autofluorescence operation.

Here we introduce a deployable, label-free molecular imaging platform designed for video-rate biochemical mapping in living plant tissue. The system integrates a passive spectral encoder into a 22-mm miniaturized microscope and converts each UV-excited autofluorescence frame into a single encoded measurement. In this implementation, the passive spectral encoder is a low-angle scattering layer positioned near the detector plane, but the framework is not tied to this specific physical element. A supervised computational decoder then maps the encoded measurement directly to biomolecular abundance maps associated with endogenous molecular contrast, including lignin, chlorophyll, and suberin. We use established probabilistic image-to-image learning to estimate molecular abundance maps together with uncertainty. The methodological contribution is the integration of compact passive spectral encoding, task-specific supervised inference, and uncertainty-aware deployment for direct biomolecular mapping in living tissue. Together, these elements enable molecular measurements that are difficult to obtain with conventional hyperspectral imaging.

Conceptually, the system replaces datacube-first hyperspectral imaging with direct biomolecular inference. Instead of first reconstructing a spectral datacube and then applying spectral unmixing, the passive spectral encoder and decoder are used to estimate the molecular quantities of interest directly from the encoded measurement. We validate this approach in living poplar stems by capturing molecular videos of embolism propagation through xylem vessels and wound-induced biochemical remodeling over time. These dynamic processes are vulnerable to temporal mixing during sequential spectral acquisition because the molecular contrast can evolve during the measurement itself. We further show that the platform resolves genotype-dependent reductions in lignin-associated autofluorescence in engineered poplar lines and can report uncertainty under spectral mismatch and distribution shift. The latter result clarifies an important scope of the method: the system is deployable after task-specific calibration, rather than universally transferable across arbitrary tissue types without training data.

Together, these results establish task-specific passive spectral encoding as a practical strategy for video-rate, label-free molecular mapping in living plant tissue. By shifting the goal from reconstructing hyperspectral datacubes to measuring biologically relevant molecular states, this work opens a route to deployable molecular videography for observing biochemical dynamics in intact living systems.

Deployable passive spectral encoding for living-tissue molecular imaging

To implement the task-specific molecular imaging framework introduced above, we developed a compact platform that integrates passive spectral encoding into a 22-mm miniaturized microscope architecture (Fig. 1). The deployed probe does not scan wavelength, switch spectral filters, or use a multispectral mosaic. Instead, a passive spectral encoder positioned near the detector plane introduces wavelength-dependent spatial modulation into the autofluorescence image, converting each exposure into a single encoded measurement, g(u,v). In this implementation, the encoder is a low-angle scattering layer selected to provide weak, wavelength-dependent modulation while preserving the native imaging geometry of the miniscope. We use the term passive spectral encoder to emphasize its function in the measurement process; the framework is not restricted to this specific physical implementation.

Deployable passive spectral encoding for label-free molecular imaging in living tissue. Under 385 nm excitation, endogenous autofluorescence from living poplar tissue is encoded by a passive spectral element integrated near the detector plane, producing a single wavelength-encoded measurement, g(u,v), without wavelength scanning or filter switching. A hyperspectral decoder can reconstruct a hyperspectral datacube from this encoded image, which can then be unmixed to estimate biomolecular abundance maps, fi(x,y) in a conventional two-step pipeline. Alternatively, a dedicated biomolecule decoder maps the same encoded measurement directly to biomolecular abundance maps derived from autofluorescence contrast in one step. In the representative example shown here, direct one-step biomolecular inference yields lower molecular-mapping error than the reconstruction-first two-step pipeline. Separate component maps identify lignin- and chlorophyll-associated contrast.

The 80-μm polyethylene layer was selected as an inexpensive, lightweight proof-of-concept encoder that integrates without custom microfabrication. Its measured transmission is ≈80% (Fig. S2). A fabricated phase-only diffractive element could improve photon efficiency in future designs: multilevel phase-only optics have demonstrated greater than 86% absolute transmission across 405–633 nm (peak near 92%), although throughput also depends on diffraction efficiency, angular redistribution, and the sensor aperture (17). A unique surface-relief pattern is not required, however. Once fixed, the encoder’s wavelength-dependent response is measured or learned after fabrication, and it is the encoder geometry and encoder-to-sensor spacing, not a bespoke profile that set the response decorrelation and conditioning.

Under 385-nm excitation, endogenous plant biomolecules produce overlapping autofluorescence spanning ≈450–700 nm (18, 19). This spectral overlap makes direct molecular interpretation difficult from intensity images alone, but it also provides intrinsic biochemical contrast that can be exploited by an appropriately designed optical measurement. The passive spectral encoder preserves spatial information while imprinting wavelength-dependent structure on the sensor image. The resulting frame therefore contains spatial and spectral cues needed to estimate molecular contrast associated with lignin, chlorophyll, and suberin from a single low-photon exposure.

This architecture differs from conventional hyperspectral microscopy in both form factor and measurement objective. Scanning hyperspectral instruments can provide detailed spectral information from static fields, but sequential acquisition cannot faithfully capture molecular contrast that evolves during the scan. Benchtop snapshot systems can improve temporal resolution, but they are often not designed for direct mounting on living tissue or for low-photon autofluorescence operation. The present architecture preserves the 1.5 to 60 frames per second of the underlying miniscope platform (20, 21) enabling video-rate acquisition from intact living tissue without mechanical scanning or spectral filter switching.

The output of the probe is not a hyperspectral datacube, but a stream of encoded autofluorescence frames. These frames are interpreted by a supervised molecular decoder trained using paired measurements from the deployed probe and a benchtop hyperspectral reference path. The reference path provides training supervision but is not part of the deployed device. Thus, the physical architecture separates calibration from use: hyperspectral measurements are used to teach the system what molecular information is present in the encoded frames, while deployment requires only the compact passive encoder and miniscope.

By combining passive spectral encoding with a tissue-mounted miniaturized microscope, the platform creates a measurement stream suitable for video-rate biochemical mapping in living tissue. The next section describes how these encoded frames are converted directly into molecular abundance maps.

Direct molecular inference from encoded measurements

Each frame acquired by the passive spectral encoder contains multiplexed spatial and spectral information, but recovering molecular contrast from this compressed measurement remains an ill-conditioned inverse problem under low-photon autofluorescence conditions. Conventional reconstruction-first pipelines would estimate a hyperspectral datacube and then apply spectral unmixing to obtain molecular abundance maps. In contrast, we train the deployed system for the biological measurement task itself: direct estimation of biomolecular maps from encoded measurements.

Using paired supervision data acquired during calibration, a computational decoder learns the mapping from g(u,v) to spatially resolved molecular contrast associated with endogenous biomolecules, including lignin and chlorophyll (Fig. 1). This approach does not require explicit calibration of a full spectral system matrix during deployment. Instead, the effective encoding produced by the passive spectral encoder, optical aberrations, detector response, and measurement noise are incorporated into the learned mapping. By bypassing datacube reconstruction, the system reduces data burden and computational latency while avoiding error propagation from sequential reconstruction and unmixing steps.

The decoder imposes no explicit sparsity constraint on the encoded image or the reconstructed spectrum. Its validity is instead set by the paired training distribution: spectral signatures, tissue structures, illumination conditions, or optical configurations not represented during training may not be predicted accurately. In such cases the per-pixel uncertainty and ensemble disagreement flag the measurement as out-of-distribution rather than returning a confident but unsupported estimate.

We use established probabilistic image-to-image learning to report both predicted molecular maps and associated uncertainty (22, 23). This established probabilistic learning strategy provides a practical safeguard for deployable biological imaging. In living tissue, illumination, motion, scattering, photon noise, and tissue composition can vary substantially across measurements. Per-pixel uncertainty estimates and ensemble disagreement allow the system to identify regions or samples where the encoded measurement is ambiguous or differs from the training distribution (Section S10).

After task-specific calibration, the 22-mm probe operates independently of the hyperspectral reference path. Each acquired frame is converted directly into molecular abundance maps, with uncertainty estimates that indicate prediction reliability. This inference strategy enables video-rate biochemical mapping while making clear the scope of deployment: the model is trained for a defined biological measurement task and should be recalibrated when applied to substantially different tissue types, species, or molecular targets.

Results

Direct molecular mapping improves estimation relative to a reconstruction-based pipeline

We first evaluated whether encoded autofluorescence measurements contain sufficient information to recover spatially resolved molecular contrast without reconstructing an intermediate hyperspectral datacube. Reference molecular abundance maps were derived from measured hyperspectral stacks using spectral unmixing (Materials and methods), providing paired supervision and quantitative targets for evaluation (24, 25). The direct inference model was then applied only to single-shot encoded measurements acquired by the deployable probe.

The predicted molecular maps reproduced the expected anatomical organization of living poplar stem tissue (Fig. 1, Section S12). Lignin-associated signal was enriched in interior xylem structures, whereas chlorophyll-associated signal was localized primarily toward outer tissue regions. These spatial patterns were recovered from encoded measurements alone, demonstrating that the passive spectral encoder preserves molecularly informative features even under low-photon autofluorescence conditions.

We compared direct molecular inference against a reconstruction-based baseline in which a hyperspectral datacube is first estimated from the encoded measurement and then unmixed to obtain biomolecular abundance maps. Across the evaluation dataset, direct inference achieved MAE = 0.0105 for biomolecular abundance maps, compared with MAE = 0.0269 for the two-step reconstruction-based baseline; a nonprobabilistic ablation yielded a nearly identical MAE of 0.0264. This improvement reflects the advantage of training the measurement pipeline for the biological task itself rather than for recovery of an intermediate spectral representation. A representative comparison between the one-step and two-step strategies is summarized in Fig. 1 and shown in detail in Fig. S19.

The probabilistic decoder also produced stable uncertainty estimates for the direct molecular maps. In contrast, the reconstruction-based pipeline produced poorly calibrated uncertainty estimates in which predicted uncertainty collapsed across large regions (Fig. S19). These results support the central design principle of the platform: when the goal is molecular mapping rather than datacube recovery, direct inference from encoded measurements can improve biomolecular estimation while reducing data burden and computational latency.

Video-rate molecular imaging captures transient dynamics in living tissue

A central motivation for deployable molecular videography is that many biological processes evolve on time scales that are incompatible with sequential spectral acquisition. Scanning hyperspectral microscopy can provide rich spectral information from stationary specimens, but dynamic molecular contrast may change during the scan itself, producing temporal mixing between spectral channels. Single-shot encoded acquisition addresses this limitation by recording a molecularly informative measurement in every video frame, thereby preserving the temporal correspondence between structure, spectral contrast, and biological dynamics.

Xylem embolism provides a direct example of this challenge. During embolism propagation, a gas–liquid interface advances through xylem conduits that normally transport water under tension. We used the deployable probe to image embolism propagation within xylem vessels of living poplar stems (Fig. 2; Videos S1 and S2). Embolism formation and spread are physiologically important because they disrupt water transport during hydraulic stress, yet their onset and trajectory are difficult to predict from external measurements. In one recording, six embolism events were observed within the vascular network. Five events propagated relatively slowly, with apparent interface speeds between 150 and 500 μm/min, whereas one rapid event crossed the field of view within a single frame, corresponding to an apparent speed greater than 4.5 mm/s. The transverse dimensions of the observed embolisms ranged from 30 to 55 μm. Prior work has shown that embolism spread is governed by thresholded air entry between hydraulically connected vessels, occurring when the pressure difference across pit membranes exceeds their capillary stability (26–28). The motion measured here should therefore be interpreted as local gas–liquid interface propagation through xylem, rather than as bulk sap-flow velocity. This distinction is important because interface motion can be intermittent, can accelerate through open vessel regions, and can pause or redirect at anatomical restrictions.

Two embolisms, E1 and E2, propagate through neighboring vessels, whereas a third event, E3, advances more slowly through a nearby vessel. For each event, single-shot encoded autofluorescence frames were acquired without wavelength scanning and decoded directly into molecular contrast maps by the trained model. The displayed channel images show lignin-associated contrast and chlorophyll-associated contrast separately, with representative merged maps showing lignin-associated contrast in blue and chlorophyll-associated contrast in red. Velocity traces quantify embolism motion over time for E1, E2, and E3. Additional embolism events are shown in Videos S1 and S2.

A simple hydraulic model supports the interpretation that this wide speed range reflects heterogeneous air invasion through xylem microstructure, rather than smooth viscous flow through an open, uniform conduit. Because the imaged region was ≈1 cm from a surface wound, the relevant hydraulic path was unlikely to be a straight, open conduit. That pathway likely included local constrictions, pit fields, vessel interconnections, and moving gas-liquid interfaces. As a reference case, an ideal 40 μm-diameter tube over a 1 cm path would require pressure drops of only 0.5–1.7 Pa for the slower events and ≈0.9 kPa for the rapid event (Fig. S15). These values are far below the MPa-scale vulnerability pressures reported for hybrid poplar and the air-injection thresholds measured in Populus tremuloides (29, 30). Conversely, if the relevant driving pressure for air entry were in this physiological range, the slower observed events would imply an effective conductance ≈6×105–8×106 times lower than that of the idealized open tube. This comparison is not a direct measurement of xylem hydraulic resistance, but it indicates that local anatomical resistance and capillary interface thresholds, rather than open-lumen viscous drag alone, likely dominated embolism motion in this preparation (27, 28).

The inferred contrast maps showed that embolism propagation was accompanied by changes in autofluorescence-derived channels. Air entry associated with embolism formation can alter local scattering and light collection, producing measurable changes in the inferred autofluorescence-associated channels. During the rapid embolism event, the chlorophyll-associated channel increased by 70% and the lignin-associated channel increased by 114% at a representative location. We interpret these changes as embolism-associated optical and molecular contrast changes, rather than as direct concentration changes alone. This result illustrates why sequential spectral acquisition is poorly matched to such dynamics: both the physical structure and the molecularly informative optical contrast can evolve during sequential spectral acquisition. The encoded video stream instead captures each transient event in a single exposure, avoiding spectral-channel misregistration and temporal aliasing.

For these biological experiments, the camera was operated at 10 frames/s to increase signal levels under low-light autofluorescence conditions, although the platform supports acquisition rates up to 60 frames/s. Biomolecular inference was performed at 71 frames/s on an NVIDIA RTX 3060 GPU, allowing encoded frames to be converted into molecular maps at video rate. Together, these results show that compact passive spectral encoding, combined with task-specific supervised inference, can capture fast structural dynamics and associated autofluorescence-derived contrast changes in living tissue using a deployable, tissue-mounted probe.

Longitudinal molecular videography reveals wound-induced biochemical remodeling

We next used the same platform to monitor slower physiological remodeling after a minimally invasive incision in living poplar stems (Fig. 3). Unlike embolism propagation, which occurs over seconds, wound response unfolds over minutes to hours and requires stable longitudinal imaging without destructive sampling or repeated spectral scans. Time-lapse molecular maps acquired over 43 h revealed dynamic redistribution of autofluorescent components during tissue recovery (Videos S3–S6).

The chlorophyll-associated signal decreased progressively over time (Fig. S24), consistent with stress-induced physiological responses in damaged plant tissue (31). The time-lapse image sequence also revealed mechanical remodeling around the wound site. The surrounding tissue underwent a two-phase shrinkage during healing, consistent with moisture-loss-driven deformation, with an initial contraction rate of ≈20 nm/s followed by a slower phase of ≈7.5 nm/s.

Together with the embolism measurements, these experiments demonstrate that the same deployable molecular imaging platform can capture both rapid transient events and slower biochemical remodeling in intact living tissue. This range of temporal access is central to the value of single-shot molecular inference: each frame provides molecularly informative contrast without requiring sequential spectral acquisition.

Genotype-resolved biochemical discrimination in living poplar stems

To test whether the platform can resolve genetically programmed biochemical variation in situ, we imaged transgenic Populus lines engineered to reduce lignin biosynthesis through accumulation of 2-pyrone-4,6-dicarboxylic acid (PDC) (32). Imaging was performed across 65 independent sites spanning multiple stems and biological replicates. Compared with wildtype (WT) controls, PDC transgenic samples exhibited reduced lignin-associated autofluorescence, particularly in the 500–550 nm spectral region (Fig. 4).

Top: WT xylem tissue exhibits strong emission near 500 nm and pronounced lignin-associated contrast within vascular cell-wall structures. These results show that the deployable molecular imaging platform can resolve genotype-dependent biochemical differences in living plant tissue without requiring hyperspectral acquisition during deployment.

The molecular maps were consistent with expected anatomical and biochemical differences. In WT stems, strong green autofluorescence delineated lignified secondary cell walls within the xylem. In contrast, PDC transgenic stems exhibited weaker lignin-associated signal and altered xylem-associated autofluorescence patterns, consistent with reduced lignification. These differences were recovered from single-shot encoded measurements during inference, without requiring hyperspectral reference stacks during deployment.

Quantitative analysis confirmed a reduced normalized lignin-associated signal in the PDC transgenic samples relative to WT controls (Fig. S26). These results show that deployable molecular imaging can report genotype-dependent biochemical remodeling in living plant tissue. Although this optical measurement is not a replacement for destructive molecular assays, it provides a rapid and label-free route to in situ biochemical phenotyping at cellular-scale resolution.

Uncertainty identifies spectral mismatch beyond the calibration distribution

Because the decoder learns a task-specific mapping from paired calibration data, its predictions are expected to be most reliable for tissues and molecular compositions represented in the training distribution. We therefore evaluated how the model behaves when applied to a tissue with substantially different autofluorescence composition. Specifically, we imaged cork oak (Quercus suber), a suberin-rich species whose spectral signatures differ from the poplar tissues used for calibration (33–35).

The model retained useful performance for lignin-associated mapping and hyperspectral inference over the 450–650 nm range, but it overestimated chlorophyll-associated content and fluorescence intensity in the 650–700 nm range (Fig. S27). This behavior is important because it shows that the trained model should not be interpreted as universally transferable across arbitrary species or tissue classes. Instead, deployment beyond the calibration distribution requires either task-specific retraining or uncertainty-gated interpretation.

The predicted uncertainty increased in regions and wavelength ranges where the model encountered spectral mismatch relative to the training distribution. This response indicates that uncertainty estimates can help flag unreliable predictions rather than allowing the system to report overconfident molecular maps outside its validated operating regime. Biomolecular maps of suberin-rich tissue are shown in Fig. S28.

We further assessed whether predicted uncertainty tracks reconstruction error across multiple datasets, including five time-lapse recordings from Populus nigra × P. deltoides, a Quercus suber bark sample, and three datasets from Populus tremula × P. alba. For the main 30-channel hyperspectral test dataset, predicted scale correlated strongly with MAE (r2=0.849; Fig. S13 and Table S3). The correlation was weaker for biomolecule-mapping negative matrix factorization models (r2=0.572), indicating that uncertainty is most reliable when evaluated within the calibrated inference task.

These results define the practical scope of the platform. The system is deployable after task-specific calibration and can identify measurements likely to fall outside that calibration domain, but new tissue types, species, or molecular targets should be accompanied by new paired training data.

Conclusions

We have introduced a deployable, label-free molecular imaging platform that enables video-rate biochemical mapping in living plant tissue. By integrating passive spectral encoding into a 22-mm miniaturized microscope and training a task-specific molecular decoder, the system converts single-shot autofluorescence measurements directly into spatial maps of endogenous molecular contrast. This approach bypasses hyperspectral datacube reconstruction during deployment and shifts the measurement objective from spectral recovery to inference of biologically relevant molecular states.

Together, the experiments establish three complementary capabilities. First, direct molecular inference from encoded measurements recovers biomolecular abundance maps associated with lignin and chlorophyll in poplar tissue, with suberin-associated contrast evaluated in suberin-rich tissue, without requiring a hyperspectral reference path during use. Second, single-shot encoded acquisition enables molecular videography of living-tissue dynamics, including embolism propagation and wound-induced biochemical remodeling, for which sequential spectral acquisition can introduce temporal mixing as contrast evolves during the scan. Third, the same framework resolves genotype-dependent biochemical differences in living poplar stems and uses uncertainty estimates to flag spectral mismatch and measurements outside the calibration distribution.

The platform therefore addresses a measurement regime that is poorly served by conventional hyperspectral microscopy. Scanning hyperspectral systems can provide detailed spectral information from static specimens, and benchtop snapshot systems can acquire spectral data without scanning. However, living-tissue autofluorescence imposes a different set of constraints: weak endogenous signals, tissue scattering, motion, limited photon budget, long-term viability, and the need for compact tissue-mounted instrumentation. Under these constraints, maximal datacube fidelity is not always the most useful objective. Instead, the relevant goal is reliable measurement of biological quantities under realistic photon, form-factor, and temporal limits.

The use of established probabilistic image-to-image learning provides a practical mechanism for reporting prediction reliability during deployment. In particular, uncertainty estimates help identify ambiguous regions, spectral mismatch, and distribution shift, rather than allowing the system to report overconfident molecular maps outside its validated operating regime. This capability is important for living biological samples, where illumination, tissue structure, autofluorescence composition, and motion can vary across specimens and over time. At the same time, uncertainty does not eliminate the need for calibration: new tissue types, species, excitation conditions, optical configurations, or molecular targets should be accompanied by new paired training data.

The present work is demonstrated in plant systems, where endogenous autofluorescence provides label-free contrast for lignin-, chlorophyll-, and suberin-associated molecular states. The same task-specific measurement principle may be extendable to other biological settings that rely on spectral contrast, provided that the encoder, training data, and inference target are calibrated for the intended application (14, 15). Qu et al. (36) showed that sparsity in a known material-identity library enables material identification from few mid-infrared measurements without repeated model training. That material-level approach is complementary to the spatially resolved fluorescence mapping considered here: incorporating spectral-library constraints could reduce our training burden but would not remove the need to calibrate the optical encoder or validate under tissue-dependent scattering and autofluorescence. Future work will focus on improving photon efficiency, exploring alternative passive spectral encoders, reducing the need for paired calibration data through self-supervised or physics-informed learning strategies (9), and implementing edge-based inference for distributed or field-deployed molecular sensing.

These results support a broader shift in computational molecular imaging: optical systems need not always be designed to reconstruct the richest possible intermediate representation. Instead, passive optical encoding and computational inference can be co-designed to estimate the biological quantities needed for a specific task from task-optimized measurements. For living-tissue molecular videography, this task-specific approach provides a practical route to observing spatiotemporal biochemical dynamics outside traditional laboratory hyperspectral microscopy.

Materials and methods

Snapshot molecular miniscope with passive spectral encoding

The deployable imaging platform was based on the open-source Miniscope V4 architecture (Open Ephys). The system used an Aptina MT9V032 CMOS sensor (608 × 608 pixels; 6 μm pitch) with spectral sensitivity spanning 452–703 nm (20). Autofluorescence excitation was provided by a 385 nm UV LED (Lumileds, LHUV-0385-A045). Excitation light was bandpass filtered (Chroma ET385/70x, 385±35 nm), separated from emission using a dichroic mirror (Chroma T412lpxt), and rejected from the detection path using a long-pass emission filter (Chroma ET425lp).

Passive spectral encoding was implemented by placing a translucent 80 μm-thick low-density polyethylene layer between the emission filter and the CMOS sensor. This element introduced weak wavelength-dependent spatial modulation while preserving the native imaging geometry of the miniscope. We refer to this component as a passive spectral encoder in the main text to emphasize its measurement function; physically, it was implemented here as a low-angle scattering layer. The encoder was selected for its low diffusion angle of 0.046 ∘ and wavelength-dependent response (Figs. S1 and S2). Cross-correlation matrices were computed across 30 wavelength bands for both point-source and plane-wave excitation to characterize the wavelength dependence of the encoded measurements (Fig. S3).

Lens configuration 1 of the Miniscope V4 platform provided a field of view of ≈940 μm (21). An electrowetting variable-focus lens (Varioptic A25H) enabled axial scanning over a 400 μm depth range without translating the sample or probe. The housing was fabricated by 3D printing from publicly available STL files. Power and data were transmitted through a single coaxial cable interfaced with a DAQ PCB module (Labmaker).

Reference hyperspectral acquisition for paired supervision

Paired training data were acquired using a calibration system that imaged the same optical scene with both the passive-encoded miniscope and a benchtop hyperspectral reference path. A dual-channel 4f relay projected the sample plane to the deployable probe and to the reference hyperspectral microscope. The relay used two matched 10× objectives (Amscope PA10X-V300) mounted in a cage system (Thorlabs C4W). A short-pass dichroic mirror (Newport 10SWF-400-B) at the Fourier plane directed light to the reference arm.

Spectral selection in the reference path was performed with a linear variable bandpass filter (LVBF; Edmund Optics 88–365) positioned between two additional 10× objectives. Translating the LVBF across a focused beam produced narrow spectral slices with ≈10 nm bandwidth spanning 452–703 nm (Fig. S5). The LVBF was mounted on a motorized linear stage (RATTMMOTOR ZBX80, 100 mm stroke) and calibrated using a fiber-coupled spectrometer (Ocean Optics JAZ A1100). Reference hyperspectral images were captured using a Hamamatsu ORCA-Flash4.0 LT sCMOS camera and a 160 mm tube lens.

The hyperspectral reference path was used only during calibration, training, and quantitative evaluation. During deployment, molecular maps were generated directly from encoded miniscope frames without reference hyperspectral acquisition.

Data acquisition and synchronization

Automated acquisition and synchronization were implemented in LabVIEW (version 16.0f5). Each calibration acquisition cycle consisted of sequential reference spectral captures followed by a single encoded miniscope frame. LVBF translation, dichroic actuation, reference-image acquisition, and miniscope triggering were synchronized using a LabJack U3 controller. Reference images were acquired through the Hamamatsu Video Capture Library integrated in LabVIEW, and miniscope acquisition was triggered through the open-source Miniscope DAQ interface (37).

Samples were mounted on a motorized XYZ stage (Thorlabs MLJ050) and scanned along predefined 3D trajectories in 100 μm increments across curved branch surfaces. Surface contours were defined by manually placed support points and interpolated to maintain focus across the exposed stem region.

Image preprocessing and flat-field correction

Encoded miniscope images were downsampled from 608 × 608 pixels to 512 × 512 pixels. Reference hyperspectral stacks were spatially registered, cropped, and interpolated to match the encoded miniscope frame size. Images were normalized to the range [0,1] by dividing by bit depth: 256 for 8-bit miniscope images and 65,536 for 16-bit reference hyperspectral images. This normalization preserved relative intensity relationships across spectral channels.

Flat-field correction was applied to the encoded miniscope images to reduce vignetting, improve signal uniformity, and correct spatial nonuniformities introduced by the passive spectral encoder. The flat-field reference was calculated as the average of 400 randomly selected encoded images from the training dataset. Each image was divided by this flat-field reference and multiplied by the mean value of the flat-field reference.

For learning-based models, a custom normalization layer was used in place of instance normalization to preserve spectral and intensity scaling across channels.

Training and test datasets

Paired datasets consisted of single encoded miniscope frames and corresponding 30-channel reference hyperspectral stacks. Data were collected from four independent branch incisions, yielding 6,248 paired training examples and 100 randomly selected held-out test examples. The reference hyperspectral stacks were additionally processed to generate molecular abundance maps representing lignin-associated, chlorophyll-associated, and residual autofluorescence components.

The held-out test set was used for quantitative evaluation of direct molecular inference, reconstruction-based baselines, and uncertainty calibration. Dynamic embolism and wound-response videos, genotype-resolved imaging experiments, and out-of-distribution samples were analyzed separately as described below.

Reference-data processing and molecular abundance maps

Reference molecular abundance maps were generated from hyperspectral autofluorescence stacks acquired by the benchtop calibration path. Each hyperspectral stack was spatially registered, cropped, and interpolated to match the encoded miniscope frame. Images were normalized to preserve relative spectral intensity across channels. The reference stacks served two purposes: first, to provide supervised targets for training the direct molecular inference model; and second, to provide quantitative reference maps for evaluating molecular estimation accuracy.

To estimate endogenous molecular components, the hyperspectral stacks were unmixed using Poisson negative matrix factorization (NMF). The resulting abundance channels represented dominant autofluorescence components associated with lignin, chlorophyll, and residual or tissue-specific autofluorescence. For suberin-rich samples, additional component maps were generated to visualize suberin-associated contrast. The NMF-derived abundance maps were used as reference targets rather than as absolute biochemical concentration measurements. This distinction is important because autofluorescence intensity depends on molecular abundance, excitation and collection efficiency, scattering, absorption, and local tissue structure.

For the primary poplar datasets, molecular maps were generated for lignin- and chlorophyll-associated contrast. Lignin-associated emission was identified by its strong contribution in the green autofluorescence range and its anatomical localization to xylem and secondary cell wall structures. Chlorophyll-associated emission was identified by its longer-wavelength autofluorescence and localization to outer photosynthetic tissue. These assignments were supported by known autofluorescence signatures of plant biomolecules and by the anatomical organization of the stem tissue (14, 18, 19).

The reference molecular maps were used only for calibration, training, and quantitative evaluation. During deployment, the miniaturized probe acquired only encoded autofluorescence frames, and molecular abundance maps were generated directly from those frames by the trained decoder.

Direct molecular inference and uncertainty estimation

Direct molecular inference was implemented as supervised image-to-image regression from an encoded autofluorescence frame to a multichannel molecular abundance map. For each paired training example, the input was a single encoded frame acquired by the miniaturized probe, and the target was a spatially registered molecular abundance map derived from the reference hyperspectral measurement. The paired data were collected using the calibration system described above, in which the deployable probe and the benchtop hyperspectral reference path imaged the same field of view. The hyperspectral reference path was used only during calibration to generate supervision data and was not required during deployment.

The decoder used a convolutional image-to-image architecture based on a U-Net generator adapted from established pix2pix-style supervised translation models (22). To increase representational capacity without substantially increasing parameter count, we evaluated a multipass U-Net design in which decoder features were propagated to subsequent encoder stages through shared skip connections. Epistemic uncertainty was estimated using an ensemble of five independently trained networks. Each ensemble member was trained with the same paired data but with independent initialization and stochastic training order. For each test image, the ensemble produced a set of predicted molecular maps and uncertainty scales. To correct residual spatial misalignment between encoded miniscope frames and reference hyperspectral data during training, a separate registration U-Net (39) estimated a deformable displacement field that was applied before loss computation. The registration module was used only during training and was not used during deployment or inference.

During deployment, only the miniaturized probe and trained decoder were used. Each encoded frame was passed directly through the trained model to generate molecular abundance maps and uncertainty estimates. No hyperspectral reconstruction, spectral scanning, or reference hyperspectral measurement was performed during deployment. Uncertainty estimates were used to identify ambiguous regions and measurements outside the validated operating regime of the trained model; they were not treated as a substitute for calibration. When applied to new tissue types, species, optical configurations, excitation wavelengths, or molecular targets, the model should be recalibrated using paired training data because the learned mapping depends on the optical encoder, illumination spectrum, tissue autofluorescence, and biological target distribution.

Reconstruction-based baseline

To evaluate the benefit of direct molecular inference, we compared the proposed model with a reconstruction-based baseline. In this baseline, the encoded measurement was first used to reconstruct a hyperspectral datacube. The reconstructed datacube was then unmixed using the same NMF procedure used to generate molecular reference maps. This two-step approach represents the conventional pipeline in which spectral reconstruction and molecular interpretation are performed sequentially.

For a controlled comparison, the reconstruction-based model was trained and evaluated using the same paired encoded and reference data used for direct molecular inference. The resulting molecular abundance maps were compared against the same hyperspectral-derived reference abundance maps. Performance was quantified using mean absolute error (MAE), mean squared error (MSE), structural similarity index (SSIM), and peak signal-to-noise ratio (PSNR), averaged over the test set and output channels.

This baseline is not intended to represent all possible hyperspectral imaging systems. Instead, it provides a comparison between two computational uses of the same encoded measurement: one that reconstructs a datacube before molecular unmixing, and one that directly predicts the molecular quantities of interest. This comparison tests whether optimizing the deployed measurement for biomolecular inference improves molecular estimation relative to recovery of an intermediate spectral representation.

Implementation details

Models were trained for 30 epochs on a single NVIDIA RTX 3060 GPU. The same GPU was used to evaluate inference speed. Biomolecular inference with the trained direct mapping model ran at 71 frames/s. Additional architectural ablations, reconstruction models, training variants, and quantitative comparisons are reported in Table S2.

Resolution analysis

Spatial resolution was estimated using Fourier-domain analysis and USAF resolution-chart analysis. For Fourier-domain analysis, 2D FFTs were computed for each spectral slice, and azimuthal averaging yielded radial power spectra. The resolution cutoff was defined as the spatial frequency at which the logarithm of the radial power spectrum fell below 0.1 (Supplementary Section 13 and Figs. S20–S22).

Resolution analysis was performed with and without space-variant Wiener deconvolution. Deconvolution was performed using empirically measured point spread functions derived from a sparse calibration scan of 100 illuminated pixels (40, 41).

Video-rate acquisition and inference

For dynamic biological experiments, the miniaturized probe acquired encoded autofluorescence frames without wavelength scanning, filter switching, or mechanical spectral selection. The system supports acquisition rates up to 60 frames/s. During living-tissue experiments, the camera was typically operated at 10 frames/s to improve signal levels under low-photon UV-excited autofluorescence conditions.

Inference speed was measured by passing encoded frames through the trained molecular inference model on an NVIDIA RTX 3060 GPU. Biomolecular inference was performed at 71 frames/s for the trained direct mapping model, confirming that computational inference was not the limiting factor for video-rate molecular mapping under the acquisition conditions used in the biological experiments.

The acquisition time of the benchtop hyperspectral reference path depended on the number of wavelength bands, exposure time, LVBF translation, and synchronization overhead. Because such acquisition times vary across hyperspectral systems and experimental conditions, comparisons in the main text focus on the conceptual distinction between sequential spectral acquisition and single-shot encoded acquisition rather than on a fixed speed ratio.

Plant material and sample preparation

For the primary dataset, branch cuttings from 1-year-old Populus nigra × P. deltoides trees were grown under a 14 h light / 10 h dark photoperiod for 2 years. Imaging samples were collected from branches between the 5th and 10th internodes. To expose xylem tissue, a narrow strip of bark ≈3 cm long and 200 μm deep was removed. Branches were stabilized on a tip-tilt stage using a custom 3D-printed clamp.

Transgenic PDC poplar lines were generated as described previously (32). Imaging was performed at 65 sites per genotype. Cork oak bark (Quercus suber) was imaged to evaluate model behavior on suberin-rich tissue with autofluorescence composition distinct from the primary poplar training distribution (33–35).

Embolism imaging and velocity analysis

Embolism dynamics were imaged in living poplar stems using the deployed miniaturized probe under UV excitation. The probe was positioned over exposed xylem tissue, and encoded autofluorescence videos were acquired at video rate. Each frame was converted into molecular abundance maps using the trained direct inference model.

Embolism events were identified as localized moving features within xylem vessels that produced changes in autofluorescence-derived contrast. The position of each embolism was tracked across sequential frames. Propagation velocity was calculated from the displacement of the embolism centroid divided by the elapsed time between frames. For slow embolisms, velocities were reported in μm/min. For rapid events that traversed the field of view over one or a few frames, velocities were estimated from the measured displacement and frame interval and reported in mm/s.

The transverse size of each embolism was estimated from the apparent width of the moving feature in the molecular maps. Changes in lignin- and chlorophyll-associated signal were quantified by measuring the relative intensity change at representative spatial locations before and during embolism passage. These measurements were used to assess how embolism formation altered autofluorescence-derived molecular contrast and tissue scattering.

Wound-response imaging and tissue-shrinkage analysis

Longitudinal wound-response imaging was performed on living poplar stems after a minimally invasive incision. The miniaturized probe was positioned over the wound region, and encoded autofluorescence frames were acquired over a 43-h period. Molecular abundance maps were generated from each encoded frame using the trained direct inference model.

Changes in molecular contrast were evaluated by tracking lignin- and chlorophyll-associated signals over time. Chlorophyll-associated emission was quantified from selected regions of interest and normalized to the initial time point or to a reference region, as indicated in the corresponding figure legends. Progressive changes in chlorophyll-associated signal were interpreted in the context of stress-induced physiological responses in damaged plant tissue (31).

Tissue shrinkage was estimated from the changing distance between anatomical landmarks surrounding the wound site. Distances were measured across the time-lapse sequence after registration or alignment of the field of view. The resulting displacement trajectory was fit with a two-phase model to estimate an initial contraction rate and a slower long-time contraction rate.

These displacement measurements are consistent with prior live-poplar autofluorescence microscopy and with the broader stem-water literature. In an earlier deployable autofluorescence miniscope study, poplar wound geometry changed by tens of micrometers: longitudinal wound width increased from ≈50 to ≈100 μm over 7 days, cross-sectional wound width increased from ≈30 to ≈60 μm over 3 h, and measured shrinkage was strongly anisotropic, reaching 26% tangential versus 3% longitudinal over 7 days and 22% tangential versus 4% longitudinal over 3 h (40). More broadly, stem-diameter variation is commonly attributed to water-content-driven shrinkage and swelling of stem tissues (42), with reported diurnal stem-radius amplitudes of 85–109 μm in conifers exposed to soil dryness (43). The two-phase contraction measured here therefore falls within a known micrometer-scale hydraulic deformation regime, while adding co-registered autofluorescence-derived molecular contrast over the same wound field without destructive sampling or repeated spectral scans.

Genotype-resolved imaging of PDC transgenic poplar

To evaluate genotype-dependent biochemical contrast, we imaged WT and PDC transgenic Populus stems. The PDC lines were engineered to alter lignin biosynthesis through accumulation of 2-pyrone-4,6-dicarboxylic acid, as described previously (32). Imaging was performed at 65 independent sites spanning multiple stems and biological replicates.

For each site, encoded autofluorescence frames were acquired with the miniaturized probe and converted into molecular abundance maps using the trained decoder. Lignin-associated autofluorescence was quantified from the relevant molecular channel and from the 500–550 nm spectral region in the reference data when available. Signals were normalized to account for field-to-field variation in illumination, collection efficiency, and tissue geometry.

WT and PDC transgenic samples were compared using normalized lignin-associated signal and anatomical localization of autofluorescence within the xylem. The optical measurements were interpreted as label-free indicators of genotype-dependent biochemical remodeling, not as direct chemical assays of lignin concentration. Quantitative comparisons are reported in the Supplementary material.

Out-of-distribution evaluation and uncertainty calibration

To evaluate behavior beyond the calibration distribution, the trained model was applied to tissue types and species not fully represented in the primary poplar training data. These included cork oak (Quercus suber), a suberin-rich species with autofluorescence signatures that differ from chlorophyll-dominated poplar tissue (33–35). Encoded frames were acquired using the same miniaturized probe and processed with the trained molecular inference model.

Predicted molecular maps and uncertainty estimates were inspected to determine whether the model identified spectral mismatch or tissue compositions outside the training distribution. Hyperspectral reference data, where available, were used to compare predicted spectra or molecular abundance maps with measured autofluorescence signatures. Regions or wavelength ranges with elevated prediction error were compared with the corresponding predicted uncertainty scale.

To quantify uncertainty calibration, predicted scale was compared with empirical MAE over held-out datasets, including time-lapse recordings from Populus nigra × P. deltoides, cork oak bark, and Populus tremula × P. alba. The coefficient of determination, r2, was computed between image-level predicted uncertainty and measured error. This analysis was used to assess whether uncertainty estimates track prediction reliability and to define the validated operating regime of the trained model.
