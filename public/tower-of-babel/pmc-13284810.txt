Acute and 14-day effects of tiotropium/olodaterol on cardiopulmonary function in COPD
Authors: Thomas Kayser, Nadja Struß, René Pflock, Karsten Heusser, Agilo L. Kern, Andreas Voskrebenzev, Lea Behrendt, Till F. Kaireit, Philipp Badorrek, Jens Tank, Stefan Andreas, Jens Jordan, Jens Vogel-Claussen, Jens M. Hohlfeld
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLung hyperinflation in COPD adversely affects cardiac function and may influence autonomic nervous system activity. The onset of cardiac improvement with dual bronchodilator therapy remains uncertain.MethodsIn this randomised, placebo-controlled, investigator-blinded, mechanistic, single-dose crossover trial with an open-label 2-week extension we assessed tiotropium/olodaterol (T/O) effects on cardiopulmonary function and muscle sympathetic nerve activity (MSNA) in hyperinflated COPD patients. 32 participants received saline (placebo) and T/O (5 µg/5 µg) in a crossover design, followed by 14 days of open-label T/O. The primary end-point was change in left ventricular end-diastolic volume index (LV-EDVi) measured by magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). Secondary assessments included pulmonary function tests, MSNA measurements and advanced lung imaging including 129Xe-MRI.FindingsSingle-dose T/O significantly increased LV-EDVi relative to placebo (3.25 mL·m−2; 95% CI 0.95 to 5.56), driven partly by a decrease under placebo. Compared with baseline, T/O's acute LV-EDVi gain was minimal, yet it reached significance at 14 days (4.70 mL·m−2; 95% CI 1.75 to 7.65). Pulmonary parameters, in contrast, showed immediate improvement, with a marked reduction in residual volume (−0.67 L; 95% CI −0.82 to −0.51) after a single dose. MSNA demonstrated a nonsignificant numerical rise post single-dose T/O. No serious adverse events occurred.InterpretationT/O rapidly improves pulmonary function in hyperinflated COPD patients, but significant cardiac benefits seem to require sustained therapy. These results underscore the importance of continued dual bronchodilator therapy to achieve cardiovascular improvements. The nonsignificant rise in MSNA after a single dose suggests minimal immediate effect on sympathetic activity; further studies are necessary to evaluate long-term autonomic outcomes.

Introduction

COPD is a leading cause of morbidity and mortality, affecting 384 million people worldwide and accounting for a prevalence of 11.7% [1]. Globally, it is the fourth leading cause of death [2]. Although COPD primarily affects the lungs, cardiovascular disease is its most common extrapulmonary comorbidity [3], with lung hyperinflation being a significant contributor to impaired cardiac function [4]. As airflow obstruction worsens and emphysema progresses, increased air trapping with elevated intrathoracic (intrapleural) pressures likely reduces venous return and preload, reflected in diminished left ventricular filling as well as reduced stroke volume and cardiac output [5–7]. Mechanical compression from hyperinflated lungs may also directly alter cardiac geometry and chamber dimensions [4, 8].

Furthermore, beyond the mechanical impairments of cardiac function, autonomic dysfunction – particularly increased sympathetic nerve activity – also seems to play a substantial role in COPD [9]. Muscle sympathetic nerve activity (MSNA), a direct measure of efferent sympathetic discharge to skeletal muscle vasculature, is elevated in patients with COPD compared to healthy controls [10]. This heightened sympathetic tone may contribute to cardiovascular comorbidities by promoting arrhythmias, hypertension and atherosclerosis, thereby increasing morbidity and mortality [11]. The underlying mechanisms of the sympathetic overactivity are complex, involving hypoxaemia, systemic inflammation and reduced baroreflex sensitivity [9].

Regarding pharmacotherapy, the Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease (GOLD) guidelines recommend a combination of a long-acting β2-agonist (LABA) and a long-acting muscarinic antagonist (LAMA) as the preferred treatment for patients with COPD classified in groups B and E [12]. This dual therapy is recommended over monotherapy due to its superior bronchodilation, leading to improved lung function and reduced hyperinflation, as well as better symptom control and a lower rate of exacerbations [13–15]. Beyond pulmonary outcomes, bronchodilator therapy has been associated with improvements in cardiac function among patients with COPD. Such effects have been observed with LABA monotherapy [16], and with the addition of inhaled corticosteroids to LABA therapy [17]. Furthermore, a 14-day crossover study demonstrated that using a LABA/LAMA combination, specifically indacaterol/glycopyrronium (CLAIM study), led to a significant increase of ∼10% in left ventricular end-diastolic volume index (LV-EDVi), implying improved cardiac filling associated with lung deflation [18, 19].

However, the effects of dual bronchodilation on both cardiac function and sympathetic nerve activity in patients with COPD remain underexplored. Therefore, in this study, we aimed to assess the impact of tiotropium/olodaterol (T/O) – both acutely (primary objective) and after multiple dosing – on cardiac performance and autonomic regulation in COPD patients. Using magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)-based comprehensive cardiopulmonary imaging (focusing on LV-EDVi as our primary end-point), and measuring MSNA, we sought to gain detailed insights into these effects. Additionally, we performed classical pulmonary function tests, including spirometry, body plethysmography, diffusing capacity of the lung for carbon monoxide (DLCO) and impulse oscillometry (IOS), to provide a thorough assessment of pulmonary function.

Methods

Study design and participants

We conducted an investigator-initiated, randomised, placebo-controlled, investigator-blinded, mechanistic, single-dose crossover trial with an open-label, 2-week multiple-dose extension at a single centre, the Fraunhofer Institute for Toxicology and Experimental Medicine, Hannover, Germany, with the Fraunhofer Society, Munich, serving as the legal sponsor representative. The study protocol was approved by the ethics committee of Hannover Medical School and the German Federal Institute for Drugs and Medical Devices (BfArM). The trial was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT04231214).

Eligible patients were aged ≥40 years with a confirmed diagnosis of COPD, a smoking history of ≥10 pack-years, and evidence of baseline hyperinflation with a residual volume (RV) >135% predicted. Inclusion required post-bronchodilator forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1) <80% predicted and FEV1/forced vital capacity (FVC) ratio <0.7. Patients with stable cardiovascular conditions were included, but those with arrhythmias, heart failure with reduced left ventricular ejection fraction <40%, unstable ischaemic heart disease or uncontrolled hypertension were excluded (full criteria detailed in supplementary table S1). All participants provided written informed consent.

Randomisation and intervention

Following screening and baseline assessments, participants were randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio using a fixed-4-block scheme to one of two sequences: Sequence A received two puffs of nebulised physiological saline (B. Braun, Melsungen, Germany, placebo) followed by T/O after a 7-day washout, while Sequence B received T/O followed by placebo (figure 1). The active treatment consisted of tiotropium bromide monohydrate/olodaterol hydrochloride (5 µg/5 µg), administered as two puffs of Spiolto Respimat (Boehringer Ingelheim, Ingelheim am Rhein, Germany). Due to the lack of a placebo Respimat device, the study medication was administered by unblinded staff to keep the investigator blinded.

Study design illustrating treatment phases and assessments. Participants underwent a screening phase of up to 3 weeks before baseline assessments at Visit 2. The single-dose (SD) crossover treatments occurred at Visits 3 and 4 (with a washout of 7 days): Sequence A received placebo followed by tiotropium/olodaterol (T/O), and Sequence B received T/O followed by placebo. Following the crossover phase, all participants entered a 2-week open-label phase with once daily multiple-dose (MD) T/O treatment, concluding with assessments at Visit 5. Throughout the study, assessments included magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and pulmonary function tests (PFTs) to monitor cardiopulmonary function and therapeutic effects. Muscle sympathetic nerve activity (MSNA) was evaluated before and after single-dose T/O. Exhaled particles (PEx) were measured throughout the study for method development.

Following the single-dose crossover phase, all participants entered a 2-week open-label period, inhaling once-daily T/O at home. Concomitant medications for hypertension were maintained throughout the study. Prior COPD medications, except for inhaled corticosteroids (if stable for at least 30 days), were discontinued before baseline. Salbutamol was provided as maintenance substitution or as needed for symptom relief.

Outcome measures

The primary end-point was the change in LV-EDVi measured by MRI after single-dose T/O compared with placebo. Secondary end-points included additional cardiopulmonary MRI parameters, MSNA and pulmonary function tests, including spirometry, body plethysmography, diffusing capacity and impulse oscillometry, following single and multiple doses of T/O.

Assessments

At screening, echocardiography was performed to evaluate for diastolic dysfunction. Throughout the study (figure 1), pulmonary function tests, including spirometry, body plethysmography, DLCO and IOS, were conducted following established protocols [20–23]. Cardiac MRI was utilised to measure cardiac volumes and function, with radiologists blinded to treatment assignments and trial-related data including visit date. In addition, advanced lung imaging methods - including contrast-enhanced perfusion MRI [19] and hyperpolarised 129Xe MRI [24, 25] (covering both ventilation imaging and ADC mapping) – were performed at baseline and after single and multiple dosing to provide comprehensive assessments of lung ventilation and perfusion. To assess the impact on autonomic regulation, MSNA was measured via peroneal microneurography in the popliteal space before and after single-dose administration [26]. Furthermore, exhaled particles were measured throughout the study for further method development [27]. Safety assessments included monitoring vital signs, electrocardiograms, laboratory tests, and the recording and classification of adverse events (AEs) according to the MedDRA coding system (version 24.0).

Statistical analysis

Assuming a standard deviation of 10 mL and intending to detect a 4 mL difference in LV-EDVi with ∼80% power at a two-sided 5% significance level, a required sample size of 48 participants was calculated (nQuery V 8.5). Data analyses included all randomised individuals who received at least one treatment dose (full analysis set (FAS)); missing data were not imputed. Descriptive statistics were generated for all variables, presenting arithmetic means with standard deviations (sd). Inferential analyses for both the primary and secondary end-points were performed using linear mixed models in R (version 4.4) with the lmerTest package [28]. Fixed effects included treatment, period and sequence, with participants treated as random effect. Exploratory analyses added a treatment-by-diastolic dysfunction interaction to assess effect modification on LV-EDVi. Estimated marginal means (also known as least squares means) [29] and corresponding two-sided 95% CIs were obtained via the emmeans package (https://CRAN.R-project.org/package=emmeans). Model assumptions were verified in advance. No multiplicity correction was implemented for secondary outcomes.

Results

Disposition of participants

Between 30 June 2020 and 18 April 2023 we screened 81 patients and randomly assigned 32 eligible participants to single-dose treatment (16 per sequence; FAS, n=32). The intended enrolment of 48 participants was not achieved due to recruitment challenges during the COVID-19 pandemic, leading to premature study termination. Although all participants completed the dosing regimen during the multiple-dose phase, four did not complete all outcome assessments afterwards: two participants discontinued the study before these assessments (one due to COVID-19 and one due to a common cold). Two additional participants discontinued during the final in-house visit owing to dyspnoea following MRI scans, resulting in incomplete assessments. One of these was also excluded from the per-protocol analysis due to improper inhaler technique at home (empty inhaler cartridge and observed incorrect use), reducing the per-protocol population to 31 participants (figure 2). The other participants remained in the per-protocol population.

Demographic and baseline characteristics

The study included 32 Caucasian participants with a mean±sd age of 61.5±7.7 years. The sex distribution was nearly equal: 17 males (53%) and 15 females (47%). Participants had a mean±sd body mass index of 26.72±4.29 kg·m−2. A majority were current smokers (72%), with a sizable mean smoking history of 55.4±23.0 pack-years. In terms of disease severity, 69% of participants were classified as GOLD stage 2, 25% as GOLD stage 3 and 6% as GOLD stage 4, indicating that most had moderate to severe COPD. Diastolic dysfunction was confirmed in 44% and considered possible in 34% of participants. Pulmonary function tests showed marked airflow obstruction and hyperinflation, evidenced by reduced post-bronchodilator FEV1 and FVC, a decreased FEV1/FVC ratio and an elevated RV (mean 4.13±1.26 L, 186% predicted). These results are consistent with the targeted population of hyperinflated COPD patients. Further baseline characteristics are summarised in table 1.

Cardiac and pulmonary outcomes

At baseline, the arithmetic mean LV-EDVi was 58.69±14.17 mL·m−2. Following a single dose of placebo, LV-EDVi decreased to 56.17±13.40 mL·m−2, a finding replicated across both sequences. After a single dose of T/O, LV-EDVi reached 59.43±12.79 mL·m−2, slightly surpassing baseline. By the end of the 2-week multiple-dose phase, however, LV-EDVi markedly increased to 64.27±13.47 mL·m−2 (figure 3a).

For the primary end-point, application of the linear mixed model showed that a single dose of T/O significantly increased LV-EDVi compared with placebo (estimated contrast, 3.25 mL·m−2; 95% CI 0.95 to 5.56) (figure 3b). LV-EDVi rose further with multiple-dose T/O (4.45 mL·m−2; 95% CI 1.62 to 7.28). No significant period or sequence effects were detected in the crossover analysis. Comparisons with baseline provided additional context, revealing a minor increase after single-dose T/O (0.74 mL·m−2; 95% CI −2.66 to 4.14) and a numerical decrease with single-dose placebo (−2.51 mL·m−2; 95% CI −5.34 to 0.31). Notably, the sum of these absolute changes equalled the observed contrast between single-dose T/O and placebo, underscoring the consistency of the model. A statistically significant improvement over baseline was observed only after multiple-dose T/O (4.70 mL·m−2; 95% CI 1.75 to 7.65).

A very similar pattern was observed for cardiac output in terms of LV-Svi (figure 3c and d). After single-dose administration, we saw a difference of 2.27 mL·m−2 (95% CI 0.58 to 3.95) between T/O and placebo and a difference of 2.52 mL·m−2 (95% CI 0.37 to 4.66) between T/O multiple-dose and T/O single dose. Again, the breakdown of comparisons to baseline showed a trend towards a slight decrease after placebo administration (−0.38 mL·m−2; 95% CI −2.68 to 1.92), while after single-dose T/O administration an increase was observed (1.89 mL·m−2; 95% CI −0.45 to 4.22), only reaching the significance threshold after multiple-dose T/O administration (4.09 mL·m−2; 95% CI 2.31 to 5.88). RV-EDVi exhibited a temporal response pattern similar to LV-EDVi, with treatment–placebo differences evident after single dose but relevant changes from baseline emerging only after 14 days of therapy (table 2).

An additional post hoc analysis stratified by baseline diastolic dysfunction status revealed no significant interaction terms, though estimated contrasts suggested trends: acute treatment–placebo differences were consistent across subgroups, but baseline-anchored responses diverged. In patients with any diastolic dysfunction (possible/confirmed, n=25), single-dose T/O showed near-zero change from baseline, while placebo produced a directional reduction that contributed to the treatment–placebo contrast. Patients without diastolic dysfunction (n=7) demonstrated numerical gains with single-dose therapy and no placebo decline. At 14 days, both subgroups exhibited numerical increases from baseline, with greater magnitude in those with any diastolic dysfunction (supplementary table S8 and figure S1).

In contrast to the more gradual biventricular, time-dependent changes observed in cardiac indices, RV, as a representative parameter of hyperinflation, demonstrated a significant decrease from baseline already after a single dose T/O (−0.59 L; 95% CI −0.73 to −0.44). Subsequent multiple dosing resulted in a further, albeit nonsignificant, reduction in RV when compared to the single dose (−0.15 L; 95% CI −0.34 to 0.05) (figure 3e and f). Similarly, other lung function parameters such as FEV1, peak expiratory flow (PEF), mean forced expiratory flow between 25% and 75% of FVC (MFEF25-75), specific effective airway resistance (sReff), RV/TLC ratio, R5-20 and AX also showed significant improvements after a single dose of T/O, with only minor additional effects observed following multiple dosing (table 2).

Consistent with its robust effects on conventional lung function parameters, T/O demonstrated favourable changes on 129Xe MRI; however, most notably after 2 weeks of daily therapy (table 2). While single-dose T/O did not significantly alter ventilation defect percentage (VDP) relative to baseline (−0.23%; 95% CI −3.93 to 3.47), multiple-dose T/O reduced VDP by −3.88% (95% CI −7.62 to −0.14) compared to single-dose T/O and by −4.04% (95% CI −7.93 to −0.15) from baseline. Apparent diffusion coefficient measurements (ADC5000 µs) remained largely unchanged, with a slight, nonsignificant rise of 0.17 mm2·s−1 (95% CI −0.00 to 0.34) observed after multiple doses.

Pulmonary blood flow (PBF), assessed as mean by perfusion MRI, showed a significant increase after single-dose T/O compared to single-dose placebo (6.76 mL·min−1/100 mLlung volume; 95% CI 3.30 to 10.21) (table 2). Interestingly, similar to the pattern observed with LV-EDVi, this effect appeared to be also driven by a decrease in PBF following single-dose placebo when compared to baseline (−4.00 mL·min−1/100 mL; 95% CI −8.01 to 0.02).

Safety results

In the safety analysis (FAS), 49 AEs were identified in 24 patients. The most frequently affected system organ classes were respiratory (including coughing, dyspnoea and COPD exacerbations) and nervous system disorders (such as headache and dizziness). The intensity of AEs was predominantly mild to moderate, with only two events classified as severe. All AEs resolved or patients recovered, with the exception of one case of COVID-19 that resolved with sequelae. No serious adverse events occurred during the study, underscoring the safety and tolerability of the interventions. An overview of AEs is provided in supplementary table S7.

Discussion

This study assessed the effects of dual bronchodilation with T/O on cardiopulmonary function and sympathetic nerve activity in COPD patients with confirmed hyperinflation, focusing on both the acute response to a single dose and the sustained effects after 14 days of multiple dosing.

Cardiac effects of T/O may require sustained dosing

Single-dose T/O significantly increased LV-EDVi relative to placebo, highlighting the primary treatment effect and reflecting a protective effect against the decline observed with placebo. From baseline, however, the acute increase with T/O was numerically small. Thus, while the crossover design isolates the intervention's effect, baseline-referenced analyses indicate that only sustained T/O therapy results in a significant and clinically meaningful increase in LV-EDVi above baseline. Similar patterns were observed for LV-SVi. These findings suggest that substantial cardiac benefit of dual bronchodilation emerges mainly after continued treatment, underscoring the importance of sustained therapy in this population.

The necessity of sustained dosing for significant cardiac effects aligns with the CLAIM study [18], where a 14-day course of indacaterol/glycopyrronium resulted in a significant LV-EDVi increase of 5.23 mL·m−2 (95% CI 3.22 to 7.25). Likewise, our multiple-dose T/O regimen yielded a 4.53 mL·m−2 (95% CI 1.58 to 7.48) increase. Herth et al. [30] observed smaller but still positive changes with both T/O and fluticasone/salmeterol over 6 weeks (T/O: 2.317 mL·m−2 (95% CI 0.061 to 4.574); F/S: 2.855 mL·m−2 (95% CI 0.597 to 5.112)), without a significant difference between therapies. Collectively, these data reinforce the benefit of extended bronchodilator therapy for cardiac function in COPD.

Rapid improvement in pulmonary parameters

In contrast to the seemingly delayed cardiac effects, pulmonary parameters in the current study demonstrated rapid improvement following T/O administration. RV, as a key indicator of hyperinflation, showed a significant decrease from baseline already after single-dose T/O, with minimal additional reduction after multiple dosing. This rapid impact on lung hyperinflation implies that the near maximal pulmonary benefit is achieved with almost the initial dose of T/O. Similar immediate improvements were observed in other pulmonary function parameters, including FEV1, PEF, MFEF25-75, sReff, RV/TLC ratio, R5-20 and AX.

Temporal discrepancy between pulmonary function enhancement and cardiac response

Although T/O administration leads to immediate improvements in pulmonary function and rapid relief of hyperinflation, corresponding cardiac benefits above baseline do not occur acutely. The expected reduction in mechanical constraints and restoration of preload should theoretically enhance left ventricular filling. Nonetheless, our findings indicate that while hyperinflation is rapidly resolved, LV-EDVi, and thus preload, improves only gradually, suggesting a temporal dissociation between pulmonary and cardiac adaptation. As described by Alter et al. [7], hyperinflation in COPD primarily impedes left ventricular filling by reducing preload rather than by affecting myocardial relaxation.

However, the presumed delay in cardiac improvement may reflect the time required for ventricular compliance to improve, and our exploratory post hoc analysis stratified by baseline diastolic dysfunction status may provide insight: Although the acute placebo-corrected effects of single-dose T/O on LV-EDVi were consistent across diastolic dysfunction subgroups, the baseline-anchored responses revealed a distinctive pattern. In patients with any diastolic dysfunction (possible or confirmed), single-dose therapy produced minimal change from baseline, while placebo administration was associated with a directional reduction in LV-EDVi. Conversely, patients without diastolic dysfunction demonstrated numerical improvements in LV-EDVi even after single-dose therapy, with no decline observed under placebo conditions. By 14 days, both subgroups exhibited increases in LV-EDVi from baseline, though the magnitude was greater in those with preexisting diastolic impairment. Notably, the subgroup without diastolic dysfunction comprised only seven participants, and our study did not include post-treatment echocardiographic assessments of diastolic function or measurements of cardiac biomarkers such as N-terminal pro-brain natriuretic peptide. However, these exploratory findings, while hypothesis-generating, suggest that preexisting diastolic dysfunction may act as a temporal modulator of cardiac response, potentially reflecting the time required for ventricular compliance to improve or for diastolic stiffness to regress.

Impact on autonomic balance

Given the role of autonomic dysfunction in COPD-related cardiovascular comorbidities, we incorporated MSNA measurements to evaluate the impact of T/O on sympathetic nerve activity. Olodaterol, an ultra-LABA, can cause peripheral vasodilation and sympathetic activation [31], while presynaptic β2-receptors on adrenergic terminals may amplify norepinephrine release [32]. In parallel, tiotropium, a long-acting muscarinic antagonist, reduces parasympathetic tone, potentially shifting autonomic balance towards sympathetic predominance.

Reflecting these pharmacological effects, single-dose T/O produced a small rise in MSNA, consistent with hypotheses that long-acting bronchodilators may initially boost sympathetic drive and therefore transiently increase cardiovascular risk [33]. Yet the numerical change was minor and nonsignificant, and the stability of heart rate implies limited clinical impact. Furthermore, it remains possible that sustained improvements in lung function and cardiac performance, by alleviating excessive work of breathing, correcting chronic hypoxaemia and enhancing cardiac filling, could diminish stress-mediated sympathetic activation over time. Further studies are needed to determine whether extended dual bronchodilator therapy ultimately reduces MSNA.

Advanced lung imaging findings

PBF, as assessed by perfusion MRI, was significantly increased by T/O versus placebo after a single dose, suggesting an early haemodynamic response to dual bronchodilation. Notably, these improvements did not substantially progress with ongoing therapy, and, particularly with respect to baseline comparisons, the absolute magnitude of improvement in our cohort was less pronounced than reported in CLAIM – a difference likely attributable to the limited statistical power inherent to our smaller sample size for this secondary end-point. VDP, in contrast, decreased significantly only after multiple dosing. This delayed improvement likely reflects the time required for processes such as mucus clearance and the gradual recruitment of poorly ventilated or previously obstructed lung regions, necessitating repeated therapy and resolution of airway swelling or inflammation. In addition, the slight rise in ADC following prolonged therapy might reflect that even areas with pronounced tissue damage ultimately regained partial ventilation.

Safety profile

No serious AEs were reported and, in summary, our results suggest that acute administration of T/O does not pose any additional cardiovascular risk, but rather improves cardiac function in the long run. Our findings add to the known safety profile of T/O and support its continued use in the management of COPD with hyperinflation.

Limitations

Our findings should be interpreted in light of certain limitations. First, the small sample size, resulting from significant recruitment difficulties during the COVID-19 pandemic, may limit both the statistical power and external validity of the results. Nonetheless, the crossover design provided strong intra-individual controls, and the single-centre approach reduced variability. Second, the study's placebo control was restricted to a single-dose, single-blind phase, while the multiple-dose treatment phase was open-label, potentially allowing for participant bias. Lastly, the reduction in LV-EDVi observed after placebo administration warrants consideration. Normal saline was used as the placebo in accordance with established protocols [34] and is generally regarded as physiologically inert. Because patient influence on cardiac MRI outcomes is minimal, a nocebo effect appears unlikely. Notably, PBF decreased with saline inhalation compared with baseline, whereas MFEF25-75 and IOS, both measures of small-airway function, remained stable, suggesting subtle effects on vascular tone with inhaled physiological saline used as placebo. However, measurement variability remains a plausible explanation, and further investigation is warranted.

Conclusion

This study indicates that while T/O rapidly improves pulmonary function in hyperinflated COPD patients, significant cardiac benefits seem to require sustained therapy. A minimal rise in MSNA after single-dose T/O suggests limited acute impact on sympathetic activity, although long-term effects remain unclear and warrant further exploration. Taken together, these insights confirm dual bronchodilation as an integral component of COPD therapy, underscoring the complex interplay among pulmonary, cardiac and autonomic systems in this disease.
