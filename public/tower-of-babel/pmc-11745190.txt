Type B Aortic Dissection Secondary to Non-typhoidal Salmonella Aortitis: A Case Report and Literature Review
Authors: Laxman Wagle, Dhiraj R. Regmi, Alexander Reyes, Rashmita Regmi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Infectious aortitis is an uncommon but potentially fatal condition that can lead to aortic dissection or rupture. We describe a case of a 69-year-old female who developed a Stanford type B aortic dissection, presumptively caused by Salmonella, which was successfully managed with thoracic endovascular aneurysm repair (TEVAR) and long-term antibiotics. A literature review of 17 reported cases from 2000 to 2024 of aortic dissection secondary to infectious aortitis was conducted. The most frequently implicated causative organisms were Salmonella and Staphylococcus species. Presenting symptoms were non-specific, such as fever, chest or abdominal pain, and constitutional symptoms. Predisposing factors included hypertension, malignancy, diabetes, atherosclerosis, and hyperlipidemia. The proposed pathogenesis involves bacterial seeding of the aortic wall, leading to enzymatic degradation and structural weakening. Management requires prompt surgical intervention (open or endovascular) based on Stanford dissection type, combined with long-term antibiotic therapy. Early diagnosis is crucial, as infectious aortitis can rapidly progress to life-threatening sequelae like rupture or dissection. Clinicians must maintain a high index of suspicion in at-risk patients presenting with suggestive symptoms or imaging findings.

Introduction

Infectious aortitis is a rare but potentially life-threatening condition characterized by infection of the aortic wall by microorganisms, which can have devastating consequences such as aortic aneurysm and rupture.1 Salmonella infection of the aorta leading to infectious aortitis has been described in the literature.2,3 Aortic dissection secondary to salmonella aortitis is very rare and can be potentially fatal.

Case presentation

We report a case of a 69-year-old female with a history of multiple comorbidities including hypertension, type 2 diabetes mellitus, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, coronary artery disease, peripheral artery disease, atrial fibrillation, and Grave’s disease. She was brought to the emergency room by her daughter for altered mental status. Two weeks before the presentation, she developed diarrhea associated with intermittent high-grade fever, abdominal pain, nausea, and vomiting. The history was limited because the patient came in confused and was a poor historian at presentation. Her daughter found her generally weak and lethargic, prompting transfer to the hospital. Pertinent vital signs include an elevated blood pressure of 215/97 mmHg; the rest of the vitals were unremarkable. On physical examination, she was confused but was alert and oriented to time, place, and person. The rest of the examination including the neurological examination, was unremarkable including motor, sensory, and cranial nerve functions.

The patient’s recent history included admission for acute Salmonella enterocolitis, pneumonia, and acute kidney injury approximately three to four months before the current presentation. She was treated with Levofloxacin for 5 days, which was stopped following a negative polymerase chain reaction test for salmonella. Her recent admission also included management of ascites necessitating large-volume paracentesis and echocardiography demonstrating a moderate-sized pericardial effusion without tamponade.

Diagnostic tests revealed heavy growth of non-typhoidal salmonella in her stool and blood cultures. Computed Tomography (CT) angiography of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis incidentally showed two areas of aortic dissection within the aortic arch without the involvement of the great vessels, along with a large pericardial effusion. The CT showed type B aortic dissection with two dissection flaps: the first originating just distal to the left subclavian artery (2 cm in length) and the second distal to the first (3 cm in length). She was transferred to the intensive care unit for blood pressure management in the context of aortic dissection noted on the imaging. She was started on esmolol and clevidipine.

Vascular surgery was consulted and a positron emission tomography (PET) scan was recommended due to concerns about a mycotic aneurysm. The PET scan showed no uptake at the level of the bilateral iliac stents or the aorta wall. Her blood pressure was tightly controlled, and she was treated with intravenous ceftriaxone for 2 weeks. Vascular surgery then performed a thoracic endovascular aortic repair (TEVAR (thoracic endovascular aortic repair)) cover from the left subclavian to zone 5 (from the midpoint of the descending thoracic aorta to the proximal margin of the celiac artery). She also underwent both pericardiocentesis and thoracentesis. Fluid studies from both procedures were negative for malignant cells, and cultures showed no growth after 5 days of incubation.

She was also found to have an intermittent irregular heart rate and junctional bradycardia. Telemetry monitoring showed sinus bradycardia with frequent premature atrial complexes and junctional escape rhythms. The electrophysiology (EP) team was consulted and noted evidence of sinus node dysfunction. She was recommended for permanent pacemaker insertion, but the patient refused.

Infectious disease specialists were consulted for her Salmonella bacteremia and enterocolitis, as well as her Clostridium difficile colonization (toxin A/B negative and antigen positive). She was discharged with Trimethoprim/Sulfamethoxazole daily for suppressive coverage for 1 year and oral Vancomycin for Clostridium difficile prophylaxis while on antibiotics.

She was advised to follow up as an outpatient to evaluate the source of the Salmonella infection and to assess for possible malignant etiology. She was also advised to avoid the use of fluoroquinolones due to her aortic disease, as these antibiotics may increase the risk of aortic dissections or ruptures.

Discussion

Aortitis is inflammation of the aorta. Although most cases of aortitis are non-infective, it is essential to maintain some level of suspicion for infectious causes due to differing treatment strategies required. The primary infectious agents responsible for aortitis include Staphylococcus, Enterococcus, Streptococcus, and Salmonella species.1

Diabetes mellitus and hypertension are the major predisposing factors for salmonella aortitis.2 Our patient had hypertension, diabetes mellitus as well as CKD, and COPD which likely contributed to the condition.

Most patients with Salmonella aortitis also have an ongoing atherosclerotic process in their aorta, which could serve as a focus for infection. Furthermore, all cases of Salmonella aortitis result in aneurysm formation, or in rare cases, the enlargement of a pre-existing aneurysm.3 The most common location of Salmonella aortitis is the abdominal aorta, particularly its infrarenal portion, while the descending thoracic aorta is also a significant site.2

Aortic dissection is a rare complication of infectious aortitis, with some case reports identifying aortic dissection secondary to infectious aortitis caused by Salmonella.4–7 A meta-analysis has shown an association between fluoroquinolone use and increased risk for aortic dissection. The risk of aneurysm or dissection increases with the duration of fluoroquinolone use, from 3 to 14 days and beyond.8 Our patient had taken levofloxacin for 5 days. However, three out of the four controlled observational studies that were pooled in the meta-analysis did not account for infectious agents like Salmonella as a confounding factor.8,9 Gopalakrishnan found this association in pneumonia patients but not in those with urinary tract infections, suspecting confounding bias or surveillance effect.10

CT imaging is typically diagnostic, and current guidelines recommend a low threshold for its use.11 CT scans are widely available, non-invasive, and quick, allowing detailed visualization of the entire aorta and its branches for diagnostic confirmation. In our case, the diagnosis was made through CT. However, In hemodynamically unstable patients, bedside transthoracic or transesophageal echocardiography is better suited for diagnosing ascending aortic dissection.12

The treatment of aortic dissection is decided based on its Stanford type. Stanford type A aortic dissection which involves the ascending aorta usually requires emergency open surgical repair, whereas Stanford type B aortic dissection, which spares the ascending aorta, is treated with endovascular repair and/or medical therapy.13

Thoracic endovascular aneurysm repair (TEVAR) is done for Type B aortic dissection. The INSTEAD trial found no significant advantage of TEVAR combined with medical management over medical management alone.14 However, the INSTEAD-XL trial reported improved 5-year aorta-specific and delayed disease progression with TEVAR plus medical management.15 Medical management involves beta-blockers, angiotensin II receptor antagonists, and statins to control blood pressure and inflammation.11 TEVAR is indicated for complicated type B aortic dissection, characterized by aortic rupture or impending rupture, end-organ or limb ischemia, persistent pain or hypertension despite medical therapy, large entry tear, early false lumen expansion, partial false lumen expansion, or ongoing aortic inflammation.16,17 Pre-emptive TEVAR is offered for initially uncomplicated dissection.12,18,19

In the case of descending thoracic aortic dissection reported by Patil et al., the dissection occurred three weeks after salmonella typhimurium bacteremia and pneumonia.4 In the case of type B aortic dissection due to Salmonella choleraesuis as reported by Kimura et al., initial nonsurgical management was followed for 2 days before switching to open surgery due to the enlargement of the descending thoracic aorta.6 Current studies show TEVAR as being equivalent to open repair for complicated type B aortic dissection with better long-term outcomes.16,17 In the case of type B aortic dissection with penetrating ulcer, pseudoaneurysm, and intramural hematoma due to Salmonella, TEVAR was done.4 In our case, preemptive TEVAR was done for uncomplicated type B aortic dissection.

Antibiotics are added for aortic dissection due to Salmonella aortitis. Initial treatment usually involves intravenous ceftriaxone for 2–4 weeks, followed by long-term suppression with oral fluoroquinolones for 6 months to a lifetime.4,5,7 Our patient received intravenous ceftriaxone for 2 weeks. However, given the evidence linking longer-term fluoroquinolone use with aortic dissection,8–10 long-term suppression with trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole for 1 year.

Epidemiology

A literature review on aortic dissection due to infectious aortitis identified 17 case reports. Patients’ ages ranged from 22 years to 98 years. Among these, 12 were males (aged 22–89, mean age 55) and 5 were females (aged 53–98, mean age 77). There appears to be a male preponderance for aortic dissection due to infectious aortitis, with 70% of the cases occurring in male. This male preponderance is consistent with aortic dissection due to all causes, as reported by Landenhed et al., who found a 67.5% male preponderance.33 Additionally, females with acute aortic dissection due to infectious aortitis presented at an older age compared to males, which aligns with previous literature indicating that females are significantly older than males at the time of presentation.34

5.1. Etiology

Out of the 17 cases reviewed, 7 cases were due to Salmonella of various subspecies, 4 were due to Staphylococcus aureus and 2 cases were due to Clostridium septicum. There was one case of Listeria monocytogenes, Dietza maris, Campylobacter jejuni and Bacteroides fragilis (Fig. 1).

5.2. Presenting symptom

Fever was the most common symptom, present in 11 out of 17 cases (65%) at admission, often accompanied by chills and rigor (47%). Chest pain was reported in 8 cases (47%), followed by back pain in 4 cases (24%), with some experiencing sudden onset back pain. The duration of symptoms varied from a few hours to up to two weeks. Other frequent symptoms included epigastric and abdominal pain.

5.4. Possible pathophysiology

Arterial hypertension is a significant cardiovascular risk factor predisposing individuals to aortic dissection, with approximately 80% of patients with aortic dissection have arterial hypertension. Other notable risk factors include advanced age, dyslipidemia, and low levels of apolipoprotein A1.33 The association between diabetes mellitus and aortic dissection remains controversial.35 Hypertension seems to be the most common underlying condition in patients with aortic dissection secondary to infectious aortitis. It promotes the atherosclerotic degeneration of the aorta, creating a suitable environment for infection.3 Hypertension also leads to aortic wall fragility due to intimal thickening, fibrosis, calcification, extracellular fatty acid deposition, and extracellular matrix deposition, making the aorta more susceptible to dissection.33

5.5. Region involved

Of the 17 cases of aortic dissection due to infectious aortitis, 11 were Stanford type A (65%) and 6 were Stanford type B (35%). In all Stanford type B cases, the descending thoracic aorta was involved. The descending thoracic aorta was the second most commonly affected area, involved in 10 out of the 17 cases. The abdominal aorta was involved in 2 cases.

5.6. Management

Management of aortic dissection with infectious aortitis involves two aspects: addressing the aortic dissection and treating the causative infection with antibiotics. The primary treatment for aortic dissection is surgical, and the choice of surgery depends on the Stanford type. Stanford type A dissections, which involve the ascending aorta, typically require emergency open surgical repair. In contrast, Stanford type B dissections, which spare the ascending aorta, are treated with endovascular repair and/or medical therapy. The basic surgical principle for Type A aortic dissection is resection of the affected aorta and replacement with a synthetic (Dacron) vascular prosthesis.13 The antibiotic regimen depends on the causative organism, usually starting with intravenous antibiotics for 2–8 weeks, followed by long-term suppression with oral antibiotics for a duration varying from 2 months to 18 months.4,5,7

5.7. Prognosis

Mortality occurred in 6 out of the 17 cases (35%). Of these, 2 (40%) were females and 4 (33%) were males. Five deaths occurred in patients with Stanford type A aortic dissection (45%) while one death occurred in Stanford type B aortic dissection (17%). Two case reports were autopsy reports, both caused by clostridium septicum in young males (aged 32 and 22). There was one intraoperative death due to uncontrollable bleeding.25 A 53-year-old female died due to cardiac tamponade on the 17th day while awaiting surgery.29 Two patients died after 6 months; one due to complications of aortic dissection and the other from rectal cancer discovered later.28,31 The prognosis of the two patients was not mentioned in their case reports. One patient’s care was transitioned to a palliative care team.30,32 Female sex, Stanford type A dissection, and infection due to clostridium septicum are important risk factors for mortality. Poor prognosis and higher mortality rates have been observed in female patients with aortic dissection (Fig. 2).34

Conclusion

This case report and literature review underscore the critical importance of recognizing that certain infections can lead to infectious aortitis, which can subsequently result in aortic dissection with potentially devastating consequences. Additionally, it emphasizes the need for vigilant monitoring of patients with risk factors for aortic dissection who are prescribed fluoroquinolones, due to their association with an increased risk of aortic dissections or ruptures. This awareness can guide clinicians in both preventive strategies and timely interventions, ultimately improving patient outcomes.
