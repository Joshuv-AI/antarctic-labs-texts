Changes in food valence of regular diet depending on the experience of high and low preference food
Authors: Xi Cheng, Kazuto Tsuruyama, Satoshi Kida
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsEating disorders represent an aspect of mental illness involving failure to control eating behaviors. Food valence plays a regulatory role in eating behaviors and changes with eating experiences. Failure to control food valence may be associated with eating disorders. This study presents a newly developed behavior task—food reservation task, which assesses changes in food valence.MethodsOver three consecutive days, mice were fed a regular diet for 30 min and subsequently were offered either palatable or low‐palatable foods for 30 min.ResultsMice decreased regular diet consumption on the days that it was followed by a palatable food—sweet chocolate (SC) or cheese (CH) and increased it when it was followed by a low‐palatable food—bitter (dark) chocolate (BC). Our findings indicate that mice can change regular diet consumption by learning whether it will be followed by a palatable or low‐palatable food. This suggests that palatable food devaluated the food valence of regular diet, whereas low‐palatable food evaluated it.ConclusionWe developed a new food reservation task, which allows to assess experience‐dependent change in the food valence of a regular diet. This task will contribute to a better understanding of the neural mechanisms underlying those changes.Keywords: disorder, eating behaviors, food preference, food valence, regular diet intake

INTRODUCTION

Eating disorders, such as hyperphagia and anorexia, represent aspects of mental illness involving failure to control eating behaviors. 1 , 2 , 3 Importantly, these disorders are often associated with malnutrition or metabolic syndromes such as diabetes, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, or obesity. However, the neural mechanism underlying eating disorders remains unclear. 4 , 5 , 6

Food valence plays a regulatory role in eating behaviors 7 , 8 , 9 and is inherently determined, as animals, including humans, show a higher preference for sweet tastes and a lower preference for bitter tastes. 9 , 10 , 11 Importantly, food valence changes with eating experience. For example, food neophobia, an eating behavior consisting of a lower consumption of novel foods at the first experience, even if palatable, and a higher consumption at the following presentations, suggests that novel foods are valued after the first experience. 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 Dysfunction in food valence control may be associated with eating disorders. Thus, investigating the mechanisms underlying experience‐dependent changes in food valence may contribute for a better understanding of eating disorders and for the development of methods to improve them.

Two‐bottle preference test and operant conditioning involving lever pressing, in which rodents choose one food (taste) between two foods, has been established to assess food valence. 16 , 17 In this study, we attempted to establish a new task to examine food valence compared to the daily‐eaten regular diet. Therefore, the aim of this study was to develop a food reservation task to assess experience‐dependent changes in the food valence.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Animals

All experiments were conducted in accordance with the guidelines for the Care and Use of laboratory animals by the Japan Neuroscience Society and the University of Tokyo. All animal experiments performed in this study were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of the University of Tokyo. Three to 6‐month‐old male C57BL/6N mice obtained from Charles River (Yokohama, Japan) were used in this study. All mice were housed in cages of three to six mice maintained on a 12 h light/dark cycle, and fed ad libitum with a regular diet (CA‐1, CLEA Rodent Diet CA‐1, CLEA Japan, Inc., Tokyo, Japan) and water. All experiments were performed during the light period.

Food reservation task

Mice were singly housed. The task was conducted in their homecages. To prevent the food neophobia, mice were fed simultaneously with a regular diet (CA‐1) and test foods [Bitter (dark) chocolate (BC) with 86% cacao, Meiji Co., Ltd. (Tokyo, Japan); Sweet chocolate (SC), Morinaga & Co., Ltd. (Tokyo, Japan); Cheese (CH), ROKKO Butter Co., Ltd. (Kobe, Japan)] for 3 days (DAY1‐3). Fifty‐two male mice were randomly assigned to four groups (n = 13 in each group). Then, the mice were subjected to a five‐day food restriction. The food restriction period comprised three steps, as follows: (1) Mice were first fasted for 22 h (DAY4); (2) mice were fed 2–2.5 g of a regular diet (CA‐1) every 24 h for 2 days (DAY5‐6); and (3) during 2 days, mice were fed the regular diet only for 2 h (DAY 7–8). After this food restriction, the weight of mice reached 85%–90% of their weight before the food restriction. Following the food restriction period, the mice were fed the regular diet for 30 min and then fed the regular diet (Ctrl) or test foods (BC, SC, or CH) for 30 min during three consecutive days (DAY 9–11; Test 1–3). The consumed amount of regular diet and test food was measured every 15 min during the test period. Each day (Test 1–3) mice were fed the regular diet for 1 h after the test, to maintain body weight. During this task, the mice were allowed ad libitum access to water and housed under constant conditions with a temperature of 23°C and a humidity of 55% to prevent the chocolate melting or cheese dehydration.

Statistical analysis

OriginPro 2018b (Lightstone Corp., Tokyo, Japan) was used for statistical analysis. Two‐way repeated measures ANOVA followed by Bonferroni post hoc comparisons was used to analyze an effect of test food [Ctrl and Test (Test 1 to 3)] on food valence. One‐way ANOVA was used to analyze the regular diet consumption on Test 1. All data in the text and figures are represented as the mean ± standard error of the mean.

RESULT AND DISCUSSION

To avoid the effect of food neophobia, the mice were first habituated to the novel foods [bitter chocolate (BC), sweet chocolate (SC), cheese (CH)] 14 by feeding them together with the regular diet (Ctrl) for 3 days. Following habituation, the mice were food‐restricted by feeding the regular diet 2 h per day for 5 days. During the food reservation task, the mice were fed the regular diet for 30 min once a day followed by a 30‐min feeding of the test food (Ctrl, BC, SC, or CH; Figure 1A), for 3 days (Test 1 to Test 3). The consumed amount of both regular diet and test food was measured. It is important to note that all groups showed comparable body weight measured before each test from Test 1 to Test 3 (TEST foods, F[3,48] = 1.17, p > 0.05; TESTs, F[2,96] = 2.76, p > 0.05; TEST foods × TESTs, F[6,96] = 0.74, p > 0.05; Figure 1B). The consumed amount of regular diet over the 3 days was compared using two‐way repeated measures ANOVA, which revealed a significant effect of TEST foods (Ctrl, BC, SC, or CH) and a significant interaction between the TEST foods and TESTs (TEST foods, F[3,48] = 11.95, p < 0.05; TESTs, F[2,96] = 2.75, p > 0.05; TEST foods×TESTs, F[6,48] = 15.04, p < 0.05; Figure 1C). Multiple post hoc comparisons were analyzed using the Bonferroni test, which revealed a significant increase in regular diet consumption for the BC group and a significant decrease for both the CH and SC groups, from Test 1 to Test 3 (BC group, Test 1 vs. Test 2, Test 1 vs. Test 3, p < 0.05; SC group, Test 1 vs. Test 3, p < 0.05; CH group, Test 1 vs. Test 2, Test 2 vs. Test 3, p < 0.05; Figure 1C). In contrast, for the Ctrl group, regular diet consumption remained unchanged from Test 1 to Test 3 (p > 0.05; Figure 1C). It is noteworthy that for Test 1 all groups showed similar amounts of regular diet consumption (one‐way ANOVA, F[3,48] = 2.33821, p = 0.08528 > 0.05). Test foods' consumption was also compared using two‐way repeated measures ANOVA, which revealed significant effects of TEST foods and TESTs and a significant interaction between the two (TEST foods, F[3,48] = 55.66, p < 0.05; TESTs, F[2,96] = 92.82, p < 0.05; TEST foods × TESTs, F[6,96] = 14.58, p < 0.05; Figure 1D). For groups BC, SC, and CH Test food consumption increased significantly from Test 1 to Test 3 (BC group, Test 1 vs. Test 3, p < 0.05; SC/CH group, Test 1 vs. Test 2, Test 1 vs. Test 3, Test 2 vs. Test 3, p < 0.05; Figure 1D), whereas for Ctrl group no difference was found on Test food (regular diet) consumption from Test1 to Test 3 (p > 0.05; Figure 1D). These observations indicate that the mice decreased regular food consumption when it was followed by SC or CH, and increased it when it was followed by BC, which suggests that mice can learn the sequence of foods provided from Test 1 to Test 3. Importantly, our observations suggest that regular diet is evaluated or devaluated through learning the food delivery sequence. In other words, compared with the regular diet, mice showed a lower preference for BC and higher preference for both SC and CH.

We also compared regular diet consumption over the first and second 15 min of 30‐min feeding time. For the first 15 min, two‐way repeated measures ANOVA revealed a significant effect of the TEST foods and a significant interaction between the TEST foods and TESTs (TEST foods, F[3,48] = 3.16, p < 0.05; TESTs, F[2,96] = 0.13, p > 0.05; TEST foods × TESTs, F[6,96] = 6.04, p < 0.05; Figure 1E), with only the CH group(Test 1 vs. Test 3, p < 0.05 Figure 1E) decreasing regular diet consumption from Test 1 to Test 3. Similar results were found for the second 15 min (TEST foods, F[3,48] = 16.35, p < 0.05; TESTs, F[2,96] = 3.24, p < 0.05; TEST foods × TESTs, F[6,96] = 8.09, p < 0.05; Figure 1F), but this time the BC group increased regular diet consumption from Test 1 to Test 3, while both the SC and CH groups decreased it (Test 1 vs. Test 3, ps <0.05; Figure 1F), and the Ctrl group maintained it over the three‐test period. These findings indicate that the difference observed in regular diet consumption over the 30 min period of feeding reflected the consumption of the last 15 min.

Animals including humans, inherently exhibit a high‐valence for sweet tastes and a low‐valence for bitter tastes. 9 , 10 , 11 Indeed, consumption of sweet‐ or bitter‐taste water has been found to elicit positive and negative emotions, respectively. 9 , 18 , 19 Here, we suggest that, for mice, cheese and sweet chocolate are highly palatable foods, whereas bitter (dark) chocolate is a low‐palatable food, compared to regular diet. Therefore, it is important to investigate the occurrence of positive or negative emotions and underlying neural mechanisms, following the consumption of these foods, by relating them to sweet and bitter tastes. Importantly, we cannot rule out the possibility that the test foods used in our experiments may have altered feeding behaviors through influences other than food valence, such as nutrient components and calories. It is necessary to investigate the valence of other types of food using our novel task.

Interestingly, test food BC consumption increased gradually, but significantly, from Test 1 to Test 3. It is possible that the mice were not fully satiated by feeding the regular diet for 30 min and may have eaten more as they learned the sequential presentation of the regular diet and test food over 3 days.

In the tasks involving operant conditioning, the rodents choose between two foods by lever pressing according to the food valence. 16 Our task is simple without this conditioning and enables the assessment of food value compared to the daily‐eaten regular diet or between two foods. Furthermore, this task involves learning and memory processes, in which the mice learn that the test food is delivered after feeding the regular diet, and can therefore be used as a learning and memory paradigm. Importantly, this task also allows investigating mechanisms for human‐like cognitive controls of eating behaviors; the amount intake of food such as the regular diet is saved or increased when their favorite or least favorite food, respectively, is presented later.

Collectively, our observations suggest that mice change regular diet consumption according to their preference for the food delivered subsequently. Importantly, our findings suggest that this task causes mice to change the valence of their regular diet, which allows estimating their preference for other foods. This task will therefore contribute to understanding the neural mechanisms underlying experience‐dependent changes in food valence.
