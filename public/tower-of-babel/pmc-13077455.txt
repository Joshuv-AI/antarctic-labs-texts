Introduction

The British Thoracic Society guidelines recommend the use of either an indwelling pleural catheter (IPC) or pleurodesis as a first-line intervention in the management of malignant pleural effusion (MPE) [1]. The use of IPCs is increasingly considered a feasible option for managing these pleural effusions, given the comparable success rates to conventional talc pleurodesis [2]. However, IPCs are not without complications. An unblinded randomised controlled trial reported higher adverse event rates with IPCs compared to pleurodesis, including increased pleural infections and empyema [2]. Here, we report a rare complication of IPC placement.

Case report

A 52-year-old male with a 20-pack-year smoking history was referred to the chest department of Hospital Sultanah Bahiyah for evaluation of a right pleural effusion. He presented with a dry cough, shortness of breath for over 2 months, and weight loss. Physical examination revealed a massive right pleural effusion, which was confirmed by chest radiography and a computed tomography (CT) scan (figure 1).

A computed tomography of the thorax showing the presence of a large right pleural effusion.

The patient underwent medical thoracoscopy, which revealed multiple parietal pleural nodules; biopsies were taken. Bronchoscopy showed obstruction at the bronchus intermedius, and multiple biopsies were obtained. Histopathological examination of both bronchial and pleural specimens confirmed stage IV squamous cell carcinoma. The patient completed three cycles of chemotherapy (carboplatin/gemcitabine), and a follow-up CT demonstrated improvement in the mass. There was no evidence of lung abscess on CT at this stage. There was a recurrent large pleural effusion and the patient was complaining of progressive breathlessness despite therapeutic drainage twice in the past 3 months.

Given his clinical deterioration, the patient was treated for right-sided empyema. A cardiothoracic consultation was obtained, and a right chest tube was inserted. The IPC was removed without complications. The patient improved with broad-spectrum antibiotics and subsequently underwent talc pleurodesis after clearance of the pleural and lung infection. Due to the advanced primary disease and inadequate response to chemotherapy, he was referred to palliative care.

Discussion

IPCs are increasingly used in the management of MPE. Mechanical complications such as IPC displacement are rare, whereas infection-related complications are more common [3]. This case highlights the simultaneous occurrence of two complications.

Studies have shown that patients treated with IPCs often experience improved quality of life, particularly due to increased mobility and symptomatic relief [4]. In our case, the patient initially reported symptomatic improvement. However, worsening symptoms despite IPC use and ongoing chemotherapy, coupled with inadequate drainage, prompted further investigation.

In this case, the IPC was transposed into the posterior mediastinum, probably due to altered anatomy from the underlying disease creating a tract into the mediastinum. Timely removal of the IPC prevented mediastinitis; however, inadequate drainage of the pleural effusion might have contributed to the development of empyema.

Reports of IPC displacement or malposition/transposition are extremely rare, with only one previously documented case [3]. A recent review on IPC complications described pleural infections, catheter tract metastasis, loculations, chronic chest pain, immunosuppression, malnutrition from chronic pleural fluid drainage, catheter blockage, peri-catheter leakage, catheter fracture during removal, and dislodgement. However, transposition into the mediastinum or contralateral pleural space was not reported [5]. This underscores the rarity of this complication and it also highlights the utility of confirming IPC placement in cases of non-drainage, especially before administering fibrinolytics.

Conclusion

This case underscores the importance of recognising the rare complication of IPC transposition. Clinicians managing MPE with IPCs should be aware of the potential for IPC transposition, particularly when drainage is inadequate. In such cases, removal alone may not be sufficient, as empyema or mediastinitis may already be present. A thorough work-up and appropriate treatment are essential to prevent further complications.
