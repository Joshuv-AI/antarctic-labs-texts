Symmetrization of 3D Generative Models
Nicolas Caytuiro, Ivan Sipiran

Abstract
We propose a novel data-centric approach to promote symmetry in 3D generative models by modifying the training data rather than the model architecture. Our method begins with an analysis of reflectional symmetry in both real-world 3D shapes and samples generated by state-of-the-art models. We hypothesize that training a generative model exclusively on half-objects, obtained by reflecting one half of the shapes along the x = 0 x=0 plane, enables the model to learn a rich distribution of partial geometries which, when reflected during generation, yield complete shapes that are both visually plausible and geometrically symmetric. To test this, we construct a new dataset of half-objects from three ShapeNet classes (Airplane, Car, and Chair) and train two generative models. Experiments demonstrate that the generated shapes are symmetrical and consistent, compared with the generated objects from the original model and the original dataset objects.

Introduction

“
Symmetry is what we see at a glance.
” – Blaise Pascal.

Symmetry is ubiquitous in both natural and artificial objects, and it is a fundamental property in geometry. It serves as a crucial visual cue that helps humans perceive object structure and interpret spatial relationships
Treder 2010
;
Li et al. 2024
. In computer vision – either in the 2D or 3D domain – symmetry has long been leveraged as a structural constraint to simplify complex visual tasks such as pose estimation
Merrill et al. 2022
;
Zhao et al. 2023
, shape synthesis
Li et al. 2020
;
Levy et al. 2024
, and 3D reconstruction
Rock et al. 2015
;
Wu et al. 2020
;
Xu et al. 2020
.
Based on its mathematical definition, symmetry can be detected by finding the geometric invariance under the transformations of a symmetry group, such as reflection, rotation, and translation. For example, various methods have been proposed to collect evidence of symmetric correspondences under a symmetry transformation and to detect symmetry from completed or well-observed 3D shapes where sufficient evidence exists
Mitra et al. 2013
.

While current 3D generative models demonstrate strong performance in synthesizing shapes from 3D or depth data
Zhou et al. 2021a
;
Zeng et al. 2022
;
Ren et al. 2024a
;
Lyu et al. 2023
;
Ren et al. 2024b
, explicitly generating symmetrical objects is rarely an architectural or experimental design objective. This remains true even though many objects in widely used 3D shape datasets exhibit clear reflection symmetry with respect to the
x
-axis. As illustrated in Figure
1
, objects from the ShapeNet dataset highlight this property. Recent approaches face this challenge by incorporating symmetry into their architecture
Levy et al. 2024
;
Lu et al. 2025
;
Yang et al. 2024
;
Kelvinius et al. 2025
, but they still lack problem generalization.

To investigate this, we hypothesize that: if a generative model is trained exclusively on partial shapes (i.e., one half of each object), and if the data distribution is sufficiently rich, the model should be capable of generating novel, varied half-objects that, once reflected, result in complete shapes that are both visually plausible and geometrically symmetric. To test this hypothesis, we designed an experimental protocol comprising two main stages. First, we evaluate the inherent symmetry in 1000 full objects from three ShapeNet classes (Airplane, Car, and Chair) by mirroring them using the Householder transformation and computing their Chamfer Distance to the original. Then, we apply the same protocol to 1000 shapes generated by several 3D generative models (Point Voxel Diffusion (PVD), LION, XCube, and SLIDE 3D), allowing for a quantitative comparison of symmetry preservation.

In the second stage, we construct a modified dataset by mirroring the points with
x\leq 0
(left half) to
x\geq 0
(right half), thereby obtaining half-shapes with the same number of points as the original point clouds in the ShapeNet benchmark (15K points). Using PVD
Zhou et al. 2021a
and LION
Zeng et al. 2022
as our baseline architectures, we train both models from scratch on this half-shape dataset and generate novel samples, which are subsequently mirrored across the
x
-axis to reconstruct complete shapes. Finally, we assess the generation quality by computing the 1-Nearest Neighbor Accuracy (1-NNA) metric, along with Chamfer Distance and Earth Mover’s Distance, to reference shapes. Additionally, we employ the Fréchet Point Cloud Distance (FPD) to assess the perceptual and statistical realism of the generated shapes. This evaluation criterion enables us to analyze whether learning from partial inputs enhances the generation of geometrically symmetric and realistic 3D shapes.

Finally, our results demonstrate that training generative models on half-objects leads to improved symmetry in the generated shapes without requiring architectural modifications or additional supervision. These findings highlight the effectiveness of data-centric strategies for inducing structural priors in 3D shape generation.

reviews existing literature on symmetry in 3D computer vision and symmetry-aware generative models. Section

Related Works

2.1
Symmetry

According to Je et al.
Je et al. 2024
, symmetry plays a crucial role in Computer Vision and Computer Graphics, and is widely used in various downstream tasks, including 3D reconstruction
Chen et al. 2020
;
Xu et al. 2020
;
Allingham et al. 2024
, shape refinement
Mitra et al. 2007
, image manipulation
Zhang et al. 2020
; and is extended to different 3D shape representations such as 3D point clouds
Ji and Liu 2019
;
Sun 2021
, meshes
Allingham et al. 2024
, voxel grids
Tulsiani et al. 2020
, and implicit fields
Xu et al. 2020
. These priors enable more robust shape processing and help regularize ill-posed tasks, specifically under noisy or incomplete observations.

Despite its prevalence, symmetry is often treated as a post-processing step (e.g., symmetrization) or used implicitly in loss functions without directly constraining the generative process. As a result, many deep learning models are still symmetry-agnostic during training and inference, which can lead to asymmetrical or geometrically inconsistent outputs when data is sparse or noisy. This limitation has motivated a growing interest in embedding symmetry more directly into the architecture and training of 3D generative models – explored more deeply in Subsection
2.3
.

2.2
3D Reflection Symmetry

Reflectional symmetry is the most fundamental form of symmetry observed. Symmetries among shape parts can also be discovered via transform-space voting, assuming that the symmetric parts only differ by a
rigid
transformation
Xu et al. 2009
. Symmetrical objects are widespread in nature, architecture, and art, and reflectional symmetry plays a crucial role in human visual perception
Mitra et al. 2013
. This concept is being widely used in 3D computer vision research. A significant research effort in this domain focuses on applying reflectional symmetry principles to enhance the synthesis of 3D shapes.

As noted by Yang et al.
Yang et al. 2024
, symmetric objects can be split into two halves, where one half approximately mirrors the other. Based on this principle, we train generative models using one half of each object and reconstruct full shapes by reflecting the generated outputs across the symmetry plane.

2.3
Symmetry-aware 3D Generative Models

Current 3D generative frameworks, including GANs, VAEs, and more recently, diffusion models, often rely on data-driven learning to implicitly capture symmetrical patterns. However, this strategy can be sample-inefficient, specifically when training data exhibits diverse or spatial symmetries.

Recent research has begun addressing this limitation by incorporating symmetry explicitly into either the model architecture or the training objective. For instance, Wu et al.
Wu et al. 2020
propose an unsupervised method for learning 3D object categories from raw single-view images based on the idea that many object categories have, at least in principle, a symmetric structure. NeRD
Zhou et al. 2021b
focuses on detecting 3D reflection symmetry by estimating mirror planes, while GET3D
Gao et al. 2022
generates textured meshes with rich geometry through dual-branch generation. Niu et al.
Niu et al. 2023
introduce a symmetry-aware alignment pipeline that incrementally refines object orientation based on symmetry cues, such as features, planes, symmetry axes, and frontal orientations. Allingham et al.
Allingham et al. 2024
directly models approximate symmetries within generative pipelines. Additionally, Je et al.
Je et al. 2024
propose a symmetry detection framework that is robust to noise, utilizing Langevin Dynamics in a refined symmetry space.

More recently, diffusion-based approaches have been proposed. For instance, SymmCD
Levy et al. 2024
encodes crystallographic symmetries within the generative process, while WyckoffDiff
Kelvinius et al. 2025
introduces symmetry-aware Wyckoff representations. Following these advances, Lu et al.
Lu et al. 2025
introduces structure-preserving diffusion processes, a family of diffusion processes for learning distributions that possess additional structure, such as group symmetries, by developing theoretical conditions under which the diffusion transition steps preserve said symmetry. Finally, Liang
Liang 2025
further explores how pre-trained DDPMs can operate with non-standard noise distributions to facilitate image generation on devices with limited computational power.

3
Background

3.1
Reflection Symmetry

Reflectional symmetry is a common trait in many object categories
Mitra et al. 2013
;
Yang et al. 2024
.

According to Zhou et al.
Zhou et al. 2021b
and Li et al.
Li et al. 2024
, consider the set of points in homogeneous coordinates on the surface of a 3D shape,
S\subset\mathbb{R}^{4}
. The shape exhibits reflection symmetry with respect to a plane
p
if there exists a plane
p
such that for every point
\mathbf{x}\in S
, its reflected point
\mathbf{M}_{p}\mathbf{x}
is also contained within
S
. Here,
\mathbf{M}_{p}
is the reflection transformation matrix across the plane
p
. The surface properties
\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x})
at point
\mathbf{x}
are preserved under reflection. Formally:

S=M_{p}(S)\text{ and }F(\mathbf{x})=F(\mathbf{M}_{p}\mathbf{x})

(1)

Where
M_{p}(S)=\{\mathbf{M}_{p}\mathbf{x}\,|\,\mathbf{x}\in S\}
represents the set of all points in
S
reflected across the plane
p
and
F(\mathbf{x})
represents the surface properties at point
x
, such as texture.

The reflection matrix
\mathbf{M}_{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{4x4}
corresponding to plane
p
is defined as:

\mathbf{M}_{p}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{I}-2\mathbf{n}_{p}\mathbf{n}_{p}^{T}&-2d_{p}\mathbf{n}_{p}\\
0&1\end{bmatrix}

(2)

Where the plane
p
is parameterized by its normal vector
\mathbf{n}_{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
and its distance
d_{p}
from the origin.

In sum, the reflected shape is a mirrored version of the original with respect to the plane
p
.

3.2
Householder Transformation

For a given symmetry plane defined by normal
\mathbf{n}
and a point
\mathbf{m}
on it, the reflection mapping
\mathbf{R}
is a generalization of the Householder transformation (which defines a reflection about a plane passing through the origin) to reflection about an arbitrary plane
Rock et al. 2015
. Specifically,
\mathbf{R}
is defined by a matrix-vector pair (
\mathcal{A}
,
\mathbf{t}
):

\begin{split}&\mathcal{A}=\mathbf{I}-2\mathbf{n}\mathbf{n}^{\top}\\
&\mathbf{t}=2\mathbf{n}\mathbf{n}^{\top}\mathbf{m}\end{split}

(3)

Where
\mathbf{I}
is the identity matrix, and any point
p
can then be reflected about the plane by using the function
\mathbf{R}(\mathbf{p})=\mathcal{A}\mathbf{p}+\mathbf{t}
.

We use this technique to mirror an object over the YZ plane, which is orthogonal to the
X
-axis (i.e., the plane defined by
x=0
).

Analysis of Current 3D Generative Approaches

We begin by describing our proposed symmetry measurement protocol in Section
4.1
; subsequently, we analyze symmetry in current 3D shape datasets and symmetry in current 3D shapes generative models in Sections
4.2
and
4.3
, respectively.

4.1
Symmetry Measurement Protocol

In order to evaluate the degree of reflection symmetry present in the 3D shape, we propose a quantitative analysis based on point cloud representations. Specifically, for each object in a given class, we represent its 3D shape as a point cloud
\mathcal{P}=\{\mathbf{x}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\ |\ i=1,\dots,N\}
, and compute its reflected counterpart
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
using the Householder transformation (please refer to Equation
3
for further details) with normal vector
\mathbf{n}=[1,0,0]^{\top}
. This reflection is defined with respect to the plane
x=0
. The resulting reflected point cloud is then defined by
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}=\{\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\prime}=\mathbf{M}_{x}\mathbf{x}_{i}\ |\ \mathbf{x}_{i}\in\mathcal{P}\}
.

This transformation mirrors the shapes across the
x=0
plane by reflecting each point
\mathbf{x}_{i}\in\mathcal{P}
through the plane orthogonal to
\mathbf{v}
.

To quantify the degree of symmetry, we compute the Chamfer Distance (CD) between the original point cloud
S_{1}
and its reflected counterpart
S_{2}
. The Chamfer Distance is a standard metric for evaluating the geometric fidelity between a generated shape and a ground-truth shape. It is used extensively in 3D reconstruction, shape completion, and point set generation, as well as in higher-level metrics such as 1-NNA, MMD, and Coverage, which rely on pairwise distances.

Following prior works
Fan et al. 2017
;
Aguirre et al. 2025
, the Chamfer Distance between two point clouds
S_{1}
and
S_{2}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{3}
is defined as:

\begin{split}CD(S_{1},S_{2})=&\frac{1}{|S_{1}|}\sum_{x\in S_{1}}\min_{y\in S_{2}}\|x-y\|_{2}^{2}\\
&+\frac{1}{|S_{2}|}\sum_{y\in S_{2}}\min_{x\in S_{1}}\|x-y\|_{2}^{2}\end{split}

(4)

In the strict sense, the CD is not a distance function because the triangle inequality does not hold. For each point, the CD algorithm finds the nearest neighbor in the other set and sums the squared distances. The lower, the better.

4.2
Symmetry in Current 3D Shape Datasets

In the context of 3D shape generation, data plays a crucial role in guiding both the learning and synthesis processes. Using an appropriate dataset can help the learning and generation process based on the application

In this work, we use the ShapeNet dataset
Chang et al. 2015
as a reference to assess the presence of symmetry in real-world 3D objects. ShapeNet is a large-scale dataset containing richly annotated 3D CAD models across a wide variety of categories. Following previous works
Yang et al. 2019
;
Zhou et al. 2021a
;
Luo and Hu 2021
;
Ren et al. 2024a
;
Ren et al. 2024b
, we focus on three representative and commonly used classes:
Airplane
,
Car
, and
Chair
, which exhibit diverse symmetrical objects.

We apply the symmetry measurement protocol described in Section

4.3.1
Point-Voxel Diffusion (PVD)

PVD
Zhou et al. 2021a
introduces a probabilistic and flexible 3D shape generation framework, which combines the strengths of diffusion models with the hybrid, point-voxel representation of 3D shapes
Liu et al. 2019
. This hybrid representation endows point cloud processing with structured spatial locality by embedding point features into a voxel grid, enabling efficient and expressive learning of shape priors.

From Figure
3
, we observe that PVD has a similar distribution compared with the original ShapeNet dataset – in the three classes – and the overall symmetry error appears low, particularly for airplanes. Apparently, it looks like PVD generates symmetrical objects, but in terms of visual quality – as shown in Figure
1
– the generated shapes are not plausible, meaning that many of the outputs exhibit missing regions or sparse point coverage, indicating that low symmetry error alone does not guarantee realistic or structurally complete 3D shapes.

4.3.2
Latent Point Diffusion Model (LION)

LION
Zeng et al. 2022
introduces a
Latent Point Diffusion Model
for 3D shape generation, designed to operate directly on point cloud data. Its architecture integrates a Variational Autoencoder (VAE) with DDMs applied in a hierarchical latent space. Particularly, LION defines two levels of latent representations: a
global latent vector
encoding coarse shape information, and a
latent point cloud
preserving spatial structure. Both representations are learned via point-based encoders and modeled independently using separate latent-space DDMs.

As shown in Figure
3
, LION achieves lower Chamfer Distances in the airplane class, with a mean of
0.117
, indicating good symmetry preservation in this category. However, for cars and chairs, the symmetry performance deteriorates, with mean distances of
0.173
and
0.166
, respectively. Compared to the other models, LION exhibits a higher degree of asymmetry in the generated shapes for the car class. Visual inspection of the generated shapes, as shown in Figure
1
, reveals missing points in some regions and incomplete structures, further highlighting the model’s limitations in maintaining geometric consistency across generated shapes.

4.3.3
XCube (abbreviated as
\mathcal{X}^{3}
)

XCube
Ren et al. 2024a
proposes a novel
hierarchical voxel latent diffusion framework
for generating high-resolution 3D objects and scenes with rich attributes, including signed distance fields (SDFs), surface normals, and semantic labels. The core of XCube is the generation of latent
Sparse Voxel Hierarchy
– a hierarchy of 3D sparse voxel grids with latent features at each voxel – in a coarse-to-fine manner.

As shown in Figure
3
, XCube performs better in the airplane and car classes. The car class yields the lowest mean Chamfer Distance (
0.057
), indicating the highest degree of symmetry, followed by the airplane class (
0.081
). This diverges from the trend observed in other models, even in the original dataset, where the airplane class typically achieves the best symmetry. In contrast, the chair class displays a wide and sparse distribution with a mean value of
0.227
, suggesting a significant lack of symmetry. These results indicate that while XCube is capable of generating high-quality shapes – specifically in terms of visual fidelity – it does not preserve reflectional symmetry.

4.3.4
Sparse Latent Point Diffusion Model (SLIDE 3D)

SLIDE 3D
Lyu et al. 2023
introduces a novel framework for 3D mesh generation by leveraging the idea that point clouds are the intermediate representation of meshes. Rather than applying diffusion models directly in mesh or dense point cloud space, SLIDE 3D proposes a
sparse latent point representation
combined with two diffusion processes to generate high-quality, controllable 3D meshes.

Similar to XCube, Figure
3
shows that SLIDE 3D achieves its lowest Chamfer Distance in the car class (
0.088
), followed by the airplane class (
0.128
). In contrast, the chair class exhibits a much broader and more dispersed distribution, with a notably high mean of
0.39
, indicating poor symmetry preservation. This pattern suggests that SLIDE 3D, like other models, struggles to generalize symmetry in categories with greater intra-class variability and less structural regularity.

This empirical evidence supports our motivation to incorporate explicit symmetry constraints into generative models for 3D shape synthesis. Also, it is worth noting that none of the aforementioned methods consider symmetry in their training or generation processes.

5
Our Method

Figure 4
:
Overview of our proposed pipeline. The process begins with dataset preparation, where objects from
ShapeNetCore.v2.PC15K

Chang et al. 2015
are aligned and mirrored along the
x=0
plane to obtain right-side half-objects (
x>=0
), each containing 15,000 points. During generation, the synthesized half-objects are mirrored across the
x
-axis to reconstruct full shapes, which are subsequently evaluated using the original validation set.

According to Lu et al.
Lu et al. 2025
, in many generation tasks, the data involved often exhibit inherent “structures” that are invariant – or the mappings between them being equivariant – under a set of transformations.

In this context, the ShapeNet dataset
Chang et al. 2015
is one of the most widely adopted benchmarks for 3D shape synthesis, reconstruction, and conditional learning. ShapeNet includes two main subsets: ShapeNetCore and ShapeNetSem. ShapeNetCore is the most commonly used subset, which contains approximately 51,300 models across 55 object categories, each with manually verified annotations for category labels and object alignment. The standard experimental setup for evaluating generative models on ShapeNet is often adapted from the benchmark introduced by PointFlow
Yang et al. 2019
. Particularly, three popular classes are used in conditional learning: Airplane, Car, and Chair, which contain 2,832, 2,458, and 4,612 shapes for training in each class, respectively.

As shown in Figure
1
, objects in the ShapeNet dataset exhibit clear reflection symmetry with respect to the plane
x=0
. However, as demonstrated in the previous section, current 3D generative models fail to preserve this structural property after training. Figure
1
illustrates that the generated shapes are often visually inconsistent, with noticeable deformations and missing regions, and lack the symmetry observed in the training data.

Building on this assumption, we hypothesize that training a 3D generative model exclusively on half-shapes will enable it to learn a rich and diverse distribution of partial geometries, which, when reflected across the symmetry plane, yield complete shapes that are both visually plausible and geometrically symmetric. Figure
4
illustrates the overall pipeline of our proposed methodology.

In the remainder of this section, we detail the experimental setup designed to validate our hypothesis.

5.1
Dataset Preparation

Let
\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{3\times N}
be the training dataset composed of point clouds with
N=15,000
points, drawn from ShapeNetCore.v2.PC15K, and let
\mathcal{X}_{c}\subset\mathcal{X}
be the subset of point clouds corresponding to that given class
c\in\{airplane,\,car,\,chair\}
. Each object
X\in\mathcal{X}_{c}
is assumed to be reflectionally symmetric with respect to the plane
x=0
.

For each point cloud
X\in\mathcal{X}_{c}
we filter both its left half (
A
) and its right half (
B
) with respect to the symmetry plane as follows:
A=\{\mathbf{x}\in X\,|\,\mathbf{x}\leq 0\}
,
B=\{\mathbf{x}\in X\,|\,\mathbf{x}\geq 0\}
; where
x
denotes the
x
-coordinate of point
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
.

We then mirror the left half
A
across the plane
x=0
by negating its
x
-coordinates, producing the reflected set
A’
. The resulting half-object
\tilde{X}
is obtained by concatenating the mirrored points with the original right half:
\tilde{X}=A^{\prime}\cup B
.

Thus, the new dataset of mirrored half-shapes is
\tilde{\mathcal{X}}=\{\tilde{X}\,|\,X\in\mathcal{X}_{c}\}
, where each
\tilde{X}
contains exactly 15,000 points and represents a normalized right half-object derived from its symmetric full-shape counterpart.

5.2
Model Selection and Training

We selected PVD
Zhou et al. 2021a
and LION
Zeng et al. 2022
as our baseline architectures, as they have become widely adopted in recent works and provide strong reference results for comparative evaluation. For our experiments, we utilized the official implementation released by the authors and trained both models from scratch on our custom dataset of half-objects
\tilde{\mathcal{X}}
, using the Airplane, Car, and Chair classes.

5.3
Generation

We follow the same generation processes of PVD and LION. As illustrated in Figure
4
, once the model has been trained on the half-shape dataset
\tilde{\mathcal{X}}
, we generate new samples
\tilde{X}^{+}
and then reconstruct a full shape from the generated half. We apply a reflection across the symmetry plane
x=0
, obtaining the mirrored half
\tilde{X}^{-}
.

The full shape is obtained by concatenating both halves:
\tilde{\mathcal{X}}_{full}=\tilde{X}^{+}\cup\tilde{X}^{-}
.

Each full shape is normalized using the original validation statistics (per class):
\tilde{\mathcal{X}}_{full}=\tilde{\mathcal{X}}_{full}\cdot\sigma-\mu
,
it is multiplied by the standard deviation
\sigma
and then shifted by the mean
\mu
.

To ensure consistent resolution, we apply FPS to downsample the reconstructed shape to
N=2048
points:
\tilde{\mathcal{X}}_{final}=FPS(\tilde{\mathcal{X}}_{full},\,2048)
.

Finally, each generated shape
\tilde{\mathcal{X}}_{final}
is evaluated against the validation set.

Experiments

In this section, we present the experimental setup and report the results obtained from our study.

6.1
Data

Following previous works
Yang et al. 2019
;
Zhou et al. 2021a
;
Luo and Hu 2021
;
Ren et al. 2024a
;
Ren et al. 2024b
, we select the Airplane, Car, and Chair ShapeNet

6.2
Evaluation Metrics

For evaluation, we follow the process described in Section
5.3
and adopt the same protocol proposed in the original implementations of PVD and LION, which measures distributional similarity between generated shapes and ground-truth samples from the evaluation set
Yang et al. 2019
;
Zhou et al. 2021a
;
Zeng et al. 2022
. Particularly, they employ the 1-Nearest Neighbor Accuracy (1-NNA) metric (with both Chamfer Distance (CD) and Earth Mover’s Distance (EMD)).

Additionally, we compute the Fréchet Point Cloud Distance (FPD) metric to assess the perceptual and statistical realism of the generated shapes. Unlike 1-NNA, FPD operates on global distributions of point cloud features, providing a complementary view of generation quality and realism. We adopt the official implementation from tree-GAN
Shu et al. 2019
for FPD computation.

For a quantitative analysis, we extract deep features from each validation set in the three ShapeNet classes using PointNet
Charles et al. 2017
. These class-specific feature distributions are then used to compute the FPD between real and generated samples.

6.3
Training

Model training was conducted on two separate GPU clusters: one equipped with 2
\times
NVIDIA GeForce RTX A6000 (48GB each) and 96GB of RAM, and another with 3
\times
NVIDIA GeForce RTX A5000 (24GB each) and 128GB of RAM. For PVD, training required approximately 96 GPU-hours per class using the first cluster. For LION, training took approximately 96 GPU-hours for the VAE and an additional 120 GPU-hours for the diffusion prior per class on the second cluster.

7
Discussion

The obtained results support the hypothesis that training on half-objects encourages the model to internalize structural priors such as reflectional symmetry, which are prevalent in real-world 3D objects
Mitra et al. 2013
;
Xu et al. 2009
. In particular, the use of our symmetry measurement protocol provides a complementary evaluation perspective that is not captured by global distance metrics alone.

Moreover, our approach aligns with recent literature advocating for inductive biases in deep generative frameworks
Allingham et al. 2024
;
Lu et al. 2025
. By modifying the training data to emphasize symmetry, we encourage the model to generalize this property, without modifying the model architecture or loss function. Further research on this area should consider symmetry as a fundamental design principle for network architectures
Diop et al. 2024
. Our approach highlights an alternative strategy to symmetry-aware design that is simple, data-centric, and architecture-agnostic.

Nonetheless, our method presents some limitations. First, the performance drop in CD and EMD suggests a trade-off between local detail fidelity and global structure regularity. Second, the approach relies on the assumption that objects are symmetric with respect to a known plane, typically
x=0
, which may not generalize to categories with weak or complex symmetry.

8
Conclusions and Future Works

In this work, we introduced a simple and effective strategy to preserve symmetry in 3D generative models by training on half-objects and reconstructing complete shapes through reflection. Using PVD and LION as baselines, we showed that this data-centric approach improves reflectional symmetry in the generated shapes, as confirmed by our proposed symmetry evaluation protocol.

For future work, we plan to conduct a more fine-grained analysis of evaluation metrics, particularly to understand their limitations in capturing structural properties like symmetry, continuity, and completeness. Additionally, we aim to extend our half-object training methodology to other generative models such as XCube, and SLIDE 3D, in order to evaluate whether the improvements in symmetry observed with PVD and LION generalize across different architectural paradigms.
