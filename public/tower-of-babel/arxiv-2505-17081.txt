Lieb-Mattis ordering theorem of electronic energy levels in the thermodynamic limit
Manuel Calixto, Alberto Mayorgas, Julio Guerrero

Abstract
Abstract Lieb-Mattis theorem orders the lowest-energy states of total spin s s of a system of P P interacting fermions. We generalize these predictions to fermionic mixtures of P P particles with more than N = 2 N=2 spinor components/species in the thermodynamic limit P → ∞ P\to\infty . The lowest-energy state inside each permutation symmetry sector h h , arising in the P P -fold tensor product decomposition, is well approximated by a U ( N ) (N) coherent (quasi-classical, variational) state, specially in the limit P → ∞ P\to\infty . In particular, the ground state of the system belongs the most symmetric (dominant Young tableau h 0 h_{0} ) configuration. We exemplify our construction with the N = 3 N=3 level Lipkin-Meshkov-Glick model, with a previous motivation on pairing correlations and U ( N ) (N) -invariant quantum Hall ferromagnets. In the limit P → ∞ P\to\infty , each lowest-energy state within each permutation symmetry sector h h undergoes a quantum phase transition for a critical value λ c ​ ( h ) \lambda_{c}(h) of the exchange coupling constant λ \lambda , depending on h h . This generalizes standard quantum phase transitions and their phase diagrams corresponding to the ground state belonging to the most symmetric sector h 0 h_{0} .

III
Lieb-Mattis ordering theorem of electronic energy levels

The set of Young diagrams arising in the Clebsch-Gordan decomposition of the
L
-fold tensor product (
11
) can be “energetically ordered” in the following sense.
The procedure is based on the so called “dominance order”
\succeq
, or “pouring principle”, implicit in the Lieb-Mattis theorem

[
45
]
(see also
[
46
]
for some recent applications/generalizations). More precisely, given two Young diagrams
h
and
h^{\prime}
of
P
particles/boxes, it is said that
h
“dominates”
h^{\prime}
(
h\succeq h^{\prime}
) [or that
h^{\prime}
“precedes”
h
(
h^{\prime}\preceq h
), or that
h^{\prime}
can be poured into
h
(
h^{\prime}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\to}}h
)] if

[h_{1},\dots,h_{N}]\succeq[h_{1}^{\prime},\dots,h_{N}^{\prime}]\\[10.00002pt]
\Leftrightarrow h_{1}+\dots+h_{k}\geq h_{1}^{\prime}+\dots+h_{k}^{\prime}\quad\forall k\in\{1,\dots,N\}\,.

(19)

Intuitively, it means that one can go from
h
to
h^{\prime}
by moving a certain number of boxes from
upper rows to lower rows, so that
h
is “more symmetric” than
h^{\prime}
. For example, one can see that the rectangular Young diagram

[2^{2}]
in (
15
)] dominates over the rest of Young diagrams (
[2^{2}]\succeq[2,1^{2}]\succeq[1^{4}]
) arising in the Clebsch-Gordan decomposition of
[1^{2}]\otimes[1^{2}]
. In general, one can prove
the following proposition (see
[
47
]
for a proof).

Proposition III.1

All Young diagrams arising in the Clebsch-Gordan direct sum decomposition of the
L
-fold
tensor product (
11
) can be poured into the rectangular Young diagram of shape
[L^{M}]
. That is,
[L^{M}]
dominates over the rest of Young diagrams
arising in the Clebsch-Gordan decomposition of
[1^{M}]^{\otimes L}

Note that, in general, not all arbitrary
P
-particle Young diagrams
h
and
h^{\prime}
can be compared, as either
h\succeq h^{\prime}
or
h\preceq h^{\prime}
not always hold for
P>5

[
46
]
. For instance,
h=[4,1,1]
and
h^{\prime}=[3,3,0]
fulfill
h_{1}>h_{1}^{\prime}
but
h_{1}+h_{2}<h_{1}^{\prime}+h_{2}^{\prime}
, hence neither
h\succeq h^{\prime}
nor
h\preceq h^{\prime}
is true. This is why the set of
P
-particle Young diagrams is only partially ordered.

Lieb-Mattis theorem
[
45
]
states that, under general conditions on the symmetric Hamiltonian of the system (i.e., non “pathologic” potentials), if
h^{\prime}
can be poured into
h
(that is,
h\succeq h^{\prime}
) then
E(h)<E(h^{\prime})
with
E(h)
the lowest-energy eigenvalue inside each IR
h
of
\mathrm{U}(N)
. Actually, the original Lieb-Mattis theorem is restricted to
N=2
spin components, so that IRs
h=[h_{1},h_{2}]
of
\mathrm{U}(2)
with
P=h_{1}+h_{2}
particles can be labelled by
h=[(2s+P)/2,(P-2s)/2]
, with
s=(h_{1}-h_{2})/2
the total spin; the most symmetric Young diagram corresponds to
h_{1}=P=2s,h_{2}=0
, to where the ground state of the
\mathrm{U}(2)
QHF at filling factor
M=1
belongs. Remember that the degeneracy
\mathcal{M}_{k}
of each
\mathrm{U}(2)
IR of spin
s_{k}=\frac{L}{2}-k,k=0,\dots,\left\lfloor{L/2}\right\rfloor
, arising in the the Clebsch-Gordan
decomposition series of
[1]^{\otimes L}
, is given in (
13
), with
\mathcal{M}_{0}=1
.
In general, for a
\mathrm{U}(N)
QHF at filling factor
M
, the ground state belongs to the carrier Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{N}[L^{M}]
of the rectangular IR
[L^{M}]
of
\mathrm{U}(N)
inside the total Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{N}^{\otimes L}[1^{M}]
.

We want to investigate whether or not the Lieb-Mattis theorem still holds in the thermodynamic limit
P\to\infty
and how to deal with it. For this purpose, we shall introduce CSs associated to a given
IR
h
of
\mathrm{U}(N)
. When the residual interaction strength
\lambda
is not low, coherent excitations
|h,Z\rangle
(see next section) above the HW state
|h,0\rangle
turn out to faithfully reproduce the energy of the lowest-energy eigenvector of a multiparticle Hamiltonian inside each permutation symmetry sector
h
. In the thermodymamical limit
P\to\infty
, CS Hamiltonian expectation values
\langle h,Z|H|h,Z\rangle
capture the critical points for systems underlying a QPT; see e.g
[
48
,
49
]
for the case of the
N=2
level LMG model and
[
50
,
51
]
for
a system of
P
indistinguishable atoms of
N
levels interacting dipolarly with

\ell
modes of an electromagnetic field.

Let us show how to construct CS and matrix elements of
\mathrm{U}(N)
-spin operators
S_{ij}
. Eventually we shall resort to the less technical cases,
N=2
and
N=3
fermion components, to exemplify the general case.

IV
U(N) Coherent states and their operator expectation values

Coherent states
|h,Y\rangle
are defined as
\mathrm{U}(N)
rotations/excitations
\mathcal{U}(Y)
above a highest-weight vector
|h,0\rangle
as

|h,Y\rangle=\mathcal{U}(Y)|h,0\rangle=e^{\mathrm{i}\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}y_{ij}S_{ij}}|h,0\rangle,

(20)

where
y_{ij}
are complex coordinates fulfilling
y_{ji}=\bar{y}_{ij}
since
S_{ij}^{\dagger}=S_{ji}
and
\mathcal{U}(Y)
is a unitary transformation.
The HW vector
|h,0\rangle
is always invariant (up to a phase) under the stability subgroup
\mathrm{U}(1)^{N}\subset\mathrm{U}(N)
made of diagonal transformations
\exp(\sum_{i=1}^N y_{ii}S_{ii})
. Therefore, the
\mathrm{U}(N)
transformations
\mathcal{U}(Y)
that do effectively create coherent excitations above
|h,0\rangle
belong to the coset
\mathbb{F}_{N}=\mathrm{U}(N)/\mathrm{U}(1)^{N}
(
flag manifold
), which is parametrized by
Y=(y_{ij}),i>j
, arranged as a complex lower-triangular
N\times N
matrix; therefore, the complex dimension of the phase space manifold
\mathbb{F}_{N}
is
\dim_{\mathbb{C}}(\mathbb{F}_{N})=N(N-1)/2
. The stability subgroup of
|h,0\rangle
can be larger than
\mathrm{U}(1)^{N}
; for example, as we already commented after (
6
), the stability subgroup of
|[L^{M}],0\rangle
is
\mathrm{U}(M)\times\mathrm{U}(N-M)
, so that coherent excitations
|[L^{M}],Y\rangle
above
|[L^{M}],0\rangle
are labelled by
(N-M)\times M
complex coordinates
y_{ij},M+1\leq i\leq N,1\leq j\leq M
parametrizing the coset
\mathbb{G}^{N}_{M}=\mathrm{U}(N)/\mathrm{U}(M)\times\mathrm{U}(N-M)
, which we identify with a
Grassmannian manifold
. For filling factor
M=1
, the Grassmannian manifold becomes
\mathbb{G}^{N}_{1}=\mathbb{C}P^{N-1}
the complex projective space, which is the phase space of
P=L
“symmetric quNits”
[
34
]
. In fact, for
N=2
spinor components and filling factor
M=1
, the matrix
Y
becomes a scalar complex coordinate
y=-\mathrm{i}\frac{\theta}{2}e^{\mathrm{i}\phi}
on the sphere
\mathbb{S}^{2}=\mathbb{G}^{2}_{1}=\mathrm{U}(2)/\mathrm{U}(1)^{2}
(the complex projective
\mathbb{C}P^{1}
space in this case)
given in terms of the spherical polar
\theta
and azimuthal
\phi
coordinates.

The fact that the HW vector
|h,0\rangle
is annihilated by any rising
\mathrm{U}(N)
-spin operator
S_{ij},i<j
, together with the Baker-Campbell-Hausdorff-Zassenhaus factorization formula applied to the group
\mathrm{U}(N)
(see e.g.
[
33
]
), allows one to write

\displaystyle|h,Y\rangle=

\displaystyle\,K_{h}(Z)e^{\sum_{j<i}z_{ij}S_{ij}}|h,0\rangle,

\displaystyle Z=

\displaystyle\,\mathrm{i}Y(Y^{\dagger}Y)^{-1/2}\tan(Y^\dag Y)^{1/2},

(21)

where
K_{h}(Z)
is a normalization factor to be discussed later. Abusing notation we will denote by
|h,Y\rangle=|h,Z\rangle
, in the hope that no confusion arises.

For
N=2
spin components we recover spin
s=(h_{1}-h_{2})/2
,
\mathrm{U}(2)
or “atomic” CSs
[
52
]

\displaystyle|[h_{1},h_{2}],z\rangle=

\displaystyle\,(1+|z|^{2})^{-s}e^{zS_{21}}|[h_{1},h_{2}],0\rangle,

\displaystyle z_{21}=z=

\displaystyle\,\frac{y}{|y|}\tan|y|,\;y=y_{21}=\frac{\theta}{2}e^{\mathrm{i}\phi},

(22)

where
S_{21}
is the spin lowering operator
S_{-}
and
z=\tan(\theta/2)e^{\mathrm{i}\phi}
can be identified with the stereographic projection of the Bloch sphere
\mathbb{S}^{2}\ni(\theta,\phi)
onto the complex plane
\mathbb{C}
. Spanning
e^{zS_{21}}|[h_{1},h_{2}],0\rangle=e^{zS_{-}}|s,s\rangle
in terms of Dicke states
\{|s,m\rangle,m=-s,\dots,s\}
we obtain a more familiar form of spin-
s
CSs

|[h_{1},h_{2}],z\rangle=(1+|z|^{2})^{-s}\sum_{m=-s}^{s}\sqrt{\binom{2s}{s-m}}z^{s-m}|s,m\rangle.

(23)

The normalization factor
K_{[h_{1},h_{2}]}(z)=(1+|z|^{2})^{-s}
for
\mathrm{U}(2)
is generalized to
\mathrm{U}(N)
as follows. We identify the “length”
(1+|z|^{2})
as the leading principal minor

|Z^{\dagger}Z|_{1}=1+\bar{z}z,\;Z=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&0\\
z&1\end{array}\right).

(24)

The same procedure applies for
N=3
fermion components, where now we have two lengths coming from the two upper-principal minors or order one and two

\displaystyle|Z^{\dagger}Z|_{1}=

\displaystyle\,1+\bar{z}_{1}z_{1}+\bar{z}_{2}z_{2},

\displaystyle|Z^{\dagger}Z|_{2}=

\displaystyle\,1+\bar{z}_{3}z_{3}+(\bar{z}_{2}-\bar{z}_{1}\bar{z}_{3})(z_{2}-z_{1}z_{3}),

\displaystyle Z=

\displaystyle\,\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}1&0&0\\
z_{1}&1&0\\
z_{2}&z_{3}&1\\
\end{array}\right).

In general, for
\mathrm{U}(N)
, we will have
N-1
lengths coming from the
N-1
upper-principal minors of
Z^{\dagger}Z
, with
Z
a lower-triangular complex
N\times N
matrix. The normalization factor
K_{h}(Z)
then turns out to be written as

K_{h}(Z)=|Z^{\dagger}Z|_{1}^{(h_{2}-h_{1})/2}|Z^{\dagger}Z|_{2}^{(h_{3}-h_{2})/2}\dots{\\
}\dots|Z^{\dagger}Z|_{N-1}^{(h_{N}-h_{N-1})/2}.

(28)

An interesting function is the reproducing Bergman kernel (CS overlap)

\displaystyle B_{h}(Z^{\prime},Z)=

\displaystyle\,\frac{\langle h,Z^{\prime}|h,Z\rangle}{{K_{h}(Z^{\prime})}K_{h}(Z)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,|Z^{\prime{\dagger}}Z|_{1}^{h_{1}-h_{2}}|Z^{\prime{\dagger}}Z|_{2}^{h_{2}-h_{3}}\dots|Z^{\prime{\dagger}}Z|_{N-1}^{h_{N-1}-h_{N}},

(29)

defined in terms of the
N-1
upper-principal minors of
Z^{\prime{\dagger}}Z
. It is holomorphic in
Z
and antiholomorphic in
Z^{\prime}
. For example, for
N=3
we explicitly have

B_{[h_{1},h_{2},h_{3}]}(Z^{\prime},Z)=(1+\bar{z}^{\prime}_{1}z_{1}+\bar{z}^{\prime}_{2}z_{2})^{h_{1}-h_{2}}\\
\times(1+\bar{z}^{\prime}_{3}z_{3}+(\bar{z}^{\prime}_{2}-\bar{z}^{\prime}_{1}\bar{z}^{\prime}_{3})(z_{2}-z_{1}z_{3}))^{h_{2}-h_{3}}.

(30)

Coherent state expectation values
\mathbb{s}_{ij}
of the basic symmetry operators
S_{ij}
can be easily computed through derivatives of the Bergman kernel as

\mathbb{s}_{ij}=\langle h,Z|S_{ij}|h,Z\rangle=\frac{\mathbb{S}_{ij}B_{h}(Z,Z)}{B_{h}(Z,Z)}.

(31)

where
\mathbb{S}_{ij}
denotes the differential representation of the
\mathrm{U}(N)
-spin
S_{ij}
operators (
2
) on anti-holomorphic functions
\psi(Z^{\dagger})=\langle h,Z|\psi\rangle/K_{h}(Z)
. This differential representation can be obtained in several ways; one is by using operator commutation properties like
[A,e^{zB}]=\pm z\partial_{z}e^{zB}
for
[A,B]=\pm B
, etc. The procedure is in general quite technical and we shall present here the results for
N=2
and
N=3
fermion components. For example, for
\mathrm{U}(2)
we have

\displaystyle(\mathbb{S}_{ij})=

\displaystyle\,\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}h_{1}-\bar{z}\partial_{\bar{z}}&&\partial_{\bar{z}}\\
(h_{1}-h_{2})\bar{z}-\bar{z}^{2}\partial_{\bar{z}}&&h_{2}+\bar{z}\partial_{\bar{z}}\end{array}\right),

\displaystyle(\mathbb{s}_{ij})=

\displaystyle\,\frac{1}{1+|z|^{2}}\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}h_{1}+h_{2}|z|^{2}&&(h_{1}-h_{2})z\\
(h_{1}-h_{2})\bar{z}&&h_{2}+h_{1}|z|^{2}\end{array}\right).

For
\mathrm{U}(3)
, the differential representation of
\mathrm{U}(3)
-spin operators is

\displaystyle\mathbb{S}_{11}=

\displaystyle\,h_{1}-\bar{z}_{2}\partial_{\bar{z}_{2}}-\bar{z}_{1}\partial_{\bar{z}_{1}}\,,

\displaystyle\mathbb{S}_{21}=

\displaystyle\,\bar{z}_{1}(h_{1}-h_{2})-(\bar{z}_{2}-\bar{z}_{1}\bar{z}_{3})\partial_{\bar{z}_{3}}-\bar{z}_{1}\left(\bar{z}_{2}\partial_{\bar{z}_{2}}+\bar{z}_{1}\partial_{\bar{z}_{1}}\right),

\displaystyle\mathbb{S}_{31}=

\displaystyle\,(h_{1}-h_{3})\bar{z}_{2}+(h_{3}-h_{2})\bar{z}_{1}\bar{z}_{3}-\bar{z}_{3}(\bar{z}_{2}-\bar{z}_{1}\bar{z}_{3})\partial_{\bar{z}_{3}},

\displaystyle\mathbb{S}_{12}=

\displaystyle\,\partial_{\bar{z}_{1}},

\displaystyle\mathbb{S}_{22}=

\displaystyle\,h_{2}+\bar{z}_{1}\partial_{\bar{z}_{1}}-\bar{z}_{3}\partial_{\bar{z}_{3}},

\displaystyle\mathbb{S}_{32}=

\displaystyle\,(h_{2}-h_{3})\bar{z}_{3}-\bar{z}_{3}^{2}\partial_{\bar{z}_{3}}+\bar{z}_{2}\partial_{\bar{z}_{1}},

\displaystyle\mathbb{S}_{13}=

\displaystyle\,\partial_{\bar{z}_{2}},

\displaystyle\quad\mathbb{S}_{23}\,=

\displaystyle\,\partial_{\bar{z}_{3}}+\bar{z}_{1}\partial_{\bar{z}_{2}},

\displaystyle\quad\mathbb{S}_{33}=

\displaystyle\,h_{3}+\bar{z}_{3}\partial_{\bar{z}_{3}}+\bar{z}_{2}\partial_{\bar{z}_{2}}\,,

(36)

and their CS expectation values

\displaystyle\mathbb{s}_{11}=

\displaystyle\,\frac{h_{1}}{|Z^{\dagger}Z|_{1}}+\frac{h_{2}|z_{1}+z_{2}\bar{z}_{3}|^{2}}{|Z^{\dagger}Z|_{1}|Z^{\dagger}Z|_{2}}+\frac{h_{3}|z_{2}-z_{1}z_{3}|^{2}}{|Z^{\dagger}Z|_{2}},

\displaystyle\mathbb{s}_{22}=

\displaystyle\,\frac{h_{1}|z_{1}|^{2}}{|Z^{\dagger}Z|_{1}}+\frac{h_{2}|1-z_{1}\bar{z}_{2}z_{3}+z_{2}\bar{z}_{2}|^{2}}{|Z^{\dagger}Z|_{1}|Z^{\dagger}Z|_{2}}+\frac{h_{3}|z_{3}|^{2}}{|Z^{\dagger}Z|_{2}},

\displaystyle\mathbb{s}_{33}=

\displaystyle\,\frac{h_{1}|z_{2}|^{2}+h_{2}(1+|z_{1}|^{2})}{|Z^{\dagger}Z|_{1}}+\frac{h_{3}-h_{2}}{|Z^{\dagger}Z|_{2}},

\displaystyle\mathbb{s}_{12}=

\displaystyle\,\frac{(h_{1}-h_{2})z_{1}}{|Z^{\dagger}Z|_{1}}+\frac{(h_{2}-h_{3})\bar{z}_{3}(z_{2}-z_{1}z_{3})}{|Z^{\dagger}Z|_{2}},

\displaystyle\mathbb{s}_{13}=

\displaystyle\,\frac{(h_{1}-h_{2})z_{2}}{|Z^{\dagger}Z|_{1}}+\frac{(h_{2}-h_{3})(z_{2}-z_{1}z_{3})}{|Z^{\dagger}Z|_{2}},

\displaystyle\mathbb{s}_{23}=

\displaystyle\,\frac{(h_{1}-h_{2})\bar{z}_{1}z_{2}}{|Z^{\dagger}Z|_{1}}+\frac{(h_{2}-h_{3})z_{3}}{|Z^{\dagger}Z|_{2}}

(37)

and
\mathbb{s}_{ij}=\bar{\mathbb{s}}_{ji}
for the reminder. Coherent state expectation values of higher powers of
\mathrm{U}(N)
-spin operators
S_{ij}
can also be easily computed by repeated differentiation of the Bergman kernel. For example,
for quadratic powers we have

\langle h,Z|S_{ij}S_{kl}|h,Z\rangle=\frac{\mathbb{S}_{ij}\left(\mathbb{S}_{kl}B_{h}(Z,Z)\right)}{B_{h}(Z,Z)}.

(38)

This calculation will be useful when evaluating two-body interaction terms in the Hamiltonian (
4
). In the thermodynamic/semi-classical limit
P\to\infty
this calculation simplifies since quantum fluctuations disappear and we have

\lim_{P\to\infty}\frac{\langle h,Z|S_{ij}S_{kl}|h,Z\rangle}{\langle h,Z|S_{ij}|h,Z\rangle\langle h,Z|S_{kl}|h,Z\rangle}=1,

(39)

which means that, in this limit, CS expectation values of quadratic powers
S_{ij}S_{kl}
can be simply written as the product
\mathbb{s}_{ij}\mathbb{s}_{kl}
.

As we have already said, for higher two-body interaction strength
\lambda\not=0

|h,Z\rangle
above the HW state
|h,0\rangle

P\to\infty

L\to\infty

\mathrm{U}(N)
quantum Hall ferromagnets described by nonlinear sigma models which target space is the Grassmannian
\mathbb{G}^{N}_{M}
phase space manifold
[
22
,

,
24
,
25
,
26
,
27
,
28
]
. In the next section we shall use these CS expectation values to compute the energy surface
\langle h,Z|H|h,Z\rangle
of our Hamiltonian
H
for each permutation symmetry sector
h
of the system.

V
LMG model and phase diagram in each permutation symmetry sector

To test and describe the Lieb-Mattis theorem in the thermodynamic limit, we shall make use of a generalization of the traditional
N=2
-level LMG model to arbitrary
N
levels. The ubiquitous LMG Hamiltonian model acquires multiple forms. The closest to our construction (
4
), with filling factor
M=1
, is the one of an anisotropic XY Ising model

H_{XY}=\epsilon\sum_{\alpha=1}^{L}\sigma_{z}(\alpha)+\sum_{\alpha<\beta}\lambda_{x}\sigma_{x}(\alpha)\sigma_{x}(\beta)\\
+\sum_{\alpha<\beta}\lambda_{y}\sigma_{y}(\alpha)\sigma_{y}(\beta)\,,

(40)

in an external perpendicular magnetic field
\epsilon
with infinite-range constant
interactions
\lambda_{x,y}
, and
\vec{\sigma}(\alpha)=/\sigma_{x}(\alpha),\sigma_{y}(\alpha),\sigma_{z}(\alpha))
the Pauli matrices at lattice site
\alpha
. In terms of collective
U(2)
angular momentum operators

\vec{J}=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{L}\vec{\sigma}(\alpha)
, the two-level LMG Hamiltonian acquires the form
[
53
,
54
]
:

H_{XY}=\epsilon J_{z}+\frac{\lambda_{1}}{2}(J_{+}^{2}+J_{-}^{2})+\frac{\lambda_{2}}{2}(J_{+}J_{-}+J_{-}J_{+})\>,

(41)

with
\lambda_{1,2}
a function of
\lambda_{x,y}
. The
\lambda_{1}
term causes the annihilation of particles at one of the two levels and the creation of particles at the other level, and the
\lambda_{2}
term results in the scattering of one particle upwards while simultaneously scattering another particle downwards. The total number of particles
P=L=2j
and
\vec{J}^{2}=j(j+1)
are conserved. Permutation and U(2)
symmetries reduce the size of the largest matrix to be diagonalized from
2^{P}
to
P+1=2j+1
.

We extend this model to
N
-level atoms with Hamiltonian density

H_{N}=\frac{\epsilon}{P}(S_{NN}-S_{11})-\frac{\lambda}{P(P-1)}\sum_{i\not=j=1}^{N}S_{ij}^{2},

(42)

described by collective
\mathrm{U}(N)
operators
S_{ij}=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{L}S_{ij}(\alpha)
. For simplicity, we have discarded interactions of fermions with the same spinor component [i.e. we have disregarded terms of the type
S_{ij}S_{ji}+h.c.
like the ones accompanying
\lambda_{2}
in (
41
)] and kept equal interactions for fermions with different spinor components/flavors [i.e. we have maintained
S_{ij}^{2}
like the ones accompanying
\lambda_{1}
in (
41
)] so that the parity
e^{\mathrm{i}\pi S_{ii}}
of the population
S_{ii}
of each flavor
i
is conserved. We have also disposed splitting energies
\epsilon_{i}
equally spaced
\epsilon_{i}=\epsilon
and symmetrically about
i=(N+1)/2
. Since we are interested in the thermodynamic limit
P\to\infty
, we have made the Hamiltonian density (
42
) an intensive quantity by dividing one-body interactions (those proportional to
S_{ij}
) by the total number of particles
P
, and two-body interactions (those proportional to
S_{ij}^{2}
) by the number of particle pairs
P(P-1)
. In the following we shall restrict ourselves to
N=2
and
N=3
spinor components (
N
atom levels in the usual LMG jargon), for simplicity. We shall take
\epsilon=1
, which is equivalent to measure the energy and
\lambda
in
\epsilon
units.

V.1
N=2
fermion spinor components

The CS expectation value of the Hamiltonian density
H_{2}
in each permutation symmetry sector
h=[h_{1},h_{2}]=[P/2+s,P/2-s]
of
P=h_{1}+h_{2}
particles and total spin
s=(h_{1}-h_{2})/2
is

\langle h,z|H_{2}|h,z\rangle=\frac{1}{P}(\mathbb{s}_{22}-\mathbb{s}_{11})-\frac{\lambda}{P(P-1)}\sum_{i\not=j=1}^{2}\mathbb{s}_{ij}^{2},

(43)

where
s_{ij}
is defined in (
31
) and its explicit expression is given in (
IV
). The
so called
energy surface
is defined in the thermodynamic limit

\displaystyle E_{\mu}^{z}(\lambda)=

\displaystyle\,\lim_{P\to\infty}\langle h,z|H_{2}|h,z\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,(2\mu-1)\frac{|z|^{4}-1-\lambda(2\mu-1)(z^{2}+\bar{z}^{2})}{(1+|z|^{2})^{2}},

(44)

where we have defined the fraction
\mu=h_{1}/P=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{s}{P}
. Note that
\mu\in[\frac{1}{2},1]
is a continuous (non discrete) parameter in the thermodynamic limit. Note also that
h
dominates
h^{\prime}
(
h\succeq h^{\prime}
) iff
\mu>\mu^{\prime}
or
s>s^{\prime}
. Let us see whether the Lieb-Mattis theorem still holds in the thermodynamic limit. Applying Gilmore’s minimization procedure
[
52
]
, the lowest-energy inside each permutation symmetry sector
\mu
turns out to be

E^{(0)}_{\mu}(\lambda)=\min_{z\in\mathbb{C}}E_{\mu}^{z}(\lambda)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lr}1-2\mu,&\lambda<\frac{1}{2\mu-1},\\
-\frac{1+\lambda^{2}(1-2\mu)^{2}}{2\lambda}&\lambda\geq\frac{1}{2\mu-1},\end{array}\right.

(45)

with minimal coordinates

z_{0}^{\pm}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lr}0,&\lambda<\frac{1}{2\mu-1},\\
\pm\sqrt{\frac{\lambda(2\mu-1)-1}{\lambda(2\mu-1)+1}}&\lambda\geq\frac{1}{2\mu-1}.\end{array}\right.

(46)

The fact that there are two ground states
|h,z_{0}^{\pm}\rangle
with the same energy
E^{(0)}_{\mu}(\lambda)
is related to a spontaneous breakdown of the parity symmetry of the Hamiltonian
H_{2}
in the thermodymamical limit.

We identify
\lambda_{c}(\mu)=1/(2\mu-1)
as a critical value of the interaction strength control parameter for which a second order QPT inside the permutation symmetry sector
\mu
takes place. This concept was firstly introduced by us in
[
43
]
and termed “mixed symmetry” QPT (MSQPT). Standard QPTs are usually associated to the totally symmetric permutation symmetry sector
\mu=1,s=P/2
, for which
\lambda_{c}(1)=1
. MSQPTs extend this concept to general
\mu\in[\frac{1}{2},1]
(general
h=[h_{1},h_{2}]
). Other extensions of the QPT concept appear in the literature, but they still refer to the ground state (fully symmetric
\mu=1
) sector; see e.g.
[
55
,
56
]
for the concept of “Excited State Quantum Phase Transitions” (ESQPTs) when excited states above the ground state are also analyzed.

Since

\partial_{\mu}E^{(0)}_{\mu}(\lambda)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lr}-2,&\lambda<\lambda_{c},\\
-2\lambda(2\mu-1)&\lambda\geq\lambda_{c},\end{array}\right.

(47)

is non positive for
\mu\in[\frac{1}{2},1]
, the energy density
E^{(0)}_{\mu}
is a decreasing function of
\mu
, and therefore of the spin
s
, in the thermodynamic limit. Thus, Lieb-Mattis theorem also applies in this limit. The case
\mu=1/2
corresponds to the spin singlet
s=0
and does not display a MSQPT since
\lambda_{c}(1/2)\to\infty
.

V.2
N=3
fermion components

In order to take the limit
P=h_{1}+h_{2}+h_{3}\to\infty

\mu,\nu
as

\displaystyle h_{3}=\nu P,\quad h_{2}=

\displaystyle\,(1-\mu-\nu)P,\quad h_{1}=\mu P,

\displaystyle\forall\,\nu\in[0,\tfrac{1}{3}],

(\mu,\nu)

\left.\begin{array}[]{ll}-\mu+\nu,&\quad 0\leq\lambda<\frac{1}{2(2\mu+\nu-1)}\\

VI
Conclusions and outlook

The subject of quantum phase transitions is enriched when we increase the number
N
of internal components of the essential constituents or particles, and when we incorporate to the study, not only the ground state of the system belonging to the most symmetric sector, but all symmetry sectors
h
with mixed symmetries (fermionic mixtures), arising in the
P
-fold tensor product Clebsch-Gordan decomposition for
P
particles, in the thermodynamic limit
P\to\infty
. Mixed symmetry sectors
h
refer to both: permutation symmetry
S_{N}
and the different irreducible representations of the underlying symmetry group
\mathrm{U}(N)

[
36
]
. Since the Hamiltonian is
\mathrm{U}(N)
-invariant, temporal evolution does not mix symmetry sectors
h
. Therefore, QPTs can occur inside each mixed symmetry sector
h
for critical values
\lambda_{c}(h)
of the interaction strength parameter
\lambda
, thus extending the concept of QPT to Mixed Symmetry QPT (MSQPT) and the phase diagram can be enlarged from
\lambda
to
(\lambda,h)
. The Lieb-Mattis theorem provides an ordering of the lowest-energy Hamiltonian eigenstates inside each symmetry sector
h
through dominance relations. We show how this ordering can be extended to the thermodynamic limit through monotonic properties of energy density functions in the extended phase diagram
(\lambda,h)
. Coherent states on Grassmannian phase spaces
\mathrm{U}(N)/\mathrm{U}(M)\times\mathrm{U}(N-M)
(for filling factor
M
) turn out to be good variational quasi-classical states reproducing the ground state system inside each mixed permutation symmetry sector, specially in the thermodynamic limit.

We have exemplified this general construction with a generalization of the traditional Lipkin-Meshkov-Glick Hamiltonian model for
P
identical two-level particles to
N
levels, focusing on the cases
N=2
and
N=3
. The LMG model undergoes a second order QPT for certain critical values
\lambda_{c}
of the interaction parameter
\lambda
. These critical
\lambda_{c}
values are affected when we move from the ground state (fully symmetric) sector
h_{0}\sim(\mu=1,\nu=0)
to other permutation symmetry sectors
h\sim(\mu,\nu)
. We show that the corresponding lowest energy density states inside each sector
(\mu,\nu)
are still ordered according to the Lieb-Mattis theorem, even for
P\to\infty
, where dominance ordering is replaced by analytic and monotonic properties of the energy density
E_{\mu,\nu}^{(0)}(\lambda)
with respect to the
continuous
variables
(\mu,\nu)
. The phase diagram of the system is enriched when we introduce the continuous symmetry sector labels
(\mu,\nu)
as extra control parameters in addition to the interaction strength
\lambda
, with the appearance of new quantum phases as regards standard QPTs occurring in the fully symmetric sector
(\mu=1,\nu=0)
.
