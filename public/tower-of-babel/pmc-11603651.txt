Retrocaval ureter: a case report and review of the literature
Authors: Isaack Mlatie Maro, Charles John Nhungo, Shukrani John Ngereja, Joseph Martin Lori, Sylvia Bedas Nsato, Kimu Marko Njiku, Obadia Nyongole, Charles A Mkony
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe retrocaval ureter is a rare congenital anomaly resulting from abnormal development of the inferior vena cava. The obstruction is usually at the retrocaval segment of the ureter, as it lies between the inferior vena cava and the body of the third lumbar vertebra. Computed tomography intravenous urography is the gold standard for investigating this condition and can reveal ipsilateral hydronephrosis and the fishhook sign or sickle sign of the proximal ureter, depending on the type of retrocaval ureter.Case presentationWe present the case of a 30-year-old African female who presented with intermittent dull right flank pain for 3 years. Computed tomography intravenous urography revealed isolated moderate right hydronephrosis with a fishhook sign in the proximal ureter. Surgical correction was performed, and the patient was followed for 6 months with improved renal function.ConclusionSurgery remains the sole definitive management of the retrocaval ureter for symptomatic patients and those with worsening hydronephrosis or differential renal function.Keywords: Retrocaval ureter, Congenital anomaly, Inferior vena cava, Ipsilateral hydronephrosis

Introduction

The retrocaval ureter, also referred to as the pre-ureteral vena cava, is a rare congenital entity in which the ureter passes posterior to the inferior vena cava (IVC) [1]. It results from abnormal persistence of the right subcardinal vein ventral to the ureter during IVC embryogenesis [2]. The developing right ureter courses behind and medial to the IVC over the pedicle of the L3–L4 vertebrae and then exits between the IVC and the aorta, returning to its normal position. This produces varying degrees of hydroureteronephrosis [3].

Even though it is a congenital defect, individuals typically do not exhibit symptoms until their third or fourth decade of life. Symptoms arise due to pelvic-ureteric junction obstruction secondary to either kinking of the ureter, adynamic retrocaval segment of ureter, or compression of retrocaval segment of ureter against psoas muscle [4]. Also symptoms may arise from complications of pelvic ureteric junction obstruction, such as secondary nephrolithiasis and pyelonephritis [5]. The clinical symptomatology of the retrocaval ureter is not specific to the abnormality, and the diagnosis is based on radiological investigations, especially contrasted abdominal pelvic computed tomography (CT) scans [6].

We present the case of a 30-year-old female with a type I retrocaval ureter who presented with intermittent dull right flank pain for 6 years. CT intravenous urography (IVU) revealed isolated moderate hydronephrosis with fishhook sign in the proximal ureter. Surgical correction was performed by mobilization of the retrocaval ureter and uretero-ureteric anastomosis. The patient was followed for 6 months and experienced improved renal outcomes and symptom resolution.

Case presentation

We report a case of 30-year-old African female who presented at our urology outpatient clinic with a chief complaint of intermittent severe dull right flank pain for 3 years; recently, it was associated with general body malaise accompanied by episodes of nausea and vomiting. The patient denied history of fever, hematuria, or lower urinary tract symptoms. Two years prior to admission she did an abdominopelvic ultrasound at a peripheral hospital and was diagnosed with recurrent chronic cystitis and right hydronephrosis. She was treated with antibiotics and her physician decided to do endoscopic double-J (DJ) stent insertion. Stent was left for 3 months and during this time she reported improvement in abdominal pain; however, pain recurred after DJ stent removal. She reported a subsequent DJ stent insertion, which resulted again in symptom alleviation. The patient denied history of similar illness in her family.

Physical examination revealed normal findings. Abdominal examination revealed mild tenderness in the right flank with non-ballotable kidneys. The results of laboratory investigations, such as hematology and biochemistry, were within the normal range.

Kidney–ureter–bladder (KUB) ultrasonography revealed proximal hydro-ureteropelvic distension with moderate loss of corticomedullary differentiation in the right kidney. A routine X-ray of the kidney–ureter–bladder was normal, with no evidence of renal calculi. A contrasted abdominopelvic computed tomography volume-rendered image exquisitely depicted the “fishhook” or “S-shaped appearance” of the right ureter at the level of L3 vertebra with moderate hydronephrosis (Fig. 1) and dilatation of the ureter proximal to it and normal left kidney signaling the right retrocaval ureter and pelvi-ureteric junction obstruction (Fig. 2 and 3).

The patient underwent ureteroureterostomy after consenting, through a right flank incision via an extraperitoneal approach we found a dilated renal pelvis, retrocaval ureter crossing the inferior vena cava at the level of lumbar three (L3) vertebra (Fig. 4) and stenotic retrocaval segment of ureter. After isolating the right renal pelvis and ureter (Fig. 5), the ureter was marked with a vessel loop and mobilized cranially and caudally to stenotic retrocaval segment. The ureters were handled carefully and non-traumatically to spare its blood supply and for prevention of anastomotic leak or atrophy. Both ends of the ureter adventitia were freed of scarred tissue, ensured a good vascular supply, and brought together with tension free. The stenotic retrocaval segment was removed. The proximal and distal end were spatulated about 7–10 mm at 180° apart, then stent was inserted. Corner sutures were placed to both ends by PDS 5–0. Watertight anastomosis was accomplished by completing the ureteral anastomosis utilizing the corner sutures in an interrupted manner.

A surgical drain was placed and removed on day fourth when there was insignificant drainage, and a Foley catheter left indwelling for 72 h.

After 6 weeks, control KUB ultrasonography revealed resolving right sided ureteropelvic hydronephrosis, and the double-J stent was removed. Six-month patient follow-up demonstrated complete resolution of abdominal pain with no evidence of ureteral stricture.

Discussion

Retrocaval ureter is a vascular anomaly resulting in proximal ureter crossing behind the inferior vena cava. It is an abnormality of the embryological development of the venous system and not of the urinary tract [6]. It is a rare congenital malformation with prevalence of 1:1500; its more common in male compared with female, with a ratio of 3:1(4). Most commonly, retrocaval ureter is found on the right side; however, it can rarely be found on the left side in patients with situs inversus and those with double vena cava [5].

This anomaly occurs between the 4th and 8th weeks of intrauterine development and is due to abnormal formation of infrarenal IVC from anteriorly located subcardinal vein instead of supracardinal vein which are located posteriorly [5, 7]. In normal circumstances, the infrarenal IVC originates from dorsally located supracardinal vein, but when it develops from the ventrally located subcardinal vein, the ureter is trapped posteriorly leading to pre-ureteral vena cava [2, 5]. This anomaly is three times more common in males than in females [3].

Bateson et al. in 1969 classified retrocaval ureter into two types which are type 1 (low loop) of the proximal ureter and type II (high loop) of the ureteropelvic junction. Type I retrocaval ureter is the most prevalent accounting for more than 90% of deformities and has extreme medial deviation of middle ureteric segment. The ureteric obstruction is typically at the edge of the iliopsoas muscle, at which the ureter deviates cephalad before passing behind the vena cava. Normally, ureter crosses vena cava at the level of lumbar three vertebra (L3). This results in a proximal ureteral dilation and hydronephrosis which is seen in over 50% of patients, demonstrating a fishhook, reverse-J, or S-shaped ureteral curve [8].

Type II is a high loop of the ureteropelvic junction. It is less obstructive compared with type I. Its proximal ureter crosses inferior vena cava horizontally at the level of renal pelvis, forms a sickle deformity appearance and has lower medial deviation of the ureter with mild or no associated hydronephrosis and forms about 10% of all cases [8]. Most patients are asymptomatic, but those with symptoms present with flank pain and/or recurrent urinary tract infections due to urinary stasis [9].

Imaging studies are important for an accurate preoperative diagnosis and successful surgical management. In our patient, abdominal ultrasound demonstrated hydronephrosis and proximal hydroureter without calculus. In patients with retrocaval ureter, abdominopelvic CT IVU shows typical “fishhook” or “reverse J” or “S-shaped” deformity in the proximal dilated ureter with moderate hydronephrosis [10]. Nowadays, computed tomography intravenous urography (CT IVU) is preferred over IVU or retrograde pyelography (RPG), as it is less invansive and may depict the anomaly in three dimensions, giving the most “wholesome” solution to the diagnosis [8]. The abdominal CT scan also makes it possible to make a differential diagnosis with primary idiopathic or secondary retroperitoneal fibrosis, and with any other cause of ureteral obstruction [3]. In a place where CT IVU cannot be done, retrograde pyelogram may be helpful; it demonstrates spiral course of the ureter around inferior vena cava [9]. Our patient’s abdominopelvic CT scan showed moderate hydronephrosis with fishhook deformity of the right ureter and pelvi-ureteric junction obstruction.

Surgical management of retrocaval ureter is reserved for the symptomatic patient, patients with worsening hydronephrosis and deteriorating differential renal function [4]. Surgical management may be through retroperitoneal or transperitoneal approach, it involves division of the ureter and repositioning it anterior to the inferior vena cava. This may be achieved through an anastomosis between the renal pelvis and the ureter or a uretero-ureteric anastomosis over a double-J stent. The segment behind the inferior vena cava, which may be aperistaltic, is either excised or left in situ [4].

The surgical approach can be open or laparoscopic, and in some cases robot-assisted where facilities permit [4]. Laparoscopic reconstructive techniques have been described by several authors who believe that it should be the technique of choice in the surgical treatment of the retrocaval ureter; this technique offers several advantages over conventional open surgery: it is minimally invasive, allows early ambulation with minimal complications such as postoperative wall pain or gastrointestinal ileus, and a shorter recovery period [11].

Conclusion

Retrocaval ureter can easily be misdiagnosed as nephrolithiasis. Retrocaval ureter can be asymptomatic or associated with non-specific symptoms, dominated by low back pain and intermittent flank pain; the diagnosis is confirmed by the CT IVU. Clinicians need to have high index of suspicion of retrocaval ureter in patients presenting with dull intermittent right flank pain typically in their third or fourth decade. Surgical treatment of patients with symptoms commonly completely relieves symptoms. Following up with ultrasound is the most important part in the management.
