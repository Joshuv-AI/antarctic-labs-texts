Introduction

Four-dimensional printing (4DP) technology, developed from three-dimensional printing (3DP), allows for the printing of structures that can alter their shape or structure in response to external stimuli. This technology directly incorporates smart materials and deformation design into the printing process, streamlining the transition from design concepts to physical objects. It enables objects to automatically assemble themselves into different configurations and integrates product design, manufacturing, and assembly by introducing time-dependent dimensions to the 3DP process [1]. 4DP is the integration of a 3D printer, intelligent material, and a meticulously coded design. Presently, the predominant area of investigation in 4DP revolves around the capacity of printed materials to undergo shape alterations, including elongation, bending, corrugation, and twisting. 4DP utilizes materials that have the ability to alter form, as well as undergo color change upon exposure to UV and visible light [2]. The approach for 4DP is comparable to 3DP in terms of manufacturing operations. However, the innovation in 4DP lies in the selection and characterization of materials that can respond to an active agent. Plastic, ceramic, and metal are commonly used materials in 3DP; however, they are not appropriate for 4DP. The materials utilized in 4DP technology encompass a range of substances such as shape-memory polymers, shape-memory alloys, shape-memory metals, hydrogel-responsive materials, shape-memory ceramics, and liquid crystal elastomers [3].

4DP has gained attention in healthcare due to advancements in personalized dynamic structures, which can change shape or activate specific functions in response to stimuli. It is being more recognized as a technology that can have a crucial impact on assisting cancer therapies, by enabling form, function, or property modifications, hence facilitating tailored medicine for targeted cancer treatments [4]. This is the strength of the SMMs in their application to several biological areas, including the drug delivery systems, self-expanding stents, implanted devices, catheters, guide wires, atrial occlusion, and thrombectomy devices [5]. 4DP can change the probability of considering a customized medical intervention. Recent studies have concluded that the use of 3D printed parts allows the production of therapeutic as well as rehabilitative pieces of equipment that can be customized depending on the individual patients [6]. The enhanced functionality of the self-monitoring mechanism (SMM) introduced by the enhancement nourishment directly correlates with the subsequent improvement of intelligent medical devices, which have no borders, including the possible cure itself. The 4DP process has raised some attention due to its potential application in cancer treatments. It is an improved variant of the 3DP process, enabling the creation of complex structures with programmable forms, programmed movement, and customized functionalities. It is commonly accepted that applications of cancer are currently in a nascent form and that more research should be conducted on 4DP [7]. 4DP has been adopted in optimizing the effectiveness of cancer treatment strategies that involve surgery, hyperthermia, photothermal therapy, chemotherapy, as well as therapeutic devices. Therefore, it is also possible to assume that it could be seen as the next alternative to cancer treatment, offering the option of personalized treatment. A recent examination has been conducted on the utilization of 3DP and 4DP in the battle against breast cancer, which is the second most prevalent kind of cancer globally [8]. Integrating intelligent materials, such as pH- or enzyme-responsive materials, into 4DP can improve targeted therapies due to the unique cancer microenvironment. Furthermore, many categories of intelligent materials, such as those that respond to light, magnetism, and temperature, hold significant promise in the development of dynamic structures for 4DP [9,10].

How 4D printing is different from 3D printing in cancer treatment?

Introduction to 3D printing

Three-dimensional (3D) printing, also known as additive manufacturing (AM), is a cutting-edge method that constructs three-dimensional objects by incrementally creating them layer upon layer. Back in the early 1980s, Chuck Hull patented the idea of 3D printing, a technique later termed stereolithography, which has grown immensely since its conception. The process involves the progressive hardening of a fluid polymer into a durable piece by sifting it with a UV laser, one layer by one layer. Over the recent years, the availability of 3DP technology has increased owing to the reduced prices of 3D printers. Consequently, it has found application in most fields of knowledge, including electronics, robotics, and in the health sector [11].

Methods and materials in 3D printing

There are three significant 3DP types depending on the materials used, namely, solid, liquid, and powder-based methods. The prominent benefits of each category are different and are determined by the complexity and parameters of the produced object. Additive manufacturing, also known as 3DP, is the production of any object directly by use of a computer-aided design (CAD) model. The process allows the construction of complex structures that were not possible to create easily or at all using conventional manufacturing processes [12]. The adaptability of 3DP is embodied in its multiplicity of materials used and its capability in the manufacturing process. This enables fabrication of extremely delicate and specialized items, and is especially applicable in fields where precision and adaptability are required [7]. 3DP has received much attention in the medical field because it can produce specifically made implants and prosthetics for individual users. It has also played the pivotal role in regenerative medicine, where the process has been used to construct living tissue structures and scaffolds with precise and carefully controlled spatial patterns. Such scaffolds could be adapted to fulfill individual needs of patients, therefore, leading to improvement of treatments and patient outcomes [13,14].

Introduction to 4D printing

4DP is a technology that enhances AM technology by introducing a dynamic aspect into the 3DP process, which is typically static [15]. In 2013, Skylar Tibbits, an assistant professor at MIT, presented the concept of the 4DP. This term denotes the manufacture of three-dimensional printed objects, which can evolve and grow over time [16]. The schematic diagram showed the biomedical field development year-by-year using various 4D bioprinting technologies (Fig. 1). The Self-Assembly Lab at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology (MIT) has invented a new method using new materials specially designed to change when a stimulus is added. They can be altered in size, shape, color, or texture by controlling some factors in the environment, such as magnetic or electric fields, pH levels, pressure, heat, moisture, or chemicals [17].

Dynamic characteristics of 4D printing

4DP utilizes the dynamic, interactive characteristics of 3D printed buildings. Unlike 3D printed buildings, which are often seen to be rigid, 4DP utilizes materials that change over time in response to their properties or attributes. Allowing time to be a fourth dimension then allows printed objects to evolve, adapt, or self-assemble into ever more complex forms without any manual input. This cuts down on wastage besides saving time [18]. Its procedure uses smart materials, including shape memory polymers, hydrogels, and bio-inks. Such materials can be transformed based on a change of temperature, pH, or moisture. Additionally, they can integrate a functional change that occurs over time following the printing process [19,20].

Although still in its early phase, 4DP has enthralled both scholars and professionals in several industries. It has the potential to completely transform several industries, especially the field of medical services. 4DP has the potential to be utilized in healthcare for the production of self-assembling surgical equipment and intelligent pharmaceutical devices that administer therapy in a progressive manner. The printing technique utilizes stimulus-responsive materials that allow the items to alter their shape or qualities when exposed to specific stimuli. This capability marks a significant departure from 3DP, where objects remain static post-production [15]. Both 4D and 3DP utilize layer-by-layer fabrication methods and employ comparable production processes such as extrusion, vat photopolymerization, jetting, directed energy deposition, and powder bed fusion. Nevertheless, the primary distinction is in the materials employed; exclusively 4DP materials possess the capability to undergo alterations in shape, features, and functions upon exposure to certain stimuli [21]. As the development of 4DP progresses, it holds the potential to emerge as a significant trend in the manufacturing and design sectors of the future. The capacity to manufacture goods made of a single material that may change their shape and principal stress-bearing locations in order to adapt their internal tension brings unparalleled flexibility and inventiveness in design [22].

Differences between 3D and 4D printing in cancer treatment

The medical profession is being revolutionized by the use of 3DP and 4DP technologies, which provide sophisticated solutions for the development and manufacturing of biomedical equipment, including their application in cancer therapy. 3DP facilitates the construction of complex structures by systematically constructing them layer by layer based on digital models. In contrast, 4DP adds a new dimension, time, which enables printed objects to alter and react to external stimuli after they have been made.

Adaptability and responsiveness

Traditional 3DP involves creating motionless, three-dimensional objects through the use of digital models. The material that makes up the structures consists of layers added to build up a shape sequentially, usually polymers, ceramics, or metals [23]. These are printed out, and once they are done, they remain in a stagnant status and do not change. On the contrary, 4DP utilizes materials that their properties or structures can be modified in response to light, moisture, among others [24]. The flexible nature of this form of system powers construction of buildings that can adapt and modify even after being constructed. Shape-memory polymers could be programmed to change shape under specific temperatures. This property allows one to develop gadgets that may adapt to the internal environment of the human body [25].

Functionality

The primary application of 3D printing in cancer care is the production of customized forms, implants, and equipment. These comprise anatomical models specific to a given patient, surgical guidance, and patient-specific prosthesis, assisting in preoperative planning and simulation [26]. These applications have increased the accuracy of the surgical interventions and individualized care to patients. The improved functionality of 4DP is based on the idea that it will be able to make adaptive and multifunctional devices. In order to provide specific and controlled drug release, such as printed drug delivery systems, 4D ones may respond to specific biological cues by releasing therapeutic agents [27]. Moreover, the implants that have been 4D printed may also adapt to match the surrounding tissue, therefore, decreasing the risk of being rejected and the necessity of revision surgery [28].

Customization and precision

Although 3DP enables great customization of medical devices and implants by using patient-specific data, the products obtained are fixed. Examples include custom anatomical models made using imaging data that exactly fit the anatomy of the patient, which can be used to aid surgical accuracy and streamline surgical times [29]. 4DP also takes the idea of customization and extends it to include dynamic behavior. Due to this characteristic, implants and other devices are capable of aligning naturally with other components of the changing environment of the patient or with his physique. A stent made by the 4DP technology can even expand or contract due to changes in blood flow, or pressurization, ensuring better functioning and failure-free life [30,31].

Dynamic drug delivery systems

Traditional 3D printed drug delivery decoys tend to be stationary and are designed to dispense drugs at a fixed rate. The systems can be programmed to an individual degree of release profile but cannot vary according to the physiological conditions of the body [32]. Nevertheless, 4D printed delivery containers for drug products could be developed to react to some stimulus, meaning that they may release pharmaceuticals more reactively and controlled. Expanding or contracting hydrogel in response to changes in the pH level can be used as a way to dispatch any medication to a certain point of the gastrointestinal system, hence enhancing the effects and reducing the side effects at the same time [33].

Adaptive implants

A 3D-printed implant is highly customizable but does not evolve after implantation. They are custom-made to suit the anatomy of the patient, along with enhancing the results of the reconstructive surgeries and improving the time of recovery [34]. Implants designed using 4DP can alter their traits through physiological changes. An example is that bone scaffolds that stiffen with time can be used to enhance integration and accelerate recovery, where the scaffold stiffness adapts to the stiffness of the healing surrounding tissue [35]. Such adaptive features have the potential to improve the effectiveness as well as the lifespan of medical implants.

Minimally invasive procedures

Devices created through 3DP have the potential to aid in minimally invasive surgery, such as those that offer precise, patient-specific models and surgical plans. Nevertheless, the devices on their own are not dynamic, and they might need surgical interventions to accommodate emerging anatomical structures [36]. The post-implantation environment can be transformed so that procedures may be less invasive with the help of 4D printed gadgets. The example of a 4D printed catheter that could deform itself to pass through an intricately small arterial passageway would lessen the number of treatment procedures, thereby creating less risk to the patient and accelerating recovery [37].

Smart materials applied for 4D printing

4DP is an extension of additive manufacturing, notable in that it goes beyond 3DP by adding a so-called smart material or programmed material. These materials are critical to 4DP since they can undergo structural or functional changes in response to external stimuli (such as alterations in physiological or environmental conditions). Stimulated by an intelligent combination of these materials, printed constructs will change over time and align with the particular needs of application environments, which is of paramount importance, especially in medicine, a highly dynamic domain [38,39]. Smart materials, which are crucial for the advancement of 4DP, can react to various stimuli, including pH levels, enzymes, light, temperature, and magnetic fields. This responsive reaction to stimuli introduces a fourth dimension, namely time, to the production process.

The variety of the material applied in 4DP is not limited to one type and may incorporate both polymers, ceramics, alloys, composites, and metals. Such materials are now able to display unique properties, including the capacity to fix themselves, transform, perceive surroundings, and construct themselves. The materials can be prepared to make one change or multiple changes, one-way changes that keep their modified state or two-way changes where modifications could be reversible [[40], [41], [42]]. In 4D cancer treatment, printable materials that respond to an external stimulus are utilized to perform accurate therapeutic tasks. Temperature and pH-sensitive hydrogels, such as polyethylene glycol (PEG) and polyvinyl alcohol (PVA), are commonly applied because they can control drug release and perform tissue engineering. Shape-memory polymers (SMPs), including polylactic acid (PLA) and polycaprolactone (PCL), enable localized drug release at tumor sites by expanding in response to heat. Thermosensitive polymers, such as poly(N-isopropylacrylamide), located in areas of the cancer, help to release drugs subject to temperature changes. Carbon nanotubes (CNTs) have been applied based on their mechanical and thermal properties to effect hyperthermia treatment and targeted drug delivery. Tissue regenerative and regulated drug delivery is made possible by biodegradable polymers such as poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA), which deteriorates with time. An Example of photocrosslinkable polymers that can be used to release drugs on demand is PEGDA. Additionally, magnetic nanoparticles, such as iron oxide nanoparticles (Fe3O4), can be used to provide localized therapy using magnetic fields. When these materials are paired with 4D printing, it is possible to deliver more effective treatments against cancer, enhancing the delivery of drugs and the regeneration of tissue in general [38,43].

4D printing has recently attracted significant attention in cancer treatment, as it uses stimuli-responsive materials to create structures that can change over time in response to environmental cues such as temperature, pH, and moisture. Substances like shape memory polymers and hydrogels aid in the development of personalized therapeutic approaches for various cancers, including breast, bone, and skin cancers [44]. In breast cancer treatment, scholars have created 4D-printed implants that can be resized in order to facilitate a good fit in the breast cavity. The implants also have the capability of delivering chemotherapy drugs, which could minimize the chances of cancer relapse in the region. In the case of bone cancer, reports on the application of 4D-printed scaffolds that utilize polymers with sensitive characteristics have been conducted. These scaffolds can change both shape and stiffness in response to environmental factors, supporting tissue regeneration and improving therapeutic agent delivery to the tumor site. Also, 4D printing can be used to create models of tumors with similar complexities as those of human cancer. The models have more physiologically relevant environments than the use of a traditional two-dimensional model, thus a better experiment on tumor advancement and drug response [45].

At the same time, 4D bioprinting has opened up new potential. Dynamic structures can be built with this technology, where natural changes in shape can take place within the structure using shape-memory polymers and other special materials. These breakthroughs are promising in many contexts, whether in neural regeneration, drug screening, or models of disease, and also have new potential in personalized diagnostics and personalized medicine, especially in brain cancer. Amiri et al. (2023) examined the promising future of 4D printing and its emerging applications, as well as the potential of five-dimensional (5D) and six-dimensional (6D) printing. The superior methods, which involve the use of time and biological processes, have significant potential to enhance our capacity for producing more functional neural structures in neuroscience research and clinical practice [13]. On the whole, the applicability of 4D printing to address the context of cancer results in encouraging directions in personalized therapy, making the process of interventions much more effective and specific to numerous cancer types.

Although 3DP offers considerable returns in its medical applications, whose adherence is strictly implied in well-defined architectures and customized constructs, there are associated limitations that include the unsuitability of structures in terms of their scalability, and their failure to adapt with time or with other varying biological circumstances. 4DP is seen as the solution to these limitations through the use of dynamic buildings or constructs that can adapt as circumstances around the particular magnetic cancer cells change. Due to its suitability, 4DP has an immense potential to enhance the implementation of localized cancer therapy, such as surgery, hyperthermia, photothermal therapy, chemotherapy, and therapeutic devices [7,8,10]. The implementation and capabilities of 4DP should be broadened, which makes current research essential to increase the number of smart materials available. Here, not only is the development of new materials involved, but also their compatibility with existing 4DP technologies and the creation of materials that respond precisely. The ongoing innovative prospects of the smart materials will also propel the possibility of 4DP in producing solutions that are resilient and reactive to multifaceted medical requirements [42]. Types of smart materials

Shape memory alloys

Shape Memory Alloys (SMAs) are metallic alloys that are well-known for their ability to return to their original shape after being deformed. The remarkable feature is driven by two essential attributes: the shape memory effect and super elasticity. Shape memory alloys (SMAs) can regain their initial shape if they are exposed to temperatures surpassing a certain transition temperature, owing to the shape memory effect. Conversely, super elasticity enables these alloys to recover from large deformations without heating, simply by removing the applied stress [46,47]. The functioning of SMAs relies on the reversible transformation between two distinct crystalline phases: austenite and martensite. Each phase exhibits distinct structural characteristics that are critical to the behavior of SMAs:

Austenitic phase

Characterized by a cubic structure, this phase is stable at higher temperatures. During the process of cooling, it undergoes a metamorphosis into martensite, which begins at the martensitic start temperature (Ms) and is fully completed at the martensitic completion temperature (Mf).

Martensitic phase

This phase varies in structure (e.g., tetragonal, orthorhombic, or monoclinic) depending on the alloy composition. It can appear in different orientations, known as variants. When one variant predominates, the martensite is referred to as "detwinned". Conversely, if multiple variants coexist, it is termed "twinned martensite". The transition back to the austenitic phase begins at the austenitic start temperature (As) and completes at the austenitic finish temperature (Af). Applying enough stress to the martensitic phase can initiate a detwinning process, causing a transient distortion of the orientations. The detwinned orientation of the alloys can persist even after the stress is removed, demonstrating their ability to undergo transitory shape change under mechanical force [[48], [49], [50]].

Material considerations and applications

SMAs, such as Nitinol (Nickel-Titanium alloys), copper-based alloys (CuZn, CuAl), and titanium alloys, have significant potential in 4D printing for cancer treatment, particularly in designing devices for controlled drug delivery and localized therapy. Nitinol, the most used SMA, exhibits excellent biocompatibility and undergoes significant shape changes in response to temperature variations. In cancer treatment, Nitinol can be used to create stents or implants that expand at body temperature, enabling targeted drug release directly at tumor sites or opening blocked blood vessels. In the same manner, copper-based alloys such as CuZn and CuAl are under investigation due to their distinctive thermal characteristics, and they are considered in developing implants with properties of deformation as opposed to external changes in temperature, to ensure a drug is released within the tumor vicinity. Mechanically strong and biocompatible titanium-based alloy is used in 4D printed drug release systems/stents that expand in the presence of thermal stimuli and drug release properties after being chemotherapeutically activated. Moreover, polymers can also be used in combination with SMAs to produce composite materials that would combine the drug-reservoir of polymers and the shape-changing characteristics of SMAs. Such a combination enables the development of sophisticated drug delivery designs, as the device's deformation in response to environmental conditions, such as temperature, results in the release of drugs. All in all, these combinations provide promising answers to precision medicine, making cancer treatment more effective by localizing the treatment and also controlling this treatment [[48], [49], [50]].

Shape-memory polymers

Another series of intelligent materials that are of significance to 4DP are known as shape-memory polymers or SMPs. These polymers can deform transiently, from a distorted state into the original form under the influence of some stimuli, such as heat or light, among others [51]. SMPs have a unique property that qualifies them to be used in building dynamic structures that can easily modify their shape or pattern, once predetermined conditions are achieved. This characteristic makes them more practical in a great variety of uses [52,53]. SMPs, however, undergo a metamorphosis when exposed to a given temperature and transition when heat is applied. However, at this temperature, polymer chains have sufficient freedom of movement due to the rise of entropy, and the material can assume a temporary shape. This object has a shape that is preserved because the temperature of the material in which it is made is reduced below the transition temperature. Such a reduction in temperature minimizes disorder and motion in the material, thereby locking the polymer chains and preventing them from shifting shape. Under heating, the material transforms to the prior state in the absence of an external force on the polymer above the transition temperature. The deformation-recovery cycle can be repeated many times, allowing the material to be given different shapes temporarily [41,54].

Thermo-responsive polymers have been widely researched in the biomedical field as possible components of the next generation of surgical technologies that will be minimally invasive. Specifically, polylactic acid (PLA) has demonstrated potential in 4DP applications that produce vascular stents as well as bone tissue scaffolds. The devices use SMPs, which enable them to adapt to physiological changes, like heat within the body, which may increase the precision and success of operations like those of tracheal repairs. One such example is a scaffold manufactured with the use of a concept known as SMP that could adapt its structure to more closely resemble the position of the trachea upon exposure to the heat of the body. This enhances the outcome of the tracheal repair surgeries [55]. SMPs can be used in ways that are exclusive to each other; however, using these materials in 4DP has some risks. The main issues would be the intrinsic drawbacks of the polymers, including the lack of strength and stiffness, which will limit their real-life applications in areas where high structural durability is required [56].

The SMPs are an interesting type of material that finds application in 4D printing to treat cancer, especially when it comes to targeted drug delivery and other tumor-specific implants. With such characteristics as biocompatibility and responsiveness to temperature changes, polyurethane (PU), one of the most popular SMPs, is particularly sensitive to temperature changes, and, therefore, it is suitable for the fabrication of temperature-sensitive drug delivery devices. As an example, PU-based SMPs have been utilized to develop drug-loaded hydrogels, which undergo enlargement or shrinkage in response to changes in local temperature and drive the release of anticancer drugs at the site of the cancerous tumor [57]. Another SMP biodegradable type is polycaprolactone (PCL), which has been introduced in 3D-printed drug delivery systems and implants. SMPs based on PCL can be programmed to switch shape in response to temperature, releasing chemotherapy drugs like paclitaxel in a controlled, direct manner to cancerous tissue. Likewise, it has been indicated that polylactic acid (PLA) is a biodegradable thermoplastic polymer that has been investigated for use in drug-eluting implants or scaffolds. Temperature changes can be used to activate PLA-based SMPs to deliver their drugs in a localized area and thus minimize the side effects because the drug delivery will be localized to the cancerous tissues [58]. A thermos-responsive polymer like Poly(N-isopropylacrylamide) (PNIPAM) has a phase transition at a definite temperature and hence the best choice as a temperature-responsive drug delivery. PNIPAM-based SMPs are studied as a means of developing a thermoresponsive hydrogel able to release drugs upon entering the high tumor temperatures [59]. Finally, a common biocompatible and biodegradable copolymer, PLGA, is used with SMPs to incorporate pH or temperature-sensitive drugs into the SMPs. SMPs based on PLGA can act by responding to the acidic niche of the tumor to deliver dose agents directly to the tumor, e.g., doxorubicin or paclitaxel [60]. Using 4D printing, such materials can result in ultra-localized and controlled drug delivery, providing an improvement to the efficacy of cancer treatments with reduced systemic side-effects.

Hydrogels

An excellent example of intelligent materials, which can be used in 4D printing, is hydroGels They are highly absorbent polymer networks that can potentially change size in response to changes in environmental conditions, such as temperature or pH. These materials possess special properties that make them highly suitable for most biomedical applications, particularly in tissue engineering and drug delivery systems. One such system is a hydrogel-based system designed to employ 4DP methods of creation and can allow controlled drug release when under certain temperature conditions. This allows their medication delivery to be specific and precise [19,61]. Hydrogels borrow the properties of 3DP in fabricating structures that can either unfold or fold automatically when exposed to external stimuli, like water or other liquid stimuli. Such organisms have the ability to respond to a fluid in an adaptive manner, which makes them highly adaptable in an application like the accurate delivery of medication. The study demonstrated the efficacy of micro-robots coated with a hydrogel layer to target malignant tumors via pH-responsive treatment. It indicated that hydrogels provide an effective tool to interact directly with the tumor microenvironment and facilitate controlled release of drugs [[62], [63], [64]].

Hydrogels can react to a variety of environmental stimulating factors, including alterations in ionic settings, strain, pH, electric and magnetic fields, heat, or light. Their special properties, such as the controlled sol-gel transition, their self-healing characteristics, and memory retention, enhance their performance when used in smart material applications [65]. These characteristics provide interactive capabilities needed in multifaceted medical procedures and practices. New technology has resulted in the formulation of hydrogels analogous to the poly(N-isopropylacrylamide) (PNIPAM) based capsule shell created by Zu et al. This new material offers temperature sensitivity. It is a hydrogel that can change its internal pore size based on the change in temperature around the lower critical solution temperature (LCST), so that the release of the drug can be controlled by the patient-specific demands [66]. However, despite their diverse applications, hydrogels face challenges related to mechanical stability, strength, and manipulation. Crosslinking of hydrogels with polymers is frequently used to stabilize their structures in order to reduce the shortcomings. In 4DP applications, the printed object can encompass responsive (active) and nonresponsive (passive) physical elements.

The 4D printed hydrogels are emerging in cancer treatment since they can respond to external factors, i.e., to changes in temperature, pH level, and light, which can provide localized and controlled drug delivery. One of the most common hydrogel types used to carry out drug delivery procedures is poly(ethylene glycol) (PEG) due to its ease of adaptation and biocompatibility. Since tumors have an acidic environment, these hydrogels can be made to be released only when the drug reaches areas of the body with high acidity due to cancerous cells [67]. Hydrogels formed by poly(N-isopropylacrylamide) (PNIPAAm) are thermo-responsive materials that change phase at body temperature, making possible the on-demand release of drugs, especially hyperthermia-based therapies [68].

Hydrogels or PLGA are biodegradable and thus can be used in sustained drug delivery at temperatures that provide long-term delivery of chemotherapy agents to tumors [69]. The naturally sourced polymer chitosan produces hydrogels that are pH sensitive to the tumor microenvironment and can be functionalized to target specific areas to deliver drugs with higher accuracy [70]. Likewise, alginate hydrogels are based on a naturally occurring polysaccharide, which can trap drugs and deliver them in a regulated manner upon a change in the environment [71]. The drug delivery system proposed by natural analogs, such as the HA hydrogels, has limited penetration into healthy tissues unless the conditions are pH and temperature-specific, in cases such as cancerous cells that have a heightened expression of the HA receptors, delivering the drugs to the site of the cancerous cells [72]. With their special characteristics of stimulus-responsiveness and biocompatibility, these hydrogels have immense prospects of coming up with advanced cancer cure strategies that are both effective and minimally invasive. Typically, the active hydrogel layers absorb water and swell, while the passive polymer layers provide structural support. This layered configuration facilitates the creation of devices that self-fold, although the response time and deformation precision may vary, requiring careful design and execution [41,73].

Liquid crystal elastomers

Liquid Crystal Elastomers (LCEs) are new materials that combine the properties of elastomers and liquid crystals. LCEs are made up of materials that can dynamically react to different environmental factors, such as light, heat, and electric fields, due to their composition. High degree of fast and effective adaptation to changes. Even in 4DP applications, response to changes should be rapid and efficient, which is why the materials need the ability to adjust and change themselves even after production [74]. A significant feature of LCEs is that during the orientation process, the long axis of the liquid crystal molecules is aligned along the elongated axis of the liquid crystal in the elastomer, and this alignment of neighboring molecules is referred to as the director axis. With the help of the deformation of this director axis, scientists can set the direction and properties of deformation of the material. The anisotropic properties of LCEs enable the fabrication of devices that can engage in complex shape changes such as bending, twisting, and folding. This would make them fit in many applications [75]. LCEs can be designed to produce adaptive implants capable of matching the tissue around them as they grow, and this may limit post-implantation rejection and increase the integration within the host tissue. For instance, LCE-based stents may have the ability to expand relative to body temperature, and hence offer dynamic support to blood vessels, any physiological modification enabling additional surgical interventions, all to no effect [61]. In the realm of biomedical devices, LCEs can function as actuators in minimally invasive surgical tools, which can alter shape to navigate through complex anatomical passages. Additionally, these actuators can precisely control the site-specific release of medications within the body [76].

The first LCEs were created in the 1980s by innovative polymer chemists, primarily Finkelmann, but also Shibaev and Talroze, Zentel, Keller, Mitchell, and Davies. In all early studies, the LCEs were formed by cross-linking the side-chain liquid-crystalline polymers, which combine rigid-rod mesogenic molecular groups with the backbone polymer chain in the configuration. In general, the backbone polymer chain was either polysiloxane, attaching the side groups via the hydrosilylation reaction, or polyacrylate formed by homopolymerization of acrylate-terminated mesogenic molecules [77]. LCEs are innovative materials for 4D printing in cancer treatment, offering shape transformations in response to external stimuli like temperature, light, and electric fields. Polymeric LCEs, such as polyacrylate (polyacrylate-based LCE), allow for controlled drug release through their stable network [78]. LCEs have potential applications in the development of artificial muscles, mimicking the contraction and expansion characteristics of natural muscle fibers. These are particularly useful in prosthetics, where they can provide more natural and efficient movements [79]. LCEs are also being explored for use in smart wound dressings that can actively respond to changes in the wound environment, such as variations in temperature or pH levels. These dressings can change shape or release therapeutic agents accordingly, fostering a more effective healing process [80].

Fabrication methods for 4D printing

Various manufacturing methods have been adapted for 4DP, each offering specific advantages and limitations depending on the materials used and the intended use.

Fused deposition modelling

3DP technique Fused Deposition Modeling (FDM) is widely employed in 4DP for biological applications. This method uses a piston or screw-driven extrusion mechanism to correctly distribute materials. FDM uses shape-memory polymers, which change form in reaction to heat, to build structures efficiently [81]. Personalizing bone implant lattice architectures using FDM technology is a unique approach. These implants may be tailored to a patient's anatomy via FDM. Customization improves osseointegration, surgical results, and patient comfort, solving some of the biggest orthopedic surgery concerns [82]. For accurate and ordered plastic filament deployment, FDM printers must operate at high temperatures. Magnetic forces, electric fields, and light may also change the printed materials. However, printing many materials at once is difficult with FDM. Interface adhesion may be affected by thermoplastic printing temperatures, compromising product structural integrity and operational effectiveness [35,83]. FDM is highly efficient for printing with shape-memory polymers but faces challenges such as poor interlayer adhesion when different thermoplastics with varying printing temperatures are used. This can affect the structural integrity of the final product, particularly when multiple materials need to be printed concurrently. Additionally, FDM requires high printing temperatures, which limits the types of materials that can be used in biological applications, especially when the material is sensitive to heat [23].

Stereolithography

Stereolithography (SLA) is an established printing method that uses a laser beam at different frequencies to chemically bond resins and liquid polymers, creating structures that are either gel-like or solid. This procedure has notable benefits compared to extrusion-based printing methods, such as superior surface quality and a high level of printing precision, which are essential for intricate and accurate fabrications [84,85]. Laser curing during SLA is a dynamic and complex procedure vulnerable to numerous factors, among them being laser source, curing depth, polymerization contraction, and curing rate. These aspects play decisive roles in establishing the quality and performance of the final printed products [86]. Through the use of smart materials, SLA can expand its applications to common uses by incorporating these materials into the resin. Scientists have successfully used SLA to create photo-curable resins and photo-cross-linkable hydrogels that can form after being printed. These materials are highly beneficial in the field of biomedical applications. SLA has been utilized to generate hydrogels that exhibit swelling or shrinking behavior in response to pH variations. The versatility and potential of this method in the field of medicine are demonstrated by the use of hydrogels in drug delivery systems to offer precise and controlled medication release [87,88].

Complex 3D functional tissues may be created utilizing a 3D bioprinter, biocompatible materials, and live cells for drug screening, wound healing, transplantation, bio-actuators, bio-robotics, and biosensors. For stationary constructions that cannot alter form, standard 3D printing is limited. To solve this, 4D printing technology adds time to 3D printing. Bio-printed structures may adjust to temperature, pH, light, moisture, electrical, and magnetic fields. 4D printing employs materials designed to change characteristics when stimulated. 4D printing uses extrusion-based processes and vat polymerization, like SLA and two-photon polymerization (2PP). Due to its high-resolution structures and fine surface finishes, SLA is perfect for 4D printing in biological applications that need accuracy. SLA's capacity to generate complex, dynamic structures that change over time makes it helpful in developing responsive medical devices. 2PP is utilized in 4D printing to build micro-swimmers, micro-actuators, and volumetric stereolithography in seconds, although SLA's high-resolution is better for fine details and exact shapeshifting. Despite its potential, 4D printing faces challenges in material development, design limits, and production efficiency. It has great promise for developing effective and customized medical gadgets that can adjust to changing situations in real time [89]. SLA offers high resolution and surface quality, but its limitations include the restricted material choices, as it primarily uses photo-curable resins. The laser curing process is highly sensitive to factors such as laser energy and curing depth, leading to inconsistencies in the final product, which could affect mechanical properties and print quality. Furthermore, SLA is slower than other 3D printing techniques and struggles with residual stresses in the printed structures, which can affect the durability of the parts in real-world applications [90].

Digital light processing

DLP printing uses a digital light projector to solidify resin instead of a laser, like Stereolithography. DLP can print more quickly and accurately because it can distinguish between different objects. This is useful for creating sophisticated 4D structures in biomedical engineering [91]. Digital light pattern curing accelerates the curing of resin layers in the reservoir, compared to layer-by-layer laser curing in SLA. Surface-projection-based manufacturing speeds printing up compared to SLA. DLP printers use cost-effective projectors, making them a practical choice for many applications [92]. DLP printing requires a projector and a Z-direction motor. The interaction of these components allows correct resin cross-linking, which is essential for mechanical properties and product customization. The level of cross-linking affects internal tensions, swelling ratios, and printed object mechanical properties. These difficulties resemble SLA printing [[93], [94], [95]]. DLP (Digital Light Processing) has helped scientists create bioresorbable devices that react to near-infrared light, are photothermal, and have shape-memory functionalities. The gadgets use 4DP technology. Drug distribution systems and less invasive surgery may benefit from DLP-printed devices' advanced characteristics. Customizable, dynamic, and efficient solutions are crucial for these applications [96]. DLP printing offers higher speed compared to SLA and provides better resolution and accuracy. However, it faces challenges in material compatibility, as it predominantly uses photo-curable resins. The degree of cross-linking during printing must be carefully controlled to avoid inconsistent material properties in the final print, leading to potential issues with mechanical strength and swelling ratios. Moreover, DLP systems can be costly, and the layer thickness and resolution are often constrained by the projector's capabilities [97].

Selective laser sintering

Scientists are progressively integrating shape-memory polymers and other intelligent materials into the powder utilized for Selective Laser Sintering (SLS) 4DP. This innovation enables the creation of structures that can change their shape in response to specific stimuli, such as heat. One of the high potentials involves the creation of shape-memory thermoplastic polyamide elastomers using SLS 4DP. These materials can resist large deformations and still assume a temporary form before being triggered by an external stimulus [6]. Studies have succeeded in developing complex printed objects using shape-memory by simply controlling the printing parameters and the temperature regimes of heating and cooling. Biological applications offer a vast potential to this feature because flexibility and accuracy are of paramount concern here. One of the most promising applications of these resources, developed by 4DP, is in the field of tissue engineering. When the 4DP technology is involved, scientists have developed various scaffolds that can be injected into the body in one form and then transformed into the actual form through biological stimuli or body heat [98]. The scaffolds can adapt to a particular anatomical position within the body, which contributes to the regeneration of tissues and enhances contact with the surrounding living environment. SLS offers high flexibility, allowing for the use of various thermoplastic materials in the process. However, its drawbacks include the quality of the powder bed and the potential for significant material waste during printing. SLS bonding between layers is potentially unworkable, especially with dense bodies, and a constant temperature control is a prerequisite, notably when using shape-memory plastics [99]. Moreover, SLS is slower compared to other methods and requires special equipment, which may add cost.

Multi-Material jetting

It is a complex printing technique known as multi-material jetting which allows depositing large numbers of materials simultaneously during the whole printing process. This attribute enables the creation of structures with a range of mechanical characteristics or stimulus sensitivity, which are used in biological applications if required. Additionally, its ability to print multi-functional objects that are difficult to fabricate is a valuable feature [100]. Researchers who have used this technique have managed to produce advanced biomedical devices. The potential of these gadgets is high in applications such as lab-on-a-chip systems and microfluidic computing devices that are invaluable in contemporary biomedical studies and diagnostic purposes [101]. Advanced procedures such as multi-material jetting enable other multi-material printing, but present issues with material compatibility. Material incompatibility may have an impact on printed structure adhesion and integrity, and fail in real-world applications. Moreover, the equipment price and the complexity of the process itself may be the factors that disallow its extensive use [102].

Direct ink writing

Direct Ink Writing (DIW) is another printing technology, which prints complicated shapes using thick inks that can be fibers or particles. When it comes to biomedical 4DP, DIW can be applied especially in producing structures with bio-inks comprising living cells. This allows the fabrication of the functional tissue structures required to perform tissue engineering and regenerative medicine [103,104]. With DIW-based 4DP, scientists have managed to develop intelligent hydrogel scaffolds. These scaffolds can alter their structure in a controlled way on exposure to some environmental trigger. This characteristic is beneficial when it comes to the design of tailor-made implants concerning patients. This technology not only offers solutions benefiting patients by increasing the reliability of fit and integration with the patient's body, but also drives forward areas such as minimally invasive surgery and drug administration by providing a more precise and custom treatment application [105]. DIW offers adjustable control and creativity for bio-inks, but it lacks effective extrusion control for highly viscous bio-inks. It is challenging to incorporate intricate structures or maintain their degree of fidelity to print, particularly when working with living cells or hydroGels Additionally, the mechanical properties of the printed scaffold material may not be applicable in specific biomedical applications, and bio-ink-based printing might give rise to cell viability-related problems [106].

Biofabrication

The integration of living cells and macromolecules into 3D and 4D printed objects has advanced significantly thanks to advances in biofabrication techniques like bioprinting and cell-laden hydrogel printing. The progress shown here is critical to fields such as tissue engineering and regenerative medicine, where the interaction of printed materials with living cells is critical to the successful conclusion [107,108]. The progress in biofabrication has enabled the development of tissue constructions that are more biomimetic and functional. Through enhancing the integration of these structures within the body, they facilitate the healing process and provide more efficient regeneration results, representing a notable advancement in medical treatments and uses [109]. Biofabrication of cell-laden hydrogels enables more biomimetic and functional structures, but challenges include cell viability and uniform distribution within printed materials. Moreover, the integration of living cells into 3D-printed constructs requires careful management of growth factors, nutrient diffusion, and long-term stability in a biological environment. Additionally, the scalability of these systems for large-scale production remains a significant barrier [110].

Enhancing 4D printing with AI and machine learning

The utilization of machine learning and AI-driven design is becoming increasingly crucial in the progress and enhancement of fabrication processes, specifically in the domain of 4DP. These high-tech tools are critical to outlining how the materials and structures in 4DP will behave. AI and machine learning have successfully achieved the proper functionality by suggesting the appropriate material, geometries, and process parameters [111]. The use of machine learning and artificial intelligence during the design process would allow exploring as many design options within the framework of exploration in order to find the most efficient solutions more easily and predict the properties and effectiveness of materials. The implementation of this technology complements the level of production of the processes with the quality of the resultant 4D-printed structures [112]. The ongoing technological advancements in artificial intelligence and machine learning are expected to support the work of 4DP technology, thereby integrating it into industrial processes. This will make it easy to develop more advanced, efficient, and multi-purpose materials and structures which can be deployed in many industries and uses [113]. The combination of AI and machine learning with 4D printing processes enables the optimization of printing material design and functionality. Nevertheless, these technologies demand extensive data and computing resources to forecast behavior and material properties reasonably. Moreover, AI models cannot always take into consideration the unpredictable factors of the actual printing conditions, including inconsistencies of materials, environmental issues, and the constraints of the system [114].

Mechanisms of 4D printing approaches

Current 4DP methods allow printed structures to respond to configure upon any number of physical and chemical stimuli, including light, temperature, mechanical load, moisture concentration, pH, magnetic and electric fields, among other biological triggers. The mechanisms are: shape memory, the magnetic driving effect, the photothermal effect, and swelling and shrinking under controlled conditions. Such measures can enable the development of accurately engineered designs, as shown in Fig. 3 [7,37].

Mechanisms of 4D printing approaches.

Shape memory effect

When materials are subjected to different stimuli, such as heat, light, humidity, pH, electricity, or magnetic fields, they can maintain and regain their original shape or experience reversible changes in shape thanks to the shape memory effect (SME) [115,116]. This effect is also helpful in lowering the size of biomedical equipment, such as implants or scaffolds, which lowers the risk of patient injuries and incisions. SME is seen in a variety of materials that go through phase changes to acquire these characteristics, such as alloys, polymers, hydrogels, and composites. A noteworthy study showed how shape memory polyurethane (PU) could be used to create bifurcated stents that could pass through a simulated conduit with branching sections, demonstrating the usefulness of SME in medical devices [95,117].

Magnetic driving effects

Magnetic driving effects harness magnetic energy and fields to activate smart materials, rendering magneto-responsive materials appropriate for fabricating 4D structures. These materials can change shape when subjected to magnetic energy, allowing them to be controlled from a distance. This property is particularly helpful for manipulating tiny objects and in biological applications such as medication administration and fastening mechanisms. Magnetic fields are also employed for non-invasive treatments, providing deep tissue penetration and localized heat sources, which are crucial for therapies like targeted drug delivery and dynamic construct design [16,[118], [119], [120], [121], [122], [123]].

Photothermal effects

Photothermal phenomena can be achieved by using photothermal processes in which radiation is transferred to heat energy. In 4D printed architectures, temperature-sensitive components are exposed to high temperatures and undergo structural changes. Such temperature-responsive materials are most commonly based on polymers that include azobenzene groups and are found in pharmaceutical drug delivery systems, for optoelectronic behavior, and also in sensors. These polymers exhibit reversible trans-cis isomerization when exposed to light, and the heat generated by these photothermal effects can be directly utilized in various medical applications, such as photothermal treatment for destroying cancerous cells. This capability allows for the tailoring of activation and control over dynamic constructs [37,124,125].

Swelling and shrinking effects

Differential expansion and contraction that is widely noticed in 4DP can be attributed to the use of water as a mediating agent in the interactions giving rise to marked morphological changes in hydrophilic polymers like polyvinyl alcohol (PVA), hydrogels, and polyethylene glycol (PEG). The phenomena form the basis of manufacturing stimulus-sensitive devices such as the actuators and sensors. Additionally, biomedical delivery systems have employed controlled volumetric change, in which the speed of drug release is controlled by swelling and shrinking, which can be controlled. A particular example, the methacrylated alginate hydrogel, would exemplify the shape modulation using sequential chemical treatments, in order to promote the development of dynamically responsive devices that can respond to the environmental variables [24,[126], [127], [128]].

Shape memory effect

Materials can revert into their original form from any given different kind of shape under a range of stimuli, including high temperature, humidity, light, pH, electricity, and magnetic fields. This phenomenon is known as one-way change, but materials can also undergo reversible shape changes, which can be called two-way or multiple-way variations. Any scaffolds, implants, or biomedical devices that are inserted into the body might have their volume decreased thanks to the Shape Memory Effect of 4DP technology. It also can minimize the severity of patient injuries and the number of incisions that are made [49]. The shape memory effect encompasses several types, including SMA, SMP, and shape memory composites (SMC), which are materials that, when exposed to specific stimuli, such as frequent heat, can distort and then remember and reappear in their original shape [115].

Shape memory alloys

The shape memory effect can be attained in alloys through different phases, such as the martensite phase, where alloys can be deformed at a specific temperature. Additionally, the transition phase involves twinned martensite and detwinned martensite, with twinned martensite being the internal structure arranged symmetrically. Detwinned martensite is the deformation of materials reappears as a response [155]. Some examples of shape memory alloys are Cu-Sn, Cu-Zn-Al, Au-Cd, Ni-Al, Fe-Mn-Si, Ti-Pd, Ni-Ti-Cu, nitinol, and titanium alloy [54]. The research conducted using special needle along with flexible Shape memory alloys inner needles to change the shape of the it, as SMAs can return for their original shape, within a single puncture the drug can be injected into multiple spots. It can reach to its precise areas targeting and effective delivery of therapeutic agents, where the cancer treatments efficacy will be enhanced without much damaging to the healthy tissue [156].

Shape memory polymers

SMPs are intelligent materials that undergo temporary shape modification and then revert to their original form when activated by various stimuli, including temperature, moisture, pH, light, electricity, alternating magnetic fields, radiation heating, laser heating, microwaves, pressure, solvents, or solvent vapors. Their shape memory effect is achieved through specific compositions and programming conditions. The SMPs are classified into the thermal-induced, electric-induced, and solvent-induced systems with a capability to shape progress under distinctive stimuli [157]. SMPs have many uses, including their use as contrast agents in magnetic resonance imaging and hyperthermia techniques for cancer therapy. They are also utilized in general medicine, nanomedicine, dentistry, and orthopedics. Some examples of SMPs include poly(vinylidenefluoride), polylactide, poly(ethylene glycol), polyethylene, poly(vinyl/vinylidene) chloride, and their copolymers. Additionally, amorphous polymers such as poly(vinyl acetate), poly methyl acrylate, poly ethyl acrylate, atactic/isotactic/syndiotactic poly methyl methacrylate, and other polyacrylates are also considered SMPs. These polymers can be either crystalline or present in blends of amorphous and semicrystalline polymers. It has been shown that polymeric components can create biomaterials with several functions, suitable for application in medical devices such as orthopedic devices, vascular stents, and orthodontic wires [158].

Shape memory composites

SMCs came into existence to conquer the limitations of SMPs. These are materials that combine SMPs with other strengthening materials, such as fibers or particles, to enhance their properties. Thermogravimetric analysis, differential scanning calorimetry, and Fourier infrared spectroscopy were used to characterize the composite's characteristics. The multifaceted 3D structure of the composite was arranged by a fused deposition modeling (FDM) printer [159]. Magnetically triggered SMC has many evident benefits. Magnetic fields are widely used in the biomedical field due to their helpful nature, and medical professionals utilize this technology to make instantaneous mechanical modifications to a patient's body. The advantage of magnetically initiated SMC is that the shape memory effect can be remotely activated. Another potential application for magnetically activated SMC is remote cancer medication delivery [160].

Shape memory balloon

Shape-Memory Balloon (SMB) is a medical device designed to assist in treating bone diseases, including bone cement injections and localized drug delivery, particularly for bone tumors. SMB is composed of decomposable poly (ε-caprolactone), anticancer drugs such as doxorubicin, and heat-producing magnetic nanoparticles (MNPs). With the use of magnetic nanoparticles (MNPs) and alternating magnetic field (AMF), the method will be generated by heat locally to achieve effective hyperthermic temperatures fast and controllably, encouraging postoperative thermo/chemotherapy. Although this minimally invasive technique ensures good thermal accumulation at the tumor site, further research and expansion are necessary to optimize and monitor the synergistic thermo/chemo effects for improved bone tumor treatment [161].

Stimuli-responsive hydrogels

Numerous hydrogel-based preparations have been established over a period for the diagnosis, management, and inhibition of cancer; specific formulations have even been involved in clinical studies. Certain hydrogels may undergo sol-to-gel transitions in response to external stimuli, such as temperature, pH, light, ionic force, or pressure, enabling regulated gelation and drug release at specific/targeted sites. Hydrogels that respond to stimuli provide a plethora of new possibilities for precisely targeting the tumor site. By utilizing the unique physiological features of the tumor microenvironment, including differences in external and intracellular pH levels, drug release at the tumor site can be regulated. Moreover, the speed and location of drug release may be regulated by the utilization of light, magnetic fields, or alterations in ionic strength [157].

Magnetic responsive materials

Since magnetic responsive materials have potential applications in the biomedical, coatings, microfluidics, and microelectronics sectors, they are now the focus of a great deal of study. It also has great potential in the treatment of cancer. Predominantly, the magnetic-based nanomaterials consist of a variety of features, including a large surface area and particular optical qualities. Since they have great surface-area-to-volume ratios along with large magnetic moments, they are appealing for targeted medication administration and cancer hyperthermia therapy, which facilitates the establishment of innovative tests for the management of cancer [162].

Photothermal therapy

Photothermal treatment (PTT) is a method where heat energy is produced from light radiation. This technique can assist in producing a cytotoxic effect that damages cells or results in the demise of cancerous cells. Photothermal materials such as organic dyes or nanoparticles are used in this approach. To enhance the effectiveness of treatment, PTT can be used in combination with other therapeutic techniques, such as immunotherapy, radiation therapy, chemodynamic therapy, photodynamic therapy, or gene therapy [163]. A study was conducted on PTT using a near-infrared (NIR) absorbent, ICG lactosome, which revealed that during NIR irradiation, cancers were treated when their temperature reached or exceeded 43 °C. NIR absorbents are used to facilitate effective heat generation, and PTT destroys malignant cells using a photo-absorbent in the near-infrared (NIR) band. In comparison to reaching a temperature above 43 °C for treating the cancer cells, it was discovered that the fluence rate and irradiation duration were less important. This level of temperature threshold is essential for effectively treating the irradiated tumors. Accordingly, to achieve total cancer eradication, using an NIR absorber to monitor thermal dosage during PTT is a very reliable way [164].

Biologically driven effects

Currently, the growth of cancer medication is encouraged by biological attention based on the remarkable advancement accomplished in understanding the fundamental biology of illness. Natural chemicals produced by the body or in a lab are used in biological cancer therapy, which aims either directly at cancer cells or supports the immune system's ability to fight cancer. Vaccines against cancer, oncolytic viruses, immune conjugates, gene therapy, adoptive cell transfer, monoclonal antibodies, and cytokine treatment are particular examples of it [165].

Challenges in 4D printing materials

While 4DP holds significant promise and potential, it also involves difficulties and obstacles that must be resolved before the technology can be widely used and implemented. The issues related to 4DP technology encompass a range of aspects, including materials, biocompatibility, degradation rate, fabrication complexities, Resolution and Accuracy, Scalability, Precision and Control, environmental impact, and Regulatory and Ethical Considerations, such as Regulatory Compliance and Ethical Compliance, as well as education and workforce [170]. The performance of 4D-printed materials is profoundly reliant on the materials used, especially for biomedical devices and structures. For smart 4D materials, such as hydrogels, shape-memory polymers, and stimuli-responsive materials, to fulfill their intended purposes, they must exhibit the expected behavior and response to external stimuli. It might not be easy to locate materials with the right qualities, such as strength, flexibility, and durability. Moreover, it is sometimes challenging to integrate many smart materials into a single 4D-printed structure, which limits the range of functionality that may be achieved. Biocompatibility is another important factor to consider since it guarantees patient safety by preventing the use of materials that are poisonous or immunogenic. Developing biocompatible smart materials that show the desired shape-changing behavior while meeting strict standards is a continuous struggle. The deterioration rate of 4D-printed biomedical equipment is significant in applications such as drug delivery systems, tissue engineering, and implantable devices.

The 4DP technique is further complex than the 3DP design. Despite its promising advantages, 4DP technology faces several limitations that hinder its widespread clinical application in cancer treatment. One of the most significant challenges is material stability. While 4DP materials can respond to external stimuli, ensuring their long-term stability and biocompatibility remains difficult. Complication of fabrication is another barrier. Control accuracy on the environmental level is also part of it. In cases where 4DP is used as, say, a drug delivery method, frequent requirements include careful control of outward stimuli that might be temperature, light, or pH. Moreover, there is regulatory control that acts as an obstacle. Since these technologies involve the implementation of new materials and devices in the human healthcare field, obtaining approval can be quite exhaustive. This is because the level of safety and efficiency of the 4D-printed materials used in such cases has to be proven extensively, which leads to a postponement of their availability to the clinic. Even though the financial implications of a 4DP are increasingly dropping, it still presents a challenge to the broad adoption in treating cancer patients. The high costs of specific machines, intellectual property materials, and problematic access to skilled practitioners limit access in low-resource settings and limit regular clinical practice [178]. In sum, the promise of 4DP in cancer therapy, centered on personalized treatments, target drug delivery, and tissue regeneration methods, is offset by the ongoing issues of material stability, a high degree of complexity, the control of precision, and regulatory leniency. These matters must be resolved to fully incorporate 4D printing into clinical oncology. Regulatory considerations

The application of 3DP technology is growing in the medical and healthcare sectors. An American business called Oxford Performance Materials created this revolutionary implant [190]. Another notable accomplishment is the development of the first 3D-printed titanium-based spinal device, the CASCADIA Lateral Interbody System. This device was created by K2M Group Holdings, which was later acquired by Stryker in 2018. The FDA approved it in January 2016 and was CE marked. Many 3DP businesses have so far been granted FDA 510(k) approval for their 3D printed medical equipment, according to the FDA's published data [191]. There is evidence that 3D-printed medical devices can be effectively inserted into the human body, as later studies have shown. In the realm of 3D bioprinting, there is currently a lack of uniformity, especially about the tools, materials, and procedures utilized. Conclusions

The use of 4DP technology in cancer therapy represents a notable progression from conventional 3DP, providing dynamic and adaptable capabilities that have the potential to transform the profession. 4DP allows for the fabrication of structures that may dynamically alter their shape, function, or qualities over time by utilizing smart materials that respond to different stimuli. Personalized medicine is especially beneficial in tailoring treatments to meet the specific needs of individual patients, hence improving the precision and effectiveness of cancer therapy. The extensive manufacturing techniques developed in 4DP have diversified the potential uses of 4DP throughout the biomedical field, which includes Fused Deposition Modeling (FDM), Stereolithography (SLA), Digital Light Processing (DLP), and Selective Laser Sintering (SLS). Such approaches make it possible to create flexible implants, adaptive drug delivery devices, and self-assembling medical devices, which deliver unprecedented potential in the field of cancer treatment. Application of artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) to processes in 4DP leads to a much more intelligent choice of material as well as optimization of designs, increasing the quality and utility of printed structures. Despite its promising future, 4DP as an intervention in cancer treatment remains in its infancy, necessitating further development and research. Some of the major threats include the development of materials, regulatory complexities, and fabrication challenges. These and other challenges need to be confronted with further development of smart materials and their interactions with biological surroundings to further develop this technology. Research will continue to advance in 4DP, likely to become a foundation of the next generation of disease management approaches, including cancer treatment, efficient and personalized adaptive treatments. The shift from 3D to 4DP is a revolutionary change in cancer treatment, which can resolve the 3D inability to manipulate characterized structures. This technology promises to deliver ground-breaking advancements in patient care and treatment outcomes, making it a crucial area for ongoing interdisciplinary collaboration and innovation.
