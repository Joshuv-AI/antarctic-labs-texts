Mapping caregivers’ distress: a network analysis of burden, meaning in life, and mental health in families facing suicidal behavior
Authors: Sandra Pérez-Rodríguez, Ginnette Muñoz Rocha, Joaquín García-Alandete, Verónica Guillén Botella, Jose H. Marco
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSuicide profoundly impacts not only individuals but also their relatives, who often experience high levels of burden, distress, and isolation. Yet the interplay between risk and protective factors in this population remains understudied. This study explored the network structure of caregivers’ emotional states, focusing on meaning in life, perceived burden, family empowerment, emotion dysregulation, anxiety, stress, depressive symptoms, and quality of life.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted with 185 Spanish relatives (139 women, 75.1%, and 46 men, 24.9%) age-ranged between 18 and 73 years old (M = 50.26, SD = 10.23) of individuals with suicide attempts or suicidal behavior disorder in the past two years. Networks were estimated with EBICglasso after redundant node analysis.ResultsThe network included 11 nodes and 30 edges. Depressive symptoms emerged as the most central node, followed by stress and anxiety, indicating that emotional distress is a central component of caregivers’ functioning. Emotion dysregulation bridged distress with subjective burden. In contrast, meaning in life (especially its experiential dimension of Meaning) and quality of life appeared as protective but peripheral. Family empowerment also occupied a peripheral role, while objective and subjective burden were closely interconnected but less structurally influential.ConclusionFindings highlight the central position of depressive symptoms within caregivers’ emotional networks, suggesting that future research should further explore how distress and protective resources such as meaning in life and quality of life interact in shaping caregivers’ well-being.

Introduction

Burden in caregivers of people at suicide risk

Currently, suicide and related behaviors represent one of the main public health problems, being one of the leading causes of death worldwide and having a significant impact on social, family, community, and healthcare networks [1]. According to the World Health Organization, around 720,000 people die by suicide each year, representing more than 1.4% of global deaths [2]. Suicide rates have increased over the past 45 years, making it a major contributor to premature mortality worldwide [3].

Suicide has a profound impact not only on the individuals directly involved but also on their families. Relatives who have lost a loved one to suicide frequently experience complicated grief, depression, hopelessness, and intrusive memories, which can place them at heightened risk of severe psychological distress and even suicidal behavior themselves [4, 5]. Similarly, family members who live with or care for individuals experiencing suicidal ideation or attempts often endure persistent emotional distress, fear, guilt, and uncertainty, describing their situation as a constant struggle between hope and despair while lacking sufficient information, skills, or professional guidance to cope effectively [6].

Research consistently highlights the heavy caregiving burden carried by families living with a person at risk of suicide. Relatives frequently report feelings of helplessness, guilt, and overwhelming responsibility, often compounded by the secrecy and shame with which the suicidal person conceals their ideation [7]. This emotional strain is further exacerbated in emergency settings, where family members report feeling poorly informed, stigmatized, and insufficiently supported when accompanying their relative after a suicide attempt [8]. The caregiving experience is frequently described as emotionally exhausting, marked by grief and the need to be extremely cautious in interactions with the suicidal family member, which in turn restricts relatives’ capacity to express their own concerns or seek support, leaving them isolated and underserved [7, 9–11]. Beyond these burdens, self-harm in children is particularly traumatic for parents, who often report feelings of helplessness and fear about their ability to cope, especially when their child is discharged from hospital and the risk of recurrence remains salient [12].

The stigma surrounding suicidal behaviour further intensifies this emotional toll. For individuals with suicidal ideation, stigma is linked to feelings of being a burden, loneliness, and misunderstanding, all of which may prevent them from disclosing their struggles to relatives or professionals [13]. Families, in turn, describe suicidal crises as traumatic experiences that trigger guilt, powerlessness, fear of possible loss, and anxiety about recurrence [7, 9]. Recent qualitative studies reinforce these findings, showing that carers frequently report profound anxiety, uncertainty about how to provide adequate support, and difficulties accessing timely professional help (underscoring the urgent need for interventions tailored to their specific needs) [14]. Likewise, systematic evidence emphasizes that living with a family member who has attempted suicide profoundly disrupts relatives’ well-being: constant vigilance, relational strain, loneliness, and unaddressed needs dominate their daily lives, while professional support often remains insufficient [4, 5, 15].

Meaning in life and family empowerment as protective factors against psychological distress among caregivers of relatives at suicidal risk

It becomes crucial to identify and examine protective factors that may serve as buffers against psychological distress among caregivers of relatives at suicidal risk. Among these, meaning in life has emerged as a particularly robust protective factor, extensively buffering against suicidal ideation, depressive symptoms, emotional dysregulation, and trauma-related symptoms across diverse populations [16, 17]. Evidence indicates that meaning in life mediates the association between hopelessness and suicidal behaviors [17]. Consistently, Guo et al. [16] showed that both the presence and search for meaning are differentially connected with specific dimensions of suicidal ideation. This protective role has been further identified in Spanish populations, where meaning in life reduces the risk of suicidal ideation and non-suicidal self-injury, while also being associated with lower psychopathology in clinical groups with diagnosis of eating disorders and borderline personality disorder [18–20]. More broadly, systematic reviews emphasize that higher levels of meaning in life are consistently linked to lower psychological distress, including depression, anxiety, and posttraumatic symptoms, underscoring its transdiagnostic relevance across clinical and non-clinical populations [21].

Family empowerment (a construct that reflects caregivers’ perceived ability to influence decisions and mobilize support across familial, service, and community domains) has been increasingly acknowledged as a protective feature in caregiving contexts [22]. Empirical studies have found that family-centered empowerment programs can bolster caregiving capacity and reduce distress; for instance, a systematic review with caregivers of adults with chronic diseases concluded that these interventions improve quality of life and mental health [23]. Collectively, these findings suggest that empowerment may operate as a resilience factor in caregiving by enhancing self-efficacy, reducing psychological strain, and improving navigational capacity (even though specific studies in suicide-related caregiving remain limited) [24].

The present study

Despite robust evidence supporting the protective role of meaning in life and family empowerment across different populations, no studies to date have specifically examined this construct among family members caring for individuals with suicidal behavior. In addition, in the Spanish context, no studies have jointly explored the relationships between perceived burden, meaning in life, family empowerment, quality of life and psychopathological symptoms in caregivers of individuals with suicidal behavior. This represents a critical gap, as caregivers in this context are exposed to elevated levels of stress, uncertainty, and emotional burden, and addressing this gap is essential to guide the design of future effective interventions.

Understanding how caregivers’ emotional and cognitive characteristics are interrelated is crucial to advancing both research and practice. Traditional approaches have examined isolated associations between distress and single protective factors [25], but they overlook the dynamic interplay through which emotional states mutually reinforce or buffer one another. Network analysis provides a framework to capture these complex interconnections [26] and reveals which elements—such as depressive symptoms or meaning in life—occupy central or bridging positions within caregivers’ psychological functioning. Beyond its application to psychopathological symptom networks, network analysis has been increasingly employed to examine broader psychological systems involving both maladaptive and adaptive constructs (e.g., emotion regulation, meaning in life, resilience, and burden), particularly in caregiving and health contexts [26, 27]. This approach is grounded in the premise that psychological functioning emerges from the mutual influence among interrelated components rather than from a single latent dimension [28]. Thus, exploring connections among distress indicators and protective attributes allows identifying which elements—such as depressive symptoms or meaning in life—act as central or bridging nodes within caregivers’ emotional networks. Although the variables assessed here span different domains, they form a theoretically coherent system representing caregivers’ emotional adjustment to chronic stress [26]. Following current best practices, redundant indicators were screened prior to estimation to ensure model stability and interpretability [29]. Identifying these key nodes can guide the development of more precise and targeted interventions: for instance, prioritizing the reduction of highly central distress components (e.g., depressive symtptoms, stress) while strengthening peripheral but protective dimensions (e.g., meaning in life [30], family empowerment [31]. From a research perspective, this approach could contribute to informing theoretical models of caregiver adaptation under chronic stress conditions, offering a nuanced view of the interrelations among factors that co-occur with or buffer burden in families living with suicide risk.

The present study used network analysis to examine the interrelations among maladaptive (distress, emotion dysregulation, burden) and adaptive (meaning in life, family empowerment, quality of life) components of caregivers’ psychological functioning. We aimed (1) to identify the most central and structurally influential nodes within this system and (2) to detect bridge connections that may explain the co-activation of distress and protective processes.

Method

Participants

A total of 185 relatives -mothers (n = 106, 49.8%), fathers (n = 31, 14.6%), or other direct family members (n = 76, %)- of individuals diagnosed with suicide behaviour disorder (SBD) or with suicide attempts in the previous two years participated in the study. All diagnoses were carried out by clinical psychologists or general health psychologists with expertise in the assessment of mental disorders members of the research team.

The sample was recruited between November 2021 and September 2023 from different Spanish clinical centers: the Clinical Hospital of the University of València and its Mental Health Services, the PREVI Clinical Center and its three sites in Castellón, València, and Alicante, as well as associations of relatives of people with mental disorders from València, Murcia, and Zaragoza.

Inclusion criteria were the following: (1) being a relative of a person with a suicide attempt in the previous two years who was undergoing treatment or follow-up in one of the recruitment centers, (2) providing signed informed consent; not presenting psychotic disorders, severe personality disorders, or other pathologies that could prevent participation (e.g., substance dependence), (3) completing the baseline assessment, and (4) showing interest in participating in the Family Connections program, a 12-session weekly psychoeducational intervention based on Dialectical Behavior Therapy [32] aimed at relatives of individuals with suicide attempts [33].

Procedure

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the University of València (UV-INV_ETICA-1623849). Family members of patients with suicide attempts were invited to participate in the study, either through the centers where their relatives were receiving treatment or through associations. After providing informed consent, participants underwent a diagnostic interview using the clinician version of the Structured Clinical Interview for DSM-5 (SCID-5-CV) [34], conducted by psychologists with over ten years of experience in the assessment and treatment of mental disorders. During this assessment, inclusion and exclusion criteria were verified, and it was confirmed that their relative had attempted suicide within the previous two years. After eligibility was confirmed, participants completed the baseline assessment via an online interview. Throughout the process, participants were informed about the nature of the study, the confidentiality of their data, and the voluntary nature of their participation.

Study design

A cross-sectional descriptive study was conducted as a preliminary phase of a two-arm randomized controlled trial (experimental group vs. control group).

Instruments

An ad hoc register was specifically developed for this study to assess the frequency of critical incidents involving the family member with SBD during the previous three months. The register included information on the frequency of suicide attempts, nonsuicidal self-injury (NSSI), episodes of verbal or physical violence with caregivers, running away from home, and hospital admissions.

Burden Assessment Scale, BAS [35] The García-Alandete et al.’s [36] Spanish adaptation was used in the present study. The BAS assesses the objective and subjective caregiver burden related to the illness of a loved one. It consists of 19 items rated on a 4-point Likert scale (1 = Not at all, 4 = A lot), where higher scores indicate greater burden. In this study, the BAS showed internal consistencies of α = 0.89 for objective burden and α = 0.85 for subjective burden.

Family Empowerment Scale, FES [22]; Spanish version [37]. The FES was administered to assess empowerment in three domains: family, service system, and community participation. It comprises 34 items rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = completely false, 5 = completely true) with higher scores indicating greater empowerment. In the present study, the FES demonstrated high internal consistency, α = 0.92.

Depression, Anxiety, and Stress Scale-21 items, DASS-21 [38]. The Spanish adaptation by Daza et al. [39] was used to evaluate negative emotional symptoms experienced during the previous week. Items are rated on a 4-point Likert scale (0–3), with higher scores reflecting more frequent symptoms. In this study, the DASS-21 showed an excellent internal consistency: α = 0.91 for depression, α = 0.87 for anxiety, and α = 0.88 for stress.

Quality of Life Index, QOL, Spanish version by Mezzich et al. [40]) was applied to assess perceived quality of life across different domains, including physical and emotional well-being, work functioning, satisfaction with personal relationships, autonomy, community support, spiritual well-being, and overall life satisfaction. The 10 items are rated on a 0–10 Likert scale, where higher scores indicate greater perceived quality of life. In this study, internal consistency for the QLF was α = 0.93.

Purpose in Life Test, PIL [41]. The García-Alandete et al.’s [42] Spanish adaptation (PIL-10) was used, a 10-item scale for assessing the perceived sense of meaning and purpose in life. Items are rated on a 7-point Likert scale (from 1 to 7, with a specific anchor for each item), with higher scores indicating greater meaning in life. The scale comprises two subscales: Satisfaction and Meaning in Life (Meaning), and Goals and Purposes in Life (Purpose), both of which have shown good psychometric properties [42]. In this study, internal consistency was excellent for the global score, α = 0.94, and for both subscales Meaning α = 0.86 and Purpose α = 0.86.

Difficulties in Emotion Regulation Scale, DERS [43]. The DERS assess difficulties in emotion regulation. In this study, the Hervás and Jódar’s [44] Spanish adaptation was used. The DERS consists of 28 items covering five dimensions: lack of emotional control, life interference, emotional inattention, emotional confusion, and emotional rejection. Items are rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = almost never, 5 = almost alway), where higher scores indicate greater difficulties in emotion regulation. In this study, the DERS showed an excellent internal consistency, α = 0.93.

Statistical analyses

Descriptive analyses and Pearson correlations were conducted using SPSS version 29.0 [45]. In addition, network analysis was performed with JASP version 0.95.1 [46] to explore the associations among study variables.

To avoid redundancy and unstable partial correlations, a redundant node analysis was conducted using the goldbricker function from the networktools package in R, version 4.5.1 [47–49]. Node pairs showing correlations ≥ 0.70 and less than 20% unique correlations with other variables were flagged as potentially redundant. However, no redundant nodes were detected, confirming that all variables contributed uniquely to the network structure. The final network was then estimated including all measures of interest, with nodes representing variables and edges representing partial correlations between them. The EBICglasso estimator was applied, which combines graphical LASSO regularization with the Extended Bayesian Information Criterion for model selection [29].

Following current methodological recommendations [29], we assessed the accuracy and stability of the estimated network through nonparametric bootstrapping. Confidence intervals for edge weights were examined to ensure precision, and the correlation stability coefficient (CS-coefficient) for node strength exceeded the recommended threshold of 0.25, indicating acceptable stability for a network of this size. Given the 11 nodes and 185 participants, the sample meets the minimum ratio (approximately 15–20 cases per node) typically considered adequate for reliable network estimation in psychological data.

This approach yields a sparse and interpretable structure by shrinking small partial correlations to zero, thereby reducing the likelihood of spurious connections while retaining the most robust associations. Accuracy of edge weights and stability of centrality estimates were further assessed via non-parametric bootstrapping procedures implemented in bootnet [29], with correlation stability coefficients (CS) above 0.50 considered acceptable. Centrality indices, including strength, expected influence, betweenness, and closeness, were computed to evaluate the relative importance of each node in the network.

Results

Participants’ sociodemographic and clinical characteristics

Participants consisted of 185 relatives (139 women, 75.1%, and 46 men, 24.9%) of individuals with a history of suicide attempt in the previous two years. The mean age of the relatives was 50.26 years (SD = 10.23; range = 18–73 years), while the mean age of the patients was 23.42 years (SD = 9.95; range = 13–69 years).

Sociodemographic, clinical, and critical incident characteristics of the sample are presented in Table 1. Overall, the majority of participants reported higher education and being married. One-third of participants presented with a current psychological problem, most commonly anxiety or depression. Among relatives, borderline personality disorder was the most frequent diagnosis. Regarding critical incidents in the last three months, violent episodes and psychiatric emergency room admissions were the most reported.

Correlations

Pearson correlations were conducted to examine the associations between meaning in life, perceived burden, and other study variables (see Table 2). Meaning in life was negatively correlated with perceived burden (r = –.44, p < .01), symptoms of anxiety and depression (r = –.50, p < .001), emotion dysregulation (r = –.54, p < .001), and both objective burden (r = –.35, p < .01) and subjective burden (r = –.42, p < .01). In contrast, meaning in life was positively correlated with family empowerment (r = .34, p < .01).

When analyzing the PIL-10 subscales, Meaning subscale showed the strongest negative association with perceived burden (r = –.47, p < .001), as well as significant negative correlations with objective burden (r = –.41, p < .001) and subjective burden (r = –.45, p < .001). Purpose subscale was also negatively correlated with perceived burden (r = –.29, p < .001), objective burden (r = –.28, p < .01), and subjective burden (r = –.28, p < .01), though associations were weaker compared to Meaning.

Perceived burden showed strong positive associations with objective burden (r = .89, p < .001), subjective burden (r = .86, p < .001), symptoms of anxiety and depression (r = .61, p < .001), and emotion dysregulation (r = .46, p < .001).

Network analysis

No redundant nodes were detected in the network. The estimated network consisted of 11 nodes and 30 non-zero edges out of 55 possible, yielding a sparsity of 0.46, which indicates a moderately connected structure. Blue edges represent positive associations and red edges negative associations, with edge thickness reflecting the magnitude of the relationship (Fig. 1).

The strongest positive associations were observed between stress and depressive symptoms, and between depressive and anxiety symptoms, while the strongest negative associations emerged between quality of life and both stress and depressive symptoms. Centrality indices further revealed that depressive symptoms, stress, and anxiety were the most central nodes in the network, whereas quality of life and escapes from home were the most peripheral (Table 3).

Centrality measures of the estimated network (N = 185)

Centrality indices (Fig. 2; Table 3) revealed that depressive symptoms were the most central node, showing the highest betweenness (2.39), closeness (1.85), and strength (1.61). Stress also played a prominent role (strength = 0.93; expected influence = 1.58), followed by anxiety (expected influence = 1.06), which was closely connected with stress. Regarding protective factors, Meaning showed a stronger role (strength = 0.75; expected influence = 0.27) than Purpose (strength = 0.48; expected influence = 0.54), suggesting partially distinct contributions of these two facets of meaning in life. Quality of life presented moderate strength (0.18) but a strong negative expected influence (–1.45), underscoring its role as a consequence of distress rather than as a driver in the network. In contrast, running away from home (strength = − 2.10; closeness = − 2.43), family empowerment (strength = − 0.97), and caregiver burden (objective and subjective) were located at the periphery of the network.

The bootstrapped difference test indicated that depressive symptoms, stress, and anxiety had significantly higher expected influence compared to most other nodes (p < .05), confirming their central role. Stability analysis showed that the centrality estimates were adequately stable (CS coefficient > 0.50), supporting the robustness of the findings.

Overall, this model underscores depressive symptoms as the main hub in caregivers’ emotional network, with stress and anxiety reinforcing its centrality. In turn, meaning in life (particularly its experiential dimension, that is, Meaning) and quality of life appeared as protective buffers, although more peripheral in structural influence.

Discussion

The present study aimed to explore the network structure of caregivers’ emotional states, examining how meaning in life, perceived burden, family empowerment, emotion dysregulation, anxiety, stress and depressive symptoms, and quality of life are interconnected.

The network analysis revealed that depressive symptoms occupied the most central position, showing the highest betweenness, closeness, and strength values, followed by stress and, to a lesser extent, anxiety. Rather than implying causal primacy, these findings likely reflect the presence of a shared latent structure of emotional distress—sometimes referred to as general negative affectivity—that permeates caregivers’ psychological functioning. This interpretation aligns with prior literature suggesting that broad distress dimensions naturally occupy central positions in psychopathology-related networks [27]. In this sense, centrality indices should be viewed as descriptive measures of how strongly a given node is connected within the network, rather than as indicators of causal or hierarchical importance [29].

Consistent with this perspective, the observed centrality of the DASS subscales in our data may capture the clustering of general emotional distress rather than discrete symptom processes. This pattern suggests that emotional distress represents a central component of caregivers’ psychological functioning, consistent with prior network studies showing that depressive symptoms tend to occupy a central position within caregivers’ emotional networks and are closely connected with a wide range of distress-related variables [50, 51]. In our model, the centrality of depression, stress, and anxiety reflects not only their strong interconnections but also their links with lower meaning in life, greater emotion dysregulation, and higher perceived burden. This configuration reflects the convergence of psychopathological symptoms and caregiving appraisals, highlighting the emotional vulnerability of family members exposed to suicidal crises. Stress also showed high expected influence, indicating its reinforcing role within the network, while anxiety (although weaker in strength) remained closely connected to both stress and depression.

In contrast, emotion dysregulation did not emerge as the central node in this model but still showed relevant associations, particularly in bridging distress symptoms with subjective burden. This partially supports prior research suggesting that difficulties regulating emotions are associated with higher distress and reduced resilience [52, 53], although in our data its structural influence was weaker compared to depressive symptoms and stress. Notably, higher meaning in life (especially its experiential dimension of Meaning) was associated with lower levels of emotion dysregulation, suggesting that protective resources may buffer caregivers against maladaptive emotional responses. This finding resonates with previous evidence that meaning in life contributes to more adaptive regulation strategies and mitigates the emotional toll of caregiving [17, 21, 54]. Thus, while emotion dysregulation continues to represent a process variable shaping caregivers’ responses to stressful demands, its role appears secondary to the centrality of depressive symptoms but nonetheless sensitive to the protective influence of meaning in life.

Conversely, quality of life appeared more peripheral within the network (low strength and strong negative expected influence), suggesting that it may be more closely related to the outcomes rather than the antecedents of caregivers’ emotional adjustment. This interpretation is consistent with prior studies showing that burden and psychological distress predict poorer quality of life over time [55–57]. Importantly, in our data, higher meaning in life was positively linked to both quality of life and family empowerment, underscoring its role in sustaining protective resources even when these occupy peripheral positions within the network.

Of particular interest, meaning in life showed moderate strength and betweenness, indicating that it operates as a bridge variable connecting protective and risk processes. Notably, the Meaning showed stronger protective associations than Purpose, which displayed weaker or non-significant links with burden. This distinction suggests that perceiving life as coherent and fulfilling may buffer more effectively against distress than the motivational aspect of pursuing goals. This nuance is consistent with prior research highlighting the experiential dimension of meaning as central to resilience [16, 17, 21, 54]. Importantly, despite this growing evidence, no studies to date have explored meaning in life among family members of individuals with suicidal behavior, underscoring a critical gap in the literature.

Finally, objective and subjective burden were strongly interconnected, but both occupied relatively peripheral positions in the network. This indicates that, although burden is an important correlate of distress, the immediate dynamics of caregivers’ emotional functioning are more strongly reflected in depressive and stress-related symptoms.

Complementary correlation analyses further suggested that subjective burden showed stronger associations with anxiety, depression, and stress than objective burden, highlighting the particularly detrimental role of caregivers’ subjective appraisal of demands. This pattern is consistent with prior evidence indicating that subjective perceptions of caregiving demands are more strongly linked to well-being than the objective amount of care provided [58].

Overall, the present model identifies depressive symptoms as the most central node within caregivers’ emotional networks, with stress and anxiety showing close interconnections that reinforce the overall structure of emotional distress. In contrast, meaning in life (particularly its experiential dimension of Meaning) and quality of life appeared as protective yet more peripheral components. Taken together, these findings offer a preliminary yet informative picture of how emotional distress, emotion regulation, and protective resources interrelate within caregivers’ psychological functioning. Although the cross-sectional nature of this study precludes causal inferences, the identified network structure may inform future longitudinal and intervention research aimed at clarifying how specific components—such as depressive symptoms, meaning in life, and family empowerment—interact over time to influence caregivers’ adjustment and well-being.

Conclusions

This study sheds light on the emotional dynamics of relatives caring for individuals with suicidal behaviour. Network analysis revealed depressive symptoms as the central node, reinforced by stress and anxiety, while meaning in life and quality of life emerged as protective but peripheral factors.

Findings highlight the central position of depressive symptoms within caregivers’ emotional networks, suggesting that future research should further explore how distress and protective resources such as meaning in life and quality of life interact in shaping caregivers’ well-being. Rather than indicating causal primacy, the observed centrality of distress-related nodes reflects their structural interconnections and the clustering of general emotional distress within the caregiving context. These results should be interpreted as preliminary and hypothesis-generating, contributing to a better understanding of how distress and protective factors co-occur in families living with suicide risk.

Clinical implications

Future intervention efforts could benefit from exploring approaches aimed at reducing emotional distress while fostering adaptive strategies for emotion regulation. Strengthening meaning in life may represent a promising focus for future intervention studies, given its observed associations with lower psychopathology. In this regard, meaning-centered psychotherapies (e.g [59]). have been proposed as potentially valuable approaches, as they offer structured opportunities to enhance coherence, purpose, and satisfaction, which may help caregivers reinterpret stressful demands and reduce perceived burden. Although family empowerment was more peripheral in the present network, involving relatives in treatment decisions and care processes may represent an important long-term direction to enhance collaboration with professionals and systemic resilience. In this regard, family-based interventions such as the Family Connections program have been associated with improvements in support, reduced burden, and more adaptive coping among relatives of individuals with high emotional and behavioural dysregulation [26, 52].

Overall, these implications should be regarded as exploratory, serving to guide future hypothesis testing and intervention design rather than prescriptive recommendations.

Limitations and future directions

Several limitations must be acknowledged. The cross-sectional design precludes causal inferences, and the use of self-report measures may introduce bias. Moreover, the study focused on a Spanish sample, which may limit generalizability to other cultural contexts. Future research should employ longitudinal and cross-cultural designs to clarify causal pathways and test whether interventions targeting meaning in life and emotion regulation yield measurable benefits for caregivers of suicidal relatives. Additionally, integrating caregivers more actively into treatment processes could enhance both empowerment and perceived support, potentially mitigating the intense emotional toll documented here. Addressing these gaps will be essential to inform evidence-based, family-centered suicide prevention strategies. Although the sample size was adequate for the estimated network, larger samples would increase the precision of edge-weight and centrality estimates—particularly for weaker connections—and allow more robust assessment of network stability through bootstrapping procedures.
