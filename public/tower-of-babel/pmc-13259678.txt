Case Report: Erdheim–Chester disease with liver involvement successfully treated with trametinib
Authors: Long Chang, Kai-ni Shen, Ya-Ping Luo, Xin-xin Cao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundErdheim–Chester disease (ECD) is a rare form of histiocytosis with various clinical manifestations. Liver involvement has been infrequently reported.Case presentationWe report a case of ECD with liver involvement, presenting with progressive elevation of liver enzymes, a manifestation rarely reported in the literature. Follow-up liver function testing and FAPI-PET/CT performed 6 months after initiation of trametinib therapy demonstrated a dramatic response.ConclusionTrametinib holds promise as a therapeutic option for patients with non-BRAF-V600E mutant ECD presenting with liver involvement.

Introduction

Erdheim–Chester disease (ECD) is a rare histiocytic disorder characterized by recurrent activating mutations in the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathway and diverse organ system manifestations. Diagnosis of ECD requires integration of pathological findings with clinical features and radiographic evidence (1). To date, only a few cases of ECD with liver involvement have been reported. The patterns of liver involvement, associated abnormalities in liver function, radiologic findings, and therapeutic approaches remain poorly defined. This report describes a case of ECD with severe liver disease.

Case description

In November 2023, a 59-year-old man presented to our hospital with a 10-month history of polyuria and a 7-month history of jaundice. Physical examination revealed cutaneous jaundice and yellowish plaques around the eyelids and the mouth, without ascites or hepatosplenomegaly. His medical history included hyperlipidemia for 1 year and lung segmentectomy for limited lung cancer 5 years earlier. Family history was negative for genetic conditions or malignancies. He reported no history of smoking or alcohol consumption.

After admission, routine laboratory testing was performed. Complete blood count and renal function tests were within normal limits. Liver enzymes were abnormal, with marked elevation of total bilirubin (171 µmol/L; normal range: 5–22 µmol/L), direct bilirubin (133 µmol/L; normal range: < 6.8 µmol/L), γ-glutamyl transpeptidase (GGT; 1,024 U/L; normal range: 10–60 U/L), alkaline phosphatase (ALP; 557 U/L; normal range: 45–125 U/L), aspartate aminotransferase (AST; 78 U/L; normal range: 15–40 U/L), and alanine aminotransferase (ALT; 85 U/L; normal range: 9–50 U/L). C-reactive protein (CRP; 52 mg/L; normal range: 0–3 mg/L) and erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR; 86 mm/h; normal range: 0–15 mm/h) were also elevated. Serologic testing for viral hepatitis and antinuclear antibody was negative.

Enhanced magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed thickening of the bile duct wall, dilatation of the intrahepatic bile ducts, and abnormal foci of enhancement in the gallbladder wall and the left hepatic duct wall. 99mTc bone scintigraphy demonstrated intense uptake in the anterior rib. To evaluate systemic involvement, the patient underwent 68Ga-fibroblast activation protein inhibitor (68Ga-FAPI) PET/CT, which showed multiple lesions with increased uptake in the liver, pituitary, skin, periorbital region, and pancreas (Figures 1A, D).

Diagnostic assessment, therapeutic intervention, and follow-up

To further clarify the diagnosis, skin biopsy and histological examination revealed numerous foamy histiocytes and occasional multinucleated giant cells embedded in fibrotic tissue, with positive staining for CD68 and S100, and negative staining for CD1a, Langerin, and B-Raf proto-oncogene, serine/threonine kinase (BRAF), confirming ECD (Supplementary Figure 1). A paraffin-embedded skin tissue sample was obtained, and next-generation sequencing was performed as previously described (2), which identified a TP53 positive (exon 4 c.97-1G-A; VAF: 6.1%).

Based on these findings, the diagnosis of ECD was established. Due to the progressive liver involvement, treatment with trametinib at 2 mg orally once daily was initiated in a patient with non-BRAF-V600E. Significant improvement in skin papules and liver enzymes was observed after 1 month. Bilirubin, GGT, and ALP demonstrated a durable response (Supplementary Figure 2). Hepatic lesions also improved, as shown by follow-up FAPI-PET performed at 6 months (Figures 1B, E) and 12 months (Figures 1C, F) after initiation of trametinib therapy. However, polyuria did not resolve.

Discussion

ECD is a rare form of systemic histiocytosis, typically presenting with bilateral osteosclerosis and involvement of organs such as the lungs, pituitary gland, heart, central nervous system, skin, and retroperitoneum, including the kidneys. Liver involvement in ECD is extremely uncommon (3), and the characteristics of this manifestation remain poorly defined. We report the case of a 59-year-old man who presented with newly diagnosed polyuria and jaundice. To our knowledge, this is the first reported case of ECD with liver involvement successfully treated with targeted therapy.

The features of liver involvement in ECD remain poorly defined. To date, only five cases of liver involvement in patients with ECD have been reported (4–9) (Table 1). In the prior literature, liver involvement has been described as nodular lesions, tumor-like lesions of the biliary tract, and diffuse infiltration. These patients presented with jaundice, ascites, or nonspecific abdominal symptoms. Biochemical testing showed cholestasis with mild transaminase elevation or normal liver function, findings similar to those observed in patients with Langerhans cell histiocytosis (LCH) involving the liver (10). Histopathological findings of ECD with liver involvement demonstrated histiocytic infiltrates, marked fibrosis, and chronic inflammation. Bile duct involvement resulted in fibrosis with moderate intra- and extrahepatic cholestasis, which followed a more aggressive course. Of the five reported cases, two patients were treated with interferon but died from progressive liver dysfunction. For the remaining patients, details regarding treatment, follow-up of liver function, and imaging were not clearly documented. Empiric treatment with MEK inhibitors for wild-type ECD without an identified MAPK pathway mutation is a reasonable approach in acutely ill patients. As our patient showed worsening liver enzyme levels, targeted therapy was considered (3). This patient was successfully treated with trametinib, although the optimal duration of therapy remains unclear.

To date, little is known about the imaging characteristics of liver involvement in ECD. Hepatomegaly, multiple nodules, dilated intrahepatic bile ducts, and cirrhosis have been described in previous studies. Progressive elevation of liver enzymes may indicate sclerosing cholangitis or end-stage liver disease. In patients with LCH, MRI has demonstrated higher sensitivity for early recognition of liver involvement; a wide range of imaging patterns—including diffuse patchy/nodular lesions, cirrhosis, dilated bile ducts, and fatty liver—have been reported (10). In this study, 68Ga-FAPI PET/CT emerged as a promising tool to assess disease extent in patients with ECD, showing superiority over 18F-FDG PET/CT in our previous work (11).

ECD can involve any organ, and clinicians should remain aware of the condition and include it in the differential diagnoses in suspicious clinical settings, even when the likelihood is low. In this case, liver involvement without typical manifestations obscured the clinical diagnosis of ECD. The presence of characteristic immunohistochemical findings indicated a histiocytic origin with xanthomatous features and fibrosis, which was instrumental in establishing the final diagnosis.

Conclusion

We present the first case of ECD with liver involvement successfully treated with trametinib. This report suggests that targeted therapy may hold promise as a treatment option for patients with ECD and progressive liver involvement.
