Toward Scalable and Valid Conditional Independence Testing with Spectral Representations
Alek Fröhlich, Vladimir Kostić, Karim Lounici, Daniel Perazzo, Massimiliano Pontil

Abstract
Conditional independence (CI) is central to causal inference, feature selection, and graphical modeling, yet it is untestable in many settings without additional assumptions. Existing CI tests often rely on restrictive structural conditions, limiting their validity on real-world data. Kernel methods using the partial covariance operator offer a more principled approach but suffer from limited adaptivity, slow convergence, and poor scalability. In this work, we explore whether representation learning can help address these limitations. Specifically, we focus on representations derived from the singular value decomposition of the partial covariance operator and use them to construct a simple test statistic, reminiscent of the Hilbert-Schmidt Independence Criterion (HSIC). We also introduce a practical bi-level contrastive algorithm to learn these representations. Our theory links representation learning error to test performance and establishes asymptotic validity and power guarantees. Preliminary experiments suggest that this approach offers a practical and statistically grounded path toward scalable CI testing, bridging kernel-based theory with modern representation learning.

Contributions.
In summary, our main contributions are:

•

We identify a key limitation of modern CI tests: their restrictive structural assumptions hinder adaptation beyond their intended settings. To address this, we recast CI testing through the partial covariance operator and introduce a simple, scalable method for learning its leading spectral features.

•

We provide a comprehensive theoretical analysis of CI testing using the learned representations and simple HSIC-like statistics. This includes type I error and power guarantees along with the characterization of the null distribution of the test statistic as asymptotically chi-squared.

•

We report preliminary experiments on synthetic data which suggest that our approach offers a practical and statistically grounded path toward scalable CI testing, bridging kernel-based theory with modern representation learning.

Statistical hypothesis testing.
We are given a sample

Under the alternative, the

\text{Power}=\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{H}_{1}}(\widehat{T}_{n}\geq c_{\alpha}).
The goal is to design tests that achieve high power while controlling the Type I error at level

Our objective is thus to develop conditional independence tests that rigorously control Type I error and retain competitive power in a wide range of structural scenarios. We refer to
Section

B.1

Learning Partial Covariance Operators

In this section, we describe a practical algorithm for learning the leading spectral features of the partial covariance operator
\mathsf{\Sigma}_{X\ddot{Y}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\,Z}

Assumption 3.1

\mathsf{\Sigma}_{X\ddot{Y}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\,Z}:L^{2}(\ddot{Y})\to L^{2}(X)

CI Testing with Spectral Representations

We begin this section by recasting nonparametric CI testing in terms of the partial covariance operator:

\mathcal{H}_{0}\!:\lVert\mathsf{\Sigma}_{X\ddot{Y}\vphantom{\ddot{X}}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\,Z}\rVert_{\text{HS}}^{2}\!=\!0\;\;\text{vs.}\;\;\mathcal{H}_{1,n,d}\!:\lVert\mathsf{\Sigma}_{X\ddot{Y}\vphantom{\ddot{X}}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\,Z}\rVert^{2}_{\text{HS}}\!\geq\!\epsilon_{n}.

(8)

Under
\mathcal{H}_{0}

\mathsf{\Sigma}_{X\ddot{Y}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\,Z}

Related Work

This section provides an overview of existing CI tests and spectral representation learning.

Kernel tests of CI.

Kernel-based measures of conditional dependence were first introduced in
(
11
)
, building on the conditional cross-covariance operator from
(
10
)
.
The first kernel-based conditional independence (CI) test, KCIT
(
50
)
, uses the Hilbert–Schmidt norm of this operator and provides an asymptotic null distribution via a gamma approximation, but without power guarantees.
To improve scalability,
42
introduced RCIT and RCoT, which employ random Fourier features to approximate KCIT while also offering an asymptotic null characterization.
More recently, kernel partial correlation
(
16
)
and tests based on analytical kernel mean embeddings
(
36
)
have been proposed.
Despite their simplicity and effectiveness, kernel-based tests are inherently tied to kernel mean embeddings
(
28
)
, which results in slow convergence rates
(
32
)
and a loss of power as the dimension of the conditioning variable increases
(
33
)
.

Permutation tests of CI.

Permutation
(
35
;
20
;
2
;
11
;
38
)
tests rely on the factorization
P_{X|Y=y,Z=z}=P_{X|Z=z}
to generate samples by permuting the x values condition on binned/clustered
Z
values. This results in approximately valid samples from
P_{XYZ}
under the null hypothesis if
Z
is not high-dimensional and the clusters are sufficiently populated. Using these additional samples, the tests are able to compute approximate p-values, which lead to approximately valid tests under similar conditions
(
20
)
.

Model-x tests of CI.

Model-X tests are based on knowledge of the conditional distribution
P(X|Z)
and were originally introduced alongside the Conditional Randomization Test (CRT) in
(
5
)
, which demonstrated that the method produces valid p-values when the conditional distribution is known exactly, by sampling from
P(X|Z)
. CPT
(
4
)
extended the CRT framework by designing a permutation test that requires only approximate knowledge of the conditional distribution. Motivated by the success of generative modeling with neural networks,
2
proposed learning
P(X|Z)
and then performing a permutation test using GANs, with theoretical guarantees based on the GAN loss. However,
(
51
)
showed that these bounds can be very loose even in simple linear settings and proposed a doubly robust alternative. Another double generative approach was provided in
(
40
)
. More recently,
(
49
)
introduced a diffusion-based variant of the approach.

Neural network tests of CI.

Neural network-based methods
(
2
;
51
;
49
)
promise strong representation learning capabilities but raise further challenges for CI testing. Estimating dependence is only part of the problem; crucially, one must also characterize the null distribution of the test statistic, which is analytically intractable for neural network-based models. Consequently, many methods rely on empirical
p
-values obtained via permutations or conditional resampling, which are computationally costly and often lack rigorous statistical guarantees.

Spectral representation learning.

Expansions in orthogonal bases such as Fourier, wavelets, and smoothing splines have a long history in nonparametric statistical inference
(
8
)
. While these bases are well understood, they perform poorly in high dimensions and lack adaptivity to the data distribution. This limitation motivated the use of bases derived from the spectral decomposition of linear integral operators defined by symmetric positive semi-definite kernels
(
18
)
. More recently, research has shifted toward learned spectral bases, or features, which have been applied across diverse settings including dynamical systems
(
45
)
, reinforcement learning
(
15
)
, and nonparametric causal inference
(
48
;
44
;
27
;
26
)
. Many of these methods resemble contrastive approaches in self-supervised learning
(
13
)
. In cases where the objective is to learn linear integral operators, the problem often reduces to estimating their associated kernel
(
23
;
30
)
; for the conditional expectation operator, this kernel can be expressed as a density ratio, for which a variety of estimation strategies have been developed
(
43
)
.

Conclusions

In this work, we revisited conditional independence testing through the lens of the partial covariance operator and learned spectral features, aiming to overcome the scalability and adaptivity limitations of kernel-based tests while retaining their generality. By characterizing conditional dependence via the Hilbert–Schmidt norm of this operator, our formulation implicitly adapts to diverse structural settings including smoothness, sparsity, and latent low-dimensionality.
The adoption of learned spectral features enables efficient optimization and GPU acceleration, making the method substantially more scalable than traditional kernel approaches.
At the same time, we established rigorous statistical guarantees: under the null, the test statistic converges to a chi-squared distribution, while under the alternative it achieves power against a fixed alternative as sample size increases.
Preliminary experiments further support the practicality of this framework, suggesting a promising direction for scalable CI testing that bridges kernel-based theory with modern representation learning.

Appendix B
Proofs of Statistical Results

B.1
Background on statistical hypothesis testing

Statistical hypothesis testing.

We briefly recall the fundamental concepts of statistical hypothesis testing, as they will be used throughout the paper.

Given a sample
\mathcal{D}_{n}=\{(X_{i},Y_{i},Z_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
drawn i.i.d. from a joint distribution
P_{XYZ}
, a
statistical test
is a decision rule that determines whether to reject a null hypothesis
H_{0}
in favor of an alternative hypothesis
H_{1}
. This decision is typically based on a
test statistic

T_{n}=T(\mathcal{D}_{n})
, a real-valued function designed to capture evidence against
H_{0}
.

The test proceeds by defining a
rejection region

\mathcal{R}_{n}\subset\mathbb{R}
such that:

\text{Reject }H_{0}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad T_{n}\in\mathcal{R}_{n}.

(15)

Commonly,
\mathcal{R}_{n}
takes the form of a threshold rule
\mathcal{R}_{n}=\{t\in\mathbb{R}:t\geq t_{\alpha}\}
, where
t_{\alpha}
is the
critical value
chosen to control the
Type I error rate
at a desired significance level
\alpha\in(0,1)
. Specifically, the Type I error corresponds to the probability of incorrectly rejecting
H_{0}
when it is true:

\alpha=\mathbb{P}_{H_{0}}(T_{n}\in\mathcal{R}_{n}).

(16)

Conversely, the
Type II error
is the probability of failing to reject
H_{0}
when the alternative hypothesis holds:

\beta(P_{XYZ})=\mathbb{P}_{H_{1}}(T_{n}\notin\mathcal{R}_{n}),

(17)

where we emphasize that
\beta
depends on the specific alternative distribution
P_{XYZ}\in H_{1}
. The
power
of the test is defined as the probability of correctly rejecting
H_{0}
under
H_{1}
:

\text{Power}=1-\beta(P_{XYZ}).

(18)

The objective in hypothesis testing is to design test statistics and rejection regions that simultaneously control the Type I error at level
\alpha
and achieve high power against relevant alternatives. In nonparametric settings, where minimal assumptions are made on
P_{XYZ}
, this is particularly challenging.

A fundamental notion in the asymptotic analysis of hypothesis tests is that of
contiguity

(
46
)
. A sequence of distributions
\{Q_{n}\}
is said to be contiguous with respect to the sequence of distributions
\{P_{n}\}
if, for every sequence of events
\{A_{n}\}
, we have:

P_{n}(A_{n})\to 0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad Q_{n}(A_{n})\to 0,

(19)

as
n\to\infty
. In the context of statistical testing, contiguity implies that the alternative becomes asymptotically indistinguishable from the null: no test statistic can achieve both vanishing Type I and Type II error rates.

B.2
Background on sub-Gaussian random variables

We recall the definition of a sub-Gaussian random variable and some of its useful properties.

Let
\psi_{2}(x)=e^{x^{2}}-1
,
x\geq 0
. We define the
\psi_{2}
-Orlicz norm of a random variable
\eta
as

\lVert\eta\rVert_{\psi_{2}}:=\inf\left\{C>0\,:\,\mathbb{E}\left[\psi_{2}\left(\frac{|\eta|}{C}\right)\right]\leq 1\right\}.

Definition B.1

(Sub-Gaussian random vector)
.

A centered random vector
X\in\mathbb{R}^{\underline{d}}
, with probability distribution denoted
\mu_{X}
, will be called sub-Gaussian iff, for all
u\in\mathbb{R}^{\underline{d}}
,

\lVert\langle X,u\rangle\rVert_{\psi_{2}}\lesssim\lVert\langle X,u\rangle\rVert_{L_{2}(\mu_{X})}.

Lemma B.1

((Sub-Gaussian random variable) Lemma 5.5. in
(
47
)
)
.

Let
Z
be a random variable. Then, the following assertions are equivalent with parameters
K_{i}>0
differing from each other by at most an absolute constant factor.

1.

Tails:

\mathbb{P}\{|Z|>t\}\leq\exp(1-t^{2}/K_{1}^{2})
for all
t\geq 0
;

2.

Moments:

(\mathbb{E}|Z|^{p})^{1/p}\leq K_{2}\sqrt{p}
for all
p\geq 1
;

3.

Super-exponential moment:

\mathbb{E}\exp(Z^{2}/K_{3}^{2})\leq 2
.

A random variable
Z
satisfying any of the above assertions is called a sub-Gaussian random variable. We will denote by
K_{3}
the sub-Gaussian norm.

Consequently, a sub-Gaussian random variable satisfies the following equivalence of moments property. There exists an absolute constant
c>0
such that for any
m\geq 2
,

\big(\mathbb{E}|Z|^{m}\big)^{1/m}\leq cK_{3}\sqrt{m}\big(\mathbb{E}|Z|^{2}\big)^{1/2}.

(20)

Let
\eta_{1},\dots,\eta_{n},\eta
be i.i.d
K
-sub-Gaussian random vectors in
\mathbf{R}^{d}
. Then there exists an absolute constant
C>0
such that for all
\delta\in(0,1)
,

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\big\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{I=1}^{n}\eta_{i}-\mathbb{E}[\eta]\big\|\leq CK\left(\sqrt{\frac{d}{n}}+\sqrt{\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{n}}\right)\right)\geq 1-\delta.

where
\|\cdot\|
is the Euclidean norm.

B.3
Background on Lindeberg-Feller Multivariate Central Limit Theorem

We use the multivariate CLT for independent (not necessarily identically distributed) vector-valued random variables:

Theorem B.1

(Multivariate Central Limit Theorem)
.

Let
\{W_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
be independent, mean-zero random vectors. Assume that

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}[W_{i}W_{i}^{\top}]\to\Sigma\quad\text{and}\quad\text{for all }\varepsilon>0,\quad\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[\lVert W_{i}\rVert^{2}\cdot\mathbbm{1}_{\{\lVert W_{i}\rVert>\varepsilon\sqrt{n}\}}\right]\to 0.

Then

\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}W_{i}\xrightarrow{d}\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma).

B.6
Diagonal Concentration of the Projection Matrix

We end this subsection with a concentration result on

Define

Lemma B.4

Assume that

–sub-Gaussian random variables. Therefore, their product
A_{i}B_{i}
is
2K^{2}\,d
–sub-exponential.

As a result, for all
t>0
,

\mathbb{P}(A_{i}B_{i}>t)\leq ce^{-c^{\prime}\frac{t}{K^{2}\,d}},

for some absolute constants
c,c^{\prime}>0
.

Therefore, elementary calculus (based on integration by parts for Gamma function) gives the following upper bound

\mathbb{E}\left[(A_{i}B_{i})^{2}\cdot\mathbbm{1}_{\{A_{i}B_{i}>\varepsilon\sqrt{n}\}}\right]\leq cK^{2}\,d\,n\,\epsilon^{2}\,e^{-c^{\prime}\frac{\epsilon\sqrt{n}}{K^{2}\,d}},

for absolute constants
c,c^{\prime}>0
.

Thus,

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[\lVert w_{i}\rVert^{2}\cdot\mathbbm{1}_{\{\lVert w_{i}\rVert>\varepsilon\sqrt{n}\}}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\lVert w_{1}\rVert^{2}\cdot\mathbbm{1}_{\{\lVert w_{1}\rVert>\varepsilon\sqrt{n}\}}\right]\leq cK^{2}\,d\,n\,\epsilon^{2}\,e^{-c^{\prime}\frac{\epsilon\sqrt{n}}{K^{2}\,d}}\to 0,

provided that
n\geq\frac{3}{2\epsilon^{2}\,c^{\prime}}\,K^{4}\,d^{2}\log n
as
n\to\infty
so that the exponential term is dominating and thus leading the right-hand-side to
0
.

Since the Lindeberg condition is satisfied, by the multivariate CLT, the sequence
\mathrm{vec}(Z_{n})
converges in distribution to a multivariate Gaussian random vector. Therefore,

Z_{n}\xrightarrow{d}\mathcal{Z}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d},\quad\text{where }\mathrm{vec}(\mathcal{Z})\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma),

with

\Sigma=\mathrm{Cov}(u_{\theta}(x))\otimes\mathrm{Cov}(v_{\theta}(\ddot{y})).

∎
