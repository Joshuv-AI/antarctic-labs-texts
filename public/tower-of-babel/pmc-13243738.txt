Agreement between noninvasive hemoglobin monitoring (SpHb) and laboratory hemoglobin in patients undergoing free-flap reconstruction for oral squamous cell carcinoma
Authors: Giyoung Yun, Ji-Young Yoon, Cheul-Hong Kim, Hee Young Kim, Eun-Jung Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSurgery for head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) often involves extensive resection with free-flap reconstruction, leading to significant blood loss. Accurate intraoperative hemoglobin (Hb) monitoring is crucial to guide transfusion. Non-invasive Hb monitoring (SpHb) offers continuous Hb measurement; however, its reliability in major oncological surgeries remains uncertain. Thus, this study evaluated the agreement between SpHb and point-of-care testing-measured Hb (POCT-Hb).MethodsIn this retrospective study, patients undergoing oral squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) resection with free-flap reconstruction (Sep 2023–Oct 2025) were analyzed. Hb levels were measured at the time of anesthesia induction (T1) and surgical closure (T2) using POCT and simultaneous SpHb. The postoperative central-laboratory Hb level validated the POCT accuracy. Correlation (Pearson), intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC), and agreement (Bland–Altman) analyses were performed. Effects of intraoperative blood loss and dopamine infusion on SpHb accuracy were assessed using subgroup analyses, t-tests, and a univariate general linear model.ResultsFifty-five patients were included. SpHb showed a moderate correlation with POCT-Hb at T1 (r = 0.619, ICC = 0.563) and T2 (r = 0.639, ICC = 0.563), with wider Bland–Altman limits at T2. Furthermore, changes in SpHb underestimated ΔHb compared with POCT (r = 0.439). Intraoperative blood loss did not significantly affect SpHb accuracy, whereas dopamine use was associated with larger absolute differences and wider limits of agreement. The dopamine level remained an independent predictor of SpHb–POCT discrepancy.ConclusionSpHb showed moderate agreement with POCT-Hb and was affected by dopamine use. Although its absolute accuracy is limited, SpHb may serve as a useful adjunct for trend monitoring and for guiding the timing of laboratory testing.

INTRODUCTION

Head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC), the predominant subtype of head and neck cancer, has been reported to be the seventh most prevalent cancer worldwide [12]. Despite advances in radiotherapy, chemotherapy, and immunotherapy, radical surgery with free-flap reconstruction remains the standard treatment of advanced HNSCC [34]. These procedures are lengthy and often accompanied by substantial intraoperative blood loss, making the timely detection of anemia essential for maintaining hemodynamic stability and preventing adverse outcomes.

Therefore, accurate hemoglobin (Hb) assessment is a critical component of intraoperative management, guiding fluid therapy, and transfusion decisions. Traditionally, Hb levels are measured using a clinical laboratory hematology analyzer or point-of-care testing (POCT) device in the operating room [5]. Noninvasive Hb monitoring (SpHb) using pulse CO-oximetry has emerged as a potential alternative [67], offering the advantage of continuous, real-time assessment without repeated blood draws [8]. However, previous studies have reported mixed results: some have shown that SpHb adequately tracks Hb trends [7], while others have demonstrated limited agreement with laboratory measurements, particularly in settings of peripheral vasoconstriction or in patients with diverse physiological conditions [9101112]. Furthermore, data on the reliability of SpHb in major head and neck cancer surgeries are scarce.

Given the clinical importance of timely Hb evaluation in high-risk surgical populations, further investigations into the agreement between SpHb and laboratorys-measured Hb (POCT-Hb) are warranted. Because perioperative transfusion decisions are frequently based on rapid POCT results obtained in the operating room, understanding how SpHb compares specifically with POCT-measured Hb (POCT-Hb) is clinically relevant. Therefore, this study aimed to assess the correlation and agreement between SpHb and POCT-Hb in patients undergoing extensive surgery for HNSCC with the goal of clarifying the reliability and practical utility of SpHb as a continuous monitoring tool during major oncological procedures.

METHODS

1. Study design and participants

This retrospective study included patients who underwent wide excision and free-flap reconstruction at Pusan National University Dental Hospital (Yangsan, Republic of Korea) between September 2023 and October 2025. Patients with American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status classifications of I–III were included. The Institutional Review Board of the Pusan National University Dental Hospital (PNUDH 2025-11-007) approved the study protocol and waived the requirement for informed consent owing to the retrospective nature of the analysis. All data were assessed and analyzed for research purposes only after approval by the Institutional Review Board.

Based on a Bland–Altman analysis, assuming a standard deviation of 1.0 g/dL for Hb differences and aiming to restrict the 95% limits of agreement (LOA) within ± 0.5 SD of the mean difference, a minimum of 50 patients was required. Considering an anticipated dropout rate of 10%, the target sample size was set at 60 patients.

Patients without squamous cell carcinoma (SCC), those with benign tumors, those who received intraoperative blood transfusion, and those with missing data were excluded to minimize potential confounding effects on the study outcomes.

2. Data collection

The following clinical variables were extracted from medical records: patient demographics (age, sex, and body mass index), ASA physical status, type of free-flap reconstruction (radial forearm free flap, fibular free flap, anterolateral thigh flap, or deep circumflex iliac artery flap), operative time, intraoperative blood loss, and intraoperative use of dopamine infusion.

Hb measurements were collected at two points: immediately after the induction of general anesthesia (T1) and at the time of surgical closure (T2). Hb levels at both time points were measured using arterial blood gas analysis (ABGA) via the epoc® Blood Analysis System (Siemens Healthineers, Oakville, Ontario, Canada), a POCT device routinely used in the operating room. Noninvasive Hb (SpHb) values recorded by pulse CO-oximetry at the same time points were also obtained for comparison.

In addition, postoperative Hb values measured by the central clinical laboratory immediately after the patient left the operating room were reviewed and were included in the analysis to evaluate the accuracy of POCT measurements relative to the institutional laboratory standard.

3. Anesthesia and monitoring protocol

All patients received general anesthesia with standardized intraoperative monitoring, including noninvasive blood pressure measurement, electrocardiography, pulse oximetry, and bispectral index (BIS) monitoring. A pulsed CO oximetry sensor was applied to the index finger on the same side as the arterial cannulation and was shielded from ambient light to ensure reliable SpHb measurements. SpHb was monitored continuously throughout the procedure.

General anesthesia was induced using propofol, remifentanil, and rocuronium, followed by endotracheal intubation and arterial line placement for invasive blood pressure monitoring. An additional infusion line for the colloid administration was established in anticipation of significant blood loss.

During the maintenance of anesthesia, patients received volume-controlled mechanical ventilation with a standardized tidal volume and fraction of inspired oxygen (FiO2) of 0.4 to maintain stable end-tidal CO2. Sevoflurane was titrated to maintain an appropriate depth of anesthesia, and BIS values were maintained within the target range. These settings were consistently applied to minimize physiological factors that could influence SpHb accuracy, such as peripheral perfusion, ventilation status, and hemodynamic variability.

4. Statistical analysis

Pearson’s correlation analysis was performed to evaluate the linear association between SpHb and POCT-Hb at T1 (immediately after induction) and T2 (at surgical closure). The agreement between the two measurements was assessed using Bland–Altman plots at both time points. Agreement in Hb changes (ΔHb, T2–T1) between SpHb and POCT-Hb was also analyzed using Bland–Altman methods.

To validate the accuracy of POCT-Hb measurements, T2 POCT-Hb and SpHb values were compared with Hb results obtained from the central laboratory immediately after the patients left the operating room, using both Pearson correlation and Bland–Altman plots to evaluate agreement.

Potential factors influencing the agreement between SpHb and POCT-measured Hb were evaluated by examining the association between intraoperative blood loss and the absolute difference between SpHb and POCT-Hb at T2. The effect of dopamine administration on SpHb accuracy was further assessed by subgroup analysis using Bland–Altman plots and a comparison of absolute differences using an independent t-test. Finally, a univariate general linear model was constructed to determine whether dopamine use independently predicted SpHb inaccuracy, after adjusting for intraoperative blood loss.

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 25.0; IBM Corp., USA), and two-sided P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. The normality of the data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. All primary variables, including SpHb and POCT-Hb at each time point satisfied the normality assumption (P > 0.05), and parametric tests were subsequently employed for all analyses.

RESULTS

In total, 65 patients were initially enrolled in this study. Among these, four patients requiring intraoperative transfusion were excluded to avoid potential bias related to transfusion effects. In addition, one patient who was not pathophysically confirmed to have SCC, four patients who underwent free-flap reconstruction for benign oral tumors, and one patient with missing laboratory data were excluded. Ultimately, 55 patients were included in the final analysis. Baseline characteristics of the study population are summarized in Table 1.

Pearson’s correlation analysis demonstrated a statistically significant moderate correlation between SpHb and POCT-Hb at both time points, with correlation coefficients of 0.619 at T1 (Fig. 1A) and 0.639 at T2 (both P < 0.01) (Fig. 1B). The intra-class correlation coefficient (ICC) for absolute agreement was 0.563 for both T1 (95% CI: 0.340–0.723) and T2 (95% CI: 0.286–0.740) images. Bland–Altman analyses were performed sequentially. At T1, the mean bias was −0.2527 g/dL, with 95% LOA ranging from −2.81 to +2.30 g/dL (Fig. 2A). At T2, the mean bias shifted to +0.78 g/dL, and the 95% LOA widened to −2.07 to +3.65 g/dL (Fig. 2B).

The correlation between changes in SpHb and POCT-Hb from T1 to T2 was also significant (r = 0.439, P = 0.001). A Bland–Altman analysis of the change values demonstrated a mean bias of −1.04 g/dL, indicating slight tendency for SpHb to underestimate the change in Hb compared with POCT-Hb. The 95% LOA ranged from −3.44 to +1.36 g/dL.

When comparing POCT-Hb at T2 with postoperative central-laboratory Hb levels, a strong and statistically significant correlation was observed (r = 0.903, P < 0.01) (Fig. 3A). A Bland–Altman analysis revealed a mean bias of +0.41 g/dL, indicating that central-laboratory Hb values tended to be higher than POCT-Hb. The 95% LOA ranged from −1.19 to +2.00g/dL (Fig. 3B). The correlation between SpHb and postoperative central-laboratory Hb levels was moderate (r = 0.637, P < 0.01). A Bland–Altman analysis demonstrated a mean bias of −0.3745 g/dL, showing a tendency for SpHb values to be higher than the corresponding central-laboratory Hb measurements. The 95% LOA ranged from −3.19 to +2.44 g/dL.

At T2, intraoperative blood loss was not significantly associated with the absolute difference between SpHb and POCT-Hb (r = −0.053, P = 0.703). To assess the potential effect of dopamine on SpHb measurement accuracy, patients were divided into two subgroups: those who did not receive dopamine (n = 19) and those who did (n = 36). The mean absolute difference between the SpHb and POCT-Hb was 0.9211 g/dL in the non-dopamine group and 1.5778 g/dL in the dopamine group (Fig. 4). An independent t-test confirmed that this difference was statistically significant (Levene’s test for equality of variances: P = 0.589; t = −2.561; df = 53; P = 0.013).

A Bland–Altman analysis comparing the two subgroups demonstrated clear differences in measurement accuracy. In the non-dopamine group (n = 19), the mean bias between SpHb and POCT-Hb was 0.3316 g/dL, with a standard deviation of 1.2374 g/dL, yielding 95% LOA ranging from −2.09 to +2.75 g/dL. In contrast, the dopamine group (n = 36) showed a larger mean bias of 1.0278 g/dL and a greater standard deviation of 1.52496 g/dL, resulting in wider 95% LOA (−1.96 to +4.02 g/dL).

A univariate general linear model was constructed to determine whether dopamine use independently predicted the absolute difference between SpHb and POCT-Hb at T2 after adjusting for intraoperative blood loss. The overall model was statistically significant (F = 3.391, P = 0.041), with an R2 of 0.115 (adjusted R2 = 0.081). Dopamine administration remained an independent predictor of increased measurement discrepancy (B = −0.665, P = 0.013), whereas intraoperative blood loss was not a significant predictor (B = 0.000, P = 0.582). The estimated marginal means indicated that the adjusted absolute difference between SpHb and POCT-Hb was 0.92 g/dL (95% CI: 0.50–1.34) in the non-dopamine group and 1.581 g/dL (95% CI: 1.27–1.89) in the dopamine group, confirming that dopamine administration was significantly associated with a greater discrepancy between SpHb and POCT-Hb.

DISCUSSION

In the present study, SpHb measured at T1 (immediately after anesthesia induction) showed a moderate correlation with POCT-Hb (r = 0.619), yet displayed relatively wide LOA (−2.81 to +2.30 g/dL). This broad variability at such an early stage led us to hypothesize that the inherent accuracy of noninvasive monitoring may be compromised in populations with high comorbidity burdens, such as oncologic patients. Supporting this possibility, a prior study from our institution involving younger, healthier orthognathic-surgery patients and using the same equipment reported a noticeably tighter LOA range (−3.085 to +1.03 g/dL) [10]. Furthermore, our findings align with previous reports in high-risk surgical populations [911], where patients with multiple comorbidities or cardiovascular instability similarly exhibited wider LOA than healthier cohorts. The contrast between these two populations suggests that SpHb accuracy is sensitive to the physiological and vascular status of the monitored subjects. While we did not directly measure individual perfusion index reactivity, the wider discrepancy observed in our older comorbid patients suggests that underlying vascular health could be a primary factor affecting measurement reliability [9]. This interpretation is consistent with the established principle that SpHb technology depends on adequate peripheral blood flow for stable optical signal detection [13141516]. Thus, in high-risk patients where peripheral perfusion may be chronically or acutely impaired, the baseline reliability of SpHb should be interpreted with caution. This suggests that baseline physiological variability, rather than intraoperative factors alone, influences SpHb accuracy even during the early anesthetic phase.

At T2, the mean bias increased to +0.78 g/dL with wider 95% LOA (−2.07 to +3.65 g/dL). This shift indicates that SpHb tends to overestimate absolute Hb levels at later intraoperative stages, potentially underestimating the true degree of intraoperative decline. This pattern is consistent with previous studies evaluating SpHb during acute blood loss; for instance, one study reported a mean bias of +1.49 g/dL (LOA: −2.2 to 5.0 g/dL) [17], underscoring the clinical limitations of SpHb in detecting rapid Hb drops [18]. In addition, several reports have noted that SpHb may substantially overestimate Hb, particularly in patients with anemia [19], suggesting that low true Hb levels further exacerbate measurement discrepancies in noninvasive Hb monitoring [81120]. Given the wide LOA observed, such variability may be clinically significant and could affect transfusion decision-making.

To ensure that our analysis was based on a reliable reference, we validated POCT-Hb against the Hb results obtained from the central laboratory. Our comparison revealed a very high correlation (r = 0.903) and relatively narrow LOA (−1.19 to +2.00 g/dL), confirming that POCT-Hb closely reflects institutional laboratory reference measurements in our clinical setting. This is further supported by prior evidence showing that POCT (e.g., iSTAT) tracks Hb changes more accurately (76% concordance) than SpHb (60% concordance) [17]. These findings validated POCT-Hb as a stable baseline, allowing us to investigate other physiological factors that affect SpHb accuracy.

Given that SpHb overestimation is often exacerbated at lower Hb levels, we first investigated whether surgical blood loss drives the widening discrepancy at T2. Notably, our multivariable analysis confirmed that intraoperative blood loss was not a significant predictor of measurement errors (P = 0.582). Even after adjusting for the effects of bleeding, dopamine administration emerged as the sole independent factor associated with an increased discrepancy (P = 0.013). In fact, the adjusted absolute error in the dopamine group was 1.58 g/dL (95% CI: 1.27–1.89), approximately two-fold higher than the error of 0.92 g/dL (95% CI: 0.50–1.34) observed in the non-dopamine group.

While previous studies have reported decreased SpHb accuracy at lower Hb levels [81120], our findings indicate that the observed measurement discrepancies may not be solely explained by Hb decline or intraoperative blood loss. Rather, we propose that the physiological stressors associated with such states, specifically the administration of vasopressors such as dopamine to manage hemodynamic instability, may partially contribute to the observed measurement discrepancies.

Although direct evidence specifically linking dopamine to SpHb signal quality is limited, there is a strong physiological rationale for this association. Dopamine at higher doses exerts dose-dependent effects on vascular tone by stimulating α-adrenergic receptors, leading to peripheral vasoconstriction and reduced capillary blood flow in the extremities where noninvasive optical measurements are obtained [2122]. Furthermore, the surgical stress response triggers a substantial release of endogenous catecholamines, which may act synergistically with exogenous dopamine. Under such high-sympathetic-tone conditions, even relatively low doses of dopamine can shift the physiological balance toward α-adrenergic predominance [2324], further impairing peripheral perfusion at the sensor site. Such changes in the vascular tone have been shown to affect the quality of pulse oximetry signals. Although consistent documentation of dopamine dosage was not available—which is a limitation of our study—our analysis revealed a significant association between dopamine administration and increased SpHb measurement variability. Mechanistically, this effect is plausible because dopamine-induced vasoconstriction can diminish capillary blood flow, thereby affecting the optical signal on which the SpHb is reliant. Importantly, despite the resulting systemic shift in the absolute SpHb values, the ICC remained stable throughout the procedure. This observation indicates that although pharmacological interventions, such as vasopressors, may introduce bias in baseline readings, the device’s ability to accurately track relative trends in Hb changes is preserved. Consequently, SpHb can serve as a reliable continuous trend-monitoring tool to guide the timing of confirmatory laboratory measurements during surgery. Rather than replacing invasive gold standard, SpHb serves as a continuous 'real-time trigger' to optimize the timing of intermittent laboratory testing, thereby bridging the information gap and improving resource efficiency in major surgeries.

In conclusion, advanced age and dopamine use may reduce the absolute accuracy of SpHb in patients with HNSCC and introduce clinically relevant variability, limiting its use in transfusion decision-making. However, the preserved trend-tracking ability of SpHb supports its role as a real-time adjunct for monitoring Hb changes. Accordingly, SpHb may be useful in guiding the timing of confirmatory laboratory testing rather than serving as a standalone tool for transfusion management in major oncological surgery.

Limitations

First, the retrospective, single-center design and small sample size may limit the generalizability and statistical power. Second, the absence of a central-laboratory Hb level at T1 is a limitation. To avoid interval bias from asynchronous preoperative values, we utilized POCT-Hb as a clinically relevant baseline. Although T2 data confirmed a strong correlation between POCT and laboratory Hb levels (r = 0.903), the early-phase agreement should be interpreted cautiously. Third, factors affecting SpHb, such as temperature and sensor positioning, could not be fully controlled. Most importantly, the absence of continuous perfusion index data is a significant limitation. This finding warrants caution when interpreting SpHb reliability in high-risk patients who may experience peripheral vasoconstriction. Fourth, to minimize the confounding bias due to rapid physiological shifts, we excluded cases of intraoperative transfusion. This may limit the applicability of our findings to the "danger zone" (Hb < 7–9 g/dL) where transfusion decisions are most critical. Future studies focusing on this threshold and the impact of peripheral perfusion, including the direct measurement of the perfusion index, are warranted.
