Prevalence of Eating Disorders Among Young Adults in the Western Region of Saudi Arabia
Authors: Sariya Khan, Ayesha Mohtashim Jamal, Dana Kauther, Husna Irfan Thalib, Mazen Ahmed Alayidh, Eyad Ibrahim Alghamdi, Hadiyah Nadeem, Nadeem Muhammad Ikram
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Eating disorders (ED) are complex mental health conditions that can affect individuals of all ages, with a higher prevalence observed among young adults aged 18–26. They involve disruptions in eating behaviors, thoughts, and emotions, leading to significant physical and psychological implications. There is currently a lack of ED prevalence studies in the Western region of Saudi Arabia. By conducting such studies, healthcare professionals and policymakers can utilize the data to identify at-risk populations, evaluate the burden of ED in the region, and develop evidence-based strategies to address this pressing public health issue. Furthermore, prevalence studies can help raise awareness about ED, reduce stigma, and improve access to timely and appropriate care for individuals in need.Methods:A 6-month cross-sectional descriptive study between September 2023 and February 2024, including 390 young adults aged 18–26 residing in the Western region of Saudi Arabia, using an Arabic translation of the Eating Disorder Examination Questionnaire, was carried out.Results:Over the course of this study, 403 participants were surveyed, and 390 of them were included in the analysis. The mean age of included participants was 20.41 for males (n = 167) and 19.71 for females (n = 223). Complying with the recommended cutoff score of 2.8 for ED screening, the suspected prevalence of ED in this study is 20.5% and showed high gender association: 26.5% of females and 12.6% of males (P = 0.001).Conclusions:The current study presents a high prevalence (20.5%) of disordered eating behaviors in adolescents in the Western region of Saudi Arabia, especially in females, and shows a high correlation with BMI. Further prevalence studies with larger sample sizes in the Western region of Saudi Arabia, especially within male populations, are required for developing enhanced screening methods and more targeted awareness campaigns.

Introduction

Eating disorders (ED), characterized by persistent disturbances in eating or eating-related behavior that significantly impair physical health or psychosocial functioning, represent a serious public health concern worldwide. These complex psychiatric conditions, including anorexia nervosa, bulimia nervosa, and binge ED, often manifest during adolescence and young adulthood, leading to detrimental physical, psychological, and social consequences. The burden of EDs among young adults is substantial, with a significant impact on morbidity, mortality, and quality of life.[1] With a global incidence of EDs being 7.8%, individuals with ED are at increased risk of developing medical complications such as cardiovascular abnormalities, electrolyte imbalances, gastrointestinal disturbances, and osteoporosis.[23] Moreover, the psychological effects of ED, such as depression, anxiety, and suicidal ideation, can further exacerbate the overall disease burden and contribute to impaired functioning and reduced quality of life. In addition to the individual-level consequences, ED also poses a significant economic burden on healthcare systems and society overall. The costs associated with medical treatment, hospitalizations, and long-term care for individuals with ED are substantial, highlighting the need for effective prevention and intervention strategies to mitigate the impact of these conditions.[1]

Recent research conducted by the Saudi National Mental Health Survey shed light on the prevalence of ED in Saudi Arabia. The study revealed that the 12-month prevalence of any of the three ED was 3.2%, while the overall lifetime prevalence stood at 6.1%. Notably, the 12-month prevalence of ED in the Saudi population surpassed rates reported globally, indicating a pressing concern within the country.[4] Using the cut-off score of 20 on the Eating Attitudes Test-26 (EAT-26), a study conducted in Taif university revealed a hefty 35.4% of students at risk for developing ED, with medical and obese students achieving the highest significant EAT scores.[5] In another study carried out in Arar, Saudi Arabia, it was noted that, according to Bediagnostic criteria of EAT-26, 80 students (25.4%) were at risk.[6] A survey done in 2023 in Saudi Arabia revealed that more than half (53.69) of adolescent girls scored at or above the cut-off point of EAT-26.[7] Research conducted in Alfaisal University, Saudi Arabia, showed that among the study subjects (289 students) that participated, the prevalence of high-risk or suspected ED students was 27.7%, with nationality, gender, and body mass index (BMI) being the most effective predictors.[8]

These findings highlight the importance of raising awareness about ED among the Saudi population and emphasize the need for tailored initiatives and interventions to address this significant public health issue. Given the limited data on the prevalence of ED in young adults in the Western region of Saudi Arabia, there is a critical need for epidemiological studies to understand the scope of the problem and inform targeted interventions. Additionally, the impact of cultural and societal influences on the development and expression of ED in young adults in Saudi Arabia should be explored. Cultural norms, body image ideals, and social pressures can contribute to the onset of disordered eating behaviors, making it essential to consider these factors in the context of prevention and treatment. In addition to this, early detection and intervention play a crucial role in effectively addressing ED in young adults.[9] This area has not been widely studied in the Western region of Saudi Arabia; this can be attributed to the fact that there is a lot of social stigma surrounding the issue. Thus, provision of education and awareness campaigns, as well as accessible mental health resources, is of high importance in order for healthcare providers to help individuals seek and receive appropriate treatment before the condition escalates. By fostering a culture of awareness, early intervention, and holistic care, healthcare providers and community members can work towards improving the well-being and quality of life of individuals afflicted with ED in the kingdom. Thereby, the objective of this study is to estimate the prevalence of EDs among young adults in the Western region of Saudi Arabia and to examine associations with gender, BMI, and menstrual irregularities.

Methodology

Study design

A 6-month cross-sectional study was conducted from September 2023 to February 2024 among young adults of both genders aged 18–26 residing in the Western region of Saudi Arabia. The sample size was calculated using the online software OpenEpi, utilizing the prevalence data from a previously conducted study in AlFaisal University, Riyadh, Saudi Arabia. Considering a confidence level of 95% and a margin of error of not more than 5%, it was found to be 308 participants.

Sampling method

Participants were selected using convenience sampling, and data were collected through an anonymous questionnaire to determine the prevalence of ED. This approach enables the inclusion of individuals who are easily accessible and willing to take part, thereby facilitating data collection within the allotted time frame. Moreover, convenience sampling offers benefits for studies facing resource and time limitations, as it allows researchers to swiftly gather data from readily available populations without extensive recruitment endeavors.

Study subjects

The sample included both university students and nonstudent young adults residing in the Western region, thereby capturing a broader demographic within the target age range. The final study sample consisted of 390 participants: 167 males (42.82%) and 223 females (57.17%). Inclusion criteria for study subjects included adults aged 18-26 residing in the Western Region of Saudi Arabia, regardless of their nationality. Exclusion criteria comprised patients hospitalized for any reason and individuals aged below 18 and above 26 to ensure the focus on the target population.

Questionnaire

The questionnaire utilized was the Eating Disorder Examination Questionnaire (EDEQ), a 28-item self-report tool adapted from the semistructured interview, the Eating Disorder Examination (EDE). The EDEQ was designed to assess the range, frequency, and severity of behaviors associated with a diagnosis of an ED. To accommodate the primary language of the study area, the questionnaire was translated from English to Arabic by an authorized translator [Supplementary 1], and further back-translated and reviewed by proficient speakers of both languages to ensure accuracy.

Data collection

A digital form using Google Forms, incorporating the questions from the designed instrument, was created. Once the form was ready, it was distributed to the target population through various channels such as email and social media. Participants then accessed the form and provided responses at their convenience. Throughout the data collection period, responses were monitored, and any technical issues were addressed to encourage participation.

Ethical consideration

Abiding by all ethical principles, the information collected will strictly be kept confidential by employing secure data management protocols. The approval for the study was acquired by the institutional review board (IRB), to make sure that the study is compliant with guidelines relevant to the ethical standards. Informed consent was taken from the participants. The entirety of the data collected was kept confidential by using encryption methods, allowing access only to the investigators.

Statistical analysis

The collected data were analyzed using the statistical package for social studies (SPSS) version 26, created by IBM, Chicago, IL, USA. The quantitative variables were analyzed using frequencies and means, while the qualitative variables were analyzed using Chi-square, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Potential confounders considered included age, gender, and BMI. However, due to the study design and sample size limitations, no multivariable adjustment was conducted. Future studies should include regression models to adjust for these factors.

Results

During the course of this study, a total of 403 participants were surveyed, with 390 that were compliant with our criteria. 13 were excluded due to incomplete data, which primarily reflected skipped items on the EDEQ-6 or missing self-reported weight/height. No systematic pattern of missingness was observed. The mean age of included participants was 20.41 for males (n = 167) and 19.71 for females (n = 223). Only 24.2% of female participants reported not experiencing fear of weight gain within the 28 days prior to the study, compared to 45.5% of the male participants (P < 0.001). Additionally, 10.8% of females indicated that they feel guilt associated with eating at most times, compared to 5.4% of males (P = 0.001). When questioned about concerns around weighing themselves, around 33% of females indicated none at all compared to 53% of the male participants (P = 0.003). Nearly 30% of all participants reported having thoughts about food and eating that disrupt their concentration on daily activities, also showing a higher association with female gender (P = 0.044). The participants were asked if they were unsatisfied with how they look; notably, around 74% of all participants responded yes. Almost 30% reported exercising vigorously for at least 6 days a week solely with the intention of losing weight. Respondents were asked how many days they vomited forcefully in order to lose weight, and it was found that 8.5% of all respondents vomited forcefully at least on 6 days in a month.

BMI scores for both genders were calculated based on the participants’ estimated weights and heights collected by the survey [Table 3], and were classified into four categories: <18.5, 18.5–24.9, 25–29.9, and ≥30. Discussion

Using the recommended cutoff score of 2.8 for ED screening, the suspected prevalence in this study was 20.5%, with a significant gender difference (26.5% in females versus 12.6% in males, P = 0.001).[10] When applying the lower cutoff score of 1.6, validated for male populations by Schaefer et al. (2018), the prevalence among males rose to 39.5%, increasing the overall prevalence to 32%.[11] This finding highlights the sensitivity of prevalence estimates to gender-specific thresholds and underscores the need for validated tools across subpopulations. The observed prevalence aligns with studies from Arar (25.4%) and Riyadh (27.7%) but is lower than the rate reported in Jeddah, the largest city in the western region (56.3%).[67] A key explanation for this discrepancy is the difference in screening instruments: while the present study employed the EDEQ-6, which assesses symptoms over the preceding 28 days, earlier Saudi studies often used the EAT-26, which does not specify a timeframe and may therefore overestimate prevalence.

ED risk has long been reported to be higher in females, especially those residing in high-income countries.[12] This finding has been confirmed in our study, where data pointing to ED diagnosis was more prominent in the female participants. Disordered eating behaviors can have profound implications for female health. A common manifestation of ED that was reported in our study is menstrual irregularities, such as amenorrhea or oligomenorrhea, which are often linked to hormonal disturbances induced by inadequate nutrition or excessive exercise. These disruptions can lead to imbalances in estrogen and progesterone levels, compromising normal menstruation and fertility.[13141516] Prolonged menstrual irregularities not only impact fertility but can also have long-term consequences on bone health, predisposing individuals to a higher risk of osteoporosis. The relationship between disordered eating and osteoporosis is well-established, with nutritional deficiencies and hormonal imbalances playing a pivotal role in its pathogenesis.[1718] This is of paramount importance in the Saudi female subpopulation, as it remains at elevated risk of bone disease due to exposure to additional risk factors, especially vitamin D deficiency.[19]

While the reported percentage of individuals using self-induced vomiting as a weight-controlling method (8.5%) is relatively low in this study compared to others, it should be once again noted that the results obtained reflect behavior from the 28 days preceding the study [Figure 1]. Furthermore, purging behavior is a major facilitator of oral and esophageal ED complications, including dental erosive damage, caries,[2021] and esophageal cancer.[21]

While EDs were once considered more common in Western countries, recent evidence shows a rising prevalence in Eastern societies as well.[2223] This trend has been linked to rapid social and economic changes, urbanization, and the adoption of Westernized lifestyle patterns. Beyond these structural factors, sociocultural influences play an important role, particularly among young people. Several studies highlight the impact of social media in shaping body image ideals, often promoting thinness for females and muscularity for males. Such exposure has been consistently associated with body dissatisfaction, low self-esteem, and an increased risk of disordered eating behaviors in adolescents.[24252627]

In the Saudi context, these sociocultural pressures may help explain the growing prevalence of EDs. Cultural norms surrounding appearance, combined with high engagement with digital platforms among Saudi youth, create an environment where unrealistic body standards are reinforced. This interplay of social media influence, evolving cultural expectations, and globalized ideals of attractiveness contributes to greater vulnerability to disordered eating in this population.[22242627]

These findings showcase the rising prevalence of ED in the Western region of Saudi Arabia and highlight the importance of screening and early detection strategies. The use of convenience sampling may have introduced selection bias, as participation was limited to those with access to the survey link and willingness to respond. This restricts the generalizability of the findings to the broader population of young adults in the Western region. Another limitation is that our analyses were not adjusted for potential confounding variables such as socioeconomic status or physical activity, which may influence the observed associations between gender, BMI, and disordered eating. Additionally, the study was restricted to young adults aged 18–26. Findings may not generalize to adolescents or older adults, who may exhibit different risk profiles for ED. Cultural stigma surrounding EDs may have introduced reporting bias, with some participants underreporting disordered behaviors due to fear of judgment. This could result in an underestimation of true prevalence. However, our study contributes significant results that motivate the timely identification of high-risk individuals is crucial for facilitating prompt access to evidence-based interventions and support services, which in turn can mitigate the severity of symptoms and enhance treatment outcomes. Implementation of standardized screening protocols, such as provision of the EDEQ-6 in the native language, in healthcare facilities, educational institutions, and community settings, is vital to identify individuals demonstrating ED behaviors and symptoms. Finally, raising awareness about ED through campaigns, workshops, and community programs facilitates destigmatization of the condition, enhancement of knowledge about its etiology, and ridding communities of body-negative culture.

Conclusions

The current study presents a high prevalence (20.5%) of disordered eating behaviors in young adults in the Western region of Saudi Arabia, especially in females, and shows a high correlation with BMI. Further prevalence studies with larger sample sizes in the Western region of Saudi Arabia, especially within male populations, are required for developing enhanced screening methods and more targeted awareness campaigns. Moreover, a multidisciplinary approach involving mental health professionals, nutritionists, and other specialists is vital in the comprehensive care of individuals with ED. Collaborative efforts to address the physical, emotional, and psychological aspects of the condition can enhance treatment outcomes and support long-term recovery for young adults facing these challenges. Therefore, understanding the prevalence and complexities of ED, as well as the causes and symptoms observed in young adults in Saudi Arabia, is instrumental in developing targeted interventions and support services to address this mental health issue effectively.
