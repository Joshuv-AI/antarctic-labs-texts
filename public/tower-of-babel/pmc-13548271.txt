Moving beyond monitoring: wearable technology (PKG) for the diagnosis of Parkinson’s disease
Authors: Michael William Hayes, Niroshan Jeyakumar, James Burrell
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe use of wearable technology results in objective and reliable monitoring of motor activity in Parkinson’s disease (PD) but its utility as a diagnostic aid has not been assessed.ObjectivesTo assess whether a Personal KinetiGraph (PKG) performed at baseline and again following a long-form levodopa (LD) challenge (increasing to 300 mg daily over a minimum of 6 weeks) is a useful discriminator in those with parkinsonism but diagnostic uncertainty.MethodsOver a 10-year period, 26 patients with parkinsonism in whom there was diagnostic uncertainty due to clinical complexity or conflicting evidence were tested with a PKG, recording bradykinesia and tremor before and after formalised treatment with LD. Patients were followed for a median of 46.5 months (mean 49.8) to establish a ‘final’ clinical diagnosis.ResultsOf the 15/26 diagnostically uncertain patients with a ‘final’ diagnosis of PD, the PKG demonstrated bradykinesia (mean bradykinesia score ≥25) at baseline in 14/15; in 12/15, bradykinesia improved with LD and, when present, tremor was similarly reduced. In the 11/26 patients who had a final diagnosis other than PD, the PKG recorded bradykinesia in 3/11 with improved bradykinesia and reduced tremor in only 1/11. PKG-defined bradykinesia coupled with LD responsiveness was 88% sensitive (95% CI 57% to 98%) and 89% specific (95% CI 52% to 99%) for a ‘final’ PD diagnosis in this select group. Additionally, PKG-defined tremor and LD responsiveness had a sensitivity of 85% (95% CI 55% to 98%) and specificity of 89% (95% CI 52% to 99%).ConclusionPKG-defined bradykinesia and tremor coupled with LD responsiveness appear to be predictive of an eventual PD diagnosis in diagnostically challenging cases. Larger studies are required to verify these conclusions.

Introduction

It is well established that the diagnosis of Parkinson’s disease (PD) can be challenging and not always reliable.1 2 Meta-analysis indicates that PD diagnostic accuracy is less than 75% in non-experts and less than 85% in movement disorder specialists even after follow-up.3 Diagnostic revision, mainly in the first 2 years, is in the order of 15% and globally only a small proportion of patients with PD will ever see a specialist.4 The Movement Disorder Society (MDS)-PD criteria describe motor parkinsonism as the core feature of the disease, defined as bradykinesia plus rest tremor or rigidity.1 However, they also state that mild parkinsonian syndromes can occur in up to 25% of elderly people without PD and comment on potential ‘confounding factors’ such as arthritis and weakness. Consequently, the initial clinical problem can often be deciding whether the essential criterion of parkinsonism is present before considering the other PD diagnostic criteria.

Wearable technology, mainly smartwatches and trackers, is now a mainstream global phenomenon. A specialised medical wearable device, the Personal KinetiGraph (PKG), has been used for about a decade to monitor movement disorders such as PD but has not been used as a diagnostic tool, presumably due to the absence of established diagnostic metrics and the often lengthy time required to establish a reliable final diagnosis.

Levodopa (LD) responsiveness is an MDS diagnostic supportive criterion and is particularly important in those cases where uncertainty exists. Patient self-assessment of bradykinesia and tremor responsiveness to LD is subjective, and the LD challenge test is dependent on subjective rating scales ascertained over a very short period. A study examining the LD challenge test with a video protocol and blinded assessments reported less improvement than expected and a 43% discrepancy between examiners and patient self-reporting.5 Ideally, it would be better to assess bradykinesia and tremor, as well as LD responsiveness, objectively.

The purpose of this study was to assess whether PKG-derived measures of bradykinesia and tremor acquired at baseline (ie, pre-LD exposure) followed by a long form of LD challenge (minimum 6 weeks’ exposure) were a sensitive and accurate diagnostic tool for PD in those with parkinsonism but initial diagnostic uncertainty. A prolonged follow-up period was required to increase the reliability of a final diagnosis.

Methods

Over a 10-year period from December 2014, a single movement disorder specialist (MWH) performed PKG assessments, before and after a prolonged trial of LD, on a small number of patients in whom there was clinical uncertainty at presentation about a diagnosis of PD. There were no specific inclusion or exclusion criteria beyond clinical equipoise, such that MDS-PD diagnostic clinical criteria1 were not met or were confounded by other factors. The patients were drawn from a movement disorder clinic at Concord Repatriation General Hospital, a teaching hospital in the Sydney Local Health District, or from private practice in the New South Wales Central Coast region. Patients were either primary referrals or were referred by other neurologists due to diagnostic uncertainty. They were advised about the concept and purpose of the PKG in this study setting (ie, to objectively monitor movement and to see if it changed with LD medication), and this was documented. Patients were not billed for the PKG assessments, and the study did not receive any funds or grants.

There were 26 patients in whom there was initial uncertainty as to whether the diagnosis was PD (PD uncertain group). This number is estimated to represent about 10% of those presenting with newly diagnosed PD. All presented with apparent bradykinesia though usually with confounding features or coexistent pathology, as well as some form of tremor or rigidity. The MDS clinical diagnostic criteria for ‘Clinically Probable’ PD could not be confidently applied to these patients due to uncertainty about whether bradykinesia was clearly present and doubt about the nature of the tremor. Additionally, several patients were included who had been referred ostensibly without an observable response to high-dose LD (>600 mg daily) despite at least moderate severity of disease, an absolute exclusion criterion, if correct. None had red flags though two patients were on neuroleptic medication (absolute exclusion criterion) at the time of the PKG assessments. Three additional LD-naïve patients with clinically established PD, as per MDS clinical diagnostic criteria with rest tremor and olfactory loss, were included as disease controls. However, olfactory loss was only determined by subjective report rather than by ancillary diagnostic testing. One patient, lost to follow-up, was not included.

The PKG is a device approved by the Food and Drug Administration, like a smartwatch, that quantifies kinematics of movement disorder symptoms (figure 1). It collects movement data continuously for an average of 6 days, is typically worn on the more symptomatic side and offers medication reminders. The device measures bradykinesia, tremor, dyskinesia, immobility and gait characteristics with results made available in a report. The report charts and graphically illustrates the data, including daily plots and summaries. Median bradykinesia scores (BKS) and percentage of time with tremor (PTT) are derived from data obtained between 6:00 am and 10:00 pm (figure 2) though the specific reference period for data analysis of mean BKS and PTT was 9:00 am to 6:00 pm, a time considered to best capture the awake and active day.6 There is a highly significant correlation between BKS and UPDRS (Unified Parkinson’s Disease Rating Scale) Part III, though the clinical scale incorporates both rigidity and tremor as well as various components of bradykinesia.7 Additionally, the PKG only measures distal movement in one (more symptomatic) limb. The current PKG4 Interpretation Guide defines mild bradykinesia as a median BKS ≥25.0, with severe bradykinesia being >39.3 based on the recommendation from a peer-reviewed expert panel.6 Consequently, for the purpose of post hoc study analysis, BKS ≥25 was used to define the presence of bradykinesia. PTT is the percentage time of tremor in seconds for which tremor is detected over the reference period (9:00 am to 6:00 pm). The presence of tremor is defined in the PKG4 Interpretation Guide as PTT >1%, while PTT 0–0.5% indicates no tremor. PTT of 0.6–1% is considered inconclusive.

The minimum necessary difference in PTT or BKS that defines clinical responsiveness to LD has not been established by Global Kinetics. For this study, a PTT difference between baseline and post-LD treatment of >1% point (ie, the lowest identifiable tremor metric) was arbitrarily defined as evidence of LD responsiveness. Meanwhile, a change in BKS of ≤0.5 with LD was defined as no change, of 0.6–1 as inconclusive and a reduction of >1 (ie, lower BKS) as evidence of LD responsiveness. The dyskinesia score was not considered clinically relevant in this patient group due to an early stage of disease at the time of initial assessment.

The PKG was applied to the side with more obvious signs or symptoms. Following a baseline PKG assessment over a minimum of 5–6 days, patients were commenced on LD, initially 50 mg two times per day for a minimum of 2 weeks, then 100 mg two times per day for a further minimum of 2 weeks, and finally 100 mg three times per day (300 mg daily) before a repeat PKG (same side) was performed after a minimum of 6 weeks. Monitoring over several weeks of LD therapy increased the likelihood of capturing any long-duration response (LDR).8 9 In most cases, the PKG was applied remotely with the device sent out by mail to the patient and returned similarly. There was telephone support via a PD nurse. In those patients previously reported to be unresponsive to LD, the medication had been ceased for a minimum of 3 months prior to the study.

Patients either persisted with LD, or it was ceased, depending on a clinical decision after the repeat PKG. If diagnostic uncertainty remained, the bias was to maintain LD and to continue follow-up. No additional PKG studies were performed. Consequently, the investigator’s decision to persist with LD (or not) relied on a combination of clinical impression and an assessment of the raw PKG scores. The PKG cut-off scores and definitions were only applied during the post hoc data analysis of the whole study group. The PKG data analysis was undertaken after the accumulated data entry had been anonymised and prior clinical diagnoses obscured.

A ‘final’ diagnosis of PD was made at the time of the last review (median follow-up of 46 months) according to the MDS-PD diagnostic clinical criteria.1 Alternative ‘final’ diagnoses such as multiple system atrophy parkinsonian type (MSA-P), progressive supranuclear palsy (PSP) and essential tremor (ET) and dystonic tremor were based on MDS diagnostic criteria. PKG data were not used to establish a ‘final’ diagnosis.

Results

There were 26 patients with uncertain PD diagnosis. Reasons contributing to doubt or uncertainty about a diagnosis of PD included: reportedly unresponsive to LD (5 patients), rapid-onset bilateral tremor (2 patients), possible drug-induced parkinsonism (3 patients), parkinsonism and major depression (1 patient), parkinsonism and possible functional neurological disorder (2 patients), possible PSP (2 patients), possible MSA-P (3 patients), ET±parkinsonism (3 patients), family history of unspecified tremor (2 patients), parkinsonism and extensive vascular disease (2 patients) and unilateral upper limb dystonic tremor (1 patient).

The patients with an uncertain PD diagnosis consisted of 16 males and 10 females with a mean age of 67.3 years (range 39–81). The median follow-up was 46.5 months (mean 49.8 months, range 19–114 months). Of the patients with uncertain PD diagnosis, 15 eventually met MDS clinical diagnostic criteria for ‘Clinically Established’ PD (12 patients) or ‘Clinically Probable’ PD (3 patients) with a mean age of 69.4 years while 11 patients did not (non-PD group), mean age 63.8 years.

The ‘final’ diagnoses in the 11 patients without PD were dystonic tremor (1), neuroleptic-induced parkinsonism (2), ET (3), MSA-P (1), PSP (1), vascular parkinsonism (1), mixed pathology including vascular disease and brain radiotherapy (1) and functional neurological disorder (1). In the three patients with clinically established PD at initial assessment, the diagnosis remained unchanged at the time of the last review.

Although about two-thirds had the follow-up PKG at 8–12 weeks, the remainder were delayed due to COVID-19 and other factors, with an overall mean follow-up time of 12.9 weeks. Likewise, although the majority had the follow-up PKG on 300 mg LD daily, there was some variation with a mean dose of 367 mg daily. The higher doses were due to idiosyncratic reasons rather than by design.

Bradykinesia

Bradykinesia was common at baseline. Specifically, 17/26 patients with uncertain PD diagnosis had bradykinesia on PKG, defined as BKS >25. Of the 15/26 patients with uncertain PD diagnosis, but with a final diagnosis of PD, bradykinesia was present in 14/15 at baseline; it was subsequently noted that the PKG device had been inadvertently placed on the less symptomatic side for both assessments on this one patient. LD responsiveness, defined as BKS reduction of >1, was established in 12/15 (table 1). The 3/15 without defined LD responsiveness had low baseline BKS of 21.9, 25.4 and 27. LD responsiveness to bradykinesia was confirmed on PKG in 4/5 patients who had been referred for a second opinion due to reported lack of response to LD.

Tremor

At baseline, tremor data were available on 24/26 patients with uncertain PD diagnosis. Tremor, as defined on PKG as PTT >1%, was present in 20/24 patients with uncertain PD diagnosis. The remaining four had inconclusive tremor scores (PTT 0.9% in 3 and 0.2%).

Tremor was present at baseline in 11/13 patients with uncertain PD diagnosis who eventually met criteria for PD (the other 2/13 had PTT 0.9%). Post-LD treatment, 9/11 showed reduction in tremor, 1/11 no change and 1/11 increased tremor (table 1). All five patients initially thought to be unresponsive showed a reduction in tremor following LD treatment. Of 3/15 patients with a final diagnosis of PD who did not show an LD response to bradykinesia, two demonstrated tremor reduction with LD and one no change.

Tremor was common in patients with a final non-PD diagnosis, but LD responsiveness was not. Specifically, of the 11/26 patients with a non-PD final diagnosis, 9/11 had tremor at baseline, one had no tremor (ie, PTT 0.2%) and one inconclusive tremor (PTT 0.9%). No change in tremor with LD treatment was evident in 5/9; increased tremor was seen in 3/9 and decreased tremor in 1/9 (table 2).

The three additional LD-naïve patients with clinically established PD at initial assessment (patients 27–29) had a mean BKS 35.7 compared with the ‘final’ PD group mean BKS 34.6. The corresponding mean BKS reduction with LD was 4.8 in the clinically established PD group compared with 3.6 in the ‘final’ PD group.

Discussion

The study showed that, when the device was used correctly, all patients with uncertain PD diagnosis, but with a final diagnosis of PD, had bradykinesia at baseline as defined by PKG assessment. Most patients with uncertain PD diagnosis, but with a final diagnosis of PD, demonstrated LD-responsive bradykinesia; those who did not show responsiveness had BKS close to the threshold score of ≥25 at initial assessment. This low BKS group showed tremor responsiveness to LD in three of four patients, consistent with a tremor-dominant PD phenotype. The study also demonstrated that patients with PD may have rather modest objective bradykinesia (BKS) and tremor (PTT) responsiveness to LD as recently reported,5 and that patients are not always reliable in self-reporting LD responsiveness. Incorrect patient feedback may adversely affect clinician judgement.

The patients with a final diagnosis other than PD (non-PD group) had a mean BKS of 26.6, though when a single outlier patient on high-dose neuroleptic medication (BKS 59.1) was excluded, the mean BKS was 23.3. To put this level of bradykinesia into clinical context, it has been recommended6 that PD treatment PKG target scores should optimally aim for BKS <23, that BKS 23–25 are acceptable and treatment is recommended when BKS is >25. This is to emphasise that, although only 3/11 in the final non-PD group had study-defined bradykinesia (BKS >25) at baseline, clinically there was sufficient impairment of movement to flag possible bradykinesia. Not surprisingly, only one individual in the non-PD group demonstrated reduced BKS with LD, a young patient (aged 39) with a final diagnosis of functional neurological disorder who also had inconclusive tremor (0.9%). Following LD, there was no change in tremor in 4/9 and increased tremor in 4/9 in the non-PD group.

A clear and dramatic response to LD is a MDS supportive diagnostic criterion.1 It has been reported that a marked to moderate LD response is evident in >90% of autopsy-proven PD cases,10 although it may be poor or nil in about 5%.11 Some patients with MSA-P can show LD responsiveness though other early clinical features are usually helpful in making that alternate diagnosis. LD responsiveness in PSP is typically very limited. The LD challenge test for de novo PD can be used to determine LD responsiveness, but the use of a PKG to confirm LD responsiveness may be more sensitive and more specific. An LD challenge using a one-third reduction in the UPDRS Part III (motor) offers a sensitivity of 70% and a specificity of 71%.12 Overall, PKG-defined bradykinesia coupled with LD responsiveness was 80% sensitive and 91% specific for a ‘final’ PD diagnosis in this select group. Additionally, PKG-defined tremor and LD responsiveness had a sensitivity of 91% and specificity of 89% although in an uncontrolled, long-form study. Further studies with a PKG or similar device could reassess the standard LD challenge test or use a longer but more pragmatic LD assessment such as over a week for adequate data acquisition.

A potential benefit of assessing outcome after more than 6 weeks of LD exposure is that it might incorporate the long-duration response (LDR) (8,9), possibly making assessment of LD responsiveness more sensitive than the standard LD challenge test. The Levodopa in Early Parkinson’s Disease (LEAP) and other cohort studies indicate that the LDR takes weeks to months to exert maximum benefit.13 Recent evidence suggests that the contribution of the LDR may be as much as 50-60% of the total LD response.14

The PKG objectively assesses both slowness of movement and decrement in amplitude, the definition of bradykinesia used in the MDS clinical diagnostic criteria. Determining bradykinesia clinically is often difficult due to factors such as age-related changes, effort, anxiety and comorbidity (eg, arthritis). Challenging diagnostic scenarios include patients with a history of dystonic tremor or ET who develop some parkinsonism or altered tremor; patients who are thought to have PD, often tremor-dominant, but report no response to LD; older patients with mild parkinsonism who lack typical non-motor supportive evidence; patients with a psychiatric condition and parkinsonism on neuroleptic medication; and those from culturally and linguistically diverse communities in whom outcome may be difficult to assess. It is important not to miss modest LD responsiveness since this can make a functional difference to the patient and help corroborate the diagnosis.

The use of wearable technology has become commonplace in the general population and could potentially be leveraged to improve the diagnosis of PD. The PKG and similar devices were developed for monitoring motor fluctuations and response to dopaminergic medication over the 24-hour dose cycle.6 15–17 The diagnostic potential role for the PKG has not really been explored because it was not the initial purpose of the device, and cut-offs for defining bradykinesia, tremor and particularly LD responsiveness are not broadly established. Nonetheless, the use of PKG as a diagnostic tool is appealing because it enables continuous measurement of bradykinesia and tremor before and after treatment over several days, effectively a biomarker. The device is user-friendly, and the data acquisition is reliable and not subject to inter-rater variability. Clinical assessment can only provide a single snapshot in time, whereas PD symptoms and signs are inherently variable.

Limitations of this observational study include the small number of participants, assessment by a single movement disorder specialist and the lack of pathological confirmation of the diagnosis. Dopamine transporter scans (DaTscan) and alpha-synuclein seed amplification assays were not available. The use in this study of predominantly 300 mg daily of LD, rather than a ‘high’ dose (eg, ≥600 mg as per MDS criteria), might have reduced overall sensitivity of LD responsiveness. The PKG only monitors movement in a single (most symptomatic) upper limb although other parameters such as gait, immobility and sleep scores can be inferred from this. There are no accepted PKG diagnostic metrics for PD, and the use of the current PKG4 Interpretation Guidelines to assist in post hoc analysis was a pragmatic decision. While cut-offs for BKS and PTT were chosen arbitrarily, the results of the present study suggest they have clinical utility.

In summary, when there is clinical uncertainty, the PKG may assist the diagnostic process by enabling continuous and objective measurement of core PD features such as bradykinesia and tremor, both before and after LD treatment.
