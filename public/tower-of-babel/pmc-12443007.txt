Patients’ race/ethnicity in relation to their experiences of and attitudes toward therapist referral/assignment
Authors: Averi N Gaines, Michael J Constantino, Heidi M Kmetz, James F Boswell
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTGrowing research demonstrates that psychotherapists have measurement-based effectiveness strengths and weaknesses, both in treating patients with specific mental health concerns and, distinctly, racial/ethnic identities. Moreover, emerging research indicates that mental health care (MHC) patients in general would value having such therapist “performance” information inform their personal referrals and case assignments. However, prior work has not examined whether patients’ experiences of and attitudes toward therapist referral/assignment, including the integration of effectiveness data, differ by racial/ethnic identity. Addressing this gap, the present study surveyed treatment-familiar, engaged, or seeking adults (N = 308) via Amazon’s Mechanical Turk to explore differences between People of Color (POC; i.e. those possessing a non-White and/or Latinx identity) and White stakeholders regarding their (a) experiences of and inputs into identifying and initiating MHC with a therapist, and (b) attitudes toward using therapist performance data to inform referral/assignment. Results generally suggested that MHC stakeholders have experienced some difficulty finding a good-fitting therapist and would value using therapist effectiveness data to enhance this process. Notably, POC versus White stakeholders endorsed more positive attitudes toward harnessing therapist data, supporting that doing so could potentially help address the needs of marginalized populations and redress the MHC disparities they have historically faced.KEYWORDS: therapist effectiveness, psychotherapy referrals/case assignment, patient stakeholder perspectives, racial/ethnic mental health care disparities

Research has established that psychotherapists evidence various forms of effectiveness differences (Coyne, in press; Wampold & Owen, 2021). As one type, growing research based on multidimensional patient-reported outcomes assessment has demonstrated reliable within-therapist effectiveness strengths and weaknesses when treating patients with different types of presenting problems, which hold even when controlling for other patient case-mix factors (e.g., initial severity, health status, demographic characteristics) that may influence treatment response (Constantino et al., 2021; Kraus et al., 2011, 2016). As a hypothetical example, the average patient of a given therapist may achieve exceptional outcomes (e.g., on a measure of global psychological distress) when their primary presenting problem is depression or anxiety (i.e., two measurement- and problem-based provider strengths). For the same therapist, though, their average patient who presents with suicidal ideation may fail to improve, or even get worse over treatment (i.e., a measurement- and problem-based provider weakness). Still for the same therapist, their average patient with sleep disturbance or quality of life concerns may show some improvement, but nothing beyond or below what might be expected (e.g., when determined by a predictive analytic). In this latter case, the domains of sleep disturbance and quality of life concerns would reflect neither a measurement- and problem-based exceptional strength nor weakness for this provider. Across these outcome domains, this hypothetical therapist’s effectiveness differences create a problem-specific performance profile, or “report card”, which research has indicated is likely to be stable over time (in the absence of explicit efforts to improve one’s performance; Constantino et al., 2021; Kraus et al., 2016).

Similarly, but now based on a patient’s personal characteristic versus problem domain, budding research has indicated reliable within-therapist effectiveness strengths and weaknesses when treating patients who identify with different racial/ethnic identities (e.g., White vs. an underrepresented racial/ethnic minority; Hayes et al., 2016; Kivlighan et al., 2019). As a hypothetical example, a given therapist may be consistently and exceptionally effective when treating their average patient who identifies as an underrepresented racial/ethnic minority (a measurement- and identity-based provider strength), but ineffective or harmful when treating their average patient who identifies as White (a measurement- and identity-based provider weakness). For these broad identity domains, this hypothetical therapist’s effectiveness differences create an identity-specific performance profile. Whether focusing on presenting problems or racial/ethnic identity (or, plausibly, their interactions), such within-therapist effects suggest that some therapists are better positioned to help certain patients more so than others. Thus, once enough outcomes data are accumulated to establish therapists’ multifaceted performance profiles (e.g., within a given mental health care [MHC] network), this measurement-based information holds promise for personalizing the referral/assignment process for any new patient—before the first session has even occurred (Constantino & Muir, in press).1

Unfortunately, though, it is currently the case that most therapists and the systems within which they work are unaware of their measurement-based effectiveness profiles, which undermines the ability to use such data to optimize patient-therapist fit at the time of referral/assignment—a key inflection point in MHC. Thus, navigating the search for a therapist remains challenging for MHC patients, as they have relatively limited autonomy to direct and a considerable absence of information (including therapist performance data) to guide their treatment decision-making process (Boswell et al., 2021). Regarding the former, given high demand for MHC services and numerous barriers to access (e.g., cost, location, appointment waitlists; National Council for Mental Wellbeing, 2022), patients often do not have the luxury of being selective about who they see for therapy and may instead need to prioritize being seen by the first available provider who meets basic logistical needs (as opposed to one who is most likely to be personally effective). Regarding the latter, even when equipped to “shop around” for a therapist, the information to which patients have access often consists mainly of therapists’ self-proclaimed areas of specialty, such as those posted on consumer satisfaction rating or web-based referral sites (e.g., Psychology Today). Recent evidence suggests, however, that therapists’ ability to accurately perceive their own measurement-based areas of strength and weakness is considerably limited (Constantino et al., 2023a). More specifically, without knowing their data-informed performance profiles, therapists tend to overestimate their strengths and underestimate their weaknesses. When considering together the aforementioned pragmatic factors that drive referral and case assignment as usual (whether out of or within the patient’s control), the potential for connecting with an empirically and personally good-fitting therapist is largely left to chance.

Given this information vacuum, and consistent with the extant within-therapist effectiveness literature, one means of optimizing therapist selection could involve the systematic tracking and transparent reporting of therapists’ empirically derived strengths (e.g., in treating patients with certain specific problems and/or broad racial/ethnic identities) to patients seeking psychotherapy (or to those making a referral on their behalf). Of course, it is one thing for researchers to see value in this idea; it is another to know if patients themselves would see merit in it. Underscoring such patient stakeholder valuing, recent research has substantiated that generating and candidly disseminating therapist problem-specific performance data would be consistent with MHC patients’ preferences and values (Boswell et al., 2021, 2023). For example, results from a survey of 403 community mental health outpatients demonstrated that over 90% of respondents indicated that (a) they would be interested in using these data to inform their personal referral and assignment to therapists, (b) patients should have access to therapists’ historical effectiveness track records when making treatment decisions, (c) they would be more confident about their therapist options if they knew their primary care doctor had accessed these data while making a referral, (d) it is important to be assigned to therapists according to their track records, especially when having previously failed to benefit from therapy, and (e) prospectively using track record data for therapist selection and assignment would increase the likelihood of treatment success (Boswell et al., 2021).

Although such work provides MHC systems with some assurance that leveraging therapist effectiveness data would be consistent with patient-centered care, prior work has lacked explicit consideration of the potential varying experiences and needs of specific patient populations. For example, given the longstanding marginalization and other harms that drive racial/ethnic disparities in MHC, People of Color (POC), broadly defined, experience unique treatment initiation and engagement difficulties. Namely, a wealth of research supports that structural barriers, attitudinal barriers, and past instances of discrimination within MHC systems influence the likelihood that POC can access treatment, receive it, stay in it, and perceive it as being helpful (Alegría et al., 2008; de Haan et al., 2018; Lê Cook et al., 2014; Mays et al., 2017; Owen et al., 2012; Sonik et al., 2020; Walker et al., 2015). It is therefore possible that any POC (i.e., anyone with a non-White and/or Hispanic, Latino, Latina, or Latinx identity) might endorse different attitudes than their White counterparts about factors related to therapist selection or assignment informed by this lived experience—including how and to what extent therapist measurement-based performance information (whether problem- and/or identity-based) should factor into this process.

Although examining the experiences of POC at this broadest level relative to White individuals has limitations, it may be an important starting point, as it parallels the aforementioned within-therapist effects findings and has merit when considering the minority stress model; that is, individuals likely experience (to at least some degree) similar mental health distress and disparities by virtue of having any stigmatized and marginalized social identity (see Meyer, 2003). Such comparative examination would represent one preliminary step that can help inform future attempts to innovate MHC referral and assignment processes without overlooking the potential unique needs of marginalized populations, recapitulating harms, and reinforcing uniformity myths. To that end, the present survey study examined differences between POC and White individuals on their (a) experiences of and inputs into identifying and initiating MHC with a therapist, and (b) attitudes toward using the two aforementioned types of therapist effectiveness data to inform the referral/assignment process.

Method

Participants

We administered the present study through Amazon’s Mechanical Turk (MTurk) platform, a crowd-sourcing platform commonly used in social psychological research (Anderson et al., 2019) and with growing use in clinical psychological research (e.g., Bernecker et al., 2017; Constantino et al., 2019; Swift et al., 2015). This platform allows for adults (age 18 years and older) to complete straightforward human intelligence tasks (HITs), such as psychological surveys, for compensation. The demographic and clinical characteristics of MTurk workers rendered the platform suitable for the present study’s aims, as approximately 36% identify their race/ethnicity as something other than wholly White (Buhrmester et al., 2011). In addition, MTurk workers have been shown to report mental health difficulties at a rate that is comparable to and in some cases higher than the general population (Shapiro et al., 2013), suggesting that a meaningful subset have likely interacted with (or intend to interact with) MHC therapists in some capacity.

Participants were 479 individuals (ns = 253 POC,2 226 White) who: (a) were current US residents (as determined by their IP address); (b) were treatment-familiar (i.e., had previous experience seeing a MHC therapist for services, such as individual psychotherapy, group psychotherapy, hospitalization, etc.), treatment-engaged (i.e., were currently seeing a MHC therapist for some form of services), or treatment-seeking (i.e., were currently searching for a MHC therapist to receive some form of services) at the time of participation; and (c) had completed at least 50 prior HITs in MTurk, had a 95% approval rate across these HITs, and had a designation of “MTurk Master” (i.e., they had historically provided valid and high-quality responses to HITs).

Measures

Participants completed a survey that included items assessing: (a) demographic and clinical characteristics; (b) experiences of and inputs into identifying and initiating MHC with a therapist; and (c) attitudes toward using therapist problem- and identity-specific effectiveness data to inform referral/assignment. The survey also included two instructional manipulation attention check questions, which were used to assess the validity of each participant’s data. As an additional validity check, we asked participants at the end of the survey to report on the quality of their data, noting there was no risk to their compensation for responding honestly.

Demographic and clinical information

Patients first reported on their demographic characteristics (i.e., age, gender, sexual orientation, race/ethnicity, marital status, annual household income,3 religious affiliation, and highest level of education) and the mental health concerns that were pertinent to their previous engagement with, current engagement with, and/or active search for MHC services.

Procedures

Data were collected between October 2021 and June 2022. To ensure we had large enough subsamples to examine group differences, we intentionally first sampled POC who met the above inclusion criteria. We subsequently expanded recruitment to participants across racial/ethnic identities, including White. From the MTurk platform, all participants were directed to Qualtrics, where they were asked to complete a one-time, web-based consent form and survey. Patients were compensated at a rate of $0.20 per minute, for a total of $5 for the estimated 25-minute survey—payment that is on the upper end of standard industry rates (which typically range from $0.10 to $0.20 per minute) and is responsive to recent MTurk worker advocacy for fair wages. Participants were informed their participation was voluntary and that they could discontinue at any time by simply closing the webpage. The institutional review board at the first author’s institution (IRB Protocol number: #2931) approved analyses of de-identified data.

Data analyses

As the first data analytic step, we examined the validity of participants’ response patterns in order to exclude those who (a) failed either of the two attention check questions; (b) completed the survey more than once based on duplicate IP addresses; (c) completed the survey in an inordinately brief amount of time (i.e., fewer than 8 minutes, as determined by piloting with undergraduate research assistants who were instructed to complete the survey as quickly as possible while still reading and processing the instructions); (d) did not endorse that they were either treatment-familiar, treatment-engaged, or treatment-seeking; (e) endorsed every possible racial/ethnic identity; (f) did not endorse any racial/ethnic identity; (g) did not complete the full survey; and/or (h) indicated we should not use their data due to poor self-reported quality.

Notably, we also excluded participants who clearly failed to follow instructions on a section of the survey that was not the focus of the present study (but called into question the validity of all responses). More specifically, as part of the full survey, we asked participants to complete a version of a discounting task with 18 items (see Constantino et al., 2023b for review). As part of our data screening procedure for the full survey, two trained undergraduate research assistants independently reviewed each participants’ responses to all 18 of the discounting tasks in order to identify instances of not following instructions, carelessness, and/or illogical response patterns. The first author then reviewed and made a final determination for any discrepancies in these data validity ratings. In essence, this discounting task served as a supplemental attention check procedure for the present study, and we opted to exclude any participants who provided invalid on two or more of 18 items. Based on previously published MTurk studies in psychotherapy research (e.g., Bernecker et al., 2017; Constantino et al., 2019; Swift et al., 2015), we anticipated that approximately 30% of the target sample would provide invalid data for any of the reasons noted.

As the second data analytic step, we conducted descriptive analyses on participants’ demographic and clinical characteristics, experiences of and inputs into identifying and initiating MHC with a therapist, and attitudes toward using therapist problem- and identity-specific effectiveness data to inform referral/assignment. For the third step, we used independent sample t-tests and Pearson’s chi-square analyses to determine if POC and White individuals differed significantly on any demographic or clinical characteristics. For the fourth step, for any variable for which the groups differed, we tested with bivariate correlations, t-tests, or Pearson’s chi-square analyses whether this variable associated significantly with any of the experiential and attitudinal items.

Finally, for our primary analyses that compared whether experiential and attitudinal items differed between POC and White respondents, we used linear or logistic regression analyses. For these analyses, we controlled for any demographic or clinical characteristics that were significantly related to the pertinent experiential or attitudinal item, as determined in the previous step. As measures of effect size, we report standardized betas for the linear regressions or odds ratios for the logistic regressions, using established cutoffs to classify the effects as small, medium, or large (Cohen, 1988). To guard against Type I error, we set the p value conservatively at p < .01 for both determining covariates and for our primary analyses.

Results

Data validity checks

We excluded 171 (35.70%) total participants because of invalid data. More specifically, 19 participants (3.97%) failed either of the two attention check questions; 11 participants (2.30%) provided duplicate IP addresses; 60 participants (12.53%) completed the survey in fewer than 8 minutes; 25 participants (5.22%) did not endorse that they were either treatment-familiar, treatment-engaged, or treatment-seeking; 3 participants (0.63%) endorsed every possible racial/ethnic identity; 2 participants (0.42%) did not endorse any racial/ethnic identity; 60 participants (12.53%) did not complete the full survey; and 6 participants (1.27%) indicated we should not use their data. After these initial exclusions (26.51%), we were left with a sample of 352 respondents. We then excluded an additional 44 participants (9.19%) who clearly failed to follow instructions on the vast majority of the discounting task items, as described previously.4 Thus, our effective sample for all subsequent analyses included 308 participants (ns = 162 POC, 146 White). As noted previously, this percentage was consistent with prior MTurk studies on psychotherapy topics (e.g., Constantino et al., 2019).

Demographic and clinical information

Discussion

In light of mounting evidence that who a patient sees for psychotherapy is a meaningful determinant of their treatment outcome, this study preliminarily aimed to better understand patient stakeholders’ experiences of and inputs into identifying and initiating MHC with a therapist, as well as their attitudes toward using therapist performance data to inform referral/assignment. In light of well-documented MHC disparities that POC face, this study also aimed to determine preliminarily whether these experiences, inputs, and attitudes differed among historically minority versus majority populations in the US. Across the full sample, our results substantiate that getting connected to a therapist is a challenging experience for individuals interested in MHC. Thus, it is unsurprising that many respondents sought assistance with this process, such as from a friend or family member, primary/family care doctor, and/or their insurance company.

Additionally, and replicating Boswell et al. (2021) study, the present respondents were astutely aware that not all therapists are equally effective, and they most frequently based their confidence in a specific therapist’s ability to help on that clinician’s personal characteristics and/or recommendations they received from others. That said, in thinking about the future (and also consistent with Boswell et al’s. findings), participants endorsed positive attitudes about using therapist effectiveness data to inform therapist referral/assignment. Notably, though, the present study extended these attitudes to problem- and identity-based effectiveness profiles, which further supports that routinely assessing patient outcomes and salient characteristics multidimensionally, analyzing these data at the therapist level, and leveraging them to enhance treatment decision-making is consistent with patient-centered and valued care (Constantino & Muir, 2024; Muir et al., 2019).

Although these trends emerged across the full sample, we observed important experiential and attitudinal differences among POC and White participants. Namely, although participants across identities endorsed similar levels of difficulty getting connected with a therapist, POC indicated that their racial/ethnic identity was a stronger contributor to such challenges. Additionally, when asked to indicate the factors that influenced their confidence in a therapist’s ability to help, POC placed a greater emphasis on the therapist’s reputation. Also, POC endorsed more positive attitudes about using both therapist problem- and identity-based effectiveness data for therapist referral/assignment than White participants. This was especially apparent for identity-based report cards, for which POC endorsed a higher level of trust in therapist performance data, importance placed on performance data, likelihood of following through on a referral when aware of performance data, and confidence in the therapist’s ability to help when aware of performance data.

Of course, it is worth reiterating that these findings are a preliminary attempt at understanding differences between POC (i.e., those possessing a non-White and/or Latinx identity) and White stakeholders regarding their experiences of and inputs into identifying and initiating MHC with a therapist, and their attitudes toward using therapist performance data to inform referral/assignment. Moreover, our study did not speak to potential differences between individuals with different minoritized and marginalized racial/ethnic identities. This will be an important area for future research. However, as noted previously, this initial step in comparing POC and White individuals has value when considering that individuals likely experience (to at least some degree) similar mental health distress and disparities by virtue of having any stigmatized and marginalized social identity (as per the minority stress model; Meyer, 2003). Moreover, even when considering any group differences with caution, the fact that POC did not endorse significantly more negative attitudes than White individuals seems meaningful when interpreted alongside the findings of Boswell et al. (2021). Namely, the present findings suggest that efforts to leverage therapist effectiveness information for patient referral/assignment would not overlook the needs and preferences of a population often marginalized in MHC.

Broadly, our findings illustrate the importance of studying treatment-related experiences, inputs, and attitudes in relation to important sociocultural identities (including those beyond race/ethnicity); failing to do so could mask meaningful differences for certain minoritized populations. Were the differences we observed between POC and White individuals to be replicated, we could speculate that they would also carry important clinical implications specifically for referral/assignment of POC. More pointedly, it may be important to prioritize the transparent reporting of therapist effectiveness reputation when helping POC get connected to a good-fitting provider (e.g., a therapist in whom they will have more confidence). It is plausible that such valuing of this specific therapist-relevant variable among POC could reflect their understandable concerns about a therapist’s cultural competence, orientation, and responsiveness in light of their historic marginalization within MHC systems. Such concerns could conceivably persist even if a given therapist was offering a treatment (perhaps even a relevant culturally adapted one) that a POC viewed favorably. Indeed, in any psychotherapy, when patients are subjected to cultural misattunements or microaggressions (an unfortunately too common occurrence; Hook et al., 2016), they are largely experienced through a relational encounter with a therapist, as opposed to being grounded in a theory-specific intervention. Accordingly, in an effort to stave off the potential for recapitulated harm in a new therapeutic relationship, POC may be especially motivated to acquire information about the person providing them with MHC. POC may intuit that: “one or more, or even all, treatments may not be for me, but this particular therapist could be” (so long as they can effectively identify and access that clinician). Again, though, at this stage of the research, these notions are speculations that require further empirical evaluation through quantitative, qualitative, and community-engaged methods.

Consistent with the idea of prioritizing the therapist over other factors in making treatment decisions, recent research has tested the effectiveness of matching patients to an empirically good-fitting provider (i.e., one who has a history of exceptional outcomes when treating patients with certain types of presenting problems). Specifically, a double-masked, randomized trial showed that adult outpatients who were matched in this way—prior to receiving naturalistic treatment as usual—improved to a significantly greater degree than patients who were assigned to therapists through normal clinic procedures (based on pragmatic considerations, such as therapist self-reported expertise and availability; Constantino et al., 2021). Moreover, the match effect in the Constantino et al. (2021) trial was moderated by patient race/ethnicity. Specifically, purposefully matching patients to therapists’ problem-based strengths (and away from their weaknesses) was twice as strong for POC than White patients (Boswell et al., 2022). When integrating the trial and present survey results, prospective matching at the time of referral/assignment seems to be a value-consistent evidence-based process for POC. Of course, given the trial’s masking, patients did not know they were being matched in the Constantino et al. trial. Thus, future research could test whether unmasked matching has an even stronger beneficial effect for POC because they would know it is meeting a preference for using therapist performance data to inform referral/assignment (and it is established meta-analytically that meeting patient preferences is associated with better treatment outcomes; Swift et al., 2018).

Additionally, research can aim to replicate and extend the problem-specific match effects (main and moderator) to therapists’ identity-specific strengths, which holds promise for further refining optimal personalized care (Coyne, in press). Such work could also examine whether one mechanism through which identity-based matching works for POC is by increasing their retention in MHC. At present, research has demonstrated lower retention rates among minoritized populations (e.g., de Haan et al., 2018). Moreover, for communities that demonstrate lower trust in MHC systems (e.g., Hwang et al., 2008) and lower rates of initiating treatment (e.g., Lê Cook et al., 2014), it may be especially hope-inspiring for a POC to know they are being purposefully and transparently connected to a therapist with a track record of success in treating patients with a similar racial/ethnic identity(ies). Such optimism could translate into higher expectation for a given therapist and corresponding course of treatment to help—a transtheoretical and transdiagnostic variable that has also been shown to associate meta-analytically with greater improvement (e.g., Constantino et al., 2018).

As one additional progression, future research could also examine the potential additive benefit of employing both problem- and identity-specific matching simultaneously; that is, one could be assigned to a therapist with a historical strength in treating patients with the same primary presenting concern (e.g., depression) and racial/ethnic identity (e.g., Black). The presents results foreshadow an acceptance of this idea in that POC seem to value using both types of provider performance information and expect that such harnessing can help. In this case, “double-matching” would imply that more information and more harnessing could conceivably result in more helpfulness.

Of course, the present study was not without limitations. First, although we adapted our survey items from a published study (Boswell et al., 2021), they were not part of a psychometrically established measure. Thus, despite high face validity for our purposes, we cannot report on other traditional psychometrics, including those that require more than the single items we used in our primary analyses. Given the unknown psychometrics, the fact that single-item measures can be more unreliable than multi-item measures, and the lack of precise clinical meaning of movement along our multipoint scale, our results should be considered preliminary. Second, because we conducted a large number of exploratory analyses, it is possible that some of the differences we observed between groups on specific items were due to chance. Third, generalizability could be limited by the US-based sample, which was also fairly educated and predominantly heterosexual.

Fourth, we grouped patients who endorsed any racial/ethnic identity other than White into a single, heterogeneous group of POC. While driven by limitations of the existing literature and a need to preserve statistical power to examine group differences, this decision masked possibly meaningful differences among POC. Fifth, we did not account for the influence of identity salience or degree of racial/ethnic marginalization on reported experiences and attitudes, which could have concealed important interactions. Sixth, we did not prescreen MTurk workers for eligibility or use a more stringent MTurk worker qualification of 100 prior human intelligence tasks (HITs) with a 99% approval rate across HITs, which are now the recommended parameters to maximize the quality of MTurk data (see Bauer et al., 2020); thus, replication efforts are needed that implement such parameters. Finally, as with any survey study, it is possible that people were deceitful in representing themselves in relation to our inclusion criteria.

These limitations notwithstanding, our findings may help to preliminarily inform how the field can leverage existing within-therapist effectiveness differences to improve patient outcomes while prioritizing patients’ identity-informed needs and values. In a growing age of MHC transparency, therapists represent an important piece of the accountability puzzle. As the field looks to repair systemic harms (e.g., American Psychological Association, 2021), efforts to harness therapist performance data may be particularly poised to benefit marginalized populations.
