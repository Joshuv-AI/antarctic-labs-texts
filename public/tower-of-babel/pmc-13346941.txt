Evaluation of greenhouse gas emissions from Bonga and Menz sheep breeds managed under community-based breeding program
Authors: Assemu Tesfa, Mengistie Taye, Aynalem Haile, Zerihun Nigussie, Dina Najjar, Shigdaf Mekuriaw, Shimels E. Wassie, Shanbel Besufkad, Zelalem Abate, Checkol Demis, Tesfaye Getachew, Suzanne van Dijk, Andreas Wilkes, Dawit Solomon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Livestock systems contribute substantially to Greenhouse Gas (GHG) emissions, highlighting the need for interventions that enhance productivity while lowering environmental impact. This study assessed GHG emissions and emission intensity (EI) in Ethiopian Menz and Bonga sheep managed under Community-Based Breeding Programs (CBBP) compared with non-CBBP flocks. Using FAO’s GLEAM-i model with long-term performance data (2009–2022) and surveys of 321 households, we found that CBBPs reduced total emissions by 14.56% in Menz and 7.04% in Bonga sheep, while protein output rose by 42% and 2%, respectively. EI declined by 21.49% in Menz and 6.29% in Bonga. At the household level, CBBP flocks achieved markedly lower EI, 39.39% in Menz and 30.68% in Bonga, despite higher absolute emissions from larger flocks. Methane, mainly from enteric fermentation, accounted for a higher proportion compared to other GHG gases. EI was positively associated with reproductive inefficiencies while negatively associated with growth traits, underscoring key levers for mitigation. These results demonstrate that CBBPs deliver both productivity gains and environmental co-benefits, offering a scalable model for climate-smart small ruminant development with relevance beyond Ethiopia.

Introduction

The increasing demand for animal-source foods worldwide, driven by population growth, urbanization, and rising incomes, is reshaping livestock systems but also intensifying concerns about environmental sustainability1,2. This trend presents both opportunities and challenges for developing economies where livestock remain central to livelihoods, food security, and trade. In Ethiopia, for instance, small ruminants occupy a particularly critical position within the agricultural economy, contributing about 90% of the live animal and meat export value, 92% of skin and hide export earnings4, and generating about 40% of cash income and 19% of household food derived from livestock sector—of which more than half is attributable to sheep5.

Globally, livestock contributed an estimated 14.5% of anthropogenic global greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions, with methane from enteric fermentation and manure among the primary sources3. While cattle dominate global discussions, small ruminants such as sheep are increasingly recognized as critical in developing countries, for their dual role as critical livelihood assets and contributors to rising GHG emissions. In Ethiopia, the agriculture sector accounts for 79% of total emissions, of which 60% is attributed to livestock6. Within this subsector, sheep emissions constitute about 7% of total livestock-related GHG output7 and have exhibited a steady upward trend, from 4.5 to 6.5 million tons of CO₂-eq between 2003/04 and 2017/18, representing an average annual increment of 0.175 million ton, a trajectory that mirrors global patterns of small ruminant expansion1,8–10. With the national sheep flock projected to increase by 27% by 205011, strategic mitigation is needed not only to meet Ethiopia’s climate commitments but also to inform global pathways for low-emission small ruminant systems.

In response to these challenges, Ethiopia has embedded livestock-specific targets within its Climate Resilient Green Economy (CRGE) strategy, aiming to reduce GHG emissions by 1% (-1.9 Million tones (Mt) CO₂-eq) with domestic resources and by 7.6% (-14.8 Mt CO₂-eq) with international support by 20309. These targets are consistent with global climate action frameworks that prioritize sustainable intensification—enhancing productivity while lowering emissions per unit of output. Interventions such as improved animal health, feed optimization, reproductive efficiency, and early off-take have been identified internationally as effective in lowering emission intensity (EI)12.

One flagship Ethiopian initiative that embodies these principles is the Community-Based Breeding Program (CBBP), introduced in 2009 to enhance the genetic, economic, and production environment of small ruminants through participatory, locally tailored strategies13,14. The CBBP model integrates the selection of superior sires, reproductive management, improved feed systems, strategic disease and parasite control, and capacity building at the community level. To date, more than 150 CBBP villages are operational across Ethiopia, with Menz and Bonga sheep breeds serving as early adopters and proving grounds for scaling genetic innovations15,16. Empirical studies have documented measurable gains in growth rates, survival, and reproductive performance in these systems13,17–21. Importantly, similar breeding-for-efficiency strategies are being trialed in other developing countries, highlighting the potential global relevance of CBBPs as models for climate-smart small ruminant development22–24.

Crucially, recent assessments have begun to link these productivity gains in small ruminant systems with environmental co-benefits. For instance, preliminary evaluations of the small ruminant transformation (SmaRT) packages—combining genetics, herd health, feeds, and forages—using the CLEANED tool, estimated EI of 0.90 t CO₂-eq/kg protein for Menz sheep and 0.37 for Bonga sheep14, suggesting the potential for well-designed breeding programs to simultaneously improve production efficiency and lower EI. These figures align with global findings that productivity-enhancing interventions can reduce EI in ruminant systems. Nonetheless, current knowledge is largely based on short-term assessments, offering limited insight into long-term emission dynamics or the interactions between genetic and management traits12,25. Further research is therefore needed to elucidate these relationships and guide the development of sustainable, low-emission livestock strategies.

This study addresses a critical gap by providing a systems-based evaluation of GHG emissions and EI in Menz and Bonga sheep flocks managed under both CBBP and non-CBBP setups. It integrates household survey data with over a decade of longitudinal records and employs the FAO’s Global Livestock Environmental Assessment Model-interactive (GLEAM-i) as the analytical framework. Specifically, it quantifies total emissions, protein outputs, and EI; identifies key herd and management traits associated with emissions; and evaluates the broader climate mitigation potential of CBBP interventions. The findings are relevant to international debates on sustainable livestock intensification, highlighting how community-driven genetic improvement can serve as a replicable model for climate-smart livestock systems in developing countries.

Synergy and trade-offs between GHG emissions and input variables in the herd module

The correlation analysis highlighted that EI was positively and significantly associated (P < 0.05) with age at first lambing (months), and lambing interval with slight but positive correlation with lamb mortality rate in both breeds (Fig. 3). Conversely, EI exhibited significant negative correlations (P < 0.05) with growth-related traits such as birth weight and yearling weight, as well as with the number of adult male animals. Total emissions and protein production were positively correlated with flock size, and growth-related traits, respectively. Discussion

This study shows that CBBPs in Ethiopia effectively reduce environmental impact while increasing protein output in small ruminants. Both Menz and Bonga sheep under CBBP management had lower EIs than baseline and non-participant households. This consistent decline in EI across scenarios highlights the synergistic benefits of combining genetic selection with improved health, feeding, and flock management. These outcomes align directly with Ethiopia’s broader climate and development objectives. By improving biological efficiency and reducing EI at the household level, CBBPs offer a practical, scalable pathway for contributing to the livestock-sector mitigation targets articulated in the Climate Resilient Green Economy (CRGE) strategy. The magnitude of improvement observed here positions CBBPs as a credible component of Ethiopia’s commitment to reduce total national emissions by 68.8% by 2039 while also strengthening the country’s role in global efforts to lower livestock-related emissions3,26. The results also reinforce global evidence on the environmental benefits of genetic selection, which has been shown to reduce emissions by 5–20% through productivity gains27. Importantly, the observed reductions in EI are linked to heritable methane-related traits, enabling sustained genetic progress that is transmitted across generations12,27–29.

Compared to previously reported benchmarks, such as Mwema et al.14, who found EIs of 0.90 t CO₂-eq/kg protein for Menz and 0.37 t CO₂-eq/kg protein for Bonga sheep, this study reveals substantial progress. The observed improvements likely reflect both sustained programmatic advancements and methodological refinements. Whereas Mwema et al.14 applied the CLEANED model, this study employed the GLEAM-i framework, which incorporates broader datasets and more precise tracking of animal performance and feed conversion. These findings position Ethiopia’s CBBPs as a model with global relevance, demonstrating how participatory breeding can achieve both production and environmental benefits in developing countries where small ruminants play a central role in rural livelihoods.

A key finding of this study is that reproductive efficiency and survival are central to climate-smart livestock development. Traits such as reduced age at first lambing, shorter lambing intervals, and lower lamb mortality were strongly linked to lower EIs. Although genetically improved animals produced higher absolute GHG emissions, largely driven by increased yearling weights, the concurrent gains in protein output substantially offset these emissions, yielding lower EIs. This outcome underscores the synergistic effects of integrated genetic, nutritional, and management interventions within the CBBP framework. These relationships are consistent with global evidence showing that reproductive traits significantly reduce emission intensities by lowering the number of replacement or “follower” animals needed to sustain flock size12,27,30. Integrating these traits into breeding indices offers a high-leverage strategy applicable worldwide, including in regions where genetic progress in small ruminants has lagged behind that in cattle.

Methane, particularly from enteric fermentation, was identified as the dominant contributor to total emissions—consistent with previous literature7,10,14,26,30,31. While genetic improvement is a critical lever for mitigating enteric emissions through enhanced feed conversion efficiency, persistent challenges remain in extensive grazing systems where forage quality is poor1,14. Practical solutions such as promoting forage legumes, introducing high-quality forage species, and advancing ration formulation with scalable feed additives32–34 must complement genetic progress. These lessons extend beyond Ethiopia, highlighting the universal challenge of improving diets in smallholder systems while maintaining economic feasibility.

The study also surfaces a classic paradox: improvements in EI often coincide with rising total emissions. Increased flock size and weight enhance outputs but elevate absolute emissions due to higher feed consumption and manure generation. This finding mirrors global debates on efficiency vs absolute mitigation26. Addressing this trade-off requires systems-level approaches, where genetic gains are combined with early offtake strategies, market incentives, and policy alignment to balance productivity growth with climate goals35–37.

Complementary measures, including targeted animal health interventions, improved feeding, and early-age marketing to increase off-take rates, were also equally pivotal in reducing EI. Literature evidence confirms the significant role of higher offtake rates in lowering the environmental footprint of sheep production, aligning with Ethiopia’s national mitigation strategies35,36 and global climate action frameworks6. Although methane remains the dominant GHG, emissions of N₂O and CO₂ from manure management and feed production are increasingly significant as systems transition from extensive to semi-intensive models. This pattern is illustrated in scenario set two, where total GHG emissions, driven mainly by CH₄, were higher in Menz than in Bonga sheep. These differences likely stem from both breed characterstics and management contexts. Menz sheep rely on year-round communal grazing and are typically supplemented with low-quality barley residues, a feeding regime that can elevate enteric methane output. Their smaller body size also translates into lower protein yield per unit of emission. By contrast, Bonga sheep are managed under a system that combines tethered grazing on private land with supplementation using improved forages and legumes. Their larger size, coupled with better-quality feed resources, supports more efficient nutrient utilization and higher protein output per animal. These factors help explain the lower CH₄ emissions observed in Bonga sheep relative to Menz under comparable scenarios.

Adoption of nutrient recycling, composting, and biogas technologies offers dual benefits, curbing emissions from manure management while enhancing soil fertility, underscoring the potential of integrated management to advance both productivity and environmental sustainability3,38.

From a policy perspective, the findings reinforce the potential of CBBPs to serve not only as tools for genetic improvement but also as entry points for climate finance and carbon markets. Measurable reductions in EI could justify farmer participation in voluntary carbon markets, eco-labeling schemes, or performance-based subsidies. Internationally, such models could inform payment-for-ecosystem-services frameworks that reward smallholders for adopting low-emission practices. To achieve this, robust Measurement, Reporting, and Verification (MRV) systems are needed, combining standardized tools such as GLEAM-i with on-farm data to ensure credible carbon accounting31,39,40. Given that this study employed indirect GHG estimation methods, future research should prioritize direct measurement of enteric methane emissions and replace literature-derived parameters, such as fertility rates, finished-animal weights, and adult mortality, with empirical observations. Such refinement would not only enhance the precision of emission estimates but also deepen understanding of how production dynamics influence the environmental performance of small ruminant systems.

By coupling genetic improvement with environmental performance, CBBPs can be repositioned as a globally relevant climate-smart investment model for smallholder livestock systems. To scale impact, breeding goals should be harmonized with climate policy, GHG metrics integrated into extension services, and incentive mechanisms embedded into national livestock strategies. Such approaches align with global climate resilience agendas, offering pathways to simultaneously address food security, rural incomes, and environmental sustainability12,31,36,37.

Finally, this study extends significance beyond national boundaries. As developing countries grapple with the dual imperatives of food security and climate resilience, these findings provide timely, policy-relevant evidence on transforming smallholder livestock systems into sustainable growth drivers. By using real-world systems and actionable metrics, this work strengthens the empirical case for CBBPs and offers a replicable framework for other developing countries aiming to decarbonize livestock sectors without compromising equity or food security.

Materials and methods

Ethics declarations

This study involving human participants was conducted in accordance with institutional and national ethical standards. Ethical approval was obtained from the Research Ethics Review Committee of the College of Agriculture and Environmental Sciences, Bahir Dar University (Approval No. 011/8/2017 and Date 15/4/2025). All procedures involving human participants adhered to relevant local regulations and institutional guidelines. Informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to their involvement in the study. Additionally, all procedures involving animals complied with the ARRIVE guidelines, ensuring appropriate care and handling during body weight measurements.

Study areas

The study focuses on sheep CBBP villages in the Menz and Bonga sheep production systems. In the Menz area, spanning districts within the North Shoa Zone of the Amhara Region, CBBPs have been implemented in five villages: Molale and Zeram (Menz Mama district), and Dargegn, Sinamba, Tsehaysina (Menz Gera Midir district). For this study, three representative villages operating since 2009, Dargegn, Sinamba, and Molale, were selected due to their continuous participation and data availability. These villages are situated over 2,800 m above sea level and lie approximately 280 km north of Addis Ababa21. The area experiences a bimodal rainfall pattern, with the main rainy season from June to September and a short rainy season from February to March. The dominant farming system is mixed crop-livestock production, specifically a subalpine sheep-barley system41. Given the area’s limited crop productivity, sheep account for an estimated 84.8% of household income, underscoring their economic importance14,41,42.

In the southwestern highlands of Ethiopia, the Bonga sheep CBBP has been established across 14 villages within four districts of the Kaffa Zone17. For this study, six CBBP villages, namely Boka, Shuta, Meduta, Buta 1, Alrgeta located within Adiyo Kaka district and Dirbedo, located at Gesha district, were purposively selected. The Kaffa Zone is situated at 7° 34′N latitude and 37° 6′E longitude, about 467 km southwest of Addis Ababa. The area spans a wide altitudinal gradient (1,600–3,348 m.a.s.l.) and experiences high annual rainfall (1,079–2,032 mm), with average temperatures around 24 °C43. Farming systems are primarily mixed crop–livestock, with sheep integrated into perennial cropping. Within these systems, Bonga sheep contribute roughly 21.9% of total household income, underscoring their relevance in diversified livelihoods41.

To facilitate comparative analysis, paired CBBP and non-CBBP sites were selected within ecologically and socioeconomically matched kebeles. In the Menz Mama districts, Yecha and Zeram kebeles (CBBP villages) were compared against Emegua kebele (non-CBBP village). Similarly, in the Bonga context, Boka kebele (Adiyo Kaka district) and Shada kebele (Telo district) represented CBBP sites, while Mera kebele (Adiyo Kaka district) served as the non-CBBP comparator (Fig. 4). Data source and data sets used for the input modules

Herd module

GHG emissions were estimated using two scenario sets, each representing different dimensions of the sheep production systems under study. The primary datasets for the first scenario was drawn from long-term performance monitoring records maintained by the Debre Birhan Agricultural Research Center (DBARC) for Menz sheep and the Bonga Agricultural Research Center (BARC) for Bonga sheep. These datasets, originally compiled for genetic evaluation and ram selection, provided the foundation for modeling animal-level productivity and population dynamics. For the second scenario set, primary data were collected through household surveys and direct animal measurements. This involved structured interviews with 321 households, including 210 households from CBBP villages (96 in Menz and 114 in Bonga) and 111 households from matched non-CBBP villages (50 in Menz and 61 in Bonga). The sample size for the survey was determined by Cochran’s sample size formula44 for both infinite and finite populations, with 11% of the sampling proportion. Additionally, live weight measurements were recorded from 168 Menz sheep and 164 Bonga sheep in non-CBBP flocks, while corresponding data from CBBP flocks were retrieved from institutional archives. The household interviews, animal measurements, and expert elicitations were conducted on the timeframe between mid of April to end of May 2025.

To parameterize the herd module across all scenario sets, key reproductive, demographic, and productivity variables were compiled from a combination of empirical data, peer-reviewed literature, and expert elicitation. Variables such as fertility rates (ewes mated which lambed), age at first lambing, lambing intervals, and adult/lamb mortality rates were derived from both primary field data and published sources (e.g.,14,17,45–47. Inputs for live weights at various life stages, birth, weaning, yearling, and market age, were supplemented with animal performance records and cross-validated through consultations with senior animal scientists and nutritionists at DBARC and BARC. The expert elicitations were mainly made to avoid uncertainties in the survey results related to the proportion of feed items and manure management along with literature-based values. Together, they target both genetic potential and environmental constraints affecting animal performance14,43,48,49. Methods of data analysis

GHG emissions were estimated using the Global Livestock Environmental Assessment Model-interactive (GLEAM-i Version 1.9) (https://gleami.apps.fao.org), a web-based modeling tool developed by FAO to quantify emissions across livestock systems using the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) Tier 2 methods39. The model is a representation of different calculation sequences implemented worldwide and includes the main livestock production activities, and quantifies the related GHG emissions (Fig. 5). GLEAM-i integrates key emission sources throughout the livestock value chain, including: (a) pre-farm emissions arising from the manufacture of agricultural inputs; (b) on-farm emissions arising from feed production, animal digestion, and manure management; and (c) post-farm emissions arising from product processing, distribution, and retail25.Fig. 5Methodological flow of the GLEAM-i software used for data analysis (adapted from10,31. The software utilized three distinct input parameters: the herd, feed, and manure module. The outputs generated by the software included total emissions, protein production, and emission intensities, as well as the various sources of gases (CH4, N2O, and CO2) corresponding to the predefined set of scenarios.

Methodological flow of the GLEAM-i software used for data analysis (adapted from10,31. The software utilized three distinct input parameters: the herd, feed, and manure module. The outputs generated by the software included total emissions, protein production, and emission intensities, as well as the various sources of gases (CH4, N2O, and CO2) corresponding to the predefined set of scenarios.

The model comprises three core modules—herd, feed, and manure—each driven by field data and literature inputs, detailed under the herd, feed, and manure subheadings. The herd module characterizes flock demographics and life-cycle parameters, enabling calculation of energy and protein yields, and also informs manure quantities40. The feed module estimates emissions associated with the production, nutrient content, and usage of different feed types per kilogram of ration26. The manure module estimates GHGs emitted from storage, handling, and application of manure, including emissions left on pasture or used as household fuel. The model also accounts for indirect emission sources such as energy use, chemical inputs (e.g., fertilizers, pesticides), and mechanical operations (e.g., forage harvesting and transport)31. The outputs from the analysis are based on the predefined scenarios.

The main GHG emission sources assessed included CH4 from enteric fermentation and manure management, N₂O from manure and urine deposition, and CO₂ from feed and energy-related activities. Simulations were conducted under the two scenario sets described in the subheading of the herd module to compare programmatic effects (WP vs. WOP), and adoption outcomes (W-CBBP vs. WO-CBBP). Emission outputs included both absolute emissions (t CO₂-eq/year) and EI (t CO₂-eq/kg protein produced), used as a measure of environmental efficiency. The CO₂-eq estimates were derived using the default global warming potential (GWP) factors embedded in the GLEAM-i model, which are based on the 100 years IPCC Fifth Assessment Report (AR5): CO₂ = 1, CH₄ = 34, and N₂O = 298. To assess the relationships between EI and key performance traits, Pearson correlation analyses were performed using JMP Pro 16 statistical software50. This helped identify potential synergies and trade-offs between genetic and management improvements and environmental performance outcomes.
