Bronchoscopic Direct Drainage of a Treatment‐Resistant Lung Abscess via Endoscopic Balloon Dilation in a High‐Risk Patient
Authors: Nana Yazawa, Yusuke Nakamura, Akihiro Takemasa, Yuri Nomura, Minori Hanato, Noriaki Nishimura, Nobuaki Yoshida, Azusa Tsukada, Nobuhiko Tsukada, Hiroaki Okutomi, Yasuo Shimizu, Seiji Niho
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTA 49‐year‐old man presented with a cough, hemoptysis and fever. Computed tomography revealed a lung abscess in the right upper lobe. The patient's medical history included chronic renal failure with haemodialysis, myocardial infarction and developmental disorders. On admission, he was administered piperacillin/tazobactam and minocycline, but they were ineffective. Bronchoscopy was performed with a guide sheath to drain the abscess, but complete drainage could not be achieved. Percutaneous transthoracic drainage or surgical resection were not indicated because of complications, so direct endoscopic balloon dilation was performed on the narrowed bronchus communicating with the abscess cavity. This treatment resulted in spontaneous drainage of the abscess, allowing the ultra‐thin bronchoscope to access the cavity and achieve complete drainage. The abscess subsequently decreased in size. Bronchoscopic direct drainage after balloon dilation can be a viable treatment option in patients with a treatment‐resistant abscess and severe comorbidities who are ineligible for invasive procedures.

Introduction

A lung abscess is a locally necrotic cavity in the lung parenchyma that is usually caused by aspiration of oropharyngeal secretions. Potential risk factors for lung abscess include advanced age, dental infections, alcoholism, neuromuscular disorders, intellectual developmental disorder and an immunocompromised state [1]. If lung abscesses do not respond to systemic antibiotic therapy, invasive interventions such as percutaneous transthoracic tube drainage (PTTD), endoscopic catheter drainage (ECD) and surgical resection may be necessary [1]. Although no clear guidelines exist on how to select the most suitable procedure, some reports suggest that fewer adverse events occur with ECD than with PTTD [2]. ECD has been reported since around 2005 and is considered an alternative approach when percutaneous drainage is not feasible [3]; it can be repeated and has demonstrated a high success rate (81.3%–90.5%) [3, 4]. A pigtail catheter was used in earlier reports (e.g., 2005) [3], whereas since around 2017, several reports have described the use of a guide sheath [5, 6]. Balloon bronchoplasty is commonly used for central airway stenosis; however, in recent years, attempts have also been made to dilate more peripheral bronchial stenoses [7]. In patients with uncontrolled psychiatric disorders, maintaining adequate immobility is difficult, while in elderly patients, limited physiological reserve may preclude tolerance of invasive procedures, making percutaneous or surgical abscess drainage less feasible. In such cases, ECD may be considered; however, it may be insufficient for large or high‐viscosity abscesses. Therefore, alternative treatment approaches are needed. We present a case of an EDC‐refractory lung abscess treated by bronchoscopic direct drainage after endoscopic balloon dilation. There are few reports on balloon dilation of the peripheral bronchi for lung abscess, suggesting that this approach may be novel.

Case Report

A 49‐year‐old man presented with a cough, hemoptysis and fever. His medical history included chronic kidney dysfunction requiring haemodialysis, myocardial infarction, hypertension, aspiration pneumonia and a developmental disorder. He was taking an antiplatelet drug because of a previous percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI). A computed tomography (CT) scan of his chest revealed a lung abscess measuring approximately 8 cm in diameter in his right upper lobe (Figure 1A,B). The right bronchus (rB)2b was narrowed by pressure from the abscess (Figures 2A and 3A,B). The abscess volume was estimated to be 230 mL based on preprocedural CT imaging. After admission, the patient was treated with piperacillin/tazobactam and minocycline, but they were ineffective. Subsequently, an endoscopic drainage procedure was performed with a guide sheath. The initial bronchoscopic procedure was performed using an Olympus BF‐Q290 bronchoscope (outer diameter 4.8 mm; working channel 2.0 mm). Suction was attempted using a guide sheath (SG‐400C; outer diameter 1.9 mm). However, effective suction was limited by the high viscosity of the sputum and difficulty in adjusting the position of the guide sheath, particularly in blind areas with limited visualisation.

The abscess was partially drained, and culture of the pus revealed hypermucoviscous Klebsiella pneumoniae , which was susceptible to ceftriaxone. Antibiotic treatment was changed to ceftriaxone, but the patient did not respond well. PTTD or surgical resection were not indicated because of complications. Therefore, to attempt complete drainage the patient underwent endoscopic balloon dilation and direct endoscopic drainage under intubation: First, a curette was inserted into the abscess through the rB2b; then, endoscopic balloon dilation of the narrowed rB2b was performed with a CRE PRO GI Wireguided Balloon Dilation Catheter 6–8 mm (#5874, Boston Scientific) (Figure 2B). The target bronchus was the right B2b, and the luminal diameter at the orifice was approximately 3.0 mm on CT imaging. Since bronchial dilation balloons for pulmonary use have a minimum expansion diameter of 8 mm (CRE Pulmonary Balloon Dilator, 8–10 mm, Cat#5033), considering the risk of bronchial injury, we selected a smaller‐diameter device, the CRE PRO GI Wireguided balloon (6–8 mm), which allows more controlled dilation.

A total of three dilation procedures were performed, during which the pressure in the balloon was gradually increased to 3, 6 and 9 atm, respectively, and the bronchus was dilated for 2 min at a time. The procedure successfully dilated the rB2 (Figure 2C), enabling pus to spontaneously flow out of the abscess (Figure 2D).

Subsequently, an ultra‐thin bronchoscope (BF‐P290 [OLYMPUS]; outer diameter 4.2 mm) was inserted into the abscess cavity via the dilated bronchus. The reddish‐brown pus was aspirated, and the abscess was flushed with 360 mL of saline (Figure 2E,F). No severe adverse events occurred during the procedure. The initial bronchoscopy was performed without endotracheal intubation under moderate sedation with propofol and fentanyl, whereas balloon dilation was performed under the same sedation with endotracheal intubation. The bronchoscopic procedure was performed under fluoroscopic guidance.

The response to ceftriaxone was insufficient, and the regimen was switched back to tazobactam/piperacillin based on the culture results. Tazobactam/piperacillin was administered for a total of 1 month, followed by 2 weeks of oral levofloxacin, after which the patient was discharged. The laboratory data trends in this case were as follows: before EDC, WBC (neutrophils) 13,500/μL (83.7%) and CRP 15.63 mg/dL; 1 day after EDC, WBC 12,100/μL (80.6%) and CRP 15.32 mg/dL, with no radiological improvement of the abscess cavity; 4 days after balloon dilation and direct drainage, WBC 8400/μL (86.0%) and CRP 9.73 mg/dL; and 1 month after balloon dilation and direct drainage, WBC 5400/μL (55.2%) and CRP 1.38 mg/dL. The patient's clinical symptoms improved, and a CT scan performed 2 weeks after the drainage procedure revealed that the lung abscess had decreased in size (Figure 1C,D) and follow‐up CT at 3 months demonstrated marked improvement of the abscess cavity (Figures 1E,F and 3C).

Effective drainage was achieved in two sessions: the initial catheter drainage and a second bronchoscopic drainage with balloon dilation.

Discussion

Lung abscesses measuring more than 6 cm in diameter are less likely to respond to antibiotic therapy and may require additional drainage procedures [8]. In the present case, the lung abscess measured around 8 cm in diameter and did not respond to antibiotic therapy.

A previous paper reported that ECD results in clinical and radiological improvement in over 90% of cases, with a complication rate of less than 3%, whereas the success rate of PTTD is 80%, with a 20% complication rate, including pneumothorax and bronchopleural fistula [2]. Furthermore, the ECD group had a lower complication rate and a higher success rate [2]. However, if the abscess cavity is not connected to the bronchi, ECD may not be effective enough.

Based on previous reports, the use of a pigtail catheter [4], which has a larger diameter than a guide sheath, was also considered. However, in the present case, the abscess was large and complete evacuation with a catheter alone was deemed difficult. In addition, we considered it necessary to relieve the stenosis at the entrance to promote natural drainage. Therefore, we selected direct bronchoscopic drainage following balloon dilation.

The current patient had undergone haemodialysis because of chronic kidney dysfunction and was being treated with an antiplatelet drug after PCI; thus, he was at risk of bleeding. Because of his severe comorbidities, he was deemed ineligible for PTTD or surgery. The abscess could not be fully aspirated by endoscopic drainage with only a guide sheath, and the bronchus leading to the abscess cavity was stenotic, making spontaneous drainage unlikely. Therefore, the narrowed branch was expanded by endoscopic balloon dilation, which enabled bronchoscopic drainage and spontaneous drainage of pus. Subsequently, the abscess was completely drained by bronchoscopy and rinsed with saline. The patient experienced no severe adverse events. Bronchoscopic drainage is valuable because it is less invasive than PTTD and surgery and results in fewer complications.

Hypermucoviscous Klebsiella pneumoniae was found in the pus. This bacterium is a gram‐negative, gas‐producing, facultative anaerobe. Hypermucoviscosity is a virulence factor of Klebsiella pneumoniae , characterised by the presence of the regulator of mucoid phenotype A (rmpA) gene, which enhances capsule production, resulting in a hypermucoid phenotype and increased viscosity of infected material [9]. It is also genetically associated with hypervirulent Klebsiella pneumoniae . Lung abscesses due to a hypervirulent Klebsiella pneumoniae infection can be serious. Hypervirulent Klebsiella pneumoniae abscesses can be multiloculated or contain highly viscous material that limits standard drainage; therefore, balloon dilation and direct endoscopic drainage may be required to expand the bronchial orifice and facilitate drainage.

Although this procedure may be performed in clinical practice, we could not find any case reports describing the use of bronchoscopic balloon dilation combined with direct drainage for lung abscesses in our search. This report suggests that balloon dilation of the peripheral bronchi may be a potential treatment option for lung abscess.

Direct bronchoscopic drainage following balloon dilation may be considered under the following conditions: (1) when surgical intervention or PTTD is not feasible; (2) when the pus is highly viscous and difficult to aspirate through a catheter; (3) when the volume of purulent material is too large for adequate catheter drainage and (4) when communication between the airway and the abscess cavity is present but severely narrowed, precluding adequate postprocedural drainage.

The present case suggests that peripheral balloon dilation and direct drainage may be an effective treatment option for ECD‐resistant lung abscesses, particularly in patients with severe comorbidities who are ineligible for PTTD or surgery.
