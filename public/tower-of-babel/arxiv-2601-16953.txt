Distribution of independent sets in perfect r r -ary trees
Daniel Iľkovič, Jun Yan

Abstract
Given a graph G G , the family of all independent sets of size k k containing a fixed vertex v v is called a star with centre v v , and is denoted by ℐ G k ​ ( v ) \mathcal{I}_{G}^{k}(v) . Motivated by a generalisation of the Erdős-Ko-Rado Theorem to the setting of independent sets in graphs, Hurlbert and Kamat conjectured that for every tree T T and every k k , the maximum of | ℐ T k ​ ( v ) | |\mathcal{I}_{T}^{k}(v)| can always be attained by a leaf of T T . While this conjecture turns out to be false in general, it is known to hold for specific families of trees like spiders and caterpillars. In this paper, we prove that this conjecture holds for a new family of trees, the perfect r r -ary trees, by constructing injections from stars centred at arbitrary vertices to stars centred at leaves. We also show that the analogous property holds for every forest 𝒯 \mathcal{T} that is the disjoint union of perfect trees with possibly varying sizes and arities, and determine the leaf that maximises | ℐ 𝒯 k ​ ( v ) | |\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{T}}^{k}(v)| .

Introduction

A family
\mathcal{F}
of sets is called

. Moreover, if

\mathcal{F}=\{A:i\in A,|A|=k\}
for some

. Since then, there have been many generalisations and variants of the Erdős-Ko-Rado Theorem in the literature. In this paper, we focus on a generalisation to the following setting of independent sets in graphs proposed by Holroyd, Spencer, and Talbot
[
7
,
8
]
in 2005.

Definition 1.1
.

Let

, and let

•

A subset

•

The family

Note that every star

Definition 1.2

Let

•

Observe that using this language, the Erdős-Ko-Rado Theorem can be restated as saying the empty graph on

Several families of graphs have been shown to be

[
12
]

[

, and certain graphs with an isolated vertex

For a graph

2
Perfect
r
-ary trees

Let
T
be a perfect
r
-ary tree. For every non-leaf vertex
v\in V(T)
, denote its
r
children from left to right by
c_{1}(v),c_{2}(v),\ldots,c_{r}(v)
. For every non-root vertex
v\in V(T)
, denote its parent by
p(v)
. Due to the symmetries in the perfect
r
-ary tree
T
, the size of the star
|\mathcal{I}_{T}^{k}(v)|
depends only on the level of
v
, so in particular
|\mathcal{I}_{T}^{k}(\ell)|
is independent of the choice of a leaf
\ell\in V(T)
. Therefore, from now on we will assume without loss of generality that
\ell
is the leftmost leaf of
T
and
v
is an ancestor of
\ell
. Denote the path from
v
to
\ell
by
P_{v,\ell}
.

To prove Theorem
1.5
, it suffices to find an injection from
\mathcal{I}_{T}^{k}(v)
to
\mathcal{I}_{T}^{k}(\ell)
. We define the following families of independent sets of size
k
:

•

\mathcal{A}_{v,\ell}=\{I\in\mathcal{I}^{k}_{T}:v\in I,\ell\notin I\}

•

\mathcal{B}_{v,\ell}=\{I\in\mathcal{I}^{k}_{T}:v\notin I,\ell\in I\}

•

\mathcal{C}_{v,\ell}=\{I\in\mathcal{I}^{k}_{T}:v\in I,\ell\in I\}

Since
|\mathcal{I}_{T}^{k}(v)|=|\mathcal{A}_{v,\ell}|+|\mathcal{C}_{v,\ell}|
and
|\mathcal{I}_{T}^{k}(\ell)|=|\mathcal{B}_{v,\ell}|+|\mathcal{C}_{v,\ell}|
, Theorem
1.5
is equivalent to showing
|\mathcal{A}_{v,\ell}|\leq|\mathcal{B}_{v,\ell}|
. Therefore, it suffices to construct an injection
\phi:\mathcal{A}_{v,\ell}\to\mathcal{B}_{v,\ell}
.

For every independent set
I\in\mathcal{A}_{v,\ell}
containing
v
but not
\ell
, the corresponding independent set
\phi(I)\in\mathcal{B}_{v,\ell}
containing
\ell
but not
v
will be constructed by an iterative process. The first step of this process is to remove
v
from
I
and replace it with
\ell
. Of course, this may create a conflict if the parent of
\ell
was originally in
I
. In fact, the goal of each subsequent iteration of the process is to check for and resolve a conflict created by the vertex movement in a previous iteration. We will show that this process must terminate and output the desired independent set
\phi(I)
, with the resulting mapping
I\mapsto\phi(I)
being injective. This will be done in detail in Section
2.2
and Section
2.3
depending on the parity of the distance
d(v,\ell)
. The case when
d(v,\ell)
is even is more involved. To improve readability, we isolate part of the iterative process used there and present it as the CAS operation in Section
2.1
.

Lemma 2.2

(Injectivity of CAS)
.

For
T_{d}
and
T_{u}
satisfying the assumptions in Definition
2.1
, the map
I\mapsto\cas(I,T_{d},T_{u})
is injective.

Proof.

Let
I,I^{\prime}\subseteq V(T_{d})\cup V(T_{u})
be distinct independent sets containing
u
but not
d
. We want to show that
\cas(I,T_{d},T_{u})\not=\cas(I^{\prime},T_{d},T_{u})
. This is immediate if
|I|\not=|I^{\prime}|
as the CAS operation preserves size, so suppose
|I|=|I^{\prime}|
.

For each
i\geq 1
, let
I_{i}
and
I_{i}^{\prime}
be the sets obtained at the beginning of the
i
-th iteration of the CAS process applied to
I
and
I^{\prime}
, respectively.

Observe that if the CAS process applied to
I
and the CAS process applied to
I^{\prime}
agree in their first
i-1
iterations, then at the beginning of the
i
-th iteration, both processes have the same queues
Q_{i}^{s}
,
Q_{i}^{t}
, and so the same
s_{i}
and
t_{i}
. If
Q_{i}^{s}
is empty, then they both terminate, and we deal with this case below. If not, then the outcomes of the
i
-th iterations for

and
I^{\prime}
entirely depend on whether
s_{i}\in I_{i}
and whether
s_{i}\in I_{i}^{\prime}
. By
A2
,

has not been modified, so this is equivalent to whether
s_{i}\in I
and whether
s_{i}\in I^{\prime}
. If they agree, then the
i
-th iteration also agree, while if not, then as
s_{i}
and

are not modified in future iterations, the final outputs
\cas(I,T_{d},T_{u})
and
\cas(I^{\prime},T_{d},T_{u})
differ as one of them contains

and the other one doesn’t. Therefore, the outputs differ if they disagree in some iteration of the process.

If both processes agree in each iteration up until termination, then they must terminate at the same time, say at the beginning of the

-th iteration. From the paragraph above,

and

agree on all vertices that had been in

for some

. They also agree on all vertices that have been in

for some

and

, these vertices are not in

. Hence,

agree on

and

are distinct, so they disagree on some

. Since only vertices in

are modified by this process,
\cas(I,T_{d},T_{u})\not=\cas(I^{\prime},T_{d},T_{u})
as they also disagree on

.

, we say we are at the beginning of the

, let

be the subtree of

3
Multiple disjoint perfect trees

In this section, we consider a forest
\mathcal{T}
consisting of disjoint perfect trees. Note that every independent set in
\mathcal{T}
is formed by taking the disjoint union of an independent set in each perfect tree within
\mathcal{T}
, and every such disjoint union of independent sets is an independent set in
\mathcal{T}
. Hence, by Theorem
1.5
, for every
k\geq 1
,
|\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{T}}^{k}(v)|
can be maximised by taking
v
to be a leaf of some perfect tree in
\mathcal{T}
.

To prove Theorem
1.6
, we need to determine which perfect tree in
\mathcal{T}
should the maximising leaves come from. This is done via the following two lemmas. The first one compares the leaves of two perfect trees with different arities.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\mathcal{T}
be a forest consisting of disjoint perfect trees. Let
T_{1},T_{2}
be two perfect trees in
\mathcal{T}
with arities
r_{1}
and
r_{2}
, respectively, where
r_{1}<r_{2}
. Let
\ell_{1}
be a leaf of
T_{1}
and
\ell_{2}
be a leaf of
T_{2}
. Then
|\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{T}}^{k}(\ell_{1})|\leq|\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{T}}^{k}(\ell_{2})|
.

Proof.

By symmetry, we may assume that
\ell_{1}
is the leftmost leaf of
T_{1}
, and
\ell_{2}
is the leftmost leaf of
T_{2}
. Let
\mathcal{A}_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2}}=\{I\in\mathcal{I}^{k}_{\mathcal{T}}:\ell_{1}\in I,\ell_{2}\not\in I\}
and
\mathcal{B}_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2}}=\{I\in\mathcal{I}^{k}_{\mathcal{T}}:\ell_{1}\not\in I,\ell_{2}\in I\}
. Like in Section
2
, it suffices to construct an injection
\phi:\mathcal{A}_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2}}\to\mathcal{B}_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2}}
. Let
I\in\mathcal{A}_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2}}
.

1.

Leaf Swap:
Set
I_{1}=(I\setminus\{\ell_{1}\})\cup\{\ell_{2}\}
.

2.

Primary Check:
If
p(\ell_{2})\not\in I_{1}
, then
I_{1}\in\mathcal{B}_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2}}
. In this case, let
\phi(I)=I_{1}
and terminate. Otherwise,
p(\ell_{2})\in I_{1}
and we have a conflict. Set
I_{2}=(I_{1}\setminus\{p(\ell_{2})\})\cup\{p(\ell_{1})\}
and continue.

3.

Secondary Check:
For each
2\leq i\leq r_{1}
, if
c_{i}(p(\ell_{1}))\in I_{2}
, then remove it from
I_{2}
and replace it with
c_{i}(p(\ell_{2}))
, otherwise do nothing. If
p(p(\ell_{1}))\in I_{2}
, then remove it and replace it with
c_{r_{1}+1}(p(\ell_{2}))
, otherwise do nothing. Note that as
p(\ell_{2})\in I_{1}
,
c_{i}(p(\ell_{2}))\not\in I_{2}
for every

, and
c_{r_{1}+1}(p(\ell_{2}))
exists as
r_{1}<r_{2}
. Let
I_{3}
be the resulting set. Observe that
I_{3}\in\mathcal{B}_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2}}
. Let
\phi(I)=I_{3}
and terminate.

It is clear that
\phi
is an injection, so
|\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{T}}^{k}(\ell_{1})|\leq|\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{T}}^{k}(\ell_{2})|
.
∎

,

. The next lemma compares leaves in two perfect trees with the same arity.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
\mathcal{T}
be a forest consisting of disjoint perfect trees. Let

•

If

|\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{T}}^{k}(\ell_{1})|\leq|\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{T}}^{k}(\ell_{2})|
.

•

If
h_{1}
is odd, then
|\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{T}}^{k}(\ell_{1})|\geq|\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{T}}^{k}(\ell_{2})|

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{A}_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2}}=\{I\in\mathcal{I}^{k}_{\mathcal{T}}:\ell_{1}\in I,\ell_{2}\not\in I\}

\mathcal{B}_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2}}=\{I\in\mathcal{I}^{k}_{\mathcal{T}}:\ell_{1}\not\in I,\ell_{2}\in I\}

, so we will be less formal and omit some details. Let

to
\ell_{2}

, and

Case 2:

is odd. We similarly construct an injection

∎

We can now put everything together and quickly deduce Theorem
1.6
.

Proof of Theorem
1.6
.

By Theorem
1.5
,
|\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{T}}^{k}(v)|
is maximised by the leaves of some perfect tree in
\mathcal{T}
. By Lemma
3.1
,
|\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{T}}^{k}(v)|
is maximised by a perfect tree with the highest arity in
\mathcal{T}
. Finally, by Lemma
3.2
, if every perfect tree with the highest arity in
\mathcal{T}
has an even number of levels, then a maximising leaf belongs to the one with the highest number of levels, otherwise it belongs to the one with the lowest odd number of levels.
