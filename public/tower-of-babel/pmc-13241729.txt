Family care and social alienation in patients with colorectal cancer and an ostomy: The sequential mediating effects of resilience and stigma
Authors: Baochuan Chen, Huifen Zhao, Xiaoyan Yang, Juying Hong, Lihui Wang, Yaping Xie, Xue'e Su, Yanjun Wang, Fenfen Wang, Jingjing Liu, Mianmian Chen, Baoyuan Xie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aimed to examine the association between family care and social alienation among patients with colorectal cancer (CRC) and an ostomy, and to explore the sequential mediating roles of resilience and stigma based on the ABC–X model.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted among 290 CRC patients with an ostomy in Fujian Province, China from October 2024 to August 2025. The participants completed questionnaires assessing family care, resilience, stigma, and social alienation. Serial multiple mediation analysis using the PROCESS macro tested the hypothesized pathways. Bootstrapping with 5000 resamples and bias-corrected 95% confidence intervals was used for testing the significance of indirect effects.ResultsFamily care negatively correlated with social alienation (r = -0.526, P < 0.001) and stigma (r = -0.483, P < 0.001) and positively correlated with resilience (r = 0.473, P < 0.001). Using serial mediation analysis, resilience and stigma jointly and partially mediated the association between family care and social alienation. The total indirect effect accounted for 54.26% of the total effect, with three significant pathways via: resilience (25.25%), stigma (22.91%), and the chained path “resilience→stigma” (6.10%). Family care was associated with higher resilience, reduced perceived stigma, and consequently alleviated social alienation.ConclusionsFamily care was associated with lower social alienation among CRC patients with an ostomy, potentially through higher resilience and lower stigma. These findings highlight the important role of family-based psychosocial resources in promoting adaptive adjustment following body-altering surgery. Nursing interventions should prioritize strengthening family care, cultivating resilience, and reducing illness-related stigma to promote social reintegration and overall well-being in CRC patients with an ostomy.Keywords: Colorectal cancer, Ostomy, Family care, Social alienation, Resilience, Stigma

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is among the most prevalent malignancies worldwide, with persistently high incidence and mortality rates. According to the Global Cancer Statistics 2022 report, CRC ranks third in global incidence and second in mortality.1 In China, the incidence of CRC has been increasing steadily in recent years, accompanied by a trend toward younger onset of disease.2 Within the comprehensive management of CRC, some patients undergo ostomy surgery, which plays a crucial role in preserving life and restoring intestinal function, thereby significantly extending patients' survival.3 However, ostomy creation not only alters patients’ physical anatomy, but also profoundly impacts their daily lives, social roles, and psychological well-being.4,5 Consequently, individuals living with an ostomy constitute a distinct and vulnerable population, wherein sustained nursing attention and holistic care are required.

Previous researches have shown that patients with CRC who undergo ostomy surgery commonly experience pronounced social alienation.6,7 Social alienation refers to an individual's subjective sense of alienation, rejection, or detachment from social networks.8 Due to concerns about altered body image, odor, leakage, and social stigma, CRC patients with an ostomy often avoid social interactions, limit outdoor activities, and even withdraw from employment and social roles.5,7 Such social alienation not only impedes the recovery of social functioning but may also contribute to the development of psychological problems such as depression, anxiety, and diminished self-esteem, thereby seriously compromising quality of life.9 Danielsen et al. found that patients with an ostomy frequently experience social restrictions and feelings of isolation, which are significantly associated with reduced quality of life.9 Similarly, Nichols reported that loneliness and death distress are major contributors to social withdrawal and psychological distress among ostomy patients.10 Therefore, exploring the influencing factors and underlying mechanisms of social alienation among CRC patients with an ostomy holds substantial clinical significance for informing targeted nursing interventions.

Psychosocial factors may play a crucial role in the social alienation process. Yuan et al.11 evaluated 209 patients with an ostomy in China and found that 44% reported high levels of stigma, with young age, poor body image, stool leakage, low self-efficacy, and lack of family acceptance being significant predictors. This highlighted the significance of stigma as a key variable for explaining social withdrawal among ostomy patients. Stigma refers to an individual's psychological experience of shame, discrimination, or social exclusion resulting from illness or physical changes.12 Due to alterations in physical appearance and limited control over excretion, patients with an ostomy are prone to perceiving “strange looks” from others, thereby internalizing negative labels.13 This internalization can lead to lowered self-evaluation, increased social avoidance, and intensified social alienation. Previous research has demonstrated that stigma is an important mediating factor in the psychological adjustment of cancer patients.7,13,14 However, its underlying mechanisms among individuals with an ostomy have not yet been adequately elucidated.

Resilience, a positive psychological component, refers to an individual's capacity to maintain or regain psychological equilibrium despite facing adversity.15 Patients with higher resilience are better able to cope with the discomfort, shame, and social stress associated with ostomy, thereby alleviating negative emotions and promoting social reintegration.16 Higher psychological resilience can help patients positively reframe their illness, buffering the adverse effects of stigma, and potentially forming a chain mediating pathway affecting social alienation.16

In the Chinese cultural context, family is the core of patients’ social support system. Influenced by Confucian philosophy intertwined with collectivist values, family care refers to the emotional, informational, and practical support provided by family members that helps patients cope with illness, adapt to physical changes, and maintain social functioning.17 In individuals with an ostomy, family understanding and acceptance could reduce loneliness and shame, enhancing confidence for confronting disease. However, the mechanisms through which family care could influence social alienation via psychosocial factors such as resilience and stigma remain insufficiently investigated.

Family care provides emotional reassurance and practical support, which strengthens patients' coping resources and promotes resilience. Higher resilience enables patients to reinterpret illness-related challenges through cognitive reappraisal and adaptive coping strategies. This psychological adjustment may reduce the internalization of negative social labels, thereby lowering perceived stigma and ultimately decreasing social alienation. Therefore, this study adopts the ABC-X model of family stress as the theoretical framework.18 According to this model, an individual's adaptation outcome (X) in response to a stressor (A) is shaped not only by the stressor itself but also by available resources (B) and cognitive appraisal (C).18

In the present study, under the stressor of living with an ostomy (A), family care (external resource) may enhance resilience (internal resource), which in turn influences cognitive appraisal (stigma), ultimately affecting adaptation outcomes (social alienation) (Fig. 1).

A hypothesized serial mediation model.

Based on this framework, we proposed the following hypotheses: H1, stigma mediates the relationship between family care and social alienation; H2, resilience mediates this relationship; and H3, resilience and stigma jointly form a sequential mediating pathway between family care and social alienation. Accordingly, this study aims to examine the association between family care and social alienation among patients with colorectal cancer and ostomies, and to test the proposed mediating mechanisms based on the ABC-X model.

Methods

Study design and participants

This study was conducted in the ostomy clinic of a tertiary general hospital in Fujian Province, China, from October 2024 to August 2025. Convenience sampling was used to recruit patients with CRC who underwent permanent or temporary ostomy. The inclusion criteria were: (1) age ≥ 18 years; (2) undergoing ostomy surgery for CRC with a postoperative period of ≥ 1 month; (3) being able to understand the questionnaire and complete it independently or with guidance; (4) and provision of informed consent and voluntary participation in the study. The exclusion criteria were (1) having other severe diseases (e.g., severe organ failure); and (2) severe psychiatric disorders or cognitive impairment preventing participation in the survey. Our study was conducted in accordance with the established guidelines and adhered to the principles of the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology statement.

The sample size was determined based on statistical power considerations for mediation analysis. Previous simulation study suggests that for detecting mediation effects with bias-corrected bootstrap methods, a sample size of approximately 148–200 is required to achieve 80% power for medium-sized indirect effects (e.g., path coefficients around 0.3).19 Given the absence of prior studies providing precise effect size estimates for the current sequential mediation model, a medium effect size was assumed. Therefore, a minimum sample size of approximately 150–200 participants was considered adequate.

Measures

Socio-demographic characteristics

We designed a study form to collect socio-demographic characteristics, including age, sex, residential status and location, marital status, educational level, employment status, monthly household income (CNY), medical insurance type, postoperative duration (months), ostomy type and site, self-care capacity, ostomy-related complications, and chronic disease.

Family adaptation, partnership, growth, affection, resolve index (family APGAR Index)

Family care was evaluated by the Family APGAR Index, developed by Smilkstein.20 The scale comprised 5 items covering five dimensions of family function: adaptation, partnership, growth, affection, and resolve. Each item was rated on a three-point Likert scale from 0 (“hardly ever”) to 2 (“almost always”). Total scores ranged between 0 and 10, with higher scores indicating better perceived family care and functioning. The scale has been widely used in clinical and community settings and demonstrated good reliability. In this study, the Cronbach's α for the Family APGAR Index was 0.89.

Connor-Davidson resilience Scale-10 (CD-RISC-10)

Resilience was assessed by the CD-RISC-10, which was validated by Campbell-Sills and Stein.21 The scale consisted of 10 items measuring resilience as a unidimensional construct. The participants rated each item on a five-point Likert scale from 0 (“not true at all”) to 4 (“true nearly all the time”). The total scores ranged between 0 and 40, with higher scores indicating greater psychological resilience. In this study, the Cronbach's α for the CD-RISC-10 was 0.84.

Social Impact Scale (SIS)

Perceived stigma was measured by the Social Impact Scale (SIS), originally developed by Fife and Wright.22 The scale consisted of 24 items across four dimensions: social rejection, financial insecurity, internalized shame, and social isolation. The items were rated on a four-point Likert scale from 1 (“strongly disagree”) to 4 (“strongly agree”), the positive items were scored from 4 to 1. The total score ranged between 24 and 96, with higher scores indicating greater perceived stigma. The Chinese version has demonstrated good psychometric properties. In this study, the Cronbach's α for the SIS was 0.88.

General alienation scale (GAS)

Alienation, developed by Jessor, was used for assessing patients' perceived social isolation and uncertainty about their engagement in social activities.23 The scale consisted of four dimensions (self-alienation, alienation from others, suspicion, and sense of meaninglessness), with a total of 15 items. The items were rated on a four-point Likert scale from 1 (“strongly disagree”) to 4 (“strongly agree”). The total score ranged between 15 and 60. Higher scores corresponded to a greater sense of alienation. In this study, the Cronbach's α for the GAS was 0.80.

Data collection

After obtaining approval and support from the hospital administrators and head nurse of the ostomy clinic, data were collected through a pen-and-paper version of the questionnaires. Before distributing the questionnaire, the purpose, significance and content were explained to the patients to obtain their consent. The participants completed the questionnaires independently or, if necessary, the investigator assisted by recording the patient's verbal responses. The completion time for each questionnaire was controlled within 15–30 minutes. After questionnaire collection, all responses were screened for completeness and response quality. Two researchers independently checked whether key demographic and clinical information was complete, and assessed the completeness and response quality of each questionnaire. Questionnaires were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: incomplete responses (more than 20%), or obvious response patterns (the same response option for 90% or more of the Likert-scale items). In this study, a total of 300 patients were recruited, 290 valid questionnaires were included, yielding a valid response rate of 96.7%.

Data analysis

Data analysis was performed using SPSS 27.0 (IBM, Chicago, IL, USA). The significance level was set at P < 0.05 (2-tailed). Continuous variables are expressed as means with standard deviations. Categorical variables are expressed as proportions or percentages. Independent-samples t-tests and one-way ANOVA were used to examine differences in social alienation across socio-demographic characteristics. Correlation analysis was used to detect correlations among continuous socio-demographic characteristics and variables.

To test the study hypotheses and explore the potential mechanistic pathways linking family care, stigma, resilience, and social alienation, serial multiple mediation analyses were performed using SPSS PROCESS macro version 4.0 (Model 6).24 Family care was treated as the predictor, psychological resilience and perceived stigma as the mediators, and social alienation as the outcome variable. Sociodemographic variables that were significantly associated with the outcome variable in bivariate analyses (P < 0.05) were entered as covariates in the model. The indirect effects were assessed by bootstrapping with 5000 resamples; bias-corrected 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated. A mediation effect was considered statistically significant if the 95% CI did not include zero. The PROCESS macro allowed for simultaneous estimation and testing the direct, indirect, and total effects, providing a robust assessment of the hypothesized serial mediation pathways.24

Discussion

Main findings

Based on the ABC-X model of family stress, in this study, we systematically explored the impact of family care on social alienation among CRC patients with an ostomy and assessed the sequential mediating roles of resilience and stigma. The results revealed that the patients experienced generally high levels of social alienation, whereas family care significantly mitigated this alienation. Furthermore, resilience and stigma functioned as partial and sequential mediators in this relationship. These findings provide insights into elucidating the psychological mechanisms underlying social dysfunction in CRC patients with an ostomy and offer a theoretical foundation for developing family-centered, multi-component nursing interventions.

Social alienation among CRC patients with an ostomy was reported at a moderate-to-high level, a pattern that aligns with prior evidence of post-ostomy psychosocial adaptation in Chinese clinical contexts.6 The disease mechanism—loss of bodily integrity and continence—often triggers a self-schema of a “stigmatized body,” which predisposes patients to concealment and avoidance.25 In a sociocultural context, people place great value on physical integrity and adherence to social norms,17 wherein illness is more likely to be privatized and emotional disclosure restrained, thereby attenuating access to visible social support and compounding alienation risk.11

Our model showed a significant negative association between family care and social alienation, underscoring family care as the base resource of the support system. Beyond emotional and instrumental aid, family interactions validate role identity, interrupt self-stigma, and scaffold resocialization—a mechanism specifically salient in collectivist settings.26 This emphasis is consistent with the findings of Kim et al.’s research in Chinese cohorts wherein perceived social support reliably predicts adjustment, with robust psychometric performance.4,12 In addition, Shen et al. conducted a SEM study, reporting that social support co-acts with resilience and hope to buffer distress in caregiving settings, suggesting that family resources mitigate perceived burden through both direct and mediated paths.18 From a nursing standpoint, routine assessment of family functioning and targeted coaching to enhance supportive competencies (acceptance communication, contingency planning) are warranted to reduce alienation via family-level leverage.

Moreover, our research confirmed that family care significantly promotes resilience, and that higher resilience, in turn, predicts lower social alienation—indicating partial mediation. From the perspective of the stress and coping theory, supportive family care serves as a critical external resource that enhances individuals' perceived coping capacity. Family members who provide emotional reassurance, illness-related information, and practical assistance may help patients reinterpret ostomy-related changes as manageable rather than overwhelming, thereby enabling patients to reinterpret ostomy-related change with greater control and less self-rejection.16,27 Within collectivist cultural contexts such as China, family is often the primary source of identity validation and social belonging.28 Adequate family care not only provides instrumental support but also reinforces patients’ sense of worth and role continuity, which may otherwise be disrupted by bodily changes following ostomy. This process mitigates threats to self-concept and promotes psychological stability, thereby fostering resilience. Studies have shown that culturally embedded family care plays a pivotal role in enhancing resilience among patients with chronic illnesses, particularly in societies where interdependence is highly valued.28,29 Resilient individuals are more likely to engage in active coping, maintain social participation, and resist maladaptive cognitive patterns such as self-stigmatization and social withdrawal.28,30 They tend to perceive illness-related challenges as less identity-defining and are therefore less prone to disengage from social roles. This aligns with prior findings indicating that resilience can buffer the negative effects of illness-related stress on psychosocial adaptation, with effect sizes comparable to or even exceeding those of social support in some contexts.29,30

Illness stigma significantly mediated the relationship between family care and social alienation, which can be explained through interconnected cognitive and social mechanisms. Stigma originates from the internalization of negative societal labels into self-concept, leading to self-devaluation and anticipated rejection.11,22 In the context of ostomy, visible bodily changes and concerns about leakage or odor may heighten patients’ sensitivity to social judgment, thereby increasing the risk of internalized stigma. Family care plays a critical role in interrupting this process. Supportive family interactions can provide alternative, affirming appraisals that counterbalance negative societal messages. Through expressions of acceptance, normalization of the illness experience, and maintenance of pre-illness roles within the family, patients may reconstruct a more positive self-identity, thereby weakening the internalization of stigma.31 In turn, reduced stigma is associated with lower levels of social alienation. Patients with lower stigma are less likely to engage in avoidance behaviors, concealment, and social withdrawal, and are more willing to maintain interpersonal interactions and social roles. Prior studies have demonstrated that stigma is a key predictor of social disengagement and impaired psychosocial functioning across chronic illness populations.22

The sequential mediation pathway observed in this study suggests that family care may reduce social alienation by enhancing resilience and subsequently reducing stigma. This finding can be interpreted within the ABC–X model. From this perspective, family care operates as a foundational resource that not only supports coping processes but also shapes how stressors are cognitively appraised. Rather than functioning solely as emotional support, family care represents a multidimensional protective system encompassing informational guidance, instrumental assistance, and appraisal-related feedback. Through these mechanisms, it strengthens patients’ perceived control and facilitates meaning-making in response to chronic illness and body-altering procedures.32 In this context, resilience emerges as a proximal psychological resource, cultivated through sustained supportive interactions, that enables individuals to reinterpret illness experiences in less threatening and more adaptive terms.33 Importantly, this enhanced resilience may precede and contribute to reductions in stigma by buffering the internalization of negative societal evaluations. Patients with greater adaptive capacity are more likely to resist self-stigmatizing beliefs and maintain a more stable sense of self, thereby reducing the downstream risk of social alienation. This ordered relationship between resilience and stigma provides a more fine-grained understanding of the adaptation process. Consistent with prior research in caregiving and ostomy populations, multiple psychosocial resources (including resilience, hope, and social support) often operate in combination to promote adjustment.12,34 However, the present study extends existing evidence by empirically delineating the sequential ordering of these mechanisms, suggesting that resilience may function as an upstream facilitator of stigma reduction within the broader adaptation pathway.

Implications for nursing practice and research

The findings of this study provide important implications for nursing practice and research. Understanding how family care, resilience, and stigma interact to influence social alienation among CRC patients with an ostomy offers a theoretical and practical basis for developing targeted nursing interventions. From a clinical perspective, nursing interventions should prioritize strengthening family care as an upstream factor. In addition, given the mediating role of resilience, nurses should focus on supporting patients’ adaptive coping capacity and promoting more positive cognitive appraisal of illness-related challenges. Importantly, the significant mediating role of stigma suggests that reducing perceived stigma should be an essential component of nursing care. Approaches that address negative self-perceptions and facilitate social reintegration may help mitigate the internalization of stigma and its adverse effects on social functioning.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be considered. First, participants were recruited from a single tertiary hospital in Fujian Province, China, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Regional differences in socioeconomic status, health care resources, and cultural attitudes toward illness and family involvement may influence patients’ experiences of family care, stigma, and social alienation. Therefore, caution is warranted when generalizing these findings to populations in other regions or health care settings, and future multi-center studies across diverse geographic and cultural contexts are needed. Moreover, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference among the studied variables. Although the proposed mediation model is theoretically grounded, the temporal relationships between family care, resilience, stigma, and social alienation cannot be confirmed. Longitudinal or prospective studies are needed to further examine the dynamic changes of these variables over time.

Conclusions

This study explored the psychological pathways associated with family care and social alienation among CRC patients with an ostomy. Family care was directly associated with lower levels of social alienation and was indirectly associated through higher resilience and reduced stigma. The sequential pathway “family care→resilience→stigma→social alienation” suggests that adaptive family dynamics and positive psychological resources may jointly buffer the adverse psychosocial impact of body-altering conditions. Nursing interventions that empower families, foster resilience, and address stigma are essential for improving patients’ social functioning and overall quality of life.
