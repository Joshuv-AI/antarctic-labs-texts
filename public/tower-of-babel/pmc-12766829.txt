Self-harm and suicidal behaviours among undergraduate students in Gandaki Province, Nepal: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Manu Kumari Lamtari Magar, Abhishek Sapkota, Alisha Thapa, Upkar Gurung, Arvind Kumar Shahi, Mani Neupane, Krishna Prasad Sapkota
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionSelf-harm and suicidal behaviours are major public health issues among adolescents. Suicide is considered the third cause of death among 15–29-year-old individuals worldwide. The study aimed to determine the prevalence of self-harm and suicidal behaviours among undergraduate students in Nepal and identify the sociodemographic, behavioural and psychological factors associated with these behaviours.MethodsA school-based cross-sectional study was conducted among undergraduates from four schools of Vyas Municipality, Nepal. The Deliberate Self-Harm Behaviour Scale and the General Health Questionnaire-28 were the standard tools used to measure self-harm and suicidal behaviour.ResultsAmong 538 participants, the prevalence of self-harm behaviour, suicidal ideation, plans and attempts was found to be 15.8% (n=85), 30.3% (n=163), 3.2% (n=17) and 1.9% (n=10), respectively. Relationship difficulty was significantly associated with self-harm (adjusted OR (AOR)=2.42, 95% CI 1.10 to 5.35, p=0.028), while relationship difficulty (AOR=3.29, 95% CI 1.44 to 7.54, p=0.005) and family mental health issues (AOR=4.69, 95% CI 1.64 to 13.47, p=0.004) were significantly associated with suicidal ideation.ConclusionsRelationship difficulty and family mental health issues are identified as major causes of the alarmingly high rates of self-harm and suicidal acts among Nepalese adolescents.Keywords: Adolescent, Mental Health, Public Health

Introduction

Self-harm and suicidal behaviours are major public health issues among adolescents, and the prevalence of self-harm during adolescence is at 16%, with suicide ranking as the third leading cause of death among 15–29-year-old individuals worldwide.1 2 By definition, self-harm is a persistent, socially unacceptable behaviour of inflicting cuts, bruises or other self-injuries that result in mild to moderate bodily injury.3 Self-harm is present in all age groups; however, young adults and adolescents are more prone to this problem.4

In the context of Nepal, the situation is particularly concerning. According to WHO data, Nepal had the third-highest rate of female suicide deaths in South Asia in 2016, with a rate of 11.6 per 100 000 population.5 Suicide rates in the country have shown a steady rise, with a reported 14% increase in 2021 alone.6 Between 2019 and 2023, all seven provinces experienced rising suicide rates, with the most significant increases in Koshi, Madhesh and Gandaki provinces.7 While national statistics primarily document suicide mortality, there is limited surveillance on non-fatal suicidal behaviours such as ideation, planning and attempts, especially among young people, hindering early intervention. Young people, particularly adolescents and young adults, are disproportionately affected by both suicidal ideation and self-harm. Numerous studies have found that interpersonal issues, such as family conflict and relationship problems, as well as mental health issues, including depression, anxiety and loneliness, are major contributing factors.8 While age ranges for adolescence and young adulthood vary, individuals aged 15–29 are commonly grouped in suicide statistics, as this group encompasses both secondary school and tertiary education students.9 However, most undergraduate college and university students typically fall within the 17–24 age range, a transitional life stage marked by academic pressure, social changes and increased independence, all of which may exacerbate vulnerability to emotional distress.10

Evidence suggests that college and university students are a particularly vulnerable subgroup.11 In some studies, suicide was found to account for up to 19% of all student deaths in higher education institutions.12 The prevalence of suicidal ideation and attempts remains alarmingly high in this group, despite slight declines in suicide mortality in some regions.4 Notably, the vast majority of research in this area has focused on completed suicides, with relatively less attention paid to non-fatal suicidal behaviours such as ideation and attempts, which are more common and potentially preventable.13 Furthermore, much of the literature on college students’ mental health comes from high-income countries, with limited evidence from low- and middle-income contexts like Nepal.

Sociocultural factors in Nepal significantly influence adolescent mental health and well-being.14 Extended or joint family structures, academic and social expectations, and adherence to cultural norms can contribute to stress and psychological distress. Mental health problems are stigmatised, and limited awareness in communities often discourages help-seeking.15 Gender roles, social hierarchy, caste, geographic location and household economic status further shape adolescents’ mental health outcomes.14 Evidence from Pokhara shows that adolescents’ psychological well-being is influenced by family support, exposure to violence and parental presence or migration, highlighting the complex interplay of familial, social and cultural factors in Nepalese adolescents.16 Given the increasing rates of suicide in Nepal and the vulnerability of college-aged populations, there is a critical need to examine the prevalence and associated factors of both self-harm and suicidal behaviours, including ideation, plans and attempts among undergraduate students. Understanding the underlying behavioural, psychological and socio-demographic correlates can inform targeted interventions and campus-based mental health strategies. Therefore, this study aims to assess the prevalence and factors associated with self-harm and suicidal behaviours among undergraduate students in Vyas Municipality, Nepal.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

A school-based cross-sectional study was conducted in Vyas municipality, located in Gandaki province of Nepal, about 150 km west of Kathmandu, the capital city. The study population was undergraduate students of Vyas Municipality, Tanahun. Vyas Municipality was selected because it represents a mix of rural and urban settings, offering a diverse population in terms of sociodemographic and economic characteristics. Its accessibility facilitated data collection within the constraints of limited time and resources. In addition, the presence of several higher education institutions allowed us to reach undergraduate students, the target population of this study.

Sample size and sampling technique

The required sample size for the study was 538, determined using the following parameters: a 62.2% combined prevalence of self-harm and suicidal behaviours,4 an allowable margin of error of 5% (0.05), a design effect of 2 to account for clustering in the sampling, a 95% CI, and an anticipated non-response rate of 10%. The formula for calculating the sample size for a single proportion was applied: n0=Z.2p.(1−p)/d2, where n0=initial sample size; Z=standard normal deviate corresponding to 95% CI (1.96); p=estimated prevalence (0.622); d=allowable error (0.05). After calculating the initial sample size (n0), it was multiplied by the design effect (2) to adjust for clustering. After adjusting for the design effect, the sample size was further increased by 10% to account for potential non-response, resulting in a final required sample size of 538 participants.

A proportionate stratified random sampling technique was used to ensure fair representation of students across all institutions. First, all four colleges in Vyas Municipality were identified and included in the study. The total number of students enrolled in each college was obtained through visits to the institutions and consultation with administrative staff. The study sample was then allocated to each college proportionally based on its student population. To determine how many students to select from each college, the average number of students per class was calculated, and the number of classes needed to reach the target sample for each college was identified. Classes were then randomly selected, and all students present in those classes on the day of the survey were invited to participate. In cases where a selected class had more students than the college’s allocated sample, additional random selection was performed within the class to meet the target.

Data collection techniques and tools

Data collection was conducted using a paper-and-pencil, self-administered questionnaire. The questionnaire was prepared through an extensive literature review and continuous consultation with the supervisor.

The Deliberate Self-Harm Behaviour Scale, which consists of 17 items, was adapted from the Deliberate Self Harm Inventory to assess self-harm behaviour (whether participants cut their wrists, burned themselves with cigarette, burned themselves with a lighter or match, carved words or images into their skin, scratched and bit themselves, rubbed sandpaper on their bodies, dripped acid on their skin, scrubbed their skin with bleach or other chemicals, stuck sharp objects into their skin, rubbed glass into their skin, broken their bones, banged their head against something, punched themselves, prevented wounds from healing, or done anything else to hurt themselves in the previous year).17 Following their actual conduct during the previous year, respondents chose whether to answer yes (marked up as 1) or no (marked up as 0) to the 17 items. A yes in any of the harm questions was marked as the presence of self-harm behaviour.

Questionnaires on suicidal plan and ideation were adopted from a study conducted among Ugandan university students.18 The questions to assess suicidal ideation included: (a) ‘Have you ever felt that life isn’t worth living in the last 12 months?’ (b) ‘Have you ever found yourselves wishing you were gone and far away from it all in the past twelve months?’ (c) ‘Have you explored thoughts that you might eliminate yourself in the past 12 months?’ and (d) ‘Has the thought of ending your life repeatedly crossed your mind over the past 12 months?’ These four questions were graded on a 4-point scale: 0=‘not at all/not’, 1=‘no more than usual/I don’t think so’, 2=‘rather more than usual/has crossed my mind’ and 3=‘much more than usual/definitely’. Suicidal ideation was graded on a scale from 0 to 3, with 2 or 3 being considered positive and 0 or 1 being negative. A score of 0 was assigned to a negative response, and 1 was assigned to a positive response. Suicidal ideation was scored on a continuous scale of zero to four using the binary results for each of the four questions. Suicidal ideation was measured using a binary scale, where a score of 1 or above was regarded as indicative of the condition.

A suicidal plan was assessed by asking, ‘Have you thought about committing suicide in the last 12 months? (yes/ no)’ and suicidal attempt was examined by asking, ‘Have you tried to commit suicide in the last 12 months? (yes/no)’.

Quality assurance

The adopted tools for self-harm and suicidal behaviours were in the English language. The adopted tools were merged with the sociodemographic and behavioural characteristics questionnaire, which was developed in consultation with the supervisor. The English tool was translated into the Nepali language. The validity and reliability of the tools were established through pretesting the tools among 54 randomly selected undergraduate students of the School of Health and Allied Sciences, Pokhara University. A few minor adjustments were made to the wording of the questions based on the pretest results, in consultation with experts, and some response options for key variables were modified to reflect the characteristics of the pretest participants. For living arrangements, the original options included only ‘with family’ and ‘alone’; however, since some students reported living with friends, this option was added. For marital status, the original options included ‘never married’, ‘married’, ‘divorced’, ‘separated’, ‘living together’ and ‘widow’. As all participants in the pretest were either married or never married, the options were narrowed to these two categories.

Data management and analysis

The collected data were checked for accuracy and completeness. Data were compiled, edited and checked to maintain consistency. Duplications and omissions of data were corrected before coding and entering them in Epi-Data (V.3.1). Then, the data were exported to SPSS (V.25) for analysis. Frequency distribution, mean and percentage were calculated as descriptive statistics to determine the characteristics of the participants. For inferential analysis, both bivariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were conducted. In the bivariate analysis, unadjusted ORs with 95% CIs and corresponding p values were calculated to assess the association between each independent variable and the outcome variables: self-harm and suicidal ideation. Following this, all independent variables were included in the multivariate logistic regression model, regardless of their statistical significance in the bivariate analysis. This approach was chosen to control for potential confounding factors and to examine the independent effect of each variable on the outcomes of interest. The results of the multivariate analysis were presented as adjusted ORs (AOR) with 95% CIs and p values. A p<0.05 was considered statistically significant. The goodness-of-fit of the final model was evaluated using the Hosmer-Lemeshow test.

Results

Sociodemographic characteristics

The total number of students who participated in this study was 538, with a response rate of 100%. Their mean age was (20.51±1.775) years. The majority, 424 (78.8%) of the participants were female. Academic year distribution showed that most participants were in their first year, 188 (34.9%). Regarding religion, the vast majority identified as Hindu, 491 (91.3%), with minorities belonging to the Muslim faith, 13 (2.4%). Ethnically, over half were Janajati, 291 (54.1%), followed by Brahmin/Chhetri, 160 (29.7%). Most students were never married, 462 (85.9%) and the majority lived with family, 441 (82%), while smaller proportions lived alone, 69 (12.8%), or with friends, 28 (5.2%). Behavioural and health-related factors showed low prevalence of substance use: 16 (3%) were past cigarette smokers and 6 (1.1%) current smokers; 26 (4.8%) were past alcohol consumers and 14 (2.6%) currently consumed alcohol; psychoactive substance use was minimal at 5 (0.9%). Physical health illnesses in the past year were reported by 59 (11%), while mental health illnesses were reported by 98 (18.2%) of participants. Stressful life events within the past year were experienced by 201 (37.4%) of students. Among these, family problems were most common, 133 (66.2%), and campus ragging was rare, 1 (0.5%). Family history of psychiatric conditions was present in a small proportion of students: 41 (7.6%) reported family mental health illness, 10 (1.9%) had a family history of suicide, and 9 (1.7%) had family members who attempted suicide (table 1).

The prevalence of self-harm behaviour, suicidal ideation, plans and attempts was found to be 15.8% (n=85), 30.3% (n=163), 3.2% (n=17) and 1.9% (n=10), respectively. Among these subgroups, the majority were in their first to second academic years, with 54 (63.5%) of self-harm cases, 101 (62.0%) of those with suicidal ideation, 11 (64.7%) of those with suicide plans, and 8 (80.0%) of those who had attempted suicide belonging to early academic years. The age group 18–23 years was predominant across all behaviours, accounting for 79 (92.9%) of self-harm, 158 (96.9%) of suicidal ideation, 15 (88.2%) of suicide plans and 9 (90.0%) of suicide attempts.

Females were disproportionately represented in all behaviour categories, comprising 65 (76.5%) of the self-harm group, 130 (79.8%) of those with suicidal ideation, 14 (82.4%) with suicide plans, and all 10 (100%) of the suicide attempts. Participants from disadvantaged ethnic groups made up the majority across all categories, particularly among those reporting self-harm, 66 (77.6%) and suicidal ideation, 119 (73.0%). Hindu participants were the majority in all subgroups, with more than 89% representation. While most participants with suicidal or self-harming behaviours were unmarried, a notable proportion of those with suicide plans, 7 (41.2%), and attempts, 4 (40.0%) were married, indicating possible associations with more severe outcomes. Additionally, the majority of these participants resided at home (over 78% across all subgroups) and lived with their parents (table 2).

Discussion

A cross-sectional study was conducted to study the prevalence and factors relating to self-harm and suicidal behaviours among undergraduate students of Vyas municipality in the past 12 months. In the present study, the prevalence of self-harm behaviours was 15.8%, which was lower compared with a study among adolescents in Pokhara, Nepal (57.9%).19 The prevalence of self-harm behaviour was also lower compared with a cross-sectional analytical study among young adults aged 18–24 years residing in Kathmandu district of Nepal (18.5%),20 but was similar to research involving adolescents studying in grades 9 to 12 of public and private schools of Pokhara Metropolitan city, Nepal (15.6%).4 The prevalence is higher compared with European studies (4.1%), which may result from differences in the sociodemographic characteristics of the participants, study design and methods.21

The prevalence of suicidal ideation in this study (30.3%) was higher than the study conducted among students of grades 7–11 in five development regions of Nepal (13.59%), and a study in Pokhara metropolitan, Nepal (8.8%).4 8 This finding was similar to a study among adolescents in selected higher secondary schools of Kathmandu Valley, Nepal (29%).22

Of the participants in this study, 3.2% reported suicidal planning, while 1.9% reported suicidal attempts in the past 12 months. The prevalence of suicidal planning in this study is lower than a study among adolescents in Pokhara, Nepal (6.5%), but was higher than a study conducted in the capital city of Nepal (2%).19 20 Regarding suicidal attempts, the figure in this study was lower than a cross-sectional study in Western Nepal (3.5%) and the Global School Health Survey in Nepal, which reported that 10.3% school students had attempted suicide.4 8

The relatively lower prevalence of self-harm behaviours, suicidal planning and attempts observed in this study may be attributed to several contextual and methodological factors. First, the study population consisted of undergraduate students who may have greater access to peer networks, academic counselling and social support systems that could mitigate suicidal tendencies. Second, the awareness and mental health literacy among university students are likely higher than among adolescents or community populations, which may reduce high-risk behaviours. Third, cultural stigma surrounding self-harm and suicide in Nepal could have led to underreporting, particularly in face-to-face surveys. Additionally, regional and institutional differences in stressors, such as academic pressure, family expectations or exposure to violence, may explain the variation in prevalence across studies conducted in different settings within Nepal and abroad. These findings raise the need for tailored interventions, taking into consideration the specific socio-cultural and psychological factors that may influence the prevalence of suicidal behaviours.

In this study, multiple logistic regression analysis was conducted to examine associations between various sociodemographic, behavioural and psychological factors with self-harm and suicidal ideation among undergraduate students. Relationship difficulty showed a significant association with self-harm behaviour (AOR=2.42, 95% CI 1.10 to 5.35, p=0.028). This finding is consistent with previous research conducted in Egypt and Taiwan, which also highlighted the role of relationship difficulties as a significant risk factor for self-harm behaviours among university students.23 24 Stressful life events, such as relationship problems, have long been recognised as contributing to self-harm tendencies. For suicidal ideation, relationship difficulty (AOR=3.29, 95% CI 1.44 to 7.54, p=0.005) and family mental health issues (AOR=4.69, 95% CI 1.64 to 13.47, p=0.004) were significantly associated. This finding of the association between relationship difficulties and suicidal ideation agrees with the studies conducted in Bangladesh, which reported that life stressors such as relationship problems contributed to suicidal ideation among university students.9 Moreover, the significant association found between family mental health issues and suicidal ideation is consistent with several other studies, which also found a strong link between familial mental health history and the development of suicidal ideation.25,28 In the context of Nepal, there is a scarcity of empirical studies exploring the association between relationship difficulties and suicidal ideation or self-harm behaviours among students. Most existing studies in Nepal have primarily focused on estimating prevalence and identifying general correlates such as gender, age, depression, substance use and academic stress. The limited exploration of interpersonal and romantic relationship factors may be due to the cultural sensitivity surrounding discussions of romantic relationships, particularly among unmarried youth, which could discourage both open disclosure by participants and academic inquiry. Therefore, this study adds value by highlighting relationship difficulties as a potential risk factor, suggesting the need for future research in Nepal to examine this association in greater depth within culturally appropriate frameworks. The history of both personal and familial factors, including relationship difficulties and family mental health issues, plays an important role in the development of self-harm behaviours and suicidal ideation among undergraduate students. The fact that family mental health problems predict suicidal ideation underlines the relevance of the family approach when considering university students’ mental health.

The findings of this study carry important implications for mental health intervention strategies among undergraduate students in Nepal. The high prevalence of suicidal ideation and self-harm behaviours, particularly in association with relationship problems and family mental health issues, highlights the need for proactive mental health services in academic institutions. University counselling centres should be adequately equipped to provide psychological support that includes family history assessments and relationship counselling. Moreover, stigma surrounding mental health issues and help-seeking behaviour remains a significant barrier in Nepal; thus, awareness campaigns and peer-led programmes may help improve mental health literacy and reduce stigma among students. Given the significant link between familial mental illness and suicidal ideation, interventions should consider involving families in student support systems where feasible.

Implications

These findings suggest that suicide prevention efforts should extend beyond severe mental illness to also address everyday psychosocial stressors that students face. Policy-level interventions may include integrating mental health screening into routine university health services and training faculty and staff to recognise early signs of distress. Furthermore, as Nepal lacks a comprehensive national suicide prevention framework, evidence from this study can inform localised, evidence-based strategies aimed at early identification and intervention.

Limitations

Whereas the findings make several valuable contributions, some critical limitations have to be acknowledged. The study also suffers from a relatively small sample size for some variables, which may reduce statistical power. Besides this, the mental and physical health conditions are based on self-report data, potentially biased and inaccurate. Additionally, as a cross-sectional study design, the study limits the ability to infer causality. Since the study was confined to undergraduate students in a single municipality, the findings may not be generalisable to students in other regions or educational settings.

Future research

Future studies should employ longitudinal designs to examine changes in self-harm and suicidal behaviours over time and to identify causal pathways. Expanding research to multiple municipalities and diverse academic institutions would enhance generalisability and help identify regional or institutional differences. Qualitative studies exploring students’ lived experiences and coping mechanisms would also provide deeper insight into the psychosocial contexts of these behaviours.

Conclusions

This study highlights the considerable prevalence of self-harm and suicidal behaviours among undergraduate students in Nepal, with strong associations identified between these behaviours and factors such as relationship difficulties and family history of mental health issues. These findings underscore the urgent need for targeted mental health interventions within university settings. Strategies should include relationship counselling, family-focused mental health assessments and stigma reduction programmes to encourage help-seeking behaviour. Moreover, the evidence presented can inform the development of contextually appropriate mental health policies and preventive frameworks to better support the well-being of young adults in Nepal’s academic institutions.
