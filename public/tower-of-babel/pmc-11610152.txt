Dysregulation of protein degradation and alteration of secretome in α-synuclein-exposed astrocytes: implications for dopaminergic neuronal dysfunction
Authors: Aishwarya Raj, Roon Banerjee, Vikram Holla, Nitish Kamble, Ravi Yadav, Pramod Kumar Pal, Indrani Datta
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundA key factor in the propagation of α-synuclein pathology is the compromised protein quality control system. Variations in membrane association and astrocytic uptake between different α-synuclein forms suggest differences in exocytosis or membrane cleavage, potentially impacting the secretome's influence on dopaminergic neurons. We aimed to understand differences in protein degradation mechanisms of astrocytes for both wild-type (WT) and mutant forms of α-synuclein, specifically during periods of reduced degradation efficiency. We also investigated α-synuclein release into the secretome and its effects on healthy dopaminergic neurons.MethodsCellular models used were rat primary astrocytes alongside hiPSC-derived astrocytes, whose impact on rat primary dopaminergic neurons and the human SH-SY5Y cell line was investigated. We examined the release and accumulation of α-synuclein resulting from impaired degradatory pathways, including matrix metalloprotease-MMP9, the ubiquitin proteasomal pathway-UPS, and the autophagy-lysosomal pathway-ALP, using immunocytochemical analysis and flow cytometry. Additionally, we explored the effect of astrocytic secretome on dopaminergic-neuronal survival, neurite collapse and function.ResultsAt early stages, astrocytes were able to deal efficiently with monomeric α-synuclein (via UPS), and larger aggregates (through MMP9 and autophagy), clearing extracellular α-synuclein and maintaining neuronal health. However, extended exposure to extracellular monomeric and aggregated α-synuclein compromised their proteasomal activity, inhibiting MMP9 and destabilizing autophagy, transforming astrocytes from protectors to promoters of neurodegeneration. This study is the first to elucidate the astrocytes' preferred degradation pathways for both monomeric and aggregated forms of α-synuclein, along with the subsequent effects of these payloads on the cellular degradation machinery. The astrocytic transformation is characterized by α-synuclein expulsion, increased release of inflammatory cytokines, and diminished secretion of growth factors leading to dopaminergic neuronal apoptosis and dysfunction, particularly neurite collapse, intracellular Ca2+ response and vesicular dopamine release. The presence of phosphorylated and nitrated α-synuclein species in astrocytes also suggests their potential involvement in modifying both forms of the protein.ConclusionThe initial protective action of astrocytes in clearing and degrading extracellular α-synuclein is severely compromised at latter stages, leading to astrocytic dysfunction and impairing neuron-glia cross-talk. This study underscores the criticality of integrating astrocytes into treatment paradigms in synucleinopathies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12964-024-01928-9.

Introduction

Neurodegenerative disorders such as Parkinson’s disease (PD), dementia with Lewy bodies (DLB), multiple system atrophy (MSA) etc. have shown intense pathology of α-synuclein accumulation in the form of deposits [16, 30, 36]. In particular, PD has been associated with α-synuclein duplication, mutation and misfolding [86, 101, 105]. This protein inherent in dopaminergic neurons forms aggregates, which are known to be released into the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) facilitated by unconventional exocytosis, due to the absence of a secretory signal in α-synuclein [37, 39, 83]. Increased levels of α-synuclein aggregates observed in the CSF of PD patients [40, 83, 112] further suggest the significance of these aggregates in the etiology of PD.

Initially, the role of astrocytes in the progression of PD pathology was overlooked, primarily owing to the number of astrocytes remaining consistent and not decreasing in PD post-mortem findings [71, 112]. However, a recent and expanding body of evidence from post-mortem studies has highlighted the association of astrocytic dysregulation with synucleinopathies in general [74, 103]. Astrocytes exhibit dystrophic morphological changes, heightened immunoreactivity, and presence of α-synuclein aggregates, indicating dysregulation [11, 53, 54, 56]. These cells lining the blood–brain barrier directly interact with extracellular α-synuclein in CSF, a phenomenon widely observed in PD and other LB diseases [2, 6, 53, 54]. Hishikawa et al., [42] demonstrated that PD brains, regardless of duration of illness, displayed α-synuclein-positive glial inclusions. These inclusions were found not only in regions with neuronal loss and gliosis but also in the cerebral cortex, where their distribution correlated with the appearance of LBs and catecholaminergic neurons and fibers. This suggests that glial inclusions may appear early in PD pathology, even before apparent neuronal loss, with their distribution paralleling that of LBs and catecholaminergic neurons and their projections.

Initially, astrocytes play a neuroprotective role by internalizing and degrading α-synuclein [80, 110, 113]. However, a critical threshold may be reached, limiting astrocytic uptake of the protein and fostering LB pathology. Once this threshold is reached, astrocytes may induce inflammatory cytokine production, fostering an inflammatory microenvironment and sustained neuronal damage, accompanied by aberrant calcium signaling as reported in our earlier study [87]. The simultaneous bidirectional prion-like transmission of α-synuclein between neurons and astrocytes further exacerbates this process. The astrocytic response to neuronal α-synuclein release thus represents a 'double-edged sword', with potential neuroprotective or neurotoxic outcomes depending on the astrocytic capacity for α-synuclein internalization. In individuals with PD who have undergone fetal neuron transplants, the emergence of Lewy pathology in previously unaffected grafted neurons strongly suggests the transmission of α-synuclein pathology from neighboring PD-affected neurons [49, 55].

Our previous study revealed a time-dependent correlation of α-synuclein in astrocytes, with a saturation point observed at 24 h of exposure to extracellular α-synuclein, suggesting a potential slowdown in protein degradation and subsequent accumulation [88]. Furthermore, differences were observed in the engulfment mechanisms between monomeric and aggregated forms of the protein, with the latter exhibiting higher membrane association [28, 79, 87], as well as elevated endoplasmic reticulum (ER) calcium deregulation mediated by interaction with membrane store-operated calcium entry (SOCE) proteins, resulting in higher calcium influx [87]. These membrane contact sites exert widespread influence, impacting not just ER stress but also the protein degradation mechanisms of macroautophagy and intracellular cargo transport systems, potentially altering the early stages of vesicle/vacuole formation [70, 118]. Moreover, disruptions in calcium homeostasis and ER stress can perturb the protein degradation machinery, affecting proteasomal activity, autophagy, and matrix metalloproteases (MMPs) [17]. Calcium plays a crucial role in regulating autophagy through various mechanisms [91]. In MCF-7 cells, Høyer-Hansen et al., [44] demonstrated that elevated cytosolic calcium induced by various calcium-mobilizing agents robustly activated autophagy. Thapsigargin, known for depleting ER calcium, acted as a potent autophagy activator by inhibiting rapamycin (mTOR) in a calcium/calmodulin-dependent protein kinase kinase (CAMKK)-dependent manner. Furthermore, Medina et al., [65] reported that thapsigargin alone induced nuclear translocation of transcription Factor EB (TFEB), a master regulator of autophagy, suggesting that ER calcium release indeed triggers autophagy activation. Dysregulation of calcium homeostasis in the ER stores may result in reduced activity of extracellular proteases and lysosomal impairments [78, 84, 90], and the activity of MMPs is directly influenced by the availability of their co-factor, divalent calcium ions [84].

The differential membrane association and endocytosis of monomeric and aggregated forms of α-synuclein suggest a potential disparity in the exocytosis or membrane cleavage of the protein from astrocytes treated with α-synuclein, and its consequential impact on the secretome's influence on healthy dopaminergic neuronal cells. Given these variations in association modes, it is critical to investigate the preferred degradation pathways for α-synuclein in its monomeric and aggregated states and the subsequent impact of these payloads on the cellular degradation machinery. In this study, our objective is to understand the engagement of distinct protein degradation mechanisms in astrocytes for monomeric and aggregated forms of α-synuclein (both wild type—WT and mutant). We seek to explore their impact on the protein degradation machinery precisely at the juncture when degradation processes exhibit a slowdown. Additionally, we also aim to evaluate the release of α-synuclein into the secretome at this critical time point and subsequently investigate the impact of the secretome on the survival and functionality of other healthy dopaminergic neuronal cells. This inquiry is crucial for therapeutic strategies involving the transplantation of healthy human dopaminergic neurons or progenitors into PD or DLB patients, where the astrocytes have already encountered extracellular α-synuclein. This will also shed further light on the importance of the microenvironment in neurodegenerative conditions.

Materials and methods

Reagents

All reagents used in this work are mentioned in the supplementary information.

Ethical clearance

Animal ethics approval was obtained from the Institutional Animal Ethics Committee (IAEC) of National Institute of Mental Health and Neuro Sciences (NIMHANS) for all the animal experiments (IAEC reference no. AEC/70/456/B.P and AEC/70/455/B.P). Animals were housed in an advanced environment at the Central Animal Research Facility [Reg. No. 12/GO/ReBi/S/99/CPCSEA] situated at NIMHANS with 12:12 light and dark cycles and food and water ad libitum. All experiments were conducted as per the guidelines of the Committee for the Purpose of Control and Supervision of Experiments on Animals, Government of India.

Institutional Committee for Stem Cell Research (IC-SCR) approval with IC-SCR no. SEC/05/030/BP was obtained for the generation, differentiation and utilization of human induced pluripotent stem cells (hiPSCs) used in this study.

Cell cultures

Astrocytes were isolated from the midbrain of postnatal day 2–4 rat pups and further purified by agitation to obtain pure astrocytes. Cells from the P1 passage was used for experimentation. This is described in our earlier publication [88].

Astrocytes were generated from human induced pluripotent stem cell (hiPSC) lines through a two-step method, involving a glial progenitor cell (GPC) stage and the terminally differentiated astrocytes as described in an earlier publication from our group [4]. These cells were used for confirmation of results only when mentioned in the text.

DA neurons were isolated from the midbrain region of rat embryos of day point 16–18. The protocol followed from an earlier work of our group [15, 34], briefly described below. The midbrain region was dissected and dissociated using StemPro™ Accutase™ for 10 min at 37 °C. These were pelleted at 1000 rpm for 5 min. 5X106 per drop were plated by hanging drop method on the lid of a culture dish in proliferative media [Neurobasal™ media supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 1X N2 supplement, 1X GlutaMAX™ and 1X penicillin–streptomycin]. Incubation at 37 °C with 5% CO2. After two days in vitro (DIV) the spheres were gathered onto a non-adherent dish and allowed to grow till five DIV in proliferative media. Further, the neurospheres were plated on poly-L-lysine (PLL) coated dishes with differentiation media [Neurobasal™ media supplemented with 1X N2 supplement, 30 mM glucose, 100 μM ascorbic acid, 1X GlutaMAX™, 1X penicillin–streptomycin and 20 ng/mL basic fibroblast growth factor (bFGF)]. Incubation at 37 °C with 5% CO2. Media change given every second day till day five after plating and then taken for experimentation. The characterization data is included in the supplementary information.

The human neuroblastoma cell line (SH-SY5Y) was a kind gift from Dr. Asok Mukhopadhyay, National Institute of Immunology (NII), New Delhi. The SH-SY5Y cells were maintained in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium/Ham’s F12 nutrient mixture (DMEM/F12) medium supplemented with 10% FBS, 1X Glutamax™ and 1X penicillin–streptomycin solution. These cells were used for confirmation of results only when mentioned in the text.

Preparation and labelling of α-synuclein aggregates

WT and A30P/A53T double mutant monomeric α-synuclein were aggregated and labelled with a fluophore, DyLight™ 488 NHS ester as described and characterized in our earlier works [87, 88]. To verify that the observed green fluorescence during α-synuclein treatment originated from the tagged protein and not the tag alone, an immunocytochemistry (ICC) experiment was conducted. Astrocytes treated with fluorophore-tagged α-synuclein were probed with an α-synuclein antibody, followed by detection with an Alexa Fluor® 647-conjugated secondary antibody. The fluorescence overlap is shown in Supplementary Fig. 1A, accompanied by a Pearson’s coefficient plot illustrating the degree of fluorescence colocalization in Supplementary Fig. 1B.

Extracellular treatment on the astrocyte culture

WT and A30P/A53T double mutant α-synuclein monomeric and aggregated forms were added extracellularly to the cells (either primary or hiPSC derived), in the culture media at a sub-lethal dosage of 0.5 µM for 24 h, unless otherwise stated. Thus, we have five groups: control, WT monomer, WT aggregate, A30P/A53T double mutant monomer and A30P/A53T double mutant aggregate treated astrocytes. Additionally, as per requirement, the primary astrocytes were also treated with MMP9 inhibitor (MMP9-I; 25 μM for six hours) or proteasome inhibitor, MG132 (10 μM for six hours) or autophagy inhibitor, 3-methyladenine (3MA, 5 mM for six hours). The inhibitors were treated simultaneously with α-synuclein and the experimental values were compared to astrocytes treated with α-synuclein alone. The viability of these inhibitors at their respective concentrations were verified using phase contrast imaging and a survival assay with 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)−2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) and observed to be comparable to control cells (Supplementary Fig. 2A&B).

Conditioned media (CM)

For all enzyme linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA), media from the α-synuclein treated primary or hiPSC derived astrocytes was replaced with fresh media (after a step of three PBS washes) for another 24 h. Confluent cultures of astrocytes as represented in Supplementary Fig. 3A&B were exposed to extracellular α-synuclein wherein no visible space is available for α-synuclein attachment with the dish surface. The CM was then collected and sequentially centrifuged at 1000 g for 10 min and further at 10,000 g for 20 min. The recovered supernatant, treated with protease inhibitor cocktail, was stored at −80 °C until the ELISA analysis. The CM from primary astrocytes treated with α-synuclein in the differentiation media of DA neurons was used for DA neuronal treatment. The CM from hiPSC derived astrocytes treated with α-synuclein in the differentiation media of astrocytes was used for SH-SY5Y treatment.

Exocytosis inhibition

Low temperature incubation was used to inhibit exocytosis. Control and α-synuclein treated astrocytes were incubated at 16 °C for 2 h followed by 16 h incubation at 37 °C. An exocytosis uninhibited group was also maintained where the control and α-synuclein treated astrocytes were incubated at 37 °C for 18 h.

Enzyme linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Sandwich ELISA was performed on the CM of astrocytes or DA neurons for the quantification of α-synuclein [α-Synuclein (SNCa) ELISA Kit – Sunlong Biotech (NSL22227Hu); which was validated to detect both monomers and aggregates of α-synuclein equally (Supplementary Fig. 3C)]. The ELISA kit [Sunlong Biotech (NSL22227Hu)] was tested using equal concentrations (900 pg/ml) of monomeric and aggregated α-synuclein and it returned comparable values for both forms. TNFα [Rat TNF-alpha ELISA – RayBiotech (ELR-TNFa)], IL-6 [Rat IL-6 ELISA – RayBiotech (ELR-IL-6)], IL-1β [Rat IL-1 beta ELISA – RayBiotech (ELR-IL-1b)], GDNF [Rat GDNF ELISA Kit PicoKine®—Booster Bio (FEK0363)], BDNF [Rat BDNF ELISA – RayBiotech (ELR-BDNF)] and dopamine [Immunotag™ DA (Dopamine) ELISA – G-Biosciences (IT7958)]. All the protocols were followed as per manufacturer’s instructions. For dopamine ELISA, basal release and release after 56 mM potassium chloride (KCl) stimulation were analyzed. The optical density was determined at 450 nm using Infinite® 200 Microplate reader (Tecan).

Immunocytochemistry (ICC)

Primary astrocytic CM treated DA neurons, hiPSC astrocytic CM treated SH-SY5Y cells, primary astrocytes or hiPSC derived astrocytes adhered on 12 mm coverslips were taken for ICC with the procedure as described in our earlier works [87, 88]. Primary antibodies used were anti-α-synuclein, anti-β-III-tubulin, anti-tyrosine hydroxylase (TH), anti-vesicular monoamine transporter (VMAT2), anti-Nurr1, anti-engrailed-1, anti-early endosome antigen-1 (EEA1) and anti-lysosomal associated protein-2 (LAMP2). Secondary antibodies conjugated with Alexa Fluor® 488 or Alexa Fluor® 647 was added against the respective host of the primary antibodies. 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) was used to counterstain the nuclei. For confocal microscopy, the cells were perceived using Axio Observer.Z1/7, Zeiss LSM 980 confocal microscope and Plan Apochromat 40X/1.3Oil DIC (UV) VIS-IR M27 objective. Whereas for the normal fluorescence microscopy, the cells were visualized under Semi-Apochromats-40x/0.80 PL FLUOTAR objective connected to the camera (Leica DFC3000G) in Leica DMi8 fluorescent microscope. Three separate samples and at least 10 fields were observed in each condition. ZEISS ZEN 3.7 software (ZEISS ZEN Microscopy Software, RRID:SCR_013672) and Leica Application Suite X (LasX) software (Leica Application Suite X, RRID:SCR_013673) was used to acquire and process the images from confocal and fluorescence microscopy respectively. For the acquisition using the confocal microscopy, the software was also used to obtain the orthogonal view of the images along with the Pearson’s coefficient value (minimum of n = 5) for the colocalization of the required fluorescent markers. As required, the corrected total cell fluorescence (CTCF) was calculated as shown in our earlier publications [87, 104], from 30 regions of interest (ROIs) using the formula:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$CTCF=Integrated\,density-\left(Area\,of\,selected\,cell\,X\,Mean\,fluorescence\,of\,background\,readings\right).$$\end{document}CTCF=Integrateddensity-AreaofselectedcellXMeanfluorescenceofbackgroundreadings.

Neurite outgrowth analysis

The neurite outgrowths of astrocytic CM treated DA neurons were analyzed using the neurite outgrowth staining kit, following manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, the cultures were incubated with 1:1 ratio of 1X cell viability indicator and cell membrane stain in HBSS for 20 min at 37 °C. Further, the cultures were rinsed with PBS and 1X background suppression dye was added. Fluorescence visualization was performed using the EVOS M5000 microscopic system viewed with the EVOS™ 20X Objective (PLAN FLUOR, 0.45NA/6.23WD) and the CMOS monochrome camera. The microscope is supported by the EVOS M5000 software (EVOS M5000 Imaging System, RRID:SCR_023650). Data analysis was performed using ImageJ software (ImageJ, RRID:SCR_003070). Collapsed neurites were defined as the neurites with blebs on them.

Intracellular calcium ([Ca2+]i) analysis in DA neurons and lysosomal calcium dynamics in astrocytes

Adhered cells (astrocytic CM treated DA neurons or astrocytes) on 22 mm coverslips with 90–95% confluency were used for the live cell fluorescence assay to measure [Ca2+]i using Fluo-4 acetoxymethyl (AM; cell permeant) according to earlier studies from our lab [33, 104]. Stained cells were observed under Semi-Apochromats-40x/0.80 PL FLUOTAR objective connected to the camera (Leica DFC3000G) in Leica DMi8 fluorescent microscope supported with LAS X image acquisition software (Leica Application Suite X, RRID:SCR_013673). DA neurons in complete Hank’s balanced salt solution (HBSS) was stimulated with 56 mM KCl at the 20th frame during acquisition. The calcium hike in the cytosol due the stimulation is detected by the Fluo-4. Astrocytes in HBSS buffer devoid of calcium along with ethylene glycol-bis(β-aminoethyl ether)-N,N,N′,N′-tetraacetic acid (EGTA) were supplemented extracellularly with 1 μM bafilomycin (viability verified in Supplementary Fig. 2A&C). This releases all lysosomal calcium into the cytosol, which is detected by the Fluo-4 and fluoresces. Data analysis in both cases were performed using ImageJ software (ImageJ, RRID:SCR_003070) and all data were expressed as F1/F0 and the area under the curve was plotted for each group.

Apoptosis analysis

1X106 DA neurons or SH-SY5Y after astrocytic CM treatment (primary and hiPSC derived respectively) were stained with 5μL Alexa Fluor® 488 Annexin V and 1μL 100 μg/mL propidium iodide (PI) in the annexin-binding buffer and incubated at room temperature for 15 min. The cells were washed and pelleted. The cells were analyzed by flow cytometry.

Quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR)

As described in our earlier report [88], qPCR was performed and analyzed from cDNA obtained from the control and aggregate treated cells. The primers used were 18S (Forward: 5’-CGGCTACCACATCCAAGGAA-3’, Reverse: 5’-GCTGGAATTACCGCGGCT-3’) and MMP9 (Forward: 5’-TTCGACGCTGACAAGAAGTG-3’, Reverse: 5’-AGGGGAGTCCTCGTGGTAGT-3’). qPCR was performed in the QuantStudio™ 6 Flex Real-Time PCR System (applied biosystems® by life technologies™) and the data was represented as fold change in mRNA levels in the treated astrocytes normalized with 18S mRNA and MMP9 mRNA levels in respective control astrocytes.

Gelatin zymography

15 μg/mL of 10X concentrated conditioned media (of control or protein treated cells) along with non-reducing sample buffer (125 mM tris, 4% SDS, 20% glycerol, 0.01% bromophenol blue; pH adjusted to 6.8 using HCl) was loaded onto to a 7.5% acrylamide gel containing 1 mg/mL gelatin. The gel was run as per a normal sodium dodecyl sulfate – polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) to separate the proteins in the conditioned media [88]. Further, the gel was washed to remove any SDS with a wash buffer consisting of 2.5% triton-X, 50 mM Tris HCl (pH: 7.5), 5 mM calcium chloride, 1 μM zinc chloride and 0.016% sodium azide. For the activity of the gelatinases to occur, the gel was incubated at 37 °C for 24 h with an incubation buffer of the same composition as the wash buffer, except for 1% triton-X. The gel was then stained with Coomassie® brilliant blue G – 250 and then destained until clear bands were visible. The bands were visualized using the ChemiDoc™ MP Imaging System (BioRad). Densitometric analysis for the bands were represented with respect to control. This analysis was performed using the ImageJ software (ImageJ, RRID:SCR_003070).

20S proteasome activity assay

Proteasome activity was measured in 80,000 control and α-synuclein monomer treated astrocytes plated on a 96 well plate using the Proteasome 20S activity assay kit, as per the manufacturer’s instructions. The fluorescence intensity was monitored at an excitation wavelength of 490 nm and emission wavelength of 525 nm using Infinite® 200 Microplate reader (Tecan).

Flow cytometry

DA neurons after astrocytic CM treatment and astrocytes in suspension were analyzed for flow cytometry as described in our earlier work [88]. Primary antibodies used were anti-VMAT2, anti-β-III-tubulin, anti-TH, anti-Nurr1, anti-dopamine transporter (DAT), anti-glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP), anti-MMP9, anti-ubiquitin, anti-beclin-1, anti- microtubule associated protein 1 light chain 3 beta (MAP1LC3B), anti-EEA1, anti-LAMP2, anti-nitrated α-synuclein Tyr125, Tyr133 and anti-α-synuclein (phospho S129) antibodies. Secondary antibodies conjugated with Alexa Fluor® 488 or Alexa Fluor® 647 was added against the respective host of the primary antibodies. The cells were measured using FACS Verse (BD Biosciences) and analyzed using the software BD FACSuite. Six replicates (each obtained from a separate isolation, in the case of primary astrocytes or DA neurons) were analyzed for each experiment. Cells were identified by light scatter for 10,000 gated events. Cells stained with the secondary antibody alone was used for gating (indicated by red line) out non-specific staining and represented by the shaded peak in the flow cytometry histograms [88, 104]. For apoptosis analysis, fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC) and propidium iodide (PI) were the ranges used. A bi-variant plot for the two ranges were plotted for each group. Late apoptotic population of the quadrant II was analyzed statistically.

Sodium dodecyl sulfate – polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and western blotting

40 μg of protein extracted from control and α-synuclein aggregate treated cells were separated using SDS-PAGE (loading buffer: 0.5 M tris 9% SDS, 50% glycerol, 9% β-mercaptoethanol, 0.03% bromophenol blue; pH adjusted to 6.8 using HCl) and transferred to a polyvinyl difluoride (PVDF) membrane by semi-dry transfer method. The presence of LC3B and β-actin were detected on this membrane by following the method that is described in details in our earlier publications [87, 88]. The primary antibodies against LC3B and β-actin were used. The membranes were then incubated with a secondary antibody conjugated with HRP at a concentration of 1:2000.

Transfection of astrocytes

80% confluent astrocytes on 12 mm coverslips placed on a 35 mm culture dish were transfected with mCh Gal3 plasmids using the Lipofectamine™ 3000 transfection reagent in the OptiMEM™ reduced serum medium. pmCherry-Gal3 was a gift from Hemmo Meyer (Addgene plasmid # 85,662; RRID:Addgene_85662) [81]. After transfection, the cells were treated with α-synuclein aggregates and further processed for ICC.

Statistical analysis

All the results are expressed as mean ± SD. Statistical comparisons were made using one way analysis of variance (ANOVA) or two way ANOVA as per requirement, followed by Bonferroni posthoc analysis, using R software (R Foundation; R Project for Statistical Computing, RRID:SCR_001905) or Sigma Plot 12.5 (Systat Software, Inc.; SigmaPlot, RRID:SCR_003210). A P value less than 0.05 was considered significant. Graphs were prepared using GraphPad Prism 6 (GraphPad Software; GraphPad Prism, RRID:SCR_002798) or Sigma Plot 12.5. Statistical significance was denoted by * = control versus protein treatment; # = monomer versus aggregate; $ = WT versus A30P/A53T; € = exocytosis uninhibited/inhibitor untreated versus exocytosis inhibited/inhibitor treated [MMP9 or MG132 or 3MA]. @ = Pearson’s coefficient of association of EEA1 versus LAMP2 with α-synuclein. Single symbol: P < 0.05; triple-symbol: P < 0.001.

Results

α-synuclein release into the secretome of protein treated primary and iPSC derived astrocytes

Previous studies from our laboratory [87, 88] have reported a reduction in endocytosis in astrocytes treated with α-synuclein. However, the release of α-synuclein from these treated astrocytes has not been assessed. In this study, we quantified α-synuclein levels in the CM collected from both control and treated astrocytes (both primary and iPSC derived) using ELISA (Fig. 1A). Significantly higher levels of α-synuclein were detected in the CM of protein-treated cells compared to control astrocytes (primary and iPSC derived astrocytes, Fig. 1B&C respectively; P < 0.001, control versus protein treatment). Moreover, the release of α-synuclein from aggregate treated cells was higher than that from monomer treated astrocytes for both WT and A30P/A53T double mutant cases (Fig. 1B&C; P < 0.001 monomer versus aggregate treatment). The α-synuclein levels in the conditioned media from control astrocytes was below the detection threshold of the ELISA kit hence represented as non-detectable (ND). To further investigate exocytosis, primary astrocyte cultures were incubated at lower temperatures to inhibit exocytosis. Astrocytes subjected to lower temperatures showed reduced α-synuclein levels in the CM, with similar patterns observed for both monomer and aggregate treated astrocytes (Fig. 1D). While the levels of α-synuclein released into the CM from aggregate treated astrocytes were higher compared to monomer treated astrocytes, the exocytosis mediated release of α-synuclein was notably higher from the monomer treated astrocytes than from the aggregate treated astrocytes (Fig. 1E). Confocal imaging on SH-SY5Y cells revealed the association of DyLight™ 488 labeled α-synuclein released by the α-synuclein treated astrocytes in the CM. The association of DyLight™ 488 tagged α-synuclein with TH positive cells confirms that the release of α-synuclein aggregates was higher than that of the monomers (Fig. 1F-I). This was also confirmed from the plot of the ratio of labelled α-synuclein associated with TH positive cells (Fig. 1J) calculated from the ICC images Fig. 1F-I (P < 0.001 monomer versus aggregate CM treatment). Therefore, astrocytes treated with the protein can release α-synuclein, potentially contributing to the propagation of α-synuclein pathology. Increased pro-inflammatory cytokine and decreased neurotrophic factor release in the secretome of α-synuclein treated astrocytes

Autophagy affected in α-synuclein aggregate treated astrocytes

Autophagy serves as the mechanism for degrading long-lived proteins, organelles, and cell debris [10], especially for large aggregates that are typically inaccessible to the proteasomal core [85]. Thus, autophagy is crucial for engulfed aggregates' degradation, which we have targeted in this section, as described in the schematic Fig. 6A&B. To determine the α-synuclein form targeted by autophagy, rat primary astrocytes were simultaneously treated with 5 mM 3MA and 0.5 μM pre-labeled α-synuclein (either of the four types) for six hours. Significant increases in α-synuclein quantity were observed only in the aggregate + 3MA treated cells compared to untreated cells (Fig. 6C; P < 0.001, 3MA untreated versus 3MA treated), suggesting the involvement of autophagy in degrading α-synuclein aggregates of WT or A30P/A53T double mutant origin. To assess the influence of aggregate treatment on autophagy, the expression of two autophagy markers, Beclin and LC3B, was examined in control and α-synuclein aggregate treated astrocytes using flow cytometry (Figs. 6D-F) and ICC (Fig. 6G). While Beclin expression remained consistent among the three groups (Fig. 6D, E&G), LC3B expression significantly decreased in the aggregate treated cells (Figs. 6D, F&G) compared to controls (P < 0.001, control versus aggregate treatment), particularly in the mutant aggregate treated cells (P < 0.001, WT versus A30P/A53T double mutant aggregate treatment). This suggests that while autophagy initiation remains unaffected, aggregate treatment impacts autophagosome formation in astrocytes. LC3B expression was further confirmed through western blot analysis (Fig. 6H), with densitometry (Fig. 6I) showing a reduced expression in aggregate treated astrocytes compared to controls (P < 0.001, control versus aggregate treatment), with a further reduction observed with double mutant aggregate treatment (P < 0.001, WT versus A30P/A53T double mutant aggregate treatment), indicating decreased autophagosome formation. Additionally, increased ratio of LC3B-II to LC3B-I (P < 0.001, control versus aggregate treatment) was observed, which heightened with the double mutant aggregate treatment (P < 0.001, WT versus A30P/A53T double mutant aggregate treatment; Fig. 6J). Intact endosomes and compromised lysosomes of autophagy

Autophagy converges with the endo-lysosomal pathway to form the autophagy lysosomal pathway (ALP). Thus, we examined the physiology of endosomes and lysosomes in astrocytes treated with 0.5 μM α-synuclein aggregates (after 24 h of treatment) (Fig. 7A). The EEA1 immunopositive population significantly increased in aggregate treated cells compared to controls (Fig. 7B; P < 0.001, control versus aggregate treatment), indicating an upsurge in endosomes. Conversely, the LAMP2 immunopositive population significantly decreased in aggregate treated cells compared to controls (Fig. 7C; P < 0.001, control versus aggregate treatment), suggesting a decline in lysosomes. To visualize the localization of EEA1 and LAMP2 alongside pre-labeled α-synuclein, confocal images were captured (iPSC-derived astrocytes in Fig. 7D&E respectively, and primary astrocytes in Supplementary Fig. 7A&B respectively). While EEA1 fluorescence increased in α-synuclein treated cells, prominent association of EEA1 with α-synuclein (yellow punctate region) was not observed, indicating healthy endosomes (Fig. 7D and Supplementary Fig. 7A). Enhanced association of LAMP2 with α-synuclein (yellow punctate region; Fig. 7E and Supplementary Fig. 7B). The Pearson’s coefficient of the colocalization of α-synuclein with EEA1 or LAMP2 quantified this association (iPSC-derived astrocytes in Fig. 7F&G respectively, and primary astrocytes in Supplementary Fig. 7C&D respectively). Figure 7F and Supplementary Fig. 7C shows the minimal association of EEA1 with α-synuclein, whereas, Fig. 7G and Supplementary Fig. 7D shows the very high association of LAMP2 with α-synuclein (For EEA1: P < 0.001, control versus monomer, P < 0.01, control versus aggregate treatment; for LAMP2: P < 0.001, control versus treatment, P < 0.00 monomer versus aggregate, P < 0.01 WT monomer versus double mutant monomer treatment). Upon comparing the Pearson’s coefficients for EEA1 and LAMP2, it was observed that LAMP2 exhibited a higher Pearson’s coefficient (Fig. 7H and Supplementary Fig. 7E; P < 0.001, EEA1 versus LAMP2 in all four treated astrocytes). The orthogonal view of EEA1 or LAMP2 (Supplementary Fig. 7F&G respectively) alongside α-synuclein aggregates at a higher magnification (63X) gives a better picture of the minimal association of EEA1 with the aggregates compared to the increased association of LAMP2 with aggregates. These results suggest compromised lysosomal health, with observed lysosomes predominantly associated with α-synuclein. In addition, Supplementary Fig. 7H&I indicates the positive control for the calculation of Pearson’s coefficient by analyzing Leucine-rich repeat kinase 2 (LRRK2) colocalization with a lipid raft marker, Caveolin-1. Mutations in LRRK2 RocCOR domain cause increase in GTP-binding and decrease GTPase activity leaving LRRK2 in a GTP bound kinase active form [77]. Additionally, Rab29 and Rab32 binds to cytosolic LRRK2 and recruit it to biological membranes [115]. Further, this LRRK2 is known to be associated with caveolin on the membrane [1, 41]. As observed in the graph (Supplementary Fig. 7I), the Pearson coefficient obtained in WT LRRK2 cells itself is 0.59 ± 0.02. Lysosomal rupture

To study if there were any sort of vesicular rupture or degradation, we analyzed the rearrangement of galactin-3 in these cells (Fig. 8A). For this, we first transfected astrocytes with mCh Gal3 plasmid and then treated them with 0.5 µM α-synuclein aggregates for 24 h. Association of α-synuclein with any ruptured vesicles were identified using ICC (Fig. 8B). We observed that in the aggregate treated cells, the pre-labelled α-synuclein were associated with the mCh Gal3 as evidenced by the yellowish puncta. Impaired lysosomal calcium dynamics

Given the observed lysosomal damage and rupture, it's plausible that lysosomal calcium dynamics are affected, potentially compromising lysosomal integrity. To investigate this, we quantified calcium release from lysosomes into the cytosol using live cell fluorescence imaging with the calcium fluorescent probe Fluo-4 AM in both control and aggregate treated cells (Fig. 9A). Cells were supplemented with 1 μM bafilomycin to induce calcium release from lysosomes [8, 61, 62]. While control cells showed complete release of lysosomal calcium into the cytosol, aggregate treated cells exhibited significantly reduced cytosolic calcium levels (Fig. 9B&C; P < 0.001, control versus aggregate treatment), indicating compromised lysosomal integrity and consistent reduction in lysosomal calcium levels. Accumulation of modified α-synuclein

Previous studies have demonstrated the accumulation of α-synuclein in astrocytes, either through engulfment or mere membrane association, in the presence of extracellular α-synuclein [87, 88]. However, evidence suggests the presence of modified species of α-synuclein in astrocytes in Lewy body disorders [2]. We investigated the accumulation of nitrated and phosphorylated α-synuclein in astrocytes treated with α-synuclein monomers and aggregates (Fig. 10A). Our results, through ICC and flow cytometry, showed significantly increased accumulation of nitrated α-synuclein, particularly in the monomer treated cells. Both WT monomer and double mutant monomer treated cells exhibited similar levels of nitrated α-synuclein, while double mutant aggregate treated cells showed increased accumulation compared to WT aggregate treated cells (Fig. 10B&C; P < 0.001, control versus protein treatment; P < 0.001 monomer versus aggregate treatment; P < 0.001, WT aggregate versus double mutant aggregate treatment). Additionally, we observed significantly increased accumulation of phosphorylated α-synuclein, especially in the monomer treated cells (Fig. 10D&E; P < 0.001, control versus protein treatment; P < 0.001 monomer versus aggregate treatment]. Comparison between WT and double mutant forms revealed greater accumulation of phosphorylated α-synuclein in the double mutant treated cells [P < 0.001 WT and double mutant treatment]. Discussion

There is strong evidence to indicate that extracellular α-synuclein aggregates exert direct neurotoxic effects on DA neurons. While in vivo studies and post-mortem reports suggest a relatively lower impact on astrocyte survival, astrocytes have been shown to exhibit dystrophic features with heightened immunoreactivity [11, 53, 54, 56], and Kovacs et al., [50] have demonstrated that astrocytic α-synuclein localizes in endo-lysosomal compartments in diseased brains.

Our earlier in vitro investigations had revealed cell-intrinsic impairments in astrocytes following exposure to extracellular α-synuclein, compromising their critical supportive functions [88]. We had also suggested a potential deceleration in protein degradation and accumulation by studying the time-dependent association of α-synuclein in astrocytes, which reached a saturation point after 24 h [88]. The differences in membrane association and endocytosis of monomeric and aggregated forms of α-synuclein point to a potential discrepancy in exocytosis or membrane cleavage of the protein. In this study, we used rat primary astrocyte cultures and astrocytes derived from healthy control hiPSCs to understand how astrocytic protein degradation mechanisms handle monomeric and aggregated forms of α-synuclein, as well as their subsequent release into the secretome. We further assessed the downstream impact of the secretome from α-synuclein treated astrocytes on the survival and function of primary dopaminergic neuronal cells.

Building upon our previous research [87, 88], we have used the WT and double-mutant protein forms of α-synuclein in this study. Previous studies have demonstrated that α-synuclein with a single mutation fails to replicate PD pathology in rodents, highlighting the necessity of utilizing double-mutant α-synuclein for rodent-origin cells [47, 58]. In the brain microenvironment, the size of α-synuclein aggregates is often undefined, ranging from 100 nm to 1 μm [23, 60, 68]. In line with our previous studies [87, 88], we opted to use aggregates without artificial interventions such as sonication, to mimic conditions found in Lewy Body Disease brains more accurately. Supplementary Fig. 8A&B clearly demonstrate that sonication reduces the size of aggregates to that of monomers [87, 88], validating our choice of unsonicated aggregates.

We also chose to assess the release of α-synuclein by astrocytes treated with the protein after 24 h of treatment, as this was the “saturation point” arrived at in our earlier study, after which there was no further association of extracellular α-synuclein [88], indicating a potential slowdown in protein degradation. α-synuclein, despite not being native to astrocytes, was detected in the CM of protein treated astrocyte groups, indicating their expulsion of the protein. The presence of DyLight™ 488 tagged α-synuclein in the CM secreted by astrocytes and its association with SH-SY5Y cells confirms this finding. In the case of monomer treated groups where higher engulfment was observed, exocytosis contributed markedly to the expulsion of the protein. In case of aggregate treated cells, the notable increase in DyLight™ 488 tagged α-synuclein expressing astrocytes in the presence of MMP inhibitor suggests that α-synuclein release into the extracellular milieu is predominantly mediated by membrane cleavage via metalloproteases. Both monomer and aggregate treated astrocytes showed significant release of α-synuclein in their secretome. While this expulsion may represent a protective or survival mechanism for astrocytes (as evidenced by no significant decrease in astrocyte number), its release could be neurodegenerative for surrounding healthy DA neurons.

Post-mortem studies of PD patients have revealed the expression of MMP9 in astrocytes and reactive microglia within the SNpc [5] and the calcium-dependent zinc endopeptidase MMP9 is known to modulate PD pathology caused by α-synuclein by cleaving the peptide [100]. Additionally, alterations in MMP9 levels have been detected in the serum of PD patients when compared to age-matched healthy controls [7, 12, 57]. Beyond its presence in PD patients, preclinical studies using MPTP-induced PD and A53T α-synuclein PD models have demonstrated that MMP9 plays a critical role in regulating the basement membrane-astrocyte endfeet, and also contributes to glymphatic dysfunction in PD by inhibiting aquaporin-4 [63, 99], leading ultimately to blood brain barrier (BBB) leakage and a pro-inflammatory environment. Owing to the known involvement of MMP9 in PD pathology, and given that our study observed an increase in the pro-inflammatory profile of α-synuclein treated astrocytes, we evaluated the expression and activity of MMP9 in the astrocytes.

Despite observing no difference in MMP9 expression at the 24 h mark following aggregate exposure, we noted a significant reduction in MMP9 activity consistent with the previously reported decrease in membrane fluidity [87]. MMP9 typically interacts with the cell membrane to positively regulate its activity [32, 117], a process that may have been compromised by the increased membrane rigidity caused by aggregated α-synuclein association. Thus, the aggregated form of α-synuclein led to impairment of MMP9 activity, possibly by increasing membrane rigidity.

After endocytosis, monomeric α-synuclein undergoes degradation by the UPS or autophagy combined with lysosomal degradation in the ALP [106]. Physiologically, neurons degrade excess or small oligomeric forms of ubiquitinated WT or mutated monomeric α-synuclein primarily through the UPS [24, 97, 109]. Autophagy, on the other hand, is responsible for degrading large, long-lived proteins that tend to aggregate [10, 82]. For astrocytes, we noticed that inhibition of proteasomal activity led to increased association of α-synuclein monomers, while there was minimal change for the aggregate-treated astrocytes, suggesting that the proteasome contributes significantly to the degradation of monomeric α-synuclein. In neurons, too, studies have reported that ubiquitinated WT or mutated monomeric α-synuclein present in excess amounts or small oligomers are degraded by the UPS [24, 97, 109]. However, with longer exposure to α-synuclein monomers and aggregates, the proteasomal activity is progressively impaired. Higher accumulation of monomeric α-synuclein in cells can directly impair proteasomal activity, as is also reported in other cell types [18, 114]. From our earlier study, we know that aggregates associate preferentially with the membrane, which leads to ER stress through increase in SOCE, which in turn has been shown to compromise the UPS proteasome system [67]. In line with that study, here too an impairment of proteasomal activity is noticed upon exposure to aggregates. Furthermore, nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2) regulates proteasomal activity; and Lastres-Becker et al., [51] observed α-synuclein accumulation due to reduced UPS activity in Nrf2 knockout mice stereotaxically injected with α-synuclein. Our previous findings had also indicated reduced Nrf2 expression in α-synuclein treated cells [88], potentially contributing to the diminished UPS activity observed here. Consequently, ubiquitinated α-synuclein accumulates within the cell, and this accumulation of modified α-synuclein may serve as seeds for aggregation and fibrillation [102]. Thus, astrocytes preferentially degrade monomeric α-synuclein via the UPS.

In the context of impaired UPS function, autophagy becomes crucial for clearing intracytosolic proteins prone to aggregation [93]. Notably, we observed a significant increase in the association of α-synuclein with astrocytes treated with α-synuclein aggregates in the presence of an autophagy inhibitor, suggesting that autophagy plays a role in degrading this endocytosed protein form. As observed in our earlier work, a substantial proportion (approximately 80–90%) of both WT and A30P/A53T double mutant aggregates remained associated with the membrane without undergoing engulfment, within the first hour of protein treatment. The endocytosis data also indicated a minor degree of endocytosis, likely corresponding to smaller-sized aggregates, as detailed in the dynamic light scattering (DLS) analysis of the aggregate size distribution [88]. Further cleavage of the protein aggregates by MMP9 may lead to the formation of smaller aggregates, thereby facilitating their engulfment by the cells. Previous studies in neurons have also shown that mutated and aggregated α-synuclein undergo degradation via autophagy [14, 52, 108, 114]. Consistent with these findings, our results suggest that engulfed α-synuclein aggregates are degraded via autophagy, even in astrocytes.

In α-synuclein aggregate treated astrocytes, the expression of Beclin, which is necessary for autophagosome assembly [82], remains unchanged compared to control. However, there is a significant reduction in LC3B protein expression, which regulates autophagosome stability [82, 107]. This decrease in LC3B expression suggests impaired autophagy in the aggregate treated astrocytes. Similar reductions in LC3B expression and autophagy have been observed in astrocytes derived from PD patient iPSCs [19] and in those overexpressing α-synuclein [25]. Further, increased LC3B-II to LC3B-I ratio was also observed in the aggregate treated cells compared to the control. This directly indicates the inhibitory effect of autophagosome formation of lysosomes [98] which we have observed and discussed later on in this study. Additionally, this increased ratio can also be attributed to the increased association of LC3B-II with membrane compartments and LC3B-I within the cytoplasmic fraction [46].

Membrane contact sites formed by intrinsically disordered proteins (IDPs) directly influence the protein degradation machinery by affecting intracellular vesicle formation [118]. We previously reported perturbations in astrocytic membranes due to α-synuclein aggregate association [87], and disruption of membrane contact sites are known to impair critical functions such as autophagosome and lysosome formation [70]. Additionally, calcium plays a regulatory role in autophagy, with various calcium-mobilizing agents activating autophagy physiologically [44, 65, 91]. Our previous work had demonstrated calcium dysregulation and ER stress in α-synuclein aggregate treated astrocytes [87], and ER stress-induced calcium sequestration via Orai and stromal interaction molecule (STIM) activation may inhibit autophagy. Thus, ER stress mediated by membrane perturbation due to α-synuclein aggregate association might also have contributed to the inhibition of autophagy observed here.

Autophagy and the endo-lysosomal clearance pathway form the ALP, which plays a crucial role in degrading accumulated α-synuclein aggregates in neurons [22]. At the same time, there is evidence of impaired astrocytic pickup of extracellular α-synuclein and its accumulation in the lysosomes [19, 56, 73, 92]. In our work, we observed that the endosome levels had risen in the α-synuclein treated cells, with minimal association with α-synuclein and no degeneration. This may indicate that the α-synuclein included vesicles had progressed to the lysosomal stage already.

However, in astrocytes treated with α-synuclein aggregates, an increased colocalization of α-synuclein with LAMP2 was observed. This finding aligns with previous studies in both neuronal cells and astrocytes, which reported colocalization of α-synuclein with lysosomal proteins such as LAMP1 and LAMP2, indicating disruptions in the ALP [19, 21, 31, 43, 50, 56]. Furthermore, electron microscopy studies have shown that lysosomes containing α-synuclein lose their spherical shape and smooth membrane structure [48, 66], suggesting that the increased association of α-synuclein with LAMP2 could be indicative of compromised lysosomal integrity.

Moreover, the heightened mCh Galectin-3 association with LAMP2 confirmed the lysosomal damage in the α-synuclein aggregate treated astrocytes. Galectin-3 is a cytosolic lectin that plays a crucial role in the repair and removal of damaged lysosomes. Its function begins with recognizing lysosomal damage by binding to glycans exposed when lysosomal membranes rupture [45]. During this, the β-galactoside sugars on the interior leaflet of the membrane of intracellular vesicles are exposed, allowing the galectin molecules to bind to it [20]. Previous studies in astrocytes have pointed out the association of engulfed α-synuclein with the lysosomal protein [19, 56] in the case of monomer treatment, but this is the first study reporting lysosomal degeneration or rupture, and its correlation with the membrane association in aggregates. In sum, therefore, the α-synuclein aggregates induced autophagy inhibition, potentially manifested as autophagosome instability and lysosomal rupture, and likely attributable to membrane perturbation and ER stress.

It is established that ER stores are the primary suppliers of lysosomal calcium [35], crucial for maintaining lysosomal integrity [78]. A significant reduction in lysosomal calcium replenishment in cells treated with aggregates was observed here, possibly due to the ER store dysregulation that we had reported previously [87]. This study thus highlights the importance of addressing the membrane association of α-synuclein aggregates, which may potentially influence intracellular events such as lysosomal calcium levels, mediated by ER alterations resulting from changes in membrane fluidity. The extracellular expulsion of α-synuclein by astrocytes in their secretome is thus likely attributable to this compromised protein degradation machinery.

Post-mortem examinations of PD brains have revealed the accumulation of both nitrated and phosphorylated forms of α-synuclein in astrocytes [2]; however, it remains unclear if astrocytes can modify α-synuclein aggregates upon association. Monomeric α-synuclein induced the highest levels of modified α-synuclein species, probably due to their increased availability and the presence of modifying precursors such as nitric oxide (NO), reactive oxygen species (ROS), and kinases. In spite of the chief association of aggregates being merely with the membrane, both nitrated and phosphorylated α-synuclein accumulated in the treated cells. NO is capable of diffusing through cell membranes [29], thus nitrating aggregates associated with astrocytic membranes. The increased phosphorylation of α-synuclein in the treated astrocytes may also be correlated with kinase activity on membrane proteins. Studies on erythrocytes in sickle cell anemia have demonstrated increased kinase activity of membrane proteins due to membrane disruption [26], and the observed membrane perturbation with aggregate treatment [87] may have similarly induced kinase activity on membrane-bound α-synuclein.

We then set to assessing the potential impact of this astrocytic secretome on other healthy DA neurons in the same microenvironment. In line with previous research, we observed a distinctive pro-inflammatory profile in α-synuclein treated astrocytes, characterized by increased release of pro-inflammatory cytokines TNFα, IL-6, and IL-1β, along with decreased release of neurotrophic factors GDNF and BDNF. The α-synuclein CM treated DA neurons showed higher neurite collapse and reduced [Ca2+]i response upon KCl stimulation, with a corresponding decrease in vesicular dopamine release. These functional deficits, coupled with heightened pro-inflammatory cytokines, may contribute to DA neuron degeneration [72, 75, 94] as evidenced by the increased population of apoptotic DA neurons treated with CM from α-synuclein treated astrocytes, particularly with double mutant and/or aggregate treatment. Despite levels of cytokines and trophic factors being similar between mutant and WT α-synuclein treated astrocytes, the greater detrimental effect of the CM from mutant α-synuclein treated astrocytes can be attributed to two key factors: i) The presence of mutant α-synuclein in the CM. In our previous work, we demonstrated that the mutant form induces greater stress in astrocytes, characterized by elevated levels of ROS and NO, along with increased SOCE-induced Ca2⁺ entry, which disrupts [Ca2⁺]i homeostasis and further contributes to cellular stress [87]. Neuronal cells exposed to the CM of mutant α-synuclein treated astrocytes also seem to respond in a similar manner, indicating a shared vulnerability to the mutant α-synuclein present in the CM. Building on this observation, our next study will investigate the effects of extracellular α-synuclein in its four distinct forms. ii) The release of higher levels of ROS and NO, coupled with a reduced supply of glutathione in the secretome of mutant and/or aggregate treated astrocytes, as reported in our earlier study [88]. Reactive oxygen and nitrogen species, being diffusible, can significantly impact neighboring cells and exacerbate cellular damage [69].

Exposure of healthy dopaminergic (DA) neurons to the secretome of α-synuclein-exposed astrocytes compromised their survival and function, indicating that an impaired microenvironment can influence the viability and functionality of healthy neurons. Thus, as the disease progresses and severity increases, astrocytes, while still surviving, have reduced ability to handle extracellular α-synuclein due to impairment of their protein degradation machinery.

Conclusion

The compromised protein quality control system is a key factor in the propagation of α-synuclein pathology, as highlighted by Lee et al., [53]. At the early stages, astrocytes are able to deal with monomeric α-synuclein via UPS efficiently, as well as handle larger aggregates through MMP9 and autophagy, clearing extracellular α-synuclein and maintaining neuronal health. However, extended exposure to extracellular monomeric and aggregated α-synuclein compromises their proteasomal activity, inhibits MMP9, and destabilizes autophagy, transforming astrocytes from protectors to promoters of neurodegeneration. This study is the first to elucidate the astrocytes' preferred degradation pathways for both monomeric and aggregated forms of α-synuclein, along with the subsequent effects of these payloads on the cellular degradation machinery, as summarized schematically in the accompanying Fig. 11. Furthermore, the presence of phosphorylated and nitrated α-synuclein species in astrocytes suggests their potential involvement in modifying both forms of the proteins. The astrocytic transformation is characterized by α-synuclein expulsion, increased release of inflammatory cytokines, and diminished secretion of growth factors. This shift highlights the pivotal role of astrocytes as emerging hubs for propagating α-synuclein pathology via their secretome, disrupting crucial glia-neuron communication and exacerbating neurodegeneration. While the importance of cell–cell contact has been emphasized in prion diseases [111], our study highlights the role of the secretome of affected astrocytes in the presence of extracellular α-synuclein, which has been largely unexplored so far. The adverse impact of the modified secretome on the survival and function of healthy dopaminergic neurons underscores the criticality of integrating astrocytes into treatment paradigms in synucleinopathies. This can enhance current therapeutic strategies, including exosomal therapy, stem cell transplantation, and growth factor supplementation, to foster a conducive microenvironment and thus improve dopaminergic neuronal survival and viability.
