Mapping epoxyquinoid biosynthesis: Enzyme functions across bacteria and fungi
Authors: Hsin-Yu Wei, Wei-Hsuan Lin, Hsiao-Ching Lin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractEpoxyquinoids are a unique class of natural products featuring an epoxide embedded within a quinone/quinol scaffold, typically as an epoxycyclohexenone (ECH). Their striking stereochemical diversity arises from multiple permutations of epoxide and hydroxyl configurations across the epoxyquinol and epoxyhydroquinone families. These highly oxygenated cores contain contiguous stereocenters and reactive functionalities, and their structural diversity correlates with broad bioactivity spanning antibacterial, antifungal, antiparasitic, anti-inflammatory, and antiproliferative effects. A shared epoxide–quinone (or keto-epoxide) pharmacophore acts as an electrophilic warhead that covalently engages protein nucleophiles via Michael addition and epoxide opening, underpinning diverse modes of action. Recent biosynthetic advances have uncovered tailoring enzymes with unusual catalytic strategies that forge the epoxide, install additional functionality, and tune oxidation states through redox chemistry. This review highlights current knowledge of experimentally characterized pathways and enzyme functions across Actinobacteria, Ascomycota, and Basidiomycota, illuminating common logic and organism-specific innovations in epoxyquinoid assembly.

Introduction

Epoxyquinoids are a family of secondary metabolites that feature an epoxide embedded in a quinone/quinol framework, frequently featuring as an epoxycyclohexenone (ECH) motif. Early members, including the Streptomyces metabolite LL-C10037α (1) (Whittle & Gould, 1987) and fungal products such as epoxydon (Sakamura et al., 1969) and phyllostine (9) (Sekiguchi & Gaucher, 1978), highlighted both the structural variety and potent bioactivities associated with this scaffold. Since then, numerous congeners have been reported from bacteria and fungi, spanning epoxyquinol and epoxyhydroquinone subfamilies and decorated by additional functionality (e.g., enyne units, prenylation, and redox tailoring). Overall, epoxyquinoids display broad antibacterial, antifungal, antiparasitic, anti-inflammatory, and antiproliferative activities, often at low micromolar to nanomolar levels.

A unifying chemical theme is the epoxide–quinone pharmacophore, which serves as an electrophilic warhead capable of covalently modifying nucleophilic residues in proteins through Michael addition and epoxide opening (Han et al., 2010; Kamiyama et al., 2008; Yamamoto et al., 2008). Such irreversible adduct formation underpins diverse mechanisms of action and helps explain reported engagement of signaling and proteostasis pathways. Representative examples include manumycin-type ECHs that perturb NF-κB signaling via IKKβ (Bernier et al., 2006) and the basidiomycete metabolite panepophenanthrin, an inhibitor of the ubiquitin-activating enzyme E1 (Sekizawa et al., 2002); synthetic probes such as epoxyquinone A monomer (Liang et al., 2006) further illustrate target-guided design concepts within this chemotype.

The biosynthetic logic of epoxyquinoids is rapidly emerging. A polyketide- or shikimate-derived quinoid core is assembled first, followed by enzymatic tailoring steps that install the defining epoxide, incorporate additional functionalities (e.g., 1,3-enyne units), and refine oxidation levels via redox tailoring. Recent works combining genome mining, heterologous reconstruction, and in-vitro enzymology have elucidated the stepwise construction of the epoxide-bearing cyclohexenone scaffolds (Fig. 1). Notably, enzymes that forge epoxide arise from distinct families in different lineages. In Actinobacteria, for example, a flavin adenine dinucleotide (FAD)-dependent monooxygenase, AsuE3 in asukamycin (6)/manumycin-type biosynthesis has been characterized. In S. coelicolor A3(2), the two-component flavin-dependent monooxygenase ActVA-ORF5/ActVB exhibits tailoring epoxyquinone-forming activity, in addition to their role in quinone–hydroquinone interconversion during actinorhodin (3) biosynthesis (Fig. 3). In Ascomycota, cupin domain-containing proteins AtD, PatJ, and ParE catalyze epoxidation steps in the biosynthetic pathways of terreic acid (7), patulin (8) (Fig. 5C), and paraphaeones (10) (Fig. 5D), respectively. AtD shares 66% and 38% sequence identity with PatJ and ParE, respectively. On the other hand, AtyE, the epoxidase in the (–)-asperpentyn (15) pathway, belongs to the cupin superfamily and shows moderate sequence identity to IacF, BisC, and VirH (64%, 65%, and 50%, respectively), which are responsible for epoxide formation in the epoxyquinone pathways leading to iso-A82775C (13), biscognienyne B (14), and trichoxide (17). In basidiomycota, panepoxydone (18) biosynthesis features a cytochrome P450 PanH that epoxidizes a prenylhydroquinone intermediate.

Chemical scaffolds of epoxyquinone, epoxyquinol, and epoxyhydroquinone, together with epoxyquinoid natural products whose biosynthetic pathways have been experimentally characterized in Actinobacteria, Ascomycota, and Basidiomycota.

This review highlights experimentally characterized biosynthetic pathways leading to epoxyquinoid natural products across Actinobacteria, Ascomycota, and Basidiomycota, focusing particularly on the functions of the enzymes involved.

LL-C10037α, Actinorhodins, and Asukamycins from Actinobacteria

LL-C10037α (1) is a shikimate-pathway-derived antibiotic from Streptomyces LL-C10037 (Fig. 2). Its biosynthesis depends critically on stereoselective aromatic epoxidation: dihydroxyacetanilide epoxidase I (DHAE I) converts 2,5-dihydroxyacetanilide (19) to the 5R,6S-epoxyquinone (20), which is subsequently reduced to 1 (Gould et al., 1989; Lee et al., 1984; Shen & Gould, 1991). A parallel route operates in Streptomyces MPP3051, where dihydroxyacetanilide epoxidase II (DHAE II) generates the enantiomeric 5S,6R-epoxyquinone (21); additional reduction and deacetylation steps then furnishes the desacetyl analog MM 14201 (2) (Box et al., 1983; Shen et al., 1990). Unlike flavin- or heme-dependent monooxygenases, DHAE I/II act as dioxygenases that require only molecular oxygen and substrate, effecting a rare four-electron oxidation on a hydroquinone scaffold (Gould et al., 1996). These properties place them in a distinct subfamily of hydroquinone dioxygenases. DHAE I and DHAE II also display strict facial selectivity. Stereochemical analyses indicate that DHAE I delivers oxygen to the (3-si,4-re) face of the aromatic ring, whereas DHAE II attacks the (3-re,4-si) face, yielding mirror-image epoxyquinones. Two mechanisms have been proposed to explain this divergence: (i) opposite-face oxygen delivery with a shared binding mode, or (ii) same-face delivery with flipped substrate orientations (Shen & Gould, 1991). Together, these findings account for the stereo-divergent formation of 1 vs. 2 and highlight DHAE enzymes as enantio-complementary biocatalysts for aromatic epoxidation.

Enzymatic functions of DHAE I and DHAE II in the biosynthesis of LL-C10037α (1) from Streptomyces LL-C10037 and MM 14201 (2) from Streptomyces MPP3051 (Shen & Gould, 1991).

Actinorhodin (ACT, 3) from S. coelicolor A3(2) is a benchmark type II polyketide with a benzoisochromanequinone (BIQ; pyranonaphthoquinone) chromophore (Brockmann et al., 1966) (Fig. 3). It is secreted as a blue pigment and has long served as a model for actinomycete genetics and type II PKS enzymology. The act cluster has been characterized for its enzymes function; the minimal gene set for 6-deoxydihydrokalafungin (DDHK, 22) biosynthesis include actI-ORF1/2/3 (minimal PKS, for octaketide chain), actIII/actVII (ketoreductase/aromatase, C-9 reduction & A-ring formation), actIV (cyclase–thioesterase, B-ring formation & product release), actVI-ORF1 (ketoreductase, C-3 reduction & C-ring formation), and actVI-ORF2 (enoyl reductase, pyran-side-chain reduction at C15) (Ishikawa et al., 2022; Itoh et al., 2007; McDaniel et al., 1993; Okamoto et al., 2009; Taguchi et al., 2017). Next, the quinone formation on the BIQ ring is govern by actVA-ORF5/actVB (two-component flavin-dependent monooxygenase), which catalyze stepwise C-6 and then C-8 hydroxylation on DDHK (22) to produce T3HN (23) and T4HN (24); in vitro biochemical studies show ActVB not only supplies reduced cofactor to ActVA-ORF5, but also regulates the interconversion of quinone–hydroquinone intermediates such as dihydrokalafungin (DHK, 25) to 23 and 8-hydroxy-DHK (DHK-OH, 27) to 8-hydroxy-tetrahydrokalafungin (THK-OH, 26) or 24 (Hashimoto et al., 2020; Okamoto et al., 2009; Taguchi et al., 2013; Valton et al., 2004, 2006, 2008). On the other hand, ActVA-ORF6 (ABM-family monooxygenase) serves as an alternative C-6 oxygenation to produce 25 from 22 in the ΔactVB mutant (Okamoto et al., 2009).

(A) The act gene cluster; (B) biosynthesis of actinorhodin in S. coelicolor A3(2) and enzymatic functions of ActVA-ORF5/ActVB in converting kalafungin (29) into epoxykalafungins, (5S,14R)- and (5R,14S)-epoxides (4 and 5) (Hashimoto et al., 2025; Taguchi et al., 2011).

Beyond hydroxylations, the ActVA-ORF5/ActVB system shows additional tailoring capacity (Okamoto et al., 2009; Taguchi et al., 2011). With kalafungin (29) as substrate in vitro, it produces two epoxykalafungins, assigned by nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR)/modeled coupling constants and X-ray, as (5S,14R)- and (5R,14S)-epoxides (4 and 5), revealing an epoxyquinone-forming activity of the same two-component flavin-containing monooxygenase (FMO). This expands the recognized catalytic repertoire of ActVA-ORF5/ActVB and suggests latent oxidative chemistries may operate (or be co-opted) in related BIQ pathways.

The terminal C–C bond dimerization that yields ACT is carried out by two enzymes. ActVA–ORF3, a cofactor–independent quinone-forming oxidase, first generates the monomeric coupling substrate DHK–OH (27). ActVA–ORF4, an NAD(P)H–dependent NmrA–family enzyme, then uses DHK–OH (27) as its true substrate to catalyze biaryl C–C coupling, forming the THK–OH dimer (28). A final oxidation by ActVA–ORF3 converts this dimer to 3. Together, ActVA–ORF3 and ActVA–ORF4 furnish 3 in vitro, completing the assignment of the late steps in the pathway (Hashimoto et al., 2023; Taguchi et al., 2012, 2015). Since ACT (3) is mainly detected as its lactone form, γ-ACT (30), in culture media, recent biochemical work assigns complementary roles to two ActVI enzymes: ActVI-ORF3 (cofactor-independent enzyme) catalyzes lactonization such as 3 to γ-ACT (30), and DHK (25) to kalafungin (29); ActVI-ORF4 (NAD(P)H-dependent oxidoreductase) performs delactonization of 29 back to 25. These rationalize the observed pools of lactone versus non-lactone intermediates and the prevalence of γ-ACT (30) in cultures (Hashimoto et al., 2025).

Manumycin-type natural products are epoxyquinone metabolites first reported in 1963; more than 30 members have since been described (Sattler et al., 1998). They share the mC7N core, a 2-amino-4-hydroxy-5,6-epoxycyclohex-2-enone, decorated with two acyl side chains: an N-acyl “upper” chain on the core amine and a C4-linked “lower” chain (Fig. 4B). The family displays broad bioactivity (antibacterial, anticoccidial, and antifungal) and has been explored as leads against cancer, inflammation, atherosclerosis, and Alzheimer’s disease, in part via farnesyltransferase and other targets (Bernier et al., 2006; Hara et al., 1993; Sugita et al., 2007; Tanaka et al., 1996; Zheng et al., 2007). Asukamycin (6), a prototypical manumycin, was isolated from S. nodosus subsp. asukaensis in 1976 (Omura et al., 1976). Its architecture comprises the mC7N core flanked by two trans-triene polyketide chains: the upper chain begins with a cyclohexane unit, and the lower chain terminates in a 2-amino-3-hydroxycyclopent-2-enone (C5N) moiety. Reduction of the C5, C6 epoxide yields type II asukamycins (31) with attenuated activities. The asu BGC has been characterized through heterologous expression in S. lividans K4-114 and mutagenesis in the host Streptomyces nodosus, enabling functional assignment (Rui et al., 2010). Genes are organized into functional groups: asuA1-A3 (3-amino-4-hydroxybenzoic acid, 3,4-AHBA formation and adenylation), asuB1-B4 (cyclohexylcarbonyl-CoA formation), asuC1-C15 (polyketide chain assembly), asuD1-D3 (C5N biosynthesis/attachment), and asuE1-E3 (post-assembly oxygenations), with additional regulators (asuR1-R6) and an efflux protein (asuM1) (Fig. 4A). Groups asuA–D assemble protoasukamycin (32); Groups asuE1-E3 install the epoxyquinone on the aromatic ring to furnish asukamycin (6). The late-stage mC7N formation proceeds via tandem flavin-dependent monooxygenations: AsuE1 hydroxylates the 3,4-AHBA ring of protoasukamycin (32) to give 4-hydroxyprotoasukamycin (33), with AsuE2 acting as a pathway-specific flavin reductase that boosts AsuE1 activity; AsuE3 then epoxidizes the C5–C6 bond to generate the epoxyquinone. These steps have been established by heterologous reconstitution, mutagenesis, and in vitro biochemistry.

(A) The asu gene cluster; (B) biosynthesis of asukamycin (6) from S. nodosus subsp. Asukaensis (Rui et al., 2010).

Mutagenesis and substrates feeding experiments highlight pathway flexibility. The adenylation enzyme AsuA2 shows broad substrate tolerance for meta- and para-substituted aryl acids (e.g., 4-aminobenzoic acid, 3-aminobenzoic acid, 4-hydroxybenzoic acid, and 3-amino-4-chlorobenzoic acid in vitro (Rui et al., 2013). Feeding these acids to a S. nodosus ΔasuA3 mutant (asuA3 encodes a condensation enzyme) led to incorporation into protoasukamycin-like products; however, the downstream flavin monooxygenases AsuE1 (protoasukamycin 4-monooxygenase) and AsuE3 (epoxidase) did not install the epoxyquinone on these analogs, indicating that the aromatic substitution pattern strongly governs processing at the mC7N stage. Together with the characterization of AsuE1/E3 as the two-step hydroxylase/epoxidase pair, these results support that the epoxyquinone pharmacophore is functionally indispensable: structure–activity analysis showed that abolishing the epoxide markedly reduces anti-MRSA activity in manumycin-type metabolites, including asukamycin analogs.

Terreic Acid, Patulin, Paraphaeones, Trichoxide, Iso-A82775C, Biscognienyne B, and Asperpentyns from Ascomycete Fungi

Terreic acid (7) is a polyketide from Aspergillus terreus (Yamamoto et al., 1980) with antibacterial activity and potential anticancer application as a Bruton’s tyrosine kinase inhibitor implicated in mast-cell activation and B-cell development (Kawakami et al., 1999) (Fig. 5B). Its biosynthetic cluster encodes atX (polyketide synthase), atA (salicylate 1-monooxygenase), atB (transporter), atC (GMC oxidoreductase), atD (cupin-domain protein), atE and atG (cytochrome P450 monooxygenases), and atF (transcription factor) (Fig. 5A) (Boruta & Bizukojc, 2014; Fujii et al., 1996; Guo et al., 2014). The pathway was reconstituted by combinatorial expression in Pichia pastoris (Kong et al., 2018). AtX assembles 6-methylsalicylic acid (34) from acetyl- and malonyl-CoA; AtA performs oxidative decarboxylation to yield 3-methylcatechol (35); AtE hydroxylates 35 to 3-methyl-1,2,4-benzenetriol (36); AtD epoxidizes 36 to produce terremutin (37); and AtC carries out the final oxidation to 7. Collectively, these steps outline an oxidative tailoring cascade from 34 to 7 and establish the minimal enzymatic set required for 7 biosynthesis.

(A) The gene clusters of at, pat and par; (B) biosynthesis of terreic acid (7) from Aspergillus terreus, (C) patulin (8) from Penicillium expansum, and (D) paraphaeones A (10) from Paraphaeosphaeria sp. C-XB-J-1 (Jiang et al., 2025; Kong et al., 2018; Li et al., 2019; Liu et al., 2019).

Patulin (8) is a toxic fungal secondary metabolite and a frequent mycotoxin in fruits and fruit-derived foods (Fig. 5C). Its biosynthesis has long served as a model for dissecting polyketide assembly. The patulin gene cluster is found in Aspergillus (Artigot et al., 2009) and Penicillium species (Li et al., 2015) (Fig. 5A). In Penicillium expansum, substrate-feeding combined with targeted knockouts verified most enzymatic steps (Li et al., 2019). PatK, a 6-methylsalicylic acid synthase, generates 6-methylsalicylic acid (34) from acetyl- and malonyl-CoA. PatG (6-methylsalicylic acid decarboxylase) converts this to m-cresol (38); PatH (P450 monooxygenase) hydroxylates to m-hydroxybenzyl alcohol (39); and PatI (P450 monooxygenase) introduces a further hydroxyl to give gentisyl alcohol (40). The cupin-domain protein PatJ is involved in oxidative conversion to isoepoxydon (41) from 40. PatN (dehydrogenase) then oxidizes 41 to phyllostine (9); PatF (hypothetical protein) converts 9 to neopatulin (42); PatD (alcohol dehydrogenase/enoylreductase) reduces to E-ascladiol (43); and PatE (oxidoreductase) completes the pathway to patulin (8).

Paraphaeones, a series of fungal ECH-containing meroterpenoids (e.g., paraphaeone A, 10), were isolated from the endophytic fungus Paraphaeosphaeria sp. C-XB-J-1 (Fig. 5D) (Li et al., 2024). These natural products exhibit SARS-CoV-2 Mpro inhibitory and anti-inflammatory activities (Li et al., 2024). The putative par gene cluster has been identified for paraphaeones biosynthesis (Fig. 5A) (Jiang et al., 2025) and it is homologous to yan and mac clusters, which produce yanuthones and macrophorins, respectively (Holm et al., 2014; Tang et al., 2017). The biosynthetic pathway was partially elucidated through heterologous gene combination in Aspergillus oryzae NSAR1 strain (Jiang et al., 2025). Initially, the polyketide synthase ParA generates 6-methylsalicylic acid (34) from acetyl-CoA and malonyl-CoA, followed by the decarboxylase ParB that catalyzes decarboxylation to afford m-cresol (38). The hydroxyl group is introduced at C-4 position by cytochrome P450 monooxygenase ParC to produce 2-methyl-hydroquinone (44). Subsequently, the prenyltransferase ParG installs isoprenyl chain at C-2 position to form farnesyl-hydroquinone (45) along with oxidative derivative, 3-desmethylplastochinon (46). The cupin domain-containing protein ParE performs epoxidation at C2–C3 position to yield intermediate paraphaeones H (47) with epoxyquinone moiety. Finally, the SDR ParD catalyzes hydroxylation at C-4 carbonyl group to give product, 7-deacetoxyyanuthone A (11). The enzymatic function of ParE and ParD was further studied by in vitro assay. The cupin domain-containing protein ParE acts as an epoxidase using Zn2+ as optimal cofactor and incorporates oxygen atom from O2 into substrate, while the SDR ParD acts as the C-4 reductase to form hydroxyl group in ECH moiety. The protein alignments, molecular dynamics simulations and site-directed mutagenesis of ParE revealed that the key residues His62, His64, Glu69, and His104 play the critical roles in substrate binding and coordinate metal ion Zn2+, facilitating oxidative catalysis.

Iso-A82775C (13), a prenylated epoxycyclohexane bearing an exocyclic isopropenyl-allene, was co-isolated from the plant endophyte Pestalotiopsis fici with chloropupukeananin and chloropestolides (e.g., chloropestolide A, 12), chlorinated tricyclic/bicyclic metabolites with antimicrobial, antitumor, and anti-HIV activities (Liu et al., 2008; Shareef et al., 2015) (Fig. 6B). Structural relationships among the co-isolated precursors, intermediates, and products suggested that these metabolites originate from maldoxin (49), derived from pestheic acid (48), and 13 through a Diels–Alder reaction. (Liu et al., 2011, 2009, 2008). The 34.55-kb iac gene cluster has been identified, which includes a prenyltransferase gene, iacE, whose function was verified by targeted gene deletion (Pan et al., 2018) (Fig. 6A). Based on genetic and chemical evidence, the pathway to iso-A82775C begins with IacE-catalyzed prenylation of siccayne (50) to give pestalodiol E (51). The cupin domain-containing protein IacF is crucial for the epoxidation of 51 to pestalodiol F (52). Subsequent transformations involved by the short-chain dehydrogenase/reductase IacG yield pestalofone A (53). Finally, the SDR IacC participates in the biosynthetic steps from 53 to 13. These findings elucidate the pathway to iso-A82775C and shed light on the assembly of its complex chlorinated scaffold.

Biscognienyne B (14), a meroterpenoid with an acetylenic isoprene unit and an epoxycyclohexenol, was isolated from the endolichenic fungus Biscogniauxia sp. (71–10-1–1) (Fig. 6C) (Zhao et al., 2017). Guided by homology to the prenyltransferase IacE and cupin IacF, the bis gene cluster was identified (Lv et al., 2020; Pan et al., 2018) (Fig. 6A). Heterologous expression in A. oryzae NSAR1 enabled stepwise elucidation: the UbiA-type prenyltransferase BisJ prenylates 4-hydroxybenzoic acid (54) to 4-hydroxy-3-prenylbenzoic acid (55). The cytochrome P450 BisI then performs an unusual alkynylation, C8–C9 desaturation, to furnish eutypinic acid (56). Subsequently, the BisD decarboxylates 56 to give siccayne (50). The ABBA-type prenyltransferase BisB appends a second prenyl group to give pestalodiol E (51). In downstream reconstitution, introduction of the cupin domain-containing protein BisC into the bisB/D/I/J co-expressing background abolished accumulation of compound 51 without the detection of epoxidized intermediates. Full production of biscognienyne B (14) required co-expression of bisC and the short-chain dehydrogenase/reductase bisF together with bisB, bisD, bisI, and bisJ. Feeding and in vitro assays further showed that BisI is promiscuous toward both C5 and C15 prenyl chains.

Meanwhile, the BGC aty involved in asperpentyn biosynthesis was reported (Chen et al., 2020) (Fig. 6A). (+)-Asperpentyn (16), a 1,3-enyne cyclohexanoid terpenoid, was isolated from the soil fungus Aspergillus sp. PSU-RSPG185 (Fig. 6D). The aty gene cluster was identified, and the upstream pathway enzymes were characterized through heterologous expression in Saccharomyces cerevisiae, substrate-feeding experiments, and in vitro assays. In vitro assays confirmed that AtyH converts l-phenylalanine to cinnamic acid (57). Heterologous expression of atyH in S. cerevisiae produced p-coumaric acid (58), supporting a role for AtyH in supplying benzenoid precursors such as 4-hydroxybenzoic acid (54). The UbiA prenyltransferase (AtyB) preferentially transfers a five-carbon prenyl chain, rather than a polyprenyl chain, to 4-hydroxybenzoic acid acceptor (54) and form 4-hydroxy-3-prenylbenzoic acid (55). The P450 monooxygenase AtyI catalyzes a four-electron oxidation that converts 55 to eutypinic acid (56), installing the hallmark 1,3-enyne via a (7Z)-diene intermediate. AtyI is bifunctional: it first dehydrogenates the prenyl chain to a cis-diene, then catalyzes acetylenation to generate the alkyne moiety. Downstream enzymes were characterized by heterologous reconstitution in A. oryzae, coupled with chemical synthesis, chiral analysis, and in vitro assays (Wang et al., 2025). The FAD-dependent monooxygenase AtyG catalyzes oxidative decarboxylation of eutypinic acid (56) to give siccayne (50); notably, AtyG does not accept 4-hydroxy-3-prenylbenzoic acid, contrasting with VibMO1 from the basidiomycete Boreostereum vibrans (Yang et al., 2016). The cupin domain-containing protein AtyE (conserved domain, COG0662; mannose-6-phosphate isomerase type) then oxidizes the hydroquinone of 50 and performs a stereoselective (2R,3S) β-epoxidation to furnish (2R,3S)-β-epoxyquinone (59). Next, the (S)-selective ketoreductase AtyD reduces the C-4 keto group of 59 to produce (+)-harveynone (60). Crucially, the epoxide configuration governs AtyD regioselectivity: β-epoxide substrates undergo C-4 reduction, whereas the off-pathway α-epoxyquinone (61) is processed via a four-electron, (S)-selective reduction to 4-epi-(+)-asperpentyn (62). The (R)-selective ketoreductase AtyC subsequently reduces the C-1 keto group of 60 to an R-alcohol, yielding (–)-asperpentyn (15). Site-directed mutagenesis of atyD identified active-site residues that govern recognition of (2S,3R)-α- vs. (2R,3S)-β-epoxyquinones and determine C-1 vs. C-4 ketoreduction. Notably, a one-pot reaction combining the AtyD_F97A variant with AtyC converted the chemically synthetic (2S,3R)-α-epoxyquinone (61) to (+)-asperpentyn (16). Together, these findings reveal a modular oxidative–reductive cascade that couples P450-mediated enyne installation with cupin epoxidation and epoxide-directed, stereoselective ketoreductions, explaining how alternative enzyme homologs generate the stereochemical diversity observed across epoxyquinoid natural products.

Trichoxide (17) is an epoxycyclohexenol natural product from T. virens, identified by genome mining and derived from a highly reducing polyketide synthase (HR-PKS) pathway that generates salicylaldehyde intermediates (Liu et al., 2019) (Figure 6E). The HR-PKS VirA programs a linear, partially reduced polyketide aldehyde, virensol C (64) in which the first two ketides are fully reduced, the next three are dehydrated, and the final three remain as β-hydroxy units; co-expression of the cupin domain-containing protein VirC increases 64, suggesting a role in product turnover. Short chain reductase/dehydrogenase (SDR) VirB oxidizes the C7 alcohol of 64 to the ketone 65, and SDR VirD then drives oxidative aromatization to salicylaldehyde 5-deoxyaurocitrin (68). Verified by the in vitro assays, VirD first performs C3 dehydrogenation to a β-keto aldehyde (66), enabling intramolecular C2 to C7 aldol condensation (67) and dehydration, giving 68. Downstream tailoring converts the salicyl framework into the epoxycyclohexene scaffold. SDR VirG reduces 68 to the benzylic alcohol, virensol B (69), and the P450 monooxygenase VirE installs an additional phenolic hydroxyl to furnish the hydroquinone, virensol A (70). The epoxide is then introduced by a proposed two-component oxidative step: the cupin domain-containing enzyme VirH, together with the FAD-dependent oxidoreductase VirI, catalyzes oxidative dearomatization/epoxidation of 70 to a putative epoxycyclohexene-1,4-dione intermediate (71) observed at low titer in the reconstitution (assignment from mass spectrometry and ultraviolet absorption spectroscopy). Finally, two additional SDRs, VirK and VirL, reduce the 1,4-dione to the 1,4-diol, completing trichoxide (17).

Panepoxydone from Basidiomycete Fungi

Panepoxydone (18) is an ECH natural product isolated from the basidiomycete mushroom Panus rudis in 1970 (Fig. 7B) (Isaka et al., 2015; Kis et al., 1970; Rukachaisirikul et al., 2005; Si-Xian et al., 2020; Vásquez et al., 2018). It is known as an NF-κB pathway inhibitor and shows antimicrobial and antitumor activities (Erkel et al., 1996). The panepoxydone biosynthetic locus (pan-BGC) was identified in P. rudis, (Fig. 7A) and the biosynthetic pathway was partially elucidated (Yang et al., 2023). First, a phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PanA) is involved in the pathway from l-phenylalanine to 4-hydroxy-benzoic acid (54). A FAD-dependent monooxygenase (PanI), homologous to VibMO1 from B. vibrans and located adjacent to the core cluster, then oxidatively decarboxylates 54 and 4-hydroxy-3-prenylbenzoic acid (55) to produce hydroquinone (72) and prenylhydroquinone (73), respectively. A UbiA-family prenyltransferase (PanE) generates prenylhydroquinone (73) from hydroquinone (72). These assignments were validated by heterologous expression and feeding experiments that produced the expected prenylhydroquinone intermediate 73. From 73, a cytochrome P450 (PanH) performs the regio-/stereoselective epoxidation furnish an ECH intermediate 74, which is then reduced by endogenous reductases in the yeast host to give lentinoid E (75). A panH-expressing S. cerevisiae BY4742 strain functioned as a whole-cell biocatalyst to generate a small library of ECH analogues.

(A) pan gene cluster; (B) biosynthesis of panepoxydone (18) from P. rudis (Yang et al., 2023).

Conclusion

Epoxyquinoids possess highly oxygenated cores with multiple contiguous stereocenters and reactive functionalities. Their striking stereochemical diversity arises from numerous permutations of epoxide and hydroxyl configurations across the epoxyquinol and epoxyhydroquinone families. Consequently, stereoselective synthesis of epoxyquinoids remains challenging, particularly due to the densely substituted cyclohexane ring. This review summarizes experimentally characterized biosynthetic pathways and enzyme functions that assemble epoxyquinoids across Actinobacteria, Ascomycota, and Basidiomycota. Notably, the enzymes that install epoxides derive from distinct families in different lineages. In Actinobacteria, the FAD-dependent monooxygenase AsuE3 operates in asukamycin/manumycin biosynthesis, while the two-component flavin-dependent monooxygenase ActVA-ORF5/ActVB in actinorhodin biosynthesis exhibits a tailoring epoxyquinone-forming activity alongside quinone–hydroquinone interconversion. In Basidiomycota, the mushroom cytochrome P450 PanH epoxidizes prenylhydroquinones and related derivatives. In Ascomycota, cupin domain-containing proteins such as AtD and PatJ are implicated in epoxyquinone formation. How these catalysts enforce regio- and stereoselective epoxidation remains a central mechanistic question. For PanH, molecular docking and molecular dynamics simulations with four substrates identified residues that likely underpin activity and specificity: Leu138, Thr222, Asn503, and Val505 may contribute to structural stability and catalysis, whereas Ile312, Glu315, Ala316, and Thr320 appear involved in substrate recognition. As an illustrative case, the dioxygenases DHAE I and DHAE II convert 2,5-dihydroxyacetanilide into complementary epoxides, yielding 5R,6S- and 5S,6R-epoxyquinones, respectively. High-resolution structures of enzyme-substrate complexes will be invaluable for defining the determinants of regio- and stereocontrol and for guiding biocatalyst engineering toward programmable epoxidation in complex natural product frameworks.

Diverse organisms have evolved distinct enzymatic solutions to build epoxyquinoid scaffolds. An exemplified comparison (Yang et al., 2023) is panepoxydone (18) vs. (–)-harveynone, the enantiomer of (+)-harveynone (60): aside from a 1,3-enyne vs. prenyl substitution, their cores are highly similar, yet the reaction order and key enzymes differ markedly. Core entry enzymes, phenylalanine ammonia-lyase and 4-hydroxybenzoate 1-hydroxylase (oxidative decarboxylase), are widespread in both ascomycetes (e.g., AtyH/AtyG from Aspergillus sp. PSU-RSPG185 (Chen et al., 2020); BisK/BisD from Biscogniauxia sp. 71–10-1–1 (Lv et al., 2020) and basidiomycetes (e.g., PanA/PanI from P. rudis) (Yang et al., 2023). However, prenylation diverges: PanE in the panepoxydone (18) pathway clusters phylogenetically with 4-hydroxybenzoic acid geranyltransferases, whereas BisJ in the biscognienyne B (14) pathway, homologous to AtyB in (–)-asperpentyn (15) biosynthesis, belongs to UbiA-type prenyltransferases. In the downstream pathway, ascomycetes typically employ cupin domain proteins to convert hydroquinone derivatives to the ECH scaffold, in contrast to basidiomycetes, which use a cytochrome P450 monooxygenase for this chemical conversion. Collectively, these lineage-specific solutions reveal that epoxyquinoid biosynthetic pathways evolved independently in ascomycetes and basidiomycetes.

Beyond epoxide installation, downstream tailoring steps generate additional stereocenters and functionalities. In the (–)-asperpentyn (15) pathway, AtyC and AtyD act as R- and S-selective ketoreductases, respectively, underscoring that multiple stereoselective reductases govern epoxyhydroquinone assembly. In addition, biscognienyne B (14) and trichoxide (17) possess vicinal trans-diols (R, R), whereas iso-A82775C (13) carry cis-diols (R, S), revealing that additional ketoreductases with distinct selectivities remain to be discovered. Special functionality can also arise from oxidative carbon–carbon remodeling: in both biscognienyne B (14) and (–)-asperpentyn (15) biosyntheses, P450 enzymes (BisI and AtyI) effect four-electron oxidation of prenyl groups to install a 1,3-enyne; notably, BisI accepts both C5 and C15 prenyl chains. These enzyme-enabled structural features help underpin the pharmacological promise and chemical-biology relevance of epoxyquinoids.

With the continued expansion of microbial genome sequences, BGCs for epoxyquinoids can be rapidly identified and reconstituted in heterologous hosts. This capability enables combinatorial biosynthesis—mixing and matching enzymes from distinct pathways to generate “unnatural” natural products. For example, Streptomyces chassis could be engineered to produce novel manumycin-type analogues by swapping epoxidases or modulating polyketide chain-length-determining elements. In parallel, individual enzymes such as the cupin-domain protein AtyE and the ketoreductases AtyC/AtyD are promising stand-alone biocatalysts for accessing epoxide intermediates and achieving highly regio- and stereoselective ketoreduction in biosynthesis.

Despite substantial progress in experimentally elucidating several epoxyquinoid pathways, important gaps remain. The epoxide-installing enzymes, their mechanisms, and the rules governing product regio- and stereo-selectivity remain less known. Structural and mechanistic studies will be essential to resolve these questions. As additional epoxyquinoids continue to be discovered and structurally characterized across diverse taxa, new BGCs and biosynthetic logics will emerge, elucidating how Nature programs stereo-defined ECH assembly and expanding the enzyme toolkit for pathway engineering and chemoenzymatic synthesis.
