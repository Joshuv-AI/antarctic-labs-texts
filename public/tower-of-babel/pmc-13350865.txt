Survival outcomes with matched targeted therapy in advanced biliary tract cancer: a large global cohort analysis

ABSTRACT
The prognosis of advanced biliary tract cancer remains poor despite up to 40% of tumors harboring actionable molecular alterations. This international collaborative study from Mayo Clinic, Duke Cancer Institute, National Cancer Center East (Japan), and the SCRUM-Japan GOZILA/MONSTAR-SCREEN projects evaluated clinical outcomes in 1049 patients with advanced BTC based on tumor molecular profiling and matched treatment selection. Patients were categorized into non-actionable, actionable with matched targeted therapy (matched), and actionable without matched therapy (unmatched) groups. Among 358 patients (34.1%) with actionable alterations, 160 (44.7%) received matched therapy. The matched group demonstrated significantly longer median overall survival (23.3 months; 95% CI: 19.5–30.4) compared to unmatched (14.7 months; 95% CI: 12.8–17.9) and non-actionable groups (17.1 months; 95% CI: 15.5–18.7; HR 0.57, 95% CI: 0.46–0.71; p 0.0001). In 700 patients receiving ≥2 lines of therapy, matched therapy remained independently associated with improved survival (HR 0.62; 95% CI 0.43–0.90). Notably, patients with actionable tumors who did not receive matched therapy experienced worse outcomes than those with non-actionable disease. These findings highlight the critical need for universal molecular profiling and equitable access to targeted therapies in advanced BTC.

Introduction

The prognosis of advanced biliary tract cancer (BTC) remains poor, with a five-year survival rate of less than 5%1. Tumor gene sequencing technologies substantially improved understanding of the oncogenic process in BTC, which in turn led to the discovery of actionable molecular alterations and the development of matched targeted agents. Up to 40% of BTC tumors harbor at least one actionable or potentially actionable target. In intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma, IDH1 mutations and FGFR2 fusions are found in respectively 10–20% and 9–15% of cases and confer a more indolent biology with improved survival. Conversely, extrahepatic tumors frequently harbor KRAS mutations (up to 40%) and ERBB2 amplifications (5–20%)2–4. Moreover, immune checkpoint inhibitors expanded treatment options and are recommended as single modality treatment for certain tumors with predictive biomarkers, including high tumor mutational burden (TMB), mismatch repair deficiency (dMMR), or high microsatellite instability (MSI-H)5,6.

Despite continued efforts in drug development, chemoimmunotherapy remains the recommended first-line treatment for patients diagnosed with advanced BTC, while most targeted agents are reserved for use upon first-line disease progression7. Notably, up to 45% of the patients with advanced BTC may become ineligible for second-line therapy, thus limiting their opportunity to benefit from targeted agents8–10. A few retrospective studies have shown that using matched targeted therapy was associated with improved survival in real-world settings10–12. Nevertheless, these results were limited by factors such as small sample size and underrepresentation of extrahepatic and gallbladder BTCs. In this international multicenter study, we examined the real-world survival impact of targeted therapy on advanced BTC.

Results

Baseline characteristics in the overall cohort

The study cohort comprised 1,049 patients with advanced BTC, including 625 (59.6%) from Japan and 424 (40.4%) from the United States. Molecular profiling identified actionable alterations in 358 patients (34.1%), of whom 160 (44.7%) received matched targeted therapy at any time during their treatment course, while 691 patients (65.9%) had non-actionable tumors. Patients receiving matched therapy were significantly younger (median age at diagnosis 60 years) compared to those in the unmatched (65.8 years) and non-actionable groups (67 years; p < 0.001). Gender distribution varied significantly, with female patients comprising 58.1% (N = 93/160) of the matched group vs. 38.9% (N = 77/198) in the unmatched and 43.3% (N = 299/691) in the non-actionable groups (p < 0.001).

The cohort was predominantly Asian (61.3%), particularly in the unmatched group (N = 137/198, 69.2%), while Caucasian patients were more likely to receive matched therapy (N = 95/160, 59.4%). Tumor location differed substantially between groups, with intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma predominating in the matched group (N = 118/156, 75.6%) compared to the unmatched (N = 96/181, 53.0%) and non-actionable groups (N = 293/626, 46.8%) (p < 0.001). Conversely, gallbladder tumors were underrepresented in the matched group (17 of 156, 10.9%) relative to the unmatched (N = 52/181, 28.7%) and non-actionable groups (N = 161/626, 25.7%).

Treatment patterns revealed that patients receiving matched therapy underwent more lines of systemic therapy (median lines of treatment: 3) compared to both the unmatched and non-actionable groups (median lines of treatment: 2 in both) (p < 0.001). Similar differences in age, gender, race, and tumor location were observed in the three groups for patients who received two or more total lines of treatment (supplemental Table 1).

Survival analysis in the main cohort

There were notable differences in ethnic distribution across the molecular alterations. Asian patients accounted for 97 of 120 (80.8%) somatic BRCA1/2 mutations and 51 of 69 (73.9%) ERBB2 amplifications. Conversely, FGFR2 fusions were predominantly found in Caucasian patients (N = 60/80, 75.0%). Compared to the other subgroups, the median ages at diagnosis were lower in the FGFR2 fusion (median age at diagnosis 53 years) and KRAS-G12C groups (62.6 years), compared to those with TMB-high tumors (69.3 years).

Primary tumor location also differed by molecular subtype. Intrahepatic BTCs tumors harbored 73 of 76 (96.1%) FGFR2 fusions and all 42 (100%) IDH1 mutations, while gallbladder tumors accounted for 34 of 65 (52.3%) ERBB2-amplified cases.

Survival outcomes differed by molecular subtype. The IDH1-mutated cohort had the longest median OS (23.7 months; 95% CI 17.3–30.1), followed by FGFR2 fusion patients (23.3 months; 95% CI 20.8–34.6). By comparison, KRAS-G12C mutations carried the poorest prognosis (median OS 11.1 months; 95% CI 6.6-NE), though this subgroup included only six patients (supplementary fig. 1).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the largest international cohort to date evaluating real-world treatment patterns and survival outcomes in advanced BTC based on molecular profiling. In patients with molecularly actionable tumors, matched targeted therapy was associated with significantly longer OS. Of notable significant, the omission of therapy in this eligible group resulted in inferior survival compared even to patients with non-actionable advanced BTC. In the context of the rising global incidence of BTC10,11, these results underscore the critical importance of universal molecular testing and timely access to targeted therapies in this aggressive neoplasia.

Prior studies with limited sample size have suggested a survival benefit with matched therapy in advanced BTC. Doleschal et al. examined the survival trends of 127 patients with advanced BTC, 36 (28.3%) of whom received matched therapy after first-line disease progression10. The median OS was 22.3 months (95% CI 14.7–29.2) in the targeted group, while the chemotherapy group (which included all patients regardless of actionable alterations in the tumor) had an inferior median OS of 11.7 months (95% CI 8.2–16.6) (HR 0.45, 95% CI 0.28–0.72, p = 0.001). The authors also stratified the OS analysis according to the European Society of Medical Oncology (ESMO) scale for the clinical actionability of molecular targets (ESCAT). ESCAT provides an evidence-based framework in which molecular targets are ranked into six tiers based on the quality of applicability of clinical evidence13. In their cohort, 23 patients received targeted therapy based on the presence of an ESCAT tier I/II alteration (defined as clinically relevant or potentially relevant alterations: FGFR2 fusion, BRAF-V600E mutation, ERBB2 amplification, high PD-L1 expression, or MSI-H tumor) while 13 were treated based on an ESCAT tier III/IV molecular target (mutations in FGFR2/3, BRCA1/2, PIK3CA, EGFR, BRAF non-V600E). The OS benefit of targeted therapy was limited to those with an ESCAT tier I/II alteration (HR 0.44, 95% CI 0.22–0.87, p = 0.02), and not to those with tier III/IV alterations (HR 0.78, 95% CI 0.4–1.56, p = 0.47). Nevertheless, data interpretation should be done cautiously due to small sample sizes as well as the overrepresentation of FGFR2 fusions in the ESCAT tier I/II group (n = 11 out of 23, 47.8%).

Similarly, Verdaguer et al. examined the outcome of 327 patients with advanced BTC (78.9% with intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma), which included 184 patients whose tumor had actionable alterations per ESCAT I-IV alterations. Consistent with our findings, in their cohort of 50 patients who received molecularly matched therapy, median OS was 22.6 months vs. 14.3 months in those without actionable ESCAT alterations (HR 0.58, p = 0.005). Subgroup analysis showed that patients with ESCAT I-II alterations experienced significantly longer median progression-free survival than those with ESCAT III-IV alterations (5.0 vs. 1.9 months; HR 0.36, p = 0.02)11. In another single-center retrospective study with 80 patients, molecularly matched targeted therapy was associated with significantly longer progression-free survival (HR 0.60, 95% CI 0.37–0.99, p = 0.047) after adjusting for patient age, gender, performance status, tumor type, extent of disease, prior radical surgery, number of prior regimens, and the number of drug components in the regimen. OS data was not provided12.

Our study reported similar conclusions on the survival benefits of targeted therapy in advanced BTC. Nevertheless, in contrast to prior reports, our analysis was not limited by a small sample size or the underrepresentation of extrahepatic and gallbladder subtypes.

Major societal guidelines endorse tumor molecular profiling in all patients with advanced BTC eligible for systemic therapy, yet differ in their recommendations for specific gene alterations (Supplemental Table 2). For instance, the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) omits BRCA1 and BRCA2 from the list of genes for routine testing7. In our cohort, somatic BRCA1/2 were the most commonly altered genes, identified in 11.4% (120 of 1049) of all patients. Notably, 23 (18.2%) of these BRCA1/2-altered cases originated from U.S. centers, where NCCN recommendations would have precluded the identification of their tumor as actionable. As a result, these patients would not have been eligible for potentially beneficial clinical trials. In contrast, the remaining 97 of 120 (80.3%) patients with BRCA1/2 alterations were from Japan. The current Pan-Asian adapted ESMO Clinical Practice Guidelines consider BRCA1/2 or PALB2 mutations as ESCAT tier III-A and report a 100% consensus that these patients should be considered for PARP inhibitors, preferably within clinical trials. However, their proposed workup algorithm excludes BRCA1/2 or PALB2 from testing in newly diagnosed patients, potentially delaying trial enrollment and therapeutic intervention14. Despite these discrepancies, all guidelines acknowledge the rapidly evolving landscape of targeted drug development, in which new therapeutic approvals may outpace updates to clinical practice guidelines.

Liquid biopsy utilizing blood-based circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) analysis offers a promising non-invasive alternative for identifying actionable tumor alterations. A recent analysis of 102 patients with BTC reported strong overall concordance between ctDNA and tissue-based molecular profiling, with blood-based testing showing 84.8% sensitivity and 79.4% positive predictive value15. Nevertheless, the liquid biopsy performance varied significantly by tumor location, being the highest in intrahepatic and gallbladder tumors (93.5% and 92%, respectively) and the lowest in extrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (56.8%). Concordance rates also differed by the type of actionable alteration. The sensitivity of blood-based analysis was 100% for alterations in IDH1, PIK3CA, BRCA1/2, MET, and MSI-high status, while significantly decreased to 40% for ERBB2 amplification (detected in two of five altered tumor) and 66.7% for FGFR2 fusions (detected in two of three).

A large-scale study analyzed 2,068 cell-free DNA (cfDNA) samples from 1,671 patients with advanced BTC and compared the findings with the tissue-based genomic data from the AACR Project GENIE16. While the prevalence of IDH1, ERBB2, and BRAF V600E alterations was statistically similar between the two datasets, cfDNA testing significantly underdetected FGFR2 fusions (1.4% vs. 4.3%; p = 0.0018) while uncovering more FGFR2 mutations (5.3% vs. 2.0%; p = 0.0077). In their subset analysis, only 18% (N = 12/67) of tissue-confirmed FGFR2 fusions were detected in blood cfDNA analysis. Additionally, the sensitivity of liquid biopsy is significantly influenced by the tumor burden and shedding as well as the timing of sample collection during the treatment course16,17. These limitations, combined with infrastructure needs and cost, prevents using liquid biopsy as a standalone testing. Currently, both NCCN and ESMO guidelines position liquid biopsy as complementary to tissue-based testing, while the Pan-Asian adapted ESMO guidelines do not specifically address its clinical role7,14,18.

Despite the growing number of approved and investigational targeted therapies for advanced BTC19–21, up to 45% of the patients may become ineligible for second-line treatment due to rapid clinical decline from disease progression or worsening performance status8–10. Reserving matched therapy beyond front-line treatment may potentially result in the loss of productive life, particularly patients with tumor molecular subtypes with an earlier disease onset, such as FGFR2 fusions or BRAF mutations22. Moreover, the inferior outcomes observed in patients with actionable alterations who did not receive matched therapy may reflect the inherently aggressive biology of certain molecular subtypes, such as ERBB2-amplified or KRAS-G12C-mutated tumors, further emphasizing the need for timely therapeutic intervention. Hence, in addition to expanding access to these agents in subsequent lines of treatment, trial development should focus on exploring their efficacy in the frontline setting. Noteworthy, the SAFIR ABC-10 study is currently evaluating a precision medicine strategy in the first-line setting. In this international phase 3 trial, patients with molecularly actionable advanced BTC will receive 12 weeks of first-line standard of care and then be randomized to either continue the same regimen or to be switched to matched targeted therapy. The trial has a primary endpoint of progression-free survival and aims to enroll 800 patients with an estimated study completion date by June 2028 (NCT05615818)23.

Several limitations to our study warrant consideration. First, as a retrospective multicenter analysis, our findings may be influenced by inherent selection biases and source data documentation. To increase the objectivity of our analyses, we opted for OS as our primary outcome instead of using progression-free survival or time-on-treatment, which are more inherent to biases and charting errors. To minimize immortal time biases, survival analysis was conducted for all patients who received at least first-line palliative chemotherapy, and further analysis of the benefit of targeted treatment was restricted to those who received two or more total lines of treatment. We acknowledge that restricting the analysis to patients who received ≥2 lines of therapy does not fully eliminate immortal time bias, particularly for those who received matched therapy in later lines. A Cox model with time-dependent covariates was considered, but not feasible due to inconsistent documentation of exact treatment dates across international sites, small line-specific subgroup sizes, and patients receiving multiple sequential targeted therapies. We also acknowledge differences in clinical practice in participating centers, which may lead to disparities in survival outcomes. Consequently, we incorporated the site of patient enrollment in our multivariable model. These factors should be considered when interpreting our results and their generalizability to broader BTC populations. Lastly, we lack detailed documentation of the clinical and logistical factors that precluded the use of matched targeted therapy in some patients with actionable alterations. Additionally, while IPTW analysis was performed, we are unable to provide detailed diagnostic tables (e.g., covariate balance, weighted baseline characteristics) in the supplementary materials due to data sharing restrictions across participating institutions, which limited access to granular patient-level data for post hoc analyses. Nevertheless, this study was designed to describe real-world outcomes and broad practice patterns, rather than the granular determinants of individual therapeutic decisions.

In summary, this is the largest international analysis providing real-world evidence that matched targeted therapy significantly improves overall survival in patients with a corresponding actionable molecular alteration. Moreover, in eligible patients with actionable advanced BTC, failure to deliver matched targeted therapy was associated with worse outcomes compared to those with non-actionable tumors.

Our findings underscore the unmet needs in the management of advanced BTC. Future research should investigate potential barriers to treatment access, including financial and geographic disparities in therapy availability, which may contribute to the observed gap between identification of actionable alterations and receipt of matched targeted therapy. Lastly, it is imperative to develop strategies to deliver targeted therapies earlier in the disease course, including trial investigations in frontline settings.

Methods

Patient selection

This is an international collaborative study involving Mayo Clinic Comprehensive Cancer Center (Arizona, Minnesota, and Florida, United States), Duke Cancer Institute (Durham, North Carolina, United States), National Cancer Center East (Japan), as well as a combined dataset from the SCRUM-Japan GOZILA, MONSTAR-SCREEN-1, MONSTAR-SCREEN-2 projects. After retrospectively reviewing the electronic medical records, we retrieved and included all patients aged 18 years or older with histologically confirmed unresectable or metastatic BTC, who had comprehensive molecular profiling of their tumor or metastatic lesion performed using commercial testing panels, including Caris Molecular Sciences, Tempus, or Foundation Medicine, Guardant360, and OncoGuide NCC Oncopanel.

All research procedures were conducted in accordance with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. This retrospective, multicenter study was approved by the local institutional review board (IRB) at each participating institution (Mayo Clinic IRB protocol 24-000212; National Cancer Center Hospital East IRB protocol 2018-313; and Duke Cancer Institute IRB protocol Pro00114674). As this study involved retrospective analysis of existing clinical and molecular data and posed minimal risk to participants, the requirement for informed consent was waived by all participating IRBs in accordance with local regulations.

Definition of actionable alterations

A tumor was considered actionable if it harbored one or more molecular alterations associated with commercially available matched targeted therapies. The list of genes was selected based on NCCN and ESMO guidelines recommendations for cholangiocarcinoma-specific targeted therapies (including IDH1 mutations, KRAS-G12C mutations, FGFR2 fusions, BRAF V600E mutations, somatic mutations of BRCA1/2, and ERBB2 amplification)7, as well as FDA-approved tumor-agnostic indications for specific biomarkers (including NTRK fusions, RET fusions, MSI-H/dMMR, and TMB-high, defined as having ≥10 mutation/megabase). Platinum chemotherapy was not considered a targeted therapy in case of tumors with somatic alterations of BRCA1/2. Additionally, patients who received experimental targeted therapy in clinical trials for other molecular targets were excluded from the survival analysis to avoid confounding by investigational treatments. This was to ensure that our findings reflect the real-world survival impact of commercially available targeted therapies rather than investigational agents with unestablished efficacy.

Based on the presence of actionable alterations in tumor and the treatment course, patients were categorized into three groups: 1) patients without any actionable alterations (non-actionable group), 2) those with actionable tumors who received matched targeted therapy at any line of therapy (matched group), and 3) those with alterations who did not receive matched therapy (unmatched group).

Statistical analysis

Patient demographics and clinical characteristics were compared between the three groups using chi-square test for categorical data or Kruskal–Wallis test for continuous data. The primary objective was to compare overall survival (OS) among the three patient groups. All included patients were required to have received at least 30 days of palliative systemic therapy. Patients with incomplete data related to their disease status or treatment course were excluded from the analysis.

We used Kaplan-Meier methods to estimate the OS, which was calculated from the start date of first-line palliative systemic therapy until death. Patients were censored at the last known date of follow-up if death had not occurred. Three-year OS rates were estimated, and log-rank test was used to compare the survival distribution between groups. This comparison was made for all patients and for patients receiving two or more total lines of treatment in order to minimize immortal time bias. In patients receiving two or more lines of therapy, a Cox proportional hazards multivariable model was used to compare matched vs. unmatched therapy for the actionable groups only. The model included age, gender, prior surgical resection, primary tumor location, enrollment site, and mutational subgroup (which includes actionable alterations of KRAS-G12C, BRCA 1/2, IDH1, ERBB2, FGFR2, as well as tumors with high TMB).

Additionally, we performed a propensity score (PS) analysis derived via logistic regression and included covariates, including age, gender, prior surgical resection, primary tumor location, enrollment site, and mutational subgroup. Inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) was applied for matched therapy, with weights trimmed at the 1st and 99th percentiles to account for extreme values. Multivariable Cox regression model was then used to assess the association between matched targeted therapy and OS within the actionable subgroup, including the weighted PS and matched vs. unmatched group status. Hazard ratios (HR) were reported with 95% confidence intervals (CI), and a two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
