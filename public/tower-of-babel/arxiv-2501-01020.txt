Nobuaki Obata


Abstract




Quadratic Embedding Constants of Strongly Regular Graphs

Abstract
We obtain an explicit formula for the
quadratic embedding constant (QEC) of a strongly regular graph

\mathrm{srg}(n,k,\lambda,\mu)
with
\mu\geq 1
.
By using QEC we give a necessary and sufficient condition
for a strongly regular graph to admit a quadratic embedding
in a Euclidean space.

Key words
adjacency matrix,
distance matrix,
strongly regular graph,
quadratic embedding constant

Introduction

Let
G=(V,E)
be a finite connected graph on
n=|V|\geq 2
vertices,
and
D=[d(x,y)]_{x,y\in V}
the distance matrix of
G
,
where
d(x,y)
is the standard graph distance
defined as the length of a shortest
walk (or path) connecting
x
and
y
.
Let
C(V)
denote the space of real column vectors

f=[f_{x}]_{x\in V}
indexed by
V
and

\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
the canonical inner product.
The
quadratic embedding constant

(
QE constant
for short) of
G
is defined to be
the conditional maximum of
the quadratic function
\langle f,Df\rangle
,

f\in C(V)
, subject to two constraints

\langle f,f\rangle=1
and
\langle{\bm{1}},f\rangle=0
,
where
\bm{1}\in C(V)
is the column vector whose entries are all one.
In short,

\mathrm{QEC}(G)=\max\{\langle f,Df\rangle\,;\,f\in C(V),\,\langle f,f\rangle=1,\,\langle{\bm{1}},f\rangle=0\}.

(1.1)

The QE constant was first introduced
in
[
23
]

keeping a profound relation to Euclidean distance geometry
and quantum probability.

A
quadratic embedding
of
a connected graph
G=(V,E)
is
a map
\psi:V\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{N}
satisfying

\|\psi(x)-\psi(y)\|^{2}=d(x,y),\qquad x,y\in V,

where the left-hand side is the square of the Euclidean distance
and the right-hand side is the graph distance.
This concept is fundamental in Euclidean distance geometry

[
1
,
8
,
11
,
12
,
13
]
,
tracing back to Schroenberg
[
24
,
25
]
.
Moreover, it gives a criterion for a state associated with the

Q
-matrix
Q=[q^{d(x,y)}]
to be positive definite,
which is important in harmonic analysis and quantum probability,
see
[
9
]
and references cited therein.
Here, as a consequence of Schoenberg’s theorem,
we note that a graph
G=(V,E)
admits a quadratic embedding
if and only if
\mathrm{QEC}(G)\leq 0
.
Such a graph is called
of QE class
.
Graphs of both QE class and non-QE class are interesting
from various aspects.

In these years,
beyond the original motivation
towards quantitative approach to quadratic embedding of graphs,
there is growing interest in the QE constant
as a new numeric invariant of connected graphs.
For some classes of graphs, the QE constants are obtained explicitly,
for example, graphs on five or less vertices

[
23
]
,
complete graphs and cycles
[
23
]
,
complete multipartite graphs
[
22
]
,
double star graphs
[
7
]
,
fan graphs
[
17
,
18
]
,
hairy cycle graphs
[
10
]
,
paths
[
15
]
,
wheel graphs
[
20
]
, and so on.
Moreover, formulas and estimates are investigated
in relation to some graph operations

[
7
,
14
,
16
]
.
It is a challenging problem to classify graphs
by using the QE constants,
for example, an attempt at classification of graphs
with
\mathrm{QEC}(G)<-1/2
was initiated
in
[
2
,
3
]
,
where graphs with certain block structures emerge.
On the other hand, it is also a highly non-trivial problem to
explore non-QE graphs and their QE constants

[
19
,
21
]
.

With the above-mentioned backgrounds, in this short paper
we obtain an explicit formula for
the QE constant of a strongly regular graph.
In fact, with the help of the well-known result
on the eigenvalues of the adjacency matrix and the fact that

\mathrm{QEC}(G)
coincides with the second largest eigenvalue
of the distance matrix for a transmission regular graph
G
,
we will prove the following results.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
G=\mathrm{srg}(n,k,\lambda,\mu)
be
a strongly regular graph with
\mu\geq 1
.
Then we have

\mathrm{QEC}(G)=-2-\frac{\lambda-\mu-\sqrt{(\lambda-\mu)^{2}+4(k-\mu)}}{2}\,.

(1.2)

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
G=\mathrm{srg}(n,k,\lambda,\mu)
be
a strongly regular graph with
\mu\geq 1
.
Then
\mathrm{QEC}(G)\leq 0
if and only if
k-2\lambda+\mu\leq 4
.
Moreover,

\mathrm{QEC}(G)=0
if and only if
k-2\lambda+\mu=4
.

Theorem 1.3
.

For any strongly regular graph

G=\mathrm{srg}(n,k,\lambda,\mu)
with
\mu\geq 1
,
we have
\mathrm{QEC}(G)\geq 0
except
G=C_{5}=\mathrm{srg}(5,2,0,1)
,
and

\mathrm{QEC}(C_{5})=-\frac{3-\sqrt{5}}{2}<0.

The paper is organized as follows.
In Section
2

we assemble basic notions and notations
of strongly regular graphs,
and in Section
3

we recall some properties of their adjacency matrices.
In Section
4
we obtain the
eigenvalues of the distance matrix
in terms of those of the adjacency matrix.
In Section
5

we prove the main results
(Theorems
1.1
–
1.3
).
In Section
6
we show some examples.

2
Strongly Regular Graphs

To avoid ambiguity, we begin with some basic notions and notations.
A
graph

G=(V,E)
is a pair of a finite non-empty set
V

and a set
E
of two-element subsets of
V
.
As usual, an element of
V
is called a
vertex

and one of
E
an
edge
.
Two vertices
x,y\in V
are called
adjacent
if

\{x,y\}\in E
, and in that case we write
x\sim y
.
We also say that

y\in V
is a
neighbour
of
x\in V
if
x\sim y
.

A graph
G=(V,E)
is called
regular
if
every vertex has the same number of neighbors,
i.e.,

k=|\{y\in V\,;\,y\sim x\}|

(2.1)

is constant, regardless of the choice of
x\in V
.
In that case,
k
is called the
degree
of
G
,
and
G
is also called a
k
-regular graph
.
A regular graph
G=(V,E)
is called
strongly regular
if

(SR1)

every two adjacent vertices have the
same number of common neighbours, i.e.,

\lambda=|\{z\in V\,;\,z\sim x,\,\,z\sim y\}|

(2.2)

is constant, regardless of the choice of
x,y\in V

such that
x\sim y
;

(SR2)

every two non-adjacent vertices have the
same number of common neighbours, i.e.,

\mu=|\{z\in V\,;\,z\sim x,\,\,z\sim y\}|

(2.3)

is constant, regardless of the choice of
x,y\in V

such that
x\not\sim y
and
x\neq y
.

Those numbers appearing in conditions (i) and (ii)
together with
n=|V|
and the degree
k

are characteristics of a strongly regular graph
G
,
and we write
G=\mathrm{srg}(n,k,\lambda,\mu)
for convenience.
It is noted, however, that the parameters

n,k,\lambda
and
\mu
do
not specify a strongly regular graph uniquely
(up to graph isomorphisms).

Remark 2.1
.

Condition (i) is fulfilled
if there is no pair of adjacent vertices,
though the number
\lambda
is not determined.
This occurs if and only if
G
is
an empty graph
\bar{K}_{n}
with
n\geq 1
.

Remark 2.2
.

Condition (ii) is fulfilled
if there is no pair of non-adjacent vertices,
though the number
\mu
is not determined.
This occurs if and only if
any pair of distinct vertices are adjacent,
that is,
G
is a complete graph
K_{n}
with
n\geq 1
.

It is convenient to refer to a general strongly regular graph

G
with its parameter
\mathrm{srg}(n,k,\lambda,\mu)

even though

\lambda
is not determined (Remark
2.1
)
or

\mu
is not determined (Remark
2.2
).
In this connection, we note the following result
which is verified easily by definition.

Proposition 2.3
.

For a strongly regular graph
G=(V,E)
the following three conditions
are equivalent:

(i)

G
is disconnected;

(ii)

\mu=0

(this presupposes that
\mu
is determined);

(iii)

G=pK_{q}
with
p\geq 2
and
q\geq 1
;

First of all, the QE constant is defined only for
a connected graph on two or more vertices,
see Introduction.
Moreover, the QE constant of the complete graph
K_{n}
is
known as
\mathrm{QEC}(K_{n})=-1
for
n\geq 2
,
see
[
23
]
.
Thus, our target is the class of strongly regular graphs
which are connected and are not complete graphs.
As is mentioned in Remark
2.2
,
the strongly regular graphs
\mathrm{srg}(n,k,\lambda,\mu)

with undetermined
\mu
are the complete graphs.
It follows from Proposition
2.3
that
the strongly regular graphs
\mathrm{srg}(n,k,\lambda,\mu)

which are not connected are characterized as
\mu=0
.
Therefore, the class of strongly regular graphs

\mathrm{srg}(n,k,\lambda,\mu)

which are connected and are not complete graphs
is characterized by
\mu\geq 1
.

Below we list some properties of strongly regular graphs

\mathrm{srg}(n,k,\lambda,\mu)
with
\mu\geq 1
.
The proofs are known and straightforward,
see e.g.,
[
4
,
5
,
6
]
.

Proposition 2.4
.

For a strongly regular graph
G=\mathrm{srg}(n,k,\lambda,\mu)

with
\mu\geq 1
, we have

\mathrm{diam}(G)=2,

where
\mathrm{diam}(G)=\max\{d(x,y)\,;\,x,y\in V\}

is the diameter of
G
.

Proposition 2.5
.

For a strongly regular graph
G=\mathrm{srg}(n,k,\lambda,\mu)

with
\mu\geq 1
, we have

n\geq 4,\qquad 2\leq k\leq n-2,\qquad 1\leq\mu\leq k,\qquad 0\leq\lambda\leq k-2.

(2.4)

Proposition 2.6
.

For a strongly regular graph
G=\mathrm{srg}(n,k,\lambda,\mu)

with
\mu\geq 1
, we have

(n-k-1)\mu=(k-\lambda-1)k.

(2.5)

Remark 2.7
.

Both sides of (
2.5
)
do not vanish for any
G=\mathrm{srg}(n,k,\lambda,\mu)
with
\mu\geq 1
.
For a general strongly regular graph
the relation (
2.5
)
is accepted as
0=0
, though
\lambda
or
\mu
may not be defined.

3
Adjacency Matrices

The adjacency matrix
A
of a graph
G=(V,E)
is
a matrix with index set
V\times V
defined by

(A)_{xy}=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if $x\sim y$},\\
0,&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}\qquad x,y\in V.

Below we list some results on the
adjacency matrices of strongly regular graphs.
The proofs are known, see e.g.,

[
4
,
5
,
6
]
.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
A
be the adjacency matrix of a strongly regular graph

G=\mathrm{srg}(n,k,\lambda,\mu)
with
\mu\geq 1
.
Then we have

A^{2}=\mu J-(\mu-\lambda)A-(\mu-k)I,

(3.1)

where
J
is the all-one matrix and
I
the identity matrix
(their sizes are understood in the context).

Proof.

Let
\bar{G}=(V,\bar{E})
be the complement of
G=(V,E)

and
\bar{A}
its adjacency matrix.
Then by definition we have

\bar{A}=J-I-A.

(3.2)

Since
G=\mathrm{srg}(n,k,\lambda,\mu)
is a strongly regular graph,
observing that

(A^{2})_{xy}
counts the number of 2-step walks from
x
to
y
,
we obtain

A^{2}=kI+\lambda A+\mu\bar{A}.

(3.3)

Then (
3.1
) follows from
(
3.2
) and (
3.3
).
∎

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
A
be the adjacency matrix of
a strongly regular graph
G=\mathrm{srg}(n,k,\lambda,\mu)
with
\mu\geq 1
.
Then we have

(A-k)(A^{2}+(\mu-\lambda)A+(\mu-k)I)=0.

(3.4)

Moreover, the above cubic polynomial is the minimal
polynomial of
A
.

Proof.

Relation (
3.4
) follows
from Proposition
3.1

and
AJ=JA=kJ
.
The last statement follows
by showing that
A^{2}+pA+qI\neq 0
for any
p,q\in\mathbb{R}
.
∎

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
G
be a strongly regular graph
\mathrm{srg}(n,k,\lambda,\mu)
with
\mu\geq 1
.
Then the eigenvalues of its adjacency matrix are given by

\{s,r,k\}
, where

\displaystyle s

\displaystyle=\frac{(\lambda-\mu)-\sqrt{(\lambda-\mu)^{2}+4(k-\mu)}}{2}\,,

\displaystyle r

\displaystyle=\frac{(\lambda-\mu)+\sqrt{(\lambda-\mu)^{2}+4(k-\mu)}}{2}\,.

Moreover, we have

s<r<k.

(3.5)

Proof.

The eigenvalues of
A
are obtained from its minimal
polynomial in Proposition
3.2
.
Since

(s-r)^{2}=(\lambda-\mu)^{2}+4(k-\mu)=(\lambda-\mu+2)^{2}+4(k-1-\lambda)>0

by Proposition
2.5
,
we obtain
s<r
.
Using
s+r=\lambda-\mu
and
sr=\mu-k
, we have

\displaystyle(k-s)(k-r)

\displaystyle=k^{2}-(\lambda-\mu)k+\mu-k

\displaystyle=k(k-\lambda-1)+\mu(k+1)

\displaystyle=(n-k-1)\mu+\mu(k+1)=n\mu>0,

(3.6)

where Proposition
2.6

is taken into account.
We thus see that
k-s
and
k-r
have the same signature.
If
k<s
and
k<r
,
all eigenvalues of
A
become positive and

\mathrm{Tr\,}A=0
is not satisfied.
Consequently, we have
k>s
and
k>r
,
and (
3.5
).
∎

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
G
be
a strongly regular graph
\mathrm{srg}(n,k,\lambda,\mu)
with
\mu\geq 1
.
If
2k+(n-1)(\lambda-\mu)\neq 0
, then the three eigenvalues
k,r,s

are all integers.

Proof.

We first note that the eigenvalue
k
of
A
is simple.
This is proved directly or by the Peron-Frobenius theorem.
Let
f
and
g
be the multiplicities of the eigenvalues

r
and
s
, respectively.
Since the size of
A
is
n
and
\mathrm{Tr\,}A=0
, we have

1+f+g=n,\qquad k+rf+sg=0.

Solving the above linear system, we obtain

f=\frac{-s(n-1)-k}{r-s}\,,\qquad g=\frac{r(n-1)+k}{r-s}\,,

where we note that
s<r
by Proposition
3.3
.
Using the explicit expressions of
s
and
r

in Proposition
3.3
, we obtain

f-g=\frac{2k+(n-1)(\lambda-\mu)}{\sqrt{D}}\,,\qquad D=(\lambda-\mu)^{2}+4(k-\mu).

(3.7)

Since
f-g
is an integer and

2k+(n-1)(\lambda-\mu)\neq 0
by assumption,
we see that
\sqrt{D}\in\mathbb{Q}
.
Since
D\geq 1
is an integer, so is
\sqrt{D}
.
Thus, both
r-s=\sqrt{D}
and
r+s=\lambda-\mu
are
integers.
It is easy to see that the parities of
r-s
and
r+s

coincide.
Consequently,
s
and
r
are integers.
∎

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
G
be
a strongly regular graph
\mathrm{srg}(n,k,\lambda,\mu)
with
\mu\geq 1
.
If
2k+(n-1)(\lambda-\mu)=0
, then

n\equiv 1(\mathrm{mod}\,4)
and

k=\frac{n-1}{2}\,,\qquad\lambda=\frac{n-5}{4}\,,\qquad\mu=\frac{n-1}{4}.

(3.8)

Moreover,

s=\frac{-1-\sqrt{n}}{2}\,,\qquad r=\frac{-1+\sqrt{n}}{2}\,,

(3.9)

and their multiplicities match and become
(n-1)/2
.

Proof.

By assumption we have

k=\frac{1}{2}(n-1)(\mu-\lambda).

(3.10)

Using
2\leq k\leq n-2
in Proposition
2.5
,
we obtain

\frac{4}{n-1}\leq\mu-\lambda\leq 2-\frac{2}{n-1}\,.

Since
\lambda
and
\mu
are integers,
we obtain
n\geq 5
and
\mu-\lambda=1
.
Then
k=(n-1)/2
by (
3.10
)
and (
3.8
) follows
by Proposition
2.6
.
Finally, since
\lambda
and
\mu
are integers in
(
3.8
),
it follows that
n\equiv 1\,(\mathrm{mod}\,4)
.
Finally, for (
3.9
) we only note
that
D=n
by (
3.8
)
and use Proposition
3.3
.
∎

A strongly regular graph

\mathrm{srg}(n,k,\lambda,\mu)

with parameters given as in (
3.8
) is
called a
conference graph
.
However, it is not fully known
which
n
’s have corresponding conference graphs.

4
Distance Matrices

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
D
be the distance matrix of a strongly regular graph

G=\mathrm{srg}(n,k,\lambda,\mu)
with
\mu\geq 1
.
Then we have

D=\frac{1}{\mu}\,(2A^{2}-(2\lambda-\mu)A-2kI).

(4.1)

Proof.

Since
\mathrm{diam}(G)=2
by Proposition
2.4
,
we have
D=2J-2I-A
.
Then, applying Proposition
3.1
,
we obtain (
4.1
).
∎

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
G=\mathrm{srg}(n,k,\lambda,\mu)

be a strongly regular graph with
\mu\geq 1
,
and
s<r<k
the eigenvalues of the
adjacency matrix as in Proposition
3.3
.
Then the eigenvalues of the distance matrix are given by

-r-2<-s-2<2(n-1)-k.

(4.2)

Proof.

In view of (
4.1
) we define
a polynomial by

\varphi(\xi)=\frac{1}{\mu}\,(2\xi^{2}-(2\lambda-\mu)\xi-2k).

Then by the spectral mapping theorem,
the eigenvalues of the distance matrix
D
are given by

\{\varphi(s),\varphi(r),\varphi(k)\}
.
We start with
\varphi(k)
.
By definition we have

\displaystyle\varphi(k)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mu}\,(2k^{2}-(2\lambda-\mu)k-2k)

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\mu}\,k(k-\lambda-1)+k.

With the help of Proposition
2.6

we have

\varphi(k)=\frac{2}{\mu}\,\mu(n-k-1)+k=2(n-1)-k.

For
\varphi(r)
, we note that

r^{2}=-(\mu-\lambda)r-(\mu-k)
,
which follows by observing that

r
is a root of the equation

\xi^{2}+(\mu-\lambda)\xi+(\mu-k)=0
.
Then we have

\displaystyle\varphi(r)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mu}\,(2r^{2}-(2\lambda-\mu)r-2k)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mu}\,(-2(\mu-\lambda)r-2(\mu-k)-(2\lambda-\mu)r-2k)

\displaystyle=-r-2.

Similarly, we have
\varphi(s)=-s-2
.
Thus, the three numbers

\varphi(k)=2(n-1)-k,\qquad\varphi(r)=-r-2,\qquad\varphi(s)=-s-2

are the eigenvalues of the distance matrix.

We next prove the inequalities in (
4.2
).
Since
s<r
by Proposition
3.3
,
we have
\varphi(r)<\varphi(s)
.
We will prove that
\varphi(s)<\varphi(k)
.
Note first that

\varphi(\xi)=\frac{2}{\mu}\bigg(\xi-\frac{2\lambda-\mu}{4}\bigg)^{2}-\frac{(\mu-2\lambda)^{2}+16k}{8\mu}\,,

and

s<\frac{2\lambda-\mu}{4}<k,

which are verified directly.
Then for
\varphi(s)<\varphi(k)
it is sufficient to show that

\frac{2\lambda-\mu}{4}-s<k-\frac{2\lambda-\mu}{4}\,.

(4.3)

Using the explicit expression of
s
,
we see that (
4.3
) is equivalent to
the following:

2k-\lambda>\sqrt{(\lambda-\mu)^{2}+4(k-\mu)}.

(4.4)

This is verified by observing that

(2k-\lambda)^{2}-\{(\lambda-\mu)^{2}+4(k-\mu)\}=(2k-\mu)(2(k-2-\lambda)+\mu+2)+2\mu>0

and both sides of (
4.4
) are positive.
∎

5
Quadratic Embedding Constants

In general, a connected graph
G=(V,E)

is called
transmission regular

if its distance matrix
D
has a constant row sum,
or equivalently if
\bm{1}\in C(V)
is an eigenvector of
D
.
It follows from the Peron-Frobenius theorem that
the largest eigenvalue of
D
, denoted by
\delta_{1}(G)
, is simple
and the associated eigenspace is spanned by
\bm{1}
.
Since the eigenspaces of
D
are mutually orthogonal,
the second largest eigenvalue
\delta_{2}(G)
is obtained as

\max\{\langle f,Df\rangle\,;\,f\in C(V),\,\,\langle f,f\rangle=1,\,\,\langle\bm{1},f\rangle=0\},

which coincides with
\mathrm{QEC}(G)
by definition.
We thus come to the following noteworthy result.

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
G
be a transmission regular grpah
and
\delta_{2}(G)
denote the second largest eigenvalue of
the distance matrix
D
.
Then we have

\mathrm{QEC}(G)=\delta_{2}(G).

Remark 5.2
.

For a general connected graph
G
, we have merely the inequality

\delta_{2}(G)\leq\mathrm{QEC}(G)<\delta_{1}(G)
.
It is an interesting open question to determine the class of
graphs
G
such that
\mathrm{QEC}(G)=\delta_{2}(G)
.
This class contains all transmission regular graphs
by Proposition
5.1

and more.
For example, a path
P_{n}
with an even
n\geq 2

is not transmission regular but
satisfies
\mathrm{QEC}(P_{n})=\delta_{2}(P_{n})
,
see
[
15
]
.

Now we go back to strongly regular graphs.

Proposition 5.3
.

Let
G
be
a strongly regular graph
\mathrm{srg}(n,k,\lambda,\mu)
with
\mu\geq 1
.
Then
G
is transmission regular and

D\bm{1}=\delta_{1}(G)\bm{1},\qquad\delta_{1}(G)=2n-k-2.

Proof.

By Proposition
4.1
we have

\sum_{y\in V}(D)_{xy}=2n-2-\sum_{y\in V}(A)_{xy}=2n-2-k,

(5.1)

which means that
G
is transmission regular and

\delta_{1}(G)=2n-k-2
.
∎

We now prove the main results
(Theorerms
1.1
–
1.3
).

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Since
G=\mathrm{srg}(n,k,\lambda,\mu)
is a strongly regular graph
with
\mu\geq 1
,
we see from Propositions

5.1

and

5.3

that
\mathrm{QEC}(G)=\delta_{2}(G)
.
On the other hand, it follows from
Proposition
4.2
that

\delta_{2}(G)=-s-2
, where
s
is the minimal eigenvalue
of the adjacency matrix
G
.
Finally, the formula

\mathrm{QEC}(G)=-s-2=-2-\frac{\lambda-\mu-\sqrt{(\lambda-\mu)^{2}+4(k-\mu)}}{2}

(5.2)

follows from Proposition
3.3
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

In view of the formula in
Theorem
1.1

or (
5.2
)
we see that
\mathrm{QEC}(G)\leq 0
if and only if

\sqrt{(\lambda-\mu)^{2}+4(k-\mu)}\leq\lambda-\mu+4.

(5.3)

Then, (
5.3
) is equivalent to the following

\displaystyle\lambda-\mu+4\geq 0,

(5.4)

\displaystyle(\lambda-\mu)^{2}+4(k-\mu)\leq(\lambda-\mu+4)^{2}.

(5.5)

By simple algebra, (
5.5
) becomes

k-2\lambda+\mu\leq 4.

(5.6)

Note that (
5.4
) follows from
(
5.6
).
In fact, from (
5.6
) we have

\lambda-\mu+4\geq k-\lambda\geq 2,

where Proposition
2.5
is taken into account.
Finally, it is readily shown that

\mathrm{QEC}(G)=0
if and only if
k-2\lambda+\mu=4
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

Suppose first that
2k+(n-1)(\lambda-\mu)=0
.
By Proposition
3.5
and
the formula in
Theorem
1.1

or (
5.2
)
we see that
n\equiv 1(\mathrm{mod}\,4)
and

\mathrm{QEC}(G)=-2-s=\frac{\sqrt{n}-3}{2}\,.

Hence
\mathrm{QEC}(G)<0
if and only if
n=5
,
that is,
G=C_{5}=\mathrm{srg}(5,2,0,1)
.

We recall
[
2
]
that

\mathrm{QEC}(G)\geq-1
for any connected graph
G

and the equality holds only when
G
is a complete graph
K_{n}

with
n\geq 2
.
Now consider a strongly regular graph
G=\mathrm{srg}(n,k,\lambda,\mu)

with
\mu\geq 1
and
2k+(n-1)(\lambda-\mu)\neq 0
.
It follows from Proposition
3.4

that
s
is an integer, so is
\mathrm{QEC}(G)
.
Therefore,
\mathrm{QEC}(G)<0
means that
\mathrm{QEC}(G)=-1

and
G
is a complete graph.
But this contradict
G=\mathrm{srg}(n,k,\lambda,\mu)

with
\mu\geq 1
.
∎

During the above proof we have already established the following
result.

Corollary 5.4
.

Let
G
be a strongly regular graph
\mathrm{srg}(n,k,\lambda,\mu)

with
\mu\geq 1
.
If
2k+(n-1)(\lambda-\mu)\neq 0
,
then
\mathrm{QEC}(G)\geq 0
is a non-negative integer.

6
Examples

We collect some examples of strongly regular graphs with their
QE constants.
The computation is simple application
of Theorem
1.1
.

Example 6.1

(cycles)
.

Among cycles
C_{n}
with
n\geq 3
only three are strongly regular graphs:

C_{3}=K_{3}=\mathrm{srg}(3,2,1,*)
,

C_{4}=\mathrm{srg}(4,2,0,2)
and
C_{5}=\mathrm{srg}(5,2,0,1)
.
Therefore, only
C_{4}
and
C_{5}
are strongly regular graphs:

\mathrm{srg}(n,k,\lambda,\mu)
with
\mu\geq 1
.
We obtain

\mathrm{QEC}(C_{4})=0,\qquad\mathrm{QEC}(C_{5})=\frac{-3+\sqrt{5}}{2}<0.

The explicit value of
\mathrm{QEC}(C_{n})
for
n\geq 3
is known

[
23
]
.
Here we only recall that
\mathrm{QEC}(C_{n})=0
for an even
n\geq 4

and
\mathrm{QEC}(C_{n})<0
for an odd
n\geq 3
.

Example 6.2

(complete multipartite graphs)
.

Let
p\geq 2
and
q\geq 2
.
Let
K_{p\times q}=K_{q,q,\dots,q}
(
q
is repeated
p
times) be
the complete multi-partite graph which consists of

p
islands and each island consists of
q
vertices.
It is noted that
K_{p\times q}=\overline{pK_{q}}
, the complement of
the disjoint union of
p
copies of the complete graph
K_{q}
.
We see that
K_{p\times q}
is a strongly regular graph
with
\mathrm{srg}(pq,(p-1)q,(p-2)q,(p-1)q)
and
the QE constant is given by

\mathrm{QEC}(K_{p\times q})=q-2.

In particular,
\mathrm{QEC}(K_{p\times 2})=0
for
p\geq 2
.
The complete multipartite graph
K_{p\times 2}=\overline{pK_{2}}
is
also called the
p
-cocktail party graph
.
A formula for the QE constant of a general
complete multipartite graph
K_{m_{1},m_{2},\dots,m_{k}}
is
obtained in
[
22
]
.

Example 6.3

(Conference graphs)
.

A strongly regular graph
is called a conference graph if its
parameter is of the form

\mathrm{srg}(n,(n-1)/2,(n-5)/4,(n-1)/4)
,
where
n\geq 5
and
n\equiv 1(\mathrm{mod}\,4)
,
see Proposition
3.5
.
We have

\mathrm{QEC}(G)=\frac{-3+\sqrt{n}}{2}\,.

In particular, we see that
all conference graphs are of non-QE class
except two cases
\mathrm{srg}(5,2,0,1)=C_{5}
and

\mathrm{srg}(9,4,1,2)
.

Example 6.4

(line graphs of
K_{n}
)
.

For
n\geq 2
the line graph of
K_{n}
, denoted by

,
is a strongly regular graph with
\mathrm{srg}(n(n-1)/2,2(n-2),n-2,4)
.
We have

\mathrm{QEC}(L(K_{n}))=0,\qquad n\geq 2.

For
n=8
we see that

has a parameter

\mathrm{srg}(28,12,6,4)
,
which is shared with the Chang graphs,
see Example
6.6
.
It is also noted that the Petersen graph
is the complement of

.

Example 6.5

(line graphs of

For

The line graph
