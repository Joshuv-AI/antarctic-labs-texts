Economic Burden of Spinal Muscular Atrophy in Singapore: A Societal Perspective Cost-of-Illness Study
Authors: Zhi Zhen Lim, Yan Ling Lim, Furene Sijia Wang, Simon Robert Ling, Stacey Kiat Hong Tay, Yi Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThe objective was to evaluate the overall economic burden of spinal muscular atrophy (SMA) in Singapore.MethodsA retrospective cohort study of electronic medical records and billing data was used to obtain medical costs from public healthcare consumption. Private medical costs, nonmedical costs from transportation and hired domestic helpers, and indirect costs due to caregiver productivity loss were estimated with a cross-sectional caregiver survey. The survey also examined intangible negative and positive effects on these caregivers. All costs were adjusted to 2024 Singapore dollar (SGD).ResultsA total of 61 patients were identified in the electronic medical records, and 15 caregivers were interviewed. One caregiver who took care of two patients with SMA was excluded from the analysis. The gross cost of inpatient admission is SGD 1663.81 (standard deviation [SD] = 986.03) per day with mean length of stay of 6.22 days (SD = 10.89) per year, while annual mean outpatient cost is SGD 940.31 (SD = 1071.28). In addition to public healthcare services, private services incur an additional SGD 5992.35 (SD = 7557.33) per year, with fixed costs for respiratory devices and mobility aids at SGD 5677.06 (SD = 4519.57) and SGD 12,412.70 (SD = 8307.35), respectively. Nonmedical costs from hiring a domestic helper, medical-related transport, and nonmedical transport amount to SGD 7002.22 (SD = 6516.36), SGD 387.86 (SD = 196.36), and SGD 840.95 (SD = 1067.81) per annum, respectively. Paid work productivity loss is estimated at SGD 42,932.88 per family per year. Average overall economic burden is estimated at SGD 62,004.03, SGD 10,840.13, and SGD 59,629.64 per patient per year from the societal perspective, healthcare system perspective, and patient perspective, respectively.ConclusionsSMA poses a significant economic burden on patients, families, and the healthcare system. This study informs policy discussions for SMA management, advocating for a comprehensive, compassionate approach and societal perspective in economic evaluations to capture all costs and benefits.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s41669-026-00655-w.

Key Points for Decision Makers

SMA imposes a substantial economic burden on patients, families, and the healthcare system in Singapore despite existing government subsidies.Taking a healthcare system perspective may significantly underestimate the burden of SMA to patients and society.Economic evaluations used for policy decisions should adopt a societal perspective to capture the full impact of SMA on patients, families, and the healthcare system.

Introduction

Spinal muscular atrophy (SMA) is a rare genetic disease characterized by dysfunction and loss of spinal cord alpha motor neurons, leading to progressive muscle weakness and atrophy [1, 2]. SMA is classified along a clinical spectrum from type 0 to type 4, distinguished by the age of symptom onset, including prenatal, < 6 months, 6–18 months, > 18 months, and adulthood, respectively, and by the maximum motor function achieved, including profound weakness at birth (type 0), never achieving independent sitting (type 1), achieving independent sitting but not independent walking (type 2), walking independently with an abnormal gait (type 3), and mild motor impairment (type 4) [1–3]. In addition to causing severe physical disability, SMA significantly affects life expectancy in patients with types 0–2, often resulting in early mortality.

Beyond health impact, SMA presents considerable economic burden for patients, caregivers, and healthcare systems [4, 5]. Total cost of illness, including direct medical, direct nonmedical, and indirect costs reported in a systematic review ranged from US $17,790 to US $234,930 per patient per year [5]. Besides expenditure within the hospital, which includes ward charges, ventilators, diagnostic tests, and rehabilitation services, caring for patients with SMA also requires resources outside the public healthcare system [5]. With recent availability of disease-modifying treatments, it is crucial to quantify the true cost to healthcare systems, immediate families, and wider society to better anticipate the management of healthcare costs for these families.

Direct nonmedical costs arise from informal care, transport expenses, and other costs. It contributes to a significant 68% of total direct costs, which includes direct medical and nonmedical expenses, and was approximated between US $25,880 and US $136,800 per patient per year [5]. Caregivers’ productivity and income are heavily impacted owing to the added responsibilities of informal care provision, with average annual indirect costs ranging from US $9440 in Germany to US $74,910 in Australia [5, 6]. This causes hefty burden to patients and their families, with up to 78% of SMA caregivers having financial difficulties [7–9].

Additionally, SMA has intangible effects on caregivers’ health-related quality of life (HRQoL), contributing to emotional strain, stress, and mental health issues such as anxiety and depression [6]. These effects are difficult to quantify monetarily, despite great relevance to patients and their families.

Globally, countries have implemented policies to support patients with rare diseases such as SMA by improving coordination of care, providing timely diagnosis and access to medicines, and encouraging research [10]. Efforts to improve coordination of care include setting up centers of expertise on rare diseases, integrated healthcare administrative databases, and national registries for rare diseases, such as in France and the UK [10]. In Singapore, patients with SMA are provided with holistic care at multidisciplinary combined clinics where they are seen by pediatricians specializing in neurology, pulmonology, sleep, orthopedics, endocrinology, and cardiology, as well as physiotherapists, occupational therapists, and orthotists [11]. Neonatal screening facilitates timely diagnosis of rare diseases and is especially crucial when an effective intervention capable of alleviating severe outcomes and mortality exists [10, 12]. Globally, there are now 30 countries with partial- or whole-population screening, with almost half implemented in 2023. Patient advocacy groups also play an important role in raising public awareness, providing patient education, engagement, advocacy, and access to useful resources [10]. The Muscular Dystrophy Association of Singapore (MDAS) was set up in 2000 to provide care and support to people with neuromuscular disorders, such as SMA, in Singapore [13].

There are currently three Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-approved treatments for SMA (nusinersen, onasemnogene abeparvovec, and risdiplam), which have been granted reimbursement by several countries to improve patient access to these disease-modifying treatments after health technology assessments and price negotiations [4, 14–16]. Despite the above efforts, access to these hyper-expensive treatments remains limited in many countries, and current strategies may not be sufficient to support all the financial needs of patients and their caregivers. In Singapore, most outpatient drug costs are borne by the patient. While there is an Orphan Drug Act in Singapore that allows access to rare disease drugs, there is no strategic funding framework for hyper-expensive treatments needed for rare diseases. According to a systematic review by Landfeldt et al., 57% of cost-of-illness studies employed a healthcare payer perspective, though indirect and direct nonmedical costs make up a substantial burden to overall costs [5]. Hence, to evaluate economic burden of SMA holistically and understand the needs of patients’ families, it is important to understand costs from a societal perspective.

This research aims to fill the knowledge gap of direct nonmedical costs and indirect costs borne by patients and caregivers, offering insights for healthcare policy and decision-making [6]. The objective of this study is to estimate the overall economic burden of SMA in Singapore by examining the associated costs incurred by pediatric and adolescent patients with SMA both within and outside the healthcare system.

Methods

Overall, this study adopts a societal perspective by comprehensively capturing direct medical costs, direct nonmedical costs, indirect costs, and intangible caregiver impacts associated with caring for patients with SMA. Direct medical costs from public healthcare consumption, which includes both inpatient and outpatient costs in a tertiary hospital, were obtained using a retrospective cohort study of hospital electronic medical records and billing data. Direct medical costs outside of public healthcare consumption, direct nonmedical costs from transportation and hired domestic helpers, and indirect costs due to loss in productivity of caregivers providing informal care to patients with SMA were estimated with a cross-sectional caregiver survey administered via online interview. Retrospective Cohort Study (Direct Medical Costs from Public Healthcare Consumption)

Public healthcare direct medical costs associated with SMA were estimated with a retrospective cohort. Deidentified data from electronic medical records, including hospital billing data obtained from the National University Hospital (NUH) and Kandang Kerbau Women’s and Children’s Hospital (KKH), were extracted from January 2004 to December 2019. Both hospitals are the main SMA treatment hospitals and the only public institutions in Singapore looking after children and adolescents with SMA. Inpatient admission data were available from 2014 to 2019 for KKH and 2014 to 2018 for NUH, while outpatient data were available from 2015 to 2019 for KKH and 2004 to 2019 for NUH. The overlapping periods of data availability, 2014 to 2018 for inpatient data and 2015 to 2019 for outpatient data, between KKH and NUH were analyzed to ensure comparability of costs between the two institutions.

Data Extraction and Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

Patients with SMA were identified using the International Classification of Diseases 10th Edition (ICD-10) codes for cases from 2011 onward and ICD-9 codes for cases before 2011. Additional outpatient cases were identified from Systematized Medical Nomenclature for Medicine (SNOMED) codes. The detailed list of codes or extraction methods is provided in Appendix 1. Data from patients who were citizens or permanent residents (PR) of Singapore were included, while foreign patients were excluded from the analysis to ensure generalizability of results to residents and reimbursement policies.

Statistical Analysis for Retrospective Cohort Study

Annual direct medical costs incurred by each patient with SMA from the two hospitals were analyzed with descriptive statistics. The total cost of health services utilization was calculated using inpatient and outpatient hospital billing data extracted for each patient across all years. The average annual direct medical cost (from public healthcare consumption) for patients was estimated from their out-of-pocket hospital bills. The corresponding cost to the public payer was estimated from the amount of government subsidies included in those bills. Subsidized patients were admitted to Ward class B2 and C, while private patients were admitted to Ward class A and B1. Inpatient and outpatient expenditure was analyzed separately. Costs were further studied on the basis of the types of healthcare services utilized, encompassing hospitalization charges, consultations, diagnostic tests, dispensed medications, surgical procedures, and various therapies. Subgroup analyses were conducted on the basis of SMA subtype and subsidy classes to identify differences in cost. Additional Goods and Services Tax (GST) was 7% during the period that this study examined, where applicable, e.g., private patients pay GST, and the government absorbs the GST for subsidized patients in public hospitals.

All costs were adjusted to 2024 Singapore dollars (SGD) using the healthcare consumer price index to account for inflation. The analysis aimed to examine public healthcare resources utilized by patients with SMA to provide insights into government subsidies and out-of-pocket payments by patients.

The analysis was conducted using Microsoft Excel and statistical program R [17].

Cross-Sectional Survey with SMA Caregivers (Indirect and Other Costs Outside Public Healthcare)

Indirect and additional direct costs associated with SMA incurred outside of the public healthcare system were estimated with a cross-sectional survey using structured caregiver interviews.

Participant Recruitment

Caregivers of patients with SMA (type 1 to type 4), who were Singaporean citizens or PR, receiving care at NUH or KKH, were recruited between October 2022 and January 2023. Patients with SMA type 0 were not included owing to poor prognosis, as infants with SMA type 0 typically survive only a few months after birth, and some may be stillborn. Consequently, caregivers of patients with SMA type 0 were not available for recruitment during the study period, making inclusion in this study not feasible. Exclusion criteria included participants (caregivers) below the age of 21 years, pregnant women, hired domestic helpers, and nonresidents. Caregivers were recruited by the clinical team when they accompanied a patient with SMA to the clinic. The potential participants (caregivers) were then referred to the research team, and the research team followed up with the participants by email or telephone.

Questionnaire

Caregiver interviews were conducted via teleconferencing using Zoom. The questionnaire collected information on patient characteristics, healthcare costs outside of public healthcare institutions, nonmedical expenses, caregiver employment status, caregiver productivity loss, and costs related to hiring domestic helpers in the past 3 months. The reported 3-month costs were subsequently extrapolated to a 12-months period to derive an annualized cost estimate. Caregivers’ productivity loss was measured in terms of missed workdays, reduced working hours, and wage reduction, attributed to caregiving of patients with SMA. Nonmedical expenses, such as transportation costs to work or school, were included from a societal perspective to capture the nonmedical burden associated with SMA-related mobility impairment on daily living. Domestic helper cost was estimated on the basis of yearly cost to hire a domestic helper and the amount of time this person spends on caring for a patient with SMA.

The questionnaire is presented in Appendix 2. We aimed to capture costs as comprehensively as possible. Direct medical costs included expenditures for essential equipment, supplements, and healthcare services, such as mobility-assist devices, feeding devices, physiotherapy, and traditional therapies. Direct nonmedical costs captured expenditures to accommodate patients’ daily living needs, for example, wheelchair-accessible transportation and home renovations to facilitate daily activities. Some households hired foreign domestic helpers to care for the patients. Participants were instructed to only consider the helpers hired for patient care and the activities for caring the patients with SMA, although such helpers may occasionally perform other household tasks. Indirect cost included productivity loss attributable to caregiving responsibilities, such as taking leave and changes in employment status to care for the patients. While the caregivers may take leave for various reasons, including minor illnesses of patients, caring for patients with SMA may require substantially more time and effort, even with minor illness.

In addition to tangible costs, intangible effects on caregiver’s physical well-being, social and recreational activities, social relationships, effects on other children in the family, and positive changes due to caring for patients with SMA were elicited through qualitative interviews.

Statistical and Narrative Analysis for Cross-Sectional Survey

All monetary figures obtained during the caregiver interviews were standardized to 2024 Singapore dollars using the healthcare consumer price index to maintain consistency and account for inflation. Annual direct medical costs incurred outside the public healthcare system, indirect costs due to loss in productivity, and direct nonmedical costs incurred by each patient due to transportation and domestic helper expenses were analyzed with descriptive statistics. As the types of health service utilization differ between patients, an average cost per cohort patient was obtained after accounting for utilization rate. The quantitative analysis was conducted with Microsoft Excel.

Qualitative information on intangible costs was summarized narratively on the basis of the responses to the five questions in the questionnaire regarding caregiver mental and physical wellness, changes in social relationships, impacts on other children and extended family, presence of positive changes, as well as wish for areas of support apart from monetary subsidies.

Overall Economic Burden of SMA in Singapore

Annual costs obtained from the cross-sectional caregiver survey and the retrospective cohort study with electronic medical records were aggregated to estimate the overall economic burden of SMA.

Ethical Considerations

The research adhered to ethical principles and obtained necessary approvals from the National Healthcare Group Domain Specific Review Board (NHG DSRB 2020/01428). Informed consent was obtained from all participating caregivers for the survey administered via online interview, ensuring that privacy and confidentiality were maintained throughout the study. An exemption from patient consent was granted for the hospital electronic medical records as the extracted data were de-identified.

Results

Overview of the Study Participants

A total of 61 participants were identified from the hospital electronic medical records from NUH and KKH to estimate the direct medical costs from the public healthcare sector. In addition, 15 caregivers representing 16 patients with SMA participated in the interviews to estimate the direct medical costs from the private healthcare sector, direct nonmedical cost, and indirect cost; however, data from the caregiver responsible for two siblings with SMA was excluded from the analysis because the direct nonmedical and indirect costs were shared between the two patients. Among the 14 caregivers and the patients included in the study, the average age of patients was 11.4 years, and caregivers had an average age of 42.4 years. Most patients had SMA type 2 (n = 10), followed by type 3 (n = 3) and type 1 (n = 1). No caregivers of patients with SMA type 4 were recruited.

Direct Medical Costs from the Public Healthcare Sector

Among the 61 patients, 49 inpatient admissions were identified using ICD and SNOMED codes. Gross cost, subsidy, and bill payable extracted from the hospital electronic medical records were analyzed.

Inpatient Healthcare Utilization

Table 1 presents the results of direct inpatient medical costs from the public healthcare sector. Among the 49 admissions, 53.0% were day admissions, primarily for sleep study. The average duration of non-elective hospitalizations was 14.4 days, incurring a gross cost of SGD 1663.81 per day before taxes and subsidies. Of this amount, patients’ families paid SGD 778.83 per day. Private patients (Ward A and B1 class), who accounted for 14.2% of total admissions, paid SGD 1212.68 per day, while the remaining subsidized patients (Ward B2 and C class) paid SGD 703.19 per day. In practice, a patient's total payable bill is calculated by subtracting the subsidy from the pre-tax gross amount, with additional 7% GST payable by private patients.

Healthcare Utilization by Subtypes

SMA subtypes were identified in 34 out of 61 patients in the records, of which 18.0% (n = 11) had SMA type 1, 13.1% (n = 8) had SMA type 2, and 24.6% (n = 15) had SMA type 3. The remaining 27 patient subtypes were unknown and could potentially be any type from 1 to 4. Average gross cost of admission per day for patients with SMA type 1 was the highest at SGD 1851.97 compared with the other SMA subtypes. Conversely, mean annual gross outpatient cost and the average frequency of outpatient visits was highest for patients with SMA type 2.

Annual Direct Healthcare Costs per Capita within Public Healthcare Institutions

The annual average direct public healthcare expenditure per capita for SMA in Singapore, encompassing both inpatient and outpatient costs, stood at around SGD 4847.78. Among these, SGD 2473.39 was paid by patients’ families.

Direct Medical Costs from the Private Healthcare Sector

Direct medical costs incurred in the private healthcare setting include private healthcare services, respiratory devices, and mobility aids, which were all paid by patients’ families. As presented in Table 3, more than half (57.1%) of patients sought private healthcare services, including physiotherapy, traditional medicine, speech therapy, occupational therapy, pediatrician, dentist, general physician, and other therapy. The annual cost of private healthcare services per patient amounted to SGD 10,486.63, translating to SGD 5992.35 per cohort patient after accounting for the utilization rate.

Direct Nonmedical Costs

Direct nonmedical costs included the cost of hiring a domestic helper and transportation costs to medical appointments, school, or work. About 57.1% of SMA families hired a domestic helper to share the caregiving burden. The annual cost of hiring a domestic helper was SGD 10,892.35 per helper. After accounting for the family without a domestic helper, the average annual cost per cohort patient was SGD 7002.22. Supplementary Table 1 in Appendix 1 summarizes the direct nonmedical costs of SMA by patients and their families.

The average annual frequency of medical-related appointments was 10.57, with an average transportation cost of SGD 387.86 per patient per year. Transportation costs included both the cost for the accompanying caregiver and the patient. The annual cost of transportation due to school or work was SGD 840.95 per patient. The estimated total annual transportation cost was SGD 1228.80.

Indirect Costs

Working parents missed an average of 2.7 workdays over a 3-month period owing to childcare responsibilities, extrapolating to 10.8 missed workdays annually due to SMA caregiving. Based on the 2024 Singapore Labour Force report, the median gross monthly income of employees aged 40–44 years is SGD 7470. The projected annual productivity loss from paid work is estimated at SGD 3764.88 per full-time working caregiver.

Half of the caregivers, who were all mothers of the children, left the workforce owing to their child’s SMA condition, and an additional 14.2% transitioned from full-time to part-time employment to accommodate caregiving duties. The median gross monthly wage of women aged 40–44 years is SGD 6528. The opportunity cost of income forgone is estimated at SGD 78,336 per year for those who left the workforce entirely. However, there are no official statistics on income from part-time employment, making it difficult to estimate productivity losses for individuals who transition from full-time to part-time work. Considering caregivers taking leaves and half of them leaving the workforce entirely, the total indirect cost was estimated to be SGD 42,932.88 per family per year.

Intangible Costs

Qualitative information on intangible costs was summarized narratively on the basis of the responses in the questionnaire. While participants expressed the strains due to caring for the patients, certain positive aspects also emerged.

Physical Toll of Caregiving

Caregivers reported suffering from chronic pains due to repetitively straining tasks such as transferring of patients with SMA. These tasks negatively impacted caregivers’ health and well-being through problems such as back injuries. Many caregivers also reported worsening in overall physical health due to a lack of time to upkeep a healthy lifestyle, which includes regular exercise and a balanced diet. These further exacerbated challenges caregivers faced in physical well-being.

Accessibility and Other Challenges to Recreation and Social Well-Being

Poor accessibility of recreational and social venues such as playgrounds and restaurants was a common issue faced by caregivers, as special arrangements and extra effort were required to bring the family out or arrange for relief care. These difficulties caused some parents to miss or intentionally avoid social and recreational events, which could potentially isolate caregivers socially. Though some caregivers reported spending less time with friends and extended family owing to the overwhelming burden of caregiving, others developed closer ties with extended family members who provided much needed physical and emotional support.

Sibling Involvement of Patients with SMA

Siblings of patients with SMA assumed supporting roles in their siblings’ day-to-day tasks by assisting the patient in various aspects of daily life. These children often become aware of and accept their siblings’ SMA condition from a young age.

Varied Perspectives on Positive Change

When asked about the presence of positive changes after their child’s diagnosis, some caregivers expressed pessimistic viewpoints and declined further discussion. On the contrary, other caregivers appreciated the meaning of life more and cherished time spent with family and child in view of the uncertain future. Positive changes identified due to their SMA caregiving experiences demonstrated the importance of familial support and the value of shared quality time.

Overall Economic Burden of SMA

Table 5 presents the overall economic burden per year considering societal perspective, healthcare system perspective, and patient perspective that recurs over time. One-time costs such as a wheelchair were excluded. Total costs were highest from the societal perspective (SGD 62,004.03), followed by the patient perspective (SGD 59,629.64), and substantially lower for the healthcare system perspective (SGD 10,840.13). This difference was driven by indirect (SGD 42,932.88) and direct nonmedical costs (SGD 8231.02), which are excluded from the healthcare system perspective. Indirect costs were the main driver of overall burden, and there is a substantial patient burden on private-sector direct medical costs.

Discussion

This study highlights the significant healthcare costs and broader societal implications associated with this rare disease by quantifying the economic burden of SMA from the societal perspective. With median annual household income in Singapore at SGD 135,564 in 2024, annual costs of managing SMA for the household before government subsidies, which amounts to SGD 19,071 excluding indirect cost, would encompass 17.0% of annual household income [18].

The results from our study align with published literature examining the costs of illness of SMA in other countries. According to a systematic review by Landfeldt et al., which included eight other developed countries in the USA and Europe, mean per-patient annual direct medical cost of SMA ranged from US $3320 for SMA type 3 in Italy to US $324,410 for SMA type 1 in the USA [5]. For direct medical cost of SMA in Asia, annual cost ranged from US $15,303 to US $97,320 based on a Hong Kong study by Chan et al. [19]. In comparison, the annual direct medical cost in Singapore (SGD 10,840.13; approximately US $8500) is substantially lower than estimates reported in Western countries and Hong Kong. This could partially be owing to the exclusion of one-time costs, such as wheelchairs.

Indirect costs of SMA in Australia, Germany, the UK, France, and Spain ranged from US $36,530 to US $12,830 in 2020 [6]. Unlike our study, which valued loss in productivity on the basis of loss in income due to absenteeism and income forgone using the human capital approach, other studies included loss in productivity due to presenteeism of paid and unpaid work, as well as informal care cost based on the proxy-good method.

Nevertheless, our findings and published studies agreed on the high economic burden of SMA. Additionally, in view of the copaying nature of healthcare financing in Singapore, our study explored patient’s perspective, healthcare system’s perspective, and societal perspective costs to understand financial burden of patients and the system. Government subsidies continue to play an important role in alleviating the financial burden on out-of-pocket payments in public healthcare sector [20]. However, expenses from unsubsidized private health services resulted in large payable bills for patients with SMA and their families as they turn to private healthcare to fill supply and demand gaps in specialized allied health services such as physiotherapy and occupational therapy [21, 22]. Nonrecurring items such as respiratory devices and mobility aids, although not factored into the annual cost of SMA, contribute significantly as a cumulative one-time expense of approximately SGD 14,289 and pose a considerable financial burden on patients as a big-ticket item [4]. Financial burden on patients and families is closely tied to the type and amount of healthcare services utilized, which varies according to SMA subtypes. Despite government subsidies, large increases in out-of-pocket costs are incurred when there is an inpatient admission, especially an ICU or HD admission. With patients with SMA type 1 being more vulnerable to lengthy admissions, emphasis is placed on targeted interventions to provide financial risk protection from catastrophic health expenditures during phases when patients require more intensive inpatient care [23]. Moreover, anecdotal observations from the Muscular Dystrophy Association Singapore (MDAS) indicate that a proportion of patients above 16 years of age within this support group are not under regular medical or hospital care owing to healthcare costs. These observations highlight potential financial barriers to ongoing care and reinforce the need for policies that strengthen financial protection and improve access to long-term care for individuals with SMA in Singapore.

Full-time working parents of citizens and PR are entitled to 6 and 2 days of paid childcare leave per year, respectively [24]. However, our survey approximated 11 missed days per working caregiver per year, which far exceeds the number of entitled childcare leave. This meant that parents had to sacrifice annual vacation leave to take care of their child or take unpaid leave, which would negatively impact their income. For caregivers with flexible and part-time working arrangements (in 21.4% of households), time off work to take care of a child directly reduced their income as there were fewer billable hours. Indeed, caregivers are exposed to financial difficulties due to reduced income or impaired performance and high costs of healthcare [6]. Local initiatives, such as the Tripartite Standards on Flexible Work Arrangements (FWA) and on Unpaid Leave for Unexpected Care Needs introduced in 2017 and 2018, encourage family friendly work environment to help parents manage work and caregiving responsibilities [25]. However, adoption of these recommendations is dependent on employers’ attitudes, and they are not mandatory or enforceable. Time off and FWA are strongly recommended to keep caregivers gainfully employed to better manage their health, caring, and work responsibilities [26].

Caregiving for patients with SMA is a time-consuming and heavy responsibility that incurs a profound intangible cost on physical health and social and mental well-being of caregivers [6, 27]. More than half of families engaged domestic helpers, and 28.5% of families rely on grandparents to share the heavy caregiving burden. Additionally, 64.2% of mothers with children with SMA exited the workforce or take up part-time work instead to care for their child, resulting in high caregiver unemployment rates, which is consistent across countries [6]. Social policies and support are pivotal to dealing with intangible cost of SMA on families affected. Social support and leisure activities are shown to reduce caregiver burden, and better respite care options may be useful in helping caregivers to cope [6, 27, 28]. Timely and routine mental health screening is also relevant to ensure adequate psychosocial support for caregivers, who are at risk of mental health issues [6, 27]. This is also one of the first studies to consider positive aspects of informal caregiving [6].

This study highlights the need for sustainable healthcare financing and targeted measures to reduce the financial burden borne by patients with SMA and their families. It is important to consider broader societal implications to develop effective interventions and comprehensive support systems. With recent approvals of SMA disease-modifying treatments locally, medication costs are projected to increase. Potential reduction in intangible effects and indirect and direct nonmedical costs, which are relevant to patients and their caregivers, should be included for a holistic valuation of new therapies. For example, in the USA and China, disease-modifying therapy has shown to improve family functioning, which was an intangible effect of SMA [29, 30].

Limitations

Indirect costs of adult patients with SMA, including any patients with SMA type 1–3 who survive into adulthood and those with SMA type 4 who are diagnosed as adults, were not included. These patients are often engaged in full-time or part-time employment with loss productivity due to worsening condition [31]. Direct medical cost incurred outside the public sector, direct nonmedical cost, and indirect cost were derived from a survey, which could be subject to recall bias. Certain costs are difficult to recall and to attribute precisely to the care of patients with SMA. For example, domestic helpers caring for patients may also perform other household tasks. Parents take leaves to care for children without SMA as well. A better estimate would be the incremental difference in leave taken for caring for children with SMA compared with caring for children without SMA.

Additionally, caregiver burden and emotional distress scores were similar for caregivers of pediatric patients with SMA and adult patients with SMA, suggesting that there exists high caregiving burden even in adult patients with SMA [7]. Given the rarity of the disease in our setting and the lack of population-level data, it was challenging to recruit a representative sample of adult patients with SMA and their caregivers. We were also not able to recruit patients with SMA who are out of the public health system. Anecdotal data from MDAS has shown that a significant proportion of patients over 16 years in this support group are not under medical or hospital care owing to healthcare costs.

Conclusions

SMA results in significant economic burden to patients, their families, and the healthcare system, despite current government subsidies. Findings from the study serve as a basis for ongoing discussions to shape healthcare policies and interventions for management of SMA. The insights gained foster a more compassionate and comprehensive approach to managing rare diseases with substantial economic burdens. Economic evaluations on SMA to support decision-making should be supplemented with a societal perspective to encompass all relevant costs and benefits to patients and the healthcare system.
