Public acceptance and attitudes toward procedures performed by medical students in Japan: a nationwide online survey
Authors: Eriko Okada, Nobutoshi Nawa, Shoko Yoshida, Yu Akaishi, Ayako Kashimada, Masanaga Yamawaki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundClinical clerkships are essential in medical education, but ethical and legal restrictions limit the invasive procedures performed by medical students in Japan. The 2023 revision of the Medical Practitioners’ Act permits students to conduct supervised procedures, such as venous blood collection and ultrasound examinations, after passing certain exams. This study investigates the public acceptance of these procedures, identifies barriers, and explores strategies to enhance medical student education patient–student collaboration.MethodsA cross-sectional internet-based survey was conducted in Japan from April 4–6, 2023, targeting 3,771 participants aged 18–79, stratified by sex and age. The attitudes and consent toward the medical procedures performed by students were assessed using a seven-point scale and bipolar adjective items. Demographic and socioeconomic factors were investigated as covariates. Open-ended responses were analyzed qualitatively. Linear regression with covariate adjustments was performed using Stata SE version 16.ResultsOut of 3,771 participants, 37.8% were from the Kanto region, 59.9% were married, 56.7% had children, and 44.7% had at least a college-level education. Women generally held more negative views toward the attitudes and consent process of medical students performing abdominal ultrasound examinations. In contrast, elderly individuals (70–79 years) and homemakers had a more positive perception regarding blood collection. The primary reason for consent was contributing to medical education (93.2%), whereas the key reasons for declining consent involved students’ skills (82.7%) and lack of trust (81.8%).ConclusionOlder individuals and homemakers were more receptive toward procedures performed by medical students, particularly invasive ones. Addressing concerns about technical competence, privacy, and anxiety through improved communication, transparency, and supervised training can enhance patient acceptance and trust in medical education.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-09460-9.

Background

Clinical clerkship is important in pre-graduate medical education to produce competent physicians. It provides an avenue to learn from patients and experienced physicians, promotes professionalism, introduces students to various professions, and prepares them for clinical training. Since 1998, the Association of American Medical Colleges (AAMC) has maintained that medical school graduates should be able to perform basic medical skills and techniques, which they aimed to achieve through the Medical School Objective Project [1]. However, some criticisms argue that involving real patients in medical training is inappropriate, and ethical issues have been raised regarding relying on patients for educational purposes [2, 3]. Additionally, there could be potential conflict between the rights of medical students to clinical practice and the rights of patients to refuse [4]. Unfortunately, some reports have shown that many medical students are not acquiring important skills [5, 6]. Simulation-based education can potentially address these problems and protect patients from any risks [7], but there is evidence that actively involving patients in the education of healthcare professionals benefits both patients and students alike [8, 9].

To balance patient safety with educational needs, medical regulatory bodies worldwide have established guidelines to legitimize and standardize student participation in clinical care. For instance, the General Medical Council (GMC) in the UK sets clear expectations for medical students, and the framework of Entrustable Professional Activities (EPAs) has gained global traction, particularly internationally, to assess student competence in performing medical procedures under supervision [10, 11].

In the context of these global trends, the legal framework in Japan has also evolved. Initially, the Medical Practitioners’ Act in Japan [12] prohibited medical practice by anyone other than physicians, with unclear guidelines regarding students performing medical procedures, and thus even basic medical procedures were not performed. In 1991, the Ministry of Health, Labour, and Welfare (MHLW) allowed medical students to practice medicine under supervising physicians, and this was formalized by the final report of the Committee for Investigation of Clinical Practice (i.e., the Maekawa Report) [13]. In 2018, the MHLW indicated medical procedures that students could perform under the supervision of senior physicians, but this did not spread in Japan. However, due to the international promotion of clinical training for medical students, as well as the demand for seamless practice between clinical training and residency training, the Medical Practitioners’ Act [12] was revised in 2023, allowing students to perform medical procedures other than prescribing drugs. Before performing these procedures, however, students must first pass the computer-based testing (CBT) and the pre-clinical clerkship obstructive structured clinical examination (pre-CC OSCE) of the Common Achievement Tests prior to clinical practice. These tests are conducted by The Common Achievement Tests Organization (CATO) [14].

Given this historical background, medical students in Japan have been delayed in terms of performing medical procedures, but after the 2023 revision of the Medical Practitioners’ Act [12], students were expected to participate in medical treatment, including procedures, as a member of the medical team. In a Japanese study conducted prior to this revision, about 25% of the study participants accept medical students performing procedures, while about 50% preferred the presence of a senior doctor to supervise [15]. Medical students who were diligent and kind were more favorably accepted as well [15]. However, the acceptance of more specific invasive medical practices, whether physically or psychologically invasive, remained unclear. According to the latest domestic survey [16], approximately 51% of medical students perform venous blood collection, a representative physically invasive procedure, and 51% have conducted abdominal ultrasound, a representative psychologically invasive procedure, at least once; these procedures are always carried out under the supervision of a mentor. Therefore, understanding the barriers that patients perceive when receiving these procedures is crucial.

This study conducted a survey regarding the acceptance of blood sampling and abdominal ultrasound procedures performed by medical students across a wide range of age groups in Japan to clarify the awareness barriers of patients undergoing these procedures, to examine demographic characteristics (e.g., age, sex, occupation) associated with their acceptance, and explore strategies to strengthen patients and medical students’ collaboration within medical education.

Methods

Study participants

This cross-sectional study was conducted through an internet-based survey (Supplementary Method 1) in Japan from April 4–April 6, 2023. The survey operation was outsourced to Macromill, Inc., a Japanese online survey company that maintains 1.3 million survey panel members and, together with partner panels, reaches a total of approximately 36 million survey panel members. From a panel of monitors in Japan, responses were collected from 3,771 participants, ensuring that the age- and sex-based distribution of this cohort mirrored that of the Japanese population. Regional adjustments were not applied during sampling; however, region was included as a covariate in the analyses. Those who declined participation were excluded from this study. Participants were grouped by age (18–29, 30–39, 40–49, 50–59, 60–69, and 70–79 years), and the number of participants for each group was adjusted to be similar to the population composition of Japan [17].

Exposure, outcome, and variable definitions

Attitudes toward basic medical procedures performed by medical students

First, the questionnaire included a statement discussing the prerequisites before medical students can perform procedures: passing a preclinical examination at the university hospital, obtaining informed consent from the patient, and the supervision and guidance of a teaching physician. Afterward, we evaluated attitudes of participants to undergo blood sampling or abdominal ultrasound examinations performed by medical students, assuming that they were admitted to a university hospital. Blood sampling and abdominal ultrasound were chosen for this survey because these invasive medical procedures, whether physically or psychologically invasive, are familiar and can easily be imagined by the public. Here, it should be noted that while abdominal ultrasound examinations do not cause physical pain, they were considered psychologically invasive procedures due to the exposure of the abdomen and potential embarrassment. Furthermore, the Essential Items Classification in the Model Core Curriculum [18] (i.e., the guidelines for medical education in Japan) includes venous blood collection and abdominal ultrasound as medical procedures that should be started during the clinical training of physicians. The questionnaire describes abdominal ultrasound as merely a basic examination performed by medical students under supervision. This survey does not make it clear that interpreting ultrasound findings and diagnostic elements are involved.

Participants’ attitudes were measured using an attitude scale that consists of seven-interval bipolar adjective items adapted from the studies of Norman C (2015) [19] and Burgoon (1978) [20]. The statement: “Your overall attitude toward having blood drawn or abdominal ultrasound examinations conducted by medical students is that it is…” was followed by six adjective pairs: negative/positive, wrong/right, unfavorable/favorable, unacceptable/acceptable, worried/not worried, and not important/important for education. The overall attitude score was calculated as the sum of the six items rated on a seven-point scale. For the statistical analysis, each item was re-coded to a 0–6 scale; thus, the total attitude score ranged from 0 to 36, where 0 indicates a strongly negative attitude and 36 indicates a strongly positive attitude.

Covariates

The online survey collected demographic variables, including age, sex, residential district, marital status, presence of children, highest educational background, annual household income, and occupation. Consent to medical basic procedures performed by medical students

The consent of participants to undergo blood sampling or abdominal ultrasound examinations conducted by medical students was evaluated through a seven-point scale, ranging from non-consent to consent. The 7-point Likert scale ranges from 0, indicating “strongly disagree”, to 6, indicating “strongly agree”, with 3 representing neutrality (originally measured as 1–7 and re-coded for analysis). Furthermore, we obtained responses on the reasons for consent and non-consent using multiple-choice options. The full text of the questionnaire items are available in the Supplementary Data 1. The survey system allowed participants to select only one response per item. Incomplete responses were automatically excluded by the survey system, and only complete responses without missing values were included in the final dataset.

Statistical analysis

The association of demographic characteristics with attitude or consent was evaluated via linear regression analysis. Covariate adjustments were made for age, sex, residential district, marital status, presence of children, highest educational background, annual household income, and occupation. All statistical analyses were conducted using the Stata SE statistical package, version 16 (StataCorp. 2019. College Station, TX: StataCorp LLC).

Qualitative analysis

The open-ended responses to the reasons for declining consent were subject to a thematic content analysis, which was conducted as described previously [21, 22]. Briefly, all responses were read, then thematic codes were applied to the responses. A clarification that two authors (EO and NN) independently coded the qualitative responses, with discrepancies resolved through discussion until consensus was achieved. All thematic codes were reviewed and organized into several higher level themes. Finally, the higher level theme was assigned to each response, which could be associated with one or more themes. Qualitative analysis focused on identifying barriers and concerns, hence it was limited to responses declining consent and reasons for consent were sufficiently addressed in the quantitative results. The qualitative questions were displayed only to respondents who selected agreement or disagreement (scores 0–2 or 4–6). Neutral respondents (score 3) were not asked these additional items (originally measured as 1–7 and re-coded for analysis).

Results

Table 1 shows the characteristics of the study participants. The total number of participants was 3,771. Among the different prefectures, the Kanto region, which includes Tokyo, had the most respondents (n = 1,424 [37.8%]). More than half were married (n = 2,260 [59.9%]) and had children (n = 2,140 [56.7%]). Most had a college-level education or higher (n = 1685 [44.7%]) and an annual household income of 2–6 million yen (1492 respondents, 39.6%). In terms of occupation, 2116 (56.1%) were employed.

In comparison with national benchmarks, the respondents in our sample tended to have slightly higher levels of educational attainment and household income. According to the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare, the median annual household income in 2022 was 5.2 million yen [23], whereas 39.6% of our respondents reported an annual household income of 2–6 million yen and 21.2% reported 6–10 million yen, indicating a mild upward shift in income distribution. Regarding educational attainment, national data from Statistics Bureau, Ministry of Internal Affairs and Communications show that in 2022, 20.4% of adults aged 20–64 had completed a university degree or higher [24]. In contrast, 44.7% of our respondents reported having a college-level education or higher (Table 1). Although our sample broadly reflects the demographic structure of Japan in terms of age and sex, these discrepancies suggest that participants in our online survey may be more likely to have higher socioeconomic status than the general population.

Table 2 presents the multivariable analysis of consent for abdominal ultrasound examinations performed by medical students. Women were significantly less likely to consent, whereas older participants (70–79 years), married individuals, those with children, and respondents who were not working were more likely to provide consent. These findings remained significant in the adjusted models.

Discussion

This study examined the attitudes and acceptance of the general public in Japan across different demographics toward clinical procedures performed by medical students. This survey focused on abdominal ultrasound (a psychologically invasive and painless procedure) and venous blood collection (a physically invasive and painful procedure), because these are the most basic medical procedures which are serve as the starting point for students’ participation in medical care. For abdominal ultrasound, a consistent trend was observed in terms of attitude and consent, with a lower level of acceptance among women. Previous studies have also reported that female patients exhibit lower tolerance towards invasive procedures performed by medical students. This tendency is influenced by psychosocial factors such as concerns about bodily exposure, feelings of embarrassment, and vulnerability during medical procedures [25, 26]. To address this issue, past literature indicates that having the examination performed under the supervision of a senior doctor or a healthcare professional of the same sex, as well as providing a sufficient explanation before the examination, can reduce discomfort and increase acceptance [27].

In addition to age and sex, race, ethnicity, and religious background may also influence patients’ acceptance of psychologically invasive procedures performed by medical students. White participants or patients have been reported to show more favorable attitudes toward medical students’ involvement, whereas non-White participants or patients may be less accepting [28–30]. Religious background may also shape attitudes toward psychologically invasive procedures; Muslim women, in particular, may be more likely to refuse examinations by male medical students, especially for gynecological or abdominal conditions [31], while individuals without religious affiliation may be more willing to consent [32]. These findings indicate that the lower acceptance of abdominal ultrasound among women in our study may be related not only to sex but also to broader cultural and religious norms concerning modesty, bodily exposure, and gender concordance.

However, our survey did not collect information on race, ethnicity, nationality, or religious affiliation, and we were therefore unable to directly examine these factors. In Japan, foreign residents accounted for 3.04% of the total population at the end of 2024, and the Muslim population has been estimated at approximately 350,000 in 2023/2024 [33, 34]. Thus, the overall influence of these factors may have been limited in the present nationwide sample. Nevertheless, as foreign residents and patients with diverse religious backgrounds continue to increase in Japan, future studies and medical education programs should address cultural and religious considerations in patient consent for medical students’ clinical participation.

In contrast, the findings regarding venous blood collection differed from the ultrasound results. Unlike the gender-based difference seen in ultrasound, there was a significant trend related to age, wherein participants aged 18–29 years and 70–79 years were more likely to express unfavorable and favorable attitudes, respectively, compared to the reference group of 30–39 years old. These higher levels of consent among the elderly may indicate their trust and familiarity with medical interventions. It has previously been reported that elderly patients have positive attitudes about their involvement in medical student education, considering it necessary for education [35]. This finding also aligns with a report by Rockey et al. [36] indicating that, compared to younger cohorts, elderly patients were more likely to agree that they gained learning from participating in medical education.

Regarding occupation, homemakers had more positive attitudes and expressed greater consent more than other workers. Homemakers showed higher acceptance and more favorable attitudes toward procedures performed by medical students; however, the reasons for this association remain unclear based on the available data.

In this survey, around 17% (n = 642) leaned toward not consenting to both procedures, these respondents had concerns about the ability of students to correctly perform the techniques, while 78.4% emphasized the need for formal confirmation of students’ abilities prior to clinical practice. Others expressed a feeling of being used as “test subjects” in the educational setting, as well as the possibility of suffering personal disadvantages. On this point, Santen et al. note that when adequate consideration and explanation are lacking, patients may feel they are being treated not as subjects of care but as ‘practice material’ for educational purposes [37]. This suggests that the concern about being ‘guinea pigs’ observed in this survey represents a universal challenge within medical education.

In the free-response section, the respondents cited anxiety as the main reason for their concerns, while others highlighted problems regarding the procedures becoming time-consuming and inefficient. A minority of respondents (11.4%) cited a general distrust of medicine, while 8.6% specifically expressed disagreement with the involvement of medical students due to their lack of experience. A lack of patient care, invasion of privacy, general anxiety, and a widespread distrust of medicine were also serious concerns.

Kondo et al. [38] showed that “perceived ability” and “value similarity” are important for the acceptance of medical students in clinical practice. Patients were also more willing to undergo blood collection done by medical students if they were provided information about the medical students’ educational program and certifications [38]. These findings suggest that acceptance is influenced not only by technical skills but also by how patients understand and trust the medical students. Furthermore, patient-centered care and effective communication have been shown to enhance learners’ benefits [39], and systematic reviews have revealed that patients’ trust in healthcare hinges on sincerity, communication, and competence [40]. These findings indicate that comprehensive training programs, including the development of communication skills and empathy, contribute to both fostering patient trust and improving patients’ receptivity toward medical students. Another key aspect is building patient trust through supervised practice under senior doctors. Patients’ trust is grounded in physicians’ competence in providing medical care [40] and that, as previously reported, medical students are required to practice under the supervision of senior physicians [15], it is important that informed consent for clinical participation be obtained in close collaboration with senior physicians.

From these points, medical education should therefore aim to improve not only students’ technical skills but also their ethical sensitivity. Enhancing these capabilities enables students to address patient anxiety with transparency and to respond with empathy and sincerity. Indeed, previous research has shown that education in ethics and professionalism positively impacts medical students’ attitudes and behaviors [41, 42]. This is particularly important considering that students are expected to actively participate during their clinical clerkship.

Based on these findings, future research should include intervention studies evaluating the effectiveness of faculty-development programs on the role of senior physicians in medical students’ clinical training. Specifically, future work should assess whether training supervisors in transparency and communication strategies effectively reduces patient anxiety about medical students’ participation in clinical care and further enhances trust in clinical education for medical students.

Limitations

Our research had some limitations. First, the survey targets were ordinary healthy citizens. Hospitalized patients could have different opinions regarding consent and acceptance. Second, due to reflecting the population distribution patterns within Japanese online survey panels, sample sizes at the prefectural level are small, analytical limitations exist, and sampling bias towards participants residing in the Kanto region may have occurred. Third, although the sample was adjusted by age and sex, the use of an Internet survey panel may still have introduced selection bias. Fourth, because the survey did not explore setting-specific attitudes, we cannot determine how acceptance may differ across clinical environments. Fifth, neutral respondents (score 3 on the 0–6 scale (re-coded from the original 1–7)) did not receive follow-up qualitative questions, which prevented us from exploring the reasons underlying neutral attitudes. Sixth, this survey did not collect information on race, ethnicity, nationality, or religious affiliation. Therefore, although previous studies suggest that these factors may influence patients’ acceptance of psychologically invasive examinations by medical students, we were unable to assess their effects in the present study.

Conclusions

Our findings indicate that older individuals and homemakers were generally more receptive to medical students, whereas younger individuals and women held less favorable opinions, particularly regarding invasive procedures. Primary concerns centered on technical competence and anxiety. To address these issues and enhance patient trust, we recommend implementing rigorous simulation-based pre-clinical assessments to ensure technical proficiency. Furthermore, medical curricula should emphasize communication training on patients-centered care and to foster a partnership with patients. By integrating these practical measures with supervised training and the cultivation of ethical sensitivity, medical education can balance patient safety with the effective professional development of future physicians.
