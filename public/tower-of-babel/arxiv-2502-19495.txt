Rates of Strongly Lensed Tidal Disruption Events
E. Mamuzic, T. Ryu, S. H. Suyu, K. Szekerczes, S. Huber, L. Dai, M. Oguri

Abstract
In the coming years, surveys such as the Rubin Observatory’s lsst ( lsst ) are expected to increase the number of observed tde substantially. We employ Monte Carlo integration to calculate the unlensed and lensed tde rate as a function of limiting magnitude in u u , g g , r r , and i i -bands. We investigate the impact of multiple luminosity models, bhmf , and flare temperatures on the tde rate. Notably, this includes a semi-analytical model, which enables the determination of the tde temperature in terms of bh ( bh ) mass. We predict the highest unlensed tde rate to be in g g -band. It ranges from 16 16 to 5,440 ​ yr − 1 ​ ( 20,000 ​ deg 2 ) − 1 5,440\;\mathrm{yr}^{-1}\;(20,000\;\mathrm{deg}^{2})^{-1} for the ztf ( ztf ), being more consistent with the observed rate at the low end. For lsst , we expect a rate in g g -band between 3,580 3,580 and 82,060 ​ yr − 1 ​ ( 20,000 ​ deg 2 ) − 1 82,060\;\mathrm{yr}^{-1}\;(20,000\;\mathrm{deg}^{2})^{-1} . A higher theoretical prediction is understandable, as we do not consider observational effects such as completeness. The unlensed and lensed tde rates are insensitive to the redshift evolution of the bhmf , even for lsst limiting magnitudes. The best band for detecting lensed tde is also g g -band. Its predicted rates range from 0.43 0.43 to 15 ​ yr − 1 ​ ( 20,000 ​ deg 2 ) − 1 15\;\mathrm{yr}^{-1}\;(20,000\;\mathrm{deg}^{2})^{-1} for lsst . The scatter of predicted rates reduces when we consider the fraction of lensed tde ; that is, a few in ten thousand tde will be lensed. Despite the large scatter in the rates of lensed tde , our comprehensive considerations of multiple models suggest that lensed tde will occur in the 10 10 -year lsst lifetime, providing an exciting prospect for detecting such events. We expect the median redshift of a lensed tde to be between 1.5 1.5 and 2 2 . In this paper, we additionally report on lensed tde properties, such as the bh mass and time delays.

Introduction

When a star ventures too close to a
bh
(
bh
), tidal forces tear it apart. We can observe such an event as a bright transient, a so-called
tde

(
Hills 1988
;
Rees 1988
,
tde
;)
. These events typically last one to two years for main sequence stars
(
Gezari 2021
, e.g.,)
, but in rare cases, they can extend much longer than that
(
Lin et al. 2017
)
.
By analyzing
tde
, we can infer critical properties such as the mass of the
bh
and the disrupted stars
(
Ryu et al. 2020a
, e.g.,)
, as well as gain insights into
bh
demographics and accretion physics
(
Rees 1988
;
Gezari 2013
, e.g.,)
.
Understanding TDE rates and their observational characteristics is vital for detecting them in current and future surveys such as the
ztf

(
Wozniak et al. 2014
,
ztf
;)
and the Rubin Observatory’s
lsst

(
Ivezić et al. 2019
,
lsst
;)
. Estimating the rate of
tde
across different survey depths allows for better planning of observational campaigns and the design of strategies to maximize scientific returns
(
Bricman & Gomboc 2020
;
Lin et al. 2022
, e.g.,)
.

A key advancement in this study is the inclusion of lensed
tde
in our analysis. Gravitational lensing provides a powerful mechanism to magnify and detect
tde
from deep within the cosmos. Beyond enhancing detectability, lensing offers the potential to probe cosmological parameters such as the Hubble constant
(
Refsdal 1964
)
and the size of the emission region via microlensing effects, as demonstrated for active galactic nuclei
(
Kochanek 2004
, e.g.,)
. Despite these promising applications, the abundance and characteristics of lensed
tde
remain uncertain. Addressing this gap, we calculate the lensed
tde
rate for various models and assess whether these events are numerous enough to serve as a tool for future research.

In this paper, we employ Monte Carlo simulations to calculate
tde
rates as a function of limiting magnitude, following the methodology of
Szekerczes et al. 2024
. Likewise, we generate mock catalogs to analyze the population of unlensed and lensed
tde
. Estimating the
tde
rate requires two primary components: a
tde
luminosity model and the
bhmf
(
bhmf
). Building upon the calculations of
Szekerczes et al. 2024
, in addition to the phenomenological fit model
(
Thomsen et al. 2022
)
, we incorporate the full semi-analytical stream-stream collision model for
tde

(
Piran et al. 2015
;
Ryu et al. 2020a
)
. This model enables the determination of the
tde
temperature as a function of
bh
mass, eliminating the need to assume a temperature. In addition to the local
bhmf
adopted in
Szekerczes et al. 2024
, we include two more
bhmf
that account for redshift dependence, enhancing the accuracy of our predictions. For lensed
tde
, we also investigate the fraction of
tde
that are lensed for the different models, leading to a more robust forecast.

The structure of the paper is as follows: In Sect.
2
, we provide an overview of the relevant theoretical background, focusing on the
tde
models used to calculate the
tde
magnitude. We also detail our methodology for determining the unlensed
tde
rate and extending it to create a mock
tde
catalog. Sect.
3
presents the results for the unlensed case, analyzing the effects of various model parameters and comparing them with current observations. In Sect.
4
, we shift our focus to the lensed rate, describing the creation of mock catalogs of lensed
tde
. We then explore the lensed
tde
rate, the fraction of lensed events, and the parameter distributions for the source
tde
, the lens, and the lens system. Finally, we summarize our findings and conclusions in Sect.
5
.

2.2.1
Phenomenological Luminosity Model

The first model is an empirical fit between the observed TDE luminosity and the debris fallback rate found by

L=0.01\cdot\dot{M}_{\mathrm{fb}}\cdot c^{2}

(10)

The second model we consider is by

2.3
Solving the Rate Integral

The rate integral in Eq.

3.3
Impact of Temperature

The assumed temperature contributes the most to the spread of the models; see Fig.
8
. The temperature strongly impacts the redshift distribution of the observed

bb

10,000\;\mathrm{K}
; hence, models with this temperature show the highest rates at bright limiting magnitudes
\lesssim 22

The
bb
peak is always shifted into a given observing band at some redshift. Therefore, when a faint limiting magnitude
\gtrsim 26
is assumed, all bands should roughly observe the same number of

as every

can be observed in every band. This is what we observe in Fig.

The odd one out is the

or

-band. For this temperature, the peak of the

spectrum lies at

at

(see Eq.

, contributing to this model’s shallower slope. Hence, it takes longer to catch up to the models.

4
Lensed TDE Rate

4.1
Calculating the Lensed Rates

The lensed TDE rate is calculated with the code developed by
Oguri & Marshall 2010
. Some optimizations have been introduced to the code to speed up calculation times significantly. For details, refer to Appendix
C
.
For the calculations, the code from
Oguri & Marshall 2010
assumes that all lens galaxies are singular isothermal ellipsoids with convergence

\kappa=\frac{\theta_{\mathrm{E}}}{2}\cdot\frac{\lambda(e)}{\sqrt{(1-e)^{-1}\cdot x^{2}+(1-e)\cdot y^{2}}},

(19)

where
\lambda(e)
is the dynamical normalization,
e
is the ellipticity, and

\theta_{\mathrm{E}}=4\pi\cdot\left(\frac{v}{c}\right)^{2}\cdot\frac{D_{\mathrm{ds}}}{D_{\mathrm{s}}}

(20)

the Einstein radius, dependent on the velocity dispersion
v
of the lens galaxy, the angular diameter distance from the observer to the source
D_{\mathrm{s}}
, and from the lens to the source
D_{\mathrm{ds}}
. These equations for the singular isothermal ellipsoid were derived by
Kormann et al. 1994
.
The code calculates a lensed
tde
mock catalog by taking in an unlensed mock catalog and simulating all possible lens galaxies as elliptical galaxies, which make up
\sim 80\%
of the lensing probability
(
Turner et al. 1984
;
Fukugita et al. 1992
;
Kochanek 1996
;
Chae 2003
;
Oguri 2006
;
Möller et al. 2007
)
. Hence, every lens can be described by its redshift and velocity dispersion in a two-dimensional parameter space. The same technique for generating a mock catalog of unlensed
tde
s can now be used to create a catalog of possible lens galaxies.
By estimating an area on the sky larger than any possible lens system, they can calculate a rate of finding a
tde
in the vicinity of a lens galaxy, assuming both are spread uniformly on the survey area. This assumption is equivalent to the isotropy of the universe
(
Goodman 1995
;
Clarkson & Maartens 2010
;
Kumar Aluri et al. 2023
;
Bengaly et al. 2024
, e.g.,)
. By sampling a Poisson distribution with that rate estimate, they can find the number of candidate systems. The lens equation is then solved for these systems to determine whether it is an actual lens or just a close separation with no multiple images. All systems that produce multiple images are then saved to a mock catalog.
A
tde
lensed into a double is considered observable when both images are brighter than the limiting magnitude. The third brightest image must be brighter than the magnitude limit for a quad lensed
tde
. Calculating rates from such a mock catalog is as simple as counting the entries brighter than the magnitude limit. But, to calculate rates smaller than
1\;\mathrm{yr}^{-1}\;(20,000\;\mathrm{deg}^{2})^{-1}
, we need to oversample. We achieve this by calculating
N
catalogs and, in the end, dividing the summed number of systems by
N
. Thus, every entry in a mock catalog only counts as
1/N
towards the rate. This mock catalog counting suffers from Poisson noise, so the error scales with
1/\sqrt{N}
.

4.2
Lensed Rate Results

Following
Szekerczes et al. 2024
, we only select lensed events with an image separation in the range
[0.5^{\prime\prime},4^{\prime\prime}]
to ensure that the multiple images can be resolved by ground-based surveys such as
lsst
. For a quad, we measure the maximal distance between two images. Here, we include all images of a quad. In a cusp configuration, the fourth image typically sets the largest image separation, but it is also demagnified. However, deeper imaging can reveal it, and we consider the system resolvable. We only consider the lensed rate for
lsst
limiting magnitudes. We don’t examine
ztf
limits here because many models predict a smaller rate than our numerical limit of
10^{-3}\;\mathrm{yr}^{-1}\;(20,000\;\mathrm{deg}^{2})^{-1}
, with an oversampling factor of
1000
.

tde

\sim 50
between the lowest and highest fractions. We predict the fraction of lensed to unlensed
tde
to be between
1.7\cdot 10^{-5}
(

, TRINITY,

,

8.7\cdot 10^{-4}
(

-indep.,

,

,

has to observe thousands to tens of thousands of unlensed
tde
.

The lensed
tde
population includes many
tde
at a higher redshift than found in the unlensed population. Thus, a larger lensed fraction implies that the fading of
tde
is more strongly counteracted either due to an increasing
bhmf
with redshift or due to a better
bb

tde
at low and high redshifts. This becomes further clear when comparing the first row of Fig.
11
to the first row of Fig.
B.1
. The lensed
tde
have a median redshift between
2
and
3
, while the unlensed
tde
have a median redshift between
0.2
and
0.7
.

To better understand Fig.
10
, we consider the observing bands individually. In
u
-band, we observe the smallest lensed fractions. Here, we know that a temperature of
10,000\;\mathrm{K}
aligns the
bb
peak well with
u
-band at low redshifts. But this alignment is broken at larger redshifts, and only a few
tde
are observed. Thus, we find a small lensed fraction.
The fraction increases with temperature because the low redshift alignment of the
bb
peak gets worse, while the alignment gets better for a larger redshift. We expect most lensed
tde
to be around
z=1
; see the first row of Fig.
11
. At this redshift, a temperature between
20,000\;\mathrm{K}
and
30,000\;\mathrm{K}
is best aligned with
u
-band. Hence, we do not expect the increasing trend of the lensed fraction to continue much beyond this temperature.
The temperature-dependent models can compensate for
bb
peak alignment and reach higher temperatures and redshifts. Therefore, they observe many
tde
at low redshift. But with the possibility of very high temperatures, they produce many observable
tde
in
u
-band at high redshift. Thus, temperature-dependent models yield the highest fraction in
u
-band.

The same trend of the lensed fraction is seen in
g
-band; see the upper right panel of Fig.
10
. But the rising of the fraction continues up to
50,000\;\mathrm{K}
. This is due to the possibility of observing lensed
tde
up to
z\lesssim 4
in
g
-band, while unlensed ones fade from view at

.

In
r
and
i
-band, the peak of the lensed fraction moves to lower temperatures because these temperatures perform best in the respective bands at high redshifts. It is worth noting that in
i
-band at
50,000\;\mathrm{K}
, we find the lowest lensed fraction, despite the fact that
i
-band observes very few unlensed
tde
at this temperature. But
i
-band lies too far away from the

tde
from view long before the

-band is not observing many lensed

either, indicating a small lensed fraction.

The different

increases the fraction compared to the TRINITY

for low mass

bhmf
amplifies the influence of the temperature the most. This makes sense as a decreasing

with redshift always leads to a decrease in

tde

5
Conclusion

In this paper, we have numerically calculated the predicted unlensed and lensed
tde
rate as a function of limiting magnitude in four bands. We covered the ongoing
ztf
and the future
lsst
surveys and different model parameters such as the
bhmf
,
tde
luminosity model, and assumed flare temperature.
For the unlensed rates, our models predict between
16
and
5,440\;\mathrm{yr}^{-1}\;(20,000\;\mathrm{deg}^{2})^{-1}
for
ztf
in
g
-band. For
lsst
, the rate is in the range from
3,580
to
82,060\;\mathrm{yr}^{-1}\;(20,000\;\mathrm{deg}^{2})^{-1}
. In both surveys,
g
-band stands out as the best option to observe
tde
because it has the faintest limiting magnitude.
The sizable scattering factor of
\mathcal{O}(10^{2})
up to
\sim 10^{3}
that the models display can be attributed to the assumed
tde
temperature, making it the most critical assumption for
tde
rate estimates.
Despite the uncertain predictions, we demonstrate that only the local
bhmf
is relevant for current and near-future surveys. Our redshift-dependent
bhmf
only showed a significant deviation from the redshift-independent model at a limiting magnitude of
25
or fainter. As
lsst
will only reach about magnitude
24
, the redshift evolution of the
bhmf
is not important to
lsst
.
Compared to the observational unlensed
tde
rate around
10
to
15\;\mathrm{yr}^{-1}\;(20,000\;\mathrm{deg}^{2})^{-1}
obtained from
ztf
, most of our models overestimate the rate. It should be expected that we overestimate the observed rate, as we have not accounted for completeness or any observational effects in our calculations. For a more accurate estimate in the future, we could include the host galaxy light or dust extinction to better match observations. Furthermore, removing the assumption that every star is solar-like would yield even more accurate results. Last, certain physical processes in TDEs can also make the detected TDE rates signifcantly deviate from the theoretical rates
(
Wong et al. 2022
)
.

From our investigation into the lensed fraction, we can approximate that for every
10,000
unlensed
tde
, we expect
lsst
to find a few lensed events. The results show a reduced scatter to around a factor of
50
. As our unlensed rates only poorly match observational ones, it seems improbable that a large sample of hundreds of lensed
tde
can be obtained. Yet,
lsst
will observe a few lensed
tde
over its
10
-year survey time.
Lensed
tde
are also best observed in
g
-band due to its faint limiting magnitude. For
lsst
, our predictions range from one every two years to more than ten yearly, assuming
20,000\;\mathrm{deg}^{2}
in
g
-band, still showing much of the scatter also observed for the unlensed rate. However, analogously to the unlensed rate, we were able to show that the
tde
temperature is the most important assumption and only the
bhmf
is relevant even beyond
lsst
limiting magnitudes.

Appendix A
Magnitude Integral

To calculate the flux integral in the numerator of Eq.
6
, we will first introduce the variables
a
and
b
to simplify calculations. The flux now takes the form

F_{\lambda_{\mathrm{obs}},\mathrm{o}}=\frac{a}{\lambda_{\mathrm{obs}}^{5}}\cdot\frac{1}{e^{\frac{b}{\lambda_{\mathrm{obs}}}}-1}.

Compare to Eq.
7
for the expressions of
a
and
b
. The integral can now be computed as follows:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\lambda\;\lambda\cdot S_{\mathrm{X}}\cdot F_{\lambda,\mathrm{o}}

\displaystyle=\int_{\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}}^{\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}}\mathrm{d}\lambda\;\lambda\cdot\frac{a}{\lambda^{5}}\cdot\frac{1}{e^{\frac{b}{\lambda}}-1}=

\displaystyle=a\int_{\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}}^{\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}}\mathrm{d}\lambda\;\frac{1}{\lambda^{4}}\cdot\frac{1}{e^{\frac{b}{\lambda}-1}}=

\displaystyle\left[\mathrm{Subs.}\;\frac{b}{\lambda}=x\right]\;\;\;

\displaystyle=a\int_{\frac{b}{\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}}}^{\frac{b}{\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}}}\mathrm{d}x\;\left(-\frac{b}{x^{2}}\right)\cdot\left(\frac{b}{x}\right)^{-4}\cdot\frac{1}{e^{x}-1}=

\displaystyle=-\frac{a}{b^{3}}\int_{\frac{b}{\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}}}^{\frac{b}{\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}}}\mathrm{d}x\;\frac{x^{2}}{e^{x}-1}.

To solve this integral we will first solve

\int\mathrm{d}x\;\frac{1}{e^{x}-1},

by substituting
u=1-e^{-x}
. From which follows:

\int\frac{\mathrm{d}u}{1-u}\cdot\frac{1}{\frac{1}{1-u}-1}=\int\frac{\mathrm{d}u}{1-u}\cdot\frac{1-u}{u}=\int\mathrm{d}u\;\frac{1}{u}=\ln(u).

By re-substituting, we find:

\int\mathrm{d}x\;\frac{1}{e^{x}-1}=\ln(1-e^{-x}).

We can now solve the flux integral by applying partial integration (
\mathrm{PI}
) twice

\displaystyle\int\mathrm{d}x\;\frac{x^{2}}{e^{x}-1}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{PI}}}{{=}}\left[x^{2}\cdot\ln(1-e^{-x})\right]-\int\mathrm{d}x\;2x\ln(1-e^{-x})=

\displaystyle=-x^{2}\cdot\mathrm{Li}_{1}(e^{-x})+2\int\mathrm{d}x\;x\mathrm{Li}_{1}(e^{-x})=

\displaystyle\left[\mathrm{Subs.}\;u=e^{-x}\right]\;\;\;

\displaystyle=-x^{2}\cdot\mathrm{Li}_{1}(e^{-x})+2\int\mathrm{d}u\;\frac{\ln(u)}{u}\mathrm{Li}_{1}(u)=

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{PI}}}{{=}}-x^{2}\cdot\mathrm{Li}_{1}(e^{-x})+2\left[\ln(u)\cdot\mathrm{Li}_{2}(u)\right]

\displaystyle\quad-2\int\mathrm{d}u\;\frac{1}{u}\mathrm{Li}_{2}(u)=

\displaystyle=-x^{2}\cdot\mathrm{Li}_{1}(e^{-x})+2\ln(u)\cdot\mathrm{Li}_{2}(u)

\displaystyle\quad-2\left[\mathrm{Li}_{3}(u)\right]=

\displaystyle\left[\mathrm{Re-subs.}\right]\;\;\;

\displaystyle=-x^{2}\cdot\mathrm{Li}_{1}(e^{-x})-2x\cdot\mathrm{Li}_{2}(e^{-x})-2\mathrm{Li}_{3}(e^{-x}),

where
\mathrm{Li}_{n}(x)=\int_{0}^{x}\mathrm{d}t\;\mathrm{Li}_{n-1}(t)\,/t
is the polylogarithm, with
\mathrm{Li}_{1}(x)=-\ln(1-x)
. The final result can now be expressed as

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\lambda\;\lambda\cdot S_{\mathrm{X}}\cdot F_{\lambda,\mathrm{o}}=

\displaystyle=\frac{a}{b^{3}}\left[\left(\frac{b}{\lambda}\right)^{2}\cdot\mathrm{Li}_{1}\left(e^{-\frac{b}{\lambda}}\right)+2\frac{b}{\lambda}\cdot\mathrm{Li}_{2}\left(e^{-\frac{b}{\lambda}}\right)+2\mathrm{Li}_{3}\left(e^{-\frac{b}{\lambda}}\right)\right]^{\frac{b}{\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}}}_{\frac{b}{\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}}}.

Appendix C
Implementation

In the original implementation from
Oguri & Marshall 2010
, all possible lens galaxies are described by two parameters, the redshift and velocity dispersion. Hence the code calculates through a two dimensional grid. At each grid point, the number of galaxies is calculated, and for every lens galaxy, the number of possible
tde
is drawn from a Poisson distribution. As an example, when considering
20,000\;\mathrm{deg}^{2}
on the sky, we calculate above
358
billion galaxies up to a redshift of
2
. Drawing
1
billion samples from a Poisson distribution takes on the order of one minute, which means a lot of time is spent on this portion of the code.

As all lens galaxies are considered the same at a given grid point, we only need to calculate the total number of possible
tde
for
N
galaxies. Here we can use the fact that the sum of two independent Poisson distributed variables is also Poisson distributed. Assume
X\sim\mathrm{Poisson}(\lambda)
and
Y\sim\mathrm{Poisson}(\mu)
then

\displaystyle P(X+Y=k)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{k}P(X+Y=k,X=i)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{k}P(Y=k-i,X=i)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{k}P(Y=k-i)\cdot P(X=i)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{k}e^{-\mu}\frac{\mu^{k-i}}{(k-i)!}\cdot e^{-\lambda}\frac{\lambda^{i}}{i!}

\displaystyle=e^{-(\mu+\lambda)}\cdot\sum_{i=0}^{k}\frac{\mu^{k-i}}{(k-i)!}\cdot\frac{\lambda^{i}}{i!}

\displaystyle=e^{-(\mu+\lambda)}\cdot\sum_{i=0}^{k}\frac{1}{k!}\frac{k!}{(k-i)!\cdot i!}\cdot\mu^{k-i}\cdot\lambda^{i}

\displaystyle=e^{-(\mu+\lambda)}\cdot\frac{1}{k!}\cdot\sum_{i=0}^{k}\binom{k}{i}\cdot\mu^{k-i}\cdot\lambda^{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{(\mu+\lambda)^{k}}{k!}\cdot e^{-(\mu+\lambda)}.

Hence
X+Y\sim\mathrm{Poisson}(\lambda+\mu)
. Therefore, we do not need to draw
N
times from a Poisson distribution but can draw from a Poisson distribution with
N\cdot\lambda
as its expectation, significantly speeding up calculations.

Other optimizations have been made by removing the need to instantiate large data arrays not needed for this application of the code, yielding significant performance improvement without changing the code’s fundamental logic. These speed increases are needed as the rate of lensed
tde
is close to or even below unity. This creates the need to over-sample the detection area by up to a factor of
1,000
to find a few samples at faint magnitudes and bring the Monte Carlo error down.
