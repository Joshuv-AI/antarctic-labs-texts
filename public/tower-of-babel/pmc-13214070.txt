Conservative management of accidental tracheal perforation during surgery for descending necrotizing mediastinitis: a case report and literature review
Authors: Hui Zhou, Tieniu Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSurvival of tracheal perforation secondary to descending necrotizing mediastinitis (DNM) is very rare, and it is difficult to manage accidental tracheal perforation during DNM surgery. We present a case of a 54-year-old man who had a large tracheal perforation due to DNM, which could not be confirmed preoperatively. A conservative treatment strategy was adopted during surgery to ensure adequate chest drainage. Ultimately, the fistula healed spontaneously, eliminating the need for tracheostomy or additional surgery. Finally, we proposed some suggestions for managing accidental tracheal perforation in this study. Moreover, physicians in primary health care institutions should also increase their awareness of DNM.Keywords: Descending necrotizing mediastinitis, Tracheal perforation, Surgery

Introduction

Descending necrotizing mediastinitis (DNM) is a potentially lethal condition, and survival of tracheal perforation caused by DNM is very rare [1]. Treatment can be conservative, surgical or endoscopic, depending on the condition and status of the patient. We present a case in which an accidental tracheal perforation secondary to DNM during surgery was successfully treated.

A 54-year-old man complained of a sustained toothache and received a broad-spectrum antibiotic treatment at a township healthcare facility. Three days after onset, the patient presented with a sore throat, accompanied by neck swelling and a high fever, leading to their referral to a district hospital. Laboratory findings revealed a white blood cell count of 18.69 × 109/L and a C-reactive protein level of 22.84 mg/dL. The infection was treated with a combination of ornidazole, levofloxacin, and ceftizoxime. However, the aforementioned symptoms progressively aggravated, and he presented with persistent cough and expectoration of white viscous phlegm. Eight days after onset, the patient was referred to our institute. During admission, there was a redness and palpable painful swelling on his neck. A chest computed tomography (CT) scan revealed a gas-collection abscess extending from the neck into the mediastinum. Additional findings included multiple pulmonary infiltrates, a small pericardial effusion, and bilateral pleural effusions (Fig. 1a). Therefore, surgical intervention was immediately performed. Cervico-mediastinal drainage was performed through a neck collar incision combined with right thoracoscopic surgery. In the thoracic cavity, the necrotic tissue in the retrosternal and pretracheal space was completely removed. The mediastinal pleura was also widely incised to perform debridement and pus drainage. When we attempted to open the paratracheal space above the azygos arch, a few gas bubbles emerged from the pus. We subsequently irrigated the thoracic cavity with saline solution, but intermittent bubbles still emerged in the irrigation solution with the delivery of air from the ventilator. The anaesthesiologist attempted to examine the trachea using a bronchoscope; however, the status of tracheal rupture could not be accurately determined because of the double-lumen endotracheal tube. Considering the stable lung ventilation and minimal bubble leakage, we opted for a conservative treatment strategy and preserved the mediastinal pleura surrounding the tracheal fistula. Moreover, two irrigation tubes and a drainage tube were retained during the surgery to ensure adequate drainage (Fig. 1b). One irrigation tube was placed in the anterior mediastinum (retrosternal space), while the other followed the course of the azygos vein and passed beneath the umbilical vein arch. The drainage tube was placed in the pleural cavity along the lateral chest wall. The patient was transferred to the intensive care unit (ICU) with a double-lumen tube after surgery.

CT image showing a gas-forming infection in the mediastinum (a); three chest tubes (thick arrows denote irrigation tubes; thin arrows denote drainage tubes) are inserted during surgery (b); the irrigation tube in the anterior mediastinum and the drainage tube was removed (c)

While on mechanical ventilatory support (V-SIMV mode with a PS of 10 cm H2O, a PEEP of 5 cm H2O and a tidal volume of 6 mL/kg), the patient received inotropic agents, sedatives, and analgesia. We administered a combination of metronidazole and imipenem for anti-infective therapy, along with mediastinopleural irrigation.

Three chest tubes were connected to a triple-chamber drainage bottle without the application of negative-pressure suction. Each irrigation tube underwent once-daily irrigation with 250 mL of saline. Neck wound dressings were changed on the basis of the amount of exudate.

The patient’s overall condition improved gradually after surgery. The white blood cell count was 7.62 × 109/L, and the C-reactive protein concentration was 7.89 mg/dL on the first day after surgery. Both parameters subsequently remained within the normal range. The patient’s haemodynamic parameters tended to stabilize, and inotropic drugs were discontinued on the third day after surgery. With the patient’s respiratory status remaining stable, the endotracheal tube was assessed and replaced with a single-lumen tube to facilitate airway management on the fourth day after surgery. Moreover, bronchoscopy was performed and confirmed a 4-cm-diameter perforation in the right lateral wall of the mid-trachea (Fig. 2a). The patient was successfully weaned from mechanical ventilation 6 days after surgery and transferred to the general ward. When the drainage fluid became clear and follow-up CT scans indicated significant improvement in the thoracic and mediastinal infections, the irrigation tube in the anterior mediastinum was removed (7 days after surgery), and the drainage tube was removed 15 days after surgery (Fig. 1c).

After more than one month of rehabilitation, no air bubbles leaked from the last irrigation tube. A CT scan revealed that the abscess in the pleural and mediastinal spaces was mostly absorbed. Bronchoscopy revealed limited mucosal hyperplasia at the perforation site without any stenosis (Fig. 2b). The other irrigation tube was then removed, and the patient was discharged 46 days after surgery.

Discussion

The mortality rate of DNM has decreased significantly with the evolution of diagnostic and treatment methods [2, 3]. Early and aggressive surgical treatment with drainage is paramount [4]. However, tracheal or bronchial perforation following DNM remains a life-threatening complication [5, 6]. The treatment can be conservative, surgical or endoscopic, depending on the size and location of the perforation and the ventilatory efficiency [1, 6–8].

In existing reports, tracheal perforations are mostly identified postoperatively, enabling a comprehensive assessment of perforation size, surrounding tissue conditions, and the patient status. The majority of cases have been managed conservatively [1, 7]. However, re-surgical intervention is also required to rescue patient under certain conditions [6]. Therefore, the management of accidental tracheal perforation during surgery poses increasing challenges and raises the critical problem of whether a thorough evaluation is necessary. If we perform radical surgical debridement of the mediastinum and explore the perforation completely, it might be difficult to maintain lung ventilation during surgery. Moreover, the repair of tracheal perforation under acute infection poses an immense challenge and has a notably low success rate [9, 10]. In contrast, tracheal perforation also poses extremely great challenges for the management of postoperative patients when adopting a conservative strategy. On the one hand, inadequate lung ventilation poses an imminent threat to the patient’s life; on the other hand, the patient’s physical condition may not be amenable to further surgical surgery. Murakawa T and colleagues reported a case of life-threatening tracheal perforation that occurred after DNM surgery. The patient underwent a second surgery to repair the perforation with veno-venous extracorporeal membranous oxygenation (ECMO) support [6]. However, the tracheal ulcer that failed to heal by postoperative Day 9 and progressed to perforation with massive air leakage and life-threatening ventilation failure might be associated with the radical surgical debridement performed during the initial surgery.

In this case, adequate lung ventilation could be maintained for the patient during surgery. We preserved the mediastinal pleura surrounding the azygos arch, which limited perforation exploration. In addition, three chest tubes (two irrigation tubes and a drainage tube) were used to ensure sufficient drainage effectiveness, and mediastinopleural irrigation was performed after surgery. Fortunately, the patient was discharged home and did not require a tracheostomy or a second surgery.

Conclusion

In our opinion, adequate debridement around the perforation and drainage remain the fundamental components of conservative treatment, which could prevent ongoing infection of the trachea and enlargement of the fistula [2, 3]. Meanwhile, maintenance of lung ventilation is crucial for determining whether tracheal repair should be performed during surgery.

With ageing of the population, the incidence rates of diabetes and oropharyngeal and odontogenic infections have gradually increased, which to a certain extent has increased the occurrence of DNM. The initial symptoms of DNM lack specificity, yet early recognition of DNM is crucial for developing therapeutic strategies and ensuring effective communication with patients [2]. Therefore, more effort at smaller district hospitals should be focused on improving physicians’ awareness of DNM.
