miR-652-5p promoter hypermethylation enhances glioblastoma malignancy through the SDC1/TGFβ2/pERBB4 complex via HIF2α-mediated regulation under hypoxia
Authors: Pan Wang, Sheng Gong, Bin Liao, Jie Liu, Lu Zhao, Hui Yao, Nan Wu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGlioblastoma multiforme (GBM) is highly malignant, and hypoxia often exacerbates its aggressiveness. The aim of this study was to explore the molecular mechanism by which miR-652-5p promoter hypermethylation augments the malignancy of glioblastoma under hypoxic conditions.MethodsThis study combined data from public glioma databases, clinical tissue analysis, and cellular assays. Techniques such as RT‒qPCR, western blotting, miRNA sequencing, and ELISA were used to assess the impact of miR-652-5p under hypoxic conditions. A BALB/c-nu mouse model injected with GBM cells was used for in vivo evaluation.ResultsmiR-652-5p was markedly upregulated in HIF2α-knockout cells, with promoter hypermethylation linked to reduced methylation post-knockout. Inhibition of miR-652-5p upregulated SDC1, SDC1-Intra, and TGFβ2 expression. Live-cell imaging and fluorescence staining revealed the role of SDC1 in exosome-mediated TGFβ2 secretion. SDC1 knockdown reduced exosome release and TGFβ2 secretion and confirmed the binding of TGFβ2 to SDC1/sSDC1 via HS chains and to ERBB4 on recipient cells. Downregulation of SDC1 and TGFβ2 expression decreased the levels of PI3K, PDK1, p-PDK1, AKT, p-AKT, and mTOR, weakened tumorigenic properties, and extended OS. In vivo results from a nude mouse model supported these findings.ConclusionsUnder hypoxic conditions, miR-652-5p promoter hypermethylation promotes GBM malignancy via the SDC1/TGFβ2/pERBB4 axis in a HIF2α-dependent manner. Understanding this mechanism may lead to the development of epigenetic treatments and personalized medical approaches to improve patient outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12935-025-04122-0.

Background

Glioblastoma (GBM) is the most common malignancy of brain tumours, with a median survival time of 14.6 months for afflicted patients [1, 2]. The aetiology of GBM remains largely elusive, with potential contributing factors, including both genetic predispositions and environmental exposure to radiation and certain chemicals [3]. GBM is characterized by rapid proliferation and extensive infiltration, which complicates surgical resection and often results in significant mortality [4]. Despite the implementation of conventional treatments such as surgery, radiation therapy, and chemotherapy, the prognosis for GBM patients remains poor, with a five-year survival rate of less than 5% [4, 5]. The limitations of current therapeutic strategies are evident, with surgery often constrained by the tumour location and invasiveness, radiation therapy potentially causing damage to adjacent healthy tissues, and chemotherapy being impeded by drug resistance and the blood‒brain barrier, which restricts the penetration of chemotherapeutic agents to the tumour site [6, 7].

Previous studies have shown that IDH1 mutation and MGMT promoter methylation strongly affect GBM malignancy. In addition, hypoxia-inducible factors (HIFs) have emerged as pivotal regulators of a tumour’s adaptive response to its microenvironment. Hypoxia inducible factor-1 alpha (HIF1α) has been extensively studied for its role in the proliferation and invasive behaviour of GBM cells [8–10]. HIF1α orchestrates a variety of cellular responses to hypoxia, including angiogenesis, metabolism, and cell survival, thereby contributing to tumour malignant progression [8, 9]. However, recent advancements in our understanding of the HIF family have revealed the importance of hypoxia inducible factor-2 alpha (HIF2α) in GBM. Our recent studies have demonstrated that genetic ablation of HIF2α results in the upregulation of HIF1α expression [11–14] and that both HIF1α and HIF2α regulate the chemotherapy resistance of GBM [15]. These findings suggest a complex interplay between the two HIF isoforms, with HIF2α potentially playing a more substantial role in the malignant progression of GBM than previously appreciated. Despite the growing body of evidence implicating HIF2α in GBM pathogenesis, the detailed regulatory mechanisms of HIF2α in GBM remain to be fully elucidated. Unravelling the specific contributions of HIF2α to GBM biology may offer novel therapeutic targets and strategies to combat this devastating disease.

MicroRNAs (miRNAs), a class of small noncoding RNAs ranging from 18 to 25 nucleotides in length, are pivotal regulators of gene expression, orchestrating either the degradation or translational inhibition of target mRNAs. Various miRNAs, such as miR-210-3p [11], miR-125a-3p [16] and miR-93 [17], have been implicated in the malignant progression of GBM. Previous studies have confirmed that miR-652-5p is downregulated in oesophageal squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) due to miR-652-5p promoter hypermethylation and that downregulation of miR-652-5p promotes OSCC cell proliferation and metastasis [18]. However, little is known about the influence of miR-652-5p on GBM, and the potential regulation of miR-652-5p promoter hypermethylation by HIF2α merits investigation.

In the present study, we utilized miRNA sequencing to identify the significant upregulation of syndecan-1 (SDC1) in the context of miR-652-5p promoter hypermethylation, further elucidating its role in the pathophysiology of GBM. SDC1, a transmembrane protein adorned with heparan sulphate (HS) and chondroitin sulphate (CS) chains, is involved in the pathophysiology of GBM [19]. The purpose of this study was to clarify how miR-652-5p promoter hypermethylation increases glioblastoma multiforme (GBM) malignancy under hypoxia. These findings offer new therapeutic targets and strategies for the clinical treatment of GBM.

Materials and methods

Public data collection

The Chinese Glioma Genome Atlas (CGGA) and The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) databases were used to analyse HIF2α, SDC1, TGFβ2, ERBB4, and DNMT1 expression in GBM. The relationships between these proteins and OS and DFS were analysed by the Gene Expression Profiling Interactive Analysis (GEPIA) web server on the basis of data from the TCGA database. Correlation analysis among HIF2α, SDC1, TGFβ2, ERBB4, and DNMT1 was performed on the basis of data from the CGGA database.

Patients and specimens

Forty glioma tissues were obtained during surgery, and the pathological grade (WHO II, WHO III and WHO IV) was verified after surgery; the details are shown in Supplementary Table S17. These tissues were analysed for mRNA and protein expression using RT‒qPCR and western blotting, respectively.

Primary GBM cell isolation and cell culture

U87, U118 and primary GBM cells were used in this article, and the detailed steps of the primary GBM cell sorting are shown in the supplementary materials and methods. The cells were cultured in DMEM (Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% foetal bovine serum (FBS) in 21% O2 or 1% O2 for the assays.

miRNA-seq analysis and identification of miR-652-5p

HIF2α-knockout and control cells were subjected to miRNA-seq analysis [11], with raw data deposited in the NCBI Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database under accession number GSE142719 (www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo). Differentially expressed miRNAs were identified and analysed accordingly.

Detection of miR-652-5p promoter methylation

DNMT1, DNMT3A, and DNMT3B expression was measured in control, empty vector, and HIF2α-knockout cells under hypoxic conditions by western blotting. The methylation status of the miR-652-5p promoter was determined using a methylation-specific PCR (MSP) kit and a bisulphite conversion kit from an undisclosed manufacturer, followed by agarose gel electrophoresis. Methylation levels were further quantified using a methylated DNA immunoprecipitation (MeDIP) kit (Epigentek, USA) and RT‒qPCR for HIF2α-knockout and DNMT1-knockout cells. The primers used for the detection of miR-652-5p promoter methylation by MeDIP are shown in Supplementary Table S9, and the detailed steps are presented in the supplementary materials and methods.

Western blot analyses and RT-qPCR

The protein levels of HIF2α, SDC1, TGFβ2, MMP9, ERBB4, and pERBB4 in glioma tissues and GBM cells under varying oxygen conditions were measured by western blotting. The expression levels of SDC1 and TGFβ2 were assessed in miRNA-652-5p mimic and inhibitor cells using RT‒qPCR, with subsequent western blot analysis for various proteins as described. The sequences of primers used for RT‒qPCR analysis are shown in Supplementary Table S6, and the related information regarding the primary antibodies is presented in Supplementary Table S10. The detailed steps used for RT‒qPCR and western blotting are presented in the supplementary materials and methods.

Immunofluorescence and multiplex Immunofluorescence staining

GBM cells were cultured in 1% O2 for 72 h to evaluate the expression of HIF2α, and the detailed steps followed for immunofluorescence staining are described in the supplementary materials and methods. Two samples from GBM patients were used for multiplex immunofluorescence staining. The samples were obtained during surgery, placed in 4% paraformaldehyde, sent to Panovue (Beijing, China) and used for the measurement of HIF2α, SDC1, TGFβ2, ERBB4 and CD63 expression. The related information regarding primary antibodies is presented in Supplementary Table S11. Multiplex immunofluorescence staining of patient-derived GBM samples was performed to measure the expression of HIF2α, SDC1, TGFβ2, ERBB4, and CD63, and the quantitative data are presented in Supplementary Table S5.

Binding sequences between SDC1 and TGFβ2 and miR-652-5p

The bioinformatics tool TargetScan (https://www.targetscan.org/vert_72/) was used to predict the binding sites of miR-652-5p within SDC1 and TGFβ2. Luciferase reporter vectors were used to verify the interaction between miR-652-5p and the predicted binding sites.

ELISA

ELISA was used to measure sSDC1 and TGFβ2 levels in the culture medium of miR-652-5p mimic and inhibitor cells, as well as in exosomes derived from knockdown cells. The detailed steps of exosome extraction and purification are presented in the supplementary materials and methods. A human TGFβ2 ELISA kit (Invitrogen, USA) and an SDC1 ELISA kit (BBI, Shanghai) were used to quantify expression using an ELISA plate reader (Varioskan Flash, Thermo Scientific, USA) at 450 nm.

BIFC assay

In this test, fusions with an N-terminal mVenus (VN173) tag and a C-terminal mCerulean (CC155) tag were used for BIFC. The pBIFC-VN173 and pBIFC-CC155 plasmids were purchased from Youbio Biology (Hunan, China). The detailed sequences inserted into the plasmid are presented in Supplementary Table S12, and the detailed steps are presented in the supplementary materials and methods.

Live cell imaging assay

U87 cells carrying pHluorin-CD63 and SDC1-RFP or TGFB2-RFP were seeded at a 1:1 ratio with U87 cells in a 24-well plate. After the cells had adhered to the plate, real-time fluorescence imaging was performed using a live cell workstation (Zeiss LSM880) under conditions of 37 °C, 5% CO2, and stable humidity. Images were acquired every 15 min, and the process was repeated continuously for 12 h.

Generation of SDC1-kd, TGFβ2-kd, simultaneous SDC1/TGFβ2-kd, DNMT1-ko cells and HIF2α-ko cells

Lentiviral shRNA and sgRNA constructs were used to generate SDC1-knockdown, TGFβ2-knockdown, DNMT1-knockout and HIF2α-knockout GBM cells, with subsequent selection and analysis of targeted gene expression. The detailed steps followed for lentiviral transduction were similar to those described in our previously published article [11], and a simplified description if provided in the supplementary materials and methods. The sequences of the lentiviral shRNA and sgRNA oligonucleotides described above are listed in Supplementary Tables S14 and S15. The cells described above were cultured in DMEM + 10% FBS to maintain growth.

Paracrine signalling assay

GBM cells (1 × 105 or 2 × 105) expressing the TGFβ2-RFP fusion protein were seeded in the upper chamber (Group A) of 0.4 μm cell chamber inserts, and GBM cells (2 × 105) were seeded in the lower chamber (Group B). After 24–48 h of coculture, the amount of RFP in the lower chamber (Group B) was quantified by flow cytometry in the PE channel.

Migration and invasion assays

Invasion and migration assays were performed in 24-well plates containing transwell chambers under hypoxic conditions. Negative control, SDC1-knockdown, TGFβ2-knockdown and SDC1/TGFβ2-knockdown U87 cells (1 × 105) in serum-free medium were plated in the upper chambers, which contained a membrane with (invasion assay) or without (migration assay) matrigel (40 µl). Afterwards, 600 µL of high-glucose DMEM + 10% FBS was added to the lower chambers. After being incubated for 48 h at 37 °C, the cells were fixed with methanol, stained with 0.1% crystal violet solution for 15 min, and counted under a microscope. The numbers of cells counted in 5 random fields under a microscope were averaged. For the recovery experiment, U87-NC, SDC1-OE (overexpression), TGFβ2-OE and SDC1/TGFβ2-OE cells were plated in the lower chambers, and these cells were cultured under hypoxic conditions for 48 h to obtain the same experimental results described above.

CCK-8 assay

A CCK-8 assay was used to examine cell proliferation. Negative control, SDC1-knockdown, TGFβ2-knockdown and SDC1/TGFβ2-knockdown GBM cells in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS were plated in 96-well plates (2,000 cells/well) and cultured in 1% O2 in the presence of temozolomide (TMZ) (0 µM, 200 µM, 400 µM and 800 µM). Cell growth was evaluated on days 1 and 2. Ten microlitres of CCK-8 reagent and 90 µl of DMEM + 10% FBS were mixed, the suspension was added to each well, and the cells were cultured for an additional 2 h. An ELISA plate reader (Thermo Scientific, USA) was used to measure the absorbance at 450 nm.

Flow cytometry (FCM)

FCM was used to analyse the cell cycle of negative control, SDC1-knockdown, TGFβ2-knockdown and SDC1/TGFβ2-knockdown cells cultured under hypoxia for 48 h. The detailed steps of cell cycle detection were performed according to a previous study [12].

In vivo experiments

BALB/c-nu mice (4 ~ 6-week-old males) were used in this study. Negative control, SDC1-knockdown, TGFβ2-knockdown, and both SDC1/TGFβ2-knockdown and DNMT1-knockout cells (8 × 104) were implanted into the brains of 25 mice, and the mice were fed in an atmosphere of 10% O2. Live imaging was used to determine the tumour volume in randomly selected mice on day 21. Tumour tissues were collected from other mice, and PDK1, p-PDK1, AKT, p-AKT, mTOR and PCNA protein levels were measured using western blotting. The remaining fifteen mice were used for survival analysis.

Statistical analysis

SPSS 22.0 was used for statistical analysis. Data are shown as the means ± standard deviations (SDs). The significance of differences between two groups was analysed using a paired samples t test, and one-way ANOVA was used for comparisons among three groups. The log-rank test was used to analyse the significance of differences in survival time. The Pearson correlation coefficient was used to analyse correlations between genes. P < 0.05 was considered to indicate a statistically significant difference.

Results

MiRNA sequencing (miRNA-seq) analysis of HIF2α-ko GBM cells

Our miRNA sequencing (miRNA-seq) analysis of HIF2α-knockout (ko) GBM cells, corroborated by the CGGA and TCGA databases, revealed elevated HIF2α expression in GBM, particularly under hypoxic conditions (Fig. 1A, B; Supplementary Fig. 1A-C and Table S16). This upregulation associated with a significant increase in miR-652-5p expression exhibited the greatest log2-fold change in expression after HIF2α knockout, highlighting a negative regulatory relationship (Fig. 1C-D, Supplementary Fig. 1D and Table S1). KEGG analysis revealed the most significant proteoglycans in the cancer pathway, with SDC1, TGFβ2, and ERBB4 identified as potential miR-652-5p targets (Fig. 1E-F, Supplementary Table S2-D, S3 and S4). As a key signal transduction component in tumour cells is pERBB4 [20], we evaluated pERBB4 expression rather than ERBB4 expression. We subsequently used the CGGA database to explore the correlations among HIF2α, SDC1, and TGFβ2 expression and reported that the expression of both SDC1 and TGFβ2 was positively correlated with that of HIF2α (Fig. 1G), suggesting that HIF2α a played pivotal role in modulating the expression of miR-652-5p and its target genes in GBM. HIF2α enhanced GBM malignancy through miR-652-5p promoter hypermethylation

RNA expression analysis of 40 glioma tissues (Supplementary Table S17) revealed lower miRNA-652-5p levels in GBM (WHO Grade IV) than in low-grade glioma (Fig. 2A), with an inverse correlation with HIF2α expression (Fig. 2B). Compared with that under normoxia, miRNA-652-5p expression was lower in hypoxia and increased significantly after HIF2α knockout (Fig. 2C). GO analysis revealed enrichment of methylation-related terms (Supplementary Fig. 2A-C and Table S2), with DNMT1 identified as a key regulator according to a Venn diagram of the above gene sets (Supplementary Fig. 2D and Table S2) and the CGGA database (Fig. 2D). High DNMT1 expression was negatively correlated with overall survival (OS) and disease-free survival (DFS) (Fig. 2E), and in vivo studies verified that mice in which DNMT1-knockout cells were implanted had lower tumour volumes and longer survival times (Supplementary Fig. 3A-B). In addition, DNMT1 expression was positively correlated with HIF2α expression in the 40 glioma tissue samples (Fig. 2G). Western blot analysis revealed that after HIF2α knockout, DNMT1 expression decreased, but DNMT3A and DNMT3B expression did not change (Fig. 2F). Treatment with both HIF2α-ko and the demethylating agent 5-aza-CdR led to decreased DNMT1 expression and increased miR-652-5p levels (Fig. 2H). RNA expression analysis confirmed that miR-652-5p expression in GBM cells increased after DNMT1 knockout (Fig. 2I and Supplementary Fig. 3D). MSP and MeDIP assays confirmed the demethylation effect on the miR-652-5p promoter under these conditions (Fig. 2J, K and Supplementary Fig. 2E and 3 C), underscoring the epigenetic regulation of miR-652-5p in GBM. Through in vivo study, the results showed there were higher volume of tumours and shorter survival period of mice after intracranial injection of miR-652-5p-inhibitor cells, and the volume of tumours after intracranial injection of miR-652-5p-mimic cells decreased and with longer survival period of mice compared with that in the control (Supplementary Fig. 6A-C).

Hypermethylation of the miR-652-5p promoter increased SDC1, sSDC1, TGFβ2 and pERBB4 protein levels

CGGA database and RNA detection in 40 glioma tissues revealed that SDC1, TGFβ2 and ERBB4 were expressed in glioma tissues and that SDC1 and TGFβ2 expression was higher in GBM than in LGG, but ERBB4 expression was lower in GBM than in LGG (Fig. 3A and Supplementary Fig. 4 A). Multiplex immunofluorescence staining revealed that GBM tissues expressed SDC1, TGFβ2 and ERBB4 (Fig. 3C), and the results of western blotting confirmed that the expression of SDC1, TGFβ2 and pERBB4 was higher in GBM than in LGG (Supplementary Fig. 4 C). The data from the TCGA database suggested that both SDC1 and TGFβ2 were negatively correlated but that ERBB4 was positively correlated with OS and DFS (Fig. 3B). Analysis of mRNA expression in 40 glioma tissues revealed that the expression of SDC1 and TGFβ2 was negatively correlated with that of miRNA-652-5p, but the expression of ERBB4 and miR-652-5p was positively correlated (Supplementary Fig. 4B). RT‒qPCR analysis revealed that the expression of SDC1 and TGFβ2 was higher in GBM cells cultured under hypoxia than in cells cultured under normoxia; moreover, compared with the corresponding vector control cells, the expression of SDC1 and TGFβ2 was higher in miRNA-652-5p inhibitor cells cultured under normoxia, while it was lower in miRNA-652-5p mimic cells cultured under hypoxia (Fig. 3D and Supplementary Fig. 4E). Western blot analysis revealed that under hypoxia, SDC1-Intra, SDC1, TGFβ2 and MMP-9 expression increased, ERBB4 expression decreased, and pERBB4 levels increased significantly compared with control (Fig. 3E and Supplementary Fig. 5). In addition, the SDC1-Intra, SDC1, TGFβ2, MMP-9 and pERBB4 protein levels increased but the ERBB4 protein level decreased after miR-652-5p inhibition under normoxic conditions, and the opposite changes were detected in miR-652-5p mimic cells cultured under hypoxic conditions (Fig. 3E and Supplementary Fig. 5). The same results were shown via vivo study, and the tumour tissues of intracranial injection of miR-652-5p-inhibitor cells had higher TGFβ2, SDC1-intra, SDC1, p-ERBB4 and MMP-9 compared with the control group. Compared with control mice, mice that were intracranially injected with miR-652-5p-mimic cells presented lower expression of TGFβ2, SDC1-intra, SDC1, p-ERBB4, and MMP-9 (Supplementary Fig. 6D). ELISA revealed that the culture medium of GBM cells in the hypoxia group contained higher levels of sSDC1 and TGFβ2 than that of cells in the normoxia group. Additionally, compared with the corresponding vector control cells, the miRNA-652-5p inhibitor cells in the normoxia group had higher expression of sSDC1 and TGFβ2, while the culture medium of the miRNA-652-5p mimic cells in the hypoxia group had lower levels of sSDC1 and TGFβ2 (Fig. 3F and Supplementary Fig. 4 F). Supplementary Fig. 5 illustrates the in vivo effects of miR-652-5p modulation on tumour volume and mouse survival, as well as the expression levels of TGFβ2, SDC1-Intra, SDC1, p-ERBB4, and MMP-9, demonstrating a significant increase in tumour growth and a decrease in survival with miR-652-5p inhibition; the opposite effects were observed with miR-652-5p overexpression. Bioinformatics analysis was used to identify the binding sites of miRNA-652-5p in the 3′-UTRs of SDC1 and TGFβ2. The predicted binding sequences of miRNA-652-5p were 5′-AGGGUU-3′ in SDC1 and 5′-UAGGGUU-3′ in TGFβ2 (Supplementary Fig. 4D). The luciferase activity of SDC1 and TGFβ2 decreased after overexpression of miR-652-5p and increased in miR-652-5p inhibitor cells (Fig. 3G and Supplementary Fig. 4G). The predicted binding sequences in SDC1 and TGFβ2 were subsequently mutated, and the luciferase activity of SDC1 and TGFβ2 in GBM vector-transfected cells decreased compared with that in cells without binding sequence mutations. There was no difference in luciferase activity between vector- and miR-652-5p mimic-transfected GBM cells after mutation of the binding sequences (Fig. 3H and Supplementary Fig. 4H).

TGFβ2 was secreted in exosomes via a mechanism regulated by SDC1

GO analysis (Supplementary Fig. 2B and Table S2A-C), TEM and NTA provided evidence for exosome-mediated TGFβ2 transfer (Supplementary Fig. 7A-B). Coexpression of CD63, TGFβ2, and SDC1 in GBM tissues was observed (Fig. 4A and Supplementary Table S5). In GBM cells, CD63 was labelled with pHluorin and TGFβ2 and SDC1 were individually labelled with RFP, after which live-cell imaging was applied to monitor changes in protein expression over the next 12 h. Some cells expressed both SDC1 and CD63, and most cells expressed both TGFβ2 and CD63 in the intracellular space. The TGFβ2/sSDC1/pERBB4 complex promotes GBM malignancy

GO analysis revealed that many molecular functions involved in the PI3K/AKT-mTOR pathway, such as phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase activity, phosphatidylinositol-4,5-bisphosphate 3-kinase activity, and transmembrane receptor protein tyrosine kinase activity, were activated (Supplementary Fig. 2 C and Supplementary Table S2-C). We measured the levels of key proteins in this pathway following SDC1 or TGFβ2 knockdown and detected significant decreases in the protein levels of PI3K, PDK1, p-PDK1, AKT, p-AKT, and mTOR (Fig. 6A), as well as a decreases in the level of pERBB4 (Supplementary Fig. 8 A). GO analysis revealed that the biological process of the cell cycle was activated, as were cellular components involved in invasion and migration, including focal adhesion, cell–substrate junctions and cell–substrate adherens junctions (Supplementary Fig. 2A-B). Consistent with the results of the GO analysis, the results of the cell cycle analysis revealed that compared with the other groups, the groups with TGFβ2 knockdown, SDC1 knockdown, and simultaneous SDC1/TGFβ2 knockdown exhibited G1 arrest, with the simultaneous knockdown group exhibiting a notably greater rate of G1 arrest (Fig. 6B and Supplementary Fig. 8B). Western blot analysis also revealed a decrease in PCNA expression after SDC1 or TGFβ2 knockdown, suggesting that cell proliferation was inhibited (Supplementary Fig. 8 A). The results of the CCK-8 assay demonstrated that cells with SDC1 knockdown, TGFβ2 knockdown, and simultaneous SDC1/TGFβ2 knockdown exhibited reduced proliferation rates, with the most significant difference observed between control cells and those with SDC1 knockdown or simultaneous SDC1/TGFβ2 knockdown (Fig. 6C and Supplementary Fig. 8 C). In addition, the expression of N-cadherin, which is important for cell adhesion, decreased following the knockdown of SDC1 or TGFβ2 (Supplementary Fig. 8 A). Migration and invasion were inhibited after the knockdown of SDC1 or TGFβ2, and cells with simultaneous SDC1/TGFβ2 knockdown exhibited the lowest migration and invasion (Fig. 6D). Recovery experiments in which SDC1 and TGFβ2 levels in the medium were increased resulted in enhanced migration and invasion for all cell types, with the simultaneous increase yielding the most significant effect (Fig. 6E). In vivo assays in a nude mouse model mirrored these findings, with reduced tumour growth and increased overall survival in the knockdown groups (Fig. 6F-I). Western blot analysis confirmed the downregulation of the expression of PI3K/AKT-mTOR pathway proteins, indicating that the TGFβ2/sSDC1/pERBB4 complex promoted GBM malignancy (Fig. 6J). Discussion

Our findings underscore the role of HIF2α in GBM malignancy, a hypoxic microenvironment-driven condition in which HIF1α has been traditionally implicated [8, 21]. Our results demonstrate that HIF2α-mediated hypermethylation of the miR-652-5p promoter leads to the upregulation of SDC1 and TGFβ2, which in turn promote GBM malignancy through the formation of a complex with pERBB4. Targeting this regulatory axis may offer novel therapeutic strategies for GBM.

Historically, miR-652-5p has been implicated in the regulation of various cellular processes in tumours, with its role varying across different studies [18, 22]. Some studies have suggested that miR-652-5p may act as a tumour-promoting factor, targeting genes that promote cell proliferation and invasion, which are key characteristics of tumour malignancy [22, 23]. However, its expression levels and function are inconsistent, with some studies reporting a downregulation of miR-652-5p in certain cancers, which is associated with a more aggressive phenotype and poor prognosis [18, 24]. This discrepancy highlights the complexity of miRNA regulation and function in cancer biology.

Owing to the hypermethylation of the upstream promoter of miR-652-5p in OSCC, the expression of miR-652-5p is downregulated, leading to increased malignant potential in OSCC [18]. In the context of our study, we were interested in the finding that miR-652-5p was also downregulated in GBM and that the upstream promoter of miR-652-5p was also hypermethylated; therefore, we sought to determine whether the same phenomenon occurred in GBM. These findings underscore the unique regulatory mechanisms at play in GBM and the potential for miR-652-5p to serve as a critical node in the response to hypoxic stress.

The hypermethylation of the miR-652-5p promoter, which was regulated by DNMT1 in our study (Fig. 7 and Supplementary Fig. 4D), introduces an additional layer of epigenetic control that has not been extensively explored in GBM. This mechanism may contribute to GBM progression through the downregulation of miR-652-5p, which in turn affects the expression of genes involved in tumour growth and invasion. Epigenetic alterations, such as DNA methylation, play crucial roles in cancer development and progression through the silencing of tumour suppressor genes or the activation of oncogenes [25, 26]. In the context of GBM, the hypermethylation of specific gene promoters is associated with poor prognosis and resistance to treatment [27–29]. This epigenetic regulation may represent a novel avenue for therapeutic intervention, as it could be targeted to modulate the expression of miR-652-5p and its downstream effects on GBM malignancy. While our study builds upon the established foundation of the role of miR-652-5p in GBM, it introduces innovative concepts that could reshape our approach to GBM therapy. By highlighting the distinct regulatory mechanisms and clinical potential of miR-652-5p hypermethylation, our research provides new insights for both basic science investigations and translational medicine in the fight against GBM.

In addition, SDC1 and TGFβ2, which are direct targets of miR-652-5p, have been implicated in various cancers [30–32]. In glioma, SDC1 is highly expressed, which leads to advanced tumour progression and is considered an independent factor for poor prognosis [33, 34]. TGFβ2, another gene of interest in our study, is also upregulated in glioma and has been shown to contribute to tumour initiation and malignant progression in previous studies [35, 36]. In our study, we first analysed the expression of SDC1 and TGFβ2 and reported that both SDC1 and TGFβ2 were highly expressed in GBM. The target sequences of miR-652-5p in SDC1 and TGFβ2 were subsequently verified to be 5′-AGGGUU-3′ and 5′-UAGGGUU-3′, respectively, through a dual luciferase reporter assay, and miR-652-5p-mediated inhibition of SDC1 and TGFβ2 expression was verified by western blot analysis (Fig. 7 and Supplementary Fig. 4D). Additionally, whether miR-652-5p regulates the shedding of SDC1 and the secretion of TGFβ2 is important, as the secretion of sSDC1 and TGFβ2 promotes GBM proliferation and invasion [33, 35]. Therefore, we used ELISA and found that the medium of the miRNA-652-5p mimic cells had lower levels of sSDC1 because of the decreased level of MMP-9, as verified by western blot analysis (Figs. 3 and 7). In GBM, the upregulation of these proteins resulting from the suppression of miR-652-5p expression indicated a pivotal role in disease progression. The interplay between SDC1, its soluble form sSDC1, and TGFβ2, in the context of exosome-mediated communication, presents a nuanced mechanism of GBM cell behaviour regulation [37–40]. Previous studies have suggested that TGFβ2 is secreted through exosomes [37] and that the production and transfer of exosomes are regulated by SDC1 [19]. SDC1 induces the production of exosomes through the interaction of its cytoplasmic domain with syntenin and Alix [38], and upon shedding, these exosomes transport sSDC1 and other contents (e.g., growth factors) to recipient cells, thus leading to continuous regulation through autocrine or paracrine signalling [39, 40]. Therefore, multiplex immunofluorescence staining and a live-cell imaging system were used to verify this phenomenon, and the results revealed that CD63, a marker of exosomes [41], and SDC1 were coexpressed at the same location. After SDC1 was knocked down, both the expression of CD63 and the number of exosomes decreased. To examine the relationship between TGFβ2 and exosomes, we also used multiplex immunofluorescence staining and a live-cell imaging system to confirm that CD63 and TGFβ2 were coexpressed at the same location. In addition, we knocked down SDC1 in GBM cells, and ELISA revealed that the culture medium of the SDC1-knockdown cells contained lower amounts of TGFβ2 because of the inhibition of exosome production. In conclusion, miR-652-5p inhibits SDC1 and TGFβ2 expression in GBM cells, and the downregulation of SDC1 leads to a decrease in the number of exosomes released into the medium. Thus, after culture in the medium produced by another group of RFP-labelled GBM cells (Group A), we verified the paracrine effect, specifically, that cells without TGFβ2-RFP (Group B) had high expression levels of TGFβ2-RFP.

The formation of the TGFβ2/sSDC1/pERBB4 complex and its influence on GBM malignancy reveal a sophisticated signalling network. The binding of SDC1 and TGFβ2 through HS chains to ERBB4, leading to its phosphorylation and the promotion of the GBM phenotype, offers a mechanistic understanding of GBM aggressiveness [20, 42]. Three types of interactions are involved in autocrine signal transduction: TGFβ2–full-length SDC1–pERBB4, sSDC1–TGFβ2–pERBB4, and TGFβ2–sSDC1–pERBB4. Two interactions are associated with paracrine signal transduction: sSDC1–TGFβ2–pERBB4 and TGFβ2–sSDC1–pERBB4 (Fig. 7). After the HS chains are degraded, the ternary complex becomes a binary complex consisting of ERBB4 and TGFβ2, and without its HS chains, SDC1 separates from the ternary complex, which results in attenuation of the malignant GBM phenotype (Figs. 5D and 7). Determining which malignant phenotype of GBM cells is influenced by the SDC1/TGFβ2/pERBB4 complex also attracted our attention. GO analysis revealed that the cell cycle, cell proliferation, invasion and migration might be affected. Hence, we performed relevant assays and reported that after knockdown of SDC1 or TGFβ2, the cell cycle was arrested in G1 phase, cell proliferation was inhibited, and the numbers of invading and migrating cells decreased. In addition, these phenomena became much more obvious when SDC1 and TGFβ2 were knocked down simultaneously. The results of the in vivo assay further confirmed that the tumour volume and weight decreased and that the OS time increased when SDC1 and TGFβ2 were knocked down. Previous studies have shown that the PI3K-AKT signalling pathway can be activated by pERBB4/pERBB4 dimers [43, 44]. Therefore, we verified these findings and determined that the protein levels of PI3K, PDK1, p-PDK1, AKT, p-AKT and mTOR decreased after SDC1 and TGFβ2 knockdown. These findings indicated that malignant phenotypes were regulated through the PI3K-AKT signalling pathway upon formation of the SDC1/TGFβ2/pERBB4 complex (Fig. 7).

A limitation of this study is the lack of investigations into chromatin structural changes although the results of the GO analysis suggested significant alterations (Supplementary Fig. 2). Future research will address this gap and elucidate the epigenetic interplay and signalling dynamics in GBM cells. Additionally, while our findings indicated a decrease in TGFβ2 secretion after transfection with the miRNA-652-5p mimic, the precise mechanisms, including potential inhibition of TGFβ2 secretion, warrant further exploration. Understanding these mechanisms could provide insights into the development of targeted therapies that disrupt these critical signalling pathways in GBM.

Conclusions

The present study reveals a novel epigenetic mechanism in glioblastoma (GBM), in which hypermethylation of the miR-652-5p promoter, mediated by HIF2α, enhances tumour malignancy under hypoxic conditions. Our findings reveal that this hypermethylation leads to the upregulation of SDC1 and TGFβ2, which, through their interaction with the pERBB4 complex, promote GBM progression. Importantly, this regulatory axis is amenable to modulation, suggesting its potential as a therapeutic target. Our research provides evidence that targeting miR-652-5p promoter hypermethylation and the SDC1/TGFβ2/pERBB4 complex could offer new strategies for GBM treatment. Furthermore, our study suggests the possibility of using sSDC1 and TGFβ2 as serum biomarkers for GBM malignancy, warranting further investigation.
