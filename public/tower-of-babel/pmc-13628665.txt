The First Dental Photographs in Croatian Professional Periodicals
Authors: Stella Fatović-Ferenčić, Vjekoslav Jerolimov
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThe aim of this study was to analyze Žiga Hercog’s article “Protheze iza resekcije čeljusti” (Prostheses after Jaw Resection), published in Liječnički vjesnik in 1904, as an early example of Croatian surgical prosthetics and the first known visual representation of a dental topic in Croatian professional medical periodicals.Materials and MethodsThe study is based on a historical-medical, textual, and visual analysis of Hercog’s article, with comparisons to Croatian dental historiography, the development of Croatian medical terminology, and the international development of dental photography and prosthetic materials.ResultsThe analysis shows that Hercog’s work extends beyond an individual case report. It reflects a transition from the descriptive presentation of a clinical case to visually supported rehabilitative medicine. Hercog’s text documents the critical adoption of European professional models, the local adaptation of methods, the use of tin as the prosthetic material of choice, and the challenges of early Croatian dental terminology. The accompanying photographs of the prosthesis and the patient served evidentiary and documentary functions and were identified as the first dental photographs published in Croatian professional medical literature.ConclusionHercog’s 1904 article may be interpreted as an early Croatian contribution to the development of surgical prosthetics, dental photography, and medical visual culture. The study indicates the need for further research into Croatian dental history through the analysis of textual, terminological, and photographic sources.

Introduction

During the second half of the nineteenth century, Zagreb attracted members of various professions who participated in the modernization of the city and in the shaping of its health-care, cultural, and scientific institutions. University professors, physicians, writers, musicians, craftsmen of different profiles, and specialists who would gradually shape individual branches of medicine arrived in Zagreb from neighboring countries. From 1866, when Zagreb appointed its first municipal dentist, Josip Hafner, the number of dental practitioners gradually increased. Their work contributed to greater awareness of oral health, the establishment of professional associations, and the development of educational, clinical, and scientific institutions in Croatia. (1)

Following the establishment of the Society of Physicians of the Kingdoms of Croatia and Slavonia in 1874, dentists were also represented among its members, although at the time they were usually general physicians specializing in diseases of the mouth and teeth. Between 1879 and the establishment of the Croatian Dental Cooperative in 1903, twenty-three dentists were members of the Society of Physicians, including two masters of dentistry, Josip Hafner and Namon Gizelt. Dentists participated in monthly and annual meetings and presented lectures on diseases of the mouth, teeth, and jaws, as well as on related surgical topics. (2) Their presence was also recorded during the celebration of the Society’s twenty-fifth anniversary in 1899, when the first international congress organized by the Society was held. Among the participants were Ivan Matković Sr., Adolf Müller, Žiga Hercog, Eugen Rado, Pavao Orešković, Gjuro Dimović, Žiga Altstädter, Ivan Isidor Vinski, Martin Wolf, Josip Fuchs, and Josip Hafner. Adolf Müller was the only one who gave a lecture. (3)

Despite their strong representation within the Society of Physicians, dentists increasingly recognized the need for their own professional organization. This goal was achieved with the establishment of the Croatian Dental Cooperative in 1903. Ten dentists attended the founding meeting; Eugen Rado was elected president, Ante Pavelić vice-president, and Žiga Hercog secretary and recording secretary. The foundation of the Society of Croatian Dentists, the predecessor of the present Croatian Dental Society of the Croatian Medical Association, marked the beginning of organized dentistry in Croatia. (4, 5)

Nevertheless, several more decades passed before a specialized dental journal was founded. Until then, the principal medium for publishing dental papers was Liječnički vjesnik, the oldest Croatian medical journal and the central forum for professional communication within the Croatian medical community.6 Its pages provide insight into the work of pioneers of Croatian dentistry, among whom Žiga Hercog stands out as one of the most important representatives of early Croatian dentistry, oral surgery, and surgical prosthetics.

The aim of this study was to analyze Hercog’s article “Protheze iza resekcije čeljusti,” published in Liječnički vjesnik in 1904, as an example of early Croatian surgical prosthetics and as an important source for the history of dental photography. Particular attention was paid to Hercog’s role in the prosthetic rehabilitation of patients after extensive jaw resections, his relationship to European prosthetic methods, his choice of materials for an individualized prosthesis, and the function of the photographs accompanying the article. The study proceeds from the assumption that Hercog’s article is not merely a report of an individual clinical case, but also a document of the transition from descriptive case reporting to visually supported rehabilitative medicine.

Materials and Methods

The study is based on a historical-medical, textual, and visual analysis of Žiga Hercog’s article “Protheze iza resekcije čeljusti,” published in Liječnički vjesnik in 1904. Hercog’s clinical cases, the terminological features of his language, his references to international literature, and the function of the accompanying photographs were analyzed. The article was contextualized in relation to the history of organized dentistry in Croatia, the role of Liječnički vjesnik as a communication forum for the medical community, and historiographical studies of medical photography in Croatia. For the international context, studies on the history of dental photography, the development of clinical photography in orthodontics, and the history of denture-base materials were used. Methodologically, the study approaches photography as a specific historical source and as a medium of clinical evidence, professional self-representation, and the epistemological shaping of medical knowledge.

Žiga Hercog and the Beginnings of Surgical Prosthetics in Croatia

Žiga Hercog (born Herzog) (Figure 1) was one of the key figures in early Croatian dentistry, particularly in the surgical-dental treatment of maxillofacial diseases and in the construction of special prostheses for large postoperative defects. He was born in Šarengrad on October 21, 1871, and died in Zagreb on July 22, 1955. He graduated from the Faculty of Medicine in Vienna in 1897 and was professionally formed within the Viennese medical and dental milieu. He worked at the Dental Clinic of the University of Vienna as an assistant to Julius Scheff von Hohentruan, where, in addition to diseases of the mouth and teeth, he received further training in diseases of the ear, nose, and throat. After returning to Zagreb in late 1898, he worked at the Brothers of Mercy Hospital. The Provincial Government appointed him physician for jaw injuries and for the prosthetic rehabilitation of defects of the nose and ear. In collaboration with Dragutin Schwarz and later with other physicians, including Teodor Wickerhauser and Adolf Müller, he participated in team-based surgical-dental treatment of maxillofacial diseases and postoperative defects. In this way, he contributed to shaping the field later designated in Croatian professional literature as surgical prosthetics. (7-11) In later Croatian literature, the term surgical prosthetics encompassed prosthetic rehabilitation following surgical procedures, injuries, and the loss of parts of the jaws, oral cavity, and surrounding structures. Čupar discussed this field within head and neck surgery, whereas Miše systematically addressed it within oral surgery.8,9 In contemporary professional classification, such procedures are generally included within the broader field of maxillofacial prosthetics. When historically defining Hercog’s work, however, the term surgical prosthetics is justified because it corresponds to the Croatian professional tradition and to the way in which the field was later systematized.

Hercog’s professional profile illustrates the transitional nature of Croatian dentistry at the turn of the twentieth century. On the one hand, he belonged to a generation of physicians trained within the Central European, particularly Viennese, medical environment. On the other hand, he worked in Zagreb at a time when dentistry was gradually separating from the broader medical framework and developing into a more autonomous professional field. His publications in Liječnički vjesnik span the period from 1899 to 1926 and include prosthetics, diseases of the oral cavity, maxillofacial pathology, jaw injuries, and related topics. (7) Through these publications, he informed the broader medical community about the specific problems of dentistry and contributed to legitimizing dentistry as an important component of modern medical knowledge.

Žiga Hercog’s article “Protheze iza resekcije čeljusti” was published in Liječnički vjesnik in 1904 after having been presented as a lecture at a meeting of the Society of Physicians held on December 22, 1903. (10) In terms of content, it can be placed among the early Croatian contributions to the development of surgical prosthetics. Its importance lies in linking jaw surgery, prosthetic rehabilitation, material innovation, and photographic documentation. Hercog regarded the defect resulting from jaw resection as a functional, aesthetic, and social problem. Such an approach indicates an understanding of prosthetics as a rehabilitative procedure intended to restore mastication, speech, swallowing, and an aesthetically acceptable appearance.

The Emergence and Affirmation of Photography in Dentistry

In the international context, the development of photography in dentistry occurred in parallel with the professionalization of the discipline itself. Histories of dental photography often identify 1840 as a symbolic turning point, when the first modern dental school was established and the work of Alexander S. Wolcott further reinforced the early connection between dentistry and photographic experimentation. (11) From the mid-nineteenth century onward, dental photography gradually ceased to be merely a means of recording unusual or curious cases and became established as a form of clinical documentation. Orthodontics made a particularly important contribution to this change because the discipline developed the principle of standardized repetition of identical views, thereby enabling comparison of findings, treatment planning, and assessment of therapeutic outcomes. (12-14)

During the second half of the twentieth century, photography became strongly integrated into orthodontics, aesthetic dentistry, prosthodontics, oral surgery, and forensic documentation. The digital revolution further transformed its status: photography was no longer merely an illustration of a completed case, but became an integral part of diagnostic, communicative, educational, and medico-legal processes. Contemporary literature particularly emphasizes its value for documenting clinical procedures, communication between dentists and dental technicians, patient education, monitoring the course of treatment, and scientific presentation of cases. (15)

In the Croatian context, Liječnički vjesnik is of particular importance because, from 1877 onward, it served as an important forum for the publication of medical and later dental topics. During a century and a half of continuous publication, it developed into an indispensable textual and visual archive of Croatian medicine. (6) On its pages, photography shaped Croatian medical and dental culture primarily through case reports, depictions of surgical reconstruction, jaw injuries, prosthetic replacements, and rare pathological conditions. Within this framework, Hercog’s 1904 article can be recognized as a paradigmatic example of the early entry of dental photography into Croatian professional publishing. The photographs accompanying the article—one showing two prostheses and the other showing the patient—provide visual evidence of technical execution and clinical effect. In the course of this research, they were identified as the earliest known visual records of Croatian dental practice.

Hercog’s Article “Protheze iza resekcije čeljusti”

In the introductory section of the article, Hercog reviewed the development of various procedures associated with jaw resection from the eighteenth century onward. He emphasized that numerous cosmetic and functional postoperative complications were already recognized in patients undergoing such operations. He also described methods used by surgeons and dentists to alleviate the severe consequences of resection. Among them, he mentioned Eibes, Larrey, and Mursina, whose names he associated with a device called a silver chin (menton d’argent), that is, an artificial replacement for a lost portion of the lower jaw and chin, made of silver or silver-plated. The device, together with a leather bandage resembling a funda maxillae, was fastened beneath the mandible and served as a support for a sponge intended to absorb saliva. However, it did not substantially improve speech or mastication and did not prevent subsequent displacement. (10)

This introductory section alone demonstrates Hercog’s familiarity with international professional literature and European therapeutic traditions. French, German, and Latin terms appear in the text, including silver chin (menton d’argent), Prothese mit Gleitgelenk, and funda maxillae. Such multilingualism was not merely a stylistic feature of the author’s expression but reflected a period in which Croatian dental and medical terminology had not yet been fully standardized. (16, 17) The Latin terms retained the authority of classical medical language and anatomical precision; the German terms reflected the influence of the Central European, especially Viennese, medical environment; and the French expressions pointed to broader European knowledge transfer and the prestige of particular therapeutic schools.

Hercog then introduced readers to a series of authors and methods that he adopted critically rather than unreservedly. After the introductory review, he described his own patient, in whom he attempted to apply Witzel’s method from Dortmund, published in Wiener zahnärztliche Monatsschrift in 1902, following resection of the lower jaw. He soon concluded that this approach was unsuitable in the particular case because the surgeon had removed considerably more tissue than anticipated. The prosthesis could not be attached to the posterior teeth, was too short posteriorly, and was too large in other areas because the resection had removed an important part of the transitional mucosa between the lower lip and the tongue. The anterior oral cavity no longer provided a recess in which the prosthesis could be stably positioned. (10) Hercog interpreted this as evidence that a prosthesis fabricated before surgery could only exceptionally serve its intended purpose. His conclusion demonstrates a critical attitude toward existing approaches and emphasizes the importance of individualizing the prosthetic solution after wound healing.

To avoid leaving the patient without a prosthesis, Hercog removed the anterior part of the existing appliance and replaced it with malleable wire, adapting its arch to the actual need. This temporary solution also proved unsuccessful: the patient felt better without the prosthesis because the weakened musculature immediately after surgery could not overcome even the weight of the relatively light rubber appliance. The mouth remained open, saliva escaped, and a fistula developed. (10) When Hercog again assumed the patient’s care, contractions had already appeared. He attempted to correct them with an inclined plane temporarily made of black gutta-percha. He subsequently constructed another prosthesis according to Witzel’s principle, a Prothese mit Gleitgelenk, which was intended to provide lateral stability. However, he was dissatisfied with this solution because it was complicated, bulky, and uncomfortable for the patient. At that point, Hercog decided to follow his own concept and construct an appliance of his own design.

Hercog’s observation that “many German authors support the French school, although almost all acknowledge that the same results can also be achieved by the so-called German method” is particularly important for understanding the transfer of knowledge into the Croatian setting. It shows that Croatian dental practice did not develop in isolation but was shaped in relation to European professional debates. At the same time, Hercog did not adopt a dogmatic position. He presented the French and German methods as comparable therapeutic options, but not as flawless models. His writing therefore demonstrates a critical mediation of knowledge in which foreign methods were studied, compared, translated, and adapted to local clinical circumstances.

Tin as a Material and the Experimental Nature of Early Prosthetics

Hercog’s choice of tin as the material for constructing the prosthesis is particularly noteworthy. In the history of dental prosthetics, tin played a secondary but recognizable role, primarily as a less expensive alternative to costly noble metals. Historical reviews of denture-base materials list tin among the materials used and tested during the nineteenth century before the predominance of aluminum, rubber, and later synthetic materials. (18) Tin was suitable for mechanical shaping, casting, and the production of soldering alloys, and in older dental practice it was regarded as relatively acceptable for use in the oral environment.

The contemporary concept of the biocompatibility of dental materials includes the requirements that a material should not cause irritation in the mouth, should not be subject to harmful corrosion or biodegradation, and should not be toxic, allergenic, or carcinogenic. (19) Although Hercog did not discuss these categories, his selection of tin shows a practical assessment of material properties in relation to workability, durability, availability, and the specific clinical situation. Because he submitted the paper to a general medical journal, Hercog did not describe the fabrication process in detail. Nevertheless, the description and accompanying photograph suggest that he sought to create a stable, horseshoe-shaped prosthetic base adapted to the relatively flat floor of the oral cavity between the tongue and cheek, thereby preventing destabilization.

Additional stability was achieved with bilateral elastic springs attached by rings to the maxillary molars. Considering the period, the prosthetic context, and the appearance of the appliance, it may be assumed that Hercog most likely used porcelain teeth, which had already become established as standard, aesthetically and hygienically acceptable elements of removable dentures. The teeth were probably fixed in recesses in the prosthetic base, while their cervical portions were additionally secured with tin solder. (20) Hercog did not conceal the limitations of his solution. On the contrary, he noted that the weight of the prosthesis gradually depressed the floor of the mouth. The porcelain teeth probably contributed to this effect. Immediately after insertion, three-quarters of the anterior teeth were not covered by the lip, whereas at the earlier presentation before the Society only one-third had been visible. According to Hercog, this displacement improved the cosmetic result, but it simultaneously demonstrated the problem of tissue loading in a patient who had lost substantial bony support of the mandible. (10)

This section is particularly important for understanding early prosthetics as a field of trial, improvisation, and situational rationality. Tin did not become the dominant or the most important long-term material for denture fabrication, but in Hercog’s case it documents a phase in which practice did not yet rely on a stable set of standardized materials. The choice of material was linked to availability, workability, clinical need, and the author’s assessment of what could be functionally achieved in the individual case.

Photographs as Evidentiary and Documentary Media

The photographs accompanying Hercog’s article are of particular interest. The first shows two prostheses, whereas the second documents the patient (Figures 2 and 3). The photograph of the prostheses is typical of early medical and dental photography. The appliances are isolated against a muted background, with emphasis on their construction, shape, symmetry, retentive elements, and the relationship of the artificial teeth to the metal base. The photographic focus is on the technical execution of the prosthesis as both an achievement and a material demonstration of the author’s ingenuity.

The second photograph shows the patient, a boy, frontally with his mouth partly open. It is a classic early twentieth-century clinical portrait. The patient is not individualized in a psychological sense, but presented as the bearer of a defect and of a therapeutic result. In accordance with contemporary practice, before standardized rules for privacy protection had been established, the boy’s eyes were not concealed. The photograph is descriptive, frontal, and documentary; its purpose is to make visible the relationship between the defect, the prosthesis, and the rehabilitative outcome.

Together, the two photographs form a classic medical-visual pair: device and body, reconstructive means and its application. The text describes the problem, the author’s reasoning, and the technical solution, while the photographs confirm that the solution was constructed and clinically used. Photography thus serves as evidence of the procedure and as confirmation of therapeutic success.

In the international context, this type of photographic publication corresponds to the development of dental and maxillofacial photography in Europe during the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. The history of dental photography shows a gradual transition from experimental and curiosity-oriented images to standardized clinical documentation. (11, 12) Hercog’s article, however, belongs to an earlier, pre-standardization phase in which photography documented the condition, presented a technical solution, and confirmed the therapeutic effect. In this respect, it preceded the major expansion of maxillofacial photography after the First World War, when photographs of facial war injuries, reconstructive procedures, prosthetic replacements, and rehabilitation outcomes became far more frequent in European medical literature. Hercog’s example shows that elements of this visual logic were already present in Croatian dental practice before the war.

In the continuation of the article, Hercog discussed the characteristics of prostheses following resection of the maxilla and described another case from his own practice. The patient was a woman who had undergone surgery for sarcoma at the Brothers of Mercy Hospital. Her entire left maxilla, part of the right maxilla, and the floor of the orbit had been removed. She was referred to Hercog fourteen days after surgery, but contractions had already developed and made impression taking difficult. (10)

He then described his own impression technique. The cavity was filled with softened black gutta-percha while the nose was packed with cotton. The next day, he removed the mass and placed a sponge between the cheek and the repositioned gutta-percha. As expected, the secretion caused the sponge to swell and protrude the cheek. Because ankylosis made insertion of a prosthesis with an obturator difficult, he divided the appliance into three sections that were inserted separately. The two principal sections articulated through a spherical segment so that they would adapt automatically if the patient inserted them incorrectly, which, according to Hercog, she frequently did. The third, smaller section was constructed to articulate like a clasp with both principal parts, while cheek pressure kept them connected. (10)

This section clearly demonstrates the practical, improvisational, and individualized character of early surgical prosthetics. Hercog did not offer an abstract model; rather, he described a solution developed through direct engagement with a specific case. He anticipated tissue reaction, monitored changes in cheek volume, and modified the prosthetic plan accordingly. He conceived the appliance as a mechanism that had to compensate not only for the anatomical defect but also for the difficulties of everyday use. In this way, he showed that early prosthetics was a field of observation, testing, and technical adaptation rather than the application of pre-established standardized protocols. No photograph accompanied this second case. Its absence further emphasizes the value of the photographs in the first case and points to the selectivity of the author or possibly the editor in publishing visual material.

Discussion

The results of this analysis support previous research indicating that, before the emergence of specialized dental periodicals, Liječnički vjesnik was an important communication forum in which dental expertise was gradually articulated within the broader medical field. Juraj Kallay drew attention to these aspects in the mid-twentieth century by listing individual papers in gnathofacial surgery. (21)

When historically defining Hercog’s work, it is necessary to consider the changing professional terminology and the gradual formation of boundaries between dental and surgical fields. In later Croatian literature, the construction of resection prostheses, obturators, and other replacements for large postoperative defects was encompassed by the term surgical prosthetics and discussed within oral surgery and head and neck surgery. (8, 9) In contemporary terminology, such procedures are generally included within the broader field of maxillofacial prosthetics. Therefore, Hercog’s article is defined here as an early contribution to surgical prosthetics, with the qualification that, according to contemporary classification, it belongs to maxillofacial prosthetic rehabilitation.

A review of Croatian dental historiography shows that, although it has recognized the institutional and biographical frameworks of the profession’s development relatively well, it still insufficiently examines individual professional contributions, their epistemological assumptions, and their visual strategies. This is particularly true of the use of photography. Although institutional history, the development of professional societies, and organizational structures have been systematically addressed, the question of how dentists visually represented pathological conditions, therapeutic procedures, and rehabilitation outcomes has remained at the margins of broader research interest.

Consequently, an important analytical level has been missing—one in which photography is considered as a specific medium of clinical evidence and professional self-representation in Croatian professional periodicals. Its role in shaping dental knowledge, visualizing clinical cases, and legitimizing professional authority has largely remained outside the focus of previous research. This neglect is particularly evident when compared with the international development of photography, which began as early as the 1840s, almost simultaneously with improvements in photographic technology and the professionalization of dental medicine. (10, 11)

When this international development is compared with the Croatian situation, a marked chronological gap becomes apparent between the first medical photograph published in Liječnički vjesnik in 1887 and the first photograph in the field of dentistry, published seventeen years later. (15) This delay was probably not merely the result of technical lag, but also reflected the slower institutionalization of dentistry, its transitional position between craft and medicine, and the specific technical difficulties of photographing intraoral structures. The uneven speed with which individual disciplines achieved visual presence in professional periodicals also reflects their unequal rates of professional emancipation.

Hercog’s article is also important at the terminological level. The use of foreign expressions reflects the process by which Croatian professional language developed through contact with Latin, German, and French terminological traditions. The occasional use of foreign terminology reflects an effort to transmit professional knowledge as precisely as possible at a time when Croatian equivalents were not yet firmly established. This aspect of Hercog’s article may be related to the broader efforts of Liječnički vjesnik to cultivate Croatian professional expression and develop a medical language in which, during Hercog’s time, international scientific terminology still coexisted with the need to create Croatian terms. (17)

In this respect, Hercog’s text represents an early historical stage of a process that contemporary projects such as Stomatološko nazivlje by Marin Vodanović, Drago Štambuk, and Ana Ostroški Anić have sought to standardize and regulate terminologically, with the aim of establishing a precise, transparent, and professionally acceptable conceptual apparatus in Croatian. (22) Viewed from this perspective, Hercog’s foreign expressions can be read as a document of an earlier transitional phase in which Croatian dental terminology was still being shaped through borrowing, adaptation, and translation of international professional terms. The contemporary terminology project represents a later normative response to the same need: harmonizing international professional communication with Croatian linguistic and terminological standards.

Hercog’s work shows that Croatian dentistry developed through the transfer of knowledge from abroad, but also through technical adaptations, material experimentation, and individualized clinical solutions. The fact that Hercog did not discuss the prosthetic procedure in greater technical detail probably reflects his decision to address the broader medical community rather than dentists alone. His principal aim was to argue that a prosthesis following jaw resection should be fabricated only after the surgical wound had healed, thereby contributing to a broader professional debate.

Hercog’s selection of tin further confirms the experimental character of early prosthetics. In the history of dental biomaterials, tin appears as one of the materials used before standardized denture bases became predominant. (18, 23) Its use by Hercog points to the situational rationality of clinical practice, in which the author selected a material he considered workable, durable, and functional for the particular defect. This finding shifts interpretation away from a linear history of material progress toward a more complex understanding of early prosthetics as a field of trial, adaptation, and practical ingenuity.

When such practice was additionally supported by photography, it acquired a new level of persuasiveness. The photograph did not serve merely as a representation of the final result, but as evidence of the procedure, confirmation of therapeutic effect, and a means of communication with colleagues. This opens the possibility of a more analytically developed historiography of Croatian dentistry in which photography is recognized as an important source for understanding professionalization, epistemology, and the visual culture of the discipline.

The methodological limitations of the study should nevertheless be acknowledged. The conclusions are based on published material, which does not encompass the entirety of everyday dental practice or exhaust all forms of visual documentation produced within professional work. Material published in a professional journal had already undergone selection, editorial filtering, and representational shaping. The analyzed corpus therefore cannot be understood as a complete or direct reflection of the actual breadth of dental visual practice in the period under study. It is reasonable to assume that part of the photographic and other visual material remained outside published sources, probably in archival, institutional, or private collections. This study should therefore also be regarded as an incentive for further research involving a broader range of sources and enabling more comprehensive documentation of early Croatian dental photography.

Conclusion

Within Croatian dental historiography, Žiga Hercog stands out as one of the key figures in the professionalization and institutional formation of dentistry. His early twentieth-century publications on resection prostheses, obturators, mucosal diseases, maxillofacial defects, oral cosmetics, neuralgia, and jaw injuries demonstrate the breadth of his professional interests and his pioneering role in shaping Croatian surgical prosthetics and early Croatian dentistry.

The article “Protheze iza resekcije čeljusti,” published in Liječnički vjesnik in 1904, represents an example of an early epistemological, linguistic, and visual transition. Epistemologically, it marks a shift from descriptive case reporting toward rehabilitation-oriented medicine, in which a surgical defect is understood as a functional and social problem requiring prosthetic replacement. Linguistically, it shows that professional communication still relied on the coexistence of loanwords, original foreign expressions, and newly emerging Croatian equivalents. Visually, the accompanying photographs of the prosthesis and patient show that, by the beginning of the twentieth century, photography had entered Croatian dental periodicals as a medium of documentation, evidence, and professional legitimation.

Hercog’s article is therefore not only the first known example of dental photography in Croatian professional periodicals but also an important contribution to understanding the beginnings of Croatian surgical prosthetics and the visual culture of rehabilitative medicine. Its analysis shows that Croatian dentistry was shaped not only through institutional development but also through specific clinical challenges, technical adaptation, international knowledge transfer, and visually mediated professional argumentation.
