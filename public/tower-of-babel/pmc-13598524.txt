Pediatric button battery associated spondylodiscitis after delayed endoscopic removal: A case report and literature review
Authors: Christopher Husson, Jessica Sensbach, Grace Guo, Kara Brads, Caroline Kolb, Veronica Rooks
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Spondylodiscitis is a rare complication of pediatric button battery (BB) ingestion with 12 reported cases. Delayed BB removal tends to be the underlying scenario. A posteriorly oriented BB anode on radiographs may also be a pattern seen in these cases. We report the case of a 2-year-old male with chronic intermittent upper respiratory symptoms, initially managed expectantly, without imaging. The child had no neurological symptoms or neck pain. Five months after symptom onset, radiographs unexpectedly revealed an esophageal BB with a posteriorly oriented anode. Emergent endoscopic removal was performed, and multiple BB-related complications were identified. CT and MRI demonstrated T2-T4 spondylodiscitis with chronic osseous bridging. The child was treated with IV antibiotics and was without related clinical sequelae 1 year later. While rare, spondylodiscitis should be sought out in cases of delayed BB removal. Since the absence of neurologic symptoms may not reliably exclude spondylodiscitis, the diagnosis heavily relies upon imaging. CT and MRI both play key roles, but MRI of the cervical and thoracic spine is the study of choice. Radiologists can play a key role in early diagnosis by being familiar with this unique entity.

Introduction

Button battery (BB) ingestion is a uniquely dangerous clinical scenario disproportionately impacting children under the age of 5 [1,2]. The increasing frequency of pediatric BB ingestions and related complications is well documented and has been on the rise since the early 2000s [[2], [3], [4]].

This phenomenon has been attributed to increased consumer usage of 3 V 20 mm lithium BBs [3,4], which are found in numerous common electronic devices including car keys, watches, and greeting cards. BB-powered devices are likely to be present in nearly all households [3,[5], [6]].

Unfortunately, a wide variety of opportunities exist for children to ingest a BB. A child may obtain and ingest a BB from the product itself, the packaging, or from a loose or discarded battery [3]. Fully charged or new BBs pose the greatest risk for causing harm [5]. However, nonfunctional BBs maintain enough residual charge to be lethal when ingested [2,7]. Both the size and composition of modern BBs pose significant problems. BBs that are 20 mm in size or larger tend to result in esophageal impaction and are overwhelmingly responsible for cases with poor outcomes [3,8]. One study demonstrated that 20 mm BBs were responsible for 92% of BB-related serious and fatal outcomes [5,9]. Furthermore, 12.6% of children under 6 who ingest a 20 mm BB experience a major complication or death [3,5]. More recent studies have supported this finding, identifying 20 mm BBs as being associated with more severe complications [2].

Establishing the diagnosis of BB ingestion can be challenging for several reasons. These patients tend to present at a vulnerable stage of life, with a median age of 1.75 years at presentation [2]. Patients under 2 years of age are at higher risk for poor outcomes and disproportionately account for BB-related deaths [2]. Young children may be asymptomatic initially after a BB ingestion event, which may occur in as many as 36% of cases [3]. When symptoms do occur, they are nonspecific and may mimic more common benign clinical entities [3].

Foreign body ingestion events are frequently unwitnessed, and the lack of a supporting history may provide false reassurance. Unwitnessed ingestions carry high risk, occurring in 92% of fatal outcomes and 56% of major complications [3]. The combination of these factors may lead to delayed presentation and endoscopic BB removal. Delayed removal is known to correspond to worse clinical outcomes and increased complications [1,2]. BB exposure for greater than 6 hours has been identified as a critical predictor for poor outcomes [2]. As a result of these factors, imaging is of critical importance in making the diagnosis of BB ingestion, assisting in timely endoscopic removal, and detecting complications. When there is concern for foreign body ingestion, radiographs of the neck, chest, abdomen, and pelvis should be obtained to ensure an accurate diagnosis and that no foreign bodies are missed [7].

Modern 20 mm BBs most frequently become impacted at a physiologic narrowing of the proximal esophagus and therefore may be less commonly seen in the stomach [2]. BBs have a specific radiographic appearance that usually allows them to be distinguished from coins or other foreign bodies. On the frontal view, BBs demonstrate a characteristic “double ring” (also known as “double rim,” “double density,” or “halo sign”), which is specific for BB ingestion [1,7]. Zooming in and aggressive windowing will improve confidence in the identification of these findings. The lateral view is valuable for anatomic localization and confirmation of BB morphology, specifically the orientation of the anode. The anode can be identified by the “step-off,” as the smaller-diameter component of the BB represents the anode and can further distinguish a BB from a coin [5]. An important limitation is that this step off is not always visible and a proper lateral view must be obtained [3]. The anode is the site of greatest damage because hydroxide ions cause high alkaline burns resulting in soft tissue liquefactive necrosis.

If there is any radiographic concern for esophageal BB ingestion, these findings should be immediately relayed to the clinical team, as tissue damage occurs within 15 minutes. Emergent endoscopic removal within 2 hours is indicated per NASPGHAN guidelines [1]. Tissue damage continues after removal and significant complications may arise days to weeks following the procedure.

When the patient is stabilized and/or the BB is safely removed, further imaging is crucial to detect complications, which tend to occur more often in the setting of delayed removal [2]. The most feared and lethal complication is aorto-esophageal fistula, which should always be considered in cases of delayed removal [5]. Additional complications include esophageal perforation, tracheo-esophageal fistula, esophageal and tracheal strictures, vocal cord paralysis, empyema, and spondylodiscitis [[5], [10]].

Case presentation

A 2-year-old male presented with chronic intermittent upper respiratory symptoms. Over the course of 5 months, he had presented to primary care on 4 separate occasions with varying symptoms including irritability, low-grade fever to 100.5°F, increased salivation, spitting up, congestion, trouble eating, intermittent coughing and gagging, and occasional emesis. He maintained his appetite and continued to tolerate both solids and liquids with no weight loss. At each of these visits, the patient was managed expectantly without imaging.

At five months after initial symptom onset, the patient presented to primary care with worsening symptoms including coughing, wheezing, and frequent spitting up after feedings. These symptoms were not responsive to albuterol or over-the-counter medications. He was afebrile and appeared well on examination. Wheezing, rhonchi, and crackles were detected on physical exam. No neurologic deficits or neck pain were elicited.

Radiographs of the chest revealed the unexpected presence of a disc-shaped metallic foreign body with the characteristic “double density” or “halo” appearance, consistent with an impacted esophageal button battery (Fig. 1A-C). Emergent endoscopic removal was performed and confirmed the presence of a CR2025 3V lithium button battery in the esophagus (Fig. 2A and B). MRI sagittal STIR image obtained shortly following endoscopic removal demonstrates marrow edema, anterior disc space collapse, endplate irregularity, and prevertebral inflammation consistent with T2-T4 spondylodiscitis. No abscess or epidural extension was identified.

Overall, the unwitnessed ingestion, patient symptomatology, endoscopic findings, and chronic spinal imaging changes indirectly suggest that the BB ingestion event occurred on the order of weeks to months prior to presentation.

Throughout the hospitalization, the patient remained neurologically intact with no neck pain or deformity on physical exam. Laboratory evaluation at the time of admission revealed a non-elevated C-reactive protein (CRP) and blood cultures with no growth. The patient did have a microcytic anemia, for which the patient was started on elemental iron supplementation. Pediatric infectious disease was consulted and started the patient on a course of intravenous ceftriaxone. No surgical intervention or bracing was deemed necessary. Two follow up CT exams performed in the weeks following the diagnosis demonstrated unchanged spondylodiscitis. The patient successfully completed a 5-week course of ceftriaxone. One year after the BB diagnosis, no clinical sequelae relating to spondylodiscitis were identified. No neck pain, deformity, or neurological deficits were reported. However, the patient did require frequent endoscopic dilations relating to an esophageal stricture as well as other BB-related complications (Fig. 6). Discussion

Spondylodiscitis is a rare complication of pediatric BB ingestion. We propose using the term button battery spondylodiscitis (BBS) in this scenario. Based on our review of the literature and including our case, there are 12 reported cases of BBS (Table 3). It may be possible that BBS could be underrepresented in literature since this diagnosis may be missed initially. BBS tends to occur in the setting of delayed presentation and/or BB removal. Among the 12 reported cases of BBS, this was confirmed in 11 of the cases and not specified in the other case. Younger children may be unusually well-appearing or unable to adequately communicate their symptoms, leading to delays in imaging evaluation and diagnosis. The median age at diagnosis across the 12 reported cases was 16.5 months, with the youngest patient being 5 months old and the oldest 3 years old. When the timing of BB ingestion is remote or unknown, complications should be anticipated.

Delays in detecting BBS are common. Of the 10 applicable cases where timing was specified, the median time to diagnosis of BBS from the time of BB removal was 2.5 weeks. BBS was most often diagnosed when the patient returned with new or worsening symptoms weeks following BB removal, occurring in 9 of the 12 reported cases. This may reflect under-diagnosis. Many of the patients were not reported to have received advanced imaging with CT or MRI prior to discharge. It is also possible that BBS may not yet be radiographically evident at the time of initial BB removal.

Clinical symptoms and physical examination alone may be unreliable in raising suspicion for BBS. In 6 of 12 cases, patients presented with more specific neck symptoms such as immobility, stiffness, pain, posturing, or stridor which may prompt clinical suspicion for BBS (Table 3). In the remaining cases, patients were variably symptomatic. Given the potential for misleading or absent clinical findings, imaging becomes paramount in the diagnosis of BBS.

A posteriorly oriented BB anode is a potential pattern observed in reported BBS cases. In the 5 of 12 BBS cases that specified, all 5 documented a posteriorly oriented anode. The remaining 7 cases did not specify anode orientation. Since it is well established that the anode side corresponds to the site of greatest tissue damage, this pattern may be of relevance [[5], [12]]. Although limited by incomplete data and small sample size, this data from the literature could support further research into an association between posterior anode orientation and BBS.

Regarding the imaging work up for BBS, radiographs are not adequately sensitive and should not be relied upon to rule out spondylodiscitis. MRI is the superior modality for detecting BBS, but CT is complementary and may well be the first imaging study to demonstrate BBS. In cases of delayed presentation and/or BB removal, CT with contrast is likely to be obtained initially to evaluate for emergent complications, such as aorto-esophageal fistula, vascular injury, esophageal perforation, and mediastinitis (Fig. 7). Once the patient is stable, MRI of the cervical and thoracic spine should be considered to provide optimal sensitivity in diagnosing BBS (Fig. 7). Separately, MRI should also be considered if the patient returns with new or worsening neurologic or musculoskeletal symptoms after initial evaluation. All 12 cases of BBS occurred in the lower cervical and/or upper thoracic spine. Therefore, the cervicothoracic junction should be considered a high yield search area when reviewing the imaging in these patients on both CT and MRI. Once dedicated imaging evaluation is performed, the diagnosis of BBS should be relatively straightforward with the typical findings of spondylodiscitis.

Although BBS demonstrates the same imaging findings seen with a more classic infectious spondylodiscitis, these patients may present differently clinically. For example, our case lacks a causative organism and elevated inflammatory markers. This pattern is not unique to our case. Negative blood cultures and near-normal inflammatory markers were also documented in at least 3 additional cases and none of the 12 BBS cases reported a causal organism [17,[11], [18],15]. These findings may raise the question of whether BBS represents a true infection or is more driven by a sterile chemical/inflammatory process. Nonetheless, the typical treatment for BBS appears to be IV antibiotics and conservative management with pain control, bracing, and/or physical therapy. This strategy appears to be successful in the majority of cases in the short term. None of the 12 reported BBS cases required surgical intervention. Eleven of 12 cases did not report any further complications, though long term follow up is limited. In 1 case, a patient experienced continued episodes of upper extremity weakness and incomplete resolution of imaging findings [17].

Our case and literature review are primarily limited by small sample size and lack of long term follow up data. Only 2 cases document clinical follow up after 1 year [15]. The long-term course of the remaining 10 patients is also not well described. More data are needed to understand the potential for long-term adverse outcomes for BBS patients as well as some of the other potential patterns we have identified in this article.

Conclusion

Spondylodiscitis is a rare but important complication of BB ingestion in pediatric patients. It typically occurs after delayed BB removal and patients are variably symptomatic. This diagnosis heavily relies on multiple imaging modalities. Radiographs establish the diagnosis of BB ingestion and can reveal additional relevant findings, such as anode orientation. CT quickly evaluates for emergent BB injuries but may be the first imaging study to show spondylodiscitis. MRI of the cervical and thoracic spine best evaluates for BB spondylodiscitis which most often occurs at the cervicothoracic junction. Radiologists can play a key role by being familiar with this unique entity and its potential patterns.
