The influences of physical activities on college students’ SWB: based on the mediating effect of subjectively perceived health and the moderating effect of implicit mentality
Authors: Gang Tang, Peijie Tang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn recent years, with the increase of mental health burden on Chinese college students, the level of SWB (subjective perception of well-being)has been decreasing. However, physical activities have been verified to have a significant positive impact on the physical and mental health of individuals.MethodIn this paper, questionnaire was used to conduct three substudies and one supplementary study. Among them, Study One examined the relationship between physical activities and SWB among college students, with a total of 244 college students completing the study; On the basis of Study 1, Study 2 examined the mediating effect of subjectively perceived health on the relationships between physical activities and SWB among college students. A total of 248 college students completed the study; Based on Study 2 and Study 3, the moderating role of implicit mentality in this process of mediation was examined, with a total of 280 college students completing the study; A supplementary study was conducted to further validate the applicability of the research mechanism by changing the measurement scales of variables. A total of 332 college students completed the study.Results(1) physical activities positively affect SWB, i.e., the higher the level of physical activities, the stronger SWB; (2) subjectively perceived health partially mediates the relationship between physical activities and SWB among college students, i.e., the higher level of physical activities, the higher level of subjectively perceived health for the college students, which in turn lead to higher SWB; and (3) the implicit mentality moderates the first stage of this mediating pathway and also has a direct influence on the relationship between physical activities and SWB.Conclusion(1) The implicit mentality moderates the relationship between physical activity and subjectively perceived health. Compared with college students with a fixed mentality, those with a developing one exhibit greater subjectively perceived health while being more involved in physical activities, which in turn contributes to the enhanced SWB; (2) The implicit mentality also moderates the association between physical activities and SWB. Relative to students with a fixed mentality, students with a developing one are more engaged in physical activities and reported to have higher levels of SWB; (3) Physical activities serve as a positive restorative mechanism for alleviating physiological and psychological stress among college students, thereby exerting a beneficial influence on overall well-being.Keywords: Physical activities, SWB, Subjectively perceived health, Implicit mentality

Introduction

By 2020, the national physical health failure rate of Chinese college students had reached 30%, and the rates of eyesight loss and obesity were on the rise. According to the 2020 Chinese College Student Health Survey Report, jointly published by China Youth Daily, China Youth School Media, and Dr Dingxiang, students’ health evaluations tended to decline progressively as their grade level increases [1]. From 38% of first-year college students suffering from emotional problems in the past year to 47% of third-year students, the rate of mental health issues increases with each grade. Additionally, half of the undergraduates and doctoral students fail to get enough sleep [2]. Fifty-one percent of college students use distraction techniques to relieve stress, escape negative emotions, and remain in their comfort zones to experience temporary happiness. However, unlike short-term happiness, subjective well-being is considered a long-term concept, influenced by external events, the environment, and individual characteristics.. Health has been identified as an important factor affecting subjective well-being, focusing on both objectively defined physical health indicators and subjectively perceived health. Therefore, physical activities and exercise have gained increasing attention, as ample evidence suggests that physical exercise not only influences cognitive function and physical health—physical activities are positively associated with lower levels of obesity and better cardiometabolic health—but also impacts well-being and mental health [3, 4]. In addition, regular and organized physical activities has been shown to improve executive function in areas such as inhibitory control, working memory, cognitive flexibility and planning [5].

In summary, physical activities plays a crucial role in shaping perceived health, and this perception may mediate the relationship between physical activity and the SWB among college students. Furthermore, implicit theory distinguishes between two types of mentality: fixed and developing. Applied to sports health, individuals with a fixed mentality believe that their abilities or personal traits are inherently static and cannot undergo substantial development. In contrast, those with a developing mentality view their abilities and characteristics as adaptable and capable of improvement. AS a result, college students with a developing mentality are more likely to perceive themselves as healthier after engaging in physical exercise [6].

However, it is an indisputable fact that Chinese college students lack scientific guidance on how to address mental health issues. The long-term mechanism and boundary conditions of using physical activity to enhance their SWB remain largely unexplored. Based on this issue, the research addresses three specific questions: (1) Investigate the impact of college students’ physical activities on their subjective perception of well-being. (2) Exploring the mechanisms of this impact, specifically the mediating role of subjectively perceived health. (3) Investigating the boundary conditions of this influence, namely the moderating effect of implicit mentality.

Literature review

With "Mechanism of influence of physical activities on SWB of college students and its boundary conditions" as the problem orientation, relevant researches mainly focus on (1) physical activities and psychological status of college students; (2) Physical activities and SWB; (3) subjectively perceived health and SWB; (4) Implicit state of mind and SWB.

Physical activities and psychological status of college students

A substantial body of research has demonstrated that physical activities exert a significant influence on psychological well-being. Physical activities are broadly defined as "any bodily movement produced by skeletal muscles that results in energy expenditure" [7].

There are more than ten methods for measuring sports activities, which are usually divided into subjective and objective categories [8]. The most common type of measurement is subjective (or self-reported) measurement, which includes tools such as sports activity diaries and recall questionnaires and is considered practical, universal, low-cost, and easy to use [9].

Currently, the three self-reported questionnaires most commonly used to assess physical activities levels in populations are the Single Item Measurement (SIM) [10]. Brief Binomial Measurement (PACE)) [11], and The National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES)1, The investigation section on sports activities in (Anonymous, EB, no date). SIM demonstrated moderate effectiveness in adults using an ActiGraph GT3X accelerometer (r = 0.46, p < 0.01) [12]. Hardie Murphy et al. found that PACE +, ActiGraph, and GT3X accelerometers have moderate effectiveness in children (r = 0.34–0.49, p < 0.01) [11]. However, this measurement method has not been validated in adults. In college students, the IPAQ-SF questionnaire demonstrated moderate effectiveness in comparison to accelerometers (r = 0.45, p < 0.01) [13].

The FITT principle—comprising frequency (F), intensity (I), time (T), and type (T)—is commonly employed to characterize physical activities [14]. An increasing body of evidence indicates that physical activities is a moderately effective intervention for anxiety, and particularly for depression, with an effect size of 0.72 [15], although its efficacy remains considerably lower than that of the most potent medications and psychotherapeutic treatments. Nevertheless, recent studies have provided compelling evidence supporting the potential of physical activities as a valuable strategy for managing depression [16]. In conclusion, physical activities plays a vital role in influencing perceived health, and their health perceptions may mediate the link between physical activities and the SWB of college students. Moreover, implicit theory differentiates between two types of mentalitys: fixed and developing. Applied to the context of sports health, individuals with a fixed mentality tend to believe that their abilities or personality traits are immutable and resistant to significant change. In contrast, individuals with a developing mentality perceive their abilities and characteristics as flexible and capable of development. As a result, college students with a developing mentality are more inclined to view themselves as healthier after engaging in physical exercise [6].

The perception and evaluation of physical activities are primarily influenced by an individual’s subjective cognitive level [17], which has been a focal point of research over the past five years. Extensive reviews on the relationship between physical activities and cognitive function have provided compelling evidence supporting a causal link. Physical activities has long been recognised as a critical component of children’s overall physical and cognitive development [18], and the present study both corroborates and expands upon this concept [19]. One potential mechanism through which physical activities impacts cognition is by enhancing executive function (EF), which de Greef et al. define as "higher-order cognitive functions responsible for initiating [20], adapting, regulating, monitoring, and controlling information processing and behaviour" [21]. While the impact of physical activities on executive function (EF) is well-established in older populations, its effects in younger individuals are still emerging. Practical executive function is essential for goal-directed behaviour, memory, and attention, and it plays a significant role in academic performance through mechanisms such as inhibition and memory, as well as in the development of writing and reading skills. Researchers have demonstrated that physical activities interventions result in improvements in executive function (EF) among young people, with further evidence highlighting changes in brain structure and function that bolster confidence in these conclusions [22]. These findings provide valuable insights into the impact of physical activities on brain structure, including neural architecture and brain function, as measured using fMRI. In older populations, improvements in aerobic capacity, balance, strength, or body composition help mitigate the adverse effects of ageing on brain health, thereby buffering cognitive decline and supporting sustained mental functioning. Promoting physical exercise has, therefore, become a proven strategy for enhancing the resilience of older adults to environmental stressors, maintaining or improving their health, and enhancing their overall quality of life [23].

In terms of sports activities, there is an increase in non exercise during the transition period from adolescence to adulthood and throughout university [24]. Researchers estimated that among college students in 23 low, middle, and high-income countries, the proportion of non-active students is 41% [25]. Data from the American Student Physical Health Survey show that, in recent years, the explosiveness, strength, and endurance of college students aged 18–22 have been slowly declining [26]. Scholars generally believe that this is due to a lack of awareness of physical fitness and unhealthy lifestyles, where a sedentary lifestyle leads to insufficient physical exercise [27]. Another study found that only 4.3% of Saudi medical students exercise for 30 min or more per day, whereas students in other majors generally exhibit lower levels of physical activities [28].

After conducting a survey on the sports activities of 700 college students in some universities in Changsha, Li Xianxiong et al. found that one-third of the college students had significantly insufficient physical activities, with a high frequency of sitting down every day, and only a small number of college students had a good level of physical activities [29].A study on the sports activities of college students in Beijing found that there are various types of extracurricular sports activities among college students, but their participation is poor and there is no regular exercise plan. Nearly 40% of college students never participate in extracurricular sports activities, and the proportion of female students is higher than that of male students [30].

1Physical activities and SWB

Previous meta-analytic studies have consistently shown a positive correlation [31] between physical activities and subjective well-being. Subjective well-being(SWB), defined as an individual’s overall evaluation of life, is generally conceptualized as include both an emotional and a cognitive component. serves as a comprehensive measure of an individual’s life satisfaction as well as their positive and negative emotional states, reflecting their feelings and thoughts about life [32]. Therefore, physical activities are considered an important health-promoting behaviour. However, whether this positive relationship is statistically significant remains debated. Some studies provide evidence that physical activities can lead to positive changes in well-being [33], while others suggest that the relationship is largely influenced by shared genetic factors that affect both physical activity levels and well-being [34].

Previous meta-analyses that have examined correlational studies not only encompassed correlational designs but also included experimental studies in which physical activities were systematically manipulated [35]. While there is general consensus on the positive relationship between physical activities and SWB, research on the mediating and moderating factors influencing SWB remains inconclusive. This ambiguity arises from the focus on various subdomains of SWB, inconsistent measures of physical activity characteristics, and the reliance on individual studies with diverse sample populations [36]. Consequently, it remains unclear whether the overall positive correlation holds across different study characteristics, dimensions of physical activities and types of samples — a question that this study aims to address and clarify [37].

Research has suggested that moderate physical activities yields the most significant benefits for subjective well-being compared to both high- and low-intensity activities. Nonetheless, studies have demonstrated positive effects on SWB across all levels of intensity, with moderate-intensity exercise being particularly effective [38]. Some studies, however, have shown improvements in SWB following high-intensity physical activities, while others found no significant relationship between light or moderate-intensity activities and well-being [39]. In contrast, Bae et al. reported that light and moderate physical activities, but not vigorous activity, had positive effects on well-being [40]. A systematic review also revealed that engaging in as little as 10 min of physical activities per week, or even just one day of activity, can notably enhance happiness, primarily through aerobic exercises and stretching/balance exercises [40].

Subjectively perceived health and SWB

Subjectively perceived health has long been considered an extrinsic variable influencing SWB [17]. The Health Belief Model, grounded in self-efficacy theory, provides a framework for understanding the relationship between perceived health and SWB. Self-efficacy [41], a key component of social cognitive theory, refers to an individual’s belief in their ability to achieve specific outcomes. Accordingly, when individuals perceive themselves as healthy, they are less likely to view their lives as negatively affected by illness. Positive health perceptions act as a buffer against negative psychological states, thereby enhancing overall life satisfaction [42–44].

Empirical research also suggests that the experience or subjective dimension of health (i.e. self-perceived health) may be more closely related to measures of well-being [45] than actual health status. Previous research has consistently found that both the cognitive (i.e., life satisfaction) and emotional components (i.e., positive and negative emotions) of SWB are linked with subjective health perceptions. For example, researchers found a positive correlation [46] between life satisfaction and self-rated health in 32 European countries. In addition, higher positive emotions higher positive emotions were predictive of better self-rated health, while negative emotions were predictive of poorer self-rated health [47]. In a recent study utilizing data from the Gallup World Poll (GWP), Geerling and Diener found a stronger correlation between self-reported health problems and negative emotions than between health problems and positive emotions or life satisfaction [48].

Implicit mentality and SWB

Considering its subjectivity, researchers often measure happiness through self-report scales. Generally, single-item scales such as the Positive and Negative Affect Scale (PANAS) are used [49]. The SWB measurement scale for individuals also uses self-report methods [50], which has been widely used abopted internationally: (1) the Overall Satisfaction Scale (SWLS), a five-point scoring system, with higher scores indicating higher levels of well-being [51]. Diener et al. proposed that an individual’s life satisfaction is determined by comparing their current living conditions with their expectations [52], which is related to their desires and past life experiences [53].

In human perception and social interaction, it is commonly believed that people hold specific beliefs about the social and personal attributes of others. These beliefs are firmly held due to their impact on cognition, emotions, and behavior, though they are rarely expressed. Since the 1960 s, these beliefs have sparked extensive research and are known as the "implicit personality theory" [54]. Research on implicit personality theory has since expanded into areas beyond personality, such as intelligence [55], relationships [56], and morality [57].

In the context of college students’ perceptions and social interactions, it is widely acknowledged that they hold specific beliefs about the social and personal attributes of others [58]. These beliefs, while strongly held, are often not openly expressed due to their potential impact on cognition, emotionsl, and behavior [59]. Such beliefs have been the focus of extensive research since the 1960 s and are collectively referred to as the "implicit mentality." This concept was later categorized into two distinct types: the fixed mentality (also known as entity theory) and the developing mentality (also known as incremental theory) [54]. Individuals with a fixed mentality believe that attributes such as intelligence, personality, and morality are stable and immutable [60]. In contrast, those with a developing mentality view these attributes as malleable and controllable, suggesting that they can be developed through effort and perseverance [61].

Studies comparing developing and fixed mentality have consistently shown that, with few exceptions, developing mentality is linked to more adaptive cognitive, emotional, and behavioural outcomes. Specifically, a developing mentality functions either as a protective factor or as a facilitator of learning. As a protective factor, it has been found to reduce biases and stereotypes among college students, mitigate the adverse effects of poor economic conditions, and guide individuals in adopting adaptive strategies when facing mental health challenges [62]. As a facilitator, a developing mentality is positively correlated with improved academic performance, greater motivation, diligence, focus, and a stronger preference for constructive learning goals over less productive performance goals [63]. Given the consistent evidence linking developing mentality to positive outcomes, it is reasonable to emphasize their role in promoting an individual’s overall well-being and optimal functioning. Howell et al. argue that the developing mentality is essential for understanding happiness and should be regarded as a defining feature of prosperity, encompassing both emotional well-being and functional success. They further support this claim by reviewing research that connects developing mentality to various aspects of happiness, proposing that developing mentality represents a character strength with potential as an intervention for enhancing individual happiness. Consequently, due to the limited research in this domain, exploring the contribution of a developing mentality as a character strength to an individual’s positive well-being is a logical focus for this study [64].

Well-being serves as a mediator between a developing mentality and academic achievement. When students experience mental and physical stability, they are better equipped to function effectively in daily life, including within the academic environment [65]. Numerous factors can influence a student’s well-being, which in turn can impact their academic performance. Students with a developing mentality are more adept at recovering from challenges, setbacks, and adversity and maintaining better mental and physical health compared to those with a fixed mentality. Higher levels of mental well-being are associated with improved academic performance.

In contrast, students who experience stress, depression, and anxiety due to a fixed mentality tend to show lower academic achievement. A developing mentality enables students to navigate challenges and sustain their health and daily functioning. It helps them avoid becoming discouraged by failure or overwhelmed by difficulties by fostering resilient coping strategies [66]. Resilience is the ability to respond effectively to past, present, and future challenges and problems. A student’s mentality plays a crucial role in determining their resilience, with those possessing a developing mentality exhibiting greater resilience than those with a fixed mentality. Consequently, their mental health tends to be positively influenced rather than adversely affected. Resilience equips students with a developing mentality to endure adversity, an essential quality for success [67].

Furthermore, both developing mentality and resilience contribute to higher levels of well-being, self-esteem, and happiness in students. In contrast, students with a fixed mentality often experience emotional difficulties such as anxiety, depression, and stress. The positive emotions and resilience fostered by a developing mentality enable students to form harmonious relationships with themselves and others, which, in turn, positively influences both their well-being and academic achievement [68].

Research hypotheses

The impact of physical activities on SWB

The Effort Recovery Theory states that in order to fulfill life responsibilities, individuals must fully engage in work and study, which can induce a range of physiological and psychological pressures that negatively impact happiness. Furthermore, individuals need to cease work in order to promote recovery. The effort recovery model emphasizes the recovery process, suggesting that an individual’s resources are restored from a state of fatigue or exhaustion to baseline levels [69]. This theory is applicable to college students under academic pressure, who may feel exhausted after daily study activities [66]. However, after a holiday or a pleasant evening, they may feel refreshed and rejuvenated. Over the past decade, scholars have attempted to study the factors that either promote or hinder this recovery process. Current research shows that physical activities can also aid in energy recovery, accelerate the recovery process, and reduce fatigue and other negative feelings [70]. Building on the effort recovery theory, while many studies have identified a positive relationship [71] between levels of physical activities and SWB, some findings have shown conflicting results regarding this relationship in healthy adults. Moderate to intense physical activities are associated with improvements in self-reported health and related quality of life [72]. However, one study using a subjective measure [73] of physical activities (i.e., a questionnaire) found that participation in light-intensity recreational physical activities produced the highest overall SWB, while moderate intensity activities were associated with the lowest subjective well-being in healthy adults. Another study using subjective measures of physical activities found that the number of days people participated in moderate-intensity physical activities was positively associated with subjective well-being. In contrast, the time spent on vigorous-intensity activities was negatively associated with subjective well-being [74]. Some differences between studies that used subjective versus objective measures of physical activities may stem from behavioral changes, measurement errors, and reliability issues (i.e., lack of overlap in reliability results) and the accuracy of subjective measures (such as questionnaires). For example, populations in better physical condition may have different tolerances to various intensities of physical activities and may interpret and report activity intensity differently from populations in worse physical condition. Another possible explanation for the differences between study results is the use of different study designs (i.e., cross-sectional vs. longitudinal). Longitudinal studies can look beyond a single moment and observe how established sequences of events may cause relationships to change over time. Additionally, none of the studies examined all possible levels of physical activity intensity, especially among college students. Therefore, the following hypothesis is proposed:

H1: There is a positive correlation between physical activities and SWB, i.e. the higher level of physical activities, the higher SWB.

H1: There is a positive correlation between physical activities and SWB, i.e. the higher level of physical activities, the higher SWB.

The mediating role of subjectively perceived health

Studies have shown that college students who engage in daily physical activities are 1.3 times happier than those who do not. However, Piqueras et al. ‘s study did not examine the cognitive component of SWB, namely life satisfaction and potential mediating factors [75]. Lera-Lopez et al. found that perceived health mediated the positive cross-sectional relationship between physical activities and SWB in middle-aged and older adults [76]. Li et al. found that people’s perceived health had a more substantial impact [77] on their SWB than actual physical impairments. However, whether perceived health also moderates this relationship among college students remains unknown. Therefore, more research is needed to investigate the relationship between physical activities and SWB among college students, with a focus on examining potential mediating mechanisms. Given the solid evidence showing the positive relationship between health and subjective well-being and the beneficial effects of physical activities on health, the following hypotheses are proposed:

The moderating effect of implicit mentality

Previous research on implicit mentality has primarily focuses on intelligence-related studies, which shows that students with a developing mentality are more resilient when facing challenging academic transitions and ultimately achieve higher grades [78]. This implies that a developing mentality is a protective factor that enables students to face college challenges and cope with the highly competitive and stressful learning environment. While empirical support for a positive relationship between a developing mentality and well-being is scarce, some studies suggest that individuals with a developing mentality are more likely to experience positive emotions and develop higher levels of well-being. Kern et al. tested the relationship between happiness and a developing mentality by measuring happiness using four of the five PERMA dimensions. The results showed a positive correlation between happiness and a developing mentality [79]. Zeng et al. also demonstrated a significant positive correlation between a developing mentality and happiness in academic settings [68].

However, recent work has extended the findings to health. In health, a growing body of research suggests that mentality influence people’s motivation and effort to form physical activities behaviours. For example, a developing mentality about physical activities can predict self-reported previous exercise habits and future exercise intentions [80]. mentality about weight can also predict health-related outcomes. Individuals who believe that weight is malleable show better self-regulation after dieting setbacks, and a developing mentality [81] about weight has been experimentally induced in a laboratory setting through positive changes in diet choices. That is to say, individuals with a developing mentality view the health consequences of physical activities in a more positive light, believing that physical activities has a greater impact on improving and changing their health status. Therefore, individuals with a developing mentality are more likely to believe that sports can positively impact their health after sports, so the subjective perception of health will be higher and thus produces higher SWB. Based on this, the following hypotheses are proposed:

Research Idea

The influence of physical exercise on SWB is a process. Therefore, questionnaires and experiments were combined to determine the current physical activities status through literature and related policy research. Then, research questions were put forward after the research team had discussed them. Three studies were conducted to verify the hypothesis. The detailed technical roadmap is shown in Fig. 2.

Technical roadmap

Research process

The study did not explore the relationship between variables through single study for the following reasons: (1) The net effect of physical activities on subjective well-being (SWB) may be overlooked if other variables are measured simultaneously when the main effect is unclear; (2) After determining the impact of physical activities on subjective well-being, it is hoped that the mechanism and boundary conditions of physical activities’ effects will be further studied; (3) The progression of multiple studies and the verification of previous research results can help to avoid the risk of biased research findings; (4) The variable measurement of multiple studies may cause participant fatigue, which could negatively impact the results. Similar methods have been used in previous studies on the design of such research methods. For example, Lin and Chan et al. studied the relationship between happiness motivation and happiness through two studies, and then discussed the mediating mechanism of this relationship [82].

This study is divided into three sub-studies and one supplementary study. The first study primarily verifies whether physical activities affect the SWB of college students. Based on the first study, the second study will further explore the mechanism by which physical activities influence subjective well-being and its boundary conditions. Finally, to verify the robustness of the research results, a supplementary study was conducted, which involved changing the measurement methods of the main variables to assess the robustness of the results.

Study 1: Physical activities and SWB

Purpose of this study

To verify Hypothesis 1, namely, physical activities are positively correlated with SWB.

Object of study

Using G*Power, a significance level α of 0.05 and an effect size f = 0.15 were used to achieve a statistical power of 1 − β = 0.95. The results showed that at least 146 participants were required to participate in the study. Therefore, this study used a simple random sampling method to develop questionnaires through the Credamo platform and distribute questionnaires to university students in Chengdu, Sichuan Province. The researchers invited 249 college students from Chengdu, including those majoring in liberal arts, science and engineering, and postgraduates, excluding sports students and unhealthy students. The final sample consisted of 244 valid responses, with 114 male and 130 female participants. The mean age was 21.14 (± 2.17) years. Each student received 5 yuan in cash after completing the questionnaire.

Research materials

The physical activities measure were adapted from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey questions: "How many days a week do you do more than 60 min of physical activities (regardless of type) that causes your heart to race? (e.g., walking, low-impact cycling) "; "On how many days a week do you do 20 min or more of moderate-to-vigorous physical activities?" The responses were recorded on a scale from 1 to 7, with higher scores indicating higher levels of physical activity. SWB was measured using the Overall Satisfaction Scale (SWLS), which included five items: "My life is close to my ideal", "the conditions of my life are excellent", "I am delighted with my life", "I get the important things I want in life so far", "If I could live my life, I would hardly change". Using a five-point scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree), the higher the score, the higher the SWLS. At the same time, other characteristic information of the sample was collected: gender, age, grade, major (sports/non-sports), family changes (excluding the influence of typical life events on SWB), and health status.

Research methods

The questionnaire survey method was adopted, with data collected via an online survey platform. Subjects first completed the physical activity items and then filled out the subjective well-being scale. Finally, demographic information such as gender, average monthly disposable income, age, grade, major, and family changes was collected.

Discussion

The results of Study 1 show a significant positive correlation between physical activities and the SWB among college students. The higher the physical activities, the higher the level of SWB, which is consistent [74] with previous studies. In this regard, both physiological and psychological mechanisms can explain the positive correlation between physical activities and SWB. Studies have shown that with a higher level of physical activities, individuals’ sleep can be improved, especially high-intensity physical activities, which leads to the release of endorphins and positive effects. The "endorphin hypothesis" proposes that depression and anxiety [83] are reduced due to the release of endogenous opioid secretions in the brain. Studies in adults have highlighted the potential role [84] of endocannabinoids in exercise-induced emotion, well-being, anxiety, post-exercise calm, and improved feelings of pain. From a psychological perspective, engaging in multiple high-intensity physical activities over time can help college students develop a healthy and attractive physical appearance, thereby enhancing their self-efficacy and body self-concept, ultimately increasing their SWB [85]. Based on these findings, Study 1 has preliminarily revealed the impact of physical activities on SWB, but the underlying mechanisms remain unclear. Therefore, Study 2 aims to address this gap and explore the mechanisms of how physical activities influence SWB.

Discussion

The results of study 2 again tested Hypothesis 1 and also tested hypotheses 2, 3, and 4. Specifically, college students’ subjective perceived health plays an intermediary role in the relationship between their physical activities and SWB. The higher the level of physical activities, the higher the level of subjective perceived health, which, in turn, leads to a higher level of SWB. This result is consistent with the hypotheses of this study.

One explanation for this is that physical activities can objectively improve the physical fitness of individuals; for example, various studies have reported the relationship [87] between physical activities and reduced risk of death from all causes, reduced probability of cardiovascular disease [88], obesity levels [89], and contribute to better mental health and quality of life [90]. What is more, high-intensity exercise has a greater effect on reducing the risk of cardiovascular disease than moderate-intensity exercise [91]. Therefore, objectively, physical ability is indeed influenced by physical activities, which can significantly enhance college students’ self-assessment of their health.

On the other hand, psychological factors also contribute. Relevant studies suggest that individuals’ perception of having adequate physical activity may affect their health behaviors. First, the belief that a person’s level of physical activity is adequate can improve self-efficacy, and increased self-efficacy, in turn, promotes further participation in physical activities [31]. Second, a belief in the adequacy of physical activities may trigger a placebo-like effect, a therapeutic response [92] stemming from individuals’ beliefs and expectations. Simply believing that one is engaging in physical activities can account for much of the mental health benefits of exercise [93]. Additionally, the belief that one is engaging in sufficient physical activities can improve physical health. Conversely, the belief that one’s activity level is insufficient or harmful may lead individuals to view themselves as less healthy. Moreover, perceived health is strongly linked to various physical and mental health measures [94], including mortality.

The subjectively perceived health itself is a reflection of the mentality of college students. However, combined with the theory of implicit mentality, individuals have two distinct implicit mentality: a developing mentality and a fixed mentality, leading to different behaviour patterns and cognitive rules. Then, will this implicit mentality as a trait impact the positive association between college students’ physical activities and SWB? Based on the above background, this study conducted study 3 to explore the moderating effect of implicit mentality, that is, to test hypothesis 5 and hypothesis 6.

Study 3: The moderating effect of implicit mentality

Research purpose

To verify Hypotheses 5 and 6 and explore the moderating role of implicit mentality both in the process of mediation exercised by subjectively perceived health and in the direct connection between physical activities and SWB.

Object of study

Using G*Power, a significance level α of 0.05 and an effect size f = 0.15 were used to achieve a statistical power of 1 − β = 0.95. The results indicated that at least 160 participants would be required to participate. Therefore, this study used a simple random sampling method to develop questionnaires through the Credamo platform and distribute questionnaires to university students in Chengdu, Sichuan Province. The researchers invited 283 college students in Chengdu, Sichuan province, including those majoring in liberal arts, science and engineering and postgraduates, excluded sports students (2) and unhealthy college students (1), and the remaining valid data were 280. Among them, 135 were male and 145 were female. The mean age was 21.05 (± 2.01) years. Each student will be paid 5 yuan in cash after completing the questionnaire.

Research materials

The implicit mentality was measured using the Implicit Personality Theory Questionnaire, a seven-point scale in which four items were developing mentality, such as "People can change even their most basic qualities", and four items were solid mentality, such as "Everyone is a certain type of person, and there is not much they can do to change that mentality", Scores for the fixed mentality items were processed in reverse. The scores of these eight questions were then averaged as a state of mind index, with the implicit state of mind index centred on the mean to reduce potential problems caused by multicollinearity, with higher scores indicating a stronger developing mentality and lower scores indicating a stronger fixed mentality. This scoring is based on the work of Carnevale et al. [95].

Physical activities, subjectively perceived health, and SWB were measured in the same study, along with other sample characteristics: sex, age, grade, major (sports/non-sports), and health status.

Study design

A questionnaire-based survey was conducted using an online data collection platform. Participants first completed the implicit mentality scale, followed by the physical activity survey, subjective perceived health items, and the SWB scale. Finally, demographic information such as gender, average monthly disposable income, age, grade, major, and whether there were any family changes was collected. The implicit mentality score was calculated based on Carnevale et al.’s [95] method. A high score indicates a stronger fixed mentality, reflecting a belief in a fixed and unchangeable world, while a low score indicates a stronger developing mentality, reflecting a belief in a dynamic and changeable world.

Test for the mediating effect of subjectively perceived health

aAs shown in Table 19, this study also adopts the stepwise regression method for analysis. Since the measurement dimensions of physical activities, subjective perceived health and SWB variables are inconsistent, the variables are standardized before further analysis. After standardization, gender, average monthly disposable income, age, grade, discipline and family changes were used as control variables, physical activities was used as an independent variable, subjectively perceived health was used as an intermediary variable, and SWB was used as the dependent variable. The results showed that physical activities had a significant effect on SWB (β = 0.470, P < 0.001) and had a significant effect on subjective perceived health (β = 0.375, P < 0.001). Moreover, after the mediation variable of subjective perceived health was included in the model, The effect of physical activities on SWB was still significant (β = 0.334, P < 0.001), and the effect of subjective perceived health on SWB was also significant (β = 0.362, P < 0.001). It can be concluded that subjective perceived health still partially mediates between physical activities and SWB. In other words, the higher the level of physical activities of college students, the higher the degree of perceived subjective perceived health, and the higher the SWB.

In addition, this experiment also tested the mediation effect again by referring to the Bootstrap method proposed by Hayes (model 4 was selected). The sample size was 5000. At 95% confidence interval, the results of the mediating test on subjective perceived health did not contain 0(LCI = 0204, ULCI = 0.0838), and the direct effect of the independent variable physical activities did not contain 0(LLCI = 0.004, ULCI = 0.0943). It indicates that the mediating effect of subjective perceived health is significant, and the mediating effect size is 0.05, accounting for 24.038% of the total effect proportion. In conclusion, both methods can verify that subjective perceived health significantly mediates between college students’ physical activities and SWB.

Discussion

The supplementary research results show that the mechanism of influence of college students’ physical activities on SWB is also established after different measurement sources of physical activities and SWB are used, which further enhances the robustness of the effect of this study’s results.

Discussion

The impact of college students’ sports activities on subjective perception of well-being

Previous studies mainly focused on whether physical activities positively impacts subjective perception of well-being (SWB), with only a few examining whether the frequency of physical activities makes a difference, often neglecting the intensity of physical activities as a key variable. Recent research has indicated that the SWB of contemporary Chinese college students is relatively low [98], while sports psychology studies suggest that SWB is closely related to sports persistence. The results of this study show that college students’ physical activities have a significant positive impact on their SWB. The higher the intensity of physical activities, the stronger their perceived SWB. This aligns with the conclusions of previous studies on adults, which suggest that both moderate and high-intensity physical activities contribute to the improvement of SWB.

This can be explained through the effort-recovery theory. Learning is an inherently demanding activity, requiring continuous effort from college students, who must constantly invest their energy and time to overcome challenges and complete tasks. However, without an effective recovery and adjustment mechanism, these efforts can lead to physical and psychological fatigue, even causing excessive stress and emotional problems. Therefore, physical activities serve as a significant recovery and adjustment mechanism for college students. Through sports, college students have the opportunity to relax and relieve stress, reduce anxiety, and simultaneously enhance their body’s metabolism and recovery abilities, making them more resilient and adaptable in the face of various pressures. For instance, every victory of the CUBA "Nine Crown Champion" Huaqiao University men’s basketball team is celebrated as a school-wide festival, greatly boosting students’ sense of belonging and pride as "Hua Da Ren." Additionally, physical activities help enhance self-management and control abilities, providing practical guidance on regularity in daily life, nutrition, and rest.

Mediating role of subjectively perceived health

Study 2 confirmed the mediating role of subjective perceived health between college students’ physical activities and SWB. The higher the level of daily physical activities, the higher the degree of subjective perceived health, which in turn leads to higher SWB. Extensive literature has supported the positive impact of physical activities on health. On one hand, regular physical activities effectively prevent several chronic diseases and contribute to both physical and mental health. On the other hand, increasing evidence confirms the positive effects [99] of physical activities on an individual’s mental and spiritual health. However, earlier studies focused on the effects of physical activities on objective health indicators.

Recently, the importance of subjective health factors has grown, as evidence suggests that young people are increasingly affected by stress and depression, which may profoundly influence their self-perception of health. A series of studies has shown that self-reported physical activities are positively related [100] to perceived health in the general population and adults, and that positive body perception has a significant impact [101] on an individual’s cognitive and mental health. In other words, how individuals perceive their bodies positively influences their mental health, including activities related to physical and mental health as well as healthy eating. The higher an individual’s subjective perceived health, the more satisfied they are with life and well-being. For example, Shi Yigong, the president of Xihu University, set an example by actively encouraging and leading students to participate in the 6–7 am campus long run around Xihu University, which significantly enhanced students’ SWB.

This finding further supports the applicability of the Health Belief Model within the framework of self-efficacy theory to elucidate the relationship between perceived health and SWB. Specifically, when college students recognize the positive effects of physical activities on their physical health, this belief enhances their SWB [70]. In contrast, conventional health assessment methods, which rely on medical evaluations of physical function, often overlook subjective experiences and the beneficial influence of individual perceptions. Consequently, this study underscores the importance of considering subjective factors of physical health [98].

The moderating effect of implicit mentality

Study 3 found the boundary conditions of the above mediating mechanism: the moderating effect of implicit mentality. Previous studies have found that individuals with a developing mentality believe that health behaviours can be changed, and a developing mentality reflects that a given attribute is relatively flexible or can be changed with effort. Research on athletes has shown that athletes with a developing mentality believe that improving athletic ability is possible through hard work and regular training. In contrast, athletes with a fixed mentality believe that athletic ability is stable, genetically determined, and challenging to change even through hard training [102]. In terms of weight, individuals who believe in weight malleability are more optimistic about weight loss, exhibit greater persistence, and gain less weight during weight loss activities than those with a fixed weight mentality. Developing mentalitys were associated with a range of positive cognitive, emotional and behavioural outcomes, while fixed mentalitys were associated with more negative outcomes. Therefore, physical activities is regarded as a kind of healthy behaviour, so under the developing mentality, college students are more likely to recognize and believe that physical and mental status is malleable and can be improved through physical activities. Therefore, after physical activities, with the increase in physical activities, college students of this type will obtain a higher degree of subjective health perception. Therefore, they will have a higher SWB.

The results show that when promoting college students’ participation in sports activities, it is essential to focus on individual cognition, encouraging students to recognize that sports activities can improve their health and SWB. Only by believing in the malleability and changeability of these activities can the positive effects of physical activities be fully realized.

Research conclusions

Using the questionnaire method, this study examined the relationship between sports activities and subjective perception of well-being (SWB) through three sub-studies. It investigated the mediating role of subjectively perceived health between the two, as well as the moderating effect of implicit mentality. In the previous section, the research results of the three sub-studies were analyzed and summarized separately. The research conclusions regarding the entire topic are as follows:

College students’ engagement in physical activities has a positive impact on their SWB, with higher levels of physical activities leading to higher levels of SWB;

Subjectively perceived health plays a mediating role between physical activities and SWB among college students – the higher the level of physical activities among college students, the higher level of subjectively perceived health and, thus, higher SWB;

When college students have a developing mentality, the mediating effect of subjectively perceived health is significant, meaning that sports activities will have a significant impact on their subjectively perceived health, which in turn affects their SWB.

In summary, this study explored and validated the mechanism and boundary conditions of the impact of physical activities on SWB among college students through three sub-studies.

Research limitations and prospects

Research Limitations

The limitations of this study mainly focus on the following three aspects:

First, this study primarily employed a self-report method to assess the physical activities, physical health status, subjective perception of well-being (SWB), and physical activities level of college students. Although these measurement methods have been validated in numerous previous studies and cross-cultural research, social expectation effects may influence variable measurements, and some students may intentionally or unintentionally misunderstand specific questions in the questionnaire. Future research can adopt a combination of subjective and objective measurements, such as combining accelerometers, to measure adequate activity time and the intensity of a given activity;

Second, the sample of this study is mainly college students from Chengdu, and future research can further expand the geographical coverage of the sample;

Third, the measurement of subjective perception of health and physical activities adopts a single item. Although these are standard, validated measurement methods [103], future research could consider developing measurement methods for multiple items.
