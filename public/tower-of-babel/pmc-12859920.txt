Heterogeneity in vaccination attitudes and behavior within an outwardly homogenous religious group: a cross-sectional study of the Ultra-Orthodox Jewish population in Safed, Israel
Authors: Avraham Jacobson, Sivan Spitzer, Michael Edelstein
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundUltra-Orthodox Jewish (UO) communities often show suboptimal and delayed vaccine uptake, leading to recurrent outbreaks. Health equity efforts often treat minorities as uniform, overlooking internal diversity. The UO population comprises distinct subgroups with varying social norms; how this impact on vaccination is not described. We use Safed, a UO-majority city with low vaccination coverage as a case study to examine subgroup differences in vaccination behavior and attitudes.MethodsIn 2023–24, a cross-sectional survey was conducted among parents of children aged 0–10. Respondents were categorized as Litvish, Hasidic, Sephardic, non-mainstream UO, or non-UO. The survey assessed DTP and MMR vaccination status, vaccination attitudes using a validated hesitancy index, sources of information and trust. subgroups were compared using chi-square tests, t-tests, and ANOVA.Results318 parents participated (201 UO). MMR coverage ranged from 100% (Litvish) to 60% (non-mainstream, p < 0.001); DTP showed a similar gap (100% vs. 65.9%, p < 0.01). Significant differences in vaccine attitudes were found among UO subgroups (p < 0.001), with the Litvish showing the least hesitancy and non-mainstream groups the most. Information from healthcare professionals was sought by 36.8% of Litvish vs. 24.6% of non-mainstream. Religious authorities were the primary source of information for 68.8% of Litvish vs. 36.2% of non-mainstream respondents (p = 0.007). Trust in rabbis and healthcare professionals varied significantly between UO subgroups.ConclusionsSignificant intra-UO variation in vaccination attitudes and behavior challenges the assumption of community uniformity. Local health services should identify and consider the particulars of their population to deliver equitable and effective vaccine programmes.

Introduction

Vaccination is a fundamental pillar of global public health, playing a critical role in preventing the spread of infectious diseases and reducing mortality, particularly among children [1]. Vaccines targeting measles, mumps, and rubella (MMR), as well as diphtheria, tetanus, and pertussis (DTP), are integral to routine childhood immunization programs worldwide [2]. The World Health Organization (WHO) recommends achieving at least 90% coverage for the third dose of the DTP vaccine and 95% coverage for the first dose of a measles-containing vaccine to establish herd immunity and interrupt disease transmission [3]. While global coverage of MMR and DTP vaccines has improved significantly over the past decades, substantial disparities persist across regions [4]. Key challenges, including differential vaccine access, misinformation, resource constraints, and political or social instability, exacerbate inequities in vaccine coverage between and within countries [5].

The COVID-19 pandemic led to a marked decline in global vaccine coverage. Although coverage has improved post-2022, many areas have yet to recover to pre-pandemic levels [6]. Intra-country inequities also contribute to disparities, with certain population subgroups, such as ethnic minorities, religious communities, migrants, and socioeconomically disadvantaged groups, often experiencing lower vaccine coverage compared to the general population. In the WHO European region, for example, reduced coverage has been reported among migrants, Roma populations, Irish Travelers, and Ultra-Orthodox (UO) Jewish communities [7]. Addressing these inequities is at the heart of the WHO’s immunization 2030 agenda [8].

In Israel, as of 2022, national vaccination coverage at 24 months reached 91% for the fourth dose of the DTP vaccine and 95.6% for the first dose of the MMR vaccine [9]. However, certain communities, particularly UO Jewish populations, exhibit lower-than-average vaccine coverage [10], contributing since the 1990s to periodic outbreaks of vaccine-preventable diseases (VPD’s). For instance, the UO community experienced measles outbreaks in 2003–2004, 2007–2008, and 2018–2019 [11–14] a pertussis outbreak in 2023 [15] and reported polio cases in 2022 [16]. Several of these outbreaks had documented international consequences, including the export of measles cases to communities in London and New York, illustrating the broader global impact of localized immunity gaps [17, 18].

Approximately 75–79% of the Israeli population is Jewish. Most of the non-Jewish population is Arab and either Muslim, Christian or Druze. The Jewish population can be broadly divided in three categories: secular, traditional-religious and UO. The UO community in Israel, also commonly referred to as Haredi (in Hebrew), constituted about 13% of the country’s population in 2023 [19]. Defined by its unwavering commitment to Jewish law and traditions, the Haredi way of life is centered on religious observance [20]. Interestingly, a systematic review regarding barriers to vaccination in the UO community as a whole, indicated that vaccination barriers have been predominantly logistical rather than religious [21]. Nevertheless, recent years have witnessed a decline in the influence of traditionally pro-vaccine mainstream religious leadership. Concurrently, anti-vaccination movements have increasingly targeted the UO community, promoting anti-vaccination sentiment that has gained traction. These trends coincide with broader shifts within the UO population, including processes of radicalization and isolation in some parts of the community alongside greater exposure to general society, the Internet, and the adoption of more individualistic attitudes in other parts [21].

While the UO population may seem homogenous to the external observer (including public health authorities), it is a heterogeneous population comprised of many subgroups with substantial differences. The main subgroups include the Litvish, Hasidic, and Sephardic communities, each with their own ways of life and leadership models [20]. The Litvish community, with roots in Eastern Europe, is known for its intense focus on intellectual study, particularly of the Torah, the central text of Jewish law. This group places a strong emphasis on Yeshivas (religious schools), where men dedicate their lives to studying Jewish texts. The community is led by prominent rabbis called Gedoley Hador (meaning generation greats in Hebrew), who guide on religious, politics and daily life matters through scholarly interpretation of Jewish law [20]. The Litvish community tends to maintain a more conservative approach, often preserving a separation from the secular world.

In contrast, the Hasidic community, is structured around various dynasties or “courts”, each led by a spiritual leader called Rebbe or Admor. The Rebbe serves as the central figure in both religious and social aspects of the community, and Hasidic life is deeply communal, with distinct practices, clothing, and customs [20]. The Sephardic community, comprising Jews of Middle Eastern, North African, and Iberian descent, exhibits considerable internal diversity. Certain segments maintain lifestyles closely aligned with those of the Litvish sector, while others span a broader continuum of religious observance and display greater openness toward, and engagement with, general society and culture [20, 22].

In addition to the three major ultra-Orthodox (UO) subgroups, several smaller sub-communities exist on the periphery of the broader UO population. These include groups who consider themselves as Hasidic but may not be viewed as such by mainstream Hasidic groups, modern Haredim [23] who are more integrated to general society compared to traditional haredim, and Baalei Teshuva (BT), individuals who have adopted an ultra-Orthodox lifestyle despite not being raised in religious households [24]. Although BT families sometimes integrate into one of the established UO subgroups (Hasidic, Litvish, or Sephardi-Haredi), their distinct background, having entered the community rather than been raised within it, often places them in a different social and informational position. This may influence their access to external knowledge sources as well as their attitudes toward healthcare systems, making them analytically distinct from non-BT members of the same subgroup. The UO communities in Israel tend to be concentrated in specific cities or neighborhoods that reflect their cultural and religious affiliations. The largest and most prominent centers are found in dedicated neighborhoods in Jerusalem and in the city of Bnei Brak, alongside other cities and neighborhoods across the country. One such city is Safed, home to a diverse range of UO subgroups.

Safed is a socioeconomically disadvantaged city in northern Israel with a population of approximately 40,000 residents, of which 53% are UO in2022, a proportion projected to grow, and 40% are under the age of 18 [19, 25]. In recent years, Safed has experienced outbreaks of VPDs, including a measles outbreak in 2018 [26] and a polio case in 2023 [16], highlighting suboptimal herd immunity. A previous analysis revealed that vaccine coverage in Safed for MMR and DTP was lower than the national average, and even lower in UO-majority neighborhoods where coverage did not reach target coverage thresholds [27].

The ultra-Orthodox (UO) population in Safed is notably more diverse than that of major UO population centers in Israel such as Jerusalem and Bnei Brak. Alongside mainstream communities, comprising various Hasidic groups, as well as Litvish and a Sephardic community, Safed also serves as home to non-mainstream UO populations [28] such as BT, immigrants, and others UO individuals who do not identify with any specific subgroup. Though all of these groups share a commitment to Jewish law, they differ significantly in their approaches to leadership, lifestyle, and engagement with secular society. While some communities remain insular and prioritize maintaining traditional ways of life, others have adapted to the modern world, engaging with non-UO Jews and the wider population through outreach and community building [29].

These differences highlight the diversity within the UO population and emphasize the varied ways in which religious observance is practiced and experienced across the broader Jewish community. We hypothesize that these differences might influence health behaviors, including vaccination attitudes and practices. However, research and health policy to date, have often treated the UO population as a homogeneous group, overlooking the distinctions between sub-communities. This study challenges this assumption by exploring heterogeneity in the UO population in the city of Safed, Israel and its impact on vaccination attitude and behavior. The study aimed to identify and describe differences between different UO Safed communities in terms of reported vaccine coverage, general attitudes towards vaccination, and sources of information regarding vaccines.

Methods

Participants

We conducted a cross-sectional survey. The study population included Jewish adults aged 18 and older, residing in Safed and its surroundings. Participants were selected to include a range of UO sub-groups, with non-UO participants recruited as a comparator group. Study participants were asked to self-categorize into UO subgroups. Data were collected between June 2022 and June 2024.

Recruiting participants in Safed proved to be highly challenging, as we encountered significant resistance and reluctance to cooperate. To achieve a sufficient sample size, multiple rounds of questionnaire distribution were conducted. Printed questionnaires were delivered in person to directors and teachers in several UO daycare centers across the city. Staff were asked to distribute the questionnaires to parents and we collected completed forms a few days later. In parallel, an online version of the questionnaire was disseminated through community channels: we contacted members of our advisory board, prominent leaders within different UO subgroups, who distributed the survey link in their WhatsApp and email networks. Despite these efforts, recruitment remained difficult. Many individuals approached in person expressed discomfort or declined participation, reflecting the sensitivity of the topic within the UO community, particularly in the post–COVID-19 context. Because questionnaires were distributed through multiple informal channels and not via a single unified list, we were unable to determine the exact number of families approached, and therefore could not calculate a response rate.

In the second phase, printed versions of the questionnaire were distributed at the pediatric emergency room of Ziv Medical Center in Safed and in grocery stores throughout the city. During these stages participation was entirely voluntary and without any form of compensation.

Subsequently, to increase the number of participants, questionnaires were distributed at UO yeshivahs (centers of religious learning) in Safed, with participation incentivized through a lottery offering vouchers as prizes. In addition, and to augment the sample size, we distributed the survey through an online survey panel company specialized in recruiting survey participants in the UO population (Askaria). To recruit non-UO controls, we used an additional online survey panel company (iPanel) who distributed the survey to panel participants in Safed and its surroundings.

The questionnaire distribution process spanned two years, from July 2022 to July 2024, reflecting the extensive efforts required to ensure adequate representation of both UO and non-UO populations in the study.

Instruments

A structured questionnaire, available in both printed and online formats, in Hebrew and English, was used to collect the data. The questionnaire included several components: sociodemographic information, such as age, gender, marital status and number of children. Parents were asked to report MMR and DTP vaccination status of their youngest children aged 0–10 years, and if applicable, to provide reasons for declining one of the vaccines. Vaccination status was initially recorded using three categories: full vaccination, partial vaccination, and non-vaccination. For analytical purposes, however, the partial and full-vaccination categories were subsequently merged, both to increase statistical power and to reflect a conceptually meaningful distinction between families who rejected vaccination altogether (often reflecting ideological opposition) and those who engaged with vaccination to any extent, whether partially or fully.

Participants were also asked to identify their primary sources of information about vaccinations and to rate the perceived reliability of each source using a single-item measure on a 5-point scale, from “unreliable” (1) to “very reliable.” (5) Examples of primary sources included religious leaders (e.g., rabbis), healthcare providers (e.g., nurses), social media platforms, and family or friends.

Attitudes toward vaccinations were assessed using a 22-item questionnaire, consisting of statements to which participants indicated their level of agreement on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from “strongly agree” to “strongly disagree.” Scores were interpreted such that a value of 3 represented a neutral stance, with lower scores indicating greater vaccine confidence and higher values reflecting increased hesitancy. Of the 22 items, 18 were adapted from a validated vaccination hesitancy questionnaire [30]. consisting of 6 indexes: 1. Usefulness and benefits of vaccinations, such as “the whole community benefits childhood vaccination”. 2. Safety of vaccinations, such as “I am afraid of the adverse events that may occur immediately after vaccination”. 3. Combined and co-administered vaccines such as “Combined vaccines overload the immune system”. 4. Information received on vaccinations, such as” When I took a decision about my child vaccination, I did not feel properly Informed”. 5. Beliefs on family pediatrician, such as “The opinion of my pediatrician is crucial to make a decision about vaccinations and, 6. Beliefs in the health system, such as “Pharmaceutical companies influence the Ministry of Health’s guidelines for vaccinations”. We added a seventh index, comprised of four items, asking about the perceived influence and position of religious law and values on vaccine attitudes. For example, we asked whether in the respondent’s opinion, “Halacha (Jewish law) requires vaccination for children”. The original questionnaire details were translated into Hebrew and reviewed by 2 experts in the field. The questionnaire had a Cronbach’s α coefficient of .905. The survey can be found in supplementary 1.

Finally, participants were asked to self-report their religious identity as UO or non-UO and specify their UO affiliation, if relevant. There are many small religious groups in Safed, and our survey included 10 subgroups to capture the breadth of these groups. Because the number of respondents per group was small and groups could be mapped to broader categories, we aggregated the results into Sephardic, Litvish, Hasidic, non-mainstream UO, traditional religious (i.e., individuals who identify as religious but are not part of the ultra-Orthodox community)) and secular.

Data analysis

To compare UO and non-UO participants, as well as subgroups within the UO community, the reported vaccination coverage rates, and sources of information, we performed two-sided Fisher’s chi-square tests. Two separate comparisons were conducted within the UO population: (1) between the four religious-identity subgroups (Litvish, Hasidic, Sephardi, and non-mainstream), and (2) between Baalei Teshuva (BT) and non-BT participants. To identify the differences between the groups and subgroups in vaccine attitudes, as well as the reliability ratings of information sources, we conducted a one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc analysis.

Ethical approval

The Ethics Committee of the Bar Ilan University, Faculty of Medicine, approved the study, approval number: 11-2021. We also received approval to recruit patients in ZIV Medical center from the Helsinki Committee of the Ziv hospital in Safed, Israel. approval number: 0094-22-ZIV. Prior to participation, all respondents were provided with information about the study. Participation was voluntary and anonymous, and completion of the questionnaire was contingent upon participants’ confirmation that they had read, understood, and agreed to the study conditions.

Results

Demographic characteristics

A total of 318 participants were included in the study, of which 201 (63%) were UO and 117 (37%) were non-UO. Participants’ average age was 38 years (SD = 13), with a range from 18 to 81 years. 42.5% were male and 57.5% female. The majority of participants were married (79%), and the average number of children per family was 4.3 (SD = 2.4) for UO and 2.63 (SD = 1.3) for non-UO. Among the participants, 205 had children aged 0–10 years, including 168 in the UO group (84%) and 37 in the non-UO group (32%). A total of 171 children were over one year of age and therefore eligible for the MMR vaccination, with 135 in the UO group and 36 in the non-UO group. Only those with vaccine-eligible children were included in the relevant analyses. Table 1 presents other relevant demographic data. Because of the low number of UO participants who are not married, and no significant difference in family size across UO groups, these variables are not adjusted for in further analyses comparing UO groups.

Vaccination coverage rate

In our sample, MMR vaccine coverage was lower in the UO population compared to the non-UO population, but the difference was not statistically significant (77.8 vs. 88.6%, p = 0.404). However, significant variation was observed within the UO population (χ2 = 17.4, p = 0.001), with the non-mainstream UO subgroup having the lowest coverage (60%), while all Litvish parents reported having vaccinated their child with MMR. No significant difference was found between BT and those coming from a religious background (χ2 = 1.056, p = 0.523). For the DTP vaccine, there was no statistically significant difference between UO and non UO (82.2 vs. 86.1, p = 0.569). As with MMR, subgroup analysis revealed significant variation within the UO population (χ2 = 17.655, p = 0.002), with the lowest coverage observed in the non-mainstream UO subgroup (65.9%) and full coverage (100%) in the Litvish subgroup. No significant difference was found between BT and those coming from a religious background (χ2 = 2.076, p = 0.370).

Source of information

Overall, the most common reported source of information on vaccinations among the UO population were religious authorities (rabbis or/and Gedoley Hador, together 55.2%) followed by healthcare professionals (34.2%), including nurses and physicians. In contrast, among the non-UO population, healthcare professionals were the leading source of information (62.4%), followed by family (33.7%) and official publications from the Ministry of Health (32.7%). Figure 1 details all the reported sources of information used on vaccination by religious affiliation.

Notably, significant variation existed within the UO community itself. Among the Sephardic, Litvish, and Hasidic subgroups, religious authorities were the most frequently cited source of information (60.7, 68.4 and 63.2% respectively). However, within the non-mainstream UO subgroup, a significantly smaller proportion (36.2%) regarded religious authorities as a key source (χ² = 12.083, p = 0.007).

The trend reversed when considering independent websites (i.e. Websites not from an official health organization) as an information source. Non-mainstream UO participants cited these websites (29.8%) as a source of information about vaccines more than any other groups, including secular respondents, whereas much lower percentages were observed among the Sephardic, Litvish, and Hasidic subgroups (7.1, 5.3 and 11.6%). The difference between non-mainstream UO and other UO groups was significant (χ² = 44.69, p < 0.001).

When distinguishing within the UO population between BT and those raised within the community, significant differences emerge in their reliance on information sources. BT are considerably less likely to view religious authorities (χ² = 6.785, p = 0.011) and family members (χ² = 8.975, p = 0.004) as trusted sources of vaccination information.

The perceived reliability of information sources varied among UO subgroups (Fig. 2). Sephardim, Litvish, and Hasidim regard rabbis or Gedoley Hador as highly trusted sources of information, (4.23, 4.79 and 4.38 for rabbis, 4.41, 4.74 and 4.1 for Gedoley Hador, respectively) whereas the non-mainstream UO subgroup assigns them significantly lower reliability scores (3.51 for rabbis and 3.37 to Gedoley Hador). An ANOVA test revealed a significant effect (F(3,183) = 7.708, p < 0.001). Tukey’s post hoc analysis showed that the non-mainstream UO subgroup had significantly less confidence in rabbis’ opinions on vaccines compared to the Sephardi (p = 0.010), Litvish (p < 0.001), and Hasidic (p = 0.01) subgroups.

A similar difference was observed in the perceived reliability of healthcare professionals. While Sephardim, Litvishs, and Hasidim reported higher mean reliability scores (3.11, 3.58 and 3.25, respectively) the non-mainstream UO subgroup rated them significantly lower (2.6). An ANOVA test again revealed a significant effect (F(3,182) = 4.881, p = 0.003). Tukey’s post hoc analysis indicated that the non-mainstream UO subgroup had significantly less confidence in healthcare professionals compared to the Litvish (p = 0.009) and Hasidic (p = 0.009) subgroups.

A significant difference was found between BT and non- BT in their trust in rabbis (t(192) = 2.109, p = 0.036) and family members (t(192) = 2.375, p = 0.019) with BT showing lower levels of trust in both sources. Overall, social media was consistently perceived, across all groups, as the least reliable source of information.

Vaccine attitudes

Based on the original, validated 18 items, excluding religion-specific questions, vaccine attitudes did not significantly differ between UO (M = 2.98, SD = 0.82) and non-UO participants (M = 2.79, SD = 0.98; p = 0.279, Table 3).

The attitude analysis of UO subgroups, based on the full 22-item scale, revealed significant between-group differences (F(3,165) = 6.518, p < 0.001). The Litvish subgroup reported the lowest levels of hesitancy, while the non-mainstream group reported the highest (Table 3). Tukey’s post hoc tests showed that non-mainstream UO participants had significantly more negative attitudes toward vaccines compared to Litvish (p = 0.001), Sephardic (p = 0.011), and Hasidic (p = 0.006) participants.

In comparison to non-BT participants, BT reported less belief in the health system (3.23 vs. 3.65, p = 0.048) and accorded more weight to religious arguments (2.23 vs. 2.68, p = 0.005, Table 3).

Religiously-framed arguments for vaccination were associated with a positive attitude among the UO respondents, compared to a neutral response among non UO (2.27 vs. 3, p < 0.001, Table 3). Analysis of specific indices revealed a significant difference between UO and non-UO participants on the “information received” index (p = 0.018), with UO participants reporting higher scores.

Within the UO population, non-mainstream participants consistently exhibited fewer positive attitudes across multiple indices. Significant between-group differences were found in perceived usefulness of vaccines (F(3,165) = 4.488, p = 0.005), vaccine safety (F(3,165) = 3.286, p = 0.022), information received (F(3,165) = 3.389, p = 0.019), and belief in the health system (F(3,165) = 4.926, p = 0.003). The most pronounced gap was observed in the religious aspects index (F(3,165) = 13.124, p < 0.001), where non-mainstream UO participants reported significantly higher religiously based hesitancy compared to other subgroups (Table 3).

No significant subgroup differences were found in trust in family pediatricians (F(3,165) = 1.210, p = 0.308) or in attitudes toward co-administered vaccines (F(3,165) = 1.892, p = 0.133), suggesting relative consensus on these aspects within the UO population.

Discussion

Under-vaccination in Ultra-Orthodox Jewish (UO) populations is well-documented globally [21], but to our knowledge, this is the first study to explore within-group UO heterogeneity in routine childhood vaccination. Our findings bring important nuance and complexity to the broader public health discourse on vaccination by highlighting meaningful differences between UO subgroups. Our UO and non UO participants show different characteristics in terms of marital status, and number of young children reflecting an UO population that is characterized by its young age, high level of social norms with regards to marriage and high fertility rate. In Safed, Northern Israel, a city with generally low vaccination coverage, we found that that disparities in vaccination attitudes and uptake between UO subgroups sometimes exceeded those observed between the broader UO and non-UO populations. Specifically, vaccine coverage, sources of information, and attitudes toward vaccination varied significantly across UO subgroups. Non-mainstream UO subgroups exhibited markedly lower vaccine coverage and confidence in vaccines compared to Litvish, Sephardic, and mainstream Hasidic subgroups. The Litvish subgroup demonstrated near-full compliance with both MMR and DTP vaccines, although this finding should be interpreted with caution due to the small number of eligible respondents, surpassing even the non-UO population, likely reflecting strong compliance with rabbinic authority, which supports vaccination [31].

A 2025 study [32] examined vaccination attitudes and behaviors among UO populations in major UO centers in Israel using a different classification and found relatively high vaccination coverage among “Modern” UO respondents, contrasting with lower coverage among Litvish, Sephardic, and Hasidic subgroups. The study focused primarily on participants from major UO centers such as Jerusalem and Bnei Brak and explored attitudes toward the internet as a source of vaccination-related information, without examining views on other sources such as rabbinic leadership, family, or the healthcare system. Our study expands on these results by focusing on UO communities in Israel’s socio-geographic periphery, introducing the distinction between mainstream and non-mainstream UO subgroups and describing the role of additional information sources, particularly religious authorities, in shaping vaccination behavior.

These findings contribute to a growing body of research emphasizing the importance of recognizing heterogeneity within minority populations in public health contexts. For instance, intra-group disparities in attitudes and COVID-19 vaccination rates have been identified among Black and Hispanic subpopulations in Los Angeles [33], Asian subgroups in knowledge and breast cancer screening in San Diego [34], and within Hispanic/Latino populations regarding birthweight outcomes in New York City [35]. Studies have also shown health disparities within racially defined groups, for example, differing in hypertension risk between Black immigrants and African Americans, or cystic fibrosis risk between Finnish and other Caucasians [36]. One study found greater variability in disabilities’ prevalence within the white non-Hispanic populations than between white and non-white groups [37]. Similar patterns of internal variation have also been observed in religious minority groups. Such heterogeneity has been documented, for example, in individual-level vaccination attitudes and in intergenerational shifts in vaccination uptake among Orthodox Protestants in the Netherlands [38–40]. These examples underscore the critical need to account for intra-group diversity when designing and implementing public health interventions.

While prior research suggests a decline in the influence of rabbinic authority on vaccination decisions within UO communities, our findings suggest this pattern is not uniform [41, 42]. Religious authorities remain a primary and trusted source of vaccination information for substantial parts of the UO population, particularly among Litvish, Sephardic, and mainstream Hasidic subgroups. The proportion of non-mainstream UO subgroups relying on religious authorities for vaccination decisions was much smaller. Notably, across all UO subgroups, religious leaders remained a more common source and trusted of information than healthcare professionals. Reassuringly, social media was both the least used and least trusted source of information across both UO and non-UO participants.

The central role of religious leadership in shaping vaccination decisions is also evident in other religious minority settings, although the direction of influence may differ substantially. Among Dutch Orthodox Protestants [43], identified distinct patterns of religious authority, ranging from leaders who fully accept vaccination to those who hold explicit religious objections, and demonstrated that these positions significantly guided congregants’ decisions. In contrast to that context, where religious doctrine can serve as a basis for vaccine hesitancy, rabbinic leadership in most UO communities generally supports vaccination and often functions as an enabling factor. Taken together, these examples illustrate that across diverse minority groups, understanding the internal structure and authority of religious leadership is essential for interpreting vaccination behaviors.

Our findings also revealed difference in religious authority structures. Among Hasidim, one’s personal rabbi held more influence than that of the overarching Gedoley Hador, whereas among Litvish, the two figures held more comparable authority. This distinction reflects broader patterns in rabbinic hierarchy, with centralized versus group-specific leadership models. These patterns underscore the continued influence of rabbinic leadership within much of the UO population yet highlight the importance of recognizing differences in authority structures across subgroups. It also highlights the potential limits of local rabbinic endorsement if broader authority figures, whether Gedoley Hador or Hasidic rebbes who often reside outside of peripheral cities like Safed, hold greater sway. It is therefore essential that public health authorities offering vaccination services to UO populations understand in depth the structure of their local community in order to engage with the right leaders when implementing public health interventions.

An important and often overlooked factor is the role of non-mainstream groups sitting at the fringes of the UO community, such as Baalei Teshuva (BT), in shaping communal attitudes, as they can exert outsized influence, particularly when it comes to processes such as vaccine skepticism and information flow. In our study, we found that BT were less likely to defer to rabbinic authority in vaccination decisions, likely due to their liminal position between the secular and religious worlds [24]. This “cultural border” allows for hybrid identities that may facilitate the entry of alternative views into UO discourse. This dynamic is particularly significant given that mainstream rabbinic leadership in UO communities generally supports vaccination and often acts as an enabling factor; thus, vaccine-hesitant ideas typically enter the community from external sources rather than from rabbinic doctrine itself [21]. Unlike those raised in insular UO settings, BT can integrate perspectives and values from their pre-religious backgrounds, contributing to a more complex identity that may be reflected in their health-related choices [44]. This dynamic serves as conduit for ideas that later diffuse into the Ultra-Orthodox world, influencing attitudes and behaviors within more established and mainstream segments of the community, underscoring the importance of understanding how these groups contribute to shaping intra-community changes.

Our classification of intra-UO subgroups was based on the division of different communities in Safed, as well as the distinction between BT and UO. The questionnaire included a detailed list of all known UO subgroups in Safed, formulated in consultation with community stakeholders, with the aim of enabling comparisons across various measures. However, due to recruitment challenges and small numbers within subgroups, we opted to consolidate categories into three main UO communities: Litvish, Hasidic, and Sephardic, along with an additional category for non-mainstream UO groups. While our approach enables a new level of granularity in understanding vaccination within the UO population, categorizing individuals into defined categories remains challenging. As religious worldviews increasingly intersect with secular or alternative influences, especially among the non-mainstream or fringe segments of the UO population, traditional taxonomies may no longer suffice. Lee Cahaner, a scholar of the Ultra-Orthodox world in Israel proposes a classification beyond the Litvish, Sephardic, and Hasidic divisions, using typologies based on a conservatism-modernism spectrum, offering a new analytical dimension for understanding diversity within UO society [45]. Unlike our classification, which treats non-mainstream groups as a separate category, Cahaner’s typology applies the conservative–modern spectrum across all groups, characterizing each of them along this axis. Nahumi Yaffe further, further expanded this framework by introducing an additional dimension of integration into the broader Israeli society in addition to adherence to the norms of UO society [46]. While both approaches offer richer analytical tools and offer potentially a more relevant way to segment UO society, they do not capture the granularity needed for public health research. Future studies on vaccination in the UO community should consider the challenge of classification when examining vaccine attitudes and behaviors, as they may have significant implications for trust in state institutions on the one hand and compliance with rabbinic authority on the other.

Limitations and strengths

Our study encountered several challenges. First and foremost, recruitment presented a major challenge. Response rates were frequently low, and feedback from potential participants indicated that vaccines constitute a particularly sensitive topic within the UO community, especially following the COVID-19 pandemic, contributing to hesitancy and refusal to engage. This likely impacted sample representativeness and generalizability of the findings: first, the distribution of UO subgroups within our sample may not correspond to the actual demographic composition of Safed’s UO population; second, participants’ reluctance to engage on the topic of vaccination, especially among some subgroups, could mean that those who replied held views that were not indicative of the entire UO population in Safed or of their subgroup. Similarly, the non-UO group in our sample includes a disproportionately high percentage of individuals born abroad and English speakers, further limiting the extent to which comparisons between UO and non-UO groups can be extrapolated to the broader Israeli population. Moreover, because recruitment relied on multiple informal distribution pathways, including daycare staff, advisory board members, and community WhatsApp/email networks, we were unable to estimate how many families were approached. Consequently, a response rate could not be calculated. These further limits our ability to fully assess representativeness.

An additional limitation pertains to the reliance on self-reported data. Parents were asked to recall their child’s vaccination status, which is prone to recall bias, especially in families where the mean number of children is high.

“Finally, we did not collect information which health service providers (e.g., HMOs, municipal clinics) each individual used, limiting our ability to assess accessibility-related factors; future research should examine this dimension more directly.”

Despite these limitations, our findings yield novel and valuable insights. While the sample size and non-representativeness precludes us from making definitive conclusions regarding precise vaccination coverage rates within each subgroup, the pronounced disparities observed within the UO population point to meaningful intra-UO heterogeneity. Furthermore, the data are consistent with broader evidence indicating that Safed exhibits generally low vaccine coverage across both UO and non-UO populations.

The findings of this study have several implications for public health strategies targeting UO populations. First, our study clearly demonstrates that the UO community cannot be treated as uniform or homogeneous when it comes to vaccines. The differences in coverage and attitudes between the UO subgroups are substantial, with some subgroups being well vaccinated, while others are vulnerable to VPD outbreaks. This suggests that UO population-wide vaccination campaigns may be suboptimal, inadvertently reaching segments that are already fully vaccinated, while overlooking those that are most vulnerable and in need of greater attention.

Public health efforts should be tailored to the specific characteristics of each subgroup. Our study shows that the Litvish subgroup strictly follows religious leadership who advocate for vaccination, therefore achieving high vaccine coverage. In contrast, the non-mainstream UO subgroup lacks centralized leadership, is more likely to use alternative sources of (mis)information, and exhibits lower vaccination coverage and less positive attitudes, making it the subgroup most urgently in need of targeted attention. For Hasidic and Sephardic subgroups, engaging local religious leaders is likely to be most effective as they continue to serve as an important source of information. However, for the non-mainstream subgroup, alternative approaches are needed as religious authorities are neither trusted nor followed for health advice. Finally, improving trust in healthcare providers remains crucial across all segments of the UO population. Co-creation models, in which the healthcare system collaborates directly with local UO communities to design hyper-contextualized interventions, offer a promising strategy and have already shown initial success [47].

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study reveals significant intra-group differences in vaccine behavior and attitudes among the UO community in Safed, a group that appears, and is often treated as homogenous. These findings emphasize the need for tailored vaccination programs that account for the diversity within this population. Different subgroups within the UO community have distinct attitudes, practices, and source of information, which significantly impact vaccination coverage. Engaging the right religious authorities as well as the community itself is needed for immunization programmes to navigate the intricacies of ultra-orthodox society, to improve vaccine coverage as well as to increase trust and reliance on the healthcare system. Moreover, this highlights the broader need to examine other minority groups with similar sensitivity to subgroup differences, ensuring that health interventions are appropriately tailored to the unique needs of each community.
