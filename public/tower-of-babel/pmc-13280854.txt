Preparation of Expanded Graphite/Paraffin-Based Composite Phase Change Materials (PCMs) via Low-Temperature In Situ Synchronous Expansion
Authors: Hang Yang, Yunhai Zhang, Baoyu Cui, Weiguang Li, Qingchao Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In this study, an expanded graphite/paraffin-based phase change material (PCM) was efficiently and sustainably prepared via a low-temperature synchronous expansion-adsorption method. The mechanism was further elucidated utilizing FTIR, SEM, BET, XPS, XRD, TG-DSC, and VASP simulation calculations. The results indicate that since paraffin adsorption occurs during graphite expansion, it better adsorbs into the interstitial spaces of expanded graphite, further enhancing its leak resistance while improving thermal conductivity (0.20 W/(m·K)). The EG:Paraffin = 1:4 sample exhibits minimal pore volume (0.002667 cm3), indicating the fewest surface pores on the phase change material, demonstrating effective paraffin adsorption. Simultaneously, without excessive paraffin adsorption, the EG:Paraffin = 1:4 sample exhibited the lowest total weight loss at 71.3%. The interlayer spacing of expanded graphite was effectively increased at low temperatures, resulting in a final expansion volume of 203 mL/g. Throughout the system, the characteristic wax peaks remained distinctly present, indicating that the composite process between expanded graphite and wax was a physical mixture. This finding is consistent with both the XPS spectra and simulation calculations. This study provides an innovative method with greener, more energy-efficient, simplified preparation process to obtain the freshly synthesized porous graphite for the synchronous integration with PA, which can facilitate the rapid development of controllable microstructure and large-scale production of EG/PA-based composite PCMs.

Introduction

Driven by the appearance of global dual-carbon goals, the proportion of renewable energy sources has increased rapidly, such as wind power and photovoltaics, but their intrinsic intermittency and volatility have imposed higher demands on the energy storage and thermal management technologies. Notably, owing to the high latent heat and near-isothermal thermal storage characteristics, phase change materials (PCMs) can absorb/release a large amount of latent heat under near-constant-temperature conditions, which can be regarded as critical thermal energy storage (TES) media for breaking the bottleneck of the volatility of renewable energy and the spatiotemporal mismatch of end-use energy consumption, and thereby PCMs progressively become key enabling materials for both single thermal storage and thermal-electric synergy in the new energy systems. − Nevertheless, traditional organic PCMs (e.g., paraffin (PA)) often exhibit two primary bottlenecks, including low thermal conductivity (∼0.2 W m–1 K–1) and liquid phase leakage, which severely limit their application in high-power scenarios such as solar-thermal storage and thermal management of power batteries. Meanwhile, inorganic salt-based PCMs exhibit several issues such as supercooling, corrosion, and inferior thermal stability, which significantly restrict their large-scale engineering application.

Expanded graphite (EG) possesses a three-dimensional worm-like porous network with a porosity of 70–90%, and thereby PA loading of ≥90 wt % driven by capillary adsorption. Besides, with the intrinsic thermal conductivity as high as 80–120 W m–1 K–1, it can establish continuous thermally conductive pathways even at relatively low amounts of filler loading. The effective thermal conductivity of composite PCMs is enhanced by a factor of 10–20, and the response rate of phase transformation to the heat sources can be remarkably improved, so that the heat can be rapidly transferred from the heat sources to the material of phase transformation. As the latent heat loss is maintained below 10%, the occurrence of leakage can be effectively suppressed, while due to the intrinsic chemical inertness, low density, and low cost of EG, along with its weak van der Waals interaction with PA, both the structural integrity and thermal storage performance are retained even after ≥1000 thermal cycles. Accordingly, EG is extensively recognized as one of the most promising skeleton encapsulation materials for industrialization. −

Currently, in order to effectively address the issues of interfacial compatibility, thermal conductivity improvement, and shape stability, the fabrication process of EG/PA-based composite PCMs has mainly placed emphasis on the pretreatment of EG (i.e., expansion, compression, and surface modification) and the impregnation of PA (i.e., melt impregnation and vacuum impregnation). , First, the surface modification of EG (e.g., TX-100) can enhance the wettability and compatibility with PA, and such an approach is also applicable to PA-based systems as well as CaCl2·6H2O/EG composite PCMs. Second, the vacuum impregnation method is adopted to obtain relatively uniform penetration of PA into the pore structure of EG. By regulating the porosity and compression degree of EG, it has been reported that the bulk materials with various porosities can be fabricated by compressing EG and subsequently impregnating it with PA, while an effective balance between thermal conductivity and energy storage density can be achieved with a porosity range of 60–90%. Third, the thermal performance of PA-based composite PCMs can be further enhanced by constructing a thermally conductive network in conjunction with EG via the template-assisted method or the introduction of additives (e.g., MgO nanoparticles). , Recent studies in 2025 − have further pointed out that the particle-size distribution and compression density of EG were the critical determinants of the overall performance of composite PCMs, indicating that the large-particle EG (>300 μm) can form a complete skeleton, leading to the enhanced thermal conductivity by 1695% and the reduced melting-solidification temperature hysteresis. Through regulating the compression density (ρ = 200–500 kg m–3), the optimal balance between thermal conductivity and latent heat can be achieved, and it can be found that there was an unfilled dead zone in the atmospheric pressure impregnation, and a vacuum-high pressure coupled impregnation process was required to enhance the utilization rate of PA. In terms of functionalization, ZIF-12 metal–organic frameworks were in situ anchored on the surface of EG, and the as-prepared ZIF-12@EG/PA composite PCM maintained a high latent heat of 119 J g–1, while the thermal conductivity increased by 20 times and the solar-thermal conversion efficiency of 91.3% can provide an innovative material design strategy for the solar cogeneration system. ,

Based on the literature review on the EG/PA composite PCMs, much attention has been paid to the processing techniques, performance evaluation, and hydrated salt system rather than to the selection of raw materials, ratio optimization, or the development of novel raw materials. For instance, Zhao et al. were the first to systematically report essential raw material parameters, including the grade of commercial PA, the particle size and purity of EG, as well as the parameters of the expansion process. Additionally, Duan et al. revealed that the raw materials were composed of industrial-grade PA (melting point of 60–62 °C) and 80-mesh EG (purity of 95%), while EG can be obtained via thermal treatment at 900 °C for 1 h (BET surface area of 42.53 m2 g–1, pore volume of 0.14 cm3 g–1) with the mass fractions of EG ranging from 0.5 to 4 wt %, and then the pore volumes were characterized. Furthermore, Mallow et al. also compared the effects of different ratios of commercial PA to EG on the heat transfer rate.

Due to significant merits including strong porous adsorption capacity, good thermal conductivity, and low cost, EG has been extensively used as the dominant thermal conductive support material. Specifically, the commonly used EG is a kind of loose and porous worm-like material with predominantly capillary micropores (10–50 μm) obtained from the natural flake graphite through the process of graphite intercalation, water washing, drying, and high-temperature expansion, while such porous carbon-based materials with high thermal conductivity and high porosity can theoretically optimize the adsorption capacity and thermal conductivity of PA. In an earlier study, the effects of pore structures (micro-, meso-, and macropores) within EG on CO2 chemical adsorption sites were systematically compared (a maximum adsorption capacity of 0.858 mmol g–1). Feng et al. quantified the mesh, fissure-like, and open macropore distributions of EG with three different particle sizes, confirming that the EG-50 (50 mesh) exhibited the highest specific surface area and PA adsorption capacity. Meanwhile, Zhang et al. utilized waste EG to construct the layered double hydroxide (LDH) with a maximum Cu2+ adsorption capacity of 154.8 mg g–1, and the synergistic mechanism between interlayer pores and surface functional groups was also revealed. Overall, EG/PA-based composite PCMs have successfully overcome the bottleneck that it is difficult to simultaneously achieve high latent heat, high thermal conductivity, as well as zero leakage.

The preparation of traditional expanded graphite (EG)-based composites is often hindered by complex procedures, high energy consumption, and limited control over performance regulation. Conventional high-temperature expansion (e.g., >800 °C) not only incurs substantial energy demands but also complicates precise modulation of the pore structure, porosity, and specific surface area. Furthermore, excessive thermal compression treatment risks pore collapse, disrupts the hierarchical architecture of EG, and compromises its encapsulation capacity for phase change materials such as paraffin (PA). Despite these challenges, technical research on the preparation of EG/PA-based composite PCMs remains insufficient, with recent studies failing to comprehensively address the correlation between the porous structure of EG and its adsorption performance. Notably, the formation of porous graphite structures originates from the intercalation and expansion of natural flake graphite; thus, the rational design and process control of this “worm-like” porous architecture may offer a promising pathway for optimizing material performance. Therefore, during the initial design phase, should we consider whether it is feasible to utilize freshly synthesized porous graphite to adsorb more paraffin compared to conventional materials, while avoiding the traditional high-temperature expansion process? To address these bottlenecks, this study focuses on developing a greener, more energy-efficient, and controllable synthesis strategy.

It is worth emphasizing that the judicious selection of intercalating oxidants is pivotal for process simplification. Potassium persulfate (K2S2O8) serves as a single intercalating agent capable of driving a one-step intercalation-expansion process within a concentrated sulfuric acid medium. The feasibility of this approach has been experimentally verified: it significantly shortens the preparation cycle, curtails acid consumption, and mitigates cumulative errors inherent in multistep reactions. Building on this, we propose an innovative low-temperature synchronous expansion-adsorption method. Using K2S2O8 as the oxidant, a synchronous intercalation-expansion reaction is triggered at 80 °C; the high-activity fresh surfaces exposed instantaneously during expansion are then leveraged to achieve in situ, immediate adsorption and filling of paraffin (PA). This constitutes a mild variant of the room-temperature intercalation-expansion process. The core advantage of this method lies in its one-step integration and inherent greenness. By merging the construction of the EG pore structure, surface activation, and PA encapsulation into a single step, the process eliminates the energy consumption associated with high-temperature expansion, prevents pore damage from thermal compression, and circumvents the tediousness of multistep processing.

Materials and Methods

Materials

PA with a melting point of 44 °C was used as the component of PCM and purchased from Nanyang Paraffin Fine Chemical Plant, China. Natural flake graphite (supplied by China Minmetals (Heilongjiang) Graphite Industry Co., Ltd., particle size of +300 μm and fixed carbon content ≥94%) was used. Potassium persulfate K2S2O8 (Hengxing Chemical Reagent Manufacturing Co., Ltd., Tianjin, China, content ≥99.5%) was used as an intercalation oxidant. Concentrated sulfuric acid (H2SO4, ρ = 1.84 g mL–1, w = 98.3%, Fe ≤ 0.005%, Cl– ≤0.001%) can meet the first-class product indicators of GB/T 534–2014. Millipore ultrapure water with a resistivity of 18.2 MΩcm produced by Lichen Co., China, was used for solution preparation and sample dispersion.

Methods

Preparation of the Composite Phase Change Material (PCM)

In this study, expanded graphite/paraffin (EG/PA) composite phase change materials were prepared using a self-designed “low-temperature synchronous expansion-adsorption method”. The core innovation of this method lies in the spatiotemporal synergy between the paraffin adsorption process and the chemical expansion process of graphite. This synergy enables the instant and efficient filling of paraffin onto the highly active graphite surfaces generated in situ during expansion. By systematically varying the amount of paraffin added while keeping the mass of EG constant, we prepared a series of samples with EG-to-PA mass ratios of 1:0, 1:2, 1:4, 1:6, and 1:8, which are designated as EGPC-0, EGPC-2, EGPC-4, EGPC-6, and EGPC-8, respectively. The entire process is explicitly divided into the following four steps, as schematically illustrated in Figure .

Schematic diagram illustrating the synthesis process of EG/PA-based PCM composite.

Paraffin Pretreatment

A measured amount of solid paraffin (PA) was placed in a container and heated in a water bath at 80 °C for approximately 10 min until completely melted into a liquid state, which was set aside for later use. This step aimed to ensure good fluidity of the paraffin for subsequent mixing.

Preparation of the Graphite Intercalation Precursor

In a separate reaction vessel, 1.0 g of natural flake graphite was mixed with 25 mL of concentrated sulfuric acid (H2SO4) at room temperature with continuous mechanical stirring for 30 min to form a homogeneous suspension. This process allowed for the preliminary wetting of the graphite layers by sulfuric acid molecules. Subsequently, under continuous stirring and cooling in an ice–water bath (<5 °C), a measured amount of potassium persulfate (K2S2O8) was slowly added. The addition of K2S2O8 and the low-temperature operation were intended to control the decomposition rate of the oxidant, forming a stable precursor mixture for the intercalation reaction. At this stage, the system did not contain paraffin.

Synchronous Expansion and Adsorption Reaction

Upon completion of the intercalation precursor preparation, the preheated molten paraffin was rapidly added to the above graphite-acid-oxidant mixture and immediately stirred thoroughly to achieve preliminary mixing of the paraffin with the reaction system. Subsequently, the entire mixture was quickly transferred to a constant-temperature water bath preheated to 80 °C and reacted for 5 min.

This is the key stage of the “synchronous” process: At 80 °C, K2S2O8 rapidly decomposes, generating highly active species such as sulfate radicals. These reactive oxygen species vigorously attack the graphite layers, triggering intense oxidation and intercalation reactions, which cause the graphite to expand instantaneously along the C-axis. Simultaneously, as the graphite layers are “pried open”, exposing nascent surfaces with high surface energy, the molten paraffin molecules predispersed within the system are driven by capillary forces and van der Waals interactions to be adsorbed in situ and instantly, embedding into the newly formed nanopores and expanded interlayer spaces of the expanding graphite. The “expansion” and “adsorption” are thus completed cooperatively within this brief spatiotemporal window.

Post-Treatment

After the reaction, the resulting product was repeatedly washed with copious amounts of deionized water until the filtrate reached neutrality (pH ≈ 7) to thoroughly remove residual acid and soluble salts. The washed material was then vacuum-filtered and dried in a forced-air drying oven at 60 °C for 5 h, finally yielding a dry EG/PA composite phase change material powder.

The aforementioned preparation method “low-temperature synchronous expansion-adsorptio” method is not merely a “slightly different interfacial adsorption scenario”, but rather a novel, single-step process that integrates three critical operations into one mild, low-energy procedure: (i) graphite expansion driven by K2S2O8 oxidation at 80 °C; (ii) in situ surface activation, exposing fresh, high-energy sites on the expanded graphite (EG); and (iii) simultaneous PCM encapsulation via capillary-force-driven paraffin adsorption into the EG pores.

It should be noted that the addition sequence of PA was also investigated via multiple exploratory experiments (Figure a,b). Besides, the comparisons of leakage experiments were also conducted in a water bath at 80 °C after liquid PA was added. As presented in Figure a,b, after heating at 40 °C for 30 min, the EG/PA-based PCM prepared by the addition of PA before the treatment in a water bath at 80 °C exhibits superior adsorption performance. Accordingly, the method of PA addition before the treatment in a water bath at 80 °C was adopted in the subsequent experiments, i.e., the one-step low-temperature expansion process of EP-based PCM.

Theoretical Simulation Methods

In model construction, a periodic slab model representing the basal plane of graphite was configured as 3 × 3 × 4 supercells with a 15 Å vacuum spacing along the z-direction to avoid spurious interactions between periodic images. Density functional theory (DFT) calculations were carried out using the Vienna Ab initio simulation package (VASP) with the combination of the projector augmented-wave (PAW) approach for structural optimization. The Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE) potential function was adopted for the exchange potential function in combination with the DFT-D3 correction term. In the optimization process structure, the cutoff energy for the plane-wave basis function was set to 450 eV. During the optimization process, all atomic positions were fully relaxed while in-plane lattice parameters were fixed at the optimal values of original graphite. Brillouin zone integration was conducted using the Monkhorst–Pack k-point grid (4 × 4 × 1). The convergence energy threshold for self-consistent calculations was set to 10–5 eV. With regards to the optimization of equilibrium geometry and lattice constants, the maximum stress value imposed on each atom was required to be controlled within 0.02 eV Å–1.

In addition to the total energy calculations, the charge density difference was also obtained to visualize the redistribution of electrons. The charge density difference can be calculated by eq 1 Δρ=ρadsorbate+surface−ρadsorbate−ρadsorbate+surface where ρadsorbate+surface is the charge density of the adsorption system, ρadsorbate is the charge density of the isolated defective graphite surface, and ρsurface is the charge density of the isolated adsorbate. Meanwhile, the projected density of states (PDOS) of H, C, O, and S atoms were also calculated to analyze the interactions of electrons.

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were performed using the ReaxFF potential energy, and the diffusion behavior was examined using LAMMPS software. The system was in an equilibrium state under the NVT statistical mechanics framework at 298 K with a time step of 0.5 fs. Characterizations

The thermal conductivities of as-prepared porous geopolymer samples were measured using the TPS2500 hot disk thermal constants analyzer produced by Hot Disk AB company. During the testing process, each sample was compressed into a cylinder with a diameter of 50 mm and a height of 15 mm using a hydraulic pellet press. Each measurement was repeated three times, and the corresponding average value was reported to ensure accuracy and reproducibility. The thermophysical properties of as-prepared samples (including phase transformation and peak temperatures) were analyzed using the DSC2910 differential scanning calorimeter produced by TA Instruments Inc., with the temperature range of 0 to 65 °C and the heating rate of 5 °C/min, and thereby the solidification and melting behavior were evaluated under a nitrogen atmosphere. Chemical composition and distribution were analyzed using the Nicolet-10 Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectrometer produced by Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc. With regard to the FTIR analysis, the sample (1 mg) was mixed with spectral-grade potassium bromide KBr (100 mg) and then ground in an agate mortar, pressed into a pellet for the subsequent measurement. Additionally, the surface morphologies of as-prepared samples were observed using the JSM-5610LV scanning electron microscope (SEM).

Results and Discussion

Leakage Analysis

Excellent leakage resistance performance is always regarded as a research focus for EG-based PCMs, and thus, in this section, the effect of PA ratio on the leakage resistance of PCMs. As shown in Figure a,b, when the mass ratio (EG:PA) is below 1:6, the EG/PA-based PCM prepared by the one-step method shows no obvious PA leakage after heating, indicating that when the mass ratio is 1:4, PA can effectively fill the pores between the EG particles to cause the properties of phase transformation.

Expansion Volume Variation

In general, the abundant thermally conductive channels formed by EG can significantly enhance the rapid thermal response of the PCMs to heat sources. In this section, based on the optimal EG/PA ratio, the volume variation trend of EG during the one-step synthesis of EG/PA-based PCMs is presented in Figure . Figure a shows that when the amount of concentrated sulfuric acid increases from 10 to 30 g, the volume variation range of EG prepared by the one-step method remains relatively small.

Effects of various influencing factors on the expansion volumes of PCMs: (a) amount of concentrated sulfuric acid; (b) amount of potassium persulfate; (c) expansion temperature.

Figure b shows the effect of the amount of potassium persulfate on the volume of EG. As presented in Figure b, as the amount of potassium persulfate increases (from 3 g to 13 g), the volume of EG exhibits a pronounced variation trend, indicating that the expansion process is significantly affected. Compared with the amount of concentrated sulfuric acid, the volume variation of EG caused by the amount of potassium persulfate is more remarkable within the temperature range from 50 °C to 90 °C (Figure c).

Specifically, when the amount of concentrated sulfuric acid increases to 25 g, the EG exhibits the optimal expansion volume (147 mL/g), which is attributed to the fact that insufficient concentrated sulfuric acid cannot disrupt the arrangement of C atoms on the edge planes of graphite crystals, thereby preventing effective opening of the interlayer spacing d to form intercalation channels and ultimately hindering the ingress of intercalating agent. Conversely, as shown in Figure a, a larger amount of concentrated sulfuric acid is enabled by its strong oxidizing property to oxidize the C atoms on the edge planes of graphite, and then the electron loss and the accumulation of positive charges are induced, resulting in the generation of electrostatic repulsion that drives the graphite atomic layers to be pushed apart along the C-axis. When the amount of potassium persulfate increases to 11 g, the volume of EG reaches 197 mL/g. As reported, the active oxygen species (e.g., •SO4 –) can be generated from the potassium persulfate in the concentrated sulfuric acid system, which are subsequently inserted into the graphite interlayers to facilitate graphite expansion, as shown in Figure b. Figure c shows that a relatively low reaction temperature is insufficient to effectively promote the formation of EG. When the temperature increases to 80 °C, the chemical reaction rate is accelerated by the higher temperature, which is conducive to the generation of EG with a high expansion ratio (203 mL/g).

Variation trend of expansion volumes of PCMs: (a) amount of concentrated sulfuric acid; (b) amount of potassium persulfate; (c) expansion temperature.

FTIR Analysis

The interactions between the materials and the composite mechanism can be determined through the analysis of the absorption peaks at different wavenumbers. As shown in Figure , with the combination of the FTIR spectra of EG/PA with different mass ratios (1:0, 1:2, 1:4, 1:6, 1:8), it can be found that as an alkane PCM, the PA exhibits the characteristic absorption peaks at 2916 cm–1, 2850 cm–1, and 1469 cm–1. Specifically, the peaks at 2916 cm–1 and 2850 cm–1 correspond to the stretching vibrations of CH2 and CH3, reflecting the characteristics of the alkane chain. The peak at 1469 cm–1 can be assigned to the bending vibration of CH2, further verifying the presence of an alkane structure.

FTIR analysis of PCMs before and after the addition of PA with various mass ratios: (a) full spectra; (b, c) magnified spectra of selected narrow regions.

Additionally, in the FTIR analysis of EG, the peak at 3433 cm–1 corresponds to the stretching vibration of hydroxyl groups (−OH), suggesting the presence of oxygen-containing functional groups (e.g., hydroxyl and carboxyl groups) on the surface of EG. As shown in Figure b, when EG is composited with PA, the characteristic peaks of PA (i.e., 2916 cm–1, 2850 cm–1, and 1469 cm–1) can be still detected in the composite material, and no new absorption peaks appear, indicating that the composite process of EG and PA can be ascribed to a physical mixing rather than a chemical reaction (if chemical bonding occurs, new peaks or significant changes in peak position/intensity would appear in the FTIR spectra).

After the formation of the composite, as the mass ratio of EG to PA progressively increases from 1:0 (pure EG) to 1:2, 1:4, 1:6 and 1:8, the intensities of characteristic peaks of EG (i.e., 1628 cm–1 and 1469 cm–1) are gradually reduced (Figure c), while the intensities of characteristic peaks of PA (2916 cm–1 and 2850 cm–1) are gradually enhanced. This demonstrates that with the decrease of mass ratio, the proportion of EG in the composite material is reduced, while the proportion of PA correspondingly increases. The XRD peak position and half-width information, combined with the sp2-C content measured by XPS, collectively indicate that the used EG exhibits a high degree of graphitization, which provides the basis for its high intrinsic thermal conductivity.

Morphology Analysis

Figure shows the SEM images of the morphologies of PCMs with various addition ratios of the PA. As presented in Figure a, the original graphite exhibits a plate-like distribution with relatively thin particles. Furthermore, EG was prepared by a one-step method at a relatively low temperature (Figure b). It can be found that the morphology of graphite becomes fluffy and porous along the C-axis, while the particle volume rapidly increases (Figure b). With the control of the PA content, the PCMs prepared by the low-temperature one-step method exhibit significant differences in morphologies. Specifically, when the addition ratio is 1:2, the composite exhibits finer and more uniformly distributed pores compared to the EG, indicating that the PA is effectively adhered to the surface of EG and eventually occupies the pore volume (Figure c). When the addition ratio of PA increases from 1:4 to 1:8, the pores on the surface of the PCM are largely filled. In contrast, when the addition ratio of PA is 1:4, the surface of PCM exhibits the fewest observable pores, indicating that PA is effectively adsorbed (Figure d). As the addition ratio of PA continues to increase, the pores on the surface of PCMs become irregularly distributed, which is likely attributed to excess PA not adhering to the inner walls of pores of PCM but instead accumulating on the exterior of particles, leading to the occurrence of encapsulation (Figure e,f).

SEM images of PCMs before and after the addition of PA with various mass ratios: (a) graphite; (b) EG:PA = 1:0; (c) EG:PA = 1:2; (d) EG:PA = 1:4; (e) EG:PA = 1:6; (f) EG:PA = 1:8.

In order to further elucidate the adsorption process of PA in PCMs, we examined the elemental distributions of PCMs with various PA ratios. As listed in Table , compared with the flake-like graphite, the C atomic fraction in the PCM prepared by the low-temperature one-step method exhibits the trend of an initial increase followed by stabilization. Specifically, since no potassium persulfate is added into the flake-like graphite, the atomic fractions of S can be determined as only 0.03%, and the presence of K element cannot be detected, resulting in a high atomic fraction of C element (99.97%). After the low-temperature one-step method, the atomic fraction of the C element is reduced by the presence of S and K atoms. Trace amounts of sulfur (S) and potassium (K) were detected in pure EG (1:0) samples, originating from the preparation process (K2S2O8 and H2SO4). The signals of S and K significantly attenuated with the addition of paraffin and the progression of the composite formation, indirectly confirming that the thorough washing steps during preparation effectively removed most soluble intercalation agent residues. EDS surface scan spectra revealed a uniform micrometer-scale distribution of carbon (C) element signals across all composites, with no observable elemental segregation. This finding serves only as supplementary evidence supporting the excellent physical dispersion of paraffin within the EG matrix. When the addition ratio of PA increases from 1:0 to 1:4, the atomic fraction of the C element in the PCM steadily increases, indicating that the PA adsorption is enhanced (since the PA is mainly composed of hydrocarbons, the atomic fraction of the C element reveals the corresponding PA adsorption status). When the addition ratio of PA further increases to 1:8, the atomic fraction of the C element in the PCM remains relatively stable, demonstrating that the PA adsorption reaches its optimal value. SEM images reveal the uniform and dense morphology of the composite material on the micrometer scale. Higher-resolution transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analysis can be utilized for future studies to observe the atomic arrangement of graphite layers and the nanoscale interface details.

BET Analysis

As shown in Figure , based on the IUPAC classification of adsorption isotherms, the N2 adsorption–desorption isotherms of PCMs all exhibit typical Type IV isotherms accompanied by H3-type hysteresis loops, suggesting that the PCMs are mainly composed of slit-like mesoporous structures formed by the accumulation of plate-like particles, and thereby the desorption hysteresis behavior caused by capillary condensation can be observed. The differences between various samples are mainly reflected in the adsorption capacity variation and the hysteresis loop width. Particularly, both the EG/PA = 1:0 sample and the EG/PA = 1:2 sample exhibit significantly higher adsorption capacity in the medium-high relative pressure region with more open hysteresis loops, demonstrating that these two samples possess more developed mesopores and macropores. In contrast, the adsorption capacities of the EG/PA = 1:4 sample, EG/PA = 1:6 sample, and EG/PA = 1:8 sample are progressively reduced, and the hysteresis loop range is markedly narrowed, suggesting that the pore structure becomes increasingly sparse and the number of pores is diminished. Overall, none of these five samples exhibit significant micropore filling characteristics (as evidenced by the absence of a sharp rise in the low-pressure region), and thus, the material system is classified as being dominated by mesopores, but the degree of mesopore development is progressively reduced with the increase of the sample number.

BET analysis of PCMs before and after the addition of PA with various mass ratios.

Table lists the BET specific surface areas and pore structures of the PCMs before and after the addition of PA. It can be found that the BET specific surface areas of the EG/PA = 1:0 sample and EG/PA = 1:2 sample reach 2.4008 and 2.6633 m2/g, respectively, and the corresponding total pore volumes can be determined as 0.022999 and 0.009273 cm3/g, respectively, which can be assigned to the two groups with the most developed pore structures among these five samples. Particularly, the pore volume of the EG/PA = 1:0 sample is much larger than that of the EG/PA = 1:2 sample, whereas the latter exhibits a slightly higher specific surface area. This demonstrates that a higher proportion of macropores or wide mesopores is present in the EG:PA = 1:0 sample, whereas the EG:PA = 1:2 sample is characterized by a more concentrated pore size distribution and an increased amount of pore walls. Furthermore, this trend is also verified by the average pore size, which exhibits obvious macropore characteristics with an average pore size as high as 38.3 nm. Meanwhile, the average pore size of the EG:PA = 1:2 sample reaches 13.9 nm, which is within the typical mesoporous range. In contrast, both the BET specific surface areas and the pore volumes of the EG:PA = 1:6 sample and the EG:PA = 1:8 sample are dramatically reduced (0.95–0.82 m2/g, < 0.004 cm3/g), demonstrating that the pore structure is gradually simplified and the number of pores is reduced. Notably, the EG/PA = 1:8 sample exhibits the smallest pore volume of only 0.001798 cm3/g, while the sample has the smallest average pore size (8.72 nm), indicating the characteristics of finer mesoporous pores.

The micropore volumes of all five samples are on the order of 10–4 cm3/g, which account for only 1–3% of the total pore volume, suggesting a very small number of micropores and a negligible contribution to the overall pore structure and adsorption behavior. Conversely, the high proportion of external specific surface area (i.e., mesopores and macropores) can determine the overall pore structure characteristics of PCMs. The EG:PA = 1:4 sample and EG:PA = 1:2 sample exhibit the highest external specific surface areas, indicating the formation of numerous open slit-like pores between the plate-like particles. For the EG:PA = 1:4 sample, EG:PA = 1:6 sample, and EG:PA = 1:8 sample, the external specific surface area is gradually reduced, reflecting a progressive sparseness of pore structure.

Overall, all five samples can be identified as the mesoporous material system with H3-type hysteresis loops, but significant differences can be observed in the development of their pore structures. The EG:PA = 1:0 sample exhibits the most developed mesopores and macropores, the largest pore volume, as well as the most open structure. The EG:PA = 1:2 sample shows the highest specific surface area and the moderate pore size, which is characterized by the mesoporous structure with more pore walls and uniform pore size distribution. For the EG:PA = 1:4 sample, EG:PA = 1:6 sample, and EG:PA = 1:8 sample, the progressively simplified pore structures, reduced pore volumes, diminished pore size distribution peaks, and decreased mesopore quantities can be detected.

In this study, the pure EG (EG:paraffin = 1:0) exhibited a moderate BET specific surface area (2.4008 m2/g) and a pore structure dominated by mesopores and macropores, with an average pore size of 38.3 nm. Notably, upon the initial addition of paraffin (EG:paraffin = 1:2), the BET surface area of the composite showed a slight increase (2.6633 m2/g). This phenomenon contrasts with the subsequent monotonic decreasing trend in surface area observed with further increases in the paraffin content (samples 1:4, 1:6, and 1:8). This nonmonotonic variation is not a data discrepancy but rather a direct manifestation of the paraffin filling dynamics within the complex hierarchical pore channels of EG under the specific low-temperature synchronous expansion-adsorption preparation process.

The core of the employed “low-temperature synchronous expansion-adsorption method” lies in the presence of paraffin during the dynamic expansion of graphite layers driven by chemical oxidants. The EG obtained via this process, with its surface chemistry (e.g., CO functional groups indicated by XPS) and pore structure formed in a relatively mild, low-temperature (80 °C) oxidative environment, differs from traditional EG produced by instantaneous high-temperature (>800 °C) expansion, which typically possesses an extremely high surface area and a developed worm-like structure. Therefore, its initial surface area value (∼2.4 m2/g) inherently reflects the substrate characteristics conferred by the low-temperature chemical expansion processfeaturing abundant mesopores and a moderate amount of surface functional groups.

On this substrate, the adsorption and filling of paraffin are not a simple, uniform coverage from the surface inward. Analyzing the BET data (Table ), the process can be divided into two probable stages: selective filling and structural remodeling. In the initial stage of paraffin addition (e.g., ratio 1:2), molten paraffin, driven by capillary forces, preferentially enters and fills part of the macropores and wider mesopores. This directly leads to a sharp decrease in total pore volume (from 0.0230 to 0.0093 cm3/g) and a significant reduction in average pore size (from 38.3 to 13.9 nm). Concurrently, this process may open up or connect some secondary, smaller mesopore channels, allowing nitrogen molecules to access a greater internal surface area. Consequently, this manifests as a temporary slight increase in the external surface area (t-plot external surface area) and total BET surface area. This reveals the pore accessibility optimization effect induced by the filling process.

Comprehensive filling and pore blocking: as the paraffin ratio continues to increase (1:4, 1:6, 1:8), the filling enters a comprehensive and deep stage. More mesopores and even small mesopores become occupied by paraffin, and the pore entrances are gradually blocked. This results in a systematic decrease in the surface area accessible to nitrogen molecules, leading to the expected monotonic decline in both the BET surface area and pore volume. At an optimal ratio of 1:4, the pore volume reaches a very low level, indicating highly efficient filling.

The aforementioned pore structure evolution mechanism provided a microstructural basis for understanding the variation in thermal conductivity (Figure ). The enhancement in thermal conductivity primarily stems from the effective replacement of low-thermal-conductivity air by higher-thermal-conductivity paraffin and the role of paraffin as “thermal bridges” connecting the EG. The slight increase in the surface area of the 1:2 sample corresponds precisely to its “transitional” state, where the pore volume has already decreased substantially and air has been partially displaced, but filling is not yet optimal. Accordingly, its thermal conductivity begins to rise but has not reached the peak. In contrast, the 1:4 sample possesses the lowest pore volume, signifying the most complete filling and the lowest air residue. This is entirely consistent with this sample achieving the highest thermal conductivity (0.20 W/(m·K)).

XRD Analysis

Figure shows the XRD patterns of PCMs before and after the addition of PA with various mass ratios. As presented in Figure , a broad and strong diffraction peak appears at 2θ ≈ 21.16°, which can be assigned to the (002) crystal plane of the graphite layered structure. It demonstrates that the EG remains the layered crystal structure of graphite, although the interlayer spacing is slightly increased compared with the standard graphite (2θ ≈ 26.5°), which is consistent with the expansion treatment process. With the increase of mass ratio of PA, all composite materials exhibit a main peak within the range of 2θ ≈ 21.16°–22.76° corresponding to the (002) crystal plane of EG, and a slight shift of the peak toward higher angles can be detected, indicating that the introduction of PA may bring about the slight compression or rearrangement effect on the graphite interlayers.

XRD patterns of PCMs before and after the addition of PA with various mass ratios.

When the EG/PA ratio is 1:0, the intensity of the characteristic diffraction peak is the highest (∼48,000 cps). Besides, with the increase of mass ratio of PA, the intensity of the characteristic diffraction peak is gradually reduced to ∼40,000 cps at the mass ratio of 1:8, indicating that the crystal signal of EG is diminished by the amorphous characteristics of PA. The characteristic peaks of PA with relatively low intensities appear at 2θ ≈ 21.3° and 23.1°, which correspond to the crystal phase of PA (mainly the CH2 segments with the orthorhombic structure), and their intensities are enhanced with the increased mass ratio of PA, especially at the ratios of 1:6 and 1:8. Pure PA exhibits sharp diffraction peaks at 2θ ≈ 21.3°, 23.1° and 27.7°, indicating its high crystallinity. The peak at 27.7° is the main characteristic peak of PA, which corresponds to the (110) crystal plane of long-chain alkanes. The graphite exhibits a sharp (002) diffraction peak at 2θ ≈ 26.18° with an interlayer spacing of 0.335 nm, which is significantly smaller than that of EG. This phenomenon confirms that the interlayer spacing of graphite can be effectively enhanced by the expansion treatment. The experimental results demonstrate that the layered structure of EG can be maintained in the EG/PA composite, and the introduction of PA cannot disrupt the crystal framework of EG, but leads to a decrease in diffraction peak intensity and a slight peak shift. The PA exists in the composite material in a crystalline state, and the corresponding intensities of characteristic peaks are enhanced with the increase of mass ratio of PA, suggesting that the two phases coexist without undergoing significant chemical reactions and that the composite is formed primarily through physical interactions, which is consistent with the XPS results.

Thermal Conductivity Analysis

As shown in Figure , the thermal conductivity curve exhibits a segmented variation trend of rapid increase, followed by slow decrease. With the increase of the mass ratio of PA, the thermal conductivity rapidly increases from ∼0.08 W/(m·k) to ∼0.12 W/(m·k), demonstrating that the thermal conductivity at this stage significantly increases with the horizontal-axis variable. Additionally, the thermal conductivity sharply increases from ∼0.12 W/(m·k) to ∼0.20 W/(m·k), which is identified as the fastest enhancement range, indicating that the EG:PA = 1:4 sample possesses excellent thermal conductivity. When the mass ratio of PA further increases to 1:8, the thermal conductivity is slowly reduced from ∼0.20 W/(m·k) to ∼0.18 W/(m·k), which is probably derived from the excessive PA coating on the surface of EG, and then the PA melts and creates voids between PCM particles during the heating process, thereby negatively affecting the thermal conductivity.

Thermal conductivity analysis of PCMs before and after the addition of PA with various mass ratios.

As shown in Figure , the effective thermal conductivity of the composites exhibits a characteristic “increase-peak-decrease” trend with increasing paraffin content, reaching a maximum of ∼0.20 W/(m·K) at the EG:PA ratio of 1:4. This nonmonotonic behavior is a direct consequence of the evolving competition between the expanded graphite (EG), the phase change medium (paraffin, PA), and the initially present insulating air within the pores.

In the increasing stage (from EG:PA = 1:0 to 1:4), the thermal conductivity rises primarily through two synergistic mechanisms enabled by the “low-temperature synchronous expansion-adsorption″ process. First, efficient air replacement occurs as molten PA (λ_PA ≈ 0.2 W/(m·K)) infiltrates and physically displaces the low-conductivity air (λ_air ≈ 0.026 W/(m·K)) within the EG pores, drastically reducing the interfacial thermal resistance. Second, the infiltrated PA acts as continuous thermal bridges between adjacent EG sheets, effectively connecting the otherwise isolated EG network. This interpretation is strongly supported by the concurrent sharp decrease in total pore volume observed via BET (Table ) and the densest, most pore-free surface morphology for the 1:4 sample revealed by SEM (Figure d), indicating optimal air expulsion and pore filling.

Conversely, in the decreasing stage (beyond the optimal EG:PA = 1:4 ratio), excess paraffin begins to degrade the thermal pathway. The primary negative effect is matrix “dilution”, where the overabundant, lower-conductivity PA phase forms a continuous matrix that surrounds and isolates the EG network, forcing heat to travel through thicker PA layers. Furthermore, interface deterioration is likely: excess PA coating the exterior of EG particles (as hinted in SEM images, Figure e,f) may detach during the phase change due to volumetric expansion, creating micrometer-scale air voids or weak interfaces that act as significant thermal barriers. The anomalous BET data for the 1:8 sample further suggest pore structure compromise due to overfilling.

The employed “synchronous expansion-adsorption” process is the key to achieving the peak performance at the 1:4 ratio. By adding PA before triggering the expansion reaction, the paraffin achieves in situ adsorption onto the highly active, freshly exposed graphite surfaces, promoting tight interfacial contact (as evidenced by stable surface chemistry in XPS) and the formation of an efficiently filled structure. Beyond the optimal filling point, however, physical space limitations lead to detrimental effects.

In summary, the peak thermal conductivity at the EG:PA = 1:4 ratio represents the optimal balance between the positive effect of “the replacement of low-conductivity air in the EG pores with higher-conductivity paraffin, coupled with improved thermal contact at the EG-PA interface”. caused by excess PA. The measured value of ∼0.20 W·m–1·K–1 represents the macroscopic effective performance under the specific conditions of this study (no high pressure, ∼20 wt % EG loading), achieved through a process designed to balance thermal enhancement with high latent heat storage and form stability. The 2014 study measured thermal conductivity on compacted samples (via high-pressure pressing), whereas our work starts with a loose, porous EG precursor. The fundamental difference in sample state (compacted vs loose) leads to distinct thermal contact pathways and effective conductivity values.

TG-DSC Analysis

Figure shows the TG-DSC analysis results of PCMs before and after the addition of PA at various mass ratios. All five samples show the initial weight loss in the early stage of the experiment, indicating the possible presence of water or solvents, which are volatilized with the drying gas flow. During the entire experiment, no noticeable endothermic or exothermic phenomena were observed in these samples. Meanwhile, no obvious endothermic, exothermic, or mass changes can be detected in these samples during the cooling process, indicating that these samples are completely decomposed during the heating process.

The EG:PA = 1:4 sample exhibits the weight loss of ∼4.1% at room temperature to 150 °C, ∼55.2% from 150 to 280 °C (peak at 264.5 °C), ∼9.8% from 280 to 460 °C (peak at 434.9 °C), and ∼2.0% from 460 to 800 °C, leading to a total weight loss of ∼71.3% (Figure c).

The comparative analysis demonstrates that the total weight loss of EG:PA = 1:0 sample is significantly lower than that of other samples, and its primary decomposition stage occurs at a relatively high temperature. In contrast, the EG:PA = 1:2 sample, EG:PA = 1:4 sample, and EG:PA = 1:6 sample exhibit similar thermal behavior, with the main weight loss concentrated within the temperature range of 150–280 °C. It should be noted that the total weight loss of EG:PA = 1:8 sample is comparable to that of the EG:PA = 1:6 sample, but its main decomposition stage occurs in a broader temperature range with the lowest peak temperature, indicating the unique thermal decomposition characteristics.

The total weight loss in the TG curve represents the entire mass loss of the sample from room temperature to high temperature. For the EG:Paraffin = 1:4 sample, Figure c in the document shows that its weight loss occurs mainly in two steps: 150–280 °C (the major step, 55.2%) and 280–460 °C (the minor step, 9.8%). This 71.3% total weight loss includes not only the complete decomposition of the primary paraffin phase but also a potential minor contribution from the thermal decomposition of a small amount of less thermally stable oxygen-containing functional groups on the EG surface (which decompose around ∼250–400 °C, as seen in the weight loss of pure EG in Figure a). Therefore, strictly speaking, the 71.3% is the sum of the thermal weight loss of “paraffin + trace EG surface groups”. While this might cause the value to be slightly higher than the pure paraffin content, the amount of EG surface groups in this system is very small, making this a minor effect. The primary reason remains the actual paraffin loading.

The multistage thermal decomposition behavior, with each stage corresponding to the removal or decomposition of different material components, can be directly correlated with the XPS data in Table . The XPS data clearly show the presence of various oxygen-containing functional groups on the sample surface. Its C 1s spectrum can be fitted with the following characteristic peaks: the CO bond at a binding energy of ∼287.17 eV (relative content 2.5%) and the C–O bond at ∼285.74 eV (relative content 11.38%), along with sp2 C–C, etc. This confirms, from a static chemical state perspective, the existence of functional groups such as carboxyl, carbonyl, and hydroxyl on the EG surface.

The decomposition at ∼252.4 °C corresponds mainly to the C–O components detected by XPS (corresponding to carboxyl groups, unstable ethers, etc.) and part of the CO (e.g., from anhydrides).

The decomposition at ∼476.7 °C primarily corresponds to the more stable CO components detected by XPS (e.g., carbonyls in ketones, quinones) and other high-temperature-stable oxygenated structures.

These thermal behaviors correspond directly to the CO, C–O, and other functional groups detected by XPS. Together, they confirm that the EG in this study is an expanded graphite material containing an appropriate amount of oxygen functional groups with good thermal stability. This provides the chemical basis for its good interfacial compatibility with paraffin, as discussed previously.

Conclusions

The low-temperature preparation of expanded graphite/paraffin-based composite phase change materials is systematically discussed in this paper. The results indicate that in the potassium persulfate-concentrated sulfuric acid system, the optimal preparation conditions for expanded graphite are 25 g of concentrated sulfuric acid, 11 g of potassium persulfate, and a reaction temperature of 80 °C. The optimal expansion volume is 203 mL/g. Furthermore, simultaneous paraffin adsorption during expanded graphite preparation enhances the paraffin’s leak resistance. When the EG:Paraffin mass ratio was 1:4, paraffin filled the interstitial pores of expanded graphite particles, endowing it with remarkable leak resistance (the EG:Paraffin = 1:4 sample exhibited the lowest total weight loss at 71.3%) and optimal thermal conductivity of approximately 0.20 W/(m·K). BET results indicate that as the EG:Paraffin ratio increases to 1:4, adsorption capacity gradually decreases and the range of the hysteresis loop narrows significantly. This reflects a progressively sparser pore structure with reduced pore count, consistent with morphological analysis findings. FTIR results indicate that when EG is blended with paraffin, the characteristic peaks of paraffin remain clearly present, suggesting the blending process is a physical mixture. XPS results indicate that the C–O bond energy at 285.58 eV for the EG:Paraffin = 1:4 sample exhibits the smallest shift relative to graphite (285.59 eV), suggesting a tendency toward physical adsorption; XRD results confirm that the layered structure of EG is preserved in the EG-paraffin composite, with the introduction of paraffin not disrupting EG’s crystal framework. These findings are consistent with the simulation calculations.
