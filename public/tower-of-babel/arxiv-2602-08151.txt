A second order regret bound for NormalHedge
Yoav Freund, Nicholas J. A. Harvey, Victor S. Portella, Yabing Qi, Yu-Xiang Wang

Abstract
We consider the problem of prediction with expert advice for “easy” sequences. We show that a variant of NormalHedge enjoys a second-order ϵ \epsilon -quantile regret bound of O ⁡ ( V T ​ log ⁡ ( V T / ϵ ) ) O\big(\sqrt{V_{T}\log(V_{T}/\epsilon)}\big) when V T > log ⁡ N V_{T}>\log N , where V T V_{T} is the cumulative second moment of instantaneous per-expert regret averaged with respect to a natural distribution determined by the algorithm. The algorithm is motivated by a continuous time limit using Stochastic Differential Equations. The discrete time analysis uses self-concordance techniques.

Introduction

Prediction with expert advice is a classic topic in online learning,
for which numerous generalizations of the model have been extremely well studied

(
7
)
.
For example, several bounds involving variance of the losses have been known for two decades

(
8
)
,
whereas bounds that compare to a quantile of the experts have been known for over 15 years

(
9
)
.
Ten years ago,
16
posed an open question of developing
an algorithm that simultaneously achieves quantile- and variance-based bounds.
Moreover, he conjectured that NormalHedge
(
9
)
achieves this bound.

The main contribution of this work is a
positive
answer to this question.
We confirm the conjecture that NormalHedge does indeed have a regret bound that is
simultaneously quantile- and variance-based.
The intuition for our proofs comes from a continuous time viewpoint of online learning
that has been explored in various prior work, e.g.,

(
15
;
13
;
17
;
19
;
39
;
20
;
21
)
.
One advantage of this approach is that it ushers in the helpful viewpoint of differential
equations.
A key challenge, however, is discretizing the continuous-time solutions without sacrificing performance

(
17
)
.
Our main technical contribution is a technique to prove perturbation bounds
on derivatives of the NormalHedge potential function via
self-concordance
.
This is a well-studied concept in continuous optimization
(
30
;
35
)

that has also been explored in theoretical statistics
(
2
;
32
)

and more recently in online learning
(
4
)
.
The notions of self-concordance of which we are aware have a global nature that makes them ineffective
in our setting.
To overcome that obstacle, we introduce a notion of
local self-concordance
that is crucial to
our analysis.

Many existing algorithms adapt to easy sequences with “small variance”

Hedge algorithms using potential functions

We focus on hedging algorithms based on
potential functions
\phi(y,t)
, where
y=x_{i}
is the regret coordinate and
t\geq 0
is a continuous “time” variable.

Definition 1
.

Let
\mathcal{D}=\mathbb{R}
or
\mathcal{D}=[y_{0},\infty)
. A function
\phi:\mathbb{R}\times[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
is a
good potential function
on
\mathcal{D}
if it is (1) jointly strictly convex, (2) three-times differentiable, and satisfies:
(3)
\partial_{y}\phi\geq 0
;
(4)
\partial_{t}\phi\leq 0
;
(5)
\lim_{t\to\infty}\phi(y,t)=\inf_{y,t}\phi(y,t)\geq 0
, and
(6)
\phi(\Pi_{\mathcal{D}}(\bm{x}),t)\leq\phi(\bm{x},t)
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
. Furthermore, we assume
(7)
the backwards heat equation

\partial_{t}\phi=-\frac{1}{2}\partial^{2}_{yy}\phi
.

Properties (1-6) are natural, while the motivation for property (7) might not be apparent at this point. We discuss the motivation for property (7) in Section
5
.

Each good potential function
\phi
(with domain
\mathcal{D}
) induces a hedge algorithm
CP
(
\phi,\mathcal{D}
) (see the pseudocode in Figure
1
). The key idea of the algorithm is to maintain a constant

total potential

\Phi(\bm{x},t)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\phi(x_{i},t).

(1)

CP
(
\phi
,
\mathcal{D}
) algorithm

Initialize
t=t_{0},x_{0,i}=0\;\forall i\in[N],V_{0}=0.
Let
\Pi_{\mathcal{D}}
be the projection to
\mathcal{D}
, and
\tilde{\bm{x}}_{0}\leftarrow\Pi_{\mathcal{D}}(\bm{x}_{0}).

For
j=1,...,T

1.

The algorithm plays probability
\bm{p}_{j}
where
p_{j,i}\propto\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\phi(\tilde{x}_{j-1,i},t_{j-1})
for
i\in[N]
.

2.

Nature reveals loss
\ell_{j,i}
for every expert
i\in[N]
.

3.

Algorithm incurs instantaneous regret
\Delta x_{j,i}:=\mathbb{E}_{k\sim\bm{p}_{j}}[\ell_{j,k}]-\ell_{j,i}
for
i\in[N]
.

4.

Algorithm updates
\bm{x}_{j}\leftarrow\bm{x}_{j-1}+\Delta\bm{x}_{j}
;

\tilde{\bm{x}}_{j}\leftarrow\Pi_{\mathcal{D}}(\bm{x}_{j})
.

5.

Algorithm finds
\Delta t_{j}\geq 0

\bm{q}_{j}\in\Delta^{N-1}

q_{j,i}\propto\partial_{xx}\phi(\tilde{x}_{j-1,i},t_{j-1})

Return

.

Properties (1)-(5) in Definition

Properties (1) and (3) imply non-negativity of

Efficient computation for

.

(2)

(

Lemma 2

(Generic regret bound template)
.

Assume
\phi,\mathcal{D}
satisfies Definition
1
.
Let
t
be the time variable that
CP
encounters at any iteration
j
.
Then the corresponding quantile regret at that iteration can be bounded as follows.

\mathrm{Regret}_{\epsilon}~\leq~y\quad\text{where $y$ is the unique solution to}\quad(\epsilon N)\cdot\phi(y,t)=\Phi(\Pi_{\mathcal{D}}(\bm{0}),t_{0}).

(3)

Proof.

The solution
y
exists and is unique
since
\phi(\Pi_{\mathcal{D}}(\bm{0}),t)\leq\phi(\Pi_{\mathcal{D}}(\bm{0}),t_{0})
by property (4) of Definition
1
, and since

y\mapsto\phi(y,t)
is continuous and diverges by (1), (2) and (3).
Let the corresponding regret vector at time
t
be
\bm{x}
.
By (
2
) and non-negativity of
\phi

\Phi(\Pi_{\mathcal{D}}(\bm{0}),t_{0})=\Phi(\Pi_{\mathcal{D}}(\bm{x}),t)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\phi(\Pi_{\mathcal{D}}(x_{i}),t)\geq(\epsilon N)\cdot\phi(\Pi_{\mathcal{D}}(x_{(\epsilon N)}),t).

Thus

(\epsilon N)\cdot\phi(y,t)\geq(\epsilon N)\cdot\phi(\Pi_{\mathcal{D}}(x_{(\epsilon N)}),t)
.
Thus, using property (3) again,

y\geq\Pi_{\mathcal{D}}(x_{(\epsilon N)})\geq x_{(\epsilon N)}=\mathrm{Regret}_{\epsilon},

since
z\mapsto\Pi_{\mathcal{D}}(z)
is non-decreasing.

Results

Our main contribution is to establish

The following three theorems encapsulate our results. Theorem

Theorem 5

(Exponential Weights)
.

CP
(
\phi_{\mbox{\tiny exp}},\mathbb{R}
) with parameter
\eta>0
returns

, such that

\mathrm{Regret}_{\epsilon}(T)\leq\frac{e^{2\sqrt{2}\eta B}\eta}{\sqrt{2}}V_{T}+\frac{\log(1/\epsilon)}{\sqrt{2}\eta}\quad\text{ for any }0<\epsilon\leq 1.

Choosing
\eta\asymp\min\left\{\sqrt{\log(1/\epsilon)/V_{T}},1/B\right\}

\mathrm{Regret}_{\epsilon}(T)=O\left(B\log(1/\epsilon)+\sqrt{V_{T}\log(1/\epsilon)}\right).

Adaptivity and the Open Problem.

This bound involves the cumulative variance
V_{T}
(which can be
\ll T
) but requires tuning

based on the unknown

.
16

Theorem 6

(NormalHedge.BH)
.

Let
t_{0}=\max\{512e^{2}B^{2}\log N,1\}
.

(
\phi_{\mbox{\tiny NH}},\mathbb{R}_{+}
) returns

such that for all

\mathrm{Regret}_{\epsilon}(T)\leq\sqrt{(t_{0}+2V_{T})(\log(t_{0}+2V_{T})+2\log(1/\epsilon))}.

Resolution & “Impossibility”.

Theorem

resolves the COLT’16 open problem
(
16
)
modulo (1) we allow a mild additive

), and (2) our

When is
\bm{q}
different from
\bm{p}
?

For Exponential Weights,
\bm{p}=\bm{q}
. For NormalHedge.BH,

\displaystyle p_{i}

\displaystyle\propto\partial_{x}\phi(x_{i},t)=\frac{x_{i}}{t}\phi(x_{i},t)\propto\sqrt{\frac{x_{i}^{2}}{t}}\phi(x_{i},t)

\displaystyle q_{i}

\displaystyle\propto\partial^{2}_{xx}\phi(x_{i},t)=(\frac{1}{t}+\frac{x_{i}^{2}}{t^{2}})\phi(x_{i},t)\propto\left(1+\frac{x_{i}^{2}}{t}\right)\phi(x_{i},t).

Here

is the

, indicating the cumulative per-expert regret

(clipped to

. While

Theorem 7

(Lower Bound)
.

Consider a “random walk” adversary where each loss is chosen independently as
\ell_{j,i}\in\{-\sigma_{j},\sigma_{j}\}
with equal probability (where

).
There is an explicit constant
\epsilon_{0}
such that,

This immediately implies that

that outputs

. Thus Theorem

5
A stochastic calculus perspective

Our algorithm and its analysis are motivated by previous work on the
problem of combing expert advice in continuous time using stochastic
differential equations (SDEs)
(
15
;
20
;
21
)
. A rigorous analysis of the
SDE-based analysis is beyond the scope of this paper. However, by
providing an informal analysis we justify two choices that might
otherwise be bewildering, namely, the requirement that the potential
function obeys the backwards heat equation, and the weighting of the
variance using the second derivative of the potential function.

SDE’s are typically defined over time (a scalar
t
) and
space (a vector
\bm{x}
). SDEs are studied in the
diffusion scaling

where
B\to 0
,
\|\Delta\bm{x}\|_{\infty}\leq B
and
\Delta t\leq B^{2}
.
Diffusion scaling is formally captured by Itô’s Lemma
(
22
)
, the stochastic calculus analogue of the fundamental theorem of calculus.

Similarly to the
{\sf CP}
algorithm, consider a learner aiming to maintain a constant total potential
\Phi(\bm{x},t)
as in (
1
). In a given round with a regret vector
\bm{x}
changing by
\Delta\bm{x}
, the learner must advance the time
t
by some
\Delta t>0
such that
\Phi(\bm{x}+\Delta\bm{x},t+\Delta t)\leq\Phi(\bm{x},t).
A Taylor expansion yields

\Phi(\bm{x}+\Delta\bm{x},t+\Delta t)-\Phi(\bm{x},t)\approx\langle\nabla_{x}\Phi(\bm{x},t),\Delta\bm{x}\rangle+\partial_{t}\Phi(\bm{x},t)\Delta t+\frac{1}{2}(\Delta\bm{x})^{\textsf{T}}\nabla^{2}_{x}\Phi(\bm{x},t)\Delta\bm{x}.

(6)

As we are operating in the diffusion scaling limit the terms

\partial_{t}\Phi\cdot\Delta t
and

\partial_{x_{i}x_{i}}\Phi\cdot(\Delta x_{i})^{2}
are of the same order
of magnitude, while higher-order terms are negligible in the
limit. Our discrete time analysis essentially controls these
higher-order terms via self-concordance.

The role of the Backwards Heat Equation.

Since the
learners pick probabilities
\mathbf{p}\propto\nabla_{x}\Phi(\bm{x},t)
, one
can show that the first term

\langle\nabla_{x}\Phi(\bm{x},t),\Delta\bm{x}\rangle
in
(
6
) vanishes. Moreover, since
\Phi=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\phi(x_{i},t)
, the last
term in (
6
) simplifies to

\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\partial_{x_{i}x_{i}}\Phi(\bm{x},t)(\Delta x_{i})^{2}
. One can think of this term as the increase in potential due
to the variance in the regret. Thus, to keep the potential
non-increasing it suffices to choose
\Delta t
large enough such that

\partial_{t}\Phi(\bm{x},t)\Delta t+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\partial_{x_{i}x_{i}}\Phi(\bm{x},t)(\Delta x_{i})^{2}\leq 0.

The Backwards Heat Equation (Property (7) in
Definition
1
) guarantees that this
holds with equality as
\Delta t\rightarrow 0
.

Definition of the weights used to define
V_{j}
.

We can plug
\partial_{t}\Phi(\bm{x},t)=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\partial_{x_{i}x_{i}}\Phi(\bm{x},t)
into above, yielding

\Delta t\leq\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\partial_{x_{i}x_{i}}\Phi(\bm{x},t)(\Delta x_{i})^{2}}{\sum_{j=1}^{n}\partial_{x_{j}x_{j}}\Phi(\bm{x},t)}=\mathbb{E}_{i\sim\mathbf{q}}[(\Delta x_{i})^{2}],\text{~ where ~}q_{i}=\frac{\partial_{x_{i}x_{i}}\Phi(\bm{x},t)}{\sum_{j=1}^{n}\partial_{x_{j}x_{j}}\Phi(\bm{x},t)}.

These
q_{i}
values are the probabilities used to calculate
V_{j}
in line 6 of the
CP
algorithm.

6
Analysis

To prove a regret bound for
CP
, we need to bound the growth of

t
. We will do so by showing that

\Delta t=O(\mathbb{E}_{i\sim\mathbf{q}}[(\Delta x_{i})^{2}])

in each iteration. This
requires analyzing the total potential function with perturbed inputs and
calculating how much
\Delta t
needs to be for

\Phi(\widetilde{\bm{x}}+\widetilde{\Delta\bm{x}},t+\Delta t)=\Phi(\widetilde{\bm{x}},t)
.

Recall that in the asymptotic analysis, a critical insight due to the choice of
p_{i}\propto\partial_{x}\phi(x_{i},t)
in
CP
is that
\langle\nabla_{x}\Phi(\bm{x},t),\Delta\bm{x}\rangle=0
. This is a property that we need for the discretized analysis too.

However, we first need to deal with a subtle problem. Due to the projection to
\mathcal{D}
,
\widetilde{\Delta\bm{x}}
is not necessarily equal to
\Delta\bm{x}

6.2
Local Generalized Self-Concordant Analysis

Self-concordance is a classical tool for analyzing Newton methods
(
30
;
35
)
and statistical estimators
(
2
)
. We introduce a local variant for multivariate functions depending on an arbitrary norm
\|\cdot\|_{*}
.

Definition 14

(Local Generalized Self-Concordance)
.

Let
\mathrm{dom}(f)\subset\mathbb{R}^{p}
be open. A
C^{3}
function

satisfies
(M,2)
-generalized self-concordance in norm
\|\cdot\|_{*}
on

if
\forall\bm{x}\in\mathcal{C},\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}

\bigl|\nabla^{3}f(\bm{x})[\mathbf{u},\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}]\bigr|\;\leq\;M\,\|\mathbf{v}\|_{*}\mathbf{u}^{\textsf{T}}\nabla^{2}f(\bm{x})\mathbf{u}.

(9)

Remark.
This definition generalizes
2
;
35
in two ways: (1) it allows a general norm
\|\cdot\|_{*}
rather than Euclidean; and (2) it restricts the property to a local set
\mathcal{C}
. The standard global self-concordance is a special case where

and
\|\cdot\|_{*}=\|\cdot\|_{2}
. These extensions are essential for handling functions that are not globally self-concordant or where perturbations scale with non-Euclidean norms (e.g.,
\ell_{\infty}
).

Self-concordance controls the Hessian’s change via a “sandwich” bound. The following lemma extends the standard bound to our local, general-norm setting (Proof in Appendix
E.1
).

Lemma 15

(Semidefinite ordering under
(M,2)
-GSC)
.

If

satisfies
(M,2)
-generalized self-concordance in norm
\|\cdot\|_{*}
on a convex set
\mathcal{C}
, then for any
x,y\in\mathcal{C}
, we have the semidefinite ordering:

e^{-M\|y-x\|_{*}}\,\nabla^{2}f(x)\;\preceq\;\nabla^{2}f(y)\;\preceq\;e^{M\|y-x\|_{*}}\,\nabla^{2}f(x).

(10)

and
{\log\Phi}=\log Z
, for any direction
\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
,

\nabla p_{i}[\mathbf{w}]=p_{i}\cdot\big(\nabla f_{i}[\mathbf{w}]-\nabla G[\mathbf{w}]\big).

(44)

Indeed,
\nabla p_{i}[\mathbf{w}]=p_{i}\,\nabla f_{i}[\mathbf{w}]-p_{i}\,\nabla G[\mathbf{w}]
by the quotient rule.

Step 2: gradient identity.

Differentiating
{\log\Phi}=\log Z
yields

\nabla{\log\Phi}[\mathbf{w}]=\frac{1}{Z}\sum_{i=1}^{n}e^{f_{i}}\,\nabla f_{i}[\mathbf{w}]=\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}\,\nabla f_{i}[\mathbf{w}].

In particular, for
\mathbf{w}=\mathbf{u}
we have
\nabla f_{i}[\mathbf{u}]=A_{i}
, so

\nabla{\log\Phi}[\mathbf{u}]=\mathbb{E}[A_{I}].

(45)

Step 3: Hessian identity.

Differentiate (
45
) in direction
\mathbf{u}
:

\nabla^{2}{\log\Phi}[\mathbf{u},\mathbf{u}]=\nabla\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}A_{i}\Big)[\mathbf{u}]=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\nabla p_{i}[\mathbf{u}]\;A_{i}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}\,\nabla A_{i}[\mathbf{u}].

Now
\nabla A_{i}[\mathbf{u}]=\nabla^{2}f_{i}[\mathbf{u},\mathbf{u}]=B_{i}
, and by (
44
) with
\mathbf{w}=\mathbf{u}
,

\nabla p_{i}[\mathbf{u}]=p_{i}(A_{i}-\nabla{\log\Phi}[\mathbf{u}])=p_{i}(A_{i}-\mathbb{E}[A_{I}]).

Therefore

\nabla^{2}{\log\Phi}[\mathbf{u},\mathbf{u}]=\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}(A_{i}-\mathbb{E}[A_{I}])A_{i}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}B_{i}=\mathbb{E}[B]+\big(\mathbb{E}[A_{I}^{2}]-(\mathbb{E}[A_{I}])^{2}\big),

which is (
29
).

Step 4: differentiate the Hessian in direction
h
.

From (
29
),

\nabla^{3}{\log\Phi}[\mathbf{u},\mathbf{u},\mathbf{h}]=\nabla\big(\mathbb{E}[B_{I}]\big)[\mathbf{h}]+\nabla\big(\mathrm{Var}(A_{I})\big)[\mathbf{h}].

Step 5: derivative of the expectation term.

Using the product rule,

\nabla\big(\mathbb{E}[B_{I}]\big)[\mathbf{h}]=\nabla\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}B_{i}\Big)[\mathbf{h}]=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\nabla p_{i}[\mathbf{h}]\;B_{i}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}\,\nabla B_{i}[\mathbf{h}].

Here
\nabla B_{i}[\mathbf{h}]=\nabla^{3}f_{i}[\mathbf{u},\mathbf{u},\mathbf{h}]=T_{i}
. Also, by (
44
) with
\mathbf{w}=\mathbf{h}
and since
H_{i}=\nabla f_{i}[\mathbf{h}]
,

\nabla p_{i}[\mathbf{h}]=p_{i}(H_{i}-\nabla{\log\Phi}[\mathbf{h}])=p_{i}(H_{i}-\mathbb{E}[H_{I}]),

where in the last equation we used that
\nabla{\log\Phi}[\mathbf{h}]=\sum_{i}p_{i}\nabla f_{i}[\mathbf{h}]=\mathbb{E}[H_{I}]
.
Hence,

\nabla\big(\mathbb{E}[B_{I}]\big)[\mathbf{h}]=\mathbb{E}[T_{I}]+\mathbb{E}\big[(H_{I}-\mathbb{E}[H_{I}])B_{I}\big]=\mathbb{E}[T_{I}]+\mathrm{Cov}(B_{I},H_{I}).

Step 6: derivative of the variance term.

Since
\mathrm{Var}(A_{I})=\mathbb{E}[A_{I}^{2}]-(\mathbb{E}[A_{I}`^{\prime}])^{2}
, we have

\nabla\big(\mathrm{Var}(A_{I})\big)[\mathbf{h}]=\nabla\mathbb{E}[A_{I}^{2}][\mathbf{h}]-2\,\mathbb{E}[A_{I}]\;\nabla\mathbb{E}[A_{I}][\mathbf{h}].

(46)

We compute each piece. First,

\nabla\mathbb{E}[A_{I}][\mathbf{h}]=\nabla\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}A_{i}\Big)[\mathbf{h}]=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\nabla p_{i}[\mathbf{h}]\;A_{i}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}\,\nabla A_{i}[\mathbf{h}].

Now
\nabla A_{i}[\mathbf{h}]=\nabla^{2}f_{i}[\mathbf{u},\mathbf{h}]=C_{i}
, and
\nabla p_{i}[\mathbf{h}]=p_{i}(H_{i}-\mathbb{E}[H_{I}])
,
so

\nabla\mathbb{E}[A][h]=\mathbb{E}[C]+\mathrm{Cov}(A,H).

(47)

Second,

\nabla\mathbb{E}[A^{2}][h]=\nabla\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}A_{i}^{2}\Big)[h]=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\nabla p_{i}[h]\;A_{i}^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}\,\nabla(A_{i}^{2})[h].

Since
\nabla(A_{i}^{2})[h]=2A_{i}\,\nabla A_{i}[h]=2A_{i}C_{i}
, we get

\nabla\mathbb{E}[A_{I}^{2}][\mathbf{h}]=\mathbb{E}[2A_{I}C_{I}]+\mathrm{Cov}(A_{I}^{2},H_{I}).

(48)

Substitute (
47
)–(
48
) into (
46
):

\nabla\big(\mathrm{Var}(A_{I})\big)[\mathbf{h}]=2\mathbb{E}[A_{I}C_{I}]+\mathrm{Cov}(A_{I}^{2},H_{I})-2\mathbb{E}[A_{I}]\mathbb{E}[C_{I}]-2\mathbb{E}[A_{I}]\mathrm{Cov}(A_{I},H_{I}).

Note that

2\mathbb{E}[A_{I}C_{I}]-2\mathbb{E}[A_{I}]\mathbb{E}[C_{I}]=2\,\mathrm{Cov}(A_{I},C_{I}),

and also the covariance identity

\mathrm{Cov}\big((A_{I}-\mathbb{E}[A_{I}])^{2},H_{I}\big)=\mathrm{Cov}(A_{I}^{2},H_{I})-2\mathbb{E}[A_{I}]\mathrm{Cov}(A_{I},H_{I})

follows from expanding
(A_{I}-\mathbb{E}[A_{I}])^{2}=A_{I}^{2}-2\mathbb{E}[A_{I}]A_{I}+(\mathbb{E}[A_{I}])^{2}

and using
\mathrm{Cov}(\mathbb{E}[A_{I}],H_{I})=0
since
\mathbb{E}[A_{I}]
is a constant.
Therefore,

\nabla\big(\mathrm{Var}(A_{I})\big)[\mathbf{h}]=2\,\mathrm{Cov}(A_{I},C_{I})+\mathrm{Cov}\big((A_{I}-\mathbb{E}[A_{I}])^{2},H_{I}\big).

Step 7: conclude.

Combining Step 5 and Step 6 with Step 4 gives (
30
).
∎

Proof of Lemma
26
.

Recall that

\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y)=\mathbb{E}\big[(X-\mathbb{E}X)(Y-\mathbb{E}Y)\big].

Since
\mathbb{E}(Y-\mathbb{E}Y)=0
, we may also write

\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y)=\mathbb{E}\big[X(Y-\mathbb{E}Y)\big].

Using that
X\geq 0
, we obtain

|\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y)|\leq\mathbb{E}\big[X\,|Y-\mathbb{E}Y|\big].

If
\|Y\|_{\infty}:=\sup|Y|<\infty
, then

|Y-\mathbb{E}Y|\leq|Y|+|\mathbb{E}Y|\leq 2\|Y\|_{\infty}.

Therefore,

\mathbb{E}\big[X\,|Y-\mathbb{E}Y|\big]\leq 2\|Y\|_{\infty}\,\mathbb{E}[X].

∎

Lemma 36

(Shifted self-normalizing bounds)
.

Let
\Delta t_{1},\dots,\Delta t_{T}\geq 0
, let

, and define

t_{j}:=t_{0}+\sum_{i=1}^{j}\Delta t_{i}\qquad(j=1,\dots,T).

\sum_{j=1}^{T}\frac{\Delta t_{j}}{\sqrt{t_{j-1}}}\;\leq\;2\sqrt{1+\frac{M}{t_{0}}}\;\bigl(\sqrt{t_{n}}-\sqrt{t_{0}}\bigr).

Proof of Lemma
36
.

Fix
j\in\{1,\dots,T\}
. Using

\sqrt{t_{j}}-\sqrt{t_{j-1}}=\frac{t_{j}-t_{j-1}}{\sqrt{t_{j}}+\sqrt{t_{j-1}}}=\frac{\Delta t_{j}}{\sqrt{t_{j}}+\sqrt{t_{j-1}}}.

I.1.1
The model selection construction

Theorem 4 concerns the difficulty of
model selection
in contextual
bandits. The goal is to design a single proper algorithm that competes
simultaneously with two nested policy classes

\Pi_{1}\subset\Pi_{2},

where
\Pi_{1}
is small and
\Pi_{2}
is much larger.

The construction uses
K=3
actions and defines

\Pi_{2}=\{\pi_{0},\pi_{1},\dots,\pi_{k}\},\qquad\Pi_{1}=\{\pi_{0}\},

for a large integer
k
. A family of stochastic contextual bandit
environments
\{E_{i^{*}}\}_{i^{*}=1}^{k}
is constructed with the following
properties:

•

In environment
E_{i^{*}}
, policy
\pi_{i^{*}}
is uniquely optimal,
while all other policies incur a small additional loss
\Delta
.

•

Distinguishing which environment
E_{i^{*}}
is in force requires
significant exploration.

•

Excessive exploration causes regret relative to
\Pi_{1}
, while
insufficient exploration prevents identifying the optimal policy
in
\Pi_{2}
.

This creates an unavoidable tradeoff between regret with respect to

\Pi_{1}
and regret with respect to
\Pi_{2}
.

Theorem 4 formalizes this tradeoff.

There exist constants

The theorem shows that no single proper algorithm can achieve

\Pi_{1}
and
\Pi_{2}

This is due to the following construction:
\pi_{0}
only choose action 3 with a constant loss of

. In environment

In environment

I.1.3
The wrapper reduction

We describe the wrapper reduction of Appendix B.4, which converts a
full-information experts algorithm into a proper contextual bandit
algorithm. A key technical step is the duplication of policies in order
to apply a quantile experts guarantee.

Experts representation and duplication.

Each policy
\pi\in\Pi_{2}
is treated as an expert. To compete with a
single policy
\pi_{0}\in\Pi_{1}
using a quantile regret bound, the experts
instance is augmented by duplicating
\pi_{0}
multiple times.

Concretely, let
\Pi_{2}=\{\pi_{0},\pi_{1},\dots,\pi_{k}\}
. We construct a
multiset of experts

\widetilde{\Pi}_{2}=\{\underbrace{\pi_{0},\dots,\pi_{0}}_{k\ \text{copies}},\pi_{1},\dots,\pi_{k}\},

where each copy of
\pi_{0}
behaves identically and incurs the same loss
at every round. The experts algorithm is run on
\widetilde{\Pi}_{2}
.

This duplication ensures that
\pi_{0}
constitutes a constant fraction of
the expert pool. In particular,
\pi_{0}
belongs to the best half of
experts, so the quantile regret guarantee with parameter

\varepsilon_{1}=1/2
applies.

Experts distribution.

At round
t
, the experts algorithm outputs a distribution
p_{t}
over

\widetilde{\Pi}_{2}
. All copies of
\pi_{0}
are interchangeable; we abuse
notation and write
p_{t}(\pi_{0})
for their total mass.

Action selection.

Given context
x_{t}
, define the action distribution

q_{t}(a\mid x_{t})=\frac{\gamma}{3}+(1-\gamma)\sum_{\pi\in\widetilde{\Pi}_{2}:\,\pi(x_{t})=a}p_{t}(\pi),\qquad a\in\{1,2,3\}

where
\gamma\in(0,1)
is the exploration parameter. The wrapper samples
an action
A_{t}\sim q_{t}(\cdot\mid x_{t})
and observes the bandit loss

\ell_{t,A_{t}}
.

Importance-weighted loss estimator.

For each expert
\pi\in\widetilde{\Pi}_{2}
, define the IPS estimator

\widehat{\ell}_{t}(\pi)=\frac{\mathbf{1}\{\pi(x_{t})=A_{t}\}}{q_{t}(A_{t}\mid x_{t})}\,\ell_{t,A_{t}}.

This estimator is unbiased for the true loss
\ell_{t,\pi(x_{t})}
.

To ensure bounded losses, define the scaled loss

\tilde{\ell}_{t}(\pi)=\frac{\gamma}{3}\,\widehat{\ell}_{t}(\pi)\in[0,1],

and feed the full vector
\tilde{\ell}_{t}
to the experts algorithm.

Regret decomposition.

Let
\varepsilon_{1}=1/2
and
\varepsilon_{2}=1/(2|\Pi_{2}|)
. The wrapper
satisfies the following regret bounds:

\mathrm{Regret}(T,\Pi_{m})\;\leq\;\gamma T\;+\;\frac{3(1-\gamma)}{\gamma}\,\mathrm{Regret}^{\mathrm{exp}}_{\varepsilon_{m}}(T;\tilde{\ell}),\qquad m\in\{1,2\}.

where the
\gamma T
is due to the probability spent in exploration and the second term uses the regret of the full-information online learner (dividing that
\gamma/3
normalization factor).

For
\Pi_{1}
, the duplication of
\pi_{0}
ensures that
\pi_{0}
lies in the
top
\varepsilon_{1}
-fraction of experts, so the quantile regret bound
implies regret with respect to
\pi_{0}
. For
\Pi_{2}
, the duplication only doubles the number of policies so
needed and
\varepsilon_{2}=1/2k
corresponds to competing with the single best
policy in
\Pi_{2}
.

The two (contextual bandits) regret upper bounds above allow us to inspect different kinds of regret bounds for full-information online learner and see if it results in a
contradiction
with Theorem
46
. This is how the Theorem 6 of
28
works.

I.2
How the contradiction arises when
r_{t}=p_{t}

We briefly recall the key argument from Appendix B.4 of
28
(the proof of Theorem 6 of
28
)
showing that a second-order experts bound

with variance measured under the learner’s own distribution

leads to a contradiction with Theorem
46
.

I.2.1
The experts assumption used in the paper

The paper assumes the existence of a full-information experts algorithm
satisfying the following second-order quantile bound.

Assumption 47

(Second-order experts bound with self-variance)
.

There exists a constant
G>0
such that for every loss sequence

\ell_{t,i}\in[0,1]
and every
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
,

\mathrm{Regret}^{\mathrm{exp}}_{\varepsilon}(T)\;\leq\;G\sqrt{\Bigl(\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathrm{Var}_{i\sim p_{t}}(\ell_{t,i})\Bigr)\ln(1/\varepsilon)},

where
p_{t}
is the distribution played by the algorithm at round
t
.

The crucial feature is that the variance is taken with respect to
the learner’s own randomization.

I.2.2
Key variance identity when
r_{t}=p_{t}

The critical technical step is that, when the variance is taken under
the learner’s own distribution
p_{t}
, the IPS estimator satisfies

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\mathrm{Var}_{\pi\sim p_{t}}(\tilde{\ell}_{t,\pi})\right]\;\leq\;\frac{\gamma^{2}}{3}.

As a result,

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathrm{Var}_{\pi\sim p_{t}}(\tilde{\ell}_{t,\pi})\right]\;\leq\;\frac{\gamma^{2}T}{3}.

This
quadratic
dependence on
\gamma
is the decisive difference from the arbitrary-
r_{t}
case that we will talk about in Section
I.3
.

I.2.3
Consequences for the wrapper bounds

Applying Assumption
47
and (SV):

Regret to
\Pi_{1}
.

\mathrm{Regret}(T,\Pi_{1})\;\leq\;\gamma T+O\!\left(\frac{1}{\gamma}\sqrt{\gamma^{2}T}\right)=O(\gamma T+\sqrt{T}).

Regret to
\Pi_{2}
.

Let
k=|\Pi_{2}|-1
. Then

\mathrm{Regret}(T,\Pi_{2})\;\leq\;\gamma T\;+\;O\!\left(\frac{1}{\gamma}\sqrt{\gamma^{2}T\ln k}\right)=O(\gamma T+\sqrt{T\ln k}).

Crucially, the
\sqrt{\ln k}
term is
not multiplied
by any power
of
1/\gamma
.

I.2.4
Choosing
\gamma
and deriving the contradiction

Set
\gamma=T^{-1/3}
. Then:

\mathrm{Regret}(T,\Pi_{1})=O(T^{2/3}),\qquad\mathrm{Regret}(T,\Pi_{2})=O(T^{2/3}+\sqrt{T\ln k}).

Thus the Theorem
46
parameter satisfies
C\asymp T^{1/6}
.

Theorem
46
then implies the existence of an environment such that

\mathrm{Regret}(T,\Pi_{2})\;\geq\;\Omega\!\left(\frac{\ln k}{C}\sqrt{T}\wedge T\right)=\Omega\!\left(\ln k\cdot T^{1/3}\right).

Choosing
\ln k\asymp T^{1/2}
yields

\Omega(T^{5/6})\;\leq\;\mathrm{Regret}(T,\Pi_{2})\;\leq\;O(T^{3/4}),

which is a contradiction for large
T
.

In fact the claim of
28
is even stronger in the sense that the second-order quantile regret bound with self-variance not possible for any variance
O(T^{\alpha})
with any
\alpha<1
. This can be obtained by choosing
\gamma=T^{-1/2+\alpha/2}
for any
0<\alpha<1
then the
\sum_{t}\mathrm{Var}_{p_{t}}=O(\gamma^{2}T)=O(T^{\alpha})
, which gives

\mathrm{Regret}(T,\Pi_{1})=O(T^{1/2+\alpha/2}),\qquad\mathrm{Regret}(T,\Pi_{2})=O(T^{1/2+\alpha/2}+\sqrt{T\ln k}).

Then Theorem
46
’s parameter is
C\asymp T^{\alpha/2}
and that

\mathrm{Regret}(T,\Pi_{2})\;\geq\;\Omega\!\left(\frac{\ln k}{C}\sqrt{T}\wedge T\right)=\Omega\!\left(\ln k\cdot T^{1/2-\alpha/2}\right).

Choosing
\ln k=\Theta(T^{\alpha+\delta})
for a small
\delta>1
implies that

\mathrm{Regret}(T,\Pi_{2})=\Omega(T^{(1+\alpha+2\delta)/2}
and
\mathrm{Regret}(T,\Pi_{2})=O(T^{(1+\alpha+\delta)/2})
at the same time, thus giving rise to a contradiction.

I.3
The argument may fail when the variance is w.r.t. a different distribution

We consider the following strengthened experts hypothesis.

Assumption 48

(Second-order experts bound with general variance)
.

There exists a constant
G>0
such that for every full-information experts game
with losses
\ell_{t,i}\in[0,1]
the algorithm can output both a sequence of decisions
(p_{t})_{t=1}^{T}
a sequence of distributions
(r_{t})_{t=1}^{T}

over experts, such that for every
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, the algorithm satisfies

\mathrm{Regret}^{\mathrm{exp}}_{\varepsilon}(T)\;\leq\;G\sqrt{\Bigl(\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathrm{Var}_{i\sim r_{t}}(\ell_{t,i})\Bigr)\ln(1/\varepsilon)}.

This is a generalization of the hypothesis in (
47
) that requires
r_{t}=p_{t}
.

We ask whether Assumption
48
contradicts the contextual bandit
lower bound of Theorem
46
via the proper wrapper
reduction of Appendix B.4.

I.3.1
Step 1: The wrapper and the variance bound

We apply the same wrapper that runs the experts algorithm on scaled IPS losses.

A key difference from the original proof is that the experts hypothesis controls
variance with respect to
arbitrary
distributions
r_{t}
, not the learner’s
own distribution. In this case the best bound one can show is

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathrm{Var}_{\pi\sim r_{t}}(\tilde{\ell}_{t,\pi})\right]\;\leq\;\frac{\gamma T}{3}.

Only a single factor of
\gamma
is recovered. The bound cannot be improved beyond a constant factor because if we take
r_{t}
to be such that we get a uniform distribution of the three actions, which gives a variance of
\gamma^{2}
every time the action with non-trivial reward is chosen.

I.3.2
Step 2: Upper bounds from the experts hypothesis and the resulting lower bound

Using the standard wrapper decomposition, one obtains the following bounds.

Regret to
\Pi_{1}
.

Let
\varepsilon=1/2
. Assumption
48
and (V) imply

\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{Regret}(T,\Pi_{1})]\;\leq\;\gamma T+A\sqrt{\frac{T}{\gamma}},\qquad A=3G\sqrt{\frac{\ln 2}{3}}.

Equivalently,

\mathrm{Regret}(T,\Pi_{1})\;\leq\;C(\gamma)\sqrt{T},\qquad C(\gamma):=\gamma\sqrt{T}+\frac{A}{\sqrt{\gamma}}.

(76)

Regret to
\Pi_{2}
.

Let
k=|\Pi_{2}|-1
and
\varepsilon=1/(2k)
. Then

\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{Regret}(T,\Pi_{2})]\;\leq\;\gamma T+B\sqrt{\frac{T}{\gamma}}\sqrt{\ln(2k)},\qquad B=\sqrt{3}\,G.

(77)

From Theorem
46
(Theorem 4 in
28
) we have that

\Delta=\min\!\left\{\frac{c_{1}\ln k}{C(\gamma)\sqrt{T}},\frac{1}{4}\right\},\qquad c_{1}=\frac{1}{160},

there exists an environment such that

\mathrm{Regret}(T,\Pi_{2})\;\geq\;\frac{\Delta T}{32}=\min\!\left\{\frac{c_{1}\ln k}{32C(\gamma)}\sqrt{T},\;\frac{T}{128}\right\}.

(78)

The proof further assumes the regime

c_{2}C(\gamma)^{2}\;\leq\;\ln k\;\leq\;\frac{T}{2}.

I.3.3
Step 4: Where a contradiction would need to occur

To obtain a contradiction with Theorem
46
, we would need
to choose
\gamma\in(0,1)
and
k
such that the lower bound (
78
) exceeds
the upper bound (
77
). Throughout this section we write
L:=\ln k
and
note that
\ln(2k)\asymp L
up to additive constants, which we ignore for
readability.

We consider separately the two regimes
\gamma<T^{-1/3}
and
\gamma>T^{-1/3}
.
The intuition is that
T^{-1/3}
is precisely the scale at which the two terms

\gamma\sqrt{T}
and
A/\sqrt{\gamma}
in
C(\gamma)
balance.

When
\gamma<T^{-1/3}
,
\sqrt{T/\gamma}>\gamma T
,

A/\sqrt{\gamma}\leq C(\gamma)\leq 2A/\sqrt{\gamma}

and the upper and lower bounds of
\mathrm{Regret}(T,\Pi_{2})
gives

L\sqrt{T\gamma}\lesssim\mathrm{Regret}(T,\Pi_{2})\lesssim\sqrt{TL/\gamma}.

For this to be a contradiction, we need
L\gg 1/\gamma^{2}>T^{2/3}
, but this makes the lower bound
\gg T
, thus invalid.

When
\gamma>T^{-1/3}
,
\sqrt{T/\gamma}<\gamma T
,

\gamma\sqrt{T}\leq C(\gamma)\leq 2\gamma\sqrt{T}

and the upper and lower bounds of
\mathrm{Regret}(T,\Pi_{2})
gives

\frac{L}{\gamma}\lesssim\mathrm{Regret}(T,\Pi_{2})\lesssim\gamma T+\sqrt{\frac{T}{\gamma}}\sqrt{L},

For this to be a contradiction,
\frac{L}{\gamma}\gg\gamma T
and
\frac{L}{\gamma}\gg\sqrt{\frac{T}{\gamma}}\sqrt{L}
, both these two requires
L\gg\max\{\gamma^{2}T,\gamma T\}=\gamma T
. But this would require the lower bound
L/\gamma\gg T
, thus invalid.

To say it differently, there is no choices of
\gamma
and
L
combination that would lead to a contradiction.

I.4
What if the regret bound is
\sqrt{(\log N+V_{T})\log(1/\varepsilon)}
?

In this section we check whether the Marinov–Zimmert wrapper argument can still yield a contradiction
if the full-information experts guarantee has the form

\mathrm{Regret}^{\mathrm{exp}}_{\varepsilon}(T)\;\leq\;G\sqrt{\bigl(\log N+V_{T}\bigr)\log(1/\varepsilon)},\qquad V_{T}:=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathrm{Var}_{i\sim p_{t}}(\ell_{t,i}),

where
N
is the number of experts and the variance is the
self-variance
under
p_{t}
.

Throughout, we take
N=2k
in the wrapper instance and write

L:=\log(2k)\asymp\log k.

I.4.1
Step 1: Wrapper upper bounds under the new hypothesis

We apply the same wrapper reduction (Appendix B.4 of
28
) that runs the experts
algorithm on the scaled IPS losses
\tilde{\ell}_{t,\pi}\in[0,1]
. In the self-variance case one has

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathrm{Var}_{\pi\sim p_{t}}\bigl(\tilde{\ell}_{t,\pi}\bigr)\right]\;\lesssim\;\gamma^{2}T,

(up to absolute constants).

Plugging into the wrapper decomposition yields, for
\Pi_{1}
(quantile
\varepsilon=1/2
so
\log(1/\varepsilon)=\log 2=O(1)
),

\mathbb{E}\mathrm{Regret}(T,\Pi_{1})\;\lesssim\;\gamma T\;+\;\frac{1}{\gamma}\sqrt{L+\gamma^{2}T},

(79)

and for
\Pi_{2}
(quantile
\varepsilon=1/(2k)
so
\log(1/\varepsilon)=\log(2k)=L
),

\mathbb{E}\mathrm{Regret}(T,\Pi_{2})\;\lesssim\;\gamma T\;+\;\frac{1}{\gamma}\sqrt{(L+\gamma^{2}T)\,L}.

(80)

The only change relative to the earlier sections is that the experts term now depends on
L+\gamma^{2}T
rather than only
\gamma^{2}T
.

I.4.2
Step 2: Theorem 4 lower bound in terms of
C=\mathbb{E}\mathrm{Regret}(T,\Pi_{1})/\sqrt{T}

Define the usual parameter

C:=\frac{\mathbb{E}\mathrm{Regret}(T,\Pi_{1})}{\sqrt{T}}.

From (
79
) we have

C\;\lesssim\;\gamma\sqrt{T}\;+\;\frac{1}{\gamma\sqrt{T}}\sqrt{L+\gamma^{2}T}.

(81)

In particular, since the second term is nonnegative, we always have the simple lower bound

C\;\geq\;\gamma\sqrt{T}.

(82)

Now recall Theorem 4 of
28
(our Theorem
46
): in the regime

c_{2}C^{2}\leq L\leq T/2
, there exists an environment
E_{i^{*}}
such that

\mathrm{Regret}_{E_{i^{*}}}(T,\Pi_{2})\;\gtrsim\;\min\left\{\frac{L}{C}\sqrt{T},\ T\right\}.

(83)

Using (
82
) gives the crude but very useful upper bound on the lower bound:

\min\left\{\frac{L}{C}\sqrt{T},\ T\right\}\;\leq\;\min\left\{\frac{L}{\gamma},\ T\right\}.

(84)

I.4.3
Step 3: Why no contradiction can occur

We compare (
84
) with the upper bound (
80
).
First, note that (
80
) always implies

\mathbb{E}\mathrm{Regret}(T,\Pi_{2})\;\geq\;\frac{1}{\gamma}\sqrt{(L+\gamma^{2}T)\,L}\;\geq\;\frac{1}{\gamma}\sqrt{L\cdot L}\;=\;\frac{L}{\gamma}.

(85)

Therefore the
\frac{L}{\gamma}
branch of (
84
) can never exceed the upper bound.

It remains to check the
T
branch. For (
83
) to yield the
T
term, it is necessary that

\frac{L}{C}\sqrt{T}\gtrsim T
, i.e.
L\gtrsim C\sqrt{T}
. Using (
82
) this implies

L\;\gtrsim\;\gamma T\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad\gamma\;\lesssim\;\frac{L}{T}.

But then
\frac{L}{\gamma}\gtrsim T
, and hence (
85
) implies

\mathbb{E}\mathrm{Regret}(T,\Pi_{2})\gtrsim T
as well. Thus even in the
T
branch, the model-selection lower bound
cannot exceed the upper bound.

Conclusion.

The wrapper argument of
28
does
not
contradict an experts guarantee of the form

\sqrt{(\log N+V_{T})\log(1/\varepsilon)}
in the self-variance setting: for every choice of
\gamma
and
k
,
the resulting Theorem 4 lower bound is always bounded above (up to constants) by the wrapper upper bound
(
80
). Equivalently, the model-selection construction becomes vacuous for ruling out this type
of regret guarantee.
