Nitrogen fertilizer value of dehydration products of urine-and pine bark mixtures
Authors: Samukelisiwe P. Vilakazi, A. O. Odindo, P. Muchaonyerwa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The application of human urine in agriculture has gained significant attention due to its high nitrogen (N) content. This study evaluated effects of products derived from dehydration of urine mixed with materials from pine bark (PB) on spinach growth, macronutrient uptake and micronutrients concentrations under glasshouse conditions. The products were prepared by applying urine to various PB products: feedstock (PB), biochar (pyrolysed at 350 and 650 °C) and ash, and various processes: dehydration at 45, 60 °C, and in a glasshouse. The products after dehydration at 45 and 60 °C were applied to 2 kg of soil (0–20 cm depth) at recommended N rate for spinach. The pot trial ran for 84 days with plant sampling at 6 and 12 weeks for dry matter measurements while macronutrient and micronutrients analysis were done for the combined biomass (6 and 12 weeks sampling). The residual soil samples were analysed for pH and extractable phosphorus (P). The experiment was repeated with PB products dehydrated in the glasshouse. The products increased dry matter, which was comparable to the positive control (ammonium nitrate). The urine-based products increased dry matter and N uptake of spinach. Plant micronutrients concentration were within permissible limit, although manganese was high in PB feedstock and biochar dehydrated in the glasshouse under controlled conditions. Overall, urine-derived products from pine bark show potential as alternatives to synthetic N fertilizers for smallholder farmers. However, monitoring sodium and manganese levels in plant tissue is necessary to optimise crop growth and prevent toxicity. In addition, the concentrations of these elements in the soil should be investigated.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-55193-6.

Introduction

Sustainable sanitation poses significant challenge in rural and peri-urban areas1. Improper disposal of urine can result in excess nitrogen (N) entering waterways, leading to eutrophication and algal blooms, with severe consequences on both human and ecosystem health2. On the other hand, N is a crucial yield-determining nutrient required by plants; consequently, the high N content of urine has drawn significant attention for its potential agricultural application3. Human urine has been successfully used as fertiliser for maize4, tomato5, cucumber6 and spinach7. A study conducted by Pradhan et al.5, in a pot trial, showed that addition of urine+ wood ash to soil produced > 3 fold improvement in fruits yield (tomato) compared to the control. Field and pot studies have further shown that nitrogen in human urine is rapidly plant-available and can achieve fertiliser use efficiencies comparable to mineral nitrogen sources, confirming its agronomic suitability for crop production4,5,8. This serves to suggest that urine and urine-based products could be used as source of N in agricultural production systems.

The ECOSAN technologies, such as urine-diverting dry toilets, enable source separation of urine and faeces, facilitating the recycling of nutrients for agricultural purposes. Literature indicates that human urine is richer in nutrients than faeces, with most nutrients present in readily available ionic forms9. Human urine, which contains high levels of essential nutrients like nitrogen (N), phosphorus (P), and potassium (K), has been recognized as a valuable fertiliser resource9,10. However, the high-water content (approximately 97%) and alkaline pH of urine present challenges for its direct use as a fertiliser, complicating storage, transportation, application logistics, and associated costs9,11,12. In this study, artificial human urine, formulated to represent the chemical composition of urine from a healthy individual, was used to allow controlled analysis of nitrogen recovery and retention during dehydration. Ammonia losses and high volumes of water compromise the value of urine as a fertiliser, especially considering the need to store, transport and apply it at predetermined N rates. Dehydration of the urine, under conditions that limit ammonia volatilisation, is required. Preserving nitrogen during urine stabilisation is critical from an agronomic perspective, as nitrogen losses directly reduce fertiliser effectiveness and crop response following soil application9,13.

The key drivers for N recovery are to prevent pollution and produce a valuable and easy-to-handle fertiliser that will improve soil fertility and productivity. Different treatment options for N recovery from human urine exists, including electrolysis, nitrification and struvite formation14. However, high technological and equipment requirements and costs, associated with such methods, limit their practical application especially at small scale. Alkaline amendments such as Birch wood ash (pH > 12.5) have been found to stabilise urine, minimise nitrogen losses, and allow for effective dehydration9. Similarly, biochar derived from various feedstocks, such as agricultural residues, has gained attention for its ability to adsorb nutrients, improve soil fertility, and sequester carbon15. By improving nutrient retention and soil physical properties, biochar amendments have been shown to enhance nutrient use efficiency and crop productivity, particularly in low fertility soils15,16. Preliminary experiments showed that pine bark (PB) and its biochars and ash, were effective in storing N from artificial human urine17. Although there are several studies on the recovery of N from urine by dehydration of solid materials there is limited literature on the agronomic aspects of such urine-based products. An incubation study indicated that the application of the urine-based products, generated by dehydration of mixtures of urine with materials from PB under different conditions, increased concentrations of ammonium, nitrate and extractable phosphorus (P) in the soil17. These nutrients are expected to contribute significantly to crop productivity when used as a fertiliser. The efficacy of the urine-based products from PB materials still needs to be assessed as N sources for plants.

While previous studies have investigated urine stabilisation using ash or biochar, limited attention has been given to how they influence nutrient recovery and subsequent fertiliser performance. Furthermore, direct comparisons between pine bark feedstock materials, their derived biochars, and ash as urine stabilisation agents remain scarce. This study addresses these gaps by evaluating urine-derived fertilisers stabilised with pine bark feedstock, pine bark biochar, and pine bark ash produced at contrasting dehydration temperatures. By linking nitrogen recovery to plant nutrient uptake under controlled conditions, this work assists on current understanding of agronomic potential of urine-based fertilisers.

Depending on soil conditions, crops can utilize most of the released nutrients, leading to improvements in yield. In addition to soil characteristics, effectiveness of the products, may be controlled by their characteristics, which are controlled by dehydration conditions and the properties of the solid material from which they are made. The objective of this study was to determine the effect urine-based products of feedstock, biochar and ash from pine bark, when dehydrated at 45 and 60 °C and under glasshouse conditions, on spinach productivity on amended soils.

Method and materials

Soil

The glasshouse study was conducted at the University of KwaZulu Natal (UKZN), Pietermaritzburg campus (29° 37’ 33.9’’ S; 30° 24’ 14’E), South Africa. The soil used in this study was collected from a grassland, which had no history of cultivation, at the Ukulinga Research Farm, UKZN. The soil was loam in texture and classified as Westleigh18, which was translated to Plinthic Acrisols19. Bulk soil samples were collected from the 0–20 cm depth, mixed, homogenised, air-dried, and sieved (< 2 mm), before analysis. The clay content of the samples was estimated using the near infrared reflectance (HTS-XT, Bruker, Germany), while total carbon (C) and nitrogen (N) were measured using the LECO Trumac CNS Autoanalyser (Leco corporation, 3000, Lakeview, Ave, ST, Joseph, MI, USA). Available phosphorus (P) was extracted with the AMBIC-2 method20 and analysed with a UV/VIS spectrophotometer following the molybdenum blue method21. Soil pH, exchangeable bases and acidity were analysed using standard methods22. The characteristics of the soils are shown in Table 1.

Urine-based product production and characterisation

Pine bark (Pinus patula) waste (PB), and its biochars and ash, were used in this study. The PB was ground to < 2 mm particle size using a Retsch KG 5657 HAAN (West Germany) machine and stored in plastic bags. Portions of the PB samples were used to produce ash through combustion in a muffle furnace at 750 °C for 6 h. Other portions were pyrolysed at 350 and 650 °C for 2 h in a muffle furnace, with limited oxygen. The characteristics of the materials used were as reported by Vilakazi et al.23. A summary of the material results is provided in supplementary Table 1. Another PB biochar was produced using a two-container kiln, which was fabricated by the School of Engineering, at UKZN. Proximate analysis results for this biochar showed 58.3% volatile matter, 40.8% fixed carbon and 0.82% ash and the total C, N, P and K were 65, 0.32, 0.005 and 0.355%, respectively. Its characteristics fell between the two biochars produced at 350 and 650 °C. The pH was determined in KCl at a ratio of 1:1024 and calcium carbonate equivalent (CCE) was evaluated following the method by25. The pH and CCE were 4.32 and 8.92%, respectively.

The artificial human urine (AHU) used this study had 12.2 g L−1 urea, 0.17 g L−1 uric acid, 0.45 g L−1 creatinine, 1.49 g L−1 tri-sodium citrate, 3.17 g L−1 sodium chloride, 2.25 g L−1 potassium chloride, 0.805 g L−1 ammonium chloride, 0.17 g L−1 sodium bicarbonate, 0.445 g L−1 calcium chloride, 0.5 g L−1 magnesium sulphate, 1.29 g L−1 disodium sulphate, 0.5 g L−1 monosodium phosphate and 0.55 g L−1 disodium phosphate26. The concentrations of the multiple components AHU fell within the physiological ranges observed in normal human urine26.

For the urine-based products, 50 g of either feedstock, biochar or ash were added initially to each storage 1.8 L rectangular, heat resistant glass containers with dimensions (17 × 13 × 5 cm) and surface area of 0.0742 m2. The area covered in the glass container by the material used for dehydration (length × breadth) was measured. The corresponding urine volume was calculated as Area × 5.1 L m⁻² for 60 °C or Area × 4.5 L m⁻² for 45 °C. The average of these volumes was then applied (33 mL for 5.1 L m⁻²; 29 mL for 4.5 L m⁻²) to ensure that all materials dehydrated at the same temperature received an equivalent amount of urine and the urine was added followed by stirring of the mixtures for 5 s9, and dehydration in ovens at 45 and 60 °C to allow rapid dehydration. Fresh urine was applied weekly during the first 14 days, after which applications were twice per week until day 28, at a rate of 4.5 (45 °C) and 5.1 L m− 2 (60 °C), with dehydration after each addition. The experiment ran for 28 days (six urine additions), with 29 mL (45 °C) and 33 mL (60 °C) per addition. The ovens could only produce small quantities of product at a time, some products were produced dehydrating mixtures of urine with PB feedstock, biochar or ash under glasshouse conditions (G), with the cooling system switched off and temperature was not controlled, resulting in temperatures ranging from a minimum of 9 °C to a maximum of 57 °C. This approach allowed urine-based products to be produced at a larger scale than could be achieved with ovens, with intentions to simulate field solar dehydration. During the study period the air temperature (HMP60, Vaisala, Sweden) were measured and recorded on a datalogger (CR3000, Campbell Scientific, Utah, Logan, USA). Each medium (7 kg) was added initially to 52 L plastic (polypropylene) basins, followed by AHU application at a rate of 5.1 L m− 2, and after each application the mixtures were stirred and allowed to dehydrate. Although materials were processed to a similar particle size, porosity and bulk density were not directly measured and thus were not used to adjust application rates. Total carbon (C) and N were measured. The results for the end products are shown in Table 2. The recovery of N from added urine was significantly higher in biochar 650, exceeding 100% at both dehydration temperatures (45 and 60 °C), compared to ash G. The pH was also significantly higher for ash dehydrated at 45 and 60 °C than for feedstock dehydrated at the glasshouse and 60 °C. Ash treatments had significantly lower carbon (C) content compared to biochar 650 60, which had 80% C. The highest N content was recorded for biochar 650 60 (2.97%), while ash G had the lowest N (1.38%). The C: N ranged from 1.26 to 27.9, with biochar 650 dehydrated at 45 °C and 60 °C having significantly higher C: N than ash at all dehydration temperatures.

Experimental design

Pot trial experiment 1

The experiment was conducted under glasshouse conditions at about 25 °C for 84 days, following a randomised complete block design with four replicates. The treatments consisted of PB feedstock products dehydrated at 45 °C and 60 °C (PB 45 and PB 60, respectively); pine bark ash products dehydrated at 45 °C and 60 °C (PB ash 45 and PB ash 60, respectively); pine bark biochar produced at 350 °C, then dehydrated at 45 °C and 60 °C (PB 350 45 and PB 350 60, respectively); and pine bark biochar produced at 650 °C, then dehydrated at 45 °C and 60 °C (PB 650 45 and PB 650 60, respectively). Negative (no urine products; -ve) and positive (mineral N; +ve) controls were also included. A summary of the treatments used are shown in Table 3. The treatments were applied to 2 kg of soil, in pots, at the recommended N rate for spinach (equivalent to 100 kg ha− 1). The mineral N in the positive control was applied as ammonium nitrate (NH4NO3) to achieve a rate equivalent to 100 kg N ha− 1 for optimum yield. Equivalent amounts of 124 kg P ha− 1 and 117 kg K ha− 1 were applied as NaH2PO4 and KCl, respectively20. The pots had saucers underneath to trap any leachate, which would then be returned to the pots. The amended soils were moistened to 100% water holding capacity (WHC). Two spinach seedlings were planted to each pot and allowed to grow for 84 days. Distilled water was used to correct moisture based on the amount lost through weight reduction. After 6 weeks (42 days), the spinach was harvested by cutting 1 cm above the soil surface and leaving two younger leaves allowed to grow. The harvested shoots were washed to remove soil particles, and bloated with a paper towel, before weighing (fresh weight). The biomass was then dried at 60 ˚C for three days and weighed (dry weight). The same was done after 12 weeks, when all the spinach leaves were harvested. The dried plant tissue samples harvested at 6 and 12 weeks were combined to form a composite sample, ground (< 1 mm) using a blender and analysed for total N, P, K, calcium (Ca), magnesium (Mg), sodium (Na), zinc (Zn), copper (Cu), manganese (Mn) and iron (Fe). The N, P and K uptake were calculated from their tissue concentrations and the dry matter yield. After harvesting at 12 weeks the soils were analysed for pH and extractable P. The soils were oven dried over night at 105 °C for moisture correction of the soil P results.

Pot trial experiment 2

The second pot trial experiment was set-up the same way as the first one (Sect. 2.3.1) in terms of management. Urine-based products from PB feedstock, biochar (from kiln) and ash dehydrated in the glasshouse (PB G, PB biochar G and PB ash G) were used as treatments, with all management and measurements being the same. The treatments evaluated in this study are summarised in Table 3.

Analysis

Tissue N was analysed using the Leco Trumac CNS Autoanalyser (Leco corporation, 3000, Lakeview, Ave, ST, Joseph, MI, USA). For other elements, the oven- dry and ground spinach sample (0.5 g) was placed into a crucible, treated with 10 ml of concentrated HNO3 and HCl (1:3)27, and digested for one hour using a MARS CEMS microwave digester. Thereafter, the samples in the digest were analysed for P, Ca, Mg K, Fe, Mn, Zn and Cu concentration using an inductively coupled plasma atomic emission spectrometry (Varian 720-ES ICP-AES). The N and P uptake were calculated using Eq. 1.

Residual soil analysis (pH and P)

Soil pH and extractable P were analysed in the residual soils. Soil pH was measured in KCl at a ratio of 1:5 using 1 M KCl. Extractable P was read using the molybdenum-blue method21.

Statistical analysis

All data was analysed using GenStat 18th edition by subjecting them to the one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). The Fishers protected least significant difference (LSD) was used to compare the treatment means at 5% level.

Results

Dry weight

Application of urine-based products increased the dry matter yield of spinach, in both experiments (Figs. 1a, b). The positive control yielded (5.9 g pot− 1) the highest dry matter (p < 0.05) comparable to products PB ash and PB biochar 650, which were all higher than the negative control (Fig. 1a). Dry matter yield for PB biochar 350 and PB 45 was similar to the negative control (3 g pot− 1). In the second experiment, all the products from PB materials dehydrated at the glasshouse had similar spinach dry matter to the positive control and were significantly higher (p < 0.05) than the negative control (3 g pot⁻¹) (Fig. 1b). The PB ash (6.7 g pot⁻¹) resulted in significantly higher (p < 0.05) dry matter yield than that from PB feedstock product.

Cu, Zn, Fe, and Mn concentration

In Experiment 1, the Cu concentration did not vary significantly across the treatments, while the Zn concentration was higher (p < 0.05) in PB feedstock, PB 350 60, PB 650 60 and PB ash 60 than the negative control (Table S2). Tissue Fe concentration was highest in PB ash 45 which was significantly (p < 0.05) greater than the concentration in the positive control. The PB feedstock had higher Mn concentration than other treatments, except for PB 650 60.

Both Cu and Zn concentration in Experiment 2 were significantly (p < 0.05) higher in the PB ash treatment than all other treatments, which had low concentrations (Table S3). The PB biochar treatment exhibited a higher Fe concentration than all other treatments. The PB biochar treatment also had the highest (p < 0.05) Mn concentration among treatments, except for PB ash, which showed a similar Mn concentration.

Soil pH and extractable P after spinach harvest

In Experiment 1, there was no significant difference in soil pH across the treatments (Table 6). Extractable P was significantly higher (p < 0.05) in the PB feedstock and PB ash treatments compared to the negative control, PB biochar at 350 °C, and PB 650 45. Discussion

The apparent N recovery exceeding 100% in biochar 650 most likely reflects the accumulation of analytical and mass balance uncertainties rather than a true increase in N (Table 2). The consistently higher recovery relative to ash G indicates that biochar provides a more effective medium for N retention, likely due to its sorptive capacity and surface functional groups that can stabilise N24. In contrast, the significantly higher pH observed in ash treatments (Table S1) would favour the conversion of ammonium to ammonia, thereby enhancing volatilisation losses during dehydration. This is consistent with the lower N contents measured in ash (Table 2) and supports the likelihood that substantial NH₃ losses occurred, particularly at elevated temperature. Nitrogen loss estimates in this study were indirect, based on the difference between applied and recovered nitrogen. Direct quantification of NH₃ volatilisation and other loss pathways was not conducted, which introduces some uncertainty in the interpretation of loss mechanisms. Future work should incorporate comprehensive nitrogen mass balance analyses to better constrain these pathways.

The markedly lower C content in ash compared to biochar 650 (notably 80% C at 60 °C) further reflects the extensive loss of organic materials during ashing, resulting in a mineral-dominated material with limited capacity to retain N. The wide range in C: N ratios, with biochar treatments shows substantially higher values, suggests the formation of more carbon dense and potentially recalcitrant materials, whereas ash products are comparatively depleted in both C and N.

Collectively, these results highlight that both processing pathway (ashing vs. pyrolysis) and dehydration temperature strongly influence N retention and, consequently, the nutrient retention and composition of the final products. Although normalisation accounts for input differences, we note that higher absolute nitrogen loads may influence sorption saturation or volatilization kinetics; however, the consistency of trends in normalised recovery suggests material properties are the primary driver of observed differences.

The large difference in C reflects the effect of dehydration temperature on the relative concentration of carbon in the final products. The addition of urine might have led to the loss of some functional groups. At 60 °C, more moisture removal and volatilisation of labile N containing compounds likely occurred, resulting in a proportional enrichment of carbon in the remaining materials. In contrast, the lower dehydration temperature (45 °C) would retain more moisture and volatile components, thereby lowering the relative C concentration.

The higher dry matter yield after application of urine-based products, compared to the negative control, can be attributed to the greater availability and uptake of N from these nitrogen-rich products. This rapid release increased N availability and uptake by the spinach resulting in greater growth, and yield. While these results indicate improved plant yield in urine enriched treatments, they show correlations rather than direct causation.

In the first experiment, while N uptake could have contributed to biomass production, other factors could have also contributed. This is because only products from PB biochars (both 350 and 650 °C) resulted in greater N uptake than the negative control. The results may not be explained by the uptake of other macronutrients either (Table 4). The observed effects suggest that the biochars may have had more positive effects on soil moisture than the other treatments, resulting in greater biomass production. The current study did not measure soil moisture; future research including this parameter would better substantiate the underlying processes. Biochars are known to have high water retention28,29.

The similar dry matter yield in the PB products dehydrated in the glasshouse to the positive control, could be explained by N uptake (Fig. 2b), and to some extent K uptake but not P uptake (Table 5). When applied at the same N rate these urine-based products dehydrated in the glasshouse had similar effects as the mineral fertiliser, indicating that the release of the urine N is as rapid as mineral N fertiliser. Comparable yield and nitrogen uptake responses between urine-based products and mineral fertiliser observed in this study are consistent with previous findings showing that human urine can serve as an effective substitute for inorganic nitrogen fertilisers in vegetable production systems30,31. The rapid increase in nitrogen availability may be explained by the presence of urea in the products, which hydrolysed quickly when incorporated into moist soil. Although this was not quantified in the current study, it will be good to explore in future research. To support these findings, an incubation experiment, PB ash had the highest mineral N release (equivalent to 93 kg N ha− 1), which was sufficient for growing spinach for optimum yield17, since plant preferentially takes up the nitrate-N form32. The findings indicate that these treatments have potential as alternatives to synthetic nitrogen fertilizers.

While the study was performed in a controlled environment, we anticipate comparable results in field conditions. This approach would be beneficial for smallholder farmers, as they often have limited access to synthetic nitrogen fertilizers. Similar findings were observed in tomatoes, where application wood ash+ human urine significantly increased yield5. Other studies have also reported yield increase in maize4,31, carrot, beetroot10, and cucumber6 following the application of human urine. These yield improvements were generally attributed to the higher availability of N provided by urine. The advantage with the PB based products compared to urine is that the volume is lower, making it easier to store transport and apply, while the limited ammonia loss ensured that greater benefit could be derived from the urine N.

The negative control consistently showed lower dry matter than other treatments (Fig. 1a, b), which can be attributed to limited N uptake (Fig. 2a, b). This resulted from the insufficient soil N content (0.165%) and a lower pH (5.08) (Table 1). These findings were further supported by low pH values presented in Tables 5 and 6. The pine bark (PB) products are characterised as having low nutrient concentration (P and K), which could likely explain similar P and K uptake in all the PB treatments (Table 4). Human urine has high Na concentration33,34. The PB ash dehydrated at the glasshouse showed high Na concentration, plausibly because when ash is being applied to the soil it releases all the nutrients. However, it is well documented that high Na affects plant growth and yield3,34. Despite this, spinach did not show negative effects, possibly because, it is considered a moderately salt-tolerant crop35,36. These results suggest that urine-based products from PB ash should be applied only to crops tolerant of high Na concentrations, and their continuous use should be monitored to prevent sodium accumulation in soil, especially in arid areas where salt leaching is limited. However, these observations need to be validated under field conditions. Over the long term, increased Na accumulation could potentially lead to soil sodicity and negatively affect soil structure, nutrient availability, and overall soil fertility37.

The plant micronutrients (including Cu, Fe, and Zn) found in spinach were all in the permissible limit38. The high Mn concentration (Table S2 and 3) after fertilisation with PB feedstock products and PB biochar dehydrated at the glasshouse (Table S3), was above the 500 ppm tissue threshold38,39. The high tissue Mn was attributed to the acidifying effect of PB feedstock due to presence of acidic functional groups40, while for PB biochar it could be caused by the nitrification process, which increases the solubility of Mn40. Excess available soil Mn leads to bio-accumulation in plants41 and inhibits chlorophyll biosynthesis, photosynthesis rate42, CO2 assimilation and stomatal conductance43. It further changes the uptake, redistribution and use of nutrients as N, P, Ca, Mg, and Fe, which are essential for normal plant growth44. Thus, Mn toxicity decreases the biomass yield. Spinach is considered a hyper-accumulator of heavy metal and toxic ions45, which explains the observed high yield irrespective of Mn solubility. Similar finding were reported were spinach yield was not affected by Mn toxicity following application of wastewater45,46. Although higher than the normal sufficiency range, the Mn levels did not reach phytotoxic thresholds, suggesting that the urine-based fertilisers supplied essential micronutrients without compromising crop or food safety38. The concentrations of micronutrients in spinach tissue following application of urine-based products were within permissible limits for human consumption47.

Although Mn and Na concentrations were elevated in PB ash and glasshouse-dehydrated products, these results represent short-term glasshouse conditions and may not directly reflect field scenarios. Repeated application of these products could potentially lead to soil salinity increases and Mn buildup, which were not assessed in this study. Long-term field trials are therefore necessary to evaluate cumulative effects on soil health and food safety before recommending widespread use.

At the end of the experiment, PB ash (18%) showed high soil pH due to its high liming potential, these also increased extractable P a resulted of decrease in soil acidity, which improved P availability25,48. This also aligns with the observed increase in P uptake. The residual soil treated with PB feedstock and PB ash had high extractable P, this implies that the remaining nutrient (P) can be used for the subsequent crop. Residual increases in soil pH and available phosphorus following application of pine bark-based urine products suggest potential long-term soil fertility benefits, which are agronomically important for sustaining crop productivity beyond a single growing season49. To achieve optimal results, it is recommended that urine-derived fertilizers be carefully prepared to provide a balanced supply of essential nutrients, applied based on crop requirements, and used with consideration of soil pH to ensure maximum nutrient availability. Additionally, applying these fertilisers which are rich in C can improve soil fertility, and enhance nutrient use efficiency.

Conclusion

Application of the urine-based products, increased nitrogen uptake and dry matter yield of spinach, soil pH and available P compared to the unamended control. When the urine PB products are applied, they provide positive impact on spinach yields, which will be beneficial for smallholder farmers, who do not usually apply sufficient mineral N fertiliser due to high cost. However, using urine-based products increases Na and Mn levels in plant tissues, so care must be taken to apply them only to salt-tolerant crops and there is need for monitoring these two elements in plants and soil where these products are used. Similar studies should be conducted in field conditions to observe the effects in an uncontrolled environment.
