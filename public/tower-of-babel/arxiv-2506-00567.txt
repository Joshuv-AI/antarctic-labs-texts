Existence and Properties of Frames of Iterations
A. Aguilera, C. Cabrelli, F. Negreira, V. Paternostro

Abstract
. Let T T be a bounded operator acting on an infinite-dimensional Hilbert space. We provide necessary and sufficient conditions for the existence of a Parseval frame of iterations generated by T T . Based in this result, we give a new proof of a known characterization concerning the existence of (not necessarily Parseval) frames of iterations. As a consequence of our results, we obtain that certain operators lack Parseval frames despite possessing frames of iterations. In addition, we introduce the index of a bounded operator T T as the minimal number of vectors required to generate a frame via its iterations. We obtain the exact value of this index for both Parseval frames and general frames of iterations, and we give a constructive method for generating such frames. Assuming that T T satisfies the conditions ensuring that both T T and T ∗ T^{*} admit frames of iterations, we show how to construct Parseval frames generated by iterations of T T and also by iterations of T ∗ T^{*} . This construction relies on universal models in vector-valued Hardy spaces and the theory of universal dilations. Furthermore, we provide necessary and sufficient conditions under which the frames generated by T T and T ∗ T^{*} are similar, in terms of the inner function of the associated model in the Hardy space of the operator T T .

1.
Introduction

Let
{\mathcal{H}}
be a complex, separable, infinite-dimensional Hilbert space. We are interested in understanding which operators
T
in
{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}})
admit a
frame of iterations
; that is, a frame of the form
\{T^{n}v_{i}\}_{i\in I,n\geqslant 0}
where
\{v_{i}\}_{i\in I}
is an at most countable collection of vectors of
{\mathcal{H}}
(called generators).

Understanding the conditions under which an operator
T\in{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}})
admits such a frame is a central question, driven by its deep connections to operator dynamics and its potential across various applications.

Recall that a collection of vectors
\{x_{i}\}_{i\in I}\subseteq{\mathcal{H}}
is a
frame
for
{\mathcal{H}}
if there exist constants
A,B>0
such that the inequalities

A{\left\|{x}\right\|}^{2}\leqslant\sum_{i\in I}{\left|{{\langle{x,x_{i}}\rangle}}\right|}^{2}\leqslant B{\left\|{x}\right\|}^{2},

hold for all
x\in{\mathcal{H}}
. When
A=B=1
we say that
\{x_{i}\}_{i\in I}
is a
Parseval frame
.
If
\{x_{i}\}_{i\in I}
is a frame for
{\mathcal{H}}
there always exists another frame
\{y_{i}\}_{i\in I}
for
{\mathcal{H}}
such that every vector
x
in the space can be written as
x=\sum_{i\in I}{\langle{x,x_{i}}\rangle}\,y_{i}
. The family
\{y_{i}\}_{i\in I}
in general is not unique, which is a crucial property for applications.

In this article we settle the problem of the existence of frames of iterations, building upon previous results while further developing the theory in this direction. In particular, we consider the problem of existence of Parseval frames of iterations and give necessary a sufficient conditions for their existence.These results also answer whether there exist operators that do not admit a frame of iterations.

We explore several fundamental questions related to frames of iterations other than the existence of such collections. In particular, we analyse what we call the
index
of an operator, that is, the minimal number of vectors required to construct these frames. We obtain the exact value of this index for general and Parseval frames of iterations.

Beyond establishing these theoretical conditions, our proof techniques provide a constructive framework for generating frames of iterations for a given operator. We further develop these constructions to yield optimal frames of iterations, characterized by the use of a minimal number of generators.

Furthermore, we address the problem of whether the property of having a frame of iterations implies the same for the adjoint operator. That is, assuming that there exists a set of vectors

\{v_{i}\}_{i\in I}\subseteq{\mathcal{H}}
such that
\{T^{n}v_{i}\}_{i\in I,n\geqslant 0}
forms a frame, does there always exist another set of vectors
\{w_{j}\}_{j\in J}\subseteq{\mathcal{H}}
such that
\{(T^{*})^{n}w_{j}\}_{j\in J,n\geqslant 0}
is also a frame of
{\mathcal{H}}
?
If such a frame exists, what is the relation with the one generated by
T
? Are they similar in the sense that there exists an isomorphism (not necessarily unitary) that maps one to the other? We were able to answer these questions.

The strategy to derive these results, was to employ functional models defined in Hardy spaces with multiplicity. By exploiting the rich structure of these spaces, we solved the central issues in this setting and subsequently transferred the results to the abstract framework.

The motivation for addressing these questions arises from the field of
Dynamical Sampling
. In dynamical sampling, a signal evolving over time is sampled at fixed spatial locations at different time instances. When the spatial locations are sparse, the challenge lies in reconstructing the complete signal at the initial time (time zero) using these space-time samples. This involves compensating for the missing spatial information by leveraging samples taken at future times. The problem of dynamical sampling can be framed in terms of determining when the orbits of an operator
T
(the evolution operator) form a frame, specifically a frame of iterations (see
[
6
]
). There is a rich and extensive literature in dynamical sampling and it is an active area of research. See for instance
[
4
,
6
,
3
,
7
,
10
,
15
,
11
,
13
,
25
,
29
,
1
,
2
,

]
.

1.1.
Organization of the paper and main results

In the present work, we first examine the problem of the existence of Parseval frames of iterations. We are able to characterize exactly which operators have the property of having a Parseval frame of iterations.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
T\in{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}})
. There exists a set of vectors
\{v_{i}\}_{i\in I}\subseteq{\mathcal{H}}
such that
\{T^{n}v_{i}\}_{i\in I,n\geqslant 0}
is a Parseval frame in
{\mathcal{H}}
if and only if
{\left\|{T}\right\|}\leqslant 1
and
(T^{*})^{n}v\to 0
as
n\to+\infty
for all
v\in{\mathcal{H}}
.

An operator whose norm is at most
1
is called a
contraction
. The existence of frames of iterations, not necessarily Parseval, has been already characterized in
[
10
]
.

Theorem 1.2

(
[
10
]
)
.

Let
T\in{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}})
. Then there exists a frame of iterations by
T
if and only if
T
is similar to a contraction and
(T^{*})^{n}v\to 0
as
n\to+\infty
for all
v\in{\mathcal{H}}
.

In this paper, we give a different proof of Theorem
1.2
using Theorem
1.1
. Our proof exploits the connection between frames of iterations and
model spaces
, i.e. spaces of analytic vector-valued functions in
L^{2}({\mathbb{T}},{\mathcal{K}})
whose orthogonal complement in
H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}}
is shift-invariant. The origin of this idea can likely be traced back to the work of Rota
[
28
]
. This connection is crucial for the results developed in the subsequent sections.

The property of being similar to a contraction has attracted significant interest in the operator theory and functional analysis communities. In fact, Paulsen showed in
[
24
]
that this condition is equivalent to being
completely polynomially bounded
. On the other hand, the property that the powers of an operator converge pointwise to
0
in norm is called
strong stability
. These two properties are independent: it was shown in
[
21
]
that there exist strongly stable operators that are not completely polynomially bounded, and, clearly, any isometry (which is a contraction) is not strongly stable.

In Section
2
, we establish the fundamental concepts and definitions required for this paper. Section
3
is dedicated to presenting the proofs of the previously mentioned existence results, as well as a methodology for constructing examples of frames of iterations.

In the case that the operator
T
has a frame of iterations, it is relevant to ask, what is the minimum number of vectors in
\mathcal{H}
necessary to get such a frame.
For a bounded operator
T
, we will say that its
index

\gamma(T)
is
n\in{\mathbb{N}},
if
n
is the minimum number of vectors needed to form a frame of iterations. The index is infinite if it has frames of iterations but no one is finitely generated. If
T
does not have frame of iterations we will say that the index is zero. The Parseval index,
\gamma_{p}(T)
has the same definition, but requiring that the frame of iterations is a Parseval frame.

In this paper we obtain an exact value for the Parseval index of any bounded operator
T
. Further, we provide a set of vectors that attains such index, see Theorem
4.2
. We also show a link with the linear independence of the generators that realize the index.

For the general index of
T
, we give a formula in terms of the Parseval index of contractions similar to
T
in Proposition
4.3
.

In the case that
T
has a Parseval frame of iterations, i.e.
\gamma_{p}(T)
is positive, clearly
\gamma(T)
is also positive and
\gamma(T)\leqslant\gamma_{p}(T)
.
However, these two indices are not always equal for certain operators. For instance, we provide an example of an operator
T
where
\gamma(T)=1
and
\gamma_{p}(T)=\infty
. Also, note that there exist operators with Parseval index zero and positive general index (operators similar to contractions but with norm bigger than one that have adjoint strongly stable.) All the results concerning the index of an operator are presented in Section
4
.

Using Theorem
1.2
and the fact that an operator
T
is similar to a contraction if and only if its adjoint
T^{*}
also possesses this property, we get immediately the following result.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
T\in{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}})
. Assume that
T
has a frame of iterations. Then
T^{*}
has a frame of iterations if and only if
T
is strongly stable.

Observe that if a bounded operator
T
and its adjoint
T^{*}
both admit a frame of iterations, a necessary consequence is that both are strongly stable. We term this latter property
joint strong stability
.

In Section
5
, we examine in detail the joint strong stability in the setting of vector-value Hardy spaces. With the rich structure of the functional models and using the theory of
minimal dilations
of Nagy and Foias
[
23
]
we were able to link the joint strong stability with the concept of
full range
shift-invariant subspaces of the Hardy spaces. The theory of full range invariant subspaces were developed by Helson in the sixties
[
20
]
.

This connection allow us to construct frames of iterations by the adjoint of the shift operator in the models of the Hardy spaces to then be transferred to the abstract setting of the Hilbert spaces. In particular, in section
6
we developed a method to construct
optimal
frames for contractions with the joint strongly stability property. Optimality here means minimum number of generators.

Finally in Section
7
, we address the question of when the frames generated by
T
and
T^{*}
are similar. This part of the work relies on advanced tools, including the Beurling-Lax-Halmos theorem, which characterizes the invariant subspaces under the unilateral shift operator acting on
H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}}
, as well as the concept of inner functions in this context, and their properties. We obtain necessary and sufficient conditions in order that the frames of iterations by
T
and
T^{*}
are similar in terms of properties of the inner functions of the associated functional model in the Hardy space.

2.
Preliminares

Let us recall some notation regarding operators in Hilbert spaces. All the Hilbert spaces considered in this paper are assumed to be separable. Given
{\mathcal{H}}
and
{\mathcal{K}}
two Hilbert spaces,
{\mathcal{B}}(\mathcal{H},{\mathcal{K}})
denotes the set of bounded operators from
{\mathcal{H}}
to
{\mathcal{K}}
. When
{\mathcal{K}}={\mathcal{H}}
, we will simply write
{\mathcal{B}}(\mathcal{H},{\mathcal{K}})={\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}})
. If
N\subseteq\mathcal{H}
is a closed subspace we denote
P_{N}
the orthogonal projection of
{\mathcal{H}}
onto
N
. Finally, if
T\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
, a closed subspace
N\subseteq\mathcal{H}
is said to be
invariant under

T
or
T
-invariant
if
T(N)\subseteq N
or, equivalently,
P_{N}TP_{N}=TP_{N}
.

We finally recall that
T_{1}\in\mathcal{B}({\mathcal{H}}_{1})
and
T_{2}\in\mathcal{B}({\mathcal{H}}_{2})
are said to be
similar
if there exists an isomorphism
V:{\mathcal{H}}_{1}\to{\mathcal{H}}_{2}
such that

VT_{1}V^{-1}=T_{2}.

2.1.
Model spaces

Let us now give a brief exposition about model spaces, for more details see e.g.
[
20
, Lecture
\lx@sectionsign
VI]
. Let
{\mathbb{T}}
denote the one-dimensional torus in
{\mathbb{C}}
, i.e.

{\mathbb{T}}:=\{z\in{\mathbb{C}}:{\left|{z}\right|}=1\}

endowed with the normalized Lebesgue measure.

Given
{\mathcal{K}}
a Hilbert space,
L^{2}({\mathbb{T}},{\mathcal{K}})
is the set of all measurable and square-integrable
{\mathcal{K}}
-valued functions, which is a Hilbert space with the inner product

{\langle{f,g}\rangle}:=\int_{\mathbb{T}}{\langle{f(z),g(z)}\rangle}_{\mathcal{K}}\,{\mathrm{d}}z\qquad f,g\in L^{2}({\mathbb{T}},{\mathcal{K}}).

If
\{e_{i}\}_{i\in I}
is an orthonormal basis of
{\mathcal{K}}
and
{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\chi$}}}:{\mathbb{T}}\to{\mathbb{T}}
with
{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\chi$}}}(z)=z
, then it can be shown that
\{e_{i}{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\chi$}}}^{n}\}_{i\in I,n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}
is an orthonormal basis of
L^{2}({\mathbb{T}},{\mathcal{K}})
. Using this basis one can write any function
f\in L^{2}({\mathbb{T}},{\mathcal{K}})
uniquely as

f(z)=\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}a_{n}z^{n}

for almost every
z\in{\mathbb{T}}
, where
a_{n}=\sum_{i\in I}{\langle{f,e_{i}{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\chi$}}}^{n}}\rangle}e_{i}
for all
n\geqslant 0
. In fact,
\{a_{n}\}_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}
is a square-summable sequence in
{\mathcal{K}}
with
\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}{\left\|{a_{n}}\right\|}_{{\mathcal{K}}}^{2}={\left\|{f}\right\|}^{2}
. The
n^{\text{th}}
-Fourier coefficient of
f
is
a_{n}
.

The
Hardy space with multiplicity

\alpha=\dim({\mathcal{K}})
, denoted by
H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}}
, is the closed subspace of
L^{2}({\mathbb{T}},{\mathcal{K}})
of functions whose negative Fourier coefficients are zero. This is, the
L^{2}({\mathbb{T}},{\mathcal{K}})
subspace spanned by
\{e_{i}{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\chi$}}}^{n}\}_{i\in I,n\geqslant 0}
. Observe that the Hilbert space
{\mathcal{K}}
is identified with the subspace of
H^{2}_{{\mathcal{K}}}
consisting of constant functions. Alternatively, functions in
H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}}
can be seen as the radial limit of vector-analytic functions in
{\mathbb{D}}
such that its power series coefficients are square-summable. This is, if
f\in H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}}
then its expansion

f(z)=\sum_{n\geqslant 0}a_{n}z^{n}

defines an holomorphic function in
{\mathbb{D}}
with values in
{\mathcal{K}}
. Under this identification, we have
a_{n}=\frac{f^{n}(0)}{n!}
for all
n\geqslant 0
.

We will denote
U:L^{2}({\mathbb{T}},{\mathcal{K}})\to L^{2}({\mathbb{T}},{\mathcal{K}})
the
bilateral shift
, which is given by

Uf(z):=zf(z)

for almost every
z\in{\mathbb{T}}
and all
f\in H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}}
. Observe that
U
defines a bounded unitary operator in
L^{2}({\mathbb{T}},{\mathcal{K}})
whose adjoint verifies
U^{*}f(z)=\frac{f(z)}{z}
for almost every
z\in{\mathbb{T}}
and all
f\in L^{2}({\mathbb{T}},{\mathcal{K}})
.

Since
H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}}
is invariant under
U
, taking the restriction
S:=U|_{H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}}}
gives an isometry
S:H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}}\to H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}}
usually called the
unilateral shift
. Note that
S^{*}:H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}}\to H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}}
is given by

S^{*}f(z)=P_{H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}}}U^{*}f(z)=\dfrac{f(z)-f(0)}{z}

for almost every
z\in{\mathbb{T}}
and all
f\in H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}}
and where
P_{H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}}}
denotes the projection of
L^{2}({\mathbb{T}},{\mathcal{K}})
onto
H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}}
.

A
model space

N
is a closed subspace of
H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}}
that is invariant under
S^{*}
. We will consider the
shift compression to

N
as the operator
A_{N}:N\to N
given by

A_{N}f:=P_{N}Sf

for all
f\in N
. Note that, since
N
is
S^{*}
-invariant, then
(A_{N})^{*}f=S^{*}f
for all
f\in N
.

2.2.
Basic frames

Model spaces and its associated shift compression are key to characterize frames of iterations in
any
Hilbert space. Particularly, we can reduce ourselves to study sets of the form

\{A_{N}^{n}(P_{N}e_{i})\}_{i\in I,n\geqslant 0}

where
N\subseteq H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}}
is a model space for some Hilbert space
{\mathcal{K}}
and
\{e_{i}\}_{i\in I}
is an orthonormal basis of
{\mathcal{K}}
. Such sets are indeed always Parseval frames:

Proposition 2.1

(
[
2
, Proposition
3.6
]
)
.

Let
{\mathcal{K}}
be a Hilbert space,
N\subseteq H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}}
a model space and
\{e_{i}\}_{i\in I}
is an orthonormal basis of
{\mathcal{K}}
. Then
\{A_{N}^{n}(P_{N}e_{i})\}_{i\in I,n\geqslant 0}
is a Parseval frame of
N
.

Let us now recall the concept of
similar frames of iterations
defined in
[
2
,
\lx@sectionsign
3]
. Given
{\mathcal{H}}_{1},{\mathcal{H}}_{2}
two Hilbert spaces and
\{T_{1}^{n}v_{i}\}_{n\geqslant 0,i\in I}
and
\{T_{2}^{n}w_{i}\}_{n\geqslant 0,i\in I}
two frames of iterations in
{\mathcal{H}}_{1}
and
{\mathcal{H}}_{2}
respectively, we say that they are
similar frames
if
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
are similar with isomorphism
V
and
V(v_{i})=w_{i}
for all
i\in I
. We further say they are
unitarily equivalent
frames of iterations if
V
is unitary.

It is not difficult to see that this defines an equivalence relation among frames of iterations. Further, the following proposition states that every frame of iterations in a Hilbert space has a
corresponding frame
in a model space. For a proof see
[
9
, Theorem 2.1]
,
[
13
, Theorem 3.4]
and
[
2
, Theorem
3.9
]
.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
T\in{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}})
and
\{v_{i}\}_{i\in I}\subseteq{\mathcal{H}}
. Then,

(i)

If
\{T^{n}v_{i}\}_{i\in I,n\geqslant 0}
is a frame, then it is similar to a frame of the form

\{A_{N}^{n}(P_{N}e_{i})\}_{i\in I,n\geqslant 0}

where
N\subseteq H^{2}_{\ell^{2}(I)}
is a model space and
\{e_{i}\}_{i\in I}
is the canonical basis of
\ell^{2}(I)
.

(ii)

If
\{T^{n}v_{i}\}_{i\in I,n\geqslant 0}
is a Parseval frame, then it is unitarily equivalent to a frame of the form

\{A_{N}^{n}(P_{N}e_{i})\}_{i\in I,n\geqslant 0}

where
N\subseteq H^{2}_{\ell^{2}(I)}
is a model space and
\{e_{i}\}_{i\in I}
is the canonical basis of
\ell^{2}(I)
.

For this reason, frames of the form
\{A_{N}^{n}(P_{N}e_{i})\}_{i\in I,n\geqslant 0}
have been coined with the term
basic frames
.

3.
Existence of frames of iterations

In this section, we provide necessary and sufficient conditions for operators to admit frames iterations.
For a given Hilbert space
{\mathcal{H}}
we denote by
{\mathcal{F}}_{\mathcal{H}}
the subset of operators in
{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}})
that admit a frame of iterations, and by
{\mathcal{F}}^{p}_{\mathcal{H}}
the subset that admit a Parseval frame of iterations.

Regarding similarities and unitary equivalences, we have the first easy but very useful result:

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
T_{1}\in{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}}_{1})
and
T_{2}\in{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}}_{2})
. Then,

(i)

If
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
are similar and
T_{1}\in{\mathcal{F}}_{{\mathcal{H}}_{1}}
, then
T_{2}\in{\mathcal{F}}_{{\mathcal{H}}_{2}}
.

(ii)

If
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
are unitarily equivalent and
T_{1}\in{\mathcal{F}}^{p}_{{\mathcal{H}}_{1}}
, then
T_{2}\in{\mathcal{F}}^{p}_{{\mathcal{H}}_{2}}
.

Proof.

We only prove
(i)
as
(ii)
is analogous. Suppose then that
T_{2}=VT_{1}V^{-1}
for some isomorphism
V:{\mathcal{H}}_{1}\to{\mathcal{H}}_{2}
and that
\{T_{1}^{n}v_{i}\}_{i\in I,n\geqslant 0}
is a frame in
{\mathcal{H}}_{1}
. Then define, for each
i\in I
,
w_{i}:=Vv_{i}
, and note that

T_{2}^{n}w_{i}=V(T_{1}^{n}v_{i})

holds for all
i\in I
and
n\geqslant 0
. Since frames are preserved by isomorphisms, we get that
\{T_{2}^{n}w_{i}\}_{n\geqslant 0,i\in I}
is a frame in
{\mathcal{H}}_{2}
.
∎

Together with Propositions
2.1
and
2.2
this can be summarized as follows:

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
T\in{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}})
. Then,

(i)

T\in{\mathcal{F}}_{{\mathcal{H}}}
if and only if there exists a model space
N\subseteq H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}}
for some Hilbert space
{\mathcal{K}}
such that
T
is similar to
A_{N}.

(ii)

T\in{\mathcal{F}}^{p}_{\mathcal{H}}
if and only if there exists a model space
N\subseteq H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}}
for some Hilbert space
{\mathcal{K}}
such that
T
is unitarily equivalent to
A_{N}.

Proof.

Now we prove
(ii)
since
(i)
is also analogous.

First, if
\{T^{n}v_{i}\}_{i\in I,n\geqslant 0}
is a frame in
{\mathcal{H}}
for some vectors
\{v_{i}\}_{i\in I}\subseteq{\mathcal{H}}
, then by
(ii)
of Proposition
2.2
such frame must be unitarily equivalent to a basic frame
\{A_{N}^{n}(P_{N}e_{i})\}_{i\in I,n\geqslant 0}
where
N\subseteq H^{2}_{\ell^{2}(I)}
is a model space. In particular, this means that
T=VA_{N}V^{-1}
for some isomorphism
V:N\to{\mathcal{H}}
.

Conversely, suppose
T=VA_{N}V^{-1}
for some unitary map
V:N\to{\mathcal{H}}
and a model space
N\subseteq H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}}
. Then, as Proposition
2.1
tell us that
A_{N}\in{\mathcal{F}}^{p}_{N}
, we can use Proposition
3.1
to conclude that
T\in{\mathcal{F}}^{p}_{\mathcal{H}}
.
∎

When trying to exploit the similarity or unitary equivalence given by Theorem
3.2
, there are two properties of
A_{N}
that stand out:
A_{N}
is a contraction and its adjoint is strongly stable. Recall that given a Hilbert space
{\mathcal{H}}
, we say that a bounded operator
T\in{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}})
is a
contraction
if
{\left\|{T}\right\|}\leqslant 1
. We say that
T
is
strongly stable
if
\lim_{n\to+\infty}T^{n}v=0
holds for all
v\in{\mathcal{H}}
. Once these two properties are verified for shift compressions, one of the directions of Theorem
1.1
can be obtained by showing that the properties are preserved by unitary equivalences.

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
T\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
. If
T\in{\mathcal{F}}^{p}_{\mathcal{H}}
, then
T
is a contraction and
T^{*}
is strongly stable.

Proof.

Assume that
T\in{\mathcal{F}}^{p}_{{\mathcal{H}}}
. Then, from
(ii)
of Theorem
3.2
, we know that there exists a Hilbert space
{\mathcal{K}}
and a model space
N\subseteq H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}}
such that
T=VA_{N}V^{-1}=VA_{N}V^{*}
for a unitary map
V:N\to{\mathcal{H}}
.

First, since
V
is unitary,
{\left\|{T}\right\|}={\left\|{A_{N}}\right\|}={\left\|{P_{N}S}\right\|}\leqslant{\left\|{P_{N}}\right\|}{\left\|{S}\right\|}\leqslant 1
and hence,
T
is a contraction.

Second, as
(A_{N})^{*}=S^{*}|_{N}
, then for
f=\sum\limits_{n=0}^{+\infty}a_{n}{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\chi$}}}^{n}
we have

{\left\|{(S^{*})^{n}f}\right\|}^{2}=\sum\limits_{m\geqslant n}\|a_{m}\|_{\mathcal{K}}^{2}

which goes to zero when
n\to+\infty
as it is the tail of a convergent sum. The strong stability of
T^{*}
then follows from the identity
(T^{*})^{n}=V(S^{*}|_{N})^{n}V^{*}
for all
n\geqslant 0
.
∎

To prove the converse of Proposition
3.3
, by Theorem
3.2
, it is enough to show that contractions with strongly stable adjoints are unitarily equivalent to shift compressions in a model space. The following result uses Rota functional model
[
28
]
, refined by De Branges an Rovnyak in
[
8
]
. Since its proof will be important in what follows, we include it below.

Theorem 3.4

(
[
22
, p. 18]
)
.

Let
T\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
. If
T
is a contraction and
T^{*}
is strongly stable, then there exists a Hilbert space
{\mathcal{K}}
such that
T
is unitarily equivalent to
A_{N}
for some model space
N\subseteq H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}}
.

Proof.

As
{\left\|{T}\right\|}\leqslant 1
we have that
I_{\mathcal{H}}-TT^{*}
is a self-adjoint semidefinite positive operator. Set
D:=(I_{\mathcal{H}}-TT^{*})^{1/2}
,
{\mathcal{K}}=\overline{D({\mathcal{H}})}
and define
L:{\mathcal{H}}\to H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}}
as

Lv:=\sum_{n\geqslant 0}D(T^{*})^{n}v\>{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\chi$}}}^{n}

Thus,
L
defines an isometry:

\displaystyle{\left\|{Lv}\right\|}^{2}=\sum_{n\geqslant 0}{\left\|{D(T^{*})^{n}v}\right\|}_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\geqslant 0}{\langle{D(T^{*})^{n}v,D(T^{*})^{n}v}\rangle}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\geqslant 0}{\langle{(I_{\mathcal{H}}-TT^{*})(T^{*})^{n}v,(T^{*})^{n}v}\rangle}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\geqslant 0}\left({\left\|{(T^{*})^{n}v}\right\|}^{2}-{\left\|{(T^{*})^{n+1}v}\right\|}^{2}\right)={\left\|{v}\right\|}^{2}

where the last equality follows from the fact that
(T^{*})^{n}v\to 0
as
n\to+\infty
.

Also, note that
S^{*}L=LT^{*}
. Indeed, for every
v\in{\mathcal{H}}

S^{*}Lv=\sum_{n\geqslant 0}D(T^{*})^{n+1}v\>{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\chi$}}}^{n}=\sum_{n\geqslant 0}D(T^{*})^{n}(T^{*}v)\>{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\chi$}}}^{n}=LT^{*}v.

Therefore, the image of
L
is an
S^{*}
-invariant subspace of
H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}}
and we call it
N
. Moreover if
V:N\to{\mathcal{H}}
is the inverse of
L:{\mathcal{H}}\to N
, we have that
T^{*}=VS^{*}|_{N}V^{-1}
and that
V
is a unitary map. Taking adjoints we get
T=VA_{N}V^{-1}
.
∎

For a contraction
T
, the operator
D=(I-TT^{*})^{1/2}
is called
defect operator
, their closed range
{\mathcal{K}}=\overline{D({\mathcal{H}})}

defect space
and the
defect index
is
\dim{\mathcal{K}}

[
23
]
. As a consequence of Theorem
3.4
, we derive the remaining part of Theorem
1.1
.

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
T\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
. If
T
is a contraction and
T^{*}
is strongly stable, then
T\in{\mathcal{F}}^{p}_{\mathcal{H}}
.

Proof.

If
T
is a contraction and
T^{*}
is strongly stable then Theorem
3.4
tells us that
T
is unitarily equivalent to a shift compression
A_{N}
for some model space
N\subseteq H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}}
. Then, by
(ii)
of Theorem
3.2
we have that
T\in{\mathcal{F}}^{p}_{\mathcal{H}}
.
∎

Combining Propositions
3.3
and
3.5
, Theorem
1.1
follows. With this result, we can provide another proof for Theorem
1.2
which differs from the one given in
[
10
]
.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

If
T\in{\mathcal{F}}_{\mathcal{H}}
, by
(i)
of Theorem
3.2
, we have that there exists a Hilbert space
{\mathcal{K}}
and a model space
N\subseteq H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}}
such that
T=VA_{N}V^{-1}
where
V:N\to{\mathcal{H}}
is an isomorphism. As
A_{N}\in{\mathcal{F}}^{p}_{N}
by Proposition
2.1
, applying Proposition
3.3
to
A_{N}
says that
A_{N}
is a contraction with strongly stable adjoint. But then
T
is similar to a contraction and it also has a strongly stable adjoint.

Assume now that
T
is similar to a contraction
L:{\mathcal{H}}_{1}\to{\mathcal{H}}_{1}
and
T^{*}
is strongly stable. Thus
T=C^{-1}LC
for an isomorphism
C:{\mathcal{H}}\to{\mathcal{H}}_{1}
. Then, we have
(L^{*})^{n}=(C^{*})^{-1}(T^{*})^{n}C^{*}
for all
n\geqslant 0
, and thus
L^{*}
is also strongly stable. Therefore, applying Proposition
3.5
to
L
, we have that
L\in{\mathcal{F}}^{p}_{{\mathcal{H}}_{1}}
. Finally, since
T
and
L
are similar, we can use Proposition
3.1
to conclude that
T\in{\mathcal{F}}_{\mathcal{H}}
.
∎

3.1.
Examples of frames of iterations

As a consequence of Theorem
1.2
we have the following results about the existence of frame of iterations for bounded operators: first, as noted in
[
7
]
, if an operator
T:{\mathcal{H}}\to{\mathcal{H}}
is unitary, then it cannot have frames of iterations as its adjoint is not strongly stable. Second, extending a result from
[
12
]
, if an operator
T:{\mathcal{H}}\to{\mathcal{H}}
is hypercyclic (i.e. there exists a vector
v\in{\mathcal{H}}
for which
\{T^{n}v\}_{n\geqslant 0}
is dense in
{\mathcal{H}}
) then it cannot have frames of iterations since contractions cannot be hypercyclic and this property is preserved by similarity.

In
[
7
]
the authors construct a Parseval frame for any operator satisfying the hypothesis of Theorem
1.1
.
It is not difficult to modify that example to construct a bigger family of frames of iterations:

Proposition 3.6
.

Assume that
T\in{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}})
is a contraction and
T^{*}
is strongly stable. Let
D=(I_{{\mathcal{H}}}-TT^{*})^{1/2}
and
{\mathcal{K}}=\overline{D({\mathcal{H}})}
. If
{\mathcal{G}}=\{g_{i}\}_{i\in I}
is a frame of
{\mathcal{K}}
with bounds
A,B
, then
\{T^{n}Dg_{i}\}_{i\in I,n\geqslant 0}
is a frame of
{\mathcal{H}}
,
with the same bounds.
Furthermore, if
{\mathcal{G}}
is a Riesz basis or an orthonormal basis of
{\mathcal{K}}
then

\{T^{n}Dg_{i}\}_{i\in I,n\geqslant 0}

also forms a Riesz basis or an orthonormal basis of

{\mathcal{H}}
.

Proof.

Let
n\geqslant 0
and
f\in{\mathcal{H}}
, then using that
D
is self-adjoint, we have

\sum_{i\in I}{\left|{{\langle{f,T^{n}Dg_{i}}\rangle}}\right|}^{2}=\sum_{i\in I}{\left|{{\langle{D(T^{*})^{n}f,g_{i}}\rangle}}\right|}^{2}.

Since
{\mathcal{G}}
is a frame of
{\mathcal{K}}
we have that

(1)

A{\left\|{D(T^{*})^{n}f}\right\|}^{2}\leqslant\sum_{i\in I}{\left|{{\langle{D(T^{*})^{n}f,g_{i}}\rangle}}\right|}^{2}\leqslant B{\left\|{D(T^{*})^{n}f}\right\|}^{2}.

Now, for every
n\geqslant 0
,

\displaystyle{\left\|{D(T^{*})^{n}f}\right\|}^{2}

\displaystyle=\langle D(T^{*})^{n}f,D(T^{*})^{n}f\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle D^{2}(T^{*})^{n}f,(T^{*})^{n}f\rangle

\displaystyle={\left\|{(T^{*})^{n}f}\right\|}^{2}-{\left\|{(T^{*})^{n+1}f}\right\|}^{2}.

Then, summing in
n\geqslant 0
in(
1
) and using the strong stability of
T^{*}
, we get that

A{\left\|{f}\right\|}^{2}\leqslant\sum_{n\geqslant 0}\sum_{i\in I}{\left|{{\langle{f,T^{n}Dg_{i}}\rangle}}\right|}^{2}\leqslant B{\left\|{f}\right\|}^{2},

which shows that
\{T^{n}Dg_{i}\}_{i\in I,n\geqslant 0}
is a frame of
{\mathcal{H}}
.
The Riesz basis and orthonormal basis use the same argument.
∎

Note that in Proposition
3.6
if
{\mathcal{G}}
is a Parseval frame of
{\mathcal{K}}
, then the frame of iterations of
T
generated by
\{Dg_{i}\}_{i\in I}
is also Parseval.

We will give now another construction of frames of iterations.
Let first prove the following lemma. Recall that a pure isometry
L
is an isometry whose unitary part
\bigcap_{n\geqslant 0}L^{n}({\mathcal{H}})=\{0\}.

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
L\in{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}})
be a pure isometry and
W={\mathcal{H}}\ominus L({\mathcal{H}})
the associated wandering subspace. If
{\mathcal{G}}=\{g_{i}\}_{i\in I}
is a frame of
W
with bounds
A
and
B
, then
\{L^{n}g_{i}\}_{i\in I,n\geqslant 0}
is a frame of
{\mathcal{H}}
, with the same bounds.
Furthermore, if
{\mathcal{G}}
is a Riesz basis or an orthonormal basis of
W
then

\{L^{n}g_{i}\}_{i\in I,n\geqslant 0}

also forms a Riesz basis or an orthonormal basis of

{\mathcal{H}}
.

Proof.

When
L
is an isometry, then
I_{\mathcal{H}}-LL^{*}=I_{\mathcal{H}}-P_{L({\mathcal{H}})}=P_{W}
. Thus, the defect space of
L
is equal to
W
. Moreover, since
L
is a pure isometry, then
L^{*}
is strongly stable. See for example
[
26
, Section 1.3]
.

Therefore, by Proposition
3.6
,
if
\{g_{i}\}_{i\in I}
is a frame of
W
, then
\{L^{n}g_{i}\}_{i\in I,n\geqslant 0}
is a frame of
{\mathcal{H}}
because
Dg_{i}=P_{W}g_{i}=g_{i},
for every
i\in I
.
The same holds for the Riesz and orthonormal basis cases.
∎

Now, we can apply this result to construct frames of any model subspace in
H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}}
, in particular of
H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}}
itself.

Proposition 3.8
.

Let
{\mathcal{K}}
be a Hilbert space and
N\subseteq H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}}
a model space.
If
\{g_{i}\}_{i\in I}
is a frame of
{\mathcal{K}}
, then
\{A^{n}_{N}(P_{N}g_{i})\}_{i\in I,n\geqslant 0}
is a frame of
N
.

In particular,
\{S^{n}g_{i}\}_{i\in I,n\geqslant 0}
is a frame of
H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}}
.

Proof.

Since
{\mathcal{K}}
is the wandering subspace associated to
S
in
H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}}
and
S
is a pure isometry, we can apply Lemma
3.7
to conclude that
\{S^{n}g_{i}\}_{i\in I,n\geqslant 0}
, is a frame of
H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}}
. Then, the projection of it to
N
,
\{P_{N}(S^{n}g_{i})\}_{i\in I,n\geqslant 0}
is a frame of
N
.
Thus, the result follows by noticing that for every
n\geqslant 0
and
i\in I
,
P_{N}(S^{n}g_{i})=(P_{N}S)^{n}P_{N}g_{i}=A^{n}_{N}(P_{N}g_{i})
.
∎

Combining this proposition with Theorem
3.2
we can explicitly construct frames of iterations for any
T\in{\mathcal{F}}_{\mathcal{H}}
.

Corollary 3.9
.

Let
T\in{\mathcal{F}}_{\mathcal{H}}
and
N\subseteq H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}}
be a model space such that
T
is similar to
A_{N}
through the isomorphism
C:N\rightarrow{\mathcal{H}}
. Then,
\{T^{n}(C(P_{N}g_{i}))\}_{i\in I,n\geqslant 0}
is a frame of
{\mathcal{H}}
, where
\{g_{i}\}_{i\in I}
is any frame of

{\mathcal{K}}.

4.
Index of an operator

4.1.
Frame indexes

We now turn our attention to the problem of determining the minimum number of generating vectors to have a frame of iterations by a given operator.

Recall that, for a Hilbert space
{\mathcal{H}}
and
T\in{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}})
, we say that a collection of non-zero vectors
\{v_{i}\}_{i\in I}\subseteq{\mathcal{H}}
is a set of
generators
for
T
if
\{T^{n}v_{i}\}_{i\in I,n\geqslant 0}
is a frame of
{\mathcal{H}}
. We will further say that
\{v_{i}\}_{i\in I}
is a set of
Parseval generators
for
T
if
\{T^{n}v_{i}\}_{i\in I,n\geqslant 0}
is a Parseval frame of
{\mathcal{H}}
.

Definition 4.1
.

The frame index of
T
, denoted
\gamma(T)
, is defined as:

\gamma(T)=\min\{d\in{\mathbb{N}}:\text{there exists a set of generators of d elements}\},

if
T
possesses a frame of iterations that admits a finite generating set. We say that
\gamma(T)=0
if
T
does not possess a frame of iterations, and
\gamma(T)=+\infty
if
T
possesses a frame of iterations, but none admit a finite generating set.

The Parseval frame index,
\gamma_{p}(T)
has the same definition, but requiring that the frame of iterations is a Parseval frame.

For the Parseval index we are able to obtain a precise formula and along the way show that such index can be realized in a set of generators which are linearly independent.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
T\in{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}})
. If
T\in{\mathcal{F}}^{p}_{\mathcal{H}}
, then

\gamma_{p}(T)=\dim\overline{(I_{\mathcal{H}}-TT^{*})({\mathcal{H}})}.

Moreover, the index
\gamma_{p}(T)
is attained with linearly independent generators.

Proof.

As an initial step, we will show that if
\{w_{i}\}_{i\in I}
is a set of Parseval generators
\#I\geqslant\dim\overline{(I_{\mathcal{H}}-TT^{*})({\mathcal{H}})}
. To see this note first that if
\{T^{n}w_{i}\}_{i\in I,n\geqslant 0}
is a Parseval frame, by Proposition
2.2
, we know that there exists a model space
N\subseteq H^{2}_{\ell^{2}(I)}
and a unitary operator
V:N\to{\mathcal{H}}
such that
T=VA_{N}V^{-1}=VA_{N}V^{*}
. Then, on one hand

I_{\mathcal{H}}-TT^{*}=VV^{*}-VA_{N}V^{*}VS^{*}|_{N}V^{*}=V(I_{N}-A_{N}S^{*}|_{N})V^{*}.

On the other hand, if
f\in N
is written as
f=\sum_{n\geqslant 0}a_{n}{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\chi$}}}^{n}
with
\{a_{n}\}_{n\geqslant 0}\subseteq{\mathcal{K}}
then

\displaystyle(I_{N}-A_{N}S^{*}|_{N})f=P_{N}(I_{H^{2}_{{\mathcal{K}}}}-SS^{*})f

\displaystyle=P_{N}\left(\sum_{n\geqslant 0}a_{n}{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\chi$}}}^{n}-\sum_{n\geqslant 1}a_{n}{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\chi$}}}^{n}\right)

\displaystyle=P_{N}(a_{0})=\sum_{i\in I}{\langle{a_{0},e_{i}}\rangle}P_{N}(e_{i}),

where
\{e_{i}\}_{i\in I}
is the canonical basis of
\ell^{2}(I)
. Thus, altogether,

\#I\geqslant\dim\overline{(I_{N}-A_{N}S^{*}|_{N})(N)}=\dim\overline{(I_{\mathcal{H}}-TT^{*})({\mathcal{H}})}.

Now, since
T\in{\mathcal{F}}^{p}_{{\mathcal{H}}}
, by Proposition
3.3
,
T
must be a contraction and
T^{*}
is strongly stable. Then, Theorem
3.4
tells us that
T
must be unitarily equivalent to a shift compression
A_{N_{o}}
where
N_{o}\subseteq H^{2}_{{\mathcal{K}}_{o}}
is a model space with
{\mathcal{K}}_{o}:=\overline{(I_{\mathcal{H}}-TT^{*})^{1/2}({\mathcal{H}})}
. Mapping a basic frame
\{A_{N_{o}}^{n}(P_{N_{o}}e_{i})\}_{i\in I,n\geqslant 0}
of
N_{o}
through the unitary equivalence as in Proposition
3.1
, we can construct a Parseval frame in
{\mathcal{H}}
of the form
\{T^{n}v_{i}\}_{i\in I,n\geqslant 0}
where
\#I=\dim{\mathcal{K}}_{o}=\dim\overline{(I_{\mathcal{H}}-TT^{*})^{1/2}({\mathcal{H}})}
. Since
(I_{\mathcal{H}}-TT^{*})^{1/2}
is self adjoint then
\overline{(I_{\mathcal{H}}-TT^{*})^{1/2}({\mathcal{H}})}=\overline{(I_{\mathcal{H}}-TT^{*})({\mathcal{H}})}
, which implies

\#I=\dim\overline{(I_{\mathcal{H}}-TT^{*})({\mathcal{H}})}.

Let us end by showing that the set of generators
\{v_{i}\}_{i\in I}
we obtained is actually linearly independent. To see this, note that it is enough to show that the set of vectors
\{P_{N_{o}}e_{i}\}_{i\in I}
are linearly independent when
\{e_{i}\}_{i\in I}
is an orthonormal basis of
{\mathcal{K}}_{o}
. Indeed, as
\{v_{i}\}_{i\in I}
are the image under an isomorphism (in fact a unitary map) of
\{P_{N_{o}}e_{i}\}_{i\in I}
then, linear independence of
\{P_{N_{o}}e_{i}\}_{i\in I}
implies that of
\{v_{i}\}_{i\in I}
.

The result follows from the fact that
{\mathcal{K}}_{o}\cap N^{\perp}_{o}=\{0\}
since, in such case, we would have that
P_{N_{o}}|_{{\mathcal{K}}_{o}}
is injective and thus
\{P_{N_{o}}e_{i}\}_{i\in I}
is linearly independent.

Recall that
N_{o}
is the image of the map
L:{\mathcal{H}}\to H^{2}_{{\mathcal{K}}_{o}}
defined as

Lv=\sum_{n\geqslant 0}D(T^{*})^{n}v\>{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\chi$}}}^{n},\quad v\in{\mathcal{H}},

where
D=(I_{\mathcal{H}}-TT^{*})^{1/2}
is the defect operator of
T
. Take now
k\in{\mathcal{K}}_{o}
such that
k\perp N_{o}
. We then have that

\textstyle{\langle{k,\sum_{n\geqslant 0}D(T^{*})^{n}v\>{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\chi$}}}^{n}}\rangle}_{H^{2}_{{\mathcal{K}}_{o}}}=0

for all
v\in{\mathcal{H}}
. But
\textstyle{\langle{k,\sum_{n\geqslant 0}D(T^{*})^{n}v\>{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\chi$}}}^{n}}\rangle}_{H^{2}_{{\mathcal{K}}_{o}}}={\langle{k,Dv}\rangle}_{\mathcal{H}}
, thus

{\langle{k,Dv}\rangle}_{\mathcal{H}}=0

for all
v\in{\mathcal{H}}
, or equivalently
k\in(D({\mathcal{H}}))^{\perp}={\mathcal{K}}_{o}^{\perp}
. Hence
k=0
, and we are finished.
∎

Let us remark that the inequality
\#I\geqslant\dim\overline{(I_{\mathcal{H}}-TT^{*})({\mathcal{H}})}
, where
I
is the index set of a generating set of vectors, can also be deduced from the arguments given in
[
10
]
which again differ from the ones we use here.

For the general index, we know that when a bounded operator
T
has a frame of iterations, i.e.
\gamma(T)>0
, then by Theorem
1.2
,
T
should be similar to a contraction, say
Q\in{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{K}})
, and
T^{*}
should be strongly stable. Now, it is easy to see that
T^{*}
is similar to
Q^{*}
and that strong stability is preserved by similarity. Thus,
Q
is a contraction and
Q^{*}
is strongly stable, which, by Theorem
1.1
, implies
\gamma_{p}(Q)>0
.

On the other hand, as these indexes are preserved by similarity, we have

0<\gamma(T)=\gamma(Q)\leqslant\gamma_{p}(Q)=\dim\overline{(I-QQ^{*})({\mathcal{K}})}.

Moreover, this holds for any contraction
Q
similar to
T
.

Further, if we take a frame of iterations
\{T^{n}v_{i}\}_{i\in I,n\geqslant 0}
with
\#I=\gamma(T)
generators we know from
(i)
of Proposition
2.2
that there exists a model space
N\subseteq H^{2}_{\ell^{2}(I)}
such that
A_{N}
is similar to
T
. Now, as
\{A_{N}^{n}(P_{N}e_{i})\}_{i\in I,n\geqslant 0}
is a Parseval frame of
N
, cf. Proposition
2.1
, we have that

\gamma(T)=\#I\geqslant\gamma_{p}(A_{N}).

These arguments prove the following proposition.

Proposition 4.3
.

Given a bounded operator
T\in{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}})
we have:

\displaystyle\gamma(T)

\displaystyle=\min\;\{\gamma_{p}(Q):{\mathcal{K}}\text{ a Hilbert space, }Q\in{\mathcal{F}}^{p}_{\mathcal{K}},Q\text{ similar to }T\}

\displaystyle=\min\;\{\dim\overline{(I-QQ^{*})({\mathcal{K}})}:{\mathcal{K}}\text{ a Hilbert space, }Q\in{\mathcal{F}}^{p}_{\mathcal{K}},Q\text{ similar to }T\}.

Here we take
\min\{\emptyset\}=0
.

However, even if
T
is a contraction, the quantity
\dim\overline{(I_{\mathcal{H}}-TT^{*})({\mathcal{H}})}
is far from being accurate when trying to determine the number of generating vectors for a frame of iterations that are not necessarily Parseval. In fact, as we show in the next example, there exist operators
T
for which
\gamma(T)=1
but
\gamma_{p}(T)=+\infty
.

Example 4.4
.

We consider the operator and frames introduced in
[
6
]
. Let
\{\lambda_{k}\}_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
a sequence in the complex unit disc
\mathbb{D}
satisfying
\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}(1-{\left|{\lambda_{k}}\right|}^{2})<\infty
and the Carleson condition

\inf_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}\prod_{j\neq k}\frac{{\left|{\lambda_{k}-\lambda_{j}}\right|}}{1-\overline{\lambda_{k}}\lambda_{j}}>0

and define
T:\ell^{2}({\mathbb{N}})\to\ell^{2}({\mathbb{N}})
as
Te_{k}=\lambda_{k}e_{k}
for all
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, where
\{e_{k}\}_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is the canonical basis of
\ell^{2}({\mathbb{N}})
. It is proved in
[
6
]
that there exists a vector
\varphi\in\ell^{2}({\mathbb{N}})
such that
\{T^{n}\varphi\}_{n\geqslant 0}
is a frame of
\ell^{2}({\mathbb{N}})
. Thus,
\gamma(T)=1
.

On the other hand, it is easily seen that
{\left\|{T}\right\|}=\sup\{{\left|{\lambda_{k}}\right|}:k\in{\mathbb{N}}\}
. Moreover, since
\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}(1-{\left|{\lambda_{k}}\right|}^{2})<\infty
, it holds that
|\lambda_{k}|\to 1
as
k\to+\infty
and therefore
{\left\|{T}\right\|}=1
. This means that
T
is a contraction and as
T\in\mathcal{F}_{\ell^{2}({\mathbb{N}})}
, we have that
(T^{*})^{n}v\to 0
as
n\to+\infty
for all
v\in\ell^{2}({\mathbb{N}})
. Therefore, by Proposition
3.5
,
T\in\mathcal{F}^{p}_{\ell^{2}({\mathbb{N}})}
. Finally, Theorem
4.2
gives us that
\gamma_{p}(T)=\dim\overline{(I_{\ell^{2}({\mathbb{N}})}-TT^{*})(\ell^{2}({\mathbb{N}}))}
and a direct calculation shows that
\dim\overline{(I_{\ell^{2}({\mathbb{N}})}-TT^{*})(\ell^{2}({\mathbb{N}}))}=+\infty
.

In fact, frames of iterations for
normal
operators with
finite index
where completely characterized and all possible collections of generators were obtained in
[
3
,
6
,
7
,
10
,
11
]
. From those results and reasoning like in Example
4.4
we can conclude that a normal operator has Parseval index either
0
or infinite, or in other words that a normal operator can never have a finite number of vectors generating a Parseval frames of iterations.

The fact that in the previous example we have
{\left\|{T}\right\|}=1
is a consequence of
T
having a Parseval frame of iterations one hand, and, on the other hand, a general frame of iterations with finite generators. Indeed, in
[
7
]
it was shown that frames of iterations generated by a finite set of vectors force the operator to have norm at least
1
. Altogether, we have the following:

Proposition 4.5
.

Let
{\mathcal{H}}
be an infinite dimensional Hilbert space and
T\in{\mathcal{F}}^{p}_{\mathcal{H}}
. Assume further that
\gamma(T)<+\infty
. Then
{\left\|{T}\right\|}=1
.

Proof.

On one hand, if
\gamma(T)<+\infty
,
[
7
, Theorem 9]
says that
{\left\|{T}\right\|}\geqslant 1
. On the other hand, if
T\in{\mathcal{F}}^{p}_{\mathcal{H}}
, Proposition
3.3
says that
{\left\|{T}\right\|}\leqslant 1
. Altogether,
{\left\|{T}\right\|}=1
.
∎

At this point it is worth noting that there exist operators with norm
1
but which do not admit
any
frame of iterations. Indeed, if
H^{2}:=H^{2}(\mathbb{T})
is the Hardy space without multiplicity, take
T=S^{*}:H^{2}\to H^{2}
the adjoint of the shift in
H^{2}
. Then
{\left\|{T}\right\|}=1
but
T^{*}=S
is not strongly stable and hence, by Theorem
1.2
,
T
does not admit a frame of iterations.

Moreover, even if
T
admits a frame of iterations and
{\left\|{T}\right\|}=1
it might happen that
T
has infinite index. This can be seen, for example, by taking
T
as the shift
S
in a Hardy space
H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}}
, where
{\mathcal{K}}
is a Hilbert space with infinite dimension.

4.2.
Linear independence of generators

Taking up from the result of Theorem
4.2
that says the Parseval index can always be attained on a linearly independent set of vectors, we now move to study whether this also happens for the general index. Additionally, we address the question about the necessity of linear independence for a generator set which has minimal size.

To that end, we begin with a result that shows we can always shrink the size of a set of generators to a set of linearly independent vectors that are also generators.

Proposition 4.6
.

Let
T\in{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}})
and
\{v_{i}\}_{i\in I}\subseteq{\mathcal{H}}
such that
\{T^{n}v_{i}\}_{i\in I,n\geqslant 0}
is a frame (Parseval frame) of
{\mathcal{H}}
. Then there exists a linearly independent set of vectors
\{w_{j}\}_{j\in J}
with
\#J\leqslant\#I
and such that
\{T^{n}w_{j}\}_{j\in J,n\geqslant 0}
is a frame (Parseval frame).

Moreover, the vectors
\{w_{j}\}_{j\in J}
can be chosen so that
{\overline{\textrm{span}}}\{w_{j}:j\in J\}={\overline{\textrm{span}}}\{v_{i}:i\in I\}
.

Proof.

By item
(i)
of Proposition
2.2
we know that there exists a model space
N\subseteq H^{2}_{\ell^{2}(I)}
and an isomorphism
V:N\to{\mathcal{H}}
such that
v_{i}=V(P_{N}e_{i})
for all
i\in I
, where
\{e_{i}\}_{i\in I}
is the canonical basis of
\ell^{2}(I)
.

Now, set
{\mathcal{K}}:=\ell^{2}(I)
,
{\mathcal{K}}_{0}:={\mathcal{K}}\cap N^{\perp}
and
{\mathcal{K}}_{1}:={\mathcal{K}}\ominus{\mathcal{K}}_{0}
. Next, construct another orthonormal basis of
{\mathcal{K}}
as
\{f_{i}\}_{i\in I_{0}}\cup\{g_{j}\}_{j\in J}
with
\{f_{i}\}_{i\in I_{0}}
an orthonormal basis of
{\mathcal{K}}_{0}
and
\{g_{j}\}_{j\in J}
is an orthonormal basis of
{\mathcal{K}}_{1}
. Then

\#J=\dim{\mathcal{K}}_{1}\leqslant\dim{\mathcal{K}}=\#I

and since
P_{N}(f_{i})=0
for
i\in I_{0}
we have that

\{(A_{N})^{n}P_{N}g_{j}\}_{j\in J}
is Parseval frame in
N
with at most
\#I
generators. Therefore, defining for each
j\in J
,
w_{j}:=V(P_{N}g_{j})
, we get that

\{T^{n}w_{j}\}_{j\in J,n\geqslant 0}=\{V((A_{N})^{n}P_{N}g_{j})\}_{j\in J,n\geqslant 0}

is a frame of
{\mathcal{H}}
with at most
\#I
generators. Also, since
\{g_{j}\}_{j\in J}
is a linearly independent subset of
{\mathcal{K}}_{1}
and
\ker(P_{N}|_{\mathcal{K}})={\mathcal{K}}_{0}
, then
P_{N}
restricted to
{\mathcal{K}}_{1}
is one to one what implies that
\{P_{N}g_{j}\}_{j\in J}
is linearly independent. We conclude that
\{w_{j}\}_{j\in J}
is a linearly independent set of vectors.

Moreover,

{\overline{\textrm{span}}}\{v_{i}:i\in I\}=V(\overline{P_{N}({\mathcal{K}})})=V(\overline{P_{N}({\mathcal{K}}_{1})})={\overline{\textrm{span}}}\{w_{j}:j\in J\}.

Finally notice that if
\{T^{n}v_{i}\}_{i\in I,n\geqslant 0}
is Parseval, we can take
V
to be unitary and thus
\{T^{n}w_{j}\}_{j\in J,n\geqslant 0}
would also be Parseval.
∎

As a corollary we obtain the following result about linear independence for generators with minimal cardinal.

Corollary 4.7
.

Given an operator
T\in{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}})
with positive index, there always exists a linearly independent set of generators of cardinal
\gamma(T).

Morover, if the index is finite and positive, every set of generators with cardinal
\gamma(T)
must be linearly independent.

The same holds for the Parseval index
\gamma_{p}(T).

Proof.

We will only provide the proof for the index
\gamma(T)
, as the proof for
\gamma_{p}(T)
is analogous. If
\{v_{i}\}_{i\in I}\subseteq{\mathcal{H}}
is a set of generators with
\#I=\gamma(T)
then by Proposition
4.6
we can obtain a set of linearly independent generators
\{w_{j}\}_{j\in J}
such that
\#J\leqslant\#I
. However, by definition,
\#J\geqslant\gamma(T)
. In sum,
\#J=\gamma(T)
.

Now if
\gamma(T)
is finite, take an index set
I
with
\#I=\gamma(T)
and suppose that
\{v_{i}\}_{i\in I}\subseteq{\mathcal{H}}
is a set of generators that is linearly dependent. Then, if
{\mathcal{K}}_{0}
and
{\mathcal{K}}_{1}
are as in the proof of Proposition
4.6
, we would have
{\mathcal{K}}_{0}\neq\{0\}
as there would be a non-trivial linear combination of
\{P_{N}e_{i}\}_{i\in I}
summing
0
. This means that
\dim{\mathcal{K}}_{1}<\dim{\mathcal{K}}=\#I
and we would then obtain a set of generators with strictly less elements than
\#I
, which is a contradiction.
∎

In light of this result, we can then make the following definition of
optimal frame of iterations
.

Definition 4.8
.

We say that a frame of iterations
\{T^{n}v_{i}\}_{i\in I,n\geqslant 0}
is optimal when the set of generators
\{v_{i}\}_{i\in I}
is linearly independent and
\#I=\gamma(T)
.

We have a corresponding definition for the Parseval frame case.

Proposition
4.6
signals a relation between a frame of iterations and the vector space spanned by its generators. Furthermore, in Parseval frame case we can show that the dimension of such spaces is always the same. Moreover, it is equal to the Parseval index.

Proposition 4.9
.

Let
T\in{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}})
and
\{v_{i}\}_{i\in I}\subseteq{\mathcal{H}}
such that
\{T^{n}v_{i}\}_{i\in I,n\geqslant 0}
is a Parseval frame. Then

\gamma_{p}(T)=\dim({\overline{\textrm{span}}}\{v_{i}:i\in I\}).

To show this we will first prove it when
{\mathcal{H}}=N
a model space and
T=A_{N}
, which in the sense of Proposition
2.2
, represents all cases.

Lemma 4.10
.

Let
N\subseteq H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}}
be a model space, then

\overline{(I_{N}-A_{N}S^{*})(N)}=\overline{P_{N}({\mathcal{K}})}.

Proof.

First note that given
f=\sum_{n\geqslant 0}a_{n}\chi^{n}\in N
we have

\displaystyle(I_{N}-A_{N}S^{*})f=P_{N}(I_{H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}}}-SS^{*})f

\displaystyle=P_{N}\left(\sum_{n\geqslant 0}a_{n}\chi^{n}-\sum_{n\geqslant 1}a_{n}\chi^{n}\right)

\displaystyle=P_{N}(a_{0})=P_{N}(f(0)),

identifying
f(0)=a_{0}
. Thus, if we define
{\mathcal{K}}_{N}:=\overline{\{g(0):g\in N\}}
,

\overline{(I_{N}-A_{N}S^{*})(N)}=\overline{P_{N}({\mathcal{K}}_{N})}

since
P_{N}
is continuous and
{\mathcal{K}}_{N}
is a closed subspace of
{\mathcal{K}}
(and hence of
H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}}
) .

The inclusion
\overline{(I_{N}-A_{N}S^{*})(N)}\subseteq\overline{P_{N}({\mathcal{K}})}
is immediate from
{\mathcal{K}}_{N}\subseteq{\mathcal{K}}
. To see the converse inclusion we will show that
P_{N}({\mathcal{K}})\subseteq P_{N}({\mathcal{K}}_{N})
. To than end, let
k\in{\mathcal{K}}
and take
k_{1}:=P_{{\mathcal{K}}_{N}}k
where
P_{{\mathcal{K}}_{N}}:{\mathcal{K}}\to{\mathcal{K}}
is the projection onto
{\mathcal{K}}_{N}
. This means that
k-k_{1}\perp{\mathcal{K}}_{N}
, and in particular, we have

{\langle{k_{1}-k,g(0)}\rangle}_{\mathcal{K}}=0\quad\forall g\in N.

Further, since
k_{1}-k
is a constant we have that
{\langle{k_{1}-k,g}\rangle}_{H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}}}={\langle{k_{1}-k,g(0)}\rangle}_{\mathcal{K}}
for all
g\in N
. Whence
k-k_{1}\perp N
, and thus

P_{N}(k-k_{1})=0,

or equivalently,
P_{N}(k)=P_{N}(k_{1})
. This shows that
P_{N}(k)\in P_{N}({\mathcal{K}}_{N})
and the proof is finished.
∎

Proof of Proposition
4.9
.

This actually follows from the proof of Proposition
4.6
. Indeed, if
\{A_{N}^{n}(P_{N}e_{i})\}_{i\in I,n\geqslant 0}
is the basic frame unitarily equivalent to
\{T^{n}v_{i}\}_{i\in I,n\geqslant 0}
given by
(ii)
of Proposition
2.2
, we have that

V(\overline{P_{N}({\mathcal{K}})})={\overline{\textrm{span}}}\{v_{i}:i\in I\}

where
V:N\to{\mathcal{H}}
is the unitary map realizes the equivalence, i.e.
T=VA_{N}V^{*}
and
v_{i}=V(P_{N}e_{i})
for all
i\in I
.

Now, from Theorem
4.2
, we know that the relation
T=VA_{N}V^{*}
derives

\gamma_{p}(T)=\dim\overline{(I_{N}-A_{N}S^{*})(N)}

And from the previous lemma we have that
\overline{(I_{N}-A_{N}S^{*})(N)}=\overline{P_{N}({\mathcal{K}})}
. Altogether,

\gamma_{p}(T)=\dim{\overline{\textrm{span}}}\{v_{i}:i\in I\}

and the proof is finished.
∎

5.
Strongly Stable Contractions with Strongly Stable Adjoints

In this section, we focus on contractions acting in a general separable Hilbert space, possessing the
joint strong stability
: the property that both the operator and its adjoint are strongly stable. This property means that
T
and
T^{*}
have frames of iterations.

We first work in
H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}}
. For every model space
N\subseteq H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}}
, the adjoint of the compressed shift
A_{N}
coincides with the backward shift
S^{*}
, which is strongly stable. Then, the
joint
strong stability of
A_{N}
reduces to requiring only the strong stability of
A_{N}
.

We prove that the strong stability of the shift compression
A_{N}
is equivalent to requiring the orthogonal complement of
N
in
H^{2}_{{\mathcal{K}}}
to be a
full range subspace,
a concept studied by Helson in the 1960s in the context of shift-invariant spaces.

5.1.
Full range subspaces and inner functions

The characterization of model subspaces in a Hardy space
H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}}
comes from the description of their orthogonal complement. Such complements are, by definition, invariant under the unilateral shift
S
.

Before presenting this characterization, we need to introduce a few definitions. Let
Q:{\mathbb{T}}\rightarrow{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{K}})
an operator-valued measurable function such that

{\left\|{Q}\right\|}_{\infty}:={\mathrm{ess}\sup}_{z\in{\mathbb{T}}}{\left\|{Q(z)}\right\|}_{op}<\infty.

We define the operator
\widehat{Q}:L^{2}({\mathbb{T}},{\mathcal{K}})\rightarrow L^{2}({\mathbb{T}},{\mathcal{K}})
by

(\widehat{Q}f)(z):=Q(z)f(z)\qquad\text{a.e. }z\in{\mathbb{T}},f\in L^{2}({\mathbb{T}},{\mathcal{K}}).

Note that
\widehat{Q}
is well-defined and bounded with
{\left\|{\widehat{Q}}\right\|}_{op}\leqslant{\left\|{Q}\right\|}_{\infty}
.

We will say that
Q
is
analytic
if
\widehat{Q}(H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}})\subseteq H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}}
. Denote by
{\mathcal{A}}={\mathcal{A}}({\mathcal{K}})
the set of all
analytic
operator-valued functions.

Being analytic can also be shown through a Fourier-type expansion. To see this fix
\{e_{i}\}_{i\in I}
an orthonormal basis of
{\mathcal{K}}
and let
Q\in{\mathcal{A}}({\mathcal{K}})
. For each
n\geqslant 0
, define
Q_{n}:{\mathcal{K}}\to{\mathcal{K}}
the operator given by

Q_{n}v=\sum_{i\in I}{\langle{\widehat{Q}v,e_{i}{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\chi$}}}^{n}}\rangle}e_{i},\qquad v\in{\mathcal{K}}.

Note that
Q_{n}
is indeed well-defined and bounded since

(2)

\sum_{i\in I}{\left|{{\langle{\widehat{Q}v,e_{i}{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\chi$}}}^{n}}\rangle}}\right|}^{2}\leqslant\sum_{m\geqslant 0}\sum_{i\in I}{\left|{{\langle{\widehat{Q}v,e_{i}{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\chi$}}}^{m}}\rangle}}\right|}^{2}={\left\|{\widehat{Q}v}\right\|}^{2}\leqslant{\left\|{\widehat{Q}}\right\|}_{op}^{2}{\left\|{v}\right\|}^{2}_{\mathcal{K}}.

Moreover, as
Q_{n}v
is the
n^{th}
-Fourier coefficient of
\widehat{Q}v
we may write, for almost all
z\in{\mathbb{T}}
,

(3)

Q(z)=\sum_{n\geqslant 0}Q_{n}z^{n}

in the strong operator topology, i.e.
Q(z)v=\sum_{n\geqslant 0}Q_{n}vz^{n}
for all
v\in{\mathcal{K}}
. Indeed, convergence is assured by the right-hand side of(
2
). Further, due to the uniqueness of Fourier coefficients for functions in
H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}}
(constants or otherwise), the operators
Q_{n}
in this expansion are also unique. We will thus say that
Q_{n}
is the
n^{th}
-Fourier (operator) coefficient of
Q
.

Analytic functions are indeed a key to describe shift-invariant subspaces as the following result shows.

Theorem 5.1

(Beurling-Lax-Halmos Theorem
[
20
,
\lx@sectionsign
VI]
)
.

Let
{\mathcal{K}}
be a Hilbert space and
M\subseteq H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}}
a closed subspace. Then
M
is
S
-invariant if and only if there exists a subspace
{\mathcal{K}}_{1}\subseteq{\mathcal{K}}
and a function
Q\in{\mathcal{A}}({\mathcal{K}})
such that

(4)

M=\widehat{Q}(H^{2}_{{\mathcal{K}}_{1}})

and
Q(z):{\mathcal{K}}\to{\mathcal{K}}
is a partial isometry with initial space
{\mathcal{K}}_{1}
for almost every
z\in{\mathbb{T}}
.
Moreover, if
{\mathcal{K}}_{2}\subseteq{\mathcal{K}}
and
\widetilde{Q}\in{\mathcal{A}}({\mathcal{K}})
are another pair satysfying(
4
) then there exists a unitary map
R:{\mathcal{K}}_{1}\to{\mathcal{K}}_{2}
such that
Q(z)=\widetilde{Q}(z)R
for almost all
z\in{\mathbb{T}}
.

Let us remark that in
[
20
]
,
{\mathcal{K}}_{1}
is taken as an
arbitrary
separable Hilbert space. But it follows from the fact that there is an isometric embedding of
{\mathcal{K}}_{1}
into
{\mathcal{K}}
, that one can take
{\mathcal{K}}_{1}
as a subspace of
{\mathcal{K}}
, as we do here.

In what follows we will work with a subclass of shift-invariant subspaces called
full range
that we define now.

Let
M
be an
S
-invariant subspace of
H^{2}_{{\mathcal{K}}}
and let
W=M\ominus S(M)
denote its wandering subspace. Consider an orthonormal basis
\{E_{j}\}_{j\in I}
of
W
. For almost every
z\in{\mathbb{T}},
define

(5)

J(z)={\overline{\textrm{span}}}\{E_{j}(z):j\in I\}.

We note that this definition does not depend on the orthonormal basis
\{E_{j}\}_{j\in I}
chosen. The function
J
defined in the torus
{\mathbb{T}}
and taking values in the closed subspaces of
{\mathcal{K}},
is referred to as an
analytic measurable range function
. This concept was introduced by Henry Helson in
[
20
]
where he conducted a thorough analysis of range functions to characterize
S
-invariant subspaces.

Definition 5.2
.

A subspace
M\subseteq H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}}
is called full range if
M
is invariant under
S
and
its range function as defined in(
5
) is almost everywhere equal to
{\mathcal{K}}
.

Note that if
M=\widehat{Q}(H^{2}_{{\mathcal{K}}_{1}})
then

J(z)=Q(z)({\mathcal{K}}_{1}),

holds for almost every
z\in{\mathbb{T}}
.

There is a way of describing full range subspaces through the function provided in(
4
) by the Beurling-Lax-Halmos Theorem. In fact, as we will see, in such case the function is
inner
in the sense of the following definition.

Definition 5.3
.

Given
Q\in{\mathcal{A}}({\mathcal{K}})
, we say that
Q
is inner if
Q(z):{\mathcal{K}}\to{\mathcal{K}}
is a unitary operator for almost every
z\in{\mathbb{T}}
.

We will denote by
{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathcal{I}}={\mathcal{A}}_{\mathcal{I}}({\mathcal{K}})
as the subclass of inner functions in
{\mathcal{A}}({\mathcal{K}})
.

We are now ready to present a characterization of S-invariant full-range subspaces using inner functions. Furthermore, we provide two additional equivalent conditions for a subspace to be full range (see
[
16
,
20
]
).

Theorem 5.4
.

Let
M\subseteq H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}}
be an
S
-invariant subspace and
W=M\ominus S(M)
. The following statements are equivalent:

(i)

M
is full range.

(ii)

M=\widehat{Q}(H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}})
for some
Q\in{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathcal{I}}({\mathcal{K}})
.

(iii)

L^{2}({\mathbb{T}},{\mathcal{K}})=\displaystyle\bigoplus_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}U^{n}W
.

(iv)

Every othonormal basis
\{E_{i}\}_{i\in I}
of
W
satisfies that
\{E_{i}(z)\}_{i\in I}
is an orthonormal basis of
{\mathcal{K}}
for almost all
z\in{\mathbb{T}}
.

Proof.

(i)\Rightarrow(ii)
. Let
{\mathcal{K}}_{1}\subseteq{\mathcal{K}}
and
Q_{1}\in{\mathcal{A}}({\mathcal{K}})
a subspace and a function associated to
M
by(
4
). Since
M
is full range we have that
Q_{1}(z)({\mathcal{K}}_{1})={\mathcal{K}}
for almost every
z\in{\mathbb{T}}
, which implies that
{\mathcal{K}}_{1}
and
{\mathcal{K}}
are isometrically isomorphic. Take
R:{\mathcal{K}}\to{\mathcal{K}}_{1}
a fixed isometric isomorphism and set
Q:=Q_{1}R
, i.e.
Q(z)=Q_{1}(z)R
for almost all
z\in{\mathbb{T}}
. Then,
Q(z):{\mathcal{K}}\to{\mathcal{K}}
is an isometry and

Q(z)({\mathcal{K}})=Q_{1}(z)R({\mathcal{K}})=Q_{1}(z)({\mathcal{K}}_{1})={\mathcal{K}}

for almost all
z\in{\mathbb{T}}
. In other words,
Q(z)
is a unitary operator in
{\mathcal{K}}
for almost all
z\in{\mathbb{T}}
.

Also, since
R:{\mathcal{K}}\to{\mathcal{K}}_{1}
is an isomorphism and
Q
is analytic, we have

\widehat{Q}(H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}})=\widehat{Q_{1}}(H^{2}_{{\mathcal{K}}_{1}})\subseteq H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}},

from where it follows that
Q
is also analytic and
M=\widehat{Q}(H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}})
.

(ii)\Rightarrow(iii)
. It is enough to observe that if
(ii)
holds, in particular
W=\widehat{Q}({\mathcal{K}})
and then, using that
\widehat{Q}:L^{2}({\mathbb{T}},{\mathcal{K}})\to L^{2}({\mathbb{T}},{\mathcal{K}})
is a unitary operator that commutes with
U
(
[
27
, Corollary 3.19]
), we have

\bigoplus_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}U^{n}W=\bigoplus_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}U^{n}\widehat{Q}({\mathcal{K}})=\widehat{Q}\left(\bigoplus_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}U^{n}({\mathcal{K}})\right)=L^{2}({\mathbb{T}},{\mathcal{K}}).

(iii)\Rightarrow(iv)
. Let
\{E_{i}\}_{i\in I}
be an orthonormal basis of
W
. By
(iii)
,
\{U^{n}E_{i}\}_{i\in I,n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}
is an orthonormal basis of
L^{2}({\mathbb{T}},{\mathcal{K}})
. To see that
\{E_{i}(z)\}_{i\in I}
is an orthonormal set of
{\mathcal{K}}
first note that

\int_{\mathbb{T}}{\langle{E_{j}(z),E_{i}(z)}\rangle}z^{-n}\,{\mathrm{d}}z={\langle{E_{j},U^{n}E_{i}}\rangle}=\begin{cases}1&j=i\text{ and }n=0\\
0&\text{ other case}\end{cases}.

This means that, when
i\neq j
, all the Fourier coefficients of the function
z\mapsto{\langle{E_{i}(z),E_{j}(z)}\rangle}
are zero and when
i=j
, all the Fourier coefficients are zero except the coefficient
n=0
which is equal to one. Thus
{\langle{E_{j}(z),E_{i}(z)}\rangle}=0
for almost every
z\in{\mathbb{T}}
for
i\neq j
and
{\left\|{E_{i}(z)}\right\|}=1
for almost every
z\in{\mathbb{T}}
.

To prove the completeness suppose that
x\in{\mathcal{K}}
satisfies
\langle x,E_{i}(z)\rangle=0
for almost every
z\in{\mathbb{T}}
and for all
i\in I
. Then

0=\int_{\mathbb{T}}\langle x,E_{i}(z)\rangle z^{-n}\,dz=\langle x,U^{n}E_{i}\rangle

for all
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}
and
i\in I
. Since
\{U^{n}E_{i}\}_{i\in I,n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}
is complete in
L^{2}({\mathbb{T}},{\mathcal{K}})
, we get that the constant function
x
must be
0
.

(iv)\Rightarrow(i)
. It is immediate.

∎

In the next theorem we show that a sufficient condition to construct explicit frames of iteration of the backward shift within a model space
N
is precisely that
H^{2}_{{\mathcal{K}}}\ominus N
is full range.

Theorem 5.5
.

Let
N\subseteq H^{2}_{{\mathcal{K}}}
be a model space and suppose that
M=H^{2}_{{\mathcal{K}}}\ominus N
is full range. Then, for every orthonormal basis
\{E_{i}\}_{i\in I}
of
W
, the system
\{(S^{*})^{n}S^{*}E_{i}\}_{i\in I,n\geqslant 0}
is a Parseval frame of
N
.

Proof.

First, notice that
S^{*}E_{i}\in N
for all
i\in I
. Indeed, since

{\langle{S^{*}E_{i},S^{n}E_{i^{\prime}}}\rangle}={\langle{E_{i},S^{n+1}E_{i^{\prime}}}\rangle}=0

for every
n\geqslant 0
and
i^{\prime}\in I
,
S^{*}E_{i}
is orthogonal to the orthonormal basis
\{S^{n}E_{i^{\prime}}\}_{i^{\prime}\in I}
of
M
and hence
S^{*}E_{i}\in N
. Second, as
N
is
S^{*}
-invariant then the system
\{(S^{*})^{n}S^{*}E_{i}\}_{i\in I,n\geqslant 0}
is included in
N
.

To show that it forms a frame, we use that
(S^{*})^{n}=P_{H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}}}(U^{*})^{n}|_{H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}}}
for all
n\geqslant 1
, where
U
is the bilateral shift operator in
L^{2}({\mathbb{T}},{\mathcal{K}})
. Indeed, for
n=1
the equality follows from the definitions of
U^{*}
and
S^{*}
. Assume now that it is true for
n>1
. Then

\displaystyle(S^{*})^{n+1}

\displaystyle=S^{*}P_{H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}}}(U^{*})^{n}|_{H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}}}=P_{H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}}}U^{*}|_{H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}}}P_{H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}}}(U^{*})^{n}|_{H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}}}=(P_{H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}}}UP_{H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}}})^{*}(U^{*})^{n}|_{H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}}}

\displaystyle=(UP_{H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}}})^{*}(U^{*})^{n}|_{H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}}}=P_{H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}}}^{*}U^{*}|_{H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}}}(U^{*})^{n}|_{H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}}}=P_{H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}}}(U^{*})^{n+1}|_{H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}}}

since
P_{H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}}}
is a projection and
H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}}
is
U
-invariant.

Using this, we get that for all
i\in I
and
n\geqslant 1

(S^{*})^{n}E_{i}=P_{N}(S^{*})^{n}E_{i}=P_{N}P_{H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}}}(U^{*})^{n}|_{H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}}}E_{i}=P_{N}(U^{*})^{n}E_{i}=P_{N}U^{-n}E_{i}.

On the other hand, when
n\geqslant 0
we have
U^{n}E_{i}=S^{n}E_{i}\in M\perp N
for all
i\in I
and thus
P_{N}U^{n}E_{i}=0
for every
i\in I
and
n\geqslant 0
. In sum,
\{(S^{*})^{n}S^{*}E_{i}\}_{i\in I,n\geqslant 0}
is the projection in
N
of
\{U^{n}E_{i}\}_{i\in I,n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}
. Since
N\subseteq\bigoplus_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}U^{n}W
and
\{U^{n}E_{i}\}_{i\in I,n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}
is an orthonormal basis of
\bigoplus_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}U^{n}W
, we conclude that
\{(S^{*})^{n}S^{*}E_{i}\}_{i\in I,n\geqslant 0}
is a Parseval frame for
N
.
∎

5.2.
Strong stability of the shift compression and full range subspaces

In this section we prove that given a model space
N
in
H^{2}_{{\mathcal{K}}}
and
A_{N}
the compression of the shift operator
S
to
N
, the condition of strong stability of
A_{N}
is equivalent to require that the subspace
M=H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}}\ominus N
is full range.

One of the implications can be derived quickly from Theorem
5.5
and Proposition
3.3
as we see immediately.

Proposition 5.6
.

Let
N
be a model space in
H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}}
. If
M=H^{2}_{{\mathcal{K}}}\ominus N
is full range, then
A_{N}
is strongly stable.

Proof.

Since
M=H^{2}_{{\mathcal{K}}}\ominus N
is full range, from Theorem
5.5
we know that
\{(S^{*})^{n}S^{*}E_{i}\}_{i\in I,n\geqslant 0}
is a Parseval frame for any orthonormal basis
\{E_{i}\}_{i\in I}
of
W=M\ominus S(M)
. Thus
S^{*}|_{N}\in{\mathcal{F}}^{p}_{N}
. By Proposition
3.3
this implies that
(S^{*})^{*}=A_{N}
is strongly stable.
∎

In order to prove the converse of Proposition
5.6
, we make use of the concept of
dilations
.
Let
T_{1}\in\mathcal{B}({\mathcal{H}}_{1})
and
T_{2}\in\mathcal{B}({\mathcal{H}}_{2})
. Then,
T_{2}
is a
dilation
of
T_{1}
if
{\mathcal{H}}_{1}\subseteq{\mathcal{H}}_{2}
and

T_{1}^{n}=P_{{\mathcal{H}}_{1}}T_{2}|_{{\mathcal{H}}_{1}}^{n}
for al
n\in \mathbb{N}
.
When
T_{2}
is unitary, we call it
unitary dilation
. It is proven in
[
23
, Chapter 1,
\lx@sectionsign 4
, Theorem 4.2]
that for every contraction
T_{1}
, there exists an unitary dilation
T_{2}
on some Hilbert space
{\mathcal{H}}_{2}
that is minimal in the sense that
{\mathcal{H}}_{2}
can be taken to be
\bigoplus_{n\geq 0}T_{2}^{n}{\mathcal{H}}_{1}
.

Now, if
N\subseteq H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}}
is a model space and
P_{N}:L^{2}({\mathbb{T}},{\mathcal{K}})\to L^{2}({\mathbb{T}},{\mathcal{K}})
is the orthogonal projection onto
N
,
it is not difficult to see that
U:L^{2}({\mathbb{T}},{\mathcal{K}})\to L^{2}({\mathbb{T}},{\mathcal{K}})
is a unitary dilation of
A_{N}
. Moreover, a minimal unitary dilation can then be constructed by restricting
U
to the subspace

\mathcal{G}:={\overline{\textrm{span}}}\{U^{n}N:n\in{\mathbb{Z}}\}.

We need the following result.

Theorem 5.7

(
[
23
, Theorem 1.2, Chapter 2]
)
.

Let
N
be a model space in
H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}}
and
let
\mathcal{L}:=\overline{(U-A_{N})N}
. Then
A_{N}
is strongly stable if and only if
\mathcal{G}=\bigoplus_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}U^{n}\mathcal{L}
.

We now have the following theorem.

Theorem 5.8
.

Let
N
be a model space in
H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}}
. If
A_{N}
is strongly stable then
M=H^{2}_{{\mathcal{K}}}\ominus N
is full range.

Proof.

To demonstrate that
M
is full-range, it suffices, by Theorem
5.4
, to show that

\bigoplus_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}U^{n}W=L^{2}({\mathbb{T}},{\mathcal{K}}),
where
W=M\ominus S(M)
, the wandering subspace of
M
.

This will be established by proving that
H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}}\subseteq\bigoplus_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}U^{n}W.
If this inclusion holds, then:

L^{2}({\mathbb{T}},{\mathcal{K}})={\overline{\textrm{span}}}\{U^{n}H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}}:n\in{\mathbb{Z}}\}\subseteq\bigoplus_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}U^{n}W\subseteq L^{2}({\mathbb{T}},{\mathcal{K}}).

Since
H^{2}_{{\mathcal{K}}}=N\oplus M
and
M\subseteq\bigoplus_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}U^{n}W
, it remains to show
that
N\subseteq\bigoplus_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}U^{n}W.

Applying Theorem
5.7
we have,

N\subseteq{\overline{\textrm{span}}}\{U^{n}N:n\in{\mathbb{Z}}\}=\bigoplus_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}U^{n}\mathcal{L}.

Using that
{\overline{\textrm{span}}}\{U^{n}M:n\in{\mathbb{Z}}\}=\bigoplus_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}U^{n}W,

the proof will be complete if we show that
\mathcal{L}=\overline{(U-A_{N})N}\subseteq M.

This inclusion follows from two facts:
{\mathcal{L}}\subseteq H^{2}_{{\mathcal{K}}}
and
{\mathcal{L}}\perp N
.

To see that
{\mathcal{L}}\subseteq H^{2}_{{\mathcal{K}}}
, notice that since
H^{2}_{{\mathcal{K}}}
is
U
-invariant,
N
is
A_{N}
-invariant and
N\subseteq H^{2}_{{\mathcal{K}}}
, from where it follows that
(U-A_{N})N\subseteq H^{2}_{{\mathcal{K}}}
. Therefore, the closure of this set,
{\mathcal{L}}
, is also contained in
H^{2}_{{\mathcal{K}}}
.

Secondly,
{\mathcal{L}}\perp N
holds because for every
f\in N
we have
(U-A_{N})f=Uf-P_{N}Uf=P_{N^{\perp}}Uf
. Thus
(U-A_{N})N\perp N
and then
{\mathcal{L}}
is also orthogonal to
N
.

Altogether, we get
\mathcal{L}\subseteq H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}}\ominus N=M
.
∎

We summarize our results in the following theorem.

Theorem 5.9
.

Let
N
be a model space in
H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}}
. The following statements are equivalent:

(i)

The subspace
M=H^{2}_{{\mathcal{K}}}\ominus N
is full range.

(ii)

The shift compression
A_{N}
is strongly stable.

(iii)

For every orthonormal basis
\{E_{j}\}_{j\in I}
of the wandering subspace of
H^{2}_{{\mathcal{K}}}\ominus N
, the system
\{(S^{*})^{n}(S^{*}E_{j})\}_{j\in I,n\geqslant 0}
is a Parseval frame of
N
.

Finally, we can characterize the joint strong stability property for general contractions as follows:

Theorem 5.10
.

Let
T\in\mathcal{B}({\mathcal{H}})
be a contraction. Then,
T
has the joint strong stability property if and only there exists a model space
N\subseteq H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}}
satisfiying that
H^{2}_{{\mathcal{K}}}\ominus N
is full range, such that
T
is similar to
A_{N}
.

Proof.

First, assume that
T
has the joint strong stability property. Then, by Theorem
3.4
, there exists a model space
N\subseteq H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}}
such that
T
is similar to
A_{N}
. Therefore,
A_{N}
must have the joint strong property and thus, by Theorem
5.9
,
H^{2}_{{\mathcal{K}}}\ominus N
is full range.

Conversely, suppose that
T
is similar to
A_{N}
with
H^{2}_{{\mathcal{K}}}\ominus N
being full range. Then, again by Theorem
5.9
,
A_{N}
has the joint strong stability property. So, the same holds for
T
.

∎

6.
Optimal Parseval frames of iterations

In this section, we address the construction of optimal Parseval frames of iterations for contractions, namely, Parseval frames of the form
\{T^{n}v_{i}\}_{i\in I,n\geqslant 0}
where
I
has cardinality equal to the Parseval index of
T
.

Moreover, for joint strong stability contractions
T
, we show that the Parseval index of
T
and its adjoint
T^{*}
coincide, and we provide a simultaneous construction of optimal Parseval frames of iterations for both
T
and
T^{*}
.

We begin by working in the model space setting, where we construct Parseval frames of iterations for the shift compression operator and its adjoint using the minimal number of generators. This construction is then transferred to obtain optimal Parseval frames of iterations for
T
and
T^{*}
.

6.1.
Optimal Parseval frames in model spaces

To clarify our exposition, we now fix the setting in which all subsequent results will take place. Given
N\subseteq H^{2}_{{\mathcal{K}}}
a model space, we define:

•

The
S
-invariant orthogonal complement of
N
, namely
M:=H^{2}_{{\mathcal{K}}}\ominus N
, which we assume to be full range;

•

The wandering subspace of
M
, given by
W:=M\ominus SM
;

•

The subspace
W_{1}:=W\ominus(W\cap{\mathcal{K}})
;

•

The subspace
{\mathcal{K}}_{1}:={\mathcal{K}}\ominus(W\cap{\mathcal{K}})
;

•

The subspace
\mathcal{L}:=\overline{(U-A_{N})N}
.

These assumptions and the notation introduced above will serve as the standing hypotheses for the remainder of this section.

Lemma 6.1
.

\dim(W_{1})=\dim({\mathcal{K}}_{1})
.

Proof.

Let
{\mathcal{K}}_{0}=W\cap{\mathcal{K}}
. We first note that
H^{2}_{{\mathcal{K}}}=H^{2}_{{\mathcal{K}}_{0}}\oplus H^{2}_{{\mathcal{K}}_{1}}.

On the other hand, since
W
is the wandering subspace of
M
and
W={\mathcal{K}}_{0}\oplus W_{1}
we have that

(6)

M=\bigoplus_{n\geqslant 0}S^{n}W=\bigoplus_{n\geqslant 0}S^{n}{\mathcal{K}}_{0}\oplus\bigoplus_{n\geqslant 0}S^{n}W_{1}=H^{2}_{{\mathcal{K}}_{0}}\oplus M_{1},

where we have called
M_{1}
to the
S
-invariant space with wandering subspace
W_{1}
, that is,
M_{1}=\bigoplus_{n\geqslant 0}S^{n}W_{1}
.

As
H^{2}_{{\mathcal{K}}}=M\oplus N
, using(
6
), we obtain the decomposition
H^{2}_{{\mathcal{K}}}=H^{2}_{{\mathcal{K}}_{0}}\oplus M_{1}\oplus N
. Combining this fact with the first observation, we conclude that

H^{2}_{{\mathcal{K}}_{1}}=M_{1}\oplus N.

Now, by Theorem
5.9
we know that
A_{N}
is strongly stable, which is a property that only depends on
N
. Therefore, using again Theorem
5.9
, we get that
M_{1}
is a full range
S
-invariant subspace of
H^{2}_{{\mathcal{K}}_{1}}
. Then, from item
(iv)
of Theorem
5.4
applied to
M_{1}
, we obtain
\dim(W_{1})=\dim({\mathcal{K}}_{1})
as we wanted.
∎

Remark 6.2
.

We notice that from the proof of Lemma
6.1
, one can always reduce the Hardy space
H^{2}_{{\mathcal{K}}}
to
H^{2}_{{\mathcal{K}}_{1}}
, and the model space
N\subseteq H^{2}_{{\mathcal{K}}}
is still a model space in
H^{2}_{{\mathcal{K}}_{1}}
. This in particular says that the series defining the elements in
N
has all its coefficients in
{\mathcal{K}}_{1}
and it is easy to see that
{\mathcal{K}}_{1}
is optimal with this property. Moreover, since the decomposition in(
6
) holds without assuming that
M
is
S
-invariant, we conclude that
M
is full range if and only if
M_{1}
is full range.

In the next proposition, we construct Parseval frames of iterations in
N
for the operators
A_{N}
and
S^{*}
.
We will later prove the optimality of these frames.

Proposition 6.3
.

Let
\{E_{j}\}_{j\in J}
be an orthonormal basis of
W_{1}
and
\{e_{j}\}_{j\in J}
an orthonormal basis of
{\mathcal{K}}_{1}
.
Then,
\{A_{N}^{n}(P_{N}e_{j})\}_{j\in J,n\geqslant 0}
and

\{(S^{*})^{n}S^{*}E_{j}\}_{j\in J,n\geqslant 0}
are Parseval frames of
N.

Proof.

We first notice that by Lemma
6.1
, it is valid to take the same index set
J
for the orthonormal bases in
W_{1}
and
{\mathcal{K}}_{1}
.

Now, let
\{F_{i}\}_{i\in I}
be an orthonormal basis of
W\ominus W_{1}
, so
\{E_{j}\}_{j\in J}\cup\{F_{i}\}_{i\in I}
is an orthonormal basis of
W.

We know by Theorem
5.5
that
\{(S^{*})^{n}S^{*}E_{j}\}_{j\in I,n\geqslant 0}\cup\{(S^{*})^{n}S^{*}F_{i}\}_{i\in I,n\geqslant 0}
is a Parseval frame of
N.

Since
S^{*}F_{i}=0
for
i\in I,
because
F_{i}\in W\cap\,{\mathcal{K}}
for all
i\in I
, then
\{(S^{*})^{n}S^{*}E_{j}\}_{j\in J,n\geqslant 0}

is also a Parseval frame of
N
.

For the frame of iterations of
A_{N}
the proof goes in the same way. We extend the orthonormal basis
\{e_{j}\}_{j\in J}
of
{\mathcal{K}}_{1}
to an orthonormal basis of
{\mathcal{K}}
, say
\{e_{j}\}_{j\in J}\cup\{f_{i}\}_{i\in I}.

Then we observe that
P_{N}f_{i}=0
for
i\in I
since
f_{i}
are in
W.

Thus,
\{A^{n}_{N}(P_{N}e_{j})\}_{j\in J,n\geqslant 0}
is a Parseval frame of
N
.
∎

In what follow we will show that the Parseval frames of iterations by
A_{N}
and
S^{*}
constructed on
Proposition
6.3
are optimal.
For this it will be enough to show that,
\gamma_{p}(A_{N})=\gamma_{p}(S^{*}|_{N})=\dim(W_{1})=\dim({\mathcal{K}}_{1}).

We need first the following proposition.

Proposition 6.4
.

For the Parseval index of
S^{*}|_{N}
and
A_{N}
we have that

\gamma_{p}(S^{*}|_{N})=\gamma_{p}(A_{N})=\dim\mathcal{L}.

Proof.

From Theorem
4.2
applied to the operator
A_{N}
and the operator
S^{*}
acting in
N
we have that

\displaystyle\gamma_{p}(A_{N})=\dim\overline{(I-A_{N}S^{*})(N)},

\displaystyle\gamma_{p}(S^{*}|_{N})=\dim\overline{(I-S^{*}A_{N})(N)}.

Thus, using
[
23
, Theorem 1.2]
applied to the Hilbert space
N
, we get that
\gamma_{p}(S^{*}|_{N})=\gamma_{p}(A_{N})=\dim\mathcal{L}
.
∎

Proposition 6.5
.

Under the standing setting of this section, we have
\mathcal{L}=W_{1}
.

Proof.

From Proposition
6.4
we know that
\gamma_{p}(S^{*}|_{N})=\dim\overline{(U-A_{N})N}
. Note that

\overline{(U-A_{N})N}=\overline{\text{rg}(U-P_{N}U)P_{N}}

where the operator
(U-P_{N}U)P_{N}
is understood as acting in
L^{2}({\mathbb{T}},{\mathcal{K}})
. Noting that
(U-P_{N}U)P_{N}=(I-P_{N})UP_{N}=P_{N^{\perp}}UP_{N}
where
N^{\perp}=L^{2}({\mathbb{T}},{\mathcal{K}})\ominus N
and
P_{N^{\perp}}
is the projection in
L^{2}({\mathbb{T}},{\mathcal{K}})
onto
N^{\perp}
, we have that

\overline{\text{rg}(U-P_{N}U)P_{N}}=\ker(P_{N}U^{*}P_{N^{\perp}})^{\perp}.

Let us now study the kernel of
P_{N}U^{*}P_{N^{\perp}}:L^{2}({\mathbb{T}},{\mathcal{K}})\to L^{2}({\mathbb{T}},{\mathcal{K}})
. To that end, we decompose
L^{2}({\mathbb{T}},{\mathcal{K}})
as follows

L^{2}({\mathbb{T}},{\mathcal{K}})=\left(L^{2}({\mathbb{T}},{\mathcal{K}})\ominus H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}}\right)\oplus N\oplus SM\oplus W

where, recall,
M=H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}}\ominus N
and
W=M\ominus SM
.

First, note that since
L^{2}({\mathbb{T}},{\mathcal{K}})\ominus H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}}
is a
U^{*}
-invariant subspace of
N^{\perp}
then

U^{*}P_{N^{\perp}}(L^{2}({\mathbb{T}},{\mathcal{K}})\ominus H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}})=U^{*}(L^{2}({\mathbb{T}},{\mathcal{K}})\ominus H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}})\subseteq L^{2}({\mathbb{T}},{\mathcal{K}})\ominus H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}}\subseteq N^{\perp},

and thus
P_{N}U^{*}P_{N^{\perp}}(L^{2}({\mathbb{T}},{\mathcal{K}})\ominus H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}}))=\{0\}
. Second, is clear that
N\subseteq\ker(P_{N}U^{*}P_{N^{\perp}})
since
P_{N^{\perp}}(N)=\{0\}
. Third, note that as
SM=UM\subseteq N^{\perp}
then

U^{*}P_{N^{\perp}}(SM)=U^{*}U(M)=M,

and thus
P_{N}U^{*}P_{N^{\perp}}(SM)=\{0\}
.

Finally, let
f\in W
and note that

P_{N}U^{*}P_{N^{\perp}}f=P_{N}U^{*}f=P_{N}S^{*}f=S^{*}f

because
W\subseteq N^{\perp}
,
S^{*}W\subseteq N
and
N
is
S^{*}
-invariant. Thus, if we decompose
W={\mathcal{K}}_{0}\oplus W_{1}
, where as before
{\mathcal{K}}_{0}=W\cap{\mathcal{K}}
, and we recall that
\ker S^{*}={\mathcal{K}}
, we have, altoghether,

\ker(P_{N}U^{*}P_{N^{\perp}})=\left(L^{2}({\mathbb{T}},{\mathcal{K}})\ominus H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}}\right)\oplus N\oplus S(M)\oplus W_{0}.

From where it follows that
\ker(P_{N}U^{*}P_{N^{\perp}})^{\perp}=W_{1}
and the proof is finished.
∎

Corollary 6.6
.

The Parseval frames constructed in Proposition
6.3
are optimal.

Proof.

We first notice that if
\{e_{j}\}_{j\in J}
and
\{E_{j}\}_{j\in J}
are orthonormal basis of

and

respectively then

and

are non-zero for all

This is true because for

we have

then

what implies that

.
Thus

Also since

, then

, so

Now, from Lemma
6.1
, Proposition

and Proposition

we know that

\gamma_{p}(A_{N})=\gamma_{p}(S^{*}|_{N})=\dim(\mathcal{L})=\dim(W_{1})=\dim({\mathcal{K}}_{1}).

Altogether, we have,

∎

6.2.
Construction of optimal frames for
T
and

.

This subsection presents a construction of optimal Parseval frames of iterations for

and

where

is a contraction with the joint strong stability property. Specifically, for

where

(7)

T=VA_{N}V^{-1}\text{ and }T^{*}=VS^{*}|_{N}V^{-1},

for ever

7.
Similarity between frames of iterations of

In this section we examine the similarity between the frames of iterations of an operator

constructed in Section

. For this we exploit Theorem
3.2
giving explicitly the similarity between
T
and a shift compression in a model space.

If
\{T^{n}v_{i}\}_{i\in I,n\geqslant 0}
is a frame of iterations of
{\mathcal{H}}
, where

I
is an at most countable index set, and
\{v_{i}\}_{i\in I}
is a collection of vectors in
{\mathcal{H}},
this frame determines its synthesis operator defined as follows.

Definition 7.1
.

The synthesis operator of the frame of iterations
\{T^{n}v_{i}\}_{i\in I,n\geqslant 0}
is the operator
C:H^{2}_{\ell^{2}(I)}\to{\mathcal{H}}
defined as

Cf=\sum_{i\in I,n\geqslant 0}\langle f,S^{n}e_{i}\rangle T^{n}v_{i},

where
\{e_{i}\}_{i\in I}
is the canonical orthonormal basis of
\ell^{2}(I)
and
S
is the unilateral shift operator acting on
H^{2}_{\ell^{2}(I)}
.

It is easily seen that
C
intertwines
S
with
T
, that is,
TC=CS
and consequently the subspace
\ker(C)\subseteq H^{2}_{\ell^{2}(I)}
is
S
-invariant. Moreover, as
C
is surjective, we have that its restriction to the subspace
N:=H^{2}_{\ell^{2}(I)}\ominus\ker(C)
, which is a model space (since it is
S^{*}
-invariant), is a bijective map. Also,
C
satisfies
C(P_{N}e_{i})=v_{i}
for all
i\in I
. Thus,
V:=C|_{N}
is a similarity between
T
and the shift compression
A_{N}=P_{N}S|_{N}
that maps the frame
\{T^{n}v_{i}\}_{i\in I,n\geqslant 0}
in
{\mathcal{H}}
to the frame
\{A_{N}^{n}(P_{N}e_{i})\}_{i\in I,n\geqslant 0}
in
N
.

In short, recognizing that the synthesis operator establishes the similarity and that the representing model space is defined by its kernel allows us to determine when the frames of iterations of an operator and its adjoint are similar. Since, frames of iterations of
T
and
T^{*}
are similar to frames of iterations of
A_{N}
(basic frame) and
S^{*}
(
adjoint frame
) respectively, in the same model space
N
, we start by studying the similarity between the two latest.

7.1.
Similarity between frames of iterations of
A_{N}
and
S^{*}
in model spaces

In order to study the similarity between the frames of iterations of
A_{N}
and
S^{*}
in a model space, we introduce the following definition.

Definition 7.2
.

Let
\{e_{i}\}_{i\in I}
be an orthonormal basis of
{\mathcal{K}}
,
Q\in{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathcal{I}}({\mathcal{K}})
, and
N=H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}}\ominus\widehat{Q}(H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}})
. The adjoint frame of the basic frame
\{A_{N}^{n}(P_{N}e_{i})\}_{i\in I,n\geqslant 0}
is

\{(S^{*})S^{*}E_{i}\}_{i\in I,n\geqslant 0}

where
E_{i}=\widehat{Q}e_{i}
for ever
i\in I
.

Notice that since
Q\in{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathcal{I}}({\mathcal{K}})
,
\widehat{Q}(H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}})
is a full range
S
-invariant space and then, the adjoint frame of the above definiton, is the frame constructed in Theorem
5.5
for a particular choice of the orthonormal basis
\{E_{i}\}_{i\in I}
of
W
, namely
E_{i}=\widehat{Q}e_{i}
for ever
i\in I
.

We address the question of when a
basic frame
is similar to its
adjoint frame
by providing a complete characterization in terms of the inner functions that define them.

At this point, we would like to briefly summarize the results and concepts we have obtained so far in an informal way before moving on to the next result.

Model spaces, basic frames and adjoint frame

As a consequence of Theorem
3.2
, every frame of iterations is similar to a basic frame
\mathfrak{B}_{N}=\{A_{N}^{n}(P_{N}e_{i})\}_{i\in I,n\geqslant 0}
in a model space
N\subseteq H_{{\mathcal{K}}}^{2}.
This basic frame is uniquely determined once a basis of
{\mathcal{K}}
is fixed.
We denote by

\mathfrak{B}_{N}^{*}=\{(S^{*})^{n}S^{*}E_{i}\}_{i\in I,n\geqslant 0}
the adjoint frame of
\mathfrak{B}_{N}
.

Now, given a pair
(\mathcal{E},Q)
where
\mathcal{E}=\{e_{i}\}_{i\in I}
is an orthonormal basis of
{\mathcal{K}}
and
Q\in{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathcal{I}}({\mathcal{K}})
, it univocally determines the following objects:

•

M=\widehat{Q}(H_{{\mathcal{K}}}^{2})
a full range
S
-invariant subspace of
H_{{\mathcal{K}}}^{2}
. (see Theorem
5.4
);

•

N=H_{{\mathcal{K}}}^{2}\ominus M
the model space associated to
M
, who is
S^{*}
- invariant;

•

\mathfrak{B}_{N}=\{A_{N}^{n}(P_{N}e_{i})\}_{i\in I,n\geqslant 0}
the basic frame for
N
;

•

\mathfrak{B}_{N}^{*}=\{(S^{*})^{n}S^{*}E_{i}\}_{i\in I,n\geqslant 0}
the adjoint frame of
\mathfrak{B}_{N}.

The question we now seek to answer is whether a basic frame of iterations
\mathfrak{B}_{N}
is similar to its adjoint frame
\mathfrak{B}_{N}^{*}.

Below, we provide an answer.

We start with the following map, that will be crucial for the characterization:

Definition 7.3
.

Let
\rho:{\mathcal{A}}({\mathcal{K}})\to{\mathcal{A}}({\mathcal{K}})
the mapping given by
\rho(Q)(z):=Q(\overline{z})^{*}
for almost every
z\in{\mathbb{T}}
.

Remark 7.4
.

(i)

The map
\rho
is well-defined. To see this, first recall that the adjoint of a partial isometry is again a partial isometry. Now fix
\{e_{i}\}_{i\in I}
an orthonormal basis of
{\mathcal{K}}
and let
Q\in{\mathcal{A}}({\mathcal{K}})
. Then, given
f\in H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}}
, we have for every
i\in I
and
n>0
,

\displaystyle{\langle{\widehat{\rho(Q)}f,e_{i}{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\chi$}}}^{-n}}\rangle}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{T}}{\langle{Q(\overline{z})^{*}f(z),e_{i}\overline{z}^{n}}\rangle}_{\mathcal{K}}\,{\mathrm{d}}z

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{T}}{\langle{f(z),\overline{z}^{n}Q(\overline{z})e_{i}}\rangle}_{\mathcal{K}}\,{\mathrm{d}}z=0

because
Q\in{\mathcal{A}}({\mathcal{K}})
and
n>0
implies that
g
defined by
g(z)=z^{n}Q(z)e_{i}
is analytic and not constant. Thus, the function
\tilde{g}
defined by
\tilde{g}(z)=g(\overline{z})
belongs to
L^{2}({\mathbb{T}},{\mathcal{K}})\ominus H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}}
. Hence,
\widehat{\rho(Q)}f\in(L^{2}({\mathbb{T}},{\mathcal{K}})\ominus H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}})^{\perp}=H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}}
.

(ii)

From the definition is clear that
\rho
is an involution, that is,
\rho(\rho(Q))=Q
.

(iii)

The map
\rho
sends inner functions to inner functions. Indeed, if
Q\in{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathcal{I}}({\mathcal{K}})
then
\rho(Q)
is analytic because of
(i)
and
\rho(Q)(z)=Q(\overline{z})^{*}
is a unitary map for almost every
z\in{\mathbb{T}}
because
Q(\overline{z})
is.

We are now ready to prove the main result of this section.

Theorem 7.5
.

Let
\mathcal{E}=\{e_{i}\}_{i\in I}
be an orthonormal basis of
{\mathcal{K}}
and
Q\in{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathcal{I}}({\mathcal{K}})
and consider the model subspace
N=H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}}\ominus\widehat{Q}(H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}})
. Let
\mathfrak{B}_{N}^{*}=\{(S^{*})^{n}S^{*}E_{i}\}_{i\in I,n\geqslant 0}
be the adjoint frame of
\mathfrak{B}_{N}=\{A_{N}^{n}(P_{N}e_{i})\}_{i\in I,n\geqslant 0}
. Then, the kernel of the synthesis operator
C:H^{2}_{{\mathcal{K}}}\to N
of
\mathfrak{B}_{N}^{*}
is given by

\ker C=\widehat{\rho(Q)}H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}}.

Proof.

A function
f\in H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}}
belongs to
\ker C
if and only if for all
m\geqslant 0
and
j\in I
we have that
{\langle{Cf,S^{m}e_{j}}\rangle}=0
. Fix
m\geqslant 0,j\in I
and use the definition of
C
to get

\displaystyle{\langle{Cf,S^{m}e_{j}}\rangle}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in I,n\geqslant 0}{\langle{f,S^{n}e_{i}}\rangle}{\langle{(S^{*})^{n}(S^{*}E_{i}),S^{m}e_{j}}\rangle}.

Then, by replacing
E_{i}=\widehat{Q}e_{i}
we have

{\langle{Cf,S^{m}e_{j}}\rangle}=\sum_{i\in I,n\geqslant 0}{\langle{f,S^{n}e_{i}}\rangle}{\langle{(S^{*})^{n+m}(S^{*}\widehat{Q}e_{i}),e_{j}}\rangle}=\sum_{i\in I,n\geqslant 0}{\langle{f,S^{n}e_{i}}\rangle}{\langle{\widehat{Q}e_{i},S^{n+m+1}e_{j}}\rangle}.

Note that

{\langle{\widehat{Q}e_{i},S^{n+m+1}e_{j}}\rangle}=\int_{\mathbb{T}}{\langle{Q(z)e_{i},z^{n+m+1}e_{j}}\rangle}\,{\mathrm{d}}z=\int_{\mathbb{T}}{\langle{e_{i},z^{n+m+1}Q(z)^{*}e_{j}}\rangle}\,{\mathrm{d}}z.

Next, we will use the following fact: if
g(z)=\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}a_{n}z^{n}
is an scalar function in
L^{2}({\mathbb{T}})
then
h(z):=g(\overline{z})=\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}a_{n}\overline{z}^{n}=\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}a_{-n}z^{n}
has the same
0^{th}
-Fourier coefficient. Applying this to
g(z)={\langle{e_{i},z^{n+m+1}Q(z)^{*}e_{j}}\rangle}
yields

\displaystyle{\langle{\widehat{Q}e_{i},S^{n+m+1}e_{j}}\rangle}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{T}}{\langle{e_{i},\overline{z}^{n+m+1}Q(\overline{z})^{*}e_{j}}\rangle}\,{\mathrm{d}}z

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{T}}{\langle{z^{n+m+1}e_{i},Q(\overline{z})^{*}e_{j}}\rangle}\,{\mathrm{d}}z

\displaystyle={\langle{S^{n+m+1}e_{i},\widehat{\rho(Q)}e_{j}}\rangle}

\displaystyle={\langle{S^{n}e_{i},(S^{*})^{m}S^{*}\widehat{\rho(Q)}e_{j}}\rangle}

Altogether, this gives

{\langle{Cf,S^{m}e_{j}}\rangle}=\sum_{i\in I,n\geqslant 0}{\langle{f,S^{n}e_{i}}\rangle}{\langle{S^{n}e_{i},(S^{*})^{m}S^{*}(\widehat{\rho(Q)}e_{j})}\rangle}.

By using Parseval’s identity with the orthonormal basis
\{S^{n}e_{i}\}_{i\in I,n\geqslant 0}
we get

{\langle{Cf,S^{m}e_{j}}\rangle}={\langle{f,(S^{*})^{m}S^{*}(\widehat{\rho(Q)}e_{j})}\rangle}.

Therefore

\ker C=\{(S^{*})^{m}S^{*}(F_{j}):j\in I,m\geqslant 0\}^{\perp}

where
F_{i}=\widehat{\rho(Q)}e_{i}\in H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}}
for every
i\in I
.

Now, since
Q\in{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathcal{I}}
,
Q(z)
is a unitary operator for almost every
z\in{\mathbb{T}}
and then, so is
Q(\overline{z})^{*}
for almost every
z\in{\mathbb{T}}
. Thus, as for every
i\in I
,
F_{i}(z)=Q(\overline{z})^{*}e_{i}
for almost every
z\in{\mathbb{T}}
, we get that
\{F_{i}(z)\}_{i\in I}
is an orthonormal basis of
{\mathcal{K}}
for almost every
z\in{\mathbb{T}}
.
Further, the following identity holds

Q(\overline{z})^{*}Q(z)^{-1}E_{i}(z)=Q(\overline{z})^{*}e_{i}=F_{i}(z),

for almost every
z\in{\mathbb{T}}
. Therefore, the analytic operator-valued function
V:{\mathbb{T}}\to{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{K}})
given by
V(z):=Q(\overline{z})^{*}Q(z)^{-1}
for almost every
z\in{\mathbb{T}}
satisfies that
V(z)
is a unitary operator that sends the orthonormal basis
\{E_{i}(z)\}_{i\in I}
to
\{F_{i}(z)\}_{i\in I}
for almost every
z\in{\mathbb{T}}
.
Then,
\widehat{V}:L^{2}({\mathbb{T}},{\mathcal{K}})\to L^{2}({\mathbb{T}},{\mathcal{K}})
defines isometry in
L^{2}({\mathbb{T}},{\mathcal{K}})
that commutes with the bilateral shift
U
(in
L^{2}({\mathbb{T}},{\mathcal{K}})
) and maps
\{E_{i}\}_{i\in I}
to
\{F_{i}\}_{i\in I}
.

Denoting by
M=\widehat{Q}(H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}})
, as
\{E_{i}\}_{i\in I}
is an orthonormal basis of
M\ominus S(M)
and
\widehat{V}(M)
is
S
-invariant,
\{F_{i}\}_{i\in I}
must be an orthonormal basis of
\widehat{V}(M)\ominus S\widehat{V}(M)
. Then, on one hand, by Lemma
3.7
,
\{S^{n}F_{i}\}_{i\in I,n\geqslant 0}
is an orthonormal basis of
\widehat{V}(M)
. On the other hand, we can use Theorem
5.5
to conclude that system
\{(S^{*})^{n}S^{*}(F_{i})\}_{i\in I,n\geqslant 0}
is a Parseval frame of
\widehat{V}(M)^{\perp}
.
Finally,

\displaystyle\ker C

\displaystyle=\{(S^{*})^{n}S^{*}(F_{i}):i\in I,n\geqslant 0\}^{\perp}=(\widehat{V}(M)^{\perp})^{\perp}=\widehat{V}(M)

\displaystyle=\overline{\text{span}}\{S^{n}F_{i}\}_{i\in I,n\geqslant 0}=\widehat{\rho(Q)}H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}}.

which finishes the proof.
∎

As a consequence of Theorem
7.5
and Theorem
3.2
we obtain the following result.

Corollary 7.6
.

Let
\{e_{i}\}_{i\in I}
be an orthonormal basis of
{\mathcal{K}}
,
Q\in{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathcal{I}}({\mathcal{K}})
and consider the model space
N=H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}}\ominus\widehat{Q}H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}}
. Then, the basic frame associated to
\mathfrak{B}_{N}^{*}=\{(S^{*})^{n}S^{*}E_{i}\}_{i\in I,n\geqslant 0}

is given by

\mathfrak{B}_{N_{\rho}}=\{A_{N_{\rho}}^{n}(P_{N_{\rho}}e_{i})\}_{i\in I,n\geqslant 0}

where
N_{\rho}=H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}}\ominus\widehat{\rho(Q)}H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}}
.

Note that, by item
(iii)
of Remark
7.4
, the
S
-invariant subspace
\widehat{\rho(Q)}H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}}
is full range.

The involutive nature of
\rho
implies that if we start with the basic frame
\mathfrak{B}_{N_{\rho}}
and repeat the process of taking the adjoint frame and then finding its associated basic frame, we return to
\mathfrak{B}_{N}
.
To summarize, let us denote by
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
the correspondence that assigns to a frame of iterations, its similar basic frame.
Furthermore, given any basic frame
\mathfrak{B}_{\widetilde{N}}
in a model space
\widetilde{N}
, whose orthogonal complement is full range, we denote by

\boldsymbol{\beta}(\mathfrak{B}_{\widetilde{N}})
its adjoint frame.

Then, taking the adjoint frame of a basic one and then its associated basic frame, must be performed twice to recover the original basic frame we started with. That is,
(\boldsymbol{\alpha\beta})^{2}(\mathfrak{B}_{N})
is similar to
\mathfrak{B}_{N}
as we show in the following diagram:

\begin{CD}\mathfrak{B}_{N}=\left\{A_{N}^{n}(P_{N}e_{i})\right\}_{i\in I,n\geqslant 0}@>{\boldsymbol{\beta}}>{}>\mathfrak{B}_{N}^{*}=\left\{(S^{*})^{n}S^{*}(\widehat{Q}e_{i})\right\}_{i\in I,n\geqslant 0}\\
@A{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}A{}A@V{}V{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}V\\
\mathfrak{B}_{N_{\rho}}^{*}=\left\{(S^{*})^{n}S^{*}(\rho(\widehat{Q})e_{i})\right\}_{i\in I,n\geqslant 0}@<{\boldsymbol{\beta}}<{}<\mathfrak{B}_{N_{\rho}}=\left\{A_{N_{\rho}}^{n}(P_{N_{\rho}}e_{i})\right\}_{i\in I,n\geqslant 0}\end{CD}

Proposition 7.7
.

Let
\{e_{i}\}_{i\in I}
be an orthonormal basis of
{\mathcal{K}}
,
Q\in{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathcal{I}}({\mathcal{K}})
and consider the model space
N=H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}}\ominus\widehat{Q}H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}}
. Then the basic frame associated to
\mathfrak{B}_{N}^{*}=\{(S^{*})^{n}S^{*}(\widehat{Q}e_{i})\}_{i\in I,n\geqslant 0}
, has an adjoint frame which is similar to
\{A_{N}^{n}(P_{N}e_{i})\}_{i\in I,n\geqslant 0}
.
That is,

\boldsymbol{\alpha}\boldsymbol{\beta}\boldsymbol{\alpha}(\mathfrak{B}_{N}^{*})=\mathfrak{B}_{N}.

Proof.

By Corollary
7.6
, the basic frame associated to
\mathfrak{B}_{N}^{*}
is
\{A_{N_{\rho}}^{n}(P_{N_{\rho}}e_{i})\}_{i\in I,n\geqslant 0}

where
N_{\rho}=H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}}\ominus\widehat{\rho(Q)}H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}}
. The adjoint frame of the latter is
\{(S^{*})^{n}S^{*}\widehat{\rho(Q)}e_{i}\}_{i\in I,n\geqslant 0}
.

Again, by Corollary
7.6
the basic frame associated to
\{(S^{*})^{n}S^{*}\widehat{\rho(Q)}e_{i}\}_{i\in I,n\geqslant 0}
is built up from the model space
H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}}\ominus\widehat{\rho(\rho(Q))}H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}}
. But, since
\rho
is an involution, we have that

\widehat{\rho(\rho(Q))}H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}}=\widehat{Q}H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}}=N,

and thus, the basic frame obtained is
\{A_{N}^{n}(P_{N}e_{i})\}_{i\in I,n\geqslant 0}
as we wanted.
∎

Due to Corollary
7.6
, the question of when a basic frame and its adjoint frame are similar reduces to ask when
\widehat{Q}
and
\widehat{\rho(Q)}
coincide.

To answer this, we need to recall that the partial order between
S
-invariant subspaces of
H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}}
characterized by inner functions induces a relation between the corresponding inner functions as the following proposition says.

Proposition 7.8

(
[
20
, Theorem 10]
)
.

Let
Q_{1},Q_{2}\in{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathcal{I}}
. Then
\widehat{Q}_{1}(H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}})\subseteq\widehat{Q}_{2}(H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}})
if and only if
Q_{2}^{*}Q_{1}\in{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathcal{I}}
.

Also, in the finite-dimensional case the next result holds.

Proposition 7.9

(
[
20
, Corollary of Theorem 11]
)
.

Let
{\mathcal{K}}
a finite-dimensional space and
Q\in{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathcal{I}}
. If the determinant
q:{\mathbb{T}}\to{\mathbb{C}}
,
q(z)=\det(Q(z))
is constant, then so is
Q
.

The final ingredient we need is to see how
\rho
acts on the Fourier expansion(
3
). If
Q\in{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathcal{I}}
can be written as

Q(z)=\sum_{n\geqslant 0}Q_{n}z^{n}

where
Q_{n}
are bounded operators and the convergence is understood in the strong operator topology, then it follows that

\rho(Q)(z)=Q(\overline{z})^{*}=\sum_{n\geqslant 0}Q_{n}^{*}z^{n}.

And since the coefficient-operators of a Fourier expansion are unique, this implies that
Q_{n}^{*}
is the
n^{th}
-Fourier coefficient of
\rho(Q)
for all
n\geqslant 0
.

We now give several characterizations of the case when between a basic frame and its adjoint are similar.

Theorem 7.10
.

Let
{\mathcal{K}}
be a separable Hilbert space and
Q\in{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathcal{I}}
with Fourier coefficients
\{Q_{n}\}_{n}
. The following statements are equivalent:

(i)

\widehat{\rho(Q)}H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}}=\widehat{Q}H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}}
;

(ii)

There exist a unitary operator
A:{\mathcal{K}}\to{\mathcal{K}}
such that
AQ_{n}=Q_{n}A=(AQ_{n})^{*}
for all
n\geqslant 0
.

Moreover, if
\dim({\mathcal{K}})<\infty
then (i) and (ii) are also equivalent to

(iii)

\widehat{\rho(Q)}H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}}\subseteq\widehat{Q}H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}}
;

(iv)

\widehat{Q}H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}}\subseteq\widehat{\rho(Q)}H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}}
.

Proof.

We prove that
(i)\Leftrightarrow(ii)
. Suppose first that
\widehat{\rho(Q)}H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}}=\widehat{Q}H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}}
. Then, by the unicity part of Theorem
5.1
there exists a unitary operator
\widehat{R}
such that
R(z)=R:{\mathcal{K}}\to{\mathcal{K}}
almost all
z\in{\mathbb{T}}
and
\widehat{\rho(Q)}=\widehat{Q}\widehat{R}
. Applying
\rho
on both sides yields

\widehat{Q}=\widehat{\rho(\rho(Q))}=\widehat{\rho(R)}\widehat{\rho(Q)}=\widehat{R}^{*}\widehat{Q}\widehat{R}.

Then, since the Fourier coefficients of
Q
are unique, we get the following relation
Q_{n}^{*}=RQ_{n}=Q_{n}R
for all
n\geqslant 0
.

Now, since
R
is unitary, we can apply the functional calculus for normal operators to find a unitary operator
A
such
A^{2}=R
and
AQ_{n}=Q_{n}A
for all
n\geqslant 0
. Also

(AQ_{n})^{*}=Q_{n}^{*}A^{*}=(Q_{n}R)A^{*}=Q_{n}A^{2}A^{*}=Q_{n}A=AQ_{n}

holds for all
n\geqslant 0
.

Reciprocally, assume there exist a unitary operator
A
such that
AQ_{n}=Q_{n}A=(AQ_{n})^{*}
for all
n\geqslant 0
and define
R:=A^{2}
. Then

Q_{n}R=Q_{n}A^{2}=(Q_{n}A)A=(AQ_{n})A=(AQ_{n})^{*}A=Q_{n}^{*}A^{*}A=Q_{n}^{*}

for all
n\geqslant 0
. Hence,
\rho(Q)(z)=Q(z)R
for almost all
z\in{\mathbb{T}}
which implies that
\widehat{\rho(Q)}H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}}=\widehat{Q}H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}}
.

Suppose now that
\dim({\mathcal{K}})=d
. Then, it is clear that
(i)\Rightarrow(iii)
and
(i)\Rightarrow(iv)
. For the converses we will only show that
(iv)\Rightarrow(i)
as the proof for
(iii)\Rightarrow(i)
is analogous.

The inclusion
\widehat{Q}H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}}\subseteq\widehat{\rho(Q)}H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}}
implies by Proposition
7.8
that
\rho(Q)^{*}Q
is inner.
Set
q(z)=\det(Q(z))
and note that

\det(\rho(Q)(z)^{*}Q(z))=\det(Q(\overline{z})Q(z))=q(\overline{z})q(z)

for almost every
z\in{\mathbb{T}}
. The fact that
\rho(Q)^{*}Q
is analytic, implies that the
L^{2}({\mathbb{T}})
-function
f(z)=q(\overline{z})q(z)
must be analytic. But since
f(z)=f(\overline{z})
for almost every
z\in{\mathbb{T}}
,
f
must be constant. Thus, by Proposition
7.9
,
\rho(Q)^{*}Q
must also be constant. In other words, there exists a fixed unitary operator
R
such that
Q(z)=\rho(Q)(z)R
for almost every
z\in{\mathbb{T}}
and thus
\widehat{\rho(Q)}H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}}=\widehat{Q}H^{2}_{\mathcal{K}}
.
∎

7.2.
More results in the scalar case

In this section we show that when
{\mathcal{K}}={\mathbb{C}}
in Theorem
7.10
we are able to establish two more equivalent conditions that allows us to say when a basic frame is similar to its adjoint frame.

Notice that the word ‘inner’ in Definition
5.3
is chosen so that it extends that of scalar inner functions. Recall that we say that
q:{\mathbb{T}}\to{\mathbb{C}}
is inner if it is in
H^{2}
and
{\left|{q(z)}\right|}=1
for almost every
z\in{\mathbb{T}}
. Thus, trivially, the map
\zeta\mapsto q(z)\zeta
defines a unitary operator in
{\mathbb{C}}
for almost every
z\in{\mathbb{T}}
. Additionally, since
q\in H^{2}
and is bounded, the product
z\mapsto q(z)f(z)
is in
H^{2}
for any
f\in H^{2}
. Altogether, this means that scalar inner functions belong to
{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathcal{I}}({\mathbb{C}})
and moreover it is not hard to see that they are actually the entire set.

Applying Theorem
5.1
to this case we obtain the following.

Theorem 7.11

(Beurling)
.

A closed subspace
M\subseteq H^{2}
is
S
-invariant if and only
M=qH^{2}
for some inner function
q
. If
q_{1}
and
q_{2}
are inner functions such that
q_{1}H^{2}=q_{2}H^{2}
, then
q_{1}=c\,q_{2}
, for some
c\in{\mathbb{T}}
.

We remark that, in this case the associated function in(
4
) is always inner and thus all
S
-invariant subspaces of
H^{2}
are full range.

The basic types of inner functions are complex constants of module one,
Blaschke products and singular functions. We recall that a Blaschke product is a function

(9)

B(z)=\alpha z^{M}\prod_{n\geqslant 1}\frac{\overline{a_{n}}}{|a_{n}|}\frac{a_{n}-z}{1-\overline{a_{n}}z},\quad z\in{\mathbb{D}}

where
\alpha\in{\mathbb{T}}
,
M\geqslant 0
and
\{a_{n}\}_{n}\subseteq{\mathbb{D}}\setminus\{0\}
is a Blaschke sequence, i.e., it satisfies the condition
\sum_{n\geqslant 1}(1-|a_{n}|)<\infty
.

Moreover, a
singular inner function
has the form

(10)

S_{\mu}(z)=\alpha\exp\left(-\int_{\mathbb{T}}\frac{w+z}{w-z}\,d\mu(w)\right),\quad z\in{\mathbb{D}}

where
\alpha\in{\mathbb{T}}
and
\mu
is a positive measure on
{\mathbb{T}}
which is singular respect to the normalized Lebesgue measure on
{\mathbb{T}}
.

In fact, a theorem due to Nevanlinna and F. Riesz (see
[
18
, Theorem 2.14]
) shows that every inner function
q
can be factorized as
q(z)=z^{k}B(z)S_{\mu}(z)
, where
k\geqslant 0
,
B
is a Blaschke product and
S_{\mu}
is a singular inner function. For more details on the structure of inner functions we refer the reader to
[
18
, Section 2.3]
.

The
S
-invariant subspaces of
H^{2}
can be partially ordered according to the divisibility of inner functions. This is,
q_{1}H^{2}\subseteq q_{2}H^{2}
if and only if
q_{2}
divides
q_{1}
in the sense that there exists a function
q\in H^{2}
such that
q_{1}=q_{2}q
.

The following lemma will be useful for the proof of Proposition
7.14
and it is a consequence of
[
18
, Proposition 4.10]
.

Lemma 7.12
.

(i)

If
B_{1}
and
B_{2}
are two Blaschke products, then
B_{1}
divides
B_{2}
if and only if the set of zeros of
B_{1}
is contained in the set of zeros of
B_{2}
.

(ii)

If
S_{\mu_{1}}
and
S_{\mu_{2}}
are two singular functions, then
S_{\mu_{1}}
divides
S_{\mu_{1}}
if and only if
\mu_{2}-\mu_{1}
is a positive measure.

(iii)

If
q_{1}=c_{1}B_{1}S_{\mu_{1}}
and
q_{2}=c_{2}B_{2}S_{\mu_{2}}
are two inner functions with their respective factorizations, then
q_{1}
divides
q_{2}
if and only if
B_{1}
divides
B_{2}
and
S_{\mu_{1}}
divides
S_{\mu_{2}}
.

Theorem
7.5
here reads as follows. Observe that the involution
\rho

\rho(q)(z)=q(\overline{z})
for any inner function
q

Proposition 7.13
.

Let
q\in H^{2}
be an inner function and denote
N=H^{2}\ominus qH^{2}
. The kernel of the synthesis operator
C
for
\{(S^{*})^{n}S^{*}q\}_{n\geqslant 0}
given by

\ker C=\rho(q)H^{2}

where
\rho(q)(z)=\overline{q(\overline{z})}
for almost all
z\in{\mathbb{T}}
.

Now, we give the analogue of Theorem
7.10
that characterize when the kernel of the synthesis operators of a basic frame and its adjoint frame coincide. In this case we add two more equivalent conditions to those given in Theorem
7.10
.

Theorem 7.14
.

Let
q\in H^{2}
be an inner function. The following statements are equivalent

(i)

\rho(q)H^{2}=qH^{2}

(ii)

\rho(q)H^{2}\subseteq qH^{2}

(iii)

qH^{2}\subseteq\rho(q)H^{2}

(iv)

The set of zeros of
q
in
{\mathbb{D}}
is closed under conjugation counting multiplicities, and the singular measure
\mu
associated to
q
is also invariant under conjugation.

(v)

There exist a constant
\alpha\in{\mathbb{T}}
such that
\alpha\,\hat{q}(n)
is real for all
n\geqslant 0
.

Proof.

The equivalences
(i)\Leftrightarrow(ii)\Leftrightarrow(iii)
were already proven in Theorem
7.10
. To prove
(iii)\Leftrightarrow(iv)
first note that the inclusion
qH^{2}\subseteq\rho(q)H^{2}
is equivalent to
\frac{q}{\rho(q)}\in H^{2}
. Next, write
q
as
q(z)=b(z)s_{\mu}(z)
where
b
is a Blaschke product and
s_{\mu}
a singular inner function. Thus
\rho(q)(z)=\rho(b)(z)\rho(s_{\mu})(z)
. Is clear that
\tilde{b}(z):=\rho(b)(z)
is a Blaschke product with the set of zeros been the conjugates of those of
b(z)
(counting multiplicity), and as for
\rho(s_{\mu})(z)
note that

\rho(s_{\mu})(z)=\overline{s_{\mu}(\bar{z})}=\overline{\exp\left(-\int_{\mathbb{T}}\frac{\zeta+\bar{z}}{\zeta-\bar{z}}\,{\mathrm{d}}\mu(\zeta)\right)}=\exp\left(\overline{-\int_{\mathbb{T}}\frac{\zeta+\bar{z}}{\zeta-\bar{z}}\,{\mathrm{d}}\mu(\zeta)}\right)\\
=\exp\left(-\int_{\mathbb{T}}\frac{\overline{\zeta+\bar{z}}}{\overline{\zeta-\bar{z}}}\,{\mathrm{d}}\mu(\zeta)\right)=\exp\left(-\int_{\mathbb{T}}\frac{\bar{\zeta}+z}{\bar{\zeta}-z}\,{\mathrm{d}}\mu(\zeta)\right)=\exp\left(-\int_{\mathbb{T}}\frac{\zeta+z}{\zeta-z}\,{\mathrm{d}}\tilde{\mu}(\zeta)\right)

where
\tilde{\mu}
is the push-foward measure by conjugation, i.e.
\tilde{\mu}(A)=\mu(\bar{A})
for all measurable
A\subseteq{\mathbb{T}}
. In sum,
\rho(q)(z)=\tilde{b}(z)s_{\tilde{\mu}}(z)
is the decomposition of
\rho(q)
into a Blaschke product and a singular inner function. By Lemma
7.12
,
\frac{q}{Lq}\in H^{2}
if and only if all zeros in
B
are closed under conjugation with the same multiplicity and the measure
\mu
is invariant under conjugation as
\mu(A)-\mu(\bar{A})\geqslant 0
for all measurable subset
A\subseteq{\mathbb{T}}
(which implies
\mu(A)\geqslant\mu(\bar{A})\geqslant\mu(\bar{\bar{A}}))=\mu(A)
for all
A\subseteq{\mathbb{T}}
measurable).

To finish the proof we will see the equivalence
(i)\Leftrightarrow(v)
. Suppose that
\rho(q)H^{2}=qH^{2}
. By the unicity part of Beurling’s Theorem there exists
c\in{\mathbb{T}}
such that
q(z)=c\,\rho(q)(z)
for almost all
z\in{\mathbb{T}}
. Then

\widehat{q}(n)={\langle{c\rho(q),S^{n}1}\rangle}=c{\langle{\rho(q),S^{n}1}\rangle}=c\,\overline{\widehat{q}(n)}

holds for all
n\geqslant 0
. Let
c=e^{i\theta}
for some
\theta\in[0,2\pi)
. Then multiplying both sides in the previous equation by
\widehat{q}(n)
gives us
\widehat{q}(n)^{2}=e^{i\theta}{\left|{\widehat{q}(n)}\right|}^{2}
for all
n\geqslant 0
, or equivalently,
\widehat{q}(n)=\pm e^{i\theta/2}{\left|{\widehat{q}(n)}\right|}
. Therefore, if we call
\alpha=e^{-i\theta/2}
, we have
\alpha\,\widehat{q}(n)\in{\mathbb{R}}
for all
n\geqslant 0
.

Conversely, if
q
is an inner function whose Fourier expansion is

q(z)=\alpha\sum_{n\geqslant 0}\beta_{n}z^{n}
for some
\alpha\in{\mathbb{T}}
and
\{\beta_{n}\}_{n}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}
then
\rho(q)(z)=\overline{\alpha}\sum_{n\geqslant 0}\beta_{n}z^{n}=\frac{\overline{\alpha}}{\alpha}q(z)
and thus
qH^{2}=\rho(q)H^{2}
.
∎
