Prediction models and associated uncertainty for dynamic shear modulus and damping ratio of Beijing silty clay
Authors: Yi Fang, Guanlan Xu, Zhengyang Zhang, Guoxing Chen, Yuejun Lv, Dandan Xu, Qi Wu, Zhenglong Zhou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Based on experimental data of normalized shear modulus and damping ratio from undisturbed silty clay within 120 m depth in the Beijing area, the Davidenkov backbone curve is used to characterize the normalized shear modulus curve, and the damping ratio is formulated as a composite function of the small-strain damping ratio and the normalized shear modulus. It is found that the key parameters A, B, γr describing the Davidenkov model, as well as the small-strain damping ratio, all exhibit significant depth dependence. Accordingly, depth-dependent prediction models for the normalized shear modulus and damping ratio are established. Furthermore, the first-order second-moment method is employed to systematically analyze the uncertainty in the predicted values of the normalized shear modulus resulting from the combined effects of parameters A, B, and γr, as well as the evolution characteristics of the uncertainty in the predicted values of the normalized shear modulus caused solely by the individual uncertainties of parameter A, B, or γr with depth and shear strain. Simultaneously, the uncertainty in the predicted values of the damping ratio arising from the combined uncertainties of the normalized shear modulus and the small-strain damping ratio is quantified, along with the variation patterns of the uncertainty in the predicted values of the damping ratio caused solely by the uncertainties of either the normalized shear modulus or the small-strain damping ratio with depth and shear strain. The related research can provide guidance for determining regional soil dynamic parameters and precisely quantifying their uncertainties.

Introduction

The dynamic shear modulus ratio G/Gmax and damping ratio λ reveal the essential nonlinear behavior of soil under complex dynamic loading1–7. They are indispensable parameters for site seismic response analysis and soil-structure dynamic interaction analysis1–14 The variability of G/Gmax and λ can cause a high degree of uncertainty in the results of seismic response analysis of the site15–27. G/Gmax is the ratio of the dynamic shear modulus G of soil under dynamic loading to its initial shear modulus Gmax at small strains, directly reflecting the nonlinear degradation law of soil stiffness with increasing strain. The damping ratio λ characterizes the level of energy dissipation during soil vibration and is a key indicator for measuring the vibration attenuation efficiency of dynamic systems. Their coupled action not only determines the amplification or filtering effect of seismic waves by the site but also profoundly influences the analysis and solution of key engineering problems such as soil-structure interaction and foundation liquefaction risk. For example, soft soil sites may experience a significant decrease in dynamic shear modulus during strong earthquakes, leading to an extension of the site’s fundamental period. If the site’s fundamental period approaches the natural vibration period of the superstructure, resonance effects can be triggered, exacerbating structural damage. Furthermore, in soil liquefaction analysis, the rise in pore water pressure of saturated sandy soil under cyclic seismic loading leads to a loss of effective stress. At this point, the dynamic shear modulus approaches zero, and identifying this critical state is crucial for predicting liquefaction extent and assessing the risk of foundation instability. The analysis of the disaster mechanisms in the widespread liquefaction-induced building tilting and underground structure uplift in the Tokyo Bay reclaimed areas during the 2011 Tohoku earthquake in Japan relied heavily on in-depth studies of the degradation law of the sand’s dynamic shear modulus ratio and the growth law of its damping ratio.

By studying the G/Gmax-γa and λ-γa curves (where γa is the shear strain amplitude) of different soil types, the dynamic characteristics of a site can be precisely quantified, providing data support for site seismic response. Numerous scholars have investigated the main factors influencing the dynamic shear modulus G and damping ratio λ of soil using various methods, including resonant column tests28–30, cyclic simple shear tests31,32, cyclic triaxial tests33–35, torsional shear tests36,37, shaking table tests38, and centrifuge tests39,40. The results indicate that the shear strain amplitude γa, initial effective confining pressure \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\sigma ^{\prime}_{\text{c}}}$$\end{document} (or soil depth H), and plasticity index PI have a significant influence on G and λ values. In contrast, factors such as cyclic loading frequency, number of loading cycles, over-consolidation ratio, void ratio, saturation, and particle characteristics have a relatively minor influence41.

However, the limitations of testing methods increase the degree of data uncertainty: while resonant column tests can accurately measure parameters within the small strain range, they are inadequate for characterizing the laws of dynamic shear modulus attenuation and damping ratio increase under large deformations; field wave velocity testing, although reflecting in-situ soil properties, cannot directly obtain modulus degradation data under large strains. The spatial variability of geotechnical materials and testing errors lead to significant dispersion in dynamic parameters, necessitating the use of probabilistic and statistical methods to quantify the impact of parameter uncertainty on engineering analysis results.

Site response analysis requires providing the variation of G/Gmax-γa and λ-γa curves with depth for different soil types. Constrained by the costs of field sampling and laboratory testing, typically only a relatively sparse number of experimental results for soil G/Gmax-γa and λ-γa curves can be obtained, which is insufficient to meet the demands of site response analysis. Research indicates a significant correlation between soil G/Gmax, λ, and soil layer depth H42. This depth-dependence provides a basis for establishing parameter prediction models using limited representative borehole data.

Existing prediction models for G/Gmax and λ generally contain multiple correlated parameters42–46. The uncertainty caused by regression errors in their predictions essentially stems from a cascading effect caused by the propagation of variability in model parameters through an error transmission mechanism. Although current research often evaluates model reliability by directly comparing predicted values with experimental data44–46, few scholars have delved into investigating the fluctuation patterns of prediction results arising from the mathematical mapping of parameter uncertainty. This research gap makes it difficult to quantify the true propagation path of model errors in engineering practice, thereby hindering the establishment of an effective error correction framework. Therefore, there is an urgent need to construct a comprehensive research framework encompassing parameter sensitivity analysis and error propagation quantification to fundamentally enhance the engineering applicability of prediction models for soil dynamic property parameters.

This study, based on resonant column test results from 93 undisturbed silty clay specimens within 120 m depth in the Beijing area, establishes empirical relationships between the depth H and the Davidenkov model parameters (A, B, γr) as well as the small-strain damping ratio λmin, which describe the dynamic properties of the soil. Furthermore, the first-order second-moment (FOSM) method is employed to quantify the impact of uncertainty in parameters A, B, and γr on the uncertainty caused by regression errors of G/Gmax predictions through error propagation. Simultaneously, the influence of uncertainty in G/Gmax and λmin on the uncertainty caused by regression errors of λ predictions is revealed.

Experiment scheme

Soil sample overview and testing program

The test soil samples were obtained from boreholes ZK1 to ZK10 in the Beijing area. Figure 1 presents the spatial distribution map of boreholes ZK1 to ZK10. Undisturbed soil samples were continuously collected from the ground surface to a depth of 120 m along the same borehole using the static pressing method. Figure 2 shows the stratigraphic structure and shear wave velocity profile for boreholes ZK1 to ZK3. As evident from Fig. 2, the stratigraphic structures and shear wave velocity profiles of boreholes ZK1 to ZK3 exhibit distinct differences from each other. This fully demonstrates the significant spatial variability of stratigraphy and soil dynamic property parameters in the Beijing area. As shown in Fig. 1(b), these 10 boreholes are mainly located in the central area of Beijing, with no boreholes set up in the south and north. Therefore, the results of this study mainly reflect the soil characteristics of the central area of Beijing.

Testing apparatus

As shown in Fig. 3, the tests were conducted using the GCTS TSH-100 high-precision fixed-free resonant column apparatus (USA). This system employs a pneumatic servo system for precise control of the confining pressure and utilizes a fully automatic suspension-type torsional drive mechanism to apply excitation to the top of the specimen. The shear strain of the soil sample is monitored in real-time by an accelerometer mounted on the extended arm of the drive motor. Combined with an eight-channel digital acquisition system, the dynamic strain response is recorded at 0.2 ms intervals. Based on the time-history data, dynamic parameters such as the soil’s resonant frequency, maximum shear strain, shear wave velocity, and shear modulus can be derived. The measurement resolution of the system is on the order of 10⁻⁶.

Testing methodology

The testing procedure consisted of three stages: First, the undisturbed soil was processed into standard solid cylindrical specimens (geometric dimensions: diameter 50 mm, height 100 mm). Second, after mounting the specimen on the test base, the top suspension-type torsional drive mechanism and displacement transducer were connected. Following the sealing of the pressure chamber, isotropic consolidation was performed according to the natural stress state corresponding to the specimen’s in-situ burial depth.

There are no in-situ pore pressure measurements and oedometer tests. Isotropic consolidation for each specimen was conducted in this paper. Test results and analysis

Variation of G/Gmax and λ with γa and H

Figure 4 presents the variation of G values with shear strain amplitude γa for silty clay from different depth ranges within boreholes ZK1 to ZK8. At similar depths, G decreases as γa increases. At the same γa, G increases with increasing H.

The dynamic shear modulus value at γa<10⁻6 is termed the maximum dynamic shear modulus Gmax. Figure 5 shows the variation of Gmax with depth H. Gmax increases nonlinearly with increasing H, and their relationship can be described by a power function:

4\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$G_{{\max }} = a_{1} \left( {H/H_{{10}} } \right)^{{b_{1}}}$$\end{document}

where a1 and b1 are fitting parameters; H10 is the reference depth, H10 = 10 m.

Variation of dynamic shear modulus G with γa and H.

Fig. 5Variation of maximum dynamic shear modulus Gmax with H.

Variation of maximum dynamic shear modulus Gmax with H.

Figure 6 presents the variation of the dynamic shear modulus ratio G/Gmax with γa and H. Under small shear strains (γa ≤ 10⁻6), interparticle contacts are primarily elastic, the skeleton structure remains intact, G approaches its maximum value Gmax, and G/Gmax approaches 1. As γa increases, relative sliding and rotation between soil particles and pore structure adjustments occur, leading to a gradual loss of soil stiffness and a consequent decrease in G and G/Gmax. Simultaneously, the increase in γa causes relative sliding between soil particles, enhancing the energy dissipation efficiency of the system and resulting in an increase in λ. The figure also includes the G/Gmax curve for soil with PI = 15 (similar to the plasticity index of silty clay) proposed by Vucetic and Dobry49. It can be observed that the range of the G/Gmax curve for Beijing silty clay is significantly wider than that of the soil with PI = 15 presented by Vucetic and Dobry49, reflecting the considerable variability in the G/Gmax values of Beijing silty clay.

An increase in soil burial depth H leads to higher effective overburden pressure, increased interparticle contact pressure, reduced void ratio, and a denser soil particle microstructure. These factors significantly constrain the space available for particle sliding and structural reorganization, thereby enhancing the overall soil stiffness (increasing G and G/Gmax values). The densification of the soil under high confining pressure reduces the magnitude of relative displacement between particles and decreases frictional energy dissipation between particles. This leads to a reduction in energy dissipation efficiency per unit strain, resulting in a decrease in λ with increasing H.

The predicted values of parameters A, B, and γr from Eqs. (6), (7), and (8) inherently possess uncertainty. This uncertainty propagates to the predicted G/Gmax values. Similarly, when predicting λ using Eqs. (10), (13), and (14), the uncertainty in G/Gmax and λmin propagates to the predicted λ values. Therefore, this study employs the first-order second-moment (FOSM) method to analyze the variation of uncertainty in predicted G/Gmax and λ values, caused by the propagation of uncertainty in individual parameters, with depth H and shear strain amplitude γa.

Fundamental principle of the first-order second-moment (FOSM) method

The first-order second-moment (FOSM) method is a common technique for analyzing error propagation. Its principle involves approximating the mean and variance of a function by performing a first-order Taylor series expansion. Depending on the expansion point, the FOSM method can be classified into the mean-value method (also known as the center point method) and the design point method. The mean-value method is straightforward to implement and has been widely applied in error propagation and reliability analysis. This subsection introduces the fundamental principle of the mean-value method.

It is noteworthy that the process of the aforementioned sensitivity analysis has ignored the correlation between variables. Existing sensitivity analysis methods generally focus primarily on the independent impact of single-variable inputs on uncertain systems, and their sensitivity analysis frameworks often carry the implicit premise that ‘variables are mutually uncorrelated’. Multi-input multi-output (MIMO) systems are ubiquitous in practical engineering problems, yet research targeting this domain remains in a developing phase. Gamboa et al.57 proposed the covariance matrix trace method and applied it to sensitivity analysis of multi-input multi-output systems. Xu et al.58 have proposed a novel global sensitivity analysis method that utilizes the relationship of vector projection to comprehensively assess the impact of multiple inputs on multiple outputs. Lambonil59 defined new generalized sensitivity indices based on the spectral norm of the first-order covariance matrix and the total covariance matrix, and developed a minimum variance unbiased estimator (MVUE) to address multi-output problems. The aforementioned method provides insights for sensitivity analysis that considers the correlation between variables.

The G/Gmax and λ values in this paper were obtained from resonance column tests. The strain range covered by resonance column tests is 10⁻⁶ to 10⁻⁴. However, this paper provides the mean values and standard deviations of G/Gmax and λ across an extended strain range of 10⁻⁶ to 10⁻². In other words, this work quantifies the uncertainty of predicted G/Gmax and λ values beyond the strain range measurable by resonance column tests. In practical applications, the magnitude of uncertainty in the predicted G/Gmax and λ values (beyond the resonance column test range) provided herein can be used to determine whether dynamic triaxial test results or other methods should be employed to modify structural predictions.

The plasticity index and over-consolidation ratio of soil also affect G/Gmax and λ41,49,61.This paper did not investigate the effects of soil plasticity index and over-consolidation ratio on the values and associated uncertainties of G/Gmax and λ; these influences need to be analyzed in subsequent studies.

Application in seismic response analysis of sites

After calculating the mean and standard deviation of soil G/Gmax and λ using the FOSM method, the site seismic response analysis considering uncertainties in G/Gmax and λ can be performed through the following procedure:

In the site calculation model, the random variability of soil dynamic parameters can be characterized by inputting corresponding representative variation levels of soil dynamic parameter values. The random seismic parameters y at the surface position i can be described by an implicit function:

Conclusion

Based on experimental data of G/Gmax–γa and λ–γa relationships from undisturbed silty clay specimens within 120 m depth in the Beijing area, empirical equations for G/Gmax and λ varying with depth H were established. The variation of uncertainty in predicted G/Gmax and λ values, caused by uncertainty in the parameters of these empirical equations, with depth H and shear strain amplitude γa was analyzed. The main conclusions are as follows:

(1) The G/Gmax–γa relationship curve for Beijing silty clay can be described by the Davidenkov model, and the λ–γa relationship curve can be described by the sum of the small-strain damping ratio λmin and a polynomial containing G/Gmax. The Davidenkov model parameters (A, B, γr) and the damping ratio model parameters (λmin, λ0, β) exhibit strong correlations with soil layer depth H.

(2) Empirical equations for G/Gmax and λ with depth H as the variable were established. The accuracy of the empirical equations was verified using independent data.

(3) The uncertainty in predicted G/Gmax generated by the empirical equation for G/Gmax with depth H as the variable is primarily caused by the uncertainty in parameter γr. The uncertainty in predicted G/Gmax caused by the combined uncertainty of parameters A, B, and γr initially increases then decreases as γa increases and decreases as H increases. The uncertainty in predicted G/Gmax caused solely by the uncertainty of a single parameter (A, B, or γr) also initially increases then decreases as γa increases. The uncertainty caused solely by the uncertainty in A increases as H increases; the uncertainty caused solely by the uncertainty in B or γr decreases as H increases.

(4) The uncertainty in predicted λ generated by the empirical equation for λ with depth H as the variable is primarily caused by the uncertainty in predicted G/Gmax. Both the uncertainty in predicted λ caused by the combined uncertainty of G/Gmax and λmin and the uncertainty caused solely by the uncertainty in G/Gmax initially increase then decrease as γa increases and decrease as H increases. The uncertainty in predicted λ caused solely by the uncertainty in λmin remains constant as γa increases and increases as H increases.

Although this study specifically analyzed the uncertainty of G/Gmax and λ for silty clay specimens in the Beijing area, the related methodology can be extended to the uncertainty analysis of G/Gmax and λ for other soil types.
