3D-printed tantalum artificial vertebral body or titanium mesh in anterior column reconstruction post-spondylectomy for thoracic vertebral tumors: a retrospective comparative cohort study and finite element analysis
Authors: Hui Lv, Meitao Xu, Qiuchi Ai, Fuyou Wang, Zhongrong Zhang, Tianyong Hou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThe optimal strategy for spinal reconstruction following total en bloc spondylectomy (TES) for spinal tumors remains controversial. This study aimed to assess the efficacy of a 3D-printed tantalum artificial vertebral body (AVB) in anterior column reconstruction by comparing its fusion efficiency and implant-related complications with those of traditional titanium mesh. In addition, this study elucidated biomechanical differences among titanium mesh, titanium AVB, and 3D-printed tantalum AVB using finite element (FE) analysis.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study included patients who had undergone TES for thoracic spinal tumors, followed by anterior column reconstruction using 3D-printed tantalum AVBs or titanium mesh cages. Clinical outcomes, including Visual Analog Scale (VAS) scores, American Spinal Injury Association (ASIA) scale assessments, fusion rates, and complications, were evaluated. In addition, radiographic parameters, such as subsidence and Cobb angle, were measured and analyzed. Complementary FE models (T2-T10) compared von Mises stress, micro subsidence, and micro slippage across three reconstructions: titanium mesh (model A), titanium AVB (model B), and 3D-printed tantalum AVB (model C) under physiological loading.ResultsThe comparison between tantalum AVB and titanium mesh in the clinical cohort revealed similar operative metrics. However, tantalum AVB demonstrated superior outcomes for 2-year pain relief (VAS: 0.7 ± 0.6 vs. 1.8 ± 0.6; p < 0.05) and fusion rates (100% vs. 38.9%) than the titanium mesh. Titanium mesh was associated with a higher incidence of subsidence (66.7% > 1 mm; mean 4.1 mm) than tantalum (0.37 mm), and 33.3% of patients experienced rod fractures necessitating revision. Neurological improvement with an increase of at least one ASIA grade was observed in 95.2% of the patients. In the FE analysis, model A produced 2.03-fold higher von Mises stress levels than models B and C during right rotation (Δ = 5.351 MPa). Furthermore, model A exhibited a tenfold higher peak implant stress than models B and C (515.7 MPa vs. < 50 MPa). Regarding micro slippage and subsidence, model A showed the greatest micro subsidence during rotation, particularly in left rotation (Model A > Model B > Model C; Δ > 200%). Model C demonstrated minimal interface displacement.ConclusionsAVBs made of 3D-printed tantalum demonstrate superior biomechanical and clinical performance compared to titanium mesh for anterior reconstruction after TES. Tantalum significantly reduces endplate stress, implant subsidence, and hardware failure rates while promoting earlier fusion. Restricting postoperative trunk axial rotation may help mitigate mechanical complications.

Introduction

Spinal tumors are a common cause of biomechanical destabilization and are frequently accompanied by pathological fractures and neural compression [1, 2]. Surgical management of spinal neoplasms remains challenging. Although traditional piecemeal resection alleviates symptoms with lower perioperative morbidity, it is associated with increased local recurrence rates and suboptimal long-term outcomes, particularly for aggressive tumors such as chordomas and osteosarcomas [3]. In this context, en bloc resection has emerged as the gold standard for radical tumor excision and local recurrence minimization [4, 5]. However, the proximity of spinal tumors to critical neural and vascular structures increases perioperative risks during en bloc resection. Furthermore, optimal spinal reconstruction strategies post en bloc resection remain controversial [6–8]. The titanium mesh anterior column reconstruction technique developed by Harms et al. is widely used; however, its geometric mismatch with vertebral endplates generates stress at serrated interfaces, resulting in high subsidence and reconstruction failure rates [9]. Artificial vertebral body (AVB) reconstruction addresses these limitations by expanding endplate contact areas and dispersing interface stresses, thereby reducing collapse rates [10]. However, conventional implants offer inadequate biomechanical compatibility and limited osseointegration efficiency. The elastic modulus of titanium alloys (e.g., Ti6Al4V) significantly exceeds that of human cortical bone, inducing stress-shielding effects that promote periprosthetic bone resorption, osteoporosis, and compromised long-term anterior support [11, 12]. In addition, titanium alloys produce substantial artifacts on computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), potentially obscuring postoperative tumor surveillance [13]. Standardized designs of traditional vertebral prostheses fail to accommodate individualized anatomical variations.

Tantalum, a novel biomaterial, has gained prominence in orthopedic reconstruction because of its favorable mechanical properties, biocompatibility, and osteogenic potential [14, 15]. Cuzzocrea et al. reported that the tantalum metal cage demonstrated a higher fusion rate in a clinical study compared to the polyetheretherketone cage [16]. When combined with the 3D printing technology, tantalum enables precise anatomical matching between customized implants and vertebral endplates for personalized defect reconstruction [17]. 3D-printed porous tantalum AVB combines these advantages, enhancing osseointegration efficiency. Successful applications of 3D-printed porous tantalum in revision arthroplasty have been reported. Ying et al. demonstrated no loosening of 3D-printed tantalum acetabular components in hip revision cases over a follow-up period of 2.8–4.3 years [18]. Nevertheless, evidence regarding 3D-printed tantalum AVB in spinal reconstruction remains scarce, warranting rigorous validation.

This retrospective study analyzed 21 patients who had undergone thoracic tumor en bloc resection to (1) evaluate the efficacy of 3D-printed tantalum AVB in anterior column reconstruction, (2) compare fusion efficiency and implant-related complications (e.g., radiographic subsidence) of 3D-printed tantalum AVB with those of the traditional titanium mesh, and (3) investigate biomechanical differences among titanium mesh, titanium alloy, and 3D-printed tantalum AVBs through finite element (FE) analysis. This study aimed to provide key evidence for optimizing spinal reconstruction strategies following oncological spondylectomy.

Material and methods

Study design and patient population

This retrospective observational study included patients with primary or metastatic thoracic spinal tumors who had undergone total en bloc spondylectomy (TES) with either 3D-printed tantalum AVB reconstruction or titanium mesh anterior column reconstruction between January 2015 and December 2022. This study was approved by the institutional ethics committee. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) individuals with pathologically confirmed localized primary malignancies, solitary metastases, or aggressive benign tumors via preoperative biopsy and PET-CT; (2) those who had undergone TES with subsequent 3D-printed tantalum AVB or titanium mesh reconstruction; and (3) those who were postoperatively followed up for more than 24 months. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) individuals undergoing piecemeal resection or palliative treatment; (2) those with tumors involving cervical, lumbar, or sacral regions; and (3) those with comorbid severe osteoporosis (T value < − 3.0).

Prosthesis design

Preoperative CT and MRI data were used to reconstruct 3D anatomical models of target vertebrae by aligning them with postresection defect morphology. While bulk tantalum has an elastic modulus of approximately 185 GPa, 3D-printed porous architectures have a substantially lower effective modulus. Preliminary optimization revealed that a porous structure with 75% porosity, 0.35-mm pore size, and 0.7-mm strut thickness demonstrated an elastic modulus of 3 GPa, between that of trabecular bone and cortical bone, while enhancing osteoconductivity. The porous tantalum prostheses were fabricated using an electron beam selective melting additive manufacturing system (Sailong-Y150). High-purity tantalum powder was fused layer by layer in a vacuum environment. After printing, residual unmelted particles were removed, support structures were excised via wire cutting, and the implants were ultrasonically cleaned and sterilized with ethylene oxide (Fig. 1).Fig. 1A photograph of a 3D printed tantalum artificial vertebral body

Surgical procedure

Patients were positioned prone with thoracic elevation to maintain physiological curvature. A standard midline incision was made, spanning two vertebrae above and below the pathological segment. Sequential dissection through the skin, subcutaneous tissue, and deep fascia via subperiosteal paravertebral muscle stripping exposed the spinous processes, laminae, facet joints, and transverse processes. Combined transthoracic and extrapleural approaches were employed for anterior column tumors requiring extended exposure. Pedicle screws were inserted into adjacent normal vertebrae using the rib–transverse process junction as an anatomical landmark, with the entry point at the lateral aspect of the upper third of the transverse process. Screw trajectories were guided by preoperative CT measurements and confirmed intraoperatively via X-ray fluoroscopy to avoid medial wall breach. Following screw placement, rib osteotomy was performed: the periosteum was incised along the rib axis and stripped with elevators, and 5–6 cm of the rib posterior segment was resected using rib cutters. The soft tissue was stripped along both sides of the vertebral body toward its anterior aspect and then joined at the midline. A wire saw guide was used to protect the dura and nerve roots during the osteotomy of laminae, facets, and pedicles. A 2–3-mm safety margin from the dural sac was maintained during osteotomy, with postresection verification of complete pedicle separation. The vertebral body was dissected from the surrounding vasculature, pleura, sympathetic chain, and disks. A temporary rod was used to stabilize the spine before en bloc tumor resection using vertebral body elevators. Major blood vessels (aorta, azygos vein) and the thoracic duct were protected, and selective embolization or cement augmentation was used for hemorrhage control. The defect was measured and reconstructed using a 3D-printed porous tantalum prosthesis or a titanium mesh packed with autograft. Allogeneic bone debris and cancellous bone graft particles were densely packed into the intervertebral space to ensure structural stability. The implant position was confirmed fluoroscopically before connecting posterior rods under compression. A drainage tube was placed before layered closure. Outcome assessment

Postoperative efficacy was evaluated using multidimensional metrics. Subjective outcomes were evaluated based on the Visual Analog Scale (VAS) for pain (0–10, 10-cm scale) and American Spinal Injury Association (ASIA) grade for neurological function at admission and during the 2-year follow-up period. Radiological results were systematically analyzed by assessing the fusion rate, implant subsidence, and implant-related complications. The fusion rate was determined according to the Brantigan–Steffee criteria. Implant subsidence was measured on mid-sagittal CT as the intervertebral height loss between midpoints of the upper and lower endplate landmarks of the adjacent vertebrae (Fig. 2m). For assessing differential implant subsidence, the maximum subsidence height of the implant was measured. If subsidence occurred predominantly anteriorly, the anterior height loss of the implant was measured to assess this uneven settling (Fig. 3m). Mild subsidence was defined as a 1–3-mm reduction from the postoperative baseline, whereas severe subsidence was defined as a reduction of ˃3 mm [19]. Instrumentation failure, including screw loosening (radiolucent halo > 1 mm), rod or screw breakage, or pedicle fracture, was diagnosed via X-ray or CT imaging.

FE analysis

The study protocol commenced with the acquisition of thoracic spinal imaging data from a healthy 31-year-old male volunteer. Axial CT scans (slice thickness: 1 mm) were obtained using a spiral CT scanner (GE, USA) and exported in the Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine (DICOM) format. Subsequently, the DICOM datasets were imported into Mimics 21.0 (Materialise NV, Leuven, Belgium) for multithreshold segmentation based on Hounsfield unit differentials between osseous structures and soft tissues. This process generated preliminary 3D mesh models spanning from T2 to T10 vertebrae. Individual vertebral segments were exported as stereolithography (STL) files and subjected to surface optimization using Geomagic Wrap 2021 (3D Systems, Rock Hill, SC, USA). The refinement protocol included the sequential application of (1) surface relaxation algorithms to minimize triangulation artifacts, (2) spike removal to eliminate scanning-induced protrusions, and (3) nonisotropic noise-reduction filtering. Critical anatomical features were preserved through manual refinement using the Remove Features tool, followed by global smoothing operations to achieve physiologically representative surface contours. The optimized models were subjected to NURBS-based surface reconstruction via the Precision Surfacing module, generating watertight STEP (ISO 10303–21) format files. These parametric solid models were imported into SolidWorks 2022 (Dassault Systèmes, Vélizy-Villacoublay, France) for anatomical alignment and assembly verification. The final composite model was exported in Parasolid (.x_t) format to ensure geometry translation fidelity for subsequent FE processing. The computational domain was established in ANSYS Workbench 2021 R2 (ANSYS Inc., Canonsburg, PA, USA) using the “Material property assignment” protocol. Homogeneous isotropic material models were implemented based on published biomechanical parameters (Table 1). Cortical bone, cancellous bone, intervertebral disk, and endplate components were assigned distinct material properties through Boolean partitioning (Table 1). Meshing protocol: An adaptive tetrahedral meshing scheme (10-node quadratic elements) was employed with local refinement at bone–implant interfaces. Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 21.0 (IBM Corp., USA). All datasets were subjected to normality (Shapiro–Wilk test) and homogeneity of variance (Levene’s test) tests before analysis. Categorical variables, including screw fracture and fusion rates, were compared using Fisher’s exact probability method. Continuous variables are expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Continuous variables were analyzed using the independent samples t-test. A two-tailed p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Clinical study

Between January 2015 and December 2022, 206 patients with spinal tumors were screened, and 21 patients with thoracic tumor met the inclusion criteria. Among the 21 patients, 10 were males and 11 were females (mean age: 37.3 ± 13.5 years; range: 15–59 years). Single-level involvement was observed in 19 patients, two-level involvement in 1, and four-level involvement in 1. Primary tumors (n = 17) included giant cell tumors (n = 4), plasmacytomas (n = 4), aggressive hemangiomas (n = 3), aneurysmal bone cysts (n = 2), osteosarcoma (n = 1), osteoblastoma (n = 1), Ewing sarcoma (n = 1), and schwannoma (n = 1). Metastatic tumors (n = 4) originated from the thyroid (n = 2), lung (n = 1), and breast (n = 1) cancers. Patient demographics for two groups

Eighteen patients underwent titanium mesh reconstruction, and three received 3D-printed tantalum prostheses. The operative time for the titanium mesh group was 280–530 min (mean: 364.4 ± 60.9 min), with a blood loss of 1000–4500 mL (mean: 1927.8 ± 813.7 mL). The operative time for the tantalum group was 230–410 min (mean: 323.3 ± 90.2 min), with a blood loss of 1300–3000 mL (mean: 1966.7 ± 907.4 mL). No statistically significant differences were observed for operative duration and blood loss between the two groups. Postoperative 1-year VAS scores improved significantly from preoperative baseline in both groups (titanium: 1.6 ± 1.1 vs. tantalum: 1.3 ± 0.6), although the intergroup differences were not significant. At the 2-year follow-up, the tantalum group demonstrated superior pain relief (0.7 ± 0.6) compared to the titanium group (1.8 ± 0.6, p < 0.05). Preoperatively, all 21 patients presented with varying degrees of neurological impairment: 12 had ASIA grade C and 9 had ASIA grade D. Two years postoperatively, 20 patients (95.2%) demonstrated neurological improvement of at least one ASIA grade, including 1 patient at grade C, 6 at grade D, and 14 at grade E. At the 6-month follow-up, trabecular bone ingrowth was observed in three titanium mesh cases (16.7%) compared with two tantalum cases (66.7%). Fusion rates had increased to 38.9% in the titanium group and 100% in the tantalum AVB group by the 2-year follow-up.

Regarding radiographic parameters and implant-related complications, the segmental Cobb angle in the titanium mesh group was 19° at 3 days postoperatively and 24.5° at 2 years postoperatively. Subsidence of the titanium mesh (> 1 mm) occurred in 12 patients, with an average subsidence depth of 4.1 mm and a subsidence rate of 66.7%. Severe mesh subsidence was observed in eight patients, with an average subsidence depth of 6.9 mm. Rod breakage occurred in six patients; of these, four underwent revision with dual-rod fixation and two underwent revision with rod exchange and extension of fixation to the superior vertebral body. Bone graft resorption within the mesh was observed in 11 patients (61.1% of cases). In the tantalum AVB group, the segmental Cobb angle was 21.1° at 3 days postoperatively and 22.3° at 2 years postoperatively. The average subsidence of the tantalum AVB was 0.37 mm. There were no instances of pedicle screw loosening or rod fracture. FE analysis

This investigation focused on von Mises stress distribution at the endplate–prosthesis interface, within pedicle screw fixation systems, and on prosthetic micromotion and subsidence. As shown in Fig. 5, the maximum stress at the T7 superior endplate occurred during right axial rotation, with model A (titanium mesh) exhibiting the stress of 10.529 MPa, which significantly exceeded the values for models B (Ti6Al4V) and C (tantalum) across all motion modes. Notably, model A demonstrated 2.03-fold higher endplate stress than models B and C during right rotation (Δ = 5.351 MPa) and flexion (Δ = 3.03 MPa). In contrast, model C showed 17.4% and 41.8% greater endplate stress than model B during right lateral bending and left rotation, respectively, although model B outperformed model C in other loading directions (Fig. 9e). At the T5 inferior endplate, model C exhibited peak stress during left rotation (6.824 MPa), surpassing that of models A and B. Micro slippage

Regarding the comparison of implant micro slippage distances among the three models, micro slippage during rotational motion was relatively more pronounced. Under flexion, left lateral bending, and left rotation motions, the micro slippage distance at the implant–endplate interface—between the implant and the inferior endplate of T5 or superior endplate of T7—was in the following order: Model A > Model B > Model C, with the difference being most pronounced during left rotation. Under extension and right rotation, the micro slippage was in the following order: Model B > Model A > Model C. Under right lateral bending, the micro slippage was in the following order: Model B > Model C > Model A (Fig. 9c).

Comparison of implant subsidence distances among the three models revealed that subsidence during rotational motion was notably greater. Discussion

The primary findings of this study demonstrate that 3D-printed tantalum AVB exhibits superior interbody fusion rates, lower implant subsidence incidence, and more stable clinical outcomes compared to those of conventional titanium mesh in the en bloc resection of spinal tumors, confirming the feasibility of short-term stability in anterior spinal reconstruction using tantalum prostheses. To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to systematically compare 3D-printed tantalum AVB and traditional titanium mesh in spinal tumor en bloc resection. Furthermore, FE analysis demonstrated that the biomechanical performance of 3D-printed tantalum AVB in single-level TES surpasses that of both titanium mesh and titanium tantalum replacements.

Previous studies have predominantly focused on tantalum applications in arthroplasty and reported favorable outcomes. Zhao et al. [20] reported that selective laser melting-fabricated tantalum hip prostheses exhibit enhanced bioactivity, promoting osteogenesis and suppressing inflammation compared with those of Ti6Al4V counterparts. Wang et al. [21] successfully reconstructed bone defects in revision total knee arthroplasty using 3D-printed porous tantalum augments, achieving short-term biomechanical stability. Halbedl et al. [22] reported excellent mid- to long-term survival rates of tantalum prostheses in revision knee arthroplasty for patients with severe metaphyseal bone defects. Rossi et al. [23] reported that porous tantalum trabecular prostheses demonstrate favorable clinical and radiographic outcomes in the reconstruction of bone defects encountered during total knee revision arthroplasty, with a five-year survival rate of 90.2% for the implants. However, anterior spinal reconstruction demands exceptional longitudinal load-bearing capacity and shear resistance within vascular and neural-rich environments, and clinical evidence regarding tantalum prosthesis efficacy and safety remains scarce. This study validates the fusion advantages of 3D-printed tantalum in spinal oncology, addressing the gap in the evidence regarding its performance in dynamic spinal environments.

Our results demonstrated that 3D-printed tantalum prostheses achieved higher fusion rates than conventional titanium mesh following en bloc resection. This superiority likely stems from the unique material properties and structural design of tantalum. In general, tantalum exhibits superior osteoconductivity and elastic modulus compatibility. Although the elastic modulus of bulk tantalum (185 GPa) substantially exceeds that of cortical bone (20 GPa), our powder bed electron beam additive manufacturing technology achieved a 75% porous structure with a 0.35-mm pore size, reducing the effective modulus to 3 GPa, which closely matches the modulus of trabecular bone and minimizes stress shielding of the bone–prosthesis interface. In contrast, the modulus of the titanium mesh (110 GPa) exceeds that of both cortical and trabecular bones, inducing significant stress shielding. This mechanical mismatch reduces osteogenic stimulation in titanium mesh bone grafts, impairing osteoblast proliferation and differentiation, and may lead to progressive bone resorption (61.1% incidence in our series), potentially contributing to lower fusion rates and higher instrumentation failure in titanium mesh cohorts. Moreover, the precision-controlled 75% porosity of 3D-printed tantalum mimics the trabecular microstructure, facilitating the migration, adhesion, and differentiation of mesenchymal stem cells and osteoblasts, while promoting vascularization and osseointegration. Existing evidence indicates that 70%–80% porous tantalum scaffolds provide optimal bone conduction, integration, and mechanical performance [24, 25]. Girolami et al. histologically confirmed enhanced osseointegration through 3D-printed microporous structures in a retrieved osteosarcoma revision prosthesis [26]. Yang et al. [27] demonstrated that tantalum particle deposition modulates calcium signaling pathways and promotes M2 macrophage polarization, enhancing bone repair via exosomal miR-378a-3p-mediated proliferation and osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs. Conversely, titanium implants tend to induce proinflammatory M1 macrophage polarization, which may impede bone healing.

Regarding the incidence of implant subsidence, 3D printing enables customization of endplate contact surface morphology, enhancing the bone–prosthesis contact area and stress distribution while reducing subsidence risk. FE analysis revealed that under axial loading during simulated flexion, peak stresses at the upper and lower endplates for tantalum prostheses decreased by approximately 79.7% and 81.4%, respectively, compared with those of titanium mesh. As shown in Fig. 5, stress distribution patterns were more homogeneous in tantalum implants. Although titanium mesh and its bone grafts achieve considerable endplate contact area, the stress-shielding effect of titanium alloy reduces mechanical stimulation to bone grafts, and the limited axial surface area of titanium mesh further concentrates axial loads on the mesh. Together, these factors result in a “point–surface” contact pattern with the endplates. Figure 5 shows circular stress concentration zones around the edges of titanium mesh, with minimal stress distribution in the contained bone grafts. These mechanical disparities predispose the endplates to cutting effects and subsequent mesh subsidence.

Despite these theoretical advantages, clinical evidence supporting the superiority of 3D-printed AVB in subsidence prevention remains controversial. Guo et al. demonstrated that 3D-printed modular prostheses represent effective low-complication options for multilevel thoracolumbar tumor reconstruction, reporting a 37.0% subsidence rate (mean: 1.8 ± 1.0 mm) [28]. Similarly, Liu et al. observed an average subsidence of 1.60 ± 1.79 mm in thoracolumbar tumor spondylectomies [29]. Notably, a systematic review of 11 studies (65 patients) on 3D-printed titanium AVB by Hiras et al. [30] revealed a 47.6% subsidence rate (mean: 3.8 ± 4.7 mm) in thoracolumbar anterior reconstructions, which was comparable to that of traditional titanium cages but with lower symptomatic revision rates. A retrospective comparison of 35 patients with thoracolumbar tumor by Chen et al. [31] revealed equivalent clinical/radiographic outcomes between 3D-printed off-the-shelf prostheses and titanium mesh cages, with no significant differences in subsidence or fixation failure rates.

Our FE analysis demonstrated that 3D-printed tantalum AVBs significantly reduced stress on the posterior instrumentation, endplates, and adjacent disks during anterior column reconstruction following TES for spinal tumors, compared with titanium mesh cages. These biomechanical findings are consistent with previously reported data [32]. Clinical outcomes in our cohort further substantiate this advantage: the titanium mesh cage group exhibited a 33.3% rod fracture rate, whereas rod breakage was absent (0%) in the tantalum AVB group. Consequently, long-segment or four-rod reinforced fixation should be considered when using titanium mesh cages for reconstruction after TES. Furthermore, the titanium mesh group exhibited significantly greater implant positional micro slippage during axial rotation. In contrast, tantalum AVB demonstrated minimal interfacial micromotion among all constructs. This enhanced stability is likely attributable to the high frictional coefficient of porous tantalum [33, 34], which promotes superior initial fixation at the endplate–implant interface. Moreover, FE analysis revealed minimal vertical displacement of 3D-printed tantalum AVB across all motion modes, outperforming both titanium mesh and titanium alloy implants. Maximum subsidence was observed in the titanium mesh group during right rotation (0.078 mm), suggesting insufficient shear resistance. Clinically, tantalum AVB showed 0% subsidence, whereas titanium mesh showed 66.7% subsidence, with a 33.3% revision rate in the latter. However, cautious interpretation is warranted regarding the superiority of the subsidence of tantalum. The small sample size of 3D-printed tantalum cases in this study may have limited the ability to detect true intergroup differences. Therefore, it is essential to further elucidate the advantages of tantalum implants through randomized controlled trials compared with titanium alloy implants. Key outcome measures should include intervertebral fusion rate over 5 years, maintenance of the intervertebral space height, and failure rate of the internal fixation system. Additionally, randomized controlled trials should assess whether the clinical efficacy of tantalum metal implants is sustained in patients with osteoporosis or those undergoing revision surgery and whether their cost-effectiveness surpasses that of currently available titanium alloy implants. Such evidence would provide robust scientific support for the clinical translation and adoption of 3D-printed tantalum implants.

This study had several limitations. First, the application of 3D-printed tantalum AVBs in anterior spinal reconstruction represents a preliminary exploratory analysis. The small cohort size in this early-stage investigation may limit the stability of the results, necessitating cautious interpretation of the findings. Second, the follow-up period of over 2 years remains insufficient to evaluate the mid- to long-term outcomes of 3D-printed tantalum prostheses, warranting an extended follow-up of this cohort. Third, the lack of micro-CT and histological data on early-stage osseointegration of 3D-printed porous tantalum AVBs partially limits the evidence supporting their superior osteoconductivity compared with that of titanium alloys in anterior spinal reconstruction. Finally, intervertebral osteogenic capacity in both groups was influenced by multifactorial variables, including age and prior local radiotherapy history, which may have confounded the observed discrepancies in fusion rates.

Conclusion

Anterior reconstruction using 3D-printed tantalum AVB following en bloc resection of thoracic spinal tumors demonstrates short-term feasibility and reliability as a surgical strategy. 3D-printed tantalum prostheses demonstrate higher fusion rates, lower implant subsidence, and lower instrumentation failure rates than conventional titanium mesh reconstruction. Postoperative rehabilitation protocols emphasizing restricted axial rotation of the trunk may mitigate mechanical complications.
