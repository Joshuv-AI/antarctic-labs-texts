Stratified Therapeutic Evaluation of Presbyphonia Based on Degree of Vocal Fold Atrophy: Efficacy and Prognostic Differences of Semi‐Occluded Vocal Tract Training Across Different Stages
Authors: Jieyu Lu, Wen Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveThis study aimed to evaluate the stratified therapeutic effects of semi‐occluded vocal tract (SOVT) training based on the degree of vocal fold atrophy and to explore response trajectories.MethodsIn this single‐center prospective cohort study, 180 patients with presbyphonia were stratified into mild, moderate, and severe groups. All participants received standardized SOVT training and were followed for 8 weeks. The primary outcome was change in VHI‐10. Secondary outcomes included GRBAS, maximum phonation time (MPT), cepstral peak prominence (CPP), acoustic perturbation parameters (jitter, shimmer, PPQ, APQ, and NHR), and glottal gap score. Linear mixed‐effects models and latent class growth analysis (LCGA) were applied. Time effects referred to repeated measurements obtained at baseline, Week 4, and Week 8.ResultsSignificant overall improvements were observed in VHI‐10, MPT, and CPP (all p < 0.001). Additional acoustic perturbation and noise‐related parameters, including jitter, shimmer, PPQ, APQ, and NHR, also improved significantly over time (all p < 0.001). A significant time × severity interaction was found for VHI‐10 (p < 0.001), with the moderate group demonstrating the greatest improvement. Overall, 68.9% achieved the minimal clinically important difference (MCID) threshold. LCGA identified three response trajectories, with moderate cases more likely to show rapid or sustained improvement.ConclusionsSOVT training is effective for presbyphonia, with therapeutic response varying according to the severity of vocal fold atrophy. Both cepstral and perturbation‐based acoustic analyses supported progressive improvement after intervention. Patients with moderate vocal fold atrophy appeared to derive the greatest therapeutic benefit, suggesting a potential optimal therapeutic window for intervention. Stratified assessment and trajectory‐based analysis may facilitate individualized voice rehabilitation strategies.Keywords: presbyphonia, semi‐occluded vocal tract training, stratified therapy, vocal fold atrophy, voice handicap index‐10, voice rehabilitation

Introduction

Presbyphonia, an age‐related voice disorder, is increasingly prevalent in aging populations and significantly affects communication, social participation, and quality of life [1]. Structural and functional alterations of the larynx, particularly vocal fold atrophy, bowing, and reduced glottal closure, are considered primary pathophysiological mechanisms [2]. Importantly, presbylarynx and presbyphonia are related but distinct entities. Presbylarynx refers to age‐related structural laryngeal changes, whereas presbyphonia describes the associated functional voice impairment [3]. Because structural abnormalities do not always correlate with symptom severity, both structural and functional factors should be considered when evaluating treatment response and individualized rehabilitation strategies. These changes often lead to breathy voice quality, reduced vocal intensity, vocal fatigue, and decreased speech intelligibility [4]. With global population aging, presbyphonia has become an increasingly important clinical concern [1].

Voice rehabilitation remains the first‐line intervention for presbyphonia [5]. Among behavioral approaches, semi‐occluded vocal tract (SOVT) training has gained increasing attention due to its physiologic rationale and therapeutic efficacy. By creating partial occlusion at the level of the lips or vocal tract, SOVT exercises enhance vocal fold vibration efficiency, improve source‐filter interaction, and optimize glottal closure [6, 7]. Several studies have demonstrated improvements in acoustic parameters and patient‐reported outcomes following SOVT‐based interventions [8, 9]. However, treatment response remains heterogeneous across individuals, particularly in patients with varying degrees of vocal fold atrophy and functional impairment [10]. Therefore, integrating structural laryngeal findings with baseline functional voice status may help optimize individualized rehabilitation strategies. Notably, severity‐based stratification and longitudinal trajectory analysis have been increasingly adopted in neurological and speech rehabilitation to support personalized treatment planning and improve functional outcomes [11, 12], providing a potential framework for understanding variable treatment responses in presbyphonia.

The Voice Handicap Index‐10 (VHI‐10), a validated patient‐reported outcome measure, is widely used to quantify perceived voice‐related disability and monitor therapeutic effectiveness [13]. While many studies report pre‐post treatment differences, limited research has examined treatment response trajectories over time or explored stratified therapy models tailored to baseline severity and laryngeal characteristics. Understanding response patterns may facilitate personalized rehabilitation strategies and optimize resource allocation.

Therefore, investigating stratified therapy approaches incorporating SOVT training and analyzing longitudinal treatment response trajectories using VHI‐10 may provide valuable insights into individualized management of presbyphonia. Clarifying which patient subgroups derive the greatest benefit from specific rehabilitation protocols could enhance clinical decision‐making and improve functional voice outcomes in the aging population.

Material and Methods

Study Design and Subjects

This study was designed as a single‐center prospective cohort study. Patients with presbyphonia due to vocal fold atrophy who met the diagnostic criteria were consecutively enrolled. All participants received SOVT training as part of routine clinical practice and were followed up for 8 weeks. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of Beijing Haidian Hospital (approval no. 2023‐063), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment.

Sample Size Estimation

The sample size was calculated based on the requirement to detect the time × stratification interaction effect in a repeated‐measures design. Referring to previous voice rehabilitation studies reporting a moderate effect size for changes in the VHI‐10 [5], with a significance level of α = 0.05, statistical power (1 − β) = 0.80, and three measurement time points (0, 4, and 8 weeks), the estimated minimum sample size was approximately 50 patients per group. Considering potential loss to follow‐up and missing data, a total of approximately 180 participants were planned to be enrolled to ensure the stability of the linear mixed‐effects model and trajectory analyses.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

Participants were eligible if they met the following criteria: (1) age ≥ 60 years, (2) diagnosis of presbyphonia due to vocal fold atrophy confirmed by clinical assessment and laryngoscopic examination, with a chief complaint of persistent voice change, (3) ability to comply with SOVT training and follow‐up evaluations as assessed at enrollment through clinician evaluation of cognitive status, communication ability, and the capacity to understand and follow SOVT training instructions during the initial voice assessment session, and (4) no prior history of vocal fold injection, laryngeal framework surgery, or other phonosurgical procedures before study initiation.

Participants were excluded if they had: (1) vocal fold paralysis or significant structural lesions (such as tumor or severe scarring), (2) uncontrolled severe laryngopharyngeal reflux disease, (3) neurological disorders affecting phonation, or (4) severe cognitive or communication impairment preventing completion of SOVT exercises, inability to complete follow‐up assessments, or poor adherence to the training protocol.

Grouping Method

Based on baseline videolaryngoscopic and stroboscopic findings, patients were stratified into three groups according to the severity of vocal fold atrophy: mild, moderate, and severe. The mild group was characterized by slight vocal fold bowing or mildly reduced vibratory amplitude, with nearly complete glottal closure during phonation. The moderate group exhibited obvious bowing and a persistent glottal gap, although partial vibratory activity was still observed. The severe group demonstrated marked bowing with significant glottal insufficiency or a persistent spindle‐shaped glottal gap [14]. All laryngoscopic recordings were independently evaluated by two voice specialists with more than 10 years of clinical experience, who were blinded to patients' clinical information. In cases of disagreement, a consensus was reached through discussion with a third reviewer. To assess inter‐rater reliability of the stratification, the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) was calculated. To further standardize severity classification, glottal closure patterns were semi‐quantitatively graded during videolaryngoscopic and stroboscopic examination while participants sustained phonation. Glottal closure configuration was visually evaluated according to morphological characteristics using a semi‐quantitative grading system: 0 points, complete glottal closure; 1 point, mild gap with a small slit confined to the mid‐membranous portion; 2 points, moderate gap characterized by a persistent spindle‐shaped or posterior glottal gap; and 3 points, marked insufficiency with a large or persistent glottal gap during phonation. Two experienced voice specialists independently reviewed the recordings, and a consensus score was used for severity classification [15].

Intervention Method

All participants received SOVT training under the supervision of an experienced voice therapist. The training program included commonly used SOVT techniques such as straw phonation, lip trills, and water‐resistance (bubble) phonation. The initial session was conducted in the outpatient clinic, during which the therapist demonstrated the techniques and corrected posture, breath support, and resonance patterns to ensure standardization. Each training session lasted approximately 20 min and consisted of vocal warm‐up exercises, sustained phonation tasks, and pitch glides or scale practice. Patients were instructed to perform the exercises daily at home according to the standardized training protocol. To enhance intervention consistency, all sessions followed a unified training procedure, avoiding individualized modifications that might introduce variability. Participants were required to document their home practice to monitor adherence.

Follow‐Up Method

All patients underwent follow‐up assessments at baseline (Week 0), Week 4, and Week 8. At each visit, participants completed subjective questionnaires, perceptual voice evaluations, and acoustic and aerodynamic measurements, along with standardized voice recordings conducted in a quiet environment. The order of assessments was kept consistent across visits to minimize measurement bias. To ensure data reliability, all follow‐up evaluations were performed by the same assessment team, whose members were blinded to patients' stratification status during the analysis phase. If a patient was unable to attend the scheduled visit, the assessment was completed within an allowable time window, and the exact date was recorded for subsequent longitudinal analysis. No additional surgical or pharmacological interventions were administered during the study period to avoid potential confounding effects on the outcomes of SOVT training.

Definition and Assessment of Primary Outcome

The primary outcome was the change in the VHI‐10 score. The VHI‐10 is a patient‐reported outcome measure derived from the original VHI, consisting of 10 core items designed to assess the impact of voice disorders on daily activities and emotional well‐being. VHI‐10 is a widely validated and commonly used instrument in clinical voice research, with good responsiveness and feasibility for repeated longitudinal assessments [13]. In addition, its concise format reduces patient burden during serial follow‐up evaluations in older adults. Each item is rated on a 5‐point Likert scale: 0 = “never,” 1 = “almost never,” 2 = “sometimes,” 3 = “often,” and 4 = “always.” The total score ranges from 0 to 40, with higher scores indicating greater perceived voice‐related impairment. All participants completed the questionnaire independently in a quiet environment. Assessors provided standardized instructions only and did not influence scoring [13].

The minimal clinically important difference (MCID) was adopted as the response criterion. MCID was defined as the smallest change in score perceived by patients as clinically meaningful. A reduction of ≥ 6 points in the VHI‐10 score was considered to have reached the MCID threshold, based on previous studies that established clinically meaningful improvement in patients with voice disorders using anchor‐based approaches [16]. Although this threshold has not been specifically validated in presbyphonia populations, it has been widely used as a clinically relevant reference value in voice‐related outcome research. Based on this criterion, participants were categorized as responders (VHI‐10 decrease ≥ 6 points) or non‐responders (VHI‐10 decrease < 6 points) [16].

Definition and Assessment of Secondary Outcomes

Secondary outcomes included perceptual voice evaluation, aerodynamic measures, and acoustic parameters to comprehensively reflect changes in vocal function.

Perceptual voice evaluation: Perceptual assessment was performed using the GRBAS scale, a standardized perceptual evaluation system originally proposed by Hirano for clinical assessment of voice quality [17]. Two experienced voice evaluators independently rated patients' standardized voice samples. The GRBAS scale assesses Grade (G), Roughness (R), Breathiness (B), Asthenia (A), and Strain (S) on a 4‐point scale (0–3), with higher scores indicating greater voice impairment. All evaluations were conducted in a quiet environment using standardized recordings. The raters were blinded to patients' stratification status and follow‐up time points to minimize observer bias [18].

MPT is defined as the longest duration of sustained vowel /a/ phonation following deep inhalation. Participants performed three trials in a seated position under standardized conditions, and the maximum value was used for analysis to improve measurement reliability [19].

Acoustic analysis: Acoustic analysis was performed using standardized sustained vowel /a/ recordings. Participants were instructed to sustain the vowel /a/ at a comfortable pitch and loudness in a quiet environment. Voice recordings were obtained using a standardized head‐mounted microphone positioned approximately 10 cm from the participant's mouth under identical recording conditions. For each recording, a stable mid‐portion of sustained phonation was selected for analysis to minimize the influence of voice onset and offset. All acoustic measurements were conducted under identical recording and analysis conditions across all follow‐up time points to ensure consistency.

Cepstral peak prominence (CPP) was used as the primary acoustic parameter. CPP is defined as the amplitude difference between the dominant cepstral peak and the corresponding regression‐based trend line beneath it in the cepstral domain. It quantifies the degree of periodicity and harmonic organization of the voice signal. During processing, the voice signal was first subjected to cepstral transformation. Within a predefined pitch range, the cepstral peak was identified, and its prominence was calculated relative to a fitted linear regression trend line. Smoothed CPP values were extracted to improve measurement stability and reduce the influence of signal variability and background noise [20].

In addition to CPP, conventional acoustic perturbation and noise‐related parameters were extracted from the same sustained vowel/a/recordings using Praat software, including jitter, shimmer, pitch perturbation quotient (PPQ), amplitude perturbation quotient (APQ), and noise‐to‐harmonics ratio (NHR). Jitter and PPQ were used to quantify cycle‐to‐cycle and short‐term frequency perturbations, whereas shimmer and APQ were used to quantify cycle‐to‐cycle and short‐term amplitude perturbations. NHR was calculated to estimate the relative amount of noise energy in the voice signal. Voice samples with severe aperiodicity or insufficiently detectable pitch periods were carefully inspected before perturbation analysis because perturbation‐based measures depend on reliable cycle detection.

For representative visualization, mild, moderate, and severe cases were selected according to the predefined severity classification. Within each severity group, the representative case was defined as the case with a CPP value closest to the median CPP of that group. Representative cepstral diagrams were also generated for the selected mild, moderate, and severe cases using Python 3.11 to illustrate changes in CPP across severity levels.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0. Continuous variables were normally distributed and are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), while categorical variables are expressed as counts and percentages. Baseline characteristics were compared using one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) for continuous variables or the χ 2 test for categorical variables, as appropriate. Longitudinal changes were analyzed using linear mixed‐effects models to examine the main effect of time and the time × severity interaction effect. The model structure was specified as: Outcome ~ Time × Severity + (1|Subject). To evaluate the independent contribution of each variable to outcome changes, semi‐partial R 2 (partial R 2), as well as marginal R 2 and conditional R 2, were calculated within the mixed‐effects framework. Latent class growth analysis (LCGA) was conducted to identify distinct longitudinal VHI‐10 trajectories across baseline, 4‐week, and 8‐week assessments. The optimal model was selected based on Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) and entropy values, and patients were assigned to the trajectory class with the highest posterior probability. The LCGA‐based trajectory classification (rapid, gradual, and minimal responders) reflected longitudinal patterns of symptom improvement and was distinct from the MCID‐based responder analysis, which evaluated whether clinically meaningful improvement was achieved at follow‐up. All statistical tests were two‐sided, and a p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline Characteristics

A total of 180 patients with presbyphonia due to vocal fold atrophy were consecutively enrolled in this study, including 60 patients in the mild group, 64 in the moderate group, and 56 in the severe group. There were no statistically significant differences among the three groups in terms of age, sex distribution, or baseline VHI‐10 score, total GRBAS score, MPT, and CPP (all p > 0.05) (Table 1). Glottal gap characteristics were incorporated into the predefined severity stratification criteria and were therefore not subjected to separate between‐group statistical comparisons. Inter‐rater reliability for the grading of vocal fold atrophy was good, with an ICC of 0.86.

Overall Efficacy of Semi‐Occluded Vocal Tract Training

Linear mixed‐effects model analysis revealed significant main effects of time for VHI‐10 (F = 152.36, p < 0.001), MPT (F = 89.47, p < 0.001), and CPP (F = 41.82, p < 0.001), indicating progressive improvement in both subjective and objective voice parameters across baseline, week 4, and week 8 (Table 2). Post hoc comparisons further demonstrated statistically significant differences between baseline and week 4, as well as between week 4 and week 8 (all p < 0.01). The total GRBAS score also gradually decreased over time, although the magnitude of change was relatively modest, which may partly reflect a ceiling effect related to the limited scoring range of the GRBAS scale (0–3 for each parameter), thereby reducing sensitivity to subtle longitudinal improvements in voice quality.

Differences in Improvement Trajectories According to Severity of Vocal Fold Atrophy

Linear mixed‐effects modeling revealed a statistically significant time × severity interaction effect for VHI‐10 (F = 6.84, p < 0.001), indicating that improvement trajectories differed according to the severity of vocal fold atrophy (Table 3). Notably, the moderate group demonstrated the greatest therapeutic improvement, whereas the severe group showed comparatively attenuated gains. Although the mild group also achieved clinically meaningful improvement, the difference between the mild and moderate groups did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.08). A similar interaction pattern was observed for MPT (F = 4.27, p = 0.006), while the interaction effect for CPP did not reach statistical significance (F = 1.92, p = 0.11). Further pairwise comparisons at Week 8 showed that the VHI‐10 score in the severe group was significantly higher than those in both the mild group (p = 0.01) and moderate group (p = 0.0012).

Clinical Responder Phenotype Analysis

Using a ≥ 6‐point reduction in VHI‐10 score as the MCID criterion, 124 patients (68.89%) achieved the MCID threshold. Responder rates differed significantly across severity groups (χ 2 = 6.669, p = 0.036), with the moderate group showing the highest response rate and the severe group the lowest (Table 4). Importantly, therapeutic response was not solely associated with lower baseline symptom severity, as patients in the moderate group demonstrated the highest responder rate and greatest magnitude of improvement. Responders also had significantly lower baseline MPT values than non‐responders (p = 0.007), whereas no significant differences were observed for age, sex, or baseline CPP values (all p > 0.05).

Discussion

In this prospective cohort study, we systematically evaluated the short‐term efficacy of SOVT training in older adults with presbyphonia due to vocal fold atrophy and further explored severity‐based heterogeneity and trajectory phenotypes. Overall, both subjective and objective voice parameters improved progressively over the 8‐week intervention, with a significant time effect observed for VHI‐10, MPT, and CPP, and additional acoustic perturbation parameters. Importantly, treatment response varied according to baseline severity, and distinct longitudinal trajectories were identified. The main novelty of this study lies in the severity‐stratified longitudinal analysis and LCGA‐based trajectory classification, which together provide a more individualized and prognostic perspective on voice rehabilitation in presbyphonia.

The overall reduction in VHI‐10 scores and concomitant improvement in MPT and CPP are consistent with prior studies demonstrating the beneficial effects of SOVT exercises on glottal efficiency and vocal economy [6, 21, 22]. Previous investigations in patients with functional dysphonia and age‐related voice changes have reported improvements in patient‐reported voice handicap and aerodynamic performance after structured therapy [23, 24]. Our findings further confirm that clinically meaningful improvement can be achieved within a relatively short training period. Notably, 68.89% of participants reached the MCID threshold, supporting the clinical relevance of SOVT training in this population [25].

A key contribution of the present study lies in the stratified analysis by severity of vocal fold atrophy. Although the moderate group exhibited the greatest numerical improvement and highest responder rate, the difference between the mild and moderate groups did not reach statistical significance. Importantly, patients in the mild group also achieved clinically meaningful improvement following SOVT training. This pattern is in line with existing pathophysiological understanding [26]. One possible explanation is that compensatory mechanisms in mild atrophy may already help maintain relatively adequate glottal closure, thereby limiting the observable magnitude of change despite favorable treatment responsiveness [27]. In contrast, severe atrophy is characterized by marked bowing and persistent glottal insufficiency, which may not be fully reversible through behavioral intervention alone and may require adjunctive procedures such as injection laryngoplasty [28]. Patients with moderate atrophy, however, may represent an optimal therapeutic window in which neuromuscular coordination and glottal configuration remain sufficiently modifiable. Few previous studies have formally examined severity‐dependent differences in SOVT response; thus, our findings provide empirical support for individualized treatment planning based on baseline laryngoscopic grading.

The significant time × severity interaction observed in the linear mixed‐effects model further underscores the heterogeneity of longitudinal change. Compared with conventional pre‐post analyses, our repeated‐measures design enabled characterization of dynamic recovery trajectories over time. The partial R 2 analysis demonstrated that time contributed most strongly to VHI‐10 improvement, followed by the interaction effect, suggesting that both sustained training and baseline structural severity influence therapeutic outcomes. In addition, lower baseline MPT was associated with greater improvement, consistent with previous reports suggesting that patients with greater aerodynamic inefficiency may derive greater benefit from resonance‐ and flow‐based therapy approaches [29].

The LCGA‐based trajectory analysis further extended these findings by identifying rapid, gradual, and minimal responder phenotypes. Similar trajectory‐based response patterns have been described in other rehabilitation fields, emphasizing the importance of response heterogeneity rather than average treatment effects alone [30, 31, 32]. In our cohort, patients with moderate atrophy were more likely to demonstrate rapid or gradual improvement, whereas severe cases were more frequently classified as minimal responders. These findings support the potential value of severity‐stratified rehabilitation strategies and suggest that severe cases may benefit from earlier multimodal interventions combining behavioral and procedural therapies.

Interestingly, although CPP improved significantly over time, the interaction effect across severity strata did not reach statistical significance. This may reflect the relative stability of cepstral measures compared with subjective perception and aerodynamic performance, as CPP primarily reflects harmonic organization rather than gross glottal closure patterns [33, 34], supporting our findings. In addition, perturbation‐ and noise‐related parameters, including jitter, shimmer, PPQ, APQ, and NHR, also demonstrated progressive improvement following SOVT training, supporting enhanced phonatory stability and reduced acoustic irregularity after intervention. However, these measures showed relatively modest severity‐specific interaction effects compared with VHI‐10 and MPT, suggesting that patient‐reported and aerodynamic outcomes may be more sensitive for differentiating stratified therapeutic trajectories in presbyphonia.

Several methodological strengths enhance the robustness of our findings, including the prospective design, standardized intervention protocol, blinded assessment procedures, and good inter‐rater reliability (ICC = 0.86). In addition, the use of mixed‐effects modeling and semi‐partial R 2 quantification allowed for a more nuanced assessment of explanatory factors than traditional repeated‐measures approaches. Clinically, our findings may help guide hierarchical treatment strategies, with behavioral intervention potentially sufficient for mild cases, intensive SOVT‐based rehabilitation most suitable for moderate cases, and earlier multimodal management considered for severe cases.

Nevertheless, this study has several limitations. First, this was a single‐center study without a non‐intervention control group, and spontaneous symptom fluctuation cannot be entirely excluded. Second, the follow‐up duration was limited to 8 weeks, and the long‐term sustainability of therapeutic benefits remains unclear. Finally, although multiple cepstral and perturbation‐based acoustic parameters were incorporated, more advanced imaging‐based and high‐resolution aerodynamic assessments may further improve objective characterization of therapeutic changes in future studies.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our findings confirm that SOVT training yields clinically meaningful improvements in older adults with presbyphonia and that baseline severity significantly influences treatment response and trajectory. Notably, patients with moderate vocal fold atrophy appeared to derive the greatest therapeutic benefit, suggesting a potential optimal therapeutic window for SOVT intervention. Severity‐stratified assessment and trajectory‐based modeling may help guide hierarchical treatment selection and facilitate more personalized rehabilitation strategies in age‐related voice disorders.
