Pregnancy testing prior to iodine-131 thyroid therapy: a literature review of current recommendations
Authors: Stephen J. Sozio, David Cohen, William Y. Raynor, Jeffrey S. Kempf
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeIodine-131 (I-131) thyroid ablation is strictly contraindicated during pregnancy due to the risk of fetal thyroid damage after 10 weeks of gestation. Current society guidelines lack consistency regarding the best timing and method for pregnancy testing. This review aims to consolidate current recommendations and propose a standardized testing algorithm.MethodsA literature review was conducted using the PubMed/MEDLINE database over a 55-year period to identify peer-reviewed publications on professional society guidelines for pre-radioiodine therapy pregnancy testing, as well as performance characteristics of serum and urine pregnancy tests.ResultsFetal susceptibility to radioiodine begins around 8–10 weeks of gestation. While serum hCG tests are approximately 2.5 times more sensitive than standard urine tests, they still have a “biological window” during which early pregnancy might be undetectable. Current guidelines differ on testing timelines, ranging from 24 to 72 h before therapy.ConclusionPatients should be advised to abstain from sexual activity for at least two weeks before therapy and provide a written attestation on the day of treatment confirming they completed this abstinence period. Serum pregnancy testing is preferred because of its higher sensitivity and should be performed within 72 h prior to therapy, ideally within 24 h.

Introduction

Iodine-131 (I-131) is a radioactive isotope of iodine that is widely used in the treatment of hyperthyroidism and for ablation of both residual thyroid tissue and cancer cells following thyroidectomy in patients with well-differentiated thyroid carcinoma of papillary or follicular subtypes. Its mechanism of action is rooted in the emission of beta radiation, which is predominantly concentrated in thyroid follicle cells and well-differentiated thyroid carcinoma cells due to a high presence of the sodium-iodide symporter in these cell types, and the short range of the beta radiation emitted from I-131. It is important to note that this symporter (and subsequently I-131 uptake) is not limited to thyroid tissue; it naturally occurs in the lacrimal glands, salivary glands, gastrointestinal tract, genitourinary tract, and placenta. Therefore, radiologists and endocrinologists must take extra precautions to prevent adverse effects arising from excessive radioiodine exposure in these other organ systems (Wyszomirska 2012).

Therefore, I-131 therapy is strictly contraindicated during confirmed or suspected pregnancy because radioiodine readily crosses the placenta and concentrates within fetal thyroid tissue as early as 8–10 weeks of gestation (Iijima 2021). Unintentional destruction of fetal thyroid tissue during organogenesis can have severe consequences, including fetal hypothyroidism, fetal growth restriction, multiple developmental anomalies, and, in severe cases, cretinism (Hyer et al. 2011). Because of this, current nuclear medicine society guidelines and recommendations require exclusion of pregnancy prior to I-131 administration. However, there is significant heterogeneity among different societies’ recommendations and current clinical practices regarding how this testing should be conducted, including differences in the types of pregnancy tests used and the timing of testing.

Given the serious risk of adverse effects if a fetus is exposed to I-131 and the variability across current professional society recommendations, it is crucial to establish clear, consistent guidelines that ensure accurate and early pregnancy detection before therapy. This review examines current society guideline recommendations, assesses the performance of various pregnancy testing methods, and proposes an institutional testing protocol based on these findings.

Methods

A literature review of published peer-reviewed literature regarding pregnancy testing recommendations and practices related to radioiodine therapy was performed in March 2026 to include most recent versions of relevant professional society guidelines, in addition to peer-reviewed literature published on this topic spanning from 1970 to 2025. The review focused on two key areas: Professional society guideline recommendations regarding pregnancy testing prior to I-131 administration, and the analytical performance of common pregnancy testing methods relevant to this clinical setting. PubMed/MEDLINE was used as the primary database. Representative search terms included but were not limited to: “iodine-131,” “radioiodine ablation,” “thyroid cancer,” “Graves’ disease,” “pregnancy testing,” “human chorionic gonadotropin,” “urine hCG,” “serum hCG,” and “guidelines”. Guideline documents were included if published as peer-reviewed practice guidelines, consensus statements, or society recommendations. Case reports and observational studies were included when relevant to pregnancy detection in the setting of radioiodine therapy. As no patients were actively enrolled or retrospectively analyzed in this review, institutional review board approval was not required.

Results/findings

Overview of radioiodine effect on the fetal thyroid

Fetal organogenesis begins approximately 3 weeks following conception, with the fetal thyroid gland first developing around 8 weeks of gestation (Rossito et al. 2022, The American Thyroid Association Taskforce on Radioiodine Safety et al. 2011). The fetal thyroid develops the ability to uptake iodine between weeks 10–13 and is particularly sensitive to changes in iodine concentration due to its high mitotic activity and higher affinity for iodine compared to the adult thyroid gland (Green et al. 1971). Therefore, exposure of the fetus to radioiodine therapy at or after 10 weeks of gestation will almost certainly result in fetal hypothyroidism (The American Thyroid Association Taskforce on Radioiodine Safety et al. 2011, Green et al. 1971). In contrast, exposure of the fetus to radioactive iodine therapy prior to 8–10 weeks gestational age may result in fetal demise due to radiation-induced progenitor cell apoptosis; however, in fetuses which survive I-131 exposure during this period, to our knowledge, there are no reported cases of radioiodine-attributed extra-thyroidal fetal birth defects, as major organogenesis has not yet occurred (Iijima 2021, Hyer et al. 2011, Rossito et al. 2022, The American Thyroid Association Taskforce on Radioiodine Safety et al. 2011).

The clinical effects of intrauterine I-131 exposure beyond 10 weeks of gestational age develop due to resultant hypothyroidism; symptoms of fetal hypothyroidism vary greatly depending on the absorbed dose, time of administration, and how quickly detection and treatment occur (Iijima 2021). After birth, fetal hypothyroidism may present with a range of symptoms, including macroglossia, lethargy, hypotonia, poor feeding, constipation, and hypothermia. If untreated, this can result in profound and irreversible sequelae, including neurocognitive impairment, motor delay, speech and language deficits, short stature, and delayed puberty. Additionally, even at lower doses, in utero radiation exposure has been linked to a higher long-term risk of thyroid cancer and functional thyroid problems in population studies, though data specific to isolated fetal I-131 exposure are limited (Iijima 2021).

Overview of mechanism of action and efficacy of commonly utilized pregnancy tests

Pregnancy testing primarily detects human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG), a glycoprotein that is one of the earliest hormones secreted during pregnancy, making it detectable in both maternal serum and urine. Following fertilization of the ovum, the resulting blastocyst normally implants in the uterine lining, after which trophoblast cells of the placenta produce hCG. In uncomplicated intrauterine pregnancies, hCG levels initially double every 48–72 h, typically reaching serum concentration of approximately 10 mIU/mL by 10–11 days after conception (Iijima 2021, Wilcox et al. 2001, Fritz and Guo 1987). Serum hCG tests can detect hyperglycosylated hCG at approximately 10 mIU/mL, which allows these tests to yield positive results in up to 98% of uncomplicated pregnancies by 11 days after conception. In contrast, standard urine pregnancy tests typically detect hCG in urine when levels reach approximately 25 mIU/mL, leading to positive results by 15–17 days after conception. Given the differences in sensitivity and a typical hCG doubling time of 48–72 h, serum hCG testing offers a notable advantage by detecting pregnancy several days earlier than standard urine pregnancy tests. Of note, a false-positive result may be seen due to heterophilic antibodies, trophoblastic disease, and physiologically increased hCG secretion in menopausal women (Rossito et al. 2022, Wilcox et al. 2001, Watson 2005, Chard 1992, Minkin 2009, Emancipator et al. 1988, Christensen et al. 1990, Tran et al. 2010, Bohuslavizki et al. 1999).

Newer generation high-sensitivity urine pregnancy tests have become available, occasionally referred to as “early result” and “enhanced sensitivity” tests. These tests may detect urine hCG at levels as low as 10 mIU/mL, and may offer equivalent sensitivity to serum hCG tests (Lei et al. 2024). Wilcox et al. sequentially assayed over 150 women who were planning to become pregnant utilizing ultrasensitive immunoradiometric urine hCG assays, which were approximately one hundred times more sensitive than standard urine pregnancy tests. Despite the considerable increase in sensitivity, the ultra-sensitive tests failed to detect approximately 10% of pregnancies when tested the day after a missed period, and approximately 3% on the seventh day after a missed period (Wilcox et al. 2001, Watson 2005).

Current society recommendations regarding pregnancy testing prior to radioiodine ablation

All eight guidelines/recommendations unanimously agree that pregnancy testing is essential for all female patients of reproductive potential, and pregnancy must be ruled out before administering radioiodine therapy. However, there is considerable variability in the level of detail and specificity of the recommendations regarding testing, including differences in the type of test, timing of testing, and whether testing should be performed in all patients or only those of reproductive potential (The American Thyroid Association Taskforce on Radioiodine Safety et al. 2011, Silberstein et al. 2012, Weatherman et al. 2025, Bartel et al. 2020, Trout et al. 2025, European Association of Nuclear Medicine 2003, Campenni et al. 2023, Administration of Radioactive Substances Advisory Committee 2025, Silverstein 2013).

Serum hCG pregnancy tests are explicitly recommended as the preferred testing method by the SNMMI Practice Guideline for Therapy of Thyroid Disease with I-131 3.0, SNMMI/ACNM Practice Guideline for the Use of Radiopharmaceuticals 5.0, and EANM Guideline on Radiotherapy of Benign Thyroid Disease. In contrast, other guidelines either suggest both modalities as viable testing options, or do not state a preferred testing modality. Notably, SNMMI Practice Guideline for Therapy of Thyroid Disease with I-131 3.0 highlights the generally higher sensitivity of serum hCG testing compared to urine testing and emphasizes the importance of considering serum hCG testing and/or delaying radioiodine therapy if a woman has engaged in sexual intercourse within ten days prior to treatment (Silberstein et al. 2012).

All guidelines align on performing pregnancy testing no earlier than 72 h before administering radioiodine therapy. However, there is considerable variability regarding the specific timing of testing. SNMMI Practice Standard for Thyroid Cancer Scintigraphy recommends testing on the day of therapy, utilizing either serum or urine analysis, while the SNMMI Practice Guideline for Therapy of Thyroid Disease with I-131 3.0 advises testing within 24 h prior to therapy. The ACR-ACNM-ARS-SNMMI-SPR Practice Parameter for Treatment of Benign and Malignant Thyroid Disease With I-131 Sodium Iodide permits testing up to 72 h prior to therapy if the patient practiced abstinence, although the exact duration of abstinence is not specified. Both EANM guidelines and ATA guidelines recommend testing as early as 72 h before therapy. Of note, while both the SNMMI/ACNM Practice Guideline for the Use of Radiopharmaceuticals 5.0 and EANM Guideline on Radiotherapy of Benign Thyroid Disease expressed preference for serum hCG testing, they do not agree on the timing of this test (The American Thyroid Association Taskforce on Radioiodine Safety et al. 2011, Silberstein et al. 2012, Weatherman et al. 2025, Bartel et al. 2020, Trout et al. 2025, European Association of Nuclear Medicine 2003, Campenni et al. 2023, Administration of Radioactive Substances Advisory Committee 2025, Silverstein 2013).

Discussion

Radioiodine therapy is a widely accepted and effective method for the treatment of well-differentiated papillary and follicular thyroid carcinoma. In female patients of reproductive potential at the time of therapy, it is essential to correctly exclude pregnancy prior to administration of therapy, as in utero exposure of I-131 to a fetus that has begun thyroid organogenesis can have severe short- and long-term effects (Berlin 2012, Greenlee et al. 2011). Exposure of the fetus to radioiodine prior to 8–10 weeks of gestational age carries a comparatively lower risk of subsequent fetal hypothyroidism, as the fetal thyroid has either not developed yet, or has not developed the ability to uptake iodine. This has been affirmed in multiple case reports in which inadvertent fetal exposure to I-131 prior to 8–10 weeks gestational age did not result in fetal hypothyroidism (Rossito et al. 2022, Green et al. 1971, Tran et al. 2010, Radacic-Aumiler et al. 2016, Sadakata et al. 2014, Ali et al. 2004). For example, Tran et al. reported a case of radioiodine ablation for Graves’ disease which was administered to a pregnant woman carrying an approximately 8-day-old gestation who had undergone a negative urine pregnancy test (although the exact type of test was not specified); the fetus did not experience pre-natal or post-birth evidence of hypothyroidism, which was attributed to a lack of fetal radioiodine uptake as exposure occurred prior to thyroid organogenesis (Tran et al. 2010). In contrast, administration of radioiodine after 10 weeks of gestation consistently causes fetal hypothyroidism (Berg et al. 1998, Basbug et al. 2010, Fisher et al. 1963, Arndt et al. 1994). To date, the authors noted no published case reports of fetal I-131 exposure after 10 weeks of gestation that did not result in fetal hypothyroidism. It is important to note that the fetal thyroid becomes susceptible to radioiodine by 8–10 weeks of gestation, when both serum and urine hCG levels have increased to detectable levels utilizing either serum or urine pregnancy testing tests (Iijima 2021).

It is important to note that simply asking a patient whether they might be pregnant does not provide enough medical-legal grounds to exclude pregnancy, as shown by Berlin et al. In this case, a woman who was unknowingly 15-weeks pregnant was administered 2 mCi for I-131 for thyroid scintigraphy after verbally stating she was not pregnant during pre-examination questioning. The patient later stated in an affidavit that she never recalled being asked about pregnancy prior to the examination, and no written documentation regarding pregnancy was obtained. No pre-examination pregnancy test was performed, the patient received radioiodine therapy, and was later diagnosed as being pregnant weeks later. The fetus subsequently developed severe hypothyroidism because the exposure was calculated to have occurred well beyond 10 weeks of gestation. A subsequent lawsuit was ultimately settled for a “substantial payment” to the mother and child (Berlin 2012). This case highlights the critical importance of not solely relying on a patient’s verbal report of possible pregnancy at the time of evaluation, as patients may be unknowingly pregnant at the time of questioning. Thus, pregnancy testing should always be performed in all female patients capable of pregnancy during pre-therapy evaluation, ideally coupled with a written statement signed by the patient affirming she is not pregnant.

Multiple clinically validated pregnancy tests are available for use in the personal and healthcare settings. Serum pregnancy tests are generally about 2.5 times more sensitive in detecting hCG than standard urine pregnancy tests, which can be attributed to higher physiologic concentrations of hCG in serum compared to urine, as well as intrinsically higher sensitivity of the serum testing methods (Iijima 2021). Serum pregnancy tests can typically detect an uncomplicated intrauterine pregnancy by 10–11 days after conception, compared to 15–17 days with a standard urine pregnancy test. This offers a diagnostic advantage if the patient conceived a pregnancy within 14 days prior to therapy administration or if urine pregnancy testing is inconclusive. Several next-generation high-sensitivity urine pregnancy tests have recently become available, offering roughly equivalent sensitivity to serum pregnancy tests. However, their use in clinical practice is widely variable and organization specific. Because hCG sensitivity varies among different pregnancy tests, organizations should confirm their preferred test sensitivity when developing their pre-ablation testing protocols (Wilcox et al. 2001, Watson 2005).

Proposed institutional protocol model of pregnancy testing prior to I-131 therapy

Before administration of radioiodine therapy, it is essential to discuss the risks, benefits, and alternatives with the patient. This discussion should follow current guidelines to avoid pregnancy within 6–12 months following therapy, due to the need for post-therapy radiation clearance and stabilization of thyroid function prior to pregnancy. Given the broadness of this timeframe, the time to avoid pregnancy specific to each patient should depend on a joint decision making between the patient and the treating provider, including consideration of each individual’s biokinetic data, including (but not limited to) the distribution and retention of I-131 in the individual patient. Specific to those undergoing treatment for Grave’s Disease who wish to become pregnant within 6–12 months following therapy, alternative treatment options such as anti-thyroid medications or thyroidectomy should be discussed. Patients should also be counseled about the potential for temporary menstrual irregularities and infertility following radioiodine ablation, which are thought to be related to therapy-related ovarian radiation exposure. Dosimetric studies demonstrate that, while ovarian absorbed doses from radioiodine therapy are generally below thresholds associated with permanent ovarian failure, cumulative exposure may result in permanent effects, particularly in younger patients or those requiring repeated treatments (Piek et al. 2021, Navarro et al. 2022).

As abstinence is the only completely effective form of contraception, we recommend advising patients to abstain from intercourse for at least 2 weeks before radioiodine therapy. Traditional urine pregnancy tests are unreliable for at least 8–10 days after implantation, so maintaining abstinence during this period significantly reduces the risk of false-negative results during the biological window of invisibility (Rossito et al. 2022). Patients should also provide a written declaration on the day of therapy confirming they have adhered to this abstinence period. Accordingly, as an additional safety measure, I-131 therapy should ideally be scheduled during the follicular phase of the menstrual cycle, ensuring no ovum is physically available to be fertilized at the time of administration. Unfortunately, scheduling therapy based on menstrual cycle timing, even if operationally feasible, can often be impractical due to variability in cycle length or irregularity caused by different factors which may be exacerbated by common thyroid dysfunction, stress, and chronic illness, as often seen in patients with thyroid disease and thyroid cancer. Therefore, establishing a consistent approach to pregnancy testing is essential.

Based on current society guidelines, the effectiveness of readily available pregnancy tests, and knowledge of embryo development, our proposed institutional protocol model recommends that pregnancy testing be performed within 72 h prior to therapy, and ideally within 24 h prior. Serum pregnancy testing is preferred over urine testing due to its intrinsically higher sensitivity for hCG detection (Iijima 2021, Berg et al. 2008). Pregnancy testing should be ordered by the primary responsible specialist, with the result verified by both the ordering provider and the treating nuclear medicine physician prior to administration of therapy. In the setting of an inconclusive pregnancy test result (for example, a serum quantitative hCG of 0.1–10 mIU/mL), we recommend delaying therapy and repeating serum quantitative hCG analysis within 48–72 h, in alignment with the typical hCG doubling time in an uncomplicated intrauterine pregnancy (Iijima 2021). Conclusion

Inadvertent fetal exposure to I-131 is a rare but potentially devastating preventable event, underscoring the sentinel importance of pregnancy exclusion before therapy. Although all major professional societies agree that pre-therapy pregnancy testing in all females of reproductive potential is essential, this review highlights substantial heterogeneity in recommended testing methods, timing, and risk mitigation strategies. Administration of radioiodine within the first 10 days of the menstrual cycle may serve as a physiological control method to ensure that no pregnancy is present, although this is often not practical or feasible. In turn, patients should be counseled to avoid unprotected intercourse for at least two weeks prior to therapy, recognizing the biological window during which early pregnancy may not yet be detectable, in addition to obtaining a written attestation from the patient on the day of therapy confirming abidance to the two-week pre-therapy abstinence period. However, relying solely on the patient’s report of abstinence is not sufficient from a medical-legal standpoint, so pregnancy testing must be performed on all women of reproductive potential before radioiodine administration. Serum quantitative hCG must be obtained within 72 h prior to therapy, ideally within 24 h. In the setting of an inconclusive pregnancy test result, we recommend delaying therapy and repeating serum quantitative hCG analysis within 48–72 h. Ultimately, future prospective studies are needed to establish more uniform societal guidelines and standardized institutional protocols that balance maternal and fetal safety, as well as clinical feasibility in contemporary clinical practice.
