Introduction to quantitative De Giorgi methods
Giovanni Brigati, Clément Mouhot

Abstract
. The theory of De Giorgi (1958) and Nash (1959) solves Hilbert’s 19th problem and constitutes a major advance in the analysis of PDEs in the 20th century. This theory concerns the Hölder regularity of solutions to elliptic and parabolic equations with non-regular coefficients, and it was extended by Moser (1960) to include the Harnack inequality. This course reviews the classical De Giorgi method in the elliptic and parabolic cases and introduces its recent extension to hypoelliptic equations which appear naturally in kinetic theory. The simplest case is the Kolmogorov equation with a rough diffusion coefficients matrix in the kinetic variable. We present compactness arguments but emphasize the recently developed quantitative methods based on the construction of trajectories. These lecture notes are self-contained and can be used as a general introduction to the topic.



1.
Introduction

This section presents the motivation and scientific context. The new theory we will present in the last section can be called
regularity theory of kinetic equations with rough coefficients
. But it is a new extension of a classical one. It has three independent origins which we briefly summarise in the three next subsections.

1.1.
The solution to Hilbert’s
19
th Problem

At the ICM (International Congress of Mathematicians) of 1900,
[
46
]
, D. Hilbert posed twenty-three mathematical problems to the mathematical community for the incoming century. The
19
th of them asked
which are the regular variational problems
.

Hilbert defined a
regular variational problem
as a variational problem whose Euler-Lagrange equation is an elliptic partial differential equation with analytic coefficients. Therefore, concretely Hilbert’s
19
th problem asks whether, for the class of nonlinear partial differential equations arising as Euler-Lagrange equations of such variational problem, solutions are analytic; this implies that the local minimisers of the original variational problem exists and are analytic. The underlying motivation is that Laplace’s equation admits analytic solutions only, and, at the same time, it is the Euler–Lagrange equation associated with the minimisation of the Dirichlet energy over a bounded domain. Hilbert’s
19
th problem consists therefore in determining how general this observation is.

Hilbert’s question can be reduced to investigating the smoothness of local minimisers, as analyticity follows by studying the convergence of the Taylor series, as observed by Bernstein
[
13
]
. Let
\mathcal{U}
be a bounded domain of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Let
\mathrm{L}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
be a
Lagrangian
function, assumed to be convex. We then consider the variational problem

(1)

\min_{u}\,{\mathcal{E}}(u):=\int_{\mathcal{U}}\mathrm{L}(\nabla_{x}u)\,\mathrm{d}x,

where
u:\mathcal{U}\to\mathbb{R}
varies in an appropriate functional space (which can and often does include suitable boundary conditions). If the Lagrangian is given by

(2)

\mathrm{L}(p)=\frac{1}{2}\,|p|^{2},

then
{\mathcal{E}}
is the Dirichlet energy, and solutions to (
1
) satisfy the linear Laplace equation

\Delta u=0\ \text{ on }\ \mathcal{U}

with appropriate boundary conditions. If

(3)

\mathrm{L}(p)=\sqrt{1+|p|^{2}}

is the area element of the surface defined by the height function
u
, then
{\mathcal{E}}
is the area functional, and the variational problem corresponds to finding
minimal surfaces
. The nonlinear Euler-Lagrange equation is then

(4)

\nabla_{x}\cdot\left(\frac{\nabla_{x}u}{\sqrt{1+|\nabla_{x}u|^{2}}}\right)=0\ \text{ on }\ \mathcal{U}

with, again, appropriate boundary conditions.

In general, we derive the Euler-Lagrange equations associated with (
1
). Let
u
be a solution to (
1
), then for all
w\in\mathrm{C}^{\infty}_{c}(\mathcal{U})
we have

\forall\,\varepsilon>0,\int_{\mathcal{U}}\mathrm{L}\left(\nabla_{x}u+\varepsilon\nabla_{x}w\right)\,\mathrm{d}x\geq\int_{\mathcal{U}}\mathrm{L}(\nabla_{x}u)\,\mathrm{d}x.

By Taylor-expanding the left-hand side of the last display, and dividing by
\varepsilon
, we get

\int_{\mathcal{U}}\Big[\nabla\mathrm{L}(\nabla_{x}u)\,\cdot\,\nabla_{x}w\Big]\,\mathrm{d}x=0,

which is the weak formulation for (the notation “
:
” stands for contracting tensors)

(5)

\nabla_{x}\,\cdot\,\big[\nabla\mathrm{L}(\nabla_{x}u)\big]=\left[\nabla^{2}L(\nabla_{x}u)\right]:\nabla^{2}_{x}u=0

in the space of distributions
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathcal{U})
. This is an elliptic PDE in non-divergence form in general. Let us reason a priori and consider a partial derivative
f=\partial_{x_{k}}u
for
k=1,\dots,n
. Then, if
u
solves (
5
), the new unknown
f
solves (repeated indices are summed)

(6)

\partial_{x_{i}}\cdot(a_{ij}(x)\,\partial_{x_{j}}f)=0,\quad\text{ with }\quad a_{ij}(x)=\left[\nabla^{2}\mathrm{L}(\nabla_{x}u(x))\right]_{ij},

which is a second-order partial differential equation in divergence form. Note that when the matrix of coefficients
A=(a_{ij})
is definite positive—which corresponds to the strict convexity of
L
—then
u
satisfies the maximum principle and Bernstein’s method allows estimating the gradient of
u
in a pointwise manner (see for instance
[
61
]
). Therefore, given suitable boundary conditions, we can assume
\nabla u\in L^{\infty}
.

In these lecture notes, we focus on the
interior regularity
of solutions to equations of the form (
6
), and of their kinetic counterparts. This means working away from the boundary, thus we will not discuss further boundary conditions. The regularity at the boundary is an interesting problem, still currently mostly open in kinetic theory. Solving the
19
th Hilbert problem consists in establishing the analytic regularity of solutions to (
5
)-(
6
). The difficulty is that the matrix of coefficients
A=(a_{i,j})
depends on the gradient of the solution
\nabla u
, resulting in a non-linear bootstrap.

We make the following hypotheses on
\mathrm{L}
.

(H1)

\displaystyle\mathrm{L}\in\mathrm{C}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n}),

(H2)

\displaystyle\exists\,\Lambda>0\,:\,\forall\,p\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\ \Lambda^{-1}\leq\nabla^{2}\mathrm{L}(p)\leq\Lambda.

The first hypothesis can be relaxed appropriately when one is interested in establishing only finite regularity of the solution
u
. The second hypothesis is a uniform coercivity/ellipticity condition on the matrix
A
, combined with an upper bound. In terms of
L
, it corresponds to a uniform convexity combined with a subquadratic control on the growth, together with a pointwise bound on the second derivative.

Going back to our two examples, the Dirichlet Lagrangian (
2
) yields
A=\nabla^{2}L={\text{Id}}
which satisfies our assumption, and the minimal surface Lagrangian (
3
) yields

\nabla^{2}_{p}L(p)=\frac{{\text{Id}}+\left(|p|^{2}{\text{Id}}-p\otimes p\right)}{\left(1+|p|^{2}\right)^{\frac{3}{2}}}

which satisfies a modified form of the hypothesis when we restrict
p
to a bounded region (this turns out to be sufficient for the local regularity theory).

Here are chronologically the main steps in the resolution of Hilbert’s
19
th problem:

1904:

Bernstein
[
13
]
proves in dimension
n=2
that analyticity follows from
C^{3}
regularity for solutions to (
1
)-(
5
), under our assumption on
L
, see also
[
85
]
for general dimensions.

1934:

Schauder
[
86
]
proves that if
A\in C^{\alpha}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathcal{U})
for
\alpha\in(0,1)
(Hölder regularity), then the solution
u
to the non-divergence elliptic equation (
5
) is
C^{2+\alpha}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathcal{U})
. A bootstrap argument can then be implemented by differentiating the equation (the Schauder theory survives when lower order terms are added). It shows that solutions
u
are
\mathrm{C}^{\infty}
. Since we know already
\nabla_{x}u\in L^{\infty}
, the missing link is the Hölder regularity of
\nabla_{x}u
(implying that of
A
), which triggers Schauder’s estimates.

1938:

Morrey
[
69
]
gives a solution which is specific to the two-dimensional case
n=2
.

1957:

De Giorgi and Nash independently
[
22
,
80
]
solve the problem. De Giorgi shows that weak
L^{2}
solutions to

(7)

\begin{cases}&\nabla_{x}\cdot\left(A\,\nabla_{x}f\right)=0\ \text{on }\ \mathcal{U},\\[2.84526pt]
&A\ \text{ symmetric measurable and }\ \Lambda^{-1}\leq A\leq\Lambda\ \text{ for some }\ \Lambda>0,\end{cases}

are such that
f\in\mathrm{C}^{\alpha}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathcal{U})
for some
\alpha>0
. Such an elliptic PDE is said to have
rough coefficients
. Nash proves a similar theorem for linear parabolic equations with rough coefficients.

1960:

Moser
[
70
,
71
,
73
]
introduces an alternative approach closer to “energy estimates” in PDE analysis, which contains many influencing ideas. The related Kruzhkov contribution
[
58
]
simplifies this approach. In particular, these papers contain the first proof of the
Harnack inequality
for elliptic and parabolic equations with rough coefficients.

Together, the last steps of this program are referred to as the
De Giorgi–Nash–Moser theory
(DGNM). The viewpoint adopted in this theory, which had a huge influence in PDE analysis, is that
the nonlinearity is traded for the understanding of a linear problem with
low regularity coefficients
.

Nash performed integral estimates on the fundamental solution to (
7
) and its logarithm, while De Giorgi studied the level sets, see for instance
[
21
,
23
]
for modern presentations. De Giorgi’s proof is elliptic, while Nash’s proof is parabolic, but both approaches can treat both cases. They also both adapt thoroughly to the following
hypoelliptic equations of type I
(see the next subsection)

\sum_{i,j=1}^{m}D_{i}\left(a_{ij}D_{j}f\right)=0

where
(D_{i})_{i=1}^{m}
is a collection of first-order differential operators such that they generate a Lie algebra of full rank

\mathrm{Lie}\left(D_{1},\cdots,D_{m}\right)=\mathrm{span}\left(\partial_{x_{1}},\cdots,\partial_{x_{n}}\right),

where the Lie bracket of the Lie algebra is the commutator of vector fields. The hypotheses on the matrix
A=(a_{i,j})_{1\leq i,j\leq m}
are the same as in (
7
). This case is important in sub-Riemannian geometry, see for instance
[
1
]
, but will not be discussed in these notes.

The classical DGNM theory had two important restrictions. The first one is that it deals with divergence-form PDEs. This is not merely a technical restriction, as all approaches—by De Giorgi, Nash or Moser—crucially rely on
energy estimates
. These energy estimates are also called
Caccioppoli inequalites
: see (
20
) in the elliptic case and (
40
) in the parabolic case and (
60
) in the kinetic (hypoelliptic) case.

A similar result of Hölder regularity was later obtained by Krylov and Safonov
[
62
]
for solutions to non-divergence equations of the form

a_{ij}(x)\,\partial^{2}_{x_{i}x_{j}}f=0,

with a symmetric matrix
A=(a_{i,j})_{i,j}
of rough coefficients that satisfies
\Lambda^{-1}\leq A\leq\Lambda
for some
\Lambda>0
. We also refer to
[
3
,
64
,
60
]
for important previous contributions. The methods are however different in the non-divergence case.

The second restriction of the classical DGNM theory is that it does not cover the
hypoelliptic case of type II
(see the next subsection) which is ubiquitious in physics, and such an extension is the object of these lecture notes.

We however also focus in these notes on obtaining constructive proofs with quantitative estimates on the constants and exponents. This leads us to revisit certain results even in the classical elliptic and parabolic cases.

There are two motivations behind this. The first motivation is rooted in mathematical physics. The classical DGNM theory was developed to solve Hilbert’s
19
th problem about the regularity of energy minimisers. And the extension of this classical theory to the hypoelliptic equations appearing in kinetic theory was developed to obtain the conditional regularity of solutions to the Landau equation (and to the Boltzmann equation for long-range interactions, in the non-local case). In both problems, a mathematical solution is only truly satisfying if the constants of regularity obtained are of orders compatible with the physics itself. And it is difficult to ensure this without developing quantitative methods of proof. The second motivation is internal to mathematics. The search for quantitative methods often deepens the understanding of the mathematical structures and suggests new connexions. In our case, it has lead us to connecting the control of integral oscillation (through inequalities of Poincaré-type) to that of constructing particular trajectories solving a control problem.

1.2.
Hörmander’s hypoellipticity theory

The DGNM theory revolutionised the study of elliptic and parabolic equations. It was however not covering a fundamental class of related equations, whose importance has been recognised at least since Kolmogorov
[
56
]
. In this note, Kolmogorov investigates a model equation driven by a first-order differential operator combined with diffusion only in certain directions. Let
x,v
be the variables respectively in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. Then,
Kolmogorov’s equation
reads, in its simplest version:

(8)

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}f+\,v\cdot\nabla_{x}f=\Delta_{v}f,\quad f=f(t,x,v),\\[5.69054pt]
f(0,\cdot,\cdot)=f_{0}\ \text{ which is a probability density on }{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}.\end{cases}

This equation corresponds to the evolution of the law of the random process

(9)

\begin{cases}\mathrm{d}X_{t}=V_{t}\,\mathrm{d}t,\\
\mathrm{d}V_{t}\,=\mathrm{d}W_{t},\end{cases}

where
(W_{t})_{t}
is a standard
d-
dimensional Brownian motion. Indeed, (
9
) is a time-integrated Brownian motion, which gives its name to the note
[
56
]
.

Kolmogorov computes an explicit formula for the fundamental solution to (
8
), by combining Fourier analysis and Duhamel’s principle. Given the initial data
f_{0}=\delta_{x_{0},v_{0}}
in (
8
), one gets

(10)

G(t,x,v)=\left(\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2\,\pi\,t^{2}}\right)^{d}\,\mathrm{exp}\left(-3\,\frac{\left|x-x_{0}-t\,(v+v_{0})/2\right|^{2}}{t^{3}}-\frac{|v-v_{0}|^{2}}{4t}\right).

The fundamental solution (
10
) is smooth in all variables for
t>0
, and therefore all solutions to (
8
) are smooth as well for
t>0
. However, ellipticity in
x
fails for (
8
). Intuitively, the noise in
v
is “transferred” to the
x
variable by the transport drift operator
v\cdot\nabla_{x}
, see Figure
1
.

Figure 1.
Noise in
v
propagates to the
x
variable thanks to the transport drift
v\cdot\nabla_{x}
.

After some seminal works in specific cases such as
[
83
]
, a complete understanding of this phenomenon was developed by Hörmander
[
48
]
in 1967. His starting point was Kolmogorov’s calculation showing that for second-order operators with smooth coefficients, while ellipticity is a sufficient condition for the smoothness of the fundamental solution (he called the latter property “hypoellipticity”), it is
not
necessary. For the Kolmogorov equation, regularisation is produced by the interaction between the vector fields
\nabla_{v}
and
v\cdot\nabla_{x}
.

More precisely, we say that a linear operator
L
is
hypoelliptic
if

Lu\in\mathrm{C}^{\infty}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathcal{U})\ \Longrightarrow\ u\in\mathrm{C}^{\infty}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathcal{U}),

in an open subset
\mathcal{U}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
(again, we do not address the issue of the boundaries). When restricting to second-order operators with smooth coefficients, the standard
ellipticity
, i.e. the coercivity of the principal symbol, implies this condition. It is however not necessary. Consider, for
m\in{\mathbb{N}}^{*}
, a family
(X_{i})_{i=1}^{m}
of smooth vector fields and the operator

(11)

L=X_{0}+\sum_{i=1}^{m}X_{i}^{2}.

The main theorem of
[
48
]
is a necessary and sufficient condition for operators of the form (
11
) to be hypoelliptic: the Lie algebra of
X_{0},\dots,X_{m}
must satisfy

\dim\mathrm{Lie}(X_{0},\cdots,X_{m})=n.

When
X_{0}=0
(no first-order term) such operators write as a sum of squares and we call them
hypoelliptic operators of type I
; otherwise we call them
hypoelliptic operators of type II
. Hypoelliptic operators of type II have also been called
ultraparabolic
in the literature.

The Kolmogorov operator is the archetypical example of the type II. Let

(12)

\mathcal{K}:=\partial_{t}+v\,\cdot\,\nabla_{x}+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\partial^{\star}_{v_{i}}\partial_{v_{i}},

be the operator driving (
8
), with
t\in{\mathbb{R}}
,
x,v\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.
This has the form (
11
) with

n=2d+1,\quad X_{0}=\partial_{t}+v\,\cdot\,\nabla_{x},\ \text{ and }\ X_{i}=\partial_{v_{i}}\ \text{ for }\ i=1,\cdots,d.

The fundamental computation is

[X_{0},X_{i}]=-\partial_{x_{i}},\quad\forall\,\,i=1,\cdots,d

which allows recovering all
\partial_{x_{i}}
’s, and finally
\partial_{t}
is recovered by linear combination. Hence,

\dim\mathrm{Lie}(X_{0},X_{1},\cdots,X_{d})=n

at every point and
\mathcal{K}
is hypoelliptic. By inspecting the
(x,v)
-Fourier transform of the Kolmogorov fundamental solution, one sees that hypoelliptic operators have in general
Gevrey
rather than
analytic
regularisation (with exponent
2/3
in the case of the Kolmogorov operator, but this exponent depends on the Lie algebra).

1.3.
Mathematical kinetic theory

The bedrock of kinetic theory is Newton’s classical mechanics. Along Newton’s theory, decisive progresses were later made for electrico-magnetic forces (Ampère, Faraday, Maxwell…), large velocities and large scales (Lorentz, Poincaré, Einstein, Minskowski…), small scales and quantum physics (Planck, Einstein, Bohr, Heisenberg, Schrödinger, Dirac, Bose…). However, all these works concern the study of a single object (a planet, an electron,…) or a small number of those, such as the planets in the solar system or the electrons in an atom. Many natural phenomena pertain to systems made of a very large number of objects (or particles, or agents…). The observable universe contains
10^{9}
–
10^{12}
galaxies, each of them made of billions of stars (about
10^{10}
in the Milky Way). A gas is composed of about
10^{23}
molecules per mole (the Avogadro constant). Crowds, flocks of birds, shoals of fishes, are made up of thousands of individuals (sentient or not). In such situations, it is often impossible to track each particle/molecule/indidividual.

In the 1867 paper
[
67
]
(and in subsequent papers), Maxwell laid the mathematical foundations of statistical mechanics. The statistical approach was developed to understand these large systems through their statistical distributions in a way that is rigorously connected to the microscopic dynamics. This is in contrast with the previously invented fluid mechanics, which combines conservation laws and adhoc closure laws to derive evolution equations at the macroscopic level. The theory invented by Maxwell is called
kinetic
due to the presence of a “kinetic” variable, the microsopic velocity, which is inaccessible to observation. We have therefore three scales of description of complex systems, ordered by increasing level of detail:

(1)

a
macroscopic
scale (fluid dynamics, observable);

(2)

a
mesoscopic
scale (statistical mechanics, where the behaviour of the unknown in some variables is only observable on average);

(3)

a
microscopic
scale (Newton’s mechanics, fully not observable).

At the mesoscopic scale, the fundamental equation—and also the oldest one—is the
(Maxwell-)Boltzmann equation
, invented in
[
67
,
15
]
:

(13)

\partial_{t}f+v\,\cdot\,\nabla_{x}f=Q(f,f).

The unknown is a non-negative time-dependent probability distribution function
f=f(t,x,v)
, with
x\in\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and
\Omega
a spatial domain with suitable boundary conditions. The
collision operator

Q
is given by

Q(f,f)(t,x,v)=\\
\int_{v_{\star}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\,\int_{\sigma\in{\mathbb{S}}^{d-1}}\mathrm{B}(v-v_{\star},\omega)\,\left[f(t,x,v^{\prime})\,f(t,x,v^{\prime}_{\star})-f(t,x,v)\,f(t,x,v_{\star})\right]\,\mathrm{d}\omega\,\mathrm{d}v_{\star},

where the variables
v_{\star}
,
v^{\prime}
,
v^{\prime}_{\star}
are related to
v
as follows (given
\sigma\in{\mathbb{S}}^{d-1}
)

v^{\prime}=\frac{v+v_{\star}}{2}+\frac{|v-v_{\star}|}{2}\sigma,\qquad v^{\prime}_{\star}=\frac{v+v_{\star}}{2}-\frac{|v-v_{\star}|}{2}\sigma.

This is a parametrisation of the degrees of freedom left over once the conservation laws of the elastic binary collision between particles with identical mass—see Figure
2
—have been taken into account:

\begin{cases}v+v_{\star}=v^{\prime}+v^{\prime}_{\star},\qquad&\text{(conservation of momentum)}\\[5.69054pt]
|v|^{2}+|v_{\star}|^{2}=|v^{\prime}|^{2}+|v^{\prime}_{\star}|^{2},\qquad&\text{(conservation of kinetic energy)}.\end{cases}

Observe that
Q(f,f)
statistically accounts for collisions at time
t
, at a point
x
, between two particles with pre-collisional velocities
v^{\prime},v^{\prime}_{\star}
, and post-collisional velocities
v,v_{\star}
, see Figure
2
. The collision kernel
\mathrm{B}
encodes the frequencies at which collisions with different pairs of velocities occur, and
Q(f,f)
collects the weighted balance-sheet of all possible collisions in the velocity space.
Q
is a bilinear nonlocal operator acting only in the kinetic variable
v
.

Figure 2.
A collision between two particles.

By invariance (see
[
14
]
), the collision kernel
B
is a function of
|v-v_{\star}|
and
\cos\theta
, so that
\mathrm{B}=\mathrm{B}(|v-v_{\star}|,\cos\theta)
,
where
\theta
is the angle between
v-v_{\star}
and
v^{\prime}-v^{\prime}_{\star}
, i.e. between
v-v_{\star}
and
\sigma
. The computation of Maxwell shows that
\mathrm{B}=|v-v_{\star}|
for hard spheres interacting by elastic collisions. When particles interact through a repulsive force proportional to
r^{-\alpha}
with
r
the distance between particles and
\alpha>2
, we have in dimension
d=3

B=|v-v_{\star}|^{\gamma}\,b(\cos\theta),\quad b(\cos\theta)\sim_{\theta\to 0}\theta^{-2-2s},

with

\gamma=\frac{\alpha-5}{\alpha-1},\qquad s=\frac{1}{\alpha-1}.

Except in the hard-sphere case, the kernel
B
is therefore
not
integrable on
{\mathbb{S}}^{d-1}
:

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{2}}B

\displaystyle=|v-v_{\star}|^{\gamma}\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{2}}b(\cos\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\sigma

\displaystyle=2\pi|v-v_{\star}|^{\gamma}\int_{0}^{\pi}b(\cos\theta)\sin\theta\,\mathrm{d}\theta

\displaystyle\approx 2\pi|v-v_{\star}|^{\gamma}\int_{0}^{\pi}\theta^{-1-2s}\,\mathrm{d}\theta=+\infty.

This singularity in
\theta
implies that
Q
manifests non-local fractional ellipticity. Artificially removing such singularity is called
Grad’s cutoff
, and accordingly the non-modified regime is called
non-cutoff long-range interaction
. The challenge of extending the De Giorgi–Nash–Moser theory to the Boltzmann equation with non-cutoff long-range interaction was, to our knowledge, first raised in
[
91
]
.

It helps intuition if one draws an analogy with local differential operators. The dependence of
\mathrm{B}
on
|v-v_{\star}|
is analogous to the growth or decay of the coefficients of a local differential operator, while the singularity of order
2s
at
\theta\sim 0
is reminiscent of the order of differentiability for this operator.

It means heuristically that one expects the collision operator
Q
to share similar features with the following rough approximation

Q\approx\nabla_{v}^{s}\cdot\,\left(A\,\nabla_{v}^{s}\right)\ +\text{ lower order terms,}

with
A
growing like
|v|^{\gamma}
at large
v
. This analogy is backed up by the regularity estimates
[
4
,
6
]
and by the spectral gap estimates
[
78
,
38
]
. Moreover, for
\gamma=0
, Fourier methods apply just like they do for constant coefficients PDEs. And when
\alpha\to 2
and
s\to 1
the Boltzmann kernel indeed resembles more and more a local diffusion operator (with diffusion coefficients depending on the solution in a non-local manner). The limit case
\alpha=2
corresponds to the
Coulomb repulsive interaction
; the Boltzmann operator is ill-defined in this case, and must be modified, as we discuss below.

Another fundamental observation is that Boltzmann’s equation (
13
) “contains” the compressible hydrodynamic equations on the macroscopic fields (local density, local momentum and local temperatures):

\rho=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}f\,\mathrm{d}v,\qquad\rho\,u=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}f\,v\,\mathrm{d}v,\qquad\rho\,T=\frac{1}{d}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}f\,|v-u|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}v.

More precisely: such dynamics can, at least formally, be derived from the Boltzmann equation in certain scaling limits. Viscous incompressible hydrodynamics can also be obtained by a scaling limit. Hence, the Cauchy problem for (
13
) is expected to be at least difficult as the Cauchy problem for the Euler and Navier-Stokes systems.

Going back to the limit case
\alpha=2
, one sees that the singular integral does not make sense anymore, and the Boltzmann operator must be modified. Instead, one must use the
Landau equation
(invented by Landau
[
63
,
88
]
in 1936)

(14)

\partial_{t}f+\,v\cdot\nabla_{x}f=Q_{\mathrm{L}}(f,f),

with the
Landau operator
(since it does not act on the variables
t,x
, these variables are omitted from the formula for clarity)

(15)

\displaystyle Q_{\mathrm{L}}(f,f)(v)=

\displaystyle\nabla_{v}\cdot\left(\int_{v_{\star}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}|v-v_{\star}|^{-1}\,\Pi_{(v-v^{\star})^{\perp}}\Big[f(v_{\star})\,\nabla_{v}\,f(v)-f(v)\,\nabla_{v_{\star}}f(v_{\star})\Big]\,\mathrm{d}v_{\star}\right).

On can rewrite
Q_{\mathrm{L}}
as a non-linear drift-diffusion operator with coefficients that depend in a non-local manner on the solution:

(16)

Q_{\mathrm{L}}(f,f)=\nabla_{v}\cdot\left(A[f]\,\nabla_{v}f+B[f]\,f\right).

The global Cauchy theory for the Landau–Coulomb equation is open in general, but has recently been solved in the spatially homogeneous case
[
43
]
, following many previous partial progresses
[
92
,
24
,
25
,
31
,
30
,
5
,
94
,
87
,
33
]
(this non-exaustive list is restricted to works dealing with the spatially homogeneous Landau equation). We give two open problems hereby motivating a regularity theory for nonlinear hypoelliptic PDEs.

We denote by
hydrodynamic fields
the functions

(t,x)\quad\mapsto\quad\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}f\,\mathrm{d}v,\quad\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}vf\,\mathrm{d}v,\quad\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}|v|^{2}f\,\mathrm{d}v,\quad\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}f\ln f\,\mathrm{d}v.

Problem 1

(Conditional regularity for the Boltzmann equation)
.

Show that a solution on
[0,T]
to (

) with non-cutoff long-range interactions, whose hydrodynamic fields satisfy suitable a priori pointwise bounds on
[0,T]
, is smooth on
(0,T]

This problem is now solved for
\alpha>3

•

conditionally to bounds on the hydrodynamic fields by
[

,

•

polynomial decay estimates hold on
f
conditionally to bounds on the hydrodynamic fields by
[
51
]
,

•

finally weak Harnack inequality, Hölder regularity, Schauder estimates, and smoothness are proved, conditionally to bounds on the hydrodynamic fields, in the series of papers
[
52
,
53
,
54
]
.

For
\alpha\in(2,3)
the problem is open.

Problem 2

(Conditional regularity for the Landau equation)
.

Show that a solution on
[0,T]
to (
14
), which satisfies suitable a priori pointwise bounds on the hydrodynamic fields, is smooth on
(0,T]
.

This second problem in roughly the limit case
\alpha=2
of the first problem. It is still open, but a partial answer has been obtained for less singular, mollified versions, of the Landau operator. Consider for
\gamma\geq-3
, a modification of the operator (

|v-v_{\star}|^{-1}
in the integrand is replaced by
|v-v_{\star}|^{\gamma+2}
. The original case of Coulombian interactions corresponds to

, we have the following results:

•

L^{\infty}
bounds hold on
f
conditionally to bounds on the hydrodynamic fields by
[
87
]
,

•

Harnack inequality and Hölder regularity hold on
f
conditionally to bounds on the hydrodynamic fields by
[
34
]
,

•

Smoothness is proved conditionally to bounds on the hydrodynamic fields by
[
45
,
50
]
.

For

the problem is open.

To finish this brief overview, let us add that many other problems in mathematical physics would certainly benefit from a robust and quantitative theory combining the approaches of De Giorgi–Nash–Moser and Hörmander. We will present some steps towards such theory, but much remains to be explored; in particular the boundary regularity and non-divergence equations.

2.1.
The result of De Giorgi

Let us go back to Hilbert’s
19^{th}
problem. We consider
\mathcal{U}
an open subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, a Lagrangian
L:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\to{\mathbb{R}}
and the minimisation problem (
1
). When
L(p)=|p|^{2}/2
, then
{\mathcal{E}}

is the standard Dirichlet form and the Euler-Lagrange equation of the minimisation problem is simply the Laplace equation. Weak solutions to the Laplace equation are harmonic in
\mathcal{U}
, from which one deduces the smoothness and real analyticity in
\mathcal{U}
(interior regularity). Hilbert’s question asked whether this interior regularity hold for more general Lagrangians
\mathrm{L}
that satisfy (
H1
)-(
H2
). The original motivation of De Giorgi was to answer this question.

Lemma 3
.

The following two steps reduce Hilbert’s
19
th problem to the regularity theory of elliptic PDEs with rough coefficients:

(1)

Let
\mathrm{L}\in C^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
so that
\nabla^{2}\mathrm{L}\leq\Lambda
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
for some
\Lambda>0
. Then all minimisers of (
1
) in
\mathrm{H}^{1}(\mathcal{U})
are also weak solutions to the Euler–Lagrange equation (
5
).

(2)

Let
\mathrm{L}\in C^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
so that
\Lambda^{-1}\leq\nabla^{2}\mathrm{L}\leq\Lambda
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
for some
\Lambda>0
. Let
u\in\mathrm{H}^{1}(\mathcal{U})
be a weak solution to (
5
),
\tilde{\mathcal{U}}\,\subset\subset\mathcal{U}
, and
i=1,\cdots,n
. Then
u\in H^{2}(\tilde{\mathcal{U}})
and
f:=\partial_{x_{i}}u\in H^{1}(\tilde{\mathcal{U}})
is a weak solution to (
6
) on
\tilde{\mathcal{U}}
.

Proof.

(i) Given
\varepsilon>0
and
w\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathcal{U}),
we have

\int_{\mathcal{U}}\mathrm{L}(\nabla_{x}u\pm\varepsilon\nabla_{x}w)\,\mathrm{d}x\geq\int_{\mathcal{U}}\mathrm{L}(\nabla_{x}u)\,\mathrm{d}x.

Note that integrands are integrable because
\nabla_{x}u
,
\nabla_{x}w\in L^{2}(\mathcal{U})
and
\mathrm{L}
is sub-quadratic. Then, for all
x\in\mathcal{U}
, we have by Taylor expansion

\mathrm{L}(\nabla_{x}u(x)\pm\varepsilon\,\nabla_{x}w(x))\leq\mathrm{L}(\nabla_{x}u(x))\pm\varepsilon\nabla\mathrm{L}(\nabla_{x}u(x))\cdot\nabla_{x}w(x)+\Lambda\,\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}\,|\nabla_{x}w(x)|^{2}.

The last two equations yield

\pm\int_{\mathcal{U}}\nabla\mathrm{L}(\nabla_{x}u)\cdot\nabla_{x}w\,\mathrm{d}x\geq-\Lambda\,\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\,\int_{\mathcal{U}}|\nabla_{x}w|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x.

By letting
\varepsilon\to 0
, we deduce

\forall\,w\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathcal{U}),\quad\int_{\mathcal{U}}\nabla\mathrm{L}(\nabla_{x}u)\cdot\nabla_{x}w\,\mathrm{d}x=0,

which is the weak form of (
5
).

(ii) By symmetry, a covering argument, translation and dilation, it is enough to prove the claim when
\mathcal{U}=B_{3}
,
\tilde{\mathcal{U}}=B_{1}
,
i=1
.
For
-1\leq h\leq 1,
consider

\tau_{h}u(x):=\frac{u(x+h\,e_{1})-u(x)}{h},

and
\eta
a smooth function such that

\eta\equiv 1\text{ on }B_{1},\quad\eta\equiv 0\text{ on }B_{2}^{c},\quad\text{ and }\ |\nabla_{x}\eta|\leq 2.

The operators
\eta\,\tau_{h},
and
\tau_{h}\,(\eta\,\cdot)
map
L^{2}(B_{3})
to itself and map
H^{1}(B_{3})
to
H^{1}_{0}(B_{3})
. We then test (
5
) against
\phi:=\tau_{-h}\left(\eta^{2}\tau_{h}u\right)
. Note that squaring the localisation function
\eta
is standard in energy estimates and related to the
L^{2}
structure. We obtain:

\displaystyle 0=\int_{B_{3}}\nabla_{x}\phi\cdot\nabla L(\nabla_{x}u)\,\mathrm{d}x=

\displaystyle-\int_{B_{3}}\eta\,\nabla_{x}(\tau_{h}u)\cdot\eta\,\tau_{h}(\nabla L(\nabla_{x}u))\,\mathrm{d}x-2\int_{B_{3}}(\nabla_{x}\eta)\,(\tau_{h}u)\,\eta\tau_{h}(\nabla L(\nabla_{x}u))\,\mathrm{d}x=

\displaystyle-\int_{B_{3}}\eta\,\nabla_{x}(\tau_{h}u)\,\left(\int_{0}^{1}\nabla^{2}L(\nabla_{x}u+\theta\,(\nabla_{x}u(x+h\,e_{i})-\nabla_{x}u(x)))\,d\theta\right)\,\eta\nabla_{x}(\tau_{h}u)\,\mathrm{d}x+

\displaystyle-2\int_{B_{3}}(\nabla_{x}\eta)(\tau_{h}u)\left(\int_{0}^{1}\nabla^{2}L(\nabla_{x}u+\theta\,(\nabla_{x}u(x+h\,e_{i})-\nabla_{x}u(x)))\,d\theta\right)\,\eta\,\nabla_{x}(\tau_{h}u)\,\mathrm{d}x.

Using
\Lambda^{-1}\leq\nabla^{2}L\leq\Lambda
, we deduce

\frac{1}{\Lambda}\,\int_{B_{3}}\eta^{2}|\nabla_{x}\tau_{h}u|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\leq 2\Lambda\left(\int_{B_{3}}|\nabla_{x}\eta|^{2}\,|\tau_{h}u|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\right)^{1/2}\,\left(\int_{B_{3}}\eta^{2}\,|\nabla_{x}\tau_{h}u|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\right)^{1/2},

which shows

\int_{B_{3}}\eta^{2}|\nabla_{x}\tau_{h}u|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\lesssim\,\Lambda^{4}\int_{B_{3}}|\nabla_{x}\eta|^{2}\,|\tau_{h}u|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\leq\Lambda^{4}\,\|u\|^{2}_{H^{1}(B_{3})}.

By letting
h\to 0
, we deduce
f=\partial_{i}u\in H^{1}(B_{1})
and

\|f\|_{H^{1}(B_{1})}\lesssim\Lambda^{2}\|u\|_{H^{1}(B_{3})}

(see
[
27
]
for results on generalised derivatives). Then given
\phi\in H^{1}_{0}(B_{1})
, we have
\tau_{-h}\phi\in H^{1}_{0}(B_{2})
and

0=-\int_{B_{2}}\tau_{h}\phi\,\nabla_{x}\cdot(\nabla L(\nabla_{x}u))\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{B_{2}}\nabla_{x}\phi\cdot\tau_{h}(\nabla L(\nabla_{x}u))\,\mathrm{d}x.

Finally, by letting
h\to 0
, we deduce

0=\int_{B_{1}}\nabla_{x}\phi\cdot\partial_{x_{1}}\nabla L(\nabla_{x}u)\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{B_{1}}\nabla_{x}\phi\cdot\nabla^{2}L(\nabla_{x}u)\nabla_{x}f\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{B_{1}}\nabla_{x}\phi\cdot A\nabla_{x}f\,\mathrm{d}x,

which is the weak form of (
18
) on
B_{1}
for
f
.
∎

As we have discussed above, the previous works of Bernstein, Schauder and others have showed that if
u\in C^{1+\alpha}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathcal{U})
solves (
5
), then
u
is smooth and real analytic in
\mathcal{U}
. The solution to Hilbert’s
19
th problem is therefore reduced to proving
\nabla_{x}u\in C^{\alpha}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathcal{U})
for some
\alpha\in(0,1)
, i.e. that any weak
H^{1}
solution
f
to (
6
) is Hölder continuous.

Let us recall the definition of Hölder spaces. For any open set
\mathcal{U}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we define the
Hölder norm

C^{\alpha}(\mathcal{U})
as follows:

(17)

\|f\|_{C^{\alpha}(\mathcal{U})}:=\|f\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{U})}+[f]_{C^{\alpha}(\mathcal{U})},\qquad[f]_{C^{\alpha}(\mathcal{U})}:=\sup_{x,y\in\mathcal{U}}\,\frac{|f(x)-f(y)|}{|x-y|^{\alpha}},

where
[f]_{C^{\alpha}(\mathcal{U})}
is the
Hölder seminorm
and
\alpha\in(0,1)
. The standard notation
C^{\alpha}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathcal{U})
denotes the set of functions that are in
C^{\alpha}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\tilde{\mathcal{U}})
for every
\tilde{\mathcal{U}}\subset\subset\mathcal{U}
.

Theorem 4

(Elliptic De Giorgi–Nash–Moser)
.

Given
\Lambda>0
, there is
\alpha\in(0,1)
, depending only
\Lambda>0
and the dimension
n
, and there is
C>0
, depending only on
\Lambda
and the dimension
n
, so that the following holds:

Let
f\in H^{1}(\mathcal{U})
be a weak solution on the open set
\mathcal{U}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
to the equation

(18)

\nabla_{x}\cdot(A\,\nabla_{x}f)=0,

with
A:\mathcal{U}\to\mathbb{R}^{2n}
a symmetric matrix with measurable entries so that

\quad\Lambda^{-1}\leq A(x)\leq\Lambda\quad\text{for almost every }x\in\mathcal{U}.

Then, we have
f\in C^{\alpha}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathcal{U})
.

Moreover, given
\delta>0
, there is
C>0
, depending only on
\Lambda
,
\delta
and the dimension
n
, so that for any
\tilde{\mathcal{U}}\subset\subset\mathcal{U}
so that
\mathrm{dist}(\tilde{\mathcal{U}},\mathcal{U})\geq\delta
, we have

\|f\|_{C^{\alpha}(\tilde{\mathcal{U}})}\leq C\|f\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathcal{U})}.

Remarks 5
.

(1)

Notice that the strict convexity

\nabla^{2}\mathrm{L}\geq\Lambda^{-1}
is necessary. In dimension

n=1
, let
\mathrm{L}
be a double-well shaped function

\mathrm{L}(p)=|p|^{2}-2|p|.

Then, one can check that
u(x)=|x|
is
a minimiser of (
1
) in
H^{1}((-2,2))
, but
u^{\prime}
is not Hölder
continuous.

(2)

Nash proved a similar result for parabolic equations with rough coefficients, which includes this theorem as a particular case. We discuss the parabolic case in the next section. Moser and Kruzhkov later also recovered this theorem by other proofs.

(3)

We emphasize that this is
not
a perturbation result around the case of constant coefficients: the equation may not be locally close to an elliptic PDE with constant coefficients, whichever the scale one zooms in. The entries of
A
being merely measurable, they may oscillate fast at
every
scale.

2.2.
The first De Giorgi Lemma

The argument of De Giorgi is divided into two parts, corresponding roughly first to the control of the
L^{\infty}
part of the Hölder norm at every scale (first De Giorgi Lemma) and second to the control of the oscillation and thus the Hölder semi-norm (second De Giorgi Lemma). The first lemma plays a key role in the proof of the second lemma.

Lemma 6

(First De Giorgi Lemma: gain of integrability)
.

Given
\Lambda>0
, there is
\delta>0
, depending only on
\Lambda
and the dimension
n
, so that the following holds:

Let
f\in H^{1}(B_{1})
be a weak subsolution to

\nabla_{x}\cdot(A\nabla_{x}f)\geq 0

with
A
symmetric measurable so that

\forall\,x\in\mathcal{U},\quad\Lambda^{-1}\leq A(x)\leq\Lambda.

Then, we have

\|f_{+}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(B_{1})}\leq\delta\quad\implies\quad\|f_{+}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(B_{1/2})}\leq\frac{1}{2}.

Remarks 7
.

(1)

This lemma is scale-invariant provided that the integrals are normalised:

\forall\,r\in(0,1],\qquad\left(\int_{B_{r}}f_{+}^{2}\,\frac{dx}{|B_{r}|}\right)^{1/2}\leq\delta\,\implies\|f_{+}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(B_{r/2})}\leq\frac{1}{2}.

(2)

By covering, translation and dilation, this result implies that, when the Lagrangian
\mathrm{L}
satisfies (
H1
)-(
H2
), any minimiser
u
of (
1
) in
H^{1}(\mathcal{U})
is in
W^{1,\infty}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathcal{U})
.

(3)

Lemma
6
implies that
f\in H^{1}(\mathcal{U})
weak solution to (
18
) is
\mathrm{L}^{\infty}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathcal{U})
. As soon as
n\geq 1
, this is not implied by Sobolev’s embeddings. Let us also emphasize that even in situations where the
L^{\infty}
bound on
f
can be obtained by maximum principle arguments, this lemma proves a stronger result because of the the scale-invariant estimates.

(4)

The super-exponential convergence of the De Giorgi–Moser iteration below is reminiscent of Newton’s iterative method, used in the KAM Theorem
[
57
,
8
,
72
]
and in the so-called Nash–Moser implicit function theorem
[
79
,
75
,
74
]
.

Proof of Lemma
6
(De Giorgi’s version).

The proof combines an iterative scheme, a localised energy estimate, the use of the Sobolev embedding and the Chebyshev inequality.

A. The iterative scheme.
For all
k\geq 0
, we consider balls with radii

r_{k}=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2^{k+1}}\ \text{ so that }\ r_{0}=1\text{ and }r_{k}\downarrow\frac{1}{2}

(see Figure
3
), and energy levels

e_{k}:=\frac{1}{2}-2^{-k-1}\ \text{ so that }e_{0}=0\text{ and }e_{k}\uparrow\frac{1}{2}.

Figure 3.
The sequence of balls in the De Giorgi–Moser iteration.

The goal is to establish estimates by induction on

\forall\,k\geq 0,\qquad E_{k}:=\int_{B_{r_{k}}}(f-e_{k})_{+}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x.

Indeed
E_{0}=\|f\|_{L^{2}(B_{1})}^{2}
, and if we prove that there are
C>0
and
\beta>1
such that

(19)

\forall\,k\geq 1,\quad E_{k}\leq C^{k}\,E_{k-1}^{\beta}

then we deduce by induction

E_{k}\leq C^{k+\beta(k-1)+\beta^{2}(k-2)+\cdots+\beta^{k-1}}\,E_{0}^{\beta^{k}}\leq\left(C^{\prime}\,E_{0}\right)^{\beta^{k}}.

where we have used

k+\beta(k-1)+\cdots+\beta^{k-1}=\frac{k+1}{1-\beta}+\beta^{k}\,\frac{\beta}{(1-\beta)^{2}}\lesssim\beta^{k}.

If
E_{0}\,C^{\prime}<1
, which is ensured by
\|f_{+}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(B_{1})}\leq\delta
with
\delta<1/C^{\prime}
, then

\int_{B_{\frac{1}{2}}}\left(f-\frac{1}{2}\right)_{+}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x=E_{\infty}=\lim_{k\to\infty}\,E_{k}=0,

which concludes the proof.

The rest of the proof is devoted to establishing (
19
). This inequality is nonlinear, due to the hidden collision of scalings in the equation which relates the
L^{2}
norms of
f
and of the gradient of
f
: this collision of scalings is revealed by the energy estimate, Sobolev’s inequality and Chebyshev’s inequality.

B. Localised energy estimate.
The following inequality is a localised version of the by-now standard
L^{2}\to H^{1}
ellipticity estimate, which still holds for divergence-form elliptic equations with rough coefficients:

(20)

\forall\,\phi\in C^{1}_{c}(B_{1}),\quad\int_{B_{1}}|\nabla_{x}(\phi f_{+})|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\leq\Lambda^{2}\,\|\nabla_{x}\phi\|^{2}_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(B_{1})}\,\int_{B_{1}\cap\mathrm{supp}\,\phi}f_{+}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x.

It is also called
Caccioppoli inequality
, in memory of
[
19
]
where Caccioppoli introduced such inequalities in his study of elliptic equations. The motivation of Caccioppoli was to provide an
L^{2}
analogue of the Cauchy inequalities for holomorphic functions, and mean value type inequalities for harmonic functions. Holomorphic functions indeed satisfy the Cauchy-Riemann equations, an elliptic system, and the Cauchy integral formula implies a pointwise control on the first derivative of a function on a small ball by the values of the function itself on a larger ball, with a constant that involves the difference in the radii of the two balls. Similar estimates can be derived for harmonic functions thanks to the mean value property. Caccioppoli worked on Hilbert’s
19
-th problem in the 1930s
1
1

1

See the biographical note at
https://mathshistory.st-andrews.ac.uk/Biographies/Caccioppoli/
.
: he extended the method of Bernstein beyond the two-dimensional case, and reduced the assumption from
C^{3}
to
C^{2}
regularity on the minimiser to (
1
).

To prove it, we integrate
\nabla_{x}\cdot(A\nabla_{x}f)\geq 0
against
\phi^{2}\,f_{+}
to get

\displaystyle 0\leq

\displaystyle\int_{B_{1}}\nabla_{x}\cdot(A\nabla_{x}f)\,\phi^{2}\,f_{+}\,\mathrm{d}x=-\int_{B_{1}}(A\nabla_{x}f_{+})\cdot\nabla_{x}(\phi^{2}f_{+})\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\int_{B_{1}}(A\nabla_{x}f_{+})\cdot\nabla_{x}(\phi f_{+})\,\phi\,\mathrm{d}x-\int_{B_{1}}(A\nabla_{x}f_{+}\cdot\nabla_{x}\phi)\,(f_{+}\,\phi)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\int_{B_{1}}A\nabla_{x}(\phi f_{+})\cdot\nabla_{x}(\phi f_{+})\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\qquad-\int_{B_{1}}A\nabla_{x}\phi\cdot\nabla_{x}(f_{+}\phi)\,f_{+}\,\mathrm{d}x-\int_{B_{1}}(A\nabla_{x}f_{+}\cdot\nabla_{x}\phi)(f_{+}\phi)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\int_{B_{1}}A\nabla_{x}(\phi f_{+})\cdot\nabla_{x}(\phi f_{+})\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{B_{1}}(A\nabla_{x}\phi\cdot\nabla_{x}\phi)f_{+}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x,

(the integration by parts have no boundary terms since
\phi
being zero on
\partial B_{1}
), which implies

\int_{B_{1}}A\nabla_{x}(\phi f_{+})\cdot\nabla_{x}(\phi f_{+})\,\mathrm{d}x\leq\int_{B_{1}}(A\nabla_{x}\phi\cdot\nabla_{x}\phi)f_{+}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x.

We deduce (
20
) thanks to the inequality
\Lambda^{-1}\leq A\leq\Lambda
almost everywhere on the matrix
A
.

C. Sobolev’s embedding.
Let
(\phi_{k})_{k}\subset C^{\infty}_{c}(B_{1})
be a family of cutoff functions such that

\forall\,k\geq 1,\quad\mathbf{1}_{B_{r_{k}}}\leq\phi_{k}\leq\mathbf{1}_{B_{r_{k+1}}},\qquad|\nabla_{x}\phi_{k}|\leq c_{0}2^{k},

for some
c_{0}
which does not depend on
k
. We bound from above

E_{k}=\int_{B_{r_{k}}}(f-e_{k})_{+}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\leq\int_{B_{1}}\phi_{k}^{2}\,(f-e_{k})_{+}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x.

For all
k\geq 1
, the function
(f-e_{k})
also satisfies (
18
) by linearity and because constants are solutions. We then apply (
20
) with
\phi:=\phi_{k}
to get

(21)

\int_{B_{1}}|\nabla_{x}(\phi_{k}\,(f-e_{k})_{+})|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\leq\Lambda^{2}\,\|\nabla_{x}\phi_{k}\|^{2}_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(B_{1})}\,\int_{B_{1}\cap\mathrm{supp}\,\phi_{k}}(f-e_{k})_{+}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x.

Let us define the following integrability exponent

p_{*}:=\frac{2d}{d-2}\text{ for }d\geq 3,\ \text{ or any }\ p_{*}\in(2,\infty)\text{ for }d=1,2.

Then, Sobolev’s inequality
[
27
]
applied to the function
\phi_{k}\,(f-e_{k})_{+}
over
B_{1}
combined with the localised energy estimate (
21
) yields

\displaystyle\|\phi_{k}\,(f-e_{k})_{+}\|^{2}_{L^{p_{*}}(B_{1})}

\displaystyle\leq C_{\mathrm{Sob}}^{2}\,\int_{B_{1}}|\nabla_{x}(\phi_{k}(f)e_{k})_{+})|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq C_{\mathrm{Sob}}^{2}\,\Lambda^{2}\,c_{0}^{2}\,2^{2k}\int_{B_{1}\cap\mathrm{supp}\,\phi}(f-e_{k})_{+}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x.

On
\mathrm{supp}\,\phi_{k}\subset B_{r_{k-1}}
, we have
\phi_{k-1}\equiv 1
so

\displaystyle(f-e_{k})_{+}\leq\,(f-e_{k-1})_{+}\,\phi_{k-1}

and we deduce

\displaystyle\|\phi_{k}\,(f-e_{k})_{+}\|^{2}_{L^{p_{*}}(B_{1})}

\displaystyle\leq C_{\mathrm{Sob}}^{2}\,\Lambda^{2}\,c_{0}^{2}\,2^{2k}\int_{B_{1}\cap\mathrm{supp}\,\phi}(f-e_{k})_{+}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq C_{\mathrm{Sob}}^{2}\,\Lambda^{2}\,c_{0}^{2}\,2^{2k}E_{k-1}.

D. Chebyshev’s inequality.
We apply Hölder’s inequality with
p_{*}
and its conjugate exponent. (To fix ideas, we write things for
d\geq 3
and
p_{*}=2d/(d-2)
, the case of
d=1
or
2
is an easy variation.)

(22)

\forall\,k\geq 1,\quad E_{k}=\int_{B_{1}}(\phi_{k}\,(f-e_{k})_{+})^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\leq\|\phi_{k}(f-e_{k})_{+}\|^{2}_{L^{p_{*}}(B_{1})}\,|\left\{\phi_{k}(f-e_{k})_{+}>0\right\}|^{\frac{2}{n}}.

Equation (
22
) is the key-step of the proof: together with Chebychev’s inequality, it will allow us to exploit the nonlinearity hidden in the relation between
f
and
\nabla_{x}f
(Caccioppoli’s inequality) and Sobolev’s inequality.

We observe that

\left\{\phi_{k}(f-e_{k})_{+}>0\right\}\subset\left\{\phi_{k-1}\,(f-e_{k-1})_{+}\geq 2^{-k-1}\right\}

since
\phi_{k-1}\equiv 1
on the support of
\phi_{k}
, and

f\geq e_{k}=1-2^{-k-1}\ \Longrightarrow\ f-e_{k-1}=f-\left(\frac{1}{2}-2^{-k}\right)\geq 2^{-k}-2^{-k-1}=2^{-k-1}.

Therefore, by Chebyshev’s inequality:

\displaystyle|\left\{\phi_{k}(f-e_{k})_{+}>0\right\}|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\left\{\phi_{k-1}\,(f-e_{k-1})_{+}\geq 2^{-k-1}\right\}\right|

\displaystyle\leq 2^{2(k+1)}\int_{B_{1}}\left(\phi_{k-1}(f-e_{k-1})_{+}\right)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=2^{2(k+1)}\,E_{k-1}.

The last display, combined with (
22
), gives

E_{k}\leq\,C_{\mathrm{Sob}}^{2}\,c_{0}^{2}\,\Lambda^{2}\,2^{2k+2(k+1)\,\frac{2}{d}}\,E_{k-1}^{1+\frac{2}{d}},

which proves (
19
) with
\beta\geq 1+\frac{2}{d}>1
.
∎

Proof of Lemma
6
(Moser’s version).

The proof by Moser
[
70
]
of the first De Giorgi lemma is also based on an iterative scheme. The proofs of De Giorgi and Moser are conceptually close, so that they are often collectively close the “De Giorgi–Moser iteration”. The main idea of Moser is to fully exploit the fact that the energy estimate and the Sobolev embedding both continue to yield the same inequality for weak subsolutions, not just for weak solutions, i.e. for functions
f
so that

(23)

\nabla_{x}\cdot(A\nabla_{x}f)\geq 0.

Moser’s iteration uses the same sequence of radii, but instead of considering energy levels to measure integrability, it considers a
sequence of Lebesgue exponents

p_{k}:=2\left(\frac{p_{*}}{2}\right)^{k}

with

p_{*}:=2+\frac{4}{d-2}\text{ for }d\geq 3\ \text{ or }\ p_{*}\in(2,+\infty)\text{ in }d=1,2.

So we have
p_{0}=2
and
p_{k}\uparrow\infty
. Instead of
E_{k}
, the proof establishes estimates by induction on the following Lebesgue norms:

\forall\,k\geq 0,\quad F_{k}:=\|f_{+}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p_{k}}(B_{r_{k}})}.

Note that
F_{0}=\|f_{+}\|_{L^{2}(B_{1})}
and
F_{\infty}=\|f_{+}\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{1/2})}
.

The key remark is that if
f
is a subsolution (
23
) and
\Phi:{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is
C^{2}
with
\Phi^{\prime},\Phi^{\prime\prime}\geq 0
, then
\Phi(f)
is also a subsolution:

\displaystyle\nabla_{x}\cdot(A\nabla_{x}\Phi(f))=\Phi^{\prime\prime}(f)A\nabla_{x}f\cdot\nabla_{x}f+\Phi^{\prime}(f)\nabla_{x}\cdot(A\nabla_{x}f)\geq 0.

By approximation it implies in particular that
f_{+}
is a subsolution, and then that any
f_{+}^{p_{k}/2}
is also a subsolution, since

\Phi(z):=z^{\frac{p_{k}}{2}}

satisfies the assumptions above for
p_{k}/2>1
. Applying (
20
)—which is valid for subsolutions—to
f_{+}^{p_{k}/2}
, we find

\forall\,k\geq 1,\quad\left\|\nabla_{x}\left(\phi_{k}\,f_{+}^{\frac{p_{k}}{2}}\right)\right\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(B_{1})}\lesssim\Lambda^{2}2^{k}\,F_{k}^{\frac{p_{k}}{2}},

where
(\phi_{k})_{k}
is the same cutoff family as in the proof of De Giorgi. Sobolev’s inequality then yields

\|f_{+}\|^{\frac{p_{k}}{2}}_{\mathrm{L}^{p_{k}\,\left(1+\frac{2}{d-2}\right)}(B_{r_{k+1}})}\lesssim\Lambda^{2}2^{k}\,F_{k}^{\frac{p_{k}}{2}}.

This shows

F_{k+1}\leq\Lambda^{2}C^{\frac{2}{p_{k}}}\,2^{\frac{2}{p_{k}}}\,F_{k},

for an explicit
C=C(n,\Lambda)
. Since the Cauchy product
\prod_{k\geq 0}C^{\frac{2}{p_{k}}}2^{\frac{2}{p_{k}}}
is converging, we deduce
F_{\infty}\lesssim\Lambda^{2}F_{0}
, which concludes the proof. Note that this approach makes it clear that
\delta\sim\Lambda^{-2}
in Lemma
6
.
∎

2.3.
The improved first De Giorgi lemma

In fact, the first De Giorgi lemma can be improved so that the initial integrability assumption can be a positive power as low as wanted. This only requires one more iteration argument.

Lemma 8

(Improved first De Giorgi Lemma)
.

Given
\Lambda>0
and
\zeta_{0}\in(0,2]
, there is
C>0
, depending only on
\Lambda
,
\zeta_{0}
and the dimension
n
, so that the following holds:

Let
f
be a weak subsolution in
H^{1}(B_{R})
to

\nabla_{x}\cdot(A\nabla_{x}f)\geq 0

with
A
symmetric measurable so that

\Lambda^{-1}\leq A(x)\leq\Lambda\ \text{ for almost every }x\in B_{R}.

Then, for any
\zeta\in(\zeta_{0},2]
and
0<r<R
, we have

\|f\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{r})}\leq C(R-r)^{-\frac{n}{\zeta}}\left(\frac{1}{|B_{R}|}\int_{B_{R}}f^{\zeta}\,\mathrm{d}x\right)^{\frac{1}{\zeta}}.

Proof.

By using the scaling of the equation in the first De Giorgi lemma, one can prove the following inequality between two general balls
B_{r}
and
B_{R}
:

\|f\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{r})}\lesssim(R-r)^{-\frac{n}{2}}\left(\frac{1}{|B_{R}|}\int_{B_{R}}f^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Together, the Young inequality and the inequality

\left(\frac{1}{|B_{R}|}\int_{B_{R}}f^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\lesssim\left(\frac{1}{|B_{R}|}\int_{B_{R}}f^{\zeta}\,\mathrm{d}x\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\|f\|^{1-\frac{\zeta}{2}}_{L^{\infty}(Q_{R})},

imply that the function
\mathcal{N}(r):=\|f\|_{L^{\infty}(Q_{r})}
verifies the following inequality for
0<r<r^{\prime}<R
and some
C>0
:

\mathcal{N}(r)\leq\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{N}(r^{\prime})+C(r^{\prime}-r)^{-\frac{n}{\zeta}}\left(\frac{1}{|B_{R}|}\int_{B_{R}}f^{\zeta}\,\mathrm{d}x\right)^{\frac{1}{\zeta}}.

We define the increasing sequence of radii:

r_{k+1}:=r_{k}+\varepsilon\left(1-\alpha^{-1}\right)\alpha^{-k}

for
k\geq 0
, some
\alpha>1
,
\varepsilon:=(R-r)
and
r_{0}:=r
. The sequence converges to
r_{\infty}=R
. Note that the scaling ratio
\alpha>1
between the successive balls is not
2
as in the proof of Lemma
6
; it will be chosen later so as to make the geometric growth slow enough.

Then, the previous display implies

\mathcal{N}(r_{k})\leq\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{N}(r_{k+1})+C\varepsilon^{-\frac{n}{\zeta}}(\alpha-1)^{-\frac{n}{\zeta}}\alpha^{\frac{kn}{\zeta}}\left(\frac{1}{|B_{R}|}\int_{B_{R}}f^{\zeta}\,\mathrm{d}x\right)^{\frac{1}{\zeta}},

so that for any
\ell\geq 1
,

\mathcal{N}(r)\leq\frac{\mathcal{N}(r_{\ell+1})}{2^{\ell}}+C\varepsilon^{-\frac{n}{\zeta}}(\alpha-1)^{-\frac{n}{\zeta}}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}2^{-k}\alpha^{\frac{kn}{\zeta}}\right)\left(\frac{1}{|B_{R}|}\int_{B_{R}}f^{\zeta}\,\mathrm{d}x\right)^{\frac{1}{\zeta}}.

The series in the right-hand side converges for the choice
\alpha:=(3/2)^{\zeta/n}
:

\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}2^{-k}\alpha^{\frac{kn}{\zeta}}\leq\frac{2}{2-\alpha^{n/\zeta}}=4,

and we finally deduce by letting
\ell\to\infty
:

\mathcal{N}(r)\lesssim\varepsilon^{-\frac{n}{\zeta}}(\alpha-1)^{-\frac{n}{\zeta}}\left(\frac{1}{|B_{R}|}\int_{B_{R}}f^{\zeta}\,\mathrm{d}x\right)^{\frac{1}{\zeta}},

from which the conclusion follows (using that
\zeta\geq\zeta_{0}>0
).
∎

Remark 9
.

On can track the dependency in
\zeta
of the constant in this proof: at first order the constant diverges as
\zeta\to 0
like
\zeta\exp[-n(\ln\zeta)/\zeta]
.

2.4.
The second De Giorgi Lemma

This second lemma concerns regularity, not just integrability. Since the coefficients of equation (
18
) have no regularity, it is not possible to access the regularity of
f
by differentiating the equation. The approach of De Giorgi is to study the
pointwise oscillation
of the solution. The oscillation compares locally the supremum of
f
with its infimum. De Giorgi proves that such oscillation decreases geometrically with the scaling when one zooms in on a point.

The overall structure of the proof of the second De Giorgi lemma is as follows:

(24)

\boxed{\begin{array}[]{l}\text{Intermediate value Lemma }\Longrightarrow\text{ Decrease of supremum Lemma }\\[5.69054pt]
\Longrightarrow\text{ Reduction of oscillation Lemma }\Longrightarrow\text{ Local Hölder regularity}.\end{array}}

Lemma 10

(Second De Giorgi Lemma: reduction of oscillation)
.

Given
\Lambda>0
, there is
\nu\in(0,1)
, depending only on
\Lambda
and the dimension
n
, so that the following holds:

Let
f
be a weak solution in
H^{1}(B_{3})
to

\nabla_{x}\cdot(A\nabla_{x}f)=0

where
A
is symmetric measurable and so that

\Lambda^{-1}\leq A(x)\leq\Lambda\ \text{ for almost every }x\in B_{3}.

Then, we have

(25)

\mathrm{osc}_{B_{\frac{1}{2}}}f\leq\nu\,\mathrm{osc}_{B_{2}}f,

where we denote
\mathrm{osc}_{B}f:=\sup_{B}f-\inf_{B}f
.

Note that this lemma requires
f
to be
both
a subsolution and a supersolution, i.e. to be a solution. Intuitively, being a subsolution and taking values far below the supremum imposes decrease of the supremum when zooming in on a point (see Lemma
12
), while being a supersolution and taking values far above the infimum imposes increase of the infimum when zooming in on a point (which is Lemma
12
applied to the opposite of the supersolution). Both are combined to obtain the reduction of oscillation.

Let us show that Lemma
10
implies the Hölder regularity, which thus concludes the proof of Theorem
4
.

Corollary 11
.

Given
\Lambda>0
, there is
\alpha\in(0,1)
, depending only on
\Lambda
and the dimension
n
, so that the following holds:

Let
f
be a weak solution in
H^{1}(\mathcal{U})
with
\mathcal{U}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
open set, to

\nabla_{x}\cdot(A\nabla_{x}f)=0

where
A
is symmetry measurable so that

\Lambda^{-1}\leq A(x)\leq\Lambda\ \text{ for almost every }x\in\mathcal{U}.

Then, we have
f\in C^{\alpha}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathcal{U})
.

Moreover, given any
\delta>0
, there is
C>0
, depending on
\Lambda
,
\delta
and the dimension
n
, so that for any
\tilde{\mathcal{U}}\subset\subset\mathcal{U}
with
\text{dist}(\tilde{\mathcal{U}},\partial\mathcal{U})\geq\delta
, we have

(26)

\|f\|_{C^{\alpha}(\tilde{\mathcal{U}})}\leq C\|f\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{U})}.

Proof.

We already have that
f\in\mathrm{L}^{\infty}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathcal{U})
by Lemma
6
with the estimate

(27)

\forall\,\hat{\mathcal{U}}\subset\subset\mathcal{U},\quad\|f\|_{L^{\infty}(\hat{\mathcal{U}})}\leq C\|f\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{U})}

for a constant
C>0
depending on
\Lambda
,
\text{dist}(\hat{\mathcal{U}},\partial\mathcal{U})
and the dimension
n
.

Let
\mathcal{\tilde{U}}\subset\subset\mathcal{\hat{U}}\subset\subset\mathcal{U}
so that

(28)

d_{0}:=\text{dist}(\mathcal{\tilde{U}},\partial\mathcal{U})\leq\frac{1}{2}\text{dist}(\mathcal{\tilde{U}},\partial\mathcal{\hat{U}}).

Given
x_{0}\in\mathcal{\tilde{U}},
we define the sequence of centred rescaled functions

\displaystyle\forall\,k\geq 1,\ \forall\,y\in B_{1},\quad\tilde{g}_{k}(y):=g_{k-1}\left(\frac{y}{4}\right)

\displaystyle\forall\,y\in B_{1},\quad g_{0}(y):=f\left(x_{0}+\frac{d_{0}y}{4}\right).

Note that each function
g_{k}
is a rescaling of
f
and solves

\nabla_{x}\cdot[A_{k}\nabla_{x}g_{k}]=0,

in
B_{2}
, with the diffusion matrix

A_{k}(y):=A\left(x_{0}+d_{0}2^{-2k}y\right).

This new matrix is still measurable and satisfies

\Lambda^{-1}\leq A_{k}(x)\leq\Lambda\ \text{ for almost every }x\in B_{2}.

Therefore, we can apply Lemma
10
to each
g_{k}
and deduce

\forall\,k\geq 1,\quad\mathrm{osc}_{B_{\frac{1}{2}}}g_{k}\,\leq\,\nu\,\mathrm{osc}_{B_{2}}g_{k}.

Combined with the observation

\forall\,k\geq 1,\quad\mathrm{osc}_{B_{2}}\,g_{k+1}=\mathrm{osc}_{B_{\frac{1}{2}}}g_{k},

we obtain by induction

\mathrm{osc}_{B_{4^{-k}}}g_{1}\leq\nu^{k-1}\mathrm{osc}_{B_{2}}g_{1}\leq 2\,\nu^{k-1}\,\|f\|_{L^{\infty}(\hat{\mathcal{U}})}.

Then, given
x\in B(x_{0},1/4)
we pick up
k\geq 0
so that

|x-x_{0}|\in\left[4^{-k-2},4^{-k-1}\right]

and write

|f(x)-f(x_{0})|\leq 2\,\nu^{k-1}\,\|f\|_{L^{\infty}(\hat{\mathcal{U}})},

so that (using
|x-x_{0}|\geq 4^{-k-2}
)

|f(x)-f(x_{0})|\lesssim\left(4^{\alpha}\nu\right)^{k}\,|x-x_{0}|^{\alpha}\,\|f\|_{L^{\infty}(\hat{\mathcal{U}})}.

We then choose

\alpha:=-\frac{\ln\nu}{2\,\ln 2}

so that
4^{\alpha}\nu=1
, and deduce
f
is
C^{\alpha}
at
x_{0}
. The
\alpha
does
not
depend on
x_{0}
, but only on the constant
\nu
of Lemma
10
.

We have therefore proved

\|f\|_{C^{\alpha}(\tilde{\mathcal{U}})}\leq C\|f\|_{L^{\infty}(\hat{\mathcal{U}})}

with a constant depending only on
\nu
and
d_{0}
. Combined with (
27
) and (
28
), it implies (
26
) and concludes the proof.
∎

We shall show that Lemma
10
is implied by the following lemma.

Lemma 12

(Decrease of supremum—measure-to-pointwise upper bound)
.

Given
\Lambda>0
and
\mu>0
, there is
\lambda\in(0,1)
, depending only on
\Lambda
,
\mu
and the dimension
n
such that the following holds:

Let
f\leq 1
be a weak subsolution in
H^{1}(B_{2})
to

\nabla_{x}\cdot[A\nabla_{x}f]\geq 0,

where
A
is symmetric measurable so that

\Lambda^{-1}\leq A(x)\leq\Lambda\ \text{ for almost every }x\in\mathcal{U}.

We assume that
f
satisfies the following condition in measure:

|B_{1}\cap\{f\leq 0\}|\geq\mu|B_{1}|.

Then, we have

\sup_{B_{\frac{1}{2}}}\,f\leq 1-\lambda.

.

. Note that the infima and suprema are in fact essential infima and suprema. A posteriori however, once the Hölder regularity is established, those would be bounds on a continuous function which is defined everywhere.

Proof of Lemma
10
when assuming Lemma
12
.

Take
f
a solution to (
18
) in
H^{1}(B_{3})
. It is then bounded on
B_{2}
by the first De Giorgi lemma. Assume
\mathrm{osc}_{B_{2}}f>0,
otherwise the statement is trivial. Then, we define

(29)

g(x):=\frac{2}{\mathrm{osc}_{B_{2}}f}\left[f(x)-\frac{\sup_{B_{2}}\,f+\inf_{B_{2}}\,f}{2}\right].

This new unknown
g
is a solution to (
18
), is valued in
[-1,1]
, and satisfies

\mathrm{osc}_{B_{2}}\,g=2.

Then, certainly,

\text{ either }\ |B_{1}\cap\{g\leq 0\}|\geq\frac{1}{2}\,|B_{1}|\quad\text{ or }\quad|B_{1}\cap\{g\geq 0\}|\geq\frac{1}{2}\,|B_{1}|.

In the first case, we apply Lemma
12
to the subsolution
g
, with
\mu:=1/2
:

\mathrm{osc}_{B_{\frac{1}{2}}}g\leq(2-\lambda)\leq\left(1-\frac{\lambda}{2}\right)\,\mathrm{osc}_{B_{2}}g

for some
\lambda\in(0,1)
. Going back to
f
, this means

\mathrm{osc}_{B_{\frac{1}{2}}}f\leq\left(1-\frac{\lambda}{2}\right)\,\mathrm{osc}_{B_{2}}f,

yielding Lemma
10
with
\nu=1-\lambda/2
. In the second case, we perform the same argument on the subsolution
-g
. Note that we are therefore using that both
f
and
-f
are subsolutions, i.e. that
f
is a solution.
∎

To show Lemma
12
, the main idea in De Giorgi’s approach is to establish an
H^{1}
version of the intermediate value lemma. Indeed, if
f
were
nearly everywhere non-positive
in the sense

|B_{1}\cap\{f\leq 0\}|\geq|B_{1}|-\delta^{2},

for some small
\delta>0
, we would have

\|f_{+}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(B_{1})}=\left(\int_{B_{1}}f_{+}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq\left(\int_{B_{1}\cap\{f>0\}}1\,\mathrm{d}x\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq\delta,

where we have used
f\leq 1.
Then, provided
\delta
is small enough to apply Lemma
6
, we would conclude

\sup_{B_{1/2}}f\leq\frac{1}{2}

as required. Our assumption is however merely

|B_{1}\cap\{f\leq 0\}|\geq\mu|B_{1}|.

The following
H^{1}
intermediate value lemma of De Giorgi allows bridging the gap between these two assumptions by a finite induction.

Lemma 14

(De Giorgi intermediate value Lemma)
.

There is
C>0
, depending only on the dimension
n
, such that the following holds:

Let
g
be a measurable function on
B_{1}
so that
g_{+}\in H^{1}(B_{1})
.

Then, we have

(30)

|B_{1}\cap\{0<g<1/2\}|\geq\frac{C}{\|\nabla_{x}g_{+}\|^{2}_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(B_{1})}}\,|B_{1}\cap\{g\geq 1/2\}|^{2}\,|B_{1}\cap\{g\leq 0\}|^{2-\frac{2}{n}}.

.

(1)

The case

(2)

Inequalities of this form would be straightforward to prove if

. The non-constructive proof below shows in fact that the result still holds when replacing the bound on

in

by the weaker fractional Sobolev norm

with

. However, the statement becomes false with the fractional Sobolev norm

for

Nevertheless, importantly, the De Giorgi intermediate value lemma still holds, once properly formulated, for subsolutions to nonlocal elliptic equations with rough coefficients, even for orders of fractional ellipticity

Proof of Lemma
14
.

We present different methods of proof.

A. Non-constructive method by contradiction.
It is possible to give a short proof by contradiction at the cost of constructive estimates. Consider a contradiction sequence
g_{k}
so that

|B_{1}\cap\{0<g_{k}<1/2\}|\xrightarrow{k\to\infty}0,

and

\displaystyle|B_{1}\cap\{g_{k}\geq 1/2\}|\geq a_{1},

\displaystyle|B_{1}\cap\{g_{k}\leq 0\}|\geq a_{2},

for some
a_{1},a_{2}>0
independent of
k
. Let the sequence
\tilde{g}_{k}
be defined by

\tilde{g}_{k}(x)=\begin{cases}0,\quad&\text{for }g_{k}(x)\leq 0,\\[2.84526pt]
g_{k}(x),\quad&\text{for }0<g_{k}(x)<1/2,\\[2.84526pt]
1/2,\quad&\text{for }g_{k}(x)\geq 1/2.\end{cases}

We can assume by compactness that a subsequence of the sequence
\tilde{g}_{k}
is converging to some
g_{\infty}
strongly in
L^{2}(B_{1})
, weakly in
H^{1}(B_{1})
, and almost everywhere. These convergences imply

g_{\infty}\in H^{1}(B_{1})

and

\displaystyle|B_{1}\cap\{0<g_{\infty}<1/2\}|=0,

\displaystyle|B_{1}\cap\{g_{\infty}\geq 1/2\}|\geq a_{1}>0

\displaystyle|B_{1}\cap\{g_{\infty}\leq 0\}|\geq a_{2}>0.

Therefore
2g_{\infty}=\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{B}}
is the indicator function of a Borel set
\mathcal{B}\subset B_{1}
whose measure is positive and strictly less than
|B_{1}|
.

Let us prove that
\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{B}}
is not in
H^{1}(B_{1})
. A function
g\in H^{1}(B_{1})
satisfies, for any
\epsilon\in(0,1)
, the following inequality

\forall\,h\in B_{\epsilon},\quad\int_{B_{1-\epsilon}}|\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{B}}(x+h)-\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{B}}(x)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\lesssim\|g\|_{H^{1}(B_{1})}|h|^{2}.

When we consider
g=\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{B}}
, the integrand
|\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{B}}(x+h)-\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{B}}(x)|^{2}
is valued in
\{0,1\}
, so the exponent
2
can be changed to
1
indifferently. Given
m\in{\mathbb{N}}^{*}
and some fixed
h_{0}\in B_{\epsilon}
, we have then

\displaystyle\int_{B_{1-\epsilon}}|\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{B}}(x+h_{0})-\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{B}}(x)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{B_{1-\epsilon}}|\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{B}}(x+h_{0})-\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{B}}(x)|\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\int_{B_{1-\epsilon}}|\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{B}-jh_{0}/m}(x)-\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{B}-(j+1)h_{0}/m}(x)|\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\int_{B_{1-\epsilon}+jh_{0}/m}|\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{B}}(x)-\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{B}-h_{0}/m}(x)|\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\int_{B_{1-\epsilon}+jh_{0}/m}|\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{B}}(x)-\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{B}-h_{0}/m}(x)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\left|\frac{h_{0}}{m}\right|^{2}\lesssim m^{-1}|h_{0}|,

where in the last line, we have used the
H^{1}
bound on
\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{B}}
. Therefore, by letting
m\to\infty
, we deduce

\int_{B_{1-\epsilon}}|\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{B}}(x+h_{0})-\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{B}}(x)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x=0.

This implies that
\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{B}}
is constant in
B_{1-\epsilon}
, and by letting
\epsilon\to 0
, we deduce that
\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{B}}
is constant in
B_{1}
, which contradicts the fact that the measure of
\mathcal{B}
is non-zero and strictly less than that of
B_{1}
.

Note that this argument can be easily generalised to assuming only
H^{s}(B_{1})
for
s\in(1/2,1)
, rather than
H^{1}(B_{1})
. The fractional Sobolev space
H^{s}(B_{1})
can indeed by defined by requiring that the following seminorm is finite:

\int_{x,y\in B_{1}}\frac{|g(x)-g(y)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y<\infty

for
g\in H^{s}(B_{1})
. When we consider
g=\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{B}}
, again the integrand
|\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{B}}(y)-\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{B}}(x)|^{2}
is valued in
\{0,1\}
, so the exponent
2
can be changed to
1
indifferently. Given
m\in{\mathbb{N}}^{*}
, we have then

\displaystyle\int_{x,y\in B_{1}}\frac{|\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{B}}(y)-\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{B}}(x)|^{2}}{|y-x|^{n+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{x\in B_{1}}\int_{h\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\mathbf{1}_{x+h\in B_{1}}\frac{|\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{B}}(x+h)-\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{B}}(x)|^{2}}{|h|^{n+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}h\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{x\in B_{1}}\int_{h\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\mathbf{1}_{x+h\in B_{1}}\frac{|\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{B}}(x+h)-\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{B}}(x)|}{|h|^{n+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}h\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\int_{x\in B_{1}}\int_{h\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\mathbf{1}_{x+h\in B_{1}}\frac{|\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{B}-jh/m}(x)-\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{B}-(j+1)h/m}(x)|}{|h|^{n+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}h\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\int_{x\in B_{1}+jh/m}\int_{h\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\mathbf{1}_{x+\frac{(m-j)h}{m}\in B_{1}}\frac{|\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{B}}(x)-\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{B}-h/m}(x)|}{|h|^{n+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}h\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq m^{-2s}\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\int_{x\in B_{1}+jh/m}\int_{h\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\mathbf{1}_{x+\frac{(m-j)h}{m}\in B_{1}}\frac{|\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{B}}(x)-\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{B}-h/m}(x)|^{2}}{|h/m|^{n+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}\left(\frac{h}{m}\right)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\lesssim m^{1-2s},

where in the last line, we have used the
H^{s}(B_{1})
bound on
\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{B}}
. Therefore, by letting
m\to\infty
, we deduce

\int_{x,y\in B_{1}}\frac{|\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{B}}(x)-\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{B}}(y)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y=0.

This implies that
\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{B}}
is constant in
B_{1}
, which again contradicts the fact that the measure of
\mathcal{B}
is non-zero and strictly less than that of
B_{1}
.

In the limit case
s=1/2
, it is still possible to prove that
\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{B}}
is not in
H^{1/2}(B_{1})
; the indicator function is however in
H^{s}(B_{1})
for
s\in[0,1/2)
. We refer for instance to
[
29
]
for these results.

B. Constructive method based on trajectories.
This proof was given in
[
90
]
. The idea is to connect the points where
g>1/2
with those where
g<0
via trajectories driven by the vector fields
\partial_{x_{i}}
inducing the diffusion; in this elliptic setting these trajectories are simply straight lines, see Figure
5
.

Let
\tilde{g}
be defined by

\tilde{g}(x)=\begin{cases}0,\quad&\text{for }g(x)\leq 0,\\[2.84526pt]
g(x),\quad&\text{for }0<g(x)<1/2,\\[2.84526pt]
1/2,\quad&\text{for }g(x)\geq 1/2.\end{cases}

Figure 5.
Trajectories in the elliptic case.

This modified unknown satisfies
|\nabla_{x}\tilde{g}|\leq|\nabla_{x}g_{+}|
on
B_{1}
. Given then any

\displaystyle x\in B_{1}\cap\{g\leq 0\},

\displaystyle y\in B_{1}\cap\{g\geq 1/2\},

we Taylor-expand between the two points:

\frac{1}{2}=\tilde{g}(y)-\tilde{g}(x)=\int_{0}^{1}(y-x)\cdot\nabla_{x}\tilde{g}(x+t(y-x))\,\mathrm{d}t

(the manipulations can be a posteriori justified by density of smooth functions). Hence, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{|y-x|}\left|\nabla_{x}\tilde{g}\left(x+s\frac{y-x}{|y-x|}\right)\right|\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}\left|\nabla_{x}\tilde{g}\left(x+s\frac{y-x}{|y-x|}\right)\right|\mathbf{1}_{x+s\frac{y-x}{|y-x|}\in B_{1}}\,\mathrm{d}s.

Integrating
y
over the set
B_{1}\cap\{g\geq 1/2\}
yields

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\,|B_{1}\cap\{g\geq 1/2\}|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{y\in B_{1}\cap\{g\geq 1/2\}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\left|\nabla_{x}\tilde{g}\left(x+s\frac{y-x}{|y-x|}\right)\right|\mathbf{1}_{x+s\frac{y-x}{|y-x|}\in B_{1}}\,\mathrm{d}s\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\leq\int_{B_{1}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\left|\nabla_{x}\tilde{g}\left(x+s\frac{y-x}{|y-x|}\right)\right|\mathbf{1}_{x+s\frac{y-x}{|y-x|}\in B_{1}}\,\mathrm{d}s\,\mathrm{d}y,

which, after a spherical change of variables of
y
around
x
gives

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\,|B_{1}\cap\{g\geq 1/2\}|

\displaystyle\leq\left(\int_{0}^{2}r^{n-1}\,\mathrm{d}r\right)\,\int_{\sigma\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\left|\nabla_{x}\tilde{g}\left(x+s\,\sigma\right)\right|\mathbf{1}_{x+s\sigma\in B_{1}}\,\mathrm{d}s\,\mathrm{d}\sigma

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2^{n}}{n}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\frac{|\nabla_{x}\tilde{g}(z)|}{|x-z|^{n-1}}\mathbf{1}_{z\in B_{1}}\,\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2^{n}}{n}\int_{B_{1}}\frac{|\nabla_{x}\tilde{g}(z)|}{|x-z|^{n-1}}\,\mathrm{d}z,

thanks to another spherical change of variables
(s,\sigma)\mapsto z=x+s\,\sigma
.

Integrating
x
over
B_{1}\cap\{g\leq 0\}
gives

\frac{1}{2}|B_{1}\cap\{g\leq 0\}|\,|B_{1}\cap\{g\geq 1/2\}|\leq\frac{2^{n}}{n}\,\int_{B_{1}\cap\{g\leq 0\}}\int_{B_{1}}\frac{|\nabla_{x}\tilde{g}(z)|}{|x-z|^{n-1}}\,\mathrm{d}z\,\mathrm{d}x.

We now use the standard rearrangement inequality

\displaystyle\int_{B_{1}\cap\{g\leq 0\}}\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{|z-x|^{n-1}}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{B_{r_{0}}}\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{|x|^{n-1}}

\displaystyle\leq r_{0}

\displaystyle\lesssim|B_{1}\cap\{g\leq 0\}|^{\frac{1}{n}},

with
r_{0}>0
is chosen so that

|B_{r_{0}}|=|B_{1}\cap\{g\leq 0\}|.

Altogether, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}|B_{1}\cap\{g\leq 0\}|\,|B_{1}\cap\{g\geq 1/2\}|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2^{n}}{n}\left(\int_{B_{1}}|\nabla_{x}\tilde{g}|\right)|B_{1}\cap\{g\leq 0\}|^{\frac{1}{n}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2^{n}}{n}\left(\int_{B_{1}\cap\{0<g<1/2\}}|\nabla_{x}\tilde{g}|\right)|B_{1}\cap\{g\leq 0\}|^{\frac{1}{n}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2^{n}}{n}\|\nabla_{x}g_{+}\|_{L^{2}(B_{1})}\,|B_{1}\cap\{0<g<1/2\}|^{\frac{1}{2}}\,|B_{1}\cap\{g\leq 0\}|^{\frac{1}{n}}

which concludes the proof with
C=n^{2}2^{-2-2n}
.

C. Less sharp but simpler constructive method based on the Poincaré inequality.
This argument was proposed in
[
39
,
40
]
. It is also—in another respect—close to the method we will deploy in the hypoelliptic setting. We apply the Poincaré inequality in the
L^{1}
setting to
\tilde{g}
in
B_{1}
:

(31)

\int_{B_{1}}\left|\tilde{g}-\langle\tilde{g}\rangle_{B_{1}}\right|\,\mathrm{d}x\lesssim\int_{B_{1}}\left|\nabla_{x}\tilde{g}\right|\,\mathrm{d}x

where
\langle\tilde{g}\rangle_{B_{1}}
is the average of
\tilde{g}
on
B_{1}
.

Then, we observe

\frac{|B_{1}\cap\{g\geq 1/2\}|}{|B_{1}|}\leq\frac{2}{|B_{1}|}\int_{B_{1}\cap\{g\geq 1/2\}}\tilde{g}\,\mathrm{d}x\leq 2\langle\tilde{g}\rangle_{B_{1}}

so that

\displaystyle|B_{1}\cap\{g\leq 0\}||B_{1}\cap\{g\geq 1/2\}|

\displaystyle\leq 2|B_{1}|\int_{B_{1}\cap\{g\leq 0\}}\langle\tilde{g}\rangle_{B_{1}}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq 2|B_{1}|\int_{B_{1}\cap\{g\leq 0\}}\left|\tilde{g}(x)-\langle\tilde{g}\rangle_{B_{1}}\right|\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq 2|B_{1}|\int_{B_{1}}\left|\tilde{g}(x)-\langle\tilde{g}\rangle_{B_{1}}\right|\,\mathrm{d}x.

We have added
g(x)
in the second integrand because it is zero on the domain of integration.

We then plug the Poincaré inequality (
31
) in this last estimate:

|B_{1}\cap\{g\leq 0\}||B_{1}\cap\{g\geq 1/2\}|\lesssim\int_{B_{1}}\left|\nabla_{x}\tilde{g}\right|\,\mathrm{d}x.

In this last integrand, we observe that
\nabla_{x}\tilde{g}=0
almost everywhere when restricted to the sets of points where
\tilde{g}=0
or
\tilde{g}=1/2
. These sets are respectively made up of local minima and maxima of
\tilde{g}
: the gradient would be zero for
g\in C^{1}
by standard differential calculus, and the proof in
H^{1}
is done by approximation, see
[
28
, Section 4.2.2, Theorem 4-(iii), p. 129]
. Therefore

\displaystyle\int_{B_{1}}\left|\nabla_{x}\tilde{g}\right|\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{B_{1}\cap\{0<g<1/2\}}\left|\nabla_{x}\tilde{g}\right|\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\lesssim|B_{1}\cap\{0<g<1/2\}|^{\frac{1}{2}}\left\|\nabla_{x}\tilde{g}\right\|_{L^{2}(B_{1})}

\displaystyle\lesssim|B_{1}\cap\{0<g<1/2\}|^{\frac{1}{2}}\left\|\nabla_{x}g_{+}\right\|_{L^{2}(B_{1})}

by Cauchy-Schwarz’ inequality and the comparison
|\nabla_{x}\tilde{g}|\leq|\nabla_{x}g_{+}|
. Altogether we deduce the inequality

|B_{1}\cap\{0<g<1/2\}|\geq\frac{C}{\|\nabla_{x}g_{+}\|^{2}_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(B_{1})}}\,|B_{1}\cap\{g\geq 1/2\}|^{2}\,|B_{1}\cap\{g\leq 0\}|^{2}

for some constant
C>0
. This alternative argument proves a slightly weaker inequality where the last exponent is
2
instead of
2-2/n
, which results in a worse lower bound for sets with small measure.
∎

Proof of the decrease of supremum lemma
12
from the intermediate value lemma
14
.

Let
f\leq 1
subsolution in
H^{1}(B_{2})
to

\nabla_{x}\cdot(A\nabla_{x}f)\geq 0

with
A
symmetric measurable so that
\Lambda^{-1}\leq A\leq\Lambda
almost everywhere. We assume that
f
satisfies the measure condition

|B_{1}\cap\{f\leq 0\}|\geq\mu|B_{1}|

for some
\mu>0
.

Let us define the following sequence of new unknowns on
B_{2}
:

\forall\,k\geq 0,\quad g_{k}:=2^{k}\left[f-\left(1-2^{-k}\right)\right].

They satisfy the inductive relation

\forall\,k\geq 0,\quad g_{k+1}=2g_{k}-1

and the property
g_{k}\leq 1
for all
k\geq 0
(see Figure
6
for the representation of the successive zooms on values of
f
close to
1
).

Thus,
(g_{k})_{+}
is a subsolution on
B_{2}
and is always valued in
[0,1]
. By relating the
H^{1}(B_{1})
norm of
(g_{k})_{+}
to the
L^{2}(B_{2})
norm of
(g_{k})_{+}
, the Caccioppoli inequality (
20
) (local energy estimate) therefore shows that
(g_{k})_{+}\in H^{1}(B_{1})
with a bound independent of
k\geq 0
.

close to
1
.

We now want to apply the intermediate value lemma to each
(g_{k})_{+}
. Since
f(x)\leq 0
implies
g_{k}(x)\leq 0
for all
k\geq 0
, we have

|B_{1}\cap\{g_{k}\leq 0\}|\geq|B_{1}\cap\{f\leq 0\}|\geq\mu|B_{1}|.

If the inequality

\int_{B_{1}}|(g_{0})_{+}|^{2}=\int_{B_{1}}|f_{+}|^{2}\leq\delta^{2}

holds with
\delta
as in the first De Giorgi lemma
6
, then this lemma
6
indeed applies and implies
\sup_{B_{1/2}}f\leq 1/2
, so we are done. Suppose then, instead, that

\int_{B_{1}}(g_{0})_{+}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x>\delta^{2},

and consider any
k_{0}\geq 1
such that

(32)

\forall\,0\leq k\leq k_{0},\quad\int_{B_{1}}(g_{k})_{+}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x>\delta^{2}.

Then, for
k=0,\cdots,k_{0}-1
, we have that

\displaystyle|B_{1}\cap\{g_{k}\geq 1/2\}|

\displaystyle=|B_{1}\cap\{g_{k+1}\geq 0\}|

\displaystyle\geq\int_{B_{1}}g_{k+1}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x>\delta^{2},

where we have used
g_{k}=1/2+g_{k+1}/2
and
g_{k+1}\leq 1
.

By applying Lemma

to every
(g_{k})_{+}

, we deduce that there is
\alpha>0
, depending on

,

, and the uniform bound on the

\forall\,k=0,\cdots,k_{0}-1,\quad|B_{1}\cap\{g_{k}\in(0,1/2)\}\geq\alpha|B_{1}|.

Then,

\displaystyle|B_{1}\cap\{g_{k_{0}}\leq 0\}|

\displaystyle\geq|B_{1}\cap\{g_{k_{0}-1}\in(0,1/2)\}|+|B_{1}\cap\{g_{k_{0}-1}\leq 0\}|

\displaystyle\geq\alpha|B_{1}|+|B_{1}\cap\{g_{k_{0}-1}\leq 0\}|

\displaystyle\geq\cdots

Therefore
\mu+k_{0}\alpha\leq 1
which shows that

We can therefore define

as the largest integer so that (

which translates into the bound

Remark 16
.

By tracking the behaviour of the constant
\alpha
in terms of
\delta
, and then the behaviour of
\delta
in terms of
\Lambda
, it is possible to obtain the more explicit formula

\lambda\sim Ce^{-C^{\prime}\frac{(1-\mu)}{\mu^{2-2/n}}\Lambda^{4}}

for some constant
C,C^{\prime}>0
depending only on
n
. The constant
\mu
is
1/2
in practice when applying the intermediate value lemma to derive the decrease of maximum, so we deduce

\lambda\sim Ce^{-C^{\prime\prime}\Lambda^{4}}

for some new absolute constant
C^{\prime\prime}>0
. We can also track the Hölder regularity exponent in terms of

, with the help of explicit formulae

and

2.5.
The method of Moser for controlling the oscillations

The approach of Moser
[
70
,
71
]
regarding the second De Giorgi lemma differs from that of De Giorgi in two crucial aspects:

(1)

Moser measures oscillations by
integrals
rather than

pointwise
, specifically through a
Poincaré inequality
.

(2)

Influenced by Nash’s paper
[
80
]
and in particular by the “G bound” in
[
80
]
2
2

2

See also “
The masterpieces of John Forbes Nash Jr
” by De Lellis in
[
47
]
for a recent detailed rewriting of the proof of Nash.
, Moser derives energy estimates on the logarithm of the solution and exploits the collision of scales at that level.

Moser first “pushes” further the first De Giorgi lemma. He denotes

\Phi(p,r):=\left(\frac{1}{|B_{r}|}\int_{B_{r}}f_{+}^{p}\,\mathrm{d}x\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}

which converges to
\sup_{B_{r}}f_{+}
for
p\to+\infty
and to
\min_{B_{r}}f_{+}
for
p\to-\infty
. He then shows that for
f
subsolution then

\forall\,p>0,\quad\Phi(+\infty,1)\lesssim_{p}\Phi(p,2),

and for
f
supersolution then

\forall\,p>0,\quad\Phi(-\infty,1)\gtrsim_{p}\Phi(-p,2).

These two estimates are proved by the localised energy estimate, the Sobolev inequality and the De Giorgi–Moser iteration.

He then invokes Poincaré’s inequality to prove a universal bound on the mean oscillation of the logarithm of the solution (see (
33
) below), and applies a result by John and Nirenberg
[
55
]
about functions with bounded mean oscillations (applied to the logarithm of the solution) to deduce

\Phi(p,2)\lesssim\Phi(-p,2)

for
p>0
small. To satisfy the assumptions of the John-Nirenberg result and prove the latter inequality, Moser must prove that if
f
is a positive supersolution then
F=\ln f
satisfies

(33)

\int_{B_{r}(x_{0})}\left(F-\langle F\rangle_{B_{r}(x_{0})}\right)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\lesssim|B_{r}(x_{0})|

for any ball
B_{r}(x_{0})
included in the domain, where
\langle F\rangle_{B_{r}(x_{0})}
is the average on the ball. Thanks to the Poincaré inequality, the inequality (
33
) is implied by

(34)

\int_{B_{r}(x_{0})}|\nabla_{x}F|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\lesssim r^{-2}|B_{r}(x_{0})|.

This universal bound (
34
) follows from the localised energy estimate on the subequation

\nabla_{x}\cdot(A\nabla_{x}F)+A\nabla_{x}F\cdot\nabla_{x}F\leq 0

satisfied by
F=\ln f
. The second non-negative term
A\nabla_{x}F\cdot\nabla_{x}F
is produced by having taken logarithm of the solution, and is the key to this universal estimate.

The chain of inequalities then implies
\Phi(+\infty,1)\lesssim\Phi(-\infty,1)
which finall proves the following
Harnack inequality
: there is
C_{\mathrm{H}}>0
, depending only on
n
and
\Lambda
, so that for any
f\geq 0
non-negative solution to (
18
) on
B_{2}
, then

\sup_{B_{1}}f\leq C_{\mathrm{H}}\min_{B_{1}}f.

Let us show how this powerful inequality implies the reduction of oscillation (
25
). By scaling the Harnack inequality also holds in
B_{1/2}
. The solution
f
to (
18
) on
B_{2}
is bounded on
B_{1}
by the first De Giorgi lemma. So we can perform a rescaling similar to (
29
) and define the new unknown

g(x):=\frac{2}{\mathrm{osc}_{B_{1}}f}\left[f(x)-\frac{\sup_{B_{1}}\,f+\inf_{B_{1}}\,f}{2}\right]

which solves (
18
) in
B_{2}
.

This
g
satisfies

\min_{B_{1}}g=-1\ \text{ and }\ \max_{B_{1}}g=+1,

and we denote

m_{1}:=\max_{B_{\frac{1}{2}}}g\ \text{ and }\ m_{0}:=\min_{B_{\frac{1}{2}}}g.

We then apply the Harnack inequality in
B_{1/2}
to the following two non-negative solutions to (
18
) in
B_{1}
:
1-g\geq 0
and
g+1\geq 0
. This yields

\displaystyle 1-m_{0}=\max_{B_{1/2}}(1-g)\leq C_{\mathrm{H}}\min_{B_{1/2}}(1-g)=C_{\mathrm{H}}(1-m_{1}),

\displaystyle 1+m_{1}=\max_{B_{1/2}}(g+1)\leq C_{\mathrm{H}}\min_{B_{1/2}}(g+1)=C_{\mathrm{H}}(1+m_{0}).

Summing the two inequalities we get

m_{1}-m_{0}\leq\frac{C_{\mathrm{H}}-1}{C_{\mathrm{H}}+1}2

which is exactly the reduction of oscillation with constant

\nu:=\frac{C_{\mathrm{H}}-1}{C_{\mathrm{H}}+1}\in(0,1).

We note that the Moser approach seems to yield better constants in the Harnack inequality and Hölder regularity in terms of the elliptic constant
\Lambda
: we refer to
[
26
]
for a discussion.

2.6.
The method of Kruzhkov for controlling the oscillations

Kruzhkov’s idea
[
58
]
is to push even further the study of the logarithm of the solution that was crucial in both Nash and Moser’s arguments. He extracts more information from the “collision of scales” on the logarithm of the solution (see below) and simplifies the proof by directly proving the decrease of maximum lemma. (In fact, he proves the equivalent symmetric form: the
increase of the minimum
for supersolutions.)

Let us consider
f\geq 0
a non-negative supersolution in
H^{1}(B_{2})
to

\nabla_{x}\cdot(A\nabla_{x}f)\leq 0

with the usual assumptions on
A
, and so that

(35)

|B_{1}\cap\{f\geq 1\}|\geq\mu|B_{1}|

for some
\mu>0
. The following argument of Kruzhkov shows that there is
\lambda\in(0,1)
so that
f\geq\lambda
on
B_{1/2}
. This proves Lemma
12
on
1-f
.

Let us consider the function

\forall\,z>0,\quad G(z)=(-\ln z+z-1)\mathbf{1}_{z\leq 1}.

It is twice differentiable, convex, and satisfies

\forall\,z\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{*},\quad\begin{cases}\displaystyle G^{\prime}(z)=\frac{z-1}{z}\,\mathbf{1}_{z\leq 1}\leq 0,\\[5.69054pt]
\displaystyle G^{\prime\prime}(z)=\frac{1}{z^{2}}\,\mathbf{1}_{z\leq 1}\geq 0\\[5.69054pt]
\displaystyle G^{\prime\prime}(z)\geq G^{\prime}(z)^{2}.\end{cases}

With the help of this function, we define the new unknown

g:=G\left(f_{\delta}\right)\quad\text{ with the notation }\quad f_{\delta}:=\frac{f+\delta}{1+\delta},

and for
\delta>0
to be chosen later. By translation and dilation,
f_{\delta}
is also a supersolution, and
g
is a subsolution which satisfies

\displaystyle 0\leq A\nabla_{x}g\cdot\nabla_{x}g

\displaystyle=G^{\prime\prime}\left(f_{\delta}\right)A\nabla_{x}f_{\delta}\cdot\nabla_{x}f_{\delta}

\displaystyle\leq G^{\prime\prime}(g)A\nabla_{x}f_{\delta}\cdot\nabla_{x}f_{\delta}+\underbrace{G^{\prime}(g)\nabla_{x}\cdot\left(A\nabla_{x}f_{\delta}\right)}_{\geq 0}=\nabla_{x}\cdot\left(A\nabla_{x}g\right).

Note that the term
G^{\prime}(g)\nabla_{x}\cdot(A\nabla_{x}f_{\delta})
is non-negative because
G^{\prime}\leq 0
and
f
is a supersolution, so
\nabla_{x}\cdot(A\nabla_{x}f_{\delta})\leq 0
.

Integrating this inequation against a smooth localisation function
\phi
so that

\mathbf{1}_{B_{1}}\leq\phi\leq\mathbf{1}_{B_{2}},

we deduce

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\left(A\nabla_{x}g\cdot\nabla_{x}g\right)\phi^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\leq\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\left[\nabla_{x}\cdot\left(A\nabla_{x}g\right)\right]\phi^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x.

By integration by parts, Cauchy-Schwarz’ inequality, and the bounds
\Lambda^{-1}\leq A\leq\Lambda
on the matrix
A
, we deduce from the last estimate:

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\left|\nabla_{x}g\right|^{2}\phi^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\leq\Lambda^{2}\left(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\left|\nabla_{x}g\right|^{2}\phi^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\left|\nabla_{x}\phi\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

We end up with the universal bound

(36)

\|\nabla_{x}g\|^{2}_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(B_{1})}\lesssim\Lambda^{4}\int_{B_{2}}\left|\nabla_{x}\phi\right|^{2}\lesssim\Lambda^{4}.

We then want to apply the Poincaré inequality to
G(f_{\delta})
in
B_{1}
in order to control the
L^{2}
norm. This requires to control the average. This is where we use the assumption (
35
) to control the average by the
L^{2}
norm. Indeed
f\geq 1
is equivalent to
f_{\delta}\geq 1
which is itself equivalent to
g=G(f_{\delta})=0
. Therefore

\langle g\rangle_{B_{1}}=\frac{1}{|B_{1}|}\int_{\{f<1\}\cap B_{1}}g\,\mathrm{d}x\leq\sqrt{1-\mu}\left(\frac{1}{|B_{1}|}\int_{B_{1}}g^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Since by orthogonality we have

\frac{1}{|B_{1}|}\int_{B_{1}}g^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\leq\frac{1}{|B_{1}|}\int_{B_{1}}\left[g-\langle g\rangle_{B_{1}}\right]^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+\langle g\rangle_{B_{1}}^{2},

we deduce from the two last inequalities

\frac{1}{|B_{1}|}\int_{B_{1}}g^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\leq\frac{1}{\mu|B_{1}|}\int_{B_{1}}\left[g-\langle g\rangle_{B_{1}}\right]^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x.

We then apply the Poincaré inequality to
g
on
B_{1}
:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|B_{1}|}\int_{B_{1}}g^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\leq\frac{1}{\mu|B_{1}|}\int_{B_{1}}\left[g-\langle g\rangle_{B_{1}}\right]^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\lesssim\frac{1}{\mu}\|\nabla_{x}g\|^{2}_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(B_{1})}\lesssim\frac{\Lambda^{4}}{\mu}

We finally use the first De Giorgi lemma
6
on the non-negative subsolution
g
:

\|g\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{1/2})}\lesssim\|g\|_{L^{2}(B_{1})}\lesssim\frac{\Lambda^{2}}{\sqrt{\mu}}.

But the supremum of
g
on
B_{1/2}
can be expressed in terms of the infimum of
f
since
G
is decreasing and we obtain

\left|\ln\left(\frac{\inf_{Q_{1/2}}f+\delta}{1+\delta}\right)\right|\lesssim\frac{\Lambda^{2}}{\sqrt{\mu}}.

This proves the lower bound

\inf_{Q_{1/2}}f\geq\lambda\quad\text{ with }\quad\lambda=Ce^{-C^{\prime}\frac{\Lambda^{2}}{\sqrt{\mu}}}>0

for some constant
C,C^{\prime}>0
. This concludes the proof.

3.
The parabolic case

This was the setting first considered by Nash
[
80
]
. However, the approaches of De Giorgi, Moser and Kruzhkov were also extended to this case. The parabolic second De Giorgi lemma was however only proved by non-constructive methods, until the recent works
[
39
,
40
,
42
,
41
]
. We only sketch the proofs when they resemble the elliptic case.

We consider
\mathcal{U}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
open set,
T>0
, and the equation

(37)

\partial_{t}f=\nabla_{x}\cdot\left(A\,\nabla_{x}f\right)\quad\text{in}\,\,(0,T)\times\mathcal{U}

with a matrix-valued function
A=A(t,x)
. We define the Hölder space
C^{\alpha}((0,T)\times\mathcal{U})
as in (
17
) by replacing
\mathcal{U}
by
(0,T)\times\mathcal{U}\subset{\mathbb{R}}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. The parabolic counterpart of the main theorem
4
of the elliptic case is the following statement.

Theorem 18

(Parabolic De Giorgi–Nash–Moser)
.

Given
\Lambda>0
, there is
\alpha\in(0,1)
, depending only on
\Lambda
and the dimension
n
, so that the following holds:

Let
f
be a weak solution to (
37
) on
(0,T)\times\mathcal{U}
with
\mathcal{U}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
open set, in the functional setting

L^{\infty}_{t}((0,T);L^{2}_{x}(\mathcal{U}))\cap L^{2}_{t}(0,T);H^{1}_{x}(\mathcal{U})),

and with
A:[0,T]\times\mathcal{U}\to\mathbb{R}^{2n}
a symmetric measurable matrix-valued function so that

\Lambda^{-1}\leq A(t,x)\leq\Lambda\ \text{ for almost every }t\in[0,T]\text{ and }x\in\mathcal{U}.

Then
f\in C^{\alpha}_{\mathrm{loc}}((0,T)\times\mathcal{U})
.

Moreover, given
\delta>0
and
0<\delta<T_{0}<T_{1}<T-\delta
, there is
C>0
, depending only
\delta
,
\Lambda
and the dimension
n
, so that for any
\tilde{\mathcal{U}}\subset\subset\mathcal{U}
with
\text{{\rm dist}}(\tilde{\mathcal{U}},\partial\mathcal{U})\geq\delta
, we have

\|f\|_{C^{\alpha}((T_{0},T_{1})\times\tilde{\mathcal{U}})}\leq C\|f\|_{L^{2}((0,T)\times\mathcal{U})}.

3.1.
Modification of the scaling and cylinders

The first modification is required by the scaling of the equation. Because one derivative in time is equated with two derivatives in space, the invariant scaling is different on both variables, namely

(t,x)\mapsto(r^{2}t,rx).

The different powers on the two variables
t
and
x
reflect the orders of the partial derivatives in
t
and
x
in the equation.

Combined with the fact that parabolic regularisation is forward in time, the base
parabolic cylinders
that replace the balls are

(38)

Q_{r}:=(-r^{2},0]\times B_{r}.

3.2.
The parabolic first De Giorgi lemma

The statement of the parabolic version of the first De Giorgi lemma remains quite similar to the elliptic case. Note however the additional assumption
\partial_{t}f\in L^{2}_{t}((-1,0);H^{-1}_{x}(B_{1}))
made on the weak subsolutions. This ensures that one can easily performs approximation procedures and a priori estimates. Such regularity on
\partial_{t}f
is always satisfied by weak solutions by combining the equality
\partial_{t}f=\nabla_{x}\cdot(A\nabla_{x}f)
in the sense of distributions with the estimate
A\nabla_{x}f\in L^{2}
provided by the parabolic Caccioppoli inequality (
40
) (the energy estimate) and the pointwise bounds on
A
. It is however not always satisfied by subsolutions due to the presence of a defect measure. Such a subsolution
f
indeed merely satisfies

\partial_{t}f=\nabla_{x}\cdot(A\nabla_{x}f)-m

for a non-negative defect measure
m
. In general, this measure
m
will not belong to
L^{2}_{t}((-1,0);H^{-1}_{x}(B_{1}))
.

Lemma 19

(Parabolic first De Giorgi Lemma)
.

Given
\Lambda>0
, there is
\delta>0
, depending only on
\Lambda
and the dimension
n
, so that the following holds:

Let
f
be a weak subsolution in
Q_{1}
to

(39)

\partial_{t}f\leq\nabla_{x}\cdot\left(A\nabla_{x}f\right)

in the functional setting

\displaystyle f\in L^{\infty}_{t}((-1,0);L^{2}_{x}(B_{1}))\cap L^{2}_{t}((-1,0);H^{1}_{x}(B_{1}))\ \text{ and }

\displaystyle\partial_{t}f\in L^{2}_{t}((-1,0);H^{-1}_{x}(B_{1})),

with
A
symmetric measurable so that

\Lambda^{-1}\leq A(t,x)\leq\Lambda\ \text{ for almost every }(t,x)\in Q_{1}.

Then, we have

\left\|f_{+}\right\|_{L^{2}(Q_{1})}\leq\delta\quad\implies\quad\left\|f_{+}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(Q_{1/2})}\leq\frac{1}{2}.

This estimate is translation and scaling-invariant, provided one uses the appropriate parabolic scaling
(t,x)\mapsto(r^{2}t,rx)
.

The setting of the parabolic first De Giorgi lemma is represented in Figure
7
.

Proof of Lemma
19
.

The De Giorgi–Moser iterative scheme can be easily adapted. For instance, in the De Giorgi viewpoint, the sequence of cylinders and energy levels is

\begin{cases}&T_{k}:=-\frac{1}{4}-\frac{1}{2^{k+1}},\quad T_{0}=-1,\quad T_{k}\uparrow-\frac{1}{4},\\[5.69054pt]
&r_{k}=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2^{k+1}},\quad r_{0}=1,\quad r_{k}\downarrow\frac{1}{2},\\[5.69054pt]
&e_{k}=\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{2^{k+1}},\quad e_{0}=0,\quad e_{k}\uparrow\frac{1}{2},\\[5.69054pt]
&\tilde{Q}_{k}:=(T_{k},0)\times B_{r_{k}},\\[5.69054pt]
&\displaystyle E_{k}:=\int_{\tilde{Q}_{k}}(f-e_{k})_{+}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}x.\end{cases}

Note that the cylinders
\tilde{Q}_{k}
are not exactly of the form (
38
); instead they are constructed so as to converge to
Q_{1/2}
.

One establishes again a nonlinear inequality between successive
E_{k}
terms, and the proof is based on a localised energy estimate, Sobolev’s embedding and Chebyshev’s inequality, as in the elliptic case. Only the localised energy estimate and the gain of integrability
L^{2}\to L^{p>2}
in all variables differ from the elliptic proof. We focus only on these two substeps.

A. Localised energy estimate.
We write the energy estimate in a way that is suitable for both the De Giorgi and Moser approaches to the iteration. Consider an energy level
e\geq 0
; it is used in De Giorgi’s iteration, but fixed to zero in Moser’s iteration). Consider a smooth localisation function
\phi
with support in
(-1,0]\times B_{1}
: note that
\phi
is zero at initial time but not necessarily at final time. We integrate (
39
) against
(f-e)_{+}\phi^{2}
on
(-1,\tau]\times B_{1}
for
\tau\in(-1,0]
:

\displaystyle\int_{(-1,\tau]\times B_{1}}\left(\partial_{t}(f-e)_{+}\right)(f-e)_{+}\phi^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=-\int_{(-1,\tau]\times B_{1}}\left(A\nabla_{x}(f-e)_{+}\cdot\nabla_{x}(f-e)_{+}\right)\phi^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\,\quad-2\int_{(-1,\tau]\times B_{1}}\left(A\nabla_{x}(f-e)_{+}\cdot\nabla_{x}\phi\right)\phi(f-e)_{+}\,\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}x.

The left hand side writes

\displaystyle\int_{(-1,\tau]\times B_{1}}\left(\partial_{t}(f-e)_{+}\right)(f-e)_{+}\phi^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{(-1,\tau]\times B_{1}}\partial_{t}(f-e)^{2}_{+}\phi^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\int_{B_{1}}(f-e)^{2}_{+}\phi^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\right)\Bigg|_{t=\tau}-\frac{1}{2}\int_{(-1,\tau]\times B_{1}}(f-e)^{2}_{+}\partial_{t}\left(\phi^{2}\right)\,\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}x,

and we bound the last term as

\left|\frac{1}{2}\int_{(-1,\tau]\times B_{1}}(f-e)^{2}_{+}\partial_{t}\left(\phi^{2}\right)\,\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}x\right|\lesssim\|\partial_{t}\phi\|_{\infty}\|\phi\|_{\infty}\int_{Q_{1}\cap\,\mathrm{supp}\,\phi}(f-e)^{2}_{+}\,\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}x.

Therefore we deduce

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(\int_{B_{1}}(f-e)^{2}_{+}\phi^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\right)\Bigg|_{t=\tau}+\int_{(-1,\tau]\times B_{1}}\left|\nabla_{x}(f-e)_{+}\right|^{2}\phi^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\lesssim\Lambda\|\partial_{t}\phi\|_{\infty}\|\phi\|_{\infty}\int_{Q_{1}\cap\,\mathrm{supp}\,\phi}(f-e)^{2}_{+}\,\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt+\Lambda\int_{(-1,\tau]\times B_{1}}\left|A\nabla_{x}(f-e)_{+}\cdot\nabla_{x}\phi\right|\phi(f-e)_{+}\,\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\lesssim\Lambda\|\partial_{t}\phi\|_{\infty}\|\phi\|_{\infty}\int_{Q_{1}\cap\,\mathrm{supp}\,\phi}(f-e)^{2}_{+}\,\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt+\Lambda^{2}\left(\int_{(-1,\tau]\times B_{1}}\left|\nabla_{x}(f-e)_{+}\right|^{2}\phi\,\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}x\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\int_{(-1,\tau]\times B_{1}}\left|\nabla_{x}\phi\right|^{2}(f-e)_{+}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}x\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

By splitting the last term into two squares, we get

\left(\int_{B_{1}}(f-e)^{2}_{+}\phi^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\right)\Bigg|_{t=\tau}+\int_{(-1,\tau]\times B_{1}}\left|\nabla_{x}(f-e)_{+}\right|^{2}\phi^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}x\\
\lesssim\Lambda^{4}\left(1+\|\nabla_{x}\phi\|_{\infty}+\|\partial_{t}\phi\|_{\infty}\right)\,\int_{Q_{1}\cap\,\mathrm{supp}\,\phi}(f-e)^{2}_{+}\,\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}x.

By varying the final time
\tau\in(-1,0]
, we finally obtain the following
parabolic Caccioppoli inequality
, which is the parabolic counterpart to (
20
):

(40)

\frac{1}{2}\sup_{-1<\tau\leq 0}\left(\int_{B_{1}}(f-e)^{2}_{+}\phi^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\right)\Bigg|_{t=\tau}+\int_{Q_{1}}\left|\nabla_{x}(f-e)_{+}\right|^{2}\phi^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}x\\
\lesssim\Lambda^{4}\left(1+\|\nabla_{x}\phi\|_{\infty}+\|\partial_{t}\phi\|_{\infty}\right)\,\int_{Q_{1}\cap\,\mathrm{supp}\,\phi}(f-e)^{2}_{+}\,\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}x.

Given
\phi_{k}
a sequence of smooth localisation functions such that

\mathbf{1}_{\tilde{Q}_{k+1}}\leq\phi_{k}\leq\mathbf{1}_{\tilde{Q}_{k}}\quad\text{ and }\quad|\nabla_{t,x}\varphi_{k}|\lesssim 2^{k},

the sequence of localised energy estimate is therefore

\sup_{T_{k+1}\leq\tau\leq 0}\left(\int_{B_{1}}\phi^{2}_{k+1}(f-e_{k+1})_{+}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\right)\Bigg|_{t=\tau}+\int_{Q_{1}}|\nabla_{x}(\phi_{k}(f-e_{k+1})_{+})|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}x\lesssim\Lambda^{4}2^{k}E_{k}.

B. Parabolic Sobolev embedding.

The second term of the left-hand side of the last equation yields some Sobolev regularity in
x
, which leads to a gain of integrability in this variable by the standard Sobolev embedding in
x
with
t
fixed. The gain of integrability in
t
is ensured by the first term on the left-hand side. By combining the two estimates on the
L^{2}_{t}L^{p>2}_{x}
norm and the
L^{\infty}_{t}L^{2}_{x}
norm, one controls the solution in
L^{p_{*}}_{t,x}
with
p_{*}>2
.
∎

3.3.
The improved parabolic first De Giorgi lemma

Similarly to the elliptic case, we can improve the first De Giorgi lemma as follows.

Lemma 20

(Improved parabolic first De Giorgi Lemma)
.

Given
\Lambda>0
and
\zeta_{0}\in(0,2]
, there is
C>0
, depending only on
\Lambda
,
\zeta_{0}
and the dimension
n
, so that the following holds:

Let
f
be a weak subsolution in the parabolic cylinder
Q_{R}
to

\partial_{t}f\leq\nabla_{x}\cdot\left(A\nabla_{x}f\right)

in the functional setting

\displaystyle f\in L^{\infty}_{t}((-R^{2},0);L^{2}_{x}(B_{R}))\cap L^{2}_{t}((-R^{2},0);H^{1}_{x}(B_{R}))\ \text{ and }

\displaystyle\partial_{t}f\in L^{2}_{t}((-R^{2},0);H^{-1}_{x}(B_{R})),

with
A
symmetric measurable so that

\Lambda^{-1}\leq A(t,x)\leq\Lambda\ \text{ for almost every }(t,x)\in Q_{R}.

Then for any
\zeta\in(\zeta_{0},2]
and
0<r<R
, we have

\|f\|_{L^{\infty}(Q_{r})}\leq C(R-r)^{-\frac{2+n}{\zeta}}\left(\frac{1}{|Q_{R}|}\int_{Q_{R}}f^{\zeta}\,\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}x\right)^{\frac{1}{\zeta}}.

Proof.

By using the scaling of the equation in the parabolic first De Giorgi lemma, one can prove the following inequality between two general cylinders
Q_{r}
and
Q_{R}
:

\|f\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{r})}\lesssim(R-r)^{-1-\frac{n}{2}}\left(\frac{1}{|Q_{R}|}\int_{Q_{R}}f^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}x\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Together, the Young inequality and the inequality

\left(\frac{1}{|Q_{R}|}\int_{Q_{R}}f^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}x\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\lesssim\left(\frac{1}{|Q_{R}|}\int_{Q_{R}}f^{\zeta}\,\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}x\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\|f\|^{1-\frac{\zeta}{2}}_{L^{\infty}(Q_{R})}

imply that the function
\mathcal{N}(r):=\|f\|_{L^{\infty}(Q_{r})}
verifies the following inequality for some
C>0
and any
0<r<r^{\prime}<R
:

\mathcal{N}(r)\leq\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{N}(r^{\prime})+C(r^{\prime}-r)^{-\frac{2+n}{\zeta}}\left(\frac{1}{|Q_{R}|}\int_{Q_{R}}f^{\zeta}\,\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}x\right)^{\frac{1}{\zeta}}.

By the same iteration along the sequence of radii

r_{k+1}=r_{k}+\varepsilon\left(1-\alpha^{-1}\right)\alpha^{-k}

for
k\geq 1
, some
\alpha>1
,
\varepsilon:=R-r
and
r_{0}:=r
, we deduce as in the elliptic case that

\mathcal{N}(r)\lesssim_{\zeta_{0}}\varepsilon^{-\frac{2+n}{\zeta}}\left(\frac{1}{|Q_{R}|}\int_{Q_{R}}f^{\zeta}\,\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}x\right)^{\frac{1}{\zeta}},

from which the conclusion follows.
∎

3.4.
The parabolic second De Giorgi lemma

The parabolic second De Giorgi lemma (reduction of oscillation) resembles the elliptic case, and it implies the Theorem
18
by an argument also similar to the elliptic case, but accounting for the slightly different geometry of the parabolic cylinders.

More generally, the overall structure of the proof of the parabolic second De Giorgi lemma is similar to the elliptic case (
24
), with parabolic versions of each step.

Lemma 21

(Parabolic second De Giorgi Lemma: reduction of oscillation)
.

Given
\Lambda>0
, there is
\nu\in(0,1)
, depending only on
\Lambda
and the dimension
n
, so that the following holds:

Let
f
be a weak solution in
Q_{3}
to

(41)

\partial_{t}f=\nabla_{x}\cdot(A\nabla_{x}f)

in the functional setting
L^{2}((-9,0];H^{1}(B_{3}))
, where
A
is symmetric measurable and so that

\Lambda^{-1}\leq A(t,x)\leq\Lambda\ \text{ for almost every }(t,x)\in Q_{3}.

Then, we have

(42)

\mathrm{osc}_{Q_{\frac{1}{2}}}f\leq\nu\,\mathrm{osc}_{Q_{2}}f,

where we denote
\mathrm{osc}_{B}f:=\sup_{B}f-\inf_{B}f
.

Note again that this lemma requires
f
to be
both
a subsolution and a supersolution, i.e. to be a solution. Let us show that Lemma
21
implies the Hölder regularity, which thus concludes the proof of Theorem
18
.

Corollary 22
.

Given
\Lambda>0
, there is
\alpha\in(0,1)
, depending only on
\Lambda
and the dimension
n
, so that the following holds:

Let
f
be a weak solution in
L^{2}((0,T);H^{1}(\mathcal{U}))
, with
\mathcal{U}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
an open set, to

\partial_{t}f=\nabla_{x}\cdot\left(A\nabla_{x}f\right),

where
A
is symmetric measurable and so that

\Lambda^{-1}\leq A(t,x)\leq\Lambda\ \text{ for almost every }(t,x)\in(0,T)\times\mathcal{U}.

Then, we have
f\in C^{\alpha}_{\mathrm{loc}}((0,T)\times\mathcal{U})
.

Moreover, given any
\delta\in(0,T/2)
, there is
C>0
, depending on
\Lambda
,
\delta
and the dimension
n
, so that for any
\tilde{\mathcal{U}}\subset\subset\mathcal{U}
with
\text{{\rm dist}}(\tilde{\mathcal{U}},\partial\mathcal{U})\geq\delta
and
\delta<T_{0}<T_{1}<T-\delta
, we have

(43)

\|f\|_{C^{\alpha}((T_{0},T_{1})\times\tilde{\mathcal{U}})}\leq C\|f\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{U})}.

Proof.

We already have that
f\in\mathrm{L}^{\infty}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathcal{U})
by Lemma
19
with the estimate

(44)

\forall\,\hat{\mathcal{U}}\subset\subset\mathcal{U},\quad\|f\|_{L^{\infty}((\delta/2,T-\delta/2)\times\hat{\mathcal{U}})}\leq C\|f\|_{L^{2}((0,T)\times\mathcal{U})}

for a constant
C>0
depending on
\Lambda
,
\text{dist}(\hat{\mathcal{U}},\partial\mathcal{U})
,
\delta
and the dimension
n
.

Let
\mathcal{\tilde{U}}\subset\subset\mathcal{\hat{U}}\subset\subset\mathcal{U}
so that

(45)

\text{dist}(\mathcal{\tilde{U}},\partial\mathcal{U})\leq\frac{1}{2}\text{dist}(\mathcal{\tilde{U}},\partial\mathcal{\hat{U}}),\quad\text{dist}(\mathcal{\tilde{U}},\partial\mathcal{U})\in\left[\frac{\delta}{4},\frac{\delta}{2}\right],\quad\text{dist}(\mathcal{\tilde{U}},\partial\mathcal{\hat{U}})\in\left[\frac{\delta}{2},\delta\right].

Given
(t_{0},x_{0})\in(T_{0},T_{1})\times\mathcal{\tilde{U}}
, we define the following sequence of centred rescaled functions

\displaystyle\forall\,k\geq 1,\ \forall\,(s,y)\in Q_{1},\quad\tilde{g}_{k}(s,y):=g_{k-1}\left(\frac{s}{16},\frac{y}{4}\right)

\displaystyle\forall\,(s,y)\in Q_{1},\quad g_{0}(s,y):=f\left(t_{0}+\frac{\delta}{32}+\frac{\delta s}{16},x_{0}+\frac{\delta y}{4}\right).

Note that each function
g_{k}
is a rescaling of
f
and solves

\partial_{t}g_{k}=\nabla_{x}\cdot[A_{k}\nabla_{x}g_{k}]

in
Q_{2}
, with the diffusion matrix

A_{k}(s,y):=A\left(t_{0}+\frac{\delta}{32}+\frac{\delta s}{2^{4k}},x_{0}+\frac{\delta y}{2^{2k}}\right).

This new diffusion matrix is still measurable and satisfies

\Lambda^{-1}\leq A_{k}(s,y)\leq\Lambda\ \text{ for almost every }(s,y)\in Q_{2}.

Therefore, we can apply Lemma
10
to each
g_{k}
and deduce

\forall\,k\geq 1,\quad\mathrm{osc}_{Q_{\frac{1}{2}}}g_{k}\,\leq\,\nu\,\mathrm{osc}_{Q_{2}}g_{k}.

The scaling relation between successive terms in the sequence
g_{k}
yields

\forall\,k\geq 1,\quad\mathrm{osc}_{Q_{2}}\,g_{k+1}=\mathrm{osc}_{Q_{\frac{1}{2}}}g_{k},

so we obtain by induction

\mathrm{osc}_{Q_{4^{-k}}}g_{1}\leq\nu^{k-1}\mathrm{osc}_{Q_{2}}g_{1}\leq 2\,\nu^{k-1}\,\|f\|_{L^{\infty}((T_{0}/2)\times\hat{\mathcal{U}})}.

Then, given a point

(t,x)\in\left(t_{0}-\frac{\delta}{16},t_{0}+\frac{\delta}{16}\right)\times B\left(x_{0},\frac{\delta}{4}\right),

we pick up

Proof of Theorem
26
(the parabolic Poincaré inequality).

The full, and more powerful, argument will be presented in the next section in the kinetic case. We only stress the modifications and simplifications in the parabolic case with respect to the kinetic case. The setting with the three cylinders

A. Construction of the trajectories.

We ues the letter “
z

z=(t,x)
.

Given three points

z_{+}\in Q^{+},z_{0}\in Q^{0}
and
z_{-}\in Q^{-}
, we want to
construct two paths
s\rightarrow\Gamma_{+}(s)
and

s\rightarrow\Gamma_{-}(s)
for

such that

\displaystyle\Gamma_{+}(s)=\left(T_{+}(s),X_{+}(s)\right),\qquad\Gamma_{+}(0)=z_{+},\qquad\Gamma_{+}(1)=z_{0},

\displaystyle\Gamma_{-}(s)=\left(T_{-}(s),X_{-}(s)\right),\qquad\Gamma_{-}(0)=z_{-},\qquad\Gamma_{-}(1)=z_{0},

where

(50)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\displaystyle{\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}s}}X_{\pm}(s)=\mathsf{m}_{\pm}s^{-\frac{1}{2}}\\[8.53581pt]
\displaystyle{\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}s}}T_{\pm}(s)=\delta_{\pm},\end{cases}

where

and and

. Solving the differential equations yields

(51)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}X_{\pm}(s)=x_{\pm}+2\delta_{\pm}s^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathsf{m}_{\pm},\\[5.69054pt]
T_{\pm}(s)=st_{0}+(1-s)t_{\pm}.\end{cases}

The boundary conditions
\Gamma_{\pm}(1)=z_{0}
impose
\mathsf{m}_{\pm}=(2\delta_{\pm})^{-1}(x_{0}-x_{\pm})
. We deduce
X_{\pm}(s)=\Phi_{\pm}^{s}(x_{0})
with

\displaystyle\nabla_{x_{0}}\left(\Phi_{\pm}^{s}\right)^{-1}\sim O\left(s^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right)

which remains integrable. Observe also that our choice of control function is such that its derivative

X_{\pm}^{\prime}(s)\sim O\left(s^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right)

is integrable on

, which implies that the trajectories
are bounded (with integrable tangent vector field).

B. Proof of the parabolic Poincaré inequality by following the trajectories.

We refer again to the next section for the details. Thanks to the estimates in the last two displays, we can argue exactly as in the proof detailed in the kinetic case, to obtain

\int_{Q^{+}}\Big(f(z_{+})-\langle f\rangle_{Q^{-}}\Big)_{+}\,\mathrm{d}z_{+}\lesssim\int_{Q^{+}}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{Q^{0}}\left|\left(\nabla_{x}f\right)(\Gamma_{+}(s))\right|s^{-\frac{1}{2}}\,\mathrm{d}s\,\mathrm{d}z_{0}\,\mathrm{d}z_{+}\\
+\int_{Q^{-}}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{Q^{0}}\left|\left(\nabla_{x}f\right)(\Gamma_{-}(s))\right|s^{-\frac{1}{2}}\,\mathrm{d}s\,\mathrm{d}z_{0}\,\mathrm{d}z_{-},

and the end of the proof is similar: we are now integrating in both
Q^{0}
and
Q_{\pm}
, so we are now in a position to use a change of variable not only with the intermediate variable
z_{0}
, but also with the future/past variables
z_{\pm}
. Note that it was not possible to use the integration
in
Q_{\pm}
before because of the positive value around the
Q^{0}

integral. Using these changes of variables, we deduce

\int_{Q^{\pm}}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{Q^{0}}\left|\left(\nabla_{x}f\right)(\Gamma_{\pm}(s))\right|s^{-\frac{1}{2}}\,\mathrm{d}s\,\mathrm{d}z_{0}\,\mathrm{d}z_{\pm}\lesssim\int_{Q_{1}}\left|\nabla_{x}f\right|\,\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}x,

which concludes the proof of the parabolic Poincaré inequality (
49
).

3.5.
Parabolic versions of the Moser and Kruzhkov methods

The Moser approach was extended to the parabolic case in
[
73
,
76
,
77
]
. The first paper in this series had a mistake (which was corrected a few years later), which illustrates that the John-Nirenberg result is not a convenient tool in the parabolic case. Instead, in the later paper
[
77
]
, Moser improved and simplified the argument: in order to “bridge the gap” between negative and positive exponents and prove

\Phi(p,2)\lesssim\Phi(-p,2)

for
p>0
small, Moser combines a universal bound on the
L^{2}
norm of the gradient of the logarithm of the solution with a lemma by Bombieri and Giusti
[
16
]
. This method provides a sharper dependency of the constant in the Harnack inequality in terms of the ellipticity constant
\Lambda
. We refer to the recent contribution
[
82
]
for a re-interpretation of this Moser-Bombieri-Giusti approach based on trajectories in the parabolic case, and a discussion of the dependency of the Harnack constant in terms of
\Lambda
. We also refer to
[
9
,
89
]
for the extension of Moser’s method to some quasilinear parabolic equations.

The Kruzhkov approach was extended to the parabolic case in
[
59
]
. The argument is quite close to the elliptic case. The main difference is that the right-hand side of (
36
), in the parabolic case, also includes the
L^{\infty}
norm of
g
, albeit with a power one:

\|\nabla_{x}g\|^{2}_{L^{2}(Q_{1})}\lesssim\Lambda^{4}\left(\int_{Q_{2}}\left|\nabla_{x}\phi\right|^{2}+\int_{Q_{2}}g^{2}\left|\partial_{t}\phi\right|^{2}\right)\lesssim\Lambda^{4}\left(1+\|g\|_{L^{\infty}(Q_{1})}\right).

This is due to the error terms produced by the time derivative operator.

The different powers between the left-hand and right-hand sides still allows the argument to work. One gets finally

\|g\|_{L^{\infty}(Q_{1/2})}^{2}\lesssim_{\Lambda}1+\|g\|_{L^{\infty}(Q_{1})}

and we then express the norm on the left-hand side in terms of the infimum on the solution
f
on
Q_{1/2}
, and we bound the norm on the right-hand side by the trivial upper bound on
G(f_{\delta})
.

This leads to the inequality

\left|\ln\left(\frac{\inf_{Q_{1/2}}f+\delta}{1+\delta}\right)\right|^{2}\lesssim_{\Lambda}1+\left|\ln\left(\frac{\delta}{1+\delta}\right)\right|

which implies a strictly positive lower bound on
\inf_{Q_{1/2}}f
.

4.
The kinetic case

We focus in this section on the simplest class of local hypoelliptic equations of type II (in the terminology of Hörmander): the
Kolmogorov equation
. The underlying motivation is the study of the Landau equation (
14
)-(
16

\partial_{t}f+v\cdot\nabla_{x}f=\nabla_{v}\cdot\left(A[f]\,\nabla_{v}f\right)+B[f]\cdot\nabla_{v}f+\left(\nabla_{v}\cdot B[f]\right)f

for an unknown

and

are linear integral operators on the

Following the same approach as De Giorgi and Nash in their solution to Hilbert’s
19

(52)

\partial_{t}f+v\cdot\nabla_{x}f=\nabla_{v}\cdot[A\nabla_{v}f]+B\cdot\nabla_{v}f+S,

on
[0,T]

a measurable symmetric matrix-valued function,

(53)

\left\{\begin{matrix}&\Lambda^{-1}\leq A(t,x,v)\leq\Lambda\\[2.84526pt]
&|B(t,x,v)|\leq\Lambda\\[2.84526pt]
&|S(t,x,v)|\leq C_{S}\end{matrix}\right\}\quad\text{ for almost every }t\in[0,T]\text{ and }(x,v)\in\mathcal{U},

for some
\Lambda>0
and

.

The main result reviewed this section is the local Hölder regularity of the weak solutions to (
52
), under the assumptions (
53
). This, in turn, implies the Hölder regularity of weak a priori solutions to the Landau equation (
14
), provided we assume these solutions to be bounded in
L^{\infty}
in all variables and to satisfy uniform pointwise bounds on their hydrodynamic fields. The details of this corollary are included in
[
34
]
. This is one of the steps in the solution the second open problem of the introduction (conditional regularity for the Landau equation). Because of the structure of the Landau equation, this corollary requires the presence of the lower order terms
B\cdot\nabla_{v}f
and
S
in (
52
).

In these notes, we however focus only on the simplest form of (

B=0
and
S=0

[
34
]
, and we also refer to the recent review
[
32
]
about the work
[
34
]
. We however emphasize that the
L^{\infty}
bound on the a priori solutions to the Landau is made because of the assumptions we require on the source term

in the main Hölder regularity result.

\|f\|_{C^{\alpha}((T_{0},T_{1})\times\tilde{\mathcal{U}})}\leq C\|f\|_{L^{2}((0,T)\times\mathcal{U})}.

4.1.
Modification of the scaling and cylinders

In the sequel, we write
z:=(t,x,v)\in\mathbb{R}^{1+2d}
for the full set of variables, and
\mathcal{T}:=\partial_{t}+v\cdot\nabla_{x}
for the transport operator.

The invariant transformations of the class of equations (
54
) that satisfy (
55
) are slightly more intricate. The scaling now must take into account the balance between the three differential operators in the equation, which yields the
kinetic scaling

(56)

(t,x,v)\mapsto(r^{2}t,r^{3}x,rv)\ \text{ for }\ r>0.

Besides we still have invariance by translation in time and in space, and we have an additional invariance involving the velocity variable, albeit modified by the inertia principle. This results in the
invariance by Galilean translations

(57)

(t,x,v)\mapsto(t_{0}+t,x_{0}+x+tv_{0},v_{0}+v)\ \text{ for }\ (t_{0},x_{0},v_{0})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{1+2d}.

The scaling invariance naturally leads to defining the following base
kinetic cylinders
for
r>0

(58)

Q_{r}:=rQ_{1}:=(-r^{2},0]\times B_{r^{3}}\times B_{r},

where the left multiplication
rQ_{1}
corresponds to the scaling law. The invariance by Galilean transformation naturally leads to the following more general translated kinetic cylinders

\displaystyle Q_{r}(z_{0})

\displaystyle=z_{0}\circ[rQ_{1}]

\displaystyle=\left\{(t,x,r)\,:\,-r^{2}<t-t_{0}\leq 0,\ |x-x_{0}-(t-t_{0})v_{0}|<r^{3},\ |v-v_{0}|<r\right\},

where the
\circ
left operation corresponds to the (non-commutative) Galilean translation.

4.2.
The kinetic first De Giorgi Lemma

The main difficulty in adapting the first De Giorgi lemma, by comparison with the parabolic case, lies in the degeneracy of (
54
), which lacks ellipticity in the
x
variable. We sketch two arguments for the core part of the proof, the gain of integrability from
L^{2}
to
L^{p_{*}}
with some
p_{*}>2
in all variables. We first present the approach of
[
34
]
, based on the so-called “averaging lemma”, and, second, the strategy introduced in
[
84
]
and used in
[
93
,
42
]
. The second strategy leads to sharper results. Its drawback however is that it relies on the fundamental solution of the Kolmogorov equation. So it might not be as robust as the first strategy for future applications.

Overall, the statement remains quite similar to the parabolic case. Note the additional assumption
\mathcal{T}f\in L^{2}_{t}((-1,0);L^{2}_{x}H^{-1}_{v}(B_{1}\times B_{1}))
made on the weak subsolutions. Just like the assumption made on
\partial_{t}f
in the parabolic case, it ensures that we can easily perform approximation procedures and a priori estimates. Such regularity on
\mathcal{T}f
is always satisfied by weak solutions by combining the equality
\mathcal{T}f=\nabla_{v}\cdot(A\nabla_{v}f)
in the sense of distributions with the estimate
A\nabla_{v}f\in L^{2}
provided by the kinetic Caccioppoli inequality (
60
) (the energy estimate) and the pointwise bounds on
A
. It is however not always satisfied by subsolutions due to the presence of a defect measure, as in the parabolic case.

Lemma 29

(Kinetic first De Giorgi Lemma)
.

Given
\Lambda>0
, there is
\delta>0
, depending only on
\Lambda
and the dimension
d
, so that the following holds:

Let
f
be a weak subsolution in
Q_{1}
to

(59)

\partial_{t}f+v\cdot\nabla_{x}f\leq\nabla_{v}\cdot\left(A\nabla_{v}f\right)

in the functional setting

\displaystyle f\in L^{\infty}_{t}((-1,0);L^{2}_{x,v}(B_{1}\times B_{1}))\cap L^{2}_{t}((-1,0);L^{2}_{x}H^{1}_{v}(B_{1}\times B_{1}))

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}f\in L^{2}_{t}((-1,0);L^{2}_{x}H^{-1}_{v}(B_{1}\times B_{1})),

with
A
symmetric measurable so that

\Lambda^{-1}\leq A(t,x,v)\leq\Lambda\ \text{ for almost every }(t,x,v)\in Q_{1}.

Then, we have

\left\|f_{+}\right\|_{L^{2}(Q_{1})}\leq\delta\quad\implies\quad\left\|f_{+}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(Q_{1/2})}\leq\frac{1}{2}.

This estimate is translation-invariant under the Galilean transformations (
57
), and scaling-invariant under the kinetic scaling (
56
).

Proof of Lemma
29
.

The setting of the kinetic first De Giorgi lemma is represented in Figure
9
: it is schematically very similar to the parabolic case, provided the new definition (
58
) of cylinders is adopted.

For instance, in the De Giorgi viewpoint, the sequence of cylinders and energy levels in the iteration is

\begin{cases}&T_{k}:=-\frac{1}{4}-\frac{1}{2^{k+1}},\quad T_{0}=-1,\quad T_{k}\uparrow-\frac{1}{4},\\[5.69054pt]
&r_{k}^{x}=\frac{1}{8}+\frac{1}{2^{k+1}},\quad r_{0}=1,\quad r_{k}\downarrow\frac{1}{8},\\[5.69054pt]
&r_{k}^{v}=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2^{k+1}},\quad r_{0}=1,\quad r_{k}\downarrow\frac{1}{2},\\[5.69054pt]
&e_{k}=\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{2^{k+1}},\quad e_{0}=0,\quad e_{k}\uparrow\frac{1}{2},\\[5.69054pt]
&\tilde{Q}_{k}:=(T_{k},0)\times B_{r^{x}_{k}}\times B_{r^{v}_{k}},\\[5.69054pt]
&\displaystyle E_{k}:=\int_{\tilde{Q}_{k}}(f-e_{k})_{+}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}v.\end{cases}

Note again that the cylinders
\tilde{Q}_{k}
are not exactly of the form (
58
); instead they are constructed so as to converge to
Q_{1/2}
.

One establishes again a nonlinear inequality between successive
E_{k}
terms. The proof is based on a
localised energy estimate
, a small
gain of integrability in all variables
(playing the role of a “kinetic Sobolev embedding”) and Chebyshev’s inequality. Only the localised energy estimate and the gain of integrability from
L^{2}
to
L^{p_{*}}
for some
p_{*}>2
in all variables—the “kinetic Sobolev embedding”—differ from the previous proofs. We only discuss these two substeps. The other steps in the De Giorgi–Moser iteration are similar to the elliptic and parabolic cases.

A. Localised energy estimate.
We write the energy estimate in a way that is suitable for both the De Giorgi and Moser approaches to the iteration, as in the previous proofs.

Consider an energy level
e\geq 0
; it is used in De Giorgi’s iteration, but fixed to zero in Moser’s iteration. Consider a smooth localisation function
\phi
with support in
(-1,0]\times B_{1}\times B_{1}
: note that
\phi
is zero at initial time but not necessarily at final time. We integrate (
59
) against
(f-e)_{+}\phi^{2}
on
[-1,\tau]
for
\tau\in(-1,0]
:

\displaystyle\int_{(-1,\tau]\times B_{1}\times B_{1}}\left[\mathcal{T}(f-e)_{+}\right](f-e)_{+}\phi^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}v

\displaystyle=-\int_{(-1,\tau]\times B_{1}\times B_{1}}\left[A\nabla_{v}(f-e)_{+}\cdot\nabla_{v}(f-e)_{+}\right]\phi^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}v

\displaystyle\quad-2\int_{(-1,\tau]\times B_{1}\times B_{1}}\left[A\nabla_{v}(f-e)_{+}\cdot\nabla_{v}\phi\right]\phi(f-e)_{+}\,\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}v.

The left-hand side writes

\displaystyle\int_{(-1,\tau]\times B_{1}\times B_{1}}\left[\mathcal{T}(f-e)_{+}\right](f-e)_{+}\phi^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}v

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{(-1,\tau]\times B_{1}\times B_{1}}\left[\partial_{t}(f-e)^{2}_{+}+v\cdot\nabla_{x}(f-e)^{2}_{+}\right]\phi^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}v

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\int_{B_{1}\times B_{1}}(f-e)^{2}_{+}\phi^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}v\right)\Bigg|_{t=0}-\frac{1}{2}\int_{(-1,\tau]\times B_{1}\times B_{1}}(f-e)^{2}_{+}\mathcal{T}\left(\phi^{2}\right)\,\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}v,

and we bound the last term as

\left|\frac{1}{2}\int_{(-1,\tau]\times B_{1}\times B_{1}}(f-e)^{2}_{+}\mathcal{T}\left(\phi^{2}\right)\,\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}v\right|\\
\lesssim\|\mathcal{T}\phi\|_{\infty}\|\phi\|_{\infty}\int_{Q_{1}\cap\,\mathrm{supp}\,\phi}(f-e)^{2}_{+}\,\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}v.

Therefore we deduce

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(\int_{B_{1}\times B_{1}}(f-e)^{2}_{+}\phi^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}v\right)\Bigg|_{t=\tau}+\int_{(-1,\tau]\times B_{1}\times B_{1}}\left|\nabla_{v}(f-e)_{+}\right|^{2}\phi^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}v

\displaystyle\lesssim\Lambda\|\mathcal{T}\phi\|_{\infty}\|\phi\|_{\infty}\int_{Q_{1}\cap\mathrm{supp}\,\phi}(f-e)^{2}_{+}\,\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}v

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt+\Lambda\int_{(-1,\tau]\times B_{1}\times B_{1}}\left|A\nabla_{v}(f-e)_{+}\cdot\nabla_{v}\phi\right|\phi(f-e)_{+}\,\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}v

\displaystyle\lesssim\Lambda\|\mathcal{T}\phi\|_{\infty}\|\phi\|_{\infty}\int_{Q_{1}\cap\mathrm{supp}\,\phi}(f-e)^{2}_{+}\,\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}v

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt+\Lambda^{2}\left(\int_{(-1,\tau]\times B_{1}\times B_{1}}\left|\nabla_{v}(f-e)_{+}\right|^{2}\phi\,\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}v\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\times

\displaystyle\hskip 85.35826pt\left(\int_{(-1,\tau]\times B_{1}\times B_{1}}\left|\nabla_{v}\phi\right|^{2}(f-e)_{+}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}v\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

By splitting the last term into two squares, we get

\left(\int_{B_{1}\times B_{1}}(f-e)^{2}_{+}\phi^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}v\right)\Bigg|_{t=\tau}+\int_{(-1,\tau]\times B_{1}\times B_{1}}\left|\nabla_{v}(f-e)_{+}\right|^{2}\phi^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}v\\
\lesssim\Lambda^{4}\left(1+\|\nabla_{v}\phi\|_{\infty}+\|\mathcal{T}\phi\|_{\infty}\right)\,\int_{Q_{1}\cap\,\mathrm{supp}\,\phi}(f-e)^{2}_{+}\,\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}v.

Finally we vary the final time
\tau\in(-1,0]
to deduce the following
kinetic Caccioppoli inequality
, which is the kinetic counterpart to (
40
):

(60)

\sup_{-1<\tau\leq 0}\left(\int_{B_{1}\times B_{1}}(f-e)^{2}_{+}\phi^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}v\right)\Bigg|_{t=\tau}+\int_{Q_{1}}\left|\nabla_{v}(f-e)_{+}\right|^{2}\phi^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}v\\
\lesssim\Lambda^{4}\left(1+\|\nabla_{v}\phi\|_{\infty}+\|\mathcal{T}\phi\|_{\infty}\right)\,\int_{Q_{1}\cap\,\mathrm{supp}\,\phi}(f-e)^{2}_{+}\,\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}v.

Given
\phi_{k}
a sequence of smooth localisation functions such that

\mathbf{1}_{\tilde{Q}_{k+1}}\leq\phi_{k}\leq\mathbf{1}_{\tilde{Q}_{k}}\quad\text{ and }\quad|\nabla_{t,x,v}\phi_{k}|\lesssim 2^{k},

the sequence of localised energy estimate is therefore

\sup_{T_{k+1}\leq\tau\leq 0}\left(\int_{B_{1}\times B_{1}}\phi^{2}_{k+1}(f-e_{k+1})_{+}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}v\right)\Bigg|_{t=\tau}\\
+\int_{Q_{1}}\left|\nabla_{x}(\phi_{k}(f-e_{k+1})_{+})\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}v\lesssim\Lambda^{4}2^{k}E_{k}.

Note on the one hand that the energy estimate only provides control on the Sobolev regularity in the
v
variable. It misses part of the variables (the space variable
x
), in contrast to the parabolic case. On the other hand, just like in the parabolic case, the gain of integrability in
t
is ensured by the first term on the left hand side.

B. Gain of integrability (proof by averaging lemma).
The goal of this part of the proof is to recover a gain of integrability in all variables. Once this gain of integrability is obtained, with a different (smaller) exponent
p_{*}>2
as compared to the elliptic and the parabolic cases, the structure of the iteration is exactly similar. Let us consider a smooth localisation function
\phi
so that
\mathbf{1}_{\tilde{Q}_{k+1}}\leq\phi\leq\mathbf{1}_{\tilde{Q}_{k}}
, which satisfies
|\nabla_{t,x,v}\phi|\lesssim 2^{k}
.

Then, by applying the standard Sobolev inequality in the
v
variable to (
60
) (dropping the first term on the left hand side), we obtain

\left\|(f-e)_{+}\phi\right\|_{L^{2}_{t,x}L^{p}_{v}(\tilde{Q}_{k+1})}\lesssim\Lambda^{4}2^{k}\|(f-e)_{+}\|_{L^{2}(Q_{1}\cap\mathrm{supp}\,\phi)}

for some
p>2
.

To recover the gain of integrability in
x
we would like to apply the following standard result in kinetic theory:

Lemma 30

(Averaging Lemma
[
36
,
35
,
17
]
)
.

Given
f=f(t,x,v)\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{1+2d})
which satisfies the equation

\mathcal{T}f=\partial_{t}f+v\cdot\nabla_{x}f=\nabla\cdot H_{1}+H_{0}\quad\text{ with }\quad H_{0},H_{1}\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{1+2d}),

then for any
\varphi=\varphi(v)\in C^{\infty}_{c}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
, we have

(61)

\forall\,s\in\left(0,\frac{1}{3}\right),\quad(t,x)\mapsto\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\varphi\,f\,\mathrm{d}v\ \text{ belongs to }\ H^{s}({\mathbb{R}}^{1+d})

with a bound that is controlled linearly by the sum of the
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{1+2d})
norms of
f
,
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
, and with a constant depending on
\varphi
.

We also have (Bouchut’s version in
[
17
, Theorem 1.3]
):

(62)

\left\|D^{\frac{1}{3}}_{x}f\right\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{1+2d})}^{2}+\left\|D^{\frac{1}{3}}_{t}f\right\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{1+2d})}^{2}\\
\lesssim\|f\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{1+2d})}+\left\|\nabla_{v}f\right\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{1+2d})}^{2}+\left\|H_{0}\right\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{1+2d})}^{2}+\left\|(1+|v|^{2})H_{1}\right\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{1+2d})}^{2}

where
D_{\cdot}^{1/3}=(-\Delta_{\cdot})^{1/3}
is the standard fractional derivative.

Note that we will review the proof based on (
62
) (as done in
[
34
]
), because it is slightly shorter and cleaner. It is however possible to use (
61
) instead: for non-negative solutions the estimate gives a control
H^{s}_{t,x}L^{1}_{v}
provided we localise in velocity, and combined with the energy estimate
L^{\infty}_{t}L^{2}_{x,v}\cap L^{2}_{t,x}H^{1}_{v}
and some interpolation, this would give the gain of integrability in all variables by a comparison argument as discussed below. The interest of (
61
), by comparison with (
62
), is that such estimate remain true for more general transport operators
\partial_{t}+b(v)\cdot\nabla_{x}
where
b
satisfies weak non-degeneracy assumptions (see
[
32
]
for a more detailed discussion).

In order to apply (
62
), we face a difficulty. It does not apply to subsolutions of our problem because of the lack of
L^{2}_{t,x}H^{-1}_{v}({\mathbb{R}}^{1+2d})
-regularity of the defect measure. Indeed the subsolutions, say
f
, we are dealing with, satisfy merely

\mathcal{T}f\leq h

with
h\in L^{2}_{t,x}H^{-1}_{v}({\mathbb{R}}^{1+2d})
. Expressed with a defect measure this means

\mathcal{T}f=h-m

with
h\in L^{2}_{t,x}H^{-1}_{v}({\mathbb{R}}^{1+2d})
and
m
a non-negative measure on
{\mathbb{R}}^{1+2d}
. We will solve this issue by using a comparison principle, since we are
in fine
only interested in integrability properties of
f
, which are preserved by comparison.

Let us consider a weak subsolution
f
in
Q_{1}
, and let us assume without loss of generality that it is non-negative.
We define the new unknown
\tilde{f}:=f\phi_{k}
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{1+2d}
, extending it by zero for
t>0
. It satisfies the inequation

\mathcal{T}\tilde{f}\leq\nabla\cdot(A\nabla\tilde{f})+\nabla_{v}\cdot H_{1}+H_{0}

on the whole
{\mathbb{R}}^{1+2d}
, with

\begin{cases}H_{0}:=f\left(\nabla_{v}\cdot(A\nabla_{v}\phi_{k})-\mathcal{T}\phi_{k}\right)\mathbf{1}_{t\leq 0},\\[5.69054pt]
H_{1}:=A\nabla_{v}f\cdot\nabla_{v}\phi_{k}\mathbf{1}_{t\leq 0}.\end{cases}

Note that we have used that
\partial_{t}\mathbf{1}_{t\leq 0}\leq 0
.

The kinetic Caccioppoli inequality (
60
) (local energy estimate) on
f
implies

(63)

\|\nabla_{v}f\|_{L^{2}(\tilde{Q}_{k})}\lesssim\|f\|_{L^{2}(\tilde{Q}_{k-1})}.

All supports in the equation for
\tilde{f}
are included in
\tilde{Q}_{k}
so we use (
63
) to deduce

\|H_{0}\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{1+2d})}+\|H_{1}\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{1+2d})}\lesssim\|f\|_{L^{2}(\tilde{Q}_{k})}.

The unknown
\tilde{f}
is zero at
t=-1
because of the localisation by
\phi_{1}
. We define
g
that is zero for
t\leq-1
and satisfies the equation

(64)

\mathcal{T}g=\nabla\cdot(A\nabla g)+\nabla_{v}\cdot H_{1}+H_{0}

on
[-1,+\infty)\times{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}
.

By comparison we have
0\leq\tilde{f}\leq g
on
Q_{1}
. Moreover, we integrate (
64
) on
(-\infty,\tau]\times B_{1}\times B_{1}
for any
\tau>-1
to get

(65)

\sup_{\tau>-1}\|g(\tau,\cdot,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{2d})}+\|\nabla_{v}g\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{1+2d})}\\
\lesssim\Lambda^{2}\left(\|H_{0}\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{1+2d})}+\|H_{1}\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{1+2d})}\right)\lesssim\|f\|_{L^{2}(Q_{1})}.

Therefore, given a smooth time localisation function
\theta=\theta(t)
so that

\mathbf{1}_{(-1,0]}\leq\theta\leq\mathbf{1}_{(-1,2]},

the new unknown
\tilde{g}(t,x,v):=\theta(t)g(t,x,v)
satisfies the following modified version of the equation (
64
):

\mathcal{T}\tilde{g}=\nabla\cdot(A\nabla\tilde{g})+\nabla_{v}\cdot H_{1}+\tilde{H}_{0}\quad\text{ where }\quad\tilde{H}_{0}=H_{0}+\theta^{\prime}(t)g.

And the estimate (
65
) implies

\|\tilde{g}\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{1+2d})}+\|\nabla_{v}\tilde{g}\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{1+2d})}+\|\tilde{H}_{0}\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{1+2d})}+\|H_{1}\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{1+2d})}\lesssim\|f\|_{L^{2}(Q_{1})}.

We can therefore apply (
62
) to
\tilde{g}
and, together with the last bound, it implies

(66)

\left\|D^{\frac{1}{3}}_{x}\tilde{g}\right\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{1+2d})}+\left\|D^{\frac{1}{3}}_{t}\tilde{g}\right\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{1+2d})}+\|\tilde{g}\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{1+2d})}+\|\nabla_{v}\tilde{g}\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{1+2d})}\lesssim\|f\|_{L^{2}(Q_{1})}.

Therefore by Sobolev’s embedding we get
\tilde{g}\in L^{p_{*}}({\mathbb{R}}^{1+2d})
, for some
p_{*}\in(2,p)
. This local integrability is finally inherited by
f
on
\tilde{Q}_{k+1}
since
f=\tilde{f}\leq g=\bar{g}
on
\tilde{Q}_{k+1}
. Tracking down all these estimates leads to the inequality

\left\|(f-e)_{+}\right\|_{L^{p_{*}}(\tilde{Q}_{k+1})}\lesssim 2^{k}\|(f-e)_{+}\|_{L^{2}(Q_{1}\cap\,\mathrm{supp}\,\phi_{k})}.

Remark 31
.

In the final comparison between
f
and
g
, the Sobolev regularity in
t
and
x
seems lost. However, by integrating the inequation on
f
:

\mathcal{T}f=\partial_{t}f+v\cdot\nabla_{x}f=\nabla_{v}\cdot(A\nabla_{x}f)-m

against a smooth localisation function, and without squaring the unknown, one can deduce a bound on the total variation of the defect measure
m\leq 0
in terms of the
L^{2}
norm of
f
. Such a bound in total variation translates into a control on the
W^{-1/2,q}_{t,x,v}
norm of
m
, for some
q>1
close to
1
. Then, more sophisticated forms of averaging lemmas can be invoked to obtain a gain of regularity
W^{s,q}
in
t,x
for
s>0
small. It means that even subsolutions have some amount of Sobolev regularity, which can be useful for compactness arguments for instance. This low level of Sobolev regularity for subsolutions is also used quantitatively in
[
42
]
.

C. Gain of integrability (proof by fundamental solutions).

This is an alternative proof of the step of “gain of integrability”
L^{2}\to L^{p_{*}}
with
p_{*}>0
in all variables. This argument was proposed in
[
84
]
. The idea is to use the explicit fundamental solutions to the Kolmogorov equation, when
A
is the identity matrix, and treat the actual operator as a source term. It leads to sharper results but is less robust since it crucially relies on knowning explicitly the fundamental solutions of the equation when the rough coefficients are constant.

We study the following auxiliary problem:

Lemma 32

(Estimates on Kolmogorov’s equation)
.

Let
f\geq 0
be a locally integrable function on
{\mathbb{R}}^{1+2d}
that satisfies

(67)

\mathcal{K}f:=(\mathcal{T}-\Delta_{v})f=\nabla_{v}\cdot F_{1}+F_{2}-m,

with
F_{1},F_{2}\in L^{1}\cap L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{1+2d})
,
m\geq 0
a measure on
{\mathbb{R}}^{1+2d}
. Then, we have

(68)

\displaystyle\|f\|_{L^{2+\frac{1}{d}}({\mathbb{R}}^{1+2d})}\lesssim\|F_{1}\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{1+2d})}+\|F_{2}\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{1+2d})},

(69)

\displaystyle\|f\|_{L^{1}_{t,v}W_{x}^{\sigma_{1},1}({\mathbb{R}}^{1+2d})}\lesssim\|F_{1}\|_{L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{1+2d})}+\|F_{2}\|_{L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{1+2d})}+\|m\|_{\mathrm{TV}({\mathbb{R}}^{1+2d})},

(70)

\displaystyle\|f\|_{L^{1}_{x,v}W_{t}^{\sigma_{2},1}({\mathbb{R}}^{1+2d})}\lesssim\|F_{1}\|_{L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{1+2d})}+\|F_{2}\|_{L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{1+2d})}+\|m\|_{\mathrm{TV}({\mathbb{R}}^{1+2d})},

for any
\sigma_{1}\in(0,1/3)
and
\sigma_{2}\in(0,1/2)
. In (
69
) and (
70
), the constants are proportional to
(1/3-\sigma_{1})^{-1}
and
(1/2-\sigma_{2})^{-1}
, respectively.

Remark 33
.

It is an interesting question to know whether the maximal regularity
L^{1}_{t,v}W_{x}^{1/3,1}
is attained in such an
L^{1}
setting, in which the Young inequality for weak Lebesgue spaces (see for instance
[
37
, Theorem 1.4.25, p. 73]
, and various other standard inequalities, degenerate.

Proof of Proposition
32
.

We express
f
in terms of Kolmogorov’s fundamental solution:

(71)

f(t,x,v)=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{1+2d}}G\left(t-t^{\prime},x-x^{\prime}-(t-t^{\prime})v^{\prime},v-v^{\prime}\right)\mathcal{K}(f)\left(t^{\prime},x^{\prime},v^{\prime}\right)\,\mathrm{d}z^{\prime},

where the Galilean translation is used to define the modified convolution, and

(72)

G(t,x,v)=\left\{\begin{aligned} &\left(\frac{3}{4\pi^{2}t^{4}}\right)^{\frac{d}{2}}\,\exp\,\left[-\frac{3\left|x-\frac{t}{2}v\right|^{2}}{t^{3}}-\frac{|v|^{2}}{4t}\right],&t>0,\\[8.53581pt]
&0,&t\leq 0,\end{aligned}\right.

is the fundamental solution to
\mathcal{K}(G)=\delta_{0}
. The calculation of
G
by Kolmogorov is based on the Fourier transform in
(x,v)
and the characteristic method. This classical result is exposed in
[
49
, Chapter VII, Section 7.6, p. 211]
, and a recent concise re-writing can be found in
[
18
, Appendix A]
.

Since
f,G,m\geq 0
, we deduce that

0\leq f\leq\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{1+2d}}G\left(t-t^{\prime},x-x^{\prime}-(t-t^{\prime})v^{\prime},v-v^{\prime}\right)\left[\nabla_{v}\cdot F_{1}+F_{2}\right]\left(t^{\prime},x^{\prime},v^{\prime}\right)\,\mathrm{d}z^{\prime}.

Then, we have, denoting by

the weak Lebesgue space,

|\nabla_{v}G(t,x,v)|+t|\nabla_{x}G(t,x,v)|\leq\frac{1}{t^{2d+\frac{1}{2}}}\,\exp\left[-\frac{3\left|x-\frac{t}{2}v\right|^{2}}{t^{3}}-\frac{|v|^{2}}{4t}\right]

so that

G,\ \nabla_{v}G(t,x,v),\ t\nabla_{x}G(t,x,v)\in L^{\frac{2d+1}{2d+1/2},\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{1+2d}).

By integration by parts and Young’s inequality (easily extended to our modified convolution product), we deduce (
68
).

To prove (
69
) and (
70
), the idea is to use, roughly, that
\nabla_{v}G
and

“almost” belong to
L^{1}_{t,v}W^{1/3,1}_{x}
and

Lebesgue spaces. We refer to
[

, Section 1.3, p. 7]
for the definition of weak Lebesgue spaces, as well as the more general family of Lorentz spaces they belong to. We only sketch the argument. Define for
\varepsilon>0
the following decomposition:

G:=G_{\varepsilon}+G_{\varepsilon}^{\perp},\quad\text{ with }\quad\begin{cases}G_{\varepsilon}(t,x,v)=\chi\left(\frac{t}{\varepsilon}\right)G(t,x,v)\\[5.69054pt]
G_{\varepsilon}(t,x,v)=\left(1-\chi\left(\frac{t}{\varepsilon}\right)\right)G(t,x,v)\end{cases}

for some smooth cutoff function
\chi\in C^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}_{+})
such that
\mathbf{1}_{[0,1]}\leq\chi\leq\mathbf{1}_{[0,2]}
. The formula (
71
) then implies a corresponding decomposition
f=f_{\varepsilon}+f_{\varepsilon}^{\perp}
on
f
.

Arguing similarly as above, by integration by parts and Young’s inequality, one gets

\begin{cases}\|f_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{1}_{t,x,v}}\lesssim\varepsilon^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\|F_{1}\|_{L^{1}}+\|F_{2}\|_{L^{1}}+\|m\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\right)a\\[5.69054pt]
\|f_{\varepsilon}^{\perp}\|_{L^{1}_{t,v}\mathrm{W}^{k,1}_{x}}\lesssim\varepsilon^{-\frac{1}{2}-\frac{3k}{2}}\left(\|F_{1}\|_{L^{1}}+\|F_{2}\|_{L^{1}}+\|m\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\right)\\[5.69054pt]
\|f_{\varepsilon}^{\perp}\|_{L^{1}_{x,v}\mathrm{W}^{k,1}_{t}}\lesssim\varepsilon^{-\frac{1}{2}-k}\left(\|F_{1}\|_{L^{1}}+\|F_{2}\|_{L^{1}}+\|m\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\right)\end{cases}

for any
k\geq 1
.

The convergence
O(\varepsilon^{1/2})
to zero in the first line is due to the shrinking domain of integration in time when integrating
|\nabla_{v}G(t,x,v)|+t|\nabla_{x}G(t,x,v)|\sim t^{-1/2}
. The blow-up
O(\varepsilon^{-1/2-3k/2})
in the second line follows from integrating

\nabla_{x}^{k}\nabla_{v}G(t,x,v)\ \text{ and }\ t\nabla^{k+1}_{x}G(t,x,v)\quad\sim\ t^{-\frac{1}{2}-\frac{3k}{2}}

away from the singularity
t\geq\varepsilon
(and using the time integrability ensured by
k\geq 1
). The blow-up
O(\varepsilon^{-1/2-k})
in the third line follows from integrating

\partial_{t}^{k}\nabla_{v}G(t,x,v)\ \text{ and }\ \partial_{t}^{k}t\nabla^{k}_{x}G(t,x,v)\quad\sim\ t^{-\frac{1}{2}-k}

away from the singularity
t\geq\varepsilon
(and using again the time integrability ensured by
k\geq 1
).

By the Littlewood-Paley decomposition or any equivalent interpolation argument, one then deduces that
f\in L^{1}_{t,v}W^{\sigma_{1},1}_{x}
for
\sigma_{1}\in[0,1/3)
, and that
f\in L^{1}_{x,v}W^{\sigma_{2},1}_{t}
for
\sigma_{2}\in[0,1/2)
.
∎

We now deduce the gain of integrability on subsolutions, as well as gain of (low level) Sobolev regularity in
x

f\geq 0
a sub-solution to (
59
) on

, and we claim:

\|f\|_{L^{p}(\tilde{Q}_{k+1})}+\|f\|_{L^{1}_{t,v}W^{\sigma_{1},1}_{x}(\tilde{Q}_{k+1}))}+\|f\|_{L^{1}_{x,v}W^{\sigma_{1},1}_{t}(\tilde{Q}_{k+1}))}\lesssim 2^{k}\|f\|_{L^{2}(\tilde{Q}_{k})},

with
\sigma_{1}\in[0,1/3)
,
\sigma_{2}\in[0,1/2)
and a constant degenerating as

or

.

Given

the usual smooth localisation function such that

for

for

with

\displaystyle\tilde{m}=\phi_{k}m.

and

The local energy estimate (

4.3.
The improved kinetic first De Giorgi lemma

Similarly to the elliptic and parabolic cases, we can improve the first De Giorgi lemma as follows.

Lemma 34

(Improved kinetic first De Giorgi Lemma)
.

Given
\Lambda>0
and
\zeta_{0}\in(0,2]
, there is
C>0
, depending only
\Lambda
,
\zeta_{0}
and the dimension
d
, so that the following holds:

Let
f
be a weak subsolution in a kinetic cylinder
Q_{R}
to

\partial_{t}f+v\cdot\nabla_{x}f\leq\nabla_{v}\cdot\left(A\nabla_{v}f\right)

in the functional setting

\displaystyle f\in L^{\infty}_{t}((-R^{2},0);L^{2}_{x,v}(B_{R^{3}}\times B_{R}))\cap L^{2}_{t}((-R^{2},0);L^{2}_{x}H^{1}_{v}(B_{R^{3}}\times B_{R}))

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}f\in L^{2}_{t}((-R^{2},0);L^{2}_{x}H^{-1}_{v}(B_{R^{3}}\times B_{R})),

with
A
symmetric measurable so that

\Lambda^{-1}\leq A(t,x)\leq\Lambda\ \text{ for almost every }(t,x,v)\in Q_{R}.

Then, we have, for any
\zeta\in(\zeta_{0},2]
and
0<r<R
,

\|f\|_{L^{\infty}(Q_{r})}\leq C(R-r)^{-\frac{2+4d}{\zeta}}\left(\frac{1}{|Q_{R}|}\int_{Q_{R}}f^{\zeta}\,\mathrm{d}z\right)^{\frac{1}{\zeta}}.

Proof.

By using the scaling of the equation in the kinetic first De Giorgi lemma, one can prove the following inequality between two general cylinders
Q_{r}
and
Q_{R}
:

\|f\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{r})}\lesssim(R-r)^{-1-2d}\left(\frac{1}{|Q_{R}|}\int_{Q_{R}}f^{2}\,\mathrm{d}z\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

The Young’s inequality and the inequality

\left(\frac{1}{|Q_{R}|}\int_{Q_{R}}f^{2}\,\mathrm{d}z\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\lesssim\left(\frac{1}{|Q_{R}|}\int_{Q_{R}}f^{\zeta}\,\mathrm{d}z\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\|f\|^{1-\frac{\zeta}{2}}_{L^{\infty}(Q_{R})},

imply that the function
\mathcal{N}(r):=\|f\|_{L^{\infty}(Q_{r})}
verifies the following inequality for some
C>0
and any
0<r<r^{\prime}<R
:

\mathcal{N}(r)\leq\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{N}(r^{\prime})+C(r^{\prime}-r)^{-\frac{2+4d}{\zeta}}\left(\frac{1}{|Q_{R}|}\int_{B(0,R)}f^{\zeta}\,\mathrm{d}z\right)^{\frac{1}{\zeta}}.

By the same iteration along the sequence of radii

r_{k+1}=r_{k}+\varepsilon\left(1-\alpha^{-1}\right)\alpha^{-k}

for
k\geq 0
, some
\alpha>1
,
\varepsilon:=R-r
and
r_{0}:=r
, we deduce as in the elliptic and parabolic cases that

\mathcal{N}(r)\lesssim_{\zeta_{0}}\varepsilon^{-\frac{2+4d}{\zeta}}\left(\frac{1}{|Q_{R}|}\int_{Q_{R}}f^{\zeta}\,\mathrm{d}z\right)^{\frac{1}{\zeta}},

from which the conclusion follows.
∎

4.4.
The kinetic second De Giorgi lemma

Inspired by the use of Poincaré’s inequality as an
integral measure of the oscillation
in the works of Moser
[
70
,
73
]
, Kruzhkov
[
58
]
, and later in
[
93
]
in a modified fashion, we introduce the strategy of
[
42
]
, in the form simplified and optimised in
[
81
,
7
,
26
]
. This method does not involve the equation verified by the logarithm of a solution to (
59
), as in Nash’s, Moser’s and Kruzhkov’s methods.

The kinetic second De Giorgi lemma (reduction of oscillation) resembles the elliptic case, and it implies the Theorem
28
by an argument also similar to the parabolic case, but accounting for the slightly different geometry of the kinetic cylinders.

More generally, the overall structure of the proof of the kinetic second De Giorgi lemma is similar to the elliptic and parabolic cases (
24
), with kinetic versions of each step.

Lemma 35

(Kinetic second De Giorgi Lemma: reduction of oscillation)
.

Given
\Lambda>0
, there is
\nu\in(0,1)
, depending only on
\Lambda
and the dimension
n
, so that the following holds:

Let
f
be a weak solution on
Q_{3}
to

\mathcal{T}f=\nabla_{v}\cdot(A\nabla_{v}f)

in the functional setting
L^{2}((-9,0)\times B_{27};H^{1}(B_{3}))
, where
A
is symmetric measurable and so that

\Lambda^{-1}\leq A(t,x,v)\leq\Lambda\ \text{ for almost every }(t,x,v)\in Q_{3}.

Then, we have

(75)

\mathrm{osc}_{Q_{\frac{1}{2}}}f\leq\nu\,\mathrm{osc}_{Q_{2}}f,

where we denote
\mathrm{osc}_{B}f:=\sup_{B}f-\inf_{B}f
.

Note again that this lemma requires
f
to be
both
a subsolution and a supersolution, i.e. to be a solution. Let us show that Lemma
35
implies the Hölder regularity, which thus concludes the proof of Theorem
28
.

Corollary 36
.

Given
\Lambda>0
, there is
\alpha\in(0,1)
, depending only on
\Lambda
and the dimension
n
, so that the following holds:

Let
f
be a weak solution in
(0,T)\times\mathcal{U}
, with
\mathcal{U}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}
and open set, to

\mathcal{T}f=\nabla_{v}\cdot\left(A\nabla_{v}f\right),

where
A
is symmetric measurable and so that

\Lambda^{-1}\leq A(t,x,v)\leq\Lambda\ \text{ for almost every }(t,x,v)\in(0,T)\times\mathcal{U}.

Then, we have
f\in C^{\alpha}_{\mathrm{loc}}((0,T)\times\mathcal{U})
.

Moreover, given any
\delta\in(0,T/2)
, there is
C>0
, depending on
\Lambda
,
\delta
and the dimension
n
, so that for any
\tilde{\mathcal{U}}\subset\subset\mathcal{U}
with
\text{dist}(\tilde{\mathcal{U}},\partial\mathcal{U})\geq\delta
and
\delta<T_{0}<T_{1}<T-\delta
, we have

(76)

\|f\|_{C^{\alpha}((T_{0},T_{1})\times\tilde{\mathcal{U}})}\leq C\|f\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{U})}.

Proof.

We already have that
f\in\mathrm{L}^{\infty}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathcal{U})
by Lemma
29
with the estimate

(77)

\forall\,\hat{\mathcal{U}}\subset\subset\mathcal{U},\quad\|f\|_{L^{\infty}((T_{0}/2,T)\times\hat{\mathcal{U}})}\leq C\|f\|_{L^{2}((0,T)\times\mathcal{U})}

for a constant
C>0
depending on
\Lambda
,
\text{dist}(\hat{\mathcal{U}},\partial\mathcal{U})
,
T_{0}
and the dimension
n
.

Let
\mathcal{\tilde{U}}\subset\subset\mathcal{\hat{U}}\subset\subset\mathcal{U}
so that

(78)

\text{dist}(\mathcal{\tilde{U}},\partial\mathcal{U})\leq\frac{1}{2}\text{dist}(\mathcal{\tilde{U}},\partial\mathcal{\hat{U}}),\quad\text{dist}(\mathcal{\tilde{U}},\partial\mathcal{U})\in\left[\frac{\delta}{4},\frac{\delta}{2}\right],\quad\text{dist}(\mathcal{\tilde{U}},\partial\mathcal{\hat{U}})\in\left[\frac{\delta}{2},\delta\right].

Given
(t_{0},x_{0},v_{0})\in(T_{0},T_{1})\times\mathcal{\tilde{U}}
, we define the sequence of centred rescaled functions

\displaystyle\forall\,k\geq 1,\ \forall\,(s,y,w)\in Q_{1},\quad\tilde{g}_{k}(s,y,w):=g_{k-1}\left(\frac{s}{16},\frac{y}{64},\frac{w}{4}\right)

\displaystyle\forall\,(s,y)\in Q_{1},\quad g_{0}(s,y,w):=f\left(t_{0}+\frac{\delta}{32}+\frac{\delta s}{16},x_{0}+\frac{\delta y}{64},v_{0}+\frac{\delta w}{4}\right).

Note that each function
g_{k}
is a rescaling of
f
and solves

\mathcal{T}g_{k}=\nabla_{x}\cdot[A_{k}\nabla_{x}g_{k}]

in
Q_{2}
, with the diffusion matrix

A_{k}(s,y,w):=A\left(t_{0}+\frac{\delta}{32}+\frac{\delta s}{2^{4k}},x_{0}+\frac{\delta y}{2^{6k}},v_{0}+\frac{\delta w}{2^{2k}}\right).

This new diffusion matrix is still measurable and satisfies

\Lambda^{-1}\leq A_{k}(s,y,w)\leq\Lambda\ \text{ for almost every }(s,y)\in Q_{2}.

Therefore, we can apply Lemma
35
to each
g_{k}
and deduce

\forall\,k\geq 1,\quad\mathrm{osc}_{Q_{\frac{1}{2}}}g_{k}\,\leq\,\nu\,\mathrm{osc}_{Q_{2}}g_{k}.

The scaling relation between successive terms in the sequence

yields

\forall\,k\geq 1,\quad\mathrm{osc}_{Q_{2}}\,g_{k+1}=\mathrm{osc}_{Q_{\frac{1}{2}}}g_{k},

so we obtain by induction

\mathrm{osc}_{Q_{4^{-k}}}g_{1}\leq\nu^{k-1}\mathrm{osc}_{Q_{2}}g_{1}\leq 2\,\nu^{k-1}\,\|f\|_{L^{\infty}((\delta/2,T-\delta/2)\times\hat{\mathcal{U}})}.

Then, given a point

(t,x,v)\in\left(t_{0}-\frac{\delta}{16},t_{0}+\frac{\delta}{16}\right)\times B\left(x_{0},\frac{\delta}{64}\right)\times B\left(v_{0},\frac{\delta}{4}\right),

we pick up

so that

\begin{cases}\displaystyle|t-t_{0}|\in\left[\delta 16^{-k-2},\delta 16^{-k-1}\right],\\[5.69054pt]
\displaystyle|x-x_{0}|\in\left[\delta 64^{-k-2},\delta 64^{-k-1}\right],\\[5.69054pt]
\displaystyle|v-v_{0}|\in\left[\delta 4^{-k-2},\delta 4^{-k-1}\right],\end{cases}

and write

|f(t,x,v)-f(t_{0},x_{0},v_{0})|\leq 2\,\nu^{k-1}\,\|f\|_{L^{\infty}((\delta/2,T-\delta/2)\times\hat{\mathcal{U}})},

so that (using
|t-t_{0}|\geq\delta 16^{-k-2}
,
|x-x_{0}|\geq\delta 64^{-k-2}
and
|v-v_{0}|\geq\delta 4^{-k-2}
)

|f(t,x,v)-f(t_{0},x_{0},v_{0})|\lesssim\\
\left[\left(16^{3\alpha}\nu\right)^{k}|t-t_{0}|^{3\alpha}+\left(64^{2\alpha}\nu\right)^{k}\,|x-x_{0}|^{2\alpha}+\left(4^{6\alpha}\nu\right)^{k}\,|v-v_{0}|^{6\alpha}\right]\,\|f\|_{L^{\infty}((\delta/2,T-\delta/2)\times\hat{\mathcal{U}})}.

We then choose

\alpha:=-\frac{\ln\nu}{12\,\ln 2}

so that

, and deduce

is
C^{\alpha}
at
(t_{0},x_{0},v_{0})
. (In fact, note that the Hölder regularity exponent is

in
t
,
2\alpha
in
x
and
6\alpha
in
v

not
depend on
(t_{0},x_{0},v_{0})
, but only on the constant
\nu
of Lemma
35
.

We have therefore proved

\|f\|_{C^{\alpha}((\delta,T-\delta)\times\tilde{\mathcal{U}})}\leq C\|f\|_{L^{\infty}((\delta/2,T-\delta/2,\hat{\mathcal{U}})}

with a constant depending only on
\nu
and
\delta
. Combined with (
77
) and (
78
), it implies (
76
) and concludes the proof.
∎

Proof of the kinetic intermediate value lemma
39
by compactness.

This argument was first proposed in
[

]

A. The setting.
We argue by contradiction. Let us consider a contradiction sequence

(f_{k})_{k\geq 0}
of subsolutions in
Q_{2}
to

(82)

\mathcal{T}f_{k}=\nabla_{v}\cdot(A_{k}\nabla_{v}f_{k})-\mu_{k}

for some non-negative measures
\mu_{k}
, such that

for all

and, for some fixed

\displaystyle|\{f_{k}\geq 1/2\}\cap Q_{r}|\geq\delta_{1}|Q_{r}|

\displaystyle|\{f_{k}\leq 0\}\cap Q^{-}_{r}|\geq\delta_{2}|Q_{r}^{-}|

\displaystyle|\{0<f_{k}<1/2\}\cap Q_{1}|\to 0\quad\text{ as }\quad k\to+\infty.

The same assumptions still hold for the positive parts
(f_{k})_{+}
of each function

B. A priori estimates on the contradiction sequence.
First, by using the local energy estimate (

Using standard compactness theorem, we have (still writing

and

and

for the subsequences, in an abuse of notation)

(f_{k})_{+}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\ast}}{{\rightharpoonup}}F\text{ in }L^{\infty}_{\text{loc}}(Q_{2})

and

\nabla_{v}(f_{k})_{+}\rightharpoonup\nabla_{v}F\quad\text{ and }\quad A_{k}\nabla_{v}(f_{k})_{+}\rightharpoonup H\quad\text{ in }L^{2}_{\text{loc}}(Q_{2})

for some weak limit

and

, and

in the space of non-negative measure, thanks to the bound on the total variation.

By weak limit, we deduce uniform bounds on the

norm of

, on the

norm of

\mathcal{T}F=\nabla_{v}\cdot H-\mu.

Thanks to the averaging lemma (

A kinetic version of Moser’s approach had long seemed out of reach, but the critical kinetic trajectories constructed in
[
26
]
have recently made it possible to finally extend this method to the kinetic setting. In this extension, the
critical scaling
of the trajectories is essential in deriving universal bounds on the logarithm of the solution and apply the Bombieri-Giusti result, in the spirit of the paper of Moser
[
77
]
.

Regarding a kinetic version of Kruzhkov’s approach, we refer to
[
93
,
41
]
for an extension in the kinetic setting. Note, however, that these papers use a weaker form of the kinetic Poincaré inequality, with a complicated corrector, which adds layers of technical complexity to the proofs. These Kruzhkov-type proofs could be simplified by using the kinetic Poincaré inequality (
