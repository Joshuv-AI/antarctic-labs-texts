Assessing the stability and adaptability of wheat genotypes in well-watered and rainfed conditions using AMMI, BLUP, GGE biplot and MTSI approaches
Authors: Nishtman Abdi, Hadi Alipour, Reza Darvishzadeh, Ehsan Rabieyan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aimed to analyze genotype × environment interaction (GEI), assess yield stability and adaptability, and identify superior Iranian wheat genotypes using multiple statistical approaches. A total of 266 genotypes, comprising 86 cultivars and 180 landraces, were evaluated under rainfed (S2017-2020) and well-watered (N2016-2020) conditions using a lattice design with two replications. The Additive Main Effects and Multiplicative Interaction (AMMI) model revealed significant effects of genotype, environment, and GEI on grain yield (GY), with the first three principal components accounting for 81.1% of the variation in GEI. Based on AMMI1 and AMMI2 biplots, genotypes 90 (625047), 231 (Moghan3), 153 (627423), 234 (Naz), and 82 (624944) were identified as both stable and high-yielding. The WAASB (Weighted Average of Absolute Scores from the BLUP interaction matrix) analysis further confirmed that genotypes 90 (625047), 204 (Dayhim), 153, 241 (Pishgam), 235 (Neishabour), and 207 (Falat) exhibited the highest stability. These findings were corroborated by GSI-based AMMI and BLUP-based stability parameters. A significant correlation between WAASB and the Multi-Trait Stability Index (MTSI) facilitated the selection of overlapping genotypes: 204 (Dayhim), 138 (627066), 126 (626904), 139 (627072), 107 (626261), 8 (621704), and 58 (624381) as superior and stable performers. The GGE biplot method identified genotype 11 (621908) as the best performer in the main mega-environment, while genotypes 178 (628088), 186 (Alvand), and 45 (623507) excelled in different environments. Additionally, GGE “ideal genotype” analysis highlighted genotypes 90 (625047), 224 (Koohdasht), 108 (626358), 137 (627057), 24 (623136), 194 (BAM), 240 (Parsi), 199 (Chamran2), and 144 (627356) as top performers. Overall, genotypes 90 (625047), 204 (Dayhim), and 153 (627423) were consistently recognized across methods as high-yielding and stable, making them strong candidates for wheat improvement programs aimed at enhancing grain yield stability in diverse Iranian environments.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-54262-0.Keywords: Biplot analysis, Drought, Grain yield stability, Mega-environments, Multi-environment trials, WheatSubject terms: Genetics, Plant sciences

Introduction

Wheat (Triticum aestivum L.) is a vital crop species globally, providing essential food for the growing population due to its high yield potential and stability in diverse environments1,2. Iran is a significant producer and consumer of wheat. However, in recent years, the country has faced recurrent droughts exacerbated by climate change, which have drastically reduced wheat production. As a result, the average wheat yield in Iran remains below the global average3. Considering the projected negative impacts of climate change on crop productivity, it is critical to focus on breeding efforts that prioritize the development of wheat cultivars that not only increase yield but also exhibit enhanced stability under changing environmental conditions.

Wheat’s tolerance to biotic and abiotic stresses is partly governed by genetic factors, making it possible to breed cultivars with greater resilience and higher yield potential4. A fundamental step in developing cultivars with broad adaptability is understanding how genotypes interact with their environments (G × E interaction, GEI). When genotypes are tested across varying environments whether they are different locations, years, or their combinations, their yield performance and phenotypic characteristics can differ significantly5. The presence of GEI often complicates the selection of high-yielding genotypes that are universally adapted to multiple conditions, making it a challenge for breeders to achieve consistent results. Breeders typically focus on key agronomic traits, such as yield and its components, when assessing GEI to identify genotypes that can perform well in a wide range of conditions6. Multi-environment trials (METs) are invaluable tools in breeding programs, as they help identify genotypes that not only yield well but are also stable across different environments. A comprehensive understanding of GEI is essential to accurately evaluate genotype performance under diverse environmental conditions. By considering GEI and employing suitable statistical analyses, breeders can select genotypes with high and stable yields, contributing to enhanced productivity and farmer satisfaction7. Hence, a variety of statistical models and tools have been developed to analyze GEI within METs.

One such method is the Additive Main Effect and Multiplicative Interaction (AMMI), which combines analysis of variance (ANOVA) and principal component analysis (PCA) in a unified framework to evaluate GEI effects8. Gauch and Zobel.9 suggested that AMMI is well-suited for multi-location trials. The AMMI model also provides a graphical biplot tool that aids in visualizing GEI, making it easier to interpret results and group genotypes based on their performance8. Several stability statistics have been derived from AMMI, including the AMMI Stability Value (ASV), AMMI Stability Index (ASI), the average of the squared eigenvector value (EV), the parameter of Annicchiarico10, the distance of IPC point with origin in space (DZ), the stability statistic based on the fitted AMMI model (FA), the modified AMMI stability value (MASV), the sums of the absolute value of the IPC scores (SIPC), the averages of the square eigenvector values (EV), the absolute value of the sum across environments (AVAMGE), the absolute value of the relative contribution of IPCs to the interaction (Za), Modified AMMI Stability Index (MASI), and AMMI Based Stability Parameter (ASTAB)10. A notable advancement by Farshadfar11 introduced the Genotype Selection Index (GSI), which combines the AMMI stability value with mean yield to assess overall yield stability. Utilizing both AMMI and GSI parameters has proven effective in identifying genotypes with broad adaptability and high grain yield under both rainfed and irrigated conditions12,13.

In addition to AMMI, methods like the harmonic mean of relative performance of genotypic values (HMRPGV)14 and the Weighted Average of Absolute Scores from Singular-Value Decomposition of the Matrix of BLUPs (WAASB) have been used to quantify genotypic stability and GEI effects15. The combination of the AMMI graphical tool and the predictive accuracy of BLUP has been widely adopted in studies on genotype × environment interaction16–19.

To improve selection efficiency, breeders must consider multiple traits simultaneously. However, identifying genotypes that exhibit both high yield and stability across various traits is challenging. To address this, Olivoto et al.15 proposed the multi-trait stability index (MTSI), a novel method that allows for the selection of genotypes based on their performance and stability across multiple traits. The MTSI integrates fixed- and mixed-effects models to estimate the distance between genotypes and an ideal "ideotype," optimizing selection based on mean performance and stability.

Another promising approach for analyzing GEI is the Genotype plus GE Interaction (GGE) biplot, which helps identify high-yielding cultivars across different environments. The GGE biplot evaluates the main effects of genotypes and their interactions with the environment, distinguishing between genotypic yield and stability. It also assesses the ability of environments to differentiate genotypes and the representativeness of those environments20,21. Unlike AMMI, which uses double-centered PCA, GGE utilizes an environment-centered PCA, offering different insights into genotype-environment interactions22. Studies have shown that combining AMMI and GGE biplot methods provides a more comprehensive understanding of GEI in METs, aiding in the selection of superior wheat genotypes that perform well across diverse conditions21–26.

In this study, we assessed the seed yield of 266 wheat genotypes across nine different environments in Iran. The data were collected from both well-watered and rainfed conditions over five and four cropping seasons, respectively. The specific objectives of the study are: (i) to analyze and interpret the main effects of genotype and environment, as well as the G × E interaction, on grain yield; (ii) to identify stable and high-yielding wheat genotypes across nine distinct environments using various stability parameters; (iii) to determine whether the nine environments can be classified as a single mega-environment or multiple distinct environments; (iv) to evaluate the discriminating ability and representativeness of these environments; and (v) to compare the effectiveness of different stability models (AMMI, GSI, BLUP, WAASB, and MTSI) in identifying superior genotypes.

Result

AMMI analysis

According to ANOVA (Supplementary Table 1), the main effects of all traits were statistically significant. Furthermore, the genotype × environment (G × E) interaction showed significant differences for all traits. The AMMI ANOVA indicated that grain yield was significantly influenced by environments (E), genotypes (G), and their interaction (GEI) (P < 0.001) (Table 1). The environment accounted for 22.17% of the total variation in grain yield, whereas genotype and GEI effects contributed 4.57% and 8.11%, respectively. This highlights the important role of GEI in explaining yield variation in multi-environment trials. The AMMI ANOVA further revealed that the first three principal components (PCs) were significant at the 1% probability level and explained 81.1% of the GEI variation. The first PC accounted for 47.1% of GEI, while the second, third, and fourth PCs contributed 19.1%, 18.6%, and 14.9%, respectively (Table 2). IPCA scores indicate the degree of genotype adaptation across environments. Higher IPCA values reflect specific adaptation, whereas values close to zero indicate general adaptability and stability. Based on PC1 scores, genotypes 51 (623953), 10 (621735), 84 (624947), 40 (623428), 126 (626904), 6 (621668), 256 (Sistan), 139 (627072), and 161 (627760) showed high general adaptability (Table 4). In contrast, genotypes 224 (Koohdasht), 175 (627987), 53 (624215), 202 (Darya), 81 (624941), 178 (628088), 47 (623510), and 254 (Siosson) were identified as the most stable based on their low PC2 scores (Table 3; Supplementary Table 2).

BLUP-based analysis

The BLUP approach was used to evaluate genotype adaptability and stability. Based on the geometric mean, genotypes 90 (625047), 11 (621908), 199 (Chamran2), 234 (Naz), 262 (Uroum), and 108 (626358) were identified as desirable. Using REML-derived parameters (HMGV, RPGV, and HMRPGV), genotypes 11 (621908), 90 (625047), 108 (626358), 199 (Chamran2), 262 (Uroum), and 186 (Alvand) were identified as stable and high-performing. These parameters showed consistent ranking patterns (Table 3 and Supplementary Table 4).

WAASB analysis

Based on the WAASB index, which estimates stability by integrating all significant components of the AMMI model with appropriate weights, genotypes 8 (621704), 175 (627987), 107 (626261), 84 (624947), 204 (Dayhim), and 193 (Bahar) were identified as the most stable. In contrast, genotypes 178 (628088), 260 (Tobari), 201 (Darab2), 78 (624911), and 186 (Alvand) were the least stable (Table 3 and Supplementary Table 4). To better interpret the relationship between stability and yield performance, a WAASB × GY biplot was constructed (Fig. 3), enabling the simultaneous evaluation of genotypes based on both criteria.

The biplot separated genotypes and environments into four quadrants. The first quadrant included 40 genotypes along with environments S2017, S2018, S2019, S2020, and N2018, which were associated with below-average yield and high WAASB values, indicating low performance and strong GEI effects. MTSI analysis

MTSI was utilized for the simultaneous selection of genotypes based on mean performance and stability across multiple traits. The genotypes were ranked according to their MTSI values, with the highest values located at the center and the lowest values at the outermost circles (Fig. 4). Genotypes highlighted with red dots were selected based on their MTSI values at a selection intensity of 15%. A total of 40 genotypes were selected using the MTSI index. Genotype 65 (624805), followed by 204 (Dayhim), 138 (627066), 106 (626260), 93 (625123), 135 (627054), 161 (627760), 94 (625127), 102 (626215), and 52 (623980), which were located closer to the center of the plot, were identified as the most ideal and stable genotypes (Fig. 4 and Supplementary Table 6). These selected genotypes showed an increase in the mean values of all traits, in agreement with the breeding objectives. Overall, the multi-trait selection approach demonstrated high efficiency in selecting genotypes with desirable selection differentials (Table 4).

Correlation analysis

In the present study, a significant correlation was observed between mean grain yield (GY) and HMPRGV, RPGV, HMGV, ASTAB, AVAMGE, DZ, EV, PC1, and PC2 stability statistics (Figs. 5 and 6). Therefore, these statistics are associated with the dynamic concept of stability, and selection based on any of them can be considered reliable (Becker and Leon, 1988). The results also showed that all AMMI-based stability statistics (ASV, ASI, AVAMGE, ASTAB, DA, FA, SIPC, ZA, DZ, EV, MASI, and MASV) were positively and significantly correlated with each other, as well as with WAASB and MTSI (Figs. 5 and 6). In the cluster analysis, WAASB, all AMMI-based statistics, and MTSI were grouped together, indicating strong similarity among these stability measures (Fig. 6). In contrast, grain yield (GY), along with PC1, PC2, RPGV, HMGV, and HMRPGV, formed a separate group (Fig. 6). These results indicate consistency among stability assessment methods.

GGE biplot analysis

GGE biplot analysis explained 69.38% of the total variation (51.25% by PC1 and 18.13% by PC2) (Fig. 7). The polygon (“which-won-where”) view of the GGE biplot provides an effective graphical tool for identifying mega-environments and superior genotypes adapted to specific environments27. In this approach, the polygon is formed by connecting the outermost genotypes with straight lines, and perpendicular lines drawn from the origin divide the biplot into different sectors, each representing a mega-environment (ME)20,21. Based on this structure, the 266 genotypes were distributed across 10 sectors, while the environments were grouped into three main mega-environments (Fig. 7). The first mega-environment included S2017, S2018, S2019, S2020, and N2018, with genotypes 21, 86, 196, 164, 82, 184, 222, 137, 144, 88, 131, 235, 33, 221, 245, 176, 193, and 195 showing the best adaptation. The second mega-environment comprised N2017, N2018, N2019, and N2020, where genotypes 11, 108, 240, 90, 234, 207, 251, 153, 256, 248, 180, 147, and 218 were identified as the most adapted. The third mega-environment consisted of N2016, in which genotypes 178 (628088), 186 (Alvand), and 45 (623507) showed the best performance and specific adaptation.

Discussion

Genotype × environment interaction (GEI) is a fundamental component of multi-environment trials, reflecting differential genotype responses across environments and often leading to changes in genotype rankings. Rather than being viewed solely as a limitation, GEI provides an opportunity to identify both broadly and specifically adapted genotypes for targeted breeding strategies15,28. In the present study, GEI was substantial for grain yield, indicating strong environmental modulation of genetic performance, primarily driven by variation in water availability across growing seasons. This dominant environmental influence (22.17% of total variation) confirms that moisture stress is the main driver of yield instability in the studied wheat germplasm, consistent with previous reports in wheat under contrasting water regimes15,19,29–32.

The concept of yield stability is not universal and depends on the breeding objective and target environments33 and different statistical approaches capture different aspects of stability. Therefore, relying on a single index may lead to incomplete inference34. In this study, we adopted a multi-model framework integrating AMMI, GSI, BLUP-derived parameters, WAASB, MTSI, and GGE biplots to obtain a comprehensive and biologically meaningful interpretation of stability and adaptation. This integrative approach allowed simultaneous assessment of mean performance, responsiveness, and stability, providing a stronger basis for selection decisions35–38.

The AMMI model revealed that the first three interaction principal components explained 81.1% of GEI variation, indicating that a limited number of latent factors control most genotype responses to environments. This suggests that genotype adaptation in this panel is largely governed by a few major environmental gradients, mainly associated with water availability and seasonal variability31,32,39–42. Similar patterns have been reported in wheat, where the first few IPCAs capture most GEI structure43. Genotypes such as 234 (Naz), 237 (Ofogh), and 6 (621668), located near the origin of AMMI2 biplot, showed minimal interaction effects, indicating broad adaptation, whereas genotypes positioned farther from the origin exhibited specific adaptation to either rainfed or irrigated conditions.44, These findings are in agreement with previous studies highlighting the efficiency of AMMI in detecting stability and adaptability in wheat and other crops36,44–47.

Due to The large number of evaluated genotypes (n = 266) reduced the clarity of AMMI biplots due to overlapping points; therefore, AMMI-derived stability statistics together with the Genotype Stability Index (GSI) were used to improve discrimination among genotypes. The high agreement among AMMI-based indices (ASV, ASTAB, AVAMGE, DA, DZ, EV, FA, MASI, and MASV) indicates that these statistics capture similar dimensions of stability, consistent with previous reports in wheat45. Moreover, several indices, including ASTAB, AVAMGE, DZ, and EV, showed significant positive correlations with grain yield, reflecting a dynamic stability pattern. These results are consistent with earlier reports suggesting that certain AMMI-based indices can effectively identify genotypes with both high yield and stability45,48. In addition, all GSI-based AMMI models showed similar efficiency in identifying stable and high-yielding genotypes, supporting their use as reliable alternative selection tools, as also reported by Cheloei et al.49 and Anuradha et al.50.

Several indices, including ASTAB, AVAMGE, DZ, EV, GSI, GSI-AMMI based, and BLUP-based parameters (HMGV, RPGV, HMRPGV), showed correlations with grain yield, indicating their alignment with the dynamic concept of stability and their suitability for simultaneous selection of yield and stability51. Consistent identification of superior genotypes such as 90 (625047), 234 (Naz), 241 (Pishgam), 186 (Alvand), 153 (627423), 199 (Chamran2), and 207 (Falat) across GSI and BLUP-based approaches confirms the robustness of the results52,53.

To address limitations of individual indices, a multi-index framework integrating WAASB, GSI-based AMMI, BLUP-derived parameters, and MTSI was applied. An important characteristic of effective stability measures is low correlation with mean yield, as yield-dependent indices provide limited additional information34. In this context, WAASB captured stability more independently by incorporating all significant interaction components15, whereas GSI and HMRPGV were more effective for identifying high-yielding genotypes.

Across these complementary approaches, genotypes 90 (625047), 204 (Dayhim), and 153 (627423) consistently combined high grain yield with low GEI, indicating broad adaptation. The WAASB biplot further supported these findings by identifying genotypes 90 (625047), 204 (Dayhim), 153 (627423), 241 (Pishgam), 235 (Neishabour), and 207 (Falat) as stable and high-performing. Notably, these genotypes were also selected by GSI -based AMMI and BLUP-based methods, reinforcing the reliability of the results.

The MTSI provided an additional biological perspective by enabling simultaneous selection for yield and multiple agronomic traits. Selected genotypes, including 204 (Dayhim) and landraces 65 (624805), 138 (627066), and 106 (626260), exhibited favorable trait combinations, including increased spike weight, seed number per spike, and thousand kernel weight, along with slightly reduced phenological duration. This pattern suggests improved reproductive efficiency and a potential adaptive advantage under terminal drought conditions, where a shorter growth cycle can help maintain grain filling54. Similar findings have been reported in recent studies highlighting the effectiveness of MTSI for multi-trait selection in wheat and barley55,56.

The GGE biplot provided a complementary perspective to numerical stability indices by graphically partitioning genotype plus genotype × environment (G + GE) effects21,57. The polygon view revealed three mega-environments. The first grouped N2018 together with all rainfed environments (S2017–S2020), while the second comprised the well-watered environments excluding N2016; N2016 alone formed a third mega-environment. This pattern indicates that moisture regime had a stronger influence on genotype performance than seasonal variation alone, which is consistent with the known sensitivity of wheat to water availability during key developmental stages58,59.

Within the rainfed mega-environment, genotypes 137 (627057), 235 (Neishabour), 82 (624944), and 144 (627356) showed specific adaptation with relatively high yield, highlighting their suitability for drought-prone conditions. In contrast, genotypes 90 (625047) and 224 (Koohdasht) were located near the ideal genotype, combining high yield with broad adaptation across environments. This distinction emphasizes the importance of selecting both specifically adapted and widely adapted genotypes in breeding programs.

Based on the AMMI2 biplot and GGE biplot, N2019, N2020, and N2017 were characterized by relatively shorter vectors, indicating lower discriminating ability and more consistent genotype responses across these environments, which may facilitate the identification of broadly adapted genotypes. In contrast, environments such as S2017 and S2018 exhibited shorter vectors, suggesting limited contribution to genotype differentiation and potentially lower efficiency in discriminating among genotypes in future trials60. Moreover, the predominantly acute angles among environment vectors indicated positive correlations and the prevalence of non-crossover GEI, suggesting relatively stable genotype rankings across environments61,62.

Although all trials were conducted at a single location, limiting spatial inference, the GGE biplot still provided practical insights by identifying drought-adapted genotypes, broadly adapted lines, and efficient test environments for selection under contrasting moisture conditions.

Overall, the convergence among AMMI-, GSI-, BLUP-, WAASB-, MTSI-, and GGE-based analyses strengthens confidence in the identified superior genotypes. In particular, genotypes 90 (625047), 234 (Naz), 153 (627423), and 204 (Dayhim) were consistently recognized as high-yielding and stable across multiple methods. These genotypes represent valuable genetic resources for future wheat breeding programs aimed at improving yield stability under both rainfed and well-watered conditions.

Conclusion

This multi-environment study confirmed the strong influence of genotype × environment interaction on wheat yield performance, highlighting the necessity of combining multiple analytical approaches for reliable genotype selection. Across all applied methods, genotype 90 (625047) was consistently identified as the most stable and high-yielding genotype, indicating its strong potential as a candidate for direct release or use as a parent in breeding programs targeting broad adaptation. In addition, genotypes 204 (Dayhim) and 153 (627423), along with 234 (Naz), demonstrated high and stable performance across diverse environments and can be recommended as valuable genetic resources for improving yield stability under both rainfed and well-watered conditions. The integration of AMMI, WAASB, BLUP, GSI, MTSI, and GGE analyses proved effective in capturing both general and specific adaptation patterns, enabling more precise identification of superior genotypes. These findings provide a practical framework for breeders to select robust and widely adapted lines, as well as to exploit specific genotypes for targeted environments. Overall, the identified genotypes offer promising opportunities for enhancing wheat productivity and resilience in variable agro-climatic conditions of Iran.

Material and method

Plant materials, locations and experimental design

A total of 266 Iranian wheat accessions were evaluated in this study, comprising 86 officially released cultivars developed between 1942 and 2014, along with 180 traditional landraces originally collected between 1931 and 1968 (Supplementary Table 5). These accessions represent a broad genetic base and were selected to assess genotype × environment interaction (GEI), yield stability, and adaptability under contrasting moisture conditions.

Field trials were conducted at the agricultural research fields of the Department of Agronomy and Plant Breeding, University of Tehran, located in Karaj, Iran (35.80°N, 50.95°E). An alpha-lattice design with two replications was used to improve precision and minimize experimental error. Plant materials were provided by the University of Tehran and the Seed and Plant Improvement Institute (SPII), Karaj. The study area has a temperate climate, and detailed weather data for the experimental years, including rainfall and temperature, are presented in Supplementary Table 6 to describe the environmental conditions during the study period63,64.

Multi-environment trials were conducted across nine environments under both well-watered and rain-fed conditions to capture a wide range of environmental variability. Under rain-fed conditions, experiments were carried out over four cropping seasons (2016–2017, 2017–2018, 2018–2019, and 2019–2020). Under well-watered conditions, trials were conducted over five cropping seasons (2015–2016, 2016–2017, 2017–2018, 2018–2019, and 2019–2020). This multi-year, multi-environment setup enabled a robust evaluation of genotype performance, adaptability, and yield stability under varying moisture regimes, thereby improving the reliability of GEI analysis.

Experimental trial

Plant development was assessed using the Zadoks growth scale. The number of days to emergence (Zadoks 12), heading (Zadoks 50), anthesis (Zadoks 65), and physiological maturity (Zadoks 91) was recorded. The grain filling period was determined based on the time required for 50% of plants within each plot to reach the respective developmental stages. After harvesting, grain yield and associated traits, including spike weight, spike area, seed number per spike, and thousand kernel weight, were measured across all experimental years.

Statistical analysis

Grain yield data were subjected to combined analysis of variance (ANOVA), and the significance of genotype × environment interaction (GEI) was tested using the F-test. All analyses were performed using SAS statistical software65. AMMI-based stability parameters, including eigenvector values (EV;66), Annicchiarico’s D parameter (Da;10), sum of the absolute values of interaction principal component scores (SIPC;67), ASV68, Zhang’s D parameter (DZ;69), ASTAB70, average sum of absolute GEI effects modeled by AMMI (AVAMGE; [91]), fitted AMMI stability index (FA;71), relative contribution of IPCs (Za;71), ASI72, MASI73, MASV73, and the Genotype Stability Index (GSI;11) were calculated using the metan package in R version 4.2.174. Best Linear Unbiased Prediction (BLUP)-based parameters were used to assess stability and adaptability, including HMGV (harmonic mean of genotypic values), RPGV (relative performance of genotypic values), and HMRPGV (harmonic mean of relative performance of genotypic values), which simultaneously evaluate yield, stability, and adaptability75. A combined approach integrating AMMI and BLUP, known as WAASB (weighted average of absolute scores from the singular value decomposition of the matrix of BLUPs for GEI effects), was used to identify stable genotypes. The multi-trait stability index (MTSI), as proposed by Olivoto15, was used to rank genotypes based on multiple traits simultaneously. GGE biplots were constructed based on the first two principal components derived from G + GEI effects to visualize genotype performance and stability across environments. Spearman’s rank correlation and principal component analysis (PCA) were performed to assess relationships among stability statistics using the ‘corrplot’ and the ‘factoextra’ packages in R, respectively.
