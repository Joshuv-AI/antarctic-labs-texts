Thermally-modulated dissipation dilution in 2D NEMS resonators with explicit model and temperature-stable damping
Authors: Pengcheng Zhang, Luming Wang, Jiankai Zhu, Yueyang Jia, Bo Xu, Zenghui Wang, Rui Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractStrain-diluted dissipation has emerged as a promising technique to modulate the quality (Q) factor of resonant nanoelectromechanical systems (NEMS). However, comprehensive understanding and precise control of this effect under varying temperatures have remained elusive. Here we investigate the temperature-modulated dissipation dilution mechanisms in two-dimensional (2D) NEMS resonators. We develop an explicit temperature-dependent dissipation dilution model highlighting the roles of thermally-induced strain and temperature fluctuations during vibration, which well captures experimental observations: with temperature increasing from 77 K to 355 K, Q factor in graphene resonators first decreases and then increases, while that in molybdenum disulfide (MoS2) resonators monotonically decreases. Furthermore, based on the model, we design a graphene–MoS2 heterostructure NEMS resonator with near-zero effective thermal expansion, which experimentally exhibits high temperature stability in both frequency and Q factor. Our results advance the understanding of dissipation dilution mechanisms and pave the way for developing thermally-stable resonant transducers and logic components. Subject terms: NEMS, Nanosensors

Introduction

Optimizing the quality (Q) factor is critical for mechanical resonators/oscillators, which is strongly correlated with energy dissipation, resonance linewidth, signal-to-noise ratio (SNR), stability, and response speed1,2, all essential for advanced sensing and signal processing applications3. Dissipation dilution synergizes static tensile strain with dynamic strain4, providing effective strategies and straightforward guidelines to modulate Q. It has been demonstrated that dissipation dilution predominantly occurs through elongation or tensile stress5–8. For instance, by tuning large tensile strain, Q has been significantly modulated in resonant microelectromechanical/nanoelectromechanical systems (M/NEMS) at room temperature—including Si3N4 resonators9, Si3N4 membranes10,11, Si nanostring resonators12, diamond resonators13, and carbon nanotube resonators14,15, resulting in a higher Q desirable in radio-frequency (RF) filter and oscillator applications16, or a larger damping which has been reported to enhance the sensing performance17 and decrease the response time18,19. Therefore, it is critical to precisely model Q, in order to tune Q towards different requirements of various applications.

Emerging two-dimensional (2D) materials such as graphene and molybdenum disulfide (MoS2) provide unique opportunities to explore strain-diluted dissipation, thanks to their atomic-scale thickness, large strain limit over 10%, and ultrahigh sensitivity to tensile strain20–26,27. 2D graphene NEMS resonators have demonstrated Q tuning of more than ten-fold via joule-heating-induced strain28, and 2D MoS2 NEMS resonators show 448% Q tuning using gate voltage29. Due to the exceptional strain sensitivity of 2D NEMS resonators, a larger Q tuning range should be expected, facilitating the investigation of dissipation dilution mechanism for these 2D NEMS resonators.

Despite these advances, in real-world applications where the environment temperature varies, frequency and Q fluctuation with temperature is often detrimental for device performance. Therefore, it is important to understand and control the temperature dependence of dissipation. While 2D NEMS resonators have been measured at low temperatures30, the physical mechanisms underlying strain-diluted dissipation, particularly the temperature dependence and its interplay with thermoelastic damping (QTED−1), remain largely underexplored. Consequently, NEMS resonators with high temperature stability of Q factor have not yet been designed or achieved.

In this study, we harness 2D NEMS resonators’ ultrahigh sensitiveness to external strain to investigate their temperature-dependent strain-diluted dissipation, focusing on graphene, MoS2, and graphene–MoS2 heterostructure resonators. We explore their dynamic behaviors across a broad temperature range (77 K to 355 K), and the results reveal distinctive and unconventional dissipation trends, which is highly correlated with dissipation dilution due to thermally-induced strain. Specifically, the temperature-dependent Q in graphene NEMS resonators first decreases and then increases with increasing temperature, in contrast to previous reports where a higher Q factor is always achieved at lower temperatures31,32. We develop an explicit thermally modulated dissipation dilution model by incorporating both thermally-induced static strain and temperature-dependent thermal fluctuations, which well explains the observed trends. Furthermore, based on the model, we design and demonstrate a graphene–MoS2 heterostructure resonator that exhibits unprecedented Q-factor stability and fast and stable response time across various temperatures. Our temperature-dependent dissipation dilution model provides important guidelines for designing NEMS resonators towards robust temperature stability and desirable dissipation properties for sensing, communications, memory, and computing applications under diverse environmental temperature conditions.

Results and Discussion

To investigate strain-diluted dissipation mechanisms in mechanical resonators, we model and measure fully-clamped circular 2D NEMS resonators, where tensile strain effects are especially pronounced (see Materials and Methods Section for details about the fabrication). The choice of resonator materials, i.e., graphene, MoS2, and their bimorph heterostructures (Fig. 1a‒c), is primarily based on their contrasting thermal expansion coefficients (TECs): across a wide temperature range, MoS2 exhibits a positive TEC (αMoS2)33, while graphene shows a negative TEC (αGraphene) (Fig. 1d, e)34,35. Such contrast enables the investigation of dissipation tuning by various thermally-induced strain. These two materials further facilitate the construction of heterostructures (Fig. 1f, i) to engineer the effective TEC (αeff). The variations in thermally induced strain can lead to distinct dynamic responses among devices, including material-dependent shifts in resonance frequency (Fig. 1). We also take into account the thermal expansion of the Si substrate in our analysis. For the MoS2 resonator, the positive TEC of MoS2 is higher than that of the Si substrate, so the αeff is always positive, and throughout the temperature range, the tensile strain always decreases as temperature increases (Fig. 1a, d, g). In contrast, for the graphene resonator, in the temperature range we consider, as temperature increases, due to the negative TEC of graphene and the shift from negative to positive TEC of the Si substrate, the αeff changes from positive to negative. This leads to a shift from net expansion to contraction, causing the tensile strain to first decrease and then increase as temperature rises (Fig. 1b, e, h). Leveraging such contrast, the graphene–MoS2 heterostructure resonator should exhibit reduced or even near-zero αeff due to the cancelation of thermal contraction/expansion with respect to Si, at least within a certain temperature range (Fig. 1c, f, i). To achieve the near-zero αeff, the thickness ratio should be optimized (Supporting Information Section S2.2), which leads to Rt=tMoS2/tgraphene of about 2.2 using typical material parameters.

We next experimentally examine the above theoretical predictions by performing temperature-varying resonance measurements from 77 K to 355 K for MoS2 and graphene NEMS resonators. The initial tensile strain at T0 = 300 K is extracted through gate-tunable resonance frequency measurements (Materials and Methods Section, and Fig. S2a). During the temperature sweep, we maintain linear resonance response during measurements to ensure accurate extraction of resonance frequency and Q factor (Fig. S2b)38, and at each temperature the system is allowed to stabilize for ~ 5 min before measurement.

We first examine the MoS2 resonators. They exhibit a monotonic temperature dependence: with increasing temperature, the resonance frequency continuously decreases, and the rate of change ∣Δf/ΔT∣ increases with temperature (Fig. 2a and S6), consistent with the expectation based on the thermal expansion (Fig. 1d, g), and with the strain evolution extracted from experimental data (Fig. S7). These MoS2 resonators exhibit a monotonic decrease in Q with increasing temperature (Fig. 2b‒d and Fig. S6b‒c), as expected due to the lower tensile strain and increased energy dissipation at elevated temperatures39.

We next examine the graphene drumhead resonators (Fig. S1a‒c). In clear contrast to MoS2 resonators, the graphene devices exhibit a strikingly different temperature-dependent behavior: with increasing temperature, the resonance frequency and Q initially decrease and then increase (Fig. 3a‒e), with a turn near 265 K. Such a distinct “V”-shaped temperature dependence is reproducibly observed in all three graphene resonators measured (Fig. S3). It is also worth noting that the observed Q‒T curves are in sharp contrast to prior reports where lower temperatures consistently produce higher Q31,32,40.

To understand the observed behavior of graphene resonators, we extract the αeff from the temperature-dependent resonance frequency using a strain–frequency model (Supporting Information Section S2.2). We find that the resonance frequency tuning is primarily due to thermally-induced strain (Supporting Information Section S2.1), with the Young’s modulus (EY) and mass density (ρ) assumed to be constant41,42. We observe that the thermally-modulated tensile strain in graphene resonator (Fig. S4) well matches the shapes of the frequency and Q tuning curves (Fig. 3a‒e). Such nonmonotonic temperature dependence of Q suggests the onset of dissipation dilution: given the change of sign in αeff (positive αeff at cryogenic temperature and negative αeff at elevated temperature), the tensile strain first decreases and then increases with increasing temperature, reaching the minimum near 265 K. Therefore, whether the temperature is higher or lower than the 265 K, the resonator stores more elastic energy, thereby reducing the dissipation and increasing the Q.

Further analysis reveals a more intricate physical picture for graphene resonators. To understand this behavior, we extract the temperature–strain relation (Fig. 3f), where each given tensile strain value corresponds to two different temperatures (one each below and above 265 K). Furthermore, when plotting Q as a function of tensile strain or resonance frequency (Fig. 3g), we obtain two distinct branches. This implies that a single strain can be associated with two different Q factors. Interestingly, at any given strain or resonance frequency, the Q factors in the “cold” branch (below 265 K) are consistently higher than the corresponding Q factors measured in the “hot” branch (above 265 K). The existence of two distinct Q–strain and Q–frequency correspondences within the same device cannot be explained by existing dissipation dilution theory. The phenomenon strongly suggests the presence of an unconventional dissipation mechanism that functions differently in the low- and high-temperature regimes.

The key towards understanding this seemingly unusual behavior is revealed in Fig. 3g, where there is coexistence of two distinct Q values for the same strain level: for each frequency in Fig. 3g, while the static tensile strain is determined, dynamic thermal effects vary significantly depending on temperature. Based on this revelation, we derive a dissipation dilution theory from the classical Zener model43, with modifications for the thermal strain effects in these 2D resonators. In our model, stress inhomogeneities in a vibrating membrane induce temperature gradients, leading to localized heat currents. Due to the viscoelastic behavior, strain does not instantaneously reach equilibrium during vibration but constantly undergoes a relaxation process. Under an oscillating stress, the lag between strain and stress results in thermoelastic damping (QTED−1), which is strongly influenced by the external temperature and the induced thermal strain44, and thus the overall energy dissipation and Q show clear temperature dependence.

Our theoretical model quantitatively describes such temperature-dependent energy dissipation by including the effects of dynamic thermal fluctuations or anharmonic phonon interactions. Specifically, we divide the total strain into two parts: a static tensile strain due to αeff, and a vibration-induced dynamic strain. To accurately explain and model the Q vs. T relationship, all the temperature-dependent parameters such as α2D and Cv are incorporated into the strain-modulated dissipation dilution framework.

Using this model, the measured temperature dependence of the Q in graphene resonators can be well reproduced by the fitting Q–T and Q–frequency curves (Fig. 3e–g, S3b–c, and S3e–f). This shows that our model effectively explains the emergence of the distinct “cold” and “hot” Q branches in Fig. 3g: lower temperatures are associated with reduced thermal fluctuations, thereby suppressing energy dissipation. Meanwhile, at identical tensile strain levels or resonance frequencies, the mechanical energy stored in the suspended membrane remains approximately constant. As a result, the “cold” branch consistently exhibits higher Q factors than the “hot” branch for every single strain value (Fig. 3g, S11c, d). The model also well describes the monotonic decrease of Q as temperature increases in MoS2 resonators, given the combined effect of higher temperature and reduced tensile strain.

With this theoretical tool, we can design 2D NEMS resonators with tailored temperature response in damping. One useful example is towards sensing and computing applications, in which maintaining stable frequency and Q under varying temperatures is important for avoiding temperature-induced performance drift. Furthermore, recent reports demonstrate that low Q can sometimes be desirable for enhancing the signal-to-noise ratio, stability, and response speed in NEMS sensors17–19. We therefore design and fabricate a graphene–MoS2 heterostructure NEMS resonator to achieve a stable frequency and Q across a range of temperatures, which would not increase upon cooling (Fig. 4a, b). We optimize the thickness ratio using our model (Supporting Information Section S5) to achieve temperature stability for both strain and frequency, which in turn stabilizes the strain-dependent elastic energy U. The resonance-frequency stability is mainly governed by thermal-strain compensation, while the Q-factor stability is not only related to the near-zero thermal stress ΔΓth which stabilizes the stored elastic energy, but also involves the temperature-independent or weakly temperature-dependent damping term Qother⁻1, which is important especially with the interlayer-friction- and interface-related dissipation due to the additional graphene–MoS2 interface (Fig. S11e, f)45,46.

We then experimentally demonstrate the superb temperature stability for both frequency and Q in these tailor-designed devices. Measurements at varying temperatures for graphene, MoS2, and graphene–MoS2 heterostructure resonators across the 242K–350 K temperature range during the cooling cycle are shown in Fig. 4c‒i. In clear contrast to both the MoS2 and graphene devices, the heterostructure resonator exhibits minimal resonance frequency and Q drift over the entire temperature range (Fig. 4e, h, and S13). Additional temperature-sweep measurements during the heating cycle are performed for the same graphene–MoS2 heterostructure resonator (Fig. S15). The heating-cycle data show no obvious hysteresis with the cooling cycle data in resonance frequency, confirming the reproducibility of the near-zero temperature coefficient of frequency in the heterostructure device. This demonstrates that it is possible to design and realize NEMS resonators whose resonance frequency and Q values are both robust and immune to temperature variations. The moderate Q factor can enable faster sensing, as manifested by the shorter ring-up/ring-down time τ0 = Q/(πf0) compared with the MoS2 or graphene devices (Fig. 4i). Interestingly, such response time also exhibits excellent temperature stability, thus allowing robust and highly stable device performance across a wide range of temperature. The relative contributions of QTED−1 and Qother−1 are separated by fitting to the experimentally measured Q at varying temperatures. The results are consistent with previous low-temperature nanomechanical-resonator studies, where resonators measured at millikelvin temperatures exhibit very high Q factors by removing thermal effects20,30,32,47. Therefore, additional measurements of these 2D NEMS resonators at temperatures close to absolute zero could further reduce QTED−1 and elucidate damping mechanisms in Qother−1.

The temperature-dependent dissipation dilution model incorporating both thermally-induced strain and temperature fluctuation should be applicable to other 2D materials48,49. Based on the different thermal expansion coefficient, the temperature dependence of the Q factor can be well predicted by the model, which has been verified using graphene and MoS2 NEMS resonators. Furthermore, thermal-strain compensation strategy demonstrated here is not limited to graphene–MoS2 heterostructures. More generally, 2D heterostructure resonators can be designed by combining materials with opposite thermal expansion coefficients. Besides graphene, other measured or theoretically predicted negative-TEC 2D materials, such as h-BN and blue phosphorene50,51, may also be considered as the negative-TEC layer. These materials can potentially be paired with positive-TEC 2D materials, including MoS2 and other transition metal dichalcogenides such as WS2, WSe2, or MoSe252,53. In such mechanically coupled heterostructure membranes54, the effective thermal expansion coefficient is determined by the stiffness- and thickness-weighted contributions of all constituent layers, after considering the substrate thermal expansion effect (Supporting Information Sections S2.2 and S5). Therefore, by selecting suitable material pairs and optimizing their thickness ratios, the net thermal-strain variation can be minimized over a target temperature range.

Overall, the ability to passively stabilize both resonance frequency and Q factor is important for practical NEMS applications55–57. In resonant mass sensors and other frequency-shift-based sensing applications, suppressing temperature-induced frequency drift can reduce error and improve measurement reliability3,58. In RF resonators and oscillators, stable frequency and linewidth are essential for maintaining signal stability under varying environmental conditions. In addition, a stable and moderate Q factor leads to a stable response time, which is beneficial for fast sensing and resonance-frequency tracking17–19. Such stable transient dynamics can also benefit mechanical memory and computing devices that require reproducible switching and signal propagation24. Therefore, the heterostructure-based thermal-strain compensation strategy provides a material-level route toward thermally robust 2D NEMS devices without relying solely on active temperature-control or compensation circuits.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates an explicit analytical model of temperature-dependent dissipation dilution, which reveals the interplay between thermal-strain-induced dissipation dilution and temperature-fluctuation-modulated thermoelastic damping during vibration. The temperature-dependent dissipation dilution model well explains the unconventional increase of Q factor with higher temperature, and the different Q factors obtained at the same strain or frequency at different temperatures, in graphene NEMS resonators. Guided by the model, we experimentally design and demonstrate graphene–MoS2 heterostructure NEMS resonators with unprecedented thermal stability of frequency and Q, surpassing those for non-engineered devices reported in the literature (Table S2). Such an approach can be further expanded to include other 1D/2D materials, 2D heterostructures59,60, and even bulk materials, for achieving thermally stable performance while maintaining the functionality for specific applications, through proper material choice and structure design. As wafer-scale synthesis and transfer of 2D materials become increasingly mature61–63, integrating these resonators into larger-scale arrays or hybrid systems could enable the development of next-generation energy-efficient systems.

Materials and Methods

Fabrication process

The fabrication starts from the circular microtrenches defined lithographically and etched using reactive ion etching (RIE) on an oxidized silicon wafer with 300 nm thermal SiO2. The contact electrodes are then defined using photolithography followed by evaporation of a 5 nm Ti adhesion layer and a 50 nm gold layer. We exfoliate the 2D materials from bulk crystals (Shenzhen Six Carbon Technology Co., Ltd), and identify the suitable flakes on the polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) stamps. We then transfer the 2D materials onto the microtrenches and in contact with the electrodes under an optical microscope using a 3D transfer platform (Shanghai OnWay Technology Co., Ltd), forming the suspended 2D NEMS resonators.

Resonance measurements

After wire bonding, the devices are mounted in a vacuum chamber with an optical window. The RF driving voltage vRF is generated by a network analyzer (AGILENT HP3577A), the DC gate voltage VGS is provided by a DC voltage source (ITECH IT6322B). We measure the driven resonances by applying VGS and vRF through a bias tee to the highly doped silicon back gate, with the contact electrode to the 2D material grounded. The vibrations of the NEMS resonators are measured using a custom-built laser interferometry system in vacuum. The optical interferometry signal is converted to electrical signal using a photodetector (FEMTO HCA-S-200M-SI-FS) and then sent back to the network analyzer for driven resonance measurements. The thicknesses of the MoS2 and graphene membranes are measured using atomic force microscopy (AFM).

Temperature-Variable Measurement

We first ramp the devices from room temperature (~300 K) to 400 K and anneal them under high vacuum at 400 K for 10 hours. Afterwards, the temperature is lowered in 5 K steps. At each step, we measure the resonance response only after the temperature has been stable for at least 5 min and the resonance shows no discernible frequency drift.
