Repairing the defective folding of alpha-sarcoglycan is needed to promote myogenic cell engraftment in 3D artificial muscle models of LGMDR3
Authors: Edoardo Maghin, Alberto Benetollo, Martina Scano, Paola Caccin, Eugenia Carraro, Martina Piccoli, Dorianna Sandonà
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Limb-girdle muscular dystrophy 2D (or LGMDR3) is a rare autosomal recessive disorder caused by mutations in the SGCA gene, which encode α-sarcoglycan (α-SG). α-SG is a critical component of the dystrophin-associated protein complex, whose role in differentiated muscle is to distribute contraction force and protect the sarcolemma from mechanical damage. Most SGCA mutations are missense, leading to a folding-defective α-SG that is degraded by the ubiquitin-proteasome system, destabilizing the sarcolemma and causing progressive muscle weakness. Notably, pharmacological restoration of α-SG function using cystic fibrosis transmembrane conductance regulator (CFTR) correctors, such as C17, can rescue the SG-complex, improving muscle strength in an LGMDR3 mouse model. Our initial aim was to generate 3D diaphragm-like models of LGMDR3 by seeding patient-derived myoblasts onto a decellularised diaphragm scaffold, thereby mimicking the disease environment and enabling drug screening beyond the limitations of 2D cultures. While the models did not behave as anticipated, the unexpected outcome led us to uncover a previously underappreciated role of α-SG. Specifically, we found that α-SG expressed by immature myoblasts is crucial for cell adhesion and migration, key processes for muscle development, regeneration, and successful engraftment into a decellularized extracellular matrix. These processes, compromised in LGMDR3 cells, can be rescued through CFTR correctors, further supporting their potential therapeutic application in LGMDR3.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13395-025-00408-z.

Introduction

Mutations in the α-sarcoglycan gene (SGCA) cause limb-girdle muscular dystrophy 2D (LGMD2D), also known as LGMDR3 [1], a rare autosomal recessive muscle wasting disorder primarily affecting muscles of the shoulder and pelvic girdles. The onset occurs in childhood; it is progressive and often forces the affected subjects to use a wheelchair [2]. Along with β-, γ-, and δ-sarcoglycan (SG), α-SG forms a tetramer that is a key component of the dystrophin-associated protein complex (DAPC). This complex plays a crucial role in distributing the force generated during muscle contraction and acts as a shock absorber, protecting the sarcolemma from damage caused by the mechanical stress due to repeated contractions [3, 4].

The majority of SGCA disease-causing defects are missense mutations, and it has been observed that a single amino acid substitution may lead to a folding-defective α-SG, recognised by the cell quality control and delivered to the ubiquitin-proteasome system of muscle cells for degradation [5–8]. Consequently, the SG complex is disrupted and unable to traffic to the cell membrane. This results in sarcolemma instability, muscle fibre damage, and progressive muscle weakness [9, 10]. Moreover, it is known that human myoblasts isolated from muscle biopsies express α-SG during proliferation, and reduction or absence of α-SG expression from the sarcolemma alters the localization of the fibroblast growth factor receptor 1 (FGFR1), leading to its intracellular accumulation [11]. Since FGFR1 is present in both myogenic and non-myogenic cells in adult skeletal muscle [12], and muscle development and homeostasis are severely affected when FGFR1 is absent [13], insufficient presence of α-SG on the plasma membrane of LGMDR3 muscle cells may indicate that SGs play a crucial role not only for the main tissue function, i.e., sarcolemma protection during muscle contraction, but also in muscle development, homeostasis and regeneration.

The unveiling of the pathogenic mechanism of LGMDR3 allowed the identification of potential novel druggable targets and evidenced that most of the missense mutants are still functional if prevented from being degraded [7, 8]. In this context, we recently demonstrated that small molecules, known as cystic fibrosis transmembrane conductance regulator (CFTR) correctors, are effective in recovering missense mutants of α-SG. This was shown in vitro using both HEK cell models and human myogenic cells derived from subjects with LGMDR3-4 [14–16]. Additionally, in vivo treatment with the CFTR corrector C17 of an LGMDR3 mouse model [17, 18] led to a reduction in myopathic features at the histological level, the rerouting of the SG complex at the sarcolemma, and the recovery of muscular strength. Considering that a mild disease phenotype seems to correlate with a residual amount of SGs at the sarcolemma [10, 19], it was reasonable to suppose that the recovery of some quantity of protein is sufficient to assure the amelioration of muscle histological phenotype and function [15, 18].

Since several CFTR correctors are already approved for use in cystic fibrosis treatment [20, 21], their application in other diseases may lead to shorter clinical approval timelines. Therefore, a translational study that demonstrates the therapeutic potential of these molecules using in vitro muscle models, which closely replicate the muscular environment of patients with LGMDR3, is of utmost importance.

In the last decades, tissue engineering approaches were employed to generate 3D in vitro models of different organs or tissues able to mimic the architecture and function of native ones. These models are valuable tools not only as replacements for in vivo transplant purposes but also as a pre-clinical setting to study disease development, progression, and response to treatment. 3D in vitro models offer a terrific chance to mimic living tissues and study pathological processes, overcoming the limitations of standard 2D cultures or non-human animal models. In recent years, many studies have been conducted using different skeletal muscle populations and natural-derived scaffolds to increase the structural complexity and biomechanical properties of 3D muscle models [22]. We recently demonstrated the possibility of producing in vitro a 3D diaphragm-like tissue by the combination of human myogenic cells and a biologic scaffold obtained through the decellularization of mouse diaphragmatic muscle [23]. This 3D model displays characteristic radially oriented myofibers and responds to physiological acetylcholine stimulation.

Nowadays, numerous studies are focusing on the translation from healthy to pathological models to obtain in vitro platforms for studying the disease in a context more similar to the patient’s condition [24, 25] and, whenever possible, exploring new drug therapies [26]. In this context, this research was initially aimed at generating both dystrophic and healthy 3D diaphragm models using primary LGMDR3 patient-derived myoblasts as well as diseased and healthy cells obtained through immortalization of the primary cells [27]. Thought, it ultimately led to the investigation of a novel role, played by α-SG at early stages of muscle development, when myogenic cells are proliferating, thus before they differentiate into myotubes and mature fibres. We demonstrate that the absence or low functional α-SG on mutated cells has a strong impact on basic myogenic cell behaviour, such as migration, substrate interaction and extracellular matrix engraftment, and that repairing α-SG function through the use of CFTR corrector C17 is necessary to allow diseased cells to recover the ability to adhere to the scaffold and produce 3D LGMDR3 diaphragm-like constructs.

Materials and methods

Cell culture and treatments

Primary human myogenic cells from an LGMDR3 patient were isolated from a bioptic fragment obtained from the Telethon Genetic Bio-Bank facility [8]. The patient was a male, 18 months old at the time of biopsy. Immortalization of myoblasts was performed at the Institute de Myologie in Paris, France, according to a consolidated protocol [27] and upon the subscription of a material transfer agreement. The same Institution provided immortalized myoblasts from an adult healthy control. Conversely, primary normal human skeletal myoblasts were purchased (Gibco-Fischer Scientific).

Immortalized LGMDR3 cells were grown in proliferating medium (PM) composed of F-12 Nut Mix (1X) + GlutaMAX-I (Gibco, 31765027), supplemented with 20% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco-Fisher Scientific), 0.2 µg/mL dexamethasone (Promocell), 5 ng/mL bFGF (Gibco), 5 µg/mL insulin (Gibco-Fisher Scientific), 5 ng/mL EGF (Gibco), 25 µg/mL Fetuin (Promocell), and 1% pen/strep (Gibco-Fisher Scientific). Healthy cells and primary patient-derived LGDMR3 cells were grown in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM 1X) + GlutaMAX-I, supplemented with 30% FBS, 5 ng/mL bFGF, 10 µg/mL insulin, 5 ng/mL EGF, and 1% pen/strep. Flasks were maintained at 37 °C, 5% CO2 with an oxygen tension of 20% in humidified incubator. Cell differentiation was achieved substituting PM with differentiation medium (DM) composed of DMEM + GlutaMAX-I high glucose supplemented with 10 µg/mL insulin, 1% pen/strep, 2% Horse Serum (HS; Gibco-Thermo Scientific), and 0.1 mg/mL transferrin (Sigma-Aldrich).

Chemicals and treatment

CFTR corrector C17 was from Exclusive Chemistry (Kiryat Motzkin) and DMSO (Sigma-Aldrich). CFTR corrector C17 was dissolved in DMSO at a 1000X concentration to have the same amount of vehicle (1‰) after the dilution at the final effective concentration of 15 µM. The molecule was added for 48 h, either as a single dose or in multiple doses, during the proliferation and/or differentiation phases.

Flow cytometry analysis

Cell surface antigen expression was analysed via flow cytometry using cultivated cells detached by trypsin-EDTA treatment. Briefly, the cell suspension was incubated with human CD56-PE (BD Bioscience) and 7-aminoactinomycin D (7AAD, BD Bioscience) for 15 min at 4 °C in the dark. After a washing step, cells were resuspended in 1X phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, Gibco-Fisher Scientific); acquisition and analyses were performed using an Accuri C6 flow cytometer (Becton Dickinson).

Immunofluorescence on 2D cultured cells

To detect and quantify the expression of proliferation and myogenic markers, 5 × 103 cells were seeded in a 96-well plate in PM and analysed after 4 days of culture. To characterize myogenic differentiation abilities, cells were seeded in a 24-well plate and grown in PM for 4 days and for an additional 11 days in DM. Briefly, cells were washed 2 times with 1X PBS, permeabilized with Triton X, saturated with goat serum and stained with primary and secondary antibodies listed in Supplementary Table 1. Cell nuclei were counterstained with Hoechst 33,342 (Gibco-Fisher Scientific). Images were taken using Leica B5000 (Leica) and Zeiss Axio Observer (Zeiss) microscopes and quantified with ImageJ software.

Growth curve and doubling time

Growth curve: 15 × 103 cells were seeded in a 24-well plate in PM. For each time point (24, 48, 72, 96 h) at least 4 wells for each different condition were detached and counted. Doubling time: 15 × 103 cells were seeded in a 24-well plate in PM. After 48 h cells were detached, counted and re-plated at the initial density concentration; the same cells were then recounted and re-plated after other 48 h. α-SG membrane Immunofluorescence

To detect α-SG membrane location, immunofluorescence was performed on living cells and then fixed. Briefly, cells were incubated for 30 min at 4 °C. Gently washings were performed with ice-cold PBS + Ca-Mg, and then cells were incubated with the primary antibody against α-SG (Novocastra) in 0.5% BSA in PBS + Ca-Mg for 4 h at 4 °C. Incubation with the secondary antibody was performed for 1.5 h at 4 °C. After the gentle washing steps, cells were fixed for 15 min with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) at room temperature. The final washing step with NH4CL 50mM was performed for 10 min before PBS addition and microscope analysis.

Biotinylating membrane proteins

The biotinylating reaction was conducted according to [16]. At the end of C17 treatments, primary and immortalized LGMDR3 and healthy cells were incubated at 4 °C for at least 10 min, and all the procedures were performed at this temperature to slow the cellular processes and the cell membrane protein recycling. After three washes with ice-cold PBS, supplemented with Ca2+ and Mg2+, cells were incubated with a solution of 0.5 mg/mL biotin (EZ-Link Sulfo-456 NHS-LC-Biotin, Thermo Fisher Scientific) in PBS for 20 min at 4 °C, under gentle rotation. The reaction was stopped by incubating with 100 mM glycine for 10 min, then the glycine solution was removed, and two washes with PBS were performed. Finally, cells were lysed with RIPA buffer, and lysates were centrifuged at 15,000 g at 4 °C for 30 min. The supernatants were incubated with streptavidin agarose beads (Thermo Fisher Scientific) (20 µL of resin for each sample), overnight at 4 °C under rotation. The next day, the streptavidin-resin was washed three times with RIPA buffer, and proteins were eluted with Laemmli sample buffer for the WB analysis.

Western blot analysis for α-SG quantification

Proteins were resolved by SDS-PAGE, blotted onto a 20 μm nitrocellulose membrane and probed with primary antibody (rabbit monoclonal anti-alpha-sarcoglycan, AB189254, Abcam). Secondary antibodies were horseradish peroxidase-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (Sigma-Aldrich), and blots were developed with Trident Femto chemiluminescent substrate from Genetex (Irvine). Chemiluminescent signals were digitally acquired with the Alliance Mini HD9 Imaging System (Uvitec), and densitometry analysis was performed with the ImageJ software.

EDTA detachment assay

About 1 × 105 cells/well in 24-well plates were seeded and coloured with HOECHST for 15 min. After 72 h, cells were detached by adding trypsin or 0.5 mM EDTA for 1, 3 and 5 minutes. Hole well images were taken using Zeiss Axio Observer before and after cell detachment. Quantification of cell detachment was performed using ImageJ software.

Cell detachment from ECM-coated plates

Cells were seeded and analysed using a commercial assay, MAPTrix™ Screen for Cell Adhesion, 24 different ECM-mimetic (Supplementary Table 2) coated 96 well-plates (Amsbio). Briefly, the MAPTrix screen plate was re-hydrated with PBS at room temperature for 20 min. About 5 × 104 cells/well pre-stained with HOECHST for 15 min were seeded for 30 min. Non-adherent cells were gently removed with 3 times PBS washes. The percentage of adherent cells was determined by measuring Hoechst fluorescence before and after the washing steps. Images were taken with a Zeiss Axio Observer, and quantification of adherent cells was performed using ImageJ software.

Cell tracking assay

Cell tracking was monitored by seeding 1.5 × 104 cells/well in 24 multi-well plates. Cells were maintained in PM after nuclei staining with HOECHST for 15 min. The distance travelled by each cell was calculated by 30-minute serial image acquisitions for a total of 15 h. Time lapses were acquired with a Leica B5000 microscope, and video analyses were performed with ImageJ software.

Wound healing assay

Home-made wound healing inserts, fabricated with a 3D bioprinter (FormLabs 3BPlus), were introduced in each well of a 24-well plate. About 1 × 105 cells, pre-stained with HOECHST, were seeded in each well of the 24-well plate and incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2 in a humidified incubator. After 24 h post-seeding, the insert was removed, and a gap was generated. The gap closure was monitored using Leica B5000, by taking images after 24-, 48- and 72-hours post-insert removal.

Animals and ethics statement

Diaphragm muscles were obtained from 3- to 6-month-old C57BL/6j wild-type (wt) mice [Protocols N. 1103/2016 and N. 418/2020-PR approved by Animal wellness local ethics committee (Organismo per il Benessere Animale - OPBA, University of Padova and Fondazione Istituto di Ricerca Pediatrica Città della Speranza) and Italian Ministry of Health.

dECM recellularization

Diaphragm decellularization was performed using a detergent enzymatic protocol in accordance with a previously published study [28]. After decellularization, diaphragms were maintained in 1X PBS and immediately used or cryopreserved in liquid nitrogen. For recellularization, we adapted and improved the dynamic setting of our homemade bioreactor platform previously used in Maghin et al. [23]. Wild-type decellularized diaphragmatic sections were manually sutured to a circular “Orto Botanico” PDMS membrane. About 1 × 106 cells were resuspended in 40 µL of resuspension medium (13% Collagen type I, 10% Fibronectin, 10% IGF-1 in PM) and injected into the decellularized scaffolds using a 30G needle syringe. After 3 h from the injection, the bioreactor chamber was assembled, and PM medium was added. The system was then connected with the hydraulic circuit governed by a tunable and programmable syringe pump (Kd Scientific). From day 0 to day 7, recellularized samples were stimulated with a slow ramp from 0% to 10% of mechanical strain. From day 8, a cyclic mechanical stimulation was applied, from 0% to 5% of mechanical strain in 30 min, and the withdrawal from 5% to 0% in 30 min followed by a resting period of 3 h. The medium was changed every 72 h, PM was used until day 6, then replaced with DM until day 14. In LGMDR3 + C17 samples, the CFTR corrector was added following the protocol set up in 2D culture. 3D recellularized samples were harvested after 7 or 14 days of culture.

3D sample fixation and Immunofluorescence

Recellularized samples were fixed in 4% PFA (Sigma) for 1 h at 4 °C. After progressive sugar dehydration (10, 15 and 30%), samples were included in O.C.T. (Kaltek). A cryostat (Leica CM1520) was used to obtain sections of 8–10 μm. Frozen sections were incubated with primary antibodies (1 h at 37 °C or overnight at 4 °C), washed 5 times in 1X PBS and then incubated for 1 h at RT with labelled Alexa Fluor secondary antibodies, as listed in Supplementary Table 1. After washing 5 times in 1X PBS, nuclei were counterstained with fluorescent mounting medium plus 100 ng/mL DAPI. For each diaphragm, random pictures were collected with an inverted microscope (Leica B5000). Fresh or decellularized diaphragms were used as controls.

Statistical analysis

Data are expressed as means ± s.e.m. or S.D. For 2D experiments, all experiments were conducted with a minimum of three independent biological replicates (n ≥ 3). The number of biological replicates (n) and technical replicates (m) for each experiment are specified in the respective figure captions. For 3D experiments and immunofluorescence analysis, a minimum of three independent biological replicates (n ≥ 3) with 15 random high-power field areas per analysed sample were considered. The number of biological replicates (n) for each experiment is specified in the respective figure captions. Using GraphPad Prism 6v, statistical significance was determined with a two-sided parametric Student’s t-test after the D’Agostino-Pearson normality test or non-parametric Mann-Whitney test. For western blot quantification, a 2-way ANOVA test with Šídák’s multiple comparisons was performed. A p-value below 0.05 was considered statistically significant (*, p < 0.05; **, p ≤ 0.01; ***, p ≤ 0.001; ****, p ≤ 0.0001).

Results

Characterization of LGMDR3 patient-derived myogenic cells

To obtain 3D muscle constructs, we began with primary cells isolated from muscle biopsies of a paediatric patient affected by LGMDR3. This patient is a compound heterozygote carrying the L31P mutation and the V247M mutation on the two SGCA alleles. Primary cells displayed a muscle phenotype, evidenced by the expression of CD56 and MYOD markers (Fig. 1A, C), but exhibited a low proliferation rate. Primary cells are sensitive to stress caused by extensive manipulation, and those derived from dystrophic patients with mutations in genes responsible for membrane integrity are particularly vulnerable. Patient-derived LGMDR3 cells, indeed, showed a mean doubling time of 188.7 ± 45.1 h (Fig. 1B) and a proportion of Ki67 + cells of 8.1 ± 0.8% (Fig. 1C) after only 8–9 passages in culture. As expected, LGMDR3 myoblasts expressed some α-SG protein, including the functional membrane form, both during proliferation (Fig. 1D, E) and after differentiation into myotubes (Fig. 1F, G), even though with significant differences compared to healthy primary myoblasts, as expected.

Given the slow proliferation rate and the need for a considerable number of cells for our tissue engineering purposes, we decided to move to immortalized cells to perform recellularization experiments and produce 3D LGMDR3 muscle constructs.

Characterization of healthy and LGMDR3 immortalized cells

After the immortalization of healthy and LGMDR3 primary cells [27], we performed several experiments to verify their general cellular behaviour and the specific myogenic phenotype. We obtained and characterized several clones from the initial immortalized population, and given the similar behaviour among these clonal lines (Supplementary Fig. 1), we decided to perform all the experimentation referring to a single clone (Clone #6). Both healthy and LGMDR3 immortalised cells demonstrated myogenic characteristics (Fig. 2A-D) and a high proliferation rate due to their immortalization, as indicated by the high percentage of Ki67 + cells (79.6 ± 2.8% for healthy samples and 59.1 ± 3.7% for the LGMDR3 sample). As expected, the total expression of the α-SG protein was lower in immortalized LGMDR3 cells; however, the amount of the membrane form was similar between healthy and myopathic proliferating cells (Fig. 2E-G).

Despite this similarity, immortalized LGMDR3 cells showed specific behaviours directly attributable to the genetic defect resulting in the misfolded α-SG. bFGF is known to be a mitogen factor able to increase, both in vitro and in vivo, the proliferation rate of a wide range of cell types [29–31]. Previous studies outlined that both mouse and human LGMD-derived myoblasts are insensitive to bFGF, given that its receptor is made unstable by the lack or poor persistence of α-SG on the membrane of myogenic cells [11]. In our hands, healthy cells correctly responded to the addition of bFGF in the culture media, decreasing their doubling time (from 53.3 h to 28.6 h) and increasing the total cell number during a 4-day growth curve (Fig. 2H, I). On the contrary, LGMDR3 cells resulted insensitive to the addition of bFGF, maintaining constant both the doubling time (38.3 h without bFGF vs. 41.2 h with bFGF) and the total cell number (Fig. 2J, K). Considering the similar amount of membrane α-SG resident in healthy and LGMDR3 cells, it is possible to infer that the impaired behaviour is mainly dependent on the quality of the α-SG membrane form. The mechanism was also supported by the visualization of FGFR1, that resulted expressed by almost all cultured healthy myogenic cells, while its signal was very low in the majority of LGMDR3 cells (Fig. 2L, M).

Given the characteristics of the two cell types and the conservation of the myopathic behaviour by immortalized LGMDR3 cells, we proceeded with the production of healthy and diseased 3D constructs starting from wild-type mouse dECM.

Recellularization of diaphragm dECM with healthy and LGMDR3 immortalized cells

Healthy and LGMDR3 cells were injected into wild-type mouse diaphragm dECM, and the constructs were cultivated for 7 days in the customized bioreactor performing a ramp of mechanical strain from 0% to 10% (Supplementary Fig. 2). In a previous study [23], this mechanical protocol allowed the spreading and migration of healthy primary myoblasts inside the scaffold. We confirmed that healthy immortalised cells were also able to repopulate the dECM, colonising the scaffold throughout its thickness. On the contrary, LGMDR3 cells were not capable of globally colonising the scaffold since we found a significantly lower cell number after 7 days of culture compared to healthy samples (210 ± 57 healthy cells/field vs. 97 ± 32 LGMDR3 cells/field), despite a similar expression of the proliferative marker Ki67 (Fig. 3F). Notably, the majority of the injected LGMDR3 cells spontaneously distributed only on the upper part of the construct, when compared to the healthy one (Fig. 3C-D), suggesting some migration impairment in the middle and lower part of the scaffold. Interestingly, we found the same difficulties when primary LGMDR3 patient cells were used to repopulate mouse dECM (Supplementary Fig. 3A). Conversely, immortalized Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD) cells behaved as healthy ones and repopulated the whole scaffold homogenously (Supplementary Fig. 3B). These data suggest a migratory impairment specifically related to the cell type rather than to a general myopathic phenotype or environmental impediments.

All these results suggest that the mutation responsible for LGMDR3 disturbs the α-SG protein, impacting a series of basic myoblast behaviours.

Docking and migration properties of healthy and LGMDR3 cells

Given the low number of LGMDR3 engrafted cells and their incapacity to repopulate the entire scaffold’s thickness, we investigated their properties in terms of spontaneous docking and migration behaviour. Mutations in the SGCA gene have been reported to affect the structure or folding of the α-SG protein, specifically modifying the extracellular portion presenting a cadherin-like (CDHL) domain and a SEA domain [32, 33]. CDHL domain proteins are adhesion molecules that modulate, in a Ca2+-dependent manner, a wide variety of processes including cell interaction, polarization and migration [34, 35]. Given that the spontaneous adhesion to the culture plate was similar between healthy and LGMDR3 immortalized cells (Supplementary Fig. 4), we next assessed the α-SG Ca2+-binding function. We found that the LGMDR3 cells exhibited impaired α-SG Ca2+-binding, as a 5-minute treatment with EDTA, a Ca2+ chelator, was sufficient to detach more than 90% of the adherent cells (93.12 ± 5.11%). In contrast, healthy cells showed no sensitivity to the chelating effect of the 5-minute EDTA treatment, with most cells remaining adherent to the culture plate (only 9.32 ± 0.48% detached; Fig. 4A, B). In support of these data, the EDTA detachment of the immortalized LGMDR3 cell population, i.e. before cloning, and of a different clone demonstrated that this defect is present in all cells carrying the mutated α-SG, and it is not a feature of the specific clone. In contrast, in cells carrying a mutation in other sarcoglycans, such as γ-SG (LGMDR5 cells), EDTA detachment showed behaviour and timing more similar to that of healthy cells (Supplementary Fig. 5).

In addition to this facility in detachment of LGMDR3 cells from the plastic plate, 3D experiments suggest that there is also some kind of defective anchoring to ECM proteins. For this reason, we wondered if diseased cells present any difficulty in anchoring specific proteins, such as collagens or laminins, that are the major components of the dECM scaffold. Using a multi-well array, we seeded healthy and LGMDR3 cells over different ECM-derived coatings, including four different type I alpha1 collagen peptides, two different type IV alpha1 collagen peptides, five alpha1 laminin chain peptides, one alpha3 laminin chain peptide, two different beta1 laminin chain peptides, and one gamma1 laminin chain peptide. We found that healthy and diseased cells had, on average, the same ability to attach to different types of collagen (Fig. 4C), except for a specific type IV alpha1 peptide to which LGMDR3 cells adhered less efficiently. Conversely, laminin coatings proved to be less attractive to diseased cells, which adhered significantly less than healthy cells (61.4 ± 8.6% for healthy cells; 45.7 ± 4.6% for LGMDR3 cells). In particular, wells coated with different peptides of the alpha1, alpha3 and gamma1 chains resulted in fewer cells populated by LGMDR3 cells (Fig. 4D, E).

In addition, we found an effective lower aptitude for LGMDR3 cells to migrate spontaneously in 2D culture. On average, diseased cells covered a shorter distance than healthy cells (111.2 ± 24.5 μm for healthy cells; 71.7 ± 26.9 μm for LGMDR3 cells), with a significantly slower speed during the 15-hour tracking period (0.11 ± 0.02 μm/minute for healthy cells; 0.07 ± 0.02 for LGMDR3 cells; Fig. 4F-I).

All these results confirm that the mutation responsible for LGMDR3 disturbs the α-SG protein, impacting a series of basic myoblast behaviours.

Treatment of proliferating LGMDR3 cells with CFTR corrector

We previously demonstrated that the addition of CFTR corrector C17 to the culture media of differentiated myogenic cells from sarcoglycanopathy donors [14–16] is effective in rescuing the SG complex at the cell membrane. For this reason, we wondered if C17 can exert a positive effect also on the α-SG expressed by myoblasts during proliferation, positively impacting cell migration and docking properties. Firstly, we verified that the dose previously used on differentiated cells (15 µM) was not toxic even for cells in the active proliferation phase, and this was the case, as cells duplicated and subsequently differentiated in a similar way, independent of the treatment (Fig. 5A, B). We add the CFTR corrector for 2 days during the culture, to treat cells with a single dose. Western blot quantification demonstrated that after 4 days of culture (of which the last 2 days of treatment), no differences are appreciable between LGMDR3 and LGMDR3 + C17 cells in terms of α-SG protein content (Fig. 5C). We then performed a wound-healing assay to analyse the effect of C17 administration on cell migration. After 48 h of culture, LGMDR3 cells covered a significantly lower free area than healthy ones, confirming a reduced ability to move and migrate. Conversely, LGMDR3 cells treated with CFTR corrector C17 covered a mean larger free area, even if not significantly different, compared to untreated myopathic cells (Fig. 5D). Despite the slight improvement, C17 treatment increased the anchoring abilities of LGMDR3 cells, given that the Ca2+ chelating effect of EDTA was very low after 5 min of incubation, with values of cell detachment similar to those of healthy cells (10.64 ± 10.10%; Fig. 5E). In addition, when the C17 treatment was performed during the 7-day 3D cell culture inside the dECM scaffold, we observed a better cell engraftment and migration since LGMDR3 + C17 cells were significantly more distributed throughout the scaffold compared to untreated cells (9.4 ± 5.2% down, 27.3 ± 10.2 middle, 63.2 ± 9.9 up for LGMDR3 + C17 cells; 5.7 ± 10.2% down, 8.6 ± 4.5 middle, 85.5 ± 9.5 up for LGMDR3 cells; p < 0.001. Figure 5F).

Set up of C17 regimen protocol

Given the promising results obtained with a single dose of CFTR corrector C17, i.e. no toxicity, stronger adhesion to the substrate and better migration capability inside the dECM, we established a 2D culture protocol involving repeated administration of the therapeutic molecule to obtain information about myoblast differentiation and α-SG production following a conceivable regimen of treatment. After the first administration during the proliferation period, C17 (15 µM) was added to the culture medium for 48 h every two days of culture for a total of fifteen days (Fig. 6A). At the end of this regimen, LGMDR3-treated cells demonstrated the formation of more adherent and organized myotubes (Fig. 6B, C) and consistently express α-SG at the sarcolemma, in a way similar to that of healthy cells. On the contrary, a few still adherent myotubes of LGMDR3 untreated cells presented an accumulation of α-SG intracellularly, confirming an active production but at the same time the inability to translocate the protein to the functional position (Fig. 6C). Importantly, the quantity of α-SG in the cell membrane increased significantly after the C17 treatment regimen, reaching values comparable to those detected in healthy cells (Fig. 6D). The effect was the myotube stabilization and a better and more uniform distribution of the MHC signal in treated cells compared to untreated ones, which instead possess myotubes of highly variable shape and size, as demonstrated by the wide spread of the MHC signal (Fig. 6E).

We then generated 3D constructs with LMGDR3 cells using the same C17 treatment regimen that resulted efficient in 2D culture. After 14 days of dynamic 3D culture, this treatment allowed for obtaining well-repopulated muscle constructs, as anticipated by previous analyses after 7 days. Different from untreated constructs that presented tissue areas both completely devoid of cells and with large nuclear agglomerates, samples treated with C17 showed a homogenous cell repopulation, with an increased number of cells per field (Fig. 6F, G) and remained vital throughout the culture period.

Discussion

The main aim of this work was to produce a reliable in vitro 3D muscle as a versatile model suitable for studying basic disease mechanisms and the therapeutic effects of small molecules, such as the CFTR corrector C17, for the treatment of LGMDR3. The first attempt to generate these artificial LGMDR3 muscle constructs unexpectedly failed, as both primary and immortalised myogenic cells derived from a sarcoglycanopathy patient—despite being capable of proliferation—were unable to engraft and effectively colonise the dECM scaffold. Examining the reasons for these difficulties revealed an interesting role of α-SG in proliferating myogenic cells, which could open new research avenues. Essentially, we observed that the presence (even in very small quantities) of α-SG on the surface of myogenic cells during proliferation is vital for migration and proper interaction with the ECM. A mutated form of α-SG disrupts these functions. While this proved detrimental to generating recellularised dECM, we found that treating LGMDR3 cells with C17, a small molecule belonging to the CFTR corrector family, restored the strength of the myoblasts’ interaction with the ECM and ultimately enabled effective engraftment of the scaffold, thereby paving the way for future experiments where LGMDR3 artificial muscles can be utilised in drug screening and related studies.

SGs are dystrophin-associated proteins that form a membrane complex mandatory for the functional activity of skeletal muscle [36, 37]. The key structural role of this complex inside the DAPC in adult skeletal muscle is well established [2]. Recently, evidence has been collected concerning the SG-complex enrichment at the neuromuscular junction (NMJ), where a reduction in α-SG expression levels is associated with age-related or disease-related NMJ structural alterations [38, 39]. Old studies suggested a role of SGs in signal transduction, as α- and γ-SG seem to interact with integrins in both differentiated myotubes and adult skeletal muscle [40, 41], and are thought to sense contractility, becoming phosphorylated [42–44]. Furthermore, α-SG has been reported to exhibit ecto-ATPase activity [45, 46], indicating that α-SG may affect the P2 receptors signalling by modulating the extracellular ATP concentration [47]. It is well documented that sequential expression of different P2 receptors on the sarcolemma is closely associated with muscle maturation during embryogenesis, postnatal growth and tissue regeneration following injury [48]. This association suggests a potential role of α-SG in skeletal muscle development and homeostasis. Moreover, in its N-terminal (extracellular) portion, α-SG has a CDHL domain with a Ca²⁺-binding pocket modulating the domain structure through the binding of the ion. These structural features imply a Ca²⁺-dependent heterotypic adhesion role for this protein, facilitating interactions with other proteins that possess similar binding sites, including components of the DAPC. Furthermore, evidence from the standard in vitro culture model of myogenic cells indicates that the absence or misfolded production of the α-SG compromises the resistance of differentiated myotubes as a consequence of sarcolemma instability [14, 15]. What is evident, however, is that most of the information concerning the function of SGs derives from studies performed with mature muscle tissue, differentiated myotubes or adult myofibers, while the role of SGs during muscle development and maturation, from a single-cell to a multicellular syncytium, has been scarcely investigated.

As for other types of muscular dystrophies, primary cells have been successfully obtained from muscle biopsies of patients with sarcoglycanopathy. In our hands, both primary and immortalized cells [27] obtained from both healthy and LGMDR3 patients exhibited classic and comparable myogenic characteristics in vitro. Notably, when we examined the quantity and localization of α-SG during the proliferative phase, healthy and diseased cells did not show major differences. Previous studies have demonstrated, both in murine and in human cells, that the absence or instability of α-SG on the surface of myogenic cells affected the stability of FGFR1, thereby influencing the cell’s ability to respond to specific external stimuli [11]. We confirmed these findings, since immortalized LGMDR3 cells showed a lower expression of the receptor, were insensitive to the exogenous addition of bFGF and had a clear, distinct behaviour in comparison to the healthy ones. Moreover, the confirmation that a folding-defective α-SG in LGMDR3 [5, 7] plays a role in conditioning cell attachment and cell-cell interaction has been obtained through a simple EDTA detachment assay that highlighted the impaired Ca2+-dependent adhesion mechanism in the myopathic cells. These results suggest that, at least in the presence of this type of mutation, the altered conformation of α-SG impacts the protein functionality, despite a detectable amount of the protein being present at the cell membrane of these proliferating myoblasts.

Once used for the production of the 3D muscle model, LGMDR3 myogenic cells were unable to engraft and colonize the dECM scaffold, in contrast to the healthy ones. In healthy tissue, proteins of the ECM participate in feedback loops between the environment and cells to influence cellular behaviour and maintain homeostasis. In muscular dystrophy, muscle fibres undergo repetitive injury episodes with concomitant attempts of repair [49, 50] that lead to muscle degeneration, inflammation, inappropriate remodelling of the ECM and accumulation of matricellular proteins. It was reported that differential protein deposition into the ECM mediates cellular crosstalk in the muscular dystrophies, negatively influencing myoblast migration and differentiation [51]. Nevertheless, in our experiment of 3D modelling, healthy and dystrophic cells were both seeded into healthy dECM. Consequently, the impaired engraftment and migration of the LGMDR3 cells must necessarily be linked to the specific cell type used, whether it is present as primary or immortalized cells, and not due to an altered ECM composition. We also verified that the behaviour we described is not simply related to a dystrophic phenotype, as DMD myogenic cells are similar to the healthy ones in engrafting dECM. We have also observed that myogenic cells from an LGMDR5 patient, with γ-SG deletion, showed a behaviour closer to the healthy ones, further supporting the idea that the described impairment is linked to a defective α-SG protein.

Since their discovery [52, 53], the roles of cadherins in morphogenesis and cell migration have been intensively studied [54, 55]. Cadherins are known to serve distinct functions in cell adhesion, but it is experimentally very difficult to specifically regulate any of those functions alone [56]. They facilitate anchorage between neighbouring cells through their interaction with the contractile actomyosin network [57], and it was reported that the existence of a pathway controlled by FGFR1 and cadherin regulates cell-cell adhesion and cell migration [58]. In our experimental conditions, we cannot definitively conclude that the misfolding of the CDHL domain of the α-SG, rather than the instability of the FGFR1, is responsible for the poor cell engraftment, but it is clear that this mutation produces an impairment in the attachment of myogenic cells to ECM proteins that are abundantly present in the muscular environment, such as collagen IV and different laminin chains. Furthermore, LGMDR3 cells exhibited a significantly reduced capacity for spontaneous migration compared to healthy cells when seeded in standard 2D culture plates, mirroring the behaviour observed in the 3D culture system. Cell dispersion and migration from a confined area are fundamental in a number of biological processes, including skeletal muscle regeneration [59]. During these processes, cells coordinate their collective behaviour through cell-ECM and cell-cell contacts, thus exploiting the specific cell adhesive capacity. In LGMDR3 cells, this ability is significantly impaired and does not allow the generation of sufficiently recellularized muscle constructs. If this result can be disappointing in the experiment of artificial 3D muscle generation, it can assume a significant impact in vivo in muscle development and regeneration, particularly in the dystrophic context [60].

It is well known that in LGMDR3, the main consequence of the α-SG mutation is the strong reduction or even the absence of the SG complex from the sarcolemma of the differentiated muscle [61–63]. As a potential pharmacological approach for LGMDR3, we have evidenced that CFTR correctors, specifically the C17 molecule, improve the disease phenotype, both in vitro and in vivo [15–18], successfully rerouting the SG complex at the sarcolemma. These findings demonstrated that, even if mutated, the α-SG protein can function properly when localized at the right location of mature skeletal muscle. However, we never tested C17 in undifferentiated myogenic cells. Thus, we asked whether such treatment may also ameliorate the function of α-SG in mediating the adhesion properties of proliferating myoblasts. After just a 2-day treatment with C17, despite an unchanged total amount of α-SG, LGMDR3 cells significantly increased their resistance to the Ca2+ chelating effect of EDTA, showing a behaviour similar to healthy cells. Notably, when such treated cells were used to engraft the dECM scaffold, we obtained better populated and more homogeneous recellularized 3D constructs. This suggests that helping the folding or stabilizing the protein on the surface of the proliferating myogenic cells is needed for their adhesion to the substrate, and subsequently facilitates their migration inside the scaffold. A longer, pulsed treatment regimen with C17 was effective in producing well-differentiated myotubes in vitro, which were nearly indistinguishable from those generated by the healthy control. This extended regimen allowed the artificial construct to be more homogenously repopulated and to remain viable for up to 14 days.

The use of LGMDR3 cells as a source to produce a 3D muscle model has allowed us to highlight important aspects of the pathology that may negatively affect a fundamental behaviour of proliferating myogenic cells, such as adhesion and migration, highlighting the crucial role, previously underappreciated, of α-SG. Furthermore, the inability to generate these diseased 3D constructs allowed us to identify a phenomenon (the impaired capacity of the α-SG mutated cells to migrate inside an ECM) that would be difficult to investigate in vivo, thereby highlighting the greater reliability of artificial constructs compared to standard 2D cultures. Further investigations are needed to decipher the molecular mechanisms that prevent LGMDR3 cells from moving and migrating correctly, such as the role of the focal adhesion signalling pathway [64]. On the other hand, in a complex environmental context such as that of a dECM scaffold, C17 treatment has proven to be safe and efficacious, restoring the myogenic cell abilities impaired by the mutation. Altogether, our data shed light on an unexpected new role of α-SG expressed on myogenic proliferating cells that may have important implications both during developmental stages, when cell migration is a prominent feature, and during the regeneration of damaged muscle. Lastly, the ability of corrector C17 to enhance the engraftment of LGMDR3 cells points to novel therapeutic strategies and may help define the optimal time window for clinical application of CFTR correctors.
