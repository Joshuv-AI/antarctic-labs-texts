Quasi-adiabatic thermal ensemble preparation in the thermodynamic limit
Tatsuhiko Shirai

Abstract
We investigate a quasi-adiabatic thermal process for preparing finite-temperature ensembles in the thermodynamic limit. The process gradually transforms a thermal ensemble of a noninteracting system into that of an interacting system of interest over a finite operation time, with the temperature controlled by parameters associated with the entropy of the initial state. We analyze this process in both nonintegrable and integrable spin chains with translational invariance. For the nonintegrable case, numerical simulations show that the thermal properties of local observables are accurately reproduced with a single parameter in the high temperature regime, although the operation time increases exponentially with precision. In contrast, for the integrable transverse-field Ising model, we analytically show that an extensive number of parameters tied to local conserved quantities is generally necessary, and that the operation time increases linearly with system size, diverging in the thermodynamic limit. These results clarify the potential and limitations of the quasi-adiabatic thermal process for an ensemble preparation and highlight the role of integrability in determining its efficiency.



I
Introduction

The preparation of quantum thermal ensembles is a central problem in statistical physics, with broad relevance to condensed matter physics and quantum chemistry, among others.
Classical approaches, including quantum Monte Carlo methods
Suzuki 1993
, tensor network techniques
Orús 2019
, and machine learning approaches
Nomura et al. 2021
, have provided powerful tools for simulating thermal properties.
Significant progress has been made in extending these methods to strongly interacting systems and lower temperatures, yet they remain fundamentally limited by issues such as the negative-sign problem.

Quantum computing offers a promising alternative route to this challenge.
Several approaches have been proposed, including quantum Metropolis sampling
Temme et al. 2011
;
Yung and Aspuru-Guzik 2012
, simulations based on Lindblad dynamics
Chen et al. 2023
;
Gilyén et al. 2024
, and imaginary-time evolution
McArdle et al. 2019
;
Yuan et al. 2019
;
Motta et al. 2020
;
Coopmans et al. 2023
.
However, each method faces obstacles to practical realization on near-term noisy quantum devices.
For example, quantum Metropolis sampling requires deep circuits owing to phase estimation.
Variational algorithms based on free-energy minimization
Wang et al. 2021
;
Consiglio et al. 2024
have also been explored for the near-term devices, but these schemes require entropy estimation, which is computationally hard
Gheorghiu and Hoban 2024
, and often suffer from the absence of a systematic circuit ansatz.

On the other hand, the universality of thermodynamics implies that exact Gibbs state construction is not required to reproduce the thermal properties of local observables, i.e., operators acting on a connected region of
O(1)
sites.
Recent developments in nonequilibrium statistical physics, such as the concept of typical states
Goldstein et al. 2006
;
Popescu et al. 2006
, suggest that thermal properties can be captured without explicitly preparing Gibbs states.
From this perspective, simulating a (quasi-)adiabatic thermal process
Il‘in et al. 2021
;
Zuo et al. 2024
;
Irmejs et al. 2025
;
Granet and Dreyer 2025
emerges as a promising candidate for quantum algorithms.
In this process, a thermal ensemble of a simple system is initially prepared and then transformed into that of an interacting system of interest via a quantum adiabatic protocol executed over a finite operation time.
The ensemble average of an observable over the final state can be efficiently evaluated on a gate-based quantum device
Wang et al. 2021
;
Consiglio et al. 2024
;
Shirai and Togawa 2025
.
Although this approach generally does not yield the exact Gibbs state, the expectation values of local observables are expected to approximate the thermal values.
However, the performance in the thermodynamic limit remains to be clarified.

In this work, we study the quasi-adiabatic thermal process for preparing thermal ensembles in the thermodynamic limit.
Unlike exact Gibbs-state preparation, our aim is to reproduce the thermal properties of local observables.
To quantify accuracy, we employ the specific relative entropy as a measure of the distance from the Gibbs state
Mori 2016
.
We analyze the performance of this process in both nonintegrable and integrable spin chains with translational invariance.
For the nonintegrable case, numerical results demonstrate that a single parameter that controls the entropy of the initial state is sufficient to reproduce the thermal properties of local observables in the high temperature regime, although the operation time increases exponentially with precision.
Through the analysis, we highlight the key role of the eigenstate thermalization hypothesis (ETH)
Deutsch 1991
;
Srednicki 1994
;
Mori et al. 2018
, which is widely believed to hold in nonintegrable systems
Rigol et al. 2008
;
Kim et al. 2014
;
Yoshizawa et al. 2018
.
We also investigate the effect of time averaging on the performance.
In contrast, for the integrable case we analytically show that a fine tuning of the initial state is generally necessary, and the operation time grows linearly with the system size (i.e., diverges in the thermodynamic limit).

Section

Section

concludes with a summary and outlook for future directions.

II
Quasi-adiabatic thermal process

We consider a quasi-adiabatic thermal process
Il‘in et al. 2021
;
Zuo et al. 2024
;
Irmejs et al. 2025
;
Granet and Dreyer 2025
.
The process begins with
N
independent spins, where the initial Hamiltonian is given as

H_{\mathrm{i}}=-\sum_{i=1}^{N}h_{i}\hat{\sigma}_{i}^{x},

(1)

where
\hat{\sigma}_{i}^{\alpha}(\alpha\in\{x,y,z\})
and
h_{i}\geq 0
are the Pauli spin operators and the magnetic field acting on site
i
, respectively.
The initial state is prepared as the thermal state at inverse temperature
\beta_{\mathrm{i}}
:

\displaystyle\rho_{\mathrm{i}}=

\displaystyle\frac{e^{-\beta_{\mathrm{i}}\hat{H}_{\mathrm{i}}}}{\mathrm{Tr}[e^{-\beta_{\mathrm{i}}\hat{H}_{\mathrm{i}}}]}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bigotimes_{j=1}^{N}(\cos^{2}\phi_{j}\ket{+_{j}}\bra{+_{j}}+\sin^{2}\phi_{j}\ket{-_{j}}\bra{-_{j}}),

(2)

where
\phi_{j}
is determined from the Boltzmann weight, and
\ket{+_{j}}
and
\ket{-_{j}}
are eigenstates of
\hat{\sigma}_{j}^{x}
with eigenvalues
1
and
-1
, respectively.
The entropy density for a density matrix
\rho
is defined as the von Neumann entropy
s(\rho)=-\mathrm{Tr}(\rho\ln\rho)/N
, and for
\rho_{\mathrm{i}}
it is given by

s(\rho_{\mathrm{i}})=-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}[\cos^{2}\phi_{j}\ln(\cos^{2}\phi_{j})+\sin^{2}\phi_{j}\ln(\sin^{2}\phi_{j})].

(3)

The system then evolves according to the quantum adiabatic protocol.
The state at time
t
is given by

\rho(t)=\hat{U}_{t}\rho_{\mathrm{i}}\hat{U}_{t}^{\dagger},\quad\hat{U}_{t}=\mathcal{T}e^{-i\int_{0}^{t}\hat{H}(t^{\prime}/\tau)dt^{\prime}},

(4)

where
\cal{T}
is the time-ordering operator and
\tau
denotes the operation time.
Here, we set
\hbar=1
.
The time-dependent Hamiltonian is linearly interpolated between the initial and final Hamiltonians: for
s_{\mathrm{a}}\in[0,1]

\hat{H}(s_{\mathrm{a}})=(1-s_{\mathrm{a}})\hat{H}_{\mathrm{i}}+s_{\mathrm{a}}\hat{H}_{\mathrm{f}},

(5)

where
\hat{H}_{\mathrm{f}}
is the Hamiltonian of interest.
The final state is
\rho_{\mathrm{f}}(\tau)=\rho(\tau)
.
The energy density for
\rho_{\mathrm{f}}(\tau)
is

e(\rho_{\mathrm{f}}(\tau))=\frac{1}{N}\mathrm{Tr}(\hat{H}_{\mathrm{f}}\rho_{\mathrm{f}}(\tau)).

(6)

Since the process is unitary, the entropy density remains constant:

s(\rho_{\mathrm{f}}(\tau))=s(\rho_{\mathrm{i}}).

(7)

The process is reduced to a quantum annealing
Kadowaki and Nishimori 1998
when
\phi_{j}=0
for all
j
.
We adopt this process since it can be implemented on gate-based quantum devices using rotation-
Y
gates, controlled-
X
gates, and the Suzuki–Trotter decomposition of
\hat{U}_{\tau}

Wang et al. 2021
;
Consiglio et al. 2024
.

To further enhance thermalization, we introduce a time-averaging process.
Over an additional time window
\tau_{\mathrm{a}}
, we define the average density matrix

\bar{\rho}(\tau,\tau_{\mathrm{a}})=\int_{0}^{\tau_{\mathrm{a}}}\rho_{\mathrm{f}}(\tau,t)\frac{dt}{\tau_{\mathrm{a}}},

(8)

where
\rho_{\mathrm{f}}(\tau,t)=e^{-\mathrm{i}\hat{H}_{\mathrm{f}}t}\rho_{\mathrm{f}}(\tau)e^{\mathrm{i}\hat{H}_{\mathrm{f}}t}
.
This process increases the entropy density:
s(\bar{\rho}(\tau,\tau_{\mathrm{a}}))\geq s(\rho_{\mathrm{f}}(\tau))
.

We use the quasi-adiabatic thermal process to study the thermal properties of a quantum system governed by
\hat{H}_{\mathrm{f}}
at finite temperature
\beta^{-1}>0
.
The thermal equilibrium state is given by the Gibbs state,

\rho_{\mathrm{g}}(\beta)=\frac{e^{-\beta\hat{H}_{\mathrm{f}}}}{\mathrm{Tr}[e^{-\beta\hat{H}_{\mathrm{f}}}]}.

(9)

It should be emphasized that neither
\rho_{\mathrm{f}}(\tau)
nor
\bar{\rho}(\tau,\tau_{\mathrm{a}})
exactly reproduces the Gibbs state.
The Gibbs state is diagonal in the energy eigenbasis, with a number of parameters growing exponentially with system size.
In contrast, the final states depend on only
N
parameters
\{\phi_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N}
, making it a compact description.

However, the ETH
Deutsch 1991
;
Srednicki 1994
;
Rigol et al. 2008
;
Mori et al. 2018
implies that a single parameter that controls the value of the energy density of the final state can accurately capture thermal properties of local observables.
The ETH states that for any local observable
\hat{O}
, the diagonal matrix elements obey

[\hat{O}]_{nn}=O_{\beta_{n}},

(10)

where
[\cdot]_{mn}=\bra{m}\cdot\ket{n}
,
\hat{H}_{\mathrm{f}}\ket{n}=E_{n}\ket{n}
, and
O_{\beta_{n}}
is the thermal value at the inverse temperature
\beta_{n}
, which is determined by
E_{n}=\mathrm{Tr}(\hat{H}_{\mathrm{f}}\rho_{\mathrm{g}}(\beta_{n}))
.

The indistinguishability of a state
\rho
from the Gibbs state
\rho_{\mathrm{g}}(\beta)
with respect to local observables
\hat{O}
can be quantified by the specific relative entropy:

s_{N}(\rho\|\rho_{\mathrm{g}}(\beta))=\frac{1}{N}\mathrm{Tr}[\rho(\ln\rho-\ln\rho_{\mathrm{g}}(\beta))]

(11)

In the thermodynamic limit, the following bound holds
Mori 2016
:

s(\rho\|\rho_{\mathrm{g}}(\beta))\coloneqq\lim_{N\to\infty}s_{N}(\rho\|\rho_{\mathrm{g}}(\beta))\gtrsim(\Delta O)^{2},

(12)

where
\Delta O=\mathrm{Tr}[\hat{O}(\rho-\rho_{\mathrm{g}}(\beta))]
.
This relation implies that if
s(\rho\|\rho_{\mathrm{g}}(\beta))=0
, then
\rho
and
\rho_{\mathrm{g}}(\beta)
are indistinguishable in terms of local observables, even if
\rho\neq\rho_{\mathrm{g}}(\beta)
.

In the following, we investigate
s(\rho_{\mathrm{f}}(\tau)\|\rho_{\mathrm{g}}(\beta))
and
s(\bar{\rho}(\tau,\tau_{\mathrm{a}})\|\rho_{\mathrm{g}}(\beta))
in nonintegrable and integrable spin chains.

Results

III.1
Nonintegrable system

We consider a spin chain model subject to a tilted magnetic field.
The Hamiltonian is given by

\hat{H}_{\mathrm{f}}=g\sum_{i=1}^{N}\hat{\sigma}_{i}^{z}\hat{\sigma}_{i+1}^{z}+h_{x}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\hat{\sigma}_{i}^{x}+h_{z}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\hat{\sigma}_{i}^{z}.

(13)

We set the parameters as
(g,h_{x},h_{z})=(1,0.9045,0.809)
, for which the ETH has been numerically verified
Kim et al. 2014
, and impose a periodic boundary condition (i.e.,
\hat{\sigma}_{N+1}^{z}=\hat{\sigma}_{1}^{z}
).
Based on the ETH argument, we choose a homogeneous initial state,

\phi_{1}=\ldots=\phi_{N}=\phi.

(14)

III.2
Integrable system

We next analyze the transverse-field Ising model
Sachdev 2011
, which serves as a representative example of an integrable model:

\hat{H}_{\mathrm{f}}=-J\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}\hat{\sigma}_{i}^{z}\hat{\sigma}_{i+1}^{z}-\Gamma\sum_{i=1}^{N}\hat{\sigma}_{i}^{x},

(22)

with an open boundary condition.
The free energy density of the Gibbs state in the thermodynamic limit is given by
Lieb et al. 1961

f(\rho_{\mathrm{g}}(\beta))=-\frac{1}{\pi\beta}\int_{0}^{\pi}\ln[2\cosh(\beta\Lambda(y))]dy,

(23)

where
\Lambda(y)=\sqrt{\Gamma^{2}+J^{2}+2\Gamma J\cos y}
.
The Hamiltonian used in the quasi-adiabatic thermal process is also integrable and can be written as

\hat{H}(s_{\mathrm{a}})=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\Lambda_{k}(s_{\mathrm{a}})\left(\hat{\eta}^{\dagger}_{k}(s_{\mathrm{a}})\hat{\eta}_{k}(s_{\mathrm{a}})-\frac{1}{2}\right),

(24)

where
\hat{\eta}_{k}(s_{\mathrm{a}})
and
\hat{\eta}_{k}^{\dagger}(s_{\mathrm{a}})
are fermionic annihilation and creation operators, and
\{\Lambda_{k}(s_{\mathrm{a}})\}_{k=1}^{N}
denotes the single particle spectrum, ordered in ascending order.

III.2.1
Homogeneous initial state

First, we consider
\rho_{\mathrm{f}}(\tau)
starting from a homogeneous initial state (i.e.,
\phi_{1}=\ldots=\phi_{N}=\phi
).
The parameter
\phi
is determined to minimize the free energy density
f(\rho_{\mathrm{f}}(\tau))=e(\rho_{\mathrm{f}}(\tau))-\beta^{-1}s(\rho_{\mathrm{f}}(\tau))
at a given
\beta

Under the Jordan–Wigner transformation
Lieb et al. 1961
, the Hamiltonian is written as

\hat{H}_{\mathrm{f}}=-J\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}(\hat{c}_{i}^{\dagger}-\hat{c}_{i})(\hat{c}_{i+1}^{\dagger}+\hat{c}_{i+1})-\Gamma\sum_{i=1}^{N}(2\hat{c}_{i}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{i}-1),

(25)

where
\hat{c}_{i}
and
\hat{c}_{i}^{\dagger}
are annihilation and creation fermion operators.
The energy density of
\rho_{\mathrm{f}}(\tau)
can be expressed as

\displaystyle e(\rho_{\mathrm{f}}(\tau))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\Big[\frac{J}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}[(u_{i,j}-v_{i,j})(u^{*}_{i+1,j}+v^{*}_{i+1,j})+\mathrm{c.c.}]

\displaystyle+\Gamma\sum_{i=1}^{N}(|u_{i,j}|^{2}-|v_{i,j}|^{2})\Big]\cos 2\phi,

(26)

where
\hat{U}_{\tau}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{i}U_{\tau}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}[u_{i,j}\hat{c}_{j}+v_{i,j}^{*}\hat{c}_{j}^{\dagger}]

Caneva et al. 2007
.
When
\phi=0
, the energy density is identical to that of quantum annealing, denoted by
e_{\mathrm{a}}(\tau)
.
Therefore, we obtain

e(\rho_{\mathrm{f}}(\tau))=e_{\mathrm{a}}(\tau)\cos 2\phi.

(27)

Then, minimizing the free energy density gives

f(\rho_{\mathrm{f}}(\tau))=-\frac{1}{\beta}\ln[2\cosh(\beta e_{\mathrm{a}}(\tau))].

(28)

In the infinite operation time, quantum annealing reproduces the ground state
Morita and Nishimori 2008
:
\lim_{\tau\to\infty}e_{\mathrm{a}}(\tau)=-\sum_{k}\Lambda_{k}(1)/2
.
Thus, taking both
\tau\to\infty
and
N\to\infty
, the free energy becomes

\lim_{N\to\infty}f(\rho_{\mathrm{f}}(\infty))=-\frac{1}{\beta}\ln\left[2\cosh\left(\frac{\beta}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}\Lambda(y)dy\right)\right].

(29)

Compared to Eq. (
23
), the homogeneous initial state does not reproduce the thermal properties (i.e.,
s(\rho_{\mathrm{f}}(\infty)\|\rho_{\mathrm{g}}(\beta))=\beta(f(\rho_{\mathrm{f}}(\infty))-f(\rho_{\mathrm{g}}(\beta)))\neq 0
) except when
\Gamma J=0
or
\beta=0
.

When
\Gamma=0
at finite operation time, the energy density of quantum annealing is given by

e_{\mathrm{a}}(\tau)=-J\left(1-\frac{2}{\pi\sqrt{J\tau}}\right).

(30)

The
\tau

Zurek et al. 2005
and also appears in the specific relative entropy as

s(\rho_{\mathrm{f}}(\tau)\|\rho_{\mathrm{g}}(\beta))\simeq\frac{2\beta}{\pi}\sqrt{\frac{J}{\tau}}\tanh{(\beta J)},

(31)

which is derived by the Taylor expansion of the free-energy density with respect to
J
.

IV
Conclusion

We investigate the quasi-adiabatic thermal process for preparing thermal ensembles in the thermodynamic limit.
For nonintegrable spin chains, our numerical results demonstrate that a single parameter is sufficient to reproduce the thermal properties of local observables in the high temperature regime, although the operation time increases exponentially with precision.
We also find that time averaging does not improve performance.
In the integrable transverse-field Ising model, we clarify the differences from the nonintegrable case, which arise from the presence of local conserved quantities.
In general, fine-tuning of the initial state and a linear increase of the operation time with system size are required.
These results highlight the potential and limitations of quantum algorithms for implementing the quasi-adiabatic thermal process.
An open question is how these results extend to other systems, such as integrable models solvable by the Bethe ansatz or systems in higher spatial dimensions, where the phase transition occurs at finite temperature.

Appendix A
Derivation of Eq. (
37
)

We derive Eq. (
37
) by extending the zero-temperature result in
Mbeng et al. 2019
to finite temperatures.
Since the specific relative entropy is related to the energy density difference, we evaluate the local energy associated with sites
j
and
j+1
:

e_{j}=\mathrm{Tr}[\hat{U}_{\tau}^{\dagger}\hat{h}_{j}(J,\Gamma)\hat{U}_{\tau}\rho_{\mathrm{i}}],

(38)

where
\hat{h}_{j}(J,\Gamma)=-J\hat{\sigma}_{j}^{z}\hat{\sigma}_{j+1}^{z}-\Gamma\hat{\sigma}_{j}^{x}
.

The Lieb–Robinson bound
Lieb and Robinson 1972
implies that
\hat{U}_{\tau}^{\dagger}\hat{h}_{j}(J,\Gamma)\hat{U}_{\tau}
is effectively supported on a region of radius
N_{\mathrm{R}}\sim v\tau
around site
j
, where
v
denotes the Lieb–Robinson velocity.
We denote this region by
{\mathcal{L}}_{j}=\{j_{\mathrm{min}},\ldots,j_{\mathrm{max}}\}
, with
j_{\mathrm{min}}=j-N_{\mathrm{R}}+1
and
j_{\mathrm{max}}=j+N_{\mathrm{R}}
.
For
N_{\mathrm{R}}\ll N
and
N_{\mathrm{R}}\leq j\leq N-N_{\mathrm{R}}
, Eq. (
38
) becomes, up to
O(\epsilon)
corrections with
\epsilon=\tau/N
,

e_{j}=\mathrm{Tr}[\hat{U}_{\tau,j}^{\alpha\dagger}\hat{h}_{j}(J,\Gamma)\hat{U}_{\tau,j}^{\alpha}\rho_{\mathrm{i},j}]+O(\epsilon).

(39)

Here,
\hat{U}^{\alpha}_{\tau,j}
and
\rho_{\mathrm{i},j}
act on
{\mathcal{L}}_{j}
, and
\alpha\in\{+1,-1\}
specifies the boundary condition (periodic or antiperiodic).
It is noted that Eq. (
39
) holds irrespective of the choice of the boundary condition.
Specifically,

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\hat{U}_{\tau,j}^{\alpha}=\mathcal{T}e^{-i\int_{0}^{\tau}\hat{H}_{j}^{\alpha}\left(\frac{Jt}{\tau},\frac{\Gamma t}{\tau}+h_{j}\left(1-\frac{t}{\tau}\right)\right)dt},\\
&\rho_{\mathrm{i},j}=\bigotimes_{k\in{\mathcal{L}}_{j}}\cos^{2}\phi_{j}\ket{+_{k}}\bra{+_{k}}+\sin^{2}\phi_{j}\ket{-_{k}}\bra{-_{k}},\end{aligned}\right.

(40)

where the Hamiltonian on
{\mathcal{L}}_{j}
is defined as

\hat{H}_{j}^{\alpha}(\tilde{J},\tilde{\Gamma})=\sum_{k=j_{\mathrm{min}}}^{j_{\mathrm{max}}-1}\hat{h}_{k}(\tilde{J},\tilde{\Gamma})-\tilde{\Gamma}\hat{\sigma}_{j_{\mathrm{max}}}^{x}-\alpha\tilde{J}\hat{\sigma}_{j_{\mathrm{max}}}^{z}\hat{\sigma}_{1}^{z}.

(41)

Then, the local energy can be expressed as

e_{j}=\sum_{\alpha\in\{\pm 1\}}\mathrm{Tr}[\hat{U}_{\tau,j}^{\alpha\dagger}\hat{h}_{j}(J,\Gamma)\hat{U}_{\tau,j}^{\alpha}\rho_{\mathrm{i},j}\mathrm{P}_{-\alpha}]+O(\epsilon)

(42)

where the projection operator
\mathrm{P}_{\alpha}
is

\mathrm{P}_{\alpha}=\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\alpha\prod_{i\in\mathcal{L}_{j}}\hat{\sigma}_{i}^{x}\right).

(43)

Next, we introduce the translation operators
\hat{T}_{j}^{\alpha}
for
\alpha\in\{+1,-1\}
acting on the operators in
{\mathcal{L}}_{j}
:

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\hat{T}_{j}^{\alpha\dagger}\hat{\sigma}_{k}^{x(z)}\hat{T}_{j}^{\alpha}=\hat{\sigma}_{k+1}^{x(z)}\text{ for }k\neq j_{\mathrm{max}},\\
&\hat{T}_{j}^{\alpha\dagger}\hat{\sigma}_{j_{\mathrm{max}}}^{x}\hat{T}_{j}^{\alpha}=\hat{\sigma}_{j_{\mathrm{min}}}^{x},\\
&\hat{T}_{j}^{\alpha\dagger}\hat{\sigma}_{j_{\mathrm{max}}}^{z}\hat{T}_{j}^{\alpha}=\alpha\hat{\sigma}_{j_{\mathrm{min}}}^{z},\\
\end{aligned}\right.

(44)

Then, we obtain for
\alpha\in\{+1,-1\}

\displaystyle 2N_{\mathrm{R}}\mathrm{Tr}[\hat{U}_{\tau,j}^{\alpha\dagger}\hat{h}_{j}(J,\Gamma)\hat{U}_{\tau,j}^{\alpha}\rho_{\mathrm{i},j}\mathrm{P}_{-\alpha}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{2N_{\mathrm{R}}}\mathrm{Tr}[(\hat{T}_{j}^{\alpha\dagger})^{n}\hat{U}_{\tau,j}^{\alpha\dagger}\hat{h}_{j}(J,\Gamma)\hat{U}_{\tau,j}^{\alpha}\rho_{\mathrm{i},j}\mathrm{P}_{-\alpha}(\hat{T}_{j}^{\alpha})^{n}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}[\hat{U}_{\tau,j}^{\alpha\dagger}\hat{H}_{j}^{\alpha}(J,\Gamma)\hat{U}_{\tau,j}^{\alpha}\rho_{\mathrm{i},j}\mathrm{P}_{-\alpha}],

(45)

and the local energy is given by

\displaystyle e_{j}=\frac{1}{2N_{\mathrm{R}}}\sum_{\alpha\in\{\pm 1\}}\mathrm{Tr}[\hat{U}_{\tau,j}^{\alpha\dagger}\hat{H}_{j}^{\alpha}(J,\Gamma)\hat{U}_{\tau,j}^{\alpha}\rho_{\mathrm{i},j}\mathrm{P}_{-\alpha}]+O(\epsilon).

(46)

Applying the Jordan–Wigner transformation gives

e_{j}=\frac{1}{2N_{\mathrm{R}}}\mathrm{Tr}[\hat{U}_{\tau,j}^{\dagger}\hat{H}(J,\Gamma;\mathcal{L}_{j})\hat{U}_{\tau,j}\rho_{\mathrm{i},j}]+O(\epsilon),

(47)

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{j}(\tilde{J},\tilde{\Gamma})=-\tilde{J}\Big[\sum_{k=j_{\mathrm{min}}}^{j_{\mathrm{max}}-1}(\hat{c}_{k}^{\dagger}-\hat{c}_{k})(\hat{c}_{k+1}^{\dagger}+\hat{c}_{k+1})

\displaystyle\hat{U}_{\tau,j}=\mathcal{T}e^{-i\int_{0}^{\tau}\hat{H}_{j}\left(\frac{Jt}{\tau},\frac{\Gamma t}{\tau}+h_{j}\left(1-\frac{t}{\tau}\right)\right)dt}.

(48)

Following the argument in deriving Eq. (
27

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}[\hat{U}_{\tau,j}^{\dagger}\hat{H}_{j}(J,\Gamma)\hat{U}_{\tau,j}\rho_{\mathrm{i},j}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bra{\psi_{0,j}}\hat{U}_{\tau,j}^{\dagger}\hat{H}_{j}(J,\Gamma)\hat{U}_{\tau,j}\ket{\psi_{0,j}}\cos(2\phi_{j})

\displaystyle 2N_{\mathrm{R}}e^{\prime}_{\mathrm{a}}(\tau)\cos(2\phi_{j}),

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=N_{\mathrm{R}}}^{N-N_{\mathrm{R}}}e_{j}+O(\epsilon)

Taking the limit
