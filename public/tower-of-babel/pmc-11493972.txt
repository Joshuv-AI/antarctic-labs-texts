Real-time spatiotemporal action localization algorithm using improved CNNs architecture
Authors: Hengshuai Liu, Jianjun Li, Jiale Tong, Guang Li, Qian Wang, Ming Zhang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis paper aims to propose a faster and more accurate network for human spatiotemporal action localization tasks. Like the YOWO model, we also use convolutional neural networks (CNNs) for feature extraction, but our model differs from YOWO in three significant ways: firstly, we don’t use the feature fusion strategy, we only use spatial features extracted by 2D CNNs for action localization and spatiotemporal features extracted by 3D CNNs for action recognition; secondly, we make an improvement to the 2D CNNs network by introducing a coordinate attention mechanism and utilize the CIoU loss instead of the coordinate offset loss for bounding box regression; thirdly, we provide a more lightweight and faster spatiotemporal action localization architecture, which reduces the number of parameters by 21.76 million and achieves a speed of 39 fps on 16-frame input clips compared to the YOWO model. We test our model’s performance on three public datasets: UCF-Sports, JHMDB-21 and UCF101-24. Compared with the YOWO model, we improve frame-mAP (@IoU 0.5) by 17.09% and 7.15% on the UCF-Sports and JHMDB-21 datasets, and for video-mAP, we improve by 2.7%, 8.7% and 14.4% at IoU thresholds of 0.2, 0.5 and 0.75 on the JHMDB-21 dataset.Keywords: Spatiotemporal action localization, Real-time, 2D CNNs, 3D CNNsSubject terms: Electrical and electronic engineering, Computer science

Introduction

The human spatiotemporal action localization task refers to the ability to recognize and locate the human body in both temporal and spatial dimensions, which not only needs to recognize the behavior categories in the video but also to locate the time interval and spatial position where the behavior occurs. It plays an increasingly important role in our lives. In the field of security, it can be used to monitor the real-time location and activities of individuals, identify abnormal behavior, and issue timely alerts. In the field of sports and fitness, it can be used to analyze athletes’ movement postures and actions and provide personalized training recommendations. However, there are also certain difficulties in human spatiotemporal action localization tasks.

In this field of study, the remarkable architecture is “You Only Watch Once” (YOWO1 ), which uses 2D CNNs to extract spatial features from the current frame, and 3D CNNs to extract spatiotemporal features from a clip, and then combines the features using a channel fusion and attention mechanism (CFAM). YOWO has competitive advantages on multiple datasets, and there have been many research efforts focusing on its improvement, such as COWO2, YWOM3 and YOWOv24, but they all share a common issue: they utilize fused spatiotemporal and spatial features for action recognition and bounding box regression. However, we have found that 3D CNNs can already capture sufficient spatiotemporal information for representing action categories, adding single-frame behavior information may interfere with recognition; similarly, in action localization, the spatial features extracted by 2D CNNs are adequate for locating behaviors, and incorporating the temporal information from 3D CNNs may not necessarily improve performance. Therefore, different from YOWO, we only use spatial features extracted by 2D CNNs for action localization and spatiotemporal features extracted by 3D CNNs for action recognition.

Related work

Action recognition: action recognition refers to the process of understanding and classifying target behavior from video data, and the key to action recognition lies in modeling the temporal and spatial features in the video. An effective approach is to utilize a two-stream CNN network, extracting temporal information and spatial information through two separate branches, such as6,7,8. However, these efforts are often based on optical flow information, which comes with substantial computational costs. Alone with convolutional neural networks, there are also many other advanced models, such as R(2 + 1)D9, TSN10, TSM11, etc.12,13. While 2D CNNs have a small number of parameters, they are unable to capture sufficient spatiotemporal information. An alternative option is to use 3D CNNs. 3D CNNs can simultaneously consider the temporal relationships and spatial information between video frames, thereby capturing motion features in the video more effectively. Many outstanding works are built on 3D CNNs, such as the I3D14, C3D15, P3D16, SlowFast17, etc. Additionally, there are integrations of the state-of-the-art handcrafted features and sparse codes for human activity recognition18, as well as the utilization of histogram-oriented gradients and Zernike moments for high-dimensional abnormal human activity recognition19, and the use of average energy images for abnormal human action recognition20. There are also many studies based on transformers, which effectively capture the temporal and spatial relationships between different frames in videos using the self-attention mechanism. For example, TimeSFormer21, Video Swin Transformer22, however, transformer-based models have slower inference speeds and cannot achieve real-time performance. Additionally, there are methods based on skeleton data, which utilize information such as the positions of human joints and motion trajectories to recognize human motion states23,24,25,26,27, but this approach is highly susceptible to the data quality and variations in posture.

Spatiotemporal action localization: it is also an important branch in the field of video understanding and it is a further application of action recognition. It not only needs to identify the category of behavior in the video, but also needs to locate the location where the behavior occurs. In spatiotemporal action localization, extracting effective spatiotemporal features is crucial, yet it comes with some challenges such as the effects of human shape changes and the computational complexity of deep neural networks. To address these issues, some research has focused on methods like extracting multiscale features to manage human-scale variation28,29,30 and using depth-wise separable CNNs to reduce computational complexity31. Additionally, in the field of spatiotemporal action localization, some researchers propose to detect behaviors in each frame first and then link them together to form a video pipeline32,33,34. Moreover, some other researchers achieve behavior classification and bounding box regression at the video clip level by employing 3D CNNs32,35. Later, an innovative spatiotemporal behavior localization algorithm named YOWO appeared, which uses 2D CNNs to extract spatial features from the current frame, and 3D CNNs to extract spatiotemporal features from a clip, then combines the features using a CFAM module, and the fused features are used to complete the spatiotemporal action localization. Our model also uses CNNs for feature extraction, but we only use the extracted spatiotemporal features for classification, spatial features for bounding box regression, and then obtain the final prediction box through category information and location information.

2D CNNs branch In the 2D CNNs branch, we use the Darknet-19 network as the backbone. As shown in Fig. 1, the input images are processed by 2D CNNs to generate feature vectors of shape [C', H', W'], where C' represents the number of output channels, and H' and W' are the height and width of the output tensor. Since we use anchor-based bounding box regression, so = 25 = 5 × (tx,ty,tw,th,conf), where 5 indicates that each grid cell in the final output feature map contains 5 anchor boxes. This means each grid cell in the final feature map will output 5 predicted bounding boxes. Here, tx and ty represent the relative offsets of the predicted box center from the top-left corner of the corresponding cell, tw and th represent the width and height of the predicted bounding box relative to the anchor box, and conf represents the probability that the predicted bounding box contains an object.

3D CNNs branch

In the 3D CNNs branch, we take 3D-ResNext-101 as the backbone network, which has rich spatiotemporal feature extraction capabilities. The input data is a video sequence consisting of 16 consecutive frames, and after undergoing feature extraction by the 3D CNNs, the input data forms a tensor with a shape of [C'', D'', H'',W'']. In our model, C'' = nCls, denoting the total number of action categories to be recognized, D'' represents the processed temporal dimension, and to match the output features of the 2D CNNs, the temporal dimension needs to be compressed to 1.

It should be noted that our model’s backbone can be easily replaced by other efficient networks, which only need to satisfy and , meaning that the 2D CNNs branch and 3D CNNs have the same downsampling rate.

Action selection

As shown in Fig. 2, in the 2D CNNs branch, the output features have a shape of , where = 25, representing the position offsets and object confidence for 5 anchor boxes. In the 3D CNNs branch, the final output features have a shape of , where , representing the information of action categories.

The action selection block diagram of our model, where category information comes from spatiotemporal features and position information comes from spatial features.

Therefore, to obtain the predicted boxes, we need to add category information for each anchor box. Firstly, we perform dimension expansion on the 3D spatiotemporal features by adding an extra dimension at the first axis and repeat it 5 times along this dimension. Then, we apply softmax operation to obtain the maximum class probability and the corresponding class index. Finally, we concatenate the processed 3D features with the 2D features to obtain the candidate boxes and remove the redundant boxes through non-maximum suppression (NMS) operations.

Improved 2D CNN network

To further improve the localization accuracy of our framework, we incorporate a coordinate attention (CA)5 module into the 2D network. The CA module is an attention mechanism designed to highlight the relationships between different locations in the input feature map. As shown in the Fig. 3, The CA module’s operation mainly consists of two parts. One is coordinate transformation: it first transforms the feature maps along spatial dimensions into coordinate representations, capturing spatial information effectively. The other is attention application: it then applies attention mechanisms to these coordinate representations, focusing on both horizontal and vertical spatial dependencies separately, which helps in preserving precise positional information. Through these two operations, the CA module improves the model’s ability to capture long-range dependencies while retaining spatial details.

Experiments

Datasets and implementation details

UCF-Sports The UCF-Sports dataset consists of a series of sports actions, such as diving, golf swinging, soccer kicking, weightlifting, running, etc. In the UCF-Sports dataset, there are a total of 10 types of sports actions, comprising 9230 images and with a resolution of (320 × 240).

JHMDB-21 The JHMDB-21 dataset consists of 21 action categories, which include both daily life actions and sports activities, such as brushing hair, clapping, climbing stairs, shooting ball, shooting bow, and more. The dataset contains a total of 31,838 images, also with a resolution of (320 × 240).

UCF101-24 The UCF101-24 is a dataset also specifically designed for spatiotemporal action localization. It is an extension of the UCF101 dataset, featuring a subset of 24 action classes. It includes 3,207 video clips and a total of 475,391 images.

To validate our model’s performance, we test our model on the aforementioned three public datasets, and all experiments are conducted on an NVIDIA 3090 GPU. For UCF101-24 dataset, we train the 3D branch for 5 epochs and the 2D branch for 5 epochs, we set the initial learning rate to be 0.0001, and apply a learning rate decay of 0.5 after the 1st, 2nd, 3rd, and 4th epoch; for JHMDB-21 dataset, we train both the 3D branch and the 2D branch for 10 epochs, similarly, we set the initial learning rate to be 0.0001, but apply a learning rate decay of 0.5 after 10k, 20k, 30k, and 40k iterations; for UCF-Sports dataset, due to its small number of samples, we train for a longer number of epochs, we also set the initial learning rate to 0.0001 and apply a learning rate decay of 0.5. For evaluation metrics, we use the same evaluation metrics as YOWO: frame-mAP and video-mAP. Frame-mAP calculates the precise-recall curve’s area under each frame and video-mAP measures the detected action tubes.

Experimental results

Firstly, we test our model on the aforementioned three public datasets, and the results are shown in Table 1, where our model achieves a frame-mAP of 92.44%, 78.28% and 79.84%, respectively. Compared with the YOWO and other models, we achieve better performances on the UCF-Sports and JHMDB-21 datasets, especially on the JHMDB-21 dataset, where our model achieve a top level of 78.28%. However, our model performs somewhat worse on UCF101-24 dataset, and we think this is due to two main reasons: one is the quality of the image itself, which only has a resolution of (320 × 240); the other is the UCF101-24 dataset contains some complex scene-based action categories compared to the other two datasets, such as “BasketballDunk” and “VolleyballSpiking”, which means that a single image contains not only the target object but also a lot of other negative sample information. Thus, when dealing with these multi-target action categories, our 2D CNNs are limited in their localization capability.

Conclusion

In this paper, we provide a faster and more accurate network for human spatiotemporal action localization, we use 2D CNNs to extract spatial features for action localization, while using 3D CNNs to extract spatiotemporal features for action recognition. In addition, to further improve the localization accuracy of our model, we make an improvement to the 2D CNNs network by introducing a coordinate attention mechanism and utilize the CIoU loss instead of the coordinate offset loss for bounding box regression. As a result, our model has fewer parameters and faster inference than the YOWO model. We test our model on three datasets, and all of them perform well, especially on two lightweight datasets, UCF-Sports and JHMDB-21, where our model improves 17.09% and 7.15% on frame-mAP (@IoU 0.5) compared to the YOWO model. However, our model also has certain drawbacks, when facing complex multi-person scenes, such as “BasketballDunk” and “VolleyballSpiking” in the UCF101-24 dataset, our model has poor localization effect. Therefore, in future work, we will further improve the localization capabilities of our model to adapt to complex multi-person motion scenarios.
