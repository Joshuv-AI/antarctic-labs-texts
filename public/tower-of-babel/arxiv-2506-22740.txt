Explanations are a Means to an End
Jessica Hullman* jhullman@northwestern.edu, Ziyang Guo* ziyanggyo2027@u.northwestern.edu, Berk Ustun berk@ucsd.edu

Abstract
Modern methods for explainable machine learning are designed to describe how models map inputs to outputs—without deep consideration of how these explanations will be used in practice. This paper argues that explanations should be designed and evaluated with a specific end in mind. We describe how to formalize this end in a framework based in statistical decision theory. We show how this functionally-grounded approach can be applied across diverse use cases, such as clinical decision support, providing recourse, or debugging. We demonstrate its use to characterize the maximum “boost” in performance on a particular task that an explanation could provide an idealized decision-maker, preventing misuse due to ambiguity by forcing researchers to specify concrete use cases that can be analyzed in light of models of expected explanation use. We argue that evaluation should meld theoretical and empirical perspectives on the value of explanation, and contribute definitions that span these perspectives.

Introduction

Researchers in engineering disciplines often lose their appetite for rigor when problems involve people. In machine learning, this reflex is most pronounced when we turn to interpretability and explainability.
In contrast to other areas of machine learning that involve people such as privacy or fairness, researchers rarely define the concepts that they care about
[
Lipton 2018
,
Gilpin et al. 2018
]
, nor do they demonstrate why they matter in practice
[
Doshi-Velez and Kim 2017
,
Weld and Bansal 2019
]
. Many methods are motivated by casual claims on the inherent value of “understanding” and “trust,” and validated on the basis of internal forms of validity such as “fidelity” and “local accuracy.”

Over the past decade, researchers recognize that the science is broken on multiple fronts. On the one hand, we lack standards for evaluation and validation, including reliable metrics and user studies
[
Buçinca et al. 2020
,
Doshi-Velez and Kim 2017
,
Lage et al. 2019
,
Mohseni et al. 2021
]
. On the other, we lack theoretical foundations and formal analyses
[
Amgoud and Ben-Naim 2022
,
Chen et al. 2022
,
Garreau and Luxburg 2020
,
Jacovi and Goldberg 2021
]
. In spite of these issues, the field continues to grow. Papers at top conferences routinely propose new methods with theoretical guarantees and complex visualization and tables that highlight gains over the state-of-the-art. Practitioners in other disciplines rely on them methods to validate their hypothesis
[
Coussement and Benoit 2021
,
Oviedo et al. 2022
]
, or meet regulatory requirements
[
Veale 2019
,
Cheon et al. 2025
]
.

We see two broader reasons to worry about the lack of consensus and coherence. As a field, we are showing signs of long-term stagnation, as the critiques raised in early explainability research are now resurfacing in current work on mechanistic interpretability
[
Sharkey et al. 2025
]
. Second, our methods are increasingly deployed in settings where they can mislead by enabling inference without ensuring adequate understanding of what these tools capture
[
Kaur et al. 2020
]
, let alone if they were designed with such use cases in mind.

Our position.

Many of the problems in explainability research stem from a basic framing error: explanations are treated as ends in themselves. In machine learning, we tend to value general-purpose tools—algorithms that are robust across tasks and domains. This norm leads researchers to design explanations without considering their value to any specific use case. Instead of asking
what the explanation is for
, researchers focus on how to generate explanations that seem plausible on the surface or that satisfy internal consistency metrics like “fidelity” or “local accuracy.” As a result, explanations are often developed and evaluated without ever specifying what decisions they are meant to support or how they will be used in practice.

We argue that explanations should be cast as
a means to an end
. More precisely, explanations are only valuable insofar as they lead to better outcomes in a well-defined task. Our work introduces a decision-theoretic framework to realize this vision, in which the
value of explanation
is the expected improvement in performance on the associated task. We show how this approach can be applied widely to diverse use cases for explanations, such as clinical decision support, providing recourse, or debugging models.
This formalism helps in several key ways: first, by forcing specificity in claims made about explanation effects, preventing misuse stemming from ambiguity about how explanations are meant to be used; second, by enabling researchers to more precisely characterize the value of explanation. We present an evaluation workflow based in three formal definitions of the value of explanation: the
theoretic value of explanation
representing the best case improvement in task performance that an explanation could bring, the
human-complementary value of explanation
representing the potential for the explanation to deliver relevant information over human judgments, and the
behavioral value of explanation
, representing the treatment effect of the explanation on target human users.

Explanations as Decision Support

We consider a supervised learning task where a human decision-maker (i.e., an
agent
) is given a model
f:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}
that predicts a
label

y\in\mathcal{Y}
from a feature vector
\textbf{{x}}\in\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We assume that
f
is the empirical risk minimizer trained on a labeled dataset
\mathcal{D}_{train}={(x_{i},y_{i})}_{i=1}^{n}
, and we focus on its predictions for test instances where labels are unknown. Given a test input
x
, we denote the model’s prediction as
\hat{y}=f(\textbf{{x}})\in\mathcal{Y}
.

We consider settings where the agent may be given additional information about the model’s behavior in the form of an explanation. This explanation may come from a local method
[
Ribeiro et al. 2016
,
Lundberg and Lee 2017
, e.g.,]
, a global method
[
Bhattacharjee et al. 2025
,
Covert et al. 2020
, e.g.,]
, or from an inherently interpretable model
[
Ustun and Rudin 2019
,
Liu et al. 2022
]
. We denote any such explanation method as a function
\mathcal{E}:\mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Z}
, where
\mathcal{Z}
is the space of explanations. The structure of
\mathcal{Z}
depends on the form of explanation—e.g., it could be a set of feature importance scores, rules, or parameters of a transparent model. Given a model prediction
\hat{y}=f(\textbf{{x}})
, we define the associated explanation as
z=\mathcal{E}(\hat{y},\textbf{{x}})
.

In some cases, we may also wish to model how the agent would behave in the absence of the model. We represent the agent’s behavior in such settings using an
internal model

f^{H}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}
, and denote their prediction as
y^{H}=f^{H}(\textbf{{x}})\in\mathcal{Y}
.

Use Cases as Decision Problems

We take a pragmatic perspective that assumes that understanding a model or explaining its prediction is a
means to an end
. Explanations are valuable to the extent that they improve a user’s ability to select among actions. Consequently, we argue that
new XAI methods should be presented along with the decision task(s) they are intended to benefit.

Given a prediction task where we would present a human with an explanation, we specify its objectives and value by defining a
decision problem

Savage 1972

Definition 1
.

A
decision problem
describes a scenario in which an agent must choose an
action

a\in\mathcal{A}
under uncertainty. It consists of the following elements:

•

An
action space

\mathcal{A}
, a set of actions available to the decision-maker.

•

A
state space

\mathcal{S}
, a set of mutually exclusive states of the world.

•

A
utility function
(or
scoring rule
)
u:\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{S}\to\mathbb{R}
that scores action–state pairs.

•

A probability distribution
p
over
\mathcal{S}
.

We denote the true state at the time of the decision as
s
, a realization of the random variable
S\in\mathcal{S}
with distribution
p
. The quality of a decision is given by its
expected utility
:

u(a,p)=\mathbb{E}_{s\sim p}\left[u(a,s)\right].

This definition formalizes an use case for explanations using the elements of statistical decision theory
[
Savage 1972
]
. These elements can be specified for any use case where it is possible to define a ground-truth state and where there is uncertainty about that state at the time of the decision. As we show in Examples
4
to
6
, this includes major use cases for explanations or interpretability such as medical decision-making, providing recourse, and model debugging.

Explanations as Information Signals

A decision problem provides the foundation to determine the value of explanations. In this setup, we represent the information available to the decision maker at each instance as a
signal

V\in\mathcal{V}
. By default, we assume that a human decision-maker would have access to the following information for each decision: the features of the instance
X=\textbf{{x}}
, the model prediction
\hat{Y}=\hat{y}
, the explanation
Z=z
, and their internal prediction
\hat{Y}^{H}=\hat{y}^{H}
. Conditional on these, they select an action
a\in\mathcal{A}
.

We evaluate the performance of agents on a decision problem with respect to a
data-generating distribution
.

Definition 2
.

A
data-generating distribution

p
is a joint distribution of information signals and state
p\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{V}\times\mathcal{S})
from which the distribution of the state
p(s)
is derived, where
p(s)
denotes the probability that
S=s
.
This joint distribution assigns to each possible pairing of signal
V=v
and state
S=s
a probability
p(v,s)
.

Appendix

A
describes how to estimate a data-generating distribution from a labeled evaluation dataset.

Definition 3
.

A
decision task
is defined by the combination of a decision problem (
Definition

1
) and a data-generating distribution (
Definition

2
).

Practical Considerations

Naturally, there will be many ways to formalize general use cases such as “model debugging” or “decision support” as specific decision tasks. Beyond aiming to capture the options and incentives that operate in practice, evaluators can benefit from looking to decision theory to guide their choices. For example, when the action space is on beliefs, a distinction is made between proper and non-proper scoring rules, where only the former can be said to incentivize agents to report their true beliefs (as they cannot obtain a higher score by deviating)
[
Gneiting and Raftery 2007
]
.

At a higher level, specifying use cases as decision problems serves as a forcing function: it prompts researchers and practitioners to reflect on why, when, and how an explanation is meant to help.
Consider, for example, the use of visual explanations during model development to help a developer understand what latent concepts a model has learned
[
Subramonyam and Hullman 2023
]
. One could define a utility function that rewards identifying distinct concepts distinguished by the model’s internal representations. Alternatively, one could define utility in terms of downstream improvements in model performance that arise from the developer better understanding what and how the model is learning. Both are valid, but the latter provides a more compelling demonstration of the real-world value of the explanations.

2.1
Measuring the Value of Explanations

How do we evaluate the value of explanations? In a decision theoretic framework, performance is defined by the utility assigned to decisions over the data-generating distribution. Consequently, the value of any signal can be understood by considering how an agent’s expected performance
changes
when they get access to that signal.
Thus any measure of the
value of explanation
should correspond to the
improvement in expected decision performance of an agent associated with having access to the explanation
.

But how exactly do signals affect agents’ performance on a task?
Intuitively, a signal is useful for a decision task to the extent that it conveys information about the state to the agent.
For example, consider two ways of explaining a binary model prediction as in
Example

4
: a calibrated posterior probability versus an ordinal (low-medium-high) expression. The former clearly contains more information about the state.

However, requiring an explanation to directly contain information about the state over the instance features or model prediction may preclude explanations that are valuable to imperfect decision-makers. Consider two ways an explanation can help an agent gain information about the state:

•

It can
provide information about the state directly
beyond that contained in the other signals as in providing a posterior probability with a prediction.

•

It can help them
better integrate information about the state into their decision
, such as when a feature importance explanation causes an agent to weight a prediction more in their decision.

As we show below, the distinction between these two mechanisms becomes relevant as we compare idealized versus behavioral decision-makers. Consequently, we argue that a reasonable
minimum requirement for an XAI method is to have the potential to help agents gain information about the state
. To demonstrate this is the case, proposals of new XAI methods must be accompanied by a model of the
expected use
of the information by an agent. Given a signal
v
, such a model should assign a distribution over actions representing the expected decisions of the target agent(s).

3
How Explanations Convey Information about the State

We describe how the expected use of an explanation by an agent will depend on the information they already have about the task, including their knowledge of the data-generating distribution, and their ability to respond optimally to the information they have.

3.1
How Idealized Agents Use Explanations

What is the value of explanation to an idealized, maximally-informed learner and utility maximizer? We start by defining, for a given signal
v
, the best decision that can be made by any agent as that made by a rational Bayesian agent with prior beliefs
p(s)=\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}p(v,s)
and knowledge of the data-generating distribution
p\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{V}\times\mathcal{S})
. Upon observing a signal
v
, this agent uses their knowledge of
p
to Bayesian update their prior beliefs to arrive at
posterior beliefs
for each state
s\in\mathcal{S}
:

\displaystyle p(s\mid v)=\frac{p(v,s)}{\sum\nolimits_{s\in\mathcal{S}}p(v,s)}

(1)

They maximize their expected performance by selecting the
utility-maximizing action
under those beliefs. Their expected performance over the data-generating distribution is:

\displaystyle\textrm{R}_{V}:=\mathbb{E}_{v\sim p(\cdot)}\left[\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\mathbb{E}_{s\sim p(\cdot)}[u(a,s)\mid V=v]\right]

(2)

where
\textrm{R}(\cdot)
denotes the
rational agent benchmark
: the expected performance over the data-generating distribution of taking the utility-maximizing action after observing the signal, where the outer expectation is taken over
p(v)
, and the inner expectation is taken over
p(s\mid v)
.
As a convenience for later defining the value of an explanation over some other signal, we will slightly abuse notation and use
\textrm{R}_{V_{1}\cup V_{2}}
to represent the expected performance of the rational agent who observes a combination of
V_{1}
and
V_{2}
, i.e.,

\textrm{R}_{V_{1}\cup V_{2}}:=\mathbb{E}_{v\sim p(\cdot)}\left[\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\mathbb{E}_{s\sim p(\cdot)}[u(a,s)\mid V_{1}=v_{1},V_{2}=v_{2}]\right]

The rational agent benchmark
upper bounds
the expected performance over the data-generating distribution of any agent restricted to the same signals. We can quantify the theoretic value of any signal by comparing the rational agent benchmark to the best performance of an agent who ignores the signals entirely. We refer to the latter performance as the
rational baseline
following
[
Wu et al. 2023
]
:

\displaystyle\textrm{R}_{\varnothing}=\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\mathbb{E}_{s\sim p(\cdot)}[u(a,s)]

(3)

The difference between the rational agent benchmark and rational agent baseline is the
value of information

\Delta
of the signal.

The Value of Explanation to an Idealized Agent

We want to know what value the explanation component of the signal has to the maximally-informed, rational Bayesian agent. In other words, how does their expected performance change when we change the signal structure from
V=X\cup\hat{Y}\cup Z
to
V=X\cup\hat{Y}
?

Proposition 7
.

Given a set of features
X
, a model prediction
\hat{Y}
, and an explanation
Z
generated by a function taking as input features and model prediction (
Section

2
), gaining access to the explanation does not improve the expected performance of the idealized agent, i.e.,

\textrm{R}_{X\cup\hat{Y}\cup Z}=\textrm{R}_{X\cup\hat{Y}}

(4)

Theorem 8

((Informal proof) Blackwell’s informativeness theorem
[
Blackwell 1953
]
)
.

Given a decision task, let
V_{1}
and
V_{2}
be two random variables, with the conditional probabilities as
\sigma_{1}=p(v_{1}\mid s)
and
\sigma_{2}=p(v_{2}\mid s)
respectively. If there exists a function
f
such that
\sigma_{2}=f(\sigma_{1})
, then
\textrm{R}_{V_{1}}\geq\textrm{R}_{V_{2}}
.

We prove
Proposition

7
by showing that there exists a function
f_{1}
such that
p(\textbf{{x}},\hat{y},z\mid s)=f_{1}(p(\textbf{{x}},\hat{y}\mid s))
and a function
f_{2}
such that
p(\textbf{{x}},\hat{y}\mid s)=f_{2}(p(\textbf{{x}},\hat{y},z\mid s))
. Then by Blackwell’s informativeness theorem, we have that
\textrm{R}_{X\cup\hat{Y}\cup Z}=\textrm{R}_{X\cup\hat{Y}}
.

The first function
f_{1}
can be constructed using the fact that explanations are deterministic functions of the features and model prediction, i.e.,
Z=\mathcal{E}(X,\hat{Y})
.
Therefore, the conditional distribution of the explanation given
X
and
\hat{Y}
is deterministic, i.e.,
p(z\mid\textbf{{x}},\hat{y},s)=\mathbf{1}_{z=\mathcal{E}(\textbf{{x}},\hat{y})}
.
Then, we can construct the function
f_{1}
by Bayes’ rule:
p(\textbf{{x}},\hat{y},z\mid s)=p(z\mid\textbf{{x}},\hat{y},s)\cdot p(\textbf{{x}},\hat{y}\mid s)
.

The function
f_{2}
is straightforward to construct by calculating the conditional marginal distribution of
X
and
\hat{Y}
given
s
over the joint distribution
p(\textbf{{x}},\hat{y},z\mid s)
, i.e.,
p(\textbf{{x}},\hat{y}\mid s)=\sum_{z}p(\textbf{{x}},\hat{y},z\mid s)
.

Proposition

7
arises due to the fact that the explanations generated by the function
\mathcal{E}
is a “garbling”
[
Marschak and Miyasawa 1968
]
of the model prediction
\hat{Y}
and features
X
; i.e., at best they are equally informative. Similarly, given access to
X
, the rational agent gains no additional value from the prediction
\hat{Y}
.

3.2
How Human Agents Can Use Explanations

Proposition

7
is in a sharp contrast with our expectations about human agents, who we
do
expect to behave differently with access to an explanation. Consequently, for
XAI methods that map from
\mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{X}
to a space of explanations to improve human decision performance, it must be the case that humans’ expected performance deviates from idealized use of the information in
\mathcal{X}
.

To provide intuition for how explanations that do not directly convey information on the state can help humans, we consider how explanations can help two types of boundedly-rational agents who face cognitive costs in arriving at posterior beliefs or optimizing their action given their beliefs. We assume that both types of agents are rational in the sense of requiring evidence according to their internal model
f^{H}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}
in order to change their decision strategy given an explanation.

Misinformed Agents.

The misinformed agent has access to a less informative data-generating distribution than the rational agent.
Denote the optimal posterior beliefs of the rational agent upon receiving the signal
v
as
p^{\textrm{opt}}(v)=p(s|v)
. The misinformed agent arrives at different distribution
p^{\textrm{mis}}(v)=p^{\textrm{opt}}(v)+\xi
, where
\xi\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})
represents error affecting their beliefs. Therefore, the misinformed agent selects actions that are suboptimal relative to the rational agent:

a^{\textrm{mis}}(v)\in\argmax_{a}\mathbb{E}_{s\sim p^{\textrm{mis}}(v)}[u(a,s)]

(5)

\mathbb{E}_{(v,s)\sim p}[u(a^{\textrm{mis}}(v),s)]\leq\mathbb{E}_{(v,s)\sim p}[u(a^{\textrm{opt}}(v),s)]

(6)

Explanations may help the misinformed agent arrive at a more informative distribution over the state, i.e.,
p^{\textrm{mis}}_{z}(v)=p(s|\hat{y},z)+\xi_{z}
and
p^{\textrm{mis}}(v)=p(s|\hat{y},\textbf{{x}})+\xi_{\textbf{{x}}}
where
\xi_{z}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{z}^{2})
,
\xi_{\textbf{{x}}}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{\textbf{{x}}}^{2})
, and
\sigma_{z}^{2}<\sigma_{\textbf{{x}}}^{2}
. This occurs when explanations
imply the posterior distribution more directly than the features to agents, which we argue is an implicit assumption behind presenting explanations.
For example, by providing feature importance scores for a prediction, SHAP and LIME may make it less computationally expensive to get
p(s|\hat{y},z)
than it would be to get
p(s|\textbf{{x}})
. The misinformed agent might accept the posterior implied by the explanation whenever their internal model does not obviously contradict it (e.g.,
p(s|\textbf{{x}})
is not 0 or 1).

Misoptimizing Agents.

The misoptimizing agent has access to the true data-generating model, but fails to optimize their decisions conditional on their beliefs:

a^{\textrm{failopt}}(v)=\softmax_{a}\mathbb{E}_{s\sim p(\cdot|v)}[u(a,s)]

(7)

where
\softmax
represents noise in the agent’s action selection due to their failure to optimize, e.g., due to bounded computational resources or time.

Explanations can help the misoptimizing agent by enabling them to assess the correctness of the model prediction. For example, in a prediction task (where
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{S}
), by providing information in feature space, which is also part of the agent’s internal model, the explanation may help the agent estimate the posterior probability of the correctness of the prediction
p(\hat{y}=s\mid\hat{y},z)
, allowing them to integrate the prediction into their decision, e.g.,
a(v)=a^{\textrm{failopt}}(v)\cdot p(\hat{y}\neq s\mid\hat{y},z)+\hat{y}\cdot p(\hat{y}=s\mid\hat{y},z)
.
A heuristic strategy for the misoptimzing agent with SHAP or LIME is to form beliefs about
\hat{p}(\hat{y}=s\mid\hat{y},z)
by checking how much the importance scores highlighted by the explanation align with predictions under their internal model.

Uninformed Agents.

A decision theoretic perspective also helps clarify that agents who require internal evidence to change their decision strategy based on an explanation cannot benefit from them unless they have
some
prior information about the data-generating distribution.
Consider an agent with no information about the joint distribution over features and state and no information about the joint distribution over model predictions and state (i.e., a uniform distribution). This agent would have no ground for appraising the correctness of AI prediction or the explanation. The explanation would need to directly contain state information, such as by expressing the probability that AI prediction is correct.
Fok and Weld 2024
argue that such “verifiability” is required for an explanation to aid model-assisted decisions. Our framing makes clear that agents can benefit from explanations that don’t contain state information directly, but this depends on the alignment between their internal model and the information about the features provided by the explanation, in addition to the particular ways in which they deviate from rational behavior.
In this sense our decision-theoretic approach expands upon
Chen et al. 2022
’s point that for such explanations to improve human-AI complementarity requires assumptions about decision-makers “intuitions” about the true data generating distribution.

Upper Bounding the Value of Explanation for Human Agents

Above we show how explanations can help human agents better use available information about an instance, even if the explanations do not directly contain information about the state. Whenever we do not expect the humans to have access to additional features over
X
, we can use the value of the instance-level information to the rational agent to identify the potential of the explanation to improve human performance.

Definition 9
.

The
theoretic value of explanation

\Delta_{\mathcal{E}}

is the expected change in score of the rational Bayesian agent when they have access to the features
x
versus only the prior:

\Delta_{\mathcal{E}}=\textrm{R}_{X}-\textrm{R}_{\varnothing}
.

Example 0

(Home Pricing)
.

A real estate agent estimates home prices, with access to features and a predictive model that estimates the overall condition of the home. The agent aims to estimate prices close to market value and minimize the absolute percentage error.

•

State
s\in\mathbb{R}
, the price at which the home sells.

•

Action
a\subset\mathbb{R}
, the agent’s estimated home price.

•

Utility

u(a,s)=-\frac{\lvert s-a\rvert}{s}

•

Signals
V=X\cup\hat{Y}\cup Z
.

–

X=X_{1}\cup X_{2}\cup X_{3}
, the year built, the number of bedrooms, and the building material.

–

\hat{Y}
, a score between 0 and 10 predicting the overall condition of the home.

–

Z
, a set of feature importance scores on the predicted score.

The theoretic value of the explanation
Z
is the difference in expected score of the rational agent knowing the year built, the number of bedrooms, and the building material versus when they only know the prior distribution over the home price, which is 24.2% in Mean Absolute Percentage Error (MAPE) (95% CI: [22.4, 26.1]). Larger theoretic values of explanation imply more room for the explanation to improve performance in theory. See
Appendix

B
for full details and results.

The theoretic value of explanation provides a means of validating–
before deploying the explanation method with human decision-makers
–that an XAI method has the potential to improve agents’ performance on a target decision task or class of decision tasks.
If discriminating between individual instances leads to little improvement in the rational agent’s performance over not using the signals at all, then a priori, one cannot argue that there is good reason to expect explanations to improve agents’ performance. The theoretic value of explanation
\Delta_{\mathcal{E}}
therefore stands to make more rigorous and efficient current XAI evaluation practice, where in the absence of a theoretical foundation the only way to evaluate a method is by running a costly user study.

Specifically, we can compare
\Delta_{\mathcal{E}}
to the rational baseline
\textrm{R}_{\varnothing}
as a sanity check prior to deploying a new explanation technique with agents. If the value of information is small relative to the baseline, this tells us that agents have little to gain from better discriminating instances.

4
Demonstrating the Value of Explanation with Behavioral Agents

When we understand explanations as signals designed to help agents gain information about the state, how to evaluate a new XAI method is no longer mysterious. Evaluators should formulate a decision task (or class of tasks) that they believe explanations stand to improve, and evaluate how expected performance changes as a result of access to information that the explanation may provide directly or “unlock” in other signals.

We discuss how to use the theoretic value of explanation for this purpose in
Section

3.2
.
However, as a form of interventional science, evaluations of XAI methods with human decision-makers must ultimately be conducted to evaluate claims about the value of explanation. We propose two behaviorally-conditioned definitions of the value of explanation, to be applied after the theoretic value of explanation is used to confirm the potential for explanations to improve performance.

4.1
Potential Human-Complementary Value of Explanation

Another way to to “sanity check” the potential for an explanation to benefit human decisions is to check that the information about the state that the explanation may help human agents extract carries value
over and above
the information the human agents already have. In other words,
explanations should have the potential to help an agent gain additional decision-relevant (i.e., human-complementary) information about the state over that already available to them
.
Following common assumptions in information economic models of behavior (see, e.g., signaling theory
[
Spence 1978
]
), we assume that
any information that is available to an agent will eventually be revealed through their predictions
\hat{Y}
.
We assume the evaluator can access such predictions by querying human experts on a set of instances.

Adapting the approach of
[
Guo et al. 2024
]
, we can define the potential for the explanation to provide human-complementary value as the expected improvement in the performance of the rational Bayesian agent when they have access to the human predictions
and
the features versus when they only have access to the human predictions. Recall from
Proposition

7
that because it is a garbling of the features, the explanation offers no additional value to the rational agent with the features, and similarly the prediction is also a garbling of the features.

Definition 11
.

The
potential complementary value of explanation

\Delta_{\mathcal{E}_{\textrm{compl}}}

is the expected improvement in the performance of the rational Bayesian agent when they have access to the human prediction
\hat{Y}^{H}
and features
X
for each instance versus when they lack access to
X
:
\Delta_{\mathcal{E}_{\textrm{compl}}}=\textrm{R}_{\hat{Y}^{H}\cup X}-\textrm{R}_{\hat{Y}^{H}}
.

Example 0

(Home Pricing)
.

The theoretical potential complementary value of the explanation
Z
is the difference in expected score of the rational agent knowing the human prediction on the home price, the year built, the number of bedrooms, and the building material of the house versus when they only know the human’s price prediction, which is 9% (95% CI: [8.1, 9.9]) in MAPE. Recall that the theoretic value of explanation unconditioned on human judgments is 24.2%, hence the human predictions contain significant overlapping information with the features. See
Appendix

B
for full details and results.

By comparing it to the performance of the rational agent with only the human predictions, the potential complementary value of explanation
\Delta_{\mathcal{E}_{\textrm{compl}}}
can be used prior to observing human agents using the explanation, to confirm that explanations have the potential to improve their performance by helping them make better use of the available information. We demonstrate the usage of
\Delta_{\mathcal{E}_{\textrm{compl}}}
in
Appendix

B
, and also illustrate that the features convey greater potential complementary value than the explanation alone, hence their use in
Definition

11
. Additionally, both the theoretic value of explanation and potential human-complementary value of explanation provide additional insight for evaluation when combined with a robust analysis approach that considers ambiguity in the decision task definition, such as different scoring rules. See
[
Guo et al. 2025
]
for a demonstration.

4.2
Behavioral Value of Explanation

The canonical way to quantify the value of an intervention is to estimate its average treatment effect (ATE), the average expected difference in the outcome when the intervention is assigned versus when it is not
[
Angrist and Pischke 2014
]
. Once we have checked that the explanation carries the potential to improve decision performance among the target population by complementing humans’ information, we can design a controlled comparison between the performance of human agents with and without access to explanations to estimate the behavioral treatment effect.

Definition 13
.

Let
p^{B}
be the joint distribution over the human decisions and state, i.e.,
p^{B}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{S})
when human agents have access to “full” signals that include the explanation and AI prediction (
v=\{\textbf{{x}},\hat{y},\hat{y}^{H},z\})
.
Let
B=\mathbb{E}_{(a,s)\sim p^{B}}u(a,s)
be the expected decision loss. Let
p^{B}_{\neg\mathcal{E}}
be the joint distribution over the human decisions and state when human agents have the same signal minus the explanation (
v=\{\textbf{{x}},\hat{y},\hat{y}^{H}\})
, with associated decision loss
B_{\neg\mathcal{E}}=\mathbb{E}_{(a,s)\sim p_{\neg\mathcal{E}}^{B}}u(a,s)
. The
behavioral value of explanation

\Delta_{\mathcal{E}_{\textrm{behavioral}}}
is the difference in expected score of a human agent when they have access to the explanation versus when they do not:
\Delta_{\mathcal{E}_{\textrm{behavioral}}}=B-B_{\neg\mathcal{E}}

For some study designs
\Delta_{\mathcal{E}_{\textrm{behavioral}}}
can be estimated from the raw study results, but as a general rule we assume that we have isolated the effect of the explanation on performance by fitting a structural statistical model (e.g., a multiple regression) to estimate the effect of access to the explanation while controlling for confounding factors like trial order or random effects of specific human participants
[
Yarkoni and Westfall 2017
]
. See
[
Guo et al. 2024
]
for examples of fitting such models to the results of studies on how explanations impact reliance.

Note that unlike
\Delta_{\mathcal{E}}
and
\Delta_{\mathcal{E}_{\textrm{compl}}}
,
\Delta_{\mathcal{E}_{\textrm{behavioral}}}
may be negative, representing the case when providing the explanation worsens performance. Note also that unlike
\Delta_{\mathcal{E}_{\textrm{compl}}}
,

\Delta_{\mathcal{E}_{\textrm{behavioral}}}
may
exceed

\Delta_{\mathcal{E}}
. This can occur when behavioral participants do worse than the rational agent with only the prior (
\textrm{R}_{\varnothing}
) when they don’t have the explanation, but are able to extract significant information from the signals once given access to the explanation.

Interpreting the magnitude of the behavioral value of explanation.

Once we have calculated the behavioral value of explanation, we can interpret its magnitude through comparison to the idealized agent. How does the observed score improvement owing to the explanation
\Delta_{\mathcal{E}_{\textrm{behavioral}}}
compare to the value of all of the signals except human decisions
\Delta_{\mathcal{E}}
and all of the signals including the human decisions
\Delta_{\mathcal{E}_{\textrm{compl}}}
to a rational agent?

We can also compare the observed performance with the explanation
B
with the rational agent benchmark
\textrm{R}_{X}
, to see how close participants come to extracting the total available information. We can check where
B
falls along the span created by
\Delta_{\mathcal{E}}
, keeping in mind that it could be below
\textrm{R}_{\varnothing}
. Intuitively, we want the impact of the explanation on behavioral agents
\Delta_{\mathcal{E}_{\textrm{behavioral}}}
to be large relative to the rational agent baseline
\textrm{R}_{\varnothing}
representing expected performance from taking the best fixed action under the prior.

5
Alternative Views

Early work in interpretability and explainability led to a number of position papers. For example,
Lipton 2018
calls for a rigorous definition for “interpretability” while
Doshi-Velez and Kim 2017
call for greater rigor in evaluating explainability techniques. More recent calls concern support for evaluating human hypotheses
[
Miller 2023
]
, actionability
[
Feng and Tan 2022
]
, and interactivity
[
Lakkaraju et al. 2022
]
, among others. Our work meets these calls by providing a functionally-grounded model of how explanations add value.

The lack of rigor has drawn attention from theoretical researchers, who seek to study the formal properties of explanations
[
Garreau and Luxburg 2020
,
Amgoud and Ben-Naim 2022
, see e.g.,]
, and to develop
axiomatic
methods to return explanations that satisfy salient properties.
Some research pursues methods that obey “rigorous guarantees”
[
Tennenholtz and Zohar 2016
,
Procaccia 2019
, see e.g.,]
such as on the “faithfulness" of their explanations. For example, Shapley-value explanations
[
Lundberg and Lee 2017
]
are the unique explanation that satisfies salient properties such as “local accuracy”, “missingness’ and “consistency". However, there is no corresponding guarantee that faithfulness is necessary nor sufficient for explanations to improve human understanding or use of model predictions
[
Páez 2019
]
.

Other work points to foundational problems with explanations, such as the fact that a prediction can admit multiple explanations
[
Karimi et al. 2022
,
Darwiche 2023
]
, which may contradict one another
[
Krishna et al. 2022
]
, provide room for manipulation

[
Aïvodji et al. 2019
,
Bordt et al. 2022
,
Slack et al. 2020
]

and vary across the set of across all good models
[
Marx et al. 2020
,
Brunet et al. 2022
]
.
Some have argued that the most common form explanations–those that stop at relating features to predictions–cannot help humans achieve appropriate trust and reliance
[
Fok and Weld 2024
]
. In contrast, we use a decision-theoretic framework to clarify that promoting explanations that are functions of the features and model prediction (but not the state) implies specific assumptions about the informedness of the human agents and their irrationality relative to an idealized Bayesian utility-maximizing agent.

Explanations are commonly promoted in the name of improving human trust and reliance on model predictions. A number of empirical studies do not find evidence that explanations lead to improved decisions or reliance over the model alone
[
Bansal et al. 2021
,
Buçinca et al. 2020
,
Buçinca et al. 2021
,
Lai and Tan 2019
,
Guo et al. 2024
]
, though problematic definitions of reliance and lack of reason to expect human-model complementarity in the studied scenarios may explain negative results
[
Guo et al. 2024
]
. We provide statistically-based tools intended to help researchers and evaluators avoid studying or deploying explanations in scenarios where they are unlikely to help.

An important counterargument to the standard, decision-theoretic utility-maximization approach we adopt argues that decision quality cannot always be measured in terms of optimizing utility according to prior estimates of a data-generating process. For example, some decisions (such as choosing which potentially “disruptive” start-up to invest in) are inherently “future-based” such that historical data is not necessarily predictive
[
Sako and Felin 2025
]
. Other arguments revolve around the idiosyncratic, individualized nature of some decisions (see, e.g.,
[
Meehl 1954
]
) or flexible nature of human causal theorizing
[
Felin and Holweg 2024
]
. We agree that some decision problems render the relevance of prior data or measure of utility questionable. To some extent, a robust analysis approach that considers ambiguity explicitly can help. However, future work should consider the potential for explanations to play very different roles in scenarios where decision theory is ill-equipped to quantify explanation value, such as those described in
[
Felin et al. 2025
]
.

Data-generating process with high-dimensional signals

The time complexity of the above algorithm is linear in the number of dimensions of the signal space.
However, in practice, high-dimensional signals, such as images and text that appear in prediction for decision problems can still be computationally infeasible to quantify with the above algorithm.
To address this issue, we propose a two-step approach to estimate the data-generating distribution from a set of observations.
First, we use a reduction model to reduce the dimensionality of the signal space.
Second, we use the estimated data-generating distribution using the reduced signal space in the above algorithm.

The optimal reduction model and its generated embeddings should fulfill two properties.
First, the generated signals should be as informative as possible for the decision task, such that they maintain the maximum information value contained in the original signals.
Second, the derived data-generating distribution that is implied by the embedding signals should be feasible to estimate from the observed dataset.
We quantify the above objectives by a similar algorithm based on Train/Test split.

Implications for evaluating explanations

It is important to note that when the signal structure is coarsened to avoid overfitting the benchmarks, the coarsened signals should be deployed in the user study used to estimate the behavioral value of explanation (
Definition

13
).
To understand why, imagine that the coarsened signals are used to estimate the data-generating distribution in calculating the theoretic and potential human complementary values of explanation, but that a user study conducted to estimate the behavioral value of explanation presents participants with the original high dimensional signals with model predictions and explanations applied to those signals. It is no longer necessarily the case that the rational agent’s expected score will upper bound the human participants’ expected score. Even if the human agents were shown a representation of the coarsened signals (e.g., a composite image created by superimposing a cluster of images in the original high dimensional space), if the model and explanation function are applied to the original signals, then the model prediction and resulting explanations are no longer garblings of the features and model prediction, respectively. Thus they may offer additional beneficial information over the features to the rational agent, such that the rational agents’ expected performance with only the features is no longer necessarily an upper bound on human performance.

Appendix B
Demonstration on House Pricing Task with Synthetic Human Data

We demonstrate the complementary value of explanations on the task of house price prediction, similar to recent crowdsourced studies
[
Chiang and Yin 2021
,
Hemmer et al. 2022
,
Poursabzi-Sangdeh et al. 2021
,
Holstein et al. 2023
]
. We use the Ames Iowa dataset
[
De Cock 2011
]
.
The task is to predict a house’s sale price using three features of the house, including
year built
, the number of
bedrooms
, and the
material of the exterior covering
, and a predicted score representing the overall condition of the home from a model.
The model is a XGBoost model trained on the dataset, which is split into a training set and a holdout set with an 80/20 split.
The model takes as input the same three features of the house that humans use to predict the house sale price.
We generate the explanations using the SHAP library
[
Lundberg and Lee 2017
]
.

We generate synthetic human predictions on the house sale price using a linear model, which is the sum of the three features:

\hat{y}^{H}=\beta_{0}+\beta_{1}\cdot X_{1}+\beta_{2}\cdot X_{2}+\beta_{3}\cdot X_{3}

(10)

Human predictions in various domains are well approximated by linear models without interactions
[
O’Hagan et al. 2006
]
. We fit the coefficients using the training set, which is labeled with ground truth sale prices.

We demonstrate the theoretical value and potential complementary value of features, model predictions, and SHAP explanations on the house sale price prediction task in
Figure

1
.
First, it can be seen that empirically, as our theoretical analysis in
Proposition

7
describes, the explanations do not offer any additional value over the features, and since the model predictions are also a function of the features, the model predictions also do not offer any additional value over the features.
The explanations however do offer additional value over the model predictions, which is consistent with the fact that the explanations are generated by a function that takes as input the features and the model prediction, and therefore contain information beyond that contained in the model prediction alone.

Second, we can see the theoretical value of all signals (features, model predictions, and SHAP explanations) is higher than the potential complementary value of the signals.
The difference between the theoretical value and the potential complementary value is the information value contained in the human predictions (which is simulated by the linear model in
Eq.

10
).
