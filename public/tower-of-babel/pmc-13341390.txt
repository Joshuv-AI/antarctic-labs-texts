Semaglutide Injection in Indian Patients With Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus: A Randomised, Phase III, Active‐Controlled Study
Authors: Unnikrishnan Ambika Gopalakrishnan, Ameya Sudhakar Joshi, Richa Giri, Shalin Jagdeep Shah, Jitendra Shukla, Sanjiv Maheshwari, Rekha M Chowdaiah, Manish Kumar Singh, Aditya Srirang Bari, Niranjan Pandurang Pathak, Akash Nitinbhai Shah, Shweta Bhandari, Ambanna Gowda, Ambrish Chandrappa, Pravin Dinkar Supe, Bharat Das, Prabhat Kumar Sharma, Mehul Kalubhai Virani, Prakash Harishchandra Kurmi, Gayatri Amit Ghanekar, Mani Deepathi Dasari, Banshidhar Sahoo, Sanjay Kumar Jangid, Prakash Sadashiv Shende, Sandesh Sahebarao Patil, Avinash Narayan Kumbhar, Shrikant Vishnu Deshpande, Madhusudhan Chennappa, Nitesh Chandubhai Patel, Dipesh Sonawane, Dipak Patil, Sucheta Pandit, Pravin Ghadge, Suyog Mehta

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimTo evaluate the efficacy, safety and immunogenicity of semaglutide injection (synthetic) (Test group) compared with the Reference semaglutide injection [Ozempic, (Reference group)] in Indian patients with Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM).Materials and MethodsThis Phase III, randomised, open‐label, multi‐centre, parallel‐group, active‐controlled non‐inferiority study was conducted across 35 centres in India. Adults aged 18–65 years with T2DM and baseline glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c) ≥ 7.0% to ≤ 10.5% despite stable metformin therapy and diet & exercise control were randomised (1:1) to receive subcutaneous injections of Test semaglutide or Reference semaglutide once weekly for 24 weeks, using an identical dose‐escalation regimen (from 0.25 to 2.0 mg per week). The primary endpoint was change in HbA1c from baseline to Week 24. Secondary endpoints included changes in fasting and post‐prandial blood glucose, bodyweight and HbA1c < 7.0% achievers, safety and immunogenicity.ResultsA total of 314 patients were randomised, and 290 patients completed the study. At Week 24, both treatments produced significant and comparable reductions in HbA1c (Test: −2.04%, Reference: −1.95%; p < 0.0001 within groups). The least‐squares mean difference (Test‐Reference) was −0.09% (95% CI: −0.26 to 0.09), meeting the pre‐specified non‐inferiority criterion. Improvements in fasting and post‐prandial blood glucose, patients achieving HbA1c < 7.0% and bodyweight were similar between the two groups. The most common treatment‐emergent adverse events were predominantly mild‐to‐moderate gastrointestinal events (Test vs. Reference: 43.3% vs. 46.5%). No anti‐drug or neutralising antibodies were detected.ConclusionsThe Test synthetic semaglutide injection demonstrated non‐inferior glycaemic efficacy, comparable safety in comparison to Reference semaglutide, supporting its use as an effective therapeutic option for patients with T2DM. Introduction

In 2024, an estimated 589 million adults aged 20–79 years were living with diabetes worldwide, corresponding to a global prevalence of 11.1%. Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) accounts for more than 90% of all diabetes cases globally. In India alone, an estimated 90 million adults were living with diabetes in 2024, highlighting the substantial contribution of the country to the global diabetes burden [1, 2]. Early diagnosis, effective glycaemic control and management of associated cardiometabolic risk factors can substantially reduce diabetes‐related morbidity and mortality [1, 3].

Despite the availability of multiple glucose‐lowering therapies, a substantial proportion of patients fail to achieve sustained glycaemic control, highlighting the need for effective and accessible treatment options [4, 5]. Poor glycaemic control is common and continues to fall short of targets recommended by the World Health Organization, which aims for at least 80% of individuals with diagnosed diabetes to achieve adequate glycaemic control [1, 6]. Although the progressive nature of T2DM necessitates treatment intensification over time, therapeutic inertia, suboptimal efficacy, tolerability concerns and limited accessibility contribute to persistent treatment gaps [3, 4, 7, 8].

Glucagon‐like peptide‐1 receptor agonists (GLP‐1 RAs) have become integral to T2DM management due to their superior glycaemic efficacy, favourable effects on bodyweight and low risk of hypoglycaemia [9]. Beyond glycaemic control, GLP‐1 RAs have demonstrated clinically significant cardiovascular and renal benefits across diverse patient populations, further supporting their role in comprehensive cardiometabolic risk management [10, 11, 12]. Semaglutide, a long‐acting GLP‐1 RA, is approved in many countries, including India, as a once‐weekly subcutaneous injection for significantly improving glycaemic control in adults with T2DM. However, high cost and limited availability significantly restrict access to semaglutide, particularly in resource‐constrained settings, including India [1, 10, 13, 14, 15].

With the rising global demand for GLP‐1 RAs, the development of synthetic or equivalent versions of established agents represents a potential strategy to expand patient access whilst maintaining comparable efficacy and safety [16, 17]. Therefore, Sun Pharma has developed a synthetic formulation of semaglutide injection. Side‐by‐side comparative analyses, including UPLC‐HRMS peptide mapping (Glu‐C and chymotrypsin digestion) with full sequence coverage and < 10 ppm mass accuracy, confirmed identical primary structure and fatty‐acid modification. Orthogonal physicochemical testing, in vitro bioassays, bioequivalence studies and the Phase III clinical trial further demonstrated equivalence in potency, impurity profiles, therapeutic outcomes and absence of immunogenicity signals. These findings are fully consistent with FDA principles for highly purified synthetic peptides referencing rDNA‐origin drugs and EMA principles for development and manufacture of synthetic peptides, whereby controlled peptide‐related impurities (same or lower levels than reference; and complying with pharmacopoeial requirements with justified non‐impact on safety/effectiveness), eliminate recombinant process contaminants whilst preserving full therapeutic interchangeability [16, 17].

Accordingly, the present Phase III, comparative, multi‐centre, parallel group, non‐inferiority study was conducted to evaluate the efficacy, safety and immunogenicity of semaglutide injection of Sun Pharma (Test group) in comparison to Reference semaglutide injection (Ozempic, Novo Nordisk, Reference group) in adults with T2DM inadequately controlled on metformin and lifestyle modification.

Materials and Methods

Study Design

This Phase III, randomised, open‐label, multi‐centre, parallel‐group, active‐controlled study was conducted across 35 centres in India to evaluate the efficacy, safety and immunogenicity of a Test semaglutide injection compared with Reference semaglutide injection (Ozempic) in patients with T2DM inadequately controlled on metformin therapy and lifestyle modification (CTRI/2025/02/080592). All laboratory analyses, including HbA1c measurements, were performed by site‐specific accredited laboratories whose personnel were unaware of the treatment assignment, thereby minimising ascertainment bias for the primary endpoint.

The study included a screening period of up to 2 weeks, a 24‐week treatment period, and a 2‐week post‐treatment safety follow‐up (Figure 1). The trial was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki [18], International Council for Harmonisation Good Clinical Practice guidelines [19], and applicable regulatory requirements in India [20, 21]. Ethics committee approval was obtained at each participating centre, and all participants provided written informed consent prior to screening.

Flowchart of the study. EOS: End of study; EOT: End of treatment; ET: Early termination.

Study Population

Adults aged 18 to 65 years with a diagnosis of T2DM and inadequate glycaemic control despite stable metformin therapy and lifestyle modification were eligible for the study. Patients were required to have a glycated haemoglobin value of ≥ 7.0% to ≤ 10.5% at screening and to have received a stable dose of metformin of ≥ 1500 mg/day, or the maximum tolerated dose, for ≥ 12 weeks prior to screening.

Key exclusion criteria were FBG ≥ 270 mg/dL or serum calcitonin level ≥ 50 ng/L at screening, treatment with any medication for diabetes or obesity within 90 days before screening (other than metformin, or short‐term insulin for ≤ 14 days), personal or family history of medullary thyroid carcinoma or multiple endocrine neoplasia syndrome, history of acute or chronic pancreatitis, proliferative diabetic retinopathy or maculopathy requiring acute treatment, significant renal or hepatic impairment, clinically significant conditions or laboratory abnormalities that could interfere with study participation or outcome interpretation or known contraindications to treatment with GLP‐1 receptor agonists. Patients with anticipated initiation or change in concomitant medications (for > 14 consecutive days or on a frequent basis) known to affect weight or glucose metabolism, pre‐planned surgery or medical procedure that would interfere with the study, and known alcohol or other substance abuse were also excluded.

Randomisation and Treatment

A computer generated central, stratified randomisation sequence was generated using SAS version 9.4 by the study biostatistician. The same was uploaded in the interactive web response system (IWRS) (Octalsoft) for assigning intervention with the individual randomisation number to the study patients based on the stratification factor, that is, baseline HbA1c ≤ 8.5% or > 8.5%. The investigators randomised the patients in a 1:1 ratio to receive either the Test semaglutide injection (manufactured by Sun Pharmaceutical Industries Limited) or the Reference semaglutide injection in an open‐label manner.

Study treatments were administered as once‐weekly subcutaneous injections using an identical dose‐escalation regimen to ensure comparability between treatment groups. All randomised patients initiated treatment at Week 0 with semaglutide administered at a dose of 0.25 mg once weekly for the first 4 weeks. At Week 4, all patients were up‐titrated to a dose of 0.5 mg once weekly, which was maintained for the subsequent 4‐week period. At Week 8, further dose adjustment was guided by glycaemic response. Patients with a HbA1c < 7.5% continued to receive a dose of 0.5 mg once weekly for an additional 4 weeks. Patients with HbA1c ≥ 7.5% at Week 8 were up‐titrated to a dose of 1.0 mg once weekly, which was administered for the next 4 weeks. Dose adjustment during Weeks 12, 16 and 20 followed a similar response‐guided approach based on HbA1c values assessed at each visit. Patients with HbA1c values < 7.5% continued on their ongoing semaglutide dose once weekly for the subsequent 4‐week interval. Patients with HbA1c ≥ 7.5% receiving a dose of 0.5 mg and 1 mg once weekly were up‐titrated to 1.0 mg and 2 mg once weekly, respectively. Patients with HbA1c ≥ 7.5% who were already receiving the maximum dose of 2.0 mg once weekly continued treatment at the same dose and were eligible to receive rescue medication in accordance with the predefined rescue medication criteria (Appendix S1). This response‐adaptive dose‐escalation strategy was applied uniformly across both treatment groups to optimise glycaemic control whilst maintaining tolerability.

Injections were administered subcutaneously in the abdomen, thigh or upper arm. The allowance of multiple injection sites is standard for subcutaneous GLP‐1 receptor agonists, such as semaglutide, to promote patient convenience, adherence and comfort.

In the event of a missed dose, the dose could be administered within 5 days of the scheduled dosing day; if not administered within this period, the missed dose was skipped and the next scheduled dose was administered as planned. All patients continued background metformin therapy at a dose of ≥ 1500 mg/day or the maximum tolerated dose, as documented in the medical record, throughout the study period, along with diet and exercise control.

Efficacy Assessments

Efficacy evaluations were conducted at scheduled visits during the 24‐week treatment period. The primary efficacy endpoint was the change from baseline in HbA1c to Week 24. Secondary efficacy endpoints included changes from baseline in FBG, PPBG, bodyweight, BMI, blood pressure and fasting lipid parameters, as well as the proportion of patients achieving HbA1c levels < 7.0%. Changes from baseline in HbA1c at other timepoints were also evaluated as secondary endpoints.

Safety and Immunogenicity Assessments

Safety assessments were performed throughout the study and included monitoring of treatment‐emergent adverse events (TEAEs), serious adverse events (SAEs) and the proportion of patients requiring hypoglycaemia management. Adverse events (AEs) were coded using the Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities version 28.0 and assessed for severity and relationship to study treatment using Common Terminology Criteria for AEs version 5.0 and World Health Organization—Uppsala Monitoring Centre criteria, respectively. Ophthalmological evaluations, including fundoscopy, were performed at baseline and at predefined study visits to monitor retinal safety.

Immunogenicity was evaluated using validated assays to assess the proportion of patients with anti‐drug antibodies (ADA) and neutralising antibodies (Nab) at baseline and at Week 24.

Statistical Analysis

The baseline characteristics were evaluated on the intention‐to‐treat (ITT) population, comprising all randomised patients. The efficacy analyses were conducted in the per‐protocol population (patients who completed the study without major protocol deviations), consistent with the principles of non‐inferiority trial design. The efficacy analyses in the ITT population were conducted as supporting analyses. Safety analyses were done in the safety population, which included all patients who received at least one dose of study treatment.

Within‐group changes from baseline over time were evaluated using repeated‐measures analysis of variance (RANOVA). Between‐group comparisons for continuous endpoints were performed using a mixed model for repeated measures (MMRM), with change from baseline as the dependent variable; treatment, visit, treatment‐by‐visit interaction, and stratification factor as fixed effects and the corresponding baseline value as a covariate. Least‐squares mean estimates with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported. Model parameters were estimated using restricted maximum likelihood with Kenward–Roger degrees of freedom, and an unstructured covariance matrix was used when feasible.

Non‐inferiority for the primary endpoint was concluded if the upper bound of the two‐sided 95% CI for the treatment difference (Test‐Reference) was below the prespecified margin of 0.4%.

Categorical endpoints were summarised using counts and percentages with 95% CIs. Within‐group proportions over time were descriptively evaluated, and between‐group comparisons were conducted using the Cochran–Mantel–Haenszel test adjusted for stratification factors.

Safety was assessed by summarising TEAEs and SAEs. Laboratory parameters, vital signs, physical examinations and ECG parameters were summarised descriptively as changes from baseline. Immunogenicity, including ADA and NAb, was assessed at baseline and Week 24 and summarised using proportions and 95% CIs, with between‐group comparisons performed using the Cochran–Mantel–Haenszel test.

Missing data were imputed using a multivariate normal distribution model across included variables. Missing data (15 patients in Test group and 11 patients in Reference group) were imputed by treatment and visit by using the SAS procedure proc. mi. A total of 1000 imputed datasets were generated, and the same statistical model, as mentioned for the primary analysis, was run for all 1000 imputed samples. Finally, the results were combined by using the SAS procedure proc. mi analyz. There was no impact on the study outcome. The sensitivity analyses were also conducted (Appendix S2).

A sample size of 266 provided 90% power to demonstrate non‐inferiority, assuming a margin of 0.4% for HbA1c, a standard deviation of 1.0% and a one‐sided significance level of 2.5%. Considering an attrition rate of 15%, a total of 314 patients (157 per group) were required.

Results

Patient Disposition

A total of 465 patients were screened, of whom 314 were randomised to receive either the Test semaglutide or Reference semaglutide injection. Overall, 290 patients completed the 24‐week treatment period. A total of 14 patients in the Test group and 10 patients in the Reference group did not complete the study due to various reasons including consent withdrawal, investigator's discretion, lost to follow‐up and occurrence of AE (Figure 2).

Patient disposition. ITT: Intention‐to‐treat; PP: Per‐protocol.

In addition, following the up‐titration scheme as per the protocol, a total of 56 patients in the Test group and 59 patients in the Comparator group reached the full weekly dose (2 mg) at Week 20. The mean weekly doses administered were comparable between the Test and Reference groups at Week 8 (0.79 and 0.83 mg/week), Week 12 (1.15 and 1.23 mg/week), Week 16 (1.18 and 1.23 mg/week) and Week 20 (1.19 and 1.22 mg/week), respectively, indicating similar dose escalation patterns across both treatment groups throughout the study.

Baseline Demographic and Clinical Characteristics

Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics were similar between the two treatment groups. Mean age (48.96 ± 8.92 in the Test group vs. 48.06 ± 9.01 in the Reference group), height (161.9 ± 8.29 in the Test group vs. 163.5 ± 8.50 in the Reference group), duration of diabetes, HbA1c, FBG, PPBG, bodyweight, BMI and cardiovascular parameters were comparable between groups (Table 1).

Primary Efficacy Outcome

In the PP population, a comparable reduction in mean HbA1c was observed between the Test and Reference groups at Week 24 (Test: −2.04%, Reference: −1.95%; p = 0.3429) (Figure 3a). The change from baseline in both groups at Week 24 was statistically significant (p < 0.0001 each). The estimated least‐squares mean difference between treatments was −0.09%, with a two‐sided 95% CI of −0.26% to 0.09%. The upper bound of the CI was below the predefined non‐inferiority margin of 0.4%, thereby demonstrating non‐inferiority of the Test to the Reference semaglutide injection.

Changes in (A) HbA1c, (B) fasting blood glucose, and (C) postprandial blood glucose from baseline*. *p < 0.0001 for change from baseline to each visit for HbA1c, fasting blood glucose, and postprandial blood glucose; HbA1c: glycated haemoglobin.

Similar results were observed in the ITT population, indicating a comparable reduction in mean HbA1c between the Test and Reference groups at Week 24 (Test: −2.00%, Reference: −1.95%; p = 0.6858) (Appendix S3). The change from baseline in both groups at Week 24 was statistically significant (p < 0.0001 each).

Secondary Efficacy Outcomes

Change in Glycaemic Parameters From Baseline

Significant reductions in HbA1c, FBG and PPBG were evident as early as Week 4 and continued to decrease throughout the treatment period in both groups (Figure 3). The improvements in HbA1c, FBG and PPBG over time were comparable between the Test and Reference semaglutide groups, with no statistically significant between‐group differences at any visit (p ≥ 0.05), except the PPBG values at Weeks 20 and 24, where the Test demonstrated significant improvements over the Reference semaglutide injection. However, the between‐group difference in PPBG at Weeks 20 and 24 should be interpreted with caution, as this finding may reflect chance variation in the context of multiple comparisons and should be viewed as exploratory in nature.

Proportion of Patients Achieving HbA1c < 7.0%

The proportion of patients achieving HbA1c < 7.0% increased over time in both treatment groups, with no significant between‐group difference. At Week 8, 13.4% and 15.8% in the Test and Reference groups, respectively, achieved HbA1c < 7.0% (95% CI, −5.8 to 10.5; p = 0.5984). The proportions increased to 34.5% and 32.2%, respectively, at Week 12 (95% CI, −13.2 to 8.6; p = 0.5767) and to 50.0% in both groups by Week 16 (95% CI, −11.6 to 11.6; p = 0.9304). At Week 20, 62.0% of patients in the Test group and 61.0% in the Reference group achieved HbA1c < 7.0% (95% CI, −12.3 to 10.2; p = 0.8060), increasing further to 74.6% and 74.0%, respectively, by Week 24 (95% CI, −10.8 to 9.4; p = 0.8403).

In the current study, no patients received rescue medication during the study. However, based on the protocol defined criteria for rescue medication, three patients were qualified for rescue medications but did not receive it, since the FBG values were marginally more than the rescue cut‐off values, and as per investigator assessment, continued treatment with semaglutide in these patients was expected to achieve good glycaemic control. Protocol deviations were filed for these observations.

Change in Bodyweight, Body Mass Index, Blood Pressure and Lipid Parameters From Baseline

Both treatments were associated with progressive and significant reductions in bodyweight (Week 12, Test: −3.36 kg and Reference: −3.42 kg; Week 24, Test and Reference groups: −6.17 kg, each) and body mass index (Week 12, Test: −1.29 kg/m2 and Reference: −1.30 kg/m2; Week 24, Test: −2.36 kg/m2 and Reference: −2.34 kg/m2) over 12 and 24 weeks. Weight loss in percentage at Week 24 was 8.61% in Test group and 8.39% in Reference group. The extent of weight loss was similar between groups (p ≥ 0.05), and statistically significant within‐group reductions were observed at all scheduled visits (p < 0.0001) (Figure 4). Modest reductions in systolic and diastolic blood pressure and favourable changes in fasting lipid parameters were also observed in both study groups (Appendices S4 and S5).

Safety

The incidence of TEAEs was comparable between treatment groups (56.7% in each treatment group). Gastrointestinal events (Test: 43.3%, Reference: 46.5%), including diarrhoea, vomiting, nausea and gastritis, were the most commonly reported AEs. Most AEs were mild‐to‐moderate in severity. Local injection‐site reactions, including pain, erythema and swelling, were monitored as part of general AE reporting and were comparable between treatment groups (four events in Test group and seven events in Reference group). Two SAEs, acute ischemic stroke and myocardial infarction, were reported in the Test group. Both events were assessed as unrelated to study treatment (Appendix S6). Adequate management was provided to manage these SAEs, and they were resolved without sequelae during the study period. A total of three hypoglycaemia episodes were reported (two in the Test and one in the Reference group); all episodes were mild in severity. The hypoglycaemia episode in the Reference group required intervention with intravenous dextrose.

Most events were resolved without discontinuation of study treatment. However, three TEAEs, including two SAEs and one event of cataract progression in the Test group, led to discontinuation of the study treatment (Appendix S7). There were no clinically significant changes in laboratory parameters, including the serum calcitonin levels, during the study. No events of acute pancreatitis or retinopathy‐related AEs were reported during this study.

Immunogenicity

No ADA were detected at baseline, and no treatment‐emergent ADA were observed at Week 24 in either treatment group. As no ADA were detected, confirmatory neutralising antibody testing was not required. These findings indicate a low immunogenicity risk and support the comparability of the Test semaglutide injection with the Reference semaglutide injection.

Discussion

In this Phase III, comparative, non‐inferiority study, the Test semaglutide injection demonstrated glycaemic efficacy and safety comparable to Reference semaglutide in adults with T2DM inadequately controlled on metformin. The primary objective of demonstrating non‐inferiority for change in HbA1c at Week 24 was met, and consistent effects were observed across secondary glycaemic and metabolic endpoints, supporting the clinical relevance of the findings.

The HbA1c reduction observed in the present study is consistent with the established efficacy of once‐weekly semaglutide reported across the SUSTAIN clinical development programme [22, 23, 24, 25, 26]. The results align with findings from SUSTAIN FORTE, which demonstrated that semaglutide 2 mg provided additional glycaemic benefit (semaglutide 1 mg vs. 2 mg: −1.9% vs. −2.2%, p < 0.05) in patients who did not achieve treatment goals with standard dosing, whilst maintaining a similar safety profile (semaglutide 1 mg vs. 2 mg: 31% vs. 34%) [27, 28]. Although the current study was not designed to compare dose levels directly, the observed glycaemic improvements fall within the expected therapeutic range for semaglutide and highlights its effectiveness as a treatment intensification option in routine diabetes care.

Weight reduction observed in this study further supports the usefulness of semaglutide in T2DM management. Excess bodyweight is a key contributor to insulin resistance and poor glycaemic control, and therapies that address both hyperglycaemia and weight are particularly valuable. The bodyweight changes reported here are consistent with prior semaglutide trials in patients with T2DM, in which clinically significant weight loss was observed along with improvements in glycaemic control [23, 29, 30]. The combined effect on HbA1c and bodyweight is especially relevant in T2DM management [30].

The safety and tolerability profile of the Test semaglutide injection was comparable to that of Reference semaglutide and consistent with the known class effects of GLP‐1 RAs. Gastrointestinal events were the most frequently reported AEs and were generally mild‐to‐moderate in severity, with similar incidence between treatment groups. No new or unexpected safety concerns were identified, and the incidence of SAEs was low, with none related to the study treatments [14]. Emerging post‐marketing and observational reports have suggested a potential association between GLP‐1 RAs and rare ocular AEs, including non‐arteritic anterior ischemic optic neuropathy (NAION); however, no cases of NAION or other clinically relevant ocular safety events were observed in the present study. Additionally, no events of pancreatitis, retinopathy or gallbladder events were reported in this study. These safety findings are consistent with the overall favourable ocular safety profile reported in randomised controlled trials of semaglutide [31, 32]. Similarly, no events of acute pancreatitis were reported during the study. Immunogenicity assessments, including anti‐drug and neutralising antibodies, did not reveal clinically significant differences between treatments, supporting product comparability.

Although the present study was not designed to evaluate cardiovascular, renal or hepatic outcomes, the observed improvements in glycaemic control and bodyweight are clinically relevant given the central role of hyperglycaemia and adiposity in the development of diabetes‐related complications. Large outcome trials have demonstrated that semaglutide confers benefits beyond glycaemic control in selected high‐risk populations, including reductions in cardiovascular events and kidney disease progression [12, 33, 34, 35, 36]. In addition, emerging evidence suggests potential benefits in metabolic liver disease [37]. Given the demonstrated bioequivalence and clinical comparability of the Test semaglutide to Reference semaglutide, similar benefits may be anticipated; however, confirmation in dedicated long‐term outcomes studies is warranted.

This study demonstrates that the Test semaglutide injection is non‐inferior to Reference semaglutide in improving glycaemic control in adults with T2DM inadequately controlled on metformin and lifestyle modifications, with comparable effects on bodyweight and a similar safety and immunogenicity profile. The findings are consistent with the extensive clinical evidence supporting semaglutide as an effective and well‐tolerated therapy for T2DM and support its use as a treatment option for patients requiring improved glycaemic control.

Limitations

Whilst the study design was appropriate for the primary objective, certain limitations should be acknowledged. An open‐label design was utilised due to the distinct physical characteristics of the Test and Reference semaglutide Injection devices. The open‐label design, whilst partly mitigated by including site‐specific accredited laboratories whose personnel were unaware of the treatment assignment, may have introduced bias in patient‐reported outcomes and safety assessments; however, given the objective nature of the efficacy endpoints, including glycaemic parameters, and the use of identical dosing and escalation criteria across treatment groups, potential bias was minimised. The 24‐week study duration is consistent with regulatory standards for evaluating glycaemic efficacy and non‐inferiority of glucose‐lowering therapies, although long‐term studies may be required to assess maintenance of the therapeutic effect and effects on cardiovascular and renal outcomes. In addition, although long‐standing diabetes was not an exclusion criterion in the study, the mean duration of diabetes was short in this study. Therefore, the results may not be generalisable to patients with longer diabetes duration. The results may also not be generalisable to patients aged over 65 years and those with significant comorbidities such as renal or hepatic impairment.

The study was also not designed to evaluate rare safety events; however, the comprehensive safety and immunogenicity assessments performed were aligned with regulatory expectations and support the robustness of comparative conclusions.

Conclusions

The Test synthetic semaglutide injection demonstrated non‐inferior efficacy and comparable safety to Reference semaglutide in patients with T2DM inadequately controlled on metformin and lifestyle modifications. These findings support its use as an efficacious and well‐tolerated treatment for the management of diabetes. The availability of a synthetic formulation may further enhance treatment accessibility, potentially enabling a broader patient population to achieve improved glycaemic control.
