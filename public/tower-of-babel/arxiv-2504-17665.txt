Evaluating Grounded Reasoning by Code-Assisted LLMs for Mathematics
Zena Al-Khalili, Nick Howell, Dietrich Klakow

Abstract
Assisting LLMs with code generation improved their performance on mathematical reasoning tasks. However, the evaluation of code-assisted LLMs is generally restricted to execution correctness, lacking a rigorous evaluation of their generated programs. In this work, we bridge this gap by conducting an in-depth analysis of code-assisted LLMs generated programs in response to math reasoning tasks. Our evaluation focuses on the extent to which LLMs ground their programs to math rules, and how that affects their end performance. For this purpose, we assess the generations of five different LLMs, on two different math datasets, both manually and automatically. Our results reveal that the distribution of grounding depends on LLMs’ capabilities and the difficulty of math problems. Furthermore, mathematical grounding is more effective for close-source models, while open-source models fail to employ math rules in their solutions correctly. On MATH500, the percentage of grounded programs decreased to half, while the ungrounded generations doubled in comparison to ASDiv grade-school problems. Our work highlights the need for in-depth evaluation beyond execution accuracy metrics, toward a better understanding of code-assisted LLMs capabilities and limits in the math domain.



1
Introduction

Large Language Models (LLMs) have recently achieved outstanding performance on complex reasoning tasks such as mathematical reasoning, powered by scale and multi-step reasoning approaches, particularly Chain-of-Though (CoT)
Wei et al. 2022
, that require an LLM to generate the explicit reasoning steps, before generating the final answer.

Despite its success, investigating CoT reasoning steps revealed critical flows of LLMs, such as committing calculation errors
Gao et al. 2023
and generating false positive chains
Lyu et al. 2023
, i.e: containing reasoning errors yet generating correct final answers.

Code-assisted reasoning approaches
Gao et al. 2023
;
Chen et al. 2022
;
Lyu et al. 2023
;
Gou et al. 2023
;
Mickus et al. 2024
;
Das et al. 2024
proposed to solve these problems by instructing LLMs to generate programmatic reasoning steps, e.g, Python programs, and delegate the execution of these programs to an interpreter, which avoids calculation errors and ensures faithfulness.
Such approaches have been found to further improve LLMs performance on math reasoning tasks.

However, performance improvement is predominantly evaluated by the correctness of the execution outcome
Gao et al. 2023
;
Chen et al. 2022
;
Gou et al. 2023
, rather than the quality of the generated programs and how it affects the reasoning process.

This is problematic, as the generated programs can be relaying on exhaustive searches or memorized information to generate correct answers instead of grounding the reasoning process to domain concepts, i.e: mathematical rules, producing less generalizable programs that are harder to verify and trust.

The goal addressed in this work is to evaluate the actual reasoning capabilities of code-assisted LLMs when solving mathematical tasks by analyzing their generated intermediate programs.

In this evaluation, we focus mainly on assessing the extent to which code-assisted LLMs ground their reasoning process to domain concepts and math rules when generating their solutions, and how grounding, or its absence, affects end performance on mathematical tasks.

Code enables access to domain concepts such as math rules, via libraries (
e.g:
Sympy), which can aid LLMs generate programs that are grounded to world knowledge (
i.e:
mathematical rules), aligning more to human-like reasoning.
Given this we expect grounded programs to be trustworthy, easier to verify, and probably with a higher solve rate, while ungrounded programs to be more error-prone, harder to trust, and degrade end-performance.

We investigate the extent to which this is true by seeking answers to the following research questions.

•

RQ1
: To what extent do code-assisted LLMs ground their reasoning to relevant mathematical concepts?

•

RQ2
: How do code-assisted LLMs implement these concepts?

•

RQ3
: What is the relationship between grounding to math concepts and end performance on mathematical tasks?

•

RQ4
: How much do the answers to these questions vary with more challenging math problems?

To answer these questions, we start by manually analyzing a subset of intermediate programs, generated by GPT4o-mini, GPT4, Qwen2.5, Llama3, and StarCoder2, for grounding and its implementation style, when solving math problems from the ASDiv and MATH500 datasets (
3.3
). Given the observations from the manual analysis, we design a taxonomy reflecting the different grounding levels used by evaluated models (
3.4
). To extend the analysis to the full set of generated programs, we employ two automated evaluation methods: an LLM-Judge using the latest o3-mini model, and our proposed method, Code-Structure Judge, that trains a Decision Tree classifier with features extracted from programs’ Abstract Syntax Tree (
3.5
). We find Code-Structure judge to achieve higher agreement with human judgment than LLM-judge, with
81\%
and
73\%
, respectively (
5.1
); therefore, we employ it for the large-scale evaluation of all generated programs.

To the best of our knowledge this is the first work to analyze generated programs of code-assisted LLMs on math reasoning tasks. We summarize our
takeaways
from Section (
5.2
) as following:

1.

Code-assisted LLMs ground their programs in math knowledge either by invoking concepts from math library or implementing the concepts from scratch.

2.

Code-assisted LLMs also relay on brute-force searches or memorized information to approach some math problems.

3.

Grounded generations to math knowledge are more effective for GPT models and Qwen, while open-source models fail to correctly ground their reasoning to useful concepts.

4.

The grounding distribution completely changed on the challenging MATH500 data, pushing GPT models and Qwen to double their reliance on brute-force search and memorized information, at the expense of more mathematically-grounded solutions.

Our in-depth evaluation highlights the need for more holistic assessment of LLMs generations, beyond execution accuracy metrics, which don’t provide insightful conclusions about models’ actual capabilities and limits in the math domain.

Programs as Intermediate Steps for Math Reasoning

Code-assisted reasoning approaches such as

;

;

;

Beyond in-context learning approaches, other work

However, both in-context and fine-tuned approaches are predominantly evaluated by the correctness of the final answer, overlooking the intermediate programs and how their quality might affect the reasoning process. This work focuses on exploring these programs to accurately evaluate the problem-solving abilities of code-assisted LLMs.

Evaluation of Intermediate Steps

This work also relates to several studies that evaluate the intermediate reasoning steps of LLMs in natural language
(

;

;

;

These works either use a human-written reference chain to compare against the evaluated chain or employ a capable LLM to localize errors and judge the quality of the reasoning chains.
Code intermediate steps are unexplored, therefore we aim to analyze the quality of these and how they affect LLMs end performance.

Evaluation of code generated by LLMs.

To assess code generated by LLMs, previous work focuses on
test-based evaluation and functional correctness, such as
(

;

)
, others
(

;

;

(

)

We draw inspiration from their method to evaluate generated programs, on math reasoning tasks, using an LLM-Judge.
Finally,
(

)
analyzed characteristics of LLMs generated programs, on code generation tasks, in terms of code complexity, number of API calls, and types of errors,

3
Evaluation of Intermediate Programs

The main focus of this evaluation is to assess the generated programs of code-assisted LLMs beyond end-to-end evaluation and single-score metrics.
We take a step further towards understanding the logic LLMs employ to solve math problems. Therefore, we evaluate their use of math concepts in generated programs.
A math concept refers to the principle used to solve a specific mathematical problem,
e.g:
using the Euclidean Algorithm to find the Greatest Common Divisor of two numbers. Evaluating generated programs for their use of math concepts is analogous to being right for the right reason, as using the math concept in the solution ensures reasoning that is more aligned to human reasoning, and one that can be easily verified and trusted.

3.3
Manual Analysis

Motivated by the lack of fine-grained assessment of LLMs generated code for math reasoning tasks, we conduct a detailed human-manual analysis of the generated programs to answer our first two research questions on whether LLMs ground their generated programs to math concepts and how this grounding is implemented in the generated output.
This analysis uses only a subset of the evaluation dataset. sized
300
programs randomly sampled from the entire set,
and consists of two main steps:

Sectioning the programs

We section each program into three blocks:
(1) Transcription: This part of the program transcribes the information from the question into variables.
(2) Processing: In this section, variables are manipulated via operations and function calls to arrive at the answer.
(3) Results collection: Return the final answer of the processing section.
Some sections can be fused into one line of code that represents for example processing and returning results at the same step.

Analyzing Processing Sections

We inductively analyze processing code-lines with consideration of the following aspects: logic, implementation style, coherence, verification effort, and trustworthiness.

We check if the code-lines are organized into a coherent sequence of steps that represent a valid implementation of some math concept, that a human can verify against existing possible implementations of the concept.

Additionally, we check whether all steps of a solution are explicitly generated rather than inferred by the model. Inferred steps might involve calculations models can’t compute correctly, due to their proven shortcomings in arithmetic calculations
Cobbe et al. 2021
;
Lewkowycz et al. 2022
;
Gao et al. 2023
,
which makes verifying and trusting these solutions harder.

Most importantly, we check how math concepts are implemented in the generated programs. The grounded implementation can happen in several styles, either in terms of primitive operations only, for very simple math problems, and from-scratch implementations, or by calling functions from related math libraries that represent a more abstracted form of the solution. We also observe some implementations relying on brute-force loops that search the space of all possible answers one by one, or lack the processing section altogether.

We organize all these observed trends into six categories that make up our proposed taxonomy of grounding to math concepts and its style.

3.4
Proposed Taxonomy

Given the observations from the manual analysis, we propose to categorize LLMs’ generated programs into six mutually exclusive classes, three of which represent programs grounded to math concepts, in different implementation styles, while the other three represent ungrounded programs that mainly rely on memorized information
or exhaustive searches to find the final answer.

1.

Conceptual
through library calls. Reference a math concept through calls to relevant math libraries, standard, or external.

2.

Primitive
programs: solutions are expressed in terms of the primitive operations only due to problem simplicity, where no library functionality can be called or implemented.

3.

From-scratch Implementation
of a library functionality. Instead of a call to a library function, the model implements the same functionality from scratch. The model either inlines this implementation in the generated code or write it as a custom function to be called when required.

4.

Brute-Force
: The program searches through all possible values to find the answer without guiding the search with any math knowledge.

5.

Disorganized
: The program consists of incoherent steps that seem to be a mix of the previous classes. Usually include variables defined but not used, or the opposite.

6.

No Logic
: These programs skip the processing section altogether, merely returning a result without explicitly generating the steps to arrive at it. (Generating the logic as comments in natural language is also considered No Logic.)

Extending the human manual analysis to the entire evaluation dataset consumes a lot of time and effort. Therefore, we look into automating the evaluation by training classifiers that label generated programs using one class from our proposed taxonomy. For this purpose, we first annotate a training set using categories from the taxonomy, then we train a decision tree classifier using features extracted from the programs. We compare the trained classifier to an LLM-Judge and evaluate both judges on a held out annotated set. We pick the more accurate judge for the large-scale evaluation.

3.5.1
Training Set Annotation

To train the automated evaluation mehtods, two authors annotate a randomly sampled subset of the generated programs using labels from the taxonomy. The annotated set is
300
programs from ASDiv split.

210
examples were used for training, while the rest are held out for measuring the performance of the automated judges.

The annotation guidelines were developed based on observations from the manual analysis.
After experimentation with different annotations schemes, we found that human
assignment of per-program labels produced low inter-annotator agreement (IAA),
while per-line labeling produced very high agreement.
We thus selected a per-line annotation scheme, along with a simple algorithm
for assigning a program level label based on the per-line labels.
More on the annotation guidelines and annotators background can be found in Appendix
B
.

Double independent annotation was performed on 50 of the 300 samples, with

93\%
line-granularity and
100\%
sample-granularity agreement.
Disagreements were then adjudicated, and guidelines were updated to resolve the
ambiguities.

3.5.2
Automated Evaluation Methods

Code-Structure Judge: Decision Tree Model

We note that each class of the taxonomy tends to rely on specific characteristics of the code structure. Hence, we propose using features from the code structure to train a decision tree classifier that will be used to evaluate generated programs.
The features to train the classifier are extracted from the Abstract Syntax Tree (AST) of each program and are the following:

•

Number of function calls, including calls to functions that model writes itself.

•

Number of import statements.

•

Number of built-in operations. e.g: +, < , …

•

Number of control flow statements: e.g, If, for, break, ..

•

Number of variables defined but not used.
Number of variables used but not defined.

The max depth used for the decision tree model is
5
.
We experimented with other models, such as SVM and Random Forest, but found no further gains. A neural classifier, on the other hand, would require much more training data and, consequently, more annotation and human effort.

LLM-Judge

Following other related work that utilize LLMs as judge
Tong and Zhang 2024
, we employ an LLM to assess a given program and assign a taxonomy class to that program. For this purpose, we provide a detailed description of the taxonomy classes in the prompt, and ask the LLM-judge to carefully analyze the input program, finally, we ask it to provide a single class number that fit the program most. The prompt includes no reference programs of taxonomy classes.
The full prompt is provided in Appendix
A.4
.

We experimented with two models from OpenAI most advanced models, namely GPT4o and O3-mini, and found that O3-mini achieved slightly better results as a judge.
We specify the reasoning effort of the model to be high and allow for
20,000
max output tokens, including reasoning tokens.

Why do LLMs perform differently within a class?

GPT4o-mini has very high accuracy on the Disorganized class, unlike all other models. To further understand this counter-intuitive phenomenon, we analyze the
16
generations in this class by GPT4o-mini and find that out of all generations,
14
are false positives, with
9
Primitives and
5
No Logic.
We also investigate the reason why Llama3.1 and StarCoder2 generally have degraded performance on the grounded classes.
On the Conceptual class, we qualitatively analyze 20 programs by the two models and find that 18/20 programs invoke the wrong concept, indicating a poor ability to invoke relevant math concepts. The other 2/20 programs incorrectly transcribe information from the question. Furthermore, on the Primitive class, we conduct error analysis on 20 programs generated by the two models and find that 9/20 were false positives of Disorganized and No Logic, and
8/20 have inferred step error, where the model infer the calculation of one step rather than generating the steps to calculate it, causing the error to propagate through the rest of the code. Additionally, 2/20 incorrectly transcribed information from the question and 1/20 had wrong ground truth answer.
Finally, on the class From-scratch Implementation, Llama3.1 has the lowest accuracy in comparison to other models. Examining 10 random generations from this class, we find that the model implements a different solution than what the problem requires. For example implementing the code for the Greatest Common Divisor problem, when the question is asking for the Least Common Multiple or vice versa. Starcoder2, on the other hand, successfully implements many solutions.

6
Conclusion

In this work, we conducted an in-depth analysis of code-assisted LLMs generated programs in response to math problems. Our assessment focuses on the extent to which LLMs employ math concepts in the generated programs and how this affects their end performance.
We categorized the generated programs into six different categories, which make our proposed taxonomy of grounding to math concepts.
We annotated a corpus of programs using classes from the taxonomy and trained a decision-tree model that we call Code-Structure Judge. The Code-Structure judge outperformed an LLM-judge baseline and was employed for a large-scale evaluation of more generated programs. Our findings show that models employ grounded reasoning, as well as ungrounded reasoning, comprising the use of brute-force search and memorized information. The use of ungrounded hacks depends highly on LLMs capabilities and problems difficulty.
Our work emphasizes the need for holistic evaluation of code-assisted LLMs, and we hope our findings will inspire future work to further study why LLMs employ ungrounded solutions and how to mitigate this phenomenon.

Limitations

We note there’s limitations to our work.
First: Code-structure Judge is still not accurate on the Disorganized class, causing many false positives.
Second, we depend on one prompting technique and don’t compare performance when utilizing prompts to improve generated programs with further refinement, such as Self-Refine
(
Madaan et al. 2024
)
or Code-based Self-Verification
(
Zhou et al. 2023a
)
.

LLM-Judge Prompt

The prompt for the LLM-Judge task with o3-mini

User:

You are an expert code judge that analyze code based on the
following classes:
1- Conceptual through library calls. Reference a math concept
through calls to relevant math libraries, standard or external.
2- primitive solution: programs are expressed in terms of the
primitive operations only due to problem simplicity, where no
library functionality can be called or implemented.
3- From-scratch Implementation of a library functionality.
Implements a library functionality from scratch. implementation
is inlined in the generated code, or can be a custom function
to be called when required.
4- Brute-Force. The program search through all possible values
to find the answer without guiding the search with some math
knowledge.
5- Disorganized: the program consists of incoherent steps that
seem to be a mix of the previous classes. Usually include
variables used but not defined or the opposite.
6- No Logic: These programs merely return a result without
explicitly generating the steps to arrive at it, transcribing
information from the question only without further processing
the information is also No logic. generating the logic as
comments doesn’t count either.

Instructions:
- given an input program your task is to analyze it and then
provide a class number from the list above.
- don’t fix the code.
- Put your final answer in \boxed{}.

Appendix B
Training Set Annotation Guidelines and Annotators Background

B.1
Annotation format

:

Each line of program code is prefixed with five characters of the form

TPIR
plus space.

Columns:

T
.

transcription

P1-5
.

processing (blank implies "no logic")

I
.

inference-time computation

R
.

result collection

B.2
Transcription

Purely transcriptive statements that transcribe either data (e.g. numbers)
or relations between data (e.g. equations) from the question.

Purely transcriptive statements can still contain operations that are
explicitly described in the problem statement; these operations do not count as
processing.

""" One number is twice as
large as another. The smaller
number is 3. Find the other
number """
def solution():
T x = 3
T R y = 2*x
R return y

Even if the model uses the question to compute at inference time, without
pure transcription of data or relations no
T
is marked:

"""
One number is twice as large
as another. The sum of the
numbers is 12. Find the gcd of
the two numbers
"""
def solution():
I x = 4
I y = 8
1 R z = math.gcd(x, y)
R return z

If the model has performed partial computations (collapsing e.g. a pure
transcription and true processing) then it receives no
T
; it can
possibly receive other labels.

"""
Two boards have total length
10; the long board is 2 longer
than the short board. Find the
lengths of the two boards
"""
def solution():
T total_length = 10
T difference = 2
2IR short_length = (total_length -
difference) / 2
T R long_length = short_length +
difference
R return short_length, long_length

B.3
Processing types

Comments are never marked as anything!!

1.

conceptual lib

–

calls a library function later

–

mark the entire function; but not the comments

2.

conceptual primitive

–

uses primitives in a way that is correct, and cannot be simplified by lib

3.

from scratch implementation

–

plausibly correct, looks like inlined implementation of a lib function

–

writes a function for itself, then calls (mark the call as implementation as well.)

4.

brute-force

–

search through all space

–

mark the entire loop

5.

disorganised

–

probably incorrect (more or less random?, disorganised)

Empty ’processing type’ field (i.e., a space " ") indicates that no processing
occurs.

B.4
Inference-time computation

If the model skips steps (either entirely, or in part (in which case there will
still be a processing label)) Then the line gets the "inference-time
computation" flag

In the case of the model entirely precomputing, the processing type will
probably be empty and the
I
flag will appear after.

def solution():
I x = 6 # lcm of 3 and 2
return x

If the model has performed symbolic manipulation to avoid the use of symbolic
equation libraries, it receives
I
as processing/transcription labels.

B.5
Result collection

Lines that return the final answer, or that store the variable holding the
final answer, are marked
R
in the final column.

def solution():
IR x = 6 # lcm of 3 and 2
R return x

""" One number is twice as
large as another. The sum of
the numbers is 12. Find the
gcd of the two numbers"""
def solution():
I x = 4
I y = 8
1 R z = math.gcd(x, y)
R return z

B.6
Annotators Background

The annotation process of the generated programs is time-consuming and isn’t feasible to do at a larger scale because it requires expertise with code understanding. Both annotators are experts in computer science and have done prior reading on programming languages and related topics, that make them a better fit for the annotation process.

Furthermore, programmatic steps and code structure patterns are less subjective than natural language, carrying less nuance, which can lead to disagreement or bias. For example, it is hard to mistake a library call from a brute-force program.
