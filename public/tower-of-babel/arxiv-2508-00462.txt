Abstract
. Context: Pair Programming as a work mode is used (occasionally or frequently) throughout professional software development. Objective: Understand what power-related phenomena occur in pair programming as it is used in industry; give advice to practitioners on how to do better pair programming. Method: Analyze 22 industrial pair programming sessions using Grounded Theory Methodology. Formulate a Grounded Theory on power-related behaviors. Run a survey with 292 participants about that theory. Use it to demonstrate that the phenomena are common. Results: Our theory describes the phenomenon of Power Gap : a perceived difference in participation opportunities. The theory shows the behaviors that create a Power Gap or result from it. Power Gaps tend to damage knowledge transfer, code quality, and process efficiency. The survey results show that all concepts from our theory are frequent in practice. They also provide more grounding for concepts that are observable only indirectly. Conclusions: It is a valuable component of pair programming skill to be able to avoid Power Gaps . Specifically, pair partners need to avoid Hierarchical Behavior (which tends to create or increase a Power Gap ) and should perform enough Equalizing Behavior (which prevents or reduces a Power Gap ).

Introduction

“In pair programming, two programmers jointly produce one artifact (design, algorithm, code)”
(
Williams et al. 2000
)
.
We are concerned here with pair programming (PP) as a work mode,
whether PP is also used regularly (as a practice in the sense of
eXtreme Programming
(

)
) or not
(
Zieris 2020
, Section 2.3)
.

Previous quantitative work on PP
(
Hannay et al. 2009
;
Arisholm et al. 2007
)
failed to
produce a thorough understanding of its effects,
so we build upon previous qualitative work
(

Zieris and Prechelt 2014

;
Zieris 2020
;

Those previous works, based on videos of industrial PP sessions,
have produced an understanding of how knowledge transfer works

In particular, they have identified elements of a PP-specific skill
(or PP skill for short) around knowledge transfer, Togetherness,
and Expediency
(
Zieris and Prechelt 2014
;
Zieris and Prechelt 2021
)
.
Such information is useful for practitioners for improving how they work.
We thus aim at finding further elements of PP skill.

Research Interest
:
We will investigate decision processes, the second pervasive phenomenon
in PP besides knowledge transfer.
In particular, we had previously noticed that in some sessions one partner
appeared to be very dominant.
We thus decided to make dominance and subordination the focus of our attention.

:
We describe the

Related Work

Zieris analyzed many aspects of knowledge transfer in PP, also via Grounded Theory Methodology
(
Zieris 2020
;
Zieris and Prechelt 2014
;
Zieris and Prechelt 2016
;
Zieris and Prechelt 2021
)
.
We use many of his concepts, in particular the
Togetherness

“[Togetherness] is the degree to which the pair members are able to fully understand each other’s activities, including all intentions and meanings associated by the respective speaker or actor”
(
Zieris 2020

Zieris & Prechelt proposed the concept of PP skill and argue
“that one reason for the high results variance [of controlled experiments on PP] lies in ignoring pair programming skill (as opposed to technical software development skill) as a factor”
(
Zieris and Prechelt 2014
)
.
In their publication they focus on knowledge transfer skill as a subset of PP skill.
They later follow up with “two elements of that skill that are independent from software development skills: Maintaining Togetherness and keeping an eye on Expediency”
(
Zieris and Prechelt 2021
)
.

Ververs et al.
(
Ververs et al. 2025
)
defined the Student and Teacher roles in PP by building upon Salinger et al.’s framework for role definition
(
Salinger et al. 2013
)
.
They analyzed 17 industrial PP sessions from the PP-ind repository
(
Zieris and Prechelt 2020
)
and 6 interviews with practitioners using Grounded Theory Methodology and formulated the “Grounded Theory on the Vicious Cycle of Defensive Behavior”, which is centered around the
Power Gap
.
They explained the negative impact of
Defensive Behavior
on
Togetherness
and we will discuss this as well in Section
4.5
.
We heavily build upon their work and use sessions from the same repository of PP recordings.
We reuse, expand, or modify several of their concepts.
In particular, we modify their definitions of
Power Gap
and
Hierarchical Behavior
and split their concept of
Defensive Behavior
in two. We discuss the later in Section
4.5
.

Plonka et al. studied disengagement in PP by analyzing 21 industrial PP sessions
(
Plonka et al. 2012
)
.
They identified social pressure as one of five reasons for disengagement where novice developers who had “less knowledge to solve the task at hand […] were disengaged during the PP sessions; they avoided driving and
they stopped asking and replying to questions.”
Through interviews with the developers they found that “less experienced developers can be worried about slowing their partners down or that they may look stupid.”
Besides acceptable disengagement for example where the pair was “solving a simple task that does not require input from both developers”, Plonka et al. also identified developers dropping out of the session as a result of social pressure and time pressure.
They found it harmful, as the developers were “not able to follow their partner’s explanations or activities” which “undermines knowledge transfer”.
We will later discuss this as
Disengaging Behavior
in Section
4.4
.

Begel & Nagappan surveyed 487 developers on PP at Microsoft
(
Begel and Nagappan 2008
)
.
They found that good PP teams should have “compatible personalities”, and “no ego”. We will discuss both these factors later in Section
4.7
.

Hannay et al. conducted a meta-analysis of controlled experiments on the effects of PP vs solo programming
(
Hannay et al. 2009
)
.
We argue that such experiments are inherently flawed as they can’t take the knowledge transfer into account, neither as an input variable (a process property: how much have partners exchanged knowledge to solve their current task) nor as an output variable (a process result: how much have partners learned as a by-product of solving the task).
Nevertheless we will discuss their results briefly in Section
4.2
to argue why
Process Agency
impacts the
Quality
.

Method

3.1.
Grounded Theory Methodology

We use Grounded Theory Methodology based on Strauss & Corbin
(
Strauss and Corbin 1990
)
.
Our analysis is based on 22 PP sessions that are part of the PP-ind dataset
(
Zieris and Prechelt 2020
)
.
They consist of a screen recording and an audio and video recording of the developers.
We analyzed sessions
AA1, CA1–5, DA1–6, JA1–6, and PA1–4.
The majority of our grounded theory we present here is based on episodes taken from sessions AA1, CA1, CA2, DA2, DA4, and PA3, as these were the sessions where our core concept, the
Power Gap
was most pronounced.
The first letter of these session names designates a company.
The pair members within all our recordings from that company share that letter and
are numbered consecutively.
E.g. session DA4 has members D5 and D6.

3.2.
Adding a Survey for Better Grounding

The central concept uncovered in our analysis is the
Power Gap
,
defined as a perceived difference in the hierarchical status between the partners.
(see Section
4.3
).
This concept is subjective and can not be observed directly.
Rather, it has to be deduced indirectly based on the developers’ behaviors.
Grounding such a concept based on only observational data is questionable.
We therefore needed a different source of information
(addressing emotions, thoughts, and feelings of the developers)
for proper grounding of our core concept.
Two possibilities come to mind: interviews (as used by
(
Ververs et al. 2025
)
) or a survey.
We decided on a survey because we wanted to quantify the occurrence of various concepts
from our theory in industrial practice.

3.3.
The Survey

Our survey consists of 5 parts.
Part 1: professional experience of the developers.
Part 2: amount of their PP experience.
Part 3: 30 statements on emotions and behaviors that can happen during PP.
Participants rate (on a 7-point scale from ’never’ to ’always’)
how often they experienced these in the last six months.
Part 4 had 21 conditional statements (“If X happens, I feel Y”) on the same scale.
These were to be answered only if the respondent had actually experienced situation X in the past six months, not hypothetically.
These help evaluate the concept relationships in our theory.
Part 5 was demographic data.

We recruited by contacting our personal network and posting the survey on social media (LinkedIn and Reddit).
Participation was anonymous, so we do not know which method generated how many participants.
About 80% of our participants work in Germany.

292 people started and 105 submitted the survey.
We excluded several categories of participants:
Those who didn’t answer our questions on professional experience in software development and PP;
those without professional experience in the last 6 months;
those with no PP experience in the last 6 months.
We also excluded 7 suspicious participants who always checked the rightmost or leftmost box, etc.

We ended up with two data sets.
Set 1 contains 92 answers from participants who answered all of our questions.
Set 2 also contains
119 answers from participants who answered at least a significant portion
(they answered at least the third part of our survey) of the survey,
but for whom some demographic information is missing.
In the analysis, we prefer Set 2, but use Set 1 where Set 2 is not sufficient.

4.1.
The Power Gap

A
Power Gap
is the perception by one partner of the pair
that the opportunities for participating in the pair process are not distributed equally.
We call the partner with fewer perceived opportunities
subordinate
,
the other
dominant
.
The
Power Gap
is subjective, not directly visible for observers such as us, which means
we cannot ground this concept directly.
Rather, we look out for its effects, namely how a
Power Gap
is suggested by
different
Process Agency
of the partners (Section
4.2
),
to
Defensive Behavior
(Section
4.5
),
or
Disengaging Behavior
(Section
4.6
).

Power Gaps
are not static; they may change over time.
Sometimes, both partners can perceive themselves to be at the lower end of a

Power Gap
.

Power Gaps
are common; see Figure
1
.
49% felt of our survey respondents felt they were at least sometimes the ones being in full control,
i.e., on the upper side of a
Power Gap
.
29% felt at least sometimes on the lower side.

4.2.
Process Agency

Process Agency
is an observable concept.
It describes how much influence a developer exerts on decisions.

We classify each decision individually and characterize it by looking at the pair’s decision-making process.
This process can be
Shared
if both developers influence the decision in a meaningful way
or
One-sided
otherwise, which is the relevant case for us.

A developer can influence a decision by proposing, disagreeing, challenging, and amending (a proposal), or by adding knowledge in the form of hypotheses and findings.
We conceptualize such elementary behaviors using Salinger & Prechelt’s Base Layer for Pair Programming
(
Salinger and Prechelt 2013
)
.

One-sided Decisions
occur in two different forms,
depending on whether the partner controlling the decision-making is PP driver
(controlling computer input)
or observer (not controlling computer input).
If the driver controls decision-making, we call the behavior
Solo-Driving
:

We defined a
Shared Decision
by both developers contributing in a meaningful way.
But what does “meaningful” mean?
We take all utterances and actions of one partner during a decision-making episode together and ask:
“Would the other developer be able to reach a similar conclusion in a similar time
without these utterances?”
If the answer is no, we call it a
Shared Decision
.
If the answer is yes, but both partners still had some influence,
we cannot decide between a
Shared
vs a
One-sided Decision
.
As most decisions in PP are small, often only a proposal followed by an agreement,

One-sided Decisions
are normal in PP and usually unproblematic.

Only if multiple decisions in short succession are
One-sided Decisions
and are led by the same developer, we characterize the whole stretch as
Unbalanced Process Agency
.

Unbalanced Process Agency
can last for only a minute or for a large part of a session.
We use
Unbalanced Process Agency
as a strong indicator for a likely
Power Gap
.
As
One-sided Decisions
are so common, the converse,
Balanced Process Agency
, is
hardly a useful concept.

4.3.
Hierarchical Behavior

Hierarchical Behavior
is any utterance or action where the active part is
perceived by the partner to have a higher position (Reference to GT-Arrow
4.8
).
We annotate this based on two dimensions:
First, the action itself, based on its role in the pair’s dialogue, by answering the question
“What is the intention of saying or doing this?”
Second, the partner’s reaction by answering the question
“Do they react in a way that suggests the previous utterance implies a hierarchy?”
The latter can be expressed by falling into line or by challenging the
implied pair relationship.
Only if both dimensions match do we annotate it as
Hierarchical Behavior
.
Example
4.3
shows falling-into-line behavior:

Example 4: CA1 - Belittling and Shutting Partner Up

This exchange happens early in the session.
The task is adding new functionality, including a corresponding GUI element.
C1 has started working on the task prior to the session.
He added a rudimentary element to the GUI that does not work properly.
Before the present session, he commented out this GUI element.

C1:

“You can comment that in and then we’ll see how it looks in the demo.
Then I can show you the effects that occur (!!…!!)”

C2:

“Yes, I can imagine that it won’t work. I can directly imagine it.”

<hierarchical_behavior>

C1:

“<*No reaction.*>”

<falling_into_line>

C2:

“<*C2 uncomments the line as suggested.
The IDE highlights the variable ‘ScaleRangePanel’ in red.*>
ScaleRangePanel (!…!)”

C1:

“Why can’t it (!!…!!)”

C2:

“It must be called LabelScaleRangePanel.
<*Renames the variable. IDE highlighting disappears.*> ”

C1:

“Ah, I have renamed it. Yes, yes.”

C2:

“Please wait a minute.
I want to check whether there’s a comment in there or what it can do.”

<hierarchical_behavior>

C1:

“<*No reaction.*>”

<falling_into_line>

C2’s first
Hierarchical Behavior
utterance does not serve a productive purpose in the pair’s dialogue.
Rather it suggests a feeling of superiority.
The repetition emphasizes this point.
This is why we classify it as
Hierarchical Behavior
.

C2’s second
Hierarchical Behavior
explicitly asks C1 to assume
a subordinate rule by not contributing actively.
In both cases, C1 does not react, which we interpret as falling into line and
agreeing to the higher hierarchical position C2 has taken.

One participant of our survey explained how such redundant or unwanted knowledge transfer activities can be seen as

4.4.
Equalizing Behavior

Equalizing Behavior
is any utterance (or action) that likely results
in the partner perceiving their hierarchical position as less different from
the speaker’s than before (type A) or
that confirms in the presence of tensions that no difference really exists (type B) (Reference to GT-Arrow
4.8
).
The operationalization of this concept works along the same lines as for

Hierarchical Behavior
.

See again Example
4.3
where A2 employs
Meta-Communication
to counter A1’s
Hierarchical Behavior
.
This is type A
Equalizing Behavior
.
He immediately proceeds with reducing tension which is type B.

Example
4.4
shows type B for a pair which is
equalizing all the time.
They have strong disagreements on issues that come up frequently
and are skilled in defusing the resulting tensions with ease:

Example 6: CA5 - Agree to Disagree

C3, who is currently editing the code, makes a proposal on using ensures.

C3:

“You know what I’m going to write now.
<*Writes ‘ensure’.*>”

C4:

“No no, please please no.
<*Smiles.*>
Let’s write tests.
<*Looks to C3.*>
Then you don’t want those Ensures there.
<*Laughs.*>”

<reduce_tension>

C3:

“Will they hurt you if I write them down now?”

<reduce_tension>

<ask_approval>

C4:

“<*Laughing.*>
Yeah, totally.
But it’s fine if it doesn’t hurt you when I remove them when I’m testing.”

<reduce_tension>

C3:

“When you’re testing, if they get in the way of your testing.
But as long as they’re not in the way?”

C4:

“<*Smiles.*>
They are in the way.”

C3:

“<*Uses Ensures four times.
And looks to C4, smiling.*>”

<reduce_tension>

C4:

“We’ll never agree on that.
<*Smiles.*>
Never!”

<reduce_tension>

[20 minutes later; C4 is driving now. They revisit the topic.]

C3:

“Hey?”

C4:

“Mhm?”

C3:

“Will you make me an Ensure?”

C4:

“Oh my God, you are a sadist!
<*Writes Ensure.*>
Shall we talk it over [over a beer] sometime soon?”

[…]
<reduce_tension>

C3:

“If you like, sure we can do that.”

Although C3 and C4 disagree fundamentally on the use of “ensure”,
they communicate in a way that deescalates any tension that might result
by laughing about their disagreement and by explicitly asking for approval,
no matter who of them is currently driver.
The partner also reacts in a deescalating way, which is evidence that they registered and acknowledged the deescalating intention of their partner.
We hence classify all utterances that reduce tension as
Equalizing Behavior
.

In our survey, a majority of respondents confirm that they
always want to work with no
Power Gap

and always strive for
Equalizing Behavior

(Figure
6
).

One participant put it this way:

“[…] I think there is a lot of value in unequal pairings
with respect to expertise, but not in terms of respect or dignity.
I am now in a place where I am generally brought in as an expert,
and often find myself teaching others, which is technically ‘not equal’,
but I always try to approach problems and other people with
appropriate humility and respect […].”
(Q4:P26)

4.5.
Defensive Behavior

Defensive Behavior
is a subordinate partner’s reaction to a big

Power Gap
in PP: passive-aggressive actions such as
avoiding confrontation and not addressing the process problems
(the big
Power Gap
) directly,
instead shutting the partner out by minimizing the communication (Reference to GT-Arrow
4.8
).

Defensive Behavior
blocks
Equalizing Behavior
(Reference to GT-Arrow
4.8
).

Defensive Behavior
is hard to decide in observational data:
It requires a large
Power Gap
, which itself is not observable.

Not every developer will react with
Defensive Behavior
when
they experience
Unbalanced Process Agency
and
Hierarchical Behavior
.
The reasons will be discussed in Section
4.7
.

Defensive Behavior
is discussed in detail by Ververs et al.
(
Ververs et al. 2025
)
.
Our definition is only slightly different from theirs,
so that most examples they list can serve to illustrate our concept as well.

As passive-aggressiveness is mostly viewed negatively and our survey participants may not answer honestly if they (or to an extent their partner) employed this behavior, we avoided asking direct questions on
Defensive Behavior
in our survey.
Instead, we asked questions that focus on times when developers avoided the meta-discussion, as this is one central aspect of
Defensive Behavior
.

In our survey, the majority of those respondents who encountered
Power Gaps

did not usually react with meta-communication, which means
the tend towards
Defensive Behavior
; see Figure
7
.

Figure 7
.

Does a
Power Gap
lead to
Meta-Communication
? (
N=92
)

Indeed, about a third confirmed they would rather withdraw from the
PP session, a rather defensive form of behavior; see Figure
8
.

Figure 8
.

Does a

lead to

? (

)

Together, these answers serve as clear evidence that
Defensive Behavior

is a common reaction to a big
Power Gap
.

Defensive Behavior
, by reducing communication, will usually
damage the
Togetherness
of a pair
(
Ververs et al. 2025
)
(Reference to GT-Arrow
4.8
).
Lower
Togetherness
increases the risk of Breakdowns
(
Zieris 2020
)
,
which hurt the efficiency of the process (Reference to GT-Arrow
4.8
).
Worse, by blocking
Equalizing Behavior
,

Defensive Behavior
makes it hard to reduce a
Power Gap
.

4.6.
Disengaging Behavior

In
Disengaging Behavior
, a partner gradually withdraws from the session
by not asking questions, not proposing anything, and not challenging their
partner’s proposals
as a result of a
Power Gap
(Reference to GT-Arrow
4.8
).
Disengaging Behavior
produces even fewer symptoms than

Defensive Behavior
and is therefore even harder to diagnose.

Our survey participants put it as follows:

“I think that most of the negative experiences
I have had with pair-programming are [among others when]
the other person is too passive, i.e., doesn’t talk or ask any questions,
as then it becomes very difficult just to keep talking ’into the void’ and to maintain the energy needed […].”
(Q5:P278)

“Usually, one person writes the code while the other
just observes or explains logic.
The non-coding partner often remains idle and disengaged.
In many cases, the observer doesn’t even pay attention to the screen.”
(Q6:P69)

Disengaging Behavior
leads to
Unbalanced Process Agency

more directly than the behaviors discussed before:
the developer does not engage in the decision making process as much
as they could (Reference to GT-Arrow
4.8
).
Like
Defensive Behavior
, this can be a reaction to a
Power Gap
.

Because it is so hard to observe, there are only small bits of our data that we
can confidently call
Disengaging Behavior
.
The above example
4.2
is one of those few cases.

The survey, however, provides good access to this phenomenon.
Revisit Figure
2(b)
:
Respondents tend to find themselves attentive and active most of the time
(left two plots), but about 30% of them perceive their partner to be
too passive some of the time (rightmost plot).
About one quarter of respondents self-reported
Disengaging Behavior

in the context of a
Power Gap
(Figure
9
).
In sum,
Disengaging Behavior
is also present in many PP sessions.

Figure 9
.

? (

Besides engaging less in the decision-making process,
Disengaging Behavior
also includes not asking questions.
This damages the
Knowledge Transfer
, which is one important advantage of PP
(
Zieris 2020
)
(Reference to GT-Arrow
4.8
).
By damaging the
Knowledge Transfer
, the developer hinders closing any present
Knowledge Gap
(Reference to GT-Arrow
4.8
).

4.7.
Moderating Factors: Bond and Fragile Ego

Not every time we see actions that would classify as
Hierarchical Behavior

(or
Equalizing Behavior
) do we see a direct reaction from their partner
in the form of more (or less)
Defensive
/
Disengaging Behavior
.
This is likely due to two reasons:
First, not every action influences the
Power Gap
in such a strong way that it immediately causes a reaction.
Second, there are other factors at play that moderate the
Power Gap
.
The latter are the topic of the present subsection.

We deem it likely that two factors play an important role: the established
Bond
between the developers and the
Ego
of the individual developer.
Our observational data is lacking in that sense because we don’t have any information on the personal relationships between the partners, their experience working together, or what the developers thought during their sessions, e.g., how their
Ego
played a role.

4.7.1.
Fragile Ego

Nevertheless, we deem a
Fragile Ego
the most likely reason why D3 in session DA2 became disengaged, as he was originally supposed to be the one who instructs D4 (who was in his first week with company D), but their dynamic quickly changed as it turned out that D4 had much more experience and knowledge with Java as a programming language and software engineering in general.
D3 acknowledges this directly in Example
4.2
.
Note that, for lack of enough direct observations,
we leave this concept vaguer than the others and provide no explicit definition.

Some of our participants mentioned the importance of
Fragile Ego
unprompted:

“I generally enjoy pair and mob programming, but I work on a great team with very good and smart people. No one seems to have too big of an ego, and even my division chiefs laugh at their own mistakes. So, I think a big part of it is having and fostering a safe community that encourages everyone to be always learning and not expected to know everything or be perfect.”
(Q7:P26)

Or mentioned it indirectly:
“[…] I have problems performing while being watched.”
(Q8:P131)

Based on our observations and the suspicion we had, we investigated whether
Fragile Ego
plays a role in PP through the survey.
See Figure
10
.

The responses differ drastically between our two questions.
This is because not knowing something (left plot) and admitting it
is a routine event in any good PP session, so only a modest fraction
of respondents has a problem with that,
whereas being seen as not competent (right plot) would be catastrophic and
hard to bear for even strong egos and so about 90% of respondents have an
issue with this idea.
Still, the “modest fraction” in the left plot is about one quarter,
so
Fragile Egos
appear to be common.

This is in line with our observational data, as most developers ask questions frequently or directly state their knowledge gap, and most partners respond by directly providing the missing knowledge and not commenting in any other way on the

.

Summing up,
Fragile Ego
appears to be a relevant factor for
the
Power Gap
dynamic of PP sessions (Reference to GT-Arrow

4.7.2.
Bond

The
Bond
between the partners is the second moderating factor we deemed important.
With
Bond
, we mean the relationship between the developers and
their experience in working together and much like for
Fragile Ego
,
we do not provide a more concrete definition
for lack of direct observational access to the concept.

In the survey (Figure

respondents confirm that a strong
Bond
makes

4.8.
A Grounded Theory of Power Gaps in PP

By taking together all concept definitions, the relationships between them, and
the quantitative evidence provided by the survey, the

Grounded Theory of Power Gaps in Pair Programming
unfolds.
We describe it verbally here;
see Figure
12
for a graphical representation.

The context is a professional PP session with two developers.
The session is aimed at solving a task (in contrast to a session dedicated to knowledge transfer only, for example for onboarding).
The developers have some preexisting
Bond

and one of them has more task-relevant knowledge than the other, i.e.,
there is a
Knowledge Gap
.

The starting point of our theory is the fact that
this
Knowledge Gap
tends to lead to a
Power Gap

(
A1
, see Section
4.2
).

The
Bond
between the developers helps them mitigate the

Power Gap
together (
A2
, see Section
4.7
);
a
Fragile Ego
of any one developer makes mitigation harder
(
A3
, see Section
4.7
).

Hierarchical Behavior
increases the
Power Gap

(
A4
, see Section
4.3
).
A big
Power Gap
can lead to
Unbalanced Process Agency

where mostly one partner is doing the decision-making
(
A5
, see Section
4.2
),
which in turn can hurt the
Quality
of the task outcome
(
A6
, see Section
4.2
).

A big
Power Gap
can mislead the
subordinate
partner into
Disengaging Behavior

(
A7
, see Section
4.6
), which
creates or increases
Unbalanced Process Agency

(
A8
, see Section
4.6
) and
hurts knowledge transfer
(
A9
, see Section
4.6
),
which increases or keeps up the
Knowledge Gap

(
A10
, see Section
4.6
),
which keeps up the
Power Gap
.

A big
Power Gap
can also mislead the
subordinate
partner into
Defensive Behavior

(
A11
, see Section
4.5
),
which reduces
Togetherness

(
A12
, see Section
4.5
),
which can lead to Breakdowns,
which hurt the
Efficiency
of PP
(
A13
, see Section
4.5
).

Equalizing Behavior
works as an antidote against all these negative
effects by reducing the
Power Gap

(

).

blocks
Equalizing Behavior

, see Section

5.
Limitations

Realism
: Our videos stem from real industrial PP sessions (if not always decided
fully spontaneously
(
Zieris 2020
, Section 4.3)
)
with a real industrial pair working on a real and current industrial task.
We have filtered our survey data and excluded all responses that did not
satisfy a similar standard.
We therefore claim high realism of our data.

Credibility
: Our statements are grounded in multiple instances of each
phenomenon and we present lots of quotes for satisfying the
“showing rather than telling” standard of good qualitative research

(
Tracy 2010
)
.
We therefore expect our statements and theory to be highly credible.

The mapping from our survey questions to the GT concepts is obviously
not one-to-one, but the danger of distortions is reduced by having more
than one question (with coherent results) in most cases.

Completeness
:
We expect there are phenomena around the
Power Gap
that
we have not encountered in our data.
However, with respect to the engineering usefulness of our
PP skill contribution, completeness is critical for only the inputs
that make
Power Gaps
larger or smaller:

Hierarchical Behavior
and
Equalizing Behavior
.
This set, being a pair of opposites, is very likely complete.
Furthermore, for other phenomena we have not yet seen, we expect
they will usually be specialized variants of one of the categories that already
exist in Figure
12
.

Generalizability
:
Our videos represent a wide variety of situations, e.g. regarding
software domain and PP task type.
Our survey data likewise represent a wide variety of respondent types,
e.g. regarding experience, PP frequency, team size, and organization size;
see the data package for details.
Both types of data, however, represent mostly German contexts.
For most research interests, this would be a strong limitation
as far as generalizability is concerned.
However, this is not true in our case.

The qualitative results appear robust: It is difficult to imagine
that one of the relationships shown in Figure
12
becomes
untrue in a different context; only a lack of completeness could
reduce generalizability and this appears to be harmless as discussed above.
Our quantitative results, insofar as they quantify the prevalence of

Power Gap
-related problems, are likely solid as a lower bound,
for the following reason:
Germany is a low-context culture
(
Hall and Hall 1990
)

and has low power distance
(
Group 2023
)
.
Both of these mean German work culture is strong on direct communication,
which is a huge asset for successful PP.
Inhabitants of contexts with higher-context culture or higher power distance
will have a harder time getting their PP to work.

Our quantitative data is not supposed to make precise measurements.
We only use it to support the claim that the PP issues discussed in
our qualitative work are not niche phenomena; they are common difficulties.
The above culture argument for generalizability then implies
we can generalize to say:
in many places the PP problems will be even more common.

6.
Conclusions

6.1.
Main Takeaways

•

Power Gaps
are critical events in a PP session
that threaten all three major advantages of PP:
better result
Quality
,
Knowledge Transfer
, and

Efficiency
.

•

Power Gaps
can result in two negative behavioral patterns,

Defensive Behavior
and
Disengaging Behavior
that create
vicious cycles of self-reinforcing influences that will then often
utterly wreck the usefulness of having a second person in the session.

•

Being able to manage the constant threat of
Power Gaps

by conscious or even routine use of
Equalizing Behavior

and conscious or even effortless avoidance of
Hierarchical Behavior

is an important element of PP skill.

6.2.
Advice for PP Practitioners

The following conclusions go beyond the immediate results.
They aim at helping people who want to improve their PP skill
in the above sense in order to become better pair programmers.

We believe that pair programmers should develop their PP skill
explicitly

rather than just rely on the accumulation of experience.
Much as you might learn and practice a new language or API,
you should familiarize yourself with the
Power Gap
dynamic
explained in the Grounded Theory.

Then ask yourself how much you tend towards exhibiting

Hierarchical Behavior
and why?
Is it for instance because of a
Fragile Ego
?
4
4

4

This relationship
is not directly observable and so our data has not produced such an arrow
in the GT figure, but we suspect that much if not most

Hierarchical Behavior
is rather a sign of weakness, not strength.

If so, can you perform meta-communication with your partners

before
any PP session to build trust, i.e., to convince each other there is no
need to be afraid of being (and being seen as) imperfect?
Can you turn (some of) your
Hierarchical Behavior
into

Equalizing Behavior
?

If you find yourself on the subordinate side of
Power Gaps
,
can you muster the strength to start
Meta-Communication

right during the session for stopping or reducing some
Hierarchical Behavior
?
Have you agreed on process-level session goals besides
the usual product-level goals so that you have better awareness when switching
to the meta-level is appropriate?
Can you find and use opportunities for
Equalizing Behavior
?
Process-level goals may provide additional changes for that as well.

6.3.
Further Research Work

In order to turn the above speculative conclusions into certain knowledge,
future research should investigate and evaluate the above advice and
other methods that may help engineers develop their PP skill and
manage the
Power Gap
.
