An unusual cause of chronic recurrent vomiting and life-threatening hyperkalemia: primary adrenal insufficiency due to probable bilateral adrenal tuberculosis

Introduction

Primary adrenal insufficiency is an uncommon but potentially life-threatening endocrine disorder resulting from inadequate production of adrenal cortical hormones. Although autoimmune adrenalitis is the predominant cause in high-income countries, tuberculosis remains an important cause of primary adrenal insufficiency in many low- and middle-income countries where the burden of tuberculosis remains high [1], [2].

Adrenal involvement usually occurs through hematogenous dissemination of Mycobacterium tuberculosis and may ultimately lead to irreversible adrenal destruction. Because the clinical manifestations are often non-specific, patients frequently experience prolonged symptoms before the diagnosis is established. In resource-limited settings, recognition of characteristic clinical and radiological features is essential for timely diagnosis and treatment [2], [3].

We report the case of a 40-year-old Ethiopian woman who presented with chronic recurrent vomiting, constitutional symptoms, and severe electrolyte abnormalities and was subsequently diagnosed with primary adrenal insufficiency secondary to adrenal tuberculosis. This case highlights the diagnostic challenges associated with adrenal tuberculosis and emphasizes the importance of considering adrenal insufficiency in patients with unexplained chronic gastrointestinal complaints in tuberculosis-endemic regions.

Case Presentation

Clinical History

A 40-year-old woman from Ethiopia presented with a five-month history of intermittent abdominal pain and recurrent episodes of non-bloody vomiting of ingested matter. The symptoms were associated with generalized myalgia, arthralgia, intermittent low-grade fever, night sweats, and significant unintentional weight loss. The patient had no cough, sputum production, hemoptysis, or other respiratory symptoms.

Regarding tuberculosis exposure history, the patient resided in an area endemic for tuberculosis and denied a prior personal history of tuberculosis or contact with confirmed TB patients. However, given her residence in an endemic region and the patient's socioeconomic circumstances, the exact occupational or community TB exposure could not be reliably established. She reported no known household contacts with active tuberculosis at the time of presentation.

During the course of her illness, she had multiple outpatient and emergency department visits and received symptomatic treatment including proton pump inhibitors. She was also prescribed empirical oral medications for an unspecified rheumatologic condition, with no definitive diagnosis established. She also reported progressive generalized skin darkening over several months.

Two weeks before admission, she experienced progressive worsening of fatigue accompanied by persistent vomiting, poor oral intake, and generalized weakness, prompting presentation to our hospital.

Physical Examination

On admission, physical examination revealed hypotension with blood pressure of 86/50 mmHg, pulse rate of 108 beats per minute, and profound weakness. The patient appeared chronically ill with clinical signs of dehydration. Subtle diffuse hyperpigmentation involving the facial skin and buccal mucosa was noted.

Laboratory Investigations

Initial laboratory evaluation revealed hypoglycemia (random blood glucose 64 mg/dl), hyponatremia (serum sodium 128 mmol/L), and severe hyperkalemia (serum potassium 7.71 mmol/L). Renal function was preserved with a serum creatinine of 1.15 mg/dL. Liver enzymes were within normal limits except for mildly elevated alkaline phosphatase.

Complete blood count demonstrated mild microcytic anemia (hemoglobin 11.7 g/dL, MCV 74.4 fL) and relative lymphocytosis with mild neutropenia. Erythrocyte sedimentation rate was markedly elevated at 94 mm/hr. The patient had no cough, sputum production, or other respiratory symptoms throughout admission, and no evidence of active pulmonary tuberculosis. Consequently, a spontaneous sputum specimen could not be obtained for AFB smear microscopy, GeneXpert MTB/RIF, or mycobacterial culture. Because of the absence of respiratory symptoms and normal chest imaging, additional invasive pulmonary investigations were not pursued. HIV serology was negative, excluding HIV-associated adrenal insufficiency as a contributing diagnostic consideration.

Given the combination of constitutional symptoms, persistent gastrointestinal complaints, hyponatremia, and severe hyperkalemia, adrenal insufficiency was suspected. Morning serum cortisol was markedly reduced along with elevated ACTH level, supporting the diagnosis of adrenal insufficiency. Laboratory summary is presented in Table 1.

Imaging Studies

Pre (Fig. 1A) and post contrast computed tomography scan (Figs. 2B and 2C) of the abdomen using adrenal protocol demonstrated bilateral enlarged heterogeneous hypo-attenuating adrenal glands with internal punctate calcifications and peripheral thin rim-like contrast enhancement. The right adrenal gland measured 3.2 × 2.4 × 1.6 cm, while the left measured 2.5 × 1.9 × 1.6 cm. Multiple sub-centimeter mesenteric lymph nodes were also noted. Additional imaging findings included no ascites and no hepatosplenic lesions. Chest radiograph was normal. The radiologic differential diagnosis included adrenal tuberculosis and remote adrenal hemorrhage.

Diagnosis and Management

Considering the chronic constitutional symptoms, bilateral adrenal involvement with calcification, biochemical evidence of primary adrenal insufficiency, and the endemicity of tuberculosis, adrenal tuberculosis was considered the most likely diagnosis. During the admission, emergency treatment was initiated with intravenous calcium gluconate for cardiac membrane stabilization, intravenous insulin with dextrose to promote intracellular potassium shift, and supportive measures including close cardiac and electrolyte monitoring. The patient was also initiated on anti-tuberculous therapy together with glucocorticoid and mineralocorticoid replacement using prednisolone and fludrocortisone.

Outcome and Follow-up

At one-month follow-up, the patient reported marked clinical improvement. Persistent vomiting had completely resolved, appetite had improved, and fatigue had significantly decreased. Constitutional symptoms had subsided, she was able to resume her usual daily activities, and gained approximately 5 kg within one month. The favorable clinical response further supported the diagnosis of adrenal tuberculosis causing primary adrenal insufficiency. Timeline of clinical events is summarized in Table 2.

Discussion

Tuberculosis was historically the leading cause of primary adrenal insufficiency before effective anti-tuberculous therapy became widely available [3]. Although autoimmune adrenalitis now predominates in developed countries, adrenal tuberculosis remains an important cause of adrenal failure in tuberculosis-endemic regions. The adrenal glands are particularly vulnerable to hematogenous spread of Mycobacterium tuberculosis because of their rich vascular supply [2], [3].

Clinical adrenal insufficiency generally develops only after destruction of more than 90% of adrenal cortical tissue, which explains the often gradual and non-specific presentation. Symptoms such as fatigue, weight loss, abdominal discomfort, nausea, and vomiting may evolve over months before the diagnosis is considered [4]. Although concomitant pulmonary tuberculosis is present in a substantial proportion of patients with adrenal tuberculosis, isolated extra pulmonary adrenal involvement is also well recognized. Therefore, pulmonary tuberculosis should be actively evaluated whenever clinically feasible. In our patient, there were no respiratory symptoms, no sputum production, and chest radiography was normal, precluding microbiological evaluation from respiratory specimens. This case highlights that the absence of clinically apparent pulmonary disease does not exclude adrenal tuberculosis, particularly in tuberculosis-endemic settings when characteristic clinical, biochemical, and radiological findings are present [5].

Primary adrenal insufficiency results in deficiencies of both glucocorticoids and mineralocorticoids. Cortisol deficiency contributes to fatigue, weakness, and hypotension, while aldosterone deficiency leads to sodium loss, potassium retention, and volume depletion. In our patient, severe hyperkalemia and hyponatremia were key findings that prompted further endocrine evaluation. In addition to hyponatremia and hyperkalemia, our patient had hypoglycemia, which is consistent with cortisol deficiency and further supports the diagnosis of primary adrenal insufficiency [6].

Gastrointestinal Manifestations and Diagnostic Delay

Gastrointestinal symptoms are among the most frequent manifestations of adrenal insufficiency but are often overlooked [3]. Chronic nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, and anorexia commonly lead clinicians to focus on gastrointestinal disorders rather than endocrine disease [6]. In this case, recurrent vomiting dominated the presentation and resulted in multiple healthcare encounters before the underlying diagnosis was recognized.

Several factors likely contributed to the diagnostic delay. The presenting symptoms were non-specific and overlapped with many common conditions [3]. Hyperpigmentation, a classic sign of primary adrenal insufficiency, may be subtle and more difficult to recognize in individuals with darker skin, contributing to delayed diagnosis. In our patient, careful examination of the facial skin and buccal mucosa revealed diffuse hyperpigmentation that supported the diagnosis.

In addition, the patient's previous diagnosis of an unspecified rheumatologic condition may also have contributed to the diagnostic delay. In retrospect, her chronic myalgia and arthralgia may have been early manifestations of primary adrenal insufficiency, as musculoskeletal symptoms are recognized but often overlooked features of the disease [4], [7]. Temporary symptomatic improvement with empiric oral corticosteroids may have further masked the underlying diagnosis. The lack of pulmonary tuberculosis on chest imaging may also have lowered clinical suspicion for a tuberculous etiology [2].

Radiological Features and Differential Diagnosis

The imaging appearance of adrenal tuberculosis varies with disease stage. Active disease often presents with enlarged adrenal glands and central necrosis, whereas chronic disease is characterized by calcification and fibrosis [8], [9]. In our patient, CT demonstrated bilateral heterogeneous enlarged adrenal glands with internal punctate calcifications and peripheral rim-like enhancement, findings highly suggestive of adrenal tuberculosis in an endemic setting [10].

Autoimmune adrenalitis was considered less likely because contrast-enhanced CT demonstrated bilateral adrenal enlargement with peripheral calcification rather than the normal-sized or atrophic adrenal glands typically seen in autoimmune adrenalitis [1], [11].Other differential diagnosis included bilateral adrenal hemorrhage, metastatic disease, lymphoma, and fungal infections [12]. However, the chronic course, constitutional symptoms, elevated inflammatory markers, bilateral calcified adrenal glands, and subsequent clinical improvement after anti-tuberculous therapy favored adrenal tuberculosis. Although histopathological or microbiological confirmation was not obtained, the overall clinical, biochemical, and radiological picture strongly supported the diagnosis [10].

Clinical Implications

This case underscores the importance of considering adrenal insufficiency in patients presenting with chronic unexplained gastrointestinal symptoms, particularly when accompanied by hypotension, hyponatremia, and hyperkalemia. In tuberculosis-endemic regions, bilateral calcified adrenal lesions on imaging provide an important diagnostic clue and may support early treatment when definitive diagnostic testing is unavailable.

Limitations

No microbiological or histopathological confirmation was obtained, as GeneXpert, IGRA, Mantoux testing, and adrenal biopsy were not performed. While this represents a limitation, the diagnosis was strongly supported by constitutional symptoms, elevated ESR, bilateral calcified adrenal lesions, biochemical findings suggestive of primary adrenal insufficiency, residence in a tuberculosis-endemic region, and marked clinical and biochemical improvement following anti-tuberculous therapy and hormone replacement. The favorable therapeutic response further argues against alternative diagnoses such as metastatic disease or autoimmune adrenalitis.

Patient Perspective

Before the diagnosis, I had recurrent vomiting, weakness, and weight loss that repeatedly brought me to health facilities without improvement. After starting treatment, my symptoms resolved, my appetite returned, and I was able to resume my normal daily activities. I felt relieved that the underlying cause of my illness had finally been identified and treated.

Conclusion

Adrenal tuberculosis should be considered in patients from tuberculosis-endemic areas presenting with unexplained chronic gastrointestinal symptoms, constitutional features, hyponatremia, and hyperkalemia. Early recognition and treatment can be lifesaving and prevent adrenal crisis. This case demonstrates that the gastrointestinal manifestations of adrenal insufficiency can dominate the clinical presentation, directing diagnostic attention away from the underlying endocrine disorder and resulting in significant diagnostic delays. The characteristic combination of electrolyte abnormalities, hyponatremia with hyperkalemia, should alert clinicians to the possibility of adrenal insufficiency regardless of the presenting complaint. In tuberculosis-endemic regions, bilateral calcified adrenal masses are a suggestive imaging finding that, combined with appropriate clinical and biochemical features, may justify empirical anti-tuberculous therapy. The excellent response to treatment observed in our patient provides reassuring evidence that timely intervention can produce dramatic clinical improvement, allowing patients to return to their usual activities and preventing the potentially fatal complication of adrenal crisis.
