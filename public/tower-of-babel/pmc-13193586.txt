TL1A as a therapeutic renaissance in inflammatory bowel disease: a systematic review from molecular mechanisms to clinical translation
Authors: Raymond Fueng-Hin Liang, Cecilia Lina Pugliano, Robert Hughes, Snehali Majumder, Antonio Lo Bello, Marietta Iacucci, Subrata Ghosh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and AimsDespite major therapeutic advances, many patients with inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) continue to experience inadequate treatment response, progressive disease, and irreversible complications requiring surgery. Current management is constrained by a biological efficacy ceiling and lack of agents directly targeting key drivers of fibrosis and barrier dysfunction. This systematic review synthesizes current knowledge on tumor necrosis factor-like cytokine 1A (TL1A) and death-domain receptor 3 (DR3) signaling pathways, critically evaluates the therapeutic potential of TL1A-targeted interventions, and explores future directions for precision medicine applications in IBD care.MethodsA comprehensive systematic search of PubMed, EMBASE, Cochrane library, Web of Science, and ClinicalTrials.gov was conducted according to PRISMA guidelines. Three independent reviewers screened preclinical and clinical studies using Rayyan software. Study selection, data extraction, and quality assessment were performed in duplicate. Eligible studies underwent narrative thematic synthesis.ResultsOf 250 studies identified, 63 met inclusion criteria. Preclinical data consistently demonstrate TL1A-DR3 signaling drives inflammation, fibrosis, and barrier dysfunction. Phase 2 clinical trials showed anti-TL1A monoclonal antibodies (Tulisokibart, Afimkibart, Duvakitug) achieved clinical remission/response rates between 26% and 66% in patients with moderate-to-severe IBD at 12-14 weeks, with favorable safety profiles. Emerging themes include extended dosing intervals, therapeutic benefit in treatment-refractory populations, potential anti-fibrotic effects, and opportunities for biomarker-guided patient stratification.ConclusionTL1A inhibitors represent a promising novel therapeutic class with dual anti-inflammatory and anti-fibrotic properties, addressing critical unmet clinical needs in IBD. These agents hold the potential to become cornerstone therapies in the evolving landscape of precision medicine for IBD care.Keywords: inflammatory bowel disease, anti-TL1A, precision medicine, anti-fibrotic

1. Introduction

Over the past two decades, the therapeutic landscape for inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) including ulcerative colitis (UC) and Crohn’s disease (CD) has evolved remarkably, driven by deeper understanding of its complex pathogenetic mechanisms.1 Nevertheless, a substantial proportion of patients continue to experience incomplete or inadequate therapeutic response, particularly within aggressive or complex disease phenotypes such as stricturing CD, where surgery often remains the only viable option for non-responders.2,3 Although current treatments primarily modulate immune activation by targeting specific inflammatory pathways, a persistent gap remains in approaches that promote mucosal healing and restore intestinal homeostasis.1

Tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-like cytokine 1A (TL1A) is a member of the TNF superfamily encoded by the TNFS15 gene first identified in 2002 as an important immune mediator in T cell activation.4 Since then, it has been recognized as a potential therapeutic target across several chronic autoimmune diseases, including IBD.5 Research interest has highlighted its dual role as a pleiotropic agent on innate and adaptive immunity, along with fibrogenesis6 modulation, a property that distinguishes it from conventional anti-inflammatory targets.

Consequently, monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) against TL1A have emerged as a promising novel therapeutic class for IBD. Beyond their immunomodulatory effects, the opportunity to stratify patients through pharmacogenomics profiling offers an exciting step toward precision medicine, enabling prediction of therapeutic response and personalized disease management.

Despite increasing interest in TL1A’s role in the context of IBD, evidence supporting TL1A therapeutics remains fragmented across experimental models and Phase 2 and 3 trials, without systematic synthesis integrating mechanistic, translational, and clinical data. Accordingly, this systematic narrative review aims to provide a critical appraisal of TL1A signaling pathways and their molecular mechanisms, integrate preclinical insights with available clinical trial evidence, and evaluate the therapeutic potential of TL1A-targeted interventions. Emphasis is placed on translational relevance and future clinical applications within the evolving field of precision medicine in IBD care (Figure 1).

2. Methods

This systematic review adhered to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines.7 The review protocol was registered in PROSPERO database (registration number: CRD420251243188).

Eligible studies were grouped into two categories according to their study design, preclinical or experimental research and clinical studies.

2.2. Information sources and search strategy

A comprehensive, multi-database systematic literature search was performed on Medline (PubMed), Embase (OVID), Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL), Web of Science Core Collection (WoS) and ClinicalTrials.gov databases. Searches combined terms associated with IBD, the TL1A/DR3 pathway, and clinical effects of TL1A inhibitors. English-language studies published from the discovery of TL1A in 2002 through October 2025 were eligible for inclusion. No restrictions were applied based on ethnicity or region. Controlled vocabulary (MeSH/Emtree) and free text keywords were used and combined using Boolean operators. The full search strategies for each database are provided in Supplementary Material 1. Reference lists of the included studies were manually screened to identify additional relevant publications.

2.3. Data extraction and synthesis

Three investigators (CLP, RH, ALB) independently screened the titles and abstracts of the identified records, excluding those not meeting inclusion criteria, using Rayyan software. Discrepancies were resolved by consensus or, when necessary, by adjudication from two senior reviewers (MI, SG). In the second screening phase, four independent reviewers (CLP, RH, ALB, RL) performed full-text data extraction. Extracted data included study design, population characteristics, intervention details, outcome measures, and key mechanistic findings. The two senior reviewers verified 30% of extracted data for accuracy and consistency.

2.4. Risk of bias assessment

Risk of bias assessment results are shown in Supplementary Material 2. Quality assessment of clinical studies was performed using multiple validated tools selected based on study design. Randomized controlled trials and phase 2 studies were evaluated using the revised Cochrane risk-of-bias tool (RoB 2).8 Non-randomized clinical studies were assessed using the Risk of Bias in Non-randomised Studies (ROBINS-I) tool.9 For preclinical animal studies, SYRCLE’s tool10 was employed. Summary traffic light and bar plots were created to visualize domain-level risk using the Robvis tool11 (Supplementary Material 2). Risk of bias assessment was not performed for ongoing clinical trials that lacked published results or for those with conference abstracts that provided insufficient methodological details. Key methodological features were systematically extracted for in vitro/ex vivo human studies without formal bias assessment, given the absence of a validated tool for these study designs (Table S2).

3.1. Search results and data selection

A total of 250 studies were initially identified across all the databases and manual screening. After removing duplicates and screening titles and abstracts, 140 citations underwent full-text assessment. Of these, 63 studies met the inclusion criteria after full-text review and were included in the narrative synthesis. Among them, 29 were preclinical (22 published full papers, seven conference abstracts) while 34 were clinical (five published full papers, 12 conference abstracts, 17 ongoing clinical trials).

3.2. Data synthesis

Findings were synthesized using a structured systematic narrative approach, integrating mechanistic, translational, and clinical outcomes.

3.3. Study characteristics

Among preclinical studies, 13 employed IBD murine only models, eight used only human cells, and eight utilized complementary human and animal in vitro or ex vivo approaches. Completed clinical trials enrolled a total of 851 patients with moderate-to-severe UC (n = 658) and CD (n = 193). The characteristics of included studies are summarized in Supplementary Material 3.

Risk of bias assessment identified overall low risk for ARTEMIS-UC and TUSCANY-2 (Figure S1), while APOLLO-CD and RELIEVE-UCCD were graded at serious risk due to their open-label design (Figure S2). In preclinical animal studies, most domains were rated as having “low” or “unclear” risk of bias, with insufficient reporting relating to performance bias and blinding noted throughout the studies (Figure S3).

3.4.1. TL1A overexpression promotes T-cell-driven intestinal inflammation

Across multiple preclinical murine models (n = 12 studies), constitutive TL1A overexpression consistently induced T-cell-driven intestinal inflammation characterized by ileitis and associated fibrotic changes (Table 2).

Sustained TL1A expression led to intestinal immune hyperactivation and spontaneous inflammation, characterized by an overexpressed T-cell phenotype and an increased expression of gut-homing receptors (CCR9 and CCR10), Foxp3+ Treg cells, and Th1/Th17 cytokine activity.12–16 Furthermore, TL1A signaling also influenced the severity and anatomical distribution of mucosal inflammation. Transgenic models demonstrated increased IL-17 production, while T-cell transfer models increased interferon-γ (IFN-γ) expression, indicating pathway-specific modulation of effector responses.

Moreover, a higher TL1A expression was associated with increased prevalence of ileal or ileo-cecal inflammation and fibrotic stricturing in a human cohort of CD.17

Translational human studies (n = 11) further supported these findings. Increased expression of TL1A, DR3, and its decoy receptor in inflamed intestinal mucosa, together with activation of the TL1A–DR3 axis in mucosal and peripheral immune cells, was associated with enhanced IFN-γ production and a Th1/Th17-polarized immune response.18–21 Genetic variation in TNFSF15 further supports this mechanism, as risk haplotypes in a Jewish group of patients with CD were associated with increased TL1A expression in peripheral monocytes and enhanced immune activation.22 Furthermore, functional analyses demonstrated that the suppressive capacity of regulatory Treg cells was dependent on intact TL1A expression levels and DR3 signaling: lower TL1A expression preserved regulatory function and ameliorated experimental colitis, whereas higher TL1A expression or loss of DR3 impaired immune regulation.23

3.4.2. TL1A drives intestinal fibrvosis

Beyond its pro-inflammatory effects, TL1A signaling directly promotes intestinal fibrogenesis and tissue-remodeling (n = 8 studies). In human preclinical models, pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1, TNF-α, IFN-γ) upregulated TL1A expression in intestinal subepithelial myofibroblasts. In contrast, epithelial cells increased DR3/DcR3 expression, thereby releasing soluble mediators that trigger TL1A signaling.24 Consistent with these findings, elevated TL1A expression in intestinal tissues from patients with IBD was associated with loss of epithelial marker expression and concomitant increase of mesenchymal marker expression, supporting the role for TL1A in epithelial–mesenchymal transition-like processes.25

In vivo evidence further proved a direct profibrotic role for TL1A. TL1A-transgenic mice exhibited greater susceptibility to developing severe intestinal fibrosis, driven by an enhanced TGF-β1/Smad3 signaling pathway and overexpression of transcription factors associated with the epithelial–mesenchymal transition.25 Importantly, murine in vivo studies demonstrated that direct TL1A–DR3 signaling on fibroblasts is required for fibrogenesis, as both global and fibroblast-specific DR3 deletion reduced collagen deposition and fibroblast activation. Complementary in vitro studies (n = 3 studies) showed that TL1A directly activated intestinal fibroblasts and triggered profibrotic transcriptional programmes, involving Rho-dependent signaling pathways,26 and TGF-β1/Smad3 activation.27 Finally, human genetic data further support a profibrotic role for TL1A. In a Chinese cohort, the TNFSF15 rs6478109 GG genotype was associated with increased TL1A expression, higher levels of profibrotic proteins, enhanced profibrotic signaling via the TGFβ1/Smad3 pathway, and a higher risk of stricturing CD compared to the AA genotype.28

3.4.3. Microbiota modulates TL1A-driven fibrogenesis

Emerging evidence indicates that the intestinal microbiota critically modulates TL1A-mediated intestinal fibrosis (n = 3 studies). In accordance with this, a human study showed that stimulation of antigen-presenting cells (APCs) with multiple bacteria induced TL1A expression and enhanced IFN-γ production, supporting a functional link between microbial signals and activation of the TL1A–DR3 axis.29 Furthermore, under germ-free conditions, TL1A-driven inflammation, fibroblast activation, and collagen deposition were markedly attenuated compared to conventionally housed animals. Reconstitution with pathogen-free microbiota fully restored the profibrotic phenotype, establishing a link between microbial colonization and TL1A-dependent fibrogenesis.30 Notably, TL1A-mediated bacterial internalization was associated with a profibrotic intestinal phenotype, establishing a direct link between microbial colonization and TL1A-dependent fibrogenesis.31

3.4.4. TL1A therapeutic blockade attenuates inflammation and reverses fibrosis

Therapeutic blockade of the TL1A–DR3 axis consistently reduced intestinal inflammation and fibrosis (n = 9 studies). Anti-TL1A strategies were associated with decreased histological inflammation, reduced collagen deposition, and attenuation of fibroblast and myofibroblast activation.32,33 Importantly, both neutralization of TL1A and genetic disruption of DR3 were sufficient not only to prevent, but also to reverse established intestinal fibrosis, supporting a direct role of TL1A–DR3 signaling in gut fibrogenesis.34

Several preclinical ongoing studies (n = 6) have investigated various TL1A-targeting agents, including monoclonal, bispecific, and biparatropic antibodies, used alone or in combination with IL-23 blockade. These agents consistently blocked TL1A-mediated signaling across cellular assays and mouse models of intestinal and systemic inflammation, reducing the production of inflammatory cytokines such as IFN-γ and IL-17, and improving disease outcomes. Notably, multiple compounds showed additive or synergistic effects over single-pathway inhibition with a favorable pharmacokinetics and extended half-lives, supporting their further development for clinical translation.35–40

3.5. Clinical efficacy of anti-TL1A therapy in IBD: a cutting-edge therapeutic advance

3.5.1. Overview of therapeutic agents

Three fully humanized mAbs targeting TL1A have advanced into clinical development: Tulisokibart (humanized IgG1-kappa mAb), Afimkibart (human IgG1 mAbs), and Duvakitug (human IgG1 mAbs). All three agents inhibit TL1A-DR3 binding, with Duvakitug exhibiting reduced affinity for DcR3.41,42 Table S3 summarizes the key characteristics of completed and ongoing clinical studies evaluating these agents.

3.5.2. Key results from Phase 2 clinical trials

3.5.2.1. TL1A inhibition in UC

In the Phase 2 randomized double-blind trial ARTEMIS-UC, patients receiving Tulisokibart achieved the primary endpoint of clinical remission at week 12 significantly more often than those receiving placebo (26% vs 1%, P < .001) (Table 3). Treatment superiority was also observed across key secondary endpoints, including higher rates of mucosal healing (31% vs 4%, P < .001) and histologic improvement (46% vs 18%, P < .001).43 Notably, superior symptomatic response was observed with Tulisokibart as early as day 2,44 and maintenance of response has been reported up to week 50 in induction responders.45

TUSCANY is a Phase 2a single-arm open-label trial studying Afimkibart in UC. Overall, 38% of patients achieved the primary endpoint of week 14 endoscopic improvement, while 24% reached the secondary endpoint of endoscopic remission.46 In the subsequent randomized double-blind Phase 2b TUSCANY-2 study, no significant differences between Afimkibart and placebo were seen for the primary endpoint of week 14 clinical remission; however, significantly more patients attained secondary endpoints including endoscopic improvement, with treatment effects sustained through week 56 on maintenance therapy.47 Higher proportions of patients also achieved histologic–endoscopic improvement and remission through week 56.48 A clear dose–response relationship was not observed with Afimkibart.

In the UC cohort of the Phase 2b double-blind placebo-controlled dose-ranging basket trial RELIEVE-UCCD,49 both studied doses achieved significantly higher week 14 clinical remission rates compared to placebo (450 mg 36%; 900 mg 48%; placebo 20%).

3.5.2.2. TL1A inhibition in CD

In APOLLO-CD, a Phase 2a open-label trial, Tulisokibart was evaluated as induction therapy in moderate-to-severe CD.50 The primary efficacy endpoint of week 12 endoscopic response was met, with significantly higher response compared to the historical placebo controls. Week 12 clinical remission was also superior to historical placebo benchmarks. Exploratory endpoints showed week 12 endoscopic remission and histologic remission in approximately 15% and 20% of patients respectively. Therapeutic efficacy persisted in induction responders through week 50, particularly with the higher (250 mg) maintenance dose.51

Post-induction results have also been reported for the CD cohort in RELIEVE-UCCD. Both Duvakitug doses achieved statistically higher proportions of week 14 endoscopic response than placebo.42 On subgroup analysis of patients with moderate and severe endoscopic activity, higher rates of clinical and endoscopic response and remission were observed with Duvakitug compared to placebo.52

3.5.3. Mechanistic basis of clinical outcomes in treatment-refractory populations

A significant proportion of patients enrolled in the cited Phase 2 trials had prior exposure to advanced therapies. In some of these trials, bio-experienced patients represented the majority of the study population (47% in ARTEMIS-UC and 71% in APOLLO-CD, along with 72% of patients enrolled in TUSCANY with prior anti-TNF exposure).42,46,50 A plausible explanation for the therapeutic success of anti-TL1A agents in these difficult-to-treat populations is their ability to bypass and overcome mechanisms of biologic drug resistance, including genetic polymorphisms and altered cytokine expression.53

In ARTEMIS-UC, TNFSF15 polymorphisms were part of the genetic diagnostic assay predicting positive response to TL1A inhibition.43 Moreover, TL1A has also been shown to function upstream and independent of TNF-α,54 underscoring its efficacy in anti-TNF refractory patients.

In APOLLO-CD, clinical and endoscopic responders demonstrated significantly lower post-induction levels of inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β, IL-6, and IL-17A. Tulisokibart recipients with endoscopic response also showed down-regulation of inflammatory gene expression, with significant reductions in Th1 and Th17 cells and inflammatory monocytes.50

3.5.4. Safety considerations

Across early clinical trials, TL1A inhibition was well-tolerated, with no new safety signals identified.

In ARTEMIS-UC and RELIEVE-UCCD, adverse event (AE) rates with Tulisokibart and Duvakitug were comparable to placebo.41,43 In TUSCANY, only one serious AE (alopecia areata) was deemed treatment-related.46 In TUSCANY-2, induction treatment-related AEs with Afimkibart were marginally greater than placebo, with two treatment-related serious AEs (saddle embolism and low-grade appendicular mucinous neoplasm) in the highest dose (450 mg) group. Importantly, no treatment-related serious AEs were observed during maintenance across dose groups. Reported serious AEs occurred in patients with underlying chronicity and comorbidities that may have predisposed to such events.47

In APOLLO-CD the incidence of treatment-related AEs was low (5%), with mild-to-moderate infections as the most common AE during induction.50 Urinary tract infections and COVID-19 were the most common reported infection AEs during induction and maintenance therapy through week 50, without impact on treatment continuation.55 In RELIEVE-UCCD, overall, AE incidence was slightly higher for the 450 mg dose (67%) than the 900 mg dose (43%) and placebo (48%).42

The favorable safety profile of TL1A inhibitors may relate to their immunological mode of action. TL1A functions primarily as an immune amplifier and T cell co-stimulator rather than a core component of host antimicrobial defense;4,56,57 inhibition of its effects would dampen immune amplification while preserving host defenses. Additionally, TL1A is not constitutively expressed but induced by antigenic stimulation, and its receptor DR3 is more selectively expressed on activated T cells and innate lymphoid cells at sites of mucosal inflammation. Selective pathway engagement provides a plausible mechanistic explanation for preserved host defense and limited systemic immune suppression.58,59

3.5.5. Ongoing clinical trials

Phase 2 studies are underway to evaluate the efficacy and safety of Duvakitug maintenance therapy in adult IBD patients60 and Afimkibart induction and maintenance therapy in adult CD patients,61 alongside additional novel anti-TL1A mAbs.62,63 Phase 3 trials are also ongoing for Tulisokibart, Afimkibart, and Duvakitug, assessing both intravenous and subcutaneous administration routes across UC and CD populations.64–74

4. Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first systematic review integrating molecular biology, translational evidence, clinical trials, and therapeutic applications on TL1A inhibition in IBD. By directly linking mechanistic insights with emerging clinical outcomes, this review identifies the TL1A–D3 axis as a central pathogenic pathway linking intestinal inflammation, barrier dysfunction, and fibrotic remodeling. The clinical properties and unique mechanism of anti-TL1A mAbs position them as promising agents within IBD treatment algorithms (Box 1, Figure 3).

TL1A signals mainly via DR3, its only known active receptor,75 which is expressed on activated CD4+ and CD8+ T cell as well as innate lymphoid cells. Emerging evidence further implicates TL1A in inflammasome-driven immunity via IL-33-mediated modulation of the NLRP3 pathway.79,80 This signal axis is counterbalanced by decoy receptor 3 (DcR3), which sequesters TL1A and prevents DR3 activation,81 although the therapeutic relevance of DcR3-selective antagonism (as with Duvakitug) remains uncertain. TL1A–DR3 signaling increases intestinal permeability and facilitates bacterial translocation by disrupting tight junction proteins, including ZO-1, occludin, and claudin.82,83

The TL1A–DR3 axis promotes myofibroblast activation, collagen synthesis, and intestinal fibrosis.26,27 In fibro-stenosing CD patients, TL1A overexpression in myofibroblasts is associated with IFN-γ, TNF-α, and IL-1α signaling.24 Although definitive clinical evidence for antifibrotic efficacy is still emerging, pre-clinical and translational data are encouraging with TL1A-DR3 blockade attenuating and even reversing intestinal fibrosis in murine models.26,27,34

In APOLLO-CD, transcriptomic analysis of intestinal mucosal biopsies showed more significant down-regulation of fibrosis-related gene expression in endoscopic responders compared to non-responders.50

In fistulizing CD, TL1A inhibition may offer unique mechanistic advantages through its combined anti-inflammatory and anti-fibrotic properties, potentially inhibiting the fibroblast–fibrosis trajectory and even reversing fibrogenesis in selected high-risk patients.84 Early post-operative targeting of the TL1A axis may therefore provide sustained protection during the critical 6- to 12-month window after surgery, potentially preventing disease recurrence and improving patients’ long-term quality of life.

Phase 2 clinical trials on anti-TL1A mAbs demonstrated robust efficacy in moderate-to-severe IBD (Table 2), despite a high portion of biologic-experienced patients, including those who had received janus kinase inhibitors and sphingosine-1-phosphate modulators.43,47,50 ARTEMIS-UC and APOLLO-CD achieved meaningful remission rates in this difficult-to-treat population, reflecting TL1A’s upstream position in inflammatory cascades.43 However, variable efficacy across trials, including failure to meet primary endpoints in TUSCANY-2, suggests that therapeutic benefit may be restricted to biologically defined patient subsets.85

Biomarker-guided therapy represents an essential strategy for optimizing anti-TL1A efficacy. Interestingly, patients with elevated TNFSF15 and specific TNFSF15 polymorphisms appear to be associated with improved therapeutic response, providing proof of concept for precision medicine approaches in IBD.43 Analysis of tissue transcriptomic data from the TUSCANY trial further revealed suppression of IL17 in endoscopic responders, while TUSCANY-2 showed numerically larger treatment effects in biomarker-positive patients.47,85 Future companion diagnostics should integrate genetic polymorphisms, transcriptomic profiles, and soluble biomarkers to guide precision-based treatment algorithms and maximize therapeutic benefits.

Beyond IBD, TL1A-DR3 signaling has been implicated in multiple extra-intestinal inflammatory conditions including rheumatoid arthritis, ankylosing spondylitis, systemic sclerosis, psoriasis, and atopic dermatitis.86–93 TNFSF15 polymorphisms are also associated with acute anterior uveitis in humans.94 Preclinical data further implicate TL1A in pulmonary fibrosis and airway remodeling, with TL1A blockade attenuating fibrotic progression in murine models.33,95,96 Taken together, these findings support a broader role for anti-TL1A therapy in inflammatory–fibrotic disease states and IBD patients with extra-intestinal manifestations.

A distinctive characteristic of anti-TL1A therapy is the potential for infrequent dosing. Extended half-life formulations of novel anti-TL1A mAbs have shown promising results in non-human primate models,36,38,97 suggesting the feasibility of maintenance dosing intervals of up to 6 or even 12 months in IBD patients. Notably, the TL1A–DR3 signaling pathway appears responsive to low-grade immune stimulation,43,47,50 suggesting particular utility in chronic disease states characterized by persistent subclinical inflammation.

Their unique pharmacodynamics make them well-suited for combination treatmentwith shorter-acting drugs, enabling long-standing control of inflammation after achieving remission.5 This may allow briefer therapeutic regimens during disease flares and less frequent maintenance dosing intervals during periods of quiescence.Given TL1A’s pleiotropic effects and pivotal role in gut immunity, combining TL1A inhibitors with agents that directly promote epithelial healing may address current IBD therapeutic gaps. TL1A blockade, in combination with a tissue-reparative factor such as IL-22, may achieve synergistic effects in controlling inflammation and restoring the intestinal barrier.

Bispecific antibodies, simultaneously co-targeting TL1A and complementary inflammatory mediators, represent an emerging therapeutic evolution.98,99 Agents engineered to target a second pro-inflammatory cytokine alongside TL1A, such as IL-23,38 IL-23p19,36 or α4β7,4 have shown synergistic or even additive effects in vitro and in vivo, outperforming their parent mAbs. These dual-action biologics could enhance efficacy while reducing the need for combination therapy.

Artificial intelligence (AI)-guided antibody design represents a further paradigm shift. An AI-generated antibody specifically targeting TL1A antibody reported in early 2025 showed superior anti-inflammatory effects in preclinical models compared to vedolizumab and risankizumab, along with an extended in vivo half-life.37 Integration of multi-omics data with AI-driven drug design may accelerate development of personalized, next-generation biologics.

Future clinical trials should move beyond traditional inflammatory endpoints to assess structural and molecular disease trajectories. Phase 3 studies should incorporate advanced imaging endpoints, (eg, magnetic resonance enterography or ultrasound elastography) and fibrosis-specific biomarkers to quantify tissue remodeling. The integration of high-resolution molecular phenotyping into platforms such as single-cell RNA sequencing, multiplex protein imaging, and spatial transcriptomics can further reveal TL1A-driven cellular crosstalk among fibroblasts, stromal cells, innate myeloid populations, and adaptive lymphocytes. These technologies will enable mapping of innate immune compartments and tissue–niche interactions that have historically been difficult to target.

Given TL1A’s role as an alarmin, short-term targeted immunomodulation or combination regimens with anti-fibrotic or stromal-modulating agents may represent the next generation of precision therapy, guided by molecular and imaging signatures.

However, limitations need to be underscored. The interpretation of current data is limited by the high proportion of conference abstracts (51%, 19/34 of published studies), short-term Phase 2 outcomes, and incomplete long-term safety data. Variable efficacy across trials underscores the need for validated predictive biomarkers. The absence of head-to-head comparator studies and real-world challenges in genetic stratification further limit definitive positioning. Ongoing Phase 3 trials will be critical to define the role of anti-TL1A therapy within IBD treatment algorithms.

5. Conclusion

Anti-TL1A mAbs represent a promising new therapeutic class, with the potential to reshape treatment paradigms in IBD. By targeting a central immune hub, these agents can modulate diverse cellular pathways that drive intestinal inflammation, fibrosis, and barrier dysfunction. Their broad mechanism, coupled with the potential for extended dosing intervals and a favorable safety profile, positions them as a next-generation option distinct from current biologics. The outcomes of ongoing Phase 3 trials will determine whether TL1A inhibition becomes a cornerstone of future precision-guided therapy in IBD.
