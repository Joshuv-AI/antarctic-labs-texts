Synthesis and evaluation of vanillin-based sulfonates as potent α-glucosidase inhibitors through in vitro, in vivo, and in silico approaches
Authors: Manel Essid, Ehsan Ullah Mughal, Nafeesa Naeem, Aneela Shaheen Cheema, Bushra Shakoor, Ishtiaq Ahmed, Charlene Marais, Amina Sadiq, Samar Y Al Nami, Aisha Hossan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn the present study, a series of structurally diverse vanillin sulfonate derivatives (1–9) were synthesized and evaluated as potential α-glucosidase inhibitors through integrated in vitro, in vivo, and in silico approaches. The target compounds were obtained via sulfonation and subsequent functional modification of the vanillin scaffold and were fully characterized using standard spectroscopic techniques. Enzyme inhibition assays demonstrated that several derivatives exhibited strong α-glucosidase inhibitory activity, with inhibitory constants significantly lower than that of the reference drug, indicating enhanced potency. The synthesized compounds showed notable α-glucosidase inhibitory activity, with compounds 8 (IC50 = 7.8 ± 2.2 µM), 6 (IC50 = 10.2 ± 1.1 µM), and 5 (IC50 = 13.4 ± 1.9 µM) exhibiting stronger activity than acarbose (IC50 = 28.3 ± 1.2 µM), indicating their potential as promising antidiabetic candidates. To the best of our knowledge, this study represents the first in vivo evaluation of vanillin sulfonate derivatives for antihyperglycemic activity. Selected lead compounds were further evaluated in an oral carbohydrate tolerance model, where they produced a significant reduction in postprandial blood glucose levels without observable acute toxicity. Molecular docking and dynamic simulation studies supported the experimental findings by revealing stable binding interactions within the catalytic pocket of α-glucosidase, including hydrogen bonding and π–π stacking with key active-site residues. Density functional theory (DFT) calculations further clarified the electronic properties, frontier molecular orbitals, and reactivity descriptors of the most active derivatives, correlating electronic parameters with inhibitory performance. Collectively, these results identify vanillin sulfonates as promising lead structures for the development of new α-glucosidase inhibitors and support their further optimization as potential antidiabetic agents.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-54124-9.Keywords: Vanillin sulfonates, α-Glucosidase, In vitro, In vivo, Molecular docking, MD simulation, ADMET, DFTSubject terms: Biochemistry, Chemical biology, Chemistry, Computational biology and bioinformatics, Drug discovery

Introduction

Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is a chronic metabolic disease marked by persistent hyperglycemia and linked to serious cardiovascular, renal, and neurological complications1. Controlling postprandial blood glucose is a key therapeutic goal, largely achieved by limiting the intestinal breakdown of dietary carbohydrates2,3. The enzyme α-glucosidase catalyses the final step of carbohydrate digestion, making it an established target for reducing glucose absorption4–7.

Current α-glucosidase inhibitors such as acarbose, miglitol, and voglibose effectively lower postprandial glucose levels but are often associated with gastrointestinal side effects and variable pharmacokinetic performance8. These limitations have encouraged the search for new inhibitors, leading to the investigation of diverse small-molecule classes including heterocycles, flavonoids, chalcones, triazoles, quinolines, chromones, and polyphenols, supported by structure–activity and molecular modelling studies9–14.

Among emerging structural motifs, sulphur-containing functionalities have attracted increasing attention in drug discovery due to their ability to modulate molecular polarity, binding affinity, and biological activity14–17. Natural products such as salacinol and neokotalanol, unique sulfonium-containing sugar analogues isolated from Salacia species, exhibit remarkably potent α-glucosidase inhibition, highlighting the therapeutic relevance of sulphur-rich pharmacophores18–21. In addition, a variety of synthetic sulfonate, sulphonamide, and sulfone derivatives have also demonstrated promising α-glucosidase inhibitory activities, further supporting the value of sulfonyl-containing scaffolds in the development of next-generation antidiabetic agents22–25.

Despite these advancements, sulfonated aldehydes remain largely unexplored as a distinct and rationally designed class of α-glucosidase inhibitors. Aldehydes are chemically versatile functional groups capable of participating in hydrogen bonding, dipolar interactions, and reversible covalent-type interactions with active site residues of enzymes26–30. The aldehyde functionality may contribute to the observed biological activity through several complementary interaction mechanisms within the enzyme active site. First, the carbonyl oxygen of the aldehyde group can act as a hydrogen-bond acceptor and interact with amino acid residues such as Ser, Thr, Tyr, Lys, Arg, Phe, or Trp present in the active pocket of α-glucosidase31,32. These hydrogen-bonding interactions can stabilize the ligand-enzyme complex and improve binding affinity. Second, due to the polarized nature of the carbonyl group, aldehydes can also participate in dipole–dipole and electrostatic interactions with surrounding residues, thereby contributing to favorable molecular recognition32,33. Furthermore, the aldehyde carbon is mildly electrophilic and may undergo reversible covalent-type interactions with nucleophilic residues such as lysine, cysteine, or serine present in the enzyme active site34,35. Such transient interactions can enhance inhibitory potency while maintaining reversibility, which is often advantageous in drug design. In addition, the planar and electronically conjugated nature of aromatic aldehydes may facilitate proper orientation of the molecule within the catalytic pocket, enabling additional hydrophobic and π-based interactions with aromatic amino acid residues32,36. Incorporation of a sulfonyl or sulfonate moiety into aldehyde frameworks introduces additional polarity and strong hydrogen bond acceptor sites, which may enhance electrostatic complementarity and binding interactions within the α-glucosidase catalytic pocket32,37–39.

Vanillin and its derivatives have attracted considerable interest in medicinal chemistry owing to their antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, antimicrobial, and antidiabetic properties, as well as their favourable safety profile and natural origin35,40–42. Structurally, vanillin represents a privileged scaffold containing an aromatic aldehyde, a phenolic hydroxyl group, and a methoxy substituent, all of which can contribute to productive interactions within enzyme active sites43. Although vanillin-derived Schiff bases, hydrazones, and other functional derivatives have been explored as α-glucosidase inhibitors44–47, vanillin-based sulfonated aldehydes have not been systematically investigated for their α-glucosidase inhibitory potential. The strategic incorporation of sulfonyl or sulfonate motif on the vanillin scaffold, therefore, provides a novel molecular design strategy of combining the natural bioactivity of an existing scaffold with favourable binding properties of sulphur-containing functionalities.

In particular, vanillin- and salicylaldehyde-based derivatives have been extensively explored for antiviral, anti-inflammatory, antimicrobial, neuroprotective, and anticancer applications35. Rodrigues et al.36 reported triazole-linked vanillin derivatives exhibiting significant virucidal activity against Zika virus, highlighting the therapeutic relevance of aldehyde-bearing frameworks. Similarly, Korkmaz and Bursal synthesized salicylaldehyde-derived sulfonates and demonstrated their promising biological activities supported by molecular docking studies32. Comprehensive reviews by Kafali et al. and Raju et al. further emphasized the pharmacological significance of vanillin-derived aldehydes in diverse therapeutic areas33,34. Moreover, spectroscopic investigations by Cocinero et al.31 confirmed the strong hydrogen-bonding characteristics associated with methoxy-substituted aldehydes such as o-vanillin. Collectively, these reports establish that the retention of the aldehyde functionality is not merely structural but is scientifically justified due to its recognized contribution toward biologically relevant molecular interactions and therapeutic potential across multiple bioactive scaffolds.

Several recent reports demonstrating enhanced α-glucosidase inhibitory activity of sulfono-substituted derivatives compared with their non-sulfonated counterparts further support the rationale for exploring this chemical class23,48–54 (Fig. 1). Sulfonate ester-based scaffolds and related hybrids have been found to have significant biological relevance in recent studies. Öztürk et al.55 identified sulfonylhydrazones with sulfonate ester frameworks that showed dual inhibition activity towards acetylcholinesterase and carbonic anhydrases. Likewise, rhodamine-sulfonate hybrids have been shown to be effective aldose reductase inhibitory agents with docking and in vitro studies56. Moreover, Aslanhan et al.57 reported the structural motifs of hydrazone derivatives, which are highly effective as antioxidants and anticholinesterase, further establishing their pharmacological significance. However, systematic investigations integrating synthesis, biological evaluation, and computational modelling of sulfonated aldehydes remain scarce.

The novelty of the present work lies in the systematic design and evaluation of sulfonated aldehydes as a new and under-investigated class of α-glucosidase inhibitors, integrating synthetic chemistry with in vitro enzyme inhibition, in vivo antidiabetic assessment, and in silico studies. We hypothesized that the synergistic presence of sulfonated and aldehyde functionalities would enhance enzyme-ligand interactions through hydrogen bonding and electrostatic complementarity, thereby improving inhibitory performance.

Materials and methods

All reagents and solvents were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich and used without further purification. 1H and 13C-NMR spectra were recorded on a Bruker Advance III 400 MHz spectrometer equipped with a 5 mm BBI H-BB-D probe at 25 °C (293 K), using CDCl3 as the solvent. Chemical shifts (δ) are reported in parts per million (ppm) and coupling constants (J) are given in Hertz (Hz). Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectra were obtained using a ThermoFisher Scientific FTIR spectrophotometer, and absorption bands are reported as wavenumbers (cm-1).

General procedure for the synthesis of vanillin sulfonates / 2-formylphenyl sulfonates (1–9)

A solution of vanillin (4-hydroxy-3-methoxybenzaldehyde, 1.00 g, 6.57 mmol) in dichloromethane (10 mL) was stirred and cooled to 0 °C. To this solution, the appropriate substituted sulfonyl chloride (1.12 g, 7.88 mmol) was added dropwise, followed by the addition of trimethylamine (1.16 mL, 8.91 mmol) as a base. After complete addition, the cooling bath was removed, and the reaction mixture was allowed to stir at room temperature for 3 h. Upon completion, the reaction was quenched with ice-cold water, and the mixture was extracted with dichloromethane. The organic layer was separated, dried over anhydrous sodium sulphate, and concentrated under reduced pressure. The resulting crude product was purified by recrystallization to afford the corresponding vanillin-based sulfonated aldehyde derivatives (1–9) in pure form38.

α-Glucosidase inhibition

In vitro assay

Compounds (1–9) were evaluated for their potential to inhibit α-glucosidase activity using a previously reported methodology58–60 and also provided in the SI file. The α-glucosidase enzyme used in this study was obtained from Saccharomyces cerevisiae (Sigma-Aldrich, USA).

In vivo assay

The comprehensive methodology for the in vivo study evaluation has been previously reported by our research group59,60 and is also available in the SI file. Moreover, animal handling was performed in compliance with the “Animal Bylaws 2008 of the University of Malakand (Scientific Procedures Issue I).” Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the Institutional Research Ethics Committee (IREC), University of Malakand, Chakdara, Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, Pakistan, following the guidelines of the University of Malakand Animal Bylaws 2008, as per notification no. Pharm/EC-Thzp/41-08/21. Additionally, this study was conducted in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines for the reporting of animal research. All relevant items from the ARRIVE 2.0 checklist have been addressed to ensure transparency and reproducibility.

At the end of the experiment, all rats were humanely euthanized in accordance with institutional animal care and use guidelines. The animals were euthanized under standard recommendations for rodent euthanasia by an overdose of sodium pentobarbital (150–200 mg/kg body weight) by intraperitoneal injection. This chemical method ensures rapid induction of deep anesthesia followed by death with minimal distress. Confirmation of death was carried out by the absence of respiratory movements, lack of corneal and pedal reflexes, and cessation of cardiac activity. All procedures were conducted by trained personnel to ensure compliance with ethical standards and to minimize animal suffering.

Molecular docking assay

Protein selection and preparation

Two of the human alpha-glucosidases were molecularly docked to rationalize the in vitro inhibitory effect of the synthesized sulfonated aldehydes (1–9). Crystal structures of 5NN8 lysosomal acid α-glucosidase61 and 8CB1 human maltase-glucoamylase intestinal alpha-glucosidase were downloaded out of the Protein Data Bank62. These proteins were selected due to their essential role in carbohydrate metabolism and regulation of glucose homeostasis, making them key targets for antidiabetic drug discovery (Table 1).

α-Glucosidase protein structures used for molecular docking with both Saccharomyces cerevisiae and human enzymes for translational insights.

To overcome the translational correlation between in silico and in vitro systems, a second α-glucosidase structure from Saccharomyces cerevisiae (PDB ID: 4J5T) was used. This structure is more representative of the biological system used in the in vitro assay and provides more appropriate structural insight into the observed enzyme inhibition63. This approach facilitates a comparative evaluation, with yeast docking used to correlate the assay and human structures to provide general pharmacological perspectives.

All protein structures were first analyzed in Molegro Virtual Docker (MVD) by removing crystallographic water molecules, as well as co-crystallized ligands. Missing hydrogen atoms were added and bond orders corrected. Protein structures were then subjected to energy minimization using the integrated preparation wizard to resolve steric clashes and optimize side-chain conformations. Active sites were defined according to the native ligand binding cavities identified by the cavity detection algorithm of MVD and the largest biologically relevant cavity was used to dock in each protein64.

Ligand preparation

The chemical structure of the synthesized sulfonated aldehydes (1–9) and the reference standard acarbose were re-drawn and transformed into 3D conformations. Protonation of all ligands was performed at physiological pH and geometry optimization performed before docking. Docking procedures used defined rotatable bonds to enable complete movement of all molecular shapes. The prepared ligands were subsequently imported into Molegro Virtual Docker for simulation studies65.

Molecular docking protocol

The computations on docking used Molegro Virtual Docker to implement the MolDock scoring function, which evaluates hydrogen bonding, electrostatic and steric interaction elements. After the definition of a binding pocket, the poses of all the ligands were calculated, and the most suitable conformation was chosen in terms of the least MolDock score and positive intermolecular patterns. Identical docking parameters were used for all the test compounds and the reference drug for consistency. Binding affinities were assessed by MolDock scores and an extensive interaction profile consisting of hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic contacts was analysed for comparative assessment66. It should be noted that docking to S. cerevisiae α-glucosidase provides a direct structural interpretation of the in vitro assay, while docking to human α-glucosidase enzymes is intended to give extra perspectives on the potential translational relevance. Thus, the docking results are considered as an additional line of evidence for the mechanism of action.

Analysis of interaction and visualization

The top-ranked docked complexes were analysed in 2D and 3D interaction profiles using Discovery Studio Visualizer. Stabilizing elements such as hydrogen bond networks, hydrophobic contacts, π–π stacking and π–alkyl interactions were mapped to determine the critical residues in the stabilization of the ligand67.

Validation of docking

To validate the docking protocol used in this work, a re-docking study was carried out with Molegro Virtual Docker (MVD). Water molecules were removed and hydrogen atoms were added to the selected α-glucosidase structure, bond orders were assigned, and the structure was energy minimized to delete the already bound ligand. The native ligand was used to define the active site using the cavity detection feature of MVD. The ligand was then placed into the binding cavity using the same docking protocol as used for the newly designed compounds, namely the MolDock scoring function, grid resolution, and search algorithm. The docking accuracy was evaluated by calculating the root mean square deviation (RMSD) between the re-docked pose and the native pose of the ligand. The two conformations were also visually compared to assess the agreement of the bound conformation68.

MD simulation assay

To further confirm the stability and dynamicity of the most attractive docked complexes, the WebGRO online server, relying on the GROMACS simulation engine, was run using molecular dynamics. The MD simulations were performed during a 50 ns period to test the structural stability of the protein–ligand complexes with time in nearly physiological conditions. According to the docking and in vitro studies, the most active compound (lead compound) in complex with α-glucosidase was chosen to undergo MD analysis, and the acarbose-protein complex was taken as a control. The protein was subjected to the GROMOS96 43a1 force field by WebGRO serve. A triclinic simulation box with a minimum distance between the complex and the box sides of 1.0 nm and solvated with SPC/E water model was used with each protein–ligand complex. In order to neutralize the system, corresponding counter ions (Na+ or Cl-) were introduced69.

The algorithm was used to minimize energy in the system until the system had a convergence criterion less than 1000 kJ/mol/nm, and the steric clashes and unfavorable contacts were eliminated. This was preceded by two equilibration stages: NVT equilibration (with constant number of particles, volume, and temperature) of 100 ps with V-rescale thermostat to stabilize the temperature at 300 K and NPT equilibration (with constant number of particles, pressure, and temperature) of 100 ps with Parrinello-Rahman barostat. A 50 ns production MD simulation was conducted in periodic boundary conditions after equilibration. WebGRO tools were used in the analysis of trajectories produced during the simulation to calculate root mean square deviation (RMSD), root mean square fluctuation (RMSF), radius of gyration (Rg), hydrogen bond analysis, and solvent-accessible surface area (SASA)70.

ADMET study

In silico evaluation of ADME

The SwissADME web server was used to analyse the pharmacokinetic and drug-likeness properties of the synthesized sulfonated aldehydes (1–9) and the reference drug, acarbose. The web server provides predictive estimates of key absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion (ADME) properties based on molecular descriptors and physicochemical parameters. The optimized structures of each compound were used to obtain the canonical SMILES and were entered into SwissADME. Physicochemical descriptors (molecular weight, TPSA, hydrogen bond donors/acceptors, rotatable bonds, and lipophilicity indices), water solubility, pharmacokinetics (GI absorption, BBB permeability, P-gp substrate prediction, and CYP450 inhibition), drug-likeness filters (Lipinski, Ghose, Veber, Egan, and Muegge), bioavailability score, and medicinal chemistry alerts were calculated. To visualize oral drug-likeness and predict passive gastrointestinal absorption and brain penetration, the bioavailability radar and BOILED-Egg model, which are graphical tools offered by SwissADME, were used71.

In silico toxicity prediction (ProTox-III)

To compare the initial safety properties of the synthesized derivatives with those of the reference drug, in silico toxicity profiling was conducted using the ProTox-III web server72. ProTox-III is an approved computational system that merges molecular similarity search, fragment-based research, and machine-learned models instructed on controlled toxicological datasets to estimate the wide-ranging toxicity endpoints. The three-dimensional structures were optimized and then transformed into canonical SMILES form, and each of these was sent to the ProTox-III server. It used the platform to forecast various clinically significant toxicity parameters, including hepatotoxicity, neurotoxicity, nephrotoxicity, respiratory toxicity, cardiotoxicity, carcinogenicity, immunotoxicity, mutagenicity, cytotoxicity, blood–brain barrier (BBB) permeability, ecotoxicity, clinical toxicity, and nutritional toxicity. Each endpoint is classified qualitatively (active/ inactive) and given a probability score by ProTox-II that shows the extent of prediction confidence. These are probability values of 0 to 1, which were applied to ensure comparative analysis between the tested molecules and the reference drug. This computational toxicity binding was done along with molecular docking and SwissADME services to provide an integrated early-stage assessment of safety and drug-likeness and support the preliminary identification of candidates for further experimental validation73. Such in silico tools can be useful for initial screening and development in the early stages, but further in vitro and in vivo experiments are required to confirm the pharmacokinetic and safety characteristics of the candidate lead compounds.

DFT study

Density Functional Theory (DFT) calculations were performed to investigate the electronic structure, chemical reactivity, and stability of the synthesized sulfonated aldehyde derivatives (1–9). These computational insights were further utilized to rationalize their observed biological activity in relation to molecular docking and in vitro experiments. All the quantum chemical calculations have been run using the Gaussian program version 09 with the Becke three-parameter Lee–Yang–Parr (B3LYP) hybrid functional using 6-311G basis set, an effective balance between cost and accuracy of calculations when working with organic compounds. GaussView was used to generate the first molecular geometries, which were fully optimized in the gas phase without symmetry constraints. Optimization was repeated until there were no imaginary frequencies, and geometry optimizations were converted to real optimizations by confirming that all geometry optimizations are actually real minima on the potential energy surface. Frontier molecular orbital (FMO) energies, global reactivity markers, and molecular electrostatic potential (MEP) maps were enumerated based on the optimized geometries. Chemical stability and reactivity were assessed using the energies of the highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) and lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO). Standard equations using the Koopmans parameter of reaction. Global reactivity parameters such as energy gap (ΔE), chemical hardness (η), chemical softness (σ), electronegativity (χ), chemical potential (μ), and electrophilicity index (ω) were calculated74.

Results and discussion

Chemistry

The synthesis of vanillin-based sulfonated aldehydes was accomplished using commercially available vanillin (4-hydroxy-3-methoxybenzaldehyde) as starting material, as outlined in Scheme 1. In a typical procedure, the aldehyde precursor was dissolved in dichloromethane and reacted with a range of sulfonyl chlorides in the presence of triethylamine as a base. The reaction mixture was stirred at ambient temperature for approximately 3 h, leading to the formation of the desired 2-formylphenyl sulfonate derivatives (1–9). The crude products were subsequently purified by recrystallization, affording the target compounds in good to excellent yields. 1H (400 MHz, CDCl3) and 13C NMR (101 MHz, CDCl3) spectroscopy and mass spectrometry (ESI) confirmed the structures of the desired compounds. The characterization data of compounds 1, 3–6, and 9 correspond to those previously reported38, whereas compounds 2, 7, and 8 are reported herein for the first time.

The characterization data of compounds 1, 3–6, and 9 correspond to those previously reported38, whereas compounds 2, 7, and 8 are reported herein for the first time. In the 1H NMR spectra of compounds 1–9, resonances typical of an ABX system with a doublet of doublets (H-5'), an ortho doublet (J = ca. 8 Hz, H-6'), and a meta doublet (J = ca. 1.8 Hz, H-3') were observed in the δH 7.49–7.27 region. Among all tested compounds, compound 8, bearing an o-chloro substituent, showed the highest potency with an IC50 of 7.8 ± 2.2 µM, representing nearly a fourfold improvement over acarbose. Its superior activity is likely due to the favorable positioning of the chloro group, which enhances hydrophobic contacts and promotes optimal orientation within the enzyme active site. Compound 6, containing a naphthalene-2-sulfonate moiety, also displayed strong inhibition (IC50 = 10.2 ± 1.1 µM), likely due to its extended aromatic surface that supports π–π stacking interactions with aromatic residues in the binding pocket.

Halogenated derivatives showed moderate to good inhibitory activity. The p-chloro compound 5 exhibited notable activity (IC50 = 13.4 ± 1.9 µM), while the 2,5-dichloro compound 2 showed good inhibition (IC50 = 16.6 ± 1.4 µM). These findings indicate that halogen substitution significantly improves enzyme inhibition, likely by increasing lipophilicity and strengthening hydrophobic interactions. The improved activity of the o-chloro derivative compared with the p-chloro and dichloro analogues suggests that substitution position plays a critical role in determining binding efficiency. The 4-bromo compound 3 demonstrated moderate potency (IC50 = 21.1 ± 2.0 µM), slightly weaker than the chloro analogues, possibly due to greater steric bulk affecting binding geometry.

The nitro-substituted compound 9 also showed moderate activity (IC50 = 19.4 ± 1.9 µM). Although the nitro group is strongly electron-withdrawing and capable of hydrogen-bond interactions, its performance suggests that electronic effects alone are insufficient without favorable steric and hydrophobic complementarity.

In contrast, derivatives bearing alkyl or electron-donating substituents were less active. The mono-methyl compound 7 (IC50 = 30.9 ± 3.4 µM) and trimethyl compound 1 (IC50 = 47.4 ± 3.1 µM) showed weak inhibition, with IC50 values in the low-to-moderate micromolar range, indicating that purely hydrophobic groups lacking strong electronic or directional interaction capability are unfavorable for enzyme inhibition. The methoxy-substituted compound 4 (IC50 = 35.7 ± 2.6 µM) also displayed comparatively low activity, suggesting limited binding enhancement despite its hydrogen-bonding potential.

These results suggest that the derivatives, particularly those bearing electron-withdrawing or aromatic substituents, effectively modulate hypertriglyceridemia associated with diabetes (Table 5).

Impact on HDL levels

These results indicate that the most active derivatives protect hepatocytes from diabetes-induced oxidative and metabolic stress, thereby normalizing SGOT activity (Table 6 and Fig. 2).

Moderate reductions were observed with compounds 2, 5, and 1, indicating that structural variations in sulfonated aldehydes influence hepatoprotective potency (Table 6 and Fig. 2).

Effect on ALP levels

Serum alkaline phosphatase (ALP) was elevated in diabetic control rats (150.74 ± 8.12 IU) compared with normal controls (110.27 ± 6.33 IU, P < 0.001), suggesting cholestasis or impaired liver function. Treatment with the sulfonated aldehydes produced significant dose-dependent reductions in ALP activity. Compounds 9, 6, and 3 at 20 mg/kg reduced ALP to 134.72 ± 7.62, 140.89 ± 7.72, and 139.31 ± 9.57 IU, respectively (***P < 0.001 vs. diabetic control), whereas acarbose reduced ALP to 122.84 ± 7.14 IU (**P < 0.01). Compounds 1, 2, 4, 5, 7, and 8 also significantly lowered ALP at higher doses, indicating overall hepatoprotective activity of the synthesized derivatives (Table 6 and Fig. 2).

A limitation of the present study is that the antihyperglycemic activity was evaluated only through acute experimental models. Although the results demonstrated promising effects, longer-term chronic studies along with detailed toxicity and pharmacokinetic investigations are required to further validate the therapeutic potential and safety profile of the tested compounds (1–9).

Molecular docking study

Molecular docking was carried out to explain the binding pattern of synthesized sulfonated aldehydes (1–9) in the active sites of human alpha-glucosidase targets and to compare computational results with experimental inhibitory results. All tested compounds exhibited stable binding within the catalytic sites of both 5NN8, 8CB1, and 4J5T with docking scores that were generally superior to or comparable with those of the reference inhibitor acarbose (Table S1 in Supplementary Information (SI) file). Hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic contacts were detected between the ligands and critical catalytic as well as neighboring residues, supporting their potential as effective α-glucosidase inhibitors. Some of the derivatives tested exhibited notably favourable MolDock scores, especially compounds 5, 6, and 8, which are associated with their increased activity in vitro. Compound 8 emerged as one of the strongest binders across both targets and was therefore selected for visualization of its binding interactions.

For consistency with the experimental data, molecular docking studies were also conducted using Saccharomyces cerevisiae and human α-glucosidase models. The structure of the yeast enzyme can be directly correlated with the in vitro enzyme inhibition results, while the human enzyme structures can be used to gain additional insights into the potential translational significance. As such, the docking results are considered as providing evidence of binding and structure–activity relationships, but not a definitive mechanism of enzyme inhibition.

Docking analysis using human acid 5NN8-glucosidase

Docking with 5NN8 showed that all the synthesized compounds occupied the same catalytic site as acarbose and interacted with different residues involved in substrate recognition and catalysis, including GLU866, HIS717, LEU868, VAL867, ARG594, and MET363. Compound 8 has one of the lowest docking scores (− 128.79) in the series, which means that the compound has a great binding affinity (Table S1 in SI file).

Interaction analysis revealed that compound 8 formed multiple hydrogen bonds with GLU866, LEU865, and HIS717, while additional stability was provided through hydrophobic and π-alkyl contacts involving LEU868, VAL867, ARG594, and HIS584. The interactions were successful in binding the ligand within the binding pocket (Fig. 3). The existence of polar contacts, as well as a lot of hydrophobic packing, is a cause of the high docking performance of this compound.

Interaction of 5NN8 protein with compound 8 (A) structure of protein (B) protein–ligand interaction (C) hydrophobic interaction (D) 2D diagram of ligand.

Comparatively, acarbose adopted a more (unfavourable) MolDock score of − 102.01, and this protein depended largely on an extensive hydrogen-bonding network with such residues as GLU781, THR905, GLN839, GLN902, and PRO779 (Fig. S3 in SI file). Even though acarbose was able to form many hydrogen bonds, its size and highly polarized state restricted hydrophobic complementarity in the pocket, which perhaps explains why it scored relatively low in docking compared with several of the synthesized derivatives. Generally, the docking outcomes of the 5NN8 indicate that the sulfonated aldehydes, especially compound 8, attain a more well-balanced interaction profile based on hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic contacts that results in an improved predicted binding affinity.

Human maltase-glucoamylase (8CB1) docking analysis

An analogous binding pattern was recorded with docking in 8CB1. The compounds synthesized were located in the catalytic groove and reacted with important residues such as GLU196, ARG608, LEU195, PRO194, ALA604, VAL358, and ILE581. Compound 8 was once more among those with the most preferable docking scores (− 100.4), doing better than acarbose (Table S1 in SI file). In compound 8, hydrogen bonds with TYR609, GLY605, and GLU196 were supplemented by a hydrophobic bonding pattern with LEU195, PRO194, LEU574 and LEU577, giving it a stable binding pose (Fig. 4). These interactions indicate that the active site is efficiently occupied and highly complemented to the enzyme cavity. Conversely, acarbose formed hydrogen bonds with ARG585, THR491, GLU196, GLY605, and ARG608 residues with fewer hydrophobic interactions (Fig. S4 in SI file). Despite a good binding pose of acarbose, the docking score was still lower than the score of various synthesized compounds, which once again reflects the benefit of the sulfonated aldehyde scaffold in displaying better protein–ligand complementarity. Interestingly, compounds 5 and 6 also showed competitive docking scores with 8CB1, with several hydrogen bonds and π-alkyl interactions, which is well correlated with their inhibitory ability experimentally measured.

Interaction of 8CB1 protein with compound 8 (A) structure of protein (B) protein–ligand interaction (C) hydrophobic interaction (D) 2D diagram of ligand.

Saccharomyces cerevisiae α-Glucosidase (4J5T) docking analysis

Molecular docking studies were performed to gain insight into the binding patterns of the most active compounds in the active site of α-glucosidase, with consideration for maintaining consistency with the in vitro system. Here, docking the Saccharomyces cerevisiae α-glucosidase structure (PDB ID: 4J5T) was favored for direct comparison with the in vitro assay, while docking of the human α-glucosidase structures was used to gain additional insight into possible translational relevance. As shown in Figs. 5 and S5 in SI file, the active compounds (8, 6, and 5) showed well-defined binding poses within the active site cavity, engaging in stabilizing interactions (hydrogen bonding, π-π interactions and hydrophobic interactions) with the catalytic residues. Compound 8 showed the most favorable interaction pattern, which is consistent with the observed strong inhibitory activity, through multiple stabilizing interactions that contribute to the efficient accommodation of the ligand in the binding site. Likewise, compounds 6 and 5 showed well-established interactions, with the extended aromatic structure of compound 6 enabling π–π interactions, while the halogen atom of compound 5 provided a hydrophobic interaction. Crucially, the interactions observed for the yeast enzyme were similar to those observed for the human α-glucosidase models, suggesting that the binding mode is conserved across species. But these docking models are considered as corroborative evidence for the observed SAR rather than as conclusive evidence for the mechanism of enzyme inhibition. In conclusion, the docking study offers insights into ligand-enzyme interactions and is consistent with the experimental data, which identifies compound 8 as a lead compound.

Interaction of 4J5T protein with compound 8 (A) structure of protein (B) protein–ligand interaction (C) hydrophobic interaction (D) 2D diagram of ligand.

Therefore, the molecular docking studies using both Saccharomyces cerevisiae α-glucosidase (PDB ID: 4J5T) and related structures of human α-glucosidase (PDB IDs: 5NN8 and 8CB1) suggest that the sulfonated aldehyde compounds are likely to be well hosted in the active sites by hydrogen bonding, π-π stacking, and hydrophobic interactions. Of the compounds evaluated, compound 8, as well as compounds 5 and 6, show a stronger binding tendency than acarbose, in line with the in vitro α-glucosidase inhibition assay. The enhanced inhibitory profile of these compounds may be related to increased π-π interactions, interactions involving the halogen, and complementary hydrophobic interactions with the active site. But these data should be considered as consistent with the SAR, rather than as a definitive answer, especially given the complementary use of both yeast and human enzymes.

Validation of docking

The docking validation study indicated that the docking protocol used in Molegro Virtual Docker is able to reproduce the experimentally determined binding pose of the native ligand. In Fig. 6, the overlay of the re-docked ligand on the crystallographic ligand exhibits a high level of spatial congruence in the binding site. The re-docked ligand shows a good overlap with the original ligand pose, suggesting that the docking protocol finds the native pose. The RMSD value is in the acceptable range (usually less than 2.0 Å), indicating the accuracy of the docking parameters. As shown in Fig. 6, the native and docked ligand structures occupy the same binding pocket and show slight differences in orientation. The excellent superposition of the ligand structures confirms that the docking methodology is robust, and the computational method is appropriate to predict binding poses of the designed compounds.

Molecular dynamics simulation

To further examine the time-dependent behaviour and dynamic stability of the docked complexes, 50 ns of a molecular dynamics (MD) simulation was done with the WebGRO server of the lead compound-8-α-glucosidase complex and compared to the reference drug, acarbose. In contrast to conventional static docking, MD simulations provide deeper insights into the conformational stability, flexibility, compactness, and durability of protein–ligand interactions under simulated physiological conditions. The general trajectory plot shows that both systems had reached equilibrium at the beginning of the simulation, but the lead compound had a better stability and interaction persistence during the 50 ns run.

Figures 7A and S6A in SI file represent the backbone RMSD analysis of the lead compound complex and the acarbose complex, respectively. The first phase of the lead compound protein complex was that the initial increase in RMSD occurred between 5 and 8 ns, which was attributed to relaxation of the system, and eventually the RMSD began to be stable and oscillated between an approximate range of 0.25–0.30 nm until the conclusion of the simulation. This consistent RMSD curve shows that the protein did not lose its structure, and the ligand was strongly attached to the active site. Conversely, the acarbose complex had a relatively greater RMSD changes, and especially beyond the duration of about 25 ns, RMSD changes take up values of nearly 0.35 nm indicating a relatively more unstable binding mode and increased conformational mobility of the complex.

MD simulation of a complex of compound 8 with 5NN8 (A) RMSD (root mean square deviation) (B) RMSF (root mean square fluctuation) (C) Radius of gyration (D) Hydrogen bonds (E) SASA (solvent accessible surface area) (F) area per residue over trajectory.

Flexibility of the protein in terms of residue was assessed by RMSF analysis as in Figs. 7B and S6B in SI file. Most of the amino acid residues in the complex of the lead compound showed low variations (< 0.25 nm), particularly the residues in and around the active site which means that the catalytic region was well stabilised with the ligand. On the other hand, there were more fluctuations in the acarbose complex in a number of loop and surface regions indicating a weaker restraint of protein motion. The decrease of the flexibility of active-site residues when the lead compound is present supports more stable and more intense protein–ligand interactions.

Figures 7C and S6C in SI file show the radius of gyration (Rg), which has been determined by the general compactness of the protein structure. The lead compound complex had not changed in terms of Rg during the 50 ns simulation, indicating that binding of the ligand did not elicit any unfolding or relaxation of the protein structure. Comparatively, the acarbose complex showed a little more change in the values of Rg, which indicates a slight structural expansion and a loss of compactness with time. These results also help to affirm the increased structural stability of the protein in the presence of the lead compound.

Figure 7D, as well as Fig. S6D in the SI file, shows that the lead compound formed a steady number of hydrogen bonds with α-glucosidase at the time of the simulation. It was also observed that an average of 3–5 hydrogen bonds were preserved during the course of the trajectory, so there were strong and stable intermolecular interactions which provide much assistance to the binding stability. Even though acarbose formed the greater number of hydrogen bonds initially, these interactions were less permanent and had high frequency fluctuations, which was expected since acarbose has a higher RMSD and RMSF.

Figure 7E demonstrates the results of the solvent accessible surface analysis of the lead compound and Fig. S6E in the SI file presents the results of the solvent accessible surface analysis of acarbose. The complex of SASA values was relatively constant in the lead compound, which means that there was no significant change in the exposure of solvents, and the protein–ligand interface was stable. Conversely, the acarbose complex had greater SASA changes, indicating that it entered the binding pocket partially and resulted in a less compact association of the ligand and the protein. The results obtained are highly supportive of the docking and in vitro inhibition results and confirm the lead compound as a promising α-glucosidase inhibitor with good binding stability in physiological conditions.

SwissADME analysis

SwissADME analysis was conducted to determine whether the synthesized sulfonated aldehydes have good pharmacokinetic and drug-like properties compared to the reference inhibitor acarbose. Predicted parameters (Table S2 in SI file) refer to the fact that all of the synthesized compounds are in a physicochemical space that is typically amenable to oral drug development, whereas acarbose had numerous violations related to its large molecule size and high polarization. The compounds 1–9 had moderate molecular weights (~ 306–371 g/mol), acceptable topological polar surfaces areas (TPSA 124–78 A2), and balanced lipophilicity (consensus LogP 1.7–3.5) and indicated good overall intestinal absorption profile. Acarbose on the other hand exhibited very high TPSA (329 A2), very low lipophilicity, and high-hydrogen bond donors and acceptors, attributes previously defined as poor membrane permeability.

Physicochemical characteristics and oral pharmacokinetics

The rule of five was met by all the synthesized derivatives having zero violations, but acarbose had violations on several criteria, including molecular weight (> 500), excessive hydrogen bonds donors/acceptors and TPSA. Likewise, compounds 1–9 met Ghose, Veber, Egan, and Muegge filters, which validates their use as small-molecule orally therapeutic candidates (Table S2 in SI file). The Fugita has been predicted to have a bioavailability score of 0.55 which showed good probability of oral bioavailability whereas acarbose had a significantly lower score (0.17). Such results suggest that the sulfonated aldehydes may possess more favorable drug-likeness characteristics compared to the reference drug; however, these observations are based on computational predictions and require experimental validation. These findings are also supported by the bioavailability radar plots (Fig. 8). The majority of the synthesized compounds were comfortably enclosed in the optimum pink area spanned by lipophilicity, size, polarity, solubility, saturation, and flexibility, which represents balanced oral drug-like properties. Acarbose, conversely, was spread well beyond the optimum range, primarily because of over polarization and size which are in line with the poor permeability characteristics.

ADME radar of synthesized compounds and reference drug Acarbose.

Absorption, Distribution and BOILED-Egg analysis

It was found that pharmacokinetic predictions indicated a tendency toward higher gastrointestinal (GI) absorption for the synthesized compounds, whereas acarbose was predicted to have lower GI absorption; however, these results should be interpreted cautiously as they are derived from predictive models (Table S2 in SI file). None of the synthesized derivatives were predicted to act as P-glycoprotein (P-gp) substrates, suggesting a lower likelihood of intestinal absorption loss due to efflux. In contrast, acarbose was identified as a P-gp substrate. Predictions of BBB permeability revealed that central nervous system exposure of most of the series is limited as most compounds are non-BBB permeant with the exception of compound 7 a practical characteristic of peripheral antidiabetic agents. These trends were also validated by the BOILED-Egg model (Fig. 9) with compounds 1–9 showing the highest concentration in the white portion, which is associated with the high likelihood of passive GI absorption, but not in the yellow yolk, which relates to very low brain penetration. Acarbose was positioned outside the optimal region in the model, suggesting comparatively less favorable predicted intestinal permeability; however, such interpretations are model-dependent and should be validated experimentally.

BOILED-egg of the ADME study of synthesized compounds and reference drugs.

Medicinal chemistry and metabolic liability

The inhibition profile of cytochrome P450 indicated that a variety of compounds synthesized could inhibit CYP1A2, CYP2C19, CYP2C9 or CYP3A4, and therefore, it can be anticipated that there are metabolic liabilities that need to be taken into account and may be addressed in further optimization. Remarkably, all the compounds were not expected to inhibit CYP2D6.

The syntheses of all the derivatives produced completely zero PAINS alerts, which means that there is minimal risk of interference with assays. Alerts were mainly related to the aldehyde and sulfonate groups, which are inherent to the scaffold being designed. The synthetic accessibility scores (~ 2.83–3.2) indicated that these compounds are relatively simple to prepare and acarbose showed a significantly higher complexity score (7.25).

Together with the molecular docking and in vitro inhibition data, these ADME data indicate compounds 5, 6, and especially compound 8 as promising lead molecules, a mixture with good target binding, good biological activity, and good predicted pharmacokinetics. These compounds thus deserve optimization and experimental pharmacokinetic testing as an α-glucosidase inhibitor.

ProTox-III toxicity profiling

In silico toxicity prediction was conducted to determine the initial safety of the most promising identities of compounds 5, 6 and 8 found during the docking, ADME and biological screening and acarbose was used as a control. A summary of the findings of ProTox-II is shown in Table S3 in SI file. In general, the synthesized lead compounds showed a comparatively more favorable predicted toxicity profile than acarbose across several endpoints; however, these findings are based solely on in silico predictions and should be interpreted with caution at the majority of endpoints (Table S3 in SI file). The three derivatives (5, 6 and 8) were all found to be inactive in hepatotoxicity, neurotoxicity, nephrotoxicity, and respiratory toxicity, suggesting a lower predicted likelihood of major organ toxicity; however, this does not replace the need for experimental toxicological evaluation. Conversely, it was anticipated that acarbose was predicted to show activity in several toxicity endpoints; however, such predictions may not fully reflect its established clinical safety profile and should be interpreted cautiously.

The major predicted risks of both the synthesized compounds and acarbose were cardiotoxicity and immunotoxicity. Compounds 5, 6 and 8 were designated as cardiotoxic and immunotoxic, with an intermediate probability score, which is why these endpoints might need additional focus in the further optimization and experimental validation. The same trend was recorded with acarbose that had also predicted cardiotoxic and immunotoxic potential (Table S3 in SI file). This suggests that as much as the new derivatives have enhanced on some safety concerns there are still some immune and heart related effects that are common in this chemical space.

Compound 6 was tested as one of the results and this endpoint was predicted to be the one causing mutagenicity, as this compound is considered active, whereas Compounds 5 and 8 were predicted inactive. This difference indicates that compound 6 has structural features that are peculiar to the compound, perhaps the extended aromatic system, and thus it could be the contribution to DNA-reactive warnings that should be taken into account in subsequent structural refinement. So, all of the compounds, including the compound 6, were not anticipated to be cytotoxic, which confirmed their overall compatibility with the cells.

The compounds 5, 6 and 8 were also predicted to be active in regard to blood–brain barrier (BBB) penetration, which means that the Compounds might have CNS exposure. Although it is not necessarily harmful, peripheral selectivity of BBB is desirable in, relation to antidiabetic agents, to avoid central side effects. Acarbose on the contrary was BBB-inactive as expected, given that the molecule is highly polar and has low membrane permeability.

Comparatively, the reference drug acarbose showed a wider range in the toxicity liability since it was predicted to be active with hepatotoxicity, nephrotoxicity, respiratory toxicity, cardiotoxicity, immunotoxicity, clinical toxicity and nutritional toxicity (Table S3 in SI file). These results are consistent with its well-established poor pharmacokinetic properties and gastrointestinal adverse side-effect and may indicate the potential of the sulfonated aldehyde scaffold to achieve a balanced safety–efficacy profile, pending further experimental confirmation.

Combined, ProTox-II projections suggest that compounds 5, 6, and particularly compound 8 may exhibit improved predicted toxicity profiles relative to acarbose; however, definitive conclusions cannot be drawn without experimental validation, fewer organ-specific hazards, and fewer systemic toxicity warnings. Compound 8 in particular is especially promising as it offers a combination of a strong docking performance, desirable ADME properties, potent in vitro performance and a relatively clean toxicity profile, devoid of mutagenicity and major organ toxicities.

To sum up, ProTox-III (Table S3 in SI file) analysis confirms the development of compounds 5, 6, and 8 as potential lead candidates with compound 8 being the most balanced compound based on predicted efficacy and safety. These findings in computational toxicity should be added to the results of docking and pharmacokinetic and serve as a solid basis to proceed with the further work on these compounds, particularly compound 8, and to conduct the experiments with toxicology and the further preclinical development.

DFT analysis

The DFT analysis was also specifically interested in the lead compounds and the reference drug, which made it possible to make a meaningful comparison of their electronic descriptors. The presented parameters are calculated geometries, frontier molecular orbitals (FMOs), global reactivity parameters, and molecular electrostatic potential (MEP) surfaces that may provide a general picture of structure reactivity correlations.

Optimized molecular structures

The optimized structures of the lead compounds and the reference drug were determined at the B3LYP/6-311G level of theory and all structures converged the imaginary frequencies and proved to be real minima on the potential energy surface. Optimized molecular structures are provided in Fig. 10.

Optimized structures of lead compounds and reference drug.

The results of the inspection of the optimized geometries reveal that the sulfonated aldehyde scaffold takes a stable and energetically convenient position. The aldehyde functional group is virtually perpendicular to the aromatic ring, and thus the structure is prone to 0-conjugation, whereas the sulfonate functional group is oriented toward the exterior making it more accessible to intermolecular interactions. Such space orientation is especially significant to enzyme binding, as it allows the concomitant hydrogen bonding as well as electrostatic interaction in the active site of the enzyme, α-glucosidase.

Comparatively, the acarbose drug of interest has multiple hydroxyl groups, which is highly flexible with a bulky structure thus giving it a less compact geometry. This over-flexibility, although permitting hydrogen bonding, can decrease complementarity in shape of the best fit in the binding pocket, and this is in agreement with the relatively lower docking scores and lower predicted binding stability.

Frontier molecular orbital (FMO) analysis

The electronic behaviour of the compounds, their capability to transfer charge, and the chemical reactivity of the compounds were evaluated through the frontier molecular orbital analysis. Figure 11 depicts the spatial distribution of the HOMO and LUMO orbitals, and Table S4 in SI file sums up the corresponding energy values.

Comparative energy gap diagram of lead compounds and reference drug.

The HOMO is a measure of the electron-giving capacity of a molecule, and the LUMO is a measure of its electron-accepting capability. In the case of the synthesized sulfonated aldehydes, the HOMO density is highly concentrated on the aromatic ring and aldehyde functionality. This localization implies these regions are easily able to release electron density in the process of interacting with electrophilic or positively charged residues located in the enzyme active site.

The LUMO, on the other hand, has a predominant distribution across the sulfonate group and substituted aromatic ring, which evidences the ability of the regions to serve as preferred electron-accepting centres. This interpolation of HOMO and LUMO between functional groups facilitates intramolecular charge transfer and increases the electronically adaptable capacity of the molecule following functionality based on the binding to a target enzyme.

Especially, the lead compounds have much lower HOMO LUMO energy gaps than the reference drug. A smaller energy difference would mean a greater chemical reactivity and reduced kinetic stability, better qualities of an enzyme inhibitor because it can react effectively to the electronic environment of the binding pocket. The latter is strongly correlated with the increased docking scores and increased biological activity of the lead compounds due to this reduced energy gap.

Global reactivity parameters

The global reactivity descriptors were determined to measure the chemical reactivity, electronic stability and ability to interact with other molecules of the synthesized sulfonated aldehydes quantitatively and to explain the molecular level affinity of the binding and the inhibitory behaviour. All the parameters calculated based on HOMO and LUMO energies are presented in the Table S4 in SI file.

The ionization potential (I) values of the lead compounds are smaller than that of the reference drug, which is an increase in the capacity to give out electron density to intermolecular interactions. On the other hand, the electron affinity (A) values are also much greater, and they indicate a high propensity of such molecules to take up electrons of nucleophilic residues in the active site of α-glucosidase.

The hardness (η) and softness (S) of a substance give an idea of the adaptability of the molecules. The lead compounds are softer and have lower hardness and a higher value of softness, which is an indication that they are more polarizable, and the resistance to electronic deformation is less. This increased softness enables the molecules to change in a well-ordered manner to meet the electrostatic landscape of the binding pocket, and this facilitates greater and more versatile noncovalent interactions to the catalytic residues. Conversely, the values of nucleophilicity index (N) are smaller, meaning that the reactivity of these compounds is controlled by electrophilic rather than nucleophilic kinetics.

Molecular electrostatic potential (MEP) analysis

Figure 12 represents the molecular electrostatic potential surfaces of the lead compounds and the reference drug. MEP analysis gives a graphical account of the charge distribution and the areas that are vulnerable to electrophilic or nucleophilic attack. The regions on the MEP maps that are painted in red are with high negative electrostatic potential and those with blue color are regions with positive electrostatic potential. In the case of the synthesized sulfonated aldehydes, strong negative potential regions are evidently seen around oxygen atoms of sulfonate and aldehyde groups. These locations are optimal in accepting hydrogen bond and electrostatic interaction with positively charged or hydrogen giving residues in the enzyme active site. When the hydrogen atoms of the aromatic framework are involved in stabilizing the interactions, positive potential regions are predominantly found around them. The high polarization of the lead compounds resulted in the strong binding orientations of the compounds in the docking. Conversely, electrostatic potential of the reference drug acarbose is more diffused because of the high polar and bulky structure. Although this does permit many hydrogens bonds the focused electrostatic complementarity is minimized, which could explain its relatively poor binding capacity.

Molecular electrostatic potential (MEP) structures of lead compounds and reference drug.

To conclude, it is evident that DFT study shows excellence of the lead sulfonated aldehyde derivatives in comparison to that of the reference drug on the electronic level. The data support the rational design strategy and prove that the enhanced α-glucosidase activity inhibitory effect is the result of the optimization of the pharmacological characteristics that are conducive to the formation of strong and stable interactions with the intended enzyme.

Limitations of the study

This study has few limitations that should be considered. The in vivo analysis was performed on a single animal model that might not accurately reflect the complexity of human diabetes. The size and time of treatment was insufficient to evaluate the long-term effectiveness and safety. Despite the insights that molecular docking and MD simulations offer in the context of binding interactions, these computational methods are predictive and have to be validated by experimental means, either by crystallographic or biochemical studies. Additionally, the ADME and toxicity profiles were based on in silico predictions rather than experimental pharmacokinetic or toxicological data. The synthesized series was also relatively limited in terms of structural diversity, which can be a limitation to general SAR inferences. This should be supported by further studies using larger compound libraries, mechanistic studies, and extensive preclinical testing to support and expand these results.

Conclusions

A series of vanillin-based sulfonated aldehyde derivatives (1–9) was synthesized using a simple and efficient sulfonation protocol and fully characterized by 1H/13C-NMR and mass spectrometry. Enzymatic assays demonstrated that several derivatives exhibit potent α-glucosidase inhibitory activity, with multiple compounds surpassing the reference inhibitor acarbose. Compounds 8, 6, and 5 were identified as the most active members of the series. In vivo evaluation in streptozotocin-induced diabetic rats showed that the most active compounds (8, 6 and 5) produced significant and dose-dependent antihyperglycemic effects, improved body weight trends, and beneficial modulation of lipid profiles, including reductions in triglycerides, total cholesterol, and LDL, along with increased HDL levels. Docking studies against human α-glucosidase targets (5NN8, 8CB1 and 4J5T) revealed favorable binding energies and stable interaction patterns, particularly for compound 8, supported by hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic contacts within the catalytic pocket. MD simulations confirmed the stability and persistence of the lead compound-protein complex over time. DFT analysis further indicated favorable electronic characteristics of the lead derivatives, including reduced HOMO–LUMO gaps, higher electrophilicity, and suitable charge distribution, which collectively support strong enzyme-ligand interactions. Henceforth, these results establish vanillin-derived sulfonated aldehydes as a promising class of α-glucosidase inhibitors. Compound 8, in particular, represents a strong lead candidate for further optimization and preclinical development as a potential antidiabetic agent. The findings of this study should be interpreted with caution due to limitations including reliance on a single animal model, restricted sample size and treatment duration, dependence on predictive computational and in silico ADME/toxicity analyses, and limited structural diversity of the compounds, thereby necessitating further validation through expanded experimental and preclinical investigations.
