Interpretable machine learning model based on multimodal ultrasound for bedside diagnosis of acute exacerbations in COPD
Authors: Zhe Sun, Huilin Li, Yujin Zheng, Xinying Jia, Jiaojiao Ma, Hui Liu, Xuejiao Yu, Liangkai Wang, Yang Li, Bo Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAcute exacerbations of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (AECOPD) are associated with accelerated lung function decline and increased mortality. However, early and accurate diagnosis remains clinically challenging due to nonspecific symptoms and limitations of existing diagnostic tools. This study aimed to develop an interpretable machine learning (ML) model integrating multimodal ultrasound indicators to facilitate real-time bedside diagnosis of AECOPD.MethodsIn this prospective, single-center study, 316 patients with COPD underwent standardized lung, diaphragmatic, and quadriceps ultrasound examinations upon hospital admission. Four ML algorithms were developed using a 7:3 training-to-test data split. Model performance was assessed by area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), and interpretability was enhanced using SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP).ResultsThe support vector machine (SVM) model achieved the best diagnostic performance, with an AUC of 0.9321 in the training set and 0.9302 in the test set. The final model incorporated six routinely obtainable variables, five of which were ultrasound derived. SHAP analysis identified elevated lung ultrasound scores, diaphragmatic dysfunction, and quadriceps atrophy as the most influential predictors.ConclusionsThis non-invasive and interpretable ML model, based on bedside ultrasound features, offers a clinically feasible tool for real-time AECOPD diagnosis. Further multicenter validation is warranted to confirm generalizability and explore integration with additional biomarkers or imaging modalities.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12931-025-03411-6.

Introduction

Acute exacerbations of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (AECOPD) are pivotal events in disease progression, contributing disproportionately to morbidity, mortality, and healthcare burden [1, 2]. Epidemiological data show that 22%–40% of individuals with COPD experience at least one moderate or severe exacerbation annually, with 9%–16% experiencing recurrent episodes [3, 4]. These events accelerate lung function decline and worsen long-term outcomes [5].

Early identification of AECOPD remains challenging due to symptom variability and the lack of reliable early biomarkers. Current diagnostic strategies rely on clinical symptoms or delayed laboratory markers, such as elevated C-reactive protein, which are insufficiently sensitive to early pathophysiological changes [1, 2]. Recently, machine learning (ML)-based models have emerged for AECOPD prediction using data from environmental monitoring, wearable devices, and longitudinal clinical records [6–8]. While promising, these models depend on continuous data streams and complex infrastructures, limiting clinical applicability.

Ultrasound imaging is a non-invasive, real-time modality for bedside assessment in COPD, allowing evaluation of lung aeration, diaphragmatic motion, and peripheral muscle status [9–12]. In particular, lung and diaphragm ultrasound show potential for detecting exacerbations [13, 14]. However, no validated ML model has yet integrated multimodal ultrasound indicators for AECOPD diagnosis.

In this study, we developed and validated an interpretable ML model incorporating bedside ultrasound features of the diaphragm, lung, and quadriceps muscle to identify AECOPD. These features are objective, rapidly obtainable, and non-invasive. To enhance clinical transparency, we used SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) to visualize the contribution of each feature to model predictions.

Methods

This study followed the Transparent Reporting of a Multivariable Prediction Model for Individual Prognosis or Diagnosis Use Regression or Machine Learning Methods guidelines [15]. This study has obtained ethics approval from the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of the China–Japan Friendship Hospital [approval No. 2022-KY-186], and all participants signed an informed consent form.

Study cohort

We prospectively enrolled 325 participants at a tertiary medical center between April 7, 2023, and November 5, 2024. Inclusion criteria were: (1) aged 40–80 years; (2) COPD diagnosis based on the 2023 Global Initiative for Obstructive Lung Disease guideline [1]; (3) no other significant life-threatening comorbidities. Exclusion criteria were: (1) coexisting pulmonary diseases (n = 2); (2) external fixation or open wounds (n = 1); (3) poor image quality (n = 6). Ultimately, 316 patients were included and randomly assigned to a training set (n = 224) and a test set (n = 92) in a 7:3 ratio.

Clinical features and data processing

Clinical data were extracted from the patients’ hospitalization electronic medical records, and questionnaires, including demographics, smoking status, family history, COPD duration, GOLD stage, comorbidity, oxygen therapy, and COPD assessment test (CAT) questionnaire.

Definition of AECOPD

AECOPD was defined as an acute worsening of respiratory symptoms requiring additional therapy, in accordance with guideline recommendations [16]. The reference standard required clinician-documented worsening of dyspnea and/or cough and sputum, with concomitant treatment escalation defined as oral antibiotic therapy, systemic corticosteroids, or hospitalization.

Ultrasound measures

All ultrasound examinations were conducted at admission by a trained operator blinded to clinical data and group assignment. A Philips EPIQ Elite or CX50 system equipped with linear (18–4 MHz or 12–5 MHz) and convex (5–1 MHz) transducers was used. The operator had completed a nationally recognized point-of-care ultrasound training program that included supervised scanning and competency assessment covering lung, diaphragmatic and quadriceps protocols. A standardized operating procedure specified transducer selection, presets, patient positioning, imaging planes and calliper placement. For each quantitative metric, three measurements were obtained, and the average was used for analysis. Adherence to the procedure was monitored with a checklist and periodic quality-control audits.

Lung ultrasound (LUS)

LUS followed a 12-zone protocol (six per hemithorax), dividing anterior, lateral, and posterior regions into upper and lower segments. Artifacts such as A-lines and B-lines were assessed, along with consolidations and pleural effusion (Figs. 1A–B). Findings were scored as follows: score 0: normal A-lines; score 1: ≥3 B-lines with good spacing; score 2: coalescent B-lines; score 3: lung consolidation; score 4: pleural effusion. Total score ranged from 0 to 48.

Pleural ultrasound (PUS)

PUS used the same regions as LUS. Pleural line morphology (Figs. 1C–D) was scored as: score 0: smooth, clear, and sharp; score 1: fuzzy, regular, and continuous; score 2: rough, irregular, with continuous integrity but occasional focal interruptions; score 3: clearly irregular, diffusely interrupted, with a granular or nodular appearance. Total score ranged from 0 to 36.

Diaphragmatic ultrasound

Patients were positioned at 45°. Each parameter was measured three times and averaged.

Diaphragm thickness

A linear probe was placed at the right zone of apposition (8th–10th intercostal spaces, anterior to midaxillary line). The diaphragm was identified between the pleura and peritoneum (Fig. 1E). Thickness was measured at end-expiration (DTex), end-inspiration (DTin), and maximal inspiration (DTmax). The definition of diaphragm thickness fraction (DTF) is shown in the following formula.

Diaphragm excursion

A convex probe was placed in the subcostal region (midclavicular to anterior axillary line), identifying the diaphragm near the liver (Fig. 1F). Excursion, measured in M-mode during respiration, was recorded as DE and DEmax.

Data preprocessing

Outliers in continuous variables were identified through violin plots. An outlier is defined as a value exceeding 1.5 times the interquartile range (IQR) above the upper quartile (Q3) or below the lower quartile (Q1). Outliers were adjusted to the nearest boundary of these defined limits to align more closely with the central data distribution (e-Figure 1). The presence of missing data in both the training and test datasets is illustrated in e-Figure 2. To address missing data, multiple imputation was conducted using the ‘mice’ package. These processes were performed separately for training and test sets to avoid data leakage. Each Variable’s missingness is summarized in e-Table 1, and a predetermined exclusion criterion of 30% missingness was used.

Variable selection

Variable selection was performed exclusively on the training data to avoid information leakage. A three-stage procedure was used. First, univariate analysis was undertaken using the chi-squared or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables and the t test or Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables. Second, recursive feature elimination (RFE) [17, 18] was performed with 10-fold cross-validation. Third, collinearity was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF), and redundant variables (VIF >5) were removed based on clinical relevance.

Model selection

Four machine learning models were predefined to balance accuracy, sample efficiency and interpretability: logistic regression (LR), support vector machine (SVM), random forest (RF) and K-nearest neighbor (KNN). LR was used as a transparent linear benchmark. SVM was selected for margin-based nonlinear discrimination with few hyperparameters. RF was preferred to capture nonlinearities and interactions, and a single decision tree was not adopted because of instability and a tendency to overfit. KNN was included as a nonparametric benchmark that makes few assumptions about the data distribution. Deep neural networks were not prioritized because the dataset size and deployment constraints were expected to increase overfitting risk, add computational burden, and reduce transparency.

Model development and validation

The optimization of prediction models was carried out by refining the selection of hyperparameters. The final hyperparameters for each model were identified using the optimal feature subset, determined through five rounds of 10-fold cross-validation combined with a default hyperparameter grid search using the “caret” package (e-Table 2). For the RF model, overfitting was mitigated by evaluating a regularizing grid that varied mtry, minimum node size and split rule, and by assessing feature-reduced subsets from the RFE stage. The models were subsequently refitted on the training set, incorporating the optimized feature subset and hyperparameters derived from five rounds of 10-fold internal cross-validation. This methodological approach was aimed at enhancing the predictive robustness and accuracy of the models.

Model performance comparison

Model performance was evaluated using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) [19], sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), accuracy, F1 score, and Brier score. The log-loss metric was applied to quantify the discrepancies between the actual outcomes and the predicted probabilities. Calibration curves were utilized to depict the congruence between the predicted probabilities and the observed outcomes. DeLong test was used to compare AUCs [20]. The best-performing model was selected based on overall performance on both datasets.

Model explanation

Interpreting machine learning models is inherently complex. The SHAP method, grounded in game theory, systematically quantifies the importance of input features in predictive models, addressing the opacity often associated with these models. By calculating the contribution of each feature to the prediction outcomes, SHAP provides both local and global interpretations, thereby enhancing the transparency and interpretability of the models [21]. In this study, SHAP analyses were conducted on the training set.

Statistics

The machine learning models were implemented using R software, version 4.4.2, and the “caret” package (version 6.0.94). Model construction was facilitated through the train function using specific method parameters for logistic regression (method = “glmnet”), random forest (method = “ranger”), K-nearest neighbors (method = “knn”), and support vector machines (method = “svmRadial”). Model discrimination was assessed by AUC along with a bias-corrected 95% confidence interval (CI) derived from 1000-fold bootstrap resampling. Model calibration was evaluated using the Brier score, which ranges from 0 to 1, with values approaching 0 indicating superior calibration. The effectiveness of the prediction models was further analyzed by comparing their AUC values using the DeLong’s test. Statistical presentation of data involved displaying continuous variables with a normal distribution as mean ± standard deviation, assessed using the t-test. Variables with skewed distributions were shown as medians with interquartile ranges and compared using the Mann-Whitney U test or Kruskal-Wallis H test. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies with percentages and analyzed with the chi-square test. A two-tailed P value of less than 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

Results

Baseline clinical information

Among 316 patients enrolled, 224 were allocated to the training set and 92 to the test set. The demographic and ultrasound characteristics of both cohorts are detailed in Table 1. Of 316 patients overall, 168 (53%) were AECOPD and 148 (47%) were stable. The training set comprised 224 individuals, of whom 105 (47%) were stable and 119 (53%) were AECOPD. The test set included 92 individuals, with 43 (47%) stable and 49 (53%) AECOPD. No significant difference was observed (P = 0.98). Baseline parameters were generally well-balanced between the two groups, with no statistically significant differences in most variables (P > 0.05). These results support the suitability of the datasets for subsequent modeling. Details of the study design are displayed in Fig. 2.

Selection of predictor variables

In univariate analysis, significant differences between stable and exacerbation groups were observed for multiple demographic and ultrasound variables, particularly CAT score, DEmax, ΔDE, CSARF, MTVL, and LUS score (all P < 0.001; Table 2). Smoking status, BMI, and DTFmax, despite marginal no significance (P = 0.06, 0.07, and 0.19, respectively), were included in RFE based on previous literature and expert experience, ensuring a comprehensive evaluation of candidate predictors.

Model development and performance comparison

Five rounds of 10-fold internal cross-validation were used to construct four ML models.

In the training set, RF achieved the highest AUC (0.9644, 95% CI: 0.9340–0.9799), followed by LR (0.9374, 95% CI: 0.8925–0.9650), SVM (0.9321, 95% CI: 0.8824–0.9599), and KNN (0.9305, 95% CI: 0.8880–0.9577) (Table 3; Fig. 4A). RF also showed the highest specificity (0.9244, 95% CI: 0.8633–0.9655) and lowest Brier score (0.096, 95% CI: 0.082–0.112), indicating strong fit (Fig. 4C). However, its lower log-loss (0.3380) compared to other models suggested potential overfitting. (e-Table 4).

Model explanation

The final SVM model comprised six variables: CAT score, LUS score, ΔDE, MTVL, CSARF, QCI (e-Table 3). Model explanations were generated using SHAP.

Global explanation

The global explanation of the model, as shown in the SHAP summary bar plot (Fig. 5A), evaluates the contribution of each feature to the model’s predictions by calculating the mean SHAP values. Three most important features identified in SVM model were CAT score, CSARF, LUS score. Additionally, the SHAP summary dot plot (Fig. 5B) visually depicts the influence of each feature on the model’s output, illustrating both the direction and strength of their impact. Features such as higher CAT scores, smaller CSARF, and higher LUS scores significantly increased the risk of AECOPD. Furthermore, the SHAP dependence plot provides valuable insights into how individual features influence the output of the prediction model. As illustrated in Fig. 5D, feature thresholds associated with positive predictions: CAT score > 26, LUS score > 14, CSARF < 3.96 cm², ΔDE < 1.64 cm, QCI < 0.69, and MTVL < 1.47 cm. These cutoffs shifted the SHAP values above zero, classifying patients as exacerbation cases.

Local explanation

Local interpretation using the SHAP waterfall plot (Fig. 5C) demonstrates the model’s prediction process for the 100th patient. Key contributors included CSARF (2.07), LUS score [16], and CAT score [22], which made substantial positive contributions of + 0.09, + 0.06, and + 0.04, respectively, while a DE of 3.14 had a negative impact on the prediction. Other features, such as MTVL and QCI, also had varying impacts. The cumulative SHAP values visually explain how individual feature contributions form the final prediction, improving transparency and clinical trust.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first study to develop and validate a ML model using multimodal ultrasound indicators to predict AECOPD. Unlike previous ML models for COPD management [6–8], our approach uniquely integrates real-time, non-invasive, and bedside-acquirable ultrasound indicators to predict AECOPD. These objective ultrasound biomarkers overcome the limitations of symptom-based assessments and enable timely, data-driven intervention. This integration offers a cost-effective and accessible tool for clinical decision-making.

Using a prospective design aligned with ML reporting guidelines, we combined ultrasound indicators with clinical data in an interpretable framework. Among the four algorithms tested, SVM emerged as the most robust, demonstrating balanced sensitivity and specificity in both training (AUC = 0.9321) and test sets (AUC = 0.9302) (Figs. 4A-B). SVM’s kernel-based architecture captures nonlinear patterns while minimizing overfitting, making it well-suited for moderate-sized datasets. It also demonstrated superior calibration (Figs. 4C-D) and log-loss stability (e-Table 4) compared to RF and KNN, supporting its generalizability and growing adoption in medical imaging applications, as evidenced by several recent studies [22, 23]. The final SVM model retained six interpretable features (e-Table 3), which enables rapid bedside application without requiring complex equipment, particularly valuable in resource-limited settings.

There is no standardized guideline for selecting features in prediction models, and the optimal number of features to include remains unclear. In this study, selection was undertaken in three stages (univariate analysis, RFE, and collinearity assessment) and was confined to the training data to avoid information leakage. This ensured that retained variables were both statistically relevant and clinically interpretable. While including more features may enhance performance, it also increases complexity and risks overfitting. Our streamlined model balances these trade-offs, preserving diagnostic performance while improving clinical utility.

Among the ultrasound predictors, CSARF, LUS score, ΔDE, QCI, and MTVL were key contributors to AECOPD (e-Table 3). LUS offers a real-time, bedside assessment of pulmonary ventilation., which is closely linked to exacerbations [10, 24, 25]. The LUS score can quantify pulmonary involvement and correlate with disease severity [26–28]. In our study, the LUS score was significantly higher in the exacerbation group compared to the stable group (Table 2). The diaphragm is most important muscle of respiration [29]. A previous study in patients with AECOPD reported that DTF increased significantly from baseline to follow-up, and changes in DE were correlated with time to the next exacerbation and recovery, highlighting the prognostic value of diaphragmatic function [30]. Another study showed that DTFmax and DEmax were comparable to traditional lung function tests in detecting disease deterioration [13]. In our study, we also found that DE, DEmax, and ΔDE were significantly lower in AECOPD, with ΔDE contributing most strongly to model prediction (Table 2). Additionally, quadriceps ultrasound evaluates systemic muscle status, which is often impaired in patients with COPD [31, 32]. Indicators such as CSARF and QCI reflect muscle mass and strength and have been linked to disease severity and exacerbation frequency [33]. Our findings confirmed that both were significantly lower in AECOPD (Table 2), reinforcing the role of peripheral muscle dysfunction in COPD instability.

The robustness and clinical applicability of our SVM model were confirmed through multiple validation strategies. First, internal five rounds of 10-fold cross-validation yielded stable performance (mean AUC = 0.932 ± 0.021), while test set evaluation showed comparable AUC (0.9302), with balanced sensitivity and specificity, indicating strong generalizability (Figs. 4A-B). Calibration analysis revealed minimal deviation between predicted and observed probabilities (Brier scores: 0.098 vs. 0.114) (Figs. 4C-D), and consistent log-loss values further supported model reliability (e-Table 4). In contrast, RF exhibited near-perfect performance in the training set but declined in the test set, suggesting overfitting, and this persisted despite cross-validated tuning, regularizing constraints (varying mtry, minimum node size and split rule) and the assessment of feature-reduced subsets. This was mitigated in SVM due to its margin-maximization architecture. Although DeLong’s test showed no significant AUC difference between SVM and RF in test set (P = 0.059) (e-Table 5), SVM demonstrated greater overall stability.

ML models are often criticized for their “black box” nature, which can hinder clinical adoption due to lack of transparency [34]. To address this, we applied the SHAP method to enhance model interpretability. SHAP provides both global and local explanations, quantifying how each feature contributes to individual predictions and offering clinicians a clearer understanding of the decision-making process. This transparency facilitates clinical trust and integration into practice. Among the models assessed, SVM demonstrated the best generalizability across datasets. Importantly, the model relies on routine ultrasound indicators that are easily obtainable during hospitalization, ensuring practicality in diverse clinical environments. Within this framework, concordance with respiratory physiology was evident in the SHAP-based explanations. Specifically, higher LUS score and lower CSARF, ΔDE, QCI and MTVL were associated with a higher predicted probability of AECOPD. This pattern is mechanistically plausible because a higher LUS score reflects loss of aeration during exacerbation, reduced diaphragmatic excursion reflects diminished contractile reserve under dynamic hyperinflation, and reductions in CSARF, QCI and MTVL reflect peripheral muscle wasting and weakness that constrain ventilatory reserve. Thresholds suggested by the dependence plots were derived from the training set and should be interpreted as exploratory rather than clinically validated cut-offs (Fig. 5D). Prospective external validation would be required before any clinical application. Taken together, the combination of strong performance and explainability provides a basis for prospective evaluation as a bedside decision-support tool.

Our study has several limitations. First, it was conducted at a single center and no external validation was performed. All participants were recruited from one tertiary hospital in China, and model development and assessment relied on internal resampling with evaluation on an internal test set from the same hospital. Generalizability, calibration and threshold stability across other hospitals, ultrasound systems, acquisition protocols and patient populations therefore remain uncertain. Prospective multicenter external validation, with recalibration or model updating, is required to establish transportability. Second, although examinations were performed by a nationally trained operator under a standardized procedure with triplicate measurements and quality-control audits, ultrasound remains operator dependent. Intra- and inter-operator reproducibility were not formally evaluated in this single-operator study, and future work will include multi-operator acquisitions with standardized training and reliability assessment. Third, SHAP can be computationally expensive and may limit routine generation of explanations in real-time or low-resource settings. Computational resource requirements were not formally quantified in this study, and future work should include rigorous benchmarking of runtime and memory use across representative hardware configurations to guide bedside deployment. Moreover, while our model focused on ultrasound and clinical data, integrating inflammatory markers or imaging modalities such as CT may further improve predictive accuracy. Lastly, the reliance on hospitalization data limits the model’s utility for long-term exacerbation risk monitoring, with dynamic outpatient tracking remaining unexplored.

Conclusions

This study presents an interpretable ML model incorporating multimodal ultrasound features to predict AECOPD. The SVM algorithm demonstrated consistent performance in both internal cross-validation and test set, with SHAP analysis highlighting diaphragmatic dysfunction and quadriceps atrophy as key contributors. The model’s reliance on six routinely available clinical and ultrasound parameters enhances its feasibility for bedside use. However, given the single-center design, external validation across diverse populations is warranted. Future research should focus on: (1) incorporating inflammatory biomarkers or complementary imaging to improve predictive accuracy; (2) assessing the model’s clinical utility through muti-center randomized trials; (3) standardizing ultrasound protocols to reduce operator dependence and enhance scalability.
