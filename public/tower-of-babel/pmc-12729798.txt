Atypical lipomatous tumor/well-differentiated liposarcoma in the subcostal space of the chest wall: a case report and review of the literature
Authors: Yeung-Leung Cheng, Hsuan-Lin Chen, Der-En Keong, Chung-Tai Yue, Min-Shiau Hsieh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAtypical lipomatous tumor (ALT)/well-differentiated liposarcoma (WDL) is a rare low-grade malignant tumor primarily composed of adipose tissue. Although lipomatous tumors commonly develop in the subcutaneous tissues, those originating from the chest wall are infrequently reported. Accurate diagnosis and appropriate treatment are essential, given the tumor’s potential for local recurrence despite its low metastatic risk.Case presentationWe report the case of a 45-year-old man with a firm, enlarging, and painless mass in the left anterior lower chest wall. Imaging revealed a well-defined, fat-attenuated mass (4.8 × 5.0 × 5.2 cm) located in the left precardial subcostal space. Incisional biopsy suggested a lipomatous neoplasm. Thoracoscopic-assisted total removal excision was performed. Histopathological analysis showed mature adipose tissue with stromal cell atypia and muscle infiltration. Immunohistochemistry was negative for MDM2 and CDK4. Due to close microscopic margins and the tumor’s deep location, adjuvant radiation therapy was administered. The final diagnosis was ALT. No recurrence was noted at the about 2-year follow-up, and the patient reported excellent postoperative quality of life.ConclusionALT of the chest wall is rare, and its management requires accurate diagnosis, complete resection, and consideration of adjuvant therapy when margins are close. This case contributes to the limited literature on subcostal ALT and emphasizes a multidisciplinary approach.Keywords: Atypical lipomatous tumor, Well-differentiated liposarcoma, Intercostal space, Chest wall

Background

Lipomatous tumors encompass a spectrum from benign lipomas to malignant liposarcomas. Atypical lipomatous tumor (ALT), synonymous with well-differentiated liposarcoma (WDL), represents a low-grade adipocytic malignancy that rarely metastasizes but may recur locally [1]. While ALTs are more commonly seen in extremities or retroperitoneum, chest wall involvement, especially in the subcostal region, is unusual. Due to anatomical constraints, complete resection may be challenging.

This case report describes a rare presentation of an ALT in the chest wall’s subcostal space, managed through minimally invasive surgery and adjuvant radiation therapy (RT). We also review the literature to contextualize this case and discuss diagnostic and therapeutic considerations.

Case presentation

A 45-year-old man presented with a painless mass in the left lower anterior chest wall, first noticed two months earlier, with more rapid growth reported in the last two weeks. He had no significant underlying medical conditions. Physical examination revealed a firm, fixed 2 cm subcutaneous mass. Laboratory results were normal. Contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) scan revealed a well-defined, fat-attenuated mass measuring 4.8 × 5.0 × 5.2 cm in the left anterior precardial space (Fig. 1A, B). The lobulated mass extended to the anterior chest wall, causing a mass effect on the overlying musculature and exerting pressure on the right ventricular apex. An incisional biopsy was performed under local anesthesia to obtain adequate tissue volume for diagnosis, given superficial accessibility and concern for histologic heterogeneity. Pathology suggested a benign lipomatous neoplasm. The patient underwent thoracoscopic-assisted total removal of the tumor via a 6th intercostal space incision, with thoracoscopic and utility ports at the 7th and 5th intercostal spaces (ICSs), respectively. The tumor was adherent to the periosteum of the 6th rib, but no rib resection was required. A frozen section sample (3.0 × 2.5 × 2.0 cm) was taken intraoperatively. The gross specimen measured 6.5 × 5.5 × 2.0 cm (Fig. 2A). Microscopic examination revealed bland adipose-tissue lobules with no apparent nuclear atypia. However, foci of atypical mesenchymal cells with moderate to marked variations in adipocyte size and muscular tissue infiltration were observed. No lipoblasts were observed. An immunohistochemical analysis revealed negative staining for mouse double minute 2 homolog (MDM2) and cyclin-dependent kinase 4 (CDK4), with a Ki-67 index of 10% (Fig. 2B, C). The final pathological diagnosis was ALT. Sequential radial resection was recommended; however, the patient refused to consent.

The patient underwent postoperative RT to minimize the risk of local recurrence. The patient received a total dose of 60 Gy in 30 fractions over six weeks using intensity-modulated radiation therapy (IMRT) following 3D planning with contouring of organs-at-risk. At 2-year follow-up with contrast-enhanced CT scan of the chest and abdominal sonography, the patient reported no recurrence-related findings and symptoms and expressed satisfaction with both functional and cosmetic outcomes, indicating a favorable impact on his quality of life.

Discussion

ALP is mainly known as WDL; however, the former term is more frequently used to describe tumors that occur in the chest wall, trunk, and extremities, whereas the latter term is mostly used for describing tumors that originally grew from the abdominal cavity, retroperitoneum, and mediastinum [2, 3]. In addition, the difference in respectability between ALT and WDL is an important characteristic for distinguishing them clinically. Atypical lipomatous tumor is often curable after radical surgical resection, whereas complete surgical resection cannot always be achieved for ALT because of its locally aggressive behavior, thus resulting in repeated local recurrences and possible lethal outcomes related to vital organs, such as bowel obstruction, renal failure, or sepsis [2, 3]. A nontender slow-growing mass is often the initial presentation of ALTs and has been reported to be up to 35 cm in diameter by Chang et al. [4]. Nevertheless, in this case, a rapidly growing 6 cm-sized tumor was noticed within only two weeks. Although most ALTs grow slowly, periods of more rapid enlargement can occur due to internal changes (e.g., inflammation or necrosis). Tumor size in this case (~ 6 cm) is smaller than many reported in the literature, but it underscores the need for suspicion even in moderate-sized lesions. Based on the patient’s history, the tumor has shown a persistent growth trend. Although preliminary radiologic interpretation favors a lipoma, the tumor measures approximately 5 cm in diameter and extends across both the inner and outer aspects of the chest wall, making it impossible to rule out the possibility of malignancy completely. Because the surgical resection margin differs between benign and malignant tumors, determining the nature of the tumor before surgery would be beneficial for subsequent treatment planning. Considering the tumor’s location and size, complete excision (excisional biopsy) would be technically challenging and carries certain risks. Therefore, an incisional biopsy, rather than a core needle biopsy, has been initially planned to establish a definitive diagnosis [5, 6].

Contrast-enhanced CT and magnetic resonance imaging demonstrate a well-defined, primarily fatty lesion, possibly containing enhanced thick septa and/or nodular or patchy nonlipomatous components [7, 8]. In Wilson et al.’s systematic review and meta-analysis of 26 studies [6], radiographic features including deep depth to fascia, thickened septations, enhancing components, and large tumor size (≥ 10 cm) had the highest sensitivities (≥ 85%) for detecting ALT and malignant liposarcomas.

However, we only obtained CT images during the preoperative assessment. Ultrasonography is an emerging diagnostic tool for ALT, which revealed heterogeneous, multilobulated masses with well-defined margins [7, 8]. MDM2 amplification is a molecular hallmark of ALT. However, IHC for MDM2/CDK4 may yield false negatives. In our case, the diagnosis was made based on typical clinical, radiological, and histological features despite negative IHC. The absence of FISH is a limitation and is discussed accordingly. Despite negative IHC results, the diagnosis of ALT was made based on the combination of histopathological features: a lobulated adipocytic tumor with significant variation in adipocyte size, presence of atypical stromal cells, and muscle infiltration—features that are well-described in ALT. There are some reports documenting this morphologic diagnosis in rare cases lacking MDM2/CDK4 expression [9, 10]. However, we recognize that such cases are diagnostically challenging and now discuss this as a limitation requiring further molecular confirmation.

Surgical resection is the primary treatment for ALT. Nevertheless, when radical resection is not feasible owing to anatomical constraints, adjuvant RT has emerged as a valuable combined treatment to optimize outcomes in this rare and challenging malignancy. In our case, we performed total removal with grossly negative margins; to our knowledge, ours is one of the few studies to have discussed ALT originating from the chest wall. The complex anatomy of the chest wall, which includes the ribs, muscles, and vital organs, complicates the complete surgical excision of the tumor. Bassett et al.’s study [2], which included 51 patients with ALT with two tumors arising from the chest wall and 49 from the extremities, reported that the local disease recurrence rate was approximately 27% after marginal resection without adjuvant RT. In these patients, larger tumors had a higher chance of recurrence after resection than smaller tumors (P = 0.03). The primary mass was 10 cm in size in 10 of the 14 patients with recurrent ALTs. Disease recurrence was not associated with age at diagnosis, sex, or tumor location. Recently, Kido et al. [11], who excluded all patients who received adjuvant RT, demonstrated that local disease recurrence was not significantly higher in the marginal resection group than in the wide resection group (P = 0.05). Given the discrepancy between the pre-/intraoperative pathology and the final histopathology, the resection margins were deemed insufficient. As the patient declined a second surgical procedure, adjuvant radiotherapy was arranged. According to the latest recommendations of the NCCN Soft Tissue Sarcoma Guidelines (Version 2.2024), RT is considered in patients with recurrence or deep-seated primary tumors because of the higher risk of local recurrence, depending on the tumor location and patient age [12]. For our patient, we suggested surgical resection in combination with adjuvant RT because the primary lesion arose from the subcostal region, which made complete surgical removal difficult. A retrospective study indicated that the local relapse-free survival at 5 years was significantly higher with adjuvant RT than with surgical resection alone (P < 0.001) [13]. Another retrospective study that analyzed patients receiving either neoadjuvant or adjuvant RT reported that RT was not associated with decreased local recurrence but with decreased second recurrence in patients with recurrence (P = 0.03) [14]. However, the study did not provide sufficient details to compare the local recurrence rates between neoadjuvant and adjuvant RT [14]. Chemotherapy is not routinely indicated for ALT due to poor responsiveness. It is generally reserved for dedifferentiated or metastatic disease. Further clinical trials, such as randomized controlled trials, are required to determine the optimal treatment for ALT. The patient received a total dose of 60 Gy in 30 fractions over six weeks using IMRT. Before RT, a planning CT scan was performed for 3D treatment planning, and organs-at-risk (e.g., heart and lungs) were contoured to minimize radiation exposure.

Conclusion

This case illustrates the diagnosis and successful treatment of a rare chest wall ALT in the subcostal region. Accurate histopathological assessment, careful surgical planning, and appropriate use of adjuvant RT led to a favorable 2-year outcome. Long-term follow-up remains essential due to the potential for local recurrence.
