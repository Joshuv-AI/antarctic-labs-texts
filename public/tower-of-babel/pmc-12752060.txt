Postoperative pain and morbidity at the donor site after palatal soft tissue harvesting: a systematic review
Authors: Wee Jian Chong, Masfueh Razali, Badiah Baharin, Nik Madihah Nik Azis
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimThis systematic review aims to evaluate the nature of postoperative pain and donor site morbidity following palatal soft tissue harvesting procedures, utilizing various wound dressings at the donor site.Materials and methodsA comprehensive search of five electronic databases and the grey literature was conducted of studies to June 2025, examining postoperative pain and morbidity following palatal graft harvesting. Eligible studies reported pain outcomes using Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) scores and assessed additional morbidity parameters. Risk of bias in randomized studies was assessed using the RoB 2.0 tool. Data on VAS scores were extracted for baseline and postoperative days 1, 3, 5, and 7. Donor site complications and morbidities were analyzed across various wound management approaches.ResultsSixty-eight studies met the inclusion criteria, of which thirty-eight reported self-reported pain scores using the VAS, presented as mean and standard deviation. Patient-reported outcomes, including quality of life assessments, were commonly used to evaluate broader postoperative impacts. Additional morbidities reported included bleeding, burning sensations, altered sensory perception, and changes in feeding behavior.ConclusionsPostoperative pain is most pronounced within the first three days following surgery, with progressive reduction over time. The application of wound dressings or physical barriers can effectively reduce pain, although outcomes vary depending on the type of intervention used. Other commonly reported complications include bleeding, burning sensations, altered sensation, and changes in feeding behavior.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-025-07198-2.Keywords: Gingival graft, Palatal wound dressing, Pain, Postoperative morbidities

Background

Palatal soft tissue harvesting procedures, such as free gingival grafts (FGG) and de-epithelialized connective tissue grafts (DGG), have become increasingly common in periodontal and implant therapy due to rising aesthetic demands and the need for optimal soft tissue management [1]. These techniques involve the surgical removal of epithelialised or subepithelial connective tissue from the palate for use in recipient sites elsewhere in the oral cavity. While the initial focus was on preserving or creating a band of attached keratinised tissue, modern approaches now aim to promote periodontal health by preventing further tissue loss, restoring lost hard and soft tissues, and improving esthetic outcomes [2].

The palate is the most commonly used donor site for autogenous soft tissue grafts in periodontal and implant procedures [3]. However, harvesting tissue for procedures such as FGG or DGG results in an open wound at the donor site, which typically requires up to four weeks for complete healing. Consequently, these procedures are frequently associated with postoperative morbidity, particularly pain and discomfort at the palatal site. Wessel et al. [4] reported a mean Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) pain score of 4.8 during the first three postoperative days, with 90 percent of participants experiencing pain. Wounds left to heal by second intention are particularly prone to delayed healing and increased patient morbidity. This is primarily due to the absence of complete wound edge approximation, which hinders re-epithelialization and tissue closure, thereby prolonging the inflammatory phase of healing.

As a result, considerable attention has been directed toward interventions aimed at improving wound healing and minimising postoperative discomfort at the donor site. These include the use of medicinal plant extracts, topical agents, haemostatic products, wound dressings, and protective stents [5]. More recently, biologically active materials such as platelet concentrates have shown promising results, particularly in accelerating the initial phases of wound healing and reducing inflammation and pain. Despite the utility of these dressing materials, Zucchelli et al. reported that 28% of patients undergoing gingival graft harvest experienced dehiscence or necrosis of the primary palatal flap during the first seven days of healing [6]. Other reported complications include excessive haemorrhage, postoperative bone exposure, recurrent herpetic lesions [7], and most frequently, postoperative pain.

Previous systematic reviews have examined donor site morbidity following palatal soft tissue harvesting, yet their conclusions differ, and methodological limitations remain. Oliveira et al. [8], in a network meta-analysis, concluded that leukocyte and platelet-rich fibrin (L-PRF) was the most effective intervention for reducing postoperative pain, accelerating wound healing, and minimising complications. In contrast, Leite et al. [5] reviewed 54 studies encompassing 43 different interventions and found that collagen hemostatic sponges, platelet-rich fibrin, and photobiomodulation were among the most frequently reported. However, they noted significant challenges in comparing the effectiveness of interventions due to heterogeneity in study designs, outcome measures, and combined therapies. While improvements in postoperative pain and oral health–related quality of life were generally observed, the lack of standardised tools and the diversity in methodologies limited the strength of comparisons.

Given the discrepancies in the findings and the ongoing emergence of new evidence, the present systematic review aims to provide an updated and comprehensive synthesis of clinical studies involving human patients undergoing palatal soft tissue harvesting procedures, with a focus on evaluating the postoperative pain and other morbidities at the donor site in relationship to the various wound dressings.

Methodology

Protocol development and focused question

This review's protocol was established a priori and registered in PROSPERO (CRD) on 24th January 2024 with all post hoc changes reported (see Appendix 1). The conduct and reporting of this review are guided by the Cochrane Handbook and the PRISMA statement [9], respectively. The two focused questions this review aims to answer are as follows: a) "What is the postoperative pain score after palatal soft tissue harvesting procedures using different wound dressings at the donor site?" and b) "What other postoperative morbidities are experienced by patients undergoing palatal soft tissue harvesting procedures?".

Eligibility criteria

All studies that included palatal graft harvesting were searched to conduct a systematic review. The focused question was constructed using the PVO acronym (Population, Variable and Outcome) and study design as follows:

Population: Patients undergoing palatal soft tissue harvesting.

Population: Patients undergoing palatal soft tissue harvesting.

Variable: The utilisation of different palatal wound dressings at the donor site. No restriction was applied to the type and definition of the wound dressing.

Variable: The utilisation of different palatal wound dressings at the donor site. No restriction was applied to the type and definition of the wound dressing.

Outcomes: i) Postoperative pain evaluated at the donor site by the patient. No restrictions were applied to the method of evaluation of pain (e.g. VAS score, pain indexes, postoperative analgesic intake); ii) Postoperative morbidities evaluated at the donor site by the patient.

Outcomes: i) Postoperative pain evaluated at the donor site by the patient. No restrictions were applied to the method of evaluation of pain (e.g. VAS score, pain indexes, postoperative analgesic intake); ii) Postoperative morbidities evaluated at the donor site by the patient.

Study design: Only controlled clinical trials and randomized clinical trials were included. Studies were excluded if they were animal studies, in vitro studies, observational studies, case reports, case series, systematic reviews, meta-analyses, and narrative studies.

Study design: Only controlled clinical trials and randomized clinical trials were included. Studies were excluded if they were animal studies, in vitro studies, observational studies, case reports, case series, systematic reviews, meta-analyses, and narrative studies.

The search was limited to articles in the English language and limited to publications up to June 2025.

Information sources and search

An electronic search of the literature published up to June 2025 was performed using five online databases: MEDLINE (through PubMed), Scopus, Web of Science, Cochrane Library and Embase. The following search terms were used: (“ Postoperative” OR “ Recovery”) AND (“Morbidities” OR “pain” OR discomfort” OR “bleeding” OR” Visual analogue scale” OR” Numerical rating scale” OR “ patient outcome”) AND (“Palatal” OR “palate” OR “ donor site” AND “soft tissue harvesting” OR “de-epithelialized gingival graft” OR “subepithelial connective tissue graft” OR” connective tissue graft” OR”Free gingival graft” OR “periodontal surgery” OR”mucogingival surgery” OR”palatal graft” OR “gingival graft”). Unique and specific search strings were formulated for each database, using the key concepts in different combinations (Appendix 1). Additional records were identified through a manual search of the reference lists.

Study selection and data extraction

Records were first screened at the title and abstract level. Extraction and synthesis of information from the included studies were summarized into a table of evidence by the first reviewer (CWJ) and second reviewer (NMNA) separately to ensure that they were aligned with the research questions. Any disagreement was resolved by a third author (BB). The online literature review application, Rayyan software (http://rayyan.ai/ accessed on 1 August 2025) was used for screening of the records.

Subsequently, a full-text evaluation was independently conducted by two reviewers. Study characteristics and main findings were collected, analysed, and then summarised in tables for qualitative and quantitative analyses. Extracted data from the included studies included publication details (first author, year of publication, and country of study), study design (limited to clinical trials), study objectives, population and sample size, postoperative assessment tools and methods used at the palatal donor sites, assessments of postoperative morbidities at the donor site, types of treatments at the donor site post-surgery, and authors' conclusions.

Risk of bias assessment

Once the full-text article analysis was completed, two reviewers (CWJ, NMNA) conducted the risk of bias evaluation according to Cochrane guidelines using the risk of bias 2.0 tool [10]. All risk of bias assessments were performed using the pain score as the outcome.

Data synthesis and analysis

Standardised, pre-piloted forms were designed for data extraction and collection. All outcome variables for the PVO questions were recorded at Days 1, 3, 5, and 7 post-intervention. If the authors presented data at different time intervals, the information was not recorded. When data were absent, authors were contacted, and their responses were documented. If necessary and possible, outcome variables were calculated by the reviewers based on the data available in the individual selected studies. The heterogeneity was assessed using the I2 index, respectively; the latter was defined as the percentage of variation in the global estimate that was attributable to heterogeneity (I2 = 25%: low; I2 = 50%: moderate; I2 = 75%: high heterogeneity). Statistical significance was set at p ≤ 0.05.

Results

Study selection and characteristics

Overall, the search strategy generated 307 records from five databases. 118 duplicates were excluded, and 189 records were assessed based on their titles and abstracts. A further 7 records were removed based on the title. Of these, 182 underwent full-text evaluations. 123 articles were excluded due to incorrect study method/outcome, type of articles that were journal articles, review papers, narrative reviews, case series, book chapters, systematic reviews, and meta-analyses. A manual search was done and a total of 12 articles were identified. Two articles were excluded due to the wrong outcome. Finally, a total of sixty-nine articles were included in the final analysis. Characteristics of included studies

A total of 69 studies were included, as outlined in Table 1. All articles were published in English and represented a wide geographical distribution, spanning fourteen countries. The majority of studies were conducted in Turkey (n = 18), followed by Iran (n = 10), Brazil (n = 9), Egypt (n = 8), India (n = 6), Italy (n = 6), the United States (n = 4), Saudi Arabia (n = 3), and one study each from Jordan, Portugal, Spain, Syria, and Thailand. All studies employed either the free gingival graft (FGG) or de-epithelialized gingival graft (DGG) technique, with the palatal donor site healing by secondary intention. Except for one quasi-randomised controlled clinical trial by Oliveira et al. [8], all included studies were randomized controlled trials. Ten of these employed a split-mouth design, namely those by Bitencourt et al. [20], Doshi et al. [24], Heidari et al. [35], Karimi et al. [41], Lafzi et al. [45], Morshedzadeh et al. [50], Patarapongsanti et al. [56], Samani et al. [59], Tabari et al. [68], and Yaghobee et al. [76]. Collectively, the studies involved a total of 2,447 participants.

Among the included studies, Işler et al. featured the most extensive design, incorporating one control group and five test groups [38]. The remaining studies included between one and four groups. All studies adopted a longitudinal design, although the duration of follow-up varied significantly. The longest follow-up was reported by Eltayeb et al. [29], extending up to 270 days (9 months). In contrast, Al-Hezaimi et al. [11] assessed outcomes only up to 48 h postoperatively, while Taşdemir et al. [69] followed participants for just 13 days. Gatti et al. [32], however, did not specify the duration of their follow-up.

Risk of bias

Quality assessment was independently conducted by two reviewers, based on the RoB 2.0 tool, as all included studies were randomised controlled trials. Study designs were accepted as reported unless contradictory evidence was identified. The quality assessment results are depicted in Fig. 2. Among the 69 included studies, the majority were assessed as having a high risk of bias or raising some concern, while only 12 studies were deemed to have a low risk of bias. Among the RoB 2.0 domains, Domain 5 (selection of the reported result) had the lowest risk, while Domain 4 (measurement of the outcome) contributed most to the overall bias concerns.

Between-study heterogeneity

On Day 7, three forest plots were generated to visually demonstrate the variability in postoperative pain outcomes across different intervention types: physical barriers [21, 28, 38, 41, 63, 78], laser therapy/photobiomodulation/ozone [12, 24, 38, 54], and platelet-rich fibrin (PRF) [30, 33, 38, 59, 62, 64], all measured using the Visual Analogue Scale (VAS). Day 7 was selected as it was the most consistently reported time point across the included studies.

The weighted mean pain scores at Day 7 showed that PRF-treated sites reported the lowest pain levels (mean 16.729, 95% CI: 4.402 to 29.056), followed by laser/photobiomodulation/ozone (mean 17.636, 95% CI: −5.646 to 40.918), and physical barriers (mean 28.119, 95% CI: 7.003 to 49.205). However, the 95% confidence intervals were wide and overlapping across interventions, with some crossing zero, reflecting inconsistency in both the direction and magnitude of the reported effects.

The I2 statistics for all three intervention categories indicated substantial to considerable heterogeneity: physical barriers (I2 = 95%), laser therapy/photobiomodulation/ozone (I2 = 94%), and PRF (I2 = 94%). These findings highlight marked variability among the included studies, likely attributable to differences in study designs, intervention protocols, and outcome assessments. As a result, proceeding with meta-analysis to draw more meaningful conclusions was not feasible.

Pain indexes and other measures of pain

Pain perception is the most reported method used for post-palatal soft tissue harvesting evaluation at the donor site (Table 2). The VAS using the 10-cm (100-mm) was the most used tool for assessing pain intensity at the donor site. Out of the 69 included articles, 39 reported postoperative pain using the VAS tool. Other pain scales, such as the 101-point Numerical Rating Scale (NRS-101) and the three or four-point Verbal Rating Scale (VRS), were also employed to assess postoperative morbidities. Meza-Mauricio et al., [47] utilized the NRS tool to report postoperative pain, while Bahammam et al., [18] employed both NRS-101 and VRS-4 in addition to VAS to measure patients' postoperative pain in their study. In contrast, Castro-Gaspar et al., [21] used the VRS to evaluate the degree of inflammation. Additionally, the amount of postoperative analgesic consumption was recorded as an objective measure of pain management.

Pain perception after palatal graft harvesting

A total of 39 studies reported pain scores using the VAS in terms of mean and standard deviation; these are presented in Table 2. An additional eight studies reported VAS pain scores using median values: Alasfar et al. [13], Alpan et al. [17], Ehab et al. [26], Gatti et al. [32], Hassan et al. [34], Juber et al.[39], Khalil et al.[43], and Sousa et al. [66]. Among all studies, Day 7 was the most frequently reported time point, with pain scores recorded by 34 studies. All intervention groups showed improved pain scores compared to the control groups.

The VAS scores across different postoperative days and treatment modalities reflect varying degrees of pain relief during the healing period. Pain typically peaked on Day 3, corresponding with the acute inflammatory phase following surgery. The highest pain score on Day 3 is reported by Ozcelik et al., of 73± 11 in the collagen sponge combined with sutures group, and Yildirim et al., of 64.2 ± 18.3 in patients using periodontal dressing on the palatal donor site [54, 77]. In contrast, the lowest pain score was reported by Isler et al. [38] of a score of 0 in patients using PRF combined with sutures and Essix retainer combined with sutures.

By Day 7, a substantial reduction in pain was observed across all treatment groups, reflecting the typical progression of postoperative healing. PRF-based therapies consistently recorded the lowest pain scores, with PRF combined with sutures continuing to show no reported pain [38]. Other interventions also reporting no pain on Day 7 included the medicinal plant extract combined with a stent [42], the spontaneous healing group [45], the L-PRF and cyanoacrylate groups [53], and the PRP group [59]. In contrast, the highest VAS score on Day 7 of 75.2 ± 12.5 was observed in the cyanoacrylate group reported by Karimi et al., followed by a score of 68.0 ± 8.4 in the periodontal pack combined with stent group by Eldien et al. [27, 41].

Postoperative analgesic consumption

In this review, 43 studies reported the prescription of postoperative analgesics (see Appendix 2). The duration of analgesic consumption monitoring ranged from the immediate postoperative hours, as reported by Tavelli et al. [71], to a maximum of 21 days in the study by Tabari et al. [68]. However, 16 studies did not specify the duration of monitoring. The most commonly prescribed analgesic was ibuprofen 400 to 600 mg in 13 studies, followed by paracetamol/acetaminophen 500 to 1000 mg in 7 studies, sodium dipyrone 500 mg (n = 4), unspecified non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) (n = 2), ketoprofen (n = 1), diclofenac potassium 50 mg (n = 1), and ketoprofen lysine salt 150 mg (n = 1). Analgesic consumption was reported either by the number of tablets or pills taken or by total dosage in milligrams. Notably, 18 of the 43 studies that reported analgesic use did not specify the type of analgesic prescribed.

The evaluation of postoperative analgesic intake across various interventions highlights notable differences, with certain treatment modalities demonstrating a clear reduction in analgesic use. Bahammam et al., [18] and Heidari et al., [35] reported that the PRF combined with suture and low-level laser therapy groups required fewer analgesics compared to the control groups, suggesting that these treatments are effective in managing postoperative pain. In contrast, conventional treatments such as gelatin sponge and suture were associated with higher analgesic consumption. Femminella et al. demonstrated that the gelatin sponge combined with suture group consumed significantly more analgesics than the PRF combined with suture group, highlighting the superior pain-relieving properties of PRF [30]. Conversely, treatments involving stents or ozone therapy generally led to higher analgesic use. For instance, Tavelli et al. and Taşdemir et al. found that patients treated with stents required more analgesics than those receiving alternative treatments [69, 70].

Postoperative discomfort, morbidities, and measurement methods

Postoperative discomfort and morbidity were assessed using several parameters, including: (i) Oral Health-Related Quality of Life (OHIP-14); (ii) postoperative bleeding; (iii) changes in diet or feeding habits; (iv) burning sensation at the donor site; (v) altered sensation at the donor site; (vi) sensation loss; (vii) postoperative stress; (viii) chewing ability; and (ix) speaking ability (see Appendix 3). Eight studies utilized the OHIP-14 questionnaire at various time intervals [25, 38, 39, 48, 49, 62, 69, 78]. Changes in diet or feeding habits were reported in five studies [16, 30, 36, 37, 75]. Burning sensation at the donor site, assessed using the VAS, was reported by ten studies [16, 17, 33, 36, 37, 63, 68, 73, 74, 77].

Postoperative bleeding was reported in 28 studies, using a variety of assessment methods. These included spontaneous bleeding within the first 24 h postoperatively, with no further spontaneous bleeding typically observed beyond Day 3 [21]. Other measures encompassed classifications such as immediate versus delayed bleeding, simple binary self-reports ("yes/no"), and more detailed tools like the VAS, reflecting a wide range of monitoring approaches. Most studies documented bleeding events primarily within the first 7 days, although some extended follow-up assessments to 14 days or up to 5 weeks. Notably, several studies emphasised individual variability in bleeding patterns, with marked differences in patient recovery trajectories.

Palatal wound dressings

All studies included in this review reported postoperative outcomes related to the use of various palatal wound dressings. A wide range of interventions, including single and combined approaches, were employed across the studies. The distribution of these intervention categories is summarised in Table 3. The most frequently applied intervention overall belonged to the physical barrier category (n = 32), which included cyanoacrylate, stents, periodontal packs, and membranes, used either alone or in combination with other interventions. Among the control groups, the most common approach was no treatment or suturing alone (n = 28), followed by the use of physical barriers (n = 24).

Discussion

A core outcome set refers to a standardized, consensus-driven group of outcomes that should be measured and reported as a minimum in all clinical trials within a specific area of health or healthcare. As recommended by the Implant Dentistry Core Outcome Set and Measurement [80], these outcomes typically include life impact such as quality of life and overall satisfaction with treatment. The management of postoperative pain is particularly important, as it directly influences the evaluation of surgical efficacy and patient satisfaction.

In palatal graft harvesting surgery, donor site pain typically peaks between the first and third postoperative days, corresponding to the acute inflammatory phase and gradually subsides to near baseline by Day 7. This pattern aligns with findings by Burkhardt and Lang [81], who identified the first three days as the peak pain period, followed by a decline as healing progresses. The reduction in pain is likely due to blood clot stabilisation and the onset of reepithelialisation. Consistent with studies by Burkhardt and Lang, Nobuto et al. [81] and Oliver et al. [82] pain and burning sensations tend to diminish during the second healing phase and resolve by the third, coinciding with complete surface epithelialisation. This trend was also observed in the present review, where pain markedly decreased by Day 7. Notably, PRF therapies were associated with significantly lower pain scores, sometimes eliminating pain entirely by Day 7, and were linked to reduced postoperative analgesic use compared to conventional approaches such as suturing or spontaneous healing.

Nonetheless, inconsistencies in the reporting of analgesic type, dosage, and duration significantly limit the strength and comparability of findings across studies. Many studies lack sufficient detail regarding analgesic protocols or patient adherence, which undermines the reliability of postoperative pain assessments and complicates cross-study comparisons. Furthermore, the potential influence of analgesic use on pain intensity and other patient-reported outcome measures is rarely examined, leaving an important gap in the understanding of how analgesia may confound or modulate these outcomes.

Beyond pain, this review identified several patient-reported outcome measures related to donor site morbidity, including bleeding, altered sensation, burning, and difficulties with daily activities such as eating, chewing, and speaking. Consistent with the findings of Leite et al. [5], these important outcomes were rarely assessed in detail. Only a small number of studies utilised validated instruments such as the OHIP-14 to evaluate oral health-related quality of life. Traditionally, dental research has emphasised clinician-reported outcomes such as treatment success, healing time, and complication rates which may not fully reflect the patient’s experience. PROMs provide valuable insights into how patients perceive and are affected by treatment, yet their implementation across studies remains inconsistent.

A variety of dressing materials and mechanical protective appliances have been used to protect the palatal wound and reduce donor site morbidity. Most studies reported a reduction in postoperative discomfort and morbidity regardless of the dressing type used. Notably, PRF consistently showed strong effectiveness in reducing postoperative pain. Biologically active dressings like PRF and L-PRF appeared more effective in pain control and reducing analgesic use than conventional physical barriers. Meanwhile, laser-based therapies, including photobiomodulation and low-level laser therapy, also showed positive outcomes, although evidence was varied. In contrast, physical barriers such as cyanoacrylate, stents, and periodontal packs were more effective in managing early bleeding, with mixed results for pain relief.

Several unique combinations of palatal wound dressings were reported in only one or two studies, including advanced platelet-rich fibrin (AFG) or injectable PRF (i-PRF) with COE-PAK™, hyaluronic acid with periodontal dressing, nonthermal atmospheric pressure plasma with acrylic stents, and other combinations involving medicinal plant extracts, ozone therapy, chitosan, aloe vera, and MEBO. While these approaches show potential, the limited number of studies evaluating each combination makes it difficult to draw definitive conclusions about their effectiveness. The wide variety of dressing types and intervention combinations also complicates efforts to identify the most effective single treatment. Notably, an attempt at a network meta-analysis of wound healing agents for palatal donor sites by Oliveira et al. [8] was only able to include six trials for pain outcomes on Days 1 and 3, and five trials for pain on Day 7, highlighting the challenges posed by heterogeneity and limited data availability across studies.

In the present review, only 12 studies were judged to have a low risk of bias, with most studies rated as high risk or raising some concerns. The greatest sources of bias stemmed from inadequate outcome measurement and selective reporting. The overall methodological quality thus raises caution when interpreting the reported outcomes. Consistent use of validated scales, proper blinding, and detailed reporting of follow-up are essential to improving future research quality.

A key limitation of this systematic review is the high degree of heterogeneity among the included studies, largely due to methodological and clinical differences in the interventions. Variations in study design, including follow-up duration, number of treatment arms, outcome measures, and the use of postoperative analgesics, complicated data comparison and limited the ability to draw definitive conclusions, necessitating a narrative synthesis rather than a quantitative meta-analysis. Although all employed FGG or DGG techniques, inconsistent protocols and outcome reporting led to substantial between-study heterogeneity. This was especially evident in the pooled analyses where I2 values exceeded 99 percent.

In a network meta-analysis, Oliveira et al. [8] identified leukocyte platelet-rich fibrin (L-PRF) as the most effective intervention for reducing postoperative pain, promoting wound healing, and minimising complications following palatal soft tissue graft harvesting. However, the authors noted that the limited number of included studies may have compromised the reliability of treatment rankings. The certainty of evidence for pain outcomes was rated very low due to within-study bias, and substantial heterogeneity was observed across analyses—driven by differences in active agents, study designs, pain assessment tools, wound healing measures, and follow-up periods. In contrast, a more recent systematic review by Leite et al. [5], which included additional studies, was unable to conduct even pairwise comparisons due to the wide methodological variability. The present review, which incorporated 14 additional studies not included by Leite et al., adding 451 participants, also encountered high heterogeneity, which prevented the execution of a meta-analysis.

Conclusion

This review highlights the wide range of interventions used to manage pain and reduce morbidity at the palatal donor site, where all intervention groups showed improved pain scores compared to controls. The pain associated with graft harvesting varies significantly, with VAS scores ranging from 0 to 73 ± 11 on Day 3 and from 0 to 75.2 ± 12.5 on Day 7. Pain is typically most intense during the first three days postoperatively, with a noticeable reduction in severity after Day 3. In addition to pain, other postoperative complications such as burning sensations, altered sensitivity, and changes in feeding habits have been reported following palatal graft harvesting.

To strengthen clinical recommendations, future high-quality randomised controlled trials are needed, using standardised outcome measures that include both clinician-assessed and patient-reported outcomes. The adoption of a core outcome set, particularly one that captures pain, morbidities, and quality of life, will support more consistent, patient-centred, and evidence-based care.
