Performance of large language models in providing patient- oriented information on gingival recession: a comparative study
Authors: Sultan Albeshri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPatients are increasingly turning to large language models (LLMs) for health information, but the quality of patient-oriented information remains uncertain. This article evaluated the performance of three major LLMs in answering clinically relevant patient’s questions related to gingival recession. A descriptive comparative study was carried out utilizing twenty standardized patient-centered questions related to gingival recession. Each question was submitted once to ChatGPT 5.5, Gemini 3.5, and Claude Sonnet 5, generating 60 responses. Five board-certified periodontists independently evaluated the responses using the validated Accuracy of Information (AOI) Index, a completeness scale, and expert preference rankings. Word count and response generation time were also recorded. Non-parametric tests and generalized estimating equations (GEE) were applied for statistical analysis, with statistical significance set at p ≤ 0.05. No significant differences were found among the three LLMs in accuracy (p = 0.993) or completeness (p = 0.972). ChatGPT generated responses significantly faster (median: 5.82 s) and with fewer words (median: 232) than Gemini (8.46 s; 438 words) and Claude (14.61 s; 333.5 words) (both p < 0.001). Expert preference rankings differed significantly (p = 0.002): Gemini and Claude were each ranked first in 38% of evaluations, compared with 24% for ChatGPT. For all three models, the highest accuracy was observed in Post-treatment Care. The three LLMs offered broadly comparable levels of accuracy and completeness when answering patient-oriented questions with respect to gingival recession. ChatGPT was faster and more concise, while Gemini and Claude were more often preferred by the expert evaluators. Introduction

Artificial intelligence (AI) improved the patient’s ability to access healthcare information. (Hirani et al. 2024) Large language models (LLMs), including ChatGPT (OpenAI), Deep seek, Gemini (Google), and Claude (Anthropic), are globally available and can produce responses related to healthcare questions. (Huang et al. 2023; Wang and Zhang 2024) Distinct from traditional search engines, that require users to access information from different sources, LLMs provide direct answers in natural language. This feature has made them increasingly attractive to patients seeking for quick medical and dental information. (Salehin et al. 2025; Sivaramakrishnan et al. 2025; Wang and Zhang 2024)

The increased use of AI chatbots facilitate the gathering of patient’s information with respect to diseases etiology, treatment options, and follow-up care prior they see a healthcare professional. (Huang et al. 2023; Sivaramakrishnan et al. 2025; Tiwari et al. 2023) Although the LLMs has promising potential for patient education, concerns remain about the accuracy, completeness, consistency, and reliability of its responses, particularly when patients may use that information to guide clinical decisions. (Khan et al. 2023; Schwendicke et al. 2020; Zhang et al. 2025a, b)

In dentistry, LLMs application has grown among specialties, including, endodontics, orthodontics, oral maxillofacial surgery, prosthodontics, and periodontology. (Daraqel et al. 2024; Umer et al. 2024; Zhang et al. 2025a, b) Contemporary reviews have reported potential applications in patient education, clinical decision-making, and the interpretation of clinical guidance. At the same time, these reviews emphasize that AI-generated information should not replace professional judgment because responses may contain factual errors, outdated information, or incomplete recommendations. (Umer et al. 2024; Zhang et al. 2025a, b) Performance can also vary among LLMs depending on the topic and clinical relevance, making repeated assessment important as these systems are frequently updated. (Sivaramakrishnan et al. 2025; Zhang et al. 2025a, b)

Gingival recession (GR) is a frequently observed clinical condition characterized by apical shift of the gingival margin pass the cemento-enamel junction, resulting in root surface exposure. (Cortellini and Bissada 2018) GR can increase dentin hypersensitivity, root caries, cervical abrasion, plaque retention, esthetic issues, and affect oral health-related quality of life. (Merijohn 2016) Prior periodontal consultation, patients frequently seek for information related to etiology, progression, treatment choices, expected outcomes, postoperative care, and long-term prognosis. As AI chatbots become another source of this information, the quality of their responses becomes relevant to patient understanding and treatment expectations.

Numerous studies have investigated the quality and accuracy of LLM responses to questions and inquiries asked by patients. (Daraqel et al. 2024; Sivaramakrishnan et al. 2025; Zhang et al. 2025a, b) Systematic reviews typically demonstrate acceptable level of clarity and precision, conclusions differ according to the clinical domain and assessment tools employed. (Albalawi et al. 2025; Ma 2025; Zhang et al. 2025a, b) In periodontology, a study by Babayiğit et al. evaluated how effectively ChatGPT addressed frequently asked questions. While the responses generally exhibited high accuracy and completeness, inquiries regarding gingival recession scored significantly lower in accuracy than those related to other periodontal diseases. (Babayiğit 2023) More recent comparisons of several LLMs have found broadly similar objective accuracy, while differences remain in presentation, comprehensiveness, readability, and user preference. (Sivaramakrishnan et al. 2025; Zhang et al. 2025a, b)

Despite this growing body of work, a comprehensive comparison of contemporary LLMs focused specifically on patient-oriented information about gingival recession is still lacking. Previous studies have also seldom considered several outcomes together, including expert preference, response generation time, response length, and performance across specific clinical domains. Therefore, this study compared ChatGPT, Gemini, and Claude with respect to accuracy, completeness, response generation time, response length, expert preference, and domain-specific performance when answering standardized patient-centered questions about gingival recession.

Methods

Study design

A descriptive comparative design was used to assess responses generated by three artificial intelligence (AI) models: ChatGPT 5.5 (OpenAI), Gemini 3.5 (Google), and Claude Sonnet 5 (Anthropic). The study included 20 patient-oriented questions addressing gingival recession (GR). (Supplement 1) The questions were developed using patient education materials from the American Academy of Periodontology (AAP), including its patient FAQ database, as well as clinically relevant topics identified in the 2018 World Workshop consensus on mucogingival conditions (Cortellini and Bissada 2018; Tonetti et al. 2025). Questions addressing issues commonly discussed during periodontal consultations were also included, particularly those related to the causes, prevention, prognosis, and treatment of gingival recession.

The initial set of questions was reviewed by two board-certified periodontists, who discussed and modified the questions until a consensus was reached. The final questions were grouped into five clinical domains: (1) Assessment and Diagnosis (Q1–Q4); (2) Efficacy and Outcomes (Q5–Q8); (3) Complications and Management (Q9–Q12); (4) Post-treatment Care (Q13–Q16); and (5) Precautions and Clinical Considerations (Q17–Q20). Each question was entered separately into all three AI models, producing a total of 60 responses. To minimize the confounding effects of prompt variability, the analysis was strictly restricted to the initial response generated by each model.

All questions were submitted using the same computer with a stable high-speed internet connection. The AI models were accessed through free access mode using standardized browser setup (Google Chrome version 147) during the data-collection period of (May 2026). Each question was submitted independently in a new conversation, with previous conversation history and browser cookies cleared before each submission to minimize potential carryover effects from earlier interactions. For each response, the generation time and total word count were recorded.

Five independent expert evaluators were recruited using predefined eligibility criteria. All evaluators were required to be full-time Assistant or Associate Professors in Periodontics, Consultants in Periodontics, American Board-certified in Periodontics, and have at minimum 3 years of experience in the specialty. All five evaluators met these requirements. Each response was independently assessed by all five evaluators, resulting in 300 evaluations for each primary outcome measure.

Outcome measures

Once the responses had been collected, the three answers to each question were provided to the expert evaluators for assessment. The evaluators were blinded to the identity of the AI model that generated each response. In addition, the order in which the responses were presented was randomized for each question to reduce the possibility of bias related to response sequence or knowledge of the AI model.

Each evaluator assessed the responses using two primary measures: (1) the Accuracy of Information (AOI) Index (Daraqel et al. 2024) and (2) a completeness score recorded on a visual analog scale ranging from 0 to 10. To assess each response, the AOI Index was utilized, an instrument previously validated for measuring the accuracy of generated information (Daraqel et al. 2024). The index consists of five domains: factual accuracy, corroboration with scientific evidence and guidelines, consistency, clarity and specificity, and relevance. Each domain was assigned a score from 0 to 2, giving a possible total score of 0–10. Higher scores indicated greater accuracy.

In addition to the AOI and completeness metrics, the evaluators performed a comparative review of the three responses for each item, assigning ranks from highest to lowest quality according to their aggregate preference and quality. This ranking was performed separately for each question.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was completed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) software version 30 (Chicago, IL, USA). Descriptive statistics were summarized as mean, standard deviation, median, interquartile range (IQR), frequency, and percentage. As the Shapiro–Wilk test indicated that the quantitative data were not normally distributed, non-parametric tests were used. The Kruskal–Wallis test was applied to compare quantitative variables across more than two groups, and the Mann–Whitney U test was used for pairwise (two-group) comparisons. The Chi-square test was used to compare frequencies between groups. Univariable Generalized Estimating Equations (GEE) were used to evaluate the association of the study variables with accuracy and completeness. A p value of ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Discussion

The present study compared ChatGPT, Google Gemini, and Claude in their responses to patient-oriented questions about gingival recession. The three models performed similarly in terms of accuracy and completeness, and none of the differences in these measures reached statistical significance. In contrast, the models differed clearly in expert preference, response generation time, and response length. Thus, the main differences were not in the overall factual quality of the information, but in how the information was presented.

A key finding was the lack of a significant difference in accuracy or completeness among the three models. Median accuracy scores were above 8/10 for all three systems, and completeness scores were also relatively high. These findings suggest that the models demonstrated relatively high expert-rated accuracy for common patient questions about gingival recession. This is in line with recent dental studies reporting generally acceptable factual accuracy among leading LLMs, despite differences in response style. (Sivaramakrishnan et al. 2025) Zhang et al. likewise reported high comprehensiveness across several LLMs when answering patient questions about gingival and endodontic conditions (Zhang et al. 2025a). Taken together, these findings suggest that contemporary LLMs may have a useful role in preliminary patient education, provided that professional oversight remains available.

The expert rankings, however, did not follow the same pattern as the accuracy and completeness scores. Gemini and Claude were each ranked first in 38% of evaluations, compared with 24% for ChatGPT (p < 0.05). ChatGPT also had a significantly less favorable weighted ranking despite having similar AOI-based accuracy and completeness. One possible explanation is that the AOI measures factual quality, whereas expert preference can also be influenced by organization, readability, flow, and the balance between technical detail and patient-friendly language. Similar observations have been reported in other studies in which clinicians rated chatbot responses favorably because of their structured presentation even when factual content was comparable. (Maruska et al. 2025) Objective accuracy measures may therefore not capture every feature that affects clinicians’ overall preference.

A range of methods has been used to assess AI-generated healthcare responses. Previous studies have commonly relied on expert ratings to examine accuracy, completeness, and overall quality. (He et al. 2024; Singhal et al. 2023) Other tools include DISCERN-AI, PEMAT-AI, Global Quality Scores, and modified DISCERN, which assess areas such as understandability, actionability, safety, and educational value. (Garcia et al. 2024; Ghanem et al. 2024) Readability has also been examined using measures such as the Flesch-Kincaid Grade Level, Flesch Reading Ease, Gunning Fog Index, and SMOG Index. (Garcia et al. 2024; Ghanem et al. 2024; Grabeel et al. 2018; Tam et al. 2024) In the present study, the AOI index was selected because its domains closely matched the clinical focus of the questions. It assesses factual accuracy, corroboration with evidence, consistency, clarity and specificity, and relevance. (Daraqel et al. 2024) These areas are particularly relevant when responses include treatment instructions, contraindications, expectations, or safety information. The inclusion of evidence corroboration is also useful given concerns about hallucinations and unwarranted confidence in LLM-generated content.

Response generation time and length differed substantially between the models. ChatGPT was consistently faster and produced shorter responses than Gemini and Claude. Gemini generated the longest responses, whereas Claude had the longest processing time. These differences may reflect variation in model architecture, inference strategies, and optimization rather than differences in clinical knowledge. Importantly, a shorter response is not necessarily less useful, and a longer response is not automatically more informative. For instance, Sezer et al. found no significant relationship between response time and accuracy when comparing several AI models on dental specialty examination questions. (Sezer and Aydoğdu 2025) Very short answers may leave out clinically relevant information, while overly long responses may be difficult for patients to follow, particularly when health literacy is limited. In addition, Arslan et al. similarly reported differences in response detail between ChatGPT and Gemini despite comparable accuracy in patient questions about refractive surgery. (Arslan and Usta 2025) The useful response, therefore, is not necessarily the longest one; it is the one that delivers sufficient information in a way that patients can comprehend. (Ayers et al. 2023).

The domain-specific findings provide another important point. All three LLMs performed best in the Post-treatment Care domain, and GEE analysis identified this domain as the only category significantly associated with higher accuracy. Batool et al. reported that LLMs could generate clinically appropriate postoperative dental care instructions and suggested that they may help reinforce patient education after dental procedures, although clinician oversight remains necessary. (Batool et al. 2024)

The stronger performance on post-treatment care may be related to the standardized nature of this type of information. In contrast, assessment and diagnosis require consideration of individual factors such as recession classification, periodontal phenotype, esthetic demands, smoking status, and systemic health. These factors are difficult for an LLM to assess without a clinical examination and patient-specific information. (Kaplan and Akyol 2026) The results therefore support the potential use of LLMs for educational information more strongly than for individualized treatment recommendations.

From a clinical perspective, these findings are relevant because patients increasingly use AI chatbots before and after dental appointments to learn about disease progression, procedures, and postoperative care. Clear and accurate responses may improve understanding and reinforce instructions. At the same time, using AI without professional interpretation could contribute to unrealistic expectations or inappropriate self-diagnosis which could potentially have negative impact for patients. (Hager et al. 2024) AI-generated information should consequently be considered an educational aid rather than a replacement for clinical examination or professional judgment. This view is consistent with the broader dental AI literature, which supports the use of LLMs to assist rather than replace clinician–patient communication. (Rajan et al. 2026; Rokhshad et al. 2024; Thorat et al. 2024)

Several limitations should be considered. First, only the initial single-turn response to each standardized prompt was assessed. In practice, patients may have multi-turn conversations with an LLM, and subsequent interaction could change the accuracy or consistency of the information. Second, although all five evaluators met strict clinical and board-certification criteria, readability from the patient’s perspective was not formally measured thus, may limit the generalizability of the findings. Third, all questions were written in English and were assessed by periodontists practicing within a single institutional setting, which may limit generalizability to other languages and populations. Future investigations would benefit from exploring longitudinal patient–LLM interactions and evaluating informational accessibility across populations with varying socio-educational backgrounds.

Conclusion

The three LLMs offered broadly comparable levels of accuracy and completeness when answering patient-oriented questions with respect to gingival recession. ChatGPT was faster and more concise, while Gemini and Claude were more often preferred by the expert evaluators. These LLMs may be useful as adjuncts to patient education, but they should complement rather than replace professional periodontal consultation.
