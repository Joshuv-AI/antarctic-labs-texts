Association between cardiometabolic index and lung function in US adolescents
Authors: Wenxuan Sun, Chongyao Hao, Ling Wei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Obesity is a key determinant of pulmonary function. The cardiometabolic index (CMI), which integrates body fat distribution and lipid metabolism status, directly reflects cardiovascular metabolic risk and health status. However, previous studies have not explored its relationship with pulmonary function measurements in adolescents. This cross-sectional study analyzed data from the publicly available 2007 to 2012 cycle of the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey. Among the pediatric participants, the CMI was the exposure of interest, calculated as (triglycerides [mmol/L]/high-density lipoprotein cholesterol [mmol/L]) × waist-to-height ratio. Pulmonary function outcomes, assessed by spirometry, included forced expiratory volume in 1 second (FEV1), forced vital capacity (FVC), the FEV1/FVC ratio, and peak expiratory flow. To verify the robustness of the results, subgroup analyses and interaction tests were additionally performed. Furthermore, smooth curve fitting was applied to characterize any potential nonlinear relationships. The study population comprised 1251 adolescents aged 12 to 19 years. When the model was adjusted for all covariates, multivariable linear regression analysis revealed that each unit increase in Ln-CMI was associated with an increase in FEV1 (β = 129.01; 95% CI: 21.26–236.76), increased FVC (β = 397.76; 95% CI: 275.06–520.45), and a decreased FEV1/FVC ratio (β = −0.03; 95% CI: −0.04 to −0.02). Ln-CMI is positively correlated with FEV1 and FVC, and negatively correlated with FEV1/FVC. Our analysis of large-scale population data confirms a significant association between elevated CMI levels in American adolescents aged 12 to 19 and markedly reduced lung function indicators.

1. Introduction

In the United States, nearly 20% of children and adolescents are affected by childhood obesity, a significant public health challenge.[1] The pathobiological characteristics of obesity, such as insulin resistance and metabolic alterations, influence lung function and airway inflammation.[2] Spirometry, a key component of pulmonary function testing, is fundamental to the evaluation of respiratory status and is instrumental in identifying various lung conditions. It serves as an indispensable diagnostic tool in pediatric pulmonary care, providing objective evidence of lung function status.[3] The evaluation of pulmonary function typically involves several primary measures: forced vital capacity (FVC), forced expiratory volume in 1 second (FEV1), the FEV1/FVC ratio, and peak expiratory flow (PEF).[4]

The cardiometabolic index (CMI) is a composite metric, proposed by Wakabayashi and Daimon, designed to capture both central obesity and dyslipidemia by multiplying waist-to-height ratio by the triglycerides/high-density lipoprotein cholesterol ratio.[5] CMI reflects both fat content and blood lipid levels. Prior research has linked CMI to a spectrum of cardiometabolic and renal disorders, including diabetes, cardiovascular disease, kidney disease, and broader metabolic dysfunction.[6–10] Although studies have demonstrated that elevated CMI levels in the US adult population correlate with reduced FEV1 and FVC, the relationship between CMI and lung function in adolescent cohorts remains unclear.[11]

This cross-sectional study utilized data from a large, nationally representative sample of adolescents (aged 12–19 years) in National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) to examine the association between CMI and pulmonary function. The findings suggest that CMI may serve as a useful indicator for monitoring and managing lung capacity in this age group.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Data sources

Our analysis incorporated data from 3 consecutive NHANES cycles (2007–2012), a nationally representative cross-sectional program administered by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention’s National Center for Health Statistics. NHANES employs a unique methodology combining interview data, physical examinations, and laboratory testing to assess disease prevalence and risk factors across the U.S. population. The dataset includes detailed demographic characteristics, clinical measurements, biochemical analyses, and questionnaire responses. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the National Center for Health Statistics Review Board. All participants were required to provide written informed consent. Further methodological details can be accessed on the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention website (www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/tutorials/default.asp).

2.2. Study subjects

Data were obtained from the publicly available, nationally representative NHANES, spanning the 2007 to 2012 cycles. This yielded an initial sample of 30,442 participants for analysis. We systematically excluded 21,375 individuals with missing CMI data, 2553 with incomplete or low-quality (grade C–F) lung function measurements, and 5263 outside the target age range of 12 to 19 years. Consequently, the final analytical sample included 1251 adolescents. Figure 1 illustrates the participant selection process.

2.3. Lung function

During the 2007 to 2012 NHANES examinations, certified technicians performed spirometry using Ohio 822/827 dry-roll volume spirometers on eligible participants (6–79 years) following ATS/ERS guidelines. Exclusion criteria included: active cardiopulmonary symptoms (chest pain and hemoptysis); respiratory compromise (oxygen dependence and impaired forced expiration); recent surgical interventions (ocular, thoracic, or abdominal within 3 months); and acute medical events (myocardial infarction, cerebrovascular accident, or active tuberculosis). All spirometry measurements underwent rigorous quality review by certified experts at the NIOSH Quality Control Center, ensuring adherence to ATS/ERS technical standards. Assessment of lung function focused on the following 4 parameters: FVC, FEV1, the FEV1/FVC ratio, and PEF. The application of rigorous quality control led to the exclusion of spirometry results with grades C to F. Superior respiratory status is reflected by higher readings across these functional measures.

2.4. Cardiometabolic index (CMI)

CMI serves as a comprehensive indicator for assessing cardiovascular metabolic risk. As an index integrating waist circumference with blood lipid ratios, CMI reflects the combined impact of visceral adiposity and dyslipidemia. 2.5. Covariates

Covariate selection was informed by prior literature and clinical expertise to mitigate their potential confounding effects on the study outcomes.[11,12] Sociodemographic characteristics, medical history, and laboratory parameters were collected using standardized methods. Specifically, age, sex, race, and poverty-income ratio (PIR) were obtained from questionnaires, which also identified diabetes status and 2nd-hand smoke exposure.

2.6. Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.1.3; R Foundation for Statistical Computing) and EmpowerStats (version 2.0; X&Y Solutions Inc.) and were confined to participants with complete data. We categorized CMI into tertiles, with differences across groups for continuous and categorical variables examined using weighted t tests and weighted chi-square tests, respectively. The association between CMI and lung function was assessed using multiple linear regression with sequential covariate adjustment: an unadjusted base model (model 1); model 2 adjusted for demographics (age, sex, and race); and model 3 further adjusted for body mass index (BMI), PIR, diabetes, and 2nd-hand smoke exposure. To explore potential heterogeneity, subgroup analyses were conducted, stratified by sex, race, BMI categories, PIR, diabetes, and 2nd-hand smoke exposure. Additionally, weighted generalized additive models with smooth curve fitting were employed to characterize potential nonlinear relationships between CMI and lung function parameters.

3. Results

This study aimed to investigate the association between CMI and lung function parameters in a representative sample of US adolescents.

3.3. Subgroup analysis

To further elucidate the relationship between CMI and lung function, we performed subgroup analyses (Table 3). Subgroup variables included sex, race, PIR, diabetes, and 2nd-hand smoke exposure. We found no significant interactions between sex, race, PIR, and diabetes (all interaction P values >.05). However, the association between lung function and CMI showed significant differences in the 2nd-hand smoke subgroup. Within this subgroup, FEV1/FVC was significantly associated (P = .004), whereas FEV1 (P = .804), FVC (P = .082), and PEF (P = .479) were not significantly associated. Our analysis identified 2nd-hand smoke exposure as an important effect modifier in the association between CMI and lung function, with 2nd-hand smoke exposure significantly moderating the association between CMI and FEV1/FVC (P = .004).

4. Discussion

This cross-sectional analysis of 1251 American adolescents aged 12 to 19 years revealed that elevated CMI was significantly associated with increased FEV1 and FVC, yet concurrently correlated with a decrease in the FEV1/FVC ratio. This seemingly contradictory finding may reflect the complexity of obesity-related pulmonary function alterations. Whilst increased absolute values of FEV1 and FVC may suggest enlarged lung volumes or altered chest wall compliance, a reduced FEV1/FVC ratio signifies airflow limitation and is typically regarded as an early indicator of pulmonary dysfunction. Thus, whilst lung capacity may increase compensatorily due to obesity, signs of airway dysfunction are already present. This aligns with the mechanisms by which obesity-associated systemic inflammation and metabolic disorders impact the respiratory system. These findings suggest CMI may serve as a potential predictor of declining lung function. Our study represents the 1st report of an association between CMI and lung function in a nationally representative cohort of US adolescents.

Childhood obesity has emerged as a global health challenge.[13] Obesity, particularly abdominal obesity, has been recognized as a significant risk factor for impaired lung function and respiratory diseases.[14,15] Numerous studies have confirmed an association between obesity and impaired lung function. Birger et al found that an elevated body mass index in late adolescence was strongly associated with an increased risk of bacterial pneumonia and infective endocarditis in adulthood.[16] The study by Cui et al demonstrated that inspiratory muscle strength serves as a mediator in the association between abdominal obesity and lung function among young adults.[17] Longitudinal research shows that higher BMI trajectories across childhood are associated with a higher incidence of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease in later life.[18] Research by Lertluksana et al has revealed that obese children and adolescents with metabolic syndrome exhibit significantly reduced lung function.[19] In a multi-ethnic cohort study involving 3418 children, our findings indicate that boys and girls exhibit differences in respiratory disease risk, a disparity partially influenced by BMI.[20] Nermin et al discovered that obesity exerts a detrimental effect on pulmonary function, with central obesity potentially triggering airway inflammation.[21] A retrospective analysis of 400 pediatric patients revealed that obesity significantly impacts lung function in children with respiratory allergic diseases.[22] Research by Alena et al has found that adolescents with abdominal obesity are associated with an increased likelihood of developing chronic bronchitis.[23] Our findings further corroborate the significant findings previously established regarding the association between body morphology and pulmonary function.

Although the precise mechanism by which CMI affects lung function remains unclear, several related theories have been proposed. Weight gain significantly impacts pulmonary physiology, particularly when leading to abdominal and thoracic obesity, thereby restricting lung expansion.[24] Mediastinal and abdominal fat deposition in obesity significantly alters the mechanical properties of the lungs and chest wall. These changes result in reduced compliance of the lungs, chest wall, and the respiratory system as a whole.[25] A 2nd potential mechanism underlying this pathophysiological process centers on inflammation. Adipose tissue macrophages secrete a spectrum of pro-inflammatory mediators, which induce both localized and systemic inflammation.[2] In adults, higher CMI correlates with lower FEV1/FVC ratios.[11] The negative association observed between CMI and FEV1/FVC in adolescents in this study aligns with findings reported in adult populations, further supporting the potential of elevated CMI as a predictive marker for declining lung function. Elevated CMI may accompany improved nutritional status and enhanced growth development.[26–30] Previous reports have indicated that obesity induces adaptive physiological changes, such as vasodilation, increased blood volume and cardiac output, alongside alterations in metabolic and hemodynamic demands.[31,32] While a certain level of body fat and metabolic activity may support lung tissue development during growth and maturation, excessively high CMI may already indicate adverse effects of metabolic disorders on respiratory function.

This investigation represents the 1st large-scale analysis of CMI associations with comprehensive lung function measures in a nationally representative pediatric cohort. This investigation employs robust methodology, featuring a substantial participant cohort and multidimensional analytical approaches. We employed weighted linear regression models to account for potential confounding variables, followed by comprehensive sensitivity analyses and stratified examinations to verify the robustness of our primary findings. This multipronged analytical approach addressed both measured confounders and potential effect modification across population subgroups. As a critical final analytical phase, nonlinear associations between CMI and lung function were assessed using generalized additive models with smoothing splines. The study’s key findings offer clinically relevant insights for patient management and future lung function research.

The following limitations should be acknowledged. As with all observational analyses using NHANES data, this study cannot exclude residual confounding or establish directionality in the CMI-lung function association. Longitudinal validation is required to assess causality. Notwithstanding adjustments for multiple covariates, the influence of unmeasured factors, and thus residual confounding, cannot be excluded.

5. Conclusion

This study, based on large-scale population data, confirms that among adolescents aged 12 to 19 years in the United States, elevated CMI levels are significantly associated with increased FEV1 and FVC, yet with a decrease in the FEV1/FVC ratio. Collectively, the decline in FEV1/FVC suggests that elevated CMI may be linked to reduced lung function, particularly airflow limitation. However, the causal relationship between the 2 requires further validation through prospective studies.
