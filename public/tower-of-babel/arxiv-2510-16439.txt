FrugalPrompt : Reducing Contextual Overhead in Large Language Models via Token Attribution * Corresponding author.


Abstract
Large language models (LLMs) owe much of their stellar performance to expansive input contexts, yet such verbosity inflates monetary costs, carbon footprint, and inference-time latency. Much of this overhead manifests from the redundant low-utility tokens present in typical prompts, as only a fraction of tokens typically carries the majority of the semantic weight. We address this inefficiency by introducing FrugalPrompt , a novel prompt compression framework for LLMs, which retains only the most semantically significant tokens. Leveraging two state-of-the-art token attribution methods, GlobEnc and DecompX, we assign salience scores to every token in an input sequence, rank them to preserve the top- k % k\% tokens in their original order, and obtain a sparse frugalized prompt. We evaluate the approach across four NLP tasks: Sentiment Analysis, Commonsense QA, Summarization, and Mathematical Reasoning, using a suite of frontier LLMs. For the first three tasks, a 20% prompt reduction incurs only a marginal loss in task performance, demonstrating that contemporary LLMs can reconstruct elided context from high-salience cues. In contrast, performance on mathematical reasoning deteriorates sharply, reflecting a stronger dependence on complete token continuity. Further analysis with bottom- k % k\% and random- k % k\% tokens reveals asymmetric performance patterns that may suggest potential task contamination effects, wherein models may resort to shallow memorized patterns from pretraining exposure for conventional NLP tasks. We posit that our work contributes to a more nuanced understanding of LLM behavior in performance-efficiency trade-offs, and delineate the boundary between tasks tolerant to contextual sparsity and those requiring exhaustive context. Our source code and data are available at: https://github.com/Starscream-11813/Frugal-ICL . Keywords: Large Language Models, Prompt Compression, Token Attribution, Salience Scores, GlobEnc, DecompX, Inference Efficiency, Task Contamination, Contextual Redundancy.

1.  Introduction

As large language models (LLMs) continue to reshape the landscape of NLP, they are increasingly applied to complex and ingenious tasks
Yang et al. 2024
;
Schmidgall et al. 2025
. With the scaling of LLMs into super-large trillion-parameter models
Fedus et al. 2022
;
Hudson et al. 2023
, and multi-agent architectures requiring hundreds of API calls
Islam et al. 2024
;
Jang et al. 2024
, the computational footprint has increased drastically
Wu et al. 2025
, resulting in high inference latency
Wan et al. 2023
and energy consumption
Stojkovic et al. 2024
.

To counter the consequences of the scaling law for LLMs
Kaplan et al. 2020
, several methods were introduced to improve model efficiency
Wan et al. 2023
;
Zhou et al. 2024
. Prompt compression is one of the subfields where the input prompting tokens are reduced while retaining considerable performance
Zhang et al. 2025
. Compression is either achieved on the hard prompts (natural language input text)
Sennrich et al. 2016
or the soft prompt (trainable embedding-size vectors)
Zhao et al. 2023a
, with the former being more popular due to its interpretability and compatibility with closed-source models.

Current hard prompt compression methods fall short in three key aspects. First, some methods rely on summarizing the input prompt
Chuang et al. 2024
, which can inadvertently discard semantically critical tokens. Second, these methods tend to retain low-information tokens, such as articles and conjunctions
Jiang et al. 2023
. Third, all methods either rely on an auxiliary smaller LLM with billions of parameters or a training paradigm. For instance,
Chuang et al. 2024
use a Vicuna 7B model
Chiang et al. 2023
, which introduces a significant parameter overhead.

We address these challenges by introducing a prompt compression strategy that uses saliency scores generated from state-of-the-art pre-trained token attribution methods
Modarressi et al. 2022
;
Modarressi et al. 2023
. These saliency scores can capture the semantically rich token while filtering the redundant ones. This is achieved without any training paradigm, with minimal parameter overhead of a single 110M BERT model
Devlin et al. 2019
, substantially lower than that of existing methods.

Our criteria for filtering are grounded in two linguistic principles underlying textual redundancy. First, there is an intrinsic redundancy to any form of text produced by humans
Wit and Gillette 1999
. For instance, in the text, “What are your plans for the weekend, by the way?”, the phrase “by the way” contributes no additional meaning to an LLM. Second, the English language has low-importance tokens that add little to no semantic weight, generally in the form of articles and auxiliary verbs. For instance, most of the meaning is conveyed if the statement “I have received a parcel.”, is simplified to “I received parcel.", with the latter mimicking simplified English, often used by non-native speakers
Shubert et al. 1995
. Our filtering methods eliminate both forms of redundancy.

Our key contributions are as follows:

•

We introduce a novel, training-free prompt compression strategy for LLMs that controllably filters low-importance tokens based on saliency scores from pre-trained encoders.

•

Experiments on four tasks reveal that a 20% prompt reduction retains the performance across most models and tasks with minimal parameter overhead.

•

We reveal key behavioral insights into the performance scaling of models with the inference cost and possible task contamination in common benchmarks.

2.  Related Work

2.1.  LLM Efficiency

The current literature on LLM efficiency
Wan et al. 2023
stems from the literature on efficient transformers
Tay et al. 2022
, but for the autoregressive setting of LLMs. Inference-time efficiency can be achieved via model compression methods, such as quantization
Dettmers et al. 2023b
, parameter pruning
Ma et al. 2023
, low-rank decomposition
Li et al. 2023a
, and knowledge distillation
Timiryasov and Tastet 2023
. Training efficiency can be achieved during pre-training
Liu et al. 2023
;
Yao et al. 2024
and fine-tuning, either through parameter-efficient
Hu et al. 2022
or memory-efficient
Dettmers et al. 2023a
approaches. Finally, architectural changes also improve efficiency,
e.g.
, efficient attention
Child et al. 2019
;
Ainslie et al. 2023
and state-space models
Gu and Dao 2023
. Distinct from these methods, our work adopts a data-centric approach where the input prompt is optimized instead of changing the underlying weights or architecture of the model
Chang et al. 2024
.

2.2.  Prompt Compression

Prompt compression aims to minimize input redundancy while retaining task-relevant information. Trainable
soft-prompts
achieve these through learned embeddings
Zhao et al. 2023b
, while sacrificing the interpretability of the prompt text. In contrast, compressing
hard prompts
preserves interpretability via filtering important tokens
Li et al. 2023b
;
Jiang et al. 2023
or paraphrasing the prompt text into a reduced version
Chuang et al. 2024
. Context-heavy tasks,
e.g.
, conversation systems, benefit from prompt compression but have focused on sentential redundancy or summarization
Santra et al. 2023
. Our work instead aims at token-level reduction, offering higher granularity without losing semantic content.

While our work primarily filters the top
k\%
tokens based on the attribution scores, a key distinction is that we use smaller BERT-class text encoders to gain attribution scores. Previous works lack such efficiency as they rely on smaller auto-regressive models,
e.g.

Jiang et al. 2023
uses GPT-2
Radford et al. 2019
and
Chuang et al. 2024
uses Vicuna 7B
Chiang et al. 2023
. While the compressor model has fewer parameters than the interference model, its overhead is significantly higher than that of the discriminative token attribution methods.

2.3.  Token Attribution

The self-attention weights in transformers have been primarily explored as a proxy for token importance
Htut et al. 2019
;
Clark 2019
, yet they fail to align with the true attribution of tokens
Jain and Wallace 2019
. While including vector norms can partially mitigate this issue
Kobayashi et al. 2020
, it overlooks the intricacies of the non-attention components in a transformer. Instead, we rely on subsequent token attribution methods: GlobEnc
Modarressi et al. 2022
, which quantifies global token attribution by aggregating layer-wise analyses across the entire encoder using attention rollout and vector norms, and DecompX
Modarressi et al. 2023
, which propagates locally decomposed token representations globally to incorporate all model components, including non-linear feed-forward networks. These methods were selected for their superior ability to capture comprehensive semantic contributions beyond raw attention weights, enabling accurate identification of salient tokens with minimal computational overhead.

.

3.  Preliminaries: Token Attribution

To identify which tokens in an input text sequence carry significant influence on the language model’s decision-making process for a given task, we compute the token attribution scores using task-specific BERT-class encoders
Devlin et al. 2019
. We obtain the saliency scores from these models by adopting two state-of-the-art (SoTA) token attribution methods: the global token attribution analysis method (GlobEnc)
Modarressi et al. 2022
and the globally propagated locally decomposed attribution analysis method (DecompX)
Modarressi et al. 2023
, which are discussed in this section.

3.1.  GlobEnc

GlobEnc aggregates the layerwise analysis method
\mathcal{N}_{\text{{Enc}}}\coloneqq(\|\mathbf{\tilde{\text{$x$}}}_{i\leftarrow j}\|)\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
from input token
j
to output token
i
, across all the encoder layers using attention rollout
Abnar and Zuidema 2020
. As elucidated in
Kobayashi et al. 2020
,
\tilde{x}_{i\leftarrow j}
approximates the attributed vector contribution from input token
j
to output token
i
, computed as the weighted and transformed value vector
\alpha_{ij}f(x_{j})
, where
f
is the affine transformation to value space. The vector is adjusted for residual connections and layer normalization to capture the true
mixed
influence beyond attention weights.
For each encoder layer, the output is computed as:

\displaystyle\mathbf{\tilde{\text{$z$}}}_{i\leftarrow j}

\displaystyle=g_{z_{i}^{+}}\left(\sum_{h=1}^{H}\alpha_{ij}^{h}f^{h}(x_{i})+\mathbbm{1}[i=j]x_{i}\right)

(1)

\displaystyle\mathbf{\tilde{\text{$x$}}}_{i\leftarrow j}

\displaystyle\approx g_{\mathbf{\tilde{\text{$z$}}}_{i}^{+}}(\mathbf{\tilde{\text{$z$}}}_{i\leftarrow j})=\frac{\mathbf{\tilde{\text{$z$}}}_{i\leftarrow j}-\operatorname{mean}(\mathbf{\tilde{\text{$z$}}}_{i\leftarrow j})}{\operatorname{std-dev}(\mathbf{\tilde{\text{$z$}}}_{i}^{+})}\odot\gamma

(2)

such that
\mathcal{N}_{\text{{ResLN}}}\coloneqq(\|\mathbf{\tilde{\text{$z$}}}_{i\leftarrow j}\|)\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is the attribution analysis to measure the influence of an encoder layer’s input token
j
on its corresponding output token
i
, considering the residual connections and layer normalizations in the attention block. Here,
\alpha_{ij}^{h}
denotes the raw attention weight from the
i
th token to the
j
th token in the attention head
h\in\{1,\dots,H\}
, collectively forming the set of attention weights
\mathbf{A}^{h}=\{\alpha_{ij}^{h}\bigm|1\leq i,j\leq n\}
for the
h
th attention head and
f^{h}(x)
is the transformation function as defined in
Kobayashi et al. 2020
. For layer
l
, the attention rollout with respect to the input is computed as per the following recursive function:

\displaystyle\mathbf{\tilde{\text{$\mathbf{A}$}}}_{l}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\hat{\mathbf{A}}_{l}\mathbf{\tilde{\text{$\mathbf{A}$}}}_{l-1}\quad\text{; if}\quad l>1\\
\hat{\mathbf{A}}_{l}\quad\quad\quad\,\text{; if}\quad l=1\end{cases}

(3)

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{A}}_{l}

\displaystyle=0.5\bar{\mathbf{A}}_{l}+0.5\mathbf{I}

(4)

where
\bar{\mathbf{A}}_{l}
is the mean raw attention grid across all the heads relevant to the layer
l
.

3.2.  DecompX

While GlobEnc aggregates layer-wise analyses with a modified attention rollout and approximates FFNs via residuals and scalar norms, it risks information loss. DecompX improves this by propagating the locally decomposed vectors throughout the layers to construct a global decomposition, including FFNs with linear activation approximations, and extending attributions to prediction-based values. Formally, the locally decomposed vector at layer
\ell
is expressed as:

\displaystyle\mathbf{\tilde{\text{$z$}}}^{\ell}_{i}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{LN}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{N}\left[\mathbf{x}^{\ell}_{i\Leftarrow k}+\mathbf{z}^{\ell}_{i\Leftarrow k}\right]\right),

(5)

where the normalized contribution is given by:

\displaystyle\mathrm{LN}\left(\mathbf{z}_{i}^{+\ell}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\underbrace{g_{z^{+\ell}_{i}}\left(\mathbf{z}_{i\Leftarrow k}^{+\ell}\right)}_{\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_{i\Leftarrow k}^{\ell}}+\boldsymbol{\beta}

(6)

\displaystyle g_{z^{+\ell}_{i}}\left(\mathbf{z}_{i\Leftarrow k}^{+\ell}\right)

\displaystyle:=\frac{\mathbf{z}_{i\Leftarrow k}^{+\ell}-m\left(\mathbf{z}_{i\Leftarrow k}^{+\ell}\right)}{s\left(\mathbf{z}_{i}^{+\ell}\right)}\odot\boldsymbol{\gamma}

DecompX explicitly integrates all encoder layer components, notably the non-linear feed-forward networks, to enhance representational fidelity. The
FFN
operation is defined as:

\displaystyle\mathbf{z}_{\text{FFN}}^{\ell}

\displaystyle=\text{FFN}(\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_{i}^{\ell})

(7)

\displaystyle=f_{\text{act}}(\underbrace{\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_{i}^{\ell}\mathbf{W}_{\text{FFN}}^{1}+\mathbf{b}_{\text{FFN}}^{1}}_{\bm{\zeta}_{i}^{\ell}})\mathbf{W}_{\text{FFN}}^{2}+\mathbf{b}_{\text{FFN}}^{2}

\displaystyle z_{\text{FFN},i}^{\ell}

\displaystyle=f_{\text{act}}^{(\zeta_{i}^{\ell})}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{N}\zeta_{i\Leftarrow k}^{\ell}\right)W_{\text{FFN}}^{2}+b_{\text{FFN}}^{2}

(8)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\underbrace{\theta(\zeta_{i}^{\ell})\odot\zeta_{i\Leftarrow k}^{\ell}+b_{\text{FFN}}^{2}}_{z_{\text{FFN},i\Leftarrow k}^{\ell}}

A propagation mechanism is implemented for decomposed token representations across multiple layers, preserving their distinct identities and preventing representation conflation:

\displaystyle x_{i}^{\ell+1}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{LN}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{N}\underbrace{\left[\tilde{z}_{i\Leftarrow k}^{\ell}+z_{\mathrm{FFN},i\Leftarrow k}^{\ell}\right]}_{z_{\mathrm{FFN}+,i\Leftarrow k}^{\ell}}\right)

(9)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\underbrace{gz_{\mathrm{FFN}+,i}^{\ell}\left(z_{\mathrm{FFN}+,i\Leftarrow k}^{\ell}\right)+\beta}_{x_{i\Leftarrow k}^{\ell+1}}

The final classification mechanism utilizes these decomposed vectors to quantitatively ascertain the precise directional impact (positive or negative) exerted by each input token upon specific target classes.
The established formulations within this section permit the derivation of
x_{i\Leftarrow k}^{\ell+1}
from its antecedent,
x_{i\Leftarrow k}^{\ell}
. Through the iterative application of this procedure across the entirety of the layered architecture, the final value,
x_{i\Leftarrow k}^{L+1}
, is consequently ascertained. For the task-specific classification head,

\displaystyle y_{c}=\sum_{k=1}^{N}y_{c\Leftarrow k}

(10)

where the term
y_{c\Leftarrow k}
quantifies the specific contribution of the
k{\text{th}}
token towards the overall prediction score.

4.  Methodology

Figure
1
portrays an overview of our proposed pipeline. We obtain the saliency scores using the token attribution methods discussed in Sec.
3
and employ frugalization via token filtration to generate a condensed yet semantically representative version of an input sample. This technique identifies and retains only the most salient tokens, thereby reducing input dimensionality while preserving the core meaning for the given task.

4.1.  Ranking via Saliency Scores

We define a task-specific scoring function
\varphi_{\tau}\in[\text{GlobEnc,\ DecompX}]
for a task
\tau
, which assigns a saliency score to each token in a given text,
i.e.,

\varphi_{\tau}:\mathcal{T}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n},

where
\mathcal{T}
denotes the space of the token sequences and
n
is the length of the sequence. For the input token sequence,
T=\langle t_{1},t_{2},\dots,t_{n}\rangle
, the scoring function produces a saliency vector,

\mathbf{s}=\varphi_{\tau}(T)=\langle s_{1},s_{2},\dots,s_{n}\rangle,\quad s_{i}\in\mathbb{R},

where each score
s_{i}
reflects the relative contribution of token
t_{i}
to the model’s output for the specified task.
Tokens are then ranked in monotone decreasing order of their attribution scores to obtain a permutation,
\pi
, formed of the token indices, such that,

s_{\pi(1)}\geq s_{\pi(2)}\geq\cdots\geq s_{\pi(n)}.

4.2.  Top-
k
Filtering

To construct the
frugalized representation
, we retain only the top
p=\left\lceil\frac{k}{100}\cdot n\right\rceil
tokens, where
k
is the retention percentage. The selected indices form the sequence,

{S}_{k}=\langle\pi(1),\pi(2),\dots,\pi(p)\rangle.

We reindex
S_{k}
in monotone increasing order to produce
{S}_{k\uparrow}
. The frugalized sequence
\mathbf{F}_{k}
is produced by restoring the selected tokens to their original order,
i.e.
,

{F}_{k}=\langle t_{i}\lvert i\in{S}_{k\uparrow}\rangle

ensuring that the temporal coherence of the sequence is preserved, while uninformative tokens are discarded.

We elucidate our frugalization approach with an example.
For the input sequence
{T}=

\langle
‘‘The’’
,
‘‘movie’’
,
‘‘was’’
,
‘‘good’’
,
‘‘,’’
,
‘‘and’’
,
‘‘I’’
,
‘‘liked’’
,
‘‘it’’
,
‘‘very’’
,
‘‘much’’
\rangle
, suppose the attribution scores for sentiment analysis prioritize content and sentiment words (
e.g.
,
‘‘movie’’
:
0.95
,
‘‘good’’
:
0.90
,
‘‘much’’
:
0.85
,
‘‘liked’’
:
0.80
). Upon filtering the top
k=40\%
(
p=4
) and reordering them according to their original relative order, we get the reduced sequence

{F}_{40}=\langle\texttt{``movie''},\texttt{``good''},\texttt{``liked''},\texttt{``much''}\rangle

where the original order is preserved to maintain syntactic coherence. The frugalized output
“movie good liked much”
is thus created, discarding the less salient tokens (
e.g.
,
‘‘The’’
,
‘‘was’’
, etc.) while retaining semantic essence.

5.  Experiment

5.1.  Tasks

We evaluate the efficacy of the token reduction by experimenting across four NLP tasks: sentiment analysis (text classification), summarization (text generation), commonsense question answering, and mathematical reasoning. These tasks span both discriminative and generative paradigms, enabling evaluation under diverse linguistic and cognitive demands of the LLM.

5.2.  Dataset

For sentiment analysis, we use the IMDb movie review dataset
Maas et al. 2011
, a binary sentiment analysis corpus comprising movie reviews labeled positive or negative. We use the Argilla News dataset
Ahmed et al. 2017
to evaluate abstractive summarization on news articles. For commonsense QA, we use the CosmosQA dataset
Huang et al. 2019

7.  Conclusion

The surging cost of large language models has often made it inaccessible to people in under-resourced environments, while increasing carbon footprint and inference time latency. Our work aims to bridge this gap by introducing prompt compression at a token-attribution level, which can reduce the cost of LLM inference and make it accessible to a wider range of users. We also reveal potential instances of task contamination that should be considered while evaluating models. We wish to explore adaptive frugalization strategies that dynamically adjust token retention based on task complexity and model confidence.

Limitations

As our token reduction pipeline heavily relies on attribution methods, misalignment between salience and true causal importance could produce suboptimal performance. The task specificity of the attribution methods also makes them unsuitable for certain tasks, such as reasoning, and evaluation in multi-task settings.

Ethical Consideration

As identified in our work, performance retention in certain tasks might stem from the model’s exposure to benchmark data during pretraining. This raises concerns about the validity of LLM evaluation and the need for contamination-free datasets. Prompt compression can also potentially remove safety-critical tokens and hence should be used with caution.
