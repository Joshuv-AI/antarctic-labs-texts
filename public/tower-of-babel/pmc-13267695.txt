Positive childhood experiences relate to prenatal mental health symptoms and diagnoses independent of childhood maltreatment
Authors: Jessica A. Marino, Angela Narayan, Anna M. Zhou, Ella-Marie P. Hennessey, Jenalee R. Doom, Benjamin L. Hankin, Elysia Poggi Davis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposePrenatal mental health problems often impair maternal functioning and offspring development. Maternal childhood maltreatment is a well-established risk factor for prenatal mental health problems; however, growing evidence supports the health-promoting role of positive childhood experiences (PCEs) for better prenatal mental health. Yet joint effects of childhood maltreatment and PCEs on prenatal mental illness diagnoses, in addition to symptoms, are not understood.MethodsThis study examined associations between childhood maltreatment and PCEs on prenatal mental health symptoms and clinical diagnoses in 234 pregnant individuals (M gestational age: 16.7 weeks). Childhood maltreatment, PCEs, and prenatal depression, anxiety, and posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) symptoms were assessed with standardized self-report measures. Prenatal Major Depressive Disorder (MDD) and Generalized Anxiety Disorder (GAD) during adulthood were assessed during pregnancy via structured clinical interviews.ResultsHigher levels of childhood maltreatment predicted greater symptom levels of depression, anxiety, and PTSD during pregnancy (effect sizes: 0.17–0.23) and elevated of prenatal MDD and adult GAD (ORs: 1.31, 1.32). In contrast, higher levels of PCEs predicted less symptom severity across all domains (effect sizes: −0.31 to −0.38) and lower MDD and GAD rates (ORs: 0.68, 0.70). When maltreatment and PCEs were modeled simultaneously, PCEs were the only significant predictor of fewer symptoms and diagnoses across all domains (effect sizes: −0.29 to −0.35; ORs: 0.68, 0.70), whereas effects of childhood maltreatment dropped to non-significance.ConclusionPositive childhood experiences have enduring effects, predicting both dimensional and categorical prenatal mental health above and beyond childhood maltreatment.

Introduction

Approximately one in five pregnant individuals report elevated depression or anxiety symptoms [1], [2], and reports of prenatal traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) can also be very high and range across samples depending on demographic characteristics [3], [4]. Prenatal mental health problems can influence fetal development with lasting implications for health and well-being [5], [6], [7], [8], [9]. Untreated mental health problems also often persist into the postpartum period, disrupting caregiving and infant development [10], [11].

Pregnancy is increasingly understood as a period of psychological reorganization. Pregnant individuals form and refine caregiving-related representations of self and others and mental health problems can emerge or reemerge depending on the extent to which previous experiences – both negative and positive – have been processed and resolved [12], [13]. Among these experiences, childhood maltreatment, which includes physical, emotional, or sexual abuse and neglect, is one of the most well-established developmental risk factors for elevated prenatal mental health symptoms during pregnancy. For instance, pregnant individuals who were abused or neglected during their own childhoods may experience painful and intrusive memories of these events when they become pregnant. Pregnancy elicits reflections on one’s past experiences with caregivers as one’s expectations form about how to care for a new baby. Normative physiological and physical sensations of pregnancy (e.g., bodily discomfort, sensations of the baby kicking) can also elicit memories of previous maltreatment, thereby rendering pregnant women with unresolved childhood maltreatment particularly vulnerable to psychological distress [14]. Indeed, pregnant individuals with maltreatment histories often report elevated symptoms of depression, anxiety, and PTSD during pregnancy ([15], [16], [17]).

Alternatively, growing evidence supports the role of pregnant individuals’ positive childhood experiences (PCEs) in associating with better prenatal mental health, even after accounting for the role of childhood maltreatment [3], [18]. PCEs are operationalized as favorable experiences and resources from birth to age 18 and often include having safe and supportive childhood caregivers, friends, teachers and neighbors; predictable home routines, and a positive self-concept [18], [19]. Systematic reviews and a recent meta-analysis find that PCEs function as promotive factors as evidenced by their independent associations with psychological well-being even after accounting for adversity exposure [20], [21]. Consistently, empirical findings for PCEs during pregnancy support that they are associated with fewer prenatal depression and PTSD symptoms [3], [22], [23], [18], including after accounting for childhood adversity, particularly for prenatal PTSD symptoms [3], [18]. This pattern not only highlights that PCEs can support prenatal mental health despite elevated childhood adversity but also reinforces the need to consider risk and promotive factors jointly.

One gap in this literature is that PCEs, childhood maltreatment, and prenatal mental health symptoms are often assessed via self-report [20]. This issue increases risk for informant bias because the same individual reports on both childhood experiences and mental health. Clinician-assessed diagnoses may reduce these biases, so more studies are needed that assess whether PCEs and childhood maltreatment relate to clinician-assessed mental health diagnoses during pregnancy. Furthermore, research that evaluates links between PCEs and childhood adversity with both dimensional and categorical indices of mental health risk is essential to determine whether maternal childhood experiences are associated with not only variability in distress but also with persistent and clinically impairing disorders.

To clarify how developmental risk and promotive pathways jointly shape prenatal mental health, the present study examined the contributions of childhood maltreatment and PCEs to prenatal mental health symptoms and diagnostic rates in a sample of 234 pregnant individuals. We hypothesized that childhood maltreatment would be associated with elevated depression, anxiety, and PTSD symptoms, as well as higher likelihood of prenatal major depressive disorder (MDD) and generalized anxiety disorder (GAD) diagnoses. We further hypothesized that PCEs would be associated with lower levels of prenatal depression, anxiety, and PTSD symptoms and lower likelihood of MDD and GAD, extending prior work by now testing whether promotive developmental experiences are associated with clinical diagnoses of mental health conditions during pregnancy. Finally, we hypothesized that PCEs would remain a significant predictor of both prenatal symptom severity and diagnostic rates above and beyond effects of childhood maltreatment, addressing whether observed promotive associations persist when their childhood adversity is also present.

Methods

In early gestation, pregnant individuals (M gestational age = 16.7 weeks, SD = 4.3) provided ratings of current depression, anxiety, and post-traumatic stress symptoms and completed a Structured Clinical Interview for DSM Disorders (SCID−5) to assess MDD and GAD; clinician-rated PTSD was not assessed as part of the current study. Participants also reported on both overall PCEs and cumulative maltreatment during childhood (from birth to 18 years). Study procedures were reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board for the Protection of Human Subjects at the University of Denver and the University of Colorado Anschutz Medical Campus. All participants provided informed consent.

Participants

234 pregnant individuals were included in the present study. Pregnant individuals were recruited prenatally from obstetrics and gynecology clinics in the Denver Metro Area as a part of the Care Project (see [24], [25] for details on the parent study). Participants were oversampled for elevated depression symptoms based on standard of care obstetric screening on the Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale (EPDS). Participants randomized to the treatment group of the parent study were not included in current analyses. Inclusion criteria for the current study included: (a) 18–45 years of age, (b) gestational age of less than 25 weeks at the time of recruitment, (c) English proficiency, and (d) singleton pregnancy. Exclusion criteria were current use of illicit drugs or methadone, major medical conditions requiring invasive treatments (i.e., chemotherapy, dialysis, blood transfusions), current or past symptoms of psychosis or mania based on the SCID−5, and/or current participation in interpersonal psychotherapy or cognitive behavioral therapy. Measures

Maternal childhood experiences

Childhood Maltreatment. Participants reported on maltreatment they experienced between birth and 18 years of age using the maltreatment subscale of the Adverse Childhood Experiences scale (ACEs; [26]). Maltreatment includes verbal/emotional, physical and sexual abuse as well as emotional and physical neglect ([26], [27]). Positively endorsed items were summed, and possible scores ranged from 0 to 5, with higher scores indicating more experiences of maltreatment during childhood.

Positive Childhood Experiences (PCEs). Participants reported on PCEs between birth and 18 years of age using the Benevolent Childhood Experiences scale (BCEs; [18]). Benevolent childhood experiences include relational and internal safety and security, positive and predictable quality of life, and interpersonal support. Positively endorsed items were summed for a total BCEs score which ranged from 0 to 10. The BCE scale was designed to include items that are accessible across diverse populations, has been widely used in adult samples [20], and has previously demonstrated good prenatal-to-postnatal test–retest reliability (r = .80, p < .01; [18]).

Prenatal mental health symptoms

Depression symptoms. Prenatal depression was measured using the EPDS, a 10-item measure validated for use in the perinatal period [28]. Participants rated each item using a 3-point Likert Scale. The EPDS was administered at baseline (M gestational age = 16.7 weeks). Total scores range from 0 to 30, with higher scores indicating greater depression symptoms. Scores greater than 10 indicate probable depression [28]. Internal consistency in the current sample was α = 0.90.

Anxiety symptoms. Prenatal anxiety was measured using the 20-item State Anxiety subscale of the State Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI; [29]) at baseline (10–24 weeks’ gestation). Participants used a 4-point Likert Scale ranging from 1 (not at all) to 4 (very much so) to rate anxiety symptoms over the past week. Scores ranged from 20 to 80, with higher scores indicating more anxiety; a cutoff score of 40 indicates probable clinical levels of anxiety [30]. Internal consistency in the current sample was α = 0.96.

PTSD symptoms. Prenatal post-traumatic stress symptoms were rated using the PCL−5 [31], a 20-item measure assessing post-traumatic stress symptom severity across the past month. Participants reported symptom severity on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (never) to 4 (extremely) at baseline. Possible scores range from 0 to 80, with higher scores indicating higher post-traumatic stress symptoms. A cutoff score of 33 indicates probable post-traumatic stress disorder [32]. Internal consistency in the current sample was 0.95.

Prenatal mental health diagnoses

Depression and anxiety diagnoses. Structured Clinical Interview for DSM−5 (SCID−5). The SCID−5 is a semi-structured interview that enables determination of participants’ mental health and diagnostic status according to DSM−5 criteria. Trained independent evaluators administered the SCID during early pregnancy to ascertain participants’ diagnostic status, including current MDD as well as GAD during adulthood, as well as for exclusionary criteria (current or past symptoms of mania or psychosis). 50% of SCIDs were randomly chosen for review, and interviewers demonstrated high reliability (k > .95 for MDD).

Demographic variables

Participants reported on their age, race/ethnicity, household income, number of people living in the household, whether they were cohabitating with a partner at baseline, and parity was recorded from medical records. To compute income-to-needs ratio (INR), the total reported household income was divided by the poverty threshold corresponding to the number of adults and children in the household for the year income was recorded [33]. Parity was obtained through medical record chart review.

Statistical analysis plan

Missing data

We conducted missing data analyses to characterize patterns of missingness across study measures. Continuous self-report and diagnoses showed minimal missingness. Self-report symptoms were available for N = 226–234 (97–100%) and diagnosis data were available for N = 231 (99%). Childhood maltreatment data were available for N = 208 (89%) and PCEs data were available for N = 195 (83%). Patterns of missingness were consistent with missing at random (MAR). Accordingly, analyses used all available data with full-information maximum likelihood (FIML) applied in continuous-outcome models [34].

Analyses

We employed a multi-step analytic approach. First, we examined distributions of predictors, given that measures of childhood experiences can exhibit non-normality; neither maltreatment nor PCEs exhibited substantial non-normality (skew < 2, kurtosis < 7). We then examined bivariate correlations among study variables to characterize basic associations, using Pearson’s correlations for continuous variables and point-biserial correlations for binary variables. Childhood maltreatment and PCEs were examined as predictors of prenatal symptom severity (depression, anxiety, PTSD) and diagnostic outcomes (MDD, GAD) using linear regression for continuous outcomes and logistic regression for diagnoses, adjusting for covariates. The effects of childhood maltreatment and PCEs were estimated separately and jointly.

Identification of covariates

Covariates were selected a priori based on theoretical relevance and prior literature linking sociodemographic factors to prenatal mental health and included maternal age, INR, cohabitation status, and first pregnancy or not [35].

Does childhood maltreatment impact symptoms and diagnosis?

To test whether childhood maltreatment was associated with prenatal symptoms level and diagnostic rates, separate regression models were estimated for depression, anxiety, and PTSD, and logistic regression models with a logit link were estimated for MDD and GAD diagnoses. All models were estimated after inclusion of identified covariates.

Do positive childhood experiences impact symptoms and diagnosis?

To examine whether benevolent childhood experiences (BCEs) were associated with prenatal symptom severity and diagnostic rates, analyses paralleled those conducted for childhood maltreatment.

Do adverse and positive childhood experiences jointly impact symptoms and diagnosis?

To determine whether BCEs would remain a significant predictor of both prenatal symptom severity and diagnostic rates, childhood maltreatment and BCEs were entered simultaneously in linear regression models for prenatal symptoms, and logistic models for diagnostic outcomes, adjusting for covariates.

Results with a p < 0.05 were interpreted as statistically significant. Regression models predicting continuous outcomes were conducted in R 4.5.1 [36] using lavaan [37] with the robust maximum likelihood (MLR) estimator to address non-normality. For continuous outcomes, standardized coefficients are reported as effect size estimates. Logistic regression models predicting dichotomous diagnostic outcomes were estimated in Mplus (Version 8.10; [38]), maintaining the same MLR estimator, with coefficients exponentiated to obtain odds ratios.

Do positive childhood experiences impact symptoms and diagnosis?

In contrast, higher levels of PCEs were associated with lower symptom levels across domains (R2 ≈ 13–20%; Fig. 1B) and reduced odds of both diagnoses (Fig. 2B) after adjustment for covariates.

Do adverse and positive childhood experiences jointly impact symptoms and diagnosis?

When childhood maltreatment and PCEs were included jointly, PCEs remained significantly associated with lower symptom levels (R2 ≈ 12–17%; Supplemental Figure 1) and reduced odds of diagnoses, while the associations with childhood maltreatment were attenuated and no longer significant for all models (Supplemental Figure 2).

Discussion

The present study advances understanding of how pregnant individuals’ childhood maltreatment and PCEs are linked to prenatal mental health, including both symptom severity and clinical diagnoses of depression and anxiety. We provide new evidence that both positive and adverse childhood experiences relate to current Major Depressive Disorder (MDD) and adult Generalized Anxiety Disorder (GAD). Childhood maltreatment was associated with 31% higher odds of MDD diagnosis and 32% higher odds of GAD diagnosis assessed in early pregnancy, whereas PCEs were related to 30% and 32% lower odds of experiencing MDD and GAD, respectively. Consistent with prior research, higher levels of childhood maltreatment were associated with more symptoms of prenatal depressive, anxiety, and PTSD [39], [15]. Further, consistent with prior research higher levels of PCEs were associated with lower symptoms of depression and PTSD with new evidence for associations with prenatal anxiety symptoms. Notably, when adverse and promotive experiences were examined simultaneously as predictors PCEs retained associations with better mental health, including diagnoses, independent of child maltreatment.

Our findings extend the literature in two ways. First, we demonstrate that greater maternal experiences of childhood maltreatment were associated with clinician-rated diagnosis during early pregnancy as well as symptoms. Second, we provide new evidence that PCEs are related to clinically significant depression and anxiety as well as anxiety symptoms in addition to replicating that PCEs are associated with depressive and PTSD symptoms [3], [22], [18]. Together, these findings suggest that maternal childhood experiences – both adverse and positive – not only relate to both vulnerability and resilience for prenatal mental health symptom severity but also influence the likelihood of diagnosed psychiatric disorders.

When modeled simultaneously, we observed that PCEs remain related to better mental health, including a lower likelihood of psychiatric diagnosis, even in the context of childhood maltreatment. This finding is noteworthy in the context of literature that has focused on the impact of adversity and provide important evidence for the beneficial influence of positive childhood experiences on subsequent mental health. Perhaps surprisingly, when modeled together, childhood maltreatment no longer predicted psychopathology. This may suggest that BCEs more strongly predict psychopathology in pregnancy. This finding is consistent with some prior research [40], but not others [3], [22], [18]. One possibility is that differences across samples in the developmental timing of PCEs and maltreatment influence how these experiences are embedded and activated across contexts. Future work is needed to clarify the relative impact of adverse and positive childhood experiences.

Early experiences may shape prenatal mental health through both psychological and biological pathways, primarily by calibrating stress responsivity, emotion regulation, and expectations of social support [41], [42]. Maltreatment may bias these systems toward heightened reactivity and reduced regulatory flexibility, increasing vulnerability to distress during pregnancy, including through reactivation of prior experiences and caregiving representations [13]. In parallel, PCEs may strengthen these same systems, promoting more regulated stress responding and stable expectations of support [18]. The persistence of positive effects alongside childhood maltreatment suggests that these strengths confer independent benefits, even in the presence of developmental risk.

Clinical implications

These findings have implications for prenatal mental health assessment. Specifically, the attenuation of maltreatment associations once PCEs were considered indicates that exposure to childhood adversity alone may not sufficiently characterize vulnerability to prenatal mental health challenges. Rather, risk and resilience during pregnancy are better understood within a broader developmental context that includes both adverse and positive experiences. Assessing both developmental risk and resources may therefore allow clinicians to contextualize vulnerability more precisely and identify individuals at elevated or reduced likelihood of disorder during pregnancy.

Strengths and limitations

The present study has several methodological strengths, including the use of both self-report measures and diagnostic interview data, capturing both subjective severity and clinically significant psychopathology. In addition, by jointly modeling childhood maltreatment and PCEs in a relatively large prenatal sample, the study was able to estimate their unique and overlapping contributions to prenatal mental health, rather than treating both experiences in isolation. In light of these strengths, limitations warrant consideration. First, childhood maltreatment and positive childhood experiences were assessed retrospectively via self-report. Although prior work suggests that retrospective reports of early experiences demonstrate reasonable validity over time( [43], [44]). Prospective longitudinal designs will be important for strengthening temporal and causal inferences. Lastly, given that prenatal mental health was assessed cross-sectionally, longitudinal studies are needed to clarify how developmental history is related to symptom onset, trajectories, and potential persistence into the postpartum period.

Conclusion

In sum, the present findings indicate that PCEs are clinically relevant for prenatal mental health when considered alongside childhood maltreatment. Our findings suggest that PCEs reflect enduring influences across development and are promotive for prenatal psychopathology symptom severity and clinically diagnosed depression and generalized anxiety disorder over the lifespan. These findings extend predominantly risk-focused models of prenatal psychopathology and suggest that vulnerability during pregnancy is more accurately understood within a broader developmental context that includes both adversity and protective experiences. Together, this work highlights the importance of considering positive developmental histories in understanding clinically significant psychopathology beyond childhood maltreatment exposure alone.
