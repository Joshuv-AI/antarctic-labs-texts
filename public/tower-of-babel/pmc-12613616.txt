Broadening the microbiota–diet–sleep–fertility axis: implications for public health
Authors: Tathiana A Alvarenga, Bianca C Schimenes, Sergio Tufik, Monica L Andersen
Published: 2025

To the editor

In an era marked by rising infertility rates and the growing impact of modern lifestyle factors on health, the review by Ahmad et al. [1] offers valuable insights into the interactions between diet, intestinal microbiota and female reproductive outcomes. The contrast drawn between the health-promoting Mediterranean diet and the standard Western diet provides a useful framework for both clinical practice and public health strategies targeting infertility.

We write to suggest the inclusion of two complementary dimensions to enhance the translational reach and public health relevance of the proposed model. First, this axis is equally important for male reproductive health. The recent review by Cavalhas-Almeida et al. [2] highlights how sleep disturbances, particularly insomnia and circadian rhythm disruption, can negatively affect male fertility by impairing sperm quality, hormonal balance, and gut microbial diversity. These findings are consistent with previous studies that have linked obstructive sleep apnea to changes in the microbiota, erectile dysfunction, and reduced semen parameters [3]. Dietary interventions that support microbial health, especially when combined with sleep regulation, may yield significant reproductive benefits for men. Second, chronic sleep deprivation is a powerful yet often overlooked contributary factor to microbiota-mediated reproductive dysfunction. Preclinical evidence shows that sleep restriction induces dysbiosis, systemic inflammation, and premature ovarian insufficiency, ultimately reducing ovarian reserve [4]. Oxidative stress and mitochondrial damage caused by sleep loss directly impair oocyte quality. Alvarenga et al. [5] observed that sleep deprivation can reduce the number of viable sperm by up to 50%, reinforcing the reproductive impact of this factor in both sexes. These effects may be further exacerbated by proinflammatory diets, creating a synergistic mechanism of reproductive impairment.

The consideration of infertility through this integrated model, which encompasses microbiota, diet, sleep, and reproductive function in both sexes, reveals concrete opportunities for targeted public health interventions. Given the growing socioeconomic burden of infertility, lifestyle-based strategies, such as healthy eating and sleep promotion, offer accessible and scalable alternatives and deserve greater attention. These approaches are particularly relevant in settings with limited access to assisted reproductive technologies.

We commend Ahmad et al. [1] for their contribution to the discussion on microbiota and female fertility. Incorporating male reproductive health and sleep patterns into this conceptual framework could broaden its clinical applicability and potential policy impact. Therefore, we encourage future research to adopt integrated models of reproductive care grounded in microbial, metabolic, and behavioral science. Such research could provide evidence that significantly improves the understanding of infertility and enables the provision of more comprehensive care to couples facing challenges in conceiving.
