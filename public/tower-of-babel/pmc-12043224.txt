Effect of osseous defect on periodontal ligament stress under normal masticatory force: A three-dimensional finite element analysis of mandibular second molar
Authors: Bhavya Shetty, Safiya Fatima Khan, Khadijathul Irfana, Manjusha Nambiar
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Background:Finite element model (FEM) offers an ideal method for the accurate modeling of tooth periodontium with its complicated three-dimensional (3D) geometry. The present study aimed to assess periodontal ligament (PDL) stress under normal masticatory force in the mandibular second molar with and without osseous defect at two different angulations.Materials and Methods:Cone-beam computed tomography was used to collect 3D information on the mandibular second molar. Four 3D models were designed using FEM. The first was a mandibular second molar with normal bone support. The second, third, and fourth models had three-wall defects where the mesial wall had bone loss up to 1/3rd, 2/3rd, and complete root length, respectively. Vertical and Oblique forces were applied to each model and statistical analysis was carried out using Von Mises stress analysis.Results:Von Mises stress on PDL was seen in the cervical area when the bone was lost up to one-third of root length. As bone loss increases, the stress is concentrated in the apical region and it increases with an increase in bone defect. Maximum PDL stress was 1.84 × 10−05 megapascal (MPa) under vertical force and 1.0 × 10−04 under oblique force. Maximum stress on cortical bone was 14.019 MPa under vertical force and 45.939 MPa under oblique force and the values were statistically significant.Conclusion:Stress on PDL and cortical bone was four times higher under oblique force compared to vertical force. As periodontal support reduces, stress increases and gets concentrated on the apex, leading to apical extension of resorption. Teeth with compromised periodontium are more predisposed to occlusal trauma.

INTRODUCTION

The periodontal ligament (PDL) is a specialized connective tissue responsible for the absorption and distribution of normal masticatory and other abnormal forces to the alveolar bone.[12] The functions of PDL are broadly categorized as supportive, nutritive, sensory, formative, and protective functions.[3] The principal function of the PDL is to support the teeth, and it is the most crucial component of the periodontium in terms of deformation. It converts the mechanical signals into various biological signals, leading to a series of biological reactions that are of utmost importance for maintaining the homeostasis of periodontal tissues.[4]

The presence of normal bone architecture around the root aids in the effective transmission of these stresses into the surrounding alveolar bone.[2] If the amount of loading is too high, the bone’s self-repair processes cannot keep up with the growing damage, resulting in bone resorption.[56] Periodontal disease often leads to alteration in the morphological pattern of alveolar bone which subsequently results in periodontal tissue breakdown and bone resorption. In such cases, the stress distribution is hampered and concentrated elsewhere leading to an area prone to more bone resorption.

Since the beginning of the 20th century, the role of occlusal trauma as the cause of periodontal disease has been studied. Glickman referred to occlusion as “the Achilles heel in dental education” because of the sparsity of information and the ability of individuals to agree on what should be taught and how it should be done. The World Health Organization in 1978 defined trauma from occlusion as “damage in the periodontium caused by stress on the teeth produced directly or indirectly by teeth of the opposing jaw.”[7] The World Workshop 2017 classified the traumatic occlusal forces on the periodontium into primary trauma from occlusion, secondary trauma from occlusion, and orthodontic forces.[8]

Although more than a century has elapsed since the first publication of Karolyi which implicated the forces of occlusion in the behavior of periodontal tissues, deep-rooted controversy about the role of occlusion in the development and progression of periodontal disease remains. Various studies were conducted to study the mechanical properties of the attachment apparatus. Earlier, most of the occlusal studies in the finite element methodology were performed in two-dimensional (2-D) models. Although 2D models are simpler, easier to build, and less time-consuming compared to the three-dimensional (3D) model, they do not represent the complexity of the real problem and have a few limitations. In contrast, 3D modeling has several advantages, such as better visualization of internal areas, complete assessment, and more complex analysis, which yield accurate results at a greater computational cost.[9] Finite element analysis (FEA) has now become widely accepted as noninvasive and an excellent tool for studying biomechanics and the influence of mechanical forces on biological systems. It is a well-known method that can solve problems of complex geometry and loading conditions that are not easily solved analytically.[10] Moga et al. assessed compressive stress on PDL under orthodontic load on healthy and periodontally compromised second mandibular premolar using finite element analysis. It was concluded that in the compromised periodontium, 0.2 N is found to be safe, while higher forces were more endangering to the periodontium.[11] Huajie and Zhao have assessed PDL stress in the mandibular second molar with varying levels of horizontal bone loss in the distal wall of the tooth using FEA. They found that PDL stress increased as the alveolar bone loss increased. It shows that the supportive function of PDL is reduced after 40% of alveolar bone loss.[12] Another study by S Wang and Jiang Sun assessed the influence of three-wall defect on PDL stress under normal masticatory load. FEA model was created and three wall defects were simulated at 1/3, 2/3, and 3/3 of the root by modifying the elastic modulus of the element on the defect area. It was found that even shallow three-wall defects cause notable changes in the PDL, hence it should be considered a focus of clinical research, and the treatment should be given as early as possible.[13]

After a thorough literature review, it was found that the prevalence of angular bony defects is higher in the mandibular posterior teeth, especially the second mandibular molar.[14151617] To the best of our knowledge, the evidence available for the influence of the direction of the force and comparison of its effect on the tooth with a healthy and compromised condition in the second mandibular molar is limited. A study conducted by Abuelenain et al. assessed the PDL stress under vertical, horizontal, and oblique loading on Full ceramic and porcelain fused to model crowns replacing the mandibular second molar. There was no statistically significant difference between the two restorations, but the maximum stress was found at vertical and horizontal loading, whereas the least stress was produced under oblique load.[18] This was against the result of most of the other studies done on different teeth.[192021] Therefore, the current study was designed to provide clarity regarding the impact of load direction on the mandibular second molar. The research aim was to assess the PDL stress under normal masticatory force in a mandibular second molar tooth with and without osseous defect at two different angulations using finite element analysis.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

The present study was conducted in the Department of Periodontology from August 2022 to October 2022. This study was approved by the University Ethics Committee and was carried out in compliance with the 2013 revision of the 1964 Helsinki Declaration. A cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) scanner (KAVO, Carestream, USA) was used to screen the imaging data for the following inclusion criteria. The mandibular jaw of the patient aged between 20 and 40 years, with all the teeth intact without any horizontal or vertical bone loss or any other defects or impaction, with a bone mineral density of 850–1250 HU was included. The model bone belongs to D2 type bone classified by Misch.[22] After determining that a patient’s imaging data satisfied the inclusion requirements, the case was chosen, the patient was contacted, and informed consent was obtained. The study was carried out retrospectively as we used the available CBCT of a single healthy patient and designed the defect according to our objectives. The CBCT scan data were processed using mimics 8.11 software and only the region of interest (second molar) was extracted. Following this, four models were designed using finite element model (FEM). The first was a mandibular second molar with normal bone support. The second, third, and fourth Models had three-wall defects where the mesial wall was having bone loss up to 1/3rd, 2/3rd, and complete root length, respectively.[13] The occlusion was assumed at three contact points resenting the centric occlusion.[18] Tetrahedral elements were incorporated into the meshing of four 3D solid objects using Hypermesh 2019. Once all four models were prepared, they were exported to ANSYS Inc., Canonsburg PA, USA software (Ansys 2017.2) for further analysis.

According to the model, there were different numbers of nodes and elements [Table 1]. The mesiodistal alveolar bone cross-section was used as the reference point for all models, and no translation or rotation was permitted. As the boundary conditions for all models, all nodes on the bottom surface of the cortical bone were limited in all directions (X, Y, and Z), preventing rigid body displacement, material properties such as Elastic modulus and poisons ratio are defined for bone, teeth, dentin, enamel, cementum, and PDL. Material properties (Young’s modulus, Poisson’s ratio) of enamel, dentin, cortical bone, cancellous bone, and PDL were as follows:[19] enamel: 41 GPa, 0.30; dentin: 18.6 GPa, 0.31; cortical bone:13.7 GPa, 0.30; cancellous bone: 1.37 GPa, 0.30; PDL: 0.0689 GPa, 0.45. The structures in the model are assumed to be homogenous, isotropic, and possess linear elasticity [Figure 1].[19]

DISCUSSION

Teeth and the supporting structures are subjected to severe occlusal forces during mastication. The presence of tissue fluids and the arrangement of PDL fibers are in such a way that these forces are easily accommodated without any periodontal destruction. In the current study, a 3D FEM was constructed by Ansys software using a CBCT image scan of the mandible. The study was performed to assess the PDL stress on the tooth with normal and compromised periodontium under normal masticatory force at different angulations.

Few studies evaluated the stress on PDL under healthy and compromised periodontium. Chen and Psai have investigated the von Mises stress in mandibular molar, canine, and premolar teeth with different levels of alveolar bone support under normal occlusal forces of 100 N. It was shown that Von Mises stress and displacement were greater in teeth with decreased bone support. As the bone support decreased, it was found that the stress was centered at the root apex.[23] Another study by Geramy and Faghihi evaluated the PDL stress on the maxillary central incisor with varying bone height. They created 5 models, out of which 1 had normal bone height and the remaining had reduced alveolar bone support. A force of 1.5 N was applied on the palatal side in the palatolabial direction. According to the study, the alveolar bone loss causes greater stress on the PDLs.[25] Gupta et al. evaluated the stress distribution in the maxillary central incisor with a varying height of alveolar bone and thickness of PDL under different occlusal loads. It was concluded that the stress in the alveolar bone decreases as the thickness of PDL increases. Furthermore, it was shown that the maximum stress was distributed at the apex of the central incisor and decreased as it moved cervically.[26] All of the aforementioned studies show that bone loss causes greater stress in the PDL and bone, which is consistent with the findings of our investigation. However, none of the studies are comparable as the design and the objectives of the study were different for each study. Even though every study shows that diminished alveolar bone support increases periodontal stress, the present study varies as it compares the stress at normal and compromised conditions at different directions of occlusal load.

Yang and Chung analyzed the stress profile of mandibular premolars with four levels of bone support under axial and nonaxial loading.[24] A tooth with four levels of bone support was simulated and the 90 N load was applied axially as well as at 45° to the inner inclines of the cusp to simulate vertical and lateral stress, respectively. It was noted that Von Mises stress was highest at the cervical enamel region under nonaxial loading irrespective of bone loss. The stress concentration shifted apically along the root as bone support reduces under nonaxial loading which can result in the apical extension of the defect. This was consistent with our work, in which we found that the von Mises stress was 4–5 times higher under oblique force compared to vertical force regardless of bone loss. However, Abuelenain et al.’s analysis showed the reverse outcome, with the greatest stress found under vertical and horizontal loading and little stress under oblique load.[18] Future research may be conducted to provide statistical comparisons of different models under different loading conditions.

A tooth with periodontal bone loss experiences secondary occlusal trauma with occlusal forces that may not be abnormal by itself, but rather excessive for the reduced periodontium. As the adaptive capacity of the PDL exceeds, the traumatic occlusal forces can reduce perfusion causing ischemia and necrosis of PDL. Secondary occlusal trauma could act as an aggravating factor for the progression of periodontal disease initiated due to dental biofilm. Therefore, this study highlights the significance of early periodontal treatment and occlusal correction before bone loss exceeds one-third of its root length to prevent the bone defect from extending further apically in patients with periodontal bone loss.

CONCLUSION

Within the limitations of the study, it was found that the Von Mises stress on PDL was seen in the cervical area when the bone loss was up to one-third of the root length. As the bone loss increased the stress was concentrated in the apical region and it increased with an increase in bone defect. In addition, it was discovered that oblique loading increased PDL stress and alveolar bone stress by four times compared to vertical loading.

This study suggests that periodontal treatment and occlusal correction should be done at the earliest before bone loss exceeds one-third of its root length, as additional bone loss causes the stress concentration to shift apically under nonaxial or oblique loading, which may cause the defect to extend apically. Furthermore, as prospective research utilizing clinical trials is not ethically authorized, FEA plays a critical role in understanding the link between occlusion and its effects on the periodontium in various situations. Since the model’s dimensions are life-size, making comparisons and interpretations accurate and reliable. This research analyzed the stress under static loading conditions. Further research has to be focused on the dynamic loading condition of mastication, which would better mimic the actual clinical situation.
