Estimated glucose disposal rate stratifies mortality risk, especially in younger adults with cardiovascular-liver-metabolic overlap
Authors: Mengjie Zhao, Yan Yan, Mengxuan Li, Miaoran Wang, Fang Lu, Qiuyan Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe cardiovascular-liver-metabolic (CLM) overlap population faces an elevated risk of mortality, yet prognostic tools integrating dynamic insulin resistance (IR) remain limited. This study aimed to evaluate the prognostic impact of estimated glucose disposal rate (eGDR) on mortality within the CLM overlap population.MethodsAn analysis of a prospectively linked national dataset was conducted on 1,126 eligible NHANES participants (1999–2018) with concomitant cardiovascular disease (CVD) and metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD). eGDR was calculated using clinical parameters. Multivariable Cox regression, restricted cubic splines, Kaplan-Meier survival analysis, and causal mediation models were employed to assess mortality associations and the mediating role of inflammation.ResultsOver a median follow-up of 85 months, 453 deaths occurred, including 155 cardiovascular deaths. Lower eGDR showed a borderline inverse association with cardiovascular mortality (HR = 0.84, 95% CI: 0.70–1.01, P = 0.065). eGDR exhibited a threshold-dependent non-linear association with all-cause mortality (P for non-linearity = 0.001), with lower mortality below the inflection point of 5.5901 mg/kg/min (HR = 0.78, 95% CI: 0.66–0.91) and higher mortality above it (HR = 1.16, 95% CI: 0.99–1.36). The pathway from eGDR through systemic inflammation response index (SIRI) to mortality was statistically detectable for all-cause mortality (indirect effect = 6.530, 95% CI: 2.146–11.878), and SIRI mediated 11.3% of the total effect for cardiovascular mortality. Notably, the reduction in mortality risk per 1-unit increase in eGDR was significantly stronger in patients aged < 60 years (all-cause mortality HR = 0.71, 95% CI: 0.60–0.85, P = 0.003; P for interaction = 0.001).ConclusionIn the CLM overlap population, eGDR demonstrates a linear association with cardiovascular mortality and a non-linear association with all-cause mortality, partially mediated by systemic inflammation. Its enhanced prognostic value in younger adults highlights its potential for early risk stratification and could inform targeted interventions focusing on IR-driven pathways.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12876-026-05175-9.Keywords: Cardiovascular-liver-metabolic overlap, Estimated glucose disposal rate, Systemic inflammation response index, All-cause mortality, Cardiovascular mortality

Introduction

Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), redefined from non-alcoholic fatty liver disease in 2023 [1, 2], affects 14–32% of adults worldwide [1]. Cardiovascular disease (CVD), MASLD, and type 2 diabetes (T2D) are leading causes of mortality that share underlying pathogenic mechanisms and frequently co-occur through common pathways [3, 4]. MASLD pathological severity is strongly positively associated with cardiometabolic risk factor prevalence and CVD severity [5, 6].

The global prevalence of MASLD among adults is 38%, surging to 58.8%–72.7% in individuals with T2D, with projections reaching 55.4% by 2040 [7]. Among patients with both MASLD and T2D, over one-third have prior CVD [8], and CVD remains the primary mortality cause in MASLD [9]. Subjects with ≥ 4 metabolic disorders represent the highest-risk MASLD cohort [10], yet MASLD alone may not significantly increase mortality in individuals with cardiometabolic risk factors [11]. These findings highlight the critical need to elucidate the role of cardiometabolic status in MASLD prognosis.

The pathophysiology of MASLD involves multiple factors including chronic inflammation, obesity, insulin resistance (IR), and hepatic lipid accumulation [12]. These elements can individually or collectively drive CVD initiation and advancement, though their exact pathogenic mechanisms remain incompletely characterized [12]. Emerging evidence further implicates sympathetic nervous system (SNS) overactivation as a mechanistic link between MASLD and cardiovascular disease, wherein hepatic steatosis triggers sympathetic nerve activity through portal hypertension and RAAS activation, establishing a neuro-cardio-hepatic axis that promotes cardiac dysfunction in parallel with inflammatory cascades [13]. The estimated glucose disposal rate (eGDR), integrating hypertension, glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), and waist circumference (WC), is a validated index of insulin sensitivity [14] that predicts mortality in CVD, NAFLD, and metabolic syndrome [15–17]. Its prognostic value in overlapping cardiovascular-liver-metabolic (CLM) disorders remains unexplored. Chronic inflammation is a core mechanism linking hepatic and cardiovascular dysfunction [18–20]. This study evaluates whether eGDR predicts all-cause and cardiovascular mortality in CLM overlap patients, and whether systemic inflammation mediates this relationship.

Methods

Study population

This investigation utilized data from the NHANES, a continuous cross-sectional surveillance system representing the US non-institutionalized population. Its principal aim involves evaluating population health-nutritional metrics. The NHANES Institutional Review Board authorized the study protocol. We consolidated ten survey waves (1999–2018) to examine eGDR’s relationships with all-cause and cardiovascular death risks in individuals with coexisting CLM disorders. Individuals were excluded based on the following stringent criteria: (1) Missing survival status or follow-up time; (2) Age < 18 years; (3) Not meeting MASLD diagnostic criteria; (4) No clinical diagnosis of CVD; (5) Missing data for eGDR components or systemic inflammation response index (SIRI). After screening (Figure S1), 1,126 participants were included.

Assessment of cardiovascular, liver, and metabolic status

CLM overlap was defined as concomitant presence of CVD and MASLD. CVD history was ascertained by self-reported physician diagnosis using standardized NHANES questionnaires. Participants were specifically asked whether they had ever received a diagnosis from a healthcare professional of myocardial infarction, angina pectoris, coronary heart disease, congestive heart failure, or stroke. To validate FLI as a steatosis marker, we examined its correlation with controlled attenuation parameter (CAP) measurements from vibration-controlled transient elastography in the NHANES 2017–2018 cycle (n = 3,692 adults with available VCTE data, irrespective of CLM cohort eligibility). FLI demonstrated strong correlation with CAP (weighted r = 0.92, P < 0.001) (Figure S2) and acceptable discriminative performance for CAP-defined steatosis [22] (CAP ≥ 248 dB/m: AUC = 0.83, 95% CI: 0.81–0.84) (Figure S3).

Assessment of eGDR

The equation for determining eGDR (mg/kg/min) is presented as: eGDR = 21.158 - (0.09 × WC) - (3.407 × HT) - (0.551 × HbA1c). In this formula, HT denotes the existence of hypertension, with a value of 1 for “yes” and 0 for “no” [23].

Clinical outcomes

The main objective of the study was to assess all-cause mortality, while the secondary goal was to evaluate cardiovascular mortality. Mortality outcomes were ascertained through linkage to the National Death Index (NDI) via the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS). Follow-up extended from the examination date to December 31, 2019. All-cause mortality was defined by NDI-confirmed death from any cause. Cardiovascular mortality was defined using ICD-10 codes I00–I09, I11, I13, I20–I51, and I60–I69 (diseases of the heart, cerebrovascular diseases, and related conditions), consistent with prior NHANES mortality studies.

Data collection

Data collected for this study encompassed three primary domains: (1) demographic characteristics, including age, sex, race/ethnicity, poverty-income ratio (PIR), marital status, educational attainment, smoking behavior, and alcohol consumption history; (2) anthropometric measurements, specifically BMI and WC; and (3) laboratory parameters, comprising HbA1c, TG, HDL-C, gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT), uric acid (UA), creatinine, neutrophil count, lymphocyte count, monocyte count, and estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR). The eGFR was calculated using the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration (CKD-EPI) equation [24]. The SIRI was defined by the mathematical relationship: SIRI = (neutrophil count × monocyte count) / lymphocyte count [25].

Statistical analysis

We established the optimal eGDR stratification cutoff via R’s maxstat package [26], employing maximally selected rank statistics to dichotomize participants into low/high groups. The optimal cutoff was validated using 1,000 bootstrap resamples, in each of which the cutoff was re-estimated and the HR was recalculated. It should be noted that dichotomous analyses based on cutoff values determined by data‑driven methods may overestimate apparent effect sizes; therefore, the results of such categorical analyses should be regarded as exploratory findings. Using this cutoff, baseline demographic and clinical characteristics were compared between eGDR groups. In addition, we also divided eGDR into quartiles to reduce overfitting caused by data-driven maxstat cutoffs. Categorical data are summarized using weighted counts and percentages (%), whereas continuous measures are expressed as weighted median values with 95% CIs. Pooled-cycle weights were constructed per NHANES analytical guidelines: for 1999–2002 (4-year cycle), weight = 2/10 × WTMEC4YR; for 2003–2018 (2-year cycles), weight = 1/10 × WTMEC2YR. Missing data were addressed using complete-case (listwise deletion) analysis, the default procedure in R’s regression functions. Missing data rates are detailed in Supplementary Table 1. Core exposure (eGDR) and mediator (SIRI) variables had 0% missing. This approach ensured identical analytic samples across all models.

We employed multivariable Cox regression (Model 1, adjusted for age and gender; Model 2, further adjusted for race, education level, marital status, and PIR based on Model 1; Model 3, additionally adjusted for smoking, drinking, BMI, UA, and eGFR based on Model 2) to assess independent relationships of eGDR with both all-cause and cardiovascular death. To investigate potential non-linear eGDR–mortality correlations, we utilized restricted cubic splines (RCS) with 4 knots placed at the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles of the eGDR distribution (1.327, 3.727, 5.049, and 8.515 mg/kg/min, respectively). Hazard ratios in RCS plots were referenced to the second knot (35th percentile, eGDR = 3.727 mg/kg/min). The full 95% confidence intervals were displayed as shaded ribbons across the entire eGDR exposure range. To assess multicollinearity in Cox models, variance inflation factors (VIF) and tolerance statistics were calculated; consistent with established criteria [27], VIF < 10 and tolerance ≥ 0.2 indicated no significant multicollinearity. The proportional-hazards assumption was evaluated using Schoenfeld residual tests. No significant violations were detected for the key exposure variable (eGDR) or the primary model (global test P > 0.05).

We plotted Kaplan-Meier curves depicting cumulative survival by eGDR stratum, and conducted intergroup comparisons via log-rank testing. Stratified analyses were performed by age, sex, educational level, BMI, PIR, eGFR, smoking status, and alcohol consumption history; interaction terms between these covariates and eGDR were formally tested.

Due to the lack of available software supporting survey-weighted causal mediation with Cox proportional hazards models, we performed this analysis using standard (non-survey-weighted) methods as an exploratory mechanistic investigation. We employed VanderWeele’s product-of-coefficients framework [28, 29]: path a (eGDR to SIRI) was estimated by linear regression, paths b (SIRI to mortality, adjusting for eGDR) and c’ (eGDR to mortality, adjusting for SIRI) by Cox proportional hazards models. Effects are reported on the log-hazard scale. Model 3 covariates were included in all three regression components to address exposure–outcome, exposure–mediator, and mediator–outcome confounding. Confidence intervals were derived using the quasi-Bayesian approach as implemented in the mediation R package. Given that eGDR and SIRI were measured concurrently at baseline, this analysis is hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory causal inference. Sensitivity analyses were performed to address potential circularity between eGDR components and metabolic parameters: (i) excluding participants with diabetes; (ii) stratifying by diabetes status; and (iii) omitting BMI from adjustment models; (iv) evaluating incremental predictive value by adding categorical eGDR to the base model with calculation of C-statistic, Net Reclassification Index (NRI), and Integrated Discrimination Improvement (IDI); (v) comparing eGDR with the triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index (ln[triglycerides (mg/dL) × fasting glucose (mg/dL) / 2]) as an alternative insulin resistance marker. The optimal TyG cutoff was determined using the same maximally selected rank statistics approach; (vi) employing the Hepatic Steatosis Index (HSI) as an alternative steatosis marker [30] (HSI = 8 × (ALT/AST) + BMI (+ 2 if female, + 2 if diabetes mellitus), with steatosis defined as HSI > 36), redefining MASLD and reconstructing the CLM cohort using HSI, and repeating primary analyses. We performed all statistical analyses using R v4.6.0, with significance set at P < 0.05 (two-tailed).

Results

Baseline characteristics stratified by eGDR

This analysis comprised 1,126 subjects fulfilling predetermined eligibility requirements. The optimal cutoff value for eGDR, determined by survival analysis, was 2.49. The maxstat-derived cutoff was validated using 1,000 bootstrap resamples. The bootstrap median cutoff was 2.49 (IQR: 2.44–4.82) and the median HR was 0.46 (95% CI: 0.35–0.85), with the protective direction preserved in all successful iterations, supporting the stability of the categorical association (Table S2). Based on this cutoff, the cohort was stratified into two subgroups: a low eGDR group (eGDR ≤ 2.49, n = 151) and a high eGDR group (eGDR > 2.49, n = 975) (Figure S4). The high eGDR group had significantly lower values for WC, BMI, HbA1c, UA levels, and the SIRI compared to the low eGDR group (Table 1).

Survival curve analysis

This study had a median follow-up period of 85 months (IQR 43–135). During follow-up, 453 all-cause deaths occurred, including 155 cardiovascular deaths. Kaplan-Meier survival curves depicted in Supplementary Fig. 5A and Supplementary Fig. 5B demonstrate significantly different survival outcomes between patient groups stratified by eGDR level. These between-group differences in survival distribution achieved statistical significance for both all-cause mortality (log-rank P = 0.042) and cardiovascular mortality (log-rank P = 0.012).

Association between eGDR and all-cause and cardiovascular mortality

In multivariable-adjusted models, continuous eGDR demonstrated no significant link to all-cause death in the overlapping CLM cohort (P > 0.05); whereas categorical analysis revealed markedly reduced all-cause death risk in the high versus low eGDR strata (HR = 0.54, 95% CI: 0.37–0.77; Table 2). By quartiles, Q3 and Q4 showed significantly lower mortality versus Q1 (Q3: HR 0.51, 95% CI: 0.32–0.79, P = 0.003; Q4: HR 0.61, 95% CI: 0.38–0.98, P = 0.043). RCS modeling disclosed a non-linear association for continuous eGDR with all-cause death (P for non-linearity = 0.001; Fig. 1A), with an inflection point identified at 5.5901 (Table S3).

For cardiovascular mortality, continuous eGDR was inversely associated with risk in Cox models: the unadjusted model showed an HR of 0.91 (95% CI: 0.84–0.98, P = 0.017), while the fully adjusted model (Model 3) yielded a directionally consistent but no longer statistically significant HR of 0.84 (95% CI: 0.70–1.01, P = 0.065) (Table 2). Quartile analysis revealed attenuated associations (Q3: HR 0.44, 95% CI: 0.18–1.08, P = 0.074; Q4: HR 0.53, 95% CI: 0.21–1.32, P = 0.175), likely reflecting limited CVD events and wider confidence intervals. RCS analysis further confirmed a linear inverse trend (P for non-linearity > 0.05; Fig. 1B).

Collinearity diagnostics indicated no significant multicollinearity, with all VIF below 2 (Tables S4–S5).

Subgroup analyses

Stratified analyses by age, sex, education, PIR, smoking status, alcohol use, BMI, and eGFR assessed heterogeneity in the eGDR-mortality association (Figs. 2 and 3). For all-cause mortality, a significant interaction existed only with age (P for interaction = 0.001): among participants aged < 60 years, higher eGDR was associated with a 29% lower all-cause mortality risk (HR = 0.71, 95% CI: 0.60–0.85, P = 0.003). Similarly, for cardiovascular mortality, among participants aged < 60 years, higher eGDR was associated with a reduced risk of cardiovascular mortality (HR = 0.65, 95% CI: 0.50–0.85, P = 0.001).

Exploratory mediation analysis

We assessed whether SIRI mediates the eGDR–mortality association using standard (non-survey-weighted) methods as an exploratory mechanistic analysis. For all-cause mortality (Fig. 4A), the indirect effect of eGDR via SIRI was 6.530 (95% CI: 2.146–11.878) on the log-hazard scale. The direct effect (P = 0.210) and total effect (P = 0.092) were non-significant, consistent with the non-linear eGDR–mortality relationship. Given the non-significant total effect in the linear model and the exploratory nature of this analysis, a proportion-mediated metric is not methodologically interpretable and is not reported.

Mediation analysis of SIRI in the association between eGDR and all-cause mortality (A) and cardiovascular mortality (B)

For cardiovascular mortality (Fig. 4B), the total effect was in the expected protective direction (P = 0.020). The indirect effect via SIRI was 20.724 (95% CI: 5.468–43.799), corresponding to 11.3% of the total effect on the log-hazard scale.

Sensitivity analyses

We performed five sensitivity analyses. First, excluding diabetic participants attenuated but did not reverse the associations (CVD mortality HR = 0.78, 95% CI: 0.57–1.06) (Table S6), with no significant effect modification by diabetes status (P for interaction = 0.060 and 0.435) (Table S7). Second, excluding BMI yielded materially unchanged results (CVD mortality HR = 0.91, 95% CI: 0.82-1.00, P = 0.054) (Table S8). Third, adding categorical eGDR (high vs. low) to the base model improved the C-statistic for all-cause mortality (0.766 to 0.771) with a significant NRI of 0.162 (P = 0.04), and showed consistent direction for cardiovascular mortality (NRI = 0.100) (Table S9). Fourth, we compared eGDR with the TyG index (cutoff = 9.69). Higher TyG was associated with increased all-cause (HR = 1.51, 95% CI: 1.12–2.04, P = 0.008) and CVD mortality (HR = 1.83, 95% CI: 1.09–3.09, P = 0.023), consistent with its role as an IR promoter (Table S10). However, eGDR demonstrated superior incremental predictive value (NRI = 0.162 vs. 0.026 for all-cause mortality), likely reflecting its broader integration of metabolic, adiposity, and vascular domains (Table S9). Fifth, in the HSI-defined CLM cohort (n = 2,366), eGDR remained inversely associated with all-cause mortality (continuous HR 0.91, 95% CI: 0.84–0.99, P = 0.018; higher vs. lower eGDR: HR 0.78, 95% CI: 0.61–0.99, P = 0.043) (Table S11).

Discussion

Focusing on patients exhibiting overlap among CLM components, this cohort research demonstrates the novel capacity of eGDR to predict outcomes. Key findings indicate that: (1) eGDR demonstrated a linear inverse association with cardiovascular mortality and a threshold-dependent non-linear association with all-cause mortality, with divergent risk patterns below and above the inflection point of 5.5901 mg/kg/min; (2) Exploratory mediation analysis suggested that SIRI may partially mediate the eGDR–mortality link, particularly for cardiovascular mortality (11.3%); (3) Elevated eGDR conferred greater protection against both causes of death, especially pronounced among those younger than 60 years.

In MASLD, IR is a principal pathogenic factor for hepatic steatosis and also a major contributor to CVD risk [31]. Its pathophysiology involves exacerbated systemic inflammation, visceral fat accumulation, and dysregulated ectopic lipid deposition [32]. The eGDR, a validated noninvasive marker of whole-body insulin sensitivity, integrates key metabolic indices including hypertension, WC, and HbA1c [23]. Evidence confirms that declining eGDR predicts increased risks of both CVD and MASLD [33, 34]. Pathophysiologically, impaired tissue glucose clearance reflects dysfunctional insulin signaling, which accelerates atherogenesis through endothelial injury, dyslipidemia, and chronic inflammation [35]. Our findings that higher eGDR correlated with lower WC, HbA1c, UA, and SIRI levels further support eGDR as a composite metabolic health indicator.

MASLD represents a multisystem disorder characterized by dysregulated hepatic lipid metabolism with extrahepatic complications [36, 37]. It significantly elevates risks of T2D, CVD, and chronic kidney disease (CKD) [36, 37]. Notably, the incidence of CVD among US adults diagnosed with MASLD has doubled within the past thirty years [38], and CVD has emerged as the primary cause of mortality in this population, exceeding fatalities due to liver complications [39]. As core components of CLM overlap pathology, CVD and MASLD exhibit synergistic mortality effects when coexisting. Evidence shows MASLD patients with T2D have substantially higher CVD risk than those without T2D; however, improving metabolic risk factors can mitigate this risk [40]. Among CMRFs, T2D most strongly predicts adverse hepatic outcomes and all-cause mortality in MASLD [41]. Large cohorts further indicate MASLD patients have higher multimorbidity prevalence and 16% increased all-cause mortality versus non-MASLD populations [42]. Prior research consistently reports an inverse linear correlation linking eGDR to both all-cause and cardiovascular death among individuals affected by CVD, NAFLD, or MetS [15–17]. Conversely, our investigation revealed that in the overlapping CLM cohort, eGDR preserved a linear inverse correlation with cardiovascular death, whereas its association with all-cause mortality manifested as a moderate non-linear curve. This non-linear threshold pattern likely reflects competing risk pathways. Below the inflection point, higher eGDR was protective, consistent with improved insulin sensitivity and reduced inflammation. The paradoxical mortality increase above this threshold may reflect reverse causation (severe systemic illness elevating HbA1c and blood pressure components of eGDR), residual confounding, or—less probably—advanced disease cachexia. We acknowledge that this interpretation is hypothetical, as our data lacked direct measurements of hepatic fibrosis, sarcopenia, or nutritional status.

Liver inflammation in MASLD involves multifactorial triggers from intrahepatic or extrahepatic sources (e.g., adipose tissue, gut microenvironment) [43]. Pro-inflammatory cytokines are consistently elevated in MASLD, particularly MASH, confirming systemic inflammation as a core driver of progression [44]. This inflammatory state promotes endothelial dysfunction and accelerates atherogenesis, thereby contributing to CVD development [45]. Ye et al. demonstrated gradient increases in all-cause mortality risk with higher SIRI levels among MASLD patients, alongside significantly elevated risks of comorbid T2D and hyperlipidemia versus non-MASLD controls (P < 0.001) [46]. Zhang et al. demonstrated SIRI’s superiority over both the systemic immune inflammation index (SII) and platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR) in predicting cardiovascular death among MASLD patients [47]. Additionally, their investigation documented a markedly elevated prevalence of comorbidities—such as hypertension, T2D, CVD, and CKD—in non-survivors relative to survivors (P < 0.001). Our exploratory mediation analysis suggests that inflammation may represent one operative pathway linking eGDR to mortality. This mechanistically suggests that within CLM overlap pathology, IR contributes to multi-organ dysfunction—and ultimately mortality—partially through exacerbated systemic inflammation. Notably, while eGDR’s total and direct effects on all-cause mortality were non-significant, its indirect effect via SIRI was statistically significant (indirect effect = 6.530, 95% CI: 2.146–11.878), suggesting that inflammation represents one detectable pathway. Furthermore, the non-linear threshold relationship of eGDR to all-cause death risk, alongside non-significant overall linear effects, likely stems from competing pathophysiological processes across the eGDR spectrum that cannot be adequately captured by a simple linear model. eGDR is expressed in mg/kg/min, reflecting tissue glucose clearance rate. A 1-unit increase corresponds to approximately 1 mg/kg/min improvement in insulin-stimulated glucose disposal — a physiologically meaningful increment. In our cohort, the interquartile range spanned approximately 2.5 eGDR units, representing a clinically relevant metabolic window achievable through lifestyle or pharmacological interventions.

Beyond inflammation and metabolic dysregulation, sympathetic nervous system overactivation represents an increasingly recognized mechanism linking MASLD to cardiovascular mortality. Hepatic steatosis and insulin resistance activate the SNS through RAAS stimulation and baroreflex dysfunction, creating a feed-forward loop that exacerbates both hepatic and cardiac dysfunction [13]. In our cohort, the strong association between lower eGDR and cardiovascular mortality—partially mediated by SIRI—suggests that systemic inflammation and autonomic dysregulation may operate as parallel and intersecting pathways linking insulin resistance to cardiac death. While our study did not directly assess SNS biomarkers, the eGDR components (hypertension status, HbA1c, WC) are themselves markers of sympathetic-metabolic dysregulation, reinforcing the neuro-cardio-hepatic axis as a plausible mechanistic framework.

Notably, our subgroup analysis indicated a significantly heightened correlation of eGDR with mortality among CLM overlap patients aged < 60 years. This finding aligns with conclusions from prior studies in cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic (CKM) syndrome populations, where Chen et al. demonstrated a particularly pronounced synergistic effect of eGDR and SIRI on all-cause mortality in younger subgroups (< 60 years) using large cohort data [48]. Similarly, Cao et al. observed an enhanced synergistic impact of CKM staging combined with SIRI on mortality in this identical cohort. Further corroborative evidence indicates that among NAFLD patients, eGDR exhibits its strongest association with all-cause mortality in the 45-59-year age group [16], while research on MetS populations further reveals enhanced eGDR predictive effects among individuals younger than 60 years [17]. It should be noted, however, that one study in CVD patients found this association statistically significant across all age strata [15]. This age-related heterogeneity reflects mechanisms operating in both directions. In younger patients (< 60 years), the pronounced eGDR effect likely arises from: (i) longer cumulative exposure to metabolic injury given early disease onset [49, 50]; (ii) interaction between behavioral risk factors (smoking, alcohol, sedentarism) and CLM pathology amplifying IR and inflammation [51]; and (iii) delayed symptom recognition missing critical intervention windows. Conversely, in older patients (≥ 60 years), the attenuated effect may reflect: (i) competing mortality risks from cancer, neurodegeneration, and frailty diluting the metabolic signal; (ii) reduced eGDR discriminative power due to physiological age-related insulin resistance compressing the eGDR distribution; (iii) dominance of fixed end-organ damage (advanced atherosclerosis, CKD, cardiac remodeling) that current metabolic status cannot overcome; and (iv) selective survival, where resilient individuals with compensatory protective mechanisms survive to older age, attenuating eGDR’s prognostic signal.

eGDR can be calculated at the bedside using waist circumference, blood pressure, and HbA1c—three routinely available parameters—making it a practical risk stratification tool for CLM overlap patients. The dose–response relationship between eGDR and mortality suggests that modest improvements in insulin sensitivity may confer meaningful survival benefits. Patients in the lowest eGDR quartile should be considered for intensive management, including tighter glycemic control, blood pressure optimization, and weight reduction. The nonlinear association supports a threshold effect, emphasizing the need to identify and intensively treat those with severely impaired insulin sensitivity. Future studies should evaluate eGDR-guided intervention strategies in the CLM population.

Several methodological limitations warrant acknowledgment. First, residual confounding persists despite multivariable adjustments, as unmeasured variables may bias the observed associations. Second, while eGDR provides practical utility as an IR proxy—originally derived from type 1 diabetes cohorts and validated against the hyperinsulinemic-euglycemic clamp (HEC)—its adequacy as a comprehensive surrogate for HEC in complex metabolic populations requires large-scale verification. Third, eGDR’s dependence on laboratory assays and clinical measurements and modeling renders it vulnerable to pre-analytical variations and analytical errors, potentially introducing systematic measurement bias. Fourth, subgroup analyses by age, sex, BMI, eGFR, and lifestyle factors were performed without formal multiplicity adjustment (e.g., Bonferroni correction). Thus, the interaction findings—particularly for age < 60 years—should be interpreted as exploratory and require independent validation. Fifth, the explanation for the paradoxical increase in all-cause mortality at higher eGDR levels remains hypothetical. Our dataset lacked direct measurements of hepatic fibrosis stage, sarcopenia indices, or nutritional biomarkers, precluding definitive conclusions regarding the potential role of liver disease-related cachexia or other competing mechanisms. Sixth, the mediation analysis was exploratory: it was not survey-weighted; eGDR and SIRI were measured concurrently, preventing causal ordering; and the linear decomposition does not capture the non-linear eGDR–mortality relationship. Seventh, CVD history was ascertained through self-reported physician diagnosis, which may introduce ascertainment bias due to underreporting of asymptomatic disease or recall inaccuracy. Any misclassification would likely be non-differential, biasing effect estimates toward the null. Finally, generalizability is constrained by the cohort’s exclusive derivation from a US community-based population, where ethnic composition, healthcare access, and environmental exposures limit extrapolation to other regions or clinical subpopulations.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that the eGDR effectively stratifies both all-cause and cardiovascular mortality risk in patients with overlapping CLM. Exploratory mediation analysis suggests that SIRI may partially mediate the eGDR–mortality link, particularly for cardiovascular mortality. Integrating eGDR into risk assessment frameworks may optimize precision management and inform individualized prevention strategies for this high-risk cohort.
