No Smoker Left Behind: Evaluation of a Population-Based, Opt-Out Smoking Cessation Program for Patients With Cancer Who Smoke
Authors: Michael Wakeman, Yasmin Asvat, Raymond A. Ruiz, Emma I. Brett, Vincent Talbot, Jasmin A. Tiro, Andrea C. King
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PURPOSEWe evaluated the reach, treatment selection, and effectiveness of a population-based, opt-out smoking cessation outreach program, No Smoker Left Behind, implemented in a socioeconomically and racially diverse cancer patient population.METHODSPatients seeking oncology care at the University of Chicago Comprehensive Cancer Center between 2019 and 2024 were screened for smoking status as documented in the electronic health record. Adults age 18 years and older categorized as currently smoking (past month) were systematically contacted via interactive voice response phone calls, texts, and emails for up to 6 months. They were offered several cessation treatment options, including medication and counseling referrals (ie, group program, quitline, and text message program). Program involvement and cessation outcomes were compared by cancer type (ie, smoking- or non–smoking-related) and race. A cost-outcome analysis was also conducted.RESULTSOf 37,478 patients with cancer, 3,706 (9.9%) were deemed eligible and contacted, of whom 57.0% were male, 46.6% Black, and 63.8% publicly insured. Of those selecting cessation treatments (n = 1,089), most self-selected both medication and counseling (70.6%), with 873 receiving referrals or connection to cessation support. Among patients who completed day 180 follow-up (37.5% of those receiving referrals), 16.2% (53/327) self-reported ≥8-day abstinence. Patients with smoking-related cancers had higher abstinence rates than patients with non–smoking-related cancers (23.7% v 14.7%). Black patients had higher program involvement and treatment referral rates, but significantly lower smoking abstinence compared with White patients (14.4% v 25.7%). Costs-per-patient referred to treatment were $415 US dollars (USD) and costs-per-quit were $6,067 USD.CONCLUSIONPopulation-based, proactive opt-out outreach programs provide a feasible approach for offering multiple cessation treatment options that allow for patient preference and customization to optimize treatment accessibility. As health care systems expand access to tobacco cessation as part of cancer care, implementing resource-efficient models will become increasingly important.

INTRODUCTION

Tobacco use is the leading driver of preventable morbidity and mortality in the United States, causing 28% of all cancer deaths.1 Of patients diagnosed with cancer in 2023, 14.7% reported current smoking.2 Continued smoking by patients with cancer results in adverse health outcomes, including care-related complications,3 decreased quality of life,4 and reduced cancer treatment effectiveness.5,6 By contrast, smoking cessation after a cancer diagnosis is associated with improved survival and decreased risk of cancer recurrence and developing second primary cancers.7-10

Given the health benefits of quitting and high risk of relapse among patients with cancer,11 encouraging smoking cessation and providing evidence-based treatments is essential throughout the cancer care trajectory.12 However, smoking cessation treatment is rarely provided as part of routine cancer care.13,14 Most American College of Surgeons' Commission on Cancer (CoC)–accredited cancer programs (71.2%) advise patients to quit smoking.15 However, few prescribe approved medications (17.6%) or provide counseling (18.2%) to support cessation,15 despite clinical guidelines recommending combined behavioral-pharmacotherapy as best practice.16 Several barriers to providing cessation care for patients with cancer have been identified, including lack of provider training and perceived patient resistance to quitting.14,15

Population-based, opt-out outreach programs provide an opportunity to expand access to smoking cessation treatment,13 as all patients who smoke are systematically identified, automatically offered treatment, and given an opportunity to accept or decline. Opt-out care is associated with increased treatment engagement and quit attempts compared with opt-in care,17 where only patients who express interest in quitting smoking are offered treatment. Additionally, opt-out tobacco programs are acceptable to patients with cancer and clinicians.18 Typically, these programs have provided cancer patients with medications, counseling, and/or referrals to quitlines.19-24 However, there is little research examining which treatment options are most preferred by patients with cancer,25 and limited deployment of institutional resources to support patient demand.15

To address these limitations, we retrospectively evaluated No Smoker Left Behind® (NSLB), an opt-out tobacco cessation treatment program at the University of Chicago Comprehensive Cancer Center (UCCCC). To our knowledge, this is the only National Cancer Institute (NCI)–Designated Cancer Center program that allows patients to select from a menu of treatment options including medications and three different behavioral treatments (ie, group program, quitline, and text messaging program) to customize cessation treatment. In accordance with established program evaluation frameworks,26 we assessed the reach, treatment selection, and effectiveness of NSLB during its first 6 years of implementation (2019-2024).

METHODS

Program Development and Implementation

UCCCC is part of the University of Chicago Medicine (UCM) health system, an urban academic medical center on the south side of Chicago, with a five-county catchment area extending from northeast Illinois to northwest Indiana. Program development was initially supported by the Cancer Center Cessation Initiative (C3I), part of the NCI Cancer Moonshot, from 2017 to 2020. The primary aim of C3I was to implement effective and sustainable tobacco treatment programs at NCI-Designated Cancer Centers.27 NSLB was integrated into the standard of care at UCCCC in January 2019 and systematically contacted patients in a stepped-phase plan beginning with head and neck and thoracic oncology clinics and then expanding to all outpatient oncology clinics in May 2020. Since 2021, NSLB has been sustained with funding from UCM and UCCCC.

Program Description and Design

NSLB is centrally supported by the Quit Plan Manager system (v. 5.0, TelASK Inc), a cloud-based system that interfaces with the electronic health record (EHR) and manages a track-and-triage workflow that identifies patients' smoking status, proactively offers them cessation support, and documents their responses. A part-time tobacco treatment specialist (TTS) manages patient treatment selections and program queries within the system interface and liaises between patients and providers (eg, physician assistants prescribing medications).

Clinical Workflow

Data from the EHR on patients who attended an oncology-related outpatient visit at UCCCC and were represented in the cancer registry are imported weekly into the Quit Plan Manager system. Quit Plan Manager then screens for eligibility based on the following criteria: English-speaking adults (≥18 years) who currently smoke cigarettes. Patients were excluded if their preferred primary language was not English.

All eligible patients automatically receive multimodal communications to facilitate program engagement. These contacts include one welcome email, up to four interactive voice response (IVR) phone calls, and two text messages and/or emails. All communications are dispatched at prescribed times over a 3-day period for three contact intervals (days 3, 14, and 30 after clinic visit). IVR calls are 5 minutes in length and include pre-recorded messages that patients respond to using keypad or verbal answers. These calls assess current smoking status, offer treatment options, and conclude with a motivational message about the importance of quitting smoking to optimize cancer treatment outcomes. Patients who respond to text or email messages complete a 3-minute online survey that mirrors the IVR call structure. For those who respond to contact attempts in the first month, follow-up calls are scheduled at 90 and 180 days to assess cessation outcomes. Patients can opt-out of NSLB by contacting the program staff or declining treatment across three consecutive contact intervals.

Unique to NSLB, patients are provided with several treatment options: (1) referral to a medical provider to discuss US Food and Drug Administration–approved over-the-counter and/or prescription cessation medications (ie, varenicline and bupropion) and (2) referral to one of three behavioral counseling options including group counseling via the Courage to Quit program (provided in telehealth format starting April 2020),28-30 telephone counseling via the Illinois Tobacco Quitline,31 and the text-messaging program delivered via NCI SmokeFreeTXT.32 Both the Courage to Quit program and Illinois Tobacco Quitline provide patients with no-cost nicotine replacement therapy. Patients can select medications and a counseling option at each contact interval.

After treatment selection, patients receive an automated message detailing next steps, which could include an immediate warm transfer to the Illinois Tobacco Quitline (if selected) or notification that the program's TTS will contact them to facilitate their choices for the other treatment options. Starting in 2021, patients were offered a gift (ie, tote bag, chip clip, and/or key chain) as an incentive for responding to an IVR phone call or online survey in the first month of outreach and at 180-day follow-up. Patients who remain listed as current smokers in the EHR are reinvited into NSLB every 6 months—triggered by their most recent outpatient visit—until they quit smoking or opt out of the program.

Data Sources and Measures

Demographic and health information was extracted from the EHR (ie, age, biological sex, self-identified race and/or ethnicity, health insurance, International Classification of Diseases-10 codes, smoking status, and primary language). Data on self-reported smoking behaviors (ie, smoking frequency and quantity, quit attempts in the past month) and treatment selections were obtained from patients' responses to IVR phone calls or online surveys via the Quit Plan Manager system. The study was reviewed by the University of Chicago Institutional Review Board (IRB No. 24-0402) and received a waiver of consent process and waiver of consent documentation.

Patients were categorized as having a smoking-related or non–smoking-related cancer based on the latest surgeon general report.4 Program involvement was categorized by several outcomes, including reached: responded to the IVR phone calls or online surveys by providing information on their smoking history; engaged: selected a smoking cessation treatment option; and referred: selected and received referrals or were registered in treatment. For patients with multiple invitations to the program (n = 1,861), the current analysis only included data from the interval with the highest level of program involvement. At 180-day follow-up, outcomes were measured by three self-reported smoking behaviors: (1) ≥8-day smoking abstinence (“When did you last smoke a cigarette, even a puff?” with response options: 0 days, 1-7 days, 8-30 days, 1 month-1 year, >1 year); (2) past month quit attempts (“In the past 30 days, have you tried to quit smoking or stay off cigarettes?” with yes/no response options); and (3) smoking reduction (“For the past 30 days, about how many cigarettes did you have on the days you smoked?”). Smoking reduction was defined as a decrease in category endorsed for daily cigarette consumption from a patient's first response to 180-day follow-up (ie, categories: 1-4, 5-9, 10-14, 15-19, ≥20 cigarettes). Smoking abstinence was mutually exclusive with past month quit attempts and smoking reduction (ie, these outcomes were assessed among patients who reported that they had not quit smoking in the last 7 days). Finally, operating costs were estimated from invoices aligning with reporting requirements for C3I.33

Data Analysis

Patients contacted by NSLB from its initiation in January 2019 through the end of December 2024 were included for analysis. Descriptive statistics for program involvement and treatment selections and referrals were assessed. Self-reported smoking behaviors were examined at 180 days, which was the program's end point and aligns with expert consensus on cessation trial standards and C3I program evaluation parameters.34,35 As smoking behaviors and cessation outcomes can differ by cancer type,36,37 program outcomes were compared between patients with smoking-related and those with non–smoking-related cancers. Likewise, given UCCCC's patient population and existing tobacco-related health disparities,4 program outcomes were compared between Black and White patients. All comparisons were conducted using chi-square and Kruskal-Wallis tests. As a secondary analysis, we calculated median annual and monthly program operating costs. Operating costs, excluding start-up costs, were divided by the number of patients reached, referred, and who reported abstinence to calculate costs-per-patient reached, costs-per-patient-referred, and costs-per-quit, respectively. Statistical significance was set at α = .05. Analyses were conducted in R (v. 4.4, R Foundation) using Tidyverse packages (v. 2.0).38,39

Within an individual enrollment interval and across all contact responses, most patients selected a combination of medications with counseling (70.6%), followed by medications alone (14.8%) and the SmokeFreeTXT program (8.8%; Fig 2). Among those selecting both medications and counseling, the Courage to Quit program was most frequently chosen (23.8%).

Of the 873 patients referred to treatment, most were referred to counseling services (58.0%), followed by medications only (20.8%) and medications and counseling (11.5%). Among these patients, 327 responded to the final follow-up contact at 180 days (37.5%). Overall, 53 (16.2%) reported ≥8-day smoking abstinence, and among those who were nonabstinent, 192 (58.7%) reported a past month quit attempt, and 49 (15.0%) reported a reduction in smoking.

When comparing program outcomes for patients with smoking-related and non–smoking-related cancers, a total of 190 patients with smoking-related cancers (21.3% of those reached) and 245 patients with non–smoking-related cancers (23.9% of those reached) responded to follow-up at 180 days (Table 2). Patients with smoking-related cancers had significantly higher rates of self-reported ≥8-day smoking abstinence compared with patients with non–smoking-related cancers (23.7% v 14.7%).

DISCUSSION

This program evaluation contributes to the growing evidence-base on the effectiveness of population-based, proactive opt-out smoking cessation programs.19-24,40 NSLB leverages EHR data to systematically offer all patients with cancer who smoke a menu of evidence-based smoking cessation treatments. Such a low-touch, high-volume approach could serve as a model for other health systems to implement routine tobacco treatment. This is important for cancer care in lieu of the January 2026 American College of Surgeons' CoC accreditation standard for screening all new patients with cancer for smoking status and proactively referring those who smoke to evidence-based cessation support.41 Furthermore, the use of repeated outreach and rolling invitations in NSLB is consistent with tobacco dependence as a chronic, relapsing disorder that may require repeated attempts to quit.42 This approach may be especially valuable for patients with cancer whose readiness to quit smoking can vary throughout the course of treatment.43,44 Overall, leveraging technologies for automatic outreach may be an efficient and comprehensive approach to circumvent issues associated with traditional in-person outreach, including reduced personnel and time constraints.15

NSLB achieved a high level of providing treatment referrals (23.5%) over its first 6 years of operation, which is similar to other C3I centers using IVR phone-call systems (25.0%) and higher relative to reported outcomes from other C3I centers (15.4%).35 This suggests that low-touch interventions provide an effective outreach approach for patients with cancer. Abstinence rates over the 6-year implementation period were similar to those reported by other C3I centers (18.4%).35 Notably, patient characteristics of our Chicago sample includes more Black and publicly insured patients than other NCI-Designated Cancer Centers that use opt-in approaches with lower reach and reliance on more intensive treatments, such as in-person counseling.35 Importantly, for NSLB, there were higher smoking quit rates among patients with smoking-related cancers and we speculate that patients with smoking-related cancers attribute their illness to smoking and thus are more highly motivated to quit,36,45 as was evident by high rates of quit attempts. Additionally, patients with smoking-related cancers have more internalized stigma regarding their disease and smoking,46 and outreach via IVR phone-calls may offer a more acceptable form of outreach compared with traditional, face-to-face approaches.

The diversity of our patient population is notable, as few opt-out smoking cessation programs for patients with cancer have reported patient characteristics.24,40 Our results, along with prior work,40 indicate there is high acceptance of smoking cessation treatment among Black patients with cancer when it is actually offered. Opt-out approaches may therefore help to mitigate barriers and biases in treatment provision among patients with cancer. We also found significantly lower smoking abstinence rates among Black patients compared with White patients, similar to some studies,47,48 but not all.28 It has been speculated that smoking quit rate disparities by race may be partially attributed to differences in social determinants of health, including home ownership, income, and neighborhood disadvantage.47 Furthermore, higher rates of menthol cigarette use among Black than White individuals have been suggested to lead to more difficulty quitting.49,50 Although menthol smoking was not assessed herein, other clinical studies in our catchment area have reported menthol smoking rates in Black adults as high as 96%.51 Offering repeated treatment opportunities and a menu of choices may help to mitigate these disparities.52,53

Smoking cessation programs for patients with cancer typically provide access to state quitlines.35 To our knowledge, NSLB is the only C3I program that allows individuals to select from three different behavioral treatment modalities. Notably, most patients self-selected referrals to both medication and counseling services when offered a choice, which is considered the gold standard of cessation treatment.16,54 This may reflect patients being highly motivated to quit and thus willing to try multiple forms of smoking cessation treatment. Differences in treatment selections (ie, high rates of medications and counseling) and actual referrals (ie, higher rates of referral to counseling v medications) were likely due to patients wanting access to free medications through counseling services (ie, Courage to Quit and Illinois Tobacco Quitline) after discussions with the TTS. There was some variation in treatment selection by race, suggesting differences in treatment preferences that should be accommodated to optimize the uptake of cessation support. Relatedly, the National Comprehensive Care Network recommends that cessation treatment modalities should be guided by patient preferences.16 Furthermore, evidence suggests cancer patients prefer remote modalities of care, which may be more convenient and accessible,25 particularly for those with transportation or mobility issues.55 Likewise, offering multiple options that were low-cost or free may have circumvented traditional barriers to cessation treatment (eg, financial barriers),56 broadening overall treatment accessibility.

Our program is noteworthy for its high reach, which was facilitated by using an automatic outreach system. Consequently, NSLB spent less on personnel compared with other C3I centers, which typically represents 90% of their overall costs.33 Thus, our median monthly costs were less than half of those of other C3I centers ($11,045 USD) along with lower costs-per-patient referred ($466 USD).33 However, costs-per-quit were higher for NSLB compared with other centers ($2,688 USD).33 These differences may reflect more intensive interventions, such as in-person counseling, being more effective overall.35 Even so, these costs are substantially lower than those for continued smoking. For example, first-line cancer treatments failing due to continued smoking costs an estimated $10,678 USD per patient alone.57

Several limitations are worth noting. Invitation to NSLB relied on smoking status as documented in the EHR, which may have been underreported due to stigma associated with smoking among patients with cancer.58 Nonetheless, patients identified as not currently smoking at one visit were contacted if identified as smoking during later outpatient visits. Additionally, patients with a preferred language other than English in the EHR were excluded (n = 3, 0.1%) and future program iterations could expand to accommodate additional languages. Patients who did not have cancer or did not smoke may have been contacted incidentally, although this was a rare occurrence. Response rates to 180-day follow-up were low and differed across subgroups, limiting generalizability and subgroup comparisons. If all nonresponders to follow-up were assumed to be smoking, abstinence rates would be 6.0% among patients who received referrals. Although it is unlikely that all 180-day nonresponders were still smoking, multiple imputation techniques for handling missing data were not possible, given the relatively large proportion of patients missing these data. Missing data is common in clinical program follow-ups that lack the participant tracking and tracing resources of randomized clinical trial research.24,59,60 Self-reported outcomes may have been subject to response bias,61 although self-reported smoking abstinence is generally considered acceptable for use in real-world studies.34 Finally, as is common for track-and-triage programs, it was not possible to confirm whether patients followed up with referrals to external cessation services, such as the Illinois Tobacco Quitline or SmokeFreeTXT, or used requested cessation medications. The uptake of internal cessation services (eg, group counseling) and confirmation of prescriptions received for cessation are potentially traceable but limited by documentation variability and were outside the scope of this paper.

In conclusion, over its first 6 years of implementation, the NSLB program successfully delivered evidence-based cessation care to a population of socioeconomically and racially diverse patients with cancer. Our results underscore the real-world feasibility and challenges of implementing and evaluating population-based, proactive opt-out smoking cessation outreach programs. Moreover, our work contributes to literature supporting C3I in expanding access to cessation care and provides a resource-efficient model that can be adapted to other health systems, especially as they prepare to implement the American College of Surgeons' CoC Standard 5.9: Smoking Cessation for Patients with Cancer.41
