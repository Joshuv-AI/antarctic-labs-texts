RNA-binding proteins: a comprehensive review of multifaceted regulatory mechanisms in neuroinflammation and implications in the pathogenesis of neurological disorders
Authors: Mingze Zheng, Ming Li, Shuyu Liu, Ruili Guan, Xinqin Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Neuroinflammation stands as a cornerstone pathological hallmark across a spectrum of neurological disorders, drawing intensified scientific scrutiny owing to its profoundly intricate and multi-layered regulatory networks. At the heart of this complexity, RNA-binding proteins (RBPs) emerge as masterful post-transcriptional orchestrators, exerting precise control over a vast array of neuroinflammatory cascades. Mounting evidence underscores that RBPs transcend their classical roles in RNA sensing and innate immune recognition, actively shaping pivotal biological pathways—ranging from inflammatory signal transduction and programmed cell death to metabolic reprogramming, epigenetic remodeling and dynamic crosstalk with non-coding RNAs. Furthermore, the functional versatility of RBPs is amplified by nuanced alterations in their nucleocytoplasmic trafficking, stress granule formation, post-translational modifications, and RNA-binding specificities, all of which intricately fine-tune their regulatory impact within the neuroinflammatory milieu. Strikingly, the cell type-specific actions of RBPs in neurons, microglia, and astrocytes unveil a sophisticated tapestry of molecular specialization, offering transformative insights into their context-dependent functions. Abnormal function of RNA-binding proteins is closely related to neurodegenerative diseases such as Alzheimer’s disease, Parkinson’s disease, amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, and multiple sclerosis. In addition, RNA-binding proteins are involved in various pathological processes, including central nervous system infections, stroke, high-altitude cerebral hypoxia, and traumatic brain injury. This review systematically organizes the multifaceted regulatory mechanisms of RNA-binding proteins in neuroinflammation. It deeply explores their key roles in the occurrence and development of nervous system diseases. The review aims to construct a comprehensive theoretical framework and provide a scientific basis for developing new diagnostic methods and targeted therapeutic strategies.

Introduction

Neuroinflammation is a key pathological mechanism in neurological disorders, involving complex interactions among diverse cell types and molecular pathways. This cascade involves immune cell activation, cytokine release, signaling dysregulation, and microenvironment shifts, driving disease progression [1]. RNA-binding proteins (RBPs) are pivotal regulators of RNA metabolism and key modulators of neuroinflammation, making them a research focus [2]. Understanding RBP roles deepens insights into pathogenesis and reveals therapeutic targets.

The multicellular nature of neuroinflammation adds complexity. Microglia have dual roles: protective under physiology but damaging upon overactivation, contributing to neuronal injury. For example, TPL2 kinase enhances microglial cytokine release, accelerating neurodegeneration [3]. Astrocytes contribute via FABP7 upregulation, activating NF-κB and amplifying inflammation [4, 5]. Peripheral myeloid cells interact with CNS cells via mediators; Properdin from neutrophils/macrophages activates Mincle on microglia, exacerbating ischemia-induced neuroinflammation [6]. Cytokine networks balance neuroinflammation: pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6) are elevated in disorders like Alzheimer’s and multiple sclerosis, while anti-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-4, IL-10) suppress inflammation [7–10]. Adaptive immune cells, like Th17 cells, contribute to neuroinflammation in disorders such as multiple sclerosis, depression, and schizophrenia [11–13].

RBPs are key regulators in neuroinflammation. TIA1 modulates tau pathology, and its deficiency aggravates tau-related neuroinflammation [1]. IGF2BP2 regulates CCL2 expression, promoting cell recruitment in autoimmune neuroinflammation [14]. HuR promotes Th17 differentiation and inflammation by stabilizing transcripts; its inhibition attenuates autoimmunity [8, 15]. RBPs cooperate with non-coding RNAs; e.g., circMETTL9 with ILF3 modulates post-injury responses, and lncRNA NEAT1 with RBPs influences NLRP3 activation in microglia [16, 17]. Thus, RBPs participate in inflammatory signaling, cytokine production, and immune cell polarization, shaping neuroinflammation.

RBPs act as central modulators of neuroinflammatory diseases. Neuroinflammation links to neurodegenerative diseases; in Alzheimer’s, chronic inflammation drives progression via glial activation and protein aggregation [18, 19]. In amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) and frontotemporal dementia (FTD), pathological aggregation of RBPs—such as TDP-43—is closely linked to neuroinflammatory activation, establishing a self-reinforcing, detrimental cycle [20, 21]. Acute insults like traumatic brain injury and stroke involve robust inflammation, with RBP mechanisms offering therapeutic avenues [22, 23]. Recently characterized cell death processes like pyroptosis, ER stress responses, and RNA modifications are increasingly implicated in RBP-regulated pathways—broadening our understanding of RBP functionality beyond classical RNA metabolism [24, 25]. In the context of paraneoplastic neurological syndromes (PNS), the presence of antibodies specifically targeting RBPs serves as a crucial serological marker [26]. RBPs also play an essential role in antibacterial and antiviral immunity [27, 28].

This review encompasses diverse RNA species, including messenger RNA (mRNA), long non-coding RNA (lncRNA), circular RNA (circRNA), microRNA (miRNA), and viral/non-self RNA. These RNA molecules engage in specific interactions with RNA-binding proteins (RBPs), thereby playing a central role in the post-transcriptional regulation of neuroinflammation (Table 1).

In summary, neuroinflammation is a shared foundation in neurological diseases, governed by cell-population and molecular interactions. RBPs centralize this process through multifaceted regulatory mechanisms. A systematic synthesis of RBP-mediated mechanisms deepens our understanding of disease pathogenesis and reveals promising, mechanistically grounded targets for diagnostics and therapeutic intervention.

The Role of RNA-binding proteins in the mechanisms of neuroinflammation

Post-transcriptional regulation

RNA-binding proteins (RBPs) orchestrate a central layer of gene expression control in neuroinflammation through post-transcriptional regulation. This multifaceted axis encompasses mRNA stability, translation, splicing, editing, and decay [40]. RBPs exert precise spatiotemporal control by binding to cis-regulatory elements, particularly in the 3’ untranslated regions (3’ UTRs) of inflammation-associated mRNAs, modulating their half-life and translational efficiency [41]. For instance, PCBP1 stabilizes the transcript of the neuroinflammatory mediator LCN2, directly modulating inflammatory magnitude [2, 29]. Translational regulation by RBPs allows rapid cellular responses. In neuropsychiatric disorders, exercise-induced RBP changes influence neurotransmitter signaling and translation, and in stroke [42]. RBPs also critically regulate alternative splicing, generating diverse protein isoforms that influence inflammatory signaling. PCBP1 modulates Parkinson’s disease-linked pathways via WWP2 splicing, and MAPT mutations in tauopathies alter tau isoform ratios, driving neuroinflammation [29, 43]. HNRNPF/H family RBPs regulate splicing of inflammation-related transcripts [44]. RNA editing, notably adenosine-to-inosine (A-to-I) editing, adds another layer of control, with RBPs interacting with editing enzymes to diversify the neuroinflammatory transcriptome [45]. Finally, RBPs mediate selective mRNA decay to limit excessive immune activation, providing essential negative feedback [46]. Collectively, these RBP-driven processes form a continuous rheostat setting the amplitude and duration of neuroinflammatory responses.

Non-coding RNA–RBP interactions

RBPs interact extensively with non-coding RNAs (ncRNAs)—microRNAs (miRNAs), long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs), and circular RNAs (circRNAs)—forming multi-layered regulatory networks. LncRNAs (> 200 nt) interact with RBPs to form complexes that fine-tune gene expression, modulating RBP function and influencing inflammatory gene expression in neuroinflammation [47]. In cancer, lncRNA-RBP interactions regulate transcription, mRNA metabolism, m6A modification, and splicing, mechanisms highly relevant to neuroinflammation [48]. MiRNAs often cooperate with RBPs; emerging tools like DeepMiRBP are revealing intricate miRNA-RBP networks crucial for modulating inflammatory pathways and glial cell states [49]. CircRNAs act as miRNA sponges or directly bind RBPs, influencing splicing, stability, and transport, thereby regulating inflammatory responses [50]. Furthermore, ncRNAs serve as hubs in RBP-centered networks, participating in competing endogenous RNA (ceRNA) circuits to precisely orchestrate gene expression. LncRNAs can competitively bind RBPs and miRNAs, modulating inflammatory mRNA stability [51]. In Parkinson’s disease, specific lncRNA-RBP interactions regulate inflammatory cytokines and cell fate [52, 53]. Dissecting these interactions relies on advanced technologies like RIP, CLIP, RNA pull-down, CARPID, and Nano-SpatiaLR, enabling high-resolution mapping of ncRNA-RBP dynamics in neuroinflammation [54–56].

RNA Sensing and innate immune recognition

RBPs differentiate “self” from “non-self” RNA by identifying characteristic molecular signatures such as double-stranded RNA structures, 5’ triphosphate ends, distinct nucleotide sequences, and differential RNA modification patterns [57, 58]. For instance, RIG-I and MDA5—well-established cytosolic RNA sensors—specifically detect double-stranded viral RNA and unmodified 5’ triphosphates, activating downstream signaling cascades that drive the production of type I interferons and pro-inflammatory cytokines [59, 60]. In this context, auxiliary RBPs such as PACT, ZFP36, and STAU1 enhance RIG-I/MDA5-mediated recognition and signal transduction by binding to viral RNA, thereby reinforcing antiviral immunity [61–63].

In the process of viral RNA recognition, RBPs serve not only as direct sensors but also as key regulators of host-virus interactions, including mechanisms that counteract viral immune evasion. TRIM25, an RNA-binding E3 ubiquitin ligase, promotes RIG-I activation through K63-linked ubiquitination, thereby enhancing antiviral signaling [64]. Conversely, certain viral variants—such as the SARS-CoV-2 B.1.1.7 lineage—can evade innate immune surveillance by altering their RNA expression profiles and translational dynamics [65]. Additionally, members of the Z-DNA binding protein family, particularly ZBP1, recognize Z-conformation nucleic acids (Z-RNA or Z-DNA) formed during viral infection, leading to the activation of programmed cell death pathways and inflammatory responses that are essential for effective antiviral defense [38, 66] .

The suppression of immunogenic potential in endogenous RNA represents a fundamental mechanism for preserving immune tolerance. ADAR1 plays a pivotal role in this regulatory process by catalyzing adenosine-to-inosine (A-to-I) editing of double-stranded Alu RNA, which reduces its structural integrity and diminishes its capacity to trigger innate immune activation, thus preventing pathological interferon responses [67, 68].Furthermore, the RNA-binding protein PQBP1 functions as a sensor for both foreign RNA and DNA within the central nervous system, modulating neuroimmune signaling and restraining hyperactivation of neuroinflammatory pathways [39].

RBPs regulate the magnitude and duration of inflammatory signaling by modulating interactions between repetitive sequence-containing RNAs and TLR-mediated pathways. TRIM56, an RBP with intrinsic E3 ligase activity, directly restricts viral replication and indirectly influences type I interferon production by potentiating TLR3-dependent recognition of double-stranded RNA [69]. Moreover, LGP2—a member of the RIG-I-like receptor (RLR) family—modulates the spatiotemporal coordination of RIG-I and MDA5 signaling via interaction with the mitochondrial antiviral-signaling protein (MAVS), functioning as a key regulator that fine-tunes the strength and kinetics of RNA-sensing signals [70]. These findings highlight the intricate nature of RNA sensing and innate immune recognition and underscore the potential of RBPs as therapeutic targets for the treatment of infectious and autoimmune diseases [71, 72].

Inflammatory signaling, metabolic changes and epigenetic alterations

RBPs amplify neuroinflammatory signals by regulating key pathways (NF-κB, MAPK, NLRP3 inflammasome) and by coupling metabolic and epigenetic control. They post-transcriptionally regulate mRNAs encoding inflammatory components. EEF1A1 influences IL-6 and CCL5 expression in Parkinson’s models, and IGF2BP3 mediates inflammation via the RORα-NF-κB axis [73, 74]. The NLRP3 inflammasome, a central amplifier, is regulated by RBPs like hnRNPA1 through PKM2-STAT3 feedback loops [75]. Therapeutic agents like glycyrrhizin attenuate neuroinflammation by inhibiting HMGB1 and p38/JNK pathways, implicating RBP-regulated cytokine dynamics [76]. RBPs also participate in forming inflammatory complexes. Cold-inducible RNA binding protein (CIRP) activates IL-6R/STAT3 signaling in microglia, and extracellular CIRP impairs microglial phagocytosis via TLR4/miR-155 [77, 78]. RBPs further regulate immune cell recruitment by fine-tuning cytokine/chemokine release, as seen with astrocyte-derived CCL2 vesicles activating microglia [79]. The transcription factor Sp1 upregulates HuR to potentiate NF-κB signaling in microglia [80].

RBPs modulate mitochondrial function and the coupling of energy metabolism with inflammatory responses [81]. For example, TDP-43 shows abnormal expression in ALS and FTD, disrupting mitochondrial proteins such as SDHA and VDAC3, promoting astrocytic inflammation, and contributing to neuroinflammation [82]. Moreover, In Alzheimer’s disease (AD), neuronal mitochondrial activity decreases while microglial metabolism increases, associated with chronic neuroinflammation, indicating RBPs may regulate energy metabolism and immune signaling integration [83].

RBPs Control the Expression of Lipid Metabolism-Related Genes and Influence Inflammatory Mediator Production. Lipid metabolism is crucial in neuroinflammation, linking inflammatory mediators to lipid pathways [4, 5]. Lipid metabolism regulators such as FTO modulate lipid synthesis and accumulation by catalyzing m6A modifications on mRNAs encoding lipogenic enzymes—including fatty acid synthase (FASN)---thereby influencing metabolic flux and inflammatory outcomes [84]. These studies demonstrate that RBPs regulate lipid inflammatory mediators by controlling metabolic gene expression and modification, fine-tuning neuroinflammation.

RBPs influence inflammation gene transcription through RNA and DNA modifications, key to the neuroinflammatory network. WTAP enhances NLRP3 inflammasome expression via m6A modification in traumatic brain injury, amplifying inflammation and underscoring RBP-mediated m6A regulation’s significance in neuroinflammation [85]. DNA methylation, another major epigenetic mechanism, is also linked to brain metabolism through RBPs that regulate methyl-CpG-binding proteins such as MeCP2, thereby affecting the pathogenesis of neurological disorders [86].RBPs influence inflammation gene transcription through RNA and DNA modifications, key to the neuroinflammatory network.

Programmed cell death and DAMP regulation

RBPs regulate programmed cell death (PCD) pathways—apoptosis, pyroptosis, necroptosis, ferroptosis—that drive neuroinflammation by controlling cell death mRNAs. In humans, YBX1 stabilizes ATF4 mRNA, inhibiting ferroptosis [87]. In the nervous system, dysfunctional RBPs like HuR, with altered localization and RNA-binding, affect inflammation and cell fate pathways [88]. The RNA sensor ZBP1 recognizes Z-form nucleic acids, initiating multiple PCD forms (apoptosis, pyroptosis, necroptosis) upon viral infection or stress, coupling cell death to inflammation [89, 90]. Meanwhile, cell deaths release DAMPs (e.g., HMGB1) and cytokines (e.g., IL-1β), worsening neuroinflammation [91–93]. CIRBP stabilizes mitochondrial ferritin mRNA, protecting neurons from ferroptosis under hypoxia [94]. In spinal cord injury, RBP-regulated ferroptosis, autophagy, and pyroptosis interact to shape neuroinflammatory outcomes [95] (Fig. 1).

Stress granule, phase separation, nucleocytoplasmic transport, post-translational modifications, and RNA-binding loss loop

Under chronic inflammatory stress, RBPs can enter a pathological loop involving aberrant phase separation, impaired nucleocytoplasmic transport, and loss of RNA-binding function, perpetuating neuroinflammation. A key mechanism is stress granule (SG) formation via liquid-liquid phase separation (LLPS). SGs are membraneless organelles formed under stress, composed of mRNAs and RBPs like G3BP1, TIA-1, and UBQLN2 [96, 97]. Their assembly, driven by multivalent interactions and intrinsically disordered regions (IDRs) in RBPs, halts translation and sequesters RNA [98, 99]. G3BP1 is a core scaffolding protein whose phase separation properties determine SG dynamics [100, 101]. RNA modifications like m6A influence SG biogenesis via reader proteins (e.g., YTHDF) [102]. SGs are initially protective but persistent SGs become pathogenic, evolving into aggregates in ALS/FTD linked to RBPs like TDP-43 and FUS [103–106]. Defective SG disassembly leads to toxic inclusions, exacerbated by chronic stress impairing polysome dynamics [107]. IDRs facilitate phase separation but also predispose to aggregation, a tendency worsened by pathogenic mutations or post-translational modifications (PTMs) [105, 106, 108].

The functional state of RBPs depends on balanced nuclear-cytoplasmic shuttling mediated by nuclear pore complexes and transport receptors [109, 110]. In ALS/FTD, transport defects cause cytoplasmic accumulation of RBPs (e.g., TDP-43, SFPQ) and toxic aggregates that further impair transport homeostasis [111]. Dysregulated RBP-RNA interactions exacerbate aggregation and disrupt nucleocytoplasmic transport. The C9orf72 repeat expansion produces toxic species that interfere with nuclear transport [112, 113]. Impaired nucleocytoplasmic transport machinery initiates cytoplasmic protein aggregation, which in turn exacerbates transport dysfunction—establishing a deleterious feed-forward loop that progressively amplifies neurodegenerative pathology [114, 115]. In inflammation, transport defects can mislocalize anti-inflammatory factors and accumulate pro-inflammatory mediators [116]. Nuclear import receptors (NIRs) with chaperone activity that dissolve aggregates are potential therapeutic targets [117].

Post-translational modifications (PTMs) like methylation, ubiquitination, phosphorylation and acetylation significantly change RBP function. Phosphorylation regulates RNA-binding and aggregation; hyperphosphorylation of TDP-43 is linked to pathology in ALS/FTLD, and FUS phosphorylation modulates phase separation [118–120]. Acetylation affects localization and interactions; acetylation of TDP-43 at K84/K136 regulates nuclear import and aggregation, while acetylation of p54nrb/NONO influences IL-8 expression [121, 122]. PTMs are enriched in IDRs, altering condensation properties, and can interact, as in the RGG domain of CIRBP where methylation regulates phase separation and shuttling [123–125]. Arginine methylation by PRMT3 regulates nucleic acid sensor affinity [126]. Ultimately, stress-induced loss of RNA-binding ability—due to PTMs, mutations (e.g. frameshifts in NOVA2), or mislocalization (e.g., mutant FUS)—disrupts the regulation of inflammatory transcripts, completing a self-reinforcing cycle of RBP dysfunction, aberrant phase transitions, and sustained neuroinflammation [127–131] (Fig. 2).

Functional differences and regulatory roles of RNA-binding proteins across neural cell types

RBPs in neurons

RBPs regulate neuronal survival and function by governing the post-transcriptional processing of inflammation-associated genes. SFPQ, a nuclear RBP in neurons, is crucial for paraspeckle formation, DNA repair, transcription regulation, RNA granule assembly, and post-transcriptional control; its dysregulation is linked to neurodegenerative disorders with RNA splicing issues and protein aggregation [132]. RBPs PTBP1 and PTBP2 regulate neuron-specific splicing, affecting axonogenesis, synaptogenesis, and apoptosis, thus influencing neuronal development [133, 134].

RBPs mediate neuronal responses to various stressors and are central to the regulation of synaptic plasticity by modulating mRNA stability, subcellular localization, and translational efficiency [135]. For example, FMRP forms granules in dendrites, regulating mRNA translation for synaptic plasticity and memory; its loss causes excessive dendritic branching due to microtubule and actin protein dysregulation [136, 137]. Circadian regulation affects transport, microtubule organization, and synaptic activity by modulating ALS-linked RBPs, showing their role in neuronal stress adaptation [138].

RBPs regulate neuronal excitability and presynaptic function through interactions with non-coding RNAs. For instance, the long non-coding RNA neuroLNC stabilizes presynaptic mRNAs by binding TDP-43, influencing calcium influx and neuronal migration, highlighting RBPs’ regulatory roles in neuronal development [32]. Furthermore, the RNA helicase MOV10 shapes neuronal structure during development by influencing dendritic morphogenesis and precursor positioning [139].

Notably, the functional regulation of RBPs extends to their ability to undergo liquid-liquid phase separation, enabling the formation of membrane-less organelles such as RNA granules and stress granules [140]. These condensates are vital for mRNA processing and translation in neurons, with aberrant phase separation linked to protein aggregation in neurodegenerative diseases, disrupting homeostasis and causing cell death [141]. Under stress, NMDA receptor excitotoxicity changes RNA structures and protein-RNA interactions, impacting synaptic and neurodegenerative transcripts, highlighting RBP regulatory networks in neuronal stress [142].

RBPs in microglia

Microglia, as the resident immune cells of the central nervous system, perform diverse functions including immune surveillance, regulation of inflammation, and neuroprotection [143]. Recent studies have demonstrated that RBPs modulate the phenotypic transformation and functional states of microglia by regulating multiple aspects of RNA metabolism, such as epidemics, alternative splicing, mRNA stability, and translation [144]. Quaking (QKI) regulates micro-exon splicing in microglia, affecting pro-inflammatory cytokine secretion and phagocytosis; QKI-deficient microglia show increased cytokine release and reduced phagocytosis, hindering CNS remyelination, highlighting QKI’s role in microglial homeostasis [145]. Additionally, CPEB1 regulates RNA processing in microglia, affecting inflammation and phagocytosis; its absence increases IL-6 expression under TLR4 stimulation via multiple signaling pathways [146]. In contrast, Phagocytic function increases without CPEB1, showing RBPs’ diverse roles in regulating microglial functions.

With regard to inflammatory factor release, RBPs regulate microglial immune recognition and effector functions by controlling the stability and translational efficiency of key inflammatory signaling molecules. For example, the long non-coding RNA HOTAIR binds to MYD88 and inhibits its ubiquitin-mediated degradation, thereby promoting microglial activation and the production of pro-inflammatory factors. This illustrates the complexity of co-regulatory mechanisms involving RBPs and lncRNAs in inflammatory signaling [31]. Meanwhile, the RNA-binding protein YB-1 selectively packages miR-223 into microglia-derived exosomes, which alleviates neuronal damage and cognitive deficits, demonstrating the involvement of RBPs in intercellular communication and immunomodulation [35].

Moreover, specific RBPs participate in microglial recognition of pathogens and damage-associated molecular patterns. In viral infection models, long non-coding RNAs and RBPs cooperatively regulate the antiviral response of microglia, modulating interferon and inflammatory cytokine production, underscoring the critical role of RBPs in innate immune signaling [147]. Additionally, deubiquitinating enzymes such as YOD1 function as RBPs that maintain microglial homeostasis and polarization, regulating phagocytosis and inflammatory responses, and contributing to the pathology of Alzheimer’s disease [148].

In models of brain injury and neurodegenerative diseases, abnormal expression of RBPs is closely related to the activation of microglia.In the SAH mouse model, RBPs like Mcm7, Mtdh, SRSF3, and Hnrnpa2b1 affect protein phosphorylation and inflammation by regulating gene splicing, contributing to brain injury [149]. Differentially expressed RBPs in spinal cord injury, like Nkrf, Marcks, NDRG4, and Ryr2, relate to microglia’s immune function, revealing regulatory mechanisms and potential neural repair targets [2].

RBPs in astrocytes

Astrocytes, the most abundant glial cell type in the central nervous system (CNS), play essential roles in maintaining neuronal homeostasis, regulating neuronal metabolism, and modulating neuroinflammatory responses. Accumulating evidence indicates that RBPs not only regulate the expression of inflammatory mediators in astrocytes but also profoundly influence astrocytic metabolic states and their capacity to support neuronal function [150, 151]. In neurodegenerative disorders such as ALS, dysregulation of RNA metabolism in astrocytes is frequently associated with impaired RBP activity, resulting in aberrant secretion of inflammatory mediators and subsequent neuronal degeneration [152]. For instance, Overexpression of CIRP in astrocytes suppresses uPA expression, promoting abnormal phosphorylation of Aβ1–42 and Tau proteins in neurons, increasing Alzheimer’s disease susceptibility [153].

RBPs significantly impact the metabolic state of astrocytes and their ability to support neurons. Astrocytes support neurons by secreting neurotrophic factors and regulating metabolites. AEG-1 stabilizes TWIK-1 mRNA in astrocytes, affecting their properties and metabolism [154]. Additionally, QKI-6, a pivotal RBP, contributes not only to oligodendrocyte development but also to mRNA localization and stability in astrocytes, thereby affecting astrocyte maturation and functional integrity [155]. Through selective binding and post-transcriptional regulation of target mRNAs, these RBPs fine-tune astrocytic metabolism and enhance their supportive roles for neurons.

Furthermore, astrocytes transfer RNA species, including miRNAs, to neurons via small extracellular vesicles (sEVs), thereby influencing neuronal gene expression and synaptic plasticity [156, 157]. Within this context, RBPs such as FUS, SRSF7, and CELF5 are enriched at miRNA-binding sites in sEVs, suggesting their involvement in the selective packaging and intercellular transport of miRNAs, ultimately impacting neuronal viability and adaptability. Notably, β-Arrestin2 regulates localization by interacting with STAT3 in D2 receptor signaling, suppressing JAK-STAT3 inflammation, protecting astrocytes, and alleviating depressive behaviors [158].

RBPs in oligodendrocytes

Oligodendrocytes (OLs), the principal myelinating cells in the central nervous system (CNS), are critical for the rapid conduction of nerve impulses and for providing metabolic support to neurons. RNA-binding proteins (RBPs) play multifaceted and finely regulated roles in governing oligodendrocyte development, myelination, and participation in inflammatory responses and repair processes [159–161].

RBPs are essential regulators of oligodendrocyte development and myelination. PRMT5, found in oligodendrocytes, catalyzes histone dimethylation, influencing chromatin and gene transcription, and promotes myelination by regulating survival and differentiation genes [162]. In addition, PRMT5 is found in the nucleus and cytoplasm, interacting with RNA-binding and ribosomal proteins, affecting RNA splicing, stability, and translation. Furthermore, FMRP is vital for oligodendrocyte maturation and myelin formation; its absence causes defects and delays in maturation, highlighting its role in myelination via mRNA transport and protein synthesis regulation [163].

RBPs contribute significantly to the regulation of inflammatory responses and reparative functions in oligodendrocytes—processes that are critically implicated in neuroinflammatory and neurodegenerative diseases. RNA/DNA-binding proteins like TDP-43 and FUS aggregate pathologically and lose function in ALS and FTD patients [164]. These abnormalities not only compromise neuronal function but also impair oligodendrocyte homeostasis, resulting in disrupted myelin synthesis and diminished metabolic support for axons, thereby contributing to disease progression. RBPs like TDP-43 and PTB are dysregulated in MS oligodendrocytes, showing reduced nuclear levels and abnormal cytoplasmic accumulation, linked to myelin degradation and neurodegeneration [165]. Moreover, hnRNP A1, a well-characterized RBP, has been found to exhibit functional deficits that correlate with altered expression of myelin proteins and decreased survival of oligodendrocytes in both Alzheimer’s disease (AD) and MS [166, 167]. Additionally, PRRC2B, an m6A-binding protein, regulates the development and myelination of oligodendrocyte precursor cells (OPCs) by enhancing the stability of Sox2 mRNA, thereby revealing new molecular pathways and potential strategies for the treatment of myelin-related disorders [168].

RBPs in Endothelial Cells

Endothelial cells (ECs) are a fundamental component of the encephalic vascular system, essential for maintaining vascular homeostasis, regulating blood-brain barrier (BBB) permeability, and mediating inflammatory responses [169, 170]. RBPs influence both immune homeostasis and neuroinflammatory pathology by regulating BBB integrity and the trafficking of inflammatory molecules. For example, RBFOX1 is downregulated in endothelial cells, and its overexpression increases vascular permeability, disrupting the blood-brain barrier. RBFOX1 suppresses lncRNA LINC00673, destabilizing MAFF mRNA, which represses tight junction proteins like ZO-1, Occludin, and Claudin-5, compromising BBB function [171]. These findings demonstrate that RBPs regulate BBB properties by controlling transcription factors and non-coding RNAs, affecting the passage of inflammatory mediators into the CNS.

In addition, RBPs modulate endothelial responses to inflammation; GIGYF2, upregulated in aging endothelial cells, promotes eNOS uncoupling and activates mTORC1-S6K1, accelerating senescence and inflammation [172]. Specifically, GIGYF2 stabilizes STAU1 mRNA, increasing LAMTOR4 expression, which aids mTORC1 translocation to lysosomes and downstream signaling, showing how RBPs control inflammatory signaling via mRNA stability. In oxidative stress models, MSI2 interacts with miR-301a-3p to modify its distribution, affecting endothelial cell stress response [173]. This interaction highlights the role of RBPs in governing the localization and functional availability of non-coding RNAs, ultimately affecting the production and trafficking of inflammatory mediators.

Moreover, Other RBPs like hnRNP H1 and Csde1 respond to TGF-β signaling to regulate EndMT; hnRNP H1 enhances RNA binding to suppress mesenchymal genes, while Csde1’s reduced binding promotes transformation [174]. These opposing regulatory effects underscore the critical involvement of RBPs not only in inflammatory signal transduction but also in phenotypic plasticity of endothelial cells, with implications for vascular inflammation and tissue remodeling. Several RBPs (e.g. FUS, HNRNPA2B1, CANX, CALR, and POLR2A) in diabetic retinopathy models co-express with alternatively spliced genes, modulating inflammatory and apoptotic pathways in endothelial cells under hyperglycemia [175]. This indicates that RBPs contribute to endothelial dysfunction in metabolic disease by regulating alternative splicing and mRNA stability. Functionally, RBPs also link inflammation to cellular metabolism. IGF2BP3 stabilizes LDHA mRNA, restoring glycolysis in endothelial cells and reducing ox-LDL injury, illustrating the link between RNA regulation and energy metabolism in inflammation and adaptation [176].

RBPs in lymphocytes

RNA-binding proteins regulate lymphocyte functions and adaptive responses in neuroinflammation, influencing neuroinflammatory disorders through post-transcriptional mechanisms. RBPs are key in T cell post-transcriptional regulation, affecting mRNA stability and translation for lineage and function. ZFP36 family members (e.g. ZFP36, ZFP36L1, ZFP36L2) degrade mRNAs with AU-rich elements, suppressing CD4+ and CD8+ T cell differentiation and maintaining immune homeostasis [177, 178]. In regulatory T cells (Tregs), ZFP36L1 and ZFP36L2 regulate IFNγ availability by targeting its mRNA for decay, thus preserving Treg stability; loss of either protein results in immune dysregulation and the emergence of pro-inflammatory phenotypesm [177]. Additionally, DDX5, a multifunctional RBP, modulates the balance between terminal effector and memory CD8+ T cell differentiation, highlighting the broad regulatory influence of RBPs on T cell subset specification [179].

RBPs regulate cytokine expression for immune control, with Roquin-1/2 and Regnase-1 suppressing pro-inflammatory mediators to prevent T cell activation and autoimmune issues [180]. RBPs like ZFP36L1 and ATXN2L in CD8+ T cells regulate cytokine production, ensuring timely effector responses and preventing chronic inflammation [181] .

RBP expression profiles display distinct specificity and dynamic changes across stages of lymphocyte differentiation. Transcriptomic and proteomic analyses have revealed substantial differences in RBP expression between B and T cells, with specific RBPs exhibiting stage-specific expression patterns during the development of effector and memory cells [181]. For instance, naïve T cells preferentially express RBPs involved in translational regulation, whereas effector T cells upregulate RBPs that govern mRNA stability [182]. The spatiotemporal patterning highlights RBPs’ regulatory roles in immune cell activation and differentiation, offering insight into RBP control of adaptive immunity in neuroinflammation.

Moreover, RBPs such as contribute to the post-transcriptional regulation of T cell responses through integrated control of RNA splicing, stability, and translation. HuR enhances CD4+ T cell receptor signaling by stabilizing CD3γ mRNA and promoting the expression of downstream signaling molecules Zap70 and Malt1, thereby facilitating Th17 cell differentiation [183]. Concurrently, PCBP1 functions as an intracellular immune checkpoint by inhibiting the conversion of effector T cells into regulatory T cells, thus preserving effector functionality [184]. These findings indicate that RBPs regulate inflammatory factors and shape adaptive immune responses by modulating transcription factors and signaling.

In B cells, RBPs similarly play essential roles. hnRNP F regulates CD40 pre-mRNA splicing, thereby influencing surface CD40 expression and modulating germinal center reactions and antibody production [185]. ZFP36L1 and ZFP36L2 protect germinal center B cells from replication stress by regulating transcripts involved in cell cycle progression, supporting their survival and functional integrity [186]. Furthermore, Bcas2 mediates alternative splicing events that affect antibody class switching, actively contributing to humoral immune responses [187].

Exosomes and RNA-binding proteins in intercellular communication

RNA-binding proteins (RBPs) play a pivotal role in exosome biology by mediating the selective packaging and intercellular transfer of RNA, thereby modulating the transmission and regulation of inflammatory signals between cells [188]. As key mediators of intercellular communication, exosomes transport a diverse array of biomacromolecules, among which non-coding RNAs—particularly microRNAs (miRNAs)—are critical regulators of gene expression and cellular signaling pathways [189, 190]. Understanding how RBPs control RNA loading into exosomes could lead to new diagnostic and therapeutic advancements [191].

RBPs enable the selective encapsulation of RNA species, including miRNAs, through recognition of specific sequence motifs or structural elements within target RNAs. For example, the FMR1 protein recognizes short miRNA sequence motifs exposed following RILP protein cleavage during inflammatory responses, thereby promoting the preferential sorting of these miRNAs into exosomes [36]. Similarly, YBX1 binds to miR-223 via its “cold shock” domain, facilitating the trafficking of this miRNA from mitochondria to intracellular phase-separated condensates and ultimately directing its incorporation into exosomes [192]. In addition, SRSF1 contributes to the enrichment of specific miRNAs in exosomes by recognizing defined sequence motifs [37]. These findings collectively demonstrate that RBPs drive the selective sorting of RNA into exosomes through precise, sequence-dependent interactions with miRNAs.

RBPs not only regulate RNA cargo selection but also influence exosome biogenesis and functional activity. For instance, the hnRNP family member RALY enhances exosome production by modulating multivesicular body (MVB) formation and membrane fusion processes, thereby promoting the activation of tumor-supportive macrophages [193]. Likewise, hnRNP A/B regulates the exosomal loading of miR-27a-5p during Salmonella infection, influencing host immune responses to bacterial invasion [194]. The phase separation properties of YBX1 are key for concentrating RNA-RBP complexes into nascent exosomes [195] .

In the context of inflammatory and neurodegenerative diseases, RBPs cooperate with RNA cargoes within exosomes to propagate or amplify inflammatory signals. Notably, inflammation-induced cleavage of RILP leads to the release of FMR1, which subsequently directs the exosomal loading of inflammation-associated miRNAs [36]. Furthermore, RBPs in brain tissues of Alzheimer’s disease patients exhibit co-localization with exosomal markers, implicating their involvement in pathological protein aggregation and sustained neuroinflammatory responses [196] (Fig. 3).

Neurodegenerative diseases

RNA-binding proteins (RBPs) have emerged as crucial regulators in the pathogenesis of various neurodegenerative disorders, including Alzheimer’s disease (AD), Parkinson’s disease (PD), amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), and multiple sclerosis (MS). These proteins orchestrate complex pathological processes through their ability to regulate RNA metabolism at multiple levels, including splicing, stability, translation, and localization [197]. The dysregulation of RBPs represents a common pathological mechanism that connects different neurodegenerative conditions, despite their distinct clinical manifestations and primary pathological features [198, 199]. Understanding RBP functions provides valuable insights into disease mechanisms and offers promising therapeutic targets for these currently incurable conditions.

In Alzheimer’s disease, RBPs critically regulate the production of amyloid-β (Aβ) peptides and Tau pathology through precise control of associated mRNA species. A genetic association study of 4,314 participants identified a novel locus within RBFOX1 (β = 0.61, P = 3 × 10⁻⁹) associated with amyloidosis in the preclinical phase of Alzheimer disease, with reduced RBFOX1 expression correlating to higher amyloid-β burden (β = -0.008, P = 0.002) and worse cognition (β = 0.007, P = 0.006), suggesting this RNA-binding protein may play a role in the disease’s pathogenesis [200]. The RNA-binding protein PARP16, which shows significant upregulation in AD models, stabilizes amyloid precursor protein (APP) mRNA, leading to increased APP protein levels and enhanced Aβ generation [201].Conversely, FBXL16 promotes APP degradation through the ubiquitin-proteasome system, demonstrating how different RBPs can exert opposing effects on the same pathological pathway [202]. RBFOX1 downregulation correlates with increased amyloid burden and cognitive decline, whereas ELAVL4/HuD and G3BP2 exert protective effects by reducing phosphorylated Tau and inhibiting its aggregation, respectively [200, 203–205]. The involvement of TDP-43 in AD pathophysiology is evidenced by its dysfunctional splicing of downstream targets including STMN2 and UNC13A, which contributes to Tau-related pathology and cognitive impairment [30, 206]. Beyond protein aggregation pathways, RBPs significantly influence neuroinflammatory processes in AD. And beyond proteinopathy, RBPs like METTL3/IGF2BP2 and YOD1 modulate neuroinflammation through m⁶A methylation and deubiquitination—highlighting their broader homeostatic influence [148, 207]. These findings highlight the multifaceted roles of RBPs in AD pathogenesis.

The accumulation of TDP-43 protein pathology in Alzheimer’s disease (AD) exhibits a pronounced age-related correlation, being intrinsically linked to the aging process. Epidemiological evidence indicates that the prevalence of limbic-predominant age-related TDP-43 encephalopathy neuropathological change (LATE-NC) is as high as 30.9% in the oldest-old (≥ 90 years), compared to only 9.4% in the younger-old (< 90 years), directly establishing advanced age as a critical risk factor for TDP-43 pathology [208]. Furthermore, large-scale neuropathological studies have shown that the positivity rate for Alzheimer’s disease neuropathological change (ADNC, comprising Aβ and tau pathology) increases significantly from 46% in the 5th decade to 81% in the 9th decade of life [209]. At the molecular mechanistic level, the age-related emergence of TDP-43 pathology can be explained by the functional decline associated with cellular aging. On one hand, the clearance efficiency of stress granules (SGs) is reduced in aged neurons, making them prone to persistence and facilitating the accumulation of pathological protein aggregates such as TDP-43 [210]. On the other hand, dysregulation of microglia-mediated neuroinflammatory control is pivotal. Research has shown that deficiency of INPP5D, an important AD risk gene in microglia, promotes RIPK1 kinase-mediated neuronal TDP-43 pathology in an age-dependent and non-cell-autonomous manner [211].

From an epidemiological perspective, females are a key risk factor for AD [212]. Global data indicate that age-standardized prevalence and mortality rates of AD are significantly higher in women aged 65 and older compared to men, and the burden of AD and related dementias among postmenopausal women continues to rise [213, 214]. This sexual dimorphism is partly attributable to biological factors such as alterations in post-menopausal estrogen levels [215]. At the molecular level, a multimodal study on the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) revealed a strong sex-biased pattern in intron retention (IR) events in AD. The study found that 1,645 elevated IR events were present in the brains of female AD patients, compared to only 80 in male AD patients. These aberrantly retained introns may lead to mRNA degradation via the nonsense-mediated decay pathway, consequently impacting protein expression [216]. Notably, these differentially retained introns in female AD are enriched for the binding motif of PABPC1 (Poly(A) Binding Protein Cytoplasmic 1), which is an RNA-binding protein crucial for mRNA stability and translation, may mis-localize to Tau aggregates due to IR-generated aberrant transcripts in AD [216–218].

The contribution of RBPs to Parkinson’s disease pathogenesis primarily involves regulation of α-synuclein expression and aggregation. Members of the Pumilio protein family recognize specific sequences in the 3’ untranslated region of SNCA mRNA, negatively regulating α-synuclein expression at the post-transcriptional level [219]. The cooperative action between PUM1 and miR-7 further fine-tunes SNCA mRNA regulation, with PUM1 deficiency leading to abnormal transcript distribution and enhanced protein aggregation [220]. Additional RBPs, including EEF1A1 and ALYREF, contribute to PD progression by modulating inflammatory responses and participating in competitive endogenous RNA networks, respectively [73, 221]. These mechanisms collectively highlight the diverse functions of RBPs in PD pathophysiology.

Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis pathophysiology heavily relies on RBP dysfunction, with TDP-43 and FUS representing the most prominently affected proteins. TDP-43 pathology involves both loss of normal nuclear function and gain of toxic properties, with mislocalized TDP-43 disrupting RNA processing, stress granule dynamics, and genomic stability [222]. Similarly, mutant FUS proteins form insoluble cytoplasmic inclusions that impair RNA metabolism and ultimately lead to neuronal dysfunction [223]. TDP-43 pathology extends beyond motor neurons to actively regulate neuroinflammatory processes, driving astrocyte-mediated production of pro-inflammatory cytokines and activating innate immune sensors through accumulated double-stranded RNA [82, 224, 225]. The pathological involvement of additional RBPs, such as ANXA11, further expands the scope of RBP dysfunction in ALS, with abnormal aggregation accompanied by neuroinflammatory activation and impaired autophagy [226]. Therapeutic strategies targeting these pathways, including inhibition of TDP-43 aggregation and activation of the Nrf2 signaling pathway, show promising neuroprotective effects in preclinical models [21, 227].

In multiple sclerosis, RBPs regulate immune responses and inflammatory mediator expression through diverse mechanisms. A study found that peripheral blood mononuclear cells from patients with multiple sclerosis exhibited reduced HuR protein levels, associated with a 9.8% decreased risk of MS per 100-unit increase (OR: 0.902, 95% CI: 0.83–0.98), alongside elevated HSP70-2 protein levels, linked to an 18.1% increased risk per 100-unit increase (OR: 1.181, 95% CI: 1.03–1.36), suggesting these RNA-binding proteins may play a role in the disease’s pathogenesis [228]. HuR displays differential regulation in MS, with downregulation in peripheral blood mononuclear cells but activating effects in microglia that enhance pro-inflammatory cytokine production [8, 228]. The dysfunctional behavior of hnRNP A1 and TDP-43, characterized by nucleocytoplasmic mislocalization and stress granule formation, contributes significantly to MS neurodegeneration through disrupted RNA metabolism [229–231]. Remyelination processes also involve RBP activity, as demonstrated by HuR modulation, which promotes microglial polarization toward regenerative phenotypes and enhances myelin basic protein expression [8, 232]. The detrimental role of SARM1 in MS pathophysiology, acting through IGFBP2/NF-κB pathway activation, further underscores the importance of targeting RBPs for therapeutic intervention [233].

In summary, RBPs function as master regulators that integrate multiple pathological processes in neurodegenerative diseases. Their abilities to control key pathogenic proteins, mediate neuroinflammatory responses, and maintain RNA metabolic homeostasis position them as promising targets for novel diagnostic and therapeutic development. The multifaceted roles of RBPs across different neurodegenerative conditions highlight their central position in disease pathogenesis and their potential as biomarkers and therapeutic targets [19, 234–236].

CNS infections

Bacterial infections are major triggers of neuroinflammation, in which RNA-binding proteins (RBPs) play central roles by recognizing pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) and regulating host immune responses. During bacterial infection, host cells utilize RBPs to sense microbial RNA and initiate innate immune defenses [237]. For example, the host RBP DnaJ is required for efficient recognition of bacterial RNA by proteins like bNACHT25, highlighting RBP’s role in pathogen sensing [238]. Lipopolysaccharide (LPS) upregulates host RBPs like CIRP, whose extracellular form impairs macrophage phagocytosis and compromises host defense; CIRP neutralization restores phagocytic function, indicating its negative regulatory role [27]. Upstream, the transcription factor AP-1 directly activates CIRP expression in Streptococcus pneumoniae-induced sepsis-associated encephalopathy. AP-1 inhibition reduces CIRP levels, attenuates microglial activation, and ameliorates neuronal degeneration and cognitive deficits, highlighting the AP-1/CIRP axis as a key neuroinflammatory pathway [239]. The antimicrobial peptide CRAMP, which possesses RNA-binding properties, protects against LPS-induced neuroinflammation by negatively regulating glial activation, demonstrating that both canonical RBPs and RNA-binding molecules limit excessive inflammation [240]. RBPs also regulate intercellular communication by modulating miRNA sorting, as demonstrated by hnRNP A/B’s role in exosomal packaging of miR-27a-5p in Salmonella-infected macrophages [194].

Similarly, viral infections trigger neuroinflammation through RBP-mediated recognition of viral components and regulation of antiviral responses. In JEV and WNV infections, the non-coding RNA JINR1 binds RBM10 to repress antiviral miRNAs, promoting viral replication and neuronal death. Knockdown of JINR1 or RBM10 reduces viral load and cell death, demonstrating RBP involvement in host-virus interactions [28]. RBPs also regulate interferon signaling; for example, lncRNA RP5-998N21.4 enhances IFN-stimulated genes via RBP RMB14 [241]. Similarly, CNBP directly binds the IFNB promoter to induce type I interferons, and CNBP-deficient mice show increased susceptibility to influenza [242]. Viruses often exploit host RBPs: JEV NS3 binds HNRNPH1 to enhance viral translation while suppressing interferon production [243]; ZIKV sfRNA competes with host RBPs like RHA and PACT to impair RNA interference [244]; and hnRNP K is essential for CHIKV replication in glial cells [245].

Collectively, RBPs are master regulators of the neuroimmune landscape during infection, functioning as a double-edged sword that can be targeted for innovative anti-infective therapies [246, 247].

Stroke and hypoxia-induced brain injury

RNA-binding proteins (RBPs) have emerged as pivotal regulators of neuroinflammation induced by cerebral ischemia and hypoxia, two conditions sharing common pathological features.

Stroke, particularly ischemic stroke, induces brain tissue injury through the activation of multiple pathological cascades, including endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress, inflammatory responses, and apoptosis [248]. In this context, RBPs serve as critical post-transcriptional regulators by modulating key signaling pathways that determine neuronal survival and immune activation [249]. For instance, ALKBH5, an m6A demethylase, is upregulated in stroke models and protects neurons by stabilizing STAT5 mRNA, reducing ER stress through the PERK/eIF2α/CHOP pathway [24]. Conversely, CircPTP4A2 is markedly upregulated after cerebral ischemia and promotes pro-inflammatory microglial polarization, while circDlgap4 interacts with the RBP AUF1 to stabilize NRF2 mRNA, thereby activating antioxidant defenses and limiting ischemic brain damage [33, 34]. The dual roles of RBPs are further highlighted by the nuclear receptor NR4A1, which destabilizes TNF mRNA to suppress inflammation, and by RPS3, which promotes neuroinflammation [250]. Similarly, the extracellular form of CIRP (eCIRP) acts as a damage-associated molecular pattern that amplifies neuroinflammation in ischemic stroke. It impairs microglial phagocytic function via the TLR4/miR-155/MafB axis and activates the neuronal IL-6Ra/STAT3/Cdk5 pathway, leading to neurotoxicity and increased infarct expansion [78]. Clinically, circulating eCIRP levels correlate strongly with stroke severity and poor functional outcomes, positioning it as both a biomarker and a promising therapeutic target [251]. The RBP RPS3 promotes M1 microglial polarization by inhibiting SIRT1-mediated suppression of the NLRP3 inflammasome, thereby enhancing neuroinflammatory responses and neuronal apoptosis. Genetic or pharmacological inhibition of RPS3 improves neurological deficits and reduces brain injury, identifying it as a novel molecular target for intervention [252].

Beyond the context of stroke, RBPs play an equally critical role in the neuroinflammatory response to hypoxia. Under hypoxic conditions such as high-altitude or perinatal hypoxia, intracellular CIRP exerts protective effects by binding to the 3’-UTR of PSD95 or mitochondrial ferritin mRNA, thereby preserving synaptic architecture and suppressing ferroptosis to limit inflammatory cell death [94, 253]. It further maintains mitochondrial respiration by stabilizing complex I and V activity, preventing energy failure that amplifies neuroinflammation [254]. On the other hand, downregulation of CIRBP inhibits astroglial differentiation through its interaction with the transcription factor NFIA, which constitutes a key cellular mechanism underlying the emotional and social behavioral abnormalities in offspring induced by chronic prenatal hypoxia [255]. Conversely, CIRP downregulation leads to pathological outcomes, including impaired synaptic receptor stability and mitochondrial dysfunction, which fuel neuroinflammation and cognitive decline [256, 257]. Beyond CIRP, other RBPs like hnRNPA2/B1 fine-tune the hypoxic response through mechanisms such as Kapβ2-reversible phase transitions [258, 259].Collectively, this RBP network functions as a sophisticated post-transcriptional rheostat, dynamically modulating neuroinflammation based on the severity and context of the insult.

Paraneoplastic neurological syndromes

Paraneoplastic Neurological Syndromes (PNS) are immune-mediated disorders triggered by tumors [260]. Tumors express neuronal antigens with RNA-binding domains, leading to autoantibodies that cross-react with nervous system proteins, causing autoimmune dysfunction. RNA-binding proteins (RBPs) serve as tumor-associated antigens and modulate immune responses. For example, HuD is overexpressed in small cell lung cancer. This prompts the immune system to generate anti-Hu antibodies that target HuD-expressing neurons, causing sensory neuronopathy, cranial nerve deficits, and other manifestations [26].

In PNS, the dysregulated expression of RBPs is closely linked to the initiation and propagation of autoimmune responses. The PNMA2 protein, derived from a retrotransposon, exemplifies this mechanism: it self-assembles into non-enveloped virus-like particles that are secreted extracellularly [261]. Surface-exposed epitopes on these particles activate B cells to produce anti-PNMA2 autoantibodies, triggering severe autoimmune encephalitis and cerebellar degeneration [262]. These findings underscore the pivotal role of RBPs in mediating immunological cross-talk between tumors and the nervous system.

Clinically, PNS presents with heterogeneous features, including cerebellar degeneration, brainstem dysfunction, NMOSD, myoclonus, dystonia, and sensory neuropathy. Patients exhibit autoantibodies targeting RBPs and neural antigens [263, 264]. Anti-CV2/CRMP5 antibodies associate with limbic encephalitis and cerebellar atrophy; anti-Ri antibodies correlate with brainstem and cerebellum involvement; anti-SOX1 antibodies link to syndromes in small cell lung cancer [265–267]. These profiles reflect RBP-driven mechanisms and highlight RBPs as diagnostic markers.

Furthermore, different tumor types are associated with distinct antibody signatures and clinical phenotypes. In breast cancer, paraneoplastic NMOSD is frequently accompanied by anti-aquaporin-4 antibodies (AQP4-IgG), suggesting that tumor-induced autoimmunity may involve RBP-regulated immune pathways, even when the primary antigen is not an RBP itself [268]. In malignant lymphomas, autoantibodies such as anti-Tr/DNER and anti-mGluR5 are commonly detected and are linked to cerebellar degeneration and peripheral neuropathy, indicating a broad spectrum of RBP-related immune dysregulation across cancer types [269].

Mechanistically, RBPs act as tumor antigens and contribute to pathogenesis by regulating RNA metabolism and antigen presentation. Aberrantly expressed RBPs form extracellular complexes that enhance antigen visibility, promoting B-cell activation and neural-reactive antibodies. This leads to T- and B-cell infiltration, neuroinflammation, and neuronal injury [262].

PNS diagnosis relies on neurological symptoms, evidence of malignancy, and onconeural antibodies. Antibodies targeting RBPs provide key serological evidence for early diagnosis. Treatment combines tumor-directed therapy (e.g., surgery, chemotherapy) with immunomodulatory interventio(corticosteroids, immunoglobulins, plasma exchange). Efficacy varies by antibody specificity, timing, and patient factors, necessitating personalized strategies [26, 270].

Traumatic injuries of the central nervous system

RNA-binding proteins (RBPs) are central regulators of neuroinflammation and repair across traumatic brain injury (TBI) and spinal cord injury (SCI). A bibliometric review and meta-analysis of RNA-binding protein (RBP) research in trauma and burns, encompassing 539 publications from 2000 to 2024, identified key research hotspots in the pathophysiological mechanisms of traumatic injuries and cutaneous wound healing, with a meta-analysis of four studies involving 5,976 patients showing RBP expression levels correlate with poor prognosis in traumatic brain injury (pooled sensitivity: 0.61, 95% CI: 0.45–0.76; specificity: 0.87, 95% CI: 0.65–0.96; AUC: 0.77) [271]. A key mediator is cold-inducible RNA-binding protein (CIRP), particularly its extracellular form (eCIRP). Following injury, eCIRP is secreted by neurons and microglia, with its levels correlated with pro-inflammatory mediators; its absence in knockout models reduces atrophy, apoptosis, and glial activation and improves outcomes. Mechanistically, it promotes neuronal death via ER stress (PERK-ATF4-CHOP pathway) and modulates microglial responses through histone H3 acetylation [22, 272]. Similarly, in SCI, CIRP is rapidly upregulated in microglia, amplifying the inflammatory cascade in TBI, the astrocyte-enriched circMETTL9 binds to the RBP SND1, enhancing the expression of chemokines CCL2 and CXCL1, which amplifies neuroinflammation and contributes to neurodegeneration and cognitive impairment [25, 273].

The m6A pathway is pivotal: in TBI, WTAP upregulation promotes NLRP3 inflammasome activation via m6A methylation, and its inhibition attenuates damage [85] .In SCI, METTL3-mediated m6A methylation of YAP1 mRNA enhances IGF2BP2 binding, promoting astrogliosis yet improving locomotor outcomes [274].

The RBP HuR, for instance, drives microglial activation in both TBI and SCI models; in SCI, it stabilizes HDAC1 mRNA to exacerbate neuronal death and motor impairments, and its silencing alleviates neuroinflammation [275, 276]. In SCI, knockdown of PTB reprograms reactive astrocytes into neuron-like cells, reducing glial scar formation and improving functional recovery [277].

Other RBPs also contribute to SCI pathology, such as ELAVL1 which limits repair by promoting M1 polarization and inhibiting neural differentiation, while GRSF1 and IGF2BP2 exert protective effects by suppressing ferroptosis and attenuating inflammation, respectively [278–280]. Collectively, these studies delineate a multidimensional RBP network that integrates inflammatory, cell death, and reparative processes, presenting strategic targets for treating traumatic injuries of the central nervous system (Table 2).

RNA-binding proteins in neuroinflammatory diseases

Conclusion

RBPs regulate neuroinflammation by influencing all inflammatory phases and ensuring neural integrity through molecular interactions. They perform specialized functions in different nervous system cells: maintaining genomic fidelity in neurons, shaping inflammatory responses in microglia, and enhancing stress resilience in astrocytes. These distinct roles highlight the need for cell-specific therapies. In diseases such as Alzheimer’s and Parkinson’s, RBPs serve as diagnostic and therapeutic targets. However, the current fragmented nature of research underscores the need for a unified approach to better clarify their roles and functions.To sum up, RBPs in neuroinflammation serve as a link between basic science and clinical innovation, and their specific mechanisms, multi-omics integration, and validation require thorough exploration. Through interdisciplinary collaboration, these efforts can advance precision medicine for neuroinflammatory disorders.

Limitations and future perspectives

Several critical limitations remain in the current understanding of RBPs in neuroinflammation. First, the role of age is underexplored, as most studies rely on young animal models that fail to capture the aged CNS microenvironment. Second, sex differences constitute a major knowledge gap, with limited insights into how sex hormones modulate RBP networks. Third, the spatiotemporal complexity and cell-type specificity of RBP functions remain poorly resolved, as current evidence largely stems from bulk tissues or static measurements. Addressing these gaps through aged models, sex-inclusive experimental designs, and single-cell spatial omics approaches will be essential to advance the field.
