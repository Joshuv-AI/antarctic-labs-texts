Narrative review of neurodevelopmental and psychiatric complications associated with prematurity
Authors: Halimat Ibrahim, Alyssa Wilkins, Sumera Aziz Ali, Sarka Lisonkova, Natalie H. Chan, Joseph Y. Ting
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Prematurity, defined as birth before 37 weeks of gestation, remains a significant global health concern due to its strong association with increased infant morbidity and mortality. Despite significant advances in antenatal care and neonatal intensive care, preterm infants remain highly susceptible to complications including bronchopulmonary dysplasia, intraventricular hemorrhage, and necrotizing enterocolitis. These conditions not only affect immediate survival but also contribute to long-term neurodevelopmental and neuropsychiatric challenges that may persist throughout the life span. Survivors of preterm born infants continue to face higher risks of cognitive, motor, and behavioural impairments, as well as psychiatric disorders such as attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder, anxiety, and depression. This narrative review synthesises the recent findings regarding long-term impacts of prematurity on neurodevelopmental and neuropsychiatric outcomes. It highlights their incidence, risk factors, and the screening and assessment tools currently used in clinical and research settings. By synthesising current knowledge, the review aims to guide clinical care, support early identification of at-risk infants, and inform future research priorities.

Introduction

Prematurity, defined as birth before 37 weeks of gestation, represents a significant global health challenge due to its association with increased mortality and morbidity, with profound consequences for families and healthcare systems [1, 2]. In 2020, an estimated13.4 million infants were born preterm, accounting for 9.9% of all births worldwide [3]. Of these, approximately 15% occurred prior to 32 weeks of gestation, including 4.2% before 28 weeks and 10.4% between 28 and 32 weeks of gestation [3].

Over recent decades, survival rates for preterm infants have improved markedly [4],largely attributed to advances in antenatal care, the administration of corticosteroids to promote lung maturity, and the development of advanced neonatal intensive care units (NICUs) [4, 5]. These medical innovations have been particularly transformative for extremely preterm infants (< 28 weeks gestation), who now have a much higher likelihood of survival. Through the efforts of the Canadian Neonatal Network, including network-wide quality-improvement initiatives, survival rates for infants born at 23–32 weeks gestational age (GA) admitted to tertiary NICUs have improved, with the rate of survival without major neonatal morbidity ranging from 56.6 to 70.9% [6].

Despite these strides, the complications associated with prematurity remain a significant concern [7]. Preterm infants are particularly vulnerable to various neonatal morbidities, including bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD), intraventricular hemorrhage (IVH), and necrotizing enterocolitis (NEC) [8], which not only pose immediate health risks but are linked with long-term neurodevelopmental impairment (NDI) [8]. Many survivors continue to face complex and lifelong health challenges.

While evidence linking preterm birth (PTB) to adverse health conditions is expanding, important knowledge gaps remain in the literature. Particularly, less is known about how heterogeneous morbidities during early life shape early brain development and influence risks for neuropsychiatric and neurodevelopmental disorders across childhood and adolescence. This narrative review synthesises the current literature on long-term outcomes in survivors of prematurity and provides an overview of the most common neurodevelopmental and neuropsychiatric disorders observed in preterm infants, including their incidence, prevalence, and risk factors; and the screening and assessment tools currently used in clinical and research settings. By examining these issues, this review aims to inform clinical practice and policy, emphasising the ongoing challenges faced by preterm infants and the need for sustained research and intervention strategies to improve long-term outcomes.

Literature search strategy

A comprehensive search of PubMed, Embase, and Scopus was conducted to identify research studies published between 1990 and 2024. Search terms included combinations of prematurity, preterm birth, neurodevelopmental disorders, neuropsychiatric outcomes, cognitive development, cerebral palsy, attention deficit hyperactivity disorder, autism spectrum disorder, developmental coordination disorder (DCD), intellectual disability, seizures, hearing impairment, and visual impairment; anxiety, and depression. Additional references were identified by reviewing the bibliographies of key original research articles. Eligible publications included peer-reviewed original studies, systematic reviews, and meta-analyses that reported findings on neurodevelopmental or psychiatric outcomes in individuals born preterm. Articles were excluded if they were not published in English, focused solely on short-term neonatal outcomes without follow-up into childhood or adolescence, or were case reports, case series, qualitative studies, or conference abstracts. The search was updated in September 2025 to ensure inclusion of the most recent evidence.

Common neurodevelopmental disorders

Neurodevelopmental disorders encompass a wide range of disabilities characterized by disruptions in brain development [9]. Cerebral palsy, DCD, intellectual disability, seizures, hearing impairment, and visual impairment associated with retinopathy of prematurity (ROP) are among the most frequently recognised sequelae among infants born prematurely [10] (Table 1). Conditions such as BPD, infections, NEC, and severe ROP are known predictors of adverse neurodevelopmental outcomes in preterm infants [81]. These complications can significantly affect cognitive and motor development, emphasizing the need for early intervention and long-term follow-up.

Recent studies have indicated that while survival rates amongst extremely preterm infants have improved over time [82, 83], a significant number of survivors continue to experience NDI. For instance, the rate of survival without NDI among these infants increased from 16% in 2000–2003 to 20% in 2008–2011 [83]. Despite these improvements, the overall risk remains high [11, 84]: about 43% of surviving infants born at 22 to 24 weeks gestation demonstrate NDI by 18 to 22 months of corrected age [83].

NDI is commonly defined using the Bayley Scales of Infant and Toddler Development 3rd edition (Bayley-III), which assesses the infant’s cognition, language, social-emotional, adaptive, and motor development [85]. Mild NDI is defined as having a motor or cognitive composite score of 70–84 on the Bayley-III; moderate NDI includes a motor or cognitive composite score of less than 70 on the Bayley-III [85]. In contrast, severe NDI includes a motor or cognitive composite score of less than 55 on the Bayley-III.

Many neonatal follow-up studies focus on early assessments between 18 and 36 months. However, the predictive validity of NDI over time varies as children grow older. For instance, a retrospective cohort study conducted in the United States assessed NDI at age 2 and found that it only weakly predicted long-term NDI in middle childhood [86]. Among children with moderate to severe NDI at 2 years, 63% were re-classified to none-to-mild NDI at 10 years, while 36% of those with profound NDI at 2 years had none-to-mild impairment at 10 years [86]. This highlights either limitations of early assessment tools, potential improvement in NDI over time, or both.

Though Bayley-III is widely used both in clinical and research settings [87], their reliability in early cognitive assessments is debated [88, 89]. These assessments may overestimate cognitive impairment, with rates of impairment generally decreasing over time [88, 89]. Additionally, these early evaluations can be confounded by language, motor, and visual skills, which may obscure the assessment of pure cognitive functions. Cognitive development trajectories can vary significantly over time, with some impairments only becoming apparent as children encounter more complex tasks [90, 91]. These findings underscore the need for ongoing and repeated evaluations to accurately track neurodevelopmental outcomes.

There is no shortage of perinatal or early neonatal interventions aimed at reducing adverse neurodevelopmental outcomes. Many studies have considered treatment options such as prophylactic magnesium sulphate, prophylactic erythropoietin (EPO), indomethacin, and antenatal corticosteroids [92–95]. Antenatal corticosteroid administration for women at risk of preterm birth has been shown to reduce the incidence of IVH, and consequently brain injury [96]. Additionally, the Society of Obstetricians and Gynaecologists of Canada recommends administering magnesium sulphate for fetal neuroprotection, particularly when women present at ≤ 33 + 6 weeks with imminent preterm birth [97], supported by multiple systematic reviews and randomized controlled trials.

Evidence for other interventions is less consistent. Prophylactic administration of EPO for neuroprotection in preterm babies yielded variable results. For instance, a multicenter trial of very preterm infants (26–31 weeks’ gestation) showed no benefit at 2 years in cognition or motor development, cerebral palsy, hearing, or visual impairment with early high-dose EPO treatment [93]. The PENUT trial in extremely preterm infants (24–28 weeks of gestation) did not find a reduction in death or severe NDI at 22–26 months with high-dose EPO treatment [94]. Conversely, meta-analyses of multiple RCTs, encompassing over 1100 preterm infants, suggest that prophylactic EPO may improve cognitive scores,, but not secondary outcomes such as cerebral palsy or sensory deficits [95, 98]. Similarly, Schmidt et al. (2001) reported that prophylactic indomethacin reduces the incidence of severe IVH in extremely low birth weight infants (500–900 g), but it does not improve survival without neurodevelopmental disorders at 18 months [99].

Together, these findings indicate that the impact of certain interventions such as prophylactic EPO or indomethacin on long-term neurodevelopment remains inconsistent, underscoring the need for further studies to refine dosing strategies, optimal timing, and follow-up assessments.

A major challenge in identifying neonatal interventions with durable benefits lies in the multitude of factors intervening between the intervention and later neurodevelopmental outcomes. Social risks and social determinants of health, both as antecedents to preterm birth and as contributors to ongoing long-term impacts, exemplify this complexity. Ongoing research and the development of targeted treatments offer hope for mitigating the burden of these disorders on affected children and their families.

Cerebral palsy and prematurity

Cerebral Palsy (CP) is a group of permanent movement and posture disorders that cause activity limitations and are attributed to non-progressive disturbances in the developing fetal or infant brain [100]. Although CP only affects 2 to 3 per 1000 live births, it remains the most common cause of serious physical disability in childhood [10, 100]. Findings from recently published reviews have established preterm birth as a major risk factor for CP [13–15]. Other predictors of CP included preeclampsia, premature rupture of membranes, very low birth weight (less than 1500g), periventricular leukomalacia (PVL), grade 3 to 4 IVH, birth asphyxia, and low Apgar scores [16].

Among these predictors, preterm birth is one of the strongest determinants of CP. For instance, Larsen et al. (2022) reported that the risk of CP is notably higher in infants born at the earliest gestational ages (21–24 weeks), with a risk ratio (RR) of 70.7 compared to term births [11]. The prevalence of CP decreases insignificantly until gestational week 29, after which it declines significantly by nearly 40% per week until term [11]. Likewise, findings from a population-based cohort study suggest that preterm birth is strongly associated with CP, with odds ratios (ORs) ranging from 8.8 (GA: 28 to 31 weeks) to 18.2 (GA < 28 weeks) compared to term infants [12]. Similarly, evidence from a recent systematic review and meta-analysis suggest that PTB significantly increases the risk of CP (OR: 2.90, 95% CI: 2.13–3.93) [15].

IVH occurs in 10–20% of preterm infants born before 30 weeks of gestation, and the rate increases to 35 to 45% among those with a birth weight below 750 g [17–19]. The pathogenesis of IVH in preterm babies involves hemorrhage originating from the germinal matrix, which is a region rich in immature capillary vessels and neuroglial cells, leading to bleeding due to factors like immature blood flow regulation, inflammation, and mechanical stress [20]. Severe IVH (Grade 3–4) and PVL are among the strong predictors of CP among preterm babies. More than 50% of newborns with these lesions develop CP with a substantial magnitude of risk [21–23]. Grade 3–4 IVH is associated with an OR of 5.3 (95% CI: 2.1–13.1), while PVL carries an even higher risk with OR of 46.4 (95% CI: 20.6–104.6) for moderate to severe CP [22].

Other risk factors for CP include birth asphyxia (OR: 5.9), congenital anomalies (OR: 4.3), cord prolapse (OR: 2.0), and fetal distress (OR 2.1) [12]. In addition, low 5-minute Apgar scores, particularly among infants born at 32–36 weeks of gestation (adjusted RR: 8.27, 95% CI: 1.87–36.64 for 0 to 4 scores; adjusted RR: 4.96, 95% CI: 1.89–13.06 for scores 5 to 6), are strongly associated with CP [24]. Male sex has also been shown to increase the risk (OR 2.5, 95% CI 1.4–4.5) [22].

Early identification of high-risk infants is critical for improving outcomes. Current evidence suggests the most reliable approach for predicting CP by 12 months corrected age involves the use of magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) at term-equivalent age in combination with General Movements assessment at 12 weeks corrected age [101–104]. Electroencephalogram (EEG) abnormalities within the first month of life also represent a significant predictor of adverse neurodevelopmental outcomes at 12 to 18 months corrected age [105]. Identifying preterm infants at risk for CP through neurobehavioural and neurological assessments enables timely interventions that may improve long term outcomes [101–104]. Additionally, interventions such as prophylactic administration of magnesium sulphate have shown potential in reducing the incidence of CP among very low birth weight infants [97]​. While cerebral palsy remains a complex and multifaceted condition, a deeper understanding of its risk factors and pathogenesis is essential to the development of better prevention and treatment strategies.

Hearing and visual impairment and prematurity

ROP is a significant risk factor for visual impairment in children born preterm. In 2010, an estimated 184,700 preterm infants worldwide developed ROP, with approximately 20,000 progressing to blindness or severe visual impairment [106]. ROP is characterized by the abnormal proliferation of blood vessels into various layers of the retina [107]. The pathophysiology of ROP is believed to stem from incomplete retinal vascularization, leading to retinal hypoxia. This hypoxic environment stimulates the overexpression of vascular endothelial growth factors (VEGF), which drives aberrant vascular development [108]. High-grade ROP, particularly stage 3 and beyond, is associated with severe long-term complications, including retinal detachment, which may lead to permanent blindness [28]. Traditional laser photocoagulation is effective but carries risks of peripheral vision loss and high myopia. Anti-VEGF therapy, such as Bevacizumab, has gained prominence as a therapeutic option in recent years as it offers a less invasive and potentially safer alternative [28, 109].

Auditory development disorders are also frequently observed among preterm infants, with those born extremely preterm exhibiting an 8- to 13-fold increased risk of hearing loss compared to full-term infants [25, 26]. NICU admission, particularly when accompanied by exposure to nephrotoxic medications like aminoglycosides and furosemide, has been recognized as a significant contributing factor to this elevated risk [29]. The frequency of hearing impairment in preterm infants varies across studies and is closely associated with GA [25, 26]. For instance, Jin et al. (2022) reported a 0.6% rate of bilateral hearing loss in extremely preterm infants, with incidence decreasing progressively as GA increased [25]. Similarly, another study found that 2.1% of children born before 27 weeks GA experienced hearing impairment, compared to 0.5% in term infants [110]. Notably, recent large-scale evidence suggests that vulnerability is not limited to extremely preterm infants. A nationwide Swedish cohort study involving over 1.28 million children revealed that those born moderately preterm (32 to 33 weeks) and late preterm (34 to 36 weeks) faced significantly higher risks of NDI, including hearing loss, relative to those born at 39 to 40 weeks [27]. Although the risk declined with increasing GA, it remained elevated even among early term infants (37 to 38 weeks), underscoring that hearing loss and other neurodevelopmental deficits span the broader preterm spectrum [27].

Aside from NICU admission, elevated peak bilirubin levels represent another significant risk factor for hearing loss in preterm infants. They are particularly susceptible to hyperbilirubinemia due to a combination of physiological vulnerabilities, including a shorter red blood cell lifespan, immature hepatic conjugation mechanisms, and reduced bilirubin binding capacity [111]. High bilirubin levels are neurotoxic, particularly to the auditory system, and have been implicated in cases of neonatal hearing loss [112]. One study found that in infants born before 29 weeks of gestation, higher peak bilirubin concentrations (269 to 531 mmol/L) within the first 14 days of life were associated with an increased risk of NDI and hearing impairments [113]. Several other studies have noted similar correlations [114, 115]. These findings underscore the critical importance of early monitoring and management of bilirubin levels in preterm neonates to mitigate the risk of long-term neurological and sensory deficits.

Seizure disorder and prematurity

The prevalence of seizures is notably higher in preterm infants, at approximately 22.2%, compared to just 0.5% in term neonates [30–32]. However, prevalence rates vary depending on study design, whether hospital- or population-based, and the diagnostic methods employed, particularly in distinguishing between clinically observed and EEG-confirmed seizures [33]. The prevalence of epilepsy, defined as two or moreunprovoked seizures more than 24 h apart, declines steadily with advancing GA, decreasing by approximately 9.2% per week from 4.6% at 21–24 weeks to 13.5% closer to term [11]. The primary etiologies of neonatal seizures include IVH, central nervous system infections, hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy, and metabolic causes [34]. Seizures in preterm infants are associated with higher risks of NDIs, including CP, epilepsy, and hearing and visual impairments [33, 34]. Preterm infants who experience seizures face neurologic complications, including epilepsy, CP, and intellectual disabilities, in up to 46% of cases, whereas about 30% of term infants with seizures develop similar outcomes [34, 35].

Cranial ultrasound and MRI are recommended for detecting underlying brain injuries and guiding prognosis [33]. One study found that most affected children exhibit high risk cranial ultrasound lesions, such as posthemorrhagic hydrocephalus, cystic periventricular leukomalacia, and hemorrhagic parenchymal infarction (IVH- grade IV), which often involve the white matter of the brain [116]. Children with epilepsy and high risk cranial ultrasound lesions demonstrate greater cognitive impairment compared to those with similar lesions but without epilepsy, suggesting significant cortical grey matter damage and extensive brain injury [116]. Therefore, early identification and monitoring of high-risk lesions through neonatal cranial ultrasound are important to predict long-term neurodevelopmental outcomes.

Managing seizures in preterm infants can be complicated. Currently, there are no established guidelines for the routine use of maintenance anti-seizure medication in this population [33, 117]. Clinicians typically determine daily medication regimens, including first- or second-line treatments, based on the initial response to therapy and observed behavioural changes in the infant [33]. Due to developmental differences between preterm and term infants, conventional antiseizure medications, such as phenobarbital and phenytoin, are generally less effective in preterm infants [33]. Alternative medications, including levetiracetam, topiramate, and midazolam, have been proposed as potentially effective options. Among these, levetiracetam has gained attention for its favorable safety profile and low risk of side effects [33]. To improve outcomes in these vulnerable populations, continued research and the development of specialized treatment protocols are essential to establish evidence-based strategies to manage and mitigate the long-term effects of neonatal seizures.

Developmental coordination disorder and prematurity

Developmental Coordination Disorder (DCD) is a prevalent neurodevelopmental condition characterized by challenges in acquiring and performing motor skills, which can impact educational, social, and self-care activities [118]. Children born very premature, defined as before 32 weeks of gestation, are at significantly increased risk of developing DCD, with incidence rates estimated to be 6–8 times higher than those observed in children born at term (9.5 to 37.1% vs. 2. to 5.5%) [36, 37]. A recently published systematic review and meta-analysis of 32 studies suggested the prevalence of DCD in preterm children is approximately 21% (95% CI: 17.8 to 24.3%), with risk increasing as GA decreases [38]. The analysis further indicated that preterm children are about twice as likely to develop DCD compared to term-born peers, underscoring the heightened neurodevelopmental vulnerability associated with preterm birth [38].

DCD has also been associated with several perinatal and neonatal risk factors, reflecting both biological vulnerability and adverse early life exposure. Among the strongest predictors, often co-occurring with disturbed brain development and impaired motor pathway organization, are low birth weight and prematurity [39, 40]. Perinatal complications such as prolonged rupture of membranes, labor distress, and pathological jaundice may also predispose infants to DCD by causing hypoxic-ischemic or metabolic damage [41]. Additional risk factors include poor socioeconomic status (SES), advanced maternal age, and intrauterine growth restriction [40, 42]. Postnatal insults like steroid exposure and chronic lung disease further compound the risk, while male sex and comorbid illnesses like ROP are also linked to higher incidence of DCD [41–43]. The interaction of these variables highlights the multifactorial nature of DCD, with both prenatal and postnatal factors shaping neurodevelopmental outcomes.

Although motor delays in preterm children with DCD are often noticeable in early childhood, the diagnosis is typically not made until they reach school age [119]. This delay is largely attributed to the progressive emergence of motor challenges, which become more apparent in structured environments like schools, where both fine and gross motor skills are extensively tested [119]. Diagnosis is commonly established using the Movement Assessment Battery for Children, in which scores above the 15th percentile are considered within the normal range, scores between the 6th and 15th percentiles indicate risk, and scores at or below the 5th percentile reflect significant motor difficulties [120]. Validated screening tools such as the Little DCD-Q for preschool children and DCD-Q for older children are also used to aid in early identification [121, 122].

Early feeding behaviours among preterm infants have been shown to predict various neurodevelopmental outcomes later in life. For example, Rinat et al. (2022) discovered a strong predictive relationship between early feeding difficulties and long-term neurodevelopmental outcomes in extremely preterm infants [123]. The authors found that 40% of infants with feeding difficulties were diagnosed with DCD at ages 4–5, compared to 26% of those without feeding issues [123]. These findings highlight the necessity of routine developmental follow-up and assessments for preterm infants, with particular attention to feeding behaviours as an early marker for potential neurodevelopmental disorders.

Interventions have been recommended to support children with DCD by equipping them with skills, strategies, and accommodations to improve performance in motor tasks essential for daily living and school activities [124]. These interventions may include physical therapy, occupational therapy, and tailored support within educational settings [124]. A recent systematic review has further confirmed the effectiveness of activity-oriented, or task-oriented, interventions for children with DCD [125]. The review also found that combining body function-oriented approaches with activity-oriented interventions, including the use of active video games, significantly enhances therapeutic outcomes. These findings suggest that a multifaceted intervention strategy is particularly effective in improving motor skills and promoting overall development in children with DCD [125].

School failure, cognitive impairment and prematurity

Prematurity is associated with a broad range of cognitive challenges, including deficits in executive functioning, attention, and memory, which are crucial for academic success [126]. Children born prematurely often struggle with initiating activities, organizing tasks, thinking flexibly, solving problems, and retaining information in working memory, all of which can significantly hinder school performance [126]. Weaknesses in working memory and visuomotor integration have been consistently reported among preterm survivors across diverse international contexts [126, 127].

A large meta-analysis of 74 studies including 64,061 children found that preterm children perform about 0.50standard deviations (SD) lower in working memory and processing speed compared to their term-born peers [44]. Similarly, findings from another meta-analysis indicate that children born very preterm and/or very low birth weight score significantly lower on academic assessments [45]. Specifically, they score 0.60 SD lower in mathematics, 0.48 SD lower in reading, and 0.76 SD lower in spelling compared to full-term children [45]. These cognitive and academic deficits frequently contribute to school failure and an increased need for special educational needs (SEN). The likelihood of requiring SEN decreases by 12.5% (95% CI: 11.2–13.7%) for each additional week the fetus remains in utero up to full term gestation [11].

With widespread cognitive deficits affecting working memory, processing speed, and executive function, children born preterm typically have lower intelligence quotient (IQ) scores and face more academic challenges compared to their full-term peers [44, 45]. These difficulties can significantly impact their quality of life and overall development. Socioeconomic factors further influence cognitive outcomes, with children from disadvantaged backgrounds generally performing worse [46–48].

Findings from the UK Millennium Cohort Study show that economic deprivation and maternal depression adversely affect cognitive and emotional development at age 3, partly mediated by reduced nurturing and engaged parenting practices in such contexts [49]. Better maternal mental health has been shown to attenuate the negative association between low SES and children’s cognitive and emotional outcomes [50].

More recent longitudinal evidence from the Theirworld Edinburgh Birth Cohort provides valuable insight into the effects of preterm birth and SES on early brain development [51]. Both preterm birth and SES affect neonatal brain morphology and white matter microstructure at term- equivalent age and five years [51]. Family-level SES indicators, such as parental occupation and maternal education, were particularly associated with neonatal brain volumes, while neighborhood level SES showed stronger associations with brain development at five years of age [51]. Fractional anisotropy, a marker of white matter microstructure, was significantly correlated with both GA and maternal education in preterm infants [51]. While inflammation was linked to preterm birth, it did not show an independent association with SES, suggesting that other mechanisms mediate the influence of socioeconomic factors on early brain development. These findings highlight the importance of targeted interventions, focusing on family level SES during the neonatal period, and addressing broader socioeconomic conditions in later childhood to promote neurodevelopmental outcomes in preterm infants.

Additionally, the home environment, including maternal IQ, education, and active engagement has a strong effect on neurodevelopment. In a study including 502 mother-child pairs, maternal IQ was found to have a direct and significant effect on motor development as measured by the Bayley-III, with the home environment mediating this relationship [52]. Early intervention with family-centered strategies may enhance maternal psychosocial well-being and mitigate the impact of maternal limitations, thereby improving infants’ developmental outcomes [128].

Prematurity and psychiatric disorders

Premature birth is not only a significant risk factor for NDI but also increases the likelihood of psychiatric disorders (Table 1). As GA decreases, the impact on both individuals and the healthcare system intensifies, manifesting in elevated risks of psychiatric disorders, functional impairment, academic underachievement, behavioural problems, higher consumption of psychotropic medications, and increased rates of psychiatric hospitalization [53, 76, 129].

Large-scale cohort studies from Sweden (n = 545,628) and Denmark (n = 545,641) demonstrated that individuals born preterm are at a heightened risk of developing psychiatric conditions severe enough to warrant hospitalization during adolescence and early adulthood [130, 131]. These studies reveal a dose-response relationship: the earlier the birth, the greater the risk of psychiatric hospitalization [130, 131]. Similarly, a population-based Norwegian study involving over half a million participants found that individuals born preterm were more likely to be prescribed psychotropic medications, with stronger associations observed in those born at earlier GA [132]. Specifically, extremely preterm individuals had significantly higher prescriptions rates for all psychotropic drug classes, including antidepressants (OR: 1.7; 95% CI: 1.4–2.1) and psychostimulants (OR: 2.7; 95% CI: 2.1–3.4) [132].

Further supporting this association, an individual participant data meta-analysis comprising 10 studies of 1,385 very preterm/very low birthweight babies and 1,780 controls revealed a significant increased risk for several psychiatric disorders [54]. Compared to term-born infants, those born very preterm were over ten times more likely to meet criteria for autism spectrum disorder (ASD; OR 10.6, 95% CI: 2.50–44.7), over five times more likely to develop attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD; OR 5.42, 95% CI: 3.10–9.46), nearly twice as likely for anxiety disorders (OR 1.91, 95% CI: 1.36–2.69), and one and a half times more likely for mood disorders (OR 1.51, 95% CI: 1.08–2.12) [54]. A recent meta-analysis confirmed a significant association between PTB and ADHD in children (OR 2.76, 95% CI: 2.52–3.04) [45]. Additionally, another meta-analysis focusing on anxiety and depressive disorders in children aged 3 to 19 years found preterm-born children had increased odds of overall anxiety (OR 2.17, 95% CI: 1.43–3.29), generalized anxiety (OR 2.20, 95% CI: 1.26–3.84), and specific phobia (OR 1.93, 95% CI: 1.05–3.52), compared to term-born peers [55].

Many psychiatric disorders emerging in childhood are often linked to NDIs, reflecting both their origins in brain development and their impact on behaviour, cognition, and emotional regulation [133]. According to the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5), several childhood psychiatric disorders are classified as neurodevelopmental disorders, including intellectual disabilities, communication disorders, ASD, ADHD, and specific learning disorders [134]. These conditions often emerge prior to school entry. For example, ASD symptoms commonly appear in the first year of life, while ADHD symptoms commonly present before age 12 [134, 135]. ADHD is characterized by inattention, hyperactivity, and impulsivity, whereas ASD involves difficulties in social communications and repetitive behaviours [134]. Although anxiety and mood disorders are categorised separately in the DSM-5, they often co-occur with NDIs and share developmental components [136]. Understanding these overlaps is crucial for accurate diagnosis and effective treatment, especially in vulnerable populations such as those born preterm.

Mental health and behavioural problems in premature children are assessed either through formal diagnostic evaluations by standard tools or symptom-based questionnaires completed by parents and teachers [53, 75]. The literature typically categorises these behaviour profiles into externalizing and internalizing types [137]. Externalizing behaviours are outwardly directed symptoms and include aggression, defiance, impulsivity, and hyperactivity. Disorders in the externalizing category include ADHD, Oppositional Defiant Disorder, and Conduct Disorder [138]. Internalizing behaviours are inwardly directed and encompass symptoms such as anxiety, depression, and social withdrawal. Corresponding disorders include depression, anxiety, and obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD) [138]. While formal diagnosis requires clinical expertise in mental health, behaviours associated with these conditions are often observable by parents, teachers, or the individuals themselves. Although ASD is commonly studied in preterm populations, it does not fit neatly into the externalizing–internalizing framework due to its multifaceted presentation.

Externalizing behaviours and disorders and prematurity

Externalizing behaviours and disorders have been extensively studied in the context of premature birth, with consistent findings indicating higher rates among children born preterm compared to those born at term. Several risk factors including perinatal infection, prolonged hospital stay, mechanical ventilation, maternal smoking, and male sex have all been identified as significant contributors to the development of externalizing behaviours [71, 72].

Two meta-analyses have reported a statistically significant increase in externalizing behaviours among preterm children [53, 56]. However, findings related to specific behavioural domains remain inconsistent. Some studies reported an increased risk of conduct problems in preterm children [57, 58, 68], whereas others do not observe this association [69, 70]. Although children born extremely preterm often exhibit persistent emotional, attentional, and social challenges during middle childhood and adolescence [57, 58, 68–70], they may not necessarily be at increased risk for more severe externalizing disorders like conduct disorders or delinquency. This highlights the complex interplay of factors shaping the neurobehavioural outcomes [57, 58, 68–70].

Despite inconsistencies in conduct-related outcomes, ADHD symptoms have been consistently reported at higher rates among children born preterm [53, 56–58]. There is a broad consensus that individuals born prematurely are at a higher risk of developing ADHD compared to those born at term [53, 56–59], with this increased risk persisting from early childhood, through adolescence, and into adulthood [53, 54, 139]. Additional risk factors such as low birth weight, low Apgar score, prolonged hospitalization, mechanical ventilation, and respiratory distress syndrome further elevate the likelihood of ADHD later in life [60, 61]. ADHD-associated behavioural difficulties can be observed as early as infancy (1.5 to 3 years) [58, 140–143]. A meta-analysis revealed that all degrees of prematurity (very, moderate, and late) are associated with increased ADHD incidence, with the highest risk observed in children born very and moderate preterm [44].

Preterm born children with ADHD also demonstrate increased healthcare resource utilization, including higher rates of psychostimulant prescriptions [132, 144] and ADHD-related physician visits [145], which scale with the degree of prematurity. Studies utilizing DSM-IV criteria suggest that inattentive subtype is more predominant than either the hyperactive-impulsive or combined subtypes among this population [146–150]. Despite improvements in neonatal care and survival rates, elevated ADHD prevalence has been consistently reported across birth cohorts from the 1970 s to the present [53, 54]. For instance, a multi-cohort study in England comparing extremely preterm and term-born children found that those born in 2006 exhibited higher rates of ADHD-related behavioural problems at age 11 than those born in 1995, suggesting that increased ADHD risk has persisted despite medical advances [151].

The underlying mechanism for this heightened ADHD risk is thought to involve disruptions in central nervous system development during the second and third trimesters [152, 153]. Neuroimaging studies have revealed significant alterations in brain structure and connectivity in children born preterm, which are closely linked to adverse neurodevelopmental outcomes [154, 155]. Moreover, preterm birth is associated with reduced cognitive function [44, 56]. Low cognitive performance and NDI disorders have been associated with behavioural difficulties; however, the increased prevalence of ADHD persists even after accounting for these factors, albeit at a reduced magnitude [58, 68, 140, 156–159].

In contrast to ADHD, the risk for disruptive behavioural disorders such as conduct disorder and oppositional defiant disorder among preterm children is generally comparable to that of term-born cohorts. However, some studies report modest increases in these disorders, largely explained by co-existing neurodevelopmental conditions [77, 78, 149, 150, 160]. A meta-analysis evaluating individuals born between 1977 and 2004 at < 32 weeks’ gestation and/or with a birth weight < 1500 g (n = 1,385) compared to a control group at ≥ 37 weeks and/or with a birth weight ≥ 2499 g (n = 1,780) found a five-fold increase in ADHD prevalence among the preterm group (adjusted odds ratio (AOR) = 5.3, 95% CI: 3.1–9.2; 18.8% vs. 8.4%; p < 0.001) [54], but no significant difference in rates of disruptive behaviuor disorders (AOR = 1.3, 95% CI: 0.6–2.8; 2.6% vs. 2.2%; p = 0.55) , : [54]. Conversely, a 2011–2012 U.S. national survey (n = 84,685) of parent-reported mental health diagnoses in children aged 2–17 years found a significantly higher prevalence of oppositional defiant or conduct disorders compared to their term-born counterparts (4.5% vs. 2.95%; p < 0.001) [140]. This elevated prevalence was largely attributed to co-occurring conditions, including ASD, ADHD, developmental delay, learning disability, and intellectual disability [140].

Internalizing behaviours and disorders and prematurity

Individuals born preterm face distinct psychological challenges, particularly in relation to internalizing behaviours and disorders. Compared to externalizing behaviours, there is stronger consensus in the literature that internalizing behaviours such as anxiety, depression, social withdrawal, and somatic complaints are more prevalent in children and adolescents born preterm than in their term-born counterparts [45, 53, 54, 56, 73, 74]. This pattern is evident not only in the most vulnerable extremely premature cohorts [161, 162] but also among those born late preterm [163, 164].

Meta-analyses examining diagnostic rates of anxiety disorders from childhood through adulthood in individuals born preterm between the 1980 s and early 2000 s have shown two to three times higher odds of developing anxiety disorders compared to those born at term [53–55, 74, 75]. Specific anxiety disorders reported to be elevated in this population include general anxiety disorder, specific phobias, social anxiety disorder, and separation anxiety [74, 146, 149, 165].

Three meta-analyses focusing on depressive disorders, found that two reported significantly higher rates among individuals born preterm [53, 75]; while another meta-analysis that depression under broader mood disorders reported a 1.5-fold increase compared to term-born controls [54]. In contrast, studies investigating OCD have not found significant differences between preterm and term born individuals [76–78]. Singh et al. (2013) attributed the higher odds of anxiety and depression in preterm children to co-existing NDIs [140]. However, another study has reported increased risk even in the absence of severe NDIs, suggesting that prematurity itself may independently contribute to internalizing symptoms [166].

Several factors have been shown to be associated with increased internalizing disorders among individuals born preterm. For instance, higher exposure to neonatal pain has been correlated with higher rates of internalizing behaviours in childhood and early adulthood [79]. Pain is believed to cause substantial stress to the developing brain in premature infants. Maternal mental health, which can be substantially impacted by having a child prematurely, is strongly correlated with childhood anxiety disorders [80]. Additionally, preterm children with anxiety are more likely to present with lower IQ, ADHD, ASD, cognitive deficits, and CP compared to preterm peers without anxiety [80].

Early internalizing symptoms are predictive of later psychiatric morbidity. For instance, parent-reported behavioural problems at ages 2.5 and 6 years in children born before 26 weeks’ gestation were strong predictors of psychiatric diagnoses at 11 years of age, with an AOR of 12.3 (95% CI: 3.9–38.4, p < 0.001) for combined age groups and 6.15 (95% CI: 2.7–13.8, p < 0.001) for age 2.5 [149].

Furthermore, in children born very preterm (< 32 weeks GA), early difficulties with emotional and behavioural regulation were significantly more common than in term-born children [167] and independently predicted later diagnoses of ADHD (p = 0.006), conduct disorder (p = 0.04), anxiety disorders (p = 0.004), and any mental health disorder (p = 0.01) by age 9 [167].

These findings highlight the importance of early identification and intervention strategies targeted at internalizing symptoms in children born preterm, which may potentially mitigate long-term psychiatric morbidity and improve the overall quality of life for this vulnerable population.

ASD and prematurity

The prevalence of ASD is notably higher in children born preterm, with diagnostic rates reported to be up to 3.3 times greater than those in the general population [168, 169]. There is an inverse relationship between GA and the prevalence of ASD, with earlier gestational ages associated with higher risk [62–65]. Developmental delays, intellectual disabilities, and IQ deficits are also more prevalent in this population, especially among those born extremely preterm [66, 67].

Importantly, the elevated rates of ASD in preterm populations persist even after excluding individuals with intellectual disability and other NDIs, such as non-ambulatory CP, deafness, and blindness [63, 170]. Additional contributing risk factors associated with ASD among preterm infants include a higher incidence of intracranial hemorrhage and increased frequency of mechanical ventilation [64].

Though the exact mechanisms underlying this heightened ASD risk remain unclear, it is postulated that the immaturity and heightened vulnerability of the central nervous system in preterm infants may contribute to abnormal brain development [63, 67]. Further research is needed to better understand the underlying mechanisms and to develop more effective diagnostic and therapeutic strategies to enhance long-term outcomes in this high-risk population.

Service needs/gaps in the current health system

Preterm birth remains a significant public health challenge, with survivors facing heightened risks of cognitive, motor, and behavioural challenges [171]. Substantial service needs and gaps persist in the management of neurodevelopmental disorders among children born preterm. One of the most pressing issues is the lack of standardized, long-term follow-up programs for preterm infants [172], which are crucial for early detection and intervention of NDIs and psychiatric disorders; however, their implementation remains inconsistent across healthcare facilities [173]. This inconsistency can lead to delayed diagnoses and missed opportunities for timely interventions, ultimately compromising developmental outcomes [174]. Many health systems are inadequately equipped to address the complex and evolving needs of these children. Gaps are particularly evident in the provision of multidisciplinary follow-up care, including developmental monitoring, nutritional counseling, and psychosocial support for families [175]. These deficiencies are especially pronounced in low-resource settings, where access to specialized care is limited, and standardized care protocols are often lacking or inconsistently applied [171, 175].

Socioeconomic disparities further exacerbate these challenges. Children from socioeconomically disadvantaged backgrounds are more likely to have poor cognitive outcomes [47, 90, 120], and often face limited access to specialized care and early intervention programs [176]. This inequity is even more concerning for children born preterm, who are disproportionately affected by developmental vulnerabilities and require coordinated, family-centered care, and multidisciplinary support [177]. Addressing these disparities demands policy reforms that ensure equitable access to high-quality healthcare regardless of SES. Additionally, promoting parental education about the risks associated with preterm births and early signs of neurodevelopmental issues can empower families to seek timely and appropriate care [177].

Furthermore, there is a need for improved coordination and communication among healthcare providers involved in the care of preterm infants [178]. A fragmented approach to care, with limited information sharing between NICUs, primary care physicians, and specialists, can hinder the continuity of care and delays the detection and management of neurodevelopmental issues [178]. To mitigate this, healthcare systems should prioritize the development of shared electronic health records and establish clearly defined referral pathways and interprofessional communication protocols.

Conclusion

The findings of the current narrative review underscore that children and adolescents born preterm remain at substantially higher risk for a wide spectrum of neurodevelopmental and psychiatric disorders, despite notable improvements in neonatal and perinatal care. Some of these conditions may only become apparent until later in childhood or adolescence, emphasizing the critical need for long-term developmental surveillance, even among those who appear to thrive in early life.

Our review suggests that preterm birth is a key risk factor for a wide range of neurodevelopmental and psychiatric outcomes, such as CP, DCD, intellectual disability, seizure disorders, hearing impairment, and visual impairment. Additionally, prematurity is strongly linked with school failure, cognitive impairments, externalizing behaviours (e.g., ADHD and conduct disorders), and internalizing problems (e.g., anxiety and depression). Sociodemographic factors including lower SES, limited maternal education, and maternal mental health challenges emerge as determinants of long-term neurodevelopmental and psychiatric outcomes. Collectively, these findings underline the multifactorial nature of outcomes in individuals born preterm and the necessity of integrating both biomedical and social interventions into their care.

Future research should focus on elucidating the underlying mechanisms and causal pathways linking prematurity to long-term neurodevelopmental and psychiatric outcomes. In parallel, efforts are needed to refine early screening strategies, implement evidence-based early intervention programs, and evaluate their long-term effectiveness. Addressing the full spectrum of prematurity-related challenges will require coordinated action from policymakers, clinicians, educators, administrators, and parent partners. By addressing both medical and social determinants of health, we can offer preterm survivors the best chance to reach their full developmental potential and improve quality of life across the lifespan.
