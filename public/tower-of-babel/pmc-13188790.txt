Efficacy and safety of low-intensity chemotherapy combined with immunotherapy in elderly patients with locally advanced and metastatic non–small cell lung cancer: a retrospective analysis
Authors: Yunye Mao, An Wang, Boyu Qin, Jinzhao Zhai, Xiangwei Ge, Shu Sheng, Yi Hu, Jinliang Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
For advanced non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) lacking driver mutations, immunotherapy combined with chemotherapy is a standard treatment; however, elderly patients frequently exhibit poor tolerance to standard chemotherapy and derive limited benefit from immunotherapy alone. Thus, this retrospective study assessed the efficacy and safety of low-intensity chemotherapy combined with immunotherapy in elderly (≥ 65) patients with locally advanced and metastatic NSCLC. We included 554 patients from the PLA General Hospital who were divided into three cohorts: immune monotherapy (monotherapy group, n = 125), a combination of immunotherapy and standard chemotherapy (standard group, n = 205), and immunotherapy paired with low-intensity chemotherapy (low-intensity group, n = 224). The primary endpoints were overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS), and the secondary endpoints included the objective response rate (ORR), disease control rate (DCR), and adverse events (AEs). Compared with the monotherapy group, the low-intensity group demonstrated significantly improved median PFS (11.50 vs. 7.87 months, P < 0.001) and OS (32.20 vs. 14.97 months, P < 0.001). The inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) adjustment sustained these benefits. Compared with the standard group, the low-intensity group had similar PFS and OS rates, and better safety profiles. This study indicates that low-intensity chemoimmunotherapy is superior to immune monotherapy and offers a safer alternative to standard chemoimmunotherapy in elderly NSCLC patients, suggesting its potential as a first-line treatment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-026-15943-1.

Introduction

Lung cancer represents a paramount global health challenge, as it has the highest incidence and mortality rates among malignancies worldwide. In 2022, there were nearly 2.5 million new diagnoses of lung cancer and over 1.8 million fatalities worldwide, accounting for one in every eight new cancer cases and one in every five cancer-related deaths, respectively [1, 2]. Non–small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) constitutes the majority (approximately 85%) of lung cancer cases. The majority of patients are diagnosed at advanced stages, precluding the possibility of surgical intervention and contributing to a poor prognosis [3]. The National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) geriatric oncology guide categorizes individuals aged 65 and above as older adults [4]. This population is growing rapidly, and the incidence of NSCLC within this age group is disproportionately high [5, 6]. The physiological decline associated with aging, compounded by the presence of comorbidities and immunosenescence, diminishes immune function and the efficacy of treatment. The scarcity of older adult participants in clinical studies further exacerbates the challenges faced in their therapeutic management [7–10].

In the 1890s, Dr. William B. Coley initiated the pioneering era of immunotherapy through the innovative approach of injecting bacterial mixtures into tumors, representing the nascent stage of a transformative journey in cancer treatment [11]. The landscape of advanced NSCLC treatment, once monopolized by chemotherapy, underwent a paradigm shift with the KEYNOTE-024 study [12], which heralded a new era in which immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) emerged as a predominant therapeutic strategy for patients lacking driver gene mutations. Subsequent studies, namely, KEYNOTE-042 [13], reinforced these findings, supporting the superior survival outcomes conferred by immunotherapy monotherapy over traditional chemotherapy. The pivotal KEYNOTE-189 and KEYNOTE-407 trials reported a drastic increase in the 5-year overall survival (OS) rate from a mere 5% to an impressive 20%, thus confirming the integral role of immunotherapy combined with chemotherapy for locally advanced and metastatic NSCLC patients without driver gene mutations. In the current therapeutic paradigm, first-line immunotherapy is recognized as the gold standard for patients with advanced NSCLC who test negative for driver genes [14–16].

The majority of elderly patients often have diminished physical resilience and a spectrum of comorbidities, which significantly impedes their capacity to tolerate standard chemotherapy, raising concerns over its safety and efficacy. In such cases, the utility of immune monotherapy is frequently restricted. Consequently, few guidelines advocate for the use of ICIs in this population, as there are concerns that indiscriminate application might compromise both the therapeutic outcome and the patient’s quality of life. In addition, the results of the IMpower130 and IMpower132 studies showed that the incidence of grade ≥ 3 treatment-related adverse events (TRAEs) increased in patients aged ≥ 75 years when atezolizumab was combined with platinum-based doublet chemotherapy (70.3% vs. 62.8%) [17]. Morimoto K et al.‘s research [18] found that the incidence of grade 3–5 pneumonia in elderly patients treated with immunotherapy combined with chemotherapy was significantly increased. A retrospective study conducted by the Hayashi team [19] revealed that in elderly patients aged 70 and above with advanced lung cancer, other adverse events (AEs) such as pneumonia, fatigue, and rash occurred at a relatively high frequency, and most were grade 1–2 AEs. However, even low-grade toxic side effects can lead to chemotherapy adjustments or even discontinuation for elderly patients [20].

In response, we developed a tailored therapeutic strategy for older adults, which included a reduction in chemotherapeutic dosages or the exclusion of a drug from the standard dual-agent regimen—termed low-intensity chemotherapy. The synergistic effect of low-intensity chemotherapy and immunotherapy stems from its multiple regulations on the tumor immune microenvironment, including reducing immunosuppressive cells and enhancing T-cell activity and antigen presentation efficiency, thereby activating anti-tumor immune responses [21]. Studies have shown that low-dose chemotherapy drugs can promote CD8 + T-cell infiltration and upregulate programmed cell death ligand-1 (PD-L1) expression by activating STING and other signaling pathways, forming a synergistic effect with immunotherapy [22]. Clinical evidence indicates that in advanced NSCLC, the efficacy and safety of low-intensity chemotherapy combined with ICIs are acceptable [23]. This strategy provides a new option for balancing efficacy and toxicity for elderly or intolerant patients, but its optimal application mode still needs further exploration.

In this study, this approach was compared with both immunotherapy monotherapy and the combination of immunotherapy with standard chemotherapy. The primary objective was to meticulously evaluate the efficacy and safety profiles of immunotherapy augmented with low-intensity chemotherapy in older adults with locally advanced and metastatic NSCLC, thereby offering a refined and individualized therapeutic blueprint for this distinct population.

Methods

Patients

A retrospective analysis was performed on patients with pathologically confirmed NSCLC who were clinically staged according to the eighth edition of the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) lung cancer staging guidelines. The study population consisted of individuals treated at the First and Fifth Medical Centers of the PLA General Hospital between January 2020 and December 2022. The eligibility criteria for inclusion were as follows: (1) a definitive pathological diagnosis of NSCLC, (2) age 65 years or above, (3) having received first-line treatment and at least two cycles of immunotherapy, (4) the absence of driver gene mutations, and (5) possessing complete clinical medical records, including treatment plans, efficacy evaluations, and records of adverse events, etc. The exclusion criteria were the presence of any other malignant tumors. The study cohort comprised 554 patients categorized into three distinct groups as follows: 125 in the immune monotherapy group (monotherapy group), 205 in the immunotherapy combined with standard chemotherapy group (standard group), and 224 in the immunotherapy combined with low-intensity immunotherapy group (low-intensity group). This study was a retrospective analysis of existing medical records and data. As no new interventions were performed and no new personal data were collected, ethical approval was exempt for this study.

Study assessments

Comprehensive data were extracted from the medical records of the enrolled patients. The collected information included demographics such as age and sex; anthropometric measurements such as body mass index (BMI); and lifestyle factors such as smoking history. Clinical assessments included the Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group Performance Status (ECOG PS) score, tumor staging, and documentation of comorbidities such as hypertension, diabetes, and coronary heart disease (CHD). Additionally, the presence of tumor metastasis—encompassing liver, bone, and brain metastases and malignant pleural effusion—was noted, alongside AEs. The primary endpoints were overall survival (OS), measured from the first administration of first-line treatment to death, and progression-free survival (PFS), measured from the date of initial treatment to the date of disease progression or death. The secondary endpoints were the objective response rate (ORR), disease control rate (DCR), and safety profile of the treatments. Treatment efficacy was appraised via the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) 1.1, with outcomes categorized into complete response (CR), partial response (PR), stable disease (SD), and progressive disease (PD). The ORR was calculated as follows: ORR = (CR + PR)/total number of cases × 100%; meanwhile, the DCR was calculated as follows: DCR = (CR + PR + SD)/total number of cases × 100%. Events that had not reached endpoints at the end of the study were processed as censored data. AEs were assessed according to the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) 5.0.

Treatment regimens

Low-intensity chemotherapy is defined as a modified regimen. Previous limited studies [24, 25] have shown that reducing the dose of chemotherapy drugs by approximately 20% to 40% or reducing one or more of the standard combination chemotherapy drugs can achieve the goal of reducing toxicity. Therefore, based on previous research evidence and clinical experience, in NSCLC patients, low-intensity chemotherapy is defined as: (1) reducing the dose of chemotherapy drugs by ≥ 30% (for example, reducing the dose of the standard albumin-bound paclitaxel combined with platinum regimen by 30% or more); (2) simplifying the standard dual-drug chemotherapy regimen to a single-drug chemotherapy (for example, using albumin-bound paclitaxel alone without platinum drugs). In addition, in this study, “local treatment” is defined as the treatment targeting lesions in a local area, including metastatic lesions, especially oligometastatic lesions. Patients with locally advanced disease who underwent radical local treatment were excluded from this study.

Statistical analysis

Data management and statistical analysis were performed via R software version 4.3.2 (The R Foundation for Statistical Computing). Categorical variables are expressed as the frequency (%), and differences between groups were compared via chi-square tests or Fisher’s exact probability method. The inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) method was used to adjust for imbalances between groups, and standardized mean differences (SMDs) ≤ 0.1 were used to assess the comparability between groups. Both unadjusted and IPTW-adjusted Kaplan‒Meier survival analyses were conducted, and the survival curves of each group were compared via the log-rank test. P values were adjusted via the Bonferroni method to ensure the reliability and accuracy of the results. A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patients

This study included 554 patients until December 2023, who were placed into the monotherapy group (n = 125), standard group (n = 205), or low-intensity group (n = 224). Table 1 summarizes the baseline characteristics of the three groups before IPTW adjustment. Among the 224 patients in the low-intensity group, 150 cases (66.96%) received chemotherapy dose reduction, while 74 cases (33.04%) adopted the method of simplifying the chemotherapy regimen (Supplementary Table 1). The median age of the patients in the monotherapy group was 69 (range 65–91) years, whereas those in the standard and low-intensity groups were 68 (range 65–82) years and 69 (range 65–87) years, respectively. There were 20 (8.93%) female patients in the low-intensity group, a lower proportion than in the monotherapy group (n = 29, 23.20%) and the standard group (n = 42, 20.49%). Notably, the low-intensity group presented a greater prevalence of smoking history, with 162 (72.32%) ever smokers, surpassing the proportions of the monotherapy group (n = 125, 64.80%) and the standard group (n = 123, 59.02%). ECOG PS scores of ≥ 2 were recorded for 17 (13.60%) patients in the monotherapy group, 6 (2.93%) in the standard group, and 16 (7.14%) in the low-intensity group. A stark contrast was observed in the distribution of adenocarcinoma and squamous cell carcinoma diagnoses, with the standard group exhibiting a higher rate of adenocarcinoma (n = 153, 74.63%); meanwhile, squamous cell carcinoma predominated in the monotherapy (n = 66, 52.80%) and low-intensity groups (n = 161, 71.88%). Compared with the monotherapy (n = 96, 76.80%) and standard (n = 152, 74.15%) groups, the low-intensity group had a slightly larger subset of stage IV patients (n = 125, 55.80%). Furthermore, the low-intensity group had a greater percentage of comorbidities (n = 125, 55.80%) than did the monotherapy (n = 55, 44.00%) and standard groups (n = 95, 45.37%). Since PD-L1 expression was unknown in the majority of patients, this variable was not included for adjustment, and the remaining data were adjusted for IPTW with well-balanced data characteristics and an SMD < 0.1 (Supplementary Fig. 1; Supplementary Tables 2 and 3).

Efficacy

In this study, we conducted an in-depth comparative analysis of the efficacy and safety of monotherapy, standard therapy, and low-intensity therapy. Compared with the monotherapy group, the low-intensity treatment group exhibited a significantly longer median PFS (low-intensity treatment: 11.5 months, 95% CI: 9.93–14.97; monotherapy: 7.87 months, 95% CI: 6.10–10.67; P < 0.001) and OS (low-intensity treatment: 32.20 months, 95% CI: 28.67–NA; monotherapy: 14.97 months, 95% CI: 12.10–20.53; P < 0.001). After IPTW adjustment, the low-intensity group still demonstrated significantly superior efficacy (P < 0.001), with a median PFS of 10.63 (95% CI: 8.60–14.27) months and a median OS of 31.53 (95% CI: 26.70–46.00) months compared with 7.97 (95% CI: 5.67–11.43) months and 16.37 (95% CI: 12.97–29.00) months, respectively, in the monotherapy group (Figs. 1 and 2). Additionally, the 1-year PFS and OS rates were 33% and 46% in the monotherapy group and 42% and 62% in the low-intensity group, respectively; moreover, the 2-year PFS and OS rates were 10% and 23% in the monotherapy group and 42% and 62% in the low-intensity group, respectively (Supplementary Table 4). Subgroup analyses revealed that the low-intensity regimen significantly mitigated the risk of disease progression in the following subgroups: age 65–74 years; male sex; Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG PS) score ranging from 0 to 1; BMI ranging from 18.5 to 24; smoking history; squamous carcinoma; no liver metastases, bone metastases, intrapulmonary metastases, brain metastases, or pleural effusions; no comorbidities; and no prior local therapy (P < 0.05). Furthermore, for patients with a BMI ≥ 24, adenocarcinoma, liver or bone metastasis, or who had undergone local therapy, the low-intensity group also had a greater survival benefit than the monotherapy group did (P < 0.05) (Supplementary Tables 6 and 7).

There was no significant difference in median PFS or OS between the low-intensity group and the standard group before IPTW adjustment (PFS: 11.50 vs. 11.00 months; OS: 32.20 vs. 28.87 months) or after IPTW adjustment (PFS: 11.37 vs. 11.80 months; OS: 31.20 vs. 30.40 months) (P ≥ 0.05; Figs. 1 and 2); moreover, the 1-year and 2-year PFS and OS rates were similar (Supplementary Table 5). An extended subgroup analysis incorporating a spectrum of patient attributes, including age, sex, ECOG PS score, BMI, smoking history, diagnosis, staging, and metastatic status, demonstrated no significant association with the therapeutic effect variance between the two cohorts, and IPTW adjustment did not alter these outcomes. However, patients with hypertension who received low-intensity treatment had a 35% reduction in the risk of disease progression, and the data after IPTW adjustment revealed that patients aged ≥ 75 years had an increased risk with low-intensity treatment (hazard ratio [HR] = 2.50; P = 0.037). Conversely, low-intensity treatment was beneficial in patients with diabetes before adjustment (HR = 0.49, P = 0.047), but this effect was not significant in the subsequent analysis (HR = 0.70, P = 0.37) (Supplementary Tables 8 and 9).

In the low-intensity group, 3 (1.34%) patients achieved CR, while the majority experienced PR (n = 122, 54.46%), with SD and PD occurring in 81 (36.16%) and 18 (8.04%) patients, respectively. In contrast, no patients in the monotherapy group achieved CR, while 29 (23.2%) experienced PR, 67 (53.6%) experienced SD, and 29 (23.2%) experienced PD. Similarly, there were no CR patients in the standard group, with 104 (50.73%) patients experiencing PR, 92 (44.88%) experiencing SD, and 9 (4.39%) experiencing PD. The ORR and DCR of the low-intensity group were 55.80% and 91.96%, respectively, which were significantly greater than those of the monotherapy group (23.20% and 76.80%, respectively); the ORR of the low-intensity group was greater than that of the standard group (50.73%), but the DCR was lower than that of the standard group (95.61%) (Table 2).

Safety

On the basis of the raw data, this study analyzed the occurrence of AEs in the three treatment groups during the treatment process. Overall, all the groups showed relatively good tolerance, with most AEs being of low grade and with high-grade AEs having a low incidence. Within the monotherapy group, any grade AEs occurred in 59 (44.44%) patients, with 9 (7.20%) patients experiencing grade 3 or higher AEs. The most prevalent high-grade AEs included bone marrow suppression (3.20%), hepatotoxicity (3.20%), pneumonia (1.60%), and rash (0.80%). In the standard group, there was a greater incidence of any-grade AEs (n = 143, 69.76%), with 45 patients (21.95%) experiencing AEs of Grade 3 or higher. Higher-frequency AEs included bone marrow suppression (19.51%), gastrointestinal reactions (2.44%), hepatotoxicity (0.49%), nephrotoxicity (0.49%), and pneumonia (0.49%). In the low-intensity group, there were 160 cases of any-grade AEs (71.43%), with 32 (14.29%) classified as Grade 3 or higher AEs. Bone marrow suppression was the most common severe AE and was present in 26 patients (11.61%), followed by pneumonia in 2 patients (2.55%), and hepatotoxicity, rash, alopecia, and hypothyroidism each occurred in 1 patient (0.45%) (Table 3). The incidence of any-grade (P < 0.001), grade 1–2 (P < 0.001), and grade ≥ 3 AEs (P < 0.049) was greater in the low-intensity group than in the monotherapy group, but compared with that in the standard group, the incidence of Grade 3 or higher AEs was lower (P = 0.048); moreover, the incidence of any-grade (P = 0.704) and Grade 1–2 AEs (P = 0.622) did not differ between these two groups (Fig. 3).

Discussion

Advanced lung cancer is the main cause of death in patients with this disease, and most patients are already in advanced stages at the time of diagnosis, thus having a poor prognosis. The advent of immunotherapy, especially the application of ICIs, represents a revolutionary change in the treatment of advanced NSCLC. Moreover, ICIs have become a standard treatment option for patients with advanced NSCLC without driver gene mutations, as they have synergistic effects with chemotherapy [26, 27]. However, elderly patients with lung cancer may have reduced tolerance to treatment, as the effects of aging and physiological decline may affect pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics, with multiple underlying diseases and decreased organ reserve function also reducing treatment efficacy and tolerance [28]. Therefore, our study aimed to assess the efficacy and safety of ICIs combined with low-intensity chemotherapy in elderly patients with locally advanced or metastatic NSCLC and to compare this therapy with ICI monotherapy and ICI combined with standard chemotherapy.

In this study, compared with the monotherapy group, the low-intensity group presented significant advantages in terms of PFS and OS both before and after IPTW adjustment (adjusted PFS: 10.63 months vs. 7.97 months; adjusted OS: 31.53 months vs. 16.37 months). However, compared with the standard group, no significant difference was observed in PFS or OS for the low-intensity group (adjusted PFS: 11.37 months vs. 11.80 months; adjusted OS: 31.20 months vs. 30.40 months). The ORR and DCR in the monotherapy group were 23.20% and 76.80%, respectively, which were significantly lower than those in the low-intensity group, which were 55.80% and 91.96%, respectively. Moreover, the ORR and DCR of the standard group were 50.73% and 95.61%, respectively.

In the IMpower-110 trial [29], the median PFS for patients aged 65 years and older treated with ICI monotherapy was 8.1 months, and the median OS was 17.8 months. In the CheckMate-026 trial [30], the median OS was 13.3 months, and in the OAK trial [31], the median OS was 14.1 months. Furthermore, a meta-analysis by Marur et al. [32], which included the KEYNOTE-010, CheckMate 057, OAK, and POPLAR trials, revealed that the median OS times for patients aged 65–74 years and ≥ 75 years were 14.2 and 14.7 months, respectively. These data are essentially consistent with the median PFS and OS of the patients in the monotherapy group in our study. Additionally, the ORR reported in several studies on immunotherapy monotherapy for advanced NSCLC [12, 13, 29, 33] ranges from 27.3% to 46.1%, whereas the ORR in our study was only 23.20%, which may be related to the fact that all patients in this study were older adults and had poorer efficacy.

Zhu et al. [34] conducted a phase II prospective study involving patients aged 65 years and older and reported that the median PFS of the group treated with ICIs plus low-dose nabpaclitaxel was 9.5 months. Ouyang et al. [23] conducted a real-world observational study of 54 patients who received ICIs combined with low-intensity chemotherapy, and the median PFS was 12 months. These results are essentially consistent with the median PFS of the low-intensity group in this study but not with the median OS of this group, which was slightly longer than that reported in other clinical studies. This may be related to the fact that most of the patients included had an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status score between 0 and 1. For example, in the KEYNOTE-189 trial [14], the median OS for patients treated with ICIs combined with standard chemotherapy was 22 months, and in the CameL-sq trial [35], it was 27.4 months. However, in the IMpower-132 trial [36], the median OS for the Asian population treated with ICIs combined with chemotherapy reached 30.2 months, which aligns closely with the results of our study. In addition, the ORR yielded by immune-combination chemotherapy in several large clinical studies ranged from 49.2% to 75% [15, 35, 37, 38], whereas the DCR ranged from 81% to 91%, which is similar to the ORR and DCR of the low-intensity group in our study.

Previous studies have demonstrated a synergistic effect between low-dose chemotherapy and immunotherapy. Zhou L et al. [22] found that low-dose carboplatin could increase the infiltration of CD8+ T cells through the STING signaling pathway and increase the expression of PD-L1. He et al. [39, 40] reported that low-dose chemotherapy could facilitate antigen uptake and presentation, enhance systemic immune memory, and promote the polarization of M1 macrophages. Li et al. [41] discovered that low doses of decitabine could inhibit tumor growth by increasing the expression of genes related to the interferon signaling pathway, antigen presentation pathways, and cytokine pathways while preventing excessive toxicity. However, research on this subject remains limited, and the mechanism of the synergistic action between the two needs further research and exploration.

PD-L1, as a crucial predictive biomarker for immunotherapy, has a close correlation with the efficacy of ICIs. Patients with high PD-L1 expression are generally considered to have stronger immunogenicity and may respond better to ICIs monotherapy. Combined chemotherapy may further activate T-cell function by inducing immunogenic cell death, increasing tumor antigen release, or regulating immunosuppressive cytokines, thereby amplifying the efficacy of ICIs [42]. In this study, some patients had no PD-L1 expression, which might have an impact on the research results. Additionally, the proportion of patients with a smoking history in the low-intensity group (72.32%) was significantly higher than that in the monotherapy group (64.80%) and the standard group (59.02%). Studies [43, 44] have shown that smoking is potentially associated with increased tumor mutational burden (TMB) and upregulated PD-L1 expression. Therefore, the survival advantage of the low-intensity group may be partly attributed to biomarker-driven efficacy differences rather than merely the optimization of treatment strategies. ECOG PS is a key independent factor for treatment selection and prognosis in patients with advanced NSCLC [45]. This study observed that the proportion of patients with ECOG PS ≥ 2 in the monotherapy group (13%) was significantly higher than that in the low-intensity group (7%) and the standard group (2%). This difference may partly explain the survival disadvantage of the monotherapy group, as patients with ECOG PS ≥ 2 usually have poorer tolerance to treatment and lower survival expectations [46]. However, when the baseline characteristics were adjusted using IPTW to make the ECOG PS scores of the two groups non-significantly different, the median PFS and OS of the low-intensity group were still significantly better than those of the monotherapy group. This indicates that despite the ECOG PS difference possibly influencing the results, the survival advantage of low-intensity group is statistically significant and independent of baseline confounding. Nevertheless, as a core indicator for clinical decision-making, ECOG PS still needs to be prioritized in individualized treatment, and future studies should incorporate comprehensive geriatric assessment to further optimize patient stratification.

To examine safety, we assessed the occurrence of adverse events in elderly patients with locally advanced and metastatic NSCLC under different treatment plans. The incidence of any-grade AEs in the monotherapy group was the lowest, at 44.44%, while the incidence of Grade 3 or higher AEs was 7.20%. The incidence of Grade 3 or higher AEs in the low-intensity group was lower than that in the standard group (14.29% vs. 21.95%; P = 0.048), but there was no difference in the incidence of any-grade AEs between the two groups (71.43% vs. 69.76%; P > 0.05). In their study, Ksienski et al. [47] reported that 40.2% of patients aged 65–75 years experienced any grade AEs after receiving ICI monotherapy. In the CheckMate 078 trial [48], the incidence of grade 3 or 4 AEs in nivolumab-treated patients aged 65 years or older in the Asian population was 10%, which is similar to the incidence of AEs in the monotherapy group in our study. In contrast, in Marur et al.’s study [32], the incidence of grade 3 to 4 AEs in patients aged 75 years or older was 23%, whereas that in patients aged 65 to 74 years was 49%; moreover, the CheckMate 171 trial [49] reported an incidence of 62.9% and 69% incidence of any-grade AEs among NSCLC patients aged 70 years and above and 75 years and above, respectively, and an incidence of grade 3–4 AEs of 15.8% and 18%, respectively—figures that surpass those in our study. This discrepancy may be attributable to the relatively young median age of the patient population in previous studies.

In our study, the incidence of Grade 3 or higher AEs in the group treated with ICIs combined with low-intensity ICIs was 14.29%, which is lower than the 27.78% reported in Zhu et al.’s study [34] and higher than the 10.3% reported in Ouyang et al.’s study [23]. In contrast, the combination of ICIs with standard chemotherapy is associated with a higher frequency of grade 3 or higher AEs, as indicated in numerous clinical studies, with an incidence ranging from 71.9% to 86.6% [35, 38, 50–52]. This discrepancy underscores the enhanced safety profile provided by low-intensity therapy. However, the short follow-up time and retrospective nature of this study might have adversely impacted the accuracy of the results. Nevertheless, the incidence of any-grade AEs in the overall population ranges from 62.7% to 100.00% [35, 38, 50–52], which is consistent with the findings of our study.

As the global population ages, the proportion of elderly patients will continue to increase. For the treatment of this population, age, ECOG PS score, comorbidities, and other medical factors can affect prognosis, so a comprehensive assessment is needed. Studies have shown that the Geriatric 8 questionnaire (G8) has high sensitivity and helps to identify vulnerable or frail patients and that the Vulnerable Elders Survey 13 questionnaire (VES-13) has high specificity, suggesting that their combined use is an optimal strategy for comprehensive geriatric assessment. The Cancer and Aging Research Group (CARG) Chemotherapy Risk Assessment Scale can assess the risk of chemotherapy and predict the chemotherapeutic tolerance of elderly patients with lung cancer [53]. Moreover, the Comprehensive Geriatric Assessment (CGA) serves as an adjunct or alternative to conventional oncological assessment indicators, enhancing precision in identifying the risks of chemotherapy toxicity, mortality, functional decline, and other adverse outcomes [54]. These assessments are essential for devising personalized treatment plans that cater to the unique needs of elderly patients.

Although this study represents a significant contribution to therapeutic research in elderly patients with locally advanced or metastatic NSCLC and demonstrates the efficacy and safety of ICIs in conjunction with low-intensity chemotherapy, certain limitations should be noted. First, as a retrospective study, missing data might have led to biased results. Furthermore, as a core predictor of the efficacy of immunotherapy, the expression level of PD-L1 may have a significant impact on the outcome. The higher smoking rate in the low-intensity group may indirectly enhance the synergistic effect of immunotherapy by up-regulating the expression of PD-L1. This makes it impossible for this study to accurately screen out potential beneficiaries, which may lead to bias in the assessment of therapeutic efficacy or the omission of some suitable patients. Finally, AEs were assessed mainly on the basis of medical records, without detailed verification of patients’ laboratory results after each treatment. Despite certain limitations in the study design, our findings may serve as a valuable reference for the treatment of elderly patients with NSCLC and lay the foundation for future prospective studies.

Conclusion

The results of this study indicate that for elderly patients with advanced NSCLC, particularly those who are physically unable to tolerate standard chemotherapy, a therapeutic strategy that integrates immunotherapy with low-intensity chemotherapy may offer benefits over immunotherapy alone. Compared with a treatment plan that combines immunotherapy with standard chemotherapy, immunotherapy with low-intensity chemotherapy may provide enhanced safety, potentially serving as an alternative treatment option for older adults. Moreover, we found that different patient characteristics were variably associated with treatment efficacy, indicating that the choice of treatment needs to be tailored individually in clinical practice. However, the current evidence remains insufficient, and thus, additional, large-scale, phase III randomized controlled trials are needed to further elucidate the optimal immunotherapy combinations, refine drug dosing, determine administration schedules, and address the management of special populations.
