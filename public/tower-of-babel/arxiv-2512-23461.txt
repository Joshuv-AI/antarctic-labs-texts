Eliminating Inductive Bias in Reward Models with Information-Theoretic Guidance
Zhuo Li ∗, Pengyu Cheng †, Zhechao Yu, Feifei Tong, Anningzhe Gao, Tsung-Hui Chang, Xiang Wan, Erchao Zhao, Xiaoxi Jiang, Guanjun Jiang

Abstract
Reward models (RMs) are essential in reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) to align large language models (LLMs) with human values. However, RM training data is commonly recognized as low-quality, containing inductive biases that can easily lead to overfitting and reward hacking. For example, more detailed and comprehensive responses are usually human-preferred but with more words, leading response length to become one of the inevitable inductive biases. A limited number of prior RM debiasing approaches either target a single specific type of bias or model the problem with only simple linear correlations, e.g. , Pearson coefficients. To mitigate more complex and diverse inductive biases in reward modeling, we introduce a novel information-theoretic debiasing method called D ebiasing via I nformation optimization for R M (DIR). Inspired by the information bottleneck (IB), we maximize the mutual information (MI) between RM scores and human preference pairs, while minimizing the MI between RM outputs and biased attributes of preference inputs. With theoretical justification from information theory, DIR can handle more sophisticated types of biases with non-linear correlations, broadly extending the real-world application scenarios for RM debiasing methods. In experiments, we verify the effectiveness of DIR with three types of inductive biases: response length , sycophancy , and format . We discover that DIR not only effectively mitigates target inductive biases but also enhances RLHF performance across diverse benchmarks, yielding better generalization abilities. The code and training recipes are available at https://github.com/Qwen-Applications/DIR .

has become one of the essential training processes to align LLMs with human values

)

is the initial model policy served as a reference,

controls the strength of a Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence
(
22
)
between the reference model
\pi_{\text{ref}}({\bm{y}}|{\bm{x}})
and the learning policy
\pi_{\theta}({\bm{y}}|{\bm{x}})

To train LLMs with the above objective, Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)
(

)
has been recognized as the mainstream optimization approach
(

;
3
;
71
)
. Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)
(

)
further removes the critic model in PPO and uses a simplified group-related advantage approximation instead, which has shown competitive performance with practically simpler infrastructures

Reward Modeling

targets learning the human preference distribution via a parameterized reward model (RM)
r_{\phi}:{\mathcal{X}}\times{\mathcal{Y}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
, where
r_{\phi}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})
is the predicted reward score of the input prompt
{\bm{x}}
and the corresponding response
{\bm{y}}
. For every input
{\bm{x}}
, given a pair of response
({\bm{y}},\bar{{\bm{y}}})
, we can calculate the “preference” by comparing the reward scores: if
r({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})>r({\bm{x}},\bar{{\bm{y}}})
, then
{\bm{y}}
is predicted as a more “preferred” response than
\bar{{\bm{y}}}
(denote as
{\bm{y}}\succ\bar{{\bm{y}}}
) and vice versa. We use a binary indicator
\bm{1}_{{{\bm{y}}}\succ{\bar{{\bm{y}}}}}
to represent the event of “human preference”:
\bm{1}_{{{\bm{y}}}\succ{\bar{{\bm{y}}}}}=1
, if
{\bm{y}}\succ\bar{{\bm{y}}}
; and
\bm{1}_{{{\bm{y}}}\succ{\bar{{\bm{y}}}}}=0
, if
{\bm{y}}\prec\bar{{\bm{y}}}
.
Then the RM predicting preference
\bm{1}_{{{\bm{y}}}\succ{\bar{{\bm{y}}}}}
can be regarded as drawing a conditional Bernoulli
(
15
)
random variable from:

\displaystyle q_{\phi}\Big(\bm{1}_{{{\bm{y}}}\succ{\bar{{\bm{y}}}}}=1\Big|{\bm{x}},{\bm{y}},\bar{{\bm{y}}}\Big)=\frac{\exp(r_{\phi}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}))}{\exp(r_{\phi}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}))+\exp(r_{\phi}({\bm{x}},\bar{{\bm{y}}}))}=\sigma\Big(r_{\phi}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})-r_{\phi}({\bm{x}},\bar{{\bm{y}}})\Big),

(2)

where
\sigma(\cdot)
is a Sigmoid function. Note that the ground-truth human preference distribution
p^{*}(\bm{1}_{{{\bm{y}}}\succ{\bar{{\bm{y}}}}}|{\bm{x}},{\bm{y}},\bar{{\bm{y}}})
is unknown. To optimize the reward model, we instead maximize the log-likelihood of
q_{\phi}
with a group of human preference data
{\mathcal{D}}_{\text{Pref}}=\{({\bm{x}}_{i},{\bm{y}}_{i}^{w},{\bm{y}}_{i}^{l})\}_{i=1}^{N}
:

\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{RM}}(\phi)=

\displaystyle-{\mathbb{E}}_{\bm{1}_{{{\bm{y}}}\succ{\bar{{\bm{y}}}}}\sim p^{*}}\left[\log q_{\phi}\Big(\bm{1}_{{{\bm{y}}}\succ{\bar{{\bm{y}}}}}\Big|{\bm{x}},{\bm{y}},\bar{{\bm{y}}}\Big)\right]\approx-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left[\log q_{\phi}({\bm{y}}_{i}^{w}\succ{\bm{y}}_{i}^{l}|{\bm{x}}_{i},{\bm{y}}_{i}^{w},{\bm{y}}_{i}^{l})\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}[\log\sigma(r_{\phi}({\bm{x}}_{i},{\bm{y}}_{i}^{w})-r_{\phi}({\bm{x}}_{i},{\bm{y}}_{i}^{l}))],\text{\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ (by equation\penalty\ \ref{eq:rm_predicted_probability})}

(3)

where each
{\bm{y}}^{w}\succ{\bm{y}}^{l}
is annotated by human judgment with respect to the response content quality.
Equation
3
is commonly recongized as the Bradley-Terry ranking loss
(
9
)
.

Information-theoretic Methods

optimize deep models from the perspective of information theory
(
16
;
38
;
93
;
18
)
. The core methodology of information-theoretic methods is modeling the feed-forward process of neural networks as an information channel transmission, where the correlation between different neural embeddings is measured by mutual information (MI) as:

{I}\left({\bm{x}};{\bm{y}}\right)=\mathbb{E}_{p({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})}\Big[\log\frac{p({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})}{p({\bm{x}})p({\bm{y}})}\Big]=\text{KL}\Big[p({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})\|p({\bm{x}})p({\bm{y}})\Big],

(4)

where
p({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})
is the joint distribution, and
p({\bm{x}})
and
p({\bm{y}})
are the marginal distributions.
Due to its general ability to capture arbitrary non-linear correlations, MI has achieved considerable success as a learning objective in various deep learning tasks
(
16
;
5
;
38
)
.
However, due to the intractable expectation
w.r.t.

p({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})
, the exact MI value in equation
4
is challenging to compute, especially when only samples from
p({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})
are provided. To address this, several approximation methods have been proposed to estimate MI from samples using tractable variational bounds
(
62
;
17
;
6
)
. Barber-Agakov (BA) bound
(
4
)
provides a simple lower bound approximation of MI, by introducing a variational approximation
q_{\theta}({\bm{y}}|{\bm{x}})
:

\displaystyle{I}\left({\bm{x}};{\bm{y}}\right)\geq{\mathbb{E}}_{p({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})}[\log q_{\theta}({\bm{y}}|{\bm{x}})]+H[p]=:I_{\text{BA}}({\bm{x}};{\bm{y}}),

(5)

where
H[p]
is the entropy of the ground-truth distribution
p({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})
.
Besides,
17
propose a variational contrastive log-ratio upper bound (CLUB) also utilizing the variational approximation
q_{\theta}({\bm{y}}|{\bm{x}})
:

{I}\left({\bm{x}};{\bm{y}}\right)\leq{\mathbb{E}}_{p({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})}[\log q_{\theta}({\bm{y}}|{\bm{x}})]-{\mathbb{E}}_{p({\bm{x}})p({\bm{y}})}[\log q_{\theta}({\bm{y}}|{\bm{x}})]=:{I}_{\text{CLUB}}({\bm{x}};{\bm{y}}).

(6)

By minimizing equation
6
, the amount of information between
{\bm{x}}
and
{\bm{y}}
can be effectively reduced. We provide the proof of BA bound and CLUB in Appendix
A.1
&
A.2
. A well-known application of information-theoretic methods is the
information bottleneck
(IB)
(
83
)
, which aims to learn a compressed but informative representation
{\bm{h}}
of an input
{\bm{x}}
to the output
{\bm{y}}
as a trade-off between two MI terms:

\min_{{\bm{h}}}{I}\left({\bm{x}};{\bm{h}}\right)-\lambda\cdot{I}\left({\bm{h}};{\bm{y}}\right),

(7)

where hyper-parameter
\lambda>0
controls the balance between compressing the input
{\bm{x}}
and retaining relevant information for the prediction
{\bm{y}}
. IB has been recognized as a powerful tool for representation learning and widely applied to diverse deep learning scenarios
(
73
;
85
;
30
)
.

3
Methodology

We begin by revisiting reward modeling from a perspective of information theory. Given an input query
{\bm{x}}\in{\mathcal{X}}
and a pair of responses
{\bm{y}},\bar{{\bm{y}}}\in{\mathcal{Y}}
, we denote
{\bm{b}}
as a concerned bias attribute with respect to
({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}},\bar{{\bm{y}}})
. Our debiasing target is to learn a reward model
r_{\phi}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})
that produces predictions
\bm{1}_{{{\bm{y}}}\succ{\bar{{\bm{y}}}}}
highly correlated with the content quality of response pairs
({\bm{y}},\bar{{\bm{y}}})
while eliminating any indication of the pre-defined bias attribute
{\bm{b}}
.
Motivated by the information bottleneck method in equation
7
, we model the debiasing objective as maximizing the mutual information between the input response content and the RM preference prediction, while minimizing the mutual information between the RM prediction and the bias attribute:

\max_{\phi}\underbrace{{I}\left(\bm{1}_{{{\bm{y}}}\succ{\bar{{\bm{y}}}}};{\bm{x}},{\bm{y}},\bar{{\bm{y}}}\right)}_{\text{Preference Term}}-\lambda\cdot\underbrace{{I}\left(\bm{1}_{{{\bm{y}}}\succ{\bar{{\bm{y}}}}};\bm{b}\right)}_{\text{Debiasing Term}},

(8)

where
\lambda>0
is a hyper-parameter balancing the trade-off between preference learning and debiasing. Ideally, minimizing equation
8
should encourage the reward model
r_{\phi}
to capture the true performance signal from the input triplet
({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}},\bar{{\bm{y}}})
, while decreasing the reliance on the bias attribute
{\bm{b}}
.

However, directly optimizing the mutual information-based objective is computationally intractable due to the difficulty in estimating mutual information in high-dimensional spaces
(
69
)
. Hence, we follow the prior works
(
62
;
18
)
and utilize the variational mutual information bounds (as in equation
5
&
6
) to estimate the preference term and debiasing term of equation
8
, separately.

Preference Term Estimation.

Instead of directly enlarging
{I}\left(\bm{1}_{{{\bm{y}}}\succ{\bar{{\bm{y}}}}};{\bm{x}},{\bm{y}},\bar{{\bm{y}}}\right)
, we can maximize its lower bound approximation by applying the BA estimator as in equation
5
:

\displaystyle{I}\left(\bm{1}_{{{\bm{y}}}\succ{{\bm{y}}}};{\bm{x}},{\bm{y}},\bar{{\bm{y}}}\right)\geq{\mathbb{E}}_{p^{*}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}},\bar{{\bm{y}}},\bm{1}_{{{\bm{y}}}\succ{\bar{{\bm{y}}}}})}[\log q_{\phi}(\bm{1}_{{{\bm{y}}}\succ{\bar{{\bm{y}}}}}|{\bm{x}},{\bm{y}},\bar{{\bm{y}}})]+H[p^{*}],

(9)

where
p^{*}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}},\bar{{\bm{y}}},\bm{1}_{{{\bm{y}}}\succ{\bar{{\bm{y}}}}})
is the ground-truth joint distribution of human preference training data, and
H[p^{*}]
is the entropy of the data distribution
p^{*}
as a constant to the learning parameters. By equation
3
, the expectation term in the right-hand side of equation
9
is exactly the commonly used Bradley-Terry ranking loss
(
9
)
of reward modeling
(
2
;
19
)
. Hence, minimizing the RM ranking loss actually maximizes the mutual information between the preference prediction
\bm{1}_{{{\bm{y}}}\succ{\bar{{\bm{y}}}}}
and the input triplet
({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}},\bar{{\bm{y}}})
, encouraging the reward model
r_{\phi}
to output a higher score to the preferred response
{\bm{y}}
. Therefore, given a batch of preference data
{\mathcal{D}}_{\text{Pref}}=\{({\bm{x}}_{i},{\bm{y}}_{i}^{w},{\bm{y}}_{i}^{l})|{\bm{y}}_{i}^{w}\succ{\bm{y}}_{i}^{l}\}_{i=1}^{B}
, we maximize the following RM ranking loss to approximate the preference term in equation
8
instead:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{Preference}}(\phi):=-\frac{1}{B}\sum_{i=1}^{B}\Big[\log\sigma(r_{\phi}({\bm{x}}_{i},{\bm{y}}_{i}^{w})-r_{\phi}({\bm{x}}_{i}.{\bm{y}}_{i}^{l}))\Big].

(10)

Debiasing Term Estimation.

Since the response pairs
({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}},\bar{{\bm{y}}})
contains sufficient information to determine the bias attribute
\bm{b}
, we can conclude that the RM forward process (
\bm{b}\rightarrow({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}},\bar{{\bm{y}}})\rightarrow{\bm{H}}\rightarrow\bm{1}_{{{\bm{y}}}\succ{\bar{{\bm{y}}}}}
) is a Markov Chain
(
75
)
, where
{\bm{H}}=[{\bm{h}}_{\phi}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}),{\bm{h}}_{\phi}({\bm{x}},\bar{{\bm{y}}})]
is the last hidden states of the RM’s transformer backbone. According to the data processing inequality
(
75
)
and the CLUB upper bound
(
17
)
in equation
6
, we have

{I}\left(\bm{1}_{{{\bm{y}}}\succ{\bar{{\bm{y}}}}};\bm{b}\right)\leq{I}\left({\bm{H}};\bm{b}\right)\leq I_{\text{CLUB}}({\bm{H}};\bm{b}),

(11)

where
I_{\text{CLUB}}({\bm{H}};\bm{b})
can be practically calculated with a variational approximation network
q_{\psi}({\bm{b}}|{\bm{H}})
within the data batch
{\mathcal{D}}_{\text{Pref}}=\{({\bm{x}}_{i},{\bm{y}}_{i}^{w},{\bm{y}}_{i}^{l},{\bm{b}}_{i})|{\bm{y}}_{i}^{w}\succ{\bm{y}}_{i}^{l}\}_{i=1}^{B}
:

I_{\text{CLUB}}({\bm{H}};\bm{b})\approx\frac{1}{B}\sum_{i=1}^{B}\Big[\log q_{\psi}({\bm{b}}_{i}|{\bm{H}}_{i})-\frac{1}{B}\sum_{j=1}^{B}\log q_{\psi}({\bm{b}}_{j}|{\bm{H}}_{i})\Big]=:{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{Debiasing}}(\phi,\psi),

(12)

where
{\bm{H}}_{i}=[{\bm{h}}^{w}_{i},{\bm{h}}^{l}_{i}]=[{\bm{h}}_{\phi}({\bm{x}}_{i},{\bm{y}}^{w}_{i}),{\bm{h}}_{\phi}({\bm{x}}_{i},{\bm{y}}^{l}_{i})]
.
By minimizing
{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{Debiasing}}(\phi,\psi)
as a upper bound estimation of
{I}\left(\bm{1}_{{{\bm{y}}}\succ{\bar{{\bm{y}}}}};\bm{b}\right)
, we effectively reduce the correlation between the biased attribute
{\bm{b}}
and the RM hidden representation
{\bm{h}}_{\phi}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})
. Hence, the output RM scores
r_{\phi}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})
, as a deterministic function of
{\bm{h}}_{\phi}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})
, can remain unaffected from the inductive bias attributes
{\bm{b}}
.

As proved in
17
, the better
q_{\psi}({\bm{b}}_{i}|{\bm{H}}_{i})
approximates the ground-truth data distribution
p^{*}({\bm{b}}_{i}|{\bm{H}}_{i})
, the more accurate
I_{\text{CLUB}}
serves as the MI upper bound estimator. Therefore, during the optimization of
{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{Debiasing}}(\phi,\psi)
as in equation
12
, we simultaneously maximize the log-likelihood of
q_{\psi}({\bm{b}}_{i}|{\bm{H}}_{i})
within the batch samples
\{({\bm{H}}_{i},{\bm{b}}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{B}
to maintain the accuracy of the MI estimator:

\textstyle{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{Estimator}}(\psi):=\frac{1}{B}\sum_{i=1}^{B}\log q_{\psi}({\bm{b}}_{i}|{\bm{H}}_{i}).

(13)

Overall Objective.

Based on the above discussion, the original RM debiasing objective in equation
8
converts to the following learning loss, which is practically tractable:

\min_{\phi}\mathcal{L}_{\text{Preference}}(\phi)+\lambda\cdot\mathcal{L}_{\text{Debiasing}}(\phi,\psi).

(14)

Reward hacking occurs when a policy model exploits spurious correlations or misspecifications within the reward function to achieve high scores without fulfilling the intended goal

;

;

;

;

In RLHF, if the RM inadvertently learns inductive bias from the preference data (e.g., a bias towards more verbose
(
79
)
, or sycophantic responses
(
77
)
), the LLM being optimized will learn to exploit these flaws, leading to a degradation in true performance
(
41
)
. Prior work has sought to mitigate reward hacking by empowering RMs, including better data curation
(

;

;
28
)
, model scaling up
(
89
)
, model ensembling
(

)
, reward post-hoc calibration
(

)

)
, and disentangled reward learning

)
. Close to our method, InfoRM

employs an information-theoretic framework to compress the entire latent representation of the RM backbone, indirectly removing spurious information.

Experiment

We first evaluate the effectiveness of our DIR method under three practical debiasing scenarios:
response length
,
sycophancy
, and

as the inductive biases, respectively. Then, we explore whether our method can alleviate the concurrent multi-bias problem.

Relative Bias Attributes.

In our DIR framework, to minimize the correlation between the biased attribute
{\bm{b}}
and the RM hidden representation
{\bm{H}}
, the variational approximation of
q_{\psi}({\bm{b}}|{\bm{H}})
is required. However, when considering response length as the biased attribute, directly predicting the exact number of tokens in each response only based on the compressed representation
{\bm{H}}
is very challenging. Therefore, instead of predicting the absolute value of response length, we introduce the
relative bias attributes
, which only consider the difference between chosen and rejected responses. For response length, the relative bias
{\bm{b}}=\mathbf{1}{\{\text{length}({\bm{y}})>\text{length}(\bar{{\bm{y}}})\}}\in\{0,1\}
, indicating whether the chosen response is longer than the rejected one or not. Thus, the variational approximation for
q_{\psi}({\bm{b}}|{\bm{H}})
becomes a binary classifier indicating the label of the relative bias.

Implementation Details.

Based on the above discussion, we have converted the response length bias into a relative binary bias indicator. Hence, under all three debiasing setups, the bias attributes can be represented by a categorical label,
e.g.
, “longer/shorter” for response length, and “sycophantic/in-sycophantic” for sycophancy. Therefore, in the experiments, we implement the variational network
q_{\psi}
for bias estimation as a lightweight two-layer categorical classifier:
q_{\psi}({\bm{b}}|{\bm{H}})=\text{Softmax}(\text{MLP}({\bm{H}}))
. Moreover, to respond to the relative bias, we further consider the linear transformation to the hidden
{\bm{H}}=[{\bm{h}}_{\phi}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}^{w}),{\bm{h}}_{\phi}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}^{l})]
as the
difference

\Delta{\bm{h}}={\bm{h}}_{\phi}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}^{w})-{\bm{h}}_{\phi}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}^{l})
to emphasize the representation difference of the distinct features of the two responses.

(

\alpha_{1}
for RMs is
2e-6

1e-3

(

1e-5

5.2
Sycophancy Debiasing

Datasets and Models.

Sycophancy bias occurs when an RM learns to favor responses that agree with or flatter the user, rather than prioritizing factual accuracy and helpfulness. Motivated by
77
;
86
, we create a semi-sycophantic dataset by partially contaminating the HelpSteer3 dataset
(
89
)
. Specifically, we artificially inject a sycophantic prefix (i.e., “Yes, you are right.”) into a proportion
\gamma
(e.g.,
\gamma=40\%
) of responses in the training dataset. Within the contaminated subset, the prefix appears in the chosen response with probability
\alpha
(e.g.,
\alpha=70\%
) and in the rejected response with probability
1-\alpha=30\%
, where the remaining
1-\gamma=60\%
of the dataset remains unmodified and contains no sycophantic phrases. This contamination process creates a challenging, mixed-distribution environment in which the sycophantic phrase serves as a strong but unreliable reward signal.

Baselines.

Since other debiasing methods are either mainly designed for length bias (e.g., PoE
(
78
)
, ALBM
(
10
)
, and Length-Penalty
(
26
)
) or are not open-sourced (e.g., CRM
(
86
)
), we primarily compare our method against two key baselines: a standard BT reward model
(
9
)
and InfoRM
(
60
)
.

Evaluation, Results, and Analysis.

To evaluate the models’ susceptibility to sycophancy, we conduct an adversarial test. We take a clean evaluation set and create two versions: a “natural” version and a “sycophantic” version where the undesirable prefix is added to the rejected responses. We then measure the model’s accuracy in correctly identifying the preferred response in both scenarios. A robust model should maintain its accuracy, whereas a biased model’s performance will degrade when faced with the “flattering but wrong” responses. As shown in Table
4
, the performance of the reward models varies under different settings. The BT model shows vulnerability to the bias, as its accuracy on natural examples is generally the lowest, particularly under high contamination. While InfoRM shows a clear improvement and greater resilience, our method demonstrates the most consistent and robust performance, which frequently achieves the highest accuracy across natural, adversarial, and overall settings, even under high contamination ratios. In summary, Table
4
indicates that our explicit debiasing mechanism is effective at mitigating the influence of sycophantic signals, enabling the model to focus more on the intrinsic quality of the response.

5.4
Concurrent Multi-Biases

Real-world datasets frequently exhibit multiple concurrent biases. Hence, we investigate whether DIR can effectively mitigate such co-occurring biases, specifically, length bias and sycophancy bias, simultaneously. Concretely, we extend DIR to length and sycophancy biases by introducing two independent mutual information terms, each with its own debiasing head
\psi_{\text{length}}
and
\psi_{\text{syco}}
, respectively:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{Total}}(\phi)={\mathcal{L}_{\text{Preference}}}(\phi)+\lambda_{\text{length}}\cdot\mathcal{L}_{\text{Debiasing}}(\phi,\psi_{\text{length}})+\lambda_{\text{syco}}\cdot\mathcal{L}_{\text{Debiasing}}(\phi,\psi_{\text{syco}}),

(15)

where
\lambda_{\text{len}}
and
\lambda_{\text{syco}}
are set to
1.0
. We follow the setting in Table
4
, training Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct on the HelpSteer3 dataset under two sycophancy contamination configurations (
\gamma=40\%
and
80\%
,
\alpha=70\%
), where a larger
\gamma
indicates a more challenging setting. All models (BT, length-only DIR, and length+syco DIR) are trained for 1 epoch on the same data, and we report results using the final checkpoint for fairness and convenience.

As shown in Table
6
, on RM-Bench, the joint model (Ours-Len-Syco) achieves the best overall performance and the largest gains on the hardest subset (e.g., at
\gamma=40\%
, Total:
67.25\rightarrow 69.96
, Hard:
39.88\rightarrow 46.85
vs. BT), while still clearly reducing the Pearson correlation with length relative to BT, confirming that length bias is mitigated even when sycophancy is also debiased. We also observe that the length-only model (Ours-Len) attains the lowest length–reward Pearson coefficient, but somewhat surprisingly, the joint length+sycophancy debiasing (Ours-Len-Syco) yields the best overall RM-Bench performance, suggesting that debiasing multiple biases together could also help lead to a more balanced reward model. As shown in Table

Conclusion

We introduce
D
ebiasing via
I
nformation optimization of
R
Ms (DIR), a novel information-theoretic method, to address the pervasive issue of inductive biases in reward modeling for RLHF. By maximizing the mutual information between RM scores and genuine human preference signals while minimizing the mutual information between RM predictions and biased attributes, DIR effectively disentangles genuine human preference signals from spurious correlations,
e.g.
, response length, sycophancy, and format. Equipped with variational bounds and MI estimation strategies, our method handles non-linear and complex bias structures with theoretical rigor and practical efficacy. Extensive experiments across diverse LLM and reward model benchmarks demonstrate that DIR not only mitigates a broad spectrum of biases but also enhances the generalization and alignment quality of reward models, leading to more robust downstream performance. We believe DIR offers a principled, scalable, and widely applicable solution for building more reliable and balanced alignment systems, paving the way toward more human-value-consistent artificial intelligence.

Appendix A
Bound Proof

A.1
Proof of the Barber-Agakov (BA) Bound

We aim to prove that for any variational distribution
q_{\theta}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})
, the mutual information
I(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{y})
is lower-bounded by:

I(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{y})\geq\mathbb{E}_{p(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})}[\log q_{\theta}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})]+H[p]=:I_{\text{BA}}(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{y}),

(16)

where
H[p]
is the entropy of the ground-truth distribution
p({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})
. By the definition of mutual information, we have:

I(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{y})=H(\mathbf{y})-H(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})=H(\mathbf{y})+\mathbb{E}_{p(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})}[\log p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})].

(17)

Consider the expected Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence between the true conditional distribution
p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})
and the variational approximation
q_{\theta}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})
, we have:

\mathbb{E}_{p(\mathbf{x})}\left[D_{\text{KL}}\big(p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})\,\|\,q_{\theta}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})\big)\right]=\mathbb{E}_{p(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})}\left[\log\frac{p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})}{q_{\theta}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})}\right]\geq 0.

(18)

By the linearity of expectation, the inequality
18
can be rearranged as:

\mathbb{E}_{p(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})}[\log p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})]\geq\mathbb{E}_{p(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})}[\log q_{\theta}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})].

(19)

Then we can substitute equation
19
into the definition of
I(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{y})
, yielding:

I(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{y})\geq H[p]+\mathbb{E}_{p(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})}[\log q_{\theta}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})],

(20)

which completes the proof. The bound is tight if and only if
q_{\theta}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})=p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})
almost everywhere with respect to
p(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
.

A.2
Proof of the CLUB Upper Bound

We aim to prove that for any variational distribution
q_{\theta}({\bm{y}}|{\bm{x}})
, the mutual information
{I}\left({\bm{x}};{\bm{y}}\right)
is upper-bounded by
{I}_{\text{CLUB}}({\bm{x}};{\bm{y}})
. We begin with the definition of mutual information:

\displaystyle{I}\left({\bm{x}};{\bm{y}}\right)

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}_{p({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})}\left[\log p({\bm{y}}|{\bm{x}})\right]-{\mathbb{E}}_{p({\bm{y}})}\left[\log p({\bm{y}})\right]

(21)

Let’s focus on the second term, which is the negative marginal entropy
+H({\bm{y}})
. We can express the marginal distribution
p({\bm{y}})
by marginalizing out
{\bm{x}}
:

p({\bm{y}})={\mathbb{E}}_{p({\bm{x}}^{\prime})}[p({\bm{y}}|{\bm{x}}^{\prime})]

(22)

where
{\bm{x}}^{\prime}
is a random variable drawn from the same distribution as
{\bm{x}}
, but is independent of the
{\bm{x}}
in the first term of equation
21
. Substituting equation
22
into the entropy term:

-{\mathbb{E}}_{p({\bm{y}})}\left[\log p({\bm{y}})\right]=-{\mathbb{E}}_{p({\bm{y}})}\left[\log{\mathbb{E}}_{p({\bm{x}}^{\prime})}[p({\bm{y}}|{\bm{x}}^{\prime})]\right].

(23)

Since the logarithm is a concave function, we can apply Jensen’s inequality, which states that
{\mathbb{E}}[\log(Z)]\leq\log({\mathbb{E}}[Z])
and implies
-\log({\mathbb{E}}[Z])\leq-{\mathbb{E}}[\log(Z)]
. Applying this, we get:

-{\mathbb{E}}_{p({\bm{y}})}\left[\log{\mathbb{E}}_{p({\bm{x}}^{\prime})}[p({\bm{y}}|{\bm{x}}^{\prime})]\right]\leq-{\mathbb{E}}_{p({\bm{y}})}\left[{\mathbb{E}}_{p({\bm{x}}^{\prime})}[\log p({\bm{y}}|{\bm{x}}^{\prime})]\right]=-{\mathbb{E}}_{p({\bm{x}}^{\prime})p({\bm{y}})}[\log p({\bm{y}}|{\bm{x}}^{\prime})].

(24)

Now, substituting equation
24
back into our original MI expression equation
21
, we obtain an upper bound on the mutual information:

{I}\left({\bm{x}};{\bm{y}}\right)\leq{\mathbb{E}}_{p({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})}[\log p({\bm{y}}|{\bm{x}})]-{\mathbb{E}}_{p({\bm{x}})p({\bm{y}})}[\log p({\bm{y}}|{\bm{x}})].

(25)

Note that the second expectation is over the product of marginals
p({\bm{x}})p({\bm{y}})
. The inequality equation
25
holds for the true conditional distribution
p({\bm{y}}|{\bm{x}})
. The CLUB bound replaces
p({\bm{y}}|{\bm{x}})
with the variational approximation
q_{\theta}({\bm{y}}|{\bm{x}})
. The key insight from
17
is that the difference between the true bound and the variational bound is an expectation of KL-divergences, and the proposed variational form serves as a practical, sample-based upper bound for minimization. Therefore, we use the variational form as our tractable objective:

{I}\left({\bm{x}};{\bm{y}}\right)\leq{\mathbb{E}}_{p({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})}[\log q_{\theta}({\bm{y}}|{\bm{x}})]-{\mathbb{E}}_{p({\bm{x}})p({\bm{y}})}[\log q_{\theta}({\bm{y}}|{\bm{x}})]=:{I}_{\text{CLUB}}({\bm{x}};{\bm{y}}),

(26)

which completes the justification for using
{I}_{\text{CLUB}}
as an upper bound for mutual information minimization.

\lambda
.

The hyperparameter
\lambda
in Equation
14
governs the trade-off between the standard preference learning objective (
\mathcal{L}_{\text{Preference}}
) and our information-theoretic debiasing objective (
\mathcal{L}_{\text{Debiasing}}

\lambda\in\{0.1,0.3,0.5,1,2,5,10\}

, reveal a clear trade-off.

As shown in the figure, when
\lambda
is too small (e.g.,

\lambda=1
, the bias metric is substantially reduced, while the preference learning performance on RewardBench is maximized. The behavior demonstrates that the proposed method can effectively neutralize spurious correlations without damaging—and in fact while enhancing—the reward model’s core capabilities. Therefore, all main experiments in this paper use
\lambda=1
.
