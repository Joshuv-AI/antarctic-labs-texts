A narrative review on the evolution of outcome reporting in total pancreatectomy with islet autotransplantation
Authors: Javed Latif, Cristina Pollard, Ashley Dennison, Giuseppe Garcea
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background and ObjectiveTotal pancreatectomy with islet autotransplantation (TPIAT) has evolved as a therapeutic option for patients with chronic pancreatitis (CP) unresponsive to other treatments. This narrative review explores the historical advancements and shifting paradigms in outcome reporting associated with TPIAT, highlighting its impact on patient quality of life and long-term endocrine function.MethodsA narrative review was conducted, adhering to the Narrative Review reporting checklist. PubMed and Google Scholar were used to identify reports published using the following key words: total pancreatectomy, islet autotransplantation, islet transplantation, total pancreatectomy with islet autotransplantation, outcome reporting.Key Content and FindingsThe findings underscore the iterative advancements in TPIAT, from early feasibility studies in canine models to cohort studies documenting long-term patient outcomes. Key insights include correlations between islet yield and metabolic success, variability in outcomes across centres, and the transformative impact on pain management and quality of life. Recent collaborative efforts, such as the POST study, demonstrate the growing recognition of TPIAT’s clinical value and the shift towards large-scale multi-centre data reporting.ConclusionsTPIAT has undergone substantial evolution over the past three decades, transitioning from experimental procedures to a validated clinical approach for select CP patients. The paradigm shift in outcome reporting, from small case series to large cohort and collaborative studies, has enhanced understanding of patient selection, procedural efficacy, and long-term outcomes. Future directions should focus on refining techniques, broadening collaborative efforts, and addressing variability in patient outcomes to optimise TPIAT’s clinical utility.

Introduction

Islet transplantation (IT) provides beta cell replacement provides a potential alternative to exogenous insulin and research into this technique precedes the discovery of insulin by more than 2 decades. The concept of extracting viable pancreatic islets was not successfully realised until 1972, when Paul E. Lacy achieved normoglycaemia with an intraportal infusion of islets into rats with chemically induced diabetes (1). The success in rodent models sparked a surge of research that significantly advanced islet isolation. However, transferring these techniques to larger animals proved to be a significant challenge.

Chronic pancreatitis (CP) is a debilitating condition characterised by intractable pain, which remains the most challenging symptom to manage. A range of treatment options exists, beginning with conservative measures such as oral analgesics and regional nerve blocks, progressing to more invasive interventions including endoscopic-guided therapies, surgical drainage procedures, and pancreatic resection (2,3). Total pancreatectomy (TP), which entails the complete removal of diseased pancreatic tissue, is considered a definitive treatment for CP. However, TP is typically reserved for select cases, such as small duct disease, failure of prior drainage procedures to provide symptomatic relief, or patients with genetic mutations predisposing them to CP and increasing their risk of pancreatic cancer. Despite its potential benefits, TP presents significant challenges, including its technical complexity and the complete loss of pancreatic endocrine and exocrine function, necessitating lifelong insulin and enzyme replacement therapy (4).

Total pancreatectomy with islet autotransplantation (TPIAT) offers an alternative to mitigate the metabolic consequences of TP. This complex procedure, performed by a limited number of specialised centres worldwide, involves isolating and reinfusing a patient’s own islet cells pancreatic resection. TPIAT is primarily considered for patients without preexisting diabetes or those with C-peptide-positive diabetes, as it can significantly reduce post-procedure insulin requirements and improve glycaemic control (5). While TPIAT has demonstrated benefits in preserving endogenous insulin production and enhancing quality of life, its role within the broader spectrum of surgical interventions remains debated. Some clinicians advocate for parenchymal-preserving surgeries as a less extensive alternative, particularly in centres where TPIAT is not widely available (6). Additionally, the perception of TPIAT as an aggressive approach underscores the need for further research into patient selection criteria. Studies examining the proportion of CP and recurrent acute pancreatitis (RAP) patients ultimately requiring TP or TPIAT, as well as comparative outcomes across different surgical strategies, would help clarify its role within the treatment paradigm.

The aim of this paper is to review the evolution of how outcomes have been reported for IAT as TPIAT has become a more widely performed procedure. We present this article in accordance with the Narrative Review reporting checklist (available at https://hbsn.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/hbsn-2024-755/rc).

The search covered the period from 1972 to 2023, reflecting the earliest preclinical work on IT through to the most recent clinical series and multicentre collaborative studies. Reference lists of included studies and relevant reviews were also hand-searched to identify additional publications.

Inclusion criteria were studies addressing total pancreatectomy with islet autotransplantation (TPIAT) or islet autotransplantation (IAT), with a focus on outcome reporting. Eligible studies included animal models, early human case reports and series, cohort studies, and systematic reviews. Exclusion criteria were studies on allogeneic IT, those unrelated to TPIAT/IAT, and reports not presenting outcomes. Only articles published in English were included.

Study selection was performed by the primary author (J.L.), with input from co-authors. Consensus was achieved through collaborative review and discussion. Extracted information included study design, patient population, procedural details, primary endpoints, and outcome measures such as insulin independence, graft function, pain control, and quality of life. Key findings were summarised in narrative form and tabulated where appropriate.

Evolution of outcome reporting in IAT and TPIAT

Animal studies on IAT

Initial investigations investing approaches to mitigating the impact of type IIIc diabetes in patients with CP was conducted using canine models. One of the first animal studies on IAT was reported by Kretschmer et al. in 1977 who used a canine model to examined whether diabetes could be improved in forty-eight dogs by the autotransplantation of pancreatic fragments to the spleen (7). A K value (measure of change from glucose output from the liver and glucose removal from the periphery) during a glucose tolerance test (GTT) was performed at 2 and 10 weeks following IAT. In 12 dogs who had received an IAT, fatal diabetes was demonstrated to occur immediately following splenectomy. It was concluded that pancreatic fragments could be autotransplanted into the spleen without separation of endocrine and exocrine tissue (7). These findings supported the work of Mirkovitch and Campiche who a year earlier had shown that following TP dogs were rendered normoglycaemic by splenic IAT using pancreatic fragments dispersed using collagenase (8).

Following these initial studies, Sutherland et al. employed a number of canine models to define and optimise the conditions for collagenase digestion to maximise the islet yield and viability for splenic IAT (9). They concluded that the spleen could withstand the introduction of exocrine cells and could ensure that an high yield of islets are administered to the recipient (9). This was a crucial finding as their previous attempts to treat diabetes through allotransplantation using purified human islets or intraportal transplantation of unpurified islet tissue had been unsuccessful (10).

The studies examining isolation of pancreatic islets was performed in animal models where the pancreas was normal which limits the applicability for patients with pre-existing pancreatic pathology. Mehigan et al. reported the results of IAT in a canine model following pancreatic duct ligation, aiming to replicate the pancreatic fibrosis seen in CP patients and develop a protocol that would be suitable for human TPIAT (11). In total 26 normal dogs (control group) and 39 dogs (utilising several different techniques for IAT) who had undergone previous pancreatic duct ligation underwent TPIAT. After six weeks, acinar cell atrophy and fibrosis similar to that seen in patients with CP developed in dogs following pancreatic duct ligation. In the control group, 23 of 26 IATs were successful. However, in a group of 12 duct-ligated dogs where the same technique of IAT was utilised there was only 1 successful IAT and variation in IAT technique did not improve the success rate.

These initial animal studies provided evidence supporting the feasibility of IAT and the potential for this treatment to significantly improve the quality of life in patients who had undergone TP. The evolution of experimental studies from normal pancreatic tissue to iatrogenic diseased pancreatic tissue facilitated the assessment of the efficacy of IAT where pancreatic morphology was more closely related to that seen in CP the cohort most likely to benefit from TPIAT. These studies concluded that newer techniques were needed to ensure the success of IAT in CP patients and that human studies were the next logical step to evaluate the different approaches.

Early human studies on IAT

Following the early work in Minneapolis on canine models, and confident in their ability to implant islets within the spleen, in 1978 Sutherland et al. reported the first cases of IAT in patients who had undergone previous TP for CP (12). This initial case series included 3 patients and concluded that following IAT in humans, healthy intrahepatic islet could be maintained for a prolonged period demonstrating the technical feasibility of TPIAT in patients with CP.

These early studies were followed by Cameron et al., who reported a case of near total pancreatectomy (>95% parenchyma) with IAT in 1978 (13). The patient was followe-up for a year with metabolic studies and the index post-procedure K value was similar to that prior to pancreatectomy. Although initial results showed good response to IAT, at 6 months the patient developed hyperglycaemia and required exogenous insulin therapy and there was no evidence of graft function in the liver beyond 6 months. At 10 months, there was a significant drop in K value.

The Minnesota group subsequently published their series of ten patients who had undergone intraportal IAT following TP or partial (>90% parenchyma) pancreatectomy for CP (14). All patients had intractable pain from CP that failed to improve with conservative treatment and/or less invasive surgical procedures. The primary outcome evaluated was resolution of pain which was successfully achieved in all patients. Further reporting of the efficacy of IAT in this cohort demonstrated that 3 patients were insulin independent at 1 month, 8 months, and 3 years. One patient became insulin dependent 15 months following TPIAT, and in 3 patients insulin requirements were less than before TPIAT. This case series demonstrated that TPIAT could be considered as an option for these patients, but it remained difficult to predict in which patients IAT would be successful in the long-term.

Following the innovative work from the Minnesota group, a number of centres began utilising IAT in patients undergoing TP or partial pancreatectomy for CP. Hinshaw et al. reported outcomes following near total pancreatectomy (>95%) in five patients with prior operative intervention or where surgical drainage was not possible or appropriate (15). They employed a new method for islet preparation, with steady reduction in collagenase during the preparation until it was completely absent in their last patient. All 5 patients were followed up for 7–21 months following IAT and 2 patients were insulin-independent, 1 patient remained insulin-independent for 20 months until requiring insulin, 1 patient remained insulin-independent for 2 months and 1 patient showed worsening diabetic control immediately requiring insulin (previously borderline diabetic).

A further case series was published by Cameron et al. of 8 patients who underwent near total pancreatectomy with IAT (16). In the initial postoperative period, hyperglycaemia developed in all patients requiring insulin therapy. Six patients became normoglycaemic at 28 days and in 3 of these patients this was maintained for at least 9 months post IAT. They also commented that all patients developed transient portal hypertension except for 1 patient who developed severe portal hypertension (60 cmH2O) that necessitated reoperation where there was found to be significant venous congestion of the small and large bowel. Concern over viability of the grossly congested bowel required a mesocaval shunt but the patient subsequently died as a result of hepatic failure and postmortem showed multiple thrombi in portal venous radicles and hepatic necrosis.

This allowed other units to develop their own programmes based on the demonstration of the technical feasibility, safety and potential benefits of human TPIAT based on the work originally reported by the Minnesota group. Subsequently the interest in the approach developed with more units considering TPIAT for patients with CP and larger cohort studies began to be appear with reports of the long-term outcomes.

Human cohort studies on TPIAT

In 1998, Farney et al. reported the long-term outcomes of TPIAT in 29 patients from the Minnesota group (19). A key finding from this paper was that 11 patients who remained insulin independent at 2 years did not develop graft failure during a mean follow up period of at least 9 years providing evidence that long-term graft function was achievable following TPIAT.

The Leicester group reported their results of 40 patients undergoing TPIAT for CP in 2003 (20). They demonstrated that although grafts continued to function up to 6 years following IAT, there was a clear deterioration over time. This decline of graft function was observed to begin as early as 2 years following TPIAT as demonstrated by the increased requirements for exogenous insulin to maintain glycaemic control. Although it was reported by the Minnesota group that a greater number of islets transplanted correlated with a higher probability of patients remaining insulin-independent (19), the study from Leicester was not able to demonstrate a clear relationship between the islet cell mass and the maintenance of insulin independence possibly related to the smaller number of patients in their study.

In 2011, Morgan et al. from the University of South Carolina describes an alternative route of IAT administration using percutaneous administration (P-IAT) following TP (21) rather than the conventional intraoperative intraportal infusion. In total 36 patients underwent TP with P-IAT. And they concluded that P-IAT was a safe and feasible alternative to conventional intraportal IAT. An additional benefit of P-IAT was the cost-effective nature compared with intraportal IAT, which was supported through the use of cost modelling. It was found that P-IAT incurred a saving of $17,584 per case compared with intraportal IAT.

In 2012 Walsh et al. from the Cleveland Clinic group published their TPIAT experience (22) and from their cohort of twenty patients, 28% achieved insulin independence, comparable with that reported from other units (24–40%) (19,20).

Sutherland et al. in 2012 reported long term outcomes of 409 patients who underwent TPIAT at their institution over a 30-year period (23). The paper demonstrated the efficacy of TPIAT in alleviating pain, reducing narcotic dependence, and preserving some pancreatic function. Among 409 patients, 96% of adults and 98% of children survived after one year. At three years, insulin independence was achieved by 30% overall (25% of adults and 55% of children). The study showed significant improvements in quality of life, with 85% of patients reporting pain relief, and 59% discontinued narcotic use within 2 years. TPIAT successfully maintained glycaemic control in most patients, with 82% having a haemoglobin A1c of less than 7%. The findings advocated for TPIAT as a valuable option for CP patients refractory to other treatments.

Sutton et al. from the Cincinnati group looked into genetically linked pancreatitis and the role of TPIAT in this subgroup (24). Of the 118 patients treated with TPIAT from their unit, 18 were shown to have genetic mutations (CFTR, PRSS1, and SPINK1). The results of TPIAT in this subgroup were similar to those from the traditional cohort from Cincinnati. They also reported that there was a correlation between islet yield and insulin independence, with an average higher yield in patients that were insulin independent; 7,062 vs. 5,669 IEQ/kg/body weight for insulin dependent patients (IEQ: islet equivalents). Their results also suggested that the insulin dependence of the subset with genetic mutations from this cohort could reflect previous pancreatic surgery and a consequently reduced islet yield. This was further corroborated by the Minnesota group by Srinath et al. in 2015 where they looked at factors predicting outcomes after TPIAT, and concluded that the strongest independent risk factor for graft failure was a low islet yield (25).

In 2022 Turner et al. from the Cincinnati group published a 10-year update on outcomes and assessment of long-term durability of graft in TPIAT, demonstrating that although islet function does decline over time stable glycaemic control was still maintained (26). A year later in 2023 Pollard et al. from the Leicester group published their long-term outcomes following TPIAT with a specific focus on graft function (27). They found that islet graft function was maintained at 10-year follow-up and even in those patients in the poor response group [as per the Minnesota modified Auto-Igls criteria (28)], there is evidence of C-peptide release. C-peptide is a reliable marker for endogenous insulin function and has the advantage of a longer half-life than insulin, allowing it to be measured in blood more easily (29). This trait of C-peptide has seen it utilised in clinical trials to assess the impact of therapies designed to preserve or regenerate beta cells, making it particular useful in the setting of IAT (30,31).

This has already been developed in the United States with the POST (Prospective Observational Study of TPIAT) study, funded by the National Institutes of Diabetes and Digestive and Kidney Diseases (NIDDK), with a handful of studies already reported from this collaboration (34,35).

Recent advancements and future directions

Advancements in IAT techniques have also been explored to enhance outcomes. Traditional methods involve infusing islet cells into the liver via the portal vein; however, this approach is associated with challenges such as early graft loss due to innate inflammatory responses and relative hypoxia in the portal circulation (36). Alternative sites, including the omentum, muscle, and bone marrow, are being investigated to mitigate these issues. For instance, IT into the brachioradialis muscle has shown promising results, with early neovascularisation observed in murine models and successful engraftment in human subjects (37).

Despite these advancements, several challenges remain in optimising TPIAT outcomes. The correlation between islet yield and graft function appears to be influenced by factors such as patient age, duration of pancreatitis, and preoperative metabolic status, indicating a high degree of inter-patient variability. Accurately predicting long-term outcomes necessitates meticulous patient selection and may benefit from the identification of biomarkers indicative of islet health (38).

Future goals in TPIAT research should focus on refining patient selection criteria, enhancing islet isolation and transplantation techniques, and exploring alternative transplantation sites to improve graft survival and function. Additionally, comprehensive assessments of quality of life and pain reduction post-TPIAT are essential to fully understand the procedure’s impact and to guide further improvements in patient care (39).

The change in reporting of outcomes in TPIAT

The method of reporting outcomes in TPIAT has undergone a paradigm shift over the last three decades. The earliest TPIAT reports were lab based and based on animal studies but as the first units began to develop TPIAT as a viable clinical option for the treatment of CP, centres began reporting small series generally <10 patients. Subsequently larger series became the norm as higher numbers of TPIAT were performed. As assessment of patients with longer follow-up became possible larger cohort studies were published from specialised units with the first systematic review of TPIAT being published in 2014 (40). It is pertinent to remember that the dominant rationale for performing TPIAT is to improve the quality of life of patients suffering from intractable pain due to CP, with the additional benefit of maintaining at a minimum a certain degree of endocrine function through the use of IAT. TPIAT has shown a transformative impact on patients with CP. The POST study highlighted significant reductions in pain and opioid use post-surgery, with many patients experiencing substantial relief from the debilitating pain associated with CP (41). However, the persistence of opioid use in some cases underscores the complexity of pain resolution, likely influenced by factors such as disease duration and opioid use prior to surgery thus making weaning to complete abstinence difficult. Approximately 30–50% of patients achieve insulin independence shortly after TPIAT, though outcomes are variable depending on the islet mass transplanted and its viability. The variability in diabetes outcomes emphasises the importance of patient selection and the timing of intervention, as these are critical factors in optimising both pain relief and preservation of endocrine function. The differences in results across various centres in the recent cohort studies can be attributed to variations in patient populations, clinical protocols, treatment regimens, and data collection methods. Differences in age distribution, comorbidities, and genetic factors among participants likely influenced outcomes. Additionally, inconsistencies in diagnostic criteria, intervention timing, and follow-up procedures contributed to variations. Disparities in treatment regimens, including drug dosages, surgical techniques, and provider expertise, further impacted results. Lastly, differences in data recording, reporting, and analysis methods across centres may have introduced inconsistencies, collectively explaining the observed variations in study findings.

There is no doubt that TPIAT provides an effective solution for improving quality of life and reducing the burden of diabetes in select patients with CP, but the complexity of the procedure must be appreciated by both the clinicians and the patient. In addition, the rise of TPIAT in the U.S. is closely linked to regulatory challenges surrounding allogeneic islet cell transplantation (42). Allogeneic islet cell transplantation, which involves transplanting islet cells from a donor to a recipient, faces significant regulatory hurdles in the U.S., where islets are classified as “more than minimally manipulated” human cell and tissue products (HCT/Ps) by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA), requiring stringent regulations akin to those for biologic drugs. This classification has severely limited the availability of allogeneic islet cell transplantation, with only 11 procedures performed between 2016 and 2019, all within clinical trials. In contrast, TPIAT, which utilises the patient’s own islet cells, bypasses these regulatory complexities, making it a more accessible and practical option for patients requiring pancreatectomy in the context of CP and thus correlating with an increase in patients undergoing this approach.

The change in reporting of outcomes for TPIAT over the past few decades has reflected the availability of long-term data through ongoing follow-up. This has coincided with the majority of publications regarding outcomes being larger clinical studies, and recently multicentre collaborative studies. It is hoped this will convey significant clinical benefits for TPIAT patients as further data is collated and published.

Conclusions

The iterative evolution of TPIAT took several decades before it could be considered a safe and viable option for patients with CP. Over the last 20 years, there has been a paradigm shift in TPIAT outcome reported facilitated by the data from large cohort studies from units worldwide but predominantly from the USA. This is possibly due to an increasing number of patients being offered TPIAT, and collaboration between specialised centres is the natural approach to further refine outcome reporting and answer remaining questions about clinical protocols. This has already been demonstrated by the POST study in the USA and further large-scale collaborative studies are anticipated. In addition, the rise of TPIAT in the U.S. is closely linked to regulatory challenges surrounding allogeneic islet cell transplantation. The FDA’s classification of allogeneic islet cells as a biologic drug necessitated a license, imposing significant restrictions on their transplantation. These regulatory barriers limited allogeneic transplantation and facilitated the development of IAT, contributing to the expansion of TPIAT. Understanding this regulatory backdrop is crucial for contextualising the historical evolution of TPIAT.
