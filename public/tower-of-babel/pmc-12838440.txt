Associations of targeted next-generation sequencing with antimicrobial stewardship and clinical outcomes in pediatric lower respiratory tract infections
Authors: Zongming Yang, Dong Xu, Xiuyun Zhou, Wankai Xue, Wenjing Zhang, Xiaopei Cao, Lin He, Rui Shi, Xiaoping Luo, Yongjian Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe impact of targeted next-generation sequencing (tNGS) in pediatric lower respiratory tract infections (LRTI) remains largely unknown. We aimed to investigate whether adding tNGS to standard conventional microbiological tests (CMTs), compared with CMTs alone, was associated with antimicrobial stewardship and clinical outcomes.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study was conducted at Tongji Hospital, Wuhan, between 2023-2024. Pediatric patients hospitalized with LRTI were categorized into tNGS (tNGS in addition to CMTs, typically as a second-line test) or control (CMTs alone) groups based on testing received during the first 4 days after admission. To address potential baseline confounding, we performed 1:2 propensity score matching to balance key clinical characteristics between groups. The primary outcomes were whether and time to provision of pathogen-directed treatment within 96 hours after the index time. Secondary outcomes included other antimicrobial stewardship measures and clinical outcomes. Between-group outcomes were assessed using generalized linear models to estimate risk differences (RDs, identity link) and odds ratios (ORs, logit link) for binary outcomes, while time-to-event analyses were performed using Fine-Gray subdistribution hazard models to account for competing risk of discharge, with results reported as hazard ratios (HRs) and corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs).ResultsAfter matching, 520 participants were included (182 tNGS group; 338 control group). During follow-up, 75 patients (41.21%) in the tNGS group and 48 (14.20%) in the control group received pathogen-directed treatment, with an absolute RD of 27.01% (95% CI: 18.95%, 35.07%) and an OR of 4.23 (95% CI: 2.78, 6.51). The mean time to treatment was 58.99 hours in the tNGS group vs. 82.95 hours in the control group (mean difference, −23.96 hours; 95% CI: −31.02, −16.90), with a corresponding HR of 3.55 (95% CI: 2.47, 5.09). Patients in the tNGS group also had lower rates of non-pathogen-directed antibiotic use (OR=0.52, 95% CI: 0.33, 0.79). Clinical outcomes were similar between the two groups.ConclusionsUse of tNGS as a second-line test was associated with faster and more targeted antimicrobial treatment in pediatric LRTIs, but its impact on clinical outcomes requires further investigation.Clinical trial numberNot applicableSupplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12931-025-03462-9.

Introduction

Lower respiratory tract infections (LRTIs) were one of the leading cause of hospital admissions and mortality among children and adolescents younger than 14 years, accounting for 69.9 million episodes and 0.55 million deaths worldwide in 2021[1]. These infections present significant diagnostic challenges due to their diverse etiologies, with overlapping and indistinguishable clinical presentations. Accurate and timely identification of the causative pathogens is crucial for optimizing antimicrobial therapy, preventing antimicrobial resistance [2], and improving patient outcomes [3]. Although conventional microbiological tests (CMTs), including microscopy, culture, serology, and pathogen-specific polymerase chain reaction (PCR), remain the diagnostic cornerstone, they fail to identify a causative pathogen in more than half of patients with LRTI [4, 5]. This substantial diagnostic gap has created an urgent clinical need for broad-spectrum detection technologies capable of simultaneously identifying multiple pathogens with enhanced sensitivity.

Targeted next-generation sequencing (tNGS) has emerged as a promising solution to address these diagnostic limitations. By simultaneously screening for comprehensive panels of common pathogens (including bacteria, viruses, fungi, and atypical pathogens), tNGS offers enhanced diagnostic breadth and sensitivity compared to CMTs, making it an increasingly valuable complement to CMTs in LRTI diagnosis [6, 7]. The diagnostic performance advantages of tNGS over CMTs have been clearly demonstrated [7–11]. However, compelling evidence demonstrating its impact on patient management decisions and outcomes in real-world pediatric settings remains limited. While recent studies have investigated the potential impact of tNGS on antimicrobial use [10, 12, 13], they were constrained by before-after study designs and yielded ambiguous conclusions due to the lack of appropriate control groups.

Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate whether adding tNGS as a second-line test to standard CMTs, compared with CMTs alone, was associated with improved antimicrobial stewardship and clinical outcomes in pediatric patients hospitalized with LRTI.

Methods

Study design and participants

This retrospective cohort study was conducted at Tongji Pediatric Hospital (TJPH), affiliated with Tongji Hospital of Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology, Wuhan. TJPH is a major pediatric medical center in central China, with annual volumes exceeding 1 million outpatients and 30,000 inpatients in 2024. We included patients who were diagnosed with LRTI and hospitalized in the Department of Pediatric Respiratory Medicine from January 1, 2023, to December 31, 2024. Participants aged > 14 years or with length of stay > 30 days were excluded (Fig. 1A). An episode of LRTI was defined as having two or more of the clinical features including clinical symptoms, physical signs, and chest imaging [14–16].Fig. 1Flow diagrams and study design

Flow diagrams and study design

Since over 95% of patients received CMT results within four days of admission, and tNGS was generally performed as a second-line test for patients with either negative CMT results or complex medical conditions where symptoms were not fully explained by initial positive CMT results [17], we established the first four days after admission as the evaluation window (Fig. 1B). The index time was set at 0:00 on day 4, with the exposure period defined as the time from hospital admission to index time. Participants were categorized into the tNGS group if they received tNGS testing in addition to CMT, or the control group if they received CMT alone during the exposure period. Participants who were discharged or initiated pathogen-directed treatment during the exposure period were further excluded (Fig. 1A).

The study complied with the Declaration of Helsinki principles and was approved by the Ethics Committee of Tongji Hospital (No.TJ-IRB202506028). Informed consent was waived due to the use of anonymous and de-identified secondary data.

Sample collection and transport

Nasopharyngeal or oropharyngeal swabs and blood samples were collected within 12 hours after admission. Because children are often unable to produce adequate sputum for laboratory testing [18], nasopharyngeal aspirate (NPA) specimens were collected using a disposable suction catheter under negative pressure. Flexible bronchoscopy was performed to collect bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) in participants presenting with severe radiological findings (e.g., consolidation, atelectasis, emphysema) or when initial, less invasive specimens failed to yield a pathogen diagnosis. All samples were transported to the laboratory immediately after collection and subsequently stored at −80℃ until processing.

tNGS workflow construction

NPA or BALF samples were transported to one of two local commercial in vitro diagnostics laboratories (KingMed Diagnostics in 2023 and HUGO Biotech in 2024). Detailed methodologies are provided in the Supplementary Methods. Briefly, following nucleic acid extraction, specific primers are designed to target-specific sequences, as well as some selected antimicrobial resistance sequences for enrichment of multiplex targets, followed by library preparation for NGS sequencing and bioinformatics analysis. The multiplex PCR-tNGS (mp-tNGS) panel covered pathogens commonly encountered in clinical scenarios (Table S1 and Table S2).

Conventional microbiological tests

CMTs included microscopy, culture, immunoassays, and real-time PCR. NPA or BALF samples underwent microscopic examination after smear preparation and staining, as well as culture for detection of both bacteria and fungi. When mycobacterial infection was suspected, mycobacterial culture was also performed. Additionally, (1,3)-β-D-glucan and galactomannan tests were conducted to assist in the diagnosis of fungal infections. Serological antibody detection was performed for 4 atypical pathogens and 5 viruses (Table S3). Nasopharyngeal or oropharyngeal swabs were analyzed using real-time PCR to detect 2 atypical pathogens and 11 viruses (Table S4). Microscopy was performed on NPA samples according to guidelines. A purulent-looking portion was gram stained, and samples with < 10 squamous epithelial cells (SECs), > 25 leukocytes, or a ratio of leukocytes/SECs > 2.5 per field at a 100× magnification, were classified as valid [19]. Attending physicians determined which CMT should be performed based on the patient’s clinical manifestations. The proportions of each CMT performed in the tNGS and control groups are detailed in Table S5.

Covariates

Covariates were selected a priori as potential confounders and collected from the electronic data warehouse of TJPH. Demographics included age (months) and sex. Duration of symptoms prior to admission (days), comorbidities (chronic heart and lung diseases, preterm birth, and cesarean delivery), and vital signs (temperature [°C], respiratory rate [breaths per minute], and pulse rate [beats per minute]) were assessed at admission. A diagnosis of severe pneumonia was established if the patient met at least one of the following criteria: poor general condition; refusal to feed or signs of dehydration; impaired consciousness; significantly increased respiratory rate (infants ≥ 70 breaths/min, older children ≥ 50 breaths/min); cyanosis; dyspnea; oxygen saturation < 92% at sea level or < 90% in high-altitude areas; multi-lobar involvement or > 2/3 involvement of a single lobe; pleural effusion; or extrapulmonary complications [20]. Information on laboratory results (white blood cells [WBC, ×10⁹/L], lymphocytes [LYM, ×10⁹/L], platelets [PLT, ×10⁹/L], hemoglobin [Hb, g/L], albumin [Alb, g/L], alanine aminotransferase [ALT, IU/L], prothrombin time [PT, seconds], and serum creatinine [Scr, µmol/L]), chest radiological examinations (computed tomography [CT] and X-ray), microbiological results (bacteria, viruses, fungi, and atypical pathogens) by CMTs, antimicrobial treatment (antibiotics, antivirals, and antifungals), and oxygen administration was obtained during the exposure period. When multiple measurements of the same parameter were recorded for the same individual, only the earliest recorded value was considered. The calendar year of admission was also included as a predefined covariate. Given the low proportion of missing covariates (< 10%, Table S6), single imputation methods were employed for data processing [21]. Continuous variables were imputed using median values, while categorical variables were imputed by assigning the most frequent category.

Outcomes and follow-up

The primary outcomes were (1) binary outcome: provision of pathogen-directed treatment based on relevant microbiological test results and (2) time-to-event outcome: time to provision of pathogen-directed treatment. This approach has been used in a previous randomized controlled trial examining the impact of PCR-based panels on diagnostic stewardship in adult community-acquired pneumonia [22]. Follow-up started from the index time until receiving pathogen-directed treatment, discharge, or 96 hours after index time, whichever came first.

Two of our co-authors (D.X. and X.Y.Z.) reviewed the medical records and assessed whether and when a patient received pathogen-directed antimicrobial treatment based on microbiological test results, following the key principles outlined in the Supplementary Methods and Table S7. In cases of disagreement, a third senior physician (Y.J.H.) arbitrated. Pathogen-directed antimicrobial treatment was further categorized into seven patterns: antibiotic de-escalation, antibiotic escalation, antibiotic adjustment, initiated antiviral treatment with prior empirical antibiotic treatment, initiated antifungal treatment with prior empirical antibiotic treatment, initiated pathogen-directed antimicrobial treatment without prior empirical antibiotic treatment, and continuation of appropriate empirical antimicrobial treatment. According to the antibiotic spectrum index developed by Jeffrey and colleagues [23], we assigned frequently used antibiotics point values ranging from 2 to 13 (Table S8).

Secondary outcomes were assessed between index time and the time of discharge. These included antimicrobial stewardship measures: provision of any antibiotics, provision of intravenous antibiotics, provision of broad-spectrum antibiotics (defined as antibiotic spectrum index ≥ 7), provision of non-pathogen-directed antibiotics, provision of any antivirals, provision of non-pathogen-directed antivirals, provision of any antifungals, and provision of non-pathogen-directed antifungals. Clinical outcomes including PICU admission, oxygen administration, and time from index time to discharge were also reported.

Statistical analysis

We employed a propensity score (PS) matching approach to minimize potential confounding. We calculated the PS for receiving tNGS vs. control using a logistic regression model with the potential confounders listed above as independent variables. Patients in the tNGS group were then matched 1:2 with the control group using the nearest neighbor approach with a caliper width equal to 0.2 standard deviations of the PS scale.

Baseline characteristics were summarized using median (interquartile range, IQR) for continuous variables and counts (percentage) for categorical variables. We used standardized mean differences (SMD) to assess the balance of covariates, with differences greater than 0.1 indicating meaningful imbalances [24].

For binary outcomes, we used generalized linear models with binomial distribution, applying an identity link to estimate absolute risk differences or a logit link to estimate odds ratios (ORs) with their 95% confidence intervals (CIs) [25]. Multinomial logistic regression models were employed to estimate the relative risk ratios (RRRs) and 95% CIs for associations between tNGS and patterns of pathogen-directed treatment, with non-provision of pathogen-directed treatment as the reference group. For the time-to-event primary outcome, we used restricted mean survival time models to estimate differences in mean time to provision of pathogen-directed treatment [26], as well as Fine-Gray competing risk models to estimate subdistribution hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% CIs for tNGS vs. control, with follow-up duration as the underlying time scale and discharge as the competing event. The proportional hazards assumption was evaluated using cumulative sums of residuals [27] and there were no violations (p-value > 0.05). We also used the Aalen-Johansen estimator to plot cumulative incidence of outcomes and Gray’s tests to compare differences between groups. For continuous secondary outcomes, the bootstrap method with 1,000 resampling iterations was used to calculate median differences and 95% CIs.

The robustness of our results was examined through multiple sensitivity analyses. First, we performed complete case analyses by excluding participants with at least one missing covariate. Second, we excluded participants who received tNGS testing during the follow-up period. Third, we defined the index time as 00:00 on day 3 to mitigate potential bias associated with arbitrarily specified exposure windows. Fourth, we incorporated specific CMTs performed during the exposure period into the PS matching process to further control for residual confounding introduced by differences in the intensity of initial microbiological investigation between groups. Fifth, we excluded participants with a disease duration > 7 days prior to admission to minimize bias related to prolonged illness and potential prior antibiotic exposure that could significantly alter the respiratory microbioe profile. Prior to conducting these analyses, re-estimation of PS and re-matching were performed to ensure the robustness of intergroup balance.

All statistical analyses were performed using R software version 4.5.1 (https://www.R-project.org/). All statistical tests were two-sided, and a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Study population and baseline characteristics

After exclusions, a total of 1,509 eligible pediatric patients were identified, with 185 in the tNGS group and 1,324 in the control group. The median (IQR) age was 39.16 (13.10, 69.43) months, and 585 (38.77%) were girls. Following PS matching, the analytical cohort comprised 520 participants (tNGS group: n = 182; control group: n = 338), and all covariates achieved balance (SMD < 0.1) (Table 1). Of the samples subjected to tNGS testing, 89 were BALF and 93 were NPA. In the tNGS group, 146 of 182 (80.22%) participants had pathogens detected by tNGS plus CMTs, compared with 160 of 338 (47.34%) in the control group with CMTs alone (Table S9).

Primary outcomes

During follow-up, 75 of 182 (41.21%) patients in the tNGS group and 48 of 338 (14.20%) patients in the control group received pathogen-directed treatment (Fig. 2), with a difference in absolute risk of 27.01% (95% CI: 18.95%, 35.07%) and an OR of 4.23 (95% CI: 2.78, 6.51) (Table 2). Pathogens detected by means of tNGS and/or CMTs in patients with pathogen-directed treatment are shown in Table S10. Pathogen-directed treatment resulted in changes in antibiotic prescriptions, with de-escalation in 22 (12.09%) patients in the tNGS group and 19 (5.62%) patients in the control group (RRR = 3.14, 95% CI: 1.63, 6.03), and escalation in 12 (6.59%) patients in the tNGS group and 4 (1.18%) patients in the control group (RRR = 8.13, 95% CI: 2.57, 25.76) (Table 2, Table S11). However, sole antibiotic adjustment was relatively rare and comparable between the two groups (RRR = 2.71, 95% CI: 0.17, 43.72). In addition, patients in the tNGS group were more likely to initiate antifungal treatment with prior empirical antibiotic treatment (RRR = 13.55, 95% CI: 2.92, 62.85) and pathogen-directed antimicrobial treatment without prior empirical antibiotic treatment (RRR = 5.42, 95% CI: 1.60, 18.37), but not for antiviral treatment with prior empirical antibiotic treatment (RRR = 2.71, 95% CI: 0.93, 7.91), compared with their counterparts in the control group. Sensitivity analyses outcomes

In sensitivity analyses excluding participants with at least one missing covariate, the results were consistent in that the magnitude and direction of point estimates with those in the primary analyses (Table S12). When excluding participants who received tNGS testing during the follow-up period, defining the index time as 00:00 on day 3, matching for specific CMTs performed during the exposure period, or excluding participants with a disease duration > 7 days prior to admission, the associations between tNGS and provision of pathogen-directed treatment became more pronounced, with ORs increased to 5.32 (95% CI: 3.42, 8.38), 5.76 (95% CI: 4.14, 8.08), 5.28 (95% CI: 3.40, 8.34), and 6.07 (95% CI: 3.21, 11.85), and HRs increased to 4.29 (95% CI: 2.91, 6.34), 4.20 (95% CI: 3.23, 5.46), 4.37 (95% CI: 2.97, 6.42), and 4.82 (95% CI: 2.76, 8.41), respectively.

Discussion

In this retrospective-matched cohort study of 520 pediatric patients with LRTIs, we found that integrating tNGS into standard pathogen diagnostic workflows was associated with enhanced antimicrobial stewardship through increased the probability of and reduced the time to provision of pathogen-directed treatment, as well as reduced non-pathogen-directed antibiotic use compared with the CMTs alone. However, the addition of tNGS was not associated with short-term clinical outcomes.

Multiple observational studies have shown that tNGS offers improved diagnostic performance compared with CMTs for detecting pulmonary pathogens [7–11]. Consistent with these findings, our study demonstrated a significantly higher pathogen detection rate in the tNGS group compared to the control group using CMTs only. However, improved detection alone does not ensure clinical benefit, as it may not translate into changes in patient management. Recent studies have begun to assess the clinical relevance of tNGS. For example, Li et al. reported treatment modifications in 33 of 336 pediatric patients after receiving tNGS results [10]. Similarly, Qin et al. found that tNGS prompted empirical antibiotic escalation in 35.6% and de-escalation in 8.9% of patients [12]. These studies provide early signals of clinical impact but are limited by before-after designs and absence of control groups. In the present study, we included patients who underwent both tNGS and control groups and used PS matching to minimize indication bias [28]. Although differences in exclusion due to early discharge or initiation of targeted therapy were a concern, exclusion rates were similar across groups (72.4% vs. 72.2%), suggesting minimal selection bias. We found that addition of tNGS to CMTs was associated with a 3.23-fold increase in the likelihood of receiving pathogen-directed therapy and a nearly 24-hour reduction in time to treatment initiation. These findings not only reinforce the diagnostic advantages of tNGS but also demonstrate a measurable impact on clinical decision-making.

Because tNGS is primarily used as a targeted, second-line test for a small subset of complex cases [17], rather than as a first-line diagnostic tool, the proportion of the total hospitalized cohort who received pathogen-directed therapy based on its results is modest. Consequently, its overall impact on antimicrobial stewardship within the broader pediatric LRTI population remains limited. However, recent advancements have led to shorter turnaround times and reduced costs, thereby improving its feasibility for wider clinical adoption [7]. This could enhance its potential contribution to antimicrobial stewardship on a larger scale, a possibility that warrants further investigation.

This study also identified distinct patterns of antimicrobial modification associated with tNGS use. Patients in the tNGS group had significantly higher rates of both antibiotic de-escalation and escalation, suggesting that tNGS may support more precise antimicrobial decision-making. Notably, the study period overlapped with a resurgence of Mycoplasma pneumoniae infections in China [29], with about one third of participants testing positive. The ability of tNGS to detect Mycoplasma pneumoniae resistance genes [30] may have facilitated targeted therapy, particularly for macrolide-resistant strains, contributing to changes in treatment patterns. Although overall antibiotic use was similar between groups, patients in the tNGS group had lower rates of non–pathogen-directed antibiotic use, indicating a potential role for tNGS in minimizing inappropriate antimicrobial exposure—a key goal in antimicrobial stewardship. In addition, the tNGS group demonstrated a higher rate of pathogen-directed antifungal therapy initiation following empirical antibiotic treatment. This finding suggested improved identification of fungal pathogens that may be missed by conventional testing and is consistent with previous reports supporting the utility of tNGS in diagnosing fungal infections [31, 32].

To our knowledge, only 2 studies have investigated the association between tNGS use and clinical outcomes in patients with LRTI [12, 33]. Qin et al. reported that most patients showed clinical improvement following tNGS testing, including defervescence, resolution of respiratory symptoms, and reductions in inflammatory markers, regardless of whether antibiotic therapy was adjusted [12]. Similarly, an ICU–based study found that 123 of 142 patients (86.6%) showed clinical benefit from antibiotic treatment, defined as improvement or stabilization of infection-related symptoms, with higher benefit observed among patients in whom tNGS identified causative or possibly causative pathogens (113 of 123 [91.9%]). However, neither study included a comparator group receiving CMTs alone; thus, it remains uncertain whether the observed benefits were attributable to tNGS use. In the present study, we found no significant differences in short-term clinical outcomes between the tNGS and control groups. One possible explanation is the relatively brief 96-hour follow-up period, which may have limited the ability to detect downstream effects. Longer-term studies are needed to more fully assess the clinical impact of tNGS and its potential role in improving patient outcomes.

This study has several notable strengths. First, the evaluation of tNGS as a second-line diagnostic tool reflects real-world clinical practice, enhancing the relevance of the findings. Second, the use of comprehensive clinical data and PS matching allowed for the creation of comparable groups and reduced the potential for indication bias. Third, the study focused on assessing the clinical utility of tNGS beyond diagnostic performance, offering insights into its impact on patient management. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. As a retrospective observational analysis, the possibility of residual confounding from unmeasured variables cannot be excluded. Specifically, data on vaccination status against pneumococcus and influenza were not systematically available, which could potentially influence pathogen distribution and outcomes. However, the overall low coverage of these vaccines in the study region, along with the inclusion of calendar year in our PS matching, likely mitigates some of these concerns. The single-center design, while ensuring consistency in clinical protocols and laboratory practices, may limit the generalizability of findings to other pediatric populations or health care settings with differing tNGS platforms or workflows. In addition, the limited sample size precluded subgroup analyses, such as comparisons between different specimen types (e.g., BALF vs. sputum) or among patients with varying clinical characteristics.

Conclusions

In this retrospective cohort study, use of tNGS as a second-line diagnostic test was associated with more timely and targeted antimicrobial therapy among hospitalized children with LRTI. These findings support current consensus that recommend tNGS as a complementary tool to CMTs. However, randomized clinical trials are needed to validate these results and to assess the long-term clinical outcomes associated with tNGS implementation.
