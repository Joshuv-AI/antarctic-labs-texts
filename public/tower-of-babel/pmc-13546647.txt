Association Between the Geriatric Nutritional Risk Index and All-Cause Mortality in Elderly Patients with Acute Ischemic Stroke After Intravenous Thrombolysis
Authors: Zhihong Wang, Yu Fu, Jian Chen, Jinghong Lu, Feng Tu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMalnutrition is related to adverse outcomes across a wide range of diseases. This study aimed to examine the relationship between the Geriatric Nutritional Risk Index (GNRI) and prognosis in elderly patients with acute ischemic stroke (AIS) following intravenous thrombolysis.MethodsThis single-center cohort study recruited elderly patients with AIS who received intravenous alteplase thrombolysis, and GNRI was used to evaluate their nutritional status. All-cause mortality was the primary outcome. The relationship between GNRI and prognosis was investigated using Cox proportional hazards models and restricted cubic spline.ResultsThree hundred eighteen elderly patients with AIS who underwent intravenous thrombolysis were included and stratified into tertiles based on GNRI. The mean age was 73.05 ± 8.10 years. During a median follow-up of 17.6 months (IQR, 10.8–23.4 months), 38 (12.0%) patients died. Multivariable Cox analysis showed that GNRI (HR 0.944, 95% CI 0.912–0.978, P=0.001) was significantly related to a reduced risk of all-cause death in AIS. T3 group (HR: 0.349; 95% CI: 0.133–0.914, p=0.032) was associated with lower all-cause mortality rates, using T1 group as the reference. Additionally, a linear association between GNRI and all-cause mortality was found using RCS (nonlinear p-value = 0.478).ConclusionThis study found that among older patients with AIS, lower levels of GNRI were linked to all-cause mortality following intravenous thrombolysis.The GNRI could potentially be used to predict all-cause mortality; however, further multicenter studies are needed to confirm the generalizability of these findings.

Introduction

Acute ischemic stroke (AIS) imposes a substantial global disease burden because of its high rates of morbidity, disability, and mortality.1,2 An efficient medical treatment for AIS is intravenous thrombolysis, which dissolves thrombus formation by activating fibrinogen and quickly restoring cerebral blood flow.3–5 However, the revascularization rate of intravenous thrombolysis is low and limited by the time window,3,5 and even if revascularization occurs, the reocclusion rate is still as high as 22%, and some patients may even undergo hemorrhagic transformation, which leaves severe neurocognitive dysfunction.6 In view of the poor clinical outcome of patients with AIS intravenous thrombolysis, it is necessary to identify the risk factors affecting their prognosis especially the modifiable clinical features, and to reduce the risk of poor prognosis through intensive management.

In many disorders, malnutrition is linked to a poor clinical prognosis.7 Aortic valve calcification, the development of atherosclerosis, and elevated levels of inflammatory response have all been found to be positively correlated with malnutrition.8–10 According to Foley et al,11 between 6% and 62% in patients with stroke suffer from malnutrition. There are clinical advantages of malnutrition that can be corrected or ameliorated compared to other risk factors.12 The Geriatric Nutritional Risk Index (GNRI) is calculated based on height, weight, and serum albumin levels.10,13 Several recent studies have demonstrated that GNRI can be used to predict clinical prognosis in cardiovascular disease and ischemic stroke.8,9,14–17 A meta-analysis including 13,573 patients with stroke indicated that malnutrition, as reflected by a low GNRI, may indicate adverse outcomes.18 Given their advanced age and increased vulnerability to nutritional deterioration after stroke, elderly patients receiving intravenous thrombolysis may be particularly susceptible to malnutrition-related adverse outcomes. The impact of GNRI on clinical prognosis in older patients receiving intravenous thrombolysis for AIS is unknown. Using all-cause mortality as the endpoint event, we performed a prospective cohort research to examine the impact of GNRI among these patients.

Methods

Study Population

We performed a cohort study at the Affiliated Fuyang People’s Hospital of Anhui Medical University, China. The study population comprised patients with AIS who were admitted and received intravenous thrombolysis between January 2023 and September 2025. Patients were diagnosed with AIS based on clinical presentation and confirmed by brain CT or MRI. All participants were aged ≥60 years. Exclusion criteria comprised: (1) missing clinical data; (2) comorbid severe hepatic or renal dysfunction; (3) concomitant neurological disorders, including intracranial malignancy, acute traumatic brain injury, or dementia; (4) incomplete follow-up data; or (5) a prior history of dysphagia or long-term nasogastric tube placement. Data Collection

Baseline demographic and clinical data were collected, including age, sex, and medical history (hypertension, diabetes, coronary artery disease, atrial fibrillation, smoking, and alcohol consumption). Additionally, clinical characteristics were recorded, including admission laboratory findings and National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS).19 Stroke subtypes included large-artery disease, small-artery disease, cardioembolism, and others (other and undetermined etiology).20 Brain imaging data were independently assessed by experienced neurologists and neuroradiologists who were blinded to the patients’ clinical characteristics. The imaging findings were used to confirm the diagnosis of AIS and, together with clinical information, to determine stroke subtype. Blood samples for biochemical markers were drawn for serum albumin prior to intravenous thrombolysis, and venous blood was drawn under fasting conditions in the early morning of the day following admission for fasting lipid indices. The GNRI was calculated using the formula: GNRI = 1.489×serum albumin (g/L) + 41.7×body weight (kg)/ideal body weight (kg). Ideal body weight was estimated as follows: males, height - 100- (height - 150)/4; females, height - 100 - (height - 150)/2.5.21

Outcomes and Follow-Up

The study’s primary outcome was to determine the all-cause death rate after intravenous thrombolysis in older individuals with AIS who were enrolled and followed up with every three months by telephone interview or outpatient follow-up. Follow-up time was calculated from enrollment to the date of death or December 31, 2025, whichever occurred first, with patients who remained alive at the end of follow-up censored on that date. In the event that a patient passes away during follow-up, hospital and family physician medical records are reviewed to determine the cause of death. Medical professionals review all outcomes.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics summarized baseline data as frequencies, means±SD/medians, or median (M) and interquartile range (IQR). All enrolled patients were categorized as GNRI tertiles, and in survival analyses, the Kaplan-Meier method, and the Log rank test was used to compare the results. The correlation between all-cause mortality and underlying factors was assessed by Cox proportional hazards models, and hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were subsequently calculated. Additionally, we stratified our subgroup analysis by possible confounders. Restricted cubic splines (RCS) was used to evaluate the nonlinear relationship. Given the limited number of outcome events, three knots were selected to reduce model complexity and minimize the risk of overfitting, with knots placed at the 10th, 50th, and 90th percentiles of the GNRI distribution. R 4.1.2 and SPSS 22.0 were used for all analyses, p<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Discussion

In our cohort study, lower GNRI was found to be associated with all-cause mortality after intravenous thrombolysis in AIS. Our previous study focused on the prognostic role of the atherogenic index of plasma in patients with acute pontine infarction, highlighting the importance of metabolic and nutritional markers in stroke outcomes.22 The current study extends this concept to elderly AIS patients receiving intravenous thrombolysis, demonstrating that GNRI is an independent predictor of mortality in this population.

Several studies have pointed out that patients with ischemic stroke frequently suffer from malnutrition and that the nutritional status of the patient, especially in the acute phase, may be more likely to affect the recovery of all body functions.11,23,24 Several nutritional screening tools such as the Mini-Nutritional Assessment (MNA), Nutritional Risk in Critically Ill Patients (NUTRIC), and Nutritional Risk Screening 2002 (NRS-2002) have been used to assess malnutrition.25–28 However, the MNA requires patient cooperation in completing a structured questionnaire,26 the NRS-2002 requires subjective recall of weight changes over the past 3 months,27 and the NUTRIC is a more complex design involving the APACHII score,28 none of these nutritional screening tools are applicable to elderly patients with AIS who may be suffering from deafblindness, cognitive impairment, or aphasia. In contrast, the GNRI, which is calculated using the patient’s objective parameters is more suitable for general studies.

Some studies on ischemic disease have reported similar findings. Lee et al,29 noted that low GNRI was independently associated with exacerbation of cognitive deficits at 3-month prognosis in AIS, and that malnutrition may contribute to unfavorable outcomes in stroke patients by progressively affecting deterioration of cognitive function. A study exploring GNRI and stroke risk in haemodialysis patients showed that low GNRI was an independent risk factor for ischemic stroke and cerebral haemorrhage in patients receiving maintenance haemodialysis.15 Cheng et al conducted a 139-month follow-up of 4,532 elderly patients diagnosed with hyperlipidemia, demonstrating that a lower GNRI (HR=1.686,95% CI 1.212–2.347) was significantly associated with an increased risk of all-cause mortality.14 A study was conducted on stroke patients in 5 hospitals in the Japanese from July 2009 to June 2013. The results showed that 540 participants were included, GNRI (β= 0.89; 95% CI = 0.10–3.47) was an independent factor associated with Functional Independence Measure (FIM) gain (P < 0.05), and it was a predictive factor for lower FIM improvement in elderly stroke patients.30 Furthermore, a study from Korea that used GNRI to evaluate the impact of pre-stroke malnutrition on prognosis in patients with AIS found that nutritional status assessed by GNRI was significantly linked to adverse outcome.31 A recent study included 580 patients with AIS who were assessed for swallowing function using the Oral Food Intake Scale 14 days after admission, and found that a lower GNRI on admission was independently associated with prolonged post-stroke dysphagia.32

Although the mechanism of action between low GNRI and its poor prognosis is unclear, some explanations may be possible based on the clinical definition of GNRI. Serum albumin plays an important neuroprotective role in AIS.33 Che et al,34 analysed the association between albumin and haemorrhagic transformation in 428 patients with AIS following intravenous thrombolysis, and after adjusting for age, and NHISS scores, atrial fibrillation, the authors found that low albumin increased the risk of AIS haemorrhagic risk in patients with intravenous thrombolysis. Dziedzic et al showed that in all stroke subtypes, a worse outcome was linked to decreased albumin, and that relatively high albumin reduced this risk in patients with AIS.35 In addition, low serum albumin predicts increased venous thrombosis, implying a hypercoagulable or hyperinflammatory state.36 The strong association between malnutrition and atherosclerosis was confirmed in a previous study of patients on maintenance haemodialysis, which reported that an increase in abdominal aortic calcification indices measured by CT scans was linked to low GNRI, which may explain the increase in all-cause mortality of ischemic strokes with low GNRI.37 These findings may be the mechanism behind our findings in terms of prognosis, as low GNRI signals a poor functional prognosis for patients, and patients with a poor prognosis may have more severe post-stroke complications and poorer quality of life, ultimately leading to a higher risk of all-cause mortality.38 Slot et al found early clinical prognostic improvement correlates with long-term survival, in part due to fewer complications and more independence in life.39 Furthermore, there may be a larger degree of clinical comorbidity among patients with higher levels of disability, which may interfere with the delivery of appropriate treatment, interfere with physical activity, and, therefore, increase the risk of all-cause mortality.

In this study, the relationship between GNRI and all-cause mortality was investigated for the first time in elderly patients with AIS following intravenous thrombolysis, which provides strong support for related research. However, there are some limitations. First, although we tried to control for potential confounding by confounders in our multivariate Cox proportional risk analyses, we may still be affected by unknown confounders. Second, although the GNRI has been widely used as a nutritional assessment tool, its validity and prognostic performance may vary across populations with different ethnic and demographic characteristics. As our study included a Chinese population from a single center, further validation in more ethnically diverse populations is warranted. Third, no formal a priori sample size calculation was performed, and the relatively small sample size and limited number of outcome events may have reduced the statistical power and increased the risk of overfitting in the multivariable analyses. Fourth, the relatively short follow-up duration may limit the assessment of long-term mortality, and studies with longer follow-up periods are warranted to confirm our findings. Therefore, larger multicenter prospective studies with longer follow-up are needed to validate our findings.

Conclusion

This cohort study found that lower GNRI was linked to all-cause mortality after intravenous thrombolysis in elderly patients with AIS, suggesting that GNRI may have potential prognostic value in this population. Further external validation is warranted before its clinical application.
