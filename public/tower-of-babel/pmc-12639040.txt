Rapid screening for acute rheumatic fever using machine learning analysis of host tissue reactive antibodies
Authors: Brenda Vo, Rukshan A. M. Rafeek, Nuzhat Z. Surve, Mohan G. Karmarkar, Benjamin Reeves, Gautam Rishi, Manisha Pandey, Michael F. Good, Natkunam Ketheesan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Acute Rheumatic Fever and Rheumatic Heart Disease (ARF/RHD) affect over 45 million people globally. ARF/RHD are autoimmune complications following group A streptococcal infections. Current diagnosis of ARF requires thorough medical examination, echocardiography and laboratory tests that are unavailable in most primary care settings where patients with ARF typically first present. This pilot study was conducted to determine whether machine learning-based predictive models could be used to stratify host tissue protein reactive antibodies associated with ARF, that could be incorporated into a lateral flow point-of-care (POC) platform for ARF screening. We investigated serum antibody levels against four host tissue proteins (cardiac myosin, laminin, keratin, and tropomyosin) known to increase in ARF. Serum samples were obtained from: (i) a rat autoimmune valvulitis model (RAV) of RHD (30 streptococcal M protein-injected rats versus 30 controls); and (ii) human samples (25 newly diagnosed ARF patients versus 50 healthy controls). Four machine learning algorithms (logistic regression, decision tree, random forest, and AdaBoost) predicted ARF status using antibody levels detected by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA). Rats injected with streptococcal M protein developed cardiac pathology and demonstrated three-fold higher optical density values for all four host tissue protein reactive antibodies compared to controls. ARF patients showed significantly elevated antibody levels against all host tissue proteins tested (p < 0.01). Random forest achieved optimal performance for rat data (sensitivity 100%, specificity 92.5%, AUROC = 0.97), while AdaBoost excelled for human samples using binary biomarkers (sensitivity 85.0%, specificity 82.8%, AUROC = 0.87). We demonstrate the screening potential of known host tissue protein reactive antibodies and propose lateral flow assay POC technology as a possible advancement toward improved early screening for ARF in resource-constrained environments.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-25171-5.

Introduction

Acute rheumatic fever (ARF) and its complication rheumatic heart disease (RHD) are autoimmune conditions triggered by group A streptococcal (GAS) infections and currently affects over 45 million people worldwide1. ARF/RHD continue to be a major health concern in middle- and low-income countries reducing life expectancy by 20–30 years2. In most high-income countries it has become a rarity, except in among the First Nation populations.

The Jones’ Criteria continue to be the primary diagnostic approach for ARF3, a clinical diagnostic framework requiring confirmation of a recent GAS infection and the presence of a combination of major and minor clinical indicators aided by echocardiography and non-specific laboratory tests4. Early diagnosis, appropriate treatment and antibiotic prophylaxis significantly improve the quality of life and life expectancy for patients with ARF5. Vaccines to prevent GAS infections and associated sequalae are still under development6. A substantial obstacle for a favourable outcome is the limited diagnostic precision and prompt availability of test results for the diagnosis of ARF in primary care settings7. Additionally, only less than 50% of those with ARF have echocardiography proven carditis8. Therefore, the need for a rapid, unambiguous screening test for ARF is widely recognised, with researchers prioritising novel biomarker identification through immune profiling and proteomics3,9,10.

We developed machine learning-based predictive models for ARF screening using serum antibodies against host tissue proteins previously demonstrated to be associated with ARF/RHD pathogenesis. Our models were trained and validated using two datasets: a pre-clinical rat autoimmune valvulitis (RAV) model11,12 and human serum samples from ARF patients and healthy controls13. These preliminary findings demonstrate the potential for translating host tissue protein reactive antibody profiles into a practical ARF screening tool that could address the critical diagnostic gap in resource-limited environments where disease burden is highest.

Methods

Initial assessment of antibody reactivity to cardiac myosin, tropomyosin, keratin and laminin in serum of rats with cardiac damage and controls were determined using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)12. A second set of data was obtained from a clinical study conducted on patients with ARF and healthy individuals13. Briefly the techniques used in these studies are described below.

Samples from the rat autoimmune valvulitis of RHD

All animal experiments carried out were approved by the institutional ethics committee (UNE AEC 19-013/ARA22-065). Female Lewis rats (LEW/SsN; Albino:a, h, c:RT1) used in this study were bred at the University of New England Centre for Animal Research and Teaching (UNE CART). The RAV model has been extensively used as a preclinical model of RHD11,12,14,15. The experimental data included in this study was obtained from experiments conducted at the University of New England (Supplementary Table 1). All priming injections and electrocardiography were performed under isoflurane inhalation anaesthesia (5% induction and 2% maintenance). Briefly Lewis rats were injected with either recombinant streptococcal M5 (rM5) (500 µg) or PBS (100 µL; negative control) with Complete Freund’s Adjuvant (CFA; Sigma, Australia) into the hind hock followed by intraperitoneal injection of 0.3 µg of Bordetella pertussis toxin (Sigma, Australia) on days 1 and 3. All rats received 200 µL subcutaneous booster injections of antigen emulsified 1:1 with incomplete Freund’s adjuvant (IFA; Sigma, Australia) on days 7, 14 and 21. ECG was performed under anaesthesia a day prior to primary injection and prior to euthanasia on day 35 post primary injection. Rats were euthanised on day 35 using 260 mg/kg of Sodium pentobarbital (i.p.). Death was confirmed following cessation of heart and respiratory arrest. Serum and heart tissue were collected for further analysis. Characteristic cardiac damage was determined by changes in conduction abnormalities by ECG, histopathological changes in myocardium and valvular tissue and elevated levels of host tissue protein reactive antibodies.

Clinical samples

Data was obtained from clinical samples from a study that was previously published13 for which ethics approval was obtained from the Institutional Ethics Committee, Seth GS Medical College and KEM Hospital, Mumbai (Approval Number: EC/GOVT-7/2012), and the research conducted in compliance with ethical guidelines for research involving human subjects. Participants were recruited for the study following informed consent. Serum samples were obtained from 25 patients with ARF with carditis who were diagnosed for ARF using Revised Jones criteria and carditis confirmed by 2D Echocardiography and 50 age and sex matched healthy donors13.

Enzyme linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Indirect ELISA was performed to assess the level of host tissue (cardiac myosin, tropomyosin, laminin, and keratin) reactive antibodies in rat and human serum samples as described previously12,14. Cardiac myosin, tropomyosin, laminin, and keratin (10 μg mL–1) coated Maxisorp 96-well plates (Nunc, USA). Sera were added in duplicate at 1:400 dilution of rat sera and 1:100 dilution of human sera. Each well was incubated with 100 µL of goat anti-rat IgG (RRID: AB_2338138) or goat anti-human IgG (RRID: AB_2337584) HRP conjugated secondary antibody (1:5000; Jackson Immunoresearch, USA) for an hour at room temperature and reacted with 2–2’–azino-di (3-ethylbenzthiazoline)-6-sulphonate (ABTS) (Sigma, USA) prior to measuring absorbance at 415 nm using SpectraMax M2/M2e (Molecular Devices, USA).

Descriptive analysis

Non-parametric Mann–Whitney U tests were used to compare antibody reactivity (cardiac myosin, tropomyosin, keratin, laminin) between control and experimental groups in both rat (PBS vs. rM5-injected) and human (healthy vs. ARF) samples, with significance threshold p < 0.05. Principal component analysis (PCA) biplots visualised relationships between antibody measurements, and the discrimination between control and ARF/RHD groups.

Predictive models

Four machine learning (ML) models—logistic regression (LR)18, decision trees (DT)19, random forest (RF)20, and AdaBoost21 – were used to screen for ARF/RHD using 80/20 train-test splits. Due to class imbalance (66:33 ratio) in human data, oversampling balanced the training sets before model training and testing. Model performance was evaluated using the area under the receiver-operating characteristic curve (AUROC), sensitivity, specificity, and F1 score. The F1 score is a harmonic mean of precision and recall, ranging from 0 to 1, where 1 indicates perfect performance. It provides a balanced measure of a model’s accuracy by accounting for both false positives and false negatives, making it particularly useful when evaluating classification performance on imbalanced datasets. RF and AdaBoost hyperparameters were optimized via Leave-One-Out cross-validation. To assess model stability, we performed 100 random 80:20 train-test splits and calculated the mean and standard deviation of performance metrics across all iterations (Supplementary Table 2). All analyses were conducted using R version 4.4.1.

Results

Data from the rat autoimmune valvulitis of RHD

Rats injected with rM5 and developing cardiac pathology showed significantly higher antibody reactivity against cardiac myosin, tropomyosin, laminin, and keratin (all p < 0.0001) than control rats (Fig. 1A, Supplementary Table 2). Median reactivity against keratin and cardiac myosin was three-fold higher in rM5-injected rats, with two notable outliers in keratin measurements (OD 2.575 and 1.585) in the rM5-injected group. Individual biomarkers demonstrated exceptional discriminatory capacity using Youden Index thresholds: cardiac myosin (> 0.56) and keratin (> 0.52) achieved perfect classification (100% sensitivity and specificity), while laminin (> 0.35; 98.3% accuracy) and tropomyosin (> 0.35; 93.3% accuracy) showed near-perfect separation (Supplementary Table 3). These Youden-optimised thresholds substantially outperformed control median thresholds, which would yield only 50% specificity by design, demonstrating the importance of data-driven threshold selection for diagnostic applications. Principal component analysis revealed complete separation between control and ARF groups, with PC1 explaining 83.4% of variance and all four biomarkers contributing similarly to discrimination (Fig. 1B). Cardiac myosin reactivity positively correlated with tropomyosin and keratin reactivities, indicating coordinated antibody responses. The high performance in the rat model reflects a genuinely simple classification problem with well-separated classes, as evidenced by the clear distributional differences (Fig. 1A) and natural clustering in unsupervised PCA (Fig. 1B). We acknowledge this limits direct clinical translatability and have therefore focused our threshold optimisation analysis on the human data where performance is more realistic. The rat model serves its intended purpose: proof-of-concept that these biomarkers can discriminate cardiac damage under controlled conditions. Machine learning approaches (Random Forest, AdaBoost, Decision Trees) were initially applied to binary biomarker combinations using control median thresholds. However, when Youden Index optimization was applied, individual biomarkers achieved perfect or near-perfect classification (cardiac myosin and keratin: 100% accuracy), rendering multivariate models unnecessary as they provided no additional discriminatory benefit.

Data from clinical samples

Antibody reactivity differed significantly between ARF patients and healthy individuals across all four tissue proteins (Fig. 1C; Supplementary Table 2). Tropomyosin reactivity correlated positively with laminin and keratin, but minimally with cardiac myosin (Fig. 1D). Using anti-laminin and anti-keratin antibody reactivity, LR models demonstrated moderate performance with mean sensitivity of 75.6%, specificity of 85.2%, and AUROC of 0.81 across 100 iterations (Table 1). However, cardiac myosin and tropomyosin reactivities were not significant ARF predictors in LR models. Variance inflation factor (VIF) confirmed minimal multicollinearity (all VIF < 2). We further evaluated whether alternative threshold selection could improve classification performance. Comparison with Youden Index optimisation, which maximises the sum of sensitivity and specificity, revealed markedly different cut-points for most biomarkers (Supplementary Table 3). The Youden method generated substantially higher thresholds for keratin (0.52 vs. 0.35), laminin (0.44 vs. 0.36), and cardiac myosin (0.82 vs. 0.69), while tropomyosin remained similar (0.17 vs. 0.18). These elevated thresholds created a more stringent classification criterion, resulting in a consistent pattern across all four models: improved specificity (mean: 88.1% vs. 78.8%) but reduced sensitivity (mean: 76.6% vs. 83.2%) compared to Control Median thresholds (Supplementary Table 4). Notably, overall discriminative ability (AUROC) remained stable regardless of threshold method (differences ≤ 0.02), indicating that threshold selection determines the clinical operating point rather than fundamental model performance. This trade-off suggests Control Median thresholds suit screening applications where missing cases carries greater risk, while Youden Index thresholds benefit confirmatory testing where false positives must be minimised.

Discussion

This pilot study provides preliminary evidence for the potential use of host tissue protein reactive antibodies for screening for ARF. We demonstrated the effectiveness of ML techniques, particularly the AdaBoost models, in analyzing binary biomarkers. With a mean AUROC of 0.87 for predicting ARF, the model shows promising screening potential. Notably keratin and laminin in human samples emerged as key predictive markers.

While host tissue protein reactive antibodies against cardiac myosin, tropomyosin, laminin and keratin in ARF/RHD have been extensively described, their precise role in the pathological process is yet unknown. Furthermore, in ARF, host tissue reactive antibody kinetics can also vary during the disease process13. However, it has been also found that some antibodies to myosin-specific epitopes remain stable in acute stages and in convalescence while in some patients the reactivity shifts to neoepitopes22. Host tissue reactive antibodies are not exclusive to ARF/RHD. For example, anti-myosin antibodies are detected in several cardiac diseases including in myocarditis, dilated cardiomyopathy, Chagas’ disease, Kawasaki disease and myocardial infarction. Although the role of these antibodies in the pathological process of each of these conditions with different aetiologies could be different, they mostly remain unknown23. When planning larger studies, it will be imperative to include clinical samples from patients with other non-ARF/RHD inflammatory cardiac conditions as controls for comparison, alongside well-characterized samples from ARF patients. Until such comparative studies are conducted, host tissue protein antibody detection tests should be considered screening tools rather than specific diagnostic tests for ARF.

Early screening, diagnosis and commencement of appropriate treatment is required for optimal outcome following ARF. While echocardiography may not be easily accessible without time delays in endemic regions, it may also not be a definitive test in the early stages of the disease process. Echo proven carditis is only detected in approximately half the number of patients with ARF who seek medical attention at the early stages of the disease8,10. With a more comprehensive understanding of the immunopathogenesis of ARF4,10, the search for early biomarkers in peripheral blood that may be used for rapid screening is becoming a possibility.3 While antibodies to streptococcal and host tissue protein biomarkers for ARF diagnosis are continuing to be investigated13,24,25 more complex proteomic approaches are also being investigated26.

Detection of antibodies in blood using ELISAs requires a diagnostic laboratory and trained staff, which are not available in most ARF endemic settings. To address this limitation, we piloted machine learning-based predictive models to determine whether selected host tissue protein reactive antibodies can be stratified, with the goal of enabling future detection of such antibodies using a lateral flow assay (LFA) format. LFAs can detect multiple analytes using either a single strip with multiple test lines or multiple strips with single test lines housed in the same device—both formats are commercially available27,28. Both designs face challenges. Multiple test lines are constrained by the physical limitations of strip size, potential cross-reactivity of antigens with conjugates, and complexities in fluid dynamics, which can affect flow rates and binding kinetics. The use of multiple strips in a single assay format can help mitigate these issues. Traditional LFAs rely on visual readouts, which, while simple and instrument-free, suffer from drawbacks such as subjective interpretation, particularly near detection limits, and the inability to quantify analytes. These issues can be addressed using dedicated readers but such readers increase test costs and will not be feasible in low-resource settings29. However, in the past few years, smartphones have emerged as a cost-effective alternative for improving LFA capabilities.

As a pilot proof-of-principle study, this investigation achieved its aims. However, the number of fully characterised clinical samples available to us for this study was limited and restricted to a single endemic setting. Future studies must be replicated with higher number of patient samples and different categories of controls from different endemic regions. Serial samples collected at different stages of the disease process will also aid in understanding the spatial kinetics of each of the host tissue reactive antibodies. Future clinical validation should evaluate both Youden Index and control-based thresholds to optimise for different diagnostic scenarios. While Youden thresholds maximise overall accuracy, control-based thresholds (e.g. 50th percentile) may be preferable for screening applications requiring high specificity to minimise false positives. Threshold selection should ultimately align with intended use—population screening versus confirmatory diagnosis. In parallel peripheral blood samples for several other currently identified biomarkers for ARF must be tested by using both an ELISA and LFA format. Moreover, a combination of biomarkers may be required with machine learning capabilities to increase the specificity and sensitivity of an LFA POC that can be useful in primary care setting. As with screening tests for other diseases, primary care health workers must be familiar with basic symptomatology of ARF to appropriately identify patients who would benefit from such a screening test. Before host tissue protein reactive antibodies and other biomarkers can be used as a screening tool for ARF in primary health care settings, multicenter studies must first be undertaken to test serum from ARF patients at different disease stages, along with appropriate patient and healthy controls. Such studies would be essential prior to developing a point-of-care detection platform.

Conclusion

There is universal agreement that more sensitive methods for screening ARF at the primary care level are essential to afford early treatment and secondary prophylaxis for ARF, in particular in low-middle income countries where the disease causes significant impact4,7,9,10,30. This proof-of-principle study demonstrates the potential use of already known host tissue protein reactive antibodies for screening for ARF achieving an 80% sensitivity. Further research and development of LFA POC technology would enable screening for ARF in primary care settings, particularly in resource-poor countries.
