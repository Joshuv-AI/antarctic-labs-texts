Collective Fluorescence of Graphene Quantum Dots on a Halide Perovskite Crystal
Hugo Levy-Falk, Suman Sarkar, Thanh Trung Huynh, Daniel Medina-Lopez, Lauren Hurley, Océane Capelle, Muriel Bouttemy, Gaëlle Trippé-Allard, Stéphane Campidelli, Loïc Rondin, Elsa Cassette, Emmanuelle Deleporte, Jean-Sébastien Lauret

Abstract
This study explores the dynamical collective fluorescence of C 96 ​ t Bu 8 \text{C}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{96}}}\textit{t}\text{Bu}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{8}}} graphene quantum dots when deposited on the surface of monocrystalline halide perovskite. Despite the tendency of the graphene quantum dots to avoid aggregation in solution and polymer matrices, our findings reveal distinct collective behaviors when deposited on the perovskite surface, here CH 3 ​ NH 3 ​ PbBr 3 \text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}\text{NH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}\text{PbBr}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}} . We observed small clusters of graphene quantum dots rather than isolated single molecules through confocal fluorescence microscopy. Spectral analysis under continuous illumination shows a back-and-forth dynamical transition between an uncoupled, monomer-like state and a coupled state with a redshifted emission. In some cases, this dynamical process is followed by a drastic one-way increase in fluorescence intensity combined with a shortening of the excited state lifetime, which could characterize the emission of ordered graphene quantum dots within aggregates.

Introduction

Light-matter interaction is at the heart of many applications, such as optoelectronics, photovoltaics, and quantum technologies. In this perspective, new emitting materials are constantly being developed. One way to tailor the optical properties is to combine two or more materials to build heterostructures. It can lead to physical processes such as charge and energy transfer or proximity effects
Cummings et al. 2017
;
Fang et al. 2023
;
Zhang et al. 2020
;
Roquelet et al. 2010
. These effects have been studied extensively in heterostructures of 2D materials such as graphene,
h-BN
(
h-BN
), or
TMDCs
(
TMDCs
). The coupling between molecular and solid-state materials shows some assets in this context. Organic chemistry offers a wide choice of molecules whose properties can be tuned at will. For instance, phthalocyanine molecules were coupled to TMDCs to modulate their optoelectronic properties through photoinduced charge transfer
Choi et al. 2016
. Likewise, including perylene-3,4,9,10-tetracarboxylic diimide (PTCDI) molecules in a graphene/
h-BN
heterostructure made it possible to control their triplet state electrically
Svatek et al. 2020
. As another example, N,N’-dimethyl-3,4,9,10-perylentetracarboxylicdiimide (MePTCDI) molecules on the surface of
h-BN
tend to self-organize at 2D, leading to interesting collective states showing redshifted and narrower optical transitions in comparison to the monomer
Juergensen et al. 2023
;
Zhao et al. 2019
. In all these studies, the organization of the molecules on the surface of the solid plays a crucial role.

Here, we study the organization of recently reported elongated
GQD
(
GQD
)
Medina-Lopez et al. 2023
on the surface of a hybrid organic-inorganic perovskite single crystal (
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}\text{NH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}\text{PbBr}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
, called
\text{MAPbBr}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
). These
GQD
s, synthesized by bottom-up chemistry, are highly soluble in
THF
(
THF
) and show a high fluorescence quantum yield (
>90\%
)
Medina-Lopez et al. 2023
. Moreover, their optical bandgap can be tuned over more than 100 nm by increasing in a controlled way the number of sp
2
carbon atoms from 78 to 132
Medina-Lopez et al. 2023
. These properties make them promising materials for light-emitting devices
Lucas et al. 2025
, in particular as single quantum emitters
Levy-Falk et al. 2023
;
Zhao et al. 2018
. On the other hand,
\text{MAPbBr}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
halide perovskite is a direct bandgap semiconductor that absorbs and emits light in the visible range, and is easy to grow by solution chemistry. It is widely studied in the context of photovoltaic
Zhu et al. 2022
and light-emitting applications such as LEDs
Kim et al. 2025
and lasers
Bouteyre et al. 2020
;
Afify et al. 2022
. Interestingly, we show that, in contrast to their behavior in solution and within a polymer matrix, the
GQD
s form clusters when deposited on the surface of
\text{MAPbBr}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
crystal. Here, using time-resolved micro-photoluminescence spectroscopy, we show that the emission of these
GQD
clusters undergoes dynamical spectral jumps characteristic of a transition between coupled and uncoupled molecular states. Moreover, these jumps are sometimes further followed by a stabilization to a final state, showing a drastic increase in the
PL
(
PL
) intensity and a reduced excited state lifetime.

Discussion

Overall, the following picture emerges from our experimental results. After deposition of the
GQD
s on the perovskite surface, the distribution of emitters is composed of small clusters of molecules. The reason for the appearance of these clusters may be related to an inhomogeneous drying of the solution that contains the
GQD
s, the presence of defects at the surface of the perovskite, increased local roughness, or even dangling bonds at the surface. Two regimes can describe the temporal evolution of the
GQD
s’ optical properties. In the first one, which we call ”dancing,” the
PL
spectrum alternates between that of independent
GQD
(monomer-like) and a redshifted
PL
peak. In the second regime, which we call ”red-bright”, we observe a one-way dynamic with a continuous red shift of the
PL
line, which is sometimes associated with a drastic increase in the intensity. The latter is correlated with a much faster decay rate. We must acknowledge the variety of complex behaviors observed from cluster to cluster. Indeed, in some clusters the two regimes are observed, while in others we observe only one: ”dancing” or ”red-bright” regime.

First, we discuss the ”dancing” spectral dynamics. The swing between the two emission lines weighs in favor of a fluctuation between two emissive states rather than a denaturation of the
GQD
. This spectral dynamic, whose main signature is the red shift of the main emission line, could have several origins. One could be related to the Stark effect due to charge fluctuations in the local environment of the
GQD
s. Nevertheless, the redshift observed here is 150 to 200 meV, which seems enormous compared to the Stark shift recorded in other molecular systems. For instance, for dibenzoterrylene (DBT) molecules embedded in an anthracene crystal, a controlled Stark shift of
\sim 1
meV has been reported
Schädler et al. 2019
;
Duquennoy et al. 2024
. Likewise, a giant Stark effect has been reported on the emission of defects in
h-BN
with values up to a few dozen meV
Xia et al. 2019
. Therefore, we do not attribute the ”dancing” spectral dynamics to Stark effect.
As the second-order correlation measurements show that the
PL
does not result from a single
GQD
, the ”dancing” process could be related to a dynamical coupling-decoupling effect between two or more
GQD
s at the surface of
\text{MAPbBr}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
. This type of behavior has already been reported in other molecular systems. For instance, a ”dancing” process has been reported in co-facial
PDI
(
PDI
) derivatives
Yoo et al. 2010
or in dendrimers functionalized with several chromophores
Hofkens et al. 2000
. In their study, Hofkens
et al

Hofkens et al. 2000
designed dendrimers functionalized with several chromophores. Dendrimers are embedded in a polymer film and studied by single-molecule spectroscopy. They observe back-and-forth spectral jumps on a time scale of hundreds of seconds. The spectra arise either from isolated chromophores or from coupled ones. The emission spectra of the coupled chromophores are diverse, globally showing a redshift and modifications of the spectral shape. Likewise, Yoo
et al

Yoo et al. 2010
synthesized
PDI
molecules linked by xanthene groups. The linkers force the
PDI
molecules to stand face to face, increasing their probability of coupling together. They study the behavior of dimers and trimers. In solution, they observe a redshift of the emission spectrum compared to the monomer case, which they attributed to the coupling between
PDI
molecules in an excimer-like state. Then they perform time-resolved
PL
experiments as a function of time at the single molecule level. A lifetime fluctuation is attributed to a change in the conformation of the dimer and the trimer with time that leads to modifications of the intermolecular distance and relative orientation, and then to a modulation of the coupling. These fluctuations are observed on the time scale of a few tens of seconds. Therefore, we interpret the temporal modifications of the spectrum that we observe in the clusters as a consequence of back-and-forth jumps between coupled and decoupled states due to the movement of
GQD
s on the
\text{MAPbBr}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
substrate.

In the reference cited earlier
Yoo et al. 2010
;
Hofkens et al. 2000
, the coupled state is an excimer-like state. In situations where molecules are very close together, like in
PDI
derivatives
Yoo et al. 2010
, the point dipole approximation fails to describe the coupling. To understand the underlying mechanism, Spano and co-workers included intra- and inter-molecular vibronic coupling and charge-transfer processes
Hestand and Spano 2018
;
Bialas and Spano 2022
. When two molecules stand face to face (H-like configuration), the emission in the
ZPL
can be forbidden by symmetry. It leads to an emission spectrum in the form of a vibronic progression where the
ZPL
has been suppressed and where the main line corresponds to the 0-1 transition leading to an apparent red shift
Hestand and Spano 2018
. In our case, it is noticeable that the redshifted emission during the dancing occurs at the energy of the 0-1 vibrational replica. Therefore, we can hypothesize that the coupled state observed during the ”dancing” process is an excimer-like state due to the coupling of several
GQD
s in a cluster.

We now discuss the increase in intensity (”bright-red” regime) observed in the emission of some of the
GQD
clusters. The recorded intensity arises from the competition between several processes. First, even if their photostability on
\text{MAPbBr}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
is much better than on glass (see Supplementary Figure
), the
GQD
s can photobleach. In a given cluster, with a constant number of
GQD
s, this process decreases the
PL
intensity. However, the dynamical process described above implies that
GQD
s could be mobile on the
\text{MAPbBr}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
surface. Therefore, we can not exclude that some
GQD
s move into the excitation spot, leading to an increased
PL
intensity. Nevertheless, the spot size is diffraction-limited, which means that almost no other
GQD
s stand around the observation area. Therefore, a process where
GQD
s would enter the detection area, if existing, should be marginal. Another consequence of their mobility would be an alignment of some
GQD
s, for instance, along the polarization direction of the excitation laser. It could result in a partial organization of the
GQD
s in the cluster. We tried to test this possibility by performing polarization-dependent experiments. Indeed, the emission of single
GQD
is strongly linearly polarized along the
GQD
axis
Levy-Falk et al. 2023
. For a single
GQD
in a polystyrene matrix, the extinction ratio between perpendicular polarizations is of the order of ten (see Supplementary Figure
). For
GQD
clusters on
\text{MAPbBr}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
, a small degree of linear polarization (ratio of the order of two) has been measured (see Supplementary Figure
), showing a partial organization of
GQD
s in a given cluster. Nevertheless, we fail to relate it to a dynamical process under illumination. Significantly, results from time-resolved
PL
demonstrate that the coupled state has a shorter lifetime combined with a higher
PL
intensity. Together with the additional redshift of the emission, these results are compatible with the emergence of a partial collective emission from coupled excited states within a partially organized
GQD
cluster
Zhao et al. 2019
. Since this kind of collective state depends on the distance and relative orientation between emitters, the time evolution of the optical properties suggests a modification of the organization of
GQD
s and intermolecular distances in the clusters under illumination. However, the driving mechanism and special effect of the perovskite substrate remain unclear.

4
Conclusion

We investigated the photophysics of
\text{C}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{96}}}\textit{t}\text{Bu}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{8}}}
graphene quantum dots deposited on a perovskite substrate. Despite observing diffraction-limited
PL
spots, the samples show features readily attributed to the interaction between
GQD
s in aggregates. Under laser excitation, we observe the
GQD
s jumping from electronically coupled to uncoupled states. Indeed, the emission of the coupled state shows the characteristic features of an eximer-like state with a broader line at the energy of the 0-1 vibronic replica
Hestand and Spano 2018
;
Liu et al. 2022
. Furthermore, when the sample is kept under constant illumination, the system can reach a stable state with a slightly more redshifted emission correlated with an increase in the
PL
intensity and a shorter lifetime. We attribute this later effect to a reorganization of the
GQD
s at the surface of the perovskite, leading to an emitting collective excited state.

The driving force of these effects remains unclear. Several parameters will be varied to get more insights into the mechanisms at play. For instance, lowering the temperature to a few Kelvin will give valuable information about the dynamical coupling-decoupling process since the mobility of the
GQD
s on the surface will be modified. Likewise, lowering the number of
GQD
s in a cluster might help better understand and control the process. A detailed understanding of the organization of the
GQD
s on-surface would open the possibility of building arrays of coherently coupled emitters.
