Enhancing breast cancer diagnosis: non-invasive prediction of MKI-67 (Ki67) expression using ultrasound images
Authors: Hui Xie, Jianfang Zhang, Qing Li, Tao Tan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study explores the non-invasive prediction of MKI-67 (Ki67) expression status in breast cancer using preoperative ultrasound image heterogeneity. Data from 432 patients (training set) and 109 (test set) across two medical institutions were analyzed. Tumor regions were automatically outlined using the Swin-unet network, and habitat clustering within these regions was performed using the k-means method. Radiomics and deep learning features (ResNet-101) were extracted from both global tumor regions and habitat subregions. Laboratory data were integrated, followed by the Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) feature reduction and machine learning modeling to predict Ki67 expression status. Model performance was evaluated using accuracy (Acc), area under the curve (AUC) with 95% confidence intervals (CI), sensitivity (Sen), specificity (Spe), positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), calibration curves, confusion matrices, and decision curves. The DeLong test was used to compare the diagnostic performance of the composite model with individual models. The results showed that the combined model (Habitat + Global + Laboratory + Deep Learning) achieved the best predictive performance, with Acc, AUC, Sen, Spe, PPV, and NPV of 0.798, 0.838, 0.780, 0.809, 0.711, and 0.859, respectively, in the test set. Calibration curves and confusion matrices confirmed the model’s robustness, while decision curves demonstrated its clinical utility. The DeLong test confirmed the composite model’s significantly superior AUC compared to several individual models, though not all combined models showed significant differences. However, despite not showing significant advantages in comparisons with some combined models, the composite model, leveraging its unique strength of comprehensively integrating multi-dimensional features, has demonstrated stronger adaptability and stability in real-world clinical application scenarios, providing more reliable support for accurate prediction. In conclusion, preoperative ultrasound image heterogeneity, through the integration of habitat subregion, global tumor, laboratory, and deep learning features, provides valuable insights for predicting Ki67 expression status in breast cancer, enhancing routine preoperative ultrasonography and offering a potential non-invasive method for preoperative Ki67 prediction.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-025-15443-8.Keywords: Ultrasound image, Heterogeneity, Breast cancer, Ki67, Machine learning, Deep learning

Introduction

Breast cancer is one of the most common malignant tumors among women [1]. In 2020, approximately 685,000 people worldwide died from breast cancer [2]. In China, breast cancer accounts for about 10% of various malignant tumors, and the growth rate is increasing year by year, and the age of onset of patients is also gradually becoming younger [3]. The pathogenesis of breast cancer is complex, and the etiology is currently unknown. For breast cancer, which is a systemic disease, standardised and systematic diagnosis and treatment are related to the patients prognosis. At present, when developing treatment plans in the clinic, it is necessary to take into account the patient’s clinical manifestations and the expression of postoperative immunohistochemical (IHC) markers. Therefore, breast cancer immunomarkers have a great reference value for the standardised treatment of breast cancer after surgery [4]. Uncontrolled cell proliferation is the most direct cause of cancer development. In clinical practice, Ki67 has been widely used to assess tumour proliferation [5]. At the same time, Ki67 is also significant for the prognosis of tumours. When Ki67 expression is high, breast cancer patients have a higher chance of lymph node metastasis [6], so Ki67 is also closely related to the prognosis of breast cancer. In addition to being used as a prognostic indicator, the potential application value of Ki67 includes predicting the resistance of breast cancer patients to chemotherapy and endocrine therapy [7], assessing the residual risk of patients after receiving treatment [8], and serving as a dynamic monitoring indicator of the effectiveness of treatment for breast cancer patients [7]. It can be seen that Ki67 testing is widely used in breast cancer management.

The current routine clinical method for detecting Ki67 expression is IHC, which is an assay involving invasive or surgical samples. However, these IHC stains have some limitations in the detection of Ki67 expression levels. For example, sampling errors, subjectivity and some inoperable patients cannot be tested for Ki67 expression levels [9]. Therefore, there is a need to develop an accurate and non-invasive method for Ki67 status prediction in breast cancer patients.

Ultrasound, a non-invasive, radiation-free and inexpensive modality, has been widely used for breast screening. Some scholars have found that there is a correlation between the ultrasound manifestation of triple-negative breast cancer and the expression level of Ki67 [10]. This also provides ideas for our study. Habitat imaging is a technique that quantifies intratumoral heterogeneity by identifying subregions with distinct phenotypic characteristics [11]. It uses mathematical algorithms to segment the tumour and its surrounding region, yielding similarity sub-regions that reflect their similarity in genotype and phenotype and inferring the heterogeneity of the tumour [12]. This image processing technique is concerned with the formation of subregions with similarities within a tumour due to survival stress and their interrelationships. Tumour heterogeneity is in turn related to the biological behaviour of the tumour, the response to treatment and the clinical outcome [13]. Intratumoral heterogeneity—manifested as spatially distinct subregions with varying vascularity, cellularity, or hypoxia—has been biologically linked to Ki67 expression dynamics. For instance, perinecrotic or hypoxic niches often exhibit upregulated proliferation markers as an adaptive response [13]. Habitat imaging captures these microenvironmental variations, translating them into quantifiable imaging phenotypes that global features may overlook.

Therefore, this study aimed to construct a prediction model for Ki67 status using ultrasound image features of female breast cancer. In the process of model construction, we used deep learning technology to automatically segment the whole tumour region and cluster the habitat subregions. The model we developed enables non-invasive prediction of Ki67 expression status in breast cancer patients.

Data set

Our research adhered strictly to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. The Ethics Committee of Xiangnan University Medical Ethics Professional Committee granted its approval for this retrospective study (Approval Number: 2023YX11014). In light of the study’s retrospective nature, the requirement for patients to provide signed informed consent was exempted. This study retrospectively collected 600 cases of pathologically confirmed breast cancer from May 2018 to June 2024 (after exclusion of 59 cases, 541 cases were finally enrolled). All the enrolled cases were females with an age range of 23–84 years. Among them, 458 cases (after excluding 26 cases, final 432 cases were enrolled) from the affiliated hospital of Xiangnan University served as the training set (cohort-AH); 132 cases (after excluding 23 cases, final 109 cases were enrolled) from the affiliated rehabilitation hospital of Xiangnan University served as the test set (cohort-RH).

The inclusion and exclusion criteria for patients in this study were as follows. The inclusion criteria were (1) primary breast cancer confirmed by surgical resection or pathological biopsy; (2) patients did not receive antitumour therapy such as radiotherapy, chemotherapy, or other therapy before undergoing ultrasonography or prior to surgery; (3) available Ki67 expression results (assessed by IHC on surgical specimens); (4) preoperative ultrasound images with clear visualization of the lesion (no significant motion artifacts or obscuration by dense breast tissue). The exclusion criteria were: (1) incomplete preoperative ultrasound image examination data or poor image quality, lesions were not clearly displayed or poorly displayed, which affected the outlining of the region of interest (ROI); (2) lack of necessary clinicopathological data. According to the final pathological results, the included cases were divided into the group with high Ki67 expression level (Ki67 >14%) and the group with low Ki67 expression level (Ki67 ≤ 14%) [14]; (3) multiple primary breast cancers or metastatic lesions from other primary tumors.

Collect patient’s laboratory data from the Hospital Information System (HIS): Estrogen Receptor (ER), Progesterone Receptor (PR), Human Epidermal Growth Factor Receptor 2 (Her2), Prolactin (PRL), Estradiol (E2), Follicle-stimulating Hormone (FSH), Luteinizing Hormone (LH), Progesterone (PROG), Testosterone (TESTO) and Diameter (D), and age. Her2 staining intensity was 0, 1+, 2 + and 3+. Where 0 and 1 + labelled 0 for negative (-), 2 + labelled 1 for suspected positive (+/-) and 3 + labelled 2 for positive (+) [4].

Ultrasound image acquisition

Voluson E10 (General Electric, USA) colour ultrasound instruments were used in cohort-AH, while PHILIPS IU22 (Philips, Netherlands) colour ultrasound instruments were used in cohort-RH. The probe frequency was set to 8–14 MHz. During the examination, the patient is usually placed in the supine position, with both hands on either side of the head, so that both breasts and both axillae are fully exposed. If the breasts are large, the same breast may be examined on the side at the same time. Radial, transverse and longitudinal examination of the breast surface using a probe. It describes the background echoes of the breast, the presence or absence of ductal dilatation, the size and location of the mass, its morphology, margins, parallelism to the skin, internal echoes, posterior echoes, and the presence or absence of internal calcifications, and it also scans the axillary lymph nodes for enlargement. Colour Doppler flow imaging (CDFI) and spectral Doppler analysis (PW), were used to detect the blood flow signals inside and around the mass, and resistance measurements were performed.

The network is a deep learning model based on Swin Transformer [16] and U-Net [17] that can be used for breast ultrasound image segmentation. It combines the powerful feature extraction capability of Swin Transformer and the excellent segmentation performance of U-Net in order to achieve high-precision breast ultrasound image segmentation. In this segmentation task, we adjusted the model parameters with the initial learning rate set to 0.00001 and batch size set to 2. One representative ultrasound image per patient was systematically chosen based on high image quality (clear lesion visualization, no significant artifacts) and pathological confirmation, with some patients contributing multiple images to capture tumor heterogeneity. Habitat subregion clustering

In this study, we used an unsupervised k-means clustering algorithm to divide habitat subregions within ultrasound image regions of interest (ROI). The optimal number of clusters (k) was determined using the contour coefficient, which assesses clustering quality by balancing intra-cluster compactness and inter-cluster separation. To ensure consistency across cohorts, the optimal number of clusters (k = 3) was determined using the silhouette coefficient computed on the combined training and test datasets. The same set of input features (GLCM texture, intensity statistics, and spatial coordinates relative to tumor centroid) and Euclidean distance metric were applied uniformly to all patients in both cohorts. Clustering was performed on a concatenated feature vector derived for each pixel within the segmented tumor ROI, rather than on raw pixel intensities alone. This approach allows for the integration of multi-dimensional phenotypic information to better capture intratumoral heterogeneity. Specifically, for each pixel, we pre-calculated the following features using a local 5 × 5 pixel neighborhood window (to compute local statistics while preserving spatial resolution) via the Pyradiomics package:

Texture features: Gray-Level Co-occurrence Matrix (GLCM) metrics, including contrast, correlation, energy, and homogeneity, calculated in four directions (0°, 45°, 90°, 135°) and averaged.

Texture features: Gray-Level Co-occurrence Matrix (GLCM) metrics, including contrast, correlation, energy, and homogeneity, calculated in four directions (0°, 45°, 90°, 135°) and averaged.

Intensity-based features: Statistical measures such as mean intensity, median, standard deviation, and skewness within the local neighborhood.

Intensity-based features: Statistical measures such as mean intensity, median, standard deviation, and skewness within the local neighborhood.

Spatial features: Normalized coordinates (x, y) relative to the tumor centroid (computed as the center of mass of the ROI) and Euclidean distance from the tumor boundary (to incorporate positional context, e.g., peripheral vs. central regions).

Spatial features: Normalized coordinates (x, y) relative to the tumor centroid (computed as the center of mass of the ROI) and Euclidean distance from the tumor boundary (to incorporate positional context, e.g., peripheral vs. central regions).

These features were normalized using Z-score standardization (mean = 0, std = 1) across the ROI to ensure scale invariance. The resulting feature vector per pixel was 12-dimensional (4 GLCM + 4 intensity + 4 spatial, after averaging directions and including distance metrics). This vector was then fed into the k-means algorithm, which minimizes the within-cluster sum of squares using the Euclidean distance metric:

where and y are feature vectors of two pixels. The algorithm iteratively assigns pixels to the nearest cluster centroid and updates centroids until convergence (maximum 300 iterations, tolerance = 1e-4). To mitigate local optima, k-means was run 10 times with random initializations, selecting the result with the lowest inertia. This process groups pixels with similar multi-modal characteristics into habitat subregions, reflecting distinct microenvironmental niches (e.g., hypoxic vs. proliferative areas).

Feature extraction

In our study, we extracted ultrasound radiomics features, including first-order statistical features, morphological features, texture features, and higher-order features, from the entire tumor region and each habitat subregion of the ultrasound images by using Python v3.6 software and the Pyradiomics package (https://pyradiomics.readthedocs.io). To avoid confusion, radiomics features extracted from the entire tumor were termed ‘global features’. Features from each habitat subregion were termed ‘habitat features’ and labeled with the suffix ‘.clusteri’, where ‘i’ indicates the specific subregion.

To increase the dimension and breadth of the study, we also used the ResNet-101 [18] network to extract deep radiomics features for the entire tumor region. To increase the robustness of the features, ResNet-101 was pre-trained using the ImageNet dataset [19] before extracting the features, and then the global average pooling layer in the ResNet-101 network was used to extract the deep radiomics features.

Therefore, each ultrasound image in our study corresponds to four sets of feature sets: (a) global radiomics feature set (Global, N = 1561); (b) habitat subregion radiomics feature set (Habitat, N = 4683); (c) clinical laboratory feature set (Laboratory, N = 11); and (d) deep learning radiomics feature set (Deep learning, N = 20).

Feature screening and model construction

Given the unsupervised nature of K-means, its cluster labels (e.g., “cluster1”) lack biological alignment across patients. To tackle this, we took the following steps.

Firstly, to facilitate the identification and analysis of habitat sub - regions in images from different patients, we adopted a naming convention based on the sorting of average voxel values of each sub - region. For each image, the sub - region with the highest average voxel value is named cluster1, the second - highest cluster2, and so on. This enables a clear distinction between sub - regions within the region of interest. Using average voxel values for naming creates a more standardized reference across patients. For example, although cluster1 in Patient A and cluster1 in Patient B may differ biologically or radiologically, both represent the sub - region with the highest average voxel value in their respective images, offering some comparability in relative intensity. To further ensure the quality and consistency of our data for subsequent modeling, we first integrated all omics features by patient identity document to ensure cross-platform data consistency, followed by Z-score normalization (Eq. 6) of continuous variables to eliminate scale-related biases. Subsequently, a two-stage dimensionality reduction approach was employed: in the first stage, redundant features were removed using the Spearman correlation threshold method (r ≥ 0.9), retaining only the most representative feature from each highly correlated cluster; in the second stage, L1-regularized LASSO regression was applied, which incorporates an L1 penalty term into the loss function to progressively shrink coefficients of non-critical features to zero as the regularization parameter λ increases. Meanwhile, 10-fold cross-validation was used to systematically evaluate model errors across different λ values, with the optimal λ selected based on the minimum cross-validation error. This process identified some non-zero coefficient features as the core feature set with the highest predictive contribution and lowest redundancy for machine learning model construction. Additionally, individual risk scores were calculated using Eq. 7 to enable quantitative risk stratification. Secondly, and more crucially, during modeling, we didn’t rely on clustering labels. Instead, we independently extracted radiomics features from each sub - region (labeled as cluster1, cluster2, cluster3, etc.). Then, we employed the LASSO (Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator) algorithm to automatically select the most predictive features, regardless of their cluster labels. For instance, some top - predictive features might come from cluster2 in one patient and cluster3 in another. This way, our model focuses on learning discriminative habitat phenotypic features rather than semantic clustering labels. It ensures the model’s robustness across patients as it’s not constrained by potentially inconsistent and non - comparable clustering labels but captures truly relevant biological or radiological patterns for prediction, regardless of sub - region labels in different patients.

Mean value, SD Standard Deviation

To systematically evaluate the predictive value of different feature combinations, we constructed 13 distinct model categories based on feature modality: (a) Global radiomics only; (b) Habitat subregion radiomics only; (c) Clinical laboratory only; (d) Deep learning only; (e–m) various combinations thereof, culminating in the full composite model (Global + Habitat + Laboratory + Deep learning).

For each feature category, we trained and optimized 9 machine learning classifiers—Support Vector Machine (SVM), K-Nearest Neighbor (KNN), Random Forest (RF), Logistic Regression (LR), Naive Bayes (NB), Multilayer Perceptron (MLP), Gradient Boosting Decision Tree (GBDT), Adaptive Boosting (ADAB), and Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LGBM)—using the training set (cohort-AH). Hyperparameter tuning for each classifier was performed via 10-fold cross-validation on this training set to mitigate overfitting. The optimal hyperparameters for each classifier are detailed in Supplementary Table 2.

The best-performing classifier for each feature category was selected based on a comprehensive assessment of the cross-validation performance, with a particular focus on the Area Under the Curve (AUC), specificity (Spe), and negative predictive value (NPV)—metrics deemed critical for clinical decision-making in Ki67 prediction. This process resulted in 13 representative models (one per feature category). The final performance of these selected models was then rigorously evaluated on the held-out independent test set (cohort-RH).

Statistical analysis

For the clinical laboratory data of the patients, measures that conformed to normal distribution were expressed as mean ± standard deviation , nd measures that did not conform to normal distribution were expressed as median (upper and lower quartiles). Data were analysed by independent samples t-test, test or Mann–Whitney U test, depending on the type of data. Count data were expressed as frequencies or rates, and comparisons between two groups were made using the test.

Feature selection was performed using the Spearman threshold (P ≥ 0.9) and lasso method. Area Under Curve 95% [Confidence Interval] (AUC95% [CI]), sensitivity ( Sen ), specificity ( Spe ), accuracy ( ACC ), positive predictive value (PPV) and negative predictive value (NPV) were used to evaluate the effectiveness of the model. Confusion matrices were plotted for the test set to show the relationship between the predicted results and the actual labels. Calibration curves for the test set were plotted to assess the agreement between the predicted probabilities and the actual frequencies of the model in the validation group. The calibration curves were tested for goodness-of-fit of the model using the Hosmer-Lemeshow test. We also used decision curve analysis to estimate the value of the clinical application of the model (net benefit over a risk threshold). Finally, the DeLong test was used to compare the diagnostic performance of the composite model with that of the individual models.

All the above statistical analyses were done using the Python 3.9 sklearn package. All tests were two-tailed, with no special instructions, and statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Model construction and evaluation

A total of 117 models (13 feature combinations × 9 classifiers) were initially evaluated. For clarity, only the best-performing classifier per feature category—selected based on a comprehensive assessment of AUC, specificity, and NPV in the test set—is reported in Table 2 (The results of the training set are presented in Supplementary Table 3). The GBDT-based composite model (Habitat + Global + Laboratory + Deep Learning) was selected as optimal because it achieved the highest test AUC (0.838) among all evaluated models, while also demonstrating a superior balance of other clinically relevant metrics critical for Ki67 prediction: Acc = 0.798, Sen = 0.780, Spe = 0.809, PPV = 0.711, and NPV = 0.859. This selection prioritizes not only discriminative power (AUC) but also metrics that minimize clinical risks, such as high specificity to reduce false positives and overtreatment in low-Ki67 cases, and high NPV to confidently rule out high-Ki67 status without invasive confirmation.

Discussion

The results of this study show that the method based on Habitat + Global + Laboratory + Deep learning features can further explore the predictive information embedded in the preoperative routine ultrasound images of breast cancer patients. It showed good efficacy in predicting the Ki67 expression status of breast cancer patients, with AUC [95%CI] up to 0.870 (0.833–0.907) and 0.838 (0.786–0.930) in the training and test groups, respectively. Thus, ultrasound imaging enables non-invasive preoperative prediction of Ki67 expression, without additional radiation or economic burden, offering significant clinical value. The comprehensive performance metrics of the optimal model in the test set (Acc = 0.798 [0.713–0.880], AUC = 0.838 [0.786–0.932], Sen = 0.780 [0.689–0.875], Spe = 0.809 [0.691–0.925], PPV = 0.711 [0.612–0.811], NPV = 0.859 [0.773–0.955]) provide critical insights into its clinical applicability in Ki67 prediction. The overall accuracy of 79.8% indicates a reliable baseline for preoperative assessment, supporting the model’s potential as a supplementary tool in clinical decision-making, while the 20.2% error rate underscores the necessity of integrating its outputs with gold-standard IHC results, particularly in cases with complex tumor heterogeneity as captured by the study’s habitat analysis. The AUC of 0.838, exceeding the clinically meaningful threshold (> 0.8), confirms the model’s robust ability to distinguish between high and low Ki67 expression—an essential attribute for stratifying patients into risk groups, given Ki67’s established role in guiding treatment intensity (e.g., neoadjuvant chemotherapy for high expression vs. endocrine therapy for low expression). Sensitivity of 78.0% ensures that most patients with high Ki67 (a marker of aggressive tumor biology and increased metastasis risk) are correctly identified, minimizing the risk of undertreatment that could lead to disease progression. Conversely, the 22.0% false-negative rate highlights the need to correlate model outputs with clinical factors such as tumor diameter (Table 1, differs significantly between Ki67 groups) to avoid missing high-risk cases. Specificity of 0.809 protects 80.9% of low-Ki67 patients from unnecessary aggressive interventions, reducing exposure to treatment-related toxicities, while the 19.1% false-positive rate emphasizes the importance of confirmatory testing for positive predictions. The positive predictive value (71.1%) indicates that while a “high Ki67” prediction warrants clinical attention, it should be validated by IHC to prevent overtreatment, aligning with the study’s integration of laboratory data (ER, PR, Her2) to enhance decision confidence. In contrast, the high negative predictive value (85.9%) strengthens the model’s utility in reassuring clinicians of low Ki67 status, reducing the need for invasive re-evaluation in vulnerable patients (e.g., elderly or comorbid individuals) and reinforcing its non-invasive advantage. Collectively, these metrics demonstrate the model’s ability to balance the risks of false negatives and false positives, aligning with the goal of personalized breast cancer management by enhancing preoperative risk stratification while maintaining reliance on definitive IHC testing.

Ki67 is closely related to medical imaging as a key marker associated with cell proliferation and tumour heterogeneity. There have been previous studies exploring Ki67. Ma et al. retrospectively studied 377 Chinese female breast cancer patients with three different classification methods and showed that quantitative radiomics features extracted from dynamic contrast-enhanced magnetic resonance imaging (DCE-MRI) of breast tumours were associated with Ki67 expression in breast cancer [20]. Juan et al. found that radiomics features differed significantly between patients with high and low Ki67-expressing tumours and were significant predictors of patients’ Ki67 proliferative status on DCE-MRI images of breast cancer [21]. Zhang et al. established a categorical prediction of the status of Ki67 by quantitative analysis of diffusion-weighted images (DWI) of preoperative breast cancer patients with an AUC of (0.75 ± 0.08) [22]. Li et al. had a ROC of 78.1% for the study of Ki67 status by radiomics of preoperative CT [23]. The above studies, however, suffer from poor accuracy, basically use CT and MRI imaging histology, and are highly subjective. Notably, our cohort design addresses potential limitations of previous studies in sample representativeness. With 541 cases across two centers, including diverse tumor subtypes (Luminal A/B, Her2-positive, triple-negative) and lesion characteristics (calcified vs. non-calcified), the sample size provides sufficient power for subgroup analyses (e.g., dense breasts, which account for 43.6% of the cohort). This contrasts with smaller single-center MRI/CT studies, which often lack diversity in tumor subtypes or patient demographics, limiting generalizability. Our strict inclusion of pathologically confirmed malignancies (no reliance on imaging follow-up for diagnosis) also strengthens the validity of Ki67 correlation analyses compared to studies with mixed benign/malignant cohorts.

Our ultrasound-based method offers distinct advantages over other non-invasive approaches like CT and MRI. CT involves ionizing radiation, posing cumulative risks for patients needing repeated imaging, which ultrasound avoids. Its soft-tissue resolution is also inferior, limiting the capture of tumor heterogeneity critical for habitat analysis. MRI, while radiation-free, relies on contrast agents (risking allergies or renal issues) and is costly, with limited accessibility in resource-limited settings—unlike ultrasound, which is portable and affordable. In performance, our combined model (test AUC = 0.838) outperforms CT-based studies (e.g., LI et al.’s 78.1% ROC [23]) and most MRI studies (e.g., Zhang et al.’s DWI AUC = 0.75 ± 0.08 [22]). This stems from our integration of multi-dimensional features (habitat, global, deep learning, clinical data) that better capture Ki67-related complexity, whereas CT/MRI often rely on single-modality metrics. Additionally, our automatic segmentation (Dice = 85.2%) reduces subjectivity from manual ROI delineation—a common issue in CT/MRI studies—enhancing reliability. Thus, our approach balances superior accuracy with safety, accessibility, and standardization, making it more clinically feasible for routine use.

We used ultrasound images of breast cancer for this study. We not only performed automatic segmentation of tumours from ultrasound images, but also innovatively used a machine learning approach combining local habitat radiomics with global, deep learning radiomics and clinical laboratory features to construct a prediction model for Ki67 status. The AUC in the test set reached 0.838, and the confusion matrix and calibration curves demonstrated the model’s robustness. The clinical decision curve shows that the clinic can benefit from the model. In this study, the automatic segmentation of tumour regions on 2D ultrasound images using Swin-Unet network overcomes human subjectivity to a large extent. And through the adjustment of parameters, the study achieved an average dice of 85.2% and an average HD95 of 3.555 mm for the automatic segmentation of tumours on ultrasound images, which is also satisfactory compared to similar studies. This also indirectly enhances the robustness of our study results.

Previous studies reported a Dice coefficient of only 74.6% for tumor segmentation in ultrasound images using U-Net [24]. PAN proposed an automatic tumour segmentation method for ultrasound images by incorporating bidirectional long short-term memory (BLSTM) and spatial channel attention (SC-attention) modules into a fully convolutional network (FCN) with a dice of 81.78% [25]. You et al. used a generative adversarial network (cGAN) approach for data enhancement of ultrasound images and segmentation of tumours in breast ultrasound images can result in a Dice of 88.56% [26]. In Zhang et al.‘s study, using of U-Net combined with DenseNet as a backbone to segment tumours in ultrasound images achieved a Dice of 89.8% [27]. Therefore, there is room for improvement in our segmentation task. However, in this study our dice is 85.3% and HD95 is 3.555 mm, which achieves a moderately level for this type of segmentation task. This is able to meet the requirements for the final classification task in this study.

Unlike traditional radiomics assessments, our study incorporated four dimensions of omics features. In particular, the radiomics features of localised habitat subregions were incorporated. Solid tumours are complex ecosystems with interacting components that can produce unique pathophysiological states [28]. It has been found that intra-tumour heterogeneity can be demonstrated by imaging the whole tumour and peri-tumour parenchyma [29]. Radiomics of local habitat subregions subdivides the whole tumour more finely into different subregions, an approach that digs deeper into the information about the internal heterogeneity of the tumour embedded in the images and translates it into quantitative features [30]. Habitat analysis methods can establish clear and predictable links between radiologically observed macroscopic tumour features and the molecular, cellular and microenvironment of microscopic cancer cell populations [31]. This is more advantageous for ki67 assessment. Possible reasons for speculation at the level of current knowledge are that tumours are extremely complex and dynamically changing malignancies and that evolution is influenced by a combination of genetics and the tumour microenvironment [32]. The tumour microenvironment encompasses a complex and diverse range of cellular components such as tumour cells, stromal cells and tumour-associated macrophages, and so on [33]. During the evolutionary process of breast cancer, extensive microvessels need to be formed to meet the oxygen and nutrient requirements for cancer cell proliferation, and perivascular cancer cells secrete large amounts of pro-angiogenic growth factors to generate the required microenvironment [34]. A dysfunctional vascular system reduces blood flow leading to areas of hypoxia within the tumour and ultimately to pseudofenestrated necrosis after vascular collapse [35]. A hypoxic microenvironment further activates hypoxia-inducible factors to proliferate tumour cells [36]. Obviously, there is a clear correlation between the heterogeneous expression of the tumour microenvironment and tumour change. Some of these changes are demonstrated on biomedical images. It can be seen that the analysis of the different habitats of tumour cells through medical images can deepen our understanding of the ending events of this study. Therefore, our study is state-of-the-art, not only considering the whole tumour ecosystem but also looking at each habitat subregion in more detail. Complemented by deep learning of imaging histological features and clinical laboratory features, it can make our model more accurate. It has been reported that for prognostic studies of neoadjuvant chemotherapy in breast cancer patients, the inclusion of features of habitat subregions significantly improves the performance of predictive models [37]. In this study, six out of the final 16 selected core features were derived from habitat subregion radiomics, six from tumour global radiomics, and three from deep learning radiomics features. This finding indicates that the ultrasound-based radiomics features effectively captured essential tumour characteristics, supporting the validity of the omics approach applied in this study. We also found that, when the four feature dimensions were modelled separately, the local habitat subregion features alone achieved a higher ROC value (0.709 [95% CI: 0.605–0.813]) than the other three feature dimensions. Importantly, six of the final 16 core features screened were from habitat subregion radiomics features, underscoring their non-redundant contribution. Moreover, prior studies have demonstrated that habitat features significantly improve predictive performance for neoadjuvant chemotherapy response in breast cancer [37], validating their clinical relevance. Thus, despite a lower standalone AUC (0.709), habitat features provide complementary biological insights that are essential for a holistic assessment of tumor proliferation status. Therefore, the local habitat subregion feature better reflects the status of Ki67 in breast tumour patients. Deep learning radiomics is a favourable complement to local habitat subregion and global radiomics. Many studies have found that most deep learning features have a weak linear relationship with radiomics features, suggesting a potential complementary relationship between these two feature sets [38, 39]. Therefore, in this study, the inclusion of deep image histological features is necessary to improve the model performance.

In this study we tried 9 classifiers for the classification task and found that GBDT worked best. GBDT is a very commonly used and powerful classifier in machine learning. It can flexibly handle various types of data, including continuous and discrete values [40]. The accuracy of prediction can also be relatively high with relatively less tuning time. Compared to other machine learning algorithms, GBDT produces good results in biological data mining. Hasan et al. identified Cyclin proteins by GBDT, with an accuracy of 93.06% and an AUC value of 97.1% [41]. In a classification study of urinary stones, He et al. used four classifiers for modelling (LR, RF, ADAB and GBDT), with the GBDT scoring highest [42]. In this study, the Acc, AUC [95%CI], Sen, Spe, PPV and NPV of the prediction model built by GBDT in the test group in the classification study of Ki67 status were 0.798, 0.838 (95% CI:0.786–0.930), 0.780, 0.809, 0.711 and 0.859, respectively. Our study is another successful application of GBDT in biology.

The DeLong test indicated statistically significant differences between the composite model and other individual models, but non-significant differences with some (e.g., Habitat + Laboratory, P = 0.057), so overstating superiority should be avoided. This closeness is biologically plausible, as Habitat + Laboratory shares core features (tumor heterogeneity and clinical markers) linked to Ki67, overlapping in discriminative signals. Notably, the combined model has practical advantages: in the test set, it shows higher specificity (0.809 vs. 0.667) and NPV (0.859 vs. 0.525) than Habitat + Laboratory, reducing false positives (19.1% vs. 33.3%) and overtreatment risks, with better cross-cohort stability. Thus, while not universally statistically superior, its balanced performance and comprehensive integration make it more clinically reliable.

This study has some limitations. First, the study is a retrospective trial and there may be selection bias. Second, the accuracy of the automated segmentation could be improved again. Thirdly, no genomics data were incorporated. Some of these limit the further improvement of the prediction model.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we developed a non-invasive ultrasound-based model that integrates intratumoral habitat heterogeneity, global radiomics, deep learning, and clinical laboratory data to predict Ki67 expression in breast cancer with high accuracy (AUC = 0.838), specificity (80.9%), and negative predictive value (85.9%). This approach offers a practical, radiation-free alternative to immunohistochemistry—particularly valuable for inoperable or biopsy-ineligible patients and in resource-limited settings. By linking imaging phenotypes to tumor proliferative activity through habitat analysis, our model provides a radiogenomic proxy for Ki67 that supports personalized treatment decisions while mitigating sampling bias inherent in tissue-based assays. Future work should validate this framework in prospective multicenter trials, explore integration with genomic data, and extend habitat analysis to 3D or contrast-enhanced ultrasound to further enhance clinical utility and enable real-time decision support.
