Baseline ALBI Grade as an Independent Prognostic Factor of Survival in High-Risk Real-World HCC Under Atezolizumab–Bevacizumab
Authors: Filiz Araz, Fatih Köse, Timuçin Çil, Berna Bozkurt Duman, Burcu Arslan Benli, Elif Karadeli, Okan Dilek
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground/AimAtezolizumab plus bevacizumab is the current first-line standard for unresectable hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). However, real-world populations often differ substantially from clinical trials particularly in terms of hepatic reserve and tumor burden. We aimed to evaluate survival outcomes and hepatic functional changes in a real-world cohort, specifically focusing on the prognostic role of baseline ALBI grade and portal vein tumor thrombosis (PVTT).MethodsThis multicenter retrospective cohort study analyzed 43 patients with BCLC-B/C unresectable HCC treated between January 2022 and June 2025. Tumor response was assessed according to mRECIST. Overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS) were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method. Cox regression analyses were performed to identify independent prognostic factors.ResultsThe cohort was characterized by advanced disease (72.1% BCLC-C stage, 74.5% ALBI grade ≥2, and 41.9% PVTT). Median OS and PFS were 9.5 months (95% CI, 6.5–12.4) and 6 months (95% CI, 3.8–8.2), respectively. Patients with ALBI grade 1 had significantly longer OS compared to those with ALBI ≥2 (21 vs 8 months; p=0.018). In multivariable Cox regression analysis, higher baseline ALBI grade was independently associated with shorter OS (HR 3.1, 95% CI 1.1–8.3; p=0.027) and PFS (HR 2.8, 95% CI 1.2–6.9; p=0.021). Hepatic decompensation occurred in 41.9% of patients and was associated with deterioration in liver function scores.ConclusionIn this high-risk real-world cohort of patients with HCC treated with atezolizumab plus bevacizumab, baseline ALBI grade was independently associated with poorer survival outcomes, suggesting that hepatic functional reserve may be important for prognostic stratification. Larger prospective studies are needed to clarify the relative prognostic contributions of ALBI grade and tumor-related factors such as PVTT.Keywords: hepatocellular carcinoma, atezolizumab, bevacizumab, ALBI

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is the sixth most common malignancy worldwide and the third leading cause of cancer-related mortality.1 Curative treatment options, including surgical resection, liver transplantation, and local ablation, are applicable only to a limited subset of patients. Individuals with unresectable HCC generally have poor prognosis and short overall survival (OS).2

Systemic therapy is the standard of care for patients with unresectable lesions in the intermediate or advanced stages of the Barcelona Clinic Liver Cancer (BCLC) classification (B–C), who are either ineligible for locoregional therapies or have experienced disease progression despite such interventions.3 Currently approved systemic therapies for HCC are mainly composed of immune checkpoint inhibitors plus bevacizumab or dual immune checkpoint inhibitor (IO) therapies.4

Hepatocellular carcinoma commonly develops in the setting of chronic liver disease or cirrhosis, where it remains a major cause of mortality.5 Patient survival depends largely on liver function and tumor burden. The Child–Pugh- Turcotte (CPT) classification is widely used to evaluate hepatic function and prognosis in patients with cirrhosis and portal hypertension. However, 15–20% of HCC cases in Western populations occur in non-cirrhotic livers.6,7 The ALBI (Albumin-Bilirubin) grade is a validated, objective liver-function score that reliably predicts survival in HCC, including patients receiving tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) and immune checkpoint inhibitors.8–10 Portal vein tumor thrombosis (PVTT) is common in HCC and one of the strongest predictors of poor survival. Large cohorts confirm that more extensive PVTT generally means worse overall survival (OS).11 The extent of PVTT further correlates with overall survival.12

Previous real-world studies have already demonstrated that baseline ALBI grade is associated with survival in patients with unresectable HCC receiving atezolizumab plus bevacizumab.13–15 However, evidence remains limited in high-risk populations characterized by impaired hepatic reserve and a high prevalence of advanced PVTT. We hypothesized that poorer baseline hepatic functional reserve, reflected by a higher ALBI grade, would be associated with shorter overall survival in this population. Accordingly, we evaluated survival outcomes and changes in hepatic function and explored the prognostic associations of baseline ALBI grade and PVTT in a multicenter real-world cohort.

Material and Method

Study Population

This retrospective multicenter cohort study was conducted in accordance with the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) guidelines. This study included all cirrhotic and non-cirrhotic patients with advanced (BCLC-C) or intermediate (BCLC-B) stage HCC, ineligible for surgical or locoregional therapies, who initiated therapy with atezolizumab plus bevacizumab at Başkent University Adana Training and Research Center and Adana City Hospital. The study included all consecutive patients who received atezolizumab plus bevacizumab as part of routine clinical care between January 2022 and June 2025. Eligible patients were those presented with histologically or clinically confirmed diagnosis of HCC according to the international guidelines and were systemic treatment naive. Patients with missing or incomplete data were excluded from the population of final study. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of Başkent University (KA25/406; December 9, 2025). The requirement for written informed consent was waived by the Ethics Committee because of the retrospective design and the use of de-identified clinical data.

Diagnosis of HCC and Radiological Assessment

The diagnosis of HCC was established by radiologic imaging criteria or confirmed by histopathologic examination, as indicated in guidelines.3,16 All patients had dynamic liver magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) before treatment. Radiologic images were re-evaluated by a radiologist for number, size, extrahepatic or lymph node metastasis and vascular invasion of the tumor, and status of cirrhosis. Portal vein thrombosis was graded.17 The therapeutic response was evaluated by MRI at every 8–12 weeks thereafter and classified as complete response (CR), partial response (PR), stable disease (SD), or progressive disease (PD). Tumor response was assessed according to the modified Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors for hepatocellular carcinoma (mRECIST).18

Clinical and Laboratory Assessment

Baseline demographic findings, radiologic characteristics and laboratory findings were collected from the hospital database. Patients underwent laboratory tests every 2–4 weeks on scheduled follow-up visits during treatment. For all patients, end-of-treatment laboratory values were defined as the last available laboratory measurements obtained at or immediately before treatment discontinuation, including discontinuation due to disease progression, hepatic decompensation, adverse events, or death. Paired baseline and end-of-treatment measurements were available for all 43 patients. Baseline and end of treatment CPT and MELD scores were calculated for cirrhotic patients to evaluate functional status of liver. ALBI scores and ALBI grading were utilized to assess the liver functional reserve for all patients at baseline and at the end of treatment. All patients underwent upper gastrointestinal endoscopy to assess esophageal and gastric varices. High-risk varices were treated by band ligation before initiation of treatment as recommended. Follow up clinical records and laboratory measures were closely checked for the presence of hepatic decompensation which was defined as the occurrence of new ascites, variceal bleeding, hepatic encephalopathy, or jaundice.19

Treatment Regimes and Outcomes Measures

The combination therapy of atezolizumab plus bevacizumab was performed according to the pharmaceutical recommendations. 1200 mg atezolizumab plus bevacizumab (15 mg/kg) were administered intravenously every 3 weeks. Treatment regimen was continued until disease progression, unacceptable toxicity, death or patient discontinuation on their own discretion. Treatment duration and number of treatment cycles were recorded.

Overall survival, PFS and time to progression (TTP; defined as the time from initiation of treatment to the first occurrence of disease progression) were assessed for study patients. According to RECIST version 1.1 criteria, objective response rate (ORR) defined as a complete or partial response and disease control rate (DCR) defined as combination of objective response and stable disease were investigated. Changes in liver functional reserve and treatment-related or severe adverse events were also investigated.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics software (version 20.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Categorical variables were presented as numbers and percentages, whereas continuous variables were summarized as mean ± standard deviation or median (IQR), as appropriate. The normality of distribution for continuous variables was confirmed with the Shapiro–Wilk test. Comparisons of dependent (paired) continuous variables measured before and after treatment were performed using the paired samples t-test or the Wilcoxon signed-rank test, depending on the fulfillment of normality assumptions. Overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS) were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method, and differences between survival curves were assessed with the Log rank test. OS was defined as the time from initiation of treatment to death from any cause or last clinical follow-up. Clinical progression was defined as a composite outcome of death or radiological evidence of disease progression. PFS was defined as the time from treatment initiation to radiological progression, death, or last clinical follow-up. Cox proportional hazards regression analyses were performed to identify factors associated with OS and PFS. Clinically meaningful covariates with established prognostic relevance in HCC were predefined based on prior evidence and incorporated into the Cox regression models. Separate univariate Cox analyses were conducted for each variable, and variables with a p value < 0.25 were subsequently included in the multivariate Cox regression models. Hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported. A two-sided p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Study Population

A total of 43 patients were included. Mean age of diagnosis was 62.5±10.3 years. Patients were males in 97.7% of the cases. The most frequent underlying etiology was chronic hepatitis B which is present in 23 (53.5%) patients. Other defined risk factors for HCC included metabolic-associated steatohepatitis (MASH) in 4 (9.3%), HCV in 2 (4.7%) and alcohol in 2 (4.7%) patients. For 12 (27.9%) patients, no underling etiology was defined.

Median number of HCC lesions was 3.0 (1.0–6.0) with median largest and total diameter of 70.0 (38.0–109.0) and 108.0 (67.3–191.8) mm, respectively. Among 18 (41.9%) cases with PVTT, grade 2 and grade 3 PVTT were each present in 8 (44.4%) patients. Extrahepatic metastasis was present in 51.2% of the patients. Thirty-one (72.1%) patients were in BCLC stage C. Mean ALBI score was −2.2±0.5. Twelve (27.9) patients had grade 1 ALBI score, while 29 (67.4%) patients had ALBI grade 2 score. HCC was diagnosed with cirrhotic liver in 24 (55.8%) patients. Varices were present on diagnosis in 15 (34.9%) of patients. Child A cirrhosis was present in 18 (75%) patients, whereas 6 (25%) patients had Child B cirrhosis. Prior treatment included surgical resection (16.3%) and loco-ablative treatments (41.9%). Change in Hepatic Function

Paired baseline and end-of-treatment liver function measurements were available for all 43 patients. Mean ALBI score changed significantly from −2.2±0.5 at baseline to −1.6±0.7 at the end of treatment assessment (p<0.001). Among patients with cirrhosis, baseline mean CPT score of 6.2±1.0 differed significantly to 8.3±1.8 at the end-of-treatment assessment (p<0.001), whereas median baseline MELD score of 10.0 (8.0–12.8) increased significantly to 14.0 (10.3–19.8) (p<0.001) at the end of treatment. Albumin and bilirubin levels changed significantly between baseline and the end-of-treatment assessment (p<0.001 and p=0.002, respectively). Although median AFP level was decreased numerically, this change did not reach the statistical significance (p=0.396). Mean sodium and median AST levels changed significantly, whereas other laboratory measures did not show any significant change with the treatment. Efficacy

Patients were followed up for mean 21.9 ± 11.7 months. The median duration of treatment was 5.0 months (3.0–8.0). Median number of treatment cycles was 7.0 (4.0–12.0). At the time of the final follow-up, 19 patients (44.2%) exhibited disease progression, while 10 patients (23.3%) maintained stable disease. Complete response was achieved in 2 patients (4.6%), and partial response in 7 patients (16.3%) (Table 3). Objective response rate (ORR) was thus 20.9%, whereas disease control rate (DCR) was 44.2%. In 5 patients (11.6%), treatment response could not be evaluated due to death before the scheduled assessment date.

At the last follow up, 16 (37.2%) patients were still alive. Clinical progression with the composite end point of death or radiological progression was seen in 36 (83.7%) patients. Median time to progression was 5.5±3.0 months. Decompensation was seen in 18 (41.9%) patients within median 2.0 (1.4–6.5) months. Main component of decompensation was appearance of ascites, seen in 17 (39.5%) patients on median 2.0 (1.8–8.0) months. Variceal bleeding occurred in 4 (9.3%) patients. At the last follow up, 9 (20.9%) patients were on treatment. Discontinuation of the treatment was due to disease progression in 15 (34.9%), death in 10 (23.3%) and decompensation in 5 (11.6%) patients. Severe adverse events and poor performance status led to treatment withdrawal each in 1 (2.3%) patient. Two (4.6%) patients terminated the treatment with their own decision (Table 3).

Median OS was 21 months in ALBI grade 1 and 8 months in ALBI gr ≥2 (p=0.018) (Figure 1), demonstrating a clinically substantial survival gradient driven by baseline hepatic reserve. Median PFS for ALBI subgroups were comparable (p=0.141). Median OS and PFS did not differ significantly according to PVTT status, cirrhosis, hepatic decompensation, or ECOG performance status (0 vs ≥1) (p>0.05 for all) (Table 4).

Safety

A total of 18 serious adverse events occurred in 14 (32.6%) patients including proteinuria (n:3), immune-related colitis (n:2), hypophysitis/adrenal insufficiency (n:2), heart failure (n:2) and hypertension (2), with pneumonitis, thrombocytopenia, myocardial infarction, grade 2 skin rash, fatigue, gastric perforation and gastrointestinal bleeding each encountered once. In one (2.3%) patient, severe adverse event resulted in drug discontinuation. Immune-related pneumonitis concluded with drug withdrawal in an 84-year-old male patient which survived 7 months thereafter.

Discussion

In this multicenter real-world cohort, we evaluated the outcomes of atezolizumab plus bevacizumab in patients with unresectable HCC characterized by advanced disease and impaired hepatic reserve. The observed median OS and PFS were comparable to previously reported real-world outcomes, suggesting that this combination may provide clinically meaningful benefit in selected high-risk patients. No statistically significant differences in OS were observed according to age, PVTT, ECOG performance status, cirrhosis, or hepatic decompensation. Baseline ALBI grade was independently associated with both OS and PFS in the multivariable analysis, supporting the importance of hepatic functional reserve in prognostic stratification.

The IMbrave150 trial established atezolizumab plus bevacizumab as the first-line treatment, demonstrating significant improvements in both OS and PFS compared with sorafenib (median OS, 19.2 vs 13.2 months; median PFS, 6.9 vs 4.3 months).20 In our real-world cohort, the median OS and PFS were 9.5 and 6.0 months, respectively. These survival outcomes were broadly consistent with those reported in multicenter real-world studies from Europe and Asia.21,22 Notably, unlike IMbrave150—which excluded patients with Child–Pugh B cirrhosis—approximately one-quarter of our cohort comprised such patients. These findings support the reproducibility of clinical benefit with atezolizumab plus bevacizumab in real-world populations.

Although the prognostic relevance of ALBI grade has been previously established, our findings provide additional real-world evidence from a clinically challenging population enriched for impaired hepatic reserve, advanced-stage disease, and PVTT. In this cohort, baseline ALBI grade was independently associated with poorer OS and PFS in the multivariable Cox regression analysis, supporting the clinical relevance of objective hepatic reserve assessment in patients receiving atezolizumab plus bevacizumab. Although median PFS did not differ significantly between ALBI subgroups in the Kaplan–Meier analysis, baseline ALBI grade emerged as an independent predictor of PFS in the multivariable Cox model. This difference likely reflects adjustment for other prognostic variables included in the multivariable analysis. Post-hoc analyses from the IMbrave150 trial have shown that baseline ALBI score is a robust prognostic biomarker for both OS and PFS and may help to identify patients most likely to benefit from first-line atezolizumab plus bevacizumab in unresectable HCC.13 This observation is consistent with multiple real-world studies reporting significant association of low ALBI grade with better survival and reinforcing the importance of objective liver function assessment in clinical decision-making.14,15 Therefore, our findings should be regarded as confirmatory, while providing additional evidence from a high-risk population characterized by impaired hepatic reserve and a high prevalence of advanced PVTT.

In a multicenter cohort including 26.7% of patients with Child–Pugh B cirrhosis, ORR and DCR were reported as 29.5% and 62.7%, respectively.14 Similarly, large real-world studies have demonstrated ORRs of 25–36% and DCRs of 73–76%, with no significant differences across Child–Pugh classes, although survival remained superior in Child–Pugh A patients.22,23 In contrast, a recent meta-analysis indicated that patients with Child–Pugh B cirrhosis had lower response rates and poorer survival outcomes compared to those with Child–Pugh A disease.24 In our cohort, ORR was consistent with prior real-world data, whereas DCR (44.2%) was notably lower than that reported in clinical trials and large observational studies. This discrepancy is likely attributable to the unfavorable baseline characteristics of our population, including a high prevalence of impaired liver function (ALBI ≥2), Child–Pugh B cirrhosis, advanced-stage disease, and extensive vascular invasion. This reflects a clinically challenging population frequently underrepresented in randomized studies. In addition, early hepatic decompensation occurred in a substantial proportion of patients, leading to treatment discontinuation or death prior to formal radiologic assessment in some cases. Collectively, these factors may have contributed to reduced disease stabilization and a potential underestimation of DCR in this high-risk real-world cohort.

A clinically notable finding was the high rate of hepatic decompensation, which occurred in 41.9% of patients, frequently early during the treatment course. The ALBI, CPT, and MELD scores deteriorated between baseline and the end-of-treatment assessment. Although paired measurements were available for all patients, end-of-treatment assessments were performed at different time points and often coincided with disease progression, hepatic decompensation, or treatment discontinuation. However, given the retrospective design, hepatic deterioration cannot be attributed solely to treatment and may also reflect underlying liver disease and tumor progression. Early hepatic decompensation may represent a competing risk that limits the ability to derive sustained benefit from systemic therapy. The observed deterioration in ALBI, CPT, and MELD scores underscores the vulnerability of this patient population and highlights the need for close monitoring and early intervention. A German real-world study reported mild increases in MELD score and bilirubin levels within the first 6 months of treatment.23 In patients with HCC, liver function is usually compromised due to the underlying liver fibrosis or cirrhosis. But a high tumor burden may also impair liver reserve. For patients with high tumor burden, response to treatment may be associated with improvement or stabilization of liver function.24

In our cohort, PVTT status was not associated with survival outcomes, despite its well-established role as an adverse prognostic factor in HCC. Approximately 42% of patients had PVTT, yet OS and PFS were comparable between PVTT subgroups. These findings are consistent with emerging evidence suggesting that patients with macrovascular invasion may still derive clinical benefit from atezolizumab plus bevacizumab.20 Exploratory analyses from IMbrave150 further indicate that, although absolute outcomes may be inferior in patients with extensive PVTT (eg, Vp4), the relative treatment benefit remains maintained.25 In contrast to prior real-world studies reporting PVTT as an independent predictor of survival,15 in our cohort, no statistically significant association was observed between PVTT status and OS or PFS. However, this finding should not be interpreted as evidence that PVTT lacks prognostic significance. Given the limited cohort size and the inclusion of limited number of patients with PVTT, the analysis may have been insufficiently powered to detect a clinically relevant difference. Therefore, the null finding may reflect a Type II error, and the prognostic role of PVTT under immunotherapy-based combinations requires further evaluation in larger cohorts.

The safety profile observed in our cohort was consistent with prior real-world data, with predominantly manageable adverse events. Treatment-related adverse events occurred in 32.6% of patients, while hepatic decompensation was observed in 41.9%, most commonly due to new-onset ascites. These findings are in line with previously reported real-world rate of hepatic decompensation.23 Gastrointestinal bleeding occurred in 9.3% of patients, comparable to prior real-world cohorts (9–12%) and IMbrave150.14,23,26 This finding underscore the importance of proactive variceal screening and management in reducing bleeding risk during atezolizumab–bevacizumab therapy.

Our findings support atezolizumab plus bevacizumab as an effective first-line option for advanced HCC in real-world settings, with survival outcomes comparable to those reported in international cohorts. Baseline ALBI grade was significantly associated with overall survival, reinforcing the clinical relevance of hepatic functional reserve in prognostic assessment. The inclusion of a substantial proportion of patients with impaired liver function and advanced PVTT provides additional real-world evidence from a high-risk population that is often underrepresented in clinical trials. Collectively, these findings support incorporating objective assessment of hepatic reserve alongside tumor-related characteristics when considering immunotherapy-based treatment; however, larger prospective studies are needed to clarify their relative prognostic contributions.

This study has several limitations. First, its retrospective design and small cohort size (n=43), with only 27 deaths, limited statistical power and the number of covariates that could be reliably included in the multivariable Cox regression model. The resulting estimates may therefore be unstable and susceptible to overfitting, particularly for PVTT, may reflect a Type II error rather than the absence of a prognostic effect. Furthermore, because multiple subgroup comparisons were performed without adjustment for multiplicity, the possibility of Type I error cannot be excluded, and these findings should be interpreted with appropriate caution. In addition, the lack of centralized radiologic review may have introduced variability in response assessment. These findings should therefore be regarded as exploratory and require validation in larger prospective cohorts. Nevertheless, the multicenter design and inclusion of a high-risk real-world population provide clinically relevant observations.

In conclusion, median survival outcomes with atezolizumab plus bevacizumab in this real-world cohort were broadly consistent with those reported in previous studies. Baseline ALBI grade was independently associated with overall survival and progression-free survival in this high-risk real-world cohort, suggesting that hepatic functional reserve may play a key role in prognostic stratification. Given the small sample size, retrospective design, and evaluation of a single treatment combination, these findings should be considered hypothesis-generating and require validation in larger prospective cohorts.
