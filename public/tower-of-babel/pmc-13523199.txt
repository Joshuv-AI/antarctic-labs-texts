Lactate-activated astrocytes promote NSCLC brain metastasis through extracellular vesicle-mediated miR-8085/TRIM67/ELK1 signaling axis
Authors: Haiqin Peng, Guilong Tanzhu, Wen Shi, Gang Xiao, Qian Zeng, Liu Chen, Xin Wan, Di Jing, Haibin Deng, Thomas Michael Marti, Jun Fu, Rongrong Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Brain metastasis represents an advanced complication in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), characterized by therapeutic resistance and dismal survival outcomes. Although astrocytes are known to influence tumor progression within the brain microenvironment, their prognostic significance and mechanistic contributions to NSCLC brain metastasis (NSCLC-BM) remain largely unresolved. In this study, we analyze brain metastatic tumors from 66 patients with NSCLC and demonstrate that high infiltration of astrocytes is significantly associated with reduced overall survival. Functional assays reveal that astrocytes enhance the stemness of metastatic tumor cells, a phenotype that is significantly attenuated by silencing astrocytic monocarboxylate transporter 1 (MCT1), thereby blocking lactate uptake. Mechanistically, lactate reprograms astrocytes to release extracellular vesicles enriched in miR-8085, which downregulates the E3 ubiquitin ligase TRIM67 in tumor cells. This suppression stabilizes the transcription factor ELK1 through inhibition of ubiquitin-mediated degradation, promoting stemness maintenance and tumorigenic capacity. Clinically, low TRIM67 and high ELK1 expression correlate with poorer survival in patients with NSCLC-BM. Together, our findings uncover a novel lactate-induced miR-8085/TRIM67/ELK1 signaling cascade that drives brain metastasis progression and highlight potential prognostic biomarkers and therapeutic targets for patients with NSCLC involving the brain.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s11658-026-00957-1.

Introduction

Brain metastasis (BM) represents a leading cause of mortality, predominantly occurring in advanced stages of lung cancer [1, 2]. In NSCLC, brain metastasis (NSCLC-BM) develops in approximately 40–50% of patients, with 10–30% presenting NSCLC-BM at initial diagnosis [3]. The clinical prognosis of BM remains extremely poor, reflecting its highly malignant nature. Patients with untreated NSCLC-BM demonstrate a median overall survival (mOS) of merely 2–6 months, while treated patients show modest improvement with a median survival of 14–16 months [3, 5]. Despite revolutionary advancements in immunotherapies and targeted therapies for various solid tumors, therapeutic outcomes for brain metastatic lesions have shown limited improvement. This therapeutic challenge primarily stems from multiple factors including the blood–brain barrier (BBB), unique immunological niches, tumor cell heterogeneity, and the inevitable development of treatment resistance [4, 6, 9]. Consequently, there is an urgent need to enhance our understanding of the molecular aberrations and mechanistic underpinnings driving NSCLC-BM progression, which is crucial for developing novel therapeutic strategies.

As the most abundant central nervous system (CNS)-resident glia, reactive astrocytes actively remodel the tumor microenvironment (TME) through cytokine secretion and metabolic coupling [10]. Meanwhile, astrocytes constitute a critical cellular component within the brain metastatic niche and demonstrate complex dual regulatory functions throughout metastatic progression. On the one hand, astrocytes exhibit anti-metastatic properties by maintaining blood–brain barrier integrity and secreting plasminogen to inhibit tumor colonization [8, 11]. On the other hand, following tumor cell extravasation, astrocytes actively facilitate metastatic outgrowth through multiple mechanisms including metabolic coupling, angiogenesis promotion, and immune microenvironment remodeling [12, 15]. While the pro-metastatic role of astrocytes has been established, large-scale clinical analyses investigating the correlation between astrocytic infiltration and patient prognosis remain scarce. Notably, while astrocyte-mediated promotion of tumor cell stemness is recognized as a key pro-tumorigenic phenotype in gliomas [16], its role in brain metastases is poorly understood. Given that tumor cell stemness critically drives cancer progression, metastasis, and therapeutic resistance [17, 18], elucidating the mechanisms by which astrocytes promote stemness in brain metastases holds significant clinical implications for diagnosis and treatment.

Lactate, a key metabolic byproduct of anaerobic glycolysis, has recently emerged as a crucial signaling molecule in tumor development. Accumulating evidence demonstrates that lactate plays multifaceted roles in regulating cancer metabolism, modulating immune responses, and facilitating intercellular communication [19, 20]. Emerging evidence highlights lactate as a key regulator of cellular stemness and metastasis. Our previous research demonstrated that inhibiting lactate utilization significantly suppresses the tumor stemness and metastatic process of lung cancer cells by inducing mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) damage and reducing mitochondrial glutathione (GSH) catabolism [21, 22]. Intriguingly, emerging evidence indicates that lactate and lactate-mediated post-translational modifications (e.g., protein lactylation) contribute to the regulation of cancer stemness and the anti-tumor activity of immune cells. Although substantial progress has been made in understanding the functions of lactate in tumor biology, its precise impact on the NSCLC brain metastatic microenvironment remains largely uncharacterized and requires further exploration.

Extracellular vesicles (EVs) with diameters of 50–150 nm are recognized as key regulators of intercellular crosstalk in TME [23]. As major secretory cells in the central nervous system, astrocytes actively engage in tumor–stroma crosstalk through EV-mediated signaling [10, 24, 25]. Activated astrocytic extracellular vesicles can transfer regulatory mRNAs that induce chemoresistance and CCL2-mediated myeloid cell recruitment, thereby establishing an immunosuppressive microenvironment [26]. Notably, astrocytic extracellular vesicles can facilitate BM by delivering miR-19a to downregulate PTEN expression in breast cancer cells [13]. Furthermore, recent studies have uncovered temporal heterogeneity in the miRNA profiles of extracellular vesicles within the brain metastatic niche [27, 28], suggesting stage-specific roles for EV-mediated signaling during metastatic progression. Despite these advances, it remains unclear how tumor-derived lactate reprograms astrocytes to modulate EV content and function, and how this reprogramming influences the development of brain metastases in NSCLC.

In this study, we firstly investigated the infiltration and prognostic value of astrocytes in NSCLC-BM by analyzing patient tissue samples, integrated with single-cell RNA sequencing and bulk transcriptomic data. Our analysis revealed that increased astrocyte infiltration is significantly correlated with unfavorable survival, highlighting a potential prognostic value in NSCLC-BM. To elucidate their functional relevance, we established astrocyte–tumor co-culture systems and orthotopic intracranial xenograft models, demonstrating that astrocytes enhance tumor cell stemness and proliferation both in vitro and in vivo. Building on the paradigm of EV-mediated intercellular crosstalk, our findings demonstrate that miRNAs enriched in astrocyte-derived extracellular vesicles orchestrate key oncogenic programs within the brain metastatic microenvironment. Collectively, this study provides novel mechanistic insights into astrocyte–tumor interactions and identifies potential diagnostic biomarkers and therapeutic targets for patients with NSCLC-BM.

Materials and methods

Public scRNA-seq data analysis of NSCLC-BM

A total of 18,689 NSCLC-BM cells from the GSE164366 dataset were analyzed [29]. Dimensionality reduction was conducted employing Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP), followed by cellular clustering. Distinct cell populations were discerned utilizing the FindAllMarkers function. The thresholds were set as |log2 (fold change) |> 0.5 and FDR < 0.05. Cellular subpopulations within the NSCLC-BM microenvironment were characterized through the integration of single-cell analytical packages with the CellMarker 2.0 repository.

Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was carried out employing glycolysis-related gene sets obtained from MSigDB, analyzing 10,747 metastatic tumor cells (MTCs) alongside 40 astrocytes derived from the GSE164366 dataset. Gene sets demonstrating significant enrichment were determined based on false discovery rate (FDR) ≤ 0.05 (Supplementary Table S1). Subsequent Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis was executed utilizing the DAVID platform. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis was subsequently conducted with ClusterProfiler (v3.18.1), applying a statistical significance threshold of an adjusted P < 0.05.

Patients and specimens of NSCLC-BM

The archived paraffin-embedded tumor tissues from 66 patients with NSCLC-BM used in this study were approved by the Ethics Committee of Xiangya Hospital (approval no: 202207391). Clinical characteristics of the cohort are presented in Supplementary Table S2. To analyze lactate levels and establish primary cell cultures, matched pairs of fresh tumors and adjacent tissues (serving as controls) were intraoperatively obtained from five patients with NSCLC-BM. The corresponding clinical characteristics are comprehensively documented in Supplementary Table S3. Tissue specimens were promptly rinsed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Primary NSCLC-BM cells were subsequently isolated through enzymatic digestion with collagenase type II (40508ES60, Yeasen) and hyaluronidase (20426ES60, Yeasen).

Reagents and cell lines

Primary antibodies were purchased from the sources listed in Supplementary Table S4. Cycloheximide (CHX), proteasome inhibitor MG132, G418, and puromycin (Beyotime Biotech Shanghai, China). Lipofectamine 3000 reagent (Invitrogen, CA, USA), pReceiver plasmids encoding empty vector (EV, EX-NEG-M35), Flag-tagged TRIM67 (EX-Y4262-M35), and His-tagged ELK1 (EX-Z6227-M77) were purchased from Genecopoeia (TX, USA). Normal human astrocytes (HA-1800, ScienCell, CA, USA), NSCLC-BM cell lines NCI-H1915 (CRL-5904; ATCC), and 95D (a highly metastatic subline of PLA-801; Procell, Wuhan, China) were grown in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂ at 37 °C. Specifically, HA-1800 astrocytes were propagated in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (DMEM, C11995500BT, Gibco), whereas NCI-H1915 and 95D were cultivated in RPMI-1640 medium (A4192301, Gibco). DMEM and RPMI-1640 media were supplemented with 10% FBS (10099141C, Gibco) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin antibiotic solution. STR analysis was used for cell line authentication of NCI‑H1915 and 95D. STR profiling was routinely conducted every 3–6 months to confirm cell line authenticity. Human astrocytes cultured within five passages. Immunocytochemical staining confirmed high expression of the astrocyte‑specific marker glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) in these astrocytes (Supplementary Fig S2D).

Plasmids transfection

Plasmid transfection was performed using Lipofectamine 3000 reagent according to the manufacturer’s instructions. NCI-H1915 and 95D cells were seeded to reach 70–90% confluence at transfection. Plasmids and P3000 reagent were diluted in Opti-MEM medium, while Lipofectamine 3000 was diluted separately in the same medium. The two mixtures were combined and incubated at room temperature to form DNA–lipid complexes, which were then added dropwise to cells. After 36 h of incubation, cells were harvested for subsequent analysis.

Tumorsphere culture and tumorsphere formation assay

Primary brain metastatic cells were isolated from fresh NSCLC-BM tissues through sequential PBS–antibiotic washes, followed by enzymatic dissociation using hyaluronidase and collagenase type IV. Tumorsphere cultures of NCI-H1915, 95D, and primary BM cells (BrM) were established in complete neural stem cell (NSC) medium supplemented with LIF, EGF, and bFGF, as previously described [30]. All experimental tumorspheres were used within ten passages. Single-cell suspensions of NSCLC-BM cells (1 × 103 cells) were plated in 24-well plates. After 7–10 days of co-culture, tumorspheres > 50 μm in diameter were quantified using ImageJ software.

Histology/immunohistochemistry (IHC) and immunofluorescence (IF)

Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining and IHC analysis were based on consecutive 5-μm sections. Paraffin-embedded tumor tissues were collected for IHC. The tissue sections were dewaxed, rehydrated, and subjected to heat-induced antigen retrieval. Antigen–antibody reactions were visualized by exposure to the chromogen substrate. IF staining was performed as follows. The sections were incubated in primary antibodies with appropriate dilutions overnight at 4 °C, followed by Alexa488- or Alexa594-conjugated secondary antibodies. The DAPI solution was incubated for nuclear counterstaining. Antibody sources and dilutions are listed in Supplementary Table S4.

Measurement of lactate levels

Lactate production was quantified in HA-1800 astrocytes, NSCLC-BM cells, and tumor tissues. Culture supernatants and tissue homogenates were deproteinized, and lactate concentrations were determined using an L-lactate assay (EBCK004S2, Elabscience) according to user’s manual.

qRT–PCR

Total RNA was extracted from cells using the RNAeasy™ isolation kit (R0024, Beyotime Biotech). cDNA synthesis and quantitative reverse-transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT–PCR) were conducted using SYBR Green Master Mix (11201ES03, Yeasen) per standard protocols. miR-8085 and TRIM67 mRNA levels were quantified by the 2−ΔΔCT method (β-actin normalized). Primer sequences are presented in Supplementary Table S5.

Lentiviral transfection of miRNAs and genes

Tumorspheres were transfected with lentiviruses encoding hsa-miR-8085 (LPP-HmiR1893-MR03-500; Genecopoeia), with stable selection via puromycin (1 μg/mL) for 48 h. For TRIM67 and ELK1 overexpression, cells were transduced with pLV105-based TRIM67/ELK1 lentiviral particles and subsequently selected with puromycin (1 μg/mL) for 48 h. Firefly luciferase-expressing lentiviruses (Lenti-luciferase-P2A-Neo) were used for in vivo imaging applications, and G418-resistant cell pools were selected. Complete reagent specifications and target sequences are documented in Supplementary Table S6.

RNA interference

Chemically synthesized target-specific and nonspecific control (NC) small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) used in this study were purchased from Santa Cruz Biotechnology (Dallas, TX, USA). HA-1800 astrocytes were transfected with NC (sc-37007) or siRNAs targeting MCT1 (sc-37235). NCI-H1915 and 95D cells were transfected with NC or siRNAs targeting TRIM67 (sc-78674), SBK1 (sc-93038), or RNF144B (sc-44824). All siRNA transfections were performed at a final concentration of 20 nM using Lipofectamine 3000 (L3000015, Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For stable knockdown, TRIM67-targeting short hairpin RNA (shRNA) lentiviruses (psi-LVRU6P) were transduced into tumorspheres, followed by puromycin selection (1 μg/mL) for 72 h. All oligonucleotide sequences are detailed in Supplementary Tables S6.

Transwell co-culture and tumorsphere formation assay

HA-1800 astrocytes (1 × 103 cells) were seeded in cell culture inserts (3-μm pore, Corning). Single-cell suspensions of NSCLC-BM cells (1 × 103 cells) were plated in the lower chambers of 24-well plates. Both compartments were maintained in serum-free medium (as specified earlier). After 7–10 days of co-culture, tumorspheres > 50 μm in diameter were quantified using ImageJ software.

Aldefluor assay

The cancer stem cell (CSC) fraction in NSCLC-BM tumorspheres was assessed using the Aldefluor assay. Dissociated NSCLC-BM tumorsphere cells were incubated with 1 μmol/L BODIPY-aminoacetaldehyde (BAAA; ALDH substrate) per 2 × 105 cells in DMEM/F12 for 45 min at 37 °C. DEAB (20 μmol/L; specific ALDH inhibitor) was used in parallel control samples. Cells were centrifuged and resuspended in Hank’s balanced salt solution (HBSS), and analyzed by flow cytometry (BD FACSDiva™; BD Biosciences). ALDH⁺ populations were quantified using FACSDiva™ software (v6.1.3). Data represent the mean ± standard deviation (SD) of three biological replicates.

Purification and characterization of astrocyte-derived extracellular vesicles (EVs)

HA-1800 astrocytes were propagated in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin–streptomycin antibiotics. After incubation with PBS or lactate (10 mM/L) for 24 h, the medium supernatants were collected and processed through sequential centrifugation to remove cellular debris by filtrating through sterile filters (0.22 μm). EVs were then purified from the processed supernatant using the exoEasy Maxi Kit (Qiagen, 76,064).

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM), nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA), and western blot were used to characterize astrocyte-derived EVs. Subsequently, EV miRNAs were extracted using the miRNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen, 217004) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

EV internalization assay

HA-1800-derived EVs were labeled with PKH67 green-fluorescent dye (UR52302, Umibio) per the manufacturer's protocol. In brief, purified EVs were incubated with PKH67 for 10 min, washed with PBS via ultracentrifugation (100,000g, 70 min), and resuspended in PBS for immediate use. NCI-H1915 tumorspheres on Matrigel-coated plates were incubated with labeled EVs at 37 °C overnight. Lysotracker Red staining was performed for 30 min to label cellular lysosomes before fluorescence microscopy (Axio Observer, Carl Zeiss, Jena, Germany).

EVs treatment

Tumorspheres were centrifuged and resuspended into single-cell suspensions 1 day before EVs treatment. HA1800-derived EVs (200 μg) were directly added to tumorsphere cultures (1 × 106 cells) and incubated for 48 h.

Western blot

Cellular proteins were extracted by RIPA buffer (Thermo Scientific, 89901) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Beyotime, P1046). The proteins were separated by SDS–PAGE, and transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes. Membranes underwent sequential processing including: (1) blocking with 5% nonfat dry milk for 1 h; (2) incubation with dilution of primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C; and (3) probing with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h. Protein bands were identified through enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL), with images captured using a Bio-Rad imaging system. Detailed antibody dilutions are provided in Supplementary Table S4.

miRNA transcriptomic profiling

Total RNA was extracted from HA-1800 cells or EVs by TRIzol (Beyotime, R0016). miRNA and mRNA expression were measured using Affymetrix GeneChip arrays (Human Transcriptome miRNA Array 4.0 and Human Transcriptome Array 2.0, respectively). Array hybridization and primary data processing were conducted by Jiangxi HaploX Biotechnologies.

Dual luciferase reporter assay

To assess TRIM67 reporter activity, a DNA fragment encompassing the predicted miR-8085 binding site in the TRIM67 3′ untranslated region (UTR) was subcloned into the pGL3-Basic vector, generating the TRIM67 3′ UTR wild-type (WT) reporter construct. The miR-8085–binding site was mutated from CUCUCCC into AUAUAAC to obtain the TRIM67 mutant reporter using the primers listed in Supplementary Table S7. Luciferase activity was determined using the DLR™ Assay System (Promega) with a BioTek Synergy H1 microplate reader, and the firefly/Renilla ratio was quantified.

Cignal finder 45-pathway reporter array

Signaling pathway activity in NCI-H1915 tumorspheres expressing miR-8085 or NC control was assessed using Cignal™ 45-Pathway Reporter Arrays (QIAGEN/SABiosciences, Frederick, MD) per the manufacturer’s protocol [31]. In brief, dissociated NCI-H1915 tumorsphere cells were seeded in 96-well white plates and incubated overnight at 37 °C. Cells were transiently transfected with pathway-specific transcription factor reporter plasmids and controls, incubated overnight, and then cultured for a further 48 h. Pathway activities in NCI-H1915 tumorspheres (shTRIM67/scramble control) were determined by firefly and Renilla luminescence using the Dual-Glo Luciferase Assay System (Promega) on a BioTek Synergy H1 microplate reader, and the firefly/Renilla ratio was quantified, with scramble shRNA control set to 100%.

Intracranial xenograft models

Animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Hunan SJA (protocol no. SJA202401701). Athymic male mice (6–8 weeks) were anesthetized with xylazine/ketamine. Dissociated NCI-H1915 tumorsphere cells (1 × 105) labeled with firefly luciferase were microinjected into the cortex (right frontal lobe, n = 8) [32]. For in vivo studies of PBS- or lactate-induced astrocytes EVs for tumorigenesis, NCI-H1915 tumorspheres were treated with EVs for 48 h, then dissociated into single cells for intracranial injection. Intracranial tumor growth was monitored weekly using an IVIS Spectrum imaging system, and survival was recorded. Mice were humanely euthanized upon reaching predefined experimental endpoints or at the conclusion of the study period. Mouse specimens were harvested for paraffin embedding and sectioned for H&E and IHC.

Immunoprecipitation

Cells were harvested and lysed in NP-40 buffer (Thermo Scientific™, J62805.AK) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Beyotime, P1046). Cell lysates were subjected to immunoprecipitation using anti-His tag antibody (Sigma-Aldrich, A2220) for 3 h at 4 ℃ with gentle rotation, and captured by Protein A/G Dynabeads. After three washes with lysis buffer, the immunocomplexes were eluted and processed for immunoblot analysis.

Statistical analysis

Data visualization and statistical analyses were carried out using GraphPad Prism 9.0. Results were reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Significance was determined by Student’s t-test, with thresholds defined as follows: *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001; not significant (n.s.), P ≥ 0.05.

Results

High infiltration level of tumor-associated astrocytes was associated with poor clinical outcomes in NSCLC-BM

To investigate the clinical significance of astrocyte infiltration in patients with NSCLC-BM, tumor specimens from a cohort of 66 patients with NSCLC-BM were analyzed, along with integrated scRNA-seq data (GSE164366) and bulk transcriptomic datasets. Astrocyte presence was characterized using GFAP as a canonical marker (Fig. 1A). Initial analysis of public scRNA-seq dataset confirmed the presence of GFAP⁺ astrocytes within the brain metastatic niche (Supplementary Fig. S1A, B, C). The baseline clinical characteristics of IHC cohort are summarized in Supplementary Table S2, and a heat map is shown in Supplementary Fig. S1D. Dual immunofluorescence staining for CK7/8 and GFAP revealed direct physical interaction between metastatic tumor cells (MTCs) and infiltrating astrocytes (Fig. 1B, Supplementary Fig. S1E), suggesting functional tumor–astrocyte crosstalk. Immunohistochemical analysis further demonstrated notable interpatient heterogeneity in GFAP⁺ astrocyte infiltration across NSCLC-BM samples (Fig. 1C, D).Fig. 1High infiltration of astrocytes correlates with poor clinical prognosis in NSCLC-BM

High infiltration of astrocytes correlates with poor clinical prognosis in NSCLC-BM

Kaplan–Meier analysis demonstrated that increased astrocyte infiltration is significantly correlated with unfavorable mOS (positive versus negative: 14.50 versus 43.00 months; hazard ratio (HR) 2.488, 95% confidence interval (CI) 1.158–5.345, P = 0.0049) (Fig. 1E). These findings were corroborated by bulk RNA-seq data, which revealed that, compared with patients exhibiting low GFAP expression, those with high expression had markedly poorer outcomes (Fig. 1F). Moreover, univariate Cox regression identified astrocyte infiltration as an independent prognostic factor, alongside targeted therapy history, radiotherapy, and number of metastatic lesions (Supplementary Fig. S1F).

Taken together, these data provide robust, multidimensional evidence—from single-cell transcriptomics to immunohistochemistry and bulk RNA analysis—that GFAP⁺ astrocyte infiltration is associated with poor clinical outcomes in NSCLC-BM. Importantly, the presence of astrocytes is shown to be an independent prognostic biomarker, highlighting its potential utility for patient stratification and outcome prediction. These findings not only illuminate a previously underappreciated aspect of the brain metastatic microenvironment, but also lay the foundation for the development of astrocyte-targeted therapeutic strategies in NSCLC-BM.

Lactate uptake by astrocytes promotes tumor stemness in NSCLC-BM

We further performed comparative analysis of gene expression profiles between MTCs and astrocytes using the scRNA-seq (GSE164366) dataset. Differential expression analysis identified 389 significantly altered genes, with volcano plot visualization demonstrating distinct expression patterns between tumor cells and astrocytes (Supplementary Fig. S2A, Supplementary Table S1). GSEA revealed significant upregulation of glycolytic pathways and increased lactate production in MTCs cancer cells (Supplementary Fig. S2B). To investigate the metabolic differences between metastatic tumor cells and astrocytes, we first measured lactate levels in four paired fresh NSCLC-BM tissue samples. Tumor tissues exhibited significantly higher lactate concentrations compared with adjacent normal brain tissues (P < 0.001, Supplementary Fig. S2C), indicating elevated glycolytic activity in the metastatic lesions. To further explore lactate production at the cellular level, we established a primary NSCLC-BM cell line (BrM; characterization provided in Supplementary Fig. S2E). All NSCLC-BM cell lines (BrM, NCI-H1915, and 95D) produced significantly more lactate than astrocytes (Supplementary Fig. S2F), reinforcing the notion that tumor cells serve as a major source of lactate in the brain metastatic microenvironment. These findings suggest that astrocytes may utilize tumor-derived lactate as a metabolic substrate, supporting their functional role in NSCLC-BM progression.

Tumor stemness, particularly in the form of cancer stem cells, is a key factor in the formation of metastatic tumors [33]. To investigate whether astrocytes enhance tumor stemness in BM (similar to their role in gliomas [16]), we established an astrocyte–tumorsphere transwell co-culture system (Fig. 2A). Tumorsphere formation assays demonstrated that, across all three NSCLC-BM cell lines (NCI-H1915, 95D, and BrM), the astrocyte–tumorsphere co-culture significantly increased tumorsphere formation compared with tumor cell single culture, indicating that astrocytes enhanced the tumor stemness of NSCLC-BM cells (Fig. 2B, C).Fig. 2Astrocytes Promote Tumor Stemness of MTCs in NSCLC-BM Through Lactate-Dependent Mechanisms

Astrocytes Promote Tumor Stemness of MTCs in NSCLC-BM Through Lactate-Dependent Mechanisms

Given that tumor cells exhibited substantially higher lactate production than astrocytes, and that astrocytes enhance tumor stemness in brain metastatic cells, we next explored whether astrocytic lactate uptake contributes to this pro-stemness effect. To test this, we used the astrocyte–tumorsphere co-culture in which MCT1—the primary transporter for lactate uptake—was silenced in astrocytes (siMCT1-HA-1800) (Fig. 2D). Compared with control astrocytes (siNC-HA-1800), MCT1 silencing significantly reduced tumorsphere formation by NSCLC-BM cells (Fig. 2E, F). CSCs are characterized by high aldehyde dehydrogenase (ALDH) activity. Remarkably, tumorsphere-derived NSCLC-BM cells showed markedly higher ALDH activity than serum cultured monolayer cells (Supplementary Fig. S2G). We further assessed ALDH activity and observed a marked decrease in the proportion of ALDHhigh tumor cells in the siMCT1-HA-1800 group compared with the siNC-HA-1800 group (Fig. 2G, H, Supplementary Fig. S2H). Consistently, immunofluorescence analysis revealed decreased expression of stemness markers CD44, CD147, SOX2, and CD133 in tumor cells co-cultured with MCT1-silenced astrocytes (Fig. 2I, J). These findings indicate that lactate uptake by astrocytes is critical for their ability to promote tumor stemness in NSCLC-BM (Fig. 2K).

Lactate-activated astrocytic EVs promote brain metastasis by enhancing tumor stemness

Emerging evidence underscores the role of astrocytic EVs in promoting metastasis and facilitating intercellular communication [15, 28]. We hypothesize that lactate-activated astrocytes enhance tumor stemness in NSCLC-BM, potentially through astrocyte-derived EV pathways. To test this hypothesis, we isolated EVs from HA-1800 cells cultured with either lactate or PBS for 48 h, and subsequently incubated these EVs with NSCLC-BM cells (Fig. 3A). Comprehensive characterization of lactate-induced astrocyte-derived EVs (Lac-ev) and PBS-treated astrocyte-derived EVs (PBS-ev) verified the successful isolation of extracellular vesicles (Fig. 3B, Supplementary Fig. S3A–C). Furthermore, EV uptake assays demonstrated efficient internalization of astrocyte-derived EVs by NCI-H1915 and 95D cells (Supplementary Fig. S3D). Subsequent evaluation of stem-like properties revealed that NSCLC-BM cells treated with Lac-ev exhibited enhanced tumorsphere formation capacity and increased expression of stemness-associated markers, including SOX2, CD44, and CD147 (Fig. 3C, D, Supplementary Fig. S3E). Furthermore, Aldefluor™ flow cytometry analysis confirmed an increased ALDHhigh subpopulation in the Lac-ev group (Fig. 3E, F, Supplementary Fig. S3F).Fig. 3Lactate-activated astrocytic extracellular vesicles promote brain metastasis by enhancing tumor stemness

Lactate-activated astrocytic extracellular vesicles promote brain metastasis by enhancing tumor stemness

To investigate whether lactate-activated astrocytic EVs could enhance tumor stemness and promote BM in vivo, we established an orthotopic BM xenograft model by stereotactically injecting 104 lung cancer cells/5 μL into the brains of nude mice (n = 8). The tumor growth was monitored every 7 days using bioluminescence imaging (BLI) to quantify fluorescence intensity and track survival time (Fig. 3G). Survival analysis demonstrated markedly reduced median overall survival in the Lac-ev group (Fig. 3H). Compared with the PBS-ev group, the Lac-ev group exhibited an increased mean fluorescence intensity throughout the entire monitoring period (Fig. 3I, J), demonstrating significantly enhanced tumor growth in the Lac-ev group. H&E staining confirmed a larger tumor burden in the Lac-ev group, and immunohistochemistry demonstrated increased expression of the proliferation marker Ki-67 as well as stemness-associated markers (CD44, CD147, and SOX2) (Fig. 3K, Supplementary Fig. S4).

The in vivo findings further demonstrate that lactate-activated astrocytic EVs significantly enhance the proliferation and stemness of NSCLC-BM cells in the brain, indicating that lactate-activated astrocytes promote BM by enhancing tumor stemness.

Astrocyte-derived EVs miR-8085 mediates lactate-induced stemness maintenance in brain metastasis

Emerging evidence establishes EV miRNAs as critical regulators of cancer progression, particularly in angiogenesis and metastatic processes [34, 35]. To determine whether lactate-activated astrocytic EVs enhance stemness and promote BM through EV miRNAs, we performed microarray analysis comparing miRNA expression profiles between Lac-ev and PBS-ev. Differential expression analysis revealed 14 significantly upregulated and 2 downregulated EV miRNAs in the Lac-ev group compared with the PBS-ev group (Fig. 4A). KEGG analysis demonstrated that these differentially expressed miRNAs were associated with stem cell pluripotency regulation pathways (Supplementary Fig.S5A). Among the top five candidate miRNAs, miR-8085 exhibited the strongest inhibitory effect on tumorsphere formation when silenced in astrocytes during transwell co-culture (Fig. 4B, Supplementary Fig. S5B). qRT–PCR analysis confirmed dose-dependent upregulation of miR-8085 expression in both astrocytes and astrocyte-derived EVs following lactate treatment for 24 h (Fig. 4C). Indeed, lactate promotes the expression of miR-8085 in astrocyte-derived EVs, which is involved in the regulation of tumor stemness.Fig. 4Astrocyte-Derived EV miR-8085 Mediates Lactate-Induced Stemness Maintenance in Brain Metastasis

Astrocyte-Derived EV miR-8085 Mediates Lactate-Induced Stemness Maintenance in Brain Metastasis

To determine whether miR-8085 from lactate-activated astrocytic EVs can be directly taken up by NSCLC-BM cells, we measured miR-8085 levels in NCI-H1915 and 95D cells following treatment with Lac-ev or PBS-ev. A significant increase in miR-8085 expression was observed in both cell lines after 24 h of Lac-ev treatment compared with PBS-ev controls (Supplementary Fig. S5C). To mechanistically characterize miR-8085's involvement in modulating cancer stem cell properties in NSCLC-BM, we established stable miR-8085-overexpressing cell lines in NCI-H1915, 95D, and BrM cells. Consistent with our hypothesis, miR-8085 overexpression significantly enhanced tumorsphere formation capacity (Fig. 4D, E, Supplementary Fig. S5D), increased the ALDHhigh tumor cell subpopulation, and upregulated the expression of stemness markers SOX2, CD44, and CD147 compared with negative control (NC) groups (Fig. 4F–H, Supplementary Fig. S5E).

In vivo experiments further confirmed that miR-8085 overexpression (miR-8085) significantly promoted intracranial tumor growth compared with the negative control (NC) group (Fig. 4I, J). In addition, mice bearing miR-8085-overexpressing tumors exhibited significantly reduced overall survival (Fig. 4K). IHC analysis showed elevated proliferation marker Ki-67 and stemness markers CD147, CD44, and SOX2 in the miR-8085 group compared with the NC group (Fig. 4L, Supplementary Fig. S6).

Taken together, our findings demonstrate that lactate-activated astrocytes secrete extracellular vesicles enriched with miR-8085, which is taken up by NSCLC-BM cells, enhance the stemness, and promote brain metastatic potential.

miR-8085 targets TRIM67 to regulate tumor stemness in brain metastasis

To identify potential target genes of miR-8085, we first performed RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) to screen genes downregulated in miR-8085-overexpressing NCI-H1915 tumorspheres compared with NC control. Candidate target genes (miR-8085 versus NC, fold change < 0.5) were further filtered using miRNA target prediction databases, including TargetScan and miRDB. The overlapping genes from both the RNA-seq downregulated dataset and in silico target prediction were selected as high-confidence putative miR-8085 target genes (top 3 targets) for subsequent validation and functional analysis. RNA sequencing of miR-8085-overexpressing NCI-H1915 tumorspheres revealed 241 differentially expressed genes (DEGs), including 162 downregulated genes (Supplementary Fig. S7A). KEGG enrichment analysis showed that these DEGs were significantly associated with multiple cancer-related signaling pathways (Supplementary Fig. S7B). Integration of RNA-seq data with miRNA target predictions highlighted several candidate targets of miR-8085 (Fig. 5A, B). Tumorsphere formation assays were performed following siRNA-mediated knockdown of the top three candidate targets in NCI-H1915 and 95D cells. TRIM67 and ELK1 are associated with clinical prognosis in patients with NSCLC-BM

To determine pathological relevance, we performed immunohistochemical staining for TRIM67 and ELK1 on 66 NSCLC-BM clinical samples. Correlation analysis was then conducted between protein expression levels and patient clinical characteristics, aiming to validate the role of the miR-8085/TRIM67/ELK1 axis in NSCLC-BM.

IHC analysis revealed that TRIM67 expression was inversely correlated with ELK1 expression (Fig. 8A, B). Patients negative for TRIM67 had significantly shorter median overall survival compared with those positive for TRIM67 (Fig. 8C). This miRNA downregulates TRIM67 in tumor cells, stabilizing ELK1, a transcription factor that enhances tumor stemness and progression (Fig. 8E). Clinically, high astrocyte infiltration and TRIM67 suppression correlate with poor prognosis in patients with NSCLC-BM. Discussion

The tumor microenvironment (TME) is widely recognized as a critical regulator of tumor cell proliferation, invasion, and distant metastasis. The crosstalk between tumor cells and the organ-specific metastatic TME resembles the classic “seed and soil” relationship [38]. The brain metastatic tumor microenvironment fosters a permissive milieu that promotes lung cancer brain metastasis—supporting cancer cell survival, invasion, proliferation, and therapeutic resistance via crosstalk between cancer cells and resident neural cells. Numerous lines of evidence indicate that reciprocal tumor cell–astrocyte crosstalk critically drives brain metastatic progression. Functioning as adaptive regulators within the brain metastatic niche, astrocytes orchestrate multifaceted pro-tumorigenic processes including metabolic reprogramming, immunosuppressive microenvironment remodeling, and angiogenic induction [14, 15, 39]. Reactive astrocytes have been shown to promote small cell lung cancer (SCLC) progression by influencing angiogenesis and providing nutritional support to cancer cells [40]. Sherona et al. demonstrated that the TGLI1 transcription factor mediates breast cancer BM by activating cancer stem cells and astrocytes that initiate metastasis in TME [41]. Consistently, we observed that astrocyte infiltration in brain metastasis significantly correlated with patient survival outcomes. Therefore, unveiling the interaction between astrocytes and metastatic lung cancer cells may provide clues for future therapeutic strategies.

Aerobic glycolysis in tumor cells promotes lactate-mediated acidification of the microenvironment, a hallmark feature of the brain metastatic niche [20]. Emerging evidence highlights lactate as a critical regulator of the brain metastatic microenvironment. The lactate-altered tumor microenvironment promotes immune evasion by suppressing monocyte maturation, dampening cytokine secretion, and impairing the cytotoxic activity of T cells and NK cells [42, 44]. However, it remains unclear how lactate within the BM microenvironment regulate astrocyte function, and how tumor cells respond to lactate-activated astrocytes—insights that could inform the development of targeted therapeutic strategies. In our study, GSEA analysis demonstrated elevated glycolytic activity in metastatic tumor cells relative to astrocytes. These results were further validated by comparative measurements of lactate concentrations between the two cell types. Nevertheless, we demonstrated that astrocytes enhance the stemness of NSCLC brain metastasis cells through lactate-dependent mechanisms, as knockdown of the astrocytic lactate transporter MCT1 attenuates the stemness of these metastatic cells. Therefore, our results substantiate the pivotal role of lactate in mediating symbiotic interactions between NSCLC-BM cells and astrocytes, while highlighting the importance of this metabolic crosstalk in promoting tumor cell self-renewal and metastatic colonization.

Extracellular vesicles have emerged as crucial mediators of intercellular communication in TME [27, 45]. Recent studies have demonstrated that exosomal miR-19a derived by astrocyte promotes BM through reversible downregulation of PTEN in metastatic cells, subsequently enhancing CCL2 secretion and myeloid cell recruitment [13]. Furthermore, accumulating evidence indicates that tumor cell-absorbed extracellular vesicles can modulate cancer stem cell-related pathways, thereby maintaining tumor heterogeneity [35]. Our study identifies lactate-induced astrocytic extracellular vesicle miR-8085 as a key regulator of tumor stemness and brain colonization in brain metastasis. Mechanistically, elevated lactate in the TME promotes astrocyte secretion of miR-8085-enriched extracellular vesicles. Our study also provides the first demonstration that extracellular vesicles containing miR-8085 function as critical molecular mediators of communication between tumor cells and astrocytes within the lactate-modified tumor microenvironment of brain metastatic niches. These findings support the therapeutic potential of targeting miR-8085-mediated symbiosis for patients with BM.

In mechanistic investigations, we showed that miR-8085 enhances tumor stemness and growth by downregulating TRIM67 expression in brain metastatic cells. TRIM67, a well-documented tumor suppressor in colon, gastric [46], and lung cancers [47]. Emerging evidence positions TRIM67 as a clinically significant prognostic marker in BM of breast cancer [48]. Our findings demonstrate that both miR-8085 overexpression and TRIM67 downregulation enhance tumor stemness in vitro and tumor growth in vivo, suggesting that TRIM67 acts as a functional target of miR-8085. TRIM67 belongs to the TRIM protein family and exhibits E3 ubiquitin ligase activity. In addition, our results identified ELK1 as a downstream target of TRIM67. As a transcriptional activator within the MAPK/ERK pathway, dysregulation of ELK1 contributes to lung cancer development and metastasis [49, 51]. However, the mechanism by which ELK1 is activated in brain metastasis remains unclear. Teixeira et al. demonstrated that ELK1 functions as a ubiquitination substrate and is degraded via a proteasome-dependent pathway [52]. Consistent with previous studies, our results demonstrate that TRIM67 mediates the ubiquitination and subsequent proteasomal degradation of ELK1. We further showed that ELK1 overexpression in NSCLC brain metastatic cells enhances tumor stemness in vitro and tumor growth in vivo, indicating that ELK1 serves as a functional downstream target of TRIM67. Functional experiments revealed that miR-8085 overexpression in NSCLC-BM cells downregulates TRIM67 and upregulates ELK1, thereby promoting tumor stemness and tumorigenesis. The clinical relevance of the TRIM67/ELK1 signaling pathway was further validated using patient-derived tissue specimens, indicating that the miR-8085/TRIM67/ELK1 axis may serve as a promising multilevel therapeutic target for brain metastasis.

In summary, our study demonstrates that infiltration of astrocytes predicts poor prognosis in patients with NSCLC-BM and identifies lactate as a key mediator of astrocyte-driven tumor stemness and growth. We further reveal a novel regulatory axis in which lactate activated astrocyte-derived extracellular vesicles carrying miR-8085 modulate tumor stemness and progression via the TRIM67/ELK1 pathway. These findings highlight the critical role of lactate-mediated tumor–astrocyte crosstalk in brain metastasis, providing multiple potential therapeutic targets and a scientific rationale for the development of effective treatment strategies for patients with NSCLC-BM.
