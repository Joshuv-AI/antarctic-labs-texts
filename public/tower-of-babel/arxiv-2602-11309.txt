Cactus barriers
Jarosław Buczyński

Abstract
Determinantal methods for bounding the rank and border rank of tensors or polynomials are subject to a major barrier. For instance, it is known that using determinantal method one cannot prove a lower bound for the border rank of tensor in ℂ m ⊗ ℂ m ⊗ ℂ m {\mathbb{C}}^{m}\otimes{\mathbb{C}}^{m}\otimes{\mathbb{C}}^{m} that exceeds 6 ​ m − 4 6m-4 . We explain the precise geometric reason for this number (and analogous bounds in more general tensor spaces) using cactus varieties and, more generally, scheme theoretic methods in algebraic geometry.

Introduction

Throughout the article
{\Bbbk}
is
an algebraically closed base field of any characteristic.

1.1
Linear rank methods

The determinant of a square matrix, and more generally, the minors of a given order of any matrix
M
, are famously useful invariants throughout mathematics.
In the context of this article their relevance is given by the relation to a rank of
M
and to bounds on tensor-type ranks.

In many areas of science, one frequently seeks to decompose a complicated state as a combination of simple ones.
More mathematically, given a vector space
W
(the set of all potentially possible states, including the complicated ones) and its subset
\hat{X}\subset W
of simple states we want to write an element
F\in W
as a linear combination of vectors from
\hat{X}
using as few such vectors as possible.
The minimal number of these elements is called
X
-rank, and it witnesses how complicated
F
is.

There is a classical method of bounding the
X
-rank (and a related notion of
X
-border rank) of
F
that involves a matrix
M
of linear forms on
W
.
For each
w\in W
we can evaluate each entry of
M
at
w
and obtain a matrix
M(w)
whose coefficients are in
{\Bbbk}
.
For each integer
k
we can define the loci
\left\{\rk M\leqslant k\right\}=\left\{w\in W\mid\rk M(w)\leqslant k\right\}
.
Now suppose
k:=\max\left\{\rk(M(x))\mid x\in\hat{X}\right\}
, so that
\hat{X}\subset\left\{\rk M\leqslant k\right\}
.
If the
X
-rank of
F
is
r
, then
\rk(M(F))\leqslant k\cdot r
, or vice versa, if
\rk(M(F))>k\cdot r
for some integer
r
, then the
X
-rank of
F
must be at least
r+1
.
Thus each matrix
M
of linear forms gives us a lower bound for
X
-rank (and in fact also for the
X
-border rank).
These types of lower bounds are called in the literature “rank methods for complexity”, see for instance
[
EGOW18
]
.
In view of
[
DM26
]

in this paper we will call them
linear rank methods
instead.

Since varying the matrix
M
one gets different lower bounds,
a considerable effort has been devoted to finding the optimal matrix and bound for a given problem, see
[
LO13
]
for an overview.
However, at some point the rank methods stop being effective,
and eventually barriers for the tensor cases were proven by
[
EGOW18
]
,
and
[
GMOW19
]
.

1.2
Border and cactus ranks

In this article we present the geometric explanation for the barriers,
and generalise it to any other setting outside the tensor world.
This geometric explanation has the name
cactus variety
and our arguments build on earlier work, including
[
BB14
]
,

[
BR13
]
,

[
Gałą23a
]

[
Gałą17
]
,

[
Gałą23b
]
.

In order to achieve our goal we move to the world of algebraic geometry and formalise the setting.
In Appendix
A
we explain why doing this we do not lose any generality compared to the situation outlined above.

From the perspective of algebraic geometry it is more convenient to work in the projective space
{\mathbb{P}}(W)=(W\setminus\left\{0\right\})/{\Bbbk}^{*}
instead of the vector space
W
.
On
{\mathbb{P}}(W)
we consider the Zariski topology.
For a subset (or subscheme)
R\subset{\mathbb{P}}(W)
by
\left\langle R\right\rangle\subset{\mathbb{P}}(W)
we denote the projective linear span of
R
, that is the smallest projective linear subspace of
{\mathbb{P}}(W)
that contains
R
.
Equivalently, if
I(R)
is the homogeneous ideal of all the functions vanishing on
R
,
then
\left\langle R\right\rangle=Z(I(R)_{1})
.

Suppose
X\subset{\mathbb{P}}(W)
is a locally closed subvariety.
We assume that
\left\langle X\right\rangle={\mathbb{P}}(W)
.
For each positive integer
r
define the following subsets of
{\mathbb{P}}(W)
:

\displaystyle\sigma^{\circ}_{r}(X)

\displaystyle:=\bigcup\left\{\left\langle x_{1},\dotsc,x_{r}\right\rangle\mid x_{i}\in X\right\},

(1.1a)

\displaystyle\sigma_{r}(X)

\displaystyle:=\overline{\sigma^{\circ}_{r}(X)},

(1.1b)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{K}^{\circ}_{r}\left(X\right)

\displaystyle:=\bigcup\left\{\left\langle R\right\rangle\mid R\text{ is a finite subscheme of $X$ of degree }r\right\},

(1.1c)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{K}_{r}\left(X\right)

\displaystyle:=\overline{\mathfrak{K}^{\circ}_{r}\left(X\right)}.

(1.1d)

These sets allow us to define, respectively, the
X
-rank,
X
-border rank,
X
-cactus rank,
X
-border cactus rank.

Definition 1.2
.

With
X
as above,
for any
F\in{\mathbb{P}}(W)
define:

•

the
X
-rank
of
F
:

r_{X}(F):=\min\left\{r\in{\mathbb{N}}\mid F\in\sigma^{\circ}_{r}(X)\right\}
,

•

the
X
-border rank
of
F
:

\operatorname{br}_{X}(F):=\min\left\{r\in{\mathbb{N}}\mid F\in\sigma_{r}(X)\right\}
,

•

the
X
-cactus rank
of
F
:

\operatorname{cr}_{X}(F):=\min\left\{r\in{\mathbb{N}}\mid F\in\mathfrak{K}^{\circ}_{r}\left(X\right)\right\}
,

•

the
X
-border cactus rank
of
F
:

\operatorname{bcr}_{X}(F):=\min\left\{r\in{\mathbb{N}}\mid F\in\mathfrak{K}_{r}\left(X\right)\right\}
.

If
F\in W
instead, and
[F]\in{\mathbb{P}}(W)
is its projective class,
then we define
X
-rank of
F
to be
r_{X}([F])
, and analogously for all the border and cactus variants.
By convention,
r_{X}(F)
(and
\operatorname{br}_{X}(F)
,
\operatorname{cr}_{X}(F)
,
\operatorname{bcr}_{X}(F)
)
is
0
if and only if
F=0\in W
, and it is never
0
for
F\in{\mathbb{P}}(W)
.

We always have

r_{X}(F)\geqslant\operatorname{br}_{X}(F)\geqslant\operatorname{bcr}_{X}(F)

and

r_{X}(F)\geqslant\operatorname{cr}_{X}(F)\geqslant\operatorname{bcr}_{X}(F)
.
Moreover the cactus variants of ranks are typically much smaller than the corresponding
X
-rank or
X
-border rank.
The main result of this article is that in cases of interest the linear rank methods outlined in Subsection
1.1
can at best detect

\operatorname{bcr}_{X}(F)
, and never
\operatorname{br}_{X}(F)
(unless when they coincide).

1.3
Barriers in terms of cacti

Let
X_{0}\subset X
be the smooth locus of
X
and let
\overline{X}\subset{\mathbb{P}}(W)
be the closure of
X
.
In most cases of interest (such as Segre-Veronese, or Grassmannian varieties)
X
is itself smooth and projective and thus
X_{0}=X=\overline{X}
.
Suppose further
M
is a matrix of linear forms on
W
.
By slight abuse of notation, we write
\left\{\rk M\leqslant k\right\}\subset{\mathbb{P}}(W)
, the rank locus, also in the projective space.

In the notation of Subsection
1.2
,
the linear rank method outlined in Subsection
1.1
can be rephrased as the following statements:
if
X\subset\left\{\rk M\leqslant k\right\}
then all of the following statements hold:

\displaystyle\forall_{F\in W}\ r_{X}(F)

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{\rk M(F)}{k},

\displaystyle\forall_{r\in{\mathbb{N}}}\ \sigma^{\circ}_{r}(X)

\displaystyle\subset\left\{\rk M\leqslant k\cdot r\right\},

\displaystyle\forall_{F\in W}\ \operatorname{br}_{X}(F)

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{\rk M(F)}{k},

\displaystyle\forall_{r\in{\mathbb{N}}}\ \sigma_{r}(X)

\displaystyle\subset\left\{\rk M\leqslant k\cdot r\right\}.

Theorem 1.3

(Cactus barrier for linear rank method)
.

With variety
X
and matrix
M
as above, if in addition
X\subset\left\{\rk M\leqslant k\right\}

then
\mathfrak{K}_{r}\left(X_{0}\right)\subset\left\{\rk M\leqslant k\cdot r\right\}
.
In particular, if
g
is an integer such that

\mathfrak{K}_{g}\left(X_{0}\right)={\mathbb{P}}(W)
(for instance,
g
is the generic
X_{0}
-cactus rank), then the linear rank methods will never provide a better lower bound on
X
-border rank than
g
.

The exact value of
X
-generic rank (thus the maximal possible
X
-border rank) is known when
X
is a Veronese variety (the famous Alexander-Hirschowitz Theorem
[
AH95
]
,
[
BO08
]
), some families of Segre varieties
[
AOP09
]
, two factor Segre-Veronese varieties
[
ABGO24
]
,
[
DK26
]
and in further sporadic cases.
Moreover, it is straightforward to notice
that it is at least
\frac{\dim W}{\dim X+1}
.
On the other hand, generic cactus rank is rarely known, and usually much lower than
\frac{\dim W}{\dim X+1}
.
For instance, for the Segre embedding

{\mathbb{P}}^{a-1}\times{\mathbb{P}}^{b-1}\times{\mathbb{P}}^{c-1}\subset{\mathbb{P}}^{abc-1}
,
it is known that for
g=2(a+b+c-2)
the
g
-th cactus variety
\mathfrak{K}_{g}\left(X\right)={\mathbb{P}}^{abc-1}
, while we need at least
\frac{abc}{a+b+c-2}
-th secant variety to fill in
{\mathbb{P}}^{abc-1}
.
Thus for large values of
a
,
b
,
c
, the gap between the border rank of most tensors and the best possible lower bound that we can obtain using linear rank methods is huge.
Analogous gaps show up in all the other tensor spaces provided the dimension of
X
is sufficiently large and the embedding is sufficiently involved.

Combining Theorem
1.3

with known results about generic cactus rank
[
Gałą23a
, Cor. 1.7]
(and earlier work)
recovers the asymptotic barriers for linear rank methods
[
EGOW18
, Thms 1 and 2]
,
[
GMOW19
, Thm. 8.4, Cor. 8.5]
.

Prior work

Weaker versions
of Theorem
1.3

appeared previously since the introduction of cactus varieties.
In particular, in
[
BB14
, Prop. 3.6]

the claim is proven for
M
arising
as a catalecticant matrix (over complex numbers).
Later
[
Gałą17
, Thm 5]
proved the barrier whenever
M
arises
from so called vector bundle construction
[
Gałą17
, Constr. 2]
.
Finally,

[
Gałą23b
, Thm 1.18]
presents the statement whenever
X
is contained in the constant rank locus,
attributing it to the author of this article.

1.5
Strengthening of barriers

As consequences of results of this paper we outline further possibilities of research direction.
Firstly, so far all the lower bounds on the border rank that were discovered seem to also be bounds on border cactus rank, with two notable exceptions discussed in Subsection
1.6
.
That is, cactus barriers probably apply to much wider class of methods, than just linear rank methods.
For instance, the flag conditions

[
LM17
, Cor. 2.3]
,

[
CHL22
, Prop. 2.3 and 2.5]

are not in any obvious way related to the rank method,
but, again, the lower bounds for border rank obtained in this way are also lower bounds for border cactus rank.
Analogously,
the lower bound on Waring rank
[
BT16
, Prop. 4]

is also subject to the cactus barriers. Indeed,
the lower bound is never higher than the length of an apolar scheme outside of a specific hyperplane by
[
BT16
, Rem. 6]
.
Furthermore,
[
BR13
, Thm 3]
construct such a scheme of relatively small length and this is precisely the upper bound for generic cactus rank.
Also Weak Border Apolarity

[
BB21
, Thm 1.2]
, which proved to be more effective than many earlier rank based lower bounds,
is subject to the barriers by the results of

[
BB26
, Thm 1.2]
.

Thus an important open problem is to determine whether all other existing methods for bounding rank or border rank are subject to some versions of the cactus barrier.

Another direction would be strengthening the cactus bounds. Focusing on tensors, or more generally on
X={\mathbb{P}}(A)\times Y\subset{\mathbb{P}}(A\otimes V)
with
Y\subset{\mathbb{P}}(V)

there is a well known correspondence between
secant varieties of
X
and Grassmann secant varieties
\sigma_{r,i}(Y)
of
Y
.
See
[
CC01
]
or
[
BL13
]
or Section
3
for definition of Grassmann secant varieties.
In particular,
if
\dim A\leqslant\dim V
,

\sigma_{r}(X)={\mathbb{P}}(A\otimes V)

if and only if
r\geqslant\dim A
and
\sigma_{r,\dim A}(Y)=\Gr(\dim A,V)
.
However, this correspondence does not generalise to Grassmann cactus varieties
(Definition
3.1
).
If
\mathfrak{K}_{r}\left(X\right)={\mathbb{P}}(A\otimes V)
,
then
\mathfrak{K}_{r,\dim A}\left(Y\right)=\Gr(\dim A,V)
,
but not necessarily vice versa.
For instance, if
\dim A=m

and
Y={\mathbb{P}}^{m-1}\times{\mathbb{P}}^{m-1}\subset{\mathbb{P}}({\Bbbk}^{m}\otimes{\Bbbk}^{m})
,
so that

X={\mathbb{P}}^{m-1}\times{\mathbb{P}}^{m-1}\times{\mathbb{P}}^{m-1}\subset{\mathbb{P}}({\Bbbk}^{m}\otimes{\Bbbk}^{m}\otimes{\Bbbk}^{m})

then we know that

\mathfrak{K}_{g_{1}}\left(X\right)={\mathbb{P}}(A\otimes V)
for
g_{1}=6m-4
, while
\mathfrak{K}_{g_{2},m}\left(Y\right)=\Gr(m,V)
already for
g_{2}=3m-1
.
It seems that
3m-1
is closer to the best lower bounds for tensor rank known for any family of large tensors
[
LM25
]
.
This suggests the question if there is any version of the barriers that involves Grassmann cactus variety instead of cactus variety.
With this purpose in mind we prove some of our intermediate results in the more general setting of Grassmann cactus varieties.

1.6
Barrier breaking

Breaking the cactus barrier—that is, finding a method or bound for border rank that exceeds the analogous bound on border cactus rank—is strictly related to the problem of smoothability of finite schemes,
see
[
Jeli17
]
for an overview, or more generally with problems in deformation theory.
In particular, any result on smoothability should imply a corresponding result on breaking the barrier.
Conversely, any barrier breaking has consequences in deformation theory.

To the author’s knowledge there are currently only two results breaking the cactus barrier, both groundbreaking.
The first one
[
GMR23
]

exploits the known facts on smoothability and translates them to the language of tensors.
The second one
[
DM26
]

does not rely on deformation theory, instead hopefully in the future it will bring new information about smoothability of finite schemes.

2
Finite subschemes

In this section we gather facts about schemes that are needed to prove our main result.
We denote these schemes by
R
, perhaps with some decoration (for instance,
R^{\prime}
, or
R_{1}
,
R_{2}
,…, or
R_{t}
when we consider a family of schemes parametrised by
t\in T
for some base
T
).
Our main interest is in the case
R
is a finite scheme over
{\Bbbk}
and has a fixed embedding into a projective space
{\mathbb{P}}(W)
. Some statements cover a more general setting.

Suppose
X
and
T
are schemes over
{\Bbbk}
.
By a
flat family of subschemes
of
X
parameterised by
T
we mean a closed subscheme
{\mathcal{R}}\subset X\times T
such that the projection
\pi\colon{\mathcal{R}}\to T
is flat and surjective.
In this setting for each
{\Bbbk}
-point
t\in T

we denote by
R_{t}\subset X
the fibre
\pi^{-1}(t)
.
We frequently distinguish a special
{\Bbbk}
-point
0\in T
, and consider
R_{0}
to be the special fibre.
By
curve
we mean a smooth quasiprojective integral scheme over
{\Bbbk}
of dimension
1
.

The first claim is that on a smooth variety we can deform any finite subscheme into any open dense subset.
It appears to be a standard folklore result for which we have not found a reference.

Proposition 2.1
.

Suppose
X
is a smooth quasiprojecive scheme over
{\Bbbk}
,
U\subset X
is an open dense subset,
and
R\subset X
is a finite subscheme.
Then there exists a curve
T
, a
{\Bbbk}
-point
0\in T
and a finite flat family
{\mathcal{R}}\to T
of subschemes of
X
such that
R_{0}=R
and for all
t\in T\setminus{0}
we have
R_{t}\subset U
.

Proof.
We first prove the claim when
R
is local, that is, its support is a single

{\Bbbk}
-point
x\in X
.
Without loss of generality,
replacing
X
by the connected component containing
x
,
we may suppose

X
is connected, and let
n=\dim X
.
We may also suppose
X
is affine by replacing it by any of its open affine subsets containing
x
(and
U
is replaced by the intersection with this open affine subset).
Since
X
is smooth at

\phi\colon X\to{\mathbb{A}}^{n}
(defined by the local coordinate functions near

)
such that
\phi(x)=0\in{\mathbb{A}}^{n}

.
In particular,

is an isomorphism onto its image
\phi(R)\subset{\mathbb{A}}^{n}

Again replacing

by its open subset, we may assume in addition that
\phi
is etale on

.
Note that

Lemma 03WT
]

Pick any
{\Bbbk}

\tau\colon{\mathbb{G}}_{a}\times{\mathbb{A}}^{n}\to{\mathbb{A}}^{n}

Let
{\mathcal{R}}^{\prime}\simeq{\mathbb{G}}_{a}\times\phi(R)

Now suppose
R=R_{1}\sqcup\dotsb\sqcup R_{k}

with each

We find the curves

Lemma 2.2
.

Suppose
R
is a scheme, which is either:

•

a local scheme, or

•

a finite scheme.

Moreover, suppose
{\mathcal{E}}
is a vector bundle of rank
k
on
R
.
Then
{\mathcal{E}}
is trivial, that is
{\mathcal{E}}\simeq{\mathcal{O}}_{R}^{\oplus k}
.

Proof.

First suppose
R
is local, in particular, it has only one closed point
p\in R
and any (non-empty) closed subscheme of
R
contains
p
.
Let
U\subset R
be any open neighbourhood of
p
.
Then the complement of
U
is a closed subscheme which does not contain
p
thus the complement is empty and
U=R
.
The locally trivial sheaf
{\mathcal{E}}
is trivial on some open neighbourhood of
p
, hence it is trivial on
R
.

Now if
R
is finite (or more generally a discrete union of local schemes),
then the restriction of
{\mathcal{E}}
to each connected component
R^{\prime}\subset R

is trivial, and isomorphic to
{\mathcal{O}}_{R^{\prime}}^{\oplus k}
.
The trivialisations can be glued together to
{\mathcal{E}}\simeq{\mathcal{O}}_{R}^{\oplus k}
.
∎

Lemma 2.3
.

Suppose
R
is a finite scheme of length
r
,
B
is a vector space, and
{\mathcal{E}}
is vector bundle of rank
k
on
R
.
Assume
\varphi\colon{\mathcal{E}}\hookrightarrow{\mathcal{O}}_{R}\otimes B
is an embedding of vector bundles on
R
.
Then there exists a linear subspace
B^{\prime}\subset B
of dimension at most
k\cdot r
such that
\varphi
factorises

{\mathcal{E}}\hookrightarrow{\mathcal{O}}_{R}\otimes B^{\prime}\hookrightarrow{\mathcal{O}}_{R}\otimes B
.

Proof.

The vector bundle
{\mathcal{E}}
is trivial by Lemma
2.2
, since
R
is a finite scheme.
First we prove the statement for
k=1
, that is
{\mathcal{E}}\simeq{\mathcal{O}}_{R}
.
Passing to the projectivisations (which is possible, since
\varphi
is an embedding) we get a section
R\to R\times{\mathbb{P}}B
.
Consider the image under the projection to
{\mathbb{P}}B
.
The image of the composed map is a finite subscheme in
{\mathbb{P}}B
of length at most
r
.
Therefore its linear span
{\mathbb{P}}(B^{\prime})\subset{\mathbb{P}}B
is a (projective) linear subspace of dimension at most
r-1
, that is
\dim B^{\prime}\leq r
,
and
\varphi
factorises as claimed in the lemma.

If
k>1
is arbitrary, that is
{\mathcal{E}}\simeq{\mathcal{O}}_{R}^{\oplus k}
, then for each copy of
{\mathcal{O}}_{R}
we have a vector space of dimension at most
r
.
Taking as

the algebraic sum of these vector space we obtain the claim.
∎

Let
A

On the projective space

\phi\colon{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathbb{P}}(A\otimes B)}\otimes A^{*}\to{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathbb{P}}(A\otimes B)}(1)\otimes B

Explicitly, in coordinates, suppose
\left\{\alpha_{i}\right\}
,
\left\{b_{j}\right\}
,
\left\{\beta_{j}\right\}
and
\left\{\gamma_{ij}\right\}
are, respectively, bases of
A^{*}
,
B
,
B^{*}
, and
A^{*}\otimes B^{*}
.
Suppose further, that
\left\{\beta_{j}\right\}

is the dual basis to
\left\{b_{j}\right\}

and
\gamma_{ij}=\alpha_{i}\otimes\beta_{j}
.
Then the matrix of
\phi
in bases

\left\{\alpha_{i}\right\}
of
A^{*}
and
\left\{b_{j}\right\}
of
B
is the matrix
(\gamma_{ij})
of linear forms on
{\mathbb{P}}(A\otimes B)
.
In a coordinate free way,
over a point
[T]\in{\mathbb{P}}(A\otimes B)
for
\alpha\in A^{*}
,
up to rescaling
(which is possible to achieve consistently due to the twist by

{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathbb{P}}(A\otimes B)}(1)
),
we have
\phi_{[T]}(\alpha)=\alpha\lrcorner T\in B
,
where
\lrcorner
denotes the tensor contraction

A^{*}\otimes(A\otimes B)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\lrcorner}}{{\longrightarrow}}B
.

Lemma 2.4
.

Suppose
R
is any scheme with a morphism
f\colon R\to{\mathbb{P}}(A\otimes B)
and suppose
B^{\prime}\subset B
is a linear subspace.
Then the following two conditions are equivalent:

•

the scheme-theoretic image of
f
is contained in
{\mathbb{P}}(A\otimes B^{\prime})
,

•

the pullback
f^{*}\phi\colon{\mathcal{O}}_{R}\otimes A^{*}\to f^{*}{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathbb{P}}(A\otimes B)}(1)\otimes B

factorises through

{\mathcal{O}}_{R}\otimes A^{*}\to f^{*}{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathbb{P}}(A\otimes B)}(1)\otimes B^{\prime}\to f^{*}{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathbb{P}}(A\otimes B)}(1)\otimes B
.

Proof.

Consider
(B^{\prime})^{\perp}=\left\{\beta\mid\forall_{b^{\prime}\in B^{\prime}}\ \beta(b^{\prime})=0\right\}\subset B^{*}
.
Then
A^{*}\otimes(B^{\prime})^{\perp}\subset A^{*}\otimes B^{*}
is the set of linear equations of
{\mathbb{P}}(A\otimes B^{\prime})\subset{\mathbb{P}}(A\otimes B)
and it generates the homogeneous ideal of this linear subspace.
The scheme-theoretic image of
f
is contained in

{\mathbb{P}}(A\otimes B^{\prime})
if and only if
f^{*}(A^{*}\otimes(B^{\prime})^{\perp})=0
.
This vanishing occurs
if and only if
for all
\alpha\in A^{*}
and
\beta\in(B^{\prime})^{\perp}

one has
f^{*}(\alpha\otimes\beta)=0
.
Equivalently, for all
\alpha\in A^{*}
and
\beta\in(B^{\prime})^{\perp}

we have
\beta\bigl((f^{*}\phi)(\alpha)\bigr)=0
,
or more briefly
(B^{\prime})^{\perp}(f^{*}\phi)=0
.
The last vanishing happens if and only if the image of
f^{*}\phi
is contained in
f^{*}{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathbb{P}}(A\otimes B)}(1)\otimes B^{\prime}

as claimed.
∎

3
Cacti

Suppose
X\subset{\mathbb{P}}(W)
is a locally closed (hence quasiprojective) subscheme.
The main interest is in the case
X
is a smooth projective variety. For technical reasons and arguments we also need the case when
X
is not closed in
{\mathbb{P}}(W)
, only locally closed.
Furthermore, some definitions and intermediate results are also valid in the more general setting of singular varieties and even quasiprojective schemes.

Recall, that for projective variety
X
the
Grassmann secant variety
is:

\sigma_{r,i}:=\overline{\left\{E\in\Gr(i,W)\mid\exists x_{1},\dotsc,x_{r}\in X\text{ s.t.}E\subset\left\langle x_{1},\dotsc,x_{r}\right\rangle\right\}}.

Definition 3.1
.

Fix integers
r
and
i
.
The
r
-th
Grassmann cactus variety
of
X
is the closed subset
\mathfrak{K}_{r,i}\left(X\right)\subset\Gr(i,W)
defined by:

\mathfrak{K}_{r,i}\left(X\right):=\overline{\left\{E\in\Gr(i,W)\mid\exists\text{ subscheme }R\subset X\text{ s.t.}\dim R=0,\deg R\leqslant r,E\subset\left\langle R\right\rangle\right\}}.

The
generic Grassmann cactus variety

gen\mathfrak{K}_{r,i}\left(X\right)
of
X
is:

gen\mathfrak{K}_{r,i}\left(X\right):=\bigcap_{U\subset X}\mathfrak{K}_{r,i}\left(U\right)

where the intersection is over all dense Zariski open subsets of
X
.

In particular, if
i=1
then
\mathfrak{K}_{r,i}\left(X\right)=\mathfrak{K}_{r}\left(X\right)
is the cactus variety defined in (
1.1d
).

Theorem 3.2
.

Suppose
X\subset{\mathbb{P}}(W)
is as above and
X_{0}\subset X
is the
smooth locus of
X
. Then:

gen\mathfrak{K}_{r,i}\left(X\right)=\mathfrak{K}_{r,i}\left(X_{0}\right).

In particular,
if
X
is smooth then for any open dense

U\subset X
we have
\mathfrak{K}_{r,i}\left(U\right)=\mathfrak{K}_{r,i}\left(X\right)
.

Proof.
Since
X_{0}\subset X
is an open subset, by definition we have

gen\mathfrak{K}_{r,i}\left(X\right)\subset\mathfrak{K}_{r,i}\left(X_{0}\right)
.
Similarly to (
1.1c
), define:

\mathfrak{K}^{\circ}_{r,i}\left(X_{0}\right):=\left\{E\in\Gr(i,W)\mid\exists\text{ subscheme }R\subset X_{0}\text{ s.t.}\dim R=0,\deg R\leqslant r,E\subset\left\langle R\right\rangle\right\}.

Let
U\subset X
be any open subset.
Since
\mathfrak{K}_{r,i}\left(U\right)
is closed and
\overline{\mathfrak{K}^{\circ}_{r,i}\left(X_{0}\right)}=\mathfrak{K}_{r,i}\left(X_{0}\right)
, to prove the theorem it is enough to show that

\mathfrak{K}^{\circ}_{r,i}\left(X_{0}\right)\subset\mathfrak{K}_{r,i}\left(X_{0}\cap U\right)\subset\mathfrak{K}_{r,i}\left(U\right).

The second inclusion is clear.
To prove the first one,
take any
E\in\mathfrak{K}^{\circ}_{r,i}\left(X_{0}\right)
so that there exists a finite subscheme
R\subset X_{0}
of degree
r
such that
E\subset\left\langle R\right\rangle
.
Consider a flat family of subschemes
{\mathcal{R}}\subset T\times X_{0}
parametrised by a curve
T
with a special point
0\in T
, such that special fibre is
R_{0}=R
and for general
t\in T
the scheme
R_{t}
is contained in
X_{0}\cap U
.
This family exists
by Proposition
2.1
.
Then we have:

E\subset\left\langle R\right\rangle=\left\langle\lim_{t\to 0}R_{t}\right\rangle\subset\lim_{t\to 0}\left\langle R_{t}\right\rangle.

The last inclusion is the principle that

the linear span of a limit is contained in the limit of the linear spans
.
Although intuitively clear,
a formal proof requires some work

[
BJ17
, Prop. 5.29]
.
Note that the opposite inclusion may fail, as illustrated by famous example of three points in general position converging to three points on a line.

Locally trivialising
\left\langle R_{t}\right\rangle
over general points
t
, we can find a subbundle
E_{t}\subset\left\langle R_{t}\right\rangle
of rank equal to
\dim E
, such that
E=\lim_{t\to 0}E_{t}
.
Since each (general)
E_{t}
is in
\mathfrak{K}_{r,i}\left(X_{0}\cap U\right)
, and since
\mathfrak{K}_{r,i}\left(X_{0}\cap U\right)
is closed, it follows that also the limit
E
is in the same cactus variety, concluding the proof.
∎

4
Determinantal spines

In this section we restrict to the cactus varieties

\mathfrak{K}_{r}\left(X\right)=\mathfrak{K}_{r,1}\left(X\right)

as defined in (
1.1d
), or equivalently,
as in Definition
3.1
for
i=1
.

Theorem 4.1
.

Suppose
X\subset{\mathbb{P}}(W)
is a locally closed subscheme and
M
is a matrix, whose entries are linear forms on
W
(elements of
W^{*}
).
If
X
is contained in the locally closed locus

\left\{\rk M=k\right\}=\left\{\rk M\leqslant k\right\}\setminus\left\{\rk M\leqslant k-1\right\}\subset{\mathbb{P}}(W),

then the cactus variety
\mathfrak{K}_{r}\left(X\right)
is contained in the locus
\left\{\rk M\leq k\cdot r\right\}
.

By
[
Gałą23a
, Rem. 7.5]
, if we only assume
X\subset\left\{\rk M\leqslant k\right\}
the claim
\mathfrak{K}_{r}\left(X\right)\subset\left\{\rk M\leq k\cdot r\right\}
may fail.
Namely,
[
Gałą23a
, Ex. 7.4]
shows a singular variety
X
, a matrix such that
X\subset\left\{\rk M\leqslant 1\right\}

and a point
F\in\mathfrak{K}_{2}\left(X\right)
such that
\rk M(F)=3
.
Similar examples may be constructed if
X
is nonreduced.
However, if
X
is smooth,
then in Theorem
4.2
and Corollary
4.4

we show that such examples cannot be constructed.

Proof of Theorem
4.1
.

We may assume
k>0
as otherwise the statement is vacuous.
The
a\times b
matrix
M
determines a linear map
g\colon W\to A\otimes B
,
where
A
is an
a
-dimensional vector space and
B
is a
b
dimensional vector space.
We denote by the same letter the projectivised restriction
g\colon X\to{\mathbb{P}}(A\otimes B)
.
Note that
g\colon X\to{\mathbb{P}}(A\otimes B)
is a regular map, since
M(x)
is never
0
for
x\in X
.

On
{\mathbb{P}}(A\otimes B)
we have the natural map of vector bundles

{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathbb{P}}(A\otimes B)}\otimes A^{*}\to{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathbb{P}}(A\otimes B)}(1)\otimes B

as in Section
2
.
We denote by
\phi\colon{\mathcal{O}}_{X}\otimes A^{*}\to{\mathcal{O}}_{X}(1)\otimes B
the pullback of this map to
X
.
Note that our assumptions imply that
\phi
has constant rank
k
.
In particular, its image
{\mathcal{E}}
is a vector bundle on
X
.

Let
R\subset X
be a finite subscheme of length
r
.
Denote by
f\colon R\to{\mathbb{P}}(A\otimes B)
the composition of embedding
R\subset X
and
g
.
Consider the restriction
{\mathcal{E}}|_{R}
as a subbundle of
f^{*}{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathbb{P}}(A\otimes B)}(1)\otimes B
.
By Lemma
2.3
, there exists a linear subspace of dimension at most
k\cdot r

such that
{\mathcal{E}}|_{R}\subset f^{*}{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathbb{P}}(A\otimes B)}(1)\otimes B^{\prime}
.
By Lemma
2.4
this implies that
f(R)\subset{\mathbb{P}}(A\otimes B^{\prime})
.

Thus
\langle R\rangle
is contained in the linear space
g^{-1}({\mathbb{P}}(A\otimes B^{\prime}))\subset{\mathbb{P}}(W)
.
Next, since
\dim B^{\prime}\leq k\cdot r
, the linear space
g^{-1}({\mathbb{P}}(A\otimes B^{\prime}))
is contained in the locus
\left\{\rk M\leq k\cdot r\right\}
.
Therefore

\mathfrak{K}^{\circ}_{r}\left(X\right)=\bigcup\left\{\langle R\rangle\mid R\subset X,\dim R=0,\deg R\leqslant r\right\}\subset\left\{\rk M\leq k\cdot r\right\},

and since
\left\{\rk M\leq k\cdot r\right\}

is closed the claim of the theorem follows:

\mathfrak{K}_{r}\left(X\right)=\overline{\mathfrak{K}^{\circ}_{r}\left(X\right)}\subset\overline{\left\{\rk M\leq k\cdot r\right\}}=\left\{\rk M\leq k\cdot r\right\}.

∎

Next we focus on the case of
X
being smooth.
The main examples include Segre-Veronese varieties or Grassmannians.

Theorem 4.2
.

Suppose
X\subset{\mathbb{P}}(W)
is a smooth connected quasiprojective variety and
M
is a matrix of linear forms.
If
X\subset\left\{\rk M\leqslant k\right\}
,
then
\mathfrak{K}_{r}\left(X\right)\subset\left\{\rk M\leq k\cdot r\right\}
.

Proof.
Argue by induction on
k
.
If
k=0
, that is if
M|_{X}\equiv 0
,
then for any point
p\in\left\langle X\right\rangle

the evaluated matrix
M(p)
is also
0
,
because the entries of
M
are linear.
Clearly,
\mathfrak{K}_{r}\left(X\right)
is contained in the linear span of
X
, hence the conclusion.

For any
k>0
, let
U\subset X
be the open subset

U:=\left\{\rk M=k\right\}\cap X
.
If
U
is empty,
then
X\subset\left\{\rk M\leqslant k-1\right\}
,
and by induction,

\mathfrak{K}_{r}\left(X\right)\subset\left\{\rk M\leq(k-1)\cdot r\right\}\subset\left\{\rk M\leq k\cdot r\right\}
.
Thus we can assume
U\neq\emptyset

and therefore
U
is dense in
X
.
By Theorem
3.2
and the definition of
gen\mathfrak{K}_{r}\left(X\right)
we have:

\mathfrak{K}_{r}\left(X\right)=gen\mathfrak{K}_{r}\left(X\right)=gen\mathfrak{K}_{r}\left(U\right)=\mathfrak{K}_{r}\left(U\right).

By Theorem
4.1
we also have

\mathfrak{K}_{r}\left(U\right)\subset\left\{\rk M\leq k\cdot r\right\}
.
Combining the above, we get the claim
\mathfrak{K}_{r}\left(X\right)\subset\left\{\rk M\leq k\cdot r\right\}
.
∎

In the final steps we generalise Theorem
4.2
to the case where
X
is not connected.
First recall, that for two reduced subschemes
Y,Z\subset{\mathbb{P}}(W)
,
their join
Y+Z\subset{\mathbb{P}}(W)
is defined as:

Y+Z:=\overline{\bigcup\left\{\left\langle y,z\right\rangle\colon y\in Y,z\in Z\right\}}.

We choose the convention that
Y+\emptyset=\emptyset+Y=Y

(we treat
\emptyset\subset{\mathbb{P}}(W)

as the projectivisation of
\left\{0\right\}\subset W
).
Moreover, consistently with the convention in Definition
1.2
we set
\mathfrak{K}_{0}\left(X\right)=\emptyset
.

Lemma 4.3
.

Suppose
X_{i}\subset{\mathbb{P}}(W)
for
i=1,2

are two locally closed subschemes
and that
X_{1}\cap X_{2}=\emptyset
.
Let
X=X_{1}\cup X_{2}\subset{\mathbb{P}}(W)
and assume that
X_{i}\subset X
is open for both
i=1,2
.
Then:

\mathfrak{K}_{r}\left(X\right)=\bigcup_{q=0}^{r}\Bigl(\mathfrak{K}_{q}\left(X_{1}\right)+\mathfrak{K}_{r-q}\left(X_{2}\right)\Bigr).

Proof.
The
\supset
inclusion is clear.
Also both sides of the equation are closed, hence it is enough to prove

\mathfrak{K}^{\circ}_{r}\left(X\right)=\bigcup\left\{\langle R\rangle\mid R\subset X,\dim R=0,\deg R\leqslant r\right\}\subset\bigcup_{q=0}^{r}\bigl(\mathfrak{K}_{q}\left(X_{1}\right)+\mathfrak{K}_{r-q}\left(X_{2}\right)\bigr).

Thus it suffices to show that for each subscheme
R\subset X

we have
\langle R\rangle\subset\mathfrak{K}_{q}\left(X_{1}\right)+\mathfrak{K}_{r-q}\left(X_{2}\right)
for some
q
.
Indeed, let
R_{i}=R\cap X_{i}
and let
q=\deg R_{1}
.
Since
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
are disjoint, and
X_{i}\subset X
are open we must have
\deg R_{2}=r-q
.
Therefore
\left\langle R_{1}\right\rangle\subset\mathfrak{K}_{q}\left(X_{1}\right)

and
\left\langle R_{2}\right\rangle\subset\mathfrak{K}_{r-q}\left(X_{2}\right)
.
Further:

\left\langle R\right\rangle=\left\langle R_{1}\right\rangle+\left\langle R_{2}\right\rangle\subset\mathfrak{K}_{q}\left(X_{1}\right)+\mathfrak{K}_{r-q}\left(X_{2}\right)

as claimed.
∎

Corollary 4.4
.

Suppose
X\subset{\mathbb{P}}(W)
is a smooth locally closed subscheme and
M
is a matrix of linear forms.
If
X\subset\left\{\rk M\leqslant k\right\}
,
then
\mathfrak{K}_{r}\left(X\right)\subset\left\{\rk M\leq k\cdot r\right\}
.

Proof.
We argue by induction on the number
n
of connected components of
X
.
If
X
is connected, that is
n=1
, then the claim is identical to Theorem
4.2
.
If
n\geqslant 2
then write
X=X_{1}\sqcup X_{2}
with both
X_{i}
nonempty and open in
X
.
Then by the inductive assumption

\mathfrak{K}_{q}\left(X_{1}\right)\subset\left\{\rk M\leq k\cdot q\right\}
and

\mathfrak{K}_{r-q}\left(X_{2}\right)\subset\left\{\rk M\leq k\cdot(r-q)\right\}
for any
q\in\left\{0,\dotsc,r\right\}
.
For any points

F_{1}\in\mathfrak{K}_{q}\left(X_{1}\right)
and
F_{2}\in\mathfrak{K}_{r-q}\left(X_{2}\right)
,
we have
\left\langle F_{1},F_{2}\right\rangle\subset\left\{\rk M\leq k\cdot r\right\}

and hence
\mathfrak{K}_{q}\left(X_{1}\right)+\mathfrak{K}_{r-q}\left(X_{2}\right)\subset\left\{\rk M\leq k\cdot r\right\}
.
Therefore, the claim of the Corollary follows from Lemma
4.3
.
∎

We rephrase Corollary
4.4
as a barrier-type statement.

Corollary 4.5
.

Suppose
X\subset{\mathbb{P}}(W)
is smooth locally closed and nondegenerate (that is, not contained in any hyperplane),
and let
g
be an integer such that
\mathfrak{K}_{g}\left(X\right)={\mathbb{P}}(W)
.
Assume
M
be a matrix of linear forms.
Let
k:=\max\left\{\rk M(x)\mid x\in X\right\}
.
Then for every
p\in{\mathbb{P}}(W)
we have
\rk M(p)\leqslant k\cdot g
.

In particular, linear rank methods for bounding border rank cannot yield results beyond
r
in which
\mathfrak{K}_{r}\left(X\right)
fills in the ambient space.

Theorem
1.3
follows from Corollary
4.4
, as the smooth locus
X_{0}
will be a disconnected union of irreducible open subsets
X
.

Appendix A
Appendix: from sets to algebraic varieties

Suppose
\hat{X}\subset W
is any subset of a vector space
W
and for any
F\in W
define:

r_{\hat{X}}(F):=\min\left\{r\mid F\in\left\langle x_{1},\dotsc,x_{r}\right\rangle\text{ for }x_{i}\in\hat{X}\right\}

analogously to Definition
1.2
.
In this appendix we briefly explain that the barriers to linear rank methods also apply to this setting, where
\hat{X}
is not necessarily an algebraic set or variety.

Indeed, let
{\Bbbk}^{*}\cdot\hat{X}\subset W
be the set obtained as rescaling every element in
\hat{X}
.
Note that
r_{\hat{X}}(F)=r_{{\Bbbk}^{*}\cdot\hat{X}}(F)
for any
F\in W
.
Further let
X\subset{\mathbb{P}}(W)
be the set of projecive classes of elements in
\hat{X}
(or equivalently, in
{\Bbbk}^{*}\cdot\hat{X}
).
Finally, define
\overline{X}
to be the Zariski closure of
X
in
{\mathbb{P}}(W)
.

Then for any matrix
M
of linear forms on
W
and any
k
we have:

\hat{X}\subset\left\{\rk M\leqslant k\right\}\Leftrightarrow{\Bbbk}^{*}\cdot\hat{X}\subset\left\{\rk M\leqslant k\right\}\Leftrightarrow X\subset\left\{\rk M\leqslant k\right\}\Leftrightarrow\overline{X}\subset\left\{\rk M\leqslant k\right\}.

The first equivalence holds by linearity of entries of
M
,
the second is just descending to the projective space, and finally the third equivalence follows since
\left\{\rk M\leqslant k\right\}
is Zariski closed.
But now
\overline{X}
is algebraic set (reduced scheme), and we can apply the barrier theorems, for instance Theorem
1.3
. We conclude that using linear rank methods we will never get a better lower bound for
\hat{X}
-rank than generic cactus rank of
(\overline{X})_{0}
, the smooth locus of
\overline{X}
.
