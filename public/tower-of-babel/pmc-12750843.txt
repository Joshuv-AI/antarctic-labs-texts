Schwann cell Lrp1 deletion drives trigeminal neuron sensitization and orofacial pain by modulating mitochondrial function and TRPV1/TRPA1 activity
Authors: Zhiting Gong, Morgan Zhang, Naijiang Liu, Kesava Asam, Stefano Martellucci, Bradley Aouizerat, Wendy Campana, Yi Ye
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOrofacial pain, affecting 10–15% of adults, is a prevalent form of chronic pain that remains a major clinical challenge. The Schwann cell involvement in this pathophysiology is not fully understood. Low-density lipoprotein receptor–related protein 1 (LRP1) in Schwann cells has an unclear role in orofacial pain mechanisms.FindingsWe demonstrate that Schwann cell–specific conditional knockout of Lrp1 (scLrp1−/−) in mice leads to pronounced mechanical and thermal hypersensitivity in the orofacial region. RNA-seq of trigeminal ganglia (TG) from scLrp1−/− mice revealed broad changes in mitochondrial and metabolic pathways, reactive oxygen species (ROS) signaling, calcium homeostasis, and neurodegeneration-related processes. Altered mitochondrial function and ROS production in the TG were further confirmed with Seahorse metabolic flux analysis and biochemical assays. Additionally, mechano- and thermos-sensitive ion channels TRPV1 and TRPA1 are overexpressed and sensitized in the TG isolated from scLrp1−/− mice. Schwann cells isolated from scLrp1−/− mice displayed defective oxLDL uptake and excessive H₂O₂ release. Conditioned medium from LRP1 ablated Schwann cells induced orofacial hypersensitivity in vivo and robustly activated TG neurons in vitro in a TRPV1/TRPA1 dependent manner.ConclusionsOur results demonstrate that Schwann cell LRP1 safeguards mitochondrial function and supports neuron–glia metabolic coupling in the trigeminal system. The finding that LRP1 deficiency in Schwann cells drives orofacial pain in the absence of external insults highlights Schwann cells as active drivers, rather than passive amplifiers of chronic pain and identifies LRP1 as a promising target for orofacial pain management.Graphical Abstract Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s10194-025-02250-6.

Introduction

Orofacial pain encompasses a wide range of painful conditions arising from the oral and craniofacial regions, representing one of the most prevalent pain syndromes worldwide [1, 2]. The complexity of the facial region makes orofacial pain challenging to diagnose and manage [3]. According to the International Classification of Orofacial Pain (ICOP), orofacial pain can be categorized into six groups: pain in dentoalveolar and related tissues, muscle pain, temporomandibular joint (TMJ) pain, neuropathic cranial nerve pain, pain resembling primary headaches, and idiopathic pain [4]. While each condition has distinct molecular and cellular mechanisms, a common feature is that pain signals originate from trigeminal ganglion (TG) primary afferents and are transmitted to the nucleus caudalis of brainstem and from there, to other pain regulating regions of the central nervous system (CNS).

In parallel with neuronal activity, non-neuronal cells also contribute to orofacial pain by releasing cytokines, neurotrophins, metabolites, and nociceptive mediators [5–8]. In peripheral tissues, macrophages promote peripheral sensitization, whereas in the CNS, glial cells, including microglia and astrocytes, promote central sensitization [9]. These sensitization processes are often associated with oxidative stress, metabolic alterations, and mitochondrial dysfunction, as summarized by a comprehensive review on orofacial pain [10]. More recently, peripheral glia, specifically Schwann cells, have emerged as important regulators and potential therapeutic targets in chronic pain [11–14]. We and others have shown that Schwann cells engage in complex cross-talk with neurons, cancer cells, and immune cells, playing diverse roles in cancer-associated orofacial pain [15–18]. These findings suggest that Schwann cells may also contribute to non-cancerous orofacial pain, although this role remains underexplored.

Low-density lipoprotein receptor-related protein-1 (LRP1) is an emerging target for pain regulation and neuronal metabolic homeostasis. LRP1 is a multifunctional endocytic and signaling receptor with diverse, context-dependent functions [19–22] such as lipid and cholesterol metabolism, glucose utilization, cell growth, and survival. It is also involved in neuroinflammation, innate immunity, and astrocytic function. In Schwann cells, LRP1 is essential for mitochondrial homeostasis, regulating mitochondrial dynamics, structure, and respiration, thereby supporting survival, repair, and regeneration [23, 24]. Clinically, reduced LRP1 expression has been implicated as a potential biomarker for diabetic peripheral neuropathy diagnosis and progression [25]. In animal models, conditional deletion of LRP1 in Schwann cells produces pain-like behaviors in the hind limb and impairs sensory recovery after sciatic nerve injury [23, 26]. While these studies implicated LRP1 in the pain of the spinal region, genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have linked LRP1 polymorphisms to patients with migraine in Chinese, Pakistani, Dutch, and German populations [27, 28], strongly suggesting a role for LRP1 in trigeminal-mediated pain as well.

In this study, we investigated the role of Schwann cell LRP1 in regulating trigeminal ganglion neuron physiology. We found that Schwann cell–specific Lrp1 gene deletion induced orofacial mechanical and thermal hypersensitivity in mice. Infraorbital nerves (ION; the V2 branch of the trigeminal nerve) from conditional knockout (cKO) mice showed overexpression of proteins involved in the mitochondrial electron transport chain (ETC), indicating altered mitochondrial function. TG neurons from cKO mice exhibited widespread dysregulation of mitochondrial pathways, impaired cellular respiration, heightened TRPV1/TRPA1 activity, and neurodegeneration. Furthermore, LRP1-deficient Schwann cells were impaired in oxidized lipoprotein (oxLDL) uptake and released excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS), including H₂O₂ and carbonyl groups, which induced nociception and sensitized neuronal TRPV1 and TRPA1. Together, these findings identify LRP1 as a critical regulator of mitochondrial function and oxidative balance in orofacial Schwann cells. Loss of LRP1 in Schwann cells initiates a cascade of oxidative stress, ion channel sensitization, and mitochondrial remodeling in TG neurons, ultimately producing orofacial pain hypersensitivity.

Materials and methods

Mice

C57BL/6 mice were purchased from Jackson Laboratory. To generate mice in which Lrp1 was deleted specifically in Schwann cells (referred to as scLrp1-/- mice) but not neurons, we crossed mice bearing a floxed Lrp1 gene [29] with mice expressing Cre recombinase under the control of the Mpz promoter, the B6N.FVB-Tg (Mpz-cre) 26Mes/J line that Cre combines in relatively late embryonic age (E13.5-E14.5) [30, 31]. The Mpz promoter used in our studies has been shown to be inactive in CNS glia and neurons [30–32]. Genotyping was carried out at the NYU Langone Genotyping Core, performed by PCR using specific primers for the floxed Lrp1 allele, and hemizygous P0-Cre mice were identified by a 492 bp fragment amplified using the following primers: forward 5′-CCACCACCTCTCCATTGCAC-3′ and reverse 5′-GCTGGCCCAAATGTTCGTGG-3′. Age-matched scLrp1+/+ littermates were used as control mice.

Behavioral experiments were started in mice aged 8–12 weeks. Tissues for imaging, RNA-sequencing, and culture were collected in mice aged 10–14 weeks. All procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of New York University and conducted in accordance with the ARRIVE 2.0 guidelines for reporting in vivo experiments. Animals were housed in a temperature-controlled, pathogen-free facility under a 12:12 h light/dark cycle (lights on at 6:00 AM, off at 6:00 PM), with a maximum of five mice per cage and ad libitum access to food and water. Since only subtle behavioral differences were observed between male and female mice in our assays, subsequent experiments used male mice only.

Orofacial von Frey test

To ensure reliable behavioral outcomes, mice were handled and habituated to the testing environment for at least three sessions per week for two weeks, with each session lasting approximately 30 minutes. At the beginning of the formal tests, mice were placed in a transparent acrylic chamber and were habituated for 1 h before testing. Von Frey filaments (Bioseb, France 0.008–2 g) were applied perpendicularly to the V2 branch innervated skin, five times for 1–2 s with 10-s intervals in ascending order. Responses were scored on a five-point scale (0, no response; 1, detection: mice turn their head slightly upon application of the filament to the face; 2, reaction: the mice turn the head away quickly, pull it backward or react as a single face wipe; 3, escape/attack: the mice quickly escape from the filament or attack the filament by hand or by mouth, or wipe the face two times; 4, multiple face grooming: the mice respond to the filament simulation with more than 2 facial wipes continuously). The facial nociception score was calculated as the mean response score across the 0.08–2 g range.

In experiments involving Schwann cell supernatant stimulation, 10 μl scLRP1-/- or control Schwann cell supernatant was injected into the V2 innervated facial area. Behavioral testing was performed 30 min after injection.

Behavioral spectrometer

The Behavioral Spectrometer or the Box [33] comprises a 40 × 40 cm arena with a CCD camera mounted in the center of the ceiling and a door aperture in the front area of the arena. Mouse movement and behaviors are assessed by a floor mounted vibration sensor and 32 wall mounted infrared transmitter and receiver pairs. Mice were individually placed in the center of the behavioral spectrometer and their behavior was recorded, tracked, evaluated and analyzed using a computerized video tracking system (Viewer3, BiObserve, DE) for 15 min. The arena area was divided into an inner zone and outer zone, and time spent in the inner zone was determined.

Thermal test

Mice were placed in a plastic hand-held restrainer with head out. Mice were acclimated for 1 h before the recording. The left face was rotated towards a glass surface (25 °C) where a focused radiant heat was projected using a thermal stimulator (IITC Life Sciences). Time (in seconds) required to elicit a head withdrawal was recorded by a digital timer. A cutoff of 20 seconds was used to prevent tissue damage induced by the heat [34]. Head withdrawal latency was recorded as a mean of 3 trials at 5-minute intervals.

RNA-sequencing and data analysis

Total RNA was extracted from freshly frozen TG tissues using the Qiagen RNeasy Kit (Cat# 74104). RNA quality and integrity were assessed with an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer. Ribodepleted strand-specific libraries were prepared, and sequencing was performed by the NYU Langone Genome Technology Center, with raw data subsequently uploaded to the NYU HPC Greene platform. Quality control and adapter trimming were performed with fastp (v0.20.1). Clean reads were aligned to the GRCm39 mouse genome using HISAT2 (v2.2.1), and SAM files were converted to BAM format with SAMtools (v1.12). Transcript assembly was conducted with StringTie (v2.1.6) to generate a gene count matrix. Differential gene expression analysis was performed using DESeq2 (v1.36.0), with differentially expressed genes (DEGs) defined as those with |Log₂ fold change| ≥ 1 and Padj < 0.05. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was carried out using the ClusterProfiler package with GO and KEGG databases.

Schwann cell isolation and culture

Adult Schwann cells were isolated from the mouse right ION. Following dissection, the epineurium was carefully removed, and nerve segments (~0.5 cm) were enzymatically digested in collagenase type II (8 mg/mL; Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA; cat. no. 17101015) and dispase II (9.4 mg/mL; Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA; cat. no. 17105041) at 37 °C for ~30 min with intermittent gentle trituration. The partially dissociated tissue was further processed using the gentleMACS™ Dissociator (program m_brain_03.01; Miltenyi Biotec, Germany). Enzymatic activity was quenched by the addition of DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (DMEM; Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA; cat. no. 11965092). The resulting cell suspension was filtered through a 70-µm cell strainer (Corning, USA; cat. no. 352350) and centrifuged at 300 × g for 10 min.

Cell pellets were resuspended in Schwann cell growth medium consisting of DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS, forskolin (2 µM; Sigma-Aldrich, USA; cat. no. F6886/344282), and recombinant human Neuregulin-1 (NRG1, 1 nM; R&D Systems™, Bio-Techne, USA; cat. no. 9875-NR-050), and plated onto poly-D-lysine/laminin–coated culture dishes (poly-D-lysine: Gibco, USA, cat. no. A3890401; laminin: Gibco, USA, cat. no. 23017015). After 2–3 days, non-adherent cells were removed by medium exchange. To minimize fibroblast contamination, Schwann cells were selectively detached using a cold-shock flushing method: wells were rinsed with ice-cold culture medium, and loosely attached cells were dislodged by gentle pipetting. The enriched Schwann cell suspension was collected, centrifuged, and reseeded onto freshly coated dishes.

Cells were passaged at 70–80% confluence using trypsin-EDTA (0.05%; Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA; cat. no. 25300054) and replated under identical conditions. Schwann cell identity and purity were validated by immunocytochemistry for S100β (rabbit anti-S100β, Proteintech, USA; cat. no. 15146–1-AP).

Preparation of Schwann cell supernatant

Primary Schwann cells were seeded at 1 × 10^5 cells per well in 24-well poly-D-lysine/laminin–coated plates and maintained in growth medium for 48 h to allow attachment. Wells were then replaced with serum-free DMEM (500 µL/well). After 24 h at 37 °C/5% CO₂, the medium was collected and centrifuged at 300 g for 5 min (4 °C) to remove debris. The supernatant was transferred to low-binding tubes and stored at − 80 °C; used within 1 month and avoided repeated freeze–thaw cycles.

Primary TG neuron culture

Primary neuronal culture was performed as previously described [35], with modifications introduced to optimize the procedure for our study. Mice were deeply anesthetized with isoflurane and euthanized by decapitation. TGs were then dissected, enzymatically dissociated in a digestion solution containing collagenase type II (2 mg/250 µL HBSS; Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA; cat. no. 17101015) and dispase II (1.96 mg/250 µL HBSS; Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA; cat. no. 17105041) for ~20 min at 37 °C with intermittent gentle trituration. The resulting suspension was passed through a 100-µm cell strainer (Corning, USA; cat. no. 352360), washed with F-12 medium (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA; cat. no. 11765054), and further purified by Percoll™ density gradient centrifugation (28%/12.5%; Cytiva, USA; cat. no. 17-0891-01) to remove myelin and cellular debris. The pellet was subsequently washed and resuspended in F-12 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA; cat. no. 15140122). Cell viability was assessed by trypan blue exclusion (Sigma-Aldrich, USA; cat. no. T8154). Dissociated neurons were plated onto coverslips or plates pre-coated with poly-D-lysine (20 µg/mL; Gibco, USA, cat. no. A3890401) and laminin (15–18 µg/mL; Gibco, USA, cat. no. 23017015). Cultures were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂. TG neurons adhered to the substrate and extended neurites within 24–48 h, at which point functional assays were performed.

Seahorse XF mito stress test

Mitochondrial respiration was assessed using the Seahorse XF Cell Mito Stress Test Kit (Agilent Technologies, USA; cat. no. 103015-100) on an XF24 Extracellular Flux Analyzer (Agilent Technologies). TG neurons were isolated, seeded onto Seahorse XF24 cell culture microplates (Agilent Technologies, cat. no. 100777-004), corresponding to approximately one-fifth of a ganglion equivalent per well. Prior to assay, cells were equilibrated in Seahorse XF assay medium (Agilent Technologies, cat. no. 103335-100) supplemented with 10 mM glucose (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA; cat. no. A2494001), 1 mM sodium pyruvate (Gibco; cat. no. 11360070), and 2 mM L-glutamine (Gibco; cat. no. 25030081). Oxygen consumption rate (OCR) was recorded under basal conditions and following sequential injections of oligomycin (2 µM), FCCP (2 µM), and rotenone/antimycin A (0.5 µM each), supplied with the kit. Data were analyzed using Wave software (Agilent Technologies).

Calcium imaging

TG neurons were loaded with Fura-2 AM (Invitrogen, F1221, Carlsbad, CA, USA) (2–5 µM, 0.02% Pluronic F-127) for 30–45 min at 37 °C, followed by de-esterification for 20–30 min. Ratiometric imaging was performed at 340/380 nm excitation and 510 nm emission on an epifluorescence microscope. Neurons were perfused with a buffered saline (140 mM NaCl, 3 mM KCl, 2 mM CaCl₂, 1 mM MgCl₂, 10 mM HEPES, 1 mM glucose, pH 7.4) [36]. For experiments with Schwann cell supernatant, the buffer was replaced with serum-free DMEM. For neuronal sensitization experiments, cultured neurons were preincubated with Schwann cell supernatant for 12 h. Neuronal responses were quantified as changes in the Fura-2 ratio (F340/F380) relative to baseline. All calcium imaging experiments were conducted on an inverted fluorescence microscope (Eclipse Ti, Nikon, Tokyo, Japan) and analyzed using the accompanying software.

Drugs

Capsaicin (Sigma-Aldrich, M2028) and AITC (Sigma-Aldrich, 377430) were dissolved in ethanol to prepare stock solutions, stored at 4 °C, and diluted in calcium imaging buffer to final concentrations of 1 µM (capsaicin) and 10 µM (AITC) immediately before use. Vehicle controls consisted of calcium imaging buffer containing 0.01% ethanol.

Stock solutions of A425619 (Achemblock, Y229438) and HC030031 (Sigma-Aldrich, H4415) were prepared in DMSO, stored at − 20 °C, and diluted in recording buffer to their working concentrations (0.5% DMSO) immediately before experiments. Corresponding vehicle solutions contained calcium imaging buffer with 0.5% DMSO. N-acetylcysteine (NAC; Sigma, A7250) was dissolved in DMEM at 200 mM, adjusted to pH 7.4 with NaOH, and used as a stock solution. This stock was added to Schwann cell supernatant to yield a final NAC concentration of 5 mM. For in vivo studies, mice received i.p. injections (100 µL) of NAC (100 mg/kg in PBS), PBS vehicle, or a mixture of the TRPV1 blocker AMG9810 (Tocris, SO-1805088; 5 mg/kg) and the TRPA1 blocker HC-030031 (Sigma-Aldrich, H4415; 5 mg/kg) prepared in 20% DMSO/80% PEG. Vehicle controls were 20% DMSO/80% PEG alone. All injections were administered 1 hour before orofacial von Frey testing. Doses and formulations followed previously published mouse pain studies [37, 38].

Immunofluorescence staining

Mice were deeply anesthetized with inhaled isoflurane and euthanized by transcardial perfusion with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), followed by 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA; Sigma-Aldrich, USA; cat. no. P6148). TGs were dissected, post-fixed overnight at 4 °C, cryoprotected in 30% sucrose, embedded in OCT, and sectioned at 20 µm using a cryostat (CM1950, Leica Microsystems, Germany). Sections were mounted onto glass slides and air-dried, then washed twice with PBS for 10 min each before proceeding to immunostaining [39, 40].

Sections were permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 (Sigma-Aldrich, USA; cat. no. T8787) and blocked with 10% goat serum (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA; cat. no. 16210072) plus 0.2% skim milk (BD Difco™, USA; cat. no. 232100) for 1 h at room temperature. They were incubated overnight at 4 °C with rabbit anti-TRPV1 (1:500; Alomone Labs, Israel; cat. no. ACC-030) or rabbit anti-TRPA1 (1:500; Alomone Labs, Israel; cat. no. ACC-037), together with chicken anti-NeuN (1:1000; Millipore, USA; cat. no. ABN91). After washing, sections were incubated with Alexa Fluor® 594 goat anti-rabbit IgG (H+L) (1:400; Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA; cat. no. A-11012) and Alexa Fluor® 488 goat anti-chicken IgY (H+L) (1:400; Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA; cat. no. A-11039) for 1 h at room temperature in the dark. Nuclei were counterstained with Hoechst 33342 (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA; cat. no. H3570) for 5 min, and samples were mounted with Fluoroshield™ mounting medium (Sigma-Aldrich, USA; cat. no. F6182).

Fluorescent images were acquired using a Leica TCS SP8 confocal microscope (Leica Microsystems, Germany) under identical acquisition settings, and quantification of NeuN-colocalized TRPV1- or TRPA1-positive neurons was performed with ImageJ software (NIH, USA; version 1.54). The proportion of TRPA1+ or TRPV1+ cells among NeuN+ cells was compared between groups. At least three sections were analyzed per mouse, and the mean value from these sections was used as a single biological replicate. A minimum of 3 mice per group was used.

Western blot

Fresh ION and TG tissues were lysed in ice-cold RIPA buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Lysates were cleared by centrifugation (12,000 × g, 10 min, 4 °C), and protein concentration was determined using a BCA protein assay (Pierce™ BCA Protein Assay Kits, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) [41, 42]. Equal amounts of protein (20 µg per lane) were mixed with 4× Laemmli sample buffer containing reducing agent, heated at 50 °C for 30 min, resolved by SDS–PAGE, and transferred to 0.22 µm nitrocellulose membranes (NC). Membranes were blocked with SuperBlock™ Blocking Buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) for 1 h at room temperature and incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, followed by HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Signals were developed using ECL and imaged on a chemiluminescence system (model, manufacturer).

Primary antibodies and dilutions: MFN2 (Proteintech, #12186–1-AP, Rabbit, 1:1000); Phospho-PDH (pPDH) (Cell Signaling Technology, #31866, Rabbit, 1:1000); Total PDH (tPDH) (Cell Signaling Technology, #3205, Rabbit, 1:1000); Total OXPHOS Rodent WB Antibody Cocktail (Abcam, ab110413, Mouse, 1:1000); TRPV1 (Alomone Labs, Israel, ACC-030, Rabbit, 1:500); TRPA1 (Alomone Labs, Israel, ACC-037, Rabbit, 1:500).

Secondary antibodies: Anti-Rabbit IgG, HRP-linked (Cell Signaling Technology, #7074, 1:3000); Anti-Mouse IgG, HRP-linked (Cell Signaling Technology, #7076, 1:3000).

After detection of the primary targets, membranes were stripped with Thermo Scientific™ Restore™ Western Blot Stripping Buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), washed, and re-probed with either: Calnexin (Enzo Life Sciences, #ADI-SPA-860-D, Rabbit, 1:1000), or β-Actin (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, C4, sc-47778, Mouse, 1:3000).

Band intensities were quantified in ImageJ. MFN2, TRPV1, and TRPA1 were normalized to β-Actin or Calnexin as indicated. For PDH, the pPDH/tPDH ratio was calculated per sample; pPDH levels were additionally normalized to loading controls to confirm equal protein loading. For OXPHOS complexes, individual subunit bands from ab110413 were analyzed separately or summed and normalized to β-Actin. Data are presented as relative values compared with controls.

Hydrogen peroxide assay

Hydrogen peroxide release from primary Schwann cells was quantified using the Amplex™ Red Hydrogen Peroxide/Peroxidase Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat# A22188, USA). Schwann cells were seeded at a density of 10,000 cells per well in black-wall, clear-bottom 96-well plates pre-coated with poly-D-lysine and laminin and cultured for 24 h prior to the assay. Cells were washed once with Krebs buffer (145 mM NaCl, 5.7 mM NaH₂PO₄, 4.86 mM KCl, 0.54 mM CaCl₂·2 H₂O, 1.22 mM MgSO₄, 5.5 mM glucose, pH 7.35). The reaction mixture was freshly prepared by combining Amplex Red (50 μM) and horseradish peroxidase (0.1 U/mL) in pre-warmed Krebs buffer, and 50 μL was added to each well. Plates were incubated at 37 °C under 5% CO₂, and fluorescence was recorded at Ex/Em = 530–560/590 nm using a microplate reader (CLARIOstar, BMG Labtech, Germany). H₂O₂ concentrations were calculated from a standard curve and normalized to cell number.

OxLDL uptake assay

Oxidized LDL uptake in Schwann cells was evaluated using DiI-OxLDL (Invitrogen™, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat# L34357, USA). Primary Schwann cells were seeded at a density of 1 × 10^5 cells per 3.5-cm confocal dish (poly-D-lysine–coated) and cultured for 24 h to allow attachment, followed by serum-free culture overnight. Cells were then incubated with DiL-OxLDL (10 μg/mL) dissolved in Krebs buffer for 3 h at 37 °C. After incubation, live-cell imaging was performed using a confocal laser scanning microscope (Leica SP8, Germany). Hoechst 33342 was used to counterstain nuclei for cell identification. Quantitative analysis of DiL fluorescence intensity was performed using ImageJ.

Protein carbonyl content assay

Protein oxidative damage was assessed using the Protein Carbonyl Content Assay Kit (Abcam, Cat# ab126287, UK). ION and TG tissues were homogenized in RIPA lysis buffer supplemented with protease inhibitors. Protein carbonyl groups were derivatized with 2,4-dinitrophenylhydrazine (DNPH), followed by protein precipitation, washing, and solubilization according to the kit protocol. Absorbance was recorded at 375 nm using a spectrophotometer. Carbonyl levels were normalized to total protein concentration and expressed as relative values compared to control samples.

Statistics

Statistical analysis was performed using paired/unpaired t-test, Kruskal–Wallis, one-way ANOVA, and two-way ANOVA analysis of variance (GraphPad Prism 10.0). Proportion of cells among groups were compared using Chi-square. Results are expressed as Mean ± SEM. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Loss of Schwann cell Lrp1 leads to orofacial pain and anxiety-like behavior

To investigate the role of Schwann cell LRP1 in orofacial pain, we first tested orofacial pain behaviors in mice with Schwann cell (SC)–specific knockout of Lrp1 (scLRP1-/-). Primary Schwann cells from the scLRP1-/- mice IONs exhibited decreased LRP1 protein expression (Supplemental Fig. 1a, b), suggesting a successful knockdown of Lrp1 in orofacial Schwann cells. We then performed a series of behavioral assays to examine pain, anxiety, and locomotion activities in scLrp1-/- and scLrp1+/+ littermate controls. Mechanical and thermal nociceptive sensitivity in the orofacial area was assessed using the facial von Frey and Hargreaves tests, respectively. Additionally, the behavioral spectrometer test was used to assess anxiety-like behavior, locomotor activity, grooming, and itch-related behavior responses in scLrp1-/- mice. Compared to the control mice (scLrp1+/+), scLrp1-/- mice exhibited a significantly increased facial nociception score (Fig. 1a). When analyzed by sex, male scLrp1-/- mice displayed a significant increase in mechanical sensitivity whereas females showed a similar trend that did not reach statistical significance (Fig. 1a). Both male and female scLrp1-/- mice exhibited increased thermal hyperalgesia as indicated by delayed head withdrawal latency in the thermal test (Fig. 1b). In the behavior spectrometer, we demonstrated that scLrp1-/- mice spent significantly less time in the inner zone compared with controls. This reduction was more pronounced in females, while males showed a nonsignificant trend toward reduction (Fig. 1c). Representative tracking traces in the behavioral spectrometer are shown in Fig. 1g. Loss of Lrp1 in Schwann cells alters mitochondria homeostasis and increases oxidative stress in the ION

Previously, it has been shown that Lrp1 gene deletion in Schwann cells alters the number and morphology of mitochondria in sciatic nerves [23]. Sciatic nerves are known to be functionally and structurally different from the ION [43]. ION is the largest trigeminal nerve branch that innervates the orofacial region and is the most well studied nerve for orofacial neuropathies in animal models [44]. First, we quantified key mitochondria proteins in the ION to determine how Lrp1 gene deletion affects orofacial Schwann cell mitochondrial function. We found key mitochondrial proteins involved in ETC and oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS), including SDHB (complex II) and ATP5A (complex V) (Fig. 2a), were markedly increased in the scLRP1-/- group, suggesting impaired respiratory chain function. Other mitochondrial complex proteins like NDUDB8 (complex I), MTCO1 (complex IV), and UQCRC2 (complex III) were not changed (Fig. 2a). Loss of Lrp1 in Schwann cells alters neuronal mitochondrial function

To determine whether Lrp1 ablation in Schwann cells affects neuronal gene expression, we performed RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) of the TG. Volcano plot analysis identified numerous differentially expressed genes (DEGs), and hierarchical clustering of the top 100 genes demonstrated a clear separation between scLrp1-/- and scLrp1+/+ samples, confirming the robustness of the transcriptomic changes (Fig. 3a, b). The most significantly upregulated genes include Calb2, Ppp2cb, Cdk5rp1, Ngp, Chil3, with the most downregulated genes include Kcne4, Inmt, Emilin1, Cxcl10, Sall1. The known function of these genes spans calcium signaling, mitochondrial regulation, immune response, neuronal excitability and ion channel function, extracellular matrix remodeling, and cell survival. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis demonstrated that the most significantly affected categories were indeed mitochondrial-related processes and structures. In particular, pathways enriched in the cellular component (CC) include “mitochondrial inner membrane” and “mitochondrial protein-containing complex” (Fig. 3c). Schwann cell Lrp1 deletion induces enhanced neuronal TRPV1 and TRPA1 signaling due to impaired oxLDL uptake and increased ROS production

We next questioned whether Schwann cells lacking LRP1 release ROS species that are known to induce mitochondrial stress and activate and sensitize TRP channels. Altered mitochondrial ETC and OXPHOS are associated with the production of ROS such as hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) [50]. Excess H₂O₂ promotes the production of reactive protein carbonyl species and oxidized LDL (oxLDL), creating a feed-forward cycle of H₂O₂ accumulation [50]. H2O2, reactive protein carbony groups, and oxLDL, are all known to activate TRPV1/A1 and alter their expression [50, 51]. We isolated, cultured, and purified Schwann cells from the ION. S100 staining showed that 90% of the cultured cells are Schwann cells (Supplemental Fig. 2a, b). We then collected the supernatant from orofacial Schwann cells. Analysis of Schwann cell supernatant showed that scLrp1-/- cultures contained significantly higher levels of ROS byproduct H₂O₂ relative to controls (Fig. 6a). Reactive carbonyl groups are also increased in IONs from mice lacking Lrp1 (Fig. 6b). The presence of H₂O₂ and reactive carbonyl groups often lead to oxidation of membrane lipids such as LDL. Since LRP1 is a major receptor responsible for binding and internalizing LDL and oxLDL, and macrophages lacking LRP1 exhibited impaired ability to clear away oxLDL [52, 53], we next investigated whether LRP1 deletion in Schwann cells alters oxLDL clearance which may lead to oxLDL accumulation. In the oxLDL uptake assays, we found that primary Schwann cells lacking Lrp1 exhibited a significantly impaired capacity to internalize exogenous oxLDL compared with control cells (Fig. 6c). Pharmacological interrogation revealed that these effects were mediated via TRPV1 and TRPA1 channels (Fig. 7d–i). The concentration of TRPV1 antagonist A425619 and TRPA1 antagonist HC030031 was determined from preliminary screening experiments (Supplemental Fig. 3); these concentrations effectively suppressed TRPV1 and TRPA1 activation, as indicated by reduced responses to capsaicin and AITC, respectively. The TRPV1 antagonist A425619 reduced peak scLrp1-/- Schwann cell supernatant responses (Fig. 7d, e) without affecting the percentage of responding neurons (Fig. 7f). The TRPA1 antagonist HC030031 reduced both peak scLrp1-/-supernatant responses (Fig. 7g, h) and the percentage of responding neurons (Fig. 7i).

Discussion

The main finding of the present study is that deletion of Lrp1 in Schwann cells is sufficient to induce orofacial pain in mice, manifested as both mechanical and thermal hypersensitivity. Mechanistically, we show that loss of LRP1 alters mitochondrial function and metabolism in orofacial Schwann cells. This Schwann cell mitochondrial changes secondarily alters trigeminal neuronal metabolism, sensitizes TRPA1 and TRPV1 channels, contributing to axonal degeneration and a hypersensitive pain state.

The role of Schwann cells in chronic pain has gained increasing recognition. However, Schwann cells are heterogeneous, displaying anatomical and functional diversity depending on their regional and neural associations [58, 59]. A comprehensive molecular understanding of Schwann cell function in region-specific sensory systems is therefore critical. In mouse migraine models, neuronal CGRP stimulates Schwann cells to release ROS and NO, sensitizing trigeminal TRPA1 [60]. Similarly, oral cancer has been shown to induce myelin injury, stimulating Schwann cells to release cytokines and chemokines that drive mechanical pain [15–18]. However, these studies are disease-specific, making it difficult to determine whether Schwann cells initiate pain or merely amplify it. Our findings demonstrate that Schwann cells can, on their own, induce painful peripheral neuropathies in the absence of exogenous inflammatory or traumatic stimuli.

Our results also support the notion that Schwann cells and sensory neurons are metabolically coupled - a process that remains poorly understood. In the sciatic nerve, Schwann cell mitochondrial genes such as Cox, Tfam, and Lkb1 regulate axonal survival and myelination [61–63]. Moreover, sciatic Schwann cell mitochondrial dysfunction has been shown to alter neuronal metabolism [23, 61], consistent with our observations. Extending these findings, we show that metabolically impaired Schwann cells can promote neuronal hypersensitivity and metabolic reprogramming by increasing ROS production, which activates ROS-sensitive ion channels such as TRPV1 and TRPA1 in trigeminal neurons. Both TRPV1 and TRPA1 are mechano- and thermo-sensitive nonselective cation channels highly expressed in sensory neurons of the TG; ensitization of these channels has been strongly implicated in orofacial pain [48]. Prior work has shown that TRPV1 and TRPA1 can be activated by ROS, oxidized lipids, and lipid metabolites [64]. In line with this, our data show that TRPV1 and TRPA1 are hypersensitive in Lrp1-deficient mice, and that Schwann cell–derived ROS-containing supernatant induces nociception and neuronal excitation. These effects can be reversed by pharmacological blockade of TRPV1 and TRPA1. We also observed increased Calbindin 2 expression and enrichment of neurodegeneration-related genes in TG neurons of mutant mice. These results are consistent with the idea that TRPV1/TRPA1 hyperactivation drives calcium overload, mitochondrial remodeling, and excitotoxicity, leading to chronic pain and/or afferent degeneration [64].

Although the physiological role of LRP1 is still being elucidated, emerging evidence supports its neuroprotective, anti-inflammatory and anti-nociceptive functions. LRP1 activation has been shown to promote neuronal survival and regeneration following injury, as well as to enhance the clearance of toxic amyloid-β, a key protective mechanism against Alzheimer’s disease pathology [19]. Importantly, LRP1 activation has been shown to improve painful peripheral neuropathy [65], while decreased LRP1 expression has been proposed as a potential biomarker for diabetic neuropathy [25]. Our study extends these observations by identifying Schwann cells as a potential cellular target of LRP1-mediated pain regulation in the orofacial region.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, although TRPV1/TRPA1 hyperactivation appears to be a key mechanism driving neuronal metabolic reprogramming in the Lrp1-deficient model, we cannot exclude the possibility that Schwann cell mitochondria are directly transferred to neurons. Indeed, LRP1 has been implicated in astrocyte-to-neuron mitochondrial transfer [66]. However, since the ETC protein expression profiles differ between the ION and the TG, this suggests that although mitochondrial transfer may occur, it is unlikely to be the sole mechanism underlying TG-related mitochondrial changes. Second, Schwann cells may influence neuronal sensitization through mechanisms not explored in this study. For example, Lrp1-deficient Schwann cells may release growth factors, cytokines, or other lipid metabolites and ROS species that could further sensitize TRPV1/TRPA1 or activate other mechano-/thermo-sensitive ion channels in TG neurons. Furthermore, Schwann cells are known to express TRPA1 [11, 67], suggesting a potential ROS-mediated autocrine signaling pathway. Our gene deletion strategy may impact nociceptive terminal Schwann cells, which may directly alter mechanical and thermal sensation in the orofacial region. In addition, deleting Lrp1 in Schwann cells early in development may influence mechanosensory circuit maturation independent of the metabolic changes described here. Finally, because most mechanistic experiments were performed in male mice lacking LRP1, future studies are required to determine whether these pathways are conserved in females and whether neuronal hypersensitivity and orofacial pain can be reduced by restoring LRP1 in Schwann cells.

In conclusion, our findings establish Schwann cell LRP1 as a key regulator of mitochondrial integrity and neuron–glia metabolic coupling in the trigeminal system. By showing that loss of LRP1 in Schwann cells is sufficient to drive orofacial pain in the absence of injury or inflammation, this study highlights Schwann cells as an underappreciated driver of chronic pain and identifies LRP1 as a promising therapeutic target for orofacial neuropathies.
