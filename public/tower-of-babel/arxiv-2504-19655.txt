Symmetry-protected phases in a 1D active solid with mechanochemical feedback
Soumyadeep Mondal, Phanindra Dewan, Lakshman Santhosh Kumar, Sumantra Sarkar

Abstract
We present a framework for mechanochemical self-organization in active solids where elasticity is reciprocally coupled to Hopf oscillators. Our model reveals a rich landscape of symmetry-protected phases, identified through amplitude equations and group-theoretic analysis. We uncover a universal transition to compression-driven oscillation death (COD), providing a physical basis for localized signaling dampening in biological tissues that resolves inconsistencies in previous models. Our work demonstrates that complex self-organization in active solids can be classified purely through symmetry arguments.

Generality of the transition:

The existence of COD in the HHS with Brusselator is reassuring, but the generality of the result remains unclear. Fortunately, for coupled Hopf oscillators in a 1D periodic ring, we can use the so-called equivariant Hopf bifurcation theorems
[
19
,
20
,
28
]
to establish the generality. These powerful theorems state that the symmetry group of the oscillator network dictates the possible patterns and, crucially, the specific details of the Hopf oscillators
does not matter
, implying that the transitions predicted using the Brusselator should hold universally across all Hopf oscillators as long as the symmetry of the interaction and the network topology is preserved! To test this prediction, we replaced Brusselators with Fitzhugh-Nagumo oscillators
[
72
]
in the HHS, which recovered exactly the same phenomenology as
\mu
was varied (Fig.
2
C-D). Crucially, these results prove that complex biological morphologies and patterns can arise purely from the symmetries of the underlying interactions and does not require specific biochemical details. Hence, the results that we derive here for Brusselators should apply to more complex Hopf oscillators, such as ERK.

To understand the origin of the patterns, we perform a linear stability analysis (LSA) of Eqn.

Discussion:

In this Letter, we have resolved a fundamental ambiguity in the collective dynamics of active epithelial tissues: that mechanochemical feedback triggers COD.

By investigating a 1D periodic chain of coupled Hopf oscillators, we demonstrate that these patterns are not idiosyncratic to epithelial signaling. Instead, they emerge from the universal properties of mechanochemically coupled systems near a Hopf bifurcation, fundamentally constrained by the symmetry and topology of the interaction network. Crucially, our results suggest that the phase architecture predicted by our minimal model constitutes a universality class that governs complex biological networks regardless of specific biochemical details
[
32
,
57
,
59
]
. This robustness, which we have verified in a companion study
[
59
]
, implies that the underlying dynamical transitions are protected against local microscopic fluctuations (see End Matter) and the topology and the dimensionality of the system
[
59
]
. Beyond epithelial monolayers
[
62
,
12
,
37
,
9
,
63
,
13
,
57
,
71
,
59
]
, our framework provides a predictive lens for exploring spatiotemporal self-organization across a broad spectrum of synthetic active matter, from chemically active droplets to adaptive metamaterials
[
65
,
78
,
45
,
83
,
7
,

]
.

The search for universal principles in biological pattern formation remains a cornerstone of nonequilibrium physics
[

79
,

. Our findings establish that the “zoo” of biological patterns can be systematically classified into universal classes through the underlying symmetry and the topology of the active solids.

Noisy HHS:

In the main text, we explore HHS in the absence of noise. However, all biological systems are subjected to external and internal noise. Hence, here we assess the robustness of our results under stochastic fluctuations. To do so, we modified the
R
-equation, which describes the motion of the vertices.

\tau_{R}\,\partial_{t}R=\partial_{x}^{2}R-\partial_{x}L_{0}+\tilde{\eta}(x,t)

(8)

Here,
\tilde{\eta}(x,t)=\eta(x,t)-\frac{1}{L}\int^{L}_{0}\eta(x^{\prime},t)dx^{\prime}
is a conservative noise.
Subtracting the spatial mean ensures that the total length
\int_{0}^{L}\partial_{x}Rdx
remains conserved for a 1D periodic system. In the discrete implementation, this is imposed as

\displaystyle\tau_{R}\dot{R_{i}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(R_{i+1}+R_{i-1}-2R_{i})-(L_{i}-L^{0}_{i-1})+\tilde{\eta}

(9)

\displaystyle\tilde{\eta}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\eta-\frac{1}{N}\sum^{N}_{j=1}\eta_{j}

(10)

where,
\eta(x,t)
is a Gaussian white noise with,

\langle\eta(x,t)\eta(x^{\prime},t^{\prime})\rangle=2D\,\delta(x-x^{\prime})\,\delta(t-t^{\prime}),

(11)

where
D
denotes the noise strength.

On the use of the term “symmetry-protected phases”:

In this work, the term symmetry-protected phases refers to phases whose existence is determined by the symmetry of the mechanochemical interactions together with the translational invariance of the periodic ring. Because of the equivariant Hopf theorem
[
19
,
20
,
28
]
, these symmetries fix the admissible phase structure independently of the specific microscopic oscillator model. Consequently, the same phase diagram is obtained for both the Brusselator and FitzHugh–Nagumo models. Thus, the phase organization depends only on the underlying symmetry constraints and the topology of the oscillator network, and not on the detailed chemistry of the Hopf-oscillator dynamics.
It is in this sense, we use the term “symmetry-protected phases”, which is different from the term commonly used in quantum condensed matter
[
68
]
.

Near the dynamic phase transition boundaries, the state variables, such as

Single-cell Model

The single-cell model with mechanochemical feedback is written as:

\begin{gathered}\partial_{t}M=a-(b+1)M+cM^{2}E\\
\partial_{t}E=bM-cM^{2}E-DE\\
\tau_{D}\partial_{t}D=-(D-D_{0})-\beta D(L-1)\\
\tau_{L}\partial_{t}L_{0}=-(L_{0}-1)-\alpha(E-E_{0})\\
\tau_{R}\partial_{t}L=-(L-L_{0})+f(L,t)\end{gathered}

(S2)

Let us take
f(L,t)=0
for simplicity. We can linearize the equations above to write:

\begin{gathered}\partial_{t}\delta M=[-(b+1)+2cM_{0}E_{0}]\delta M+cM_{0}^{2}\delta E\\
\partial_{t}\delta E=(b-2cM_{0}E_{0})\delta M+(-cM_{0}^{2}-D_{0})\delta E-E_{0}\delta D\\
\tau_{D}\partial_{t}\delta D=-\delta D-\beta D_{0}\delta L\\
\tau_{L}\partial_{t}\delta L_{0}=-\alpha\delta E-\delta L_{0}\\
\tau_{R}\partial_{t}\delta L=\delta L_{0}-\delta L\end{gathered}

(S3)

The above equation can be written as:

\partial_{t}\begin{bmatrix}\delta M\\
\delta E\\
\delta D\\
\delta L_{0}\\
\delta L\end{bmatrix}=\underline{J}\begin{bmatrix}\delta M\\
\delta E\\
\delta D\\
\delta L_{0}\\
\delta L\end{bmatrix}

(S4)

where the Jacobian of the above linearized system of equations is given by:

\underline{J}=\begin{bmatrix}-(b+1)+2cM_{0}E_{0}&cM_{0}^{2}&0&0&0\\
b-2cM_{0}E_{0}&-cM_{0}^{2}-D_{0}&-E_{0}&0&0\\
0&0&-1/\tau_{D}&0&-\beta D_{0}/\tau_{D}\\
0&-\alpha/\tau_{L}&0&-1/\tau_{L}&0\\
0&0&0&1/\tau_{R}&-1/\tau_{R}\end{bmatrix}

(S5)

II.3.1
Construction of the Routh-Hurwitz table

A
5\times 5
Matrix has a 5th-order characteristic equation, as shown below.

a_{5}x^{5}+a_{4}x^{4}+a_{3}x^{3}+a_{2}x^{2}+a_{1}x+a_{0}=0

Here,
\{a_{0},\ldots,a_{5}\}
are the coefficients of the characteristic equations, from which we can construct the Routh-Hurwitz Table.

\begin{matrix}a_{5}&a_{3}&a_{1}\\
a_{4}&a_{2}&a_{0}\\
b_{1}&b_{2}&0\\
c_{1}&c_{2}&0\\
d_{1}&0\\
e_{1}\\
\end{matrix}

This table contains the Routh-Hurwitz coefficients,
\{b_{1},b_{2},c_{1},c_{2},d_{1},e_{1}\}
, which are obtained from the coefficients of the characteristic equation, as below.

b_{1}=-\frac{1}{a_{4}}\biggl|\begin{bmatrix}a_{5}&a_{3}\\
a_{4}&a_{2}\end{bmatrix}\biggr|=\frac{a_{4}a_{3}-a_{5}a_{2}}{a_{4}}

Similarly,

b_{2}=\frac{a_{4}a_{1}-a_{5}a_{0}}{a_{4}}

c_{1}=\frac{b_{1}a_{2}-a_{4}b_{2}}{b_{1}}

c_{2}=\frac{b_{1}a_{0}}{b_{1}}=a_{0}

d_{1}=\frac{c_{1}b_{2}-b_{1}c_{2}}{c_{1}}

e_{1}=\frac{d_{1}c_{2}}{d_{1}}=c_{2}=a_{0}

1D Continuum Model

To model a linear chain of cells, we consider each cell to be a spring whose rest lengths

can be coupled to the chemistry within it. If each cell is bounded by two vertices

\zeta(R_{i}(t+\Delta t)-R_{i}(t))=k_{i}(R_{i+1}(t)-R_{i}(t)-L^{0}_{i}(t))-k_{i-1}(R_{i}(t)-R_{i-1}(t)-L^{0}_{i-1}(t))

(S9)

If we take homogeneous stiffness constants,

\tau_{R}\partial_{t}R=\partial_{xx}R-\partial_{x}L_{0},

(S10)

where

is the vertex position and
L_{0}(x,t)
is the preferred length of the springs in the continuum limit. This is equivalent to the single-cell model, which can be seen by taking a spatial derivative on both sides of the equation.

\tau_{R}\partial_{t}(\partial_{x}R)=\partial_{xx}(\partial_{x}R-L_{0})

(S11)

This equation is a continuity equation of
\partial_{x}R
and arises from the conservation of the total length. This is a novel form of conservation law that arises in confluent tissues.

The time scale of relaxation,
\tau_{R}=\zeta/k
depends on the friction coefficient of the medium and the spring constant of the cells
[
12
]
. This is the continuum version of a 1D vertex model. This derivation is similar to the one in
[

]
.

As a minimal model of the chemistry, we consider a Brusselator model for the ERK pathways
[

]
. The Brusselator equations are:

\begin{gathered}\partial_{t}M=a-(b+1)M+cM^{2}E\\
\partial_{t}E=bM-cM^{2}E-DE\\
\tau_{D}\partial_{t}D=-(D-D_{0})\end{gathered}

(S12)

The species
E
and
M
are the oscillating species in the usual Brusselator. These two species are analogous to ERK and its regulatory protein MEK. Here, an extra term
-DE
is present because we consider a degrader

.
D
follows relaxation dynamics to

with a timescale of relaxation,

Now if we consider that the instantaneous lengths of the cells

affects the degrader
D
and the ERK level affects in the rest lengths

\begin{gathered}E\ \raisebox{-0.86108pt}{\rotatebox{90.0}{\scalebox{1}[1.2]{$\bot$}}}\ L_{0}\\

(S13)

then the equations for the chemical dynamics become:

\begin{gathered}\partial_{t}M=a-(b+1)M+cM^{2}E\\
\partial_{t}E=bM-cM^{2}E-DE\\
\tau_{D}\partial_{t}D=-(D-D_{0})-\beta D(\partial_{x}R-1)\end{gathered}

(S14)

The equations for the mechanics will also be modified and are written as:

\begin{gathered}\tau_{L}\partial_{t}L_{0}=-(L_{0}-L_{0}^{eq})-\alpha(E-E_{0})\\
\tau_{R}\partial_{t}R=\partial_{xx}R-\partial_{x}L_{0}\end{gathered}

(S15)

Finally, the 1D continuum vertex model is given by,

\begin{gathered}\partial_{t}M=a-(b+1)M+cM^{2}E\\
\partial_{t}E=bM-cM^{2}E-DE\\
\tau_{D}\partial_{t}\delta=-(\delta+\delta^{3})-\beta D(\partial_{x}R-1)\\
\tau_{L}\partial_{t}l=-(l+l^{3})-\alpha(\epsilon+\epsilon^{3})\\
\tau_{R}\partial_{t}R=\partial^{2}_{x}R-\partial_{x}L_{0}\end{gathered}

(S16)

Here,
a
,

, and

are the parameters of the canonical Brusselator, and E and M are the oscillating species. Here,
R(x)
is the location of the cell vertex, and
\partial_{x}R
is the continuum equivalent of the cell length,
L
.
\tau_{D},\tau_{L}
and
\tau_{R}
are the relaxation timescales. Moreover,
\delta=D-D_{0}
,
\epsilon=E-E_{0}
and

). The cubic terms are included due to the numerical stability and do not change the general behavior of the system.

III.1
Cubic term in the
\delta
and
l
equation

The cubic term in Eqn.
S16
is introduced as the minimal non-linear saturation needed to keep the amplitudes of the variables finite in non-linear simulations. In the absence of such terms, the linear equations correctly capture the onset of oscillatory or unstable modes, but the amplitudes of
\delta
and
l
can grow without limit under sustained coupling. Adding a cubic contribution, e.g.
-(\delta+\delta^{3})
is the simplest symmetric regularization that (i) leaves the linear stability unchanged, (ii) corresponds to the gradient of the potential function,
V(\delta)=\frac{1}{2}\delta^{2}+\frac{1}{4}\delta^{4}
. This choice follows standard practice in amplitude-equation theory (Landau expansion) and in related mechanochemical models
[
33
]
, where cubic saturation terms are used to regularize ERK dynamics. Furthermore, the nonlinear term in the potential function affects the quantitative location of the phase boundaries.

Where,

III.3
Exceptional point of the uncoupled system

The matrix
\underline{A}_{q}
is non-Hermitian, i.e.,
\underline{A}_{q}^{\dagger}\neq\underline{A}_{q}
. Consequently, its left and right eigenvectors are not the same. In the absence of Mechano-Chemical Coupling (MCC) (
\mu=\alpha\beta=0
), the matrix
\underline{A}_{q}
decouples into two independent blocks: a block
\underline{A}^{{chem}}_{q}
, which describes Brusselator dynamics and a block
\underline{A}^{{mech}}_{q}
, describing the mechanical part, spring network, represented as,

\underline{A}^{\text{chem}}_{q}=\begin{bmatrix}-1-b+c_{1}&c_{2}&0\\
b-c_{1}&-(c_{2}+D_{0})&-E_{0}\\
0&0&-1/\tau_{D}\end{bmatrix}

(S18)

\underline{A}^{\text{mech}}_{q}=\begin{bmatrix}-1/\tau_{L}&0\\
-iq/\tau_{R}&-q^{2}/\tau_{R}\end{bmatrix}

(S19)

The eigenvalues corresponding to the mechanical part is given by,

\begin{gathered}\lambda_{1}=-\dfrac{1}{\tau_{L}}\\
\lambda_{2}=-\dfrac{q^{2}}{\tau_{R}}\end{gathered}

(S20)

The corresponding right eigenvectors are given as,

\begin{gathered}\ket{R_1}=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{1+\dfrac{q^{2}/\tau_{R}^{2}}{(\frac{1}{\tau_{L}}-\frac{q^{2}}{\tau_{R}})^{2}}}}\left[1,\dfrac{\frac{iq}{\tau_{R}}}{\left(\frac{1}{\tau_{L}}-\frac{q^{2}}{\tau_{R}}\right)}\right]^{T}\\
\ket{R_2}=[0,1]^{T}\end{gathered}

(S21)

Now, Exceptional points are defined as the point at which two eigenvectors and eigenvalues collapse. The condition where the two eigenvectors collapse is given by,

q=\sqrt{\frac{\tau_{R}}{\tau_{L}}}
. At this value of
q
, the eigenvector
\ket{R_1}=\ket{R_2}
. However, at
\mu>0
the coupling between the mechanical and the chemical components results in a region of broken
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetry that ends at two exceptional points,
q_{ep}^{\pm}
.

The analysis is based on ref.

Consider a non-Hermititan matrix,

\begin{gathered}\lambda_{n}=\lambda^{0}_{n}+\epsilon\lambda^{(1)}_{n}+\epsilon^{2}\lambda^{(2)}_{n}+...\\

\bra{\chi_n}=\bra{\chi^{(0)}_n}+\epsilon\bra{\chi^{(1)}_n}+\epsilon^{2}\bra{\chi^{(2)}_n}+...\end{gathered}

(S23)

The perturbed equation is given by,

(H_{0}+\epsilon V)\ket{\phi_n}=\lambda_{n}\ket{\phi_n}

(S24)

Substitute the perturbative expansion from Eq.

\displaystyle\text{{Zeroth Order}}~(\epsilon^{0})

First Order (

\displaystyle:H_{0}\ket{\phi^{(1)}_n}+V\ket{\phi^{(0)}_n}=\lambda_{n}^{(0)}\ket{\phi^{(1)}_n}+\lambda_{n}^{(1)}\ket{\phi^{(0)}_n}

(S26)

\displaystyle:H_{0}\ket{\phi^{(2)}_n}+V\ket{\phi^{(1)}_n}=\lambda_{n}^{(0)}\ket{\phi^{(2)}_n}+\lambda_{n}^{(1)}\ket{\phi^{(1)}_n}+\lambda_{n}^{(2)}\ket{\phi^{(0)}_n}

S25

For second order eigenvalue correction, we project

III.5
The cubic term in the
\delta
and
l
equation

The cubic term in Eq.(3) is introduced as the minimal non-linear saturation needed to keep the amplitudes of the variables finite in non-linear simulations. In the absence of such terms, the linear equations correctly capture the onset of oscillatory or unstable modes, but the amplitudes of
\delta
and
l
can grow without limit under sustained coupling. Adding a cubic contribution, e.g.
-(\delta+\delta^{3})
is the simplest symmetric regularization that (i) leaves the linear stability unchanged, (ii) corresponds to the gradient of the potential function,
V(\delta)=\frac{1}{2}\delta^{2}+\frac{1}{4}\delta^{4}
. This choice follows standard practice in amplitude-equation theory (Landau expansion) and in related mechanochemical models
[
33
]
, where cubic saturation terms are used to regularize ERK dynamics. Furthermore, the nonlinear term in the potential function affects the quantitative location of the phase boundaries.

We expand the dynamics of a fixed point

Linearizing the local reaction subsystem

The operator

IV.2
Spectral Decomposition and Center Manifold

We decompose the vector field as
F(\mathbf{X},\gamma)=J\mathbf{X}+f(\mathbf{X},\gamma)
,
where
J=D_{\mathbf{X}}F|_{\mathbf{X}=0}
and

Evaluating the Jacobian at the homogeneous fixed point
\mathbf{X}=0

0&0&-\frac{1}{\tau_{D}}&0&-\frac{\beta D_{c}}{\tau_{D}}\\

0&0&0&0&0\end{pmatrix}.

(S43)

The Jacobian has eigenvalues

\lambda_{1,2}=\pm i\omega_{c},\qquad\lambda_{3}=0,\qquad\mathrm{Re}(\lambda_{4,5})<0,

(S44)

field), and the remaining eigenvalues lie in the left half-plane.

IV.3
Invariance Condition and Determination of

We seek the dynamics on
W_{c}
. The full system is

\partial_{t}\mathbf{X}=J\mathbf{X}+f(\mathbf{X},\gamma)+D\,\partial_{x}^{2}\mathbf{X}.

(S50)

We look for a reduced flow of the form

\partial_{t}y=Jy+g(y,\gamma),\qquad g=\mathcal{O}(|y|^{2}),

(S51)

where
g
is to be determined. To find both
h
and
g
, we enforce
that
\mathbf{X}=y+h(y,\gamma)
satisfies (
S50
) whenever
y

satisfies (
S51
). Differentiating
\mathbf{X}=y+h
with respect
to

,

\partial_{t}\mathbf{X}=\partial_{t}y+\frac{\partial h}{\partial y}\,\partial_{t}y=\left(I+\frac{\partial h}{\partial y}\right)\partial_{t}y.

(S52)

Substituting
\partial_{t}y
from (
S51
) on the left and

\mathbf{X}=y+h
into the right-hand side of (
S50
),

\left(I+\frac{\partial h}{\partial y}\right)\bigl(Jy+g(y,\gamma)\bigr)=J(y+h)+f(y+h,\gamma)+D\,\partial_{x}^{2}(y+h).

(S53)

The linear terms
Jy
cancel from both sides. Expanding all remaining
functions in multi-index Taylor series
[
39
]
,

f(y{+}h,\gamma)=\sum_{\mathbf{p},q}f_{\mathbf{p}q}\,y^{\mathbf{p}}\gamma^{q},\quad h=\sum_{\mathbf{p},q}h_{\mathbf{p}q}\,y^{\mathbf{p}}\gamma^{q},\quad g=\sum_{\mathbf{p},q}g_{\mathbf{p}q}\,y^{\mathbf{p}}\gamma^{q},

(S54)

where
\mathbf{p}=(p_{1},p_{2},p_{3})\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{3}

, and

|\mathbf{p}|+|q|\geq 2
throughout. Collecting (

)
at each order

[

(S55)

where

\Phi_{\mathbf{p}q}=f_{\mathbf{p}q}-\sum_{\mathbf{p}^{\prime},q^{\prime}}h_{\mathbf{p}^{\prime}q^{\prime}}\sum_{j}p^{\prime}_{j}\,\mathbf{u}_{j}^{*}\cdot g_{(\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{p}^{\prime}+\bm{\delta}_{j})(q-q^{\prime})}

here the sum over
\mathbf{p}^{\prime},q^{\prime}
runs over all indices for which

\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{p}^{\prime}+\bm{\delta}_{j}\geq 0
componentwise and

; since

Solvability and Resonance

Projecting (

Two cases arise.

Non-resonant

the prefactor is nonzero, so we choose

setting

and eliminating this term
from the reduced dynamics. For stable-subspace components of

Resonant

the prefactor vanishes for any

\textbf{u}_{i}^{*}\cdot h_{\mathbf{p}q}=0

(S59)

[

[

\left(J-\sum_{j}p_{j}\lambda_{j}\,I\right)h_{\mathbf{p}q}=-Q_{\mathbf{p}}\cdot\Phi_{\mathbf{p}q},\qquad R_{\mathbf{p}}\cdot h_{\mathbf{p}q}=0,

To incorporate diffusion and isolate slow spatial modulation, we

Multiplying by

R
satisfies

\partial_{t}R=a_{1}\gamma R+a_{2}zR+a_{3}z^{2}R+a_{4}z^{3}R+a_{5}R^{3}

(S68)

Assuming fast relaxation we set
\partial_{t}R=0
which gives

R^{2}=-\frac{a_{1}\gamma+a_{2}z+a_{3}z^{2}+a_{4}z^{3}}{a_{5}}

(S69)

Substituting this into the equation for
z
we obtain

\partial_{t}z=\nabla^{2}\left(b_{1}\gamma+b_{2}z+b_{3}z^{2}+b_{4}z^{3}\right)

(S70)

where the coefficients are

b_{1}=-\frac{a_{1}}{a_{5}}d_{ww}

(S71)

b_{2}=d_{z}-\frac{a_{2}}{a_{5}}d_{ww}

(S72)

b_{3}=-\frac{a_{3}}{a_{5}}d_{ww}

(S73)

b_{4}=-\frac{a_{4}}{a_{5}}d_{ww}

(S74)

The evolution equation can be expressed in the form of a conserved dynamics,

\partial_{t}z+\nabla\cdot\mathbf{J}=0,

(S75)

where the associated current is

\mathbf{J}=-\nabla\mu_{z},

(S76)

and the chemical potential is defined through the variational derivative

\mu_{z}=\frac{\delta\mathcal{L}[z]}{\delta z}.

(S77)

The corresponding Lyapunov functional is

\mathcal{L}[z]=\int dx\left[b_{1}\gamma z+\frac{b_{2}}{2}z^{2}+\frac{b_{3}}{3}z^{3}+\frac{b_{4}}{4}z^{4}\right]

(S78)

Its temporal evolution is obtained as

\displaystyle\frac{d\mathcal{L}}{dt}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int dx\frac{\delta\mathcal{L}}{\delta z}\,\partial_{t}z

(S79)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int dx\mu_{z}\partial_{t}z=-\int dx\mu_{z}\nabla^{2}\mu_{z}=-\int dx|\nabla\mu_{z}|^{2}\leq 0

Here integration by parts has been performed assuming periodic boundary conditions. Therefore, the functional
\mathcal{L}
decreases monotonically with time and acts as a Lyapunov functional for the dynamics.

Under periodic boundary conditions, the total mass of the real mode is conserved,

\int z\,dx=0.

(S80)

The dynamics is controlled by the local free-energy density

l(z)=b_{1}\gamma z+\frac{b_{2}}{2}z^{2}+\frac{b_{3}}{3}z^{3}+\frac{b_{4}}{4}z^{4}.

(S81)

As the parameters are varied, the local potential develops a double-well structure when

l^{\prime\prime}(0)<0,

(S82)

signaling the linear instability of the homogeneous state.

Because the dynamics decreases
\mathcal{L}
while preserving the conserved quantity

\int z\,dx=0,

(S83)

the system cannot relax toward a spatially uniform configuration. Instead, it approaches states that minimize
\mathcal{L}
subject to the conservation constraint.

For a non-convex free-energy density
l(z)
, the minimizing configurations correspond to coexistence between domains near the two minima of the potential, leading to phase separation.
We further observe that one branch satisfies

R^{2}>0

(S84)

corresponding to sustained oscillations while the other satisfies

R^{2}<0

(S85)

where only the solution
R=0
is allowed. This leads to coexistence of oscillatory and non oscillatory states characteristic of oscillation death.

IV.8
Generality of the reduced description

Although the derivation above was carried out for the Harmonic Brusselator model, the resulting mechanism is not specific to the details of the underlying chemical kinetics. Near a Hopf bifurcation, a generic oscillatory reaction system reduces, under center manifold reduction, to the universal Stuart–Landau equation governing the complex Hopf amplitude.

The essential effect of the mechanochemical feedback is the introduction of an additional slow neutral mode associated with the conserved mechanical degree of freedom. Consequently, the dynamics is governed by coupled amplitude equations for the oscillatory mode
w
and the conserved field
z
. The conserved field locally modulates the effective distance from the Hopf bifurcation, leading to phase separation between oscillatory and non-oscillatory states.

We therefore expect the emergence of oscillation-death patterns to be robust across a broad class of Hopf oscillators provided the symmetry and conservation structure of the coupling are preserved.

IV.9
Phase diagram construction

The phase diagram in Fig. 3(C) is constructed from the reduced amplitude equations by combining the amplitude condition
R^{2}(z)
with the effective potential
l(z)
. For each set of parameters, the classification is determined using the following criteria:

•

Amplitude condition:

We evaluate
R^{2}(z)
over the range of
z
. If
R^{2}>0
for all
z
, the system is classified as oscillatory. If
R^{2}<0
for all
z
, only the solution
R=0
is allowed, corresponding to amplitude death. If
R^{2}
changes sign, coexistence between oscillatory and non-oscillatory states is possible.

•

Energetic selection:

In the coexistence regime, we restrict attention to the regions of
z
where each branch is allowed and evaluate the local Lyapunov density
l(z)
within those regions. The corresponding states are retained only if
l(z)
attains minima at finite
z
with values lower than
l(0)
, ensuring that they are energetically favorable.

•

Instability condition:

We additionally require
l^{\prime\prime}(0)<0
, so that the homogeneous state is linearly unstable and small perturbations grow.

Together, these criteria determine the boundaries between oscillatory, amplitude death, and oscillation death regimes shown in Fig. 3(C).

V
Phase turbulence

To analyze the weakly nonlinear oscillatory regime, we consider the
long-wavelength dynamics of the Hopf amplitude
w
obtained from the
reduced amplitude equations derived in the previous section. In the
weak-coupling regime, fluctuations of the conserved mode
z
remain
small, and the leading-order dynamics of the oscillatory mode reduces
to the complex Ginzburg–Landau equation

\partial_{t}w=d_{w}\nabla^{2}w+(\lambda_{r}+i\lambda_{i})w+(g_{r}+ig_{i})|w|^{2}w,

(S86)

where

d_{w}=d_{r}+id_{i},\qquad g=g_{r}+ig_{i}.

(S87)

Equation (
S86
) is the universal amplitude equation
governing oscillatory media near a Hopf bifurcation

[
47
, Chs. 4–6]
. Following the standard phase
reduction procedure
[
47
, Ch. 7]
, we write the
complex amplitude in polar form,

w(x,t)=R(x,t)e^{i\Theta(x,t)}.

(S88)

For weak spatial modulation, the amplitude
R
relaxes rapidly to its
local limit-cycle value, while the phase
\Theta
evolves on a much
slower timescale. Eliminating the fast amplitude fluctuations yields
the nonlinear phase diffusion equation

\partial_{t}\Theta=\omega_{0}+D_{\Theta}\nabla^{2}\Theta+N_{\Theta}(\nabla\Theta)^{2}+\cdots,

(S89)

where

\omega_{0}=\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{r}\frac{g_{i}}{g_{r}},

(S90)

and

D_{\Theta}=d_{r}+\frac{g_{i}}{g_{r}}d_{i},\qquad N_{\Theta}=-d_{i}+\frac{g_{i}}{g_{r}}d_{r}.

(S91)

Higher-order gradient terms appear at next order in the long-wave
expansion and regularize short-wavelength modes, but are not required
for determining the onset of the instability.

The stability of the spatially homogeneous oscillatory state is
controlled by the sign of the phase diffusion coefficient

D_{\Theta}
. When

D_{\Theta}<0,

(S92)

long-wavelength phase perturbations grow, corresponding to the
Benjamin–Feir instability and the onset of phase turbulence

[
47
, Ch. 7]
.

For sufficiently weak coupling
\mu
, the system remains globally
oscillatory but develops irregular spatial fluctuations in the phase of
the Hopf mode. To characterize this regime, we project the full
dynamics onto the critical Hopf eigenspace using the adjoint
eigenvectors obtained in the center manifold reduction and extract the
slow phase field
\theta(x,t)
after subtracting the uniform Hopf
rotation.

Following Ref.
[
47
, Ch. 7]
, we define the
stationary phase fluctuation spectrum

S(k)=\left\langle|\psi_{k}|^{2}\right\rangle,

(S93)

where

\psi_{k}(t)=\frac{1}{L}\int_{0}^{L}dx\,\theta(x,t)e^{ikx},

(S94)

and
\langle\cdot\rangle
denotes a long-time average over the
statistically stationary state.

The spectra obtained from direct numerical simulations are shown in
Fig.
S11
. At low wavenumbers we observe the
approximate scaling

S(k)\sim k^{-2},

(S95)

consistent with the long-wavelength phase turbulence spectrum of the
Kuramoto–Sivashinsky phase equation

[
47
, Ch. 7]
.

To interpret this scaling, we introduce the phase-gradient field

v(x,t)=\partial_{x}\theta(x,t).

(S96)

In Fourier space,

v_{k}=ik\psi_{k},

(S97)

so that

\left\langle|v_{k}|^{2}\right\rangle=k^{2}S(k)\sim\mathrm{const.}

(S98)

for small
k
. Thus, the phase-gradient fluctuations are approximately
white in space,

\langle v(x)v(x^{\prime})\rangle\sim\delta(x-x^{\prime}).

(S99)

The phase field may therefore be expressed as

\theta(x)-\theta(0)=\int_{0}^{x}v(s)\,ds,

(S100)

from which the mean-square phase fluctuation becomes

\displaystyle\left\langle|\theta(x)-\theta(0)|^{2}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{x}\int_{0}^{x}\langle v(s)v(s^{\prime})\rangle\,ds\,ds^{\prime}

(S101)

\displaystyle\sim\int_{0}^{x}\int_{0}^{x}\delta(s-s^{\prime})\,ds\,ds^{\prime}

(S102)

\displaystyle\sim x.

(S103)

Thus, the phase undergoes random-walk-like spatial wandering,
resulting in the loss of long-range phase coherence characteristic of
phase turbulence.

VI

Chimera State Characterization

To characterize chimera states, we will compute
local order parameter

[
2
]
and
coherence fraction

[
70
]
. The local order parameter gives a local measure which changes over time whereas the coherence fraction here is a global measure which can either be shown to evolve with time or taken as an average over time. These measures require the phase of each oscillator, which is obtained from the ERK signal
E(t)
by converting it into an analytic signal
[
4
]
.

The local order parameter tells us whether an oscillator is in a coherent neighbourhood or not. For an oscillator
i
, it is defined as:

R_{i}(t)=\left|\frac{1}{2\delta}\sum_{|i-j|\leq\delta}e^{i\theta_{i}(t)}\right|

(S109)

where,

\displaystyle\theta_{i}(t)

\displaystyle=\text{phase of oscillator $i$}

\displaystyle\delta

\displaystyle=\text{neighbourhood of oscillator $i$}

And
R_{i}(t)\in[0,1]
, where

\displaystyle R_{i}\approx 1

\displaystyle:\text{oscillator $i$ is in coherent domain}

\displaystyle R_{i}\ll 1

\displaystyle:\text{oscillator $i$ is in incoherent domain}

The coherence fraction is the fraction of oscillators that belong to coherent domains at a given time:

g_{0}(t)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Theta(R_{i}(t)>\eta)

(S110)

where
\Theta
is the Heaviside step function and
\eta
is the threshold for
R_{i}
above which we consider oscillators to be coherent. We will use
\eta=0.95
in our calculations.

This can also be calculated as an average over time:

g_{0}=\left<g_{0}(t)\right>_{t}

(S111)

where, we can infer that:

\displaystyle g_{0}\approx 1

\displaystyle:\text{global synchrony}

\displaystyle g_{0}\approx 0

\displaystyle:\text{global incoherence}

\displaystyle 0<g_{0}<1

\displaystyle:\text{chimera state}

Lyapunov Exponents

Following Ref.
[
40
]
, for an n-dimensional continuous-time dynamical system,

\dot{\mathbf{x}}=\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x},t)\;\;\;\text{where,}\;\;\;\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},x_{2},...,x_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

The Jacobian of the system can be calculated as:

\mathbf{J}=\frac{\partial\mathbf{F}}{\partial\mathbf{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}

The infinitesimal perturbations in the system grow according to:

\dot{\mathbf{\xi}}=\mathbf{J}(\mathbf{x},t)\mathbf{\xi}

Now, if we start with orthogonal initial perturbations for the variables in the system,
\mathbf{\xi}(0)=(100...0)
, etc. and then write out the full equation as:

\dot{\mathbf{\Xi}}=\mathbf{J}\mathbf{\Xi}

(S112)

where,

\mathbf{\Xi}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&\cdots&0\\
\\
0&1&\cdots&0\\
\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\\
0&0&\cdots&1\end{pmatrix}

(S113)

Now, if we write
\mathbf{\Xi}=\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{R}
, the Lyapunov exponents for each component of
\mathbf{x}
are the corresponding diagonal elements of
\mathbf{R}
.

values and observed that
