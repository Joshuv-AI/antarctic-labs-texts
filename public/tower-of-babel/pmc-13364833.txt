Microwave-assisted synthesis of lithium-bismuth co-doped hydroxyapatite with enhanced antimicrobial and biological performance
Authors: Oyku Copur, Zafer Evis, Hasan Gocmez, Mustafa Tuncer, Sait Altun, Katia Barbaro, Anna A Zhukova, Iulian V Antoniac, Julietta V Rau, Aurel G Mohan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHydroxyapatites (HA) co-doped with Li⁺ and Bi³⁺ ions were synthesized by a microwave-assisted wet precipitation method to improve their structural, biological, and antibacterial performances. Dual doping of Li⁺ and Bi³⁺ ions led to an increase in crystallinity and crystallite size compared to single doping, indicating successful incorporation of dopants into the HA lattice. Co-doping also resulted in minor lattice distortions without altering the phase purity of HA. Biological characterization using adipose-derived mesenchymal stromal cells revealed that Bi incorporation enhanced cell proliferation and osteogenic activity, whereas excessive Li addition caused partial cytotoxicity. Interestingly, Li/Bi co-doping balanced this effect, leading to improved cell viability and differentiation compared to single-doped samples. Moreover, Li and Bi substitutions imparted significant antimicrobial activity against E. coli, S. aureus, P. aeruginosa, E. faecalis, and C. albicans, demonstrating the dual bioactive and antimicrobial nature of the material. The combined influence of Li⁺ and Bi³⁺ ions effectively modified the crystallinity and biological response of HA, suggesting that Li–Bi co-doped HA is a promising candidate for multifunctional coatings and bone tissue engineering applications.Graphical Abstract

Introduction

Hydroxyapatite (HA) is a fundamental material commonly used in bone defects and implants. HA, which forms the inorganic component of natural bone, exhibits close compositional and structural similarities to stoichiometric HA (Ca₁₀(PO₄)₆(OH)₂). In natural bone, the crystal lattice is structurally irregular and contains ion vacancies, where various ions such as boron (B³⁺), calcium (Ca²⁺), fluoride (F⁻), magnesium (Mg²⁺), phosphate (PO₄³⁻), silicate (Si⁴⁻), silver (Ag⁺), strontium (Sr²⁺), vanadium (V⁵⁺), and zinc (Zn²⁺) are present in trace amounts [1]. Thanks to their high biocompatibility, strong bioactivity, and excellent in vivo adaptation, HA-based materials have been extensively used for bone defect filling and bone grafting applications [2]. However, the inherent brittleness and limited mechanical strength of pure HA restrict its use in load-bearing applications, motivating researchers to explore various strategies for enhancing its structural and functional performance. One of the most effective approaches involves ionic substitution within the HA lattice, which can significantly alter its crystallinity, lattice parameters, and biological behavior [3].

Biofilm formation associated with bacterial infections is a widespread and significant public health concern. Biofilm formation is recognized as one of the primary mechanisms contributing to the rise in bacterial antibiotic resistance. For this reason, antimicrobial biomaterials have been designed to prevent and treat implant and tissue-engineered-related infections [4, 5]. The primary functions of antimicrobial biomaterials include antibacterial activity, inhibition of biofilm formation, and antifouling properties. These functionalities can be achieved through the rational design of material surfaces and are exemplified by systems such as nanotopographical surfaces, hydrogels, and drug-releasing coatings. [6–9].

Co-doped hydroxyapatites have been designed to better mimic the composition of natural bone mineral and to impart new functions, such as antibacterial activity and enhanced mechanical properties [10]. These materials are emerging as multifunctional biomedical systems, particularly suitable for infection-preventive treatments owing to their biocompatibility and antimicrobial properties [11]. For example, HA co-doped with Ag/Mg and Ag/Zn demonstrated 99% efficacy in antimicrobial assays, and it was observed that osteoblast proliferation significantly increased compared to HA doped with a single dopant [12]. In another study, nitrogen, an essential element for the human body, enhanced bioactivity when incorporated into HA. A thin film based on N/Br co-doped HA was fabricated and exhibited strong in vitro antimicrobial activity against both bacterial and fungal strains, confirming its high antibacterial efficacy [13]. Recent investigations have further explored a variety of co-doped hydroxyapatite systems, highlighting their considerable potential in bone tissue engineering and in enhancing both biological performance and antimicrobial functionality [14–16].

Among various substituent ions, lithium (Li⁺) and bismuth (Bi³⁺) have drawn increasing attention due to their unique effects on the physicochemical and biological characteristics of HA. The Li element is a promising candidate for enhancing the mechanical properties of HA as well as promoting osteogenesis and osteoblast proliferation [17]. The results obtained in a study conducted by Florian et al. showed that Li doped HA coated titanium implants induced an enhanced adhesion of the primary human mesenchymal stem cells (hMSC) as compared to HA-coated or non-coated implants [18]. Furthermore, it has been demonstrated that the cellular response strongly depends on the Li concentration in the HA lattice. Changes in surface topography induced by Li incorporation directly influence cell proliferation behavior. Low-level doping (0.5Li HA and 1Li HA) was found to slightly modify the surface morphology, leading to increased HA density and enhanced cell proliferation. However, higher Li substitution levels (2Li HA and 4Li HA) increased dissolution and Li⁺ ion release, which induced mild cytotoxic effects on MG63 cells, resulting in reduced proliferation and cell viability. These findings indicated that 1Li HA exhibited the optimum Li concentration for achieving the best biocompatibility and cellular proliferation performance [19].

Bi³⁺ is a heavy metal that is not toxic and has different chemical and physical properties compared to other elements, which is why it is widely used in many fields such as chemistry, physics, and materials science [20]. On the other hand, Bi3+ ions are known for their antibacterial and radiopaque properties [21]. Bi³⁺ can successfully replace Ca²⁺ in the HA lattice, resulting in slight lattice distortion and decreased crystallinity [22]. The effect of Bi³⁺ addition on cell viability has also been investigated in previous studies. A study examining HA samples doped with increasing amounts of Bi reported that all doped samples exhibited increased cell viability compared to the control, demonstrating that Bi³⁺ substitution did not induce any cytotoxic responses within the concentration range examined. Additionally, moderate Bi addition (0.11 mol Bi) maintained high cellular activity, while a slight decrease in viability was observed at higher levels (0.22 mol Bi), likely due to increased ionic release and local pH changes at higher substitution rates. Interestingly, further Bi addition (up to 0.44 mol Bi) restored or even improved cell viability, suggesting that cells adapt to the altered surface environment and that the optimal Bi concentration enhances adhesion and proliferation [23].

Despite the promising results of Bi and Li doped HA individually, studies investigating their dual incorporation remain scarce. The co-doping of Li⁺ and Bi³⁺ into HA is expected to combine the osteogenic stimulation induced by Li⁺ with the antimicrobial advantages provided by Bi³⁺, potentially resulting in a synergistic improvement in performance. Moreover, dual substitution is anticipated to influence the crystallinity, lattice parameters, and physicochemical stability of HA, thereby impacting its biological and mechanical behavior [24, 25].

In this study, lithium and bismuth dual-doped HA (Li-Bi HA) powders were synthesized via a wet chemical precipitation method. The effects of Li and Bi co-doping on the phase structure, crystallinity, and microstructural features were systematically analyzed using X-ray diffraction (XRD) and Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR). Biological evaluations were conducted, including MTT assays, osteogenic differentiation studies, and antimicrobial activity assessments. The aim of this work is to elucidate the correlation between dopant incorporation, structural modification, and improved biological performance, contributing to the development of multifunctional HA-based biomaterials for bone tissue engineering applications.

Materials and methods

Pure HA synthesis

The HA powder was synthesized using a microwave-assisted wet precipitation method under reflux conditions [26]. Firstly, calcium nitrate (Ca(NO3)2.4H2O) and ammonium phosphate ((NH4)2.HPO4) were dissolved in pure water to create two separate solutions. After the solutions were stirred separately for 1 h, the ammonium phosphate solution was added drop by drop to the continuously stirred calcium nitrate solution to form the final mixture. The solution’s pH was then adjusted to 10 using an appropriate amount of ammonia solution. Following vigorous stirring of the suspension for another 30 min, the resulting mixture was placed in a microwave oven and exposed to 800 W power for 15 min. After the microwave treatment, the mixture was filtered and washed with pure water until its pH reached 7. The filtered residue was dried in an atmospheric oven at 120 °C overnight, ground into fine powder particles (≤212 μm) using a mortar and pestle, and finally calcined in a muffle furnace at 500 °C for 1 h.

Bi and Li-doped HA synthesis

Based on the use of calcium nitrate tetrahydrate and diammonium hydrogen phosphate as precursors, bismuth nitrate pentahydrate (Bi(NO3)3.5H2O) was used for Bi-doped samples, and lithium carbonate (Li2CO3) was used for Li doped samples. Although Bi and Li doping processes were considered separately when selecting HA doping ratios, the rationale for their selection is similar. Bi³⁺ ions replace Ca²⁺ ions within the HA structure. Despite the larger ionic radius of Bi³⁺ compared to Ca²⁺ ions, this region is sufficiently flexible to accommodate the substitution. However, increasing Bi content can affect the lattice parameters, leading to lattice distortions. For this reason, a limit value of 50% was selected based on the literature [27, 28]. In contrast, for Li⁺ ions, the situation is different since the ionic radius is smaller. They occupy the Ca sites rather than replacing the Ca²⁺ ion. At low doping levels, Li incorporation induces only minor structural distortions; however, higher concentrations may affect crystallinity and promote the formation of secondary phases. Therefore, in the present study, the Li doping level was selected to be lower than that of Bi [29–31].

In the synthesis of ion-doped HA, two separate solutions were formed. First, the Li+ dopant, the Bi3+ dopant and calcium nitrate in the specified molar ratio were dissolved in pure water. The other solution was ammonium phosphate in the specified molar ratio, which was dissolved in pure water. After the solutions were stirred separately for 1 h, they were mixed using the same procedure corresponding to the synthesis of pure HA (P-HA). Then, the resulting mixture was placed in a microwave oven and exposed to 800 W power for 15 min. After the microwave treatment, the mixture was filtered and washed with pure water until its pH reached 7. The filtered residue was dried in an atmospheric oven at 120 °C overnight, ground into fine powder particles (≤212 μm) using a mortar and pestle, and finally sintered in a muffle furnace at 500 °C for 1 h.

In-vitro biological characterization

Adipose-derived mesenchymal stromal cells (aMSCs) were isolated from adipose tissue collected from a 24-month-old male horse immediately after slaughter. The adipose sample was extensively washed with sterile physiological saline to remove blood residues and impurities. The tissue was then finely minced using sterile scissors and subjected to enzymatic digestion with collagenase IA (Sigma-Aldrich, UK) to promote cell release. Digestion was carried out at 37 °C for approximately 60 min under gentle agitation. The resulting cell suspension was centrifuged at 800 × g for 10 min to obtain cell sedimentation. The cell pellet was resuspended in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM, Life Technologies, Paisley, UK) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Life Technologies, Paisley, UK) and subsequently seeded into culture flasks. Cultures were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO₂ until reaching ~85% confluence.

Second-passage aMSCs were detached using trypsin, counted, and seeded at a density of 20,000 cells/mL in 6-well plates. After 24 h, the medium was replaced with fresh growth medium containing 1 mg/mL of P-HA, 25Bi, 50Bi, 25Li, 25Bi25Li, or 50Bi25Li (sterilized by autoclaving at 121 °C for 20 min). Control wells received standard growth medium only. After an additional 24-hour incubation, the medium was removed and replaced with MTT solution (Sigma-Aldrich, UK) at a final concentration of 0.5 mg/mL in DMEM, followed by incubation for 3 h at 37 °C with 5% CO₂. Under these conditions, metabolically active cells reduced MTT to insoluble purple formazan crystals. After incubation, the solution was discarded, and 1 mL of isopropanol (Sigma-Aldrich, UK) was added to each well for 30 min at room temperature to solubilize the crystals. Optical density (OD) was measured at 600 nm using a BioPhotometer (Eppendorf, Hamburg, Germany), and values were compared to control cells maintained under standard conditions.

For differentiation assays, cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 50,000 cells/mL in both the treated and control groups. After 24 h of incubation at 37 °C, osteogenic differentiation medium containing ascorbic acid (50 µg/mL), β-glycerophosphate (10 mM), and dexamethasone (10⁻⁷ M) (all from Merck, Germany) was added. Treated groups received 1 mg/mL of P-HA, 25Bi, 50Bi, 25Li, 25Bi25Li, or 50Bi25Li, with or without osteogenic medium.

The positive control consisted of cells exposed to osteogenic medium only, while the negative control was maintained in standard DMEM. Cultures were incubated for 21 days at 37 °C and 5% CO₂, with medium renewal every 48–72 h to maintain nutrient supply and treatment consistency. Test compounds (P-HA, 25Bi, 50Bi, 25Li, 25Bi25Li, 50Bi25Li) were retained throughout the induction period. After 21 days, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Sigma-Aldrich, UK) for 30 min and stained with 3% Alizarin Red S (Merck, Germany) for 30 min to detect calcium deposits as an indicator of osteogenic differentiation. Quantification of calcium deposits was performed by treating samples with 5% sodium dodecyl sulfate in 0.5 N HCl for 30 min, followed by absorbance measurement at 490 nm using a BioPhotometer [34].

Antimicrobial activity was evaluated against four bacterial species (Escherichia coli (E. coli), Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus), Pseudomonas aeruginosa (P. aeruginosa), and Enterococcus faecalis (E. faecalis)) and one fungal species (Candida albicans (C. albicans)). Microorganisms were cultured in Brain Heart Infusion broth (BHI, DIFCO, USA) in the presence of 1 mg/mL of P-HA, 25Bi, 50Bi, 25Li, 25Bi25Li, or 50Bi25Li. Bacterial cultures were incubated at 37°C, whereas fungal cultures were maintained at 28 °C, all for 24 h. Microbial growth was assessed by measuring optical density at 600 nm with a BioPhotometer, and results were compared with untreated controls cultured in BHI without compounds.

Statistical analysis

Each experiment (MTT assay, osteogenic differentiation, and antimicrobial assay) was performed in triplicate to ensure data reproducibility. Data were analyzed using Dunnett’s test with SAS JMP Statistical Discovery v14 Pro software. Statistical significance was set at p ≤ 0.05 (*), p ≤ 0.01 (**) and p ≤ 0.001 (***).

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) analysis

The FTIR spectra of pure and doped HA samples recorded in the wavenumber range of 4000–400 cm⁻¹ are presented in Fig. 3. The spectrum of P-HA exhibited the characteristic absorption bands corresponding to phosphate (PO₄³⁻) and hydroxyl (–OH) functional groups, confirming the formation of the HA structure. Specifically, the stretching vibration of structural –OH groups appeared as a weak and broad band near 3572 cm⁻¹, and the corresponding bending vibration was observed at ~628 cm⁻¹. The phosphate group showed multiple strong peaks, including the asymmetric stretching vibration (ν₃) at ~1090 and ~1022 cm⁻¹, symmetric stretching (ν₁) at ~967 cm⁻¹, and bending modes (ν₄ and ν₂) at ~597, 563, and 474 cm⁻¹ [35, 36].

FTIR spectra of P-HA and Bi- and/or Li-doped HA powders sintered at 500 °C for 1 h. a HA; b 25Bi HA; c 50Bi HA; d 25Li HA; e 25Bi25Li HA; f 50Bi25Li HA

Upon doping with Bi and/or Li, these characteristic bands remained present in all spectra. However, noticeable changes in peak intensities, slight shifts in wavenumber positions, and band broadening were observed, particularly in the phosphate region (~1030 and 560 cm⁻¹). The most prominent changes were detected in the 50Bi25Li co-doped sample, where phosphate bands appeared broadened and slightly shifted.

MTT assay

Cell viability assessed by MTT assay revealed the effects of the different substrates (P-HA, 25Bi, 50Bi, 25Li, 25Bi25Li, 50Bi25Li) on the proliferation and metabolic activity of second-passage adipose-derived mesenchymal stromal cells (aMSCs) (Fig. 4). Optical density (OD) values, proportional to formazan production, were normalized to the control group (cell control, cells maintained in growth medium). Results showed a differential response depending on the tested substrate. Specifically, P-HA, 25Bi, and 50Bi increased cell viability compared to control, with relative growth values of 121.32%, 117.94%, and 115.14%, respectively. Conversely, treatment with 25Li and 50Bi25Li significantly reduced cell growth, with viability values of 72.46% and 73.06% of the control, respectively. These correspond to growth inhibition rates of 27.54% and 26.93%. The combined formulation 25Bi25Li showed intermediate effects, with viability at 106.15%, slightly above control levels. Overall, aMSCs displayed distinct responses to the different substrates, with high compatibility for P-HA, 25Bi, and 50Bi, intermediate effects for 25Bi25Li, and significant viability reduction for 25Li and 50Bi25Li.

Antimicrobial activity assessment

The antimicrobial properties of P-HA, 25Bi, 50Bi, 25Li, 25Bi25Li, and 50Bi25Li were evaluated against four bacterial species (E. coli, E. faecalis, P. aeruginosa, S. aureus) and one fungal species (C. albicans). Growth inhibition varied depending on the microorganism and substrate tested, showing selective patterns (Fig. 7). For E. coli, growth was significantly inhibited by most substrates, with the strongest effects observed for 50Bi25Li (−35.9%) and 50Bi (−32.3%), followed by 25Bi25Li (−31.1%). 25Li induced moderate inhibition (−21.3%), 25Bi lower inhibition (−15.7%), while P-HA showed only minor reduction (−4.6%). For E. faecalis, inhibition was less pronounced. P-HA slightly increased bacterial growth (+1.35%), while the highest reductions were achieved by 50Bi25Li (−30.2%) and 50Bi (−29.9%), with moderate effects for 25Bi25Li (−20.9%) and 25Li (−11.9%). The lowest inhibition was observed with 25Bi (−7%). Against P. aeruginosa, strong inhibition was recorded with 50Bi (−45.9%) and 50Bi25Li (−42.4%), followed by 25Bi25Li (−29.9%) and 25Li (−27.6%). P-HA and 25Bi displayed minimal effects (−5.5% and −15.3%, respectively). For S. aureus, the most effective substrates were 50Bi25Li (−30.8%) and 50Bi (−27.5%), while 25Bi25Li and 25Li showed moderate inhibition (−16.1% and −11.5%). 25Bi produced negligible effects, and P-HA slightly promoted growth (+3.9%). For C. albicans, inhibition was particularly pronounced with combined formulations, especially 50Bi25Li (−47.5%), followed by 25Bi25Li (−35.2%) and 50Bi (−34.6%). 25Li induced moderate inhibition (−18.8%), while 25Bi and P-HA showed weaker activity (−8.3% and −4.8%).

Discussion

The XRD results indicate that the crystalline HA structure was maintained upon Bi and/or Li doping, which means Bi and Li ions were successfully incorporated into the hydroxyapatite lattice without altering the primary hexagonal phase structure. The slight peak broadening and intensity reduction observed in Li- and Bi-doped samples suggest that ion substitution affected the crystallographic order of the HA lattice and may indicate a reduction in crystallite size and/or partial structural disorder. This observation is consistent with the FTIR results, where decreased band intensity and peak broadening were also detected. The p-HA samples' lattice parameters are consistent with literature-reported values for stoichiometric HA [37].

The increase in lattice parameters observed in Bi-doped samples reflects the incorporation of the larger Bi³⁺ ion (1.03 Å) in place of Ca²⁺ (0.99 Å). This led to a moderate expansion of the unit cell and structural distortion. A substantial reduction in crystallinity and crystallite size is likely, due to lattice distortion and the disruption of long-range order. Similar structural changes have been reported in previous studies on metal-ion substituted hydroxyapatite systems [33, 38].

Despite the small ionic radius of Li⁺ (0.76 Å), which might be expected to cause less lattice distortion, its incorporation can generate local strain or defect structures due to its high mobility and charge differences, thus negatively impacting crystal growth [35, 39, 40].

Interestingly, co-doping with low concentrations of Bi and Li resulted in improved crystallinity compared to the single-ion doped samples, particularly those with high dopant concentrations. In this case, the structural parameters (a=9.091 Å, c=6.866 Å) remained close to those of P-HA, suggesting that the low-level substitution of both ions may exert a compensatory effect, where the potential lattice distortions introduced by one ion are partially stabilized by the other. However, when the Bi concentration was increased, the crystallinity decreased again, consistent with the destabilizing effect of excess Bi.

These findings indicate that not only the ionic radius but also the dopant concentration and combination strategy play critical roles in determining the structural properties of doped HA. While both large (Bi³⁺) and small (Li⁺) ions can reduce crystallinity when introduced at high levels, careful tuning of the dopant ratios, particularly co-doping at low concentrations, can mitigate lattice distortion and preserve crystallinity, which is advantageous for maintaining the functional integrity of HA in biomedical applications.

The morphological images obtained from SEM are consistent with those reported in the literature [41, 42]. The EDX analysis provided important insights into compositional modifications induced by Bi and Li incorporation in the HA structure. In pure HA, the theoretical value of 1.67 indicates a chemically balanced structure. Upon Bi doping, a gradual increase in Bi content suggests that Bi ions were successfully incorporated into the HA structure, possibly substituting for Ca²⁺ ions. Although Li could not be detected by EDX due to the mentioned limitations in the Results section, the pronounced calcium-rich profile observed particularly in the 25Li HA samples suggests that the indirect evidence of its incorporation supports the Ca/P ratio. In co-doped samples, the Ca/P ratio approached the theoretical value, indicating a more stable substitutional balance. However, a slight calcium deficiency observed in the 50Bi25Li sample led to a decrease in the Ca/P ratio.

The presence of the characteristic FTIR bands in all doped samples indicates that the fundamental HA structure was retained, consistent with similar observations reported in the literature[43]. However, the variations observed in band intensity and slight shifts in peak positions suggest that the Li dopant may influence the local bonding environment within the HA lattice[29].

The absence of secondary phases, as confirmed by XRD and FTIR analyses, together with the preservation of the phase structure and functional groups after doping, indicates that the biocompatibility of the system is maintained. Moreover, the lack of significant morphological changes observed in SEM analyses suggests that the differences identified in the biological assays are not attributable to structural variations. Furthermore, EDX results confirm the incorporation of Bi and Li within the structure, which is likely responsible for the observed antibacterial activity and enhanced osteogenic performance.

The results of the MTT assay indicate that Bi-doped substrates are non-cytotoxic and may even promote cell proliferation, suggesting a favorable interaction between cells and the matrix that could enhance trophic signaling and biocompatibility. The reduced cell growth caused by Li incorporation in the system is indicative of partial cytotoxicity or interference with cellular metabolism. The improvement in cell viability observed in the combined formulation suggests that the Bi component may mitigate the negative influence of Li, likely balancing cell–matrix interactions. The MTT assay revealed that Bi-based substrates (25Bi and 50Bi) promoted aMSC viability, suggesting a favorable interaction between the material and the cellular microenvironment. In contrast, substrates containing only Li exerted less favorable effects, likely related to metabolic modulation, a phenomenon previously reported for biomaterials incorporating lipidic or organic components.

The mineralization results indicate that all substrates enhanced mineral deposition compared to the positive control, with a clear synergistic effect observed in the presence of osteoinductive medium. Because of the significant enhancement in mineralization observed for P-HA, it indicated intrinsic osteoconductive properties further amplified by the osteogenic environment. The most pronounced effect observed with Bi-doped HA highlights strong synergy between the substrate and chemical inducers. The highest mineralization observed for the combined formulation indicates intrinsic osteoinductive potential strongly enhanced by the osteogenic medium. In contrast, the minimal mineralization observed in the negative control (DMEM only) confirms the absence of spontaneous osteogenesis. Among the tested materials, 25Bi25Li demonstrated the highest osteoinductive potential. Substrates containing only Bi, particularly at high concentrations (50Bi), showed comparatively lower activity than Li-containing or combined formulations. An important methodological consideration in the interpretation of osteogenic differentiation concerns the type of analytical endpoint employed. In the present study, Alizarin Red S staining, coupled with quantitative spectrophotometric analysis, was used to assess extracellular calcium deposition. Although this assay is technically simple, it represents a highly informative and functionally relevant endpoint, as it directly measures the formation of mineralized extracellular matrix, which is a hallmark of advanced osteogenic differentiation. In contrast, molecular approaches such as gene expression analysis of osteogenic markers (e.g., RUNX2, ALP, osteocalcin) provide information on early or intermediate stages of the differentiation, but remain indirect indicators of the process. Indeed, transcriptional activation does not necessarily translate into effective matrix mineralization or a complete phenotypic commitment of mesenchymal stromal cells toward the osteogenic lineage. Within this context, the significant increase in calcium deposition observed in the present study, particularly in co-doped systems such as 25Bi25Li, supports the ability of these materials to promote a functionally advanced osteogenic response. Therefore, while additional molecular analyses would further strengthen the biological characterization, the current results already provide direct evidence of mineralized matrix production, representing a biologically meaningful indicator of osteogenic maturation.

The antimicrobial properties of all samples, according to the inhibitory effects observed against E. coli, varied depending on the dopant composition and concentration. E. Coli results suggested that high Bi concentrations, particularly in combination with Li, are most effective against this Gram-negative species. For E. faecalis, the increasing reduction in microbial growth observed with increasing Bi doping levels highlights the superior activity of high-Bi formulations. Against P. aeruginosa, the strong inhibition observed for formulations containing higher Bi concentrations underscores the relevance of high-Bi formulations in controlling this multidrug-resistant pathogen. Regarding C. Albicans, the pronounced inhibition observed in formulations both Bi and Li indicates a marked sensitivity of C. albicans to formulations containing both Bi and Li at high concentrations. In summary, 50Bi and 50Bi25Li displayed the most consistent and broad-spectrum antimicrobial effects, significantly inhibiting both bacteria, including P. aeruginosa, and fungi such as C. albicans. Composite formulations (25Bi25Li and 50Bi25Li) generally enhanced antimicrobial activity compared to individual components, suggesting synergistic interactions between Bi and Li. Collectively, these findings support the potential of Bi-enriched substrates, particularly when combined with Li as selective antimicrobial agents against clinically relevant pathogens. Taken together, the antimicrobial activity results showed that the formulations 50Bi and 50Bi25Li demonstrated the highest efficacy, significantly inhibiting the growth of E. coli, P. aeruginosa, S. aureus, E. faecalis, and C. albicans.

The antimicrobial activity observed in the present study can likely be attributed to a multifactorial mechanism involving both ionic and surface-related effects. A key contribution likely arises from the release of Bi³⁺ and Li⁺ ions from the doped HA, which can interfere with microbial cellular functions, disrupt membrane integrity, and impair metabolic activity, ultimately reducing cell viability. In parallel, doping-induced modifications of the HA structure, including lattice distortion, changes in crystallinity, and altered surface reactivity, may influence microorganism–material interactions, affecting adhesion and early biofilm formation. The enhanced antimicrobial performance observed in co-doped samples, particularly the 50Bi25Li composition, suggests a synergistic effect between Bi and Li. Moreover, the broad-spectrum activity against both bacterial and fungal strains indicates that the material acts through multiple concurrent mechanisms. Although the exact pathways were not directly investigated, these findings provide a plausible explanation for the improved antimicrobial efficacy of Li/Bi co-doped hydroxyapatite and support its potential as a multifunctional biomaterial for regenerative and anti-infective applications.

Conclusions

In this study, Bi and Li co-doped HA powders were successfully synthesized, and their structural, morphological, and biological properties were evaluated. The XRD results confirmed that all doped samples retained the hexagonal HA phase, while slight peak broadening and intensity reduction, particularly in 50Bi25Li and 25Bi25Li compositions, indicated minor lattice distortions and reduced crystallite sizes. SEM images demonstrated homogeneous morphologies with nanoscale features, with doped samples exhibiting slightly smaller average grain sizes than pure HA.

The MTT assay showed that Bi-based samples (25Bi, 50Bi) enhanced aMSC viability, indicating good material-cell interactions. In contrast, Li only samples reduced viability, likely due to metabolic modulation. Thus, balancing Bi and Li is crucial for optimal stromal cell-material interactions. The superior performance of mixed Bi+Li formulations suggests a molecular and microenvironmental synergy of considerable interest for the design of advanced scaffolds in tissue engineering. Osteogenic differentiation assays provided particularly compelling results. Among them, the 25Bi25Li sample emerged as the most promising, producing more than a fourfold increase in mineralization relative to the control, thus indicating strong intrinsic osteoinductive properties.

The findings of this study demonstrate that the investigated substrates exhibit high biocompatibility, osteoinductive potential, and antimicrobial activity. Collectively, these properties highlight their potential application as innovative platforms for bone regeneration in clinically challenging settings. Overall, the evidence indicates that the developed substrates are not merely biocompatible but rather multifunctional materials capable of simultaneously promoting cell growth, inducing osteogenic differentiation, and preventing infection.
