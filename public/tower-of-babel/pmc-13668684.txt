The unmet burden of secondary SCI complications: lost in translation
Authors: Marco D Sorani, Alexander G Rabchevsky
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSpinal cord injury (SCI) carries one of the highest disability weights among conditions tracked in the WHO’s Global Burden of Disease framework, yet federal research investment remains skewed toward locomotor restoration rather than the secondary complications—bowel and bladder dysfunction, autonomic dysreflexia, pressure injuries, and neuropathic pain—that people with chronic SCI consistently identify as their greatest daily burden. Drawing on patient perspectives, a systematic funding analysis, and two decades of survey data, this piece shows that SCI receives approximately 0.2% of NIH funding against roughly 1% of U.S. disease burden, and that within SCI research itself, movement/mobility studies (2017–2023) drew nearly twice the funding allocated to pain, bladder, bowel, and pressure-injury research combined. This misalignment persists despite a 2004 survey establishing that the functional priorities of people living with SCI diverge sharply from prevailing research goals. Secondary complications are argued to warrant treatment as primary research targets rather than downstream concerns, alongside compounding structural barriers: a shortage of trained SCI physiatrists, underpowered and poorly reproducible preclinical models, and inconsistent translation of lived-experience data into funding decisions. Researchers, clinicians, and advocates are called upon to realign scientific priorities, clinical training, and policy with the actual burden of chronic SCI, closing a gap that has persisted largely unaddressed for over twenty years.Subject terms: Translational research, Quality of life, Cooperation, Outcomes research

Patient perspectives

Marco Sorani has lived with a complete cervical (C6) spinal cord injury (SCI) for 32 years, enduring autonomic dysreflexia, painful bladder-related headaches, hospitalizations for heat exhaustion and urinary tract infections, rotator cuff tears, and shoulder/elbow pain. Early spasticity nearly threw him from his chair; as it subsided, muscle atrophy and leg edema increased, along with osteoporosis, yearly falls, lost toenails, and scoliosis. His greatest complication has been periodic bowel incontinence and constipation and the toll they exact on travel, work and peace of mind.

Sasha Rabchevsky has lived with a complete thoracic (T5) SCI for 40 years, studying the secondary consequences of paralysis, notably arguing that autonomic dysreflexia warrants research priorities comparable to locomotor recovery. His recent case history includes a septic stage-4 sacral pressure wound, renal dysfunction, a colostomy for fecal incontinence, pancreatic tumors requiring a Whipple procedure, and MRSA infection of his 2002 abdominal stimulator implant, spreading to its eight leads in his legs and backside. This required eight months of repeated out-of-state travel for staged surgical removal of each infected electrode, followed by final decubitus repair by an out-of-network provider.

While such experiences are anecdotal, collectively they mirror functional priorities identified in SCI patient preference studies. Most researchers are not unaware that people with SCI struggle with unpredictable bowel and bladder routines and many other conditions. But while scientific peers acknowledge the gap between lived reality and bench-side SCI studies, most remain predominantly focused on their chosen research models, offering clinical relevance only in the abstract.

Evidence base: the gap between research priorities and lived reality

There is rationale for continued investment in locomotor restoration to increase mobility and independence, as well as cardiovascular and respiratory health, muscle mass and bone density. In addition, clinical studies report that neuromodulation interventions often improve autonomic function simultaneously, if not exclusively. Although substantial research efforts have targeted bowel, bladder, cardiovascular, autonomic, and pain outcome measures, current funding distributions do not fully reflect the burden of these complications experienced by individuals living with chronic SCI.

We posit that secondary conditions should be regarded as primary research targets, studied as equitably as restoring the ability to locomote after SCI. More than two decades ago, a landmark survey exposed a disconnect: the functional priorities of people with SCI did not match research goals, which favored restoring locomotion above all [1]. Most people with chronic SCI are not pleading for a distant cure via regenerative medicine; they are asking for better management of relentless daily complexities, pressure sores, musculoskeletal deformation, neuropathic pain, respiratory decline, and autonomic dysreflexia, all of which add to the financial and psychological stress of living with paralysis.

As illustrated in Fig. 1, the acute period finds people with SCI focused on survival in the first weeks, often in an ICU after trauma like a car accident, open water injury, or spinal tumor diagnosis, sometimes extending for those on ventilators or fighting infections. After discharge, hope shifts toward regaining pre-injury function, especially motor control and independence from a wheelchair. The chronic period begins roughly a year post-injury, once neurological function plateaus; its length depends on age at onset: decades for younger people, shorter for older or more severely injured ones. Though generally healthy otherwise, hope of walking fades here, replaced by firsthand experience of secondary complications. The rapid aging period, beginning around age 40–50 depending on injury duration and severity, sees hope for recovery wane while impediments mount toward life-threatening severity.

Aging after SCI accelerates regardless of activity level. Yet there are no universal clinical protocols for management of secondary complications, and there is a shortage of trained SCI physiatrists [2]. Meanwhile, federal funding has flowed disproportionately toward locomotion research [3], marginalizing urgently needed studies and treatments for predictable secondary sequelae. This must change through restructured funding and incentives.

Based on our collective professional observations, there is a broad lack of awareness of SCI burden well beyond durable medical equipment and a deficiency of familiarity with bowel and bladder management among community practitioners. This article asks: is the public truly aware of SCI’s true burdens beyond highly cited studies? Why does research remain misaligned with community priorities? And what changes in research design, clinical training, funding, and policy are needed to close this gap? Change must begin now, if not for us, then for those who have yet to face these conditions.

Evidence base: SCI burden, funding, and research priorities

Disability weight is a single number between 0 and 1 that represents how severe a non-fatal health state is, relative to two anchor points: 0 = full health, 1 = a state equivalent to death. Cervical SCI carries the second-highest disability weight among 234 conditions in the WHO’s Global Burden of Disease framework, behind only acute schizophrenia (Fig. 2). Such a devastating impact is likely unknown to the public, scientists, and policymakers.

Despite this burden, SCI remains disproportionately underfunded by the NIH compared with other neurological disorders (Fig. 3). SCI represents roughly 1% of U.S. disease burden but receives only 0.2% of NIH funding, underfunded by a factor of five. Growth in NIH’s SCI budget has lagged the overall extramural budget [4]; SCI research received $986 million from 2013–2023, near the bottom of the sample. Most NINDS funding, via R01 grants, supports disease mechanisms, prosthetics, and biologic/pharmacologic therapies without translational milestones.

Bowel and bladder: the most burdensome complications

SCI is now recognized as a systemic disorder involving severe autonomic compromise, with higher thoraco-cervical injuries causing physiological compromise across all organ systems. Yet in funding and research, the 2004 survey’s findings still hold today [1]. A recent analysis of federal SCI funding [3] found that between 2017–2023, $225 million funded movement/mobility studies, almost twice the $130 million funding pain, bladder and bowel dysfunction, pressure injuries, and all other complications combined (Fig. 4).

Among all secondary dysfunctions, neurogenic bowel management is consistently identified as the most disruptive aspect of living with SCI, warranting new approaches in therapeutics, devices, surgical interventions, and data analysis such as “bowel-omics” [5]. Yet, a published rebuttal to Dr. Sorani’s “bowel-omics” proposal claimed such data collection was “premature without proven added value.” On the contrary, the need for these standards was evident then and is even more obvious now with the advent of advanced machine learning and AI tools. Neither bladder nor bowel protocols are universally taught in medical education.

Resilience—an individual’s psychological capacity to adapt—independently predicts bowel-related quality of life after SCI, separate from clinical severity [6]. But because not everyone demonstrates such resilience, and we don’t yet know in whom or how it manifests, patient-centered bowel management with enhanced professional education is needed [7]. Many people with SCI who experience even common GI dysfunction resort to colostomies for practical reasons despite the risks, since complication rates for this established procedure remain unacceptably high.

Neurogenic bladder management is only slightly less troublesome than bowel dysfunction, albeit more frequent, yet the social isolation chronic incontinence imposes goes unrecognized because it is managed behind closed doors. Given growing venture capital investment into translational studies like gene therapy for bladder dysfunction (EG427 data; SCI Ventures), government agencies and research foundations must consider redirecting funding to better align with disease burden and patient priorities.

Preclinical research: trapped in the valley of death

Fifteen years ago, it was reported that most NIH-funded preclinical replication SCI studies were underpowered and not reproducible [8] which has manifested in trivial findings shaping literature often based on weak statistical thresholds, leaving meta-analyses inconclusive. This is also often true of clinical studies [9]. A deeper problem: preclinical animal models poorly resemble human SCI. Certain dimensions of chronic human SCI, particularly long-term systemic and autonomic dysfunction, remain difficult to model adequately in rodents. Recent breakthroughs like epidural spinal cord stimulation emerged from clinical observation, yet clinical prediction models themselves remain underdeveloped—prompting calls for clinically grounded, real-world validation [10] with rigorous rehabilitation embedded in trial protocols [11].

Clinicians, scientists, and people living with SCI convened at the Craig H. Neilsen Foundation bowel and bladder workshop and reported momentum toward improvements [12]. While citing the Anderson survey (2004), the question remains: what consensus or incentives have followed to drive a change in research focus to target “secondary complications” as primary outcomes?

Policy implications: what researchers, clinicians, and advocates must do now

Researchers must redirect scientific translation toward dysfunctions of bowel, bladder, blood pressure, neuropathic pain, pressure injuries, and respiratory compromise that significantly erode quality of life after SCI. They must raise the bar for meaningful findings through stricter statistics, consistent endpoints, and scrutiny of rodent models, while building infrastructure for accountability and mandated data-sharing through repositories like the SCI Open Data Commons [13].

Clinically, there is a worsening shortfall in SCI medicine trainees in North America, with recent fellowship positions going unfilled [2]. Given the mounting costs of poorly managed chronic SCI, closing this gap requires more expert clinicians trained in autonomic dysreflexia, neurogenic bowel and bladder care, and complex UTI management. SCI clinicians must teach evidence-based protocols beyond model SCI centers, incorporate lived-experience feedback, develop clinical prediction models grounded in real-world outcomes, and ensure every patient receives a full discussion of treatment options across the continuum of care.

The SCI community and its advocates must push for accurate SCI prevalence data to replace outdated estimates skewed toward well-resourced urban centers, and for rigorous health economic analyses quantifying SCI’s true societal cost. They should insist that lived experience genuinely shape research priorities rather than being surveyed but insufficiently translated into funding priorities, since 2004. Moreover, they must highlight the toll on patients and caregivers who are forced to become their own best medical advocates for a complex condition with various syndromes that pharmaceutical companies often neglect due to low commercial profit potential.

Summary

The research and clinical challenges, and the hardships from complications deemed secondary, are a daily reality for hundreds of thousands of Americans and millions worldwide. The data and tools exist; what’s needed is the will to act. We call on scientists and clinicians to target “secondary complications” matching community priorities as primary research objectives to more readily manifest clinical translation and implementation.
