CRISPR/Cas9-engineered Bacillus subtilis chassis for tailored chitooligosaccharide production from marine waste chitosan
Authors: Jiayu Liang, Tengyue Liang, Chengjia Wei, Lanjuan Li, Yirong Li, Shengbin He, Zhihong Liao, Lanyu Cui
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study establishes a sustainable bioprocess for converting chitosan from marine waste into high-value chitooligosaccharides (COSs), offering an eco-friendly alternative to conventional methods that often generate chemical waste. We achieved heterologous production of chitosanase in an engineered Bacillus subtilis chassis by knocking out its endogenous chitosanase, leveraging the dual advantages of this bacterium as a robust synthetic biology platform and an industrial microorganism.ResultsThe endogenous chitosanase gene (BsCsn) in Bacillus subtilis WB800N was deleted via CRISPR/Cas9-mediated editing, generating the chassis strain B. subtilis WB800N ΔBsCsn. A codon-optimized GH46 chitosanase (CsnA) from Streptomyces coelicolor, fused to the AprE signal peptide, was then expressed in this host. Response surface methodology optimized the fermentation process, enabling a high extracellular CsnA activity of 540.08 ± 6.20 U/mL, in a 5-L bioreactor under DO-stat-controlled fed-batch conditions. This process achieved a productivity of 11.25 U/(mL·h) and a carbon conversion efficiency of 1682.86 U/g glycerol. Furthermore, MALDI-TOF MS analysis confirmed that CsnA produces COSs with defined degrees of polymerization (DP2-DP4).ConclusionThis integrated platform enables the upcycling of marine waste into high-value COSs, establishing B. subtilis as an eco-efficient cell factory and providing a valuable framework for the heterologous expression of other chitosanases in this host.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12934-025-02881-z.

Background

The global biomass of chitin exceeds 1011 tons annually [1], making it the second most abundant natural polysaccharide on Earth after cellulose. Chitosan, derived from chitin by partial deacetylation, serves as a substrate for chitosanase (EC 3.2.1.132), which specifically hydrolyzes β-1,4-glycosidic bonds to produce bioactive chitooligosaccharides (COSs) [2]. COSs exhibit diverse bioactivities, including eliciting plant systemic resistance, regulating postprandial blood glucose, and inhibiting tumor metastasis [3–5], which drive interest in their efficient enzymatic production [6]. The global COSs market reached US$92.5 million in 2023, with DP2-DP4 fractions commanding premium prices (> US$500/kg) in nutraceuticals [7]. Notably, crustacean shell waste represents a major renewable source of chitosan, with 6–8 million tonnes generated annually worldwide [8], yielding approximately 180 g chitin per kg of shell waste [9].

Compared to chemical hydrolysis, enzymatic production of COSs offers critical advantages, including mild reaction conditions (pH 4.0–7.0, 40–60 °C), precise control over product degree of polymerization (DP), and avoidance of hazardous waste and high energy consumption [10]. Although enzymatic yields are generally lower than those of acid hydrolysis, the substantially shorter reaction time and environmental benefits make it a preferred strategy. Moreover, chitosanases from different glycoside hydrolase families exhibit distinct product profiles. For instance, GH46 family enzymes predominantly generate DP2-4 oligomers, while GH75 members yield DP2-6 [11, 12]. This functional divergence underscores the importance of discovering and characterizing novel chitosanases with specific product spectra.

In this study, we focus on the GH46 chitosanase CsnA (SCO0677) from Streptomyces coelicolor A3(2) M145 [13], which shows high activity and substrate specificity. Although over 70 microbial chitosanases have been identified (CAZy database v2023), their industrial application remains limited by low yields (< 50 U/mL in wild-type strains), high purification costs due to intracellular expression, and instability during processing (> 30% activity loss within 24 h) [14]. Bacillus subtilis has been widely used as a microbial cell factory for enzyme and nutraceutical production, owing to its high protein secretion capacity, minimal fermentation requirements, and GRAS status [15]. Advances in synthetic biology have further enhanced its potential as a chassis for heterologous protein expression [16, 17]. However, the native enzymatic background of B. subtilis, including the presence of an endogenous chitosanase gene (BsCsn), may interfere with the activity and product specificity of heterologous expressed chitosanases. To address this, we selected the protease-deficient strain B. subtilis WB800N, which lacks eight major extracellular proteases and significantly improves the functional half-life of secreted proteins [18]. As a food-grade safe microorganism (FDA GRAS No. 000153), B. subtilis also offers advantages in secretion system versatility and scalability, as demonstrated in the production of proteins such as cyclodextrin glucosyltransferase [19] and nattokinase [20]. To establish a defined background for exclusive production of the heterologous chitosanase, we first knocked out the endogenous BsCsn gene via CRISPR/Cas9, generating B. subtilis WB800N ΔBsCsn. We then implemented a multi-pronged optimization strategy, including: (1) codon optimization of CsnA (increasing CAI from 0.69 to 0.99); (2) use of an AprE signal peptide–propeptide fusion system to enhance secretion; and (3) a dynamic dissolved oxygen (DO)-stat feedback-controlled glycerol feeding strategy, which achieved a carbon conversion yield of 1682.86 U/g glycerol. This approach resulted in an extracellular CsnA activity of 540.08 ± 6.20 U/mL in a 5-L fermenter—a 2.14-fold increase over shake-flask cultivation. Product analysis via MALDI-TOF MS confirmed endo-type hydrolysis, yielding primarily chitobiose (DP2) to chitotetraose (DP4), a profile distinct from that of other GH46 chitosanases such as the one from Streptomyces sp. SirexAA-E (predominantly DP3–6) [21].

Our work establishes a robust and efficient platform for the secretory production of a Streptomyces-derived chitosanase in an engineered B. subtilis chassis, enabling the tailored synthesis of specific COSs from marine waste chitosan. This study highlights the potential of synthetic biology-driven chassis engineering to overcome native host limitations and advance the bioconversion of renewable resources into high-value products.

Materials and methods

Experimental materials

Chitin and D-(+)-Glucosamine hydrochloride (DP1) were obtained from Beijing Coolaber Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). Chitosan (from waste shrimp chaff, deacetylation degree ≥ 95%, viscosity average molecular weight ~ 500 kDa); chitosan samples (from waste shrimp chaff, deacetylation degree: ≥ 90%, ≥ 85%, 80%, ≥ 75%, 70%, viscosity average molecular weight ~ 500 kDa) were purchased from Jiangsu Chitin Biotech Co., Ltd. (Yancheng, China). N, O-Carboxymethyl chitosan was purchased from Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). COSs standards (DP2-DP5) were sourced from Qingdao Bozhihuili Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Qingdao, China). Gibson Assembly® Master Mix was purchased from New England Biolabs (Ipswich, MA, USA). Pfu High-Fidelity DNA Polymerase Premix was acquired from TransGen Biotech Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). Restriction enzymes were supplied by Thermo Fisher Scientific (Waltham, MA, USA).

Media and culture conditions

Shake-flask cultivations were performed at 37 °C with agitation at 200 rpm. Media compositions: (1) LB medium (g/L): Tryptone 10.0, yeast extract 5.0, NaCl 10.0 (pH 7.0). (2) M9 minimal medium (g/L): 5 × salt solution (Na2HPO4·7H2O 64.0, KH2PO4 15.0, NaCl 2.5, NH4Cl 5.0) sterilized and diluted to 1 × concentration, supplemented with MgSO4 0.241, CaCl2 0.011, and glucose 4.0 (pH 7.0). (3) 2 × YT medium (g/L): Tryptone 16.0, yeast extract 10.0, NaCl 5.0 (pH 7.0). (4) NZCYM medium (g/L): Casein Enzymatic Hydrolysate (NZ amine) 10.0, yeast extract 5.0, casamino acids 1.0, NaCl 5.0, MgSO4·7H2O 2.0 (pH 7.0). (5) SOC medium (g/L): Tryptone 20.0, yeast extract 5.0, NaCl 0.5, KCl 0.186, MgCl2·6H2O 2.033, glucose 3.6 (glucose filter-sterilized and added post-autoclaving). (6) TB medium (g/L): Tryptone 12.0, yeast extract 24.0, glycerol 5.0, KH2PO4 2.31, K2HPO4 12.54 (pH7.0). (7) TSB medium (g/L): Tryptone 17.0, soytone 3.0, NaCl 5.0, glucose 2.5, K2HPO4 2.5 (pH 7.0).

CRISPR/Cas9-mediated gene deletion

The BsCsn gene in Bacillus subtilis WB800N was deleted using CRISPR/Cas9-mediated genome editing coupled with homology-directed repair (HDR) using a temperature-sensitive plasmid. The procedure consisted of the following steps: (1) Construction of the HDR template: Flanking homology arms (~ 500 bp each) targeting BsCsn were amplified using primers BsCsn-up-SalIF/BsCsn-up-R and BsCsn-down-F/BsCsn-down-XbaIR. Upstream (UP) and downstream (DOWN) fragments were fused by overlap extension PCR to generate the repair template (UD). (2) Vector assembly: The UD fragment was digested with SalI/XbaI and ligated into CRISPR/Cas9 vector pJOE8999(Cm) (expressing sgRNA targeting BsCsn). (3) Transformation and selection: Electrocompetent B. subtilis WB800N cells were prepared in sorbitol-supplemented LB (0.5 mol/L sorbitol) at mid-exponential phase (OD600 ≈ 0.9), washed with ice-cold electroporation buffer (10% glycerol, 0.5 mol/L sorbitol, 0.5 mol/L mannitol), and transformed by electroporation (2.0 kV, 5 ms). Transformants were selected on LB agar with chloramphenicol (25 µg/mL) at 30 °C. (4) Deletion verification: Primary screening used flanking primers (BsCsn-JD-F/R) and internal primers (BsCsn-ter-F/R) (Fig. S1A, B). Putative deletions were confirmed by Sanger sequencing. (5) Plasmid curing: Temperature-sensitive plasmid elimination was achieved by culturing at 42 °C for 48 h in antibiotic-free LB. Plasmid loss was confirmed by chloramphenicol sensitivity. The final marker-free deletion strain was designated B. subtilis WB800N ΔBsCsn.

Codon optimization, recombinant strain construction, and chitosanase expression

The CsnA coding sequence (SCO0677; GenBank: NC_003888.3), excluding its native signal peptide and adding a C-terminal His-tag, was chemically synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China) after codon optimization for B. subtilis (CAI target = 0.99).

The CsnA gene fused to the AprE signal peptide-propeptide was assembled with linearized pBE2R(Cm) (primers: pBE2R(Cm)-line-F/R) via Gibson Assembly® (50 °C, 30 min), yielding pBE2R(Cm)-CsnA. All primers, strains, and plasmids used are listed in Table 1 and Table S1 (Supporting Information). After Sanger verification, the plasmid was transformed into B. subtilis WB800N ΔBsCsn competent cells by electroporation. Protein purification and enzyme analysis

CsnA purification: Fermentation supernatant was loaded onto pre-equilibrated Ni–NTA magnetic beads (Sangon Biotech, China). After binding, beads were washed with equilibration buffer (20 mmol/L sodium phosphate; 500 mmol/L NaCl; 5 mmol/L imidazole; pH 8.0). Target protein was eluted with elution buffer (20 mmol/L sodium phosphate; 500 mmol/L NaCl; 250 mmol/L imidazole; pH 8.0). All steps were performed at 4 °C.

Chitosanase activity was determined using the 3,5-dinitrosalicylic acid (DNS) method [22]. Reactions (1 mL) contained 1% (w/v) chitosan (deacetylation degree ≥ 95%) in 100 mmol/L sodium acetate buffer (pH 6.0) and diluted enzyme. After incubation at 50 °C for 10 min, reactions were stopped with 1 mL DNS reagent and boiled for 10 min. Absorbance at 540 nm was measured, and the activity was calculated using a D-glucosamine standard curve. One unit (U) of activity was defined as the amount of enzyme releasing 1 µmol of reducing sugar equivalents (as glucosamine) per minute. Protein concentration was determined by Bradford assay using bovine serum albumin as the standard [23]. All measurements were performed in triplicate.

The effects of temperature and pH on enzyme activity were evaluated as previously described [13]. Substrate specificity was analyzed using chitosan (deacetylation degrees: 70%, ≥ 75%, 80%, ≥ 85%, ≥ 90%, ≥ 95%), N, O-carboxymethyl chitosan, chitin, microcrystalline cellulose, carboxymethyl cellulose, dextran, and soluble starch. Ten grams of chitin powder were slowly added to 150 mL of concentrated hydrochloric acid under continuous stirring at 4 °C for 12 h. The mixture was then treated with 2000 mL of pre-chilled ethanol and stirred overnight at room temperature. The resulting precipitate was collected by filtration and thoroughly washed with distilled water until the filtrate reached neutral pH. Finally, the purified chitin was resuspended in 1000 mL of sodium acetate buffer (100 mmol/L, pH 6.0) to obtain a 1% (w/v) colloidal chitin suspension. The preparation method was adapted from established protocols for chitinase substrate preparation [24, 25]. Reactions were conducted at 60 °C and pH 6.0 for 10 min. Effects of metal ions and chemicals were evaluated by adding them to the reaction mixture at final concentrations of 1, 5, or 10 mmol/L. Residual activity was measured relative to untreated controls. Statistical analysis was performed using unpaired t-test and one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post-hoc test.

The kinetic parameters of CsnA were determined using chitosan substrates with varying degrees of deacetylation (≥ 95%, ≥ 90%, ≥ 85%, 80%, ≥ 75%, 70%). The reactions were carried out in sodium acetate buffer (100 mmol/L, pH 6.0) at 60 °C for 10 min. The enzyme (0.5 μg) was incubated with substrate concentrations ranging from 0.1 to 20 mg/mL. The Michaelis–Menten constants (Km and Vmax) were derived by fitting the initial velocity data to the Michaelis–Menten equation using Origin 10.5. The catalytic constant (Kcat) and catalytic efficiency (Kcat/Km) were subsequently calculated based on the values of Vmax and Km. The methodological approach for this analysis was adapted from prior studies [26].

PB/RSM experiments for optimizing fermentation medium composition

Initial optimization was performed using single-factor experiments in shake flasks to investigate the effects of: (1) pH optimization: LB medium (pH 5.0–9.0 in 0.5-unit intervals) was inoculated with 1% (v/v) seed culture and incubated at 37 °C, 200 rpm for 48 h. Chitosanase activity and dry cell weight (DCW) were measured. (2) Medium screening: Seven media (M9, LB, 2 × YT, NZCYM, SOC, TB, TSB; pH 7.0) were tested under identical inoculation and incubation conditions. Activity and DCW were analyzed. (3) Inoculum size: TB medium (pH 7.0) was inoculated with 1–10% (v/v) seed culture (1% increments) and incubated at 37 °C, 200 rpm for 48 h. Activity and DCW were recorded. (4) Temperature optimization: TB medium (pH 7.0) inoculated with 4% (v/v) seed culture was incubated at 23–47 ℃ (23, 28, 32, 37, 42, 47 °C; 200 rpm, 48 h). Activity and DCW were quantified. (5) Carbon source: TB medium was supplemented with equimolar concentrations of glycerol, glucose, maltose, sucrose, COSs (MW 800–1000) or lactose. Cultures were incubated at 37 °C, 200 rpm for 48 h, and activity and DCW were determined. (6) Nitrogen source: TB medium with glycerol was tested with equimolar concentrations nitrogen sources: tryptone (TRP), NZ amine (NZA), yeast extract (YE), soybean cake powder (SCP), corn steep powder (CSP), beef extract (BE), peptone (PET), and casamino acids (CA). Activity and dry cell wight (DCW) were measured after 48 h at 37 °C, 200 rpm. Cell biomass was quantified as dry cell weight (DCW) by centrifuging culture samples (4000 × g, 20 min, 4 °C). After discarding the supernatant, the cell pellet was thoroughly washed with normal saline and subsequently dried at 120 °C until a constant weight was attained. All DCW values presented are the mean ± standard deviation of three independent biological replicates [27].

Based on single-factor results, SCP, CSP, NZA, glycerol, KCl, NaCl, MgSO4·7H2O, CaCl2, KH2PO4, NH4Cl, and Na2HPO4·7H2O were selected for Plackett–Burman (PB) design. PB design and response surface methodology (RSM) are well-established, routinely employed approaches for optimizing fermentation culture conditions [28]. In the PB design, each factor was tested at high (+1) and low (−1) coded levels (Table S2). Triplicate experiments were performed, with mean activity as the response variable. Fed-batch fermentation in 5-L bioreactor

A single colony of the recombinant strain was cultured in 20 mL LB medium at 37 °C, 200 rpm until OD600 reached 1.50. This culture (1% v/v) was transferred to 120 mL LB and grown to OD600 = 3.00 as seed culture. The seed was inoculated into a 5-L bioreactor (Baoxing Bio-engineering, China) containing 3 L optimized medium (8.57 g/L SCP, 4.43 g/L NZA, 24.99 g/L glycerol, 5.44 g/L Na2HPO4·7H2O). Dissolved oxygen (DO) was maintained at 35% saturation via cascade control of aeration rate (2–6 L/min) and agitation speed (200–800 rpm). Upon detecting a DO spike (~ 8 h post-inoculation) indicating glycerol depletion, fed-batch operation commenced with continuous feeding of 50% (w/v) glycerol, maintaining glycerol concentration at 23–26 g/L. Glycerol levels were monitored by HPLC (Shimadzu LC-20A; Aminex HPX-87C column: 7.8 mm × 300 mm, 9 μm; RID detector; mobile phase: 5 mmol/L H2SO4; flow rate: 0.6 mL/min) [30]. Fermentation conditions: 37 °C, pH 7.0 (automatically controlled with 25% NH4OH/20% H3PO4). Antifoam (polyether-based, Sigma-Aldrich) was manually added as needed. Samples were periodically analyzed for enzyme activity, protein concentration, DCW and glycerol concentration.

Hydrolysis product analysis

Enzymatic Hydrolysis: Purified CsnA (15 U/mL) was incubated with 2% (w/v) chitosan (deacetylation degree ≥ 95%) in 100 mmol/L sodium acetate buffer (pH 6.0) at 40 °C for 4 h. Reactions were terminated by boiling for 10 min. Thin-Layer Chromatography (TLC): Hydrolysates were separated on silica gel 60 F254 plates (Merck) using n-butanol/glacial acetic acid/water/25% ammonia (10:5:5:1, v/v) as the developing solvent. Oligosaccharides were visualized by spraying with 0.5% (w/v) ninhydrin in ethanol and heating at 80 °C for 5 min.

MALDI-TOF MS Analysis: Samples were analyzed using an Autoflex Speed MALDI-TOF/TOF mass spectrometer (Bruker Daltonics, Germany) in positive reflection mode. Operating parameters: Ion source voltage: 29 kV; Mass range: m/z 100–800; Matrix: 2,5-dihydroxybenzoic acid (10 mg/mL in 50% acetonitrile/0.1% trifluoroacetic acid); Calibration: Peptide calibration standard.

Results and discussion

Deletion of the endogenous chitosanase gene

The BsCsn gene (834 bp; GenBank: WOA97711.1) was identified in the genome of Bacillus subtilis WB800N. To construct a knockout strain, a gene deletion strategy was employed and verified through a combination of PCR and sequencing. As demonstrated in Fig. S1A, PCR amplification using flanking primers (BsCsn-JD-F/R) produced an amplicon of the expected size, confirming the occurrence of homologous recombination. Conversely, no PCR product was obtained with internal primers (BsCsn-ter-F/R) (Fig. S1B), demonstrating the absence of the target gene. Sanger sequencing of the flanking primer amplicon (base maps in Fig. S1C-D; sequence data in Table S4) followed by alignment with the wild-type WB800N genome confirmed the precise deletion of the BsCsn gene (Fig. 1A, B). The physiological impact of the BsCsn deletion was assessed by comparing the growth kinetics of the knockout and wild-type strains in LB medium. As demonstrated in Fig. 1C, both strains exhibited nearly identical growth curves, entering the logarithmic phase within 0–16 h and reaching a stationary phase with optical densities (OD600) of 5.90 ± 0.03 (knockout) and 6.04 ± 0.10 (wild-type). Statistical analysis using an unpaired t-test revealed no significant difference (P = 0.0697), indicating that the deletion of BsCsn did not impose a substantial physiological burden on the strain.

CsnA exhibited negligible activity towards other carbohydrates such as chitin, microcrystalline cellulose, carboxymethyl cellulose, dextran and soluble starch. The effects of various metals ions and chemicals on CsnA activities were investigated (Table 4). The strong inhibition by EDTA (residual activity was 71.17% ± 1.99) suggested that metal ions are essential for maintaining CsnA’s tertiary structure, consistent with GH46 family catalytic mechanisms [11, 32]. Notably, Zn2+, Ag+, Al3+, Pb2+, Cu2+, Ni2+, Fe3+ and SDS could strongly reduce the activity of CsnA by 85–90%. In contrast, Mn2+, K+, Ca2+, Na+, Sr2+, Cs+, Co2+, dithiothreitol (DTT), 2-mercaptoethanol (2-ME) and dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) enhanced the activity of CsnA. The activity of CsnA increased with increasing concentrations of K+ and Ca2+. Mg2+ and Li+ acted as activators for CsnA at low concentrations, while CsnA activity decreased at 10 mmol/L concentrations. Mn2+ increased the activity of CsnA by 3.51 ± 1.99 folds, similarly to previous studies [33]. However, this stimulatory effect was concentration-dependent and diminished at higher concentrations. A similar effect of DTT on the activity of chitosanase from Chromobacterium violaceum [34] has been reported, however the mechanism remains elusive. Fermentation optimization for CsnA expression

CsnA characterization revealed maximal hydrolysis rates at pH 5.0–6.5. The enzyme exhibited optimal activity at pH 6.0 and 60 °C, respectively. Notably, it retained > 80% relative activity after 30-min incubation across pH 4.5–10.0 and 20–40 °C, demonstrating high operational stability for industrial applications (Fig. 2B, C). All subsequent fermentations employed these parameters.

Single-factor optimization revealed coordinated maxima for activity and biomass. (1) pH 7.0: Maximal activity (23.75 ± 0.48 U/mL) and DCW (3.14 ± 0.17 g/L) (Fig. 3A). (2) TB medium: Peak activity (154.02 ± 2.52 U/mL) and DCW (5.52 ± 0.17 g/L) (Fig. 3B). (3) 4% inoculum: Highest activity (166.16 ± 1.17 U/mL) and DCW (5.91 ± 0.16 g/L) (Fig. 3C). (4) 37 °C: Maximum activity (167.56 ± 2.76 U/mL; P = 0.04) and DCW (5.91 ± 0.04 g/L) (Fig. 3D). Carbon and nitrogen sources were further optimized based on TB medium. As demonstrated in Fig. 3E, cultivation of the B. subtilis WB800N ΔBsCsn/pBE2R(Cm)-CsnA with glycerol as the carbon source yielded the highest chitosanase activity and biomass, reaching 167.61 ± 1.78 U/mL and 5.80 ± 0.11 g/L, respectively. As exhibited in Fig. 3F, SCP yielded the highest chitosanase activity (137.27 ± 4.52 U/mL), followed by NZA (130.37 ± 1.76 U/mL). However, CSP resulted in the highest biomass (7.80 ± 0.07 g/L, DCW). Initial pH screening was performed in LB medium due to its low buffering capacity, which helps to isolate the effect of pH with minimal interference from other factors. The subsequent optimization of other parameters was performed under production-oriented conditions using TB medium. This decision was justified by the significant performance enhancement observed in TB medium (Fig. 3A, B), which is known to support higher cell densities and thus greater volumetric enzyme productivity. This work establishes the first high-yield secretory production of Streptomyces coelicolor CsnA in B. subtilis, representing a 99.09-fold enhancement versus native host expression [35]. While demonstrating robust model applicability for industrial bioprocessing, potential strain-dependent performance variability warrants further investigation.

Fed-batch fermentation in 5-L bioreactor

Fed-batch fermentation in a 5-L bioreactor achieved unprecedented productivity for Streptomyces coelicolor CsnA secretion in B. subtilis WB800N ΔBsCsn/pBE2R(Cm)-CsnA (Fig. 5). While the optimized shake-flask cultivation yielded maximum biomass (8.43 ± 0.11 g/L DCW at 20 h) and enzyme activity (252.79 ± 2.68 U/mL at 40 h), scaled operation under DO-stat control (35% saturation) with dynamic glycerol feeding (23–26 g/L) demonstrated exceptional intensification: peaking at 84.99 ± 1.83 g/L DCW after 36 h and 540.08 ± 6.20 U/mL after 48 h, corresponding to 10.08-fold and 2.14-fold enhancements, respectively. This process achieved a volumetric productivity of 11.25 U/(mL·h) and a carbon conversion efficiency of 1682.86 U/g glycerol. This productivity represents a superior metric compared to existing reports, outperforming P. pastoris systems by 86.26% [36] and being 61.6% higher than that in a previous study (4.32 U/mL·h) [13], which establishes the highest reported activity for a heterologous chitosanase in B. subtilis.Fig. 5Fed-batch fermentation kinetics. Hydrolysis product analysis

Time-course TLC profiling (Fig. 6A) revealed progressive chitosan depolymerization by CsnA over 0–4 h. In contrast to the endo-random cleavage patterns yielding predominantly DP ≤ 2 products in some GH46 homologs [33], analysis demonstrated time-dependent accumulation of defined oligomers: after 2 h, chitobiose (DP2) and chitotriose (DP3) constituted 82.3 ± 3.5% of soluble products-distinct from Streptomyces sp. SirexAA-E (primarily DP3-6) [21]. TLC profiling demonstrated that CsnA did not degrade chitobiose (DP2) or chitotriose (DP3). However, chitotetraose (DP4) was cleaved into chitobiose units, while chitopentaose (DP5) was broken down into chitobiose and chitotriose, indicating that CsnA acts on chitotetraose and larger oligosaccharides, producing chitobiose and chitotriose as primary end products (Fig. S3).Fig. 6Chitosan hydrolysis product analysis. Although TLC analysis revealed a diverse range of products spanning DP2 to DP5, MALDI-TOF MS analysis at the 30-min time point (Fig. 6B) confirmed endo-type cleavage via dominant ions: m/z 340.02 [(GlcN)₂ + H]⁺ (DP2), 501.16 [(GlcN)₃ + H]⁺ (DP3), and 663.17 [(GlcN)₄ + H]⁺ (DP4). The prevalence of these even-numbered oligosaccharides (DP2, DP4) suggests that CsnA preferentially cleaves glycosidic bonds to generate chitobiose units as a major product. Based on these results, a hydrolytic pattern for CsnA was proposed, as illustrated in Fig. 6C. This reproducible DP2–DP4 profile enables precision synthesis of low-DP COSs through reaction time modulation.

Notably, catalytic specificity mirrors engineering opportunities: structure-guided mutagenesis of Streptomyces sp. N174 chitosanase (quadruple deletion A198-A199-H200-D201) shifted products toward chitopentaose (DP5)-a modulator of murine gut microbiota [33]. CsnA’s defined cleavage fingerprint provides a template for analogous bioengineering targeting tailored COSs therapeutics.

Conclusion

This study establishes, for the first time, a high-yield secretory production platform for Streptomyces coelicolor CsnA in an engineered Bacillus subtilis chassis. By integrating CRISPR/Cas9-mediated genome editing, codon optimization, and systematic bioprocess engineering, we achieved an unprecedented extracellular CsnA activity of 540.08 ± 6.20 U/mL in a 5-L bioreactor—a 99.09-fold improvement over the native host and the highest titer reported for a heterologous chitosanase in B. subtilis.

The enzyme exhibited a defined product profile, generating chitooligosaccharides (DP2-DP4) as confirmed by MALDI-TOF MS. This specificity enables the tailored synthesis of these high-value oligomers. This work provides an efficient and sustainable strategy for the valorization of marine waste chitosan and establishes B. subtilis as a versatile chassis for the expression of biotechnologically important enzymes.
