Intercropping-mediated enrichment of core microbiome enhances suppression of Verticillium wilt in cotton
Authors: Chuanzhen Yang, Hongchen Yue, Aixin Sun, Zili Feng, Hongjie Feng, Yalin Zhang, Lihong Zhao, Jinglong Zhou, Heqin Zhu, Feng Wei
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundVerticillium wilt, caused by Verticillium dahliae Kleb., is a devastating soilborne disease threatening global cotton production. Intercropping is a sustainable agricultural practice known to suppress soilborne diseases, yet the microbiome-mediated mechanisms underlying its efficacy against Verticillium wilt remain poorly understood.ResultsA three-year field trial (2019–2021) showed that intercropping cotton with mustard significantly reduced Verticillium wilt severity (32.11–39.2%), increased yield (13.88–23.22%), and lowered soil microsclerotia density. Intercropping reshaped soil microbial communities and enriched a core set of beneficial taxa compared to monocropping, generating more complex and cooperative rhizosphere networks during flowering and boll stage. We then constructed an intercropping-enriched synthetic community (IC-SynCom) from the enriched core microbiotas with multiple beneficial traits; this consortium, comprising Bacillus altitudinis strain CRB-021, Lysobacter firmicutimachus strain CRB-253, Rhizobium soli strain CRB-314, Enterobacter hormaechei strain CRB-070, and Pantoea sp. strain CRB-006, achieved the highest control efficacy at 72.83 ± 1.31%, promoted cotton growth, and outperformed single-strain inoculants. qRT-PCR further showed that IC-SynCom activated systemic plant defenses by the upregulation of key defense-related genes, including phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (GhPAL), cinnamate 4-hydroxylase (GhC4H1), pathogenesis-related protein 10 (GhPR10), peroxidase (GhPOD), and β-1,3-glucanase (Ghβ-1,3-glucanase), which are involved in salicylic acid signaling and lignin biosynthesis.ConclusionsOur findings demonstrate that intercropping enhances soil’s capacity to suppress Verticillium wilt by reshaping root-associated microbiomes. A core consortium of intercropping-enriched beneficial microbes (IC-SynCom) effectively suppresses Verticillium wilt through direct antagonism and activation of plant immunity. These results highlight the potential of microbiome-based strategies for sustainable management of soilborne diseases.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40793-025-00840-0.Keywords: Intercropping, Core microbiome, Verticillium wilt, Synthetic microbial community, Soilborne disease suppression

Introduction

Cotton (Gossypium hirsutum L.) is one of the world’s most important fiber crops, contributing approximately 35% of global fiber production [1]. However, its production is severely constrained by Verticillium wilt, a devastating soilborne disease caused by Verticillium dahliae Kleb. [2, 3]. Intensive monocropping practices in major producing regions, such as the Xinjiang region of China–which accounts for over 90% of the nation’s yield [4, 5]–have exacerbated the accumulation of V. dahliae, resulting in substantial yield and fiber quality losses, raising the global concerns about its management and mitigation [6, 7]. Current control methods rely heavily on chemical pesticides and soil fumigation; However, these approaches pose environmental risks, disrupt soil micro-ecology, and often undermine long-term agricultural sustainability [8–10]. Therefore, developing innovative and eco-friendly strategies is essential for mitigating Verticillium wilt and reducing yield losses in cotton production.

Intercropping—cultivating two or more crop species in the same field—is a traditional eco-friendly practice that has been applied globally for centuries [11]. Certain companion plants can actively suppress soilborne diseases through the release of biocidal hydrolysis products. For example, intercropping with Brassica juncea cultivars can mitigate Verticillium wilt via isothiocyanates (ITCs) [12–14]. However, the field efficacy of ITCs is often inconsistent [15], and recent evidence suggests that disease suppression by Brassica napus can be primarily mediated through the modulation of the soil microbiome rather than chemical toxicity alone [16]. Despite this, the microbial mechanisms underlying disease suppression in cotton-mustard intercropping system remain poorly elucidated under field conditions.

Microbial communities adhering to the plant rhizosphere, collectively termed the rhizosphere microbiome, play a critical role in disease suppression through multiple mechanisms such as enriching probiotic microbes, enhancing microbe-microbe cooperation, and inducing plant immune responses [17–19]. For instance, intercropping tomato with onion can prevent Verticillium wilt by increasing Bacillus species colonization, which in turn creates a protective rhizosphere microbiome [20]. Yet, intercropping remains underutilized in certain regions like Xinjiang due to challenges in changing agricultural practices (e.g., early sowing, high planting density, and the cultivation of dwarf cotton), as well as specific soil conditions (e.g., saline-alkaline soils). Thus, exploring the microbiome-mediated mechanisms of disease suppression in intercropping systems is vital for developing sustainable management strategies. However, single-strain biological control agents often fail in complex field environments [21–24]. This limitation underscores the need for synthetic microbial communities (SynComs)—assembled from functionally complementary strains—as a more robust strategy for achieving sustainable disease management [25–28].

A major challenge in SynCom construction is identifying and culturing suitable microbial strains with beneficial traits. The core microbiome, a conserved group of host-associated microorganisms that persist across time to environments, provides a rational basis for this selection [29, 30]. Strains for SynCom construction can be identified from this core microbiome based on community composition, persistence informed by “omics” data, and beneficial functions for holobiont fitness derived from “culture” data [31–33]. Leveraging this functionally defined core microbiome to construct SynComs is crucial not only for developing more stable and effective consortia for disease control but also for advancing our comprehension of the plant-microbiome interactions in mitigating Verticillium wilt.

Here, we conducted a three-year field trial to investigate how cotton-mustard intercropping modulates the root-associated microbiome and suppresses Verticillium wilt. We hypothesized that intercropping enriches beneficial core microbiota, thereby enhancing disease suppression. Specifically, we sought to (1) characterize the dynamics of bulk and rhizosphere microbial communities across different cotton growth stages under monocropping and intercropping, and (2) identify intercropping-enriched core microbiota and evaluate their disease-suppressive and growth-promoting functions, both as individual strains and as a assembled SynCom. By integrating field monitoring, amplicon sequencing, culture-based functional assays, and SynCom inoculation experiments, this study aims to elucidate the microbiome-mediated mechanisms of disease mechanisms of disease suppression and contribute to sustainable cotton production.

Materials and methods

Field experiment design, disease and yield assessment

A three-year field trial was conducted from September 2018 to October 2021 at the National Regional Experimental Resistance Identification Station of the Cotton Research Institute, Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences, in Anyang, Henan Province, China (36°03′44″ N, 114°28′52″ E). The experiment included two agricultural regimes: (1) Monocropping: Cotton (cv. Lumianyan 21) was sown annually in late April (approximately April 25) and harvested in November. (2) Intercropping: Cotton was planted as in the monocropping regime, with mustard (Brassica juncea var. gracilis) intercropped in alternate rows at the cotton boll-opening stage (approximately September 10) each year (Fig. 1A).

The experiment followed a randomized complete block design with three replicate plots per treatment. Each plot measured 5.0 m × 5.0 m. Each monocropping plot contained six rows of cotton with an inter-row spacing of 0.8 m, totaling 120 cotton plants. Each intercropping plot was divided into six strips, each featuring alternating rows of cotton and mustard spaced 0.4 m apart, containing a total of 120 cotton and 120 mustard plants. For intercropping plots, mustard plants were manually harvested at maturity (approximately November 25) each year. The mustard plants were manually chopped into 3–5 cm pieces, and the residues were evenly spread on the soil surface before being incorporated into the top 10 cm soil layer through manual tillage. The plots were then covered with transparent plastic mulch to facilitate residue decomposition during winter. The mulch was removed 20 days before cotton sowing in the following year.

In 2019, 2020, and 2021, all plots were grown with cotton. The severity of Verticillium wilt was monitored monthly on individual cotton plants following the appearance of typical symptoms. During each assessment, 15 randomly selected cotton plants per plot were rated using a five-class rating scale (0, no symptoms; 1, ≤ 33% leaves wilting; 2, > 33% and ≤ 66% leaves wilting; 3, > 66% to ≤ 99% leaves wilting; 4, plant death). The disease index (DI) was calculated for each plot using the formula:

(Ni: the number of plants in each disease class, i: the numerical value of the disease scale (0–4 class), and N: the total number of plants assessed in each plot) [34]. Final data were analyzed when the DI of monocropping cotton reached 35. In total, 90 plants (three plots, two agricultural regimes) were assessed for examination.

For yield assessment, two standardized harvests were conducted annually on October 15 and October 30. During each harvest, all open cotton bolls within a plot were manually collected and immediately weighed to determine the fresh weight. The total yield per plot was calculated as the sum of weights from both harvests and then converted to equivalent yield (kg/ha) based on planting density and plot area.

Sample collection and processing

Bulk soil and rhizosphere soil samples were collected before the cotton harvest across three consecutive years (2019–2021). Bulk soil samples were collected from six plots under two distinct agricultural regimes at four growth stages: pre-seeding (April 20), flowering and boll (July 20), boll opening with mustard planting (September 10), and post-cotton harvest (November 5). For each plot, nine soil cores (15 cm deep) were randomly collected and thoroughly homogenized to form a composite bulk soil sample. In total, 648 soil cores (nine soil cores per plot × three plot/biological replicates × four growth stages × two regimes × three cropping years) were collected, finally generating 72 bulk soil samples (three biological replicates × four growth stages × two regimes × three cropping years). For rhizosphere collection, five cotton plants were randomly selected per plot during the flowering and boll stage. The roots of these cotton were carefully excavated using a 75% ethanol-sterilized stainless steel spade to preserve complete root architecture. After gently shaking off large soil particles, the remaining soil adhering within 5 mm cotton roots (defined as rhizosphere soil) was combined into one composite biological replicate per plot, sieved (2 mm pore size) and homogenized [34]. This process yielded 18 rhizosphere soil samples (three plots/biological replicates × two regimes × three cropping years), from a total of 90 cotton plants (five cotton × three plots/biological replicates × two regimes × three cropping years).

Each soil sample was divided into two parts: (1) one stored at 4 °C for subsequent assessment of V. dahliae microsclerotia density (bulk soil only) and for later isolation of root-associated microorganisms (both bulk and rhizosphere soil samples); and (2) the other stored at -80 °C for microbiome analysis.

Determination of the density of V. dahliae microsclerotia

The density of V. dahliae microsclerotia in bulk soil was quantified using a modified wet-sieving and plating method [35]. Initially, all bulk soil samples were air-dried at 25 ± 2 °C for 28 days to eliminate residual hyphae and microbial spores before the sieving process. A 20 g subsample from each replicate was placed into a 200-mL screw-cap bottle containing 100 mL of sterile distilled water. The mixture was shaken vigorously at 175 rpm for 1 h at 28 °C on a reciprocating shaker. The soil suspension was then poured through a nested 20 μm sieve and rinsed with tap water until the effluent ran clear. The sieve-retained material was collected and resuspended in 20-mL sterile distilled water. A 2 mL aliquot of the suspension was pipetted onto each of 10 MSEA medium plates (see detailed medium components in Table S1). After a four-week incubation at 22 °C in darkness, soil particles on the plates were gently washed off with tap water. Colonies of V. dahliae were examined under a dissecting microscope (SMZ-10 Nikon, Japan) at 24 × magnification, with each colony-forming unit (CFU) considered to represent a single germinated microsclerotium. The microsclerotial density was expressed as CFU per gram of dry soil (CFU g−1).

DNA extraction and sequencing

Genomic DNA was extracted from 0.5 g of soil using the FastDNA® Spin Kit (MP Biomedicals, Solon, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The primers 314F and 806R, ITS5 and ITS2 respectively amplify the 16S rDNA gene’s V3-V4 region in bacteria, as well as the fungal ITS rDNA gene’s ITS1 region [36, 37]. The sequences of these primers and the detailed PCR amplification procedures are provided in Table S2. Sequencing libraries were constructed using the TruSeq® DNA PCR-Free Sample Preparation Kit (Illumina, San Diego, California, USA) in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocol. The quality of sequencing libraries was assessed using Qubit® 2.0 Fluorometer (Thermo Scientific, USA) and the Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100 system. The DNA samples were subjected to paired-end sequencing on the IonS5™XL platform (250 bp paired-end reads; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) following standard protocols at Novogene Bioinformatics Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). The detailed protocols for the amplicon sequencing processing are described in the Supplementary Methods.

Co‑occurrence network analysis

Co-occurrence network analysis was performed to examine the complexity of the rhizospheric microbial community under two agricultural regimes [38, 39]. Total 18 rhizosphere soil samples, collected during the cotton flowering and boll stage from 2019 to 2021, were categorized into two groups: monocropping group (9 samples each for bacterial and fungal analysis) and intercropping group (9 samples each for bacteria and fungi). Co-occurrence patterns were constructed based on the Spearman correlation matrix using the “picante”, “reshape2” and “dplyr” packages in R [40]. Only operational taxonomic units (OTUs) with a relative abundance > 0.01% and a prevalence > 50% across all samples were retained for co-occurrence network construction. Networks were visualized at the genus level. A robust co-occurrence relationship was defined by a Spearman’s correlation coefficient (|r|> 0.80) and a Benjamini–Hochberg adjusted P-value < 0.05 [41]. Gephi (v 0.9.2) was used to visualize the co-occurrence networks [42] and calculate key topological properties, including the total number of nodes, total number of links, network diameter, average degree, average clustering coefficient, average network distance, and network modularity. In these networks, nodes represent individual microbial genera, and edges denote statistically significant pairwise correlations between these nodes, reflecting potential biological interactions [43].

Isolation of culturable bacteria and fungi from intercropping cotton soils

Ten grams of bulk and rhizosphere soil were suspended in 100 mL of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; Solarbio, Beijing, China) with 3-mm glass beads. The suspension was shaken at 37 °C and 180 rpm for 1 h to disperse microbial cells. To maximize culturable diversity, serial dilutions (10−4 to 10−6) of the soil suspension were plated on various culture media, including 1/10 TSA, TSA, LB, NA, R2A, YMA for bacteria, as well as RBM and PDA media for fungi (see detailed components of these media in Table S1). Based on distinct morphological characteristics (color, shape, size), 504 bacterial and 115 fungal colonies were initially obtained from all media following in incubation at 37 °C for 2—7 days. Pure colonies were obtained by re-streaking isolated colonies onto fresh Tryptic Soy Agar (TSA) for bacteria and PDA for fungi (AOBOX Biotechnology, Beijing, China). For molecular identification, the 16S rDNA gene was amplified using paired primers 27F/1492R and the ITS gene was amplified using paired primers ITS1/ITS4 (Table S2) [44, 45]. All PCR products were sequenced at Sangon Biotech Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The resulting sequences were taxonomically classified using BLASTn against the Ribosomal Database Project for bacterial 16S rDNA gene sequences and the ITS Database Project for fungal ITS sequences within the NCBI, applying a ≥ 99% similarity as the clustering threshold. Taxonomic assignment for each isolate was based on the top matches to published type strain sequences. After removing duplicates (defined as isolates with identical 16S rRNA or ITS gene sequences isolated from the same medium), 76 unique bacterial and 15 fungal strains were retained. All strains were cryopreserved at − 80 °C in 1 mL of 25% (v/v) glycerol.

All isolates in this study were designated according to standard microbiological conventions, which combine taxonomic identification with a unique laboratory code. The full species name and strain designation are provided at first mention, e.g., Bacillus altitudinis strain CRB-021 (hereafter referred to as CRB-021). This naming convention applies uniformly to all isolates.

Screening for plant growth-promoting properties, including phosphate solubilization, potassium solubilization, nitrogen fixation, and indole-3-acetic acid (IAA) production

The plant growth-promoting potential of 76 unique bacterial isolates from intercropping soil was assessed using multiple selective media by halo zone method. For each assay, each bacterial isolate was individually inoculated into 100 mL of TSB (AOBOX Biotechnology, Beijing, China) and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h with shaking at 180 rpm. After adjusting the bacterial suspension to an OD600 of 0.8, 2 µL aliquots were spot-inoculated at the four equidistant corners on each multiple selective medium plate, with three technical replicates per isolate. Negative controls consisted of 2 µL aliquots of uninoculated TSB.

The phosphate-solubilizing potential was detected by spotting the bacterial cultures on Pikovskaya (PVK) medium (pH = 7.0) containing 10-g glucose, 5-g MgCl2, 0.25-g MgSO4·7H2O, 0.2-g KCl, 0.1-g (NH4)2SO4, 5.0 g of Ca3(PO4)2 as the sole phosphate source, and 15-g agar in 1-L deionized water. Following incubation at 28 °C for 7 days, phosphate solubilization was indicated by the formation of clear halo zones around bacterial colonies, and efficiency was calculated as the ratio of clear halo zone diameter (Dh) to colony diameter (Dc).

The ability of potassium-solubilizing was assessed on solid medium plates (pH = 7.0) containing 10-g glucose, 2-g NaH2PO4, 0.2-g MgSO4·7H2O, 0.1-g CaCO3, 0.05-g FeCl3, 1 g of insoluble Potassium feldspar as a potassium source, 15-g agar in 1-L deionized water. Plates were incubated at 28 °C for 7 days, and potassium solubilization was determined by halo zone formation, with efficiency expressed as Dh/ Dc.

The nitrogen-fixing ability was detected on Ashby nitrogen-free culture medium (pH = 7.0) containing 5-g Mannitol, 5-g Glucose, 5-g CaCO3, 0.5-g KH2PO4, 0.2-g NaCl, 0.2-g MgSO4·7H2O, 0.2-g K2SO4·2H2O, and 15-g agar in 1-L deionized water. Following incubation at 28 °C for 7 days, nitrogen fixation was indicated by clear halo zones around colonies, and efficiency was calculated as Dh/ Dc.

The capability of IAA production for bacterial isolates was evaluated using Salkowski’s colorimetric method [46]. Briefly, bacterial isolates were individually inoculated into 4 mL of TSB supplemented with 200 mg/L L-tryptophan and cultured at 30 °C for 48 h with shaking at 180 rpm. After incubation, the cultured bacterial suspensions were centrifuged at 8000 × g for 10 min. Then, 100 µL of each supernatant was mixed with an equal volume (100 µL) of Salkowski’s colorimetric solution (prepared as 35% HClO4: 0.5 M FeCl3 = 1: 20, v/v). Negative controls consisted of 100-µL aliquot of sterile TSB mixed with 100 µL Salkowski’s colorimetric solution, with positive controls contained 100 µL of TSB medium containing 2 µg pure IAA standard mixed with 100 µL Salkowski’s colorimetric solution. Each isolate and control was assayed in triplicate. All mixtures were incubated in the dark at 30 °C for 30 min, and absorbance was measured at 530 nm (OD530) using a multifunctional microplate reader (Synergy HT, BioTek Instruments Inc., USA). The appearance of a pink-red color in the mixture indicated the IAA-producing capacity of the bacterial isolate. The concentrations of IAA was quantified using a standard curve established with analytical grade IAA standards at concentrations of 10, 20, 30, 40, 50, 60, 70, 80, 90, and 100 µg/mL, yielding the linear regression equation y = 0.0294x + 0.0240 (R2 = 0.995). Finally, IAA production was normalized to bacterial growth (OD₆₀₀) and expressed as µg IAA per mL per OD₆₀₀ (µg mL⁻1 OD₆₀₀⁻1).

Evaluation of the disease-suppressing and plant growth-promoting efficacy of individual bacterial strains and IC-SynCom in cotton

By integrating amplicon sequencing with culture-based analyses, five bacterial isolates—Bacillus altitudinis strain CRB-021, Lysobacter firmicutimachus strain CRB-253, Rhizobium soli strain CRB-314, Enterobacter hormaechei strain CRB-070, and Pantoea sp. strain CRB-006—were identified as core beneficial taxa enriched in intercropping and selected for the construction of the intercropping-derived SynCom (IC-SynCom).

To evaluate their potential in suppressing cotton Verticillium wilt and promoting plant growth, three inoculant types were applied: (1) individual strain (CRB-021, CRB-253, CRB-314, CRB-070, and CRB-006), (2) the IC-SynCom, prepared as an equal-volume mixture of the five isolates, and (3) a sterile PBS control (CK) (Table S3). All bacterial cultures were grown in TSB medium at 37 °C with shaking 180 rpm for 24 h, centrifuged at 5000 × g for 8 min, and the pellets resuspended in sterile PBS to a final concentration of approximately 5 × 107 cells/mL.

Cotton seeds were surface-sterilized by immersion in 75% ethanol (1 min) and 2% NaClO (10 min), followed by three rinses with sterile water. A bottomless paper cup containing 400 g of sterilized substrate (vermiculite: sand: nutrient soil = 3:2:1, v/v) and was inoculated with microbial suspensions at 5 × 107 cells per g of substrate. The cups were then sealed and incubated for 3 days before planting. Ten germ-free cotton seeds were sown per cup, and three to five seedlings were retained for subsequent growth.

After 15 days of cultivation, cotton seedlings (three per cup) were inoculated with three types of treatments using the root-dipping method. Roots were immersed in 10 mL of either individual bacterial suspensions or the IC-SynCom suspension (approximately 5 × 107 cells/mL in PBS). Three days later, seedlings were similarly inoculated with 10 mL of V. dahliae suspension (approximately 107 spores/mL in Czapek medium) using the same root-dipping procedure. Each experimental group included six biological replicates, and the greenhouse assay was repeated in ten independent experiments under controlled conditions (25 °C, 16/8-h day/night cycle). Disease severity of each treatment was assessed 25 days post-inoculation using a 0–4 scale as previously described, and control efficacy was calculated as:

(DIs: the DI of the sterile PBS control, and DIt: the DI of the respective treatment group).

To assess growth-promoting effects, the same inoculation and cultivation procedures were applied as in the disease suppression assay, except that seedlings were not challenged with V. dahliae. After 21 days, various plant parameters, including plant height, root length, fresh weight, and dry weight, were measured for each seedling. Each treatment included six biological replicates, and the greenhouse assay was repeated in ten independent experiments under controlled conditions (25 °C, 16/8-h day/night cycle).

Induced systemic resistance assays

For microbial inoculation assays, three-day-old germ-free cotton seedlings were introduced to the IC-SynCom at the density of 5 × 107 cells per g of substrate to assess induced systemic resistance. A sterile PBS buffer control (CK) was included to exclude effects from the inoculation procedure or the buffer solution itself, and a heat-killed control (CK-HKIC) was prepared by boiling an equal volume of the IC-SynCom suspension at 100 °C for 30 min to abolish microbial viability while retaining cell components. At 25 days post-germination, cotton seedlings from each treatment were challenged with V. dahliae (approximately 107 spores/mL, prepared in Czapek medium) by root-dip inoculation.

For defense-related gene expression analysis, the first true leaves of five randomly selected cotton seedlings per treatment were sampled at 1, 12, 24, 48, 72, and 120 h post-inoculation (hpi). The leaf samples were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at − 80 °C until RNA extraction. Total RNA was extracted using the RNAprep Pure Plant Kit (Tiangen, China), and cDNA was synthesized with the TransScript® Uni All-in-One First-Strand cDNA Synthesis SuperMix for qPCR (One-Step gDNA Removal) (Transgen Biotech, China), following the manufacturer’s instructions. Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was performed using PerfectStart® Green qPCR SuperMix on ABI 7900 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, USA). Each sample was analyzed in three biological replicates and three technical replicates. Detailed methods are available in the Supplemental Materials.

The expression profiles of five defense-related genes were quantified, including peroxidase (GhPOD), phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (GhPAL), pathogenesis-related protein 10 (GhPR10), β-1,3-glucanase (Ghβ-1,3-glucanase), and cinnamate 4-hydroxylase (GhC4H1) [47–49]. The cotton ubiquitin gene GhUBQ7 served as an internal reference to normalize template variation. Primer sequences are listed in Table S2. Melting curve analysis was used to verify the specificity of amplification, and relative expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.3.1 (http://www.r-project.org/). Analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to assess differences in the DI and the density of V. dahliae microsclerotia. Beta-diversity of the bacterial and fungal communities was analyzed by computing Bray–Curtis dissimilarities at the operational taxonomic unit (OTU) level, followed by ordination using principal coordinate analysis (PCoA). Permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) was used to evaluate the effects of agricultural regimes and sampling time on the microbial community structure, based on a Bray–Curtis distance matrix as an input with 999 permutations. Differences in bacterial and fungal community composition at the phylum level were visualized using the “ggalluvial” package. Linear discriminant analysis (LDA) of effect size (LEfSe) was applied to identify differentially abundant bacterial and fungal genera (serving as biomarkers) between monocropping and intercropping soil samples collected during the cotton flowering and boll stage from 2019 to 2021. Prior to LEfSe analysis, the OTU tables were filtered to remove low-abundance taxa (relative abundance < 0.01%) and taxa with low prevalence (present in fewer than 50% of all samples). For pairwise comparisons, the Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used with an FDR-adjusted p < 0.05. An absolute LDA score > 2.25 was set as the threshold for statistical significance.

Results

Verticillium wilt suppression, yield enhancement, and reduced V. dahliae density in intercropping versus monocropping

A three-year field experiment demonstrated that intercropping cotton with mustard significantly suppressed Verticillium wilt severity and enhanced crop yield compared to monocropping (Fig. 1B). The DI was reduced by 39.2%, 37.33%, and 32.11% in 2019, 2020 and 2021, respectively (P < 0.01) (Fig. 1C), while yield increased by 23.22%, 14.8%, and 13.88% over the same years (P < 0.001) (Fig. 1D). These results suggest that intercropping cotton with mustard could serve as an effective strategy for Verticillium wilt mitigation and yield improvement.

To elucidate the potential mechanism of disease suppression, we further tracked the temporal dynamics of the V. dahliae by measuring the density of microsclerotia across four cotton growth stages from 2019 to 2021. A consistent seasonal pattern was observed: densities were low at pre-sowing, peaked significantly during the flowering and boll stages, and then sharply declined following cotton harvest, coinciding with the initial incorporation of mustard (Fig. 1E–G). Although the reductions were not statistically significant at pre-sowing and boll-opening stage (P > 0.05), intercropping significantly reduced microsclerotia density by 10.5 and 15.7% during flowering and boll stage in 2020 and 2021, respectively, compared to monocropping (both for P < 0.05) (Fig. 1F, G). This declining trend continued into the post-harvest period across the 2019–2021 seasons, during which a marked reduction in microsclerotia density coincided with the initial phase of mustard incorporation.

Intercropping reshapes bulk and rhizosphere microbial communities

To assess the impact of intercropping on the soil microbiome, we profiled the bacterial and fungal communities in bulk and rhizosphere soils using amplicon sequencing. A total of 5,044,841 bacterial sequences and 5,901,218 fungal high-quality sequences were obtained from 90 soil samples.

In bulk soil, PERMANOVA confirmed that the cropping year exerted a significant influence on microbial community structure in bulk soils (PERMANOVA, P < 0.01) (Fig. S1). Based on Bray–Curtis dissimilarity, PCoA further showed clear separations between monocropping and intercropping for both bacterial and fungal communities across the four different stages (PERMANOVA, P < 0.01) (Fig. S2). However, the magnitude of community divergence varied among years: differences were relatively minor in 2019 but became significant in 2020 and 2021 (PERMANOVA, P < 0.05), particularly for bacterial communities (Fig. S2A). A similar significant divergence in the fungal community was observed in 2021, particularly during the flowering and boll stages (PERMANOVA, P < 0.05) (Fig. S2B). The findings indicated that the microbial communities in cotton bulk soil are shaped by growth stages, cropping years and agricultural regimes, while the influence of intercropping becoming more pronounced over consecutive years, likely reflecting a cumulative impact of continuous intercropping on soil microbial assemblages.

The rhizosphere microbiome, a critical interface for plant health, exhibited a more specific response. During the flowering and boll stage, the bacterial community structure differed significantly between intercropping and monocropping (PERMANOVA, P < 0.05) (Fig. 2A), whereas the fungal community was less affected (PERMANOVA, P > 0.05) (Fig. 2B). Co-occurrence network analyses were further conducted to evaluate the co-occurrence patterns of the cotton rhizosphere microbiome by integrating all rhizosphere samples from 2019 to 2021 into monocropping and intercropping groups. Our results indicated that intercropping fostered a more complex microbial co-occurrence network with increased synergistic interactions relative to monocropping, as demonstrated by a greater number of links, a higher average degree, and an increased ratio of positive to negative edges (Fig. 2C, D; Table S4). Furthermore, intercropping resulted in lower modularity, network diameter, and average path length compared to monocropping (Table S4).

Intercropping assembles a distinct and stable core microbiome in cotton root.

Significant differences in bacterial and fungal community compositions were observed between monocropping and intercropping systems in both bulk (Fig. S3) and rhizosphere (Fig. S4) soils. To identify the microbial taxa driving these differences, we performed LEfSe analysis. In bulk soil, a total of 117 unique bacterial biomarkers were identified (FDR-adjusted p < 0.05, the LDA score > 2.25) after excluding unclassified genera (Fig. 3A). Notably, the number of bacterial biomarkers significantly enriched in intercropping increased consistently over the three-year study (monocropping/intercropping: N2019 = 22/12, N2020 = 10/46, N2021 = 18/55) (Table S5). From this dynamic, a core set of 20 bacterial biomarkers emerged as consistently associated with intercropping for at least two consecutive years. This intercropping-enriched core microbiome included the bacterial genera Flavobacterium, Allorhizobium-Neorhizobium-Pararhizobium-Rhizobium, Devosia, Brevundimonas, Enterobacter, Polycyclovorans, Acidibacter, Cellvibrio, Rhizobacter, Pantoea, Lysobacter, Caulobacter, Variovorax, Luteolibacter, Sphingopyxis, Hassallia, Falsirhodobacter, Bosea, Delftia, and Hydrogenophaga.

A similar trend was observed in the rhizosphere, where 52 unique bacterial biomarkers were identified (Fig. 3B), with intercropping-enriched taxa also increasing over time (monocropping/intercropping: N2019 = 10/4, N2020 = 4/21, N2021 = 3/21) (Table S6). Among the six core bacterial taxa (RB41, Pseudoxanthomonas, mle1-7, Hassallia, Dongia, and Bacillus) identified in the rhizosphere, Bacillus was the only genus consistently enriched as a biomarker across all three years, underscoring its potentially essential role in the intercropping system.

Fungal communities also responded to the cropping system. We identified 42 and 25 unique fungal biomarkers in bulk (monocropping/intercropping: N2019 = 16/2, N2020 = 3/1, N2021 = 18/7) (Fig. 3C; Table S7) and rhizosphere soils (monocropping/intercropping: N2019 = 0/1, N2020 = 4/9, N2021 = 4/10) (Fig. 3D; Table S8). Additionally, four core fungal genera—Amanita, Mortierella, Syncephalis, and Titaea—were consistently enriched in the rhizosphere soil under intercropping, whereas no persistent core fungal biomarkers were recognized in the bulk soil.

Isolation and functional screening of bacteria from the intercropping microbiome for IC-SynCom construction

To functionally validate the potential role of the intercropping-enriched microbiome, we employed culture-based methods to isolate microorganisms from both bulk and rhizosphere soils. In total, 76 bacterial isolates were obtained, dominated by six genera: Bacillus (24 isolates), Pseudomonas (13 isolates), Enterobacter (6 isolates), Pantoea (5 isolates), Rhizobium (4 isolates), and Lysobacter (4 isolates) (Fig. S5). Several of these dominant genera overlapped with the amplicon-defined core taxa, whereas the 15 fungal isolates obtained (Aspergillus, Trichoderma, and Fusarium) did not correspond to the fungal biomarkers identified by sequencing.

Functional screening revealed widespread plant growth-promoting and pathogen-suppressive activities of bacterial isolates. Specifically, 39 strains fixed nitrogen, 22 solubilized phosphate, two solubilized potassium, and 19 produced IAA (Table S9). For example, CRB-314 showed the strongest nitrogen-fixing ability. In addition, 37 isolates exhibited direct antagonistic activity against V. dahliae, with inhibition rates ranging from 53.27 to 75.08% (Fig. S6). Among these, Bacillus spp. contributed the majority of strong antagonists, with CRB-021 exhibiting the strongest inhibitory effect (75.08% inhibition). Enterobacter species possessed the most diverse beneficial functions, among which CRB-070 showed the broadest spectrum, including phosphate and potassium solubilization, nitrogen fixation, IAA production, and strongly V. dahliae inhibition (57.42% inhibition).

To assemble a representative and functionally robust IC-SynCom for greenhouse validation, five bacterial isolates were selected: CRB-314 (efficient nitrogen-fixer), CRB-021 (strong antagonist), CRB-070 (multi-functional), CRB-006, and CRB-253. Together, these isolates represented the dominant enriched genera and encompassed a broad spectrum of plant growth-promoting and pathogen-suppressive functions.

IC-SynCom promotes cotton growth and disease resistance by activating inducible defense pathways

Following inoculation with V. dahliae, all microbial inoculant treatments significantly reduced the DI compared with the CK (DI = 36.64 ± 1.06) (Fig. 4A, B). Among the individual strains, CRB-021 achieved the highest control efficacy (41.82 ± 2.05%), corresponding to a DI of 21.32 ± 1.17. Notably, the IC-SynCom treatment exhibited markedly superior biocontrol performance, achieving a control efficacy of 72.83 ± 1.31%—an absolute improvement of approximately 31.01% over CRB-021 (Fig. 4C). In addition to disease suppression, IC-SynCom significantly enhanced cotton growth compared to the other treatments (Fig. 4D–G). Among the individual strains, CRB-070 exhibited superior growth-promoting effects on plant height, root length, fresh weight, and dry weight of cotton. These findings align with in vitro growth-promoting tests, indicating that CRB-070 demonstrated various beneficial functions (Fig. 4E–G).

To further clarify the mechanisms underlying the superior biocontrol efficacy of IC-SynCom, we examined its effects on the expression of cotton defense-related genes. Compared with the CK, IC-SynCom significantly upregulated all tested defense-associated genes at 12 and 24 hpi (Fig. 5). The expression of GhPAL, GhPR10, and GhPOD reached their highest levels at 24 hpi, showing 5.41-, 5.33-, 8.15-fold increases, respectively, relative to the CK. In contrast, GhC4H1 and Ghβ-1,3-glucanase earlier peaked at 12 hpi, with 5.63- and 14.27-fold increases, respectively, under IC-SynCom treatment. Among them, Ghβ-1,3-glucanase exhibited the most pronounced and sustained induction, rising rapidly by 5.73-fold at 1 hpi, peaking at 14.27-fold at 12 hpi, and maintaining high expression levels (14.61-fold) at 24 hpi, and this high expression persisted at 48 and 120 hpi. Conversely, the CK-HKIC treatment triggered only transient and weak activation of these genes, particularly at 1 hpi and 12 hpi, and failed to sustain defense-related transcriptional upregulation over time.

Discussion

In this study, we demonstrated that intercropping cotton with mustard not only substantially suppressed Verticillium wilt and improved plant growth but also consistently reshaped the soil microbial community across years. By integrating amplicon sequencing and culture-based data, we identified an intercropping-enriched core microbiota in both bulk and rhizosphere soils. Functional analyses revealed that these core microbiotas conferred benefits through a multi-pronged mechanism: facilitating nutrient acquisition (e.g., phosphate solubilization, potassium solubilization, nitrogen fixation), producing IAA, and directly antagonizing against V. dahliae. Crucially, by constructing and applying IC-SynCom, we provided direct evidence that these enriched bacteria further enhance plant resistance by activating inducible defense responses. Therefore, we propose that intercropping enhances cotton health primarily by assembling a protective and growth-promoting soil microbiome. Our findings underscore the potential of harnessing core microbiomes through SynCom-based strategies as sustainable tools for agricultural disease management.

Interspecific interactions are recognized as a key factor in shaping sustainable agroecosystems. Our three-year field trial revealed significant reductions in disease severity of Verticillium wilt and the density of V. dahliae microsclerotia under intercropping (Fig. 1). While part of this effect can be attributed to the direct inhibition from mustard-derived glucosinolates [50], the consistent suppression observed during the cotton flowering and boll stages—a period distant from mustard incorporation—points to an additional, microbially mediated mechanism. This aligns with the growing concept that agricultural regimes leave a persistent microbial “legacy” of interspecific interactions that modulates pathogen pressure and enhances plant resilience [51–54]. Such a “legacy” is particularly relevant for soilborne disease suppression, which hinges on microbial activities in both bulk and rhizosphere soils [55]. In our study, intercropping progressively reshaped the bulk and rhizosphere soil microbiomes, with bacterial communities exhibiting the most pronounced shifts (Fig. 2A). The preferential restructuring of bacterial communities is likely driven by the faster growth rates and higher nutrient demands, allowing them to respond more rapidly to the enhanced carbon and nitrogen inputs resulting from interspecific interactions [56]. Consequently, we propose that the recruitment of a beneficial, bacterial-dominant microbiota is key to the sustained biocontrol effect observed in this intercropping system.

No microbiota exists in isolation; rather, it engages in complex interactions with biotic and abiotic factors, exchanging resources and signals. Our network analysis demonstrated that intercropping significantly enhanced the structural complexity and connectivity of the rhizosphere microbial community (Fig. 2C, D). The specific topological shifts—including increases in edge number, average degree, and the ratio of positive correlations, alongside decreases in modularity, network diameter, and average path length—collectively indicate the assembly of a more cohesive and synergistic microbial architecture (Table S4) [57]. Such an intricate and interconnected network structure is widely considered vital for plant health, a conclusion consistent with earlier studies on intercropping systems [58–60]. We propose that this structural integration facilitates efficient resource exchange and functional synergy among microbial members, thereby bolstering the adaptive resilience of the cotton rhizosphere ecosystem against biotic challenges including pathogen invasion [61, 62]. Consequently, the enhanced disease suppression observed in our intercropping system can be attributed more to the emergent properties of the collectively interacting microbiome than to the action of any single taxon. These findings elucidate the mechanisms linking plant diversity to soil health and highlight the importance of engineering synergistic microbial interactions for sustainable agriculture.

Understanding the core microbiome assembled by intercropping is essential for leveraging plant-microbiome interactions against Verticillium wilt. For example, Chen et al. identified Pantoea, Pseudomonas, Sphingomonas, and Alternaria as the core microbiome of Salvia miltiorrhiza seed using membership and persistence methods, demonstrating that these beneficial core microbiota may act as a gene reservoir for secondary metabolism [63]. In this study, we identified a core set of 26 bacterial (20 in bulk soils; 6 in rhizosphere soil) and four fungal taxa (0 in bulk soil; 4 in rhizosphere soil) that were consistently enriched in intercropping soils for at least two consecutive years, based on the criteria of specific recruitment and persistence (Fig. 3). Many of these taxa, such as Allorhizobium-Neorhizobium-Pararhizobium-Rhizobium, Rhizobacter, Pantoea, Lysobacter, Bacillus, and Mortierella, are well-documented beneficial microbes involved in nutrient cycling and pathogen suppression [64–67]. Notably, Bacillus emerged as the most prominent and consistently enriched rhizobacterium across three years, underscoring its central role in intercropping-mediated microbial legacy, a finding congruent with other studies on interspecies interactions [20, 68]. We hypothesize that intercropping fosters the enrichment of this core microbiome by altering the soil environment, particularly through root deposition and modified nutrient dynamics [69].

Although numerous studies have shown that taxonomic composition helps define the members of the core microbiome to a certain extent, the functional traits and ecological behaviors of these taxa should also be considered [70]. The culture-based validation in this study provides direct mechanistic evidence that the intercropping-enriched microbiome contributes to Verticillium wilt suppression and plant growth promotion. Among the dominant bacterial genera enriched under intercropping, Rhizobium, Lysobacter, Enterobacter and Bacillus displayed diverse plant growth-promoting and antagonistic functions, including phosphate and potassium solubilization, nitrogen fixation, and IAA production, as well as inhibition of V. dahliae. The prevalence of such multifunctional taxa within the intercropping microbiome suggests a high degree of functional redundancy and complementarity, features that enhance ecosystem resilience and ensure stable functional performance under fluctuating environmental conditions [71].

The pronounced reduction of Verticillium wilt incidence and the concurrent enhancement of cotton growth following IC-SynCom inoculation highlight the synergistic potential of intercropping-enriched microbial consortia for sustainable disease management. Compared with individual strains, the IC-SynCom exhibited markedly greater disease control efficacy and growth promotion (Fig. 4), suggesting that functional complementarity among consortium members amplifies their collective impact on host physiology and pathogen suppression. This finding is consistent with previous studies showing that microbial consortia often outperform single isolates due to enhanced resource sharing, metabolic cooperation, and niche occupation, which together reinforce plant resilience under biotic stress [72, 73]. The expression patterns of cotton defense-related genes provide mechanistic insight into how IC-SynCom mediates disease resistance. The strong induction of GhPAL, GhPR10, and GhPOD at 24 hpi indicates activation of the salicylic acid (SA)-dependent defense pathway and ROS detoxification, both of which are key components of systemic acquired resistance [74]. Meanwhile, the earlier and sustained upregulation of the GhC4H1 and Ghβ-1,3-glucanase suggests the reinforcement of structural and biochemical barriers, enhancing lignin deposition and hydrolysis of pathogen cell walls [75, 76]. Particularly, Ghβ-1,3-glucanase exhibited rapid and persistent induction up to 120 hpi, implying a prolonged immune priming effect that may protect cotton roots during pathogen colonization [77]. The absence of comparable gene activation in the CK-HKIC underscores that viable microbial activity, rather than passive microbe-associated molecular patterns (MAMPs), is essential for eliciting sustained plant immune responses. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that the IC-SynCom enhances cotton’s resistance to V. dahliae by simultaneously triggering inducible immune pathways and promoting host vigor, thereby linking the functions of microbial community with host defense activation [21, 78].

Despite these promising findings, several limitations warrant further investigation. First, although the growth-promoting and disease-suppressive effects of IC-SynCom were clearly demonstrated under controlled conditions, its long-term stability and efficacy in diverse field environments remain to be evaluated. Second, the potential ecological impact of introducing IC-SynCom into the native soil microbiomes requires careful assessment. Previous studies have shown that beneficial taxa, such as Bacillus, can recruit resident “helper” microbes (e.g., Lysobacter and Bradyrhizobium) through mechanisms including mixed biofilm formation and resource competition, thereby reinforcing pathogen suppression [62, 79, 80]. Accordingly, future research should focus on assessing the interactive dynamics between the introduced SynCom and the resident soil community to ensure its agricultural application is both sustainable and ecologically predictable.

Conclusions

In summary, our findings revealed that root-associated microbiomes, significantly reshaped by agricultural practices, are pivotal in enhancing soil’s capacity to suppress Verticillium wilt. We identified a core set of intercropping-enriched beneficial microbes that, when assembled into the IC-SynCom, achieved robust disease suppression through direct antagonism and activation of inducible systemic resistance in cotton. Mechanistically, IC-SynCom activated SA-dependent defense pathways, ROS detoxification, and structural barriers, collectively reinforcing plant immunity. These findings highlight the potential of leveraging root-associated microbiomes through targeted agricultural practices and rationally designed SynCom to sustainably manage soilborne diseases and improve crop health.
