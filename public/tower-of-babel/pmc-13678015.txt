GATA3-driven CDC20 activation facilitates tumor growth and metastasis through suppressing Bax/Bcl-2 ratio and boosting EMT process in neuroblastoma
Authors: Xiaona Fang, Yingxia Lan, Mengzhen Li, Weiji Xie, Lei Mao, Jinlin Huang, Qiuting Li, Yu Zhang, Mengjia Song, Yi Que, Suying Lu, Juan Wang, Junting Huang, Jia Zhu, Yi Wang, Feifei Sun, Yizhuo Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNeuroblastoma (NB) is a heterogeneous pediatric malignancy in which many high-risk tumors lack MYCN amplification, necessitating the identification of alternative molecular drivers. Here, integrative transcriptomic analyses of MYCN-nonamplified NB cohorts identified cell division cycle 20 (CDC20) as a candidate associated with high-risk disease and metastasis. CDC20 expression was elevated in aggressive NB across subtypes and correlated with adverse clinical outcomes, with particularly informative prognostic value in MYCN-nonamplified patients. Functional studies established CDC20 as a potent oncogene that promotes proliferation, migration, apoptosis resistance, and tumor progression in NB models, regardless of MYCN status. Pharmacological inhibition of CDC20 further supported CDC20 as a potential therapeutic vulnerability in NB. Mechanistically, we found that the transcription factor GATA3 directly bound a specific motif within the CDC20 promoter, as validated by ChIP-qPCR and site-directed point mutagenesis, and transcriptionally activated its expression. Reciprocal genetic epistasis experiments further showed that CDC20 depletion attenuated GATA3-driven oncogenic phenotypes, whereas restoring CDC20 rescued the impaired growth, migration, and survival caused by GATA3 depletion. At the downstream level, CDC20 facilitates NB progression via dual mechanisms: suppressing the pro-apoptotic Bax/Bcl-2 ratio and inducing an epithelial-mesenchymal transition-like phenotype conducive to metastasis. Collectively, our study delineates a previously uncharacterized GATA3-CDC20 regulatory axis that promotes NB progression and identifies CDC20 as a potential therapeutic vulnerability across NB subtypes, while highlighting its particular potential for risk stratification in MYCN-nonamplified disease.Keywords: cell division cycle 20, GATA-binding protein 3, MYCN Proto-oncogene, tumorigenesis, metastasis, neuroblastoma

Neuroblastoma (NB) is the most common extracranial malignant solid tumor in children, accounting for approximately 10% to 15% of pediatric cancers and 15% of childhood cancer-related deaths (1). High-risk NB, as defined by the Children's Oncology Group (COG) risk stratification system, includes patients with MYCN-amplified tumors, those ≥ 18 months of age with International Neuroblastoma Staging System (INSS) stage 4 disease, or other unfavorable clinical/biological features. The 5-year survival rate for patients with high-risk NB remains less than 50%. High-risk NB is characterized by significant tumor heterogeneity and complex biology, which complicates treatment and leads to poor prognosis (2). Diverse genetic and molecular profiles contribute to the multifaceted tumor heterogeneity and unfavorable outcomes (3). In recent years, with more focus on the underlying molecular mechanisms of high-risk and relapsed NB, more innovative and therapeutic targeted strategies have been studied and exploited. For instance, the identification of gain-of-function ALK mutations in familial and sporadic NB has led to the clinical development of ALK inhibitors, such as Lorlatinib, representing a successful targeted therapeutic strategy for a molecularly defined subset of patients (4). Besides, the loss of function of neurofibromin 1 in high-risk NB has been recognized as a potential target for SHP2 inhibition (5). Studies have shown that SHP2 inhibitors can effectively block tumor growth in models with NF1 loss, offering a new avenue for the treatment of relapsed NB cases (6). While considerable progress has been made in deciphering NB's biological mechanisms, obstacles remain (7). Continued research into the molecular underpinnings and the development of novel therapeutic strategies will be beneficial to improve the prognosis of patients with high-risk NB.

MYCN amplification is a critical genetic aberration in a subgroup of high-risk NB, strongly associated with rapid tumor growth, aggressive behavior, advanced disease stages, and unfavorable prognosis (8, 9). Approximately 40% of high-risk NB harbor MYCN amplification, yet most patients do not exhibit this genetic feature, suggesting that additional genetic or molecular abnormalities drive the pathogenesis and progression of high-risk NB without MYCN amplification (10). Recent studies have highlighted the importance of other genetic and molecular factors in determining the prognosis of patients with high-risk NB. For instance, the expression of MYC, a family member of MYCN, has been recognized as a strong oncogenic driver in certain high-risk NB cases (11). This activation occurs through mechanisms such as enhancer hijacking and focal enhancer amplification, and the clinical outcomes for patients with high MYC expression are similar to those with MYCN amplification (11). This suggests that MYC could serve as an alternative biomarker for aggressive disease in the absence of MYCN amplification. Notably, the identification of other genetic alterations and functional signatures provides valuable insights into the biology of high-risk neuroblastoma and highlights the potential for developing more targeted therapeutic strategies for patients lacking MYCN amplification.

While MYCN amplification is a critical driver in a subset of high-risk NB, the molecular underpinnings of aggressive disease in MYCN-nonamplified NB remain poorly characterized, highlighting the need to identify alternative oncogenic drivers. We therefore performed integrative transcriptomic analyses including Weighted Gene Co-expression Network Analysis (WGCNA) and gene enrichment analysis focused on MYCN-nonamplified NB. Genes independently associated with high-risk classification and metastatic disease were integrated to prioritize candidates linked to both aggressive phenotypes, identifying CDC20 as a leading candidate. CDC20, a cell division cycle protein, has been reported as a hallmark of carcinogenesis and cancer progression in several cancers. In osteosarcoma, for instance, CDC20 has been shown to promote cell proliferation and invasion while inhibiting apoptosis, suggesting its potential oncogenic role in various cancers (12). This oncogenic role is partly due to the regulation of proteins such as Bim and p21, which are involved in cell cycle control and apoptosis (13). Besides, the potential of CDC20 to be a prognostic factor and therapeutic target was reported in hematological malignancies as well (14). However, the specific role of CDC20 in NB has not been extensively investigated. We subsequently established the functional relevance of CDC20 using NB models with different MYCN amplification statuses and evaluated its therapeutic vulnerability through complementary genetic and pharmacological approaches. Mechanistically, we identified GATA3 as its direct transcriptional activator and elucidated that CDC20 promotes NB progression by inhibiting apoptosis and enhancing cellular plasticity. Pharmacological inhibition of CDC20 attenuated tumor growth and metastasis. Together, our findings establish a previously uncharacterized GATA3-CDC20 regulatory axis that contributes to NB progression and support CDC20 as a potential therapeutic vulnerability across NB subtypes, while highlighting its particular potential for risk stratification in MYCN-nonamplified disease.

Results

Identification of CDC20 as a candidate oncogene in MYCN-nonamplified high-risk and metastatic NB

To delineate molecular drivers underlying high-risk and metastatic NB without MYCN amplification, we performed Weighted Gene Co-expression Network Analysis (WGCNA) using the expression matrix obtained from NB tumors in the GEO database. We defined "high-risk" per COG criteria and "metastatic" as INSS stage 4, which are available in the clinical metadata of the online datasets. Specifically, differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between the high-risk and non-high-risk groups lacking MYCN amplification (GSE49710) were determined using the "Limma" R package, applying thresholds of |log2-fold change (FC)| > 0.45 and adjusted p value < 0.05. This resulted in a set of 4536 genes for subsequent WGCNA analysis, systematically excluding data from the eight outlier samples by sample clustering (Fig. S1A). Gene clustering assigned all transcripts to specific modules, with the gray designation reserved for genes lacking meaningful co-expression relationships (Fig. S1B). The optimal soft threshold was determined to be 9, as indicated by the analysis function, achieving a signed R2 of 0.85 (Fig. S1C). Using WGCNA's dynamic hybrid tree-cutting algorithm and similar module merging, eight discrete modules were identified, and their relationships were visualized using a heatmap (Fig. 1A). Among these, the MEyellow module was significantly correlated with high-risk disease in MYCN-nonamplified NB (Fig. 1A). A scatter plot was generated to visualize the relationship between module membership and gene significance within this module (Fig. 1B). Among these genes, 108 key genes associated with high-risk NB were isolated for further analysis (designated Geneset-1) (Fig. 1B).

Similarly, DEGs between the metastatic (INSS stage 4) and the non-metastatic (INSS non-stage 4) groups lacking MYCN amplification (GSE85047) were analyzed, and a set of 1638 genes was identified for further WGCNA analysis with an adjusted p value<0.05. Within this WGCNA framework, sample clustering identified and excluded the data from the 28 outlier samples (Fig. S1D). Hierarchical clustering of all genes produced a gene dendrogram, exhibiting distinct color-coded modules (Fig. S1E). Ultimately, by selecting the optimal soft threshold of 22 (Fig. S1F), we discerned two distinct modules and their interrelationship was elucidated through heatmap visualization (Fig. 1C). Consensus clustering analysis revealed a specific gene module that displayed the highest positive correlation with tumor metastatic potential in patients without MYCN amplification, subsequently denoted as Geneset-2 (MEturquoise module, 82 genes) (Fig. 1, C and D). Similarly, correlation analysis was performed by scatter plotting module membership against gene significance values to evaluate the association between intramodular connectivity and phenotypic relevance (Fig. 1D). By overlapping genes from Geneset-1 and Geneset-2, 46 genes were screened out, which may play critical roles in NB cancer aggressiveness independent of MYCN status (Fig. 1E). These candidates were listed in Table S1. Pathway enrichment analysis based on KEGG terms revealed clusters associated with cell cycle and apoptosis pathways (Fig. 1F), while enrichment analysis leveraging Gene Ontology (GO) terms underscored multiple connections to the mitosis process (Fig. 1G). Cross-referencing the enriched genes among these three biological processes yielded five genes identified as potential candidates, including CCNB1, CDC6, TICRR, CDC20, and BIRC5 (Fig. 1H). Notably, several of these genes have been previously implicated in NB biology. For example, CCNB1, CDC6, BIRC5, and TICRR have been reported to promote NB cell-cycle progression, replication stress, tumor aggressiveness, and to show high expression in MYCN-positive NB, respectively, and are all associated with poor clinical outcomes in NB (15, 16, 17, 18). Finally, CDC20 was prioritized for further investigation, as its clinical significance and molecular regulation in NB remain incompletely defined.

CDC20 is a prognostic biomarker associated with aggressive NB, with distinct significance in MYCN-nonamplified patients

To define the clinical significance of CDC20, we analyzed its expression in NB cell lines and tumor tissues from the TARGET and GEO databases. Upregulation of CDC20 was frequently detected in both MYCN-amplified and -nonamplified NB cell lines, with no statistically significant difference between the two groups, compared to normal cell lines in the GSE66586 dataset (Fig. 2A). In patient cohorts, CDC20 expression was significantly higher in high-risk MYCN-nonamplified tumors compared to non-high-risk tumors (Fig. 2B) and in metastatic (INSS stage 4) versus non-metastatic tumors (Figs. 2C and S2A). Specifically, in patients lacking MYCN amplification, CDC20 was dramatically overexpressed in patients with distant metastases compared to those without (Figs. 2D and S2B). Also, the expression of CDC20 was elevated in high-risk tumors versus non-high-risk tumors (Fig. S2C) and in MYCN-amplified tumors versus MYCN-nonamplified tumors (Fig. S2D). These results were reinforced by immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining of CDC20 protein in 98 in-house NB clinical samples (Fig. 2E). The clinicopathological correlation of CDC20 expression in NB in the SYSUCC cohort was presented in Table 1. Upregulation of CDC20 was significantly correlated with high-risk diseases (Pearson χ2 test, p < 0.05) and distant metastases (INSS stage 4) (Pearson χ2 test, p < 0.001) (Fig. 2F). More importantly, Kaplan–Meier analysis revealed that high CDC20 expression predicted worse overall survival (OS), disease-free survival (DFS), and progression-free survival (PFS) in the overall TARGET cohort (Fig. 2G), and our in-house independent validation cohort (Fig. 2H). Importantly, when the survival analysis was restricted to MYCN-nonamplified patients, high CDC20 expression remained a significant predictor of poorer OS and DFS (Fig. 2, I and J). In contrast, within the MYCN-amplified subgroup, high CDC20 correlated only with worse DFS, not OS (Fig. S2, E and F). Furthermore, within the combined high-risk cohort (both MYCN-amplified and -nonamplified), high CDC20 predicted worse DFS but not OS (Fig. S2, G–H). CDC20 mRNA and protein levels were consistently elevated in 5/5 NB cell lines compared to normal cord blood mononuclear cells (CBMCs) (Fig. S3, A–C). Collectively, these results strongly suggest that high expression of CDC20 is closely associated with NB progression and establishes CDC20 as a potential prognostic biomarker for patients with NB. Specifically, CDC20 can be used to further stratify MYCN-nonamplified patients for both high-risk, high metastatic potential and poor prognosis.

CDC20 potentiates cell proliferation and tumorigenicity in NB

Although the prognostic value of CDC20 was particularly evident in MYCN-nonamplified NB, CDC20 expression was not restricted to this subgroup. We therefore used NB cell lines with different MYCN amplification statuses in subsequent functional experiments to determine whether the oncogenic effects of CDC20 were dependent on MYCN status. To explore the oncogenic role of CDC20 across NB subtypes, various loss-of-function studies were conducted in MYCN-amplified cell line SK-N-BE(2) and MYCN-nonamplified SK-N-SH and SK-N-AS cells. NB cell lines with CDC20 knockdown by small-interfering RNA (siRNA) or short-hairpin RNA (shRNA) and CDC20 stable overexpression were established, and the expression of CDC20 was determined by qRT-PCR (Fig. S3, D and E) and western blotting (Figs. 3, A and B and S3F). CDC20 knockdown significantly impaired cell proliferation (Fig. 3C) and colony formation (Fig. 3, D and E). Conversely, CDC20 overexpression enhanced colony formation (Fig. S3G). CDC20-downregulated cells showed enhanced apoptosis ability compared to control cells (Fig. 3, F and G). To evaluate the in vivo pro-tumorigenic function of CDC20, NB cells transfected with shNC or shCDC20 groups were subcutaneously injected into the dorsal flank or right groin areas of NSG mice. Notably, silencing of CDC20 led to a decreased tumorigenesis compared to the control group (Fig. 3, H–K). Taken together, these data indicate that CDC20 facilitates NB tumorigenicity independently of MYCN status both in vitro and in vivo.

CDC20 augments cell migration and tumor metastasis in NB

Given the close association between high CDC20 expression and metastatic status in NB, the effect of CDC20 on NB metastasis was investigated by in vitro cell migration assays and in vivo mouse experiments using the generated CDC20-downregulated cells. As expected, knockdown of CDC20 significantly suppressed the migratory capabilities of NB cells in vitro, as demonstrated by the wound healing assay (Fig. 4A) and transwell migration assay (Fig. 4, B–D). Consistently, overexpression of CDC20 promoted the migratory capacity of NB cells (Fig. S3H). Furthermore, the effect of CDC20 on metastasis was investigated by an in vivo metastatic mouse model through tail vein injection of different groups of luciferase-labelled NB cells into NSG mice. Knockdown of CDC20 potently diminished tumor metastasis in the liver as shown by bioluminescence imaging (Fig. 4E), reduced metastatic nodules on the liver surface (Fig. 4F) and histological analysis (Fig. 4G). Similar results were observed upon CDC20 knockdown in the MYCN-nonamplified SK-N-AS model (Fig. 4H). Collectively, these results indicate that CDC20 promotes NB metastasis in vitro and in vivo.

Pharmacological inhibition of CDC20 reduces tumor growth and metastasis in NB

To further validate the potential therapeutic value of CDC20 in NB, we utilized a pharmacological compound, Apcin (APC inhibitor), which is an inhibitor that targets the CDC20-binding site on the mitotic E3 ligase anaphase-promoting complex/cyclosome (APC/C), thereby blocking the interaction between APC/C and CDC20. Previous studies have revealed that Apcin treatment could suppress cell growth, cell invasion and mobile abilities, as well as induce significant apoptosis in pediatric tumors such as osteosarcoma (13). Data from the Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) database demonstrated that CDC20 was absent or present at low levels in most normal organs and cell types, supporting its propensity as a selective therapeutic target (Fig. S4A). In the present study, Apcin treatment yielded a half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) value of 150∼200 μM in NB cells (Fig. 5A). To further evaluate the therapeutic potential of targeting CDC20, we examined the effects of Apcin in NB cells. Consistent with inhibition of APC/C-CDC20 activity, Apcin treatment resulted in dose-dependent accumulation of Cyclin B1 and securin, two established APC/C-CDC20 substrates, in SK-N-BE(2) and SK-N-AS cells (Fig. S4B). Then in vitro functional assays and in vivo subcutaneous mouse model were performed using NB cells after intervening with concentrations of Apcin. CDC20 inhibition by Apcin treatment significantly attenuated cell clonogenicity (Fig. 5B) and induced apoptosis (Fig. 5C) of NB cells in vitro in a dose-dependent manner. Furthermore, the cell migration capabilities were dramatically reduced after Apcin treatment, as proved by the transwell migration assay (Fig. 5D) and wound healing assay (Fig. 5, E and F). Additionally, the effect of CDC20 inhibition on tumor growth was examined by subcutaneous implantation of NB cells into the groin area of NSG mice, followed by regular Apcin treatment via intraperitoneal injection. The results demonstrated that Apcin treatment strongly inhibited the growth of subcutaneous xenografts derived from both MYCN-amplified (Fig. 5, G and H) and MYCN-nonamplified cells (Fig. 5, I–K), with no effect on mouse body weight (Fig. 5L). To determine whether the phenotypic effects of Apcin were dependent on CDC20, we next compared its activity in control and CDC20-depleted NB cells. Apcin markedly suppressed colony formation and migration in control cells. CDC20 depletion itself significantly impaired these phenotypes, whereas the additional inhibitory effects of Apcin were substantially attenuated when CDC20 was already depleted (Fig. S4, C and D). These findings indicate that depletion of CDC20 reduces the phenotypic responsiveness of NB cells to Apcin. We further performed a reciprocal genetic-pharmacological experiment to determine whether ectopic CDC20 expression could counteract the effects of Apcin. Apcin markedly increased apoptosis and inhibited migration in vector-control cells. In contrast, ectopic CDC20 expression significantly attenuated Apcin-induced apoptosis and partially restored migratory capacity in both NB cell lines (Fig. S4, E and F). Together, the attenuated response to Apcin following CDC20 depletion and the partial reversal of Apcin-induced phenotypes by CDC20 overexpression provide complementary evidence that a substantial component of the antitumor activity of Apcin in NB cells is CDC20-dependent.

Given the relatively high concentrations of Apcin required to inhibit NB cells, we next evaluated Apcin-A as an additional CDC20-targeting pharmacological approach. Apcin-A inhibited cell viability in a dose-dependent manner, with IC50 values of approximately 98 μM in SK-N-BE(2) cells and 47 μM in SK-N-SH cells (Fig. S5A). Consistently, Apcin-A significantly suppressed NB cell proliferation over time (Fig. S5B), increased apoptosis (Fig. S5C), and markedly reduced cell migration (Fig. S5D). Thus, an alternative Apcin-derived compound targeting the APC/C–CDC20 pathway reproduced the major antitumor phenotypes observed following Apcin treatment. Collectively, these results demonstrate that pharmacological inhibition of CDC20 suppresses NB cell growth and tumor progression in models representing different MYCN amplification statuses. Together with the genetic loss-of-function findings, these data support CDC20 as a potential therapeutic vulnerability across NB subtypes.

GATA3 is identified as a direct transcriptional activator of CDC20

To delve deeper into the potential regulatory network, including upstream and downstream mechanisms of CDC20 in NB, we first analyzed the differentially expressed genes between the CDC20high and CDC20low groups. Here, we analyzed RNA-sequencing data from the NB GEO dataset (GSE45547) bioinformatically, which includes tumors across a spectrum of stages and risk groups. NB samples were ranked by CDC20 expression, and the DEGs were identified between the top 20 samples with the highest (CDC20high) and the bottom 20 cases with the lowest (CDC20low) expression. Using Limma (log2 FC ≥ 2 or ≤ −2, p < 0.05), 220 upregulated genes and 205 downregulated genes were found (Fig. 6A). The expression profile of these genes in the two groups was shown (Fig. 6B). For the upstream mechanisms, we mainly focus on the primary regulators of gene expression - transcription factors (TFs). To discern the TFs responsible for observed differential gene expression patterns in CDC20high tumors, a computational enrichment analysis was conducted using ChEA3, a web-based TF Enrichment Analysis (TFEA) platform (19). This tool systematically prioritizes TFs by evaluating the statistical enrichment of overlaps between input gene-sets and precompiled TF-targets assembled from multiple resources such as chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing (ChIP-seq) datasets, knock-down/overexpression studies, and regulatory pathway databases. Specifically, the 220 upregulated genes were presented to the ChEA3 tool, and the results showed that GATA3 was the most likely upstream TF that controlled the upregulation of observed altered genes in NB (Fig. 6C). Surprisingly, public ChIP-seq datasets in several NB cells downloaded from the JASPAR database (20) offered strong evidence that GATA3 may directly bind to the promoter region of CDC20 (Fig. 6D). DepMap analysis using the online database (The Cancer Dependency Map Project at Broad Institute) confirmed that both CDC20 and GATA3 are essential genes in NB models (Fig. 6E). GATA3, a known adrenergic (ADRN) marker and oncogenic driver in NB, has been reported to positively regulate differentiation and proliferation of NB cells (21). The expression of GATA3 was also detected in CBMC and NB cell lines at both the mRNA level (Fig. S6A) and the protein level (Fig. S6, B–D). Expression data from the public datasets also supported the high expression of GATA3 in both MYCN-amplified and -nonamplified NB cell lines relative to normal cell lines (Fig. S6E) and showed an elevated tendency in MYCN-nonamplified tumors (Fig. S6F) as well as MYCN-nonamplified high-risk tumors (Fig. S6G). Also, the pro-tumorigenic and pro-metastatic roles of GATA3 in NB cells were confirmed by various in vitro functional experiments (Fig. S7, A–F). In addition, a significant positive correlation existed between CDC20 and GATA3 expression across multiple NB cohorts, including all NB (Fig. 6F) and MYCN-nonamplified NB (Fig. 6G). Collectively, these findings strongly propose that GATA3 may be the direct upstream TF regulator of CDC20.

GATA3 directly binds to and transcriptionally activates the CDC20 promoter through a specific GATA3-responsive element

Given the possible connection between GATA3 and CDC20 expression, we next pinpointed their regulatory relationship and potential precise binding site. GATA3-silenced cells were generated by siRNA, and the knockdown effect was detected by qRT-PCR (Fig. 7A) and Western blot analysis (Fig. 7B). Upon GATA3 downregulation, the expression of CDC20 was potently reduced in NB cells both at the mRNA level (Fig. 7A) and at the protein level (Fig. 7B). To confirm whether the TF GATA3 could regulate the promoter activity of CDC20, the full-length promoter sequence of CDC20 was introduced into a firefly-luciferase and renilla-luciferase–containing plasmid, which was then co-transfected into different NB cells together with GATA3-siRNA. Dual-luciferase reporter assay was carried out, and the result showed that the CDC20 promoter activity was significantly decreased upon GATA3 knockdown (Fig. 7C), suggesting that GATA3 could directly influence the transactivation of the CDC20 promoter. Subsequently, luciferases-carrying plasmids cloned with different truncated segments were ordered (Fig. 7D). Luciferase reporter assay was then repeated in NB cells transduced with different truncated mutants of the CDC20 promoter containing plasmids. The results displayed that the segments (−1925 to −1425 and −1425 to −925) maintained consistent transactivation activity compared to the full-length of CDC20 promoter and other truncated sequences, suggesting that this region (−1925 to −925) on the CDC20 promoter is crucial for the transactivation of CDC20 (Fig. 7E). To further identify the precise binding site of GATA3 on the CDC20 promoter region, which is responsible for its promoter activity, the prediction of the GATA3 binding motif on the promoter region of CDC20 was performed by JASPAR software. The predicted binding motifs of GATA3 on the CDC20 promoter were shown in Figure 7F and Table S2. Among the six GATA3 predicted binding sites, two (Site-1: 5'-AGATAATA-3' and Site-2: 5'-CGATAATG-3') were located between −1925 and −925 region on the CDC20 promoter (Fig. 7G). Furthermore, deletion mutants of these two sites were cloned into the luciferase reporter plasmid, respectively. Upon applying site-directed mutagenesis on each binding site, we found that deletion of site-2 significantly reduced CDC20 promoter activity in the luciferase reporter assay, while mutagenesis of site-1 showed no significance (Fig. 7H). Because deletion of a promoter sequence could potentially alter local promoter architecture, we further introduced site-directed nucleotide substitutions into the core site-2 GATA3-binding motif while preserving the surrounding promoter sequence. Specifically, the wild-type sequence 5′-CGATAATG-3′ was mutated to 5′-CGCTACTG-3′. Compared with the corresponding wild-type reporter, the site-2 point-mutant reporter exhibited significantly reduced luciferase activity in both SK-N-BE(2) and SK-N-SH cells (Fig. 7I), confirming the functional importance of this motif for CDC20 promoter activity. Finally, ChIP-qPCR demonstrated significant enrichment of GATA3 specifically at the CDC20 promoter region encompassing site 2, whereas no significant enrichment was detected at the other tested promoter regions in SK-N-BE(2) cells (Fig. 7J). Altogether, these results clearly demonstrated that GATA3 could directly bind to CDC20 promoter at the −1425 to −925 region, with the specific binding site (the consensus DNA sequence 5'-CGATAATG-3') at the −1392 to −1385 region. As a result, CDC20 expression could be transcriptionally regulated by GATA3 in NB.

Genetic epistasis establishes CDC20 as a functional downstream effector of GATA3 in NB cells

To establish the functional hierarchy between GATA3 and CDC20, we first detected whether CDC20 could regulate GATA3 expression and found that CDC20 knockdown (Fig. S8, A and B) or overexpression (Fig. S8C) has no effect on GATA3 expression, supporting a unidirectional regulatory relationship in which GATA3 functions upstream of CDC20. We next performed complementary genetic epistasis experiments in SK-N-BE(2) and SK-N-AS cells to determine whether CDC20 mediates the tumor-promoting effects of GATA3. First, CDC20 was depleted in GATA3-overexpressing cells to determine whether CDC20 is required for GATA3-driven phenotypes. qRT-PCR and Western blot analyses confirmed that GATA3 overexpression increased CDC20 expression, whereas concomitant CDC20 knockdown effectively reduced CDC20 expression without substantially affecting the ectopic expression of GATA3 (Fig. 8, A and B). Functionally, GATA3 overexpression markedly enhanced colony formation and migration in both NB cell lines, whereas CDC20 depletion significantly abrogated these GATA3-induced effects (Fig. 8, C and D). These findings indicate that CDC20 is required for the pro-tumorigenic effects of GATA3. We then performed the reciprocal rescue experiment to further establish the functional relationship between GATA3 and CDC20. GATA3 was depleted and CDC20 was subsequently restored by ectopic expression. qRT-PCR and Western blot analyses confirmed efficient GATA3 knockdown and restoration of CDC20 expression in both cell lines (Fig. 8, E and F). GATA3 depletion markedly suppressed colony-forming ability and cell migration, whereas restoration of CDC20 significantly rescued both phenotypes (Fig. 8, G and H). Furthermore, GATA3 depletion increased apoptosis in both SK-N-BE(2) and SK-N-AS cells, and ectopic CDC20 expression significantly attenuated this increase in apoptosis (Fig. 8I). Together, these reciprocal genetic epistasis experiments demonstrate that CDC20 functions downstream of GATA3 and mediates, at least in part, the effects of GATA3 on NB cell growth, migration, and survival.

CDC20 promotes EMT-like properties and apoptotic resistance in neuroblastoma

To explore the downstream mechanisms of CDC20 in NB, transcriptomic analysis between CDC20high and CDC20low NB tissues was performed. In brief, the 20 NB cases with the highest CDC20 expression and the 20 NB cases with the lowest CDC20 expression in the GSE45547 datasets were selected and 220 upregulated genes in CDC20high tumors were identified as shown in Figure 6A. GSEA on these upregulated genes was performed. The hallmarks enrichment demonstrated that this cluster of genes was meticulously related to TNFα signaling via NF-κB, apoptosis, and epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) process (Fig. 9A). The KEGG enrichment revealed DNA replication, epithelial cell signaling and focal adhesion-related pathways in CDC20 high expression NB group (Fig. S9A). We also performed bulk RNA-seq in SK-N-SH cells transfected with CDC20-shNC or sh1. By using Limma (log2 FC ≥ 1 or ≤ −1, p < 0.05), 220 up-regulated genes and 342 downregulated genes were found (Fig. 9B). GSEA on the 342 downregulated genes revealed the consistent enrichment of pathways including apoptosis, migration and cell adhesion (Figs. 9C and S9, B and C). These data further support the role of CDC20 in facilitating tumor growth and metastasis in NB. Consistent with this, next, to investigate whether the pro-metastatic ability of CDC20 was triggered by EMT, the expression of multiple epithelial and mesenchymal markers was detected both at the mRNA level by qRT-PCR (Fig. S9D) and protein level by Western blot (Fig. 9D) in NB cells upon CDC20 knockdown. The results revealed that CDC20 silencing could increase epithelial marker E-cadherin and eliminate mesenchymal markers such as N-cadherin, Vimentin, Snail, Slug, and ZEB1, indicating that CDC20 could promote EMT in NB cells. IHC staining of the three representative markers in mouse tumor tissues from the liver-metastasis site confirmed these changes (Fig. 9E). Given that the upstream regulator of CDC20, GATA3, is a known adrenergic marker of NB (22), we also analyzed adrenergic/mesenchymal transcription factors upon CDC20 knockdown but found no significant changes (Fig. S9E), suggesting CDC20's effects on plasticity are distinct from core lineage regulation. Additionally, since the ratio of Bax to Bcl-2 dictates the cellular susceptibility to apoptosis (23), we used a Western blot method to detect the altered expression of Bax and Bcl-2. The results indicated that upon CDC20 downregulation, the Bax/Bcl-2 ratio was significantly upregulated, leading to higher susceptibility to apoptosis and augmented cell apoptosis (Fig. 9F). Beyond the Bax/Bcl-2 ratio, we investigated whether CDC20 influences a broader spectrum of apoptosis regulators. The qRT-PCR analysis of additional key genes revealed that CDC20 knockdown also led to a significant decrease in the mRNA expression of the apoptosis-related genes BCL2L10 and the executioner caspase CASP3 (Figs. 9G and S9F). The downregulation of BCL2L10 mRNA is consistent with a shift toward a pro-apoptotic state. While the observed decrease in CASP3 mRNA may indicate complex feedback within the apoptosis pathway, these changes collectively underscore that CDC20 modulates a network of apoptosis-associated genes. Together with the functional data on the Bax/Bcl-2 axis, this positions CDC20 as a multi-faceted regulator of apoptotic resistance in NB, with the Bax/Bcl-2 axis representing a central, functionally validated component. These findings suggest that CDC20 promotes NB progression by regulating the EMT process and Bax/Bcl-2 pathways.

Given that CDC20 regulates EMT- and apoptosis-associated proteins, we next examined whether its upstream regulator GATA3 produces corresponding molecular changes. GATA3 depletion in SK-N-BE(2) and SK-N-AS cells reduced the expression of N-cadherin, Slug, and Bcl-2 (Fig. S9, G and H). GATA3 knockdown also decreased ZEB1 and Vimentin expression while increasing Bax expression. These findings indicate that GATA3 and CDC20 regulate concordant molecular programs associated with EMT-like properties and apoptotic resistance in NB cells.

Discussion

Neuroblastoma is a complex pediatric cancer that presents significant challenges in both diagnosis and treatment. While MYCN amplification is a hallmark of high-risk NB, the molecular drivers of aggressive phenotypes in MYCN-nonamplified high-risk cases are poorly understood. This highlights the need for a broader investigation of the genetic and transcriptomic molecular landscape in NB beyond MYCN amplification. A recent study revealed that MYCN-nonamplified neuroblastomas exhibited transcriptional heterogeneity and could be stratified into three distinct molecular subtypes with divergent clinical outcomes (24). And the Subgroup_2 with the poorest outcomes exhibited an overexpression of Aurora Kinase A (AURKA), demonstrating that AURKA may be a potentially oncogenic driver of MYCN-nonamplified NB (24). Furthermore, the genomic landscapes of MYCN-nonamplified NB have been studied through whole-exome sequencing (WES) and whole transcriptome sequencing (WTS). Intriguingly, high-risk tumors exhibited enriched genomic alterations in metabolic pathways, underscoring the existence of genomic heterogeneity within MYCN-nonamplified cases (25). This study identifies and characterizes the oncogenic role of CDC20 in neuroblastoma, with a focus on addressing the unmet need for drivers in the MYCN-nonamplified high-risk subgroup. While our initial bioinformatic screen targeted this subgroup, functional validation revealed that CDC20's oncogenic role extends across NB subtypes. However, its clinical utility as a biomarker is context-dependent: in MYCN-nonamplified patients, high CDC20 expression is a powerful indicator of high-risk disease, metastasis, and poor OS and DFS. In MYCN-amplified patients, who already have a poor prognosis, CDC20 adds value primarily by predicting worse DFS, likely reflecting metastatic potential. This nuanced prognostic signature underscores CDC20's particular relevance for risk stratification within the heterogeneous MYCN-nonamplified population. The inclusion of both MYCN-amplified and MYCN-nonamplified models therefore allowed us to distinguish the subgroup-specific prognostic utility of CDC20 from its broader oncogenic activity. These observations suggest that CDC20 may represent a common functional vulnerability across molecularly distinct NB subtypes, while its prognostic relevance may be particularly useful for refining risk assessment among MYCN-nonamplified patients. Future studies in larger, prospectively characterized cohorts will be required to determine whether CDC20 can provide prognostic information independent of established clinical and molecular risk factors.

Pan-cancer analyses revealed ubiquitous CDC20 overexpression across malignancies, correlating with advanced disease stage, metastatic propensity, and inferior overall survival (26). These parallels underscore the oncogenic role of CDC20 across cancers, supporting its potential as a therapeutic target. The therapeutic potential of targeting CDC20 was substantiated by Apcin-mediated inhibition, which attenuated tumor growth and metastasis in preclinical models (26, 27, 28). In our study, therapeutic targeting of CDC20 with Apcin showed efficacy in preclinical models. The relatively high in vitro IC50 of Apcin required in our NB models warrants caution, as off-target effects at such concentrations cannot be ruled out. To address this, we integrated biochemical and genetic approaches. Apcin induced accumulation of Cyclin B1 and securin, consistent with APC/C–CDC20 inhibition. Importantly, CDC20 depletion reduced Apcin sensitivity, whereas CDC20 overexpression partially rescued Apcin-induced apoptosis and migration defects, supporting a CDC20-dependent mechanism. In addition, the alternative compound Apcin-A showed improved potency and recapitulated the major anti-tumor effects of Apcin. Together with genetic loss-of-function data, these findings support pharmacological targeting of CDC20 in NB, although compound specificity remains incomplete and more potent, selective inhibitors are needed for translational development.

The consistent effects of genetic CDC20 depletion and pharmacological CDC20 inhibition in models with different MYCN amplification statuses suggest that the oncogenic dependency on CDC20 is not restricted to MYCN-nonamplified NB. Thus, CDC20 may represent a therapeutic vulnerability across NB molecular subtypes, whereas its particular relevance in MYCN-nonamplified disease lies in its potential utility for additional prognostic and risk stratification. Nevertheless, given the relatively high concentrations of Apcin required to inhibit NB cells, further development and evaluation of more potent, and selective CDC20-targeting approaches will be necessary before this strategy can be translated therapeutically.

For the upstream mechanism, we identified GATA3 as the direct transcriptional activator of CDC20, a previously uncharacterized regulatory relationship in NB. While GATA3 is a core component of the adrenergic state often linked to MYCN-amplified tumors (21, 29, 30), our analysis shows it is also highly expressed in a subset of aggressive MYCN-nonamplified cases. In this context, GATA3 appears to activate alternative pro-tumorigenic programs, with CDC20 being a prime example. This expands GATA3's functional repertoire beyond lineage maintenance to include direct regulation of cell cycle and plasticity regulators, highlighting the multifaceted role of lineage factors in cancer progression. This provides a mechanistic hypothesis for how adrenergic tumors might co-opt a differentiation factor to fuel progression, highlighting the importance of epigenetic and transcriptional drivers in MYCN-independent tumors. Further validation in other cancers could clarify whether this axis is NB-specific or a broader oncogenic mechanism.

The multifaceted characters of CDC20 and its downstream mechanisms have been well explored and reported. As an evolutionarily conserved coactivator of the anaphase-promoting complex/cyclosome (APC/C), CDC20 serves as a critical mitotic checkpoint regulator by orchestrating the metaphase-to-anaphase transition through timed degradation of securin and cyclin B. This ensures faithful chromosome segregation and genomic fidelity. However, cancers could hijack this process to promote mitotic progression and accelerate malignant proliferation. The aberrant overexpression of CDC20 disrupts mitotic fidelity, induces chromosomal instability, and fosters tumorigenic evolution (31). In this study, CDC20 facilitated cell proliferation in NB, which may be attributed to its canonical role as an APC/C coactivator in mitotic exit. Additionally, emerging evidence suggests that CDC20 may regulate apoptotic signaling and EMT beyond its canonical mitotic function. For example, CDC20 has been reported to promote ubiquitination-mediated degradation of pro-apoptotic regulators such as Bim (32) and pro-metastatic markers such as FOXO1 (33), thereby attenuating apoptosis and metastasis in multiple cancer contexts. The non-canonical role of CDC20 in apoptotic resistance via Bax/Bcl-2 ratio suppression in NB also aligns with its role in other cancers (34, 35). The Bax/Bcl-2 axis is a pivotal determinant of apoptosis in cancer, implicated in tumorigenesis, metastasis, and therapeutic response (36, 37). In our study, CDC20 promotes NB progression through dual mechanisms: the Bax/Bcl-2 imbalance and EMT induction. These findings align with emerging evidence linking CDC20 to metastatic programs in other cancers but are newly established in NB (38, 39). Although the precise substrates of CDC20 in neuroblastoma remain to be fully elucidated, these findings provide a plausible mechanistic framework through which CDC20 upregulation may contribute to apoptosis resistance and metastasis in this disease.

In conclusion, our study integrates multi-omics data, functional assays, and clinical validation to delineate the role of CDC20 in NB oncogenicity. We establish CDC20 as a GATA3-regulated oncoprotein that drives NB progression through EMT induction and apoptotic resistance, supporting CDC20 as a potential therapeutic vulnerability across NB subtypes while highlighting its particular potential for improving risk stratification in MYCN-nonamplified disease. Further studies will be required to establish the clinical utility of CDC20-based stratification and to develop more potent and selective approaches for therapeutic CDC20 inhibition.

Experimental procedures

NB cell lines and clinical specimens

The human cell lines, including cord blood mononuclear cell (CBMC) and immortalized NB cell lines like SH-SY5Y, SK-N-SH, SK-N-BE(2), IMR32, and SK-N-AS were purchased from COBIOER BIOSCIENCES. All cell lines were confirmed to be mycoplasma-free with authenticated STR profiles. SK-N-SH and IMR32 cells were maintained in Minimum Essential Medium (MEM; Gibco, USA) with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, HyClone, USA), 1% Non-Essential Amino Acids (NEAA; Gibco) and 1% Sodium pyruvate (Gibco, USA). SK-N-AS was cultured in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; Gibco) supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% NEAA. SK-N-BE(2) and SH-SY5Y were cultured in a mixture of MEM and Ham's F-12 Nutrient Mixture (F-12; Gibco) (1:1) with 10% FBS, 1% NEAA and 1% Sodium pyruvate. All culture media contained 1% penicillin/streptomycin. Cell cultures were maintained at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 humidified atmosphere. This study utilized 98 neuroblastoma (NB) specimens collected from patients treated at Sun Yat-sen University Cancer Center (SYSUCC). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants for the research use of clinical samples. This clinical study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki principles and the protocol and sample usage were approved by the Committee for Ethical Review of Research Involving Human Subjects at Sun Yat-sen University Cancer Center (SYSUCC).

Antibodies and reagents

The following primary antibodies were used: β-actin (1:1000, P60709, Cell Signaling Technology), GAPDH (1:1000, AC001, Abclonal), CDC20 (1:1000, Q105, Bioworld Technology), GATA3 (1:1000, MA1-028, Invitrogen), N-cadherin (1:1000, ab207608, Abcam), E-cadherin (1:1000, ab231303, Abcam), Vimentin (1:1000, ab8978, Abcam), Snail (1:1000, ab216347, Abcam), Slug (1:1000, ab302780, Abcam, USA), ZEB1 (1:1000, A21794, Abclonal), Bcl-2 (1:1000, A19693, Abclonal, China), Bax (1:1000, A19684, Abclonal), Cyclin B1 (1:1000, AF6168, Affinity), Securin (1:1000, YP-mAb-00502, UpingBio). The CDC20 inhibitor, Apcin, was purchased from TargetMol Sciences (Cat. No. T8561). The CDC20 inhibitor, Apcin-A, was purchased from MCE (Cat. No. HY-130841).

Plasmids, oligos and transfection

CDC20 shRNA plasmids (pLKO.1-EGFP-Puro) were obtained from Dahong (Cat. No. SHNM_001255), GATA3 shRNA plasmids (Cat. No. HSH007165-LVRU6H), CDC20 (Cat. No. EX-B0269-Lv105) and GATA3 (Cat. No. EX-Z3102-Lv151) full-length containing plasmids were obtained from the GeneCopoeia. The lentiviral transfection procedure was performed following the manufacturer's protocol. The oligos ordered from GenePharma were as follows: negative control siRNA (siNC) or siRNAs targeting CDC20 (siCDC20) or siRNAs targeting GATA3 (siGATA3). The plasmids used for the luciferase reporter assay were purchased from GeneCopoeia: the luciferase reporter plasmids containing the full-length CDC20 promoter and its truncated plasmids, as well as site-directed mutants of the CDC20 promoter (pEZX-PL01). Lentivirus was generated by transfecting 293FT cells with viral plasmids. NB cells were infected with viral supernatants. The siRNA products were transiently transfected into NB cells. All oligo sequences were available in Table S3 (Supporting Information).

qRT-PCR and Western blotting

RNA was extracted using TRIzol (Invitrogen), followed by cDNA synthesis (TaKaRa High Fidelity kit). For quantitative analysis, qRT-PCR was executed using SYBR Green PCR Kit (Dongsheng Biotech) and the primer pairs listed in Table S4 (Supporting Information). Conventional RT-PCR was conducted with DreamTaq DNA polymerase (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Gene expression at the protein level was examined by Western blot analysis according to previously published methodology (40). Antibody specificity was assessed based on the expected molecular weight and, where applicable, validated by the expected changes in protein abundance following shRNA-mediated knockdown or ectopic overexpression of the corresponding target.

Cell viability and proliferation assays

Cell viability was quantified using a CCK-8 kit (APE x BIO) following the manufacturer's instructions. The clonogenic potential of tumor cells was assessed through colony formation assays, measuring their ability to proliferate under anchorage-dependent conditions. Briefly, 3 × 103 cells were seeded in a 6-well plate. Different concentrations of the CDC20 inhibitor Apcin or Apcin-A and DMSO were supplemented. After 14 days, viable colonies were fixed with methanol, stained using 0.5% crystal violet solution, and quantified by manual counting.

Wound healing and cell migration assays

Cells were suspended and seeded into 12-well plates with scratch plug-in components (ibidi), and cultured in 0.5% FBS medium overnight. The scratch plug-in components were removed and cell debris was washed out with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Different concentrations of the CDC20 inhibitor Apcin or Apcin-A and DMSO were supplemented. Baseline images of the scratch were captured using a phase-contrast microscope. After 24 to 48 h of standard incubation to allow cell migration, the same scratch regions at defined intervals were re-imaged. Quantification of the scratch area (Migration rate) was measured using image analysis software ImageJ. For the migration assay, transwell inserts (8 μm pore; Corning) were utilized. Following 24 h serum starvation for synchronization, cells were seeded in serum-free DMEM in the upper chamber, with 10% FBS-containing medium as a chemoattractant in the lower chamber. After 24 h standard incubation, migrated cells were fixed with paraformaldehyde, stained with crystal violet, and then photographed using a microscope. The quantification of migrated cells was performed using ImageJ software.

Apoptosis assay

The cell apoptosis was assessed by Annexin V staining (4A Biotech), according to the manufacturer's protocols. Different concentrations of the CDC20 inhibitor Apcin or Apcin-A and DMSO were supplemented to the culture medium. Briefly, 5 × 105 cells per well were suspended in 15 ml tubes. Cells were then stained with Annexin V-Alexa Fluor 647 and 7-AAD dyes in 1 × binding buffer provided by the kit after washing with PBS twice. Incubation at room temperature in the dark for 15 min was required for thorough staining, and flow cytometry was applied to detect apoptotic cells with Annexin-V positive, which was calculated as the apoptotic index.

In vivo mouse experiments

The mice were housed in a pathogen-free laboratory animal unit at the Sun Yat-sen University (SYSU). All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with protocols approved by the Sun Yat-Sen University Cancer Center Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee. For tumorigenicity assessment, 5 × 106 NB cells were subcutaneously injected into the back region of 4-week-old NSG or NOD-SCID mice. Tumor growth kinetics were monitored by weekly volumetric measurements (0.5 × length × width2). Following a period of 4 to 5 weeks, the mice were euthanized, and the subcutaneous tumors were harvested and imaged. For metastasis assessment, 2 × 106 NB cells labelled with luciferase were injected into the tail vein of 4-week-old NSG mice. Tumor incidence and tumor growth were monitored weekly using the IVIS Spectrum imaging system (PerkinElmer) following intraperitoneal administration of 100 mg/kg D-luciferin (Promega) into the mice under isoflurane anesthesia. At the study endpoint, bioluminescence images of mice were acquired prior to euthanasia, followed by liver excision for ex vivo analysis. Metastatic burden was quantified by counting surface tumor nodules on hepatic tissue. Then the livers were embedded and subjected to further histological and IHC experiments. For the in vivo drug treatment animal model, after construction of the subcutaneous tumorigenicity model as described earlier, the CDC20 inhibitor (Apcin) was utilized by intraperitoneal injection at 20 mg/kg every other day for 2 weeks, a dosing regimen previously shown to be effective and well-tolerated in preclinical xenograft models (28, 41), starting from the 14th day. Tumor volumes and mouse body weights were monitored every 2 days and calculated by the same formula. Following a period of 4 to 5 weeks, the mice were euthanized, and the subcutaneous tumors were harvested and imaged. For all subcutaneous tumor models, mice were euthanized when tumors reached the experimental endpoint (diameter > 20 mm), or earlier upon meeting predefined humane endpoints, including severe weight loss (> 20% of initial weight), tumor ulceration/necrosis, or impaired ambulation and feeding.

H&E and IHC staining

The excised liver tissues were fixed, dehydrated and embedded into paraffin blocks. Serial sections (4–6 μm thickness) were cut using a microtome and mounted on glass slides. The section slides were then stained with H&E for visualization of liver metastasis. For the IHC staining, clinical NB specimen slides were incubated with mouse anti-human CDC20 (Abcam) primary antibody as previously described (42). Mouse metastatic liver sections were subjected to E-cadherin, N-cadherin, and Slug primary antibodies for the detection of epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition. IHC images were acquired using a digital pathology slide scanner (KF-PRO-040-HI). The staining results were semi-quantitatively assessed by evaluating both staining intensity and the percentage of positive tumor cells. Intensity was graded as: 0 (negative), 1 (weak), 2 (moderate), or 3 (strong). The proportion of stained cells was categorized as: 0 (<25%), 1 (25–50%), 2 (50–75%), or 3 (75–100%). A combined Q-Score (intensity × percentage) was calculated, with scores 0 to 4 considered as low expression and 5 to 9 as high expression.

Immunofluorescence staining

Cells grown on glass-bottom culture dishes (Nest Scientific) were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA, Sigma-Aldrich), permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 5 min, and washed with PBS. Following blocking with 5% BSA for 1 h at room temperature, primary antibody incubation was conducted overnight at 4 °C in 5% BSA solution. After three PBS washes, cells were subsequently stained with a fluorescence-labeled secondary antibody (Alexa Fluor 594-conjugated antibody, FuDe, China) for 1 h at room temperature. DAPI dye (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) was used for cell nucleus staining. After cell mounting, fluorescence images were acquired with a Zeiss LSM 800 confocal system.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP)-qPCR assay

The SimpleChIP Enzymatic Chromatin IP Kit (Magnetic Beads) (CST#9003) was used to extract chromatin after lysis and sonication from formaldehyde-treated SK-N-BE(2) cells with cross-linked DNA and the binding protein on chromatin. 30 μl of pretreated Protein A/G agarose beads and anti-GATA3 antibody or control Rabbit IgG (CST#2729) antibody were added to the lysate and incubated overnight at 4 °C, and the precipitated DNA was collected. The extracted chromatin DNA was used as the template for qPCR based on specific primers (listed in Table S5) to detect the binding of GATA3 at different truncated promoter regions of CDC20.

Dual-luciferase reporter assays

The Dual Luciferase Reporter Assay Kit purchased from Promega (E1910) was used according to the manufacturer's directions. In brief, the full-length or different truncated sequences of the CDC20 promoter or site-directed mutants of the CDC20 promoter were cloned into the pEZX-PL01 control vector containing firefly luciferase as well as Renilla luciferase. The reporter plasmids (GeneCopoeia) were transfected into SK-N-BE(2) or SK-N-SH cells in 12-well plates, respectively. Two predicted GATA3-binding sites (−1649 to −1642 and −1392 to −1385) were analyzed. In addition to deletion constructs, a Site-2 point mutant (5′-CGATAATG-3′ to 5′-CGCTACTG-3′) was generated to preserve promoter context. After incubation for 24 to 48 h, cell lysis were collected, and luciferase assay substrate (Luciferase Assay Reagent II) was added. Luciferase activity was assessed using a dual-reporter system: firefly luciferase measurement followed by Renilla luciferase after signal quenching. Data were normalized as firefly/renilla ratios to control for transfection variation and cell viability.

RNA sequencing and differential expression analysis

Total RNA was extracted from SK-N-SH cells stably expressing shNC or shCDC20 and subjected to bulk RNA sequencing. After quality control, sequencing reads were processed, aligned to the human reference genome, and quantified for gene expression. Differentially expressed genes between shNC and shCDC20 groups were identified using Limma with the indicated thresholds. Pathway enrichment was evaluated by GSEA. The RNA-seq dataset has been deposited in GEO under accession number GSE318447.

Bioinformatics analyses

Transcriptomic analysis was performed using publicly available NB RNA-sequencing datasets from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) and Therapeutically Applicable Research to Generate Effective Treatments (TARGET) databases to investigate gene co-expression networks and potential downstream molecular mechanisms. For the identification of modules associated with high-risk and metastatic phenotypes in MYCN-nonamplified NB, weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) was conducted using the Limma package in R to identify biologically meaningful gene modules through the online ImageGP2 platform (43). First, the expression matrix from datasets GSE49710 and GSE85047 (MYCN-nonamplified patients) was used and the differentially expressed genes between high-risk and non-high-risk groups as well as INSS stage 4 and non-stage 4 groups were acquired using the Limma package in R. The differentially expressed genes were then submitted to further WGCNA. In brief, a gene co-expression network was constructed after data preparation and filtering. Modules (pathways) were identified through hierarchical clustering and dynamic tree cut, and their expression patterns were summarized by calculating module eigengenes (MEs, the first principal component of each module). MEs were related to external phenotypic or clinical traits (e.g., disease metastasis status, disease risk groups) to identify biologically relevant modules. Finally, key drivers in the significant modules were identified for further study. The 20 cases of patients with the highest CDC20 expression and the 20 cases with the lowest CDC20 expression were selected for downstream differential gene expression (log2 FC ≥ 2, p < 0.05) analysis using the Limma package in R (44). Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was employed to identify significantly enriched biological pathways. Genes were ranked based on their differential expression correlation. The enrichment score (ES) for each predefined gene set from the MSigDB database was calculated, reflecting the overrepresentation of its members at the ranked list's extremes. Statistical significance was determined using phenotype-based permutation testing (1000 permutations), yielding a normalized enrichment score (NES) and a false discovery rate (FDR). Gene sets with |NES| > 1.5 and FDR q-value < 0.25 were considered significantly enriched.

Statistical analysis

All reported experimental data from three independent experiments that obey a normal distribution are shown as mean ± SD, otherwise they are described by median ± quartiles. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 21.0; International Business Machines Corporation) or GraphPad Prism 9 software (GraphPad Software, Inc.). Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Parametric or nonparametric statistical tests were selected according to whether the data satisfied the normality assumption. Two-tailed unpaired Student's t test was utilized to compare the means of two distinct groups of continuous variables. For data following a normal distribution, comparisons among multiple groups were performed using one-way ANOVA followed by Dunnett's or Tukey's test for post hoc pairwise comparisons. For data that were not normally distributed, nonparametric tests were applied: the Mann-Whitney U test for comparisons between two groups, and the Kruskal–Wallis test with Dunn's post hoc analysis for comparisons across more than two groups. Two-way ANOVA with appropriate multiple-comparisons tests (Dunnett or Bonferroni) was applied for experiments involving two variables or repeated conditions. Correlations were assessed using Pearson correlation. Kaplan-Meier curves and log-rank tests were used for all survival analyses. Pearson's Chi-square (χ2) test was applied to analyze the correlation between clinicopathological parameters and gene expression. Specific statistical tests are detailed in the corresponding figure legends.
