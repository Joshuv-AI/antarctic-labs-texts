Can Magnetic Resonance Imaging Diagnose Pediatric Inflammatory Demyelinating Disorders? A Tertiary Referring Center Experience in Chennai, Tamil Nadu—A Cross-Sectional Study
Authors: Philson J. Mukkada, Teenu Franklin, Prabhu Radhan, Vinoth Thangam
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAim:This study aimed to evaluate magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) in diagnosing PIDDs in pediatric patients, analyzing imaging findings alongside clinical correlations.Materials and Method:A prospective cross-sectional study was conducted at a tertiary referring center in Chennai, involving 34 pediatric patients (aged ≤18 years) referred with suspected or confirmed PIDDs. MRI evaluations, clinical assessments, and laboratory findings were utilized for diagnosis. Demographic data, MRI findings, and clinical symptoms were collected and analyzed using descriptive statistics.Results:The study revealed a wide age distribution (mean age: 8.53 years), with males comprising 55.9% of participants. Acute demyelinating encephalomyelitis (ADEM) was the most common clinical presentation, with supratentorial lesions being the predominant MRI finding. Overall, MRI demonstrated high sensitivity (82.1%) and specificity (79.3%) in diagnosing PIDDs.Conclusion:MRI proved valuable in diagnosing pediatric inflammatory demyelinating disorders. Further research should address study limitations and explore longitudinal outcomes to enhance understanding and management of these complex neurological conditions.

INTRODUCTION

Pediatric inflammatory demyelinating disorders (PIDDs) encompass a spectrum of rare autoimmune conditions affecting the central nervous system (CNS) in children. These disorders, including but not limited to acute disseminated encephalomyelitis (ADEM), multiple sclerosis (MS), neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder (NMOSD), and acute hemorrhagic leukoencephalitis (AHLE), pose significant diagnostic challenges due to their diverse clinical presentations and overlapping features. Multiple sclerosis (MS) stands as a chronic, debilitating, and economically burdensome neurodegenerative condition.[12] Although the typical onset of MS symptoms occurs between the ages of 20 and 40, a notable portion, ranging from 3 to 10%, experience their initial symptoms at a younger age, including childhood. Compared to adults, children with MS often exhibit more frequent relapses, with rates two to three times higher, and show larger lesion volumes on MRI, particularly in the posterior fossa region.[3]

Environmental factors in early life may play a role in influencing the risk of developing MS, making pediatric populations crucial for identifying epidemiological shifts in the disease.[45] Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) has emerged as a pivotal tool in the diagnosis and monitoring of PIDDs, offering valuable insights into the extent and nature of demyelination and inflammation within the CNS.[6] In this study, we present the experience of a tertiary referring center in utilizing MRI to diagnose PIDDs in pediatric patients, aiming to evaluate the accuracy and precision of MRI in delineating these complex neurological conditions.

MATERIALS AND METHOD

A prospective cross-sectional study was to analyze the experience of a tertiary referring center in the Department of Radio-diagnosis at ACS Medical College and Hospital, Chennai. The study was conducted for a period of 12 months from April 2023 to April 2024. The sample size taken for this study was 34 patients. The study population consists of pediatric patients (aged ≤ 18 years) who presented to the tertiary referring center. The inclusion criteria of the study were pediatric patients aged ≤18 years, patients referred to the tertiary center with suspected or confirmed PIDDs, and patients who gave consent to participate in the study. The exclusion criteria were patients more than 18 years, presence of metallic implants, severe claustrophobia, and known history of PIDDs were excluded in this study. The data collection consists of demographic information like age and sex, and MRI findings are collected for each patient. MRI images are reviewed to confirm the presence of demyelinating lesions and to assess imaging characteristics relevant to PIDD diagnosis. The collected data were entered and interpreted using the IBM Statistical Package for the Social Sciences 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, New York, 2019). Descriptive statistics were used for frequency, percentage, mean, and standard deviation of PIDDs.

DISCUSSION

The present study provides valuable insights into the demographic characteristics, clinical manifestations, and diagnostic utility of magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) in pediatric patients with inflammatory demyelinating disorders. Firstly, the age distribution of the study participants revealed a wide range, with the majority falling within the 6–10 years age group, followed by the 1–5 years and 11–18 years age groups. The study conducted by Brenton in the year 2022 has similar study findings, showing that more than 5 to 10 years are affected by pediatric inflammatory demyelinating disorders.[7] This distribution highlights the importance of considering pediatric patients across various age ranges when evaluating inflammatory demyelinating disorders, as these conditions can affect children of different developmental stages. The mean age in the present study found was 8.5 years. Furthermore, our findings indicated a slightly higher prevalence of male participants compared to females, with males comprising 55.9% of the study population. The study conducted by Absoud et al.[8] in the year 2013 has similar study findings, showing that more males are affected with pediatric inflammatory demyelinating disorders than females. The study conducted by Pereira et al.[9] in the year 2021 has contrasting findings, showing that more females are affected with pediatric inflammatory demyelinating disorders than males.

Next, regarding clinical symptoms, the most common presentation among our study cohort was acute demyelinating encephalomyelitis (ADEM), followed by optic neuritis, transverse myelitis, and neuromyelitis optica disorders. The study conducted by Manori prasadani Thambiliyagodage et al.[10] in the year 2021 has similar study findings, showing that acute demyelinating encephalomyelitis was more affected in pediatric inflammatory demyelinating disorders.

In MRI findings, our study demonstrated the presence of various lesion types, including supratentorial, infratentorial, and spinal cord lesions, as well as optic nerve involvement. In the present study, MRI findings show that supratentorial lesions (44.1%), infratentorial lesions (29.4%), spinal cord lesions (52.9%), and optic nerve involvement (35.3%). Among these findings, the spinal cord lesion is more prevalent than others. The study conducted by Sakshi Jeswani et al.[11] in the year 2023 has similar study findings, showing that the spinal cord lesion is the most common finding found in the present study. These results underscore the importance of MRI as a valuable diagnostic tool in pediatric neurology, providing clinicians with essential information for diagnosing pediatric patients with inflammatory demyelinating disorders.

CONCLUSION

Despite its limitations, this study contributes valuable insights into the diagnostic approach and clinical characteristics of pediatric inflammatory demyelinating disorders, emphasizing the importance of MRI in facilitating accurate diagnosis and management decisions. Overall, this study highlights the critical role of MRI in improving the diagnosis and management of pediatric inflammatory demyelinating disorders, ultimately enhancing patient care and outcomes.
