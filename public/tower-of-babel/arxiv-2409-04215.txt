A Method of Fundamental Solutions for Large-Scale 3D Elastance and Mobility Problems
Anna Broms, Alex H. Barnett, Anna-Karin Tornberg

Abstract
The method of fundamental solutions (MFS) is known to be effective for solving 3D Laplace and Stokes Dirichlet boundary value problems in the exterior of a large collection of simple smooth objects. Here we present new scalable MFS formulations for the corresponding elastance and mobility problems. The elastance problem computes the potentials of conductors with given net charges, while the mobility problem—crucial to rheology and complex fluid applications—computes rigid body velocities given net forces and torques on the particles. The key idea is orthogonal projection of the net charge (or forces and torques) in a rectangular variant of a “completion flow.” The proposal is compatible with one-body preconditioning, resulting in well-conditioned square linear systems amenable to fast multipole accelerated iterative solution, thus a cost linear in the particle number. For large suspensions with moderate lubrication forces, MFS sources on inner proxy-surfaces give accuracy on par with a well-resolved boundary integral formulation. Our several numerical tests include a suspension of 10000 nearby ellipsoids, using 2.6 × 10 7 2.6\times 10^{7} total preconditioned degrees of freedom, where GMRES converges to five digits of accuracy in under two hours on one workstation. Key words: Elliptic PDE, mobility, Stokes flow, rigid bodies, completion formulation

Systems of microscale rigid particles immersed in viscous fluids describe a wide range of phenomena in nature and industry. Examples include transport or diffusion processes

[

, collective order in biological systems or in materials science
[

,
and assemblies of
functionalized nanoparticles, with applications in imaging and drug delivery
[

.
Numerical modeling of the dynamics of such systems at zero Reynolds number (negligible inertia) requires solving at each time-step the so-called
mobility problem
for the unknown rigid body motions of every particle, given their net forces and torques, with the Stokes equations governing the flow in the fluid domain.
Efficient solvers for this boundary value problem (BVP) are thus needed which can scale to large particle numbers.

For an overview of methods for solving the Stokes mobility problem, see Maxey
[
16
]
, and the PhD theses of Bagge
[
17
]
and Peláez
[
18
]
. In the literature on approximate methods, such as the rigid multiblob method and Stokesian dynamics, the mobility problem is solved via saddle-point linear systems, where the given net forces and torques on each particle appear as additional constraints. These square systems can be efficiently preconditioned and solved via acceleration by fast summation techniques
[
19
,
20
]
. Turning to convergent methods involving exact Green’s functions, the most popular is boundary integral equations (BIE), in which the flow is represented as a surface layer potential whose jump relation is exploited to give a Fredholm second-kind system
[
21
,
22
,
23
,
24
,
25
,
5
]
.
While iterative solution with fast summation is effective
[
24
,
26
]
,
the challenges of discretization of on-surface weakly-singular integral operators, and quadratures to accurately evaluate the potential near to particle surfaces, remain. For smooth deformations of a sphere, the self-interaction (“one-particle”) operator may be discretized globally with high order or spectral accuracy using either Galerkin
[
27
]
or Nyström
[
28
,
21
]
methods.
For spherical particles, symmetry may be exploited to diagonalize the on-surface operators and the close-evaluation problem using vector spherical harmonics
[
22
]
. In state-of-the-art work by Yan et al.
[
23
]
, this was applied to systems of up to 80,000 spheres, using
4\times 10^{7}
degrees of freedom, distributed across 1792 CPU cores.

Also using exact Green’s functions—but rarely applied in the Stokes setting—is the method of fundamental solutions (MFS). Here an interior rather than surface source distribution is used, and boundary conditions are directly applied at surface collocation nodes in the least-squares sense

[
29
,
30
,
31
,
32
,
33
,
34
,
35
]
.
Moving the source away from the surface renders the quadratures for both self-interaction and close-evaluation trivial, but brings the disadvantage of a
rectangular exponentially ill-conditioned self-interaction operator.
Yet, this need not pose a problem if each particle needs only a moderate enough number of unknowns to be amenable to dense direct least-squares solution.
The authors recently demonstrated an efficient such solution strategy for the Stokes resistance problem (the velocity BVP that is the
inverse
of the mobility problem) for spheres
[
36
]
. There, following Liu and the 2nd author in the Helmholtz setting
[
37
]
, we proposed a one-particle (i.e., block-diagonal) preconditioner via dense factorization of each one-particle MFS matrix, leaving a well-conditioned global square system (with identity diagonal blocks) involving surface velocity unknowns, which is solved iteratively using a Stokes fast multipole method.
When the geometry is a large collection of simple smooth particles,
this MFS approach appears to be as efficient as BIE methods
[
32
,
38
]

(and, when augmented by image charges, even more so
[
36
]
),
while being much simpler to implement in terms of quadrature and close-evaluation.
Indeed, for Stokes spheres, Appendix
A.1
shows that the MFS needs a very similar number of unknowns as a BIE,
while avoiding all of the algebra of vector spherical harmonics
[
22
]
.
When compared against the rigid multiblob method, the MFS is also much more accurate, as Appendix
A.2
shows.

It is thus appealing to try to apply the MFS to Stokes mobility problems with smooth particle shapes.
However, a direct application fails because imposing the given net forces and torques leads to a constrained least squares problem. This can be written as a
rectangular
saddle point system, where, in contrast to the rigid multiblob method and Stokesian dynamics, efficient preconditioning and fast summation techniques are difficult to apply. The main contributions of the present work are then: 1) to present a different MFS formulation that is free from such additional constraints, and 2) to show how it may be combined with one-particle preconditioning and a fast iterative solution to tackle multi-particle mobility problems efficiently.
This combination allows, we believe for the first time, mobility problems with
10^{4}
non-spherical particles to be solved to several accurate digits on a single shared-memory node.

Our main idea is inspired by “completion” or “compound” flows in the Stokes literature; see, for example, Power & Miranda
[
39
]
and Pozrikidis
[
40
]
, following Mikhlin
[
41
]
.
Recall that a double-layer BIE representation—being incapable of generating non-zero net force or torque—must be augmented by another type of representation
which supplies the given net force and torque.
The latter is usually a single interior Stokeslet and rotlet (e.g.,
[
42
,
24
]
), but may also be a line source
[
43
]

or a single-layer surface potential
[
44
]
.
In contrast, in the present MFS case, the proxy Stokeslets
can
supply net force and torque.
Thus we emulate a zero-net-force-and-torque source by orthogonal projection, then use the projected-out proxy-source subspace itself as the completion source;
one might call this a “recompleted” representation.
It can be viewed as an MFS variant of the recent BIE work of Malhotra and the 2nd author
[
44
, Sec. 4.2]

(which is the adjoint of a popular interior traction mobility formulation
[
45
,
25
,
21
]
),
and it will demand both square and rectangular “ones matrices” (low-rank matrix perturbations that can remove nullspaces
[
46
]
).

The entire formulation is simpler for the (scalar Laplace) elastance problem than for the (vector-valued Stokes) mobility problem; therefore, following Rachh & Greengard
[
25
]
,
we start our exposition and numerical tests with elastance.
This application is of independent interest in electrostatics
with large numbers of smooth conductors of given charges and unknown potentials. Although the MFS is popular in the engineering literature, we have not found prior work applying the MFS to elastance, so believe that this is also a useful contribution.

Remark 1

(Close interactions)
.

Particles moving relative to each other at diminishing separation distances experience increasingly strong lubrication forces. These manifest themselves as peaked force densities near any surface point close-to-touching with another particle
[
42
, Ch. 9]
, resulting in high resolution requirements, both for BIE methods and for methods based on a volumetric grid
[
42
,
47
]
. The same high resolution requirements appear for closely interacting conductors in the Laplace capacitance and elastance problems
[
48
]
. However, recent MFS work by the authors
[
36
]
tackled this in the Stokes resistance problem for spheres, via discretized line-sources that approximate pair-wise infinite reflection image series, enabling controllable accuracy with few unknowns, down to separations of
10^{-3}R
, where
R
is the radius.
For simplicity, we do not attempt to incorporate such images into the presented mobility framework, but expect that it will be straightforward, and leave it for future work.
We thus confine our tests to moderate particle separations (
\geq 0.05R
), as in other work
[
23
]
.

As a motivational example, we solve the mobility problem
for the random cluster of 10000 ellipsoids shown in Fig.
1
,
to 5-digit accuracy in the rigid body velocities.
This needs only a single large-memory workstation.The solution converges in 1.64 hours, in only 7 GMRES iterations, using about
2.6\times 10^{7}
unknowns. For comparison, a system of 131 spheroids was solved in a BIE method using 707,400 unknowns in
[
24
]
, but the particles were stationary, making it an easier problem due to the absence of lubrication singularities. Note also that, in contrast to
[
24
]
, the ellipsoids here need not be spheroids.

1.1
The capacitance and elastance problems

We complete this introduction with a mathematical statement of the problems to be solved, starting with the scalar case.
Let
\Omega^{(k)}
,
k=1,\dots,P
, be a collection of smooth bounded
disjoint objects in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
,
each of which has boundary
\partial\Omega^{(k)}
,
and let
\Omega\coloneqq\bigcup_{k=1}^{P}\Omega^{(k)}
denote the collection,
and
\partial\Omega
denote the union of all boundaries.
Given boundary voltage data
\eta
, the Laplace Dirichlet boundary value problem (BVP) is to find
u

such that

\displaystyle\Delta u

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0\qquad\mbox{ in }\mathbb{R}^{3}\backslash\overline{\Omega},

(1)

\displaystyle u

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\eta\qquad\mbox{ on }\partial\Omega,

(2)

with the decay condition that
u(\bm{x})\to 0
as
\|\bm{x}\|\to\infty
, uniformly in angle.

The special case where the data in (
2
) takes the form

u=\phi^{(k)}\qquad\mbox{ on }\partial\Omega^{(k)},

(3)

where
\phi^{(k)}\in\mathbb{R}
is a given constant voltage on the
k
th boundary,
is called the
capacitance problem
.
One seeks to know the resulting net charges

q^{(k)}\coloneqq-\int_{\partial\Omega^{(k)}}u_{n}\,\mathrm{d}S_{\bm{y}},

(4)

where
u_{n}\coloneqq\partial u/\partial n=\mathbf{n}\cdot\nabla u
,
and
\mathbf{n}
is the outward unit normal on the boundary.
The BVP solution defines a linear map from the input voltage vector

\bm{\phi}\coloneqq\{\phi^{(k)}\}_{k=1}^{P}
to the output charge vector
\mathbf{q}\coloneqq\{q^{(k)}\}_{k=1}^{P}
,
i.e., a positive semidefinite capacitance matrix
\bm{C}\in\mathbb{R}^{P\times P}
acting as

\bm{q}=\bm{C}\bm{\phi}.

(5)

The full matrix
\bm{C}
could in principle be extracted by solving the BVP in (
1
)–(
3
)
P
times, with
u\equiv 1
on
\partial\Omega^{(k)}
and
u\equiv 0
on
\partial\Omega\setminus\partial\Omega^{(k)}
, for each of
k=1,\ldots,P
. However, this is rarely a practical proposition.

The
elastance problem
is the inverse of the capacitance problem.
Namely, given net charges
q^{(k)}
,
k=1,\dots,P
, we seek the solution
u
to the PDE (
1
) that is constant on each boundary, as in (
3
). The voltages
\phi^{(k)}
are, however, unknown and must be determined as part of the problem.
If the capacitance matrix
\bm{C}
were known, from (
5
) we would also have
\bm{\phi}=\bm{C}^{-1}\mathbf{q}
, where
\bm{C}^{-1}
is called the elastance matrix.
The goal is to solve the elastance problem for
\bm{\phi}
given
\bm{q}
, without constructing nor inverting
\bm{C}
, with a computational cost linear in
P
.

1.2
The resistance and mobility problems

The Stokes analog of capacitance is the resistance problem, where the
P
objects represent rigid bodies immersed in a Newtonian fluid of constant viscosity
\mu
; see
[
25
,
21
]
.
The Stokes Dirichlet BVP is

\displaystyle-\mu\Delta\bm{u}+\nabla p

\displaystyle=\bm{0},

\displaystyle\text{ in }\mathbb{R}^{3}\backslash\overline{\Omega},

(6)

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\bm{u}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\text{ in }\mathbb{R}^{3}\backslash\overline{\Omega},

\displaystyle\bm{u}

\displaystyle=\bm{g},

\displaystyle\text{ on }\partial\Omega,

\displaystyle\bm{u}(\bm{x})

\displaystyle\to\bm{0},

\displaystyle\|\bm{x}\|\to\infty,

with
\bm{u}(\bm{x})\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
the flow velocity at the location
\bm{x}
and
p(\bm{x})\in\mathbb{R}
the pressure. With the particles centered at coordinates
\{\bm{c}^{(k)}\}_{k=1}^{P}
and moving with rigid body velocities and angular velocities
\{\bm{v}^{(k)},\bm{\omega}^{(k)}\}_{k=1}^{P}
, where
\bm{v}^{(k)},\bm{\omega}^{(k)}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
, no-slip boundary conditions are applied such that

\bm{g}(\bm{x})=\bm{v}^{(k)}+\bm{\omega}^{(k)}\times(\bm{x}-\bm{c}^{(k)})\quad\text{ on }\partial\Omega^{(k)},\qquad k=1,\ldots,P.

(7)

Once the solution has been determined, the quantities of interest are the total net force and torque on each particle. With the stress tensor
\bm{\sigma}
and surface traction
\bm{T}
defined as usual by

\bm{\sigma}=-p\bm{I}+\mu\left(\nabla\bm{u}+\nabla\bm{u}^{T}\right),\qquad\bm{T}=\bm{\sigma}\cdot\bm{n},

(8)

we can extract from a solution pair
(\bm{u},p)
the net force and torque on the
k
th particle as

\bm{f}^{(k)}=\int_{\partial\Omega^{(k)}}\bm{T}(\bm{y})\,\mathrm{d}S_{\bm{y}},\qquad\bm{t}^{(k)}=\int_{\partial\Omega^{(k)}}(\bm{y}-\bm{c}^{(k)})\times\bm{T}(\bm{y})\,\mathrm{d}S_{\bm{y}},

(9)

analogous to the net charge in (
4
).
The solution to (
6
)–(
7
) then defines a linear map from the input velocities
\{\bm{v}^{(k)}\}_{k=1}^{P}
and
\{\bm{\omega}^{(k)}\}_{k=1}^{P}
, which we stack into a vector
\bm{U}\in\mathbb{R}^{6P}
, to the forces and torques,
\{\bm{f}^{(k)}\}_{k=1}^{P}
and
\{\bm{t}^{(k)}\}_{k=1}^{P}
, which we stack into a vector
\bm{F}\in\mathbb{R}^{6P}
. This linear map is

\bm{F}=\bm{R}\bm{U},

(10)

where the resistance matrix
\bm{R}
could in principle be recovered by solving (
6
)–(
7
)
6P
times with different right-hand sides, corresponding to
\bm{U}
being columns of the identity matrix.

Inverting (
10
) is termed the
mobility problem
:
one seeks a solution to (
6
)–(
7
) that matches given forces and torques (
9
), where the rigid body velocities in (
7
) are now unknown.
This has a unique solution
[
25
]
.
Thus the mobility matrix
\bm{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{6P\times 6P}
exists which maps the stacked vector of forces and torques on all particles to their
velocities, i.e.
\bm{M}=\bm{R}^{-1}
, and
\bm{U}=\bm{M}\bm{F}
.
By energy dissipation considerations
\bm{R}
, and thus
\bm{M}
, is positive definite
[
42
]
.
Our goal is then to solve for
\bm{U}
given
\bm{F}
, in only
{\mathcal{O}}(P)
computational cost (which thus precludes forming
\bm{M}
).

Remark 3

(Mobility generalizations)
.

Two generalizations of the above arise in applications. i) There may be a given
background flow

\bm{u}_{\infty}
that is a Stokes solution in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, such as a shear flow; then one defines
\bm{u}+\bm{u}_{\infty}
as the physical flow and finds that
\bm{u}
solves the above problem, except with an extra term
-\bm{u}_{\infty}|_{\partial\Omega^{(k)}}
on the right-hand side of (
7
).
ii) Active swimmer particles may have a given nonzero slip velocity
[
49
,
19
]
, which again adds a given term to (
7
).
These generalizations may be simply handled by corresponding extra right-hand side terms in the presented method, thus will not be discussed further.

2
The MFS for Dirichlet boundary value problems

Here we summarize the MFS technique for capacitance and resistance, which serves to introduce ideas and notations needed later. (For resistance, this is the basic scheme presented in
[
36
]
.) Consider the
k
th particle, and let
\mathbf{y}^{(k)}_{j}
,
j=1,\dots,N
, be proxy (source) points inside
\Omega^{(k)}
; these will be chosen to lie on a surface a constant separation
\Delta_{\text{sep}}
from the physical surface
\partial\Omega^{(k)}
.
Thus, for a sphere of radius
R
, the proxy-surface has radius
R_{p}=R-\Delta_{\text{sep}}
.
Let
\mathbf{x}^{(k)}_{i}
,
i=1,\dots,M
, be collocation points on
\partial\Omega^{(k)}
; these are chosen as the nodes of a high-order accurate quadrature scheme for the surface. The stacked vectors of source and collocation points on particle
k
are denoted respectively
\bm{Y}^{(k)}
and
\bm{X}^{(k)}
.
For high collocation accuracy with the MFS one usually sets
M
slightly larger than
N

[
32
,
36
]
.
For simplicity of notation we take
N
and
M
independent of
k
.

2.1
The capacitance problem

Recall the Laplace fundamental solution,

G(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=G(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y})=\frac{1}{4\pi\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|},

(11)

where
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
is a target point and
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
a source point.
This obeys
-\Delta G(\cdot,\mathbf{y})=\delta_{\mathbf{y}}
in the distributional
sense. The block of the MFS matrix
\bm{S}^{(kk^{\prime})}

from sources in body
k^{\prime}
to targets on body
k

has entries

\bm{S}^{(kk^{\prime})}_{ij}=G(\mathbf{x}^{(k)}_{i},\mathbf{y}^{(k^{\prime})}_{j}),\quad i=1,\dots,M,\quad j=1,\dots,N.

(12)

In the case of a single particle (
P=1
), the MFS then solves in the least-squares sense the formally overdetermined
M\times N
system

\bm{S}^{(11)}\bm{\alpha}^{(1)}=\bm{\eta}^{(1)},

(13)

where
\bm{\eta}^{(1)}\coloneqq\{\eta(\mathbf{x}^{(1)}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{M}
is the Dirichlet data at the collocation points of particle 1
and
\bm{\alpha}^{(1)}\coloneqq\{\alpha^{(1)}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N}
are the unknown proxy strengths (coefficients).
For a general
P\geq 1
this becomes

\begin{bmatrix}\bm{S}^{(11)}&\bm{S}^{(12)}&\dots&\bm{S}^{(1P)}\\
\bm{S}^{(21)}&\bm{S}^{(22)}&\dots&\dots\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\bm{S}^{(P1)}&\bm{S}^{(P2)}&\dots&\bm{S}^{(PP)}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\alpha}^{(1)}\\
\bm{\alpha}^{(2)}\\
\vdots\\
\bm{\alpha}^{(P)}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\eta}^{(1)}\\
\bm{\eta}^{(2)}\\
\vdots\\
\bm{\eta}^{(P)}\end{bmatrix}.

(14)

In the capacitance problem, here one sets
\bm{\eta}^{(k)}=\phi^{(k)}\bm{1}_{M}
, with
{\mathbf{1}}_{M}
the vector with all
M
entries being
1
.

Having solved (
14
) in the least-squares sense for the stacked solution vector

\bm{\alpha}\coloneqq\{\bm{\alpha}^{(k)}\}_{k=1}^{P}
, the representation of the
solution is

u(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{k=1}^{P}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\alpha^{(k)}_{j}G(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}^{(k)}_{j}),\qquad\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\backslash\overline{\Omega},

(15)

which we abbreviate by the notation

u=\sum_{k=1}^{P}{\cal S}^{(k)}\bm{\alpha}^{(k)},

(16)

by analogy with layer potentials (
{\cal S}^{(k)}
is a single-layer proxy source from body
k
).

The net charges
q^{(k)}
may be extracted either by evaluation of
(
4
) (which requires accurate quadrature weights
for the set of collocation points, and evaluations of
\nabla G
),
or more conveniently via

q^{(k)}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\alpha^{(k)}_{j},\qquad k=1,\dots,P,

(17)

the sum of source strengths in the
k
th body, which follows by Gauss’ law for
G
.

Each matrix block
\bm{S}^{(kk^{\prime})}
is a discretization of the 1st-kind layer operator from the
k^{\prime}
th proxy-surface to the
k
th boundary (no Nyström quadrature weights are needed, since they emerge through the linear solve).
Each block, including the diagonal (self-interaction) blocks
\bm{S}^{(kk)}
,
becomes exponentially ill-conditioned upon convergence (growing
N
), standard behavior for the MFS
[
32
]
.
Such ill-conditioning is of course inherited by the global system
(
14
), but
in Section
2.3
we show how to precondition this system to make it square and amenable to iterative solution.

2.2
The resistance problem

The tensor-valued Stokes fundamental solution, known as the Stokeslet, is

{\@index{\@indexphrase{Stokeslet}}}\bm{G}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\frac{1}{8\pi\mu\|\bm{x}-\bm{y}\|}\left(\bm{I}_{3}+\frac{(\bm{x}-\bm{y})(\bm{x}-\bm{y})^{T}}{\|\bm{x}-\bm{y}\|^{2}}\right),

(18)

with
\bm{I}_{3}\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}
the identity matrix.
A flow field obeying the Stokes equations, with the associated decay condition at infinity, can be expressed as

\bm{u}(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{k=1}^{P}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\bm{G}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}^{(k)}_{j})\bm{\lambda}^{(k)}_{j},\qquad\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\backslash\overline{\Omega},

(19)

where the coefficients
\{\bm{\lambda}^{(k)}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N}
,
k=1,\dots,P
, are to be determined such that the boundary conditions in (
6
) are satisfied. We abbreviate the representation in (
19
) by

\bm{u}=\sum_{k=1}^{P}{\cal S}^{(k)}\bm{\lambda}^{(k)}.

(20)

Letting
\bm{b}
contain the stacked Dirichlet velocity data
\bm{g}
evaluated at all collocation nodes
\{\bm{X}^{(k)}\}_{k=1}^{P}
,
the proxy coefficients
\bm{\lambda}
solve the overdetermined
3MP\times 3NP
system

\bm{S}\bm{\lambda}=\bm{b}.

(21)

We now specialize to rigid body motion data.
Assume first that
P=1
, and let
\bm{U}^{(1)}=\left[{\bm{v}^{(1)}}^{T},{\bm{\omega}^{(1)}}^{T}\right]^{T}
be the vector of rigid body velocities for one particle. Further, let
\bm{K}_{M}^{(1)}\in\mathbb{R}^{3M\times 6}
be the matrix relating these velocities to particle surface velocities at its
M
collocation points. Then, a block row of the matrix
\bm{K}_{M}^{(1)}
determines the velocity at the surface point
\bm{x}_{i}^{(1)}
belonging to the particle, so that

\bm{b}_{i}=(\bm{K}_{M}^{(1)}\bm{U}^{(1)})_{I}=\bm{v}^{(1)}+\bm{\omega}^{(1)}\times(\bm{x}_{i}^{(1)}-\bm{c}^{(1)}),

(22)

with
i
th index set
I=\{3(i-1)+k\}_{k=1}^{3}
.
In matrix form,
\bm{K}_{M}^{(1)}
may be written as

\bm{K}_{M}^{(1)}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{I}_{3}&(\bm{x}_{1}^{(1)}-\bm{c}^{(1)})_{\times}\\
\bm{I}_{3}&(\bm{x}_{2}^{(1)}-\bm{c}^{(1)})_{\times}\\
\vdots&\vdots\\
\bm{I}_{3}&(\bm{x}_{M}^{(1)}-\bm{c}^{(1)})_{\times}\end{bmatrix},\qquad\text{using notation }(\bm{d})_{\times}\coloneqq\begin{bmatrix}0&d_{3}&-d_{2}\\
-d_{3}&0&d_{1}\\
d_{2}&-d_{1}&0\end{bmatrix},

(23)

the skew-symmetric matrix performing a cross-product. The least squares problem in (
21
) for this one particle (alone in the fluid) hence takes the form
\bm{S}^{(11)}\bm{\lambda}^{(1)}=\bm{K}_{M}^{(1)}\bm{U}^{(1)}
.
The net forces and torques on the particle can be determined (analogously to (
17
))
from computed proxy coefficients
\bm{\lambda}^{(1)}
via

\bm{f}^{(1)}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bm{\lambda}_{i}^{(1)},\quad\bm{t}^{(1)}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}(\bm{y}_{i}^{(1)}-\bm{c}^{(1)})\times\bm{\lambda}_{i}^{(1)}.

(24)

With more compact notation, (
24
) can be written as

\begin{bmatrix}\bm{f}^{(1)}\\
\bm{t}^{(1)}\end{bmatrix}={\bm{K}_{N}^{(1)}}^{T}\bm{\lambda}^{(1)},

(25)

with the rigid body proxy matrix
\bm{K}_{N}^{(1)}\in\mathbb{R}^{3N\times 6}
defined as in (
23
)
except that we use the subscript change from
M
to
N
to indicate that source points
\bm{Y}^{(1)}
have replaced collocation nodes
\bm{X}^{(1)}
.

Generalizing this to the full system of
P\geq 1
particles,
the matrices
\bm{K}_{M}
and
\bm{K}_{N}
both have a block diagonal structure, with

\bm{K}_{M}\coloneqq\begin{bmatrix}\bm{K}_{M}^{(1)}&\bm{0}&\dots&\bm{0}\\
\bm{0}&\bm{K}_{M}^{(2)}&\dots&\bm{0}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\bm{0}&\bm{0}&\dots&\bm{K}_{M}^{(P)}\end{bmatrix},\quad\bm{K}_{N}\coloneqq\begin{bmatrix}\bm{K}_{N}^{(1)}&\bm{0}&\dots&\bm{0}\\
\bm{0}&\bm{K}_{N}^{(2)}&\dots&\bm{0}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\bm{0}&\bm{0}&\dots&\bm{K}_{N}^{(P)}\end{bmatrix}.

(26)

We may then solve the global system (
21
) in the
least-squares sense, with the right-hand side given by
\bm{b}=\bm{K}_{M}\bm{U}
, for
\bm{U}\in\mathbb{R}^{6P}
the stacked vector of given translational and angular velocity data. After solution, the desired forces and torques in the stacked vector
\bm{F}
can then extracted directly from
\bm{\lambda}
using

\bm{F}=\bm{K}_{N}^{T}\bm{\lambda},

(27)

analogously to (
17
), which avoids evaluation of any surface traction integrals.
Finally, should the traction function be needed on surfaces, it may be determined from (
8
), via (
19
) and using the pressure solution given by

p(\bm{x})=\frac{1}{8\pi}\sum_{k=1}^{P}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\bm{\Pi}(\bm{x},\bm{y}_{j}^{(k)})\cdot\bm{\lambda}_{j}^{(k)},

(28)

using the standard vector-valued pressure fundamental solution
\bm{\Pi}(\bm{x},\bm{y})=2\dfrac{(\bm{x}-\bm{y})}{\|\bm{x}-\bm{y}\|^{3}}
.

2.3
One-body preconditioning for large-scale Dirichlet problems

The full least-squares
systems (
14
) and (
21
) are large, dense, and ill-conditioned.
Here, we describe how they can be preconditioned to allow for an accelerated iterative
solution. We start with the capacitance problem (
14
).
Let
\bm{S}^{(kk)}=\bm{\mathcal{U}}^{(k)}\bm{\Sigma}^{(k)}{\bm{\mathcal{V}}^{(k)}}^{T}
be the singular value decomposition (SVD) of the self-interaction matrix of particle
k
,
with
\bm{\Sigma}^{(k)}
the diagonal matrix with entries the singular values
\sigma_{1}^{(k)}\geq\sigma_{2}^{(k)}\geq\dots\sigma_{N}^{(k)}
.
Then, the solution operator to its one-body linear system

\bm{S}^{(kk)}\bm{\alpha}^{(k)}=\bm{\eta}^{(k)}

may be approximated using the pseudo-inverse,

\bm{\alpha}^{(k)}\approx{\bm{S}^{(kk)}}^{+}\bm{\eta}^{(k)}\coloneqq\bm{\mathcal{V}}^{(k)}{\bm{\Sigma}^{(k)}}^{+}\bigl({\bm{\mathcal{U}}^{(k)}}^{T}\bm{\eta}^{(k)}\bigr),

(29)

where
\bm{\eta}^{(k)}
is the Dirichlet data vector,
while
{\bm{\Sigma}^{(kk)}}^{+}
is the diagonal matrix with entries
1/\sigma_{j}^{(k)}
when
\sigma_{j}^{(k)}>\sigma_{1}^{(k)}\varepsilon_{\text{trunc}}
, or zero otherwise.
Typically, one sets
\varepsilon_{\text{trunc}}
smaller than the desired error, but, for stability, somewhat larger than machine precision.
Note that, due to catastrophic cancellation,
{\bm{S}^{(kk)}}^{+}
cannot be stably formed then applied as a matrix. Rather, the two-step application in the final expression in (
29
) is needed for numerical stability
[
50
,
51
,
52
,
38
,
53
]
, and is implied whenever we
write
{\bm{S}^{(kk)}}^{+}
in what follows.

We
right-precondition
with
{\bm{S}^{(kk)}}^{+}
in the following way
[
37
,
36
]
:
let the new unknowns (which represent surface potentials)
be given by the vectors
\bm{\gamma}^{(k)}=\bm{S}^{(kk)}\bm{\alpha}^{(k)}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
,
for
k=1,\dots,P
.
To find the system that
\bm{\gamma}^{(k)}
satisfies,
one substitutes

\bm{\alpha}^{(k)}={\bm{S}^{(kk)}}^{+}\bm{\gamma}^{(k)},\quad k=1,\dots,P,

(30)

into the original system (
14
),
which would result in each diagonal block becoming

\bm{S}^{(kk)}{\bm{S}^{(kk)}}^{+}
.
Yet the tall (rectangular) nature of
\bm{S}^{(kk)}
brings about a twist:
\bm{S}^{(kk)}{\bm{S}^{(kk)}}^{+}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times M}
must be strictly rank-deficient, since
N<M
,
which would be very far from creating a well-conditioned system!
Thus, a key step to make the formulation well-conditioned is to replace all diagonal blocks
\bm{S}^{(kk)}{\bm{S}^{(kk)}}^{+}
by
\bm{I}
, the
M\times M
identity matrix.
This is expected to maintain solution accuracy because
{\bm{S}^{(kk)}}^{+}
is presumed to give an accurate set of proxy-strengths for the one-body problem, for all
\bm{\gamma}^{(k)}
in the subspace of smooth surface vectors,
and the use of
\bm{I}
merely enforces good conditioning also in the (irrelevant) complementary subspace. Summarizing the above, the preconditioned version of (
14
) is

\begin{bmatrix}\bm{I}&\bm{S}^{(12)}{\bm{S}^{(22)}}^{+}&\dots&\bm{S}^{(1P)}{\bm{S}^{(PP)}}^{+}\\
\bm{S}^{(21)}{\bm{S}^{(11)}}^{+}&\bm{I}&\ldots&\ldots\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\bm{S}^{(P1)}{\bm{S}^{(11)}}^{+}&\ldots&\ldots&\bm{I}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\gamma}^{(1)}\\
\bm{\gamma}^{(2)}\\
\vdots\\
\bm{\gamma}^{(P)}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\eta}^{(1)}\\
\bm{\eta}^{(2)}\\
\vdots\\
\bm{\eta}^{(P)}\end{bmatrix}.

(31)

We propose to solve (
31
) iteratively (using, for instance, GMRES), to get the stacked surface value vector

\bm{\gamma}\coloneqq\{\bm{\gamma}^{(k)}\}_{k=1}^{P}
. This is outlined in Algorithm
1
.
For large-scale problems (
PM\gg 10^{4}
) the
square matrix-vector multiply needed in each iteration requires three steps, where step 2 uses the FMM, as
explained in the function
MATVEC
in Algorithm
1
. Finally, from the proxy strengths, one can extract the charges via (
17
), or use (
16
) to evaluate the solution anywhere in the domain, including accurately on boundaries.

Remark 4

(Exploiting congruent or resized particles.)
.

In the case where all proxy and collocation point sets are translates of those for a single body, the self-interaction matrices
{\bm{S}^{(kk)}}
are identical for all
k
(see
[
36
]
for the sphere case). More generally, for congruent non-spherical particles with different orientations, or for resized copies of a given shape, a single SVD per unique shape suffices to determine the action of the self-interaction pseudo-inverses
{\bm{S}^{(kk)}}^{+}
required for preconditioning.
In the case of spatial rescaling of a particle (and its discretization) by a factor, then
{\bm{S}^{(kk)}}^{+}
is simply multiplied by this factor.
Rotations need a little more bookkeeping:
let a base particle at the origin with a given reference orientation be discretized by source points stacked in
\bm{Y}^{(0)}\in\mathbb{R}^{3N}
and collocation points stacked in
\bm{X}^{(0)}\in\mathbb{R}^{3M}
. Then, a particle with a general orientation described by the rotation matrix
\bm{\mathcal{R}}^{(k)}\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}
is discretized by
\bm{X}^{(k)}=\bm{\mathcal{R}}^{(k)}_{N}\bm{X}^{(0)}
and
\bm{Y}^{(k)}=\bm{\mathcal{R}}^{(k)}_{M}\bm{Y}^{(0)}
. Hence,

\bm{K}_{N}^{(k)}=\bm{R}_{N}^{(k)}\bm{K}_{N}^{(0)}\bm{R}_{2}^{(k)}\text{ and }\bm{K}_{M}^{(k)}=\bm{R}_{N}^{(k)}\bm{K}_{M}^{(0)}\bm{R}_{2}^{(k)}.

(32)

Here,
\bm{\mathcal{R}}_{N}^{(k)}\in\mathbb{R}^{3N\times 3N}
,
\bm{\mathcal{R}}_{M}^{(k)}\in\mathbb{R}^{3M\times 3M}
and
\bm{\mathcal{R}}_{2}^{(k)}\in\mathbb{R}^{6\times 6}
are matrices with the small matrices
\bm{\mathcal{R}}^{(k)}
in their diagonal blocks. Given
\bm{B}=\bm{\mathcal{U}}^{(0)}\bm{\Sigma}^{(0)}{\bm{\mathcal{V}}^{(0)}}^{T}
, a factorization of the system matrix for the base particle,
\bm{\lambda}^{(k)}={\bm{S}^{(kk)}}^{+}\bm{\gamma}^{(k)}
is then determined as

\bm{\lambda}^{(k)}=\bm{\mathcal{R}}_{N}^{(k)}\bm{\mathcal{V}}^{(0)}{\bm{\Sigma}^{(0)}}^{+}\biggl({\bm{\mathcal{U}}^{(0)}}^{T}\bigl({\bm{\mathcal{R}}_{M}^{(k)}}^{T}\bm{\gamma}^{(k)}\bigr)\biggr).

(33)

3
Formulations for elastance and mobility

We now turn to the main contribution:
well-conditioned MFS elastance and mobility formulations free from constraints associated with the given net particle quantities.
We start with the simpler elastance case in Section
3.1
, proceed to mobility
in Section
3.2
,
and then explain how
the resulting systems are effectively solved with one-body preconditioning in Section
3.3
.

3.1
Elastance formulation

We explain first the case
P=1
for simplicity.
Let
\bm{L}=\frac{1}{N}{\mathbf{1}}{\mathbf{1}}^{T}
be the
N\times N
matrix
with all entries
1/N
. It is the orthogonal projector
onto the constant vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
,
while
\bm{I}-\bm{L}
is the orthogonal projector onto the complement space.
Recalling the representation notation (
15
)-(
16
),
we set up a “completion potential”
{\cal S}^{(1)}\bm{\alpha}_{0}^{(1)}

where
\bm{\alpha}_{0}^{(1)}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
is a known strength vector
designed to impart the desired net charge
q^{(1)}
to the body.
The simplest such choice
is the constant vector
\bm{\alpha}_{0}^{(1)}=\frac{1}{N}q^{(1)}\bm{1}_{N}
.
In terms of an unknown source vector
\bm{\alpha}^{(1)}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
,
our proposed representation is

u={\cal S}^{(1)}(\bm{I}-\bm{L})\bm{\alpha}^{(1)}+{\cal S}^{(1)}\bm{\alpha}_{0}^{(1)}\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\mbox{($P=1$ case)}.

(34)

We have here projected out the non-zero mean part of
\bm{\alpha}^{(1)}
so that the
first term cannot change the net body charge (a property reminiscent of a double-layer potential), so that the net charge is
q^{(1)}
by construction.
Inserting (
34
) into the boundary condition (
3
)
and enforcing this at all collocation nodes gives

\bm{S}^{(11)}(\bm{I}-\bm{L})\bm{\alpha}^{(1)}+\bm{S}^{(11)}\bm{\alpha}_{0}^{(1)}=\bm{\eta}^{(1)},

(35)

where
\bm{S}^{(11)}
is the MFS matrix, and again
\bm{\eta}^{(1)}=\phi^{(1)}{\mathbf{1}}_{M}
some constant
vector, as in (
13
), but now with unknown constant.
The system is closed by choosing a representation for
\phi^{(1)}
in terms of

\bm{\alpha}^{(1)}
. The subspace Span
\{{\mathbf{1}}_{N}\}
is available for this, it
having no effect on the first term
\bm{S}^{(11)}(\bm{I}-\bm{L})\bm{\alpha}^{(1)}
. Hence, we let
\bm{L}_{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times N}
be the rectangular matrix with all entries
1/N
, and make the ansatz

\bm{\eta}^{(1)}=-\bm{L}_{r}\bm{\alpha}^{(1)}.

(36)

This is inspired by ideas in Stokes mobility (see, e.g.,
[
44
, Sec. 4.2]
).
Substituting this into (
35
) gives the linear system

\bigl[\bm{S}^{(11)}(\bm{I}-\bm{L})+\bm{L}_{r}\bigr]\bm{\alpha}^{(1)}=-\bm{S}^{(11)}\bm{\alpha}_{0}^{(1)}.

Note that we do not solve for the constant voltage
\phi^{(1)}
directly; however, it can easily be extracted from any row of (
36
), to give
\phi^{(1)}=-\frac{1}{N}{\mathbf{1}}^{T}\bm{\alpha}^{(1)}
.

The case for a general number of bodies
P\geq 1
is now mostly a matter of notation.
The representation is

u=\sum_{k=1}^{P}{\cal S}^{(k)}\bigl[(\bm{I}-\bm{L})\bm{\alpha}^{(k)}+\bm{\alpha}_{0}^{(k)}\bigr],

(37)

recalling that
{\cal S}^{(k)}
is the MFS charge representation from body
k
.
Here the completion flow is constructed with each block of the vector
\bm{\alpha}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{NP}
constant,

\bm{\alpha}_{0}^{(k)}=\frac{q^{(k)}}{N}{\mathbf{1}}_{N},\qquad k=1,\dots,P.

(38)

The following summarizes the formulation,
and verifies that it solves the elastance problem in the case of exact solution of the linear system.

Proposition 1

(Elastance formulation.)
.

Let
\bm{\alpha}\in\mathbb{R}^{PN}
solve the formally overdetermined linear system with block rows

[\bm{S}^{(kk)}(\bm{I}-\bm{L})+\bm{L}_{r}]\bm{\alpha}^{(k)}+\sum_{k^{\prime}\neq k}\bm{S}^{(kk^{\prime})}(\bm{I}-\bm{L})\bm{\alpha}^{(k^{\prime})}=-{\mathbf{u}}^{(k)}_{0},\quad k=1,\dots,P,

(39)

where, recalling
\bm{\alpha}_{0}
defined by (
38
), the right-hand side vector has block entries

{\mathbf{u}}^{(k)}_{0}=\sum_{k^{\prime}=1}^{P}\bm{S}^{(kk^{\prime})}\bm{\alpha}^{(k^{\prime})}_{0},\qquad k=1,\dots,P.

(40)

Then the potential
u
given by the representation (
37
)
is harmonic in
\mathbb{R}^{3}\backslash\overline{\Omega}
, constant
on the collocation nodes for each body, and has the
desired net charges (
17
).
The constant voltages may be read off as the negative mean strengths

\phi^{(k)}=-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\alpha^{(k)}_{j},\qquad k=1,\dots,P.

(41)

Proof.

Replacing
k
by
k^{\prime}
in the representation (
37
),
then evaluating it on the collocation nodes of the
k
th body,
gives the vector of potentials

\{u(\mathbf{x}^{(k)}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{M}=\sum_{k^{\prime}=1}^{P}\bm{S}^{(kk^{\prime})}\bigl[(\bm{I}-\bm{L})\bm{\alpha}^{(k^{\prime})}+\bm{\alpha}^{(k^{\prime})}_{0}\bigr],\qquad k=1,\dots,P.

Subtracting the equation (
39
) leaves
\{u(\mathbf{x}^{(k)}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{M}=-\bm{L}_{r}^{(k)}\bm{\alpha}^{(k)}
,
which shows that the potential has the same value in all collocation points on the
k
th body,
and that this constant value is given by (
41
). Since it is a sum of fundamental solutions,

u
is harmonic,
and has the correct charges by construction from (
38
).
∎

Note that precomputing the right hand side
\bm{u}_{0}:=\{\bm{u}_{0}^{(k)}\}_{k=1}^{P}

via (
40
) can be performed with a single FMM call.
The global system (
39
) is no less ill-conditioned than (
14
) for the
Dirichlet problem.

Remark 5

(Other types of MFS sources.)
.

The inquisitive reader may wonder: why not instead use double-layer sources for the MFS, obviating the projector
\bm{I}-\bm{L}
and allowing point-like completion sources
as in
[
39
,
40
]
?
While this would work, it has two disadvantages over what we propose:
1) it is more cumbersome, requiring two source types, including double-layer sources which are more singular and more expensive to evaluate; and
2) the MFS may also accurately handle close-to-touching lubrication interactions
through admixtures of Stokeslets, rotlets, and doublets
[
36
]
, which generate nonzero force and torque, thus would require the
re-insertion of a projector in any case.
We plan to pursue point 2) for the mobility problem in future work.

Remark 6

(Elastance in 2D.)
.

The 2D case for elastance is more complicated, due to additional constraints
on the total charge, and a more subtle asymptotic form as
|\mathbf{x}|\to\infty
; see
[
25
]
. We stick to 3D in this work.

3.2
Mobility formulation

Our proposal is the Stokes generalization of the previous subsection.
The rank-1 single-body projector
\bm{L}^{(k)}
now becomes rank-6, projects onto the space of rigid body motions, and is obtained by

\bm{L}^{(k)}=\bm{K}_{N}^{(k)}\left({\bm{K}_{N}^{(k)}}^{T}\bm{K}_{N}^{(k)}\right)^{-1}{\bm{K}_{N}^{(k)}}^{T}.

(42)

We also need a recipe (following
[
44
]
) for a proxy strength vector
\bm{\lambda}_{0}^{(k)}
that produces the given net forces
\bm{f}^{(k)}
and torques
\bm{t}^{(k)}
for the
k
th particle.
Analogously to the constant
\bm{\alpha}_{0}^{(k)}
given by (
38
) in the elastance case, we restrict

\bm{\lambda}_{0}^{(k)}
to be in the rigid body space on the proxy points, meaning

\bm{\lambda}_{0}^{(k)}=\bm{K}_{N}^{(k)}\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\nu}^{(k)}\\
\bm{\xi}^{(k)}\end{bmatrix}

(43)

for some rigid body velocity
\bm{\nu}^{(k)}
and angular velocity
\bm{\xi}^{(k)}
.
Inserting this into (
25
) gives a
6\times 6
linear system to solve
for each particle,

{\bm{K}_{N}^{(k)}}^{T}\bm{K}_{N}^{(k)}\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\nu}^{(k)}\\
\bm{\xi}^{(k)}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{f}^{(k)}\\
\bm{t}^{(k)}\end{bmatrix}.

(44)

The resulting
\bm{\lambda}_{0}^{(k)}
in fact solves the minimization problem

\min\limits_{\bm{\mu}}\,\|\bm{\mu}\|^{2}_{2},\text{ s.t.}\medspace{\bm{K}_{N}^{(k)}}^{T}\bm{\mu}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{f}^{(k)}\\
\bm{t}^{(k)}\end{bmatrix}.

(45)

With
\bm{\lambda}_{0}^{(k)}
determined for each particle
k
,
the flow representation is

\bm{u}=\sum_{k=1}^{P}{\cal S}^{(k)}\left[\left(\bm{I}-\bm{L}^{(k)}\right)\bm{\lambda}^{(k)}+\bm{\lambda}_{0}^{(k)}\right],

(46)

recalling that
{\cal S}^{(k)}
denotes the
k
th particle MFS Stokeslet representation

\bigl({\cal S}^{(k)}\bm{\beta}\bigr)(\bm{x})\;\coloneqq\;\sum_{j=1}^{N}\bm{G}(\bm{x},\bm{y}_{j}^{(k)})\bm{\beta}_{j},

(47)

for any MFS strength vector
\bm{\beta}
formed by stacking
\bm{\beta}_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}

for
j=1,\dots,N
.
Because the projectors
(\bm{I}-\bm{L}^{(k)})
remove net force and torque from each body,
(
46
) generates a flow with the desired net forces and torques.
Enforcing that this flow
\bm{u}
is an unknown
rigid body motion on the
k
th particle surface
collocation nodes gives

\sum_{k^{\prime}=1}^{P}\bm{S}^{(kk^{\prime})}\left[\left(\bm{I}-\bm{L}^{(k)}\right)\bm{\lambda}^{(k^{\prime})}+\bm{\lambda}_{0}^{(k^{\prime})}\right]\;=\;\bm{K}_{M}^{(k)}\bm{U}^{(k)},\qquad k=1,\dots,P.

(48)

By analogy with the elastance case (
36
), we close the linear system via an ansatz that
the
k
th body unknown rigid body motion is controlled by the (thus far unused) rigid body subspace of its Stokeslet coefficients:
\bm{U}^{(k)}=-{\bm{K}_{N}^{(k)}}^{T}\bm{\lambda}^{(k)}
.
The right hand side of (
48
) then becomes
-\bm{L}_{r}^{(k)}\bm{\lambda}^{(k)}
, where

\bm{L}_{r}^{(k)}\;:=\;\bm{K}_{M}^{(k)}{\bm{K}_{N}^{(k)}}^{T}\;\in\mathbb{R}^{3M\times 3N},\qquad k=1,\dots,P

(49)

is a rank-6 rectangular rigid body coupling matrix for each particle.

Rearranging (
48
), the formulation and its solution of the mobility problem is summarized in the following.

Proposition 2

(Mobility formulation.)
.

Let
\bm{\lambda}:=\{\bm{\lambda}^{(k)}\}_{k=1}^{P}
solve the tall linear system with block rows

\left[\bm{S}^{(kk)}(\bm{I}-\bm{L}^{(k)})+\bm{L}_{r}^{(k)}\right]\bm{\lambda}^{(k)}+\sum\limits_{k\neq k^{\prime}}\bm{S}^{(kk^{\prime})}\left(\bm{I}-\bm{L}^{(k)}\right)\bm{\lambda}^{(k^{\prime})}=-\bm{u}_{0}^{(k)},\quad k=1,\dots,P,

(50)

where, in terms of (
43
)–(
44
), the right hand side has block components

\bm{u}_{0}^{(k)}=\sum\limits_{k^{\prime}=1}^{P}\bm{S}^{(kk^{\prime})}\bm{\lambda}_{0}^{(k^{\prime})},\qquad k=1,\dots,P.

(51)

Then, the flow field
\bm{u}
given by (
46
) satisfies the PDE (
6
) in
\mathbb{R}^{3}\backslash\overline{\Omega}
, obeys rigid body motion on each particle surface, and corresponds to the desired net forces and torques in (
24
) on the particles. The stack of all rigid body motions may be extracted via

\bm{U}=-\bm{K}_{N}^{T}\bm{\lambda},

(52)

with
\bm{K}_{N}
defined in (
26
).

Proof.

The proof is exactly analogous to that of Proposition
1
.
∎

The above global rectangular linear system is ill-conditioned, as with the resistance MFS formulation, but is amenable to one-body preconditioning, as explained in the next subsection.

Remark 7

(Relation to saddle-point systems for mobility.)
.

A straightforward approach to the mobility problem is to solve
\bm{S}\bm{\lambda}=\bm{K}_{M}\bm{U}
in the least-squares sense, subject to the constraints
\bm{K}_{N}\bm{\lambda}=\bm{F}
, with unknown rigid body velocities
\bm{U}
, but known forces and torques
\bm{F}
. In saddle point form, this can
for the case
P=1
be written as

\begin{bmatrix}\bm{S}^{(11)}&-\bm{K}_{M}^{(1)}\\
-{\bm{K}_{N}^{(1)}}^{T}&\bm{0}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\lambda}^{(1)}\\
\bm{U}^{(1)}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{0}\\
-\bm{F}^{(1)}\end{bmatrix},

(53)

and the mobility matrix
\bm{M}
giving
\bm{M}\bm{F}^{(1)}=\bm{U}^{(1)}
can be solved via the Schur complement

\bm{M}=\left({\bm{K}^{(1)}}^{T}_{N}{\bm{S}^{(11)}}^{+}\bm{K}^{(1)}_{M}\right)^{-1}.

However, when it comes to the multi-particle
P\gg 1
case, this does not scale, and we are unaware of a fast algorithm. Yet, the above recompleted formulation has similarities to the null space method in the literature on least squares problems with inequality constraints
[
54
,
55
]
and saddle point problems
[
56
]
. In a saddle point problem of the form (
53
),
\bm{\lambda}^{(1)}
is then split into two components, one in the null space of
{\bm{K}_{N}^{(1)}}^{T}
and one in the orthogonal complement, with the goal of solving a system of reduced size. However, that technique, in contrast to ours, is typically beneficial only when the number of constraints is large, and it exploits neither the projectors
\bm{L}^{(k)}
nor the relation
(
52
) that we use.

3.3
One-body preconditioning for elastance and mobility

A dense direct least squares solution of the discrete systems introduced in Sections
3.1
and
3.2
would require
{\mathcal{O}}(P^{3}N^{3})
effort, which quickly becomes prohibitive as
N
or
P
is increased.
The elastance and mobility MFS linear systems may however be right-preconditioned by factorizing
the diagonal blocks, in a similar style to the Dirichlet BVP case from Section
2.3
, resulting in well-conditioned square linear systems amenable to iterative solution.
We now present the formulae, unifying the elastance and mobility cases.
Let
\bm{S}_{L}^{(k)}
denote the diagonal matrix block corresponding to particle
k
, that is,

\bm{S}_{L}^{(k)}\coloneqq\bm{S}^{(kk)}(\bm{I}-\bm{L}^{(k)})+\bm{L}_{r}^{(k)},\qquad k=1,\dots,P.

(54)

For elastance,
\bm{L}_{r}^{(k)}=\bm{L}_{r}
and
\bm{L}^{(k)}=\bm{L}
for
k=1,\dots,P
. The same is true if all particles in a mobility problem have the same shape and orientation; see (
32
) in Remark
4
.
Furthermore, for the elastance problem,
\bm{S}_{L}^{(k)}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times N}
, and for
k=1,\dots,P
,
\bm{\gamma}^{(k)}=\bm{S}^{(k)}_{L}\bm{\alpha}^{(k)}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
. For the mobility problem,
\bm{S}^{(k)}_{L}\in\mathbb{R}^{3M\times 3N}
, and
\bm{\gamma}^{(k)}=\bm{S}_{L}^{(k)}\bm{\lambda}^{(k)}\in\mathbb{R}^{3M}
, for
k=1,\dots,P
.
For a general
P
, the resulting square linear system in both cases takes the form

\small\begin{bmatrix}\bm{I}&\bm{S}^{(12)}\left(\bm{I}{-}\bm{L}^{(2)}\right){\bm{S}_{L}^{(2)}}^{+}&\dots&\bm{S}^{(1P)}\left(\bm{I}{-}\bm{L}^{(P)}\right){\bm{S}_{L}^{(P)}}^{+}\\
\bm{S}^{(21)}\left(\bm{I}{-}\bm{L}^{(1)}\right){\bm{S}_{L}^{(1)}}^{+}&\bm{I}&\dots&\dots\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\bm{S}^{(P1)}\left(\bm{I}{-}\bm{L}^{(1)}\right){\bm{S}_{L}^{(1)}}^{+}&\dots&\dots&\bm{I}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\gamma}^{(1)}\\
\bm{\gamma}^{(2)}\\
\vdots\\
\bm{\gamma}^{(P)}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}-\bm{u}_{0}^{(1)}\\
-\bm{u}_{0}^{(2)}\\
\vdots\\
-\bm{u}_{0}^{(P)}\end{bmatrix},

(55)

recalling the right-hand side definition (
40
) for elastance,
or (
51
) with
\bm{\lambda}_{0}^{(k)}
given by (
43
)–(
44
) for mobility.
In either case the right-hand side may be computed by a single FMM.
For each body, one first precomputes the two matrices that apply the pseudoinverse

{\bm{S}_{L}^{(k)}}^{+}
backward-stably, via the SVD of
\bm{S}_{L}^{(k)}
, as in (
29
). For congruent particles, a single SVD is required; rotations determine
{\bm{S}_{L}^{(k)}}^{+}
from a base discretization as in Remark
4
.
Then, one solves (
55
) iteratively for the stacked surface value vector

\bm{\gamma}\coloneqq\{\bm{\gamma}^{(k)}\}_{k=1}^{P}
, in analogy with Algorithm
1
used for the Dirichlet BVPs. There are two sole differences here:

1.

the form of the one-body blocks on the diagonal of the system to be preconditioned (
\bm{S}^{(kk)}
versus
\bm{S}^{(k)}_{L}
), and,

2.

the application of
\bm{I}-\bm{L}^{(k)}
to each particle’s block vector after Step 1 of the
matvec
function in Algorithm
1
.

Upon convergence for elastance, one recovers
\bm{\alpha}^{(k)}={\bm{S}_{L}^{(k)}}^{+}\bm{\gamma}^{(k)}
for each
k
,
finally reporting their averages
\phi^{(k)}
via (
41
). For mobility,
\bm{\lambda}^{(k)}={\bm{S}_{L}^{(k)}}^{+}\bm{\bm{\gamma}}^{(k)}
,
k=1,\dots,P
, and velocities are finally computed via (
52
). The solution fields in
\mathbb{R}^{3}\backslash\overline{\Omega}
can be recovered via the representations in (
37
) (for elastance) or (
46
) (for mobility).

This completes the description of the preconditioned algorithms, including the option for FMM accelerated iterative solutions.

Remark 8

(MFS convergence rate regimes.)
.

By analogy with rigorous MFS theory in 2D

4.1.2
Stokes: comparison to a boundary integral reference

A useful numerical test is to solve the resistance and mobility problems in succession,
since they are supposed to be each other’s inverse.
This starts with a known vector
\bm{U}_{\text{ref}}\in\mathbb{R}^{6P}
of stacked translational and angular velocities, and
computes
\bm{F}\in\mathbb{R}^{6P}
, a stacked vector of the net forces and torques on all particles, by solving the resistance problem. We then insert
\bm{F}
as the input to the mobility solver to get an output vector
\bm{U}
, and
finally report

\epsilon_{\text{2-way}}\coloneqq\|\bm{U}-\bm{U}_{\text{ref}}\|_{\infty}/\|\bm{U}_{\text{ref}}\|_{\infty}

(56)

as an error metric. We call this the
2-way error
. When the 2-way error is quantified here and later, distinct proxy radii are used for resistance and mobility to avoid misleading cancellations (“inverse crimes”): specifically, the offsets for the proxy-surface in the normal direction from the particle surface are set such that
\Delta_{\text{sep}}^{\text{mob}}=1.05\Delta_{\text{sep}}^{\text{res}}
. In the sphere examples, we will report on the radius of the resistance problem,
R_{p}^{\text{res}}\coloneqq 1-\Delta_{\text{sep}}^{\text{res}}
.

One first should validate the 2-way error against an independent error metric. For the latter we compare our mobility solve particle velocity outputs to those from an established BIE reference method using QBX quadrature
[
24
]
with 900 unknowns per body and a quadrature error tolerance of
10^{-6}
, and report

\epsilon_{\text{BIE}}\coloneqq\|\bm{U}-\bm{U}_{\text{BIE}}\|_{\infty}/\|\bm{U}_{\text{BIE}}\|_{\infty}.

(57)

The validation results are in Fig.
4
.
We chose 56 test geometries consisting of five randomly positioned unit spheres driven by randomly sampled forces and torques. The BIE solution is much more costly to compute than the MFS solution, due to the quadrature setup.
The radius of the proxy-surface is set to
R_{p}=0.59
and
N=686
.
We report
\epsilon_{\text{BIE}}
in Fig.
4(a)
versus the minimum particle-particle distance
\delta
in each geometry. For small
\delta
, the interaction becomes increasingly difficult to resolve, both with MFS and the BIE reference, due to
lubrication effects between particles under relative motion.
However, Fig.
4(b)
demonstrates that
\epsilon_{\text{2-way}}
is a faithful surrogate for
\epsilon_{\text{BIE}}
, at least within
\pm 1
digit of accuracy,
in the range above the MFS GMRES tolerance of
10^{-7}
.
Thus,
\epsilon_{\text{2-way}}
alone will be used as the error metric from now on.

defined pointwise by

\epsilon_{\text{res}}(\bm{x})\coloneqq\|\bm{u}(\bm{x})-\bm{g}(\bm{x})\|_{2}/\|\bm{g}(\bm{x})\|_{2},\qquad\bm{x}\in\partial\Omega,

(58)

with
\|\cdot\|_{2}
the Euclidean norm in
\mathbb{R}^{3}

\bm{u}
the solution flow field given by the MFS representation, and
\bm{g}
the rigid-body surface velocity (
7
) using the computed particle velocities.
This is simply the Stokes analog of the residual shown in Figure

Its maximum is estimated using a large set of points on all particle surfaces.

Both the proxy and collocation surfaces are discretized with the quasi-uniform ellipsoid grid described by Stein and the 2nd author in
[
38
]
. In brief, an ellipsoid is parameterized
as
(a\sqrt{1-t^{2}}\cos{s},b\sqrt{1-t^{2}}\sin{s},ct)

in Cartesians relative to its center, where

.
These parameters are discretized with

Gauss–Legendre nodes in the
t
-direction, then periodic trapezoidal nodes in the
s
-direction. The number of the latter are roughly bounded by
0.75N_{v}
, and varied with

to give an approximately uniform surface density.
The number of nodes on the proxy-surface, normal-shifted from the true surface, is then
N\approx 17+3.9N_{v}+0.44N_{v}^{2}
. For the collocation surface, we increase the number of Gauss-Legendre nodes such that
M\approx 17+3.9(1.15N_{v})+0.44(1.15N_{v})^{2}
, resulting in
M\approx 1.3N
. The rectangularity
M/N
must be larger than for spheres, we believe due to the greater variation in node uniformity and curvature. However, this does not affect the dominant cost, which is the FMM scaling only with
N
.

5
Conclusions

We present spectrally accurate well-conditioned solvers for the Laplace elastance and Stokes mobility problems involving a large number of smooth bodies, based on the method of fundamental solutions.
The formulations are free from additional constraints, achieved by projecting the linear space of source strengths into a) the subspace of constant vectors (for elastance) or the subspace of rigid body motions (for mobility), plus b) its orthogonal complement.
The former subspace directly controls the unknown constant potentials or rigid-body motions, while the latter generates a zero-net-charge (or zero-net-force-and-torque) solution potential to which a completion flow is added to account for the
known net charges (or forces and torques).
We call this a “recompleted” formulation.
With one-body preconditioning and FMM-acceleration, the scaling is linear in the number of particles, assuming identical particles, and problems with of order
10^{4}
particles can be solved with of order one workstation-hour of computation.
For spheres, the number of MFS unknowns needed for a given accuracy is similar to that of the sphere-specialized boundary integral method of
[
22
,
23
]
, while avoiding its elaborate analytic formulae.
Yet our method is more general, applying to any particle shape amenable to an MFS solution; for instance, many of our tests are for triaxial ellipsoids. Although not explicitly demonstrated through simulations in this paper, the method efficiently handles polydisperse systems as well.

Our findings suggest several future directions. For random particle clusters of approximately constant density the GMRES iteration count stays constant with the number of particles for the mobility problem, rather than growing weakly as for the resistance problem
[
36
]
, and we would like to understand this difference (see
[
19
]
).
For ellipsoids, we have only tested particles with moderate aspect ratios, and it would be interesting to push this limit further. At high aspect ratio, a proportionally larger
N
per body is expected, at which point it could be valuable to exploit azimuthal symmetry for axisymmetric particles in the one-body dense direct preconditioning method
[
37
]
. Further, the present method could naturally be combined with the lubrication-adapted image systems of
[
36
]
, enabling accurate mobility solutions at much closer separations
\delta
. The present formulation is also expected to aid in dynamic simulations of complex fluids and rheology. Efficient time-stepping will then need a contact-avoiding strategy
[
63
,
23
,
64
]
. It is also of great interest to investigate how to utilize the MFS to sample Brownian (thermal) hydrodynamic fluctuations.

A.2
Comparison against the rigid multiblob method

We repeat the experiment with five randomly positioned spheres presented in Fig.
4(a)
, employing the rigid multiblob method to compare its accuracy against that of our MFS scheme. The rigid multiblob method is an approximate solution technique widely used in the engineering community, thouroughly described in e.g.
[
19
]
, where a collection of “blobs” approximates the particle surface.
Mathematically, it leads to a saddle point system similar to that of the MFS, but with a regularized kernel—the Rotne–Prager–Yamakawa (RPY) tensor—replacing the Stokeslet. This similarity allows for the same solution strategy as with the MFS, where a recompleted formulation can be solved with one-body preconditioning (non-standard in the rigid multiblob literature). Unlike in MFS, the source and collocation points coincide, and must be placed slightly offset from the particle boundary to reproduce the correct far-field behavior; this also improves near-field accuracy
[
65
]
. Following
[
65
]
, we therefore optimize the discretization of the multiblob particles with respect to the offset and the regularization parameter. We then solve the mobility problem for the same 56 test geometries used in Section
4.1.2
, which consist of five randomly positioned unit spheres driven by randomly sampled forces and torques. Accuracy is reported as a function of the minimum particle-particle distance
\delta
in Figure
11
.
Despite optimization, and the inclusion of spherical design grids with the same number of degrees of freedom as the MFS, the multiblob method only achieved 1–4 digit accuracy, in contrast with the 4–8 digits using MFS.
