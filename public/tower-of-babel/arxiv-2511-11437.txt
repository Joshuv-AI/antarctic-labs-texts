Guowei Zhang, Yun Zhao, Moein Khajehnejad, Adeel Razi, Levin Kuhlmann

Abstract
Mapping human brain activity to natural images offers a new window into vision and cognition, yet current diffusion-based decoders face a core difficulty: most condition directly on fMRI features without analyzing how visual information is organized across the cortex. This overlooks the brain’s hierarchical processing and blurs the roles of early, middle, and late visual areas. We propose Hi-DREAM , a brain-inspired conditional diffusion framework that makes the cortical organization explicit. An Region of Interest (ROI) adapter groups fMRI into early/mid/late streams and converts them into a multi-scale cortical pyramid aligned with the U-Net depth (shallow scales preserve layout and edges; deeper scales emphasize objects and semantics). A lightweight, depth-matched ControlNet injects these scale-specific hints during denoising. The result is an efficient and interpretable decoder in which each signal plays a brain-like role, allowing the model not only to reconstruct images but also to illuminate functional contributions of different visual areas. Experiments on the Natural Scenes Dataset (NSD) show that Hi-DREAM attains state-of-the-art performance on high-level semantic metrics while maintaining competitive low-level fidelity. These findings suggest that structuring conditioning by cortical hierarchy is a powerful alternative to purely data-driven embeddings and provides a useful lens for studying the visual cortex.

Related Work

2.1
fMRI-based image reconstruction.

A growing line of work decodes natural images from human fMRI by coupling a brain encoder with a powerful image generator. Early diffusion-era approaches such as Mind-Vis
[

[

[

[
59

[
1
,
36
]
; performance then depends on how well the neural code is aligned with the generator’s internal representations and at which layers the conditioning is injected.

3
Method

3.1
Motivation and Overview

Motivation.
Neuroscience characterizes human visual cortex as a hierarchical and cooperative system: early areas encode local/retinotopic structure, mid-level areas integrate contours and parts, and late ventral regions represent category-level semantics. Our goal is to
leverage this organization to guide generation
, not merely to fit a data-driven mapping. By structuring the conditioning signal according to cortical hierarchy and injecting it at matching depths of a diffusion backbone, we aim to (i) improve reconstruction fidelity where each type of information is most useful and (ii) use the trained model as an
operational hypothesis
to probe the roles of different visual areas—i.e., once
Hi-DREAM
reliably decodes vision, we can analyze its components to generate and test hypotheses about cortical function in human brain.

Overview.
We mirror the cortical organization by transforming fMRI into
hierarchical, group-specific, multi-scale
cortical priors and injecting them at matched U-Net depths. Concretely, our decoder comprises two lightweight modules: (1) a
ROI adapter
that converts fMRI into multi-scale cortical maps aligned with early/mid/late ROI groups; and (2) a
ROI-conditioned ControlNet
that injects those maps into the U-Net at the corresponding resolutions to guide the denoising trajectory with anatomy-aware structure (Fig.
2
). The design is brain-inspired and interpretable, rather than purely data-driven.

3.2
ROI Adapter: Hierarchical Multi-Scale Pyramid

Scale-space construction.
We build a spatial pyramid at resolutions
\mathcal{S}=\{64,128,256,512\}
to match U-Net blocks. For each scale
s\in\mathcal{S}
, ROI masks are blurred and downsampled to produce
\tilde{m}_{r,s}
; optionally a band-pass (Laplacian) version emphasizes structures specific to that scale. Intuitively, shallow scales preserve coarse retinotopy while deeper scales retain finer parts and categories.

Per-scale weighting and grouping.
Each ROI contributes a scaled map
c_{r,s}=w_{r,s}\,a_{r}\,\tilde{m}_{r,s}
, where
w_{r,s}\in[0,1]
is a learnable gate encoding our depth prior (larger for early ROIs at shallow scales; larger for late ROIs at deep scales). Stacking maps within a ROI group
g\in\{e,m,l\}
yields a tensor
\mathbf{C}_{g,s}\in\mathbb{R}^{R_{g}\times h_{s}\times w_{s}}
.

Lightweight channelization.
A
1\!\times\!1
Conv/MLP mixer
M_{g,s}
compresses
\mathbf{C}_{g,s}
into guidance channels
\Xi_{g,s}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{s}\times h_{s}\times w_{s}}
(typically
d_{s}\in\{8,16,32\}
). We then aggregate groups with nonnegative coefficients
\alpha_{g,s}
to form the scale-wise cortical pyramid

\Xi_{s}\;=\;\sum_{g\in\{e,m,l\}}\alpha_{g,s}\,\Xi_{g,s},\qquad s\in\mathcal{S}.

(1)

This implements the selective-depth hypothesis: early groups dominate shallow scales, late groups dominate deep scales, and mid-level groups bridge them.

Stability and regularization.
We discourage degenerate single-group dominance via
L_{2}
on
\alpha_{g,s}
and encourage sparse, stable gating on
w_{r,s}
with a mild KL prior that biases shallow scales toward early ROIs and deep scales toward late ROIs. All quantities are bounded in
[0,1]
for stability.

3.3
ROI-Conditioned ControlNet: Selective Depth Injection

Our ControlNet branch translates the adapter’s pyramid
\{\Xi_{s}\}
into
resolution-aligned
hints that
guide generation
. Rather than serving as generic features, these hints are injected exactly at the U-Net depths where they are most informative, shaping the denoising trajectory toward anatomy-aware solutions.

Resolution-aligned injection.

For each scale
s
(matched to a U-Net block with activation
h_{s}
), a shallow stack
G_{\phi,s}
of
3{\times}3
convs produces a hint
\xi_{s}=G_{\phi,s}(\Xi_{s})
. We inject it by a residual + FiLM fusion:

\hat{h}_{s}\;=\;h_{s}\;+\;\lambda_{s}\Big(A_{s}(\xi_{s})\;+\;\gamma_{s}(\xi_{s})\odot h_{s}\;+\;\beta_{s}(\xi_{s})\Big),

(2)

where
A_{s}
is a
1{\times}1
conv and
(\gamma_{s},\beta_{s})
are per-channel affine parameters. The scalar
\lambda_{s}\in[0,1]
controls guidance strength per depth.

Hint normalization and calibration.

We normalize each hint to zero-mean/unit-variance and bound total injected energy via a soft budget

\sum_{s}\lambda_{s}\cdot\mathbb{E}\!\left[\|A_{s}(\xi_{s})\|_{1}\right]\leq\eta
,
implemented by on-the-fly rescaling of
\{\lambda_{s}\}
, to prevent shallow scales from overwhelming deeper layers.

Selective depth and schedule.

We bias contributions so that early groups peak at small
s
, late groups at large
s
, and mid-level in between. Guidance is further scheduled over diffusion time via

\lambda_{s}(t)=\lambda_{s}^{\max}\cdot\tfrac{1}{2}\big(1+\cos(\pi\cdot\mathrm{clip}(t/T,0,1))\big)^{\rho_{s}}
,
so shallow hints dominate early noise removal while deeper hints refine semantics later.

Per-ROI sparsity (optional).

Sparse channel use within
\Xi_{s}
can be encouraged (e.g.,
L_{1}
or entmax gating) to enhance interpretability.

3.4
Hi-DREAM: Overall Architecture & Notation

Let the fMRI input be
\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times H\times W\times D}
and let
\{\mathcal{R}^{e},\mathcal{R}^{m},\mathcal{R}^{l}\}
denote ROI index sets for early/mid/late groups. For ROI
r
, we compute a regional summary
a_{r}\in[0,1]
(normalized beta amplitude over selected TRs) and a subject-specific 2D mask
m_{r}\in\{0,1\}^{h\times w}
aligned to the image input. These form the evidence used downstream.

End-to-end flow.

(1)
ROI adapter.
From
\{a_{r},m_{r}\}
we build a multi-scale cortical pyramid
\{\Xi_{s}\}_{s\in\mathcal{S}}
using per-group mixing and depth-aware aggregation (Eq. 1).
(2)
Depth-matched ControlNet.
Each
\Xi_{s}
is translated into a hint
\xi_{s}
and injected into the corresponding U-Net block with strength
\lambda_{s}(t)
.
(3)
Backbone.
A latent-diffusion U-Net denoises while being guided by
\{\xi_{s}\}
; shallow injections preserve layout and edges, deep injections steer semantics.
(4)
Outputs and inspectability.
We log
\gamma_{s}(\xi_{s})
maps and group contributions to attribute which ROI groups influenced which depths and timesteps—useful for neuroscientific interpretation.

3.5
Training and Inference

Objective.
We optimize the standard diffusion loss

\mathcal{L}_{\text{DM}}=\mathbb{E}\big[\|\epsilon-\epsilon_{\theta}(x_{t},t\mid\{\Xi_{s}\})\|_{2}^{2}\big]

with regularizers on
\alpha
(discourage single-group collapse), on
w
(stable gates), and optional TV on
\{\Xi_{s}\}
for spatial smoothness.

Two-stage schedule.

Stage A: Adapter warm-up.
We freeze the diffusion backbone and train only the ROI adapter to produce stable, informative pyramids.
\alpha_{g,s}
start conservative (near uniform), while
\lambda_{s}
ramps up from small values; this encourages the adapter to encode anatomy-aware structure without overfitting sampler idiosyncrasies.

Stage B: ControlNet alignment and partial unfreeze.
We enable the ROI-Conditioned ControlNet and unfreeze only the U-Net blocks aligned to each scale (depth-matched). The ControlNet learns to translate
\{\Xi_{s}\}
into effective guidance signals; the selectively unfrozen backbone adapts locally to those signals while the rest remains fixed to preserve generative priors. A cosine schedule for
\lambda_{s}(t)
is activated, shaping when (early vs. late denoising) each depth exerts influence.

Validation and ablations.
Per-scale strengths
(\lambda_{\text{early}},\lambda_{\text{middle}},\lambda_{\text{late}})
are chosen on validation to balance structure and semantics. Ablations toggling early/mid/late inputs verify the intended division of labor: early scaffolds structure, middle bridges, late drives semantics.

Inference and complexity.
At test time,
\{\Xi_{s}\}
is computed in one pass and DDIM/PLMS sampling proceeds with fixed
(\alpha_{g,s},\lambda_{s})
. The adapter and ControlNet operate on compact 2D pyramids, adding negligible FLOPs/parameters relative to the U-Net and avoiding the I/O cost of conditioning on full 3D fMRI volumes.

Experiment

4.1
Dataset and Implementation

Dataset.
We use the Natural Scenes Dataset (NSD), a 7T fMRI dataset with natural-image stimuli. NSD includes
8
healthy adults, each completing
\sim
30–40 weekly sessions over about one year. Per subject,
\sim
9–10k unique photographs sampled from the COCO-73k pool were presented. NSD provides preprocessed trial-wise BOLD betas (e.g.,
func1pt8mm
), anatomical derivatives (retinotopy/FreeSurfer), and a stable stimulus identifier (
73KID
) for cross-session/subject alignment.

Implementation details.

We use a latent-diffusion backbone (U-Net in latent space,
256{\times}256
); the backbone is frozen in Stage-1 and partially unfrozen at depth-matched blocks in Stage-2. The ROI adapter builds a four-level pyramid
\mathcal{S}=\{64,128,256,512\}
, where per-group mixers
M_{g,s}
are
1{\times}1
conv–MLP stacks producing
d_{s}\in\{8,16,32\}
channels; for each scale
s
, a lightweight ControlNet branch
G_{\phi,s}
(two
3{\times}3
convs +
1{\times}1
projection) outputs hints
\xi_{s}
that modulate the corresponding U-Net block via residual+FiLM. We optimize with AdamW
[
33
,
14
]
(adapter/ControlNet
1{\times}10^{-4}
; unfrozen U-Net
1{\times}10^{-5}
;
\beta{=}(0.9,0.999)
; wd
1{\times}10^{-2}
), batch size 8 and mixed precision; training uses a two-stage warm-up (Stage-1: adapter+ControlNet with a linear ramp of
\lambda_{s}

L_{2}
on
\alpha_{g,s}
, a mild KL prior on
w_{r,s}
, and optional TV on
\{\Xi_{s}\}
. Data follow NSD conventions (no horizontal flip; light color jitter
\leq 0.1
; inputs in
[0,1]
). Inference uses DDIM-50 with
(\lambda_{\text{shallow}},\lambda_{\text{mid}},\lambda_{\text{deep}})=(0.8,0.6,0.5)
and a cosine time schedule

; CFG is off unless stated. All runs use a single NVIDIA A100 (80GB).

Why

helps.

Compared with prior art, two design choices appear decisive:
(i) a
brain-inspired ROI adapter
that separates early/mid/late groups and builds a multi-scale cortical pyramid. This aligns the information each group provides with the depth of the U-Net (edges/layout at shallow layers; parts/semantics at deeper layers), reducing representational frictions that arise when a single embedding is forced to serve all depths.
(ii) a
selective, depth-matched ControlNet
that injects ROI evidence where it is most useful, with per-scale strength control. This avoids over-conditioning and prevents shallow detail from drowning out deeper semantic cues.
Together, these choices yield stronger semantic metrics without exploding compute: the adapter and ControlNet operate on compact subject-specific maps rather than full 3D fMRI volumes, improving data throughput and simplifying optimization.

Relation to specific baselines.

Hi-DREAM
is orthogonal and complementary: instead of seeking a single best fMRI embedding, we
structure
the conditioning by cortical hierarchy and scale, which preserves interpretability (each signal maps to a brain-like role) while driving gains on semantically grounded metrics. The consistent advantage on Inception/CLIP and feature distances, averaged over the four full-coverage NSD subjects, supports this interpretation.

4.4
Discussion

Brain-inspired conditioning vs. monolithic embeddings.

Our results indicate that structuring fMRI conditioning by cortical hierarchy offers a practical alternative to a single, global embedding. By aligning early/mid/late ROI evidence with shallow/middle/deep U
-
Net depths,
Hi-DREAM
improves semantic alignment without sacrificing much low-level fidelity and provides a clearer attribution of “which brain signals guide which stages” of generation. The ablations support a division of labor consistent with neuroscience: Early ROIs scaffold geometry and landmarks, Late ROIs inject category/identity semantics (e.g., faces via FFA), and Middle ROIs stabilize part-level contours across depths.

Interpretability and controllability.

Because each module carries a brain-like role,
Hi-DREAM
exposes meaningful “knobs”: per-depth guidance strengths
(\lambda_{\text{shallow}},\lambda_{\text{mid}},\lambda_{\text{deep}})
trade texture/layout against semantics, and group weights
\{\alpha_{g,s}\}
visualize which ROIs are influential at each resolution. Depth-wise modulation maps further allow post hoc inspection of where guidance acted in the denoiser. This interpretability is a step toward using generative models as operational hypotheses about cortical processing rather than purely as function approximators.

Efficiency and practicality.

The ROI adapter operates on compact 2D cortical pyramids instead of full 3D fMRI volumes, reducing I/O and easing optimization while retaining spatial specificity. In practice, the extra FLOPs/parameters are negligible relative to the diffusion backbone, enabling training on a single GPU and speeding up iteration for neuroscientific probing.

4.5
Limitations

Our reconstructions still underperform on low-level fidelity (edges, textures) relative to the best pixel/structure metrics, and improving these details without sacrificing the current high-level semantic accuracy remains challenging. In addition, our anatomy is partial: the model conditions on a limited subset of visual ROIs (e.g., a late-stage emphasis on FFA), whereas cortical organization is far richer. This subset choice constrains the breadth of semantics and may introduce subject-specific bias through ROI masks and registrations.
We will (i) strengthen shallow-depth structural guidance and explore learned structural priors to raise low-level fidelity while preserving semantics; (ii) expand the ROI set to include more ventral/dorsal areas and interactions; and (iii) pursue a
universal
ROI representation—e.g., shared parcellations or learned canonical masks—to reduce subject dependence and move toward a generalizable, subject-agnostic decoder.

5
Conclusion

In this paper, we introduced
Hi-DREAM
, a brain-inspired diffusion framework that conditions on fMRI via a cortical hierarchy. By converting early/mid/late ROIs into a multi-scale cortical pyramid and injecting it with a depth-matched ROI-ControlNet,
Hi-DREAM
provides efficient, anatomy-aware guidance and interpretable control over where structure and semantics enter the generator. On the NSD test set,
Hi-DREAM
reaches state-of-the-art performance on high-level semantic metrics while remaining competitive on low-level fidelity. Looking ahead, we aim to strengthen low-level detail without sacrificing semantics and to broaden anatomical coverage and temporal modeling, moving toward a decoder that more closely mirrors human visual processing.
