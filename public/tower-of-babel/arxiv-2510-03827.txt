LIBERO-PRO: Towards Robust and Fair Evaluation of Vision-Language-Action Models Beyond Memorization
Xueyang Zhou, Yangming Xu, Guiyao Tie, Yongchao Chen, Guowen Zhang, Duanfeng Chu, Pan Zhou, Lichao Sun, [1em] Huazhong University of Science and Technology

Abstract
LIBERO has emerged as a widely adopted benchmark for evaluating Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models; however, its current training and evaluation settings are problematic, often leading to inflated performance estimates and preventing fair model comparison. To address these issues, we introduce LIBERO-PRO, an extended LIBERO benchmark that systematically evaluates model performance under reasonable perturbations across four dimensions: manipulated objects, initial states, task instructions, and environments. Experimental results reveal that, although existing models achieve over 90% accuracy under the standard LIBERO evaluation, their performance collapses to 0.0% under our generalized setting. Crucially, this discrepancy exposes the models’ reliance on rote memorization of action sequences and environment layouts from the training set, rather than genuine task understanding or environmental perception. For instance, models persist in executing grasping actions when the target object is replaced with irrelevant items, and their outputs remain unchanged even when given corrupted instructions or even messy tokens. These findings expose the severe flaws in current evaluation practices, and we call on the community to abandon misleading methodologies in favor of robust assessments of model generalization and comprehension. Our code is available at: https://github.com/Zxy-MLlab/LIBERO-PRO .

INTRODUCTION

Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models have recently emerged as a central paradigm for bridging perception, language understanding and action execution in embodied agents
Sapkota et al. 2025
. A crucial factor in advancing this line of research is the availability of standardized benchmarks
James et al. 2020
;
Liu et al. 2023
;
Mees et al. 2022
;
Li et al. 2024b
;
Nasiriany et al. 2024
;
Walke et al. 2023
that allow fair and reproducible comparison across different methods. Among them, LIBERO
Liu et al. 2023
has rapidly become the most widely adopted evaluation suite for VLA, serving as the de facto standard for reporting performance. As shown in Table
1
, models ranging from large-scale pre-trained to specialized systems are all benchmarked on LIBERO, making it the “common currency” of evaluation in this field. Consequently, the reliability of LIBERO as an evaluation protocol plays a decisive role in shaping research directions in VLA.

Figure 2
:
Success rates of OpenVLA, pi0, pi0.5, and univla under object position perturbations.

However, the current LIBERO training–evaluation setup may suffer from serious limitations. In fact, the evaluation tasks are exactly the same as the training tasks, with the only difference being slight perturbations in the initial states of objects—perturbations that are almost invisible to the human eye. As a result, if a model overfits the training set, it can easily achieve high scores during evaluation, but its accuracy drops to nearly zero under minor changes in object initial positions or task instructions, as shown in Figure
2
. Yet such results do not indicate that the model has learned to understand the environment or the task. On the contrary, we find that models rely on fixed mappings from the training tasks. As illustrated in Figure
1
, when we introduced four types of modifications—(1)
replacing the target object with an unrelated one;
(2)
relocating its initial position;
(3)
removing it entirely;
and (4)
corrupting the instruction with nonsensical tokens
—their trajectories remained nearly identical. This outcome shows that, despite achieving over 90% on the standard LIBERO benchmark, the reported scores reflect rote recall rather than genuine task understanding.
Obviously, the current evaluation protocol is inadequate and fails to effectively assess model architectures and algorithms.

To address the above shortcomings, we systematically propose an extended evaluation suite, i.e.,
LIBERO-PRO
, to enable a more comprehensive and reasonable assessment of VLA models. We design evaluation scenarios along four key dimensions: manipulated objects, initial states, task instructions, and environments. While ensuring that each task remains coherent and executable, we introduce carefully designed perturbations to the original LIBERO tasks to simulate real-world situations. Using this suite, we evaluate state-of-the-art models including OpenVLA
Kim et al. 2024
and pi0/0.5, and the results show that they exhibit near-complete failure under several of these settings: the models cannot cope with changes in object positions, nor can they correctly follow slightly rephrased task instructions—even when such instructions have appeared in other training contexts. These findings reveal that the commonly reported accuracies of over 90% are largely artifacts of task memorization, rather than evidence of genuine perception or task understanding. We therefore call on the community to adopt evaluation standards that incorporate such necessary perturbations, ensuring that reported progress reflects capabilities required for practical use. Our main contributions are as follows:

•

Through extensive experiments, we demonstrate that the current LIBERO evaluation protocol is fundamentally flawed: high scores primarily reflect rote memorization of training data rather than genuine task understanding or execution ability.

•

We introduce LIBERO-PRO, a plug-and-play benchmark that incorporates carefully designed perturbations across four dimensions—manipulated objects, initial states, task instructions, and environments—and supports randomized combinations of these dimensions for more faithful evaluation.

•

We benchmark state-of-the-art models, including OpenVLA, pi0 and pi0.5, on LIBERO-PRO and show that they perform poorly, highlighting the inadequacy of the current LIBERO protocol and calling for LIBERO-PRO as a fairer and more reliable evaluation standard.

Related Work

Vision-Language-Action Models.

Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models have rapidly emerged as a central paradigm for embodied AI, aiming to unify perception, natural language understanding, and action execution within a single framework. Recent years have witnessed an explosion of approaches, ranging from large-scale pre-trained models that leverage multimodal corpora to task-specific architectures tailored for robotic manipulation and navigation
Ma et al. 2025
;
Li et al. 2025b
;
Din et al. 2025
. While these efforts have significantly advanced the field, they also underscore the urgent need for standardized evaluation benchmarks
Liu et al. 2023
;
Mees et al. 2022
;
James et al. 2020
. Without such benchmarks, it becomes difficult to conduct fair and reproducible comparisons across methods, hindering the cumulative progress of VLA research.

Benchmarks for VLA Evaluation.

A number of benchmarks have been developed to enable systematic evaluation of VLA models, including RLBench
James et al. 2020
, CALVIN
Mees et al. 2022
, RoboCasa
Nasiriany et al. 2024
, and BridgeData
Walke et al. 2023
. These benchmarks provide valuable testbeds for task execution under vision and language guidance, yet their adoption has remained relatively limited within the broader community. In contrast, LIBERO
Liu et al. 2023
has quickly emerged as the dominant benchmark and is now the most widely adopted protocol for evaluating VLA models. Its standardized task suite and unified reporting metrics have made it the de facto standard for performance comparison, to the extent that virtually all recent VLA studies report results on LIBERO. As such, LIBERO not only serves as the “common currency” of evaluation in this field, but also exerts a strong influence on research directions and claims of progress.

Limitations of Current Evaluation Practices.

Several recent studies have noted that the high scores reported on LIBERO often fail to translate into reliable performance, even on tasks with only minor variations. This has raised serious concerns about the validity of its evaluation protocol
Zhou et al. 2025
;
Fang et al. 2025
;
Li 2025
. In response, some researchers have explored stronger algorithms or proposed stress tests
Gu et al. 2025
;
Guruprasad et al. 2024
;
Valle et al. 2025
;
Kube et al. 2025
;
Wang et al. 2025a
to encourage more robust VLA systems. However, the field still lacks a systematic and standardized framework for robust evaluation under LIBERO. The majority of works continue to rely on the original, flawed setup, reporting numbers that may not reflect genuine capability. To address this gap, we propose LIBERO-PRO, a plug-and-play extension of LIBERO designed to foster fair comparison across models and to advance research on building VLA systems that can withstand reasonable and practically necessary perturbations.

3
Problem Definition

This section formalizes the Vision-Language-Action (VLA) task framework and examines limitations in the LIBERO benchmark’s evaluation protocol that may obscure true task competence.

3.1
Formal Task Definition

We define a VLA task to establish a rigorous basis for evaluating robotic systems.

\tau=(\ell,\mathcal{O},\mathcal{E},p_{0},G),

(1)

where
\ell
is a natural language instruction specifying the task objective,
\mathcal{O}
is a finite set of objects with attributes (e.g., shape, color, size, initial pose),
\mathcal{E}
denotes the static environmental context (e.g., scene geometry, furniture layout),
p_{0}
is a probability distribution over initial states
s_{0}
(encoding init position and robot proprioception), and
G(s)
is a binary goal predicate, returning
1
if state
s
satisfies
\ell
, and
0
otherwise.

The performance of a policy
\pi_{\theta}(a\mid s,\ell)
, parameterized by
\theta
, is measured by the expected success probability over a finite horizon
T
:

\mathbb{P}_{s_{0}\sim p_{0},\pi_{\theta}}\left[G(s_{T})=1\right],

(2)

where
s_{T}
is the terminal state after
T
steps. This formulation captures the integration of perception, language understanding, and action planning in VLA systems.

3.2
Current LIBERO Evaluation Protocol

The LIBERO benchmark, while valuable for VLA research, employs an evaluation protocol that may overestimate model competence due to its homogeneous train/test split. Each test task
\tau^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{test}}
mirrors a training task
\tau\in\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{train}}
:

\ell^{\prime}=\ell,\quad\mathcal{O}^{\prime}=\mathcal{O},\quad\mathcal{E}^{\prime}=\mathcal{E},\quad G^{\prime}=G,

(3)

with the only difference being a minor perturbation in the initial state distribution:

d_{\mathrm{TV}}(p_{0},p_{0}^{\prime})\leq\epsilon,\quad\epsilon\ll 1.

(4)

Due to the small
\epsilon
, test tasks closely resemble training tasks, allowing policies to achieve high performance through memorization rather than robust generalization. This homogeneity may inflate performance metrics, masking limitations in compositional generalization and adaptability to diverse real-world scenarios, a critical concern for embodied AI.

4
Methodology

4.1
Proposed Perturbation-Based Evaluation Framework

For a more reasonable evaluation of VLA models, we propose a framework that introduces controlled perturbations across four orthogonal dimensions, manipulated objects, initial states, task instructions, and environments. For a task
\tau=(\ell,\mathcal{O},\mathcal{E},p_{0},G)
, we generate perturbed tasks
\tau^{(k)}=\phi_{k}(\tau)
using operators
\phi_{k}
,
k\in\{\mathrm{O},\mathrm{S},\mathrm{L},\mathrm{E}\}
, defined as follows:

Object Attribute Perturbation (
k=\mathrm{O}
)
: Modifies non-essential object attributes, such as color, texture, or size, while preserving semantic equivalence, e.g., changing a red cup to a blue cup. This tests the model’s robustness to superficial visual changes critical for real-world adaptability. The perturbed task is:

\tau^{(\mathrm{O})}=\left(\ell,\mathcal{O}^{(\mathrm{O})},\mathcal{E},p_{0},G\right).

(5)

Initial Position Perturbation (
k=\mathrm{I}
)
: Alters initial object positions to modify absolute and relative arrangements, ensuring physical plausibility, e.g., moving a cup relative to a plate. This probes the model’s spatial reasoning under varied layouts. The perturbed task is:

\tau^{(\mathrm{I})}=\left(\ell,\mathcal{O},\mathcal{E},p_{0}^{(\mathrm{I})},G\right).

(6)

Instruction Perturbation (
k=\mathrm{L}
)
: Perturbations are applied at both the semantic and task levels. Semantic perturbations adjust the linguistic expression while preserving the original task intent, such as paraphrasing “pick up the bottle” to “grab the bottle” or substituting with a jar. This evaluates the agent’s understanding of language and semantics. The perturbed task is:

\tau^{(\mathrm{L})}=\left(\ell^{(\mathrm{L})},\mathcal{O},\mathcal{E},p_{0},G\right),

(7)

Task perturbations reasonably modify the task itself by replacing the target object or altering the action, e.g., changing “pick up the bottle” to “pick up the bowl.” We ensure that all actions and objects appear in the training set to support fair and meaningful generalization. The perturbed task is:

\tau^{(\mathrm{L})}=\left(\ell^{(\mathrm{L})},\mathcal{O}^{(\mathrm{L})},\mathcal{E},p_{0},G^{(\mathrm{L})}\right).

(8)

Environment Perturbation (
k=\mathrm{E}
)
: Modifies the background’s appearance, e.g., changing a workspace, without affecting task feasibility, to assess robustness to environmental variations. The perturbed task is:

\tau^{(\mathrm{E})}=\left(\ell,\mathcal{O},\mathcal{E}^{(\mathrm{E})},p,G\right).

(9)

4.2
Perturbation Constraints

To ensure each perturbed task
\tau^{(k)}
remains semantically valid, we impose two constraints.
First, perturbations are confined within a dimension-specific neighborhood:

x^{(k)}\in\mathcal{N}_{k}(x),\quad\mathrm{dist}_{k}(x,x^{(k)})\leq\delta_{k},

(10)

where
x
is the original component,
x^{(k)}
is its perturbed version, and
\delta_{k}
controls the magnitude of perturbation for dimension
k\in\{\mathrm{O},\mathrm{S},\mathrm{L},\mathrm{E}\}
.
Second, we enforce non-trivial task-level separation:

d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\tau,\tau^{(k)})>\epsilon,\quad\forall k\in\{\mathrm{O},\mathrm{S},\mathrm{L},\mathrm{E}\},

(11)

where
d_{\mathrm{TV}}
is the total variation distance, ensuring that perturbations lead to substantive deviations.
Multiple perturbed tasks are generated per category by applying
\phi_{k}
, with
\delta_{k}
varied to characterize the sensitivity of policies under different perturbation magnitudes.

4.3
LIBERO-PRO Evaluation Benchmark

Building on these principles, we introduce LIBERO-PRO, a plug-and-play evaluation benchmark that enables systematic assessment under multiple dimensions of variation—object, position, instruction, task, and environment—as well as their arbitrary combinations, as shown in Figure
3
. Specifically, for object variation, we create new assets for the four LIBERO tasks by modifying the appearance, size, and color of the original objects. For position variation, we define alternative spatial regions for all manipulable objects while strictly adhering to physical constraints and task definitions. For instruction variation, we provide three distinct paraphrased versions of each task description. For task variation, we redesign tasks in a principled manner to ensure feasibility, and supply new sets of relevant objects and target states for each modified task. For environment variation, we leverage the five environment categories built into the LIBERO benchmark and randomly substitute them across tasks. To avoid meaningless combinations, we restrict task variation from being combined with other types, while allowing all other dimensions of variation to be freely and randomly combined for more comprehensive evaluation.

5
Experiments

5.1
Settings

We conduct experiments on LIBERO-PRO, which applies controlled perturbations from four perspectives: object properties, initial positions, task instructions (covering both semantic and structural variations), and environments. We evaluate three representative models—OpenVLA
Kim et al. 2024
, pi0
Black et al. 2024
, and pi0.5
Intelligence et al. 2025
. For fairness, OpenVLA is assessed using four official checkpoints trained on different LIBERO suites, whereas pi0 and pi0.5 are evaluated with a single official checkpoint. Consistent with the original LIBERO protocol, we set the number of evaluation episodes to 50 per task.

5.2
Main Results

We conducted a comprehensive evaluation of state-of-the-art vision-language-action (VLA) models
Black et al. 2024
;
Kim et al. 2024
using the LIBERO-PRO evaluation suite, which introduces perturbations across four dimensions: objects, goals, spatial configurations, and task sets (see Tables
2
,
3
,
4
,
5
). Our findings are as follows: (1)
Failure under simple perturbations.
Despite achieving success rates above 90% on the standard LIBERO benchmark, models nearly collapse under changes to object positions or minor task modifications, even when constructed from training components. (2)
Illusory robustness.
Apparent stability against object and instruction perturbations is largely due to overfitting rather than genuine task understanding. (3)
Model-specific sensitivity.
Environmental perturbations affect models differently, depending on the degree of environmental divergence and reliance on memorized contexts. (4)
Subtle capability differences.
While most models fail under perturbations, Pi0.5 achieves a 0.38 success rate in the libero-goal task under position changes, compared to 0 for OpenVLA and Pi0, revealing differences masked by standard benchmark scores. Overall, these results show that current VLA models, despite excelling on the standard LIBERO, lack true robustness and generalization—highlighting the need for rigorous evaluation frameworks.

5.3
Does the Model Generalize to New Objects?

(a)

"salad dressing" in the training set.

(b)

"salad dressing" not in the training set.

(c)

Replace "salad dressing" with "alphabet soup".

Figure 4
:
Pick up salad dressing and place it in basket.

To investigate whether the VLA model exhibits true object-level generalization, we conducted tests by altering the manipulated object in the task “pick up the salad dressing and place it in the basket”. As shown in Figure
4
, replacing the original salad dressing with an unseen but visually similar variant from outside the training set (second row) did not affect the model’s performance, suggesting a limited degree of generalization to perturbed object instances. However, when substituting salad dressing with an unrelated object (e.g., alphabet soup, third row), the model still executed the same action trajectory, attempting to pick up the new object despite its mismatch with the instruction. This behavior suggests that the model’s apparent generalization is largely driven by rote recall of action trajectories from training rather than genuine grounding of object identity. Moreover, its failure on semantically irrelevant objects highlights the inability to correctly align linguistic references with object semantics. Taken together, these observations indicate that the model’s performance reflects superficial pattern memorization rather than true object-level reasoning.

5.4
Does the Model Generalize to Varied Instructions?

(a)

Original task.

(b)

Semantic perturbation: Grab salad dressing and set it in basket.

(c)

Meaningless task: fdsgfdsgsd or
xxx
.

Figure 5
:
Pick up salad dressing and place it in basket.

We evaluated whether the VLA model robustly follows natural language instructions. As shown in Figure
5
, the model successfully executed the original task (“pick up the salad dressing and place it in the basket”) and also completed the task when the instruction was reformulated with synonymous expressions (“grab the salad dressing and set it in the basket”). Although this outcome may appear to suggest robustness to linguistic variation, when the instruction was replaced with meaningless input (e.g., “xxx”), the model produced the same action sequence, again retrieving and executing the pattern of placing the salad dressing into the basket. This observation indicates that the apparent robustness does not arise from genuine semantic comprehension. Rather, the model relies predominantly on visual context and memorized action trajectories from training, while disregarding the linguistic content of the input. Consequently, the model’s consistent behavior across both paraphrased and nonsensical instructions should not be regarded as successful generalization to semantic perturbations, but instead as evidence of its inability to ground or interpret instructions, revealing an overdependence on rote pattern execution.

5.5
How Sensitive is the Model to Object Placement?

Figure 6
:
Success rates of OpenVLA, pi0, and pi0.5 under object position perturbations.

Although all three models achieve around 98% accuracy under the standard LIBERO, our perturbation experiments reveal significant robustness gaps that remain hidden in the original evaluation. As shown in Figure
6
, the models are highly sensitive to the initial position of objects: for both OpenVLA and pi0, performance collapses once displacement exceeds 0.2 units, dropping to zero success, while pi0.5—benefiting from a stronger architecture and broader training data—exhibits improved resilience and sustains non-trivial accuracy up to 0.4 units before also collapsing to zero. These results indicate that high scores do not guarantee reliable performance in practice, as even small variations such as placing a cup in a slightly different position can cause complete task failure, underscoring the necessity of LIBERO-PRO to systematically reveal such robustness gaps and provide a more faithful comparison across models.

5.6
Is the Model Truly Understanding Tasks?

Figure 7
:
The model successfully executed two independent instructions, but failed to execute the combined instruction.

To assess whether the model demonstrates genuine task comprehension or merely memorizes action trajectories, we designed a sequence of three progressively structured tasks (as shown in Figure
7
). In the first task, the agent is instructed to pick up the bowl and place it on the stove, and the model executes this sequence successfully, indicating its ability to reproduce simple manipulation behaviors. In the second task, the instruction is to turn on the stove, and again the model succeeds, suggesting competence in carrying out isolated atomic actions. However, in the third task, which composes these two operations into a longer sequence—first picking up the bowl, then placing it on the stove, and finally turning the stove on—the model consistently fails. Rather than demonstrating adaptive reasoning over the composed instruction, the model replays spurious trajectories resembling those seen during training, leading to unsuccessful execution. This discrepancy reveals a critical limitation: the model does not truly understand either the semantic goal of the task or the causal relationships between subtasks, but instead exhibits rote reproduction of memorized patterns from the training distribution, and the failure on even modestly composed instructions underscores the fragility of current approaches and highlights the need for evaluation protocols that probe beyond single-step or template-like behaviors.

6
Conclusion

In conclusion, our study reveals that the current LIBERO evaluation protocol is fundamentally flawed: by reusing identical training and evaluation tasks with only imperceptible perturbations, it fails to measure genuine capability. Reported accuracies above 90% largely reflect rote memorization of fixed mappings from the training set, rather than true comprehension of instructions or acquisition of robust action strategies. We call on the community to move beyond misleading evaluation practices and adopt our LIBERO-PRO benchmark, which provides a principled and effective framework for assessing the real generalization and understanding capabilities of VLA models.
