Barriers and facilitators in preventing and managing parastomal hernias: a qualitative study from the perspective of enterostomal therapist
Authors: Xinyan Che, Yisen Meng, Dong Pang, Han Song, Jianing Han, Jianfeng Zhang, Shuhui Yu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundA parastomal hernia is a common complication of ostomies. Once developed, it is typically irreversible and can significantly impair a patient’s quality of life. Despite their prevalence, current strategies for the prevention and management of parastomal hernias remain inadequate. Previous research has explored the influencing factors from the patient’s perspective; however, there is a notable lack of in-depth analysis from the viewpoint of Enterostomal Therapists. Therefore, the present study explores the barriers and facilitators affecting the prevention and management of parastomal hernias in patients with ostomies from the perspective of Enterostomal Therapists.MethodsThis study was a descriptive qualitative research design. Using purposive snowball sampling, semi-structured interviews were conducted between August and November 2024 from 18 tertiary hospitals across 11 Chinese provinces. Based on a review of the literature and discussions within the research team, an interview outline was developed. The interview data were organized using MAXQDA 2022 software and systematically analyzed through thematic analysis. A combination of dual independent coding and respondent feedback verification was employed.ResultsThis study included 22 Enterostomal Therapists, with an average of 15 years of experience in stoma care. The main barriers were identified in three areas: (1) Insufficient professional support, driven by limited capacity amid expanding responsibilities and fragmented collaboration that disrupted care continuity; (2) Overburdened patients struggling to cope, evident in shifting needs, psychological distress, and emotional strain; and (3) An inadequate supportive environment, marked by uneven resource distribution and an overload of information of inconsistent quality. The facilitating factors mainly included: (1) Comprehensive professional involvement, featuring full cycle professional management and continuous prevention-oriented supervision; (2) Patient empowerment and self-driven engagement, manifested through active participation, behavioral internalization, self-awareness, and continuous growth; and (3) Digital connectivity and home-based support, including technology‑assisted communication and continuous home‑oriented care.ConclusionsTo improve parastomal hernia prevention and management in patients with ostomies, nursing practice and continuing education should prioritize targeted training of Enterostomal Therapists, structured multidisciplinary collaboration, stratified patient education, optimized resource allocation, and pragmatic use of digital health.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12912-025-04230-2.

Background

Stoma surgery involves creating an artificial outlet in the patient’s abdomen to replace or assist normal excretory functions. The parastomal hernia (PH), one of the most common long-term complications of stoma surgery, is a critical factor affecting patient quality of life. Data from the 2017 edition of the European Hernia Society Guidelines on the Treatment of PH indicate that the incidence of PH exceeds 30% within the first year after surgery, rises above 40% within two years, and can reach 50% or higher with prolonged survival [1]. This condition not only causes pain but also complicates the adhesion of stoma products, leading to leakage and severely limiting the patients’ ability to travel, socialize, and return to work [2]. In severe cases, PHs may result in intestinal obstruction and strangulation, necessitating emergency surgical intervention. However, the surgical treatment of PH carries high risks, is costly, and has a high recurrence rate, making it far from an ideal solution [3, 4]. In summary, effectively preventing the occurrence of PHs and scientifically managing them have become critical issues that urgently need to be addressed in stoma care.

Studies have shown that with weight management, avoidance of heavy lifting after surgery, and the use of an abdominal binder or light supportive garments, the incidence of PH at one year is only 1% [5], markedly lower than the 30% reported in previous observational studies [1]. Despite these recommendations, many patients face substantial challenges in managing their stomas and related complications in real life. One survey [6] found that only 10% of respondents first learned about PHs from healthcare professionals. Among those with prior knowledge (71%), most became aware of the condition only after diagnosis through reading relevant materials. This indicates that a substantial proportion of stoma patients may have either never received education on PHs or have forgotten the information over time. Furthermore, a national survey highlighted concerning trends [7]: the majority of home-based stoma patients did not engage in abdominal muscle exercises (88%) and neglected medical advice regarding exercise (82%), and a considerable number failed to use abdominal binders (64%). According to Munro et al. [8], the primary self-management strategy for PH among ostomy patients was the use of support garments. These findings underscore the deficiencies in patient adherence to the recommended prevention and management practices for PHs, reflecting gaps in patient education and support systems.

At present, the key elements related to the global prevention and management of PH include preoperative stoma education and site marking, non-surgical preventive strategies (e.g., weight management, wearing support garments, and abdominal muscle exercises), continuous follow-up led by Enterostomal therapists (ETs), as well as the use of prophylactic mesh under appropriate indications. These are consistent with the recommendations of international guidelines [1, 9–12], suggesting that the mechanisms identified in this study have transferability across different health systems. Furthermore, ETs as the professional instructors and supporters, play an indispensable role in the prevention and management of PH. At the same time, as the hub of multidisciplinary collaboration, ETs serve as the primary implementers of non-surgical preventive strategies and standardized care pathways, bridging evidence-based findings with practical clinical procedures [13].

The prevention and management of PHs among ostomy patients are influenced by factors at multiple levels: individual (sociodemographic characteristics, physiological and psychological status), interpersonal (support and communication from family, peers, and healthcare professionals), and societal levels (government policies, hospital resources, community services) [14]. This multidimensional view is reinforced by a qualitative meta-synthesis showing that knowledge, social support, and psychological state are pivotal determinants of ostomy management [15]. However, existing research has largely examined PH prevention and management from the patients’ perspectives [16–18], with limited attention to the perspectives of ETs. To address this gap, this study uses ETs as key informants, aiming to identify barriers and facilitators of PH prevention and management from their professional viewpoints, explore the priority competency areas and potential development directions for ET continuing education, thereby providing a practice-oriented basis for the optimization and promotion of ostomy care pathways.

Methods

Study design

This study employed a descriptive qualitative research design [19, 20], aiming to provide a concise, comprehensive, and in-depth description of this phenomenon. The results of the analysis are presented as concepts or categories. Through interviews with ETs, the study delved into and explored their subjective experiences and perceptions regarding prevention and management of PHs. Therefore, this method aligns closely with the study objectives.

Participants and sampling

In this study, purposeful snowball sampling methods were employed to ensure comprehensiveness and representativeness of the data. The initial six participants were faculty members from the International School of Enterostomal Therapy at our hospital, selected based on the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria in the study. They possessed professional certification as ETs, held intermediate or higher professional titles, and had extensive experience in ostomy care. These experts served as the starting point for the snowball sampling process. Through their professional recommendations, additional participants were identified from different healthcare settings. This approach ensured regional and institutional diversity within the sample. The sequence of interviews was arranged according to participants’ availability to maintain flexibility and feasibility in data collection.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria for participants were as follows: (1) completion of professional training and possession of certification as a ET from a national, academic, or provincial-level accrediting body; (2) a professional title at an intermediate or higher level; and (3) a minimum of five years of experience in ostomy care. Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) individuals who were no longer actively involved in practical ostomy care, such as those exclusively engaged in nursing education or management for more than one year; and (2) individuals working in departments that no longer admitted patients with ostomies. Participants’ characteristics

Data collection

Semi-structured interviews were conducted from August to November 2024, The mean duration of the interviews was 32 min, ranging from 25 to 45 min. The researchers explained the study’s purpose and significance to potential participants through voice calls or face-to-face meetings and subsequently obtained verbal or written consent. With respect to the interview methodology, formats such as WeChat, Tencent Meeting, video conferences, and in-person interviews were selected based on participants’ preferences and convenience. Among the 25 potential participants contacted, 22 participated in the interviews. Of these, six participants were interviewed face-to-face, three via WeChat, and 13 through Tencent Meeting. Three individuals unable to participate owing to their work commitments assisted in identifying other ETs from their respective institutions as replacements. To ensure data authenticity and reliability, following participant approval, nonverbal cues, including pauses, intonation, facial expressions, and gestures, were recorded to ensure a holistic capture of communication dynamics. Data collection continued until theoretical saturation was achieved, ensuring a comprehensive dataset for subsequent analyses. Within 24 h of recording, audio files were transcribed verbatim into Word documents using iFlytek digital transcription software and subsequently verified and refined through manual review to maintain accuracy and fidelity. We formulated the interview outline based on the literature review [1, 9–11] and the research group discussions. The interview questions are presented in Appendix 1.

Data analysis

During data analysis, we applied Braun and Clarke’s six-phase framework using an inductive approach [21], allowing themes to emerge directly from participants’ narratives. Two researchers independently coded the data, attending to both semantic and latent meanings. They generated codes through line-by-line open coding and then iteratively clustered them into candidate themes, which were reviewed against the coded extracts and the full dataset for coherence. We refined themes through regular discussions to reach consensus, and a senior qualitative researcher helped resolve any disagreements. Final themes were supported by representative quotations. To enhance credibility, participants reviewed summaries of preliminary themes to confirm the accuracy of our interpretations. No further member checking was conducted after data saturation and thematic stability were achieved. After completing the interviews and data analysis of 20 participants, we found that data saturation had been reached. To further verify this, two additional participants were interviewed, and no new information was identified, confirming data saturation.

Rigor

This study adhered to the guidelines for qualitative reporting [22]. To ensure credibility, all interviews were conducted by a single researcher with 12 years of experience in ostomy care and management, who had no prior clinical relationship with the participants and was formally trained in qualitative research methods [23, 24]. We acknowledged that this positionality could facilitate rapport building but might also introduce social desirability bias. To address this potential bias, the following measures were implemented: (a) emphasizing the interview’s non-evaluative purpose; (b) explicitly inviting participants to share critical or negative experiences; (c) employing open-ended and non-leading prompts; and (d) maintaining reflexive memos to document assumptions and analytical decisions throughout the process. Additionally, team members not involved in stoma care reviewed the coding and theme definitions to incorporate outsider perspectives and enhance analytical balance.

For participants who chose WeChat or Tencent Meeting, the entire interview process was meticulously recorded and, with explicit consent, nonverbal cues (pauses, intonation, facial expressions, gestures) were documented. Face-to-face interviews were conducted in quiet, private settings to minimize distractions and enhance data quality. For dependability, two researchers independently coded the data and extracted themes, then cross-validated the results through team discussions to reduce subjective bias. Preliminary analyses were returned to a subset of interviewees for feedback to verify interpretive accuracy and credibility. To strengthen confirmability and transferability, the study maintained comprehensive documentation from project initiation and research design through data collection, analysis, results formulation, and report writing. We used MAXQDA 2022 for data management to support reproducibility and auditability. Detailed descriptions of methods (including participants and data collection procedures) and citation-supported findings were provided to calibrate analytic precision and facilitate assessment of applicability in other contexts.

Results

Participant demographics

This study included 22 ETs from 18 hospitals in 11 provinces. According to the 2024 edition of the Fudan Best Hospitals Ranking, seven of the hospitals included in this study are among the top ten hospitals in China. Participants’ ages ranged from 33 to 61 years, with an average age of 45 years. Among them, 11 held intermediate professional titles, and 11 held senior professional titles; 11 participants had a bachelor’s degree, and 11 had a postgraduate degree. Participants had between 11 and 44 years of nursing experience, with an average of 25 years, and their experience in ostomy care ranged from 5 to 34 years, with an average of 15 years. Regarding their professional roles, nine participants worked full-time in ostomy outpatient clinics, nine worked part-time in ostomy outpatient clinics, and four did not participate in outpatient work and engage in stoma-related work only part-time in the ward. Additionally, 50% of stoma therapists were instructors in ostomy care training schools. The interviewees in this study represent highly qualified specialized professionals in ostomy care in China, possessing extensive clinical and teaching experience. Meanwhile, as instructors at the school for ETs, their perspectives have, to some extent, influenced the training of ETs.

Subtheme 2: Fragmented collaboration and continuity challenges

Although responsibilities across different departments—such as the inpatient ward, rehabilitation unit, nutrition department, and outpatient clinic—are clearly defined, cross-cycle coordination remains weak. Ward‑based medical and nursing teams focus on inpatient treatment, while post‑discharge management shifts to outpatient or community care. This creates segmented stoma management with limited continuity. Effective handover and feedback channels are largely absent. ETs are often positioned at the end of the collaboration chain, facing the dual challenge of addressing individual patient needs while having limited access to systematic support from other professionals. Moreover, the absence of a unified information‑sharing platform hinders the dynamic tracking of patient risks across multidisciplinary teams.There are clear boundaries between workstations and departments. For example, when they were in the ward, I definitely could not intervene in ward-related tasks. After being discharged, the patient was transferred to our outpatient clinic. (Participant 11)

Theme 2: Overburdened and coping-challenged patients

Subtheme 1: Diverse needs and uneven responses

During hospitalization, patients undergo numerous diagnostic evaluations and surgical preparations, leading to substantial psychological stress that hinders their ability to engage with health education. In the early postoperative phase, as they begin adapting to and accepting the stoma, priorities center on maintaining basic quality of life, such as addressing pain, fatigue, and other discomforts resulting from surgery, eating, changing ostomy bags, and preventing peristomal skin complications. Proactive demand for the preventive management of PHs remains low. Demographically, ostomy patients are predominantly middle-aged and older adults; age-related degeneration of the rectus abdominis compromises effective training, contributes to muscle laxity, and reduces recovery capacity, thereby limiting intrinsic motivation for exercise. Subtheme 2: Psychological distress and emotional barriers

The alterations in excretion patterns and body image following ostomy surgery often lead patients to experience significant psychological fluctuations. Many patients exhibit anxiety, shame, or avoidance behaviors, and some even deny or resist accepting their new physical condition. Although such psychological defense mechanisms may temporarily alleviate emotional distress, they can, in the long term, hinder rehabilitation motivation and the development of health-promoting behaviors. Concerns about odor, leakage, or changes in appearance may cause patients to limit social interactions, resulting in tendencies toward isolation and withdrawal. This psychological distress can make it challenging for patients to maintain adherence to stoma rehabilitation training.People with ostomies usually feel really insecure and don’t like talking to others much. So, we do work on using (abdominal muscle exercises) to improve PHs, but not a whole lot of it right now. (Participant 11)

People with ostomies usually feel really insecure and don’t like talking to others much. So, we do work on using (abdominal muscle exercises) to improve PHs, but not a whole lot of it right now. (Participant 11)

Theme 3: Inadequate supportive environment

Subtheme 1: Uneven resource distribution

Given increasingly strained medical resources and difficulty securing appointments, extended appointment wait times are particularly acute in major hospitals in metropolitan areas. However, when managing late-stage complications such as PHs, clinicians often redirect patients to ETs, who then assume responsibility for ongoing management and care.

Nowadays, after patients go through so much trouble securing an appointment with a doctor, the doctor takes one look, sees it’s a PH that does not require surgery, and simply refers the patient to us (ETs). (Participant 18)

Nowadays, after patients go through so much trouble securing an appointment with a doctor, the doctor takes one look, sees it’s a PH that does not require surgery, and simply refers the patient to us (ETs). (Participant 18)

Owing to geographical constraints and the uneven distribution of medical resources, patients face numerous challenges in self-managing PH prevention. Healthcare professionals are willing to assist patients; however, the limited reach of medical institutions, particularly for patients from outlying areas, result in a lack of systematic guidance and support.

For patients from out of town, we definitely intend to help, but extending our reach that far is impossible. (Participant 2)

Subtheme 2: Information overload and quality imbalance

In the context of the rapid dissemination of online information, some unscreened content may be misinterpreted, exaggerated, or even misleading, negatively affecting patient decision-making and health management. Such inaccurate information may not only lead patients to disregard professional advice but also result in misconceptions about their condition, delaying treatment, or even worsening their health. Furthermore, the current public and patient awareness of self-management for PH prevention remains relatively low, leading to limited dissemination of professional knowledge. This unequal distribution of health education resources and platforms prevents certain regions and groups from accessing high-quality medical support promptly.

For example, some patients refuse to wear an abdominal belt. They say they saw online that wearing one presses on the stoma and might even cause the stoma to become necrotic or things like that. Facilitators

Theme 1: Comprehensive professional involvement

Subtheme 1: Full cycle professional management

ETs play a critical role throughout the patient’s hospitalization. Their responsibilities encompass assessing risk factors for PH, evaluating nutritional status, and determining the optimal stoma site. Preoperatively, the ET, along with trained nurses, conducts assessments and marks the stoma location by measuring abdominal circumference and identifying a suitable site. Postoperatively, they educate patients on stoma self-care, including practical skills like appliance change and cleansing, and manage complications such as irritant dermatitis. To enhance patients’ self-management competence, multiple strategies are employed, including instructional videos, written materials, and stoma support groups. During follow-up, patients can access guidance and solutions for stoma-related problems through the therapist’s outpatient clinics or via online consultations.

We make it a point to discuss a few key things with all our patients. One is weight management. The other is to avoid anything that increases abdominal pressure, like chronic coughing, holding in urine, or lifting heavy objects… For those who are physically active, we might introduce specific abdominal muscle training. This is usually suitable for patients with a background in, say, yoga or going to the gym.-- (Participant 3 and 4)

Awakening patients’ self-management awareness is a gradual process that requires guidance from the medical team and the patients’ own efforts. During hospitalization, therapists can create additional opportunities for hands-on practice by increasing the frequency of ostomy pouch replacements. This approach not only helps patients become familiar with the operational procedures but also gradually enhances their confidence and independence.

Before discharge, during the last one or two pouch replacements, patients were encouraged to apply the pouch themselves and manage tasks such as emptying it. One week after discharge, when the patients visited the clinic to remove the pouch, they were advised to buy a mirror to clean and replace the pouch on their own. (Participant 3)

Subtheme 2: Continuous prevention-oriented supervision

During hospitalization, ETs increase the frequency of stoma pouch changes to provide patients with more hands-on practice. Furthermore, patient education includes detailed advice on preventing and managing complications such as PH, integrating preventive concepts into the context of patients’ daily activities.

During educational sessions in the hospital, patients are informed that if they plan to be active for a long time, it is better to wear slightly tighter clothing and, ideally, a non-vented abdominal belt, as this can help prevent PHs. (Participant 7)

During clinic visits, the ET provides contextual prompts and corrects improper techniques based on individual patients’ potential risk points. Through subsequent real-time monitoring, the therapist also helps identify potential issues, emphasizes their clinical significance, and reinforces the principle that “prevention is better than cure,” thereby strengthening patients’ self-management awareness.

When I was in the clinic, I met a patient with a PH. The bulge was quite noticeable… So, I told the patient to lie down, and based on the location of the bulge, I gently pushed it back into the abdomen manually, then applied an abdominal binder. This helped relieve the pain. After following this method and securing the binder, the patient said the pain was much better.” (Participant 8)

Theme 2: Patient empowerment and self-driven engagement

Subtheme 1: Active participation and behavioral internalization

Patients’ active participation is reflected not only in their proactive acquisition of disease-related knowledge but also in their conscious exploration of personal health management. Differences in individuals’ living environments, economic conditions, and educational levels lead to diversity in their health needs and behavioral patterns. Patients who pay close attention to health details demonstrate a proactive attitude toward managing their own health and a strong sense of responsibility. Comparatively, those who are younger, better educated, and in more favorable economic circumstances tend to show greater receptiveness and adherence to health education and nursing guidance. They are adept at absorbing and applying professional knowledge, integrating it into daily life practices, and continuously improving their self-management behaviors.

Subtheme 2: Self-awareness and continuous growth

In actual nursing practice, it can be observed that the awakening of patients’ self-management awareness is a gradual process that requires both professional guidance and the patients’ own effort. The ETs help patients build confidence, enabling them to gradually develop an understanding of self-management. As their comprehension deepens, patients begin to actively adjust their exercise methods and adhere to rehabilitation training, demonstrating a shift from passive acceptance to active participation. Furthermore, some younger patients not only ask targeted questions (such as how to prevent PH), but also report having obtained relevant information online, reflecting an enhanced awareness of health information acquisition and self-education. This phenomenon indicates that patients are no longer content with merely relying on medical personnel for guidance, but are proactively exploring health management approaches that best suit their own needs.

Theme 3: Digital connectivity and home-based support

Subtheme 1: Technology-assisted connectivity

With the ongoing advancement of medical informatization, applying digital technology in patient management has become increasingly widespread. Most large hospitals have begun to use digital tools to improve patient management’s efficiency. For example, hospitals publish text and video materials related to ostomy care through their official public WeChat accounts. In addition, some hospitals offer real-time communication channels between patients and ETs through dedicated WeChat groups, allowing patients to provide immediate feedback on postoperative issues or physical discomfort and receive timely professional responses. Such groups also foster a sense of community among patients for experience sharing and emotional support; this helps reduce feelings of isolation and enhance treatment adherence. Some large medical institutions have also assigned full-time research assistants to conduct continuous follow-up communication, further optimizing the long-term management chain between medical staff and patients. This helps achieve seamless integration from in-hospital treatment to out-of-hospital health maintenance.

Subtheme 2: Continuous home-oriented support

In economically developed regions, health authorities are promoting the digitalization and intelligent transformation of home medical services, creating a service loop centered on “online appointment-intelligent allocation-on-site service.” Patients scan a hospital or government platform QR code to enter a unified appointment system, which automatically matches them with nearby medical institutions based on location and coordinates professional healthcare personnel for home visits and treatment. The services include stoma assessment, wound dressing, rehabilitation guidance, and health monitoring. Regular fees (covering transportation, consultation, and medical consumables) are reasonable and transparent, generally ranging from 300 to 500 RMB per visit. Some cities have also developed electronic medical record sharing and mobile payment functions, enabling healthcare personnel to record data and collect fees on-site, thereby improving service efficiency and patient satisfaction.

Discussion

This study highlights that PH prevention and management remains underdeveloped and insufficiently prioritized. ETs play a vital role in educating patients, boosting their confidence, and providing psychosocial support. However, their potential is limited by resource constraints, heavy workloads, and gaps in specialized knowledge. As key stakeholders, patients often show low engagement owing to difficulties in acquiring skills, adapting psychologically, and accessing social support. This undermines their abilities to manage PHs effectively. Digital tools and online booking platforms promise to improve patient capabilities but require better content quality, reliability, and accessibility.

In this study, 40.9% (9/22) of ETs were exclusively engaged in stoma care. In China, the proportion of full-time ETs is low; most practice part-time, limiting dedicated time for ostomy care and hindering professional development [25]. Community nurses frequently lack ET-standard training, resulting in insufficient primary-level services [26]. Multidisciplinary collaboration often stops at information exchange and lacks mechanisms such as shared decision-making, joint clinics, and pathway-based management, thereby constraining team effectiveness [27]. Consistent with prior research, this study identified gaps in ET knowledge related to nutrition and exercise for PH prevention and management; other evidence likewise shows that 79.8% of ETs require additional education in this area [28]. Although existing guidelines offer dietary and physical activity recommendations for patients undergoing ostomy surgery, the content remains fragmented and insufficiently systematized, hindering timely and consistent implementation in routine care [9, 11]. Given the limited and predominantly part-time ostomy nursing workforce, there is an urgent need to build capacity through structured continuing professional development and multidisciplinary collaboration, supported by digital technologies such as virtual reality [29, 30]. A scoping review [31] indicates that simulation-based approaches are most commonly used in advanced practice nursing education; however, reliance on a single modality is inadequate to meet complex competency goals, and instructional strategies should be integrated according to learning objectives and clinical context. In response to rising demand for wound, ostomy and continence specialists, the Canadian Nurses Specialized in Wound, Ostomy and Continence, in partnership with a research-intensive university, implemented a competency-based, standards-driven collaborative academic progression program, a 12-month, stepwise online curriculum that integrates authentic clinical scenarios with advanced coursework, enabling a practice-reflection-practice cycle and supporting the transition from experienced to expert practice [32]. Thus, to implement competency-based training programs for ETs that standardize risk assessment, strategies and early follow-up to reduce PH risk and improve management is needed to develop across care levels, which include hospitals and community care services.

Against the backdrop of shortened hospital stays, our study indicates that patients struggle to fully assimilate ostomy care information delivered during admission, while expressing substantial demand for continuity of guidance from ETs. Drawing on the Transtheoretical Model of behavior change [33], most inpatients appear to occupy early stages, with unstable knowledge and skills; without staged post-discharge support, progression to action and maintenance is unlikely. Health education should therefore be conceptualized as a dynamic, life-course process that matches intervention content and intensity to patients’ stage, and is delivered through a partnership between patients and educators to sustain motivation and consolidate behavior [34]. Concurrently, patients face physical, psychological, and social challenges in preventing and managing PH and often report considerable uncertainty upon returning home; ETs highlighted unmet needs for social support and continuity of care, particularly among older adults who require targeted psychological support for emotion regulation and stress management and who, owing in part to limited digital literacy, experience greater difficulty accessing reliable information. Consistent with prior research [35], psychological distress is pronounced within the first three months postoperatively, with reported rates exceeding 88.6%, underscoring this window as critical for psychosocial intervention. In addition, the paucity of specific physical activity guidance (types, frequency, intensity) observed in this study may constrain recovery, aligning with evidence that 62.5% of patients undertook no vigorous activity and 42.0% no moderate activity one week after surgery [36]. Informed by self-efficacy theory [37], these gaps likely reflect low confidence that limits both action and help-seeking, exacerbated by the absence of attainable stepwise goals, sufficiently specific professional advice and timely feedback; accordingly, we recommend that stoma therapy teams provide clear, staged and actionable exercise prescriptions with prompt feedback, coupled with encouragement, peer support, and role-modeling, to strengthen self-efficacy and improve outcomes.

With the rise of information technology, digital tools such as AI, wearables, mobile devices, and online platforms can enhance patients’ knowledge and improve self-management behaviors [38]. However, relevant research remains limited, and the small screens of mobile devices are not well suited for complex tasks. This study found that many patients face significant challenges when accessing ostomy care-related websites. Although some government or nonprofit websites provide high-quality content, they are updated infrequently and lack any design tailored to specific patient needs [39]. Research has shown [40] that nonprofit ostomy patient support programs offered by companies can serve as a complementary form of ostomy care services; these programs provide personalized support throughout the full cycle of ostomy care, addressing patients’ product needs and selection, and connecting them with various support organizations, including nursing services and access to health education resources. Going forward, stronger digital health strategies should be deployed within healthcare systems, while addressing gaps such as insufficient digital interventions for healthcare professionals and inadequate patient-led data reporting mechanisms [30]. In addition, under varying policies and administrative contexts across countries and regions, efforts should be strengthened in data validation, long-term follow-up, and close integration with real-world application scenarios to develop digital health strategies that are more inclusive, locally adapted, and effective.

Strengths and limitations

This study included a broad, representative range of participants. These experts not only possess solid clinical experience and extensive teaching backgrounds but are also at the forefront of stoma-related PH management, holding high academic standing and influence in their field. Their perspectives and insights into stoma care can guide future directions in ostomy management. This study, however, has some limitations. Since it was conducted in China, transferability to other cultural or health‑system contexts should be comprehensive considered. Recruitment primarily from tertiary hospitals introduces institutional selection bias that may overestimate feasibility in high-resource settings and under-represent challenges in secondary or community care. Participants were predominantly experienced ETs, with many having more than 15 years’ experience and professional titles at intermediate or higher levels; while this provides strategic insight, it may under-represent early-career perspectives and support needs. Social desirability bias is possible given participants’ professional standing. Future studies should consider including ETs at different career stages to provide a more balanced understanding of professional development needs.

Conclusion

Patients with ostomies face multifaceted challenges in effective PH prevention and management, including insufficient knowledge and skills, psychological distress, lack of social support, and difficulty accessing reliable information. ETs exhibit gaps in nutritional management and abdominal muscle rehabilitation protocols, and many hospitals lack standardized, feasible health management pathways spanning the preoperative period, inpatient rehabilitation, and home-based recovery. Moreover, resources in economically underdeveloped regions are insufficient to meet patient needs. Going forward, the most impossible actionable change is to implement competency-based training programs for ETs across care levels to reduce PH risk and improve management. Then, it is essential to advance digital innovations, strengthen ET training, encourage patient-reported outcomes, enhance international collaboration to share experience and resources, and disseminate evidence-based, easily implementable PH prevention and management strategies.
