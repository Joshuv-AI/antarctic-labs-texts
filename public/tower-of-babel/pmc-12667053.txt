Pyrethroid susceptibility and resistance patterns in Anopheles gambiae populations from Rwanda
Authors: Dunia Munyakanage, Lambert Nzungize, Torbert Migambi, Pascal Mosengo, Gerald Habimana, Aimable Mbituyumuremyi, Alphonse Mutabazi, Emmanuel Hakizimana
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEvidence-based vector control interventions depend on understanding the distribution and mechanisms of insecticide resistance in malaria vectors. This study assessed the status of insecticide resistance in Anopheles gambiae sensu lato (s.l.) to pyrethroids, permethrin, deltamethrin, and alphacypermethrin across 18 sites in 15 districts of Rwanda, representing key malaria transmission strata.MethodsLarvae were collected from October 2022 to April 2023 using the dipping method, reared to adulthood, and tested using WHO standard bioassays. Susceptibility profiles were analysed with chi-square tests to compare mortality rates.ResultsThere was a high resistance to permethrin in various sites, such as Gashora (42%), Mubuga (48%), Rwaza (69%), and Kirarambogo (70%). Pre-exposure to piperonyl butoxide (PBO) only partially restored susceptibility, with mortality between 70 and 80% at resistant sites. In Kigali city, mortality with alphacypermethrin was 94%, and with PBO, it failed to restore susceptibility, suggesting non-metabolic resistance was involved. Overall, the evidence indicates that metabolic mechanisms play a major role in pyrethroid resistance, though target-site mutations, such as kdr, cannot be ruled out. The reliance on phenotypic data without molecular confirmation remains a limitation, underscoring the need for molecular assays to identify resistance-associated genetic markers.ConclusionsThis study provides essential baseline data on the resistance status of An. gambiae s.l. in Rwanda and highlights the urgent need to integrate molecular tools into routine surveillance to guide more effective vector control strategies.

Background

Malaria remains a significant public health and socioeconomic burden in Rwanda despite remarkable progress over the past decade. Between 2018 and 2023, malaria fatality rates declined from 2.72 to 0.26 deaths per 100,000 people, while incidence decreased sharply from 345 to 40 cases per 1000 population. Among children under five, incidence dropped from 331 to 52 per 1000, and mortality from 5 to 0.45 per 100,000 during the same period [1]. The main vector responsible for the spread of malaria in Rwanda is Anopheles gambiae, particularly in low altitude areas such as the Eastern province, Southern province [2]. Entomological surveillance conducted in Eastern Rwanda between 2017 and 2020 demonstrated that An. gambiae exhibited sporozoite rates 28 times higher and a human blood index four times higher than Anopheles arabiensis, underscoring its disproportionate contribution to malaria transmission [3]. While Anopheles funestus and other species also contribute to transmission, the dominance and efficiency of An. gambiae sensu lato (s.l.) makes it the principal target for national vector control strategies.

Rwanda has relied heavily on two core interventions: indoor residual spraying (IRS) and long-lasting insecticidal nets (LLINs). IRS was first introduced in Kigali in 2007 and later expanded to high-burden districts, while LLINs have been scaled nationwide [4]. Both interventions rely predominantly on insecticides, particularly pyrethroids, which remain the backbone of malaria vector control globally. Several mechanisms cause insecticide resistance, including insecticide detoxification, referred to as metabolic resistance, reduced target-site sensitivity, cuticular resistance, and behavioural resistance [5, 6]. A primary cause of pyrethroid resistance is the decreased sensitivity of the voltage-gated sodium channel (VGSC), the target site. Knockdown resistance (kdr) refers to the group of mutations in the Vgsc gene that result in amino acid substitutions and confer pyrethroid resistance [7, 8]. Other mechanisms of pyrethroid resistance are associated with overexpression of detoxifying enzymes such as cytochrome P450 monooxygenases, glutathione-S-transferases (GSTs), and carboxylesterases, which metabolize or sequester insecticides before they reach their target sites [9, 10]. Yet, resistance to pyrethroids and other insecticide classes has become a major threat. Nationwide surveys conducted in 2011 and 2013 revealed widespread resistance to DDT, lambda-cyhalothrin, permethrin, and deltamethrin in An. gambiae s.l., driven by both metabolic mechanisms and target-site mutations such as knockdown resistance (kdr) in the voltage-gated sodium channel gene [2]. Although these findings highlighted the early warning signs of resistance, more recent data on the extent, distribution, and underlying mechanisms of insecticide resistance in Rwanda remain limited. This knowledge gap hampers evidence-based decision-making and risks undermining malaria control gains.

Given the high transmission potential, ecological adaptability, and emerging resistance of An. gambiae s.l., this species must remain central to Rwanda’s vector control priorities. Importantly, while operational challenges such as insecticide resistance are increasingly documented, there is little understanding of vector–parasite interactions in Rwandan An. gambiae populations and of the molecular pathways driving resistance. Addressing these gaps is critical to refining vector control strategies, anticipating resistance dynamics, and sustaining Rwanda’s trajectory toward malaria elimination.

Methods

Study sites and mosquito collection

From October 2022 to April 2023, Anopheles larvae were collected across Rwanda in 15 Districts from four provinces and Kigali city (Fig. 1), such as Western province (Karongi, Nyamasheke), Eastern province (Bugesera, Nyagatare, Rwamagana, Kirehe, Kayonza, Ngoma), Northern province (Burera, Rulindo, Musanze, Gicumbi), Southern province (Ruhango, Nyamagabe), and Kigali City (Kicukiro). The collections targeted multiple ecological zones and aquatic habitats, including puddles, maize swamps, rice paddies, and temporary pools, using 350 ml standard dippers. Larvae were transported to the Rwanda Biomedical Center insectary for rearing to adulthood before WHO susceptibility tests. Adults were identified morphologically as An. gambiae complex and the An. funestus group [11]. Study sites

From October 2022 to April 2023, Anopheles larvae were collected across Rwanda in 15 Districts from four provinces and Kigali city (Fig. 1), such as Western province (Karongi, Nyamasheke), Eastern province (Bugesera, Nyagatare, Rwamagana, Kirehe, Kayonza, Ngoma), Northern province (Burera, Rulindo, Musanze, Gicumbi), Southern province (Ruhango, Nyamagabe), and Kigali City (Kicukiro). The study sites represent regions of varying malaria transmission intensity across Rwanda. Sites were selected based on three main criteria, like high malaria burden documented through national surveillance data, particularly in low-altitude areas of the Eastern and Southern provinces, where transmission is perennial; historical reports of insecticide resistance in An. gambiae populations; and ecological diversity, including differences in altitude, rainfall, and agricultural insecticide use that may contribute to local selection pressures. These areas are recognized hotspots for malaria transmission, with parasite prevalence rates ranging between 5.9% to 14% in children under five and seasonal entomological inoculation rates among the highest in the country. The prevalence of malaria parasitaemia among preschool children is reported at 5.9% in rural settings [12].

Environmental characteristics

The study sites are characterized by a bimodal rainfall pattern, with long rains from March to May and short rains from October to November. Annual rainfall averages approximately 1000 mm annually in most regions [13], supporting extensive mosquito breeding habitats, particularly rice paddies and other irrigated agricultural zones [14–16]. Average daily temperatures range between 20 and 26 °C, with relative humidity typically around 70–80%, conditions highly favourable for both mosquito proliferation and malaria transmission [17].

Mosquito species composition

Entomological surveillance data and previous studies indicate that An. gambiae s.l., particularly An. gambiae sensu stricto (s.s.) and An. arabiensis are the dominant malaria vectors in these regions, contributing most of the transmission events [2]. Anopheles funestus is also present in some sites, though at lower densities and with more focal distribution. While An. funestus was collected during the present study, their numbers were insufficient for statistically robust insecticide susceptibility testing; therefore, resistance assays focused on An. gambiae populations.

Insecticide susceptibility assays

Susceptibility tests were performed on adult female An. gambiae s.l. mosquitoes following WHO insecticide susceptibility tube-test protocols using standard diagnostic doses [18]. Insecticide-impregnated filter papers included permethrin (0.75%), deltamethrin (0.05%), and alphacypermethrin (0.05%). Control tests were performed using filter papers treated with silicone oil. For each insecticide, approximately 150 non-blood-fed female mosquitoes, aged 2–4 days, were tested. Four replicates of 20–25 mosquitoes were exposed per insecticide, alongside two control tubes without insecticide.

Knockdown was recorded at 15, 30, and 60 min post-exposure. After 60 min, mosquitoes were transferred to clean holding tubes, supplied with 10% sugar solution, and maintained under controlled insectary conditions (20–26 °C, 80% relative humidity). Mortality was scored 24 h later, and results were interpreted according to WHO criteria. Following the assays, all mosquitoes were individually preserved in 1.5 ml Eppendorf tubes for downstream molecular analysis, including species identification and genotyping of resistance-associated mutations.

Piperonyl butoxide synergist assays

The adult mosquitoes were pre-exposed for one hour to piperonyl butoxide (4% PBO), and then exposed to either alphacypermethrin, permethrin, or deltamethrin for another hour. The mortality rate was assessed after 24 h and compared with results obtained without pre-exposure to PBO to determine any possible involvement of cytochrome P450-mediated resistance.

Data analysis

The mortality rates of the exposed mosquitoes and silicone oil as control samples were calculated for each insecticide used after bioassay. To determine susceptibility status, the percentage mortality was interpreted using WHO guidelines (1) as follows: if the mortality rate is less than 90%, resistance is confirmed; a mortality rate between 90 and 97% indicates possible resistance; and a mortality rate of 98% or higher indicates susceptibility (2). A Chi-Square test was performed to compare the mortality results from insecticide exposure alone, such as alphacypermethrin, deltamethrin, and permethrin, with those from exposure to insecticides combined with PBO. The level of significance was set at P < 0.05. Customized figures were generated using R packages including ggpubr, dplyr, forcats, and ggplot2. Results were presented based on standard errors (± SE), and p-values were calculated from chi-square tests. To calculate the 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for KD30 and KD60, we used the sample mean, standard deviation (SD), and sample size, then determined the critical value from the t-distribution corresponding to 95% confidence. These bounds indicate the range within which the true knockdown rate is likely to fall with 95% confidence.

Discussion

The findings indicate a high prevalence of pyrethroid resistance in An. gambiae s.l. populations throughout Rwanda, with particular focus on the Western, Eastern, Northern, and Southern provinces, as well as the City of Kigali. This aligns with broader trends observed in other African countries where malaria vectors are increasingly resistant to insecticides [19, 20]. The primary mechanism driving this resistance appears to be metabolic resistance, often involving the overexpression of cytochrome P450 genes. This is supported by multiple observations where the use of PBO, a synergist that inhibits oxidase enzymes, restored insecticide susceptibility [21, 22]. For instance, in the Eastern province, mosquitoes from Mimuli, Mwogo, and Mareba showed resistance to permethrin, alphacypermethrin, and deltamethrin, but subsequent exposure to PBO resulted in a 100% mortality rate, confirming the role of metabolic resistance (Fig. 3). This finding is consistent with transcriptomic data from Tanzania, which identified specific P450 genes, including CYP9K1, CYP6M2, CYP6P4, CYP6AA1, CYP6Z3, and CYP6P3 associated with permethrin resistance [23]. In the Northern province, An. gambiae from Rwesero exhibited high resistance to pyrethroids, but became fully susceptible after exposure to PBO, indicating that metabolic resistance is a key factor (Fig. 4). Similarly, in the Southern province, mosquitoes from Karambi showed resistance to permethrin and alphacypermethrin, but PBO exposure fully restored susceptibility (Fig. 5), suggesting a complete involvement of metabolic resistance. This metabolic resistance is also noted in neighbouring countries like the Democratic Republic of Congo [24] and Cameroon, where studies have identified similar P450 genes, such as CYP6P3, CYP6M2, CYP9K1, CYP6P4, and CYP6Z1 associated with pyrethroid resistance [25, 26].

While metabolic resistance appears to be the dominant mechanism, the data also point to the involvement of other factors. The high frequency of the kdr mutation, which is known to cause target-site resistance, may contribute to cross-resistance between permethrin and DDT, as seen in An. gambiae populations in Kenya [27]. Additionally, in the City of Kigali, mosquitoes from Kicukiro showed resistance to alphacypermethrin, and even after PBO exposure, susceptibility was not fully restored (Fig. 6), suggesting the involvement of a different, non-metabolic resistance mechanism.

Rwanda's use of insecticides for both crop and public health purposes may be a significant driver of this resistance. The widespread use of agrochemicals belonging to similar chemical classes as those used in public health campaigns could be forcing mosquitoes to develop metabolic resistance mechanisms [28]. The observed pyrethroid resistance in Rwanda (Fig. 7) is also consistent with findings in nearby countries such as Uganda [29], Tanzania [30], and Namibia [31], which also show widespread pyrethroid resistance in An. gambiae populations [29, 30]. The findings indicate some regional variations in resistance levels. Conclusion

The evidence suggests that metabolic resistance, primarily mediated by cytochrome P450 enzymes, is the major mechanism driving pyrethroid resistance in Anopheles populations across multiple districts in Rwanda, as supported by the ability of PBO to restore susceptibility. Nonetheless, the presence of other mechanisms, such as target-site mutations like kdr, cannot be excluded, particularly given findings in the Kigali district. While synergist assays indicate the contribution of metabolic pathways, these inferences are based on phenotypic data alone, and molecular confirmation is needed to validate specific pathways and identify resistance-associated genetic markers. The absence of molecular assays represents a key limitation, highlighting the need for further research to fully elucidate the complex resistance landscape. Despite these constraints, the study provides valuable baseline data on the resistance status of An. gambiae s.l. in Rwanda and emphasizes the importance of integrating molecular tools into routine resistance surveillance to guide effective vector control strategies.

Study limitation

The major limitations of this study are the lack of bioassay results of insecticides after PBO pre-exposure in some study sites, and not maintain larvae collection over time to evaluate insecticide resistance in the same study sites. These important studies will be the subject of future work.
