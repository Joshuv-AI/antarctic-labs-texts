Breastfeeding during pregnancy and tandem breastfeeding: knowledge and opinions of neonatal intensive care nurses in Turkey
Authors: Dilek Konukbay, Yaren Ugurlu, Emine Kapan, Dilek Yildiz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBreastfeeding is a unique and irreplaceable means of providing optimal nutrition for an infant. Breastfeeding during pregnancy and tandem breastfeeding are among the important factors that influence mothers’ breastfeeding behaviors. Neonatal intensive care nurses play a key role in providing education and counseling to mothers, and their knowledge in this field is essential for supporting appropriate breastfeeding practices and improving maternal and infant outcomes. This study aimed to determine the knowledge and opinions of neonatal intensive care nurses regarding breastfeeding during pregnancy and tandem breastfeeding.MethodsThis descriptive, cross-sectional study was conducted with 290 neonatal intensive care nurses in Turkey between December 2021 and February 2022 using an online questionnaire and a convenience sampling method. Data were collected using a 31-item questionnaire developed based on the literature and expert opinion and pilot-tested for clarity. The questionnaire included items on sociodemographic characteristics, knowledge, and opinions regarding breastfeeding during pregnancy and tandem breastfeeding.ResultsAmong the neonatal intensive care nurses, 53.7% were aged 25–39 years, 75.2% held a bachelor’s degree, and 39.3% had five or more years of Neonatal Intensive Care Unit experience. A total of 76.6% reported that breastfeeding during pregnancy should be continued, and 75.5% stated that mothers should be supported during this process. A total of 55.9% reported knowing the concept of tandem breastfeeding, and 66.2% stated that a woman could practice tandem breastfeeding postpartum. Additionally, 44.8% reported that tandem breastfeeding would not negatively affect the growth and development of the neonate, while 50.7% reported that it would not affect the older child. Significant associations were found between nurses’ knowledge and opinions and factors such as age (p = 0.013), marital status (p = 0.004), parental status (p = 0.002), number of children (p = 0.046), and professional experience (p = 0.001).ConclusionsThe study found that most neonatal intensive care nurses held positive opinions regarding breastfeeding during pregnancy and tandem breastfeeding; however, their knowledge regarding tandem breastfeeding appeared to be limited, as a considerable proportion reported not knowing the concept. These findings highlight the need for targeted educational programs and the development of evidence-based guidelines to support nurses in providing effective counseling to mothers.

Background

Breastfeeding is a unique and irreplaceable means of providing optimal nutrition for infants [1, 2]. What makes breastfeeding so valuable is that breast milk is personalized for the infant by varying according to the infant’s age and health status [1, 3]. The World Health Organization (WHO) recommends exclusive breastfeeding for the first six months, continuation of breastfeeding up to two years of age or beyond, and maintenance of breastfeeding alongside safe complementary foods thereafter [4].

Many factors influence mothers’ breastfeeding behaviors, including maternal age, gestational week, level of education, occupation, timing of return to work, receiving prenatal breastfeeding education, desire to breastfeed, previous breastfeeding experience, knowledge about breastfeeding and breast milk, mode of delivery, postpartum mood and psychosocial stress, sociocultural differences, and the family’s socioeconomic status [5–8]. In addition, breastfeeding during pregnancy and tandem breastfeeding are among the important conditions that influence mothers’ breastfeeding behaviors [9, 10].

Although the exact prevalence of breastfeeding during pregnancy and tandem breastfeeding is not well established, existing evidence suggests that these practices occur in various populations but remain insufficiently documented, making it difficult to determine their true frequency. Breastfeeding during pregnancy may occur at different stages of gestation and is often continued for a variable duration after pregnancy is recognized. Studies from Turkey report that breastfeeding during pregnancy ranges between 5% and 15%, particularly in regions with high fertility rates [1, 11, 12]. Globally, rates are generally between 5% and 10%. In a meta-analysis by Sinan and Kurnaz (2025), 25.3% of 2,617 women in Egypt reported breastfeeding during pregnancy, whereas the rate was 5% among 341 women in the United States [13]. In Jordan, 8.9% of women continued breastfeeding during pregnancy, with higher rates among those with younger children, particularly under 12 months [14]. Tandem breastfeeding appears to be less common, with prevalence rates generally reported below 5% both globally and in Turkey [1, 11, 12].

Evidence regarding the duration and patterns of breastfeeding during pregnancy remains limited; however, available studies provide important insights. In a study conducted in Turkey (n = 267), 58.8% of women reported becoming pregnant while breastfeeding, and 84.7% continued breastfeeding for 1 to 20 weeks after learning about their pregnancy [1]. Another study conducted in Turkey (n = 101) found that a small proportion of mothers (5%) continued breastfeeding into the later stages of pregnancy, particularly between the 7th and 9th months [11]. Regarding tandem breastfeeding, evidence on its duration is limited; a qualitative study conducted in Spain (n = 15) reported a duration ranging from 2 months to 3.7 years [15].

Despite the limited evidence, these practices have been suggested as potential approaches to support breastfeeding continuity and may contribute to reducing morbidity-related mortality among infants born with short birth intervals [16, 17]. Therefore, understanding their prevalence and duration, and promoting these practices, particularly in high-fertility settings, may be important for improving overall breastfeeding rates [1].

Studies examining breastfeeding during pregnancy indicate that when breastfeeding and pregnancy occur simultaneously, mothers often discontinue breastfeeding due to lack of knowledge, social stigma, and unsolicited recommendations and criticisms from society that are not based on evidence [18, 19]. In a study conducted by In and Koruk (2024) with 267 postpartum women in Turkey, only 15.7% of women reported continuing breastfeeding during pregnancy [1]. The literature highlights that mothers experience confusion regarding whether breastfeeding might harm the ongoing pregnancy, whether breast milk content, color, or quantity changes, whether breastfeeding during pregnancy is appropriate, and whether it is acceptable to breastfeed both the younger and older child postpartum [12, 18–20].

Mothers who continue breastfeeding while pregnant may also need to breastfeed two or more children of different ages simultaneously after childbirth. This feeding practice is referred to as tandem breastfeeding. Tandem breastfeeding is defined as breastfeeding two or more children (not twins or multiples) at the same time [1, 19, 21]. There is no universally accepted standard regarding the age gap between children involved in tandem breastfeeding. Tandem breastfeeding refers to breastfeeding children who are not from the same pregnancy and implies a minimum age difference of approximately 10–12 months. However, in practice, this interval may vary from infancy to early childhood depending on the duration of breastfeeding [22, 23]. Although the number of studies on this topic is limited, research reports both positive and negative effects of breastfeeding during pregnancy and tandem breastfeeding. Positive effects include prolonged breastfeeding duration for the older child, increased milk production due to the frequency and duration of breastfeeding, and increased protein, fat, and energy content of breast milk [1, 21]. Negative effects reported include perceptions of insufficient milk for the younger child, perceived reduced milk production during pregnancy, and perceived changes in the taste of breast milk. However, there is no evidence indicating that breastfeeding during pregnancy or tandem breastfeeding has adverse health effects on the infant or fetus [1, 21, 24].

Breastfeeding during pregnancy and tandem breastfeeding may also have important implications for maternal health and well-being. Evidence indicates that, in healthy pregnancies, breastfeeding during pregnancy and tandem breastfeeding do not result in significant adverse outcomes for the mother or fetus; however, in cases of inadequate nutrition, short interpregnancy intervals, or high-risk pregnancies, maternal nutritional status may be compromised and the risk of certain complications may increase [2, 12, 13]. In addition, mothers practicing tandem breastfeeding have reported physical challenges such as breast-related problems, fatigue, and sleep disturbances, as well as psychosocial difficulties including sibling jealousy, social pressure, and lack of support [12, 25]. On the other hand, long-term breastfeeding has been associated with protective effects against conditions such as diabetes, hypertension, and certain types of cancer [26]. Overall, the existing literature suggests that tandem breastfeeding is generally safe, although careful monitoring of maternal nutritional status is recommended [13, 21]. Therefore, understanding the potential effects of these practices is essential for providing appropriate guidance to mothers.

Neonatal intensive care nurses occupy a critical position due to their continuous interaction with mothers during the care of preterm and high-risk newborns, as well as their active role in initiating, maintaining, and managing the breastfeeding process. Mothers of infants admitted to the Neonatal Intensive Care Unit (NICU) often require increased breastfeeding support due to factors such as separation, stress, delayed lactation, and breastfeeding difficulties [27]. In this context, nurses serve as primary healthcare professionals providing counseling on milk expression, maintenance of lactation, and promotion of breast milk feeding [28]. Therefore, neonatal intensive care nurses represent one of the main professional groups to whom mothers turn for guidance in complex situations such as breastfeeding during pregnancy and tandem breastfeeding. A review of the literature revealed no studies investigating the knowledge and opinions of neonatal intensive care nurses regarding breastfeeding during pregnancy and tandem breastfeeding. Therefore, this study is pioneering in this field and was conducted to determine the knowledge and opinions of neonatal intensive care nurses working in the NICU regarding breastfeeding during pregnancy and tandem breastfeeding.

Methods

Design

The study employed a descriptive and cross-sectional design. An online survey was used as the data collection method. This design is appropriate for examining prevalence of knowledge/opinions and exploring associations between variables at a single time point.

Ethics declarations

Ethical approval

for the study was obtained from the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of an educational and research hospital in Turkey, with the reference number 2021/113. Electronic informed consent was obtained online from all participating nurses. All participants provided their consent to participate in the study.

Participants

The eligible population of the study consisted of neonatal intensive care nurses working in Turkey. However, the total number of this population could not be clearly determined from available sources. Therefore, the population was considered unknown, and the sample size was calculated accordingly. The sample size was determined based on the planned analyses of the study hypotheses, which involved examining the relationships between categorical variables. Sample size estimation was performed using the G*Power 3.1.9.2 software at a 95% confidence level. As no previous study with a similar design was available in this field, a standardized effect size of 0.30 (medium), as recommended by Cohen (1988) for analyses of relationships between categorical variables, was adopted [18]. With a significance level of α = 0.05 and a statistical power of 0.95, the minimum required sample size was calculated as 172.

All neonatal intensive care nurses who voluntarily agreed to participate in the study were included. No restrictions were applied regarding duration of work experience. All participants were registered nurses working in neonatal intensive care units. Nurses who did not provide informed consent were excluded from the study. Participants were recruited using a convenience sampling method through online platforms and social media channels. The questionnaire was created using Google Forms and distributed via social media platforms.

Completion of all items was mandatory, preventing submission of incomplete responses; thus, there were no missing questionnaires. The study was ultimately conducted with the participation of 290 neonatal intensive care nurses.

Measurement

The questionnaire employed in this study was developed by the researchers based on their clinical experience and a comprehensive review of the relevant literature on breastfeeding during pregnancy and tandem breastfeeding [18–21]. An initial pool of items was generated to assess neonatal intensive care nurses’ knowledge and opinions regarding these topics. The draft questionnaire was reviewed by an expert panel consisting of four pediatric nurses with experience in neonatal care to obtain feedback on the clarity, relevance, and content of the items. Based on the experts’ feedback, revisions were made to improve the wording and comprehensibility of the questions.

Subsequently, a pilot study (n = 15) was conducted to evaluate the clarity and comprehensibility of the questionnaire items. The data obtained from the pilot study were not included in the main study. Following the pilot testing, minor modifications were made to improve the phrasing and formatting of the questions. The questionnaire consisted of closed-ended items with predefined response options. Most questions were structured using two- or three-category response formats (e.g., yes/no or yes/no/not sure), and some items allowed multiple responses. Knowledge-related items were assessed based on participants’ responses to these options, and no composite scoring system was used. The final version of the questionnaire consisted of 31 items, including 8 questions on sociodemographic characteristics (gender, age, marital status, having children, number of children, educational status, years of experience in the nursing profession, and years of experience in the neonatal intensive care unit), 10 questions regarding breastfeeding during pregnancy, and 13 questions on tandem breastfeeding.

Data collection

Data were collected between December 2021 and February 2022. An anonymous online survey created using Google Forms was distributed to neonatal intensive care nurses working in NICUs in Turkey. The survey link was disseminated through the researchers’ social media accounts (WhatsApp, Instagram, Facebook, etc.). An online information sheet explaining the purpose of the study, assuring participants that their personal information would be kept confidential, and indicating that no identifying questions were included was shared with the nurses. Nurses who accessed the online link and approved the informed consent form were able to proceed to the survey questions. Research data were stored on a password-protected computer. A total of 290 volunteer neonatal intensive care nurses participated in the study.

Data analysis

Descriptive statistics (frequency and percentage) were initially presented. For the analysis of relationships between categorical variables, the Pearson chi-square test was used when the sample size assumption (n > 5) was met; otherwise, Fisher’s exact test was applied. Analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics 25. The statistical significance level was set at p < 0.05.

Dıscussıon

Neonatal intensive care nurses, who play an active role during the breastfeeding period, have a crucial role in informing society and providing support and counseling to mothers, particularly in complex situations such as breastfeeding during pregnancy and tandem breastfeeding [21]. In the present study, neonatal intensive care nurses were found to have generally positive opinions regarding breastfeeding during pregnancy and tandem breastfeeding. Most nurses reported that mothers should continue breastfeeding during pregnancy (76.6%), could practice tandem breastfeeding (66.2%), and that breastfeeding during pregnancy (75.5%) and tandem breastfeeding (76.9%) should be supported. However, despite these positive opinions, only 55.9% of the participating nurses reported knowing the concept of tandem breastfeeding. Having adequate knowledge of evidence-based breastfeeding practices is considered a professional requirement for neonatal intensive care nurses, and this competency is emphasized in international guidelines [4, 17]. Nevertheless, the absence of a standardized criterion for evaluating knowledge of tandem breastfeeding suggests that the reported level of knowledge may not reflect sufficient competency and may lead to limitations in clinical practice. In this context, it is essential that healthcare professionals involved in pregnancy follow-up and breastfeeding support provide scientific, evidence-based guidance and counseling to mothers regarding breastfeeding during pregnancy and tandem breastfeeding.

Despite the generally positive opinions identified in this study, the limited level of knowledge regarding tandem breastfeeding among nurses is noteworthy. Current evidence indicates that breastfeeding during pregnancy does not significantly increase the risk of adverse outcomes such as miscarriage, preterm birth, preeclampsia, or bleeding [13, 29]. Similarly, a study conducted among Turkish women reported no significant differences in pregnancy complications between those who continued breastfeeding and those who did not [30]. Although breastfeeding during pregnancy and tandem breastfeeding have been subjects of ongoing debate due to their potential benefits and perceived risks, the growing body of evidence suggests that these practices can be safe when maternal nutritional status is adequate and the pregnancy is not high-risk [1, 15, 16, 31]. WHO recommends exclusive breastfeeding for the first six months of life, followed by continued breastfeeding up to two years and beyond, alongside appropriate complementary feeding. In this context, continued breastfeeding during a subsequent pregnancy or tandem breastfeeding after the birth of a new infant may occur and can be considered part of the natural continuation of breastfeeding practices [32]. These practices may support breastfeeding continuity and could have positive implications for child health outcomes, particularly in settings with short birth intervals, especially in low- and middle-income countries. Accordingly, understanding the prevalence of these practices and promoting appropriate, evidence-based support—especially in regions with high fertility rates—may be important for improving overall breastfeeding practices [1].

The American Academy of Pediatrics’ current policy statement and technical report on “Breastfeeding and the Use of Human Milk” indicate that, in healthy and uncomplicated pregnancies, there is no evidence of direct adverse effects of breastfeeding on fetal growth or birth outcomes [33]. In line with the available evidence, a substantial proportion of neonatal intensive care nurses reported that breastfeeding during pregnancy would not cause complications in the ongoing pregnancy (43.1%). Similarly, nearly half of the nurses indicated that breastfeeding during pregnancy or tandem breastfeeding would not increase problems such as nipple cracking (45.5%), while a considerable proportion reported that tandem breastfeeding may contribute to increased milk production (43.4%). Consistent with these findings, Aker et al. (2024) reported in a qualitative study that mothers reported that tandem breastfeeding was effective in resolving breast refusal, facilitated milk secretion immediately after birth, and reduced breast-related problems [12]. However, given the limited and heterogeneous nature of the available evidence, these findings should be interpreted with caution, and further research is needed to provide more comprehensive and high-quality evidence.

Current evidence suggests that tandem breastfeeding does not have a negative effect on infant growth when maternal nutrition is adequate, and it is generally associated with neutral or positive outcomes in children’s physical growth, cognitive development, and neurodevelopmental outcomes [13, 24]. In line with this evidence, approximately half of the neonatal intensive care nurses in the present study reported that tandem breastfeeding would not adversely affect the growth and development of either the newborn (44.8%) or the older child (50.7%). At the same time, 26.2% of nurses reported that tandem breastfeeding may negatively affect the newborn, while 29.0% stated that they did not know; similarly, for the older child, 23.8% reported potential negative effects and 25.5% stated that they did not know. In another study of healthcare professionals in Turkey, Koruk et al. found that a higher proportion (87.5%) of participants supported the evidence-based perspective and reported no negative effects on the mother and 90.9% reported no negative effects on the newborn. In contrast, in Zengin et al.’s study of Turkish health professionals only 36.5% of their sample correctly stated that tandem breastfeeding does not have negative effects on the mother and the newborn [34, 35]. A review of the literature indicates that most studies in this area have been conducted with mothers, and studies involving healthcare professionals remain limited. In this context, the development of evidence-based guidelines on breastfeeding during pregnancy and tandem breastfeeding is essential to support healthcare professionals in providing accurate education and effective counseling.

When the factors influencing neonatal intensive care nurses’ knowledge and opinions regarding breastfeeding during pregnancy and tandem breastfeeding were examined, age, level of education, and duration of work in the nursing profession and NICU were found to be significantly associated with their knowledge and opinions. Receiving training on breastfeeding during pregnancy and/or tandem breastfeeding was also associated with better knowledge of the concept and more positive opinions among nurses. These findings suggest that both professional experience and targeted training may contribute to improved understanding of breastfeeding practices, particularly in more complex situations such as tandem breastfeeding. Increased age and longer professional experience may enhance familiarity with breastfeeding processes, while training on these topics may support evidence-based knowledge and clinical decision-making [1, 34, 35]. Although the available literature on this topic is limited, previous studies also found that although healthcare professionals were generally supportive and aware of these practices, their knowledge particularly regarding tandem breastfeeding remained limited [34, 35]. For example, Zengin et al. (2025) reported that a majority of healthcare professionals recommended both breastfeeding during pregnancy and tandem breastfeeding [34]. Likewise, Koruk et al. (2025) found that although healthcare professionals were generally aware of these practices, their knowledge—particularly regarding tandem breastfeeding—remained limited [35]. However, no significant differences were identified according to gender, professional group, age, or years of professional experience. These differences may be related to variations in sample characteristics, study settings, and measurement approaches.

Mothers practicing tandem breastfeeding have reported various challenges during the tandem breastfeeding process in the literature [12, 21, 36]. Erdoğan and Turan (2023) reported that mothers practicing tandem breastfeeding had a higher perception of insufficient milk compared with those who did not practice tandem breastfeeding [36]. Similarly, Aker et al. (2024) found that mothers experienced anxiety and concerns about the adequacy of colostrum and breast milk, and that increased nighttime breastfeeding was associated with sleep disturbances and fatigue [12]. In line with these findings, Sinkiewicz-Darol et al. (2021) reported that 55% of tandem breastfeeding mothers experienced fatigue and frequent nighttime awakenings [21]. Erdoğan and Turan (2023) also noted that 10.2% of mothers experienced difficulties, primarily related to fatigue caused by frequent nighttime awakenings and the inability to rest due to continuous breastfeeding [36]. In addition to these physical challenges, psychosocial factors such as sibling jealousy, social pressure, and lack of psychosocial support have also been reported to complicate the tandem breastfeeding experience [12, 25]. Consistent with the literature, neonatal intensive care nurses in the present study reported that women who breastfeed during pregnancy and/or practice tandem breastfeeding may experience fatigue due to prolonged breastfeeding (27.1%), difficulty finding personal time (24.5%), and concerns regarding whether breast milk would be sufficient for both children (26.7%). These findings are in line with previous studies which highlight that both physical and psychosocial challenges may affect the breastfeeding experience, much of which can be addressed with appropriate support.

Cultural and religious norms, concerns about maternal and fetal health, and inaccurate or incomplete information regarding breastfeeding during pregnancy and/or tandem breastfeeding may contribute to the development of negative opinions among both mothers and healthcare professionals [37]. In the present study, a notable proportion of nurses expressed such concerns: 22.1% reported that breastfeeding during pregnancy may cause complications, 23.4% stated that the child should not be breastfed during pregnancy, 33.8% reported that tandem breastfeeding could not be practiced after birth, and 21.0% indicated that tandem breastfeeding may cause health problems for the mother. However, despite these concerns, most nurses (89.7%) emphasized that the decision should be left to the mother after adequate information is provided. This finding highlights a generally supportive and autonomy-respecting approach among nurses, even in the presence of uncertainties or negative perceptions. The literature indicates that decisions related to breastfeeding during pregnancy and/or tandem breastfeeding are often influenced not only by maternal preference but also by environmental and familial factors [1, 18, 37]. For example, In and Koruk (2024) reported that more than half of the mothers (54.2%) identified family elders as their primary source of information regarding tandem breastfeeding, whereas only 8.3% received information from healthcare professionals [1]. Similarly, Çınar et al. (2022) found that 37% of mothers who discontinued breastfeeding during pregnancy did so based on advice from family members or neighbors, while only 26% made this decision independently [37]. Furthermore, Anitasari et al. (2019) reported that family elders played a decisive role in many family decisions, including those related to breastfeeding [18]. In line with these findings, the results of the present study suggest that neonatal intensive care nurses should adopt a holistic approach when providing education on breastfeeding during pregnancy and tandem breastfeeding, involving not only mothers but also spouses, family elders, and the broader social environment.

Studies indicate that healthcare professionals may be insufficient in providing effective counseling due to limited access to up-to-date scientific evidence, highlighting the need for evidence-based and up-to-date training on breastfeeding during pregnancy and tandem breastfeeding [17, 38, 39]. In the literature, Çınar et al. (2022) reported that nearly all mothers (95%) who learned they were pregnant during the breastfeeding period discontinued breastfeeding, and that this decision was largely influenced by recommendations from physicians, nurses, or midwives. Similarly, in a qualitative study, Aker et al. (2024) found that mothers practicing tandem breastfeeding reported receiving insufficient information and support from healthcare professionals and described the process as challenging. These findings underscore the critical importance of healthcare professionals having adequate knowledge and competencies regarding breastfeeding during pregnancy and tandem breastfeeding [12, 37]. Therefore, strengthening educational programs, developing evidence-based guidelines, and improving counseling services provided to mothers are essential steps to support the continuity of breastfeeding and promote maternal and child health.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, although the required sample size was achieved, participants were recruited through online platforms using a convenience sampling approach. Therefore, access to the survey and the total number of potential participants could not be accurately determined, as the survey was distributed via social media. Second, the study was conducted with neonatal intensive care nurses who voluntarily participated, which may lead to potential selection bias. Third, the questionnaire used in the study was developed by the researchers and was not externally validated. Fourth, closed-ended questions were used, limiting the in-depth understanding of participants’ views. Fifth, the total number of neonatal intensive care nurses in Turkey could not be clearly determined from available sources. Therefore, the generalizability of the findings may be limited. Lastly, it is important to note that the study was conducted within a specific context (Turkey) and focused on the perspectives of neonatal intensive care nurses. A strength of the study is that existing research on the topic is limited, and the findings of this study are expected to guide future research.

Conclusions

The study found that most neonatal intensive care nurses held positive opinions regarding breastfeeding during pregnancy and tandem breastfeeding; however, their knowledge regarding tandem breastfeeding appeared to be limited, as a considerable proportion reported not knowing the concept. Additionally, increases in nurses’ age and level of education, being married and having children, and longer work experience in the NICU and in the nursing profession were associated with more positive knowledge and opinions regarding tandem breastfeeding. These findings highlight the need for targeted educational programs and the development of evidence-based guidelines to support nurses in providing effective counseling to mothers. Future research should focus on developing and evaluating structured training programs and clinical guidelines, as well as exploring the effectiveness of educational interventions in improving nurses’ knowledge and counseling practices in this area.
