Effects of combined high-sensitivity C-reactive protein and residual cholesterol levels on new-onset diabetes: evidence from two prospective cohort studies
Authors: Yue Shao, Zhenghao Li, Qingchen Wu, Liu Ye, Haoming Shi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHigh-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hsCRP) and residual cholesterol (RC) have been pinpointed as potential diabetes risk factors. Nonetheless, the combined impact of these biomarkers on the incidence of diabetes is still unclear. The objective of this analysis is to explore the joint effects of RC and hsCRP on the incidence of new diabetes.MethodsWe analyzed 9,309 individuals in the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) and 4,743 participants in the English Longitudinal Study of Ageing (ELSA), all of whom had no history of diabetes at baseline. To examine the joint effect of RC and hsCRP on diabetes risk, individuals were sorted into four groups based on median RC levels and an hsCRP threshold of 1 mg/L. To comprehensively assess the effects of RC and hsCRP on diabetes, a Residual Cholesterol-Inflammation Index (RCII) was established. Cox proportional hazards regression analysis and mediation analysis were conducted.ResultsFollowing comprehensive adjustments, individuals with elevated hsCRP alone, elevated RC alone, and both elevated hsCRP and RC demonstrated progressively increased risks of diabetes relative to those with low levels of hsCRP and RC across two databases. Multivariate analyses demonstrated that subjects in the highest quartile of RCII experienced a 79% increased likelihood of diabetes in CHARLS and a 99% increase in ELSA, relative to those in the lowest quartile of RCII. In the relationship between RC and diabetes, hsCRP acts as a mediator, accounting for 4.8% of the effect in CHARLS and 5.1% in ELSA. Conversely, in the relationship between hsCRP and diabetes, RC mediates 12.7% of the impact in CHARLS and 10.5% in ELSA.ConclusionOur findings demonstrate the joint and mediating impacts of RC and hsCRP in diabetes risk assessment. Consequently, we recommend assessing both RC and hsCRP levels together and taking further steps to reduce the risk of diabetes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-025-07544-6.Keywords: CHARLS, Diabetes, ELSA, High-sensitivity c-reactive protein, Remnant cholesterol

Introduction

Type 2 diabetes is a complex chronic condition that markedly elevates the risk of cardiovascular disease (CVD), chronic kidney disease, and mortality [1–4]. An estimated 536.6 million adults, or 10.5% of the global population, were living with diabetes in 2021. This number is anticipated to grow to 783.2 million by 2045, representing 12.2% of the world’s population. The prevalence of diabetes increases progressively with age, reaching approximately 15%-24% among individuals aged 50–79 [5]. Notably, it is reported that around half of these individuals remained diagnosed [6]. Consequently, there is a critical need to transition from diabetes management to diabetes prevention.

Risk factors such as dyslipidemia contribute to the occurrence of diabetes [7, 8]. Dyslipidemia primarily promotes the development of diabetes through mechanisms involving inflammation, insulin resistance, and lipid accumulation [9–12]. Residual cholesterol (RC), a triglyceride-rich lipoprotein, represents a form of dyslipidemia that is not amenable to management with statins. It contains very low-density lipoprotein, intermediate-density lipoprotein, and chylomicron remnants [13]. Our prior study has suggested that long-term exposure to elevated RC levels was linked with a greater risk of diabetes, regardless of whether low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), total cholesterol (TC), and triglycerides (TG) are within an ideal level [14]. In addition, other observational studies have also confirmed the potential link between RC and diabetes [15–17]. Inflammation is also a key factor in the occurrence and development of diabetes [18–20]. High-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hsCRP) is commonly utilized in clinical settings to indicate inflammation and has been linked to an elevated likelihood of diabetes [21, 22]. Importantly, there exists an interaction between inflammatory processes and dysregulated lipid metabolism, which further exacerbates insulin resistance [11]. This suggests that the interplay between inflammation and abnormal lipid metabolism may jointly contribute to the occurrence of diabetes. Thus, assessing both RC and hsCRP together might offer a more thorough evaluation of diabetes risk compared to using a single biomarker. Currently, no studies have investigated the joint effect of RC and hsCRP on the incidence of new-onset diabetes, nor is it clear whether RC and hsCRP might mediate the development of the disease.

To fill these gaps, we conducted a prospective evaluation of how RC and hsCRP together affect the development of new-onset diabetes, using data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) and the English Longitudinal Study of Ageing (ELSA) cohort studies. A mediation analysis was performed to investigate the possible mediating effects of hsCRP and RC in diabetes pathogenesis.

Materials and methods

Study population

Data for this analysis come from two prospective cohort studies: CHARLS and ELSA, which target middle-aged and senior populations in China and the United Kingdom, respectively. Given that the first wave (2011–2012) of CHARLS and the second wave (2004–2005) of ELSA include blood sample data, these waves were designated as the baseline for analysis.

Figure 1 provides a detailed outline of the criteria for inclusion and exclusion applied in the study. Initially, the study comprised 17,705 participants from the CHARLS and 9,432 participants from the ELSA. Exclusions were made for participants younger than 45 years in CHARLS and younger than 50 years in ELSA. Additionally, participants were excluded if they did not have hsCRP and RC data, had a diabetes diagnosis at baseline, or lacked diabetes-related information during follow-up.

Assessment of exposure

RC was calculated utilizing the formula: total cholesterol (TC) minus LDL-C and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) [23]. Participants were stratified into four distinct categories based on the median RC levels and a high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hsCRP) threshold of 1 mg/L. Group 1 comprised individuals with low levels of both hsCRP and RC, defined as hsCRP < 1 mg/L and RC < 19 mg/dL in the CHARLS study, and hsCRP < 1 mg/L and RC < 24 mg/dL in the ELSA study. Group 2 included subjects with low RC levels but elevated hsCRP levels, specified as hsCRP ≥ 1 mg/L and RC < 19 mg/dL in CHARLS, and hsCRP ≥ 1 mg/L and RC < 24 mg/dL in ELSA. Group 3 consisted of individuals with elevated RC levels and low hsCRP levels, defined as hsCRP < 1 mg/L and RC ≥ 19 mg/dL in CHARLS, and hsCRP < 1 mg/L and RC ≥ 24 mg/dL in ELSA. Lastly, Group 4 was characterized by elevated levels of both RC and hsCRP, with criteria of hsCRP ≥ 1 mg/L and RC ≥ 19 mg/dL in CHARLS, and hsCRP ≥ 1 mg/L and RC ≥ 24 mg/dL in ELSA. Following previous research [24], the Residual Cholesterol-Inflammation Index (RCII) was computed using the formula: RCII = (RC [mg/dL]×hsCRP [mg/L]) /10.

Assessment of outcome

In the CHARLS and ELSA, new-onset diabetes was determined through self-reported questionnaires, the administration of anti-diabetic medications, a fasting blood glucose level of ≥ 7.0 mmol/L, or a glycosylated hemoglobin level of ≥ 6.5%. For participants without diabetes, the time-to-event was determined as the time from the initial assessment to the most recent investigation. For participants who precisely recorded their diabetes diagnosis timing, the follow-up time was determined from the baseline assessment to the date of documented diabetes onset. In cases where the exact timing of the diabetes diagnosis was uncertain, the follow-up period was estimated using the formula: (investigation year before diabetes- baseline year)+(reported year of diabetes- investigation year before diabetes)/2. The follow-up time determination of diabetes was consistent with previous studies using the CHARLS and ELSA cohorts [14].

Assessment of covariates

We gathered a comprehensive set of covariates encompassing demographic variables (age, sex, body mass index [BMI], education attainment, and marital status), lifestyle factors (smoking and alcohol habits), systolic blood pressure (SBP), medical conditions (hypertension, dyslipidemia and CVD), and medication usage (antihypertensive, and lipid-lowering drugs). To maintain consistency between the two databases, educational levels were standardized into three categories: high school or below, high school, and college or above. Alcohol consumption was categorized as either yes or no, whereas smoking status was split into current smoker, non-smoker, and former smoker.

Statistical analysis

The missing values of covariates are processed through multiple imputation with the “mice” package. Categorical variables were described using counts and percentages, and their associations were assessed with the Chi-square test. Due to their non-normal distribution, continuous variables were shown as interquartile ranges and analyzed using the Mann–Whitney U test.

We implemented three Cox proportional hazards models to examine the effect of the combined hsCRP and RC on the occurrence of Diabetes. Model I did not make any adjustments, Model II included adjustments for age and sex, and Model III incorporated additional adjustments for demographic variables, lifestyle factors, SBP, complications, and medication usage. Participants were categorized into six distinct groups based on the median RC level and hsCRP clinical thresholds of 1 mg/L and 3 mg/L. The association between the combined hsCRP and RC and the onset of new diabetes was further quantified using multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression. Additionally, we assessed the influence of hsCRP on new-onset diabetes stratified by RC levels, and vice versa. Restricted cubic splines (RCS) with four knots were utilized to visualize whether a non-linear relationship existed between RCII and the onset of diabetes. We analyzed the RCII by quartile and examined its correlation with the risk of diabetes. A mediation analysis was performed to determine the mediating role of hsCRP in the link between RC and diabetes. Simultaneously, the potential role of RC as a mediator in the association between hsCRP and diabetes was also assessed. Bootstrapping with 1000 resamples was employed to estimate the 95% CI of the mediation effect.

We applied various sensitivity analyses to validate the robustness of our outcomes. First, subgroup analyses were performed based on age, gender, BMI, and the presence or absence of hypertension and dyslipidemia. Subsequently, given the critical role of LDL-C in the lipid profile, a subgroup analysis was executed for individuals with LDL-C levels below and above 2.6 mmol/L. This analysis was not performed in the ELSA due to the limited number of participants (n = 560) with LDL-C levels below 2.6mmol/l. Second, acknowledging the increased risk of prediabetes turning into diabetes, subjects with prediabetes at baseline were excluded, and the primary analysis was repeated. Third, to minimize the risk of reverse causation, individuals diagnosed with diabetes within the first two years of follow-up were removed. Fourth, considering that RC levels may be influenced by medication, the primary analysis was repeated after excluding individuals who used lipid-lowering drugs. Fifth, the reassessment of the risk of type 2 diabetes following co-exposure was conducted by stratifying participants according to the median levels of hsCRP and RC. In ELSA, participants were categorized into four groups based on a median RC level of 24 mg/dL and a median hsCRP level of 1.90 mg/L. This sensitivity analysis was not performed in CHARLS because the median hsCRP level was 1 mg/L, which coincides with the clinically recommended cutoff. Sixth, considering that RC was a derived variable dependent on TG, we further adjusted the baseline TG and LDL-C levels in the multivariate analysis, and the main study was repeated under conditions of TG level (< 1.7 mmol/L). Finally, we conducted Schoenfeld residuals tests on each variable in both databases. Schoenfeld residual tests revealed violations of the proportional hazards assumption for variables such as education level, smoking status, use of antihypertensive medication, and a history of cardiovascular disease within the ELSA dataset (all p < 0.05). Similarly, in the CHARLS dataset, violations were observed for age and gender (all p < 0.05). Therefore, we employed a time-varying Cox model for covariates that violated the proportional hazards assumption to enhance the robustness of our findings.

Data analysis was conducted using R software (v4.2.2), with statistical significance set at a p-value < 0.05.

Results

Baseline characteristics

Following the exclusion criteria, a total of 9,309 subjects from the CHARLS were incorporated into the final cohort, with a median follow-up time of 7.6 years. Of these individuals, 1,336 subjects were diagnosed with diabetes, exhibiting an average age of 58 years, and 47% of this subgroup were male. Additionally, the ELSA comprised 4,743 participants and observed them for a median span of 10 years. Within this cohort, 574 individuals were identified as having diabetes, with an average age of 64 years, and males constituted 43.7% of this group. Supplementary Table 1 provides a comparative analysis of the participants’ characteristics stratified by diabetes status. In these two databases, compared with non-diabetic individuals, participants with diabetes demonstrated higher levels of hsCRP, RC, and RCII. In addition, a higher proportion of participants with diabetes exhibited elevated levels of hsCRP and RC, while a lower proportion of individuals had lower levels of hsCRP and RC (P < 0.001).

Relationship of RCII with diabetes

A non-linear association between RCII and diabetes was identified in the CHARLS study (Fig. 2A, P for non-linearity < 0.001). The multivariable model in Table 4 indicated that the risk of diabetes was elevated by 37%, 51%, and 79% for subjects in Q2, Q3, and Q4, respectively, compared to those in Q1. The ELSA study similarly demonstrated a non-linear correlation between RCII and diabetes risk through RCS analysis (Fig. 2B, P for non-linearity < 0.001). After controlling for various variables, the risk of diabetes increased in individuals in Q2, Q3, and Q4 by factors of 1.37, 1.78, and 1.99, respectively, relative to those in Q1 (Table 4).

Mediation analysis

The mutual mediating effects of RC and hsCRP in the association with diabetes were illustrated in Fig. 3. Within the correlation between RC and diabetes, hsCRP serves as a mediator, accounting for 4.75% (95% CI: 2.31% − 8.40%, P < 0.001) of the effect in the CHARLS and 5.08% (95% CI: 1.79% − 9.00%, P < 0.001) in the ELSA. Conversely, in the correlation between hsCRP and diabetes, RC mediates 12.71% (95% CI: 6.60%-23.21%, P < 0.001) of the effect in CHARLS and 10.51% (95% CI: 5.94%-24.17%, P < 0.001) in ELSA.

Sensitivity analyses

The primary findings remain robust irrespective of age, gender, BMI, and whether hypertension and dyslipidemia are present or not, as detailed in Supplementary Tables 2–6. The principal findings persisted across various LDL-C thresholds, as illustrated in Supplementary Table 7. When individuals with prediabetes at baseline or those diagnosed with diabetes within the initial two years of follow-up were removed, the main results remained largely unchanged, as evidenced in Supplementary Tables 8–11. Similarly, consistent results were observed when participants who were using lipid-lowering medications at baseline were excluded, as shown in Supplementary Tables 12 and 13. Moreover, similar results were obtained when participants were stratified based on the median levels of hsCRP and RC in ELSA (Supplementary Table 14). The results remained robust even after further adjusting for TG and LDL-C in multivariate analysis (Supplementary Table 15). Even when TG was below 1.8 mmol/L in both databases, the main results did not change significantly (Supplementary Table 16). The association between the combination of hsCRP and RC with diabetes risk remained consistent across both the original and time-varying Cox models in both databases (Supplementary Table 17).

Discussion

Using two prospective databases, our study is the first to confirm that elevated levels of hsCRP alone, RC alone, and their combination link to a progressively higher risk of diabetes than participants with low levels of both RC and hsCRP. Furthermore, a higher RCII level in subjects is connected to a heightened diabetes risk. Our results suggest a reciprocal mediation association between RC and hsCRP concerning the occurrence of diabetes. Furthermore, sensitivity analyses confirm the robustness of these findings.

Studies have previously identified a link between RC or hsCRP levels and a greater risk of diabetes. Elevated baseline RC levels or cumulative RC exposure have been connected to a heightened risk of developing diabetes [14, 25, 26]. Furthermore, existing studies have documented positive associations between RC and prediabetes or insulin resistance [25, 26]. Several studies provide evidence supporting the link between hsCRP and insulin resistance or diabetes [27–29]. A meta-analysis has demonstrated the predictive capacity of hsCRP for diabetes [21]. Studies have recently shown that using both hsCRP and the atherogenic index of plasma together enhances the identification of diabetes risk [30, 31]. Inflammation and abnormal lipid metabolism do not exist in isolation, but jointly affect the occurrence of diabetes. Increasing evidence indicates that the combined impact of RC and hsCRP may be more significant than their individual effects [24, 32, 33]. This study aims to investigate the co-exposure effect of RC and hsCRP in individuals newly diagnosed with diabetes using two prospective cohort databases. The threshold for hsCRP is typically established at 1 mg/L and 3 mg/L to distinguish between low, medium, and high cardiovascular risk categories [34]. Using these thresholds, we classify hsCRP and RC into various combinations. Furthermore, we have introduced an innovative comprehensive indicator, RCII, to assess the synergistic interaction between blood lipids and inflammatory processes [24]. Participants exhibiting higher RC and hsCRP levels, or those in the highest RCII group, demonstrated the greatest risk for developing diabetes in both databases. Furthermore, subsequent subgroup analyses indicated that the relationship between RC and diabetes is independent of hsCRP, and vice versa. LDL-C is frequently regarded as a risk factor for CVD. The impact of the combined presence of RC and hsCRP on diabetes was found to be independent of LDL-C levels, aligning with previous research findings [16, 35]. The current study underlines the significance of integrated management of hsCRP and RC in preventing diabetes.

In the context of mutual mediation, the pivotal role of RC in diabetes is partially mediated by hsCRP. There exists a causal relationship between RC and low-grade inflammation [36]. Chronic low-grade inflammation may exacerbate abnormalities in glucose metabolism by intensifying insulin resistance and amplifying systemic inflammation [37, 38]. Conversely, our research findings indicate that hsCRP serves as a risk factor for diabetes, partially mediated by elevated RC levels. Inflammation can influence the distribution and accumulation of lipids. Abnormal lipid accumulation, particularly in the pancreas and liver, may impair the function of pancreatic beta cells and inhibit insulin secretion [11, 39, 40] and hence exaggerate diabetes development [41]. These findings suggest that RC and hsCRP are interrelated and collectively contribute to the concurrence of diabetes. Our research supports prior studies [24, 32, 33], highlighting the importance of thoroughly evaluating RC and hsCRP levels in clinical settings.

This study presents several notable advantages. Firstly, it is pioneering in examining the relationship between RC combined with hsCRP and diabetes. Furthermore, our findings are based on two large-scale prospective cohort studies in China and the United Kingdom, characterized by a substantial sample size and high data quality. The consistency of our results across both datasets suggests their generalizability. In terms of exposure assessment, we incorporated both the combined stratification of hsCRP and RC, as well as the RCII, which provides a comprehensive evaluation of inflammation and RC. Furthermore, various sensitivity analyses were undertaken to validate the robustness of our findings. However, certain limitations of this analysis must be acknowledged. Firstly, the accuracy of diabetes diagnosis may be subject to bias. Self-reported diabetes is prone to bias, and the absence of blood test data for some individuals may lead to an underestimation of diabetes diagnoses. Furthermore, even when blood test results are available, both databases lack postprandial blood glucose measurements taken two hours after a meal, which may further contribute to the underestimation of diabetes diagnoses. Secondly, some follow-up periods are estimated based on methodologies from previous studies. Thirdly, despite conducting multivariate and sensitivity analyses, the potential influence of unmeasured covariates, such as family history of diabetes, cannot be entirely ruled out. Fourthly, our evaluation of RC and hsCRP was limited to baseline measurements; thus, the relationship between their longitudinal changes and diabetes incidence during follow-up warrants further investigation in future research. Finally, although we conducted sensitivity analyses to mitigate the impact of TG on RC, classification bias remains inevitable. In future research, the direct detection method is used to determine RC, thereby improving the accuracy of RC assessment.

Conclusion

The combination of elevated RC and hsCRP is linked to an increased likelihood of diabetes. Therefore, a comprehensive evaluation of the joint risk associated with hsCRP and RC, coupled with prompt intervention, could reduce the risk of diabetes onset.
