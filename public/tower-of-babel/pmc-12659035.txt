Knowledge-level comparison in pulpal and periapical diseases: dental students versus artificial intelligence models (Gemini, Microsoft Copilot, ChatGPT-3.5, ChatGPT-4o): cross-sectional study
Authors: Özge Kurt, Emine Şimsek
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study explored the diagnostic accuracy of artificial intelligence (AI) chatbots and dental students when responding to questions related to pulpal and periapical diseases. Rapid advancements in AI have led to increased interest in their applicability to clinical education and decision-making in dentistry.ObjectiveTo compare the accuracy rates of responses given by dental students and various AI-based chatbots (ChatGPT-3.5, ChatGPT-4o, Gemini, and Microsoft Copilot) to multiple-choice questions designed to assess knowledge related to pulpal and periapical diseases.MethodsThe study included third- and fifth-year dental students representing different levels of clinical training, along with four distinct AI-based chatbots. A total of 327 responses were collected from students, while each chatbot generated 450 responses. The evaluation was based on 15 multiple-choice questions developed in accordance with the 2020 version of the American Association of Endodontists (AAE) clinical guidelines. The accuracy rates of the groups were compared using descriptive statistics, one-way ANOVA, Bonferroni post hoc tests for significant differences, and Chi-square tests for correct versus incorrect response ratios.ResultsThe highest accuracy rate was observed among fifth-year dental students (85.1%), followed by ChatGPT-4o (79.6%), ChatGPT-3.5 (75.1%), Gemini (71.6%), third-year students (64.9%), and Microsoft Copilot (61.3%). A statistically significant difference was found among the groups (p < 0.05). ChatGPT-4o demonstrated a comparable accuracy rate to fifth-year students with more clinical experience (p > 0.05), whereas other chatbots and third-year students showed lower performance.ConclusionChatbots exhibited varying levels of accuracy in diagnosing pulpal and periapical diseases. ChatGPT-4o performed at a level similar to that of more clinically experienced students, suggesting its potential as a supportive tool in dental education and clinical decision support systems. However, the relatively lower accuracy rates of models such as Gemini and Microsoft Copilot underscore the continued importance of human expertise. These findings suggest that while AI systems may serve as complementary tools in education, they cannot fully replace clinical judgment grounded in human experience.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-025-08263-8.Keywords: Artificial intelligence, Dental students, Education, Dental, Large language models periapical periodontitis, Pulp disease

Background

Artificial intelligence (AI) is a technological domain that focuses on developing computer systems capable of executing cognitive functions traditionally attributed to human intelligence [1]. Advances in machine learning and deep learning have facilitated the integration of AI across data-intensive disciplines such as education and healthcare, contributing to enhanced efficiency and improved decision-support mechanisms [2].

Within the field of education, AI promotes personalized learning, performance assessment, and the optimization of instructional methodologies [3]. When integrated with interactive technologies such as virtual reality and simulation-based platforms, AI supports the acquisition of practical and clinical competencies, particularly in healthcare training. In medicine and dentistry, AI-driven chatbots have emerged as innovative tools that assist learners and practitioners alike in both theoretical education and clinical decision-making processes [4].

Large language models (LLMs), endowed with advanced natural language processing (NLP) capabilities, have garnered increasing attention due to their capacity to generate human-like responses and to analyze complex data [5]. ChatGPT, developed by OpenAI and based on the Generative Pre-trained Transformer (GPT) architecture, has been employed across a range of applications, including patient education, rapid information retrieval, and clinical reasoning support [6, 7].

Despite the expanding interest in these technologies, research specifically exploring the use of LLMs within dental education remains limited. The majority of existing literature has focused on general medical applications or specific subdisciplines of dentistry. Empirical data directly comparing the diagnostic accuracy and reasoning consistency of LLMs in dentistry-oriented clinical scenarios are particularly scarce. Addressing this research gap is crucial for evaluating their potential as cognitive aids in dental diagnostic decision-making.

Snigdha et al. compared the performance of ChatGPT-3.5 and ChatGPT-4o in operative dentistry and endodontics, reporting no statistically significant differences in diagnostic accuracy or reasoning consistency [8]. Similarly, Tomo et al. demonstrated that ChatGPT-4o achieved expert-level diagnostic accuracy but exhibited only moderate consistency across responses in oral and maxillofacial pathology [9]. Yılmaz et al. assessed eight distinct LLMs using national dental examination questions and observed variable accuracy levels, with ChatGPT-4o showing superior overall performance [10]. Uranbey et al. evaluated ChatGPT-3.5 on oral pathology cases, reporting high diagnostic accuracy while emphasizing the potential influence of algorithmic bias [11]. Additionally, Akpınar H. conducted a comparative analysis of responses generated by various artificial intelligence–powered chatbots concerning the All-on-four dental implant concept, revealing significant variations in informational consistency and content accuracy [12]. Collectively, these findings underscore the growing academic attention toward LLMs in clinical reasoning research within the dental domain.

ChatGPT-3.5 (OpenAI, San Francisco, CA, USA; March 2023 version) gained widespread recognition for its baseline text generation and comprehension capabilities. However, ChatGPT-4o, introduced in May 2024, offers significant improvements in language understanding, logical reasoning, and mathematical processing [13]. This version also includes multimodal input capabilities (encompassing text, image, audio, and video) broadening its potential applications in sectors such as education and healthcare [14].

Similarly, Gemini, released by Google in December 2023 (February 2024 version; Google, Mountain View, CA, USA), also features multimodal functionality and presents comparable potential, especially in decision-support applications within the healthcare domain [15]. With its advanced algorithms, extensive language support, and user-friendly interface, Gemini is positioned as a tool capable of enhancing data-driven decision-making [16]. Microsoft Copilot (Microsoft, Redmond, WA, USA; April 2024 version) is another language model-based system that has gained attention, particularly in disciplines requiring the integrated interpretation of textual, visual, and clinical data [17].

Pulpal and periapical diseases are inflammatory conditions that affect the dental pulp and the surrounding periapical tissues [18]. The pulp responds to stimuli such as caries, trauma, or restorative procedures through an inflammatory response that may result in either reversible or irreversible pulpitis [19]. When infection progresses to the periapical region, it may lead to the development of apical periodontitis, apical abscesses, or radicular cysts [20]. Accurate diagnosis requires comprehensive anamnesis, detailed clinical examination, radiographic evaluation, and pulp vitality testing. Diagnostic errors may compromise treatment outcomes and result in unnecessary or invasive procedures. Therefore, the ability to distinguish pulpal and periapical pathologies accurately is critical for rational and effective clinical decision-making [21].

Dental education is an interdisciplinary process that aims to equip students with both theoretical knowledge and clinical competencies [22]. During this training, students are expected to develop clinical decision-making skills, especially in areas such as diagnosis and treatment planning [23]. The evaluation of pulpal and periapical diseases requires not only sufficient knowledge but also strong clinical reasoning, analytical, and interpretive abilities [24].

There is a limited number of studies in the literature directly comparing the clinical decision-making performance of AI-based chatbots with dental students [25, 26]. Some earlier studies focused on comparing chatbots with medical students or lay users, typically using knowledge-based questions. However, these studies did not extend to the evaluation of clinical decision-making in complex case scenarios. Our study differs from prior research by incorporating both knowledge-based items and diagnostic decision-making tasks related to pulpal and periapical diseases.

The primary aim of this study is to compare the diagnostic performance of dental students and AI-based chatbots (ChatGPT-3.5, ChatGPT-4o, Gemini, and Microsoft Copilot) in the context of pulpal and periapical conditions. The questionnaire comprises both factual and case-based questions. In this way, the study aims to provide a comprehensive assessment of human and AI performance across complex diagnostic tasks.

The null hypothesis tested in this study stated that there would be no statistically significant difference between the ChatGPT-4o and ChatGPT-3.5 models in terms of diagnostic accuracy and response consistency related to pulpal and periapical diseases.

Methods

Conceptual and methodological framework

This study was designed as a single-center, exploratory, and comparative cross-sectional research aiming to evaluate the diagnostic accuracy of AI-based chatbots (ChatGPT-3.5, ChatGPT-4o, Gemini, Microsoft Copilot) and dental students in the context of pulpal and periapical diseases. The assessment instrument consisted of scenario-based multiple-choice questions designed to measure both factual knowledge and contextual clinical reasoning. Within this framework, the study systematically compared human diagnostic reasoning with AI-generated textual outputs in endodontic decision-making processes.

Data collection procedure

The same set of questions was presented to each chatbot three times per day during the morning (T1), noon (T2), and evening (T3) sessions over a consecutive ten-day period, resulting in 30 independent sessions per chatbot. This repeated query protocol was implemented due to the probabilistic nature of generative AI, where responses may vary across sessions for the same input due to factors such as contextual memory, temperature settings, and linguistic prioritization.

Each session was initiated using a “new chat” feature to reset the context and ensure clean, context-independent responses. As a result, each output was treated as a discrete and independent instance. A total of 450 valid responses were collected from each chatbot (15 questions × 3 repetitions × 10 days).

Part 2 – student data collection

Ethical approval and sample size determination

The study was approved by the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of Mersin University (Protocol No: 2025/613). All procedures adhered to the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. The required sample size was calculated using G*Power 3.1 (Heinrich Heine University, Düsseldorf, Germany). A power analysis based on a fixed-effects one-way ANOVA (omnibus test) indicated that, assuming α = 0.05, an effect size (f) of 0.25, and 95% power (1-β = 0.95), a minimum of 324 valid responses would be required for statistical significance.

Accordingly, some items were constructed to assess direct theoretical knowledge (e.g., identifying a pulpal diagnosis based on a single symptom), while others were designed to simulate real-life diagnostic scenarios that required the integrated interpretation of patient history, clinical findings, and radiographic data.

Particular attention was paid to ensuring alignment between the complexity of the clinical scenarios and students’ academic levels. Third-year students had just started clinical rotations and were familiar with endodontic diagnostic principles primarily at the theoretical level. Therefore, scenarios were tailored to assess both foundational knowledge and entry-level reasoning abilities. The difficulty level was balanced to enable fair comparisons across groups and minimize potential bias related to academic seniority.

Each question was designed with contextual clinical depth, including elements such as detailed patient histories, unique symptom patterns, and prior treatment records. This format was intended to assess participants’ diagnostic reasoning in addition to their factual knowledge.

Only endodontically-derived pathologies were included. Scenarios involving restorative, periodontal, or systemic differential diagnoses were deliberately excluded. This restriction aimed to improve contextual validity while acknowledging that the generalizability of the findings is limited to the endodontic diagnostic domain.

Implementation process

The finalized questionnaire was digitized via Google Forms (Google LLC, Mountain View, CA, USA) and distributed to student participants via WhatsApp and email. The introduction page clearly stated that participation was voluntary, no personal data would be collected, and all responses would be anonymized.

Informed consent was obtained from all participating students through a consent form provided on the first page of the online questionnaire. No financial or academic incentives were offered to participants, and the survey was conducted solely for scientific research purposes. The question set was not directly related to exam preparation and was not associated with academic evaluation or educational outcomes. The entire participation process was carried out based on the principle of students’ autonomy in decision-making.

Data collection took place over 10 days, between May 11 and May 20, 2025. The same questionnaire form was also administered to the chatbots by the researcher, and all responses were recorded using Microsoft Excel (Microsoft, Redmond, WA, USA).

Response evaluation

All responses were independently reviewed by two researchers using the diagnostic criteria set forth in the AAE Clinical Guidelines. Student and chatbot responses were categorized as “correct” or “incorrect,” allowing for both individual- and group-level accuracy analyses.

To assess inter-rater reliability in classifying chatbot responses by two researcher, Cohen’s kappa coefficient was used. The mean kappa value was 0.93, ranging from 0.86 to 0.98, indicating a very high level of internal consistency.

Study design considerations

This study was designed as a single-center, exploratory cross-sectional investigation comparing the diagnostic accuracy of dental students and AI-based chatbots.

Given the use of text-based, multiple-choice assessment, the findings reflect diagnostic decision-making and knowledge levels, but do not directly represent clinical competence. The results must be interpreted within the contextual limitations of the study design, and the generalizability is restricted to diagnostic performance in endodontic scenarios.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS software, version 27 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics were presented as frequencies, percentages, means, standard deviations, medians, and minimum–maximum values. Prior to statistical analysis, the normality of the distribution was checked using the Shapiro-Wilk test, and it was determined that the distribution was normal. The use of parametric tests was deemed appropriate for statistical analysis. Group comparisons were performed using one-way ANOVA. When statistically significant differences were found, Bonferroni post hoc tests were conducted for pairwise comparisons. The Chi-square test was used to compare the proportions of correct and incorrect responses. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Discussion

The application of chatbots in healthcare is rapidly expanding, with increasing emphasis on their potential roles in education and clinical decision support systems. In this context, assessing the knowledge level of AI models in dental education is essential for understanding both their complementary roles in education and their limitations in clinical competence. This study aimed to compare the diagnostic accuracy of dental students and various chatbots (ChatGPT-3.5, ChatGPT-4o, Gemini, and Microsoft Copilot) in answering multiple-choice questions related to pulpal and periapical diseases. In this regard, the present study offers a significant contribution to the literature as one of the few original investigations that not only assess theoretical knowledge, but also directly compare the diagnostic decision-making performance of AI-powered conversational agents and dental students within case-based clinical scenarios.

The findings revealed that 5th-year dental students achieved the highest accuracy rate, followed by ChatGPT-4o, ChatGPT-3.5, Gemini, 3rd-year students, and finally Microsoft Copilot. These findings indicate that both the level of clinical experience and the technical capacity of LLM-based conversational agents have a decisive influence on diagnostic accuracy. Within this framework, the null hypothesis stated in the introduction namely, that there would be no statistically significant difference between ChatGPT-4o and ChatGPT-3.5 in terms of diagnostic accuracy and response consistency related to pulpal and periapical diseases was rejected.

However, as the results were derived from a specific test environment consisting solely of text-based multiple-choice questions, they should not be interpreted as evidence of equivalence in overall clinical competence. Instead, the findings must be evaluated within the contextual limitations of the study. While it is noteworthy that ChatGPT-4o achieved accuracy rates comparable to those of students with advanced clinical experience, this should not be taken to imply that LLMs are equivalent to clinically trained individuals. On the contrary, given their current capabilities, such models are more appropriately positioned as complementary digital tools within educational processes rather than as autonomous decision-makers.

In this context, advanced models like ChatGPT-4o have considerable potential to function as instructional scaffolds that support student development. Moreover, LLM-based AI systems are increasingly being explored in medical education not only as tools for information retrieval but also as generative instruments that facilitate meaningful and effective learning. For instance, tasks such as generating clinical case scenarios, producing alternative diagnostic hypotheses, or allowing students to compare their own clinical reasoning with chatbot-generated responses may foster deeper thinking and reflective learning.

Similarly, using these models as clinical simulation partners could enhance the development of cognitive skills such as open-ended reasoning and justification of medical decisions. Additionally, incorporating these systems as sources of constructive feedback during student self-review may support iterative learning processes and reinforce critical appraisal skills.

The high accuracy of ChatGPT-4o may be attributed to its advanced natural language processing capabilities, multimodal infrastructure (text/audio/image), and updated knowledge base. Uehara et al. reported that ChatGPT-4o achieved an accuracy rate of 84.63% on the Japanese National Dental Examination and performed well in clinical reasoning tasks [28]. In a recent study conducted by Tomo et al. [9], ChatGPT-4 demonstrated diagnostic accuracy comparable to that of expert clinicians in the field of oral and maxillofacial diseases. However, the consistency of its responses was reported to be moderate, highlighting potential variability in its outputs. Similarly, in our study, which also used text-based multiple-choice questions, high accuracy rates were observed. Other studies have shown that ChatGPT-4o also performs well in medical contexts, achieving 89.9% on the USMLE Step 1 [29] and 94% on the UK Medical Licensing Assessment [30]. In visual tasks, ChatGPT-4o achieved 95.7% accuracy in brain tumor MRI diagnostics [31], further supporting its potential as a reliable tool not only for theoretical knowledge but also for clinical and educational applications requiring complex information synthesis.

Although the existing literature on the Gemini model remains limited, a study conducted in 2024 reported an accuracy rate of 80.5% based on items from the Turkish Dental Specialization Examination (DUS) [32]. This performance appears to be higher than the 71.6% accuracy observed in the present study. However, it is important to note that the aforementioned study focused exclusively on theoretical knowledge questions, whereas the current investigation employed scenario-based questions specifically designed to assess clinical decision-making in the context of pulpal and periapical pathologies. This methodological distinction likely contributed to the observed discrepancy in performance, suggesting that Gemini may be less effective in case-based diagnostic reasoning tasks.

The relatively lower performance of Gemini in clinical scenarios may not be solely attributable to the question format, but may also reflect the model’s underlying training data and intended application scope. Although Gemini is a multimodal language model capable of processing text, visual, and audio inputs, it has not been explicitly optimized for use in medical or clinical settings. As such, the model tends to adopt a more generalized response strategy, which may limit its effectiveness when confronted with complex, scenario-based questions that require nuanced clinical reasoning. This observation underscores the importance of aligning AI models with specific educational and diagnostic objectives, particularly when applied to health professions education.

Microsoft Copilot had the lowest accuracy rate in our study, consistent with findings in the literature. Similar results have been reported in recent studies evaluating clinical decision-making. For instance, in a comparative ophthalmology study, ChatGPT-4o achieved 72.3% accuracy, Gemini 62.0%, and Microsoft Copilot only 53.7% [33]. In cardiovascular pharmacology, Shastri et al. found that Microsoft Copilot’s accuracy dropped to 53% for complex medical questions, whereas ChatGPT models performed significantly better [34]. These findings suggest that Microsoft Copilot has more limited capabilities in clinical reasoning and multidimensional thinking compared to other large language models. This outcome is presumed to be largely attributable to the model’s architecture and its intended use-case design. Microsoft Copilot has primarily been developed as a productivity-oriented artificial intelligence tool, optimized for tasks such as document editing, spreadsheet generation, and email composition within office environments. Given this orientation, its relatively lower performance in clinical applications particularly those requiring advanced cognitive processes such as differential diagnosis and clinical decision making is not considered unexpected. The model’s core functionalities are not specifically aligned with the demands of diagnostic reasoning in healthcare, which may limit its applicability in medical education contexts where domain-specific accuracy and interpretive depth are essential.

In the present study, the performance of ChatGPT-3.5 was found to be significantly lower compared to that of ChatGPT-4o. This finding is consistent with the results reported by Yılmaz et al. [32], in which eight different LLMs were evaluated using items from the Dental Specialty Examination (DUS). In that study, substantial variability in diagnostic accuracy was observed across models, with ChatGPT-4o demonstrating the highest performance among them. Similarly, previous research has reported that ChatGPT-3.5 exhibits limited accuracy and inferential capacity within the context of dental education [35]. For instance, Jin et al. [35] reported a diagnostic accuracy of only 63.2% for ChatGPT-3.5 on dentistry-related examination questions. In contrast, the current study found a higher accuracy rate of 75.1%, which may be attributable to differences in question types and case formats utilized.

Despite this relative improvement, multiple studies in the literature continue to highlight the superior performance of ChatGPT-4o in tasks requiring clinical reasoning and knowledge integration [35, 36]. Conversely, a study conducted by Uranbey et al. noted that ChatGPT-3.5 achieved high diagnostic accuracy and acceptable treatment planning in oral pathology scenarios [11]. However, the same study emphasized the potential presence of algorithmic biases in the model and recommended cautious interpretation of its clinical recommendations, particularly in terms of decision reliability.

Today, AI-based chatbots can achieve or even surpass human performance in certain diagnostic and decision-making tasks. Our findings show that final-year dental students performed comparably to ChatGPT-4o in diagnostic accuracy, suggesting that advanced clinical experience can provide a level of response competence similar to that of high-performing chatbots. In a study by Qutieshat et al., ChatGPT-4o achieved 99% accuracy, while human participants averaged 77% [36]. Although this suggests that chatbots can outperform humans, our results show that experienced individuals (5th-year students) can achieve similar diagnostic success. In contrast, 3rd-year students had a lower accuracy rate of 64.9% and a broader IQR, indicating greater variability in knowledge due to limited clinical exposure. This highlights the importance of clinical experience not only for knowledge acquisition but also for case-based decision-making.

On the other hand, Özbay et al. reported that ChatGPT-4o achieved only 11% accuracy in the radiographic evaluation of periapical lesions [37]. However, that study focused solely on direct image-based diagnosis. In contrast, our study used only text-based clinical scenarios and multiple-choice questions. This method may have allowed the LLM to perform with greater accuracy and consistency, as prior research also suggests that while AI models underperform in visual diagnostic tasks, they achieve higher accuracy in text-based clinical scenarios [38].

As emphasized in the literature, AI models hold significant potential in healthcare education and clinical support [28]. Multimodal models like ChatGPT-4o stand out for their ability to provide rapid feedback, facilitate access to information, and enhance case-based reasoning skills [28, 36]. The findings of this study suggest that advanced AI models may achieve diagnostic accuracy comparable to that of experienced students in certain clinical scenarios. Notably, the performance of ChatGPT-4o was found to be similar to that of senior dental students with substantial clinical training, which is a compelling observation. However, this should not be interpreted as evidence that such models are capable of replacing human expertise. In their current state, AI-based conversational agents should not be regarded as final decision-makers in clinical contexts. Rather, they ought to be positioned as supplementary tools designed to support and enhance clinical education, offering assistance in knowledge reinforcement, differential diagnosis practice, and structured reasoning exercises.

Considering potential clinical applications, AI-based conversational agents appear most promising as decision-support adjuncts and as educational materials. In particular, models such as ChatGPT-4o may provide formative, case-based feedback that supports knowledge reinforcement, error analysis, and the integration of concepts across domains. Nevertheless, in their current form these systems should not be positioned as final decision-makers in clinical care; rather, they should be integrated as complementary digital tools within curricula to scaffold clinical reasoning and differential diagnosis practice.

This study relied exclusively on multiple choice items and did not include open-ended scenarios or direct, practice-based clinical tasks. The single-center design and limited sample size further constrain generalizability. Beyond item-level accuracy, additional constructs such as explanation quality, response consistency, and decision reliability warrant independent evaluation. Our item set targeted text-based diagnostic judgments; however, prior work suggests that AI models may perform less consistently in radiographic or other image-based diagnostic tasks [39, 40]. Consequently, high accuracy observed on text-only assessments may not transfer to multimodal clinical domains. Moreover, large language models can reflect biases present in their training data and may over-rely on spurious cues, which can undermine reliability. We also observed sensitivity to the formal structure of multiple-choice questions, indicating that performance can vary with linguistic framing and distractor design. These factors may limit consistency and external validity across different languages, cultures, and clinical settings; thus, findings should be interpreted with appropriate contextual caution.

Multicenter studies with larger, heterogeneous cohorts spanning institutions and training levels are needed. Comparative evaluations should incorporate open-ended and unstructured case vignettes, as well as multimodal inputs (e.g., radiographs, clinical photographs). Systematic assessment of models’ capacity to provide transparent, educationally useful rationales alongside measures of calibration, uncertainty communication, and consistency over time should be prioritized. Finally, simulation-based assessments that emulate patient clinician communication and clinical reasoning in authentic contexts are likely to be critical for determining real-world educational utility.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that dental students’ knowledge of pulpal and periapical diseases improves with clinical training. Fifth-year students showed greater accuracy and consistency compared to third-year students. Among the evaluated AI models, ChatGPT-4o demonstrated the highest diagnostic accuracy on the text-based clinical scenarios used in this study and, in select cases, performed comparably to final-year dental students. However, these findings reflect relative performance within a controlled test environment and should not be interpreted as evidence of equivalent clinical competence. ChatGPT-4o, in particular, holds promise as an educational tool to facilitate access to domain knowledge and to support the development of clinical reasoning skills. Nonetheless, in their current iterations, such AI systems should be considered as adjunctive educational aids rather than autonomous clinical decision-makers.

This study is exploratory in nature, given its single-center design and reliance on a limited set of multiple-choice, text-based items. These constraints limit the generalizability of the findings to other educational levels and clinical environments. While the results support the applicability of AI-based conversational agents in select areas of dental knowledge, further research is warranted. Future investigations should employ broader samples, incorporate open-ended case formats, and integrate expert evaluation to more comprehensively assess the capabilities and limitations of these tools across diverse educational and clinical contexts.
