Survival under armed conflict: displacement, dialysis continuity, and financial burden among hemodialysis patients in Sudan
Authors: Mazin M. T. Shigidi, Nazik M. Abdulfatah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPatients receiving maintenance hemodialysis are highly vulnerable during armed conflict, particularly when healthcare systems are disrupted. However, factors associated with survival under prolonged disruption remain poorly understood. This study examined survival and key associated factors among hemodialysis patients during the armed conflict in Sudan.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort study of 158 adults receiving maintenance hemodialysis in Khartoum, Sudan, at the onset of the April 2023 conflict, with follow-up extending up to 18 months. Outcomes were confirmed for 122 patients, while 36 were untraceable and censored at their last verified contact. Participants were categorized according to displacement trajectory within the first three months. The primary outcome was survival. Key variables included displacement trajectory, dialysis frequency during follow-up, and out-of-pocket costs. Risk ratios and time-to-event approaches were used to examine associations, with sensitivity analyses conducted to assess the impact of loss to follow-up.ResultsOf the 122 patients with confirmed outcomes, 53.3% remained alive at the end of follow-up. Survival rates differed across displacement trajectories: 81.8% among those who relocated internationally early, 53.3% among those who achieved internal settlement, 34.6% among those who experienced secondary displacement, and 13.8% among those who remained in the capital. Higher survival was observed among patients receiving at least two dialysis sessions per week compared with those receiving one or fewer sessions or emergency-only dialysis (68.8% vs. 43.2%; RR 1.59, 95% CI 1.15–2.20). Financial burden was substantial, with monthly dialysis-related costs ranging from USD 720–3,600 during early displacement and remaining high thereafter (USD 500–3,500).ConclusionsSurvival differed across displacement patterns and dialysis continuity, and was influenced by financial burden. Background

Patients requiring long-term maintenance hemodialysis depend on uninterrupted health system support, including specialized infrastructure, trained personnel, reliable supply chains, and routine biochemical monitoring. Interruptions in hemodialysis during armed conflict may precipitate severe metabolic and volume-related complications, including hyperkalemia, acidosis, and fluid overload. The rate and severity of deterioration vary according to residual kidney function, dietary intake, comorbidity burden, and duration of interruption; however, even short gaps in treatment may be life-threatening in patients with established dialysis dependence [1, 2].

The armed conflict that began in Sudan in April 2023 caused widespread disruption of healthcare services, particularly in Khartoum and other urban centers. Dialysis services were disproportionately affected due to their reliance on electricity, treated water, consumables, and specialized staff [3–5]. For dialysis-dependent individuals, such disruptions can transform a chronic condition into an immediate threat to survival, reflecting the limited physiological reserve associated with end-stage kidney disease [2].

Prior to the conflict, maintenance hemodialysis services in Sudan were delivered through a partially government-supported system coordinated by the National Center for Renal Diseases and Surgery, supplemented by commercial insurance coverage and out-of-pocket payments. Despite this structure, substantial variability in treatment quality, medication availability, and adherence to clinical guidelines persisted, reflecting underlying systemic constraints [6–8]. These pre-existing limitations are important for contextualizing patient outcomes during the conflict.

Previous studies of dialysis care in disaster and conflict settings have primarily focused on service interruption and short-term mortality [9–11]. Although excess mortality is consistently reported, less is known about the factors that enable some patients to survive prolonged periods of health system disruption. Survival in these contexts is likely influenced by a complex interplay of factors, including reduced dialysis frequency, disrupted medication access, fragmented follow-up, forced displacement, and financial constraints [12–14].

Displacement is a key component of these survival pathways, though its effects are multifaceted and context-dependent. Patients may remain in active conflict zones, relocate internally to resource-limited areas, or cross international borders to access care. Internal displacement is often associated with reduced dialysis availability and overcrowding, whereas cross-border relocation may improve access to treatment but often entails substantial financial burden and loss of continuity in care [15–17].

Chronic non-communicable diseases, including end-stage kidney disease, remain underrepresented in humanitarian response frameworks, which have traditionally prioritized trauma and communicable diseases. Infrastructure-dependent therapies such as hemodialysis further expose systemic vulnerabilities in conflict-affected settings and raise important questions regarding equity, access, and responsibility for life-sustaining care.

Within this context, Sudan provides a critical setting to examine survival among patients receiving long-term hemodialysis during armed conflict. This study aims to characterize survival patterns and identify factors associated with survival, including displacement trajectories, dialysis continuity, and financial burden, during a period of sustained health system disruption.

Methods

Study design and setting

This retrospective cohort study included adult patients with end-stage kidney disease (ESKD) receiving maintenance hemodialysis in Khartoum, Sudan, at the onset of the armed conflict in April 2023. Eligible patients had been on hemodialysis for at least three months prior to conflict onset.

Before the conflict, dialysis services in Khartoum were delivered through multiple centers, with Baraha Medical City (BMC) serving as a major tertiary referral institution providing integrated nephrology care, including outpatient follow-up, inpatient services, and dialysis support. The center also maintained longitudinal clinical records for patients receiving care across multiple facilities.

The onset of conflict was abrupt, with active fighting reaching the vicinity of the hospital within 48 h and rapidly spreading across Khartoum North. This escalation left little opportunity for advance planning or coordinated patient transfer, and most patients left with minimal preparation. Hospital operations were rapidly reduced to essential services and ceased entirely within approximately two weeks following progressive structural damage and loss of infrastructure.

Patients were subsequently followed for up to 18 months to assess survival and continuity of care during a period of prolonged health system disruption. Investigators were directly involved in patient care or had authorized access to institutional clinical records.

This analysis represents one component of a broader investigation examining outcomes among hemodialysis patients during armed conflict in Sudan using the same underlying cohort. Given the observational design, findings are interpreted as descriptive and non-causal.

Study population

The study cohort comprised all adult patients (n = 158) with ESKD receiving maintenance hemodialysis immediately prior to April 2023. Eligible patients had been on hemodialysis for at least three months and had either received dialysis or attended outpatient nephrology follow-up at BMC within the three months preceding the onset of the conflict.

The cohort included patients undergoing dialysis at BMC as well as those receiving dialysis at other centers in Khartoum while maintaining regular outpatient follow-up at BMC.

Hemodialysis was the only modality of renal replacement therapy in this cohort, as peritoneal dialysis was not routinely available due to infrastructural constraints and limited access to essential materials [3, 18].

Data sources and follow-up procedures

Data were collected using a predefined structured abstraction framework with standardized variables. Baseline information was extracted from electronic medical records, which remained accessible despite damage to physical infrastructure.

Follow-up information was obtained through direct communication with patients or caregivers and, where available, from receiving dialysis facilities. Participants were contacted via phone calls, WhatsApp (including text and voice messages), and standard messaging services. If direct communication was not feasible, information was obtained through relatives or healthcare personnel. At least three contact attempts at different time points were made before a participant was classified as untraceable.

Data were recorded using a standardized template without audio recording. Where feasible, information was cross-verified from multiple sources to enhance accuracy, consistent with triangulation approaches in conflict and humanitarian research [20–22]. All data were anonymized at collection and stored in secure, password-protected systems accessible only to the study investigators.

Follow-up and outcome ascertainment were conducted between May 2023 and October 2024. Follow-up was conducted longitudinally over an 18-month period using flexible communication strategies adapted to the conflict setting [10–12]. The frequency of contact varied according to patient location, clinical status, and communication feasibility, with a minimum of two contact attempts per participant and repeated follow-up where feasible.

For patients with confirmed death, contact was often limited to early follow-up periods, particularly among those who remained in high conflict areas. In contrast, survivors, especially those who relocated outside the country, were more likely to have ongoing follow-up, typically at intervals of approximately 2 to 3 months.

Contact frequency was highest among patients displaced within Sudan, where communication was frequently required to facilitate access to dialysis services, coordinate emergency care, and provide ongoing clinical guidance. Accordingly, contact patterns in this group were more intensive and variable, reflecting the need for real-time support in a resource-constrained and unstable care environment. Under more stable conditions, follow-up intervals extended to monthly or bimonthly contact.

Among patients who relocated outside Sudan, follow-up interactions were primarily advisory and included medication guidance, identification of lower-cost options, interpretation of laboratory results, and second opinions when requested, with additional contact on an as-needed basis.

The 18-month period represents the maximum observation window rather than a fixed schedule of assessments. Outcome ascertainment was based on the most recent verified information, and time-to-event analyses incorporated the date of last confirmed contact, consistent with approaches used in conflict-affected populations with variable follow-up intensity [10–12]. The variability in follow-up intensity reflected operational constraints and clinical need rather than a predefined schedule.

Given the conflict setting, loss to follow-up was likely non-random and may reflect differences in survival, displacement, or access to care; accordingly, patients were censored at last verified contact and findings are interpreted as descriptive rather than causal, in line with established methodological considerations in humanitarian epidemiology [11, 12].

A standardized structured data collection instrument was used for follow-up; the template is provided as Supplementary File 1.

Exposure and outcome definitions

Dialysis frequency during follow-up was defined as the average number of hemodialysis sessions per week, based on patient or caregiver report. This pragmatic approach was used in the absence of standardized biochemical measures of dialysis adequacy, as recommended in disaster nephrology settings [1, 2].

The primary outcome was all-cause mortality over an 18-month follow-up period. Survival status was determined through direct patient contact, caregiver report, or confirmation from dialysis facilities. The date of last verified contact was used as the censoring point in time-to-event analyses. Cause-of-death data were inconsistently available and not systematically verified; therefore, cause-specific mortality was not formally analyzed.

Secondary outcomes included dialysis frequency, continuity of medication use, access to laboratory monitoring, and patient-reported out-of-pocket expenditures related to dialysis care.

Displacement classification

Participants were categorized according to their predominant location during the first three months following the onset of conflict: (1) remained within the capital, (2) internally displaced within Sudan, or (3) relocated internationally. This classification was used to evaluate the relationship between displacement patterns, access to care, and survival outcomes, given the recognized importance of mobility in conflict-affected populations [19–21].

Displacement categories were based on the predominant location during the first three months, representing the period of greatest disruption. Subsequent movements were not incorporated, and classifications should therefore be interpreted as early-phase exposure markers rather than fixed trajectories.

Cost assessment

Dialysis-related expenditures were obtained through patient or caregiver reports and supported by documentation where available. Reported costs included dialysis session fees, medications, consumables, transportation, and accommodation expenses when applicable. All costs were converted to United States dollars (USD) using prevailing exchange rates at the time of reporting.

As the data were based on self-reported information and inconsistent documentation, these estimates were considered approximate and interpreted with caution. Financial burden was assessed using monthly expenditure data collected at different stages of follow-up, including early displacement and later periods of relative stabilization. These data were obtained from repeated measurements in the same individuals whenever follow-up information was available, allowing estimation of changes in financial burden over time rather than comparison across separate patient groups.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were expressed as proportions and compared using risk ratios (RR) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CI), while continuous variables were summarized using distribution-appropriate descriptive statistics.

Time-to-event outcomes were analyzed using the Kaplan–Meier method, with comparisons performed across key exposure groups, including displacement trajectory and dialysis frequency. To assess the robustness of these findings, a multivariable Cox proportional hazards model was applied as a sensitivity analysis, with particular attention to the potential impact of informative censoring.

Individuals who could not be contacted after multiple attempts were classified as lost to follow-up and censored at their last verified point of contact. Given the likelihood of non-random loss to follow-up in conflict settings, additional sensitivity analyses were conducted to evaluate its potential influence on survival estimates, in line with established methodological approaches in humanitarian epidemiology [3, 11].

Missing data were not imputed due to variability in data sources and collection conditions. Given the observational design and the potential for residual confounding from factors such as socioeconomic status and baseline health characteristics, the findings are interpreted as associations rather than causal effects. Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided p-value < 0.05.

Reporting standards

This study was conducted and reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines [23].

Results

Study population and baseline characteristics

Immediately prior to the onset of armed conflict, 158 adults were receiving maintenance hemodialysis. Clinical outcomes were confirmed for 122 individuals (77.2%), while 36 (22.8%) remained untraceable despite repeated contact attempts and were censored at their last verified contact. Baseline characteristics are presented in Table 1, with emphasis on patients with confirmed outcomes, who constitute the primary analytic cohort.

Among patients with confirmed outcomes, the median age was 58 years (IQR 44–69), and 76 (62.3%) were male. In the overall cohort, the median age was 55 (IQR 40–65) years, and 104 (65.8%) were male. Diabetes mellitus and cardiovascular disease were more common among patients with confirmed outcomes, while hypertension prevalence was similar across groups.

Dialysis duration was longer in patients with confirmed outcomes (median 4.1 [IQR 2.2–7.2] years). Prior to the conflict, most patients received thrice-weekly dialysis (82.0% in the confirmed group), and access to erythropoiesis-stimulating agents and routine laboratory monitoring was generally higher in this group.

In contrast, individuals who were lost to follow-up tended to be younger, had a shorter duration of dialysis, and exhibited fewer comorbid conditions. They were also less likely to have received consistent dialysis, erythropoiesis-stimulating agents, or routine laboratory monitoring prior to the conflict. Variations in vascular access were noted, with lower use of arteriovenous fistulas and higher catheter dependence in this group.

These differences suggest that loss to follow-up was not random. Accordingly, analyses restricted to patients with confirmed outcomes should be interpreted as descriptive rather than causal.

Unadjusted survival by displacement and dialysis continuity

Among patients with confirmed outcomes (n = 122), 65 (53.3%) were alive at the end of follow-up, while 57 (46.7%) had died. Crude survival proportions and relative risks are presented in Table 2.

Information on cause of death was incomplete and derived primarily from caregiver reports and available clinical context. In the absence of systematic diagnostic confirmation during the conflict, cause-specific mortality was not formally analyzed and is therefore presented descriptively. The reported estimates reflect observed outcomes among patients with confirmed follow-up.

Documented survival differed across displacement trajectories, with the highest survival observed among patients who evacuated abroad early (81.8%), followed by those achieving internal settlement (53.3%) and those undergoing secondary or tertiary displacement (34.6%). Patients who remained in the capital under active conflict had the lowest survival (13.8%). Compared with patients who remained in the capital, early evacuation abroad was accompanied by a higher observed probability of survival (RR 5.93, 95% CI 2.34–15.05).

Dialysis continuity during follow-up was also associated with survival. Patients receiving at least two dialysis sessions per week had higher observed survival than those receiving one or fewer sessions or emergency-only dialysis (68.8% vs. 43.2%; RR 1.59, 95% CI 1.15–2.20).

Sensitivity time-to-event analysis

A multivariable Cox proportional hazards model including all enrolled patients (n = 158) was conducted as a sensitivity analysis, with death defined as the event and right-censoring applied to patients who were alive or untraceable at last contact (Table 3).

Within this model, lower dialysis frequency and adverse displacement trajectories were associated with higher mortality. In contrast, early relocation outside the country was associated with lower mortality. Increasing age was associated with a modest increase in mortality risk (HR 1.02 per year, 95% CI 1.00–1.04), whereas diabetes mellitus was not independently associated after adjustment.

These findings are presented as sensitivity analyses and should be interpreted cautiously given the exploratory nature of the model and the limited number of outcome events relative to the number of covariates.

Post-conflict care continuity among survivors

Among surviving patients with confirmed outcomes (n = 65), substantial disruption of routine care was observed (Table 4). Continuous access to erythropoiesis-stimulating agents was reported in 33.8% of patients, while 63.1% retained access to basic laboratory monitoring. Extended laboratory testing was available to 38.5% of patients, whereas parathyroid hormone testing was available in only 13.8%.

Among patients with diabetes, 65.9% reported continued access to antidiabetic therapy.

Financial burden during displacement

Direct patient-borne healthcare expenditures varied across host countries and phases of displacement (Table 5), including costs related to dialysis sessions, essential medications, transportation, and temporary accommodation where applicable.

During the early displacement period (first 2–3 months), monthly expenditures ranged from USD 720–3,600 across destinations. After partial stabilization (> 3 months), costs decreased slightly but remained substantial, ranging from USD 500–3,500. These estimates represent reported cost ranges and should be interpreted as descriptive.

Discussion

This study provides a detailed assessment of survival among adults receiving long-term hemodialysis during the armed conflict in Sudan and characterizes the clinical, logistical, and economic conditions under which survival was sustained.

Survival was observed under conditions characterized by adaptive strategies, including displacement and reduced dialysis frequency; however, these findings reflect associations within a constrained environment and should not be interpreted as causal. For dialysis-dependent individuals, survival during armed conflict therefore represents a fragile equilibrium between physiological requirements and systemic constraints.

Differences in survival were observed across displacement trajectories. Patients with confirmed outcomes were older and had a higher burden of comorbidity. It is plausible that individuals with greater perceived vulnerability were more likely to seek early evacuation, which may have influenced the observed distribution of survival across displacement groups. Patients who achieved early relocation to relatively stable host countries demonstrated substantially higher survival, despite rarely restoring full thrice-weekly dialysis schedules. Survival in this group was observed alongside maintenance of at least twice-weekly dialysis, access to essential medications, and a more stable care environment. Similar patterns have been described in disaster and conflict settings, where partial restoration of dialysis services reduces early mortality compared with complete interruption [24–27].

In contrast, patients who remained in the capital during the early phase of hostilities experienced markedly lower survival. This is consistent with abrupt loss of dialysis access and rapid onset of life-threatening metabolic complications following treatment interruption, as reported in dialysis-dependent populations [9, 24].

Internal displacement alone was not consistently associated with improved survival. Patients relocating within Sudan often faced overcrowded facilities and strict rationing, which frequently restricted dialysis to once-weekly or emergency-only sessions. While these strategies enabled short-term survival, they were associated with increased complications and delayed mortality. Comparable patterns have been reported in other conflict-affected dialysis populations [16, 24, 25]. Differences in mortality were observed according to dialysis frequency, with lower mortality among patients receiving at least two sessions per week [23, 28, 29].

The financial burden of survival was considerable. Patients who relocated internationally often incurred substantial out-of-pocket expenses, particularly during early displacement. Even where informal or charitable support was available, families remained responsible for dialysis fees, medications, transport, accommodation, and management of comorbid conditions. As a result, twice-weekly dialysis appeared to represent a financially driven compromise, with frequent discontinuation of supportive treatments such as erythropoiesis-stimulating agents. Comparable financial constraints have been reported among displaced individuals undergoing dialysis in settings without adequate health coverage [14, 30].

Limitations in laboratory monitoring further highlight that survival was often maintained at the expense of long-term disease management. Limited access to routine investigations and near absence of parathyroid hormone testing resulted in a reactive care model focused on acute complications rather than prevention of anemia, mineral bone disease, and cardiovascular risk. Comparable care patterns have been reported in humanitarian nephrology settings and are associated with cumulative morbidity over time [13, 14].

The findings of this study should be interpreted within the context of pre-existing structural limitations in dialysis care delivery in Sudan. Although dialysis sessions were nominally provided without direct charge, prior evidence indicates incomplete adherence to clinical practice guidelines and variability in care quality. In particular, essential adjunctive therapies such as erythropoiesis-stimulating agents and iron supplementation were not consistently available through dialysis units and often required out-of-pocket acquisition [6].

These underlying vulnerabilities likely intensified the impact of the conflict and may have contributed to observed disparities in treatment continuity and outcomes. The present findings therefore reflect a severe disruption of an already resource-constrained system rather than the emergence of entirely new deficiencies.

The findings also raise important concerns regarding equity. Survival was strongly influenced by financial resources, social support, and the ability to access cross-border care, rather than equitable availability of essential treatment. Similar inequities have been described in humanitarian settings and highlight persistent challenges in delivering life-sustaining therapies during crises [13, 17, 22].

From a policy perspective, these results underscore the need to recognize dialysis-dependent individuals as a high-priority group within humanitarian response planning. Establishing structured evacuation pathways, coordinated cross-border referral systems, simplified dialysis delivery models, and financial protection mechanisms for essential therapies may help reduce preventable mortality among conflict-affected populations requiring renal replacement therapy. Although international frameworks increasingly acknowledge dialysis as a non-deferrable intervention during emergencies, implementation remains inconsistent in protracted conflict settings [19–23].

This analysis has several methodological limitations. The study combined pre-conflict clinical data with post-conflict patient and caregiver reports, introducing potential recall bias and misclassification, particularly regarding dialysis frequency and expenditure estimates [12, 23, 30]. Data collection relied on decentralized communication networks following destruction of institutional infrastructure, which may have contributed to incomplete follow-up among highly mobile individuals [10–12]. Loss to follow-up was non-random, with untraceable patients tending to be younger, less comorbid, and more likely to rely on catheter-based access, suggesting potential underestimation of risk in vulnerable groups. Variation in follow-up intensity may have affected the completeness of outcome ascertainment. If untraceable patients experienced higher mortality during active conflict, survival estimates may be biased upward and should be interpreted cautiously [11, 12]. In addition, disruption of laboratory services precluded standardized biochemical assessment of dialysis adequacy, necessitating reliance on dialysis frequency as pragmatic surrogate indicators, consistent with disaster nephrology guidance [1, 2]. Given the observational design, causal inferences cannot be drawn. In addition, residual confounding from unmeasured factors, including baseline nutritional status and access to informal support networks, remains possible.

Conclusion

Survival among long-term hemodialysis patients during the armed conflict in Sudan was rarely synonymous with adequate chronic disease management. Instead, it reflected resilience, social support, displacement opportunity, and the capacity to sustain minimal dialysis exposure during systemic disruption. For most survivors, care continued under conditions of reduced dialysis frequency, inconsistent medication access, limited monitoring, and substantial financial burden. These findings highlight the urgent need for conflict-adapted renal care strategies that prioritize protection of dialysis infrastructure, equitable access to treatment, coordinated cross-border systems, and financial risk protection for patients dependent on life-sustaining therapies.
