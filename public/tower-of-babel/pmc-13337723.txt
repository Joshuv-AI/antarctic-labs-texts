The Editor,

We thank the authors for their thoughtful engagement with our viewpoint titled “Hepatic Elastography in Critical Illness: Moving from Physiological Signal to Clinical Utility.” We appreciate the opportunity to further clarify and expand on several important considerations regarding the role of hepatic elastography in critically ill patients.

Hepatic elastography is an exciting and promising critical care tool. Its real strength lies in being a dynamic, noninvasive marker that reflects the complex interplay of venous congestion, inflammation, and perfusion abnormalities seen in critical illness. Rather than serving as a standalone diagnostic test, its greatest value is likely as part of a broader, multimodal assessment that helps clinicians better understand the patient's evolving physiology.1

Liver stiffness can be influenced by multiple factors, including underlying chronic liver disease—limits its reliability when used in isolation. This underscores the need to interpret elastography findings within the wider clinical context, ideally alongside complementary tools such as venous Doppler and integrated hemodynamic assessment or maybe with any new scoring system.2,3

The intensive care unit (ICU) environment itself introduces additional complexity. Factors such as mechanical ventilation, vasoactive support, intra-abdominal pressure, and even patient positioning can significantly influence liver stiffness measurements. In this setting, reliance on single absolute values may be misleading; instead, greater emphasis should be placed on standardizing acquisition protocols and interpreting serial trends over time.1,4 Future work should focus on defining context-specific reference ranges and understanding how changes over time relate to critically ill patients. We also agree that applying stiffness thresholds derived from outpatient populations may not be appropriate for them.

Finally, while early data suggest potential roles in monitoring congestion and guiding fluid management, robust outcome-based evidence remains limited. Well-designed prospective studies are needed to determine whether hepatic elastography can meaningfully influence clinical decisions and improve patient outcomes.2,4

In summary, we support the authors' cautious optimism. Hepatic elastography holds considerable promise, but its role in routine practice will depend on careful validation, standardization, and thoughtful integration into clinical decision-making. For now, it is best viewed as a valuable adjunct—one that complements sound clinical judgment.
