Midterm Outcomes of Revision Total Hip Arthroplasty With Cementation of a Monoblock Dual-Mobility Bearing Cup in a Porous Revision Acetabular Shell
Authors: Sophia S. Antonioli, Garrett Ruff, Farouk Khury, Vinay K. Aggarwal, Joshua C. Rozell, Ran Schwarzkopf
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDual-mobility (DM) articulations and porous acetabular shells are increasingly used to address instability and mechanical loosening in revision total hip arthroplasty (rTHA). However, reports of longer-term outcomes with the new generation of DM articulations and porous shells remain limited. Our study reports the use and outcomes of a cemented monoblock DM cup in a fully porous acetabular shell in complex rTHA cases with midterm follow-up.MethodsA retrospective study was conducted of rTHAs with an acetabular construct of a monoblock DM cup cemented into a fully porous acetabular shell between June 2016 and December 2019. Baseline demographics, operative information, and outcomes were gathered for a total of 55 patients. Ten patients died, and 4 were lost to follow-up at 5 years; 41 patients were included in the analysis of midterm outcomes.ResultsThe most common indications for rTHA were acetabular component loosening (43.6%), periprosthetic joint infection (PJI) (16.4%), dislocation (12.7%), and periprosthetic fracture (9.1%). Preoperative acetabular bone loss assessment found 43.7% type IIA, 9.1% IIB, 20.0% IIC, 23.6% IIIA, and 3.6% IIIB according to the Paprosky classification. Six patients (10.9%) were readmitted within 90 days for PJI (n = 5; 9.1%) or dislocation (n = 1; 1.8%). Four patients (9.8%) required revision of the construct: 2 for PJI, 1 case of instability, and 1 case of aseptic loosening of the acetabular component with significant acetabular bone loss. The average time to revision was 1.4 years (range, 0.1–3.8 years). The mean follow-up period was 6.4 years (range, 4.7–8.2 years). The cup in cup construct had an all-cause survivorship of 95.8% and 90.0% at 1 and 5 years, respectively, and aseptic survivorship rates of 97.9% and 94.7% at 1 and 5 years, respectively.ConclusionsThis cohort of 55 patients who underwent complex rTHA with a monoblock DM cup cemented into a porous acetabular shell exhibited reliable fixation and low revision rates. In complex revision cases where instability and inadequate fixation are concerns, these outcomes support continued consideration and use of this unique and durable construct.

Introduction

Revision total hip arthroplasty (rTHA) continues to result in high costs to the healthcare system [[1], [2], [3]]. As the rates of primary THA continue to rise [4,5], rTHA incidence is expected to increase by 42% and 101% from 2019 to 2040 and to 2060, respectively [6]. As instability is the most common indication for revision and re-revision after THA [[7], [8], [9]], it is increasingly important for surgeons to have versatile implants to manage complex revision cases, improve implant stability, and minimize the risk of re-revision.

Current techniques to manage instability during rTHA include utilizing constrained acetabular liners, large femoral heads, and dual-mobility (DM) articulations [10,11]. Constrained liners effectively lock the femoral head into the liner, thus improving stability but at the detriment of range of motion [12,13]. Constrained liners increase the forces on the liner-cup interface, predisposing these implants to higher catastrophic failure rates, limiting their use to patients with low-demand activities [[10], [11], [12]]. Conversely, the use of large femoral heads increases the head-neck ratio, decreases impingement, and increases jump distance, all theoretically limiting rates of dislocation [11,14,15]. However, upsizing the femoral head can increase volumetric polyethylene wear, increase cantilever trunnion forces, and increase fracture risk of the correspondingly thinner acetabular liner [11,16,17]. Thus, there has been renewed interest in DM articulations to reduce dislocation risks without the complication profiles of constrained acetabular liners or large femoral heads.

DM articulations consist of an articulation between the prosthetic femoral head, polyethylene outer head, and the acetabular shell or liner, depending on implant design. This design effectively decreases impingement through the added articulation between the smaller and larger heads, while also increasing the jump distance due to the large diameter polyethylene head [18,19]. Current generations of DM constructs have shown impressively low dislocation rates in primary and revision THA, with low rates of mechanical failure [[20], [21], [22], [23]]. Cementing a DM cup into a porous metal acetabular shell is an increasingly popular option to manage patients at high risk of recurrent instability after rTHA, although outcomes have been mixed and primarily limited to short-term outcomes [20,24,25]. This study aims to analyze midterm all-cause and aseptic survivorship, as well as the complication rate and functional outcomes of an acetabular construct consisting of a DM cup cemented into a highly porous revision acetabular shell. The combination of host bone contact of the outer porous shell combined with the customized anatomic placement and inherent advantages of the DM articulation may decrease instability without the corresponding increase in mechanical failure of complex rTHA constructs in a high-risk patient population.

Material and methods

Study design and cohort selection

Institutional review board approval (i17-01223) was obtained prior to study commencement. The aim of this large retrospective case series was to investigate and report on the use and outcomes of cemented monoblock DM cups in fully porous acetabular shells in complex rTHA cases with midterm follow-up. The decision to utilize this construct was based on surgeon preference for cases deemed high-risk for dislocation, or those requiring cementation due to compromised host bone quality within the shell. Intraoperatively, the initial porous nonmodular acetabular shell is impacted and secured using both nonlocking and at least 1 locking screw. Next, either bone wax or implant specific hole covers are placed over all of the screw heads and empty screw holes. One batch (40 g) of polymethylmethacrylate bone cements, either high or low viscosity depending on surgeon preference, is then mixed. The inner cemented DM cup is impacted and held in appropriate inclination and version to achieve maximal hip stability until the cement cures and fixation is achieved.

A retrospective review of cases was conducted at 2 large, urban, academic medical centers to identify patients who received this acetabular construct during complex rTHA. Patients were included if they received the construct of a monoblock DM cup (POLARCUP, Smith & Nephew, Memphis, TN, USA) cemented into a fully porous, nonmodular acetabular shell (REDAPT, Smith & Nephew, Memphis, TN, USA) during revision THA, as seen in the Figure 1. Patients were excluded if the date of surgery was within 5 years from the date of data collection. A final cohort of 55 patients met inclusion criteria with surgery dates between June 2, 2016, and December 4, 2019.Figure 1Radiographs of staged bilateral rTHA with novel construct. Data collection and outcome measures

Patient baseline characteristics including age, sex, body mass index, race, smoking status, American Society of Anesthesiologists class, Charlson Comorbidity Index, and preoperative ambulatory status were collected from the electronic medical record (EPIC, Verona, Wisconsin, USA). Perioperative information was also collected by chart review including the Paprosky classification for acetabular bone loss, indication for rTHA, laterality of procedure, operative approach, type of anesthesia utilized, surgical time, estimate blood loss, transfusions, length of stay, and discharge disposition. The Paprosky classification was determined intraoperatively, these data were then gathered from operative notes. Surgical information was collected by chart review including implant size and manufacturer, which components were revised in addition to the acetabular construct, and whether additional surgical techniques (allograft, screws, extended trochanteric osteotomy, acetabular cage) were used. Postoperative outcomes were captured, including emergency department (ED) visits and readmission within the 90-day postoperative period, as well as reoperations or revisions within the follow-up interval and ambulatory status at latest follow-up.

Data analyses

The primary outcome of this study was survivorship of the construct at a minimum of 5 years postoperatively. All 55 patients were included in the evaluation of patient baseline characteristics, surgical information, and short-term outcomes. Ten patients died, and 4 were lost to follow-up before reaching 5 years post-rTHA, resulting in 41 patients being included in the analysis of midterm outcomes. Data cleaning and analysis was performed using Excel software (Microsoft Corporation, Richmond, WA, USA) and Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) (Version 28; IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables were reported as means and ranges. Categorical variables were expressed as counts and percentages. A Kaplan-Meier plot was utilized to assess all-cause and aseptic revision survival rates of the novel construct over time. The Kaplan-Meier survival analysis was employed to estimate survivorship due to the small number of events, which precluded the use of a multivariable semiparametric model. Mean construct survivorship was estimated by calculating the area under the Kaplan-Meier curve and reported with its 95% confidence interval and standard error.

Results

Patient demographics and baseline characteristics

Within the 55 patients included in the study, the average age was 63.2 years old, and the cohort consisted of 29 females (52.7%) and 26 males (47.3%). The average body mass index was 29.6 kg/m2, and the majority of the cohort (61.8%) was white. Twenty-two (40.0%) patients were never smokers, 28 (50.9%) were former smokers, and 5 (9.1%) were current smokers. Regarding comorbid status, most patients were American Society of Anesthesiologists class II (25, 45.5%) or III (28, 50.9%), and the average Charlson Comorbidity Index was 2.9 (range, 0-12). Preoperatively, most patients ambulated with either a cane (23, 41.8%) or a rolling walker (15, 27.3%). Regarding intraoperative evaluation of acetabular bone stock, 24 patients (43.7%) were Paprosky classification IIA, 5 (9.1%) were IIB, 11 (20.0%) were IIC, 13 (23.6%) were IIIA, and 2 (3.6%) were IIIB. The indications for complex rTHA varied, including 24 patients (43.6%) undergoing surgery for aseptic loosening of the acetabular component, 9 (16.4%) for periprosthetic joint infection (PJI), 6 (10.9%) for periprosthetic fracture including 2 acetabular fractures (3.6%) and 4 femur fractures (7.3%), and 7 (12.7%) for hip instability. All patients diagnosed with PJI underwent surgical management (including either 1- or 2-stage revision).

Ten patients (18.2%) received intraoperative or postoperative blood transfusions. The average length of stay was 5.1 days (range, 0.6–20.1 days). Of the 55 patients, 40 (72.7%) were discharged home, 14 (25.5%) were discharged to a skilled nursing facility, and 1 (1.8%) was discharged to an acute rehabilitation facility. Clinical outcomes

All 55 patients were included in the evaluation of short-term clinical outcomes. Six patients (10.9%) reported to the ED within 90 days postoperatively, 4 (7.3%) due to surgical complications, and 2 (3.6%) due to nonsurgical complications. The surgical complications included 2 cases of wound drainage (3.6%), 1 case of postoperative pain (1.8%), and 1 case of periprosthetic fracture (1.8%). The nonsurgical complications leading to ED visits included 1 case of gastrointestinal upset and 1 case of angina. Eight patients (14.5%) were readmitted to the hospital within 90 days of the index rTHA, 6 (10.9%) of which were related to surgery. These included 5 cases (9.1%) of infection and 1 case (1.8%) of hip dislocation. The nonsurgical complications leading to readmissions included 1 case of gastrointestinal upset and 1 case of aseptic loosening of the contralateral THA.

Forty-one patients were included in the evaluation of midterm outcomes after 14 patients were removed from this analysis since they died or were lost to follow-up before reaching the 5 years post-THA time point. These 14 patients did not have revisions or failures of the construct at their latest follow-up. Of the 41 patients with at least 5 years follow-up, 5 patients (12.2%) underwent nonrevision reoperation procedures including 4 irrigation and debridements (9.8%) and one open reduction and internal fixations of a distal femur fracture (2.4%). Six patients (14.6%) with 5 years follow-up underwent a re-rTHA at a mean of 1.5 years after the initial rTHA, due to PJI (n = 3, 7.3%), aseptic loosening of the acetabular component (n = 1, 2.4%), instability (n = 1, 2.4%), and periprosthetic femur fracture (n = 1, 2.4%). Four of the 6 patients who underwent a re-rTHA included revisions of the acetabular component. The indications for the acetabular component revisions included 2 cases of PJI, 1 case of instability where only the DM cemented cup was revised, and 1 case of aseptic loosening of the fully porous shell with significant acetabular bone loss. The revision for instability occurred in a patient with Paprosky classification IIIA and the revision for aseptic loosening of the acetabular component occurred in a patient with Paprosky classification IIIA. The femoral stem was not revised in these 2 cases of aseptic failure. The average time to revision of these acetabular components was 1.5 years (range, 0.1–3.5 years). Survivorship

This acetabular construct of a monoblock DM cup cemented into a fully porous acetabular shell had all-cause survivorship rates of 95.8% and 90% at 1 and 5 years, respectively, and aseptic survivorship rates of 97.9% and 94.7% at 1 and 5 years, respectively. Discussion

This study assessed the longevity of a monoblock DM cup cemented into a fully porous revision acetabular shell for complex rTHA with moderate-to-severe acetabular bone loss. Overall, the construct exhibited an impressive all-cause survivorship rate of 95.8% at 1 year and 90% at 5 years. Short-term complications within 90 days were mainly due to PJI, accounting for 9.1% of cases while dislocation was less frequent at 1.8%. All-time acetabular cup revisions were primarily indicated for PJI, instability, and aseptic loosening, with an average time to revision of 1.5 years. At the final follow-up, significant functional improvements were reported, as the number of unassisted ambulators increasing from 12 preoperatively to 22 postoperatively. Although previous biomechanical and retrospective studies [24,[26], [27], [28]] have reported this construct to be robust in the short-term, our findings support the midterm construct’s effectiveness in restoring mobility and maintaining durable fixation in complex rTHA.

Recurrent instability is the most common cause of failure requiring repeat revision following rTHA [7,29]. A number of surgical treatment modalities have been proposed to decrease the incidence of this devastating complication in high-risk patients, including the use of DM bearings [[30], [31], [32], [33]]. The unconstrained, dual-articulated design of the DM implant enhances stability by effectively increasing the femoral head size while increasing the range of motion to impingement and jump distance. Although there were initial concerns regarding increased early implant wear from the additional bearing surface, as well as potential risks of aseptic loosening and intraprosthetic dislocation, subsequent evidence has largely mitigated these fears, as recent data have shown a 97.7% survivorship rate and a low 0.3% aseptic loosening and 0.7% intraprosthetic dislocation rates [21]. While one patient in our study had subsequent aseptic loosening, no intraprosthetic dislocations were observed. Despite the high long-term survivorship documented with DM in rTHA [[30], [31], [32], [33], [34]], a fraction of high-risk patients will continue to experience dislocation, particularly since the dislocation rate in rTHA has been reported to be almost 3 times as high as in primary THA, reaching up to 35% [35]. This persistent risk, despite enhanced implant designs, suggests that more complex constructs may offer unique benefits in further reducing dislocation rates.

Blumenfeld et al. described an early “cup-in-cup” method of a cementing a second tantalum shell into a well-fixed porous tantalum shell with the hope of restoring offset in severe acetabular protrusio defects. This study resulted in no evidence of loosening within a 28-month period [36]. Many studies have since described a variation of this cup-in-cup technique which includes cementation of a standard polyethylene liner into the larger shell. This construct is well investigated in the literature with reports of dislocation rates of 11.3% at 7.7 years [37], 17% at 10 years [38], and 16% at 10 years [39].

Our study investigated a similar but different construct in which the cemented “inner” cup is a DM cup, and the bearing surface includes the DM articulation instead of a standard polyethylene liner. Our analysis demonstrated that the use of a cemented DM cup in an acetabular shell construct resulted in a relatively low dislocation rate of 2.4% (1 out of 41 cases) (Table 5), even when compared to the dislocation rates found in long-term studies of cemented standard polyethylene liners, as described above. The excellent survivorship and low dislocation rates observed in this study are notable, particularly given the complexity of the patient cohort. Additionally, our results exhibit favorable outcomes when comparing long-term survivorship of rTHA with cemented constrained liners. Brown et al. reported a dislocation rate in the stated population of 18%, PJI rate of 11%, and overall revision rate of 24% at 7 years follow-up [40], each metric inferior to the findings of our study that involved cementation of a DM articulation.

While a previous short-term study by Chen et al. investigating cemented DM liners in a similar cohort reported a 2.6% dislocation rate and an 8.6% PJI rate [41], the current study, with a larger cohort, showed a comparable 1.8% dislocation rate and a 9.1% PJI rate within 90 days. The incidence of PJI observed in our study (9.1%) and the study by Chen et al. (8.6%) [41] is likely due to the complexity of this patient population and the high number of patients undergoing rerevision procedures. Wilson et al. investigated the same construct but reported a dislocation rate of 7.7% at 2 years [42]. Bellova et al. reported a dislocation rate of 6.1% [43], however investigated cases in which the DM cup was cemented directly into an already well-fixed cup. Compared to Wilson et al. and Bellova et al., our study’s and Chen et al.’s dislocation rates were low. This study's findings therefore support the continued use of this unique technique of a cemented DM cup in a porous acetabular shell construct as a promising solution for complex rTHA, particularly in patients at high risk for instability.

Other than instability, revision THA is frequently necessitated by complications such as aseptic loosening and PJI [44]. These same complications often lead to subsequent re-rTHAs, further increasing the burden on patients and healthcare systems. The patient cohort investigated in this study presented with a high degree of acetabular bone loss, with nearly half (47.2%) classified as Paprosky type IIC, IIIA, or IIIB. While the majority of our cohort included Paprosky type II defects, the inclusion of 15 cases (27.2%) of type IIIA/IIIB bone loss (Table 1) allows for limited assessment of construct durability in the face of severe defects, a finding of value given the established outcomes for less severe bone loss. Notably, both cases of aseptic failure in our cohort, representing 1 case of instability and 1 case of aseptic loosening, occurred in patients with Paprosky type IIIA defects. However, patients with Paprosky type IIIA defects represented 23.6% of our cohort, and it is unclear if this specific defect pattern represents a particular challenge for this acetabular system; future studies with larger cohorts and longer follow-up are needed to further investigate this. Surgeries addressing aseptic loosening are particularly challenging and frequently necessitate more than one revision procedure to achieve functional recovery and adequate stability [7]. Because of the severity of bone loss and defects seen in this study, the operating surgeons determined that a fully porous revision shell was necessary instead of a modular acetabular cup to ensure appropriate fixation to host bone and customizable options for locking and nonlocking screw trajectory. Furthermore, while other cup designs and custom-made implants may achieve similar results [[45], [46], [47]], cost may be prohibitive, and existing literature is limited with few comparative studies available. Despite the prevalence of moderate-to-severe bone loss, bone allograft was utilized in only 47.2% of cases to fill cavitary voids. The observed high survivorship rates, with only 4.9% of the patients (2 of 41) requiring cup revision due to aseptic causes, despite this varied use of allograft, suggest the construct's ability to achieve stable fixation even with limited bone stock. Given the high proportion of patients presenting with moderate-to-severe bone loss and the inherent complexity of their revision cases, the specific construct investigated appears to offer superior stability. This is likely attributable to the unique surgical technique, which facilitates optimal placement of the porous shell for maximal host bone contact to facilitate ingrowth, and subsequent cementing of the DM cup in an ideal “safe-zone” position for hip stability [24]. Beyond the clinical benefits of improved stability and reduced revision rates, the economic implications of utilizing DM constructs warrant further consideration. Although DM implants typically incur a higher upfront cost compared to traditional fixed-bearing implants, their use in complex rTHA cases may be economically justified. Studies have shown that DM THA can be cost-effective, and even cost-saving, especially in younger patient populations and over long follow-up intervals [48,49]. The significant reduction in dislocation risk offered by DM (from 8-10% with traditional implants to 0.5-2% with DM) in rTHA directly translates into fewer costly rerevisions [48]. The same argument could be made regarding the higher up-front cost of utilizing 2 acetabular implants, as done in the cases described in this study. Higher up-front costs may still be cost-effective when considering the relatively low dislocation and revision rates we observed in this high-risk population. It is, however, worth recognizing that a comparison of cost between this construct and fixed-bearing implants or traditional DM implants could be argued to be irrelevant as, by nature of utilizing this unique construct, it has been deemed unlikely that traditional constructs can offer the specific inclination and version to achieve improved implant stability required in this high-risk patient population.

Limitations

This study, while providing valuable midterm data, is subject to several limitations that warrant consideration. The exclusive use of a single type of fully porous acetabular shell and DM cup means that the results may not be directly generalizable to other implant designs or constructs. A notable limitation is the attrition of 14 patients, representing 25.5% of the initial cohort, who either died (10 patients) or were lost to follow-up (4 patients) before reaching the 5-year post-rTHA time point. This patient attrition introduces a potential for attrition bias, as the revision status of patients lost to follow-up could not be definitively ascertained, suggesting the reported survivorship rates may represent an upper-bound estimate. Finally, the study does not include a direct comparison of the outcomes of this construct with other established revision options, such as constrained liners or large femoral heads, in a randomized fashion.

Conclusions

This midterm study of 55 patients undergoing complex rTHA with a monoblock DM cup cemented into a porous acetabular shell demonstrated reliable fixation and low revision rates. The construct achieved excellent all-cause and aseptic survivorship rates at both 1 and 5 years, indicating its utility and durability in a challenging patient population characterized by significant acetabular bone loss.
