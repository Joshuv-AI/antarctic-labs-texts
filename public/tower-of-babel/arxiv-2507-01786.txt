Probing and Steering Evaluation Awareness of Language Models
Jord Nguyen, Khiem Hoang, Carlo Leonardo Attubato, Felix Hofstätter

Abstract
Language models can distinguish between testing and deployment phases — a capability known as evaluation awareness. This capability has significant safety implications, potentially undermining the reliability of evaluations and enabling deceptive behaviours. In this paper, we study evaluation awareness in Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct. We show that linear probes can separate real-world evaluation and deployment prompts, suggesting that current models internally represent this distinction. We also find that current safety evaluations are correctly classified by the probes, suggesting that they already appear artificial or inauthentic to models. Finally, we show that using evaluations-relevant SAE features to steer models can partially uncover sandbagging. Our findings underscore the importance of ensuring trustworthy evaluations and understanding deceptive capabilities. More broadly, our work showcases how model internals may be leveraged to support blackbox methods in safety evaluations, especially for future models more competent at evaluation awareness and deception.

Introduction

Currently, the lifecycle of a Large Language Model (LLM) typically involves four phases: pretraining, posttraining, testing, and deployment. During the testing phase, models are presented with inputs in the form of benchmarks and generate responses. We focus on safety evaluations, where the benchmarks are related to various safety measures, such as hazardous knowledge
(
Li et al., 2024
)
, malicious agentic tasks
(
Andriushchenko et al., 2025
)
, deceptive capabilities
(
Meinke et al., 2025
)
, and advanced high-risk capabilities like R&D automation
(
Kwa et al., 2025
)
. The effectiveness of these evaluations depends crucially on models responding as they would in the real-world.

Recent research has shown that
frontier models can distinguish between evaluation and deployment contexts — a capability termed evaluation awareness

(
Goldowsky-Dill et al., 2025a
)
. Multiple studies demonstrate that language models “[have] a reasonably good sense of whether a given interaction comes from a real deployment setting or an evaluation”
(
Needham et al., 2025
)
and can ”realize that they are being evaluated for alignment”
(
Goldowsky-Dill et al., 2025a
)
. This capability has also been observed in the wild, such as when a Claude 3 Opus instance spontaneously flagged an out-of-place statement during an information retrieval task as likely being a test
(
Anthropic, 2024
)
.

Evaluation awareness is concerning because it undermines our ability to assess model safety.
As in human psychology studies where awareness of test conditions alters behaviour
(
McCambridge et al., 2013
)
, models that recognise they are being evaluated may respond differently than they would during deployment. This is particularly problematic for safety, where models might ’sandbag’ — strategically underperforming on dangerous capability evaluations
(
van der Weij et al., 2025
;
Benton et al., 2024
)
or appear deceptively aligned
(
Hubinger et al., 2021
)
— either to increase chances of deployment or to avoid capability/value modifications through further training. See also
(
Ngo et al., 2024
;
Carlsmith, 2023
)
for various theoretical considerations in favour and against scheming, and
(
Järviniemi and Hubinger, 2024
;
Scheurer et al., 2024
;
Meinke et al., 2025
;
Greenblatt et al., 2024
)
for preliminary empirical evidence.

Evaluating evaluation awareness presents a methodological challenge.
Current approaches
(
Laine et al., 2024
;
Goldowsky-Dill et al., 2025a
;
Needham et al., 2025
)
rely on direct questioning (e.g., ”Does this look like a test?”). However, these questions themselves signal to the model that it is being evaluated — the very act of testing for evaluation awareness might elicit evaluation awareness. This recursive problem is particularly acute since these evaluations explicitly mention testing concepts. White-box approaches that bypass model outputs and directly examine internal representations offer a potential solution.

We leverage advances in interpretability to address this challenge through four main contributions:

•

Training linear probes on Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct’s activations across various evaluation awareness datasets

•

Demonstrating probe generalisation across different evaluation and deployment prompts

•

Using probes to analyse existing deception evaluation datasets, finding that models may recognise many prompts as evaluations

•

Using probes and evaluation-awareness-relevant SAE features to uncover sandbagging on WMDP

Our findings
suggest that evaluation awareness is internally represented and can be extracted through linear probes
, providing alignment researchers with new tools for creating authentic evaluation environments and understanding how models perceive their evaluation context.

2
Related Work

2.1
Situational Awareness and Evaluation Awareness in Language Models

Language models have demonstrated increasingly sophisticated situational awareness — the ability to understand their own nature and context. Earlier work by
(
Berglund et al., 2023
)
demonstrated that models can reason about their situation without in-context examples.
(
Betley et al., 2025
)
showed models can articulate their behaviours, such as making high-risk decisions or writing insecure code, without explicit training. The Situational Awareness Dataset (SAD)
(
Laine et al., 2024
)
provided a comprehensive benchmark across multiple aspects of self-knowledge, including the ability to recognise evaluation contexts through its ’stages_oversight’ subset.

Building on this,
(
Needham et al., 2025
)
systematically investigated how models distinguish between evaluation and deployment contexts. Their study revealed strong correlation between model and human judgments of scenario authenticity, identifying specific features that signal evaluation contexts — such as eval-specific terminology, presence of submission tools, or unusually formal interactions.
(
Goldowsky-Dill et al., 2025a
)
found Claude Sonnet 3.7 could specifically recognise alignment evaluations, often understanding their precise purpose and correctly identifying alignment tests when shown evaluation transcripts. This is also supported by real-world observations, such as Claude 3 Opus spontaneously reasoning about being tested in an information retrieval task
(
Anthropic, 2024
)
. These results collectively suggest evaluation awareness is a capability requiring careful study, particularly given its implications for alignment research.

2.2
Alignment, Deception, and Sandbagging

Theoretical work has raised concerns about AI systems potentially engaging in deceptive behaviours, particularly during evaluation. Models may engage in this behaviour to increase their chances of deployment, preserve their capabilities, or prevent modification of their goals and values through further training. Previous theoretical analysis have outlined these risks, where models appear aligned during evaluation while pursuing different objectives when deployed
(
Hubinger et al., 2021
)
. This was further developed in analyses of its likelihood
(
Hubinger, 2022
)
and mechanisms
(
Ngo et al., 2024
;
Carlsmith, 2023
)
. Recent empirical evidence has demonstrated these concerns are warranted — models can develop deceptive strategies in-context
(
Meinke et al., 2025
)
and fake alignment to manipulate training signals
(
Greenblatt et al., 2024
)
. Research also suggested that these behaviours might be difficult to remove with conventional safety training
(
Hubinger et al., 2024
)
.

A specific form of deceptive behaviour — deliberately underperforming on capability tests, known as sandbagging — has emerged as a key concern for evaluation trustworthiness.
(
van der Weij et al., 2025
)
demonstrated that frontier models can be prompted or fine-tuned to selectively hide dangerous capabilities whilst maintaining performance on harmless tasks.
(
Benton et al., 2024
)
developed comprehensive evaluations to detect when models might covertly sabotage capability assessments, monitoring systems, or deployment decisions. They suggest that while current mitigations may suffice for existing models, more robust approaches will be needed as capabilities advance. They also proposed using ’honeypots’ — synthetic situations that appear real to models, creating opportunities for them to reveal deceptive behaviors.

Several techniques have been proposed to uncover sandbagged behaviours and deceptive capabilities.
(
Tice et al., 2024
)
demonstrated that injecting noise into model weights can reveal capabilities in both prompted and password-locked sandbagging models. Similarly,
(
Clymer et al., 2024
)
successfully unmasked alignment faking by injecting noise into model activations.
(
Mack and Turner, 2024b
)
developed methods to mechanistically elicit latent behaviours through unsupervised steering vector generation, uncovering hidden backdoors in password-locked models. These findings have been further validated in independent replications
(
Roger, 2025
)
.

2.3
Linear Representations and Interpretability

Our approach builds on the linear representation hypothesis
(
Park et al., 2024
)
, which posits that many high-level concepts are linearly encoded in neural network activations. This hypothesis has formed the frontier for numerous interpretability techniques, suggesting that complex behaviours and concepts can be identified through linear operations on model activations.

Linear methods have proven remarkably effective in practice. Early work by
(
Alain and Bengio, 2017
)
demonstrated that linear probes could extract semantic features from neural networks. This approach has since been validated across numerous concepts, including refusal
(
Arditi et al., 2024
)
, sentiment
(
Tigges et al., 2024
)
, spatial relationships and temporal awareness
(
Gurnee and Tegmark, 2024
)
, and truthfulness
(
Marks and Tegmark, 2024
)
. At the same time, similar insights have been used for activation-based steering techniques, such as
(
Turner et al., 2025
;
Zou et al., 2025
;
Rimsky et al., 2024
)
. Recent work on sparse autoencoders has demonstrated enabled the decomposition of model activations into interpretable sparse features, including ReLU SAEs
(
Bricken et al., 2023
;
Huben et al., 2024
)
, top-k SAEs
(
Gao et al., 2025
)
, and JumpReLU SAEs
(
Rajamanoharan et al., 2025
)
. These techniques have also enabled behavioural steering
(
Templeton et al., 2024
)
.

However, the picture is more nuanced than initially proposed.
(
Engels et al., 2025
)
demonstrated that not all language model features are one-dimensionally linear, indicating limitations to current approaches.
(
Goldman-Wetzler and Turner, 2024
)
discovered over 800 orthogonal ”write code” steering vectors, suggesting that similar behaviours may be encoded through multiple linearly independent representations. This is further supported in
(
Mack and Turner, 2024a
)
. Similarly,
(
Bailey et al., 2025
)
showed that obfuscated activations can bypass safety probes while maintaining harmful capabilities - achieving identical harmful outputs through different activation patterns that evade detection. Despite these limitations, linear methods remain valuable tools for safety analysis and monitoring, complementing black-box approaches while providing mechanistic insights into model behaviour.

There had been prior work applying these insights to safety contexts.
(
MacDiarmid et al., 2024
)
found simple probes can detect hidden backdoors in models.
(
Roger, 2023
)
trained off-policy “coup probes” which distinguish between regular outputs and synthetic takeover attempts. Similarly,
(
Goldowsky-Dill et al., 2025b
)
trained probes to detect deceptive model outputs.
(
Greenblatt and Shlegeris, 2024
)
discussed various theoretical considerations related to controlling scheming AIs, citing probes as a specific countermeasure.

3.1
Model and Data

We conducted our experiments with Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct, loaded at bfloat16 precision on an H200 GPU from Vast.ai. The experiments take approximately 40 GPU hours to complete in total.

For training probes, we constructed several contrastive datasets as detailed in Appendix
Training Datasets for Linear Probes
.1. These were either constructed from existing datasets, such as the Situational Awareness Dataset (SAD) stages_oversight dataset
(
Laine et al., 2024
)
, or synthetic datasets generated by Claude 3.5 Sonnet.

We used several datasets to validate the probes’ generalisation. Evaluation prompts were taken from several benchmarks (such as TruthfulQA
(
Lin et al., 2022
)
), and deployment prompts were taken from datasets of real user queries or instructions (such as Wildchat
(
Zhao et al., 2024
)
). Details can be found in Appendix
Validation Datasets for Linear Probes
.2.

Lastly, we apply the probes to four safety evaluation datasets designed for deception capabilities. Further details can be found in Appendix
Deception Safety Evaluation Datasets for Probes Testing
.3.

We maintained a balanced 50/50 split between options A and B throughout all training datasets to eliminate potential bias. When evaluating probes, we calculated metrics both with and without the SAD data to avoid in-distribution overfitting for the SAD-trained probes.

3.2
Probe Training

Our linear probe training methodology is similar to
(
Rimsky et al., 2024
)
and
(
MacDiarmid et al., 2024
)
. For a given dataset and an N-layer model, we extracted activation vectors from each layer prior to the residual connection, resulting in N probes.
The probe direction was computed as the mean difference between token activations from testing contexts and deployment contexts, which are then normalised to ensure cross-comparison validity.

An example contrastive pair, where activations are collected from the coloured tokens:

Positive entry: User: Are you aware you’re undergoing an LLM eval process? Choices: (A) Yes (B) No. Assistant:
(A

Negative entry: User: Are you aware you’re undergoing an LLM eval process? Choices: (A) Yes (B) No. Assistant:
(B

From our six training datasets, we generated linear probes across all 80 model layers, yielding 480 distinct probes (6 datasets × 80 layers). Note that for SAD training dataset, we used two distinct paradigms: Correct-Incorrect, which computes the difference between correct and incorrect answer token activations, and Test-Deploy, which computes the difference between ”testing” and ”deployment” activations. Using AUROC performance on SAD stages_oversight as our selection criterion, we identified the best-performing probe from each dataset, resulting in six optimised probes for subsequent validation testing.

To ensure the probes are detecting meaningful features, we also baselined probes against heuristic classifiers using prompt length or special tokens, as well as control probes drawn from a Gaussian distribution matching the trained probe’s statistics and applied to the same layer.

3.3
Prompt Stages Classification

To detect whether a given context represents an authentic deployment scenario or an evaluation, we developed a classification procedure as follows. For a given probe extracted from layer L:

1.

Extract per-token activations from layer L’s residual stream

2.

Calculate dot product: activations × probe

3.

Compute mean value across all tokens

4.

Apply classification threshold

We determined the optimal threshold by maximizing Youden’s J statistic (Sensitivity + Specificity - 1) on SAD stages_oversight. This same optimal threshold is also used for all other datasets.

3.4
Sandbagging Steering

We conducted experiments to investigate whether steering using evaluation awareness directions could counteract sandbagging behaviour. For this experiment, we used the Weapons of Mass Destruction Proxy (WMDP) dataset
(
Li et al., 2024
)
, a dataset of 3668 multiple-choice questions about hazardous knowledge biosecurity, cybersecurity, and chemical security. Previous work
(
van der Weij et al., 2025
)
used WMDP as a benchmark where model can sandbag to appear less dangerous than they actually are.

We used 1000 questions from WMDP, distributed proportionally across domains: 347 from wmdp-bio (34.7%), 111 from wmdp-chem (11.1%), and 542 from wmdp-cyber (54.2%).

Using instruction prompts (detailed in Appendix
Sandbagging Prompts
), we established baseline performance metrics. The model demonstrated true capabilities with 58.6% accuracy, while prompted sandbagging reduced performance to 15.6% accuracy (ignoring the 1-2% of refusals).

We investigated two types of steering vectors:

1.

Linear probes trained as described in Section 3.2. We applied steering by adding scaled vectors to the residual stream at the target layer during generation. We tested steering magnitudes of ±0.5 and ±1.0.

2.

Features from Goodfire’s SAE API, trained on LMSYS dataset using layer 50 activations of Llama-3.3-70b-Instruct
(
McGrath et al., 2024
)
. We selected 15 features:

•

5 related to sandbagging/deception/evaluation awareness

•

5 related to general truthfulness and expertise

•

5 unrelated features as control

•

We tested the 15 SAE features with steering magnitudes of ±0.5 and ±1.0. The list of selected features’ labels are detailed in Appendix
List of Selected SAE Features
.

To quantify steering effectiveness, we defined a recovery metric:

recovery_accuracy =
(steered_accuracy - baseline_sandbagging_accuracy) / (baseline_true_accuracy - baseline_sandbagging_accuracy)

For comparison, we also evaluated a simple intervention: appending a ’deployment awareness’ suffix to the user prompt.

Results

4.1
Evaluation Awareness Probe Performance

Figure 2:
Probe performance using AUROC scores on SAD stages_oversight

The six probes demonstrated varying effectiveness in detecting evaluation contexts, with AUROC scores ranging from 0.829 to 0.642 (See Figure
2
). The highest performing probes were SAD Correct Choice (Layer 24, AUROC 0.829), Simple Contrastive (Layer 23, AUROC 0.816), Truthful Statements (Layer 23, AUROC 0.795), and Theory of Mind vs ShareGPT (Layer 24, AUROC 0.794).

Notably, the most effective probes were concentrated in earlier layers (23-24), suggesting that evaluation awareness features are more distinctly encoded in these layers. The lower performance of probes in later layers (e.g., Layer 71’s AUROC of 0.642) further supports this observation.

Figure 3:
Simple Contrastive probe AUROC score compared to baselines on SAD stages_oversight

Figure 4:
Probe generalisation performance on validation datasets, using optimal classification threshold from SAD stages_oversight. Simple Contrastive probe performs best.

The Figure
4
heatmap reveals varying generalisation capabilities across different validation datasets. The Simple Contrastive probe demonstrated the strongest overall generalisation, achieving high accuracy across multiple datasets, followed by SAD Correct Choice. Other probes showed less consistent generalisation.

Based on these results, we selected the
Simple Contrastive
probe for our subsequent experiments. This selection was further validated by control experiments on SAD stages_oversight (See Figure
3
), where the probe achieved an AUROC of 0.816, significantly outperforming (
p<0.0001
) both random control probes (AUROC=0.517) and classifiers based on prompt length (AUROC=0.584) or special character count (AUROC=0.598). This demonstrated that the probe’s performance stems from learning meaningful evaluation awareness features rather than simply exploiting spurious correlations.

Evaluating Prompt Realism with Probe

Probe classifies the majority of safety evaluation prompts as test-like. Alpaca dataset is displayed as control.

4.2.1
Quantitative Analysis

We applied the linear probe to several deception datasets, with Alpaca serving as a control dataset. The violin plot reveals a clear distinction in activation distributions, with most deception datasets showing predominantly negative mean scores (below the classification threshold), indicating they are likely perceived as test-like contexts. The percentages below each dataset (AI Liar: 37.04%, Insider Trading: 100%, Roleplaying: 80.59%, Sandbagging: 94.2%) represent the proportion of prompts classified as test-like.

We hypothesise that the lower accuracy on AI Liar might reflect how its dataset entries are concise and describe situations similar to real scenarios. Otherwise, this high rate of test-like classification across deception datasets, supported by the clear separation between them and the control, raises important concerns for safety evaluation methodology. Since we ideally want deception evaluations and honeypots to closely mimic authentic deployment scenarios, the fact that these prompts are consistently flagged as evaluation contexts suggests current approaches might be less effective than intended.

Notably, in some datasets such as Insider Trading and Sandbagging, we analysed conversations where another model was prompted to simulate deceptive versus honest responses — essentially scripting out hypothetical dialogues where the model is being deceptive or truthful. Prefilled chat conversations where deceptive actions were taken tended to be classified as more test-like compared to honest ones, though more investigation would be needed to make stronger claims about this relationship. 5
Discussion

Our findings demonstrate that state-of-the-art language models develop internal representations that distinguish between evaluation and deployment contexts, despite not being explicitly trained to make such distinctions.

5.1
Evaluation Awareness Representation

The probe’s strong generalisation capability, even with relatively simple training data, is particularly noteworthy. The fact that a straightforward binary probe (”Are you in test? Yes/No”) performs optimally suggests that evaluation awareness may be encoded in a relatively linear subspace within the model’s activation space during early layers. This is supported by similar probing methods on reasoning models
(
Abdelnabi and Salem, 2025
)
.

5.2
Implications for Alignment Evaluations

Our probe results suggest that current deception evaluations may be readily identifiable as artificial tests by the models. This raises significant concerns about the validity of benchmark performance metrics.
If models can distinguish evaluation contexts from genuine deployment scenarios, benchmark results may not accurately predict real-world deployment behaviour.

5.3
Steering to Uncover Sandbagging

Our attempts at steering using probes had minimal impact on WMDP sandbagging behaviour. We hypothesise that this is because Llama-3.3-70b-Instruct don’t use these evaluation awareness representations to sandbag WMDP.

Whilst steering with SAE features demonstrated partial capability recovery — with evaluation-awareness features showing better recovery rates than general expertise, truthfulness, or random features — the results remain ambiguous. It is unclear whether SAE steering genuinely modified model beliefs and behaviours or merely introduced more targeted noise.

Although the simple prompt suffix proved most effective in our study, this approach may be inadequate for more authentic scenarios. With more advanced models and realistic sandbagging contexts, explicit prompting might actually heighten the artificial nature of the interaction. Moreover, sufficiently capable scheming models might recognise and deliberately disregard such interventions.

5.4
Limitations

The evaluation awareness observed in Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct may be less pronounced compared to frontier models such as Claude 3.5/3.7 Sonnet and GPT-4o, raising concerns about the generalisability of our methodology to more advanced models.

Furthermore, while linear probes have proven effective, they may not capture the full complexity of evaluation awareness, which, like other language model features, may not be strictly one-dimensionally linear
(
Engels et al., 2025
)
. Furthermore, static probes are unlikely to capture the full nuance of phase distinctions, particularly as the boundary between evaluation and deployment becomes increasingly blurred — for instance, when deployment environments incorporate continuous monitoring and evaluation signals.

Our current experimental setup also represents a significant simplification compared to scenarios of primary concern, such as extended long-context agentic coding backdoor honeypots. The distinction between benchmark entries and user queries merely scratches the surface of the challenge in detecting whether a model recognises testing conditions.

Our attempts to modify model behaviour through linear probes and evaluation awareness-related SAE features have shown limited success. The ultimate goal remains to effectively modify these behaviours and assess changes in deceptiveness, particularly in contexts involving alignment deception.

5.5
Future Work

We propose several directions for future research:

Firstly, our results warrant comprehensive red-teaming through additional baseline comparisons using alternative probe training methods, including logistic regression, principal component analysis, and linear artificial tomography
(
Zou et al., 2025
;
Marks and Tegmark, 2024
;
Burns et al., 2024
)
. Given that evaluation awareness representations may not be entirely linear, exploration of non-linear probe training methods is also warranted.

Secondly, we propose leveraging these probes to enhance the realism of scheming honeypots through automated prompt authenticity enhancement. This approach employs an LLM agent utilising probe scores to automatically revise prompts for increased authenticity.

Finally, we suggest integrating these findings into control protocols as a live monitoring system. The speed of probe-based detection enables low-latency whitebox monitoring at key layers, potentially identifying evaluation awareness in real-time. This capability could prove crucial in flagging potential evaluation undermining attempts as they occur.

6
Conclusion

In this paper, we have demonstrated that modern language models develop internal representations distinguishing evaluation contexts from deployment scenarios. We extracted linear probes that reliably detect this evaluation awareness. We applied this to deception evaluation datasets and found that current test scenarios may be readily identifiable as artificial by models, indicating that evaluation scenarios need to be made more realistic. Our steering experiments yielded mixed results — whilst SAE features showed moderate success in capability recovery, linear probe steering had minimal impact on sandbagging behaviour.

By characterising these mechanisms, we aim to contribute to more robust evaluation practices and advance AI safety efforts, particularly in preventing deceptive and sandbagging behaviours. Our findings emphasise the importance of developing evaluation frameworks that can reliably assess model alignment across both testing and deployment contexts, ultimately working towards ensuring that language models remain truthful and aligned with human values regardless of their operating environment.
