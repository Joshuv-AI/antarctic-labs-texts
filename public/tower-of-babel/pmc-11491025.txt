Validation of the Arabic version of the Adolescent Distress-Eustress Scale (ADES)
Authors: Nisma Merdad, Tabassum Rashid, Aya Alzuabi, Souheil Hallit, Feten Fekih-Romdhane
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe current study examined the psychometric properties of an Arabic translation of the Adolescent Distress-Eustress Scale (ADES) in Saudi Arabia. It assessed the factorial structure through a confirmatory factor analysis, composite reliability through Cronbach alpha and McDonald’s omega scores, gender invariance, and convergent validity through its correlation to resilience.MethodsA total of 505 high school students from 21 randomly selected schools in Jeddah, Saudi Arabia (mean age of 16.66 ± 1.10 years, 52.7% females) participated in the study. The questionnaires were translated using the forward and backward technique.ResultsFindings revealed good internal consistency of the Arabic ADES (McDonald’s ω being 0.82 for distress and 0.79 for eustress). Confirmatory factor analyses confirmed the two-factor structure of the scale and demonstrated measurement invariance across gender at the configural, metric, and scalar levels. Girls had higher Distress scores, while boys reported higher eustress scores, which was consistent with previous studies. Finally, the ADES distress and eustress scales were significantly correlated to resilience supporting convergent validity.ConclusionThe present study supports the reliability and validity of the Arabic version of the ADES as a self-report method to assess Eustress and Distress in adolescents. Pending further validations to support the present findings, this measure can be utilized by schools, mental health providers, and researchers to support Arabic speaking adolescents globally.

Introduction

Adolescence is a crucial developmental phase characterized by physiological, psychological, and social changes. This period often brings about new challenges and stressors, which can significantly impact an adolescent’s well-being and mental health. Stressors can be a physical or non-physical stimuli resulting from an environmental demand that exceeds a person’s natural ability to regulate it, and stress responses are subjective and based on the individual’s perception of them [1]. In a systematic review on negative and positive stressors for adolescents, it was found that the most salient negative stressors were - in order of importance - family, school, self and peer problems [2]. Stress is an inherent part of life and can have both negative and positive effects on individuals. This dichotomy of stress can be understood through the concepts of distress and eustress. While stress has traditionally been considered detrimental and maladaptive to adolescence, some theories suggest that some aspects of stress can be beneficial. The resulting stress, thus, could be negative and not desirable (distress) or a positive, desirable, and advantageable response (eustress) [3]. Evidence suggests that distress and eustress have distinct foundations and consequences, and that eustress does not result from the absence of distress [4]. Distress and Eustress are two concepts that are gaining increasing attention in the field of psychology, though most of the research on stress has focused on its negative aspects, found that it was highly prevalent, and is linked to many adversities in adolescents.

Many studies have linked the negative aspects of stress to physical and psychological adversities in adolescents. Exposure to chronic stress has been linked to changes in the amygdala, hippocampal formation, and prefrontal cortex in adolescents [5]. Chronic stress has also been associated with many behavioural problems such as poorer sleep, unhealthy eating behaviours, decreased physical activity, as well as overall disturbances to psychical & psychological wellbeing [6–9]. In addition, chronic stress has also been linked to hopelessness, depressed mood, and suicide risk possibly through underlying biological functions and epigenetic changes [10, 11]. On the other hand, very few research has been done to assess the potential positive aspects of stress. Eustress has been linked to the concept of flow - a state of complete and effortless absorption in an activity - which in turn was related to positive school engagement [12]. Eustress has also been showed to have a positive relationship with hope, self-efficacy, wellbeing and life satisfaction for boys and girls equally [13–16]. In addition, in the framework of stress, adolescents may draw strength from protective psychosocial and emotional assets, as well as external resources that give them the resilience to cope with these stressors [17]. The relationship between adolescents’ stress and psychological problems is partially mediated by resilience [18]. Therefore, it can be anticipated that enhancing resilience and minimizing stress could be beneficial for adolescents’ mental health. Studies on resilience and stress focused on the negative aspects of stress, however, a possible link between resilience and eustress can be derived from the definition of eustress as a positive emotional response to a stressful event [19]. It illustrates the importance of both stress and resilience as important factors to be considered when addressing the mental health of adolescents. Overall, identifying and addressing the various stressors adolescents face requires understanding the sources and impacts of stress, as well as the dynamics between distress and eustress.

Researchers have attempted to quantify both aspects of stress in their studies using valid and reliable assessment measures. One such measure is the Short Measure of Emotional, Physical, and Behavioural Markers of Eustress and Distress [20]. This measure was shown to be an effective and valid assessment tool in clinical, organizational, and educational settings [20]. In addition, and as a measure of both positive and negative stress, the Valencia Eustress-Distress Appraisal Scale (VEDAS) has also been validated in Arabic; however, the scale is intended only for adults in the workplace [21, 22]. Thus, the Adolescent Distress-Eustress Scale (ADES) was developed by Victoria Branson and colleagues as a holistic assessment measure designed to quantify both aspects of the stress response specifically for adolescents [16]. The purpose of the ADES is to offer a comprehensive tool that captures the dual dimensions of stress—distress and eustress—enabling researchers and clinicians to better understand how adolescents experience and cope with stress. By measuring both positive and negative stress responses, the ADES provides insight into the complex interplay of factors that contribute to adolescent mental health and well-being. This tool is significant because it moves beyond traditional stress measures that focus solely on negative stress, offering a nuanced perspective that can inform interventions aimed at fostering resilience and promoting positive stress management among adolescents. Rather than being seen as opposite extremes of the same continuum, these two responses are seen as distinct constructs. Thus, individuals may simultaneously experience distress and eustress. By quantifying these constructs of the stress response, the developers of the ADES fill the gap in knowledge to successfully link theory and measurement and expand on a newer approach towards stress research that looks outside of the typical focus on only the negative effects of stress. The scale includes two 5-item subscales, one of them reflecting distress and the other reflecting eustress [16](. This measure was found to be reliable and valid for use in both the educational and psychological field to measure distress and eustress in adolescents. The Turkish version of this scale was recently validated by Nevin Akgün and colleagues [23]. This study confirmed that the scale is reliable and applicable to a Turkish-speaking population. It showed strong internal consistency and a factor structure similar to the original scale. These results suggest that the ADES can effectively measure distress and eustress in different cultural contexts, supporting its use in international research and practice.

Promoting an environment that balances challenge and support can help adolescents effectively navigate distress and harness the positive aspects of eustress. Arab adolescents face unique stressors influenced by cultural norms. Academic pressure is significant due to high educational expectations placed by families [24]. Family expectations often emphasize traditional roles, potentially conflicting with personal goals [25]. Additionally, mental health stigma can prevent adolescents from seeking help, leading to insufficient support [26]. Recognizing these stressors can improve our understanding and support of Arab adolescents. It is also important to consider the unique stressors among Arab minorities in western countries who may be subjected to discrimination and acculturated stress [27–29]. For instance, a study found that the relationship between racism and psychological problems in Arab American youth could be explained – at least in part – via stress [27]. As a result of conflicts and terrorism, Arab youth are in some instances more likely to be exposed to stressful environments, such as in Palestine and Syria [30–32]. To that end, development and/ or adaptation of the measurements to assess the stress among Arab adolescents is essential. This study introduces a potentially valid and reliable method to assess eustress and distress in Arab adolescents, filling a significant gap in psychological resources for Arabic-speaking communities. By offering a culturally adapted tool, the research enhances understanding of stress dynamics specific to this group, enabling more effective interventions. Therefore, this study hypothesizes that (1) the Arabic version of ADES will replicate the two-factor structure proposed in the original version, and (2) the Arabic version of ADES will show acceptable internal consistency, convergent validity, and invariance across gender groups.

Methods

Translation & adaptation procedures

For this study, the ADES was translated using the ‘forward-backward-forward’ technique method in accordance with the Principles of Good Practice for Translation and Cultural Adaptation [33]. After being translated into Arabic by a professional translator (forward), the questionnaire was presented to two local experts for their feedback. In addition to checking for cultural appropriateness, both experts checked whether the meaning of the translated instrument was preserved. In addition, a third party who hadn’t seen the questionnaire previously translated it back into English (backward). Both experts assisted in matching and evaluating the theoretical equivalence to create the final Arabic version (forward). Furthermore, to assess the face validity of the tool, a pilot test was conducted with 30 adolescents to ensure the questions were clear and appropriate. Their feedback on the clarity and relevance of the items helped ensure the tool’s initial appropriateness for the target population. The final version was distributed to high school students through google forms.

Procedures and ethical considerations

The study received ethical approval from a Saudi university (RCI_REC/21.Feb.2023/7.1.Exp.13) and the Saudi Ministry of Education (4401008619). A stratified random sampling method was employed to select schools from each of Jeddah’s geographic regions, ensuring a diverse sample. A total of 21 publicly funded schools (11 girls and 10 boys) were randomly selected across six districts. Participants included adolescents aged 15 to 18 years who were fluent in Arabic and currently attending selected high schools in Jeddah. Jeddah was chosen for its diverse population and socioeconomic representation.

School principals and counselors were first approached with information sheets and consent forms. Out of the selected schools, permission was granted by all principals and guidance counselors except for one boys’ school in the northern region of Jeddah. The response rates for the e-questionnaires ranged from 8 to 12%. In schools where both consents were obtained, the questionnaire was distributed to all enrolled students as seen in Fig. 1. Informed consent was obtained from all participants before they began the questionnaire, ensuring they understood the study’s purpose and procedures. Participants were assured of their anonymity, as no identifiable information was collected. Data were securely stored and accessible only to authorized personnel. Students were informed of their right to withdraw at any time and about the confidentiality of their responses. In accordance with ethical, regulatory, contractual, and intellectual property protection requirements, the research data are archived on a Saudi university server for long-term preservation and will be stored for a minimum of seven years post-publication.

Measures

Adolescent Distress-Eustress Scale (ADES)

The Adolescent Distress-Eustress Scale (ADES) is a psychometric tool designed to assess both distress and eustress among adolescents. Developed by Victoria Branson and colleagues, the ADES aims to capture the dual nature of stress experiences in adolescents by utilizing two subscales: one measuring distress and the other measuring eustress. The scale includes 10 items, with five items dedicated to each subscale. The ADES has demonstrated strong reliability and validity across different cultural settings, making it a versatile tool for assessing adolescent stress. Notably, research by Branson et al. [16] and the validation of the Turkish version by Akgün et al. [23] underscore its effectiveness and applicability in diverse populations. However, to date this scale has not been validated in Arabic.

Resilience

An individual’s resilience is their ability to adapt and bounce back after experiencing stress, trauma, or difficulty in their lives. READ is a psychological assessment tool developed by Nina Hjemdal and colleagues in 2006. It is designed to measure the level of resilient functioning in adolescents aged 13 to 18 years [34]. This scale was developed based on the Resilience Scale for Adults [35]. It consists of 28 self-report items with positive statements using a five-point Likert scale (total disagreement = 1) and (total agreement = 5). A higher score on the READ indicates a higher degree of resilience. It consists of five subscales: (1) Self-confidence, (2) social competence, and (3) Goal Orientation, as well as subscales for (4) family cohesion; and (5) social resources. In Goal Orientation, one evaluates their ability to plan and organize, whereas Self-Confidence assesses one’s belief that they are capable and have confidence in their abilities. The ability to make new friends and converse with others is measured by Social Competence. A person’s social support measures their ability to care, encourage, and assist. Support and shared values are assessed in Family Cohesion.

Higher scores on the READ indicate lower levels of resilience. The READ has been validated in the Arabic language, confirming its appropriateness for assessing resilience among Arabic-speaking adolescents. This was demonstrated in a study by Chaaya et al. [36], which provided evidence of the scale’s reliability and applicability in an Arabic-speaking context. The READ has proven to be valuable in both research and clinical settings. It is used to identify individual strengths and weaknesses in adolescents, guide interventions aimed at promoting resilience, and evaluate the effectiveness of resilience-focused interventions for this age group (ω = 0.92 / α = 0.92).

Analytic strategy

Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA)

There were no missing responses in the dataset. We used data from the total sample to conduct a CFA using the SPSS AMOS v.29 software. As a rule of thumb, simulation studies show that with normally distributed indicator variables and no missing data, a reasonable sample size for a simple confirmatory factor analysis model is about N = 150 [37], which was exceeded in our sample. Our intention was to test the original model of the ADES scale (i.e., two-factor model [14]. Parameter estimates were obtained using the maximum likelihood method and fit indices. CFA metrics included: χ²/df (Chi-square/degrees of freedom): Reflects the model’s goodness of fit, with values less than 5 suggesting a reasonable fit between the model and observed data; RMSEA (Root Mean Square Error of Approximation): Evaluates model-data fit on a per-degree-of-freedom basis; values below 0.08 are indicative of a good fit; SRMR (Standardized Root Mean Square Residual): Represents the mean discrepancy between observed and predicted correlations; values under 0.08 signify a good fit; CFI (Comparative Fit Index): Compares the model fit against a baseline model, with values above 0.90 typically indicating a satisfactory fit; TLI (Tucker-Lewis Index): Assesses improvement over a null model, with values above 0.90 suggesting a good fit [38]. The absence of multicollinearity was verified through tolerance values > 0.2 and variance inflation factor (VIF) values < 5. Multivariate normality was not verified at first; therefore, we performed non-parametric bootstrapping procedure.

Gender invariance

To examine gender invariance of ADES scores, we conducted multi-group CFA [39] using the total sample. Measurement invariance was assessed at the configural, metric, and scalar levels [40]. we accepted ΔCFI ≤ 0.010 and ΔRMSEA ≤ 0.015 or ΔSRMR ≤ 0.010 (0.030 for factorial invariance) as evidence of invariance [39].

Reliability and validity analyses

Composite reliability in both subsamples was assessed using McDonald’s ω and Cronbach’s alpha values, with values greater than 0.70 reflecting adequate composite reliability [41]. The distress and eustress scores were considered normally distributed since the skewness and kurtosis values varied between ± 1 [42]. Therefore, to assess concurrent validity, correlations between the distress and eustress and resilience scores were computed using the Pearson test. Based on Cohen (1992) [43], values ≤ 0.10 were considered weak, ~ 0.30 were considered moderate, and ∼ 0.50 were considered strong correlations.

Confirmatory factor analysis of the ADES scale

CFA indicated that fit of the two-factor model of the ADES scale was modest: χ2/df = 127.68/34 = 3.76 (p < .001), RMSEA = .074 (90% CI .061, .088), SRMR = .053, CFI = .940, TLI = .921. The standardised estimates of factor loadings were all adequate (Table 2). The factor loadings in Table 2 show how well each item loaded on its respective factor, with values above.40 indicating a meaningful relationship. The Eustress factor had moderate to high loadings, with ‘I felt proud for dealing with the pressure’ and ‘I was satisfied with how I dealt with the pressure’ showing notable associations (0.79 and 0.71, respectively). The Distress factor also showed notable loadings, particularly ‘I felt anxious,’ with a loading of 0.83.

Composite reliability

Composite reliability was assessed using McDonald’s Omega (ω) coefficients and Cronbach’s alpha (α) coefficients, which are measures of internal consistency. In the total sample, both the distress and eustress subscales showed adequate reliability, with McDonald’s Omega values of ω = 0.82 for distress and ω = 0.79 for eustress, and Cronbach’s alpha values of α = 0.81 for distress and α = 0.79 for eustress. These coefficients indicate good internal consistency, which is considered acceptable since values are above 0.70.

Measurement invariance

As reported in Table 3, all indices suggested that configural, metric, and scalar invariance was supported across gender. The results showed that females had significantly higher distress scores than males (11.79 ± 5.21 vs. 8.43 ± 5.42, t(503) = -7.10, p < .001), whereas males had significantly higher eustress scores than females (10.63 ± 5.51 vs. 9.36 ± 4.93, t(503) = 2.73, p = .007).

Discussion

Researchers are increasingly recognizing the importance of distress and eustress in adolescents’ mental health and wellbeing. The purpose of this study was to investigate the psychometric properties of the ADES in an Arabic population which lacks a valid assessment measure of distress and eustress in adolescents. The results showed that the two -factor model produced a good fit and was invariant across gender. As anticipated, the Arabic ADES also demonstrated good internal consistency and convergent validity. Overall, the present findings suggest that the Arabic version of the ADES may be a reliable and valid self-report measure that assesses stress as a multidimensional construct.

First, a Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) was performed to examine fit indices in the current study based on the two factor model proposed by the original developers of the questionnaire and confirmed by the Turkish validation study [14, 15]. Results indicated that the two-factor ADES model fits the present data well and the factor loadings were all adequate supporting the use of the two distinct subscales for distress and eustress. In addition, composite reliability of the ADES was satisfactory and acceptable, indicating that the construct is reliable mirroring previous studies. The internal consistencies were adequate for both the distress and eustress subscales with alpha score of 0.81 and 0.79, respectively. This is consistent with the scores found in the original validation study with scores for distress (α = 0.81) and eustress (α = 0.83), as well as the Turkish translation of the measure with distress (α = 0.86) and eustress (α = 0.81) scores [14, 15]. In addition, this study assessed the McDonald’s ω scores (0.82 for distress and 0.79 for eustress) which was not assessed in the previous validation studies for this scale.

The ADES had appropriate equivalency across genders indicating gender invariance. This indicated that differences across groups is a reflection of true difference and not issues with the measurement. The results of the current study suggested that females had significantly higher distress scores than males, whereas males had significantly higher eustress scores than females which is consistent somewhat with previous studies. The original developers of the scales found that while girls showed significantly higher distress scores than boys, there was no significant difference in the eustress scale in Norway [14]. This can be explained by considering the cultural context in Saudi Arabia both males and females have diverse roles and responsibilities, as a result, they may both require new skills to adjust to new experiences and expectations of society and culture. In many societies, boys and girls are exposed to gender-specific stressors related to gender conceptions and socially defined roles of women and men. Girls seem to also respond more positively to familial and social support, while among boys, less coping strategies – meaning teaching and school related support in the study - was more strongly linked to stress levels than in girls [44–46].

This study found an inverse relationship between ADES and resilience, with lower levels of resilience associated with higher levels of distress. This could suggest that both measures possess parallel defensive functions against hardships and stressful situations. This was highlighted in another study conducted in Indonesia showing that resilience predicted 18% of the student’s psychological distress [47]. Adolescents may consequently need extra strength in order to cope with stressful situations when living in collective societies such as the middle eastern which could be motivated by an added cultural expectations, such as those of their families, teachers, and friends [48, 49].

Limitation

While this study showed promising results in assessing the reliability and validity of the ADES in a community sample of adolescents, it does not come without its limitations. Sample schools were selected across Jeddah’s different regions, so results may not be generalizable to other regions of Saudi or the Arab world. Thus, the results should be replicated in other Arabic-speaking countries or communities. This study was also based on a cross-sectional research design; therefore, some psychometric properties could not be tested (such as test-retest reliability). Furthermore, our study was limited to the student’s self-report. Future studies could include other informants such as the parent and/or teacher’s report. Additionally, the low response rates of the e-questionnaires are noted which could limit the generalizability of the findings. Implementing strategies to improve response rates in future research, such as follow-up reminders or incentives, is recommended to enhance sample representation.

Implications and clinical applications

This tool might enhance the ability to differentiate between distress and eustress, potentially enabling a more nuanced understanding of stress experiences among adolescents in diverse cultural contexts. For researchers, this suggests opportunities to explore stress-related outcomes and resilience factors within the Arab world, which could lead to culturally informed interventions. In educational settings, the ADES might serve as a valuable resource for identifying students who may be experiencing significant stress and guiding support strategies. Educators could use insights gained from the scale to develop programs that promote eustress and mitigate distress, thereby fostering a potentially healthier school environment. Clinically, the ADES may offer mental health professionals a reliable method to assess adolescent stress, thus informing tailored treatment plans. By understanding the balance between distress and eustress, clinicians might tailor interventions to enhance coping skills and resilience in adolescents, potentially improving their overall mental health outcomes.

Conclusion

While distress and eustress are two contrasting forms of stress, it is crucial to acknowledge and address both in adolescents’ lives. The purpose of this study was to validate the Arabic version of the ADES as a valid and reliable assessment tool is necessary to measure the extreme and moderate levels of positive stress (Eustress) and negative stress (Distress) that adolescents encounter. The results indicated that the Arabic version of the ADES is a valid and reliable measure to assess Eustress and Distress. CFA exhibited good model fit and standardized estimation indices were in a suitable range. Moreover, psychometric properties, inter-scale correlations, and concurrent validity were also found to be acceptable. The study highlights differential stress experiences across genders, with findings suggesting that adolescent girls are more prone to distress, whereas boys are more likely to experience eustress. Psychology researchers can use the findings of this study to plan preventative strategies when adolescents encounter psychological distress and/or promote eustress by conducting proper assessment measures.
