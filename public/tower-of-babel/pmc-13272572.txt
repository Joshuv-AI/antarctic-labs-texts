Centering Asian voices in global aging research: strengthening data infrastructure in East and Southeast Asia
Authors: Bussarawan Teerawichitchainan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryOver the past two decades, substantial investments in aging data infrastructure have transformed East and Southeast Asia from a region characterized by fragmented data coverage into one of the better empirically documented regions for aging research. Nationally and regionally representative longitudinal studies, expanded measures of health and well-being, and growing harmonization efforts have greatly enhanced the comparability and analytic value of Asian aging data. Yet, despite these achievements, Asian scholarship remains insufficiently centered in global aging research, with the region often serving primarily as a source of data rather than a locus of agenda-setting, theory development, and methodological innovation. This article argues that Asia now has the demographic weight, technical capacity, and institutional maturity to assume a more central role in shaping global aging research. It outlines a forward-looking agenda to strengthen data infrastructure by expanding coverage, improving measurement, enhancing visibility and governance, and investing in human capital, thereby positioning Asian voices within more equitable regional and international partnerships that can advance global aging research.Keywords: Aging, Asia, Data infrastructure, Surveys

Introduction

Population aging is advancing rapidly and unevenly in East and Southeast Asia.1,2 Asia—by a considerable margin—accounts for the largest share of the global population aged 65 and older, creating profound challenges for health, long-term care, economic security, and intergenerational solidarity, among others. As emphasized more than a decade ago in the seminal report Aging in Asia: Findings from New and Emerging Data Initiatives, robust population-based survey data are indispensable for understanding these challenges and informing effective policy responses.3

Since that time, Asia has made substantial progress in developing survey data infrastructure for aging research.4, 5, 6 Nationally representative cross-sectional and longitudinal studies of older populations have expanded across the region; measurement has improved to capture multidimensional aspects of health and well-being across the life course; and global harmonizing efforts have increased the visibility and accessibility of Asian aging data.7,8 These developments have transformed East and Southeast Asia from a region once marked by fragmented data coverage into one of the better empirically documented world regions for the study of aging.

Yet, despite these achievements, Asian scholarship remains insufficiently centered in global aging research. Too often, Asia functions primarily as a source of data,9 while agenda-setting, theory development, and methodological innovation continue to be shaped primarily elsewhere.10,11 This imbalance has become increasingly consequential at a moment when global scientific infrastructure—historically supported by a small number of major contributors, including the United States—is facing heightened political and financial volatility.12,13 Disruptions to public research funding, shifting political priorities that selectively constrain certain areas of inquiry, and growing uncertainty surrounding long-term support for large-scale data initiatives have exposed the fragility of aging research that relies heavily on a limited number of global hubs. Such instability threatens not only the continuity of longitudinal data collection, but also the training pipelines and collaborative networks on which cumulative scientific progress depends. In this shifting landscape, the sustainability and intellectual vitality of aging research must increasingly be anchored in regional capacity, leadership, and long-term institutional commitment within East and Southeast Asia.

This article argues that Asia is now positioned to move beyond data provision and assume a more central role in shaping the priorities, methods, and interpretations that define global aging research. Drawing on past recommendations, recent accomplishments, and persistent challenges in aging and health data infrastructure across East and Southeast Asia, I outline a forward-looking agenda for strengthening data systems in ways that center Asian voices in knowledge production, interpretation, and policy relevance.

What has been accomplished

A growing number of countries in East and Southeast Asia have fielded nationally and regionally representative studies that focus explicitly on later life, with older persons as focal respondents rather than peripheral household members.6 Many of these studies adopt longitudinal designs, allowing researchers to track health, socioeconomic trajectories, and family dynamics over time.9 These surveys have collectively enabled the study of aging within broader life course and family contexts.

The rapid expansion of aging data infrastructure in the region reflects diverse funding and governance models. Several of the existing surveys are part of, or closely aligned with, the Health and Retirement Study (HRS) family of studies and were developed through collaboration with researchers from the United States and other high-income countries, sometimes with initial or partial support from the U.S. National Institutes of Health.3,5 Prominent examples include the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS). Other surveys have benefited from technical advice while being primarily funded by national governments, including South Korea’s Korean Longitudinal Study of Aging (KLoSA), Thailand’s Health, Aging, and Retirement in Thailand (HART) study, and Malaysia’s Malaysia Ageing and Retirement Survey (MARS).

Asian aging data infrastructure, however, extends beyond the HRS family of studies. The Chinese Longitudinal Healthy Longevity Survey (CLHLS), launched in 1998 with a focus on the oldest old, provides an important example of a regionally anchored initiative supported through international collaboration. While early funding from the Max Planck Institute and support from the U.S. National Institute on Aging were important, the study was led by Chinese scholars and institutionally rooted in Peking University and other Chinese research organizations, which carried out much of the survey implementation and analysis.14 Regional institutions have also supported aging data initiatives, including longitudinal surveys in the Philippines and Vietnam funded by the Economic Research Institute for ASEAN and East Asia (ERIA).15,16 This hybrid landscape underscores that successful aging data infrastructure in Asia often depends on both domestic institutional ownership, regional investment, and sustained international collaboration.

Another major achievement has been the broadening of measurement beyond morbidity and mortality.6 Contemporary aging surveys increasingly capture cognitive functioning, mental health, functional limitations, social participation, intergenerational exchanges, and economic security, reflecting a more holistic understanding of aging, health, and well-being. In several aging surveys, self-reported measures have been complemented by biomarkers, performance-based assessments, and harmonized cognitive protocols, strengthening analytic rigor and facilitating cross-study comparability.17 These advances have also been accompanied by greater attention to context-sensitive measurement, including the use of age thresholds that better reflect Asian demographic and health profiles.3

This methodological diversification increasingly extends beyond population surveys. Aging research in the region is drawing on data sources such as neuroimaging, non-invasive digital tools, and wearable devices, suggesting growing innovation in aging and health research.8,9 Survey data are also increasingly being linked with administrative and clinical records, insurance claims, and disease registries. For example, Singapore’s Retirement and Health Study sought consent to link survey responses to administrative records18 and the Korean Frailty and Aging Cohort Study has been linked to the National Health Insurance Database.19 Such linkages strengthen the capacity to validate self-reported health measures, examine health-care use and costs, and study disease incidence and progression over time.

Progress has also been made in harmonization and data accessibility. The expansion of HRS-family studies and the development of global data platforms such as the Gateway to Global Aging Data and the WHO’s Study on Global Ageing and Adult Health (SAGE) have facilitated cross-national research.5,20 By making data and metadata more comparable and accessible, these platforms have enhanced the visibility of Asian data in comparative aging research, reduced barriers to secondary data use, and contributed to rapid growth in peer-reviewed publications examining the determinants and consequences of aging and health.21

Institutionally, the region has witnessed the emergence of research centers, training programs, and regional networks dedicated to aging and population health, spearheaded by universities, governments, and non-governmental organizations. These developments include nationally anchored aging research centers linked to major longitudinal surveys, such as CHARLS at Peking University’s Institute of Social Science Survey; regionally coordinated research and training programs supported by intergovernmental organizations, UN agencies, and international NGOs; and a growing number of cross-national workshops, short courses, and collaborative networks focused on survey design, data management, and advanced statistical analysis. These institutional investments have strengthened local technical capacity and fostered sustained collaboration across disciplines and national boundaries.4

Persistent challenges

Despite these advances, significant challenges continue to constrain the maturation of aging data infrastructure in East and Southeast Asia.

First, progress has been uneven across countries, particularly with respect to longitudinal data collection.9 Existing longitudinal aging surveys remain concentrated in more economically developed societies—most notably Japan, mainland China, Singapore, South Korea, and Taiwan. Investments in aging data infrastructure have tended to prioritize countries where population aging is already well advanced, while countries experiencing lower—but rapidly accelerating—population aging, such as Cambodia, Lao PDR, and Mongolia, have received comparatively less attention.6 Although longitudinal surveys are costly and logistically demanding, they are essential for examining causal relationships across the life course, such as how health shocks shape labor force exit or caregiving arrangements, and for assessing the impacts of major policy shifts. Without longitudinal data, research remains overly reliant on descriptive evidence, limiting its capacity to inform policy. Many existing datasets also remain limited in their ability to support network-based, dyadic, or multilevel analyses, while the integration of survey data with administrative or health-system data remains uneven across the region.

Myanmar illustrates both the promise and fragility of aging data initiatives in lower-resource settings. The Myanmar Aging Survey, conducted in 2012, represented one of the first nationally focused efforts to document the living conditions, health, and family support of older persons in the country.22,23 Separately, more recent collaborative initiatives have aimed to develop longitudinal and health-focused aging data in Myanmar through collaborations among research teams in Asia.24 However, political instability has halted follow-up data collection, underscoring the vulnerability of aging data infrastructure in contexts marked by institutional and governance volatility.

Funding and governance structures also present a second major challenge. Many large-scale aging surveys in East and Southeast Asia rely on fragmented or time-limited funding that is vulnerable to shifting national priorities or external donor agendas.8 Securing stable institutional support from national governments remains a central constraint, even for established studies such as HART. This instability limits long-term planning, threatens continuity across survey waves, and often leaves research teams dependent—whether partially or transitionally—on international funding sources. Recent political and fiscal disruptions affecting major global science funders further underscore the risks of relying too heavily on a narrow set of international funding streams.13

Beyond financial sustainability, human resource constraints remain equally important. Even where high-quality aging datasets exist, principal investigators often lack dedicated funding for research staff, data managers, and graduate students needed to curate, document, and analyze complex data.6,8 In many settings, short-term, project-based funding supports data collection but provides limited resources for sustained research teams, long-term mentoring, or cumulative expertise in aging research.3 This fragmentation slows scientific output, reduces opportunities for secondary analysis, weakens continuity across survey waves, and makes it harder for scholars—especially early-career researchers—to build long-term research agendas around aging.

Data governance and access present additional barriers.6 In several countries, restrictive data sharing policies, high access fees, and opaque approval processes limit broad scientific use and cross-national collaboration. A long-standing tradition of treating survey data as proprietary has hindered timely public release and constrained secondary analysis by the wider research community. These barriers are shaped not only by institutional reluctance, but also by limited data-curation capacity, privacy concerns, uneven resources for preparing data for wider use, and the absence of clear norms around embargo periods and secondary access.25 Where access is possible, procedures may be unclear, approval timelines lengthy, or fees prohibitively high for scholars with limited resources, particularly in low- and middle-income settings. Such barriers narrow participation in aging research, privilege well-resourced institutions and researchers, and slow the development of broader regional research communities.

At the same time, calls for broader data access must contend with increasingly complex governance constraints. Across the region, aging datasets increasingly intersect with sensitive administrative, health, geospatial, biometric, and digital information, raising legitimate concerns about privacy, confidentiality, data sovereignty, and compliance with national data protection and security laws.26 These concerns are especially salient when survey cohorts are linked to electronic health records, insurance claims, disease registries, or digital trace data. As a result, the challenge is not only limited access, but also the absence of clear, trusted, and workable governance arrangements for responsible data sharing and secondary use.

Finally, Asian aging datasets often remain less visible globally than comparable aging data resources in the United States and Europe, partly because dissemination is less systematic and English-language documentation is sometimes limited, especially for datasets outside major harmonized platforms. This uneven visibility may slow the region’s scientific progress and perpetuate asymmetries in knowledge production.

Centering Asian voices in global aging research

Datasets from East and Southeast Asia are increasingly visible and widely used in aging research. Yet, they are typically mobilized to test externally generated frameworks rather than to generate concepts grounded in the region’s own social, institutional, and historical realities. Addressing this asymmetry requires deliberate investments in data infrastructure that enable scholars and institutions in Asia to define research questions, innovate methodologically, and produce policy-relevant knowledge grounded in regional priorities. This does not imply retreat from international collaboration; rather, it calls for partnerships in which regional expertise, institutional ownership, and local interpretive authority are central. Expanding coverage and reducing geographic inequities

A priority is to address the uneven geographic distribution of aging data across Asia. While several countries now field robust aging surveys, lower-middle- and lower-income countries in the region remain underrepresented despite rapid growth in the absolute number of older persons. Comprehensive surveys of older adults are needed even in countries where population aging currently appears modest, as demographic momentum ensures that today’s middle-aged populations will soon enter later life.1,27 Delayed investment not only limits empirical understanding but also constrains governments’ ability to anticipate future demands related to healthcare, long-term care, and social protection.

Where resource or capacity constraints preclude the immediate establishment of dedicated aging surveys, existing longitudinal studies offer a practical and cost-effective entry point. The Indonesia Family Life Survey, for instance, illustrates how a general household panel can be expanded to incorporate aging modules, enabling life course analyses that link early- and mid-life conditions to later-life outcomes.28 Similar strategies could be adopted in other countries to generate aging-relevant data without duplicating infrastructure. In contexts lacking longitudinal studies altogether, national censuses and Demographic and Health Surveys (DHS) remain underutilized resources. Adding short, well-designed modules on older persons’ health and functioning—as demonstrated by the 2009 Maldives DHS—would substantially strengthen the evidence base.29 Likewise, post-census thematic reports should routinely include focused analyses of older populations rather than treating aging as a residual category.

Grounding survey content in Asian social, family, and historical realities

Centering Asian voices also requires moving beyond inherited assumptions embedded in global survey models. While harmonization with international frameworks such as the HRS or SAGE has facilitated comparability, simply replicating Western instruments risks obscuring key features of aging in Asian contexts. Conceptual leadership depends on asking questions about family, work, care, and inequality that emerge from regional realities rather than being retrofitted into existing templates.

Family and care networks provide a clear example. In much of Asia, later-life support is not organized solely around a spouse or a single child, but may involve multiple children, in-laws, grandchildren, extended kin, domestic workers, neighbors, and formal care providers, with caregiving responsibilities shaped by gender, kinship, and filial norms.30 Yet many surveys rely primarily on older respondents’ reports and broad measures of support receipt, making it difficult to distinguish primary from secondary caregivers, compare sons’ and daughters’ roles, or assess how care responsibilities are shared. Future survey designs should more systematically capture the full care network, including caregivers’ relationships to the older person, care tasks, proximity, frequency of assistance, and availability of backup support. Where feasible, data collection from both care recipients and caregivers would enable dyadic and network-based analyses of care arrangements. Such designs would better capture the relational nature of aging, illuminate gendered caregiving dynamics, and show how care responsibilities are negotiated amid demographic and social change.

Migration is another defining feature of Asian family change that aging data infrastructure should capture more systematically.31 Rural-to-urban and trans-provincial migration, for example, have contributed to geographically dispersed families, “left-behind” older adults, and care arrangements that extend across households and localities.32 These shifts complicate conventional measures of coresidence and family support because adult children may provide financial transfers and emotional contact from afar while being unavailable for hands-on care.33 At the same time, older adults often remain active caregivers themselves, especially through grandchild care when middle-generation adults migrate for work.34 Future survey designs should therefore collect information on children’s migration histories, distance and travel time, frequency and mode of contact, remittances, emergency availability, and grandchild care responsibilities.

Equally important is the need to challenge stereotypical portrayals of older persons as passive recipients of care. Surveys should explicitly measure older adults’ contributions to families and communities, including caregiving for grandchildren, household labor, and the sharing of assets such as housing.35,36 Capturing reciprocal intergenerational exchanges, rather than focusing solely on support received, offers a more balanced and accurate representation of later life and helps counter ageist assumptions in both research and policy discourse.

Life history is equally central. Later-life health and well-being reflect cumulative advantages and disadvantages shaped by education, work, family trajectories, and exposure to historical shocks.37 Asian societies have experienced distinctive historical events, including war and forced migration, that continue to shape the lives of today’s older cohorts.38 The Vietnam Health and Aging Study (VHAS), for instance, illustrates the analytic value of explicitly incorporating early-life war exposure into aging surveys, allowing researchers to examine how war trauma, military participation, and early-life stress reverberate across the life course and generations.39 Embedding such historically grounded measures enables theory generation that speaks directly to Asian experiences while also enriching global debates on life course processes.

Strengthening measurement validity through context-sensitive innovation

Leadership in aging research also hinges on measurement quality. In many low- and middle-income Asian countries, high levels of undiagnosed disease and unequal access to healthcare undermine the validity of self-reported health measures. Future surveys should prioritize the systematic collection of anthropometric data and biomarkers to address well-documented biases in self-reported chronic conditions.17 Similarly, self-reported functional limitations should be complemented with performance-based assessments, such as grip strength or balance tests, to provide more reliable indicators of physical capacity. Cognitive measurement also warrants innovation. Heavily verbal instruments, for example, should be supplemented with non-verbal tasks that are less sensitive to education and literacy, thereby improving cross-population comparability.40,41

At the same time, measurement strategies must be tailored to local realities. Age thresholds for survey eligibility should reflect earlier onset of chronic conditions and functional decline observed in some Asian populations.3 Functional limitation measures should be grounded in locally relevant activities rather than imported wholesale from Western settings. These adaptations not only improve validity but also create opportunities for Asia-led methodological contributions that can inform global standards.

Asia’s expanding digital infrastructure also creates opportunities to improve measurement beyond conventional survey instruments. High levels of mobile phone ownership, smartphone use, and platform-based digital activity in many East and Southeast Asian societies could support new approaches to measuring mobility, social engagement, health behaviors, service use, and environmental exposures through wearable devices, passive sensing, and digital trace data.42,43 These approaches may be especially valuable when integrated with survey cohorts, administrative data, and qualitative research. At the same time, such innovations require careful attention to consent, privacy, algorithmic bias, digital exclusion, and the risk that digitally visible behaviors may not represent the experiences of older adults who are poorer, rural, cognitively impaired, or less digitally connected.44

Strengthening institutional and human capacity

Methodological innovation cannot be sustained without institutional investment. Long-term leadership in aging research depends on stable national funding, dedicated research institutions, and a strong pipeline of trained scholars. Major surveys (e.g., CHARLS, HART, MARS, and VHAS) demonstrate the feasibility of researcher-initiated, academically led models, but their continued success ultimately hinges on consistent domestic institutional backing. Governments play a critical role in supporting aging research by funding survey infrastructure, protecting academic autonomy, and investing in institutions capable of managing complex longitudinal studies that inform both policy planning and cumulative scientific knowledge.9

Sustained investment in human capital for data use and analysis is also essential. Even when high-quality datasets exist, insufficient support for research staff, data managers, and early-career scholars limits the ability to fully exploit complex longitudinal, biomarker, and multimodal data. Expanding training programs in advanced methods, data stewardship, and ethical data sharing—and embedding these within survey infrastructure rather than treating them as standalone activities—would help build durable analytic capacity and intellectual leadership.

Regional capacity-building initiatives offer promising models. Long-standing efforts in lower-middle-income countries—advanced by scholars such as the late John Knodel, of the University of Michigan and Chulalongkorn University, and the late Yasuhiko Saito, of Nihon University, along with regional workshops and training programs—show how sustained collaboration can build methodological expertise and foster intellectual exchange across countries.15,16,23,45 Expanding and institutionalizing such initiatives would reduce reliance on ad hoc training and short-term, externally led capacity building efforts, while strengthening regionally embedded research communities.

Enhancing visibility, responsible accessibility, and data governance

Centering Asian voices requires rethinking data governance while improving the global visibility and accessibility of Asian aging data. The tradition of tightly guarding data, still prevalent in parts of Asia, limits scientific progress and constrains broader collaborative research. Public release or controlled access to survey data within a reasonable timeframe should become the norm rather than the exception. Platforms that facilitate secure, ethical, and equitable access, including public-use regional data repositories and controlled-access systems, can expand the reach and impact of aging research. Proactive dissemination strategies, such as dedicated data portals, standardized English-language metadata, user guides, training workshops, and targeted outreach to regional and international research communities, would increase data uptake while ensuring appropriate recognition of data producers and local collaborators.

Strengthening data accessibility, however, does not require a one-size-fits-all model of unrestricted public release. A more pragmatic approach is to develop tiered access systems. Public-facing resources can include study metadata, questionnaires, codebooks, harmonized variable descriptions, and user guides. More sensitive microdata can be made available through controlled-access application systems or secure data enclaves, where approved researchers analyze data within protected environments. For highly restricted cross-national data, federated analysis offers another option: data remain within national or institutional boundaries, while common analytic code is run locally and only aggregate results are shared. Such models would help preserve the benefits of cross-national comparison while respecting national data governance requirements. In this context, emerging AI-assisted tools may further support data access and use by improving metadata documentation, cross-language harmonization, and the organization of linked survey, administrative, and clinical data. However, these tools should complement—not substitute for—sustained investment in representative, longitudinal, and ethically governed aging data.46

Harmonization efforts should continue, but they should not be limited to aligning survey measures across countries; they should also support shared governance and regional priority-setting. Regional frameworks that allow countries to agree on core measures while retaining flexibility for locally salient modules would also support both comparability and innovation. International organizations and NGOs, such as UNESCAP, UNFPA, the Asian Development Bank, and HelpAge International, and the Tsao Foundation, can play a convening role by fostering communication, supporting cross-national projects, and amplifying Asian-led research agendas in global forums.

Conclusion

The future of global aging research is unfolding at a moment of profound demographic transformation and growing scientific uncertainty. In this shifting landscape, the role of East and Southeast Asia is not simply to supply data or serve as a testing ground for externally generated hypotheses and theories, but to exercise leadership in defining research priorities, methodological standards, and policy interpretations with global relevance. The region’s demographic weight, expanding scientific capacity, and increasingly mature institutional infrastructure—evident in nationally anchored aging surveys and regional initiatives supported by academic, governmental, and non-governmental organizations—position Asia to play a critical role in shaping the next generation of global aging research agendas. Realizing this potential, however, requires deliberate investments in inclusive survey design, equitable data governance, regional collaboration, and the cultivation of regionally anchored intellectual leadership. Moving from data provision and theory testing to agenda setting and conceptual leadership is therefore essential if the region is to shape global aging research. Centering Asian voices will not only enhance the resilience of the field but also support more equitable international collaboration and generate more inclusive, contextually grounded knowledge to inform aging societies worldwide.
