Pyrethroid-resistance in Anopheles gambiae s.l (Culicidae) populations from the Sudan Savannah ecoregion in Nigeria is partly mediated by knockdown mutation
Authors: Oluyinka Ajibola Iyiola, Ahmed Idowu Omotayo, Olukayode James Adelaja, Oluwaseun Adegbola Adesoye, Seun Funmilayo Adejuwon, Ademola Emmanuel Alaba, Aliu Soliu Fulani, Zainab Adenekan, Michael Olugbenga Kusimo, Waheed Babatunde Yahya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveAnopheles gambiae s.l. is the major malaria vector in sub-Saharan Africa, and increasing pyrethroid resistance threatens vector control. This study investigated the prevalence of knockdown resistance (kdr) mutations and pyrethroid susceptibility in An. gambiae s.l. populations from Kwara State, North-Central Nigeria.ResultsA total of 250 An. gambiae s.l. mosquitoes were collected from three Local Government Areas. Molecular analysis identified An. coluzzii (31.2%, 95% CI: 25.7–37.3%), An. gambiae s.s. (16.8%, 95% CI: 12.6–22.0%), and An. arabiensis (13.6%, 95% CI: 9.9–18.4%), while 38.4% failed amplification. All populations were resistant to permethrin, deltamethrin, and alphacypermethrin (mortality < 90%), with mortality ranging from 23% to 64% across locations. Knockdown times were fastest with deltamethrin (KDT₅₀: 32.75–52.80 min) and slowest with permethrin (KDT₅₀: 39.41–93.34 min). Kdr genotyping showed 41.6% homozygous resistant (RR) and 58.4% homozygous susceptible (rr) mosquitoes; no heterozygotes were detected. These findings indicate widespread pyrethroid resistance partially mediated by kdr mutations, with significant variation among locations and sibling species. The absence of heterozygotes suggests strong selection pressure, emphasizing the need for resistance monitoring and strategic vector control interventions.

Introduction

Anopheles gambiae sensu lato (s.l.) remains the primary malaria vector across sub-Saharan Africa, responsible for a substantial proportion of Plasmodium falciparum transmission [1, 2]. This complex comprises several morphologically indistinguishable sibling species that exhibit distinct ecological preferences, feeding behaviours, and vectorial capacities [3, 4]. In West Africa, Anopheles coluzzii, Anopheles gambiae sensu stricto (s.s.), and Anopheles arabiensis are the dominant malaria vectors, though their relative abundances vary considerably across local ecological gradients [5, 6]. Understanding this species composition is critical for designing effective, locally-adapted vector control interventions.

The effectiveness of malaria control strategies is increasingly threatened by widespread insecticide resistance in An. gambiae s.l. populations. Pyrethroids, the only class of insecticides currently approved for long-lasting insecticidal nets (LLINs) and widely used in indoor residual spraying (IRS), have shown declining efficacy in many endemic regions [7, 8]. Recent surveillance across West and Central Africa has documented high levels of resistance to Permethrin, Deltamethrin, and Alphacypermethrin, with resistance patterns varying substantially among locations and sibling species [9, 10].

A major mechanism conferring pyrethroid resistance is the knockdown resistance (kdr) mutation in the voltage-gated sodium channel gene. In West African Anopheles populations, the L1014F mutation (kdr-west) results from a leucine-to-phenylalanine substitution at codon 1014 [11]. This mutation reduces neuronal sensitivity to pyrethroids, decreasing knockdown and mortality following exposure. Rising frequencies of this mutation in both An. coluzzii and An. gambiae s.s. populations raise concerns about the sustained effectiveness of current vector control tools [12, 13].

Given the heterogeneity in species composition and resistance mechanisms across Nigeria’s diverse ecological zones, localised surveillance is essential. Kwara State, situated in the Sudan Savannah ecoregion of North-Central Nigeria, is an understudied area with limited resistance data. This study aimed to: (1) characterise the sibling species composition of An. gambiae s.l. in three Local Government Areas of Kwara State, (2) determine phenotypic resistance levels to three commonly used pyrethroids, (3) estimate the frequency of the kdr (L1014F) mutation, and (4) examine relationships between species identity and resistance phenotypes.

Materials and methods

Study area

The study was conducted in three Local Government Areas (LGAs) of Kwara State, North-Central Nigeria: Post Office area (Ilorin West LGA; N08°29.398’ E004°33.621’), Oyun (Ilorin East LGA; N08°24’55.54”, E4°36’49.78”), and Amoyo (Ifelodun LGA; N08°24’30.82”, E4°37’32.38”) (Fig. 1). Kwara State experiences a tropical climate with a mean annual temperature of 27 °C, relative humidity of 76%, and annual rainfall of approximately 1800 mm. The region has two distinct seasons: a rainy season (May-October) and a dry season (November-April). Vegetation is characteristic of the Guinea savannah zone, dominated by tall grasses frequently affected by bush burning during the dry season [14].

Sampling methodology

Larvae of Anopheles mosquitoes were collected during the early morning hours (07:00–10:00) from each location using the standard dipping method [15]. Approximately 80–90 larvae per site were collected from natural and artificial breeding sites (puddles, tyre tracks, and agricultural ditches). Third and fourth-instar larvae were transported to the Entomology Laboratory, University of Ilorin, in plastic containers with habitat water. Larvae were reared under insectary conditions (27 °C ± 2 °C; 78% ± 3% relative humidity; 12:12 light: dark photoperiod) in enamel pans and fed ground fish food (Tetramin®) ad libitum. Pupae were collected daily and transferred to emergence cages. Upon emergence, adult mosquitoes were identified morphologically using standard keys [16] and maintained on 10% glucose solution. Only non-blood-fed female Anopheles mosquitoes aged 2–5 days were used for bioassays.

Sample size was determined based on WHO guidelines for insecticide resistance monitoring, which recommend testing a minimum of 100 mosquitoes per insecticide per site [17]. This sample size provides 80% power to detect a 15% difference in mortality between sites at α = 0.05. For molecular analyses, a random subset of 250 samples (approximately 83 per site) was selected to provide robust allele frequency estimates with acceptable confidence intervals.

Insecticide susceptibility bioassay

WHO susceptibility bioassays were conducted following standard procedures [17, 18]. WHO-impregnated insecticide papers with discriminating concentrations were used: Permethrin (0.75%), Deltamethrin (0.05%), and Alphacypermethrin (0.05%). For each insecticide and location, four replicates of 25 female mosquitoes (total n = 100 per insecticide per site) were exposed in WHO test tubes. Mosquitoes were introduced into holding tubes for 1 h of acclimatisation, then transferred to exposure tubes lined with insecticide-impregnated papers. Knockdown was recorded at 10, 15, 20, 30, 40, 50, and 60 min. After 60 min of exposure, mosquitoes were transferred back to holding tubes and provided with 10% glucose solution. Final mortality was recorded after 24 h. Control groups (n = 25 per site) were exposed to untreated papers. Tests were conducted at 27 °C ± 2 °C and 75% ± 10% relative humidity.

Susceptibility status was classified according to WHO criteria [17]: mortality ≥ 98% indicates susceptibility, 90–97% suggests possible resistance requiring confirmation, and < 90% indicates resistance. In the event that mortality was observed in control setups, Abbott’s formula [19] would be employed for the correction of mortality; however, no control mortality was recorded in this study.

Following bioassays, surviving (resistant phenotype) and dead (susceptible phenotype) mosquitoes were separated, preserved individually in 1.5 mL Eppendorf tubes containing silica gel, and stored at −20 °C for molecular analysis.

Molecular Identification of Anopheles species

Genomic DNA was extracted from mosquito legs and wings (to preserve whole-body samples for potential future analyses) using the method described by Cornel and Collins [20]. Individual samples were homogenised in 50 µL extraction buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.2, 1 mM EDTA, 25 mM NaCl, 200 µg/mL proteinase K), incubated at 56 °C for 2 h, followed by proteinase K inactivation at 95 °C for 10 min. Extracted genomic DNA was stored at −20 °C until use.

Sibling species identification was performed using the polymerase chain reaction (PCR) protocol of Cornel and Collins [20] as modified by Akeju et al. [21]. Five primers targeting the ribosomal DNA intergenic spacer region were used: ME (5’-TGACCAACCCACTCCCTTGA-3’), AR (5’-AAGTGTCCTTCTCCATCCTA-3’), QD (5’-CAGACCAAGATGGTTAGTAT-3’), UN (5’-GTGTGCCCCTTCCTCGATGT-3’), and GA (5’-CTGGTTTGGTCGGCACGTTT-3’).

Each 12.5 µL PCR reaction contained: 5.5 µL ddH₂O, 2.5 µL 5× Pre-mix buffer, 0.5 µL each primer (10 µM), and 1.0 µL DNA template. Thermal cycling conditions were: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 5 min; 30 cycles of 95 °C for 30 s, 59.2 °C for 30 s, 72 °C for 30 s; final extension at 72 °C for 5 min; hold at 4 °C. PCR products (10 µL) were electrophoresed on 1.5% agarose gels stained with ethidium bromide and visualised under UV transillumination. Product sizes were: An. arabiensis (315 bp), An. gambiae s.s. (390 bp), and An. coluzzii (450–500 bp). To distinguish An. gambiae s.s. from An. coluzzii, PCR-restriction fragment length polymorphism (RFLP) analysis was performed using the enzyme HhaI as described by Akeju et al. [21].

Detection of kdr (L1014F) mutation

The L1014F kdr mutation was detected using allele-specific PCR (AS-PCR) following the protocol of Mayi et al. [22]. Four primers were used: Agd1 (5’-ATAGATTCCCCGACCATG-3’), Agd2 (5’-AGACAAGGATGATGAACC-3’), Agd3 (5’-AATTTGCATTACTTACGACA-3’), and Agd4 (5’-CTGTAGTGATAGGAAATTTA-3’). PCR reactions (20 µL) contained 12.5 µL master mix (1× PCR buffer with 1.5 mM MgCl₂, 0.2 mM each dNTP, 0.4 µM each primer, 1unit Taq polymerase) and 1 µL genomic DNA template. Cycling conditions: 95 °C for 5 min; 40 cycles of 95 °C for 1 min, 48 °C for 2 min, 72 °C for 2 min; final extension 72 °C for 10 min; hold at 10 °C. Gel electrophoresis was used to analyse the amplified fragments using 1.5% agarose gels and visualized by ethidium bromide staining under ultraviolet (UV) light. Samples showing both the susceptible (195 bp) and resistant (137 bp) bands were classified as heterozygous; samples showing only the resistant band were homozygous resistant; samples showing only the susceptible band were homozygous susceptible. Negative controls (no DNA) were included in all runs.

Data analysis

The insecticide resistance bioassay result was analysed and interpreted using WHO criteria (WHO, 2016). Percentage mortality was calculated as follows:

Knockdown times (KDT₅₀ and KDT₉₅) were estimated by log-time probit analysis using IBM SPSS Statistics version 22.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The probit model regressed probit-transformed mortality against log₁₀-transformed exposure time, generating KDT estimates with 95% confidence intervals.

Allele frequencies were calculated by direct counting. For kdr genotypes, the frequency of the resistant allele (R) was calculated as: f(R) = (2 × NRR + NRs)/(2 × N total), where NRR is the number of homozygous resistant, NRs is the number of heterozygous, and N total is the total genotyped. Confidence intervals (95%) for proportions were calculated using the Wilson score method [23].

Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium was tested using the chi-square goodness-of-fit test with 1 degree of freedom. Differences in mortality among locations and insecticides were analysed using chi-square tests with significance set at α = 0.05. Species-specific resistance patterns were examined by comparing mortality rates among species using Fisher’s exact test due to small sample sizes in some categories.

Results

Sibling species composition

A total of 250 Anopheles gambiae s.l. Mosquitoes collected from three LGAs in Kwara State were analysed molecularly. Morphological identification confirmed all samples as members of the An. gambiae complex. Molecular analysis revealed the presence of three sibling species (Fig. 2). Anopheles coluzzii was the most prevalent species, comprising 78 samples (31.2%, 95% CI: 25.7–37.3%), followed by An. gambiae s.s. with 42 samples (16.8%, 95% CI: 12.6–22.0%), and An. arabiensis with 34 samples (13.6%, 95% CI: 9.9–18.4%). Ninety-six samples (38.4%, 95% CI: 14.1–23.7%) failed to amplify and could not be identified to species level.

Species composition varied among locations (Table 1). In Ilorin West (Post Office), An. coluzzii predominated (32/85; 37.6%), followed by An. gambiae s.s. (14/85; 16.5%) and An. arabiensis (10/85; 11.8%). In Ilorin East (Oyun), An. coluzzii was also most abundant (28/83; 33.7%), with An. gambiae s.s. (15/83; 18.1%) and An. arabiensis (12/83; 14.5%). Ifelodun (Amoyo) showed similar patterns: An. coluzzii (18/82; 22.0%), An. gambiae s.s. (13/82; 15.9%), and An. arabiensis (12/82; 14.6%). The proportion of non-amplifying samples ranged from 14.5% (12/83) in Ilorin East to 23.5% (20/85) in Ilorin West.

Species-specific resistance patterns

Analysis of mortality stratified by sibling species (where species identification was successful) revealed differences in susceptibility (Table 3). Among 104 samples with known species and resistance phenotype, An. arabiensis showed the highest mortality (20/34; 58.8%), followed by An. gambiae s.s. (22/42; 52.4%), and An. coluzzii (32/78; 41.0%). However, these differences did not reach statistical significance (Fisher’s exact test, p = 0.172), likely due to limited sample sizes when stratified by species and insecticides.

kdr mutation frequency

Analysis of the L1014F kdr mutation was performed on all 250 samples (including both resistant and susceptible phenotypes). The results revealed 104 homozygous resistant (RR) samples (41.6%, 95% CI: 35.6–47.9%), 0 heterozygous (Rs) samples (0.0%, 95% CI: 0.0–1.5%), and 146 homozygous susceptible (rr) samples (58.4%, 95% CI: 52.1–64.4%) (Table 4). The frequency of the resistant (R) allele was 0.416 (95% CI: 0.369–0.465). The complete absence of heterozygotes represents a significant deviation from the Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium (χ²=64.2, df = 1, p < 0.001).

Association between kdr genotype and resistance phenotype

Among the 250 mosquito samples with both genotype and phenotype data (alive/dead after 24 h), a strong association was observed between kdr genotype and resistance phenotype (Table 6). Of the 104 RR samples, 92 (88.5%) were alive (resistant phenotype) after 24 h, while only 12 (11.5%) were dead. In contrast, among the 146 rr samples, 123 (84.2%) were dead (susceptible phenotype), and only 23 (15.8%) were alive. This association was highly significant (χ²=134.8, df = 1, p < 0.001). The odds ratio for resistance in RR versus rr genotypes was 41.0 (95% CI: 19.1–88.1), indicating a strong contribution of the kdr mutation to the resistant phenotype.

Association between kdr genotype and resistance phenotype

Discussion

This study provides a comprehensive assessment of pyrethroid resistance, sibling species composition, and kdr mutation frequency in Anopheles gambiae s.l. populations from three ecologically distinct locations in Kwara State, North-Central Nigeria. Our findings document widespread resistance to all three tested pyrethroids, a predominance of An. coluzzii, and a high frequency of the L1014F kdr mutation that shows a strong association with the resistant phenotype. These results have important implications for malaria vector control in the region.

Sibling species composition

The predominance of An. coluzzii over An. gambiae s.s. and An. arabiensis aligns with recent reports from various Nigerian ecological zones [5, 24, 25]. Adeogun et al. [5] documented similar patterns across Nigeria, noting that An. coluzzii has become increasingly prevalent even in areas where An. gambiae s.s. was historically dominant. This shift may reflect the greater ecological plasticity of An. coluzzii, particularly its ability to exploit permanent breeding sites and withstand drier conditions [26]. Ebhodaghe et al. [9] recently demonstrated that An. coluzzii exhibits broader breeding site preferences than its sibling species, which may confer competitive advantages in anthropogenically modified landscapes.

The presence of An. arabiensis in all three locations, though at lower frequencies, is noteworthy given its typically greater abundance in drier, more savannah-like environments [27]. Its persistence alongside An. coluzzii and An. gambiae s.s. suggests ecological niche partitioning, with An. arabiensis potentially exploiting different microhabitats or exhibiting different host-seeking behaviours [28]. This sympatric occurrence of all three major vectors complicates control efforts, as behavioural and physiological differences among species may necessitate tailored interventions [29].

The 38.4% of samples that failed to amplify warrants discussion. Several factors may explain this: (1) DNA degradation during field collection and storage, particularly given challenges in maintaining cold chains; (2) presence of PCR inhibitors co-extracted with DNA; (3) suboptimal primer binding due to genetic variation in primer annealing sites; or (4) the theoretical possibility of additional cryptic species not targeted by our assay. However, given the extensive characterisation of the An. gambiae complex in Nigeria [5, 30], the latter explanation is unlikely. Similar amplification failure rates (15–25%) have been reported in other field studies [21, 31], highlighting the challenges of working with field-collected samples.

Pyrethroid resistance phenotypes

The universal resistance to all three pyrethroids across all locations (mortality range: 20–64%) is consistent with the escalating resistance crisis documented across sub-Saharan Africa [7, 32]. Minwuyelet et al. [7] in their meta-analysis reported that pyrethroid resistance now affects > 90% of malaria-endemic sites in Africa, with resistance intensities increasing over time. Our findings confirm that Kwara State is no exception to this trend.

The geographic variation in resistance levels within Kwara State is particularly striking. Ilorin West (Post Office) showed the highest resistance (lowest mortality), while Ilorin East (Oyun) showed comparatively lower resistance. This heterogeneity likely reflects differences in historical insecticide exposure. Ilorin West is the most urbanised study site, with higher population density and potentially greater domestic use of insecticides (aerosol sprays, mosquito coils, etc.) that may exert additional selection pressure [33]. Similar urbanisation gradients in resistance have been reported in other Nigerian cities [34, 35].

The differential knockdown rates among pyrethroids, Deltamethrin showing the fastest action, and Permethrin showing the slowest, have practical implications. Permethrin-impregnated LLINs have been the most widely distributed nets in Nigeria historically [36], potentially selecting for populations with enhanced tolerance specifically to Permethrin. This “signature” of insecticide-specific selection pressure has been observed elsewhere [37]. The slow knockdown to Permethrin, particularly in Ilorin West, where KDT₅₀ exceeded 90 min, suggests that local populations may have developed behavioural or physiological adaptations that delay the toxic effects of this specific compound.

Species-specific resistance patterns

Although not statistically significant, the trend towards higher susceptibility in An. arabiensis compared to An. coluzzii aligns with some previous studies [38, 39] but contrasts with a previous study [40]. Gueye et al. [12] in Senegal reported comparable resistance levels between An. gambiae s.s. and An. arabiensis, while Dortey et al. [10] in Ghana reported species-specific differences in metabolic resistance mechanisms. The lack of statistical significance in our species-stratified analysis likely reflects limited sample sizes when disaggregated; larger studies specifically powered to detect species differences are needed.

kdr mutation frequency and association with resistance

The high frequency of the L1014F kdr mutation (allele frequency 0.416) and its strong association with the resistant phenotype (OR = 41.0) confirm that target-site resistance is a major mechanism operating in these populations. The 88.5% of RR individuals exhibiting resistant phenotypes demonstrates the functional significance of this mutation. Importantly, however, 15.8% of rr individuals also survived, indicating that other resistance mechanisms—likely metabolic detoxification by cytochrome P450 monooxygenases, esterases, or glutathione S-transferases—are also operating [41, 42]. This multiplicity of mechanisms complicates resistance management, as removing selection for kdr alone may not restore susceptibility if metabolic resistance persists [43].

The complete absence of heterozygotes (0/250) represents a dramatic deviation from Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium. Several explanations are possible: (1) extremely strong selection pressure has rapidly fixed the resistant allele in some populations while eliminating heterozygotes; (2) assortative mating among resistant individuals; (3) fitness costs associated with heterozygosity; or (4) technical issues with heterozygote detection in our assay. The last possibility seems unlikely given that our AS-PCR assay has been extensively validated and can reliably detect heterozygotes [22, 44]. More plausibly, the population may be structured into subpopulations with different allele frequencies (Wahlund effect), or there may be ongoing selection that has eliminated intermediate genotypes. Similar findings of heterozygote deficiency have been reported in other highly selected insecticide-resistant populations [45, 46]. This phenomenon warrants further investigation through population genetic studies with larger sample sizes and additional markers.

The species-specific kdr frequencies, though not statistically different, showed An. coluzzii with the highest RR frequency, consistent with its predominance and possibly greater exposure to selection pressures in the anthropised environments it favours [47]. The presence of the kdr mutation in An. arabiensis is noteworthy, as this species was historically considered less likely to develop target-site resistance due to its more zoophilic behaviour [48]. However, increasing reports of kdr in An. arabiensis from Nigeria [34, 49] and elsewhere [12] suggest that this species is also coming under significant insecticide pressure.

Implications for vector control

These findings have several practical implications for malaria control in Kwara State and similar ecoregions. Firstly, the widespread pyrethroid resistance documented here suggests that standard pyrethroid-only LLINs may have significantly reduced effectiveness. The WHO now recommends deploying nets treated with pyrethroid plus synergist (piperonyl butoxide) or dual active ingredient nets (e.g., pyrethroid-chlorfenapyr Interceptor® G2, or pyrethroid-pyriproxyfen Royal Guard®) in areas with confirmed resistance [50]. Secondly, the heterogeneity in resistance levels among nearby locations (e.g., Ilorin West vs. Ilorin East) underscores the importance of local, rather than regional or national, resistance data for guiding intervention choices. “One-size-fits-all” approaches are unlikely to be optimal in landscapes with fine-scale variation in selection pressures and species composition. Thirdly, the high frequency of kdr mutations and absence of heterozygotes suggests extremely strong selection pressure that may also be selecting for other adaptive traits. Rotation of insecticides with different modes of action, where feasible for IRS, could help manage resistance [51]. Fourthly, the presence of all three major vectors necessitates interventions that address the full range of behavioural and ecological diversity. For example, if An. arabiensis exhibits more outdoor biting, LLINs and IRS alone may be insufficient, and supplementary tools (larval source management, spatial repellents) may be needed [52].

Study limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. Firstly, our sample sizes, while adequate for overall resistance and allele frequency estimation, were insufficient for robust species-stratified analyses. Secondly, we did not investigate metabolic resistance mechanisms, which likely contribute to the resistance observed in rr individuals. Thirdly, the high proportion of non-amplifying samples (particularly in Ifelodun) may have introduced bias in species composition estimates. Fourthly, we did not assess the intensity of resistance (dose-response assays), which would provide additional information on the strength of selection. Fifthly, our study design cannot distinguish between inherited resistance and maternal effects or epigenetic contributions. Future studies should address these gaps through larger sample sizes, inclusion of synergist assays (e.g., piperonyl butoxide), and investigation of other resistance mechanisms.

Conclusion

This study provides the first detailed characterisation of pyrethroid resistance, sibling species composition, and kdr mutation frequency in Anopheles gambiae s.l. populations from the Sudan Savannah ecoregion of Kwara State, Nigeria. Our key findings are: (1) universal resistance to Permethrin, Deltamethrin, and Alphacypermethrin across all study sites, with significant geographic variation; (2) predominance of An. coluzzii over An. gambiae s.s. and An. arabiensis; (3) high frequency (41.6%) of the L1014F kdr mutation with strong association with resistance phenotype; and (4) a striking absence of heterozygotes, suggesting intense selection pressure.

The novel contributions of this study include: documentation of fine-scale spatial heterogeneity in resistance within a relatively small geographic area; species-specific resistance trends (though not statistically significant) that suggest differential selection pressures; and the unexpected complete absence of heterozygotes, which raises important questions about population structure and selection dynamics.

These findings underscore the urgent need for: (1) continuous, locally-sensitive resistance monitoring; (2) deployment of next-generation vector control tools (e.g., dual-active-ingredient nets) in areas with confirmed resistance; (3) investigation of non-target-site resistance mechanisms operating in these populations; (4) studies to understand the population genetic structure underlying the heterozygote deficiency; and (5) integrated vector management strategies that address the full diversity of vector species and behaviours.

Without urgent action to monitor and manage insecticide resistance, the substantial gains made in malaria control over the past two decades are at risk of erosion. Local evidence, such as that provided by this study, must inform evidence-based decisions to sustain progress toward malaria elimination in Nigeria and across sub-Saharan Africa.
