A qualitative study of Chicago gay men and the Mpox outbreak of 2022 in the context of HIV/AIDS, PrEP, and COVID-19
Authors: Daniel Hughes, Jeffrey Ai, Parsia Vazirnia, Tyson McLeish, Chandler Krajco, Richard Moraga, Katherine Quinn
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
During the 2022 mpox outbreak, our study conducted 30 interviews with a recruited sample of Chicago gay men (age 18+) during June-September to investigate their experiences of mpox, HIV/AIDS, and COVID-19. Participants were interviewed with a semi-structured guide about gay sexual identity and social experiences; HIV/AIDS, ART, and PrEP; and COVID-19 behaviors and vaccination. All 30 interview respondents had been fully vaccinated for COVID-19 and expressed minimal COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy. All the men living with HIV in our study were on ART with HIV well controlled. A majority of HIV- participants (70%) were on PrEP, with participants universally aware of PrEP benefits. Additionally, most participants had already received at least one shot of the Jynneos mpox vaccine, with many interviewees enduring long lines, sometimes at multiple locations, before vaccination in primarily gay social spaces. These Chicago gay men demonstrated widespread enthusiasm for mpox vaccination as a disease prevention strategy and most of them had already been vaccinated despite significant barriers. The enthusiasm of the participants in our study emerged within a medical landscape shaped by both COVID-19 vaccination and HIV/AIDS-related health interventions including ART and PrEP, which may have helped instill increased medical trust among this population. Our study suggests that out urban gay men may comprise a distinctive minority population with increased medical trust due to specific social, sexual, and historical experiences.

Introduction

The mpox (formerly known as monkeypox) epidemic of 2022 in North America and Europe marked the first widespread incidence of the disease with extensive community spread outside of Africa. As of September 2023, the Centers for Disease Control estimated 31,010 U.S. cases with 55 deaths, and 91,328 global cases during the 2022 Mpox Outbreak [1]. In the United States, approximately 99% of mpox cases were among men and 94% of cases reported recent male-to-male sexual or intimate contact [2]. As it became clear that most mpox infections were occurring among men who have sex with men (MSM), how to best message preventive strategies, including limited supplies of a vaccine, became an acute challenge for public health authorities, particularly in populous urban areas in the United States and Europe where case incidence was rising sharply in the summer of 2022 among MSM. Simultaneously, the mpox epidemic occurred during the tail end of the COVID-19 pandemic which resulted in the death of over 1 million Americans, introduced a mass public vaccination effort that was controversial, and brought about the most stringent public health measures taken in the United States since the “Spanish Flu” (H1N1) epidemic of 1918–1920—including masking measures that were also often contentious [3]. Moreover, HIV/AIDS, while having become a manageable chronic disease since the 1990s due to pharmaceutical interventions including ART and PrEP, continued to present acute health challenges to MSM during the 2022 mpox outbreak [4].

In Chicago, the first mpox case of the 2022 outbreak was reported on June 2 [5]. On a national level, President Joseph Biden announced the first phase of the national mpox vaccine strategy on June 28 [6]. While the 2003 mpox outbreak had seen the Centers for Disease Control recommend an older smallpox vaccine (given the similarity between mpox and smallpox as fellow members of the orthopoxvirus family), the 2022 outbreak utilized the newer Jynneos vaccine made by Bavarian Nordic in Denmark as a cornerstone of prevention strategy. In Chicago, Jynneos vaccine distribution to MSM and others at high risk began the week of June 18, 2022 [7]. Across the United States, the Jynneos vaccine roll-out was plagued, particularly in June and July 2022, by limited supply, at a time when cases were skyrocketing among MSM [8]. This trend was true in Chicago as well. In a July 22 statement, the Chicago Department of Public Health stated: “We simply need more [Jynneos] vaccine in Chicago” [9].

Cases in Chicago peaked the week of July 30, 2022, and an mpox public health emergency was declared in the state of Illinois on August 1 [10]. By the end of August 2022, weekly cases in Chicago would be at less than a third of the late July peak, and by November, every subsequent week would see reporting of cases in the single digits [7]. On December 2, officials from the Biden administration stated that they would lift the mpox public health emergency, and on January 31, 2023, the designation expired without renewal [11]. As of October 2023, over 1200 people, overwhelmingly MSM, had been diagnosed with mpox in Chicago, with 66 hospitalizations and 4 deaths; over 50,000 doses of the Jynneos vaccines had been distributed in the city [7]. Mpox infection data were almost certainly heavily undercounted given challenges with testing accessibility [12]. Unfortunately, Chicago experienced a small resurgence of mpox cases beginning in spring 2023. Worryingly, several of these cases occurred in individuals who had been vaccinated for mpox and at least one occurred in a patient who had formerly contracted mpox [13].

Within this complex social and medical context, our qualitative study sought, via thirty in-depth interviews conducted during the summer of 2022, to assess the views of gay men living in Chicago, Illinois toward mpox and vaccination, particularly in relationship to social dynamics and their interrelationship with the ongoing health crises of COVID-19 and HIV/AIDS.

Unfortunately, stigma against gay men has a long history in the United States including the legacy of anti-sodomy laws, many of which were still in place in the 1980s when the public HIV/AIDS crisis began [14]. Anti-gay discrimination hindered some early efforts to combat HIV/AIDS, sparking a wave of HIV activism in the 1980s and 1990s that sought to lessen the effect of anti-gay stigma in medical care. Stigma often stems from processes of “othering,” whereby certain groups are labeled as abnormal and of lower value, resulting in social distancing, mistreatment, and reduced access to resources [15].

The emergence of mpox as a global public health emergency had a disproportionate impact on gay men and other MSM. This trend was exacerbated by homophobic discourse on social media platforms including Twitter, where hashtags including “#gaypox” perpetuated harmful stereotypes and scapegoated gay men for the mpox outbreak [16]. Embedded within these broader societal challenges, the stigma surrounding mpox, HIV/AIDS, and male homosexuality further complicated intervention efforts, underscoring the imperative for both combating anti-gay stigma and utilizing gay community resources. Gay spaces and gay neighborhood presence have been found to be positively correlated with protective sexual behaviors among gay men [17]. Moreover, commercial gay spaces including bars and clubs have evolved during recent years to become occasional delivery venues of health care services for gay men. In the 1980s during the first stage of the public HIV/AIDS crisis, vigorous debates occurred over whether gay bathhouses and sex clubs in San Francisco and other cities should be closed due to risk of HIV transmission, or whether these venues should remain open and be utilized as part of safer sex campaigns [18]. Voices urging their closure prevailed in several cities including San Francisco. Nonetheless, health interventions including STI testing have subsequently been made available in many gay spaces including remaining or more recently opened bathhouses [19]. Offering testing services in these gay spaces may both reduce stigma and make healthcare resources more accessible to at-risk MSM populations, including some closeted men who may otherwise be difficult to reach [20]. One study found a reduction in HIV-related risk behaviors and a greater propensity to undergo STI testing correlated with participation in bathhouse-based public health outreach [19]. Efforts to undertake mpox vaccination campaigns in gay spaces in Chicago, including at a popular Boystown bathhouse as well as at bars and clubs, thus emerged within a context where similar interventions against HIV and for STI testing had been proven successful in gay spaces.

Methods

Recruitment and study procedures

Between June 2022 and September 2022, semi-structured, in-depth interviews were conducted via Zoom with 30 self-identified gay men living in Chicago to understand more about their attitudes and behaviors related to the unfolding mpox epidemic. Self-identified cisgender gay men living in Chicago who were 18 + years of age were eligible to participate.

Recruitment occurred via flyers, direct recruiting, and snowball referrals. Most direct recruiting occurred on North Halsted Street in Chicago in the heart of the Boystown neighborhood and primarily during popular street events for Gay Pride (June 2022) and Market Days (August 2022). Interested participants were screened for eligibility and scheduled a time for the interview. Immediately prior to completing the interview, participants completed the informed consent process and were sent a brief Qualtrics survey1 to collect relevant demographic data. The interviews lasted for approximately one hour each. Our interview questions focused primarily on participants’ experiences or knowledge of HIV, including treatment and PrEP; COVID-19 experiences and behaviors; and mpox awareness and vaccination. Participants were asked detailed questions about their beliefs and behaviors in the context of overlapping mpox, COVID-19, and HIV/AIDS health crises, as well as related social and sexual dynamics and experiences of clinical medicine more broadly. A representative sample of questions is included as Table 1: Representative Interview Guide Questions. The participants were compensated for their participation with $30 Amazon gift cards. All interviewees are referred to by pseudonyms.

Data analysis

All interviews were conducted and recorded via Zoom. After those digital recordings were made, interviews were transcribed into written format via Rev.com and checked for accuracy by the lead author. Corrections were made as needed for misspelled words or other grammatical inaccuracies. All interviews were then coded using MAXQDA software2 by a team of four researchers using a codebook of inductive and deductive codes that covered the primary content areas including HIV/AIDS and PrEP; COVID-19, including social and sexual aspects of the pandemic and vaccination; and mpox, including awareness, social effects, and vaccination. The interview team used a consensus-based coding approach, which required frequent group discussions about coding decisions, definitions, and approaches [21]. Coded interviews were analyzed using codebook thematic analysis, which included an iterative process of reviewing transcripts, writing, discussing preliminary themes, situating themes within the literature, and returning to the transcripts [22].

Our sample was relatively well educated (7 men with having “started college” and the other 23 men having “completed college”), employed (83% with either full time or part time work), and economically comfortable (only 10% of interviewees reported income less than $30,000 per year). Three of the study participants were living with HIV; these men were all on ART. From among our 27 participants who were HIV-negative, 19 (70%) were currently taking PrEP to prevent HIV infection. The HIV negative men who were not taking PrEP at the time of our interview were universally aware of its effectiveness; several of them had taken PrEP in the past and were now either monogamous with one male partner or not sexually active. All thirty of the participants stated that they had been fully vaccinated for COVID-19 and 29/30 had received at least one booster. Furthermore, 28 out of the 30 participants had received at least one shot of the Jynneos mpox vaccine at the time of our interview.

Our data are organized around the following themes: (1) Stigma, HIV, and “Gay Disease,” (2) Mpox & COVID-19, (3) Mpox Vaccination & Gay-Oriented Social Spaces. These themes were selected to understand more about the social and medical context in which the 2022 mpox outbreak emerged among Chicago gay men. The investigation of these themes undergirds our interpretation of why mpox vaccination was embraced heartily by gay men in Chicago during the summer of 2022.

At the time of interviews (June-September 2022), mpox was referred to nearly universally as “monkeypox.” Because in November 2022, the World Health Organization suggested the new synonym of “mpox” for “monkeypox,” this article uses the mpox convention, while interview participants near universally used the term “monkeypox” [23]. We retain the use of “monkeypox” when quoting participants throughout the results.

As is evidenced in this commentary, PrEP has thus created a context wherein participants perceived themselves to have much more freedom with sexual behavior choices and partners while largely obviating the need to worry about HIV/AIDS. Because medical providers in the United States typically require STI check-ups every three months for other sexually transmitted infections including gonorrhea, chlamydia, and syphilis, PrEP necessitates more frequent interactions with medical care for gay men who use the medication. The 2022 mpox outbreak thus emerged within this context of a remarkably close confluence of modern medicine with the ability to make freer sexual choices that had reshaped the social dynamics of the Chicago gay male community in the prior decade.

Many participants referenced mpox as a purported “gay disease,” given the strong concentration of the North American 2022 mpox outbreak among gay men. Accordingly, the public discussion of mpox as a specifically “gay disease” in parallel to HIV/AIDS was salient in the minds of many interviewees, including the possibility of inviting stigma and backlash against gay men. These perceptions existed alongside the observation that as with the early years of HIV/AIDS, informal gay social networks and gay owned and operated businesses played an important role in promoting gay men’s health. In various attributes, namely (1) the role of disease stigma, (2) a worry that the perception of mpox as a gay disease could boomerang negatively upon gay men, and (3) the reliance on gay-oriented social networks and businesses for public health information and intervention, participants evoked parallels between the mpox outbreak of 2022 and the ongoing crisis of HIV/AIDS among gay men.

Mpox & COVID-19

Mpox vaccination occurred in the summer of 2022 in Chicago during the waning days of the COVID-19 pandemic when nearly all interviewees had embraced COVID-19 vaccination. Indeed, 100% of participants had been vaccinated for COVID-19. Accordingly, participants often perceived COVID-19 vaccination as simply a commonsense health intervention for gay men within a spectrum of other appropriate health promotion behaviors. Discussion

Results from this study demonstrate how gay men in Chicago viewed and responded to the mpox outbreak in the summer of 2022. Perhaps the most striking finding from our results is the enthusiastic embrace of both mpox vaccination and COVID-19 vaccination by nearly all our thirty gay male participants. Gay men represent a minority population even in urban centers like Chicago. Research has shown that broadly speaking, minorities within the United States—particularly Blacks and Hispanics as compared with Whites—often have reduced trust for physicians and medicine [24]. By contrast, we found a particularly enthusiastic embrace of mpox vaccination among our sample of urban gay men, admittedly a predominantly White sample. A community-led response to the mpox health crisis proved singularly successful against an alarming pathogen with the potential for wider community spread. While minority status of varying kinds is often associated with barriers to health care, the gay community of Chicago was able to aggressively deploy mpox vaccination to stem the tide of an alarming new disease in its midst.

In part, the findings reflect the sample demographics, which included an enthusiastic embrace of COVID-19 vaccination and high rates of PrEP use (70%), which suggests greater engagement in preventive healthcare. Additionally, all study participants were “out,” which may also have influenced willingness to receive an mpox vaccine. One study from the Netherlands found that MSM were more likely to accept mpox vaccination if they felt a sense of connection to the local gay community [25]. As such, the gay community can serve an important role in supporting health and vaccination efforts both directly, by partnering with health departments to provide vaccines, and via creating supportive community spaces.

As evidenced in this study, the 2022 mpox outbreak in Chicago was largely tamed by the efforts of community groups and gay-owned and operated businesses—Steamworks Bathhouse prominent among them. In part, this stemmed from the limited availability and slow rollout of the mpox vaccine and recent public health lessons learned about the need for community-placed public health initiatives [26]. The CDC partnered with community organizations across the United States to improve vaccination coverage and overcome stigma including through use of mobile vans and at large festivals and Pride events [27–29]. Such events were widely successful, achieving many vaccinations. Additionally, other mpox vaccination events nationwide, including those described by study participants, included grassroots efforts, information sharing by word-of-mouth, and partnerships with community bars, clubs, and bathhouses. Health care delivery occurring in gay venues likely lessened a sense of stigma around mpox and vaccination.

Such community-engaged strategies were, in part, developed to support equitable rollout of the vaccine [26]. However, despite the value of having community-based mpox vaccine events, this strategy may have also contributed to inequities in distribution and disparities in mpox vaccination in some instances. For example, men noted they waited in lines outside Steamworks Bathhouse, while local news stations covered the story. For individuals who did not openly identify as gay and those who were anxious about stigma or confidentiality, this may have been a barrier to access. Furthermore, as study participants explained, vaccines were at times preferentially available to paying customers, which limited who was able to receive a vaccine. Indeed, there were disparities in mpox vaccination in some locales, with White men and insured patients more likely to get vaccinated [30, 31]. Nevertheless, Steamworks Bathhouse and similar community organizations and advocates appear to have filled a critical public health gap. The response to mpox from within the gay community represents an important community-driven intervention.

The enduring context of HIV/AIDS is crucial to understanding the embrace of the mpox vaccine among Chicago gay men. Increased medical trust among gay men appears to be buttressed by the crucial role that antiretrovirals continue to play in rendering HIV a chronic illness and the role of PrEP in HIV prevention. As our participants’ responses revealed, PrEP both enables condomless sex without worry of contracting HIV and mediates a closer relationship between patients and medical providers [32]. It is not an understatement to say that PrEP revolutionized gay sexual and social life, both for gay men who are HIV-negative and for men living with HIV, whose HIV-negative partners were able to ensure their own continued sero-negativity via PrEP. Among other factors, HIV/AIDS-related medical interventions and their extraordinary success may well have paved the way for urban gay men to be more receptive to medical interventions, including the mpox vaccine.

The 2022 mpox outbreak emerged on the heels of the COVID-19 pandemic. The Chicago gay men in our study had embraced COVID-19 vaccination at an essentially universal level for reasons that included increased trust in science and medicine and the desire to return to socialization as rapidly as possible. Within that context, mpox vaccination often seemed like a similarly logical next step that could both protect personal health and act as a social portal to gay milieu that are often crowded and sexually adventurous.

In the early days of the 2022 mpox outbreak, some media reports declined to mention that almost all cases were occurring among MSM [33]. While this may have been a well-intentioned move to limit stigma, it may also have slowed down targeting of resources to the people who most needed mpox prevention strategies, including vaccination. As participants mentioned, they too worried about stigma, but their predominant concern was protecting their health.

Unfortunately, mpox cases were once again recorded in Chicago beginning in April 2023, with dozens of 2023–2024 cases recorded [7]. Many of these cases have occurred in vaccinated men and at least one occurred in a man who had formerly been diagnosed with mpox. These apparently vaccine-resistant mpox cases are intimidating new territory. In concert with the University of Illinois Chicago Response Team, Steamworks Bathhouse periodically re-instituted weekly mpox vaccination clinics. While the path forward to fully eradicating mpox may be murky, the representative institutions and attitudes among gay men are already strong.

The enthusiastic embrace of vaccines by the Chicago gay men in this sample is useful to contemplate on other fronts. Perhaps there are lessons that can be learned concerning how a distinct American demographic minority came to have trust in medical interventions that might be applied in building greater trust between medical providers and communities that are vaccine-resistant or disproportionately inclined to lower trust in medicine due to historical legacies of medical mistreatment or neglect. Additionally, as both the mpox and COVID-19 health crises demonstrate, novel pathogens (and known pathogens outside of endemic regions) will likely play an outsize role in future epidemics due to globalization, rising world populations, and other factors. Understanding more about what factors build trust in novel vaccines will be important in facing down this brave new world of novel pathogens. Along these lines, a medical team from Bellevue Hospital in New York City has written a case report from the 2022 Mpox Epidemic that is intended to be applicable to future outbreaks of novel pathogens based on what has been learned in the mpox context [34].

There are limitations to note to our study. Our sample was predominantly White, relatively well educated, economically comfortable, and well employed. These factors may have influenced increased medical trust in our sample. Furthermore, the interviewees were primarily recruited at gay-oriented public events and tended to be “out” and relatively comfortable discussing their sexual orientation and related topics. Also, while the focus of Chicago Market Days is LGBT + entertainment and community, mpox vaccinations were offered at that event, likely drawing MSM who were more favorably inclined toward vaccination. 70% of HIV-negative participants were also taking PrEP, which is significantly higher than national averages [35]. By contrast, the Centers for Disease Control estimated that during 2022, 31.4% of PrEP-eligible individuals had recently received a prescription. Our participants’ engagement with the healthcare system and comfort with preventive medicine may have increased their favorability toward the mpox vaccine.

The structure of recruitment for our study thus meant that we interviewed openly gay men in the urban setting of Chicago. No effort was made to recruit MSM who may not identify as gay or who may not feel comfortable in gay community settings. The fact that all our participants were “out” may also be a factor in their near universal endorsement of mpox vaccination. Finally, most of this sample was White. Black and Latino MSM may not have the same high levels of mpox vaccination trust and endorsement, and additional research is needed that more broadly examines their perceptions of and access to mpox vaccine.

The thirty Chicago gay men interviewed for our study showed widespread enthusiasm for mpox vaccination as a disease prevention strategy and most of them had already been vaccinated themselves despite significant barriers. Their enthusiasm emerged within a medical landscape shaped by both COVID-19 vaccination and HIV/AIDS-related health interventions including ART and PrEP, which may have helped instill increased medical trust in our sample population. Out urban gay men may comprise an unusual minority population with increased medical trust due to specific social, sexual, and historical experiences.
