Age-related histopathological and immunophenotypic variations in early-stage mycosis fungoides: a multicentre retrospective study in Türkiye

Abstract AbstractObjectivesEarly-stage mycosis fungoides (MF) is diagnostically challenging due to overlap with inflammatory dermatoses. Age-related immunological and cutaneous changes may modify histopathological presentation. We aimed to compare clinical, histopathological and immunophenotypic features of early-stage MF between geriatric and non-geriatric patients.DesignMulticentre retrospective cross-sectional study.SettingDermatology departments of tertiary centres in Türkiye.ParticipantsA total of 541 patients diagnosed with early-stage MF were included and stratified into geriatric (≥65 years) and non-geriatric (18–64 years) groups.Primary and secondary outcome measuresThe primary outcomes were age-related differences in histopathological and immunohistochemical features. Secondary outcomes included clinical characteristics and quality of life measures. Background

Mycosis fungoides (MF) is the most common primary cutaneous T-cell lymphoma. While early-stage MF usually follows an indolent course, advanced disease can be aggressive, underscoring the importance of accurate early diagnosis.1 However, distinguishing MF from benign inflammatory dermatoses such as eczema, psoriasis or atopic dermatitis remains challenging due to overlapping clinical and histopathological features.1 2 Typical histological findings include epidermotropism of atypical lymphocytes, papillary dermal fibrosis and band-like dermal infiltration, supported immunohistochemically by an increased CD4/CD8 ratio or loss of pan–T-cell antigens such as CD7, CD2 or CD5.3 4 Yet, these features are neither consistent nor pathognomonic, and repeated biopsies are often required, with diagnostic delays ranging from 2 to 4 years.5,7

Aging introduces further complexity. Geriatric skin shows epidermal and dermal thinning, reduced repair capacity and dermatoporosis, while immunosenescence leads to diminished naïve T cells, impaired CD8+ cytotoxic responses and chronic low-grade inflammation (‘inflammaging’). These alterations may influence the histopathological expression of MF. Although age has been studied as a prognostic factor in advanced-stage disease, little is known about its effect on early diagnostic features.8 9

To address this gap, we performed a large multicentre analysis comparing the histopathological and immunophenotypic characteristics of early-stage MF in geriatric versus non-geriatric patients. Identifying age-related diagnostic variations may refine histopathological criteria and support more tailored diagnostic approaches.

Methods

Study design and participant selection

This cross-sectional study was conducted at the Dermatology Outpatient Clinics of Sakarya University Training and Research Hospital, Mersin University Faculty of Medicine, Ankara Bilkent City Hospital, Pamukkale University Faculty of Medicine, Ankara Etlik City Hospital, Istanbul Medeniyet University Faculty of Medicine, Sisli Hamidiye Etfal Training and Research Hospital, Tekirdag Namık Kemal University, Kocaeli University Faculty of Medicine, Uludag University Faculty of Medicine, Dicle University Faculty of Medicine, S.B.U. Basakşehir Cam and Sakura City Hospital, and Necmettin Erbakan University Faculty of Medicine.

The study included patients aged 18 years and older who were diagnosed with early-stage MF (stages IA, IB, IIA) between January 2000 and January 2024 based on histopathological and/or clinicopathological correlation. In cases without classical histopathological features, diagnosis was based on clinicopathological correlation and expert consensus. Patient data were retrospectively collected from medical records and hospital databases. In this study, patients aged 65 years and older were classified as the geriatric group. For the non-geriatric group, patients aged 18 to 64 years were selected from each centre by reviewing patient records starting from the most recent and moving backward chronologically until a number comparable to the geriatric group was reached. This approach may introduce calendar time bias because the geriatric group spans a wider period, during which immunohistochemistry (IHC) panels, diagnostic terminology and report structure may have changed; accordingly, comparisons should be interpreted cautiously.

Data collection

The histopathological findings from the initial diagnosis of MF and the corresponding clinical features at that time were evaluated for the patients included in the study. Demographic characteristics, comorbidities, medications, family history of MF, disease subtype and duration, stage, and other clinical features related to MF were recorded from patient files. Histopathological findings from skin punch biopsies were retrieved from hospital databases, and the existing reports were documented. Definition of histopathological variables: Dermal lymphocytic infiltration was recorded as present when the original pathology report described a clearly appreciable superficial dermal lymphocytic infiltrate (lichenoid/band-like or patchy superficial infiltrate), rather than only a minimal perivascular lymphocytic infiltrate that may be seen in nonspecific inflammatory dermatoses. Histopathological findings from skin punch biopsies were retrieved from hospital databases, and the existing reports were documented. When available in pathology or clinical records, biopsy site information (eg, trunk, upper extremities, lower extremities) was recorded descriptively. Because biopsy site documentation was inconsistent across centres and reports, stratified analyses by biopsy site or sensitivity analyses excluding lower-extremity biopsies could not be systematically performed.

Immunohistochemistry definitions

Immunohistochemical variables were recorded as documented in the original pathology reports. Loss of CD7 expression was defined as reduced or absent CD7 staining in atypical (tumour) T cells compared with internal/reactive T-cell staining, as stated in the report. CD20 positivity was recorded when CD20-positive B cells were reported within the infiltrate. CD30 positivity was recorded when CD30 expression was reported (in atypical lymphocytes when specified, or otherwise as reported) in the original report. For the CD4/CD8 assessment, immunophenotypes were categorised according to the information available in pathology reports. When both CD4 and CD8 staining were described, the CD4/CD8 relationship was classified using a pragmatic threshold consistent with commonly reported diagnostic support in MF: CD4/CD8≥2 was considered ‘CD4-dominant’, 1<CD4/CD8<2 was recorded as CD4+/CD8+, and CD8(+)/CD4(−) cases were recorded separately. Because numerical CD4/CD8 ratios were not consistently reported in routine pathology reports, continuous ratio values (eg, median or IQR) could not be reconstructed retrospectively.

In this retrospective study, only existing histopathology records were used, and no new histopathological examination or additional staining was performed. Slides were not centrally re-reviewed, and no harmonised scoring criteria or central adjudication was applied; thus, histopathological and immunohistochemical variables represent report-based documentation at each centre and may be influenced by interobserver differences and centre-specific reporting habits, as well as temporal evolution of diagnostic practice across the 2000–2024 study period.

Sample size justification

All consecutive patients fulfilling the inclusion criteria during the predefined study period were included. Due to the retrospective design, a formal a priori sample size calculation was not feasible. The final sample size (n=541) was determined by the number of available complete medical records across participating centres. A study flow diagram describing case identification, inclusion and final analytical sample is provided in online supplemental figure S1.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and/or the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this research.

Efforts to minimise bias included the recruitment of all consecutive eligible patients during the study period and the use of standardised histopathological and immunophenotypic evaluation across participating centres.

Study reporting

This study was reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology recommendations for observational studies.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics for the data included mean, SD, median (IQR), frequency and percentage values. The distribution of variables was assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov and Shapiro-Wilk tests. For the analysis of normally distributed independent quantitative variables, the independent samples t-test was used, while the Mann-Whitney U test was applied for non-normally distributed independent quantitative variables. The χ2 test was used for categorical independent variables, and Fisher’s exact test was applied when the χ2 test assumptions were not met. All analyses were performed using SPSS V.28.0.

Given the retrospective and multicentre design of the study and the report-based nature of several variables, multivariable regression modelling was not undertaken. Accordingly, comparisons between geriatric and non-geriatric patients are based on univariable analyses. Potential confounding related to sex distribution, clinical stage, centre-specific reporting practices and calendar time should be considered when interpreting the findings.

To address multiplicity, we prespecified a limited set of primary endpoints a priori (epidermotropism, basilar lymphocytes, epidermal atrophy, dermal lymphocytic infiltration, papillary dermal fibrosis, and CD4-dominant versus CD8(+)/CD4(−) phenotypes). For these primary endpoints, a two-sided p value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. All remaining histopathological and immunohistochemical variables were analysed as exploratory outcomes; their p values are reported descriptively and should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating.

Results

Study population

A total of 541 patients were included in the study, of whom 286 (52.9%) were male. The mean age of the patients was 58.2±17.0 years. The mean age was 72.7±6.3 years in the geriatric group and 45.6±12.8 years in the non-geriatric group. The proportion of male patients was significantly higher in the geriatric group (59.5%) compared with the non-geriatric group (47.1%) (p=0.004). All comparisons between geriatric and non-geriatric patients were based on univariable analyses; primary endpoints were prespecified, whereas the remaining comparisons were exploratory.

Data completeness varied across variables. Lesion morphology was available for 118/541 patients overall (non-geriatric: 94/289; geriatric: 24/252), and MF subtype classification was available for 535/541 (non-geriatric: 285/289; geriatric: 245/252). For immunophenotyping, CD4/CD8 status was documented for 404/541 patients (non-geriatric: 213/289; geriatric: 191/252), whereas loss of CD7 expression was reported for 66/541 (non-geriatric: 39/289; geriatric: 27/252). Accordingly, each comparison was performed as a complete-case analysis for the relevant variable, using the reported denominators. The observed missingness may reflect centre-specific reporting habits and temporal changes in diagnostic work-up (including IHC panel composition) over the 2000–2024 interval; therefore, the possibility of differential missingness by age group should be considered when interpreting results for variables with limited availability. All between-group comparisons were therefore conducted as complete-case analyses for each variable, which served as a minimum sensitivity analysis. Because missingness was largely driven by centre-specific reporting practices and evolving IHC panel use over calendar time, it may not be random; accordingly, results for variables with limited availability should be interpreted cautiously. Biopsy site information was inconsistently reported in the available records; therefore, distribution by anatomical site could not be reliably summarised across centres, and stratified analyses by biopsy site were not feasible.

In the non-geriatric group, females were more prevalent (52.9%), whereas males predominated in the geriatric group (59.5%; p=0.004).

Among patients with available data on lesion morphology (n=118), there was no statistically significant difference between geriatric and non-geriatric patients in terms of the proportion of patch-type and plaque-type lesions (figure 1). Similarly, when assessing MF subtypes, no statistically significant difference was observed between the groups, with classic MF being the most common form in both. Among MF variants, folliculotropic MF was the most frequently observed subtype in both groups.

Histopathological findings

In geriatric patients, the most common histopathological findings were epidermotropism (81.3%), lymphocytic infiltration (75.8%), basal alignment (57.1%) and dermal papillary fibrosis (55.2%). In non-geriatric patients, the most frequently observed histopathological findings were the epidermotropism (63.3%), lymphocytic infiltration (58.5%), presence of halos (56.7%) and basal alignment (45.7%) (figure 2).

(a) Atypical lymphocytes distributing with a lichenoid pattern in the fibrotic dermis (H&E, ×100). (b) Lymphocytes with perinuclear halos show basilar alignment (H&E, ×200). (c) Epidermotropism of lymphocytes (H&E, ×400).

Compared with the non-geriatric group, the geriatric group exhibited significantly higher rates of basal alignment (57.1% vs 45.7%; p=0.008), epidermotropism (81.3% vs 63.3%; p<0.001) and epidermal atrophy (26.6% vs 13.8%; p<0.001). Additionally, the rates of dermal lymphocytic infiltration (75.8% vs 58.5%; p<0.001) and dermal papillary fibrosis (55.2% vs 38.4%; p<0.001) were significantly higher in geriatric patients. Furthermore, the presence of plasma cells (13.5% vs 6.2%) and eosinophils (21.8% vs 13.8%) was significantly more common in the geriatric group compared with the non-geriatric group (p=0.004 and p=0.015, respectively) (table 2); however, these findings should be interpreted cautiously because they may also reflect concomitant inflammatory dermatoses or site-related background inflammation.

Patients with immunohistochemical evaluations documented in their histopathology reports were included in the analysis. Therefore, the immunophenotypic frequencies presented below should be interpreted as report-based observations and may be influenced by centre-specific documentation practices and by changes in IHC panel composition and reporting terminology over the study period. In the geriatric patient group, the ‘CD4-dominant’ immune phenotype was observed at a significantly higher rate compared with the non-geriatric group (70.2% vs 60.6%). Discussion

Our findings indicate that, although clinical features did not differ significantly between geriatric and non-geriatric patients, notable histopathological distinctions were observed. Epidermotropism, basilar alignment of lymphocytes, epidermal atrophy, dermal lymphocytic infiltration and papillary dermal fibrosis were all significantly more frequent in older patients. Immunohistochemically, the ‘CD4-dominant’ phenotype was more common in geriatric patients, whereas the ‘CD8(+), CD4(-)’ phenotype was more often detected in younger individuals.

The diagnosis of early-stage MF remains challenging and often requires repeated biopsies and clinicopathological correlation; this was also the case in our study, consistent with previous reports. The median number of biopsies until diagnosis was comparable between groups without statistical significance. Time from symptom onset to diagnosis did not differ significantly between groups; however, the median time was numerically shorter in geriatric patients (6.5 (IQR 4.0–12.0) months) than in non-geriatric patients (12.0 (IQR 4.0–60.0) months) (p=0.212).

According to the WHO-EORTC classification, only folliculotropic MF, pagetoid reticulosis and granulomatous slack skin are recognised as distinct variants.10 In our series, no significant differences were noted between age groups regarding subtypes, lesion duration, extent of involvement or morphology, with classic MF being the most prevalent form in both. Given that folliculotropic MF was the most frequent variant in both age groups in our cohort, we note that early follicular/folliculotropic presentations may show subtle or ‘spiky/keratosis pilaris-like’ clinical patterns and pose specific clinicopathological pitfalls, underscoring the importance of targeted clinicopathological correlation and sampling.10

MF typically affects middle-aged and older individuals, with a median age at diagnosis ranging between 55 and 60 years.3 The prognostic impact of cutaneous T-cell lymphomas (CTCLs) in elderly patients remains a subject of debate in the literature. While some studies suggest that older age is associated with a poorer prognosis, potentially due to age-related immune dysfunction, comorbidities and an increased mortality risk from treatment-related toxicity, other studies have found no significant association between age and prognosis.11,15 In a large-scale analysis, Lebowitz et al16 reported that advanced age (>65 years) was associated with reduced overall survival but did not increase the risk of disease progression or MF/SS-related mortality. Additionally, a population-based study found that the incidence of CTCL has significantly increased over the years in individuals aged 70 and older, with the highest incidence rates observed in this age group.17 This trend may reflect a biologically higher likelihood of MF developing with age. Alternatively, as observed in our study, it may be due to more pronounced histopathological findings in geriatric patients, leading to a more straightforward diagnosis.

One of the key epidermal findings in MF is epidermotropism. Epidermotropism is defined as the presence of atypical lymphocytes with cerebriform, hyperchromatic nuclei within the epidermis, either individually or in small clusters, without causing significant spongiosis.3 However, this feature may not always be prominent and can also be observed in certain inflammatory dermatoses.2

Basilar alignment is characterised by the orderly arrangement of lymphocytes along the basal layer of the epidermis and serves as an important supportive feature in the histopathological diagnosis of MF.18,20 In our study, both epidermotropism and basilar alignment were found to be significantly more frequent in geriatric patients. Epidermotropism was observed in 81.3% of geriatric patients compared with 63.3% in the non-geriatric group, while basilar lymphocytes were present in 57.1% of geriatric patients and 45.7% of non-geriatric patients.

Pautrier microabscesses, characterised by the accumulation of clonal T cells within the epidermis, are considered a pathognomonic feature of MF. However, they are more commonly observed in advanced stages of the disease.3 21 Previous studies have reported their presence in approximately 40% of cases.18 20 In a large-scale study of early-stage MF conducted by Massone et al, which included patients with a median age of 52 years, epidermotropism was observed in 96% of cases, whereas Pautrier microabscesses were detected in only 19% of cases, supporting their low sensitivity in early-stage disease.19 In our study, the frequency of Pautrier microabscesses was 28.2% in the geriatric group and 21.5% in the non-geriatric group, with no statistically significant difference between the two groups.

In our study, epidermal atrophy was significantly more frequent in geriatric patients. Age-related epidermal changes include a reduction in epidermal thickness due to decreased cellular proliferation, as well as flattening of the dermoepidermal junction, leading to the loss of rete ridge prominence.8 These may explain the higher prevalence of epidermal atrophy observed in geriatric individuals. In MF, epidermal atrophy may be encountered as an accompanying finding, but it is non-specific and should not be used in isolation for diagnosis. No significant differences were observed between the groups in terms of parakeratosis and epidermal hyperplasia in our study. Similarly, there were no differences between the two groups regarding lesion duration and lesion type. This finding may suggest that these features, which are more reflective of a chronic inflammatory process, occur with similar frequency in both groups. Importantly, epidermal atrophy is a frequent background finding in geriatric skin; therefore, its presence should not be over-interpreted as MF-specific and is best considered only in combination with more characteristic features such as epidermotropism and basilar alignment.

In the early stages of MF, dermal lymphocytic infiltration is typically confined to the superficial dermis and exhibits a band-like pattern. Unlike inflammatory dermatoses, its distribution is irregular.2 3 In our study, dermal lymphocytic infiltration was significantly more frequent in geriatric patients compared with the non-geriatric group (75.8% vs 58.5%). The increased lymphocytic infiltration in geriatric patients may reflect a more prolonged or pronounced chronic inflammatory response. However, there were no significant differences between the groups in terms of disease or lesion duration. Therefore, age-related alterations in immune responses and chronic inflammation (inflammaging) may contribute to the more pronounced lymphocytic infiltration observed in geriatric patients.

In MF, dermal papillary fibrosis develops as a result of chronic dermal inflammation. In the early stages, minimal fibrosis may be observed in the papillary dermis, while more pronounced fibrotic changes tend to emerge over time.2 20 A study reported papillary dermal fibrosis/thick collagen bands in 97% of cases, leading the authors to suggest that papillary dermal fibrosis could be a key histopathological finding in the diagnosis of early-stage MF.19 We found that dermal papillary fibrosis was significantly more frequent in geriatric patients compared with the non-geriatric group (55.2% vs 38.4%). Fibrosis is commonly observed in the perifollicular regions and superficial dermal layers of aged skin.8 Our findings suggest that age-related changes may contribute to a more pronounced fibrotic process in MF, potentially influencing its histopathological presentation in older individuals. Because superficial fibrosis can also be accentuated in aged skin and in certain anatomical sites, we interpret the higher frequency in geriatric patients as a potential background amplifier of dermal changes rather than a standalone diagnostic criterion. Benchmarking our report-based frequencies against key series underscores both concordant patterns and expected variability across cohorts and methodology. In large classic patch-stage material, epidermotropism is typically the most frequent diagnostic clue, whereas Pautrier microabscesses show lower sensitivity in early disease.19 In contemporary overviews, supportive epidermal findings such as basilar alignment and epidermotropism remain central, but their reported rates vary with case mix, biopsy site and interpretive thresholds, and may overlap with inflammatory mimickers.2,4 Similarly, papillary dermal fibrosis has been emphasised as a helpful feature in early lesions, yet its frequency is influenced by chronicity, anatomic site and background age-related dermal change.8 19 Accordingly, the between-group differences observed in our multicentre report-based dataset are best interpreted in the context of real-world diagnostic practice rather than as absolute histologic ‘rates’.

In our study, the presence of plasma cells was significantly higher in geriatric patients compared with the non-geriatric group (13.5% vs 6.2%). In a study by Boehncke et al, plasma cells were observed in 9 out of 50 MF patients (18.0%).22 It has been suggested that various immunological and microenvironmental interactions may contribute to the increase in plasma cells observed in MF.23 The low-grade chronic inflammation known as inflammaging, which is characteristic of ageing skin, may further amplify this increase, potentially explaining the difference observed between the two groups in our study. The exact role of plasma cells in MF remains unclear; however, it has been proposed that they may have an immune-modulatory function.23

Our analysis revealed a higher prevalence of eosinophils in geriatric patients (21.8%) compared with non-geriatric patients (13.8%). However, these rates are notably higher than those reported in the literature. In a study by Kaya et al, eosinophils were detected in the dermis in only 2.8% of early-stage MF cases.20 In fact, eosinophils are not typically prominent in MF, and their presence can sometimes pose a diagnostic challenge in distinguishing MF from inflammatory dermatoses.2 In early-stage MF, the T-cell response is predominantly Th1-driven, whereas a shift toward a Th2-dominant response occurs as the disease progresses. The Th2 immune response is characterised by increased levels of cytokines such as IL-4, IL-5, IL-6 and IL-10, which are known to enhance eosinophil chemotaxis. It has been shown that eosinophils are rare in the patch and plaque stages of MF but become significantly more prominent as the disease progresses to the tumour stage.24 In elderly patients, the chronic inflammatory process known as inflammaging, characterised by an increase in proinflammatory cytokines, may contribute to the elevated presence of eosinophils.8 However, it is essential to consider alternative diagnoses such as spongiotic dermatitis in cases of suspected early-stage MF with prominent eosinophilic infiltration. Notably, dermal eosinophils and plasma cells may be increased in several common mimickers or coexisting conditions in older adults, including spongiotic dermatitis, stasis-related inflammation, drug eruptions and arthropod reactions, particularly in lower-extremity biopsies. Because our histopathological variables were extracted from routine reports without central slide re-review, we could not systematically exclude these concomitant processes; therefore, these observations should be considered hypothesis-generating rather than MF-specific. Future studies with central slide re-review and stratification by biopsy site and calendar period would be required to clarify whether these inflammatory-cell differences reflect true age-related MF biology vs concomitant dermatoses/background inflammation.

The increased prominence of these features in elderly patients may result from immunosenescence and chronic inflammatory processes, structural alterations in biopsy sites, or the inherent heterogeneity of MF. For instance, biopsies from the lower extremities may obscure MF-specific findings due to venous stasis, chronic inflammation and fibrosis. While evidence on whether MF behaves more aggressively in older patients remains conflicting, it is unclear whether these histopathological differences merely facilitate diagnosis or reflect variations in disease biology.

Classic MF is characterised by CD3+/CD4+/CD45RO+memory T cells, with a tendency for CD8(+) T cells to decrease. As a result, the CD4/CD8 ratio is elevated, and a ratio exceeding 4–6 has been suggested as a potential diagnostic immunophenotypic marker for MF. However, rare cases of CD8-dominant MF or CD4/CD8 double-negative MF have also been described.24 25 Although the prognostic implications of these subtypes remain uncertain, CD4/CD8 double-negative MF, which is extremely rare, has been reported to have no significant prognostic impact, following a clinical course similar to classic CD4(+) MF, while CD8+MF tends to have a more indolent course compared with classic CD4+ MF.25 26

In our study, ‘CD4 dominance’ (CD4/CD8≥2) was the most frequently observed immunohistochemical feature in both groups, followed by the ‘CD4(+), CD8(+)’ phenotype (1<CD4/CD8<2). However, ‘CD4 dominance’ was significantly more frequent in geriatric patients compared with the non-geriatric group (70.2% vs 60.6%), whereas ‘CD8 dominance’ was significantly more common in the non-geriatric group (8.5% vs 2.6%). With immunosenescence, functional decline and a reduction in CD8+ cytotoxic T cells have been observed, while CD4+ T cells are generally preserved or may show a slight increase.27 This phenomenon may explain the more pronounced elevation in the CD4/CD8 ratio observed in the geriatric group in our study. Because CD4/CD8 ratios were derived from routine pathology reports rather than re-calculated from raw immunostaining counts, the threshold used in this study should be interpreted as a pragmatic categorisation rather than a strict quantitative cut-off.

Loss of CD7 expression, a T-cell surface antigen, is considered a supportive feature for the diagnosis of early-stage MF.2 However, in our study, no significant differences were observed between the geriatric and non-geriatric groups in terms of loss of CD7 expression in atypical T cells. Our study also found no statistically significant differences between geriatric and non-geriatric patients in terms of CD20 and CD30 positivity, large-cell transformation or follicular infiltration. Additionally, none of the patients had a history of cyclosporine or dupilumab use for their lesions prior to diagnosis.

MF can negatively impact quality of life, primarily due to itching, cosmetic concerns and psychosocial effects.28 In geriatric patients, additional comorbidities and changes in skin barrier function may contribute to a slightly greater perception of discomfort. However, in our study, although DLQI scores were slightly higher in geriatric patients compared with the non-geriatric group, the difference was not statistically significant. This finding may, to some extent, be explained by the possibility that geriatric patients have adapted to chronic dryness and itching over time.

This study has several noteworthy limitations. The retrospective design may have resulted in incomplete documentation of some clinical or histopathological findings. Additionally, the multicentre nature of the study required histopathological evaluations by different pathologists, introducing the risk of interobserver variability. We acknowledge potential residual confounding because age is correlated with sex distribution, disease stage, biopsy site, centre-specific diagnostic practice and temporal changes in diagnostic work-up across the long study interval. As the analyses were based on univariable comparisons within a retrospective framework, residual confounding related to these factors cannot be excluded. Furthermore, immunohistochemical analyses relied on existing pathology reports, and no additional staining or advanced immunophenotyping was performed, which may have limited the consistency of immunological findings. A key limitation is that histopathological and immunohistochemical data were extracted from existing routine pathology reports rather than obtained through a central slide re-review with harmonised scoring criteria and adjudication. Accordingly, we could not uniformly adjudicate potential concomitant dermatoses (eg, spongiotic or stasis-related dermatitis) that may influence inflammatory cell composition, including eosinophils and plasma cells, particularly in biopsies from site-specific locations such as the lower extremities. Because biopsy site information was not uniformly documented in all records, we could not perform stratified analyses by anatomical site or exclude lower-extremity biopsies in sensitivity analyses. Consequently, some observed between-group differences may partly reflect inter-centre and interobserver variability in reporting habits. In addition, the strategy used to select non-geriatric cases (most recent cases reviewed backward to achieve comparable numbers) may introduce calendar time bias vs the geriatric group, because diagnostic terminology, report structure and IHC panels evolved over the 2000–2024 interval. These factors should be considered when interpreting the reported frequencies and between-group comparisons.

In conclusion, geriatric patients with early-stage MF exhibit distinct histopathological features, including more frequent epidermotropism, dermal fibrosis and a CD4-dominant phenotype, despite comparable clinical presentations. These age-related differences, likely reflecting immunosenescence and chronic inflammation, may contribute to pattern recognition in challenging early cases when integrated with clinicopathological correlation, while acknowledging that certain features may be influenced by age-related and site-related background changes.
