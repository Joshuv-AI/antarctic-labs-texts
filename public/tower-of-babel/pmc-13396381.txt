Evolution of the frontal aslant tract and implications for primate vocalization and human speech
Authors: Salvatore Citro, Matt S. Dawson, Ahmad Beyh, Flavio Dell’Acqua, Carlo Cavaliere, Tim B. Dyrby, Kristine Krug, Maurice Ptito, Lorenzo Carnevale, Sergio Della Sala, William D. Hopkins, Marsel M. Mesulam, Marco Catani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Monkeys, chimpanzees and humans share dorsomedial and ventrolateral frontal regions for vocalization, yet only humans have speech. These regions are interconnected by the frontal aslant tract (FAT), a pathway increasingly recognized as central to speech production. Using spherical deconvolution tractography, we compared the FAT across primate species. In humans, the FAT is left-lateralized and enlarged, predominantly in its anterior parts connecting prefrontal areas. This expansion parallels the enlargement of the arcuate fasciculus, which conveys auditory input to the ventrolateral frontal region. To probe the functional significance of these anatomical modifications, we measured correlations between degeneration of FAT subsegments and language deficits in patients with primary progressive aphasia. Posterior FAT degeneration correlated with impaired verbal fluency whereas anterior FAT degeneration disrupted syntactic processing. Together, these findings support the view that an ancestral vocalization network was exapted in humans to sequence speech sounds, providing a neural substrate for the emergence of language.

Introduction

Humans and non-human primates share the ability to express emotional states or refer to objects and events through gestures and vocalization. Evolutionary continuity theories suggest that these forms of communication may represent the substrate for the emergence of speech in humans1. Specifically, some researchers argue that speech relies on the hierarchical sequencing of movements into goal-directed actions seen in all primate species2. These perspectives stand in opposition to the view that the evolution from non-syntactic to combinatorial speech represents a unique leap forward in human evolution3.

Despite the apparent theoretical divergence, most theories of speech evolution highlight the same brain regions as crucial for the development of gestures, vocalization and the hierarchical organization of behavior in all primate species4–10. These regions, corresponding to the ventrolateral and dorsomedial frontal cortex, are cytoarchitectonically similar in macaques, chimpanzees, and humans11–13. In humans, these areas are activated during tasks involving orofacial movements, as well as vocalization and speech4. These anatomical and functional observations are considered supportive of an exaptive mechanism in speech evolution.

The frontal aslant tract (FAT) is a recently identified pathway composed of fibers linking ventrolateral and dorsomedial frontal areas14. The FAT has been identified in monkeys15, chimpanzees16 and humans14 but a direct comparison of its anatomy across the three species is still lacking17. It has been demonstrated that the FAT volume is significantly left-lateralized in humans14, and its degeneration is associated with deficits in spontaneous verbal fluency18. There is also evidence suggesting a posterior-to-anterior functional organization of frontal regions connected by the FAT, with posterior motor areas (primary motor, premotor) involved in action selection in response to stimuli, posterior prefrontal areas involved in action planning, and anterior prefrontal areas supporting superordinate knowledge for cognitive control7,19. However, using diffusion tensor tractography, a preliminary comparison with monkeys indicate larger absolute but not proportional FAT volume in humans17.

In this study, we used spherical deconvolution tractography to dissect the FAT in Old World monkeys, chimpanzees, and humans and obtain a comprehensive characterization of its anatomy and lateralization across these primate species. By subdividing the FAT into a posterior precentral segment and an anterior prefrontal segment based on its cortical connectivity, we tested the hypothesis that the anterior prefrontal FAT is responsible for the relative increased volume of the tract in the human brain.

To provide converging evidence on the functional segregation of the FAT, we performed white matter tractography dissections in subjects with primary progressive aphasia (PPA). PPA has proven to be a disease model for studying the neuroanatomy of language, as it specifically targets brain regions associated with different aspects of language processing, including verbal fluency and syntax20. We divided the prefrontal FAT into three segments based on its anatomical connectivity with the inferior frontal gyrus. For each segment, we extracted volume and microstructural diffusion measures including fractional anisotropy (FA), radial diffusivity (RD), and hindrance modulated orientational anisotropy (HMOA). We then correlated these measures with symptom severity in spontaneous speech and syntactic production.

Results

Comparative anatomy

In all datasets, we used diffusion tractography based on spherical deconvolution21. This approach permits tracking through regions of fiber crossing and therefore obtaining a more comprehensive and differentiated description of the FAT’s anatomy compared to diffusion tensor tractography. We used a two-regions of interest approach to define tracts and their subdivision into segments. The whole FAT and its posterior precentral and anterior prefrontal segments were dissected in each hemisphere of nine Old World monkeys (all males), 43 chimpanzees (23 females, 20 males) and 43 healthy humans (21 females and 22 males; Edinburgh handedness = 93.5 ± 8.8). We used anatomical landmarks of the ventrolateral frontal cortex (arcuate sulcus in monkeys and precentral sulcus in chimpanzees and humans) to separate precentral from prefrontal FAT segments (Fig. 1A-C). Extracted tract volumes were normalized to the whole brain white matter volume to obtain corresponding proportional volumes and group differences analyzed using one-way Welch's ANOVA. Total FAT

The total FAT proportional volume was different among the three species (W = 88.7; df =2; p < 0.0001; η²=0.71; 95% CI [0.59, 0.78]): larger in humans compared to Old World monkeys (p < 0.0001) and chimpanzees (p < 0.0001). There were no differences between Old World monkeys and chimpanzees (p = 0.766) (Fig. 1D).

Precentral and prefrontal FAT segments

Differences among the three species were statistically significant for the proportional volume of both precentral (W = 116.2; p < 0.0001; η²=0.82; 95% CI [0.73, 0.88]) and prefrontal (W = 479.5; p < 0.0001; η²=0.89; 95% CI [0.85, 0.91]) FAT segments. Old World monkeys had a larger precentral FAT proportional volume than both chimpanzees (p < 0.0001) and humans (p < 0.0001) with no differences between chimpanzees and humans (p = 0.376) (Fig. 1E). The proportional volume of the prefrontal segment was larger in chimpanzees compared to Old World monkeys (p < 0.0001), and even larger in humans compared to chimpanzees (p < 0.0001) and Old World monkeys (p < 0.0001) (Fig. 1F). The ratio between the prefrontal FAT and the total FAT was different for the three species (W = 2728.0; p < 0.0001; η²=0.98; 95% CI [0.98, 0.99]), with Old World monkeys showing a lower percentage (14%) compared to chimpanzees (67%; p < 0.0001) and humans (86%; p < 0.0001) (Fig. 1G). The difference between humans and chimpanzees was also statistically significant (p < 0.0001).

Inter-hemispheric lateralization of the FAT

The volume of the total FAT, as well as those of its precentral and prefrontal segments were symmetrical between the two hemispheres in Old World monkeys and slightly left-lateralized in chimpanzees although this was not statistically significant (Fig. 1H, I). In humans, a significant left lateralization was evident for the total FAT volume (t(42) = 5.175; p < 0.0001; η²=0.39; 95% CI [0.06, 0.15]), which was driven primarily by its prefrontal segment (t(42) = 5.144; p < 0.0001; η²=0.39; 95% CI [0.06, 0.14]), and to a lesser extent by the precentral segment (t(41) = 2.846; p = 0.0069; η²=0.16; 95% CI [0.04, 0.25]) (Fig. 1J).

Extended FAT connectivity

The dorsomedial and ventrolateral frontal regions connect to other brain regions through an extended network of association fibers. We analyzed this extended network to determine if the inter-species differences in volume were specific to the FAT or involved other association tracts that could contribute to speech evolution (Fig. 2A–C). In all three species the majority of association fibers projecting to the dorsomedial FAT territory belong to the cingulum, a limbic pathway connecting to the cingulate cortex, medial frontal and parietal cortex, and parahippocampal gyrus. We assessed two aspects of speech production: verbal fluency and syntactic processing. To assess verbal fluency, we used the Words Per Minute (WPM) score from the Cinderella story test22,23. WPM corresponds to the mean number of words per minute each participant produces while telling the story of Cinderella from a wordless picture book. To assess syntactic production the Northwestern Assessment of Verbs and Sentences - Sentence Production Priming Test (NAVS-SPPT) and the Northwestern Anagram Test (NAT) were used. In the NAVS-SPPT, participants are presented with two reversible pictures depicting the same action involving the same characters. After listening to the examiner's description of the picture on the left, participants are asked to generate a corresponding sentence for the picture on the right24. By requiring overt production, this task engages motor planning and articulation and is therefore influenced by fluency and/or motor speech impairments, such as apraxia of speech, which may co-occur with syntactic deficits. The NAT bypasses this problem, by asking participants to rearrange individual word cards presented in scrambled order to form grammatically correct sentences25.

Correlations were computed with age as covariate.

Spontaneous verbal fluency (WPM)

Figure 3C illustrates the correlations between WPM scores and FA across the FAT segments. Positive correlations were statistically significant for the precentral FAT and the most posterior segment of the prefrontal FAT (opercularis). The correlation with the FAT triangularis did not survive the Bonferroni correction. Correlations remained significant after covarying for Mini Mental State Examination (MMSE).

Hence, the correlation coefficients between WPM scores and FA exhibited a progressive decrease from the most posterior to the anterior regions (Fig. 3E - G). Correlations between WPM and other diffusion measures showed a similar trend (see Table S5 in Supplementary Information).

Syntactic processing (NAVS-SPPT and NAT)

The correlational analysis between NAVS-SPPT scores and FA values of the FAT segments revealed significant positive correlations for the FAT orbitalis (r(79) = 0.298, p = 0.009) and the FAT triangularis (r(79) = 0.370, p < 0.001). Positive correlations were also observed for the FAT opercularis (r(79) = 0.259, p = 0.020) and precentral FAT (r(79) = 0.251, p = 0.024), although these did not survive Bonferroni correction. Correlations remained statistically significant for both FAT orbitalis and FAT triangularis, after controlling for WPM and MMSE (see Supplementary results).

Figure 3D shows the correlations between NAT scores and FA of the FAT segments. Positive correlations were statistically significant for the FAT orbitalis and FAT triangularis, although the latter did not survive Bonferroni correction.

Figure 3F, G show how the correlation coefficients between NAT and FA progressively decrease from the most anterior to the most posterior FAT segment. This posterior-to-anterior gradient remained after controlling for word reading and MMSE (see Supplementary results).

Correlations between NAT and RD also showed a similar trend whereas correlations with HMOA and volume were not significant (see Table S5 in Supplementary Information).

Discussion

Our comparative analysis of frontal connectivity underlying vocal communication showed that the volume of the total FAT is relatively larger in humans than in monkeys and chimpanzees. At first glance, this may suggest an evolutionary divergence of humans from non-human primates. However, our analysis of precentral and prefrontal FAT subsegments revealed a more complex picture. Compared to monkeys, the precentral FAT is equally smaller in both chimpanzees and humans, while the prefrontal FAT is four times larger in chimpanzees and eleven times larger in humans. This highlights opposing evolutionary trends for the precentral and prefrontal FAT which may have operated at different stages in primate evolution. The results for the precentral FAT suggest that its reduction may have occurred for the last common ancestor of chimpanzees and humans. The gradient of prefrontal FAT enlargement observed from monkeys to chimpanzees, and further from chimpanzees to humans, suggests an evolutionary mechanism that was already active in chimpanzees and became more pronounced in humans. Finally, the left lateralization of both precentral and prefrontal FAT indicates that inter-hemispheric asymmetry is a unique characteristic of human evolution.

The differences in the precentral FAT may reflect variations in the structural anatomy of the precentral cortical areas in the three species26, a functional rearrangement within the precentral cortex in primate evolution, or both. Our results are consistent with previous studies of gray matter structure, which have shown that the precentral cortex (including the precentral FAT cortical projection areas) expands to a lesser extent than prefrontal growth in chimpanzees and humans compared to monkeys27. Functionally, electrostimulation studies have demonstrated a posterior shift of the laryngeal motor cortex from area 6 in non-human primates to area 4 in humans28. Therefore, the reduction of the precentral FAT projections in humans and chimpanzees compared to Old World monkeys may be related to a progressive reorganization of the ventrolateral frontal cortex involved in the motor control of vocal cords. In addition to its role in vocalization, the precentral FAT is also involved in limb motor control and in gestures29. Consequently, the left lateralization of the precentral FAT in humans may be associated with the high prevalence of right-handedness in our species30.

Although cortical and white-matter changes show some cross-species convergence, other factors clearly modulate the pattern. We find a comparable reduction of the precentral FAT in humans and chimpanzees relative to monkeys, yet Donahue et al. report that area 4 myelination is relatively expanded in chimps but reduced in humans26. This dissociation implies convergent white-matter changes without parallel cortical changes across lineages. Because cortical morphology is shaped not only by the FAT but also by inputs and outputs from other tracts, the complexity of intracortical short-range connections, and U-fiber volume, multimodal imaging studies will be required to disentangle the co-evolution of cortical and white-matter anatomy.

The posterior shift and contraction of the precentral FAT may also in part result from the gradual increase of the prefrontal FAT’s proportional volume and the ventrolateral prefrontal cortex, from Old World monkeys to chimpanzees and humans27. The prefrontal FAT connects regions of the ventrolateral and dorsomedial prefrontal cortex that exhibit a significant progressive enlargement from monkeys to chimpanzees31, and from chimpanzees to humans27.

The prefrontal cortex plays a key role in cognitive processes underlying abstract thinking, social interaction and communication32. Some of these processes are shared among the three species, including cognitive control of vocalization4,8, decision-making33, and cognitive set-shifting34–36, though they differ in degree across species. Other processes appear to be particularly developed or unique to chimpanzees and humans, such as complex behavior in competitive strategic interactions37, gestural communication38,39, and generative vocal communication adapted to the social context40,41. The expansion of the prefrontal FAT in chimpanzees and humans could therefore reflect in part a shared cognitive leap forward in the evolution of both species compared to monkeys. Further modifications of the prefrontal FAT in the human brain compared to chimpanzees, including the highest ratio of prefrontal to precentral fibers and its left lateralization might be linked to the evolution of speech.

To identify which component of the FAT may have contributed to specific aspects of speech evolution, we carried out a further segmentation of the prefrontal FAT and a correlation with severity of speech deficits in a large group of patients with PPA. This analysis revealed a posterior-to-anterior gradient for at least two aspects of spoken language, namely word production in spontaneous speech and syntax. Notably, spontaneous verbal fluency showed a progressively weaker correlation from precentral (highly significant) to orbitalis (not significant). Conversely, the correlation for syntax followed the opposite trend, with the strongest correlation with the orbitalis segment, then progressively decreasing in significance for the most posterior FAT segments. The two gradients overlap in Broca’s area, with possible functional subdivision into a posterior specialization for fluency (FAT opercularis), and anterior for syntactic processing (FAT triangularis). This is in line with functional imaging studies showing the same subdivision within the Broca’s area42. There is, however, no agreement on whether this functional subdivision encompasses more anterior or posterior regions of the ventrolateral frontal cortex. These discrepancies may depend on the study population, the imaging methods, and the complexity of syntactic processing42–45.

Previous studies showed a posterior to anterior gradient in the IFG, with syntactic functions lodged in BA44/BA45 and semantic processing in BA47. Hence our findings of a correlation between syntactic deficits and FAT orbitalis (projecting to BA47) were unexpected. Nevertheless, when parametric approaches are used (e.g. sentences with variable constituent structure), syntactic manipulation tasks activate BA45 and BA4743.

Our results indicate that this variability for syntactic processing may also depend on the type of neuropsychological test used to measure agrammatism. Correlations with NAVS-SPPT involved the FAT triangularis and FAT orbitalis while those with NAT were highly significant only for the FAT orbitalis. In both tests stimuli are visually presented as figures but they differ in the response, verbal in the NAVS-SPPT and based on word tokens re-arrangement in the NAT. Hence, the NAVS-SPPT can be affected by articulation problems while the NAT by reading difficulties. We tried to account for these limitations by co-varying the SPPT and NAT correlations with verbal fluency (WPM) and reading abilities, respectively. The results of these analyses confirm significant correlation with the two most anterior FAT segments, suggesting a prominent role of these two segments in syntactic processing irrespective of the output modality.

Previous studies suggest that a similar functional segregation is present also in the dorso-medial frontal cortex and its connections. Of note, direct electrical stimulation of the precentral FAT or its cortical projections (i.e. supplementary motor area, SMA) can affect limb movement and speech initiation46, whereas electrical stimulation of the prefrontal FAT (or pre-SMA) can impair syntax in sentence production without affecting articulation47. In general, our findings fit well with recent revisitation of Broca's area involvement in speech production with more posterior region (BA44) participation in articulatory-phonological functions and more anterior region (BA45) in linear morpho-syntactic functions42,48. On the basis of these theoretical models and our findings we can therefore predict a spectrum of non-fluent speech syndromes resulting from lesions in different portions of the FAT. Lesions to the precentral FAT and FAT opercularis would likely manifest with isolated or combined deficits in orofacial motor control, severe dysarthria (i.e. aphemia), and apraxia of speech49; lesions to the FAT opercularis and triangularis would result in severe agrammatic non-fluent aphasia (posterior transcortical motor aphasia); and lesions in the FAT triangularis/orbitalis would lead to a moderate reduction of verbal fluency for long sentences due mainly to syntactic difficulties (anterior transcortical motor aphasia).

Previous comparative anatomy studies50,51 and observations of speech impairments in patients with lesions sparing the dorsomedial and ventrolateral frontal regions suggest that the FAT is unlikely to be the only network involved in speech evolution. Our comparative analysis of the extended FAT networks showed no difference for the connections to the dorsomedial frontal region through the cingulum. This indicates that the limbic afferents to the FAT network, which have been considered as essential for innate vocalization4, have remained preserved throughout primate evolution.

Conversely, compared to monkeys and chimpanzees, humans have a strikingly greater volume of the connections to the ventrolateral frontal region through the arcuate fasciculus. The arcuate fasciculus is a non-limbic associative tract that carries complex auditory and visuo-spatial information from the temporal and parietal regions to the ventrolateral frontal cortex. Further dissections of the arcuate fasciculus showed no differences in the volume of its fronto-parietal segment among the three species, whereas the fronto-temporal component was greater in humans compared to non-human primates. Our interpretation is that the FAT, a network involved in hierarchical organization of behavior and motor sequencing, exapted and remodeled during speech evolution. We propose that a portion of the FAT network, which receives limbic input conveyed by the cingulum, is phylogenetically older and facilitates complex innate vocalization in all primate species. In humans, the greater expansion of the anterior FAT and the arcuate fasciculus which conveys auditory input to the ventrolateral frontal areas, enabled to produce complex speech sounds, ultimately dedicated to vocal syntactic communication.

In our study different acquisition parameters and tractography pipelines were necessarily used to optimize data quality for each species and imaging condition (in vivo vs. ex vivo), including volume resolution tailored to brain size, in line with established methodological results17. Although these choices were required, they may contribute to inter-species variability and should therefore be considered when interpreting comparative differences.

We minimized false negative artifacts (i.e., inability to visualize existing fibers) that can occur in tractography in regions containing fiber crossings. To do so, we adopted a spherical deconvolution approach which helps to recover multi-fibers that might not be detected with the more traditional diffusion tensor single-fiber models52. Still, false negatives remain a potential factor to consider when interpreting interspecies or interhemispheric differences in tract anatomy. Furthermore, the use of spherical deconvolution can introduce false positives (i.e., showing non-existing fibers), exaggerating anatomical differences.

Additionally, the human data we used did not support susceptibility distortion correction, which may have reduced the anatomical fidelity of the reconstructed tracts; however, the anatomical regions we assessed (superior and lateral frontal and temporal regions) are minimally influenced by these distortions, so we believe any such impact to be minimal.

We acknowledge that the Old World monkey sample consists exclusively of males, which precludes examination of sex differences in this group. Additionally, there is a disparity in sample sizes between Old World monkeys and the larger chimpanzee and human groups. However, to address this issue, we employed statistical methods to ensure more reliable control of type I error under conditions of unequal group sizes and variances.

The NAVS-SPPT and NAT tasks require semantic–structural integration and constituent ordering which relies on visual presentation of stimuli and both verbal and non-verbal production, respectively. These tasks engage an extended network involving temporal regions connected to the ventrolateral frontal cortex via the uncinate and arcuate fasciculus. Considering that the uncinate projections extend to pars orbitalis and triangularis and much less so to pars opercularis and precentral, the high correlation we found between syntax errors and anterior FAT components may reflect a role of BA47 and BA45 in visually presented syntactic stimuli rather than a supramodal syntactic function of these areas.

We also recognize that NAT and NAVS-SPPT examine the assignment of thematic roles and the ordering of sentence constituents, without targeting other syntactic functions, such as recursive hierarchical processing which have been demonstrated to engage more posterior areas (i.e. BA45 and 44)53,54. Our findings on the involvement of the FAT orbitalis and triangularis in syntactic processing should therefore be interpreted in this context43. Aware of these limitations, we made every effort to support our conclusions with previously published results using other anatomical or functional mapping methods. Finally, we emphasize that we derived our findings in patients with PPA from a clinical-anatomical covariance approach. This needs to be supported by a causal manipulation through brain stimulation paradigm.

In conclusion, our findings indicate that the FAT, a frontal network involved in vocalization, gestures and executive functions, underwent anatomical modifications in both chimpanzees and humans compared to monkeys. The modifications of the precentral and prefrontal FAT were in opposite directions, indicating other factors than allometric enlargement at play. The striking anatomical differences in the arcuate fasciculus between humans and non-human primates suggest that the convergence of a pre-existing FAT network and a temporo-frontal network for auditory processing was pivotal for the emergence of speech in humans. Our correlative analysis in PPA further supports this conclusion. Together, these results are in line with the view that unique aspects of human speech, including concatenation of phonemes into words, and words into sentences according to syntactic rules, may have evolved from the anatomical modifications of a frontal network that already existed in the brain of non-human primates for motor coordination and vocalization.

Methods

Participants

For the comparative anatomy study, diffusion data were obtained from nine Old World monkeys (seven ex vivo and two in vivo), 43 chimpanzees (in vivo), and 43 human participants (in vivo). For the clinical-anatomical correlation study, data were acquired from 98 participants with PPA (in vivo).

Old World Monkeys.

The Old World monkey datasets included three ex vivo vervet monkeys (Chlorocebus aethiops), three ex vivo and two in vivo rhesus macaques (Macaca mulatta), and one ex vivo cynomolgus macaque (Macaca fascicularis), acquired from 2003 to 2018. All three post-mortem, ex vivo datasets from the vervet monkey brains were obtained from the Behavioral Science Foundation of St Kitts, where the experimental protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Behavioral Science Foundation, acting under the auspices of the Canadian Council on Animal Care. The vervet monkeys were socially housed in enriched environments. All three post-mortem, ex vivo datasets from the rhesus macaque brains were obtained from a research project at the University of Oxford. These animals were also socially housed with enrichment. All procedures and care were performed in accordance with UK Home Office regulations and European Union (EU) guidelines (EU directive 86/609/EEC; EU Directive 2010/63/EU). The tissue fixation protocol is detailed in Dyrby et al.55 and Large et al.56.

The in vivo rhesus macaque datasets were obtained from the Icahn School of Medicine at Mount Sinai (ISMMS) New York. The experimental procedures were approved by the ISMMS Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee and conformed to the U.S. Public Health Service Policy on Humane Care and Use of Laboratory Animals, the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals, and the Association for Assessment and Accreditation of Laboratory Animal Care. Rhesus monkeys were socially housed as a group in an enriched environment. Scanning was performed under light isoflurane anesthesia57. Anesthesia was induced using ketamine (10 mg/kg, i.m.) and maintained with isoflurane at a low concentration (0.9–1.7% expired; mean, 1.38%). Anesthesia was supplemented with meloxicam (0.2 mg/ kg, i.v.) and ranitidine (0.05 mg/kg, i.v.). Monkeys were intubated and ventilated throughout each experiment. Physiological parameters including capnography, inspired and expired isoflurane concentration, SP02, core temperature, heart rate, and blood pressure were monitored and kept constant to maintain normal physiological function. The one postmortem, ex vivo dataset from the cynomolgus macaque brain was obtained from the Martinos Center for Biomedical Imaging Boston. As detailed in Crespigny et al.58, housing, transport, and experimental procedures were approved by the appropriate institutional animal care panels. Tissue was prepared as described in D’Arceuil et al.59.

Chimpanzees (Pan troglodytes).

All 43 in vivo datasets came from chimpanzees that were members of a captive colony at Yerkes National Primate Research Center (YNPRC) in Atlanta, Georgia, USA. Data were acquired starting from 2009 and transferred to our tractography laboratory prior to 2019, which determined the upper bound of the available sample at that time. Chimpanzees were socially housed in enriched environments. The study protocol was approved by the YNPRC and by the Emory University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC)60,61. All data were obtained prior to the 2015 implementation of U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service and NIH regulations governing research with chimpanzees. Prior to scanning, all chimpanzees underwent procedures designed to minimize stress. Chimpanzees were sedated with ketamine (10 mg/kg) or telazol (3–5 mg/kg) and then anesthetized with propofol (40–60 mg/(kg/h) before being transported to the scanner62.

Humans (Homo sapiens).

Forty-three healthy controls (HCs) for the comparative anatomy study, and 98 individuals with PPA for the speech correlation study were recruited at the Cognitive Neurology and Alzheimer’s Disease Center, Northwestern University, Chicago, from 2010 to 2018. Participants with PPA were recruited through one of the following mechanisms: 1) following clinical evaluation and diagnosis of PPA made by one of the behavioral neurologists working at the Northwestern University Neurobehavior and Memory Clinic, which is a full-service clinic dedicated to the diagnosis and care of individuals with neurodegenerative disorders, 2) following referrals made by outside clinicians, 3) by completing an on-line questionnaire that links to an Institute-wide centralized research registry. All participants were assessed by a behavioral neurologist (M-M.M.) who made or confirmed the diagnosis of PPA according to the current guidelines63, and were additionally screened by research personnel to ensure compliance with inclusion/exclusion criteria for the research study. Participants were included in the study if a) they had a diagnosis of PPA, b) they were native or fluent English speakers, c) they had a visual acuity (corrected or uncorrected) of 20/30, d) their comprehension was sufficiently preserved to be able to follow simple instructions, e) they had no contraindications to undergo MRI, f) they did not suffer from a medical condition (e.g., psychiatric illness) that would interfere with their continued participation in the study. People with PPA received a descriptive diagnosis of non-fluent/agrammatic (n = 36), logopenic (n = 21), semantic (n = 22), and mixed/severe/unclassified (n = 19). Informed consent was obtained from all human participants and the Institutional Review Board of Northwestern University approved the study. Demographic and clinical characteristic of the PPA cohort are listed in Table S3. The sampling procedure was based on convenience and sample size calculation performed according to previous DTI studies in PPA18,64,65. Given the rarity of the condition, the final sample represents one of the largest datasets currently available for this population.

Diffusion MRI acquisition

Full details about the diffusion MRI acquisition parameters are reported in Table S1 of the Supplementary Information and briefly described here.

The ex vivo vervet monkey and rhesus macaque datasets were acquired using a 4.7 T Varian Inova MRI scanner (Varian, Palo Alto, CA, USA) using a SE pulse sequence as detailed in ref. 55. The imaging procedure is described in Tang-Wright et al.66

The ex vivo cynomolgus macaque data were acquired using a 4.7 T Oxford magnet interfaced to a BioSpec Avance console (Bruker, Billerica, MA, USA). This used the same spin echo pulse sequence as described in D’Arceuil et al.59.

The in vivo rhesus macaque datasets were acquired with a 3 T Philips Gemini scanner with a custom-built eight-channel phased-array coil, with a single-loop local transmit coil (Windmiller Kolster Scientific), and an EPI pulse sequence.

The in vivo chimpanzee datasets were acquired using a 3 T Trio MRI Scanner (Siemens, Erlangen, Germany), with their head steadied in a human head coil. This used a spin echo, echo planar imaging (SE EPI) pulse sequence as detailed in Hopkins et al.62.

The human data (PPA patients and control participants) were acquired on a 3 T Trio MRI scanner (Siemens, Erlangen, Germany) using an SE EPI pulse sequence with a diffusion weighting value of b = 1000 s⋅mm−2, 60 gradient directions, and eight b = 0 s⋅mm−2 images.

Diffusion MRI pre-processing

All processing steps were performed in the native space of the individual brain, and all datasets were inspected for artifacts, both visually and with the ExploreDTI (www.exploredti.com) outlier profile tool.

The need for corrections varied by species. For Old World monkeys, the ex vivo data did not require corrections because the type of spin echo sequence used was robust to eddy current and geometric distortions. The in vivo macaque datasets were acquired with eight averages per brain (four with left–right phase-encoding direction and three with right–left), which facilitated correction for distortions along the phase-encoding direction. The in vivo datasets were first corrected for noise67 and Gibbs-ringing artifacts68. Susceptibility-induced off-resonance field distortions were then estimated using the topup tool in FSL69. Data were then corrected for motion and eddy currents with the FSL eddy tool (https://fsl.fmrib.ox.ac.uk/fsl/fslwiki/eddy)17.

The chimpanzees' data were first corrected for noise and Gibbs-ringing. A susceptibility-induced off-resonance field was then estimated using the tool topup as implemented in FSL. The FSL eddy tool was then used to correct the data for motion, eddy currents, signal dropout70 and geometric distortions using the previously computed field. During the eddy step, data from the two reverse-phase encoding directions were combined via least squares resampling to generate the best distortion-corrected version of the data. Given the high number of redundant directions resulting from the acquisition approach, spherical harmonic resampling was performed to more efficiently integrate the information from all acquired images on a set of 60 non-collinear directions while preserving all angular information up to spherical harmonic order l = 8. This step also served to reduce random noise while preserving the underlying angular structure of the diffusion signal.

Finally, the corrected data were then resampled in angular space using spherical harmonics to remove angular noise, and were upsampled to a finer grid with a voxel size of 1.5×1.5×1.5 mm371.

The human data were first corrected for noise67 and Gibbs-ringing artifacts68. Correction for motion and eddy currents followed using eddy with signal dropout replacement70.

Diffusion tractography processing

For all preprocessed diffusion MRI datasets, StarTrack (http://www.mr-startrack.com) was used to generate spherical deconvolution tractography (SD). First, the fiber orientation distribution functions (fODF) were estimated using the damped Richardson-Lucy spherical deconvolution algorithm72. Tracking was performed using deterministic tractography based on the Euler algorithm73 for all monkey and human data, and dispersion tractography for the chimpanzee data. A whole-brain approach was used, with one seed point per voxel and one streamline generated for each peak of the fODF above the set hindrance modulated orientational anisotropy (HMOA)73 threshold. A diffusion tensor model was also fit to the human data at this stage to allow for the extraction of tensor-based metrics for later analysis.

Given that the ex vivo monkey datasets varied by both voxel size and levels of noise, the processing parameters used to model spherical deconvolution and tractography were optimized for each dataset17.

Spherical deconvolution and tractography parameters are detailed in Table S2.

Tract dissection and analysis

Dissections were performed by a single operator (SC), who received dedicated training from an expert neuroanatomist (MC). After training, ten randomly selected humans, ten randomly selected chimpanzees, and all 9 Old World monkeys were independently dissected by both raters. Inter-class correlation coefficients (ICC) and volume correlations were then computed on paired dissections of the same tracts, demonstrating excellent inter-rater reliability (ICC > 0.90 across all species). Specifically, for Old World monkeys: ICC = 0.980, p < 0.001; volume correlation: r = 0.987, p < 0.001. For chimpanzees: ICC = 0.907, p < 0.001; volume correlation: r = 0.923, p < 0.001. For humans: ICC = 0.924, p < 0.001; volume correlation: r = 0.928, p < 0.001. All dissections were performed using the TrackVis (http://trackvis.org) software.

SC was blind to the identity and any general information of individual datasets. Tracts were dissected using multiple, manually drawn inclusion and exclusion regions of interest (ROIs) on different planes, combined according to “AND” and “NOT” Boolean rules. A stepwise dissection approach was used14,15 (Fig. S1 in Supplementary Information). For humans and chimpanzees, a large ROI was delineated on the deep white matter of the inferior frontal and precentral gyri. A second ROI was placed on the deep white matter of the superior frontal gyrus. All tracts passing through both regions were included. This approach maximizes the inclusion of streamlines (e.g. streamlines that fall short to reach the cortex) while excluding artifacts due to a visually guided ROI selection.

A similar approach was used for Old World monkeys, where a large ROI was manually delineated on the deep white matter of the ventrolateral frontal region. A second ROI was placed on the deep white matter of the dorsolateral and dorsomedial frontal regions (Fig. S2 in Supplementary Information).

Precentral and prefrontal FAT segments were identified by subdividing the lateral ROI into two distinct ROIs: one corresponding to the prefrontal segment and the other to the precentral segment.

In chimpanzees and humans, the prefrontal ROI was placed in the deep white matter of the inferior frontal gyrus, whereas the precentral ROI was located in the deep white matter of the inferior portion of the precentral gyrus.

In Old World monkeys, the prefrontal ROI was delineated within the deep white matter underlying the ventrolateral prefrontal cortex, using the principal sulcus as the upper boundary and the arcuate sulcus as the posterior boundary. The arcuate sulcus serves as a reliable anatomical landmark approximating the cytoarchitectonic transition between areas 44 and 6, corresponding to the boundary represented by the precentral sulcus in chimpanzees and humans74,75. The precentral ROI in monkeys was positioned in the deep white matter of the inferior premotor and motor regions, bounded anteriorly by the arcuate sulcus and posteriorly by the central sulcus.

The same ventrolateral frontal ROI utilized for the FAT dissections was subsequently employed to identify the fascicles comprising the ventrolateral FAT extended networks. The identified fronto-temporal and fronto-parietal segments of the arcuate fasciculus represent the streamlines sharing the same frontal projections of the FAT (rather than the complete arcuate fasciculus which encompasses streamlines projecting to frontal lobe regions beyond those shared with the FAT). An analogous approach was applied to dorsomedial FAT extended network (i.e., cingulum) using the corresponding dorsomedial FAT ROI.

For all participants, artifactual streamlines (e.g. looping) were removed using a “NOT” ROI.

For Old World monkeys, the axonal tracing atlas by Schmahmann and Pandya76 and the tractography atlas by Thiebaut de Schotten et al.15 were used as an anatomical reference. For chimpanzees, the diffusion MRI atlas by Bryant et al.16 and the sulcal anatomy by Hopkins et al.77 were used. The atlases by Catani were the guide used for the human white matter tracts14,64. For each participant we utilized surface anatomy landmarks as referenced in Amiez et al.78,79 In the PPA datasets, the anterior prefrontal FAT was further divided into three segments based on the anatomy of the inferior frontal gyrus (pars opercularis, pars triangularis, pars orbitalis) using anatomical surface references: the horizontal ramus of the Sylvian fissure was used to delineate the boundary between the pars orbitalis and pars triangularis, while the ascending ramus served to separate the pars triangularis from the pars opercularis (Fig. 3A, B).

Tract volumes and microscopic diffusion measurements

The volume of the FAT from each hemisphere was extracted from TrackVis and calculated as the sum of the volumes of all voxels intersected by the FAT streamlines. These raw volumes constituted the absolute volumes. To reduce biases related to cross-species differences in brain size, proportional volumes were calculated by normalizing the absolute volumes by the total volume of the white matter for each brain. Comparison of fascicle volumes across species is then reported as proportional.

HMOA, as well as the tensor-based FA, MD, and RD, as microscopic diffusion measurements, were also extracted for the analysis in the PPA dataset. Tract volume and microscopic diffusion measurements provide complementary information about observed variations in white matter18.

Neuropsychological assessment of PPA

Individuals with PPA underwent a comprehensive neuropsychological examination for the assessment of a variety of language functions80. Spontaneous speech fluency and syntactic production were assessed in detail. For the assessment of spontaneous verbal fluency, speech samples were recorded from each participant while they told the Cinderella story from a wordless picture book22. Words per minute from the told Cinderella storytelling task was considered as the metrics of spontaneous verbal fluency81. The 30-item version of the Northwestern Anagram Test (NAT) and the Sentence Production Priming Test from the Northwestern Assessment of Verbs and Sentences (NAVS-SPPT) were used to assess syntactic processing24,25,82.

Reading abilities were evaluated using the Psycholinguistic Assessment of Language Processing in Aphasia (PALPA)83. Specifically, PALPA subtest 35 was administered to access single-word reading. PPA participants were presented with a list of 10 regular words. Each word was displayed individually, and participants were instructed to read the list aloud. Responses were audio-recorded and subsequently scored as either correct or incorrect (no partial credit). Paraphasias, phonological or articulatory errors were scored as incorrect, whereas atypical stress patterns and self-corrections were accepted as correct. The total score ranged from 0 to 10, reflecting the number of correctly read words.

Statistical analysis

For the comparative anatomy study, data were divided into three groups: humans (n = 43), chimpanzees (n = 43), and Old World monkeys (n = 9). RStudio (v2023.12.0, Posit Software PBC, Boston, MA, United States) was used to perform all statistical analyses. Normality and variance tests were applied to all experimental data. The graphs included in the figures were created using Prism (v9.5.0, GraphPad Software Inc., La Jolla, CA, United States).

A lateralization index was calculated for the volume of the dissected tracts according to the formula:

Lateralization index = (Right volume – Left volume) / (Right volume + Left volume)

where a negative value indicates a leftward volume asymmetry and a positive value a rightward asymmetry84. Statistical significance of the degree of lateralization for each tract within a species was determined using a one-sample t test (test value = 0). A post-hoc Bonferroni correction was applied to adjust for multiple comparisons. Species group differences in the proportional volume measures were identified using a one-way Welch ANOVA85, with an asymptotically distributed F statistic. A Games-Howell post-hoc analysis was conducted to identify the specific differences between species groups86. These statistical tests were chosen for their robustness against the small size of one group and inhomogeneity of variance between groups86. The Games-Howell post-hoc analysis also controls for multiple comparisons. Pearson bivariate or Spearman correlation analyses were utilized to assess the correlation between tract-specific measurements and language scores within the PPA group, depending on whether the variables were normally or non-normally distributed, while controlling for age. Bonferroni correction was then performed.

To quantify the relative strength of associations for the two tasks within each FAT segment, we calculated a correlation ratio by dividing the FA–WPM correlation coefficient by the corresponding FA–NAT correlation coefficient (Fig. 3G).
