The interplay between metabolism and neuroinflammation in epilepsy: mechanisms and therapeutic perspectives
Authors: Leqing Xu, Zhao Zhao, Yufei Zhang, Chunlai Ma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractEmerging evidence underscores the critical role of neuroinflammation and metabolism in the pathophysiology of epilepsy. Metabolic dysregulation and neuroinflammation exacerbate each other, creating a vicious cycle. Although this interplay offers novel insights into the mechanisms underlying neurological disorders and potential therapeutic approaches, its role in epilepsy remains unclear. In this study, we review the interplay between metabolism and neuroinflammation, focusing on glucose, glutamate /γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA), tryptophan-kynurenine, adenosine, and lipid metabolism, aiming to contribute to the development of innovative therapeutic strategies for epilepsy. We also discuss the potential anti-neuroinflammatory and antiepileptic effects of metabolic therapies.Keywords: Epilepsy, Metabolic dysfunction, Neuroinflammation, Glucose, Adenosine, Glutamate, Γ-aminobutyric acid, Tryptophan, Lipid, Metabolic therapy

Background

Epilepsy is a common neurological disorder, affecting individuals of various ages, ethnicities, and geographical regions, with an estimated 50 million people affected worldwide [1]. Although over 30 anti-seizure medications (ASMs) are available, approximately one-third of patients with epilepsy fail to achieve seizure control through pharmacological interventions [2–4]. Epilepsy imposes significant economic burdens, contributes to disability, and drastically reduces the patients’ quality of life. Extensive research over several decades has yielded limited therapeutic advancements, and epilepsy management remains a major challenge [5]. Therefore, further exploration of underlying mechanisms and identification and development of novel therapeutic targets are of critical importance. Neuroinflammation is defined as an innate and adaptive inflammatory response within the central nervous system (CNS) that initially serves as a defense mechanism, characterized by glial cell activation, infiltration of peripheral immune cells, and the release of proinflammatory mediators [6, 7]. However, a persistent and dysregulated state of neuroinflammation can be detrimental and contribute to neurological disorders including Alzheimer’s disease (AD) and Parkinson’s disease (PD) [8–10]. Accumulating evidence indicates that neuroinflammation contributes to the onset and progression of epilepsy through various mechanisms, including alterations in the functions of neurons, glial cells, and the blood-brain barrier (BBB) [11–13]. Recent studies suggest that metabolic dysfunction plays a significant role in seizures, epileptogenesis, and maintenance of epileptic states, with multiple dysregulated metabolic pathways commonly observed in both animal models and patients with epilepsy [14, 15]. In addition, several metabolic therapies, such as the ketogenic diet (KD), have demonstrated potential in epilepsy treatment [16–18]. Notably, there may be a bidirectional relationship between neuroinflammation and metabolic states in the CNS [19–21]. Inflammatory signals induce metabolic reprogramming in CNS cells, particularly in microglia, which modulates their immune functions and drives neuroinflammation [15]. Although the interplay between neuroinflammation and metabolic dysfunction has been explored in the context of neurodegenerative diseases, its specific role in the pathophysiology of epilepsy has not been thoroughly investigated [22, 24]. Therefore, we herein focus on their interaction in epilepsy and discuss novel therapeutic strategies targeting this interplay.

Neuroinflammation in epilepsy

Neuroinflammation can act as both the cause and consequence of epilepsy [25, 26]. It commonly occurs after epileptogenic events such as traumatic brain injury and infectious diseases [11, 27]. Neuroinflammatory molecules affect synaptic plasticity and the excitation/inhibition (E/I) balance by modulating voltage-gated ion channels, neurotransmitter release, and receptor-coupled ion channels, thereby promoting the initiation of seizures [28, 29]. Neuroinflammation is increasingly being recognized as a driver of long-term structural remodeling in the epileptic brain [30, 31]. Seizures induce a range of pathological alterations such as neuronal loss, aberrant neurogenesis, mossy fiber sprouting, and network reorganization, all of which contribute to the formation of recurrent excitatory circuits, ultimately increasing network hyperexcitability [32, 33]. Furthermore, seizure-induced neurodegeneration can provoke glial activation and cytokine release, establishing a self-reinforcing loop between neuroinflammation and neuronal damage [34, 35].

A notable clinical example is encephalitis, in which intense neuroinflammatory responses serve as key drivers of seizure and epileptogenesis [36, 37]. Moreover, substantial evidence suggests the involvement of neuroinflammation in the initiation and progression of epilepsy. The brain tissues of patients with epilepsy show upregulated proinflammatory cytokines and chemokines, including tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), interleukin (IL)−1β, IL-6, and C-X-C motif chemokine Ligand 2 (CXCL2) [29, 38–41]. Neuroinflammation-related molecules are potential biomarkers in epilepsy [11, 42]. A large cohort study demonstrated that patients with epilepsy exhibited abnormal plasma levels of cytokines, such as TNF-α, interferon (IFN)-γ, and IL-2. Although these inflammatory mediators cannot differentiate epilepsy subtypes, they are potential biomarkers of epilepsy and seizures [43]. Collectively, the evidence indicates that neuroinflammation in epilepsy is not simply a downstream consequence, but also a critical driver of neuronal network remodeling and hyperexcitability.

Cells involved in neuroinflammation

Microglia and astrocytes are key regulators of neuroinflammatory processes in the CNS [8]. Both transition from resting to reactive states in response to stimulation and stress [44]. Glial cells respond to pathogen-associated molecular patterns or damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), such as high mobility group box-1 protein (HMGB1), via pattern recognition receptors (e.g., Toll-like receptors (TLRs)) [45, 46]. The interactions between proinflammatory signals and specific receptors trigger downstream signaling cascades, such as nuclear factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells (NF-κB) signaling pathways, leading to the release of proinflammatory mediators [47]. Elevated proinflammatory mediators can induce seizures through various mechanisms, such as disrupting the homeostasis of glutamate and γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA) [12]. Activated microglia and astrocytes have been detected in animal models of epilepsy and hippocampal tissue of patients with epilepsy [48–51]. Translocator protein (TSPO) expression is regarded as a neuroinflammatory marker that reflects gliosis and microglial activation [52]. Several TSPO-positron emission tomography (PET) studies have identified neuroinflammation in the brains of patients with epilepsy [53–57]. These findings highlight the role of glial cells as key mediators exacerbating both inflammatory cascades and neuronal excitability in epilepsy.

In addition to glial cell activation, the infiltration of peripheral immune cells (e.g., neutrophils and monocytes) is a key feature of epilepsy-associated neuroinflammation [25, 58]. Seizures lead to increased C-C motif chemokine Ligand 2 (CCL2) production by neurons and microglia, which attracts CCR2 + monocytes to the CNS [59]. Together with proliferating microglia, these monocytes contribute to microgliosis [60]. In addition, monocyte infiltration may exacerbate BBB disruption, thereby promoting epileptogenesis [61]. Infiltrating monocytes also produce proinflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β, leading to neuronal death [59]. Furthermore, studies have shown that the percentage of IL-1β- and IL-6-positive monocytes in the blood of patients with drug-resistant epilepsy (DRE) is significantly higher than that in drug-sensitive patients, and the intracellular IL-1β levels of monocytes are associated with seizure frequency [62, 63]. CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T cells have also been detected in the hippocampal tissue of patients with epilepsy, and the quantity of T cells in the hippocampus directly correlates with the degree of neuronal loss [64–66]. CD4⁺ T cells infiltrate into the brain across the disrupted BBB, where they promote proinflammatory polarization of microglia, thereby contributing to neuroinflammation and pathological progression of epilepsy [67]. In contrast, regulatory T cells (Tregs) appear to play a protective role. Higher Treg numbers are associated with reduced disease severity, whereas Treg depletion exacerbates neuroinflammation, oxidative stress, neuronal damage, and seizure frequency in animal models [66, 68]. Additionally, in the hippocampus of pilocarpine-induced epileptic mouse models, infiltrated Ly6G⁺ Ly6C⁺ neutrophils mediate neuroinflammation and promote seizure activity through the secretion of IL-1β and TNF-α [69].

Neuroinflammatory pathways in epilepsy

Multiple neuroinflammatory pathways are activated in epilepsy [11, 70]. HMGB1 regulates transcriptional activity, maintains the chromatin structure, and supports DNA repair [71]. During seizures, HMGB1 is translocated from the nucleus to the cytoplasm and is released extracellularly, which acts as a DAMP and mediates various physiological and pathological responses, including neuroinflammation [72]. HMGB1 is involved in the pathophysiology of epilepsy mainly by interacting with TLR4 [73]. Activation of the HMGB1/TLR4 pathway stimulates the NF-κB signaling pathways, increasing IL-1β secretion. IL-1β acts on IL-1 receptor type 1 (IL-1R1) located on neurons and glial cells, further promoting glial cell activation, leading to enhanced neuroinflammatory responses and increased seizure risk. The release of proinflammatory factors and brain cell damage caused by seizures further promote HMGB1 release, creating a vicious cycle [74, 75]. In parallel, activation of the IL-1β/IL-1R signaling pathway can enhance the phosphorylation of the NR2B subunit of the N-methyl-D-aspartate receptor (NMDAR), leading to excessive Ca²⁺ influx into neurons, and may also suppress inhibitory GABAergic neurotransmission. These mechanisms collectively lower the seizure threshold and promote neuronal hyperexcitability, leading to epilepsy [76–78]. Recent research has shown that elevated HMGB1 and TLR4 expression levels are associated with an increased risk and severity of epilepsy [79]. Suppressing the TLR4/NF-κB pathway inhibits microglial M1 polarization, the proinflammatory phenotype of microglia, thus attenuating DRE [80, 81].

In addition, the activation of microglial IL-1R1 and TLR4 promotes the assembly of inflammasome complexes, leading to neuronal damage, glial activation, and neuronal hyperexcitability [82]. NOD-like receptor protein 1 (NLRP1) and NLRP3 inflammasome can activate caspase-1 to induce IL-1β secretion, further stimulating the release of IL-6 and TNF-α [83, 84]. In addition to IL-1β, IL-6 and TNF-α also increase seizure susceptibility through various mechanisms, including disruption of the BBB and E/I balance [85, 86]. Elevated levels of NLRP1 and NLRP3 inflammasomes occur in patients with temporal lobe epilepsy (TLE), and pharmacological inhibition of these inflammasomes has shown antiepileptic effects by reducing neuroinflammation [87–90].

Mitochondrial dysfunction is also associated with NLRP3 inflammasome activation. Defects in mitophagy and accumulation of damaged mitochondria have been observed in the hippocampus of patients with refractory TLE with hippocampal sclerosis [91]. Insufficient mitophagy may hinder the clearance of dysfunctional mitochondria, which can promote inflammasome activation and trigger neuroinflammatory responses [92, 93]. Enhanced mitophagy reduced NLRP3 inflammasome activation in microglia under inflammatory stimulation, thereby attenuating neurotoxicity [94]. Interestingly, a recent study reported that in the pilocarpine-induced epilepsy model, increased NLRP3 activation and elevated IL-1β levels may further suppress hippocampal mitophagy [95]. These findings suggest a bidirectional interaction between mitophagy and neuroinflammation and highlight the need for further investigation into the role of mitophagy impairment in epileptogenesis.

Cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2) catalyzes the conversion of arachidonic acid into prostaglandin (PG) H₂, which is then converted into one of five major PGs. These PGs activate PG receptors and mediate inflammatory processes [96]. Early studies identified COX-2 upregulation in the hippocampal tissue of patients with TLE [97]. Proinflammatory cytokines produced by activated astrocytes and microglia promote COX-2 activation, leading to increased PGs. Specifically, PGD₂-mediated microglial activation contributes to delayed neuronal death in the hippocampus [98]. Activation of the PGE₂ receptor EP2 enhances neuroinflammation and neurotoxicity, whereas EP4 activation reduces neuroinflammation [99, 100]. COX-2 activation increases PGE₂ and EP2, but decreases EP4 [101, 102]. Additionally, the activation of PG receptors on the astrocyte surface may promote astrocyte activation and stimulate neuroinflammatory responses [103].

Anti-neuroinflammation therapies

Notably, clinical results suggest that anti-inflammatory treatment is a promising therapeutic strategy for epilepsy. For example, early anakinra therapy (an IL-1R antagonist) may reduce seizures in patients with febrile infection-related epilepsy syndrome (FIRES) [104]. Tocilizumab treatment (a humanized monoclonal antibody against the IL-6 receptor) was effective in patients with FIRES and in cases of new-onset refractory status epilepticus [105, 106]. Anti-TNF-α therapy (adalimumab) improved seizure control in patients with Rasmussen’s encephalitis [107]. Several compounds that target neuroinflammatory pathways also demonstrated antiepileptic potential in animal models. For example, celecoxib, a COX-2 inhibitor, reduced seizure susceptibility and exerted neuroprotective effects in kainic acid (KA)-induced rat models of epilepsy by inhibiting the HMGB1/TLR4 signaling pathway [108]. In mouse models of pilocarpine-induced epilepsy, anti-HMGB1 monoclonal antibodies prevented BBB permeability and decreased inflammatory cytokines during acute seizures [109]. Additionally, VX-765, a selective inhibitor of IL-converting enzyme/caspase-1, reduced acute seizures and drug-resistant chronic epileptic activity by inhibiting the biosynthesis of IL-1β [110]. Furthermore, the TLR4 inhibitors TAK-242 and microRNA-181b significantly attenuated seizure activity and improved spatial memory abilities in KA-induced rat models [111].

However, the clinical translation of anti-inflammatory therapies for epilepsy still faces several challenges. First, the current case reports are mostly limited to specific epilepsy subtypes, such as FIRES, in which immune mechanisms are clearly implicated. Epilepsy is a multifactorial disease in which inflammation is only one of the contributing drivers [2, 112]. Therefore, targeting inflammatory pathways alone may yield therapeutic benefits only in a subset of patients with defined immune-mediated pathologies [2, 112]. In addition, key parameters such as the optimal therapeutic window, onset of efficacy, and duration of the effect of anti-inflammatory interventions may differ significantly from those of conventional ASMs [113]. These uncertainties may complicate the design of clinical trials and hinder further clinical translation of anti-inflammatory approaches. Nevertheless, with continued research and a deeper understanding of immune mechanisms in epilepsy, the potential role of immunomodulatory strategies is expected to become increasingly clear.

The interplay between metabolism and neuroinflammation in epilepsy

Glucose metabolism

The brain consumes approximately 20% of the glucose-derived energy, making it the main consumer of glucose [114, 115]. Disease-associated glucose metabolism dysfunction can impair neuronal function by triggering neuroinflammation, increasing oxidative stress, or accumulating glycated toxic proteins [115]. Dysregulation of glucose metabolism associated with neuroinflammation may play a critical role in the pathogenesis of epilepsy [116]. Similar mechanisms have also been implicated in other CNS diseases, including AD, PD, and stroke [117].

Glucose transporters (GLUTs) facilitate sodium-independent transport of glucose into brain cells. GLUT3 is primarily expressed in neurons, while GLUT1 is predominantly found in endothelial cells and astrocytes [118, 119]. Interestingly, studies have identified GLUT1 as a major glucose transporter expressed in microglia [120]. Cell type-specific GLUT1 expression appears to play a distinct role in epilepsy. For example, endothelial GLUT1 mediates glucose transport across the BBB [121]. During seizures, energy consumption in the brain increases markedly [14, 122]. [18F]fluorodeoxyglucose ([18F]FDG)-PET studies in patients and animal models have shown status epilepticus (SE)-induced hypometabolism in the epileptogenic areas, including the hippocampus, cortex and striatum [115, 123–125]. In the lithium-pilocarpine epilepsy model for immature and adult rats, GLUT1 mRNA expression showed reduces the expression of proinflammatory cytokineswidespread, age-specific upregulation during the acute phase [126]. Therefore, downregulation of endothelial GLUT1 can significantly impair glucose transport into the brain, thereby disrupting astrocytic potassium buffering and glutamate uptake and contributing to neuronal hyperexcitability [127, 128]. Supporting this, GLUT1 deletion from endothelial cells led to seizures and severe neuroinflammatory responses in adult rats [129]. In contrast, GLUT1 has a different pathological role in microglia. Under inflammatory conditions, GLUT1 is upregulated in microglia, facilitating glucose uptake and enhancing glycolytic activity. This may competitively suppress GLUT3-mediated neuronal glucose uptake, resulting in insufficient energy supply to the neurons [120]. As a result, neurons fail to maintain their membrane potential via ATP-dependent ion pumps (such as the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase), contributing to neuronal hyperexcitability [114, 130].Reprogramming and Inflammatory Regulation in Microglia

Glycolytic reprogramming is a hallmark of epilepsy. During seizures, the aerobic pathway (i.e., the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle) cannot supply adequate energy, and glycolysis is the primary source of energy [131]. The activity of enzymes involved in the aerobic pathway, including pyruvate dehydrogenase, aconitase, malate dehydrogenases, and succinate dehydrogenases, is decreased, whereas the activity of enzymes involved in glycolysis (e.g., phosphofructokinase and glucokinase) and lactate levels are increased [131–134]. The flux of the pentose phosphate pathway (PPP) is also elevated in epilepsy [135, 136].

Metabolic reprogramming in microglia is essential for the activation of their inflammatory responses in CNS disorders including epilepsy [22, 137, 138]. Lipopolysaccharide (LPS) is widely used to induce immune responses in vitro to mimic the neuroinflammatory environment observed in epilepsy and related conditions [139–141]. Under proinflammatory stimuli such as LPS, microglia rapidly shift their metabolism from oxidative phosphorylation (OxPhos) to aerobic glycolysis. This process is mediated by the activation of TLRs and downstream phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K)/protein kinase B (Akt)/mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) signaling pathway, which upregulates hypoxia-inducible factor-1α (HIF-1α), GLUT1, and key glycolytic enzymes [142–145]. This shift to glycolysis leads to an increased flux of PPP, which generates NADPH [146]. In turn, elevated NADPH levels can promote the activation of NF-κB, ultimately further exacerbating neuroinflammation [147]. Additionally, the impaired TCA cycle leads to the accumulation of succinate, which not only promotes IL-1β production via HIF-1α stabilization, but may also be involved in epileptogenesis and neurodegeneration through the activation of mitophagy [148–150]. Moreover, increased GLUT1 expression facilitates glucose uptake and glycolysis, further reinforcing NF-κB activation and the production of proinflammatory cytokines [120, 151] (Fig. 1). Conversely, the inhibition of GLUT1-mediated glucose uptake shifts microglial metabolism toward OxPhos and significantly reduces the expression of proinflammatory cytokines [120].

Transcriptional co-repressor C-terminal binding protein (CtBP) is required for the transactivation of proinflammatory genes in LPS-stimulated microglia [152]. NADH and NAD+ modulate the activity of CtBP, implying that they regulate transcription in response to metabolic changes [153]. A recent study found that elevated level of glycolysis in microglia increased the ratio of NADH to NAD+, thereby promoting the dimerization of CtBP and increasing NF-κB transcriptional activity, ultimately activating neuroinflammatory responses [154, 155] (Fig. 1). Previous studies have already investigated the role of CtBP and increased NF-κB in epilepsy [156, 157]. The antiepileptic effect of glycolytic inhibitors relies on the CtBP-dependent regulation of chromatin structure [156]. However, contradictory findings have also been reported. Using the pilocarpine-induced SE model of rats, a previous study performed [18F]FDG-PET and a correlation analysis with previously acquired TSPO-PET data, which showed no clear influence of activated microglia on [18F]FDG signals during epileptogenesis [158]. The discrepancy between this result and other findings may be due to differences in the experimental methods, as the [18F]FDG-PET and TSPO-PET data were obtained from different animals.

In summary, dysregulated glucose metabolism and neuroinflammation are closely interconnected in the pathogenesis of epilepsy. Figure 1 illustrates the metabolic reprogramming and inflammatory signaling interactions in microglia. Neuronal glucose uptake is mediated by GLUT3, while GLUT1 plays a distinct role in endothelial glucose transport across the BBB and microglial activation. Under inflammatory conditions, microglia undergo metabolic reprogramming, shifting from OxPhos to glycolysis and PPP, a transition driven by PI3K/Akt/mTOR/HIF-1α signaling. This metabolic shift enhances NF-κB and CtBP-mediated proinflammatory transcription. Elevated microglial GLUT1 expression further promotes glycolysis and may limit glucose availability in neurons, impairing ATP-dependent processes and contributing to neuronal hyperexcitability. While these mechanisms highlight the interplay between metabolism and inflammation, inconsistencies in PET imaging studies suggest that further investigations are needed to clarify the cell-specific metabolic dynamics in epilepsy.

Glutamate/GABA metabolism

Glutamate is the main excitatory neurotransmitter and an essential metabolic substrate in the CNS [159]. However, excessive extracellular glutamate induces neurotoxicity, also called excitotoxicity, via the overactivation of glutamate receptors [160]. In contrast, GABA is the most important inhibitory neurotransmitter in the brain [161]. The relative levels of glutamate and GABA are critical for maintaining E/I balance and regulating neural excitability [162]. This balance in the brain is sustained through the glutamate/GABA-glutamine cycle, in which astrocytes take up synaptic glutamate and GABA, convert them into glutamine, and shuttle it back to neurons for neurotransmitter resynthesis [163–165]. Within astrocytes, glutamate is converted to glutamine by GS. Glutamine is then transported back to neurons, where it is converted to glutamate. In GABAergic neurons, glutamate is converted into GABA via GAD. GABA released into the synaptic cleft is taken up by astrocytes through GABA transporters, where it is metabolized to succinate, entering the TCA cycle for glutamate resynthesis. During neuroinflammation, proinflammatory cytokines (such as IL-1β and TNF-α) released by activated microglia, astrocytes, and infiltrating monocytes promote glutamate release and impair its uptake, leading to elevated extracellular glutamate. One possible mechanism is that inflammatory cytokines disrupt glutamate clearance by downregulating GS expression in glial cells. In parallel, HMGB1 released from neurons amplifies inflammation and further disrupts the excitation/inhibition balance by interacting with GLAST and downregulating GAD67, resulting in reduced GABA synthesis. These alterations impair the glutamate/GABA-glutamine cycle, enhance neuronal excitability, and promote seizure generation. Moreover, excess extracellular glutamate and diminished GABA can activate microglia and exacerbate neuronal injury, forming a vicious cycle linking excitotoxicity and inflammation. Previous findings suggest that the glutamate/GABA-glutamine cycle may be disrupted in epilepsy, primarily characterized by elevated extracellular glutamate concentrations, decreased glutamine levels, and insufficient GABA production in epileptogenic regions [166–169]. Increased extracellular glutamate may result from the loss of glutamine synthetase (GS), the enzyme responsible for converting glutamate to glutamine, and dysfunction of the excitatory amino acid transporters (EAATs), which mediate glutamate reuptake [170–173].

Notably, neuroinflammation is related to glutamatergic hyperactivity in epilepsy [11, 28]. Proinflammatory cytokines (such as IL-1β and TNF-α) released by activated inflammatory cells promote glutamate release from astrocytes and microglia while inhibiting its uptake, leading to elevated glutamate levels in neuronal synapses and increased neuronal excitability [27, 174, 175]. One possible mechanism is that inflammatory cytokines disrupt glutamate clearance by downregulating GS expression in glial cells [176, 177]. Despite growing evidence that GS in microglia is regulated by proinflammatory signals and plays a critical role in microglial activation and neuroinflammatory responses, its specific role in epilepsy remains poorly understood [176, 178]. As previously mentioned, in addition to GS dysfunction, alterations in the expression and activity of EAATs contribute to glutamate accumulation. In human and animal models, although EAAT levels and functionality can change widely in epilepsy, the main trend is a downregulation of EAAT1/glial glutamate-aspartate transporter (GLAST) and EAAT2/glutamate transporter-1 (GLT-1), accompanied by reduced activity in astrocytes [172]. Proinflammatory cytokines, especially IL-1β and TNF-α, are negative regulators of the expression and activity of astrocytic EAATs [179, 180]. HMGB1 is also involved in the regulation of glutamate/GABA metabolism; exogenous HMGB1 may enhance glutamate release by interacting with GLAST [181]. Moreover, an increase in extracellular HMGB1 downregulates the expression of glutamate decarboxylase (GAD) 67 and increases glutamate levels [182]. GAD67 catalyzes the conversion of glutamate to GABA, and its deficiency may lead to reduced GABA production, thereby impairing GABAergic signaling [165].

Reciprocally, the dysfunction of the glutamate/GABA cycle may further promote neuroinflammation. Glutamate released by neurons can bind to glutamate receptors on microglia, Such as the metabotropic glutamate receptor 2, triggering inflammatory responses [183]. In contrast, GABA may exert anti-inflammatory effects and reduced GABA facilitates neuroinflammation [184]. Additionally, E/I imbalance may lead to excitotoxicity, resulting in neuronal death and release of DAMPs (such as HMGB1), which further activate inflammatory pathways [34, 185].

The bidirectional interaction between neuroinflammation and glutamate/GABA imbalance is illustrated in Fig. 2, which provides an overview of how glial cells and inflammatory mediators disrupt neurotransmitter homeostasis and contribute to network hyperexcitability in epilepsy. Overall, neuroinflammation and glutamate/GABA dysregulation form a self-perpetuating vicious cycle. Proinflammatory cytokines enhance glutamate release while suppressing its uptake and conversion by microglia and astrocytes, resulting in elevated extracellular glutamate levels. Concurrently, inflammation impairs GABA synthesis and signaling, thereby reducing inhibitory neurotransmission. This imbalance contributes to excitotoxicity and promotes neuronal damage and cell death. In turn, dying neurons release DAMPs that activate glial cells and amplify neuroinflammation, thereby forming a vicious cycle that exacerbates epileptogenesis.

Tryptophan-kynurenine metabolism

In addition to glutamate and GABA, tryptophan (TRP) is another amino acid whose metabolism may serve as a critical link between neuroinflammation and epilepsy. TRP is an essential amino acid that can be metabolized into various bioactive molecules and is reportedly extensively involved in the pathogenesis of CNS diseases [186, 187]. The kynurenine pathway (KP), initiated by indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase (IDO) or tryptophan 2,3-dioxygenase (TDO), is a major pathway for the metabolism of TRP [186]. In this pathway, TRP is catabolized to kynurenine (KYN), which is further transformed into the neuroprotective kynurenic acid (KYNA) via kynurenine aminotransferase (KAT), or neurotoxic 3-hydroxykynurenine and quinolinic acid (QA) [188] (Fig. 3).

Simplified Tryptophan-Kynurenine (TRP-KYN) Pathway and its Role in Neuroinflammation and Excitotoxicity. Proinflammatory cytokines (such as IL-1β and IL-6) upregulate indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase (IDO), promoting the conversion of TRP to KYN. KYN is subsequently metabolized either into neuroprotective kynurenic acid (KYNA) via kynurenine aminotransferase (KAT), or into the neurotoxic metabolite quinolinic acid (QA) through a series of downstream enzymes. KYNA, predominantly synthesized by astrocytes, antagonizes NMDAR and α7nAChR, inhibits glutamate release, and may modulate GABA signaling. QA, primarily produced by activated microglia and infiltrating macrophages, acts as an NMDAR agonist and disrupts glutamate uptake, contributing to excitotoxicity and neuroinflammation. KATII is mainly expressed in astrocytes, and KYNA is predominantly synthesized by astrocytes [189, 190]. KYNA acts as an antagonist of NMDAR and α7 nicotinic acetylcholine receptors (α7nAChR), thereby inhibiting glutamate release and reducing excitotoxicity [191, 192]. KYNA may also modulate GABAergic signaling [193]. In contrast, activated resident microglia and infiltrating macrophages are the main sources of QA in the brain [194]. QA not only acts as an NMDAR agonist but also impairs glutamate uptake by astrocytes and promotes its release [195–197]. Therefore, the balance between KYNA and QA is important for maintaining normal excitatory and inhibitory activity, and its disruption may lead to increased excitability and excitotoxicity, which are key pathological mechanisms in epilepsy (Fig. 3).

Disrupted TRP-KYN metabolism has been observed in animal models and patients with epilepsy. KYNA levels are significantly reduced whereas QA levels are increased in the hippocampus of patients with TLE, accompanied by increased IDO activity and decreased KATII activity [192]. Similar findings have been reported in hippocampal and anterior temporal lobe tissues of TLE rats [198]. Marked reductions in KYNA levels and KYNA/KYN ratios have been reported in children with epileptic spasms [199]. Recently, a metabolomic study of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) from adult patients with SE identified TRP metabolism as the most significantly altered pathway. In contrast to previous findings, patients exhibited higher levels of KYN, KYNA, and QA [200]. This difference may stem from variations in sample types. Another study investigated the utility of CSF metabolites in the diagnosis of neuroinflammatory diseases. The results showed that KYN, QA, and KYN/TRP are valid diagnostic and monitoring biomarkers for both acute and chronic neuroinflammation. Moreover, KYNA/KYN levels were significantly decreased in the epilepsy group [201].

The dysfunction of TRP-KYN metabolism is closely related to immune responses and neuroinflammation [202, 203]. Increased proinflammatory mediators (such as IL-1β, TNF-α, IL-6, and LPS) stimulate the expression of IDO1 and other KP enzymes [204]. In rats with chronic TLE, IL-1β and IL-6 increased IDO1 expression, contributing to epilepsy-associated depressive-like behavior [205]. In an infantile spasm model, treatment with a specific IDO1 inhibitor increased KYNA levels and reduced seizure frequency [206]. IDO1 deficiency also attenuated astrocyte and microglial activation and suppressed neuroinflammatory responses after SE [207]. LPS and IFN-γ significantly induced QA synthesis from TRP in vitro, in human microglia and macrophages [208, 209]. Notably, in AD models, the inhibition of IDO1 restored glucose metabolism in the hippocampus, suggesting that KP regulates metabolic reprogramming, a process previously discussed in the context of epilepsy-associated neuroinflammation [210]. However, its specific role in epilepsy remains unclear. KP metabolites are also key regulators of the inflammatory responses [211]. KYNA regulates peripheral T cell activity, although its role in modulating infiltrating T cells within the CNS remains unclear [212]. Furthermore, QA stimulates astrocytes to secrete proinflammatory cytokines and chemokines, and KYN promotes chemotaxis and activation of peripheral monocytes and their interaction with astrocytes, thereby exacerbating neuroinflammation [213, 214].

Taken together, these findings suggest that proinflammatory cytokines can activate KP, leading to increased excitotoxicity and epilepsy progression. KP metabolites may further amplify neuroinflammation by modulating immune cell activation. However, despite growing evidence, the precise contribution of KP to epilepsy remains unclear and warrants further investigation.

Adenosine metabolism

Adenosine, an evolutionarily ancient metabolic regulator of energy homeostasis, plays a vital role in physiological regulation by acting through specific adenosine receptors (ARs; i.e., A1, A2A, A2B, and A3) [215, 216]. Activation of A1Rs can reduce glutamate release, induce neuronal hyperpolarization, and modulate GABAergic transmission, thereby suppressing neuronal excitability and exerting anticonvulsant effects [217–219]. In contrast, activation of A2ARs can inhibit glutamate reuptake by suppressing astrocytic glutamate transporters GLAST and GLT-1, enhance GABA uptake, and promote excitatory synaptic transmission, thereby facilitating seizure activity and impairing cognitive function [218, 220–223] (Fig. 4).

Adenosine Metabolism and its Role in Hyperexcitability and Neuroinflammation. Activation of A1Rs suppresses neuronal excitability by reducing glutamate release, inducing neuronal hyperpolarization, and modulating GABAergic transmission. In contrast, activation of A2ARs promotes excitatory neurotransmission by inhibiting astrocytic glutamate reuptake (via suppression of GLAST and GLT-1), enhancing GABA uptake, and facilitating glutamate release, thereby promoting seizure activity. Extracellular ATP released by neurons can sequentially be converted to AMP via CD39 and then to adenosine via CD73 on the astrocyte surface. CD73-derived adenosine preferentially activates A2ARs, promoting neuroinflammation, glutamatergic activity, and reducing GABAergic signaling. ADK regulates adenosine clearance by catalyzing its conversion to AMP. NLRP3 inflammasome increases the expression of ADK through the CREB/REST/Sp1 pathway. NLRP3 inflammasome activation enhances the maturation of pro-IL-1β to IL-1β. IL-1β may also activate REST and consequently increase ADK. In the adult brain, adenosine levels are primarily regulated by the astrocyte-based adenosine cycle [224, 225] (Fig. 4). Adenosine metabolism is disrupted in epilepsy; during seizures, neuronal tissue upregulates its ATP production capacity to meet increased energy demands and adenosine levels are increased, which exerts potent anti-seizure effects [226–228]. Conversely, extracellular adenosine levels are decreased in the hippocampal tissue of kindled rats [229].

Adenosine kinase (ADK) is a key enzyme in adenosine metabolic clearance, catalyzing the conversion of adenosine to 5’-adenosine monophosphate (AMP), which is crucial for the regulation of adenosine levels [230, 231] (Fig. 4). The overexpression of astrocyte-based ADK in the brain contributes to epileptogenesis and disease progression by enhancing neuronal excitability [219, 232–234]. Notably, increased ADK expression may be associated with neuroinflammation. IL-1β results in increased ADK in human cultured astrocytes [235]. Studies have revealed the alteration of IL-1β levels in epileptogenic states, seizures, and epilepsy [86, 236, 237]. IL-1β may regulate the expression levels of ADK in epilepsy. ADK expression is significantly increased in perivascular areas with inflammation in the brain tissues of RE patients with refractory epilepsy, indicating a crosstalk between the astrocyte-based adenosine cycle and neuroinflammation [238].

Inflammation may increase ADK expression via transcriptional regulation. The RE1-silencing transcription factor (REST) is a negative regulator of neuronal gene expression [239]. REST activity is positively modulated by cAMP response element-binding (CREB) protein [239]. REST inhibits the transcriptional activity of specificity protein 1 (Sp1), while Sp1 mediates Adk gene suppression [240, 241]. CREB and REST are overexpressed in patients with TLE [242–244]. NLRP3 inflammasome activation can modulate the CREB/REST/Sp1 signaling pathway, leading to increased ADK expression, and subsequently facilitating the progression of epilepsy [245]. Moreover, activated NLRP3 inflammatory corpuscles increase the release of IL-1β, which can also activate REST [246–248].

ADK is primarily associated with the regulation of global adenosine levels and its interaction with A1Rs. In contrast, ecto-5′-nucleotidase (CD73) catalyzes the extracellular formation of adenosine, preferentially activating A2ARs due to physical co-localization [249]. The activation of astrocytic A2ARs may promote the secretion of proinflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β, thereby exacerbating neuroinflammation [250].

CD73 and A2ARs are upregulated in the hippocampal astrocytes of human patients with mesial TLE [251]. Notably, CD73 colocalizes with A2ARs in the hippocampus of these patients, suggesting a functional interaction [251]. In the KA-treated mouse model, CD73/A2AR density is increased in both neurons and glial cells [252]. CD73-mediated activation of A2ARs contributes to alterations in hippocampal synaptic plasticity and cognitive function [252]. Interestingly, the magnitude of changes in ATP release and A2AR density is greater in neurons than in astrocytes; therefore, genetic deletion of CD73 primarily affects neurodegeneration rather than morpho-functional alterations in astrocytes, at least in the early stages after a convulsive period [252].

Growing evidence suggests that CD73/A2AR signaling plays a crucial role in the regulation of neuroinflammation. Although most current data are derived from models of other neurological disorders, these findings may offer important insights into epilepsy. For instance, in the PD model, upregulated CD73/A2AR signaling promotes the activation of microglia and increases proinflammatory cytokine production, whereas CD73 knockdown significantly attenuates these responses [253]. Moreover, endothelial CD73 is involved in BBB permeability; in experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis (EAE), CD73-mediated activation of A2ARs facilitates the transmigration of peripheral proinflammatory immune cells across the BBB, whereas in cerebral ischemia models, CD73-mediated A2BR activation appears to suppress immune cell infiltration [254, 255].

In summary, adenosine metabolism exerts dual effects on seizure activity and neuroinflammation through the distinct actions of the ADK/A1R and CD73/A2AR signaling axes. Astrocytic overexpression of ADK, which may be driven by inflammatory signals, reduces neuroprotective adenosine levels and enhances seizure susceptibility. In parallel, CD73-derived adenosine may play a critical role in seizure-associated neurodegeneration. A schematic of these processes is shown in Fig. 4. While increasing evidence supports their roles in epilepsy, especially in human and animal studies, further research is required to clarify therapeutic opportunities within the adenosine-inflammation regulatory network.

Lipid metabolism

Lipids constitute approximately 50% of the dry weight of the brain, making it the organ with the highest lipid content, aside from adipose tissue [256]. Lipids and their intermediates are essential for brain structure and function, serving as energy substrates, structural components of cell membranes, and bioactive molecules [257]. Hence, their dysregulation can cause CNS disorders, including neurodegenerative and psychiatric diseases [258].

An increasing number of studies indicate the role of disrupted lipid metabolism in the initiation and progression of epilepsy, which is evidently linked to neuroinflammation. Lipid droplets (LDs) are dynamic intracellular fatty acid (FA) storage organelles that not only control cellular metabolism but also protect cells by influencing the immune response [259, 260]. Recently, a pioneering study identified a new state of reactive astrocytes in the epileptic brain called lipid-accumulated reactive astrocytes (LARAs) [261]. Researchers confirmed abnormal lipid accumulation and disrupted lipid metabolism, characterized by elevated levels of neutral lipids such as triglycerides, and a marked increase in LD formation in astrocytes and neurons in both patients with TLE and the KA mouse model [261]. They also found that apolipoprotein E, which mediates FAs transfer from hyperactive neurons to astrocytes, was indispensable for LD formation in astrocytes of epileptic mice [261]. Further investigation revealed that LARAs upregulate A2AR expression, which in turn downregulates EAAT expression, reduces glutamate reuptake, and leads to epilepsy disease progression [261]. As discussed before, altered A2AR and EAAT expression is possibly related to neuroinflammation in epilepsy. Neuron-derived Lipid transport can elevate astrocytic hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase-like 2 (HSDL2) expression, which serves as a compensatory response to excessive lipid stimulation [262]. Upregulated HSDL2 diminished lipid accumulation and decreased A2AR expression through peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor α (PPARα) pathway activation [262]. The activation of PPARα can inhibit the activity of NF-κB, thereby downregulating proinflammatory gene expression such as IL-1β and IL-6 in the brain [263].

Furthermore, another study highlighted the essential role of astrocytic mitochondrial OxPhos in the degradation of FAs and the maintenance of lipid homeostasis in the brain [264]. Aberrant astrocytic OxPhos impaired lipid clearance, which leads to LD accumulation, reactive astrogliosis, enhanced glycolysis, and IL-3-mediated microglia activation, thereby triggering neuroinflammation and neurodegeneration [264]. Although this study primarily focused on AD, it also offers valuable insights into the potential contribution of astrocytic lipid metabolism to epilepsy-related neuroinflammation [265]. A previous study reported that LPS induced LD accumulation in microglia [266]. Nevertheless, no significant changes in LDs were observed in microglia in either the cortex of patients with TLE or the hippocampus of epilepsy mouse models [261].

Accumulation of free fatty acids (FFAs) in the brain exerts various harmful effects, such as neuroinflammation [267]. Several studies reported FA abnormalities in patients with DRE. In the plasma of Sudanese patients with DRE, saturated and monounsaturated FAs levels were significantly higher than those in healthy controls, whereas n-6 and n-3 polyunsaturated FAs (PUFAs) were lower [268]. Similarly, compared with responders to valproic acid monotherapy, non-responders had significantly decreased PUFAs and increased triglyceride levels [269]. However, the study did not include a control group of individuals without epilepsy. Different FAs have varying effects on the neuroinflammatory responses. Saturated FAs (e.g., palmitic acid) and n-6 PUFAs (e.g., arachidonic acid (ARA)) increase the proinflammatory phenotype of microglia, whereas n-3 PUFAs such as docosahexaenoic acid (DHA) increase the anti-inflammatory phenotype [270]. Additionally, astrocytes release DHA and ARA after LPS stimulation, probably to control acute inflammation [271]. PUFAs like DHA and docosapentaenoic acid (DPA) inhibit several inflammatory mediators such as IL-1β, IL‐6, and TNF‐α, which can raise seizure thresholds [272].

Studies have reported conflicting results regarding the changes in glycerophospholipids (GPs) in epilepsy. In the Kv1.1-KO mouse model of epilepsy, most glycerophospholipids, including phosphatidylcholine (PC) and phosphatidylethanolamine (PE), were upregulated [273]. Conversely, a study using mass spectrometry imaging found that the expression of various lipids, notably PC and PE, was reduced in the human TLE hippocampus [274]. Although these results appear to be contradictory, they display the complexity of GP metabolism in the progression of epilepsy. The involvement of PC in microglial inflammatory responses has been previously demonstrated [275, 276].

Overall, the role of lipid metabolism in the pathogenesis of epilepsy has attracted increasing attention. LDs may serve as key metabolic mediators linking neuroinflammation and epilepsy, particularly when they accumulate excessively in astrocytes. These LDs may contribute to epileptogenesis by modulating pathways such as the A2AR and NF-κB signaling. Moreover, different types of FFAs play distinct roles in epilepsy-associated neuroinflammation. Saturated FAs and n-6 PUFAs are generally proinflammatory, whereas n-3 PUFAs exhibit anti-inflammatory effects. Although studies on GPs in epilepsy remain limited, emerging evidence suggests that GPs may also contribute to disease progression via neuroimmune mechanisms.

Anti-neuroinflammatory effects of metabolic therapies in epilepsy

Accumulating evidence has highlighted the potential of metabolic therapies for epilepsy. In addition to modulating neuronal excitability, these approaches may target neuroinflammatory responses that occur in epilepsy [17, 18]. Understanding these anti-neuroinflammatory mechanisms will provide new insights into the therapeutic potential of metabolism-based strategies for epilepsy management. KD and exogenous ketone supplementation

KD is a high-fat, low-carbohydrate, and moderate-protein diet designed to shift the body’s primary fuel source from glucose to fat, thereby producing ketone bodies (β-hydroxybutyrate [BHB], acetoacetate, and acetone) [277]. KD has been considered a metabolic therapy for DRE for centuries and has demonstrated favorable efficacy and safety in clinical studies involving patients with DRE [278–281]. While the mechanisms by which KD treats epilepsy remain unclarified, growing evidence indicates that its antiepileptic effects may be linked to the regulation of neuroinflammation [282, 283].

KD attenuates neuroinflammation in epilepsy through several potential mechanisms. KD reduces inflammatory mediators, including IL-1β, TNF-α, IL-6, and COX-2 in various epilepsy models by inhibiting the NF-κB signaling pathway [284, 285]. The inhibition of NF-κB may be mediated by activation of PPARγ [285, 286]. Moreover, KD regulates microglial activation [284]. As previously discussed, activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome may contribute to the upregulation of ADK in epilepsy (Fig. 4). Notably, KD attenuates the NLRP3 inflammasome activation in both EAE models and healthy individuals [287, 288]. In addition, KD decreases ADK expression and increases ATP levels in the brain [289–291]. This, in turn, activates A1Rs, exerting anticonvulsant effects and potentially suppressing inflammatory responses [289, 292]. Moreover, excessive glutamate accumulation may contribute to neuroinflammation (Fig. 2). KD influences glutamate metabolism in multiple ways. A recent study revealed that BHB produced by KD inhibits the histone deacetylases HDAC1/HDAC2, which increases H3K27Ac levels and facilitates transcription of Sirt4 and Gad1 [293]. By upregulating SIRT4 and GAD1 and inactivating glutamate dehydrogenase (the enzyme that oxidizes glutamate to α-ketoglutarate), BHB increased GABA levels and the GABA/glutamate ratio, thus inhibiting epilepsy in the pentetrazol-induced acute seizure mouse model [293]. Additionally, proinflammatory palmitic acid was significantly decreased in the cortex of patients treated with the modified Atkins diet, a variation of the classic KD [294].

However, it is worth noting that KD can induce metabolic disorders, especially glucose and lipid metabolism, by influencing the gut microbiota [295]. A decreased abundance of Lactobacillus murinus, Bifidobacterium, and Eubacterium rectale was found in patients and mice treated with KD, leading to reduced production of anti-inflammatory short-chain FAs (SCFAs) such as butyrate and acetate [295, 296]. Moreover, KD inhibited TRP-KYN metabolism; specifically, it significantly reduced the KYNA/QA ratio, which may promote neuroinflammation and increase seizure activity [297].

Owing to the low adherence to KD and its potential side effects, exogenous ketone supplements (EKS) that release or serve as precursors to ketone bodies are being explored to treat epilepsy [298]. EKS increases adenosine levels and activates A1Rs, which inhibits both astrocytic proliferation and microglia activation [299]. It has been explored that BHB inhibits NLRP3 inflammasome-mediated IL-1β production in human monocytes and attenuates caspase-1 activation and IL-1β secretion in mice [300].

In summary, both KD and EKS exert antiepileptic effects not only by altering brain metabolism but also by modulating key inflammatory pathways.

Glycolysis inhibitors

Metabolic reprogramming from OxPhos to glycolysis plays a critical role in microglial activation and neuroinflammatory cascades, potentially driving the progression of epilepsy (Fig. 1). Given that glucose restriction is a key mechanism by which the KD exerts its therapeutic effects, glycolytic inhibitors are considered potential alternatives to strict dietary interventions [168]. 2-Deoxy-D-glucose (2-DG), a non-metabolizable glucose analog, disrupts glycolysis by competitively inhibiting the conversion of glucose to glucose-6-phosphate [301].

2-DG has demonstrated both acute anticonvulsant and long-term antiepileptic effects in various in vitro and in vivo models, which may be partly attributed to its anti-neuroinflammatory properties [302–304]. 2-DG suppresses LPS-induced activation of NF-κB signaling pathways by inhibiting NAD+/SIRT1-mediated p65 acetylation, modulating the AMPK/mTOR/IKKβ/p65 signaling pathway in microglial cells, and inhibiting CtBP dimerization [142, 154]. However, the role of NAD+/SIRT1-mediated p65 acetylation in epilepsy remains unclear. Another potential anti-neuroinflammatory mechanism of 2-DG in epilepsy is suppressing the inflammatory gene expression in astrocytes, such as IL-1β, TNF-α, and complement component 3 [305]. Furthermore, in KA-treated rats that received 2-DG treatment, infiltrated monocytes were replaced by big CD11b+ macrophages, and the progranulin level in the brain was increased [306]. Monocyte-derived macrophages can exert anti-inflammatory effects by increasing anti-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-10 and TGF-β [307, 308]. Additionally, progranulin, a growth factor, also antagonizes neuroinflammation [309].

Surprisingly, in the 6 Hz corneal kindling model, post-stimulation treatment with 2-DG attenuated epileptogenesis and seizure severity, but simultaneously increased microglial activation [310]. This difference may result from variations in activated microglial phenotypes, as microglia can polarize into the proinflammatory M1 or anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype [311]. The influence of 2-DG on the microglial phenotype in different stages of seizures and epilepsy remains unelucidated.

In conclusion, as a glycolysis inhibitor, 2-DG has demonstrated antiepileptic and anti-inflammatory potential in various models, possibly by modulating inflammatory responses in microglia, astrocytes, and infiltrating monocytes.

Adenosine-target therapies

In epilepsy, adenosine metabolism may act as a bridge linking neuroinflammation and hyperexcitability of neural networks, particularly through metabolic disruption mediated by ADK and CD73 (Fig. 4). Moreover, because adenosine is an endogenous anticonvulsant with potent anti-inflammatory properties, modulation of adenosine metabolism may have broad therapeutic potential in epilepsy [299, 312, 313]. In the early 21 st century, the potential of ADK inhibitors in treating epilepsy, chronic pain, and inflammation was recognized [314, 315]. The non-selective ADK inhibitor 5-iodotubercidin reduced epilepsy development in KA-treated mice [233, 234, 316]. Additionally, knockdown of hippocampal astrocytic ADK expression reduced neuroinflammatory responses and showed anti-seizure and neuroprotective effects in KA-induced epilepsy in rats [317]. Hyperibone J, a major component of Hypericum bellum flowers, exer anti-inflammatory effects [318]. A recent study revealed that Hyperibone J reduced LPS-induced overexpression of ADK in the depression model of mice, resulting in decreased ATP production and inhibition of the microglial ATP/P2X7 receptor (P2X7R) signaling pathway, which subsequently led to the inactivation of the caspase-1/IL-1β cascade [319]. P2X7R plays a crucial role in the development of epilepsy. Activation of P2X7R promotes the release of proinflammatory mediators (such as IL-1β), driving seizures and epilepsy development [320, 321]. In addition, P2X7R regulates neutrophil infiltration in the frontoparietal cortex of epileptic rats [322]. These findings suggest that suppression of ADK expression may have potential anti-seizure effects by inhibiting the ATP/P2X7R pathway.

Downregulating CD73 expression may be another therapeutic approach for treating neuroinflammation. Cytosine-based 5’-α, β-methylene diphosphonates (e.g., MRS4598), a novel CD73 inhibitor, inhibited membrane-bound CD73 and attenuated reactive astrocytes in vitro [323]. However, no studies have investigated the effects of CD73 inhibitors on neuroinflammation in epilepsy models.

Fatty acid supplementation

As discussed previously, lipid metabolism plays an important role in epilepsy-induced neuroinflammation. Specifically, some FAs, such as n-3 PUFAs, appear to exert anti-inflammatory effects in epilepsy.

One of the key anti-inflammatory mechanisms of n-3 PUFAs is the inhibition of the NLRP3 inflammasome. The NLRP3 inflammasome is composed of NLRP3, apoptotic speck-containing protein (ASC), and pro-caspase-1 [324, 325]. Supplementation with various forms of n-3 PUFAs alleviates increased NF-κB (p65), NLRP3, ASC, caspase-1, and IL-1β caused by pentetrazol to varying extents [326]. Nevertheless, different n-3 PUFAs may inhibit the activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome through distinct mechanisms. Eicosapentaenoic acid primarily reduces ASC levels and inhibits the binding between NLRP3 and ASC, whereas DHA preferentially lowers the protein levels of both ASC and caspase-1 [327].

In addition to suppressing NLRP3 inflammasome activation, n-3 PUFAs modulate microglial polarization by reducing M1 polarization and promoting M2 polarization [327]. The anti-inflammatory effects of n-3 PUFA supplementation in epilepsy may be mediated by the pro-resolving lipid mediators derived from n-3 PUFAs. Intracerebroventricular injection of n-3 DPA-derived protectin D1 during epileptogenesis in KA-injected mice led to a dose-dependent reduction in the hippocampal expression levels of Il1b and Tnfa mRNAs [328].

Conclusions

Metabolic dysfunction and neuroinflammation are closely interconnected and together promote disease progression and contribute to pharmacoresistance in epilepsy. Dysregulated metabolic pathways, such as glycolysis, glutamate/GABA-glutamine cycle, KP, adenosine cycle, and lipid metabolism, initiate and amplify inflammatory responses. Conversely, neuroinflammation exacerbates metabolic dysregulation by impairing glial cell function, promoting immune cell infiltration, and stimulating the release of proinflammatory mediators (Fig. 5).

The Bidirectional Relationship between Neuroinflammation and Metabolic Dysfunction in Epilepsy. Neuroinflammation, mediated by activated astrocytes, microglia, and infiltrated peripheral immune cells, leads to the release of inflammatory mediators, ultimately resulting in neuronal hyperexcitability. Dysregulated metabolic pathways, including glucose metabolism, lipid metabolism, the glutamate-GABA cycle, adenosine metabolism, and TRP-KYN pathway, trigger neuroinflammation. In return, neuroinflammation reinforces metabolic dysfunction, forming a vicious cycle that contributes to epilepsy progression. Developing new therapeutic strategies is essential for treating patients with epilepsy, particularly those with DRE. Preclinical and clinical evidence suggests that metabolic therapies may exert antiepileptic effects by mitigating neuroinflammation (Table 1). Specifically, KD, EKS, glycolysis inhibitors, FA supplementation, and ADK inhibitors can potentially regulate neuroinflammation and attenuate seizure activity. Although the mechanisms underlying these treatments remain under investigation, they offer new perspectives and hope for the treatment of epilepsy. Targeting the dysregulated metabolic pathways and neuroinflammation may be a promising option for managing DRE.

Despite these encouraging advances, the translation of neuroimmune-metabolic therapies into clinical practice poses substantial challenges. One major obstacle is the heterogeneity across different cell types and metabolic pathways [329]. For instance, downregulation of GLUT1 in endothelial cells may result in exacerbated neuroinflammation, whereas suppressing GLUT1 expression in microglia may produce anti-inflammatory and anticonvulsant effects [120, 129]. Furthermore, current research on epilepsy has predominantly focused on glial cells and glucose metabolism, with limited attention to peripheral immune cells or other metabolic pathways such as lipid and amino acid metabolism. Moreover, although the bidirectional relationship between neuroinflammation and metabolic dysfunction is increasingly recognized in other neurological disorders, epilepsy-specific mechanisms remain poorly understood and require further investigation to support clinical translation [330]. In addition, given that the mechanisms of metabolic therapies are different from those of conventional ASMs, the design of clinical trials may be challenging.

Future studies should pay particular attention to how this metabolic-inflammatory interplay varies across various epilepsy subtypes and explore the roles of diverse metabolic pathways within different immune cell populations. In addition, it is essential to comprehensively investigate the mechanisms by which metabolic therapies modulate neuroinflammation and to rigorously evaluate their safety and efficacy in patients with epilepsy. Additionally, evidence from other diseases suggests that combining anti-inflammatory and metabolic therapies or integrating multiple metabolic interventions may improve efficacy and reduce adverse effects [331, 332]. Exploring such strategies for treating epilepsy could be a valuable direction for future studies. A more comprehensive understanding of these context-specific mechanisms will be essential for developing targeted therapeutic strategies. Such research could pave the way for more effective, personalized treatments for epilepsy.
