Introduction

The homogeneous number of an
n
-vertex graph
G
is defined as

\displaystyle\hom(G):=\max_{U\subset V(G)}\big\{|U|:G[U]\mbox{ is a complete or empty graph}\big\}.

This parameter is of central interest in Graph Theory and, in particular, in Ramsey Theory. One version of Ramsey’s theorem
[
35
]
, which is a cornerstone result in Combinatorics, states that
\hom(G)\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
. A more quantitative bound, due to Erdős and Szekeres
[
17
]
, gives that
\hom(G)\geq\log_{2}n/2
. Random graphs show that this logarithmic growth is best possible, as proven by Erdős
[
16
]
in one of the earliest applications of the probabilistic method
[
4
]
. However, no explicit constructions of
n
-vertex graphs with
\hom(G)=O(\log n)
, also called
Ramsey graphs
, are known, despite carrying a
\$100
Erdős prize. There are several compelling results proving that graphs produced via certain natural constructive procedures cannot attain this level of behaviour, along with outstanding conjectures asserting even broader restrictions.

A guiding belief in Ramsey theory is that large graphs with small homogeneous number must resemble the behaviour of random graphs and, as such, on might view the homogeneous number of a graph as a measure of quasirandomness. However, while several other similar measures are known to be equivalent (see for example
[
39
]
,
[
10
]
,
[
38
]
), this measurement is generally far weaker. Over time, Erdős and his coauthors made several influential conjectures
[
11
]
,asserting that
n
-vertex Ramsey graphs resemble the typical behaviour of the random graph
G(n,1/2)
. The vast majority of these questions have been affirmatively answered: Erdős and Szemerédi
[
18

proved that Ramsey graphs have density bounded away from

and

[
37
]
showed they must have exponentially many distinct induced subgraphs, Rödl and Prömel

[

The focus of this paper is on the relationship between the homogeneous number of a graph

\displaystyle f(G):=\max\big\{k\in{\mathbb{N}}:G[U]\mbox{ has }k\mbox{ vertices with distinct degrees, for some }U\subset V(G)\big\}.

Understanding the relationship between

In their paper, Bukh and Sudakov raised the broader question of understanding

Proof overview.
Before closing the introduction, we briefly discuss the proof of Theorem
1.2
.In
[
28
]
we introduced a general approach to establishing lower bounds on
f(G)
for a graph
G
, which we used to prove Theorem
1.1
.
Roughly speaking, instead of finding sets
U\subset W\subset V(G)
such that
U
has many distinct degrees in
G[W]
, it suffices instead to find such a set
U
and a probability distribution
{\cal D}
on
[0.1,0.9]^{V(G)}
such that certain small-ball like quantities associated with
U
and
{\cal D}
are well-controlled (see Section
3
for more details). This resulted in a more technical task, but with the benefit that it allowed us to blend behaviour from different neighbourhoods of the graph together to find distinct degrees, and that it was more robust to an inductive approach, which seems forced upon us in this type of problem.

Our proof of Theorem
1.2
follows the same approach, although several substantial new ideas are required in the current regime. To begin, observe that if we could find sets
U
and
S
with
|U|
large, which have diverse neighbourhoods and such that the pressure from
U
to
S
is small, then one could use Theorem
1.5
to prove the theorem, as illustrated in the remark for
G(n,p)
above. We thus restrict our attention to graphs
G
in which one of the conditions (i) or (ii) from Theorem
1.5
fails for such sets.

Should (i) fail then there are vertices in
G
whose neighbourhoods cluster or significantly correlate. We investigate this correlation using the notion of ‘cluster neighbourhood’, which allows us to separate vertices which are strongly correlated from others which have a smaller correlation. Under natural assumptions, we obtain a partial decomposition of a significant proportion of the graph using cluster neighbourhoods (see Lemma
4.7
). We might now hope to find distinct degrees within each of these clusters by induction (as in
[
28
]
), and then combine them together to obtain
U
using the weak correlation between the clusters in place of (i) in Theorem
1.5
.

Although this sounds plausible, it results in a new difficulty. Indeed, observe that this partition into clusters naturally complicates achieving (ii) from Theorem
1.5
. Indeed, the ‘pressure parameter’
\gamma
is necessarily forced to be much larger if
U
has many vertices of a similar structure. To bypass this new restriction, we instead build our probability distributions
{\cal D}
based on the coarse structure of the cluster neighbourhoods, rather through vertex neighbourhoods which were used previously. This enables us to work with a measure of pressure based onthe number of
clusters
, rather than the sought number distinct degrees (as in Theorem
1.5
). We make this connection by
exploiting the metric structure of the neighbourhoods (Lemma
3.5
),which will in turn be controlled through our definition of clusters.

Lastly, with these tools in hand, we prove Theorem
1.2
via a delicate inductive argument. In particular, we were only able to guarantee a low enough level of pressure for the cluster partition in certain circumstances (see Case 3 of the proof), but luckily a trivial level of pressure (
\gamma=1
) turned out to be sufficient outside of this (see Case 2 of the proof).

, which is given by

\displaystyle({\bf u})_{v}=\begin{cases}1\quad\mbox{ if }uv\in E(G);\\
0\quad\mbox{ otherwise}.\end{cases}

Note that, considering the standard inner product on
{\mathbb{R}}^{V(G)}
, given by
{\bf x}\cdot{\bf y}=\sum_{v\in V(G)}x_{v}y_{v}
, this notation leads us to the following useful representation:

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\big[d_{G(\textbf{\lx@text@underline{p}})}(u)\big]=\bf u\cdot\textbf{\lx@text@underline{p}}.

(2)

Expected degrees in suitable
G(\textbf{\lx@text@underline{p}})
’s prove to be useful in finding bounds for
f(G)
, however this notion is still too rigid for our purposes. The parameter we are about to define turns out to be robust enough.

Let
G
be a graph and let
{\cal D}
be a probability distribution on
[0.1,0.9]^{V(G)}
. Given distinct vertices
u,v\in V(G)
and a set
S\subset V(G)
, we define

\displaystyle\operatorname{bad}_{\mathcal{D}}^{S}({u,v}):=\max_{c\in\mathbb{R}}\ \underset{\textbf{\lx@text@underline{p}}\sim\mathcal{D}}{\mathbb{P}}\big(|\mathbb{E}[d_{G(\textbf{\lx@text@underline{p}})}^{S}(u)]-\mathbb{E}[d_{G(\textbf{\lx@text@underline{p}})}^{S}(v)]-c|\leq 1\big).

(3)

This quantity can be viewed as a small ball probability
-
a measurement for two vertices
u,v\in V(G)
of how likely the expected degrees to
S
in
G(\textbf{\lx@text@underline{p}})
are to differ by an (almost) fixed amount. Given sets
U,S\subset V(G)
, we also set

\operatorname{bad}_{\mathcal{\cal D}}^{S}({U}):=\displaystyle\sum_{\{u,v\}\subset U}\operatorname{bad}_{\mathcal{\cal D}}^{S}({u,v}).

Given another set
V\subset V(G)
we can also write

\operatorname{bad}_{\mathcal{\cal D}}^{S}({U,V}):=\displaystyle\sum_{(u,v)\in U\times V}\operatorname{bad}_{\mathcal{\cal D}}^{S}({u,v}).

We will sometimes suppress the superscript when
S=V(G)
, e.g.
\operatorname{bad}_{\mathcal{D}}({U})=\operatorname{bad}_{\mathcal{\cal D}}^{V(G)}({U})
. Lastly, let us remark that in (
3
) we do not need
\mathcal{D}
to be defined on all vertex coordinates of the set
[0.1,0.9]^{V(G)}
; any vertex set
T
with
S\subseteq T\subseteq V(G)
is enough so that we can define
\mathcal{D}
on
[0.1,0.9]^{T}
, as we can see by looking at the RHS of (
3
).

As highlighted in
[
28
]
, in order to find many distinct degrees in a graph
G
it suffices to find a large set
U\subset V(G)
and a probability distribution
{\cal D}
such that
\operatorname{bad}_{\mathcal{D}}({U})
is small.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
G
be a graph, let
{\cal D}
be a probability distribution on
[0.1,0.9]^{V(G)}

and let
U\subset V(G)
with
\operatorname{bad}_{\mathcal{D}}({U})=\alpha\cdot|U|
. Then

f(G)=\Omega\left(\frac{|U|}{(1+\alpha)\cdot\log^{3/2}|U|}\right).

Thus producing a set
U
and a distribution
{\cal D}
with strong ‘bad’ control results in a lower bound for
f(G)
. We also proved in
[
28
]
that one can reverse this process, to obtain a distribution with strong bad control from a lower bound on
f(G)
giving that the two approaches are comparable, although we will not need this reverse direction here.

For the remainder of this section we will collect a number of results together, which will be used in combination to exhibit distributions
{\cal D}
with bad control. As indicated earlier, the key benefit of working in this generalised setting is the flexibility of combining distributions on different sets while maintaining bad control. This is illustrated by the following result.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
G
be a graph with vertex partition
V(G)=\bigsqcup_{i=1}^{L}V_{i}
and for each
i\in[L]
let
\mathcal{D}_{i}
be a probability distribution on
[0,1]^{V_{i}}
. Then taking
\mathcal{D}
to denote the product distribution
\Pi_{i\in[L]}\mathcal{D}_{i}
on
[0,1]^{V(G)}
, for any distinct vertices
u,v\in V(G)
and any set
S\subset V(G)
, one has:

\operatorname{bad}_{\mathcal{D}}^{S}({u,v})\leq\min_{i\in[L]}\operatorname{bad}_{\mathcal{D}_{i}}^{S\cap V_{i}}({u,v}).

Proof.

See Lemma 4.1 in
[
28
]
.
∎

Our second lemma gives a simple situation in which we can obtain ‘bad’ control. Let
G
be a graph and let
S\subset V(G)
. Let
{\cal U}_{S}
denote the
uniformly constant distribution
on
[0.1,0.9]^{S}
, given by selecting
\alpha\in[0.1,0.9]
uniformly at random and setting
\textbf{\lx@text@underline{p}}=\alpha{\bf 1}_{S}\in[0.1,0.9]^{S}
.

It is also convenient to write
{\cal T}_{S}
to denote the
trivial
S
-induced probability distribution
, the distribution on
[0.1,0.9]^{S}
which simply selects the vector
\textbf{\lx@text@underline{p}}_{0}=\frac{1}{2}\cdot{\bf 1}_{S}
with probability
1
.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
G
be a graph,

for some

and that

. Then

Proof.

See Lemma 4.2 from
[

]
.

We next seek to provide ‘bad’ control for a set by blending neighbourhood structures together
-
the idea here has some similarities to that of
[
22
]
. Let
G
be a graph, let
U,S\subset V(G)
, where
U:=\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{k}\}
, and let
\beta\in[0,0.4]
. We now let
{\cal B}_{\beta}(U,S)
denote the
blended probability distribution
on
[0.1,0.9]^{S}
, which is defined as follows. First independently select
\alpha_{i}\in[-\beta,\beta]
uniformly at random for each
i\in[k]
and set:

\displaystyle\textbf{\lx@text@underline{p}}^{\prime}:=\frac{1}{2}\cdot{\bf 1}+\sum_{i\in[k]}\alpha_{i}\cdot\operatorname{proj}_{S}({\bf{u}}_{i})\in\mathbb{R}^{S}.

(4)

Having made these choices, the distribution then returns
p
, a truncated version of
\textbf{\lx@text@underline{p}}^{\prime}
, where

\displaystyle\textbf{\lx@text@underline{p}}_{v}=\begin{cases}\textbf{\lx@text@underline{p}}_{v}^{\prime}\quad\mbox{if }\textbf{\lx@text@underline{p}}_{v}^{\prime}\in[0.1,0.9];\\
0.9\quad\mbox{if }\textbf{\lx@text@underline{p}}_{v}^{\prime}>0.9;\\
0.1\quad\mbox{if }\textbf{\lx@text@underline{p}}_{v}^{\prime}<0.1.\end{cases}

Our next lemma gives a useful extension of Lemma 4.3 from
[
28
]
which will allow us to obtain strong control on
\mbox{bad}_{\cal D}(v_{i},v_{j})
. In particular, it is designed to be used in conjunction with the partial decomposition from cluster neighbourhoods given in Section
4
(see Lemma
4.7
).

Definition 3.4
.

Given a graph
G
, vertex sets
U,S\subset V(G)
and a parameter
\gamma\in[0,1]
, we say
U
is
\gamma
-
balanced
to
S
if for all
v\in S
we have
d_{G}^{U}(v)\leq\gamma|U|
.

Observe that
U
is always
1
-
balanced to

S
.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
G
be a graph,
\beta\in(0,0.1),\ \gamma\in(0,1],\ D\geq 1
and let
U:=\{u_{1},u_{2},\ldots,u_{t}\}
and
S
be subsets of
V(G)
such that
U
is
\gamma
-balanced to
S
. Suppose there are
d_{1},d_{2},\ldots,d_{t}>0
and pairwise disjoint sets
V_{1},V_{2},\ldots,V_{t}\subset V(G)
with
u_{i}\in V_{i}
for all
i\in[t]
such that:

•

|\text{\emph{div}}^{S}_{G}(u_{i},v_{i})|\leq d_{i}
for each
v_{i}\in V_{i}
and
i\in[t]
;

•

|\text{\emph{div}}^{S}_{G}(u_{i},u_{j})|\geq D+d_{i}+d_{j}
for all distinct
i,j\in[t]
.

Suppose further that
{\cal D}
denote the product distribution
{\cal D}={\cal B}_{\beta}(U,S)\times{\cal D}^{\prime}
on
[0.1,0.9]^{V(G)}
where
{\cal B}_{\beta}(U,S)
denotes the blended probability distribution on
[0.1,0.9]^{S}
and
{\cal D}^{\prime}
is any distribution on
[0.1,0.9]^{V(G)\setminus S}
. Then for all
v_{i}\in V_{i}
and
v_{j}\in V_{j}
with
i\neq j
in
[t]
one has

\operatorname{bad}_{\mathcal{D}}({v_{i},v_{j}})\leq\dfrac{2}{\beta D}+2\cdot\max\big\{d^{S}_{G}(u_{i}),d^{S}_{G}(u_{j})\big\}\cdot\exp\left(\dfrac{-0.045}{\gamma\beta^{2}|U|}\right).

(5)

Proof.

For each
i\in[t]
, given the vector
\textbf{\lx@text@underline{p}}^{\prime}
on
\mathbb{R}^{S}
from (
4
), define the random vector
\textbf{\lx@text@underline{q}}^{i}
on
\mathbb{R}^{S}
by
\textbf{\lx@text@underline{q}}^{i}:=\textbf{\lx@text@underline{p}}^{\prime}-\alpha_{i}\cdot\operatorname{proj}_{S}({\bf{u}}_{i})
. A key observation is that
\textbf{\lx@text@underline{q}}^{i}
is independent of
\alpha_{i}
. We will slightly abuse notation by writing
p
for both a vector in
[0.1,0.9]^{V(G)}
and its projection
\operatorname{proj}_{S}({\bf{\underline{p}}})
onto the coordinate set
S
. Since
\mathcal{D}
is the product distribution
{\cal B}_{\beta}(R,S)\times{\cal D}^{\prime}
, we can do this without much concern due to Lemma
3.2
.

Fix
i,j\in[t]
with
i\neq j
and
v_{i}\in V_{i},\ v_{j}\in V_{j}
. Given
c\in\mathbb{R}
, let
E^{\ i,j}_{v_{i},v_{j}}(c)
denote the event that
\big|\mathbb{E}[d_{G(\textbf{\lx@text@underline{p}})}(v_{i})]-\mathbb{E}[d_{G(\textbf{\lx@text@underline{p}})}(v_{j})]-c\big|\leq 1
. According to (
3
), it will suffice to show that:

\mathbb{P}\big(E^{\ i,j}_{v_{i},v_{j}}(c)\big)\leq\dfrac{2}{\beta D}+2\max\{d^{S}_{G}(u_{i}),d^{S}_{G}(u_{j})\}\cdot\exp\left(\dfrac{-0.045}{\gamma\beta^{2}|U|}\right).

Let us assume that
d_{G}^{S}(v_{i})\geq d_{G}^{S}(v_{j})
. Call a vertex
v\in N_{G}^{S}(u_{i})

naughty
if
\textbf{\lx@text@underline{q}}_{v}^{i}\notin[0.2,0.8]
. We call the vertex
u_{i}

problematic
if
N_{G}^{S}(u_{i})
contains a naughty vertex and we denote this event by
F_{i}
.By the law of total probability we get that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big(E^{\ i,j}_{v_{i},v_{j}}(c)\big)=\mathbb{P}\big(E^{\ i,j}_{v_{i},v_{j}}(c)|F_{i}\big)\cdot\mathbb{P}(F_{i})+\mathbb{P}\big(E^{\ i,j}_{v_{i},v_{j}}(c)|\overline{F_{i}}\big)\cdot\mathbb{P}(\overline{F_{i}})\leq\mathbb{P}(F_{i})+\mathbb{P}\big(E^{\ i,j}_{v_{i},v_{j}}(c)|\overline{F_{i}}\big).

(6)

To upper bound
\mathbb{P}(F_{i})
, let
v\in S
and note that
\textbf{\lx@text@underline{q}}_{v}^{i}
is a sum of
d_{G}^{U\setminus\{u_{i}\}}(v)
uniform independent random variables, as the
v
-coordinate of
\textbf{u}_{i}
is non-zero when
v\sim u_{i}
. Therefore, we have

\mathbb{P}\big(\textbf{\lx@text@underline{q}}_{v}^{i}\notin[0.2,0.8]\big)=\mathbb{P}\big(|\textbf{\lx@text@underline{q}}_{v}^{i}-1/2|>0.3\big)\leq 2\exp\left(\dfrac{-2\cdot 0.09}{4\beta^{2}d_{G}^{U\setminus\{u_{i}\}}(v)}\right)\leq 2\exp\left(\dfrac{-2\cdot 0.09}{4\beta^{2}\gamma|U|}\right),

where the first inequality follows from Hoeffding’s inequality, whereas the second one uses that
d_{G}^{U\setminus\{u_{i}\}}(v)\leq d_{G}^{U}(v)\leq\gamma|U|
as
U
is

\gamma
-balanced to
S
. By the union bound we then get

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(F_{i})\leq d_{G}^{S}(u_{i})\cdot\mathbb{P}\big(\textbf{\lx@text@underline{q}}_{v}^{i}\notin[0.2,0.8]\big)\leq 2d_{G}^{S}(u_{i})\cdot\exp\big(-0.045(\gamma\beta^{2}|U|)^{-1}\big).

(7)

To compute
\mathbb{P}\big(E_{v_{i},v_{j}}^{\ i,j}(c)|\overline{F_{i}}\big)
we condition on any choice of
\bm{\alpha}:=(\alpha_{l})_{l\neq i}
such that
F_{i}
does not hold. Given such a choice, let us first note that
\textbf{\lx@text@underline{p}}_{v}^{\prime}=\textbf{\lx@text@underline{q}}^{i}_{v}+\alpha_{i}\textbf{u}_{i_{v}}\in[0.1,0.9]
for all
v\in N_{G}^{S}(u_{i})
since
|\alpha_{i}|<0.1
. So none of the
N_{G}^{S}(u_{i})
-coordinates of
\textbf{\lx@text@underline{p}}^{\prime}
will get truncated and recall that
\alpha_{i}
is independent of
F_{i}
. Given a choice of
\bm{\alpha}
, consider now the following expression as a map of
\alpha_{i}
:

\displaystyle f_{c}(\alpha_{i}):=\mathbb{E}[d^{S}_{G(\textbf{\lx@text@underline{p}})}(v_{i})]-\mathbb{E}[d^{S}_{G(\textbf{\lx@text@underline{p}})}(v_{j})]-c=(\textbf{v}_{i}-\textbf{v}_{j})\cdot\textbf{\lx@text@underline{p}}-c.

(8)

Having conditioned on
\bm{\alpha}
above, note that the event
E_{v_{i},v_{j}}^{\ i,j}(c)
holds only if
f_{c}(\alpha_{i})
lies in an interval of length
2
. To bound the probability of this happening we have to understand how
f_{c}
changes as
\alpha_{i}
increases. Observe the contribution from each coordinate
v
of
p
to the inner product on the right hand side of (
8
) is
0
if
v\notin N^{S}_{G}(u_{i})
and exactly
\alpha_{i}
otherwise, since none of these coordinates were truncated from
\textbf{\lx@text@underline{p}}^{\prime}
when conditioning on
\overline{F_{i}}
. It follows that for
\varepsilon>0
:

f_{c}(\alpha_{i}+\varepsilon)-f_{c}(\alpha_{i})=\varepsilon\displaystyle\sum_{v\in S}\big(({\bf v}_{i})_{v}-({\bf v}_{j})_{v}\big)\cdot\mathbbm{1}_{v\sim u_{i}}=\varepsilon\big(|N_{G}^{S}(u_{i})\cap N_{G}^{S}(v_{i})|-|N_{G}^{S}(u_{i})\cap N_{G}^{S}(v_{j})|\big).

However, it is not hard to see that

\displaystyle 2|N_{G}^{S}(u_{i})\cap N_{G}^{S}(v_{i})|

\displaystyle=d_{G}^{S}(u_{i})+d_{G}^{S}(v_{i})-\text{div}_{G}^{S}(u_{i},v_{i}),

\displaystyle 2|N_{G}^{S}(u_{i})\cap N_{G}^{S}(v_{j})|

\displaystyle=d_{G}^{S}(u_{i})+d_{G}^{S}(v_{j})-\text{div}_{G}^{S}(u_{i},v_{j}).

It follows that

\displaystyle 2|N_{G}^{S}(u_{i})\cap N_{G}^{S}(v_{i})|-2|N_{G}^{S}(u_{i})\cap N_{G}^{S}(v_{j})|

\displaystyle=d_{G}^{S}(v_{i})-d_{G}^{S}(v_{j})-\diver^{S}_{G}(u_{i},v_{i})+\diver^{S}_{G}(u_{i},v_{j})

\displaystyle\geq\diver^{S}_{G}(u_{i},v_{j})-\diver^{S}_{G}(u_{i},v_{i})

\displaystyle\geq\diver^{S}_{G}(u_{i},u_{j})-\diver^{S}_{G}(u_{j},v_{j})-\diver^{S}_{G}(u_{i},v_{i})\geq D,

by using the hypothesis, that
d^{S}_{G}(v_{i})\geq d^{S}_{G}(v_{j})
and triangle’s inequality. From here we can then deduce that
f(\alpha_{i}+\varepsilon)-f(\alpha_{i})\geq\varepsilon D/2
.

Therefore, conditioned on
\bm{\alpha}
as above, if
E^{\ i,j}_{v_{i},v_{j}}(c)
occurs then
\alpha_{i}
lies in an interval of length at most
4/D
. This implies that
{\mathbb{P}}(E^{i,j}_{v_{i},v_{j}}(c)|{\overline{F}}_{i})\leq 2\beta^{-1}D^{-1}
. Combined with (
6
) and (
7
), this proves the lemma.
∎

E_{i}=0
and
W_{i}=\{u_{i}\}

in the previous lemma we recover the following result, which is similar to Lemma 4.3 from

.

and

such that

-balanced to

(9)

As a quick application of the previous corollary, we obtain a proof of Theorem

.

Proof of Theorem
1.5
.

To begin, we note some convenient assumptions between the parameters. Observe first that we may assume that
D\leq|U|^{3/2}
. Indeed the conclusion for larger values just asserts that
f(G)\geq\widetilde{\Omega}(|U|)
, which follows from the case
D=|U|^{3/2}
. We can also assume that
|S|\leq D|U|^{2}
since for larger values of

it is easily seen that if properties (i) and (ii) from the Theorem hold, then they also hold for a subset

with this bound. Thus, working with

Set

and apply Corollary

. This gives

\displaystyle\operatorname{bad}_{\mathcal{D}}({u,v})\leq\frac{20\sqrt{\gamma|U|\log|U|}}{D}+|S|\cdot 2\exp(-4.5\log|U|)\leq\frac{40\sqrt{\gamma|U|\log|U|}}{D},

where the final inequality uses that
\gamma|U|\geq 1
and that

. Summing over all pairs of vertices in

, this gives
\operatorname{bad}_{\mathcal{D}}({U})\leq\alpha|U|
where

. An application of Theorem

then completes the proof.

The last lemma of this section provides a hypothesis to maintain ‘bad’ control when combining multiple disjoint sets with ‘bad’ control under different well-behaved distributions.

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
1\leq m_{0}\leq M\leq 2M\leq M_{0}
and let
f:[m_{0},M_{0}]\to[0,\infty)
be a differentiable map such that
f
is increasing, while
f^{\prime}
is decreasing on
[m_{0},M_{0}]
. Suppose
G
is a graph which contains disjoint vertex sets
V_{1},V_{2},\ldots,V_{t},S
satisfying the following properties:

(i)

There are sets
U_{i}\subset V_{i}
such that
\sum_{i\in[t]}|U_{i}|\geq M
with
\max_{i\in[t]}|U_{i}|\geq m_{0}
;

(ii)

For each
i\in[t]
there is a distribution
{\cal D}_{i}
on
[0.1,0.9]^{V_{i}}
such that
\operatorname{bad}_{\mathcal{{\cal D}}_{i}}^{V_{i}}({U_{i}})\leq|U_{i}|f(|U_{i}|)
;

(iii)

There is a distribution
{\cal E}
on
[0.1,0.9]^{S}
such that
\operatorname{bad}_{\mathcal{\cal E}}^{S}({u_{i},u_{j}})\leq f^{\prime}(M_{0})
for all
u_{i}\in U_{i}
and
u_{j}\in U_{j}
with
i\neq j
.

Then there is a set
U\subset V(G)
with
|U|\geq M
and a distribution
{\cal D}
on
[0.1,0.9]^{V(G)}
such that

\operatorname{bad}_{\mathcal{D}}({U})\leq|U|f(|U|).

Proof.

We first note that if
a\in[m_{0},M]
and
0\leq b\leq a
then
m_{0}\leq a+b\leq 2M\leq M_{0}
and

\displaystyle a\cdot f(a)+b\cdot f(b)+ab\cdot f^{\prime}(M_{0})\leq(a+b)\cdot f(a+b).

(10)

Indeed, by the mean value theorem there is
\xi\in[a,a+b]\subset[m_{0},M_{0}]
with the property that
f(a+b)=f(a)+bf^{\prime}(\xi)
. As
f^{\prime}
is decreasing on
[m_{0},M_{0}]
, we have
f^{\prime}(M_{0})\leq f^{\prime}(\xi)
and so

\displaystyle af(a)+bf(b)+ab\cdot f^{\prime}(M_{0})

\displaystyle=a\big(f(a)+b\cdot f^{\prime}(M_{0})\big)+bf(b)

\displaystyle\leq a\big(f(a)+b\cdot f^{\prime}(\xi)\big)+bf(b)

\displaystyle\leq af(a+b)+bf(b)\leq(a+b)\cdot f(a+b),

where the final inequality uses that
f
is increasing and
b\leq a+b
.

We are now able to prove the lemma. Order the sets so that
|U_{1}|\geq|U_{2}|\geq\ldots\geq|U_{t}|
. Note that if
|U_{1}|>M
then the conclusion is trivial taking
U=U_{1}
and
{\cal D}={\cal D}_{1}\times{\cal T}
, where
{\cal T}
is the trivial distribution on
[0.1,0.9]^{V(G)\setminus U_{1}}
. Thus we may assume that
|U_{1}|\leq M
. Setting
U_{<j}:=\bigcup_{i<j}U_{i}
for all
j\in[2,t+1]
, by discarding sets
\{U_{i}\}_{i>t^{\prime}}
for some
t^{\prime}\leq t
we can additionally assume that
|U_{<j}|<M
for all
j\leq t
and that
U:=U_{<t+1}
satisfies
M\leq|U|\leq 2M<M_{0}
. Observing that
m_{0}\leq|U_{1}|\leq|U_{<j}|\leq M
for
j\in[2,t]
, we can apply (
10
) to obtain

\displaystyle|U_{<j}|\cdot f(|U_{<j}|)+|U_{j}|\cdot f(|U_{j}|)+|U_{<j}||U_{j}|\cdot f^{\prime}(M_{0})\leq|U_{<j+1}|\cdot f(|U_{<j+1}|).

(11)

Let
X:=V(G)\setminus\big(S\cup\bigcup_{i=1}^{t}V_{i}\big)
and take
{\cal D}
to be the product distribution
{\cal D}:=\prod_{i=1}^{t}{\cal D}_{i}\times{\cal E}\times{\cal T}
on
\prod_{i=1}^{t}[0.1,0.9]^{V_{i}}\times[0.1,0.9]^{S}\times[0.1,0.9]^{X}=[0.1,0.9]^{V(G)}
, where
{\cal T}
is the trivial
X
-induced distribution. By Lemma
3.2
and (i)-(iii) from the hypothesis we obtain

\displaystyle\operatorname{bad}_{\mathcal{D}}({U})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in[t]}\operatorname{bad}_{\mathcal{D}}({U_{i}})+\sum_{\{i,j\}\subset[t]}\operatorname{bad}_{\mathcal{D}}({U_{i},U_{j}})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i\in[t]}\operatorname{bad}_{\mathcal{\cal D}_{i}}^{V_{i}}({U_{i}})+\sum_{\{i,j\}\subset[t]}\operatorname{bad}_{\mathcal{\cal E}}^{S}({U_{i},U_{j}})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i\in[t]}\operatorname{bad}_{\mathcal{\cal D}_{i}}^{V_{i}}({U_{i}})+\sum_{\{i,j\}\subset[t]}|U_{i}|\cdot|U_{j}|\cdot\max_{(v_{i},v_{j})\in U_{i}\times U_{j}}\left\{\operatorname{bad}_{\mathcal{\cal B}}^{S}({v_{i},v_{j}})\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i\in[t]}|U_{i}|\cdot f(|U_{i}|)+\sum_{\{i,j\}\subset[t]}|U_{i}|\cdot|U_{j}|\cdot f^{\prime}(M_{0})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in[t]}|U_{i}|\cdot f(|U_{i}|)\ +f^{\prime}(M_{0})\cdot\sum_{j\in[t]}|U_{<j}|\cdot|U_{j}|\leq{|U|\cdot f(|U|)},

where the final inequality follows by repeatedly applying (
11
). This completes the proof.

4
Cluster neighbourhoods

In order to prove Theorem
1.2
it will be crucial to understand how vertex neighbourhoods correlate, or cluster, in the graph. The following definition gives a useful measurement of this neighborhood clustering.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
G
be a graph, let
v
be a vertex of
G
and let
S\subset V(G)
. Given
M>1
and
t\in{\mathbb{N}}\cup\{0\}
, the
(M,t)
-
cluster neighbourhood
of
v
to
S
is defined as

\displaystyle W_{t}^{S}(v;M):=\bigg\{u\in V(G):\big|N_{G}^{S}(u)\Delta N_{G}^{S}(v)\big|\leq\bigg(\frac{4^{t}}{M}\bigg)\cdot\big|N_{G}^{S}(v)\big|\bigg\}.

When
S
and
M
are clear from the context we simply write
W_{t}(v)
or
W^{S}_{t}(v)
.

Given
\lambda>1
, setting
\Theta:=(M,\lambda)
,
the
\Theta
-
moment of

v

to

S
is given by

\displaystyle T_{\Theta}^{S}(v):=\min\bigg\{t\in{\mathbb{N}}\cup\{0\}:|W^{S}_{t}(v;M)|\leq\lambda\cdot|W^{S}_{t}(v;M)|\bigg\}.

The
\Theta
-
cluster
of
v
to
S
is then given by
W_{T_{\Theta}(v)}^{S}(v;M)
. Once again, when
S,M
are clear from the context we write
W_{*}(v):=W_{T_{\Theta}(v)}^{S}(v;M)
and
W_{+}(v):=W_{T_{\Theta}(v)+1}^{S}(v;M).

In the next subsection we present some useful properties of cluster neighbourhoods. The second subsection then proves a key lemma, which allows a partial decomposition of a graph using its cluster neighbourhoods. This result will be central in our proof of Theorem
1.2
.

4.1
Simple properties of cluster neighbourhoods

The next lemma captures several useful facts about cluster neighbourhoods and
\Theta
-clusters.

Lemma 4.2
.

Given
G,S
and
\Theta=(M,\lambda)
as above, the following hold:

(i)

W^{S}_{t}(v)\subset W^{S}_{t+1}(v)
for all
t\geq 0
.

(ii)

|W_{*}(v)|=|W^{S}_{T_{\Theta}(v)}(v)|\geq\lambda^{T_{\Theta}^{S}(v)}
.

(iii)

T_{\Theta}^{S}(v)\leq{\log_{\lambda}|S|}\leq{\log_{\lambda}|G|}
.

(iv)

|W_{+}(v)|\leq\lambda|W_{*}(v)|
.

Proof.

Part (i) is clear from the definition of
W^{S}_{t}(v)
. Parts (ii) and (iv) hold by the definition of
T_{\Theta}^{S}(v)
, noting that
v\in W_{0}^{S}(v)
. Lastly, part (iii) follows from (ii) since
|W_{*}(v)|\leq|S|\leq|G|
.
∎

We next present the following simple, yet useful lemmas.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
G,S
and
\Theta=(M,\lambda)
be as above. Then for any vertex
v
and non-negative integer
t<\log_{4}M-1
one has
|W^{S}_{t}(v)|\leq 2\Delta(G)
.

Proof.

Fix
v\in V(G)
and double count the edges (say there are
e_{v}
of them) with one endpoint in
W_{t}^{S}(v)
and the other in
N^{S}(v)
. On one hand, each vertex in
N^{S}(v)
contributes at most
\Delta(G)
edges, hence
e_{v}\leq\Delta(G)\cdot d^{S}(v)
. On the other hand, each vertex in
W_{t}^{S}(v)
must be adjacent to at least
d^{S}(v)(1-4^{t}M^{-1})
vertices in
N^{S}(v)
, thus
e_{v}\geq|W^{S}_{t}(v)|\cdot d^{S}(v)(1-4^{t}M^{-1})
. The result follows by combining these two bounds and using that
4^{t}<M/2
.
∎

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
G,S
and
\Theta=(M,\lambda)
be as above. Let
t_{1},t_{2}\in\mathbb{N}
and suppose
v_{1},v_{2}\in V(G)
such that
v_{2}\notin W_{t_{1}+1}^{S}(v_{1})
and
3\cdot 4^{t_{1}}d^{S}(v_{1})\geq 4^{t_{2}}d^{S}(v_{2})
. Then

W_{t_{1}}^{S}(v_{1})\cap W_{t_{2}}^{S}(v_{2})=\emptyset
.

Proof.

Assume by contradiction there is
u\in W_{t_{2}}^{S}(v_{2})\cap W_{t_{1}}^{S}(v_{1})
. Then, by triangle inequality

\displaystyle|\text{div}_{G}^{S}(v_{1},v_{2})|\leq|\text{div}_{G}^{S}(v_{1},u)|+|\text{div}_{G}^{S}(v_{2},u)|\leq M^{-1}\big(4^{t_{1}}d^{S}(v_{1})+4^{t_{2}}d^{S}(v_{2})\big)\leq 4M^{-1}4^{t_{1}}d^{S}(v_{1}),

which means that
v_{2}\in W_{t_{1}+1}^{S}(v_{1})
, contradicting our hypothesis. Therefore our assumption is false and so
W_{t_{1}}^{S}(v_{1})\cap W_{t_{2}}^{S}(v_{2})=\emptyset
.
∎

Corollary 4.5
.

Let
G,S
and
\Theta=(M,\lambda)
be as above. Let
t_{1},t_{2}\in\mathbb{N}
and suppose
v_{1},v_{2}\in V(G)
such that
v_{3-i}\notin W_{t_{i}+1}^{S}(v_{i})
for both
i\in\{1,2\}
. Then
W_{t_{2}}^{S}(v_{2})\cap W_{t_{1}}^{S}(v_{1})=\emptyset
.

Proof.

Relabelling if necessary, we can assume that
4^{t_{1}}d^{S}(v_{1})\geq 4^{t_{2}}d^{S}(v_{2})
. The degree condition in Lemma
4.4
then holds, implying the result.
∎

We note the following short lemma shows that having many clusters of small size and high enough degree in a graph guarantees a certain level of diversity. It also serves as a preamble to the next result, which states that otherwise vertices cluster around some centers of mass.

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
G,S
and
\Theta=(M,\lambda)
be as above. Let
n:=|V(G)|
and suppose there is a set
A\subset V(G)
with
|A|=m\geq\delta n\geq 1
such that
|W_{t}^{S}(v)|\leq n/m
and
d_{G}^{S}(v)\geq d
for all
v\in A
. Then there is
U\subset A
with
|U|\geq\delta m/2
such that
|\diver^{S}(u,v)|\geq 4^{t}d/M
for all
u\neq v
in
U
.

Proof.

Let us build a new graph
H
on the vertex set
A
by drawing an edge between two vertices
u
and
v
if
|\diver^{S}(u,v)|<4^{t}d/M
. Now, given
v\in A
, let us observe that
N_{H}(v)\subset W_{t}^{S}(v)
, therefore the average degree of
J
is at most
n/m
. By Turán’s theorem we find an independent set
U
in
H
with
|U|\geq|A|(n/m+1)^{-1}\geq m|A|/(n+m)\geq\delta m/2
, which is the desired set.
∎

4.2
Partial decomposition through cluster neighbourhoods

This subsection is dedicated to a central result in our proof of Theorem

. To check the remaining condition, observe that

M\log^{2}m\leq k^{1/2}\leq D\leq d_{H}(v)\leq\Delta(H)<m/2
, hence:

\displaystyle A\subset\Big\{v\in V(H):M\log^{2}m\leq d_{H}(v)\leq m/2\mbox{ and }\big|W_{*}(v)\big|\leq\alpha m\Big\}\subset V(H).

Thus Lemma
4.7
applies with these parameters to give sets
U,V_{1},\ldots,V_{t},S
and values
\{d_{i}\}_{i\in[t]}
which satisfy conditions (
i
)-(
v
) (with
n
replaced by
m
in these statements). As advised by (
v
),we also let
\gamma:=\log^{5}m\cdot\max(\Delta(G)\cdot m^{-1},t^{-1})\leq\log^{8}n\cdot\max(D/n,1/t)
.

We once again show the
Strategy
holds when applied to some of the
\{V_{i}\}_{i\in[t]}
. To see that
(A)
holds, recall from Lemma
4.7
(
v
) that
U
is
\gamma
-balanced to
S
. Set
\beta^{-1}=10(\gamma|U|\log^{4}k)^{1/2}
and take
{\cal E}={\cal B}_{\beta}(U,S)
to be the blended distribution on
[0.1,0.9]^{S}
. By recalling the definition of the
\Theta
-cluster, Lemma
3.5
for
D/M
gives that for all
v_{i}\in V_{i}
and
v_{j}\in V_{j}
with
i\neq j
we have

\displaystyle\operatorname{bad}_{\mathcal{\cal E}}^{S}({v_{i},v_{j}})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2M}{\beta D}+2\cdot(5D\log n)\cdot\exp\bigg(\frac{-0.045}{\gamma\beta^{2}|U|}\bigg)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{20\cdot M\sqrt{\gamma|U|\log^{4}k}}{D}+\dfrac{10\cdot k^{3/2}\log n}{T}\cdot\exp\big(-4.5\cdot\log^{4}k\big)

\displaystyle\leq\dfrac{20M\log^{6}k}{D}\cdot{\max\bigg(\sqrt{\frac{D|U|}{n}},1\bigg)}+k^{-3}\leq\frac{42M\log^{7}k}{k}\leq g_{1}^{\prime}(2k).

(15)

The final inequality above holds since
D\geq k^{3}/(n\log^{2}n)\geq k^{3}/(4n\log^{2}k
), which implies that
\sqrt{|U|/Dn}\leq\sqrt{4\log^{2}k\cdot k^{-2}}=2\log k/k
. Thus
part
(A)
of the
Strategy
holds.

Case 3(a)
:
k\geq|U|=t\geq k/\lambda^{3}
.

As before, in this case we proceed directly since

|U|=k/\lambda^{3}\geq k/g_{1}(k)
and there is already a distribution
{\cal D}={\cal E}\times{\cal T}
on
[0.1,0.9]^{V(G)}
, where
{\cal T}
is the trivial distribution on
[0,1,0.9]^{V(G)\setminus S}
. From (
15
) we deduce that
\operatorname{bad}_{\mathcal{D}}({U})\leq\binom{|U|}{2}\cdot 42Mk^{-1}\log^{7}k\leq|U|\cdot g_{1}(|U|)
, using
|U|=t\leq k
, as required.

Case 3(b)
:
|U|=t<k/\lambda^{3}
.

Here we continue with the
Strategy
, verifying that
(B)
holds. Let
[t]=I_{1}\cup I_{2}
denote again the partition with
I_{1}=\{i\in[t]:|V_{i}|\geq n^{4}/k^{6}\}
and
I_{2}=\{i\in[t]:|V_{i}|<n^{4}/k^{6}\}
. As
t<k
, by Lemma
4.7
(
ii
) we obtain that

\sum_{i\in I_{1}}|V_{i}|+\sum_{i\in I_{2}}|V_{i}|\geq\frac{m}{10^{3}\lambda\log^{2}m}\geq\frac{n}{10^{3}\lambda\log^{4}n}\geq 2\bigg(\frac{n}{\lambda^{2}}\bigg).

By using the pigeonhole principle, there is
j\in\{1,2\}
such that

\sum_{i\in I_{j}}|V_{i}|\geq\dfrac{n}{\lambda^{2}}.

Moreover,
\lambda(n/k)\leq\sum_{i\in I_{j}}|V_{i}|/t\leq\max_{i\in I_{j}}|V_{i}|\leq\alpha n\leq k^{3/2}\log^{2}n/T\leq n/\lambda^{6}
. It follows that the
Claim
from Case 2 above can be applied to the current sets
\{V_{i}\}_{i\in I_{j}}
as well, so
(B)
holds. Therefore the
Strategy
once again applies, completing the proof of Case 3.

We finish the proof by noting that our three cases exhaust all possibilities, since otherwise
|A|=|A_{1}|+|A_{2}|+|A_{3}|<0+(n/4)+(n/4)=n/2
, contrary to the assumption that
|A|\geq n/2
. Therefore one of these cases must apply and so the theorem is proved. ∎
