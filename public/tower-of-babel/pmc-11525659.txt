Phase II study of uPAR-PET/CT for staging of primary breast cancer in comparison with ultrasound and fine needle biopsies
Authors: Marie Øbro Fosbøl, Esben Andreas Carlsen, Malene Brandt-Larsen, Camilla Christensen, Jacob Madsen, Annika Loft, Anne Kiil Berthelsen, Tove Filtenborg Tvedskov, Niels Kroman, Kim Francis Andersen, Eric von Benzon, Andreas Kjaer
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Accurate initial staging of patients with breast cancer is essential for planning optimal treatment strategies. However, currently, no imaging modality is able to detect lymph node metastases preoperatively with sufficient reliability; therefore, the N status depends on the sentinel node procedure for ~ 70% of patients. In a prospective clinical trial of breast cancer patients, we compared head-to-head uPAR-PET/CT with current standard-of-care, ultrasound (US) and fine needle biopsy (FNB) as staging methods. Forty-nine patients (48 women and 1 man) with biopsy-proven early breast cancer underwent uPAR-PET/CT prior to surgery. All image data were analyzed by two separate teams, each consisting of a highly experienced certified specialist in nuclear medicine and a highly experienced certified specialist in radiology for visualization of primary tumor lesions and detection of lymph node and distant metastases. Histopathological assessment and verification of malignancy in the excised tissues (primary tumors and lymph nodes) were considered standard-of-truth. On a per patient basis, uPAR PET/CT demonstrated a sensitivity of 94% [CI: 83–99%] for detecting the primary tumor (both teams). For the detection of axillary lymph nodes the pooled sensitivity of uPAR PET/CT was 33.3% [CI: 16.5–54.0%], specificity 87.0% [CI: 66.4–97.2%] and accuracy 58.0% [CI: 43.2–71.8%]. In comparison, the standard-of-care preoperative clinical staging algorithm with US and FNB had a sensitivity of 41% [CI: 22–61%] and specificity of 100% [CI: 85–100%] for axillary lymph node metastases. We conclude that the results do not support the use of uPAR PET/CT for staging in breast cancer patients. However, the finding that 94% of primary tumors were uPAR-PET positive may be encouraging for pursuing uPAR theranostics in localized disease. Additionally, other potential applications, such as using uPAR-PET as a prognostic imaging biomarker of tumor aggressiveness, remain to be investigated.

Introduction

Accurate initial staging of patients with breast cancer is essential for planning an optimal treatment strategy and is currently based on clinical examination, bilateral mammography and ultrasound of the breast and regional lymph nodes; confirmed by histopathological assessment1,2. The axillary lymph node status is the most reliable prognostic indicator of recurrence and overall survival in patients with breast cancer. However, currently, no imaging modality is able to detect lymph node metastases with sufficient reliability; therefore, the N status depends on the sentinel node procedure for ≈ 70% of patients1. 2-[18F]fluoro-2-deoxy-D-glucose ([18F]FDG) PET/CT is not routinely recommended for preoperative staging and has been shown to be of limited value in the earlier stages of the disease and lobular carcinoma3,4. A major challenge with [18F]FDG PET is false-positive results commonly observed in areas of active inflammation and the inability to differentiate malignant from benign breast lesions. Consequently, there is a search for cancer-specific markers that can serve as candidates for tumor imaging ligands with superior sensitivity and specificity, giving patients with breast cancer the potential benefit of the combination of anatomic and molecular imaging with hybrid anatomical and molecular imaging5. Metastasis is one of the “hallmarks of cancer”, and is the ability of the cancer cells to invade the surrounding stromal tissue and form distant metastases, which leads to progression and poor prognosis6. A recent clinical phase I trial of the novel PET ligand [68Ga]Ga-NOTA-AE105 showed that this method is promising for providing noninvasive imaging of the metastatic process7. The radioligand targets the urokinase-type Plasminogen Activator Receptor (uPAR); a GPI-anchored receptor attached to the outside surface of the cell membrane of cancer cells and in tumor-associated stroma cells. The natural ligand uPA binds with high affinity to uPAR, facilitating pericellular plasmin-mediated extracellular matrix (ECM) degradation and subsequent tumor cell migration and invasion8–10. High levels of uPAR in primary tumor of patients with breast cancer have been linked to shorter progression-free survival and overall survival and to the prediction of treatment resistance in multiple studies9,11–14. The expression of uPAR is low or undetectable in normal, noncancer tissues15,16.

In this article, we report our initial results from a prospective clinical phase II trial of uPAR-PET/CT in breast cancer patients with the purpose of evaluating whether this method could be clinically beneficial for staging purposes.

We hypothesized that the uPAR-targeting radioligand [68Ga]Ga-NOTA-AE105 could be used to visualize primary tumor lesions and detect lymph node and distant metastases in patients with newly diagnosed breast cancer. The current standard of care for staging, ultrasound (US) and fine needle biopsy (FNB) results served as comparators.

Materials and methods

The study was approved by the Danish Health and Medicines Authority (2015-004503-23, first approved 17th of December 2015) and the Ethical Committee of the Capital Region of Denmark (protocol H-15017230, approved 18th of December 2015). The study was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT02681640) and was performed in accordance with the recommendation for Good Clinical Practice, including independent monitoring by the Good Clinical Practice Unit of the Capital Region of Denmark.

We included 54 patients who were enrolled from March 2016 to February 2017. All patients provided written informed consent before inclusion. All patients were referred to Rigshospitalet, University Hospital of Copenhagen, with biopsy-proven breast cancer greater than 2 cm in size (estimated by US) and were scheduled for lumpectomy/mastectomy with sentinel node procedure or full axillary lymph node dissection without neoadjuvant therapy. The exclusion criteria were age younger than 18 years, current pregnancy or lactation and prior malignancy. There were no restrictions on sex or race.

uPAR PET/CT acquisition

Enrolled patients underwent uPAR-PET/CT prior to surgery. No patient-specific preparation was required prior to [68Ga]Ga-NOTA-AE105 administration. PET/CT scans were conducted on an integrated system (Biograph mCT, Siemens Medical Solutions, Erlangen, Germany) with an axial field of view of 216 mm. Image acquisition was initiated 20 min after i.v. administration of [68Ga]Ga-NOTA-AE105 (≈ 200 MBq). The ligand was synthesized as previously described7. Acquisition time was 2 min per bed position for whole-body PET scans from skull base to proximal femur. PET data were attenuation and scatter-corrected and reconstructed iteratively using a 3D ordinary Poisson ordered-subset expectation-maximization (OSEM) algorithm that included a point spread function, prompt gamma correction and time-of-flight information (2 iterations, 21 subsets, a 2 mm Gaussian filter and a 400 × 400 matrix). Prior to the PET scan, a whole-body diagnostic CT scan was performed (2-mm slice thickness, 120 kV, quality reference of 225 mAs).

The first five consecutive patients were subjected to a special protocol with a dynamic PET scan with the purpose of determining the optimal timing for PET/CT. Initially, for attenuation correction purposes a low-dose CT scan (2-mm slice thickness, 120 kV, and 40 mAs) was acquired, followed by 60-minute dynamic emission scans of the breast region starting at the time of i.v. administration of [68Ga]Ga-NOTA-AE105 (≈ 200 MBq). Attenuation and scatter-corrected PET data were reconstructed iteratively with a 28-frame protocol (10 frames of 30 s, 5 frames of 60 s, 5 frames of 120 s, and 8 frames of 300 s). Based on the dynamic scans from the first 5 patients, image acquisition from 20 to 25 min post injection (p.i) was found to be optimal, and this acquisition window was used for the remaining patients.

uPAR PET interpretation

uPAR PET/CT scans were analyzed by two separate teams, each consisting of an experienced board-certified specialist in nuclear medicine and a board-certified specialist in radiology. To reduce the risk of bias, the teams were blinded to the clinical information, including tumor status, tumor size, tumor type and lymph node status, on prior diagnostic workup. Foci of increased uptake were identified as suspected tumor lesions and categorized as follows: For consensus readings (within each team), all foci, including primary tumors and lymph nodes/ distant foci were categorized as positive and suspicious for malignancy if the uptake on uPAR-PET was focal, above background level, or related to anatomical structures or pathological findings on the corresponding CT slices. A focus of high uPAR-PET uptake without suspicious characteristics on CT was categorized as PET-“positive” or CT-nonsuspicious. A suspected tumor lesion identified on CT that showed low or no uptake of [68Ga]Ga-NOTA-AE105 was considered to be a suspicious lesion on CT and PET-“negative”. For patients no. 1–5 (dynamic scanning 60 min), imaging interpretation was based on the reconstructed time frame 20–25 min post injection to ensure alignment with the rest of the cohort.

Verification of malignancy

All patients had biopsy-proven primary breast cancer prior to enrollment in the clinical trial. All patients (except one) underwent prospectively planned surgical removal of the primary breast cancer and sentinel node biopsy (SNB) and/or axillary lymph node dissection (ALND). Histopathological assessment with verification of malignancy in the excised tissues (primary tumors and lymph nodes) were considered standard-of-truth (SOT).

Statistical methods

Descriptive statistics were calculated for patient characteristics. McNemars test was used to compare the sensitivity and specificity between the two separate teams as compared to standard clinical staging method (US + FNB). Power calculation was based on detection of 65% of patients with axillary metastases preoperatively. Using a significance level of 0.05 (α = 0.05) and a power of 80%, 42 patients should be included. Taking into account technical/device errors or possible “drop-outs”, we aimed for inclusion of 50 patients. A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All the statistical analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics 25 (IBM Corp.) and Prism 9 (GraphPad Software).

Results

Fifty-four patients (53 women and 1 man) with biopsy-proven, early cancer were included in the study. Four patients did not receive the radioligand [68Ga]Ga-NOTA-AE105 due to technical challenges leading to synthesis failure and were therefore excluded from further analysis. In addition, one patient experienced a serious adverse event (SAE) with an acute allergic reaction starting immediately following administration of iodinated IV-contrast agent for CT. The SAE resolved following standard treatment with no long-term complications. This patient was not included in the data analysis because no full investigation was available. uPAR PET/CT unexpectedly revealed disseminated disease with multiple bone metastases and suspected spinal cord compression in one patient; consequently, the operation for this patient was cancelled. Therefore, only data regarding the histologically verified primary tumor of this patient were included in the analyses, whereas lymph node metastases were not evaluated. The patients received a median of 15.1 µg (range, 9.5-32-1 µg) of [68Ga]Ga-NOTA-AE105, and the activity was 193 MBq (range, 125–218 MBq) prior to whole-body PET/CT.

Interpretation of uPAR PET

Forty-nine patients were subjected to uPAR PET/CT, and the uptake of [68Ga]Ga-NOTA-AE105 in primary tumors and lymph node metastases was evaluated by two separate teams. Ten of the patients had multifocal primary tumors (> 1), as evidenced by tissue sampling/histopathology: seven patients had two separate tumors in the same breast, one had bilateral breast cancer, and two had one or more unilateral tumors. Thirty-one patients underwent lumpectomy, and 17 underwent mastectomy. Sentinel node biopsy (SNB) only was performed in 24 patients, whereas axillary lymph node dissection (ALND) was performed in 24 patients, including 13 patients, who were subjected to both SNB and ALND (Table 1).

On a per patient basis, both teams identified primary tumors in 46 of 49 patients on uPAR PET/CT, i.e., a sensitivity of 94% for detecting the primary tumor [CI: 83–99%] (Image example in Fig. 1). On a per tumor basis, Team 1 identified 58 uPAR-positive tumors, and Team 2 identified 57 uPAR-positive primary tumors out of a total of 62 tumors. Both teams described > 1 uPAR-PET-positive tumor in patients for whom histopathology revealed only one tumor (team 1: three patients and team 2: three patients). In addition, both teams failed to identify eight histopathologically verified tumors (Table 2), five of which were concordant between the teams. On US 59 tumors were correctly identified, resulting in a sensitivity of 95% for detecting primary tumor [CI: 87–99%]. The three tumors not identified on US measured 5, 6, and 11 mm, respectively. The same three tumors were not identified on uPAR PET/CT. There was no difference in the ability to diagnose axillary involvement according to relevant clinicopathological parameters: ductal versus lobular, malignancy grade, ER- and HER-2 status, Ki67 index and tumor size (data not shown). However, the chance of finding metastatic lymph nodes via uPAR PET/CT was associated with a high metastatic burden in the axilla (odds ratio 6.75 [95% CI 1.4–35] (p = 0.01)), defined as > 2 lymph nodes with metastases.

Regarding foci other than axillary lymph nodes, two patients had bone and lung metastases on uPAR-PET/CT, and one patient had a synchronous uPAR-PET/CT-positive primary lung tumor (NSCLC), for which the patient subsequently received treatment according to the established guidelines. In these three patients, the results of uPAR PET/CT had a significant impact on the treatment strategy. Possible extra-axillary lymph node involvement was diagnosed via uPAR PET/CT in the intramammary lymph nodes (ILNs) in two patients. No attempt was made to verify malignancy for ethical reasons since these patients had concomitant axillary lymph node involvement, and lymph nodes were included in the radiation field; therefore, this approach did not lead to a change of treatment strategy.

Discussion

This is the first study to investigate the use of uPAR-PET/CT for staging early breast cancer. We hypothesized that the upregulation of uPAR expression in primary tumors and lymph node metastases could favor enhanced [68Ga]Ga-NOTA-AE105 uptake and allow clinically valuable imaging. The results of the present study support this hypothesis, but the sensitivity of our head-to-head comparison for detecting axillary lymph node metastases was not significantly different from that of the current standard-of-care preoperative staging methods; US and FNB.

uPAR PET/CT had a combined (consensus reading) sensitivity of ≈ 33% for detection of axillary lymph node metastases and was comparable to the current preoperative staging method, with US and FNB detecting 41% of the actual lymph node metastases. In comparison to a published meta-analysis wherein ultrasound (US) exhibited a sensitivity of 55% (95% CI: 49–92%) in axillary staging, our reported sensitivity appears relatively low17. A possible explanation could be the low number of metastatic involved lymph nodes in our patient cohort, which is a predictor for non-detection18.

In three patients (6%), uPAR PET/CT revealed distant metastases/synchronous primary cancer (NSCLC), all of which had immediate therapeutic consequences. Furthermore, two patients had uPAR PET/CT-positive intramammary lymph nodes, which might represent true metastatic disease but did not lead to a change in postsurgical treatment.

Surprisingly, both teams identified the same three patients with uPAR-PET false-positive (nonmalignant) lymph nodes. A plausible explanation is an inflammatory response in these particular lymph nodes, e.g., after a primary biopsy procedure, as activated macrophages may present with increased uPAR expression10.

Detection of axillary lymph nodes with micrometastases or isolated tumor cells is likely out of reach with uPAR-PET/CT and other imaging technologies.However, studies in patients with T1-T2 and cN0 invasive breast cancer suggest that patients with micrometastatic spread or isolated tumor cells (< 0.2 mm) in the SNB and patients with limited involvement of the sentinel lymph node (1–2 metastases), who are receiving tangential breast irradiation and adjuvant systemic treatment may not need any further axillary surgical procedures1,3. Studies on the de-escalating treatment of breast cancer have suggested the omission of further axillary surgery in patients with macrometastatic disease in the sentinel node19. However, complete axillary nodal status, including the number of metastatic lymph nodes, is needed for adjuvant chemotherapy in certain patient subgroups20,21. Therefore, noninvasive imaging methods that visualize macrometastatic disease and broad axillary staging have become increasingly clinically relevant. Eight patients had two or more lymph nodes with macrometastases, and in parallel with what is seen with standard procedures in staging the axilla (US + FNB), the possibility of finding axillary involvement with uPAR-PET/CT was associated with a high metastatic burden in the axilla.

For the detection of primary tumors, a relatively high sensitivity of 94% per patient was found. This observation is in accordance with previous reports demonstrating by immunohistochemistry or ELISA that uPAR-positive tumor cells are present in the majority of primary breast cancer specimens16,22,23. An unresolved issue that could provide an explanation for the results of this study and likely influence the applicability of uPAR PET in breast cancer staging is whether lymph node metastases encompass uPAR-positive malignant cells that can disseminate and seed further metastases into other distant organs. In fact, studies have revealed disconcordance between uPAR expression on primary and metastatic tumor cells in breast cancer22, providing a likely explanation for not fulfilling our hypothesis in the present study—a significantly greater sensitivity for the detection of axillary metastases than US + FNB.

It is conceivable that [18F]FDG PET/CT would have demonstrated higher sensitivity and specificity in axillary staging of breast cancer patients than the methods used in the present study. A published meta-analysis reported that [18F]FDG PET/CT had a sensitivity of 51% [95% CI: 40–63%] and specificity of 93% [95% CI: 89–96%] in the detection of axillary lymph node metastases in breast cancer17. However, except for breast cancer patients with clinical stage IIB or higher, where [18F]FDG PET/CT has a substantial yield3, the use of [18F]FDG PET/CT in the staging of local/regional disease is generally not recommended.

A significant challenge with uPAR PET/CT is the persistent high level of [68Ga]Ga-NOTA-AE105 in the blood pool, likely secondary to plasma protein binding, which limits rapid clearance from soft tissue and diminishes the tumor-to-background ratio. Accordingly, lesions with lower avidity and smaller lesions, e.g., those close to vessels, may be difficult to identify. In the present study, PET acquisition was initiated 20 min after injection of the radioligand. Results from the phase I trial of [68Ga]Ga-NOTA-AE105 and from the dynamic PET of the initial five patients in the present cohort indicate that the blood pool activity indeed decreases within the first 60 min p.i., but the SUVmax of the malignant tumors equivalently declines in the time period7. Consequently, we assessed that tumor-to-background ratios were not likely to improve by postponing image acquisition.

Another disadvantage of the [68Ga]-labelled radioligand is the positron range of 4 mm, leading to reduced detection ability for small lesions. In comparison, 18F or 64Cu has a positron range of approximately 1 mm, leading to better lesion detection ability. Accordingly, the use of the 64Cu-labeled uPAR-PET tracer [64Cu]Cu-DOTA-AE105, which was tested in a phase I clinical trial24, would likely have improved the detection of smaller metastatic lesions. Indeed, it has been previously shown that the use of 64Cu versus 68Ga in somatostatin receptor imaging leads to better lesion detection24. A conceivable future study could include comparison between 64Cu-labelled uPAR PET tracer and [18F]FDG PET for staging breast cancer.

In this prospective study investigating uPAR PET/CT and comparing it on a head-to-head basis with US + FNB, we found that the diagnostic performance was on par with that of US + FNB, which we consider insufficient to support the routine use of uPAR PET/CT for staging in breast cancer patients. Nevertheless, the fact that 94% of the primary tumors were uPAR-PET/CT positive may be encouraging for further studies on the use of uPAR theranostics in primary breast cancer. In addition, in other malignant diseases, such as neuroendocrine neoplasms, brain cancer, and head and neck cancer, uPAR PET has consistently proven to be of prognostic value and associated with disease aggressiveness26–29. However, whether the uPAR PET can also serve as a prognostic biomarker in breast cancer remains to be investigated.
