Pediatric osmotic demyelination syndrome triggered by post-traumatic pituitary stalk injury: a case report
Authors: Maolin Sheng, Ming Zhang, Weifeng Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOsmotic demyelination syndrome (ODS) is an acute neurological disorder resulting from demyelination, typically triggered by rapid osmotic shifts. This syndrome is rare in adults and even more uncommon in children.To date, no reported cases have documented ODS secondary to hyponatremia caused by post-traumatic pituitary stalk injury. We report a pediatric patient with traumatic brain injury who developed ODS subsequent to pituitary stalk disruption and hyponatremia, and discuss the corresponding diagnostic and therapeutic strategies.Case presentationWe report the case of a 13-year-old Chinese girl who presented with seizures, central diabetes insipidus, dysarthria, dysphagia, and painful hypertonia following traumatic brain injury. Laboratory investigation revealed Severe hyponatremia. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)confirmed the diagnosis of ODS. The child was managed accordingly and demonstrated a favorable clinical response, with substantial recovery in physical function and quality of life.ConclusionEarly diagnosis of ODS is based on recognizing key risk factors, typical clinical symptoms, and characteristic neuroimaging findings. Contrast-enhanced MRI can be critical in elucidating the underlying etiology. Introduction

Osmotic demyelination syndrome (ODS) is an acute non-inflammatory demyelinating disorder of the central nervous system, characterized by the destruction of the myelin sheaths of nerve cells. When it affects the central pontine region, it is termed central pontine myelinolysis (CPM); when it involves extrapontine areas such as the basal ganglia, hippocampus, or cerebellum, it is termed extrapontine myelinolysis (EPM). EPM may occur alone or concurrently with CPM [1]. This syndrome is most commonly observed in chronic alcoholism (39%), followed by rapid correction of hyponatremia (21.5%) and liver transplantation (17.4%) [2]. Pediatric ODS is a rare entity, with only a few cases documented worldwide [3]. In particular, cases of ODS secondary to traumatic brain injury (TBI) in the pediatric population remain exceedingly rare.

TBI defines as a disruption of normal brain function caused by an external physical force and it is a major cause of disability and mortality worldwide.Electrolyte disturbances, such as hyponatremia, hypernatremia, and hyperkalemia, are a common complication of TBI and can significantly affect patient outcomes.Hyponatremia following TBI is most frequently attributed to the syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH) or cerebral salt wasting syndrome (CSWS) [4]. However, etiologies associated with post-traumatic hypopituitarism (PTHP) are often overlooked [5]. This report describes a case of severe hyponatremia in a pediatric patient with TBI, who presented with diverse clinical manifestations. The diagnosis of osmotic demyelination syndrome (ODS) was ultimately confirmed based on recognizing key risk factors, the clinical symptoms, and characteristic findings on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). Notably, the contrast-enhanced MRI findings suggested a possible pituitary stalk injury as the underlying cause.

Case report

We report a 13-year-old Chinese girl who was transferred to our hospital from another institution due to seizures following a traffic accident. She had no prior medical history and no underlying conditions.At the initial presentation to a local hospital, her Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score was 10. A computed tomography (CT) scan demonstrated traumatic subarachnoid hemorrhage. She received standard medical management comprising mannitol, hemostatic agents, and neuroprotective interventions, subsequently, she regained consciousness and her condition stabilized. However, on the 7th day post-injury, she developed seizures, prompting her transfer to our institution for further evaluation and care.Upon admission to our hospital, the patient had a GCS score of 6. A repeat CT scan showed improved resolution of the traumatic subarachnoid hemorrhage compared to prior imaging (Fig. 1a) Her serum sodium was severely low at 113 mmol/L (Table 1). Hyponatremia was corrected with intermittent infusions of 150 mL of 3% hypertonic saline, and serum sodium was monitored every 8 h.Within the first 24 h, serum sodium levels were 117 mmol/L, 120 mmol/L, and 128 mmol/L, respectively.As hyponatremia persisted, intermittent infusions of 150 mL 3% saline were continued, with subsequent levels of 130mmol/L, 135 mmol/L, and 144 mmol/L, respectively.A total of 17.2 g of sodium chloride was supplemented in the first 24 h, followed by an additional 19.6 g over the next 24 h.Meanwhile, morning fasting hormonal blood tests were obtained 3 days after admission (Table 2). No other diuretic agents were administered during treatment.Concomitantly, the patient received therapy for intracranial pressure reduction and neuroprotective support.Throughout this period, her fluid balance, central venous pressure, blood pressure, and heart rate remained within normal limits.Following treatment, she regained consciousness but presented with episodic irritability. Ten days after admission, the patient developed polyuria and polydipsia. Over the subsequent three days, her daily fluid intake was 8,024 ml, 11,116 ml, and 9,283 ml, respectively, with corresponding daily urine outputs of 7,520 ml, 9,878 ml, and 7,413 ml. Throughout this period, her central venous pressure, heart rate, and blood pressure remained within normal limits. Her dysarthria and dysphagia had lessened, with a reduction in choking episodes, and left-sided hypertonia had resolved. Additionally, her central diabetes insipidus had completely remitted.

Discussion

ODS was first described in 1959 by Adams who identified distinct demyelinating lesions in the central pons of patients with alcoholism and malnutrition, coining the term CPM [6]. Subsequently, it has been recognized that similar demyelinating lesions can occur in extrapontine regions due to osmotic stress, leading to the designation EPM [7]. Given their identical histopathological features, CPM and EPM are now collectively termed ODS.ODS is a rare neurological disorder, accounting for approximately 0.4–0.56% of neurological hospital admissions in tertiary care centers and 0.06% of all medical admissions [8–10].Bansal et al. [3] performed a review of all reported pediatric ODS cases from 1960 to 2018.A total of 106 pediatric cases with ODS were noted. It is probable that this number is an underestimate owing to the presence of asymptomatic and unreported cases, but it occurs notably less frequently in the pediatric population than in the adult population.Lampl et al. [2] noted that ODS is most commonly associated with chronic alcoholism (39%), followed by rapid correction of hyponatremia (21.5%) and liver transplantation (17.4%). Less commonly, ODS has been associated with electrolyte disturbance such as hypernatremia [11], hypokalemia [12], and hypophosphatemia [13]; renal failure [14]; diabetes mellitus [15]. Despite numerous known triggers, no prior case reports have been published describing ODS developing secondary to post-traumatic pituitary injury following pediatric TBI.

ODS presents with a wide range of clinical manifestations depending on the specific neuroanatomical regions involved. CPM typically manifests with symptoms such as impaired consciousness (ranging from coma to lethargy), progressive spastic quadriparesis, pseudobulbar palsy, dysarthria, dysphagia, nystagmus, and cranial nerve palsies. Severe cases may progress to a “locked-in” state or even death [16, 17]. EPM presentation depends on the location of the lesion. EPM may present as ataxia, various involuntary movements (including parkinsonism), dystonia, and tremor [18]. Our case deviates from these classic descriptions. The patient regained consciousness after rapid correction of hyponatremia, only to subsequently develop central diabetes insipidus, which was managed with oral desmopressin. During rehabilitation, she developed progressive recurrence of dysarthria, dysphagia, and left-sided hypertonia with associated pain. This atypical, progressive clinical presentation often contributes to misdiagnosis or diagnostic delay in clinical practice.

The diagnosis of ODS relies on clinical neurological assessment and is confirmed by neuroimaging. MRI is the modality of choice, being significantly more sensitive than CT for this purpose [17]. In CPM, characteristic MRI findings include symmetric, T2-weighted hyperintense and T1-weighted hypointense lesions located centrally within the pons. These often exhibit distinctive shapes described as “trident-shaped,” “batwing-shaped,” or “pig’s hoof-shaped” [18]. For EPM, MRI typically reveals bilateral, symmetrical T1 hypointense and T2/FLAIR hyperintense lesions. Common sites of involvement include the cerebellar peduncles, globus pallidus, thalamus, lateral geniculate bodies, putamen, external capsule, and the splenium of the corpus callosum [19]. In the present case, the patient exhibited symmetrical T1 hypointense and T2/FLAIR hyperintense lesions within the bilateral basal ganglia. Diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) demonstrated hyperintense extrapontine lesions, with low apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC).These imaging abnormalities demonstrated clear progression over time, which paralleled the worsening of her clinical symptoms. Notably, while the child presented clinically with dysphagia and dysarthria—symptoms often associated with pontine involvement—repeated MRI scans showed no signal abnormalities in the pons. This discrepancy may arises because early-stage disease involves functional lesions characterized by glial metabolic dysfunction and myelin edema, which elicit clinical symptoms but have not yet advanced to structural demyelination detectable by MRI. Furthermore, the exceptionally dense fiber bundles within the brainstem lead to severe neurological deficits even with minimal functional disruption.This dissociation between the primary clinical symptoms (suggestive of CPM) and the imaging findings (localized to extrapontine structures) underscores an atypical presentation of ODS.

ODS has multiple causes and typically occurs during the rapid correction of hyponatremia. Unlike the acute setting, chronic hyponatremia does not induce rapid cellular volume changes, thereby avoiding osmotic injury. When plasma osmolality is rapidly increased, brain cells cannot swiftly generate enough intracellular solutes to maintain osmotic balance. Consequently, water rapidly effluxes from the cells, leading to cellular shrinkage and apoptosis, which ultimately results in cerebral demyelination [20]. This pediatric case also developed ODS secondary to hyponatremia from TBI.Sodium imbalance is a common and clinically significant complication following TBI, with acute-phase hyponatremia or hypernatremia occurring in 15%–40% of patients [21, 22]. Post-TBI hyponatremia is predominantly attributed to SIADH or CSWS. In some cases, pituitary injury may induce hypopituitarism, potentially resulting in transient alternation between arginine vasopressin deficiency (AVP-D) and SIADH [23]. In the early phase of this case, the patient’s urine osmolality and urinary sodium level were low, while serum osmolality, serum hormone levels and blood pressure remained within normal limits.The combination of the aforementioned laboratory findings and hyponatremia were inconsistent with the diagnostic criteria for either syndrome of inappropriate SIADH or CSWS.Although laboratory findings were normal, no T1-hyperintense lesions in the posterior pituitary on the patient’s MRI still warrants suspicion of pituitary injury.Subsequent contrast-enhanced MRI identified no visible pituitary stalk, suggesting a pituitary stalk injury that led to secondary hypopituitarism. The patient initially presented with findings suggestive of PTHP, manifesting as either cortisol deficiency or SIADH. Later, the clinical picture evolved into central diabetes insipidus. This progression occurred due to failure of AVP transport to the posterior pituitary, a consequence of the pituitary stalk injury.

PTHP was first reported in 1918 [24] and was long considered a rare complication of TBI throughout the 20th century [25]. It was not until two landmark studies published in 2000 and 2001 that the incidence of PTHP was recognized to be substantially higher than previously thought [26]. A 2024 international meta-analysis, encompassing 72 datasets from 52 studies and 7,367 subjects, reported an overall prevalence of approximately 33% for PTHP involving any hormonal axis [27]. The incidence of pediatric PTHP is also significant [28, 29]. Neuroimaging studies in PTHP patients have identified the most common lesions as hypothalamic hemorrhage (29%), posterior pituitary hemorrhage (26.3%), anterior pituitary infarction (25%), pituitary stalk transection (3.9%), and posterior pituitary infarction (1.3%) [30]. Thus, isolated pituitary stalk injury is relatively uncommon. In the present case, the rapid acceleration-deceleration forces sustained during ejection from the vehicle likely caused pituitary stalk injury, thereby disrupting the vascular supply to the hypothalamus and pituitary gland. This injury triggered the initial phase of the response: either a reduction in cortisol secretion or an uncontrolled release of pre-stored AVP from the posterior pituitary during the stress response, either of which could lead to hyponatremia. Early hormone measurements failed to reflect the true extent of PTHP, and subsequent sodium repletion in the context of critically low serum sodium levels precipitated ODS with its clinical manifestations.Subsequently, the damaged pituitary tissue began to degenerate, a process affecting the hypophyseal portal system and causing neuronal damage that impaired neurohormone transmission. The eventual depletion of preformed AVP led to the onset of central diabetes insipidus. The thorough analysis of potential etiologies for hyponatremia and the timely elucidation of its underlying mechanism in this case were crucial for avoiding severe adverse outcomes.

Conclusion

We report a rare pediatric case of hyponatremia-associated ODS that developed secondary to traumatic pituitary stalk injury. Herein, we detail the patient’s clinical manifestations, imaging findings, and therapeutic regimen, alongside an analysis of the underlying pathophysiological mechanisms. Currently, no standardized treatment protocol for ODS has been established, and clinical outcomes remain inconsistent. Our therapeutic strategy yielded favorable efficacy, however, the underlying mechanisms—especially those pertaining to the use of B-complex vitamin supplements, a measure that has not been previously documented in relevant literature—remain to be elucidated.

During the acute phase following TBI, close monitoring of serum electrolytes (particularly serum sodium levels), fluid balance, and pituitary function (e.g., assessments of cortisol and AVP dynamics) is essential. Should hyponatremia occur, cautious electrolyte correction is mandatory. For patients with chronic, severe hyponatremia, the serum sodium concentration should not be elevated by more than 10–12 mmol/L within the first 24 h to minimize the risk of ODS. If clinical suspicion of ODS remains high despite an initially non-diagnostic MRI scan, a follow-up cranial MRI within 1–2 weeks is recommended. In TBI patients with suspected pituitary injury, contrast-enhanced pituitary MRI should be performed to evaluate the structural integrity of the hypothalamic-pituitary axis. No effective treatment has been defined for ODS. B-complex vitamin supplementation and hyperbaric oxygen therapy represent an empirical management approach for ODS.
