Epidemiological analysis of brain tumours in dogs in Australia between 1994 and 2024
Authors: Haileyesus Dejene, Kalana Manika Hennedige, José Luis Granados-Soler, Rachel Allavena, Joerg Henning
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBrain tumours are a relatively rare but a clinically important cause of neurological diseases in dogs. This study aimed to estimate the incidence of canine brain tumours in Australia, assess spatio-temporal patterns, explore tumour type-dog breed relationships, evaluate post-diagnosis survival for selected brain tumours, and identify animal-level risk factors. A retrospective cross-sectional analysis was conducted using clinical records from 1994 to 2024 from veterinary clinics participating in the VetCompass Australia (VCA) registry. Records were retrieved using targeted keyword search terms, further processed using an AI-assisted extraction tool, and manually reviewed for accuracy. Of 926 identified cases, 109 (11.8%) were primary tumours, 33 (3.6%) secondary tumours, and 784 (84.7%) were classified as unconfirmed brain tumours. The overall incidence rate was 8.7 (95% CI: 8.2–9.3) cases per 100,000 dog-years at risk. Spatial analysis revealed geographic differences in the incidence rates of primary and secondary brain tumours across Australia, with the highest incidence observed in New South Wales (2.3 cases per 100,000 dog-years). A statistically significant spatial clusters of combined primary and secondary brain tumour cases (RR = 5.8, p = 0.001) was identified in New South Wales. Temporal trend analysis showed a steady increase in the incidence from 2008 to 2015, reaching 3.9 cases per 100,000 dog-years at risk. A hierarchical cluster analysis suggested a grouping of glioma and lymphosarcoma cases with breeds such as French bulldogs and boerboels overrepresented. The median survival time of dogs following diagnosis differed significantly between tumour types (log-rank test, p ≤ 0.001); it was 6.0 and 0.2 months for astrocytoma and glioma, respectively. Advancing age was strongly associated with brain tumour incidence (p ≤ 0.001), and utility breeds had a higher incidence rate than crossbreed dogs (IRR = 3.9; 95% CI: 2.3–6.6, p ≤ 0.001). This study provides the first nationwide description of canine brain tumour incidence, spatio-temporal patterns, risk factors, and survival outcomes in Australia. Introduction

Brain tumours are defined as abnormal cell growth within the cranial cavity1; they are relatively rare but a clinically important cause of neurological disease in dogs2. Brain tumours are classified as primary or secondary. Primary tumours arise from central nervous system tissues such as neurons, glial cells, or meninges, whereas secondary tumours developed through metastatic spread or direct extension from nearby structures, including the pituitary, nasal cavity or skull3.

Dogs diagnosed with primary or secondary brain tumours are at increased risk of seizures, particularly when tumours involve the frontal lobe4,5. Snyder et al.6. reported that most tumours were located within the telencephalon, with seizures being the most common presenting clinical sign. The same study also noted that, of the 168 brain tumours identified at post-mortem, 79 (47.0%) involved multiple brain regions6. Accurate diagnosis is critical for guiding appropriate therapeutic strategies in the management of intracranial lesions7. Recent advancements in computed tomography (CT), magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and biopsy techniques have significantly enhanced the accuracy of antemortem diagnosis in dogs2. Among available neurosurgical procedures, stereotactic tumour biopsy is considered as the safest technique, offering reliable diagnostic outcomes with minimal surgical risk7.

Primary brain tumours constitute approximately 2–5% of all canine neoplasia and are particularly prevalent in brachycephalic breeds, including English bulldogs, French bulldogs, and boxers8. In a retrospective analysis of 186 brain tumours recorded in a veterinary histopathological database between 2007 and 2017 in Japan, 85.5% were classified as primary and 14.5% as secondary9. Similarly, a retrospective postmortem evaluation of brain tumour reported at a Veterinary Hospital at the University of Pennsylvania indicated an overall prevalence of 4.5%2. Among primary brain tumours, meningiomas are the most common, accounting for approximately 50% of cases2,6, followed by glioma, which represent 30–40%2. Astrocytomas, oligodendrogliomas, and choroid plexus tumours comprise 17%, 14%, and 7% of cases, respectively6. A prevalence of 0.29% for secondary brain tumours was reported in a retrospective study9, with hemangiosarcoma, metastatic carcinoma, lymphosarcoma9,10, pituitary tumours10, osteosarcoma, and histiocytic sarcoma9 being the most frequently observed types. Primary brain tumours occur most frequently in middle-aged to older dogs8,9, while secondary brain tumours have also been reported predominantly in middle to older-aged dogs9,10. Survival outcomes vary among canine brain tumour types and are influenced by tumour characteristics, treatment, and patient factors, with longer survival generally observed in dogs with definitive therapy compared to palliative management alone11,12.

Canine cancer registries serve as a valuable resource for epidemiological research as they allow comparisons of frequencies of tumour types in populations, they can reveal atypical cancer occurrences, guide future investigations and generate hypotheses regarding drivers of cancer occurrence in specific regions13,14. To the best of our knowledge, there are no published Australian studies reporting the incidence or prevalence of canine brain tumours using canine cancer registries or veterinary clinical databases.

The study presented here addresses that gap by examining the frequency, incidence, and associated risk factors of brain cancers using data from the VetCompass Australia database. In addition, it explores the temporal and spatial distribution of canine brain tumours across the Statistical Area Level 2 (SA2) regions of Australia, which represent medium-sized communities that interact socially and economically15. Understanding the epidemiology and geographic distribution of brain tumours can help veterinary clinicians or veterinary government offices to formulate evidence-based approaches for early detection, improved clinical management, and targeted prevention efforts. Furthermore, the aim of the study was to explore clustering of tumour types by dog breeds and to evaluate post-diagnosis survival by analysing time to death in dogs with different tumour types.

Materials and methods

Data source

This study employed a retrospective cross-sectional analysis of canine brain tumour cases recorded in the VCA registry, a national repository of de-identified electronic patient records (EPRs) collected from participating veterinary practices across Australia. VetCompass Australia is a collaborative initiative involving all Australian veterinary schools and forms part of the international VetCompass programme, which aims to generate high-quality clinical data for companion animal health research14. The registry includes demographic and clinical information from a large and geographically diverse population of dogs receiving primary veterinary care across Australia. Although VCA provides broad national coverage, participating clinics may not be fully representative of all Australian veterinary practices or the entire Australian dog population, and findings should therefore be interpreted accordingly.

Case population

All clinical records of dogs attended in VCA clinics between 1994 and 2024 were reviewed to estimate brain tumour frequency and incidence rates (IRs) and to investigate spatial and temporal patterns of brain tumour occurrence within the VCA dog population.

Keyword search strategy for cases

A keyword search strategy was developed to identify potential brain tumours in dogs recorded in the VCA database between 1994 and 2024. Search terms applied to clinical consultation notes included “meningioma”, “oligodendroglioma”, “astrocytoma”, “glioblastoma”, “glioma”, “neuroblastoma”, “choroid plexus”, “GBM” (with spacing to detect variations) or “Glioblastoma multiforme (including codes to capture spelling variants using tilde)”, “intracranial” AND (“neoplasia” OR “mass”), “brain” AND (“neoplasia” OR “cancer”) AND (“tumor” OR “tumour”). Data extracted from the VCA database included clinic identifiers, Statistical Area Level 2 (SA2) codes of owners, canine patient demographics (date of birth, sex, neutered status, breed, and insurance status), and other clinical consultation information (date of diagnosis and death). A total of 7,357 unique patients with suspected brain tumour related records and 105,656 associated clinical examination notes were retrieved based on these search terms (i.e. more than one clinical examination note was obtained for all unique patients).

AI search refinement for cases

Clinical examination records identified through the keyword search strategy were processed using a GPT-4o model via the OpenAI API (Application Programming Interface; Batch API) (Supplementary File 1). Prompts were developed using the COSTAR framework (Context, Objective, Style, Tone, Audience, Response) and refined iteratively to generate structured JSON (JavaScript Object Notation) outputs containing tumour presence, tumour type, and the earliest confirmed diagnosis date. Refinement was conducted using three randomly selected test batches (3 * 100 records), where misclassified cases informed prompt adjustments, and cross-checking and prompt-sensitivity were evaluated by re-testing on earlier batches to confirm improved and consistent performance while avoiding excessive optimisation that could bias classification toward specific record types. After optimisation, the final prompt was applied to the full dataset. Full search terms and extraction criteria are provided in Supplementary File 1. Performance was evaluated across three validation runs (n = 300 records per run) and the final model achieved an average classification accuracy of 91.1%, sensitivity of 99.1%, and specificity of 83.1%, with consistent performance across runs. Records identified through the search strategy and the corresponding AI-extracted outputs were manually reviewed against the source clinical information to ensure accuracy and consistency, with any discrepancies resolved during this verification process.

Brain tumour cases from VCA records were classified into three cohorts based on the level of diagnostic evidence available. The first group consisted of primary brain tumours, with tumours confirmed using histopathology and/or diagnostic imaging modalities such as MRI or CT. These primary brain tumours were defined as tumours originating from brain tissue or the meninges1. The second group consisted of secondary brain tumours, identified through diagnostic imaging and histopathology. Secondary brain tumours were defined as lesions metastasizing to the brain from distant sites or extended directly from adjacent tissues8,16. The third group consisted of unconfirmed cases, but where a brain tumour was suspected. Identification was based on clinical presentations, including multifocal brain masses, progressive neurological deterioration, seizures, or other tumour-associated neurological signs. These cases did not have histopathological or diagnostic imaging confirmation, but a brain tumour was often listed as the primary differential in the clinical record. Tumour type classifications using AI were fully concordant with the manual classification of cancer-positive cases. Flowchart illustrating the inclusion and selection of dogs with brain tumours from the VetCompass Australia (VCA) dataset between 1994 and 2024.

Dog breeds were classified into nine groups according to the Australian National Kennel Council (ANKC) and Dog New Zealand (NZKC) breed categories17, gundogs, hound, non-sporting, terriers, toy, utility and working dogs, and crossbreed and unrecorded. Dogs resulting from crossbreeding, such as moodle, groodle, cavoodle, spoodle, pugalier, schnoodle and labradoodle, or those with an “X” following the breed name in VCA were categorized as crossbred. Dogs without recorded breed information were classified as unrecorded. Age was calculated from the date of birth to the date of diagnosis. Dogs younger than one year of age and those with missing date of birth information were excluded because brain tumours are rare in juvenile dogs and age information was required for incidence estimation and risk factor analyses. Dog age was grouped into five categories (1–3, 4–6, 7–9, 10–12, and ≥ 13 years), following the companion-animal age stratification framework described by Salt et al.18.

Denominator population

The denominator dataset was obtained from the VCA repository and comprised of dogs attending participating veterinary clinics across Australia. VCA represents a large geographically diverse population of dogs receiving routine veterinary care across Australia14. Although records were available from 1950 onwards, the denominator dataset was restricted to dogs with records from 1994 onwards to align the time periods of the denominator data with the brain tumour case data. The dataset included clinic identifiers, patient identifiers, date of birth, SA2 codes, consultation dates, breed, sex, and other demographic variables. Data cleaning involved removing duplicate records, standardising variables, resolving incomplete geographic information where possible, and excluding records with missing key demographic data. These procedures were performed using the R package dplyr19, tidyr20,21, and writexl22 with additional processing conducted using base R function23. Age was calculated from the date of birth to the last recorded consultation. Similarly to case data, dogs younger than one year of age and dogs with missing information were excluded. These exclusions also ensured consistency between the case and denominator datasets and allowed accurate age-based analyses. The final denominator dataset comprised 1,537,397 dogs and was used for incidence rate calculations (Fig. 2).

Statistical analysis

Brain tumours frequencies

Frequency of cases within each tumour category were calculated as the proportion of cases among the total number of cases. Association between tumour categories and animal-level risk factors were assessed using cross tabulation and Fisher’s exact test23. Differences in mean age at diagnosis among tumour categories were evaluated using one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s HSD post-hoc comparisons after assessment of model assumption24. Data management and analysis were performed in R using the car package25.

Brain tumours incidence rates

An incidence rate is defined as the number of new disease cases divided by the total amount of time the population remains at risk of developing the disease26. In this study, the total time at risk was expressed as dog-years at risk (DYAR), representing the cumulative number of years during which dogs were at risk of developing a tumour during the observation period. Tumour IRs, expressed as the number of cases per 100,000 DYAR, were calculated using the formula described by Dhein et al.27 for a canine tumour incidence study conducted in Switzerland.

Incidence rates were also calculated for breed, sex, and age categories using the same approach, with the relevant denominator dataset used for these variables. Poisson 95% confidence intervals for IRs were calculated by the exact method using the PoissonCI(x, n, method = “exact”) function in the R package DescTools (version 0.99.60)28. Results were scaled by 100,000 to express IRs per 100,000 dog-years at risk.

Spatio-temporal analysis of brain tumours incidence rate

Although data on brain cancers were obtained from all Australian jurisdictions, the spatial analyses were only conducted for Queensland, New South Wales, and Victoria, where the majority of brain tumour cases occurred. Tumour and denominator datasets were aggregated at the SA2 level, and geographical centroids were calculated for each SA2 to support spatial mapping of incidence rate. Spatial visualization of SA2-level incidence rates was performed in QGIS (v3.20)29. Spatial data processing was performed in R using sf package30.

Weighted kernel density estimation (KDE), a non-parametric smoothing technique was used to estimate the spatial intensity of events; it was applied to identify areas of elevated brain tumour incidence while accounting for spatial variability31. A weighted KDE surface was generated using SA2 centroid coordinates, weighted by the corresponding brain tumour incidence rate for each SA2. The resulting density surface was mapped to visualise areas of elevated brain tumour incidence using the ks package32,33. Spatial clustering was assessed using Kulldorff’s spatial scan statistic at the SA2 level, a method widely used to detect statistically significant geographic concentration of disease while accounting for the underlying population at risk34,35. A Poisson-based model, suitable for analysing rare event counts across population of varying sizes, was implemented with 999 Monte Carlo simulations was implemented in R using the smerc package36. Statistically significant clusters were characterized by their relative risk and p-values34,37.

Temporal trends were assessed by calculating annual brain tumour incidence rates and the corresponding yearly number of clinics providing data to VCA. Trends over time were visualized using ggplot2 package38.

Clustering of brain tumour types by breeds of dogs

A two-step clustering approach combining Multiple Correspondence Analysis (MCA) and Hierarchical Clustering on Principal Components (HCPC) was used to explore patterns of clustering of brain tumour types by dog breeds39. MCA was first applied to identify underlying structures in the categorical data40,41, followed by HCPC to group observations with similar profiles based on principal component scores40,42. In this study, MCA was used to explore associations between dog breeds and brain tumour types, while HCPC was used to identify groups of dogs with similar breed-tumour characteristics. This approach is suitable for high-dimensional categorical data; however, it is exploratory approach and does not provide direct measures of association or risk. Cluster membership and variable contributions were then used to aid interpretation, and visualisations were generated using the factoextra43, ggplot238 and ggrepel44 packages in R.

Time to death for selected brain tumour types

Survival analysis was performed for the four most common brain tumour types: glioma, meningioma, pituitary macroadenoma, and astrocytoma. Kaplan-Meier methods were used to estimate survival probabilities from the date of diagnosis to the date of death. Censoring at the last recorded veterinary consultation was used for dogs without a recorded death date. Survival time was calculated as the interval between diagnosis and death or censoring. Median survival times and survival probabilities were estimated for each tumour type, and differences between tumour-specific survival curves were assessed using the log-rank test. Survival analyses and visualisations were conducted using the survival45 and survminer46 packages. The effect of tumour type, breed, sex, age at diagnosis, and neuter status on survival were evaluated using Cox proportional hazards regression, with results reported as hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence interval47. The proportional hazards assumption was assessed using Schoenfeld residuals. Variables that violated the proportional hazard assumption were accommodated using time-dependent effect or stratification within the Cox modelling framework48,49.

Animal-level risk factors for brain tumours incidence rates

Negative binomial regression (NBR) was used to estimate the incidence rate ratios (IRRs) for brain tumours while accounting for overdispersion in the count data. The model included sex, breed, and age category as explanatory variables, with the logarithm of the population at risk incorporated as an offset term. IRRs and corresponding 95% confidence intervals were calculated from the fitted model. Model fitting and inference were conducted using the R MASS50 and broom51 packages.

Results

Brain tumours frequencies

Over the study period from (1994–2024), of the 926 cases of brain tumour identified across veterinary clinics providing data to the VCA, 11.8% (95% CI: 9.9–14.0) were primary tumours, 3.6% (95% CI: 2.5–4.9) were secondary brain tumours and 84.6% (95% CI: 82.2–86.8) were unconfirmed brain tumour cases. The frequency of primary brain tumours was 77.9% in neutered dogs, 60.6% in males, and 16.5% in utility breeds. Clustering brain tumour types by breeds of dogs

Cluster analysis revealed a cluster, where 93.1% of dogs had glioma, with French bulldogs being overrepresented, comprising 13.8% of the dogs (v-test values > 2 indicating significant overrepresentation). Similarly, English springer spaniels were grouped with nasal lymphoma (v-test ≥ 2, p < 0.05) (Supplementary Table 6).

Time to death for selected brain tumour types

Of 111 dogs analysed, 61 were euthanized at diagnosis. Kaplan-Meier analysis demonstrated a progressive decline in survival time across all four brain tumour types, with a statistically significant difference in median survival times by brain tumour types (log-rank test p ≤ 0.001) (Fig. 7). Dogs with astrocytoma had a median survival time of 6.0 months (183 days), compared with 5.2 months (158 days), 2.7 months (83 days), and 0.2 months (6 days) for dogs with meningioma, pituitary macroadenoma, and glioma, respectively (Supplementary Table 7).

Discussion

Brain tumours are a clinically important cause of neurological disorders in dogs, although they occur infrequently2. Brain tumours also have a low frequency in humans, and dogs could represent a valuable translational model for human brain tumours, as spontaneous canine brain tumours share histopathological and genetic characteristics with their human counterparts8.

There had been no published data on the incidence or prevalence of canine brain tumours in Australia. In this study, meningioma was the most common primary brain tumour, representing 60.6% of diagnoses. This aligns with studies from Asia and North America, where meningioma accounted for 45–51% of canine brain tumours2,6,9,52, although Hayes et al.53. found glial tumours more common than meningioma. The discrepancy is likely attributable to differences in tumour classification methodology; our study classified each tumour type separately, whereas Hayes et al.53, grouped several tumour types under glial tumours. Overall, the literature consistently identifies meningioma as the predominant primary canine brain tumour54,55, likely reflecting its origin from arachnoid cap cells of the meninges and its extra-axial location, which typically produces clearer margins on imaging; this makes meningiomas easier to detect than many intra-axial tumours55,56.

In the current study, secondary brain tumours accounted for 3.6% of all cases, a lower proportion than the 14.5% reported by Kishimoto et al.9. Pituitary macroadenoma was the most frequently identified secondary tumour (36.4%). Although the pituitary gland lies within the cranial cavity, pituitary neoplasms are often classified as secondary brain tumours because they originate from endocrine rather than neural tissue. Snyder et al.10 also reported pituitary tumours as the predominant secondary brain tumours. In contrast, Okonji et al.57 identified hemangiosarcoma, carcinoma, and melanoma as the most common metastatic tumours. The variation in reported frequency among studies likely reflects differences in data sources used, dissimilar sampling approaches and tumour classification systems. Studies restricted to specific tumour types or based on data from referral-centre populations may also influence the observed frequency of brain tumours.

In the current study, 84.6% of cases were classified as unconfirmed brain tumours, representing dogs with a high clinical suspicion of brain tumours rather than with a definitive diagnosis. This high proportion reflects the fact that dogs presenting with severe or rapidly progressive central neurological signs often show rapid clinical deterioration and have a poor prognosis and therefore a definite diagnosis is not obtained. In fact, many suspected end-stage brain tumour cases are euthanised before advanced imaging or histopathological examination can be performed58,59. Consequently, suspected tumour cases based on clinical signs from veterinary clinic records might underrepresent true tumour incidence (primary and secondary brain tumours) because definitive confirmation requires biopsy or post-mortem examination, which is not always feasible in clinical practices8. On the other hand, absence of diagnostic confirmation requires cautious interpretation of suspected cancer cases based on clinical signs8,59. Therefore, in contrast to previous studies6,9,10,53, which included only confirmed primary and secondary tumours, the present study incorporates both confirmed tumours and cases with a high clinical suspicion of brain tumours, representing a key methodological difference in case selection that should be considered when comparing results between different studies. However, the consistency of patterns between confirmed and unconfirmed cases suggests that inclusion of unconfirmed cases is unlikely to materially influence the overall conclusions.

In this study, the overall incidence rate of brain tumours was 8.7 per 100,000 dog-years at risk, which is lower than the 14.5 per 100,000 dogs at risk reported by Vandevelde60,61, for central nervous system tumours. Within primary tumours, meningiomas had the highest incidence at 0.62 per 100,000 dog-years, followed by glioma (0.25; 95% CI: 0.16–0.35). These findings are consistent with those reported by Dhein et al.27, who reported a meningioma incidence of 1.2 (95% CI: 1.0-1.5) in Swiss dogs based on population-level pathology data. The current results also correspond with human data from Rohringer et al.62, who reported crude incidence rates of 2.3 per 100,000 for all meningiomas and 0.17 per 100,000 for malignant meningiomas in Manitoba, Canada. Variations in incidence rates likely reflecting variations in study designs, case ascertainment, and population denominators. However, overall, the findings suggest a broadly similar pattern in the relative frequency and predominance of meningioma across dogs and humans. This cross-species consistency in relative meningioma predominance underscores the value of dogs as a translational model for human meningioma epidemiology and therapeutic research.

Across age groups, brain tumour incidence was highest in dogs aged 10–12 years (12.7 per 100,000 dog-years), followed by those ≥ 13 years (11.2) and 7–9 years (8.3) old, consistent with previous studies identifying older age as a major risk factor in dogs2,9,52,53. The higher incidence in elderly dogs likely reflects biological ageing process, including the accumulation of somatic mutations and age-related immune decline, which reduce the ability to eliminate abnormal cells63,64. In humans, the incidence of brain and central nervous system cancers is estimated at 1.3 per 100,000 persons for children aged 0–14 years65, and paediatric brain and central nervous system cancers rank second globally for both incidence and mortality within this age group66. Brain cancer incidence also increases with age in humans and in Australia, incidence rates among those aged 80 years and older were 24 cases per 100,000 people in 202167. While some dog breeds show elevated risk, most human brain tumours have no recognised inherited predisposition at the population level68, underscoring species differences in susceptibility.

Among breed categories, utility breeds (such as boxers, bullmastiffs, schnauzers) had the highest incidence of brain tumours (14.3 per 100,000 dog-years), followed by terriers (10.8), non-sporting breeds (such as bulldogs, French bulldogs, poodles, chow chows, dalmatians) (8.9), and crossbreeds (8.2). These findings are consistent with previous studies reporting that boxers and terriers are frequently affected by glial tumours53, while higher rates of brain tumours have been reported in golden retrievers, schnauzers, bulldogs, and boxers9. Song et al.2 also noted increased prevalence of primary brain tumours in crossbreeds, golden retrievers, schnauzers, boxers, and bulldogs. Variation in incidence across breeds likely reflects genetic predisposition, breed-specific anatomy, and inherited risk factors that elevate susceptibility in certain pedigree populations. The current findings support that underlying heritable determinants play a role in tumour development. This pattern is consistent with evidence that some breeds have a disproportionately higher risk for specific tumour types, indicating substantial genetic contribution to susceptibility69.

Spatial analysis revealed geographic variation in primary and secondary brain tumour occurrence across Australia. New South Wales had the highest incidence rate 2.3 per 100,000 dog-years), followed by the ACT (2.2), Victoria (1.4), and QLD (1.2). In contrast, cases in Queensland were predominantly concentrated in coastal regions. These spatial differences may reflect geographic variations in dog populations. In addition, the uneven representation of clinics within the VetCompass Australia network may have influenced the spatial distribution of recorded brain tumour cases. Consequently, observed geographic hotspots should be interpreted cautiously, as they may reflect differences in dog population structures and veterinary access by dog owners, as well as variations in clinic density rather than true brain tumour risk13,27.

The temporal trend analysis of primary and secondary brain tumour incidence revealed considerable variation over the study period. Incidence increased from 0.6 cases per 100,000 dog-years at risk in 2001 to 3.9 cases per 100,000 dog-years at risk in 2015. This upward trend may partly reflect improvements in diagnostic technologies, clinical awareness, and reporting practices rather than a true increase in disease occurrence. Over the same period, the number of participating veterinary clinics within the VCA program increased, potentially enhancing case detection and ascertainment. Advances in diagnostic imaging and clinical have improved the differentiation of neoplastic from non-neoplastic brain lesions and facilitated more accurate tumour classification and grading70–73. Consequently, lesions that may previously have remained undetected or misclassified are now more likely to be diagnosed as brain tumours. Improved data recording and increasing completeness of records in the VCA database may also have contributed to the observed trend. Therefore, the increase in incidence should be interpreted cautiously, as it may partly reflect improved case ascertainment rather than a true increase in disease occurrence.

Kulldorff’s spatial scan analysis identified a significant cluster of primary and secondary brain tumour cases in NSW (RR = 5.8, p = 0.001), together with two significant clusters of unconfirmed brain tumour cases. These findings suggest geographic variations in brain tumour occurrence; however, the clusters should be interpreted carefully given potential influences of dog population distribution, clinic participation in VCA and diagnostic access by dog owners.

The cluster analysis identified a grouping of glioma and lymphosarcoma cases with particular breeds, including French bulldogs and boerboels. Within this cluster, 93.1% of dogs were diagnosed with glioma. French Bulldogs comprised 13.8% of the cluster, while Australian Bulldogs, Pugs, Rottweilers, and Beagles each accounted for 6.9%. These findings suggest a greater representation of brachycephalic breeds within the glioma-associated cluster, consistent with previous studies reporting increased glioma occurrence in short-skulled breeds74,75. Secondary brain adenocarcinoma clustered with Australian cattle dogs and Irish terriers, while glioblastoma clustered with Moodle dogs, a Maltese-poodle crossbreed. In cluster 5, English springer spaniels were grouped with nasal lymphoma (v-test ≥ 2, p < 0.05). As the MCA/HCPC approach is exploratory, these findings should be interpreted as patterns of co-occurrence rather than direct measures of breed-specific risk. Nevertheless, previous studies have reported increased susceptibility to certain cancers among some pedigree breeds, suggesting that genetic and morphological factors may contribute to observed brain tumour patterns69.

The limited treatment options for many brain tumours, particularly those unsuitable for surgery, likely contribute to the survival differences observed across tumour types. Median survival times for glioma, astrocytoma, and pituitary macroadenomas in this study were shorter than those reported in studies involving advanced interventions such as surgical debulking or radiotherapy, which generally result in longer survival12,76,77. In contrast, the median survival for meningioma was higher than those reported by Heidner et al.78 and Rossmeisl et al.58, but remained lower than the 386 days reported by Forward et al.54 following surgical resection. These difference highlights how treatment modalities influence outcomes, and multiple studies have demonstrated significantly improved survival following radiotherapy for canine brain tumour cases79–81.

In addition to treatment effects, biological factors may also contribute to the observed variation in reported survival times. Smaller breeds typically living longer than larger breeds, and this interaction between body size, morphology, and longevity may partly explain extended survival in some cohorts82. When examining survival across tumour types, glioma showed the highest mortality risk compared with meningioma, consistent with findings by Jose-Lopez et al.76 and Rossmeisl et al.58, who noted poor prognoses in dogs with glioma. Overall, survival estimates should be interpreted with caution, as they may be influenced by treatment availability, access to specialist care, owner decision-making, and determining whether dogs are euthanised at or shortly after diagnosis.

Among the animal-level risk factor assessed for survival following tumour diagnosis, neutered dogs exhibited a slightly higher risk of death than intact dogs (HR = 1.1; 95% CI: 0.5–2.3; p > 0.05), contrasting with Rossmeisl et al.58, who reported reduced primary brain tumour risk in spayed females. Such discrepancies likely reflect differences in study designs, treatment pathways, and the distribution of sex and neuter status across populations. Overall, canine brain tumours generally carry a poor prognosis57,78, often resulting in euthanasia as clinical signs progress78. Although neuter status has not been directly linked to primary brain tumour risk, it has been associated with increased risk of other cancers, including hemangiosarcoma, lymphoma, osteosarcoma and mast cell tumours, which may influence broader survival patterns in dogs83.

In the analysis of animal-level risk factors for tumour incidence, male dogs had a higher incidence of both confirmed and unconfirmed brain tumours than females, a finding that contrast with Kishimoto et al.9, who reported similar prevalence between sexes in Japan. This discrepancy may reflect differences in study design, population characteristics, or the influence of reproductive status on disease occurrence8. Among breed categories, utility breeds (e.g., boxer, bullmastiff, akita, schnauzer, rottweiler) had an incidence rate 3.9 times higher than that of crossbreed dogs (p ≤ 0.001), while terrier breeds showed a 1.8-fold higher (p < 0.05). These findings are consistent with previous studies reporting increased occurrence of brain tumours in breeds such as boxers, French bulldogs, rat terriers, and Boston terriers2.

Age showed a strong positive association with brain tumour incidence in confirmed cases. Compared with dogs aged 1–3 years, those aged 4–6 years had IRRs of 3.0, while IRRs increased to 6.4, 7.0, and 4.1 for dogs aged 7–9, 10–12 and ≥ 13 years, respectively (p ≤ 0.001). This age-related pattern is consistent with previous studies reporting a higher occurrence of brain tumour in middle-aged and older dogs2,8,9. Differences across studies may reflect variation in tumour types, study populations, and their underlying demographic characteristics. Biologically, the strong age effect may reflect the cumulative impact of genetic and environmental damage, reduced DNA repair capacity, declining immune surveillance, and prolonged exposure to carcinogenic factor in older dogs84.

In the current study, a GPT-4o model accessed through the OpenAI Batch API was used to process a large dataset of VCA clinical records. This AI-assisted approach enabled structured extraction of tumour classification and related variables from unstructured diagnostic text, demonstrating the potential of large language models to support scalable and efficient retrospective research. However, careful validation remains essential to minimise misclassification and ensure data reliability when using AI approaches and had been implemented in this study.

This study has several limitations. VCA dogs were used as the denominator population because no comprehensive state- or SA2-level dog population registry exists in Australia, therefore, findings may not be fully representative of the wider Australian dog population. In addition, differences in diagnostic access, referral patterns, and clinic participation may have influenced the observed incidence, spatial patterns, and temporal trends.

The findings of this study have practical implications for both veterinarians and pet owners. A clear understanding of canine brain tumour incidence, spatio-temporal patterns, tumour type-breed-cluster, time to death, and risk factors for both, incidence of brain tumours and survival following diagnosis can support earlier recognition of brain tumours, enhance informed decision-making by clinicians, and the application of appropriate management strategies. Importantly, the ability to identify areas with higher incidence and spatial clustering can guide targeted clinical attention and resource allocation, ultimately improving pet welfare.

In conclusion, this study provides the first nationwide description of the incidence, spatio-temporal patterns, and survival outcomes of canine brain tumours in Australia using VCA data from 1994 to 2024. Future research should build upon the VCA registry to further investigate these patterns and refine prognostic models. Establishing a standardized, population-based canine registry would enhance the accuracy of representativeness, and generalizability of epidemiological analyses across Australia.
