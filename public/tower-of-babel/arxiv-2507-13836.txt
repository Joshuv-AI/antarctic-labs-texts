Newton’s method for nonlinear mappings into vector bundles Part II: Application to Variational Problems
Laura Weigl, Ronny Bergmann, Anton Schiela

Abstract
. We consider the solution of variational equations on manifolds by Newton’s method. These problems can be expressed as root finding problems for mappings from infinite dimensional manifolds into dual vector bundles. We derive the differential geometric tools needed for the realization of Newton’s method, equipped with an affine covariant damping strategy. We apply Newton’s method to a couple of variational problems and show numerical results.



2020 Mathematics Subject Classification
49Q99

1.
Introduction

Newton’s method is one of the standard algorithms for solving nonlinear problems numerically. It can be formulated for mappings between very general spaces, classically between Banach spaces, and is thus applicable in particular to problems, involving nonlinear partial differential equations (PDEs). Many PDEs are formulated in weak form as variational problems. Here, we are looking for solutions of the equation
F(x)=0
, where
F\colon X\to E^{*}
maps between a Banach space
X
and a dual space
E^{*}
. With the help of test functions
e\in E
this can be written equivalently as

(1)

\text{find }x\in X\text{ such that}\quad F(x)e=0\quad\text{ for all }e\in E.

Some, but not all, variational problems arise from an energy principle of the form
\min_{x\in X}f(x)
, resulting in a stationarity condition of the form
f^{\prime}(x)=0
with
f^{\prime}\colon X\to X^{*}
.

The class of variational problems, however, is not restricted to mappings between linear spaces. Plenty of interesting problems involve nonlinear spaces, in particular, differentiable manifolds. One of the most well known is the problem of finding a geodesic that connects two given points by minimizing its Dirichlet energy among all connecting curves
\gamma\colon[0,T]\to\mathcal{M}
on a Riemannian manifold
\mathcal{M}
:

(2)

\min_{\gamma}\int_{[0,T]}\frac{1}{2}\lVert\dot{\gamma}(t)\rVert^{2}_{\gamma(t)}\,dt,\quad\gamma(0)=\gamma_{0},\gamma(T)=\gamma_{T}.

More generally, harmonic maps minimize a similar energy functional of mappings, defined on more general domains (cf.
[
19
]
). Other important classes of examples are found in continuum mechanics
[
24
]
, including cosserat materials
[
28
]
and models of inextensible solids
[
27
]
, in micro-magnetism, or in density functional theory
[
3
]
, to name just a few, see also
[
6
,
18
]
for an account on the numerical analysis.
In this case, an energy principle of the form
\min_{x\in\mathcal{X}}f(x)
, where
\mathcal{X}
is a differentiable, possibly infinite dimensional manifold, will lead to the equation
f^{\prime}(x)=0
, where
f^{\prime}\colon\mathcal{X}\to T^{*}\mathcal{X}
maps into the cotangent bundle
T^{*}\mathcal{X}
of
\mathcal{X}
.
Slightly more generally, we can formulate variational problems via mappings
F\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{E}^{*}
, where
\mathcal{E}
is a vector bundle, and
\mathcal{E}^{*}
its so-called dual bundle. Then our problem is of the form (which we will elaborate in detail, below):

(3)

\text{find }x\in\mathcal{X}\text{ such that }\quad F(x)e=0\quad\text{ for all }e\in E_{y(x)},

where
E_{y(x)}\subset\mathcal{E}
is the appropriate linear space of test functions, which depends on
x
. In contrast to (
1
), determining the correct space of test functions is part of the problem. In our example of a geodesic,
\mathcal{X}
is a Sobolev manifold of mappings into
\mathcal{M}
, and the variational equation is written as follows:

(4)

0=\int_{[0,T]}\langle\dot{\gamma}(t),\dot{\delta\gamma}(t)\rangle_{\gamma(t)}\,dt\quad\text{ for all }\delta\gamma\in T_{\gamma}\mathcal{X}.

In this work we consider the application of Newton’s method to problems of the form (
3
). We build upon the results of
[
35
]
, where Newton’s method for mappings from a manifold
\mathcal{X}
into a vector bundle
\mathcal{E}
has been introduced and analyzed.
One of the main outcomes of this work is that a linear connection on
\mathcal{E}
is required to render Newton directions well defined.
This generalizes the well known insight that covariant derivatives have to be employed in Newton methods for finding zeros of vector fields on Riemannian manifolds (cf. e.g.
[
15
,
4
,
14
,
23
]
) or one-forms
[
33
,
32
]
. For an account on Newton’s method for vector fields and further literature we refer to
[
2
, Section 6]
.
In the semismooth context, Newton’s method for
vector fields on Riemannian manifolds has already been discussed in
[
13
,
11
,
31
]
. Newton’s method for shape optimization was considered in
[
30
]
.

To obtain convergence from remote initial guesses, Newton’s method is typically equipped with a globalization strategy.
Especially in Newton’s method applied in optimization on Riemannian manifolds some strategies including damping and trust region methods were introduced, see e.g.
[
9
,
1
]
. In
[
35
, Sec. 6]
we presented an affine-covariant damping scheme. Here, a vector transport on
\mathcal{E}
is needed in addition, which has to be consistent with the connection in a certain sense. In the current paper, we will discuss, how these concepts can be implemented, if the co-domain of our mapping is a dual vector bundle, so that variational equations on manifolds can be solved.

Our paper is structured as follows: we start by a brief recapitulation of the main differential geometric concepts, required to define Newton steps for mappings into general vector bundles in Section
3
.
We consider the specific case of Newton’s method for mappings into dual vector bundles, and discuss the differential geometric tools, including dual connections, necessary to define Newton’s method for variational equations.
This includes an affine covariant damping strategy that we present for dual vector bundles in Section
4
. As an important special case we consider embedded submanifolds in detail.
Finally, we turn to a couple of examples in Section
6
, two of which can be seen as a generalization of the geodesic problem, namely elastic geodesics in a force field and with obstacle avoidance. Finally, as an example from continuum mechanics we consider the simulation of an elastic inextensible rod.

Preliminaries

In this section we recall some notations, definitions and basic properties of differential geometry and from
[
35
]
used throughout the work.
Let
\mathcal{X}
be a
Banach manifold
, i. e. a topological space
\mathcal{X}
and a collection of charts
(U,\phi)
, where each chart
\phi\colon U\to\mathbb{X}
maps an open subset
U
of
\mathcal{X}
homeomorphically into a real Banach space
\mathbb{X}
with norm
\|\cdot\|_{\mathbb{X}}
.
Unless otherwise noted we will assume that the manifold is of class
C^{1}
, cf.
[
21
, II, §1]
.
We call mappings
F\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{M}
between two Banach manifolds
continuous, differentiable
or
locally Lipschitz
, if their representations in charts, i. e.
\phi_{\mathcal{M}}\circ F\circ\phi_{\mathcal{X}}^{-1}
, have the respective property.
It can be shown that these properties are independent of the choice of charts.
The
tangent spaces
of a manifold
\mathcal{X}
, denoted by
T_{x}\mathcal{X}
for
x\in\mathcal{X}
, can be defined by using charts, see, e.g.,
[
21
, II §2]
. We will equip each
T_{x}\mathcal{X}
with a norm that is equivalent to
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{\mathbb{X}}
and thus renders
T_{x}\mathcal{X}
a Banach space. In
[
35
, Sec. 5.1]
we showed that, if the mapping
x\mapsto\lVert\cdot\rVert_{x}
is sufficiently regular, a construction similar to the one, performed on Riemannian manifolds, leads to a metric distance on
\mathcal{X}
that can be used to study convergence of Newton’s method.

A map
F\colon\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathcal{M}
between two
C^{1}
-manifolds
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{M}
is called
continuously differentiable
, if its representation in charts has the same property. Then this mapping induces a linear map
F^{\prime}(x)\colon T_{x}\mathcal{X}\rightarrow T_{F(x)}\mathcal{M}
, termed
tangent map
or
derivative
of
F
at any
x\in\mathcal{X}

[
21
]
.
A map
W\colon X\rightarrow Y
between normed linear spaces is called Newton-differentiable (or semi-smooth) (cf.
[
34
,
25
,
26
]
) at
x_{*}\in X
with respect to a Newton-derivative
W^{\prime}\colon X\to L(X,Y)
, if it satisfies the following property:

\lim_{x\to x_{*}}\frac{\lVert W^{\prime}(x)(x-x_{*})-(W(x)-W(x_{*}))\rVert_{Y}}{\lVert x-x_{*}\rVert_{X}}=0.

A map
F\colon\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathcal{M}
between two
C^{1}
-manifolds is called
Newton-differentiable
(cf.
[
35
, Def. 4.4]
), if its representation in charts is.

Vector bundles.

Consider a
vector bundle

(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{Y},p)
, which will be assumed to be of class
C^{1}
, unless otherwise stated.
Its
bundle projection

p\colon\mathcal{E}\rightarrow\mathcal{Y}
is a surjective
C^{1}
-map, which assigns to
e\in\mathcal{E}
from the
total space

\mathcal{E}
its base point
y=p(e)
in the
base manifold

\mathcal{Y}

[
21
, III §1]
.
Since the bundle projection contains all necessary information, we will use the mapping
p\colon\mathcal{E}\to\mathcal{Y}
or simply
\mathcal{E}
, if the context is clear, to denote the vector bundle in the following. Vector bundles can be seen as manifolds with special structure: for each point
y\in\mathcal{Y}
on the base manifold the so-called
fibre

E_{y}\coloneqq p^{-1}(y)
over
y
is a topological vector space whose topology is induced by some norm, rendering
E_{y}
complete. The zero element in
E_{y}
is denoted by
0_{y}
.
A mapping
v\colon\mathcal{Y}\to\mathcal{E}
, which is a right inverse of the vector bundle projection, i. e.
p(v(y))=y
for all
y\in\mathcal{Y}
, is called
section
of
\mathcal{E}
. The set of all sections is denoted by
\Gamma(\mathcal{E})
. The most prominent example for a vector bundle is the tangent bundle
\pi\colon T\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{X}
of a manifold
\mathcal{X}
with fibres
T_{x}\mathcal{X}
. A section of
T\mathcal{X}
is called a
vector field
.

Vector bundles can be described via
local trivializations
. For a Banach space
(\mathbb{E},\lVert\cdot\rVert_{\mathbb{E}})
and an open set
U\subset\mathcal{Y}
, these are diffeomorphisms
\tau\colon p^{-1}(U)\to U\times\mathbb{E}
, such that
\tau_{y}\coloneqq\tau|_{E_{y}}\in L(E_{y},\mathbb{E})
are linear isomorphisms.
Thus, any element of
e\in\mathcal{E}
can be represented as a pair
(y,e_{\tau})\in\mathcal{Y}\times\mathbb{E}
where
y=p(e)
is the base point and the fibre part
e_{\tau}
depends on the trivialization. For two trivializations
\tau
and
\tilde{\tau}
around
y\in\mathcal{Y}
we can define smooth transition mappings
A\colon y\mapsto\tilde{\tau}_{y}\tau_{y}^{-1}
with
A(y)\in L(\mathbb{E},\mathbb{E})
, and obtain
e_{\tilde{\tau}}=A(y)e_{\tau}
.

Consider the tangent space
T_{e}\mathcal{E}
of the vector bundle
\mathcal{E}
at
e
.
Similar to vector bundle elements, every element
\delta e\in T_{e}\mathcal{E}
can be represented in local trivializations as a pair of elements
(\delta y,\delta e_{\tau})\in T_{y}\mathcal{Y}\times\mathbb{E}
, where the tangential part
\delta y=p^{\prime}(e)\delta e
is given canonically.
The representation
\delta e_{\tau}
of the fibre part, however, depends crucially on the chosen trivialization: if
e_{\tilde{\tau}}=A(y)e_{\tau}
, then
\delta e_{\tilde{\tau}}=A(y)\delta e_{\tau}+(A^{\prime}(y)\delta y)e_{\tau}
by the product rule.
In particular, there is no natural splitting of
\delta e
into a tangential part and a fibre part.
For a vector bundle
p\colon\mathcal{E}\to\mathcal{Y}
we can consider the corresponding
dual vector bundle

p^{*}\colon\mathcal{E}^{*}\to\mathcal{Y}
which occurs naturally in many applications.
Its fibres are given by
E^{*}_{y}=(p^{*})^{-1}(y)=L(E_{y},\mathbb{R})
, i. e. the dual space of
E_{y}
. A prominent example is the cotangent bundle
T^{*}\mathcal{X}
of a manifold
\mathcal{X}
which is the dual bundle of the tangent bundle
T\mathcal{X}
.

Given a mapping
s\colon\mathcal{Y}_{1}\to\mathcal{Y}_{2}
between two manifolds and a vector bundle
p_{2}\colon\mathcal{E}_{2}\to\mathcal{Y}_{2}
, we denote the
pullback bundle

s^{*}\mathcal{E}_{2}\coloneqq\{(x,e)\in\mathcal{Y}_{1}\times\mathcal{E}_{2}\colon s(x)=p_{2}(e)\}
of
\mathcal{E}_{2}
via
s
by
s^{*}p\colon s^{*}\mathcal{E}_{2}\to\mathcal{Y}_{1}
. Its fibres are given by
(s^{*}E_{2})_{x}\coloneqq\{x\}\times E_{2,s(x)}
.

Fibrewise linear mappings.

Consider two vector bundles
p_{1}\colon\mathcal{E}_{1}\to\mathcal{Y}_{1}
,
p_{2}\colon\mathcal{E}_{2}\to\mathcal{Y}_{2}
. Let
y\in\mathcal{Y}_{1}
and
z\in\mathcal{Y}_{2}
and denote

L(E_{1,y},E_{2,z})\coloneqq\{l\colon E_{1,y}\to E_{2,z}\;|\;l\text{ continuous and linear}\}.

Given a mapping
s\colon\mathcal{Y}_{1}\to\mathcal{Y}_{2}
we can collect all sets of linear mappings
L(E_{1,y},E_{2,s(y)})
for all
y\in\mathcal{Y}_{1}
in a set
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{E}_{1},s^{*}\mathcal{E}_{2})
.
We observe that
p_{\mathcal{L}}\colon\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{E}_{1},s^{*}\mathcal{E}_{2})\to\mathcal{Y}_{1}
is a vector bundle itself with fibres
L(E_{1,y},E_{2,s(y)})
.
In trivializations an element
H
of
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{E}_{1},s^{*}\mathcal{E}_{2})
can be represented by a pair
(y,H_{\tau})
where
y\in\mathcal{Y}_{1}
and
H_{\tau}\in L(\mathbb{E}_{1},\mathbb{E}_{2})
. If the co-domain is a fixed fibre, i.e,
E_{z}\cong\{z\}\times E_{z}
, we use the simplified notation
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{E}_{1},E_{z})
instead of the cumbersome
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{E}_{1},s_{z}^{*}(\{z\}\times E_{z}))
, where
s_{z}\colon\mathcal{Y}_{1}\to\{z\}
is the constant map.
A section
S\in\Gamma(\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{E}_{1},s^{*}\mathcal{E}_{2}))
of the vector bundle
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{E}_{1},s^{*}\mathcal{E}_{2})
, i.e, a mapping

S\colon\mathcal{Y}_{1}\to\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{E}_{1},s^{*}\mathcal{E}_{2})\text{ with }p_{\mathcal{L}}\circ S=Id_{\mathcal{Y}_{1}}.

is called
fibrewise linear mapping
, also known as
vector bundle morphism
, see, e.g.,
[
21
, III §1]
.

S
is called
locally bounded
, respectively
differentiable
, if its representation in local trivializations

(5)

S_{\tau}\colon U\to L(\mathbb{E}_{1},\mathbb{E}_{2}),\;S_{\tau}(y)=\tau_{\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{E}_{1},s^{*}\mathcal{E}_{2})}(S)\coloneqq\tau_{\mathcal{E}_{2},s(y)}\circ S(y)\circ\tau_{\mathcal{E}_{1},y}^{-1}\in L(\mathbb{E}_{1},\mathbb{E}_{2})

has the respective property.

If
S
is differentiable, then its derivative is a section
S^{\prime}\in\Gamma(T\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{E}_{1},s^{*}\mathcal{E}_{2}))
of the tangent bundle
p_{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}\colon T\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{E}_{1},s^{*}\mathcal{E}_{2})\to T\mathcal{Y}_{1}
, i.e., a mapping

S^{\prime}\colon T\mathcal{Y}_{1}\to T\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{E}_{1},s^{*}\mathcal{E}_{2}),\text{ such that }p_{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}\circ S^{\prime}=Id_{T\mathcal{Y}_{1}}.

From
S\in\Gamma(\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{E}_{1},s^{*}\mathcal{E}_{2}))
we can construct a mapping
{\langle{S}\rangle}\in C^{1}(\mathcal{E}_{1},\mathcal{E}_{2})
as follows:

\displaystyle{\langle{S}\rangle}\colon\mathcal{E}_{1}

\displaystyle\to\mathcal{E}_{2},\quad e\mapsto{\langle{S}\rangle}(e)\coloneqq S(p_{1}(e))e.

By this, we obtain a
natural inclusion
of the sections of the bundle of fibrewise linear mappings into the
C^{1}
-mappings:

(6)

\varphi\colon\Gamma(\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{E}_{1},s^{*}\mathcal{E}_{2}))\to C^{1}(\mathcal{E}_{1},\mathcal{E}_{2}),\quad S\mapsto{\langle{S}\rangle}.

Comparison of the derivatives
S^{\prime}\in\Gamma(T\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{E}_{1},s^{*}\mathcal{E}_{2}))
and
{\langle{S}\rangle}^{\prime}\in\Gamma(\mathcal{L}(T\mathcal{E}_{1},S^{*}T\mathcal{E}_{2}))
in trivializations yields by the product rule

(7)

{\langle{S}\rangle}_{\tau}^{\prime}(y,e_{\tau})(\delta y,\delta e_{\tau})=S_{\tau}(y)\delta e_{\tau}+(S_{\tau}^{\prime}(y)\delta y)e_{\tau}\quad\text{ for all }(\delta y,\delta e_{\tau})\in T_{y}\mathcal{Y}_{1}\times\mathbb{E}_{1}.

Example 2.1
.

Given a twice differentiable mapping
F\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{M}
, the derivative
S\coloneqq F^{\prime}\in\Gamma(\mathcal{L}(T\mathcal{X},F^{*}T\mathcal{M}))
is a fibrewise linear mapping with
\langle F^{\prime}\rangle\in C^{1}(T\mathcal{X},T\mathcal{M})
.

Vector transports.

Special fibrewise linear mappings are the so-called
vector transports
.

Definition 2.2
.

For a vector bundle
p\colon\mathcal{E}\rightarrow\mathcal{Y}
and
y\in\mathcal{Y}
we define a
vector transport
as a section
{\mathop{V}\limits^{\vbox to-1.05487pt{\kern-2.0pt\hbox{\!$\scriptscriptstyle\rightarrow$}}}}_{\!\!y}\in\Gamma(\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{Y}\times E_{y},\mathcal{E}))
, i.e.

{\mathop{V}\limits^{\vbox to-1.05487pt{\kern-2.0pt\hbox{\!$\scriptscriptstyle\rightarrow$}}}}_{\!\!y}\colon\mathcal{Y}\to\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{Y}\times E_{y},\mathcal{E}),\ \text{with }p_{\mathcal{L}}({\mathop{V}\limits^{\vbox to-1.05487pt{\kern-2.0pt\hbox{\!$\scriptscriptstyle\rightarrow$}}}}_{\!\!y}(\hat{y}))=\hat{y}\;\text{ for all }\hat{y}\in\mathcal{Y}

with the properties that
{\mathop{V}\limits^{\vbox to-1.05487pt{\kern-2.0pt\hbox{\!$\scriptscriptstyle\rightarrow$}}}}_{\!\!y}(y)=Id_{E_{y}}
and
{\mathop{V}\limits^{\vbox to-1.05487pt{\kern-2.0pt\hbox{\!$\scriptscriptstyle\rightarrow$}}}}_{\!\!y}(\hat{y})
is invertible for all
\hat{y}\in\mathcal{Y}
.

We define a
vector back-transport
as a section
{\mathop{V}\limits^{\vbox to-1.05487pt{\kern-2.0pt\hbox{\!$\scriptscriptstyle\leftarrow$}}}}_{\!\!y}\in\Gamma(\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{E},E_{y}))
, i.e.

{\mathop{V}\limits^{\vbox to-1.05487pt{\kern-2.0pt\hbox{\!$\scriptscriptstyle\leftarrow$}}}}_{\!\!y}\colon\mathcal{Y}\to\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{E},E_{y}),\ \text{with }p_{\mathcal{L}}({\mathop{V}\limits^{\vbox to-1.05487pt{\kern-2.0pt\hbox{\!$\scriptscriptstyle\leftarrow$}}}}_{\!\!y}(\hat{y}))=\hat{y}\; \text{ for all }\hat{y}\in\mathcal{Y}

with the property that
{\mathop{V}\limits^{\vbox to-1.05487pt{\kern-2.0pt\hbox{\!$\scriptscriptstyle\leftarrow$}}}}_{\!\!y}(y)=Id_{E_{y}}
and
{\mathop{V}\limits^{\vbox to-1.05487pt{\kern-2.0pt\hbox{\!$\scriptscriptstyle\leftarrow$}}}}_{\!\!y}(\hat{y})
is invertible for all
\hat{y}\in\mathcal{Y}
.

In the literature vector transports are often defined as mappings
V(\cdot,\cdot)\colon\mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{Y}\to\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{E})
with
V(y,\hat{y})\in L(E_{y},E_{\hat{y}})
. These definitions admit vector transports as well as vector back-transports by
{\mathop{V}\limits^{\vbox to-1.05487pt{\kern-2.0pt\hbox{\!$\scriptscriptstyle\rightarrow$}}}}_{\!\!y}(\hat{y})=V(y,\hat{y})={\mathop{V}\limits^{\vbox to-1.05487pt{\kern-2.0pt\hbox{\!$\scriptscriptstyle\leftarrow$}}}}_{\!\!\hat{y}}(y)
. Nevertheless, the derivatives of these two objects show subtle, but important differences.

Connections on vector bundles.

The concept of a
connection
on a vector bundle
p\colon\mathcal{E}\to\mathcal{Y}
is fundamental to differential geometry. It adds a geometric structure imposed on
\mathcal{E}
that describes in an infinitesimal way, how neighboring fibres are related. Considering the tangent bundle
T\mathcal{X}
of a Riemannian manifold
\mathcal{X}
the most prominent example is the
Levi-Civita connection
(see, e.g.,
[
21
, VIII §4]
).
Connections give rise to further concepts like the covariant derivative, curvature, geodesics, and parallel transports.
They are described in the literature in various equivalent ways (cf., e.g.,
[
21
, IV §3 or X §4 or XIII]
).
Here we choose a formulation that emphasizes the idea of a connection map
Q_{e}\colon T_{e}\mathcal{E}\to E_{p(e)}
, which then induces a splitting of
T_{e}\mathcal{E}
into a vertical and a horizontal subspace.
Using the bundle projection
p\colon\mathcal{E}\to\mathcal{Y}
, it is well known that the kernel of the derivative
p^{\prime}\in\Gamma(\mathcal{L}(T\mathcal{E},p^{*}T\mathcal{Y}))
canonically defines the
vertical subbundle

V\mathcal{E}
of
\mathcal{E}

[
21
, IV §3]
.
Its fibres are closed linear subspaces, called the
vertical subspaces

\mathrm{Vert}_{e}\coloneqq\ker p^{\prime}(e)\subset T_{e}\mathcal{E}
.
We can identify
E_{p(e)}\cong\mathrm{Vert}_{e}
canonically by the isomorphism

|_{E_{p(e)}}\colon E_{p(e)}\rightarrow\mathrm{Vert}_{e}
, given by
w\mapsto\frac{d}{dt}(e+tw)|_{t=0}
. Elements of
\mathrm{Vert}_{e}
are represented in trivializations by pairs of the form
(0_{y},\delta e_{\tau})\in T_{y}\mathcal{Y}\times\mathbb{E}
.
We define a
connection map
as a section

Q\in\Gamma(\mathcal{L}(T\mathcal{E},p^{*}\mathcal{E}))
, i.e., for each
e\in\mathcal{E}
we get a continuous linear map
Q_{e}\in L(T_{e}\mathcal{E},E_{p(e)})
,
with the additional property that
Q_{e}|_{E_{p(e)}}=Id_{E_{p(e)}}
.
The kernels
\mathrm{Hor}_{e}\coloneqq\ker Q_{e}
are called
horizontal subspaces
, collected in the
horizontal subbundle

H\mathcal{E}
.
While
V\mathcal{E}
is given canonically, a choice of
Q
(or equivalently
H\mathcal{E}
), imposes additional geometric structure on
\mathcal{E}
. Using the identification
\mathrm{Vert}_{e}\cong E_{p(e)}
, we can view
Q
as a fibrewise “projection” onto
\mathcal{E}
, with
\langle Q\rangle\colon T\mathcal{E}\rightarrow\mathcal{E}
.
In trivializations
(y,e_{\tau})\in\mathcal{Y}\times\mathbb{E}
for
e\in\mathcal{E}
and
(\delta y,\delta e_{\tau})\in T_{y}\mathcal{Y}\times\mathbb{E}
for
\delta e\in T_{e}\mathcal{E}
, the representation for a connection map
Q
can be written in the form

(8)

Q_{e}\delta e\sim Q_{e,\tau}(\delta y,\delta e_{\tau})=\delta e_{\tau}-B_{y,\tau}(e_{\tau})\delta y,

where
B_{y,\tau}\colon\mathbb{E}\to L(T_{y}\mathcal{Y},\mathbb{E})
assigns a linear mapping
B_{y,\tau}(e_{\tau})
to each
e_{\tau}\in\mathbb{E}
.
Since
Q_{e,\tau}(0_{y},\delta e_{\tau})=\delta e_{\tau}
, we see that
Q_{e,\tau}
indeed represents a projection onto
\mathrm{Vert}_{e}
.
To reflect the linearity of the fibres we want the connection to be
linear
(fibrewise with respect to
e
). To this end, we consider the fibrewise scaling
{m_{s}\colon\mathcal{E}\rightarrow\mathcal{E},e\mapsto se}
and require the condition
Q\circ m_{s}^{\prime}=m_{s}\circ Q
for all
s\in\mathbb{R}
,
which reads in trivializations:

Q_{se,\tau}(\delta y,s\delta e_{\tau})=sQ_{e,\tau}(\delta y,\delta e_{\tau})\quad\text{ for all }s\in\mathbb{R}.

It can be shown that
Q
is linear, if and only if the mapping
e_{\tau}\mapsto B_{y,\tau}(e_{\tau})
is linear, or put differently, the mapping
(\delta y,e_{\tau})\mapsto B_{y,\tau}(e_{\tau})\delta y
is bilinear.
If
\mathcal{E}=T\mathcal{X}
in classical Riemannian geometry, where
Q
is given by the Levi-Civita connection, the bilinear mapping is represented by Christoffel symbols, and it is symmetric.
As we have seen in
[
35
, Lemma 2.2]
connection maps
Q_{e}
at
e\in\mathcal{E}
can be derived from differentiable vector back-transports
{\mathop{V}\limits^{\vbox to-1.05487pt{\kern-2.0pt\hbox{\!$\scriptscriptstyle\leftarrow$}}}}_{\!\!p(e)}
by setting
Q_{e}={\langle{{\mathop{V}\limits^{\vbox to-1.05487pt{\kern-2.0pt\hbox{\!$\scriptscriptstyle\leftarrow$}}}}_{\!\!p(e)}}\rangle}^{\prime}(e)
.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
e\in\mathcal{E}
and
y=p(e)
. We call a vector back-transport
{\mathop{V}\limits^{\vbox to-1.05487pt{\kern-2.0pt\hbox{\!$\scriptscriptstyle\leftarrow$}}}}_{\!\!y}\in\Gamma(\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{E},E_{y}))

consistent
with a connection map
Q\in\Gamma(\mathcal{L}(T\mathcal{E},p^{*}\mathcal{E}))
at

Q_{e}={\langle{{\mathop{V}\limits^{\vbox to-1.05487pt{\kern-2.0pt\hbox{\!$\scriptscriptstyle\leftarrow$}}}}_{\!\!y}}\rangle}^{\prime}(e)
.

Newton directions for mappings into vector bundles.

Consider a Newton-differentiable mapping
F\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{E}
between a Banach manifold
\mathcal{X}
and a vector bundle
p\colon\mathcal{E}\to\mathcal{Y}
with Newton-derivative
F^{\prime}\colon T\mathcal{X}\to T\mathcal{E}
. By the composition

(9)

y\coloneqq p\circ F\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y},\quad y(x)\coloneqq p(F(x))\in\mathcal{Y},

we can compute the base point
y(x)\in\mathcal{Y}
of
F(x)\in\mathcal{E}
for given
x\in\mathcal{X}
.
In
[
35
]
, we presented a Newton method to solve the following root finding problem:

F(x)=0_{{y(x)}}.

In contrast to classical root finding problems, the linear space
E_{y(x)}
in which
F(x)
is evaluated now depends on the iterate
x\in\mathcal{X}
. Hence, the linear space
E_{y(x)}
changes during the iteration. We have seen that connection maps
Q\in\Gamma(\mathcal{L}(T\mathcal{E},p^{*}\mathcal{E}))
allow us to derive suitable Newton directions by solving the following linear operator equation:

(10)

Q_{F(x)}\circ F^{\prime}(x)\delta x+F(x)=0_{{y(x)}}.

If
Q_{F(x)}\circ F^{\prime}(x)
is invertible, then the Newton direction
\delta x\in T_{x}\mathcal{X}
is given as the unique solution of this equation, and
\delta x=0
holds if and only if
x
is a zero of
F
, i. e.
F(x)=0_{y(x)}
.

3.
Newton’s Method for variational equations on manifolds

In mathematical physics we frequently encounter variational problems, which often yield differential equations in weak form. If these problems are posed on manifolds,
dual vector bundles
can be used for their treatment.
In this section we discuss Newton’s method for mappings into general dual vector bundles.
To do this, we will adapt the method presented in
[
35
]
to this case.

3.1.
Dual vector bundles

Let
p\colon\mathcal{E}\to\mathcal{Y}
be a vector bundle. Consider its dual bundle
p^{*}\colon\mathcal{E}^{*}\to\mathcal{Y}
where each fibre
E^{*}_{y}=(p^{*})^{-1}(y)=L(E_{y},\mathbb{R})
is the dual space of
E_{y}
, so
\mathcal{E}^{*}=\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{E},\mathbb{R})
.
If
\tau_{\mathcal{E}}\colon\mathcal{E}\to\mathbb{E}
is a trivialization of
\mathcal{E}
with
e_{\tau}=\tau_{\mathcal{E},y}(e)
for
e\in E_{y}
, then by (
5
) dispensing with a trivialization of the co-domain
\mathbb{R}
of
\ell\in E_{y}^{*}=L(E_{y},\mathbb{R})
, we obtain a local trivialization
\tau_{\mathcal{E}^{*}}\colon\mathcal{E}^{*}\to\mathbb{E}^{*}
of the dual bundle
\mathcal{E}^{*}
by the mapping
l_{\tau}=\tau_{\mathcal{E}^{*}}(l)\coloneqq l\circ\tau_{\mathcal{E},y}^{-1}
, so that for
e\in E_{y}
with representation
e_{\tau}
:

(11)

l_{\tau}(e_{\tau})=l(e).

We denote the fibrewise dual pairing for
\ell\in\Gamma(\mathcal{E}^{*})
and
e\in\Gamma(\mathcal{E})
by
\ell(e)\colon\mathcal{Y}\rightarrow\mathbb{R},\;y\mapsto\ell(y)e(y)\in\mathbb{R}
. Its derivative at a point
y\in\mathcal{Y}
is given by
\ell(e)^{\prime}(y)\in L(T_{y}\mathcal{Y},\mathbb{R})
.

3.2.
Variational problems on manifolds

Consider a mapping
F\colon\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathcal{E}^{*}
between a Banach manifold
\mathcal{X}
and a dual vector bundle
p^{*}\colon\mathcal{E}^{*}\to\mathcal{Y}
. According to (
9
) we denote the base point of
F(x)
by
y(x)=p^{*}(F(x))
for every
x\in\mathcal{X}
. Then we would like to find the solution of the following root finding problem:

F(x)=0_{y(x)}^{*}.

Since this is a problem in a dual space it can equivalently be stated as a
variational problem
, where
e\in E_{y(x)}
plays the role of a test function:

F(x)(e)=0\quad\text{ for all }e\in E_{y(x)}.

Example 3.1
.

Consider a twice differentiable function
f\colon\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, defined on a
C^{2}
-Banach manifold
\mathcal{X}
. In order to find a critical point of
f
, we consider the
covector field

f^{\prime}\colon\mathcal{X}\rightarrow T^{*}\mathcal{X}
which is a mapping into the cotangent bundle
\pi^{*}\colon T^{*}\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{X}
, i. e. a dual vector bundle. Then, we want to compute a point
x\in\mathcal{X}
such that

f^{\prime}(x)=0_{x}^{*}\in T_{x}\mathcal{X}^{*}.

In contrast to classical variational problems, the space of test functions
E_{y(x)}
now depends on the state
x\in\mathcal{X}
.

To derive Newton’s method for this problem, we need to define a Newton equation similar to (
10
) to derive a Newton direction
\delta x\in T_{x}\mathcal{X}
as well as an update formula for the
Newton step

x_{+}\in\mathcal{X}
. In particular, the definition of the Newton equation requires a
dual connection map
.

3.3.
Dual connection maps

Consider a connection map
Q
on the vector bundle
p\colon\mathcal{E}\to\mathcal{Y}
. Then a corresponding dual connection map
Q^{*}
on the dual vector bundle
p^{*}\colon\mathcal{E}^{*}\to\mathcal{Y}
can be defined as follows
[
22
, Chap. 4]
:

Definition 3.2
.

Let
Q
be a connection map on
p\colon\mathcal{E}\rightarrow\mathcal{Y}
.
The
dual connection map

Q^{*}\in\Gamma(\mathcal{L}(T\mathcal{E}^{*},(p^{*})^{*}\mathcal{E}^{*}))
on
p^{*}\colon\mathcal{E}^{*}\rightarrow\mathcal{Y}
corresponding to
Q
is defined by

(12)

(Q_{\ell(y)}^{*}\circ\ell^{\prime}(y)\delta y)(e(y))\coloneqq\ell(e)^{\prime}(y)\delta y-\ell(y)(Q_{e(y)}\circ e^{\prime}(y)\delta y)

for all
e\in\Gamma(\mathcal{E})
,
\ell\in\Gamma(\mathcal{E}^{*})
and for all
\delta y\in T_{y}\mathcal{Y}
where
y=p(e)=p^{*}(\ell)
.

To show that this is a valid definition, we have to find a representation of
Q^{*}
in trivializations. Suppose that the connection map
Q
is represented as in (
8
) by

Q_{e}\delta e\sim Q_{e,\tau}(\delta y,\delta e_{\tau})=\delta e_{\tau}-B_{y,\tau}(e_{\tau})\delta y\in\mathbb{E}.

Recall that in trivializations
l\in\mathcal{E}^{*}
is represented by
(y,l_{\tau})\in\mathcal{Y}\times\mathbb{E}^{*}
and
\delta l\in T_{l}\mathcal{E}^{*}
by
(\delta y,\delta l_{\tau})\in T_{y}\mathcal{Y}\times\mathbb{E}^{*}
.

Proposition 3.3
.

For
l\in\mathcal{E}^{*}
and
y=p^{*}(l)
the linear mapping
Q_{l}^{*}\in L(T_{l}\mathcal{E}^{*},E_{y}^{*})
is given by

(13)

(Q^{*}_{l}\delta l)(v)=Q^{*}_{l,\tau}(\delta y,\delta l_{\tau})(v_{\tau})=\delta l_{\tau}(v_{\tau})+l_{\tau}\circ B_{y,\tau}(v_{\tau})\delta y

for all
v\in E_{y}
,
\delta l\in T_{l}\mathcal{E}^{*}
and
\delta y\in T_{y}\mathcal{Y}
.
In particular, (
12
) yields a valid definition of a connection on
\mathcal{E}^{*}
.

Proof.

Let
e\in\Gamma(\mathcal{E})
and
\ell\in\Gamma(\mathcal{E}^{*})
. We will show that (
12
) coincides with (
13
) if we set
l=\ell(y)
,
v=e(y)
and
\delta l=\ell^{\prime}(y)\delta y
.
Indeed, using the above notations and the representation of
e^{\prime}(y)\delta y
by
(\delta y,\delta e_{\tau})
we obtain by the product rule and cancellation of the term
\ell_{\tau}(\delta e_{\tau})(y)\delta y
:

\displaystyle((Q_{\ell,\tau}^{*}\circ\ell_{\tau}^{\prime})(y)\delta y)(e_{\tau}(y))

\displaystyle=\ell_{\tau}(e_{\tau})^{\prime}(y)-\ell_{\tau}(y)(\delta e_{\tau}-B_{y,\tau}(e_{\tau}(y))\delta y)

\displaystyle=\delta\ell_{\tau}(e_{\tau}(y))+\ell_{\tau}(y)(B_{y,\tau}(e_{\tau}(y))\delta y).

Thus,
(Q_{\ell(y)}^{*}\circ\ell^{\prime}(y)\delta y)(e)
does not depend on
e_{\tau}^{\prime}(y)
, but on
e_{\tau}(y)
only, so indeed
Q_{\ell(y)}^{*}\circ\ell^{\prime}(y)\delta y\in E_{y}^{*}
.
∎

Remark 3.4
.

Classically, connections are closely related to covariant derivatives:

\nabla_{\delta y}e(y)\coloneqq Q_{e(y)}\circ e^{\prime}(y)\delta y\mbox{ for }y\in\mathcal{Y},e\in\Gamma(\mathcal{E}),\delta y\in T_{y}\mathcal{Y},

in which case Definition
3.2
yields a product rule:

\ell(e)^{\prime}(y)\delta y=\nabla_{\delta y}^{*}\ell(y)(e)+\ell(\nabla_{\delta y}e(y)).

3.4.
Newton equation

With the help of a dual connection map we can now define a Newton equation similar to (
10
). Let us consider the mapping
F\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{E}^{*}
again. The tangent map of
F
is a mapping
F^{\prime}\in\Gamma(\mathcal{L}(T\mathcal{X},F^{*}T\mathcal{E}^{*}))
. Since
F^{\prime}(x)\delta x\in T_{F(x)}\mathcal{E}^{*}
and
F(x)\in E^{*}_{y(x)}
, these two quantities cannot be added, and thus the classical Newton equation
F^{\prime}(x)\delta x+F(x)=0
is not well defined.
To resolve this issue, we now use a dual connection map
Q^{*}\in\Gamma(\mathcal{L}(T\mathcal{E}^{*},p^{*}\mathcal{E}^{*}))
. With its help we can define a linear mapping at
x\in\mathcal{X}
, which maps into the suitable space
E^{*}_{y(x)}
, as follows:

Q^{*}_{F(x)}\circ F^{\prime}(x)\colon T_{x}\mathcal{X}\to E^{*}_{y(x)}.

Then the
Newton equation
is well defined as the following linear operator equation

(14)

Q_{F(x)}^{*}\circ F^{\prime}(x)\delta x+F(x)=0^{*}_{{y(x)}}

which is equivalent to

(Q_{F(x)}^{*}\circ F^{\prime}(x)\delta x+F(x))(e)=0\quad\text{ for all }e\in E_{y(x)}.

If
Q^{*}_{F(x)}\circ F^{\prime}(x)
is invertible, then the Newton direction
\delta x\in T_{x}\mathcal{X}
is given as the unique solution of this equation.
Setting
\ell=F(x)
, we obtain by application of (
13
) with representations
F^{\prime}(x)\delta x\sim(y^{\prime}(x)\delta x,(F^{\prime}(x)\delta x)_{\tau})
:

(15)

(Q_{F(x)}^{*}\circ F^{\prime}(x)\delta x)(e)=(F^{\prime}(x)\delta x)_{\tau}(e_{\tau})+F_{\tau}(x)\circ B_{y(x),\tau}(e_{\tau})y^{\prime}(x)\delta x

for all
e\in\mathcal{E}
with
e_{\tau}=\tau_{\mathcal{E},y}(e)\in\mathbb{E}
.

3.5.
Definition of Newton’s method

As we have seen in Sec.
3.4
dual connection maps can be used to obtain a well defined Newton equation (
14
):

Q^{*}_{F(x)}\circ F^{\prime}(x)\delta x+F(x)=0_{{y(x)}}^{*}.

The solution of this equation yields the Newton direction
\delta x\in T_{x}\mathcal{X}
.
Since the iterate
x\in\mathcal{X}
and the Newton direction
\delta x\in T_{x}\mathcal{X}
cannot be added, the classical additive Newton update
x_{+}=x+\delta x
is also not well defined. To obtain a new iterate
x_{+}
, we have to map
\delta x\in T_{x}\mathcal{X}
back to the manifold
\mathcal{X}
in an appropriate way. This can be done by applying a
retraction
(cf., e.g.,
[
2
, Chap. 4]
or
[
10
, Sec. 3.6]
) to the Newton direction.

Definition 3.5

(Retraction)
.

A
C^{1}
-
retraction
on a manifold
\mathcal{X}
is a
C^{1}
-mapping
R:T\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathcal{X}
, where its restriction
R_{x}\colon T_{x}\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{X}
to a fixed
x\in\mathcal{X}
satisfies the following properties:

(i)

R_{x}(0_{x})=x

(ii)

R_{x}^{\prime}(0_{x})=Id_{T_{x}\mathcal{X}}

Thus, after successfully computing the Newton direction
\delta x\in T_{x}\mathcal{X}
we use a local retraction to generate the new iterate, i. e. we perform a
Newton step
, by

x_{+}\coloneqq R_{x}(\delta x).

We summarize our results in Algorithm
1
. It can be shown that under suitable semismoothness assumptions the method converges locally with a superlinear rate. For a detailed proof see
[
35
, Prop. 5.10]
.

Algorithm 1

Newton’s method on vector bundles

0:

x_{0}
(initial guess)

for

k=0,1,2,\ldots

do

\delta x_{k}\leftarrow Q_{F(x_{k})}^{*}\circ F^{\prime}(x_{k})\delta x_{k}+F(x_{k})=0_{{y(x_{k})}}^{*}

x_{k+1}=R_{x_{k}}(\delta x_{k})

end

for

Remark 3.6
.

In the case of a minimization problem
\min_{x\in\mathcal{X}}f(x)
we obtain the variational equation
f^{\prime}(x)=0_{x}^{*}
, and the corresponding Newton equation is formulated in the cotangent space
T_{x}\mathcal{X}^{*}
:

(16)

Q^{*}_{f^{\prime}(x)}\circ f^{\prime\prime}(x)\delta x+f^{\prime}(x)=0^{*}_{x}.

This is in accordance with the approach, discussed in
[
32
]
.

On Riemannian manifolds, where a Riesz-isomorphism
\mathcal{R}\in\Gamma(\mathcal{L}(T\mathcal{X},T^{*}\mathcal{X}))
is available, one frequently considers instead the equivalent problem
\mathrm{grad}\,f(x)=0_{x}
, where
\mathrm{grad}f(x)=\mathcal{R}_{x}^{-1}(f^{\prime}(x))\in T_{x}\mathcal{X}
is the Riemannian gradient of
f
. This yields a Newton equation on the tangent space
T_{x}\mathcal{X}
(cf.
[
2
, Section 6]
), using the covariant derivative, induced by the Levi-Civita connection
Q
on
\mathcal{X}
:

(17)

\nabla\,\mathrm{grad}f(x)\delta x+\mathrm{grad}f(x)=0_{x}.

The linear operator
\nabla\,\mathrm{grad}f(x):=Q_{\mathrm{grad}f(x)}\circ(\mathrm{grad}f)^{\prime}(x)\in L(T_{x}\mathcal{X},T_{x}\mathcal{X})
is called the Riemannian Hessian.

If
Q^{*}
is the dual connection of
Q
, then (
16
) and (
17
) yield the same Newton steps. However, (
16
) does not require any Riemannian structure on
\mathcal{X}
. In particular the application of
\mathcal{R}_{x}^{-1}
for the computation of the gradient can be dispensed with. This is a practical advantage if, for example,
\mathcal{X}
is a Sobolev manifold, where
\mathcal{R}_{x}^{-1}
is expensive to evaluate.

4.
Affine covariant damping

In
[
35
, Sec. 6]
, a damped version of Newton’s method was derived. Using an affine covariant damping strategy in the spirit of
[
12
]
this paper introduced a geometrically motivated strategy based on following so-called
Newton paths
. For the sake of completeness and clarity, we recall the main ideas.

Consider a mapping
F\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{E}
into a vector bundle
p\colon\mathcal{E}\to\mathcal{Y}
. Denote by
x(0)\in\mathcal{X}
the starting point of the path. For
\alpha\in[0,1]
and
x(\alpha)\in\mathcal{X}
the base point of
F(x(\alpha))\in\mathcal{E}
is denoted by
y(\alpha)\coloneq p(F(x(\alpha)))\in\mathcal{Y}
. Let
{\mathop{V}\limits^{\vbox to-1.05487pt{\kern-2.0pt\hbox{\!$\scriptscriptstyle\leftarrow$}}}}_{\!\!y}\in\Gamma(\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{E},E_{y}))
be a vector back-transport.
Then the
Newton path problem
, which is based on the idea of scaling down the residual by a factor of
1-\alpha
, is given as

(18)

{\langle{{\mathop{V}\limits^{\vbox to-1.05487pt{\kern-2.0pt\hbox{\!$\scriptscriptstyle\leftarrow$}}}}_{\!\!{y(0)}}}\rangle}(F(x(\alpha)))=(1-\alpha)F(x(0)),\;\alpha\in[0,1].

Since the residuals
F(x(\alpha))
and
F(x(0))
do not lie in the same fibre, we need a vector back-transport on the left-hand side. This vector back-transport allows us to formulate the Newton path problem on a linear space, namely in the fixed fibre
E_{y(0)}
. The mapping
\alpha\mapsto x(\alpha)
(where it is defined) is called the
algebraic Newton path
starting at
x(0)
. Then, the idea is to scale the tangential direction at
x(0)
by a suitable
damping factor
\alpha\in(0,1]
in order to follow the
Newton path. For our algorithm it is crucial that the vector back-transport
{\mathop{V}\limits^{\vbox to-1.05487pt{\kern-2.0pt\hbox{\!$\scriptscriptstyle\leftarrow$}}}}_{\!\!y}
used to define a Newton path and the connection map
Q_{e}
at
e
with
p(e)=y
used to compute Newton directions are consistent, i. e.
Q_{e}={\langle{{\mathop{V}\limits^{\vbox to-1.05487pt{\kern-2.0pt\hbox{\!$\scriptscriptstyle\leftarrow$}}}}_{\!\!y}}\rangle}^{\prime}(e)
. For this choice it can be shown that Newton directions are tangential to the Newton paths (cf.
[
35
, Sec. 6]
).

In the following we will work out the quantities necessary for the algorithm in the case of dual vector bundles and include an adapted algorithm. We will see that for dual bundles the necessary back-transports of the covectors
l\in\mathcal{E}^{*}
can be constructed from adjoints of vector forward transports in the primal bundle. With this, we can derive consistent dual connection maps by differentiation.

4.1.
Dual connections induced by covector back-transports.

Let
p\colon\mathcal{E}\to\mathcal{Y}
be a vector bundle and
p^{*}\colon\mathcal{E}^{*}\to\mathcal{Y}
its dual bundle.
Consider a vector transport
{\mathop{V}\limits^{\vbox to-1.05487pt{\kern-2.0pt\hbox{\!$\scriptscriptstyle\rightarrow$}}}}_{\!\!y}\in\Gamma(\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{Y}\times E_{y},\mathcal{E}))
.
We can define a
covector back-transport

{\mathop{V^{*}}\limits^{\vbox to-1.20552pt{\kern-2.0pt\hbox{\!$\!\!\scriptscriptstyle\leftarrow$}}}}_{\!\!\!\!\!\!y}\;\in\Gamma(\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{E}^{*},E_{y}^{*}))
, which acts as a vector back-transport on the dual bundle by using fibrewise adjoints
{\mathop{V^{*}}\limits^{\vbox to-1.20552pt{\kern-2.0pt\hbox{\!$\!\!\scriptscriptstyle\leftarrow$}}}}_{\!\!\!\!\!\!y}\;(\hat{y})\coloneqq({\mathop{V}\limits^{\vbox to-1.05487pt{\kern-2.0pt\hbox{\!$\scriptscriptstyle\rightarrow$}}}}_{\!\!y}(\hat{y}))^{*}\in L(E_{\hat{y}}^{*},E_{y}^{*})

{\mathop{V^{*}}\limits^{\vbox to-1.20552pt{\kern-2.0pt\hbox{\!$\!\!\scriptscriptstyle\leftarrow$}}}}_{\!\!\!\!\!\!y}\;(\hat{y})\hat{l}\coloneqq\hat{l}\circ{\mathop{V}\limits^{\vbox to-1.05487pt{\kern-2.0pt\hbox{\!$\scriptscriptstyle\rightarrow$}}}}_{\!\!y}(\hat{y})\quad\text{ for all }\hat{l}\in E_{\hat{y}}^{*}.

p^{*}\colon\mathcal{E}^{*}\to\mathcal{Y}

l\in\mathcal{E}^{*}
we set

(19)

Q_{l}^{*}\coloneqq{\langle{{\mathop{V^{*}}\limits^{\vbox to-1.20552pt{\kern-2.0pt\hbox{\!$\!\!\scriptscriptstyle\leftarrow$}}}}_{\!\!\!\!\!\!y}\;}\rangle}^{\prime}(l):T_{l}\mathcal{E}^{*}\to E^{*}_{y},\;y=p^{*}(l).

\mathcal{E}^{*}
. To prove this, we first define an appropriate connection map on the primal bundle
\mathcal{E}
and then show that the product rule (
12
) is satisfied for these choices.

Using the vector transport
{\mathop{V}\limits^{\vbox to-1.05487pt{\kern-2.0pt\hbox{\!$\scriptscriptstyle\rightarrow$}}}}_{\!\!y}

\mathcal{E}
by taking fibrewise inverses, i. e. we set

{\mathop{V}\limits^{\vbox to-1.05487pt{\kern-2.0pt\hbox{\!$\scriptscriptstyle\leftarrow$}}}}_{\!\!y}(\hat{y})\coloneqq\left({\mathop{V}\limits^{\vbox to-1.05487pt{\kern-2.0pt\hbox{\!$\scriptscriptstyle\rightarrow$}}}}_{\!\!y}(\hat{y})\right)^{-1}\colon E_{\hat{y}}\to E_{y}.

Then, we can define a connection map on the primal bundle
p\colon\mathcal{E}\to\mathcal{Y}
by setting
Q_{e}={\langle{{\mathop{V}\limits^{\vbox to-1.05487pt{\kern-2.0pt\hbox{\!$\scriptscriptstyle\leftarrow$}}}}_{\!\!y}}\rangle}^{\prime}(e)
for
e\in\mathcal{E}
with
y=p(e)
.
For the sake of clarity, we repeat a result on the representation of

in trivializations (cf.
[

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
{\mathop{V}\limits^{\vbox to-1.05487pt{\kern-2.0pt\hbox{\!$\scriptscriptstyle\rightarrow$}}}}_{\!\!y}\in\Gamma(\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{Y}\times E_{y},\mathcal{E}))
be a vector transport and
{\mathop{V}\limits^{\vbox to-1.05487pt{\kern-2.0pt\hbox{\!$\scriptscriptstyle\leftarrow$}}}}_{\!\!y}

,

, which is represented in trivializations by (

) with

B_{y,\tau}(e_{\tau})\delta y=-({\mathop{V^{\prime}}\limits^{\vbox to-1.80835pt{\kern-2.0pt\hbox{\!$\scriptscriptstyle\leftarrow$}}}}_{\!\!\!\!{y,\tau}}(y)\delta y)e_{\tau}=({\mathop{V^{\prime}}\limits^{\vbox to-1.80835pt{\kern-2.0pt\hbox{\!$\scriptscriptstyle\rightarrow$}}}}_{\!\!\!\!{y,\tau}}(y)\delta y)e_{\tau}.

Using this, we can show that (
19
) defines a corresponding dual connection map, i. e.
Q_{e}
and
Q_{l}^{*}
satisfy the product rule.

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
{\mathop{V}\limits^{\vbox to-1.05487pt{\kern-2.0pt\hbox{\!$\scriptscriptstyle\rightarrow$}}}}_{\!\!y}\in\Gamma(\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{Y}\times E_{y},\mathcal{E}))
be a vector transport. The choice (
19
) defines the dual connection map
Q_{l}^{*}
on
\mathcal{E}^{*}
corresponding to the connection map
Q_{e}={\langle{{\mathop{V}\limits^{\vbox to-1.05487pt{\kern-2.0pt\hbox{\!$\scriptscriptstyle\leftarrow$}}}}_{\!\!y}}\rangle}^{\prime}(e)

with

Proof.

Let
\ell\in\Gamma(\mathcal{E}^{*})
,

, and

Q_{\ell(y)}^{*}={\langle{{\mathop{V^{*}}\limits^{\vbox to-1.20552pt{\kern-2.0pt\hbox{\!$\!\!\scriptscriptstyle\leftarrow$}}}}_{\!\!\!\!\!\!y}\;}\rangle}^{\prime}(\ell(y))

12

{\langle{{\mathop{V^{*}}\limits^{\vbox to-1.20552pt{\kern-2.0pt\hbox{\!$\!\!\scriptscriptstyle\leftarrow$}}}}_{\!\!\!\!\!\!y}\;}\rangle}(\ell(\hat{y}))=\ell(\hat{y})\circ{\mathop{V}\limits^{\vbox to-1.05487pt{\kern-2.0pt\hbox{\!$\scriptscriptstyle\rightarrow$}}}}_{\!\!y}(\hat{y})
and (
7

\left({\langle{{\mathop{V^{*}}\limits^{\vbox to-1.20552pt{\kern-2.0pt\hbox{\!$\!\!\scriptscriptstyle\leftarrow$}}}}_{\!\!\!\!\!\!y}\;}\rangle}^{\prime}(\ell(y))\ell^{\prime}(y)\delta y\right)(e(y))=\delta\ell_{\tau}(e_{\tau}(y))+\ell_{\tau}(y)\circ{\mathop{V}\limits^{\vbox to-1.05487pt{\kern-2.0pt\hbox{\!$\scriptscriptstyle\rightarrow$}}}}_{\!\!y,\tau}^{\prime}(y)\delta y(e_{\tau}(y)).

The right hand side of (
12
) has the representation

\displaystyle\ell(e)^{\prime}(y)\delta y-\ell(y)(Q_{e(y)}\circ e^{\prime}(y)\delta y)

\displaystyle=\ell_{\tau}(e_{\tau})^{\prime}(y)-\ell_{\tau}(y)(\delta e_{\tau}-B_{y,\tau}(e_{\tau}(y))\delta y)

\displaystyle=\delta\ell_{\tau}(e_{\tau}(y))+\ell_{\tau}(y)(B_{y,\tau}(e_{\tau}(y))\delta y).

Since
B_{y,\tau}(e_{\tau}(y))\delta y=-({\mathop{V}\limits^{\vbox to-1.05487pt{\kern-2.0pt\hbox{\!$\scriptscriptstyle\leftarrow$}}}}_{\!\!y,\tau}^{\prime}(y)\delta y)e_{\tau}(y)=({\mathop{V}\limits^{\vbox to-1.05487pt{\kern-2.0pt\hbox{\!$\scriptscriptstyle\rightarrow$}}}}_{\!\!y,\tau}^{\prime}(y)\delta y)e_{\tau}(y)
by (

∎

The following proposition describes a practical way how to apply a dual connection to

. It will be useful for us in Section

Proposition 4.3
.

Let
{\mathop{V}\limits^{\vbox to-1.05487pt{\kern-2.0pt\hbox{\!$\scriptscriptstyle\rightarrow$}}}}_{\!\!y}\in\Gamma(\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{Y}\times E_{y},\mathcal{E}))

Proof.

Let

and

\tau

4.2.
An affine covariant damped Newton method

In the following we assume that the dual connection map
Q^{*}
on
\mathcal{E}^{*}
is consistent with a covector back-transport
{\mathop{V^{*}}\limits^{\vbox to-1.20552pt{\kern-2.0pt\hbox{\!$\!\!\scriptscriptstyle\leftarrow$}}}}_{\!\!\!\!\!\!y}\;\in\Gamma(\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{E}^{*},E_{y}^{*}))
, i. e.
Q_{l}^{*}\coloneqq{\langle{{\mathop{V^{*}}\limits^{\vbox to-1.20552pt{\kern-2.0pt\hbox{\!$\!\!\scriptscriptstyle\leftarrow$}}}}_{\!\!\!\!\!\!y}\;}\rangle}^{\prime}(l)
for
l\in\mathcal{E}^{*}
.

We employ a damping factor
\alpha\in(0,1]
to compute a damped Newton update in the following way:

x_{+}=R_{x}(\alpha\delta x).

To assess, whether
x_{+}
is acceptable, we use the affine covariant quantity

\theta_{x_{+}}(x)=\cfrac{\lVert\overline{\delta x_{+}^{\alpha}}\rVert_{x}}{\lVert\alpha\delta x\rVert_{x}}

which contains computing the simplified Newton direction
\overline{\delta x_{+}^{\alpha}}\in T_{x}\mathcal{X}
(for a detailed motivation we refer to
[
35
]
and
[
12
]
). Using the consistency of the covector back-transport and the dual connection map, this direction can be computed by solving the linear equation

(21)

Q^{*}_{F(x)}\circ F^{\prime}(x)\overline{\delta x_{+}^{\alpha}}+{\mathop{V^{*}}\limits^{\vbox to-1.20552pt{\kern-2.0pt\hbox{\!$\!\!\scriptscriptstyle\leftarrow$}}}}_{\!\!\!\!\!\!y(x)}\;(y(x_{+}))F(x_{+})-(1-\alpha)F(x)=0_{y(x)}^{*}.

In particular, the covector back-transport needed here can be performed via a forward transport of the test-vectors:

{\mathop{V^{*}}\limits^{\vbox to-1.20552pt{\kern-2.0pt\hbox{\!$\!\!\scriptscriptstyle\leftarrow$}}}}_{\!\!\!\!\!\!y(x)}\;(y(x_{+}))F(x_{+})(e)=F(x_{+})({\mathop{V}\limits^{\vbox to-1.05487pt{\kern-2.0pt\hbox{\!$\scriptscriptstyle\rightarrow$}}}}_{\!\!y(x)}(y(x_{+}))e)\quad\text{ for all }e\in E_{y(x)}.

Then (
21
) can be stated equivalently as

Q^{*}_{F(x)}\circ F^{\prime}(x)\overline{\delta x_{+}^{\alpha}}e+F(x_{+})({\mathop{V}\limits^{\vbox to-1.05487pt{\kern-2.0pt\hbox{\!$\scriptscriptstyle\rightarrow$}}}}_{\!\!y(x)}(y(x_{+}))e)-(1-\alpha)F(x)e=0\quad\text{ for all }e\in E_{y(x)}.

Remark 4.4
.

By Proposition
4.3
we see that

can be computed by differentiating these vector transports of the test vectors at
x
to obtain a consistent connection. This is of central practical importance for the numerical computations, presented below. We also stress that our requirement of consistency gives us a lot of flexibility for numerical implementations, since we are not confined to a specific, say Levi-Civita, connection. Instead, we are free to choose a
vector transport, and define our connection accordingly.

The resulting algorithm can then be stated as summarized in Algorithm

5.
Connections for mappings into embedded vector bundles

In our numerical examples we will consider mappings into vector bundles which are embedded into Hilbert spaces. In view of our globalization strategy, we need primal and dual connections that are consistent with vector transports. Thus, we will now discuss an easy way to derive vector transports and corresponding connection maps in this case by using orthogonal projections onto the fibres.

Let
p\colon\mathcal{E}\to\mathcal{Y}
be a vector bundle that is embedded into a Hilbert space
H
. Then we can use the embedding
\iota_{H}\colon\mathcal{E}\to H
with
\iota_{H}(y)\colon E_{y}\to H
for
y\in\mathcal{Y}
and the orthogonal projection
P(y)\colon H\to E_{y}
onto the fibre to define a vector back-transport locally by

\displaystyle{\mathop{V}\limits^{\vbox to-1.05487pt{\kern-2.0pt\hbox{\!$\scriptscriptstyle\leftarrow$}}}}_{\!\!y}(\eta)\coloneqq P(y)\iota_{H}(\eta)\in L(E_{\eta},E_{y}).

Analogously, we can define a vector transport locally by

{\mathop{V}\limits^{\vbox to-1.05487pt{\kern-2.0pt\hbox{\!$\scriptscriptstyle\rightarrow$}}}}_{\!\!y}(\eta)\coloneqq P(\eta)\iota_{H}(y)\in L(E_{y},E_{\eta}).

A covector back-transport is then given by taking fibrewise adjoints
{\mathop{V^{*}}\limits^{\vbox to-1.20552pt{\kern-2.0pt\hbox{\!$\!\!\scriptscriptstyle\leftarrow$}}}}_{\!\!\!\!\!\!y}\;({\eta})\coloneqq({\mathop{V}\limits^{\vbox to-1.05487pt{\kern-2.0pt\hbox{\!$\scriptscriptstyle\rightarrow$}}}}_{\!\!y}({\eta}))^{*}
, i.e.

(22)

{\mathop{V^{*}}\limits^{\vbox to-1.20552pt{\kern-2.0pt\hbox{\!$\!\!\scriptscriptstyle\leftarrow$}}}}_{\!\!\!\!\!\!y}\;({\eta})l=l\circ{\mathop{V}\limits^{\vbox to-1.05487pt{\kern-2.0pt\hbox{\!$\scriptscriptstyle\rightarrow$}}}}_{\!\!y}({\eta})=l\circ P({\eta})\circ\iota_{H}(y)\quad\text{ for all }l\in E_{\eta}^{*}.

As seen before, we may obtain consistent connection maps by differentiation.

Primal connection maps

Using the natural inclusion
\varphi
given by (
6
) we obtain the
C^{1}
-mapping
{\langle{{\mathop{V}\limits^{\vbox to-1.05487pt{\kern-2.0pt\hbox{\!$\scriptscriptstyle\leftarrow$}}}}_{\!\!y}}\rangle}\colon\mathcal{E}\to E_{y}
. Let
e\in\Gamma(\mathcal{E})
. Since
p(e(\eta))=\eta
, we get
{\langle{{\mathop{V}\limits^{\vbox to-1.05487pt{\kern-2.0pt\hbox{\!$\scriptscriptstyle\leftarrow$}}}}_{\!\!y}}\rangle}(e(\eta))=P(y)\iota_{H}(\eta)e(\eta)
. Differentiating this with respect to
{\eta}
at
{\eta}=y
, we obtain

(23)

Q_{e(y)}\circ e^{\prime}(y)\delta y={\langle{{\mathop{V}\limits^{\vbox to-1.05487pt{\kern-2.0pt\hbox{\!$\scriptscriptstyle\leftarrow$}}}}_{\!\!y}}\rangle}^{\prime}(e(y))e^{\prime}(y)\delta y=P(y)(\iota_{H}e)^{\prime}(y)\delta y\in E_{y}.

Remark 5.1
.

For a vector field
\nu\colon\mathcal{X}\to T\mathcal{X}
we obtain by using (
23
) and
\pi(\nu(x))=x
for
x\in\mathcal{X}

Q_{\nu(x)}\circ\nu^{\prime}(x)\delta x={\langle{{\mathop{V}\limits^{\vbox to-1.05487pt{\kern-2.0pt\hbox{\!$\scriptscriptstyle\leftarrow$}}}}_{\!\!x}}\rangle}^{\prime}(\nu(x))\nu^{\prime}(x)\delta x=P(x)(\iota_{H}\nu)^{\prime}(x)\delta x\in T_{x}\mathcal{X},

which is consistent with the orthogonal projection.
The
covariant derivative

Q\circ\nu^{\prime}
of a vector field
\nu
w.r.t. the connection map
Q
can therefore be determined by orthogonally projecting the derivatives of the embedded vector field onto
T\mathcal{X}
. The connection which belongs to this covariant derivative is sometimes called tangential connection and defines the Levi-Civita connection on
T\mathcal{X}
, see e.g.
[
22
, Chap. 5]
.

Dual connection maps

Consider
\ell_{H}\colon\mathcal{Y}\to H^{*}
and its restriction
\ell\colon\mathcal{Y}\to\mathcal{E}^{*}
, given by
\ell({\eta})\coloneqq\ell_{H}({\eta})\circ\iota_{H}({\eta})
.
By the natural inclusion
\varphi
, we obtain the
C^{1}
-mapping
{\langle{{\mathop{V^{*}}\limits^{\vbox to-1.20552pt{\kern-2.0pt\hbox{\!$\!\!\scriptscriptstyle\leftarrow$}}}}_{\!\!\!\!\!\!y}\;}\rangle}(\ell({\eta}))=\ell_{H}({\eta})\circ\iota_{H}({\eta})\circ{\mathop{V}\limits^{\vbox to-1.05487pt{\kern-2.0pt\hbox{\!$\scriptscriptstyle\rightarrow$}}}}_{\!\!y}({\eta})
.
Differentiating this with respect to
{\eta}
at
{\eta}=y=p^{*}(\ell)
yields

\displaystyle{\langle{{\mathop{V^{*}}\limits^{\vbox to-1.20552pt{\kern-2.0pt\hbox{\!$\!\!\scriptscriptstyle\leftarrow$}}}}_{\!\!\!\!\!\!y}\;}\rangle}^{\prime}(\ell(y))\ell^{\prime}(y)\delta y

\displaystyle=\ell_{H}^{\prime}(y)\delta y+\ell_{H}(y)(\iota_{H}\circ{\mathop{V}\limits^{\vbox to-1.05487pt{\kern-2.0pt\hbox{\!$\scriptscriptstyle\rightarrow$}}}}_{\!\!y})^{\prime}(y)\delta y

\displaystyle=\ell_{H}^{\prime}(y)\delta y+\ell_{H}(y)(\iota_{H}\circ P)^{\prime}(y)\iota_{H}(y)\delta y,

or, suppressing the embeddings notationally:

(24)

\displaystyle Q^{*}_{\ell(y)}\circ\ell^{\prime}(y)\delta y={\langle{{\mathop{V^{*}}\limits^{\vbox to-1.20552pt{\kern-2.0pt\hbox{\!$\!\!\scriptscriptstyle\leftarrow$}}}}_{\!\!\!\!\!\!y}\;}\rangle}^{\prime}(\ell(y))\ell^{\prime}(y)\delta y=\ell_{H}^{\prime}(y)\delta y+\ell_{H}(y)P^{\prime}(y)\delta y\in E_{y}^{*}.

Vice versa, if
\ell\in\Gamma(\mathcal{E}^{*})
is given, we may extend
\ell
to
\ell_{H}\colon\mathcal{Y}\to H^{*}
, for example by specifying
\ell_{H}(y)
on
\iota_{H}(E_{y})^{\perp}
.
Then by its intrinsic definition
Q^{*}_{\ell(y)}\circ\ell^{\prime}(y)\delta y
is independent of the extension, and thus also (
24
) is independent of the extension.

Submersed vector bundles.

Let us consider a smooth fibrewise linear mapping
A\colon\mathcal{Y}\to L(H,R)
and
A(y)
is assumed to be surjective for each
y\in\mathcal{Y}
. Let
p\colon\mathcal{E}\to\mathcal{Y}
be defined by
E_{y}=\mathrm{ker}\,A(y)
.
For a covector field
\ell_{H}\colon\mathcal{Y}\to H^{*}
and its restriction
\ell\colon\mathcal{Y}\to\mathcal{E}^{*}
we consider the problem:

0^{*}_{y}=\ell(y)\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad 0=\ell_{H}(y)e\quad\text{ for all }e\in E_{y}

In view of (
24
) we obtain for the connection, induced by the orthogonal projection
P(y)\colon{H}\to E_{y}
:

(25)

Q^{*}_{\ell(y)}\ell^{\prime}(y)\delta y=\ell_{H}^{\prime}(y)\delta y+\ell_{H}(y)P^{\prime}(y)\delta y\quad\text{ for all }\delta y\in T_{y}\mathcal{Y}.

Since
A(y)
is surjective, the restriction
A_{K}(y)\colon\mathrm{ker}\,A(y)^{\perp}\to R
is an isomorphism by the open mapping theorem, and so is its adjoint
A_{K}(y)^{*}\colon R^{*}\to(\mathrm{ker}A(y)^{\perp})^{*}
.
This means that there is a unique
Lagrangian multiplier

\lambda(y):=-(A_{K}(y)^{*})^{-1}\ell_{H}(y)\in R^{*},

satisfying

(26)

(\ell_{H}(y)+\lambda(y)A(y))w=0\quad\text{ for all }w\in\mathrm{ker}\,A(y)^{\perp},

and thus

\ell_{H}(y)e=0\quad\text{ for all }e\in\mathrm{ker}\,A(y)\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad(\ell_{H}(y)+\lambda(y)A(y))v\quad\text{ for all }v\in{H}^{*}.

Using
\mathrm{ker}\,A(y)=\mathrm{ran}\,P(y)
we can write (
26
) as

\begin{split}0&=(\ell_{H}(y)+\lambda(y)A(y))(Id_{H}-P(y))v\\
&=\ell_{H}(y)(Id_{H}-P(y))v+\lambda(y)A(y)v\quad\text{ for all }v\in{H}.\end{split}

Differentiation with respect to
y
in direction
\delta y\in T_{y}\mathcal{Y}
yields for all
v\in H
:

0=\Big((\ell^{\prime}_{H}(y)\delta y)(Id_{H}-P(y))-\ell_{H}(y)P^{\prime}(y)\delta y+(\lambda^{\prime}(y)\delta y)\,A(y)+\lambda(y)A^{\prime}(y)\delta y\Big)v

and thus, testing with
e\in\mathrm{ker}\,A(y)
, so that
A(y)e=(Id_{H}-P(y))e=0
, we get:

\lambda(y)A^{\prime}(y)(\delta y,e)=\ell_{H}(y)P^{\prime}(y)\delta y\,e\quad\text{ for all }e\in\mathrm{ker}\,A(y).

Hence, in (
25
) we can dispense with the use of
P^{\prime}(y)
, if
\lambda(y)
is available:

(27)

\displaystyle Q^{*}_{\ell(y)}\ell^{\prime}(y)\delta y=\ell^{\prime}_{H}(y)\delta y+\lambda(y)A^{\prime}(y)\delta y\quad\text{ for all }\delta y\in T_{y}\mathcal{Y}.

Remark 5.2
.

To cover constrained optimization problems of the form

\min_{x\in{H}}f(x)\text{ s.t. }c(x)=0

we may set
\mathcal{Y}=\{c(x)=0\}
,
\ell_{H}(y)=f^{\prime}(x)
,
\ell_{H}^{\prime}(y)=f^{\prime\prime}(x)
,
A(y)=c^{\prime}(x)
and
A^{\prime}(y)=c^{\prime\prime}(x)
. Then (
27
) yields the Hessian of the Lagrangian function

(28)

\displaystyle Q^{*}_{f^{\prime}(x)}f^{\prime\prime}(x)\delta x=f^{\prime\prime}(x)\delta x+\lambda(x)c^{\prime\prime}(x)\delta x\quad\text{ for all }\delta x\in\mathrm{ker}\,c^{\prime}(x),

which occurs in the second order analysis and in Lagrange-Newton methods for constrained optimization. It can thus be interpreted as the covariant second derivative of
f
on
\mathcal{Y}
.

6.
Applications

In this section we will discuss the computational aspects of solving variational problems on manifolds. Our main emphasis is on algorithmic questions, and in particular the realization of the abstract concepts, introduced so far, to a couple of concrete problems. We deliberately do not touch questions of (numerical) analysis, since this is beyond the scope of the current work.

6.1.
Elastic Geodesics in a Force Field

A geodesic minimizes the length, or equivalently, the Dirichlet energy (
2
) among all curves that connect two given points on a manifolds. An alternative interpretation of such a curve would be that of an elastic string, constrained to the manifold, which assumes the configuration of minimal energy. A straightforward generalization of this model is to add a force field to this problem. We end up with an
elastic geodesic
in a force field.

Let
\gamma\colon I\to\mathcal{M}
where
\mathcal{M}
is an embedded submanifold of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
\dim\mathcal{M}=m
and
I\coloneqq[0,T]\subset\mathbb{R}
for
T\in\mathbb{R}
.
We use the euclidean inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
as a Riemannian metric on
\mathcal{M}
. Let
\mathcal{X}
be a Sobolev manifold (cf. e.g.
[
17
]
)

\mathcal{X}=H^{1}(I,\mathcal{M})\coloneqq\{g\in H^{1}\left(I,\mathbb{R}^{d}\right)\;\mid\;g(t)\in\mathcal{M}\text{ a.e.}\}

and
\mathcal{E}=T\mathcal{X}
its tangent bundle.
Let
\omega\colon\mathcal{M}\to T^{*}\mathcal{M}
be a force field on
\mathcal{M}
. In general
\omega
may have no anti-derivative (is not exact as a
1
-form), so in terms of physics,
\omega
may be a non-conservative force field.
This is in particular the case, if
\omega^{\prime}
is not symmetric, or equivalently, the outer derivative
d\omega
is non-zero.

Our goal is to find a zero of the following mapping:

\displaystyle F\colon\mathcal{X}

\displaystyle\to\mathcal{E}^{*}

\displaystyle\gamma

\displaystyle\mapsto F(\gamma),\quad F(\gamma)\phi\coloneqq\int_{I}\langle\dot{\gamma}(t),\dot{\phi}(t)\rangle+\omega(\gamma(t))\phi(t)\;dt\text{ for }\phi\in T_{\gamma}\mathcal{X}.

Additionally, we have to take into account that boundary conditions
\gamma(0)=\gamma_{0}
and
\gamma(T)=\gamma_{T}
for given
\gamma_{0},\gamma_{T}\in\mathcal{M}
are satisfied. If
\omega\equiv 0
this coincides with the variational formulation (
4
) of the classical geodesic problem.
In case of a non-conservative force-field
\omega
this problem can not be phrased as an energy minimization problem. We call a solution
\gamma
of this mapping an
elastic geodesic
in a force field
\omega
. Be aware that a solution of this problem is not guaranteed to exist in general. This depends on the choice of boundary points and force fields.

In order to apply Newton’s method to find a zero of
F
, we need the linear mapping
Q_{F(\gamma)}^{*}\circ F^{\prime}(\gamma)
which can be seen as a covariant derivative of a fibrewise linear mapping.

Here, we can use the embedding of
T\mathcal{M}
into
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, define the covector back-transport according to (
22
), and derive the dual connection map applied to
F^{\prime}
by (
24
) using projections
P(\gamma(t))\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to T_{\gamma(t)}\mathcal{M}
,
t\in[0,T]
in a pointwise fashion. To this end we apply the techniques of Section
5
with
H=\mathbb{R}^{d}
for each
t\in I
. Concretely, we write

F(\gamma)\phi=\int_{I}\langle\dot{\gamma}(t),(P(\gamma(t))\phi(t))\dot{}\;\rangle+\omega(\gamma(t))P(\gamma(t))\phi(t)\;dt

for
\phi\in T_{\gamma}\mathcal{X}
. Differentiating this with respect to
\gamma
yields

(29)

\displaystyle Q_{F(\gamma)}^{*}\circ F^{\prime}(\gamma)\delta\gamma\,\phi

\displaystyle=\int_{I}\langle\dot{\delta\gamma}(t),\dot{\phi}(t)\rangle+\langle\dot{\gamma}(t),(P^{\prime}(\gamma(t))\delta\gamma(t)\phi(t))\dot{}\;\rangle

\displaystyle+\omega^{\prime}(\gamma(t))\delta\gamma(t)\phi(t)+\omega(\gamma(t))P^{\prime}(\gamma(t))\delta\gamma(t)\phi(t)\;dt

\displaystyle=F(\gamma)(P^{\prime}(\gamma)\delta\gamma\,\phi)+F_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}^{\prime}(\gamma)\delta\gamma\,\phi

where
F_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}^{\prime}(\gamma)
denotes the Euclidean derivative of
F
at
\gamma
, i.e.

F_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}^{\prime}(\gamma)\delta\gamma\,\phi=\int_{I}\langle\dot{\delta\gamma}(t),\dot{\phi}(t)\rangle+\omega^{\prime}(\gamma(t))\delta\gamma(t)\phi(t)\;dt.

Remark 6.1
.

Taking into account that
\mathcal{X}=H^{1}(I,\mathcal{M})
we observe that our pointwise defined connection is
not
the dual of the Levi-Civita connection on
\mathcal{X}
. Due to the Sobolev structure, the Levi-Civita connection would be more difficult to derive and much more expensive to evaluate. The flexibility to employ an arbitrary vector transport together with a conistent connection admits the relatively simple derivation and an efficient computation of the Newton matrix here.

Discretization

To turn our infinite dimensional problem computationally tractable, we first have to discretize the right hand side
F(\gamma)
and the Newton equation

Q_{F(\gamma)}^{*}\circ F^{\prime}(\gamma)\delta\gamma+F(\gamma)=0_{\gamma}^{*}\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad Q_{F(\gamma)}^{*}\circ F^{\prime}(\gamma)\delta\gamma\,\phi+F(\gamma)\phi=0\quad\text{ for all }\phi\in T_{\gamma}\mathcal{X}

to compute a Newton direction
\delta\gamma
. For simplicity we choose a continuous piecewise linear discretization of
\gamma
, which utilizes the embedding
\mathcal{M}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
. A discussion of more sophisticated intrinsic ways of discretization, like, for example, geometric finite elements can be found in
[
18
]
.

We consider equidistant discrete time points

t_{0}=0<t_{1}<\dots<t_{N+1}=T

with distance
h
, i. e.
t_{i}=ih,\;i=0,...,N+1
, such that

I=[0,T]=\bigcup_{i=0}^{N}[t_{i},t_{i+1}].

The set of admissible curves is then defined by the piecewise linear interpolants in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
of the points
y_{i}\in\mathcal{M}
, i.e.

\gamma(t_{i})=y_{i}\in\mathcal{M},\;i=1,..,N.

Thus,
\gamma
is represented by a point
y=(y_{1},\cdots y_{N})
on the
product manifold

\mathcal{M}^{N}
. Additionally, the points
y_{0}
and
y_{N+1}
are given by the boundary conditions
\gamma(0)=\gamma_{0}
and
\gamma(T)=\gamma_{T}
. Setting

w_{i}\coloneqq\omega(\gamma(t_{i}))=\omega(y_{i})\in T_{\gamma(t_{i})}\mathcal{M}^{*}\subset(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{*}\text{ for }i=0,\dots,N,

yields
w=(w_{0},\dots,w_{N+1})\in((\mathbb{R}^{d})^{*})^{N+2}
. Similarly, each test function
\phi
is given as a piecewise linear interpolant in the following way

(\phi(t_{0}),\dots,\phi(t_{N+1}))=(\delta y_{0},\dots,\delta y_{N+1})\in T_{y_{0}}\mathcal{M}\times\dots\times T_{y_{N+1}}\mathcal{M}

where
\delta y_{i}=\delta\gamma(t_{i})
for
i=1,\ldots,N
and
\delta y_{0}=\delta y_{N+1}=0
. To construct a basis of the set of test functions choose a basis
\{v_{i,j}\}_{j=1\dots m}
of each tangent space
T_{y_{i}}\mathcal{M}
for
i=1\dots N
. Then for each
i
,
j
we get a set of basis functions as follows:

(30)

\phi_{(i-1)\cdot m+j}(t_{k})=\begin{cases}v_{i,j}&\text{ if }i=k,\\
0&\text{ if }i\neq k,\end{cases}\quad i=1\dots N,j=1\dots m.

The support of
\phi_{(i-1)\cdot m+j}
consists of the interval
[t_{i-1},t_{i+1}]
.

Assembly of the Newton system

To compute the matrix and the right hand side for the Newton system we have to evaluate
F(\gamma)\phi
for all basis functions
\phi
and
Q_{F(\gamma)}^{*}\circ F^{\prime}(\gamma)\phi^{2}\phi^{1}
for all pairs of basis functions. More specifically, for
M=N\cdot m
we have to find
b\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
and
A\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times M}
by computing

(31)

b_{k}\coloneqq F(\gamma)\phi_{k},\qquad A_{k,l}\coloneqq Q_{F(\gamma)}^{*}\circ F^{\prime}(\gamma)\phi_{l}\phi_{k}\quad k,l=1\dots M.

Then, solving the equation
A\xi+b=0
yields a representation of the Newton direction
\delta\gamma
with respect to the chosen bases:

\delta\gamma(t)=\sum_{l=1}^{M}\xi_{l}\phi_{l}(t).

Let us first consider the right hand side

\displaystyle F(\gamma)\phi

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{N}\int_{[t_{i},t_{i+1}]}\left\langle\dot{\gamma}(t),\dot{\phi}(t)\right\rangle+\omega(\gamma(t))\phi(t)\;dt.

Due to our hypothesis of linear interpolation, in every time interval
[t_{i},t_{i+1}]
, the time derivatives of
\gamma
and
\phi
are constant and we can compute them by finite differences in the embedding, i. e. we use

\dot{\gamma}(t)=\frac{y_{i+1}-y_{i}}{h}\quad\text{and}\quad\dot{\phi}(t)=\frac{\delta y_{i+1}-\delta y_{i}}{h}\quad\text{for }t\in[t_{i},t_{i+1}].

Thus, we get

F(\gamma)\phi=\sum_{i=0}^{N}\int_{[t_{i},t_{i+1}]}\left\langle\frac{y_{i+1}-y_{i}}{h},\frac{\delta y_{i+1}-\delta y_{i}}{h}\right\rangle+\omega(\gamma(t))\phi(t)\;dt

Using the trapezoidal rule to approximate the integral, we obtain the discretized right hand side by

(32)

F(\gamma)\phi\approx h\cdot\sum_{i=0}^{N}\left\langle\frac{y_{i+1}-y_{i}}{h},\frac{\delta y_{i+1}-\delta y_{i}}{h}\right\rangle+\frac{w_{i}\delta y_{i}+w_{i+1}\delta y_{i+1}}{2}.

As we have seen above,
Q_{F(x)}^{*}\circ F^{\prime}(x)
applied to
\phi^{1},\phi^{2} \in T_{\gamma}\mathcal{X}
can be written as

\displaystyle Q_{F(\gamma)}^{*}\circ F^{\prime}(\gamma)\phi^{2}\phi^{1}

\displaystyle=F(\gamma)(P^{\prime}(\gamma)\phi^{2}\phi^{1})+F_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}^{\prime}(\gamma)\phi^{2}\phi^{1}

where
F_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}^{\prime}(\gamma)
is given by

\displaystyle F_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}^{\prime}(\gamma)\phi^{2}\phi^{1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{N}\int_{[t_{i},t_{i+1}]}\left\langle\dot{\phi^{2}}(t),\dot{\phi^{1}}(t)\right\rangle+\omega^{\prime}(\gamma(t))\phi^{2}(t)\phi^{1}(t)\;dt.

Again, setting
\delta y^{1}=(\delta y^{1}_{1},\dots,\delta y^{1}_{N})\coloneqq(\phi^{1}(t_{1}),\dots,\phi^{1}(t_{N}))
,
\delta y^{2}=(\delta y_{1}^{2},\dots,\delta y^{2}_{N})\coloneqq(\phi^{2}(t_{1}),\dots,\phi^{2}(t_{N}))
and
\delta y^{1}_{0}=\delta y^{1}_{N+1}=\delta y^{2}_{0}=\delta y_{N+1}^{2}=0
and using the trapezoidal rule yields an approximation

(33)

\displaystyle F_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}^{\prime}(\gamma)\phi^{2}\phi^{1}

\displaystyle\approx h\cdot\sum_{i=0}^{N}\left\langle\frac{\delta y^{2}_{i+1}-\delta y^{2}_{i}}{h},\frac{\delta y^{1}_{i+1}-\delta y^{1}_{i}}{h}\right\rangle

\displaystyle+h\cdot\sum_{i=0}^{N}\frac{w^{\prime}(y_{i+1})\delta y^{2}_{i+1}\delta y^{1}_{i+1}+w^{\prime}(y_{i})\delta y^{2}_{i}\delta y^{1}_{i}}{2}.

We set

P_{i}\coloneqq P^{\prime}(y_{i})\delta y_{i}^{2}\delta y_{i}^{1}\;\text{ for }\;i=0,\dots,N+1.

Thus, combining (
33
) with (
32
) applied to
P=(P_{0},...,P_{N+1})
yields an approximation

(34)

\begin{split}Q_{F(\gamma)}^{*}\circ F^{\prime}(\gamma)\phi^{2}\phi^{1}&\approx h\cdot\sum_{i=0}^{N}\left\langle\frac{y_{i+1}-y_{i}}{h},\frac{P_{i+1}-P_{i}}{h}\right\rangle+\frac{w_{i}P_{i}+w_{i+1}P_{i+1}}{2}\\
&\quad+h\cdot\sum_{i=0}^{N}\left\langle\frac{\delta y^{2}_{i+1}-\delta y^{2}_{i}}{h},\frac{\delta y^{1}_{i+1}-\delta y^{1}_{i}}{h}\right\rangle\\
&\quad+h\cdot\sum_{i=0}^{N}\frac{w^{\prime}(y_{i+1})\delta y^{2}_{i+1}\delta y^{1}_{i+1}+w^{\prime}(y_{i})\delta y^{2}_{i}\delta y^{1}_{i}}{2}.\end{split}

Observe that most of the terms in these sum are zero, if the
\phi^{i}
are basis functions, as described above, since their values are zero at most of the grid points. In our case, this implies that
A
is a block tridiagonal matrix with block of size
m\times m
.

6.2.
An Obstacle Problem for Elastic geodesics

We consider obstacle problems with elastic geodesics as a simple example for a variational inequality on a manifold. The idea is to find a curve of minimal Dirichlet energy that connects two given points, but avoids a prescribed area on the manifold. We will employ a penalty path-following method, where the sub-problems are not continuously differentiable, but only Newton-differentiable.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be an embedded submanifold of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
\dim\mathcal{M}=m
and
I\coloneqq[0,T]\subset\mathbb{R}
for
T\in\mathbb{R}
. Consider the constrained minimization problem

(36)

\displaystyle\min_{\gamma\in H^{1}(I,\mathcal{M})}\;\frac{1}{2}\int_{I}\lVert\dot{\gamma}(t)\rVert^{2}\;dt

\displaystyle s.t.\;c(\gamma(t))\leq 0\;\text{ for all }t\in I

where
c\colon\mathcal{M}\to\mathbb{R}^{l}
is a twice differentiable mapping and the
\leq
is meant pointwise. Additionally, we have the boundary conditions
\gamma(0)=\gamma_{0}
and
\gamma(T)=\gamma_{T}
for given
\gamma_{0},\,\gamma_{T}\in\mathcal{M}
. Solving this problem yields a geodesic connecting
\gamma_{0}
and
\gamma_{T}
and avoiding an obstacle that is described by the given constraints. Using a penalty method, also known as Moreau-Yosida regularization (cf.
[
20
]
), with a quadratic penalty term we can rewrite this as an unconstrained minimization problem with a penalty coefficient
p\in\mathbb{R}
:

(37)

\displaystyle\min_{\gamma\in H^{1}(I,\mathcal{M})}\;\frac{1}{2}\int_{I}\lVert\dot{\gamma}(t)\rVert^{2}+p\sum_{i=1}^{l}\max(0,c_{i}(\gamma(t)))^{2}\;dt

where
c_{i}\colon\mathcal{M}\to\mathbb{R}
denotes the
i
-th component function of
c
.
We denote the objective of the penalized problem by

\displaystyle f\colon H^{1}(I,\mathcal{M})

\displaystyle\to\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle\gamma

\displaystyle\mapsto f(\gamma)\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\int_{I}\lVert\dot{\gamma}(t)\rVert^{2}+p\sum_{i=1}^{l}m(c_{i}(\gamma(t)))^{2}\;dt.

Let
m\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R},\,m(x)\coloneqq\max(0,x)
. It can easily be shown that this function is Newton-differentiable with a Newton-derivative

(38)

m^{\prime}(x)\coloneqq\begin{cases}0&\text{ if }\;x<0\\
\text{arbitrary}&\text{ if }\;x=0\\
1&\text{ if }\;x>0\end{cases}.

By differentiating
f
we obtain the mapping
f^{\prime}\colon H^{1}(I,\mathcal{M})\to T^{*}H^{1}(I,\mathcal{M})
with

(39)

\displaystyle f^{\prime}(\gamma)\phi

\displaystyle\coloneqq\int_{I}\langle\dot{\gamma}(t),\dot{\phi}(t)\rangle+p\sum_{i=1}^{l}m(c_{i}(\gamma(t)))m^{\prime}(c_{i}(\gamma(t)))c_{i}^{\prime}(\gamma(t))\phi(t)\;dt.

For the maximum function it holds that

m(x)m^{\prime}(x)=\begin{cases}0&\text{ if }\;x\leq 0\\
m(x)&\text{ if }\;x>0\end{cases}\;=m(x)\text{ for all }x\in\mathbb{R}.

Hence, (
39
) reduces to

f^{\prime}(\gamma)\phi=\int_{I}\langle\dot{\gamma}(t),\dot{\phi}(t)\rangle+p\sum_{i=1}^{l}m(c_{i}(\gamma(t)))c_{i}^{\prime}(\gamma(t))\phi(t)\;dt.

f^{\prime}
is a semismooth mapping into a dual vector bundle, namely the cotangent bundle of
H^{1}(I,\mathcal{M})
. Thus, we can apply Newton’s method to compute a critical point of
f
. Defining the covector back-transport by orthogonal projections according to (
22
) and using the projection technique for embedded vector bundles derived in Sec.
5
, we obtain a dual connection map

Q_{f^{\prime}(\gamma)}^{*}\circ f^{\prime\prime}(\gamma)\delta\gamma\,\phi=f_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}^{\prime\prime}(\gamma)\delta\gamma\,\phi+f^{\prime}(\gamma)(P^{\prime}(\gamma)\delta\gamma\,\phi).

Here, using a Newton-derivative of the maximum function according to (
38
), we can write a Newton-derivative of
f^{\prime}
, denoted by
f_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}^{\prime\prime}(\gamma)
, as

\displaystyle f^{\prime\prime}_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(\gamma)\delta\gamma\,\phi

\displaystyle=\int_{I}\langle\dot{\delta\gamma}(t),\dot{\phi}(t)\rangle+p\sum_{i=1}^{l}m^{\prime}(c_{i}(\gamma(t)))c_{i}^{\prime}(\gamma(t))\delta\gamma(t)c_{i}^{\prime}(\gamma(t))\phi(t)

\displaystyle\quad+p\sum_{i=1}^{l}m(c_{i}(\gamma(t)))c_{i}^{\prime\prime}(\gamma(t))\delta\gamma(t)\phi(t)\;dt.

Numerical results

Consider
\mathcal{M}=\mathbb{S}^{2}\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
and a closed interval
I\subset\mathbb{R}
. We want to compute a geodesic connecting two points
\gamma_{0}
and
\gamma_{T}
on the sphere while avoiding the north pole cap. For a given height
h_{\mathrm{ref}}\in(0,1)
we consider the minimization problem

\displaystyle\min_{\gamma\in H^{1}(I,\mathcal{M})}\;\int_{I}\lVert\dot{\gamma}(t)\rVert^{2}\;dt

\displaystyle s.t.\;\gamma_{3}(t)\leq 1-h_{\mathrm{ref}}\;\text{ for all }t\in I

where
\gamma_{3}(t)
denotes the third component of
\gamma(t)\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
. Thus, we have one constraint given by
c\colon\mathbb{S}^{2}\to\mathbb{R},\;c(y)=y_{3}-1+h_{\mathrm{ref}}
.
In this case a Newton-derivative according to (
39
) of the objective of the penalized problem given by (
37
) reads

f^{\prime}(\gamma)\phi=\int_{I}\langle\dot{\gamma}(t),\dot{\phi}(t)\rangle+p\cdot m(\gamma_{3}(t)-1+h_{\mathrm{ref}})\phi_{3}(t).

Since
c^{\prime}(y)=(0,0,1)
and
c^{\prime\prime}(y)=0\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}
, the formula for the second Euclidean derivative reduces to

f^{\prime\prime}_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}(\gamma)\delta\gamma\,\phi=\int_{I}\langle\dot{\delta\gamma}(t),\dot{\phi}(t)\rangle+p\cdot m^{\prime}(\gamma_{3}(t)-1+h_{\mathrm{ref}})\delta\gamma_{3}(t)\phi_{3}(t).

To compute a stationary point of
f
by Newton’s method, we have to discretize the Newton equation

Q^{*}_{f^{\prime}(\gamma)}\circ f^{\prime\prime}(\gamma)\delta\gamma+f^{\prime}(\gamma)=0^{*}

to compute a Newton direction
\delta\gamma
. Here, we use the same approximation techniques as in the example before. The retraction which is necessary to define Newton steps is chosen as a pointwise retraction by normalization (cf. (
35
)).
For the computation of a solution of the penalized problem we use a simple path-following method increasing the penalty parameter by a factor 1.2 in each iteration. For the cap with
h_{\mathrm{ref}}=0.1
, respectively
h_{\mathrm{ref}}=0.2
, this yields the elastic geodesics shown in Fig.
2
avoiding the north pole cap and connecting two points
\gamma_{0}
and
\gamma_{T}
.

6.3.
An Inextensible Elastic Rod

As a simple example from continuum mechanics, we consider the computation of equilibrium states of an inextensible elastic rod.
First we provide the energetic formulation of the force free situation. For more details on the derivation of the model see
[
16
]
. Its solution via an SQP-method in Hilbert space was considered in
[
29
]
.
We start with the following energy minimization problem

(40)

\begin{split}\min_{y\in\mathcal{M}}&\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}\sigma(s)\langle\ddot{y}(s),\ddot{y}(s)\rangle ds,\\
\mathcal{M}&=\{y\mid y\in H^{2}([0,1];\mathbb{R}^{3}),\dot{y}(s)\in\mathbb{S}^{2}\,\mbox{on}\,[0,1]\}.\end{split}

Since
\dot{y}(s)\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
for all
s\in[0,1]
the rod is inextensible with fixed length
1
. The following boundary conditions are imposed:

(41)

\displaystyle\begin{split}y(0)=y_{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{3},\,\dot{y}(0)=v_{a}\in\mathbb{S}^{2},\;y(1)=y_{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{3},\,\dot{y}(1)=v_{b}\in\mathbb{S}^{2}.\end{split}

The quantity
\overline{\sigma}>\sigma(s)\geq\underline{\sigma}>0
is the flexural stiffness of the rod, and
\dot{y}
,
\ddot{y}
are the derivatives of
y
with respect to
s\in[0,1]
.
Introducing
v(s)\coloneqq\dot{y}(s)
we reformulate (
40
) as a mixed problem:

(42)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\min_{(y,v)\in Y\times\mathcal{V}}&\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}\sigma\langle\dot{v},\dot{v}\rangle\,ds\quad\mbox{ s.t. }\quad\dot{y}-v=0\\
&Y=\{y\in H^{2}([0,1];\mathbb{R}^{3})\,\,|\,\,y(0)=y_{a},\,\,y(1)=y_{b}\},\\
&\mathcal{V}=\{v\in H^{1}([0,1];\mathbb{S}^{2})\,\,|\,\,v(0)=v_{a},\,\,v(1)=v_{b}\}.\end{split}

To derive equilibrium conditions for problem (
42
) we define the Lagrangian function

L(y,v,\lambda)=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{2}\sigma\left\langle\dot{v},\dot{v}\right\rangle+\lambda(\dot{y}-v)\,ds

using a Lagrangian multiplier
\lambda\in\Lambda\coloneqq L_{2}([0,1];\mathbb{R}^{3})
. In the presence of an additional (possibly non-conservative) force-field
\omega\colon\mathbb{R}^{3}\to(\mathbb{R}^{3})^{*}
we obtain the following equilibrium conditions via setting the derivatives of the Lagrangian, with force term added, to zero:

(43)

\begin{split}\int_{0}^{1}\omega(y)\phi_{y}+\lambda(\dot{\phi_{y}})\,ds&=0\quad\text{ for all }\phi_{y}\in Y\\
\int_{0}^{1}\sigma\left\langle\dot{v},\dot{\phi_{v}}\right\rangle-\lambda(\phi_{v})ds&=0\quad\text{ for all }\phi_{v}\in T_{v}\mathcal{V}\\
\int_{0}^{1}\phi_{\lambda}(\dot{y}-v)ds&=0\quad\text{ for all }\phi_{\lambda}\in\Lambda\end{split}

Hence, have to find a zero of the mapping

F\colon Y\times\mathcal{V}\times\Lambda\to Y^{*}\times T^{*}\mathcal{V}\times\Lambda^{*},

defined by (
43
). For brevity we set
\mathcal{X}=Y\times\mathcal{V}\times\Lambda
and
x=(y,v,\lambda)
and obtain a mapping
F\colon\mathcal{X}\to T^{*}\mathcal{X}
.

To define Newton steps, we need retractions
R_{x}\colon T_{x}\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{X}
and vector transports
{\mathop{V}\limits^{\vbox to-1.05487pt{\kern-2.0pt\hbox{\!$\scriptscriptstyle\rightarrow$}}}}_{\!\!x}\in\Gamma(\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{X}\times T^{*}_{x}\mathcal{X},T^{*}\mathcal{X}))
, which can be defined componentwise. Since
Y
and
\Lambda
are linear spaces, we can use the identity mappings for these components, while
\mathcal{V}
is equipped with the retraction by normalization (cf. (
35
)) and the vector transport by orthogonal projections, in a similar way as in the geodesic problem.
Since we again have an embedded vector bundle, we can use the formula

Q_{F(x)}^{*}\circ F^{\prime}(x)\delta x\,\phi=F_{\mathbb{R}^{9}}^{\prime}(x)\delta x\,\phi+F(x)({\mathop{V}\limits^{\vbox to-1.05487pt{\kern-2.0pt\hbox{\!$\scriptscriptstyle\rightarrow$}}}}_{\!\!x}^{\prime}(x)\delta x\,\phi)

for
x\in\mathcal{X}
and
\phi,\delta x\in T_{x}\mathcal{X}
. We denote the components of the tangent vectors by
\phi=(\phi_{y},\phi_{v},\phi_{\lambda})
and
\delta x=(\delta y,\delta v,\delta\lambda)\in Y\times T_{v}\mathcal{V}\times\Lambda
. The Euclidean derivative
F_{\mathbb{R}^{9}}^{\prime}(x)
of
F
is given as

\displaystyle F_{\mathbb{R}^{9}}^{\prime}(x)\delta x\,\phi

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\omega^{\prime}(y)\delta y\,\phi_{y}+\delta\lambda(\dot{\phi_{y}})\,ds

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{1}\sigma\langle\dot{\delta v},\dot{\phi_{v}}\rangle-\delta\lambda(\phi_{v})\,ds

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{1}\phi_{\lambda}(\dot{\delta y})-\phi_{\lambda}(\delta v)\,ds.

The part, introduced by the connection is given by

F(x)({\mathop{V}\limits^{\vbox to-1.05487pt{\kern-2.0pt\hbox{\!$\scriptscriptstyle\rightarrow$}}}}_{\!\!x}^{\prime}(x)\delta x\,\phi)=\int_{0}^{1}\langle\dot{v},\left(P^{\prime}(v)\delta v\,\phi_{v}\right)\dot{}\;\rangle-\lambda(P^{\prime}(v)\delta v\,\phi_{v})\,ds.

Observe that this term only contains contributions from the
\mathcal{V}
-component, since the other components are linear spaces.

7.
Conclusion

We have elaborated, how Newton’s method can be used to solve variational problems on manifolds. Our key observation was that these equations can be written as root finding problems for mappings into dual vector bundles. This allowed us to derive the differential geometric tools, necessary to implement a Newton method for this setting, together with an affine covariant damping scheme. We have further demonstrated that our algorithm is capable of solving a variety of problems in this class. Clearly, these problems are just simple examples for a large number of complex applications from various domains, to which our methods could be applied. This is, however, subject to future research.
