Eigenvectors of the De Bruijn Graph Laplacian: A Natural Basis for the Cut and Cycle Space
Anthony Philippakis, Neil Mallinar, Parthe Pandit, Mikhail Belkin

Abstract
We study the Laplacian of the undirected De Bruijn graph over an alphabet 𝒜 \mathcal{A} of order k k . While the eigenvalues of this Laplacian were found in 1998 by Delorme and Tillich [1] , an explicit description of its eigenvectors has remained elusive. In this work, we find these eigenvectors in closed form and show that they yield a natural and canonical basis for the cut- and cycle-spaces of De Bruijn graphs. Remarkably, we find that the cycle basis we construct is a basis for the cycle space of both the undirected and the directed De Bruijn graph. This is done by developing an analogue of the Fourier transform on the De Bruijn graph, which acts to diagonalize the Laplacian. Moreover, we show that the cycle-space of De Bruijn graphs, when considering all possible orders of k k simultaneously, contains a rich algebraic structure, that of a graded Hopf algebra.

Introduction

In this work, we consider the class of directed graphs whose vertices are all ordered
k
-tuples over a given alphabet
\mathcal{A}
, whose edges are all ordered
k+1
-tuples, and where a directed edge connects two
k
-tuples if the last
k-1
letters of the source vertex match the first
k-1
letters of the target vertex (see Figure 1). Known as a de Bruijn (“dB") graph of order k, it is an object of fundamental importance to both pure and applied mathematics, appearing in fields as diverse as combinatorics, electrical engineering, and bioinformatics, to name just a few examples. They were first introduced by de Bruijn
[
2
]
and (independently) by Good
[
3
]
, and they have been intensely studied since then.
In particular, there has been a rich literature on the spectral theory of dB graphs, highlighted by papers in the 1990s that identified the eigenvalues of the dB graph Laplacian.
[
4
,
1
]

However, while the eigenvalues of dB graphs are now well understood, a closed-form characterization of their eigenvectors has remained elusive.

This paper provides, to the best of our knowledge, the first closed-form description of the eigenvectors of the dB graph Laplacian.
Moreover, we use these eigenvectors as the starting point for a deeper analysis of the algebraic and spectral structure of dB graphs.
In the prior works that analyzed the spectra of dB graphs, the Laplacian and adjacency matrices are obtained by dropping the directional edge information from the directed dB graph.
In our work, we follow on this line of analysis.

The primary contributions of this work are the following:

(1) We simultaneously view words of length k and k+1 as the vertices and edges of a db graph, respectively, and as order k and k+1 tensor products of a vector space whose basis is indexed by the letters of our alphabet.

This duality allows us to seamlessly translate between graph theory and multilinear algebra, leveraging results from each. In particular, we demonstrate that essentially all of the spectral theory of dB graphs can be understood in terms of the actions of a pair of simple operators and their transposes on these tensor products, and we show that the incidence matrix, adjacency matrix, and Laplacian can all be expressed in terms of this pair of operators.

(2) We present a closed-form derivation of the eigenvectors of dB graphs, and we show that they provide natural orthogonal bases for the cut space and cycle space of dB graphs that have not previously been appreciated in the literature.

Our approach relies on the aforementioned duality between dB graphs and tensor products;
while certain operations are not natural on graphs, they are very natural on tensor spaces. One such operation is the discrete Fourier transform, which can be performed on a vector space after identifying the letters of our alphabet with elements of a finite group. This discrete Fourier transform naturally extends to an action on the tensor space. We show that, in the same way that the Fourier transform diagonalizes the Laplace operator on
\mathbb{R}
, our discrete Fourier transform makes the dB graph Laplacian,
L_{V}
a “tri-diagonalized" operator. From there, it’s eigenvectors are easily recognized in Fourier space.

Moreover, it is well known that the set of all functions on the edge space of a graph can be decomposed into the
cut space
(representing all cut sets on a graph) and it’s orthogonal complement, the
cycle space
(representing all cycles on a graph).
Cuts and cycles of a graph are of fundamental importance in the fields of mathematics and computer science, and have been studied at great length in both theory and application.
2

Yi and Moon [6]

Therefore, it is of great interest to understand the structure of these function spaces.
In our analysis, we show that the eigenvectors of the dB Laplacian can be naturally transformed into a natural basis for the cycle space and cut space of the graph. These bases are orthogonal (unlike most bases for the cut and cycle space), and have a number of symmetries.
Furthermore, while our analysis uses the undirected dB graph Laplacian, we find that the eigenvectors we derive are in the null space of the
directed
incidence operator.
As such, they simultaneously form a basis for the cycle space of directed and undirected De Bruijn graphs.

(3) We demonstrate that, when considering the collection of dB graphs across all possible orders k, the collection of cycle spaces has a rich algebraic structure, that of a graded Hopf algebra.

This observation allows us to connect dB graphs to the larger fields of representation theory and algebraic topology.
In particular, we find that the eigenvectors of the cycle space form a shuffle algebra.
These, and similar structures show up in various areas ranging from combinatorics

While the focus of our efforts is to better understand the spectral theory of dB graphs, it also opens the door to future applications in bioinformatics and natural language processing. In bioinformatics, the
k
-tuples are all subsequences of
k

k

The edges then represent
(k+1)
-mers and a weighted dB graph encodes the edges with the occurrence count of
(k+1)

A vector in the edge space of this dB graph is referred to as a

A number of important algorithms, ranging from assembling genomes to understanding gene regulation, are based on this

BoW vectors are fundamental objects in representing natural language and have driven much of the field of statistical and machine learning, in particular with respect to language data.

In this manuscript, we show that

Therefore, we can think of the eigenvectors of the dB graph Laplacian as a basis for decomposing this vector. Given that these eigenvectors play a role in functions on words that is analogous to that of sines and cosines on the real line, this opens the door to performing “harmonic analysis” on word frequencies. We therefore believe that this work provides the starting point for a fruitful framework for theorists and practitioners alike.

We show here how the adjacency, incidence, and Laplacian matrices of the DeBruijn graph are represented using the deletion operators and their adjoints.
As in the case with
\theta_{L},\theta_{R}
, the graph operators presented here simultaneously apply for all
k
, as opposed to the matrix form which is defined for a specific
k
.

We first present the matrix form of the adjacency, incidence, and Laplacian matrices.
Consider the DeBruijn graph
G_{k}=(\mathcal{A}^{k-1},\mathcal{A}^{k})
.
The adjacency matrix
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{A}^{k-1}\times\mathcal{A}^{k-1}}
is constructed as follows: for vertices
v_{1},v_{2}\in\mathcal{A}^{k-1},\mathbf{A}_{v_{1},v_{2}}=1
if
\exists
a directed edge
(v_{1},v_{2})
or
(v_{2},v_{1})\in\mathcal{A}^{k}
, and
\mathbf{A}_{v_{1},v_{2}}=2
if
v_{1}=v_{2}
(e.g. if there is a directed self-loop).
The incidence matrix
\mathbf{E}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{A}^{k-1}\times\mathcal{A}^{k}}
is constructed as follows: for directed edge
(v_{1},v_{2})\in\mathcal{A}^{k},\mathbf{E}_{v_{1},(v_{1},v_{2})}=-1
and
\mathbf{E}_{v_{2},(v_{1},v_{2})}=+1
. If
v_{1}=v_{2}
then
\mathbf{E}_{v_{1},(v_{1},v_{1})}=0
.
The vertex Laplacian
\mathbf{L}_{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{A}^{k-1}\times\mathcal{A}^{k-1}}
is defined as
\mathbf{L}_{V}=\mathbf{E}\mathbf{E}^{T}=2\mathbf{D}-\mathbf{A}=2\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{A}
, as
G_{k}
is a regular graph of degree 2.
The edge Laplacian
\mathbf{L}_{E}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{A}^{k}\times\mathcal{A}^{k}}
is defined as
\mathbf{L}_{E}=\mathbf{E}^{T}\mathbf{E}
.

All of these matrices have equivalent definitions as operators, though we introduce new normalization factors in the operators for ease of bookkeeping later in the manuscript.
We define the corresponding operators here.

Definition 7

(Adjacency operator)
.

For
G_{k}=(\mathcal{A}^{k-1},\mathcal{A}^{k})
, the adjacency operator is defined as
A:\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{A}}^{k-1}\to\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{A}}^{k-1}
,

A=(\theta_{R}^{*}\theta_{L}+\theta_{L}^{*}\theta_{R}).

(5)

We note that this is a different normalization than is standard for the adjacency matrix of a graph, that instead of having 1 in the adjacency you end up with
1/\mathcal{A}
.

Figure 3
:
The De Bruijn graph
G_{4}
on the alphabet
\mathcal{A}=\{a,b\}
. We highlight two vertices and the directed edge between them. By definition, the incidence matrix encodes
+1
on the element given by the edge and the incoming vertex, and
-1
on the element given by the edge and the outgoing vertex. The incidence operator, similarly, acts as
\mathcal{E}(baab)=\theta_{L}(baab)-\theta_{R}(baab)=aab-baa
.

Definition 8

(Incidence operator)
.

For
G_{k}=(\mathcal{A}^{k-1},\mathcal{A}^{k})
, the incidence operator is defined as
\mathcal{E}:\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{A}}^{k}\to\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{A}}^{k-1}
,

\mathcal{E}=\theta_{L}-\theta_{R}.

(6)

It’s adjoint is similarly defined as
\mathcal{E}^{*}:\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{A}}^{k-1}\to\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{A}}^{k}
,

\mathcal{E}^{*}=\theta_{L}^{*}-\theta_{R}^{*}.

(7)

Observe that the operator
\mathcal{E}
is equivalent to a differently normalized version of the incidence matrix
\mathbf{E}
where instead of
+1,-1
in the incidence matrix, the operator outputs
+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mathcal{A}}},-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mathcal{A}}}
.

Definition 9

(Vertex Laplacian operator)
.

For
G_{k}=(\mathcal{A}^{k-1},\mathcal{A}^{k})
, the vertex Laplacian operator is defined as,
\Lambda_{V}:\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{A}}^{k-1}\to\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{A}}^{k-1}
,

\displaystyle\Lambda_{V}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{E}\mathcal{E}^{*}

\displaystyle=(\theta_{L}-\theta_{R})(\theta_{L}^{*}-\theta_{R}^{*})

\displaystyle=(\theta_{L}\theta_{L}^{*}+\theta_{R}\theta_{R}^{*})-(\theta_{R}^{*}\theta_{L}+\theta_{L}^{*}\theta_{R})

\displaystyle=2I-A.

Definition 10

(Edge Laplacian operator)
.

For
G_{k}=(\mathcal{A}^{k-1},\mathcal{A}^{k})
, the edge Laplacian operator is defined as,
\Lambda_{E}:\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{A}}^{k}\to\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{A}}^{k}
,

\displaystyle\Lambda_{E}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{E}^{*}\mathcal{E}

\displaystyle=(\theta_{L}^{*}-\theta_{R}^{*})(\theta_{L}-\theta_{R})

\displaystyle=(\theta_{L}^{*}\theta_{L}+\theta_{R}^{*}\theta_{R})-(\theta_{R}^{*}\theta_{L}+\theta_{L}^{*}\theta_{R})

\displaystyle=(\theta_{L}^{*}\theta_{L}+\theta_{R}^{*}\theta_{R})-A.

Note: as mentioned above this is a slightly non-standard normalization for the graph operators, however it simplifies the bookkeeping with respect to constants of
|\mathcal{A}|
going forward.

2.3
Fourier analysis

2.3.1
Discrete Fourier / Hadamard Transform

Our construction of a natural orthogonal basis for the cycle- and cut-space of the de Bruijn graph will rely on a change of basis such that, rather than using the standard basis for the vector space, we instead use an orthogonal basis for
\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{A}}^{k}
where one of the vectors is the de Bruijn vector
\bullet=\frac{1}{\sqrt{q}}\mathbf{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{q}
. While the other basis vectors can be chosen arbitrarily (as long as they are orthonormal), it is especially natural to consider the following two choices for a change of basis, as they will act to diagonalize the de Bruijn graph Laplacian in the same way that the Fourier transform diagonalizes the laplacian on the real line:

1.

the Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT),
F_{q}\in\mathbb{C}^{q\times q}
, whose
i^{\text{th}}
column is given by
F_{q}^{(i)}=

\begin{pmatrix}\omega_{q}^{0\cdot i}&\omega_{q}^{1\cdot i}&\cdots&\omega_{q}^{(q-1)\cdot i}\end{pmatrix}^{T}

where
\omega_{q}=e^{-2\pi i/q}
is a
q^{\text{th}}
root of unity.

2.

the Walsh-Hadamard transform,

H_{q^{\prime}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2^{q^{\prime}}\times 2^{q^{\prime}}}
, which in our case can be used if
\exists\ q^{\prime}\geq 1
such that
q=2^{q^{\prime}}
.
The Hadamard transform for
q^{\prime}=1
coincides with
F_{2}
, in that
H_{1}=F_{2}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&1\\
1&-1\\
\end{array}\right)
. For
q^{\prime}>1
, we have the recursive definition
H_{q}=\begin{pmatrix}H_{q^{\prime}-1}&H_{q^{\prime}-1}\\
H_{q^{\prime}-1}&-H_{q^{\prime}-1}\end{pmatrix}

For an element
v\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{A}}^{k}
, the transformation to
\widehat{v}
is given by
\widehat{v}=Fv=\underbrace{(F_{q}\otimes F_{q}\otimes\cdots\otimes F_{q})}_{k\text{ times}}(v)=\widehat{v}_{0}\widehat{v}_{1}\cdots\widehat{v}_{k-1}
.
We have analogous definitions for the transformation
v=F^{-1}\widehat{v}
.

We can now define the right and left deletion operators as they act on an element in Fourier space; given
\widehat{v}=\widehat{v}_{0}\widehat{v}_{1}\cdots\widehat{v}_{k-1}
:

\displaystyle\widehat{\theta}_{L}(\widehat{v})

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\widehat{v}_{1}\widehat{v}_{2}\cdots\widehat{v}_{k-1}&\text{if }\widehat{v}_{0}=\bullet\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

(8)

\displaystyle\widehat{\theta}_{R}(\widehat{v})

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\widehat{v}_{0}\widehat{v}_{1}\cdots\widehat{v}_{k-2}&\text{if }\widehat{v}_{k-1}=\bullet\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

(9)

The adjoints of the Fourier deletion operators,
\widehat{\theta}_{L}^{*},\widehat{\theta}_{R}^{*}
, are the same as
\theta_{L}^{*},\theta_{R}^{*}
, respectively, except without the
\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mathcal{A}}}
normalization as this is absorbed by the Hadamard / Fourier transform.

Graph theory operators on Fourier basis

All of the operators given in the prior section can be defined using the Fourier deletion operators as well. For example,

\displaystyle\widehat{A}

\displaystyle=(\widehat{\theta}_{R}^{*}\widehat{\theta}_{L}+\widehat{\theta}_{L}^{*}\widehat{\theta}_{R})

\displaystyle=(F\theta_{R}^{*}F^{-1})(F\theta_{L}F^{-1})+(F\theta_{L}^{*}F^{-1})(F\theta_{R}F^{-1})

\displaystyle=F(\theta_{R}^{*}\theta_{L}+\theta_{L}^{*}\theta_{R})F^{-1}

\displaystyle=FAF^{-1}.

The operators
\widehat{\mathcal{E}},\widehat{\Lambda}_{V},\widehat{\Lambda}_{E}
are defined analogously:

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{E}}=(\widehat{\theta}_{L}-\widehat{\theta}_{R}),

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{E}}^{*}=(\widehat{\theta}_{L}^{*}-\widehat{\theta}_{R}^{*}),

\displaystyle\widehat{\Lambda}_{V}=\widehat{\mathcal{E}}\widehat{\mathcal{E}}^{*}=2I-\widehat{A},

\displaystyle\widehat{\Lambda}_{E}=\widehat{\mathcal{E}}^{*}\widehat{\mathcal{E}}=(\widehat{\theta}_{L}^{*}\widehat{\theta}_{L}+\widehat{\theta}_{R}^{*}\widehat{\theta}_{R})-\widehat{A}.

Fourier words

We define a key tensor space that we will use throughout the manuscript.
It is given by the intersection of the kernels of the incidence operators of dB graphs and denoted by
X_{\mathcal{A}}^{k}\subset V_{\mathcal{A}}^{k}
.
Formally,

X_{\mathcal{A}}^{k}=\text{ker}(\widehat{\theta}_{L})\cap\text{ker}(\widehat{\theta}_{R}).

(10)

We call the members of
X_{\mathcal{A}}^{k}
,
Fourier words
.
Notationally, we will refer to elements of
X_{\mathcal{A}}^{k}
by the variable
\widehat{x}
.
Recalling the definitions of
\widehat{\theta}_{L},\widehat{\theta}_{R}
we see that any
\widehat{x}=\widehat{x}_{0}\widehat{x}_{1}\cdots\widehat{x}_{k-1}\in X_{\mathcal{A}}^{k}
if
\widehat{x}_{0},\widehat{x}_{k-1}\neq\bullet
.

3.1
Eigenvectors of the vertex and edge Laplacians

In this section we work with a de Bruijn graph of order
k
,
G_{k}
. First, we state a useful result and refer the reader to the proof given by
Noschese et al. [26]
.

Lemma 3.1

(Eigenvectors and eigenvalues of an
n\times n
Topelitz matrix
[
26
]
)
.

The
n\times n
tri-diagonal Toeplitz matrix takes the form:

\begin{pmatrix}\delta&\tau&&&&\\
\sigma&\delta&\tau&&\text{\huge 0}&\\
&\sigma&\delta&\tau&&\\
&&\sigma&\delta&\tau&\\
&\text{\huge 0}&&\sigma&\delta&\tau\\
&&&&\sigma&\delta\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}

The eigenvalues and eigenvectors of the
n\times n
tri-diagonal Toeplitz matrix are well known.
The eigenvalues are given by

\lambda_{h}=\delta+2\sqrt{\sigma\tau}\cos\frac{h\pi}{n+1}\ \forall\ h\in\{1,\cdots,n\}

(11)

and, for
\sigma\tau\neq 0
, the right-eigenvectors for each
h\in\{1,\cdots,n\}
are given by

\displaystyle x_{h}

\displaystyle=[x_{h,1},x_{h,2},\cdots,x_{h,n}]^{T}

(12)

\displaystyle x_{h,k}

\displaystyle=(\sigma/\tau)^{k/2}\sin\frac{hk\pi}{n+1}\ \forall\ k\in\{1,\cdots,n\}.

(13)

Before stating our main result, we provide a motivating example to show how the Fourier transform, taking
\Lambda_{V}\to\widehat{\Lambda}_{V}
, tri-diagonalizes the vertex Laplacian of
G_{k}
.
We will then generalize this to derive the eigenvectors of
\widehat{\Lambda}_{V},\widehat{\Lambda}_{E}
.

Example 4
.

Consider, once again, the dB graph for the case
\mathcal{A}=\{a,b\},k=4
given by
G_{4}
.
We denote the Fourier basis for
\mathcal{A}
by
\widehat{a}=\bullet
and
\widehat{b}
by the orthogonal basis vector
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}1&-1\end{pmatrix}^{T}
.
The nodes of
G_{4}
are given by
3
-mers with tensor basis elements
\bullet\bullet\bullet,\bullet\bullet\widehat{b},\bullet\widehat{b}\bullet,\widehat{b}\bullet\bullet,\bullet\widehat{b}\widehat{b},\widehat{b}\widehat{b}\bullet,\widehat{b}\bullet\widehat{b},\widehat{b}\widehat{b}\widehat{b}
.
We compute the vertex Laplacian,
\widehat{\Lambda}_{V}
, for this example in Figure
4
.

Immediately, we see that the vertex Laplacian can be decomposed into tri-diagonal Toeplitz block matrices along the diagonal of
\widehat{\Lambda}_{V}
.
So the eigenvectors of
\widehat{\Lambda}_{V}
are given by the eigenvectors of each of these block matrices.

As given in Lemma
3.1
, we obtain the following closed-form expressions for the eigenvectors of the
3\times 3

G_{4}

\displaystyle x_{1}

\displaystyle=(\sin\dfrac{\pi}{4})(\bullet\bullet\widehat{b})+(\sin\dfrac{2\pi}{4})(\bullet\widehat{b}\bullet)+(\sin\dfrac{3\pi}{4})(\widehat{b}\bullet\bullet)

\displaystyle x_{2}

\displaystyle=(\sin\dfrac{2\pi}{4})(\bullet\bullet\widehat{b})+(\sin\dfrac{4\pi}{4})(\bullet\widehat{b}\bullet)+(\sin\dfrac{6\pi}{4})(\widehat{b}\bullet\bullet)

\displaystyle x_{3}

\displaystyle=(\sin\dfrac{3\pi}{4})(\bullet\bullet\widehat{b})+(\sin\dfrac{6\pi}{4})(\bullet\widehat{b}\bullet)+(\sin\dfrac{9\pi}{4})(\widehat{b}\bullet\bullet)

with eigenvalues:

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}

\displaystyle=1+2\cos\frac{\pi}{4},\quad\lambda_{2}=1+2\cos\frac{2\pi}{4},\quad\lambda_{3}=1+2\cos\frac{3\pi}{4}.

The eigenvectors and eigenvalues of the
2\times 2
block in this example are similarly given by:

\displaystyle x_{1}

\displaystyle=(\sin\dfrac{\pi}{3})(\bullet\widehat{b}\widehat{b})+(\sin\dfrac{2\pi}{3})(\widehat{b}\widehat{b}\bullet),

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}=1+2\cos\dfrac{\pi}{3}

\displaystyle x_{2}

\displaystyle=(\sin\dfrac{2\pi}{3})(\bullet\widehat{b}\widehat{b})+(\sin\dfrac{4\pi}{3})(\widehat{b}\widehat{b}\bullet),

\displaystyle\lambda_{2}=1+2\cos\dfrac{2\pi}{3}

where we abuse notation by denoting these eigenvectors as
x_{1},x_{2}
again, namely to emphasize that each separate tri-diagonal block is modeled by the equations in Lemma
3.1
.

3\times 3

\displaystyle x_{1}

\displaystyle=(\sin\dfrac{\pi}{2})(\widehat{b}\bullet\widehat{b}),

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}=1+2\cos\dfrac{\pi}{2}

\displaystyle x_{1}

\displaystyle=(\sin\dfrac{\pi}{2})(\widehat{b}\widehat{b}\widehat{b}),

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}=1+2\cos\dfrac{\pi}{2}.

We see that the eigenvectors for each tridiagonal block are obtained from the elements of
\emptyset=X_{\mathcal{A}}^{0}
,
\{\widehat{b}\}=X_{\mathcal{A}}^{1}
and
\{\widehat{b}\widehat{b}\}=X_{\mathcal{A}}^{2}
being “padded out" by the element
\bullet
three, two, and one time(s), respectively.

3
-mer can be obtained from elements of
X_{\mathcal{A}}^{i}
with
i<3
.

We now state our main result, and provide some explicitly computed examples derived from it in
C
.

Theorem 3.1

(Eigenvectors of
\widehat{\Lambda}_{V},\widehat{\Lambda}_{E}
)
.

Let
\widehat{x}\in X_{\mathcal{A}}^{i}
for any
i\in\{1,\cdots,k\}
, and
j\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
such that
i+j=k
.

For each
\widehat{x}
, we obtain a
(j+1)\times(j+1)
block, tri-diagonal Toeplitz matrix in
\widehat{\Lambda}_{V}:V_{\mathcal{A}}^{k}\to V_{\mathcal{A}}^{k}
.
We have a closed-form expression for the
j+1
eigenvectors of each block, and thus the eigenvectors of
\widehat{\Lambda}_{V}
itself, indexed as
x_{h},h\in\{1,\ldots,j+1\}
.

These are given by,

x_{h}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{j+1}\sin\bigg(\frac{\ell h\pi}{j+2}\bigg)(\widehat{\theta}_{L}^{*})^{j+1-\ell}(\widehat{\theta}_{R}^{*})^{\ell-1}\widehat{x}

(14)

There is one eigenvector in the kernel of

given by
\bullet

Note that for any
\widehat{x}\in X_{\mathcal{A}}^{k}

, therefore in such cases

with eigenvalue

For each
\widehat{x}
, we obtain
j+2

\widehat{\Lambda}_{E}:V_{\mathcal{A}}^{k+1}\to V_{\mathcal{A}}^{k+1}
, indexed as
v_{h},h\in\{0,1,\ldots,j+1\}

These are given by,

v_{h}=\sum_{\ell=0}^{j+1}\cos\bigg(\frac{(2\ell+1)h\pi}{2(j+2)}\bigg)(\widehat{\theta}_{L}^{*})^{j+1-\ell}(\widehat{\theta}_{R}^{*})^{\ell}\widehat{x}

(15)

and the same, corresponding eigenvalue
\lambda_{h}=(2-2\cos\big(\frac{h\pi}{j+2}\big))
.
Note the following:

•

for

, and so
v_{0}
is in the kernel of
\widehat{\Lambda}_{E}

•

in showing the above, we first prove that
v_{0}
is in the kernel of
\widehat{\mathcal{E}}

•

we know
\bullet\in\text{ker}(\widehat{\Lambda}_{E})

,
\widehat{x}\in\text{ker}(\widehat{\Lambda}_{E})

\widehat{x}\in\text{ker}(\widehat{\mathcal{E}})

Proof.

Let
x_{h}
be as defined above with corresponding eigenvalue
\lambda_{h}=2-2\cos(\frac{h\pi}{j+2})
for some
\widehat{x}\in X_{\mathcal{A}}^{i}
.
We first compute
\mathcal{E}^{*}x_{h}
, as this will be useful later.
For notational simplicity, we will let
\beta=\frac{h\pi}{j+2}
.

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{E}}^{*}x_{h}

\displaystyle=(\widehat{\theta}_{L}^{*}-\widehat{\theta}_{R}^{*})x_{h}=(\widehat{\theta}_{L}^{*}x_{h}-\widehat{\theta}_{R}^{*}x_{h})

\displaystyle=\Bigg[\sum_{\ell=1}^{j+1}\sin(\beta\ell)(\widehat{\theta}_{L}^{*})^{j+2-\ell}(\widehat{\theta}_{R}^{*})^{\ell-1}\ \widehat{x}-\sum_{\ell=1}^{j+1}\sin(\beta\ell)(\widehat{\theta}_{L}^{*})^{j+1-\ell}(\widehat{\theta}_{R}^{*})^{\ell}\ \widehat{x}\Bigg]

\displaystyle=\Bigg[\sin\beta(\widehat{\theta}_{L}^{*})^{j+1}+\sum_{\ell=1}^{j}\Big[\sin(\beta(\ell+1))-\sin(\beta\ell)\Big](\widehat{\theta}_{L}^{*})^{j+1-\ell}(\widehat{\theta}_{R}^{*})^{\ell}-\sin(\beta(j+1))(\widehat{\theta}_{R}^{*})^{j+1}\Bigg]\widehat{x}.

Utilizing that
\sin(\phi_{1})-\sin(\phi_{2})=2\cos(\frac{\phi_{1}+\phi_{2}}{2})\sin(\frac{\phi_{1}-\phi_{2}}{2})
, the inner terms become

\displaystyle\sin(\beta(\ell+1))-\sin(\beta\ell)

\displaystyle=2\cos(\frac{(2\ell+1)\beta}{2})\sin(\frac{\beta}{2}).

Additionally, using
\sin(\phi)=2\sin(\frac{\phi}{2})\cos(\frac{\phi}{2})
, we can simplify
\mathcal{E}^{*}x_{h}=

\displaystyle\Bigg[\sin(\beta)(\widehat{\theta}_{L}^{*})^{j+1}+2\sin(\frac{\beta}{2})\sum_{\ell=1}^{j}\cos\bigg(\frac{(2\ell+1)\beta}{2}\bigg)(\widehat{\theta}_{L}^{*})^{j+1-\ell}(\widehat{\theta}_{R}^{*})^{\ell}-\sin(\beta(j+1))(\widehat{\theta}_{R}^{*})^{j+1}\Bigg]\widehat{x}

\displaystyle=\left[2\sin(\frac{\beta}{2})\sum_{\ell=0}^{j}\cos(\frac{(2\ell+1)\beta}{2})(\widehat{\theta}_{L}^{*})^{j+1-\ell}(\widehat{\theta}_{R}^{*})^{\ell}-\sin(\beta(j+1))(\widehat{\theta}_{R}^{*})^{j+1}\right]\widehat{x}

\displaystyle=(2\sin(\frac{\beta}{2}))\left[\sum_{\ell=0}^{j+1}\cos(\frac{(2\ell+1)\beta}{2})(\widehat{\theta}_{L}^{*})^{j+1-\ell}(\widehat{\theta}_{R}^{*})^{\ell}\right]\widehat{x}

where the last step comes from,

\displaystyle-\sin(\beta(j+1))=-\sin(\beta(j+2-1))

\displaystyle=-\sin(h\pi-\beta)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{h}\sin(\beta)=(-1)^{h}2\sin(\frac{\beta}{2})\cos(\beta/2)

\displaystyle=2\sin(\frac{\beta}{2})\cos(h\pi-\frac{\beta}{2})

\displaystyle=2\sin(\frac{\beta}{2})\cos((2(j+1)+1)\frac{\beta}{2}).

Now we have that,

\displaystyle\widehat{\Lambda}_{V}(x_{h})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\widehat{\theta}_{L}-\widehat{\theta}_{R})\widehat{\mathcal{E}}^{*}x_{h}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\sin\frac{\beta}{2}\Big[\cos\bigg(\beta\frac{2(j+1)+1}{2}\bigg)(\widehat{\theta}_{L})(\widehat{\theta}_{R}^{*})^{j+1}+\sum_{\ell=0}^{j}\cos\bigg(\beta\frac{2\ell+1}{2}\bigg)(\widehat{\theta}_{L}^{*})^{j-\ell}(\widehat{\theta}_{R}^{*})^{\ell}

\displaystyle-\cos\frac{\beta}{2}(\widehat{\theta}_{R})(\widehat{\theta}_{L}^{*})^{j+1}-\sum_{\ell=1}^{j+1}\cos\bigg(\beta\frac{2\ell+1}{2}\bigg)(\widehat{\theta}_{L}^{*})^{j+1-\ell}(\widehat{\theta}_{R}^{*})^{\ell-1}\Big]\widehat{x}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\sin\frac{\beta}{2}\Big[\sum_{\ell=1}^{j+1}\bigg(\cos\big(\beta\frac{2\ell-1}{2}\big)-\cos\big(\beta\frac{2\ell+1}{2}\big)\bigg)(\widehat{\theta}_{L}^{*})^{j+1-\ell}(\widehat{\theta}_{R}^{*})^{\ell-1}\Big]\widehat{x}

where the last step comes from: (1)
\widehat{\theta}_{R},\widehat{\theta}_{L}^{*}
commute, as do
\widehat{\theta}_{L},\widehat{\theta}_{R}^{*}
, and
\widehat{\theta}_{R}\widehat{x}=\widehat{\theta}_{L}\widehat{x}=0
; (2) re-indexing
\sum_{\ell=0}^{j}
as
\sum_{\ell=1}^{j+1}
.
Now, using that
\cos\phi_{1}-\cos\phi_{2}=-2\sin(\frac{\phi_{1}+\phi_{2}}{2})\sin(\frac{\phi_{1}-\phi_{2}}{2})
:

\displaystyle\widehat{\Lambda}_{V}(x_{h})

\displaystyle=(2\sin\frac{\beta}{2})\Big[\sum_{\ell=1}^{j+1}-2\sin(\beta\ell)\sin\frac{-\beta}{2}(\widehat{\theta}_{L}^{*})^{j+1-\ell}(\widehat{\theta}_{R}^{*})^{\ell-1}\Big]\widehat{x}

\displaystyle=(-4\sin\frac{\beta}{2}\sin\frac{-\beta}{2})x_{h}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{h}x_{h}.

Since
\frac{\beta}{2}=\frac{0+\beta}{2}
and
\frac{-\beta}{2}=\frac{0-\beta}{2}
we reuse the same identity to see that

\displaystyle\lambda_{h}

\displaystyle=2(-2\sin\frac{\beta}{2}\sin\frac{-\beta}{2})

\displaystyle=2(\cos 0-\cos\beta)=2(1-\cos\beta)

as desired.
Note here, the form of the eigenvalue as
\lambda_{h}=2-2\cos\frac{h\pi}{j+2}
is equivalent to re-indexing as
\lambda_{j+2-h}=2-2\cos\frac{(j+2-h)\pi}{j+2}=2-2\cos(\pi-\frac{h\pi}{j+2})=2+2\cos\frac{h\pi}{j+2}
, for
h\in\{1,\cdots,j+1\}
.

Now that we have established that
x_{h},\lambda_{h}
are eigenvectors and eigenvalues of
\widehat{\Lambda}_{V}
, we notice that we can obtain a closed form expression for the eigenvectors of
\widehat{\Lambda}_{E}
as follows:

\displaystyle\widehat{\Lambda}_{V}x_{h}

\displaystyle=\widehat{\mathcal{E}}\widehat{\mathcal{E}}^{*}x_{h}=\lambda_{h}x_{h}

\displaystyle\widehat{\Lambda}_{E}(\widehat{\mathcal{E}}^{*}x_{h})

\displaystyle=\widehat{\mathcal{E}}^{*}\widehat{\mathcal{E}}(\widehat{\mathcal{E}}^{*}x_{h})=\lambda_{h}(\widehat{\mathcal{E}}^{*}x_{h}).

We have already calculated that

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{E}}^{*}x_{h}

\displaystyle=2\sin(\frac{\beta}{2})\sum_{\ell=0}^{j+1}\cos(\frac{(2\ell+1)\beta}{2})(\widehat{\theta}_{L}^{*})^{j+1-\ell}(\widehat{\theta}_{R}^{*})^{\ell}\widehat{x}

\displaystyle=2\sin(\frac{\beta}{2})v_{h}.

Plugging in as described above, we see that

\displaystyle\widehat{\Lambda}_{E}(\widehat{\mathcal{E}}^{*}x_{h})

\displaystyle=\lambda_{h}(\widehat{\mathcal{E}}^{*}x_{h})

\displaystyle\Rightarrow 2\sin(\frac{\beta}{2})\widehat{\Lambda}_{E}v_{h}

\displaystyle=2\sin(\frac{\beta}{2})\lambda_{h}v_{h}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\widehat{\Lambda}_{E}v_{h}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{h}v_{h}.

Note that we established
\widehat{\Lambda}_{V}x_{h}=\lambda_{h}x_{h}
for
h\in\{1,2,\cdots,j+1\}
and so we also have that
\widehat{\Lambda}_{E}v_{h}=\lambda_{h}v_{h}
for the same values of
h
.
For the case of
h=0
, we immediately observe that
\lambda_{h}=0
.
With regard to
\widehat{\Lambda}_{V}
we have that
x_{0}
corresponds to the tensor with all zeros since
h=0
will cause

to be zero for any
\ell

\widehat{\Lambda}_{V}(0)=0
.
However, we see that
v_{0}
takes on the following form,

\displaystyle v_{0}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=0}^{j+1}\cos(\frac{(2\ell+1)\cdot 0}{2(j+2)})(\widehat{\theta}_{L}^{*})^{j+1-\ell}(\widehat{\theta}_{R}^{*})^{\ell}\widehat{x}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=0}^{j+1}(\widehat{\theta}_{L}^{*})^{j+1-\ell}(\widehat{\theta}_{R}^{*})^{\ell}\widehat{x}

which is not a zero-tensor. By simple calculation,

\displaystyle\widehat{\Lambda}_{E}v_{0}

\displaystyle=\widehat{\mathcal{E}}^{*}\widehat{\mathcal{E}}v_{0}

\displaystyle=\widehat{\mathcal{E}}^{*}\widehat{\mathcal{E}}\sum_{\ell=0}^{j+1}(\widehat{\theta}_{L}^{*})^{j+1-\ell}(\widehat{\theta}_{R}^{*})^{\ell}\widehat{x}

\displaystyle=\widehat{\mathcal{E}}^{*}(\widehat{\theta}_{L}-\widehat{\theta}_{R})\sum_{\ell=0}^{j+1}(\widehat{\theta}_{L}^{*})^{j+1-\ell}(\widehat{\theta}_{R}^{*})^{\ell}\widehat{x}

\displaystyle=\widehat{\mathcal{E}}^{*}(\widehat{\theta}_{L}-\widehat{\theta}_{R})[(\widehat{\theta}_{L}^{*})^{j+1}\widehat{x}+(\widehat{\theta}_{R}^{*})^{j+1}\widehat{x}+\sum_{\ell=1}^{j}(\widehat{\theta}_{L}^{*})^{j+1-\ell}(\widehat{\theta}_{R}^{*})^{\ell}\widehat{x}]

\displaystyle=\widehat{\mathcal{E}}^{*}[(\widehat{\theta}_{L}^{*})^{j}+\widehat{\theta}_{L}(\widehat{\theta}_{R}^{*})^{j+1}+\sum_{\ell=1}^{j}(\widehat{\theta}_{L}^{*})^{j-\ell}(\widehat{\theta}_{R}^{*})^{\ell}

\displaystyle\quad-\widehat{\theta}_{R}(\widehat{\theta}_{L}^{*})^{j+1}-(\widehat{\theta}_{R}^{*})^{j}-\sum_{\ell=1}^{j}(\widehat{\theta}_{L}^{*})^{j+1-\ell}(\widehat{\theta}_{R}^{*})^{\ell-1}]\widehat{x}

\displaystyle=\widehat{\mathcal{E}}^{*}[\widehat{\theta}_{L}(\widehat{\theta}_{R}^{*})^{j+1}+\sum_{\ell=1}^{j-1}(\widehat{\theta}_{L}^{*})^{j-\ell}(\widehat{\theta}_{R}^{*})^{\ell}-\widehat{\theta}_{R}(\widehat{\theta}_{L}^{*})^{j+1}-\sum_{\ell=2}^{j}(\widehat{\theta}_{L}^{*})^{j+1-\ell}(\widehat{\theta}_{R}^{*})^{\ell-1}]\widehat{x}

\displaystyle=\widehat{\mathcal{E}}^{*}[\widehat{\theta}_{L}(\widehat{\theta}_{R}^{*})^{j+1}\widehat{x}-\widehat{\theta}_{R}(\widehat{\theta}_{L}^{*})^{j+1}\widehat{x}]

where the last step comes from re-indexing summations to see that all of the inner terms cancel.
Finally, we know that
\widehat{\theta}_{L}
commutes with
\theta_{R}^{*}

\widehat{\theta}_{R}
commutes with
\theta_{L}^{*}
, and
\widehat{x}\in\text{ker}(\widehat{\theta}_{L})\cap\text{ker}(\widehat{\theta}_{R})
, resulting in:

\displaystyle\widehat{\Lambda}_{E}v_{0}

\displaystyle=\widehat{\mathcal{E}}^{*}((\widehat{\theta}_{R}^{*})^{j+1}\widehat{\theta}_{L}\widehat{x}-(\widehat{\theta}_{L}^{*})^{j+1}\widehat{\theta}_{R}\widehat{x})

\displaystyle=\widehat{\mathcal{E}}^{*}(0)=0.

Therefore,
v_{0}\neq 0
and
\widehat{\Lambda}_{E}v_{0}=0=\lambda_{0}v_{0}\Rightarrow v_{0}\in\text{ker}(\widehat{\Lambda}_{E})
.
Note that in the steps above, we first showed that

is in the null space of the

incidence operator as well.

Definition 11

We define the operator

\displaystyle\Xi^{*}_{j,h}(v)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{j+1}}\sum_{\ell=0}^{j}\cos\Bigg(\frac{(2\ell+1)h\pi}{2(j+1)}\Bigg)(\theta_{L}^{*})^{j-\ell}(\theta_{R}^{*})^{\ell}(v)

The operator
\Xi^{*}_{j,h}

with all possibilities of adding
j

\bullet

The reason we define this operator in the adjoint form is to be notationally consistent with it’s action via the adjoints
\theta_{L}^{*},\theta_{R}^{*}

We provide an example to highlight this, and then show orthgonality of these operators.

Example 5

(
\Xi^{*}_{j,0}

Let
v\in V_{\mathcal{A}}^{k}

\displaystyle\Xi^{*}_{1,0}(v)

\displaystyle\cdots

Lemma 3.2

(Orthogonality)
.

For any
\widehat{x}\in X_{\mathcal{A}}^{k-j},\widehat{x}^{\prime}\in X_{\mathcal{A}}^{k-j^{\prime}}
with
0\leq j,j^{\prime}\leq k
, for all
h\in\{0,1,\cdots,j+1\},h^{\prime}\in\{0,1,\cdots,j^{\prime}+1\}
, we have that
\Xi^{*}_{j,h}(\widehat{x})\perp\Xi_{j^{\prime},h^{\prime}}(\widehat{x}^{\prime})
if: (1)
j\neq j^{\prime}
; or (2)
j=j^{\prime}
and
\widehat{x}\neq\widehat{x}^{\prime}
.

Proof.

Let
\xi_{j}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{(j+1)}}
. Then we have,

\displaystyle\langle\Xi^{*}_{j,h}(\widehat{x}),\Xi^{*}_{j^{\prime},h^{\prime}}(\widehat{x}^{\prime})\rangle=

\displaystyle\quad\langle\xi_{j}\sum_{\ell=0}^{j}\cos\Bigg(\frac{(2\ell+1)h\pi}{2(j+2)}\Bigg)(\theta_{L}^{*})^{j-\ell}(\theta_{R}^{*})^{\ell}\widehat{x},\xi_{j^{\prime}}\sum_{\ell^{\prime}=0}^{j^{\prime}}\cos\Bigg(\frac{(2\ell^{\prime}+1)h^{\prime}\pi}{2(j^{\prime}+2)}\Bigg)(\theta_{L}^{*})^{j^{\prime}-\ell^{\prime}}(\theta_{R}^{*})^{\ell^{\prime}}\widehat{x}^{\prime}\rangle

\displaystyle=\xi_{j}\xi_{j^{\prime}}\sum_{\ell=0}^{j}\sum_{\ell^{\prime}=0}^{j^{\prime}}\langle\cos\Bigg(\frac{(2\ell+1)h\pi}{2(j+2)}\Bigg)(\theta_{L}^{*})^{j-\ell}(\theta_{R}^{*})^{\ell}\widehat{x},\cos\Bigg(\frac{(2\ell^{\prime}+1)h^{\prime}\pi}{2(j^{\prime}+2)}\Bigg)(\theta_{L}^{*})^{j^{\prime}-\ell^{\prime}}(\theta_{R}^{*})^{\ell^{\prime}}\widehat{x}^{\prime}\rangle

\displaystyle=\xi_{j}\xi_{j^{\prime}}\sum_{\ell=0}^{j}\cos\Bigg(\frac{(2\ell+1)h\pi}{2(j+2)}\Bigg)\sum_{\ell^{\prime}=0}^{j^{\prime}}\cos\Bigg(\frac{(2\ell^{\prime}+1)h^{\prime}\pi}{2(j^{\prime}+2)}\Bigg)\langle(\theta_{L}^{*})^{j-\ell}(\theta_{R}^{*})^{\ell}\widehat{x},(\theta_{L}^{*})^{j^{\prime}-\ell^{\prime}}(\theta_{R}^{*})^{\ell^{\prime}}\widehat{x}^{\prime}\rangle.

We then have the following cases:

1.

\ell<\ell^{\prime}
:

\displaystyle\langle(\theta_{L}^{*})^{j-\ell}(\theta_{R}^{*})^{\ell}\widehat{x},(\theta_{L}^{*})^{j^{\prime}-\ell^{\prime}}(\theta_{R}^{*})^{\ell^{\prime}}\widehat{x}^{\prime}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle(\theta_{R})^{\ell^{\prime}}(\theta_{L}^{*})^{j-\ell}(\theta_{R}^{*})^{\ell}\widehat{x},(\theta_{L}^{*})^{j^{\prime}-\ell^{\prime}}\widehat{x}^{\prime}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle(\theta_{L}^{*})^{j-\ell}(\theta_{R})^{\ell^{\prime}-\ell}\widehat{x},(\theta_{L}^{*})^{j^{\prime}-\ell^{\prime}}\widehat{x}^{\prime}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle 0,(\theta_{L}^{*})^{j^{\prime}-\ell^{\prime}}\widehat{x}^{\prime}\rangle=0

2.

\ell>\ell^{\prime}
:

\displaystyle\langle(\theta_{L}^{*})^{j-\ell}(\theta_{R}^{*})^{\ell}\widehat{x},(\theta_{L}^{*})^{j^{\prime}-\ell^{\prime}}(\theta_{R}^{*})^{\ell^{\prime}}\widehat{x}^{\prime}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle(\theta_{L}^{*})^{j-\ell}\widehat{x},(\theta_{R})^{\ell}(\theta_{L}^{*})^{j^{\prime}-\ell^{\prime}}(\theta_{R}^{*})^{\ell^{\prime}}\widehat{x}^{\prime}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle(\theta_{L}^{*})^{j-\ell}\widehat{x},(\theta_{L}^{*})^{j^{\prime}-\ell^{\prime}}(\theta_{R})^{\ell-\ell^{\prime}}\widehat{x}^{\prime}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle(\theta_{L}^{*})^{j-\ell}\widehat{x},0\rangle=0

3.

\ell=\ell^{\prime}
:

\displaystyle\langle(\theta_{L}^{*})^{j-\ell}(\theta_{R}^{*})^{\ell}\widehat{x},(\theta_{L}^{*})^{j^{\prime}-\ell^{\prime}}(\theta_{R}^{*})^{\ell^{\prime}}\widehat{x}^{\prime}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle(\theta_{L}^{*})^{j-\ell}\widehat{x},(\theta_{R})^{\ell}(\theta_{L}^{*})^{j^{\prime}-\ell}(\theta_{R}^{*})^{\ell}\widehat{x}^{\prime}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle(\theta_{L}^{*})^{j-\ell}\widehat{x},(\theta_{L}^{*})^{j^{\prime}-\ell}\widehat{x}^{\prime}\rangle

Similarly we can split this expression into cases based on
j,j^{\prime}
,

\displaystyle j>j^{\prime}:

\displaystyle\langle(\theta_{L}^{*})^{j-\ell}\widehat{x},(\theta_{L}^{*})^{j^{\prime}-\ell}\widehat{x}^{\prime}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\widehat{x},(\theta_{L})^{j-j^{\prime}}\widehat{x}^{\prime}\rangle=\langle\widehat{x},0\rangle=0,

\displaystyle j<j^{\prime}:

\displaystyle\langle(\theta_{L}^{*})^{j-\ell}\widehat{x},(\theta_{L}^{*})^{j^{\prime}-\ell}\widehat{x}^{\prime}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle(\theta_{L})^{j^{\prime}-j}\widehat{x},\widehat{x}^{\prime}\rangle=\langle 0,\widehat{x}^{\prime}\rangle=0,

\displaystyle j=j^{\prime}:

\displaystyle\langle(\theta_{L}^{*})^{j-\ell}\widehat{x},(\theta_{L}^{*})^{j^{\prime}-\ell}\widehat{x}^{\prime}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle(\theta_{L}^{*})^{j-\ell}\widehat{x},(\theta_{L}^{*})^{j-\ell}\widehat{x}^{\prime}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\widehat{x},\widehat{x}^{\prime}\rangle=0\text{ by definition for }\widehat{x}\neq\widehat{x}^{\prime}.

∎

Corollary 3.1
.

We have that
\text{ker}(\widehat{\Lambda}_{E})=\text{ker}(\widehat{\mathcal{E}})
.

Proof.

We show this result in two parts:

\text{ker}(\widehat{\mathcal{E}})\subseteq\text{ker}(\widehat{\Lambda}_{E})
: Let
v\in\text{ker}(\widehat{\mathcal{E}})
. Then
\widehat{\mathcal{E}}v=0\Rightarrow\widehat{\Lambda}_{E}v=\mathcal{E}^{*}\widehat{\mathcal{E}}v=\mathcal{E}^{*}0=0
.

\text{ker}(\widehat{\Lambda}_{E})\subseteq\text{ker}(\widehat{\mathcal{E}})
:
Let
\widehat{\Lambda}_{V}:\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{A}}^{k}\to\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{A}}^{k}
and
\widehat{\Lambda}_{E}:\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{A}}^{k+1}\to\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{A}}^{k+1}
.

In Theorem
3.1
we proved that for each
\widehat{x}\in X_{\mathcal{A}}^{i}
with
i\in\{1,\cdots,k\}
and
j=k-i
, the element defined by
v_{0}=\sum_{\ell=0}^{j+1}(\theta_{L}^{*})^{j+1-\ell}(\theta_{R}^{*})^{\ell}\widehat{x}=(\sqrt{j+2})\Xi^{*}_{j+1,0}(\widehat{x})\in\text{ker}(\widehat{\Lambda}_{E})
.
To do so, we showed that
\widehat{\mathcal{E}}v_{0}=0
.
Therefore, we immediately have that
v_{0}\in\text{ker}(\widehat{\mathcal{E}})
.
The case of
X_{\mathcal{A}}^{0}=\emptyset
corresponds to
v_{0}=\bullet
and by the definition of
\bullet,\widehat{\theta}_{L}
, and
\widehat{\theta}_{R}
, it is clear that
\bullet\in\text{ker}(\widehat{\mathcal{E}})
.
And in the case of
\widehat{x}\in X_{\mathcal{A}}^{k+1}
, we similarly know by definition that
\widehat{x}\in\text{ker}(\widehat{\Lambda}_{E})
and
\in\text{ker}(\widehat{\mathcal{E}})
.

Therefore, we know that there is an eigenvector
\in\text{ker}(\widehat{\Lambda}_{E})
for each
\widehat{x}\in X_{\mathcal{A}}^{i}
for
i\in\{0,\cdots,k+1\}
, and that each of these eigenvctors are also
\in\text{ker}(\widehat{\mathcal{E}})
.
What remains to be shown is that there are no more elements of
\text{ker}(\widehat{\Lambda}_{E})
other than the ones we have listed here.
In doing so, we then establish that for all
v\in\text{ker}(\widehat{\Lambda}_{E})\Rightarrow v\in\text{ker}(\widehat{\mathcal{E}})
.

In
B
we show, via a combinatorial proof, that
\sum_{i=0}^{k+1}|X_{\mathcal{A}}^{i}|=q^{k+1}-q^{k}+1
where
q=|\mathcal{A}|
.
Therefore, we have enumerated a total of
q^{k+1}-q^{k}+1
elements in
\text{ker}(\widehat{\Lambda}_{E})
.
By definition of
\widehat{\Lambda}_{E}
as a linear map, we know that
\text{dim}(\widehat{\Lambda}_{E})=|\mathcal{A}^{k+1}|=q^{k+1}
.

To show that there can be no more elements in
\text{ker}(\widehat{\Lambda}_{E})
we observe that, in order to derive the eigenvectors of
\widehat{\Lambda}_{E}
we started with eigenvectors of
\widehat{\Lambda}_{V}
that correspond to non-zero eigenvalues
\lambda_{h}
for
h\in\{1,\cdots j+1\}
.
For each of these eigenvectors, we similarly obtained an eigenvector of
\widehat{\Lambda}_{E}
with non-zero eigenvalue,
\lambda_{h}
.
Since
\widehat{\Lambda}_{V}
is a linear map of dimension
q^{k}
and there is only one vector,
\bullet
, in the null space of
\widehat{\Lambda}_{V}
(see
Veerman and Lyons [27]
for a recent exposition on the dimension of the null space of the vertex Laplacian) we get precisely
q^{k}-1
eigenvectors of
\widehat{\Lambda}_{V}
(and thus of
\widehat{\Lambda}_{E}
) with non-zero eigenvalue.

Putting it all together, we see that
\text{dim}(\text{ker}(\widehat{\Lambda}_{E}))+\text{dim}(\text{Im}(\widehat{\Lambda}_{E}))=(q^{k+1}-q^{k}+1)+(q^{k}-1)=q^{k+1}=\text{dim}(\widehat{\Lambda}_{E})
, as desired.
Note that we have already established that these eigenvectors are orthogonal to each other in Lemma
3.2
, and so we have completely filled out a basis for
\widehat{\Lambda}_{E}
.
As such, there are no elements of
\text{ker}(\widehat{\Lambda}_{E})
that are unaccounted for, and we have shown that all such elements are
\in\text{ker}(\widehat{\mathcal{E}})
.

∎

We now show that the cycle space and cut space of

Definition 12

(Eigenvectors of
\widehat{\Lambda}_{E}

)
.

Let
\widehat{\Lambda}_{E}:\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{A}}^{k}\to\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{A}}^{k}
.

The cycle space,
W_{\mathcal{A}}^{k}
, is spanned by the basis vectors of
\text{ker}(\widehat{\mathcal{E}})=\text{ker}(\widehat{\Lambda}_{E})
.
The cut space,
(W_{\mathcal{A}}^{k})^{\perp}
, is spanned by the basis vectors of
\text{Im}(\widehat{\Lambda}_{E})
.
We proved in Theorem
3.1
and Lemma
3.2
that elements given by
\Xi^{*}_{j,h}
form an orthogonal basis for
\widehat{\Lambda}_{E}
, where
\Xi^{*}_{j,0}
lie in the kernel and
\Xi^{*}_{j,h},h\in\{1,\cdots,j\}
lie in the image of
\widehat{\Lambda}_{E}
, respectively.
Furthermore, we showed that
\Xi^{*}_{j,0}\perp\Xi^{*}_{j,h}
for
h\in\{1,\cdots,j\}
.

By Theorem
3.1
, we have that

\displaystyle W_{\mathcal{A}}^{k}

\displaystyle=\Xi^{*}_{k,0}(X_{\mathcal{A}}^{0})\oplus\Xi^{*}_{k-1,0}(X_{\mathcal{A}}^{1})\oplus\Xi^{*}_{k-2,0}(X_{\mathcal{A}}^{2})\oplus\cdots\oplus\Xi^{*}_{0,0}(X_{\mathcal{A}}^{k})

\displaystyle(W_{\mathcal{A}}^{k})^{\perp}

\displaystyle=[\oplus_{h=1}^{k-1}\Xi^{*}_{k-1,h}(X_{\mathcal{A}}^{1})]\oplus[\oplus_{h=1}^{k-2}\Xi^{*}_{k-2,h}(X_{\mathcal{A}}^{2})]\oplus\cdots\oplus[\oplus_{h=1}^{2}\Xi^{*}_{2,h}(X_{\mathcal{A}}^{k-2})]\oplus[\Xi^{*}_{1,1}(X_{\mathcal{A}}^{k-1})].

These are compactly written as
W_{\mathcal{A}}^{k}=\oplus_{j=0}^{k}\Xi^{*}_{j,0}(X_{\mathcal{A}}^{k-j})
and
(W_{\mathcal{A}}^{k})^{\perp}=\oplus_{j=1}^{k-1}[\oplus_{h=1}^{j}\Xi^{*}_{j,h}(X_{\mathcal{A}}^{k-j})]
.

The edge space of
G_{k}
is then given by
\mathcal{E}(G_{k})=W_{\mathcal{A}}^{k}\oplus(W_{\mathcal{A}}^{k})^{\perp}
.

In
C
we give explicit examples of cut and cycle space bases.

4
The space of
k
-mer counts

We now present a result that motivates the importance of the cycle space, which we derive an algebraic structure for in the following section.

Let
\Omega
be the set of all circular strings.

Definition 13
.

For
\omega\in\Omega
, let

\psi_{k}(\omega):\Omega\to\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{A}}^{k}

be the operator that maps
\omega
to it’s
k
-mer count tensor. The image of this operator, for a given
k
, is the set of all possible count tensors in
\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{A}}^{k}
.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
W_{\mathcal{A}}^{k}=\textup{ker}(\theta_{L}-\theta_{R})=\textup{ker}(\mathcal{E})\subset\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{A}}^{k}
.
Then
\text{Im}(\psi_{k}(\Omega))\subset W_{\mathcal{A}}^{k}
.

Proof.

Let
w\in\text{Im}(\psi_{k}(\Omega))
. Then
w
has a representation as a weighted, linear combination of elements from the tensor basis on
\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{A}}^{k}
. In other words,
w=\sum_{i=1}^{|\mathcal{A}|^{k}}\alpha_{i}v^{(i)}
, recalling that
v^{(i)}
is the tensor basis representation of a
k
-mer given by
v^{(i)}_{0}v^{(i)}_{1}\cdots v^{(i)}_{k-1}
(assume lexicographic ordering of
k
-mers), and
\alpha_{i}
represents the occurrence count of
v^{(i)}
.

Then we have that
\theta_{R}(w)=\sum_{i=1}^{|\mathcal{A}|^{k}}\alpha_{i}\theta_{R}(v^{(i)})
, with
\theta_{L}(w)
defined analogously.
Recall that we apply
\theta_{R}
(and
\theta_{L}
) to a
k
-mer string
v^{(i)}
as
\theta_{R}(v^{(i)})=\theta_{R}(v^{(i)}_{0}v^{(i)}_{1}\cdots v^{(i)}_{k-1})=v^{(i)}_{0}v^{(i)}_{1}v^{(i)}_{2}\cdots v^{(i)}_{k-2}
.

After applying these operations to all
k
-mers we are left with
k-1
-mers, some of which are identical.
We can re-index the
k-1
-mers as
v^{(j)}
for
j\in\{1,\cdots,|\mathcal{A}|^{k-1}\}
and retrieve new counts
\beta_{j}=\sum_{i\mid\theta_{R}(v^{(i)})=v^{(j)}}\alpha_{i}
to get that
\theta_{R}(w)=\sum_{j=1}^{|\mathcal{A}|^{k-1}}\beta_{j}v^{(j)}
.
Equivalently, we have that
\theta_{L}(w)=\sum_{j=1}^{|\mathcal{A}|^{k-1}}\gamma_{j}v^{(j)}
for
\gamma_{j}=\sum_{i\mid\theta_{L}(v^{(i)})=v^{(j)}}\alpha_{i}
.
Then
(\theta_{L}-\theta_{R})w=\theta_{L}(w)-\theta_{R}(w)=\sum_{j=1}^{|\mathcal{A}|^{k-1}}(\gamma_{j}-\beta_{j})v^{(j)}
.

In
G_{k}
, all
k-1
-mers are vertices and
k
-mers are directed edges (weighted by the relevant
k
-mer occurrences).
Then for a
k-1
-mer,
v^{(j)}
,
\beta_{j}
sums over all outgoing edges for the vertex corresponding to
v^{(j)}
and and
\gamma_{j}
sums over all incoming edges for the same vertex.
Since
G_{k}
is Eulerian we have that in-degree
=
out-degree for each node, thus
\beta_{j}=\gamma_{j}\ \forall\ j
.
Therefore
(\theta_{L}-\theta_{R})w=0
and so
w\in\text{ker}(\theta_{L}-\theta_{R})
.
∎

It is known that the kernel of the incidence matrix of a graph, both undirected and directed, coincides with the cycle space of the graph.
[
23
]

As we have just shown, any
k
-mer count tensor is an element of
W_{\mathcal{A}}^{k}
= ker
(\mathcal{E})
.
In turn, ker
(\mathcal{E})
, being the cycle space of the dB graph, is spanned by the set of basis vectors we have previously derived, which comprise a cycle basis for the directed dB graph.
Further note that the dimension of the cycle space of dB graphs,
|\mathcal{A}|^{k}-|\mathcal{A}|^{k-1}+1
, is smaller than the total number of
k
-mers,
|\mathcal{A}|^{k}
.

In Section
3
, we showed a construction for the basis elements of the cut space and cycle space of
G_{k}
.
Our construction additionally leads to a combinatorial proof of the dimension of the cycle space, based on counting the elements of
X_{\mathcal{A}}^{k}
which we use to build the basis vectors of the cycle space.
We present this argument in
B
.

5
Algebraic structure

In the previous sections, we considered dB graphs of order
k
for some fixed value
k
.
In this section, we are interested in the collection of dB graphs across all orders
k
,
simultaneously
.
By definition, the graph theory operators we defined, e.g.
\theta_{L},\theta_{R}
, act independently of
k
.
Therefore, the adjacency, incidence, and Laplacian operators act across the set of all orders of dB graphs simultaneously.
By taking this perspective, we show that the cycle space has a richer algebraic structure than just that of a vector space.

5.1
Background on tensor algebras and Hopf algebras

The results of this section will be cast in the language of graded tensor algebras and graded Hopf algebras.
We therefore begin with a brief review of some relevant concepts, mostly with an eye towards establishing notation.
A reader who is unfamiliar with tensor algebras and Hopf algebras will likely find this too quick, and we invite them to review
Dascalescu et al. [28]
for a detailed exposition.

Let
Z
be a vector space over a field
K
.
For a non-negative integer
k
, recall that we define the
k^{th}
-order tensor space via the tensor product of
Z
with itself
k
times:

\displaystyle Z^{k}=Z\underbrace{\otimes\cdots\otimes}_{\textrm{$k-1$ times}}Z.

We then define the
tensor algebra
over
Z
to be

\displaystyle T(Z)=\oplus_{k=0}^{\infty}Z^{k}

where
Z^{0}=K
.
We note that for any tensor algebra we can define the filtration
F_{k}=\oplus_{i=0}^{k}Z^{i}
such that that
F_{0}\subseteq F_{1}\subseteq\cdots
and
F_{i}\odot F_{j}\subseteq F_{i+j}
for all
i,j
.
As such, any tensor algebra is also a graded, filtered algebra.

Given an algebra on
Z
, a coalgebra can be defined if there exists
K
-linear maps
\Delta:Z\to Z\times Z
and
\varepsilon:Z\to K
, such that

\displaystyle(\text{id}_{Z}\otimes\Delta)\circ\Delta

\displaystyle=(\Delta\otimes\text{id}_{Z})\circ\Delta

\displaystyle(\text{id}_{Z}\otimes\varepsilon)\circ\Delta

\displaystyle=(\varepsilon\otimes\text{id}_{Z})\circ\Delta

where
\text{id}_{Z}
is the identity on
Z
.

\Delta
is referred to as the coproduct and
\varepsilon
is the counit.

T(Z)
carries a pair of well-known products and coproducts, which we define and provide examples of here.
Let
z=z_{1}z_{2}\cdots z_{k}\in Z^{k},z^{\prime}=z^{\prime}_{1}z^{\prime}_{2}\cdots z^{\prime}_{\ell}\in Z^{\ell}
.

Definition 14

(Concatenation product)
.

We use the symbol
\mathbin{\|}
to represent concatenation of two words, given by

\displaystyle zz^{\prime}=z\mathbin{\|}z^{\prime}

\displaystyle=z_{1}z_{2}\cdots z_{k}z^{\prime}_{1}z^{\prime}_{2}\cdots z^{\prime}_{\ell}.

Definition 15

(Shuffle product)
.

The shuffle product over two words is given by the sum over all the ways in which the letters of two words can be interleaved while preserving the letter-ordering of the individual words.

We represent this operation with the symbol

\exists

and it has the following, equivalent, recursive definitions given by

\displaystyle z\thinspace\raisebox{0.0pt}{\rotatebox{270.0}{$\exists$}}\thinspace z^{\prime}

\displaystyle=z_{1}(z_{2\cdots k}\thinspace\raisebox{0.0pt}{\rotatebox{270.0}{$\exists$}}\thinspace z^{\prime})+z^{\prime}_{1}(z\thinspace\raisebox{0.0pt}{\rotatebox{270.0}{$\exists$}}\thinspace z^{\prime}_{2\cdots\ell})

\displaystyle=(z_{1\cdots k-1}\thinspace\raisebox{0.0pt}{\rotatebox{270.0}{$\exists$}}\thinspace z^{\prime})z_{k}+(z\thinspace\raisebox{0.0pt}{\rotatebox{270.0}{$\exists$}}\thinspace z^{\prime}_{1\cdots\ell-1})z^{\prime}_{\ell}

where
z_{1\cdots k-1}
is the contiguous subsequence of
z
obtained from the
1^{st}
to
(k-1)^{st}
letters of
z
, with similar definitions holding for the other indexed words given above.

Example 6

(Shuffle product of two words)
.

Given the strings
ab
and
cd
for some letters
a,b,c,d\in Z
,

\displaystyle ab\thinspace\raisebox{0.0pt}{\rotatebox{270.0}{$\exists$}}\thinspace cd=abcd+acbd+cabd+acdb+cadb+cdab.

We now provide definitions and examples for the counit and coproducts with respect to the concatenation and shuffle products.

We will refer to
\mathbb{I}
as the empty word, which is the unit of
T(Z)
and acts on the concatenation and shuffle operators as
\mathbb{I}\mathbin{\|}z=z\mathbin{\|}\mathbb{I}=z
and
\mathbb{I}\thinspace\raisebox{0.0pt}{\rotatebox{270.0}{$\exists$}}\thinspace z=z\thinspace\raisebox{0.0pt}{\rotatebox{270.0}{$\exists$}}\thinspace\mathbb{I}=z
, respectively, for all
z\in T(Z)
.
The counit
\varepsilon
is defined as
\varepsilon(\mathbb{I})=1
and
\varepsilon(z)=0
for any other
z\neq\mathbb{I}
(here we simply take the underlying field
K=\mathbb{C}
).

We use a more general notion of an indexed word in defining the coproducts.
For a word
z=z_{1}z_{2}\cdots z_{k}
define an index set
I\subseteq\{1,\cdots,k\}
such that
I=\{i_{1},i_{2},\cdots\}
preserves order, meaning
i_{1}<i_{2}<\cdots
. Then we define
z_{I}=\mathbin{\|}_{i\in I}z_{i}
.
That is,
z_{I}
is the word made from the letters of
z
corresponding to indices of
I
, in an order-preserving manner.
Note that
z_{\emptyset}=\mathbb{I}
.

Example 7

(Indexed word)
.

Let
z=abcd
and
I={1,3}
. Then
z_{I}=ac
.

The coproduct of the concatenation product is given by the de-shuffle operation, and the coproduct of the shuffle product is given by the de-concatenation operation.

Definition 16

(De-concatenation coproduct)
.

Given a word
z=z_{1}z_{2}\cdots z_{k}
, the de-concatenation operator is given by

\displaystyle\Delta_{\mathbin{\|}}=\sum_{i=0}^{k}z_{1}\cdots z_{i}\otimes z_{i+1}\cdots z_{k}

where the
i=0
term corresponds to
\mathbb{I}\otimes z
and
i=k
corresponds to
z\otimes\mathbb{I}
.

Definition 17

(De-shuffle coproduct)
.

Given a word
z=z_{1}z_{2}\cdots z_{k}
, the de-shuffle operator is given by

\displaystyle\Delta_{\thinspace\raisebox{0.0pt}{\rotatebox{270.0}{$\exists$}}\thinspace}=\sum_{I\subseteq\{1,\cdots,k\}}z_{I}\otimes z_{\{1,\cdots,k\}\setminus I}.

Example 8

(De-concatenation example)
.

Let
z=abcd
. Then

\Delta_{\mathbin{\|}}(z)=\mathbb{I}\otimes abcd+a\otimes bcd+ab\otimes cd+abc\otimes d+abcd\otimes\mathbb{I}.

Example 9

(De-shuffle example)
.

Let
z=abc
. Then

\Delta_{\thinspace\raisebox{0.0pt}{\rotatebox{270.0}{$\exists$}}\thinspace}(z)=\mathbb{I}\otimes abc+a\otimes bc+b\otimes ac+c\otimes ab+ab\otimes c+ac\otimes b+bc\otimes a+abc\otimes\mathbb{I}.

Recall that a bialgebra is a vector space that is equipped with a product and coproduct, such that the coproduct is a homomorphism of the algebra and the product is a homomorphism of the coalgebra.
A bialgebra becomes a
Hopf algebra
when it also has an
antipode
. The antipode is given by the inverse of the identity mapping (see Chapter 4 of
Dascalescu et al. [28]
for a more thorough description of bialgebras and Hopf algebras).

For
Z
a
K
-bialgebra with product
\nabla
, unit
\eta
, coproduct
\Delta
, and counit
\varepsilon
, an antipode is a linear map
\gamma:Z\to Z
such that

\displaystyle\nabla\circ(\text{id}_{Z}\otimes\gamma)\circ\Delta=\nabla\circ(\gamma\otimes\text{id}_{Z})\circ\Delta=\eta\circ\varepsilon.

For the de-shuffle and de-concatenation coproducts on
T(Z)
we have an antipode.

Definition 18

(Antipode for de-shuffle and de-concatenation coproducts)
.

Let
z=z_{1}z_{2}\cdots z_{k}\in T(Z)
. Define
\gamma:T(Z)\to T(Z)
,

\displaystyle\gamma(z)=(-1)^{k}z_{k}z_{k-1}\cdots z_{1}.

The following theorem is well know and can be found in, for example,
Crossley [11]
and
Yeats [15]
.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
T(Z)
be a filtered, graded tensor algebra.

1.

With product

\exists

, coproduct
\Delta_{\mathbin{\|}}
, and antipode
\gamma
,
T(Z)
is a Hopf algebra.

2.

With product
\mathbin{\|}
, coproduct
\Delta_{\thinspace\raisebox{0.0pt}{\rotatebox{270.0}{$\exists$}}\thinspace}
, and antipode
\gamma
,
T(Z)
is a Hopf algebra.

3.

These two Hopf algebras are dual to each other.

When we take
Z
to be the vector space over a discrete alphabet
\mathcal{A}
and carry forward the definitions given above, we obtain a
combinatorial Hopf algebra
.
We will now proceed to consider the tensor algebras over the various vector spaces of
dB
graphs that we have defined in our manuscript.

5.2
Tensor algebras associated with De Bruijn graphs

Throughout this manuscript we have considered the following three vector spaces associated with dB graphs or order
k
over an alphabet
\mathcal{A}
.
Recall that we take
V_{\mathcal{A}}
to be a vector space with an orthogonal basis indexed by the letters of
\mathcal{A}
.
Then we define

\displaystyle V_{\mathcal{A}}^{k}

\displaystyle=V_{\mathcal{A}}\underbrace{\otimes\cdots\otimes}_{\textrm{$k-1$ times}}V_{\mathcal{A}}

\displaystyle W_{\mathcal{A}}^{k}

\displaystyle=\text{ker}(\theta_{L}-\theta_{R})\subseteq V_{\mathcal{A}}^{k}

\displaystyle X_{\mathcal{A}}^{k}

\displaystyle=\text{ker}(\theta_{L})\cap\text{ker}(\theta_{R})\subseteq V_{\mathcal{A}}^{k}.

We now consider the following filtered, graded tensor algebras given by the infinite direct sums across all values of
k
,

\displaystyle T(V)

\displaystyle=\oplus_{k=0}^{\infty}V_{\mathcal{A}}^{k}

\displaystyle T(W)

\displaystyle=\oplus_{k=0}^{\infty}W_{\mathcal{A}}^{k}

\displaystyle T(X)

\displaystyle=\oplus_{k=0}^{\infty}X_{\mathcal{A}}^{k}.

Recall that for a
K
-algebra
Z
with a product
\nabla
, a
derivation
,
D
, is a
K
-linear map that acts on
z,z^{\prime}\in Z
as

\displaystyle D\nabla(z,z^{\prime})=\nabla(Dz,z^{\prime})+\nabla(z,Dz^{\prime})

which is known as Leibniz’s law.

Lemma 5.1

(
\theta_{L},\theta_{R}
are derivations of the shuffle product)
.

For words
z,z^{\prime}\in T(Z)
, we have that

\displaystyle\theta_{L}(z\thinspace\raisebox{0.0pt}{\rotatebox{270.0}{$\exists$}}\thinspace z^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\theta_{L}(z)\thinspace\raisebox{0.0pt}{\rotatebox{270.0}{$\exists$}}\thinspace z^{\prime}+z\thinspace\raisebox{0.0pt}{\rotatebox{270.0}{$\exists$}}\thinspace\theta_{L}(z^{\prime})

\displaystyle\theta_{R}(z\thinspace\raisebox{0.0pt}{\rotatebox{270.0}{$\exists$}}\thinspace z^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\theta_{R}(z)\thinspace\raisebox{0.0pt}{\rotatebox{270.0}{$\exists$}}\thinspace z^{\prime}+z\thinspace\raisebox{0.0pt}{\rotatebox{270.0}{$\exists$}}\thinspace\theta_{R}(z^{\prime}).

Proof.

Let
z\in Z^{k},z^{\prime}\in Z^{\ell}
.
We start with the first recursive definition of the shuffle product, given by
z\thinspace\raisebox{0.0pt}{\rotatebox{270.0}{$\exists$}}\thinspace z^{\prime}=z_{1}(z_{2\cdots k}\thinspace\raisebox{0.0pt}{\rotatebox{270.0}{$\exists$}}\thinspace z^{\prime})+z^{\prime}_{1}(z\thinspace\raisebox{0.0pt}{\rotatebox{270.0}{$\exists$}}\thinspace z^{\prime}_{2\cdots\ell})
.
Then it is clear that
\theta_{L}(z\thinspace\raisebox{0.0pt}{\rotatebox{270.0}{$\exists$}}\thinspace z^{\prime})=z_{2\cdots k}\thinspace\raisebox{0.0pt}{\rotatebox{270.0}{$\exists$}}\thinspace z^{\prime}+z\thinspace\raisebox{0.0pt}{\rotatebox{270.0}{$\exists$}}\thinspace z^{\prime}_{2\cdots\ell}
..
Further, we can see that
\theta_{L}(z)\thinspace\raisebox{0.0pt}{\rotatebox{270.0}{$\exists$}}\thinspace z^{\prime}=z_{2\cdots k}\thinspace\raisebox{0.0pt}{\rotatebox{270.0}{$\exists$}}\thinspace z^{\prime}
and
z\thinspace\raisebox{0.0pt}{\rotatebox{270.0}{$\exists$}}\thinspace\theta_{L}(z^{\prime})=z\thinspace\raisebox{0.0pt}{\rotatebox{270.0}{$\exists$}}\thinspace z^{\prime}_{2\cdots\ell}
. A similar argument holds for
\theta_{R}
using the second recursive definition of the shuffle product.
∎

Corollary 5.1

(
\theta_{L}-\theta_{R}
is a derivation of the shuffle product)
.

As linear maps,
\theta_{L},\theta_{R}
are derivations of the shuffle product. Therefore, so is
\theta_{L}-\theta_{R}
.

From Lemma
5.1
, we immediately get the following result.

Proposition 5.1
.

T(V),T(W),T(X)
are graded algebras and are closed under the shuffle product.

Proof.

This is clear on
T(V)
by the base definitions we have given.
Take
x,x^{\prime}\in T(X)
.
Recall the definition that
x,x^{\prime}\in\text{ker}(\theta_{L})\cap\text{ker}(\theta_{R})
.
Then
\theta_{L}x=\theta_{R}x=\theta_{L}x^{\prime}=\theta_{R}x^{\prime}=\mathbf{0}
, where
\mathbf{0}
is the zero-tensor.
Using Lemma
5.1
, we have that

\displaystyle\theta_{L}(x\thinspace\raisebox{0.0pt}{\rotatebox{270.0}{$\exists$}}\thinspace x^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\theta_{L}(x)\thinspace\raisebox{0.0pt}{\rotatebox{270.0}{$\exists$}}\thinspace x^{\prime}+x\thinspace\raisebox{0.0pt}{\rotatebox{270.0}{$\exists$}}\thinspace\theta_{L}(x^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\mathbf{0}\thinspace\raisebox{0.0pt}{\rotatebox{270.0}{$\exists$}}\thinspace x^{\prime}+x\thinspace\raisebox{0.0pt}{\rotatebox{270.0}{$\exists$}}\thinspace\mathbf{0}=\mathbf{0}.

It should be clear that the same holds for

, and so
x\thinspace\raisebox{0.0pt}{\rotatebox{270.0}{$\exists$}}\thinspace x^{\prime}\in T(X)
.

Now take
w,w^{\prime}\in T(W)
. Then
w,w^{\prime}\in\text{ker}(\theta_{L}-\theta_{R})
.
As
\theta_{L}-\theta_{R}
is a derivation of the shuffle product, we similarly have that

\displaystyle(\theta_{L}-\theta_{R})(w\thinspace\raisebox{0.0pt}{\rotatebox{270.0}{$\exists$}}\thinspace w^{\prime})

\displaystyle=(\theta_{L}-\theta_{R})(w)\thinspace\raisebox{0.0pt}{\rotatebox{270.0}{$\exists$}}\thinspace w^{\prime}+w\thinspace\raisebox{0.0pt}{\rotatebox{270.0}{$\exists$}}\thinspace(\theta_{L}-\theta_{R})(w^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\mathbf{0}\thinspace\raisebox{0.0pt}{\rotatebox{270.0}{$\exists$}}\thinspace w^{\prime}+w\thinspace\raisebox{0.0pt}{\rotatebox{270.0}{$\exists$}}\thinspace\mathbf{0}=\mathbf{0}.

Therefore
w\thinspace\raisebox{0.0pt}{\rotatebox{270.0}{$\exists$}}\thinspace w^{\prime}\in T(W)
.
∎

Proposition 5.2
.

T(V),T(X)
are closed under the concatenation product while

is not.

Proof.

It is obvious that
T(V)
is closed under
\mathbin{\|}

\widehat{x},\widehat{x}^{\prime}\in T(X)

Before proceeding, we require the following useful lemmas.

Lemma 5.2
.

For
\widehat{x}\in X_{\mathcal{A}}^{k}
we have that
\sqrt{\frac{j+1}{j}}\theta_{L}\Xi^{*}_{j,0}(\widehat{x})=\sqrt{\frac{j+1}{j}}\theta_{R}\Xi^{*}_{j,0}(\widehat{x})=\Xi^{*}_{j-1,0}(\widehat{x})

Proof.

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{j+1}{j}}\theta_{L}\Xi^{*}_{j,0}\widehat{x}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{j+1}{j}}\theta_{L}(\frac{1}{\sqrt{j+1}}\sum_{i=0}^{j}(\theta_{L}^{*})^{j-i}(\theta_{R}^{*})^{i})\widehat{x}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{j}}\sum_{i=0}^{j-1}(\theta_{L}^{*})^{j-i-1}(\theta_{R}^{*})^{i}\widehat{x}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{j}}\theta_{L}(\theta_{R}^{*})^{j}\widehat{x}

\displaystyle=\Xi^{*}_{j-1,0}\widehat{x}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{j}}(\theta_{R}^{*})^{j}\theta_{L}\widehat{x}

\displaystyle=\Xi^{*}_{j-1,0}\widehat{x}

since
\theta_{L}\widehat{x}=0
. The proof for
\theta_{R}\Xi^{*}_{j,0}
follows analogously.
∎

Corollary 5.2
.

As an immediate corollary to the prior lemma, we obtain the following recursion, for any
\widehat{x}\in X_{\mathcal{A}}^{k}
and
i\leq j
, for any
j
,

\displaystyle(\sqrt{j+1})\theta_{R}^{i}\Xi^{*}_{j,0}\widehat{x}=(\sqrt{j-i+1})\Xi^{*}_{j-i,0}(\widehat{x})

with an equivalent expression for
\theta_{L}
.

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
w\in W_{\mathcal{A}}^{k}
and
i,j
such that
i+j\leq k
.
Then
\theta_{L}^{i}\theta_{R}^{j}w\in W_{\mathcal{A}}^{k-i-j}
.
Further, for any
i,i^{\prime},j,j^{\prime}
such that
i+j=i^{\prime}+j^{\prime}
,
\theta_{L}^{i}\theta_{R}^{j}w=\theta_{L}^{i^{\prime}}\theta_{R}^{j^{\prime}}w
.

Proof.

Since
w\in W_{\mathcal{A}}^{k}
we have that
\theta_{L}w=\theta_{R}w
.
Additionally, it should be clear that

\displaystyle\theta_{L}(\theta_{L}-\theta_{R})w=(\theta_{L}-\theta_{R})\theta_{L}w=(\theta_{L}-\theta_{R})\theta_{R}w=\theta_{R}(\theta_{L}-\theta_{R})w=0

so
\theta_{L}w,\theta_{R}w\in W_{\mathcal{A}}^{k-1}
.
Repeated applications of
\theta_{R},\theta_{L}
give us that
\theta_{L}^{i}\theta_{R}^{j}w\in W_{\mathcal{A}}^{k-i-j}
.
It should be clear that this holds for any
i^{\prime},j^{\prime}
if
i+j=i^{\prime}+j^{\prime}
.
∎

We are now prepared to state a proposition, demonstrating that while we care about elements in the cycle space,
T(W)
, we need only be concerned with the space
T(X)
.

Proposition 5.3
.

Let
w_{k+\ell}\in W_{\mathcal{A}}^{k+\ell}
,
w_{k}\in W_{\mathcal{A}}^{k}
, and
\widehat{x}_{k}\in X_{\mathcal{A}}^{k}
for any
k,\ell
.
Then,

\displaystyle\langle w_{k+\ell},\Xi^{*}_{\ell,0}(\widehat{x}_{k})\rangle=\frac{\sqrt{\ell+1}}{\sqrt{|\mathcal{A}|^{\ell}}}\langle w_{k},\widehat{x}_{k}\rangle.

Proof.

We first note that by Corollary
5.2
,

\displaystyle\widehat{x}_{k}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\ell-\ell+1}\Xi^{*}_{\ell-\ell,0}(\widehat{x}_{k})

\displaystyle=(\sqrt{\ell+1})\theta_{R}^{\ell}\Xi^{*}_{\ell,0}\widehat{x}_{k}

Then noting, from Lemma
5.3
, that
\theta_{R}^{\ell}=\theta_{L}^{i}\theta_{R}^{j}
if
i+j=\ell
,

\displaystyle(\ell+1)\widehat{x}_{k}

\displaystyle=(\sqrt{\ell+1})\sum_{i=0}^{\ell}\theta_{L}^{i}\theta_{R}^{\ell-i}\Xi^{*}_{\ell,0}\widehat{x}_{k}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\widehat{x}_{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\ell+1}}\sum_{i=0}^{\ell}\theta_{L}^{i}\theta_{R}^{\ell-i}\Xi^{*}_{\ell,0}\widehat{x}_{k}.

Then for any
w_{k}
, we have that

\displaystyle\langle w_{k},\widehat{x}_{k}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle w_{k},\frac{1}{\sqrt{\ell+1}}\sum_{i=0}^{\ell}\theta_{L}^{i}\theta_{R}^{\ell-i}\Xi^{*}_{\ell,0}\widehat{x}_{k}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\ell+1}}\langle w_{k},\sum_{i=0}^{\ell}\theta_{L}^{i}\theta_{R}^{\ell-i}\Xi^{*}_{\ell,0}\widehat{x}_{k}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\ell+1}}\langle\sum_{i=0}^{\ell}(\theta_{L}^{*})^{i}(\theta_{R}^{*})^{\ell-i}w_{k},\Xi^{*}_{\ell,0}\widehat{x}_{k}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\ell+1}}\langle\sqrt{|\mathcal{A}|^{\ell}}w_{k+\ell},\Xi^{*}_{\ell,0}\widehat{x}_{k}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{|\mathcal{A}|^{\ell}}}{\sqrt{\ell+1}}\langle w_{k+\ell},\Xi^{*}_{\ell,0}\widehat{x}_{k}\rangle.

∎

We now show why the coproducts of
\Delta_{\thinspace\raisebox{0.0pt}{\rotatebox{270.0}{$\exists$}}\thinspace},\Delta_{\mathbin{\|}}
do not provide group homomorphisms on the algebra
T(X)
and
T(W)
.
We formally require that these group homomorphisms map
T(X)\to T(X)\otimes T(X)
and
T(W)\to T(W)\otimes T(W)
, respectively.
In both tensor algebras we will show that the coproducts don’t satisfy this as currently defined.
To do so, we will give examples with words in Fourier space, from an alphabet
\mathcal{A}=\{a,b\}
.
We arbitrarily assign the letter
\widehat{a}=\bullet
.

Consider the word
x\in T(X)
given by
x=\widehat{b}\bullet\widehat{b}
.
The coproducts operate on
x
as,

\displaystyle\Delta_{\mathbin{\|}}(\widehat{b}\bullet\widehat{b})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{I}\otimes\widehat{b}\bullet\widehat{b}+\widehat{b}\otimes\bullet\widehat{b}+\widehat{b}\bullet\otimes\widehat{b}+\widehat{b}\bullet\widehat{b}\otimes\mathbb{I}

\displaystyle\Delta_{\thinspace\raisebox{0.0pt}{\rotatebox{270.0}{$\exists$}}\thinspace}(\widehat{b}\bullet\widehat{b})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{I}\otimes\widehat{b}\bullet\widehat{b}+\widehat{b}\otimes\bullet\widehat{b}+\bullet\otimes\widehat{b}\widehat{b}+\widehat{b}\otimes\widehat{b}\bullet+\widehat{b}\bullet\otimes\widehat{b}+\widehat{b}\widehat{b}\otimes\bullet+\bullet\widehat{b}\otimes\widehat{b}+\widehat{b}\bullet\widehat{b}\otimes\mathbb{I}.

It is clear in both cases that the coproduct splits apart an element of
T(X)
into elements
\in T(V)
but
\not\in T(X)
, for example
\bullet\widehat{b},\widehat{b}\bullet\not\in T(X)
.

Now, take the word
w\in T(W)
given by
w=\Xi^{*}_{1,0}(\widehat{b}\widehat{b})=\frac{1}{2}(\bullet\widehat{b}\widehat{b}+\widehat{b}\widehat{b}\bullet)
.
The coproducts operate on
w
as,

\displaystyle\Delta_{\mathbin{\|}}(\bullet\widehat{b}\widehat{b}+\widehat{b}\widehat{b}\bullet)

\displaystyle=\Delta_{\mathbin{\|}}(\bullet\widehat{b}\widehat{b})+\Delta_{\mathbin{\|}}(\widehat{b}\widehat{b}\bullet)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{I}\otimes\bullet\widehat{b}\widehat{b}+\bullet\otimes\widehat{b}\widehat{b}+\bullet\widehat{b}\otimes\widehat{b}+\bullet\widehat{b}\widehat{b}\otimes\mathbb{I}+\mathbb{I}\otimes\widehat{b}\widehat{b}\bullet+\widehat{b}\otimes\widehat{b}\bullet+\widehat{b}\widehat{b}\otimes\bullet+\widehat{b}\widehat{b}\bullet\otimes\mathbb{I}

\displaystyle\Delta_{\thinspace\raisebox{0.0pt}{\rotatebox{270.0}{$\exists$}}\thinspace}(\bullet\widehat{b}\widehat{b}+\widehat{b}\widehat{b}\bullet)

\displaystyle=\Delta_{\thinspace\raisebox{0.0pt}{\rotatebox{270.0}{$\exists$}}\thinspace}(\bullet\widehat{b}\widehat{b})+\Delta_{\thinspace\raisebox{0.0pt}{\rotatebox{270.0}{$\exists$}}\thinspace}(\widehat{b}\widehat{b}\bullet)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{I}\otimes\bullet\widehat{b}\widehat{b}+2(\bullet\otimes\widehat{b}\widehat{b})+2(\widehat{b}\otimes\bullet\widehat{b})+2(\bullet\widehat{b}\otimes\widehat{b})+2(\widehat{b}\widehat{b}\otimes\bullet)+\bullet\widehat{b}\widehat{b}\otimes\mathbb{I}+

\displaystyle\quad\mathbb{I}\otimes\widehat{b}\widehat{b}\bullet+2(\widehat{b}\otimes\widehat{b}\bullet)+2(\widehat{b}\bullet\otimes\widehat{b})+\widehat{b}\widehat{b}\bullet\otimes\mathbb{I}.

Similarly, we see that both coproducts split apart an element of
T(W)
into elements
\in T(V)
but
\not\in T(W)
, for example
\bullet\widehat{b},\bullet\widehat{b}\widehat{b},\widehat{b}\bullet,\widehat{b}\widehat{b}\bullet\not\in T(W)
.

We now show that we can re-index the alphabet in such a way to preserve the spaces
T(X),T(W)
and their closure under the products of
\thinspace\raisebox{0.0pt}{\rotatebox{270.0}{$\exists$}}\thinspace,\mathbin{\|}
, while ensuring that
\Delta_{\mathbin{\|}},\Delta_{\thinspace\raisebox{0.0pt}{\rotatebox{270.0}{$\exists$}}\thinspace}
are valid co-products on
T(X)
.
Under this re-indexing, we find that these coproducts still do not preserve the group elements of
T(W)
, and it is an open question to determine whether there are any natural coproducts on
T(W)
.
However, we have shown that
T(X)
conveys all of the relevant information of
T(W)
and we will now see that
T(X)
does, indeed, form dual combinatorial Hopf algebras under the
(\thinspace\raisebox{0.0pt}{\rotatebox{270.0}{$\exists$}}\thinspace,\Delta_{\mathbin{\|}})
and
(\mathbin{\|},\Delta_{\thinspace\raisebox{0.0pt}{\rotatebox{270.0}{$\exists$}}\thinspace})
(product, coproduct) pairs.

We start by introducing the notion of a
primitive word
.

Definition 19

(Primitive words on
T(X)
)
.

An primitive word on
T(X)
, in Fourier space, is any word
\in T(X)
that cannot be represented as the concatenation of any two words in
T(X)
.
Words of length
1
that are
\neq\bullet
are also considered primitive.

What this means is that an primitive word can only contain contiguous, repeat occurrences of the letter
\bullet
. If any sequence of letters, not necessarily the same, that are
\neq\bullet
occur in a contiguous sequence, then this word can further be broken down into primitive words.

We give an example of primitive words, and of how this re-indexing works on
X
.

Example 10
.

Take
\mathcal{A}=\{\bullet,\widehat{b},\widehat{c}\}
.
Example words
\in T(X)
include:
\widehat{b}\bullet\widehat{b},\widehat{b}\widehat{b}\bullet\widehat{b},\widehat{b}\bullet\bullet\widehat{c}\bullet\widehat{b},\widehat{b}\bullet\bullet\widehat{c}\widehat{b}\bullet\widehat{c}
.

From the previous definition, we see that the words
\widehat{b}\bullet\widehat{b}
and
\widehat{b}\bullet\bullet\widehat{c}\bullet\widehat{b}
are primitive, while
\widehat{b}\widehat{b}\bullet\widehat{b}
and
\widehat{b}\bullet\bullet\widehat{c}\widehat{b}\bullet\widehat{c}
are not and, in fact, can be reduced as
\widehat{b}\mathbin{\|}\widehat{b}\bullet\widehat{b}
and
\widehat{b}\bullet\bullet\widehat{c}\mathbin{\|}\widehat{b}\bullet\widehat{c}
, respectively.

Let us now consider a new alphabet,
\tilde{\mathcal{A}}
whose letters are given by the set of all primitive words.
For the sake of our example, we arbitrarily denote letters
\tilde{a}=\widehat{b}\bullet\widehat{b},\tilde{b}=\widehat{b},\tilde{c}=\widehat{b}\bullet\bullet\widehat{c}\bullet\bullet\widehat{c},\tilde{d}=\widehat{b}\bullet\widehat{c},\tilde{e}=\widehat{b}\bullet\bullet\tilde{c}
.
Now, each of
\tilde{a},\tilde{b},\tilde{c},\tilde{d},\tilde{e}
are primitive words.
In this new alphabet, the four words above become:
\tilde{a},\tilde{b}\tilde{a},\tilde{c},\tilde{e}\tilde{d}
.

Note that re-indexing the alphabet into primitive words does not change the spaces
T(W),T(X)
, and neither does it affect their closure under the products

\exists

and
\mathbin{\|}
.
However, on
T(X)
we get that
\Delta_{\thinspace\raisebox{0.0pt}{\rotatebox{270.0}{$\exists$}}\thinspace}
and
\Delta_{\mathbin{\|}}
map to
T(X)\otimes T(X)
because the elements
\bullet
can no longer be split up by the operations of de-shuffling and de-concatenation.
We observed in our prior example that these operations, when applied on the base alphabet
\mathcal{A}
, mapped elements of
T(X)
to elements with
\bullet
on the boundaries of the word.
When we consider primitive words as a letter then we will never obtain such an undesirable split when applying the coproducts.

To see this, consider
x\in T(X)
where
x=\widehat{b}\bullet\bullet\widehat{c}\widehat{b}\bullet\widehat{c}
.
The actions of deshuffling and deconcatenation, with respect to primitive words, are given by

\displaystyle\Delta_{\mathbin{\|}}(\widehat{b}\bullet\bullet\widehat{c}\widehat{b}\bullet\widehat{c})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{I}\otimes\widehat{b}\bullet\bullet\widehat{c}\widehat{b}\bullet\widehat{c}+\widehat{b}\bullet\bullet\widehat{c}\otimes\widehat{b}\bullet\widehat{c}+\widehat{b}\bullet\bullet\widehat{c}\widehat{b}\bullet\widehat{c}\otimes\mathbb{I}

\displaystyle\Delta_{\thinspace\raisebox{0.0pt}{\rotatebox{270.0}{$\exists$}}\thinspace}(\widehat{b}\bullet\bullet\widehat{c}\widehat{b}\bullet\widehat{c})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{I}\otimes\widehat{b}\bullet\bullet\widehat{c}\widehat{b}\bullet\widehat{c}+\widehat{b}\bullet\bullet\widehat{c}\otimes\widehat{b}\bullet\widehat{c}+\widehat{b}\bullet\widehat{c}\otimes\widehat{b}\bullet\bullet\widehat{c}+\widehat{b}\bullet\bullet\widehat{c}\widehat{b}\bullet\widehat{c}\otimes\mathbb{I}.

We immediately see that when primitive words are kept together, the coproducts both map
T(X)\to T(X)\otimes T(X)
.
Equipped with the antipode given in Definition
18
, we observe that
T(X)
forms dual combinatorial Hopf algebras with respect to
(\thinspace\raisebox{0.0pt}{\rotatebox{270.0}{$\exists$}}\thinspace,\Delta_{\mathbin{\|}})
and
(\mathbin{\|},\Delta_{\thinspace\raisebox{0.0pt}{\rotatebox{270.0}{$\exists$}}\thinspace})
.

6
Discussion

In this work we studied the vector spaces of De Bruijn graphs.
To do so, we introduced a novel set of operators,
\theta_{L}
and
\theta_{R}
, that allow us to translate the graph adjacency, incidence, and Laplacian matrices into operators on tensor spaces.
When we view the space of all
k
-mers as a tensor space,
V_{\mathcal{A}}^{k}
, the graph operators
A,\mathcal{E},\Lambda_{V}
, and
\Lambda_{E}
are seen to simultaneously operate over all possible
k
.
Through a Fourier analysis of these operators we are able to obtain a closed-form expression for the eigenvectors of both
\Lambda_{V}
and
\Lambda_{E}
, which further decompose into a basis for the cut space,
(W_{\mathcal{A}}^{k})^{\perp}
and cycle space,
W_{\mathcal{A}}^{k}
of all De Bruijn graphs.

We finally show that there is a subspace of the cycle space,
X_{\mathcal{A}}^{k}
that fully characterizes the cycle space itself.
That is the subspace of Fourier words, as defined by those words, in Fourier space, that neither start nor end with the special character,
\bullet
.
When considering the direct sum of all the vector spaces
X_{\mathcal{A}}^{k}
, we obtain a graded, filtered tensor algebra,
T(X)
.
Operating over the alphabet of primitive words on
T(X)
, this space carries the structure of dual combinatorial Hopf algebras under the products of concatenation,
\mathbin{\|}
, and shuffle,

\exists

, with coproducts given by de-shuffle,
\Delta_{\thinspace\raisebox{0.0pt}{\rotatebox{270.0}{$\exists$}}\thinspace}
, and de-concatenation,
\Delta_{\mathbin{\|}}
, respectively.

Future work:

One important future direction is to define a discrete exterior calculus on the edge space of De Bruijn graphs and expand on concepts related to cohomology and discrete differential geomtry.
We additionally see future work connecting the operators defined here to other algebras, for example Rota-Baxter algebras, as well as understanding whether there exists any compatible coproducts on the cycle space.
Another interesting direction is to connect further to modern graph theory perspectives, such as finding the Ihara-Zeta function for De Bruijn graphs and examining what this means.

Another natural extension would be to move from discrete operators to continuous ones.
Naturally, the discrete Fourier transform here could be replaced by it’s continuous counterpart, and analogous definitions from
\theta_{L},\theta_{R}
can be defined.
In this way, we would see De Bruijn graphs playing a larger rule in generalized time-series and sequential data analysis.
It would also be interesting to define an analogous graph structure on higher dimensional data beyond the 1d case, such as images, etc.
To the best of our knowledge, there has not been any study of a De Bruijn graph equivalent on 2d image patches.
Finally, the work here presents a basis that can fully represent
k
-mer count tensors.
In machine learning, natural language processing, and bioinformatics, string kernels on
k
-mer counts are commonly used, for some fixed values of
k
, and have in the past been computed using suffix trees and other algorithmic techniques.
We are hopeful that this analysis will pave the way to richer string kernel designs, and perhaps even a closed-form expression for a kernel function that measures similarity between circular strings across all
k
simultaneously.
