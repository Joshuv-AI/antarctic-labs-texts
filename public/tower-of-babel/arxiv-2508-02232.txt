Eye2Recall : Exploring the Design of Enhancing Reminiscence Activities via Eye Tracking-Based LLM-Powered Interaction for Older Adults
Lei HAN 1 ,, Mingnan Wei 1 ,, Qiongyan Chen 1 ,, Anqi Wang 2 ,, Rong Pang 4 ,, Kefei Liu 1 ,, Rongrong Chen 3 ,, David Yip 1,2 ,

Abstract
Photo-based reminiscence has the potential to have a positive impact on older adults’ reconnection with their personal history and improve their well-being. Supporting reminiscence in older adults through technological implementations is becoming an increasingly important area of research in the fields of HCI and CSCW. However, the impact of integrating gaze and speech as mixed-initiative interactions in LLM-powered reminiscence conversations remains under-explored. To address this, we conducted expert interviews to understand the challenges that older adults face with LLM-powered, photo-based reminiscence experiences. Based on these design considerations, we developed Eye2Recall , an system that integrates eye tracking for detecting visual interest with natural language interaction to create a mixed-initiative reminiscence experience. We evaluated its effectiveness through a user study involving ten older adults. The results have important implications for the future design of more accessible and empowering reminiscence technologies that better align with older adults’ natural interaction patterns and enhance their positive aging.

2.3
Eye-tracking technology for cognition in older adults.

Observing cognitive abilities (including attention, memory, and concentration) is important for monitoring levels of memory nostalgia in older adults
[
54
]
. In the field of neuropsychology and behavioural science, eye-tracking has emerged as a central tool for recording and analyzing the movement characteristics of the human eye
[
55
]
. Eye-tracking technology provides a non-invasive means of understanding their visual attention and cognitive processing, particularly in studies involving older adults. Eye movements and pupil size strongly correlate with mental imagery and autobiographical memory retrieval
[
56
]
.
Eye trackers are considered the next breakthrough in wearable
[
57
]
. The role of eye-tracking in cognition in older adults has attracted attention.
Imagery makes certain aspects of a situation explicit and accessible. Memory representations combine input from multiple sources with spatial information automatically registered in working memory as part of a dynamic motor system
[
58
,
59
]
. Therefore, even when there is no need for movement or task-solving relevance, the visual system instinctively records a spatial index or pointer to a location in the visual domain as an essential element of an episodic trajectory
[
60
]
.
Bruno Laeng et al. conclude that the generation of mental imagery relies on a process of gaze enactment that can be beneficial to visual memory
[
61
]
. Steve M.J. Janssen et al. further investigated the relationship between visual imagery and autobiographical memory using eye-tracking techniques, which contribute to understanding the link between eye movements and pupil size and memory retrieval processes, particularly autobiographical memory and provides insights into the cognitive effort involved in recalling different types of memories
[
62
]
. Sabrina Hoppe et al. demonstrated that eye movements can serve as indicators of curiosity, a personality trait strongly associated with well-being and learning, thereby suggesting the potential use of eye-tracking not only for memory recall but also for comprehending and adapting to individual personality traits such as curiosity in the context of elderly care
[
63
]
.

In summary, previous studies have highlighted the benefits of leveraging eye-tracking techniques in recognizing cognition and detecting attention. However, its role in offering a critical index for reminiscence activities in the originally imperceptible cognition process is under-explored. Therefore, combining the LLM-based dialogue with speech interaction, we probe the key considerations of designing effective and targeted reminiscence for older adults.

3.1.3
Design Considerations.

Based on the results, we identified two main potential design considerations for an AI-assisted reminiscence system for older adults:

DC1: Provide a natural, accessible, inclusive and immersive interactive memory experience.

•

Ensure the natural interactions with low cognitive load.

Two participants (
PP2
&
PP4
) recommended prioritising simplicity and clarity in interactions, emphasising the avoidance of complex commands or processes that might overwhelm older adults.
"Some browser-based application interfaces are challenging for older adults and may even confuse or interrupt their train of thought"
(
PP2
). To minimise interaction complexity and reduce cognitive load for older adults,
PP2
and
PP4
suggested using natural user interfaces (NUI) - such as gaze, gesture or voice commands - for interactivity.

•

Facilitate Heuristic-based AI Conversations.
All participants noted that older adults may struggle to express their stories and emotions without positive, patient, and heuristic guidance. It is crucial to design systems capable of providing empathy and active listening, as these qualities can enhance the quality of conversations and foster deeper connections and understanding among participants.
PP1
stated,
"Reminiscence activities aim to evoke personal memories through external cues, so the dialogue structure should be inspiring, making it (the AI agent) more encouraging for older people to share their stories."

PP2
highlighted,
"It is essential to create a supportive and comfortable environment that encourages users to share their experiences and emotions openly.

•

Enhance Accessibility.

All participants mentioned the importance of improving accessibility, as older adults often face physical or cognitive limitations. For instance, they may experience vision or hearing challenges, leading to a preference for larger content and louder audio. Therefore, it is crucial to ensure high-contrast visuals, appropriately sized text and elements, and clear audio cues to accommodate their needs.
PP2
suggested,
"Using large LED screens as user interfaces can provide more impressive visual effects and a wider viewing angle, making the user interaction experience richer and more intuitive, and making the entire system feel more accessible and immersive for older adults".

•

Provide a Comfortable and Safe Experience Environment.
All participants emphasized the importance of creating a comfortable and safe environment for older adults. As
PP3
noted,
"It is vital to ensure older adults understand that the activity or system is secure and will not compromise their privacy, as this encourages them to engage and share more openly."
To foster such an environment, it is essential to establish a sense of peace and trust, allowing older adults to relax and feel at ease. Additionally, user data collected by the AI system must be safeguarded.

DC2: Considering users’ cultural backgrounds and chronological order when selecting the old photos.

•

Cultural Background
.
PP3
emphasised the importance of integrating cultural differences into the system design and selecting photos that are appropriate to the local cultural context.
"[…] select old photos that reflect local traditions and history can help them relive and appreciate past experiences and culture" (
PP3
)
.
PP2
further stated,
"Some existing reminiscence tools provide content that local Chinese older adults find difficult to relate to […]"

•

External Memory Trigger Cues
.
Deepening and enriching the reminiscence experience is essential to provide appropriate external memory trigger cues
[
65
,
66
]
. As
PP1
suggested:
"Offering generic old photos that older adults have never seen before, such as those based on historic collective memories from their era and reflecting patterns familiar to them, can serve as effective memory triggers compared to personal family photos. These photos can stimulate older adults’ curiosity and expression and encourage reflection, broadening their horizons and enhancing their cognitive engagement."
Previous research has shown that, compared to personal old photos, generic old photos serve as more effective external cues to enhance self-esteem and reduce behavioral deficits in both healthy older adults and those with dementia
[
67
,
68
,
35
]
.

•

Chronological Order
.
Within the system architecture, old photos should be organized according to chronological sequencing. This arrangement not only assists older adults in revisiting the context of their lives but also provides a clear, progressive structure that enhances the fluency and coherence of the resulting narrative. As
PP3
stated,
"The focus should be on key milestones in an individual’s life journey, such as childhood, early adulthood, marriage, parenting, career, retirement. These milestones are often linked to important events or pivotal moments in life that can evoke deep emotions and memories"
. This careful organization ensures that the life story flows logically, creating a more meaningful and reflective experience for older people.

3.2.2
Key Features of
Eye2Recall
System

We outlined three key features of the system: User Visual Interest Detection, Prompt Design, and Visual Content Design.

User Visual Interest Detection.

We utilized the glasses-based eye-tracking device to identify the user’s visual interests. After the user views the photo (during the eye-tracking recording process), the system gathers comprehensive eye-tracking data, including gaze fixation points, eye movement trajectories, and duration of gaze fixations (see Apparatus
10.3
). This data enables the system to calculate and generate attention heatmaps, which indicate users’ attention distribution and cognitive engagement strategies during their visual interaction with the photos (see Apparatus
10.4
). These heatmaps, combined with prompt design (see
3.2.2
), provide comprehensive data support for the next personalised conversation.

Prompt Design: Processing visual interest for generating personalized conversation.

We developed two tailored prompt instructions to configure the agent. These prompts enable the agent to generate personalized and context-aware conversations by analyzing the content of old photos and the user’s eye movement heatmap.

•

Prompt 1.

In order to provide additional context using historical photos as external memory cues, which can potentially aid user recall,
Prompt 1
is designed to guide the agent in summarising the content of these photos (task description see Fig.
3
). Each old photo is summarised with basic background details, introducing the conversation. It sets the stage for more specific queries and deeper discussions in subsequent stages. Through extensive testing, the Creativity Level and Response Length settings were set to 1 and 600, respectively. These parameters produced output narratives of 1-2 minutes, striking an effective balance between creativity and relevance.

•

Prompt 2.

Next, by designing the
prompts2
, we instruct the agent to generate personalised conversations by asking the user questions based on the eye-tracking heatmap data. The agent combines external cues with autobiographical memories through questions, with no more than two questions asked per round (task description see Fig.
3
). Through extensive testing, the Creativity Level and Response Length settings were adjusted to 0.5 and 200, respectively. These adjustments resulted in output responses of 20-40 seconds, optimising relevance and engagement.

The generated text was converted into spoken audio using the TTS-1 model and presented to the user. The LLM-powered agent then asked relevant questions based on the user’s interactions. After two conversation rounds, the system automatically discussed the next photo. We tested the system prototype and analyzed the transcripts of the conversations between the user and the agent, as illustrated on the right side of Figure
3
.

Visual Content Design

Based on design
DC2
, we curated the visual content by considering users’ cultural backgrounds, incorporating external memory trigger cues, and organizing them chronologically.
Taking into account the users’ cultural background and age, we collected over 2,000 generic old photos from publicly available datasets, sourced from platforms such as the
National Library of China

[
69
]
,
Beijing Silvermine Archive

[
70
]
, and
Guangzhou Digital Library

[
71
]
. These generic photos, dating back to the 1970s and 1990s, were carefully selected for their significance in Chinese history, serving as meaningful visual content for reminiscence. They are intended to provide older adults with external memory trigger cues, aiding in recall and engagement.

To make the photos in chronological order, we then applied Boyer et al.’s
City of Collective Memory
theory
[
72
,
25
]
and Lu et al.’s
Memory Places in a Local Perspective
theory
[

to categorize the generic old photos into five distinct themes:

i)
Childhood
: refers to an individual’s early childhood relationships, both within and outside the family.
ii)
Cultural Heritage
: This includes cultural symbols, traditional festivals and folklore practices as forms of cultural expression.
iii)
Urban Development
: The preservation and enhancement of historically significant urban landscapes.
iv)
Trip of a Lifetime
: People leaving their hometowns or places of origin and moving to new destinations for various reasons, such as travel, job opportunities, education, or the pursuit of a better quality of life.
v)
Life Events
: Significant moments in a person’s life, such as birthdays, weddings, graduations, and other milestones.

To ensure accessibility and immersion, we carefully selected photos based on visual elements such as sharpness, colour, spatial proximity, direction of movement, depth, and perspective. Additionally, the historical photo content is centred on a large LED screen with only the clean background displayed (refer Fig.

We carefully considered the ethics of our study due to the vulnerability of older adults. During the user study, we collected both quantitative and qualitative data. We collected supplemental data that included: 1) conversation histories, 2) researcher field notes, 3) process photographs, 4) audio recordings of the semi-structured interviews. The audio recordings were transcribed into text paragraphs using a commercial Automatic Speech Recognition (ASR) system, iFLYTEK

Three researchers independently analyzed conversational data from 10 participants using a hybrid deductive-inductive coding approach to develop a codebook for identifying user response patterns and themes. To ensure methodological rigor, intercoder reliability was established through structured training sessions and iterative consensus discussions, adhering to qualitative analysis guidelines for CSCW/HCI research proposed by McDonald et al.

Eye-tracking data.
We analyzed the eye-tracking data of all participants for each thematic old photo, including the fixation ratio, saccade frequency, and heatmaps generated by the system during the participants’ gaze interaction. Two researchers identified the similarities and differences by conducting the distribution analysis on the focus points, thereby recognizing potential patterns.

6
DISCUSSION

This section first explores how eye-tracking cues can be leveraged to enrich reminiscence experiences. Next, we address ethical, privacy, and safety considerations. We propose four design implications derived from additional insights. Finally, we summarize the limitations of our work and outline potential directions for future research.

6.1
Enhancing Accessible Reminiscence for Older Adults: Optimizing Photo-Based Reminiscence Experiences Through Usability Improvements

Our study investigates how eye-tracking cues can guide LLM agents to support reminiscence – the process of recalling autobiographical memories
[
80
]
. Building on established research demonstrating that gaze patterns help regulate conversational flow
[
81
,
82
,
83
,
84
]
. We extend these findings to older adult populations.
This research introduces a new mixed-initiative interaction system of eye-tracking data to personalize LLM-powered reminiscence interactions (Section
5.1
). Specifically, in our study, all participants could successfully articulate their past experiences during interactions with the conversational agent. It can likely be attributed to these interaction modalities’ natural and intuitive nature, significantly reducing the cognitive burden typically associated with traditional input methods, such as keyboards or touchscreens
[
21
]
.

6.2
Ethical, Privacy and Safety Considerations.

Our results demonstrate the system’s capability to deliver low-intensity emotional counseling for older adults, supporting their emotional well-being (Section
5.3.1
). To fully realize these benefits, implementation should prioritize: Integrating real-time sentiment analysis with crisis intervention protocols and creating safe, non-distracting spaces that facilitate natural emotional expression.

Participants consistently identified emotional engagement as critical to reminiscence. We observed that several participants cried during reminiscence, likely triggered by recalling negative past experiences, such as the loss of loved ones. Negative memories and emotions commonly arise during reminiscence
[
85
]
. While there were no risks to their safety or health in our study, such emotional responses could pose potential concerns in a home setting and should be carefully managed. Hence, life-saving measures and real-time sentiment recognition could be integrated into the system to enhance safety and responsiveness. If feasible, having a remote monitoring by certified therapists monitor the sessions would provide an added layer of safety, helping to prevent serious emotional trauma or accidents.

Moreover, several participants expressed privacy concerns, particularly regarding storing and handling their personal data. To address these concerns, we recommend storing all data locally rather than on the cloud. This approach ensures greater control over sensitive information and reduces the risk of unauthorized access or data breaches. Furthermore, robust data encryption and access controls can further enhance privacy protections.

Furthermore, given the uncertainty of LLM outputs and the potential for cultural biases, errors or inappropriate content, ethical considerations are paramount. Several measures can be adopted to mitigate these issues, such as diversifying training data, conducting regular bias audits, implementing user feedback mechanisms, enhancing transparency and explainability, and offering user controls, such as ’topic-switch’ or ’skip content’ commands, to avoid sensitive or undesirable material.

6.3
Design Implications

We proposed four design implications derived from the key challenges participants faced and their expectations for improvement.

6.3.1
Integrating Real-time Sentiment Analysis and Dynamic Timely Responding Users’ Emotion.

The system’s inability to respond dynamically to emotional cues may hinder engagement
[
86
,
87
]
. Some participants (4/10) exhibiting heightened emotional states during autobiographical narration, including tearfulness when discussing developmental milestones. However, the system could not provide timely comfort through speech or semantic recognition.
In the future, integrating real-time sentiment analysis may solve this problem by enabling the system to detect changes in vocal tone and adjust the chat accordingly. By recognising signs of negative emotion, the system can adapt its conversational strategy to maintain positive, supportive interactions and minimise potential discomfort
[
88
,
89
]
. Furthermore, enabling users to customise their emotional sensitivity settings would ensure that interactions align with their individual preferences, making the system more adaptive and user-friendly.

6.3.2
Preserving and Building a Personal Digital Legacy.

Participants expressed a desire to preserve memories in the form of photos that reflect their life journey, in order to build a digital legacy (Section
5.3.2
). They hope to create a collection of digital assets to share with their children and future generations. Digital legacy materials hold significant value and meaning that can be passed down through the generations
[
90
]
. Therefore, it is becoming increasingly important for researchers to consider the potential role of their systems in preserving and transmitting digital legacies
[
91
]
.
To support this, the future system should allow users to easily store and organise recalled memories, including text, photos and audio, in a personal digital archive. To support this, the future system should enable users to effortlessly store and organise their recollections, comprising photos, and audio, within a personal digital archive. This structured archiving capability would facilitate the creation of coherent biographical narratives by transforming fragmented recollections into enduring digital legacies. These legacies would preserve individual life stories and be transmitted through guided reminiscence processes across generations.

6.3.3
Improving System Robustness and AI Performance

Our study revealed that the system struggled to recognize Chinese text in images due to OCR limitations, leading to misinterpretations. To address this, integrating more advanced OCR models tailored for multilingual recognition can enhance accuracy. Moreover, conversational breakdowns, such as abrupt interruptions and unnatural pauses, suggest the need for hybrid AI models that blend rule-based approaches with machine learning for more stable and responsive dialogues. Additionally, reducing latency in text-to-speech conversion is critical, particularly for users interacting in a second language, to ensure fluid and natural communication.

6.3.4
Providing Diverse Devices to Enhance Accessibility.

Two participants reported feeling discomfort after prolonged use of the head-mounted eye-tracking device (Section
5.1.3
); additionally, the system is incompatible with those wearing glasses. Non-wearable devices, can be used by a wider range of people, including those who wear glasses or have other physical limitations that make wearable devices difficult to use. We plan to explore integrating desktop-based eye-tracking devices into our system to offer more alternative solutions.

6.4
Limitation & Futurework

Demographic Constraints.

Our study involved 10 older adults, including 8 with normal cognitive status and 2 with MCI. However, the limited sample size affects the generalizability of our findings, as it may not fully reflect the diverse experiences of the broader older adult population. As the study focused solely on Chinese older adults, the applicability of the findings to older adults in other regions with different cultural contexts may be limited. Future studies should include a larger, more diverse sample of older adults from different regions, with a wider range of cognitive conditions such as dementia and physical disabilities.

Lack of Longitudinal Evaluation.

This study was limited to a single experiment, leaving the long-term impact of
Eye2Recall
on key aspects such as cognitive ability. Future research could investigate the long-term effects of
Eye2Recall
on older adults’ cognitive and emotional well-being, including its potential to slow cognitive decline and improve memory retention. It could also explore the role of personalized adjustments based on participants’ cognitive abilities and preferences and the impact of social interactions and shared memories during the activity. Furthermore, future studies could incorporate physiological data, such as EEG and heart rate monitoring, to gain a more comprehensive understanding of users’ responses to reminiscence activities over time.

7
CONCLUSION

We presented
Eye2Recall
, an LLM-based reminiscence system designed to empower older adults through accessible, mixed-initiative interaction. Grounded in user-centered design principles, our approach synthesized insights from formative interviews with experts, prioritizing accessibility, intuitive engagement, and user agency. The system’s integration of eye-tracking technology with generative conversational AI addresses a critical gap in HCI research—bridging passive input modalities with proactive LLM-driven interaction in aging-centered applications.
Our evaluation demonstrated positive user experience outcomes, with qualitative feedback highlighting the system’s potential while revealing opportunities for iterative refinement. These findings not only validate the feasibility of our design framework but also lay the groundwork for future research in adaptive assistive technologies. Moving forward, we plan to extend this work by incorporating longitudinal usability studies and refining the system’s adaptive dialogue strategies to better support diverse cognitive and physical needs within aging populations.

Appendix III - Detailed Description of the Visual Exploration Module

10.1
Details of the
Eye2Recall
Apparatus

The study employed a custom-designed binocular glasses-based eye-tracking device (see Appendix
10.2
) to record users’ eye movements and gaze fixation points accurately. The LED screen with a resolution of 5120*1536 was used to display the visual contents to the older adults. Soaiy stereo speakers and a Gmtd wireless microphone were used for interactions during testing. Audio and system recordings were employed to ensure the accuracy and integrity of the process, as well as to facilitate subsequent analysis and evaluation of participants’ responses and experiences. The
Eye2Recall
system was programmed in Python, primarily based on OpenCV and PyTorch. The host computer specifications included an Intel Core i9-11900 CPU, Nvidia GeForce Titan RTX GPU 3090 24GB, and 64GB of RAM. These recordings provided valuable data sources to guarantee the reliability of the research findings.

10.2
Eye Tracking Device Specification

The eye-tracking device (ETv1 from the Neural Encoding & Decoding Lab) can be used both online and offline with minimal error (<0.05°). To predict the gaze point location, it captures the user’s visual points of interest using pre-trained deep neural network models, including a pupil recognition model and a pupil-environment mapping model. The glasses-based device is equipped with two wide-angle environmental cameras (RGB cameras, 60 frames per second, 640×480 resolution,
170^{\circ}
visual angle field of view) to capture frontal environmental images relative to the subject and two eye cameras (infrared cameras, 60 frames per second, 640×480 resolution) to record the subject’s eye movements. Each infrared camera is paired with an IR LED to illuminate the intricate details of the eyes. The device operates on a wired transmission model, utilizing a USB 3.0 connection and video capture cards to enable real-time transmission and synchronous storage of images on a computer. Through system calibration, the device can determine the subject’s focus of attention by identifying specific local image regions within the environmental data that correspond to the eye movement data.

10.3
Eye Tracking Calibration Method

In the calibration phase, the older adults were asked to fixate sequentially displayed dots on the screen, distributed across the entire visual field and manipulated in real-time by the experimenter using a mouse. Participants were required to track these moving dots accurately and consistently with their eyes. The eye tracker simultaneously recorded their eye movements, capturing the eye trajectories. This data allowed the eye-tracking software to analyze the alignment between the participants’ eye movements and the dot locations on the screen, resulting in the calculation of specific calibration parameters. These parameters, once calculated, were used to fine-tune each participant’s eye tracker settings. It ensured the device accurately interpreted and recorded the participant’s eye movements during the data collection phase. Calibration performance depended on the number of points collected and whether the coverage was dense and comprehensive. Each user collected more than 100 points in our experiments and covered the entire screen. The calibration task typically took 20 to 30 seconds. The calibration program then took another 10 to 20 seconds to train the mapping model with the collected data and compute the position of the fixation point corresponding to the eye position. After the calibration, the eye tracker was equipped to capture the participant’s data, including gaze fixation points, which identify where the participant was looking at any given moment, eye movement trajectories that mapped the path of the gaze as it moved across different areas of interest, and the duration of gaze fixations which quantified the length of time the gaze remained stationary on a specific point.

10.4
Attention Heatmap Generation Method and Analysis

The gaze point data from the eye-tracker device was first pre-processed, which included data formatting and cleaning. The videos captured by the eye tracking device’s environmental cameras and eye cameras were aligned according to the UNIX timestamps. Then, we removed outlier frames caused by blinking and filtered out data affected by device errors. After the preprocessing, the videos captured by the environmental cameras of the eye tracking device were identified to divide the visual content area based on image matching. We relied on feature detection algorithms of scale-invariant feature transform (SIFT) to align visual features with the old photos for frame-to-frame alignment. Eye-tracking data on visual content areas were extracted and processed by kernel density estimation (KDE) to calculate the gaze density around each point.

Based on the heatmap analysis, we identified common and distinct visual points of interest among participants when viewing the photographs. Common focal points included faces (observed in 85% of participants), high-contrast areas (72%), unique or unusual elements (68%), and objects with a sense of motion or dynamic features (61%). These areas consistently captured the participants’ attention across the group, as indicated by higher fixation durations and frequencies.

However, a key difference was observed in the distribution of attention: while 80% of participants exhibited a concentrated focus on specific objects or people in the photos, the remaining 20% displayed a more scattered heatmap distribution, with attention spread across multiple areas rather than fixating on specific visual cues. This scattered pattern may suggest a less focused or more exploratory interaction style, potentially influenced by individual differences in cognitive processing or task interpretation.

10.5
Fixation Ratio and Saccade Frequency Data Analysis

By analyzing eye movement data from older adults observing old photos, we calculated the fixation ratio and saccade frequency for each type of photo (see Table
6
). The fixation ratio reflects the degree of attention an individual gives to specific visual targets, typically lasting 200 to 300 milliseconds. We used the median plus 1.5 times the median absolute deviation to determine the distance threshold between fixation points, with effective fixations requiring a minimum duration of 300 milliseconds. Saccade frequency (Hz) measures the number of rapid eye movements per second, reflecting the strategy for information exploration; effective saccadic angular velocities generally range from 20 to 60 degrees per second; hence, values exceeding 20 degrees per second constitute an effective saccade. All participants exhibited high fixation ratios, with an average of 82.05%, while the saccade frequency was lower, averaging 0.358 events per second. It indicates that participants were highly attentive and engaged with the photographs provided.
The average value for non-risk older adults ranged between 0.205 to 0.546 events per second, while P4 was notably lower than normal, being the lowest among all participants (0.134 events per second). In contrast, P8’s saccade frequency was significantly higher than normal older adults (0.697 events per second), the highest recorded. Results from

Kruskal-Wallis

Nevertheless, the average values of current results still suggest distinct patterns in visual behaviour among older adults with a potential trend towards mild cognitive impairment, such as P4’s lower saccade frequency possibly reflecting reduced information processing capability and P8’s higher frequency indicating more frequent information-seeking behaviour.
