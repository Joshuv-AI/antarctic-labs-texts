Partial Reversibility and Counterdiabatic Driving in Nearly Integrable Systems Preprint: APS/123-QED
Rohan Banerjee, Shahyad Khamnei, Anatoli Polkovnikov, Stewart Morawetz

Abstract
Adiabatic (or reversible) processes are the key concept unifying our understanding of thermodynamics and dynamical systems. Reversibility in the thermodynamic sense is understood as entropy-preserving processes, such as in the idealized Carnot engine, whereas in integrable dynamical systems it is understood as the conservation of the action variables. Between these two idealized limits, however, where the phase space can become mixed, things are much less clear. In this work, we first determine the extent to which reversible processes are even possible in this regime. We then explore how the dissipative losses resulting from rapidly driving these kinds of systems can be fought by approximate counterdiabatic driving. Finally, we argue that much of the phenomenology should be the same for quantum many-body systems with large degeneracy in the presence of integrability breaking perturbations.



I
Introduction

Among the endless variety of non-equilibrium physics, adiabatic (reversible) processes play a special role. In thermodynamics such processes play a pivotal role in defining bounds for efficiency of heat engines or refrigerators, and they have also come to play an important role in quantum computation
42
;
4
. Due to their reversibility, they do not suffer from the same uncontrolled losses as generic non-equilibrium processes.

The great disadvantage to which adiabatic processes are subject is the requirement of long wait times. This condition will be most familiar to many readers in the quantum context, such as the famous Landau-Zener problem
58
, where in order to remain adiabatic the driving rate should be smaller than the minimum value set by the energy gap. In classical systems, one can roughly understand the adiabatic condition as requiring that the time it takes for some externally controlled parameter of a system to change by an appreciable amount must be much longer than the time for the system to explore its accessible phase space. For example, in the familiar case of a gas kept under pressure by a movable piston, adiabaticity requires that gas particles should bounce around the chamber many times in the time it takes for the piston to move by a small amount.

Adiabaticity is straightforward to quantify in two extremes. In classical dynamical systems with just one degree of freedom (DOF), adiabaticity means conservation of the well-known adiabatic invariant
\oint pdx
. For those special many-particle systems which are integrable, and can therefore be cast in terms of action-angle variables, each action is a separate adiabatic invariant
6
, each of which must be conserved for adiabaticity. On the other hand, for many particle systems which are completely ergodic (a necessary assumption of statistical mechanics), adiabaticity requires conservation of another adiabatic invariant: the volume of phase space enclosed by an energy shell
38
. This reduces to the previous invariant in the one DOF case, and its logarithm is the more familiar thermodynamic entropy.

In between these two regimes, the situation is more complicated. If integrability is broken, but not strongly enough to induce complete ergodicity, the phase space is said to be mixed, composing a mixture of periodic and chaotic orbits
45
. In this case, there is no universally agreed upon definition of adiabaticity. The conventional definition of adiabaticity is reversibility
37
– that is, if one executes some slow change in an external parameter of the system, the entropy should not increase. When this change is accompanied by a change in the phase space from regular to mixed (i.e. when breaking integrability), it is not clear
a priori
if such a process is possible without an increase of entropy. As we shall see, trying to resolve this question will lead to a rich interplay between the strength of integrability breaking perturbations and non-adiabatic effects.

Generically, any finite-time external parameter change will result in diabatic (non-adiabatic) excitations in a system. It is therefore natural to ask the extent to which these diabatic effects can be suppressed. This is the focus of the now-mature field of “Shortcuts to Adiabaticity”

II
Reversibility & Integrability Breaking Under Slow Driving

Let us begin with a basic question: can perfect reversibility survive when driving breaks integrability? In both integrable and fully ergodic systems, reversibility is achievable if external parameters are varied infinitely slowly. Thus, we classify the reversibility of driving protocols in different systems by their behaviour in the infinitely slow-driving limit. As a testing ground, we define several illustrative toy models, whose behaviour in this limit is qualitatively different.

We consider two uncoupled harmonic oscillators with a nonlinear perturbation, one which is integrable and another which is nonintegrable for nonzero perturbation strength
\beta

We need an observable to use as a probe of reversibility. In quantum mechanics, if one initializes the system in a particular eigenstate, non-adiabatic evolution means there will be excitations and hence non-zero fluctuations in final energy. Likewise, thermodynamic adiabaticity implies that any microcanonical ensemble remains microcanonical, guaranteeing reversibility. For this reason, we characterize evolution by the final energy variance of the evolved, initially microcanonical distribution in phase space. This has the advantage of being directly measurable, unlike entropy. Reversible evolution implies that after any cyclic protocol, the state returns back to itself such that the final energy variance must be zero. This criterion can be also applied to quantum models.

How can we understand the unexpected energy fluctuations? For the I-I protocol, when sampling from the microcanonical distribution at
\beta=0
, initial conditions are split between different angular momentum sectors. As we turn on the nonlinearity, each sector evolves according to its own effective one-dimensional radial Hamiltonian and hence gains its own energy. This happens regardless of how slowly the perturbation is turned on.

For this toy model, we can compute the residual energy variance exactly, since the radial motion is one-dimensional and conservation of adiabatic invariants applies within each angular-momentum sector. As we discuss below, however, this residual energy variance in the infinitely slow regime also emerges for the nonintegrable I-N protocol and, more generally, for a broad class of higher-dimensional nonintegrable models with many degrees of freedom. It does not rely on integrability per se, but rather on emergence of approximate, dressed conservation laws. A more general method for determining this residual energy variance is based on the Schrieffer–Wolff (SW) transformation

;
9

A

H_{0}
is an emergent conserved quantity in each order of the expansion. Within this framework, one can perturbatively compute the residual energy variance for both the I–I and I–N protocols with minimal effort. The leading-order result in
\beta

\beta_{f}

Having established that these residual energy fluctuations are indeed physical, we would still like a probe which can distinguish the integrable and nonintegrable regimes. This can be done by means of the following cyclic protocol: first, we ramp
\beta(t)
as before. Then, to allow the system to explore the newly accessible phase space and avoid any aliasing effects from the periodic motion, we wait for a randomized waiting time (see Appendix

\beta_{f}

3

1

\tau

The most interesting results emerge for the I–N protocol. There, during the forward ramp, the energy variance plateaus in the slow-driving limit as if the system were integrable. Most of these energy fluctuations disappear under the cyclic protocol, again as in the integrable case. However, a finite plateau remains, indicating that the dynamics are irreversible even in the infinitely slow limit. If we were to consider the ramp governed by the SW Hamiltonian computed to any order in
\beta
, the dynamics would be completely reversible in this limit, since the SW Hamiltonian conserves (dressed)
H_{0}
at every order. Therefore, the lack of reversibility is a consequence of the asymptotic nature of the SW expansion in the nonintegrable case. Physically, as
\beta
increases during the forward ramp, more and more of the phase space become chaotic and the emergent (approximate) conservation of
H_{0}
breaks down. As soon as this happens, driving becomes irreversible. This process is controlled not by
\tau
, but rather by the (trajectory-dependent) value of
\beta
at which the motion becomes irregular. As we shall argue in Section
IV
, this reasoning extends to many-body systems.

III
Reversibility & Local CD Driving

Now, we study the extent to which these diabatic excitations can be fought by local counterdiabatic driving. There is a standard derivation of CD driving present in many papers (see e.g. Eq. (62)-(68) of [
36
]) so we highlight only the main points and refer interested readers to Appendix
C
for more detail. The essence of CD driving is to evolve the system according to a modified,
counterdiabatic Hamiltonian

H_{\mathrm{CD}}(t)=H(\beta(t))+\dot{\beta}\mathcal{A}_{\beta}(\beta(t))

(4)

The second term, where
\mathcal{A}_{\beta}
, known as the
adiabatic gauge potential
, is responsible for counteracting the pseudo-forces that emerge in the moving frame when external parameters of a system are changed at a finite rate. Evolution under
H_{\text{CD}}
is perfectly “adiabatic” regardless of how fast
\beta(t)
is changed.

However, as the exact
\mathcal{A}_{\beta}
does not even exist for a generic classically chaotic system
32
, approximations are necessary. Following previous work
14
;
51
;
7
, we use an expansion in Krylov space. Although this was originally devised for quantum systems, it has also been demonstrated
25
to work well in the classical limit. In this limit, we approximate

A_{\beta}^{(l)}=\sum_{k=1}\alpha_{k}\underbrace{\{H,\{H,...\{H}_{2k-1},\partial_{\beta}H\},

(5)

where
\alpha_{k}
are obtained variationally
50
. Technically, for reasons of numerical stability we use as our basis a different linear combination of these nested Poisson brackets, namely the Chebyshev polynomials (see Appendix
D
).

Interestingly, the energy variance reaches a minimum at finite

Finally, we apply local CD for the N-N protocol within the strongly nonintegrable regime. Here, we expect that there should be reversibility in a thermodynamic sense, provided driving is sufficiently slow. We probe the efficacy of local CD in the right panel of Fig.

The results here are quite straightforward, and similar to the previous two models: regardless of whether we execute the forward or cyclic process, local CD provides limited suppression of the energy fluctuations before reaching a plateau. Further suppression beyond this plateau requires either alternative approaches to local CD driving, or longer protocol times.

IV
Implications for Quantum Many-Particle Systems

Although we have analyzed several classical systems, we argue that much of the phenomenology observed for the I–N protocol should extend to a broad class of quantum or classical many-particle systems, provided they share certain key features. The central ingredient is that the initial integrable Hamiltonian
H_{0}
possesses degenerate symmetry blocks. Such Hamiltonians are particularly stable under perturbations, since these blocks remain decoupled to any finite order in the Schrieffer–Wolff (SW) transformation; see, e.g., Refs.
9
;
59
;
3
.

Let us consider a Hamiltonian of the type

\hat{H}=\hat{H}_{0}+\epsilon\hat{V},

(6)

where, like in our harmonic example,
\hat{H}_{0}
has a degenerate spectrum split into symmetry blocks. For concreteness, we follow Ref.
3
and consider
\hat{H}_{0}=\sum_{j}\sigma_{z}^{j}
. The authors showed that in this model, random matrix level statistics appear for infinitesimally small generic perturbations
\epsilon\hat{V}
. This unusual finding was explained using the SW transformation. Namely, to
O(\epsilon^{2})
the SW Hamiltonian is

\hat{H}_{\rm SW}=\hat{H}_{0}+\epsilon\hat{V}_{0}+{\epsilon^{2}\over 2h}\sum_{\ell>0}{[\hat{V}_{\ell},\hat{V}_{-\ell}]\over\ell},

(7)

where
\hat{V}_{\ell}
are the Fourier components of the perturbation
\hat{V}_{\rm rot}(t)
in the interaction picture (rotating frame with respect to
\hat{H}_{0}
). In Ref.
3
, the authors analyzed a one-dimensional geometry with
\hat{V}
, which does not contain
\hat{V}_{0}
. We assume that
\hat{V}_{0}
is non-zero like in our toy example. The only difference with
\hat{V}_{0}=0
case is that we need to replace
\epsilon\to\epsilon^{2}/h
. The SW Hamiltonian commutes with
\hat{H}_{0}
at every order of expansion so that its spectrum is also split into symmetry blocks. Within each block
\hat{H}_{\rm SW}
is nonintegrable, and satisfies ETH without fine-tuning, leading to the Wigner-Dyson level statistics. As
\epsilon
increases from zero and exceeds
\epsilon^{\ast}\sim h/N
, where
N
is the system size, the symmetry blocks start overlapping and one observes global mixing between the blocks.

Let us now argue that ramping
\epsilon
in this model shares qualitative features of partial reversibility as in our toy example. We again assume that initially the system is initialized in the microcanonical ensemble, i.e. all states with a fixed magnetization
M=\sum_{i}\sigma_{i}^{z}
are equally probable. Since
\hat{H}_{0}
within each symmetry block acts as a constant, it can be dropped from the SW Hamiltonian. Assume now we ramp
\epsilon
from
0
to a small value
\epsilon_{f}<\epsilon^{\ast}
. If the ramp is sufficiently slow, transitions between the blocks are exponentially suppressed by a finite gap between them. Within the SW picture, perturbing the system corresponds to simply turning on the Hamiltonian
\epsilon\hat{V}_{0}
starting from
\epsilon=0
. Because the eigenstates of
\hat{V}_{0}
do not depend on
\epsilon
, any protocol looks like a sudden quench which leads to a finite energy variance.

However, provided
\epsilon_{f}<\epsilon^{\ast}
, these fluctuations will vanish when we ramp
\epsilon
back to 0 as there are no transitions between different symmetry blocks. Therefore the cyclic protocol is exactly reversible in the adiabatic limit. The situation changes if
\epsilon^{\ast}<\epsilon_{f}\ll h
. When the symmetry blocks start overlapping, global thermalization between blocks sets in with an expected rate
\Gamma(\epsilon)\sim e^{-h/\epsilon}

2
. A cartoon illustration of this is shown in the right panel of Figure
3
. At this point the dynamics becomes irreversible and the cyclic ramp leads to a non-zero energy variance even in the adiabatic limit. An interesting consequence of this analysis is that for
\tau<\Gamma^{-1}(\epsilon_{f})
we expect that slower ramps lead to more irreversible behavior, as for faster ramps there is not enough time to transition between different symmetry blocks. This kind of phenomenon has been observed before in the context of Floquet systems
22
;
57
, where slow ramps lead to stronger heating and hence greater irreversibility.

To support these arguments, we come back to our toy example and provide numerical evidence for the I-N protocol. In order to ensure that the speed is the same at all values of
\beta
, we perform a linear ramp
\beta=vt
from
t=0
to
t=\beta_{f}/v
, and then immediately perform the reverse ramp with no waiting time. We plot the energy fluctuations at the end of this protocol in the left panel of Figure

. As expected, for the cyclic protocols at small
\beta_{f}
the energy fluctuations are very small (within numerical precision of zero, not shown on plot), which we attribute to a sufficiently weak perturbation
\beta<\beta^{\ast}
so that degenerate blocks do not cross. As
\beta_{f}
increases to the regime
\beta^{\ast}<\beta_{f}<h
, we indeed see the anticipated “anti-adiabatic” regime, where slower ramps lead to greater irreversibility. This is not captured within the SW analysis and hence is due to the non-perturbative (in
\beta
) mixing between the symmetry blocks. In the language of quantum mechanics this regime is explained through Landau-Zener avoided crossings, where the “adiabatic” dynamics are in fact irreversible, as different symmetry sectors mix, analogous to mixing between the resonant Floquet energies. This is schematically shown in the blow-up of Fig.

Connecting this to many-body systems, for
\epsilon>\epsilon^{\ast}
one anticipates a finite, but generally non-extensive, entropy increase in the system in the adiabatic limit due to an irreversible increase in its explored Hilbert space resulting from the the mixing between the symmetry blocks.

V
Conclusion

While adiabaticity is a well-defined notion in systems which are either integrable or ergodic, it is not uniquely defined for systems which have mixed phase spaces. Adopting reversibility as a measure of adiabaticity, we study the extent to which slow processes are reversible. We show that breaking integrability in a system with degeneracies is a fundamentally irreversible process, and provide numerical evidence supporting the claim for a specific toy model. The underlying reason is that such models can be characterized by approximate emergent symmetries, which disappear as integrability-breaking perturbations become stronger. There is an associated increase in entropy even in the infinitely slow driving limit.

We then test the efficacy of local counterdiabatic driving in the toy model in different regimes. We find that in all the cases they give significant suppression of non-adiabatic effects even for fast driving, however, they cannot improve past the plateau, which is generally higher than the one set by the infinitely slow protocol.

One obvious direction for future work is to provide numerical evidence for the irreversible energy fluctuations generated by breaking integrability in many-body models. Another is to explore whether there are alternative variational ansatze for which local counterdiabatic driving can provide even greater suppression of dissipation than demonstrated here by, for example, augmenting the adiabatic path
40
.

Here we provide further information about the Schrieffer-Wolff calculation used to estimate the energy fluctuations during the forward process. The easiest way to perform the Schrieffer-Wolff transformation is to first write the harmonic Hamiltonian

(8)

where
a_{x}=\tfrac{x+ip_{x}}{\sqrt{2}}
and similarly for
a_{y}
. Then one can go to the rotating frame (interaction picture) with respect to
H_{0}
, which leads to
a_{x,y}\to a_{x,y}e^{-it}
,
a^{\ast}_{x,y}\to a^{\ast}_{x,y}e^{it}
. In this way the perturbation
\beta V
splits into the Fourier Harmonics oscillating with integer frequencies
\omega=\pm\ell

\ell=0,1,..

11
and reads

H_{\rm SW}=H_{0}+\beta V_{0}+i{\beta^{2}}\sum_{\ell>0}{\{V_{\ell},V_{-\ell}\}\over\ell}+\dots,

(9)

where
\{A,B\}
is the Poisson bracket, which in terms of the complex amplitudes reads

\{A,B\}=i\sum_{j}\frac{\partial A}{\partial a_{j}^{\ast}}\frac{\partial B}{\partial a_{j}}-\frac{\partial A}{\partial a_{j}}\frac{\partial B}{\partial a^{\ast}_{j}}.

H_{\mathrm{NI}}:\quad\hat{V}_{0}=\frac{\hbar^{2}}{8m^{2}\omega^{2}}\Bigl((a_{x}^{\dagger})^{2}a_{y}^{2}+(a_{y}^{\dagger})^{2}a_{x}^{2}\\
+3n_{x}^{2}+3n_{y}^{2}+4n_{x}n_{y}+5n_{x}+5n_{y}+3\Bigr)\\
\end{split}

(10)

Notice that in both cases (and in general) the non-oscillating term

commutes with

\{V_{0},H_{0}\}=0
. This emergent symmetry exists in all orders of the SW expansion. Notice that

Wait time for cyclic protocol

We shall explain out choice of wait time that was used in the cyclic protocols. We shall use the I-I protocol as an example, but the reasoning applies to each model. Fig.
5
shows cyclic process without CD driving, varying the waiting time
T_{\mathrm{wait}}
. Without waiting time, the irreversibility of fast processes is not captured since it consists of a quench to a strong nonlinearity and then immediately a quench back, without time for the trajectories to evolve in the newly accessible phase space. A finite but constant wait time results in aliasing effects due to periodic motion in the system. Hence, we add randomness and get a smooth (exponential) decay in fluctuations in the slow driving limit.

Figure 5:
We perform the cyclic I-I protocol with a variety of waiting times. If there is no waiting time (blue line), then we fail to capture the diabatic effects of fast driving. If the waiting time is not random, then we get oscillations corresponding to fact that the system has some intrinsic period. If the waiting time is randomized, there is a smooth exponential decay of the fluctuations in the slow driving limit, as expected.

Appendix C
Detailed derivation of CD driving

It is simplest to derive counterdiabatic (CD) driving within the framework of quantum mechanics and then take the appropriate classical limit. This derivation follows Ref.
36
. Consider a system whose dynamics are governed by a time-dependent Hamiltonian
H(\beta(t))
, where
\beta(t)
is a driving parameter that changes from
\beta_{i}
at
t=0
to
\beta_{f}
at
t=\tau
. Suppose the system initially occupies an eigenstate
\ket{\psi_{n}(\beta_{i})}
of
H(\beta_{i})
, and we drive it to
H(\beta_{f})
by varying
\beta(t)
. The adiabatic theorem of quantum mechanics
47
guarantees that in the limit
\tau\to\infty
, the final state approaches the corresponding eigenstate
\ket{\psi_{n}(\beta_{f})}
, i.e., the system undergoes adiabatic evolution. For any finite
\tau
, however, diabatic (non-adiabatic) transitions generally occur. If instead the system evolves under the
counterdiabatic Hamiltonian
, defined as

H_{\mathrm{CD}}(t)=H+\dot{\beta}\,\mathcal{A}_{\beta},

(13)

no diabatic transitions will occur.
\mathcal{A}_{\beta}
is known as the adiabatic gauge potential (AGP). Recall that since
\dot{\beta}\propto 1/\tau
, in the limit of infinitely slow driving,
\dot{\beta}\rightarrow 0
so we recover adiabatic evolution governed by the original Hamiltonian. One can show that the AGP satisfies the following relation

[\partial_{\beta}H-\frac{1}{i\hbar}[\mathcal{A}_{\beta},H],H]=0

(14)

What is easy to state may not be easy to solve, however, and beyond a select few simple examples, Eq. (
14
) is not useful in finding the exact
\mathcal{A}_{\beta}
. Instead, as was shown in Ref. [
50
], solving this equation is equivalent to variationally minimizing an action

S_{\beta}(\mathcal{A}_{\beta})=\|G_{\beta}\|^{2}+\mu^{2}\|\mathcal{A}_{\beta}\|^{2}\,;\quad G_{\beta}=\partial_{\beta}H-\frac{1}{i\hbar}[\mathcal{A}_{\beta},H]

(15)

where we have defined the norm

\|O\|^{2}=\langle O^{2}\rangle-\langle O\rangle^{2}

(16)

with
\langle O\rangle=\operatorname{Tr}(\rho O)
, and
\mu
is some small number added for regularization in case there are many degenerate solutions. As
\mu\to 0
, minimization of (
15
) recovers (
14
).

All of this remains perfectly well defined after the quantum to classical mapping of commutators to Poisson brackets,
\frac{1}{i\hbar}[A,B]\rightarrow\{A,B\}
. Then,

G_{\beta}=\partial_{\beta}H-\{\mathcal{A}_{\beta},H\}

(17)

Under this mapping, operators become functions on phase space, and expectation values become

\langle A\rangle=\int P(\vec{q},\vec{p},\beta)A(\vec{q},\vec{p},\beta)D\vec{q}D\vec{p}

(18)

P(\vec{q},\vec{p},\beta)
is a distribution over conjugate phase space variables
\vec{q}
and
\vec{p}
. Formally, in classical systems
\mathcal{A}_{\beta}
is the generator of adiabatic transformations
36
;
34
;
35
. Stated less formally, it specifies how to map between stationary (time-independent) distributions in phase space when an external system parameter
\beta
is changed.

To compute expectation values, we need to know
P(\vec{q},\vec{p},\beta)
. For the initial point
\beta_{i}
, we use a microcanonical distribution at energy
E_{0}=1
, which is easily sampled from in two dimensions. Ideally, we would like to choose the distribution which is adiabatically connected to this one at each
\beta
. However, since this is not usually accessible, we follow the method used in
25
, and slowly drive the initial distribution from
\beta_{i}
to
\beta_{f}
without any CD. This allows us to approximate the stationary distribution corresponding to the instantaneous Hamiltonian
H(\beta)
.

Appendix D
Construction of AGP using Chebychev Polynomials

For the original ansatz given in Eq. (
5
), the typical size of the coefficients should decay exponentially with the order in the expansion, if each term is to remain of roughly the same size. This leads to numerical instability, so we instead expand in a basis of Chebyshev polynomials, where the variational coefficients will all be of roughly the same size. The Chebyshev polynomials are defined given the recursive relation:

\displaystyle T_{0}(x)

\displaystyle=1

\displaystyle T_{1}(x)

\displaystyle=x

\displaystyle T_{n+1}(x)

\displaystyle=2xT_{n}-T_{n-1}

To formulate something in terms of operators, we can define a Super-Louvillian Operator
\mathcal{L}=\{H,\cdot\}
, which corresponds to the multiplication of the phase space function by
\omega
in frequency space. Recursive application of this defines a set of operators which are orthogonal with respect to a particular measure:

\displaystyle Q_{0}

\displaystyle=\partial_{\beta}H

\displaystyle Q_{1}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}\{Q_{0}\}

\displaystyle Q_{n+1}

\displaystyle=2\mathcal{L}\{Q_{n}\}-Q_{n-1}

Using this modified basis, we can construct the AGP as follows:

\mathcal{A}_{\beta}^{(l)}=\sum^{l}_{k=1}\gamma_{k}Q_{2k-1}

(19)

where
l
is the order at which we truncate the expansion, and
\gamma_{k}
are the variational parameters that are computed from minimizing the action in Eq.(
15
). One can see from the above that the
\alpha_{k}
of Eq. (
5
) are a linear combination of these
\gamma_{k}
.
