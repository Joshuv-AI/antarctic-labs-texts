Both Radiofrequency Microtenotomy and Physical Therapy Provide Significant Improvements in Patients With Chronic Midportion Achilles Tendinopathy at a Minimum 5‐Year Follow‐Up
Authors: Hayat Jirde Ali, Khaled Meknas, Jüri‐Toomas Kartus, Dana Meknas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo evaluate a minimum 5‐year follow‐up of patients who were treated with either bipolar radiofrequency microtenotomy (RFM) or physical therapy (PT) for midportion Achilles tendinopathy between 2016 and 2018.MethodsAll subjects who participated in a previous randomized controlled trial (n = 38) between 2016 and 2018 were invited to fill out a questionnaire between January and July 2025 to evaluate the outcomes at a minimum of 5‐year follow‐up. Patients who had undergone further surgical treatment were excluded. Visual analog scale (VAS) for pain and Foot and Ankle Outcome Score (FAOS) were used to assess clinical outcomes.ResultsA total of 25 out of the 38 (66%) invited subjects were included: 13 (65%) in the RFM group and 12 (67%) in the PT group. Both treatment groups had significantly improved VAS at the minimum 5‐year follow‐up compared with preintervention. The RFM group improved from 7.3 ± 1.2 to 0.3 ± 0.8 (P = .002), whereas the PT group improved from 5.7 ± 0.3 to 2.2 ± 2.6 (P = .010). The RFM group had significantly better VAS than the PT group at the minimum 5‐year follow‐up (P = .019). All parameters in the FAOS were significantly improved for patients in both the RFM group and the PT group. Quality of life was the only variable of the FAOS with significantly better results for RFM in comparison with PT (P = .047).ConclusionsIn this study, patients with chronic midportion Achilles tendinopathy reported significant improvements in VAS and FAOS 6 to 9 years after intervention with either RFM or PT. The RFM group reported significantly better outcomes in VAS and quality of life. However, because of loss to follow‐up, the study is underpowered, and the results should be interpreted with caution.Level of EvidenceLevel II, therapeutic, poor‐quality randomized controlled trial (<80% f/u).

Achilles tendinopathy (AT) is a major clinical problem affecting various aspects of life including physical, social, and emotional well‐being, which all contribute to reduced quality of life. 1 , 2 In a systematic review on foot and ankle overuse injuries in sports, AT was reported to be one of the most common injuries. 3 AT is categorized as acute, subacute, or chronic and is considered to be chronic when symptoms persist more than 3 months. 4 Early clinical diagnosis followed by treatment within 6 months of onset of symptoms is recognized to contribute to a better clinical outcome. 5

AT is divided into 2 groups: midportion, which is the more common site for tendinopathy, and insertional AT. 6 , 7 This study will focus on midportion AT, which is characterized by pain and swelling 2 to 6 cm proximal from the tendon insertion. 8 Interestingly, this is the region of the tendon that is most hypovascular. 8 The condition is common among both sedentary and active patients 7 and causes functional impairment. The cause of pain in AT is thought to be multifactorial. Risk factors for AT can be categorized as intrinsic, such as age, body mass index, systemic diseases, and genetic predispositions, or extrinsic, such as medications and mechanical overload. 9 , 10 , 11 , 12

The pathophysiology of AT is still not completely understood. Some authors have suggested that AT is a degenerative condition without the presence of inflammation, 11 , 13 whereas others have argued that inflammation and degeneration coexist. 14 , 15 , 16 Abnormal neovascularization accompanied by sensory nerve ingrowth, increased prostaglandins, tenocyte disorganization, and changes in metabolites are hypothesized to be the cause of pain. 11 Elevated levels of substances such as glutamate, substance P, and calcitonin gene‐related peptide are all known to increase nociception and have been observed in tendinopathies. 8 , 11 , 17 , 18

There are various treatment options for AT, both conservative and operative; however, many of these lack evidence for long‐term effectiveness. 5 , 19 Immobilization is recommended in the acute phase but should be avoided in the long term because it can be counterproductive. 11 , 20 Physiotherapy, extracorporeal shockwave therapy, nonsteroidal inflammatory drugs, corticosteroid injections, glyceryl trinitrate patches, and cryotherapy are some of the available treatment modalities.

In a previously published study, Al‐Ani et al. published results of a randomized controlled trial comparing radiofrequency microtenotomy (RFM) and physical therapy (PT) for chronic midportion AT and found promising results for both groups. 21 The technique was minimally invasive and incorporated the use of a bipolar radiofrequency probe along with microtenotomy. The procedure was performed using local anesthesia. The authors found that visual analog scale (VAS) was significantly improved in both groups at the 2‐year follow‐up. Furthermore, magnetic resonance imaging evaluation revealed decreased tendinosis and edema in the RFM group, but not in the PT group, 2 years after treatment. However, no statistically significant difference was found between the groups. 21

The concept of using bipolar RFM for tendinopathy is previously reported and is thought to be an effective and safe surgical procedure. 22 , 23 , 24 Bipolar radiofrequency probes use energized particles which cause molecular disruption. 25 This is thought to promote a healing response, partly by stimulating angiogenesis. 25 Yeap et al. reported significant pain relief combined with improvements in functional status scores after radiofrequency coablation treatment in patients with chronic tendinosis in the Achilles, posterior tibial, and peroneal tendons. 26 Takahashi et al. observed that the number of nerve fibers declined after bipolar RFM through “acute degeneration and/or ablation of sensory nerve fibers.” 27

Within PT, several different training programs exist, the Alfredson protocol being one of the most effective programs. 28 It consists of a 12‐week training program with 2 exercises for heavy eccentric loading of the calf muscles. In the study by Al‐Ani et al., this protocol was used in the PT group, and the results showed significant improvements in VAS, though still inferior compared with operative treatment (RFM). 21 Other studies have found promising results when investigating PT for chronic midportion AT. 29 , 30 , 31 Generally, a conservative approach is recommended as first‐line treatment but has been reported to fail in up to 45% of patients. 32

The purpose of this study was to evaluate a minimum 5‐year follow‐up of patients who were treated with either bipolar RFM or PT for midportion AT between 2016 and 2018. The hypothesis was that the RFM group would show superior results.

METHODS

The present study has received formal approval from the Regional Committee of Research Medical and Health Ethics North‐Norway, (No. 698802), and the data was collected in accordance with their guidelines. All included participants have given their written consent to participate in the study. This study is a midterm follow‐up of a previously published randomized controlled trial, where patients were randomized into 1 of 2 groups: RFM or PT. 21 All participants from the original study (38 patients) were invited to participate and received a letter containing a standardized consent form. Patients who received RFM were all treated by the same surgeon on an outpatient basis. Inclusion in the present study further required that a minimum 5 years had passed since the patient had received treatment, as this is defined as the recommended length for midterm follow‐up studies. 33 Patients who had undergone further (or previous) surgical treatment or had severe active organic diseases were excluded. All patients had received nonsteroidal inflammatory drugs with short‐term effect and had undergone training programs recommended by a physiotherapist without supervision before inclusion. None of the patients had been given injections around the Achilles tendon.

In the prone position under local anesthesia, a longitudinal incision of 4 cm was made, slightly medially over the midportion of the Achilles tendon. 21 Tenosynovitis was debrided, and the paratenon was incised. For the microtenotomy, a Topaz electrode (ArthroCare) connected to a sterile isotonic saline flow system was used. A radiofrequency apparatus provided the energy through the electrode, which was placed perpendicular to the tendons surface. In a grid‐like pattern, with each spot 5 mm away, 4 to 6 light touches were applied initially, followed by penetration of the whole depth of the tendon to perform the actual microneuroablation, normally 12 to 20 penetrations in total. 21 Rehabilitation included the use of crutches for 2 weeks with limited weight‐bearing and active‐assisted range‐of‐motion exercises as tolerated with a gradual increase over the next 6 weeks and return to sports after 12 weeks.

The Alfredson protocol, as described earlier, was used for patients who were allocated to PT. Patients received a 12‐week exercise program that was performed every day. Only body weight was used at first, with optional hand support if needed, progressing to added weight with a 5 kg backpack, and then ultimately, more weight was added as tolerated. Patients were instructed to tolerate some pain and to avoid nonsteroidal inflammatory drugs. All patients were supervised by the same physiotherapist and were considered compliant. 21

The same rating scales as in the original study, VAS and Foot and Ankle Outcome Score (FAOS), were used. Participants received the scores at their home address by mail to be filled out and returned, as in the original study. VAS was defined as the primary outcome. In contrast to the original study, no radiographic imaging (magnetic resonance imaging) was performed in this follow‐up study. Data collection was performed during the first half of 2025. The patients enrolled in the original study had to fulfill the following criteria: chronic pain in the Achilles tendon, impaired function both in daily and sport activities, and magnetic resonance imaging evidence of midportion AT.

VAS is a commonly used tool to assess pain in clinical practice with a scale that ranges from “no pain” (0) to “worst pain imaginable” (10). FAOS is another frequently used patient‐reported outcome measurement tool for foot and ankle conditions. It consists of a 42‐item questionnaire with 5 subscales that evaluate symptoms, pain, activities of daily living, function in sports and recreation, and impact on quality of life. The patients give a score in each category which ranges from 0 to 100, with higher scores indicating better outcomes. 34

Statistical Analysis

The unpaired t test was used to compare results of the groups (VAS and FAOS). The paired t test was used to compare the results from preintervention and the midterm follow‐up within the study groups. To compare dichotomous variables, the χ 2 test was used. Statistical significance was defined as a P value < .05. In the original study, a difference of 2 units in the VAS was considered a clinically important difference. Seventeen patients needed to be included in each group to reach a power of 80% if the standard deviation of the variable was 2 units as well. To increase the power of the study and tolerate dropouts, the aim was to include 20 patients in each group. Minimal clinically important difference defined as those who achieved ½ standard deviation (SD) of the delta value was calculated for VAS and FAOS pain in each group.

In this study, a total of 25 of the original 38 patients were included (66%), 13 patients from the RFM group (65%) and 12 patients from the PT group (67%), as illustrated in Figure 1. DISCUSSION

The most important finding of this minimum 5‐year follow‐up study is that both the RFM and PT groups showed a significant improvement in VAS and FAOS after intervention in patients with chronic midportion AT. When comparing groups, the RFM group had significantly better VAS and quality of life than the PT group at the midterm follow‐up. No statistically significant differences were found for the other FAOS variables; however, the sample size was small and this could affect the results.

In 2022, Verges et al. published a systematic review investigating the psychosocial and individual factors affecting quality of life in patients with AT. 2 They found that all included studies reported that patients with AT had worsened quality of life compared with those who did not have the condition. Both surgical and conservative interventions for AT were associated with increased quality of life. 2 These findings are in line with the present study, suggesting that RFM may provide superior long‐term effects on the quality of life. A recently published prospective long‐term follow‐up study of conservatively treated patients with midportion AT showed that 20% still have symptoms 10 years after treatment. 35 This emphasizes the need for better treatment options.

The use of bipolar RFM for tendinopathy has been found to be effective in several studies. Al‐Ani et al. conducted a study involving 27 patients diagnosed with rotator cuff tendinopathy, where 13 patients were treated with bipolar RFM and reported a significant reduction in pain after treatment. 36 Meknas et al. reported promising medium‐term results after RFM treatment for lateral epicondylitis with improvements in pain and function. 23 Viswanathan and Shanker looked at the long‐term functional outcomes after RFM for lateral epicondylitis, with a mean follow‐up of 8 years. 24 Their study included 19 patients, and 83% reported excellent or good function. 24

In another study, Yeap et al. included 15 patients with chronic tendinosis in peroneal, Achilles, and posterior tibial tendons and likewise reported a significant reduction in pain after treatment with radiofrequency coablation. 26 However, their study only looked at the short‐term clinical outcomes at 3 and 6 months after intervention. Similarly, Arnal‐Burró et al. used bipolar RFM on 17 Achilles tendons (13 patients) and reported 94% improvement of symptoms and 70% return to previous functional level, but their follow‐up time was limited to 12 months. 22 In contrast, the present study investigates the midterm outcomes and shows sustained clinical outcomes, highlighting the potential prolonged effect of RFM in the treatment of chronic midportion AT.

In 1998, Alfredson et al. investigated the effect of a 12‐week heavy‐load eccentric calf muscle training model and found promising results. 28 This protocol was used by the PT group in the present study. Although the patients in the RFM group reported significantly superior outcomes in VAS in comparison to the PT group, it is important to mention that the PT group achieved significant improvements in the midterm follow‐up compared with baseline in both VAS and FAOS.

Some of the strengths of this study include the follow‐up length, the original study design being a randomized controlled trial, and the homogenous characteristics of the study population at baseline without significant difference in duration of symptoms before intervention. Additionally, all patients in the RFM group were operated on by the same surgeon, and all patients in the PT group followed the same standardized PT session overseen by the same physiotherapist. This ensured consistency within each treatment. The use of the same parameters (VAS and FAOS) in this follow‐up study allows for comparison with the findings of the original study.

Limitations

This study is not without limitations. Although the original study enrolled 38 patients with no losses to follow‐up after 2 years, this study included 25 participants, as 12 patients did not respond to the follow‐up invitation. One patient had undergone further surgical procedures after initial treatment and was thus excluded from the analysis. Because of this, the present study is underpowered, which raises the risk of a type II error. Furthermore, the high dropout rate may lead to transfer bias. Because of this, the authors were unable to perform subgroup analysis, such as investigating differences between the sexes. The results should therefore be interpreted with caution. The evaluation of outcomes was based on validated, self‐reported scores. The subjective nature of this data creates a risk of bias. Lastly, no control group was included, which would have allowed comparison with the natural course of AT.

CONCLUSIONS

In this study, patients with chronic midportion AT reported significant improvements in VAS and FAOS 6 to 9 years after intervention with either RFM or PT. The RFM group reported significantly better outcomes in VAS and quality of life. However, because of loss to follow‐up, the study is underpowered, and the results should be interpreted with caution.
