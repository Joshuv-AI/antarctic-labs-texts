Computed tomography-guided percutaneous puncture injection of methylene blue mixed with autologous blood for preoperative localization of pulmonary nodules: analysis of 407 cases
Authors: Kang He, Bao-Rui Ren, Run-Long Sui, Cheng Zhao, Xu-Long Yuan, Zhi-Gang Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundComputed tomography (CT)-guided preoperative localization using methylene blue mixed with autologous blood is a commonly used technique for pulmonary nodules. However, its safety profile and risk factors for complications remain to be fully elucidated. This study aimed to evaluate the safety and complication risk of this technique and to explore the risk factors associated with the occurrence of complications.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective analysis of 407 patients who underwent CT-guided percutaneous puncture of methylene blue mixed with autologous blood for localization of pulmonary nodules during thoracoscopic surgery from January 2021 to December 2025 at the Department of Thoracic Surgery, Central Hospital Affiliated to Shandong First Medical University. The patients were categorized into complication group and no complication group according to the presence or absence of complications. Complications were graded by severity according to the Clavien-Dindo classification. The clinical data of patients in the two groups were compared by univariable analysis and multivariable logistic regression analysis to explore the independent risk factors for patients to develop complications during localization, and to establish and validate the nomogram prediction model.ResultsThe localization success rate was 99.75% (406/407). For the single failed case, the nodule could still be located with the aid of preoperative imaging. The overall complication rate was 8.11% (33/407). The most common complication was pneumothorax, occurring in 7.37% (30/407) of cases, none of which required special intervention. Univariable analysis showed statistically significant differences in forced expiratory volume in 1 second as a percentage of the predicted value (FEV1% prediction), prothrombin time (PT), and nodule location between the two groups (P<0.05). Multivariable logistic regression analysis suggested that FEV1% prediction [odds ratio (OR) =0.118, 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.054–0.256, P<0.001], PT (OR =0.647, 95% CI: 0.421–0.994, P=0.047), and nodule location (OR =2.916, 95% CI: 1.188–7.156, P=0.019) were correlated with localization complications. The area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve for the nomogram prediction model was 0.855. After internal validation, the calibration curve of the model was highly compatible with the ideal curve with good goodness of fit (X2=5.391, P=0.715).ConclusionsCT-guided percutaneous puncture injection of methylene blue mixed with autologous blood is a safe and effective method for localizing pulmonary nodules. In addition, FEV1% prediction, PT and nodule location were independent risk factors for complications of this procedure.

Introduction

The latest data show that lung cancer is the most common cancer in the world, with about 2.5 million new cases, and ranks first among the causes of cancer death (1). According to the National Cancer Center, lung cancer has become a pressing public health issue in China, with about 1.06 million new cases in 2022, ranking first among all cancers, which may be related to high smoking rates (2). Lung cancer causes about 733,300 deaths annually, representing 28.5% of cancer-related deaths. Survival is highly stage-dependent: the 5-year survival rate is 82% for stage IA, but only 7% for stage IVB (3). Therefore, screening and early diagnosis are vital for improving lung cancer prognosis and reducing mortality. Low-dose computed tomography (LDCT) screening has proven effective in achieving this, significantly boosting long-term survival and cutting mortality in high-risk groups (4).

Driven by rising health awareness and widespread LDCT use, the detection of pulmonary nodules has steadily increased. Approximately 16% of lung cancer patients are found to have nodules within 18 months prior to diagnosis (5), and early surgery is the best approach for suspected malignancy. While lobectomy with systematic lymph node dissection has long been the standard for early-stage lung cancer (6), its extensive resection often compromises postoperative quality of life. Thoracoscopic sublobar resection, including wedge resection and segmentectomy, maximally preserves healthy lung tissue without sacrificing treatment efficacy or safety (7,8). However, small and deeply located nodules are difficult to localize intraoperatively by vision or palpation, and failed localization accounts for 46% of conversions to thoracotomy, sometimes causing surgical failure (9). Therefore, meticulous preoperative planning and accurate localization are essential for both wedge resection and segmentectomy, as they substantially improve nodule identification and reduce the risk of conversion to open surgery.

In recent years, various techniques for localizing pulmonary nodules have continued to evolve, with percutaneous puncture localization, transbronchial localization, and computed tomography (CT)-based three-dimensional reconstruction guidance among those that have entered clinical practice. CT-guided percutaneous puncture localization is currently the most widely used method in clinical settings. Commonly employed localization materials include hookwire, microcoil, methylene blue, and autologous blood. Each approach has its own advantages and disadvantages, which has driven the ongoing search for better strategies.

Methylene blue is one of the most frequently used dye-based localization agents. After injection, it creates a distinct blue-stained area around the target lesion, providing intuitive intraoperative guidance for real-time localization (10). Its advantages include simplicity of operation, low cost, and easy availability. Intraoperatively, the marking can be visually identified without the need for fluoroscopy, and the localization procedure itself is time-efficient. Moreover, the single-use puncture needle employed in CT-guided methylene blue localization is relatively thin, which may help reduce the incidence of puncture-related complications (11). However, methylene blue also has limitations as a localizing agent. Being a water-soluble dye, it tends to diffuse readily in tissues (12). If an insufficient volume is injected, the small amount of dye may be rapidly metabolized by the body, failing to produce an effective mark. Conversely, if an excessive volume is injected, diffuse staining of the local area can easily occur (Figure S1), blurring the boundary between the marked region and the surrounding tissue and making it difficult to accurately identify the target nodule, ultimately leading to localization failure. This is especially problematic for deep-seated nodules, where diffuse staining can interfere with the delineation of lesion margins and subsequently affect postoperative pathological diagnosis. For these reasons, methylene blue is often used in combination with other localization techniques to improve accuracy and stability (13,14).

Another commonly used localization material is autologous blood. The advantages of CT-guided autologous blood localization include convenient collection, low cost, and good histocompatibility, as its autologous nature eliminates the risk of rejection (15). After autologous blood is injected, coagulation factors and platelets within it rapidly form a clot that directly seals the puncture needle tract (16). Concurrently, the blood can stimulate pleural inflammation and subsequent fibrosis, promoting adhesion between the visceral and parietal pleura, thereby effectively reducing the occurrence of puncture-related complications such as pneumothorax (17). However, the color of the blood clot is similar to that of normal lung tissue and fresh blood oozing from surgical incisions, resulting in low visual contrast that can easily cause confusion (Figure S2) and compromise the accurate identification of the lesion location.

To address the inherent drawbacks of single-agent methods, namely the easy diffusion of methylene blue and the low contrast of autologous blood, mixing the two can produce an ideal synergistic effect. The autologous blood serves as a natural carrier that encapsulates the methylene blue, markedly slowing its diffusion rate and generating a persistent blue mark with clear boundaries. Simultaneously, the blood clot seals the puncture tract and promotes pleural adhesion, further reducing complications such as pneumothorax. Based on this principle, our center has adopted a CT-guided localization method using a mixture of methylene blue and autologous blood, with satisfactory clinical outcomes. This study aimed to systematically evaluate the safety and effectiveness of this localization method and to explore the risk factors for procedure-related complications. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://qims.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/qims-2026-1143/rc).

Methods

Subject of study

Clinical data of patients who underwent preoperative CT-guided methylene blue mixed autologous blood localization in the Department of Thoracic Surgery at the Central Hospital Affiliated to Shandong First Medical University from January 2021 to December 2025 were consecutively collected (Figure 1). Inclusion criteria: (I) Pulmonary nodule diameter ≥5 mm and ≤30 mm, with CT assessment indicating a high probability of malignancy; (II) meeting the indications for video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery, with patients being informed and able to cooperate in completing preoperative localization; (III) availability of complete clinical data. Exclusion criteria: (I) incomplete patient information; (II) diffuse pulmonary nodules; (III) presence of acute infections, active tuberculosis, etc.; (IV) significant coagulation dysfunction; (V) severe immunosuppression; (VI) accompanied by severe cardiac, pulmonary, hepatic, or renal dysfunction. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Central Hospital Affiliated to Shandong First Medical University (No. 20260410008) and individual consent for this retrospective analysis was waived.

Data collection

Clinical data collected from all patients undergoing localization included age, sex, pulmonary function parameters [forced expiratory volume in one second (FEV1)/forced vital capacity (FVC), FVC% prediction, FEV1% prediction, diffusing capacity of the lung for carbon monoxide (DLCO)% prediction, maximum voluntary ventilation (MVV)% prediction], past history (hypertension, diabetes mellitus, smoking history), laboratory tests [white blood cell count (WBC), prothrombin time (PT), activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT), platelet (PLT)], nodule diameter, distance from the visceral pleura, nodule characteristic, nodule location, and patient positioning. Complications were determined based on immediate post-procedural CT findings and clinical manifestations observed during the 30-minute monitoring period. Each complication was defined as follows: (I) pneumothorax: any degree of intrapleural air identified on post-procedural CT. (II) Pulmonary hemorrhage: ground-glass opacity or consolidation along the needle tract visible on CT, recorded regardless of whether intervention was required. (III) Hemoptysis: any episode of hemoptysis occurring after the procedure. (IV) Pleural reaction: dizziness, chest tightness, diaphoresis, or hypotension occurring during or after the procedure and attributable to pleural irritation. (V) Pain: puncture site or chest wall pain requiring analgesic intervention or rated as moderate or greater on the Numeric Rating Scale (NRS). (VI) Hemothorax: pleural effusion with a density consistent with blood on CT. (VII) Vasovagal reaction: bradycardia developing during the procedure, with or without hypotension. (VIII) Cough: new-onset or worsening cough during or after needle insertion. The severity of all complications was graded according to the Clavien-Dindo classification: grade I (any deviation from the normal postoperative course without the need for pharmacological, surgical, endoscopic, or radiological intervention); grade II (requiring pharmacological treatment, including supplemental oxygen); grade IIIa (requiring surgical, endoscopic, or radiological intervention not under general anesthesia, such as closed thoracic drainage); grade IIIb (requiring intervention under general anesthesia); grade IV [life-threatening, requiring intensive care unit (ICU) management]; grade V (death).

Localization procedure

All localization procedures in this study were performed independently by a single thoracic surgeon with more than five years of experience in percutaneous lung puncture. On the day of surgery, 5 mL of peripheral venous blood was collected from each patient to prepare the localization mixture. After the patient entered the CT room, a localization fence was placed on the corresponding body surface, and thin-slice chest CT scanning was performed during quiet breathing. The puncture route and patient position were planned according to the location of the target nodule. During the procedure, every effort was made to minimize the distance from the skin to the target nodule while avoiding important anatomical structures such as bones, bronchi, and large vessels.

After routine disinfection and draping, all procedures were performed under local infiltration anesthesia, without the use of conscious sedation, monitored anesthesia care, or general anesthesia. A solution of 1% lidocaine (prepared by mixing 5 mL of 2% lidocaine with 5 mL of normal saline) was injected layer by layer along the planned route down to the parietal pleura, with a total injected volume not exceeding 10 mL. The patients remained fully awake and able to follow instructions throughout the procedure, while heart rate, blood pressure, and oxygen saturation were continuously monitored. No analgesic or sedative drugs were routinely administered.

Under CT guidance, an 18 G × 150 mm percutaneous transhepatic cholangiography (PTC) needle (Hakko Co., Ltd., Japan) was percutaneously advanced into the normal lung parenchyma approximately 1 cm from the lesion, with care taken to avoid directly puncturing the pulmonary nodule. Once the needle tip was confirmed to be at the intended target location, the stylet was removed, and a standardized volume of 3 mL of the localization mixture was injected using a syringe. The mixture was gradually injected along the puncture tract in the lung parenchyma during slow, steady withdrawal of the needle.

Considering differences in lung tissue texture among patients of different ages, an individualized mixture ratio was developed in this study. For younger patients with good lung elasticity, the amount of methylene blue could be appropriately increased (methylene blue: autologous blood =1:4) to enhance the visibility of the marking. For elderly patients, especially long-term smokers, in whom anthracotic pigmentation may interfere with dye identification (Figure S3), a higher proportion of autologous blood (1:5) was recommended to improve the visual contrast between the marked area and the surrounding tissue.

After the procedure, a repeat CT scan was performed to evaluate the localization result and to observe for any complications, and the puncture site was covered with a sterile dressing. Following localization, patients were instructed to rest in a supine position under observation for 30 minutes. After confirming stable vital signs and the absence of acute complications, the patient was transported by medical staff to the operating room for general anesthesia and thoracoscopic surgery.

Statistical analysis

This study employed R 4.5.2 to perform statistical analyses. For quantitative data, normally distributed variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (X±S), with group comparisons performed using t-tests. Skewed data are reported as median (P25, P75), with intergroup comparisons conducted using the rank-sum test. Qualitative data are expressed as frequencies and percentages, with group comparisons analyzed using chi-square (X2) tests. Univariable and multivariable logistic regression analyses identified independent risk factors for complications following localization. A nomogram prediction model was constructed and internally validated using the Bootstrap method. Calibration curves and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were plotted. Clinical applicability was ultimately assessed via decision curve analysis (DCA). P<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Analysis of risk factors for complications in pulmonary nodule localization

FEV1% prediction showed a significant inverse correlation with the risk of complications [odds ratio (OR) =0.118, 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.054–0.256, P<0.001], indicating that a higher FEV1% prediction is associated with a lower risk of complications. For each unit increase in PT, the risk of complications decreased by approximately 35% (OR =0.647, 95% CI: 0.421–0.994, P=0.047). Additionally, pulmonary nodule location was confirmed as an independent risk factor for complications, with patients having right pulmonary nodules exhibiting a significantly higher risk than those with left pulmonary nodules (OR =2.916, 95% CI: 1.188–7.156, P=0.019). Nomogram prediction model for the risk of complications in pulmonary nodule localization

Based on the results of binary logistic regression analysis, a nomogram prediction model was constructed (Figure 2A), with scores assigned to each risk factor. ROC curve analysis showed that the area under the curve (AUC) of this model was 0.855 (95% CI: 0.785–0.924). When Youden’s index reached its maximum, the optimal predictive cutoff point was 0.051, corresponding to a sensitivity of 0.879 and specificity of 0.695, indicating the model’s strong discriminatory ability in predicting complications of percutaneous needle localization (Figure 2B). The Hosmer-Lemeshow test revealed no statistically significant difference between the model’s fitted curve and the ideal curve (Figure 2C), indicating good model fit (X2=5.391, P=0.715). Further DCA results indicated that applying this model yields high clinical net benefit across a broad range of thresholds, confirming its strong clinical utility (Figure 2D).

Pathological diagnosis

Precursor lesions and malignant nodules accounted for 91.65% (373/407) of all cases. These included 20 cases of atypical adenomatous hyperplasia, 9 cases of mucinous adenocarcinoma, 205 cases of adenocarcinoma in situ, 84 cases of minimally invasive adenocarcinoma, 50 cases of invasive adenocarcinoma, 1 case of squamous cell carcinoma, and 4 cases of metastatic lung cancer. Benign nodules constituted 8.35% of the cases.

Complication

The overall incidence of localization-related complications was 8.11% (33/407). All complications were Clavien-Dindo grade I or II, with no grade III or higher serious events (Table 3). The details were as follows: (I) pneumothorax occurred in 30 cases, with an incidence of 7.37%. All were small pneumothoraces (lung compression <20%), none required closed thoracic drainage, and all were classified as Clavien-Dindo grade I, resolving spontaneously during observation. (II) Pulmonary hemorrhage occurred in 1 case (0.25%) and required no specific intervention. (III) Pleural reaction occurred in 1 case (0.25%), manifesting as dizziness, sweating, chest pain, and chest tightness after the procedure. The symptoms were relieved after administration of nasal cannula oxygen at 5 L/min and dexamethasone 5 mg. Surgery was successfully completed after a delay of approximately 5.5 hours. (IV) Pain occurred in 1 case (0.25%), with an NRS score of 4, requiring analgesic medication. Surgery was delayed by approximately 6 hours. Hemoptysis, hemothorax, vasovagal reaction, and cough were not observed.

Subgroup analysis

Given that risk factors may differ by complication type, a subgroup analysis stratified by complication type was performed. Because the numbers of events for pulmonary hemorrhage, pleural reaction, and pain were too small to construct separate multivariable regression models, univariable and multivariable analyses were conducted only for the pneumothorax subgroup. Only one bleeding-related complication occurred, and therefore the independent predictive value of PT for hemorrhagic events could not be evaluated in this study.

Univariable analysis of the pneumothorax subgroup showed that FEV1% prediction, PT, and nodule location differed significantly between the pneumothorax group and the complication-free group (Figure 3A and Table 4). Multivariable logistic regression indicated that FEV1% prediction (OR =0.123, 95% CI: 0.055–0.275, P<0.001), PT (OR =0.599, 95% CI: 0.381–0.942, P=0.026), and nodule location (OR =2.572, 95% CI: 1.036–6.385, P=0.042) were independent risk factors for pneumothorax, consistent in direction with the overall complication analysis (Figure 3B and Table 5).

The independent predictive effect of PT in the pneumothorax subgroup (OR =0.599) was consistent in direction and similar in magnitude to that in the overall complication analysis (OR =0.647), suggesting that PT influenced complication risk in this cohort primarily through the pneumothorax pathway rather than the hemorrhagic pathway. In addition, the relationship between patient positioning and pneumothorax was further analyzed. Positions were categorized into restrictive decubitus positions (left or right decubitus) and non- restrictive decubitus positions (supine or prone). The results showed that 80.00% of patients in the pneumothorax group and 79.14% in the non-pneumothorax group were placed in restrictive decubitus positions, with no statistically significant difference between groups (P=0.912). It is noteworthy that position selection in this study was not randomly assigned but was determined by the operator based on the nodule location and optimal puncture route. Therefore, there was evident collinearity between positioning and nodule location, and the above negative result may have been affected by confounding, requiring cautious interpretation.

Discussion

An ideal pulmonary nodule localization technique must strike a balance between clinical efficacy and practical applicability. At the technical performance level, it should achieve high localization accuracy, a low complication rate, and minimal patient discomfort, while reliably accommodating variations in nodule size, depth, and density. At the clinical workflow level, it should allow a flexible time window between localization and surgery without compromising the reliability of subsequent pathological diagnosis. At the health economic level, it should be cost-effective, ensuring accessibility and generalizability across different healthcare systems.

Among percutaneous puncture localization methods, hookwire localization is widely used because it is simple to perform and allows direct thoracoscopic visualization of the lesion (18,19). However, this method carries a risk of dislodgement or migration due to patient movement or lung collapse after localization, and it is associated with a relatively high postoperative complication rate (20). Microcoil localization requires the coil to be completely embedded within the lung parenchyma, with the distal end coiled on the pleural surface. This effectively reduces the risk of dislodgement and preserves intraoperative flexibility (21), but its reliance on intraoperative fluoroscopy increases radiation exposure for both the patient and the operator. In comparison, visually identifiable dye marking offers a new approach for nodule localization, avoiding permanent retention of metallic foreign bodies in the lung parenchyma while also reducing patient discomfort and the risk associated with foreign body retrieval. Several studies have confirmed that methylene blue is superior to hookwire in terms of complication rates, localization time, and length of hospital stay (22,23). Other reports have shown that methylene blue localization yields a higher success rate and fewer postoperative complications compared with microcoil localization (24).

To further overcome the limitation of easy diffusion with methylene blue alone, McConnell et al. first applied CT-guided localization using a mixture of methylene blue and autologous blood in pediatric patients, successfully achieving precise localization of pulmonary nodules at various depths. No signs of pleural extravasation or dye diffusion were observed intraoperatively, and the marked boundaries remained clear even several hours later (25). The authors attributed this primarily to the higher viscosity of blood, which slowed the diffusion of the water-soluble dye to a certain extent. Subsequently, Feng et al. extended this technique to a population with a mean age of 51.91 years, also obtaining encouraging results, with a localization success rate as high as 98.63% (26). Despite these technical refinements, preoperative localization of pulmonary nodules remains extremely challenging in certain special clinical scenarios. For example, in patients with fibrotic interstitial lung disease, widespread structural distortion and dense fibrosis are often present, making CT-guided puncture route planning extremely difficult. Furthermore, fibrosis may alter the diffusion pattern of the dye within the fibrotic tissue, causing marker displacement or indistinct identification and significantly increasing the risk of localization failure (27). Additionally, such patients exhibit markedly reduced lung compliance, which may also elevate the risk of puncture-related complications. Therefore, there is an urgent need to develop or optimize dedicated localization strategies for this population to address this weak link in current clinical practice.

In the present study, the localization success rate was 99.75% (406/407). Only one patient was unable to tolerate the pain and declined to complete the preoperative localization. Subsequent surgery was completed with the aid of preoperative imaging and intraoperative visual identification. Although the localization success rate was satisfactory (Figure 4), complications of CT-guided percutaneous puncture remain difficult to avoid entirely, with pneumothorax and pulmonary hemorrhage being the main types (28). The incidence of post-localization pneumothorax was 7.37% (30/407), which is comparable to previous reports (29), and all cases were small pneumothoraces that resolved spontaneously without specific intervention.

Univariable and multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed to explore the risk factors for localization-related complications. The results showed that a higher FEV1% prediction was a protective factor against pneumothorax (OR =0.123, 95% CI: 0.055–0.275, P<0.001), indicating that the risk of pneumothorax decreased significantly with increasing FEV1% prediction. This is consistent with the findings of Li et al., who also identified FEV1% prediction as an important independent risk factor for post-procedural pneumothorax (30,31). Poor pulmonary function is often accompanied by reduced lung compliance and increased airway resistance, which may impair the repair capacity after lung injury. Nodule location was another key risk factor for pneumothorax (OR =2.572, 95% CI: 1.036–6.385, P=0.042). The proportion of right lung nodules was significantly higher in the pneumothorax group than in the non-pneumothorax group (76.67% vs. 52.94%, P=0.012). Zhang et al. further reported that puncture of the right upper lobe was independently associated with an increased complication rate (32). We speculate that when a nodule is located in the anterior or posterior segment of the right upper lobe, especially adjacent to the horizontal or oblique fissure, the puncture needle often has to traverse the interlobar fissure, creating a double breach of the visceral pleura. The maximal negative pressure at the apex of the pleural cavity accelerates air entry and apical lung collapse, and the relatively weak elastic recoil of the right upper lobe further contributes to the high incidence of pneumothorax. Therefore, extra caution is warranted when managing right lung nodules adjacent to interlobar fissures. This study also found that a prolonged PT was associated with a reduced risk of pneumothorax (OR =0.599, 95% CI: 0.381–0.942, P=0.026), although the biological interpretation must be approached with caution. PT primarily reflects the function of the extrinsic coagulation pathway and is theoretically more closely associated with hemorrhagic complications. Given that only one hemorrhagic event occurred in this study, the independent predictive effect of PT is more likely to be mediated through an indirect association with pneumothorax risk. We propose that a moderately prolonged PT may be associated with mildly reduced coagulation function, allowing a small amount of blood oozing within the needle tract to form a localized clot around the puncture site, thereby more effectively sealing the air leak. However, this finding lacks external validation and should be regarded as exploratory; its clinical applicability awaits confirmation by large-sample, multicenter prospective studies. It is worth noting that, although Wu et al. also used CT-guided localization with a methylene blue and autologous blood mixture, they identified WBC as an independent risk factor for pneumothorax (33), whereas no such association was observed in the present study (P>0.05). This discrepancy may stem from differences in study population characteristics and procedural workflows. In addition, no significant association was found between patient positioning and overall complications or pneumothorax incidence, a result that is not entirely consistent with some reports on lung biopsy (34). This may be because position selection was guided by nodule location, leading to high collinearity between positioning and nodule location, with the effect of position potentially being absorbed by the nodule location variable. Moreover, compared with diagnostic lung biopsy, localization puncture involves a shorter needle tract and shorter procedural time, and the influence of positioning on respiratory motion and puncture stability may be relatively limited. Given the non-randomized nature of position selection, its role as an independent risk factor requires further clarification in prospective studies.

Based on the above independent risk factors, a nomogram prediction model was constructed to quantitatively estimate individual risk and provide a visual tool for clinical decision-making. ROC curve analysis demonstrated good predictive performance of the model (AUC =0.855, 95% CI: 0.785–0.924). With an optimal cutoff value of 0.051, the corresponding sensitivity was 0.879 and the specificity was 0.695. Internal validation using 1,000 bootstrap resamples confirmed satisfactory model calibration, and DCA further indicated a significant net benefit across clinically applicable probability thresholds.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, as a single-center retrospective study, it is subject to potential bias. Second, the study focused exclusively on solitary pulmonary nodules and did not include patients with multiple pulmonary nodules or fibrotic interstitial lung disease, which may introduce selection bias. In patients with fibrotic interstitial lung disease, structural distortion of the lung and the unique fibrotic microenvironment not only dramatically increase the difficulty of localization but also limit the applicability of conventional risk assessment criteria. Therefore, the findings of this study should be extrapolated to such special populations with great caution. Third, the lack of external validation means that the accuracy and generalizability of the results remain to be confirmed. Future multicenter, large-sample prospective studies incorporating additional potential influencing factors and the aforementioned special populations are warranted, with the aim of providing new evidence to advance the development of pulmonary nodule surgery toward greater precision, minimal invasiveness, and wider accessibility.

Conclusions

In summary, CT-guided percutaneous localization using methylene blue mixed with autologous blood is characterized by high accuracy, low complication rates, simplicity of operation, low cost, and easy availability of required materials, making it a safe and effective option for preoperative localization of pulmonary nodules. Furthermore, FEV1% prediction, PT, and nodule location significantly influence the complication rate of percutaneous localization.
