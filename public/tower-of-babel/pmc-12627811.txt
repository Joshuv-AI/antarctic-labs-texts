Desiccation cracking behavior of xanthan gum admixed clay
Authors: Yuxia Bai, Meihui Cheng, Henglin Xiao, Lihua Li, Qiang Ma, Jun He, Yeping Yang, Yongfeng Deng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study investigates the desiccation cracking properties of xanthan gum (XG)-treated clay and its effectiveness in repairing cracked clay by examining their evaporation characteristics, crack propagation, patterns, and the related micro-mechanisms were discussed with the aid of micro examination. Results show that (i) the evaporation rate curve of untreated and XG-treated clay exhibits a Z-shape with three stages, and the latter possesses higher water content, smaller evaporation rate, and longer evaporation durations. (ii) The crack development in both untreated and treated clay follows identical four stages, and the sample treated with higher XG content showed better resistance to wet-dry (W-D) cycles. The Rc, Wav and Lto roughly reduce as XG content increases, and are also impacted by W-D cycles. The optimal XG content was considered as 1.5% and 2%. (iii) The cracked sample repaired by XG exhibited satisfactory crack resistance after the 1st and 2nd W-D cycles, and the Rc, Wav and Lto obviously reduced, and the higher sand content promotes this effect. (iv) XG treatment reduces the clay voids, enhances the elastic connection between inter-particles, and entraps water within XG molecular structures, by its coating, bridging, and filling effects, thus enhancing clay water retention capacity and resistance to desiccation cracking.

Introduction

In natural environments, slope soils are long-term influenced by solar radiation, wind, precipitation, and other climatic factors. These factors drive continuous subsurface water evaporation, inducing soil desiccation and shrinkage1,2. Concomitantly, inter-particle cementation weakens, culminating in the formation of macroscopic crack networks. Such structural alterations profoundly impact soil mechanical and hydraulic behaviors, compromising soil fabric integrity and reducing stability3. As dry-shrinkage cracks expand and penetrate, soil structural integrity deteriorates, accompanied by marked declines in shear-strength parameters4. Notably, during rainfall infiltration, these cracks act as preferential flow pathways, expediting water ingress and potentially triggering landslide failures5. Thus, controlling and mitigating crack development in slope soils emerges as a critical determinant for maintaining slope stability.

Improving the microstructure of the soil by adding some specific functional materials, such as cement6, fibers7,8, and biopolymers9 to the topsoil to improve its cohesive strength and resistance to cracking has been proved to be an effective method. Traditional soil stabilizers, such as lime and cement, could improve soil performance to a certain extent, Al-Swaidani et al.10 reported that the addition of natural pozzolana could enhance the California Bearing Ratio (CBR) value of soil, and improve soil compatibility, strength, and shrinkage. Zi et al.11 found that lime can also enhance the soil’s resistance to W-D cycles. However, they also have problems such as high energy consumption and environmental pollution. The production processes of lime and cement are not only characterized by high energy consumption and substantial carbon dioxide emissions, which exacerbate global greenhouse effects12,13, but their application in soil improvement also alters soil chemical properties, inhibits soil microbial activity, and thereby disrupts the homeostatic balance of soil ecosystems14.

In recent years, biopolymers, as a new type and environmentally friendly soil stabilizers, have gradually attracted the attention of scholars and been used to enhance the engineering properties of soil15–18. Some researchers have indicated that the addition of xanthan gum, gellan gum, guar gum, and polysaccharide-based biopolymers can significantly improve soil strength, water stability, and reduce permeability. Chang et al.19 found that the strength of soil treated with 2% gellan gum (about 434.6 kPa) is greater than that treated with 12% cement (about 380 kPa). Moreover, even under high water content conditions, gellan gum treatment effectively enhances the shear strength of sand-clay mixtures20. In addition, Ayeldeen et al.21 reported that the permeability coefficient of the soil treated with guar gum decreases to 27% of the initial value at a concentration of 0.25% and drops below 1% at a concentration of 2%. The biopolymer treated soil showed better resistance to W-D cycles, Soldo et al.22 reported that guar gum-modified sand retained over 70% of its original mass after 10 wet-dry cycles. For sands with 1% and 2% XG content, 46% and 75% of initial strength were maintained after 7 cycles. Ohta et al.23 documented that chitosan application significantly promoted seedling growth from 6 to 13 weeks post-sowing when compared to the unfertilized control group. Mechanistically, chitosan may induce alterations in the soil microbial community structure or be directly absorbed by plant roots and assimilated into metabolic pathways. These references indicate that the biopolymer addition could effectively enhance soil strength and water retention capacity, reduce permeability, and improve the resistance to W-D cycles, which also suggests that biopolymer has great potential in improving soil desiccation cracking behaviors.

Compared with other biopolymers, xanthan gum (XG) exhibits remarkable characteristics of solubility and high viscosity at low concentrations, leading to significant thickening efficiency24. It demonstrates excellent stability against heat, acids, bases, and salts, with minimal viscosity variation over a broad temperature and pH range enabling favorable compatibility with a wide range of substances25. Additionally, it shows strong resistance to enzymatic hydrolysis, being hardly degraded by various enzymes26. Numerous studies have fully confirmed that XG can significantly enhance the compressive strength and shear strength of soil27 and indicated that XG can strengthen the stability of soil structures, providing a mechanical guarantee for resisting soil cracking. Tran et al.28 reported that the presence of XG hydrogel increases the initial and residual moisture contents of sandy soil, maintains the water balance within the soil, and reduces water loss in the soil. Chen et al.29 found that with the increase of XG content, more biopolymers surround and connect with soil particles, leading to a significant increase in shear strength (about 1.4–1.8 times) of soil. Chang et al.30 conducted experiments with different concentrations of XG and found that the strengthening effect tends to stabilize at higher concentrations, and the most effective concentration is 1% to 1.5% of the soil mass. These achievements provide a reference for the practical application of XG. However, the specific effect and mechanism of XG in mitigating soil cracking are currently unclear, and clarifying this issue is crucial for promoting its application in geotechnical engineering. Considering that, this study systematically investigates the crack resistance performance of XG-treated clay. Firstly, the evaporation characteristics, crack propagation, and patterns of XG-treated clay were monitored by W-D cycle test, and the crack morphological characteristic indicators were quantitatively analyzed using digital image processing technology (PCAS), and the effects of XG dosage and W-D cycles were considered. Secondly, the repairing efficacy of XG used as a repair agent in cracked soil was studied by testing the crack propagation and patterns at different W-D cycles. Thirdly, the microstructure and component change of the treated soil were examined by XRD, SEM, and FTIR to understand the interactions between XG and clay. Finally, the mechanism of XG on changing soil desiccation cracking behavior was discussed according to the test results.

Xanthan gum

The xanthan gum (XG) used in this study was supplied by Tianjin Huasheng Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. It is present in the form of light-yellow powder, and is easily soluble in water to form a viscous emulsion (Fig. 2(a)). Its relative molecular mass ranges from 2 × 10⁶ to 2 × 10⁷, and its viscosity could reach 2000 MPa·s. The molecular structure of xanthan gum contains amounts of hydrophilic functional groups, as shown in Fig. 2(b). These groups make it easily soluble in water to form a viscous emulsion by forming hydrogen bonds with strong intermolecular forces. In addition, the XG solution had high viscosity even at low concentration, and thus it has a strong bonding effect, showing satisfactory potential in clay stabilization.

Sample preparation and test method

In this study, the tested samples are prepared by following steps, as shown in Fig. 3(a): firstly, the XG powder was fully mixed with dry clay with proportions of 0%, 0.25%, 0.5%, 1%, 1.5%, 2%, 2%, that is, XG content. Secondly, the appropriate amount of dry mixture was poured into a mold with dimensions of 16 cm × 16 cm × 6 cm, followed by saturation with deionized water to achieve an initial water content of 50%. Thirdly, the samples vibrated for 10 min to remove air bubbles and then left to stand for 24 h to evenly distribute the water. After that, the samples were placed in a constant-temperature oven with a temperature of 40 °C and a humidity of 60% (simulating the weather in summer in the central region of China33 for continuous drying. During this process, the water content (ωt, %) and evaporation rate (vt, g/h) of the tested sample at different times were calculated according to the weighing data by following Eqs. (1) and (2), respectively. Also, the cracks on the sample were recorded by a camera. The drying test was terminated when the mass change between two measurements within two hours was less than 0.01 g. Subsequently, an appropriate amount of deionized water was added to the tested sample to saturate it again, and then the sample was allowed to stand for 24 h to ensure the water was evenly distributed in the clay sample. Then, the drying test was repeated until it reached the requirement for termination. To further investigate the repair effects of XG on clay cracks, a crack repair test was performed, as illustrated in Fig. 3(b). Previous studies show that the addition of sand exhibits an excellent improvement effect on the crack resistance of clay34. Here, the repair materials were composed of XG, clay, and sand, with XG concentrations of 1% and 2%, and sand content of 0%, 25%, 50%, 75%, and 100%, respectively. There were 10 samples in this test, and each sample was numbered according to the component content to easily distinguish the samples. For example, 1XG-25SA-75CL refers to a cracked sample that was treated by repair material with an XG concentration of 1%, a sand content of 25%, and a clay content of 75%. In addition, to ensure the fluidity of the repair material in clay cracks, the water content was controlled at 200%. The crack repair test was performed as follows: firstly, prepare the repair materials, that is, a moderate mass of XG powder and dry clay was fully mixed, and then add deionized water to adjust the target water content, to achieve a flowing state. Secondly, the repair material was poured into the cracks of the tested sample until it was filled. Subsequently, an appropriate amount of deionized water was sprayed on the treated sample surface with an initial water content of 50%. After that, the sample was stood for 24 h to ensure water distribution, and then the W-D cycle test was performed 5 times.

In this study, the crack images of tested sample were processed and analyzed by the Particle and Crack Image Recognition and Analysis Software (PCAS) developed by Nanjing University35, which mainly included four steps of binarization, crack network segmentation, crack network skeletonization, and crack characterization, and the detailly operation could be seen in references36. To mitigate boundary effects, the central region (i.e., 15 cm × 15 cm) of the cracking images was used and analyzed. After that, three geometric parameters of the crack patterns, including surface crack ratio (Rc, %), average crack width (Wav, mm), and total crack length (Tcl, mm) were used to characterize quantitatively the desiccation cracking behavior in tested clay samples.

Micro observation

SEM examination

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) examination was performed to investigate the microstructural characteristics of the tested samples. Untreated and 2% XG-treated clay samples after the 1 st and 5th W-D cycles were selected. These samples were cut first into cubic blocks with dimensions of 5 mm × 5 mm × 2 mm, and then oven-dried at 105 °C for 24 h. The prepared specimens were then sputter-coated with gold to enhance surface conductivity. After that, the microstructure of the clay sample was observed at magnifications ranging from 1000× to 10,000×.

FTIR analysis

Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy analysis was conducted to analyze the chemical structure changes of the tested samples. Untreated and 2% XG-treated clay after the 5th W-D cycle were selected and oven-dried at 105 °C for 24 h. The dried samples were then crushed into powder, passed through a 200-mesh sieve, and stored in sealed sample tubes. Before testing, the detection table was cleaned with alcohol and dried. Then, an appropriate amount of clay powder was compressed using a rotating pressure device, and the testing began. FTIR spectra were acquired over a wavenumber range of 400–4000 cm⁻¹ with a resolution of 4 cm⁻¹, utilizing a DTGS KBr detector and averaging 32 scans per spectrum.

XRD analysis

X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis was carried out on untreated and 2% XG-treated specimens after the 5th W-D cycle, which was oven-dried at 105 °C for 24 h. The tested samples treatment was similar to that introduced in the FTIR test. Before testing, approximately 1 g of clay powder was dispersed in 50 mL of deionized water using ultrasonic agitation for 10 min, and then the supernatant was pipetted onto a glass slide. After that, the XRD testing began with a 2θ scanning range of 10°– 80°, a step size of 0.01°, a scanning rate of 0.02° per step, and a total scanning time of 20–40 min.

Results and discussion

Evaporation behaviors of XG-treated clay

The change of water content with time during the 1 st, 3rd, and 5th W-D cycles was shown in Fig. 4. It can be observed that during the drying process, the water content of untreated and XG-treated clay samples changed similarly whatever the W-D cycles and XG content, that is, the water content reduced continuously with time util to a certain value and then remained stable. In addition, at the same drying time, the water content of XG-treated clay samples was higher than that of untreated clay samples, and it increased with an increase in XG content; what’s more, it became obvious as W-D cycles increased. Besides, with an increase in the number of W-D cycles, the water content of each sample was reduced, and the evaporation process was promoted.

Fig. 4Variation of water content with time at (a) 1 st, (b) 3rd, and (c) 5th W-D cycles.

Variation of water content with time at (a) 1 st, (b) 3rd, and (c) 5th W-D cycles.

The evaporation rate of each sample with time at the 1 st, 3rd, and 5th W-D cycles was displayed in Fig. 5. It appeared from Fig. 5 that all the evaporation-rate curves exhibited a Z-shape change trend, which was consistent with previous reports3,37,38. According to the previous studies, the evaporation process of XG-treated clay could be divided into three stages39,40 (Fig. 5(a)): (Ⅰ) the constant rate stage, (Ⅱ) the deceleration rate stage, and (Ⅲ) the residual stage. Among them, the water is lost mainly in stages Ⅰ and Ⅱ, and when it came into stage Ⅲ, the evaporation rate gradually approached zero, and the clay water content tends to be stable.

Fig. 5Evaporation rate with time at (a) 1 st, (b) 3rd, and (c) 5th W-D cycles.

Evaporation rate with time at (a) 1 st, (b) 3rd, and (c) 5th W-D cycles.

Figure 5 also showed that the duration of Stage Ⅰ and Ⅱ increased significantly with XG content, whatever the W-D cycles, while the duration of Stage Ⅲ decreased notably with increasing XG content. This indicated that the addition of XG enhanced the water retention capacity of clay. For example, during the 1 st W-D cycle, the durations of Stage Ⅰ, Ⅱ and Stage Ⅲ for samples with different XG contents were 10 h, 14 h, 18.5 h, 25 h, 29 h, 34 h; 36 h, 35.5 h, 35 h, 34 h, 33 h, 32 h and 24 h, 20.5 h, 16.5 h, 11 h, 8 h, 4 h, respectively. In addition, the evaporation rate in Stage Ⅰ reduced as XG content increased; accordingly, it increased at Stage Ⅱ as XG content increased. With an increase in the number of W-D cycles, the durations of Stage Ⅰ and Stage Ⅱ gradually decreased, but the change in evaporation rate was not significant. For example, the durations of Stage Ⅰ for untreated and 0.5% XG-treated clay at different W-D cycles were 10 h, 8 h, 6 h and 18.5 h, 17 h, 15 h, respectively, while the durations of Stage Ⅱ were 36 h, 35 h, 34 h and 35 h, 34 h, 33 h, respectively.

According to analyses, the addition of XG significantly affected the evaporation behavior of clay, Li et al.37 also found that the addition of polymer changed the evaporation behavior of clay in terms of evaporation rate and duration, as shown in Fig. 6. Firstly, XG treatment could not only significantly reduce the clay evaporation rate in stage Ⅰ but also prolong the duration of this stage. Secondly, the addition of XG could effectively slow down the decreasing trend of the evaporation rate in stage Ⅱ. Thirdly, XG had a significant regulatory effect on the clay evaporation process, which could prolong the total duration of the evaporation process (i.e., the sum of the first and second stages). By this, XG-treated clay possessed higher content and thus significantly enhanced the crack-resistance of clay. Note that the XG effect on clay evaporation characteristics was unrelated to the W-D cycles.

Crack propagation and patterns of XG-treated clay

Previous studies have shown that clay dehydration progresses sequentially through the loss of capillary water, followed by bound water, and ultimately adsorbed water, resulting in a substantial decrease in total water content during drying3,37,38. This loss of water initiates volumetric contraction, primarily manifested as shrinkage behavior, which in turn weakens inter-particle forces among the initially densely packed clay particles, resulting in the formation of inter-granular voids40. These voids eventually coalesce, leading to the development of macroscopic cracks. The variation of water content, evaporation rate, and crack propagation of untreated and treated clay in this study is shown in Fig. 7. The untreated specimens exhibited crack initiation during the 1 st W-D cycle, whereas cracking in XG-treated specimens was delayed until the 2nd and 3rd W-D cycles.

Fig. 7The (a) water content and evaporation rate and (b) crack propagation of untreated clay, 0.25% and 2% XG-treated clay.

The (a) water content and evaporation rate and (b) crack propagation of untreated clay, 0.25% and 2% XG-treated clay.

Even though the crack propagation process in both untreated and XG-treated clays can be categorized into four distinct stages: (i) rapid drying stage, during this stage, rapid and constant moisture evaporation leads to a sharp decline in water content. The resulting consolidation shrinkage induces significant volumetric contraction, creating the mechanical conditions necessary for crack initiation. This phase represents the preparatory stage for crack development. (ii) Initiation and growth of main cracks, as water content continues to decrease, initial desiccation cracks emerge on the clay surface. Tang et al.3 and Liu et al.40 observed that initial cracks typically develop during the constant rate evaporation stage at higher moisture contents. These cracks generally initiate from surface defects such as millimeter-scale pits and edges of undispersed aggregates, where structural heterogeneity, nonuniform evaporation rates, and spatially variable gradients collectively contribute to crack formation. Subsequent crack propagation is facilitated by stress concentration and shrinkage deformation within the surrounding clay matrix. In this study, the XG-treated clay samples formed cracks during the 2nd and 3rd W-D cycles, which suggested that the initial treated clay structure was uniform to a certain extent, and cracks were induced after defects were formed after undergoing certain W-D cycles. In addition, we found that the cracks developed in sequence and had obvious hierarchical characteristics. Here, the first crack is the main crack, and the crack that developed on the main crack is the second crack. The third and fourth level cracks were defined by analogy41. In this stage, the main crack progressively grew in length, width, and depth, as shown in Fig. 7(b). (iii) initiation and growth of sub-cracks, as dry time increased, main cracks further extended and deepened, accompanied by the formation of sub-cracks in localized regions. Crack length, width, and area ratio increased markedly, leading to the development of an interconnected and increasingly complex crack network. This phase exhibits the longest duration and the most active crack growth, significantly compromising the structural integrity of the soil42. (iv) crack relative stable period, in this stage, the rate of water loss diminishes, and the geometric structure of the crack network tends to stabilize, and no new cracks are generated, mainly involving widening existing cracks until the drying stops.

In addition, Fig. 7(b) also showed that the sample surface mainly formed intersecting “T”- and “Y”-shaped crack networks, which divided the sample surface into separate blocks43, that is, the cracks were perpendicular to each other, and the intersection angle tended to 90°. Such an interesting phenomenon was induced by that the crack propagated perpendicularly to the tensile direction, where it was a tensile failure43,44. In comparison, XG-treated samples developed many short, discontinuous cracks concentrated in central areas, while the untreated sample was divided into obviously separate blocks by longer and wider cracks. Furthermore, as XG content increased, especially in high content treated clay, both main and secondary cracks showed obvious reductions in width and number. These results confirm that XG addition effectively inhibited clay desiccation crack development.

The final crack morphology images of untreated and XG-treated clay samples after different W-D cycles are shown in Fig. 8. It appeared that there were significant differences in crack patterns between untreated and XG-treated clay samples. The untreated clay samples exhibited irregular and unpredictable changes in surface cracks after each cycle, and their surface was cut into different small pieces by cracks, with poor integrity. In contrast, XG-treated clay samples demonstrated varying crack patterns during different W-D cycles. The number of W-D cycles that caused cracks in the treated clay sample increased with the increase in XG content, and the number of W-D cycles of cracks observed in treated clay with different XG content were 2, 2, 3, 3, and 3, respectively. Correspondingly, the crack size and surface integrity of each sample were improved as XG content increased under identical W-D cycle conditions, especially for the ones with 1.5% and 2% XG content, which only exhibited a crack and high surface integrity. In addition, for the ones with low-XG content, the crack pattern became constant after 4 W-D cycles. These indicated that the addition of XG could effectively inhibit clay crack initiation and development, and the crack resistance of treated clay was positively correlated with XG content.

Crack characteristics of XG-treated clay

The surface crack ratio (Rc, %), average crack width (Wav, mm), and total crack length (Lto, mm) of each sample obtained using PCAS were shown in Fig. 9. It appeared from Fig. 9(a) that the Rc of XG-treated clay was observably smaller than that of untreated clay, whatever the W-D cycles, and this phenomenon became more obvious as XG content increased. In addition, the number of W-D cycles also affected the Rc of untreated and treated clay. For untreated clay, the Rc peaked at the 1 st W-D cycle and then reduced to about 16% and roughly kept stable as the number of W-D cycles increased. However, the Rc of XG-treated sand increased util to a certain value and then remained constant. Note that this increase was induced by that the crack was formed after undergoing specified W-D cycles, which was consistent with the founding in Fig. 8. Besides, the Rc of treated clay with different XG contents kept stable at 9.38%, 10.3%, 8.7%, 2.2% and 2.05%. This further indicated that the addition of XG could effectively inhibit the formation and development of cracks, particularly with XG content of 1.5% and 2%, which significantly reduced the promoting effect of W-D cycles on clay crack development.

Fig. 9Variation of (a) Rc, (b) Wav, and (c) Lto with W-D cycles of the tested sample.

Variation of (a) Rc, (b) Wav, and (c) Lto with W-D cycles of the tested sample.

Cracks usually serve as the main channels for the migration of water and gas in clay, especially for the clay slope with cracks when raining, and their width and length have vital effects on the migration of water and gas. The larger the crack width and the longer the crack length, the faster the migration speed of water and gas, and the lower the overall strength and stability of the soil44. Figure 9(b) and (c) showed that the change trend of Wav and Lto of the untreated sample was similar to that of Rc, as W-D cycles increased. The Wav and Lto of untreated clay peaked at the first W-D cycle and reduced continuously to around 2.5 mm and 2000 mm, respectively. For XG-treated clay, due to the presence of XG changed the water holding and evaporation characteristics of clay, the crack was formed after specified W-D cycles, and then the Wav and Lto increased obviously. After that, the Wav and Lto reduced overall.

It could be concluded from Figs. 7, 8 and 9 that the presence of XG changed the desiccation cracking behavior of clay, as manifested by, first, the crack evolution in both untreated and XG-treated clays can be categorized into four distinct stages including (i) rapid drying stage, (ii) initiation and growing of main cracks, (iii) initiation and growing of sub cracks, (iv) crack relative stable period. Secondly, the addition of XG changed the inherent behavior and microstructure of clay; the surface crack of XG-treated clay was formed after undergoing certain W-D cycles, and the number of W-D cycles needed increased as XG content increased. Thirdly, the crack parameters, including Rc, Wav and Lto of treated clay, were smaller than that of untreated clay overall whatever the W-D cycles. Between them, the clay sample treated by 1.5% and 2% XG exhibited satisfactory desiccation cracking behavior with that the surface crack was formed after about 3rd W-D cycles, and it changed unobservable as W-D cycles increased where the Rc, Wav and Lto was 2.2%, 2.024 mm and 182.1 mm, 2.05%, 0.6 mm and 243.7 mm, respectively, which was significantly smaller than that of untreated clay. Furthermore, an increase in W-D cycles significantly facilitates both crack initiation and propagation, while showing limited influence on the overall Rc40.

Crack remediation effect

According to the test results, we found that the addition of XG could effectively change the retention water capacity of clay and its crack resistance. To further explore the effect of XG on formed cracks in clay, the crack-repair test was designed and conducted, and the effect of XG concentration and sand content of the repair material was considered. The final crack patterns of each sample after different W-D cycles are presented in Fig. 10. As illustrated in Fig. 8, the crack network morphology of the unrepaired clay sample underwent significant alterations after each W-D cycle, revealing the inherent instability of crack propagation during W-D cycling41. This instability usually stems from the complex interaction of moisture migration, shrinkage-swelling stresses, and clay structural reorganization during W-D cyclic processes. By this, after each drying process, cracks tend to propagate further, interconnect, or create new branches3,41,44,45. During the initial drying stage, crack development exhibited a distinct sequential propagation pattern. Spatially, its distribution displayed a distinct hierarchical structure, primarily governed by the initial moisture distribution gradient, the progression of the evaporation front, and the constraints imposed by the soil’s inherent native structure42. However, subsequent W-D cycles progressively diminish and eventually obliterate this initial hierarchical pattern45. This indicates that W-D cyclic disrupts the initial structural homogeneity of the clay mass, leading to crack evolution towards a more stochastic or damage-dominated pattern.

Fig. 10The final crack pattern of clay samples treated with repair agents with XG contents of (a) 1%, (b) 2% after different W-D cycles.

In contrast, the clay samples treated with the repair agent maintained a relatively stable crack development trend after each W-D cycle, whatever the component of the repair agent (Fig. 10), which might be induced by the addition of the repair agent changed the material composition and internal structure of the original sample. What’s more, all treated samples exhibited satisfactory crack resistance after the 1 st and 2nd W-D cycles, and at this point, the cracks on the sample’s surface seemed to have “healed”, especially the small cracks. After that, as the number of W-D cycles increased, the cracks gradually deepened and widened, but it was noted that they did not exceed their initial state, and some small cracks disappeared. In addition, XG contents did not show a significant difference in crack repairing effectiveness, but an increase in sand content led to higher effective resistance to crack, especially after the 1 st and 2nd W-D cycles. These findings suggested that the repair agent injection provides relatively effective inhibition of crack development, but the crack remediation efficacy depended on the sand content and the number of W-D cycles.

The states of the crack on the repaired sample at different times as shown in Fig. 11. Compared to the initial state, the cracks on the sample surface have completely healed, and the sample surface is intact after 24 h of crack repairing. It might result from clay swelling and colloidal migration. Hydration-triggered expansion of clay minerals (e.g., montmorillonite) exerts radial compressive stress (usually > 1 MPa) that forcibly close the cracks40, while dispersed fine particles infiltrate the cracks, forming electrochemically stabilized seals, thereby restoring permeability to near-original levels7,45. In addition, the repaired samples retained significantly more surface water than the unrepaired ones, and obvious repair traces could be observed on their surface, especially for the samples repaired with higher sand content. This interesting phenomenon might have occurred because cracks provided preferential pathways for water infiltration, whereas XG-based agents filled the cracks and formed a membrane-like structure upon hydration, effectively inhibiting water penetration26,45,46. After complete drying, the unrepaired sample exhibited pronounced cracks, accompanied by the formation of new secondary cracks (Fig. 11(a) and 11(c)). By comparison, the crack-repaired sample showed better crack resistance. For the sample repaired with agent of 1XG-0SA-100CL, the formation and development of cracks after drying exhibited unpredictability, with few narrow cracks, indicating that the addition of the repair agent effectively improved internal structural defects in the clay. As the sand content increased, pre-existing long and deep cracks were filled with repair agent, showing obvious crack traces without the formation of new cracks.

Microstructure characteristics

XRD results

The XRD patterns of clay before and after XG treatment are shown on Fig. 13. The results indicate that the mineral composition of untreated clay is dominated by clay minerals, including kaolinite, illite, and montmorillonite, accompanied by significant amounts of hydrophilic non-clay minerals such as quartz and anorthite. Comparative analysis reveals that after XG treatment, the characteristic diffraction peaks of the clay minerals did not change significantly in terms of position, intensity, or peak shift, and the interlayer spacing remained unchanged. These observations demonstrate that the introduction of XG does not result in the formation of new minerals, nor does it disrupt the inherent layered structure of the clay, maintaining its basic crystal stability. Mansourkiaei et al.47 reported that the improvement mechanism of XG-modified soil is predominantly attributed to physicochemical interactions, specifically the formation of a flexible protective layer through surface adsorption and encapsulation of soil particles. This physicochemical modification enhances the structural stability of the soil matrix and effectively inhibits crack development.

SEM observation

The SEM images of different clay samples are shown in Fig. 14. It appeared from Fig. 14(a) that the soil particles remained in a loosely packed state with large interparticle gaps and a well-developed void structure in untreated soil. This loose structure resulted in high porosity and poor water retention capacity of the untreated soil, making it prone to cracking and accelerated structural damage during W-D cycles. After 5th W-D cycles, the bonding between soil particles was obviously weakened, and thus the size of interparticle gaps and voids increased, leading to a loose microstructure of untreated soil. In contrast, the soil samples treated with XG exhibited notable changes in their microstructure. Figure 14(c) showed that XG formed a uniform coating on the surface of soil particles and tightly bonded and encapsulated adjacent soil particles through its unique three-dimensional network structure, forming more stable and larger aggregates. This structure significantly reduced interparticle gaps, decreased the void ratio and size of treated clay, and thereby enhanced its overall integrity and stability. After 5 W-D cycles, there was no significant particle separation or structural failure in treated clay, and the soil particles also bonded effectively (Fig. 14(d)). This demonstrated that XG addition significantly improved the structural stability of clay, enabling it to maintain a robust state even after multiple W-D cycles, exhibiting excellent durability and resistance to W-D treatment.

In addition, the hydrophilic properties of XG played a crucial role in the performance of treated clay samples. Its three-dimensional network structure not only strengthened the bonding between soil particles but also effectively absorbed and retained water, significantly enhancing the water retention capacity of clay. Yu et al.48 and Reza et al.49 reported that this characteristic of XG allowed treated clay samples to better resist water evaporation and shrinkage stress during W-D treatment, further inhibiting the formation and development of cracks.

The mechanism of XG treatment on clay

The micro-examination results indicate that XG could improve the microstructure of clay by physicochemical interactions instead of altering the mineral composition of the soil or generating new substances30,48,49 and thus enhance the mechanical properties of the soil. XG forms a unique three-dimensional network structure on the surface of soil particles40, which tightly binds and encapsulates adjacent soil particles through physical and chemical interactions, creating stable aggregate structures. As illustrated in Fig. 16, the improvement effect of XG on the soil could be divided into the following parts. (i) Coating effect, XG, leveraging the adsorption properties of its polymer chains, forms a uniform biopolymer coating on the surfaces of closely spaced soil particles. This coating not only encapsulates individual soil particles but also binds multiple particles together, forming stable aggregate structures, which in turn reduce the tendency for particle separation and enhance the overall stability of the soil. (ii) Bridging effect, XG creates biopolymer bridges between distantly spaced soil particles through its long-chain molecular structure. This bridging effect not only strengthens the bonds between particles but also imparts a degree of elasticity to the soil when subjected to external forces. This mechanism significantly improves the soil’s resistance to deformation during W-D cycles. (iii) Filling effect, XG, due to its fluidity, fills the pores within the soil particles, altering the pathways of water migration and prolonging the time required for water evaporation. Simultaneously, XG traps some water within the cavities formed by its filling, thereby increasing the water content of the sample and reducing water migration. This filling effect not only decreases the void of the clay sample but also slows the evaporation rate by blocking rapid water evaporation pathways. The structure formed by XG enhances the overall strength of the clay, improves its stability, and demonstrates excellent durability and resistance to W-D cycling9.

Discussion

Mechanism of clay cracking

Drying-induced cracking is an exquisitely complex phenomenon, involving the intricate and intertwined coupling interaction between the clay matrix and the atmospheric environment. As shown in Fig. 17, during prolonged arid periods, the superficial clay of the slope exhibits acute susceptibility to cracking. Under the combined effects of solar radiation and water vaporization, the temperature at the clay-air interface increases, triggering the phase transition of liquid water to water vapor. This vapor then diffuses continuously from the clay matrix, driving the evaporation dynamics46. As evaporation progresses, clay water content gradually reduces, concomitantly leading to a significant increase in clay matric suction. When the increased suction exceeds the tensile strength (i.e., bond capacity) between soil particles, the connection between soil particles breaks and gradually develops into cracks38,48. Repeated W-D cycles significantly exacerbate this process by stress concentration in subsequent drying stages causes tensile damage in these areas, particularly at the edges of the cracks, where the soil was prone to collapse when it lost its support. The collapsed soil mass further fills the crack space, creating a visual “healing” effect and leaving “shallow ridges” along the crack lines7. This exacerbates the roughness of crack edges and induces finer microcracks within clay samples. Each drying stage triggers the initiation of new microfractures, while the wetting stage softens crack tips through water infiltration and generates swelling pressure, making cracks more susceptible to propagation in subsequent drying phases39,44.

The initiation of these cracks enhances the soil-air contact area, thereby accelerating water loss further3,7,46. This, in turn, promotes the expansion of cracks in length, width, and depth. When this crack network develops to a certain extent, it will become a preferred channel for accelerating rainwater infiltration, elevating the water table, and softening the deeper soils, while repeated W-D cycling will reduce the strength of shallow unsaturated soils, resulting in topsoil erosion44,46. When groundwater flow is coupled with the fracture network, infiltration erosion continuously takes away the fine particles on the fracture walls, enlarges the erosion area, and reduces the clay cohesion, which ultimately leads to the overall shallow instability of slopes and induces traction-type shallow landslides, and impact on nearby buildings and the ecological environment.

Mechanism of XG-treated clay cracking

The mechanism by which XG inhibits clay cracking in the slope is shown in Fig. 18. The test results demonstrate that XG treatment significantly enhances clay stability and crack resistance by forming a polymeric film in soil48,49,52. By this, the XG-treated clay is sprayed onto the cracked slopes like shotcrete, and with local adjustments to ensure it penetrates and fills larger cracks and covers the slope surface with a certain thickness, which would form a solidified layer on the slope surface, as shown in Fig. 18. The solidifies layer is different from the untreated clay in the following: (i) XG reduces the soil voids by its filling effect, whereby the biopolymer infiltrates and occludes void spaces, changing water migration pathways and prolonging the evaporation timeline3. (ii) Water entrapped within XG molecular structures sustains clay water content, reduces the water evaporation rate, and attenuates crack propagation triggered by abrupt water loss. (iii) The elastic connections between soil particles formed by XG enhance the bond strength between soil particles and confer treated clay restorative resistance against particle displacement during external load. Ma et al.53 reported that the tensile strength of sand treated with 3% XG increased by 79% in comparison with the untreated one. This elastic connection enables the clay to absorb energy through deformation when subjected to a load, effectively impeding crack propagation and improving clay toughness44. In addition, the XG connection between soil particles increases the aggregates size, which reduces the increase in clay matric suction when water content decreases and suppresses crack initiation to a certain extent13,54. This multiple-action mechanism changes the internal structure of the clay, especially in response to evaporation cracking, thereby improving the clay’s crack resistance. The laboratory test results show that when treated with optimal XG content, the treated clay keeps the structure and surface intact without forming obvious cracks, even undergoing several W-D cycles (see Figs. 7 and 8). In addition, after a certain W-D cycle, the crack size and surface crack ratio formed on the treated clay are significantly reduced, especially in high-content treated clay; both main and secondary cracks showed obvious reductions in width and number.

The schematic diagram of XG-treated topsoil cracking in a slope.

For the cracked clay repaired with XG, the repair material fills the crack sites and penetrates gradually into soil particles at crack locations and the clay surface, bonding the particles through sustained tensile force. By this, the repair material establishes a continuous connection in the crack. This mechanism inhibits water evaporation and reduces water loss. In addition, due to the incompressibility of sand particles, the more sand particles in the repair material can occupy more cracking space in the clay, which makes the repair material with higher sand content show a better repair effect55. The test results indicate that cracked clay samples treated with the repair agent exhibited satisfactory crack resistance after the first two wet-dry (W-D) cycles.

The aforementioned results show that the XG-treated clay has good resistance to desiccation cracking in the laboratory, and it holds significant application promise due to the following key advantages: (i) the XG demonstrates superior performance in crack resistance and overall durability. Previous studies indicate that the durability of XG-treated soil depends on environmental cycles, XG content, and soil properties. Wen et al. found that biopolymer treated sand still maintained high strength (about 60% of the initial strength) after 12 W-D and F-T cycles56. Additionally, suitable soil particle gradation (i.e., a fines content of 15%−25%) can enhance the durability of XG treated soil57. Reddy and Varaprasad58 reported that after 12 W-D cycles, the mass loss of XG treated soil was much lower than the allowable value (14%), and they pointed out that XG could still function in the soil after 360 days. (ii) It is readily available and easy to handle in the field. XG is a highly commercialized product with well-established supply chains, thanks to its extensive use in the food, petroleum, and personal care industries. The unit price of xanthan gum is USD 2–3/kg, which is higher than that of cement (USD 0.095/kg), a comprehensive evaluation reveals its growing economic viability. Firstly, the cost of XG has already decreased dramatically from approximately USD 30,000 per ton in the 1960 s to USD 1,500-4,000 per ton59, and its price potential remains high due to the relatively low purity requirements for geotechnical applications. Moreover, the XG usage is less than that of cement to achieve similar effectiveness. Chang et al. reported that the soil treated with 0.5% XG can achieve the strength of 10% cement content30. (iii) XG itself is an eco-friendly material that poses no environmental risk. Firstly, XG is a natural biopolymer, produced by the fermentation of Xanthomonas campestris, utilizing common agricultural feedstocks such as corn starch. Moreover, the production of 1 kg of XG consumes 4.97 kg of CO₂, whereas producing 1 kg of cement emits 1.25 kg of CO₂59. This stark contrast in carbon footprint means that the economic value of XG becomes substantially more pronounced when environmental costs are internalized. Secondly, unlike inert materials (e.g., cement) that require removal or leave permanent residues, XG naturally degrades after fulfilling its function, being assimilated by the surrounding ecosystem without creating long-term waste60. Consequently, combining xanthan gum stabilization with other techniques, such as plant reinforcement, is highly advantageous. Herein, the XG plays a transitional role by providing temporary soil stabilization and erosion control before the plant reinforcement function is fully established. Simultaneously, it can create a favorable environment for seed germination and plant growth60. As the vegetation matures and takes over the primary stabilization function, XG gradually degrades, naturally phasing out without requiring removal or replacement. Furthermore, it can be used for targeted, short-term repair of any localized damage that may occur before and after vegetation establishment. By this, it facilitates a smooth transition from an “engineered” to a “natural” ecological protection system.

Conclusion

This study aimed to systematically investigate the crack resistance performance of XG-treated clay. Firstly, W-D cycle tests were performed on XG-treated clay to study its evaporation characteristics, crack propagation, and patterns. Secondly, the repairing efficacy of XG used as a repair agent in cracked clay was studied by testing the crack propagation and patterns. Thirdly, the microstructure and component change of the treated clay were examined by micro-examination. Finally, the mechanism of XG on changing clay desiccation cracking behavior was discussed according to the test results. The conclusion was drawn as follows:
