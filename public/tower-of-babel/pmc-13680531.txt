A multi-composite disease activity score (NFKB1-DAS) for NFKB1 insufficiency
Authors: Katharina Thoma, Pia Hassunah, Manfred Fliegauf, Gabriele Müller, Andres Caballero Garcia de Oteyza, Pavla Mrovecova, Helene Kraus, Prerana Agarwal, Maximilian Frederik Russe, Birgit Sawitzki, Lev Petrov, Katrin Vogt, Nadezhda Camacho Ordonez, Aleksandra Hirsch, Christoph B Geier, Máté Krausz, Sigune Goldacker, Natalie Frede, Stephan Ehl, Sarah Salou, Ansgar Schulz, Shahrzad Bakhtiar, Maria Fasshauer, Oliver Hausmann, Anja-Dorothee Hüfner, Martin Stanulla, Anna Luijendijk, Faranaz Atschekzei, Georgios Sogkas, Ulrich Salzer, Baerbel Keller, Michele Proietti, Klaus Warnatz, Bodo Grimbacher
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNFKB1 encodes the p105/p50 NF-κB1 transcription factor. Heterozygous mutations in NFKB1 may lead to NFKB1 insufficiency affecting multiple organ systems. This study aimed to develop an NFKB1-Disease-Activity-Score (NFKB1-DAS), serving as a tool in future clinical trials. Based on published phenotypic data from 179 patients with 77 distinct NFKB1 variants, we drafted an initial score consisting of 42 parameters. This draft was subsequently analyzed in a multicenter cohort of 41 patients with 22 distinct pathogenic NFKB1 variants. Using a Leave-One-Out (LOO) analysis (cutoff differential value: 23.07), we identified the 21 most relevant parameters to construct the final NFKB1-DAS. Calculation of the NFKB1-DAS in 31 CTLA4-deficient patients as a comparison cohort showed higher scores in NFKB1-insufficient patients (p = 0.03 (95% CI [-0.16, -0.01]) and the NFKB1-DAS correlated with hospitalization (p = 0.01; OR 2.07 (95% CI [1.18; 3.62]). However, given the optimism bias occurring when testing the score in the cohort it was developed with and the limited case numbers, validation of the NFKB1-DAS in an unrelated cohort remains essential. Analysis of the NFKB1-DAS in patients with variants of unknown significance (VUS) in NFKB1 (n = 14) showed individual patients reaching high scores. Future studies could evaluate a possible utility of the score in assessing VUS pathogenicity. Ultimately, the proposed NFKB1-DAS represents a first step toward monitoring disease activity and treatment response in future clinical trials in NFKB1 insufficiency.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s10875-026-02081-3.Keywords: NFKB1, NF-κB1, Disease activity, Treatment, Score, NFKB1-DAS

Introduction

NFKB1 (Nuclear factor kappa B subunit 1) encodes the cytoplasmic precursor p105, processed to generate the NF-κB transcription factor subunit p50. The NF-κB family comprises the five members NF-κB1 (p105/p50), NF-κB2 (p100/p52), RelA (p65), RelB and c-Rel, being nearly ubiquitously expressed and forming a variety of homo- and heterodimers with activating or repressing functions depending on their combination. The NF-κB signaling system comprises the canonical (NF-κB1-dependent) and the non-canonical (NF-κB2-dependent) pathway. Canonical signaling is typically activated by inflammatory signals (such as TNF-α) via cell surface receptors, which triggers the release of mainly p50:RelA dimers from their cytoplasmic inhibitors (IκB), translocating to the nucleus to regulate their target genes including a variety of immune genes [1–3]. Reflecting extreme intolerance to inactivation, NFKB1 exhibits a pLI of 1.0 and a LOEUF of 0.15, confirming severe constraint against loss-of-function variation [4].

Previous studies demonstrated that most pathogenic heterozygous NFKB1 variants cause (haplo-) insufficiency (HI) of p105 and p50 which may result in a multiorgan disease, referred to here as NFKB1 insufficiency, affecting respiratory, gastrointestinal, lymphatic system and the skin [5]. NFKB1 insufficiency is the most common monogenic cause for primary antibody deficiency [5–7]. However, with 70%, the penetrance of proven pathogenic variants in NFKB1 is incomplete and disease severity varies considerably even among carriers of the same mutation in NFKB1. [5, 8] The disease can progress chronically over many years and remains challenging to treat, resulting in diverse therapeutic strategies. In the cohort reported in this study, the predominant therapeutic approaches were immunoglobulin replacement, immunomodulatory therapy, or antibiotic prophylaxis of infections. Lacking large-scale, systematic treatment studies, the clinical presentation and the experience of the attending physician currently determine the preferred treatment. There is neither a targeted therapy to ‘cure’ NFKB1 insufficiency, nor individualized therapies to manage unique clinical presentations or individual genetic variants [5].

Treatment studies on NFKB1 insufficiency require an objective multi-organ composit score tool to assess the benefits of various therapeutic options. Applying global disease severity scores for inborn errors of immunity, such as common variable immunodeficiency (CVID) severity scores or scores addressing single organ manifestations like the Bhalla score for lung diseases or the NANO scale (Neurologic Assessment in Neuro-Oncology) for neurologic symptoms are not sufficient, since they are not specific enough for NFKB1 insufficiency [9–14]. Our study processes previously known disease manifestations and newly collected data into a novel clinical tool, the NFKB1-DAS.

Methods

Analysis of previous NFKB1 publications leads to the generation of a preliminary score based on published reports, expert knowledge and review of patients’ records

In July 2020 seven main publications were selected describing the NFKB1-associated disease phenotype in 179 patients with 77 distinct NFKB1 variants classified as pathogenic or possibly pathogenic based on prediction tools or functional tests (Table S1). Based on the identified characteristic organ manifestations of NFKB1 insufficiency a first score draft comprising 42 parameters was established (Table S2, Fig. S1 Part 1). Repeatedly reported symptoms and diagnoses were summarized in score parameters, partly using established scores like the CHAI morbidity score (from the ABACHAI study = Abatacept for CTLA4 HAploInsufficiency), the Hartmann score, an immunoglobulin replacement score, the Medical Research Council (MRC) Dyspnoea Scale, the Neurologic Assessment in Neuro-Oncology (NANO) Scale and the Disease Activity Score including C-reactive Protein (DAS-28-CRP) [13, 15–19] or experience from previous studies [22].

The NFKB1-DAS is indicated as a percentage value corresponding to the achieved score points relative to the maximum score value (File S4). The maximum possible score value results from the maximum number of points of all parameters with available data. Zero to three points can be achieved for each parameter (0 = not present, 1 = mild, 2 = moderate, 3 = severe).

Score calculation according to the first score draft in a cohort of 41 patients with pathogenic variants in NFKB1

NFKB1 cohort and collection of clinical data

From October 2020 to February 2023, prospective and retrospective clinical data collection (going back to 2007) in a cohort of 41 patients with pathogenic variants in NFKB1 was conducted, aiming to calculate the score according to the first score draft (Fig. S1 Part 2). Patients were recruited from clinical databases and eight centers in Germany and Switzerland and have a central European background in the vast majority [20, 21]. Our study cohort comprised 41 patients carrying 22 unique pathogenic NFKB1 variants confirmed by functional in vitro assays to cause a protein defect or predicted to be pathogenic with a high probability (e.g. all truncations) (Tables 1 and 2, Table S3) [5, 6, 8, 23, 24]. In addition, 14 patients with 13 distinct variants of unknown significance (VUS) were collected.

Functional data for the evaluated NFKB1 variants were synthesized from comprehensive benchmarking studies [6, 23, 25].

The information required for the score was collected based on patient interviews, using questionnaires (File S1 and File S2), and documented clinical data. In general, symptoms that are known to be immune-related were considered as associated with the underlying NFKB1 insufficiency. To calculate the score parameter for programmed cell death protein one positive (PD-1+) T cells, 42 blood samples from 22 patients with pathogenic variants in NFKB1 were analyzed by CyTOF (Fig. S2). For the later following comparison of the NFKB1-DAS in pathogenic vs. VUS patients, PD-1+ T cells were also calculated in 12 patients with VUS in NFKB1 and analysed using Spearman correlation and negative binomial regression (Fig. 3).

Score calculation based on the initial score draft

Score values were calculated using Excel. One up to seven time points were achieved for each patient. The data used to determine the individual score parameters was not allowed to deviate by more than +/- 3 months from the survey date (resp. -6 months if it was the first survey date). The median time between the first and second time point was 9 months and 6 months between second and third time point.

Reducing the number of score parameters using a LOO analysis

First, the four CT parameters were integrated into one reducing the total number to 39 parameters. For the following LOO analysis, score data of the first time point from 41 patients with pathogenic NFKB1 variants was used. Only data from the first time point was used to minimize data gaps (not all patients had several assessments) and to avoid potential bias from longitudinal changes.

To reduce the number of score parameters, we performed a LOO analysis at the parameter level (Fig. S1 Part 3). For each of the 39 parameters, the overall score was recalculated after omitting that parameter, while keeping the remaining 38 parameters unchanged. The resulting change in score was quantified as a differential value. Specifically, the differential value was defined as the mean squared difference between the original score and the score recalculated without that parameter across all patients. Parameters causing only small changes were considered less relevant and sequentially removed. The remaining parameters with the largest differential values were retained for the final score, reflecting their relative contribution to the overall score. The analysis was performed using R (Fig. 1 and Fig. 2a). The basic principles of this approach are described by e.g. Hastie et al. 2009 (p. 241ff.) or Wang et al., who called the method a Leave-One-Covariate-Out approach, but also included machine learning methods that were not part of the methods in this study [26, 27]. To identify a cutoff to decide which parameters to exclude from the score, parameters were sorted in descending order of their differential value. A cutoff was set between the two parameters with the largest difference in the differential values, as this reflected the largest drop of impact of parameters (Fig. 2). The parameter “other autoimmune/autoinflammatory manifestations” was considered separately since it summarizes multiple parameters, therefore it gains additional data points when other parameters are removed from the score. For instance, when “autoimmune vasculitis” was removed from the score because of a differential value below the cutoff, a vasculitis disease manifestation was scored in the parameter “other autoimmune/autoinflammatory manifestations”. When we repeated the analysis with the 20 parameters above the cut-off only plus “other autoimmune/autoinflammatory manifestations”, we obtained a differential value for the latter parameter in the mid-range, compared to other included parameters (Fig. 2). Therefore “other autoimmune/autoinflammatory manifestations” was included in the final NFKB1-DAS. The remaining 21 parameters form the final NFKB1-DAS presented here (Questionnaire in File S3, Score in File S4).

Additional analysis with the final NFKB1-DAS investigating its performance

Analysis of the NFKB1-DAS in a control group of CTLA4-deficient patients

The NFKB1-DAS of 31 affected carriers with pathogenic variants in CTLA4 (cytotoxic T-lymphocyte associated protein 4) (Tables S4, S5 and S7, all variants previously published [28–32]) was compared to the NFKB1-DAS in patients with pathogenic variants in NFKB1 using a two-sided t test to have a first insight whether the score can reflect even subtle, potentially NFKB1-specific differences (Fig. S1 Part 4) [33]. The CTLA4 cohort matched our NFKB1 cohort very well regarding age and sex.

Analysis of hospitalization and correlation with the NFKB1-DAS

We chose hospitalization as a general marker to test the ability of the NFKB1-DAS to measure disease activity as a first proof-of-concept approach. NFKB1-DAS values of 41 patients with pathogenic variants in NFKB1 were correlated with the number of days spent in hospital within the last 6 months using a hurdle model with negative binomial regression to account for overdispersion in our limited dataset (Fig. S1 Part 4). In all cases, the indication for hospitalization was classified as probably related to the underlying NFKB1 insufficiency. Information on hospitalization was taken from medical records and patient questionnaires.

Calculation of the NFKB1-DAS in patients with VUS in NFKB1

During patient recruiting we found 14 patients with VUS in NFKB1 (Table S3) and decided to calculate the NFKB1-DAS in these patients to examine differences in score values over time to see whether patients with pathogenic variants would show a more severe course of disease according to the NFKB1-DAS (Fig. S1 Part 4). Patients with VUS in NFKB1 were examined and scored using the NFKB1-DAS as described before with a median time between first and second appointment of 6 months.

NFKB1-DAS in cohort of patients with pathogenic variants in NFKB1

Most patients with pathogenic variants in NFKB1 showed hypogammaglobulinemia (81%) and splenomegaly (76%). Respiratory infections affected more than half of patients (60%). 40% of patients had skin manifestations (especially rosacea, psoriasis and vitiligo), but only 8% with systemic treatment. One third of patients (36%) suffered from gastrointestinal manifestations (36%) or liver involvement (32%) (Table S6, additional information on liver manifestations can also be found in the supplemental data).

Analysis of the PD-1 expression of CD4 + and CD8 + T cells in NFKB1 insufficient patients

The PD-1 expression of CD4 + T cells was increased in 57% of patients. A Spearman correlation revealed an association between the NFKB1-DAS and CD4+ PD-1+ T cell levels in patients with pathogenic NFKB1 variants (ρ = 0.73, p < 0.001; Fig. 3A). Consistent with this finding, a negative binomial regression demonstrated a 1.37-fold increase of the CD4 + PD-1+ T cell counts per 10-percent-point increase of the NFKB1-DAS (incidence rate ratio (IRR) 1.37; p < 0.001 (95% CI [1.18;1.60]). They show a higher percentage of PD-1+ CD4+ T cells (mean/median: 38%/31%) than PD-1+ CD8+ T cells (mean/median: 18%/15%) in patients with pathogenic variants in NFKB1 (Fig. 3B), which we did not find in patients with VUS in NFKB1 (mean/median of PD-1+ CD4+ T cells 19%/18%, mean/median of PD-1+ CD8+ T cells 18%/13%) (Fig. 3C).

Autoimmune manifestations are affecting a variety of organs and appear to disproportionately affect women

54% of patients with pathogenic variants in NFKB1 had at least one autoimmune or autoinflammatory disease not scored in other parameters. Female patients were affected more frequently. Manifestations included the gastrointestinal system, skin/hair, blood vessels, joints, liver, blood cells and lungs (Fig. 4).

Additional analyses investigating the performance of the NFKB1-DAS

The NFKB1-DAS correlates with the hospitalization rate

We performed a correlation of the NFKB1-DAS with the number of days spent in hospital in the last 6 months (Fig. S1 part 4). Patients were stratified into a hospitalized group (≥ 1 hospital day within the last 6 months) and a non-hospitalized group. A hurdle model showed that every 10-percent-point increase in the NFKB1-DAS score was associated with a doubling of the probability of hospitalization, defined as at least one day spent in hospital in the last 6 months (p = 0.01, OR 2.07, 95% CI [1.18; 3.62], mean/median NFKB1-DAS hospitalized group: 53%/58%, mean/median NFKB1-DAS non-hospitalized group: 28%/27%) (Fig. 5a). Based on a descriptive analysis of the hospitalization rate, we propose classifying NFKB1-DAS values into mild, moderate, and severe categories (Fig. 6): Mild defined as an NFKB1-DAS < 10% (those patients were not hospitalized in our cohort), moderate defined as an NFKB1-DAS < 30% (with a hospitalization rate of < 10% in our cohort), and severe defined as an NFKB1-DAS of 30% or higher (with a hospitalization rate of > 10% in our cohort).

Patients with pathogenic variants in NFKB1 show higher NFKB1-DAS values than patients with variants in CTLA4

For a first approach testing whether the NFKB1-DAS differentiates between NFKB1 insufficiency and other familiar phenotypes with incomplete penetrance, the scoring system was applied to a cohort of 31 patients with CTLA4-insufficiency as a control group (Fig. S1 Part 4, Table S4, S5, S7). CTLA4-deficient patients reached a mean NFKB1-DAS of 23% (median 22%) compared to 32% in the NFKB1 cohort (median 28%) (Fig. 5b), which was significant with p = 0.03 (95% CI [-0.16, -0.01]).

Patients with pathogenic variants in NFKB1 show an increasing NFKB1-DAS over time compared to patients with VUS in NFKB1

In patients with a pathogenic variant in NFKB1, mean and median NFKB1-DAS increased over time (Fig. 7a). In contrast, patients with a VUS in NFKB1 had decreasing NFKB1-DAS scores over time (Fig. 7b). Longitudinal analysis of individual score parameters reveals divergent trends: Some parameters increase (e.g., spleen size or the percentage of PD-1⁺CD4⁺ T cells), while others decrease (e.g., severity of non-respiratory infections) or remain stable (Fig. 7c). These findings suggest that certain parameters may be more informative for tracking disease progression than others.

Almost all patients with a pathogenic variant in NFKB1, as well as patients with a VUS in NFKB1 received systemic drug therapy (85% and 86% respectively). Children and adolescents < 18 years of age had a lower median NFKB1-DAS value (26%) than patients > 18 years of age (32%).

Discussion

Selecting score parameters

Following our literature analysis in 2020, a large meta-analysis has been published in 2024 by Fathi et al. [34] The results of this meta-analysis corresponded well with the parameters we selected for our NFKB1-DAS. The only manifestations not covered in the NFKB1-DAS are cardiovascular, neurological, and malignant manifestations, which were reported by Fathi et al. with a frequency of 0.6%, 1.7% and 5.6%, respectively. On purpose, we did not include malignancies in the NFKB1-DAS to avoid bias due to chemotherapy. Likewise, the IDDA (Immune Deficiency and Dysregulation Activity) 2.1 score also excludes malignancies from scoring [14].

Recruiting patients

Since we recruited patients from immunology departments, there could be a bias regarding disease manifestations that are treated in other departments such as pyoderma. As the clinical picture of NFKB1 insufficiency is primarily known in the context of immune dysregulation and therefore among immunologists, interdisciplinary collaboration and raising awareness of NFKB1 insufficiency in other disciplines is essential to identify affected variant carriers.

Data collection

NFKB1 insufficiency is a rare disease which limits the number of patients in our study cohort. Data was collected during routine appointments which were often cancelled or postponed during the Covid-19 pandemic, both factors limiting a complete, systematic and broad data collection. We met these challenges by using a multicenter approach and extending our observation period to 2.5 years of prospective, as well as additional retrospective data collection. However, all presented analyses should be carefully interpreted considering the limited data.

Systematic cohort analysis complements previous findings on the NFKB1 phenotype

In our study we were able to perform a systematic extensive multicenter data collection on 41 patients with pathogenic variants in NFKB1 as well as 14 patients with a VUS in NFKB1 including data that have - to our knowledge - never been assessed before in NFKB1 patients, such as PD-1+ T cell analysis. All variants have been reported before, but not all patients have been published with their full clinical picture. With the exception of a higher frequency of liver manifestations, our phenotypic data assessment fits the findings published in the largest NFKB1 cohort to date by Lorenzini et al. [5] The overall higher proportion of women in our cohort (61%, Table 2) does not fully explain the higher proportion of women with autoimmune manifestations (median 71%; Fig. 4). Higher estrogen levels or incomplete X-chromosome inactivation could be contributing factors [35–38].

Development of final NFKB1-DAS

The LOO approach allowed us to use all available patient data effectively, provided a transparent view of each parameter’s impact, and ultimately resulted in a more concise and interpretable score, even when starting from many parameters and a relatively small dataset. However, to mitigate the risk of overfitting inherent to this study design, these findings require validation in independent, heterogenous and ideally larger cohorts. Rare but severe disease manifestations may be underrepresented, as the LOO method emphasizes more frequent manifestations. At the same time, this focus ensures that the score reflects the typical and most observed phenotype and is feasible in clinical practice (one-page questionnaire in File S3). The cohort analysis combined with the LOO approach brings a significant improvement of methodology e.g. compared to the CHAI Morbidity score, developed solely based on expert knowledge [17]. Since the NFKB1-DAS can be calculated for the patient’s current disease state or cumulatively for the entire medical history, it can be used for prospective or retrospective studies. By calculating a score value in percent, we address the problem of missing data in clinical application. All parameters can be collected during regular clinical patient care. Also the PD-1+ CD4+ T cell analysis can be readily implemented in routine clinical laboratories (at a cost of approximately 15€) using a simple four-color whole-blood flow cytometry panel (CD45, CD3, CD4, and PD-1) on standard diagnostic instruments, making its translation into clinical practice technically feasible and relatively inexpensive.

The NFKB1-DAS may uncover pathogenic effects of NFKB1 variants classified as VUS

For the VUS identified in this study, no mechanistic insights into the pathomechanism of the resulting protein can be provided. This is a common limitation for VUS, where not only the clinical significance but often also the primary consequences at the protein function level remain unresolved. At the same time, ’neutral’ assessments in the currently available functional tests (which do not cover all protein functions) do not necessarily confirm a variant to be benign. On the contrary, particularly rare variants with strong variant effect predictions (VEPs), which were indistinguishable from controls in these assays, cannot be considered as necessarily non-pathogenic. We hypothesize that a high NFKB1-DAS value in patients carrying a VUS in NFKB1 indicates NF-κB1 dysregulation due to a potentially pathogenic effect of the VUS. For instance, patients NFKB1-16 (carrying NFKB1-variant-30, c.736 C > A, p.Pro246Thr), NFKB1-20 (carrying NFKB1-variant-24, c.734 C > T, p.Ala245Val), and NFKB1-23 (carrying NFKB1-variant-29, c.269 A > C, p.Tyr90Ser), who all carry neutrally tested variants, have high score values of 36.84%, 35.71% and 42.11% (classified as severe according to our proposed scheme demonstrated in Fig. 6) at the first time point, respectively [6, 23]. Therefore, it could be worthwhile re-analyzing these variants for a functional defect as a first approach to test our hypothesis, followed by studying a possible correlation between the NFKB1-DAS and the pathogenicity of NFKB1 variants in unrelated and larger cohorts. Moreover, the contribution of concomitant secondary e.g. (epi)-genetic factors should be considered. One example is patient NFKB1-37, who carries a VUS in NFKB1 (NFKB1-variant-25, c.307 A > G, p.Asn103Asp) and a variant in TNFRSF13B (tumor necrosis factor receptor superfamily number 13B), encoding TACI (Transmembrane activator and CAML interactor) (c.579 C > A, p.Cys193*) who has a high NFKB1-DAS of 24%.

Performance of the NFKB1-DAS

The NFKB1-DAS was higher in NFKB1 insufficient patients than in patients with CTLA4 deficiency, although CTLA4-deficient patients reached a mean NFKB1-DAS of 23%, which is due to shared phenotypic characteristics, such as hypogammaglobulinemia, autoimmune, respiratory and gastrointestinal manifestations [5, 33]. Our multi system composite NFKB1-DAS aims to offer a more specific representation of NFKB1 insufficiency than previous scores developed for different or multiple diseases, such as the CHAI morbidity score or the IDDA 2.1 score, and to be a more suitable tool for treatment studies that rely on depicting the effects on specific disease manifestations as accurately as possible [14, 17]. The presented performance analyses (comparison with CTLA4 cohort and hospitalization analysis) should be interpreted cautiously and perceived as proof-of-concept approaches, considering the limited sample size resulting in high variance and deviation from standard distribution. These analyses were also not adjusted for age, sex, or disease duration due to the limited data available. Conclusions regarding sensitivity and specificity of the NFKB1-DAS should not be drawn, instead, validation of the NFKB1-DAS should be performed in a follow-up study unrelated to the study cohort the score development was based on, to minimize bias. The proposed severity levels (Fig. 6), should be examined and further developed in these follow-up studies.

Application of the NFKB1-DAS to guide medication strategies and track treatment success

The score data of this cohort demonstrates that disease activity increases with age even under medical treatment. This fits the long-assumed age-dependency of NFKB1-insufficiency and indicates that a disease-specific treatment is still missing. Follow-up studies should test this finding, since the number of patients with multiple score analyses was limited. NFKB1 insufficiency can lead to hyperinflammatory disease similar to A20 haploinsufficiency, where a negative regulator of NF-κB is reduced and NF-κB signaling is overactive [39]. Whether anti-inflammatory drugs like TNF-α inhibitors are superior to other treatment options beyond individual cases is currently investigated in a worldwide NFKB1 study using and validating the NFKB1-DAS. An important limitation in the assessment of complex chronic diseases, one that is also reflected in our score, is the unclear distinction between acute disease activity and chronic, permanent organ damage. Follow-up studies may be able to further refine the NFKB1-DAS in this regard.

Conclusion

Increasing NFKB1-DAS values in affected NFKB1 insufficient patients underline the urgent need for more efficient personalized treatment options. Our results pave the way for using the NFKB1-DAS as an objective tool for treatment studies in patients with NFKB1-insufficiency. Future studies should assess the ability of disease activity monitoring and potential diagnostic value of the proposed composite score.
