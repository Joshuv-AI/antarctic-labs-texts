Introduction

Non-communicable diseases (NCDs) are now central to health policy in many African countries, yet one major equity issue remains insufficiently addressed: whether people with disabilities (PWDs) are meaningfully included in prevention, diagnosis, treatment, rehabilitation and long-term care.1 This omission matters not only because PWDs face substantial barriers to health services but also because the relationship between disability and NCDs is multidirectional and deeply shaped by health system design. NCDs can lead to disability. Conversely, disability may be associated with an increased risk of NCDs and poorer health outcomes due to exclusion, poverty, inaccessibility and barriers to care. When health systems fail to provide equitable chronic care, both NCDs and impairments can worsen over time.2 If disability remains peripheral to NCD policy in Africa, strategies for universal health coverage (UHC), primary healthcare (PHC) and chronic disease control will remain incomplete. They will exclude a substantial share of the population from prevention, care and protection and they will fail to address a major driver of health inequity that operates across the entire NCD continuum.

PWDs are not a small or uniform subgroup.3 They constitute 1.3 billion of the world’s population, with the majority in low-income and middle-income countries.4 The African dimension of this burden is particularly acute. Nearly 30% of NCD-related deaths in sub-Saharan Africa occur in people aged <60 years, compared with approximately 13% in high-income countries. Furthermore, NCD-related disability-adjusted life-years (DALYs) across the region increased by approximately 67% between 1990 and 2017.5 6 These figures underscore that in Africa, the NCD–disability convergence is not a distant projection but a present and premature reality. Disability prevalence in African settings is likely underestimated due to measurement limitations, stigma, exclusion from data systems and differences in survey methods.7 Nor do PWDs form a single clinical or social category. Experiences differ by impairment type, age, sex, socio-economic position, rurality, displacement status and whether a person lives in conflict-affected or otherwise fragile settings.8 These axes do not act in isolation but intersect. An intersectionality lens, originating in scholarship on overlapping structures of disadvantage,9 helps explain why disability compounds with sex, wealth, education and geography to produce distinct patterns of NCD risk and exclusion. African evidence is instructive. Analyses of health-service coverage across the continent show that the influence of disability on healthcare access is shaped by education and wealth, with the most disadvantaged women with disabilities facing the greatest barriers. In contrust, greater socio-economic advantage can partially mitigate these barriers.10 Single-axis framings that treat disability as homogeneous therefore risk obscuring precisely those who are least reached.8 11 A woman with a mobility impairment seeking cervical cancer screening, an older man with hearing loss and undiagnosed hypertension, a young adult with psychosocial disability navigating diabetes care and a child with developmental disability transitioning into adulthood will face different combinations of risk and exclusion. As state parties to the United Nations Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities (CRPD), African governments are legally obligated under Article 25 to ensure that PWDs have access to health services of the same range, quality and standard as those provided to others, without discrimination.12

Disability and non-communicable diseases are linked through more than comorbidity

The relationship between disability and NCDs is bidirectional and should not be reduced to a one-way proposition in which disability simply increases disease burden. Existing evidence shows that NCDs can cause new impairments, worsen existing functional limitations and introduce additional forms of disability over time.2 Stroke may result in mobility, communication, cognitive or psychosocial impairments. Diabetes can lead to visual impairment, kidney disease, neuropathy and limb loss. Chronic respiratory disease may restrict mobility and daily functioning. Cancers and their treatment can produce pain, fatigue, lymphoedema or long-term functional limitations; and dementia and other long-term mental health conditions can affect cognition, independence and social participation.2 13 In these cases, the timeliness and quality of prevention, acute care, rehabilitation, assistive technology and long-term disease management strongly influence whether an impairment becomes disabling and whether autonomy and participation are preserved. For example, Otieno et al14 found that a cardiometabolic cluster comprising hypertension, diabetes, abdominal obesity, cataract and arthritis was associated with markedly higher odds of moderate and severe disability in Ghana and South Africa, whereas a representative study of older Ghanaians reported that diabetes was independently associated with higher odds of functional limitation in a dose–response manner.15

Conversely, people with pre-existing disabilities may face higher exposure to NCD risk factors and poorer NCD outcomes, not because these outcomes are inevitable but because health and social systems often fail to accommodate diverse functional needs. A growing body of evidence shows that PWDs experience inequities in access to health promotion, preventive care, diagnosis, treatment, rehabilitation and follow-up services.2 13 These inequities can delay diagnosis, reduce treatment adherence, increase complications and worsen avoidable morbidity.16–19 For example, inaccessible transport, clinics, diagnostic equipment and health information can limit access to screening and continuity of care. Exclusion from schools, workplaces and community life may reduce opportunities for physical activity and healthy social participation. Poverty, food insecurity and dependence on caregivers can make healthy diets more difficult to maintain. Some medications used for long-term physical or mental health conditions may also increase weight, metabolic risk or cardiovascular risk, while institutional neglect, prolonged immobility or inadequate support may increase the likelihood of secondary conditions.16–19 Evidence from low-income and middle-income countries further suggests that these risks are amplified where rehabilitation, assistive technology, accessible infrastructure and long-term care systems are weak.

Prynn and Kuper2 highlight stroke and dementia as examples of NCDs that are both major causes of disability and conditions whose outcomes are shaped by the availability of rehabilitation, caregiver support, inclusive health services and social protection.2 Similarly, the global scoping review by Umucu et al19 documents persistent health inequities among PWDs across multiple settings, including barriers to preventive care, poorer access to services and worse health outcomes compared with persons without disabilities.19 In a recent review, Emeto11 argues that the systematic under-representation of PWDs in the data systems, biobanks and artificial-intelligence models now driving cardiovascular epidemiology is not a peripheral concern but a stress test of the validity and equity of the entire enterprise. Together, this evidence supports a comparison between persons with and without disabilities not only in terms of disease prevalence but also in terms of exposure to risk drivers, access to timely care, quality of treatment, rehabilitation and long-term outcomes.

This distinction is important for policy. If disability is framed only as an individual vulnerability or comorbidity, the response may be limited to small targeted programmes. If disability is understood as both an outcome of NCDs and a determinant of unequal NCD prevention and care, then inclusion becomes central to mainstream NCD policy. Disability-inclusive NCD responses should therefore address the full pathway from prevention and early diagnosis to acute care, rehabilitation, chronic disease management, assistive technology, social protection and long-term care.

People with disabilities encounter barriers across the non-communicable disease continuum

The barriers PWDs face begin before contact with formal care.3 Public health campaigns on tobacco use, diet, physical activity, obesity, hypertension, diabetes, cancer warning signs and mental health are often not designed with accessibility in mind.20 Information may be unavailable in sign language, easy-read formats, Braille, captioned audiovisual formats or local communication forms usable by people with intellectual, visual, hearing or communication impairments.21 Health promotion delivered through schools, workplaces, digital platforms or community gatherings may also fail to reach those excluded from these spaces.22 As a result, disability inclusion is often missing from the prevention agenda before clinical services are even involved.

Access problems continue in screening and diagnosis.23 Access to clinics, examination rooms, toilets, transport routes and diagnostic equipment can delay or prevent care.24 Communication barriers can compromise history-taking, informed consent, risk communication and adherence counselling and can contribute to misdiagnosis. Some clinicians may incorrectly attribute new symptoms to an existing impairment or underestimate the need for routine screening, a pattern described as diagnostic overshadowing.24 In practice, this can mean later detection of hypertension, diabetes, breast or cervical cancer, chronic respiratory disease, depression or cardiovascular risk factors. For chronic diseases in which early diagnosis changes outcomes, these delays have serious consequences.

The treatment pathway is often equally fragmented. A PWD may be able to enter care but not sustain it. Long-term NCD management usually depends on repeated visits, medicine refills, dietary counselling, rehabilitation, monitoring and referral coordination.20 These are precisely the components most vulnerable to disruption by inaccessible transport, out-of-pocket costs, poorly designed appointment systems, inadequate provider communication and medicine stock-outs.25 For some patients, attending a clinic may require accompaniment, time away from work or transport that is physically possible only at high cost. Benefit packages for UHC schemes may not adequately cover rehabilitation, assistive products or repeated outpatient care. However, for many PWDs living with NCDs, these supports are not ancillary; they are central to function, adherence and continuity of care. The consequences are especially visible in conditions that require coordinated long-term management. In diabetes, exclusion may affect diagnosis, education, glucose monitoring, foot care and management of vision or mobility complications.26 In cardiovascular disease and hypertension, inaccessible routine monitoring and fragmented follow-up can undermine risk reduction and secondary prevention.27 In cancer care, barriers may arise in screening, pathology, surgery, access to radiotherapy, understanding of treatment and palliative care.28 In chronic respiratory disease, inaccessible housing, exposure to pollution, delayed diagnosis and poor continuity of inhaler treatment can worsen outcomes.29 Mental health warrants particular attention because psychosocial disability may coexist with physical NCD risks, while stigma, service separation and coercive care histories can further reduce access to person-centred chronic care.30

The policy gap is structural, not incidental

Despite increasing policy attention to NCDs across Africa, disability is still too often treated as a separate welfare or social protection issue rather than a core health systems concern. National NCD strategies in countries such as Kenya, Nigeria, South Africa, Ghana and Uganda commonly prioritise population-wide risk factors, including tobacco use, harmful alcohol use, unhealthy diets, physical inactivity, obesity, hypertension, diabetes and cancer screening.31 However, these strategies often use generic population language and provide limited operational guidance on disability-inclusive prevention, accessible screening, adapted health information, reasonable accommodation, disability-disaggregated monitoring, rehabilitation or continuity of care for people with functional limitations. Conversely, disability policies may emphasise rights, inclusion and social protection but remain weakly linked to health financing, NCD service delivery standards and chronic care models. The result is a fragmented policy landscape in which disability is acknowledged in principle but insufficiently embedded in mainstream NCD planning and accountability.

This disconnect sits uneasily with rights-based commitments. The CRPD establishes a clear normative basis for equal access to healthcare, non-discrimination, accessibility and participation.12 However, rights language alone does not produce operational change. Realising these rights requires an equity-driven translation of normative commitments into financing, facility standards, workforce competencies and disaggregated accountability. Absent this, the pledge of the CRPD12 and the Sustainable Development Goals (SDG)32 to ‘leave no one behind’ risks remaining rhetorical for PWDs living with NCDs in Africa. Framed this way, disability inclusion becomes a direct test of health equity: a measure of whether UHC reduces rather than reproduces avoidable and unfair differences in who can obtain effective chronic care. Inclusion requires translation into budgets, facility standards, communication protocols, workforce competencies, referral pathways, information systems and monitoring indicators. Where these mechanisms are absent, disability remains acknowledged in principle but neglected in practice.

Poor data on the intersection between disability and NCDs reinforce this invisibility. In many settings, routine NCD surveillance, health management information systems and facility data do not consistently capture disability status using comparable measures.33 Household surveys and administrative systems may also miss institutionalised populations, people with communication impairments or those excluded from formal service use. Without disability-disaggregated data on screening, diagnosis, treatment initiation, retention, complications, costs and patient experience, policymakers cannot easily identify inequities and ministries cannot be held accountable for reducing them.

Building disability-inclusive non-communicable disease systems in Africa

The policy response should not be the creation of parallel NCD programmes for PWDs. Rather, mainstream NCD systems should be redesigned so that inclusion is built into ordinary prevention and care. A review of selected African NCD strategies1 suggests that disability inclusion remains uneven: policies in countries such as Kenya, Nigeria, Ghana and Uganda commonly emphasise population-wide prevention, screening, treatment access and risk-factor reduction but provide limited detail on accessible services, reasonable accommodation, adapted health information, rehabilitation referral or disability-disaggregated monitoring. In some settings, including South Africa, broader commitments to disability rights, rehabilitation, community-based care and UHC offer important entry points, but these are not always systematically linked to NCD programme design and accountability.34 This suggests that the central problem is not simply the absence of disability from policy language but its weak operationalisation within mainstream NCD systems.

Encouragingly, African-led inclusion infrastructure already exists and can be built on rather than created anew. The African Network for Evidence-to-Action in Disability has convened researchers, organisations of PWDs and policymakers to translate disability evidence into action, while the African Community on Assistive Technology, hosted by the Centre for Disability and Rehabilitation Studies at Stellenbosch University under the WHO Global Cooperation on Assistive Technology, now links stakeholders across more than 20 African countries.35 The WHO Regional Framework for strengthening rehabilitation in the African region (2025–2035) provides a further continental scaffold, though African scholars caution that its pillars will fail without parallel investment in the rehabilitation workforce and professional education.36 These platforms offer ready foundations on which disability-inclusive NCD care can be constructed.

PHC is a critical starting point. As African countries strengthen PHC for chronic disease detection and management, disability inclusion should be embedded in service standards, training, outreach, referral protocols, accessible communication, flexible appointments, disability-aware risk assessment and continuity planning. These are requirements of good chronic care, not specialist extras.

Prevention must also be made accessible. Health promotion on diet, tobacco, alcohol, physical activity, obesity, hypertension, diabetes and cancer symptoms should be produced in formats that are usable by diverse populations and delivered through channels that reach those excluded from standard messaging. Community health worker programmes and outreach services could play an important role if they are trained and supported to include PWDs rather than bypass them. African experience already demonstrates the feasibility of such models: patient support-group approaches to diabetes and hypertension care in informal settlements in Nairobi, Kenya, have shown improvements in engagement and clinical monitoring under real-world, resource-constrained conditions, offering a transferable and adaptable template for disability-inclusive adaptation.37 Screening strategies for hypertension, diabetes, cervical cancer, breast cancer and other priority conditions should be reviewed for physical, communication and procedural accessibility.

Clinical care pathways require attention to infrastructure and practice. Facilities need accessible entrances, toilets, waiting areas and examination spaces. Equipment and protocols should accommodate patients with mobility, sensory, cognitive or communication impairments. Professional education should include disability competence, communication skills and awareness of diagnostic overshadowing. Referral systems should link NCD care with rehabilitation, mental health support, assistive technology provision and social services where relevant. In contexts of workforce shortages, these changes may seem ambitious, but many have a low-cost design and training improvements that also strengthen care quality for others. Concretely, ministries can begin with a defined implementation package: minimum accessibility standards for facilities and diagnostic equipment; reasonable-accommodation protocols; health information in sign language, easy-read, Braille and captioned formats; mandatory disability-competency and diagnostic-overshadowing modules in pre-service and in-service training; and standing referral links to rehabilitation and assistive-technology services. Because such measures are largely low-cost and system-strengthening, they are feasible even where budgets and workforces are constrained.36 38

Financing reforms are equally important. UHC commitments cannot be assessed only by nominal service availability; they must be judged by whether people can complete the care pathway without disproportionate hardship. For PWDs living with NCDs, repeated transport costs, assistive product needs, rehabilitation expenses and outpatient follow-up may be major determinants of access. Benefit package design should therefore consider continuity of care, not only acute service coverage. Ministries of health and financing agencies should also monitor whether disability-related exclusions are reproduced through payment systems, prior authorisation rules or fragmented procurement.

Finally, countries need better accountability. NCD targets and monitoring frameworks should include disability-disaggregated indicators wherever feasible. This includes service contact, screening coverage, treatment initiation, retention, access to medication, complications and patient-reported experience. A pragmatic starting set of implementation indicators, disability-disaggregated screening coverage, treatment initiation and retention rates, medication availability, complication incidence and patient-reported experience of accessibility would allow ministries to benchmark progress and be held accountable, consistent with established implementation-research outcome frameworks.38 Crucially, Organisations of PWDs should be involved in policy design, implementation review and accountability processes, not consulted only after plans are finalised. Their participation is essential to ensure that reforms respond to actual barriers rather than assumed needs. The principle of ‘Nothing about us without us’ should be upheld, recognising that meaningful involvement of PWDs is fundamental to developing effective, equitable, and responsive policies.

A more inclusive non-communicable disease agenda

The Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) commit to ‘leave no one behind’ that underpins the 2030 Agenda for Sustainable Development. SDG 3 (good health and well-being), SDG 10 (reduced inequalities) and SDG 17 (partnerships for the goals) all provide normative support for disability-inclusive health responses.32 Treating disability inclusion as integral to NCD policy is not only a health-system imperative; it is a means of translating the SDG equity framework into concrete, accountable action at the country level.

A disability-inclusive NCD agenda would strengthen African health systems beyond PWDs alone. Designing services around people who face the greatest barriers to care can improve access, quality and continuity for older adults, people with multimorbidity, rural populations, people living in poverty and others with complex care needs.3 4 19 23 Disability inclusion can make health communication more accessible, improve follow-up and treatment adherence, reveal hidden out-of-pocket costs, strengthen rehabilitation linkages and align NCD policy with the realities of long-term chronic illness.

As Africa’s population ages and NCD-related functional limitations increase, inclusion becomes both a rights obligation and a practical health-system strategy. This can be operationalised through accessibility standards, reasonable accommodation, disability-aware workforce training, inclusive health promotion, disability-disaggregated monitoring and referral pathways linking NCD care with rehabilitation, assistive technology and long-term support. It would help move UHC from a coverage promise to a measure of whether people can use services on fair and dignified terms.

The question, then, is not whether disability should be added to the NCD agenda as a special topic. The more important question is whether NCD strategies in Africa are prepared to confront the ways in which current systems are designed around an assumed standard patient. So long as disability remains administratively invisible and operationally marginal, NCD policy will continue to reproduce preventable inequities. Disability inclusion is not peripheral to the NCD agenda in Africa; it is a test of whether the region’s health systems can deliver equitable chronic care in practice.
