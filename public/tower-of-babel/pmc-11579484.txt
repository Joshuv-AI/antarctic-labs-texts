Dynamics of HPAM flow and injectivity in sandstone porous media
Authors: M S Mousapour, M Simjoo, M Chahardowli, B Shaker Shiran
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractPolymer flooding is a prominent chemical enhanced oil recovery (CEOR) method that involves the injection of polymer solution into the oil reservoirs to improve the sweep efficiency and maximize the ultimate oil recovery. Selecting an appropriate polymer type, molecular weight, and concentration is crucial for success of any polymer flooding project. This paper studies the flow behavior of HPAM-based EOR polymers with different molecular weights through porous media. Dynamic adsorption and injectivity tests were performed through 5 Darcies sandpacks using polymer solutions prepared with low (LM; 8–10 MDa) and high (HM; 20–25 MDa) molecular weight polymers. Polymer solutions with different target viscosity values of 7, 15 and 30 cP were flooded through sandpacks at the reservoir temperature of 80 ºC and pore pressure of 1000 psi. The results showed that HM solutions with different target viscosity have higher polymer retention through sandpacks compared to LM solutions. Furthermore, results of residual resistance factor (RRF) measurements were in line with dynamic adsorption tests results. That is, permeability reduction due to irreversible polymer retention was higher when HM polymer solutions were injected. Furthermore, the results showed that although the resistance factor (RF) and in-situ viscosity of HM and LM polymers are in the same range, however, shear thickening regime becomes pronounced in the case of HM polymer with higher target viscosity. Polymer relaxation time measurements, and consequently, Deborah number calculations were performed to describe the shear thickening behavior by polymers viscoelastic characteristics. Results demonstrate that occurrence of shear thickening regime is controlled by Deborah number which is a function of polymer molecular weight, polymer concentration and injection rate. These results shed light on the importance of the selection of optimal polymer molecular weight and concentration during the design of polymer flooding projects.Keywords: Flow in porous media, Enhanced oil recovery, Polymer flooding, Polymer adsorption, Polymer injectivity, Resistance factor, Deborah numberSubject terms: Chemical engineering, Energy science and technology, Engineering

Introduction

Due to the increasing trend of worldwide demand for energy sources such as crude oil, application of enhanced oil recovery (EOR) methods is indeed a key step to sustain the oil production level. Polymer flooding is one of the distinguished EOR methods that attracted attentions in recent years1. Due to the modification of sweep efficiency as a result of mobility ratio reduction, the oil recovery factor improves by polymer flooding over conventional waterflooding2,3.

Partially hydrolyzed polyacrylamide (HPAM) is the most widely used synthetic polymer in EOR projects4. Due to the sensitivity of HPAM to temperature, salinity and concentration of divalent cations such as Mg2+ and Ca2+ in make-up brine, application of HPAM-based co-polymers and ter-polymers was introduced by several research studies for high salinity/high temperature conditions5–8. Application of co-polymers and ter-polymers of HPAM and 2-Acrylamido-2-methylpropane sulfonic acid (AMPS), N-Vinylpyrrolidone (nVP) and acrylic acid polymers was reported for harsh reservoir conditions9–11.

Because of interactions between polymer molecules and reservoir rock, polymer retention in porous media through different mechanisms including adsorption, mechanical entrapment (straining) and hydrodynamic retention may occur during polymer flooding12,13. Polymer adsorption on the reservoir rock surface results in reduced effective polymer concentration at the injection bank, the delay in polymer propagation in the reservoir, and thereby dampening the polymer flooding efficiency14,15. Furthermore, irreversible polymer retention through porous media causes permeability reduction which affects reservoir productivity due to an increase in pressure drop over conventional waterflooding16,17. Therefore, evaluation of polymer retention through porous media is one of the key steps during the design of a polymer flooding project.

In addition to polymer retention through porous media, polymer injectivity is one of the pivotal parameters that should be assessed before the implementation of polymer flooding in a field18. As a result of the cooperative effect of porous media and HPAM molecular structure with typically high molecular weight and broad distribution of molecular weight, the polymer flow behavior through porous media is different from the one which is simply predicted by the rheometer. Unlike the bulk rheology (rheometer) which demonstrates only shear thinning flow, during HPAM flow through porous media shear thickening behavior might also occur ascribing to polymer viscoelasticity, especially at high velocities existing in near wellbore area19. The shear thickening behavior may result in mechanical degradation which increases the risk of losing polymer viscosity20.

Polymer flow parameters such as shear thickening behavior and retention/adsorption level control the flooding performance due to their effect on the viscosity of the injection bank, permeability reduction and pressure buildup over waterflooding. Therefore, the results of retention/adsorption tests and injectivity analysis could be considered conjointly during the evaluation of potential application of polymer flooding for a field. Researches have addressed the coupled analysis of adsorption and injectivity tests18,21–27. Based on the experiences of polymer flooding pilots/studies, both polymer adsorption and injectivity could be considered as the main screening parameters during the design of a polymer flooding project.

Polymer flow characteristics such as retention and shear thickening behavior may depend on the properties of porous media such as lithology, permeability, wettability and presence of oil28–30. Also, the physicochemical properties of polymer solution can affect the polymer flow through porous media. Polymer molecular weight is one of imperative parameters that controls the polymer flow behavior due to its effect on polymer retention and viscoelastic behavior31. Different research reported that an increase in polymer molecular weight results in higher adsorption32–35. However, the consequent effect of polymer adsorption on flooding performance such as pressure build up and permeability reduction was not addressed by these researches. Furthermore, several studies demonstrated that the polymer molecular weight affects polymer in-situ viscosity due to the dependency of polymer viscoelastic behavior on molecular weight30,36–39. Nevertheless, the relationship between polymer shear thickening behavior and polymer viscoelasticity, which is a function of polymer molecular weight, was not quantified in these papers.

A comprehensive review of the literature reveals that it is necessary to investigate the effect of polymer molecular weight on HPAM flow behavior in porous media through coupling rheology measurements, dynamic adsorption tests and injectivity analysis. Also, it is found that the effect of molecular weight on polymer viscoelastic characteristics, and its consequent effect on polymer shear thickening behavior has not been described. Therefore, this paper aims to extend the previous works referring to the effect of molecular weight on HPAM flow through sandstone porous media. To do this, HPAM solutions with different target viscosity values prepared from two polymers with high and low molecular weights were flooded through 5 Darcies sandpacks. The effect of molecular weight on polymer adsorption, and consequently, on permeability reduction was investigated. Polymer injectivity as a function of molecular weight was also studied, and the relationship between shear thickening behavior and polymer viscoelastic characteristics was addressed. Furthermore, polymer relaxation time measurements and Deborah number calculations were used to describe the shear thickening behavior during flow of polymers with different molecular weights in the porous media.

Polymer solution preparation

In order to prepare the polymer solution, filtered SW as the make-up brine was de-gassed under vacuum condition. Make-up brine was stirred by a magnetic stirrer, at a certain speed to provide a vortex in the container. Then the polymer powder was added gently on the side of the water vortex. Stirring at full vortex was continued until the polymer was well dispersed, and then, the stirring was continued at a lower speed to avoid any possibility of polymer mechanical degradation. To ensure complete polymer hydration, the solution was stirred continuously overnight. Thereafter, the stock polymer solution was diluted into the designed concentrations to reach the planned target viscosity values. The target viscosity of polymer solutions was designed based on the expected mobility ratio considering reservoir oil viscosity. Three target polymer viscosity were selected: 7 cP (low), 15 cP (intermediate) and 30 cP (high). Before performing rheology and coreflooding tests, polymer solutions were filtered through 5-micron cellulose acetate filter paper under pressurizing system (using nitrogen) to remove any probable agglomerates.

Polymer characterization

The Rheology of polymer solutions was measured using an Anton-Paar MCR 302 rheometer at 83 ºC. In this study, cone and plate geometry (CP 75 − 1; cone diameter = 75 mm, angle = 1º was used to perform rheology measurements. Also, in order to have minimum temperature tolerance during viscosity measurement, the Peltier system was used which circulates a hot fluid with a fixed temperature on the bottom of the test plate. Rheology of polymer solutions with different polymer concentrations was measured. Based on the viscosity of the solutions, the required polymer concentrations to reach target viscosity values of 7, 15 and 30 cP at the shear rate of 7 s−1 (expected shear rate in the reservoir) and at the temperature of 83 ºC were determined for both LM and HM polymers. Also, in order to determine relaxation time, a frequency sweep test was performed at the temperature of 83 ºC, and angular frequency in the range of 0.1 to 1000 rad/s.

Porous medium

In order to study the flow behavior of HPAM polymer solutions in a homogenous porous media, sandpacks were fabricated from sand sample of an unconsolidated sandstone reservoir. According to X-Ray Diffraction analysis (XRD), the sand sample mainly consisted of quartz (quartz: 99.8%, calcite: 0.2%). The mesh size of the sand sample was 50–250 microns. The sandpack length was 21 cm and the diameter was 3.81 cm. In order to prepare sandpack, the sand sample was washed with toluene and dried in an oven at 85 ºC. Then, the sand sample was poured into the rubber sleeve and was packed properly. The same packing procedure was followed through fabrication of all sandpacks to obtain similar and homogenous porous media with the target porosity and permeability in the range of 30 ± 2% and of 4.5 ± 0.2 Darcies, respectively.

Experimental procedure

In order to investigate the adsorption and injectivity of polymer solutions prepared from LM and HM polymers with low (7 cP), intermediate (15cP) and high (30 cP) target viscosity values, six coreflooding experiments were performed at 83 ºC and at 1000 psi. Figure 1 shows the schematic of coreflooding apparatus used in this research. The setup consists of an injection pump, 3 fluid accumulators for brine, tracer and polymer injection, an oven, a sandpack, a confining pressure unit, a series of differential pressure transmitters with different measurement ranges, a back pressure regulator and a sample collector. A data acquisition system was also used to monitor test temperature, pore pressure, differential pressure and confining pressure.

Schematic of coreflooding apparatus.

In each coreflooding test, the sandpack was vacuumed using a vacuum pump, and then saturated with SW. The porosity of the sandpack was calculated based on the weight difference between saturated and dry samples. The sandpack temperature and pore pressure were increased gradually up to 83 ºC and 1000 psi, respectively; and in each step, the difference between confining pressure and pore pressure was set to 10 bar. When the desired pressure and temperature were obtained, SW injection was continued to ensure the steady flow at the test condition. Then, SW was injected into the sandpack with different flow rates, and the stabilized pressure drop across the sandpack was measured. Absolute permeability of the sandpack was then obtained using Darcy’s law.

Dynamic adsorption tests procedure

In order to perform dynamic polymer adsorption test, three pore volumes of SW containing 100 mg/L of potassium iodide, as the tracer, with a flow rate of 0.8 mL/min was injected into the sandpack. The effluent was collected with a sampling volume of 4 cc. The tracer concentration in the effluent was then measured using UV-Vis method. In order to displace the tracer from the porous media, three pore volumes of SW were injected with the flow rate of 0.8 mL/min into the sandpack. Then, three pore volumes of the prepared polymer solution were injected into the sandpack with a flow rate of 0.8 mL/min, and liquid samples from the sandpack effluent were collected continuously for the UV-Vis analysis to determine polymer concentration in the effluent. The final polymer concentration was determined using bleach method. In this method, the polymer concentration should not be higher than 500 ppm. Therefore, the polymer solutions need to be diluted. In order to measure the polymer concentration of diluted solutions, an indicator solution containing acetic acid and sodium hypochlorite was firstly prepared. Then, a specific amount of the filtered and diluted polymer solution was weighed and was poured into a test tube containing a specific amount of indicator solution. The solutions were mixed by inverting the tube for several times; this causes a turbidity in the mixture. Thereafter, the transmittance of the mixture was measured at a wavelength of 470 nm using UV-spectrophotometer. Then, the polymer concentration of the diluted solution was determined using calibration curve that is obtained from the measurement of the transmittance of the 100, 200, 300, 400 and 500 ppm of polymer solutions at 470 nm.

Based on the UV-vis results, the plots of effluent concentration to the initial concentration (C/C0) versus pore volumes of injection were determined for both tracer and polymer injections. Subsequently, the area between the two plots was calculated and treated as the adsorbed polymer through the sandpack34.

Polymer in-situ rheology measurement and injectivity analysis procedures

Polymer injection was continued to measure in-situ viscosity and to examine the polymer injectivity. Resistance factor (RF) and residual resistance factor (RRF) were measured for both polymer types with different target viscosity values. To examine the polymer injectivity, stabilized pressure drop across the sandpack during polymer injection with a flow rate of 0.8 mL/min was measured. Then, polymer injection rate was increased in a stepwise approach in the range of 0.8 to 2 mL/min, and the stabilized differential pressure was measured in each step. The Canella equation (Eq. Bulk rheology measurements

Figure 2 shows the results of bulk rheology measurements for HM and LM polymer solutions with 2000, 3000 and 4000 mg/L concentration prepared in SW and measured by rheometer at reservoir temperature of 83º C. According to the results, as the shear rate increases from 1 to 100 s−1, the viscosity of HM and LM polymer solutions decreases i.e., shear thinning behavior. In the case of shear rates higher than 100 s−1, Newtonian behavior is observed for HM and LM polymer solutions. It means that the viscosity remains constant as the shear rate increases.

Dynamic adsorption tests

Dynamic adsorption tests were conducted to examine the polymer adsorption level in the sandpack for polymer solutions with different polymer molecular weights. Figure 4 shows the normalized concentration of tracer and polymer which is obtained by effluent analysis versus pore volume injected during dynamic adsorption tests for HM and LM polymer solutions with different target viscosity values. As the area between tracer and polymer plots reflects the polymer adsorption in the sandpacks, a visual investigation of the separation area between two curves reveals that the adsorption of HM solutions is higher than that of LM solutions for all target viscosity values studied in this research. Also, as the results indicate, the increase in polymer concentration resulted in more polymer adsorption in the sandpack for both HM and LM polymer solutions. Figure 5 presents the polymer adsorption as a function of target viscosity (polymer concentration) for both LM and HM solutions. Based on the results, for both HM and LM polymer solutions, polymer adsorption increases as the polymer concentration (viscosity) increases. This figure also shows that the polymer adsorption level tends to reach a plateau, that is, the polymer adsorption remains approximately constant with increase in polymer concentration. These results show that, for polymer samples investigated in this study, polymer adsorption in the sandpack follows Langmuir adsorption isotherm. According to Fig. 5, the polymer adsorption is a direct function of polymer molecular weight. The adsorption of HM polymer solution in the sandpack (70 mg polymer/gr rock) is more than two times higher than the adsorption of LM solution (30 mg polymer/gr rock) with the same target viscosity of 7 cP. This shows that the adsorption of polymer in the sandpack becomes tangibly higher as the polymer molecular weight increases. This is in line with the findings which were reported by previous papers34. Similarly, in the case of 15 and 30 cP viscosity, the ratio of HM adsorption to LM adsorption is about 1.5 times, that is, the difference in adsorption level of HM polymer solution compared to LM solution dampens with the increase in polymer concentration.

Injectivity tests

The injectivity tests were performed to study the effect of polymer molecular weight and concentration/viscosity on in-situ polymer rheology in the sandpack. Figure 6 shows differential pressure drop across the sandpack during polymer injection into the sandpack for both HM and LM injection with different target viscosity values. This figure consists of 3 parts: (i) 3 pore volumes of polymer injection during adsorption measurement with the flow rate of 0.8 mL/min; (ii) about 2 pore volumes of polymer injection with different flow rates in the range of 0.8 to 2 mL/min to calculate the resistance factor (RF) and in-situ rheology measurement; (iii) 20 pore volumes of polymer injection with the flow rate of 0.8 mL/min to investigate the long-term polymer injectivity. According to the data depicted in Fig. 6, no tangible increase in the pressure drop profile was observed during polymer injection over 25 pore volumes with different flow rates. This observation indicates good injectivity for both HM and LM polymers in the sandpack.

In part (ii), polymer solution was flooded through the sandpack with different flow rates in the range of 0.8 to 2 mL/min. Based on the results, for both HM and LM polymers, an increase in target viscosity and flow rate increased RF (i.e., differential pressure across the sandpack induced by polymer flooding). In the cases of lower target viscosity values, RF of HM and LM solutions was nearly similar. Furthermore, the results reveal that the difference between the RF of LM and HM becomes tangible in higher velocities (shear rates) and target viscosity values.

In order to have a better insight into the effect of polymer molecular weight on the polymer flow in porous media, Fig. 9 shows bulk and in-situ rheology for LM and HM polymer solutions with different target viscosity. According to the results, the difference between in-situ rheology of HM and LM solutions becomes sensible in higher target viscosity values, and especially in higher shear rates. In other words, shear thickening regime is observed in the case of the flow of HPAM in sandpack which is much pronounced in higher shear rates, higher polymer concentration and higher polymer molecular weights.

Based on the data, the shear thickening regime for 30 cP polymer solutions occurs in the lower ranges of shear rates compared to 7 cP polymer solutions. These findings could be described by polymer viscoelastic behavior. Residual resistance factor measurement

At the end of injectivity tests, SW was injected into the sandpack to displace the polymer and measure the permeability of the sandpack after polymer flood and estimate residual resistance factor (RRF). SW flooding was continued at different flow rates, and the differential pressure drop across the sandpack was measured. Figure 12 shows the differential pressure drop across the sandpack during SW flooding “before” and “after” polymer flooding. This figure shows that the ratio of differential pressure drop during “after” to “before” polymer flooding increases as the polymer target viscosity (concentration) increases for both HM and LM polymer solutions. Also, for all target viscosity values, this ratio for HM solutions is greater than that of LM solutions. The ratio of differential pressure drop during “after” to “before” polymer flooding reflects the residual resistance factor (RRF), and is depicted in Fig. 13-a which illustrates the RRF change for both HM and LM solutions at different target viscosities. According to the results, RRF of HM solutions is higher than that of LM solutions, and the difference becomes tangible for higher target viscosity values. For instance, RRF of HM solutions is higher than that of LM solutions for about 10, 13 and 18% for viscosity targets of 7, 15 and 30 cP, respectively. Based on differential pressure drop data, the permeability of the sandpack after polymer flooding was calculated. Figure 13-b presents the permeability reduction due to polymer flooding using HM and LM solutions. The results show that injection of HM solutions reduces the sandpack permeability more than LM solutions. The higher permeability reduction could be correlated to higher retention of HM solutions in porous media. These observations could be related to its longer molecular chain compared to LM solutions.

Discussion

Table 4 summarizes the main results of the tests/analysis presented in this paper. According to bulk rheology measurements, in order to prepare polymer solutions with the same target viscosity, higher concentration of LM polymer compared to HM polymer was needed. On the other hand, the results of dynamic adsorption tests showed that LM polymer solutions had lower adsorption levels in the sandpack compared to HM solutions. This finding is in line with the data which were reported in the previous researches34. According to the results, adsorption of polymer on rock surface could be reduced tangibly by selection of low molecular weight polymer.

Furthermore, the results showed that RRF of HM solutions was higher than that of LM solutions. RRF is an indication of permeability reduction as a consequence of polymer injection due to irreversible polymer retention in porous media28. Based on the findings, the permeability reduction due to polymer injection is sensible in the case of HM flooding. Therefore, using low-molecular weight polymer solution could be suggested to prevent higher irreversible permeability reduction as a consequence of polymer flooding.

The injectivity analysis revealed that RF (in-situ viscosity) of LM solutions was relatively in the same range as HM solutions. Therefore, considering the same range of in-situ viscosity, injection of LM solutions resulted in lower adsorption, and consequently, lower permeability reduction.

In EOR polymer flooding, mobility ratio is one of the key parameters which controls the production performance, and it is directly dependent on the viscosity of displacing phase2. Therefore, higher adsorption of high molecular weight polymer solution could affect the performance of polymer injection due to the loss of viscosity during flow through porous media. Also, considering higher polymer concentration to compensate for polymer adsorption increases the project costs sensibly. Hence, due to the direct effect on polymer adsorption and subsequently on flooding performance, and also, the tangible effect on permeability reduction, the optimal range of polymer molecular weight should be selected during the design of polymer flooding project. Additionally, due to the effect of molecular weight on polymer concentration needed to obtain the desired viscosity, economic aspects arising from the selection of polymer molecular weight should be considered besides polymer flow behavior through porous media.

Conclusion

In this paper, the results of adsorption tests and injectivity analysis were coupled to address the effect of polymer molecular weight on the flow behavior of EOR polymers through porous media. To do so, two HPAM-based polymers with different molecular weights were used: LM (low molecular weight; 8–10 MDa) and HM (high molecular weight; 15–20 MDa). Six coreflooding tests were performed at reservoir temperature of 83 ºC using sandpacks through which LM and HM polymer solutions with target viscosity of 7, 15 and 30 cP were flooded. The main finding could be listed as below:

According to the adsorption measurements results, adsorption of HM solutions were 1.5-2 times higher than that of LM solutions for all target viscosity values. Higher polymer adsorption results in loss of viscosity during polymer flow through porous media which affects polymer flooding efficiency.

However, the difference between in-situ viscosity of LM and HM solutions became tangible in higher interstitial velocities where the shear thickening regime becomes pronounced. According to the results, considering the same range of in-situ viscosity that is obtained by LM and HM solutions, selection of polymer with lower molecular weight may result in better flooding efficiency due to its lower adsorption, good injectivity and lower permeability reduction.
