Measuring Ransomware Lateral Movement Susceptibility via Privilege-Weighted Adjacency Matrix Exponentiation
Satyam Tyagi

Abstract




Abstract:

Ransomware impact hinges on how easily an intruder can move laterally and spread to the maximum number of assets.
We present a graph-theoretic method to measure lateral-movement susceptibility and estimate blast radius.
We build a directed multigraph where vertices represent assets and edges represent reachable services (e.g., RDP/SSH)
between them. We model lateral movement as a probabilistic process using a pivot potential factor
\pi(s)

for each service. This allows us to iteratively compute a
K
-hop compromise probability matrix that captures
how compromise propagates through the network. Metrics derived from this model include: (1) Lateral-Movement
Susceptibility (LMS
K
): the average probability of a successful lateral movement between any two assets (0-1 scale); and (2)
Blast-Radius Estimate (BRE
K
): the expected percentage of assets compromised in an average attack scenario.
Interactive control (SSH 22, RDP 3389) gets higher
\pi(s)
than app-only ports (MySQL 3306, MSSQL 1433),
which seldom enable pivoting without an RCE. Across anonymized enterprise snapshots, pruning high-
\pi(s)

edges yields the largest LMS
K
/BRE
K
drop, aligning with CISA guidance, MITRE ATT&CK (TA0008: Lateral Movement),
and NIST SP 800-207. The framework evaluates (micro)segmentation and helps prioritize controls that reduce
lateral movement susceptibility and shrink blast radius.

Keywords:

adjacency matrix; multi-hop reachability; privilege weighting; lateral movement; microsegmentation; blast radius; ransomware; Zero Trust

05C50; 05C90; 94C15

1.  Introduction

Ransomware impact is determined less by where an adversary first lands and more by how easily they can
move laterally
to reach high-value assets and propagate encryption. Classic reachability (Boolean adjacency,
k
-hop closure) reveals where paths exist, but it can
overstate
risk by treating an SSH or RDP foothold as equivalent to a low-privilege application socket. In practice, interactive remote services (e.g., RDP/SSH) enable immediate pivoting; app-only ports typically demand additional exploits. Our goal is a probability-weighted alternative that measures the
ease of movement
—and the expected blast radius—using only topology plus coarse service-class information.

We introduce a privilege- and account-aware adjacency model that composes per-service “pivot potentials”
\pi(s)\!\in\![0,1]
with multiplicative step success and a
noisy-OR
union across alternative routes. Iterating this algebraic path closure yields (i) a
k
-hop (or
\varepsilon
-fixed-point)
compromise probability matrix
and (ii) two deployment metrics:
LMS
K
(lateral-movement susceptibility; mean off-diagonal) and
BRE
K
(blast-radius estimate; mean including the diagonal).

CVE-free lateral movement via valid accounts.

Once an adversary holds
valid credentials
and an
interactive remote service
is exposed (RDP/SSH), lateral movement does
not
require unpatched vulnerabilities: adversaries log into remote services and act as the authenticated user
[
MITRE 2025f
;
MITRE 2025b
;
MITRE 2025c
]
. Patch status is orthogonal to pivotability when privileged landing is possible; hence our operators weight
service exposure and privilege class
, not CVE likelihoods.

2.  Background and Related Work

Account-based pivots vs. vulnerability-driven traversal.

Beyond exploit-based movement (e.g., T1210)
[
MITRE 2025d
]
, ATT&CK catalogs techniques enabling lateral movement without software flaws:
Valid Accounts
(T1078) and
Remote Services
(T1021, including RDP/SSH) describe abusing legitimate credentials to authenticate and operate on remote hosts
[
MITRE 2025f
;
MITRE 2025b
;
MITRE 2025c
;
MITRE 2025e
]
. Our formulation treats interactive services as
high-pivot
edges when paired with account access, independent of CVE posture.

Attack graphs and probabilistic security metrics.

Classical attack graphs model preconditions/exploits and enumerate paths to goals
[
Phillips and Swiler 1998
;
Sheyner et al. 2002
]
. Later work augments graphs with probabilities and costs. We deliberately
do not
encode per-CVE logic; instead we treat reachable services as coarse pivot opportunities whose probabilities compose multiplicatively with noisy-OR unions, trading granularity for deployability on routine network snapshots.

Algebraic path problems and semiring views.

Many path computations admit uniform treatment via algebraic path frameworks replacing
(+,\times)
by problem-specific
(\oplus,\otimes)
. We adopt multiplication for per-step success and probabilistic sum (noisy-OR) for unions, yielding a monotone iteration toward a least fixed point; this aligns with semiring-style closures, though our
\oplus
is non-idempotent
[
Mohri 2002
;
Sanmartín and Naranjo 2022
]
.

Noisy-OR and causal independence.

Our union operator is the standard noisy-OR from probabilistic graphical models, aggregating independent causal mechanisms with low elicitation burden
[
Heckerman 1994
]
. Generalizations (e.g., Noisy-MAX) clarify when such factorizations are appropriate.

Epidemic spreading analogies.

Multiplicative transmission along edges with union across parallel paths is mathematically analogous to network contagion; classic results connect topology to spread
[
Newman 2002
]
. Our aim is
risk measurement
(expected reach/blast), not epidemic prediction, but the analogy explains the outsized effect of a small set of high-pivot edges.

AD attack-path tooling.

In enterprise Windows domains, BloodHound operationalizes graph-based lateral-movement analysis at scale
[
SpecterOps 2025
]
. We follow similar instincts (directed, typed edges) but compute a
probability-weighted
closure and provide system-level metrics for control evaluation.

Zero Trust and ransomware guidance.

NIST SP 800-207 sets the architectural principle; ransomware advisories emphasize limiting RDP/remote services to reduce both initial access and lateral movement
[
Rose et al. 2020
;
CISA 2025a
;
CISA 2025b
;
CISA 2025c
]
.

2.1
Graph-theoretic underpinnings

k
-hop walks via adjacency powers.

Let
A
be the (directed) adjacency matrix of the network. Classical algebraic graph theory shows that
(A^{k})_{ij}
equals the number of
i{\to}j

walks
of length
k
.
1
1

1

As usual, walks may repeat vertices; paths are walks with no repeated vertices. Our probabilistic closure works with walks.
See, e.g.,
Godsil and Royle 2001
;
Biggs 1993
;
Diestel 2005
. This formalizes the “
k
-hop” notion we use when bounding series
A+AW+\cdots+AW^{K-1}
.

Boolean-OR reachability and transitive closure.

Over the Boolean semiring
(\lor,\land)
, the transitive closure (reachability) can be computed by Boolean matrix powering and union,

R\;=\;A\lor A^{(2)}\lor\cdots\lor A^{(n)}\,,

or equivalently via Warshall’s dynamic program for transitive closure
[
Warshall 1962
]
. From a more algebraic perspective, Kleene-style closure over idempotent semirings and dioids gives a unifying view of reachability and shortest paths
[
Kozen 1994
;
Gondran and Minoux 2008
]
.

Noisy-OR style union for probabilistic graphs.

When parallel, conditionally independent mechanisms can each enable a step (e.g., multiple services/credentials offering a pivot), the probability that
at least one
succeeds is the
probabilistic sum

p_{\text{union}}\;=\;1-\prod_{\ell}(1-p_{\ell})\,,

the same aggregation used in network reliability for parallel components
[
Colbourn 1987
]
. In probabilistic graphical models, this is the standard
noisy-OR
(and its generalizations), which captures causal independence with one parameter per parent
[
Pearl 1988
;
Heckerman 1994
;
Diez 1993
]
. Our use of a noisy-OR union along with multiplicative step success places the model within these classical frameworks.

3.  The Proposed Model

Our model quantifies lateral movement exposure by representing the network as a directed graph and simulating the propagation of compromise probability. This requires two foundational matrices derived from network state and security policies.

3.1
Foundational Matrices

1.

The Adjacency Matrix (
A
):

This is an
n\times n
binary matrix representing the network’s underlying structure, where
n
is the number of assets. An edge exists only if a service is both permitted by network policy and actively listening on the destination asset.

A_{ij}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if a reachable service exists from asset }i\text{ to asset }j\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

We take
A_{ii}=0
for all
i
(no self-loops). Let
I
denote the
n\times n
identity matrix.

2.

The Pivot Potential Matrix (
W
):

This is an
n\times n
matrix that encodes the inherent pivot potential of each edge. Instead of considering only the single most powerful service, the model treats each reachable service on an edge as an independent opportunity for an attacker. The entry
(W)_{ij}
represents the total probability of a successful pivot from asset
i
to
j
, calculated as the probabilistic union (or ”noisy-OR”) of the pivot potentials of all services available on that edge.

Let
S_{ij}
be the set of all services reachable from asset
i
to asset
j
. The pivot potential is calculated as:

W_{ij}=1-\prod_{s\in S_{ij}}(1-\pi(s))

Convention.

S_{ij}
includes only services that are (i) allowed by policy
and (ii) observed listening on
j
; hence
W_{ij}>0\Rightarrow A_{ij}=1
(i.e.,
W\leq A

elementwise). In particular, if
S_{ij}=\varnothing
then
W_{ij}=0
.

3.2
Computing Reachability (Unweighted Method)

Before detailing our probabilistic model, we first describe the standard, unweighted method for calculating K-hop reachability. This method answers the binary question: ”Does a path of length
K
or less exist?”
This is computed iteratively. Let
S_{k}
be the matrix where
(S_{k})_{ij}=1
if a path of at most
k
hops exists.

•

Base Case:
The 1-hop reachability matrix is the adjacency matrix,
S_{1}=A
.

•

Iteration:
The reachability matrix for at most
k
hops,
S_{k}
, is found by taking the previous state
S_{k-1}
and adding any new paths formed by one additional hop:

S_{k}=S_{k-1}\lor(S_{k-1}\cdot_{bool}A)

•

Final Result:
The result is the matrix
S_{K}
after
K-1
iterations.

While this method confirms path existence, it treats a high-risk path (e.g., via SSH) as identical to a low-risk one (e.g., via a database port), which our probabilistic model addresses.
Let
S_{1}=I\lor A
and
S_{k+1}=S_{k}\lor(S_{k}A)
; write
S_{K}
for the
K
-hop boolean reachability matrix.

3.3
Computing Compromise Probability (Probabilistic Method)

Our model calculates the
K
-hop compromise probability matrix,
P_{K}
, where
(P_{K})_{ij}
is the total probability that an attacker starting at asset
i
can compromise asset
j
in at most
K
hops. We define a specialized matrix exponentiation using two custom operators.

1.

The Probabilistic Composition Product (
\otimes
):
This operator, replacing matrix multiplication, calculates the probability of new compromise paths forming by propagating existing compromise probabilities through the pivot potentials in
W
. For matrices
X
and
Y
, the product
Z=X\otimes Y
is:

Z_{ij}=(X\otimes Y)_{ij}=1-\prod_{m=1}^{n}(1-X_{im}\times Y_{mj})

(1)

2.

The Probabilistic Union Operator (
\lor_{p}
):
This operator, replacing matrix addition, correctly combines probabilities from independent sets of paths using the union rule. For matrices
X
and
Y
, the union
Z=X\lor_{p}Y
is:

Z_{ij}=(X\lor_{p}Y)_{ij}=1-(1-X_{ij})(1-Y_{ij})

(2)

Stopping rule.
Iterate until
\|P_{k+1}-P_{k}\|_{\infty}<\varepsilon
or
k=K
.

Cost.
Each iteration is
O(n^{3})
and GPU-friendly (dense ops).

Independence Assumption

It is important to note that the probabilistic composition operator (
\otimes
) implicitly treats paths through different intermediate assets (
m
) as probabilistically independent events. This is a standard assumption in many network models that makes the problem computationally tractable. We acknowledge that in some real-world scenarios, such as an attack using a single stolen credential, these paths may not be fully independent.

Conservative bound.
For correlated routes, we can replace
1-\prod_{m}(1-X_{im}Y_{mj})
with
\max_{m}(X_{im}Y_{mj})
(lower bound).

Using these operators, we compute
P_{K}
via the following iterative algorithm:

Step 1: Initialization (Base Case)

The 1-hop compromise probability matrix,
P_{1}
, is the adjacency matrix
A
with a diagonal of 1s, as an asset that is the starting point of an attack is by definition compromised.

Step 2: Iteration

For each hop from
k=1
to
K-1
, we calculate the next state,
P_{k+1}
. The new state is the probabilistic union of the current state,
P_{k}
, with all the new compromise paths that can be formed by one additional hop.

P_{k+1}=P_{k}\lor_{p}(P_{k}\otimes W)

Step 3: Final Result

After
K-1
iterations, the resulting matrix is the final K-hop Compromise Probability Matrix,
P_{K}
.

Hop-1 semantics.
We treat the first hop as pure network reachability:
P_{1}=I\lor_{p}A
. Pivot potentials in
W
affect only subsequent hops (
k\geq 2
) via
P_{k}\otimes W
.

4.  Quantifying Susceptibility and Blast Radius

The final K-hop Compromise Probability Matrix,
P_{K}
, provides a detailed, asset-to-asset view of lateral movement exposure. To make this data actionable, we derive two high-level metrics: Lateral-Movement Susceptibility (LMS
K
) and Blast-Radius Estimate (BRE
K
).

4.1
Lateral-Movement Susceptibility (LMS
K
)

Concept:
This metric quantifies the overall ease of movement within the network. It answers the question: ”If we randomly select two different assets, what is the average probability that a successful lateral movement event can occur between them within
K
hops?”

Formal Definition:

The Lateral-Movement Susceptibility is the mean of all non-diagonal entries in the final compromise probability matrix,
P_{K}
. This focus on non-diagonal elements ensures the metric measures the probability of movement *between* distinct assets.

\text{LMS}_{K}=\frac{1}{n(n-1)}\sum_{i\neq j}(P_{K})_{ij}

(3)

where
n
is the total number of assets.

Interpretation:

LMS
K
is a score between 0 and 1. A score near 0 indicates a well-segmented network where lateral movement is highly improbable. A score near 1 indicates a ”flat” network where assets are highly entangled and movement is easy. This metric is ideal for tracking the network’s security posture over time and measuring the effectiveness of segmentation controls.

4.2
Blast-Radius Estimate (BRE
K
)

Concept:
This metric quantifies the expected scope of compromise from an attack. It answers the business-critical question: ”Assuming an attacker can start at any asset, what percentage of our entire network do we expect to be compromised?”

Formal Definition:

The Blast-Radius Estimate is the mean of *all* entries in the final compromise probability matrix,
P_{K}
, expressed as a percentage. By including the diagonal elements (which are always 1), this metric correctly incorporates the initially compromised asset as part of the total compromise.

\text{BRE}_{K}=\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(P_{K})_{ij}\right)\times 100\%

(4)

Interpretation:

BRE
K
provides a direct estimate of the potential scope of a breach. A BRE
K
of 30% signifies that, in an average-case attack scenario, 30% of the total network assets are expected to be compromised. This metric is designed to communicate exposure in clear, non-technical terms, helping to justify security investments and prioritize hardening efforts on the assets that contribute most to the overall blast radius.

5.  Formal Properties of the Model

Overview.
We prove monotonicity, boundedness, and convergence; derive a
K
-hop series upper bound and a single-path product lower bound; recover boolean reachability in the unweighted limit; characterize the limit as the least fixed point
P^{*}=F(P)=P\oplus(P\otimes W)
; and show all results persist under service-class lifting.

5.1
Monotonicity

The iterative process is monotonically non-decreasing. For any step
k\geq 1
, every element in the probability matrix
P_{k+1}
is greater than or equal to its corresponding element in
P_{k}
.

\forall i,j:(P_{k+1})_{ij}\geq(P_{k})_{ij}

This is guaranteed by the definition of the probabilistic union operator (
\lor_{p}
). This property ensures the model behaves logically, as the total probability of compromise only ever increases or remains constant as more paths are considered.

5.2
Boundedness

The values in the compromise probability matrix are always bounded within the interval
[0,1]
. The pivot potentials
\pi(s)
are defined in
[0,1]
, and both the probabilistic composition (
\otimes
) and union (
\lor_{p}
) operators are closed over this interval. This property guarantees the stability of the model, ensuring that the outputs are always valid probabilities.

5.3
Convergence

The sequence of matrices
P_{1},P_{2},\dots
is guaranteed to converge to a fixed-point matrix
P^{*}
, where further iterations no longer change the result. This is a direct consequence of the Monotonicity and Boundedness properties (a monotonically increasing sequence that is bounded from above must converge).

Unlike unweighted reachability, which converges in at most
n-1
steps, the probabilistic model may require more iterations for the probability values to become fully stable. This is because paths longer than
n-1
can still contribute a small but non-zero probability to the total, incrementally refining the final result. In practice, the algorithm is governed by a stopping rule: iterate until the change between successive matrices falls below a predefined tolerance threshold,
\varepsilon
, or a maximum number of hops
K
is reached. Formally, the iteration stops when
\|P_{k+1}-P_{k}\|_{\infty}<\varepsilon
or
k=K
.

By continuity of
\oplus
and
\otimes
on
[0,1]
, the limit
P^{*}
satisfies
P^{*}=F(P^{*})
with
F(P)=P\oplus(P\otimes W)
; thus
P^{*}
is the least fixed point above
P_{1}
.

5.4
Refinement of Binary Reachability

This is the most critical property of our model. It establishes a formal link to the unweighted model and demonstrates the value of the probabilistic approach.

Let
S_{K}
be the final reachability matrix from the unweighted (Boolean) method, and
P_{K}
be the final matrix from our probabilistic method. The following
soundness
relationship holds:

(P_{K})_{ij}>0\;\Rightarrow\;(S_{K})_{ij}=1

Consequence (off-diagonal).
For all
i\neq j
,

0\leq(P_{K})_{ij}\leq(S_{K})_{ij}.

Thus, Boolean reachability upper-bounds connectivity, while our probabilistic model provides a probability-weighted refinement (typically strictly smaller).

However, the unweighted model is a binary, all-or-nothing assessment. All reachable nodes are treated as equal. Our probabilistic model provides a continuous refinement of this binary view. It assigns a meaningful probability to each path, allowing us to differentiate between:

•

High-probability paths:
A small number of connections that represent a clear and present danger.

•

Low-probability paths:
A large number of theoretical connections that are practically irrelevant for an attacker.

This property of probabilistic refinement is what allows the model to effectively ”reduce” the set of relevant reachable nodes. By focusing only on paths above a meaningful probability threshold (e.g.,
P_{ij}>0.1
), a security team can prioritize actions and ignore the noise of low-probability connections that would dominate a standard reachability analysis. This directly demonstrates the model’s utility in reducing complexity and focusing on what matters.

5.5
Connection to Algebraic Path Problems

Our construction follows the algebraic-path template over
[0,1]
using the probabilistic-sum (noisy-OR) as the “addition”
\oplus
and ordinary multiplication as the “multiplication”
\otimes
:

x\oplus y\;=\;1-(1-x)(1-y),\qquad x\otimes y\;=\;xy.

Matrix composition then uses these operations in the usual way:

(X\otimes Y)_{ij}\;=\;\bigoplus_{m=1}^{n}X_{im}\otimes Y_{mj}\;=\;1-\prod_{m=1}^{n}\bigl(1-X_{im}Y_{mj}\bigr),

which is exactly our probabilistic composition operator. (Here
\oplus
corresponds
to the
\lor_{p}
operator used elsewhere.)

Remark (semiring-like, non-distributive).

The pair
(\oplus,\otimes)
is associative, commutative, and monotone with identities

0
(for
\oplus
) and
1
(for
\otimes
), but it is
not
distributive. For all

a,b,c\in[0,1]
,

(a\otimes b)\,\oplus\,(a\otimes c)\ \geq\ a\otimes(b\oplus c),\qquad(a\otimes c)\,\oplus\,(b\otimes c)\ \geq\ (a\oplus b)\otimes c,

with equality iff, respectively,
a\in\{0,1\}
or
bc=0
, and
c\in\{0,1\}
or
ab=0
.
We do not rely on distributivity in our results.

Fixed-point view.

Let
F(P)=P\oplus(P\otimes W)
. Since
F
is monotone on the complete lattice

[0,1]^{n\times n}
and bounded in
[0,1]
, iterating

P_{k+1}\;=\;P_{k}\oplus(P_{k}\otimes W)

computes the path-closure (least fixed point) up to either a prescribed hop limit
K

or an
\varepsilon
-tolerance, grounding our iteration in standard algebraic-path theory.

5.6
K-hop Series Bound for Lateral Propagation

Define, for
K\geq 1
, the path-sum matrix

U_{K}\;=\;A\;+\;AW\;+\;AW^{2}\;+\;\cdots\;+\;AW^{K-1},

where products are the usual matrix products. The term
AW^{t}
aggregates all
paths of length
t{+}1
(one landing hop in
A
followed by
t
pivot steps in
W
).

Proposition 1 (K-hop series upper bound).

For all
K\geq 1
and all
i\neq j
,

(P_{K})_{ij}\;\leq\;\min\!\bigl(1,\ (U_{K})_{ij}\bigr),\qquad\text{and}\qquad(P_{K})_{ii}=1.

Proof.

Fix
i\neq j
. Let
\mathcal{P}_{\leq K}(i\!\to\!j)
be the set of all directed paths
from
i
to
j
of length at most
K
. By construction,
(P_{K})_{ij}
is the probability
of the union of the path events
\{E_{p}\}_{p\in\mathcal{P}_{\leq K}}
. By Boole’s union
inequality,
\Pr(\bigcup_{p}E_{p})\leq\sum_{p}\Pr(E_{p})
. For a path of length
L\geq 1

with vertices
i\to m_{1}\to\cdots\to m_{L-1}\to j
, the model assigns probability

A_{im_{1}}\prod_{r=1}^{L-1}W_{m_{r}m_{r+1}}
, so summing over all such paths of fixed
length
L
yields
(AW^{L-1})_{ij}
. Summing over
L=1,\dots,K
gives
(U_{K})_{ij}
.
Capping at
1
preserves validity. The diagonal equality
(P_{K})_{ii}=1
follows from
the initialization
P_{1}=I\lor_{p}A
.
\square

Corollary 1 (infinite-hops bound).

If the spectral radius
\rho(W)<1
, then the Neumann series converges and

U_{\infty}\;:=\;\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}AW^{t}\;=\;A(I-W)^{-1},\qquad\text{hence}\qquad(P_{\infty})_{ij}\;\leq\;\min\!\bigl(1,\ (U_{\infty})_{ij}\bigr).

Proof.

Since
\rho(W)<1
,
\sum_{t\geq 0}W^{t}=(I-W)^{-1}
converges. Apply Proposition 1
with
K\to\infty
and linearity of matrix multiplication.
\square

Proposition 2 (single-path lower bound).

Fix
K\geq L\geq 1
. Suppose there exists a directed path

p:\ i\!=\!m_{0}\to m_{1}\to\cdots\to m_{L-1}\to m_{L}\!=\!j

in
A
of length
L
, and let the pivot weights on its
L-1
pivot steps be

w_{r}:=W_{m_{r}m_{r+1}}\in[0,1]
for
r=1,\dots,L-1
. Then

(P_{K})_{ij}\;\geq\;\prod_{r=1}^{L-1}w_{r}.

Proof.

P_{K}(i,j)
is the probability of a union over all
\leq K
-hop paths; it is at least
the probability of any particular path event. Under the model’s hop-1 semantics
(
A
gives certainty on the first hop), the path
p
succeeds with probability

\prod_{r=1}^{L-1}w_{r}
.
\square

Corollary 2 (uniform privileged pivots).

Under the conditions of Proposition 2, if every pivot step on
p
satisfies

w_{r}\geq p
for some
p\in[0,1]
, then

(P_{K})_{ij}\;\geq\;p^{\,L-1}.

Proof.
By monotonicity of products,

\prod_{r=1}^{L-1}w_{r}\geq\prod_{r=1}^{L-1}p=p^{L-1}
; combine with Proposition 2.
\square

5.7
Service-Class Lifting (Exact Land-then-Pivot Semantics)

Not all footholds are equal: landing via an interactive remote-service (e.g., RDP/SSH) typically enables immediate lateral movement, whereas landing via application protocols (e.g., SQL/HTTP) rarely does without an exploit. To encode this
service memory
exactly, we lift states from nodes to (node, class) pairs.

Classes and per-class adjacency.

Partition services into a small set of classes
\mathcal{C}
(e.g.,
\{
interactive, app-only
\}
). For each class
c\in\mathcal{C}
, let
A^{(c)}\in\{0,1\}^{n\times n}
be the adjacency restricted to class
c
. The total adjacency is the boolean union

A\;=\;\bigvee_{c\in\mathcal{C}}A^{(c)},\quad\text{with }A_{ii}=0.

Let
\pi_{\mathrm{pivot}}(c)\in[0,1]
denote the pivot probability conferred by a foothold acquired via class
c
.

Lifted transition.

A lifted state is
(j,c)
meaning “at node
j
with foothold class
c
.” From
(j,c)
one can move to
(\ell,c^{\prime})
if and only if
A^{(c^{\prime})}_{j\ell}=1
, and the step succeeds with probability
\pi_{\mathrm{pivot}}(c)
. This defines the class-conditioned transition matrix
T\in[0,1]^{(n|\mathcal{C}|)\times(n|\mathcal{C}|)}
with blocks

T_{(j,c)\to(\ell,c^{\prime})}\;=\;\pi_{\mathrm{pivot}}(c)\,\mathbf{1}\{A^{(c^{\prime})}_{j\ell}=1\}.

Initialization (hop 1: pure reach).

From a start node
i
, the first hop ignores pivot and only records the landing class:

P^{\mathrm{lift}}_{1}\big(i\to(j,c)\big)\;=\;A^{(c)}_{ij},\qquad P^{\mathrm{node}}_{1}(i\to i)=1.

Iteration (hops
\geq 2
).

For
k\geq 1
, propagate in the lifted space using the same operators as in Section
3.3
:

P^{\mathrm{lift}}_{k+1}\;=\;P^{\mathrm{lift}}_{k}\;\lor_{p}\;\big(P^{\mathrm{lift}}_{k}\;\otimes\;T\big).

Aggregation back to nodes.

The
K
-hop node-to-node compromise probability is obtained by class-wise probabilistic union at the destination:

(P_{K})_{i\ell}\;=\;\bigl(\;\lor_{p}\;\{\,P^{\mathrm{lift}}_{K}(i\to(\ell,c))\ :\ c\in\mathcal{C}\,\}\bigr).

All metrics (LMS
K
, BRE
K
) continue to use
P_{K}
exactly as defined here.

Compatibility with the base model.

If classes are collapsed by setting
W_{j\ell}=1-\prod_{c\in\mathcal{C}}\bigl(1-\pi_{\mathrm{pivot}}(c)\,\mathbf{1}\{A^{(c)}_{j\ell}=1\}\bigr)
, the lifted model reduces to the node-level model. Conversely, the lifted construction preserves hop-1 semantics and makes the dependence on the landing class explicit at every subsequent hop.

Theorems unchanged.

Proposition 1 (K-hop series upper bound) and Proposition 2 (single-path lower bound), and their corollaries, hold verbatim under this lifting. In Proposition 2 the per-step weights become the class-conditioned values
w_{r}=\pi_{\mathrm{pivot}}(c_{r})
determined by the foothold class present at the source of step
r
. In Proposition 1, the path-sum bound is obtained by summing over class-typed paths; the resulting bound is still computable via ordinary matrix products by replacing
A
with the block structure induced by
\{A^{(c)}\}
and
W
with
T
.

5.8
Computational Cost

Each iteration of the algorithm is dominated by the probabilistic composition product (
\otimes
), which has a computational complexity of
O(n^{3})
, equivalent to standard dense matrix multiplication. The operations involved (primarily multiplications and additions) are highly parallelizable and operate on dense matrices. This makes the algorithm well-suited for hardware acceleration on platforms such as GPUs, ensuring its practical applicability to real-world networks of significant size.

6.2
Evidence of Practical Utility

We now provide evidence of the model’s value in a more complex scenario where the goal is to prioritize security controls. Consider a network with four assets: a User Workstation (Asset 1), a Web Server (Asset 2), a critical Database (Asset 3), and a Domain Controller (Asset 4).

Workstation (1)

Analysis with the Unweighted Method:

A standard reachability analysis (using the Boolean method) would identify two lateral movement paths originating from the Workstation: one to the Database (length 2) and one to the Domain Controller (length 1). Both are considered ”reachable.” A security team might mistakenly prioritize the longer path involving the Web Server, or treat both paths as equally important, failing to see the true concentration of exposure.

Analysis with Our Probabilistic Model:

Our model calculates the compromise probabilities. The 2-hop compromise matrix

•

•

The probability of compromising the Domain Controller,
(P_{2})_{14}

The BRE from the Workstation would be heavily weighted by the near-certain compromise of the Domain Controller.
