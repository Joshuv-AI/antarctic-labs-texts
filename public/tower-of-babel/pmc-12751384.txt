Vaccination coverage, immune responses, and clinical characteristics in children with PFAPA syndrome: a monocentric prospective study
Authors: Emilie Sallansonnet, Renato Gualtieri, Henri Margot, Manel Mejbri, Klara Posfay-Barbe, Geraldine Blanchard-Rohner
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPFAPA syndrome (Periodic Fever, Aphthous Stomatitis, Pharyngitis, and Adenitis) is the most common periodic fever condition in children. Vaccination in this population can be challenging due to concerns about immune hyperreactivity, the potential to trigger febrile episodes, and uncertainty regarding vaccine efficacy. Data on immunization coverage and vaccine response in PFAPA remain limited. This study aimed to describe the clinical characteristics of children with PFAPA, with a particular focus on vaccination coverage and serological responses.MethodsWe conducted a prospective study of children with PFAPA followed at Geneva University Hospitals between February 2022 and April 2025. Clinical, laboratory, and genetic data were collected, along with vaccination status according to the Swiss national immunization schedule and serological results for major vaccine antigens.ResultsForty-one patients were included. All exhibited elevated inflammatory markers during febrile episodes, and none had a monogenic cause identified. Vaccination coverage was high, with 90–95% of patients receiving the primary DTaP-IPV-Hib-HBV series on time. Minor delays were observed for the third pneumococcal and second MMR doses. Meningococcal (MenC/MenACWY) vaccination was administered on time in 58% of patients. Serological testing confirmed protective antibody levels for most antigens, including diphtheria, tetanus, and measles, but only 40% achieved protective pneumococcal titers. Only 65% showed varicella immunity because of past infection, as none had received the vaccine, which was only introduced in Switzerland in 2023.ConclusionChildren with PFAPA demonstrate high adherence to vaccination schedules and adequate immune responses, supporting the safety and effectiveness of routine immunization in this group. The reduced pneumococcal seroprotection suggests that monitoring antibody levels and considering booster doses may be warranted. Larger controlled studies are needed to assess vaccine immunogenicity and reactogenicity, particularly for highly reactogenic vaccines such as meningococcal B.Keywords: PFAPA syndrome, Periodic fever, Vaccination coverage, Autoinflammatory disease, Seroprotection

Introduction

PFAPA syndrome (Periodic Fever, Aphthous Stomatitis, Pharyngitis, and Cervical Adenitis) is a common pediatric autoinflammatory disorder characterized by recurrent febrile episodes lasting 3–6 days and recurring every 3–8 weeks, typically accompanied by cervical adenitis, pharyngitis, and aphthous stomatitis [1–4]. The first symptomatic episodes generally occur between 2 and 5 years of age, with a mean age at onset around 1.5 years [3, 5]. PFAPA is considered the most frequent periodic fever syndrome in childhood, with an estimated annual incidence of up to 2.3 cases per 10,000 children under five years of age [6]. Although the incidence in Switzerland is not well documented, regional data suggest approximately 10 new cases per year in Geneva, corresponding to 3.3 cases per 10,000 children under five years [7].

Unlike other hereditary autoinflammatory syndromes, PFAPA has not been associated with a single monogenic defect, although a positive family history is reported in nearly half of affected children [4, 8–14]. Polymorphisms in genes regulating pro-inflammatory cytokines have been described, suggesting that dysregulation of the inflammasome pathway contributes to disease pathogenesis [15, 16]. The clinical course is benign but marked by recurrent febrile episodes, which can cause significant anxiety for parents and caregivers. A single corticosteroid dose administered at fever onset rapidly aborts symptoms in most patients, confirming the diagnosis and helping differentiate PFAPA from other periodic fever syndromes such as Familial Mediterranean Fever (FMF) [17–20]. However, frequent corticosteroid use can shorten the interval between attacks, posing challenges in disease management and parental reassurance [17–19, 21]. Tonsillectomy may reduce the frequency or severity of episodes, but is reserved for severe or refractory cases, and most children experience spontaneous remission by adolescence [21–25].

From an immunological perspective, PFAPA is driven by dysregulation of the innate immune response, characterized by increased production of pro-inflammatory cytokines during febrile episodes, monocytosis, and transient decreases in circulating T-cell levels, likely related to migration into lymphoid tissues [19, 26–28]. Persistent tonsillar inflammation, even during asymptomatic periods, supports a localized immune dysregulation and may explain the therapeutic effect of tonsillectomy [25, 29]. While innate immune mechanisms are central to PFAPA, the role of adaptive immunity remains incompletely understood. Because inflammation profoundly influences vaccine-induced immune responses, vaccination offers a unique opportunity to assess adaptive immune function in children with PFAPA [30–33].

Vaccination in PFAPA patients presents several challenges. Recurrent fevers and parental fears of triggering febrile relapses may delay or disrupt immunization schedules [34]. In addition, some clinicians remain reluctant to vaccinate children with heightened inflammatory reactivity, contributing to lower vaccination coverage. Nevertheless, small cohort studies evaluating serological responses to MMR and hepatitis A vaccines in PFAPA children have shown antibody titers within normal ranges for hepatitis A but a response slightly diminished for MMR [35–37].

At our center, children with PFAPA are managed collaboratively between their primary pediatricians and specialists from the Pediatric Immunology, Vaccinology and Rheumatology Unit. These patients do not require a modified immunization schedule, but they receive specific counselling to ensure that routine vaccinations are maintained without unnecessary delays related to disease activity or corticosteroid use. This coordinated approach aims to support families, reduce vaccine hesitancy, and maintain optimal seroprotection in this population.

The aim of this study was to assess vaccination coverage and serological protection in children with PFAPA syndrome, to determine whether the condition impacts vaccine-induced immunity.

To contextualize our study, it is important to consider the Swiss national vaccination framework. In Switzerland, no vaccinations are mandatory. The Federal Office of Public Health (FOPH) distinguishes between basic (routine) vaccinations, which are strongly recommended for all children, and complementary vaccinations, which are recommended only in specific situations or at parental request [38]. For children under five years, the age group most affected by PFAPA, the basic vaccination schedule includes the combined DTP-IPV-Hib vaccine (diphtheria, tetanus, pertussis, poliomyelitis, Haemophilus influenzae type b) and the pneumococcal conjugate vaccine, administered at 2, 4, and 12 months, with a booster dose of DTP-IPV recommended between 4 and 7 years. The MMR-V vaccine (measles, mumps, rubella, varicella) is administered at 9 and 12 months, and the inclusion of varicella represents a new recommendation introduced in January 2024 [39]. Complementary vaccines, such as rotavirus (2 and 4 months), meningococcal ACWY (between 12 and 18 months and booster between 11 and 15 years) and meningococcal B (at 3, 5 and between 12 and 18 months, followed by booster between 11 and 15 years), hepatitis A, tick-borne encephalitis (TBE), and influenza are offered according to individual risk and parental preference [38].

Methods

This observational study was approved by the Cantonal Research Ethics Commission of Geneva (n°BASEC 21–02395).

This prospective study included PFAPA patients followed by specialists from the Pediatric Immunology, Vaccinology, and Rheumatology Unit at HUG between February 2022 and April 2025. All patients with an active diagnosis according to the Gattorno criteria [4], currently under follow-up by our clinicians, and who provided informed consent were included in the study. The local guidelines recommend that primary pediatricians refer all cases of recurrent fever to our immunology clinic for further evaluation. Therefore, we are reasonably confident that all potential PFAPA patients in Geneva are followed in our consultation.

Clinical presentation, vaccination coverage, and vaccine serological responses were assessed, along with biological parameters measured during fever episodes (visit 1, designated as V1) and in symptom-free intervals (visit 2, designated as V2). Before the end of our study, and just prior to data analysis in April 2025, we assessed the clinical status of all patients in our cohort and collected follow-up data (visit 3, designated as V3). Genetic screening for key monogenic autoinflammatory fever syndrome genes was performed at the Department of Medical Genetics, Geneva University Hospitals (HUG). Study data were exclusively extracted from patient records and managed using REDCap tools, hosted and maintained by Geneva University Hospitals [40].

Vaccine-specific serologies were performed at the Vaccinology Laboratory of the University Hospital of Geneva, using the Euroimmun enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) for all antigens except pneumococcal serology, which was assessed via multiplex immunoassay [41].

Seroprotection thresholds were defined in accordance with the guidelines of the Swiss Federal Office of Public Health for healthy children [38]. Specifically, protective antibody levels were considered as follows: > 100 IU/l for diphtheria and tetanus, > 150 IU/l for chickenpox and measles, and ≥0.5 µg/ml for at least 4 of the 7 tested serotypes for pneumococcus.

Vaccination status was evaluated according to the official Swiss immunization schedule issued by the Federal Office of Public Health [38], applicable at the time of each child’s immunization. Children were considered up to date if their vaccination timeline corresponded to the national recommendations, accounting for a tolerance window of ± 15 days within the first six months of life and ±30 days thereafter.

In Switzerland, the Federal Office of Public Health [42] recommends vaccination against invasive meningococcal disease as a complementary vaccination. Until the end of 2018, the monovalent serogroup C vaccine (Neisvac®) was recommended. Since January 2019, the quadrivalent conjugate vaccine covering serogroups A, C, W, and Y (MenACWY, Menveo®) has been recommended [43]. In addition, since January 2024, the serogroup B vaccine (MenB, Bexsero®) is also recommended [39].

Genomic DNA was isolated from peripheral whole blood collected in EDTA tubes. Sequencing was performed using the Twist Comprehensive Exome Capture Kit on an Illumina NextSeq500 platform. Bioinformatic analysis was conducted using in-house tools developed at HUG to detect single nucleotide variants (SNVs) and copy number variants (CNVs). The obtained sequences were aligned to the hg19 (GRCh37) human genome reference from the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI). SNVs were screened using an 8-gene panel including ADA2, MVK, NOD2, TNFAIP3, MEFV, NLRP3, PSTPIP1, and TNFRSF1A. Mosaic CNVs were detected down to a 20% based on variant allele frequency (VAF). Mosaic trisomy of chromosome 8 was specifically assessed by analyzing variation of depth of coverage using SAMtools. Variants were classified according to the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) guidelines.

Statistical analyses were conducted using Stata Version 15 software (StataCorp. Stata Statistical Software: Release 15. College Station, TX: StataCorp LP; 2011). All associated graphs were produced using GraphPad Prism version 10.4.2 for macOS (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA; http://www.graphpad.com). Patient characteristics were classified as either continuous or categorical variables. Biological data were presented as concentrations or titers, appropriately categorized as continuous or categorical. For continuous variables, means with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported, while categorical variables were summarized as frequencies with corresponding percentages. To evaluate differences between the acute and asymptomatic phases, an unpaired Student test was performed, comparing data from visit 1 (during a PFAPA fever episode) with data from visit 2 (outside of a fever episode). A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Study characteristics

Of 50 eligible patients, informed consent was obtained for 41, who were included in the study. Demographic characteristics and clinical data, including age at disease onset, symptoms during fever crisis, and flare frequency, are summarized in Table 1. The gender distribution in our patient cohort was balanced. The mean age of the patients at the time of inclusion in the study was 5.6 years old. The ethnicity of the PFAPA patients was predominantly Caucasian (75%), then African (20%) and South American (5%). Among the PFAPA patients, 12% had comorbidities (1 patient with pelvic kidney, 1 patient with febrile seizures, 1 patient with prematurity at 32 weeks of gestation and 2 patients with tympanostomy tube). Almost half of the PFAPA patients had a family history for recurrent fever (44%), for tonsillectomy (14%).

Genetic analysis

Whole-exome sequencing was performed in 58% (24/41) of children with PFAPA, focusing on eight genes commonly associated with periodic fever syndromes; most tested patients had negative results. Eight patients had non-causative genetic findings. Four of them (10%) had heterozygous variants in the MEFV gene, two classified as pathogenic and two as variants of uncertain significance (see Table 1). One patient had a stop-gain variant of uncertain significance in the NOD2 gene, although reported variants in Blau syndrome are typically missense. Three had uncertain significance variants identified in the TNFRSF1A gene, which is associated with autosomal dominant periodic fever syndromes (7%). No copy number variants (CNVs) were detected, and in particular, no mosaic trisomy 8 was found.

Clinical presentation

Regarding the clinical history of PFAPA patients, the mean age at first fever episode was 2.2 years. The fever episode lasted on average 4.4 days and occurred once every 3–6 weeks for the majority (66%). Regarding the symptoms during the fever episode, all patients had fever. The most common other symptoms were pharyngitis (56%) and swollen lymph nodes (41%) (see Table 2).

Laboratory results

Blood analyses were conducted during flares and in between (see Fig. 1) and cell numbers are expressed in G/L, according to the local practices of our institution. During fever, there was a significant increase in the leucocytes (12.4 G/l in VISIT 1 and 8.2 G/l in VISIT 2, p < 0.0001), monocytes (0.9 G/l in VISIT 1 and 0.6 G/l in VISIT 2 p = 0.0003), and neutrophils (8.5 G/l in VISIT 1 and 3.8 G/l in VISIT 2 p < 0.0001). The CRP was significantly higher during fever than at steady state (69.3 mg/l at VISIT 1 and 7.5 mg/l at VISIT 2, p < 0.0001). Lymphocyte counts were significantly decreased during fever flares (2.4 G/l in VISIT 1 and 3.6 G/l in VISIT 2, p = 0.0090). There was no significant difference in the thrombocytes count. All children had a normal renal function and liver function during fever and at steady state (see Fig. 1).

The PFAPA patients who had an immunology workup showed normal levels of immunoglobulins with a mean concentration of IgG of 9.2 g/l [95%CI 8.3 to 10.1], IgA of 1.1 g/l [95%CI 0.8 to 1.2] and IgM of 1.0 g/l [95%CI 0.9 to 1.2] (data not shown).

Vaccination coverage

Regarding the core vaccinations, we observed that the first and second doses of the combined diphtheria, tetanus, pertussis, polio, Hib and hepatitis B (DTaP/IPV/Hib/HepB) vaccines were administered on time in 95% of patients and the third dose administered on time in 92% of patients (see Fig. 2). Since our patient cohort included young children who had not yet reached the age at which the fourth dose of the DTaP/IPV vaccine is typically administered, our statistical analysis focused only on those who were eligible for this dose. Among these eligible patients, 94% had received the fourth dose, and 77% had received it within the recommended timeframe.

Concerning the pneumococcal vaccine, the first dose was administered on time in 91% of patients, the second dose was administered on time in 95% patients, and the third dose was administered on time in 79% patients (see Fig. 2).

Concerning the measles, mumps, rubeola (MMR) vaccine: 85% patients received the first dose on time, and 75% received the second dose on time (see Fig. 2). No patients had received varicella vaccination, as the recommendations are recent (January 2024) and did not apply to our patient cohort.

Concerning optional vaccines, the meningococcal ACWY vaccines were generally well administered. Overall, 58% patients received the MenC or MenACWY meningococcal vaccine dose on time, with the majority (36%) receiving the MenACWY conjugate vaccine and a smaller proportion (22%) receiving the MenC conjugate vaccine. None of the patients receive the MenB vaccine which is only recommended since 2024 in Switzerland. Only 7% of the patients received the rotavirus vaccine, and. 14% received the hepatitis A vaccine. Although immunoglobulins against RSV have been authorized in Switzerland since 2024, none of the PFAPA patients were born after this authorization, which explains why no child in our cohort received them. No patients received the influenza vaccine or the tick-borne encephalitis, recommended since 2024 [44].

Follow-up visit

Concerning the visit 3, just before the end of study we assessed the clinical status of all patients (see Table 4). The mean age of patients at follow-up was 6.0 years. The children were classified according to the activity of their PFAPA syndrome, as very active (more than 6 crisis in the last year) in 33% patients, active (between 3 and 6 crises in the last year) in 10%, mildly active (less than 3 crisis in the last year) in 37%, and resolved in 20%. Regarding patients with resolved PFAPA, they had a final visit at a mean age of 6.1 years. A total of 15% were still experiencing symptomatic episodes without fever, including 10% who presented exclusively with aphthous stomatitis. These episodes occurred with a frequency similar to prior febrile PFAPA flares but were limited to non-febrile symptoms.

Discussion

In this cohort of 41 children with PFAPA, demographic and clinical characteristics were comparable to those described in larger studies [3]. The mean age at inclusion (5.6 years) was higher than age at symptom onset, reflecting the diagnostic challenges associated with nonspecific early symptoms and the time required for referral and investigations. Clinically, all patients experienced fever, while pharyngitis and cervical lymphadenopathy were the most common associated symptoms, consistent with prior reports [3, 23]. Less frequent manifestations, such as abdominal pain, skin rashes, arthralgia, and headaches, may have been underreported due to the young age of the cohort.

Laboratory findings showed a marked inflammatory response during flares, with significant increases in CRP, leucocytes, monocytes, and neutrophils, and concurrent lymphopenia, which normalized between episodes [19, 45, 46]. Renal and hepatic functions remained normal, supporting the generally benign nature of PFAPA, although subclinical organ involvement cannot be fully excluded. Serum amyloid A and erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) were measured in selected patients and were within normal ranges. In clinical practice, elevated SAA or ESR levels can support the decision to initiate colchicine therapy.

Genetic analysis revealed no monogenic cause, although half of patients reported a family history of recurrent fevers [8, 9]. Variants of uncertain significance were found in 20% of patients but are not considered causative [28, 47]. These findings support a polygenic etiology for PFAPA, while underlining the importance of investigating monogenic periodic fever syndromes in patients with overlapping clinical features due to their more aggressive management requirements [13].

A key finding of our study was the high adherence to the national vaccination schedule. Most children received DTPa-IPV-Hib (±HBV), pneumococcal, and MMR vaccines on time, contrasting with previous reports suggesting delayed vaccination in children with recurrent fevers [34]. Coverage rates were similar to those observed in the healthy Swiss population, though minor delays were observed for the fourth DTPa-IPV dose (vaccination coverage of the Swiss population at two years between 78% and 96%, and at eight years of 95%) [48, 49]. The third pneumococcal dose and the second MMR dose were also mildly delayed. This moderate drop in coverage may be partially explained by the presence of PFAPA and associated febrile episodes, which can lead to temporary delays. However, this may be due to paediatricians who separates the vaccines administered during the 12-month visit, to minimize discomfort or fever recurrence. A delay in the second MMR dose has also been observed in other cohort of healthy children [49]. Optional vaccines, including MenC and MenACWY, were administered at rates comparable to the general population, whereas MenB and tick-borne encephalitis (TBE) vaccines were not administered, consistent with recent Swiss recommendations [44]. Influenza vaccination is not recommended for healthy children in Switzerland. Since the hepatitis A vaccine is primarily administered as a travel-related immunization, assessing its coverage and immune response is challenging in this small cohort.

Serological analysis confirmed adequate protective immunity for most antigens, including diphtheria tetanus and measles. In contrast, only 65% showed varicella immunity, corresponding to past infection as none had been vaccinated. Indeed, this vaccine has been introduced in the Swiss schedule in 2023.

Another important observation of our study was that pneumococcal serology was suboptimal, with only 40% of patients demonstrating seroprotective antibody levels, despite generally high vaccination coverage. While low pneumococcal serological immunity has been reported in children with cochlear implants younger than 5 years, who have not received a booster dose of pneumococcal vaccine as recommended [50], the markedly low levels observed in this cohort raise the possibility of underlying immune immaturity or a less efficient vaccine-induced response in children with PFAPA. This finding suggests that additional pneumococcal doses or monitoring of antibody levels may be warranted in this population. However, larger studies including a control group of healthy children are needed to further explore this hypothesis.

Finally, follow-up data showed that most children experienced mild disease activity by six years of age, with complete remission in 20%. Interestingly, 15% of patients developed cyclic non-febrile episodes, primarily aphthous stomatitis or pharyngitis, resembling the early manifestations of Behçet’s disease, which similarly presents with recurrent oral ulcers and pharyngitis [51–55]. While this non-febrile pattern has previously been described following tonsillectomy [56], our study is the first to document it in patients managed solely with medical treatment, suggesting that the spectrum of PFAPA evolution may be broader than previously recognized.

It should be noted that our study has some limitations, which include the modest cohort size, lack of a control group, and potential selection bias, as 9 of 50 eligible patients declined participation, often due to blood sampling. These factors may limit the generalizability of the findings.

In conclusion, despite concerns regarding vaccine timing and reactogenicity in children with PFAPA, our study shows that this population is generally well vaccinated according to the Swiss national schedule. Serological responses were adequate for most vaccine antigens, although pneumococcal protection was suboptimal, suggesting the need for monitoring immunity and considering additional doses in selected patients. Longitudinal follow-up confirmed the typically benign and self-limiting nature of PFAPA, with most children showing low disease activity by six years of age. Notably, a subset of patients developed cyclic non-febrile episodes resembling early Behçet-like manifestations, such as recurrent aphthous stomatitis or pharyngitis. These findings warrant further investigation to better understand the potential continuum between PFAPA and Behçet’s disease.
