Thermal Pseudo-Entropy
Pawel Caputa, Bowen Chen, Tadashi Takayanagi, Takashi Tsuda

Abstract
In this work, we develop a generalisation of the thermal entropy to complex inverse temperatures, which we call the thermal pseudo-entropy. We show that this quantity represents the pseudo-entropy of the transition matrix between Thermofield Double states at different times. We have studied its properties in various quantum mechanical setups, Schwarzian theory, Random Matrix Theories, and 2D CFTs, including symmetric orbifolds. Our findings indicate a close relationship between the averaged thermal pseudo-entropy and the spectral form factor, which is instrumental in distinguishing chaotic and integrable models. Moreover, we have observed a logarithmic scaling of this quantity in models with a continuous spectrum, with a universal coefficient that is sensitive to the scaling of the density of states near the edge of the spectrum. Lastly, we found the connection between the real and imaginary parts of the thermal pseudo-entropy through the Kramers-Kronig relations.

In this paper, motivated by the above, we analyse the properties of the full complex-valued pseudo-entropy. To make the problem tractable, we focus on a simpler version of pseudo-entropy, which we call thermal pseudo-entropy (TPE). This is analogous to the relation between entanglement entropy and the standard thermal entropy, given by the statement that the entanglement entropy of a single copy in the Thermofield Double (TFD) state is simply equal to the thermal entropy. In other words, if we write the thermal entropy
S(\beta)
as a function of the inverse temperature
\beta

S(\beta+it)
. We will see below that this precisely coincides with the pseudo-entropy for the TFD state and its time-evolution.

This definition, by extending the standard thermal entropy, might be analogous to the spectral form factor (SFF)

while the latter not, we will show that there is a useful relationship between them when we take time averages. For earlier works on this connection, refer also to

Moreover, as we will see later, the imaginary part of TPE is completely determined by the real part via the Kramers-Kronig relations (and vice versa). This provides the first example with a sharp understanding of the imaginary part. We will work out several interesting properties of TPE by computing it in various examples below. This will also reveal how its time evolution depends on the energy spectra, taking into account the finite size effects.

2
Thermal pseudo-entropy

We start by briefly reviewing the notion of pseudo-entropy
Nakata:2021ubr
, define the thermal pseudo-entropy and connect its averaging to the SFF.

2.1
Pseudo-entropy

Pseudo-entropy is a generalisation of von Neumann entropy that involves the transition matrix between two quantum states
\ket{\psi}
and
\ket{\phi}
with non-zero overlap

\tau^{\phi|\psi}=\frac{\ket{\phi}\bra{\psi}}{\langle\psi|\phi\rangle}.

(2.1)

As for ordinary entanglement entropy, we assume that Hilbert space admits a decomposition to a region
A
and its complement
B
:
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B}
, and define the reduced transition matrix in analogy with reduced density matrix

\tau^{\phi|\psi}_{A}=\Tr_{B}(\tau^{\phi|\psi}).

(2.2)

The pseudo-entropy is then defined as the von Neumann entropy of the reduced transition matrix

S(\tau^{\phi|\psi}_{A})=-\Tr(\tau^{\phi|\psi}_A\log\tau^{\phi|\psi}_A).

(2.3)

In the following, we will also consider the Rényi version of pseudo-entropy for an integer
n

S^{(n)}(\tau^{\phi|\psi}_{A})=\frac{1}{1-n}\Tr\left[(\tau^{\phi|\psi}_{A})^{n}\right].

(2.4)

Since both quantities are based on the non-Hermitian transition matrix, in general, they are complex generalisations of von-Neumann and Rényi entropies. More detailed and pedagogical introduction to pseudo-entropy can be found in
Nakata:2021ubr
;
Mollabashi:2021xsd
;
Parzygnat:2023avh
.

2.2
Thermal pseudo-entropy

In the remaining part of this work, we focus on a particular choice for the two states
\ket{\psi}
and
\ket{\phi}
above. Namely, the first state will be the TFD state
Takahashi:1996zn
, that is the canonical purification of the thermal density matrix with energy eigenvalues
E_{n}
and inverse temperature
\beta=T^{-1}

\ket{\Psi_{\beta}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{Z(\beta)}}\sum_{n}e^{-\frac{\beta}{2}E_{n}}\ket{E_{n}}_{L}\otimes\ket{E_{n}}_{R}.

(2.5)

The state requires two copies of the Hilbert space that we refer to as left (L) and right (R). The overall normalisation is given by the square root of the thermal partition function

Z(\beta)=\sum_{n}e^{-\beta E_{n}}.

(2.6)

The second state will be the time evolution of the TFD with one of the Hamiltonians, say the left one, defined as

\ket{\Psi_{\beta}(t)}=e^{-iH_{L}t}\ket{\Psi_{\beta}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{Z(\beta)}}\sum_{n}e^{-\frac{\beta+2it}{2}E_{n}}\ket{E_{n}}_{L}\otimes\ket{E_{n}}_{R}.

(2.7)

Next, we can define the transition matrix
1
1

1

Since we will only use the same two states throughout the paper, we drop the super-scripts from transition matrices.

\tau=\frac{\ket{\Psi_{\beta}(t)}\bra{\Psi_{\beta}}}{\langle\Psi_{\beta}|\Psi_{\beta}(t)\rangle},

(2.8)

with the overlap expressed by analytically continued partition function to complex inverse temperatures

\langle\Psi_{\beta}|\Psi_{\beta}(t)\rangle=\frac{Z(\beta+it)}{Z(\beta)}.

(2.9)

The reduced transition matrix of the left subsystem becomes

\tau_{L}=\Tr_{R}\left(\tau\right)=\frac{1}{Z(\beta+it)}\sum_{n}e^{-(\beta+it)E_{n}}\outerproduct{E_n}{E_n}.

(2.10)

This is simply the thermal density matrix with inverse temperature continued to complex values
\beta\to\beta+it
.

Finally, we define the thermal pseudo-entropy (TPE) as von-Neumann entropy of
\tau_{L}

S(\tau_{L})=-\Tr(\tau_L\log\tau_L).

(2.11)

The name TPE is clear since it coincides with the canonical thermal entropy

S_{th}(\beta)=(1-\beta\partial_{\beta})\log Z(\beta),

(2.12)

analytically continued to complex
\beta\to\beta+it
, which at
t=0
reduces to the thermal entropy.

One intuitive way to rewrite the TPE is in terms of the complex phases of the transition matrix as

S(\tau_{L})=\log(Z(\beta+it))+\langle\theta(t)\rangle,

(2.13)

where

\theta_{n}(t)\equiv(\beta+it)E_{n},\qquad\langle\theta(t)\rangle=\frac{1}{Z(\beta+it)}\sum_{n}\theta_{n}(t)e^{-\theta_{n}(t)}.

(2.14)

This also gives an interesting perspective that could be related to general “complexity” measures designed to probe late time information hidden in the phases. We will not explore this direction here but hope to return to it in the near future.

In what follows, we will also be interested in Rényi versions of the thermal pseudo-entropies defined as

S^{(n)}(\beta+it)=\frac{1}{1-n}\log\left[\frac{Z(n\beta)}{Z(\beta)^{n}}\right]_{\beta\to\beta+it},

(2.15)

that reduce to TPE in the
n\to 1
limit.

2.3
Averaged thermal pseudo-entropy and the spectral form factor

Interestingly, we can write the real and imaginary parts of the thermal pseudo Rényi entropies as

2(1-n)S^{(n)}(\beta+it)=\log\left[\frac{|Z(n(\beta+it))|^{2}}{|Z(\beta+it)|^{2n}}\right]+\frac{1}{i}\log\left[\frac{Z(n(\beta+it))Z(\beta-it)^{n}}{Z(\beta+it)^{n}Z(n(\beta-it))}\right].

(2.16)

Note that, in general, both real and imaginary parts are highly oscillating functions of
t
.
This also shows that we can think about the TPE as a close cousin of the SFF (refer also to
Goto:2021kln
) and analogous probes that require analytic continuation of the real inverse temperatures
\beta
into complex domain
\beta+it
.

In fact, if we average over the energy spectrum, taking into account only two body correlations, the real part of the TPE reads (see also
He:2024jog
)

\displaystyle\overline{\mbox{Re}\,S^{(n)}(\beta+it)}=\frac{1}{2(1-n)}\left[\log\overline{|Z\left(n(\beta+it)\right)|^{2}}-n\log\overline{|Z\left(\beta+it\right)|^{2}}\right].

(2.17)

It is then clear that the real part can be related to the SFF
Cotler:2016fpe
, defined by

\displaystyle g_{\beta}(t)=\left|\frac{Z(\beta+it)}{Z(\beta)}\right|^{2},

(2.18)

as follows

\displaystyle\overline{\mbox{Re}\,S^{(n)}(\beta+it)}=\frac{1}{2(1-n)}\log\frac{g_{n\beta}(t)}{g_{\beta}(t)^{n}}+S^{(n)}_{th}(\beta).

(2.19)

We can make use of this connection to observe the following universal feature. Namely, if there is no degeneracy in the energy spectrum, the late time limit leads to

\displaystyle\overline{\mbox{Re}\,S^{(n)}(\beta+it)}\xrightarrow[t\to\infty]{}\frac{1}{2(1-n)}\log\frac{Z(2n\beta)}{Z(2\beta)^{n}}=\frac{1}{2}S^{(n)}(2\beta).

(2.20)

If there are degeneracies, we need to replace
Z(2n\beta)
and
Z(2\beta)
in (
2.20
) with
\sum_{n}d_{n}e^{-2n\beta E_{n}}
and
\sum_{n}d_{n}e^{-2\beta E_{n}}
, respectively, where
d_{n}\geq 1
is the degree of degeneracy. It is then a simple exercise to show that, in general
\overline{\mbox{Re}\,S^{(n)}(\beta+it)}\geq\frac{1}{2}S^{(n)}(2\beta)
.

3
General properties of thermal pseudo-entropy

In this section we describe two important properties of TPE. The first is the Kramers-Kronig relation between its real and imaginary parts. The second is the late time behaviour in cases where we can approximate (“coarse-grain”) the energy spectrum by a continuous function.

3.1
Kramers-Kronig relations

One of the most interesting and urgent questions regarding the pseudo-entropy in general are the meaning of its imaginary part and a potential relation with the real part in various setups. It turns out that, for the TPE, there is more mathematical structure that allows for progress and better understanding of these manners. This can be seen as follows.

Assuming that the function
f(t)
is analytic in the upper half plane and decays rapidly in the limit Im
(t)\to\ -\infty
, we have the following relation between the real and imaginary parts

\displaystyle\mbox{Im}[f(t)]=\frac{1}{\pi}P\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}ds\frac{{\mbox{Re}}[f(s)]}{s-t},

(3.1)

where
P
is the Cauchy principal value of the integral. This is the Hilbert transform, often referred to in physics as the Kramers–Kronig relations
kramers1927diffusion
;
de1926theory
(KK-relations for short).

We can confirm this relation for TPE as a function of
t
, i.e. by taking
f(t)=S(\beta+it)
. The decay condition in the limit Im
(t)\to\ -\infty
is satisfied because the entropy
S(\beta)
should go to zero in the zero temperature limit
\beta\to\infty
. Also, it is clear that the pseudo-entropy
S(\beta+it)
is analytic in the upper half plane Im
(t)>0
. Indeed, we will explicitly confirm, by numerical integration, that the Kramers-Kronig relation holds in all examples considered in this paper.

3.2
Time evolution under continuous spectrum approximation

Next, we assume that we can approximate the energy spectrum by a continuous function. This is sometimes understood as a coarse-graining procedure in some limit of parameters of the model (e.g. large rank
N
of a matrix). This way, we can simply write the density of states as
\rho(E)
and assume the lowest energy denoted by
E_{0}
. The partition function is then computed as

\displaystyle Z(\beta)=\int^{\infty}_{E_{0}}dEe^{-\beta E}\rho(E).

(3.2)

If we assume the behaviour of
\rho(E)
near the edge of the spectrum
E=E_{0}
as follows

\displaystyle\rho(E)\sim(E-E_{0})^{\gamma},

(3.3)

then we find, in the low temperature limit
\beta\to\infty
, the scaling of the partition function

\displaystyle Z(\beta)\sim\beta^{-1-\gamma}e^{-\beta E_{0}}.

(3.4)

This leads to the following behaviour of the thermal entropy (
2.12
)

\displaystyle S_{th}(\beta)\simeq-(1+\gamma)\log\beta,

(3.5)

for large
\beta
. We will make use of this relation momentarily. However, we should keep in mind that, in the strict
\beta\to\infty
limit of standard quantum systems, the discrete nature of energy spectrum becomes important and the behaviour (
3.5
) must be modified such that
\lim_{\beta\to\infty}S_{th}(\beta)=0
.

Now we turn to the TPE, which is obtained from the thermal entropy by replacing
\beta
with
\beta+it
. As a consequence of (
3.5
), for large
t
, we have

\displaystyle\overline{S(\beta+it)}\simeq-(1+\gamma)\log t.

(3.6)

Note that
S(\beta+it)
is in general a highly oscillating function and we need to average it
over a small time window to find the behaviour (
3.6
). Again, this logarithmic scaling occurs when we can approximate the energy spectrum by a continuous distribution. Thus the behaviour (
3.5
) and (
3.6
) is valid only when
t\ll\frac{1}{\Delta E}
, where
\Delta E
is the energy level spacing. We will show this in explicit examples below.

Moreover, from our general discussion, we expect that, as a function of
t
,
\overline{S(\beta+it)}
starts from the initial value
S_{th}(\beta)
at
t=0
and eventually gets decreased (plateau) to a positive value
S_{*}
, which satisfies

\displaystyle S_{*}\geq\frac{1}{2}S^{(n)}(2\beta),

(3.7)

as we have seen in (
2.20
). This allows us to estimate the maximal time
t_{*}
where we can trust the behaviour (
3.6
) as

(1+\gamma)\log(t_*)\sim S_{th}(\beta)-S_{*}.\ \ \

(3.8)

Next, to gain more intuition for different evolution scenarios of TPE, we turn to several explicit examples.

4
Quantum mechanical examples

In this section we analyse a few explicit examples where TPE can be computed. We also verify the KK relations between its real and imaginary parts and discuss the logarithmic contribution for the continuous spectrum models.

4.1
Two-level systems

Arguably one of the simplest models to start with is the two-level system or a particle with energy levels
E_{1}=0
and
E_{2}=\epsilon
. We can consider N non-interacting particles of this kind with partition function

Z(\beta)=\left(\sum^{2}_{n=1}e^{-\beta E_{n}}\right)^{N}=2^{N}e^{-N\beta\epsilon/2}\cosh^{N}(\beta\epsilon/2).

(4.1)

For the reference, we can easily evaluate the SFF in this model that is periodic with period
t=2\pi/\epsilon
, as it should for this simple integrable model

\left|\frac{Z(\beta+it)}{Z(\beta)}\right|^{2}=\left(\frac{\cosh(\beta\epsilon)+\cos(t\epsilon)}{\cosh(\beta\epsilon)+1}\right)^{N}.

(4.2)

The standard thermal entropy computed from (
2.12
) becomes

S_{th}(\beta)=N\log\left(1+e^{-\beta\epsilon}\right)+\frac{N\beta\epsilon}{1+e^{\beta\epsilon}}.

(4.3)

) for
\epsilon=1
and
\beta=1/3
. Red dots indicate numerically computed imaginary part using the KK-relations.

Finally, the TPE obtained by sending
\beta\to\beta+it
becomes

S(\beta+it)=N\log\left(1+e^{-(\beta+it)\epsilon}\right)+\frac{N(\beta+it)\epsilon}{1+e^{(\beta+it)\epsilon}}.

(4.4)

We plotted its real and imaginary parts on Fig.

4.2
Harmonic oscillator and Calogero-Sutherland model

Next we consider the quantum harmonic oscillator and the TFD state of two copies of infinite-dimensional Hilbert space with energy spectrum and thermal partition function given by

E_{n}=\omega(n+1/2),\qquad Z(\beta)=\frac{1}{2\sinh\left(\frac{\beta\omega}{2}\right)}.

(4.5)

Following the same steps as before, we derive the TPE which is now given by

S_{1}(\beta+it,\omega)\equiv-\log\left[2\sinh\left(\frac{(\beta+it)\omega}{2}\right)\right]+\frac{(\beta+it)\omega}{2}\coth\left[\frac{(\beta+it)\omega}{2}\right].

(4.6)

We present the real and imaginary parts on Fig.

(left). They oscillate in time with period determined by

. The amplitude of these oscillations keeps increasing with time. This can be seen from the linear

). Somewhat surprisingly, this linear growth of the amplitude of TPE with time is a feature of integrable models as well as of chaotic ones, as we will see later.

Note that the partition function is exponentially suppressed for
\beta\to\infty
. Hence, the KK relations are also satisfied and we present their verification by red dots on the imaginary part on Fig.

A closely related generalisation is the famous integrable Hamiltonian of the Calorego-Sutherland model (CSM)

H=\frac{1}{2}\sum^{N}_{i=1}\left[-\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial z^{2}_{i}}+\omega^{2}z^{2}_{i}\right]+\sum_{i<j}\frac{\lambda(\lambda-1)}{(z_{i}-z_{j})^{2}}.

(4.7)

The SFF for this example was already discussed in
delCampo:2017bzr
. It turns out, that the coupling
\lambda
only renormalises the vacuum energy and the partition function of the model can be written as a product over partition functions of a single harmonic oscillator with different frequencies as follows

Z(\beta)=\prod^{N}_{k=1}\frac{1}{2\sinh\left(k\frac{\beta\omega}{2}\right)}.

(4.8)

The SFF is periodic in time and given by
delCampo:2017bzr

\left|\frac{Z(\beta+it)}{Z(\beta)}\right|^{2}=\prod^{N}_{k=1}\frac{\cosh(k\omega\beta)-1}{\cosh(k\omega\beta)-\cos(k\omega t)}.

(4.9)

The harmonic oscillator is the
N=1
case.

Consequently, the thermal entropy of the CSM can be understood as a summation of thermal entropies of harmonic oscillators with equal spacing frequencies. Therefore, after continuation to imaginary
\beta
, the TPE becomes

S_{N}(\beta+it;\omega)=\sum_{k=1}^{N}S_{1}(\beta+it;k\omega).

(4.10)

On Fig.
2
(right), we plot the real and imaginary parts for the CS model for
N=2
. The effect of many particles adds more wiggles but the real and imaginary parts keep oscillating periodically with increasing amplitude. The KK relation is again satisfied.

4.3
Schwarzian theory

Our next example, and the first example with continuous spectrum, is the Schwarzian theory. This model has played a prominent role in understanding of the low-energy limit of the Sachdev-Ye-Kitaev (SYK) model
Sachdev:1992fk
;
kitaev
and its correspondence with Jackiw–Teitelboim gravity
Maldacena:2016hyu
(see also review
Mertens:2022irh
).

The Schwarzian theory can be defined by the characteristic continuous spectrum with the hyperbolic sine density of states

\rho_{0}(E)\equiv\frac{C}{2\pi^{2}}e^{S_{0}}\sinh\left(2\pi\sqrt{2CE}\right).

(4.11)

In this expression,
C
and
S_{0}
denote large constants and
S_{0}
computes the entropy of the 2D black hole. This density of states leads to the Schwarzian thermal partition function that is also one-loop exact
Stanford:2017thb

Z(\beta)=\int^{\infty}_{0}dE\,\rho_{0}(E)e^{-\beta E}=\frac{e^{S_{0}}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\left(\frac{C}{\beta}\right)^{3/2}e^{\frac{2\pi^{2}C}{\beta}}.

(4.12)

) for

,

Finally, the thermal entropy of the model is given by

S(\beta)=S_{0}+\frac{4\pi^{2}C}{\beta}-\frac{3}{2}\log\left(\beta\right)+\frac{3}{2}\log\left(\frac{C\,e}{(2\pi)^{1/3}}\right),

(4.13)

and TPE is obtained from it by setting
\beta\to\beta+it
. Fig.
3
shows its real and imaginary parts as well as the validity check of the KK relation.

From this analytical result we see that, at the early times, TPE is approximately

\displaystyle S(\beta+it)\simeq\frac{4\pi^{2}C}{\beta+it},

(4.14)

which can be naturally explained by the classical gravity dual. In the late time regime, we find the logarithmic behaviour

\displaystyle S(\beta+it)\simeq-\frac{3}{2}\log t,

(4.15)

dominated by the one-loop correction.
This behaviour nicely agrees with the general argument (
3.6
). Indeed, for the Schwarzian theory we have the continuous spectrum (
4.11
) which, close to the edge, is characterised by the scaling parameter
\gamma=\frac{1}{2}
.

On the other hand, in the strictly large
t
limit, we need to respect the discreteness of the spectrum in regular quantum systems. This should be dual to the non-perturbative effects in quantum gravity. Eventually the averaged value of the TPE will approach a certain positive value
S_{*}
(
3.7
).

4.4
Random matrix theory

Our second example with continuous spectrum will be the random matrix theory (RMT). In general, a RMT Hamiltonian is considered as an archetype of a quantum chaotic setup. We will thus try to draw some universal lessons from this example and compare them with integrable models.

More precisely, we consider the quenched partition function from a random ensemble, e.g. GUE. We first check its behaviour in the simplest case of the Wigner semi-circle, leading to a universal logarithmic decay of the real part of the TPE. A numerical analysis shows that taking the discreteness of the spectra, the logarithmic decay stops at the dip time, followed by a logarithmic growth until the late time value
S_{*}

Semi-circle

In RMT, the normalised density of states is usually approximated by the continuous Wigner semicircle spectrum

\rho_{\rm SC}(E)=\frac{2}{\pi\lambda}\sqrt{1-\frac{E^{2}}{\lambda^{2}}},

(4.16)

where

\displaystyle Z_{\rm SC}(\beta)=\int^{\lambda}_{-\lambda}dEe^{-\beta E}\rho_{\rm SC}(E)=\frac{2}{\beta\lambda}I_{1}(\beta\lambda).

(4.17)

Here,

\displaystyle{\rm SFF}_{\rm SC}(\beta,t)

\displaystyle=\frac{Z_{\rm SC}(\beta+it)Z_{\rm SC}(\beta-it)}{Z_{\rm SC}^{2}(\beta)}.

Adding potential

We can also test the robustness of our analysis by modifying the potential. Namely, the Wigner semi-circle comes from a Gaussian potential
V(M)=\frac{1}{2}M^{2}
. More generically, we can also include higher order terms, e.g. quartic and sextic terms. Here we consider the one-cut sextic case (see
Gaikwad:2017odv
for analysis of the SFF)

V(M)=\frac{1}{2}M^{2}+\frac{g}{N}M^{4}+\frac{h}{N^{2}}M^{6},

(4.26)

and the density of states is given by

\rho(E)=\frac{1}{\pi}\left(3hE^{4}+(2g+6ha^{2})E^{2}+\left(\frac{1}{2}+4ga^{2}+18ha^{4}\right)\right)\sqrt{4a^{2}-E^{2}}.

(4.27)

The normalisation gives the following constraint on the couplings

60ha^{6}+12ga^{4}+a^{2}-1=0.

(4.28)

For example,
g=h=0
and
a=1
give us the semi-circle solution.

The partition function for this model becomes

\displaystyle Z_{\rm sextic}(\beta)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{360ha^{3}}{\beta^{3}}+\frac{a+24ga^{3}+180ha^{5}}{\beta}\right)I_{1}(2a\beta)

(4.29)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\left(\frac{720ha^{2}}{\beta^{4}}+\frac{24ga^{2}+360ha^{4}}{\beta^{2}}\right)I_{2}(2a\beta).

Following the same steps as for the Gaussian model, we can obtain the TPE and its large time scaling

\displaystyle S_{\rm sextic}(z)

\displaystyle\sim\log\left[e^{2az}\left(\frac{\sqrt{a}(1+24ga^{2}+180ha^{4})}{2\sqrt{\pi}z^{3/2}}-\frac{3(1+152ga^{2}+2100ha^{4})}{32\sqrt{a\pi}z^{5/2}}+\dots\right)\right]

\displaystyle-2az+\frac{3}{2}-\frac{3(1+152ga^{2}+2100ha^{4})}{16(a+24ga^{3}+180ha^{5})z}+\dots

\displaystyle=-\frac{3}{2}\log z+{\rm const.}+O(1/z),

(4.30)

where in the end we just replace
z
by
\beta+it
.

This confirms that adding higher order terms in the potential does not modify the leading order behaviour of TPE. The
-\frac{3}{2}\log t
is preserved under perturbations. As is expected from the discussion in (
3.6
), the logarithmic behaviour only depends on the scaling of spectrum close to the ground state edge. RMT is equivalent to
\gamma=\frac{1}{2}
, regardless of the details of the potential. The same can be shown for multi-cut RMT spectra and the universal logarithmic behaviour is still preserved.

4.5
Averaged thermal pseudo-entropy for the RMT universality class

In this subsection we revisit the above claims, but this time more quantitatively and analytically in the RMT universality class.

We saw that the average value of the real part of TPE is directly related to the SFF via

\overline{\text{Re}\,S^{(n)}(\beta+it)}=\frac{\log\langle Z(n(\beta+it))Z(n(\beta-it))\rangle-n\log\langle Z(\beta+it)Z(\beta-it)\rangle}{2(1-n)}.

(4.31)

In this expression we assumed that we can take the average under the logarithm (no replica symmetry breaking). We will proceed with this reasonable assumption that holds in many physical systems
2
2

2

It will also be important to study TPE without this assumption, e.g.
Derrida:1981zz
. See, related
Winer:2022ciz
.
.

In RMT we can take the advantage of (
4.31
) to predict the evolution of
\overline{\text{Re}\,S^{(n)}}
at all times. Indeed, this can be derived in complete analogy to the SFF, pedagogically reviewed in
Mertens:2022irh
, that we closely follow with slight modifications for our context
3
3

3

Analysis of TPE in analogy with
Winer:2020gdp
would also be interesting.
.

The basic object that we will compute is

\langle Z(n(\beta+it))Z(n(\beta-it))\rangle=\int dEdE^{\prime}\langle\rho(E)\rho(E^{\prime})\rangle e^{-n\beta(E+E^{\prime})}e^{-int(E-E^{\prime})}.

(4.32)

The RMT universality posits that the two-point correlator of densities
\rho(E)
is universal (irrespectively of the potential) in the unitary universality class, and given by

\langle\rho(E)\rho(E^{\prime})\rangle\simeq\rho_{0}(E)\rho_{0}(E^{\prime})-\frac{\sin^{2}\left(\pi\rho_{0}(E)(E-E^{\prime})\right)}{\pi^{2}(E-E^{\prime})^{2}}+\rho_{0}(E)\delta(E-E^{\prime}),

(4.33)

where the density
\rho_{0}(E)
is identical to the Schwarzian theory (
4.11
). The second term is the sine kernel responsible for the level repulsion in quantum chaotic systems. We will separate it using the identity
\sin^{2}(x)=(1-\cos(2x))/2
as

\langle\rho(E)\rho(E^{\prime})\rangle\simeq\rho_{0}(E)\rho_{0}(E^{\prime})-\frac{1}{2\pi^{2}(E-E^{\prime})^{2}}+\frac{\cos\left(2\pi\rho_{0}(E)(E-E^{\prime})\right)}{2\pi^{2}(E-E^{\prime})^{2}}+\rho_{0}(E)\delta(E-E^{\prime}),

(4.34)

and analyse each of the contributions separately.

For early times, the product of two densities dominates. This gives the product of two Schwarzian partition functions such that

\langle Z(n(\beta+it))Z(n(\beta-it))\rangle\simeq\frac{C^{3}e^{2S_{0}}}{2\pi n^{3}(t^{2}+\beta^{2})^{3/2}}e^{\frac{4\pi^{2}C\beta}{n(t^{2}+\beta^{2})}},

(4.35)

and the real part of the averaged n-th Rényi TPE becomes

\overline{\text{Re}\,S^{(n)}(\beta+it)}\simeq S_{0}+\frac{n+1}{n}\frac{2\pi^{2}C\beta}{t^{2}+\beta^{2}}-\frac{3}{4}\log(t^2+\beta^2)+\frac{3}{2}\frac{\log(n)}{n-1}+\frac{3}{2}\log\left(\frac{C}{(2\pi)^{1/3}}\right).

(4.36)

After the collision time
t\sim\beta
the SFF decays as
t^{-3}
, what translates to the logarithmic decay of the TPE. Note that the
-3/2\log(t)
contribution is the same for all the Rényi entropies i.e. independent of
n
.

Next we turn to the second term in (
4.34
) for which we have

\langle Z(n(\beta+it))Z(n(\beta-it))\rangle\simeq-\int dEdE^{\prime}\frac{1}{2\pi^{2}(E-E^{\prime})^{2}}e^{-n\beta(E+E^{\prime})}e^{-int(E-E^{\prime})}.

(4.37)

This integral is also universal
4
4

4

We first change integration variables to
(E,E-E^{\prime})
, integrate over
E
and use the Fourier transform identity
-\int^{+\infty}_{-\infty}\frac{dx}{2\pi^{2}x^{2}}e^{iTx}=|T|/2\pi
for
T=-n(t+i\beta)
.
and independent on
n
. It gives
Ginsparg:1993is
;
Mertens:2022irh

\langle Z(n(\beta+it))Z(n(\beta-it))\rangle\simeq\frac{\sqrt{t^{2}+\beta^{2}}}{4\pi\beta}\to\frac{t}{4\pi\beta}.

(4.38)

Interestingly, on the gravity side, this term is associated with the Lorentzian double cone geometry
Saad:2018bqo
;
Chen:2023hra
which also fixes the normalisation. Consequently, the TPE changes evolution from the logarithmic decay to the dip to the logarithmic ramp regime (see Fig.
7
)

\overline{\text{Re}\,S^{(n)}(\beta+it)}\simeq\frac{1}{2}\log\left(\frac{t}{4\pi\beta}\right).

(4.39)

Again we get the universal
1/2\log(t)
growth (together with the normalisation) for all the Rényis. However, the dip time is estimated from the exchange of dominance between the two contributions

\frac{C^{3}e^{2S_{0}}}{2\pi n^{3}t^{3}_{d}}=\frac{t_{d}}{4\pi\beta}\quad\Rightarrow\quad t_{d}=(2\beta C^{3})^{1/4}e^{S_{0}/2}/n^{3/4},

(4.40)

and depends on the Rényi index
n
.

The third term in (
4.34
) is non-perturbative in
e^{-S_{0}}
and, for the SFF, results in a linear decay that cancels the linear ramp contribution
Mertens:2022irh
. It is estimated for times of order

t\sim Ce^{S_{0}},

(4.41)

and this is the plateau time. Finally, the value of the plateau is obtained from the last term

\langle Z(n(\beta+it))Z(n(\beta-it))\rangle\simeq\int dE\rho_{0}(E)e^{-2n\beta E}=Z(2n\beta),

(4.42)

and corresponds to the TPE plateau exactly as in (
2.20
).

This reproduces our claims from the previous sections and Fig.
7
in the RMT universality class.

5.3
Holographic CFTs

Now we turn to analyse the opposite limit i.e. the holographic CFT with a large central charge and in the strong coupling limit. Since we can only treat this class of theories analytically in the classical gravity limit, our analysis becomes qualitative.

In general, for a holographic CFT dual to pure gravity in AdS
3
, we expect the following spectrum

\displaystyle\rho(E)=\rho_{0}(E)+\rho_{ex}(E),

(5.7)

where
\rho_{0}(E)
represents the contributions from the vacuum state and its descendants. On the other hand,
\rho_{ex}(E)
describes the excited states contributions with
E\geq\frac{c}{12}
, which behaves according to the Cardy formula

\displaystyle\rho_{ex}(E)\sim e^{2\pi\sqrt{\frac{c}{3}(E-\frac{c}{12})}}.

(5.8)

Although we can also relax the behaviour of density of states so that the theory includes primaries with a sparse spectrum for
E<\frac{c}{12}

Hartman:2014oaa
;
Mukhametzhanov:2019pzy
, the conclusions below would not change.

Then the holographic CFT partition function looks like

\displaystyle Z(\beta)=Z_{0}(\beta)+Z_{ex}(\beta),

(5.9)

where
Z_{0}(\beta)
describes the vacuum sector contributions dual to the thermal AdS
3
. Its dependence on
\beta
is

Z_{0}(\beta)=|q|^{-\frac{c}{24}}\frac{|1-q|^{2}}{|\eta(\tau)|^{2}}\simeq e^{\frac{c}{12}\beta},

(5.10)

where

and
\tau=\frac{i}{2\pi}
. In the final expression we took advantage of the large

On the other hand,
Z_{ex}(\beta)
describes the contribution to the partition function from excited states. In the high temperature phase
\beta<2\pi
, it is dominated by the BTZ black hole saddle
Z_{BTZ}(\beta)

, obtained by the modular S-transformation
\tau\to-\frac{1}{\tau}

\displaystyle Z_{BTZ}(\beta)=Z_{0}\left(\frac{4\pi^{2}}{\beta}\right)\simeq e^{\frac{\pi^{2}c}{3\beta}},

(5.11)

Note that there is no power of
\beta
in front of

and thus there is no logarithmic term (i.e.
\log\beta
) in the thermal entropy, given by
S_{th}(\beta)=\frac{2\pi^{2}c}{3\beta}
, as opposed to the result in the Schwarzian theory.

As usual, we are assuming the black hole phase
\beta<2\pi
at

. In the early time region,

, we can then easily compute the TPE as follows

\displaystyle S(\beta+it)=\frac{2\pi^{2}c}{3(\beta+it)}.

(5.12)

However, for evaluating the TPE in the late time region

, the black hole saddle approximation breaks down and other saddle points, including the thermal AdS, start to contribute. Moreover, we do not expect
\log t
behaviour as we noted just before.

In other words, we have
\gamma=-1
in the holographic CFTs. This shows a sharp contrast with the standard “quantum chaotic” theories i.e. RMT or the Schwarzian, where we found
\gamma=1/2
.

This way, the full analysis of SFF as well as the TPE in holographic CFTs turns out to be very difficult. This motivates us to study a simpler but partially similar class of two-dimensional CFTs, namely the symmetric orbifold CFTs, as we will do in the next section. Even though the symmetric orbifolds are still free from interactions, they share the property of the sparse spectrum with the actual holographic CFTs. Also the recurrence time becomes exponentially large
TaTsu
. We will explore their imprints in the SFF and TPE below.

Z(\tau,\bar{\tau})
, the partition function of its symmetric orbifold reads

Z_{N,S}(\tau,\bar{\tau})=\sum_{\{N_{k}\}}\prod_{k=1}^{N}\frac{1}{N_{k}!}\left(T_{k}Z(\tau,\bar{\tau})\right)^{N_{k}},

(6.2)

where
\{N_{k}\}
constitutes a partition of integer
N
so that
\sum_{k}kN_{k}=N
and
T_{k}
is the Hecke operator which action on the partition function is defined as

T_{k}Z(\tau,\bar{\tau})\equiv\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i|k}\sum_{j=0}^{i-1}Z\left(\frac{k\tau}{i^{2}}+\frac{j}{i},\frac{k\bar{\tau}}{i^{2}}+\frac{j}{i}\right).

(6.3)

For example, when
N=2
we can write down the orbifold partition function as

\displaystyle Z_{2,S}(\tau,\bar{\tau})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2!}\left(T_{1}Z(\tau,\bar{\tau})\right)^{2}+T_{2}Z(\tau,\bar{\tau})

(6.4)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left[Z^{2}(\tau,\bar{\tau})+Z(2\tau,2\bar{\tau})+Z\left(\frac{\tau}{2},\frac{\bar{\tau}}{2}\right)+Z\left(\frac{\tau+1}{2},\frac{\bar{\tau}+1}{2}\right)\right].

Next we will derive the partition functions of symmetric orbifolds for different
N
and evaluate thermal pseudo-entropies. The seed theories we will choose are free fermion CFT and compact scalar CFT, the same as in the previous sections.

7
Conclusions and Discussion

In this work we analysed the TPE and its properties in various quantum mechanical and field theoretic setups. This generalisation of the thermal entropy to complex inverse temperatures has several interesting features that we now briefly summarise and point to some future directions.

Firstly, we saw that the average of the real part of the TPE is directly related to the SFF. This is an interesting avenue that may bridge between quantum chaos (the domain of SFF) and quantum information (where pseudo-entropies originate). Indeed, a lot of work is dedicated to understanding quantum information aspects of pseudo-entropies, such as e.g. its operational meaning or gravity dual. The link we found could potentially shed some of this quantum information light on the SFF and quantum chaos.

On a similar note, we introduced the TPE as a natural step in the pseudo-entropy program
Nakata:2021ubr
, but it also seems very intuitive from the non-equilibrium physics point of view. To our knowledge, the TPE has not appeared in that context before, but partition functions with complex temperatures (as well as general complex couplings) has, and are e.g. important to understand dynamical phases of matter
heyl2013dynamical
;
wei2014phase
;
Sarkar:2023pdf
. On the other hand, pseudo-entropies and their excess has already proved useful for diagnosing various phase transitions so it will be interesting to connect these seemingly distant developments in the future.

One of the main problems on which we made concrete progress with TPE is the relation between its real and imaginary parts provided by the Hilbert transform or the Kramers-Kronig relations. We saw that it holds in all our examples and argued for it based on the decay of generic partition function at large
\beta
or vanishing of thermal entropy in this limit. It would be very interesting to prove this more rigorously and also check which other, more general classes of, pseudo-entropies
Nakata:2021ubr
can be given by analytic functions with KK relations between their real and imaginary parts.

The second feature of the TPE that we found in models with continuous spectrum is the
-(1+\gamma)\log(t)
term. As we saw, it is intimately related to the scaling of the spectral density near the edge. Usually, various observables in this regime of RMT, explore the middle region of the Wigner semi-circle (away from the edges) so it is quite interesting that TPE is sensitive to this information.

More generally, logarithmic terms, often play a role of indicators of universal properties of the model. Since we found it in RMT and the Schwarzian theory (both paradigmatic examples of the quantum chaotic evolution) it is tempting to relate the
\log(t)
to chaotic or scrambling properties of the model. However, as we saw in the CFT examples (see summary Table
1
), this statement should be taken with caution because
\log(t)
may also arise in the decompactification limit of the integrable compact boson CFT. This suggests that its presence or absence may not be a sufficient condition for quantum chaos or distinguishing scrambling from chaos
Xu:2019lhc
;
Caputa:2016tgt
;
Kudler-Flam:2019kxq
. Indeed, we also find that such a logarithmic term is absent in two-dimensional holographic CFTs. Nevertheless, if will be interesting to explore this issue further.

Similarly, we observed the
1/2\log(t)
growth of TPE after the dip in RMT and JT universality class. This is the counterpart of the linear ramp in the SFF and is closely tight with the double cone geometry
Saad:2018bqo
;
Chen:2023hra
. It will be also important to understand this term better from the holographic perspective and the general proposal for pseudo-entropy
Nakata:2021ubr
.

Finally, the connection between averaged TPE and the SFF as well as new examples of SFFs in symmetric orbifold CFTs that we studied naturally align with recent developments on arithmetic chaos, modularity and SFF in rational CFTs

The structure of partition functions in orbifold CFTs governed by the elegant mathematics of Hecke operators should not only be amenable to the analysis in these works but could also provide hints on questions about analytical properties of TPE, the KK relations etc.
