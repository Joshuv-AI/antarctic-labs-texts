Interventions for enhancing child survival practices among caregivers of under-five children in Sub-Saharan Africa: a scoping review
Authors: Grace Ofem Ibor, Victory Oyakhilome, Nnodimele Onuigbo Atulomah
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChild survival is a significant public health concern, especially in low and middle-income countries. Understanding the effectiveness and scope of interventions applied hitherto, in terms of quality, is crucial for developing strategies to reduce under-five mortality.ObjectiveThis scoping review aimed to identify interventions implemented to promote child survival practices among mothers and caregivers of children under five, as well as their outcomes.MethodThe review used Arksey and O’Malley’s methodological framework and adhered to PRISMA-ScR for transparency and rigour. Sub-Saharan African studies published between 2014 and 2024 were searched using electronic databases such as PubMed, Cochrane Library, Web of Science, and Scopus.ResultThe study analyzed 32 studies and found that most were conducted in Ethiopia and Kenya (21.9% respectively). Some others were conducted in Ghana and Malawi (12.5% respectively), Tanzania (9.4%), Nigeria and Uganda (6.3% respectively), and Zambia (3.1%). In terms of study design, cluster randomized controlled trials were most commonly used (31.3%), followed by comparative or experimental interventions (18.8%) and randomized controlled trials (6.3). Merely five (15.6%) studies explicitly utilized a theoretical or conceptual framework to inform the design, execution, or assessment of their interventions. Community-based communication for behaviour change including interpersonal communication, and home visits were the most frequently deployed intervention strategies (56.3%). Media-based approaches (radio, mobile, SMS), nutrition-sensitive agriculture, and Water, Sanitation, and Hygiene (WASH) interventions were each featured in 18.8% and 15.6% of studies, respectively, while health facility- and group-based approaches were the least common (9.4%). The most frequently measured outcome focused on maternal and child health service uptake (25.0%), followed by child growth and development (15.6%), and hygiene and WASH-related behaviours (12.0%). Vaccination demand was the least studied outcome, reported in only one study (3.1%). These findings highlight the prevailing outcomes in improving child health and survival through community health promotion.ConclusionCommunity-based communication for behaviour change, including interpersonal communication and home visits, was the most commonly employed intervention in improving child survival practices among caregivers.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-25627-3.Keywords: Child health, Child survival interventions, Approaches, Outcomes.

Introduction

Globally, under-five morbidity and mortality remain a serious cause for concern that calls for urgent prioritization of child survival practices, especially in Sub-Saharan Africa. This validates its inclusion in the Sustainable Development Goal Agenda [1]. Global under-five mortality decreased from 93 to 37 deaths per 1,000 live births between 1990 and 2022, but significant regional disparities persist [2]. In 2022, over 80% of under-five deaths were in Southern Asia and sub-Saharan Africa, with sub-Saharan Africa having a neonatal mortality rate of 27, roughly eleven times higher than in high-income areas like Australia and New Zealand [2]. These underscore the urgency for targeted interventions to address these inequities and improve child health outcomes in the most affected regions.

Vaccine-preventable infectious diseases and other diseases like malaria, pneumonia, and diarrhea, as well as complications related to preterm birth, birth asphyxia, trauma, and congenital anomalies, remain the leading causes of mortality among children under five years old [2]. The United Nations Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) 3.2 calls for a global commitment to end preventable deaths among newborns and children under the age of five by 2030 through a reduction in under-five mortality. The under-five mortality rate (U5MR) is a measure of the number of children who die before reaching the age of 5, expressed as a ratio of deaths per 1000 live births [3]. It also has a direct correlation with the economic development and income levels of countries. Therefore, low and middle-income countries (LMICs) exhibit high rates due to this cause [1].

On a global scale, an estimated 5.30 million under-five children die annually, primarily due to complications from premature birth (17.7%), infections of the respiratory system (13.9%), labour-related complications (11.6%), and diarrheal diseases (9.1%). Infectious causes, including vaccine-preventable diseases like lower respiratory infections, meningitis, and measles, contribute to nearly half (49.2%) of these deaths, with vaccine-preventable diseases alone accounting for about 21.7% of total under-five mortality [4].

In light of ongoing issues in under-five morbidity and mortality, particularly in Sub-Saharan Africa, a thorough evaluation of child survival measures is required. This is indeed necessary because even though the rate of under-5 mortality around the world has dropped from 93 deaths per 1000 live births in 1990 to 37 in 2022, the regions where a higher population of children is born continue to present contrasting prospects for survival [2]. Southern Asia and sub-Saharan Africa were collectively responsible for approximately four-fifths (80%) of the 4.9 million deaths of children younger than five in 2022. Additionally, with 27 deaths for every 1000 live births [2], sub-Saharan Africa had the highest neonatal mortality rate in 2022, followed by Central and Southern Asia with 21 deaths per 1000 live births. Consequently, the risk of mortality in the initial month of life is eleven times greater for infants born in sub-Saharan Africa compared to those born in the region encompassing Australia and New Zealand [2].

The World Health Organization [3] outlines essential childcare services such as newborn assessment, preventive measures, nutrition, infant growth, and breastfeeding. Despite efforts to implement interventions at health facilities, low utilization rates, mirrored by poor care-seeking behaviours, have led to more household and community-based strategies [5–9].

This scoping review mapped and synthesized interventions implemented across all key child survival measures, focusing on strategies employed to promote child survival practices among mothers of under-five children and their documented outcomes. A preliminary review of existing literature did not identify any comprehensive synthesis encompassing the full spectrum of child survival interventions such as immunization, nutrition, skilled birth attendance, treatment of childhood illnesses, and use of preventive services. Most existing reviews tend to focus on specific aspects, such as nutrition [10], digital health interventions [11], or maternal and newborn health programs [12], without providing a holistic view. This evident gap in evidence synthesis underscores the need for a broad and integrative review to guide policy and programmatic decisions aimed at improving child survival outcomes.

Methodology

In this scoping review, the methodology employed targeted the mapping and synthesis of peer-reviewed literature on interventions for enhancing child survival practices among mothers of under-five children. This review followed a systematic approach to identify, select, analyze, and report findings from relevant studies. The process was informed by the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) [13] and the Arksey and O’Malley framework [14], which involves identifying the research question, identifying relevant studies, selecting studies, charting the data, and collating, summarizing, and reporting the results. An unpublished protocol was developed to guide the study, and ethical approval was not required. By adopting this methodological framework, we ensured adequate examination of the current landscape of interventions geared towards the enhancement of child survival practices.

Defining the research question

To guide the development of the research question and ensure clarity in the search and selection process, the Population Concept Context (PCC) framework was employed. This framework is particularly suitable for scoping reviews, as it supports the exploration of broad research questions and allows for the inclusion of diverse study designs and sources of evidence [14]. Unlike the PICO model, which is more appropriate for systematic reviews and focused on comparing specific interventions and outcomes, the PCC framework provides the flexibility needed to map existing literature across various contexts [15]. In this review, the population includes mothers of under-five children, the concept focuses on interventions that promote child survival practices, and the context encompasses relevant settings where such interventions have been implemented. Accordingly, this review examined: what interventions have been implemented to promote child survival practices among mothers of under-five children, and what are their outcomes?

Identifying relevant literature

To identify the relevant literature, a comprehensive search was conducted using Boolean logic to combine controlled vocabulary (e.g., MeSH terms), and free-text keywords related to the population (e.g., “mothers,” “under-5 children”), child survival practices (e.g., “immunization,” “breast-feeding”), geographic focus (e.g., “Sub-Saharan Africa,” “Nigeria”), intervention types (e.g., “program,” “strategy”), and social-behavioural aspects (e.g., “social support,” “behavioural change”). Four electronic databases: PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science and Cochrane Library were targeted. The search was limited to publications between January 2014 and August 7th, 2024. Study selection

This study used a systematic method to select relevant articles. Initially, studies were imported into EndNote software [16] and then moved to Rayyan software [17] to detect and remove duplicates. When duplicates were found, the most recent version of the article was retained, as it generally provided the most detailed information. Following this, two independent reviewers screened the titles and abstracts of the remaining studies according to predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Data extraction involved systematically collecting key information from each included study. This included study characteristics such as author(s), publication year, geographic location, and study design. Details about the population were recorded, including relevant demographic information. Information on the interventions was also extracted, covering the type of intervention, its duration, and the method of delivery. The outcomes measured in the studies were noted, focusing on child survival practices, maternal behaviours, and related health outcomes. Finally, key findings and conclusions from each study were summarized to capture the overall evidence. To ensure the quality of intervention studies included for the review, secondary studies and studies not found in peer-reviewed journals were excluded.

Data charting

Data extraction was carried out independently, and relevant information from the included articles was organised using Microsoft Excel. Key data fields used for charting included the article title, lead author, year of publication, study location, and research design, among other essential details.

Collation and reporting of results

A descriptive, narrative synthesis was conducted to summarize the characteristics and findings of the included studies: peer-reviewed qualitative and quantitative studies published in English between 2014 and 2024 that involved mothers or caregivers of under-five children, and evaluated interventions promoting child survival practices. The interventions identified were systematically categorized based on their type, target population, and delivery method, which provided insight into the range and focus of existing strategies aimed at promoting child survival practices.

The outcomes assessed across the studies were described in relation to their impact on child survival behaviours, maternal practices, and broader health indicators. Through this process, evidence gaps were also identified, particularly in areas with limited or inconsistent findings. These gaps informed recommendations for future research to address unmet needs and improve the effectiveness of interventions targeting child survival in sub-Saharan Africa.

Results and analysis

Study selection

A total of 315 articles were initially retrieved from four electronic databases: PubMed (n = 29), Scopus (n = 196), Cochrane Library (n = 60), and Web of Science (n = 30). Following the removal of 26 duplicates, 289 records were subjected to independent screening by two reviewers to assess their eligibility. After title and abstract screening, 58 studies were selected for full-text review. Upon further assessment against the inclusion criteria, such as involved mothers or caregivers of under-five children; evaluated interventions promoting child survival practices, such as educational, community-based, social support, or behaviour change strategies;

reported outcomes on maternal behaviour, child survival practices, or related health indicators; and were peer-reviewed qualitative or quantitative studies published in English between 2014 and 2024. These studies also reported outcomes related to maternal behaviour, child survival practices, or associated health outcomes), 32 primary studies were deemed eligible. These studies focused on child survival practices, maternal behaviours, and/or related health outcomes, specifically targeting mothers or caregivers of children under five years in sub-Saharan Africa. Characteristics of included articles

There were 32 articles reviewed, out of which seven studies (21.9%) were conducted in Ethiopia [7, 9, 18–22] and Kenya [23–29]. Ghana [30–33] and Malawi [34–37] contributed four studies (12.5%) respectively, followed by Tanzania with three studies (9.4%) [8, 38, 39]. Nigeria, South Africa, and Uganda recorded two studies (6.3%) each [5, 6, 40–43], while Zambia recorded one study (3.1%) [44]. Regarding study design, the majority of studies (ten, 31.3%) employed cluster randomized controlled trials, followed by six (18.8%) that utilized comparative or experimental interventions, and two (6.3%) that implemented individual randomized controlled trials.

Intervention types included community-based communication for behaviour change, including interpersonal means and home visits, which were reported in 18 studies (56.3%). Media-based interventions (radio, mobile, SMS) were used in five studies (18.8%), while nutrition-sensitive agriculture and WASH interventions featured in five studies (15.6%). Health facility- and group-based approaches were the least common, appearing in three studies (9.4%).

In terms of outcomes measured, most studies (n = 8, 25.0%) focused on maternal and child health as well as service uptake. This was followed by outcomes related to child growth and development (five studies; 15.6%) and hygiene/WASH-related behaviours (four studies; 12.0%). The least commonly measured outcome was vaccination demand, reported in just one study (3.1%). Also, among the 32 studies analysed, merely five (15.6%) explicitly utilized a theoretical or conceptual framework to inform the design, execution, or assessment of their interventions. Discussion

Across the included studies, community-based communication for behaviour change interventions were the most commonly used method, followed by mass media, mobile health (mHealth), and integrated WASH–nutrition programs. This prevalence can be attributed to the practicality and cost-effectiveness of employing community actors, peer groups, and health volunteers to provide interpersonal education and counselling in low-resource environments. Studies from Ethiopia, and Ghana, show that community-based communication for behaviour change techniques work well for a wide range of child survival behaviours, including as feeding infants, keeping them clean, and taking care of their mothers’ health [9, 19, 32]. On the other hand, mass media campaigns and mobile health interventions were less common, probably because of problems with infrastructure and digital literacy in rural areas. There were fewer integrated treatments that coupled community-based communication for behaviour change with other parts, including agriculture or WASH services, but they had larger synergistic benefits on health and nutrition outcomes. In general, people use community-based communication for behaviour change because it fits with the needs of the community and works well with other health programs in sub-Saharan Africa.

More than half of the research (56.3%) was focused on improving infant and young child feeding (IYCF) practices, and several of them reported success. For example, behaviour change communication programs run by community members in Ethiopia made it more likely that complementary feeding would start on time and lowered the risk of starting late by 19% [9]. In the same way, Mobile Health-supported antenatal care in Uganda led to 98% of participants using skilled birth attendants [41], which shows how useful technology-enabled social support models can be. Additionally, media and multi-platform techniques were reported to be effective. A study found that using mass media, mobile phones, and face-to-face contact to assist breastfeeding in Nigeria led to higher rates of exclusive breastfeeding at both 6 and 24 weeks [6]. Another study from Ghana [33] found that radio-based behaviour modification communication helped mothers learn more about how to feed their children.

These results suggest that integrated methods, especially those that use digital tools, mass media, and face-to-face communication, can work together to encourage caregivers to seek health care. These results are in line with what has been seen around the world. For instance, a cluster-randomized experiment in India showed that participatory women’s groups led to a 32% drop in infant mortality by modifying how care was given [45]. In the same way, a big community-based maternal health program in Bangladesh led to significant increases in attendance at prenatal care and skilled birth attendance through home visits and group counseling sessions [46]. The Social Cognitive Theory posits that observational learning, reinforcement, and self-efficacy are important for changing behaviour [47]. These interventions are in line with that theory. Peer modelling and culturally relevant messaging, which are commonly part of community-based solutions, help mothers and caregivers trust and use new behaviours.

The utilization of existing community platforms to disseminate health messages and services was a significant strength observed in numerous interventions. About 56.3% of the studies employed community-based structures, including peer groups, home visits by community health workers (CHWs), or local support groups. These platforms were typically low-cost, culturally pertinent, and integrated within the social structures of the communities, rendering them effective channels for behaviour modification. Peer-led nutrition education via care groups in Uganda and Ethiopia has been shown to enhance maternal well-being and child health outcomes [5, 7].

Community-based structures have produced favourable outcomes in other contexts as well. In Nepal, female community health volunteers have notably enhanced the utilisation of postpartum care and increased awareness of neonatal danger signs [48]. Participatory learning and action groups in Peru have been shown to significantly enhance maternal and newborn care practices [49]. The successes illustrate the application of the Diffusion of Innovations Theory [50], wherein early adopters within a community facilitate the dissemination of health-promoting behaviours via established social networks.

Several studies have implemented multi-sectoral interventions targeting broader determinants of health. Interventions that integrate WASH, nutrition, and agriculture components, for example [19, 24], demonstrated positive outcomes in hygiene practices and child growth. The effectiveness of these integrated models varied based on contextual factors, including implementation fidelity, exposure intensity, and participants’ socio-economic backgrounds. In Guatemala, comparable challenges were observed, with multisectoral strategies yielding mixed outcomes attributed to inconsistent implementation and limited intersectoral synergy [51].

Due to several interrelated factors, such as accessibility (geographic proximity and shorter travel times), affordability (minimal or no out-of-pocket costs), trust and cultural acceptability (local provision by familiar community members), and significant barriers to facility-based care (transportation issues, user fees, perceived inadequate quality or attitude of healthcare workers), community- and home-based strategies are the most common in initiatives aimed at improving child survival and caregiver practices. Systematic reviews demonstrate that Community Health Workers (CHWs) and alternative community models improve outreach and can deliver preventive and promotive care in regions with limited facility coverage [57, 58].

Geographic access remains a key challenge; many rural and peri-urban caregivers must travel long distances and have limited transportation options to reach clinics. Therefore, delivering services within the community or at home makes it easier to access care by reducing travel time and logistical challenges associated with visiting a doctor [59, 60]. The studies reviewed consistently highlight home visits, CHV/CHW outreach, or care-group visits as the primary modes of delivery, for example [9, 32, 43]. Multiple studies show that home-based approaches are linked to improved feeding practices, earlier initiation of supplemental feeding, and fewer instances of diarrhea and fever, supporting the geographic access theory.

In the case of affordability, community-based methods often eliminate or reduce both direct and indirect costs, such as transit fees, opportunity costs of time, or informal donations, making it easier for people to utilize services. Studies examining access issues in sub-Saharan Africa consistently identify cost and transportation as major barriers preventing women and children from accessing healthcare facilities [60, 61]. Some studies in this review also revealed that cost-effective interventions like radio behaviour change communication, SMS reminder systems [41], and peer support groups [23] improved knowledge and increased service uptake without requiring visits to facilities, illustrating how affordability influences the choice and success of interventions. Additionally, community agents excel at influencing behaviour because they are trusted, proximate, and culturally open-minded. Community stakeholders such as trusted neighbours, peer mothers, or community health workers can tailor messages to fit local norms, address sensitive behaviours, and harness social networks to promote new practices [57]. Many of the studies in this review employed peer-led, care-group, or community health worker models, all showing improvements in breastfeeding, hygiene, and vaccination rates, e.g [5, 22, 40]. These findings demonstrate that local actors can foster relationships and remain engaged even when facility-based personnel are not trusted or well-known.

Furthermore, caregivers often prefer community options for routine preventive care and counseling due to issues with facility-based care, including perceived low quality, staff shortages, absenteeism, disrespect, and limited supplies [59, 62]. The reviewed literature provides examples of integrated facility-community models addressing facility shortcomings [8] and highlights that community delivery can be the most effective method for behaviour change, especially when facility-based interventions are impractical or experience low attendance [38, 39]. Ultimately, programmatic and policy factors support community approaches: lower initial costs for outreach, donors’ preference for scalable community platforms, and national strategies emphasizing task-shifting to community health workers all contribute to increased community-based research [58, 63]. However, evaluations indicate that Community Health Worker (CHW) programs require ongoing support, oversight, and integration with health systems to function effectively at scale. Without these elements, successful small-scale initiatives may not be replicable [63]. The importance of consistent follow-up and integration is also emphasized in this review by [24] and [18].

Even though most of the results were good, some studies revealed limited or mixed results. This shows that there are still problems with designing and delivering interventions. For instance, the social network intervention in Kenya [23] didn’t make IYCF indicators much better, partly because the intervention and control groups weren’t very similar. Another study [38] also reported that cleanliness promotion initiatives in Tanzania did not lead to better health outcomes for children under five, even though they did improve hygiene indices. These examples show how important it is to deal with structural and contextual limitations, including group diversity, limited geographical coverage, and the inadequate time spent in the program, which can make it less effective.

South Asia has seen similar problems. Community midwife initiatives in Pakistan did not raise the number of skilled deliveries because of insufficient monitoring, logistical problems, and a lack of trust in the community [52]. In Indonesia, local health posts (Posyandu) had trouble keeping up maternal care services because volunteers weren’t very motivated and the posts weren’t well integrated with the formal health system [53]. These studies show how important it is to keep building capacity, give frontline staff incentives, and set up support systems in institutions. Some interventions also had problems with message fatigue and diminishing participation over time, especially those that used SMS or mobile platforms [27]. Process evaluations also showed that the intervention didn’t reach as many people as intended, and in certain situations, crucial factors that affect behaviour change, such as societal norms, maternal autonomy, or economic restrictions, weren’t addressed well enough.

Furthermore, this review reveals a significant deficiency in the application of theoretical frameworks. Among the 32 studies analysed, merely five (15.6%) explicitly utilised a conceptual or theoretical framework such as the Health Belief Model [19, 21] Risks, Attitudes, Norms, Abilities, and Self-Regulation (RANAS) model [35], Reach, Effectiveness, Adoption, Implementation, and Maintenance (RE-AIM) framework [8], Economic Utility Theory [34] and Theory of Change; Linnan & Steckler Process Evaluation Model [44] to inform intervention design, execution, or evaluation. The remaining 84.4% lacked a coherent theoretical framework, especially with behaviour change or implementation science. Although certain participatory methods, such as Trials of Improved Practices (TIPs) or analytical instruments like Structural Equation Modeling (SEM) were utilized, they do not replace the necessity for theory-based guiding. The lack of theory diminishes the rigour, replicability, and contextual significance of interventions. Authors like [54, 55] underscore the necessity of theoretical frameworks for comprehending health behaviours and for the methodical design, precise targeting, and sustainability of interventions.

The results all point to the importance of targeted, community-based measures for improving the health of mothers and children. Policymakers and program designers should consider expanding successful models, especially those that use behaviour change communication, community health worker home visits, and communication through multiple channels. However, scaling up must be done with strong monitoring and evaluation mechanisms that are aware of the realities of the situation and can spot problems with implementation early on.

Future initiatives should also concentrate on involving everyone by modifying plans to accommodate the requirements of populations that have limited access to related services, like underprivileged urban regions. There is also a strong need for more thorough mixed-methods and impact evaluations to find out not only how well interventions work, but also how they bring about change. Global evaluations [56] reveal that successful scale-up depends not only on “what works” but also on “how it works” in each situation.

Furthermore, Governments can sustainably improve child survival by integrating community- and home-based strategies into existing national child health programs through a multi-tiered health systems approach. Integrating community health workers (CHWs) into the primary healthcare (PHC) framework through recruitment, standardized training, equitable compensation, and oversight ensures reliable service delivery at the community level and enhances data connectivity with healthcare facilities [56–58]. Also, adding community engagement platforms like care groups, mother-to-mother support networks, and peer educators to regular health promotion activities can help build trust and keep care going while also improving institutional services [61, 63]. Including digital and mobile health tools such as SMS notifications and mobile apps for CHWs in the National Primary Health Care Development Agency (NPHCDA) will make home-based counselling, referrals, and follow-up more effective and efficient. Also, funding policy such as setting aside funds in the national budget for community-based projects, giving performance-based bonuses, and adding CHWs reporting to the national Health Management Information System (HMIS) will improve coordination and accountability. To deal with structural problems, including poverty, gender inequality, and transportation, ministries and partners will need to work together. This will make sure that community- and home-based interventions support, rather than replace, fair access to quality facility-based care.

Conclusion

This review highlights that community-based communication for behaviour change, including interpersonal communication and home visits, was the most commonly employed intervention in improving child survival practices among caregivers. It also contributes to the growing evidence that community-based communication for behaviour change interventions can improve maternal and child health outcomes across sub-Saharan Africa. To strengthen impact and equity, programs should scale up integrated BCC + WASH interventions, prioritize vaccination demand-generation strategies, and embed behavioural theory in program design to enhance effectiveness and scalability. Governments and partners should invest in underrepresented regions, particularly Central and West Africa, and reinforce community health worker platforms within primary healthcare systems to deliver home-based counselling, hygiene promotion, and early referral. Future research should deepen theoretical grounding, explore context-specific delivery innovations, and evaluate sustainability and cost-effectiveness. Advancing these priorities will help accelerate progress toward equitable child survival and the attainment of universal health coverage in sub-Saharan Africa.

Strengths and limitations

There are a number of good things about this review. It concentrated on intervention studies that had been thoroughly reviewed, with a large number using cluster-randomized controlled trial designs, which made the results more reliable and valid. The synthesis gives us useful information about the kinds of interventions, delivery methods, and health outcomes that are often looked at in sub-Saharan Africa. It also shows patterns in methods and areas of attention in different regions, which can help in planning future programs and making policies. This review also contributes significantly by highlighting the need for greater theoretical integration in community-based public health interventions. By drawing attention to this underutilized area, it encourages future research and program designers to anchor their interventions in appropriate behavioural, social, or implementation theories. In doing so, they will not only strengthen intervention design but also improve explanatory power, replicability, and impact assessment.

The review does have some problems, though. The search was limited to four peer-reviewed publications, which may have left out studies that were published in other places, such as region-specific or multidisciplinary journals. Also, grey literature and unpublished program assessments were not looked at, which could have left out new or large-scale programs run by governments, NGOs, or development partners. The studies were also not evenly spread out across the region, with only a few nations being well represented. This made it hard to apply the results to the whole region. Also, the many types of interventions, outcome measures, and study designs made it harder to do a meta-analysis or compare trials directly.

Recommendation

Future research should aim to broaden the geographic scope of intervention studies by including underrepresented countries in sub-Saharan Africa, ensuring a more comprehensive regional understanding. There is also a need to expand the evidence base by incorporating grey literature and program evaluations from NGOs and government agencies to capture innovative and scalable interventions. Researchers should consider standardizing outcome measures to facilitate cross-study comparisons and enable meta-analyses. Future research should use theoretical frameworks to develop and evaluate interventions. This improves clarity, interpretability, and replicability. Researchers should also integrate TIPs and SEM into larger theoretical paradigms rather than depending on them alone.

Additionally, more attention should be given to evaluating interventions that target vaccination demand and health facility-based service delivery, as these areas remain underexplored. Finally, strengthening multi-sectorial approaches that integrate WASH, nutrition, and health services, and leveraging digital platforms for health promotion, could enhance the effectiveness and sustainability of community-based interventions.
