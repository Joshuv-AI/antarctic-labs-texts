Immune Dysregulation and Viral Persistence in HPV-HIV Coinfection: Oncogenic and Clinical Implications
Authors: Mitra Sadrkhanlou, Hossein Nasouti Aghjehroud, Zahra Nikkhooy, Zahra Sadat Mousavian Hiagh, Sobhan Aboulhassanzadeh, Hamed Aghazadeh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionHuman papillomavirus (HPV) associated cancers constitute a major global health concern, particularly among people living with human immunodeficiency virus (HIV). Accumulating evidence suggests that HPV-HIV co-infection substantially increases the risk of genital, cervical, and oral cancers; however, the molecular and immunological mechanisms underlying this association remain incompletely elucidated.ObjectiveTo critically review the current evidence regarding HPV-HIV interactions, focusing on immunological synthesis, translation, clinical implications, and development of a comprehensive prevention strategy for viral persistence and carcinogenesis.Research MethodsThe research methods employed were a narrative review of the literature through the use of major scientific databases such as PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science, searching for articles up to the end of December 2024. Narrative synthesis of relevant studies on HPV-HIV coinfection, immune dysregulation, viral persistence, and cancer development focused on molecular, immunological, and clinical mechanisms. Limitations of this narrative review are that it may suffer from selection bias and does not include a quantitative meta-analysis.ResultsThere is epidemiological evidence that HPV-related genital, cervical, and oral cancers occur earlier, are more aggressive, and are much more common in HIV-infected persons. Specifically, clinical data have shown up to a six-fold increase in HPV persistence and an eighteen-fold increase in risk of infection among people who have low levels of CD4⁺ T-cells. Moreover, the prevalence of oral HPV is 2–6 times greater, reaching up to 32% in HIV positive cohorts and 16% in HIV negative controls. This quantitatively greater burden of persistent high-risk HPV infection is associated with immune dysfunction, which includes the depletion and dysfunction of CD4+ T cells and dendritic cells, resulting in impaired mucosal immune surveillance. Persistent HPV infection promotes oncogenic transformation through E6/E7-mediated inactivation of tumor suppressor pathways, including p53 and retinoblastoma protein. Chronic inflammation, epithelial barrier disruption, and altered cytokine signaling further contribute to a pro-oncogenic microenvironment.ConclusionHPV-HIV coinfection is a biologically synergistic disease condition, which greatly increases the risk of oral, genital, and cervical cancers. A better understanding of this interaction on a mechanistic basis is crucial for optimizing prevention strategies. For HIV-positive populations, the practical recommendations include increased screening for cervical and anal HPV, strict implementation of HPV vaccination, and monitoring of the oral/oropharyngeal HPV in high-risk groups.

Introduction

Cancer research has classified both human papillomavirus (HPV) and human immunodeficiency virus type 1 (HIV-1) as carcinogenic agents, with HPV recognized as a direct carcinogen and HIV-1 as an indirect carcinogen primarily through immune suppression mechanisms.1 HPV and HIV are known to substantially increase the risk of genital and sexually transmitted disorders, particularly among people living with HIV, whose immune defenses are compromised.2 HIV mediated immunodeficiency increases host susceptibility to a wide range of opportunistic infections, including persistent HPV infection,3 thereby facilitating viral entry, replication, and the initiation of oncogenic processes.4 HPV is capable of infecting epithelial cells of the oral and anogenital mucosa, where viral genome integration into host DNA disrupts cellular regulatory pathways and promotes malignant transformation.5 The HPV genome consists of three major regions: a non-coding long control region (LCR) that regulates viral replication and transcription, and two coding regions comprising eight open reading frames (ORFs). These include six early genes (E1, E2, E4, E5, E6, and E7) and two late genes (L1 and L2). Early proteins E1 and E2 regulate the viral life cycle and genome replication, E4 modulates early gene products, while E6 and E7 play a central role in carcinogenesis by disrupting cell cycle control and tumor suppressor pathways.6 Individuals infected with HIV who exhibit reduced CD4⁺ T-cell counts are particularly vulnerable to multiple HPV infections, prolonged viral persistence, and an elevated risk of cervical intraepithelial lesions. In cervical cancer, HPV has been shown to induce loss of heterozygosity and microsatellite instability (LOH/MSI) at the HLA-I DNA locus, a phenomenon that is further aggravated by HIV-1 co-infection.7 Notably, significantly higher frequencies of LOH/MSI at the HLA-I locus on chromosome 6p21.21 have been observed in tumor DNA from HIV-1/HPV co-infected patients compared with patients infected with HIV-1 alone.8 Integration of HPV DNA into the host genome represents a critical step in viral driven oncogenesis, leading to abnormal cellular replication and malignant progression.9 Consequently, people living with HIV are at a substantially increased risk of developing HPV associated cervical, vaginal, penile, and anal cancers. When these advanced malignancies necessitate surgical intervention, the procedures demand an optimized operating room tailored for precise execution to prevent inadvertent occupational viral transmission to the surgical team.10,11 The aggressive clinical course that ultimately leads to such complex surgical needs is predominantly driven by the underlying viral genotype. In particular, high risk human papillomaviruses (HR-HPVs), including HPV types 16, 18, 31, 33, 35, 45, 52, and 58, are responsible for more than 90% of invasive cervical cancer cases worldwide. The prevalence of cervical HPV infection is highest in sub–Saharan Africa, followed by Latin America and the Caribbean, Eastern Europe, and Southeast Asia, with reported rates of 24%, 16%, 14%, and 14%, respectively.12 Persistent infection with high-risk HPV genotypes, particularly HPV-16 and HPV-18, markedly increases cancer risk in immunocompromised populations.13 Moreover, low CD4⁺ T-cell counts have been associated with a six-fold increase in HPV persistence and up to an eighteen-fold increase in infection risk.14,15 This heightened susceptibility underscores the necessity of regular genital and oral screening among people living with HIV and other high-risk populations, including individuals with immunodeficiency, smokers, alcohol users, and those with multiple sexual partners. Given the prolonged asymptomatic period of HPV-associated malignancies and the tendency for diagnosis at advanced stages, early detection through systematic screening is critical. The risk for HPV-related cancers is still high in individuals with HIV, despite the use of antiretroviral therapy (ART). This ongoing vulnerability is due to a lack of understanding of the exact mucosal and immunological deficits that remain after ART.16 To cope with this complex overload, the specific aim of this review is to offer a focused immunological synthesis of HPV-HIV coinfection in genital (cervical, penile, anal, vaginal, vulvar) and oral/oropharyngeal sites. Keeping the translational perspective, we will connect the existing molecular mechanisms with clinically relevant implications and highlight a comprehensive prevention strategy for this high-risk population.

Research Methods

Study Design

The study was carried out as a comprehensive narrative review (semi-systematic review). As the primary goal was to synthesize broad mechanistic and clinical concepts, rather than to do a quantitative meta-analysis, strict systematic review protocols (PRISMA flow diagram, formal reviewer agreement metrics, selection bias mitigation, quantitative quality appraisal tools) were not used. The purpose of the study instead was to critically examine the available evidence on the interaction between human papillomavirus (HPV) and human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) in the development of genital, cervical, and oral cancers, with a special emphasis on immunological and molecular mechanisms involved in viral persistence and carcinogenesis.

Literature Search Strategy

Three major electronic databases (PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science) were used to conduct a comprehensive and structured literature search. Only peer-reviewed articles in English up to December 2024 were searched. The basic search strategy was designed to be reproducible, using a combination of keywords, MeSH terms, and title/abstract equivalents, with the use of Boolean logic (AND, OR) to connect the search terms. Eligibility Criteria

Studies were considered eligible if they met one or more of the following criteria:

(i) investigated HPV and HIV co-infection; (ii) evaluated the risk of HPV-associated genital, cervical, or oral cancers in HIV-infected populations; (iii) examined immunological, molecular, or cellular mechanisms related to viral persistence and carcinogenesis; or (iv) reported epidemiological data on HPV prevalence or cancer burden in people living with HIV. Case reports, conference abstracts, non-English publications, and studies lacking relevance to cancer outcomes were excluded.

Data Extraction and Synthesis

Relevant data were extracted from included studies, including study design, population characteristics, HPV genotypes, immune parameters (eg, CD4⁺ T-cell counts), molecular findings, and cancer-related outcomes. The extracted information was qualitatively synthesized and organized into thematic sections focusing on immune dysregulation, viral persistence, oncogenic pathways, and clinical implications of HPV-HIV co-infection.

Analytical Approach

Rather than quantitative pooling, evidence was interpreted through comparative and mechanistic analysis to highlight converging biological pathways and clinically relevant patterns. Particular emphasis was placed on studies elucidating how HIV-induced immune dysfunction facilitates persistent high-risk HPV infection and accelerates malignant transformation.

Study Selection Process

Inclusion of articles in the study selection process was transparently justified based on the structured screening phases. The screening process and the attrition of records are described in detail in the PRISMA flow diagram (Figure 1). After the first database search, 4,547 records were found (PubMed: 1,412, Scopus: 1,669, Web of Science: 1,466). A total of 2,858 articles were used for the first screening after 1,689 duplicate articles were removed. The titles and abstracts of these records were screened independently, and 2,536 studies were excluded because they were not directly relevant to the dual mechanisms or clinical outcomes of HPV-HIV coinfection. Then all the remaining 322 articles were requested for full-text retrieval, and 9 full texts were not available. The remaining 313 full-text articles were carefully reviewed according to the predetermined inclusion criteria. In the end, 236 studies were excluded, mainly because they did not address the specific mechanistic focus and/or because the studies did not have a clear clinical relevance for cancer outcomes. This process led to the identification of 77 highly relevant studies that were included in this narrative synthesis. Overview of Human Papillomavirus Infection

Human papillomaviruses (HPVs) belong to the family Papillomaviridae and comprise more than 200 genotypes. These viruses are broadly classified into low-risk types, which are primarily associated with benign lesions such as cutaneous and genital warts, and high-risk HPVs (HR-HPVs), which are strongly linked to the development of anogenital and oropharyngeal cancers.17–19 Based on genomic organization, HPVs are categorized into five major genera,20 among which HPV16 and HPV18 account for approximately 70% of cervical cancer cases worldwide.21 In addition to cervical malignancies, HPV DNA has been frequently detected in oropharyngeal squamous cell carcinomas.22 HPV infection is commonly diagnosed through the detection of viral DNA in cervical samples. Due to the dynamic nature of the cervical epithelium and continuous epithelial turnover, many HPV infections are transient and resolve spontaneously. However, epidemiological studies indicate that 3.3–99.4% of women who clear a specific HPV genotype may experience reinfection with the same genotype.23,24 Cyclic patterns of HPV detection,25 increased prevalence during periods of immunosuppression,26 and repeated viral reactivation have been reported,27 underscoring the importance of host immune status in HPV persistence.

The Human Papillomavirus Life Cycle

The HPV life cycle is tightly linked to the terminal differentiation program of keratinocytes within stratified squamous epithelium. Infection is initiated in basal keratinocytes and progresses as infected cells migrate toward the upper, differentiated layers of the epithelium, where viral assembly and release occur (Figure 2). Viral gene expression and genome replication are precisely regulated according to the host cell differentiation state.28 Following entry into the nucleus, the viral genome is maintained as episomal DNA at low copy numbers. Early viral genes, particularly E1, E2, E6, and E7, play critical roles in viral genome replication and transcriptional regulation.29 As keratinocytes differentiate, viral chromatin undergoes epigenetic remodeling, resulting in activation of the late viral promoter and subsequent transcription of capsid proteins L1 and L2, along with amplification of the viral genome.30 In the upper epithelial layers, newly formed virions are assembled and released. HPV oncoproteins, especially E6 and E7, contribute to immune evasion by interfering with innate immune signaling, thereby facilitating persistent infection.31 In some cases, particularly with high-risk genotypes such as HPV16, viral DNA integrates into the host genome, leading to deregulated expression of E6 and E7 and promoting malignant transformation.32 Figure 2Life cycle of human papillomavirus (HPV) and epithelial differentiation. Epidemiology and Prevalence of HPV Infection in Men and Women

Men play a crucial role in HPV transmission and can act as viral reservoirs, contributing to sustained circulation of the virus within populations.33 HPV infection in men is associated with several malignancies, including penile, anal, and oropharyngeal cancers, as well as recurrent respiratory papillomatosis. Sexually transmitted HPV infection is well established, and male HPV infection significantly influences the risk of HPV-associated diseases in female partners. Studies indicate that up to 68% of male sexual partners of infected women may present with penile HPV lesions.34 Moreover, HPV prevalence and persistence patterns differ between men and women, highlighting sex-specific epidemiological dynamics.

Genomic Organization of Human Papillomavirus

HPV is a small, non-enveloped, double-stranded DNA virus with a genome of approximately 8 kb. The genome contains eight open reading frames (ORFs), including early genes (E1, E2, E4, E5, E6, and E7) and late genes (L1 and L2). Early genes regulate viral replication, transcription, and host cell manipulation, whereas L1 and L2 encode the structural capsid proteins essential for virion assembly.35 Transcription of the HPV genome is controlled by two major promoters: an early promoter located in the long control region that regulates E6 and E7 expression, and a late promoter within the E7 gene that controls expression of E1, E2, E4, E5, L1, and L2.35 HPV types and subtypes are classified based on nucleotide sequence divergence, particularly within the L1 ORF, with a difference greater than 10% defining a new HPV type.36 As of 2022, more than 200 HPV genotypes have been identified.37

Immune Evasion Mechanisms of HPV in Carcinogenesis

HPV infection and immune evasion are influenced by host genetic factors, immune status, and the local tissue microenvironment. HPV reprograms host cellular machinery to support viral replication and is released from terminally differentiated epithelial cells without inducing significant cytolysis, thereby limiting immune detection.38 In persistent infections, expression of viral oncoproteins induces genomic instability, disrupts cell-cycle regulation, and promotes the formation of intraepithelial neoplasia, particularly in cervical tissue.39 The E6 and E7 oncoproteins inactivate key tumor suppressors, including p53 and retinoblastoma protein (pRb). Loss of p53 and pRb function results in uncontrolled cell proliferation, impaired apoptosis, and chromosomal instability, all of which are hallmarks of malignant transformation.40 HPV persistence is further facilitated by the virus’s ability to avoid robust immune activation (Figure 3), allowing reactivation under conditions such as hormonal changes or immunosuppression.41 The prolonged persistence of HPV16 compared with other genotypes is strongly associated with high-grade lesions and malignant progression.41 Failure to mount an effective cellular immune response, including HPV-specific T-cell responses, contributes to the accumulation of genetic alterations and progression to invasive cancer.42 Figure 3Immunosuppressive and cell cycle-regulatory mechanisms of HPV mediated immune evasion. Role of Immunodeficiency in HPV Persistence and Cancer Progression

Immunodeficiency is one of the most significant risk factors for severe HPV associated malignancies. Immunodeficiency can be categorized into primary immunodeficiency disorders, secondary immunodeficiency, and acquired immunodeficiency, most notably HIV infection. Among these, HIV associated immunodeficiency plays a central role in the pathogenesis and progression of HPV-related neoplasms.43

Host Immune Responses to HPV in Cervical Carcinogenesis

CD4⁺ T lymphocytes are critical for immune control of HPV infection, particularly in HPV-positive lesions. Patients with HPV-associated disease exhibit elevated levels of HPV16 E6 and E7 specific CD4⁺ T cells.44,45 In individuals living with HIV, reduced CD4⁺ T-cell counts are associated with increased susceptibility to multiple HPV infections, prolonged viral persistence, and a higher incidence of cervical intraepithelial neoplasia.46 Patients with cervical intraepithelial neoplasia display impaired Th1 immune responses, characterized by reduced production of interferon-γ and interleukin-2. The absence of these cytokines is linked to persistent HPV infection and progression to high-grade lesions. Additionally, Th17 cells have been implicated in cervical carcinogenesis, with increased Th17 infiltration correlating with progression to invasive cervical cancer.47,48

HPV Associated Oral and Oropharyngeal Cancers

HPV plays a significant role in the development of oral and oropharyngeal cancers. While tobacco use and alcohol consumption remain major risk factors for oral malignancies, HPV is responsible for approximately 33–72% of oropharyngeal cancers and around 10% of laryngeal cancers.49 The incidence of HPV-associated head and neck cancers has increased markedly in recent years.50 High risk HPV genotypes detected in head and neck cancers include HPV16, 18, 26, 31, 33, 35, 39, 45, 51–53, 56, 58, 59, 66, 68, and 70. In immunocompetent individuals, up to 80% of oral HPV infections resolve spontaneously within 6–20 months. However, HPV16 infections exhibit slower clearance, and a subset of persistent infections progresses to head and neck squamous cell carcinoma.51

Molecular and Epidemiological Interactions Between HIV and HPV in Cancer Development

Integration of HPV DNA into host cellular genomes represents a pivotal event in HPV-driven carcinogenesis.52 The viral oncoproteins E6 and E7 disrupt p53 and pRb-regulated pathways, leading to loss of cell cycle control and malignant transformation.53–55 HIV infection significantly exacerbates these processes. Epidemiological data indicate that the prevalence of penile HPV infection is markedly higher in HIV-positive individuals compared with seronegative populations.56 HIV infection independently increases the risk of HPV associated malignancies and is associated with a four-fold increase in cancer-related mortality. Moreover, progression from intraepithelial lesions to invasive cancer occurs at a younger age in HIV-positive individuals.57 HPV infection may also influence HIV replication, as extracellular vesicles released by HPV-infected cervical cancer cells enhance HIV-1 replication through oxidative stress, creating a pathogenic feedback loop between the two viruses.58

Impact of HIV on p53 Signaling in HPV Driven Carcinogenesis

HIV-Mediated Disruption of Epithelial Barriers Facilitating HPV Infection

Stratified squamous epithelium forms a critical physical barrier in the oral cavity, cervix, and genital tract.63 HIV interacts with mucosal epithelial cells through gp120 and Tat proteins, leading to disruption of tight junctions and epithelial integrity.64 This damage facilitates HPV access to basal keratinocytes, where productive infection is initiated. As a result, HIV enhances penetration of HPV virions into epithelial tissues, increasing viral persistence and the risk of HPV-associated malignancies. Notably, epithelial barrier disruption has been observed in approximately 60% of individuals living with HIV, supporting a direct role for HIV in promoting HPV-driven carcinogenesis.65

Immune Dysregulation During HIV-HPV Co-Infection

The interaction between HIV and HPV profoundly alters host immune responses, contributing to increased cancer risk in co-infected individuals. HIV-induced immunosuppression promotes HPV persistence, which is a key determinant of malignant progression.66 Individuals living with HIV exhibit reduced frequency and functionality of HPV-specific T-cell responses, particularly against E6 and E7 antigens.67,68 This immune impairment is more pronounced in advanced HIV disease and is exacerbated by inadequate antiretroviral treatment, further increasing susceptibility to HPV-associated cancers.

Dendritic Cell Dysfunction in HIV-HPV Co-Infected Cervical Tissues

Dendritic cells (DCs) are essential mediators of innate and adaptive immunity and play a critical role in the initial response to viral infections.69 In cervical tissues from individuals co-infected with HIV and HPV, increased expression of HLA-DR has been observed, suggesting enhanced antigen presentation but impaired immune coordination.70 Although the number of CD1a⁺ DCs is increased, many of these cells display an immature phenotype characterized by low expression of CD83 and CD86, limiting their ability to initiate effective T-cell responses.71 HIV further disrupts DC function by promoting immunosuppressive signaling through the PD-1/PD-L1 pathway, leading to cytotoxic T-lymphocyte exhaustion and reduced tumor surveillance.70,72 Additionally, HPV-infected cervical tumor cells inhibit DC migration to lymph nodes by downregulating CCR7 and inducing MMP-9 expression, thereby promoting tumor progression, particularly in immunocompromised HIV-positive patients.73

In order to gain a deeper mechanistic understanding of these overlapping pathways, it is important not to consider HIV-HPV coinfection as simply parallel viral lifecycles, but rather as a highly synergistic oncogenic cascade. The pathogenesis starts with disruption of the epithelial barrier by HIV (through gp120 and Tat) that allows deeper penetration of HPV into basal keratinocytes. At the same time, the systemic and local loss of CD4⁺ T-cells is accompanied by PD-1/PD-L1 induced dendritic cell exhaustion, resulting in an immunologically “cold” mucosal microenvironment devoid of effective viral surveillance. HIV-Tat directly upregulates HPV E6/E7 expression in this permissive niche. This initiates a vicious cycle: the enhanced viral oncoproteins strongly inactivate p53 and pRb, creating strong genomic instability; extracellular vesicles and localized oxidative stress by the HPV-infected cells further stimulate HIV replication. Finally, this complex interplay of structural damage, immune exhaustion, and transcriptional deregulation fuels the rapid and aggressive malignant transformation that is unique to coinfected patients.

HIV-HPV Co-Infection and Oral Carcinogenesis

HIV co infection significantly increases the risk of HPV associated oral and oropharyngeal cancers. Individuals living with HIV have a 2–6 fold higher risk of developing HPV related head and neck malignancies, and the prevalence of oral HPV infection is substantially higher than in the general population.74 Despite this, the precise determinants of oral HPV persistence and viral dynamics in HIV positive individuals remain incompletely understood. Clinical studies report that approximately 32% of HIV positive patients harbor oral HPV compared with 16% of HIV negative individuals.75 Additional evidence confirms an elevated risk of oral HPV infection among HIV-positive adults, highlighting the synergistic role of HIV-induced immunosuppression in oral HPV persistence and carcinogenesis.76

The Nuanced Impact of Antiretroviral Therapy (ART)

Although ART is now universally recommended to suppress the virus and to restore systemic immune function, its prevention of HPV-driven oncogenesis is very complex. Mechanistic evidence suggests ART does not fully restore mucosal immunity or tissue-resident memory T-cells in the anogenital and oral tracts.78 Moreover, high-risk HPV DNA integration into the host genome and epigenetic modification of tumor suppressor pathways will persist even as ART suppresses HIV replication, provided that the viral genome is present. In addition, the pro-oncogenic modifications of the host genome that occur during integration of the high-risk HPV genome and epigenetic modification of tumor suppressor pathways will be permanent, even if ART is effective at suppressing HIV replication. This deeper mechanistic integration is the reason why ART-treated people continue to be at risk for cervical and anal dysplasia and why clinical guidelines recommend ongoing high-resolution anoscopy and cervical cytology even if an individual’s HIV viral load is undetectable.

Vaccination is the most powerful HPV-associated cancer-prophylactic intervention available (Figure 5). The immunogenicity of HPV vaccines is, however, modified in immunocompromised persons. International guidelines (including CDC and ASCO) call for a full 3-dose series of the nonavalent HPV vaccine for all people living with HIV, regardless of gender or age at initiation;79 however, emerging global guidelines for immunocompetent youth also favor single-dose or two-dose schedules. This is an intensive treatment, as HIV-associated immune dysregulation reduces the longevity and magnitude of neutralizing antibodies. These vaccines are currently under ongoing clinical trials to assess their long-term seroprotection in patients with low CD4/CD8 ratios, to develop the best immunization strategy for the future. Critical Assessment of Current Evidence and Conflicting Findings

Although great strides have been made in the understanding of HPV-HIV coinfection, there are several areas of the literature that lack sufficient evidence and have conflicting results. One of its most significant drawbacks is that the mechanistic information about HPV and HIV cellular interactions, such as the HIV-Tat-mediated upregulation of HPV E6, is largely based on in vitro models or small cross-sections. The validation of these models in vivo is incomplete, with a lack of robust validation in the human mucosal tumor microenvironment.

In addition, there are contrasting results on the effect of antiretroviral therapy (ART) on the clearance of HPV and oncogenesis. Although long-term viral suppression is expected to lead to systemic immune reconstitution and thus to HPV clearance, paradoxically, epidemiological data show that the risk of some HPV-related cancers, such as anal cancer, is still disproportionately high and is increasing in people living with HIV. This paradox is actively discussed, and researchers have proposed that it could be because of improved survival of patients, incomplete resolution of local mucosal immune dysfunction after systemic resolution, or because of the permanent nature of the epigenetic changes caused by HPV infection before the start of ART.

In addition, conflicting data are also found in the immunological literature regarding particular immune subsets. For instance, although Th17 cells are classically regarded as integral to the mucosal immune system defense against pathogens, paradoxically, these cells have been shown to promote tumor pathogenesis by driving chronic inflammation and angiogenesis in the later stages of cervical carcinogenesis.47,48 The discrepancies underline the need for large-scale, longitudinal studies that sufficiently account for confounding factors like smoking, concurrent STIs, and long-term ART adherence patterns.

Strengths and Limitations of the Review

Strengths. This review integrates epidemiological and clinical observations with mechanistic insights to clarify how HIV-associated immune dysregulation promotes high-risk HPV persistence and accelerates oncogenic transformation across anogenital and oral sites. Emphasis on convergent pathways particularly impaired immune surveillance and HPV E6/E7-mediated disruption of tumor suppressor signaling provides a coherent biological framework that supports risk-stratified screening and prevention strategies in people living with HIV. Limitations. As a narrative review, the study does not apply a meta-analytic approach and is therefore not intended to estimate pooled effect sizes. The available literature is heterogeneous with respect to study design, population characteristics, ART exposure, HPV genotyping methods, and outcome definitions, which may limit direct comparability across reports. Additionally, causality cannot be inferred from predominantly observational data, and residual confounding (eg, smoking, sexual behavior, and co-infections) may influence reported associations. These limitations underscore the need for standardized protocols and prospective, well controlled studies.

Future Perspectives and Research Directions

Future work should prioritize prospective longitudinal cohorts that jointly profile HIV disease status (including CD4⁺ counts and ART history) and HPV dynamics (type-specific acquisition, persistence, clearance, and integration) across cervical, anogenital, and oral mucosa. Mechanistic studies integrating multi omics (viral genomics, host transcriptomics/epigenomics, and immune phenotyping) are needed to clarify how immune suppression reshapes the tumor microenvironment and facilitates HPV-driven genomic instability. Clinically, trials evaluating optimized screening intervals, site-specific testing strategies (including oral HPV monitoring in high-risk groups), and vaccination effectiveness in people living with HIV are warranted. Finally, implementation research should address barriers to vaccination and screening uptake in high-burden regions to reduce HPV-attributable cancers in HIV-affected populations.

Conclusion

Finally, HPV-HIV coinfection has a synergistic effect on the risk of anogenital and/or oropharyngeal cancer. Immune system dysfunction (due to HIV infection) leads to immune exhaustion (specifically of dendritic cells) and CD4+ T-cell depletion, which reduces the capacity for mucous surveillance, permitting the high-risk HPV to promote oncogenesis through E6/E7-induced disruption of tumor suppressor pathways. Although ART is widespread, this increased cancer risk remains and requires individual clinical management, including targeted vaccination and site-specific screening. The conclusions are, however, limited by the heterogeneity of the available observational data, unmeasured confounding factors (such as smoking and sexual activity), and by the absence of large-scale, prospective cohorts with a standardized ART definition to assess long-term ART-associated mucosal immunity. To optimize and risk-stratify guidelines for persons living with HIV, future research is needed to address these critical evidence gaps.
