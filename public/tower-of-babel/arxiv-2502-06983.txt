Riemann-Skorohod and Stratonovich integrals for Gaussian processes
Yanghui Liu

Abstract
. In this paper we consider Skorohod and Stratonovich-type integrals in a general setting of Gaussian processes. We show that a conversion formula holds when the covariance functions of the Gaussian process are of finite ρ \rho -variation for ρ ≥ 1 \rho\geq 1 and that the diagonals of covariance functions are of finite ρ ′ \rho^{\prime} -variation for ρ ′ ≥ 1 \rho^{\prime}\geq 1 such that 1 ρ ′ + 1 2 ​ ρ > 1 \frac{1}{\rho^{\prime}}+\frac{1}{2\rho}>1 . The difference between the two types of integrals is identified with a Young integral. We also show that the Skorohod integral is the limit of a [ ρ ] [\rho] -th order Skorohod-Riemann sum.



1.
Introduction

Let
x
be a continuous semimartingale and
f
a sufficiently smooth function.
The classical Itô-Stratonovich conversion formula states that the difference between Stratonovich integral
\int_{0}^{T}f(x_{t})dx_{t}
and Itô integral, denoted by
\int_{0}^{T}f(x_{t})\delta x_{t}
, is equal to the Riemann-Stieltjes integral
\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\Delta f(x_{t})d[x]_{t}
, where
\Delta f
is the Laplacian of
f
and
[x]
is the quadratic variation process of
x
(see e.g.
[
14
,
22
]
). Furthermore, if the integrand
f(x_{\cdot})
is replaced by a continuous semimartingale
y
then the difference is identified with the covariation
\frac{1}{2}[y,x]_{T}
.

Interestingly, both types of stochastic calculus have been extended to the setting of Gaussian processes (by entirely different approaches). The theory of rough path provides a path-wise approach for Gaussian processes (see e.g.
[
9
]
), which gives rise to Stratonovich-type integrals, while the Skorohod integrals based on Malliavin calculus generalize the Itô integrals (see e.g.
[
10
,
19
]
).
This raises a natural question: Is there a conversion formula for Gaussian processes as well that relates these two approaches? If so, to what extent does it hold?

This problem has been investigated in a number of recent works (see e.g.
[
1
,
2
,
3
,
4
,
6
,
11
,
15
,
20
,
23
]
).
The results are mainly focused on a one-dimensional setting, or assume that a geometric Gaussian rough path exists. The latter usually requires that the covariance function of the Gaussian process is of finite
\rho
-variation for
\rho<2
.
These conditions are in place to properly define the Stratonovich-type integral.
Another main obstacle for the conversion formula problem is that the Skorohod integral
\int_{0}^{T}f(x_{t})\delta x_{t}
does not exist when
x
is, for instance, a fractional Brownian motion with Hurst parameter
H\leq 1/4
. This problem is resolved by defining a proper extension of the divergence operator (see e.g.
[
5
]
). In
[
11
]
, the authors also studied this extension based on Wick product.

In this paper, we consider the conversion formula problem in a more general setting of Gaussian processes. More precisely, we will assume the following:

Hypothesis 1.1
.

Let
x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})
be a
\mathbb{R}^{d}
-valued mean-zero continuous Gaussian process such that the components
x_{i}
,
i=1,\dots,d
are mutually independent
and that
their covariance functions
R_{i}(s,t):=\mathbb{E}[x_{i}(s)x_{i}(t)]
,
i=1,\dots,d
are continuous and of finite
\rho
-variation for
\rho\geq 1
.
Assume further that one of the following two conditions is true:

(1)
\rho<2
;

(2)
\rho\geq 2
, and the one-variable function
R_{i}(t,t),t\in[0,T]
is of finite
\rho^{\prime}
-variation for some
\rho^{\prime}\geq 1
such that
\frac{1}{\rho}+\frac{1}{\rho^{\prime}}>1
.

To make sense of a Stratonovich-type integral in this setting we will focus on integrand of the form
\partial f:=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{i}}
for
f:(t,x)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
. Let
{\mathcal{P}}:0=t_{0}<\cdots<t_{n}=T
be a partition of
[0,T]
.
Consider the following compensated Riemann sum:

\displaystyle{\mathcal{J}}(\partial f,dx,{\mathcal{P}}):=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\sum_{m=1}^{[2\rho]}\frac{1}{m!}\partial^{m}f(t_{k},x_{t_{k}})\cdot(x_{t_{k+1}}-x_{t_{k}})^{\otimes m},

where
\partial^{m}f
is the collection of all
m
-th order partial derivatives of
f
in the directions of
x_{i}
’s and the product between
\partial^{m}f(t_{k},x_{t_{k}})
and
(x_{t_{k+1}}-x_{t_{k}})^{\otimes m}
should be interpreted as a dot product.
Denote by
|{\mathcal{P}}|
the mesh size of
{\mathcal{P}}
.
Then our Stratonovich-type integral is defined as follows:

(1.1)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\partial f(t,x_{t})dx_{t}:=\lim_{|{\mathcal{P}}|\to 0}{\mathcal{J}}(\partial f,dx,{\mathcal{P}}).

One can show that such integral agrees with the corresponding rough integral whenever both integrals are well-defined (see Remark
4.2
).

Consider now the following Skorohod-Riemann sum:

(1.2)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{J}}(\partial f,\delta x,{\mathcal{P}}):=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\sum_{m=1}^{[\rho]}\frac{1}{m!}\delta^{m}\Big(\partial^{m}f(t_{k},x_{t_{k}}){\beta}_{k}^{\otimes m}\Big),

where
{\beta}_{k}
is the indicate function
{\beta}_{k}=\mathbf{1}_{[t_{k},t_{k+1}]}
,
\delta^{m}
is the collection of all
m
-th order divergence operators for the Gaussian process
x
, and
\delta^{m}
is applied to
\partial^{m}f(t_{k},x_{t_{k}}){\beta}_{k}^{\otimes m}
componentwisely (see Definition
4.5
for more details).
Our
extended Skorohod integral is then defined by:

(1.3)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\partial f(t,x_{t})\delta x_{t}:=\lim_{|{\mathcal{P}}|\to 0}{\mathcal{J}}(\partial f,\delta x,{\mathcal{P}}).

The Skorohod-Riemann sum in (
1.2
) is motivated by a limit theorem of compensated Riemann sum in
[
16
]
.
In
[
11
]
the authors considered the following Wick-Riemann sum:

(1.4)

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\sum_{m=1}^{N}\frac{1}{m!}\partial^{m}f(t_{k},x_{t_{k}})\diamond\delta^{m}({\beta}_{k}^{\otimes m}),

where
N
is equal to
[2\rho]
in our notation.
It turns out that the components in our Skorohod-Riemann sum coincides with those in the Wick-Riemann sum (
1.4
). More precisely, we have the relation (see Proposition 4.1 (3) in
[
11
]
)

\displaystyle\delta^{m}\Big(\partial^{m}f(t_{k},x_{t_{k}}){\beta}_{k}^{\otimes m}\Big)=\partial^{m}f(t_{k},x_{t_{k}})\diamond\delta^{m}({\beta}_{k}^{\otimes m})

for a general
f
. We will see that our result improves
[
11
]
in several nontrivial ways.

In the following is our main result (see Theorem
4.6
for a more precise statement).
For convenience, let us denote
\partial^{2}_{ii}=\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x_{i}^{2}}
and the Riemann sum:

(1.5)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{J}}(\partial^{2}f,dR,{\mathcal{P}}):=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{d}{\partial^{2}_{ii}f}(t_{k},x_{t_{k}})\cdot\Big(R_{i}(t_{k+1},t_{k+1})-R_{i}(t_{k},t_{k})\Big).

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
f
be a function that is sufficiently smooth, and
x
be a Gaussian process satisfying Hypothesis
1.1
.
Then the limit in (
1.1
) exists and we have the relation:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\partial f(t,x_{t})dx_{t}=\lim_{|{\mathcal{P}}|\to 0}\left({\mathcal{J}}(\partial f,\delta x,{\mathcal{P}})+\frac{1}{2}{\mathcal{J}}(\partial^{2}f,dR,{\mathcal{P}})\right).

Moreover, if one of the following holds:

(a)
\rho<3/2
,

(b)
\frac{1}{2\rho}+\frac{1}{\rho^{\prime}}>1
,

then both
{\mathcal{J}}(\partial f,\delta x,{\mathcal{P}})
and
{\mathcal{J}}(\partial^{2}f,dR,{\mathcal{P}})
converge, and we have the conversion formula:

(1.6)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\partial f(t,x_{t})dx_{t}=\int_{0}^{T}\partial f(t,x_{t})\delta x_{t}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\int_{0}^{T}{\partial^{2}_{ii}f}(t,x_{t})dR_{i}(t,t),

where
\int_{0}^{T}{\partial^{2}_{ii}f}(t,x_{t})dR_{i}(t,t)
should be interpreted as a Young integral.

In the following we make some remarks about Theorem
1.2
.

Remark 1.3
.

It is well-known that
x
is of finite
p
-variation almost surely for
p>2\rho
. So the additional condition
\rho<3/2
or
\frac{1}{\rho^{\prime}}+\frac{1}{2\rho}>1
ensures that the Young integral
\int_{0}^{T}{\partial^{2}_{ii}f}(t,x_{t})dR_{i}(t,t)
is well-defined (see also Remark
2.6
).

Remark 1.4
.

Note that the condition
\frac{1}{\rho^{\prime}}+\frac{1}{2\rho}>1
is always satisfied when
\rho^{\prime}=1
. In other words,
Theorem
1.2
shows that the conversion formula (
1.6
) holds whenever the diagonoal functions
R_{i}(t,t)
,
t\in[0,T]
are of bounded variation.

Remark 1.5
.

Theorem
1.2
implies the following Itô formula:

\displaystyle f(T,x_{T})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f(0,0)+\int_{0}^{T}\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}(t,x_{t})dt+\int_{0}^{T}{\partial f}(t,x_{t})\delta x_{t}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\int_{0}^{T}{\partial^{2}_{ii}f}(t,x_{t})dR_{i}(t,t).

Theorem
1.2
improves results of
[
11
]
in several ways which we highlight in the following:

(i) We do not assume that the Gaussian process
x
generates a geometric rough path.

(ii) Theorem
1.2
shows that the
[\rho]
-th order expansion is sufficient to guarantee the convergence of
the Skorohod-Riemann sum
(
1.2
), and consequently, of (
1.4
) for all
N\geq[\rho]
.
This improves the results of
[
11
]
, in which the
[2\rho]
-th order expansion is considered. Note that the possibility of reducing the order of expansion was suggested in
[
11
, Section 4.3]
.

(iii)
We make minimal assumptions for the Gaussian process or its covariance function.

The proof of Theorem
1.2
combines Malliavin calculus with rough path techniques such as the super-additivity of a 2D control, which are summarized in Section
2
. We define the Stratonovich-type integral by examining the Taylor expansion of the function
f(t,x)
, thereby eliminating the need for a geometric rough path. This definition also accommodates non-autonomous integrands for the conversion formula.

The paper is structured as follows. In Section
2
we recall basic results of Malliavin calculus and Gaussian processes and state some useful lemmas. In Section
3
we prove Theorem
1.2
when
x
is a one-dimensional Gaussian process.
Then, in Section
4
we extend Theorem
1.2
to the multidimensional case.

(\Omega,\mathscr{F},P)
. If
X
is a random variable, we denote by
\|X\|_{L^{p}}
the
L^{p}
-norm of
X
.
The letter
K
stands for a constant independent of any important parameters which can change from line to line. We write
A\lesssim B
if there is a constant
K>0
such that
A\leq KB

[a]

a
.

.

2.
Preliminary results

In this section we recall the elements of Malliavin calculus and Gaussian processes and also prepare some useful lemmas.

2.1.
Tensor product of Hilbert spaces

We briefly recall the basics of tensor product of Hilbert spaces. A detailed discussion on this topic can be found in e.g.
[
12
,
13
,
21
]
.

Let
{\mathcal{H}}_{1}
,…,
{\mathcal{H}}_{m}
be real separable Hilbert spaces with inner products
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}_{i}}
,
i=1,\dots,m
. Denote by
{\mathcal{H}}_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes{\mathcal{H}}_{m}
the tensor product of the vector spaces
{\mathcal{H}}_{1}
,…,
{\mathcal{H}}_{m}
. We define the inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes{\mathcal{H}}_{m}}
on
{\mathcal{H}}_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes{\mathcal{H}}_{m}
such that for
h_{i},g_{i}\in{\mathcal{H}}_{i}
,
i=1,\dots,m
we have:

(2.1)

\displaystyle\langle h_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes h_{m},g_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes g_{m}\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes{\mathcal{H}}_{m}}=\langle h_{1},g_{1}\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}_{1}}\cdots\langle h_{m},g_{m}\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}_{m}}.

Then we can obtain a Hilbert space by taking the completion of
{\mathcal{H}}_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes{\mathcal{H}}_{m}
under the inner product (
2.1
). This Hilbert space will still be denoted by
{\mathcal{H}}_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes{\mathcal{H}}_{m}
. Furthermore, the association law holds, namely, there is an unitary transformation
U
from
{\mathcal{H}}_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes{\mathcal{H}}_{m_{1}+m_{2}}
to

({\mathcal{H}}_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes{\mathcal{H}}_{m_{1}})\otimes({\mathcal{H}}_{m_{1}+1}\otimes\cdots\otimes{\mathcal{H}}_{m_{1}+m_{2}})

such that

\displaystyle U(h_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes h_{m_{1}+m_{2}})=(h_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes h_{m_{1}})\otimes(h_{m_{1}+1}\otimes\cdots\otimes h_{m_{1}+m_{2}}).

2.2.
Elements of Malliavin calculus

In this subsection we recall the elements of Malliavin calculus and state some preliminary results. We refer the reader to e.g.
[
10
,
18
,
19
]
for further details.
Let
{\mathcal{H}}
be a real separable Hilbert space with inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}}
and norm
\|\cdot\|_{{\mathcal{H}}}=\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}}^{1/2}
. For
q\in\mathbb{N}
we denote by
{\mathcal{H}}^{\otimes q}
and
{\mathcal{H}}^{\odot q}
, respectively, the
q
th tensor product and the
q
th symmetric tensor product of
{\mathcal{H}}
. Let
X=\{X(h):h\in{\mathcal{H}}\}
be an isonormal Gaussian process over
{\mathcal{H}}
, namely,
X
is a centered Gaussian family defined on some probability space
(\Omega,{\mathcal{F}},P)
such that
\mathbb{E}[X(g)X(h)]=\langle g,h\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}}
for every
g,h\in{\mathcal{H}}
. Let
\mathit{S}
denote the set of random variables of the form

F=f(W(h_{1}),\dots,W(h_{n}))
,
where
n\geq 1
,
h_{1},\dots,h_{n}\in{\mathcal{H}}
, and
f:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}
is a
C^{\infty}
-function such that
f
and its partial derivatives have at most polynomial growth. The
l
th Malliavin derivative of
F\in\mathit{S}
is a random variable with values in
{\mathcal{H}}^{\odot p}
defined by:

\displaystyle D^{l}F

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i_{1},\dots,i_{l}=1}^{m}\frac{\partial^{l}f}{\partial x_{i_{1}}\cdots\partial x_{i_{l}}}(X(h_{1}),\dots,X(h_{m}))h_{i_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes h_{i_{l}}.

For any
p\geq 1
and any integer
k\geq 1
, we define the Sobolev space
\mathbb{D}^{k,p}
as the closure of
\mathit{S}
with respect to the norm:

(2.2)

\displaystyle\left\|F\right\|_{k,p}^{p}=\mathbb{E}\left[\left|F\right|^{p}\right]+\sum_{l=1}^{k}\mathbb{E}\big[\|D^{l}F\|^{p}_{\mathcal{H}^{\otimes l}}\big].

Then we can extend the domain of
D^{k}
to the space
\mathbb{D}^{k,p}
.
We denote by
\delta^{k}
the adjoint of the derivative operator
D^{k}
. We say
u\in\text{Dom}(\delta^{k})
if there is a
\delta^{k}(u)\in L^{2}(\Omega)
such that for any
F\in\mathbb{D}^{k,2}
the following duality relation holds:

(2.3)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(\langle u,D^{k}F\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}^{\otimes k}})=\mathbb{E}[\delta^{k}(u)F].

When
k=1
we simply write
\delta^{1}=\delta
. We will also use the convention that
\text{Dom}(\delta^{0})=L^{2}(\Omega)
and
\delta^{0}(u)=u
.
Recall that for
h\in{\mathcal{H}}:|h|_{{\mathcal{H}}}=1
we have the relation:

(2.4)

\displaystyle\delta^{k}(h^{\otimes k})=H_{k}(\delta(h)),

where
H_{k}(x)=(-1)^{k}e^{x^{2}/2}\frac{d^{k}}{dx^{k}}e^{-x^{2}/2}
is called the Hermite polynomial of order
k
.
For monomials it is well-known that we have the following Hermite decomposition:

(2.5)

\displaystyle x^{i}=\sum_{0\leq 2q\leq i}a^{i}_{i-2q}{H_{i-2q}(x)}\,,\qquad\text{where}\quad a^{i}_{i-2q}=\frac{i!}{2^{q}q!(i-2q)!}\,,\qquad i\in\mathbb{N}.

Let us recall the following result (see e.g.
[
18
, Proposition 2.5.4]
):

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
F\in\mathbb{D}^{1,2}
and
u\in\emph{Dom}(\delta)
be such that the expectations
\mathbb{E}[F^{2}\|u\|_{{\mathcal{H}}}^{2}]
,
\mathbb{E}[F^{2}\delta(u)^{2}]
,
\mathbb{E}[\langle DF,u\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}}^{2}]
are finite. Then
Fu\in\emph{Dom}(\delta)
and

(2.6)

\displaystyle F\delta(u)=\delta(Fu)+\langle DF,u\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}}.

Let
{\mathcal{V}}
be another real separable Hilbert space. Let
S_{{\mathcal{V}}}
be the collection of all smooth
{\mathcal{V}}
-valued random variables of the form
F=\sum_{j=1}^{n}F_{j}v_{j}
, where
F_{j}\in S
,
v_{j}\in{\mathcal{V}}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
. We define the Malliavin derivatives of
F\in S_{{\mathcal{V}}}
by
D^{k}F=\sum_{j=1}^{n}D^{k}F_{j}\otimes v_{j}
. Similar to (
2.2
), we can extend the domain of
D^{k}
to the space
\mathbb{D}^{k,p}({\mathcal{V}})
, which is defined as the closure of
S_{{\mathcal{V}}}
with respect to the norm

\displaystyle\|F\|_{\mathbb{D}^{k,p}({\mathcal{V}})}^{p}=\mathbb{E}[\|F\|_{{\mathcal{V}}}^{p}]+\sum_{l=1}^{k}\mathbb{E}[\|D^{l}F\|_{{\mathcal{H}}^{\otimes l}\otimes{\mathcal{V}}}^{p}].

The following result can be considered an extension of Lemma
2.1
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
F\in\mathbb{D}^{i,2}
,
i\in\mathbb{N}
and
h\in{\mathcal{H}}
, and set

(2.7)

\displaystyle u_{j}=\langle D^{j}F,h^{\otimes j}\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}^{\otimes j}}\cdot h^{\otimes(i-j)},\qquad j=0,\dots,i.

Then
u_{j}\in\emph{Dom}(\delta^{i-j})
, and we have the estimate:

(2.8)

\displaystyle\|\delta^{i-j}(u_{j})\|_{L^{2}}\leq K\cdot\|F\|_{i,2}\cdot\|h\|_{{\mathcal{H}}}^{i}

for all
j=0,\dots,i
, where
K
is a constant depending on
i
only.
Furthermore, we have the relation:

(2.9)

\displaystyle F\delta^{i}(h^{\otimes i})=\sum_{j=0}^{i}{i\choose j}\delta^{i-j}(u_{j}).

Proof.

We differentiate
u_{j}
in (
2.7
) and then take the
\|\cdot\|_{{\mathcal{H}}^{\otimes(i-j+L)}}
norm to get

\displaystyle\|D^{L}u_{j}\|_{{\mathcal{H}}^{\otimes(i-j+L)}}=\|h^{\otimes(i-j)}D^{L}\langle D^{j}F,h^{\otimes j}\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}^{\otimes j}}\|_{{\mathcal{H}}^{\otimes(j+L)}}

(2.10)

\displaystyle=\|D^{L}\langle D^{j}F,h^{\otimes j}\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}^{\otimes j}}\|_{{\mathcal{H}}^{\otimes L}}\cdot\|h\|_{{\mathcal{H}}}^{i-j}.

In the following we bound
\|D^{L}\langle D^{j}F,h^{\otimes j}\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}^{\otimes j}}\|_{{\mathcal{H}}^{\otimes L}}
. Take a vector
g\in{\mathcal{H}}^{\otimes L}
. Then we have

(2.11)

\displaystyle\langle D^{L}\langle D^{j}F,h^{\otimes j}\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}^{\otimes j}},g\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}^{\otimes L}}=\langle D^{j+L}F,h^{\otimes j}\otimes g\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}^{\otimes(j+L)}}.

Applying the triangle inequality to the right-hand side of (
2.11
) we obtain:

\displaystyle\langle D^{L}\langle D^{j}F,h^{\otimes j}\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}^{\otimes j}},g\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}^{\otimes L}}\leq\|D^{j+L}F\|_{{\mathcal{H}}^{\otimes(j+L)}}\cdot\|h^{\otimes j}\otimes g\|_{{\mathcal{H}}^{\otimes(j+L)}}

\displaystyle=\|D^{j+L}F\|_{{\mathcal{H}}^{\otimes(j+L)}}\cdot\|h\|_{{\mathcal{H}}}^{j}\cdot\|g\|_{{\mathcal{H}}^{\otimes L}}.

Since
g\in{\mathcal{H}}
is arbitrary,
this implies that

(2.12)

\displaystyle\|\langle D^{j+L}F,h^{\otimes j}\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}^{\otimes j}}\|_{{\mathcal{H}}^{\otimes L}}\leq\|D^{j+L}F\|_{{\mathcal{H}}^{\otimes(j+L)}}\cdot\|h\|_{{\mathcal{H}}}^{j}.

Substituting the estimate (
2.12
) into (
2.10
) we obtain

(2.13)

\displaystyle\|D^{L}u_{j}\|_{{\mathcal{H}}^{\otimes(i-j+L)}}\leq\|D^{j+L}F\|_{{\mathcal{H}}^{\otimes(j+L)}}\cdot\|h\|_{{\mathcal{H}}}^{i}.

Taking the
L^{2}(\Omega)
norm in both sides of (
2.13
) we thus obtain

(2.14)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\|D^{L}u_{j}\|_{{\mathcal{H}}^{\otimes(i-j+L)}}^{2}]^{1/2}\leq\|F\|_{i,2}\cdot\|h\|_{{\mathcal{H}}}^{i}

for all
L=1,\dots,i-j
and
j=0,\dots,i
.
It follows that
u_{j}\in\mathbb{D}^{(i-j),2}({\mathcal{H}}^{\otimes(i-j)})
and we have the relation

\displaystyle\|u_{j}\|_{\mathbb{D}^{(i-j),2}({\mathcal{H}}^{\otimes(i-j)})}\leq\|F\|_{i,2}\cdot\|h\|_{{\mathcal{H}}}^{i}\,.

By Meyer’s inequality (see e.g.
[
18
, Theorem 2.5.5]
) we obtain that
u_{j}\in\text{Dom}(\delta^{i-j})
and the estimate (
2.8
) holds.

We turn to the proof of (
2.9
).
Let
G
be any smooth random variable. That is,
G
has the form
G=f(W(h_{1}),\dots,W(h_{L}))
, where
f\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{L})
and
h_{1},\dots,h_{L}\in{\mathcal{H}}
,
L\geq 1
. Since
F\in\mathbb{D}^{i,2}
and
G
is smooth, we have
F\cdot G\in\mathbb{D}^{i,2}
, and by applying the duality relation (
2.3
) we get:

(2.15)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[GF\delta^{i}(h^{\otimes i})]=\mathbb{E}[\langle D^{i}[GF],h^{\otimes i}\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}^{\otimes i}}].

Furthermore, by applying the Leibniz rule (see e.g.
[
18
, Exercise 2.3.10]
) to
D^{i}[GF]
in (
2.15
) we obtain:

(2.16)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[GF\delta^{i}(h^{\otimes i})]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{i}{i\choose j}\mathbb{E}\big[\langle D^{i-j}G\tilde{\otimes}D^{j}F,h^{\otimes i}\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}^{\otimes i}}\big].

Applying (
2.1
) to the inner product in the right-hand side of (
2.16
) yields:

(2.17)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[GF\delta^{i}(h^{\otimes i})]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{i}{i\choose j}\mathbb{E}\big[\langle D^{i-j}G,h^{\otimes(i-j)}\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}^{\otimes(i-j)}}\cdot\langle D^{j}F,h^{\otimes j}\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}^{\otimes j}}\big].

Recall that
u
is given in (
2.7
). So equation (
2.17
) can be written as:

(2.18)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[GF\delta^{i}(h^{\otimes i})]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{i}{i\choose j}\mathbb{E}\big[\langle D^{i-j}G,u\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}^{\otimes(i-j)}}\big].

We have shown that
u_{j}\in\text{Dom}(\delta^{i-j})
. So by applying (
2.3
) to (
2.18
) we obtain:

(2.19)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[GF\delta^{i}(h^{\otimes i})]=\sum_{j=0}^{i}{i\choose j}\mathbb{E}[G\delta^{i-j}(u_{j})].

Since smooth random variables are dense in
L^{2}(\Omega)
, relation (
2.19
) implies that
(
2.9
)
holds.
This completes the proof.
∎

We will need the following technical lemma.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
i,i^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}
,
g,h\in{\mathcal{H}}
and
F\in\mathbb{D}^{i+i^{\prime},2}
. Then
\delta^{i}(Fh^{\otimes i})\in\mathbb{D}^{i^{\prime},2}
, and we have the relation:

(2.20)

\displaystyle\langle D^{i^{\prime}}\delta^{i}(Fh^{\otimes i}),g^{\otimes i^{\prime}}\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}^{\otimes i^{\prime}}}=\sum_{j=0}^{i^{\prime}\wedge i}{i\choose j}{i^{\prime}\choose j}j!\langle h,g\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}}^{j}\cdot\delta^{i-j}\Big(\langle D^{i^{\prime}-j}F,g^{\otimes(i^{\prime}-j)}\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}^{\otimes(i^{\prime}-j)}}h^{\otimes(i-j)}\Big).\qquad

Proof.

As in Lemma
2.2

we can show that
\langle D^{i^{\prime}-j}F,g^{\otimes(i^{\prime}-j)}\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}^{\otimes(i^{\prime}-j)}}h^{\otimes(i-j)}\in\text{Dom}(\delta^{i-j})
,
j=0,\dots,i
, and therefore the right-hand side of (
2.20
) is well-defined.
In the following, the proof is divided into two steps. In the first step, we consider the case when
i^{\prime}=1
. Then in the second step we prove the lemma for all
i^{\prime}
and
i
.

When
i^{\prime}=1
relation (
2.20
) becomes

(2.21)

\displaystyle\langle D\delta^{i}(Fh^{\otimes i}),g\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}}=\delta^{i}(\langle DF,g\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}}h^{\otimes i})+i\langle h,g\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}}\cdot\delta^{i-1}(Fh^{\otimes(i-1)}).

In the following, we show by induction that
\delta^{i}(Fh^{\otimes i})\in\mathbb{D}^{1,2}
and that relation (
2.21
) holds for all
i\in\mathbb{N}
.

Recall the relation (
2.6
). So we have
\delta(Fh)=F\delta(h)-\langle DF,h\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}}
. By differentiating both sides of this relation in the direction of
g
, we obtain that

\displaystyle\langle D\delta(Fh),g\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}}=\delta(\langle DF,g\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}}h)+\langle h,g\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}}\cdot F.

This implies that
\delta(Fh)\in\mathbb{D}^{1,2}
and (
2.21
) holds when
i=1
.

Applying relation (
2.9
) with
i
replaced by
i+1
we obtain

(2.22)

\displaystyle\delta^{i+1}(Fh^{\otimes(i+1)})=F\delta^{i+1}(h^{\otimes(i+1)})-\sum_{j=1}^{i+1}{i+1\choose j}\delta^{i+1-j}(\tilde{u}_{j}),

where
\tilde{u}_{j}=\langle D^{j}F,h^{\otimes j}\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}^{\otimes j}}\cdot h^{\otimes(i+1-j)}
,
j=0,\dots,i+1
. Differentiating both sides of (
2.22
) in
g
we obtain:

\displaystyle\langle D\delta^{i+1}(Fh^{\otimes(i+1)}),g\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}}=\langle DF,g\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}}\delta^{i+1}(h^{\otimes(i+1)})+(i+1)F\delta^{i}(h^{\otimes i})\langle h,g\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}}

(2.23)

\displaystyle-\sum_{j=1}^{i+1}{i+1\choose j}\langle D\delta^{i+1-j}(\tilde{u}_{j}),g\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}}.

Note that
i+1-j\leq i
in (
2.23
).
By applying the induction assumption (
2.21
) with
\langle D^{j}F,h^{\otimes j}\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}^{\otimes j}}
and
i+1-j
in places of
F
and
i
we have
\delta^{i+1-j}(\tilde{u}_{j})\in\mathbb{D}^{1,2}
and

(2.24)

\displaystyle\langle D\delta^{i+1-j}(\tilde{u}_{j}),g\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\delta^{i+1-j}(u_{j}^{\prime})+(i+1-j)\langle h,g\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}}\cdot\delta^{i-j}(u_{j}),\qquad

where
u_{j}^{\prime}=\langle D^{j}\langle DF,g\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}},h^{\otimes j}\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}^{\otimes j}}\cdot h^{\otimes(i+1-j)}
and
u_{j}
is defined as in (
2.7
).
On the other hand,
applying relation (
2.9
) with
i+1
and
\langle DF,g\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}}
in the places of
i
and
F
, we obtain that

(2.25)

\displaystyle\delta^{i+1}\big(\langle DF,g\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}}h^{\otimes(i+1)}\big)=\langle DF,g\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}}\delta^{i+1}(h^{\otimes(i+1)})-\sum_{j=1}^{i+1}{i+1\choose j}\delta^{i+1-j}(u_{j}^{\prime}).

Substituting (
2.24
) into (
2.23
) and then taking the difference between (
2.23
) and (
2.25
) we obtain:

\displaystyle\langle D\delta^{i+1}(Fh^{\otimes(i+1)}),g\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}}-\delta^{i+1}\big(\langle DF,g\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}}h^{\otimes(i+1)}\big)

\displaystyle=(i+1)F\delta^{i}(h^{\otimes i})\langle h,g\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}}-\sum_{j=1}^{i}{i+1\choose j}(i+1-j)\langle h,g\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}}\cdot\delta^{i-j}\big(\langle D^{j}F,h^{\otimes j}\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}^{\otimes j}}h^{\otimes(i-j)}\big)

(2.26)

\displaystyle=(i+1)\langle h,g\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}}\cdot\Big(F\delta^{i}(h^{\otimes i})-\sum_{j=1}^{i}{i\choose j}\cdot\delta^{i-j}\big(\langle D^{j}F,h^{\otimes j}\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}^{\otimes j}}h^{\otimes(i-j)}\big)\Big).

Note that the summation in (
2.25
) does not contain the case
j=i+1
since we have
i+1-j=0
in (
2.24
).
Now, invoking relation (
2.9
) in the right-hand side of (
2.26
) we obtain that

\displaystyle\langle D\delta^{i+1}(Fh^{\otimes(i+1)}),g\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}}-\delta^{i+1}(\langle DF,g\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}}h^{\otimes(i+1)})=(i+1)\langle h,g\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}}\delta^{i}(u_{0}).

This gives (
2.21
) when
i+1
is in the place of
i
. This proves relation (
2.21
), and thus (
2.20
) for
i^{\prime}=1
and all
i\in\mathbb{N}
.

In the following we prove by induction in the integer
i^{\prime}
that
\delta^{i}(Fh^{\otimes i})\in\mathbb{D}^{i^{\prime},2}
and relation (
2.20
) holds for all
i,i^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}
. Assume that (
2.20
) holds for some
i^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}
. It suffices to show that (
2.20
) still holds when
i^{\prime}+1
is in the place of
i^{\prime}
.

First,
by applying (
2.21
) with
i-j
and
\langle D^{i^{\prime}-j}F,g^{\otimes(i^{\prime}-j)}\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}}
in places of
F
and
i
, we have

\displaystyle\Big\langle D\delta^{i-j}\Big(\langle D^{i^{\prime}-j}F,g^{\otimes(i^{\prime}-j)}\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}}h^{\otimes(i-j)}\Big),g\Big\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}}=\delta^{i-j}\Big(\langle D^{i^{\prime}+1-j}F,g^{\otimes(i^{\prime}+1-j)}\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}}h^{\otimes i-j}\Big)

(2.27)

\displaystyle+(i-j)\langle h,g\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}}\cdot\delta^{i-j-1}\Big(\langle D^{i^{\prime}-j}F,g^{\otimes(i^{\prime}-j)}\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}}h^{\otimes(i-j-1)}\Big).

Summing both sides of (
2.27
) in
j
and then applying some elementary computations it is easy to show that we have

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{i^{\prime}\wedge i}{i\choose j}{i^{\prime}\choose j}j!\langle h,g\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}}^{j}\cdot\Big\langle D\delta^{i-j}\Big(\langle D^{i^{\prime}-j}F,g^{\otimes(i^{\prime}-j)}\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}}h^{\otimes(i-j)}\Big),g\Big\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}}

(2.28)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{(i^{\prime}+1)\wedge i}{i\choose j}{i^{\prime}+1\choose j}j!\langle h,g\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}}^{j}\cdot\delta^{i-j}\Big(\langle D^{i^{\prime}+1-j}F,g^{\otimes(i^{\prime}+1-j)}\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}}h^{\otimes(i-j)}\Big).

Note that by induction assumption the left-hand side of (
2.28
) is equal to

\displaystyle\langle D\langle D^{i^{\prime}}\delta^{i}(Fh^{\otimes i}),g^{\otimes i^{\prime}}\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}^{\otimes i^{\prime}}},g\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}}=\langle D^{i^{\prime}+1}\delta^{i}(Fh^{\otimes i}),g^{\otimes(i^{\prime}+1)}\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}^{\otimes(i^{\prime}+1)}}.

So relation (
2.28
) gives (
2.20
) with
i^{\prime}+1
in the place of

. It follows that (
2.20
) holds for all
i^{\prime}
and

. This completes the proof.
∎

The following result is a consequence of Lemma

.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
i,i^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}
,
g,h\in{\mathcal{H}}
and
F,G\in\mathbb{D}^{M,2}
for
M=\max(i,i^{\prime})
. Then the following relation holds:

(2.29)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\delta^{i}(Fh^{\otimes i})\cdot\delta^{i^{\prime}}(Gg^{\otimes i^{\prime}})]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{i\wedge i^{\prime}}{i\choose j}{i^{\prime}\choose j}j!\langle D^{i^{\prime}-j}F,g^{\otimes(i^{\prime}-j)}\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}^{\otimes(i^{\prime}-j)}}

\displaystyle\qquad\quad\cdot\langle D^{i-j}G,h^{\otimes(i-j)}\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}^{\otimes(i-j)}}\cdot\langle h,g\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}}^{j}.\quad

Proof.

When

and

are smooth random variables,
relation (

) can be shown by applying integration by parts (

) and relation (

), and then (
2.3
) again. By a density argument it is easy to show that (

) also holds for

.
∎

2.3.
Gaussian process and covariance function

Let
x
be a one-dimensional Gaussian process on
[0,T]
and denote the covariance function
R(s,t)=\mathbb{E}[x_{s}x_{t}]
,
s,t\in[0,T]
. Recall that the rectangular increment of
R
is defined as:

\displaystyle R_{uv}^{st}:=R(v,t)-R(u,t)-R(v,s)+R(u,s),\qquad u,v,s,t\in[0,T]:u\leq v,s\leq t.

Let
I_{i}=[a_{i},b_{i}]
,
i=1,2
be two sub-intervals of
[0,T]
. Let
{\mathcal{P}}_{1}=\{a_{1}=t_{0}<\cdots<t_{n_{1}}=b_{1}\}
and
{\mathcal{P}}_{2}=\{a_{2}=s_{0}<\cdots<s_{n_{2}}=b_{2}\}
,
n_{1},n_{2}\in\mathbb{N}
be partitions of
I_{i}
,
i=1,2
, respectively.
The
\rho
-variation of a two-variable function
R
on the domain
I_{1}\times I_{2}
is defined by:

\displaystyle|R|_{\rho\text{-var},I_{1}\times I_{2}}=\sup_{{\mathcal{P}}_{1}\times{\mathcal{P}}_{2}}\sum_{k=0}^{n_{1}-1}\sum_{k^{\prime}=0}^{n_{2}-1}\big|R_{t_{k}t_{k+1}}^{s_{k^{\prime}}s_{k^{\prime}+1}}\big|^{\rho},

where the supremum is taken over all grid partitions of
I_{1}\times I_{2}
(see e.g.
[
9
]
).
Throughout the paper, we define
{\mathcal{H}}
to be the completion of the space of indicator functions with respect to the inner product
\langle\mathbf{1}_{[u,v]},\mathbf{1}_{[s,t]}\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}}=R_{uv}^{st}
and let the Sobolev spaces
\mathbb{D}^{k,p}
be defined as in Section
2.2
.

We will need the following elementary lemma:

Lemma 2.5
.

The following relation holds for all
s\leq t
:

(2.30)

\displaystyle R_{0s}^{st}=-\frac{1}{2}R_{st}^{st}+\frac{1}{2}(R_{0t}^{0t}-R_{0s}^{0s}).

Proof.

Note that

\displaystyle R_{0t}^{0t}=\mathbb{E}[x_{t}^{2}]=\mathbb{E}[(x_{t}-x_{s}+x_{s})^{2}]=R_{st}^{st}+R_{0s}^{0s}+2R_{0s}^{st}.

The lemma then follows.
∎

2.4.
Definition of
p
-variation

Let
x
be a continuous path on
[0,T]
.
Let
{\mathcal{P}}:s=t_{0}<\cdots<t_{n}=t
be a partition on
[s,t]
.
Recall that the
p
-variation of
x
on
[s,t]
is defined as

\displaystyle\|x\|_{p\text{-var},[s,t]}:=\Big(\sup_{{\mathcal{P}}}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}|x^{1}_{t_{k}t_{k+1}}|^{p}\Big)^{1/p},\quad p\geq 1,

where the supremum is taken over all partitions
{\mathcal{P}}
on
[s,t]
. We say that
x
has finite
p
-variation if
\|x\|_{p\text{-var},[0,T]}<\infty
.

Remark 2.6
.

Let
x
and
y
be continuous paths on
[0,T]
. It is well-known (see
[
24
]
) that
if
x
and
y
are of finite
p
- and
q
-variations for
p,q\geq 1
, respectively, such that
\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{q}>1
,
then
the Riemann sum
\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}y_{t_{k}}(x_{t_{k+1}}-x_{t_{k}})
converges to the Young integral
\int_{0}^{T}y_{t}dx_{t}
as
|{\mathcal{P}}|\to 0
.

Remark 2.7
.

It is well-known that for a Gaussian process
x
such that its covariance function
R
is of finite
\rho
-variation for
\rho\geq 1
, the sample paths of
x
are of finite
p
-variation almost surely for any
p>2\rho
; see e.g.
[
9
, Proposition 15.22]
.

3.
Riemann-Skorohod integral for one-dimensional Gaussian process

In this section we introduce the Skorohod-type Riemann integral and consider its relation to the rough integral.
Throughout the section we assume that
x
is a one-dimensional Gaussian process satisfying Hypothesis
1.1
. We start by making some remarks about the hypothesis.

Remark 3.1
.

Assume that
R
is a continuous two-variable function on
[0,T]^{2}
with finite
\rho
-variation for
\rho\geq 1
. Denote by
I_{1}
and
I_{2}
two sub-intervals of
[0,T]
. We recall a result in
[
7
]
, which states that for
\tilde{\rho}=\rho+\varepsilon
and
\varepsilon>0
there exists a 2D control (that is, a two-variable function which is continuous, zero on degenerate rectangles, and super-additive):
I_{1}\times I_{2}\mapsto\omega(I_{1}\times I_{2})\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
such that

(3.1)

\displaystyle|R|_{\tilde{\rho}\text{-var},I_{1}\times I_{2}}^{\tilde{\rho}}\leq\omega(I_{1}\times I_{2}).

Given that Hypothesis
1.1
holds, it is easy to see that for
\varepsilon
sufficiently small, Hypothesis
1.1
still holds if
\rho
is replaced by
\tilde{\rho}=\rho+\varepsilon
. For the sake of conciseness, we will abuse notation and denote this
\tilde{\rho}
as
\rho
in the remainder of the paper. In particular, we will write (
3.1
) as:

(3.2)

\displaystyle|R|_{{\rho}\text{-var},I_{1}\times I_{2}}^{{\rho}}\leq\omega(I_{1}\times I_{2}).

Remark 3.2
.

Since
R
is of finite
\rho
-variation, the function
R(t,t)
,
t\in[0,T]
is also of finite
\rho
-variation. This shows that condition (1) in Hypothesis
1.1
implies that condition (2) holds true with
\rho^{\prime}=\rho
.

To see that
R(t,t)
,
t\in[0,T]
has finite
\rho
-variation, we can write

\displaystyle R(t,t)-R(s,s)=R_{st}^{0t}+R_{0s}^{st}.

Let
{\mathcal{P}}:0=t_{0}<\cdots<t_{n}=T
be a partition of
[0,T]
. Then we have

(3.3)

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}|R(t_{k+1},t_{k+1})-R(t_{k},t_{k})|^{\rho}\leq 2^{\rho-1}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\big(|R_{t_{k}t_{k+1}}^{0t_{k+1}}|^{\rho}+|R_{0t_{k}}^{t_{k}t_{k+1}}|^{\rho}\big)

\displaystyle\leq 2^{\rho-1}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\big(\omega([t_{k},t_{k+1}]\times[0,t_{k+1}])+\omega([0,t_{k}]\times[t_{k},t_{k+1}])\big),

where we have used relation (
3.2
).
Now by super-additivity of
\omega
we have

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}|R(t_{k+1},t_{k+1})-R(t_{k},t_{k})|^{\rho}\leq 2^{\rho-1}\omega([0,T]^{2}).

Taking supremum over all partitions
{\mathcal{P}}
of
[0,T]
we obtain that the
\rho
-variation of
R(t,t)
,
t\in[0,T]
is bounded by
2^{\rho-1}\omega([0,T]^{2})
.

Remark 3.3
.

It is easy to see that under condition (2) in Hypothesis
1.1
we can always take
\rho^{\prime}
such that
\rho^{\prime}<2
. We will make such choice of
\rho^{\prime}
in the following discussions.

Denote
x^{1}_{st}=x_{t}-x_{s}
and
x^{i}_{st}=(x^{1}_{st})^{i}/i!
,
i\in\mathbb{N}
, and
\ell=[2\rho]
. Let
{\mathcal{P}}:0=t_{0}<\cdots<t_{n}=T
be a partition on
[0,T]
. Consider the compensated Riemann-Stieltjes sum:

(3.4)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{J}}(f,dx,{\mathcal{P}}):=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}f^{(i-1)}(t_{k},x_{t_{k}})x^{i}_{t_{k}t_{k+1}},

where we denote
f^{(i)}=\frac{\partial^{i}f}{\partial x^{i}}
,
i\in\mathbb{N}
.
According to Lemma
4.4
(with
d=1
and
\partial f
replaced by
f
) and Remark
2.7
, if
f
has bounded derivatives in
x
up to order
\ell
and continuous first-order derivative in
t
, then we have the convergence:

(3.5)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{J}}(f,dx,{\mathcal{P}})\to\int_{0}^{T}f(t,x_{t})dx_{t}

almost surely as
|{\mathcal{P}}|\to 0
.

Following is our definition of the Skorohod-type Riemann integral. Recall that we denote
{\beta}_{k}=\mathbf{1}_{[t_{k},t_{k+1}]}
for
k\in\mathbb{N}
.

Definition 3.4
.

Let
f:(t,x)\mapsto f(t,x)
be a continuous function from
[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}
to
\mathbb{R}
.
Let
{\mathcal{P}}:0=t_{0}<\cdots<t_{n}=T
be a partition on
[0,T]
. Consider the following Skorohod-type Riemann sum:

(3.6)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{J}}(f,\delta x,{\mathcal{P}}):=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{[\rho]}\frac{1}{{i!}}\delta^{i}\big(f^{(i-1)}(t_{k},x_{t_{k}}){\beta}_{k}^{\otimes i}\big).

We define the Skorohod-type integral:

(3.7)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}f(t,x_{t})\delta x_{t}:=\lim_{|{\mathcal{P}}|\to 0}{\mathcal{J}}(f,\delta x,{\mathcal{P}}),

provided that the right-hand side of (
3.6
) is well-defined and that the limit in (
3.7
) exists in probability.

We also define the following Riemann sum:

(3.8)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{J}}(f^{(1)},dR,{\mathcal{P}})=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}f^{(1)}(t_{k},x_{t_{k}})\big(R(t_{k+1},t_{k+1})-R(t_{k},t_{k})\big).

Recall that the Gaussian process
x
is of finite
p
-variation for
p>2\rho
. For (
3.8
) to be convergent it suffices to assume that the diagonal
R(t,t)
,
t\in[0,T]
is of finite
\rho^{\prime}
-variation for
\frac{1}{\rho^{\prime}}+\frac{1}{p}>1
, or equivalently,
\frac{1}{\rho^{\prime}}+\frac{1}{2\rho}>1
.

Following is our main result in this section:

Theorem 3.5
.

Let
f:(t,x)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be a continuous function with bounded derivatives in
x
up to order
2\ell-1
and continuous first-order derivative in
t
.
Let
x
be a one-dimensional Gaussian process such that Hypothesis
1.1
holds.
Then the limit (
3.5
) exists and we have the following representation:

(3.9)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}f(t,x_{t})dx_{t}=\lim_{|{\mathcal{P}}|\to 0}\left({\mathcal{J}}(f,\delta x,{\mathcal{P}})+\frac{1}{2}{\mathcal{J}}(f^{(1)},dR,{\mathcal{P}})\right).

Assume further that
f^{(1)}=\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}
is Lipschitz in
(t,x)
, and that one of the following holds:

(1)
\rho<3/2
;

(2)
\frac{1}{2\rho}+\frac{1}{\rho^{\prime}}>1
.

Then both (
3.7
) and (
3.8
) converge as
|{\mathcal{P}}|\to 0
, and we have the Stratonovich-Skorohod integral formula:

(3.10)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}f(t,x_{t})dx_{t}=\int_{0}^{T}f(t,x_{t})\delta x_{t}+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}(t,x_{t})dR(t,t).

Proof.

The proof is divided into several steps. We start by deriving some useful algebraic results about (
3.4
) (from Step 1-3).

Step 1: Chaos decomposition of the compensated Riemann sum.

Denote
\sigma_{k}=\mathbb{E}[|x^{1}_{t_{k}t_{k+1}}|^{2}]^{1/2}
and
X_{k}=x^{1}_{t_{k}t_{k+1}}/\sigma_{k}=\delta({\beta}_{k}/\sigma_{k})
,
k=0,\dots,n-1
. It is clear that
X_{k}
is a standard normal random variable.
Recall that
x^{i}_{t_{k}t_{k+1}}=(x^{1}_{t_{k}t_{k+1}})^{i}/i!
. So we have:

(3.11)

\displaystyle x^{i}_{t_{k}t_{k+1}}=\frac{1}{{i!}}X_{k}^{i}\cdot\sigma_{k}^{i}.

By applying (
2.5
) and then (
2.4
) to (
3.11
) we obtain:

(3.12)

\displaystyle x^{i}_{t_{k}t_{k+1}}=\frac{1}{{i!}}\sum_{0\leq 2q\leq i}a^{i}_{i-2q}\delta^{i-2q}({\beta}_{k}^{\otimes(i-2q)})\sigma_{k}^{-(i-2q)}\cdot\sigma_{k}^{i},

where
a^{i}_{i-2q}
is given in (
2.5
).
Substituting (
3.12
) into (
3.4
) we get:

(3.13)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{J}}(f,dx,{\mathcal{P}})=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\sum_{0\leq 2q\leq i}\frac{1}{{i!}}a^{i}_{i-2q}f^{(i-1)}(t_{k},x_{t_{k}})\delta^{i-2q}({\beta}_{k}^{\otimes(i-2q)})\cdot\sigma_{k}^{2q}.

Apply Lemma
2.2
with
F=f^{(i-1)}(t_{k},x_{t_{k}})
,
h={\beta}_{k}
and with
i
replaced by
i-2q
. Then relation (
2.7
) becomes:

\displaystyle\langle D^{j}F,h^{\otimes j}\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}^{\otimes j}}\cdot h^{\otimes(i-2q-j)}=f^{(i+j-1)}(t_{k},x_{t_{k}}){\beta}_{k}^{\otimes(i-2q-j)}\cdot{\alpha}_{k}^{j}\,,

where we denote
{\alpha}_{k}=\langle\mathbf{1}_{[0,t_{k}]},{\beta}_{k}\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}}
.
From (
2.9
) we also have:

(3.14)

\displaystyle f^{(i-1)}(t_{k},x_{t_{k}})\delta^{i-2q}({\beta}_{k}^{\otimes(i-2q)})=\sum_{j=0}^{i-2q}\binom{i-2q}{j}\delta^{i-2q-j}\big(f^{(i+j-1)}(t_{k},x_{t_{k}}){\beta}_{k}^{\otimes(i-2q-j)}\big)\cdot{\alpha}_{k}^{j}\,.

Reporting (
3.14
) to (
3.13
) we obtain:

(3.15)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{J}}(f,dx,{\mathcal{P}})=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\sum_{0\leq 2q\leq i}\sum_{j=0}^{i-2q}{\mathcal{N}}({i,q,j})\,,

where

(3.16)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{N}}({i,q,j})

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\sigma_{k}^{2q}\frac{a^{i}_{i-2q}}{{i!}}\binom{i-2q}{j}\cdot\delta^{i-2q-j}\big(f^{(i+j-1)}(t_{k},x_{t_{k}}){\beta}_{k}^{\otimes(i-2q-j)}\big)\cdot{\alpha}_{k}^{j}\,.

Step 2: Some index sets.

We introduce the following useful index sets:

(3.17)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{A}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{(i,q,j)\in\bar{\mathbb{N}}^{3}:1\leq i\leq\ell,\,2q\leq i,\,j\leq i-2q\},

\displaystyle{\mathcal{A}}_{L}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{(i,q,j)\in{\mathcal{A}}:i-2q-j=L\},\qquad\qquad\qquad L=0,\dots,\ell,

(3.18)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{A}}_{L\tau}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{(i,q,j)\in{\mathcal{A}}_{L}:q+j=\tau\},\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\tau=0,\dots,\ell-L,

(3.19)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{I}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{(L,\tau)\in\bar{\mathbb{N}}^{2}:L\leq\ell,\tau\leq\ell-L\}.

It is clear that we have the relation:

(3.20)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{A}}=\bigcup_{L=0}^{\ell}{\mathcal{A}}_{L}=\bigcup_{L=0}^{\ell}\bigcup_{\tau=0}^{\ell-L}{\mathcal{A}}_{L\tau}=\bigcup_{(L,\tau)\in{\mathcal{I}}}{\mathcal{A}}_{L\tau}\,.

From (
3.15
) we can thus write

(3.21)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{J}}(f,dx,n)=\sum_{(i,q,j)\in{\mathcal{A}}}{\mathcal{N}}({i,q,j})=\sum_{(L,\tau)\in{\mathcal{I}}}\sum_{(i,q,j)\in{\mathcal{A}}_{L\tau}}{\mathcal{N}}({i,q,j})\,.

Since
i-2q-j=L
and
q+j=\tau
, we have

(3.22)

\displaystyle i+j=L+2\tau.

On the other hand, recall that
a^{i}_{i-2q}
is defined in (
2.5
). This gives:

(3.23)

\displaystyle\frac{a^{i}_{i-2q}}{{i!}}\binom{i-2q}{j}=\frac{1}{2^{q}q!j!L!}.

Substituting relations (
3.22
)-(
3.23
) into (
3.21
) we obtain:

(3.24)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{J}}(f,dx,{\mathcal{P}})=\sum_{(L,\tau)\in{\mathcal{I}}}\sum_{(i,q,j)\in{\mathcal{A}}_{L\tau}}{\mathcal{M}}({L,\tau,i,q,j})\,,

where

(3.25)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{M}}({L,\tau,i,q,j})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\sigma_{k}^{2q}\frac{1}{2^{q}q!j!L!}\cdot\delta^{L}\big(f^{(L+2\tau-1)}(t_{k},x_{t_{k}}){\beta}_{k}^{\otimes L}\big)\cdot{\alpha}_{k}^{j}\,.

In the following we make some observations about the index sets.
First, by the definition of
{\mathcal{A}}_{L}
it is easy to see that we have

(3.26)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{A}}_{L}=\{(i,q,j)\in\bar{\mathbb{N}}^{3}:1\leq i\leq\ell,\,i-2q-j=L\},\qquad L=0,\dots,\ell.

From (
3.26
) one can show that for
\tau:0\leq 2\tau\leq\ell-L
such that
(L,\tau)\neq(0,0)
we have

(3.27)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{A}}_{L\tau}=\{(L+\tau,0,\tau),(L+\tau+1,1,\tau-1),\dots,(L+2\tau,\tau,0)\}.

Indeed, it is easy to verify that the elements in the right-hand side of (
3.27
) belongs to (
3.18
). Note that the condition
0\leq 2\tau\leq\ell-L
is required to make sure that the elements satisfy the condition
i\leq\ell
in (
3.26
). On the other hand, using relation (
3.22
) and
i-2q-j=L
it is clear that (
3.18
) is contained in the set (
3.27
).

In the special case when
\tau=0
and
L=1,\dots,\ell
, relations (
3.26
)-(
3.27
) show that
{\mathcal{A}}_{L0}=\{(L,0,0)\}
and
{\mathcal{A}}_{\ell}={\mathcal{A}}_{\ell 0}=\{(\ell,0,0)\}
. Note also that since
i\geq 1
we have
{\mathcal{A}}_{00}=\emptyset
.

Step 3: A further decomposition.

Recall that
{\alpha}_{k}=\langle\mathbf{1}_{[0,t_{k}]},{\beta}_{k}\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}}
. By Lemma
2.5
we have
{\alpha}_{k}=-\frac{1}{2}\sigma_{k}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}(R_{0t_{k+1}}^{0t_{k+1}}-R_{0t_{k}}^{0t_{k}})
.
Applying the binomial expansion to
{\alpha}_{k}^{j}
in
(
3.25
) gives:

(3.28)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{M}}(L,\tau,i,q,j)=\sum_{j^{\prime}=0}^{j}{\mathcal{M}}(L,\tau,i,q,j,j^{\prime}),

where

(3.29)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{M}}(L,\tau,i,q,j,j^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\delta^{L}\big(f^{(L+2\tau-1)}(t_{k},x_{t_{k}}){\beta}_{k}^{\otimes L}\big)\sigma_{k}^{2(q+j^{\prime})}

\displaystyle\qquad\cdot\frac{1}{2^{q}q!j!L!}\cdot{j\choose j^{\prime}}(-\frac{1}{2})^{j^{\prime}}\cdot(\frac{1}{2})^{j-j^{\prime}}(R_{0t_{k+1}}^{0t_{k+1}}-R_{0t_{k}}^{0t_{k}})^{j-j^{\prime}}.

In the following steps we consider
{\mathcal{M}}(L,\tau,i,q,j,j^{\prime})
in six cases:

(i)
j-j^{\prime}\geq 2
;

(ii)
j-j^{\prime}=1
,
L+\tau>1
;

(iii)
j=j^{\prime}
,
L+\tau>\rho
;

(iv)
j-j^{\prime}=1
,
L+\tau=1
;

(v)
j=j^{\prime}
,
L+\tau\leq\rho
,
\tau>0
;

(vi)
j=j^{\prime}
,
L\leq\rho
,
\tau=0
.

Note that
it is clear that the above six cases are mutually exclusive, and that their union covers all possible values of
(L,\tau,i,q,j,j^{\prime})
. So from (
3.24
) and (
3.28
) we have

(3.30)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{J}}(f,dx,{\mathcal{P}})=\left(\sum_{\text{case (i)}}+\cdots+\sum_{\text{case (vi)}}\right){\mathcal{M}}(L,\tau,i,q,j,j^{\prime}).

Step 4: Case (i) and (ii) - a preliminary estimate.

By computing the second moment of (
3.29
) we get the following estimate:

(3.31)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[|{\mathcal{M}}(L,\tau,i,q,j,j^{\prime})|^{2}]

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\sum_{k,k^{\prime}=0}^{n-1}\Big|\mathbb{E}\Big[\delta^{L}\big(f^{(L+2\tau-1)}(t_{k},x_{t_{k}}){\beta}_{k}^{\otimes L}\big)\cdot\delta^{L}\big(f^{(L+2\tau-1)}(t_{k^{\prime}},x_{t_{k^{\prime}}}){\beta}_{k^{\prime}}^{\otimes L}\big)\Big]\Big|

\displaystyle\quad\cdot\sigma_{k}^{2(q+j^{\prime})}\sigma_{k^{\prime}}^{2(q+j^{\prime})}\cdot\Big|R_{0t_{k+1}}^{0t_{k+1}}-R_{0t_{k}}^{0t_{k}}\Big|^{j-j^{\prime}}\cdot\Big|R_{0t_{k^{\prime}+1}}^{0t_{k^{\prime}+1}}-R_{0t_{k^{\prime}}}^{0t_{k^{\prime}}}\Big|^{j-j^{\prime}}.

Now by applying Lemma
2.4
to the expected value in the right-hand side of (
3.31
) we obtain:

(3.32)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\big|{\mathcal{M}}(L,\tau,i,q,j,j^{\prime})\big|^{2}\big]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{a=0}^{L}\sum_{k,k^{\prime}=0}^{n-1}\Big|\langle{\beta}_{k},{\beta}_{k^{\prime}}\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}}^{a}\cdot\langle{\beta}_{k^{\prime}},\mathbf{1}_{[0,t_{k}]}\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}}^{L-a}\cdot\langle{\beta}_{k},\mathbf{1}_{[0,t_{k^{\prime}}]}\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}}^{L-a}\Big|

\displaystyle\cdot\sigma_{k}^{2(q+j^{\prime})}\sigma_{k^{\prime}}^{2(q+j^{\prime})}\cdot\Big|R_{0t_{k+1}}^{0t_{k+1}}-R_{0t_{k}}^{0t_{k}}\Big|^{j-j^{\prime}}\cdot\Big|R_{0t_{k^{\prime}+1}}^{0t_{k^{\prime}+1}}-R_{0t_{k^{\prime}}}^{0t_{k^{\prime}}}\Big|^{j-j^{\prime}}.\qquad

Let
p_{i}
,
i=1,2,3
be constants such that

(3.33)

\displaystyle p_{i}>1,\quad i=1,2,3\qquad\text{and}\qquad\frac{1}{p_{1}}+\frac{1}{p_{2}}+\frac{1}{p_{3}}=1.

By applying Hölder’s inequality to the right-hand side of (
3.32
) we obtain:

(3.34)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[|{\mathcal{M}}(L,\tau,i,q,j,j^{\prime})|^{2}]\leq\sum_{a=0}^{L}J_{1}\cdot J_{2}\cdot J_{3}\,,

where

(3.35)

\displaystyle J_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Big(\sum_{k,k^{\prime}=0}^{n-1}\big|\langle{\beta}_{k},{\beta}_{k^{\prime}}\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}}\big|^{ap_{1}}\Big)^{1/p_{1}}

(3.36)

\displaystyle J_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Big(\sum_{k,k^{\prime}=0}^{n-1}\big|\langle{\beta}_{k^{\prime}},\mathbf{1}_{[0,t_{k}]}\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}}\cdot\langle{\beta}_{k},\mathbf{1}_{[0,t_{k^{\prime}}]}\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}}\big|^{(L-a)p_{2}}\cdot(\sigma_{k}\sigma_{k^{\prime}})^{2(q+j^{\prime})p_{2}}\Big)^{1/p_{2}}

\displaystyle J_{3}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Big(\sum_{k,k^{\prime}=0}^{n-1}\big|R_{0t_{k+1}}^{0t_{k+1}}-R_{0t_{k}}^{0t_{k}}\big|^{(j-j^{\prime})p_{3}}\cdot\big|R_{0t_{k^{\prime}+1}}^{0t_{k^{\prime}+1}}-R_{0t_{k^{\prime}}}^{0t_{k^{\prime}}}\big|^{(j-j^{\prime})p_{3}}\Big)^{1/p_{3}}.

In the following we specify our choices of
p_{i}
’s. Recall that by Hypothesis
1.1
together with Remark
3.2
-
3.3
we can always find
\rho^{\prime}<2
such that
\frac{1}{\rho}+\frac{1}{\rho^{\prime}}>1
.
We first show that in both cases (i) and (ii),
we have the following relation:

(3.37)

\displaystyle\frac{a}{\rho}+\frac{L-a+q+j^{\prime}}{\rho}+\frac{j-j^{\prime}}{\rho^{\prime}}>1.

We rewrite (
3.37
) as:

(3.38)

\displaystyle{(L+q+j)}+(j-j^{\prime})(\frac{\rho}{\rho^{\prime}}-1)>\rho.

In case (i), since
j-j^{\prime}\geq 2
we have
L+q+j\geq 2
and thus

\displaystyle{(L+q+j)}+(j-j^{\prime})(\frac{\rho}{\rho^{\prime}}-1)\geq 2+2(\frac{\rho}{\rho^{\prime}}-1)=2\frac{\rho}{\rho^{\prime}}.

Since
\rho^{\prime}<2
(see Remark
3.3
) relation (
3.38
) and thus (
3.37
) follows.

We turn to case (ii). In this case we have
L+q+j=L+\tau\geq 2
and
j-j^{\prime}=1
. It follows that

\displaystyle{(L+q+j)}+(j-j^{\prime})(\frac{\rho}{\rho^{\prime}}-1)\geq 2+\frac{\rho}{\rho^{\prime}}-1=1+\frac{\rho}{\rho^{\prime}}.

Since
\frac{1}{\rho}+\frac{1}{\rho^{\prime}}>1
, we obtain that the above is strictly greater than
\rho
. We again have relation (
3.38
) and thus (
3.37
). In summary, equation (
3.37
) holds for both case (i) and (ii).

Relation (
3.37
) implies that we can find
p_{i}
,
i=1,2,3
such that relations in (
3.33
) holds, and we have

\displaystyle\frac{a}{\rho}>\frac{1}{p_{1}},\qquad\frac{L-a+q+j^{\prime}}{\rho}>\frac{1}{p_{2}},\qquad\frac{j-j^{\prime}}{\rho^{\prime}}>\frac{1}{p_{3}},

or equivalently,

(3.39)

\displaystyle ap_{1}>\rho,\qquad(L-a+q+j^{\prime})p_{2}>\rho,\qquad(j-j^{\prime})p_{3}>\rho^{\prime}.

Step 5: Case (i) and (ii) - convergence of
{\mathcal{M}}(L,\tau,i,q,j,j^{\prime})
.

Since
ap_{1}>\rho
we have:

(3.40)

\displaystyle J_{1}\leq\Big(\max_{k,k^{\prime}}\big|\langle{\beta}_{k},{\beta}_{k^{\prime}}\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}}\big|^{ap_{1}-\rho}\cdot\sum_{k,k^{\prime}=0}^{n-1}|\langle{\beta}_{k},{\beta}_{k^{\prime}}\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}}|^{\rho}\Big)^{1/p_{1}}.

Note that
\langle{\beta}_{k},{\beta}_{k^{\prime}}\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}}=R_{t_{k}t_{k+1}}^{t_{k^{\prime}}t_{k^{\prime}+1}}
. Substituting this into (
3.40
), and applying relation (
3.2
) in Remark
3.1
and then the additivity of the 2D control
\omega
we obtain:

(3.41)

\displaystyle J_{1}\leq\Big(\max_{k,k^{\prime}}\big|R_{t_{k}t_{k+1}}^{t_{k^{\prime}}t_{k^{\prime}+1}}\big|^{ap_{1}-\rho}\cdot\omega([0,T]^{2})\Big)^{1/p_{1}}.

By uniform continuity of
R
we thus have
J_{1}\to 0
as
|{\mathcal{P}}|\to 0
.

We turn to the estimate of
J_{2}
. By definition of
\rho
-variation of
R
together with relation (
3.2
) we have

\displaystyle|\langle{\beta}_{k},\mathbf{1}_{[0,t_{k^{\prime}}]}\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}}|\leq\omega([t_{k},t_{k+1}]\times[0,T])^{1/\rho},

(3.42)

\displaystyle\sigma_{k}^{2}=|\langle{\beta}_{k},{\beta}_{k}\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}}|\leq\omega([t_{k},t_{k+1}]\times[0,T])^{1/\rho}.

Applying (
3.42
) to
J_{2}
we obtain:

\displaystyle J_{2}\leq\Big(\sum_{k,k^{\prime}=0}^{n-1}\omega([t_{k},t_{k+1}]\times[0,T])^{(L-a+q+j^{\prime})p_{2}/\rho}\cdot\omega([t_{k^{\prime}},t_{k^{\prime}+1}]\times[0,T])^{(L-a+q+j^{\prime})p_{2}/\rho}\Big)^{1/p_{2}}

\displaystyle=\Big(\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\omega([t_{k},t_{k+1}]\times[0,T])^{(L-a+q+j^{\prime})p_{2}/\rho}\Big)^{2/p_{2}}.

Recall that from (
3.39
) we have
(L-a+q+j^{\prime})p_{2}>\rho
. As in (
3.41
) we can thus show that
J_{2}\to 0
as
|{\mathcal{P}}|\to 0
.
A similar argument together with the fact that
R_{0t}^{0t}
,
t\in[0,T]
is of finite
\rho^{\prime}
-variation (see Remark
3.2
) shows that we also have
J_{3}\to 0
as
|{\mathcal{P}}|\to 0
.

Recall the estimate (
3.34
). So the convergences of
J_{i}
,
i=1,2,3
imply that

(3.43)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[|{\mathcal{M}}(L,\tau,i,q,j,j^{\prime})|^{2}]\to 0

as
|{\mathcal{P}}|\to 0

in both cases (i) and (ii).

Step 6: Case (iii).

As for cases (i) and (ii), it can be shown that
there exist constants
p_{1},p_{2}>1
such that

(3.44)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{p_{1}}+\frac{1}{p_{2}}=1,\qquad ap_{1}>\rho,\qquad(L-a+q+j^{\prime})p_{2}>\rho.

Note that since
j=j^{\prime}
we have
(R_{0t_{k+1}}^{0t_{k+1}}-R_{0t_{k}}^{0t_{k}})^{j-j^{\prime}}=1
in
(
3.29
).
Applying Hölder inequality to (
3.32
) we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[|{\mathcal{M}}(L,\tau,i,q,j,j^{\prime})|^{2}]\leq\sum_{a=0}^{L}J_{1}\cdot J_{2}\,,

where
J_{1}
and
J_{2}
are given as in (
3.35
)-(
3.36
) with
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
defined in (
3.44
). A similar argument as in the previous step then shows that the convergence (
3.43
) also holds in case (iii).

In summary of the convergence of
{\mathcal{M}}(L,\tau,i,q,j,j^{\prime})
for case (i)-(iii) obtained in Step 5-6, we have shown that

(3.45)

\displaystyle\lim_{|{\mathcal{P}}|\to 0}\left(\sum_{\text{case (i)}}+\sum_{\text{case (ii)}}+\sum_{\text{case (iii)}}\right){\mathcal{M}}(L,\tau,i,q,j,j^{\prime})=0.

Step 7: Case (iv).

In this case we have
j=j^{\prime}+1\geq 1
. This
implies that
j=1
and thus
L=q=j^{\prime}=0
and
i=\tau=1
, and so

\displaystyle\sum_{\text{case (iv)}}{\mathcal{M}}(L,\tau,i,q,j,j^{\prime})={\mathcal{M}}(0,1,1,0,1,0)=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}f^{(1)}(t_{k},x_{t_{k}})\cdot\frac{1}{2}(R_{0t_{k+1}}^{0t_{k+1}}-R_{0t_{k}}^{0t_{k}})

(3.46)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}{\mathcal{J}}(f^{(1)},dR,{\mathcal{P}}).

Step 8: Case (v).
In this case
(
3.29
) becomes

(3.47)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{M}}(L,\tau,i,q,j,j)=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\delta^{L}\big(f^{(L+2\tau-1)}(t_{k},x_{t_{k}}){\beta}_{k}^{\otimes L}\big)\cdot\sigma_{k}^{2\tau}\cdot\frac{1}{2^{q}q!j!L!}\cdot(-\frac{1}{2})^{j},

and we have
L+\tau\leq[\rho]
. So
L+2\tau\leq 2L+2\tau\leq 2[\rho]
. Recall also that
\ell=[2\rho]
. It follows that we have
L+2\tau\leq\ell
.
This implies that the relation (
3.27
) holds. Summing up (
3.47
) for
(i,q,j)\in{\mathcal{A}}_{L\tau}
and taking into account (
3.27
) we thus have:

\displaystyle\sum_{(i,q,j)\in{\mathcal{A}}_{L\tau}}{\mathcal{M}}(L,\tau,i,q,j,j)=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\delta^{L}\big(f^{(L+2\tau-1)}(t_{k},x_{t_{k}}){\beta}_{k}^{\otimes L}\big)\cdot\sigma_{k}^{2\tau}\frac{1}{L!}

(3.48)

\displaystyle\cdot\sum_{(q,j):0\leq q+j\leq\tau}\frac{1}{2^{q}}\frac{1}{q!j!}\cdot(-\frac{1}{2})^{j}.

Note that the second summation in (
3.48
) is equal to
(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{2})^{\tau}\cdot\frac{1}{\tau!}
. Since
\tau>0
in case (v), we obtain that (
3.48
) equals 0. It follows that

(3.49)

\displaystyle\sum_{\text{case (v)}}{\mathcal{M}}(L,\tau,i,q,j,j^{\prime})=\sum_{L+\tau\leq\rho,\tau>0}\,\sum_{(i,q,j)\in{\mathcal{A}}_{L\tau}}{\mathcal{M}}(L,\tau,i,q,j,j)=0.

Step 9: Conclusion.

In case (vi) it is easy to see that we have
L=1,\dots,[\rho]
,
\tau=q=j=j^{\prime}=0
and
i=L
. Precisely, we have

(3.50)

\displaystyle\text{case (vi)}=\{({L,0,L,0,0,0}):L=1,\dots,[\rho]\}.

Applying (
3.45
), (
3.46
), (
3.49
) and (
3.50
) to (
3.30
) we obtain:

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}{\mathcal{J}}(f,dx,{\mathcal{P}})=\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{(L,\tau)\in{\mathcal{I}}}\sum_{(i,q,j)\in{\mathcal{A}}_{L\tau}}\sum_{j^{\prime}=0}^{j}{\mathcal{M}}({L,\tau,i,q,j,j^{\prime}})

(3.51)

\displaystyle=\lim_{|{\mathcal{P}}|\to 0}\left(\sum_{L=1}^{[\rho]}{\mathcal{M}}({L,0,L,0,0,0})+\frac{1}{2}{\mathcal{J}}(f^{(1)},dR,{\mathcal{P}})\right).

From (
3.29
) we have

(3.52)

\displaystyle\sum_{L=1}^{[\rho]}{\mathcal{M}}({L,0,L,0,0,0})=\sum_{L=1}^{[\rho]}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac{1}{L!}\delta^{L}(f^{(L-1)}(t_{k},x_{t_{k}}){\beta}_{k}^{\otimes L})={\mathcal{J}}(f,\delta x,{\mathcal{P}}).

Substituting (
3.52
) into (
3.51
) we obtain that relation (
3.9
) holds.

When
f^{(1)}
is Lipschitz in
(t,x)
, the fact that
x
is of finite
p
-variation for
p>2\rho
(see Remark
2.7
) implies that
f^{(1)}(t,x_{t})
is also of finite
p
-variation. So if
\rho<3/2
or
\frac{1}{2\rho}+\frac{1}{\rho^{\prime}}>1
we have that

{\mathcal{J}}(f^{(1)},dR,{\mathcal{P}})
converges to the Young integral
\int_{0}^{T}f^{(1)}(t,x_{t})dR(t,t)
as
|{\mathcal{P}}|\to 0
(see Remark
2.6
). Applying this convergence to (
3.9
) we obtain that relation (
3.10
) holds.
The proof is now complete.
∎

4.
Multidimensional Skorohod-Stratonovich conversion formula

In this section, we consider the Skorohod-Stratonovich conversion formula for a multidimensional Gaussian process. In Section
4.1
we introduce a Stratonovich-type integral for continuous paths of finite
p
-variations for
p\geq 1
. Then in Section
4.2
we introduce the Skorohod-Riemann integral and prove Theorem
1.2
.

4.1.
Stratonovich-type integral

In the following we introduce a Stratonovich-type integral. For a vector
i
in
\bar{\mathbb{N}}^{d}
we denote by
i_{l}
its
l
th component, and so we write
i=(i_{1},\dots,i_{d})
.
For a real-valued function
f(t,x)
,
t\in[0,T]
,
x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
we denote the partial differential operators:

\displaystyle\partial_{0}=\frac{\partial}{\partial t},\qquad\partial_{l}=\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{l}},\qquad l=1,\dots,d.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
x_{t}=(x_{1}(t),\dots,x_{d}(t))
,
t\in[0,T]
be a continuous path with values in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and denote
x^{1,l}_{st}:=x_{l}(t)-x_{l}(s)
,
l=1,\dots,d
and
x^{1}_{st}:=x_{t}-x_{s}
for
s,t\in[0,T]
. Let
f
be a continuous function on
[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Let
{\mathcal{P}}
be a partition of
[0,T]
:
0=t_{0}<\cdots<t_{n}=T
.
Consider the compensated Riemann sum:

(4.1)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{J}}(\partial f,dx,{\mathcal{P}}):=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\sum_{m=1}^{\ell}\frac{1}{m!}\partial^{m}f(t_{k},x_{t_{k}})(x_{t_{k}t_{k+1}}^{1})^{\otimes m},

where
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
and we denote

(4.2)

\displaystyle\partial^{m}f(t_{k},x_{t_{k}})(x_{t_{k}t_{k+1}}^{1})^{\otimes m}=\sum_{j_{1},\dots,j_{m}=1}^{d}\partial_{j_{1}}\cdots\partial_{j_{m}}f(t_{k},x_{t_{k}})x^{1,j_{1}}_{t_{k}t_{k+1}}\cdots x^{1,j_{m}}_{t_{k}t_{k+1}}.

We define the Stratonovich-type integral:

(4.3)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\partial f(t,x_{t})dx_{t}:=\lim_{|{\mathcal{P}}|\to 0}{\mathcal{J}}(\partial f,dx,{\mathcal{P}}),

given that the right-hand side of (
4.1
) is well-defined and that the limit in (
4.3
) exists.

Remark 4.2
.

Let
({\bf x}^{1}_{st},\dots,{\bf x}^{\ell}_{st})
,
s,t\in[0,T]
be a geometric rough path of finite
p
-variation such that
{\bf x}^{1}_{st}=x_{t}-x_{s}
(see e.g.
[
8
,
9
,
17
]
).
By some algebraic computations it can be shown that

\displaystyle\partial^{m}f(t_{k},x_{t_{k}})\cdot{\bf x}^{m}_{t_{k}t_{k+1}}=\frac{1}{m!}\partial^{m}f(t_{k},x_{t_{k}})\cdot(x_{t_{k}t_{k+1}}^{1})^{\otimes m},\qquad m=1,\dots,d,

where the products in both sides of (
4.4
) are dot products.
Recall that
the rough integral
\int_{0}^{T}\partial f(t,x_{t})d{\bf x}_{t}
can be defined as:

(4.4)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\partial f(t,x_{t})d{\bf x}_{t}=\lim_{|{\mathcal{P}}|\to 0}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\sum_{m=1}^{[p]}\partial^{m}f(t_{k},x_{t_{k}})\cdot{\bf x}^{m}_{t_{k}t_{k+1}}.

It follows that the rough integral
\int_{0}^{T}\partial f(t,x_{t})d{\bf x}_{t}
coincides with the Stratonovich-type integral defined in (
4.3
) given that both are well-defined.

Remark 4.3
.

For a vector
i\in\bar{\mathbb{N}}^{d}
we denote
|i|=i_{1}+\cdots+i_{d}
and

\displaystyle\partial^{i}=:\partial_{1}^{i_{1}}\cdots\partial_{d}^{i_{d}}=:\frac{\partial^{|i|}}{\partial x_{1}^{i_{1}}\cdots\partial x_{d}^{i_{d}}}.

From (
4.1
) it is easy to see that we have

(4.5)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{J}}(\partial f,dx,{\mathcal{P}})=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\sum_{i\in\bar{\mathbb{N}}^{d}:1\leq|i|\leq\ell}\partial^{i}f(t_{k},x_{t_{k}})\cdot\left[\prod_{l=1}^{d}\frac{1}{i_{l}!}(x^{1,l}_{t_{k}t_{k+1}})^{i_{l}}\right].

The following result shows that the Stratonovich-type integral in (
4.3
) is well-defined given proper regularity conditions of
f
.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
x_{t}
,
t\in[0,T]
be a continuous path with values in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that it has finite
p
-variation for
p\geq 1
. Denote
\ell=[p]
.
Let
f:(t,x)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to f(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}
be a continuous function such that it has bounded derivatives in
x
up to order
(\ell+1)
and continuous first-order derivative in
t
. Then the convergence (
4.3
) holds, and we have the relation:

(4.6)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\partial f(t,x_{t})dx_{t}=f(T,x_{T})-f(0,x_{0})-\int_{0}^{T}\partial_{0}f(u,x_{u})du.

Proof.

Take
s,t\in[0,T]
.
Define the function

\varphi({\lambda})=f(s,x_{s}+{\lambda}\cdot x^{1}_{st})
,

{\lambda}\in[0,1].

By differentiating
\varphi
for
m
times we get:

(4.7)

\displaystyle\varphi^{(m)}({\lambda})=\partial^{m}f(s,x_{s}+{\lambda}\cdot x^{1}_{st})\cdot(x^{1}_{st})^{\otimes m},\qquad m\in\mathbb{N},

where the right-hand side of (
4.7
) should be interpreted as a dot product similar to (
4.2
).
Applying the Taylor expansion we have

(4.8)

\displaystyle\varphi(1)-\varphi(0)=\sum_{m=1}^{\ell}\frac{1}{m!}\varphi^{(m)}(0)+\frac{1}{(\ell+1)!}\varphi^{(\ell+1)}(\theta),

where
\theta
is a value in
[0,1]
.
Note that the left-hand side of (
4.8
) is just
f(s,x_{t})-f(s,x_{s})
.
So relation (
4.8
) together with (
4.7
) gives:

\displaystyle f(t,x_{t})-f(s,x_{s})=[f(t,x_{t})-f(s,x_{t})]+[f(s,x_{t})-f(s,x_{s})]

\displaystyle=[f(t,x_{t})-f(s,x_{t})]+\sum_{m=1}^{\ell}\frac{1}{m!}\partial^{m}f(s,x_{s})(x^{1}_{st})^{\otimes m}

(4.9)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{(\ell+1)!}\partial^{\ell+1}f(s,x_{s}+\theta\cdot x^{1}_{st})(x^{1}_{st})^{\otimes(\ell+1)}.

Consider a partition
{\mathcal{P}}
of
[0,T]
:
0=t_{0}<\cdots<t_{n}=T
, and write

(4.10)

\displaystyle f(T,x_{T})-f(0,x_{0})=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}[f(t_{k+1},x_{t_{k+1}})-f(t_{k},x_{t_{k}})].

Applying (
4.9
) to the right-hand side of (
4.10
) with
s=t_{k}
and
t=t_{k+1}
we get:

(4.11)

\displaystyle f(T,x_{T})-f(0,x_{0})={\mathcal{A}}_{1}+{\mathcal{J}}(\partial f,dx,{\mathcal{P}})+{\mathcal{A}}_{2}\,,

where

\displaystyle{\mathcal{A}}_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}[f(t_{k+1},x_{t_{k+1}})-f(t_{k},x_{t_{k+1}})]

\displaystyle{\mathcal{A}}_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac{1}{(\ell+1)!}\partial^{\ell+1}f(t_{k},x_{t_{k}}+\theta_{k}\cdot x^{1}_{t_{k}t_{k+1}})(x^{1}_{t_{k}t_{k+1}})^{\otimes(\ell+1)},

\theta_{k}
is a value in
[0,1]
, and recall that
{\mathcal{J}}(\partial f,dx,{\mathcal{P}})
is defined in (
4.1
).
By mean value theorem it is easy to see that
{\mathcal{A}}_{1}
is equal to a Riemann sum of the integral
\int_{0}^{T}\partial_{0}f(t,x_{t})dt
. This implies that

(4.12)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{A}}_{1}\to\int_{0}^{T}\partial_{0}f(t,x_{t})dt

as
|{\mathcal{P}}|\to 0
.
On the other hand, since
\partial^{\ell+1}f
is bounded we have the estimate:

\displaystyle|{\mathcal{A}}_{2}|\lesssim\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}|x^{1}_{t_{k}t_{k+1}}|^{\ell+1}.

Invoking the assumption that
x
has finite
p
-variation and the fact that
\ell+1=[p]+1>p
, we can thus apply a similar argument as in (
3.41
) to show that
{\mathcal{A}}_{2}\to 0
as
|{\mathcal{P}}|\to 0
.
Now, sending
|{\mathcal{P}}|\to 0
in (
4.11
) and applying the convergences of
{\mathcal{A}}_{1}
in (
4.12
) and
{\mathcal{A}}_{2}
we obtain:

\displaystyle f(T,x_{T})-f(0,x_{0})=\int_{0}^{T}\partial_{0}f(t,x_{t})dt+\lim_{|{\mathcal{P}}|\to 0}{\mathcal{J}}(\partial f,dx,{\mathcal{P}}).

This shows that the limit of
{\mathcal{J}}(\partial f,dx,{\mathcal{P}})
exists, and it satisfies the relation (
4.6
). The proof is now complete.
∎

4.2.
Riemann-Skorohod integral and conversion formula

Let
x_{l}
,
l=1,\dots,d
be independent Gaussian processes with covariance functions
R_{l}(s,t)=\mathbb{E}[x_{l}(s)x_{l}(t)]
. For each
x_{l}
we can thus define the Hilbert space
{\mathcal{H}}_{l}
generated by
R_{l}
, the Malliavin derivative operator
D_{l}
and the divergence operators
\delta_{l}
as in Sections
2.2
-
2.3
.
In the following, we define the multidimensional version of the Skorohod-type integral:

Definition 4.5
.

Consider the following Skorohod-type Riemann sum:

\displaystyle{\mathcal{J}}(\partial f,\delta x,{\mathcal{P}}):=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\sum_{m=1}^{[\rho]}\frac{1}{m!}\sum_{j_{1},\dots,j_{m}=1}^{d}\delta_{j_{1}}\cdots\delta_{j_{m}}\Big(\partial_{j_{1}}\cdots\partial_{j_{m}}f(t_{k},x_{t_{k}}){\beta}_{k}^{\otimes m}\Big),

where
\delta_{j_{1}}\cdots\delta_{j_{m}}
is the divergence operator adjoint to the derivative operator
D_{j_{1}}\cdots D_{j_{m}}
.
As in Remark
4.3
, we can show that

\displaystyle{\mathcal{J}}(\partial f,\delta x,{\mathcal{P}})=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\,\sum_{i\in\bar{\mathbb{N}}^{d}:1\leq|i|\leq[\rho]}\left[\prod_{l=1}^{d}\frac{1}{i_{l}!}\right]\cdot\delta^{i}\Big(\partial^{i}f(t_{k},x_{t_{k}}){\beta}_{k}^{\otimes|i|}\Big),

where
\delta^{i}:=\delta_{1}^{i_{1}}\cdots\delta_{d}^{i_{d}}

is the divergence operator adjoint to
D^{i_{1}}_{1}\cdots D^{i_{d}}_{d}
.
We define the Skorohod-type integral as:

(4.13)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\partial f(t,x_{t})dx_{t}:=\lim_{|{\mathcal{P}}|\to 0}{\mathcal{J}}(\partial f,\delta x,{\mathcal{P}}),

given that
{\mathcal{J}}(\partial f,\delta x,{\mathcal{P}})
is well-defined and that the limit exists in probability.

Following is our main result. Recall that we denote
\partial^{2}_{ll}=\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x_{l}^{2}}
and

(4.14)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{J}}(\partial^{2}f,dR,{\mathcal{P}}):=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\sum_{l=1}^{d}{\partial^{2}_{ll}f}(t_{k},x_{t_{k}})\cdot\big(R_{l}(t_{k+1},t_{k+1})-R_{l}(t_{k},t_{k})\big).

Theorem 4.6
.

Let
x
be a
\mathbb{R}^{d}
-valued Gaussian process satisfying Hypothesis
1.1
. Let
\ell=[2\rho]
.
Let
f:(t,x)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be a continuous function with bounded derivatives in
x
up to order
2\ell
and continuous first-order derivative in
t
.
Then the limit in (
4.3
) exists and we have the relation:

(4.15)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\partial f(t,x_{t})dx_{t}=\lim_{|{\mathcal{P}}|\to 0}\left({\mathcal{J}}(\partial f,\delta x,{\mathcal{P}})+\frac{1}{2}{\mathcal{J}}(\partial^{2}f,dR,{\mathcal{P}})\right).

Assume further that
\partial_{ll}^{2}f
,
l=1,\dots,d
are Lipschitz in
(t,x)
, and that one of the following holds:

(1)
\rho<3/2
;

(2)
\frac{1}{2\rho}+\frac{1}{\rho^{\prime}}>1
.

Then both (
4.13
) and (
4.14
) converge as
|{\mathcal{P}}|\to 0
, and we have the conversion formula:

(4.16)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\partial f(t,x_{t})dx_{t}=\int_{0}^{T}\partial f(t,x_{t})\delta x_{t}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{l=1}^{d}\int_{0}^{T}{\partial^{2}_{ll}f}(t,x_{t})dR_{l}(t,t),

where
\int_{0}^{T}{\partial^{2}_{ll}f}(t,x_{t})dR_{l}(t,t)
should be interpreted as a Young integral.

Proof.

We start by defining the following index sets:

\displaystyle{\mathcal{A}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{(i,q,j)\in\bar{\mathbb{N}}^{3d}:1\leq|i|\leq\ell,\,2q\leq i,\,j\leq i-2q\},

\displaystyle{\mathcal{A}}_{L}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{(i,q,j)\in{\mathcal{A}}:i-2q-j=L\},\qquad\qquad\text{for}\qquad L\in\bar{\mathbb{N}}^{d}:|L|\leq\ell,

\displaystyle{\mathcal{A}}_{L\tau}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{(i,q,j)\in{\mathcal{A}}_{L}:q+j=\tau\},\qquad\qquad\text{for}\qquad\tau\in\bar{\mathbb{N}}^{d}:|\tau|\leq\ell-|L|,

\displaystyle{\mathcal{I}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{(L,\tau)\in\bar{\mathbb{N}}^{2d}:|L|\leq\ell,|\tau|\leq\ell-|L|\}.

In the above the inequality
2q\leq i
means that
2q_{l}\leq i_{l}
for all
l=1,\dots,d
. The other vector inequalities should be interpreted in the similar way. As in (
3.26
) it is easy to see that we have

\displaystyle{\mathcal{A}}_{L}=\{(i,q,j)\in\bar{\mathbb{N}}^{3d}:1\leq|i|\leq\ell,\,i-2q-j=L\},

and therefore

\displaystyle{\mathcal{A}}_{L\tau}=\{(i,q,j)\in\bar{\mathbb{N}}^{3d}:1\leq|i|\leq\ell,\,i-2q-j=L,q+j=\tau\}.

It follows that if
(L,\tau)
is such that
|L+2\tau|\leq\ell
, then we have

(4.17)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{A}}_{L\tau}=\Big\{(i,q,j):(i_{l},q_{l},j_{l})\in\big\{(L_{l}+\tau_{l},0,\tau_{l}),\dots,(L_{l}+2\tau_{l},\tau_{l},0)\big\},l=1,\dots,d\Big\}.

As in Theorem
3.5
by some careful algebraic computations we can show the following decomposition:

\displaystyle{\mathcal{J}}(f,dx,{\mathcal{P}})=\sum_{(L,\tau)\in{\mathcal{I}}}\sum_{(i,q,j)\in{\mathcal{A}}_{L\tau}}\sum_{0\leq j^{\prime}\leq j}{\mathcal{M}}(L,\tau,i,q,j,j^{\prime}),

where

\displaystyle{\mathcal{M}}(L,\tau,i,q,j,j^{\prime})=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\left[\prod_{l=1}^{d}\frac{1}{2^{q_{l}}q_{l}!j_{l}!L_{l}!}\right]\cdot\delta^{L}\Big(\partial^{L+2\tau}f(t_{k},x_{t_{k}})\cdot{\beta}_{k}^{\otimes|L|}\Big)

(4.18)

\displaystyle\cdot\left[\prod_{l=1}^{d}\sigma_{l,k}^{2(j_{l}^{\prime}+q_{l})}\right]\cdot\left[\prod_{l=1}^{d}\frac{1}{2^{j_{l}}}{j_{l}\choose j_{l}^{\prime}}(-1)^{j_{l}^{\prime}}(R_{l}(t_{k+1},t_{k+1})-R_{l}(t_{k},t_{k}))^{j_{l}-j_{l}^{\prime}}\right],

\sigma_{l,k}=\mathbb{E}[(x^{1,l}_{t_{k}t_{k+1}})^{2}]^{1/2}
and
\delta^{L}:=\delta^{L_{1}}_{1}\cdots\delta^{L_{d}}_{d}
.

Now we estimate
{\mathcal{M}}(L,\tau,i,q,j,j^{\prime})
in various cases as in Theorem
3.5
. Precisely, we consider the following six cases:

(i)
|j-j^{\prime}|\geq 2
;

(ii)
|j-j^{\prime}|=1
,
|L+\tau|>1
;

(iii)
j=j^{\prime}
,
|L+\tau|>\rho
.

(iv)
|j-j^{\prime}|=1
,
|L+\tau|=1
;

(v)
j=j^{\prime}
,
|L+\tau|\leq\rho
,
|\tau|>0
;

(vi)
j=j^{\prime}
,
|L|\leq\rho
,
\tau=0
.

Similar to the proof of Theorem
3.5
, given
\frac{1}{\rho}+\frac{1}{\rho^{\prime}}>1
it can be shown that

(4.19)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{M}}(L,\tau,i,q,j,j^{\prime})\to 0

as
|{\mathcal{P}}|\to 0
in cases (i)-(iii). For sake of conciseness, we omit the proof and leave it to the patient reader.

In case (v) we have
j-j^{\prime}=0
and thus
j^{\prime}+q=j+q=\tau
. Then from (
4.18
) we have

\displaystyle\sum_{(i,q,j)\in{\mathcal{A}}_{L\tau}}{\mathcal{M}}(L,\tau,i,q,j,j)=\sum_{(i,q,j)\in{\mathcal{A}}_{L\tau}}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\delta^{L}\Big(\partial^{L+2\tau}f(t_{k},x_{t_{k}})\cdot{\beta}_{k}^{\otimes|L|}\Big)

(4.20)

\displaystyle\cdot\left[\prod_{l=1}^{d}\frac{1}{L_{l}!}\sigma_{l,k}^{2\tau_{l}}\right]\cdot\left[\prod_{l=1}^{d}\frac{(-1)^{j_{l}}}{2^{j_{l}}2^{q_{l}}q_{l}!j_{l}!}\right].

As in the proof of Theorem
3.5
, since
|L+\tau|\leq\rho
we can show that
|L+2\tau|\leq[2\rho]=\ell
, and therefore (
4.17
) holds.
So we can write

\displaystyle\sum_{(i,q,j)\in{\mathcal{A}}_{L\tau}}{\mathcal{M}}(L,\tau,i,q,j,j)=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\delta^{L}\Big(\partial^{L+2\tau}f(t_{k},x_{t_{k}})\cdot{\beta}_{k}^{\otimes|L|}\Big)\cdot\left[\prod_{l=1}^{d}\frac{1}{L_{l}!}\sigma_{l,k}^{2\tau_{l}}\right]

\displaystyle\cdot\prod_{l=1}^{d}\left[\sum_{q_{l}+j_{l}=\tau_{l}}\frac{(-1)^{j_{l}}}{2^{j_{l}}2^{q_{l}}q_{l}!j_{l}!}\right].

Since
|\tau|>0
the above shows that in case (v) we have

(4.21)

\displaystyle\sum_{(i,q,j)\in{\mathcal{A}}_{L\tau}}{\mathcal{M}}(L,\tau,i,q,j,j)=0.

In case (iv) we must have
|j|=1
,
q=j^{\prime}=L=0
and
j=\tau=i
. This implies that

(4.22)

\displaystyle\text{case (iv)}=\frac{1}{2}{\mathcal{J}}(\partial^{2}f,dR,{\mathcal{P}}).

Finally, it is easy to verify that

(4.23)

\displaystyle\text{case (vi)}={\mathcal{J}}(\partial f,\delta x,{\mathcal{P}}).

In summary of the convergence (
4.19
) in case (i)-(iii), relation (
4.21
), (
4.22
) and (
4.23
) we conclude that (
4.15
) holds.

As in Theorem
3.5
we can show that if
\partial_{ll}^{2}f
,
l=1,\dots,d
are Lipschitz in
(t,x)
and if
\rho<3/2
or
\frac{1}{2\rho}+\frac{1}{\rho^{\prime}}>1
, then
{\mathcal{J}}(\partial^{2}f,dR,{\mathcal{P}})
converges to the Young integral
\sum_{l=1}^{d}\int_{0}^{T}{\partial^{2}_{ll}f}(t,x_{t})dR_{l}(t,t)
as
|{\mathcal{P}}|\to 0
.
This implies that (
4.16
) holds. The proof is now complete.
∎
