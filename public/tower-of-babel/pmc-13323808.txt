Comparisons of the Burden and Quality of Care of Nonrheumatic Valvular Heart Disease in Asia and Globally
Authors: Chaofeng Niu, Lan Wei, Juwei Dong, Peiyu Zhang, Chenxi Xu, Yi Pan, Bo Jia, Gregory Y H Lip, Meng Li, Lijing Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNonrheumatic valvular heart disease is an increasing public health concern. This study assessed the burden and quality of care for nonrheumatic valvular heart disease in Asia and at the global level from 1990 to 2021, and projected trends to 2050 using data from the GBS (Global Burden of Disease Study) 2021.MethodsIncidence, age‐standardized incidence rate, deaths, age‐standardized mortality rate, disability‐adjusted life years, and age‐standardized disability‐adjusted life years rate were analyzed. A time‐series model (autoregressive integrated moving average was used for forecasting future trends. A quality‐of‐care index was constructed through principal component analysis, and future quality‐of‐care index trends were projected using a Bayesian age‐period‐cohort model.ResultsFrom 1990 to 2021, both incident nonrheumatic valvular heart disease cases and age‐standardized incidence rate increased globally and in Asia, whereas age‐standardized mortality rate and age‐standardized disability‐adjusted life years rate declined. Projections suggest that incident cases will continue to rise through 2050, with age‐standardized incidence rate decreasing globally but remaining relatively stable in Asia. Although Asia has lower age‐standardized rates than the global average, its projected age‐standardized incidence rate increase is greater. Disease burden varied by age and sex, with incidence peaking at 70 to 74 years. Mortality exceeded that of men among women after age 70 to 74. Nonrheumatic aortic valve disease contributed to the greatest burden. Quality‐of‐care index improved markedly from 1990 to 2021 globally and in Asia, but recent gains have slowed, suggesting a plateau in care improvement.ConclusionsNonrheumatic valvular heart disease burden is rising, especially in aging Asian populations. Despite declining mortality and improved care quality, increasing incidence and slowing quality‐of‐care index gains may intensify future health system pressures.

Valvular heart disease is recognized clinically as a prevalent cardiac pathology, characterized by dysfunctional heart valves and disrupted unidirectional blood flow during cardiac cycles. 1 Rheumatic heart disease (RHD) was the leading cause of valvular heart disease globally. However, over the past 4 decades, improvements in socioeconomic conditions, widespread antibiotic use, and strengthened primary health care systems have substantially reduced RHD incidence. 2 With an aging population, the prevalence of nonrheumatic valvular heart disease (NRVD) has increased and is expected to continue rising, particularly among older populations in developed nations. 3

NRVD's significance stems from its heterogeneous and predominantly degenerative etiologies, contrasting with RHD. According to the GBD (Global Burden of Disease) 2017 study, approximately 12.6 million cases of calcific aortic valve disease and 18.1 million cases of degenerative mitral valve disease were globally documented, making them the dominant contributors to the contemporary NRVD burden. This era also saw approximately 102 700 calcific aortic valve disease‐related and 35 700 degenerative mitral valve disease‐related deaths worldwide. 4 Given these trends and their significant public health implications, a comprehensive quantitative assessment of NRVD's global influence is imperative.

NRVD develops from an intricate interplay of factors, including aging, genetic predispositions, and lifestyle influences. 5 , 6 These factors frequently coexist with cardiometabolic conditions such as hypertension, diabetes, smoking, and dyslipidemia, which vary considerably across populations and health care systems. 7 Understanding these primary risk factors associated with NRVD is pivotal. It enables governmental bodies to implement strategies at the population level, fostering an environment that actively reduces exposure to known NRVD risk factors. Importantly, the distribution of these risk factors and access to diagnostic and interventional cardiac services differ markedly between countries and regions. 8

Asia represents a particularly heterogeneous region with substantial variation in socioeconomic development, health care infrastructure, demographic aging trajectories, and epidemiological transition stages. While some East Asian countries are experiencing rapid population aging and increasing prevalence of degenerative valve disease, many South and Central Asian countries still face limited access to echocardiographic screening, specialist cardiac care, and valve intervention services, which may lead to delayed diagnosis and poorer outcomes. 9 Furthermore, comparing the findings among countries is difficult, attributable to differences across studies, such as study definitions, measures to define NRVD, study populations, and methodological limitations, underscoring the need for standardized, large‐scale comparative analyses focused on both disease burden and quality of care across Asian subregions.

The GBD 2021 study represents a comprehensive, long‐standing global initiative aimed at quantifying the health‐related burdens associated with 369 specific diseases. 10 The aim of this study was to compare the burden and quality of care of NRVD in Asia versus the global average (which includes Asia) using GBD data from 1990 to 2021, thereby facilitating the development of more tailored public health policies for NRVD in Asia. Second, we projected future trends in NRVD‐related burden to inform health system preparedness and long‐term health care resource planning. Such projections may support the development of timely public health strategies to mitigate the growing impact of NRVD.

METHODS

This study used publicly available data from the GBD 2021 study, which can be accessed through the Global Health Data Exchange query tool. All data sources are fully described and publicly available. The statistical code used for data processing and analysis is available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

Data Sources and Selection

The GBD 2021 study provides comprehensive information on the global and regional burden of 369 diseases, injuries, and territories from 1990 to 2021. 11 Nonfatal indicators were estimated using the DisMod‐MR 2.1 Bayesian metaregression model, which integrates multiple data sources and enforces internal consistency among epidemiological parameters. Mortality was estimated using the Cause of Death Ensemble model (CODEm), and systematic redistribution of garbage codes in death registration data was performed to correct misclassification of causes of death and improve attribution accuracy. All estimates are reported with 95% uncertainty intervals (UIs) to reflect the impact of data quality and model uncertainty, thereby ensuring comparability across regions and over time. 12

Specifically, data on NRVD disease were extracted, including incidence, mortality, disability‐adjusted life years (DALYs), as well as their corresponding age‐standardized rates (ASR): age‐standardized incidence rate (ASIR), age‐standardized mortality rate (ASMR), and age‐standardized DALY rate (ASDR). The data were accessed and downloaded via the Global Health Data Exchange (GHDx) platform (http://ghdx.healthdata.org/gbd‐results‐tool). The proportional distribution of the standard world population was derived from statistical estimates provided by the World Health Organization. 10 In the GBD framework, missing or sparse data in specific locations and years are addressed through Bayesian metaregression and spatiotemporal smoothing methods, which borrow strength from neighboring countries, adjacent time periods, and relevant covariates to generate stable estimates. 13

This study constitutes a secondary analysis of publicly available, fully anonymized, and aggregated data. According to Article 32, Items 1 and 2 of the Administrative Measures for Ethical Review of Life Science and Medical Research Involving Humans (issued on February 18, 2023), research that (1) uses legally obtained public data and (2) involves anonymized information is exempt from ethical review. Therefore, neither institutional review board approval nor informed consent was required for this study.

NRVD and its Subtype Definitions

NRVD refers to structural or functional abnormalities of the heart valves that are not caused by rheumatic heart disease. In the GBD 2021 study, NRVD and its subtypes were defined based on the International Classification of Diseases (ICD) codes, including nonrheumatic aortic valve diseases (NRAVD), nonrheumatic mitral valve diseases (NRMVD), and other nonrheumatic valvular diseases. For further details, please refer to previously published studies. 11 , 14

Asian Groupings and Definitions

The Asian groupings and sub‐regional divisions in this study strictly adhere to the United Nations geoscheme. No sub‐regions were excluded or combined.

Based on the GBD data, we analyzed the disease burden across the following 5 Asian subregions: Central Asia, East Asia, Southeast Asia, South Asia, and Western Asia. A total of 48 countries and territories were included, with the specific distribution as follows: Central Asia: includes Kazakhstan, Turkmenistan, Uzbekistan, Kyrgyzstan, and Tajikistan; East Asia: includes Japan, the Republic of Korea, China, Taiwan, the Democratic People's Republic of Korea, and Mongolia; Southeast Asia: includes Brunei, Singapore, Malaysia, Indonesia, the Philippines, Thailand, Viet Nam, Laos, Cambodia, Myanmar, and Timor‐Leste; South Asia: includes Sri Lanka, Iran (Islamic Republic of), Bhutan, Bangladesh, India, Maldives, Afghanistan, Nepal, and Pakistan; Western Asia: includes Cyprus, Kuwait, Qatar, the United Arab Emirates, Bahrain, Georgia, Israel, Jordan, Lebanon, Oman, Saudi Arabia, Armenia, Azerbaijan, Iraq, Syria, Palestine, and Yemen. 15

Assessment and Prediction of the Quality‐of‐Care Index

The quality‐of‐care index (QCI) was constructed based on a set of secondary indicators selected to reflect multiple dimensions of health care performance relevant to the clinical course and management of NRVD. The relevance of the selected secondary indicators to NRVD care performance was supported by both theoretical considerations and empirical consistency observed in prior GBD‐based health care quality assessments. 16 , 17 Specifically, 4 indicators were included: (1) mortality‐to‐incidence ratio, reflecting survival after diagnosis; (2) DALY‐to‐prevalence ratio, indicating average health loss among affected individuals; (3) prevalence‐to‐incidence ratio, serving as a proxy for disease duration and chronic disease management capacity; and (4) years of life lost‐to‐years lived with obesity ratio, capturing the balance between fatal and nonfatal disease outcomes. These indicators have been widely used in previous GBD–based assessments of health care quality for cardiovascular and chronic non‐communicable diseases and collectively capture diagnostic capacity, treatment effectiveness, and long‐term disease management. 18

To integrate these indicators into a composite index, Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was used. The first 2 principal components (PC1 and PC2) were retained based on their explained variance. 18 A weighted composite score was derived using the formula: PCA_score=VarPC1VarPC1+VarPC2·PC1+VarPC2VarPC1+VarPC2·PC2 QCI=PCA_score−minPCA_scoremaxPCA_score−minPCA_score×100% This score was then min–max normalized to a 0–100 scale to obtain the final QCI value, with higher values indicating better quality of care. To facilitate interpretation, QCI can be conceptually understood as a summary measure reflecting how effectively health care systems convert disease burden into survival and functional outcomes; for example, regions with similar disease prevalence but lower mortality and disability would obtain higher QCI scores.

For forecasting future trends in QCI, the Bayesian Age‐Period‐Cohort model was applied. 19 This model accounts for age, period, and cohort effects to generate robust temporal projections, providing a probabilistic assessment of future QCI trajectories. 20

Statistical Analysis

In the GBD 2021, the nonfatal burden of NRVD, including prevalence and incidence, was estimated by the GBD group using the DisMod‐MR 2.1 modeling tool. Burden attributable to each severity of valve disease was estimated by multiplying the prevalence of each severity by the corresponding disability weight. DisMod‐MR is a Bayesian geospatial disease modeling software that uses data on various disease parameters, the epidemiological relationships between these parameters, and geospatial relationships to produce estimates of prevalence and incidence. 12 To estimate cause‐specific mortality for NRVD across different ages, sexes, locations, and years, the cause of death ensemble model was used, drawing upon the GBD cause of death database. 14

To assess the long‐term trends in the global and Asian burden of NRVD from 1990 to 2021, we calculated the average annual percent change (AAPC) using Joinpoint regression analysis. The AAPC represents a weighted average of the annual percent change (APC) values over the entire study period, with its point estimate and 95% CI derived via Monte Carlo methods. A statistically significant difference between the AAPC and zero (P<0.05) was considered indicative of a significant increasing or decreasing trend. 21 , 22

This study used time‐series modeling to forecast the disease burden of NRVD. Using GBD 2021 data, risk‐removed estimates were derived through risk scalar adjustment and logit transformation. An autoregressive integrated moving average model was constructed with the Socio‐demographic Index as an external covariate for prediction, and the optimal model order was determined based on the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC). To account for potential lag effects of Socio‐demographic Index, lag periods of 1 to 5 years were tested, and the optimal lag was selected using out‐of‐sample predictive accuracy. A random walk model [autoregressive integrated moving average (0,1,0)] was applied to fit and predict the residuals. 23 Finally, risk predictions were combined with population projections to estimate the absolute number of NRVD incident cases and deaths, as well as age‐standardized rates. 24 To assess the robustness of the projections, sensitivity analyses were conducted by repeating the forecasting procedures using the bounds of the 95% UIs from the GBD estimates, in addition to the point estimates. The same autoregressive integrated moving average model structure and parameters were applied across all scenarios. Projections based on UI boundary values were compared with those based on point estimates to evaluate the stability of long‐term trends with respect to uncertainty in input data. 25

All statistical analyses and data visualizations were conducted using R (version 4.4.2). Descriptive statistics for key variables are presented as means accompanied by 95% UIs. A significance threshold of P<0.05 was applied for all trend analyses.

RESULTS

Burden of NRVD in Asia Versus the Global Average (Including Asia)

From 1990 to 2021, both globally and in Asia, the absolute numbers of NRVD incident cases and deaths increased substantially, whereas ASMR and ASDR showed declining trends. However, Asia consistently exhibited lower age‐standardized rates than global estimates, along with narrower sex disparities and distinct age distributions.

NRVD Incidence

In 2021, the incidence of NRVD increased significantly worldwide compared with 1990. The global ASIR increased from 23.90 to 25.00 per 100 000. Men consistently had higher ASIR than women, and the male‐to‐female ratio changed little over time. Asia showed the same overall pattern, with ASIR increasing from 12.85 to 13.89 per 100 000 and rising slightly faster than the global average (Table 1 and Figure 1A). Among 204 countries and regions globally, Georgia demonstrated the highest ASIR of NRVD. Geographically, Georgia is located in Asia. Similarly, in Asia, men are more susceptible to NRVD than women, though the disparity is less pronounced than the global average (Figure 2A, Tables S1 and S2).

NRVD Deaths

Global results indicated a significant increase in NRVD‐related deaths from 1990 to 2021, whereas ASMR declined from 2.66 to 2.31 per 100 000, representing a reduction of 13.13%. Sex differences in ASMR were modest, with women slightly higher in 1990 and men slightly higher by 2021. Asia likewise showed declining ASMR, from 1.40 to 1.24 per 100 000, while remaining below the global average throughout the study period (Table and Figure 1B). Although the estimated annual percentage change in mortality was slightly more favorable globally than in Asia, the absolute mortality rate in Asia remained significantly lower than the global average. Among 204 countries and regions worldwide, Cyprus had the highest ASMR and is geographically part of Europe (Figure 2B, Tables S1 and S2).

In both global and Asian populations in 2021, the number of deaths was considerably higher in the age 90‐ to 94‐year group than in other age groups, while the crude mortality rate was highest in the age 95+ group. The global crude mortality rate was lower in Asia than globally for both sexes. Up to and including the age 70‐ to 74‐year group, the number of deaths was slightly higher among men than women. Beyond this age, the number of female patient deaths substantially exceeded that of males within the same age groups. This pattern was consistent both globally and in Asia (Figure 3B, Figure S1B, Tables S3 and S4).

NRVD DALYs

Globally, DALYs related to NRVD decreased significantly. The ASDR declined from 49.31 to 39.72 per 100 000, representing a reduction of 19.44%. In Asia, the ASDR decreased from 26.14 to 21.77 per 100 000; the decline was slower in Asia than globally (Table 1 and Figure 1C). Greenland had the highest ASDR globally, whereas Georgia had the highest ASDR in Asia (Figure 2C, Tables S1 and S2).

Age‐ and sex‐specific analyses revealed that globally, the highest number of DALYs occurred in men aged 80 to 84 years and women aged 85 to 89 years. In Asia, however, the highest DALY counts were observed in men aged 70 to 74 years and women aged 90 to 94 years. The crude DALY rate was highest in the 95+ age group in both global and Asian populations (Figure 3C, Figure S1C, Tables S3 and S4).

Joinpoint Regression Analysis of NRVD in Asia Versus the Global Average (Including Asia)

From 1990 to 2021, NRVD incidence showed a slight overall increase, whereas mortality and DALYs declined significantly in both Asia and globally. All indicators displayed nonlinear, phase‐specific trends, with a recent downward shift after 2018, and greater temporal variability observed in Asia.

NRVD Incidence

Globally, the ASIR of NRVD showed a significant overall increase from 1990 to 2021, with an AAPC of 0.149 (95% CI: 0.095–0.203). The trend was nonlinear, characterized by periods of increase and decrease: a decline during 1990–1995 (APC = −0.209), followed by significant increases in 1995 to 2003 (APC = 0.408) and 2003–2010 (APC = 0.978), then a slight decline in 2010 to 2018 (APC = −0.133), and a sharp decrease in 2018 to 2021 (APC = −1.132).

In Asia, the overall AAPC from 1990 to 2021 was also 0.149 (95% CI: 0.095–0.203). The trend exhibited multiple phases: a significant increase in 1990 to 1995 (APC = 0.212), slower growth in 1995 to 2000 (APC = 0.082), accelerated increase in 2000 to 2005 (APC = 0.387) and 2005 to 2009 (APC = 1.644), modest growth in 2009 to 2018 (APC = 0.066), and a notable decline in 2018 to 2021 (APC = −0.856) (Figure 4A and Table S5).

NRVD Deaths

The global ASMR of NRVD significantly decreased from 1990 to 2021, with an AAPC of −0.466 (95% CI: −0.561 to −0.370). The trend underwent several transitions: an initial increase in 1990 to 1993 (APC = 1.181), followed by a sharp decline in 1993 to 1999 (APC = −1.363), a moderate decrease in 1999 to 2004 (APC = −0.643), an increase in 2004 to 2015 (APC = 0.400), and a substantial decline from 2015 to 2021 (APC = −1.830).

In Asia, the overall AAPC for the same period was −0.466 (95% CI: −0.561 to −0.370). The trend featured a pronounced decrease in 1990 to 1997 (APC = −3.697), followed by an increase in 1997 to 2014 (APC = 0.835), and a nonsignificant decrease in 2014 to 2021 (APC = −0.675, 95% CI: −1.565 to 0.223) (Figure 4B and Table S5).

NRVD DALYs

Globally, the ASDR for NRVD exhibited a significant decreasing trend from 1990 to 2021, with an AAPC of −0.723 (95% CI: −0.793 to −0.653). The joinpoint analysis identified fluctuations: an increase from 1990 to 1993 (APC = 0.424), a sharp decline from 1993 to 2000 (APC = −1.601), a decrease from 2000 to 2004 (APC = −1.025), a stable period from 2004 to 2015 (APC = 0.017), and a significant decline from 2015 to 2021 (APC = −1.428). In Asia, the overall AAPC was also −0.723 (95% CI: −0.793 to −0.653). The trend included an initial nonsignificant increase from 1990 to 1992 (APC = 0.512), a dramatic decline from 1992 to 1995 (APC = −6.043), a decrease from 1995 to 1997 (APC = −1.683), a slight increase from 1997 to 2018 (APC = 0.101), and a decline from 2018 to 2021 (APC = −1.065) (Figure 4C and Table S5).

Burden of NRVD Subtypes in Asia and Versus the Global Average (Including Asia)

Globally, NRAVD was the most common NRVD subtype in both 1990 and 2021, showing substantial growth in incident cases and ASIR over time. By contrast, NRMVD also increased in absolute case number but remained comparatively stable in ASIR (Table, Figure 5A and Figure S2).

Asia showed the same general pattern: NRAVD remained the predominant subtype and exhibited a marked increase in incident cases and ASIR, whereas NRMVD increased more modestly in ASIR (Table, Figure 5A and Figure S2).

NRAVD also accounted for the greatest mortality burden in both regions and showed increasing ASMR, with a steeper rise in Asia than globally. By contrast, NRMVD had lower ASMR and declining mortality trends in both regions (Table, Figure 5B and Figure S2).

Regarding DALYs, both global and Asian regions showed an increasing trend in the burden of NRAVD and NRMVD, although the rate of increase was slower in Asia for NRAVD. Conversely, the ASDR decreased for both subtypes in all regions. For other nonrheumatic valvular diseases (ONRVD), the number of incident cases, deaths, and DALYs increased globally, along with rises in ASIR and ASMR. However, the ASDR for this category declined in Asia (Table, Figure 5C and Figure S2).

Projection of NRVD Burden in Asia Versus the Global Average (Including Asia)

Projections based on the bound UI scenarios showed temporal trends highly consistent with those based on point estimates. Although absolute levels varied across scenarios, the overall direction and inflection patterns remained stable, indicating that the autoregressive integrated moving average‐based forecasts were robust to uncertainty in the GBD input data. The results of sensitivity analyses and associated projection parameters are presented in Figure S3 and Table S6.

NRVD Incidence

Overall, projections indicate that the global and Asian burdens of NRVD incidence will continue to rise in terms of absolute case numbers through 2050, despite divergent trends in ASIR. While global ASIR is projected to decline steadily, Asia is expected to maintain a relatively stable ASIR, resulting in a narrowing gap in age‐standardized incidence between the 2 regions. In both regions, males consistently exhibit higher incidence rates than females, and the sex disparity is projected to widen over time, particularly in Asia (Figure 6A).

NRVD Deaths

Temporal trends predict a dissociation between absolute death cases and ASMR through 2050. While the absolute number of NRVD‐related deaths is projected to increase globally and in Asia, ASMR is expected to stabilize or decline (Figure 6B).

Globally, ASMR is projected to decline slightly over time, although men are expected to continue showing higher mortality rates than women. Regional analysis of Asia reveals distinct patterns. The ASMR is projected to remain largely stable. In contrast to the global pattern of male predominance, females in Asia consistently exhibited higher ASMR than males, and this sex disparity is expected to persist. Consequently, despite maintaining lower ASMR than the global average, Asia is projected to experience an increasing absolute number of NRVD‐related deaths.

NRVD DALYs

Projections through 2050 indicate that while the global ASDR for NRVD will continue to decline, Asia is expected to enter a plateau phase. This divergence results in a persistent epidemiological gap; although Asia maintains lower age‐standardized rates, the absolute disease burden is projected to rise significantly because of the dominant influence of population aging (Figure 6C).

Globally, the ASDR is projected to decline over time, indicating a sustained reduction in the age‐standardized disability burden. This downward trend is evident across both sexes. A male predominance in disease burden persists globally, although the magnitude of this sex disparity is expected to diminish gradually over the study period. Regionally, Asia displays a distinct pattern characterized by stagnation rather than decline. Diverging from the global pattern of male predominance, Asia is projected to exhibit a negligible sex gap by 2050, with ASDR for males and females being nearly identical. Consequently, despite lower risk levels compared with the global average, the combination of stable ASDR and demographic shifts implies a continued escalation in the absolute DALYs attributable to NRVD in Asia.

QCI and Projection for NRVD in Asia Versus the Global Average (Including Asia)

The QCI for NRVD improved markedly from 1990 to 2021 at both the global and Asian levels. Despite a lower initial baseline, Asia experienced more rapid gains and slightly surpassed the global average by 2021. Across the study period, a pronounced age gradient was evident, with QCI values highest among older adults, while younger populations consistently exhibited lower and more heterogeneous care quality. Projections to 2049 indicate continued but modest improvements, accompanied by gradual convergence between Asia and the global average, suggesting narrowing regional disparities but also a potential plateau as health care systems approach performance saturation.

Temporal Trends in QCI

Between 1990 and 2021, the global QCI for NRVD increased substantially from 18.30 to 84.67. A similar upward trajectory was observed in Asia, where the QCI rose from 14.35 in 1993 to 85.28 in 2021, indicating significant improvements in health care quality over the past 3 decades. Notably, the most pronounced increases occurred after the year 2000 in both regions, reflecting advancements in medical management and possibly earlier detection and treatment of the disease. Age‐Specific QCI Patterns

Age‐specific QCI values in both 1990 and 2021 exhibited a clear positive correlation with age, with the highest scores consistently observed in the oldest age groups (85+ years), where the QCI reached 100 in both years and regions. DISCUSSION

In this study, our principal findings are as follows: (1) Between 1990 and 2021, the ASIR of NRVD increased globally and in Asia, while ASMR and ASDR declined. Asia showed faster ASIR growth yet lower absolute mortality than the global average. Projections indicate rising case numbers to 2050, with stable or declining age‐standardized rates, particularly a stable ASIR in Asia and a slight global decline. (2) Significant sex‐based disparities were observed: men had higher incidence, while women experienced higher mortality and disability rates, especially after age 70–74 years. (3) Globally and in Asia, NRAVD was the most common subtype of NRVD in both 1990 and 2021. (4) The QCI improved substantially over the past 3 decades, but its growth rate has recently plateaued, indicating limitations in current diagnosis and treatment models. (5) Significant disparities persist across regions and age groups, highlighting the need for equitable and targeted health strategies.

NRVD constitutes a significant and growing global public health challenge, particularly against the backdrop of population aging and shifting disease patterns. Over the past decades, the management of NRVD has undergone substantial transformation, marked by the introduction of innovative interventions such as transcatheter aortic valve replacement and transcatheter mitral valve repair, which have demonstrated considerable potential in improving outcomes for severe aortic stenosis and degenerative mitral valve disease, respectively. 26 , 27 Nevertheless, significant segments of the global population continue to grapple with the residual burden of untreated valvular heart disease, indicating significant gaps in the early identification and long‐term management of this condition.

It is important to recognize that regional variations in NRVD burden and outcomes are largely driven by marked disparities in health care infrastructure, diagnostic capabilities, and access to affordable care—especially advanced interventions like surgical valve replacement and transcatheter therapies. However, these structural factors cannot be directly modeled within the GBD framework and are therefore reflected only indirectly through outcome‐based indicators such as incidence, mortality, and DALYs. Accordingly, the regional differences observed in this study likely reflect the combined influence of epidemiological risk profiles and health care system performance.

The continued rise in ASIR alongside declining ASMR and ASDR suggests that NRVD is being detected more frequently while outcomes after diagnosis have improved. Asia remained below global age‐standardized averages, but this apparent advantage should be interpreted cautiously because Asian countries differ substantially in population aging, diagnostic intensity, and access to specialist valve care. In lower‐resource settings, limited echocardiographic screening and incomplete registration may still contribute to underdiagnosis and underestimation of burden. 28 , 29

The substantial heterogeneity within Asia supports this interpretation. Countries such as Georgia, which showed particularly high ASIR, may reflect the combined influence of population aging, cardiometabolic risk exposure, and uneven transition from acute‐care‐oriented systems to chronic disease management. These findings argue for country‐specific responses rather than a single regional narrative. 30 , 31

Accordingly, the most relevant policy implication is not only the expansion of high‐cost intervention, but also stronger early detection, referral pathways, and long‐term management of modifiable cardiovascular risk factors. In high‐burden settings, these measures are likely to influence future NRVD outcomes as much as epidemiologic change itself. 32 , 33 , 34 Second, socio‐economic conditions also play a role in this phenomenon. In many Asian settings, improvements in primary care, insurance coverage, and access to essential diagnostic services may help explain why age‐standardized mortality remains lower than the global average, whereas persistent gaps in affordability and specialist capacity may still constrain further gains. However, in many low‐ and middle‐income countries, limited insurance coverage and high out‐of‐pocket payments mean that the increasing burden of NRVD is likely to impose substantial economic pressure on both households and health systems. Expanding financial protection for valve intervention, strengthening referral networks, and integrating NRVD into existing chronic disease programs may therefore be important for reducing both clinical and economic vulnerability.

Our study highlights the importance of considering differences between crude and age‐adjusted trends across regions. Although the absolute number of deaths attributable to NRVD has increased globally, this rise is largely attributable to significant population growth and aging. Advances in the treatment of NRVD and improved management of risk factors such as hypertension have contributed to better control of NRVD mortality. This decline reflects improved NRVD outcomes resulting from enhanced treatment and management strategies. The same factors also explain the trends in DALYs. These findings underscore the importance of accounting for changes in age distribution across regions when interpreting the results. The ASIR and annual rate of change provide a more accurate depiction of NRVD's impact, revealing that age‐adjusted rates are improving even as more people are diagnosed with and die from NRVD. This suggests that, overall, advancements in health care and risk factor management are positively influencing the control of the disease.

The results indicate significant sex and age disparities in the burden of NRVD. Specifically, the age 70‐ to 74‐year group shows the highest incidence of NRVD, with men being more affected than women. However, beyond the age of 74 years, women's mortality rates significantly exceed those of men. This pattern suggests that while middle‐aged and older men are more susceptible to NRVD, older women experience higher disease fatality. The underlying mechanisms remain unclear and may involve a combination of biological, socioeconomic, and health care‐related factors. In younger populations (<70 years), the cause of NRVD is more likely linked to early‐stage calcific aortic stenosis, which shares risk factors with atherosclerosis, such as hypertension, hyperlipidemia, smoking, and diabetes. 35 Higher smoking prevalence among men—a strong driver of chronic inflammation, oxidative stress, and vascular as well as valvular calcification—likely contributes to their higher NRVD incidence. In addition, men are disproportionately represented in heavy industries and construction sectors, where long‐term exposure to air pollution, heavy metals, and occupational toxins, together with sustained physical stress, may further accelerate fibrocalcific remodeling of cardiac valves. 36 , 37 Beyond occupational factors, ambient air pollution (particularly fine particulate matter) and lifestyle‐related risks such as physical inactivity, unhealthy dietary patterns, and obesity may also promote systemic inflammatory and metabolic pathways involved in valvular degeneration. These environmental and lifestyle exposures are unevenly distributed across populations and regions and may partially contribute to observed sex‐ and region‐specific disparities in NRVD burden, although such factors cannot be explicitly quantified within the current GBD framework. 38 , 39

Women, on the other hand, typically have smaller hearts and aortic valve annuli, meaning that the same degree of valvular stenosis places a higher pressure load on the female heart, increasing the risk of heart failure. 40 , 41 Estrogen provides significant cardiovascular protection through its anti‐inflammatory, antioxidant, anticalcification, and endothelial function benefits, as well as favorable lipid metabolism regulation. 42 , 43 , 44 , 45 The sharp decline in estrogen levels after menopause abruptly diminishes this protective effect, accelerating valvular degeneration, calcification, and vascular stiffness. The loss of estrogen creates a microenvironment more conducive to valvular fibrosis and calcification, explaining why women exhibit more aggressive and fatal disease progression after age 70 years. Additionally, women often face delayed diagnosis, older age at treatment, and more comorbidities, leading to higher baseline surgical risks and relatively increased perioperative complication and mortality rates. 46 , 47

The significant improvement in the QCI over the past 3 decades is a direct result of medical technological innovations, guideline dissemination, and professional workforce development. This progress means that more patients have access to evidence‐based diagnosis and treatment, improving survival rates and quality of life. However, the predicted QCI curve shows a sharp slowdown in growth after reaching a high level, revealing the limitations of current clinical models: once QCI approaches 90, further improvement becomes exceptionally difficult. This implies that the potential of existing diagnostic and treatment paradigms has nearly been exhausted. To achieve a QCI approaching 100, the clinical focus must shift from “treating established disease” to “preventing onset” and “early management.” Although the overall QCI is high, age‐stratified data and sex‐based disparities in NRVD burden suggest that quality improvements are not universally distributed. Significant barriers likely remain for older adults, women, and rural populations in accessing continuous high‐quality care. These “averaged‐out” vulnerable groups are key to further advancing QCI.

Projections indicate that the burden of NRVD will become increasingly severe globally and in Asia over the next 3 decades. Notably, the UIs widen substantially over time, particularly in Asia, reflecting compounded uncertainty from demographic projections, health care access, and future diagnostic intensity. This disease burden, coupled with its strong association with population aging, poses unprecedented challenges to global health equity, health system resilience, and cardiovascular disease prevention strategies. Although significant historical progress has been made in the quality of care for NRVD globally and in Asia, the pace of future improvement is slowing and plateauing. This trend, together with the continued rise in NRVD incidence, signals a critical public health turning point: linear improvements in health care quality will be insufficient to counteract the exponential growth in disease burden. Without breaking through the current bottlenecks, substantial health gains over the next 30 years may be difficult to achieve.

As the region with the fastest aging population globally, Asia faces the dual pressures of “growing old before becoming rich” and “aging before being prepared.” Many low‐ and middle‐income Asian countries still focus their health systems on combating infectious diseases and maternal and child health issues. Their primary health care systems, specialized workforce, and advanced medical infrastructure (such as cardiac surgery and catheterization facilities) are not yet ready to handle the tsunami of complex chronic diseases such as valvular heart disease. The widening gap between health care resource preparedness and the growing disease burden is a core reason why Asia's incidence rate and its uncertainty interval in projections are significantly higher than the global average. This suggests that, without intervention, substantial health inequities will emerge in the future, where Asian patients may be more easily diagnosed yet have less access to timely, high‐quality treatment. This highlights the enduring importance of primary prevention. Although NRVD is associated with aging, it shares key risk factors with coronary heart disease. Controlling hypertension, diabetes, smoking, alcohol consumption, and other risk factors remain essential. Strengthening the promotion of healthy lifestyles nationwide is a cornerstone strategy for reducing the future burden of valvular disease. This demonstrates the synergistic effect of integrating NRVD prevention into existing noncommunicable disease control programs.

Beyond descriptive burden assessment, our findings emphasize the urgent need for research on early‐stage valvular degeneration, sex‐specific disease mechanisms, and implementation strategies for scalable screening programs. Future studies integrating echocardiographic surveillance data, biomarker discovery, and health system capacity modeling could improve risk stratification and guide cost‐effective prevention strategies, particularly in low‐ and middle‐income Asian countries.

Several policy recommendations can be proposed from multiple angles to improve the quality of life and prognosis of patients with NRVD. First, increase public awareness: Use media and public health campaigns to enhance public understanding of NRVD, including its symptoms, risk factors, and the importance of early diagnosis. Second, strengthen early screening and diagnosis: Regular cardiac examinations are recommended for specific populations, particularly those with a family history of heart disease. Early detection and treatment can significantly improve outcomes. For older adults, echocardiography could be incorporated into routine physical examinations. Third, support multidisciplinary teamwork, for example, collaboration among experts in cardiology, cardiac surgery, cardiac nursing, and genetics to provide more comprehensive patient care. Also, establish specialized multidisciplinary diagnosis and treatment centers for heart valve diseases to offer patients one‐stop treatment solutions. Fourth, promote health insurance coverage: Ensure that the costs of treatment and management of NRVD are included in health insurance plans to alleviate the financial burden on patients. If the expenses for valve repair or replacement surgery are covered by health insurance, the risk of NRVD‐related mortality could be significantly reduced. In addition, health departments in various countries should use such disease prediction data for forward‐looking health workforce planning and financial budgeting, strengthening global and regional cooperation, and accelerating the capacity‐building process in low‐ and middle‐income countries.

Limitations

Although this study provides important predictions and insights into the burden and quality of care for NRVD globally and in Asia, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, because of relatively poor medical conditions in some regions, the diagnosis of NRVD may be inaccurate, and a significant number of patients may remain unrecorded, affecting data completeness. Second, although the GBD 2021 study applies advanced statistical methods to improve comparability across regions, these approaches cannot fully compensate for systematic underreporting, limited diagnostic capacity, and incomplete vital registration systems in some countries. As a result, NRVD burden may be underestimated in parts, and uncertainty intervals are generally wider in data‐sparse settings, which should be considered when interpreting regional differences and long‐term projections. Third, although newer GBD releases exist, the most recent fully public data set with consistent stratification for reproducible independent analysis at the time of this study was GBD 2021. Furthermore, differences in health care infrastructure, diagnostic capacity, and access to affordable valve interventions across countries could not be directly quantified or adjusted for in our analyses. Although QCI partially reflects health care system performance through outcome‐based indicators, it does not capture structural aspects of care delivery, such as availability of echocardiography, specialist workforce density, or access to surgical and transcatheter valve therapies. As a result, some of the regional disparities observed in NRVD burden and outcomes may be attributable to unmeasured differences in health care capacity rather than true epidemiological variation.

CONCLUSIONS

During the period from 1990 to 2021, the ASIR of NRVD has shown an upward trend globally and in Asia, whereas ASMR and the ASDR have been decreasing progressively. Nevertheless, substantial regional, sex, and age disparities remain. The overall disease burden of NRVD in Asia is lower than the global average, but in comparison with other subtypes of NRVD, NRAVD exhibits the highest ASIR and ASMR both globally and in Asia.

Although the QCI showed significant improvement over the past 3 decades, its rate of growth is slowing and approaching a plateau. This trend contrasts sharply with the continuing rise in incidence, revealing a critical contradiction: linearly increasing medical quality will be insufficient to counteract the exponentially growing disease burden. NRVD burden is projected to continue rising globally and in Asia until 2050, with the ASIR in Asia increasing at a considerably faster rate than the global level. This indicates that Asian countries may need substantially greater health care resources to address the challenges expected to arise in the future from NRVD.
