Lipid nanoparticle-encapsulated DNA vaccine prevented lung consolidation following heterologous influenza A virus challenge in pigs
Authors: The N. Nguyen, Danh C. Lai, Hung Q. Luong, Kassandra Durazo-Martínez, Sushmita Kumari, Sarah Sillman, Phillip Gauger, Giao P. Trinh, Hiep L. X. Vu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The substantial antigenic diversity of Influenza A virus (IAV) presents significant challenges to the development of broadly protective vaccines for swine. Moreover, pigs vaccinated with whole-inactivated virus or hemagglutinin (HA) subunit vaccines may experience more severe lung consolidation than non-vaccinated pigs when exposed to antigenically mismatched IAV strains, a phenomenon known as vaccine-associated enhanced respiratory disease (VAERD). We recently developed a lipid nanoparticle-encapsulated DNA (LNP-DNA) vaccine encoding the HA of IAV, which elicited robust immune responses following a single immunization and protected pigs against homologous IAV challenges. In this study, we compared the immunogenicity and protective efficacy between the HA protein-based vaccine and the HA DNA-based vaccine against an antigenically mismatched IAV strain in pigs. Neither vaccine induced cross-reactive hemagglutination inhibition (HI) antibodies nor prevented viral shedding in nasal secretions following heterologous challenge. However, while the HA protein-based vaccine exacerbated lung lesions compared to non-vaccinated controls, the HA DNA-based vaccine prevented the development of gross lung pathology. Transcriptomic analyses revealed distinct gene expression profiles between the two vaccine groups. These findings suggest that the LNP-DNA vaccine platform may offer a safer and more effective strategy for developing vaccines against IAV in swine.

Introduction

Influenza A virus (IAV) is a major respiratory viral pathogen affecting swine production worldwide. Three subtypes of IAVs are co-circulating in pigs in North America: H1N1, H1N2, and H3N21. The H1 subtype is further classified into lineages 1A, 1B, and 1C, while H3 is divided into 8 lineages2,3. Currently, polyvalent whole inactivated virus (WIV) vaccines are commercially available in the United States4. These vaccines provide effective protection against antigenically matched strains but show limited efficacy against mismatched strains5–7. In addition to their limited heterologous protection, WIV vaccines may exacerbate lung pathology if the vaccinated pigs are subsequently exposed to an antigenically mismatched IAV strain8,9. This phenomenon is known as vaccine-associated enhanced respiratory disease (VAERD) and has been commonly reported in pigs vaccinated with an oil-in-water adjuvanted WIV containing an H1 strain from lineage 1A, followed by challenge with an H1 strain from lineage 1B or vice versa [reviewed in ref. 10]. The type of adjuvant incorporated into WIV vaccines significantly influences the risk of VAERD11. Similarly, VAERD has also been observed in pigs vaccinated with a subunit HA-protein-based vaccine formulated with an oil-in-water adjuvant12.

The mechanism underlying VAERD remains unclear. However, the presence of cross-reactive, non-neutralizing antibodies appears to play a significant role in the development of VAERD in pigs vaccinated with a WIV vaccine. This is evidenced by the observation that piglets with maternally derived antibodies (MDA) exhibited more severe respiratory disease than MDA-negative piglets when infected with a mismatched IAV strain13. Several studies suggest that WIV vaccines elicit antibodies that bind to both the HA1 and HA2 domains of homologous IAV strains, and that antibodies targeting a conserved epitope near the HA2 fusion peptide of antigenically mismatched strains promote viral membrane fusion, thereby enhancing viral infection and contributing to increased disease severity14,15. However, in several studies, pigs displaying VAERD did not have increased viral titers in nasal swabs or bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF)8,9,11,16, suggesting that VAERD is not solely the result of enhanced viral replication. Furthermore, in a recent study, pigs were sequentially immunized with different chimeric HA antigens containing HA1 domains from various HA subtypes and the same HA2 domain from a swine IAV H1 strain, to direct the antibody response toward the conserved HA2 domain. These pigs did not develop VAERD after challenge with heterologous swine IAV strains17. Therefore, the contribution of anti-HA2 antibodies to VAERD remains inconclusive.

Notably, intranasal administration of a live-attenuated IAV vaccine or an adenovirus-vectored vaccine expressing the HA antigen does not induce VAERD when pigs are challenged with heterologous strains15,18,19. Similarly, intramuscular vaccination with an alphavirus-derived replicon particle vaccine expressing the HA gene does not lead to VAERD20. However, the use of live-attenuated vaccines raises significant safety concerns due to the potential for genetic reassortment between the vaccine virus and circulating wild-type IAVs, which could lead to the emergence of novel strains with unpredictable virulence21. Immunization with viral vector-based vaccines induces immune responses against the vector, which may potentially interfere with the induction of immune responses by subsequent immunizations22.

We recently developed lipid nanoparticle (LNP) formulations that significantly enhance the efficacy of DNA-based vaccines. A single-dose immunization with an LNP-DNA vaccine encoding the HA antigen from either H1N1 or H3N2 strains elicited robust immune responses and protected vaccinated pigs against challenge with the corresponding homologous IAV strains23,24. The primary objective of this study was to compare the immunogenicity and protective efficacy of LNP-DNA and HA-protein-based vaccines against a heterologous IAV challenge in pigs. Pigs immunized with the HA-protein subunit vaccine derived from the H1N1-pdm09 (lineage 1A) strain exhibited exacerbated lung pathology, including severe consolidation, after challenge with an H1N2-δ1a (lineage 1B) strain. In contrast, pigs immunized with the LNP-DNA vaccine encoding the same H1N1-pdm09 HA antigen were protected from lung consolidation. Collectively, these findings support the LNP-DNA platform as a safe and effective strategy for developing vaccines against IAV in pigs.

Results

LNP‑encapsulated DNA vaccines derived from different IAV subtypes show similar physicochemical properties and transfection efficiency

Two LNP-DNA vaccines containing the HA gene of H1N1-pdm09 (pdm09-LNP) and H1N2-δ1a virus (δ1a-LNP) were produced. These freshly prepared LNP-DNA vaccines were uniform in particle size, with an average diameter of approximately 87 nm, a polydispersity index of around 0.15, and encapsulation efficiencies ranging between 74% and 78% (Table 1). LNP-DNA vaccines induce high titers of serum neutralizing antibodies against the respective homologous viruses

A vaccination and challenge study was conducted using the young pig model to evaluate the immunogenicity and protective effectiveness of the vaccines. Three groups of pigs were immunized via intramuscular injection with either the δ1a-LNP, pdm09-LNP, or the pdm09-protein vaccine (Table 2). A fourth group did not receive any treatment and served as the non-vaccinated (NV) control. The δ1a-LNP and pdm09-LNP vaccines were administered once, whereas the pdm09-protein vaccine was given twice with a 21-day interval between doses. On day 43, the pigs were challenged via intranasal and intratracheal inoculation with the H1N2-δ1a virus. Nasal swabs were collected daily post-challenge to monitor viral shedding. When tested with the H1N2-δ1a whole-virus ELISA, samples from the δ1a-LNP group showed significantly higher OD values (p < 0.01) than those from these remaining three groups (Fig. 2B). No significant difference was observed between the pdm09-LNP and pdm09-protein groups (p = 0.8847). However, the pdm09-protein group showed significantly higher OD values than the NV group (p = 0.0028), while the pdm09-LNP group did not differ significantly from the NV group (p = 0.0972).

Next, we measured hemagglutination inhibition (HI) titers against both the H1N1-pdm09 and H1N2-δ1a viruses. HI titers against the H1N1-pdm09 virus were first detected in the pdm09-LNP group on day 7 post-vaccination and increased rapidly, reaching a geometric mean titer (GMT) of 1:396 by day 21 (Fig. 2C). In contrast, sera from the pdm09-protein group showed no detectable HI titers against H1N1-pdm09 virus (Fig. 2C), despite the presence of virus-specific IgG detected by ELISA (Fig. 2A). The discrepancy between ELISA and HI results may be due to the higher sensitivity of the ELISA, which can detect low levels of binding antibodies that do not necessarily possess HI activity.

When the HI assay was performed using the H1N2-δ1a virus, only the δ1a-LNP group exhibited detectable HI titers (Fig. 2D). The kinetics and magnitude of the HI antibody responses against the respective homologous viruses were comparable between the δ1a-LNP and pdm09-LNP groups. Notably, HI antibodies specific to H1N1-pdm09 and H1N2-δ1a did not exhibit any cross-reactivity. This is expected as HI antibodies against IAV are highly strain-specific9,13.

The HI assay primarily detects antibodies that bind to the receptor-binding site located within the HA1 domain25. Thus, virus neutralization (VN) assay was performed to further evaluate the functionality of antibodies induced by the LNP-DNA and protein-based vaccines. Only two out of six serum samples from the pdm09-protein group on day 42 post-vaccination showed detectable VN titers against the H1N1-pdm09 virus (Fig. 2E). In contrast, all pigs immunized with the pdm09-LNP or δ1a-LNP vaccines developed high VN titers against their respective homologous virus strains, with no significant cross-neutralization observed (Fig. 2E, F).

When tested with the H1N1-pdm09 whole-virus ELISA, IgG antibodies were detected in BALF from both the pdm09-LNP and pdm09-protein groups, with significantly higher OD values (p < 0.0001) observed in the pdm09-LNP group compared to the pdm09-protein group (Fig. 3A). Similarly, pdm09-specific IgA ELISA OD values were significantly higher in the pdm09-LNP group (p = 0.0212) than in the pdm09-protein group (Fig. 3B). Although four of six BALF samples from the pdm09-protein group showed elevated OD values for pdm09-specific IgA, the mean OD value for this group was not statistically different (p = 0.0895) from that of the NV group (Fig. 3B). LNP-DNA vaccines induce cellular immune responses

To assess cellular immune responses, peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) collected after vaccination were stimulated with either the H1N1-pdm09 and H1N2-δ1a viruses, and the frequency of virus-specific IFN-γ-secreting cells (IFN-γ-SC) was quantified using an ELISpot assay. The results of the IFN-γ ELISpot assays when stimulated with the H1N1-pdm09 virus are presented in Fig. 4A–D. Only three of six pigs in the pdm09-protein group had notable numbers of IFN-γ-SC on day 14 post-vaccination. The IFN-γ-SC numbers increased markedly by day 28, one week after the booster immunization, but declined by day 42. However, the mean IFN-γ-SC numbers in the pdm09-protein group were not statistically different (p > 0.05) from those of the NV group at any time point tested, most likely due to marked variation among the animals in this group. In contrast, IFN-γ-SC were detected in all six pigs in the pdm09-LNP group on day 14 and the numbers of IFN-γ-SC continued to increase on days 28 and 42. Notably, the number of IFN-γ-SC in the pdm09-LNP group on day 14 were significantly higher than in the pdm09-protein group (p = 0.036), indicating that LNP-DNA vaccine induced earlier cellular immune responses than the protein-based vaccine. Interestingly, the IFN-γ-SC numbers in the δ1a-LNP group were significantly higher than those of the NV group on all three sampling days post-vaccination (day 14, p = 0.001; day 28, p = 0.015; day 42, p = 0.005). The IFN-γ-SC numbers in the δ1a-LNP group were lower than those in the pdm09-LNP group on days 14 (p = 0.0434) and 28 (p = 0.0128), but the difference was not significant on day 42 (p = 0.0875). Infectious virus titers in nasal swabs, measured by titration on MDCK cells, followed a similar trend to the viral RNA data quantified by RT-PCR (Fig. 5D, E). In the δ1a-LNP group, three of six pigs had no detectable infectious virus throughout the five-day observation period. Pigs immunized with the pdm09-LNP vaccine do not exhibit VAERD after challenge with the heterologous H1N2-δ1a virus

All four groups exhibited a transient rise in rectal temperature on day 1 post-challenge (Fig. 6A). However, the mean temperature in the δ1a-LNP group remained below 40°C, while the mean temperatures in the other three groups rose above 40 °C. During necropsy on day 5 post-challenge, the NV group exhibited typical lung consolidation associated with IAV infection, particularly in the cranial lobes, with a mean of 6.9% total lung surface affected (Fig. 6B, C). In contrast, lung consolidation was not detected in the δ1a-LNP group. In the pdm09-LNP group, lung consolidation varied between 0.2% and 6.9%, with a mean of 2.4%. The mean percentage of lung consolidation in the pdm09-LNP group was statistically lower than both the pdm09-protein (p = 0.0282) and NV groups (p = 0.0061), but not different from that of the δ1a-LNP group (p = 0.0643). Interestingly, severe lung consolidation was observed in four of six pigs in the pdm09-protein group (Fig. 6B, C). Among these four pigs, the percentage of lung surface affected varied from 24.1% to 42.2%, which was more than three times the average lung consolidation observed in the NV group. However, the difference in the mean percentage of lung consolidation between the pdm09-protein and NV groups did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.0654), mainly due to the large standard deviation (Fig. 6C). When the two outliers were excluded, the mean percentage of lung consolidation in the pdm09-protein group was significantly greater than that of the NV group (Welch’s t‑test, p = 0.0078).

Microscopic lesions were scored in three lung sections: apical, cranial, and caudal, and the mean score for each pig was used for analysis. The NV control group showed marked microscopic pathology characterized by inflammatory infiltration of the pulmonary parenchyma, peri‑bronchiolar inflammation, epithelial sloughing, and airway accumulation of mononuclear cells and necrotic debris (Fig. 6D). In contrast, the δ1a‑LNP group exhibited minimal microscopic changes across all lung lobes (Fig. 6D, E). Consequently, the δ1a‑LNP group had significantly lower microscopic lesion scores than both the NV group (p = 0.007) and the pdm09‑protein group (p = 0.0014). Although the pdm09‑LNP group showed less severe lesions overall, its microscopic scores were not statistically different from those of either the pdm09‑protein or NV groups (p > 0.05; Fig. 6D, E). The pdm09‑protein group likewise did not differ from NV (p > 0.05). Notably, the two pigs in the pdm09‑protein group that displayed reduced lung consolidation also showed correspondingly milder microscopic lesions (Fig. 6E, G3, orange asterisks).

In situ hybridization was utilized to detect virus-infected cells in the cranial lung sections. Numerous virus-infected cells were observed in the cranial lung sections of the NV group (Fig. 6F, G). In contrast, virus-infected cells were not detected in pigs in the δ1a-LNP group. Only a few virus-infected cells were sporadically detected in the lung sections of pigs in the pdm09-LNP group. Interestingly, although pigs in the pdm09-protein group exhibited severe lung lesions, the frequency of virus-infected cells in their lung sections was only sporadically detected in the lung section (Fig. 6F, G).

Together, the data indicate that vaccination with δ1a-LNP protected pigs from both lung consolidation and microscopic lesions. Interestingly, vaccination with pdm09-LNP protected against lung consolidation and significantly reduced microscopic lesions compared to the NV group. In contrast, vaccination with pdm09-protein resulted in increased lung consolidation and more severe microscopic lesions in four of six pigs.

In this study, eosinophils were only sporadically observed in lung sections of pigs in the NV control group (Fig. 7, G4), as well as in those immunized with either the δ1a-LNP (Fig. 7, G1) or pdm09-LNP (Fig. 7, G2) vaccines. Gene Ontology (GO) analysis identified 121 enriched pathways in the pdm09-LNP group, with several pathways interrelated and individual DEGs contributing to multiple pathways. Overall, the pdm09-LNP group showed enrichment in GO terms associated with the regulation of cell proliferation, differentiation, and cytokine responses (Fig. 9D). In contrast, the pdm09-protein group showed only seven enriched pathways despite having nearly twice the number of DEGs as the pdm09-LNP group (Fig. 9E). This may partly be because many DEGs in the pdm09-protein group correspond to Ensemble IDs without specific gene annotations (Supplementary Table 3). The enriched pathway in the pdm09-protein group was primarily related to cell death (GO:0008219, GO:0012501) and apoptotic processes (GO:0006915).

Both the pdm09-LNP and pdm09-protein groups had upregulated DEGs encoding for negative regulators of immune responses. They shared five upregulated transcription factors (EGR1, FOSB, KLF4, FOXJ1, and OSR2) and an enzyme (DUSP4) that act as negative regulators critical for maintaining homeostasis (Fig. 9F). EGR1 is rapidly induced in T and B cells upon receptor activation and plays a pivotal role in B cell activation27. In T cells, EGR1 activates the expression of DUSP4, which inhibits MAP kinase signaling by dephosphorylating ERK and JNK. This suppression reduces the expression of activation markers such as CD40L and ICOS, as well as cytokines like IL-4 and IL-21, thereby impairing the ability of CD4+ T-helper cells to support B-cell activation and differentiation28. FOSB, a component of the AP-1 transcription factor complex, together with c-Jun, activates the expression of CD95L, a critical mediator of apoptosis in activated T cells through the process of activation-induced cell death29. KLF4 is strongly induced in M2 macrophages but reduced in M1 macrophages, where it cooperates with STAT6 to promote an M2 genetic program and inhibits M1 targets by sequestering NF-κB coactivators30. Additionally, KLF4 acts as a negative regulator of exhausted CD8+ T cells by suppressing effector molecules such as granzyme B and perforin31.

In the pdm09-LNP group, three additional DEGs, NR4A3, NFKBIZ, and MAFB, encoding transcription factors with roles in modulating immune responses, were upregulated. NR4A3 and NFKBIZ suppress NF-κB signaling, thereby regulating inflammatory responses in T and B cells32. NR4A3 is rapidly induced upon BCR activation, modulating B cell responses by limiting excessive activation in the absence of co-stimulatory signals33. MAFB promotes macrophage differentiation into the M2 phenotype, supporting anti-inflammatory responses and tissue repair34.

Several DEGs unique to the pdm09-protein group are involved in cell death and apoptosis (Fig. 9F). CTSK, a cysteine protease, triggers caspase-dependent cell death by cleaving Bid and anti-apoptotic Bcl-2 homologs35. DNase I promotes apoptosis in normal human CD4+ T-lymphocytes by degrading DNA through its nuclease activity36. DDIT3, induced by DNA damage, ER stress, hypoxia, and starvation, drives the expression of apoptosis-related genes to facilitate apoptosis during ER stress37. Similarly, CREBRF induces apoptosis through the ER stress pathway38.

In contrast, several DEGs unique to the pdm09-LNP group encode transcription factors that promote immune activation (Fig. 9F). IRF1 promotes type I interferon production in innate immune cells which is critical for CD8⁺ T cell responses during viral infections39. BHLHE40 is expressed in T cells and myeloid cells and is rapidly upregulated upon T cell activation, facilitating CD4⁺ Th1 differentiation, particularly during lymphocytic choriomeningitis virus infection40. Similarly, EGR2 is rapidly upregulated in T cells after antigenic stimulation, playing key roles in modulating inflammation and antigen-driven proliferation41. Additionally, four inflammatory cytokine and chemokine genes (CSF1, TNF, OSM, and CXCL8) were upregulated in the pdm09-LNP group. Notably, CSF1 encodes macrophage colony-stimulating factor (M-CSF), which regulates the survival, differentiation, and function of mononuclear phagocytes42. Although type-I interferon transcripts were not detected as DEGs in the pdm09-LNP group, three interferon-stimulated genes (IFIT5, MX2, and IRF1) were identified, suggesting exposure to type-I interferons. Two non-canonical cytoplasmic poly(A) polymerases, TENT5A and TENT5C, were also upregulated in pdm09-LNP group. TENT5C is highly expressed in activated B cells, and its absence results in reduced antibody production and impaired T-cell-independent immune responses. Finally, transcripts of two G protein-coupled receptors, ADGRL2 and GPR31, were identified in the pdm09-LNP group, indicating their involvement in immune modulation through cell adhesion, migration, and chemotaxis.

Collectively, the transcriptome analysis reveals distinct immune responses between the two vaccinated groups. DEGs in the pdm09-LNP group were associated with pathways promoting immune activation, including cytokine production and cell proliferation. In contrast, DEGs in the pdm09-protein group primarily activated pathways linked to apoptosis and immune resolution. These findings suggest that the pdm09-LNP vaccine induces a more prolonged phase of immune activation compared to the protein vaccine.

Discussion

The challenges in developing an efficacious vaccine against swine IAV are twofold. First, the viral genome exhibits high diversity due to the error‑prone nature of the RNA‑dependent RNA polymerase, and spillover of human‑ and avian‑origin IAVs into swine herds further accelerates viral evolution3,43,44. Second, oil‑in‑water–adjuvanted WIV vaccines may exacerbate lung consolidation when immunized animals are exposed to antigen‑mismatched virus strains8,9. These limitations highlight the need for next‑generation vaccines that can be rapidly updated to match newly emerging variants and that, at minimum, do not induce VAERD, while ideally providing protection against heterologous strains. In this regard, LNP‑encapsulated DNA vaccines represent a promising approach. DNA plasmids are noninfectious, nonreplicating, and safe for use in animals. Furthermore, DNA plasmids are stable and can be produced in large quantities at low cost. These features make DNA‑based vaccines particularly suitable for use in farm animals such as pigs.

In our previous studies, we demonstrated that a single dose of the LNP-DNA vaccines encoding the HA gene of the H1N1-pdm09 or H3N2 viruses elicited robust immune responses and protected the animals against challenge infection with the respective homologous viruses23,24. Building on these works, the primary objective of this study was to assess the protective efficacy of the LNP-DNA vaccine against heterologous IAV challenge. We utilized a model in which pigs were immunized with the pdm09‑LNP vaccine and then challenged with the H1N2‑δ1a virus because immunization with either WIV or HA‑subunit vaccines based on H1N1‑pdm09 followed by H1N2‑δ1a challenge consistently results in VAERD8,9,12. A group of pigs immunized with the δ1a‑LNP vaccine was included as a homologous protection control, while another group immunized with the pdm09‑protein vaccine served as a reference to confirm reproducibility of VAERD induction.

Pigs immunized with either the δ1a-LNP or pdm09-LNP vaccines mounted robust humoral and cell-mediated immune responses after a single immunization. Both HI antibodies IFN‑γ-SC specific to the homologous viruses were detected by day 14 and remained stable through day 42 post-vaccination, suggesting durable immunity. Although the duration of antigen expression in pigs was not assessed in this study, previous research in mice and hamsters show that LNP-DNA vaccines can sustain antigen expression for more than 28 days45. It is therefore plausible that the durable immune responses observed in pigs vaccinated with the LNP-DNA vaccines may be attributed to extended antigen expression. Notably, while HI and VN antibodies remained strain‑specific, IFN‑γ-SC showed some degree of cross‑reactivity. Together, these findings demonstrate the effectiveness of the LNP-DNA platform across multiple HA subtypes, underscoring its potential as a platform for developing vaccines against IAVs.

In contrast, pigs immunized with the pdm09-protein vaccine mounted weaker humoral responses, as evidenced by undetectable HI titers and lower levels of virus-specific IgG against the homologous strain as compared with those immunized with the pdm09-LNP vaccine. These results differ from those reported by Rajao et al.12, who observed relatively high HI titers in pigs vaccinated with an HA-subunit vaccine. Notably, the antigen expression system, antigen dose, adjuvant, and immunization regimen were similar between the two studies. The key difference is antigen structure. Rajao et al. used HA antigen (Cat. No. NR-13691, BEI Resources) designed to preserve its native tertiary structure and biological activity12. On the other hand, the HA antigen used in our pdm09-protein vaccine lacked a trimerization domain thus, did not maintain its native structure. Therefore, it is likely that structural differences in the HA antigens used to formulate the vaccines may account for the observed differences in immune responses between the two studies.

The differences in humoral and cell-mediated immune responses between the pdm09-LNP and pdm09-protein groups were further supported by transcriptomic profiling of PBMCs collected at the time of viral challenge. PBMCs from the pdm09-LNP group showed upregulation of genes involved in immune activation pathways, including cytokine production and cell proliferation. In contrast, PBMCs from the pdm09-protein group were primarily associated with pathways linked to apoptosis and immune resolution.

After challenge with the H1N2-δ1a virus, the kinetics and magnitude of viral shedding in nasal secretions, and viral loads in BALF were similar between the pdm09-LNP and pdm09-protein groups. Thus, neither vaccine could prevent viral infection, which is consistent with the observation that neither vaccine induced notable cross-reactive neutralizing antibodies against the H1N2-δ1a virus (Fig. 2D, F). However, pigs immunized with the pdm09-LNP vaccine were protected against lung consolidation and showed significantly reduced microscopic lesions. In contrast, four of the six pigs in the pdm09-protein group developed more severe lung consolidation than the NV pigs. Our findings corroborate those of Rajao et al.12, who reported that pigs vaccinated with an oil-in-water adjuvanted HA protein vaccine developed VAERD. Notably, the two pigs in this group that did not develop VAERD had lower levels of virus-specific serum IgG and almost no virus-specific IFN-γ-SC, suggesting that they mounted weaker immune responses than the four pigs that did develop VAERD. It is possible that these minimal immune responses were insufficient to exacerbate disease outcomes. Additionally, genetic differences among the six pigs in the pdm09-protein group could contribute to the observed variation in disease outcomes. However, due to the small sample size, we were unable to conduct an association study to identify factors linked to the absence of VAERD in these two pigs.

The VAERD in pigs immunized with the pdm09‑protein vaccine was unlikely due to a non‑native conformation of the protein antigen used in this study. Previous studies have shown that pigs vaccinated with WIV formulated in an oil-in-water adjuvant consistently develop VAERD upon mismatched challenge8,9. Although HA structure may be altered during inactivation, other studies have demonstrated that the same WIV antigen, when formulated with different adjuvants, can produce different outcomes upon challenge with a mismatched strain11, arguing against the possibility that antigen alteration is the primary cause of VAERD. Furthermore, pigs immunized with a recombinant HA antigen engineered to preserve its native structure also developed VAERD when challenged with a mismatched influenza virus12. Together, these findings indicate that the non‑native structure of the recombinant protein used for immunization is unlikely to explain the VAERD observed in this study.

Vaccine-associated enhanced disease outcomes have been described for several pathogens. For example, in Dengue virus infection, antibodies can enhance disease severity through antibody-dependent enhancement of infection, a process in which non-neutralizing antibodies bind to virus particles, facilitate their entry into myeloid cells via Fcγ receptors and enhance viral infection and replication within the host46,47. Alternatively, virus-antibody complexes may bind to the Fcγ receptors on myeloid cells and trigger excessive cytokine production, leading to immunopathology without directly enhancing viral replication48. Additionally, the deposition of antibody-virus complexes in tissues may cause damage through complement activation or antibody-dependent cell cytotoxicity49. Beyond antibody-mediated effects, vaccination may also induce pathological CD4 T-cell responses, which drive the infiltration of immune cells into tissues, resulting in severe inflammation and tissue damage26. Vaccine-associated enhanced disease has been documented in human clinical trials involving vaccines for dengue, RSV, and measles50–52. In swine influenza, studies have suggested that cross-reactive, non-neutralizing antibodies targeting the conserved HA2 domain may enhance membrane fusion and thereby increase viral infectivity14,15. As a result, higher viral titers have sometimes been observed in nasal swabs and BALF samples from pigs experiencing VAERD12. In the current study, the four pigs in the pdm09-protein group that developed VAERD did not exhibit significantly higher viral loads in nasal swabs or BALF compared to those in the NV group. This finding is consistent with several other studies that also observed no increase in viral loads among pigs experiencing VAERD8,9,11,16. Thus, in the context of IAV infection in swine, VAERD may not necessarily result from antibody-dependent enhancement of viral replication.

Because WIV- or HA protein-based vaccines primarily exacerbate respiratory disease following exposure to an antigenically mismatched IAV strain, immune responses within the lung microenvironment likely play a key role in disease pathogenesis. In this study, the four pigs in the pdm09-protein group that developed VAERD exhibited significant eosinophil infiltration in the lungs. In contrast, pigs in this same group that did not develop VAERD, as well as those in the NV group, showed no notable eosinophil infiltration even though they had comparable microscopic lesion scores. These findings suggest that eosinophil infiltration could serve as a marker of VAERD in IAV infection of pigs. However, the specific role of eosinophils in the pathogenesis of VAERD remains to be elucidated. Pulmonary eosinophilia is a hallmark of lesions caused by RSV infection in humans and in animal models vaccinated with formalin-inactivated virus53. Nevertheless, a recent study in an eosinophil-deficient mouse model demonstrated that RSV infection still induces severe lung pathology in mice previously vaccinated with formalin-inactivated virus, suggesting that eosinophils may not be essential mediators of disease26.

This study has several limitations that warrant further investigation. Although we observed different immune profiles between pigs immunized with the pdm09‑LNP vaccine and those immunized with the pdm09‑protein vaccine, the causative effects of these differences on the development of VAERD remain to be determined. It is possible that interactions among multiple immune effector functions collectively influence disease outcomes, and delineating these factors will require additional studies. Additionally, while pulmonary eosinophil infiltration was consistently observed in pigs with VAERD, the functional contribution of eosinophils to disease pathogenesis remains unknown.

In conclusion, we demonstrate that pigs immunized with the HA-protein vaccine developed VAERD, while those immunized with the DNA-based vaccine were protected against severe lung consolidation after challenge with an antigenically mismatched IAV strain. Our study provides direct evidence that the same antigen, when delivered through different platforms, can lead to significantly different outcomes. IAVs evolve rapidly, and new strains or variants frequently emerge in the swine population3. The LNP-DNA vaccine described in this study has the potential to serve as a platform for the rapid updating of vaccines to match newly emerging IAV strains in swine. Furthermore, vaccine-associated enhanced diseases have been observed with pathogens such as the dengue virus and RSV, underscoring the importance of understanding how vaccine platforms interact with the immune system. We propose that IAV in pigs represents a robust model for investigating the immune mechanisms that drive protection or disease enhancement. Knowledge gained from such research will provide valuable insights to support the development of safer and more effective vaccines.

Methods

Cells, virus, peptides, and lipids

Human embryonic kidney cells (HEK-293T, CRL-3216) and Madin-Darby Canine Kidney (MDCK, CCL-34) were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection. The IAV strains H1N1 (A/swine/Iowa/A01202099/2011) and H1N2 (A/swine/Minnesota/A01392045/2013) were obtained from the National Veterinary Services Laboratories. The HA genes of these H1N1 and H1N2 strains belong to the lineages 1A (pandemic 2009) and 1B (δ1a), respectively, and are referred to as H1N1-pdm09 and H1N2-δ1a. These viruses were propagated and titrated in MDCK cells and virus titers are presented as tissue culture infectious dose 50 (TCID50) per mL.

Lipids used to form LNP included DLin-MC3-DMA (MC3) (Nanosoft Polymer), 1,2-dioleoyl-3-trimethylammonium-propane (DOTAP) (Cayman Chemical), cholesterol (Sigma Aldrich), distearoylphosphatidylcholine (DSPC) (Avanti Polar Lipids), and 1,2-dimyristoyl-rac-glycero-3-methoxypolyethylene glycol-2000 (DMG-PEG2000) (Avanti Polar Lipids).

DNA plasmid preparation

The HA sequences of the H1N1-pdm09 virus (GenBank accession no. JX092275.1) and H1N2-δ1a virus (GenBank accession no. KF715130.1) were optimized for expression in swine cells (Sus scrofa) using a free online tool provided by GenScript. The 6x histidine tag and Flag tag sequence (DYKDDDDK) were fused to the carboxyl terminus of the HA gene from H1N1-pdm09 virus and that of H1N2-δ1a virus, respectively, to facilitate protein detection. The gene fragment was synthesized using a commercial DNA synthesis service (GenScript) and cloned into the pCI plasmid backbone (Promega). The recombinant plasmid was propagated in E. coli DH5α and purified using the Giga Prep Kit (Zymo Research).

Lipid nanoparticles preparation and characterization

Lipid nanoparticles encapsulated DNA vaccine (LNP-DNA) was prepared as previously described23. To prepare the lipid mixture, MC3, DOTAP, DSPC, cholesterol, and DMG-PEG2000 were each dissolved in absolute ethanol and then combined at a molar ratio of 42:10:8:38:2, resulting in a final lipid mixture with a total lipid concentration of 15 mM. The DNA plasmid was diluted in 25 mM sodium acetate, pH 4.0. The lipid and DNA solutions were combined using the Mixer-4 chip, operated by the NanoGenerator Flex-M Nanoparticle Synthesis System (Precigenome), at a DNA-to-lipid phase flow rate ratio of 3:1 and a total flow rate of 4 mL/min. The nitrogen-to-phosphate ratio (mol/mol) was 5.5. The resulting products were dialyzed against 100 mM Tris-Cl buffer at pH 7.4 with 10% sucrose (w/v) using Slide-A-Lyzer G2 dialysis cassettes with a molecular weight cut-off of 10 kDa (Thermo Fisher Scientific). After dialysis, the LNP-DNA products were filtered through a 0.45 μm PES filter, and the concentration of DNA encapsulated within the LNPs was adjusted to 100 μg/mL. The LNP-DNA vaccines were used for immunization within 24 h after preparation. Each dose contained 500 µg of encapsulated DNA in a 5 mL volume.

The physicochemical properties, including hydrodynamic diameter, polydispersity index (PDI), zeta potential, and encapsulation efficiency (EE%), were measured as described previously24. To assess transfection efficiency, 500 ng of encapsulated LNP-DNA was added directly to 1 well of a 24-well plate containing HEK-293T cells. Forty-eight h post-transfection, the cells were fixed in cold methanol:acetone (1:1 v/v), and HA-expressing cells were detected using an indirect immunofluorescence assay. The anti-Flag monoclonal antibody (GenScript) and anti-HA monoclonal antibody (Cat. No. NR-28668, BEI Resources) were used to detect HA-expressing cells transfected with δ1a-LNP and pdm09-LNP, respectively.

Cryogenic transmission electron microscopy (cryo-EM) was used to characterize the morphology of the LNP-DNA preparations. 1.2/1.3 C-Flat Copper grids were glow-discharged for 30 s using a PELCOeasiGlow system to render the surface hydrophilic. After that, 3 µL of LNP-DNA suspension was applied to the grids and allowed to adsorb for 10 s, followed by blotting for 6 s to remove excess liquid. The grids were then rapidly plunged into liquid ethane using a TFS Vitrobot Mark IV (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at 4 °C and 100% humidity. Frozen grids were clipped and transferred into an energy-filtered ThermoScientific Glacios 200 kV cryo-electron microscope. The sample imaging was carried out at 79,000× magnification and an electron dose of 40 e/Å2 with a defocus of 4 µm to enhance LNP contrast.

HA-protein-based vaccine preparation

The pdm09-HA protein was expressed in SF9 cells using the baculovirus expression system through a commercial protein expression service (GenScript). To enhance the secretion of the protein into the culture medium, the native signal sequence (residues 1–17) of the HA protein was replaced with the baculovirus gp64 signal peptide, while the transmembrane and intracellular domains (residues 530–567) were removed. Additionally, a 6x histidine tag was added to the carboxy-terminus to facilitate purification via immobilized-metal ion chromatography. According to the certificate of analysis provided by the company, the protein was more than 90% purity.

To prepare the vaccine, the protein was diluted in PBS and mixed at a 4:1 (v/v) ratio with Emulsigen®-DL 90 (MVP Adjuvants) such that each dose of the vaccine contained 10 µg of protein in a 2 mL volume. The vaccine was administered within 2 h after preparation.

Animal experiment

The animal experiments were approved by the University of Nebraska-Lincoln (UNL) Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee under protocol number 2228, which was approved on April 12, 2022.

Twenty-four four-week-old pigs were purchased from Midwest Swine Research. The pigs tested negative for antibodies against IAV and porcine reproductive and respiratory syndrome virus using commercial ELISA kits (IDEXX Laboratories). Upon arrival at the animal biosafety level 2 facility at UNL, the pigs were randomly assigned to four groups of six, each housed in a separate room (Table 2). The pigs were fed ad libitum. After a one-week acclimatization, groups G1 and G2 received a single dose of the δ1a-LNP and pdm09-LNP vaccines, respectively, which were administered intramuscularly at four sites: two in the neck muscles behind the ears and two in the hamstring muscles. Group G3 received two doses of the pdm09 protein-based vaccine, administered intramuscularly at a single site in the neck muscle, with a three-week interval between the doses. Group G4 received no treatment and served as the NV control.

Whole blood samples without an anticoagulant were collected weekly after vaccination, and serum was isolated to measure antibody responses. Additionally, whole blood samples with EDTA were collected before vaccination and every two weeks thereafter, and PBMCs were isolated using SepMate tubes with Lymphoprep™ (STEMCELL Technologies), cryopreserved, and subsequently used to evaluate T-cell responses.

On day 43 post-vaccination, pigs were all pigs were challenged with the H1N2-δ1a virus at a dose of 2 × 10⁵ TCID₅₀ per pig. The virus was diluted in 4 mL of DMEM, and the inoculum was administered intratracheally and intranasally, as previously described23. The challenge procedure was administered under anesthesia, with pigs sedated by intramuscular injection of a combination of Telazol (tiletamine–zolazepam; 4.4 mg/kg; Dechra), ketamine (2.2 mg/kg; Zetamine, VetOne), and xylazine (2.2 mg/kg; Rompun, Dechra). Nasal swabs were collected daily from before the challenge to day 5 post-challenge using polyester flock swabs (Puritan) and placed in 1 mL of DMEM containing 100 units/mL penicillin, and 100 µg/mL streptomycin.

The animals were monitored daily by staff members of the UNL Institutional Animal Care Program, who were blinded to the experimental treatment groups. Clinical parameters assessed included rectal temperature, coughing, sneezing, nasal discharge, abnormal or labored breathing, inappetence, and lethargy.

On day 5 post-challenge, pigs were sedated by intramuscular injection with the combination of Telazol (tiletamine–zolazepam; 4.4 mg/kg; Dechra), ketamine (2.2 mg/kg; Zetamine, VetOne), and xylazine (2.2 mg/kg; Rompun, Dechra), and humanely euthanized by intravenous injection into the jugular vein with an overdose of pentobarbital sodium (85.8 mg/kg, Fatal‑Plus®, Vortech Pharmaceutical). During necropsy, a board-certified veterinary pathologist, who is a coauthor but was intentionally blinded to the experimental design, visually scored gross lung lesions. The percentage of the lung surface exhibiting consolidation typical of IAV infection was estimated for each lung lobe, and the total percentage of lung consolidation was calculated based on the weighted proportions of each lobe relative to the total lung volume, as previously described15,54. After that, BALF samples were collected using 50 mL of cold PBS. Lung samples were also collected from the left apical, cranial, and caudal lobes and immediately fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin.

Histological analysis

After 24 h of fixation, the tissue samples were submitted to the Nebraska Veterinary Diagnostic Center, where they were embedded in paraffin, sectioned, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). Each section was scored for six parameters: peribronchiolar lymphocytic cuffing, interstitial pneumonia, bronchial and bronchiolar epithelial cell changes, bronchiolitis, edema, and epithelial exocytosis, using a previously described scoring scale15. Composite scores for each section ranged from 0 (normal) to 22 (most severe lesion).

Lung sections from the cranial lobe were also subjected to in situ hybridization (ISH) using a customized probe set specific to the IAV nucleoprotein mRNA transcript (Avance Cell Diagnostic). The detection of virus-infected cells was performed with RNAscope 2.5HD detection reagent-Brown kit (Advanced Cell Diagnostics). The frequency of virus-infected cells in the airway epithelium and pulmonary parenchyma was manually counted, and scores were assigned the following scale, as previously described55: 0, none; 1, 1–10 cells; 2, 11–30 cells; 3, 31–100 cells; 4, >100 cells.

Quantification of infectious virus titer and viral RNA copy numberss

Infectious virus titers in nasal swabs and BALF samples were titrated in MDCK cells as previously described56. Virus titers are reported as log10 TCID50 per mL. Viral genomic RNA copy numbers were quantified by real-time reverse transcriptase PCR (RT-PCR). RNA was extracted from 100 μL of nasal swab or BALF samples using the Quick-RNA Viral Kit (Zymo Research) and eluted in 50 μL of elution buffer. Detection of viral RNA was performed using the VetMax-Gold SIV Detection Kit (Life Technologies). Viral RNA copy numbers were calculated by extrapolating from a standard curve generated with 10-fold serial dilutions of a synthetic RNA fragment with known copy numbers57. Data are presented as log10 viral RNA copies per 100 μL of samples.

Hemagglutination inhibition assay

The HI assay was performed as previously described58. Serum samples were pretreated with 20% kaolin clay at a 1:4 ratio for 20 min at room temperature, followed by incubation with an equal volume of 0.5% turkey red blood cells to remove nonspecific inhibitors. The treated sera were then diluted 2-fold serially in PBS in 96-well V-bottom plates, starting at an initial dilution of 1:10. After that, 25 µL of test virus containing 4 HA units of either the H1N1-pdm09 or the H1N2-δ1a viruses was added to each well, and the plates were incubated at room temperature for 30 min. Subsequently, 50 µL of 0.5% turkey red blood cells was added to each well, and the plates were incubated for 1 h at room temperature. HI titers were recorded as the reciprocal of the highest sample dilution, where red blood cell agglutination was inhibited. Samples without detectable HI activity at a 1:10 dilution were assigned a titer of 1:5 for graphical representation and statistical analysis.

Virus neutralization assay

Heat-inactivated antisera were serially diluted 2-fold in 50 µL of virus infection medium [Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM, Gibco) supplemented with 0.2% bovine serum albumin fraction V (Sigma-Aldrich), 25 mM HEPES (Hyclone), 100 U/mL penicillin and 100 μg/ml streptomycin (Gibco) and 1 μg/mL TPCK-treated trypsin (Sigma-Aldrich) in 96-well round plates. Each diluted serum sample was then incubated with 50 µL of virus suspension containing 100 TCID₅₀ of either H1N1-pdm09 or H1N2-δ1a virus for 1 h at 37 °C. Following incubation, the serum-virus mixtures were transferred to 96-well plates containing confluent monolayers of MDCK cells and incubated for 48 h at 37 °C. After 48 h of inoculation, cells were fixed with a cold acetone/methanol solution (1:1) and stained with a mouse anti-HA antibody, followed by a goat anti-mouse IgG (H + L) antibody conjugated with Alexa Fluor® 488. Infected wells were visualized using a Nikon Eclipse Ts2R fluorescence microscope. Neutralization titers were defined as the reciprocal of the highest sample dilution that completely inhibited viral infectivity in MDCK cells. Samples with undetectable virus-neutralizing activity at the initial dilution of 1:10 were assigned a titer of 1:5 for graphical representation and statistical analysis.

Whole-virus ELISA

Whole-virus ELISAs were conducted with modifications to a previously described protocol59. H1N1-pdm09 and H1N2-δ1a viruses cultured in MDCK cells were purified by ultra-centrifugation at 32,000 rpm (equivalent to 175,000 × g) using a SW 32 Ti rotor (Beckman Coulter) through a 20% sucrose cushion in the NTC buffer (100 mM NaCl, 20 mM Tris/Cl pH 7.4, 5 mM CaCl2). The purified virus was diluted in 100 mM carbonate/bicarbonate buffer (pH 9.6) to coat Nunc Maxisorp™ ELISA plates (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The coating concentration was 128 HA units per well for H1N1-pdm09 virus and 512 HA units per well for H1N2-δ1a virus.

BALF and serum samples were diluted 1:20 and 1:100, respectively, in a sample dilution buffer consisting of PBS supplemented with 25% (v/v) ChonBlock blocking/sample buffer (Chondrex Inc.) and 5% (w/v) non-fat milk. Diluted samples were added to the ELISA plates in duplicate and incubated for 1 h at room temperature. Horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated goat anti-pig IgG (H + L) antibodies (Bethyl Laboratories) and goat anti-pig IgA (H + L) antibodies (Bio-Rad), both diluted 1:2000 in the sample dilution buffer, were used to detect IgG and IgA, respectively. Between each step, the plates were washed 5 times with tris-buffered saline containing 0.1% (v/v) Tween-20 (TBS-T20). The signal was developed by adding TMB (3,3’,5,5’-tetramethylbenzidine) substrate (Alpha Diagnostic Intl. Inc.) for 5 min at room temperature and subsequently stopped using a stop solution (Invitrogen). Optical density (OD) at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader (BioTek).

IFN-γ ELISpot assay

IFN-γ ELISpot assay was reported previously57. Briefly, 96-well polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) plates (MilliporeSigma) were coated overnight with 0.5 µg per well of anti-pig IFN-γ antibody (BD Biosciences Pharmingen). The following day, 100 µL of cell suspension containing 5 × 10⁵ PBMCs, suspended in complete RPMI-1640 medium (RPMI-1640 supplemented with 10% FBS, 100 units/mL penicillin, and 100 µg/mL streptomycin), was added to each well in duplicate and the cells were stimulated overnight with H1N1-pdm09 virus or H1N2-δ1a virus at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 1 at 37 °C with 5% CO₂. After incubation, the plates were washed 3 times with PBS containing 0.1% Tween-20 (PBS-T20) and incubated with biotinylated mouse anti-pig IFN-γ antibody (BD Biosciences Pharmingen). Following 6 washes with PBS-T20, the plates were incubated for 1 h at room temperature with streptavidin-conjugated alkaline phosphatase (Southern Biotech). After 6 additional washes with PBS-T20, the plates were developed using Vector® Blue alkaline phosphatase substrate (Vector Laboratories). Spots were counted and analyzed using the CTL Immunospot S5 Analyzer system (ImmunoSpot).

Quantification of porcine cytokines

Cytokine concentrations in serum and BALF samples were measured using the ProcartaPlex™ Porcine Cytokine & Chemokine Panel 1 (Invitrogen), following the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, samples were diluted 1:1 with the assay buffer and transferred to a 96-well plate preloaded with capture beads. Plates were sealed and incubated overnight at 4 °C with continuous agitation. The following day, plates were washed 3 times, and 25 µL of detection antibody mix was added to each well. After 1 h incubation at room temperature, 25 µL of streptavidin-phycoerythrin was added and the plate was incubated at room temperature for 30 min. After 3 final washes, beads were resuspended in 150 µL of Sheath Fluid PLUS and analyzed using a Bio-Plex™ 200 system (Bio-Rad Laboratories) with Bio-Plex Manager 5.0 software. Median fluorescence intensity values were recorded, normalized, and cytokine concentrations were interpolated from a standard curve and expressed in picograms per milliliter (pg/mL).

RNA sequencing

RNA extraction, library preparation, and sequencing were performed by Azenta Life Sciences (South Plainfield). Total RNA was extracted using the Qiagen RNeasy Plus Mini Kit (Qiagen). The extracted RNA was quantified using the Qubit 2.0 Fluorometer (Life Technologies), and RNA integrity was assessed using an Agilent TapeStation 4200 (Agilent Technologies). RNA sequencing libraries were prepared using the NEBNext Ultra II RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (NEB). Briefly, mRNAs were enriched with Oligo(dT) beads and fragmented at 94 °C for 15 min. First- and second-strand cDNA were synthesized to convert RNA into complementary DNA (cDNA). The cDNA fragments were end-repaired, adenylated at the 3’ ends, and ligated to universal adapters. Indexes were added, and libraries were enriched via PCR with limited cycles. The sequencing library was validated using an Agilent TapeStation (Agilent Technologies) and quantified with both the Qubit 2.0 Fluorometer (Invitrogen) and quantitative PCR (KAPA Biosystems).

The libraries were multiplexed and clustered onto a flow cell on an Illumina NovaSeq instrument according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Sequencing was performed in a 2 × 150 bp paired-end (PE) configuration. Image analysis and base calling were conducted using NovaSeq Control Software (NCS). Raw sequence data (.bcl files) were converted into fastq files and de-multiplexed using Illumina bcl2fastq 2.20 software, allowing for 1 mismatch in index sequence identification.

RNA sequencing data analysis

RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) data were analyzed using the nf-core/rnaseq pipeline (version 3.14.0) implemented in Nextflow (version 23.04.1). RNA reads were first trimmed and filtered with Trim Galore (version 0.6.7) and read quality before and after trimming was assessed using FastQC (version 0.11.7). The trimmed and filtered reads were then aligned to the Sus scrofa genome (Sscrofa11.1; GenBank assembly number GCF_000003025.6) using STAR (version 2.7.9a) as the alignment tool with default settings60. The data matrix containing gene counts and transcript lengths obtained from the nf-core/rnaseq pipeline was used as input for the nf-core/differential abundance pipeline (version 1.5.0). Differential gene expression analysis was performed using DESeq2 (version 1.34.0) following the pipeline’s default parameters60. Genes with a Benjamini-Hochberg adjusted p-value smaller than 0.05 and an absolute log2 fold change larger than 1 were considered differentially expressed. The resulting differential expression table was annotated using gene information retrieved from Ensembl for Sus scrofa, with annotations extracted from the gene transfer format (GTF) file. All pipelines in this study were executed on Swan, a High-Performance Computing Cluster at the University of Nebraska-Lincoln’s Holland Computing Center.

Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis was conducted for significantly differentially expressed genes (DEGs), focusing on the biological process category, using the PANTHER Classification System (version 19.0) via the Gene Ontology Consortium (GOC) website61. Significant pathways were ranked based on the False Discovery Rate (FDR) calculated using Fisher’s exact test.

Statistical analysis

Before statistical analysis, HI and VN antibody titers were log₂-transformed, while viral RNA copy numbers and virus titers were log₁₀-transformed. Continuous single-variable data, such as the percentage of lung consolidation, the area under the curve (AUC) of viral loads in nasal swabs, viral load in BALF, IgG and IgA ELISA, IFN-γ ELISpot, and cytokine concentrations, were analyzed using the Brown-Forsythe and Welch ANOVA test, followed by unpaired t-tests with Welch’s correction. For continuous two-variable data, such as viral loads in nasal swabs collected daily after challenge, two-way ANOVA was used, followed by Tukey’s multiple comparison test. Ranked data, including H&E and ISH lung scores, were analyzed using the Kruskal-Wallis test, followed by Dunn’s multiple comparisons test. All graphical and statistical analyses, except those for RNA sequencing data, were performed using GraphPad Prism (version 10.2.3).
