Trichotillomania preceding central centrifugal cicatricial alopecia in a pediatric patient
Authors: Ananya Eeraveni, Adena E. Rosenblatt
Published: 2026

Introduction

Central centrifugal cicatricial alopecia (CCCA) is a form of chronic scarring alopecia. It is characterized by permanent hair loss beginning at the vertex of the scalp with progressive peripheral expansion.1 While the exact prevalence of CCCA is unknown, it has been shown to disproportionately impact middle-aged to older women of African descent.1

CCCA has been rarely shown to impact pediatric patients. To date, approximately 8 pediatric cases of CCCA have been reported in the literature.2, 3, 4 Every patient identified as Black or of African descent. Five patients were female and 3 were male, indicating further research is needed to understand whether CCCA preferentially impacts certain genders in a pediatric setting. Six patients had a known family history of CCCA. We present a case of a pediatric patient who initially had suspected trichotillomania (TTM), a repetitive hair-pulling disorder, and subsequently developed CCCA.

Case report

An 11-year-old African American boy presented to the pediatric dermatology clinic with a 1-y history of an enlarging alopecic patch on the frontal scalp (Fig 1, A). The patient’s mother and paternal grandmother had a history of unspecified hair loss. Trichoscopic examination showed decreased hair density, intact follicular ostia, and broken hairs of different lengths, diagnostic hallmarks of non-scarring alopecia and TTM. The patient also endorsed manipulating the area through frequently picking, rubbing, and pulling the hairs. Dermatophyte culture was negative, further favoring a diagnosis of trichotillomania. Behavioral modification and 3 month-follow up was recommended but he was lost to follow up.

The patient returned to clinic approximately 5 years later at 15-years-old with progressively worsening, diffuse, patchy alopecia, with evidence of new follicular dropout. The patient indicated no itching but noted mild burning, tingling sensations within the scalp. He also reported having worn hair extensions intermittently until 2 months prior to the appointment. Further history elicited that until 13 years of age, the patient had natural hairstyles and continued to manipulate the hairs in the affected area; this behavior ceased when he began wearing braids, dreadlocks and hair extensions. Application of topical clobetasol propionate 0.05% ointment once daily was prescribed due to suspected active inflammation. Follow-up at 5 months showed little to no improvement (Fig 1, B).

Two punch biopsies of the scalp were performed. Pathological examination showed hair follicles focally replaced by fibrous stelae, residual follicles exhibiting perifollicular fibrosis, eccentric thinning of the follicular wall, polytrichia, and premature desquamation of the inner root sheath. Perivascular lymphocytic infiltrates were present, but frank interface folliculitis was not prominent. Follicular density appeared to be decreased within the sample, and dermal fibrosis was noted. The biopsies were consistent with the diagnosis of central centrifugal cicatricial alopecia (CCCA).

The patient was instructed to start oral doxycycline 100 mg twice a day and continue topical clobetasol to affected areas. At 3-month follow-up, there was minimal apparent growth in the interparietal scalp area, with hair loss progression in the frontal and vertex scalp regions.

Discussion

The etiology of CCCA is complex and not fully understood. It has been associated with intricate immunopathology including a CD4+ predominant T-cell infiltrate unique from other lymphocytic scarring alopecias,5 proteomic profiling revealing upregulation of IgG-associated and profibrotic genes, including IGHG3, ITGA1, SFRP2, TPM2 and SLMAP, in affected follicles,6 and increased expression of genes implicated in fibroproliferative disorders in the scalps of CCCA patients versus unaffected counterparts.7 An autosomal dominant inheritance pattern, particularly with maternal family history of CCCA, has been implicated.3

Another potential risk factor of CCCA development is trauma to the hair follicle, as seen in our patient. While CCCA is a distinct pathologic entity from lichen planopilaris (LPP), prior studies in other lymphocytic cicatricial alopecias suggest that mechanical injury may trigger follicular inflammation. For example, Alkhalifah et al described injury through excessive traction was hypothesized to elicit koebnerization of hair follicles and subsequent LPP development.8 Although a majority of pediatric patients with CCCA reported wearing natural hairstyles prior to disease onset,2 high risk hair-care practices, such as usage of chemical relaxers, tight or damaging hairstyles and dyes, have also been correlated with CCCA in adults.9 Our patient began wearing hair extensions and dreadlocks after the initial diagnosis of TTM, which may have caused damage to the scalp. However, self-inflicted trauma may also impact the hair follicle, as seen with psychological disorders such as TTM, which our patient originally presented with. While TTM has not been traditionally recognized as a potential contributing factor, it may represent an important underexplored aspect in the development of scarring alopecia, particularly in patients with genetic predisposition. One recent meta-analysis found that any hair-pulling behaviors had a prevalence of 8.84% and TTM had a prevalence of 1.14% in the general public, indicating a substantial affected population.10 Mechanical trauma to the hair follicle and disrupted follicular anatomy resulting from TTM may have contributed to the CCCA manifestation. Alternatively, Sperling et al characterized 9 Black adult patients with biopsy-confirmed early-onset CCCA who presented with decreased hair density, breakage, and minimal to no scarring,11 suggesting that early disease may be overlooked and raising the possibility that our patient may have had underlying CCCA exacerbated by TTM or coinciding with it before clinical recognition.

While CCCA typically begins at the vertex and spreads centrifugally, our patient initially began losing patches of hair in the frontal and parietal scalp regions before vertex involvement. A case series of CCCA in 6 pediatric patients noted pruritus and scaling in only 2 patients, whereas 4 patients reported tender papules.2 No papules or pruritis were noted in our patient with mild tenderness and tingling.

It is important for dermatologists to recognize and better characterize CCCA within the pediatric population. Identifying patients with multiple risk factors, such as mechanical trauma to the hair follicle through hair-pulling disorders or high-risk hair care practices, or family history, could help mitigate progression. Early intervention can help lessen the psychosocial impact of hair loss, especially within pediatric populations. Further research is necessary to establish a correlation between TTM and subsequent CCCA development.
