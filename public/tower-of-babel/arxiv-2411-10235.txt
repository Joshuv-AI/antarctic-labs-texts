Introduction

1.1
Context

Regularity of optimal transport maps

Given two probability densities
\mu_{1}
and
\mu_{2}
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with finite second moments, the quadratic optimal transport problem consists in solving:

\inf_{T_{\#}\mu_{1}=\mu_{2}}\int\|x-T(x)\|^{2}d\mu_{1}(x),

(1)

where the transport constraint
T_{\#}\mu_{1}=\mu_{2}
imposes that for all Borel subset
A
of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\int_{T^{-1}(A)}d\mu_{1}(x)=\int_{A}d\mu_{2}(x).

Thanks to the work of
Brenier (1991)
and
McCann (1995)
, it is now well known that there exists a unique solution
T
to (
1
) and that this map can be written as
T=\nabla u
with
u
convex being solution in the weak sense to the Monge-Ampère equation

\text{det}(\nabla^{2}u)\mu_{1}(\nabla u)=\mu_{2}.

(2)

The regularity theory for the optimal transport map
T
initiated by
Caffarelli (1992)
has stimulated an intensive line of research (see the textbook
Figalli (2017)
) with many problems still opened to date. The classical statement of this theory (originally from
Caffarelli (1992)
) is the following:

Theorem 1

(Informal version of Theorem 12.50 in
Villani (2009)
)
.

Let
\mu_{1}
and
\mu_{2}
be two probability measures being absolutely continuous with respect to the
d
-dimensional Lebesgue measure and that are supported on open bounded sets
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
respectively. Provided that
their respective densities are bounded away from zero and infinity on their supports and that
\mathcal{Y}
is convex; if the densities of
\mu_{1}
and
\mu_{2}
have Hölder regularity
\beta>0
(with
\beta
a non-integer), then the optimal transport map has Hölder regularity
\beta+1
.

Unfortunately, Caffarelli’s boundedness assumptions on the domains and densities are restrictive as they exclude many important cases, such as Gaussian measures. Recent works, like
Cordero-Erausquin and Figalli (2019)
and
Figalli and Jhaveri (2023)
, extend the classical result to unbounded domains and unbounded densities, but obtain only local regularity. The main obstacle to achieving global regularity is that most methods boil down to showing that the ratio
\frac{\mu_{2}(x)}{\mu_{1}(\nabla u(x))}
is bounded above and below, which then allows the application of classical regularity theory for elliptic equations
(
De Philippis and Figalli, 2014
)
. However, obtaining boundedness for this ratio is highly challenging as even the Gaussian Lipschitz case remains unresolved (Conjecture 1 in
Fathi et al. (2024)
). This difficulty has led researchers to investigate alternative approaches for constructing transport maps that may more readily be proven smooth.

Transport maps from heat flows

A recent advance in this direction is the transport map introduced by
Kim and Milman (2012)
, which is constructed using a heat-diffusion process. The original motivation was to extend Caffarelli’s contraction theorem
(
Caffarelli, 2000
)
stating the existence of a
1
-Lipschitz map pushing forward the
Gaussian measure onto a more log-concave one. This result allows to transfer the functional inequalities satisfied by the Gaussian (like Poincaré and log-Sobolev) to the transported measure.
While Caffarelli’s theorem relies on the optimal transport map, making it difficult to achieve regularity in a wide range of settings, the Kim-Milman map offers a more accessible route to obtaining Lipschitz estimates. A sketch of the map’s construction is as follows:

For all
t>0
and
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
bounded, define the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck semigroup by

P_{t}f(x)=\int f(xe^{-t}+\sqrt{1-e^{-t}}z)d\gamma_{d}(z),

with
\gamma_{d}
the standard
d
-dimensional Gaussian measure. Then, we can show that under appropriate conditions the flow
S_{t}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
defined by

\displaystyle d_{t}S_{t}(x)

\displaystyle=-\nabla(\log P_{t}f)(S_{t}(x))

\displaystyle S_{0}(x)

\displaystyle=x,

satisfies that
(S_{t})_{\#}f=f_{t}d\gamma_{d}
with
f_{0}=f
and
f_{t}\rightarrow 1
when
t\rightarrow 1
. Therefore, the limit map
(S_{\infty})^{-1}
pushes forward
\gamma_{d}
onto
fd\gamma_{d}
.

Following this idea, several recent works build transport maps using diffusion process and prove their Lipschitz continuity under suitable conditions. We can notably cite the Brownian transport map from
Mikulincer and Shenfeld (2024)
(pushes forward the Wiener process to Euclidean semi log concave measures), the Langevin transport map from
Fathi et al. (2024)
(pushes forward log concave measures to log-Lipschitz perturbations in the Euclidean and manifolds settings) and the Föllmer transport map from
Dai et al. (2023)
(pushes forward the Gaussian measure to semi log concave measures). In this paper, we focus on the Langevin transport map which is built using the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process, a flow widely used in practice
(
Song et al., 2020
;
Björk, 2009
;
Davies, 1973
)
. We actually show that the "Föllmer transport map" from
Dai et al. (2023)
is equal to the Langevin map, which allows for a simple formulation using flows that evolve over finite time.
Although we focus on the Langevin transport map, we believe that our regularity results could be extended to other flows given that they have a similar behavior as explained in
Gao et al. (2023)
.

1.2
Contributions

In this work, we explore the regularity of transport maps constructed via diffusion processes, extending the classical theory beyond Lipschitz continuity. While Caffarelli’s seminal regularity results for Monge–Ampère equations have provided foundational insights into optimal transport maps, obtaining regularity in unbounded settings remains a significant challenge. Here, we address this gap by demonstrating that transport maps generated through diffusions can indeed exhibit higher-order regularity, leading to the construction of the first smooth transport map within this framework. These results open new avenues for understanding the structure and properties of transport maps in broader settings. In Section
2
we develop the framework in full generality, where we emphasize
the following key contributions:

1. Higher order regularity of transport maps (Theorems
2
and
3
)

Mirroring the classical regularity theory of optimal transport (Theorem
1
), we prove that the Langevin transport map between the
d
-dimensional Gaussian distribution and a log
\beta
-Hölder perturbation, is of Hölder regularity
\beta+1
. Furthermore, we obtain a Lusin-type result for the transport of the Gaussian to a class of measures supported on the ball. It is shown that a set of mass
1-\epsilon
is transported by a
(\beta+1)
-Hölder map having a norm controlled by a logarithmic power of
\epsilon
.

2. Applications

The existence of smooth transport maps enables a range of applications, which we present in the subsequent sections.

Transfer of functional inequalities (Corollary
2
)

Theorem
2
allows to transfer the functional inequalities involving higher order derivatives from the Gaussian measure to the transported one. As applications we extend the class of measures satisfying the generalized log-Sobolev inequality
(
Rosen, 1976
)
.

Applications to generative models (Theorem
5
and Corollary
4
)
We provide applications of Theorem
2
to the estimation of densities by Wasserstein Generative Adversarial Networks (WGAN) and score-based generative models. Firstly, the existence of smooth transports maps allows to show the optimality of the WGAN estimator within the (widely used) Gaussian setting. Secondly, given the strong correlation between the score function in diffusion models and the transport map’s velocity field, we show that higher regularity of the target distribution transfers to higher regularity of the score function.

1.3
Organization of the paper

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we present the main results in detail, including necessary notations and the construction of the Föllmer/Langevin transport map. Section
3
is dedicated to proving bounds on the derivatives of the transport map, whereas the non-integer regularity is treated in the appendix Section
B
. Finally, we present in Section
4
the applications to the extension of the log-Sobolev inequality as well as to density estimation via generative methods.

Preliminaries and main results

2.1
Hölder spaces

We begin by precisely defining the notion of regularity we describe. For
\eta>0
,
\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}
two open subsets of Euclidean spaces and
f=(f_{1},...,f_{p})\in C^{\lfloor\eta\rfloor}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})
the set of
\lfloor\eta\rfloor:=\max\{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}|\ k\leq\eta\}
times differentiable functions (almost everywhere), denote
\partial^{\nu}f_{i}:=\frac{\partial^{|\nu|}f_{i}}{\partial x_{1}^{\nu_{1}}...\partial x_{d}^{\nu_{d}}}
the partial differential operator for any multi-index
\nu=(\nu_{1},...,\nu_{d})\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}
with
|\nu|:=\nu_{1}+...+\nu_{d}\leq\lfloor\eta\rfloor
. For
\alpha\geq 0
, write

\|f_{i}\|_{\eta-\lfloor\eta\rfloor,\alpha}:=\sup\limits_{x\neq y}\ \frac{f_{i}(x)-f_{i}(y)}{\|x-y\|^{\eta-\lfloor\eta\rfloor}}\log\big((\|x-y\|\wedge\frac{1}{2})^{-1}\big)^{\alpha}

and let

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{\eta,\alpha}_{K}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}):=\Big\{

\displaystyle f\in C^{\lfloor\eta\rfloor}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})\ \big|\max\limits_{i}\sum\limits_{|\nu|\leq\lfloor\eta\rfloor}\|\partial^{\nu}f_{i}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})}+\sum\limits_{|\nu|=\lfloor\eta\rfloor}\|\partial^{\nu}f_{i}\|_{\eta-\lfloor\eta\rfloor,\alpha}\leq K\Big\}

denote the ball of radius
K>0
of the Hölder space
\mathcal{H}^{\eta,\alpha}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})
, the set of functions
f:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathcal{Y}
of regularity
\eta
with additional weak regularity
\alpha
. We have in particular that for all
\epsilon>0
,

\mathcal{H}^{\eta+\epsilon}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})\xhookrightarrow{}\mathcal{H}^{\eta,\alpha}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})\xhookrightarrow{}\mathcal{H}^{\eta}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}),

where we write
A\xhookrightarrow{}B
if the function space
A
compactly injects in the function space
B
and
\mathcal{H}^{\eta}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}):=\mathcal{H}^{\eta,0}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})
denote the classical Hölder space. In the case where
\eta
is not an integer, the space
\mathcal{H}^{\eta,\alpha}
is a Zygmund type space
(
Haroske, 2000
)
, which coincides with the Besov space
\mathcal{B}^{\eta,\alpha}_{\infty,\infty}

(
Haroske, 2006
)
.

2.3
Main results

Let
d\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}
and
\gamma_{d}
denote the standard
d
-dimensional Gaussian measure. We are interested in finding a transport map from
\gamma_{d}
to a probability density function
p:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
of the form

p(x):=\exp\left(-\frac{\|x\|^{2}}{2}+a(x)\right),

(3)

such that the regularity of the transport map depends on the regularity of
a
.
Let
\beta\geq 0
represent the regularity of the map
a
in (
3
) and
\theta>1
represent its additional weak regularity, i.e. we suppose that
a
belongs to
\mathcal{H}^{\beta,\theta}_{K}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R})
for a certain
K>0
. Throughout,
C,C_{2}>0
correspond to constants that can vary from line to line and that only depend on
\theta,\beta,d
and
K
. Let us state the main result of the paper.

Theorem 2
.

Let
p:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a probability density function of the form

p(x)=\exp\left(-\frac{\|x\|^{2}}{2}+a(x)\right),

such that
a\in\mathcal{H}^{\beta,\theta}_{K}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R})
with
\beta\geq 0
,
\theta>1
and
K>0
. Then, there exists a map
T\in C^{\lfloor\beta\rfloor+1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R}^{d})
satisfying
\nabla T\in\mathcal{H}^{\beta}_{C}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
T_{\#}\gamma_{d}=p
.

Theorem
2
asserts that there exists a transport map of Hölder regularity
\beta+1
from the Gaussian measure to a log-
\beta
smooth perturbation. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first result providing high-order regularity of transport in the unbounded setting. The additional logarithmic factor
\theta>1
is expected for heat flows as the solution of the linearized equation has also a logarithmic factor in its regularity (see Proposition 2.4. in
Gallouët et al. (2018)
). The map
T
exhibited in Theorem
2
is the
classical Langevin transport map generated by heat flows
(
Kim and Milman, 2012
;
Fathi et al., 2024
;
Mikulincer and Shenfeld, 2023
)
.
We first prove Section
2.4.2
that the Föllmer transport map introduced in
Dai et al. (2023)
is actually equal to the Langevin map.
This result allows to obtain a forward formulation in finite time of the Langevin transport map. Using this formulation, the proof of Theorem
2
is then divided into two parts: Section
3

focuses on establishing bounds for the derivatives, while the non-integer regularity is addressed in Appendix Section
B
.

We prove that the inverse of the transport map from Theorem
2
is actually also
(\beta+1)
-Hölder, which allows to obtain
C^{\beta+1}
diffeomorphisms between Gaussian deformations.

Corollary 1
.

Let
p,q:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be probability density functions of the form

p(x)=\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\|\Sigma_{p}^{-1/2}(x-\mu_{p})\|^{2}+a(x)\right)\quad\text{ and }\quad q(x)=\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\|\Sigma_{q}^{-1/2}(x-\mu_{q})\|^{2}+b(x)\right),

with
\mu_{p},\mu_{q}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\Sigma_{p},\Sigma_{q}
symmetric matrices satisfying
K^{-1}\text{Id}\preceq\Sigma_{i}\preceq K\text{Id}
for
i\in\{p,q\}
.
Suppose that
a,b\in\mathcal{H}^{\beta,\theta}_{K}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R})
with
\beta\geq 0
,
\theta>1
and
K>0
. Then, there exists a map
T\in C^{\lfloor\beta\rfloor+1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R}^{d})
satisfying
\nabla T,\nabla T^{-1}\in\mathcal{H}^{\beta}_{C}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
T_{\#}p=q
.

The proof of Corollary
1
can be found in Section
B.4.1
. As a by-product of Theorem
2
, we also obtain a result for densities that are supported on the ball. These densities can be written as

p(x)=\exp\left(-u(\|x\|^{2})+a(x)\right),

(4)

with
u
satisfying the following assumption.

Assumption 1
.

The map
u\in C^{(\lfloor\beta\rfloor+1)\vee 2}([0,1),[0,\infty))
satisfies
u(t)\geq(1-t)^{-\frac{1}{K}}
and
0\leq u^{(l)}(t)\leq(1-t)^{-(K+l)}
for all
t\in[0,1)
and
l\in\{1,...,(\lfloor\beta\rfloor+1)\vee 2\}
.

Assumption

imposes that the probability density

defined in (

1
). The adaptation of Theorem
2
to the compact case is the following.

Theorem 3
.

Let
p:B^{d}(0,1)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a probability density function of the form

p(x)=\exp\left(-u(\|x\|^{2})+a(x)\right),

with
a\in\mathcal{H}^{\beta,\theta}_{K}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R})
,
\beta>0
,
\theta>1
,

and

Then, there exists

such that

and
T_{\#}\gamma_{d}=p
. Furthermore, for all

, the restriction of

\mathcal{H}_{C\log(\epsilon^{-1})^{C_{2}}}^{\beta+1}\Big(B^{d}(0,\log(\epsilon^{-1})),B^{d}(0,1)\Big)

This Lusin-type result is weaker than Theorem

as the bound

on the high-order derivatives of the transport map diverges for points at infinity. Nevertheless, it guarantees that a set of mass

is transported by a

-Hölder map having a norm controlled by a logarithmic power of

near the boundary of the ball. Note that in the case

The proof of Theorem

can be found in the Appendix Section

2.4
Transport maps from diffusion processes

The map
T
used in Theorems

and

is the Langevin transport map generated by heat flows

)
. It is built using the backward flow of the Langevin dynamic (see Section

2.4.1
The Föllmer transport map

Let us recall the construction of the Föllmer transport map for a probability density
p
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
being absolutely continous with respect to the
d
-dimensional Gaussian measure and having finite 2nd moment.

Let
\epsilon\in(0,1)
, the Föllmer diffusion process
(\overline{F}_{t})_{t\in[0,1-\epsilon]}
is defined by the Itô Stochastic Differential Equation (SDE):

d\overline{F}_{t}=-\frac{1}{1-t}\overline{F}_{t}dt+\sqrt{\frac{2}{1-t}}dB_{t},\ \ \overline{F}_{0}\sim p,

with
B_{t}
a Brownian motion in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
By Theorem 2.1 in
(
Revuz and Yor, 2013
, Chapter 9)
, the diffusion process
\overline{F}_{t}
has a
unique strong solution on
[0,1-\epsilon]
. Moreover, the probability distribution
\mu_{t}
of
\overline{F}_{t}
satisfies

\mu_{t}\overset{d}{=}(1-t)X+\sqrt{t(2-t)}Y,

(5)

with
X\sim p
and
Y\sim\gamma_{d}
independent random variables. Furthermore, from Theorem 5.14. in
Santambrogio (2015)
, there exists a time-dependent vector field
W_{t}
such that the marginal distribution flow
(\mu_{t})_{t\in[0,1-\epsilon]}
satisfies the continuity equation:

\partial_{t}\mu_{t}=\nabla\cdot(\mu_{t}W_{t}),\quad\mu_{0}=p.

(6)

Knowing the density of
\mu_{t}
, simple calculation gives
W_{t}=V(1-t,\cdot)
,
t
a.e. with

V(t,x):=\frac{1}{t}\nabla\log Q_{t}r(x),\ \ r(x):=\frac{p(x)}{\gamma_{d}(x)}

(7)

and

Q_{t}r(x):=\int\varphi^{t,x}(y)r(y)d\lambda^{d}(y)=\int r(tx+\sqrt{1-t^{2}}z)d\gamma_{d}(z),

(8)

with
\varphi^{t,x}
the density of the
d
-dimensional Gaussian measure with mean
tx
and covariance
(1-t^{2})\text{Id}
. Now, for
t\in(0,1)
, we have

\displaystyle V(t,x)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{1-t^{2}}\int(y-tx)dp^{t,x}(y),

with

p^{t,x}(y):=\frac{1}{Q_{t}r(x)}\ \varphi^{t,x}(y)r(y).

Then, by dominated convergence we obtain

\lim_{t\rightarrow 0}V(t,x)=\int ydp(y),

so the velocity field
V
is well defined for all
t\in[0,1).
Under appropriate conditions (see Propositions
1
or
2
), we can define a flow of maps
\left(x\mapsto X_{t}(x)\right)_{t\in[0,1)}
with
X_{t}
solution to the equation

\partial_{t}X_{t}(x)=V(t,X_{t}(x)),\quad X_{0}(x)=x.

(9)

Then, from (
6
) we have that

(X_{t})_{\#}\gamma_{d}\overset{d}{=}\mu_{1-t},

in particular the map
X_{1}
(when well-defined) transports the
d
-dimensional Gaussian distribution to the measure
p
.
Equation (
9
) is well posed under minimal Cauchy-Lipschitz assumptions (Remark 7 in
Ambrosio and Crippa (2014)
).

Proposition 1
.

Let
t\in(0,1]
such that

V\in L^{1}\Big([0,t],W^{1,\infty}_{loc}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R}^{d})\Big)\text{ and }\frac{V}{1+\|x\|}\in L^{1}\Big([0,t],L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R}^{d})\Big),

then the equation (
9
) has a unique solution
(X_{s})_{s\in[0,t]}
,
x
almost everywhere. Furthermore, for all
s\in[0,t]
, it verifies
(X_{s})_{\#}\gamma_{d}=\mu_{1-s}
.

We aim to transport the
d
-dimensional Gaussian distribution to probability measures
p
that do not necessarily meet the conditions of Proposition
1
in time

Lemma 1

Let
\mu

\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\mu_{k}
,
\nu_{k}
two sequences of probability measures converging in distribution to

and
\nu
respectively. Suppose that the supports of
\mu
and
\mu_{k}
are open. If there exists a sequence of map
T_{k}
such that
(T_{k})_{\#}\mu_{k}=\nu_{k}
and
T_{k}\in\mathcal{H}^{\alpha}_{C}
, then there exists a map
T\in\mathcal{H}^{\alpha}_{C}
satisfying
T_{\#}\mu=\nu
.

Proof.

The existence of the Lipschitz transport map
T
is given by Lemma 2.1 in
Neeman (2022)
and the higher order regularity can be deduced from the Arzelà–Ascoli theorem applied to each derivatives.
∎

The proof of Theorems

and

2.4.2
Equality between the Föllmer and the Langevin transport map

In this work, we focus on establishing the regularity of the Langevin transport map. This map is typically constructed as the inverse of the limiting solution of an ordinary differential equation (ODE), as detailed below. In order to use a simple formulation, we show that the Föllmer transport map from the previous section is actually equal to the Langevin map.

Let us first recall the construction of the Langevin transport map. The Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process
(\overline{L}_{t})_{t\in[0,\infty)}
is defined by the following Itô SDE:

d\overline{L}_{t}=-\overline{L}_{t}dt+\sqrt{2}dB_{t},\ \ \overline{L}_{0}\sim p,

with
B_{t}
a Brownian motion in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We can show (Lemma 2 in
Mikulincer and Shenfeld (2023)
) that

\overline{L}_{t}\overset{d}{=}\mu_{1-e^{-t}}.

Defining the flow of maps

\left(x\mapsto L_{s,t}(x)\right)_{s\in[0,\infty),t\in[0,s]}
with
L_{s,t}
solution to

\partial_{t}L_{s,t}(x)=\nabla(\log Q_{e^{-(s-t)}}r)(L_{s,t}(x)),\ \ L_{s,0}(x)=x,

we obtain that

(L_{t,t})_{\#}\mu_{1-e^{-t}}\overset{d}{=}p,

in particular we have that when both the Föllmer and Langevin flows are well defined,

(X_{1})_{\#}\gamma_{d}\overset{d}{=}p\quad\text{ and }\quad(L_{\infty,\infty})_{\#}\gamma_{d}=\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}(L_{t,t})_{\#\mu_{1-e^{-t}}}\overset{d}{=}p,

Therefore, the Föllmer transport map
X_{1}
and the Langevin transport map
L_{\infty,\infty}
both transport the Gaussian measure to
p
. We now prove that these maps are, in fact, identical under classical Cauchy-Lipschitz conditions for the existence of the final maps
X_{1}
and
L_{\infty,\infty}
.

Proposition 2
.

Let
p
a probability density on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
being absolutely continuous with respect to the
d
-dimensional Gaussian measure and having finite 2nd moment. If the velocity
V
defined in (
7
) satisfies

V\in L^{1}\Big([0,1],W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R}^{d})\Big),

then
X_{1}=L_{\infty,\infty}
almost everywhere.

Proof.

Both flows are well defined as
V
satisfies the classical Cauchy-Lipschitz assumption (see Section 2 in
Ambrosio and Crippa (2014)
).
Define the flow of maps

\left(x\mapsto Y_{s,t}(x)\right)_{s\in[0,\infty),t\in[e^{-s},1]}
by

Y_{s,t}=L_{s,s+\log(t)}.

In particular
Y
is solution to

\displaystyle\partial_{t}Y_{s,t}(x)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{t}\nabla(\log Q_{e^{-(s-(s+\log(t)))}}r)(Y_{s,t}(x)),\ \ Y_{s,e^{-s}}(x)=x.

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{t}\nabla(\log Q_{t}r)(Y_{s,t}(x)).

Now, for the process
(a_{t}^{s})_{t\in[e^{-s},1]}
defined by

a_{t}^{s}:=\|Y_{s,t}(x)-X_{t}(x)\|^{2},

we have (in a weak sense) that

\displaystyle\partial_{t}a_{t}^{s}=

\displaystyle 2\left\langle Y_{s,t}(x)-X_{t}(x),\frac{1}{t}\nabla(\log Q_{t}r)(Y_{s,t}(x))-\frac{1}{t}\nabla(\log Q_{t}r)(X_{t}(x))\right\rangle

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\|\nabla V(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{\infty}}a_{t}^{s}.

Then, by Gronwall’s lemma we obtain that for all
t\in[e^{-s},1]
,

a_{t}^{s}\leq a^{s}_{e^{-s}}\exp\left(\int_{e^{-s}}^{t}2\|\nabla V(u,\cdot)\|_{L^{\infty}}du\right)\leq Ca^{s}_{e^{-s}}.

Furthermore, we have

\lim_{s\rightarrow\infty}a_{e^{-s}}^{s}=\lim_{s\rightarrow\infty}\|x-\left(x+\int_{0}^{e^{-s}}\frac{1}{t}\nabla(\log Q_{t}r)(X_{t}(x))dt\right)\|=0.

Therefore,

L_{\infty,\infty}=\lim_{s\rightarrow\infty}Y_{s,1}=X_{1}.

∎

Having the equivalence of the two maps, we use the Föllmer formulation to prove Theorems
2
and
3
.

3.1.1
A general Gronwall bound

Let
(X_{t})_{t\in[0,1)}
be the solution of (
9
) for
p
satisfying either the assumptions of Theorems
2
or
3
. For
k\in\{1,...,\lfloor\beta\rfloor+1\}
,
w\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, let us provide a bound for all
t\in[0,1)
on the process

\alpha_{t}^{k}:=\|\nabla^{k}X_{t}(x)w^{k}\|^{2}.

The map
x\mapsto\nabla^{k}X_{t}(x)
is well defined for all
t\in[0,1)
by classical regularity theory of ordinary differential equations (see for example Theorem 14.7 in
Tu (2011)
).
As

\partial_{t}X_{t}(x)=V(t,X_{t}(x))
,
we are going to compute the differentials of the map
x\mapsto\partial_{t}X_{t}(x)
using the Faa di Bruno formula. For two unit-variate functions
f,g:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, the Faa di Bruno formula states that the
k
-derivative of the composition of
f
and
g
is

(f\circ g)^{(k)}(x)=\sum_{\pi\in\Pi_{k}}f^{(|\pi|)}(g(x))\prod_{B\in\pi}g^{(|B|)}(x),

where
\Pi_{k}
denotes the set of all partitions of the set
\{1,...,k\}
. For simplicity, in the multivariate setting we will keep this notation by writing for
f,g:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\nabla^{k}(f\circ g)(x)=\sum_{\pi\in\Pi_{k}}\nabla^{|\pi|}f(g(x))\prod_{B\in\pi}\nabla^{|B|}g(x),

where the latter is defined by

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{\pi\in\Pi_{k}}\nabla^{|\pi|}f(g(x))\prod_{B\in\pi}\nabla^{|B|}g(x)\right)w^{k}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{\pi=(B_{1},B_{2},...,B_{i})\in\Pi_{k}}\nabla^{|\pi|}f(g(x))\left(\nabla^{|B_{1}|}g(x)w^{|B_{1}|},\nabla^{|B_{2}|}g(x)w^{|B_{2}|},...,\nabla^{|B_{i}|}g(x)w^{|B_{i}|}\right).

Using this notation, we obtain by Schwarz theorem that

\partial_{t}\nabla^{k}X_{t}(x)=\sum_{\pi\in\Pi_{k}}\nabla^{|\pi|}V(t,X_{t}(x))\prod_{B\in\pi}\nabla^{|B|}X_{t}(x).

We then deduce that

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\alpha_{t}^{k}

\displaystyle=2\left\langle\nabla^{k}X_{t}(x)w^{k},\partial_{t}\nabla^{k}X_{t}(x)\right\rangle

\displaystyle=2\left\langle\nabla^{k}X_{t}(x)w^{k},\sum_{\pi\in\Pi_{k},|\pi|>1}\nabla^{|\pi|}V(t,X_{t}(x))\prod_{B\in\pi}\nabla^{|B|}X_{t}(x)w^{|B|}+\nabla V(t,X_{t}(x))\nabla^{k}X_{t}(x)w^{k}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\leq 2\lambda_{1}^{k}(t)(\alpha_{t}^{k})^{1/2}+2\lambda_{2}(t)\alpha_{t}^{k},

with

\lambda_{1}^{k}(t):=\|\sum_{\pi\in\Pi_{k},|\pi|>1}\nabla^{|\pi|}V(t,X_{t}(x))\prod_{B\in\pi}\nabla^{|B|}X_{t}(x)w^{|B|}\|

and

\lambda_{2}(t):=\lambda_{\max}\left(\nabla V(t,X_{t}(x))\right).

For
k=1
, we have from Gronwall’s inequality that

(\alpha_{t}^{1})^{1/2}\leq(\alpha_{0}^{1})^{1/2}\exp\left(\int_{0}^{t}\lambda_{2}(z)dz\right)=\exp\left(\int_{0}^{t}\lambda_{2}(z)dz\right).

(10)

For
k\geq 2
, as
\alpha_{0}^{k}=0
we deduce from the non linear Gronwall’s inequality (Theorem 21 in
Dragomir (2003)
) that

(\alpha_{t}^{k})^{1/2}\leq\int_{0}^{t}\lambda_{1}^{k}(s)\exp\left(\int_{s}^{t}\lambda_{2}(z)dz\right)ds.

(11)

We will first bound
\lambda_{2}
which will give us by (
10
) that
\alpha_{t}^{1}
is bounded. Then, giving a bound on the quantity

\sum_{j=2}^{k}\int_{0}^{1}\|\nabla^{j}V(s,X_{s}(x))\|ds,

we will finally obtain the bound on
\alpha_{t}^{k}
using the following lemma.

Lemma 2
.

Let
k\in\{1,...,\lfloor\beta\rfloor+1\}
,
A>0
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that

\int_{0}^{1}\lambda_{\max}\left(\nabla V(s,X_{s}(x)\right)ds+\mathds{1}_{\{k\geq 2\}}\sum_{j=2}^{k}\int_{0}^{1}\|\nabla^{j}V(s,X_{s}(x))\|ds\leq A.

Then, there exists
C>0
depending only on
k
and
A
such that for all
t\in[0,1)
and
w\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
,

\|\nabla^{k}X_{t}(x)w^{k}\|\leq C.

Proof.

From (
10
), we have that if

\int_{0}^{1}\lambda_{\max}\left(\nabla V(s,X_{s}(x))\right)ds\leq A,

then

\sup_{s\in[0,1]}(\alpha_{s}^{1})^{1/2}\leq\exp(A).

Let us now treat the case
k\geq 2
by induction. Suppose that for all
j\in\{1,...,k-1\},
we have that

\sup_{s\in[0,1]}(\alpha_{s}^{j})^{1/2}\leq C
.
Then, from (
11
) we have

\displaystyle(\alpha_{t}^{k})^{1/2}\leq

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\lambda_{1}^{k}(s)\exp\left(\int_{s}^{t}\lambda_{2}(z)dz\right)ds

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\int_{0}^{t}\lambda_{1}^{k}(s)ds

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\int_{0}^{t}\sum_{\pi\in\Pi_{k},|\pi|>1}\|\nabla^{|\pi|}V(s,X_{s}(x))\|\prod_{B\in\pi}(\alpha_{s}^{|B|})^{1/2}ds

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\sum_{j=2}^{k}\int_{0}^{1}\|\nabla^{j}V(s,X_{s}(x))\|\prod_{i=1}^{k-1}\Big((\alpha_{s}^{i})^{1/2}\vee 1\Big)^{k-1}ds

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\sum_{j=2}^{k}\int_{0}^{1}\|\nabla^{j}V(s,X_{s}(x))\|ds

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C.

∎

Lemma
2
shows that obtaining a bound on the derivative of the transport map reduces to controlling the quantity
\int_{0}^{1}\|\nabla^{j}V(s,X_{s}(x))\|ds,
for all
j\in\{1,...,k\}
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. The subsequent sections are devoted to obtaining this estimate.

3.1.2
Derivation of
\nabla^{k}V(t,x)
for
k\in\{2,...,\lfloor\beta\rfloor+1\}

For
l\in\{0,...,\lfloor\beta\rfloor\}
and
y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, let us note

f^{l}(y):=\frac{1}{r(y)}\nabla^{l}r(y),

with
r(y)=\frac{p(y)}{\gamma_{d}(y)}
; and for
t\in[0,1)
,
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

p^{t,x}(y):=\frac{1}{Q_{t}r(x)}\ \varphi^{t,x}(y)r(y),

with
Q_{t}r(x)
defined in (
8

\varphi^{t,x}
the density of the d-dimensional Gaussian measure with mean
tx
and covariance
(1-t^{2})\text{Id}
. Let us give a first bound on the norm of the differentials of
V
.

Lemma 3
.

For all
k\in\{2,...,\lfloor\beta\rfloor+1\}
,
t\in[0,1)
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we have

\displaystyle\|\nabla^{k}V(t,x)\|\leq

\displaystyle C\sum_{l=1}^{k-1}\Biggl(\|\int f^{l}(y)\nabla^{2}_{x}p^{t,x}(y)d\lambda^{d}(y)\|\prod_{j=1}^{k-2}\left(\|\int f^{j}(y)dp^{t,x}(y)\|\vee 1\right)^{k-2}

\displaystyle+\|\int f^{l}(y)\nabla_{x}p^{t,x}(y)d\lambda^{d}(y)\|\sum_{j=1}^{k-2}\|\int f^{j}(y)\nabla_{x}p^{t,x}(y)d\lambda^{d}(y)\|\prod_{i=1}^{k-3}\left(\|\int f^{i}(y)dp^{t,x}(y)\|\vee 1\right)^{k-3}\Biggl).

Proof.

Recalling that

\displaystyle Q_{t}r(x)

\displaystyle=\int\varphi^{t,x}(y)r(y)d\lambda^{d}(y)=\int r(tx+\sqrt{1-t^{2}}z)d\gamma_{d}(z),

we have for all

\displaystyle\nabla^{l}Q_{t}r(x)=t^{l}\int\nabla^{l}r(tx+\sqrt{1-t^{2}}z)d\gamma_{d}(z).

(12)

As
\nabla^{k}V(t,x)=\frac{1}{t}\nabla^{k+1}\log Q_{t}r(x)
and

has only

derivatives, we first compute

using (
12

anymore.

Using the Faa di Bruno formula we have

\displaystyle\nabla^{k-2}V(t,x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{t}\nabla^{k-1}\log Q_{t}r(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{t}\sum_{\pi\in\Pi_{k-1}}\frac{(-1)^{|\pi|+1}}{(Q_{t}r(x))^{|\pi|}}\prod_{B\in\pi}\nabla^{|B|}Q_{t}r(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{t}\sum_{\pi\in\Pi_{k-1}}(-1)^{|\pi|+1}\prod_{B\in\pi}\frac{t^{|B|}}{Q_{t}r(x)}\int\nabla^{|B|}r(\sqrt{1-t^{2}}z+tx)d\gamma_{d}(z)

\displaystyle=t^{k-2}\sum_{\pi\in\Pi_{k-1}}(-1)^{|\pi|+1}\prod_{B\in\pi}\int f^{|B|}(\sqrt{1-t^{2}}z+tx)\frac{r(\sqrt{1-t^{2}}z+tx)}{Q_{t}r(x)}d\gamma_{d}(z)

\displaystyle=t^{k-2}\sum_{\pi\in\Pi_{k-1}}(-1)^{|\pi|+1}\prod_{B\in\pi}\int f^{|B|}(y)p^{t,x}(y)d\lambda^{d}(y),

with

f^{l}(y):=\nabla^{l}r(y)/r(y)\ \ \ \text{ and }\ \ \ p^{t,x}(y):=\frac{\varphi^{t,x}(y)r(y)}{\int\varphi^{t,x}(z)r(z)d\lambda^{d}(z)}.

Now for any set of functions
(f_{i})_{i=1,...,m}\in\mathcal{H}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R})^{m}
, we have

\nabla^{2}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{m}f_{i}\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\nabla^{2}f_{i}\prod_{j\neq i}f_{j}+\nabla f_{i}\sum_{j\neq i}\nabla f_{j}\prod_{l\neq i,j}f_{l},

we then deduce that

\displaystyle\nabla^{k}V(t,x)=

\displaystyle t^{k-2}\sum_{\pi\in\Pi_{k-1}}(-1)^{|\pi|+1}\sum_{B\in\pi}\Biggl(\int f^{|B|}(y)\nabla^{2}_{x}dp^{t,x}(y)\prod_{C\in\pi\setminus\{B\}}\int f^{|C|}(y)dp^{t,x}(y)

\displaystyle+\int f^{|B|}(y)\nabla_{x}dp^{t,x}(y)\sum_{C\in\pi\setminus\{B\}}\int f^{|C|}(y)\nabla_{x}dp^{t,x}(y)\prod_{D\in\pi\setminus\{B,C\}}\int f^{|D|}(y)dp^{t,x}(y)\Biggl),

(13)

with the notation

\int f(y)\nabla^{i}_{x}dp^{t,x}(y)=\int f(y)\nabla^{i}_{x}p^{t,x}(y)d\lambda^{d}(y),

for
i=0,1,2
. Now, as for
\pi\in\Pi_{k-1}
,
B\in\pi
,
C\in\pi\setminus\{B\}
,
D\in\pi\setminus\{B,C\}
we have that
|B|\in\{1,...,k-1\}
,
|C|\in\{1,...,k-2\}
and
|D|\in\{1,...,k-3\}
, from (
13
) we deduce that

\displaystyle\|\nabla^{k}V(t,x)\|\leq

\displaystyle C\sum_{l=1}^{k-1}\Biggl(\|\int f^{l}(y)\nabla^{2}_{x}p^{t,x}(y)d\lambda^{d}(y)\|\prod_{j=1}^{k-2}\left(\|\int f^{j}(y)dp^{t,x}(y)\|\vee 1\right)^{k-2}

\displaystyle+\|\int f^{l}(y)\nabla_{x}p^{t,x}(y)d\lambda^{d}(y)\|\sum_{j=1}^{k-2}\|\int f^{j}(y)\nabla_{x}p^{t,x}(y)d\lambda^{d}(y)\|\prod_{i=1}^{k-3}\left(\|\int f^{i}(y)dp^{t,x}(y)\|\vee 1\right)^{k-3}\Biggl).

∎

Let us now derive the different quantities that intervenes in the bound of Lemma
3
.
Writing

H(y,x):=y-\int zdp^{t,x}(z),

(14)

we have the following result on the integration against
\nabla_{x}^{i}p^{t,x}
, for

Lemma 4
.

For all
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,

and

\displaystyle\int f(y)\nabla_{x}p^{t,x}(y)d\lambda^{d}(y)=\frac{t}{1-t^{2}}\int f(y)H(y,x)dp^{t,x}(y)

and

\displaystyle\int f(y)\nabla_{x}^{2}p^{t,x}(y)d\lambda^{d}(y)=

\displaystyle\frac{t^{2}}{(1-t^{2})^{2}}\int f(y)\left(H(y,x)^{\otimes 2}-\int H(w,x)^{\otimes 2}dp^{t,x}(w)\right)dp^{t,x}(y).

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle\int f(y)\nabla_{x}p^{t,x}(y)d\lambda^{d}(y)

\displaystyle=\int f(y)\nabla_{x}\left(\frac{\varphi^{t,x}(y)}{\int\varphi^{t,x}(z)r(z)d\lambda^{d}(z)}\right)r(y)d\lambda^{d}(y)

\displaystyle=\int f(y)\frac{t}{1-t^{2}}\left(y-tx-\int(w-tx)\frac{\varphi^{t,x}(w)r(w)d\lambda^{d}(w)}{\int\varphi^{t,x}(z)r(z)d\lambda^{d}(z)}\right)\frac{\varphi^{t,x}(y)r(y)d\lambda^{d}(y)}{\int\varphi^{t,x}(z)r(z)d\lambda^{d}(z)}

\displaystyle\int f(y)

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}_{x}p^{t,x}(y)d\lambda^{d}(y)=\frac{t}{1-t^{2}}\int f(y)\Biggl(\left(-\int w\nabla_{x}p^{t,x}(w)d\lambda^{d}(w)\right)p^{t,x}(y)

\displaystyle+\left(y-\int wdp^{t,x}(w)\right)\nabla_{x}p^{t,x}(y)\Biggl)d\lambda^{d}(y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{t^{2}}{(1-t^{2})^{2}}\int f(y)\Biggl(-\int w\otimes\left(w-\int zdp^{t,x}(z))\right)dp^{t,x}(w)p^{t,x}(y)

\displaystyle+\left(y-\int wdp^{t,x}(w)\right)^{\otimes 2}p^{t,x}(y)\Biggl)d\lambda^{d}(y)

\displaystyle\frac{t^{2}}{(1-t^{2})^{2}}\int f(y)\Biggl(\left(y-\int wdp^{t,x}(w)\right)^{\otimes 2}-\int\left(w-\int zdp^{t,x}(z)\right)^{\otimes 2}dp^{t,x}(w)\Biggl)dp^{t,x}(y).

∎

3.2.1
Concentration of the mass under

To address the term of order

Proposition 3
.

For all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
t\in[0,1)
and
i,j\in\{1,...,d\}

we have

\int H(y,x)_{i}^{2}dp^{t,x}(y)\leq C(1-t^{2})

and

\int\left(H(y,x)^{\otimes 2}_{ij}-\int H(z,x)^{\otimes 2}_{ij}dp^{t,x}(z)\right)^{2}dp^{t,x}(y)\leq C(1-t^{2})^{2}.

Proof.

Let
Y,\overline{Y}
be two independent random variables of law
p^{t,x}
and
Z,\overline{Z}
two independent random variables of law
\varphi^{t,x}
. As for all
y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
we have

p^{t,x}(y)=\frac{r(y)}{Q_{t}r(x)}\varphi^{t,x}(y)\leq C\varphi^{t,x}(y),

then

\displaystyle\int H(y,x)_{i}^{2}dp^{t,x}(y)=

\displaystyle\int\left(y_{i}-\int y_{i}dp^{t,x}(z)\right)^{2}dp^{t,x}(y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\text{Var}_{p^{t,x}}[Y_{i}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{p^{t,x}}[(Y_{i}-\overline{Y}_{i})^{2}]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\mathbb{E}_{\varphi^{t,x}}[(Z_{i}-\overline{Z}_{i})^{2}]\leq C\text{Var}_{\varphi^{t,x}}(Z_{i})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C(1-t^{2}).

Likewise, using Cauchy-Schwarz inequality we get

\displaystyle\int\left(H(y,x)^{\otimes 2}_{ij}-\int H(z,x)^{\otimes 2}_{ij}dp^{t,x}(z)\right)^{2}dp^{t,x}(y)

\displaystyle=\text{Var}_{p^{t,x}}[(Y_{i}-\mathbb{E}_{p^{t,x}}(Y_{i}))(Y_{j}-\mathbb{E}_{p^{t,x}}(Y_{j}))]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{p^{t,x}}[(Y_{i}-\mathbb{E}_{p^{t,x}}(Y_{i}))^{2}(Y_{j}-\mathbb{E}_{p^{t,x}}(Y_{j}))^{2}]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{p^{t,x}}[(Y_{i}-\mathbb{E}_{p^{t,x}}(Y_{i}))^{4}]^{1/2}\mathbb{E}_{p^{t,x}}[(Y_{j}-\mathbb{E}_{p^{t,x}}(Y_{j}))^{4}]^{1/2}

and

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{p^{t,x}}[(Y_{i}-\mathbb{E}_{p^{t,x}}(Y_{i}))^{4}]\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{p^{t,x}}[(Y_{i}-\overline{Y}_{i})^{4}]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\mathbb{E}_{\varphi^{t,x}}[(Z_{i}-\overline{Z}_{i})^{4}]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C(1-t^{2})^{2},

which gives the result.
∎

Using Proposition
3
we obtain a bound on the different terms appearing in the estimate given by Lemma
3
.

Proposition 4
.

For all
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
i\in\{1,2\}
,
t\in[0,1)
and
x,\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we have

\displaystyle\|\int f(y)\nabla_{x}^{i}p^{t,x}(y)d\lambda^{d}(y)\|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{Ct^{i}}{(1-t^{2})^{i/2}}\left(\int\|f(y)-\xi\|^{2}dp^{t,x}(y)\right)^{1/2}.

Proof.

We first take care of the term
\|\int f(y)\nabla_{x}^{2}p^{t,x}(y)d\lambda^{d}(y)\|
. Using Lemma
4
and the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality we obtain

\displaystyle\|

\displaystyle\int f(y)\nabla^{2}_{x}p^{t,x}(y)d\lambda^{d}(y)\|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\|\frac{t^{2}}{(1-t^{2})^{2}}\int\left(f(y)-\xi\right)\left(H(y,x)^{\otimes 2}-\int H(w,x)^{\otimes 2}dp^{t,x}(w)\right)dp^{t,x}(y)\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{t^{2}}{(1-t^{2})^{2}}\left(\int\|f(y)-\xi\|^{2}dp^{t,x}(y)\right)^{1/2}\sum_{ij}\Biggl(\int\left(H(y,x)^{\otimes 2}_{ij}-\int H(w,x)^{\otimes 2}_{ij}dp^{t,x}(w)\right)^{2}dp^{t,x}(y)\Biggl)^{1/2}.

Then, from Proposition
3
we deduce that

\displaystyle\|\int f(y)\nabla_{x}^{2}p^{t,x}(y)d\lambda^{d}(y)\|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{Ct^{2}}{(1-t^{2})}\left(\int\|f(y)-\xi\|^{2}dp^{t,x}(y)\right)^{1/2}.

Similarly, we have

\displaystyle\|\int

\displaystyle f(y)\nabla_{x}dp^{t,x}(y)\|=\frac{t}{1-t^{2}}\|\int(f(y)-\xi)\left(y-\int wdp^{t,x}(w)\right)dp^{t,x}(y)\|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{t}{1-t^{2}}\left(\int\|f(y)-\xi\|^{2}dp^{t,x}(y)\right)^{1/2}\sum_{i}\left(\int\left(y_{i}-\int w_{i}dp^{t,x}(w)\right)^{2}dp^{t,x}(y)\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C}{(1-t^{2})^{1/2}}\left(\int\|f(y)-\xi\|^{2}dp^{t,x}(y)\right)^{1/2}.

∎

Proposition
4
highlights the fact that the integration of a function against the
i
-th derivatives of
p^{t,x}
grows at most as
(1-t^{2})^{-i/2}
. It additionally shows that it is proportional to the variance of the function with respect to
p^{t,x}
. With the estimates from Proposition
4
in hand, we are now ready to bound the derivatives of the transport map.

3.2.2
Well-definedness of the flow and bound on
\|\nabla X_{1}\|_{\infty}

The estimate on the Jacobian of
X_{1}
differs from that of the higher-order derivatives as it does not involve the derivatives of the density
p
. To apply Lemma
2
, we first demonstrate using the fact that
a\in\mathcal{H}^{\beta,\theta}_{K}
, that the quantity
\int_{0}^{t}\|\nabla V(s,\cdot)\|_{\infty}ds
remains bounded as

approaches 1.

Proposition 5
.

For all
t\in[0,1)
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we have

\displaystyle\|\nabla V(t,x)\|\leq Ct(1-t^{2})^{(\beta-\lfloor\beta\rfloor)/2-1}\log\Big(\big((1-t^{2})\wedge 1/2\big)^{-1}\Big)^{-\theta},

which gives in particular that

\int_{0}^{1}\|\nabla V(t,\cdot)\|_{\infty}dt<\infty.

Proof.

Let
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we have

\displaystyle\|\nabla V(t,x)\|=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{t}\|\nabla^{2}\log(Q_{t}r)(x)\|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\|\frac{t}{(1-t^{2})^{2}}\int(y-tx)H(y,x)dp^{t,x}(y)-\frac{t}{1-t^{2}}\text{Id}\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{t}{(1-t^{2})^{2}}\|\int(y-tx)^{\otimes 2}dp^{t,x}(y)-(1-t^{2})\text{Id}\|

\displaystyle+\frac{t}{(1-t^{2})^{2}}\|\int(y-tx)\left(tx-\int zdp^{t,x}(z)\right)dp^{t,x}(y)\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{t}{(1-t^{2})^{2}}\|\int\left(y-tx\right)^{\otimes 2}\left(\frac{r(y)}{\int r(z)\varphi^{t,x}(z)d\lambda^{d}(z)}-1\right)\varphi^{t,x}(y)d\lambda^{d}(y)\|

\displaystyle+\frac{t}{(1-t^{2})^{2}}\|\int(y-tx)dp^{t,x}(y)\|^{2}.

(15)

As
r=e^{a}\in\mathcal{H}^{\beta,\theta}_{C}\subset\mathcal{H}^{\beta-\lfloor\beta\rfloor,\theta}_{C}
, we have

\displaystyle\|\int(y-tx)dp^{t,x}(y)\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C\|\int(y-tx)(r(y)-r(tx))\varphi^{t,x}(y)d\lambda^{d}(y)\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\left(\int\|y-tx\|^{1+\beta-\lfloor\beta\rfloor}\log((\|y-tx\|\wedge 1/2)^{-1})^{-\theta}\varphi^{t,x}(y)d\lambda^{d}(y)\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\left(\int\|y-tx\|^{1+\beta-\lfloor\beta\rfloor}\log((\|y-tx\|\wedge 1/2)^{-1})^{-\theta}\varphi^{t,x}(y)\mathds{1}_{\{\|y-tx\|\leq(1-t^{2})^{1/4}\}}d\lambda^{d}(y)\right)^{2}

\displaystyle+C\left(\int\|y-tx\|^{1+\beta-\lfloor\beta\rfloor}\log((\|y-tx\|\wedge 1/2)^{-1})^{-\theta}\varphi^{t,x}(y)\mathds{1}_{\{\|y-tx\|>(1-t^{2})^{1/4}\}}d\lambda^{d}(y)\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\log\Big(\big((1-t^{2})^{1/4}\wedge 1/2\big)^{-1}\Big)^{-2\theta}\left(\int\|y-tx\|^{1+\beta-\lfloor\beta\rfloor}\varphi^{t,x}(y)d\lambda^{d}(y)\right)^{2}

\displaystyle+C\exp\left(-\frac{(1-t^{2})^{-1/4}}{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C(1-t^{2})^{1+\beta-\lfloor\beta\rfloor}\log\Big(\big((1-t^{2})\wedge 1/2\big)^{-1}\Big)^{-2\theta}.

On the other hand, we have

\displaystyle\|

\displaystyle\int\left(y-tx\right)^{\otimes 2}\left(\frac{r(y)}{\int r(z)\varphi^{t,x}(z)d\lambda^{d}(z)}-1\right)\varphi^{t,x}(y)d\lambda^{d}(y)\|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{Q_{t}r(x)}\|\int\left(y-tx\right)^{\otimes 2}\left(r(y)-r(tx)+r(tx)-\int r(z)\varphi^{t,x}(z)d\lambda^{d}(z)\right)\varphi^{t,x}(y)d\lambda^{d}(y)\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\|\int\left(y-tx\right)^{\otimes 2}\left(r(y)-r(tx)\right)\varphi^{t,x}(y)d\lambda^{d}(y)\|

\displaystyle+C\|\int(r(tx)-r(z))\varphi^{t,x}(z)d\lambda^{d}(z)\|\|\int\left(y-tx\right)^{\otimes 2}\varphi^{t,x}(y)d\lambda^{d}(y)\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\|\int\|y-tx\|^{2+\beta-\lfloor\beta\rfloor}\log((\|y-tx\|\wedge 1/2)^{-1})^{-\theta}\varphi^{t,x}(y)d\lambda^{d}(y)\|

\displaystyle+C\|\int\|x-y\|^{\beta-\lfloor\beta\rfloor}\log((\|y-tx\|\wedge 1/2)^{-1})^{-\theta}\varphi^{t,x}(z)d\lambda^{d}(z)\|\|\int\|y-tx\|^{2}\varphi^{t,x}(y)d\lambda^{d}(y)\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C(1-t^{2})^{1+(\beta-\lfloor\beta\rfloor)/2}\log\Big(\big((1-t^{2})\wedge 1/2\big)^{-1}\Big)^{-\theta}.

We then conclude that

\displaystyle\|\nabla V(t,x)\|\leq C\frac{t}{(1-t^{2})^{1-(\beta-\lfloor\beta\rfloor)/2}}\log\Big(\big((1-t^{2})\wedge 1/2\big)^{-1}\Big)^{-\theta},

which gives the result as
\theta>1
.
∎

Using Proposition
5
we obtain that the velocity
V
satisfies the assumptions of Proposition
1
, which gives well-definedness of the flow of transport maps. We conclude in the next proposition that the Langevin transport map
T:=X_{1}
satisfies that
\nabla T\in\mathcal{H}^{0}_{C}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

Proposition 6
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
2
, there exists a unique solution for all
t\in[0,1]
to the equation

\partial_{t}X_{t}(x)=V(t,X_{t}(x)),\quad X_{0}(x)=x.

Furthermore, the solution at time
t=1
verifies that

\nabla X_{1}\in\mathcal{H}^{0}_{C}.

Proof.

To apply Proposition
1
we need to bound the infinite norm of
V_{t}
. For
t\in[0,1]
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we have

\displaystyle\|V(t,x)\|=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{t}\|\nabla\log(Q_{t}r)(x)\|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\|\frac{1}{1-t^{2}}\int(y-tx)dp^{t,x}(y)\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{C}{1-t^{2}}\int\|y-tx\|\varphi^{t,x}(y)d\lambda^{d}(y)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C(1-t^{2})^{-1/2}.

Putting together this estimate with the one given by Proposition

, we get the well-definedness of the flow. Finally, combining Lemma

and Proposition

we obtain that for all

,

.
∎

.

, then for all

4
Applications

In this section we present different applications of Theorem
2
. The first application involves extending functional inequalities, a classical use of (Lipschitz) regularity theory for transport maps. The second one is the proof of the optimality of the widely used GAN estimator in the context of density estimation.

4.1
Generalized log-Sobolev inequality

The theory of Lipschitz transport maps has been highly motivated by their ability to prove functional inequalities, by transferring them from
the source measure to the target measure. Having obtained in Theorem
2
a transport map with higher order regularity, we can naturally extend Gaussian inequalities involving high order derivatives. One of such inequality is the Generalized log-Sobolev inequality.

Theorem 4

(Generalized log-Sobolev inequality
Rosen (1976)
)
.

For
\phi\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
satisfying
\phi(x)=a\|x\|^{1+s}
for
\|x\|\geq K,
with
K,a,s>0
, define the measure
\mu
having a density
d\mu(x)=\exp(-\phi(x))dx
with respect to the
d
-dimensional Lebesgue measure. Then, for all
k\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}
and
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
with
\|f\|_{L_{\mu}^{2}}=1
, we have

\displaystyle\int|f(x)|^{2}|\log(|f(x)|)|^{2sk/(s+1)}d\mu(x)\leq C\sum_{|\alpha|=0}^{k}\|D^{\alpha}f\|^{2}_{L_{\mu}^{2}}.

This inequality extends the classical log-Sobolev inequalities, which are essential tools in analysis and PDE theory, by incorporating logarithmic weights that reflect the growth conditions imposed by the function
\phi
. Using the Föllmer transport map, we can extend this inequality to measure
\mu
satisfying the assumptions of Theorem
2
.

Corollary 2
.

For
a\in\mathcal{H}^{\beta,\theta}_{K}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R})
with
\beta\geq 0
,
\theta>1
and
K>0
, define the probability measure
\mu
having a density
d\mu(x)=\exp(-\|x\|^{2}/2+a(x)+c_{a})dx
with respect to the
d
-dimensional Lebesgue measure (with
c_{a}\in\mathbb{R}
a normalizing constant). Then, for all
k\in\{1,...,\lfloor\beta\rfloor+1\}
, and
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
with
\|f\|_{L_{\mu}^{2}}=1
we have

\displaystyle\int|f(x)|^{2}|\log(|f(x)|)|^{k}d\mu\leq C\sum_{|\alpha|=0}^{k}\|D^{\alpha}f\|^{2}_{L_{\mu}^{2}}.

Proof.

From Theorem
4
with
\phi(x)=\frac{\|x\|^{2}}{2}
, we have for all
g:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
with
\|g\|_{L_{2}(\gamma_{d})}=1

\displaystyle\int|g(x)|^{2}|\log(|g(x)|)|^{k}d\gamma_{d}(x)\leq C\sum_{|\alpha|=0}^{k}\|D^{\alpha}g\|^{2}_{L^{2}_{\gamma_{d}}}.

Then, from Theorem
2
, there exists a map
T\in C^{\lfloor\beta\rfloor+1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R}^{d})
with
\nabla T\in\mathcal{H}^{\beta}_{C}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R}^{d})
such that

\displaystyle\int|f(x)|^{2}|\log(|f(x)|)|^{k}d\mu(x)=

\displaystyle\int|f\circ T(x)|^{2}|\log(|f\circ T(x)|)|^{k}d\gamma_{d}(x)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\sum_{|\alpha|=0}^{k}\|D^{\alpha}(f\circ T)\|^{2}_{L^{2}_{\gamma_{d}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle C\sum_{|\alpha|=0}^{k}\|D^{\alpha}f\|^{2}_{L_{\mu}^{2}}.

∎

The Generalized log-Sobolev inequality established by
Rosen (1976)
finds many applications in the theory of semigroups and Hypercontractivity
(
Simon, 1982
;
Davies and Simon, 1984
;
Fabes et al., 1993
)
.

4.2
Optimal density estimator on non-compact domain via GANs

Suppose that we observe an i.i.d. sample
X_{1},...X_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
from a probability measure having a density
p^{\star}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
. The problem of
estimating the density from the data was extensively studied through various methods such as kernel
(
McDonald, 2017
)
,

k
-nearest neighbors
(
Dasgupta and Kpotufe, 2014
)
and wavelets

(
Donoho et al., 1996
)
.

With increasing data dimensionality, recent years have seen the rise of Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs)
(
Goodfellow et al., 2014
)
, which enable both estimation and sampling from the target density. GANs have achieved remarkable results in fields such as image generation
(
Karras et al., 2021
)
, video synthesis
(
Vondrick et al., 2016
)
, and text generation
(
Yu et al., 2017
)
. A GAN comprises two main components: a class of generator functions,
\mathcal{G}
, and a class of discriminators,
\mathcal{D}
. Given an easily sampled distribution
\nu
defined on a latent space
\mathcal{Z}
, the generator
g\in\mathcal{G}
, where
g:\mathcal{Z}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{p}
, aims to approximate the target density
p^{\star}
by minimizing an Integral Probability Metric (IPM)
(
Müller, 1997
)
over
\mathcal{G}
:

d_{\mathcal{D}}(p^{\star},g_{\#}\nu):=\sup_{D\in\mathcal{D}}\left(\mathbb{E}_{p^{\star}}[D(X)]-\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[D(g(Z))]\right).

(18)

The goal of each discriminator
D\in\mathcal{D}
, where
D:\mathbb{R}^{p}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, is to distinguish between the true distribution
p^{\star}
and the generated distribution
g_{\#}\nu
. The efficiency of the estimator is often assessed using Hölder IPMs, defined as

d_{\mathcal{H}_{1}^{\gamma}}(p^{\star},g_{\#}\nu):=\sup_{D\in\mathcal{H}_{1}^{\gamma}}\left(\mathbb{E}_{p^{\star}}[D(X)]-\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[D(g(Z))]\right),

which, when
\gamma=1
and the measures are supported within a prescribed ball, is equivalent to the Wasserstein distance.

The GAN methods has been proven to attain theoretical optimal rates of convergence in various settings
(
Belomestny et al., 2023
;
Liang, 2021
;
Stéphanovitch et al., 2024
)
. However none of these settings treat the case where the latent space
(\mathcal{Z},\nu)
is Gaussian, whereas it is intensively used in practice
(
Goodfellow et al., 2014
;
Biau et al., 2020
;
Wang et al., 2017
)
. The existence of smooth transport map given by Theorem
2
finally allows to prove that the GAN attains optimal rates in the Gaussian setting.

4.2.1
Assumptions on the target density

We suppose that we observe an i.i.d. sample
X_{1},...X_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
from a probability measure having a density
p^{\star}
satisfying the following assumption.

Assumption 2
.

The target density
p^{\star}
belongs to

\mathcal{F}_{\beta}:=\left\{p:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\ \Big|\ p(x)=\gamma_{d}(\Sigma^{-1/2}x)\exp\left(a(x)\right),\Sigma\in\mathbb{M},\int p=1,a\in\mathcal{H}^{\beta,2}_{K}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R})\right\},

with
\mathbb{M}
the set of symmetric matrices
M
satisfying
K^{-1}\text{Id}\preceq M\preceq K\text{Id}
.

This setting is a natural extension to unbounded domains of the classical compact setting. In the classical density estimation framework (see Section 2 of
Tsybakov (2008)
), data are assumed to be sampled from a density
p\in\mathcal{H}^{\beta}_{K}(\mathbb{K},\mathbb{R})
with
\mathbb{K}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
compact and
p
bounded above and below by
K
and
K^{-1}
, respectively. In this context, the density can be represented as

f(x)=\lambda^{d}_{|\mathbb{K}}(x)\exp(a(x)),

with
\lambda^{d}_{|\mathbb{K}}
the
d
-dimensional Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{K}
and
a\in\mathcal{H}^{\beta}_{K}(\mathbb{K},\mathbb{R})
. A natural extension to the non-compact case is to assume that
p
takes the form

p(x):=\exp\left(-\frac{\|\Sigma^{-1/2}x\|^{2}}{2}+a(x)\right),

with
a\in\mathcal{H}^{\beta}_{K}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R})
and
\Sigma\in\mathbb{M}
. In this unbounded setting, the Gaussian distributions replaces the Lebesgue measure
\lambda^{d}_{|\mathbb{K}}
used in the compact case. Note that the additional weak regularity
\mathcal{H}^{0,2}_{K}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R})
supposed in Assumption
2
only impacts the rate of estimation up to a
\log(n)
factor.

4.2.2
The GAN estimator

Let us properly define the GAN estimator. For a compact class of generators
\mathcal{G}\subset L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R}^{d})
and a class of discriminators
\mathcal{D}\subset L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R})
, the GAN estimator is defined as

\hat{g}\in\argmin_{g\in\mathcal{G}}\ d_{\mathcal{D}}(p^{\star}_{n},g_{\#}\nu),

(19)

with
d_{\mathcal{D}}
defined in (
18
),
p^{\star}_{n}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{X_{i}}
the empirical measure from the data and
\nu:=\gamma_{d}
the
d
-dimensional Gaussian measure.

Let
L>0
be the constant given by Corollary
1
such that the transport map
T
from the
d
-dimensional Gaussian measure to any probability measure within
\mathcal{F}_{\beta}
satisfies
\nabla T^{-1}\in\mathcal{H}^{\beta}_{L}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Fix
n\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}
the number of data and take as the generator class

\mathcal{G}:=\left\{g\in\mathcal{H}^{\beta+1}_{C}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R}^{d})\Big|\ \inf_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\min_{r\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\|\nabla g(x)r\|\geq L^{-1}\right\}

(20)

and as the discriminator class

\mathcal{D}:=\mathcal{H}^{d/2}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R}).

(21)

Using these classes of functions, we obtain in the following theorem that the GAN estimator attains optimal rates of convergence (writing
g(n)=\tilde{O}(f(n))
for
g(n)\leq C\log(n)^{C_{2}}f(n)
).

Theorem 5
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}
,
\beta>0
,
p^{\star}\in\mathcal{F}_{\beta}
and
(X_{1},...,X_{n})
an i.i.d. sample of law
p^{\star}
. The GAN estimator
\hat{g}
(
19
) using the classes of functions
\mathcal{G}
(

\mathcal{D}
(

), satisfies for all
\gamma>0

\displaystyle\sup_{p^{\star}\in\mathcal{F}_{\beta}}\ \mathbb{E}_{X_{i}}[d_{\mathcal{H}^{\gamma}_{1}}(\hat{g}_{\#}\gamma_{d},p^{\star})]=\tilde{O}\left(\inf_{\hat{\theta}\in\Theta}\ \sup_{p^{\star}\in\mathcal{F}_{\beta}}\ \mathbb{E}_{X_{i}}[d_{\mathcal{H}^{\gamma}_{1}}(\hat{\theta},p^{\star})]\right),

where
\Theta
denotes the set of all possible estimators of
p^{\star}
based on n sample.

Proof.

Let us take
p^{\star}\in\mathcal{F}_{\beta}
and
T:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
the transport map given by Corollary

such that
T_{\#}\gamma_{d}=p^{\star}
. Let us first bound the expected error
\mathbb{E}_{X_{i}\sim p^{\star}}[d_{\mathcal{H}^{d/2}_{1}}(\hat{g}_{\#}\gamma_{d},p^{\star})]
of our estimator. To this end, write
\hat{\mu}:=\hat{g}_{\#}\gamma_{d}
and define for all
\mu\in\mathcal{F}_{\beta}

h_{\mu}\in\argmax_{h\in\mathcal{H}^{d/2}_{1}}\int h(x)d\mu(x)-\int h(x)dp^{\star}(x),

an optimal potential between
\mu
and
p^{\star}
and define

h_{\mu}^{n}\in\argmax_{h\in\mathcal{H}^{d/2}_{1}}\int h(x)d\mu(x)-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}h(X_{i}),

an optimal potential between
\mu
and
p^{\star}_{n}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{X_{i}}
. We have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[d_{\mathcal{H}^{d/2}_{1}}(\hat{\mu},p^{\star})]=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\int h_{\hat{\mu}}(x)d\hat{\mu}(x)-\int h_{\hat{\mu}}(x)dp^{\star}(x)]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\int h_{\hat{\mu}}(x)d\hat{\mu}(x)-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}h_{\hat{\mu}}(X_{i})+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}h_{\hat{\mu}}(X_{i})-\int h_{\hat{\mu}}(x)dp^{\star}(x)]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\int h_{\hat{\mu}}^{n}(x)d\hat{\mu}(x)-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}h_{\hat{\mu}}^{n}(X_{i})]+\mathbb{E}[d_{\mathcal{H}^{d/2}_{1}}(p^{\star}_{n},p^{\star})].

Furthermore, by definition of
\hat{\mu}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\int h_{\hat{\mu}}^{n}(x)d\hat{\mu}(x)-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}h_{\hat{\mu}}^{n}(X_{i})]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}[\int h_{T_{\#}\gamma_{d}}^{n}(x)dp^{\star}(x)-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}h_{T_{\#}\gamma_{d}}^{n}(X_{i})]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[d_{\mathcal{H}^{d/2}_{1}}(p^{\star}_{n},p^{\star})].

Then, using Lemma 13 from
Tang and Yang (2023)

we have that

\mathbb{E}[d_{\mathcal{H}^{d/2}_{1}}(p^{\star}_{n},p^{\star})]\leq C\log(n)^{C_{2}}n^{-1/2},

which gives us using the previous derivations

\mathbb{E}[d_{\mathcal{H}^{d/2}_{1}}(\hat{\mu},p^{\star})]\leq C\log(n)^{C_{2}}n^{-1/2}.

The density of
\hat{\mu}
is equal to
p_{\hat{\mu}}(x)=\gamma_{d}(\hat{g}^{-1}(x))|\text{det}(\nabla\hat{g}^{-1}(x))|
so as

p_{\hat{\mu}}\in\mathcal{H}^{\beta}_{C}
. Then, as

belongs to
\mathcal{H}^{\beta}_{C}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

we have that for all

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[d_{\mathcal{H}^{\gamma}_{1}}(\hat{\mu},p^{\star})]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}[\sup_{h\in\mathcal{H}^{\gamma}_{1}}\int_{B^{d}(0,\log(n))}h(x)d\hat{\mu}(x)-\int_{B^{d}(0,\log(n))}h(x)dp^{\star}(x)]+C/n

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}[\sup_{h\in\mathcal{H}^{d/2}_{1}}\left(\int_{B^{d}(0,\log(n))}h(x)d\hat{\mu}(x)-\int_{B^{d}(0,\log(n))}h(x)dp^{\star}(x)\right)^{\frac{\beta+\gamma}{\beta+d/2}}]+C/n

\displaystyle\leq C\log(n)^{C_{2}}\left(\mathbb{E}[d_{\mathcal{H}^{d/2}_{1}}(\hat{\mu},p^{\star})^{\frac{\beta+\gamma}{\beta+d/2}}]+1/n\right).

Using Jensen’s inequality, we finally get for all

\mathbb{E}[d_{\mathcal{H}^{\gamma}_{1}}(\hat{\mu},p^{\star})]\leq C\log(n)^{C_{2}}(n^{-\frac{\beta+\gamma}{2\beta+d}}\vee n^{-1/2})

and this rate has been proven to be optimal (up to the logarithmic factor) in
Belomestny et al. (2019)
.
∎

Note that the estimator (

that can be written as
T_{\#}\gamma_{d}
with

a diffeomorphism satisfying

. In the GAN setting, the existence of a
(\beta+1)
-Hölder transport map is crucial for achieving optimality, as it enables restricting the generator class

to a smooth class of function, thereby avoiding overfitting effects and enhancing generalization.

4.3
Score-based Diffusion Models

Recently, a new class of generative models known as score-based diffusion models has demonstrated state-of-the-art performance across a wide range of domains
(
Song et al., 2020
;
Ho et al., 2020
;
De Bortoli, 2022
)
. The underlying method operates as follows.

Consider the
Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process initialized at a certain density
\mu_{0}
:

dY_{t}=-Y_{t}dt+\sqrt{2}dB_{t},\ \ Y_{0}\sim\mu_{0}.

Then, taking
\tau>0
, this process can be reverted with the following SDE:

dZ_{t}=-Z_{t}dt+2\nabla\log Q_{e^{-(\tau-t)}}r(Z_{t})dt+\sqrt{2}dB_{t},\ \ Z_{0}\sim\text{law}(Y_{\tau}),

(22)

for

\displaystyle r(x):=\frac{\mu_{0}(x)}{\gamma_{d}(x)}\quad\text{ and }\quad Q_{s}r(x)=\int r(sx+\sqrt{1-s^{2}}z)d\gamma_{d}(z).

Under minimal conditions, the final time
Z_{\tau}
can be shown to satisfy
Z_{\tau}\overset{d}{=}\mu_{0}

(
Anderson, 1982
)
. Therefore, one can simulate the process
(Z_{t})_{t}
until
time
\tau
to sample from
\mu_{0}
. Achieving this requires an accurate approximation of the so-called score function

s(t,x):=\nabla\log\mu_{t}(x)=-x+\nabla\log Q_{e^{-t}}r(x),

for
\mu_{t}
the law of
Y_{t}
.
Equation (
22
) can then be written as

dZ_{t}=Z_{t}dt+2s(\tau-t,Z_{t})dt+\sqrt{2}dB_{t},\ \ Z_{0}\sim\text{law}(X_{\tau}).

The approximation of the score is typically carried out through a regression objective:

\inf_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\int_{0}^{\tau}\|f(t,\cdot)-s(t,\cdot)\|^{2}_{L^{2}_{\mu_{t}}}dt,

where
\mathcal{F}
is generally chosen to be a class of functions parameterized by a neural network. Theoretical studies often rely on controlling the Lipschitz constant of
s(t,\cdot)

(
Chen et al., 2022
;
Chen et al., 2023
;
Chen et al., 2024
;
Kwon et al., 2022
)
, as these assumptions are crucial for managing discretization errors in numerical schemes used to simulate the process. Theorems
2
and
3
extend the applicability of these assumptions by encompassing a broader range of distributions
\mu_{0}
for which these assumptions can be justified. In fact, by synthesizing Propositions
5
and
10
, we obtain the following result.

Corollary 3
.

Let
p:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a probability density function of the form

p(x)=\exp\left(-\psi(x)+a(x)\right),

such that
a\in\mathcal{H}^{\beta}_{K}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R})
with
\beta\in(0,1]
and
K>0
. If either
\psi(x)=-\|x\|^{2}/2
or it verifies Assumption
1
, then for all
t>0
we have

\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\lambda_{\max}\big(\nabla s(t,x)+\text{Id}\big)\leq Ce^{-t}(1+t^{\beta/2-1}).

This result provides a
L^{1}
control over the largest eigenvalue of the
Hessian
\nabla^{2}\log\mu_{t}(x)
, under low regularity assumptions on the target distribution. Furthermore, Theorem
2
establishes that the higher regularity of the target distribution transfers to the score function.

Corollary 4
.

Let
p:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be a probability density function of the form

p(x)=\exp\left(-\frac{\|x\|^{2}}{2}+a(x)\right),

such that
a\in H^{\beta}_{K}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R})
with
\beta\geq 1
and
K>0
. Then, for all
t\in[0,1)
we have

\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor\beta\rfloor}\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\|\nabla^{k}s(t,x)\|\leq Ce^{-t}(1+t^{-1/2})\quad\text{and}\quad\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\|\nabla^{\lfloor\beta\rfloor+1}\log s(t,x)\|\leq Ce^{-t}(1+t^{(\beta-\lfloor\beta\rfloor)/2-1}).

The estimation of the score function being the core step of the methods, its additional regularity ensures enhanced stability and accuracy in numerical approximations, ultimately improving the quality of generated samples in score-based diffusion models.

Future directions

In this work, we have established the high order regularity of the Langevin map transporting the Gaussian measure to a deformation. Not only this result advances our understanding of the properties of diffusion processes but also paves the way for further investigations into the high order regularity for other diffusion flows and other classes of transported measures.

Moreover, an intriguing direction for future research would be to estimate the value of the constant
C>0
given by Theorem
2
and its dependency with respect to
K,\beta
and
d
. This work being a first approach to study high order regularity, it does not focus on the size of this constant. However, we believe that it could be proven to be dimension free. Furthermore, having a direct dependency with respect to
K
and
\beta
would allow to extend the second-order Poincaré inequality
Chatterjee (2009)
which finds applications in statistic of eigenvalues
(
Bai and Silverstein, 2010
)
. Such explorations could reveal deeper connections between regularity, transport phenomena, and functional inequalities, thereby enriching the field of measure transportation and its applications.

A.1.2

The bound on the first derivatives differs from the one of higher order as it only needs an estimate on

Definition 1

For strictly log concave probability measures, we will use the well known Brascamp-Lieb inequality.

Proposition 9

[

]
Let a probability density function

convex. Then, for any derivable function

\int\left(S(y)-\int S(z)dq(z)\right)^{2}dq(y)\leq\int\big(\nabla^{2}\phi(y)\big)^{-1}(\nabla S(y),\nabla S(y))dq(y).

Having this tool in hand, let us provide a bound on the growth of

For all

We have

\displaystyle\lambda_{\max}(\nabla V(t,x))=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{t}\lambda_{\max}((\nabla^{2}\log(Q_{t}r)(x))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lambda_{\max}\left(\frac{t}{(1-t^{2})^{2}}\int H(y,x)^{\otimes 2}dp^{t,x}(y)-\frac{t}{1-t^{2}}\text{Id}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lambda_{\max}\left(\frac{t}{(1-t^{2})^{2}}\int H(y,x)^{\otimes 2}dp^{t,x}(y)\right)-\frac{t}{1-t^{2}}.

(23)

\alpha\in(0,1)
using the Brascamp-Lieb and Jensen’s inequalities we obtain

\displaystyle\int\|y-\int zd\nu^{t,x}(z)\|^{2+\alpha}d\nu^{t,x}(y)\leq\left(\int\|y-\int zd\nu^{t,x}(z)\|^{4}d\nu^{t,x}(y)\right)^{(2+\alpha)/4}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\int\|y-\int zd\nu^{t,x}(z)\|^{4}d\nu^{t,x}(y)-\left(\int\|y-\int zd\nu^{t,x}(z)\|^{2}d\nu^{t,x}(y)\right)^{2}\right)^{(2+\alpha)/4}

\displaystyle+\left(\int\|y-\int zd\nu^{t,x}(z)\|^{2}d\nu^{t,x}(y)\right)^{(2+\alpha)/2}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\int\left(\|y-\int zd\nu^{t,x}(z)\|^{2}-\int\|w-\int zd\nu^{t,x}(z)\|^{2}d\nu^{t,x}(w)\right)^{2}d\nu^{t,x}(y)\right)^{(2+\alpha)/4}+C\left(\frac{1-t^{2}}{t^{2}}\right)^{(2+\alpha)/2}

\displaystyle\leq\left(C\frac{1-t^{2}}{t^{2}}\int\|y-\int zd\nu^{t,x}(z)\|^{2}d\nu^{t,x}(y)\right)^{(2+\alpha)/4}+C\left(\frac{1-t^{2}}{t^{2}}\right)^{(2+\alpha)/2}

\displaystyle\leq\left(C\left(\frac{1-t^{2}}{t^{2}}\right)^{2}\right)^{(2+\alpha)/4}+C\left(\frac{1-t^{2}}{t^{2}}\right)^{(2+\alpha)/2}

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\frac{1-t^{2}}{t^{2}}\right)^{(2+\alpha)/2}.

Then, using Jensen’s inequality and putting everything together in (
27
), we obtain

\displaystyle\|\int\left(y-\int zdp^{t,x}(z)\right)^{\otimes 2}dp^{t,x}(y)-\int\left(y-\int zd\nu^{t,x}(z)\right)^{\otimes 2}d\nu^{t,x}(y)\|\leq C\left(\frac{1-t^{2}}{t^{2}}\right)^{(2+\beta-\lfloor\beta\rfloor)/2}.

Therefore, we finally have for
t\geq 1/2
that

\lambda_{\max}(\nabla V(t,x))\leq\frac{1}{t(1-t^{2})^{1-(\beta-\lfloor\beta\rfloor)/2}}.

As for
t\leq 1/2
, we have

\lambda_{\max}(\nabla V(t,x))\leq C,

we deduce that

\int_{0}^{1}\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\lambda_{\max}(\nabla V(t,x))dt\leq C.

∎

Using Proposition
10
, we finally obtain that there exists a Lipschitz transport map from the
d
-dimensional Gaussian distribution to the probability
p
.

Corollary 5
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
3
, there exists a unique solution for all
t\in[0,1)
to the equation

\partial_{t}X_{t}(x)=V(t,X_{t}(x)),\quad X_{0}(x)=x,

and the solution satisfies
X_{t}\in\mathcal{H}^{1}_{C}
.
Furthermore, there exists a limit map
T
such that
T_{\#}\gamma_{d}=p
and

T\in\mathcal{H}^{1}_{C}.

Proof.

From Lemma
5
we have the existence of the solution and from Proposition
10
and Lemma
2
, we obtain its Lipschitz regularity. We can then conclude to the existence of the Lipschitz transport map using Lemma
1
.
∎

A.2
Higher order estimates

A.2.1
Localisation of
X_{t}(x)

Let us fix
\epsilon\in(0,1/2)
, in this section we control the norm of
X_{t}(x)
for
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
t\in[0,1)
that satisfies

\|x\|^{2}\leq\log(\epsilon^{-1})\ \ \text{ and }\ \ 1-t^{2}\leq C_{\star}^{-1}\log(\epsilon^{-1})^{-C_{\star}},

(28)

for
C_{\star}
large enough to be determined later. This estimate is needed to control the norm of the derivatives of
p
at the point
X_{t}(x)
.

Proposition 11
.

There exists
C^{\star}>0
such that for
t\in[0,1)
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
satisfying

1-t^{2}\leq C_{\star}^{-1}\log(\epsilon^{-1})^{-C_{\star}}\text{ and }\|x\|^{2}\leq\log(\epsilon^{-1}),

we have

\displaystyle\|tX_{t}(x)\|^{2}\leq 1-C_{2}^{-1}\log(\epsilon^{-1}+1)^{-K}.

Proof.

Let us first recall from (
5
) that we have

(X_{t})_{\#}\gamma_{d}\overset{d}{=}tX+\sqrt{1-t^{2}}Y,

(29)

with
X\sim p
and
Y\sim\gamma_{d}
. Writing
f_{X_{t}}
the density of
(X_{t})_{\#}\gamma_{d}
, we have

\displaystyle f_{X_{t}}(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{t^{d}(1-t^{2})^{d/2}}\int p(\frac{y}{t})\gamma_{d}(\frac{z-y}{(1-t^{2})^{1/2}})d\lambda^{d}(y)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(1-t^{2})^{d/2}}\int\frac{p(y)}{\gamma_{d}(y)}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2(1-t^{2})}\big(\|z-ty\|^{2}+(1-t^{2})\|y\|^{2}\big)\right)d\lambda^{d}(y)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(1-t^{2})^{d/2}}\int r(y)\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2(1-t^{2})}\big(\|z\|^{2}-2t\langle z,y\rangle+\|y\|^{2}\big)\right)d\lambda^{d}(y)

\displaystyle=\exp(-\frac{\|z\|^{2}}{2})\int r(y)\varphi^{t,z}(y)d\lambda^{d}(y).

(30)

As for all
t\in[0,1)
we have that
x\mapsto X_{t}(x)
is a diffeomorphism, we deduce that we also have

\displaystyle f_{X_{t}}(z)

\displaystyle=\gamma_{d}(X_{t}^{-1}(z))\text{det}(\nabla X_{t}(X_{t}^{-1}(z))),

so

\displaystyle f_{X_{t}}(X_{t}(x))

\displaystyle=\gamma_{d}(x)\text{det}(\nabla X_{t}(x))^{-1}.

Therefore, from (
30
) we deduce that

\displaystyle\int r(y)\varphi^{t,X_{t}(x)}(y)d\lambda^{d}(y)

\displaystyle=\exp(\frac{\|X_{t}(x)\|^{2}}{2})\gamma_{d}(x)|\text{det}(\nabla X_{t}(x))|^{-1}

\displaystyle\geq C^{-1}\exp(\frac{1}{2}(\|X_{t}(x)\|^{2}-\|x\|^{2})),

(31)

as
X_{t}
is Lipschitz for all
t\in[0,1)
from Corollary
5
. Now, we have

\displaystyle\int

\displaystyle r(y)\varphi^{t,X_{t}(x)}(y)d\lambda^{d}(y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int r(y)\varphi^{t,X_{t}(x)}(y)\mathds{1}_{\{\|y-tX_{t}(x)\|\leq(1-t^{2})^{\frac{1}{4}}\}}d\lambda^{d}(y)+\int r(y)\varphi^{t,X_{t}(x)}(y)\mathds{1}_{\{\|y-tX_{t}(x)\|>(1-t^{2})^{\frac{1}{4}}\}}d\lambda^{d}(y)

\displaystyle\leq C\|\exp\left(-u(\|\cdot\|^{2})\right)\|_{\mathcal{H}^{0}(B^{d}(tX_{t}(x),(1-t^{2})^{\frac{1}{4}}))}\mathds{1}_{\|tX_{t}(x)\|<1+(1-t^{2})^{\frac{1}{4}}}+C\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2(1-t^{2})^{\frac{1}{2}}}\right).

(32)

Suppose that we have

\|\exp\left(-u(\|\cdot\|^{2})\right)\|_{\mathcal{H}^{0}(B^{d}(tX_{t}(x),(1-t^{2})^{\frac{1}{4}}))}\mathds{1}_{\|tX_{t}(x)\|<1+(1-t^{2})^{\frac{1}{4}}}\leq\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2(1-t^{2})^{\frac{1}{2}}}\right),

then from (
31
) we have

\displaystyle C\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2(1-t^{2})^{\frac{1}{2}}}\right)

\displaystyle\geq C^{-1}\exp(\frac{1}{2}(\|X_{t}(x)\|^{2}-\|x\|^{2})),

so

\displaystyle\log(C)-\frac{1}{(1-t^{2})^{\frac{1}{2}}}+\|x\|^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\|X_{t}(x)\|^{2}.

This is not possible for
x
and
t
satisfying (
28
) with
C^{\star}>0
large enough as

\displaystyle\log(C)-\frac{1}{(1-t^{2})^{\frac{1}{2}}}+\|x\|^{2}\leq\log(C)-(C^{\star})^{1/2}\log(\epsilon^{-1})^{C^{\star}/2}+\log(\epsilon^{-1})<0.

This gives us that

\|\exp\left(-u(\|\cdot\|^{2})\right)\|_{\mathcal{H}^{0}(B^{d}(tX_{t}(x),(1-t^{2})^{\frac{1}{4}}))}\mathds{1}_{\|tX_{t}(x)\|<1+(1-t^{2})^{\frac{1}{4}}}>\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2(1-t^{2})^{\frac{1}{2}}}\right),

so from (
32
) and (
31
) we obtain

C\|\exp\left(-u(\|\cdot\|^{2})\right)\|_{\mathcal{H}^{0}(B^{d}(tX_{t}(x),(1-t^{2})^{\frac{1}{4}}))}\mathds{1}_{\|tX_{t}(x)\|<1+(1-t^{2})^{\frac{1}{4}}}\geq C^{-1}\exp(\frac{1}{2}(\|X_{t}(x)\|^{2}-\|x\|^{2})),

which gives

\displaystyle\log(C)-2\min_{y\in B^{d}(tX_{t}(x),(1-t^{2})^{\frac{1}{4}})}u(\|y\|^{2})+\|x\|^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\|X_{t}(x)\|^{2}\geq 0.

Then, for
x
and
t
satisfying (
28
), we get

2\min_{y\in B^{d}(tX_{t}(x),(1-t^{2})^{\frac{1}{4}})}u(\|y\|^{2})\leq\log(C)+\log(\epsilon^{-1}),

so as
u(s)\geq(1-s)^{-\frac{1}{K}}
, we have

\displaystyle(1-(\|tX_{t}(x)\|-(1-t^{2})^{\frac{1}{4}}))^{2})^{-1/K}

\displaystyle\leq C\log(\epsilon^{-1}+1).

Finally, as
\|tX_{t}(x)\|<1+(1-t^{2})^{\frac{1}{4}}
, we get for
C^{\star}>0
large enough

\displaystyle\|tX_{t}(x)\|^{2}\leq 1-C^{-1}\log(\epsilon^{-1}+1)^{-K}.

(33)

∎

The lower bound (
33
) is a key result stating that
X_{t}(x)
is not too close to the boundary of the ball which gives us in particular using Assumption
1
that

\|f^{l}(X_{t}(x))\|=\|\nabla^{l}r(X_{t}(x))/r(X_{t}(x))\|\leq C\log(\epsilon^{-1})^{C_{2}}.

A.2.2
Concentration of the mass under
p^{t,x}

In this section we derive a bound on the quantity
\int\|f(y)-f(tx)\|^{2}dp^{t,x}(y)
with respect to
t\in[1-C_{\star}^{-1}\log(\epsilon^{-1})^{-C_{\star}},1]
and the growth of the function
f
around the point
tx
. Let us first prove a concentration result on
p^{t,x}
, analogous to Proposition
3
in the case of Theorem
3
.

Proposition 12
.

There exists
0<C_{\star}<C_{2}
such that if
t\in[0,1)
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
satisfy

1-t^{2}\leq C_{\star}^{-1}\log(\epsilon^{-1})^{-C_{\star}}\text{ and }\|tx\|^{2}\leq 1-C_{2}^{-1}\log(\epsilon^{-1}+1)^{-K},

(34)

then for all
i,j\in\{1,...,d\}
we have

\int H(y,x)_{i}^{2}dp^{t,x}(y)\leq C(1-t^{2})

and

\int\left(H(y,x)^{\otimes 2}_{ij}-\int H(z,x)^{\otimes 2}_{ij}dp^{t,x}(z)\right)^{2}dp^{t,x}(y)\leq C(1-t^{2})^{2}.

Proof.

Let
Y,\overline{Y}
two independent random variables of law
p^{t,x}
and
Z,\overline{Z}
two independent random variables of law
\varphi^{t,x}
. We have

\displaystyle\int H(y,x)_{i}^{2}dp^{t,x}(y)=

\displaystyle\int\left(y_{i}-\int y_{i}dp^{t,x}(z)\right)^{2}dp^{t,x}(y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\text{Var}_{p^{t,x}}[Y_{i}]=\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{p^{t,x}}[(Y_{i}-\overline{Y}_{i})^{2}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{p^{t,x}}[(Y_{i}-\overline{Y}_{i})^{2}\mathds{1}_{\{\|Y-tx\|\leq\delta\}\cap\{\|\overline{Y}-tx\|\leq\delta\}}]+\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{p^{t,x}}[(Y_{i}-\overline{Y}_{i})^{2}\mathds{1}_{\{\|Y-tx\|\geq\delta\}\cup\{\|\overline{Y}-tx\|\geq\delta\}}],

with
\delta=(1-t^{2})^{1/4}
. In order to control the first term, we are going to bound
Q_{t}r(x)^{-1}
, for
x
and
t
satisfying

1-t^{2}\leq C_{\star}^{-1}\log(\epsilon^{-1})^{-C_{\star}}\text{ and }\|tx\|^{2}\leq 1-C_{2}^{-1}\log(\epsilon^{-1}+1)^{-K},

(35)

with
C^{\star},C_{2}>0
given by Proposition
11
.
We obtain

\displaystyle Q_{t}r(x)

\displaystyle=\int\varphi^{t,x}(y)r(y)d\lambda^{d}(y)

\displaystyle\geq\min_{w\in B^{d}(tx,(1-t^{2})^{1/2}))}\exp\left(-u(\|w\|^{2})+a(w)\right)\int\varphi^{t,x}(y)\mathds{1}_{\|y-tx\|\leq(1-t^{2})^{1/2}}d\lambda^{d}(y)

\displaystyle\geq C^{-1}\min_{w\in B^{d}(tx,(1-t^{2})^{1/2}))}\exp\left(-u(\|w\|^{2})\right)\int\mathds{1}_{\|y\|\leq\frac{(1-t^{2})^{1/2}}{(1-t^{2})^{1/2}}}\gamma_{d}(y)d\lambda^{d}(y),

\displaystyle\geq C^{-1}\min_{w\in B^{d}(tx,(1-t^{2})^{1/2}))}\exp\left(-u(\|w\|^{2})\right).

(36)

On the other hand, for all
y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
we have

r(y)=\exp(-u(\|y\|^{2}+a(y))\leq C\exp(-u(\|y\|^{2})),

and for
t
and
x
satisfying (
35
), we have

\max_{z\in B^{d}(tx,\delta)}u^{(1)}(\|z\|^{2})\leq K(1-(\|tx\|+\delta)^{2})^{-(K+1)}\leq C\log(\epsilon^{-1})^{K(K+1)}\leq(1-t^{2})^{-1/4},

taking
C^{\star}>0
large enough.
We deduce for the first term that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{p^{t,x}}

\displaystyle[(Y_{i}-\overline{Y}_{i})^{2}\mathds{1}_{\{\|Y-tx\|\leq\delta\}\cap\{\|\overline{Y}-tx\|\leq\delta\}}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\int(y_{i}-\overline{y}_{i})^{2}\frac{r(y)r(\overline{y})}{Q_{t}r(x)^{2}}\varphi^{t,x}(y)\varphi^{t,x}(\overline{y})\mathds{1}_{\{\|y-tx\|\leq\delta\}\cap\{\|\overline{y}-tx\|\leq\delta\}}d\lambda^{d}(y)d\lambda^{d}(\overline{y})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\int\int(y_{i}-\overline{y}_{i})^{2}\exp\left(2\max_{z\in B^{d}(tx,(1-t^{2})^{1/2})}u(\|z\|^{2})-2\min_{w\in B^{d}(tx,\delta)}u(\|w\|^{2})\right)\varphi^{t,x}(y)\varphi^{t,x}(\overline{y})d\lambda^{d}(y)d\lambda^{d}(\overline{y})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\int\int(y_{i}-\overline{y}_{i})^{2}\exp\left(2\max_{z\in B^{d}(tx,\delta)}u^{(1)}(\|z\|^{2})\delta\right)\varphi^{t,x}(y)\varphi^{t,x}(\overline{y})d\lambda^{d}(y)d\lambda^{d}(\overline{y})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\int\int(y_{i}-\overline{y}_{i})^{2}\varphi^{t,x}(y)\varphi^{t,x}(\overline{y})d\lambda^{d}(y)d\lambda^{d}(\overline{y})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\mathbb{E}_{\varphi^{t,x}}[(Z_{i}-\overline{Z}_{i})^{2}]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\text{Var}_{\varphi^{t,x}}(Z_{i})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C(1-t^{2}).

Now, for the second term, as
p^{t,x}
is supported on the ball we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{p^{t,x}}[(Y_{i}-\overline{Y}_{i})^{2}\mathds{1}_{\{\|Y-tx\|\geq\delta\}\cup\{\|\overline{y}-tx\|\geq\delta\}}]

\displaystyle\leq C\int\frac{r(y)}{Q_{t}r(x)}\varphi^{t,x}(y)\mathds{1}_{\{\|Y-tx\|\geq\delta\}}d\lambda^{d}(y)

\displaystyle\leq C\frac{\exp(-\frac{\delta^{2}}{2(1-t^{2})})}{Q_{t}r(x)}

\displaystyle\leq C\exp\left(-\frac{(1-t^{2})^{-1/2}}{2}\right)\exp\left(\max_{z\in B^{d}(0,\|tx\|+(1-t^{2})^{1/2})}u^{(1)}(\|z\|^{2})\right)

\displaystyle\leq C\exp\left(-\frac{(1-t^{2})^{-1/2}}{2}+(1-(\|tx\|+(1-t^{2})^{1/2})^{2})^{-(K+1)}\right)

\displaystyle\leq C\exp\left(-\frac{(1-t^{2})^{-1/2}}{4}\right).

Applying the same reasoning, we obtain

\int\left(H(y,x)^{\otimes 2}_{ij}-\int H(z,x)^{\otimes 2}_{ij}dp^{t,x}(z)\right)^{2}dp^{t,x}(y)\leq C(1-t^{2})^{2}.

∎

Using Proposition
12
, we directly obtain the analog of Proposition
4
in the case of Theorem
3
.

Proposition 13
.

There exists
0<C_{\star}<C_{2}
such that if
t\in[0,1)
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
satisfy

1-t^{2}\leq C_{\star}^{-1}\log(\epsilon^{-1})^{-C_{\star}}\text{ and }\|tx\|^{2}\leq 1-C_{2}^{-1}\log(\epsilon^{-1}+1)^{-K},

(37)

then for all
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
i\in\{1,2\}
and
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we have

\displaystyle\|\int f(y)\nabla_{x}^{i}p^{t,x}(y)d\lambda^{d}(y)\|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{Ct^{i}}{(1-t^{2})^{i/2}}\left(\int\|f(y)-\xi\|^{2}dp^{t,x}(y)\right)^{1/2}.

Let us now give a result on the concentration of the mass under
p^{t,x}
.

Proposition 14
.

There exists
0<C_{\star}<C_{2}
such that if
t\in[0,1)
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
satisfy

1-t^{2}\leq C_{\star}^{-1}\log(\epsilon^{-1})^{-C_{\star}}\text{ and }\|tx\|^{2}\leq 1-C_{2}^{-1}\log(\epsilon^{-1}+1)^{-K},

(38)

then for all
l\in\{1,...,\lfloor\beta\rfloor\}
we have

\int\|f^{l}(y)-f^{l}(tx)\|^{2}dp^{t,x}(y)\leq C\log(\epsilon^{-1})^{C_{3}}(1-t^{2})^{\beta-\lfloor\beta\rfloor}\log((1-t^{2})^{-1})^{-2\theta}.

Proof.

Let
C^{\star}>0
be the constant given by Proposition
11
;
taking
\delta=C_{\star}^{-1}\log(\epsilon^{-1})^{-C_{\star}}
, we provide separate bounds on

\displaystyle\int\|f^{l}(y)-f^{l}(tx)\|^{2}\mathds{1}_{\|y\|\leq 1-\delta}dp^{t,x}(y)

and

\displaystyle\int\|f^{l}(y)-f^{l}(tx)\|^{2}\mathds{1}_{\|y\|\geq 1-\delta}dp^{t,x}(y).

For the first term, as for all
l\in\{0,...,\lfloor\beta\rfloor\}
and
y\in B^{d}(0,1-\delta)
, we have
\|u^{(l+1)}(\|y\|^{2})\|\leq C\delta^{-(K+\lfloor\beta\rfloor+1)}
and
\|\nabla^{l}a(y)-\nabla^{l}a(tx)\|\leq K\|y-tx\|^{\beta-\lfloor\beta\rfloor}\log((\|y-tx\|\wedge 1/2)^{-1})^{-\theta}
, we deduce that

\displaystyle\int\|f^{l}(y)-f^{l}(tx)\|^{2}\mathds{1}_{\|y\|\leq 1-\delta}dp^{t,x}(y)

\displaystyle\leq\int\frac{\delta^{-2(K+\lfloor\beta\rfloor+1)}\|y-tx\|^{2(\beta-\lfloor\beta\rfloor)}}{\log((\|y-tx\|\wedge 1/2)^{-1})^{2\theta}}\mathds{1}_{\|y\|\leq 1-\delta}dp^{t,x}(y).

Let
\phi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
be the
C^{1}
diffeomorphism of the polar change of coordinates, we have

\displaystyle\int

\displaystyle\frac{\|y-tx\|^{2(\beta-\lfloor\beta\rfloor)}}{\log((\|y-tx\|\wedge 1/2)^{-1})^{2\theta}}dp^{t,x}(y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\pi}...\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{\rho^{2(\beta-\lfloor\beta\rfloor)}}{\log((\rho\wedge 1/2)^{-1})^{2\theta}}\frac{r(tx+\phi(\rho,\theta_{1},...,\theta_{d-1}))}{Qtr(x)}\frac{e^{-\frac{\rho^{2}}{2(1-t^{2})}}}{(2\pi(1-t^{2}))^{d/2}}|\text{det}(\nabla\phi(\rho,\theta_{1},...,\theta_{d-1}))|d\rho d\theta_{1}...d\theta_{d-1}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{C}{Qtr(x)}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\pi}...\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{\rho^{2(\beta-\lfloor\beta\rfloor)+d-1}}{\log((\rho\wedge 1/2)^{-1})^{2\theta}}r(tx+\phi(\rho,\theta_{1},...,\theta_{d-1}))\frac{e^{-\frac{\rho^{2}}{2(1-t^{2})}}}{(1-t^{2})^{d/2}}d\rho d\theta_{1}...d\theta_{d-1}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{(1-t^{2})^{\beta-\lfloor\beta\rfloor}}{Qtr(x)}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\pi}...\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{\rho^{2(\beta-\lfloor\beta\rfloor)+d-1}}{\log((\rho(1-t^{2})^{1/2}\wedge 1/2)^{-1})^{2\theta}}r(tx+\phi(\rho(1-t^{2})^{1/2},\theta_{1},...,\theta_{d-1}))e^{-\frac{\rho^{2}}{2}}d\rho d\theta_{1}...d\theta_{d-1}.

Let
\gamma=(1-t^{2})^{-1/4}
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\pi}...\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{\rho^{2(\beta-\lfloor\beta\rfloor)+d-1}}{\log((\rho(1-t^{2})^{1/2}\wedge 1/2)^{-1})^{2\theta}}r(tx+\phi(\rho(1-t^{2})^{1/2},\theta_{1},...,\theta_{d-1}))e^{-\frac{\rho^{2}}{2}}\mathds{1}_{\{\rho\leq\gamma\}}d\rho d\theta_{1}...d\theta_{d-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\pi}...\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{\rho^{2(\beta-\lfloor\beta\rfloor)+d-1}}{(\log((\rho)^{-1})+\log((1-t^{2})^{-1/2}))^{2\theta}}r(tx+\phi(\rho(1-t^{2})^{1/2},\theta_{1},...,\theta_{d-1}))e^{-\frac{\rho^{2}}{2}}\mathds{1}_{\{\rho\leq\gamma\}}d\rho d\theta_{1}...d\theta_{d-1}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{C}{\log((1-t^{2})^{-1/2})^{2\theta}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\pi}...\int_{0}^{2\pi}\rho^{2(\beta-\lfloor\beta\rfloor)+d-1}r(tx+\phi(\rho(1-t^{2})^{1/2},\theta_{1},...,\theta_{d-1}))e^{-\frac{\rho^{2}}{2}}\mathds{1}_{\{\rho\leq\gamma\}}d\rho d\theta_{1}...d\theta_{d-1}.

On the other hand,
using (
36
) for
x
and
t
satisfying (
38
) we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{Q_{t}r(x)}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\pi}...\int_{0}^{2\pi}\rho^{2(\beta-\lfloor\beta\rfloor)+d-1}r(tx+\phi(\rho(1-t^{2})^{1/2},\theta_{1},...,\theta_{d-1}))e^{-\frac{\rho^{2}}{2}}\mathds{1}_{\{\rho\leq\gamma\}}d\rho d\theta_{1}...d\theta_{d-1}

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\pi}...\int_{0}^{2\pi}\rho^{2(\beta-\lfloor\beta\rfloor)+d-1}\exp\left(\max_{z\in B^{d}(tx,(1-t^{2})^{1/2})}u(\|z\|^{2})-\min_{w\in B^{d}(tx,\gamma(1-t^{2})^{1/2})}u(\|w\|^{2})\right)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\times e^{-\frac{\rho^{2}}{2}}\mathds{1}_{\{\rho\leq\gamma\}}d\rho d\theta_{1}...d\theta_{d-1}

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\pi}...\int_{0}^{2\pi}\rho^{2(\beta-\lfloor\beta\rfloor)+d-1}\exp\left(\max_{z\in B^{d}(tx,(1-t^{2})^{1/4})}u^{(1)}(\|z\|^{2})C(1-t^{2})^{1/4}\right)e^{-\frac{\rho^{2}}{2}}\mathds{1}_{\{\rho\leq\gamma\}}d\rho d\theta_{1}...d\theta_{d-1}

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\pi}...\int_{0}^{2\pi}\rho^{2(\beta-\lfloor\beta\rfloor)+d-1}e^{-\frac{\rho^{2}}{2}}\mathds{1}_{\{\rho\leq\gamma\}}d\rho d\theta_{1}...d\theta_{d-1}

\displaystyle\leq C,

where we used that
\|tx\|^{2}\leq 1-C_{2}^{-1}\log(\epsilon^{-1}+1)^{-K}
to obtain

\displaystyle\max_{z\in B^{d}(tx,(1-t^{2})^{1/4})}u^{(1)}(\|z\|^{2})

\displaystyle\leq C\Big(1-(\|tx\|+(1-t^{2})^{1/4})^{2}\Big)^{-(K+1)}

B.4.1
C^{\beta+1}
diffeomorphism between deformations of the Gaussian

This section is dedicated to the proof of Corollary
1
.

Proof.

Let us take
p(x):=\exp\left(-\frac{\|x\|^{2}}{2}+a(x)\right)
. We first show that the inverse of the Langevin transport map from the Gaussian to
p
is also
(\beta+1)
-Hölder. Let
(X_{t})_{t\in[0,1]}
be the flow of diffeomorphism (solution to (
9
)) such that
(X_{1})_{\#\gamma_{d}=p}
.
Let
w\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
and
\alpha_{t}=\|\nabla X_{t}(x)w\|^{2}
, we have

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\alpha_{t}

\displaystyle=2\left\langle\nabla X_{t}(x)w,\nabla V(t,X_{t}(x))\nabla X_{t}(x)w\right\rangle

\displaystyle\geq 2\lambda(t)\alpha_{t},

with
\lambda(t)=\min_{u\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\left\langle u,\nabla V(t,X_{t}(x))u\right\rangle
. Then

\displaystyle(\partial_{t}\alpha_{t}+(-2\lambda(t))\alpha_{t})\exp(-\int_{0}^{t}2\lambda(s)ds)\geq 0

so

\displaystyle\partial_{t}(\alpha_{t}\exp(-\int_{0}^{t}2\lambda(s)ds))\geq 0

which gives

\displaystyle\alpha_{t}\exp(-\int_{0}^{t}2\lambda(s)ds)\geq\alpha_{0},

so finally as
\alpha_{0}=1
we have

\displaystyle\alpha_{t}\geq\exp(\int_{0}^{t}2\lambda(s)ds).

Now, from (
64
) we have that in the case
\beta\in(0,1)
,

\displaystyle\|\nabla V(t,x)\|

\displaystyle\leq C(1-t^{2})^{\beta/2-1}\log((1-t^{2})^{-1})^{-\theta},

and in the case
\beta\geq 1
, using Proposition
3
we get

\displaystyle\|\nabla V(t,x)\|

\displaystyle=\frac{t}{1-t^{2}}\|\int\nabla a(y)\left(y-\int zdp^{t,x}(z)\right)dp^{t,x}(y)\|

\displaystyle\leq C\frac{1}{1-t^{2}}\left(\int\|y-\int zdp^{t,x}(z)\|^{2}dp^{t,x}(y)\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq C\frac{1}{(1-t^{2})^{1/2}}.

We then deduce that

\alpha_{t}\geq\exp(-C\int_{0}^{t}\left(\frac{\mathds{1}_{\{\beta\geq 1\}}}{(1-s^{2})^{1/2}}+\frac{\mathds{1}_{\{\beta\in(0,1)\}}}{(1-s^{2})^{1-\beta/2}}\right)ds)\geq C^{-1}.

Therefore, we obtain that the map
T
from Theorem
2
satisfies
\inf_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\min_{w\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\|\nabla T^{-1}(x)w\|\geq C^{-1}
so by the Faa Di Bruno formula we obtain that
\nabla T^{-1}\in\mathcal{H}^{\beta}_{C}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

Let us now take two density probabilities

p(x)=\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\|\Sigma_{p}^{-1/2}x\|^{2}+a(x)\right)\quad\text{ and }\quad q(x)=\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\|\Sigma_{q}^{-1/2}x\|^{2}+b(x)\right).

Then for
p^{{}^{\prime}}=(\Sigma_{p}^{-1/2})_{\#}p
and
q^{{}^{\prime}}=(\Sigma_{q}^{-1/2})_{\#}q
, we have that
p^{{}^{\prime}}
and
q^{{}^{\prime}}
satisfy the assumptions of Theorem
2
so there exist
T_{p},T_{q}
such that
\nabla T_{p},\nabla T_{p}^{-1},\nabla T_{q},\nabla T_{q}^{-1}\in\mathcal{H}^{\beta}_{C}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
(T_{p})_{\#}\gamma_{d}=p^{{}^{\prime}}
and
(T_{q})_{\#}\gamma_{d}=q^{{}^{\prime}}
.

Therefore, the map
T=\Sigma_{q}^{1/2}\circ T_{q}\circ T^{-1}_{p}\circ\Sigma_{p}^{-1/2}
satisfies
\nabla T,\nabla T^{-1}\in\mathcal{H}^{\beta}_{C}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
T_{\#}p=q
. The general case for non-centered Gaussian distributions follows the same way.
∎
