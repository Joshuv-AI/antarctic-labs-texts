Calabi-Yau property in derived Koszul calculus
Roland Berger, Jun Maillard

Abstract
A Poincaré Van den Bergh duality theorem for strong Kc-Calabi-Yau algebras was obtained by R. Taillefer and the first author [ 4 , Theorem 5.11] under the assumption that the derived functors of functors involved in the statement exist. We prove the existence of these derived functors by showing that the dg category defining the derived Koszul calculus is isomorphic to a dg category of dg modules over a dg algebra. Therefore we get a definition of strong Kc-Calabi-Yau algebras and a corresponding duality theorem without any existence assumption. We prove that a polynomial algebra is strong Kc-Calabi-Yau.

Introduction

Derived categories are systematically used as a fundamental framework in several important subjects. An emblematic case is given by the derived algebraic geometry, providing new approaches in algebraic geometry with a wide range of applications
[
19
]
. Cluster algebras and representation theory of quivers constitute another promising subject
[
14
]
, including Ginzburg dg algebras developed from the seminal paper of V. Ginzburg on Calabi-Yau algebras
[
11
]
.

As a common feature of derived categories appearing in various contexts, the basic objects are dg algebras
\mathcal{A}
, dg
\mathcal{A}
-modules and abelian categories
\mathcal{M}
, giving rise to derived categories
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{M})
. In our paper, we follow the recent book on derived categories by A. Yekutieli
[
24
]
. This book offers a self-contained text on the fundamentals of the theory, leading in great detail to a definition of derived categories
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{M})
and to existence theorems for derived functors.

Yelutieli’s book contains an application to dualizing complexes – a classical topic in algebraic geometry – suitably extended to noncommutative dualizing complexes over noncommutative dg rings, following an approach by M. Van den Bergh
[
21
]
developed by A. Yekutieli and J. J. Zhang (see
[
24
]
for references). In this way, derived categories appear as a natural bridge between commutative algebra and noncommutative algebra.

A standard theorem asserts that the algebra of multivector fields on a manifold
M
is isomorphic to the Hochschild cohomology algebra of the algebra
A=C^{\infty}(M)
. The scope of this theorem was widely enlarged by M. Kontsevich in his formality theorem, see e.g.
[
16
]
. Here there is a bridge between differential geometry and homological algebra, which permits to define by analogy a differential calculus on noncommutative associative algebras, called Tamarkin-Tsygan calculus
[
7
,
18
,
15
]
, also named calculus structure
[
8
,
9
]
.

Koszul calculus was introduced by T. Lambre, A. Solotar and the first author in 2018
[
1
]
in order to examine what happens for Tamarkin-Tsygan calculus when the bar resolution is replaced by the Koszul complex. In 2020, Koszul calculus was extended to quiver algebras with homogeneous quadratic relations (called
quadratic quiver algebras
) by R. Taillefer and the first author
[
4
]
. This generalization, applied to preprojective algebras, reveals some interesting interactions with the Poincaré Van den Bergh duality
[
22
]
. From the case of preprojective algebras, a new Calabi-Yau property was defined for quadratic quiver algebras in
[
4
]
, called
Koszul complex Calabi-Yau property
, abbreviated in
Kc-Calabi-Yau property
.

Explicitly, a quadratic quiver algebra
A
is said to be
n
-Kc-Calabi-Yau if the
A
-bimodule Koszul complex
K(A)
of
A
has finite length
n
and if

\mathop{\rm RHom}\nolimits_{A^{e}}(K(A),A^{e})\cong K(A)[-n]

in the bounded derived category
\mathcal{D}^{b}(A\text{-{Bimod}})
of
A
-bimodules. As usual,
A^{e}:=A\otimes_{\mathbb{F}}A^{op}
where
\mathbb{F}
is the base field.

If
A
is
n
-Kc-Calabi-Yau and Koszul, then
A
is homologically smooth and
K(A)\cong A
in
\mathcal{D}^{b}(A\text{-{Bimod}})
, so that one has

\mathop{\rm RHom}\nolimits_{A^{e}}(A,A^{e})\cong A[-n]\ \mathrm{in}\ \mathcal{D}^{b}(A\text{-{Bimod}}),

meaning that
A
is
n
-Calabi-Yau in Ginzburg’s sense
[
11
]
. In
[
4
]
, it is proved that the preprojective algebra of a connected graph distinct from A
1
and A
2
is 2-Kc-Calabi-Yau. Such a preprojective algebra is known to be not 2-Calabi-Yau in Ginzburg’s sense when the graph is Dynkin ADE, because the minimal projective resolution of
A
has then infinite length so that
A
is not homologically smooth. If the graph is not Dynkin ADE, the preprojective algebra is Koszul.

In Koszul calculus, the Hochschild cochain dg algebra
\operatorname{Hom}\nolimits_{A^{e}}(B(A),A)
of the Tamarkin-Tsygan calculus, where
B(A)
is the bar resolution of
A
, is replaced by
the Koszul cochain dg algebra of
A
, denoted by
\tilde{A}
. By definition,

\tilde{A}=\operatorname{Hom}\nolimits_{A^{e}}(K(A),A)

(1.1)

is the space of Koszul cochains of
A
with coefficients in
A
. This is a dg algebra whose grading is given by the cohomological degree of Koszul cochains, differential is the Koszul differential
b_{K}
and multiplication is the Koszul cup product
\underset{K}{\smile}
. The elements of
\tilde{A}
should be thought of as differentials on
A
. If
A
is a polynomial algebra in
n
variables, the algebra
\tilde{A}
is isomorphic to the exterior algebra
\Omega_{A}^{\ast}
constructed on the Kähler differentials of
A

[
23
]
, as we shall see it in Theorem
4.5
.

A fundamental fact is that the Koszul complex
K(A)
is a dg
\tilde{A}
-bimodule compatible with its structure of
A
-bimodule
[
4
, Proposition 3.6]
. Following the Yekutieli terminology
[
24
]
applied to dg bimodules instead of left dg modules, we say that

is a dg
\tilde{A}
-bimodule in the abelian category

of

-bimodules.

In the same spirit, notations used in the Yekutieli formalism for left dg modules will be adapted in our paper for dg bimodules. For example, we denote by
\mathcal{C}(\tilde{A},A\text{-{Bimod}})
the dg category of dg
\tilde{A}
-bimodules in
A\text{-{Bimod}}
and by
\mathcal{D}(\tilde{A},A\text{-{Bimod}})
its derived category. The strict category of
\mathcal{C}(\tilde{A},A\text{-{Bimod}})
is
\mathcal{C}_{str}(\tilde{A},A\text{-{Bimod}})
while the homotopy category is
\mathcal{K}(\tilde{A},A\text{-{Bimod}})
, giving rise to canonical functors

P:\mathcal{C}_{str}(\tilde{A},A\text{-{Bimod}})\longrightarrow\mathcal{K}(\tilde{A},A\text{-{Bimod}}),

Q:\mathcal{K}(\tilde{A},A\text{-{Bimod}})\longrightarrow\mathcal{D}(\tilde{A},A\text{-{Bimod}}).

Definitions and basic results on derived categories
[
24
]
are presented in Section

Definition 1.1

Let

Our first result concerning on derived Koszul calculus is the following proposition. This result was not observed in

Let

\mathcal{C}(\tilde{A},A\text{-{Bimod}})

K
-injectives and enough

-projectives.

This proposition, stated as Proposition

in Section

, a new general result in the Yekutieli formalism whose statement is the following.

Theorem 1.3

Assume that
\mathbb{K}
is a nonzero commutative base ring,
A
is a central
\mathbb{K}
-ring and

is a dg central
\mathbb{K}
-ring. The category
\mathcal{C}(B,A\text{-{Mod}})

-injectives and enough

-projectives.

Section

is devoted to prove this theorem. Therefore, as explained in Section

, the derived functor

\mathop{\rm RHom}\nolimits_{A^{e}}(-,A^{e}):\mathcal{D}^{b}(\tilde{A},A\text{-{Bimod}})^{op}\longrightarrow\mathcal{D}^{b}(\tilde{A},A\text{-{Bimod}})

(1.2)

exists, where the upperscript

At the end of Section
3
, we shall be ready to reformulate the definition of strong Kc-Calabi-Yau algebras
without any existence assumption
. Our motivation for defining strong Kc-Calabi-Yau algebras is that the preprojective algebra of a connected graph distinct from A
1
and A
2
appears naturally as strong 2-Kc-Calabi-Yau
[
4
, Proposition 4.7]
including an existence assumption, now unnecessary.

Definition 1.4

Let
A
be a quadratic quiver algebra. We say that
A
is strong
n
-Kc-Calabi-Yau if the complex
K(A)
has length
n
and if

\mathop{\rm RHom}\nolimits_{A^{e}}(K(A),A^{e})\cong K(A)[-n]

in the bounded derived category
\mathcal{D}^{b}(\tilde{A},A\text{-{Bimod}})
.

Then
A
is
n
-Kc-Calabi-Yau by forgetting the actions of
\tilde{A}
. Concluding Section
3
, we shall obtain a Poincaré Van den Bergh duality theorem for strong Kc-Calabi-Yau algebras
without any existence assumption
. It is a duality between Koszul cohomology and Koszul homology expressed as a cap product by a fundamental class, inducing a duality between their higher versions indicated by the subscript (upperscript)
hi
.

Theorem 1.5

Let
A
be a quadratic quiver algebra. Assume that
A
is strong
n
-Kc-Calabi-Yau. Then there is a class
c
in
\operatorname{HK}\nolimits_{n}(A)
, called fundamental class, such that

c\underset{K}{\frown}-:\operatorname{HK}\nolimits^{\bullet}(A)\rightarrow\operatorname{HK}\nolimits_{n-\bullet}(A)

is an isomorphism of
\operatorname{HK}\nolimits^{\bullet}(A)
-bimodules, inducing an isomorphism of
\operatorname{HK}\nolimits^{\bullet}_{hi}(A)
-bimodules from
\operatorname{HK}\nolimits^{\bullet}_{hi}(A)
to
\operatorname{HK}\nolimits^{hi}_{n-\bullet}(A)
. For all
\alpha\in\operatorname{HK}\nolimits^{p}(A)
, one has
c\underset{K}{\frown}\alpha=(-1)^{np}\alpha\underset{K}{\frown}c
.

Section
4
is devoted to prove that a polynomial algebra in
n
variables is strong
n
-Kc-Calabi-Yau. It is based on a duality isomorphism stated below. This isomorphism is reminiscent of the contraction map (inner product) used in differential geometry
[
16
]
. Here the quiver has a single vertex and
n
arrows, so that
k=\mathbb{F}
. Fix a basis
\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}
of the
k
-vector space
V
and define a fundamental class
c
of
A
by
c=1_{A}\otimes(x_{1}\wedge\ldots\wedge x_{n})
in
A\otimes W_{n}
.

Theorem 1.6

Let
V
be a
k
-vector space of finite dimension
n
and
A=S(V)
be the symmetric algebra of
V
. Let
M
be an
A
-bimodule. For each Koszul
p
-cochain
f
with coefficients in
M
, we define the Koszul
(n-p)
-chain
\theta_{M}(f)
with coefficients in
M
by

\theta_{M}(f)=c\underset{K}{\frown}f.

(1.3)

Then the linear map
\theta_{M}:\operatorname{Hom}\nolimits(W_{\bullet},M)\rightarrow M\otimes W_{n-\bullet}
is a
(-n)
-degree complex isomorphism of dg
\tilde{A}
-bimodules.

Next we prove that
\theta_{A^{e}}
is a
(-n)
-degree complex isomorphism in
\mathcal{C}(\tilde{A},A\text{-{Bimod}})
. Analyzing this isomorphism in the derived category, we are led to show that the objects
\mathop{\rm RHom}\nolimits_{A^{e}}(K(A),A^{e})
and
\operatorname{Hom}\nolimits_{A^{e}}(K(A),A^{e})
are isomorphic in
\mathcal{D}(\tilde{A},A\text{-{Bimod}})
for any quadratic algebra
A
over a finite quiver (Proposition
4.10
). Then the expected result is obtained.

Theorem 1.7

Let
V
be a
k
-vector space of finite dimension
n
and
A=S(V)
be the symmetric algebra of
V
. Then
A
is strong
n
-Kc-Calabi-Yau.

For the preprojective algebras, we have applied the same strategy in
[
4
]
: state a duality isomorphism
\theta_{M}

[
4
, Theorem 4.4]
, next specialize to
M=A^{e}

[
4
, Proposition 4.7]
, and finally use
\mathop{\rm RHom}\nolimits_{A^{e}}(K(A),A^{e})\cong\operatorname{Hom}\nolimits_{A^{e}}(K(A),A^{e})

A general result on categories of dg modules

2.1
The derived category associated to a dg algebra and an abelian category

We follow Yekutieli’s book
[
24
]
. In this section,
\mathbb{K}
is a nonzero commutative base ring,
B
is a dg central
\mathbb{K}
-ring and
\mathcal{M}
is an abelian category. We shall apply in Subsection
2.5
this general setting to
\mathcal{M}=A\text{-{Mod}}
the category of left modules over a central
\mathbb{K}
-ring

, hence the choice of the notation

Following

denotes the dg category of dg left

The localization functor

2.2
K
-injective resolutions

Let us recall some definitions and facts on existence of right derived functors
[
24
, Section 10.1]
. We state them in the general setting considered by Yekutieli. Fix a dg category
\mathcal{C}(B,\mathcal{M})
as above. An object
N
in
\mathcal{C}(B,\mathcal{M})
is said to be
acyclic
if
H^{p}(N)=0
for all
p
. An object
I
in
\mathcal{C}(B,\mathcal{M})
is said to be
K-injective
if for any acyclic
N
, the object

\operatorname{Hom}\nolimits_{\mathcal{C}(B,\mathcal{M})}(N,I)
in
\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{K}\text{-{Mod}})
is acyclic, which is equivalent to say that
\operatorname{Hom}\nolimits_{\mathcal{K}(B,\mathcal{M})}(N,I)=0
for every acyclic

.

In the
K
-injective terminology as in the
K
-projective terminology, the letter
K
is referred to the notation used for homotopy categories.
K
-injective (and
K
-projective) resolutions were applied by N. Spaltenstein
[
17
]
to unbounded complexes in abelian categories and to complexes in categories of sheaves. See also Gelfand-Manin’s book
[
10
]
. The same concepts were emphasized by B. Keller
[
13
]
in the context of dg modules over dg rings. See
[
24
, Remark 10.1.29]
for other references.

A

of

is a quasi-isomorphism
\rho:C\rightarrow I
in
\mathcal{C}_{str}(B,\mathcal{M})
, where

is
K
-injective. Quasi-isomorphism means that the morphisms
H^{p}(\rho)
in
\mathcal{M}
are isomorphisms for all
p
. The morphism
P(\rho):C\rightarrow I
in
\mathcal{K}(B,\mathcal{M})
is said to be a
K
-injective resolution as well.

As in
[
24
, Section 10.1]
,
\mathcal{K}(B,\mathcal{M})_{inj}
denotes the full triangulated subcategory of
\mathcal{K}(B,\mathcal{M})
on the
K
-injective objects. By definition,
\mathcal{C}(B,\mathcal{M})
has
enough K-injectives
if any

admits a
K
-injective resolution. We also say that
\mathcal{K}(B,\mathcal{M})
has enough
K
-injectives. Then, in this case, the restricted localization functor

Q:\mathcal{K}(B,\mathcal{M})_{inj}\rightarrow\mathcal{D}(B,\mathcal{M})

will be an equivalence of categories
[
24
, Corollary 10.1.15]
.

In this general setting, the existence theorem for right derived functors is the following
[
24
, Theorem 10.1.20]
.

Theorem 2.1

Assume that
B
is a dg central
\mathbb{K}
-ring and
\mathcal{M}
is an abelian category such that
\mathcal{K}(B,\mathcal{M})
has enough

-injectives. Let

be a
\mathbb{K}
-linear triangulated category, and let

\mathbb{K}
-linear triangulated functor. Then

has a triangulated right derived functor
(RF,\eta^{R}):\mathcal{D}(B,\mathcal{M})\rightarrow E

2.4
Specializations

There are two important specializations. If

is the trivial dg algebra, then
\mathcal{C}(B,\mathcal{M})
is regarded as the dg category
\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{M})

[

, Corollary 11.5.26]

[

, Corollary 11.3.19]
,
[

Theorem 2.4

If the abelian category

has enough injectives, the subcategory
\mathcal{C}^{+}(\mathcal{M})
of bounded below complexes has enough
K
-injectives.

Theorem 2.5

If the abelian category
\mathcal{M}
has enough projectives, the subcategory
\mathcal{C}^{-}(\mathcal{M})
of bounded above complexes has enough
K
-projectives.

Theorem 2.6

If
B
is any dg central
\mathbb{K}
-ring, the category
\mathcal{C}(B)
has enough

-injectives.

Theorem 2.7

If

is any dg central
\mathbb{K}
-ring, the category

has enough

-projectives.

It is interesting to note that the two specializations intersect. Assume that the dg algebra

is concentrated in degree zero so that the differential is zero. In this case, the dg category

can be regarded as the dg category
\mathcal{C}(B\text{-{Mod}})
of complexes of left

-modules where

is just considered as a central
\mathbb{K}
-ring. Then Theorem

and Theorem

apply since the abelian category

say more: the category

has enough

-injectives and enough

-projectives
with no boundedness conditions
. In that sense, the two latter theorems are stronger than the two former ones.

The fact that the category

has enough

-injectives and enough

-projectives with no boundedness conditions was obtained by N. Spaltenstein

and was a motivation for him to introduce

-projective resolutions.

For any dg central
\mathbb{K}
-ring

, the two latter theorems lie on two main theorems of the theory, namely

and
[
24
, Theorem 11.4.17]
whose proofs are due to B. Keller and are detailed in

]
. These main theorems are existence theorems of semi-cofree and semi-free resolutions, respectively. Semi-cofree (semi-free) objects in

2.5
A mixed specialization

Our Theorem

-injectives and enough

In the remainder of the present Section

-ring,

is a dg central
\mathbb{K}
-ring. In the sequel,
\alpha
and
\beta
denote elements in
A
while
f
and
g
denote elements in
B
. The product of
f
and
g
in
B
is denoted by
f\times g
and the unit of
B
is
1_{B}
. The differential of
B
is denoted by
b
.

Let
M=(M^{q})_{q\in\mathbb{Z}}
be a graded
B
-module. This means that
M=(M^{q})_{q\in\mathbb{Z}}
is a graded
\mathbb{K}
-module and that any

acts on

by defining an element

in
M^{q+p}
,
\mathbb{K}
-linearly in
f
and in
x
, such that

f.(g.x))=(f\times g).x,\ \ 1_{B}.x=x.

Then
M
is a graded
B
-module in
A\text{-{Mod}}
if moreover each
M^{q}
is a module over the
\mathbb{K}
-ring
A
such that the actions of

and

on

commute, meaning that the equality

\alpha(f.x)=f.(\alpha x)

holds for any
\alpha
in
A
.

Denote by
\mathcal{G}(B,A\text{-{Mod}})
the category of graded
B
-modules in
A\text{-{Mod}}
. A morphism in this category is a finite sum of homogeneous graded morphisms. Such an
r
-homogeneous graded morphism

is
r
-homogeneous graded linear for
B
-actions and each

is a morphism of
A
-modules. The subcategory
\mathcal{G}_{str}(B,A\text{-{Mod}})
whose morphisms are strict, i.e., of degree 0, is abelian;
\mathcal{G}_{str}(B,A\text{-{Mod}})
is called the strict category of graded
B
-modules in
A\text{-{Mod}}
.

Up to now, we have just used the graded central
\mathbb{K}
-ring

. Taking into account the differential

of
B
, that is, the whole structure of dg central
\mathbb{K}
-ring of
B
, we are ready to define the dg category
\mathcal{C}(B,A\text{-{Mod}})
of dg

-modules in

. An object

in
\mathcal{C}(B,A\text{-{Mod}})
is a graded

-module in
A\text{-{Mod}}
endowed with a differential

of

-modules, satisfying

d_{M}(f.x)=b(f).x+(-1)^{p}f.d_{M}(x)

(2.1)

for any

and

in

. The forgetful functor
\operatorname{Und}\nolimits:\mathcal{C}(B,A\text{-{Mod}})\rightarrow\mathcal{G}(B,A\text{-{Mod}})
that forgets the differentials of

and of the dg

[

, Proposition 3.8.18]
.

By definition, for two objects

and

in

, one has

\operatorname{Hom}\nolimits_{\mathcal{C}(B,A\text{-{Mod}})}(M,M^{\prime})=\operatorname{Hom}\nolimits_{\mathcal{G}(B,A\text{-{Mod}})}(M,M^{\prime}),

and this graded space has a differential

defined by

(2.2)

where

is

-homogeneous. Then
(\operatorname{Hom}\nolimits_{\mathcal{C}(B,A\text{-{Mod}})}(M,M^{\prime}),d)
is a complex in
\mathbb{K}\text{-{Mod}}
, i.e., a dg
\mathbb{K}
-module.

The subcategory
\mathcal{C}_{str}(B,A\text{-{Mod}})
of
\mathcal{C}(B,A\text{-{Mod}})
has the same objects, with morphism spaces

\operatorname{Hom}\nolimits_{\mathcal{C}_{str}(B,A\text{-{Mod}})}(M,M^{\prime})=Z^{0}(\operatorname{Hom}\nolimits_{\mathcal{C}(B,A\text{-{Mod}})}(M,M^{\prime})),

that is, the morphisms from
M
to
M^{\prime}
are of degree 0
and
are morphisms of complexes. Warning: these morphisms are still called strict morphisms. The abelian category
\mathcal{C}_{str}(B,A\text{-{Mod}})
is called the strict category of dg
B
-modules in
A\text{-{Mod}}
. The forgetful functor
\operatorname{Und}\nolimits:\mathcal{C}_{str}(B,A\text{-{Mod}})\rightarrow\mathcal{G}_{str}(B,A\text{-{Mod}})
is faithfully exact.

The homotopy category
\mathcal{K}(B,A\text{-{Mod}})
has the same objects as
\mathcal{C}(B,A\text{-{Mod}})
, with morphism spaces

\operatorname{Hom}\nolimits_{\mathcal{K}(B,A\text{-{Mod}})}(M,M^{\prime})=H^{0}(\operatorname{Hom}\nolimits_{\mathcal{C}(B,A\text{-{Mod}})}(M,M^{\prime})),

that is, the morphisms from

to

are the homotopy classes of strict morphisms. It is a triangulated category. The canonical functor

P:\mathcal{C}_{str}(B,A\text{-{Mod}})\longrightarrow\mathcal{K}(B,A\text{-{Mod}})

sends strict morphisms to their homotopy classes.

From
\mathcal{K}(B,A\text{-{Mod}})
, the derived category
\mathcal{D}(B,A\text{-{Mod}})
is constructed by inverting quasi-isomorphisms. This is a triangulated category and the localization functor

Q:\mathcal{K}(B,A\text{-{Mod}})\longrightarrow\mathcal{D}(B,A\text{-{Mod}})

is triangulated.

2.6
A dg functor
\Phi

Our Theorem

is based on a functor
\Phi
defined now. In all the sequel, by an algebra, we mean a central
\mathbb{K}
-ring. The unadorned tensor symbol
\otimes
means
\otimes_{\mathbb{K}}
.

The algebra
A
is endowed with a structure of trivial dg algebra by imposing the grading
A=A^{0}
and the zero differential. Therefore,
A\otimes B
is a dg algebra
[
24
, Example 3.3.10]
. The product is given by

(\alpha\otimes f)\times(\beta\otimes g)=(\alpha\beta)\otimes(f\times g)

for
f,g
in
B
and
\alpha,\beta
in
A
. The unit is
1_{A}\otimes 1_{B}
. The differential
\check{b}
is given by

\check{b}(\alpha\otimes f)=\alpha\otimes b(f).

Our aim is to define a dg functor

\Phi:\mathcal{C}(B,A\text{-{Mod}})\longrightarrow\mathcal{C}(A\otimes B).

(2.3)

Let
M=(M^{q})_{q\in\mathbb{Z}}
be a dg
B
-module in
A\text{-{Mod}}
. We define actions of
A\otimes B
on
x\in M
by

(\alpha\otimes f).x=\alpha(f.x)=f.(\alpha x).

(2.4)

The equality on the right is the commutation of
B
and
A
acting on
M
. Clearly

\displaystyle(\alpha\otimes f).((\beta\otimes g).x)

\displaystyle=((\alpha\otimes f)\times(\beta\otimes g)).x\,,

(2.5)

\displaystyle(1_{A}\otimes 1_{B}).x

\displaystyle=x\,.

(2.6)

So
M
is a graded
(A\otimes B)
-module.

Let
f\in B^{p}
. On one hand, one has

d_{M}((\alpha\otimes f).x)=d_{M}(f.(\alpha x))=b(f).(\alpha x))+(-1)^{p}f.d_{M}(\alpha x)=(\alpha\otimes b(f)).x+(-1)^{p}f.(\alpha d_{M}(x))

since
d_{M}
is
A
-linear. On the other hand,

\check{b}(\alpha\otimes f).x+(-1)^{p}(\alpha\otimes f).d_{M}(x)=(\alpha\otimes b(f)).x+(-1)^{p}f.(\alpha d_{M}(x))

and we obtain

d_{M}((\alpha\otimes f).x)=\check{b}(\alpha\otimes f).x+(-1)^{p}(\alpha\otimes f).d_{M}(x).

(2.7)

Thus
M
is a dg
(A\otimes B)
-module, defining an object
\Phi(M)
in
\mathcal{C}(A\otimes B)
. Note that the differential of the object
\Phi(M)
is just
d_{M}
considered as a
\mathbb{K}
-linear map.

Define now
\Phi
on morphisms. Let
u:M\rightarrow M^{\prime}
be an
r
-homogeneous graded morphism for
B
-actions such that each
u:M^{q}\rightarrow M^{\prime q+r}
is a morphism of
A
-modules. The equality

u((\alpha\otimes f).x)=u(f.(\alpha x))=(-1)^{pr}f.u(\alpha x)=(-1)^{pr}f.(\alpha u(x))=(-1)^{pr}(\alpha\otimes f).u(x)

for
f\in B^{p}
shows that
u:M\rightarrow M^{\prime}
becomes a morphism in
\mathcal{C}(A\otimes B)
from
\Phi(M)
to
\Phi(M^{\prime})
, denoted by
\Phi(u)
. The functoriality properties
\Phi(\operatorname{id}\nolimits_{C})=\operatorname{id}\nolimits_{\Phi(C)}
and
\Phi(v\circ u)=\Phi(v)\circ\Phi(u)
hold.
Consequently
\Phi
is a functor. Note that we can extend
\Phi
to any
A
-linear map from
M
to
M^{\prime}
in an obvious manner, so that we can write
\Phi(d_{M})=d_{\Phi(M)}
.

Let us prove that the functor
\Phi
is a dg functor
[
24
, Definition 3.5.1]
. Recall from Equality (
2.2
) that

d(u)=d_{M^{\prime}}\circ u-(-1)^{r}u\circ d_{M}\,.

By functoriality
\Phi(d(u))=\Phi(d_{M^{\prime}})\circ\Phi(u)-(-1)^{r}\Phi(u)\circ\Phi(d_{M})
, implying that
\Phi(d(u))=d(\Phi(u))
, as we want. In conclusion, we obtain the following.

Proposition 2.8

As defined above,
\Phi:\mathcal{C}(B,A\text{-{Mod}})\longrightarrow\mathcal{C}(A\otimes B)
is a dg functor.

2.7
A dg functor
\Psi
, inverse to
\Phi

Let
(M,d_{M})
be a dg
(A\otimes B)
-module. For
f
in
B^{p}
and
\alpha\in A
, we define the following actions
on
x\in M
by

\displaystyle f.x

\displaystyle=(1_{A}\otimes f).x

(2.8)

\displaystyle\alpha x

\displaystyle=(\alpha\otimes 1_{B}).x.

(2.9)

For these actions,

becomes a graded
B
-module in
A\text{-{Mod}}
. Moreover, one has

d_{M}(f.x)=d_{M}((1_{A}\otimes f).x)=\check{b}(1_{A}\otimes f).x+(-1)^{p}(1_{A}\otimes f).d_{M}(x),

and using
\check{b}(1_{A}\otimes f)=1_{A}\otimes b(f)
, we obtain

d_{M}(f.x)=b(f).x+(-1)^{p}f.d_{M}(x).

It is immediate that
d_{M}
is
A
-linear. Therefore,
M
becomes a dg
B
-module in
A\text{-{Mod}}
, denoted by
\Psi(M)
.

Let
M
and
M^{\prime}
be dg
(A\otimes B)
-modules. Let
u:M\rightarrow M^{\prime}
be an
r
-homogeneous graded morphism for
(A\otimes B)
-actions. Then it is direct to prove that

is a graded

-module morphism and an

-module morphism, defining

. Clearly, we get a functor

\Psi:\mathcal{C}(A\otimes B)\rightarrow\mathcal{C}(B,A\text{-{Mod}}).

(2.10)

Here again, we can extend
\Psi
to any

-linear map from

to

, so that we can write
\Psi(d_{M})=d_{\Psi(M)}
and deduce

. Thus

is a dg functor. Since
\Psi\circ\Phi
and

are identity functors, we have proved the following.

Proposition 2.9

The functor
\Phi:\mathcal{C}(B,A\text{-{Mod}})\longrightarrow\mathcal{C}(A\otimes B)
is an isomorphism of dg categories.

2.8
A result in the mixed specialization

Since
\Phi(M)
and

have the same

-linear differential, one has the commutative diagram

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{str}(B,A\text{-{Mod}})\ \ \ \stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Phi}}{{\longrightarrow}}

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{str}(A\otimes B)

\displaystyle\downarrow H\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \

\displaystyle\downarrow H

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{str}(A\text{-{Mod}})\ \ \ \stackrel{{\scriptstyle\operatorname{Und}\nolimits}}{{\longrightarrow}}

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{str}(\mathbb{K}\text{-{Mod}})

is a forgetful functor.

Proposition 2.10

(i) The object

is acyclic in

if and only if

is acyclic in

(ii) A strict morphism

\mathcal{C}(B,A\text{-{Mod}})

is a quasi-isomorphism in
\mathcal{C}(A\otimes B)
.

(iii) An object

is

-injective (

\mathcal{C}(B,A\text{-{Mod}})
if and only if
\Phi(M)
is

-injective (
K
-projective) in
\mathcal{C}(A\otimes B)
.

Proof.
(i) and (ii) are clear from the commutative diagram (
2.11
). To deduce (iii) from (i), remark that the dg isomorphism

induces an isomorphism

\operatorname{Hom}\nolimits_{\mathcal{C}(B,A\text{-{Mod}})}(M,M^{\prime})\longrightarrow\operatorname{Hom}\nolimits_{\mathcal{C}(A\otimes B)}(\Phi(M),\Phi(M^{\prime}))

in
\mathcal{C}_{str}(\mathbb{K}\text{-{Mod}})
, so that the complex
\operatorname{Hom}\nolimits_{\mathcal{C}(B,A\text{-{Mod}})}(M,M^{\prime})
is acyclic if and only if the complex

is acyclic. Therefore, an object

is

\mathcal{C}(B,A\text{-{Mod}})
if and only if the complex
\operatorname{Hom}\nolimits_{\mathcal{C}(A\otimes B)}(\Phi(N),\Phi(M))
is acyclic for any acyclic object

in
\mathcal{C}(B,A\text{-{Mod}})
. Since

is an isomorphism, any acyclic object

in

can be written

, and

\mathcal{C}(B,A\text{-{Mod}})

is

if and only if

is

-injective in
\mathcal{C}(A\otimes B)
. There is an analogous proof in the

-projective case.

As a consequence of (ii) and (iii),

is a

-injective resolution in

if and only if

is a

-injective resolution in

. There is an analogous statement in the

-projective case. Therefore, Theorem

and Theorem
2.7
imply the main result of this section.

Theorem 2.11

Assume that

is a central
\mathbb{K}
-ring and

is a dg central

-ring. The category

Let

The derived Koszul calculus of a quadratic algebra

3.1
The Koszul calculus of a quadratic algebra

Throughout the remainder of the paper, we follow the setup of
[
4
]
. Let
\mathcal{Q}=(\mathcal{Q}_{0},\mathcal{Q}_{1})
be a quiver such that the set
\mathcal{Q}_{0}
of vertices and the set
\mathcal{Q}_{1}
of arrows are finite. We assume that
\mathcal{Q}_{0}\neq\emptyset
. Let
\mathbb{F}
be a field, playing the role of the base ring
\mathbb{K}
of the general setting presented in Section
2
. The unadorned tensor symbol
\otimes
means
\otimes_{\mathbb{F}}
.

The vertex space
k=\mathbb{F}\mathcal{Q}_{0}
becomes a (nonzero) commutative ring isomorphic to the ring
\mathbb{F}^{|\mathcal{Q}_{0}|}
by associating with
\mathcal{Q}_{0}
a complete set of orthogonal idempotents
\{e_{i}\,;\,i\in\mathcal{Q}_{0}\}
. For each arrow
\alpha\in\mathcal{Q}_{1}
, denote its source vertex by
\mathfrak{s}(\alpha)
and its target vertex by
\mathfrak{t}(\alpha)
. The arrow space
V=\mathbb{F}\mathcal{Q}_{1}
is a
k
-bimodule for the following actions:
e_{j}\alpha e_{i}
is equal to zero if
i\neq\mathfrak{s}(\alpha)
or
j\neq\mathfrak{t}(\alpha)
, and is equal to
\alpha
if
i=\mathfrak{s}(\alpha)
and
j=\mathfrak{t}(\alpha)
.

The tensor
k
-algebra
T_{k}(V)
of the
k
-bimodule
V
is identified to the path algebra
\mathbb{F}\mathcal{Q}
of the quiver
\mathcal{Q}
. Fixing a
k
-subbimodule
R
of
V\otimes_{k}V\cong\mathbb{F}\mathcal{Q}_{2}
, the associative algebra

A=T_{k}(V)/(R)\cong\mathbb{F}\mathcal{Q}/(R)

where
(R)
denotes the two-sided ideal generated by
R
, is called a
quadratic
k
-algebra over the finite quiver
\mathcal{Q}
.

The algebra
A
is
\mathbb{F}
-central, but is not
k
-central in general. All the bimodules considered in this paper are assumed to be
\mathbb{F}
-central. Setting
A^{e}=A\otimes A^{op}
, an
A
-bimodule can be viewed as a left (or right)
A^{e}
-module, as usual. The algebra
A
is graded for the path length. The degree
m
component of
A
is denoted by
A_{m}
. Clearly,
A_{0}\cong k
and
A_{1}\cong V
.

The
Koszul complex

K(A)
is the subcomplex of the bar resolution
B(A)
defined by the
A
-subbimodules
A\otimes_{k}W_{p}\otimes_{k}A
of
A\otimes_{k}A^{\otimes_{k}p}\otimes_{k}A
, where
W_{0}=k
,
W_{1}=V
,
W_{2}=R
and, for
p\geqslant 3
,

W_{p}=\bigcap_{i+2+j=p}V^{\otimes_{k}i}\otimes_{k}R\otimes_{k}V^{\otimes_{k}j}

(3.1)

with
i\geq 0
and
j\geq 0
. Here
W_{p}
is considered as a
k
-subbimodule of
V^{\otimes_{k}p}\subseteq A^{\otimes_{k}p}
. The differential
d

of

\mathcal{C}(\tilde{A},A\text{-{Bimod}})

\tilde{A}

-bimodule in

in

. The forgetful functor

3.6

If

\operatorname{HK}\nolimits^{\bullet}(A)
-bimodules, inducing an isomorphism of
\operatorname{HK}\nolimits^{\bullet}_{hi}(A)
-bimodules from
\operatorname{HK}\nolimits^{\bullet}_{hi}(A)
to
\operatorname{HK}\nolimits^{hi}_{n-\bullet}(A)
. For all

, one has

.

The proof is the same as the proof given for
[
4
, Theorem 5.11]
, with the same definition of the fundamental class

and of the higher Koszul calculus
\operatorname{HK}\nolimits^{\bullet}_{hi}(A)
and
\operatorname{HK}\nolimits^{hi}_{\bullet}(A)
. However, in the statement of
[
4
, Theorem 5.11]
, it is assumed that the derived functors

\displaystyle\mathop{\rm RHom}\nolimits_{A^{e}}(-,A):\ \

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{b}(\tilde{A},A\text{-{Bimod}})^{op}\longrightarrow\mathcal{D}^{b}(\tilde{A})

(3.36)

\displaystyle A\stackrel{{\scriptstyle L}}{{\otimes}}_{A^{e}}-:\ \

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{b}(\tilde{A},A\text{-{Bimod}})\longrightarrow\mathcal{D}^{b}(\tilde{A})

(3.37)

exist. Now these derived functors exist because functors (
3.33
) and (

If

\displaystyle f\underset{K}{\frown}c=\sum_{\sigma\in Sh(p,n-p)}\mathrm{sgn}(\sigma)\,f(x_{\sigma(1)}\wedge\ldots\wedge x_{\sigma(p)})

Theorem 4.5

24
]
for these general facts.

Actually, we prove in the next proposition the existence of such an isomorphism in the unbounded derived category
\mathcal{D}(\tilde{A},A\text{-{Bimod}})
for
any quadratic algebra
A
over a finite quiver
\mathcal{Q}
. In this statement, there is no finiteness assumption on the length of
K(A)
. If
K(A)
has a finite length, the isomorphism in
\mathcal{D}(\tilde{A},A\text{-{Bimod}})
induces an isomorphism in
\mathcal{D}^{b}(\tilde{A},A\text{-{Bimod}})
. So Theorem
4.7
will be an immediate consequence of the following general result. This result was not included in
[
4
]
but was implicitly used just after
[
4
, Definition 5.10]
for proving that the preprojective algebras are strong
2
-Kc-Calabi-Yau.

Proposition 4.10

Let us go back to the general setup of Subsection
3.1
. Let
A
be a quadratic algebra over a finite quiver
\mathcal{Q}
. Then there exists an isomorphism

\phi:\operatorname{Hom}\nolimits_{A^{e}}(K(A),A^{e})\longrightarrow\mathop{\rm RHom}\nolimits_{A^{e}}(K(A),A^{e})

(4.18)

in
\mathcal{D}(\tilde{A},A\text{-{Bimod}})
.

Proof.
Let us consider the triangulated functor

F:=\operatorname{Hom}\nolimits_{A^{e}}(-,A^{e}):\mathcal{K}(\tilde{A},A\text{-{Bimod}})^{op}\longrightarrow\mathcal{D}(\tilde{A},A\text{-{Bimod}})

defined in Formula (
3.32
) of Subsection
3.4
. In this subsection, we have applied a general fact of the theory, namely Theorem
2.3
, since
\mathcal{C}(\tilde{A},A\text{-{Bimod}})
has enough
K
-projectives (Proposition
3.7
). So
F
has a triangulated right derived functor

(RF,\eta^{R}):\mathcal{D}(\tilde{A},A\text{-{Bimod}})^{op}\longrightarrow\mathcal{D}(\tilde{A},A\text{-{Bimod}})

where
\eta^{R}:F\Rightarrow RF\circ Q
is an isomorphism of triangulated functors from
\mathcal{K}(\tilde{A},A\text{-{Bimod}})^{op}
to
\mathcal{D}(\tilde{A},A\text{-{Bimod}})
. Here
Q
stands for the localization functor from
\mathcal{K}(\tilde{A},A\text{-{Bimod}})^{op}
to
\mathcal{D}(\tilde{A},A\text{-{Bimod}})^{op}
. Recall that for every
K
-projective object
P
in
\mathcal{K}(\tilde{A},A\text{-{Bimod}})
, the morphism
\eta^{R}_{P}:F(P)\rightarrow RF\circ Q(P)
in
\mathcal{D}(\tilde{A},A\text{-{Bimod}})
is an isomorphism.

Define the morphism

\phi:=\eta^{R}_{K(A)}:F(K(A))\longrightarrow RF\circ Q(K(A))

in
\mathcal{D}(\tilde{A},A\text{-{Bimod}})
. Note that

F(K(A))=\operatorname{Hom}\nolimits_{A^{e}}(K(A),A^{e})\ \ \mathrm{and}\ \ RF\circ Q(K(A))=\mathop{\rm RHom}\nolimits_{A^{e}}(K(A),A^{e}).

Our aim is to show that
\phi
is an isomorphism in
\mathcal{D}(\tilde{A},A\text{-{Bimod}})
. However we can’t conclude that immediately because we don’t know whether the object
K(A)
is
K
-projective in
\mathcal{K}(\tilde{A},A\text{-{Bimod}})
– may be it is not the case. In fact, the isomorphism
\theta_{A}
in Theorem
4.5
shows that the free
\tilde{A}
-bimodules seem too big compared to
K(A)
.

Our idea is to work with the underlying
A
-bimodules and to check that
K(A)
is
K
-projective in
\mathcal{K}(A\text{-{Bimod}})
. Let us introduce the dg functor

\operatorname{Und}\nolimits:\mathcal{C}(\tilde{A},A\text{-{Bimod}})\longrightarrow\mathcal{C}(A\text{-{Bimod}})

(4.19)

where
\mathcal{C}(A\text{-{Bimod}})
is the dg category of complexes of
A
-bimodules (see Subsection
2.4
). By definition the functor
\operatorname{Und}\nolimits
sends any object in
\mathcal{C}(\tilde{A},A\text{-{Bimod}})
described in Subsection
3.2
to its underlying
A
-bimodule, and analogously on the morphisms. A general fact
[
24
, Theorem 5.5.1]
provides a triangulated functor

\operatorname{Und}\nolimits:\mathcal{K}(\tilde{A},A\text{-{Bimod}})\longrightarrow\mathcal{K}(A\text{-{Bimod}}).

(4.20)

The commutative diagram

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}(\tilde{A},A\text{-{Bimod}})\ \ \ \stackrel{{\scriptstyle\operatorname{Und}\nolimits}}{{\longrightarrow}}

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}(A\text{-{Bimod}})

\displaystyle\downarrow H\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \

\displaystyle\downarrow H

(4.21)

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{str}(A\text{-{Bimod}})\ \ \ \stackrel{{\scriptstyle id}}{{\longrightarrow}}

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{str}(A\text{-{Bimod}})

shows that the functor
\operatorname{Und}\nolimits:\mathcal{K}(\tilde{A},A\text{-{Bimod}})\longrightarrow\mathcal{K}(A\text{-{Bimod}})
sends quasi-isomorphisms to quasi-isomorphisms and consequently passes trivially through the derived categories. Since
\operatorname{Und}\nolimits
is its own left derived functor, the so-obtained left derived functor is traditionnally still denoted by
\operatorname{Und}\nolimits
, and one has the commutative diagram

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(\tilde{A},A\text{-{Bimod}})\ \ \ \stackrel{{\scriptstyle\operatorname{Und}\nolimits}}{{\longrightarrow}}

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(A\text{-{Bimod}})

\displaystyle\downarrow H\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \

\displaystyle\downarrow H

(4.22)

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{str}(A\text{-{Bimod}})\ \ \ \stackrel{{\scriptstyle id}}{{\longrightarrow}}

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{str}(A\text{-{Bimod}})

Similarly we have the right derived functor
\operatorname{Und}\nolimits:\mathcal{D}(\tilde{A},A\text{-{Bimod}})^{op}\longrightarrow\mathcal{D}(A\text{-{Bimod}})
satisfying an analogous commutative diagram.

Let us define now the triangulated functor

G:=\operatorname{Hom}\nolimits_{A^{e}}(-,A^{e}):\mathcal{K}(A\text{-{Bimod}})^{op}\longrightarrow\mathcal{D}(A\text{-{Bimod}}).

One has the commutative digagram

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}(\tilde{A},A\text{-{Bimod}})^{op}\ \ \ \stackrel{{\scriptstyle F}}{{\longrightarrow}}

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(\tilde{A},A\text{-{Bimod}})

\displaystyle\downarrow\operatorname{Und}\nolimits\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \

\displaystyle\downarrow\operatorname{Und}\nolimits

(4.23)

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}(A\text{-{Bimod}})^{op}\ \ \ \stackrel{{\scriptstyle G}}{{\longrightarrow}}

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(A\text{-{Bimod}})

According to Subsection
2.4
,
\mathcal{C}(A\text{-{Bimod}})
has enough
K
-projectives and Theorem
2.3
applies. Thus
G
has a triangulated right derived functor
RG
and one has the commutative diagram

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(\tilde{A},A\text{-{Bimod}})^{op}\ \ \ \stackrel{{\scriptstyle RF}}{{\longrightarrow}}

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(\tilde{A},A\text{-{Bimod}})

\displaystyle\downarrow\operatorname{Und}\nolimits\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \

\displaystyle\downarrow\operatorname{Und}\nolimits

(4.24)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(A\text{-{Bimod}})^{op}\ \ \ \stackrel{{\scriptstyle RG}}{{\longrightarrow}}

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(A\text{-{Bimod}})

Recall that
\operatorname{Und}\nolimits
is identified to its left and right derived functor.

Consider the chain complex
K(A)
as a cochain complex by setting
K(A)^{p}=K(A)_{-p}
for any
p\in\mathbb{Z}
. This cochain complex is bounded above and it is a cochain complex of projective
A
-bimodules. Then
K(A)
is a semi-projective complex in
\mathcal{C}(A\text{-{Bimod}})

[
24
, Proposition 11.3.5]
, so that
K(A)
is
K
-projective in
\mathcal{C}(A\text{-{Bimod}})

[
24
, Theorem 11.3.2]
and in
\mathcal{K}(A\text{-{Bimod}})
as well. Therefore the morphism in
\mathcal{D}(A\text{-{Bimod}})
defined by

\operatorname{Und}\nolimits(\phi):G(K(A))\longrightarrow RG\circ Q(K(A))

is an isomorphism in
\mathcal{D}(A\text{-{Bimod}})
. Here
Q
is the localization functor from
\mathcal{K}(A\text{-{Bimod}})^{op}
to
\mathcal{D}(A\text{-{Bimod}})^{op}
.

Consequently, the commutative diagram (
4.22
)
shows that
H(\phi)=H(\operatorname{Und}\nolimits(\phi))
is an isomorphism in
\mathcal{G}_{str}(A\text{-{Bimod}})
. A fundamental fact says to us that the functor

H:\mathcal{D}(\tilde{A},A\text{-{Bimod}})\longrightarrow\mathcal{G}_{str}(A\text{-{Bimod}})

is
conservative

[
24
, Corollary 7.2.13]
. Therefore, since
H(\phi)
is an isomorphism,
\phi
is an isomorphism.
