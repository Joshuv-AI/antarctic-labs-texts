Hotspots and development trends of gut microbiota in atopic dermatitis: A bibliometric analysis from 1988 to 2024
Authors: Fang Liu, Haipeng Zhang, Lina Fan, Qi Yu, Siqiao Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Background:Atopic dermatitis (AD) is a prevalent inflammatory skin condition that commonly occurs in children. More and more scientific evidence suggests that gut microbiota plays an important role in the pathogenesis of AD, whereas there is no article providing a comprehensive summary and analysis. We aimed to analyze documents on AD and gut microbiota and identify hotspots and development trends in this field.Methods:Articles and reviews in the field of AD and gut microbiota from January 1, 1988 to October 20, 2024 were obtained from the Web of Science Core Collection database. Biblioshiny was utilized for evaluating and visualizing the core authors, journals, countries, documents, trend topics, and hotspots in this field.Results:Among 1672 documents, it indicated that the number of annual publications generally increased. The United States had the highest production, impact, and international collaboration. Journal of Allergy and Clinical Immunology was the journal of the maximum publications. Based on keyword co-occurrence and clustering analysis, “stratum-corneum lipids,” “probiotics,” “prebiotics,” “fecal microbiota transplantation,” “phage therapy,” “short chain fatty-acids,” “biologic therapy,” and “skin inflammation” represented current trend topics. The pathological and molecular mechanisms and associated therapeutic methods for AD and gut microbiota were the research hotspots. The incorporation of microbiota-based therapies alongside conventional treatments can contribute to better clinical outcomes.Conclusion:We highlighted that gut microbiota may exacerbate symptoms of AD through various aspects, including immunity, metabolites, and neuroendocrine pathways. More efforts are required to investigate the safety and efficacy of gut microbial management methods for the prevention and treatment of AD.

1. Introduction

Atopic dermatitis (AD) is an inflammatory skin condition with drastic effects on the pediatric health, and the multifactorial etiopathogenesis of AD remains unclear. Patients’ adaptive immune responses are affected by various environmental factors.[1,2] Gut microbiota imbalance has been suggested to impact the systemic immune system, potentially exacerbating AD.[3–7] The increasing incidence rate of AD, was considered to be caused by the over hygiene associated with the Western lifestyle decreases the exposure of the immune system of the host to beneficial gut microbiota.[8,9] Particularly, early childhood and infancy are critical and fragile periods in the development of gut microbiota, shaping the tendency to AD of individual.[10] In this field, currently, scholars are exploring the mechanisms how the gut microbiota influences immune responses at distant areas of the host, especially how the gut microbiota affects the skin. It has contributed to the new occurrence of terms, including the “gut-skin axis.”

Diverse abundance of gut microbiota regulates systemic immune responses by promoting an increase in regulatory T cells, immune tolerance and short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs).[11–14] Certain species of Bifidobacterium and Bacteroides, which have beneficial effects on health, are highly prevalent in the infants, leading to suppression of inflammatory responses.[15–17] Studies on the effects of probiotics have shown how gut microbiota influences other organs of the host. Some studies have demonstrated probiotics could affect the abundance of the gut microbiota and show immunoregulatory activity.[18,19] Based on the critical findings, the potential use of probiotics to the therapeutic interventions of hypersensitive disorders has been explored in various researches.[20,21] Recently, interests in investigating the “gut-skin axis” have been attracted by the results that the applications of unique lactobacilli in mice could alter the skin phenotypes of them significantly.[22] The specific mechanisms where the gut microbiota affects the immunity of the skin are being revealed, whereas the thematic areas remain unclear. Despite the abundance of studies on gut microbiota and AD in the past decades, there has been a lack of comprehensive bibliometric analysis.[23–26] The related studies were spread over various journals, making it difficult to identify the most impactive literature and trend topics in the field. Despite increased efforts in understanding the role of gut microbiota in AD, there remined no comprehensive bibliometric analysis to help researchers obtain a quick overview and identify meaningful hotspots. Hence, our work aimed to fill the gap based on bibliometrics to investigate the general information, emerging trends, and hot topics in this research field.

Bibliometric analysis can reveal the research trends of a given field by analyzing large amounts of data, facilitating global academic collaboration, and identifying trending topics within a given field. Nevertheless, the utilization of bibliometrics in investigating AD and gut microbiota remained underdeveloped. The present study conducted a comprehensive bibliometric analysis to reveal the research trends and knowledge structure pertaining to gut microbiota and AD over the past 30 years.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Data sources and retrieval strategies

The Web of Science Core Collection (WOSCC) database was selected as the data source due to its reputation as a high-quality online scientific resource, reaching across a diverse array of publications spanning multiple disciplines. It is widely accepted as one of the most optimal databases for performing bibliometrics.[27–29]

The studies on gut microbiota and AD were retrieved using the WOSCC database on October 20, 2024. The retrieval strategies were outlined as follows, with the inclusion of subject words such as gut, microbiota, and atopic dermatitis, along with their respective synonyms. The exact search strings used were summarized in Table S1, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/O173. The retrieval strategies were (TS = “gut” OR TS = “intestin*” OR TS = “gastrointestin*” OR TS = “gastro-intestin*”) AND (TS = “microbiot*” OR TS = “microbiome*” OR TS = “flora” OR TS = “microflora” OR TS = “bacteria” OR TS = “prebiotic” OR TS = “probiotic”) AND (TS = “eczema” OR TS = “atopic dermatitis” OR TS= “atopic eczema” OR TS= “atopic dermatit*” OR TS = “atopy”). All the research articles and reviews in English were retrieved. No additional filters were placed on the search. A total of 1782 documents published from January 01, 1988 to October 20, 2024 were obtained from the WOSCC. After excluding documents which did not meet the requirements of publication types and language, the remaining articles were assessed according to the title and abstract. To avoid bias caused by the frequent database update, data retrieval was performed on October 20, 2024, and the processed data were converted to text format and then imported into Bibliometric tools for the further analyses. Ultimately, 1672 articles were downloaded in text format from the WOSCC database for further bibliometric analysis. Therefore, through the application of innovative techniques and assessment of clinical significance, we conducted a comprehensive evaluation encompassing various factors such as authors, countries, and journals. Additionally, we utilized bibliometric methods to analyze the evolution and current trends in this field, aiming to provide scholars with a holistic framework and an overview of recent developments. The study may also forecast future research trends, and assist the medical system in conducting high-quality and more efficient research on therapeutic measures for patients.

2.2. Methodology and data analysis processes

The method of bibliometrics has been widely utilized in literature analysis for evaluating essential information from published literature.[30] By analyzing the authors, journals, institutions, countries, and keywords, the evolution trends and hotspots of a given field can be comprehensively understood.[31] Bibliometrix is a robust software that can be integrated with R Studio and R language, in addition to biblioshiny. Biblioshiny, developed in R 4.2.0 by the Institute for Statistics and Mathematics in Vienna, Austria (www.r-project.org), provides an online operating platform for bibliometrics.[32] Following inputting the raw materials into biblioshiny, it could assist in providing a quantitative evaluation and visual representation of core authors, sources, and documents for vast amounts of scientific data. Annual scientific production and average citations represented development trends in the field of gut microbiota and AD. Polynomial model fitting was used to estimate the publication output according to the publication year in 2024. The impacts of countries, institutions, journals, and authors were evaluated based on the visualization of various bibliometric parameters, such as annual publications, local/global citations, and H-index. Specifically, H-index is commonly utilized for assessing scholars’ scientific outputs and academic impacts. Inter-country and inter-author cooperative correlations were investigated. Country cooperative networks and author cooperative networks were constructed to show the collaboration relationships among them. A key word co-occurrence network was developed to show the hot topics in the field of gut microbiota and AD. The dimensionality reduction technique was used to show the conceptual structure of the most relevant key words, which can reveal the development trends and critical hot topics in the field of gut microbiota and AD.

3. Results

3.1. Analysis of annual publications

The initial literature retrieval yielded 1782 documents published between January 01, 1988 and October 20, 2024 from the WOSCC. Based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria, we downloaded 1672 articles and reviews. Finally, this study included 1672 documents downloaded from 1988 to 2024, authored by 7646 individuals and published in 554 journals. The average citation per document was 55.80, with a combined total of 61,478 references cited across all documents.

The quantity of articles published annually generally serves as an indicator of the level of attention devoted by researchers to a specific field. The annual publications on gut microbiota and AD, as depicted in Figure 1, were sporadic and consistently fewer than 10 before the year 2000, indicating the underdeveloped nature of this field. The number of publications, however, began to experience rapid growth after 2000 and peaked at over 170 outputs in 2022. The data retrieval date (October 20, 2024) accounted for the decline of publications in 2024. Furthermore, a polynomial function y = 0.1915 × 2 − 763.93x + 761863 (R2 = 0.9651, x is the year, y is the annual number of publications) was constructed to model the annual publication trend. An obvious association between publication counts and publication year was determined using polynomial model fitting. Based on the polynomial curve fitting, the counts for articles were predicted to reach around 163 in 2024.

This research field has been significantly propelled by the revolutionary advancements in AD immunotherapy and novel investigative methodologies. Recent evidence indicated that gut microbiota may play a critical role in modulating itch in AD through interactions between the gut, skin, and brain. The production of microbial metabolites and pro-inflammatory cytokines, along with a compromised immune response, may result in the regulation of histamine-independent itch.[33]

The highest globally and locally cited document, “Probiotics in primary prevention of atopic disease: a randomized placebo-controlled trial,” by Kalliomäki (1800 global citations), showed that the frequency of atopic eczema within the probiotic group was half that of the placebo group.[35] It suggested interpretations Lactobacillus GG showed benefits in the prevention of atopic disorders in early life with high risk. Hence, gut microbiota might be a potential source of immunomodulators and natural probiotics for preventing AD.[36]

The second highest globally cited document, “Prebiotic effects: metabolic and health benefits,” by Roberfroid in 2010 (1455 global citations), validated and expanded the original idea of the prebiotic concept that can be translated into “prebiotic effects.”[37] The prebiotic effects have been demonstrated to associate with regulation of biomarkers and activities of the immune system. In infant nutrition, the prebiotic effects included significant changes of gut microbiota composition, especially the increase of fecal concentrations of bifidobacterial, which may improve general well-being and reduce the incidence of AD.

The second highest locally cited document, “Distinct patterns of neonatal gut microflora in infants in whom atopy was and was not developing,” by Kalliomäki in 2001 (256 local citations), investigated whether differences in early gut microbiota precede the later development of atopic sensitization.[36] It demonstrated that differences in the neonatal gut microbiota precede the development of atopy, indicating an important role of the balance of gut microbiota for the maturation of host immunity to the nonatopic mode.

The third highest locally cited document, “Low diversity of the gut microbiota in infants with atopic eczema,” by Abrahamsson in 2012, assessed gut microbial diversity and characterized the dominant bacteria in stool in relation to the development of atopic eczema during the first year of life.[38] They identified that, low gut microbial diversity during the first month of life was related to the development of atopic eczema.

Specifically, “stratum-corneum lipids,” “stratum-corneum,” “associations,” “acne,” “health,” “gut microbiome,” “skin,” “diversity,” “chain fatty-acids,” “prebiotics,” “gut microbiota,” “food allergy,” and “inflammation” emerged after 2020, and have continuously appeared in related articles in the last 5 years, which suggested that these terms might become the trend topics to explore in the future. Stratum-corneum lipids can signal between resident and infiltrating cutaneous immunocytes, which may drive inflammation and its resolution in response to pathogens.[39] Stratum-corneum lipids were involved in the gut-skin axis, signaling between the 2 barriers. 4. Discussion

4.1. General information

Investigating the correlations between gut microbiota and AD, especially in infants, may provide novel insights into possible prevention and treatment methods. A recent bibliometric analysis based on 804 articles showed potential correlations between gut microbiota and AD, highlighting the growing interests in potential mechanisms and treatment strategies associated with the gut microbiota in AD therapy.[41] It reported that scholars have investigated the correlations between gut microbiota and AD at the early stage, and gradually paid more attention to the effects of microbial ecological agents. However, the above study only analyzed 804 gut microbiota and AD-related documents from 2014 to 2023, only accounting for a small part of the long history of this research field, which may cause incomplete included papers and certain bias.

In our study, 1672 documents that were associated with AD and gut microbiota between January 01, 1988 and October 20, 2024 were derived from the WOSCC database. The annual publications continued to increase, reflecting the popularity of the gut microbiota and AD field in the past few decades, which highlighted the great development potential of this research field. The most prolific and impactive author was “Isolauri E,” who authored 35 articles with 1152 local citations and an H-index of 28. Isolauri E and her colleagues performed several randomized placebo-controlled trials to reveal the effects of probiotics in primary prevention of atopic diseases, such as atopic eczema in children.[35,42] They showed that, gut microbiota may be hitherto unexplored sources of natural immunomodulators and probiotics, for preventing atopic diseases. It was a milestone in the development history of gut microbiota and AD.

Across all the contributed countries, the USA ranked the first in the publication counts, and the USA was also the most impactive country. The USA was the home to most of the relative affiliations, including the University of California System and Harvard University. China came in second in publication counts, showing relatively high single country publications and MCPs. Despite China’s significant engagement and enthusiasm over the past 30 years, there remained a discernible gap in the international impact and global visibility compared with several developed countries. It underscored the need for Chinese scholars to further improve the quality and writing proficiency of their publications, aiming for substantive impacts rather than mere publication counts. Multiple countries have devoted tremendous efforts to overcome the obstacles in this field, and the USA had the strongest correlations with the UK (n = 31), Canada (n = 24), China (n = 21), Germany (n = 21), and Netherlands (n = 21). The USA exhibited the highest MCP ratio, indicating that numerous papers from the USA were published by collaborating with other countries. The highest citation number of the USA might be attributed to the impact of the highly regarded source Journal of Allergy and Clinical Immunology in this research field. This may partially explain why the US led in terms of citation counts. In general, global cooperation should be strengthen further in the future.

When it comes to the journals, Journal of Allergy and Clinical Immunology, Clinical and Experimental Allergy, and Pediatric Allergy and Immunology were the journals with the highest publication number. Obviously, the most locally cited journals were Journal of Allergy and Clinical Immunology and Clinical and Experimental Allergy. Through comprehensive bibliometric analysis and extensive literature review in this field, we have summarized the following 2 hotspots: The gut microbiota can affect the occurrence of atopic dermatitis through the immune metabolism and endocrine pathways of the gut-skin axis; Emerging gut microbiota-based treatment methods for AD, including the applications of probiotics, pre- or post-probiotics, immunotherapies, phage therapies, and fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) work against AD as compared with traditional therapy. Then, we delved into this field and provided a more profound discussion of the keyword analysis and thematic evolution to reveal the dynamic hotspots in this field. 4.2. Core pathways of the gut-skin axis in AD development

4.2.1. Potential mechanisms of how gut microbiota influences the skin

Since dermatologists John H. Stokes first reported that alterations of gut microbiota might induce skin inflammation causing conditions, various studies have supported the theory of gut–skin axis.[39,43] In patients with acne vulgaris, overgrowth of bacteria within the small intestine was 10 times more frequent than in normal populations.[43] Compared to control samples without skin diseases, more than 60 % of patients with acne showed positive reactivity to the stool-isolated coliform bacteria.[43] The findings suggested toxins from the gut microbiota might be a possible problem for AD patients.

Gut-skin axis has been implicated in various skin conditions such as acne and AD. Therefore, the toxic substances produced by the gut microbiota have been shown to impact and exacerbate symptoms of AD through specific pathways including immunologic, metabolite, and neuroendocrine pathways. Further researches are needed to fully investigate the underlying mechanisms.

4.2.2. Immunologic pathways

In aspects of immunity against S. aureus, the most prevalent bacterial strain influencing AD, correlations have been established between the skin and gut microbiota, with S. aureus identified as the predominant pathogen in the skin of AD patients. Further, a previous birth cohort study has indicated that colonization by S. aureus strains may play a crucial part in the prevention of AD in infancy, highlighting the significance of early exposure to gut microbiota.[6] The exposure of the skin to S. aureus has been shown to promote the maturation of infants’ immune systems. Further, the presence of S. aureus species on the skin may exacerbate preexisting symptoms of AD. Prior to the onset of AD, the aggregation of symbiotic S. aureus strains on the mucosal membrane may confer protection effectiveness via immune-stimulation.[6] It provided evidence for the potential interconnection between the skin and gut, mediated by the regulation of immune homeostasis through the gut microbiota.

The unique gut microbiota and related metabolites, including polysaccharide A and retinoic acid from Faecalibacterium prausnitzii, Bacteroides fragilis, and other bacterial species within Clostridium clusters IV as well as XI, may enhance the accumulation of lymphocytes and regulatory T cells to promote anti-inflammatory responses. Moreover, SCFAs, particularly butyrate, played a regulatory part in the apoptosis and activation of immunocytes.[44]

A recent analysis conducted by utilizing probiotics, such as Lactobacillus rhamnosus, also validated the ameliorating effects of the gut microbiota on immunological skin irritation.[44] It showed that gut microbiota dysbiosis of mice caused by repeated water-avoiding stress was significantly eliminated after probiotics treatments.[44]

4.2.3. Metabolite pathways

Metabolites, such as SCFAs, generated from gut microbiota or supplemented orally, also accounted for the correlation between the skin and gut through the gut microbiota. Additionally, SCFAs generated from gut microbiota, including Akkermansia muciniphila, played an important part in the pathology and etiology of AD, which may account for the correlations with the skin immunity systems. Moreover, in some animal experimental analyses, linoleic acid as well as 10-hydroxy-cis-12-octadecenoic acid demonstrated efficacy in alleviating symptoms of AD and modulating the gut microbiota.[45] Additionally, it indicated that 3 distinct subclusters of the neonatal gut microbiota (NGM1–3) and related metabolic byproducts played an important part during the initial phase of allergic sensitization.[46] Across the 3 subclusters, NGM3 was associated with various allergic sensitizations as well as exhibited a decreased abundance of Facalibacterium, Akkermansia, and Bifidobacterium.[6] For instance, NGM3 was found to be abundant in 12,13-dihydroxy-9Z-octadecenoic acid, a metabolic product that has been shown to have inflammatory effects on immune regulation. Additionally, there was an increase of 12,13-dihydroxy-9Z-octadecenoic acid in the protective layer of vernix caseosa, a white waxy coating present on the skin of infants. The evidence supported the presence of a metabolite pathway in the gut-skin axis.[6]

4.2.4. Neuroendocrine pathways

The mucosal lining of the gastrointestinal tract, like the skin, was constantly exposed to the environmental factors, including micro-organisms and food. Further, one of the primary functions of the gut and skin was to prevent the entry of pathogens, and the microbiota present in these sites played a crucial role in eliminating these pathogens through immune mechanisms. Therefore, maintaining a stable microbiota and proper balance in these organs was essential for overall health. Further, gut microbiota could affect one another via the neuroendocrine signaling. The phenomenon may manifest via 2 different pathways: either direct or indirect.[6] The generation of tryptophan from gut microbiota, which may lead to skin itching in patients with atopic dermatitis, serves as an example of direct signaling. Besides, γ-aminobutyric acid produced by Lactobacillus as well as Bifidobacterium in the gut exerted an inhibitory effect on skin itching.[47]

Cortisol was a critical stress hormone in humans and it could modify the permeability and barrier function of the gut epithelium through altering the abundance of gut microbiota.[48] Further, cortisol could modulate the level of circulating neuroendocrine molecules, including trimethylamine, tryptamine, and serotonin, thereby enhancing the skin barriers.[49] In intestinal wall disorders, intestinal microbial metabolites accessed the bloodstream, accumulated within the skin, which may disrupt skin homeostasis.[44]

4.3. Perspective of gut microbiota-based treatments for AD

4.3.1. Gut microbiota-targeted therapies

4.3.1.1. Probiotics

The largest number of orally administered probiotics may pass through the hostile environment of the gastrointestinal tract and were released post approximately 7 days, which can interact with the mucous membranes where over 70% of immunocytes were located.[50] Based on the probiotic strains, interlukin-12 (IL-12), interlukin-18 (IL-18), and tumor necrosis factor-α could be produced for initiating immune-triggered signals and promoting the expression level of anti-inflammatory cytokines to induce signals for immune tolerance.[51]

The combination of L. rhamnosus 19070-2 as well as L. reuteri DSM 122460 has been utilized for the treatment of AD, demonstrating more significant influences in the patients with positive skin prick test responses and elevated IgE level.[52] A previous study conducted in children aged 1 to 12 showed that, administration of L. plantarum CJLP133 strain resulted in decreased levels of interferon-γ, eosinophil, and interlukin-4, leading to reduced AD scores.[53] In another study, the group that received probiotics showed a lower SCORing Atopic Dermatitis score 4 months post discontinuing the use of L. paracasei, L. fermentum, as well as their combination for children compared to placebo group.[54]

Supplementation of Lactobacillus brevis SBC8803 in rats can lead to a paler color of the cutaneous arterial sympathetic nerve as well as an increase in skin blood flow. The heightened release of serotonin from intestinal cells is likely to have resulted in the subsequent stimulation of neural signaling pathways.[44] Additionally, the influence was observed in a clinical study of human.[44] Following 12 weeks of oral supplementation with Lactobacillus brevis SBC8803, human subjects showed a decrease in trans-epidermal water loss, indicating improved skin barrier function. The maintenance of skin barrier function is crucial in AD, and the administration of oral probiotic supplement has been shown to be beneficial in improving this function and subsequently alleviating AD symptoms.[44]

Dysbiosis in the gut microbiota induced gluten sensitivity and low serum vitamin D (VD) levels.[55] Further, adjusting the serum levels of gluten or VD can also be a potential therapeutic intervention for AD. Several probiotics may hydrolyze gluten polypeptides, while others may elevate the VD levels and promote the expression of the VD receptors.[55,56]

A uniform prescription of probiotics might have little efficacy and adverse effects, because the causes of AD differed among individuals. Therefore, individual patients should be carefully evaluated to determine the causes and characteristics of AD, and proper probiotics are required to be prescribed.

4.3.1.2. Pre- or postbiotics

Postbiotics have been shown in recent studies to suppress cellular inflammatory responses. Probiotics work by increasing the synthesis of SCFAs, propionate, and butyrate, which alleviates AD symptoms. In addition to their anti-inflammatory properties, SCFAs could also improve the Th1/Th2 ratio, decrease the production of harmful fermentation products, and boost intestinal IgA secretion.[57] Prebiotic oligosaccharide mixes given to infants’ meals prior to their first year of life were found to lower the average incidence of AD by 44%.[58]

Postbiotics have been demonstrated in earlier research to reduce cell inflammation. Since postbiotics are byproducts of gut microbiota, they could act via the gut-to-skin metabolite pathway.[59,60] LactoSporin, an extracellular metabolin isolated from the fermented broth of Bacillus coagulans MTCC 5856, was investigated as a postbiotic for the management of acne vulgaris. The possible mechanisms of LactoSporin as a potential antimicrobial agent involved inducing a drop in pH, inhibiting microbial biofilm formation, and depleting ions from the targeted cells.[61] The use of LactoSporin in the treatment of acne vulgaris suggested its possible efficacy in managing AD as well. However, as the research was performed in vitro, the precise mechanisms regarding the impact of postbiotics and their influence on the systemic immune responses of AD, particularly within the intestines, remained unknown. The postbiotic compounds derived from Lactobacillus have been demonstrated to exhibit immune-regulating capabilities through the upregulation of Th1-associated cytokines and downregulation of Th2-associated cytokines.[62]

4.3.2. Fecal microbiota transplantation

According to previous research, FMT has emerged as a promising method for controlling gut microbiota.[63] FMT involved the transfer of functional bacteria from the fecal matter of the healthy donors into the patient’s gut for restoring the balance of gut microbiota and address disorders associated with gut microbial imbalances. Recent research indicated that the gut microbiota may have an impact on various skin conditions. Alterations in acne and AD were observed in recipients of FMT, in addition to hair loss as well as psoriasis.[64–66]

A previous study used an AD mouse model to explore the causes and effects of FMT on AD-skin lesions.[67] By raising the ratio of Firmicutes to Bacteroidetes and the quantity of butyric-producing bacteria, it demonstrated that FMT had positive and long-lasting effects on the gut microbial assemblages of AD mice. Furthermore, following FMT, there was an increase in the relative abundance of gut microbiota-regulated functional pathways linked to immune function, cell growth and death, energy, amino acid, lipid, and carbohydrate metabolisms. It showed that FMT treatment affected lesions resembling AD, which can enhance gut microbiota homeostasis. It may address the instructive impacts of FMT on alleviating AD in clinic.

4.3.3. Phage therapy

The term “phage therapy” describes the use of bacteriophages, which can infect and eliminate the host bacteria, to cure specific bacterial infections. The benefit of utilizing bacteriophages is their host specificity, which means that they don’t significantly affect the typical microbiotas of plants and animals. Though its safety is already completely assured, phage therapy has not yet been utilized clinically to treat AD; hence, this study will only discuss it as a potential future application. It has recently been demonstrated that phages, a component of the human gut microbiota, interact with the gut microbiota to influence human immune function.[68]

Because Enterococcus faecalis, which can produce toxins (cytolysin), is abundant in the gut microbiota of mice, phage therapy was employed in a previous work to treat liver disorders.[69] The results of these investigations may serve as the foundation for the application of phage treatment to treat issues related to the dysbiosis of the gut microbiota or the intestinal residence of pathogenic bacteria.

However, as it is still unknown if the gut microbiota is the precise etiology of AD, this treatment is fraught with numerous unknowns and restrictions. Every treatment based on the gut microbiota has drawbacks and restrictions. The biggest obstacle facing this field going forward appears to be overcoming these constraints.

4.3.4. Biologic therapy

The therapy landscape for AD has been transformed by the biologics targeting cytokines. AD is induced by multiple cytokines, including interlukin-4, IL-5, and IL-13. The effects of these cytokines are regulated by intracellular pathways of Janus kinase (JAK)/signal transducer and activator of transcription. JAK family is composed of tyrosine kinase 2, JAK1, JAK2, and JAK3. The agents inhibiting JAK1 and/or JAK2, upadacitinib and baricitinib, are currently approved for the therapy of AD patients. A previous bibliometric analysis provided an over-view of the top 100 most frequently cited studies on JAK inhibitors from 2012 to 2023.[70] It showed that the keyword “atopic dermatitis” was widely mentioned in 2016, and new trends emerged in 2018, with “baricitinib” being more frequently mentioned.[70] Further, various clinical trials have demonstrated the efficacy and safety of once-daily 15 or 30 mg of upadacitinib for moderate-to-severe AD.[71,72] Previous studies also showed the effectiveness and safety of oral upadacitinib plus topical corticosteroids in Japanese patients with moderate-to-severe AD.[73–76] Teppei Hagino et al also evaluated the efficacy and safety of baricitinib in real-world practice, and it showed that baricitinib was well tolerated for AD patients and achieved satisfactory effects compared with previous clinical trials.[77]

The higher therapy response in lower limbs was identified in baricitinib treatment,[77] while lower response in head and neck was identified in dupilumab treatment.[78] The possible differences of microbiota across different anatomical areas may be associated with the site-dependent therapy response. Future research may reveal anatomical area-specific findings in gut microbiota associated with the site-dependent therapy response, which can provide potential mechanisms for the site-dependent therapy response.

4.4. Emerging topics and future trends

Since 2017, there have been notable bursts in citations related to “oral tolerance” and “short-chain fatty acids.” According to a previous study, feeding peanuts to babies with severe eczema or egg allergies between the ages of 4 and 6 months may increase oral tolerance and lower the risk of AD.[79] Further, regulation of gut microbiota via pre- or postbiotics has emerged as a promising approach for promoting intestinal tolerance. A recent study investigated the correlations between AD and gut microbiota composition short-chain fatty acids in 6-month-old infants, which revealed positive correlations between the abundance of Streptococcus and negative correlations with Clostridium. Besides, infants with transient AD were identified to have decreased levels of valerate and butyrate, which may impair the epithelial barrier of gut.[80]

Recently, there has been a surge in interest in “prebiotics,” “management,” and “dysbiosis” indicating that AD therapies via modulating gut microbiota have become research hotspots.[81] A recent study reported that infant formula enriched with specific prebiotics may mimic the benefits of breast milk on gut microbiota.[82] Another analysis used an AD-like mouse model to explore the effects of orally administered prebiotics, which showed correlations between prebiotic treatment and favorable alterations in gut microbiota.[83] Further, a previous study demonstrated that integrating probiotics and topical corticosteroids may alleviate symptoms of AD.[84]

Recent researches have investigated the pro-inflammatory gut microbiota in children with food allergies, usually rich in genes associated with bacterial lipopolysaccharide production. Specifically, active Helicobacter pylori was suggested as the predominant pathogen in allergic children, which may cause the dysregulation of immune systems and changed food tolerance via correlations with gut microbiota. Emerging therapeutic strategies, such as postbiotics, prebiotics, probiotics, synthetic prebiotics, and FMT, are promising for regulating gut microbiota in food allergies.[5,85,86]

Nevertheless, the effectiveness of prebiotics/probiotics/symbiotics in modulating gut microbiota for preventing and treating AD remained controversial.[87,88] In the near future, clinicians can consider using the above regulators as adjunct treatments to prevent AD.

4.5. Limitations

Despite achieving several meaningful findings in this study, flaws inevitably exist due to the data acquisition methods and limitations inherent to bibliometric analyses. The documents were all retrieved only from the WOSCC database, and thus the documents relevant to the field in other sources are not available. Further, non-English publications were excluded from this study, which may ignore some potential important findings. Additionally, documents that were published after our retrieval date could not be involved in our analysis. In the future research, we can collect the articles from various different sources to provide a more comprehensive analysis of this field.

5. Conclusion

This study utilized bibliometric analysis to synthesize knowledge regarding the role of gut microbiota in AD and produced a visualization of the collected data. More and more articles have been published in this field, suggesting the increasing popularity of the research on AD and gut microbiota. “Stratum-corneum lipids,” “probiotics,” “prebiotics,” “fecal microbiota transplantation,” “phage therapy,” “short chain fatty-acids,” “biologic therapy,” and “skin inflammation” have become the trend topics in recent years. Unveiling the pathological and molecular mechanisms and novel therapeutic methods by targeting gut microbiota for treating AD served as popular research directions, which may be the future trends of this field. Further evidence is required for supporting the applications of microbial ecological agents as the alternative therapeutic strategies for patients with AD. This study comprehensively investigated the literature on AD and gut microbiota spanning the last 3 decades, analyzed the research hotspots and future directions, which can provide references for further research and drive medical innovation in the near future.
