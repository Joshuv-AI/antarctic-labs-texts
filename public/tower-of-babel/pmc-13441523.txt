From Non-linear Prognostic Visualization to Frailty-aware Shared Decision-making in Aneurysmal Subarachnoid Hemorrhage
Authors: Fusao IKAWA
Published: 2026

Dear Editor: We sincerely thank Dr. Demetriades1) for his thoughtful and supportive letter regarding our review article, “Chronological and Biological Age in Predicting Outcomes of Older Patients in Neurosurgery.”2) We greatly appreciate his emphasis on the clinical value of visualizing non-linear prognostic relationships in aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage (aSAH), and we are encouraged that generalized additive model (GAM)-based visualization can serve as a practical bridge between statistical modeling and bedside communication.

A central aim of our review was to highlight that chronological age alone is an incomplete proxy for vulnerability, and that biological age―often operationalized through frailty―adds important prognostic information across neurosurgical conditions. In elective neurosurgery, frailty indices can support risk stratification and individualized decision-making; however, in aSAH, the decision environment is distinct: time-sensitive, emotionally charged, and strongly influenced by acute severity (e.g., World Federation of Neurosurgical Societies grade) and treatment modality. In this context, visual tools can provide an evidence-based framework for discussions with families, who must frequently make rapid choices under uncertainty.

Dr. Demetriades1) rightly notes that the GAM/partial dependence approach represents a methodological step beyond conventional logistic regression outputs, which may obscure clinically meaningful non-linearities. As discussed in our review, the association between age and outcome in aSAH is not simply linear; rather, risk acceleration at older ages and heterogeneity across severity strata can be more clearly conveyed using spline-based methods. Importantly, our intention is not to suggest that visualization “solves” prognostication―clinicians remain accountable for integrating clinical context and patient values―but that visualization can reduce unstructured variation and improve transparency in how age-related risk is communicated.

We are grateful for the 2 constructive directions proposed by Dr. Demetriades.1)

First, interactive interfaces and digital implementation. We agree that the clinical impact of prognostic visualization will be maximized when it becomes accessible at the point of care. Static figures in print, while illustrative, cannot fully accommodate individual combinations of covariates or institutional workflow constraints. Translation into an interactive interface (e.g., a web-based calculator or an electronic health record-integrated module) would allow clinicians to input relevant variables and generate a patient-specific risk curve or risk profile in real time. At the same time, implementation must be performed carefully: visual risk displays can unintentionally amplify misunderstanding if the design does not align with how clinicians and families interpret probability, uncertainty, and time horizons. Therefore, any digital tool should be developed with attention to (i) clear labeling of outcomes and time points, (ii) explicit depiction of uncertainty (e.g., confidence intervals or credible bands), (iii) avoidance of misleading precision, and (iv) user-centered testing in real clinical conversations. In short, visual elegance should not come at the expense of clinical clarity.

Second, extending non-linear visualization to other outcomes in geriatric neurosurgery. We also agree that applying similar methods to longer-term functional dependency, discharge disposition, and post-acute trajectories would be highly valuable, particularly in elderly patients for whom “survival” alone is often not the primary goal. In aSAH, families frequently ask not only “Will the patient live?” but “What kind of life is realistically possible?” Visualizing outcomes such as functional independence, care needs, and discharge destination―ideally across time―may better support value-concordant decisions and care planning. The methodological framework is adaptable: spline-based models, stratified by severity and treatment, can be developed for outcomes beyond short-term functional status, provided that high-quality follow-up data are available.

Dr. Demetriades1) also highlights an essential point regarding shared decision-making: prognostic tools should reduce, not compound, uncertainty. We concur and would add that evaluation should extend beyond discrimination and calibration. Future studies should incorporate metrics such as decision quality, decisional conflict, patient/family comprehension, clinician confidence, and decision-curve analysis to quantify clinical utility. Pragmatic evaluation embedded in routine care―rather than purely retrospective validation―may be particularly informative.

Finally, we would welcome the opportunity to collaborate with Dr. Demetriades and colleagues to advance this agenda through multicenter validation and implementation. Specifically, a harmonized minimum dataset for aSAH (including WFNS grade, treatment strategy, key complications, standardized functional outcomes, and available frailty measures) would enable external validation, transportability assessment, and principled recalibration of non-linear models. Where frailty metrics are available, incremental prognostic value beyond chronological age can be quantified within severity strata, and identify patient subgroups in whom frailty meaningfully shifts outcome trajectories. We believe Dr. Demetriades' expertise in aSAH prognostication and his focus on shared decision-making make him an ideal partner to co-develop these clinically interpretable tools and to prospectively evaluate their impact on communication and decision quality.

In closing, we thank Dr. Demetriades for advancing the discussion from “better prediction” to “better communication.” We share his conviction that interpretable, non-linear visualization is a practical pathway toward frailty-aware, patient-centered neurosurgical care―particularly in aSAH, where decisions are urgent, and the stakes are profound.
