A novel unit-asymmetric distribution based on correlated Fréchet random variables
Roberto Vila and Felipe Quintino

Abstract
In this paper, we propose a new distribution with unitary support which can be characterized as a ratio of the type W = X 1 / ( X 1 + X 2 ) W=X_{1}/(X_{1}+X_{2}) , where ( X 1 , X 2 ) ⊤ (X_{1},X_{2})^{\top} follows a bivariate extreme distribution with Fréchet margins, that is, X 1 X_{1} and X 2 X_{2} are two correlated Fréchet random variables. Some mathematical properties such as identifiability, symmetry, stochastic representation, characterization as a ratio, moments, stress-strength probability, quantiles, and the maximum likelihood method are rigorously analyzed. Two applications of the ratio distribution are discussed.

Introduction

Probability models capable of handling data with unit support have garnered considerable attention in statistical research in recent years. The limitations on the support of real-world data naturally arise depending on the phenomena studied. One method to develop new unit models is by applying suitable transformations to already-known models. For example,
Z=X/(X+1)
,
Z=\exp(-X)
and
Z=X/(X+Y)
have the support
(0,1)
, when
X
and
Y
are positive variables.
Applications of unit models are found in several areas.

Ghitanya et al., (2019)
proposed the unit-inverse Gaussian distribution and applied this new model to the Municipal Human Development Index of some Brazilian cities.

Bourguignon et al., (2024)
reparameterized the three-parameter Generalized Beta distribution and proposed a new parametric quantile model to analyze a real COVID-19 dataset from Chile.

Martínez-Flórez et al., (2024)
introduced the unit-bimodal Birnbaum-Saunders distribution. They modeled the distribution of the body fat percentage of Australian athletes competing in different sports.

Vila et al., (2024)
proposed a class of unit‑log‑symmetric models to analyzing an internet access data.

Recently introduced unit models also include the power unit inverse Lindley distribution
(
Gemeay et al.,, 2024
)
, the unit extended exponential distribution
(
Ragab et al.,, 2024
)
, the log-cosine-power unit distribution
(
Nasiru et al.,, 2024
)
, the Gumbel–Logistic unit distribution
(
Stojanović et al.,, 2024
)
, and the bimodal beta distribution
(
Vila et al., 2024b,
)
.
The literature has widely studied models based on Birnbaum-Saunders (BS) distribution, and unit-BS models were also proposed
(
Mazucheli et al.,, 2018
;
Martínez-Flórez et al.,, 2024
;
Vila et al.,, 2025
, cf.)
.

New regression models for data on the unit interval have garnered significant attention from researchers in the past few years
(
Benites et al.,, 2022
;
Hussein et al.,, 2023
;
Karakaya et al.,, 2024
;
Mazucheli et al.,, 2021
;
Santoro et al.,, 2024
;
Stojanović et al., 2024b,
, cf.)
.
Another important class of models to highlight is the multivariate models for unit cylinder data, where the dependency between random variables can be formally described
(
Martínez-Flórez et al.,, 2022
;
Vila et al., 2024c,
;
Vila et al., 2024d,
, cf.)
.

As previously mentioned, models for unit data can naturally be constructed from ratios of random variables. Along these lines,
Bekker et al., (2014)
proposed the type I distribution of the ratio of independent Weibullized generalized beta-prime variables, while

Nadarajah and Kotz, (2006)
studied the ratio of independent Fréchet random variables.

In this work, we introduce a new class of unit models that arises from a ratio of two correlated Fréchet random variables.
Recall that the Fréchet distribution is an extreme value model used for modeling extreme events related to maximums of independent random samples.
Applications of this distribution are found in finance, natural catastrophes, and equipment failures, among others.
The books
Elshamy, (1992)
;
Embrechts et al., (2013)
;
Galambos, (1978)
;
de Haan and Ferreira, (2006)
;
Resnick, (2008)
provide extensive coverage for a detailed study of extreme value theory.

In bivariate data modeling, it is common to focus on comparing both random variables. For instance, in Finance, we may compare two assets
(
Quintino et al.,, 2023
, cf.)
, while in Engineering, we might examine the relationship between a material’s strength and the stress it is subjected to
(
Kundu and Raqab,, 2009
, cf.)
.
Several methods can be used to make these comparisons. One such method is based on stress-strength reliability (SSR). Given two random variables,
X
and
Y
, we can compare them by analyzing the probability that
\mathbb{P}(X<Y)
is greater or less than
1/2
.
Nadarajah, (2003)
and
Abbas and Tang, (2014)
studied SSR for independent Fréchet random variables
X
and
Y
.
An alternative approach is to analyze the ratio

X/Y
directly. In this context,
Nadarajah and Kotz, (2006)
examined the ratio of independent Fréchet random variables. They derived the cumulative distribution function (CDF) and the percentiles of the ratio using special functions such as the error function and the generalized hypergeometric function.

It also is possible to compare
X
and
Y
when both are dependent random variables. In this context, we can model the dependence between the random variables via a copula approach. Several applications of bivariate copula models for extreme value distributions are presented in the Literature
(see, for example,
Lima et al.,, 2024
, and the references therein)
.
Furthermore, asymptotic constructions, characterizations, and various properties of multivariate extreme value distributions are extensively discussed in
Elshamy, (1992)
and
Resnick, (2008)
.
In particular, a class of bivariate distributions with Fréchet marginals presented in
Elshamy, (1992)
served as the basis for constructing the new distribution with unit support proposed in this work. A similar approach could be applied to the bivariate classes with Fréchet marginals presented in
Resnick, (2008)
.

This paper aims to propose a new class of distributions with unit support. Our construction generalizes the ratio model proposed by
Nadarajah and Kotz, (2006)
, which was based on the ratio of independent Fréchet random variables. A Monte Carlo simulation study is presented to validate the maximum likelihood estimator of the new distribution. Finally, applications to real-world datasets are discussed.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows: Section
2
provides an introduction to the preliminaries, including the definition of the univariate Fréchet distribution and a bivariate distribution with Fréchet marginals. Section
3
introduces the new model, referred to as the unit-Fréchet distribution.
Section
4
, on the other hand, deals with the
derivation of the new unit distribution and its mathematical properties such as identifiability, symmetry, stochastic representation, characterization as a ratio, moments, stress-strength probability, quantiles, and the maximum likelihood estimation for the parameters.
In Section
5
, we discuss Monte Carlo simulations to evaluate the proposed estimator, while Section
6
addresses the modeling of two real-world scenarios involving football data and income-consumption data set. The final section presents our conclusions.

Preliminaries

2.1
The univariate Fréchet distribution

Let
X_{1},X_{2},\cdots
be a sequence of independent and identically distributed random variables with a common distribution
G(\cdot)
.
Consider the partial maximum
X_{(n)}=\max\{X_{j};~j=1,\cdots,n\}
.
The derivation of extreme distributions (asymptotic distribution functions for
X_{(n)}
) is based on the asymptotic approximation of the normalized partial maximum.
More specifically,

\frac{X_{(n)}-b_{n}}{a_{n}}\overset{d}{\rightarrow}F(x),~~\mbox{as}~~n\rightarrow\infty,

(1)

where
a_{n}>0
,
b_{n}\in\mathbb{R}
,
F(\cdot)
is a nondegenerate distribution function and
\overset{d}{\rightarrow}
denotes convergence in distribution.

Fisher and Tippet Theorem
(
Resnick,, 2008
, cf.)
guarantees us that there are three extreme values distributions
F(\cdot)
satisfying (
1
), the Fréchet, reversed Weibull, and Gumbel distributions.
The conditions on the distribution function
G(\cdot)
for (
1
) to hold true are well-documented and can be referenced in
Resnick, (2008)
;
Galambos, (1978)
.
Applications of these distributions are found in finance, natural catastrophes, and equipment failures, among others
Embrechts et al., (2013)
.

A random variable
X
has Fréchet distribution with location parameter
\mu\in\mathbb{R}
, scale parameter
\sigma>0
, and shape parameter
\alpha>0
, denoted by
X\sim
Fréchet
(\mu,\sigma,\alpha)
, if its cumulative distribution function (CDF) and probability density function (PDF), respectively, are given by

F(x;\mu,\sigma,\alpha)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}0,&x<\mu,\\
\exp\left\{-\left(\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}\right)^{-\alpha}\right\},&x\geq\mu,\end{array}\right.

and

f(x;\mu,\sigma,\alpha)=\frac{\alpha}{\sigma}\left(\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}\right)^{-\alpha-1}F(x;\mu,\sigma,\alpha)\mathbbm{1}_{(\mu,\infty)}(x),

where
\mathbbm{1}_{A}
denotes the indicator function on the set
A
.

shows the behavior of
f(x;\mu,\sigma,\alpha)
for some parameter choices.

2.2
A bivariate distribution with Fréchet marginals

Let
(X_{1},Y_{1}),(X_{2},Y_{2}),\cdots
be a sequence of independent and identically distributed bivariate random vectors with the same joint CDF
G(x,y)
. We consider the pair

(X_{(n)},Y_{(n)}):=\left(\max\{X_{j};~j=1,\cdots,n\},\max\{Y_{j};~j=1,\cdots,n\}\right).

Analogously to the univariate case, we are interested in the (nondegenerate) limit distribution satisfying

\left(\frac{X_{(n)}-b_{n}}{a_{n}},\frac{Y_{(n)}-d_{n}}{c_{n}}\right)\overset{d}{\rightarrow}F(x,y),~~\mbox{as}~~n\rightarrow\infty,

(2)

where
a_{n}>0
and
c_{n}>0
. If a joint CDF
F(x,y)
satisfies (
2
), we call it a bivariate extreme value distribution.

Two general forms for the bivariate extreme value distributions
F(x,y)
in terms of the marginal distributions were described in
Gumbel and Mustafi, (1967)

(
Elshamy,, 1992
, see also)
.

We say that a random vector
(X,Y)
follows a bivariate extreme distribution with Fréchet margins
(as discussed in
Elshamy,, 1992
, p. 14, Item 21)
if its joint CDF is given by

\displaystyle F_{X,Y}(x,y)=\exp\left\{-\left({x\over\sigma_{1}}\right)^{-\alpha}-\left({y\over\sigma_{2}}\right)^{-\alpha}+\rho\left[\left({x\over\sigma_{1}}\right)^{\alpha}+\left({y\over\sigma_{2}}\right)^{\alpha}\right]^{-1}\right\},

(3)

where
x\geqslant 0
,
y\geqslant 0
;

\sigma_{1}>0,\sigma_{2}>0,\alpha>0
and
0\leqslant\rho\leqslant 1
. The parameter
\rho
represents the degree of association between
X
and
Y
. A value of
\rho=0
indicates that
X
and
Y
are independent with distributions Fréchet
(0,\sigma_{1},\alpha)
and Fréchet
(0,\sigma_{2},\alpha)
, respectively. Figure

and

3
The unit Fréchet distribution

We say that a continuous random variable
W
, with support
(0,1)
, follows a unit-extreme (UF) distribution with parameter vector
\bm{\theta}=(\sigma,\alpha,\rho)^{\top}
,
\sigma>0
,
\alpha>0
and
0\leqslant\rho\leqslant 1
, denoted by
W\sim{\rm UF}(\bm{\theta})
, if its PDF is given by (for
0<w<1
)

\displaystyle f_{W}(w;\bm{\theta})={\alpha\over\sigma^{\alpha}}\,s^{\alpha-1}(s+1)^{2}\left\{\displaystyle{\displaystyle 2\left({s^{\alpha}\over\sigma^{\alpha}}+1\right)^{2}-\rho\left[\left({s^{\alpha}\over\sigma^{\alpha}}\right)^{2}+1\right]\over\displaystyle\left[\left({s^{\alpha}\over\sigma^{\alpha}}+1\right)^{2}-\rho\,{s^{\alpha}\over\sigma^{\alpha}}\right]^{2}}-{\displaystyle 1\over\displaystyle\left({s^{\alpha}\over\sigma^{\alpha}}+1\right)^{2}}\right\},\quad s={w\over 1-w}.

(4)

As

\displaystyle{\partial{s^{\alpha}\over\sigma^{\alpha}}\over\partial w}={\alpha\over\sigma^{\alpha}}\,s^{\alpha-1}(s+1)^{2},

by the chain rule, the PDF of
W
is written as

\displaystyle f_{W}(w;\bm{\theta})={\partial G({s^{\alpha}\over\sigma^{\alpha}};\rho)\over\partial w},

(5)

where
G(x;\rho)=\int_{0}^{x}g(t;\rho){\rm d}t
,
x>0
, is the CDF corresponding to the following PDF

\displaystyle g(x;\rho)={2(x+1)^{2}-\rho(x^{2}+1)\over[(x+1)^{2}-\rho x]^{2}}-{1\over(x+1)^{2}},\quad x>0.

(6)

It is clear that

\displaystyle G(x;\rho)={x\over x+1}\,{(x+1)^{2}-\rho\over(x+1)^{2}-\rho x},\quad x>0.

(7)

Therefore, from (
5
), the CDF of
W
is given by (for
0<w<1
)

\displaystyle F_{W}(w;\bm{\theta})=G\left({s^{\alpha}\over\sigma^{\alpha}};\rho\right)=\dfrac{\displaystyle{s^{\alpha}\over\sigma^{\alpha}}}{\displaystyle{s^{\alpha}\over\sigma^{\alpha}}+1}\,\dfrac{\displaystyle\left({s^{\alpha}\over\sigma^{\alpha}}+1\right)^{2}-\rho}{\displaystyle\left({s^{\alpha}\over\sigma^{\alpha}}+1\right)^{2}-\rho\,{s^{\alpha}\over\sigma^{\alpha}}},\quad s={w\over 1-w}.

(8)

Remark 3.1
.

Taking
\rho=0
in (
8
), we get (for
0<w<1
)

\displaystyle F_{W}(w;\bm{\theta})=\dfrac{\displaystyle{s^{\alpha}}}{\displaystyle{s^{\alpha}}+\sigma^{\alpha}}=\dfrac{\displaystyle{w^{\alpha}}}{\displaystyle{w^{\alpha}}+\sigma^{\alpha}(1-w)^{\alpha}}.

Some basic properties

We establish here some basic mathematical properties of the unit-Fréchet distribution.

Identifiability

\displaystyle{\rho_{2}-\rho_{1}\over\sigma_{1}^{\alpha}}=-\left({\rho_{2}-\rho_{1}\over\sigma_{2}^{\alpha}}\right).

If
\rho_{1}\neq\rho_{2}
then
\sigma_{2}^{\alpha}=-\sigma_{1}^{\alpha}
, which is a contradiction since
\sigma_{1}
and
\sigma_{2}
are positive. Therefore,
\rho_{1}=\rho_{2}
. Substituting this identity into (
10
), we have
\sigma_{1}^{\alpha}=\sigma_{2}^{\alpha}
, from which we obtain
\sigma_{1}=\sigma_{2}
. So we have completed the proof.
∎

4.2
Symmetry

The following result shows the symmetry of the UF distribution when
\sigma=1
. For
\sigma\neq 1
, the ULS distribution presents asymmetry.

Proposition 4.2
.

The UF PDF in (
4
) is symmetric around
w=1/2
provided

\sigma=1
.

Proof.

Set

\displaystyle s_{0}={{1\over 2}-\delta\over{1\over 2}+\delta},\quad\forall 0<\delta<{1\over 2}.

By using (
4
) with
\sigma=1
, note that

\displaystyle f_{W}\left({1\over 2}-\delta;\bm{\theta}\right)={\alpha}s_{0}^{\alpha-1}(s_{0}+1)^{2}\left\{\displaystyle{\displaystyle 2\left({s_{0}^{\alpha}}+1\right)^{2}-\rho(s_{0}^{2\alpha}+1)\over\displaystyle\left[\left({s_{0}^{\alpha}}+1\right)^{2}-\rho{s_{0}^{\alpha}}\right]^{2}}-{\displaystyle 1\over\displaystyle\left({s_{0}^{\alpha}}+1\right)^{2}}\right\}

and

\displaystyle f_{W}\left({1\over 2}+\delta;\bm{\theta}\right)

\displaystyle={\alpha}\,{1\over s_{0}^{\alpha-1}}\left({1\over s_{0}}+1\right)^{2}\left\{\displaystyle{\displaystyle 2\left({1\over s_{0}^{\alpha}}+1\right)^{2}-\rho\left({1\over s_{0}^{2\alpha}}+1\right)\over\displaystyle\left[\left({1\over s_{0}^{\alpha}}+1\right)^{2}-\rho\,{1\over s_{0}^{\alpha}}\right]^{2}}-{\displaystyle 1\over\displaystyle\left({1\over s_{0}^{\alpha}}+1\right)^{2}}\right\}.

From the last two identities above, simple algebraic manipulations show that

\displaystyle f_{W}\left({1\over 2}-\delta;\bm{\theta}\right)=f_{W}\left({1\over 2}+\delta;\bm{\theta}\right),\quad\forall 0<\delta<{1\over 2}.

The required result then follows readily. ∎

Y
be the corresponding random variable of the CDF
G(\cdot;a)

).
A simple observation shows that if

4.4
UF model arising as a ratio

In this subsection, we establish one of the most important properties of the proposed model. This property characterizes a UF random variable
W
in (
8
) as a ratio of the
form
X_{1}/(X_{1}+X_{2})
, where the random vector

(X_{1},X_{2})^{\top}
follows a bivariate extreme distribution (with Fréchet margins) as discussed in
(
Elshamy,, 1992
, p. 14, Item 21)
.

Following
Elshamy, (1992)
, a random vector
(X_{1},X_{2})^{\top}
has a bivariate extreme distribution if its CDF, denoted by
F_{X_{1},X_{2}}
, is given by (
3
), that is,

\displaystyle F_{X_{1},X_{2}}(x_{1},x_{2})=\exp\left\{-\left({x_{1}\over\sigma_{1}}\right)^{-\alpha}-\left({x_{2}\over\sigma_{2}}\right)^{-\alpha}+\rho\left[\left({x_{1}\over\sigma_{1}}\right)^{\alpha}+\left({x_{2}\over\sigma_{2}}\right)^{\alpha}\right]^{-1}\right\},

(13)

where
x_{1}\geqslant 0
,
x_{2}\geqslant 0
;

\sigma_{1}>0,\sigma_{2}>0,\alpha>0
and
0\leqslant\rho\leqslant 1
.

Setting

\displaystyle T\equiv{X_{1}\over X_{1}+X_{2}},

we have (for
0<w<1
)

\displaystyle F_{T}(w)=\mathbb{P}\left({X_{2}\over X_{1}}\leqslant s\right)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\left[\int_{0}^{sx_{2}}f_{X_{1},X_{2}}(x_{1},x_{2}){\rm d}x_{1}\right]{\rm d}x_{2},\quad s={w\over 1-w}.

(14)

Note that (for any
r>0
)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{r}f_{X_{1},X_{2}}(x_{1},x_{2}){\rm d}x_{1}={\partial\over\partial x_{2}}\int_{0}^{r}{\partial\over\partial x_{1}}F_{X_{1},X_{2}}(x_{1},x_{2}){\rm d}x_{1}={\partial\over\partial x_{2}}F_{X_{1},X_{2}}(r,x_{2}).

(15)

Then, by using (
15
) in (
14
), we have

\displaystyle F_{T}(w)=\int_{0}^{\infty}{\partial\over\partial x_{2}}F_{X_{1},X_{2}}(r,x_{2})\bigg|_{r=sx_{2}}{\rm d}x_{2}.

Using (
13
), the integral above becomes

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}F_{X_{1},X_{2}}(r,x_{2})\,{\alpha\over x_{2}}\left[\left(x_{2}\over\sigma_{2}\right)^{-\alpha}-{\rho\left(x_{2}\over\sigma_{2}\right)^{\alpha}\left[\left(r\over\sigma_{1}\right)^{\alpha}+\left(x_{2}\over\sigma_{2}\right)^{\alpha}\right]^{-2}}\right]\Bigg|_{r=sx_{2}}{\rm d}x_{2}

\displaystyle={\displaystyle 1-{{\rho\over\sigma_{2}^{2\alpha}}\left[\left(s\over\sigma_{1}\right)^{\alpha}+{1\over\sigma_{2}^{\alpha}}\right]^{-2}}\over\displaystyle 1+\left({s\sigma_{2}\over\sigma_{1}}\right)^{-\alpha}-{\rho\over\sigma_{2}^{\alpha}}\left[\left({s\over\sigma_{1}}\right)^{\alpha}+{1\over\sigma_{2}^{\alpha}}\right]^{-1}}.

(16)

From (
14
) and (
16
), we have proven that

\displaystyle F_{T}(w)={\displaystyle 1-{\rho\left[\left({\sigma_{2}\over\sigma_{1}}\,s\right)^{\alpha}+1\right]^{-2}}\over\displaystyle 1+\left({\sigma_{2}\over\sigma_{1}}\,s\right)^{-\alpha}-\rho\left[\left({\sigma_{2}\over\sigma_{1}}\,s\right)^{\alpha}+1\right]^{-1}}.

(17)

Setting
\sigma\equiv\sigma_{1}/\sigma_{2}
, the above identity is written as (for
0<w<1
)

\displaystyle F_{T}(w)=\dfrac{\displaystyle{s^{\alpha}\over\sigma^{\alpha}}}{\displaystyle\left({s^{\alpha}\over\sigma^{\alpha}}+1\right)}\,\dfrac{\displaystyle\left({s^{\alpha}\over\sigma^{\alpha}}+1\right)^{2}-\rho}{\displaystyle\left({s^{\alpha}\over\sigma^{\alpha}}+1\right)^{2}-\rho\,{s^{\alpha}\over\sigma^{\alpha}}}=F_{W}(w;\bm{\theta}),\quad s={w\over 1-w},

(18)

where in the last equality we have used the formula in (
8
).
That is,

\displaystyle W\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}{X_{1}\over X_{1}+X_{2}}.

(19)

In other words, distributionally, the UF random variable
W
can be treated as the ratio in (
19
) involving two random variables
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
with
(X_{1},X_{2})^{\top}
following a bivariate extreme distribution
(
Elshamy,, 1992
)
.

Remark 4.3
.

From (
12
) and (
19
), respectively, we have

\displaystyle W\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}{1\over 1+{1\over\sigma Y^{1/\alpha}}}\quad\text{and}\quad W\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}{1\over 1+{X_{2}\over X_{1}}}.

From which it follows that

\displaystyle Y\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\left({X_{1}\over\sigma X_{2}}\right)^{\alpha}.

4.5
Approximations for the moments

Let
g:[0,\infty)^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
be a twice differentiable integrable function. The second order Taylor expansion of
g(\bm{x})
, with
\bm{x}=(x_{1},x_{2})^{\top}
, around
\bm{\mu}=(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})^{\top}=(\mathbb{E}(X_{1}),\mathbb{E}(X_{2}))^{\top}
is

\displaystyle g(\bm{x})

\displaystyle=g(\bm{\mu})+{\partial g(\bm{x})\over\partial x_{1}}\Bigg|_{\bm{x}=\bm{\mu}}(x_{1}-\mu_{1})+{\partial g(\bm{x})\over\partial x_{2}}\Bigg|_{\bm{x}=\bm{\mu}}(x_{2}-\mu_{2})

\displaystyle+{1\over 2}\left\{{\partial^{2}g(\bm{x})\over\partial x_{1}^{2}}\Bigg|_{\bm{x}=\bm{\mu}}(x_{1}-\mu_{1})^{2}+2\,{\partial^{2}g(\bm{x})\over\partial x_{1}\partial x_{2}}\Bigg|_{\bm{x}=\bm{\mu}}(x_{1}-\mu_{1})(x_{2}-\mu_{2})+{\partial^{2}g(\bm{x})\over\partial x_{2}^{2}}\Bigg|_{\bm{x}=\bm{\mu}}(x_{2}-\mu_{2})^{2}\right\}

\displaystyle+R_{2}(\bm{x}),

where
R_{2}(\bm{x})
is a remainder of a smaller order than the terms in the equation. Hence, a better approximation for

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[g(\bm{X})]

\displaystyle\approx g(\bm{\mu})+{1\over 2}\left\{{\partial^{2}g(\bm{x})\over\partial x_{1}^{2}}\Bigg|_{\bm{x}=\bm{\mu}}{\rm Var}(X_{1})+2\,{\partial^{2}g(\bm{x})\over\partial x_{1}\partial x_{2}}\Bigg|_{\bm{x}=\bm{\mu}}{\rm Cov}(X_{1},X_{2})+{\partial^{2}g(\bm{x})\over\partial x_{2}^{2}}\Bigg|_{\bm{x}=\bm{\mu}}{\rm Var}(X_{2})\right\}.

(20)

By taking
g(\bm{x})=[x_{1}/(x_{1}+x_{2})]^{p}

p\in\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(W^{p})=\mathbb{E}\left(\left[{X_{1}\over X_{1}+X_{2}}\right]^{p}\right)\approx\left({\mu_{1}\over\mu_{1}+\mu_{2}}\right)^{p}

\displaystyle+{p\mu_{1}^{p-1}\over 2(\mu_{1}+\mu_{2})^{p+2}}\left\{{\mu_{2}\over\mu_{1}}\,[(p-1)\mu_{2}-2\mu_{1}]{\rm Var}(X_{1})+2(\mu_{1}-p\mu_{2}){\rm Cov}(X_{1},X_{2})+{(p+1)\mu_{1}}{\rm Var}(X_{2})\right\}.

In particular,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(W)

\displaystyle\approx{\mu_{1}\over\mu_{1}+\mu_{2}}-{1\over(\mu_{1}+\mu_{2})^{3}}\left\{\mu_{2}{\rm Var}(X_{1})+{(\mu_{2}-\mu_{1})}\,{\rm Cov}(X_{1},X_{2})-\mu_{1}{\rm Var}(X_{2})\right\}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(W^{2})

\displaystyle+{\mu_{1}\over(\mu_{1}+\mu_{2})^{4}}\left\{{\mu_{2}\over\mu_{1}}\,(\mu_{2}-2\mu_{1}){\rm Var}(X_{1})+2(\mu_{1}-2\mu_{2}){\rm Cov}(X_{1},X_{2})+{3\mu_{1}}{\rm Var}(X_{2})\right\}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle{\rm Var}(W)

\displaystyle\approx{\mu_{1}\over(\mu_{1}+\mu_{2})^{4}}\left\{{\mu_{2}\over\mu_{1}}\,(\mu_{2}-2\mu_{1}){\rm Var}(X_{1})+2(\mu_{1}-2\mu_{2}){\rm Cov}(X_{1},X_{2})+{3\mu_{1}}{\rm Var}(X_{2})\right\}

\displaystyle-{1\over(\mu_{1}+\mu_{2})^{6}}\left\{\mu_{2}{\rm Var}(X_{1})+{(\mu_{2}-\mu_{1})}\,{\rm Cov}(X_{1},X_{2})-\mu_{1}{\rm Var}(X_{2})\right\}^{2}

\displaystyle+{2\mu_{1}\over(\mu_{1}+\mu_{2})^{4}}\left\{\mu_{2}{\rm Var}(X_{1})+{(\mu_{2}-\mu_{1})}\,{\rm Cov}(X_{1},X_{2})-\mu_{1}{\rm Var}(X_{2})\right\}.

Since
\bm{X}=(X_{1},X_{2})^{\top}
is as in (

\bm{X}

, in the above identities we are considering that (for

\displaystyle\mu_{i}=\sigma_{i}\Gamma\left(1-{1\over\alpha}\right),\quad\alpha>1,

\displaystyle{\rm Var}(X_{i})=\sigma_{i}^{2}\left[\Gamma\left(1-{2\over\alpha}\right)-\Gamma^{2}\left(1-{1\over\alpha}\right)\right],\quad\alpha>2,

4.6
The stress-strength probability

In what follows we explicitly obtain the stress-strength probability
R=\mathbb{P}(X_{1}<X_{2})
in the case where
(X_{1},X_{2})^{\top}
follows a bivariate extreme distribution
(
Elshamy,, 1992
)
.

Indeed, by (
14

18

\displaystyle R=\mathbb{P}\left({X_{2}\over X_{1}}>1\right)=1-\mathbb{P}\left({X_{2}\over X_{1}}\leqslant 1\right)=1-F_{T}(w)\Big|_{w=1/2}=1-F_{W}(w;\bm{\theta})\Big|_{w=1/2}.

Hence, as
w=1/2
if and only if

\displaystyle R=\dfrac{\displaystyle\displaystyle\left({1\over\sigma^{\alpha}}+1\right)^{3}-{1\over\sigma^{\alpha}}\left({1\over\sigma^{\alpha}}+1\right)^{2}-\rho\,{1\over\sigma^{2\alpha}}}{\displaystyle\left({1\over\sigma^{\alpha}}+1\right)\left[\left({1\over\sigma^{\alpha}}+1\right)^{2}-\rho\,{1\over\sigma^{\alpha}}\right]}.

In the special case

4.7
Quantiles

The quantile function of a distribution is useful for the generation of random numbers from the distribution. Since quantiles are invariant with respect to monotonic transformations, from (
12
), the quantile of
W\sim{\rm UF}(\bm{\theta})
, denoted by
Q_{W}(p)
,
0<p<1
, is written as

\displaystyle Q_{W}(p)={\sigma Q_{Y}^{1/\alpha}(p)\over 1+\sigma Q_{Y}^{1/\alpha}(p)},

(21)

where
Y
has CDF
G(\cdot;\rho)
given in (
7
).
Note that
Q_{Y}(p)
can be obtained by inverting the equation
G(Q_{Y}(p);\rho)=p
. Consequently,
Q_{Y}(p)
is the only zero (in the positive half interval) of the following cubic polynomial:

\displaystyle(p-1)x^{3}+[(3-\rho)p-2]x^{2}+[(3-\rho)p+\rho-1]x+p,\quad x>0.

4.8
Maximum likelihood estimation

Let
\{W_{i}:i=1,\ldots,n\}
be a random sample of size
n
from
W\sim{\rm UF}(\bm{\theta})
and let
w_{i}
be the sample observation of
W_{i}
,
i=1,\ldots,n
. From (
5
),

\displaystyle f_{W_{i}}(w_{i};\bm{\theta})={\alpha\over\sigma^{\alpha}}\,s^{\alpha-1}_{i}(s_{i}+1)^{2}\,g\left({s^{\alpha}_{i}\over\sigma^{\alpha}};\rho\right),\quad s_{i}={w_{i}\over 1-w_{i}},\quad i=1,\ldots,n,

where
g(\cdot;\rho)
is given in (
6
).
Then, the log-likelihood
function for
\bm{\theta}=(\sigma,\alpha,\rho)^{\top}
is given by

\displaystyle\ell(\bm{\theta})=n\log(\alpha)-n\alpha\log(\sigma)+(\alpha-1)\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log(s_{i})+2\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log(s_{i}+1)+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\left(g\left({s^{\alpha}_{i}\over\sigma^{\alpha}};\rho\right)\right).

The likelihood equations are

\displaystyle{\partial\ell(\bm{\theta})\over\partial\sigma}

\displaystyle={n\alpha\over\sigma}+{\alpha\over\sigma}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{g^{\prime}(x_{i};\rho)\over g(x_{i};\rho)}\,x_{i}\,\bigg|_{x_{i}={s^{\alpha}_{i}\over\sigma^{\alpha}}},

\displaystyle{\partial\ell(\bm{\theta})\over\partial\alpha}

\displaystyle={n\over\alpha}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log(s_{i})+{1\over\alpha}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{g^{\prime}(x_{i};\rho)\over g(x_{i};\rho)}\,x_{i}\log(\sigma^{\alpha}x_{i})\,\bigg|_{x_{i}={s^{\alpha}_{i}\over\sigma^{\alpha}}}-{\log(\sigma)}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{g^{\prime}(x_{i};\rho)\over g(x_{i};\rho)}\,x_{i}\,\bigg|_{x_{i}={s^{\alpha}_{i}\over\sigma^{\alpha}}},

\displaystyle{\partial\ell(\bm{\theta})\over\partial\rho}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{i=1}^{n}{x^{4}_{i}+(\rho-2)x^{3}_{i}-6x^{2}_{i}+(\rho-2)x_{i}+1\over g(x_{i};\rho)[(x_{i}+1)^{2}-\rho x_{i}]^{3}}\,\bigg|_{x_{i}={s^{\alpha}_{i}\over\sigma^{\alpha}}},

where

\displaystyle g^{\prime}(x;\rho)=-\dfrac{2[\rho^{3}x^{3}-\rho(x-2)^{2}(x+1)^{4}+(x+1)^{6}+\rho^{2}(1+3x-5x^{3}-3x^{4})]}{(x+1)^{3}[(x+1)^{2}-\rho x]^{3}}.

Necessary conditions for the occurrence
of a maximum (or a minimum) are

\displaystyle{\partial\ell(\bm{\theta})\over\partial\sigma}=0,\quad{\partial\ell(\bm{\theta})\over\partial\alpha}=0,\quad{\partial\ell(\bm{\theta})\over\partial\rho}=0.

When a nontrivial root
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}=(\widehat{\sigma},\widehat{\alpha},\widehat{\rho})^{\top}
of the likelihood equations provides the absolute maximum of the log-likelihood function, it is called an ML estimate in the strict sense.
Any nontrivial root of these equations is called the ML estimate of
\bm{\theta}
in the loose
sense. It is not possible to solve the above likelihood equations analytically, therefore we must resort to numerical optimization
methods.

Simulation Results

In this section, we provide a study involving Monte Carlo simulations that analyze the performance of the ML estimator
\hat{\bm{\theta}}
.

To evaluate the performance of the ML estimator
\hat{\bm{\theta}}
, we fix several values of the parameters
\bm{\theta}=(\sigma
,
\alpha
,
\rho)
, and then we generate
N=1,000
Monte-Carlo samples with sample size

We analyze the estimates
\hat{\bm{\theta}}

Random samples from the UF distribution can be generated using the inversion method, based on the quantiles
F_{W}^{-1}(U)
, where
U
is a uniform random variable in
[0,1]
. The inverse function was computed numerically using the

(1)

fix the parameter
\bm{\theta}
, the sample size
n\in\{30,50,100\}
and the number of Monte Carlo replications

;

(2)

the estimate
\hat{\bm{\theta}}^{(j)}

(3)

Conclusions

This paper introduces a novel unit distribution, exploring its generation through ratios of dependent Fréchet variables. Theoretical aspects, including identifiability, symmetry, stochastic representation, characterization, moments, stress-strength probability, quantiles, and maximum likelihood estimation, are rigorously examined. Practical applications are demonstrated via Monte Carlo simulations, football data analysis, and income-consumption modeling.

Potential extensions include analyzing modality, entropy, and shape characteristics (skewness, kurtosis) using moment approximations in Subsection
4.5
. Future research can investigate alternative construction principles for unit distributions using quantiles of positive-support random variables, potentially yielding new distributional families.
