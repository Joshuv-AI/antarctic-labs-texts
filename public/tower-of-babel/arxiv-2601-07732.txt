Semisimple algebraic groups over real closed fields
Raphael Appenzeller

Abstract
. We give a self-contained introduction to linear algebraic and semialgebraic groups over real closed fields, and we generalize several key results about semisimple Lie groups to algebraic and semialgebraic groups over real closed fields. We prove that a torus in a semisimple algebraic group is maximal ℝ \mathbb{R} -split if and only if it is maximal 𝔽 \mathbb{F} -split for real closed fields 𝔽 \mathbb{F} . For the 𝔽 \mathbb{F} -points we formulate and prove the Iwasawa-decomposition K ​ A ​ U KAU , the Cartan-decomposition K ​ A ​ K KAK and the Bruhat-decomposition B ​ W ​ B BWB . For unipotent subgroups we prove the Baker-Campbell-Hausdorff formula, facilitating the analysis of root groups. We give a proof of the Jacobson-Morozov Lemma about subgroups whose Lie algebra is isomorphic to 𝔰 ​ 𝔩 2 \mathfrak{sl}_{2} for algebraic groups and a version for the 𝔽 \mathbb{F} -points, when the root system is reduced. We describe the rank 1 subgroups which are the semisimple parts of Levi-subgroups. We prove a semialgebraic version of Kostant’s convexity theorem. The main tool used is a model theoretic transfer principle that follows from the Tarski-Seidenberg theorem.

1.
Introduction and main results

Algebraic groups were first studied for algebraically closed fields, but significant progress has since been made in understanding them over arbitrary fields
[

,

,

. The
\mathbb{R}

In this paper, we extend several key results from the theory of Lie groups to algebraic and semialgebraic groups over real closed fields. In real algebraic geometry, real closed fields serve a role analogous to that of algebraically closed fields in classical algebraic geometry.

Let
\mathbb{F}

\mathbb{K}

\mathbb{K}\subseteq\mathbb{F}\cap\mathbb{R}

A torus

2.
Real closed fields

A great reference for real algebraic geometry is
[
5
]
. An
ordered field
is a field together with a total order such that the sum and the product of positive elements are positive. Note that every ordered field is of characteristic
0
.
A field
\mathbb{F}
is called
real closed
if it satisfies one of the following equivalent conditions.

(1)

There is a total order on
\mathbb{F}
turning
\mathbb{F}
into an ordered field such that every positive element has a square root and every polynomial of odd degree has a solution.

(2)

There is an order on
\mathbb{F}
that does not extend to any proper algebraic field extension of
\mathbb{F}
.

(3)

\mathbb{F}
is not algebraically closed but every finite extension is algebraically closed.

(4)

\mathbb{F}
is not algebraically closed but
\mathbb{F}[\sqrt{-1}]
is algebraically closed.

An ordered field is called
Archimedean
if every element is bounded by a natural number. The real numbers
\mathbb{R}
and the subset of real algebraic numbers are examples of Archimedean real closed fields.
A major tool when working with real closed fields is the following transfer principle from model theory.

2.1.
The transfer principle

Let
\mathbb{K}
be an ordered field. Recall that a
first-order formula of ordered fields with parameters in
\mathbb{K}
is a formula that contains a finite number of conjunctions
\wedge
, disjunctions
\lor
, negations
\lnot
, and universal
\forall
or existential
\exists
quantifiers on variables, starting from atomic formulas which are formulas of the kind
f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=0
or
g(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\leq 0
, where
f
and
g
are polynomials with coefficients in
\mathbb{K}
. A first-order formula without free variables is called a
sentence
. By the Tarski-Seidenberg theorem, any sentence is equivalent to a sentence without quantifiers, from which can be deduced that the theory of real closed fields in the language of ordered fields is complete. In practice this means the following.

Theorem 2.1
.

(Transfer principle,
[
5
]
)
Let
\mathbb{F}
and
\mathbb{F}^{\prime}
be real closed fields. Let
\varphi
be a sentence with parameters in
\mathbb{F}\cap\mathbb{F}^{\prime}
. Then
\varphi
is true for
\mathbb{F}
if and only if
\varphi
is true for
\mathbb{F}^{\prime}
, formally
\mathbb{F}\models\varphi\iff\mathbb{F}^{\prime}\models\varphi
.

Let
\varphi
be a first-order formula with parameters in some field
\mathbb{K}\subseteq\mathbb{F}\cap\mathbb{R}
with
n
free variables. Let
X
be a subset of
\mathbb{K}^{n}
which can be described as
X=\{x\in\mathbb{K}^{n}\colon\mathbb{F}\models\varphi(x)\}
. It follows from the transfer principle, that the
semialgebraic extension
X_{\mathbb{F}}=\{x\in\mathbb{F}^{n}\colon\mathbb{F}\models\varphi(x)\}
of
X
depends only on
X
and not on
\varphi
and is thus well defined. Sets of the form
\{x\in k\colon k\models\varphi(x)\}
for any ordered field
k\supseteq\mathbb{K}
are called
semialgebraic
sets.

2.2.
Examples of real closed fields

The field of
Puiseux series
over the real algebraic numbers
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}^{\operatorname{rc}}

\mathbb{F}:=\left\{\sum_{k=-\infty}^{k_{0}}c_{k}X^{\frac{k}{m}}\,\colon\,k_{0},m\in\mathbb{Z},\,m>0,\,c_{k}\in\overline{\mathbb{Q}}^{\operatorname{rc}},\,c_{k_{0}}\neq 0\right\},

is a non-Archimedean real closed field, where the usual order on
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}^{\operatorname{rc}}
is extended by
X>r
for all
r\in\overline{\mathbb{Q}}^{\operatorname{rc}}

[
5
]
.

A
non-principal ultrafilter on
\mathbb{Z}
is a function
\omega\colon\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{Z})\to\{0,1\}
that satisfies

(1)

\omega(\emptyset)=0
,
\omega(\mathbb{Z})=1

(2)

If
A,B\subseteq\mathbb{Z}
satisfy
A\cap B=\emptyset
, then
\omega(A\cup B)=\omega(A)+\omega(B)
.

(3)

All finite subsets
A\subseteq\mathbb{Z}
satisfy
\omega(A)=0
.

Ultrafilters can be thought of as finitely-additive probability measures that only take values in
0
and
1
. The existence of non-principal ultrafilters is equivalent to the axiom of choice,
[
17
]
. For a given ultrafilter
\omega
, we define the
hyperreal numbers

\mathbb{R}_{\omega}
to be the equivalence classes of infinite sequences
\mathbb{R}_{\omega}=\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}/\!\!\sim
, where
x=(x_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\sim y=(y_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
if
\omega(\{i\in\mathbb{N}\colon x_{i}\neq y_{i}\})=0
or
\omega(\{i\in\mathbb{N}\colon x_{i}=y_{i}\})=1
. We define addition and multiplication componentwise, the multiplicative inverse is obtained by taking the inverses of all non-zero entries, turning
\mathbb{F}_{\omega}
into a field. Considering constant sequences, the real numbers are a subfield of
\mathbb{R}_{\omega}
. The hyperreals are an ordered field with respect to the order defined by
[(x_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}]\leq[(y_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}]
if and only if
\omega(\{i\in\mathbb{N}\colon x_{i}\leq y_{i}\})=1
. The hyperreals are real closed, since
\mathbb{R}
is. The hyperreals are non-Archimedean, since the equivalence class containing
(1,2,3,\ldots)
is an
infinite
element, meaning it is larger than any natural number.

Let
b\in\mathbb{R}_{\omega}
be an infinite element. Then

O_{b}:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}_{\omega}\colon|x|<b^{m}\text{ for some }m\in\mathbb{Z}\}

is an order convex subring of
\mathbb{R}_{\omega}
with maximal ideal

J_{b}:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}_{\omega}\colon|x|<b^{m}\text{ for all }m\in\mathbb{Z}\}.

The
Robinson field
associated to the non-principal ultrafilter
\omega
and the infinite element
b
is the quotient
\mathbb{R}_{\omega,b}:=O_{b}/J_{b}

[
34
]
. The Robinson field is a non-Archimedean real closed field. Note that
[b]\in\mathbb{R}_{\omega,b}
is a
big
element, meaning that for all
a\in\mathbb{R}_{\omega,b}
there is an
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
a<b^{n}
.

By definition (1) of real closed fields, the sentence

\varphi\colon\quad\forall a\colon a>0\to\exists b\colon b\cdot b=a

in the language of ordered fields with parameters in
\mathbb{Q}
holds over any real closed field
\mathbb{F}
, formally
\mathbb{F}\models\varphi
. In fact, being real closed can be described by sentences in the language of ordered fields. On the other hand, being Archimedean can not be described as a sentence in the language of ordered fields. The attempt of writing the Archimedean condition as a sentence

\psi\colon\quad\forall a\colon\exists n\in\mathbb{N}\colon a<n

fails, since the use of the symbols
\in
and
\mathbb{N}
is not allowed in the language of ordered fields. This is compatible with Theorem
2.1
, as there are some real closed fields that are Archimedean and others that are not. We note that by Gödel’s incompleteness theorem, the natural numbers
\mathbb{N}
can not be described in any theory that satisfies the transfer principle.

3.
Linear algebraic groups

3.1.
Definitions

Let
\mathbb{K}\subseteq\mathbb{R}
be a subfield (usually
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{Q}
,
\mathbb{K}=\overline{\mathbb{Q}}^{\operatorname{rc}}
or
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{R}
),
\mathbb{F}
a real closed field containing
\mathbb{K}
(usually a non-Archimedean field such as the Puiseux series) and
\mathbb{D}
an algebraically closed field that contains both
\mathbb{R}
and
\mathbb{F}
. We follow a naive approach to algebraic groups, viewing them as matrix groups. Since we are working with fields of characteristic 0, the algebraic geometry can be kept at a minimum. For an extensive introduction to algebraic groups, we refer to
[
6
]
,
[
7
]
,
[
20
]
or
[
37
]
.

The general linear group
\operatorname{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{D})
can be realized as an affine algebraic variety

\operatorname{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{D})\cong\left\{\begin{pmatrix}A&\\
&t\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{D}^{(n+1)\times(n+1)}\colon\det(A)\cdot t=1\right\}.

A subgroup
\mathbf{G}<\operatorname{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{D})
is called a
linear algebraic group defined over
\mathbb{K}
, if it is a set of common zeros for a set of polynomials in the coordinate ring
\mathbb{K}[\operatorname{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{D})]:=\mathbb{K}[(x_{ij}),\det(x_{ij})^{-1}]
of
\operatorname{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{D})
.
We will not consider more general algebraic groups and hence also call
\mathbf{G}
an
algebraic group
or a
\mathbb{K}
-
group
.
For any commutative
\mathbb{K}
-subalgebra
\mathbb{B}\subseteq\mathbb{D}
containing
\mathbb{K}
, we let

\operatorname{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{B})=\left\{(a_{ij})\in\operatorname{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{D})\colon a_{ij}\in\mathbb{B}\text{ and }\det(a_{ij})^{-1}\in\mathbb{B}\right\}.

The
group of
\mathbb{B}
-points of a linear algebraic group
G

is
\mathbf{G}(\mathbb{B}):=\mathbf{G}\cap\operatorname{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{B})
. The
\mathbb{R}
-points of a linear algebraic group
\mathbf{G}
form a real Lie group
[
26
, Theorem 2.1]
.

Viewing
\mathbf{G}\subseteq\operatorname{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{D})
as an algebraic subset of
\mathbb{D}^{n+1\times n+1}
, we endow
\mathbf{G}
with the Zariski-topology, whose closed sets are given by common zeros of sets of polynomials in
\mathbb{K}[\operatorname{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{D})]
. A linear algebraic group
\mathbf{G}
is
semisimple
if it is connected and every closed connected normal abelian subgroup is trivial. Similarly, a Lie group is
semisimple
if every closed connected normal abelian subgroup is trivial, but now in the Lie group topology. Using Zariski-closures of subgroups, one sees that
\mathbf{G}
is semisimple if and only if
\mathbf{G}(\mathbb{R})
is semisimple.

3.1.1.
Examples

The multiplicative group
\mathbf{G}_{m}=\operatorname{GL}_{1}(\mathbb{D})
is a linear algebraic group defined over
\mathbb{Q}
. Note that
\mathbf{G}_{m}
is connected in the Zariski-topology. Its
\mathbb{C}
-points
(\mathbf{G}_{m})_{\mathbb{C}}=\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
are connected in the Euclidean topology but its
\mathbb{R}
-points
(\mathbf{G}_{m})_{\mathbb{R}}=\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
are not.

If
V
is a
\mathbb{D}
-vector space, then the group
\operatorname{GL}(V)

For a linear algebraic group

3.3.
The Lie Algebra and the adjoint representation

Let
e\in\mathbf{G}
be the neutral element of an algebraic
\mathbb{K}
-group
\mathbf{G}
. There are multiple ways to define the Lie Algebra of
\mathbf{G}
, a few of which can be found for instance in Chapters 5 and 9 of
[
20
]
. A first explicit definition uses the description of
\mathbf{G}
as a matrix group

\mathbf{G}=\left\{(a_{ij})\in\operatorname{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{D})\colon f(a_{ij})=0\text{ for all }f\in I\right\}

where
I\subset\mathbb{K}[(x_{ij}),\det(x_{ij})^{-1}]
is a finite subset. For the following definition we restrict ourselves to subgroups of
\operatorname{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{D})
, so that we can ignore the dependence on
\operatorname{det}(x_{ij})^{-1}
. For
f\in I
we define the
differential of
f
at
e:=\operatorname{Id}
to be the linear polynomial

\operatorname{d}_{e}\!f=\sum_{ij}\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{ij}}(e)\cdot x_{ij}\in\mathbb{K}[(x_{ij})].

and the
Zariski tangent space

T_{e}\mathbf{G}:=\left\{(a_{ij})\in\mathbb{D}^{n\times n}\colon\operatorname{d}_{e}\!f(a_{ij})=0\text{ for all }f\in I\right\}.

We now give another useful description of the tangent space in terms of derivations. A
point derivation
is a
\mathbb{D}
-linear map
\delta\colon\mathbb{D}[\mathbf{G}]\to\mathbb{D}
that satisfies the Leibnitz rule
\delta(ff^{\prime})=\delta(f)f^{\prime}+f\delta(f^{\prime})
for
f,f^{\prime}\in\mathbb{D}[\mathbf{G}]
. For
a=(a_{ij})\in T_{e}\mathbf{G}
the function
\delta_{a}=f\mapsto\operatorname{d}_{e}\!f(a_{ij})
is a point derivation and every point derivation is of this form
[
20
, Section 5.1]
.

From a more algebraic point of view, a
derivation
of the coordinate ring
\mathbb{D}[\mathbf{G}]
(viewed as a
\mathbb{D}
-algebra) of a linear algebraic group
\mathbf{G}
is a linear map

X\colon\mathbb{D}[\mathbf{G}]\to\mathbb{D}[\mathbf{G}]

that satisfies the Leibnitz rule
X(ff^{\prime})=X(f)f^{\prime}+fX(f^{\prime})
for
f,f^{\prime}\in\mathbb{D}[\mathbf{G}]
. Algebraic groups act by left (and right) translation on their coordinate rings
\mathbb{D}[\mathbf{G}]
. For
g\in\mathbf{G}
, the left translation is given by
\lambda_{g}(f)(h)=f(g^{-1}h)
for
f\in\mathbb{D}[\mathbf{G}]
,
h\in\mathbf{G}
(and the right translation by
\rho_{g}(f)(h)=f(hg)
). The
\mathbb{D}
-vector space of derivations of
\mathbb{D}[\mathbf{G}]
which commute with right translations

\displaystyle\mathfrak{g}

\displaystyle:=\left\{X\colon\mathbb{D}[\mathbf{G}]\to\mathbb{D}[\mathbf{G}]\colon\begin{matrix}X\text{ is a derivation of }\mathbb{D}[\mathbf{G}]\text{ with }\\
X\circ\rho_{g}=\rho_{g}\circ X\text{ for all }g\in\mathbf{G}\end{matrix}\right\},

is isomorphic to the space of point derivations (a derivation
X
corresponds to the point derivation
\delta\coloneq\operatorname{ev}_{e}\circ X\colon\mathbb{D}[\mathbf{G}]\to\mathbb{D}
, so
\delta(f)=(Xf)(e)
for
f\in\mathbb{D}[\mathbf{G}]
)
[
20
, Theorem 9.1]
, which in turn is identified with the Zariski tangent space
T_{e}\mathbf{G}
of
\mathbf{G}
as before.
Endowed with the Lie algebra structure defined by the bracket operation on derivations,
\mathfrak{g}
is called the
Lie algebra of

\mathbf{G}
. For a commutative
\mathbb{K}
-subalgebra
\mathbb{B}\subseteq\mathbb{D}
containing
\mathbb{K}
, we can use the identification
\mathfrak{g}=T_{e}\mathbf{G}
, to define the
\mathbb{B}
-points of the Lie algebra of
\mathbf{G}

\mathfrak{g}(\mathbb{B})=T_{e}\mathbf{G}\cap\mathbb{B}^{n\times n}.

We note that the real Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}(\mathbb{R})
is the Lie algebra of the real Lie group
\mathbf{G}(\mathbb{R})

[
26
, Theorem 2.1]
.

Let
\varphi\colon\mathbf{G}\to\mathbf{H}
be a morphism of algebraic groups
\mathbf{G},\mathbf{H}
. The transposed map
\varphi^{\circ}\colon\mathbb{D}[\mathbf{H}]\to\mathbb{D}[\mathbf{G}]
gives rise to a linear map
\operatorname{d}_{e}\!\varphi
which is called the
differential of
\varphi
at
e\in\mathbf{G}
. In terms of point derivations, the differential is defined as

\displaystyle\operatorname{d}_{e}\!\varphi\colon\mathfrak{g}

\displaystyle\to\mathfrak{h}

\displaystyle\delta

\displaystyle\mapsto\delta\circ\varphi^{\circ}.

The group
\mathbf{G}
acts on itself by conjugation
\operatorname{Int}(g)\colon\mathbf{G}\to\mathbf{G},h\mapsto ghg^{-1}
. The differential of
\operatorname{Int}(g)
at
e
is denoted
\operatorname{Ad}(g)
. The morphism
\operatorname{Ad}\colon\mathbf{G}\to\operatorname{GL}(\mathfrak{g})
is called the
adjoint representation of

\mathbf{G}
and is given by

\operatorname{Ad}(g)(X)=gXg^{-1}\in T_{e}\mathbf{G}

when
X\in\mathfrak{g}
is viewed as an element of
T_{e}\mathbf{G}
. The differential of
\operatorname{Ad}
at
e
is called the
adjoint representation

\operatorname{ad}\colon\mathfrak{g}\to\mathfrak{gl}(\mathfrak{g})
. Identifying
\mathfrak{g}\cong T_{e}\mathbf{G}
,
\operatorname{ad}
is given by

\operatorname{ad}(X)(Y)=\left[X,Y\right]

for
X,Y\in\mathfrak{g}
, where
[\cdot\,,\cdot]
is the matrix bracket.

Let

Let

\mathbb{K}

\mathfrak{g}(\mathbb{K})\subseteq\mathbb{K}^{n\times n}

\mathbb{K}

\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{F}}
be the semialgebraic extension of

for real closed fields
\mathbb{F}\supseteq\mathbb{K}
. We note that
\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{F}}=\mathfrak{g}(\mathbb{F})
, but in what follows, we will use the semialgebraic point of view. For consistency we put
\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{K}}:=\mathfrak{g}(\mathbb{K})
.

In this section, we recall facts about the real Lie group
\mathbf{G}(\mathbb{R})
and its Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{R}}
. We sometimes reference Chapter 3 of Helgason’s book
[
18
]
and chapter 6 of Knapp’s book
[
22
]
, a compact account of which can be found in
[

]
.

4.1.
Cartan involutions and Cartan decompositions

Recall that the
adjoint representation
of the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{R}}

\operatorname{ad}:=\operatorname{ad}_{\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{R}}}:=\operatorname{d}_{e}\!\operatorname{Ad}_{\mathbf{G}(\mathbb{R})}\colon\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{R}}\to\mathfrak{gl}(\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{R}})

is given by
X\mapsto[X,\cdot\,]
. The
Killing form

B
is the bilinear form on
\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{R}}
defined by

B(X,Y):=\operatorname{tr}\left(\operatorname{ad}(X)\circ\operatorname{ad}(Y)\right)

for
X,Y\in\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{R}}
.
A Lie algebra is called
simple
if it is non-abelian and does not contain any non-zero proper ideals.
A Lie algebra is called
semisimple
if it is a direct product of simple Lie algebras. By Cartan’s criterion, being semisimple is equivalent to having a non-degenerate Killing form.
Since
\mathbf{G}
is semisimple, so is
\mathbf{G}(\mathbb{R})
and
\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{R}}
.
A Lie algebra automorphism
\theta\colon\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{R}}\to\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{R}}
is called an
involution
if
\theta^{2}=\operatorname{Id}
. For any involution
\theta\colon\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{R}}\to\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{R}}
we can define a bilinear form
B_{\theta}
by

B_{\theta}(X,Y):=-B(X,\theta Y)

for
X,Y\in\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{R}}
. If
B_{\theta}
is positive-definite, then
\theta
is called a
Cartan involution
. The following is a technical result on Cartan involutions which is stated as an exercise in
[
18
]
and proven in
[

Lemma 4.1

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{R}}
be a real semisimple Lie algebra. Any two Cartan involutions of
\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{R}}
are conjugate via an element of

Proof.

Let
\theta
and
\theta^{\prime}
be two Cartan involutions of
\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{R}}
. If
\theta
and
\theta^{\prime}
commute, they have the same eigenspaces. We claim

and
E_{-1}(\theta)=E_{-1}(\theta^{\prime})
, since otherwise if for instance
\theta(X)=X
and
\theta^{\prime}(X)=-X

B_{\theta^{\prime}}(X,X)=-B(X,\theta^{\prime}(X))=-B(X,-X)=B(X,\theta(X))=-B_{\theta}(X,X),

but both

and
B_{\theta^{\prime}}(X,X)
should be positive. We conclude that if
\theta
and
\theta^{\prime}
commute, then

, since

and
\theta^{\prime}

\theta^{\prime}
do not commute, then we can apply Lemma

such that
\operatorname{Ad}(g)(\mathfrak{h})
is a
\theta
-invariant Cartan subalgebra with decomposition
\operatorname{Ad}(g)(\mathfrak{h})=\mathfrak{a}^{\prime}\oplus\mathfrak{t}^{\prime}
as described before. Since
\mathfrak{a}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathfrak{p}
, it is
\mathbb{R}
-split. In fact, for
X=X_{\mathfrak{a}^{\prime}}+X_{\mathfrak{t}^{\prime}}\in\mathfrak{a}^{\prime}\oplus\mathfrak{t}^{\prime}
,
\operatorname{ad}(X)
is only diagonalizable over
\mathbb{R}
if
X_{\mathfrak{t}^{\prime}}=0
. Since diagonalizability is preserved under conjugation,
\operatorname{Ad}(\mathfrak{a})\subseteq\mathfrak{a}^{\prime}
and by maximality of
\mathfrak{a}
, we have
\operatorname{Ad}(\mathfrak{a})=\mathfrak{a}^{\prime}
. Thus
\mathfrak{h}
is maximally noncompact.

If we instead start with a maximally noncompact Cartan subalgebra
\mathfrak{h}\subseteq\mathfrak{g}
, we similarly obtain (possibly using a conjugation to a
\theta
-stable one) an
\mathbb{R}
-split subalgebra
\mathfrak{a}
with
\mathfrak{h}=\mathfrak{a}\oplus\mathfrak{t}
. A priori
\mathfrak{a}
is maximal
\mathbb{R}
-split as a subalgebra of
\mathfrak{h}
, but if
\mathfrak{a}
was contained in a larger subalgebra
\mathfrak{a}^{\prime}
maximal
\mathbb{R}
-split in
\mathfrak{g}
, then the above construction would result in a Cartan subalgebra
\mathfrak{h}^{\prime}=\mathfrak{a}^{\prime}\oplus\mathfrak{t}^{\prime}
with
\dim(\mathfrak{a}^{\prime})>\dim(\mathfrak{a})
, which is impossible, since we assumed
\mathfrak{h}
to be maximally noncompact, which means
\dim(\mathfrak{a})
is maximal. By definition,
r_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathfrak{g})
is the maximal dimension of an
\mathbb{R}
-split abelian subalgebra, so
r_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathfrak{g})=\dim(\mathfrak{a})
.
∎

We now turn back to the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{R}}
of the
\mathbb{R}
-points
\mathbf{G}(\mathbb{R})
of a semisimple algebraic group
\mathbf{G}
and collect a few properties of
V(\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{R}})
and
\mathcal{C}(\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{R}})
.

Lemma 4.14
.

Recall that for the algebraic group
\mathbf{G}
,
\operatorname{rank}_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathbf{G})
is the maximal dimension of any abelian subspace of
\mathfrak{p}\subseteq\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{R}}
, where
\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{R}}=\mathfrak{p}\oplus\mathfrak{k}
is the Cartan decomposition associated to some Cartan involution
\theta
. We have

(1)

r_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{R}})=\operatorname{rank}_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathbf{G})
.

(2)

\mathbf{G}(\mathbb{R})
acts transitively on
\mathcal{C}(\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{R}})
and
V(\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{R}})
.

(3)

\mathcal{C}(\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{R}})
contains a
\theta
-stable element.

Proof.

A maximally
\mathbb{R}
-split Cartan subalgebra, or by Proposition
4.13
a maximally noncompact Cartan subalgebra can be obtained by starting with a Cartan involution
\theta
, taking
\mathfrak{a}\subseteq\mathfrak{p}
a maximal abelian subspace of
\mathfrak{p}
and taking
\mathfrak{t}\subseteq\operatorname{Cen}_{\mathfrak{k}}(\mathfrak{a})
a maximal abelian subspace of
\operatorname{Cen}_{\mathfrak{k}}(\mathfrak{a})
. Then

\mathfrak{h}=\mathfrak{a}\oplus\mathfrak{t}
is a
\theta
-stable maximally noncompact Cartan subalgebra, see
[
22
, Proposition 6.47]
. This shows (3).

By Proposition
4.13
,
r_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{R}})=\dim(\mathfrak{a})
and since
\mathfrak{a}
is a maximal abelian subspace of
\mathfrak{p}
,
r_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{R}})=\operatorname{rank}_{\mathbb{R}}(G)
, showing (1).

By Propositions
4.11
and
4.12
,
\operatorname{Ad}(\mathbf{G}(\mathbb{R}))
and thus
\mathbf{G}(\mathbb{R})
act transitively on
\mathcal{C}(\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{R}})
. Next, let
\mathfrak{a},\mathfrak{a}^{\prime}\in V(\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{R}})
. By Proposition
4.13
, there are maximally noncompact Cartan subalgebras
\mathfrak{h},\mathfrak{h}^{\prime}
such that
\mathfrak{a}\subseteq\mathfrak{h}
and
\mathfrak{a}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathfrak{h}^{\prime}
. By Proposition
4.12
, both
\mathfrak{h}
and
\mathfrak{h}^{\prime}
are conjugated to a
\theta
-stable maximally noncompact Cartan subalgebra
\mathfrak{h}^{\prime\prime}
. By Proposition
4.13
, the corresponding conjugates of
\mathfrak{a}
and
\mathfrak{a}^{\prime}
coincide with the intersection
\mathfrak{h}^{\prime\prime}\cap\mathfrak{p}
, hence with each other. This shows that
\operatorname{Ad}(\mathbf{G}(\mathbb{R}))
acts transitively on
V(\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{R}})
and concludes the proof of (2).
∎

4.3.
Lie Algebras over real closed fields

Let
\mathbb{K}
be a subfield of
\mathbb{R}
and
\mathbb{F}
be a real closed fields with
\mathbb{K}\subseteq\mathbb{F}
. In this section we additionally assume that
\mathbb{K}
is real closed. Let
\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{K}}\subseteq\mathbb{K}^{n\times n}
be the
\mathbb{K}
-points of the Lie algebra of a self-adjoint (
g\in\mathbf{G}
implies
g{{}^{\mathsf{T}}\!}\in\mathbf{G}
) semisimple algebraic group
\mathbf{G}
defined over
\mathbb{K}
. Let
\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{R}}
and
\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{F}}
be the semialgebraic extensions. The definitions of Section
4.2
apply also to
\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{F}}
:

An abelian subalgebra
\mathfrak{h}\subseteq\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{F}}
is called
Cartan subalgebra
if
\mathfrak{h}=\operatorname{Nor}_{\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{F}}}(\mathfrak{h})
. An abelian subalgebra
\mathfrak{a}\subseteq\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{F}}
is called
\mathbb{F}
-split
if
\operatorname{ad}(X)\colon\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{F}}\to\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{F}}
is diagonalizable over
\mathbb{F}
for every
X\in\mathfrak{a}
. Let
r_{\mathbb{F}}(\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{F}})
be the maximal dimension of such an
\mathbb{F}
-split abelian subalgebra and denote by
V(\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{F}})
the set of all
\mathbb{F}
-split abelian subalgebras with
\dim(\mathfrak{a})=r_{\mathbb{F}}(\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{F}})
. A
maximally
\mathbb{F}
-split Cartan subalgebra
is a Cartan subalgebra containing an element of
V(\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{F}})
as a subset. We denote by
\mathcal{C}(\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{F}})
the set of all maximally
\mathbb{F}
-split Cartan subalgebras. We will now use the transfer principle to relate
\mathbb{F}
-split algebras to the real subalgebras studied in the previous section. In the following Lemma we fix the Cartan-involution
\theta\colon X\mapsto-X{{}^{\mathsf{T}}\!}
that exists since
\mathbf{G}
is self-adjoint.

Lemma 4.15
.

Whenever
\mathbb{F}
and
\mathbb{K}
are two real closed fields with
\mathbb{K}\subseteq\mathbb{F}
and
\mathbb{K}\subseteq\mathbb{R}
, then

(1)

r_{\mathbb{K}}(\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{K}})=r_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{R}})=r_{\mathbb{F}}(\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{F}})
.

(2)

\mathbf{G}(\mathbb{K})
acts transitively on
\mathcal{C}(\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{K}})
and
V(\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{K}})
and those two sets are non-empty.

(3)

\mathcal{C}(\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{K}})
contains a
\theta
-stable element, i.e. there is
\mathfrak{h}\in\mathcal{C}(\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{K}})
such that
\theta(\mathfrak{h})=\mathfrak{h}
.

(4)

Let
\mathfrak{a}
and
\mathfrak{h}
be subalgebras of
\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{K}}
. Then

\displaystyle\mathfrak{a}\in V(\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{K}})

\displaystyle\iff\mathfrak{a}_{\mathbb{R}}\in V(\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{R}})\iff\mathfrak{a}_{\mathbb{F}}\in V(\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{F}})

\displaystyle\mathfrak{h}\in\mathcal{C}(\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{K}})

\displaystyle\iff\mathfrak{h}_{\mathbb{R}}\in\mathcal{C}(\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{R}})\iff\mathfrak{h}_{\mathbb{F}}\in\mathcal{C}(\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{F}}).

Proof.

Our first goal is to see
V(\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{K}})
as a semi-algebraic set. Let
k
be any real closed field containing
\mathbb{K}
. Let
\mathcal{L}
be a semisimple
k
-algebra, such that
\mathcal{L}
is also a semi-algebraic set defined over
\mathbb{K}
. Let
r\leq n:=\operatorname{dim}_{k}(\mathcal{L})
. The set
\operatorname{Grass}_{r}(\mathcal{L})
can be identified with an algebraic subset of
k^{n\times n}
, namely

\varphi\colon\operatorname{Grass}_{r}(\mathcal{L})\to\left\{A\in k^{n\times n}\colon A{{}^{\mathsf{T}}\!}=A,\ A^{2}=A,\ \operatorname{Tr}(A)=r\right\}

sends the
k
-dimensional subspace
V
to the orthogonal projection
\mathcal{L}\to V
, see
[
5
, Theorem 3.4.4]
. Moreover, for any
A\in\varphi(\operatorname{Grass}_{r}(\mathcal{L}))
we have the description

\varphi^{-1}(A)=\{v\in\mathcal{L}\colon Av=v\}.

An abelian subalgebra
\mathfrak{a}\subseteq\mathcal{L}
is
k
-split exactly when
\operatorname{ad}(x)
is diagonalizable over
k
for all
x\in\mathfrak{a}
. It is enough to require that for a basis
\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{r}\}
of
\mathfrak{a}
, the maps
\operatorname{ad}(v_{i})\colon\mathcal{L}\to\mathcal{L}
are diagonalizable over
k
. A linear map with matrix
M=(M_{ij})
is diagonalizable over
k
if and only if its characteristic polynomial decomposes into linear factors, which can be written as a first-order formula:

f(M)=\exists x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\colon\forall X\colon\det(M_{ij}-X\delta_{ij})=\prod_{i=1}^{n}(X-x_{i}).

We can write the statement “
\mathfrak{a}=\varphi^{-1}(A)
is a
k
-split abelian subalgebra” as a first-order formula

\displaystyle\exists v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}\in\mathcal{L}\colon\left(\forall a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\colon\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}v_{i}=0\to a_{1}=0\wedge\ldots\wedge a_{n}=0\right)\wedge

\displaystyle\bigwedge_{i=1}^{r}Av_{i}=v_{i}\ \wedge\bigwedge_{i,j=1}^{r}[v_{i},v_{j}]=0\ \wedge

\displaystyle\bigwedge_{\ell=1}^{r}\left(\exists M_{11},M_{12},\ldots,M_{nn}\colon\bigwedge_{i=1}^{n}[v_{\ell},v_{i}]=\sum_{j=1}^{n}M_{ij}e_{j}\,\rightarrow f(M)\right),

in words: there exists a basis of
\mathcal{L}
whose first
r
vectors form a basis of
\mathfrak{a}=\varphi^{-1}(A)
, such that
\mathfrak{a}
is abelian and for every
\ell\in\{1,\ldots,r\}

\operatorname{ad}(v_{\ell})
with matrix
M
in this basis is diagonalizable over
k
.

By quantifier elimination we get an equivalent first-order statement
g(A)
without quantifiers and we can write

\varphi(V(\mathcal{L})_{r})=\{A\in\varphi(\operatorname{Grass}_{r}(\mathcal{L}))\colon g(A)\}

as a semi-algebraic set defined by polynomials with coefficients in
k
, where
V(\mathcal{L})_{r}
denotes the set of all
k
-split abelian subalgebras of dimension
r
.

Now for
k=\mathbb{R}
we know that
V(\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{R}})=V(\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{R}})_{r_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{R}})}
is non-empty, but
V(\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{R}})_{r}
is empty for any
r>r_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{R}})
. We consider the first-order formula

\exists A\in\varphi(V(\mathfrak{g}_{k})_{r}),

which is defined over
\mathbb{K}
since
\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{R}}
and hence the brackets are defined over
\mathbb{K}
. Since the formula is satisfied for
k=\mathbb{R}
and
r=r_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{R}})
, we conclude by the transfer principle that it also holds for
k=\mathbb{K}
and
k=\mathbb{F}
, i.e.
V(\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{K}})=V(\mathfrak{g})_{r_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{R}})}\neq\emptyset
and
V(\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{F}})=V(\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{F}})_{r_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{R}})}\neq\emptyset
. For any
r>r_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{R}})
, we know that the formula is not satisfied for
k=\mathbb{R}
, therefore by the transfer principle it is also not satisfied for
k=\mathbb{K}
and
k=\mathbb{F}
, i.e.
V(\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{K}})_{r}=\emptyset
and
V(\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{F}})_{r}=\emptyset
for any
r>r_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{R}})
. It follows that
r_{\mathbb{F}}(\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{F}})=r_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{R}})=r_{\mathbb{K}}(\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{K}})
, which is statement (1) of the Lemma we are proving.

We describe

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(\mathfrak{g}_{k})=\left\{\mathfrak{h}\in\operatorname{Grass}_{r_{k}(\mathfrak{g}_{k})}(\mathfrak{g}_{k})\colon\begin{matrix}[\mathfrak{h},\mathfrak{h}]=0,\ \operatorname{Nor}_{\mathfrak{g}_{k}}(\mathfrak{h})=\mathfrak{h},\\
\exists\mathfrak{a}\in V(\mathfrak{g}_{k})\colon\mathfrak{a}\subseteq\mathfrak{h}\end{matrix}\right\}

similarly. Let
v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}
again be a basis of a semisimple
k
-algebra
\mathcal{L}
such that
v_{1},\ldots,v_{r}
is a basis of a subalgebra
\mathfrak{a}\subseteq\mathcal{L}
. We have
\operatorname{Nor}_{\mathcal{L}}(\mathfrak{a})=\mathfrak{a}
whenever
[v_{i},v_{j}]\in\mathfrak{a}\ \forall i\in\{1,\ldots,n\},j\in\{1,\ldots,r\}
, i.e. given
\mathfrak{a}=\varphi^{-1}(A)
we have

\displaystyle h(A)\colon\quad

\displaystyle\bigwedge_{i=1}^{n}\bigwedge_{j=1}^{r}A[v_{i},v_{j}]=[v_{i},v_{j}].

We have that
\varphi^{-1}(A)\in\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{L})
if and only if the following first-order formula holds

\displaystyle\exists v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}\in\mathcal{L}\colon\left(\forall a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\colon\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}v_{i}=0\to\bigwedge_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}=0\right)\wedge

\displaystyle\bigwedge_{i=1}^{r}Av_{i}=v_{i}\ \ \wedge\ \ \bigwedge_{i,j=1}^{r}[v_{i},v_{j}]=0\ \ \wedge\ \ h(A)\ \ \wedge

\displaystyle\exists B\in V(\mathcal{L})\colon\forall v\in\mathcal{L}\colon Bv=v\to Av=v.

Again we use quantifier elimination to to get an equivalent statement
s(A)
without quantifiers. Thus,
\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{L})
can be identified with the semialgebraic set

\varphi(\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{L}))=\{A\in\varphi(\operatorname{Grass}_{r_{k}(\mathcal{L})}(\mathcal{L}))\colon s(A)\}.

From the theory of real Lie groups we know by Lemma
4.14
(3) that
\mathcal{C}(\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{R}})
is non-empty and since
\exists A\in\varphi(\mathcal{C}(\mathfrak{g}_{k}))
is a first-order statement, defined over
\mathbb{K}
, we can use the transfer principle to conclude that
\mathcal{C}(\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{K}})
and
\mathcal{C}(\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{F}})
are also non-empty. Statement (4) follows from the semi-algebraic description of the sets
V(\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{K}})
and
\mathcal{C}(\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{K}})
and the transfer principle.

The group
\mathbf{G}(k)
acts by conjugation on
\operatorname{Grass}_{r}(\mathfrak{g}_{k})
. The corresponding action on
\varphi(\operatorname{Grass}_{r}(\mathfrak{g}_{k}))
is given by
g.A=A\circ\operatorname{Ad}(g^{-1})
, where
g\in\mathbf{G}(k)
,
{A\in\varphi(\operatorname{Grass}_{r}(\mathfrak{g}_{k}))}
and
\operatorname{Ad}(g)\colon\mathfrak{g}_{k}\to\mathfrak{g}_{k},X\mapsto gXg^{-1}
.

We know that this action is transitive on
V(\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{R}})
and
\mathcal{C}(\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{R}})
by Lemma
4.14
(2). As all involved sets are semi-algebraic, we can formulate transitivity as a first-order formula,

\displaystyle\forall A,B\in\varphi(V(\mathfrak{g}_{k}))\ \exists g\in\mathbf{G}\colon\forall v\in\mathfrak{g}_{k}\colon A(g^{-1}vg)=Bv.

and conclude that
\mathbf{G}(\mathbb{K})
acts transitively on
V(\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{K}})
and
\mathcal{C}(\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{K}})
, concluding the proof of (2).

Finally, for
\theta\colon\mathfrak{g}_{k}\to\mathfrak{g}_{k},X\mapsto-X{{}^{\mathsf{T}}\!}
and
A\in\varphi(\mathcal{C}(\mathfrak{g}_{k}))
, we note that
v\in\theta\left(\varphi^{-1}(A)\right)
if and only if
A(\theta(v))=\theta(v)
, that is
\theta A\theta v=v
. The condition
\theta(\mathfrak{a})=\mathfrak{a}
therefore corresponds to
A=\theta A\theta
. We know by Lemma
4.14
(3) that the first-order formula

\exists A\in\varphi(\mathcal{C}(\mathfrak{g}_{k}))\colon\forall v\in\mathfrak{g}_{k}\colon Av=\theta A\theta v

is true for
k=\mathbb{R}
and conclude that it therefore is also true for
k=\mathbb{K}
, proving (3).
∎

4.4.
Split tori of algebraic groups over real closed fields

Let
\mathbb{K}
and
\mathbb{F}
be real closed fields with
\mathbb{K}\subseteq\mathbb{R}\cap\mathbb{F}
.
Let
\mathbf{G}
be a semi-simple self-adjoint (
g\in\mathbf{G}
implies
g{{}^{\mathsf{T}}\!}\in\mathbf{G}
) algebraic
\mathbb{K}
-group. Let
\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{K}}\subseteq\mathbb{K}^{n\times n}
be the
\mathbb{K}
-points of the Lie algebra. Let
\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{R}}
and
\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{F}}
be the semialgebraic extensions, then
\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{R}}
is also the Lie algebra of
\mathbf{G}(\mathbb{R})
.

All subfields
\mathbb{K}
of
\mathbb{R}
are dense in
\mathbb{R}
, in the sense that
\overline{\mathbb{K}}=\mathbb{R}
where
\overline{\mathbb{K}}
is the closure of
\mathbb{K}
in the usual topology of
\mathbb{R}
. The following generalization of this fact will be used in the proof of Theorem
4.17
, the main result of this section.

Lemma 4.16
.

Let
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be an algebraic set defined over
\mathbb{K}
. Then we have
\overline{A_{\mathbb{K}}}=A_{\mathbb{R}}
in the usual
\mathbb{R}^{n}
topology.

Proof.

We first note that the algebraic set
A_{\mathbb{R}}
is Zariski-closed and therefore also closed in the usual topology of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
\overline{A_{\mathbb{R}}}=A_{\mathbb{R}}
. We know that
A_{\mathbb{K}}\subseteq A_{\mathbb{R}}
and therefore
\overline{A_{\mathbb{K}}}\subseteq\overline{A_{\mathbb{R}}}=A_{\mathbb{R}}
.

On the other hand, let
x\in A_{\mathbb{R}}
. Since
\overline{\mathbb{K}}=\mathbb{R}
, there are
y_{k}\in\mathbb{K}^{n}
and
\varepsilon_{k}>0
with
|y_{k}-x|<\varepsilon_{k}
and
\varepsilon_{k}\to 0
as
k\to\infty
. Now we have the following first-order formula

\varphi(y,\varepsilon)\colon\quad\exists z\in A\colon\lVert z-y\rVert<\varepsilon.

For every
k\in\mathbb{N}
, the formula
\varphi(y_{k},\varepsilon_{k})
is true over
\mathbb{R}
, we can just take
z=x
for every
k
. By the transfer principle it is also true over
\mathbb{K}
, which means that we have

z_{k}\in A_{\mathbb{K}}\colon\lVert z_{k}-y_{k}\rVert<\varepsilon_{k}.

We conclude

\lVert z_{k}-x\rVert\leq\lVert z_{k}-y_{k}\rVert+\lVert y_{k}-x\rVert<2\varepsilon_{k}\to 0

as
k\to\infty
, i.e.
x\in\overline{A_{\mathbb{K}}}
.
∎

By Subsection
3.2.1
, a torus may be split with respect to some field, while not being split in a subfield. We now prove that this does not happen as long as all involved fields are real closed.

Theorem 4.17
.

Any maximal
\mathbb{K}
-split torus
\mathbf{S}<\mathbf{G}
is maximal
\mathbb{F}
-split. Moreover, there is a maximal
\mathbb{F}
-split torus
\mathbf{S}
so that
g{{}^{\mathsf{T}}\!}=g
for all
g\in\mathbf{S}
.

Proof.

We first find a maximal
\mathbb{K}
-split torus
\mathbf{T}^{s}
with
g{{}^{\mathsf{T}}\!}=g
for all
g\in\mathbf{T}^{s}
.

Lemma
4.15
(3) states that there is a maximally
\mathbb{K}
-split Cartan subalgebra
\mathfrak{h}\in\mathcal{C}(\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{K}})
such that
\theta(\mathfrak{h})=\mathfrak{h}
. We consider the complexification
\mathfrak{h}_{\mathbb{C}}=\mathbb{C}\otimes_{\mathbb{K}}\mathfrak{h}=\mathfrak{h}_{\mathbb{R}}\oplus i\mathfrak{h}_{\mathbb{R}}\subseteq\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{C}}=\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{R}}\oplus i\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{R}}
, which by Lemma
4.10
(ii) is also a Cartan subalgebra, in the sense that
\mathfrak{h}_{\mathbb{C}}
is abelian and
\operatorname{Nor}_{\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{C}}}(\mathfrak{h}_{\mathbb{C}})=\mathfrak{h}_{\mathbb{C}}
.
Then

\mathbf{T}(\mathbb{C}):=\operatorname{Nor}_{\mathbf{G}(\mathbb{C})}(\mathfrak{h}_{\mathbb{C}})^{\circ}=\{g\in\mathbf{G}(\mathbb{C})\colon\operatorname{Ad}(g)(\mathfrak{h}_{\mathbb{C}})=\mathfrak{h}_{\mathbb{C}}\}^{\circ}.

forms a Zariski-connected closed subgroup of
\mathbf{G}(\mathbb{C})
with Lie algebra
\operatorname{Nor}_{\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{C}}}(\mathfrak{h}_{\mathbb{C}})=\mathfrak{h}_{\mathbb{C}}
. By
[
7
, Lemma 18.5]
,
\mathbf{T}(\mathbb{C})
is defined over
\mathbb{K}
. Since
\mathfrak{h}_{\mathbb{C}}
is finite dimensional,
\mathbf{T}(\mathbb{C})
can be written as the zero-set of finitely many polynomials and is therefore the
\mathbb{C}
-points of an algebraic group
\mathbf{T}
.

Since
\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{C}}
is semisimple and
\mathfrak{h}_{\mathbb{C}}
is a Cartan subalgebra,
\operatorname{ad}(H)
is simultaneously diagonalizable for all
H\in\mathfrak{h}_{\mathbb{C}}
Lemma
4.10
(v). For
H\in\mathfrak{h}_{\mathbb{C}}
,
\exp(H)\in T_{\mathbb{C}}
and
\operatorname{Ad}(\exp(H))=\exp(\operatorname{ad}(H))
is diagonalizable. Possibly not all elements in
\mathbf{T}(\mathbb{C})
are of the form
\exp(H)
with
H\in\mathfrak{h}_{\mathbb{C}}
, but
\exp(\mathfrak{h}_{\mathbb{C}})
is an open neighborhood of the identity and thus generates
\mathbf{T}(\mathbb{C})
. For any
g\in\mathbf{T}(\mathbb{C})
there are
H_{1},\ldots,H_{n}\in\mathfrak{h}_{\mathbb{C}}
such that

\displaystyle\operatorname{Ad}(g)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Ad}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{n}\exp(H_{i})\right)=\operatorname{Ad}\left(\exp\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}H_{i}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\operatorname{ad}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}H_{i}\right)\right)=\exp\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\operatorname{ad}\left(H_{i}\right)\right),

where we used that
\mathfrak{h}_{\mathbb{C}}
is abelian. Since
\operatorname{ad}(\mathfrak{h}_{\mathbb{C}})
is simultaneously diagonalizable,
\operatorname{Ad}(g)
is diagonalizable. Similarly we have
\operatorname{Ad}(g)\operatorname{Ad}(h)=\operatorname{Ad}(h)\operatorname{Ad}(g)
for all
g,h\in\mathbf{T}(\mathbb{C})
. Therefore
\operatorname{Ad}(\mathbf{T}(\mathbb{C}))
is simultaneously diagonalizable, meaning
\mathbf{T}(\mathbb{C})
is diagonalizable by
[
7
, Proposition 8.4]
. Since
\mathbf{T}(\mathbb{C})
is connected,
\mathbf{T}
is a torus by
[
7
, Proposition 8.5]
.

If we restrict to symmetric matrices

\mathbf{T}^{s}=\{g\in\mathbf{T}\colon\sigma(g)=g^{-1}\}

we see that

-split torus. Note that

Now

and

-split torus which satisfies

Next we will prove that

Let

∎

\operatorname{Ad}\circ\exp=\operatorname{Id}\cdot(\chi_{\mathbb{R}}\circ\exp)
as maps
\mathfrak{a}\to\operatorname{GL}(\mathfrak{g}_{\chi})
. Taking the derivative at
0\in\mathfrak{a}
, we get

\displaystyle\operatorname{ad}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{ad}\circ\operatorname{Id}=\operatorname{d}_{e}\!\operatorname{Ad}\circ\operatorname{d}_{0}\exp=\operatorname{d}_{0}(\operatorname{Ad}\circ\exp)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{d}_{0}(\operatorname{Id}\cdot(\chi_{\mathbb{R}}\circ\exp))=\operatorname{Id}\cdot\operatorname{d}_{e}\!\chi_{\mathbb{R}}\circ\operatorname{d}_{0}\exp=\operatorname{Id}\cdot\operatorname{d}_{e}\!\chi_{\mathbb{R}}

as maps
\mathfrak{a}\to\mathfrak{gl}(\mathfrak{g}_{\chi})
. This means that for all
X\in\mathfrak{g}_{\chi}
and all
H\in\mathfrak{a}
, we have
\operatorname{ad}(H)X=\operatorname{d}_{e}\!\chi_{\mathbb{R}}(H)\cdot X
and hence
X\in\mathfrak{g}_{\operatorname{d}_{e}\!\chi_{\mathbb{R}}}
.

Now, we will show that the map
\alpha\mapsto\chi_{\alpha}
in Lemma
5.2
,
\chi_{\alpha}
now viewed as an algebraic character in
\hat{\mathbf{S}}
, actually sends
\Sigma\to\vphantom{\Phi}_{\mathbb{K}}\Phi
. Since

(\chi_{\alpha})_{\mathbb{R}}(\exp(H))=e^{\alpha(H)},

we can easily see that
\chi_{\alpha}\neq 1
, when
\alpha\neq 0
. Next we will show that

\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}\subseteq\left(\mathfrak{g}_{\chi_{\alpha}}^{(\mathbf{S})}\right)_{\mathbb{R}}.

For
a=\exp(H)\in A_{\mathbb{R}}
, we see from the above formula that

\displaystyle\operatorname{Ad}(a)

\displaystyle=\exp(\operatorname{ad}(H))=\exp(\operatorname{Id}\cdot\alpha(H))=\operatorname{Id}\cdot e^{\alpha(H)}=\operatorname{Id}\cdot\chi_{\alpha}(a)

as functions on
\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}
. Since
A_{\mathbb{R}}
is Zariski dense in
\mathbf{S}
,
\operatorname{Ad}(s)X=\chi_{\alpha}(a)\cdot X
for all
s\in\mathbf{S}
and
X\in\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}
.

We note that the two maps between
\vphantom{\Phi}{}_{\mathbb{K}}\Phi
and
\Sigma
are inverses of each other, which follows from the fact that

\left(\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}^{(\mathbf{S})}\right)_{\mathbb{R}}\subseteq\mathfrak{g}_{\operatorname{d}_{e}\!\alpha_{\mathbb{R}}}\subseteq\left(\mathfrak{g}_{\chi_{\operatorname{d}_{e}\!\alpha_{\mathbb{R}}}}^{(\mathbf{S})}\right)_{\mathbb{R}}\quad\text{ for }\alpha\in\vphantom{\Phi}_{\mathbb{K}}\Phi

and

\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}\subseteq\left(\mathfrak{g}_{\chi_{\alpha}}^{(\mathbf{S})}\right)_{\mathbb{R}}\subseteq\mathfrak{g}_{\operatorname{d}_{e}\!(\chi_{\alpha})_{\mathbb{R}}}\quad\text{ for }\alpha\in\Sigma.

We note that since
[\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha},\mathfrak{g}_{\beta}]\subseteq\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha+\beta}
the map
\vphantom{\Phi}{}_{\mathbb{K}}\Phi\to\Sigma
extends to an isomorphism on their vector spaces. For both
\vphantom{\Phi}{}_{\mathbb{K}}\Phi
and
\Sigma
, the Weyl groups
\vphantom{W}{}_{\mathbb{K}}W
and
W_{s}=N_{\mathbb{R}}/M_{\mathbb{R}}
are generated by reflections along hyperplanes perpendicular to the roots
[
7
, Theorem 21.2]
and
[
22
, Proposition 7.32]
. This implies that the scalar products of two roots are preserved under the map
\vphantom{\Phi}{}_{\mathbb{K}}\Phi\to\Sigma
and hence the two root systems are isomorphic.
∎

We can also conclude that the various Weyl groups that can be defined coincide.

Proposition 5.5
.

The following definitions of Weyl groups are isomorphic.

(i)

The Weyl group
W
generated by reflections in roots of the root system
\vphantom{\Phi}{}_{\mathbb{K}}\Phi
.

(ii)

The Weyl group
\vphantom{W}{}_{\mathbb{R}}W=\operatorname{Nor}_{\mathbf{G}(\mathbb{F})}(\mathbf{S}(\mathbb{F}))/\operatorname{Cen}_{\mathbf{G}(\mathbb{F})}(\mathbf{S}(\mathbb{F}))
from the theory of algebraic groups.

(iii)

The Weyl group
W_{s}
generated by reflections in roots of the root system
\Sigma
.

(iv)

The Weyl group in the Lie groups setting
W(G_{\mathbb{R}},A_{\mathbb{R}})=N_{\mathbb{R}}/M_{\mathbb{R}}
.

(v)

The Weyl group
N_{\mathbb{F}}/M_{\mathbb{F}}
of the semialgebraic extensions.

Proof.

By Proposition
5.4
,
\vphantom{\Phi}{}_{\mathbb{K}}\Phi\cong\Sigma
, so (i) and (iii) coincide.
By
[
7
, Theorem 21.2]
,
W=\vphantom{W}_{\mathbb{R}}W
, so the notions (i) and (ii) agree. By
[
22
, Proposition 7.32]
,
W_{s}=W(G_{\mathbb{R}},A_{\mathbb{R}})
, so the notions (iii) and (iv) agree. Let
W_{s}=\{w_{1},\ldots,w_{|W_{s}|}\}
be a list of the finitely many elements in
W_{s}
considered as an abstract group. The first-order formula

\displaystyle\varphi\colon\quad

\displaystyle\exists n_{1},\ldots,n_{|W_{s}|}\in N\colon\left(\bigwedge_{i,j=0\atop i\neq j}^{|W_{s}|}\neg\ n_{j}^{-1}n_{i}\in M\right)\wedge

\displaystyle\left(\forall n\in N\colon\bigvee_{i=1}^{|W_{s}|}n^{-1}n_{i}\in M\right)\wedge\left(\bigwedge_{i,j,\ell=0\atop w_{i}w_{j}=w_{\ell}}^{|W_{s}|}n_{\ell}^{-1}n_{i}n_{j}\in M\right)

states that there are
|W_{s}|
many elements in
N
with distinct representatives in
N/M
and there are only
|W_{s}|
many and they satisfy the same group multiplication table as
W_{s}
. In short it says that
N/M
is isomorphic to
W_{s}
. Now, by (iv) the formula
\varphi
holds over
\mathbb{R}
and we can apply the transfer principle to get the statement over
\mathbb{F}
, showing that the notion in (v) gives the same Weyl group.
∎

5.3.
Iwasawa decomposition
G=KAU

We recall the classical Iwasawa-decomposition, which applies to
G_{\mathbb{R}}
.

Theorem 5.6
.

(
[
22
, Theorem 6.46]
)
Let
G_{\mathbb{R}}
be a semisimple Lie group. Let
\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{R}}=\mathfrak{k}\oplus\mathfrak{a}\oplus\mathfrak{n}
be an Iwasawa decomposition of its Lie algebra. Let
A_{\mathbb{R}}
and
U_{\mathbb{R}}
be the analytic subgroups with Lie algebras
\mathfrak{a}
and
\mathfrak{n}
. Then the multiplication map
K_{\mathbb{R}}\times A_{\mathbb{R}}\times U_{\mathbb{R}}\to G_{\mathbb{R}}
is a diffeomorphism. This decomposition is unique.

We note that all the groups
G_{\mathbb{R}},K_{\mathbb{R}},A_{\mathbb{R}}
and
U_{\mathbb{R}}
are semialgebraic defined over
\mathbb{K}
, hence we can consider
G_{\mathbb{F}},K_{\mathbb{F}},A_{\mathbb{F}}
and
U_{\mathbb{F}}\subseteq\mathbb{F}^{n\times n}
for any real closed field
\mathbb{F}
containing
\mathbb{K}
. We use the transfer principle to deduce the following semialgebraic version of the Iwasawa decomposition. We remark that by taking inverses, we also have a decomposition
G=UAK
in addition to
G=KAU
, both of which will be called the
Iwasawa decomposition
.

Theorem 5.7

(
G=KAU
)
.

For every
g\in G_{\mathbb{F}}
, there are
k\in K_{\mathbb{F}},a\in A_{\mathbb{F}},u\in U_{\mathbb{F}}
such that
g=kau
. This decomposition is unique.

Proof.

We write the decomposition as a first-order formula

\displaystyle\varphi\colon\quad

\displaystyle\left(\forall g\in G\ \exists k\in K,a\in A,u\in U\colon g=kau\right)\ \wedge

\displaystyle\left(\forall k,k^{\prime}\in K,a,a^{\prime}\in A,u,u^{\prime}\in U\colon kau=k^{\prime}a^{\prime}u^{\prime}\to\left(k=k^{\prime}\wedge a=a^{\prime}\wedge u=u^{\prime}\right)\right)

which holds over
\mathbb{R}
by the classical Iwasawa decomposition of Theorem
5.6
.
By the transfer principle, Theorem
2.1
,
\varphi
holds over all real closed fields
\mathbb{F}
. Note that to apply the classical Iwasawa-theorem we use that
K_{\mathbb{R}}
is maximal compact,
\operatorname{Lie}(A_{\mathbb{R}})=\mathfrak{a}
and
\operatorname{Lie}(U_{\mathbb{R}})=\mathfrak{n}
.
∎

5.4.
Cartan decomposition
G=KAK

We use the Cartan decomposition for real Lie groups to find an analogue statement over
\mathbb{F}
.

Theorem 5.8
.

(
[
22
, Theorem 7.39]
)
Every element
g\in G_{\mathbb{R}}
has a decomposition
g=k_{1}ak_{2}
with
k_{1},k_{2}\in K_{\mathbb{R}}
and
a\in A_{\mathbb{R}}
. In this decomposition,
a
is uniquely determined up to a conjugation by a member of
W(G_{\mathbb{R}},A_{\mathbb{R}})
.

Proof.

By Proposition
5.5
,

\vphantom{W}{}_{\mathbb{R}}W=\operatorname{Nor}_{G}(S)/\operatorname{Cen}_{G}(S)
is isomorphic to
W(G_{\mathbb{R}},A_{\mathbb{R}})=\operatorname{Nor}_{K_{\mathbb{R}}}(\mathfrak{a})/\operatorname{Cen}_{K_{\mathbb{R}}}(\mathfrak{a})
. Then this is the statement of
[
22
, Theorem 7.39]
.
∎

Theorem 5.9

(
G=KAK
)
.

Every element
g\in G_{\mathbb{F}}
has a decomposition
g=k_{1}ak_{2}
with
k_{1},k_{2}\in K_{\mathbb{F}}
and
a\in A_{\mathbb{F}}
. In this decomposition,
a
is uniquely determined up to a conjugation by a member of
N_{\mathbb{F}}/M_{\mathbb{F}}
.

Proof.

The existence part of the statement follows from the first-order formula

\varphi\colon\quad\forall g\in G\ \exists k_{1},k_{2}\in K\ \exists a\in A\colon g=k_{1}ak_{2},

which holds over
\mathbb{R}
by Theorem
5.8
and hence over
\mathbb{F}
by the transfer principle. For uniqueness of
a
, we consider the first-order logic formula

\displaystyle\psi\colon\quad

\displaystyle\forall a,a^{\prime}\in A,k_{1},k_{1}^{\prime},k_{2},k_{2}^{\prime}\in K\colon k_{1}ak_{2}=k_{1}^{\prime}a^{\prime}k_{2}^{\prime}\ \to

\displaystyle\left(\exists n\in N\colon a=na^{\prime}n^{-1}\ \wedge\ \left(\forall n^{\prime}\in N\colon a=n^{\prime}a^{\prime}(n^{\prime})^{-1}\to n^{-1}n^{\prime}\in M\right)\right)

which states that
a
is determined up to a conjugation by a member of
N
and that this member is unique up to an element of
M
. Over
\mathbb{R}
,
\psi
holds by Theorem
5.8
, hence
\psi
also holds over
\mathbb{F}
by the transfer principle, concluding the proof.
∎

5.5.
Bruhat decomposition
G=BWB

By
[
22
, page 398]
,
B_{\mathbb{R}}:=M_{\mathbb{R}}A_{\mathbb{R}}U_{\mathbb{R}}
is a closed subgroup of
G_{\mathbb{R}}
and we have the following Bruhat decomposition.

Theorem 5.10
.

(
[
22
, Theorem 7.40]
) Every element
g\in G_{\mathbb{R}}
can be written as
g=b_{1}nb_{2}
with
b_{1},b_{2}\in B_{\mathbb{R}}
and
n\in N_{\mathbb{R}}
. In this decomposition,
n
is unique up to multiplying by an element in
M_{\mathbb{R}}
. Since the spherical Weyl group is
W_{s}=N_{\mathbb{R}}/M_{\mathbb{R}}
, we have a disjoint union of double cosets

G_{\mathbb{R}}=\bigsqcup_{[n]\in W_{s}}B_{\mathbb{R}}nB_{\mathbb{R}}.

The group
B_{\mathbb{R}}
is semialgebraic and the Bruhat decomposition can be extended to
G_{\mathbb{F}}
.

Theorem 5.11

(
G=BWB
)
.

Every element
g\in G_{\mathbb{F}}
can be written as
g=b_{1}nb_{2}
with
b_{1},b_{2}\in B_{\mathbb{F}}
and
n\in N_{\mathbb{F}}
. In this decomposition
n
is unique up to multiplying by an element in
M_{\mathbb{F}}
. We have a disjoint union of double cosets

G_{\mathbb{F}}=\bigsqcup_{[n]\in W_{s}}B_{\mathbb{F}}nB_{\mathbb{F}}.

Proof.

The existence of the decomposition follows directly from the transfer principle and Theorem
5.10
. For uniqueness we utilize the first-order formula

\displaystyle\varphi\colon\quad

\displaystyle\forall b_{1},b_{2},b_{1}^{\prime},b_{2}^{\prime}\in B,n,n^{\prime}\in N

\displaystyle b_{1}nb_{2}=b_{1}^{\prime}n^{\prime}b_{2}^{\prime}\to n^{-1}n^{\prime}\in M,

which holds over
\mathbb{R}
by Theorem
5.10
. Since
W_{s}=N_{\mathbb{F}}/M_{\mathbb{F}}=N_{\mathbb{R}}/M_{\mathbb{R}}
by Proposition
5.5
,
G_{\mathbb{F}}
is a disjoint union as described.
∎

Let

A_{\mathbb{F}}^{+}=\left\{a\in A_{\mathbb{F}}\colon\chi_{\alpha}(a)\geq 1\text{ for all }\alpha\in\Delta\right\},

where
\chi_{\alpha}
is the algebraic character associated to
\alpha\in\Delta
. Then we may choose
a\in A_{\mathbb{F}}^{+}
in the Cartan decomposition
G_{\mathbb{F}}=K_{\mathbb{F}}A_{\mathbb{F}}^{+}K_{\mathbb{F}}
which is then unique, since the Weyl group acts simply transitively on the set of Weyl chambers.

5.6.
Baker-Campbell-Hausdorff formula

Given
X,Y\in\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{R}}
, the Baker-Campbell-Hausdorff formula gives a formal power series description of
Z=\log(\exp(X)\exp(Y))
in terms of
X,Y
and iterated commutators of
X
and
Y
, see for instance
[
21
, Proposition V.1]
or
[
25
]
. The formal power series converges in a neighborhood of the identity
[
19
, Theorem 3.1 in X.3]
, but may not converge everywhere in general. If
X,Y\in\mathfrak{n}_{\mathbb{R}}
, then the power series is given by a polynomial and thus converges everywhere
[
35
]
. Since only finitely many terms are involved, one can see directly or invoke the transfer principle to obtain the Baker-Campbell-Hausdorff formula for elements in
\mathfrak{n}_{\mathbb{F}}:=\bigoplus_{\alpha>0}(\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha})_{\mathbb{F}}
.

Proposition 5.12
.

For every
X,Y\in\mathfrak{n}_{\mathbb{F}}
, there is
Z\in\mathfrak{n}_{\mathbb{F}}
such that
\exp(X)\exp(Y)=\exp(Z)
. The element
Z
is given by a finite sum of iterated commutators, the first terms of which are given by

Z=X+Y+\frac{1}{2}[X,Y]+\frac{1}{12}\left(\left[X,\left[X,Y\right]\right]-\left[Y,\left[Y,X\right]\right]\right)-\frac{1}{24}\left[Y,\left[X,\left[X,Y\right]\right]\right]+\ldots

There are various variations in the literature. We will make use of the Zassenhaus formula

\exp(X+Y)=\exp(X)\exp(Y)\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\left[X,Y\right]\right)\exp\left(\frac{1}{3}[Y,[X,Y]]+\frac{1}{6}[X,[X,Y]]\right)\cdots

for
X,Y\in\mathfrak{n}_{\mathbb{F}}
, which can be obtained from the above by calculating
\exp(-X)\exp(X+Y)=\exp(Z)
iteratively
[
25
]
.

5.7.
The unipotent group
U

The root spaces
\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}
in the root decomposition

\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{R}}=\mathfrak{g}_{0}\oplus\bigoplus_{\alpha\in\Sigma}\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}.

corresponding to positive roots, consist of simultaneously nilpotent elements by Lemma
4.6
. Therefore the group

U_{\mathbb{R}}=\exp(\mathfrak{n})=\exp\left(\bigoplus_{\alpha\in\Sigma_{>0}}\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}\right)

is unipotent. In fact
U_{\mathbb{R}}
is the
\mathbb{R}
-points of an algebraic group
\mathbf{U}
and the semialgebraic extension of the semialgebraic
\mathbb{K}
-group
U=U_{\mathbb{K}}=\mathbf{U}(\mathbb{K})
.

Let
\alpha\in\Sigma
. When
2\alpha\notin\Sigma
, the root space
\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}
is an ideal. In any case,
\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}\oplus\mathfrak{g}_{2\alpha}
is an ideal, where
\mathfrak{g}_{2\alpha}=0
if
2\alpha\notin\Sigma
. Thus, for every
\alpha\in\Sigma
there is a unipotent subgroup
U_{\alpha}=\exp(\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}\oplus\mathfrak{g}_{2\alpha})<U
, called the
root group
which is also a semialgebraic
\mathbb{K}
-group, since the exponential function is a polynomial on nilpotent elements. Note that
U_{2\alpha}<U_{\alpha}
if both exist.

The following Lemma shows that
A_{\mathbb{F}}
normalizes the root groups
(U_{\alpha})_{\mathbb{F}}
.

Lemma 5.13
.

Let
\alpha\in\Sigma,X\in\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha},X^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{g}_{2\alpha}
and
a\in A_{\mathbb{F}}
. Then

a\exp\left(X+X^{\prime}\right)a^{-1}=\exp\left(\chi_{\alpha}(a)X+\chi_{\alpha}(a)^{2}X^{\prime}\right),

where
\chi_{\alpha}\colon A_{\mathbb{F}}\to\mathbb{F}_{>0}
is the algebraic character from Lemma
5.2
.

Proof.

We know that
\operatorname{Ad}_{a}(X)=\chi_{\alpha}(a)X
and
\operatorname{Ad}_{a}(X^{\prime})=\chi_{2\alpha}(a)X^{\prime}=\chi_{\alpha}(a)^{2}X^{\prime}
.
We then use distributivity of matrix multiplication to obtain

\displaystyle a\exp(X+X^{\prime})a^{-1}

\displaystyle=a\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(X+X^{\prime})^{n}}{n!}a^{-1}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n!}a(X+X^{\prime})^{n}a^{-1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n!}\left(a(X+X^{\prime})a^{-1}\right)^{n}=\exp\left(aXa^{-1}+aX^{\prime}a^{-1}\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\operatorname{Ad}_{a}(X)+\operatorname{Ad}_{a}(X^{\prime})\right)=\exp\left(\chi_{\alpha}(a)X+\chi_{\alpha}(a)^{2}X^{\prime}\right)

∎

We consider
\Theta\subseteq\Sigma_{>0}

closed under addition
, meaning that for any
\alpha,\beta\in\Theta
, if the sum
\alpha+\beta\in\Sigma
, then
\alpha+\beta\in\Theta
. For any
\Theta\subseteq\Sigma_{>0}
closed under addition,

\mathfrak{g}_{\Theta}:=\bigoplus_{\alpha\in\Theta}\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}

is an ideal, since
\left[\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha},\mathfrak{g}_{\beta}\right]\subseteq\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha+\beta}
for any
\alpha,\beta\in\Sigma
, see Proposition
4.4
. Hence

(U_{\Theta})_{\mathbb{R}}:=\exp(\mathfrak{g}_{\Theta})

is the
\mathbb{R}
-extension of a semialgebraic group
U_{\Theta}
, in fact for
\Theta=\Sigma_{>0}
we recover
U=U_{\Sigma_{>0}}
. As a consequence of the Baker-Campbell-Hausdorff-formula we obtain the following description of
(U_{\Theta})_{\mathbb{F}}
.

Lemma 5.14
.

Let
\Theta=\left\{\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{k}\right\}\subseteq\Sigma_{>0}
be a subset closed under addition with
\alpha_{1}>\ldots>\alpha_{k}
. Then

(U_{\Theta})_{\mathbb{F}}:=\exp\left(\bigoplus_{\alpha\in\Theta}(\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha})_{\mathbb{F}}\right)=\prod_{i=1}^{k}\exp\left(\left(\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha_{i}}\right)_{\mathbb{F}}\right)=\langle u\in U_{\alpha}\colon\alpha\in\Theta\rangle.

Proof.

We start by proving

\exp\left(\bigoplus_{\alpha\in\Theta}(\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha})_{\mathbb{F}}\right)\subseteq\prod_{i=1}^{k}\exp\left(\left(\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha_{i}}\right)_{\mathbb{F}}\right)

using induction over
|\Theta|
. If
|\Theta|=1
, then the statement is immediate. Now assume that
\Theta=\{\alpha_{1}\}\cup\Theta^{\prime}
with
\alpha_{1}>\beta
for all
\beta\in\Theta^{\prime}
and such that

\exp\left(\bigoplus_{\alpha\in\Theta^{\prime}}(\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha})_{\mathbb{F}}\right)\subseteq\prod_{i=2}^{k}\exp\left(\left(\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha_{i}}\right)_{\mathbb{F}}\right).

Let
X_{i}\in(\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha_{i}})_{\mathbb{F}}
.
Recall that
[\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha},\mathfrak{g}_{\beta}]\subseteq\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha+\beta}
for all
\alpha,\beta\in\Sigma
, see Proposition
4.4
. Thus we can use the Zassenhaus-formula in Section
5.6
about the Baker-Campbell-Hausdorff formula to obtain the finite product

\displaystyle\exp\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}X_{i}\right)

\displaystyle=\exp(X_{1})\exp\left(\sum_{i=2}^{k}X_{i}\right)\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\left[X_{1},\sum_{i=2}^{k}X_{i}\right]\right)\ldots

and then repeatedly apply the original version in Proposition
5.12
to simplify the expression back to

\displaystyle\exp(X_{1})\exp\left(\sum_{i=2}^{k}\tilde{X}_{i}\right)

for some new
\tilde{X}_{i}\in(\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha_{i}})_{\mathbb{F}}
. Applying the induction hypothesis, we conclude

\displaystyle\exp\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}X_{i}\right)

\displaystyle\in\exp(X_{1})\prod_{i=2}^{k}\exp\left(\left(\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha_{i}}\right)_{\mathbb{F}}\right)\subseteq\prod_{i=1}^{k}\exp\left(\left(\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha_{i}}\right)_{\mathbb{F}}\right).

Next, we notice that
\prod_{i=1}^{k}\exp((\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha_{i}})_{\mathbb{F}})\subseteq\langle u\in(U_{\alpha})_{\mathbb{F}}\colon\alpha\in\Theta\rangle
. Finally, we prove the inclusion

\langle u\in U_{\alpha}\colon\alpha\in\Theta\rangle\subseteq\exp\left(\bigoplus_{\alpha\in\Theta}(\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha})_{\mathbb{F}}\right)

using induction over the word length of an element in
\langle u\in U_{\alpha}\colon\alpha\in\Theta\rangle
. If the word length is
1
, then the statement holds. Now assume that

v=\exp\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}X_{i}\right)

for some
X_{i}\in(\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha_{i}})_{\mathbb{F}}
and consider
u=\exp(X)
for some
X\in(\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha})_{\mathbb{F}}
and some
\alpha\in\Theta
. We apply Proposition
5.12
to obtain

\displaystyle uv

\displaystyle=\exp(X)\exp\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}X_{i}\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(X+\sum_{i=1}^{k}X_{i}+\frac{1}{2}\left[X,\sum_{i=1}^{k}X_{i}\right]+\ldots\right)\in\exp\left(\bigoplus_{\alpha\in\Theta}(\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha})_{\mathbb{F}}\right)

concluding the proof.
∎

We point out that the order of the product expression in Lemma
5.14
starts with
\exp((\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha})_{\mathbb{F}})
corresponding to the largest
\alpha=\alpha_{1}
followed in decreasing order. Writing elements of
U_{\Theta}
as the inverses of elements in
U_{\Theta}
, also gives an expression starting with the smallest root, followed by an increasing order. The following technical Lemma is useful in applications.

Lemma 5.15
.

Let
\Theta\subseteq\Sigma_{>0}
be a subset closed under addition and
\alpha\in\Sigma_{>0}
such that
\alpha>\beta
for all
\beta\in\Theta
. Then
uU_{\Theta}u^{-1}=U_{\Theta}
for all
u\in\exp((\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha})_{\mathbb{F}})
.

For every subset
\Psi\subseteq\Sigma_{>0}
, elements
u\in U_{\Theta}
can be expressed as
u=u^{\prime}u^{\prime\prime}
with

u^{\prime}\in\prod_{\alpha\in\Theta\cap\Psi}\exp((\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha})_{\mathbb{F}})\quad\text{ and }\quad u^{\prime\prime}\in\prod_{\alpha\in\Theta\setminus\Psi}\exp((\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha})_{\mathbb{F}}).

Proof.

Let
X\in(\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha})_{\mathbb{F}}
and
\exp(Y)\in U_{\Theta}
. Then we can apply the Baker-Campbell-Hausdorff formula, Proposition
5.12
, twice to obtain

\displaystyle\exp(X)\exp(Y)\exp(X)^{-1}

\displaystyle=\exp\left(X+Y+\frac{1}{2}[X,Y]+\ldots\right)\exp(-X)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(Y+\frac{1}{2}[X,Y]+\ldots\right)\in U_{\Theta}.

We show the second statement using induction over the size of
\Theta
. If
|\Theta|=1
, the statement is clear. Now consider the subset closed under addition
\Theta^{\prime}:=\Theta\setminus\{\alpha\}
where
\alpha
is the largest element of
\Theta
. For
u\in U_{\Theta}
, we use Lemma
5.14
to obtain
u=u_{\alpha}\bar{u}
with
\bar{u}\in U_{\Theta^{\prime}}
. If
\alpha\notin\Psi
, then the first part of this Lemma can be used to instead write
u=\bar{u}u_{\alpha}
. Either way, the induction assumption gives

\bar{u}^{\prime}\in\prod_{\beta\in\Theta^{\prime}\cap\Psi}\exp((\mathfrak{g}_{\beta})_{\mathbb{F}})\quad\text{ and }\quad\bar{u}^{\prime\prime}\in\prod_{\beta\in\Theta^{\prime}\setminus\Psi}\exp((\mathfrak{g}_{\beta})_{\mathbb{F}})

such that
\bar{u}=\bar{u}^{\prime}\bar{u}^{\prime\prime}
. Then
u=u_{\alpha}\bar{u}^{\prime}\bar{u}^{\prime\prime}
or
u=\bar{u}^{\prime}\bar{u}^{\prime\prime}u_{\alpha}
as required.
∎

Lemmas
5.13
and
5.14
can be used to prove that
A_{\mathbb{F}}
normalizes all of
U_{\mathbb{F}}
.

Proposition 5.16
.

Let
\Theta\subseteq\Sigma_{>0}
closed under addition. Then
A_{\mathbb{F}}
normalizes
(U_{\Theta})_{\mathbb{F}}
: for all
a\in A_{\mathbb{F}},u\in(U_{\Theta})_{\mathbb{F}}\colon aua^{-1}\in(U_{\Theta})_{\mathbb{F}}
.
In particular
aU_{\mathbb{F}}a^{-1}=U_{\mathbb{F}}
for all
a\in A_{\mathbb{F}}
.

Proof.

Let
a\in A_{\mathbb{F}}
. By Lemma
5.14
, any
u\in(U_{\Theta})_{\mathbb{F}}
can be written as
u=u_{\alpha_{1}}\cdot\ldots\cdot u_{\alpha_{k}}
with
u_{\alpha_{i}}\in(U_{\alpha_{i}})_{\mathbb{F}}
where
\Theta=\{\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{k}\}
. By Lemma
5.13
,
au_{\alpha_{i}}a^{-1}\in(U_{\alpha_{i}})_{\mathbb{F}}
, so

where we used Lemma
5.14
again.
∎

5.8.
Jacobson-Morozov Lemma for algebraic groups

On the level of algebraic groups, the Jacobson-Morozov Lemma seems to be folklore. Since we could not find a detailed treatment in the literature we will give its statement and proof here. The classical Jacobson-Morozov Lemma applies to semisimple Lie algebras over fields of characteristic
0
. The following formulation is more general than what we proved in Lemma
4.9
.

Proposition 5.17
.

[
9
, VIII §11.2 Prop. 2]
Let
\mathfrak{g}
be a semisimple Lie algebra and
x\in\mathfrak{g}
a non-zero nilpotent element. Then there exist
h,y\in\mathfrak{g}
such that
(x,y,h)
is an
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
-triplet, meaning that

[h,x]=2x,\quad[h,y]=-2y,\quad[x,y]=h

and hence the Lie algebra generated by
x,h,y
is isomorphic to
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
.

We will use
[
7
, Chapter 7]
and
[
33
]
to show the following version of the Jacobson-Morozov Lemma for algebraic groups over an algebraically closed field
\mathbb{D}
of characteristic
0
. Note that any semisimple linear algebraic group with Lie algebra
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
is isomorphic to either
\operatorname{SL}_{2}
or
\operatorname{PGL}_{2}

[
20
, Corollary 32.2]
.

Proposition 5.18
.

Let
g\in\mathbf{G}
be any unipotent element in a semisimple linear algebraic group
\mathbf{G}
over an algebraically closed field
\mathbb{D}
of characteristic
0
. Then there is an algebraic subgroup
\operatorname{SL}_{g}<\mathbf{G}
with Lie algebra
\operatorname{Lie}(\operatorname{SL}_{g})\cong\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
and
g\in\operatorname{SL}_{g}
. The element
\log(g)\in\operatorname{Lie}(\mathbf{G})
corresponds to

\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}\in\mathfrak{sl}_{2}.

Moreover, if
g\in\mathbf{G}(\mathbb{F})
for a field
\mathbb{F}\subseteq\mathbb{D}
, then
\operatorname{SL}_{g}
can be assumed to be defined over
\mathbb{F}
.

Proof.

Since
g\in\mathbf{G}
is unipotent, the nilpotent element
x=\log(g)\in\mathfrak{g}
exists.
By the Jacobson-Morozov Lemma, Proposition
5.17
, there are
y,h\in\mathfrak{g}
such that
(x,y,h)
is an
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
-triplet. Let
\mathfrak{sl}_{g}
denote the subalgebra of
\mathfrak{g}
generated by
x,y
and
h
. Thus,
x\in\operatorname{sl}_{g}
corresponds to

\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}\in\mathfrak{sl}_{2}

under the isomorphism
\mathfrak{sl}_{g}\cong\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
.
We follow
[
7
, 7.1]
and define

\mathcal{A}(\mathfrak{sl}_{g})=\bigcap\left\{\mathbf{H}\colon\mathbf{H}\text{ algebraic subgroup of $\mathbf{G}$ with }\mathfrak{sl}_{g}\subseteq\operatorname{Lie}(H)\right\},

which is a closed connected algebraic subgroup. Let

\operatorname{SL}_{g}=[\mathcal{A}(\mathfrak{sl}_{g}),\mathcal{A}(\mathfrak{sl}_{g})]

be its commutator group, which is an algebraic group by
[
7
, 2.3]
since
\mathcal{A}(\mathfrak{sl}_{g})
is connected. We then use
[
7
]
to obtain

\displaystyle\operatorname{Lie}(\operatorname{SL}_{g})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 7.8}}{{=}}[\operatorname{Lie}(\mathcal{A}(\mathfrak{sl}_{g})),\operatorname{Lie}(\mathcal{A}(\mathfrak{sl}_{g}))]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 7.9}}{{=}}[\mathfrak{sl}_{g},\mathfrak{sl}_{g}]=\mathfrak{sl}_{g}

where the last equality follows from
\mathfrak{sl}_{g}\cong\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
. The map

\displaystyle\alpha\colon\mathbf{G}_{a}

\displaystyle\to\mathbf{G}

\displaystyle t

\displaystyle\mapsto\exp(tx)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(tx)^{n}}{n!}

is a polynomial and hence a morphism of algebraic groups. We have
\operatorname{Lie}(\alpha(\mathbf{G}_{a}))=\langle x\rangle\subseteq\mathfrak{sl}_{g}
and hence

g\in\alpha(\mathbf{G}_{a})\subseteq\operatorname{SL}_{g}

by
[
7
, 7.1(2)]
.

If
g\in\mathbf{G}(\mathbb{F})
,
\log(g)\in\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{F}}
, and we may assume
y,h\in\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{F}}
as well. Thus
\mathfrak{sl}_{g}\subseteq\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{F}}
. Now
\mathcal{A}(\mathfrak{sl}_{g})
is defined over
\mathbb{F}
as in
[
7
, 2.1(b)]
. Then by
[
7
, 2.3]
,
\operatorname{SL}_{g}
is defined over
\mathbb{F}
.
∎

Let us return to the case of real closed fields
\mathbb{K}\subseteq\mathbb{F}\cap\mathbb{R}
. Recall from Section
5.7
, that
U_{\mathbb{F}}
has subgroups
(U_{\alpha})_{\mathbb{F}}
consisting of unipotent elements for
\alpha\in\Sigma_{>0}
defined as the semialgebraic extensions of
U_{\alpha}=\exp((\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha})_{\mathbb{K}}\oplus(\mathfrak{g}_{2\alpha})_{\mathbb{K}})
. The following is another variation of the Jacobson-Morozov Lemma that only works when
\Sigma
is reduced.

Proposition 5.19
.

Let
\alpha\in\Sigma
and assume
\mathfrak{g}_{2\alpha}=0
. Let
u\in(U_{\alpha})_{\mathbb{F}}
. Then there are
X\in(\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha})_{\mathbb{F}}
and
t\in\mathbb{F}
such that
u=\exp(tX)
. Let
(X,Y,H)
be the
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
-triplet of Lemma
4.9
. Then there is a group homomorphism
\varphi_{\mathbb{F}}\colon\operatorname{SL}(2,\mathbb{F})\to G_{\mathbb{F}}
that is a restriction of a morphism of algebraic groups

\varphi\colon\operatorname{SL}(2,\mathbb{D})\to\mathbf{G}

defined over
\mathbb{F}
such that
\varphi
has finite kernel and

\displaystyle\varphi_{\mathbb{F}}\begin{pmatrix}1&t\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}=u=\exp(tX)\quad\text{and}\quad\varphi_{\mathbb{F}}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
t&1\end{pmatrix}=\exp(tY).

If
\varphi
is not injective, then
\ker(\varphi)\cong\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
and
\varphi
factors through the isomorphism

\operatorname{PGL}(2,\mathbb{D}):=\operatorname{SL(2,\mathbb{D})}/\ker(\varphi)\xrightarrow{\sim}\varphi(\operatorname{SL}(2,\mathbb{D}))

which is also defined over
\mathbb{F}
. Moreover
\varphi(g{{}^{\mathsf{T}}\!})=\varphi(g){{}^{\mathsf{T}}\!}
, for any
g\in\operatorname{SL}(2,\mathbb{F})
.

Proof.

Let
u,X,t
as in the statement. We apply Proposition
5.18
to
\exp(X)\in(U_{\alpha})_{\mathbb{F}}
to obtain an algebraic group
\operatorname{SL}_{\exp(X)}(\mathbb{D})<\mathbf{G}

\mathbb{F}

\operatorname{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{D})

[
20

, the only algebraic groups with Lie algebra

\operatorname{SL}(2,\mathbb{D})
and
\operatorname{PGL}(2,\mathbb{D}):=\operatorname{SL}(2,\mathbb{D})/Z(\operatorname{SL(2,\mathbb{D})})

\varphi\colon\operatorname{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{D})\to\operatorname{SL}_{\exp(X)}
with finite kernel.

We note that since
X\in(\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha})_{\mathbb{F}}

\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
-triplet is described by
(X,Y,H)
in Lemma
4.9
and we have a Lie algebra isomorphism
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{D})\cong\operatorname{Lie}(\operatorname{SL}_{\exp(X)}(\mathbb{D}))
. We note that the two Lie algebra isomorphisms

\displaystyle\varphi\colon\operatorname{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{D})\xrightarrow{\sim}

\displaystyle\ \operatorname{SL}_{\exp(X)}(\mathbb{D})

\displaystyle\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{D})\cong

\displaystyle\ \mathfrak{sl}_{\exp(X)}(\mathbb{D})

\displaystyle\hskip-60.27759pt\cong\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{D})

\displaystyle\ X

\displaystyle\hskip-60.27759pt\mapsfrom\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}

may not coincide. Since
\operatorname{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{D})
does not have any outer automorphisms (
[
20
, Theorem 14.1]
and
[
16

), the two isomorphisms only differ by
\operatorname{Ad}(g)
for some

\operatorname{d}\!\varphi\colon\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{D})\to\operatorname{Lie}(\operatorname{SL}_{\exp(X)}(\mathbb{D}))

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}\mapsto X,\quad\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix}\mapsto H\quad\text{and}\quad\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}\mapsto Y.

Even though the conjugation may be by an element in
\operatorname{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{D})
, the explicit description of

shows that
\varphi
is still defined over
\mathbb{F}
. The description of

and
\exp(tY)

∎

Since in the

Lemma 5.21
.

5.9.
Rank 1 subgroups

Let
\alpha\in\Sigma
. In this section we investigate the group generated by the algebraic groups
\mathbf{U}_{\alpha}
and
\mathbf{U}_{-\alpha}
. When
\dim(\mathbf{U}_{\alpha})=1
, then this group is given by the image of the Jacobson-Morozov-morphism

from Proposition
5.19
. In general, when
\dim(\mathbf{U}_{\alpha})
is not

, the group generated is larger than the image of

, but is still rank

Theorem 5.22
.

Let
\alpha\in\Sigma
. Then there is a semisimple self-adjoint linear algebraic group
\mathbf{L}_{\pm\alpha}
defined over
\mathbb{K}
such that

(i)

\operatorname{Lie}(\mathbf{L}_{\pm\alpha})=(\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}\oplus\mathfrak{g}_{2\alpha})\oplus(\mathfrak{g}_{-\alpha}\oplus\mathfrak{g}_{-2\alpha})\oplus([\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha},\mathfrak{g}_{-\alpha}]+[\mathfrak{g}_{2\alpha},\mathfrak{g}_{-2\alpha}])
.

(ii)

\operatorname{Rank}_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathbf{L}_{\pm\alpha})=\operatorname{Rank}_{\mathbb{F}}(\mathbf{L}_{\pm\alpha})=1
.

Proof.

We consider the semisimple
\mathbb{D}
-Lie algebra

\mathfrak{l}:=(\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}\oplus\mathfrak{g}_{2\alpha})\oplus(\mathfrak{g}_{-\alpha}\oplus\mathfrak{g}_{-2\alpha})\oplus([\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha},\mathfrak{g}_{-\alpha}]+[\mathfrak{g}_{2\alpha},\mathfrak{g}_{-2\alpha}]).

Similar to the proof of Proposition
5.18
we follow Borel
[
7
, 7.1]
by defining the connected normal algebraic subgroup

\mathcal{A}(\mathfrak{l})=\bigcap\left\{\mathbf{H}\colon\mathbf{H}<\mathbf{G}\text{ is an algebraic subgroup }\mathfrak{l}\subseteq\operatorname{Lie}(\mathbf{H})\right\}

of
\mathbf{G}
. We set
\mathbf{L}_{\pm\alpha}:=[\mathcal{A}(\mathfrak{l}),\mathcal{A}(\mathfrak{l})]
. Then (i) follows by

\operatorname{Lie}(\mathbf{L}_{\pm\alpha})=[\mathcal{A}(\mathfrak{l}),\mathcal{A}(\mathfrak{l})]=[\mathfrak{l},\mathfrak{l}]=\mathfrak{l},

where we used that
\mathfrak{l}
is semisimple in the step

. The algebraic group
\mathcal{A}(\mathfrak{l})
is defined over
\mathbb{K}
by
[
7
, 2.1(b)]
. Thus
\mathbf{L}_{\pm\alpha}
is defined over
\mathbb{K}
and connected by
[
7
, 2.3]
. Since
\mathfrak{l}
is semisimple, so is
\mathbf{L}_{\pm\alpha}
. Since
\theta(X)=-X{{}^{\mathsf{T}}\!}
and

and

by Proposition
4.4

\mathfrak{l}
and
\mathbf{L}_{\pm\alpha}
are self-adjoint.

For (ii), Lemma
4.8
tells us that the real rank of

is

and hence
\operatorname{Rank}_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathbf{L}_{\pm\alpha})=1
. By the theorem on split tori, Theorem

We now consider the semialgebraic subgroup

of

.

.

(L_{\pm\alpha})_{\mathbb{F}}\subseteq\operatorname{Cen}_{G_{\mathbb{F}}}(\left\{a\in A_{\mathbb{F}}\colon\chi_{\alpha}(a)=1\right\})

containing the identity can be semialgebraically extended to
(A_{\pm\alpha})_{\mathbb{F}}
. The following is a version on Lemma
5.20
, but now for the rank 1 group
(L_{\pm\alpha})_{\mathbb{F}}
.

Lemma 5.24
.

For every
t\in\mathbb{F}_{>0}
, there is an
a\in(A_{\pm\alpha})_{\mathbb{F}}
such that
\chi_{\alpha}(a)=t
.

Proof.

This is clearly a first-order statement and it therefore suffices to show it for
\mathbb{R}=\mathbb{F}
. Let
X\in(\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha})_{\mathbb{R}}\setminus 0
. From Lemma
4.8
, we know that
\exp([X,\theta(X)])\in(A_{\pm\alpha})_{\mathbb{R}}
. Given
t\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
, let

a:=\exp\left(\frac{\log(t)}{\alpha([X,\theta(X)])}\cdot[X,\theta(X)]\right).

Then

\chi_{\alpha}(a)=e^{\alpha(\log(a))}=e^{\log(t)}=t

by Lemma
5.2
.
∎

The root space decomposition then gives
U_{\pm\alpha}=\exp(\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}\oplus\mathfrak{g}_{2\alpha})=U_{\alpha}
, and the spherical Weyl group
W_{\pm\alpha}=(N_{\pm\alpha})_{\mathbb{F}}/(M_{\pm\alpha})_{\mathbb{F}}
can be defined from

\displaystyle(N_{\pm\alpha})_{\mathbb{F}}

\displaystyle:=\operatorname{Nor}_{(K_{\pm\alpha})_{\mathbb{F}}}((A_{\pm\alpha})_{\mathbb{F}})

\displaystyle(M_{\pm\alpha})_{\mathbb{F}}

\displaystyle:=\operatorname{Cen}_{(K_{\pm\alpha})_{\mathbb{F}}}((A_{\pm\alpha})_{\mathbb{F}}).

We note that as a consequence of Lemma
5.23
,
N_{\pm\alpha}\subseteq N_{\mathbb{F}}
and also
\operatorname{Nor}_{L_{\pm\alpha}}(A_{\pm\alpha})\subseteq\operatorname{Nor}_{K_{\mathbb{F}}}(A_{\mathbb{F}})
.

In the case that
\Sigma
is reduced, there is an interpretation in terms of the Jacobson-Morozov Lemma. Given
u\in(U_{\pm\alpha})_{\mathbb{F}}
, we note that the morphism
\varphi_{\mathbb{F}}\colon\operatorname{SL}(2,\mathbb{F})\to G_{\mathbb{F}}
from Proposition
5.19
takes values in
(L_{\pm\alpha})_{\mathbb{F}}
. In fact,

(A_{\pm\alpha})_{\mathbb{F}}=\varphi_{\mathbb{F}}\left(\left\{\begin{pmatrix}\lambda&0\\
0&\lambda^{-1}\end{pmatrix}\colon\lambda>0\right\}\right)

since over
\mathbb{R}
, both of these groups are connected and one-dimensional.
By Lemma
5.21
, the element

m=\varphi_{\mathbb{F}}\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
-1&0\end{pmatrix}\in(N_{\pm\alpha})_{\mathbb{F}}

is a representative of the only non-trivial element in the spherical Weyl group
W_{\pm\alpha}=\{[\operatorname{Id}],[m]\}
. We can now apply the Bruhat decomposition Theorem
5.11
to
(L_{\pm\alpha})_{\mathbb{F}}
.

Corollary 5.25
.

Let
(B_{\alpha})_{\mathbb{F}}:=(M_{\pm\alpha})_{\mathbb{F}}(A_{\pm\alpha})_{\mathbb{F}}(U_{\alpha})_{\mathbb{F}}
. Then

(L_{\pm\alpha})_{\mathbb{F}}=(B_{\alpha})_{\mathbb{F}}(N_{\pm\alpha})_{\mathbb{F}}(B_{\alpha})_{\mathbb{F}},

and the element
m\in(N_{\pm\alpha})_{\mathbb{F}}
is a representative of a unique element in
W_{\pm\alpha}
, so

(L_{\pm\alpha})_{\mathbb{F}}=(B_{\alpha})_{\mathbb{F}}\ \sqcup\ (B_{\alpha})_{\mathbb{F}}\cdot m\cdot(B_{\alpha})_{\mathbb{F}}.

The following variation is useful in applications.

Corollary 5.26
.

For

\displaystyle(B_{\alpha})_{\mathbb{F}}

\displaystyle:=(M_{\pm\alpha})_{\mathbb{F}}(A_{\pm\alpha})_{\mathbb{F}}(U_{\alpha})_{\mathbb{F}},

\displaystyle(B_{-\alpha})_{\mathbb{F}}

\displaystyle:=(M_{\pm\alpha})_{\mathbb{F}}(A_{\pm\alpha})_{\mathbb{F}}(U_{-\alpha})_{\mathbb{F}},

we obtain the decompositions

\displaystyle(L_{\pm\alpha})_{\mathbb{F}}

\displaystyle=(B_{\alpha})_{\mathbb{F}}\cdot(B_{-\alpha})_{\mathbb{F}}\ \sqcup\ m\cdot(B_{-\alpha})_{\mathbb{F}},

\displaystyle(L_{\pm\alpha})_{\mathbb{F}}

\displaystyle=(B_{\alpha})_{\mathbb{F}}\cdot(B_{-\alpha})_{\mathbb{F}}\ \sqcup\ (B_{\alpha})_{\mathbb{F}}\cdot m\cdot(B_{-\alpha})_{\mathbb{F}},

\displaystyle(L_{\pm\alpha})_{\mathbb{F}}

\displaystyle=(B_{\alpha})_{\mathbb{F}}(N_{\pm\alpha})_{\mathbb{F}}(B_{-\alpha})_{\mathbb{F}}

where in the last one, the element in
(N_{\pm\alpha})_{\mathbb{F}}
is a representative of a unique element in
W_{\pm\alpha}=\left\{\operatorname{Id},[m]\right\}
.

Proof.

We use
m^{-1}\cdot(U_{\alpha})_{\mathbb{F}}\cdot m=(U_{-\alpha})_{\mathbb{F}}
from Lemma
5.21
. By Corollary
5.25
, we choose
m^{-1}
as the representative of
[m^{-1}]=[m]\in W_{\pm\alpha}
and obtain

(L_{\pm\alpha})_{\mathbb{F}}=(B_{\alpha})_{\mathbb{F}}\ \sqcup\ (B_{\alpha})_{\mathbb{F}}\cdot m^{-1}\cdot(B_{\alpha})_{\mathbb{F}},

so

(L_{\pm\alpha})_{\mathbb{F}}=m\cdot(B_{-\alpha})_{\mathbb{F}}\cdot m^{-1}\ \sqcup\ (B_{\alpha})_{\mathbb{F}}\cdot(B_{-\alpha})_{\mathbb{F}}\cdot m^{-1}.

Multiplying this expression on the right by
m\in(L_{\pm\alpha})_{\mathbb{F}}
results in

(L_{\pm\alpha})_{\mathbb{F}}=(B_{\alpha})_{\mathbb{F}}\cdot(B_{-\alpha})_{\mathbb{F}}\ \sqcup\ m\cdot(B_{-\alpha})_{\mathbb{F}},

and further multiplying by
(B_{\alpha})_{\mathbb{F}}
on the left results in the remaining two decompositions.
∎

5.10.
Kostant convexity

Using the Iwasawa decomposition
G_{\mathbb{F}}=U_{\mathbb{F}}A_{\mathbb{F}}K_{\mathbb{F}}
, Theorem
5.6
, we associate to every
g=uak\in G_{\mathbb{R}}
its
A
-component
a_{\mathbb{R}}(g)=a\in A_{\mathbb{R}}
. The following is Kostant’s convexity theorem, which we will generalize to
G_{\mathbb{F}}
in this chapter.

Theorem 5.27
.

[
23
, Theorem 4.1]

For every
b\in A_{\mathbb{R}}
,

\left\{a_{\mathbb{R}}(kb)\in A_{\mathbb{R}}\colon k\in K_{\mathbb{R}}\right\}=\exp\left(\operatorname{conv}(W_{s}\log(b))\right),

where
\log\colon A_{\mathbb{R}}\to\mathfrak{a}
is the inverse of
\exp
,
W_{s}
is the spherical Weyl group acting on
\mathfrak{a}
and
\operatorname{conv}(W_{s}\log(b))
is the convex hull of the Weyl group orbit of
\log(b)
.

The left hand side of the equation in Theorem
5.27
is already a semialgebraic set and we will reformulate the right hand side as a semialgebraic set as well. For this, we first analyze the root system
\Sigma\subseteq\mathfrak{a}^{\star}
.

Recall from Section
4.1
, that we have a scalar product
B_{\theta}
on
\mathfrak{a}
, which can be used to set up an isomorphism
\mathfrak{a}^{\star}\to\mathfrak{a},\alpha\mapsto H_{\alpha}
that satisfies the defining property
\lambda(H)=B_{\theta}(H_{\lambda},H)
for all
H\in\mathfrak{a}
. In this section we will denote
B_{\theta}
as well as the corresponding scalar product on
\mathfrak{a}^{\star}
with brackets
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
. For
\lambda\in\mathfrak{a}^{\star}
define

x_{\lambda}=\frac{2}{\langle H_{\lambda},H_{\lambda}\rangle}H_{\lambda}=\frac{2}{\langle\lambda,\lambda\rangle}H_{\lambda}

which satisfies the property that for all
\alpha,\beta\in\Sigma
,

\langle H_{\alpha},x_{\beta}\rangle=\frac{2\langle H_{\alpha},H_{\beta}\rangle}{\langle H_{\beta},H_{\beta}\rangle}\in\mathbb{Z}

since
\Sigma
is a crystallographic root system, Theorem
4.5
. Let
\Delta=\left\{\delta_{1},\ldots,\delta_{r}\right\}
be a basis of
\Sigma
and abbreviate
x_{i}:=x_{\delta_{i}}
and
H_{i}:=H_{\delta_{i}}
. Let

\overline{\mathfrak{a}}^{+}=\left\{H\in\mathfrak{a}\colon\delta(H)\geq 0\text{ for all }\delta\in\Delta\right\}.

On the way to prove Theorem
5.27
, Kostant describes
\operatorname{conv}(W_{s}x)
using the closed convex cone

\mathfrak{a}_{p}:=\left\{x\in\mathfrak{a}\colon x=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}x_{i}\right\}

illustrated in Figure
1
.

Figure 1
.
Root system of type
A_{2}
associated to
\operatorname{SL}_{3}
. The convex cone
\mathfrak{a}_{p}
(orange stripes) can be viewed as spanned by the
H_{\alpha_{i}}
or as the intersection of the half-spaces defined by the primitive vectors
e_{i}
.

Lemma 5.28
.

[
23
, Lemma 3.3.(2)]

Let
x,y\in\overline{\mathfrak{a}}^{+}
. Then

\displaystyle y\in\operatorname{conv}(W_{s}x)\quad\iff\quad x-y\in\mathfrak{a}_{p}.

Now we want to describe
\mathfrak{a}_{p}
by inequalities. The cone
\mathfrak{a}_{p}
is an intersection of open half-spaces defined by the half-planes

E_{j}=\sum_{k\neq j}\mathbb{R}x_{k}.

Any vector
x\in\mathfrak{a}
orthogonal to
E_{j}
satisfies
\langle x,x_{i}\rangle=0
for all
i\neq j
. Writing
x=\sum_{k}\lambda_{jk}H_{k}
, we have for all
i\neq j

\langle x,x_{i}\rangle=\sum_{k}\lambda_{jk}\langle H_{k},x_{i}\rangle=0.

For every
i\neq j
, this is a homogeneous linear equation with variables
\lambda_{j1},\ldots,\lambda_{jr}
and coefficients
\langle H_{k},x_{i}\rangle\in\mathbb{Z}
. Therefore there is a rational (and hence integer) solution for the
\lambda_{jk}
.
This shows that
x\in E_{j}^{\perp}\setminus\{0\}
may be chosen to lie in the lattice
\Gamma=\sum_{l=1}^{r}\mathbb{Z}H_{k}
. There are two primitive vectors in
\Gamma\cap E_{j}^{\perp}
. Let
e_{j}
be the unique one that is on the same side of
\partial E_{j}
as
\mathfrak{a}_{p}
. Thus

\mathfrak{a}_{p}=\left\{x\in\mathfrak{a}\colon\langle e_{j},x\rangle\geq 0\text{ for all }j\right\}.

Under the isomorphism
\mathfrak{a}^{\star}\cong\mathfrak{a}
, the lattice
\Gamma
corresponds to
L=\sum_{\alpha\in\Delta}\mathbb{Z}\alpha
and we define
\gamma_{j}\in L
to be the element corresponding to
e_{j}\in\Gamma
. By Lemma
5.2
,
\gamma_{j}
defines an algebraic character
\chi_{j}\colon A_{\mathbb{R}}\to\mathbb{R}
satisfying

\chi_{j}(\exp(H))=e^{\gamma_{j}(H)}

for all
H\in\mathfrak{a}
. The multiplicative closed Weyl chamber is

A^{+}_{\mathbb{R}}:=\left\{a\in A_{\mathbb{R}}\colon\chi_{\delta}(a)\geq 1\text{ for all }\delta\in\Delta\right\}=\exp\overline{\mathfrak{a}}^{+}

which is a semialgebraic set and thus has an
\mathbb{F}
-extension
A^{+}_{\mathbb{F}}
. Using the the Iwasawa decomposition
G_{\mathbb{F}}=U_{\mathbb{F}}A_{\mathbb{F}}K_{\mathbb{F}}
, Theorem
5.7
, we associate to every
g=uak\in G_{\mathbb{F}}
its
A
-component
a_{\mathbb{F}}(g)=a\in A_{\mathbb{F}}
as before. We can now conclude the following version of Kostant’s convexity theorem for
G_{\mathbb{F}}
, illustrated in Figure
2
.

Figure 2
.
Root system of type
A_{2}
associated to
\operatorname{SL}_{3}
. The convex set in Kostant’s convexity Theorem
5.29
defined by inequalities is illustrated in purple.

Theorem 5.29
.

For all
b\in A_{\mathbb{F}}^{+}
, we have

\left\{a\in A_{\mathbb{F}}^{+}\colon\exists k\in K_{\mathbb{F}},a_{\mathbb{F}}(kb)=a\right\}=\left\{a\in A_{\mathbb{F}}^{+}\colon\chi_{i}(a)\leq\chi_{i}(b)\text{ for all }i\right\}.

Proof.

We first verify that the theorem holds for
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{R}
. An element
a\in A_{\mathbb{R}}^{+}
satisfies
a_{\mathbb{R}}(kb)=a
, for some
k\in K_{\mathbb{R}}
, if and only if
a\in A_{\mathbb{R}}^{+}\cap\exp(\operatorname{conv}(W_{s}\log(b)))
by Theorem
5.27
. This means
a=\exp(H)
for some
H\in\overline{\mathfrak{a}}^{+}
and
H\in\operatorname{conv}(W_{s}\log(b))
. By Lemma
5.28
, this is equivalent to

\log(b)-H\in\mathfrak{a}_{p}=\left\{x\in\mathfrak{a}\colon\langle e_{j},x\rangle\geq 0\text{ for all }j\right\},

and taking exponents this is equivalent to

\chi_{i}(ba^{-1})\geq 1

for all
i
, so

\chi_{i}(a)\leq\chi_{i}(b),

as in the Theorem. This concludes the case
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{R}
. Now we note that being part of either set in the statement can be formulated as a first-order formula. Since over
\mathbb{R}
the two formulae imply each other, they also imply each other over
\mathbb{F}
by the transfer principle. This concludes the proof.
∎

The following Lemma is useful in applications.

Lemma 5.30
.

For all
\eta\in L=\sum_{\delta\in\Delta}\mathbb{Z}\delta
, we have

\eta=\sum_{\ell=1}^{r}\frac{\langle\eta,\delta_{\ell}\rangle}{\langle\gamma_{\ell},\delta_{\ell}\rangle}\gamma_{\ell}

and for
\eta^{+}:=\sum_{\alpha\in\Sigma_{>0}}\alpha
,
\eta^{+}
is a positive linear combination of the
\gamma_{\ell}
,

\eta^{+}=\sum_{\alpha\in\Sigma_{>0}}\alpha\in\sum_{\ell=1}^{r}\mathbb{Q}_{>0}\gamma_{\ell}.

Proof.

By definition
\langle x_{j},e_{\ell}\rangle=0
for all
\ell\neq j
, but
\langle x_{\ell},e_{\ell}\rangle\neq 0
, since otherwise
e_{\ell}=0
. This implies that
\left\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{r}\right\}
and hence
\left\{\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{r}\right\}
are linearly independent: for if
x=\sum_{\ell}\lambda_{\ell}e_{\ell}=0
, then
\langle x_{j},x\rangle=\lambda_{j}\langle x_{j},e_{j}\rangle=0
, so
\lambda_{j}=0
for all
j
.

Therefore we can find
n_{i\ell}\in\mathbb{Q}
such that

\delta_{i}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{r}n_{i\ell}\gamma_{\ell}.

Since
\langle\gamma_{\ell},\delta_{k}\rangle=0
when
\ell\neq k
, we have
\langle\delta_{i},\delta_{k}\rangle=n_{ik}\langle\gamma_{k},\delta_{k}\rangle
. Since
x_{\ell}\in\mathfrak{a}_{p}
,
\langle e_{\ell},x_{\ell}\rangle\geq 0
and since
e_{j}\neq 0
and
\langle e_{\ell},x_{j}\rangle=0
when
\ell\neq j
, actually
\langle e_{\ell},x_{\ell}\rangle>0
. Thus also
\langle\gamma_{\ell},\delta_{\ell}\rangle>0
, so we can divide
\langle\delta_{i},\delta_{\ell}\rangle=n_{i\ell}\langle\gamma_{\ell},\delta_{\ell}\rangle
by
\langle\gamma_{\ell},\delta_{\ell}\rangle
to get

n_{i\ell}=\frac{\langle\delta_{i},\delta_{\ell}\rangle}{\langle\gamma_{\ell},\delta_{\ell}\rangle}.

For
\eta\in L=\sum_{\delta\in\Delta}\delta
we have

\displaystyle\eta

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\lambda_{i}\delta_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\sum_{\ell=1}^{r}\lambda_{i}\frac{\langle\delta_{i},\delta_{\ell}\rangle}{\langle\gamma_{\ell},\delta_{\ell}\rangle}\gamma_{\ell}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{r}\frac{\langle\sum_{i=1}^{r}\lambda_{i}\delta_{i},\delta_{\ell}\rangle}{\langle\gamma_{\ell},\delta_{\ell}\rangle}\gamma_{\ell}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{r}\frac{\langle\eta,\delta_{\ell}\rangle}{\langle\gamma_{\ell},\delta_{\ell}\rangle}\gamma_{\ell}.

For
\delta\in\Delta
, recall that the reflection
\sigma_{\delta}
permutes the elements of
\Sigma_{>0}\setminus\{\delta,2\delta\}
,
[
8
, VI.1.6 Cor. 1]
. Let

\eta^{+}=\sum_{\alpha\in\Sigma^{+}}\alpha.

If
\sigma_{\delta}(\eta-\delta)=\eta-\delta
, for instance when
\Sigma
is reduced, then we can use that the reflection
\sigma_{\delta}
preserves the scalar product to obtain

\displaystyle\langle\eta,\delta\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\sigma_{\delta}(\eta),-\delta\rangle=\langle\sigma_{\delta}\left(\eta-\delta\right)+\sigma_{\delta}(\delta),-\delta\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle(\eta-\delta)-\delta,-\delta\rangle=-\langle\eta,\delta\rangle+2\langle\delta,\delta\rangle.

so
\langle\eta,\delta\rangle=\langle\delta,\delta\rangle>0
. If
\Sigma
is not reduced and
\delta,2\delta\in\Sigma
, then
\sigma(\eta-3\delta)=\eta-3\delta
and

\displaystyle\langle\eta,\delta\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\sigma_{\delta}(\eta),-\delta\rangle=\langle\sigma_{\delta}\left(\eta-3\delta\right)+\sigma_{\delta}(3\delta),-\delta\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle(\eta-3\delta)-3\delta,-\delta\rangle=-\langle\eta,\delta\rangle+6\langle\delta,\delta\rangle,

so
\langle\eta,\delta\rangle=3\langle\delta,\delta\rangle>0
. This shows that
\eta
is a positive linear combination of elements
\gamma_{\ell}
.
∎

Note that for the slightly simpler
\eta=\sum_{\delta\in\Delta}\delta
,
\eta
may not be a positive linear combination of elements in
\sum\mathbb{Z}\gamma_{\ell}
, see Figure
3
for an example of type
\operatorname{G}_{2}
.

.

corresponding to the coroots
x_{1},x_{2}

\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}
