Clinical Context in Artificial Intelligence-Enabled Electrocardiographic Risk Stratification for Atrial Fibrillation
Authors: Hiroki Sato, Naohiko Takahashi
Published: 2026

We read with interest the evaluation by Matsuo et al1 of the artificial intelligence-enabled electrocardiography (AI-ECG) system (FCP-9900, Fukuda Denshi Co, Ltd) for atrial fibrillation (AF) risk stratification. The system assigns sinus-rhythm ECGs to 4 AF-likelihood categories: low, mid-low, mid-high, or high.

In their cohort, 50 of 137 patients with documented AF (36.5%) were assigned to the high AF-likelihood category. In our cohort, 46 of 104 patients with documented AF (44.2%) were assigned to the high AF-likelihood category using the same system.2 Similar proportions were observed in these 2 distinct clinical cohorts.

Why might some patients with AF nevertheless be assigned a lower AF-likelihood category by AI-ECG? In our study, hypertension was associated with lower odds of high or mid-high AF-likelihood assignment (OR: 0.25; 95% CI: 0.10-0.66), as was greater left ventricular mass index (OR: 0.72 per 10 g/m2; 95% CI: 0.57-0.90).2 Matsuo et al1 did not report characteristics separately for the derivation and external validation cohorts or by AF status. These data would help assess cohort comparability and clinical differences relevant to AI-ECG assignment.

Matsuo et al1 developed machine learning models to discriminate patients with and without AF using clinical and ECG-derived variables. In models 2 and 3, CHADS2 and CHA2DS2-VASc scores, which share several components, were included with their constituent clinical variables, introducing substantial predictor redundancy. Although they acknowledged multicollinearity in these models, its potential influence on SHapley Additive exPlanations feature attribution warrants consideration.3 For model 2, sensitivity analyses using a nonredundant predictor set could help assess the robustness of the reported feature importance. Model 3 SHapley Additive exPlanations results were not reported; the same redundancy would complicate their interpretation.

Prior work in incident AF showed that AI-ECG and clinical risk profiles provide complementary information.4 These findings support interpreting AI-ECG output alongside clinical and structural characteristics.
