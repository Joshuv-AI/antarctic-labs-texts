Transcriptional and cellular reprogramming in Anabaena sp. PCC7120 biofilms: implications for stress response
Authors: Irene Olivan-Muro, Alodia Liesa, Emma Sevilla, María F Fillat
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•The transition from a planktonic to a sessile lifestyle in Anabaena sp. PCC 7120 is driven by a massive transcriptomic reprogramming involving 17.7% of the genome.•Sessile growth involves cell envelope remodeling and lower anabolic activity.•While nitrogen deficiency and salinity promote biofilm formation, iron deficiency exerts contrasting effects on sessile development.•Stress-adaptation pathways are directly linked to biofilm development.Keywords: Cyanobacteria, Biofilms, Differential transcriptomics, Cellular reprogramming, Environmental stresses

Introduction

Microbes exposed to adverse conditions in their ecological niches often benefit from the establishment of organized communities which provide more favourable microenvironments, known as biofilms. These are characterised by the presence of a self-produced complex matrix composed of exopolysaccharides (EPS), lipids, extracellular DNA (eDNA) and proteins, which serves as a cohesive scaffold for the community, mediates surface attachment, hinders access of predators or toxic compounds and concentrates scarce micronutrients and moisture (Flemming and Wingender, 2010). Phototrophic biofilms are often the first colonisers of limited environments, where microorganisms such as cyanobacteria and microalgae provide energy and organic carbon (Rossi and De Philippis, 2015). Some cyanobacterial species are also capable of fixing atmospheric nitrogen, with minimal nutritional requirements. This, along with unique adaptive mechanisms and metabolic diversity, allow them to thrive in all types of ecosystems, contributing heavily to global biogeological cycles and the sustenance of the trophic chain (Field et al., 1998).

Research on biofilm formation has a direct impact on ecology and medicine, but these communities are also gaining interest due to their biotechnological potential, taking advantage of their distinct characteristics for the production of value-added products, wastewater treatment and resource recovery or engineering biodegradable self-regenerating material, among others (Germec et al., 2020, Bharathi et al., 2023). Cyanobacterial biofilms incorporate further attractive features, such as photoautotrophic growth and the characteristics of their EPS. As other prokaryotes, cyanobacteria produce both capsular (CPS) and released (RPS) polysaccharides, but these polymers show greater diversity in monosaccharide composition and modifications, including the presence of sulphate groups and uronic acids which confer a negative charge. This results in polymers with emulsifying and adhesive properties that readily interact with cationic molecules, highly suitable for areas such as the food industry or bioremediation of heavy metals and other cationic contaminants (De Philippis and Vincenzini, 1998, Pereira et al., 2009). The pathways involved in their synthesis are not fully characterised, but generally engage glycosyltransferases (GTs) that append activated monosaccharides and various proteins that assemble the full polymers and export them to the extracellular environment (Schmid et al., 2015).

Cyanobacterial biofilms present an additional layer of complexity beyond that of heterotrophic bacterial biofilms due to the influence of light incidence and self-shading, which differs significantly from planktonic growth conditions. Accordingly, comparative studies have revealed differential abundance or expression of photosystem and phycobilisome components and associated proteins between the planktonic and biofilm lifestyles in diverse cyanobacteria (Romeu et al., 2020, Simkovsky et al., 2022). Further recent works employing omics technologies have been performed on brackish isolate Halomicronema sp., which predominantly grows as a biofilm, and mixed biofilms formed by filamentous diazotrophic Tolypothrix sp. PCC7720 and heterotroph Pseudomonas taiwanensis VLB 120 (Bozan et al., 2023, Caldara et al., 2025). Nevertheless, the differences between planktonic and sessile communities of a same microorganism and the genetic factors that dictate lifestyle shift and permanence remain unclear, which hinders their understanding and applications.

Here, we explored the differences between planktonic cultures of the diazotrophic, filamentous cyanobacterium Anabaena sp. PCC7120 (also known as Nostoc sp. PCC7120) and their biofilm counterparts on a morphological and transcriptional level through microscopic observations and comparative RNA sequencing. In addition, the effect of various stresses on biofilm formation was evaluated, revealing a direct connection between the pathways involved in the adaptation to adverse conditions and sessile growth.

Results

Filaments found within biofilms present morphological alterations

Biofilm-derived Anabaena filaments exhibited larger, less uniform cells and occasionally aberrant morphologies (e.g., large spherical or elongated akinete-like cells) compared to the homogenous, oval-shaped cells of planktonic filaments (Fig. 1). Specifically, the average cell size was significantly greater (<0.0001 based on a Mann-Whitney test) for biofilm samples, approximately corresponding to the 75% percentile of the planktonic population, and its distribution more skewed (6.48 vs 0.49 skewness). Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM) images of biofilms on glass coverslips revealed the elongated filament phenotype within its native environment. The biofilm architecture was clearly supported by an extracellular matrix that enveloped and anchored the filaments to the substrate (Fig. 1D and S1). A visible 'print' or outline of detached filaments can be observed across the sample surface (Fig. 1D, red arrowheads). This feature suggests either that the biofilm development locally modified the substrate surface, resulting in differential staining upon sample loss, or that the filaments detached later during the sample processing, revealing a previously shielded surface.

Transcriptome analysis of biofilm cultures of Anabaena sp. PCC7120 reveals vast differences between sessile and planktonic cells

RNA-seq analysis of Anabaena biofilms and their corresponding planktonic fractions led to the identification of 1099 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) for a |fold change| ≤ 2 and FDR corrected p-value ≤ 0.05 threshold. These constitute 17.7% of the Anabaena genome and were distributed through the chromosome and all plasmids except for the ζ plasmid (Fig. 2). A similar number of genes displayed increased and diminished levels of expression (59% vs 41%). A volcano plot reflecting their magnitude in the context of their statistical significance is available in Fig. S2. The Principal Component Analysis (PCA) to visualize global transcriptomic relationships and confirm the clustering of biological replicates and clustered gene expression heatmap are shown in Fig. S3. To validate the results obtained, we monitored the expression of 12 diverse genes by Real Time RT-PCR in biofilm and planktonic samples, which was in good agreement with our RNA-seq data (Fig. S4).

A functional annotation was proposed for 641 differentially expressed genes (DEGs), utilizing information from KEGG, UniProt, and published data (Table S2). DEGs encoding hypothetical proteins (Tables S3 and S4) and transposons (Table S5) are reported separately. Annotated DEGs were organized into functional categories, revealing that the most highly represented categories are carbohydrate metabolism (6.7% of total DEGs) and photosynthesis and respiration (5.8% of total DEGs) (Fig. 3A, Table S3). Enrichment analysis using GO biological process and PFAM domain terms supported these findings, with significant enrichment for terms such as "carbohydrate metabolic process", "photosynthesis", "chlorophyll A-B binding", and "phycobilisome" (Fig. 3B). Further significant categories included "regulatory functions and cell signalling" (linked to the enriched PFAM term "sigma-70 region 4", 5% of all DEGs), and "ribosome biogenesis and translation" (underlined by the GO term "translation", 4.3% of all DEGs). The transcriptional profile also indicated an impact on a diverse range of other biological processes, such as nitrogen metabolism, cell shape and envelope maintenance, or transport and micronutrient homeostasis, among others.

Transcriptional profile of carbohydrate-related DEGs in biofilms

Biofilm growth is linked to widespread modulation of genes involved in polysaccharide biosynthesis, osmolyte metabolism, and central carbon pathways. Relevant DEGs include 26 genes annotated as glycosyltransferases (GTs). Genes located in EPS synthesis clusters I, II and V (Potnis et al., 2021) are downregulated, whereas genes all2285-all2286, in Cluster III were induced. Other induced genes included α-glucosidase (alr5331), gluconeogenesis enzymes, such as phosphoenolpyruvate synthase (alr3146-alr3147) and fructose-bisphosphate aldolase fbaB (all3735), and the trehalose osmolyte metabolism operon tre/mts/mth (all0166-all0168) (Table S2). The osmolyte-related genes lti2 (all0875) and susB (all1058-all1059), modulated by OrrA (Schwartz et al., 1998, Katoh et al., 2004, Katoh, 2012, Kimura et al., 2022), were strongly induced (14.0-, 18.0-, and 10.3-fold, respectively). Additionally, a sharp increase in biofilm-associated expression was observed for transketolase all4052 (14.1-fold), and transglycosylase-associated proteins asr3089 (14.9-fold) and asl4654 (8.1-fold). While most pentose phosphate pathway genes are only moderately affected, phosphoketolase pket1 (all1483), is a remarkable exception, with 452.5-fold increase compared to the planktonic fractions.

Downregulation of photosynthesis in biofilms is coupled to activation of photoprotective genes

Genes involved in photosynthesis were mostly repressed in biofilms compared to planktonic fractions (Fig. 3A, Table S2). Conversely, both Anabaena phycobilisome dismantlement-promoting NblA paralogs, asr4517 and asr8504, are upregulated in biofilms, the latter with a 8.6-fold change. A pronounced increase in expression, with fold change values ranging from 5 to 21, was observed for genes of the CAB/ELIP/HLIP family (Table S2), which are involved in protecting photosystems from photooxidative damage (Komenda and Sobotka, 2016). Similarly, the expression of the NTD-OCP (N-terminal domain of the orange carotenoid protein) homolog alr4783 was 11.6-fold higher in the sessile fraction. A similar situation applies to additional NTD-OCP paralogs all1123 and all4941, though only Alr4783 has been identified as an efficient single oxygen quencher (López-Igual et al., 2016).

Nitrogen assimilation and amino acid metabolism genes displayed diminished expression in biofilms

A clear overall tendency towards the downregulation of genes involved in nitrogen and amino acid metabolism was observed (Table S2). This includes the attenuation of transport systems for nitrate/nitrite, ammonium, and urea (Herrero and Flores, 2019), alongside biosynthetic pathways for arginine and aromatic amino acids (Table S2). Arginine metabolism may be particularly relevant, considering the significant enrichment of the “L-arginine biosynthetic process” GO term (Fig. 3B). The nitrogen signalling PII interaction protein PipX (asr0485) was also downregulated. Conversely, biofilm growth promotes inactivation of glutamine synthetase via a 22.6-fold induction of gifA (asl2329) and mobilization of cyanophycin stores through increased cphB2 (all0571) expression (Table S2).

Biofilm formation resulted in a global repression of the translational machinery

The shift to a biofilm state led to the downregulation of 39 of the 47 DEGs associated with ribosome biogenesis and translation. This repression was localized in the largest downregulated cluster, all4185-all4215, which includes 21 repressed ribosomal component genes (Fig. 2 - band r). This repression coincided with the induction (2.4-fold) of the ribosome hibernation promoting factor lrtA (alr3184) which promotes inactive 100S ribosome formation (Ehira et al., 2005), and 3- to 4-fold upregulation of several rRNA methyltransferases, including rimK (alr3303), whose product modifies ribosomal protein S6 and is crucial for translational control and environmental adaptation (Kang et al., 1989).

Remodelling of the cellular envelope and transport across membranes in biofilms

Biofilm transition triggers a profound transformation of the cell envelope, highlighted by the 23.7-fold increase of the septal structure gene sjdR (alr0248) (Schätzle et al., 2021), the stress-related membrane protein AstaP homolog alr1819 (22.2-fold) (Kimura et al., 2022), and the putative proteolipid membrane modulator/autolysin system asl0163-all0164 (22.4- and 17.6-fold). This structural shift is further supported by the induction of lipid raft-associated genes such as flotillin (alr4528), NfeD-slipin and S-layer components (Table S2). In contrast, genes involved in peptidoglycan and lipopolysaccharide (LPS) synthesis, including the mur pathway and dap genes responsible for meso-DAP production, a precursor for both lysine and peptidoglycan (Chatterjee et al., 2021), were downregulated. Decreased expression was also observed in genes required for cell-to-cell communication and septal junction formation, such as amiC3 (all1140) (Zheng et al., 2017) and fraI (alr4714) (Janović et al., 2024) .

Cell envelope remodelling also affectted micronutrient and metal transport. Transcriptomic data indicate reduced intracellular levels of iron, copper, zinc, and nickel, reflected in the downregulation of uptake systems, including iron transporters of the Fec system and, iacT (all4026) (Stevanovic et al., 2012), as well as the zinc uptake permease znuC (all0830). In contrast, efflux components, such as the P-type copper exporter alr7635 and Zn/Cd exporter aztA (alr7622) were upregulated. An exception to this overall trend was manganese, for which uptake appeared enhanced, with ManR-regulated genes all3575 and alr5134 showing up to 5.7-fold higher expression.

A major regulatory feature in biofilms was the dramatic 30-fold upregulation of the cation transport regulator of the ChaB family (asr4093) and its paralogs, together with downregulation of the Na+/Ca2+ exchanger ChaA homolog (all0626). TonB2 (all3585), involved in envelope integrity and aggregation, was induced 16.2-fold. Broad downregulation of general nutrient transporters was observed in sessile cells. This included genes governing phosphorus and sulfur homeostasis, and bicarbonate uptake (Table S2). General transport systems, such as the outer membrane porin oprB1 (alr0834) and the TonB-dependent transporter btuB1 (all3310), were also reduced, while the transport system permease alr1500 was highly upregulated (12.6-fold).

Regulatory and signalling pathways differentially expressed in biofilms

In addition to well-characterized regulators, 55 DEGs with broad or uncharacterized regulatory roles were identified in biofilms. Key changes involved cellular timing and signalling genes, including circadian clock components kaiC (alr2886) and pex (alr3979). The quorum sensing enzyme aiiC (all3924) was downregulated (-3.6-fold) potentially affecting N-acyl homoserine lactone degradation (Romero et al., 2008). Transcriptional and translational machinery were also altered, as evidenced by the reduced levels of the RNA chaperone Hfq (asl2047), altered expression of four RNAP sigma factors, and changes in the α subunit rpoA (all4191).

Global regulatory shifts were highlighted by a 2.6-fold increase of PerR/FurC (alr0957) (Sarasa-Buisan et al., 2023), which, alongside CRP, may modulate the alr2137-alr2138 two-component system (TCS) (Sarasa-Buisan et al., 2023, Xu and Su, 2009). Other relevant transcriptional regulators differentially expressed included the heterocyst-related CalB (all2080), downregulated 4.1-fold, and a low-CO₂-responsive TetR-family regulator (all7523), upregulated 5.7-fold. Signal transduction was broadly affected, with five complete TCSs showing differential expression (Table S2). Furthermore, two hybrid sensor-regulators upstream of the OrrA osmotic stress regulator, along with the CO₂-sensitive Ca2+-sensor asr1131, which modulates nitrogen metabolism and filament integrity (Walter et al., 2020), were induced up to 5.7-fold, likely reflecting responses to restricted gas exchange and osmotic stress within the biofilm matrix.

Transcriptional reprogramming of stress response is coupled with upregulation of redox homeostasis and detoxification pathways in biofilms

Biofilm growth in Anabaena involves profound transcriptional reprogramming of stress-responsive pathways and robust upregulation of redox homeostasis and detoxification systems. Defense-related genes, including the GO term "defense response to virus" and PFAM RAMPs (Repeat Associated Mysterious Proteins) domains, were significantly enriched among DEGs (Fig. 3B). CRISPR-associated and RAMP clusters 1 and 2 were severely repressed, with cluster 1 members (e.g., cas10 (all1479)) downregulated from -90.8- to -557.5-fold (Fig. 2A – band i). In contrast, most abiotic stress markers were highly induced, including the universal stress protein UspA (all1122) (Sen et al., 2019), phage shock protein PspA (all2342), and akinete markers anAKa (all4050) and anAKc (all4051) (11.2- to 25.3-fold) (Kimura et al., 2022, Ehira et al., 2005), alongside the CsbD-family stress response proteins asr4653 and asr1134 (11.2- and 23.8-fold). Activation of Dps family proteins (Singh et al., 2025), toxin-antitoxin systems (e.g. mazFE), and the putative bacteriocin asr1202 (upregulated 11.0-fold) further signals a shift toward nutrient limitation and enhanced cellular endurance or competition within the biofilm matrix .

Genomic integrity was likely prioritized through the SOS response (GO term enriched, Fig. 3B), and DNA repair machinery, involving uvrB (all1132), UV damage endonuclease uvsE (alr1263), and the DNAse hemerythrin Alr3199 (24.8-fold) (Padmaja et al., 2011). Furthermore, protein homeostasis was modulated by the induction of the heat-inducible repressor hrcA (alr4044) with concomitant downregulation of its GroEL/GroES targets (Rajaram and Apte, 2010), while the stress-responsive Pfp1-like deglycase/protease alr0893 (Katoh et al., 2004, Shrivastava et al., 2015) showed the highest induction (38.0-fold).

This comprehensive stress response was coupled with a massive upregulation of redox and detoxification pathways, whose genes exhibited some of the most pronounced fold-changes across the entire transcriptome, indicating a significant oxidative challenge in the biofilm. This group included alcohol dehydrogenases alr0895 and alr0897 (51.5 and 18.2-fold), and the Mn-catalase katB (alr3090, 23.3-fold). Both alcohol dehydrogenases are part of a highly induced cluster (all0891 to alr0898) with an average fold change of 33.7 (Fig. 2A - band g). Further upregulated detoxification genes included glyoxalases all3014 and alr3904 (6.3 and 7.8-fold) and rhodanese-related sulfurtransferase alr3790 (7.6-fold), collectively highlighting a coordinated oxidative stress and detoxification response (Table S2).

Sessile growth induces a transcriptomic signature mirroring multiple stress responses

Comparison with published transcriptomic and proteomic datasets shows that pathways affected by sessile growth substantially overlap with those altered under various stress conditions, often in the same direction (Fig. 4 and Table S6). Contingency analyses (Table S7) reveal that this overlap is particularly significant (p-value < 0.0001) for desiccation, saline or selenium stresses and sulfur limitation, with approximately 30% of the differentially expressed/accumulated elements in these studies corresponding to biofilm DEGs (Katoh et al., 2004, Kimura et al., 2022, Higo et al., 2007, Rai et al., 2013, Sen et al., 2017, Banerjee et al., 2024, Hurali et al., 2024, Kharwar and Mishra, 2024, Srivastava et al., 2023, Panda et al., 2014, Srivastava et al., 2023). A smaller yet significant overlap (p-value < 0.025) was observed for low temperature, UV-B exposure, methyl viologen-induced oxidative stress, and γ-ray irradiation. Despite numerous biofilm DEGs associated with nitrogen metabolism, overlap with proteins differentially accumulated under nitrogen deficiency at various time points (Zhang et al., 2021) was not significant (p-value 0.4543), suggesting these pathways are not intrinsically related. Based on their stress-responsive behaviour, clustering of all biofilm DEGs into 15 groups (silhouette coefficient 0.609) supports these findings, with desiccation and saline/selenium stress contributing most strongly to cluster formation (Fig. S5).

Biofilm formation by Anabaena sp. PCC7120 under adverse conditions

Biofilm growth is known to confer increased resistance to multiple biotic and abiotic stresses, and these environmental pressures can also induce the sessile lifestyle. To investigate this interplay, we evaluated the effect of nitrogen deficiency, iron deficiency and saline stress on biofilm formation. After 10 days of static growth, cultures exposed to 50 mM NaCl or nitrogen limitation exhibited increased biofilm formation, with 2.6- and 4.8-fold more surface-attached biomass, respectively. In contrast, attempting to grow biofilms from iron-starved cultures with no further iron addition led to a virtually null adhesion to the substrate (Fig. 5). Total chlorophyll a measurements, accounting for both sessile and planktonic cells, revealed no significant differences in overall growth across conditions (Fig. S6). This indicates that impaired attachment under iron deficiency reflects a specific adhesion defect rather than reduced biomass.

Discussion

The transition of Anabaena from planktonic to biofilm growth entails global transcriptional reprogramming of structural, metabolic, and regulatory pathways. Biofilm development deeply remodels the cell envelope, through the coordinated regulation of genes associated with peptidoglycan organization, LPS synthesis and septal junction maintenance. A significant enrichment of the “S-layer homology” PFAM term (Fig. 3B), alongside the increased expression of membrane-anchored transporters, suggests the adaptation of envelope architecture to enhance cell adhesion. Although S-layers are implicated in cell adhesion and surface recognition (Šmarda et al., 2002), the absence of a visible S-layer in Anabaena leaves the exact function of these proteins unknown. Conversely, downregulated OprB porins, alongside other envelope changes, may facilitate vesicle release, contributing to biofilm stability and intercellular communication within the matrix (Cardoso et al., 2021). Reduced expression of fatty acid desaturases suggests an increase in the saturated fatty acid pool, leading to enhanced membrane rigidity (Sinensky, 1974), potentially supporting lipid raft formation. This is consistent with differential expression of NfeD-slipin systems and flotillin (Green et al., 2009, Ukleja et al., 2024), which may stabilize motionless filaments and aid adhesion. Concurrently, activation of membrane stress responses is evidenced by altered expression of mechanosensitive channels (alr3692 and alr3020), which protect against membrane depolarization and hypo-osmotic shock (Kung et al., 2010). Their roles, which have not been characterized for Anabaena, may not be redundant, since both respond to desiccation but only alr3692 is linked to salt and nitrogen stress (Kimura et al., 2022, Zhang et al., 2021). Additional stress signatures include upregulation of a tandem Pmp3-like protein-autolysin (asl0163-all0164), and a PspA homolog (all2343), mirroring defensive strategies observed in E. coli biofilms (Beloin et al., 2004, Ravi et al., 2024).

Furthermore, biofilm-associated filaments exhibit enlarged, akinete-like cells (Fig. 1). While the increased expression of akinete markers anAKa and anAKc points to the induction of differentiation pathways, this morphology also resembles division-defective mutants (Camargo et al., 2019, Ramos-León et al., 2015). The concomitant upregulation of division-related genes suggests that altered morphology could result from disrupted division cues or the “enclosure” effect of the biofilm matrix, which may interfere with signals experienced by planktonic cells. Overall, these transcriptional shifts reflect probable alterations cell plasticity and rigidity in the sessile state.

At the metabolic level, biofilm cells undergo pronounced reprogramming toward resource conservation and stress adaptation. Photosynthesis, carbon fixation and anabolic processes (i.e. amino acid and protein synthesis) are broadly downregulated. The reduced photosynthetic capacity might mitigate photooxidative damage, supported by the upregulation of photoprotective genes such as CAB/ELIP/HLIP and NTD-OCP. To compensate for reduced carbon fixation, cells appear to activate alternative pathways to procure monosaccharides and energy, such as the phosphoketolase pathway or gluconeogenesis. Lower energy demand in biofilms is consistent with reduced protein biosynthesis and amino acid metabolism, evidenced by the downregulation of ribosomal components, elongation factors and amino acid uptake/synthesis genes (e.g. systems N-I, N-II and N-III, arg and trp genes). Consistent with this, nitrogen metabolism shifts from assimilation to internal buffering. The combined downregulation of glnA and upregulation of gifA suggest that nitrogen is readily available, while increased expression of cyanophycin metabolism genes (cphB2 and cphA2) indicates storage and mobilization strategies. Repression of arginine biosynthesis and decarboxylation, aligns with findings in Synechocystis sp. PCC 6803, where adc deficiency promotes biofilm formation (Kera et al., 2018). Upregulation of the extracellular nuclease nucA may contribute to matrix remodelling and nucleotide recycling by degrading extracellular DNA (eDNA), consistent with reduced cell growth and downregulated nucleotide metabolism (Lander et al., 2024).

Despite the central role of exopolysaccharides (EPS) in biofilms, most EPS synthesis DEGs were downregulated, particularly those related to capsular polysaccharides (CPS). Given that loose and highly viscous CPS are frequently reported to inhibit biofilm formation, especially during early developmental stages (Bernal-Bayard et al., 2023), their repression may favor adhesion and potentially redirect resources toward the synthesis of biofilm-promoting exopolysaccharides. While most GTs were repressed, all2285 and all2286 from cluster III (Potnis et al., 2021) are upregulated, although the biochemical composition of the EPS encoded by these clusters remains unknown. Future characterization of matrix chemistry will be essential to directly associate these transcriptomic signatures with the physical organization of the biofilm. In other cyanobacteria such as Synechocystis sp., altered GT expression modifies EPS composition, leading to increased aggregation and biofilm formation (Li et al., 2021). Similarly, differential expression of these Anabaena GTs, potentially in combination with EPS modifications such as acetylation or methylation, could contribute to the fine-tuning of EPS properties to enhance adhesion and matrix organization.

Beyond matrix production, the biofilm transcriptome reveals extensive overlap with stress acclimation pathways. Biofilm-associated transcriptional changes strongly resemble responses to desiccation, salinity, UV-B exposure, oxidative stress and gamma irradiation, suggesting shared acclimation pathways, consistent with the known stress-resistance role of biofilms. The substantial magnitude of transcriptional differences highlights the relevance of these stress-related processes in sessile lifestyle. The most pronounced case involves the upregulation of CRISPR and RAMP viral defense clusters 1 and 2, supported by PFAM domain enrichment (Fig. 2 – band i, Fig. 3B). This behaviour may be explained by the physical protection the biofilm structure offers against infections compared to exposed planktonic filaments. Abiotic stress response DEGs are mainly upregulated, including homologs known to influence biofilm formation in other prokaryotes (Olivan-Muro et al., 2025). Differential expression of anaKa, anaKc, and transketolase all4052 mirrors patterns observed in Toxifilum sp. LEGE 06021 biofilms (Romeu et al., 2022). The significant upregulation and stress link of anaKa and anaKc (Kimura et al., 2022, Ehira et al., 2005, Hurali et al., 2024, Srivastava et al., 2023) highlights their potential importance in biofilm physiology. Unlike its paralog tkt (alr3344), all4052 is required for desiccation survival, suggesting a non-redundant role in stress response and biofilm acclimation. Additionally, Alr1718, which shares sequence identity with a KGG-repeat protein contributing to biofilm formation and stress defense in P. aeruginosa (Kaleta et al., 2022), is upregulated both in Anabaena biofilms and in response to dehydration and saline stress (Kimura et al., 2022, Hurali et al., 2024, Higo et al., 2006).

Upregulation of osmolyte synthesis pathways, including trehalose, suggests accumulation of protective metabolites within the matrix, similar to responses induced by temperature downshift or osmotic shock. Although the main osmotic stress regulator OrrA remained unchanged, upstream regulatory proteins were differentially expressed. This, combined with the increased biofilm yield under saline stress (Fig. 5) and the transcriptome overlap with desiccation and saline stress genes (Fig. 4), point to a tight connection between osmotic adaptation and biofilm maintenance.

The contrasting effects of different stressors on cyanobacterial biofilms highlight the role of metabolic state in regulating the transition to a surface-associated lifestyle. While nitrogen limitation and high salinity promote biofilm development, iron starvation inhibits it. Enhanced biofilm formation under nitrogen deficiency may reflect changes in heterocyst frequency or accumulation of signalling metabolites like 2-oxoglutarate, both of which drive major physiological shifts in cyanobacteria. However, these interpretations remain hypothetical, as those parameters were not measured. Given the strong induction of biofilm formation observed (Fig. 4), future studies should address the role of specialized cell organization in sessile communities. Despite relatively limited overlap between biofilm DEGs and nitrogen metabolism genes, nitrogen limitation produced a robust increase in biofilm formation. This apparent discrepancy likely reflects the difference between regulatory signals that establish biofilm development and how a specific environmental stressor can accelerate or enhance this transition. Under nitrogen limitation, carbon flux may be re-directed toward extracellular polysaccharide production, to rebalance cellular C:N ratios and favouring biofilm formation. Likewise, salt stress promotes EPS secretion for osmoprotection. Conversely, iron starvation compromises photosynthetic electron transport imposing energetic constraints that may limit investment in biofilm assembly. Iron limitation inhibits biofilms in some bacteria (Banin et al., 2005, Heindl et al., 2016), though this is not a universal response (Chen et al., 2020, Francesca et al., 2004, Hindré et al., 2008). Iron may be crucial for Anabaena biofilm matrix structure, as a cofactor for biofilm-essential proteins or as a signal preventing biofilm formation under extremely nutrient poor environments, especially given the high iron requirements of cyanobacteria. Furthermore, the presence of sulfate groups and uronic acids in cyanobacterial EPS confers them high affinity to metal cations such as Fe3+ or Ca2+ (Rossi and De Philippis, 2015), which can act as ionic bridges that cross-link EPS polymers, providing structural integrity to the extracellular matrix. Whether iron depletion affects biofilm formation through metabolic limitation, reduced matrix stabilization, or other processes remains to be determined. These findings highlight the need for targeted investigation of iron-dependent mechanisms in cyanobacterial biofilms and underscore how stress-specific impacts on photosynthetic metabolism shape their formation.

Upregulation of DNA repair genes (uvrBC) and stress-induced hemerythrin DNAse, reflects the need to maintain DNA integrity. These genes likely function in both efficient repair and the generation of genetic diversity to facilitate adaptation (Boles and Singh, 2008). Furthermore, detoxification and redox homeostasis pathways were also enhanced in biofilms, including alcohol dehydrogenases alr0895 and alr0897, katB and oxidoreductase alr5182. Orthologs for Alr0895 and Alr0897 were linked to biofilm formation in Toxifilum and Pseudomonas aeruginosa (Olivan-Muro et al., 2025, Romeu et al., 2022, Higo et al., 2006). The highly upregulated all0891-alr0898 cluster (Fig. 3 - band g), oxidoreductase alr5182 and katB are also induced by dehydration, salt, selenium and UV-B exposure (Katoh, 2012, Kimura et al., 2022, Rai et al., 2013, Banerjee et al., 2024). As these adverse conditions induce oxidative stress, and KatB functions as a salt-inducible oxidative stress resistance factor, sessile cells likely experience greater endogenous redox stress than planktonic cells. Oxidative stress in biofilms may promote cell adhesion and secretion of extracellular matrix components, while the matrix itself provides protection and would create localized redox micro-environments within biofilm communities.

The established link between oxidative stress resistance and biofilm formation (Cruz Nizer et al., 2024) is further supported in Anabaena, as a zur deletion mutant, which exhibits reduced oxidative stress tolerance, is impaired in biofilm formation, whereas Zur-overexpression enhances it (Olivan-Muro et al., 2023). Other regulatory factors identified as biofilm DEGs have a known link to biofilm establishment, such as hfq (asl2047), whose higher expression in planktonic fractions aligns with its role in biofilm self-suppression in Synechococcus elongatus (Yegorov et al., 2021). The extracellular matrix regulatory protein A RemA homolog (asr0105) is co-expressed with guanylate kinase gmk (alr0106), which could also have a pro-biofilm function. Given that c-di-GMP is a key signal molecule in biofilm formation, Gmk levels could affect c-di-GMP recycling and signalling pathway function (Valentini and Filloux, 2016). In addition to these known regulators, our data identify several previously uncharacterized genes that may represent novel components of the regulatory network underlying biofilm development.

In summary, Anabaena sp. PCC7120 undergoes extensive morphological and metabolic reprogramming during the transition from planktonic growth to a biofilm lifestyle (Fig. 6). The sessile state is characterized by coordinated transcriptional changes affecting cell envelope structure, matrix production, stress-responses, and reduced anabolic activity, suggesting that biofilm formation represents a shift toward a metabolically constrained yet resilient physiological state. Such reorganization likely enhances the capacity of cyanobacterial communities to withstand fluctuating and adverse environmental conditions.

Summary of the metabolic and transcriptional reprogramming in Anabaena biofilms.

Importantly, our analysis identifies many previously uncharacterized genes whose differential expression in the biofilm state highlights potential regulatory and functional components of cyanobacterial biofilm biology. However, while our transcriptomic analysis identifies robust associations, they do not demonstrate causation. These findings provide a foundation for future functional studies that will be important for validate those findings, and could guide strategies to improve the stability and performance of Anabaena biofilms as nitrogen-fixing platforms in environmental biotechnology. Because biofilms are spatially structured and developmentally dynamic systems, the present transcriptomic analysis primarily reflects the physiology of established biofilms. Future work examining early attachment events and spatial microenvironments within biofilm layers will be essential to fully resolve the mechanisms governing biofilm development in filamentous cyanobacteria.

Methods

Cyanobacterial cultures

Wild-type Anabaena sp. PCC7120 was grown photoautotrophically in BG11 (Rippka et al., 1979) at 28 °C. Maintenance cultures were kept under constant illumination (25 µmol photons·m-2 ·s-1) with orbital shaking at 130 rpm unless otherwise stated. Culture conditions were adjusted based on specific experimental requirements as described below.

Microscopy approaches

Optical microscopy observations were carried out using a Nikon Eclipse 50i Epi-fluorescence microscope coupled to a Nikon DXM 1200F camera. Biofilm samples for SEM (Scanning Electron Microscopy) observations were obtained by inoculating flame-sterilized 12 mm glass coverslips in Petri dishes with late-log Anabaena sp. PCC7120 cultures adjusted to 0.15 OD750nm in BG11. Biofilms were allowed to develop for 14 days at 28 °C and 5 µmol photons·m-2·s-1 illumination. Samples were washed with phosphate buffer (66 mM Na2HPO4, 66 mM KH2PO4, pH 6.8) and fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde in the same buffer for 2 hours at room temperature. Further steps were performed by the SAI (Servicio de Apoyo a la Investigación) Service of Electronic Microscopy of Biological Systems of the University of Zaragoza, including three 5 min washes in phosphate buffer, 2% OsO4 fixation, three additional 5 min washes in distilled water, critical point drying and Au/Pd coverage. Samples were visualized with a JEOL JSM 6360 - LV microscope.

RNA extraction and sequencing

Total RNA was obtained from three independent concurrent biofilm/planktonic cultures. Late-log cultures were washed, adjusted to 0.25 OD750nm in 350 mL of fresh BG11 and grown under 15 µmol photons·m-2·s-1 for 72 hours in bespoke bubbling flasks produced by the SAI (Servicio de Apoyo a la Investigación) glass blowing unit of the University of Zaragoza (Fig. S7). Planktonic fractions were collected, discarding mixed fractions containing detached biofilm cells, and sessile fractions were collected by addition of fresh BG11 and scraping with a serological pipette. Pooled cultures were centrifuged at 3000 rpm, and pellets distributed into 80-100 mg wet mass samples for total RNA extraction as previously described (Olivan-Muro et al., 2023). RNAseq was performed by STAB VIDA Lda (Lisbon, Portugal), using a Ribosomal Depletion Library Preparation Kit and the lllumina Novaseq platform for DNA 150 bp paired-end sequencing.

cDNA obtention and Real Time RT-PCR

Total RNA (1.5 µg) was reverse-transcribed using SuperScript retrotranscriptase (Invitrogen) following the manufacturer's protocol. Real Time RT-PCR reaction setup included 12.5 μL of SYBR Green PCR Master Mix, 0.4 μL of 25 μM primer mixture (Table S1) and 10 ng of cDNA template in a final volume of 30 μL in nuclease-free water (Ambion). Amplification was performed in a QuantStudio 5 system (Applied Biosystems) at 60 °C for 40 cycles. Transcript levels were normalised to the housekeeping gene rnpB (Vioque, 1992). Relative quantification and transcription fold change were obtained through the ΔΔCt method (Livak and Schmittgen, 2001).

Bioinformatic approaches

RNAseq raw sequence data were analysed by STAB VIDA Lda using CLC Genomics Workbench 12.0.3. Sequences were quality-trimmed based on a threshold of ambiguity (two nucleotides), quality (0.01 probability error) and length (30 nucleotides minimum). High-quality reads were mapped to the Nostoc sp. PCC 7120 = FACHB-418 (GCA_000009705.1) reference genome with a length and similarity fraction value of 0.8. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were filtered through a false discovery rate (FDR) corrected p-value ≤0.05 and |fold change|≥2 threshold. Functional enrichment analysis was performed using the DAVID Bioinformatics Functional Annotation tool (davidbioinformatics.nih.gov) for Direct Biological Process and PFAM domain term enrichment analysis, with the Anabaena sp. PCC7120 genome as background (EASE 0.1 and 2 gene count minimum threshold). K-Means clustering of data was performed using the sklearn Python library, with cluster number determined by silhouette coefficient evaluations. Cell area was quantified with FIJI (Schindelin et al., 2012) by converting microscopy images to binary masks and applying the watershed and analyze particles tools after manual removal of artifact particles.

Biofilm formation assays under altered conditions

Biofilm formation was evaluated using uncoated Ibidi μ-Slide 8-well high-chambered coverslips. Cultures were adjusted to 0.15 OD750nm and grown statically for 10 days under 7.5 µmol photons·m-2·s-1 light intensity and 28 °C, with a starting volume of 300 µL per well. This timeframe and working volume provide stabilised yields while minimising artifacts such as media evaporation, for increased reproducibility. To assay the effect of nitrogen deficiency, cultures were washed three times and resuspended in BG110, lacking a combined nitrogen source. Saline stress was tested by resuspending washed pellets in BG11 containing 50 mM NaCl. To ensure iron depletion, late-log cultures were washed three times in BG11-Fe (no ferric ammonium citrate) and grown for three days in sterile acid-washed flasks. Following static growth, unbound cells were removed and biofilms were stained with crystal violet and washed twice to remove excess dye. Yields were quantified using integrated density in FIJI (Schindelin et al., 2012) as previously described (Olivan-Muro et al., 2025). Total (sessile and planktonic) biomass was evaluated in Sarstedt 24-well (1.5 mL per well) under the same conditions, using chlorophyll a as proxy for growth. After 10 days, chlorophyll a was extracted using methanol, and its concentration was quantified spectrophotometrically based absorbance at 665 nm.
