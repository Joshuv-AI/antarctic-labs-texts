Maximizing the Egalitarian Welfare in Friends and Enemies Games Conference: ;
Edith Elkind, Michele Flammini, Giovanna Varricchio*

Abstract
. We consider the complexity of maximizing egalitarian welfare in Friends and Enemies Games —a subclass of hedonic games in which every agent partitions other agents into friends and enemies . We investigate two classic scenarios proposed in the literature, namely, Friends Appreciation ( \FA \FA ) and Enemies Aversion ( \EA \EA ): in the former, each agent primarily cares about the number of friends in her coalition, breaking ties based on the number of enemies, while in the latter, the opposite is true. For \EA \EA , we show that our objective is hard to approximate within O ⁡ ( n 1 − ϵ ) O(n^{1-\epsilon}) , for any fixed ϵ > 0 \epsilon>0 , and provide a polynomial-time ( n − 1 ) (n-1) -approximation. For \FA \FA , we obtain an NP-hardness result and a polynomial-time approximation algorithm. Our algorithm achieves a ratio of 2 − Θ ⁡ ( 1 n ) 2-\Theta(\frac{1}{n}) when every agent has at least two friends; however, if some agent has at most one friend, its approximation ratio deteriorates to n / 2 n/2 . We recover the 2 − Θ ⁡ ( 1 n ) 2-\Theta(\frac{1}{n}) approximation ratio for two important variants: when randomization is allowed and when the friendship relationship is symmetric. Additionally, for both \EA \EA and \FA \FA we identify special cases where the optimal egalitarian partition can be computed in polynomial time.



1.
Introduction

Hedonic Games (HGs)
(
16
)
provide a game-theoretic framework for analyzing coalition formation among selfish agents and have been extensively studied in the literature (see, e.g.,
(
2
;
3
;
6
;
8
;
18
;
17
;
21
)
).
In these games, the objective is to partition a set of agents into disjoint coalitions, with each agent’s satisfaction determined solely by the members of her coalition.

Different preference models give rise to different
subclasses of HGs. For instance, in
additively separable
HGs (ASHGs)
(
8
)
, agents assign values to each other and evaluate coalitions by summing the values they assign to every other member. A particularly natural scenario is when agents classify others as either
friends
or
enemies
. Two canonical preference models have been studied in this setting
(
14
)
: under
Friends Appreciation
(
\FA
) preferences, agents prioritize coalitions with more friends and, in case of ties, prefer those with fewer enemies; conversely, under
Enemies Aversion
(
\EA
) preferences, agents prefer coalitions with the lowest number of enemies and, if the number of enemies is the same, favor those with more friends.

While most of the existing literature on HGs has focused on stability, i.e., the resilience of an outcome to individual or group deviations, another important concept in coalition formation is the
social welfare
, which captures the quality of a partition. Specifically, the
utilitarian
welfare, which is given by the sum of the agents’ utilities, measures the overall happiness of the agents in a given partition. However, maximizing utilitarian welfare may come at a cost of treating some agents badly if doing so increases the total sum. In contrast, the
egalitarian
welfare is determined by the minimum utility among all agents. Thus, a partition that maximizes the egalitarian welfare is fairer, in the sense that the lowest utility is as high as possible.
Despite its appeal, egalitarian welfare has received limited attention in the context of hedonic games.

1.1.
Our Contribution

We study the computation of the maximum egalitarian welfare in Friends and Enemies Games.

For
\EA
games, we prove that the problem is hard to approximate within
O(n^{1-\epsilon})
for any fixed
\epsilon>0
, where
n
is the number of agents, and complement this hardness result with a polynomial-time algorithm achieving an
(n-1)
-approximation. For
\FA
games, we show that maximizing egalitarian welfare is computationally hard. We then provide a polynomial-time approximation algorithm whose performance depends on the structure of the instance: it achieves an approximation of
2-\Theta(\frac{1}{n})
, when every agent has at least two friends, and an
n/2
-approximation in the worst case. We further improve this result in two important settings, namely, (1) when randomization is allowed, and (2) when the preferences are symmetric, that is, the friendship relations are mutual. For both cases, we design algorithms attaining an approximation ratio of
2-\Theta(\tfrac{1}{n})
. Finally, we complement these results by identifying special cases of both
\EA
games and
\FA
games where the optimal egalitarian partition can be computed exactly in polynomial time.

1.2.
Related Work

There is a substantial body of literature on hedonic games; we refer the interested reader to
(
5
)
for a comprehensive overview. In what follows, we focus on work that is directly related to ours.

Aziz et al.
(
3
)
consider
ASHGs, and show that computing an optimal egalitarian partition is strongly
NP
-hard; moreover, verifying whether a given partition maximizes the egalitarian welfare is co
NP
-complete. The complexity of this problem is further investigated by Hanaka et al.
(
24
)
, who identify several tractable cases. Peters
(
30
)
obtained a related result, showing that an optimal egalitarian partition is polynomial-time computable in graphical HGs as long as the underlying graph has bounded treewidth. In addition, egalitarian welfare has been studied in online ASHGs
(
11
)
and in ASHGs where at most
k
coalitions can be formed
(
32
)
.
For
simple fractional hedonic games
(sFHGs) with symmetric preferences, Aziz et al.
(
4
)
show that maximizing the egalitarian welfare is
NP
-hard, and provide a polynomial-time
3
-approximation algorithm. Aziz et al. also state that in instances where the underlying graph is a tree, an optimal egalitarian partition can be computed in polynomial time via dynamic programming. This idea was later formalized by Hanaka et al.
(
23
)
, who present an efficient algorithm for bounded treewidth graphs. The egalitarian welfare has also been employed as a quality measure for evaluating stable outcomes in modified fractional HGs
(
28
)
.

A closely related problem is the existence of a wonderful (a.k.a. perfect) partition in HGs where agents have
dichotomous preferences
, that is, each agent values each coalition as either
0
or
1
. A wonderful partition is one where each agent assigns value
1
to her coalition. Clearly, a wonderful partition exists
if an only if there exists a partition whose egalitarian welfare is
1
.
Establishing whether such a partition exists is, in general,
NP
-hard
(
29
;
2
)
, though some tractable cases have been identified
(
29
;
12
)
. Notably, the positive result of Constantinescu et al.
(
12
)
implies that the problem of maximizing the egalitarian welfare in anonymous HGs with single-peaked preferences is polynomial-time solvable.

Friends and Enemies Games constitute a widely studied subclass of hedonic games and have been further extended to capture more complex social contexts
(
14
;
31
;
26
;
7
;
13
;
10
)
. In this line of research, most of the focus has been on finding desirable outcomes according to various stability notions. Beyond stability, aspects related to strategyproofness have also been investigated
(
15
;
20
;
19
;
27
)
. From a welfare perspective, however, only the utilitarian welfare has been studied. In particular, for
\EA
preferences, maximum utilitarian welfare is not approximable within a factor of
O(n^{1-\epsilon})
, where
n
is the number of agents; however, there is a polynomial-time
O(n)
-approximation algorithm
(
20
)
. This bound can be improved, with high probability, in scenarios where friend and enemy relations are generated according to specific probabilistic models
(
9
)
. Even under
\FA
preferences, the problem remains
NP
-hard, though a polynomial-time
(4+o(1))
-approximation has been obtained
(
19
)
.

To the best of our knowledge, egalitarian welfare has not previously been considered in Friends and Enemies Games.

2.
Model and Preliminaries

Given a positive integer
k
, we denote by
[k]
the set
\left\{1,\dots,k\right\}
.

In hedonic games, a set of
n
agents
\mathcal{N}=\left\{1,\dots,n\right\}
needs to be partitioned into disjoint subsets. We refer to subsets of
\mathcal{N}
as
coalitions
; the set of all coalitions containing an agent
i\in\mathcal{N}
is denoted by
\mathcal{N}^{i}
.
An outcome of a hedonic game with the set of agents
\mathcal{N}
is a
coalition structure
, i.e., a partition
\pi=\left\{C_{1},\dots,C_{m}\right\}
of
\mathcal{N}
such that
\cup_{k=1}^{m}C_{k}=\mathcal{N}
and
C_{k}\cap C_{\ell}=\emptyset
for all
k,\ell\in[m]
with
k\neq\ell
. We denote by
\Pi
the set of all partitions of
\mathcal{N}
.
Given an outcome
\pi\in\Pi
, we denote by
\pi(i)
the coalition in
\pi
that contains agent
i
. The coalition
\mathcal{N}
is called the
grand coalition
;
abusing terminology,
we also refer to the partition

\left\{\mathcal{N}\right\}
as the grand coalition.
A coalition of size
1
is called a
singleton coalition
.

In hedonic games, each agent
i\in\mathcal{N}
is endowed with a preference relation
\succeq_{i}
over
\mathcal{N}^{i}
. Given two coalitions
X,Y\in\mathcal{N}^{i}
, we say that
i

weakly prefers

X
to
Y
if
X\succeq_{i}Y
. These preferences are lifted to partitions: an agent
i\in\mathcal{N}
weakly prefers
\pi
to
\pi^{\prime}
if
\pi(i)\succeq_{i}\pi^{\prime}(i)
.

2.1.
Friends and Enemies Games

In hedonic games with friends and enemies, every agent
i\in\mathcal{N}
partitions the other agents into a set of friends
F_{i}
and a set of enemies
E_{i}
, with
F_{i}\cup E_{i}=\mathcal{N}\setminus\{i\}
and
F_{i}\cap E_{i}=\emptyset
. We write
f_{i}:=|F_{i}|
,
e_{i}=|E_{i}|=n-f_{i}-1
.

The agents’ preferences
are based on
Friends Appreciation
(
\FA
) when
for each
i\in\mathcal{N}
it holds that
X\succeq_{i}Y
iff

\displaystyle\left\lvert X\cap F_{i}\right\rvert>\left\lvert Y\cap F_{i}\right\rvert\;\;\mbox{or}

\displaystyle\left\lvert X\cap F_{i}\right\rvert=\left\lvert Y\cap F_{i}\right\rvert\mbox{ and }\left\lvert X\cap E_{i}\right\rvert\leq\left\lvert Y\cap E_{i}\right\rvert\kern 5.0pt,

and they are based on
Enemies Aversion
(
\EA
) when
X\succeq_{i}Y
iff

\displaystyle\left\lvert X\cap E_{i}\right\rvert<\left\lvert Y\cap E_{i}\right\rvert\;\;\mbox{or}

\displaystyle\left\lvert X\cap E_{i}\right\rvert=\left\lvert Y\cap E_{i}\right\rvert\mbox{ and }\left\lvert X\cap F_{i}\right\rvert\geq\left\lvert Y\cap F_{i}\right\rvert\kern 5.0pt.

In other words, under
\FA
, a coalition is preferred over another one if it contains a higher number of friends; if the number of friends is the same, the coalition with fewer enemies is preferred. On the other hand, under
\EA
, a coalition is preferred if it contains fewer enemies; if the number of enemies is the same, the coalition with more friends is preferred.

Let
\mathcal{N}=\left\{1,2,3\right\}

A well-studied class of hedonic games is
additively separable hedonic games (ASHGs)
, where agents assign values
v_{i}(j)
to each other, the utility
u_{i}(C)
an agent
i
derives from a coalition
C\in\mathcal{N}^{i}
is the sum of her values for the coalition members other than herself, i.e.,
u_{i}(C)=\sum_{j\in C\setminus\{i\}}v_{i}(j)
, and
C\succeq_{i}D
iff
u_{i}(C)\geq u_{i}(D)
. Given a partition
\pi
, for each
i\in\mathcal{N}
we write
u_{i}(\pi):=u_{i}(\pi(i))
.
Both
\FA
games and
\EA

Indeed, in the
\FA
case, for every agent
i\in\mathcal{N}
, we can set

\displaystyle v_{i}(j)=\begin{cases}1,&\kern 5.0pt\mbox{ if }j\in F_{i},\\
-\frac{1}{n},&\kern 5.0pt\mbox{ if }j\in E_{i}\,.\end{cases}

so that the positive effect of one friend is greater than the overall negative effect of all enemies.
Similarly, in the
\EA
case, we can set, for every agent
i\in\mathcal{N}
,

\displaystyle v_{i}(j)=\begin{cases}1,&\kern 5.0pt\mbox{ if }j\in F_{i},\\
-n,&\kern 5.0pt\mbox{ if }j\in E_{i}\,.\end{cases}

Example 0.

Consider the instance described in
Example

\EA

An

•

The

, representing all directed friendship relationships, and

•

the

G^{f}\equiv G^{\mathit{sf}}

Throughout the paper, we may define a game instance either as

G^{f}
or

, depending on the context. For simplicity, we will often omit
\mathcal{I}
from the notation, implicitly assuming that
\mathcal{N}
and

are given.

2.2.
Egalitarian Welfare, Approximation, and Other Guarantees

For an instance
\mathcal{I}
of an ASHG, we define the
egalitarian welfare
of an outcome
\pi
as

\mathsf{ESW}^{\mathcal{I}}(\pi)=\min_{i\in\mathcal{N}}u_{i}(\pi)\,.

We are interested in finding outcomes that (approximately) maximize
\mathsf{ESW}
. Accordingly, we define
maxESW
as the problem of maximizing the egalitarian welfare. Given an instance
\mathcal{I}
, we denote by
\textsf{opt}(\mathcal{I})
the value of an optimal solution of
maxESW
for the instance
\mathcal{I}
, and by
\textsf{OPT}(\mathcal{I})
any partition of the agents such that
\mathsf{ESW}^{\mathcal{I}}(\textsf{OPT}(\mathcal{I}))=\textsf{opt}(\mathcal{I})
. When
\mathcal{I}
is clear from the context, we simply write
\mathsf{ESW}
,
opt
, and
OPT
.

Remark 1.

In any instance of
\EA
or
\FA
, if
F_{i}=\emptyset
for some
i\in\mathcal{N}
, then the maximum attainable egalitarian welfare is necessarily
0
, since
i
cannot achieve a positive utility in any coalition. In this case, partitioning the agents into singletons is optimal. Henceforth, we will always assume that
F_{i}\neq\emptyset
for every
i\in\mathcal{N}
.

Approximation Ratio.

In this work, we aim to find partitions that maximize the egalitarian welfare. Unfortunately, it turns out that
maxESW
is
NP
-hard, both for
\EA
and for
\FA
. For this reason, we will turn our attention to approximate solutions. Given an algorithm
\mathcal{A}
, we define its
approximation ratio
as follows:

\displaystyle r_{n}(\mathcal{A})=\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathcal{I}=(\mathcal{N},F):\\
|\mathcal{N}|=n\end{subarray}}\frac{\textsf{opt}(\mathcal{I})}{\mathsf{ESW}^{\mathcal{I}}(\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{I}))}\quad\ \ \text{and}\quad\ \ r({\mathcal{A}})=\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}r_{n}({\mathcal{A}})\,,

where
\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{I})
is the partition returned by
\mathcal{A}
on input
\mathcal{I}
.

Guarantees for the Agents.

In the pursuit of finding efficient algorithms that yield good approximation for
maxESW
, we will simultaneously provide some guarantees to (almost) all agents.
In particular, given an agent
i
, we say that a coalition
C
satisfies for
i
:

•

allF
, if all friends of
i
are included in
C
;

•

onlyF
, if
C
consists solely of friends of
i
, though not necessarily all of them;

•

oneF
, if
C
contains at least one friend of
i
.

Let
\Gamma
be a guarantee that a coalition may fulfill for an agent.
A partition
\pi

guarantees
\Gamma
for
A\subseteq\mathcal{N}
if each coalition
C\in\pi
guarantees
\mathcal{G}
to all agents in
A\cap C
.
Moreover,
\pi

is a
minimal-by-refinement partition guaranteeing
\Gamma
for
A\subseteq\mathcal{N}

if
it is not possible to split
C
into non-empty coalitions
C_{1},\dots,C_{k}
so that the partition
\pi^{\prime}=\pi\setminus\left\{C\right\}\cup\left\{{C_{1}},\dots,{C_{k}}\right\}

guarantees
\Gamma
for
A\subseteq\mathcal{N}
. If
A=\mathcal{N},
we say that
\pi
is a
minimal-by-refinement partition guaranteeing
\Gamma
.

We now present an example to illustrate these notions.

Example 0.

The grand coalition
\pi=\left\{\mathcal{N}\right\}
satisfies
allF
. However, it may fail
onlyF
, as there may exist an agent
i
with
F_{i}\neq\mathcal{N}\setminus\left\{i\right\}
. Under our hypothesis that each agent has at least one friend,
\pi
also satisfies
oneF
.
In fact, under this hypothesis
allF
implies
oneF
.
However,
\{\mathcal{N}\}
need not be the minimal-by-refinement partition that satisfies
allF
. Indeed, if
\mathcal{N}=\left\{1,2,3,4\right\}
and
F_{1}=\left\{2\right\},F_{2}=\left\{1\right\},F_{3}=\left\{4\right\},
and
F_{4}=\left\{3\right\}
, then the unique minimal-by-refinement partition satisfying
allF
is
\left\{\left\{1,2\right\},\left\{3,4\right\}\right\}
.

3.
Enemies Aversion

When constructing (approximately) optimal partitions under
\EA
preferences, we can restrict our attention to mutual friendship relationships only.
Indeed, if some agent is in a coalition with an enemy, her utility—and hence
\mathsf{ESW}
—is negative, whereas for the coalition structure
\pi^{\text{sing}}
that consists of
n
singleton coalitions we have
\mathsf{ESW}(\pi^{\text{sing}})=0
.
Consequently, for
\mathsf{ESW}
to be non-negative, an outcome
\pi
must satisfy
onlyF
for all agents, i.e., each coalition
C
in
\pi
must induce a clique in the strong friendship graph
G^{\mathit{sf}}
. Moreover, if
\pi
contains a singleton coalition, we have
\mathsf{ESW}(\pi)\leq 0
, even if
\pi
satisfies
onlyF
for all agents. Thus, for
\mathsf{ESW}(\pi)
to be positive,
\pi
must satisfy
onlyF
and
oneF
for all agents, i.e., induce a partition of
G^{\mathit{sf}}
into cliques of size at least
2
.

Combining these insights, we derive the following lemma.

Lemma 0.

A partition
\pi\in\Pi
satisfies
\mathsf{ESW}(\pi)\geq 0
if and only if
\pi
induces a clique partition of
G^{\mathit{sf}}
. Furthermore,
\mathsf{ESW}(\pi)>0
if and only this clique partition contains no cliques of size
1
.

Thus, in an optimal coalition structure, the set of agents must be partitioned into cliques. Moreover, to maximize the
\mathsf{ESW}
, the smallest clique in the partition must be as large as possible.

However, we will now see that this structural result does not mean that maximizing
\mathsf{ESW}
is easy.

3.1.
Hardness of Approximation

Theorem 2.

Under
\EA
, for every
\epsilon>0
, there is no polynomial-time algorithm that approximates
maxESW
within a factor
O(n^{1-\epsilon})
, unless
\textsf{P}=\textsf{NP}
. The hardness result holds even for symmetric instances.

To prove the theorem, we make use of an inapproximability result for the closely related problem:
Partition into Cliques
.

Partition into Cliques (PC)
:

Input:
An undirected graph
G=(V,E)
and a positive integer
K\leq\left\lvert V\right\rvert
.

Question:
Can the vertices of
G
be partitioned into
k\leq K
disjoint sets
V_{1},\dots,V_{k}
so that, for each
h\in[k]
, the subgraph
G[V_{h}]
induced by
V_{h}
is a clique?

Let
minPC
denote the corresponding minimization problem, i.e., finding the minimum number of cliques in a clique partition of
G
. Note that every graph admits a clique partition, since a single vertex forms a clique of size
1
. The problem
minPC
cannot be approximated within a factor
O(\left\lvert V\right\rvert^{1-\epsilon})
, for every
\epsilon>0
, unless
\textsf{P}=\textsf{NP}

(
33
)
.

Proof of
Theorem

2
.

Assume there exists an
n^{1-\epsilon}
-approximation algorithm
\mathcal{A}
for
maxESW
under
\EA
,
for some
\epsilon>0
. We will show that this assumption contradicts the known inapproximability result for the
minPC
problem.

Let
G=(V,E)
be an arbitrary instance of
minPC
. We now construct a corresponding
\EA
instance
\mathcal{I}
of the
maxESW
problem. By
Lemma

1
, it suffices to describe the friendship graph
G^{\mathit{sf}}=(\mathcal{N},F^{s})
for
\mathcal{I}
, since only coalition structures inducing clique partitions of
G^{\mathit{sf}}
offer non-negative
\mathsf{ESW}
.
To construct our instance
\mathcal{I}
, we set
\mathcal{N}=V\cup V^{\prime}
, where
|V^{\prime}|=|V|
and
V^{\prime}
is disjoint from
V
. For each

\left\{u,v\right\}\in E
, we create an edge
\left\{u,v\right\}\in F^{s}
. Additionally, we create an edge between every pair of vertices in
V^{\prime}
and connect every vertex of
V
to every vertex of
V^{\prime}
.
Note that
\mathcal{I}
satisfies
n=\left\lvert\mathcal{N}\right\rvert=2\cdot\left\lvert V\right\rvert
.

Let us now relate
\textsf{opt}(\mathcal{I})
, i.e., the optimal
maxESW
value for
\mathcal{I}
, to the optimal value of
minPC
, denoted
\textsf{opt}^{\tiny\textsc{PC}}
, i.e., the minimum number of cliques needed to cover the vertices of
G=(V,E)
. Let
V_{1},\dots,V_{k^{*}}
be a partition of
G
into
k^{*}=\textsf{opt}^{\tiny\textsc{PC}}
disjoint cliques.
Partition the vertices in
V^{\prime}
into
coalitions
C_{1}^{\prime},\dots,C^{\prime}_{k^{*}}
as evenly as possible; this ensures that

|C^{\prime}_{h}|\geq\left\lfloor\frac{\left\lvert V\right\rvert}{k^{*}}\right\rfloor
for each
h\in[k^{*}]
. Now set

C_{h}=V_{h}\cup C^{\prime}_{h}
for all
h\in[k^{*}]
,
and note that
\pi=\left\{C_{1},\dots,C_{k^{*}}\right\}
is a clique partition of
\mathcal{N}
. Since
V_{h}\neq\emptyset
for all
h\in[k^{*}]
, we obtain
\left\lvert C_{h}\right\rvert\geq\left\lfloor\frac{\left\lvert V\right\rvert}{k^{*}}\right\rfloor+1\geq\frac{\left\lvert V\right\rvert}{k^{*}}
for each
h\in[k^{*}]
.
As the utility of each agent in a clique of size
t
is
t-1
, we obtain

(1)

\displaystyle\textsf{opt}(\mathcal{I})\geq\frac{\left\lvert V\right\rvert}{k^{*}}-1=\frac{\left\lvert V\right\rvert}{\textsf{opt}^{\tiny\textsc{PC}}}-1.

Consider now the partition
\pi
computed by our hypothetical approximation algorithm
\mathcal{A}
for
maxESW
, and let
s=\mathsf{ESW}(\pi)
. By
Lemma

1
,
\pi
must be a clique partition; moreover,
\mathsf{ESW}(\pi)=s
implies that each coalition in
\pi
contains at least
s+1
vertices.
Hence,
\pi
consists of at most
\frac{2\cdot\left\lvert V\right\rvert}{s+1}
disjoint cliques of
G^{\mathit{sf}}
. We can now convert
\pi
into a partition of
G
into
k
cliques with
k\leq\frac{2\cdot\left\lvert V\right\rvert}{s+1}
, as the restriction of each coalition
C\in\pi
to the nodes of
V
is either empty or a clique of
G
. Since
\mathcal{A}
computes an
n^{1-\epsilon}
approximation of
maxESW
, we have

(2)

\displaystyle\frac{\textsf{opt}(\mathcal{I})}{s}\leq n^{1-\epsilon}.

On the other hand,

k\leq\frac{2\cdot\left\lvert V\right\rvert}{s+1}
implies
s\leq\frac{2\cdot\left\lvert V\right\rvert}{k}-1
; combining this with (
1
), we get

(3)

\displaystyle\frac{\textsf{opt}(\mathcal{I})}{s}\geq\frac{\frac{\left\lvert V\right\rvert}{\textsf{opt}^{\tiny\textsc{PC}}}-1}{\frac{2\cdot\left\lvert V\right\rvert}{k}-1}\geq\frac{\frac{\left\lvert V\right\rvert}{\textsf{opt}^{\tiny\textsc{PC}}}}{\frac{2\cdot\left\lvert V\right\rvert}{k}-1}-1>\frac{k}{2\cdot\textsf{opt}^{\tiny\textsc{PC}}}-1\,,

where the last two inequalities hold since
1\leq\frac{2\cdot\left\lvert V\right\rvert}{k}-1<\frac{2\cdot\left\lvert V\right\rvert}{k}
.

As
n=2\cdot\left\lvert V\right\rvert
,
by putting together (
3
) and (
2
),
we obtain
\frac{k}{\textsf{opt}^{\tiny\textsc{PC}}}<2\cdot(2\cdot\left\lvert V\right\rvert)^{1-\epsilon}+2
. For sufficiently large
V
, this quantity is smaller than
\left\lvert V\right\rvert^{1-\epsilon/2}
, a contradiction to the inapproximability of
minPC
.
∎

3.2.
Linear Approximation in Polynomial Time

By
Lemma

1
, to obtain a bounded approximation for a given
\EA
instance, we need to determine whether the associated strong friendship graph
G^{\mathit{sf}}
admits a
non-trivial clique partition
, i.e., a clique partition in which each agent belongs to a clique of size at least
2
.

Observation 1.

If
G^{\mathit{sf}}
does not admit a non-trivial clique partition, then a partition into singletons achieves optimal
\mathsf{ESW}
. Otherwise, any non-trivial clique partition guarantees an
\mathsf{ESW}
of at least
1
.

Note that a non-trivial clique partition exists if and only if it is possible to partition the graph into triangles and a matching. This observation enables us to design a
polynomial-time algorithm that computes an
(n-1)
-approximation to
maxESW
.

Theorem 3.

Under
\EA
, there exists a polynomial-time algorithm computing an
(n-1)
-approximation to
maxESW
. Furthermore, if
\mathsf{ESW}>0
, the computed partition satisfies
oneF
for all the agents.

Proof.

The problem of finding a perfect covering of the vertices of a graph by
\mathbb{K}_{2}
and
\mathbb{K}_{3}
, i.e. cliques of size
2
or
3
, admits a polynomial-time algorithm
(
25
)
.
We can execute this algorithm on
G^{\mathit{sf}}
.
If the algorithm reports that there is no perfect covering of
G^{\mathit{sf}}
with
\mathbb{K}_{2}
and
\mathbb{K}_{3}
, every partition of
G^{\mathit{sf}}
into cliques contains a singleton, and hence the maximum attainable egalitarian welfare is
0
(so, in particular, a partition into singletons maximizes
\mathsf{ESW}
).
On the other hand, if the algorithm returns a perfect covering, it corresponds to a partition of the agents into coalitions of size
2
and
3
, which satisfies
oneF
. Any such partition guarantees utility of at least
1
to every agent. Since the maximum utility an agent can derive is at most
n-1
, the
\mathsf{ESW}
of the partition constructed by our algorithm is within a factor of
n-1
from optimal.
∎

We also note that
maxESW
becomes easy if
G^{\mathit{sf}}
is
triangle-free
, that is, no cycle in
G^{\mathit{sf}}
has length
3
.

Theorem 4.

Under
\EA
, if
G^{\mathit{sf}}
is triangle-free, then
maxESW
is in
P
.

Proof.

In a triangle-free graph, no clique has size larger than
2
. Thus, we proceed by computing a maximum matching in
G^{\mathit{sf}}
. If
G^{\mathit{sf}}
admits a perfect matching, the maximum
\mathsf{ESW}
is
1
, and an optimal partition is attained by creating a coalition for each pair of matched agents. Otherwise, the maximum
\mathsf{ESW}
is
0
, and splitting the agents into singletons is an optimal partition.
∎

4.
Friends Appreciation

For
\FA
preferences, unless specified otherwise, we will work with the
G^{f}
graph.
Let
f_{\min}=\min_{i\in\mathcal{N}}f_{i}
denote the minimum number of friends of any agent,
and set

\mathcal{N}_{\min}=\{i\in\mathcal{N}\mid f_{i}=f_{\min}\}
.
We start by presenting simple bounds on
opt
for
maxESW
;
for the upper bound, note that agents in
\mathcal{N}_{\min}
have at most
f_{\min}
friends, and for the lower bound, consider the grand coalition.

Observation 2.

Under
\FA
,

f_{\min}-1<\textsf{opt}\leq f_{\min}
.

Consequently, in every optimal partition, every agent
i\in\mathcal{N}_{\min}
must end up in a coalition that contains all of her friends.

Corollary 0.

Under
\FA
, any
maxESW
partition satisfies
allF
for all the agents in
\mathcal{N}_{\min}
.

4.1.
NP-Hardness

It turns out that for
\FA
preferences, too, computing a
maxESW
partition is
NP
-hard; the hardness result persists even if agents’ preferences are symmetric.

Theorem 2.

Under
\FA
,
maxESW
is
NP
-hard. The hardness result holds even for symmetric instances.

Our proof proceeds by a reduction from the
Partition into Triangles
problem, which can be formulated as follows:

Partition into Triangles
:

Input:
An undirected graph
G=(V,E)
.

Question:

Is it possible to partition
V

as
S_{1},\dots,S_{k}
so that for each
S_{j}
,
j\in[k]
, its induced subgraph is a triangle?

Partition into Triangles
is known to be NP-hard
(
22
)
.
Note that we can assume that
|V|=3k
for some
k\in\mathbb{N}
, as otherwise we trivially have a “no”-instance. Thus, in what follows, we will make this assumption.

Proof.

Consider an instance
G=(V,E)
of
Partition into Triangles
with
|V|=3k
. We will construct a (symmetric) instance of
\FA
by specifying its associated graph
G^{\mathit{sf}}
.

For each vertex
v\in V
we construct four vertices of
G^{\mathit{sf}}
:
a_{v}
,
b_{v}
,
c_{v}
and
z_{v}
. Thus,
\mathcal{N}=\{a_{v},b_{v},c_{v},z_{v}\mid v\in V\}
and
n=\left\lvert\mathcal{N}\right\rvert=4\left\lvert V\right\rvert
.
For each
v\in V
, the graph
G^{\mathit{sf}}
contains edges
\{a_{v},z_{v}\}
,
\{b_{v},z_{v}\}
, and
\{c_{v},z_{v}\}
. Also, for each edge
\{u,v\}\in E
of
G
, the graph
G^{\mathit{sf}}
contains edges
\{a_{u},a_{v}\}
,
\{b_{u},b_{v}\}
, and
\{c_{u},c_{v}\}
. This completes the description of
G^{\mathit{sf}}
.

We claim that the instance of
\FA
that corresponds to
G^{\mathit{sf}}
admits a partition
\pi
with
\mathsf{ESW}(\pi)\geq 3-\frac{8}{n}
if and only if
G
is a yes-instance of
Partition into Triangles
.

Indeed, let
S_{1},\dots,S_{k}
be a partition of
G
into triangles. For each
S_{j}
,
j\in[k]
, we create a coalition
C_{j}=\{a_{v},b_{v},c_{v},z_{v}\mid v\in S_{j}\}
. This coalition contains
12
agents, and each agent in it has
3
friends and 8 enemies, so the utility of each agent is
3-\frac{8}{n}
.

Conversely, suppose our
\FA
game admits a partition
\pi
with
\mathsf{ESW}(\pi)\geq 3-\frac{8}{n}
. Since
n=4|V|\geq 12
, we have
3-\frac{8}{n}>2
, so in this partition, each agent must be in a coalition with at least
3
friends. Thus, for each
v\in V
it holds that
z_{v}
is in the same coalition as
a_{v}
,
b_{v}
and
c_{v}
(as
z_{v}
has no other friends). It follows that each coalition in
\pi
is of the form
C=\{a_{v},b_{v},c_{v},z_{v}\mid v\in S\}
, where
S
is a subset of
V
. Moreover,
z_{v}
’s coalition must contain at most
8
enemies, implying
\left\lvert S\right\rvert\leq 3
. Now, if
\left\lvert S\right\rvert<3
or if its induced graph is not a triangle, there is an agent
a_{v}
in
C
that has fewer than
3
friends in
C
, and hence her utility is lower than
3-\frac{8}{n}
, a contradiction. Thus, each coalition in
\pi
corresponds to a triangle in
G
, and these triangles are pairwise disjoint, i.e.,
G
admits a partition into triangles.
∎

In the remainder of this section, we present our approximability results.
We describe a simple algorithm whose approximation ratio is linear in the number of agents. While the linear approximation bound is tight for our algorithm, this is caused by instances with
f_{\min}=1
: we show that as soon as each agent has at least two friends, the approximation ratio of our algorithms becomes
2-\Theta\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)
. We also obtain
\left(2-\Theta\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)\right)
-approximation results for two other settings: for a randomized algorithm (in expectation) and for symmetric instances.

4.2.
Deterministic Bounded Approximation

Corollary

1
establishes that, in an optimal partition, the agents with the minimum number of friends, i.e., the ones in
\mathcal{N}_{\min}
, must be in a coalition with all their friends.
As the marginal contribution of all enemies is, in absolute terms, less than that of a single friend, a naive approach would be to partition the agents so that each agent—not only the ones in
\mathcal{N}_{\min}
—is placed in a coalition together with all of their friends, i.e., to create a partition that satisfies
allF
. Among all such partitions, we prefer ones that avoid placing enemies in the same coalition as much as possible.
Thus, an attractive strategy is to create a coalition for each weakly connected component of
G^{f}
; we refer to the resulting algorithm as
WeaklyConn
:

WeaklyConn
:

Given a graph
G^{f}
, let
\pi=(C_{1},\dots,C_{m})
be the list of weakly connected components of
G^{f}
; return the partition
\pi
.

Unfortunately, we will show that this algorithm does not always provide a constant-factor approximation to
maxESW
.

Theorem 3.

Under
\FA
, the algorithm
WeaklyConn
guarantees an approximation of
2-\frac{6}{n+3}
to
maxESW
if
f_{\min}\geq 2
and
\frac{n}{2}
otherwise.

Proof.

Consider an instance
\mathcal{I}
of
\FA
. By
Remark

1
, we can assume that
F_{i}\neq\emptyset
for all
i\in\mathcal{N}
. Let
\pi
denote the outcome returned by
WeaklyConn
on
\mathcal{I}
.

By
2
, we have
\textsf{opt}\leq f_{\min}
.
On the other hand, in
\pi
, every agent
i\in\mathcal{N}
is in a coalition with
f_{i}
friends and at most
n-f_{i}-1
enemies. Therefore,

u_{i}(\pi)\geq f_{i}-\frac{n-f_{i}-1}{n}=f_{i}\cdot\frac{n+1}{n}-\frac{n-1}{n}\,.

Let
j
be an agent with minimum utility in
\pi
; note that
j\in\mathcal{N}_{\min}
. The approximation ratio of
WeaklyConn
on
\mathcal{I}
can be bounded as

\displaystyle\frac{\textsf{opt}(\mathcal{I})}{\min_{i\in\mathcal{N}}u_{i}(\pi)}\leq\frac{f_{j}}{u_{j}(\pi)}\leq\frac{f_{j}}{f_{j}\cdot\frac{n+1}{n}-\frac{n-1}{n}}=\frac{f_{j}\cdot n}{f_{j}(n+1)-(n-1)}.

The ratio
\frac{f_{j}\cdot n}{f_{j}(n+1)-(n-1)}
is a decreasing function of
f_{j}
, so it takes its maximum value, which is
\frac{n}{2}
, if
f_{j}=1
. If
f_{\min}\geq 2
(and hence
f_{j}\geq 2
),
we have

\frac{f_{j}\cdot n}{f_{j}(n+1)-(n-1)}\leq\frac{2n}{2(n+1)-(n-1)}=\frac{2n}{n+3}=2-\frac{6}{n+3}.

This completes our proof.
∎

Theorem

3
are tight.

4.3.
Assigning Agents with Only One Friend

Theorem

3
establishes that the most challenging instances to approximate are those containing an agent who only has one friend. Indeed, a constant approximation can be achieved as soon as
\left\lvert F_{i}\right\rvert\geq 2
for all
i\in\mathcal{N}
.
In the next sections, we show that constant-factor approximation can be recovered in randomized and symmetric settings.
To this end, we need to tackle the problem of how to assign agents who only have one friend. Hereafter, we
denote by
\mathcal{N}_{1}(\mathcal{I})=\left\{i\in\mathcal{N}\mid f_{i}=1\right\}
the set of agents with a unique friend. When
\mathcal{I}
is clear from the context, we simply write
\mathcal{N}_{1}
.

If
\mathcal{N}_{1}\neq\emptyset
,
2
and
Corollary

1
establish that, to obtain any approximation, we must satisfy
oneF
\equiv
allF
for all agents in
\mathcal{N}_{1}
and, moreover,
\textsf{opt}\in(0,1]
. Given an
\FA
instance
\mathcal{I}
, we can guarantee
oneF
to all agents in
\mathcal{N}_{1}
by placing all agents in the same coalition. A more sophisticated, yet tractable, approach is to create a minimal-by-refinement partition that enforces
oneF
for all agents in
\mathcal{N}_{1}
; we will refer to this algorithm as
OneWeaklyConn
.

OneWeaklyConn
:

On an input
G^{f}=(\mathcal{N},F)
, the algorithm creates the graph
G_{1}^{f}=(\mathcal{N},F_{1})
by only maintaining the friend relationships of the agents in
\mathcal{N}_{1}
, i.e., it sets
F_{1}=\left\{(i,j)\in F\mid i\in\mathcal{N}_{1}\right\}
. Then it splits the agents into the weakly-connected components of
G_{1}^{f},
that is, applies
WeaklyConn
on
G_{1}^{f}
.

By construction, the partition computed by
OneWeaklyConn
is the unique minimal-by-refinement partition that guarantees
oneF
to all agents in
\mathcal{N}_{1}
.
Indeed, if a partition
\pi
satisfies
oneF
for all agents in
\mathcal{N}_{1}
, then every coalition
S\in\pi
must contain a weakly connected component of
G_{1}^{f}
. Moreover, if
S
contains several weakly connected components of
G_{1}^{f}
, then
\pi
is not a minimal-by-refinement partition that satisfies
oneF
, as
S
can be split into smaller coalitions—one for each weakly connected component of
G_{1}^{f}
that it contains.

Next, we establish a useful connection between the utility of agents in
\mathcal{N}_{1}
in
the minimal-by-refinement
oneF
partition and in
an optimal partition.

Lemma 0.

Let
\pi
be the unique minimal-by-refinement partition that provides
oneF
to all agents in
\mathcal{N}_{1}
. Then, for all
i\in\mathcal{N}_{1}
we have

u_{i}(\pi)\geq\textsf{opt}
,
and
\pi(i)\subseteq\pi^{*}(i)
for every optimal partition
\pi^{*}
.

Proof.

Assume
\mathcal{N}_{1}\neq\emptyset
; otherwise, the statement is immediate.

Let
\pi^{*}
be an optimal partition.
By
2
we have
\textsf{opt}>0
; hence,
\pi^{*}
satisfies condition
oneF
for all agents.
As previously observed, for
oneF
to hold for all agents in
\mathcal{N}_{1}
, the members of each weakly connected component of
G_{1}^{f}
must belong to the same coalition.
Moreover, we have already established that the unique minimal-by-refinement partition
\pi
satisfying
oneF
for the agents in
\mathcal{N}_{1}
is precisely the partition where each coalition is a weakly connected component of
G_{1}^{f}
.
Consequently, for every
i\in\mathcal{N}_{1}
we have
\pi(i)\subseteq\pi^{*}(i)
.
Moreover,
since
F_{i}\subseteq\pi(i)\subseteq\pi^{*}(i)
, we obtain
u_{i}(\pi)\geq u_{i}(\pi^{*})\geq\textsf{opt}
.
∎

By
Lemma

4
,
\text{OneWeaklyConn}(\mathcal{I})
guarantees utility of at least
opt
to the agents in
\mathcal{N}_{1}
; however, this algorithm may place some agents in
\mathcal{N}\setminus\mathcal{N}_{1}
in singleton coalitions, so that their utility is
0
.

Clearly, if
\mathcal{N}_{1}=\mathcal{N}
, then, by
Lemma

4
,
OneWeaklyConn
computes a
maxESW
partition. This leads to the following theorem.

Theorem 5.

Under
\FA
, if
f_{i}=1
for all
i\in\mathcal{N}
then
maxESW
is in
P
. Furthermore,
OPT
is the unique minimal-by-refinement partition satisfying
oneF
(which in this case coincides with
allF
) for all agents.

4.4.
Randomized Constant Approximation

In what follows, we combine
OneWeaklyConn
and
WeaklyConn
to obtain a randomized algorithm
whose approximation ratio is at most
2
.
To start, we need to define the approximation ratio in the randomized setting.
Since for a given instance
\mathcal{I}
the space of all possible outcomes is finite, a randomized algorithm
\mathcal{A}
can be viewed as a probability distribution over a finite set of deterministic algorithms
\Sigma
,
where each algorithm in
\Sigma
computes a partition of the agents in
\mathcal{I}
;
we write
\mathcal{A}\sim\Delta(\Sigma)
.

There are two natural approaches to measuring the performance of a randomized algorithm in our setting:
(1) the expected minimum utility, over the random choices of the algorithm, as given by

\expect_{\mathcal{A}\sim\Delta(\Sigma)}\left[\min_{i\in\mathcal{N}}u_{i}(\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{I}))\right]
, and
(2) the minimum expected utility over all agents, as given by
\min_{i\in\mathcal{N}}\expect_{\mathcal{A}\sim\Delta(\Sigma)}\left[u_{i}(\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{I}))\right]
.

It is easy to see that the first measure is more stringent, i.e.,

\expect_{\mathcal{A}\sim\Delta(\Sigma)}\left[\min_{i\in\mathcal{N}}u_{i}(\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{I}))\right]\leq\min_{i\in\mathcal{N}}\expect_{\mathcal{A}\sim\Delta(\Sigma)}\left[u_{i}(\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{I}))\right].

Indeed, under the first approach we cannot expect to get an improved approximation ratio from randomization, since
for every instance
\mathcal{I}
we have

\displaystyle\expect_{\mathcal{A}\sim\Delta(\Sigma)}\left[\min_{i\in\mathcal{N}}u_{i}(\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{I}))\right]\leq\max_{\mathcal{A}\in\Sigma}\min_{i\in\mathcal{N}}u_{i}(\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{I}))\,,

i.e., the expected egalitarian welfare of a randomized algorithm
\mathcal{A}\sim\Delta(\Sigma)
cannot be better than the egalitarian welfare achieved deterministically by the best algorithm in
\Sigma
.

Thus, we pursue the second approach, and define the approximation ratio as follows:

\displaystyle r_{n}(\mathcal{A}\sim\Delta(\Sigma))=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathcal{I}=(\mathcal{N},F):\\
|\mathcal{N}|=n\end{subarray}}\frac{\textsf{opt}(\mathcal{I})}{\min_{i\in\mathcal{N}}\expect_{\mathcal{A}\sim\Delta(\Sigma)}\left[u_{i}(\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{I}))\right]}\,,

and
r(\mathcal{A}\sim\Delta(\Sigma))=\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}r_{n}(\mathcal{A}\sim\Delta(\Sigma))
.

We are ready to define our randomized algorithm,

RandAlgo
.

RandAlgo
:

With probability
\alpha
, executes algorithm
WeaklyConn
, and with probability
1-\alpha
it executes algorithm
OneWeaklyConn
,
where the parameter
\alpha\in[0,1]
will be set later and will be dependent on the input instance.

Theorem 6.

Under
\FA
,
there exists
a choice of

\alpha
such that

r_{n}(\text{RandAlgo})\leq 2-\frac{5}{n+3}\,.

Proof.

Given an instance
\mathcal{I}
, let us set
\pi=\text{WeaklyConn}(\mathcal{I})
and
\pi^{\prime}=\text{OneWeaklyConn}(\mathcal{I})
. Moreover, let
v=\min_{i\in\mathcal{N}_{1}}u_{i}(\pi^{\prime})
.

If
\mathcal{N}_{1}=\emptyset
, we set
\alpha=1
: then
RandAlgo
coincides with
WeaklyConn
, so its approximation ratio for the case
\mathcal{N}_{1}=\emptyset
is bounded by
2-\frac{6}{n+3}
.

Assume now
\mathcal{N}_{1}\neq\emptyset
. We will estimate the expected utility of an agent
i\in\mathcal{N}
depending on whether
i\in\mathcal{N}_{1}
or
i\in\mathcal{N}\setminus\mathcal{N}_{1}
.

If
i\in\mathcal{N}_{1}
, then
u_{i}(\pi)\geq\frac{2}{n}
, as shown in the proof of
Theorem

3
, while
u_{i}(\pi^{\prime})\geq v
. Hence,
\expect\left[u_{i}(\text{RandAlgo})\right]\geq\alpha\cdot\frac{2}{n}+(1-\alpha)\cdot v
.

If
i\in\mathcal{N}\setminus\mathcal{N}_{1}
, then
u_{i}(\pi)\geq 1+\frac{3}{n}
, as in the worst case
i
has only two friends and is put in a coalition with all her enemies, while
u_{i}(\pi^{\prime})
may be
0
. Consequently,
\expect\left[u_{i}(\text{RandAlgo})\right]\geq\alpha\cdot\left(1+\frac{3}{n}\right)
.

Putting these two scenarios together, we get

\displaystyle\min_{i\in\mathcal{N}}\expect\left[u_{i}(\text{RandAlgo}(\mathcal{I}))\right]

\displaystyle\geq\min\left\{v+\alpha\cdot\left(\frac{2}{n}-v\right),\alpha\cdot\left(1+\frac{3}{n}\right)\right\}\,.

Since
v\geq\tfrac{2}{n}
, the function
v+\alpha\cdot\left(\tfrac{2}{n}-v\right)
is decreasing in
\alpha
, whereas
\alpha\cdot\left(1+\tfrac{3}{n}\right)
is increasing in
\alpha
. The minimum of the two is attained when they are equal, which happens for
\alpha=\frac{v}{1+\frac{1}{n}+v}
. Hence,

(4)

\min_{i\in\mathcal{N}}\expect\left[u_{i}(\text{RandAlgo}(\mathcal{I}))\right]\geq\left(1+\frac{3}{n}\right)\cdot\frac{v}{1+\frac{1}{n}+v}\,.

The right-hand side of (
4
) is an increasing function of
v
and by
Lemma

4
we have
v\geq\textsf{opt}(\mathcal{I})
. Hence,

\min_{i\in\mathcal{N}}\expect\left[u_{i}(\text{RandAlgo}(\mathcal{I}))\right]\geq\left(1+\frac{3}{n}\right)\cdot\frac{\textsf{opt}(\mathcal{I})}{1+\frac{1}{n}+\textsf{opt}(\mathcal{I})}.

Now,
\mathcal{N}_{1}\neq\emptyset
implies
\textsf{opt}(\mathcal{I})\leq 1
, so we obtain

\displaystyle r_{n}(\text{RandAlgo})

\displaystyle=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathcal{I}=(\mathcal{N},F)\\
|\mathcal{N}|=n\end{subarray}}\frac{\textsf{opt}(\mathcal{I})}{\min_{i\in\mathcal{N}}\expect\left[u_{i}(\text{RandAlgo}(\mathcal{I}))\right]}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1+\frac{1}{n}+\textsf{opt}(\mathcal{I})}{1+\frac{3}{n}}\leq\frac{2n+1}{n+3}=2-\frac{5}{n+3}\,

which is what we wanted to prove.
∎

In the appendix, we give an example showing the tightness of the analysis.

4.5.
Deterministic Constant Approximation in the Symmetric Case

In this section, we will be working with symmetric instances; therefore, we will only consider the graph
G^{\mathit{sf}}
.

Theorem 7.

Under
\FA
, there exists a polynomial-time algorithm that computes a
(2-\frac{4}{n+2})
-approximation for symmetric instances.

Proof.

If

Conclusions and Future Work

We studied the problem of maximizing the egalitarian welfare in Friends and Enemies Games, focusing on two classical preference models: Enemies Aversion (
\EA
) and Friends Appreciation (
\FA
).
We provided an almost complete picture of the complexity of this problem, by establishing both hardness results and polynomial-time approximation guarantees.
A natural next step is to determine whether a constant-factor approximation is indeed possible for
\FA
or to derive an inapproximability result. Moreover, employing the egalitarian welfare as a measure of the quality of stable outcomes, similarly to the work of Monaco et al.
(
28
)
, also represents a promising direction for future work.
More broadly, it would be interesting to explore egalitarian welfare in other variants of Friends and Enemies Games, such as those that incorporate neutral agents (with small positive or negative effects) or assign a different negative value for enemies instead of the standard
-\frac{1}{n}
or
-n
.
Finally, while the utilitarian and egalitarian objectives have received attention, the Nash welfare in hedonic games remains unexplored.
