Association of Autosomal Dominant Snowflake Vitreoretinal Degeneration with Retinoschisis
Authors: Bikash R. Pattnaik, Ken K. Nischal, Oleg Alekseev, Steven Williams, Alessandro Iannaccone, Michelle Alabek, Enes Akyuz, Amgad Eldib, Meha Kabra, Feyza Sule Aslan, Lejla Vajzovic, Bryan Carmichael, Zhiwei Ma, Xiaodong Jiao, J. Fielding Hejtmancik
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo define and characterize the clinical association of autosomal dominant snowflake vitreoretinal degeneration (SVD) with retinochisis arising from a heterozygous KCNJ13 variant in unrelated multigenerational families and to investigate its genetic, molecular, and functional consequences.DesignAffected individuals from 4 unrelated families underwent detailed ophthalmic examinations, multimodal retinal imaging, and electroretinography. Genetic testing using targeted next-generation sequencing panels and Sanger sequencing identified a heterozygous KCNJ13 c.341T > C variant (p.[L114P]). In silico analyses and functional assays were performed to assess the pathogenicity of the variant.SubjectsPatients with inherited retinal degeneration are identified through genetic testing.MethodsStandard clinical diagnosis and imaging. In silico prediction of gene association and protein structure. Heterologous expression of protein through plasmid transfection followed by protein expression and electrophysiological analysis of function.Main Outcome MeasuresAssociations between clinical findings, in silico predictions, and molecular mechanism studies.ResultsThe KCNJ13 c.341T > C (p.L114P) variant is observed in multiple affected family members and appears to be consistent with autosomal dominant inheritance. Clinically, individuals present with retinoschisis and other classical SVD features, including snowflake-like retinal deposits, peripheral pigmentary changes, and visual field loss. Functional analyses in heterologous expression systems demonstrated that the L114P substitution impaired Kir7.1 channel function, localPS3 suggesting a dominant-negative effect.ConclusionsThis study identifies KCNJ13 c.341T > C, p.(L114P) as a pathogenic variant that causes autosomal-dominant SVD in additional cases associated with retinoschisis, supported by molecular and functional findings. These findings expand the mutational spectrum of KCNJ13-associated retinopathies, suggest a dominant-negative pathogenic mechanism for KCNJ13 mutations in SVD, and underscore the importance of distinguishing between dominant and recessive KCNJ13 variants for clinical diagnosis and genetic counseling. Our results support the broader implementation of genetic testing for inherited retinal disorders to guide diagnosis and potential future therapeutic interventions.Financial Disclosure(s)Proprietary or commercial disclosure may be found in the Footnotes and Disclosures at the end of this article.

Genetic factors account for more than 70% of rare diseases, as defined by the World Health Organization, which are conditions affecting fewer than 65 individuals per 100 000 people. Advances in genetic screening technologies have accelerated the discovery of disease-causing mutations, enhanced the understanding of disease mechanisms, and expanded patient registries and the curation of rare disease genes.1 Despite these advantages, drug development efforts for the ultrarare KCNJ13 gene (MIM: 603208)2 variants are challenged by the identification of suitable patients for clinical trials. The small and heterogeneous patient populations, strict eligibility criteria, and challenges in reaching appropriate patient groups often limit trial enrollment.3 We believe that progress in understanding the clinical significance of the KCNJ13 variant would benefit greatly from identifying and carefully phenotyping additional patients. Expanding patient cohorts through collaborative registries and genetic screening efforts may help clarify genotype–phenotype relationships, strengthen evidence of segregation, and improve the interpretation of rare variants identified in clinical sequencing studies.

The KCNJ13 gene encodes the inwardly rectifying potassium channel (Kir7.1),4 which contributes to ionic homeostasis and the normal physiological function of retinal pigment epithelial (RPE) cells.5,6 This channel participates in processes such as maintaining membrane potential, supporting the phagocytosis of photoreceptor outer segments, and contributing to the integrity of the blood-retinal barrier. Variants in KCNJ13 have been associated with several retinal disorders and can display either dominant or recessive inheritance patterns. Recessive mutations in KCNJ13 are most commonly linked to severe early-onset retinal dystrophies, including Leber congenital amaurosis (LCA; MIM: 614186), which is characterized by profound visual impairment beginning in infancy.7, 8, 9, 10

In contrast, dominant KCNJ13 mutations have been reported in conditions of later onset and more variable phenotypes, such as snowflake vitreoretinal degeneration (SVD; MIM: 193230).11 Snowflake vitreoretinal degeneration is a rare autosomal-dominant retinal dystrophy characterized by progressive degeneration of the vitreous and peripheral retina, which may lead to visual impairment. Clinically, patients often exhibit snowflake-like vitreous opacities and peripheral retinal abnormalities. To date, only 3 original families carrying the p.R162W variant have been reported with SVD,11 although a Swedish family with autosomal dominant macular dystrophy, optic disc abnormalities, retinal detachment, and cataracts has also been reported to have the same variant.12 Despite its clear inheritance pattern, the molecular mechanisms underlying SVD are still being investigated.

In this study, we identified a novel heterozygous KCNJ13 variant (rs1401218860; NM_002242.4), c.341T > C, p.(L114P) in 4 unrelated multigenerational families with autosomal dominant SVD. Because this variant has not previously been established as pathogenic, it is currently classified as a variant of uncertain significance (VUS). This limits clinical care options. Therefore, we examined its potential impact through clinical characterization, genetic analysis, molecular, and functional studies. These findings contribute to the expanding spectrum of KCNJ13 variants associated with retinal disease and provide additional insights into genotype–phenotype relationships, the deleterious effects of L-to-P substitutions, and the value of genetic testing in diagnosing and managing inherited retinal disorders.

Methods

Ethical Statement

All procedures performed in studies involving human participants were in accordance with the ethical standards of the relevant institutional and/or national research committee, the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments, or comparable ethical standards. All data presented in this study were collected as part of routine care and anonymized for publication.

Patient data were collected in accordance with an approved institutional review board protocol and the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act, as mandated by the University of Pittsburgh and Duke University Health System. Best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) was measured using an ETDRS retro-illuminated cabinet (Precision Vision). Fundus pseudocolor and autofluorescence ultra-widefield images were obtained using an Optos California device (Optos). Macular spectral-domain OCT scans were acquired using a Heidelberg Spectralis imaging platform. Full-field electroretinography (ERG) was performed using a Diagnosys ColorDome, an E3 console, and Espion software, in accordance with the International Society for Clinical Electrophysiology of Vision standards.

Mutagenesis of KCNJ13

The Kir7.1 wild-type clone, pC1GFP-Kir7.1 (Addgene plasmid #79118), was used as a template for site-directed mutagenesis. We generated the p.L114P mutation using the Quick-change II XL Site-Directed Mutagenesis Kit (Agilent; #200521) and the following primers (F: CCCCGGAGACACAACTCACA, R: CCGGGGAGAAGGAGAATGCA). The mutant sequence was verified by Sanger sequencing at the UW-Madison Biotechnology Core Facility.

Cell Culture and Protein Expression Analysis

Human embryonic kidney (HEK293) cells were obtained from American Type Culture Collection and cultured in high-glucose Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, 1% L-glutamine, 1% MEM nonessential amino acids, and sodium pyruvate at 37°C in 5% CO2. Cells were passaged when they reached 80% confluence. Mycoplasma testing was performed routinely to ensure negative results across all samples. For transfection with wild-type or L114P plasmids, cells were seeded the day before and transfected with 2 μg of total DNA per 35-mm dish using TransIT (Mirus Bio) following the manufacturer’s instructions and a previous protocol.

Structure predictions were performed using the Cytoscape GeneMANIA plug-in to examine interactions among genes harboring heterozygous mutations identified in 3 patients (Table S1, available at www.ophthalmologyscience.org lists these mutations). Models of the wild-type and L114P mutant proteins were generated using SWISS-MODEL based on template searches performed with BLAST and HHblits13 against the SWISS-MODEL template library and Uniclust30.14 Target-template alignments using ProMod3,15 which preserves all conserved coordinates between the target and template, were used to produce the final model. Global and per-residue model quality assessments were performed using the QMEAN scoring function.16 Quaternary structure annotation was based on a supervised machine learning algorithm.17 Support vector machines combine interface conservation, structural clustering, and other template features to estimate the quality of the quaternary structure (QSQE).

Electrophysiology

Whole-cell patch-clamp recordings were performed on transfected HEK293 cells plated at low density on glass coverslips to facilitate the identification of individual cells. Extracellular Ringer’s solution (HEPES Ringer's) contained (in mM) 135 NaCl, 1 MgCl2, 10 HEPES, 1.8 CaCl2, 10 glucose, 5 KCl, adjusted to pH 7.4 with NaOH, and osmolarity of 300 mOsm. Pipette resistance ranged from 2.5 to 5 MΩ when filled with a solution containing (in mM) 30 KCl, 83 K-gluconate, 5.5 EGTA-KOH, 0.5 CaCl2, 4 MgCl2, 10 HEPES, and 4 ATP, adjusted to pH 7.2 with KOH, and an osmolarity of 290 mOsm. To inhibit channel activity, 10 mM Ba2+ was added to the Ringer's solution by replacing an equal amount of Na+. Current measurements were conducted by holding the cells at 0 mV and applying a 1-second voltage ramp from –150 to 50 mV. Data acquisition was performed using Axopatch 200B, Digidata 1550, and Clampex 11 (Molecular Devices). All stored data were analyzed using Clampfit 11 and plotted using Origin (Microcal).

Case Presentation

We identified a shared heterozygous KCNJ13 variant, c.341T > C, p.L114P, in 4 unrelated families exhibiting clinical features consistent with autosomal-dominant SVD. This variant is absent from population databases, including gnomAD (https://gnomad.broadinstitute.org/), and has previously been classified as a VUS.

Case 1

A 4-year-old African American child initially presented with blurred vision. The uncorrected visual acuity was 20/80 in the right eye, 20/100 in the left eye, and 20/70 bilaterally. Fundus examination showed subconjunctival bulbar melanosis with peripheral RPE changes, while the optic nerve appeared normal. The patient’s vision improved with glasses (20/30 right, 20/40 left, and 20/30 binocular), but progressive myopia (–6.00/+5.00 × 100 in the right eye and –4.25/+4.50 × 70 in the left eye) developed during follow-up. By age 8, the fundus was notable for whitish band-shaped deposits in the mid-far periphery in both eyes, and widefield fundus autofluorescence demonstrated diffuse hyper-autofluorescence involving the posterior pole and periphery (Fig 1A). Genetic counseling was sought, and a 3-generation pedigree was constructed (Fig 1B). Full-field ERGs showed largely preserved rod responses with mildly delayed mixed b-wave and slightly reduced and mildly delayed cone responses (Fig 1C) even at the age of 10 years. Spectral-domain OCT revealed foveal retinoschisis with preservation of the ellipsoid zone (Fig 1D).

The proband’s father also reported a history of traumatic retinal detachment in the right eye, spontaneous retinal detachment in the left eye, cataracts (diagnosed at 10 years of age), and glaucoma (age of diagnosis unknown). The father's examination revealed retinal scars from the buckle procedure, aphakia, and no evidence of pigmentary changes or bone spicule formation. The father was presumed to have high myopia based on being left aphakic after lensectomy. Other relatives were unavailable for examination or record review. The patient’s paternal grandfather had a history of high myopia, progressive vision loss, glaucoma, cataracts, and retinal detachment. The proband’s paternal great-grandfather had a documented history of cataracts and glaucoma during his teenage years. Other relatives were unavailable for examination and/or review of their records.

Genetic testing via a 266-gene retinal dystrophy next-generation sequencing panel identified a heterozygous VUS in KCNJ13 (c.341T > C, p.Leu114Pro), a heterozygous likely pathogenic variant of CDHR1 (c.55+1G > A), a heterozygous VUS in RBP3 (c.3715C > T, pArg1239Trp), a heterozygous VUS in PITPNM3 (c.274 + 15A > G), and a heterozygous VUS in USH2A (c.12557T > C, p.Ile4186Thr). The proband's father underwent targeted genetic testing for all 5 variants identified in the proband. Subsequently, both the USH2A and PITPNM3 VUSs were reclassified as likely benign based on the proband’s initial test.

During longitudinal follow-up, the patient developed progressive myopia, intermittent exotropia, and cystoid macular edema, requiring topical dorzolamide therapy. OCT later demonstrated persistent schisis changes.

Case 2

An unrelated 23-year-old African American patient presented with progressive bilateral central vision loss and was initially diagnosed with juvenile retinoschisis and lattice degeneration (Fig 2). At the follow-up examination at 37 years of age, the BCVA was 20/30 and 20/64 in the right and left eye, respectively. Dilated fundus examination revealed macular and peripheral intraretinal cysts, pigmentary changes in the periphery, vitreous condensations, and a lamellar macular hole in the left eye (Fig 2A). Further assessment at 47 years indicated normal vision in the right eye (BCVA, 20/40), whereas the left eye showed progressive decline (BCVA, 20/160) (Fig 2B), consistent with retinoschisis. First-line genetic testing using a 770-gene retinal dystrophy next-generation sequencing panel revealed a c.341T > C (p.L114P) variant in KCNJ13 and compound heterozygous FAM161A variants c.2011A > G (p.N671D) and c.2057A > G (p.Y686C), with no RS1 gene mutation, deletion, or duplication. Both eyes showed extensive lattice degeneration across the periphery and maculoschisis (Fig 2C). Refraction in the left eye deteriorated over time, leading to the prescription of corrective lenses: –3.50 to 2.50 × 026 in the right eye and –6.50 to 0.25 × 008 in the left eye at the age of 48 years. The patient had a low-risk suspicion of primary open-angle glaucoma. Surgical intervention, including epiretinal membrane peeling and endolaser treatment in the left eye, improved anatomical outcomes but did not significantly restore visual acuity. Additionally, the patient underwent cataract surgery in both eyes for combined nuclear and cortical cataracts 3 years later.

Case 3

A third unrelated African American female first presented at age 13 with moderate myopia and normal visual acuity (BCVA 20/20 in each eye). Manifest refraction was –4.25 + 0.25 × 121 in the right eye and –2.50 + 0.75 × 35 in the left eye. Anterior segment examination was unremarkable, as was the dilated fundus examination. Nearly a decade later, she returned with significant vision decline (BCVA 20/160 in the right eye and 20/100 in the left eye), despite spectacle correction. Fundus examination demonstrated decreased foveal reflex, macular pigmentary changes, and lattice degeneration (Fig 3A). Fundus autofluorescence showed a parafoveal ring of hyperautofluorescence, more pronounced in the right eye (Fig 3B). Widefield macular OCT scans revealed extensive macular retinoschisis extending beyond the vascular arcades in both eyes (Fig 3C). Full-field ERG showed markedly reduced rod-mediated and cone-mediated responses, as well as reduced and electronegative mixed responses (Fig 3D). Genetic testing using a 330-gene panel revealed a heterozygous variant in KCNJ13 (c.341T > C, p.L114P). Testing additionally identified RPGRIP1 (c.3100-1G > A), a VUS in ADGRA3 (c.1763G > A, p.Ser588Asn), and a VUS in COL9A3 (c.1521_1547del, p.Ile509_Gly517del), none of which correlated with the patient's clinical findings. Patient's family history revealed similar macular changes in both eyes in her mother, consistent with autosomal dominant inheritance. The patient was started on topical dorzolamide and systemic Diamox, but unfortunately this did not significantly improve the macular schisis.

Case 4

A 42-year-old African American man, unrelated to previous cases, presented with progressive vision loss in the right eye over 6 months. His BCVA was 20/800 in the right eye and 20/40 in the left eye. The intraocular pressure was 19 mmHg in the right eye and 20 mmHg in the left eye. The anterior segment examination of both eyes was unremarkable, and his lens status could not be confirmed due to loss of follow-up. Fundus examination of the right eye revealed a clear view and a cup-to-disc ratio of 0.7, with an otherwise normal-appearing optic nerve, mild peripapillary atrophy, mottling of the macular RPE, broad curvilinear lattice degeneration temporally (from 7 to 10 o’clock) in the mid-periphery, and 360° snowflake degeneration in the far periphery. Fundus examination of the left eye revealed a clear view of a small cup (0.3 C/D ratio) with a normally appearing optic nerve head, mottling of the macular RPE, scattered lattice degeneration in the mid-periphery, and 360° snowflake degeneration in the far-periphery. Spectral-domain OCT revealed significant disruption of the foveal contour, consistent with lamellar macular hole changes, and loss of the outer retinal architecture from the outer nuclear layer to the RPE, resulting in a hypertransmission defect. Macular OCT of the left eye showed perifoveal attenuation of the outer retinal structures from the outer nuclear layer to the interdigitation zone. Mild vitreomacular traction, cystoid macular edema, and nasal schisis were also observed. OCT of the nerve fiber layer demonstrated moderate diffuse thinning. Fundus autofluorescence exhibited a bull’s-eye maculopathy pattern, characterized by central hypo-autofluorescence surrounded by a ring of perifoveal hyper-autofluorescence, highlighting the areas of the mid-peripheral lattice in both eyes. The macular appearance prompted a genetic workup with targeted retinal next-generation sequencing panels, which revealed a VUS, KCNJ13 c.341T > C (p.L114P), as well as several variants in other genes (Table S1). The family history was notable for vision loss secondary to glaucoma in a grandparent and retinal detachment in his brother.

Computational Prediction of Single-Nucleotide Variants

Nearly 20% of genetic variants are classified as variants of uncertain significance (VUS), and many remain difficult to interpret despite technological advances in genome sequencing and proteomic analysis. In some cases, these variants are later reclassified as benign and remain unresolved. In our cohort, the single-nucleotide variant, NM_002242.4(KCNJ13): c.341 T > C (p.Leu114Pro) (VUS), was identified in all 4 cases, suggesting a possible association with the disease phenotype. Computational prediction tools provide additional context regarding the potential impact of this variant. VarSite (residue report for Leu114Pro in O60928) indicates a moderate disease risk with a “disease propensity” value of 3.02. Other predictive algorithms also suggest a potentially deleterious effect, including CADD (raw score of 4.68),18 PHRED (score 31.00), AlphaMissense (https://alphamissense.hegelab.org/) (score 0.997),19 MutScore (https://genome.ucsc.edu/) (score 0.95),20 and REVEL (score 0.958).21 Although these in silico predictions suggest a potential functional impact, they must be interpreted with caution and in conjunction with experimental and clinical evidence. To date, there have been no reports of nonpathogenic natural variants of L114. Considering the available genetic information from multiple families, functional evidence, in silico predictions, and conservation, the variant meets several ACMG/AMP criteria (moderate PS3, supporting-level evidence PS4, supporting/moderate-strength PP1, and supporting PP3) and may support reclassification toward pathogenicity, as evaluated using the University of Maryland online tool.22,23

This variant is located in exon 2 of the 3-exon KCNJ13 gene (Fig 4). Kir7.1 functions as a tetrameric potassium channel, and structural modeling suggests that the L114 residue lies within the ion selectivity loop (Fig 4A). This position is highly conserved across species from humans to zebrafish (Fig 4B), and its neighboring residues are also conserved among structurally related ion channel proteins (Fig 4C). Gene interaction network analysis using Cytoscape with the GeneMANIA and ClueGo plugins identified associations with 13 retina-related and RPE-related genes (Table S1), along with additional 20 key genes involved in pathways relevant to photoreceptor function, including cilia development, light detection, G protein-coupled receptor signaling, and retinal homeostasis (Fig S1, Table S2, available at www.ophthalmologyscience.org). Structural modeling further indicates that leucine at this position, with its aliphatic side chain, is located within the ion-selectivity loop, where it may help maintain the local pore architecture. Substitution with proline introduces a more rigid side chain residue that could influence the confirmation of the pore region (Fig 4D, E). The structural considerations suggest a potential effect on channel architecture (Fig S2, available at www.ophthalmologyscience.org), although further experimental studies will be necessary to fully define the functional consequences of this substitution.

Dominant-Negative Effect of L114P

Given the autosomal dominant inheritance pattern of SVD, we tested whether the mutant subunit alters the function of the wild-type protein. Confocal imaging of green fluoresence protein-fused Kir7.1 and L114P Kir7.1 proteins revealed no detectable differences in cellular localization, and quantitative analysis showed no significant changes in co-localization (Fig 5A, B), suggesting that the mutant protein might traffic to the membrane similarly to the wild-type channel. We evaluated channel function by measuring current amplitudes using whole-cell electrophysiology (Fig 5C–G). Cells expressing the L114P variant showed near-baseline inward currents across negative voltages (Fig 5D–F), in contrast to the robust inwardly rectifying currents observed with WT Kir7.1 (FigC, G). The Ba2+-sensitive component, a hallmark of Kir channel activity, was minimal in L114-expressing cells (Fig 5D), indicating markedly reduced functional channel activity at the membrane. To assess potential dominant-negative effects, WT and L114P Kir7.1 were co-expressed at a 1:1 plasmid ratio. Under these conditions, the resulting channel currents were substantially reduced compared with WT alone and were virtually identical to those observed with L114P expression alone (Fig 6A–C). This reduction in current suggests that incorporation of the mutant subunit into heteromeric channel complexes interferes with WT channel function, consistent with a dominant-negative mechanism that may contribute to the autosomal dominant inheritance observed in affected individuals.

Discussion

Across these 4 unrelated families, the shared KCNJ13 p.L114P variant was consistently associated with retinal phenotypes, including snowflake-like peripheral degeneration, lattice degeneration, retinoschisis, and variable ERG abnormalities. Although clinical severity varied, the recurrent identification of this variant in individuals with similar retinal findings and apparent autosomal-dominant inheritance suggests a potential association with SVD-like disease manifestations. Snowflake vitreoretinal degeneration typically occurs during the first or second decade of life and is associated with slowly progressive visual impairment. The most notable features associated with the novel p.L114P KCNJ13 variant identified in this study were retinoschisis and heterogeneous ERG phenotypes. While cone dysfunction was observed in all patients, a reduction in the dark-adapted b-wave was consistent with retinoschisis.24 Previous reports have described several ocular features associated with KCNJ13-related SVD, including corneal guttae, early onset cataracts, fibrillar vitreous degeneration, and crystalline snowflake deposits in the peripheral retina.11,25, 26, 27, 28, 29, 30, 31 In all 4 cases, the OCT findings were documented using different terms, such as retinoschisis, maculoshisis, and cystoid macular edema, based on the treating physician's clinical evaluation. Because of these potential confounding factors, we describe the structural findings while noting that myopia and vitreomacular traction may contribute to the schisis-like changes.

The autosomal dominant inheritance of the KCNJ13 variant has been reported in SVD and is attributed to the loss of Kir7.1 channel function, which affects retinal structure and physiology.32 Another variant, R162W, with a loss of function, has been reported in association with full-thickness macular holes, possibly resulting from an abnormal vitreous architecture anchored to the retina.31,33 These observations suggest that vitreomacular traction may contribute to the development of schisis in some patients. Kir7.1 forms a homotetramer channel characterized by relatively low conductance and unique ion selectivity. These channels contribute to stabilization of the RPE apical membrane potential, support fluid transport, and help maintain photoreceptor outer segment homeostasis.5,34, 35, 36, 37 Alterations in Kir7.1 channel activity could therefore influence fluid flow in the retina, potentially leading to edema, although the precise mechanisms remain to be fully established.

In clinical genetics, the interpretation of missense variants often relies on information from curated databases and computational prediction tools. In case 2, an additional compound heterozygous variant in FAM161A was identified and predicted to be a VUS, despite prior reports linking variants in this gene to retinitis pigmentosa. In all 4 patients, the KCNJ13 p.L114P variant was the most consistent finding; however, additional nonoverlapping variants in other retinal genes may partially influence the clinical presentation, act as genetic modifiers that influence disease expression, and contribute to the heterogeneity (severity, age of onset, and specific clinical features) observed among affected individuals. The ACMG/AMP guidelines provide a framework for evaluating variant pathogenicity.22 Based on the available genetic, structural, and functional data presented here, the L114P substitution was considered likely pathogenic. This change replaces a highly conserved leucine residue within the ionic selectivity loop of Kir7.1 with proline. Residue 114 lies within the P-region of the channel and is located near selectivity filter sites Fig S3 and S4.38

Potassium ion conduction through the channel requires coordinated occupancy of selective filter sites by multiple K+ ions, typically in either the [S1, S3] or [S2, S4] configurations under physiological conditions.39,40 Electrophysiological recordings indicated that channels containing the L114P variant are largely nonconductive in both homotetrameric and heterotetrameric configurations. These findings suggest that the L114 residue may help maintain the structural features necessary for proper pore geometry and channel gating.40 The channel functional studies were carried out in HEK293 cells as a heterologous system, which we have previously validated in multiple reports. The use of native expressions in polarized RPE cells might enhance our understanding of physiology, trafficking, and other properties.

Structural modeling further suggests that proline substitution may alter the local conformation of the selectivity loop and potentially influence K+ occupancy within the pore. Compared with leucine, proline has a distinct cyclic structure that can impose conformational constraints on protein backbones and may also undergo post-translational modifications, such as hydroxylation, acetylation, and O-linked glycosylation. Computational predictions using AlphaMissense, a deep learning model that evaluates the effects of amino acid substitutions on protein structure and function, also suggested a high likelihood of functional impact for the L114P substitution, making it highly pathogenic (Table S3, available at www.ophthalmologyscience.org and Fig 4).41

If wild-type and mutant subunits are expressed at similar levels and incorporated into pore complexes randomly, then only a small fraction (½ˆ4 or 1/32) of pore complexes would be expected to contain exclusively wild-type subunits and remain fully functional. The incorporation of mutant Kir7.1 subunits into channel complexes could therefore reduce potassium conductance even in the presence of normal subunits (Fig 6D). Such effects may disrupt membrane potential regulation in RPE cells and contribute to the associated retinal phenotypes, consistent with previous observations for other Kir7.1 variants.5,10,42 In contrast, null mutations that abolish protein expression or severely destabilize the channel typically lead to autosomal recessive forms of retinal degeneration, such as autosomal recessive LCA.9 Similarly, mouse models lacking Kir7.1 cause autosomal recessive retinal degeneration.43,44 Understanding the functional implications of KCNJ13 variants is crucial for interpreting phenotypic outcomes and developing targeted therapies, including gene augmentation and allele-specific suppression. These findings broaden the spectrum of KCNJ13 allelic variants and underscore the mechanistic continuum from loss-of-function (recessive LCA) to dominant-negative effects (dominant SVD).

In conclusion, the identification and functional validation of the KCNJ13 c.341T > C (p.L114P) variant in autosomal dominant SVD highlights the critical role of this gene in retinal disease. Our findings expand the spectrum of KCNJ13 allele variants and support a mechanistic continuum ranging from loss-of-function mutations causing recessive LCA to dominant-negative effects underlying autosomal dominant SVD. These results emphasize the importance of integrating genetic analysis with detailed clinical phenotyping, as previously noted.45 Together, these insights further refine genotype–phenotype correlations in inherited retinal disorders.
