Trajectories of Gestational Weight Gain During Early and Mid-Pregnancy and Their Association with Hypertensive Disorders of Pregnancy
Authors: Wenwen Li, Youhong Kang, Huale Zhang, Wenxin Tan, Feng Li, Kunhai Wu, Zhuanji Fang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo examine the association between trajectories of gestational weight gain (GWG) during early and mid-pregnancy and hypertensive disorders of pregnancy (HDP). A trajectory approach was used because total GWG or a single measurement cannot represent the timing and pattern of weight change.MethodsThis retrospective cohort included 11,278 women who attended the nutrition clinic and delivered at Fujian Provincial Maternity and Child Health Hospital, Fuzhou, China, between March 2014 and May 2018. Interval-specific GWG through 28 weeks was characterized using latent class growth modeling. Logistic regression estimated associations with HDP; the fully adjusted model included maternal age, pre-pregnancy body mass index, parity, platelet count, lactate dehydrogenase, and alanine aminotransferase.ResultsThe current analysis used four reported GWG patterns: stable-low (Group 1; n=9,314, 82.6%), early-slow/mid-fast (Group 2; n=70, 0.6%), early-fast/mid-slow (Group 3; n=301, 2.7%), and moderate-growth (Group 4; n=1,593, 14.1%). Compared with Group 1, the fully adjusted odds of HDP were higher in Group 3 (odds ratio [OR]=2.285, 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.370–3.613; P=0.001) and Group 4 (OR=1.356, 95% CI 1.037–1.752; P=0.023), but not Group 2 (OR=1.084, 95% CI 0.176–3.528; P=0.911).ConclusionIn this hospital-based cohort, early- and mid-pregnancy GWG patterns were associated with HDP. The estimates for the smallest groups were imprecise, and the observational design does not establish causality or predictive utility. Studies with prospectively recorded HDP diagnosis timing and external validation are required before clinical use.

Introduction

Gestational weight gain (GWG) reflects maternal, fetal, placental, and fluid-related changes across pregnancy. Appropriate GWG supports maternal and fetal health, whereas excessive gain is associated with hypertensive disorders of pregnancy (HDP), gestational diabetes, macrosomia, and postpartum weight retention; insufficient gain is associated with small-for-gestational-age birth and preterm birth.1–3 In a global meta-analysis, pre-pregnancy overweight and obesity affected 23.0% and 16.3% of pregnant women, respectively.4 In a 2015 Chinese survey, the corresponding prevalences were 14.9% and 3.1%.5 HDP affects approximately 8–10% of pregnancies worldwide and 5.22% in China and remains an important cause of maternal and perinatal morbidity.6

Most studies have related HDP to total GWG or gain within a single interval.7–11 Those summaries can obscure when rapid gain occurs and whether women follow heterogeneous longitudinal patterns. Latent class growth models (LCGMs) can identify groups with distinct patterns across repeated measurements, but their results depend on the number and timing of measurements, model specification, convergence, posterior classification quality, and the size of the smallest class. Prior trajectory studies, including work in Chinese women with obesity, suggest that the timing of GWG may matter.12 However, early- and mid-pregnancy data from broader East Asian clinical populations remain limited.

We focused on measurements through 28 weeks because this window may permit recognition of atypical gain before late-pregnancy weight changes dominate, while still providing repeated observations for trajectory modeling. We therefore aimed to characterize early- and mid-pregnancy GWG trajectories and examine their association with subsequent HDP. Because HDP is diagnosed after 20 weeks, the timing of diagnosis relative to the 24- and 28-week weight measurements is also an important source of potential reverse causation.

Materials and Methods

Study Population

This retrospective cohort used outpatient maternal and child health records and inpatient electronic medical records from Fujian Provincial Maternity and Child Health Hospital in Fuzhou, Fujian, China. Eligible women attended the hospital nutrition clinic and delivered at the hospital between March 2014 and May 2018.

Inclusion criteria were age ≥18 years, a singleton pregnancy, and no chronic hypertension before pregnancy. From 23,810 women who attended the early-pregnancy outpatient clinic and had established obstetric records, 12,532 unique women were excluded, leaving 11,278 women in the final analytic cohort (Figure 1). Exclusion reasons were fetal malformation or pregnancy termination for personal reasons (n=556), spontaneous abortion before 28 weeks (n=327), missing or incomplete data/records (n=3,732), logically inconsistent records during data entry (n=119), chronic hypertension complicating pregnancy (n=2,036), weight not recorded at the specified gestational weeks or subsequent loss to follow-up (n=7,853), and multiple pregnancy (n=243). Because some women met more than one exclusion criterion, the reason-specific counts were not mutually exclusive and should not be summed. Measurement and Collection

Data were obtained from the outpatient maternal and child health system and inpatient electronic medical records. Variables included maternal age, gravidity, parity, pre-pregnancy body mass index (BMI), pregnancy history and comorbidities, blood pressure, pre-pregnancy weight, weights at 12, 16, 20, 24, and 28 gestational weeks, and weight before delivery. Weight was recorded to 0.1 kg. Laboratory measures included white blood cell count (WBC), hemoglobin (HGB), platelet count (PLT), triglycerides (TG), lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), and aspartate aminotransferase (AST). Early-pregnancy laboratory results were used when available. No values were imputed. For trajectory fitting, observed GWG measurements were analyzed when at least two valid time points were available; duplicate records for the same participant and time point were averaged. Association models used complete cases for the covariates in each model.

Definitions

(1) Interval-specific weight gain was defined at approximately 4-week intervals (target visit ±5 days): GWG12=weight at 12 weeks minus pre-pregnancy weight; GWG16=weight at 16 weeks minus weight at 12 weeks; GWG20=weight at 20 weeks minus weight at 16 weeks; GWG24=weight at 24 weeks minus weight at 20 weeks; and GWG28=weight at 28 weeks minus weight at 24 weeks.

(2) Hypertensive disorders of pregnancy were defined according to the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists guideline.13 Gestational hypertension was new-onset systolic blood pressure ≥140 mmHg and/or diastolic blood pressure ≥90 mmHg after 20 weeks without proteinuria or severe features. Preeclampsia was hypertension after 20 weeks with proteinuria or relevant maternal organ dysfunction; eclampsia was new-onset seizure not explained by another cause in a woman with preeclampsia. The gestational week and date of first HDP diagnosis were not collected in the original dataset.

Statistical Analysis

Seven weight measures were available in the clinical record; the five interval-specific GWG variables through 28 weeks were used for trajectory modeling. No formal power calculation was performed for this retrospective analysis. The final analytic cohort comprised 11,278 women after application of the eligibility and data-availability criteria.

Analyses were conducted in R version 4.3.2. Continuous variables were summarized as mean (standard deviation) when approximately normally distributed and as median (first quartile, third quartile) otherwise. Categorical variables were summarized as number (percentage). Between-group comparisons used the Kruskal–Wallis test for non-normally distributed continuous variables and the Pearson chi-square or Fisher exact test for categorical variables, as appropriate. LCGMs with quadratic time terms were compared across one to five classes. Model selection considered convergence, Akaike information criterion (AIC), Bayesian information criterion (BIC), sample-size adjusted BIC (SABIC), entropy, average posterior probability of assignment (APPA), odds of correct classification, class proportions, clinical interpretability, and trajectory separation.

HDP (0=no, 1=yes) was the dependent variable in binary logistic regression, and trajectory group was the categorical independent variable with Group 1 as the reference. Three models were specified: unadjusted; partially adjusted for maternal age, pre-pregnancy BMI, and parity; and fully adjusted for those variables plus PLT, LDH, and ALT. Covariates were selected a priori on clinical grounds and from the available laboratory variables rather than by automated stepwise selection. Results are reported as ORs with 95% CIs. In an independent audit of the supplied final analysis file, the fully adjusted complete-case model included 11,025 women and 430 HDP events; the maximum variance inflation factor was 1.22 and the Hosmer–Lemeshow test yielded χ2=10.01 with 8 degrees of freedom (P=0.264). All tests were two-sided and P<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

LCGM Analysis of Early- and Mid-Pregnancy Weight Gain

The reported analysis classified 11,278 women into four GWG groups across intervals ending at 12, 16, 20, 24, and 28 weeks (Figure 2), and group distributions are shown in Figure 3A. All candidate one- to five-class models converged. The five-class model had the lowest AIC, BIC, and SABIC (186560.62, 186,707.23, and 186643.67, respectively), but both the four- and five-class solutions contained an extremely small class (<0.3%); their entropy values were 0.8542 and 0.8500, respectively. Therefore, statistical fit was considered together with class size, classification quality, clinical interpretability, and result stability, and the originally reported four trajectory groups were retained. Discussion

In this hospital-based retrospective cohort, the reported early- and mid-pregnancy GWG grouping was associated with HDP. Compared with the stable-low group, the early-fast/mid-slow and moderate-growth groups had higher adjusted odds of HDP. The estimates for the small early-slow/mid-fast and early-fast/mid-slow groups were less precise. Although higher-order candidate models had slightly lower information criteria, they produced extremely small classes; retaining the original four groups therefore balanced statistical fit with class size, clinical interpretability, and result stability.

Previous studies have linked excessive total or interval-specific GWG with HDP.7–11,14,15 A trajectory approach may add information by distinguishing women who reach similar total gain through different temporal patterns. Our results are consistent with the possibility that rapid gain early in pregnancy marks a higher-risk phenotype, but they do not establish that changing the trajectory will reduce HDP. The association may also vary by pre-pregnancy BMI, parity, and other maternal characteristics.

Several biological pathways could plausibly connect early rapid weight gain with HDP, including adipose-tissue expansion, inflammation, oxidative stress, endothelial dysfunction, and placental maladaptation.16–27 These mechanisms were not measured in this study; therefore, they are literature-based hypotheses rather than mediators demonstrated by our data.

A further limitation concerns the temporal sequence between weight measurement and HDP onset. The gestational week and date of first HDP diagnosis were not collected during the original data collection. We therefore could not determine whether the 24- or 28-week weight measurements preceded or followed HDP onset, or perform a sensitivity analysis restricted to measurements obtained before diagnosis. Because HDP-related fluid retention or clinical management may affect maternal weight, reverse causation cannot be excluded, and the associations should be interpreted cautiously.

Age, pre-pregnancy BMI, LDH, and ALT were positively associated with HDP, whereas parity was inversely associated.14,28–33 PLT was not statistically significant in the fully adjusted model and should not be described as an independent factor. These covariate associations may reflect baseline susceptibility or evolving disease and do not by themselves explain the relation between GWG patterns and HDP.

Clinically, repeated weight measurements can support counseling that considers both the amount and timing of gain. Women showing rapid early gain may warrant review of dietary intake, physical activity, edema, and other HDP risk factors, with individualized management guided by pre-pregnancy BMI–specific recommendations.34 The present study did not evaluate an intervention, a prediction model, or a treatment threshold; its findings should therefore be viewed as hypothesis-generating rather than a basis for preventing HDP through a specific trajectory target.

This study has several limitations. First, it was retrospective and observational, so residual confounding and reverse causation cannot be excluded. The gestational week and date of first HDP diagnosis were not collected, preventing a temporally ordered sensitivity analysis. Second, recruitment from a hospital nutrition clinic may introduce referral and selection bias and limit generalizability. Third, the smallest reported group contained only 70 women and two HDP events, and a second group contained 301 women; estimates for these groups were imprecise and class assignment may be unstable Fourth, although the five-class model had the lowest information criteria, both four- and five-class candidate solutions contained extremely small classes; the original four groups were retained by jointly considering statistical fit, class size, interpretability, and stability. Fifth, missing laboratory values led to complete-case analysis in the fully adjusted model, and unmeasured diet, physical activity, socioeconomic, and clinical factors may have confounded associations. Finally, the results have not been externally validated.

Conclusion

In this hospital-based study population, reported early- and mid-pregnancy GWG patterns were associated with HDP, with higher adjusted odds in the early-fast/mid-slow and moderate-growth groups than in the stable-low group. These findings do not demonstrate causality, prevention, or predictive performance. Studies with prospectively recorded HDP diagnosis timing and validation in other populations are needed before clinical application.
