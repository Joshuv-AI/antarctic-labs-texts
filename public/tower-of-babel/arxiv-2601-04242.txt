The holonomic triangle: from a symmetry between e \mathrm{e} and π \pi to additive Gamma functions
Benoit Cloitre

Abstract
. Two linear recurrences exhibit mirror symmetry connecting the constants e \mathrm{e} and π \pi . When parametrized, their asymptotic connection constants extend to meromorphic functions satisfying additive functional equations with rational coefficients. We call such functions additive Gamma functions (AGFs), recognizing Euler’s Γ ⁡ ( z ) \Gamma(z) as the order-1 prototype. Our theory reveals a structural dichotomy: one AGF is expressible as Gamma ratios (regular case), another involves incomplete Gamma (irregular case). AGFs complete a holonomic triangle between P-recursive sequences, additive functional equations, and differential equations, unifying discrete and continuous perspectives under the condition that Gamma factors in asymptotics have integer slopes.

1.
Introduction

Notation

Throughout this paper,
\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}:=\{0,1,2,\ldots\}
denotes the nonnegative integers, and
\mathbb{Z}
,
\mathbb{Q}
,
\mathbb{R}
,
\mathbb{C}
denote the standard number systems. We use the principal branch for
\log
and
\Gamma
, with the cut along
(-\infty,0]
for
\log
and poles at
\{0,-1,-2,\ldots\}
for
\Gamma
.

We use
n
for discrete indices and
m\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
for the discrete parameter in the recurrences. Analytic continuation is always performed in a complex variable denoted by
z
(or occasionally
w
), so that no argument of a meromorphic function is ever written with the symbol
n

1.1.
The mirror symmetry

This work originates from a mirror symmetry observed by the author in 2002 between two linear recurrences connecting the mathematical constants
\mathrm{e}
and
\pi
:

(1.1)

\boxed{\begin{aligned} u_{n+2}&=u_{n+1}+\frac{u_{n}}{n},\qquad&\text{\small(world of $\mathrm{e}$)}\\[3.00003pt]
v_{n+2}&=\frac{v_{n+1}}{n}+v_{n},\qquad&\text{\small(world of $\pi$)}\end{aligned}}

both with initial conditions
u_{1}=v_{1}=0
and
u_{2}=v_{2}=1
for
n\geq 1
. These recurrences satisfy

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{n}{u_{n}}=\mathrm{e},\qquad\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{2n}{v_{n}^{2}}=\pi.

The sequence
(u_{n})
relates to derangement numbers
[
13
]
while
(v_{n})
connects to Wallis integrals and double factorials
[
14
]
; we refer to Loya
[
11
, Section 7.7.3]
for detailed derivations. Finch
[
6
]
notes these recurrences as a counterpoint to Wimp’s dichotomy
[
19
]
, which distinguishes “
\mathrm{e}
-mathematics” (linear, explicit) from “
\pi
-mathematics” (nonlinear, mysterious).

While each sequence individually belongs to mathematical folklore, their juxtaposition suggests a deeper structure connecting
\mathrm{e}
and
\pi
. The following arithmetic duality begins to reveal this hidden structure.

1.2.
The arithmetic duality

By introducing a parameter
m\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
into the denominators of (
1.1
):

(1.2)

\boxed{\begin{aligned} u_{n+2}(m)&=u_{n+1}(m)+\frac{u_{n}(m)}{n+m},\\[3.00003pt]
v_{n+2}(m)&=\frac{v_{n+1}(m)}{n+m}+v_{n}(m),\end{aligned}}

we obtain parametrized sequences
u_{n}(m)
and
v_{n}(m)
with asymptotic behaviors

u_{n}(m)\sim f(m)\cdot n,\qquad v_{n}(m)\sim g(m)\cdot\sqrt{n}.

The analysis of these connection constants for
m\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
reveals an arithmetic duality. Once normalized by
f(0)=\frac{1}{\mathrm{e}}
and
g(0)=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}
, they take the form of linear combinations:

(1.3)

\boxed{\begin{aligned} (-1)^{m}\frac{f(m)}{f(0)}&=a_{m}-\mathrm{e}\cdot b_{m},\quad\text{with }a_{m},b_{m}\in\mathbb{Z},\\[5.0pt]
(-1)^{m}\frac{g(m)}{g(0)}&=p_{m}-\pi\cdot q_{m},\quad\text{with }p_{m},q_{m}\in\mathbb{Q}.\end{aligned}}

The explicit formulas for
a_{m}
,
b_{m}
,
p_{m}
,
q_{m}
are established in Theorems
1.3
and
1.4
.

This arithmetic duality suggests that
f(m)
and
g(m)
are not merely real sequences but discrete samplings of underlying analytic functions. The question naturally arises: can these connection constants be extended to the complex plane, and if so, do the resulting functions inherit algebraic structure from the recurrences?

1.3.
From arithmetic linear forms to meromorphic functions

From this point on, we keep the letters
m,n
for integers (parameters or discrete indices) and reserve
z
for a complex variable.

When analytically continued to the complex plane, these constants extend to meromorphic functions
f(z)
and
g(z)
satisfying additive
functional equations with rational coefficients. This motivates the central definition of this work:

Definition 1.1

(Additive Gamma function)
.

A meromorphic function
h:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}
is an additive Gamma function (AGF) of order
r
if it satisfies the following three conditions:

(i)

Additive functional equation:

h
satisfies a homogeneous additive functional equation of order
r
with rational coefficients:

\sum_{k=0}^{r}R_{k}(z)h(z+k)=0,

where
R_{k}(z)\in\mathbb{Q}(z)
are rational functions with
R_{0}\not\equiv 0
and
R_{r}\not\equiv 0
, ensuring the equation has effective order
r
.

(ii)

Moderate growth:
For any vertical strip
a\leq\mathrm{Re}\,z\leq b
not containing poles of
h
, there exist constants
C_{a,b}>0
and
N_{a,b}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
such that

|h(z)|\leq C_{a,b}(1+|\mathrm{Im}\,z|)^{N_{a,b}}

for all
z
in the strip.

(iii)

Normalization:
For any choice of
z_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
avoiding poles of
h
, the function
h
is uniquely determined by the functional equation (i), the growth condition (ii), and its values on the real segment
[z_{0},z_{0}+r)
.

Remark 1.2
.

Condition (iii) ensures uniqueness: if two functions
h_{1}
and
h_{2}
satisfy the same additive functional equation (i) with moderate growth (ii) and agree on a real segment
[z_{0},z_{0}+r)
, then
h_{1}\equiv h_{2}
by Nörlund’s uniqueness theorem
[
12
, Chapter IV, Theorem 1]
. This prevents ambiguities from periodic functions of period
r
. In particular, if the growth hypothesis (ii) is dropped, one can generally add to a given solution a nontrivial
r
-periodic meromorphic function (or, more broadly, a periodic solution of the same shift-recurrence), so the normalization (iii) alone would no longer single out a unique AGF. For instance,
f(z)
(an order-2 AGF) is uniquely determined by its values
f(0)
and
f(1)
on the interval
[0,2)
, together with the AFE of Theorem
1.3
.

The functions
f(z)
and
g(z)
introduced above are order-2 AGFs, while Euler’s
\Gamma(z)
(satisfying
\Gamma(z+1)=z\Gamma(z)
) serves as the order-1 prototype.

1.4.
Main results

The heart of the paper is the following characterization, proved in Section
6

(Theorem
6.2
): a meromorphic function is an additive Gamma
function of order
r
if and only if it arises as a connection constant from a
sequence holonomic in
(n,z)
with integer-slope asymptotics:

u_{n}(z)\sim\Lambda(n,z)h(z)\quad\text{as }n\to\infty,

where
\Lambda(n,z)=\lambda^{n}n^{\rho(z)}\prod_{j}\Gamma(n+\alpha_{j}z+\beta_{j})^{m_{j}}

with constant
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}^{*}
, affine
\rho(z)
with integer slope, integer slopes
\alpha_{j}\in\mathbb{Z}
, and
m_{j}\in\mathbb{Z}
.

Uniqueness and the regular/irregular dichotomy.

These functional equations are uniquely determined by their values on a compact interval of length 2 together with the growth conditions.
By Nörlund’s theorem (Lemma
2.2
),
this uniqueness holds under our vertical-strip growth assumptions.
The explicit formulas reveal a structural dichotomy:
f(z)
involves the incomplete Gamma function (irregular case), while
g(z)
is expressed as Gamma ratios (regular case).

Motivating examples: the worlds of
\mathrm{e}
and
\pi

The first two theorems below instantiate the general mechanism in order
r=2
.
They are meant to be read as
illustrations
of the general theory: they exhibit the two “archetypal” AGFs that motivate the
definitions (incomplete Gamma vs. Gamma ratios) and foreshadow the regular/irregular dichotomy analyzed later in
Section
7
.

Theorem 1.3

(Connection constant in the world of
\mathrm{e}
)
.

For the parametrized sequence
u_{n}(m)
defined by (
1.2
), there exists a connection constant
f(m)
such that

u_{n}(m)\sim f(m)\cdot n\quad\text{as }n\to\infty.

The function
f(m)
extends to a meromorphic function
f(z)
on
\mathbb{C}
satisfying the additive functional equation

(1.4)

f(z+2)=(z+2)f(z)-(z+2)f(z+1).

The function
f(z)
admits an explicit formula in terms of the incomplete Gamma function
\gamma(a,x)
and the confluent hypergeometric function
{}_{1}F_{1}
(see (
3.8
) and (
3.9
) in Section
3
), exhibits polynomial growth in vertical strips, and extends meromorphically to
\mathbb{C}
with poles at
z\in\{-2,-3,-4,\ldots\}
.

The arithmetic structure (
1.3
) is realized with
a_{m}=(m+1)!
and

The proof is given in Section
3
.

Theorem 1.4

(Connection constant in the world of
\pi
)
.

For the parametrized sequence
v_{n}(m)
defined by (
1.2
), there exists a connection constant
g(m)
such that

v_{n}(m)\sim g(m)\cdot\sqrt{n}\quad\text{as }n\to\infty.

The function
g(m)
extends to a meromorphic function
g(z)
on
\mathbb{C}
satisfying the additive functional equation

(1.5)

g(z+2)=g(z)-\frac{g(z+1)}{z+1}.

The function
g(z)
admits an explicit formula as a combination of Gamma ratios (see (
4.8
) in Section
4
), exhibits polynomial growth in vertical strips, and extends meromorphically to
\mathbb{C}
with simple poles at the negative integers
z\in\{-1,-2,-3,\ldots\}

The arithmetic structure (

The proof is given in Section

2.
Technical preliminaries

The method employed in this paper to derive additive functional equations from discrete recurrences relies on Carlson’s theorem, which we state for reference:

Theorem 2.1

(Carlson)
.

Let
\varphi
be a holomorphic function on the half-plane
\Re z\geq 0
such that:

(i)

\varphi(n)=0
for all integers
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
,

(ii)

|\varphi(z)|\leq C\mathrm{e}^{\tau|\mathrm{Im}\,z|}
for some constants
C>0
and
\tau<\pi
,

Then
\varphi\equiv 0
throughout the half-plane
\Re z\geq 0
.
(See
[
18
, Theorem 6.20]
.)

This theorem provides a powerful tool for proving functional equations: if a function
H(z)
satisfies a discrete recurrence
H(m)=0
for all
m\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
and has controlled exponential growth, then
H\equiv 0
, establishing the desired functional equation.

Uniqueness for solutions of additive functional equations follows from Nörlund’s classical theorem:

Lemma 2.2

(Uniqueness under AFE)
.

Let
\phi_{1},\phi_{2}
be meromorphic on
\mathbb{C}
, of exponential type
<\pi
on every
vertical strip, satisfying the same additive functional equation

\mathcal{L}(T)\phi(z):=\sum_{j=0}^{r}a_{j}(z)\,\phi(z+j)\;=\;0

with polynomial (or rational) coefficients
a_{j}
, and coinciding on a real
interval of positive length. Then
\phi_{1}\equiv\phi_{2}
.

Proof.

By Nörlund’s uniqueness theorem for difference equations
(
Vorlesungen über Differenzenrechnung

[
12
]
, Ch. IV, Th. 1),
the exponential-type bound
<\pi
together with equality on a full real
interval forces
\phi_{1}\equiv\phi_{2}
.
∎

These tools will be applied in Sections
3
and
4
to establish functional equations, with the required growth bounds verified in Section
5
.

3.
Asymptotic analysis: the world of
\mathrm{e}

We now establish Theorem
1.3
through detailed asymptotic analysis, deriving the generating function, extracting the connection constant, and proving the additive functional equation.
The holonomic differential equation satisfied by
U_{m}
is recorded in Proposition
8.1
.

3.1.
Generating function and differential equation

Let
U_{m}(x)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}u_{n}(m)x^{n}
. Multiplying the recurrence
u_{n+2}(m)=u_{n+1}(m)+\frac{u_{n}(m)}{n+m}
by
x^{n}
and summing over
n\geq 1
yields

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}u_{n+2}(m)x^{n}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}u_{n+1}(m)x^{n}+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{u_{n}(m)}{n+m}x^{n}.

With the initial conditions
u_{1}(m)=0
and
u_{2}(m)=1
, the left side equals
\frac{U_{m}(x)-u_{1}x-u_{2}x^{2}}{x^{2}}=\frac{U_{m}(x)-x^{2}}{x^{2}}
, and the first term on the right equals
\frac{U_{m}(x)-u_{1}x}{x}=\frac{U_{m}(x)}{x}
. Setting
J_{m}(x)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{u_{n}(m)}{n+m}x^{n}
and using
\int_{0}^{x}t^{n+m-1}\,\mathrm{d}t=\frac{x^{n+m}}{n+m}
:

J_{m}(x)=x^{-m}\int_{0}^{x}U_{m}(t)t^{m-1}\,\mathrm{d}t.

Substituting into the equation
\frac{U_{m}(x)-x^{2}}{x^{2}}=\frac{U_{m}(x)}{x}+J_{m}(x)
and rearranging yields the integro-differential relation

(1-x)U_{m}(x)=x^{2}(1+J_{m}(x)).

Differentiating this equation with respect to
x
and eliminating
J_{m}(x)
leads to a first-order linear ODE:

(1-x)U^{\prime}_{m}(x)=U_{m}(x)\left(\frac{x^{2}-x+2-m+mx}{x}\right)+mx.

Via standard methods, this ODE has integrating factor
\mu(x)=x^{m-2}(1-x)^{2}\mathrm{e}^{x}
, yielding:

U_{m}(x)=\frac{x^{2-m}}{(1-x)^{2}\mathrm{e}^{x}}\left(\delta_{m,0}+\int_{0}^{x}mt^{m-1}(1-t)\mathrm{e}^{t}\,\mathrm{d}t\right).

Remark 3.1

(Order of the ODE)
.

Although the P-recurrence for
u_{n}(m)
has width (order)
r=2
, the resulting ODE for the ordinary generating function
U_{m}(x)
has order 1. When the recurrence is written in standard P-recursive form (by clearing denominators):

(n+m)u_{n+2}(m)-(n+m)u_{n+1}(m)-u_{n}(m)=0,

the polynomial coefficients have maximum degree
d=1
.

More generally, for a P-recursive sequence of width
r
with coefficient polynomials of maximum degree
d
, under suitable growth conditions ensuring the ordinary generating function (OGF) is D-finite (which holds for sequences with moderate algebraic growth, as is the case for AGFs with integer-slope asymptotics), the OGF satisfies a linear ODE whose order depends on both
r
and
d
. Based on elimination theory in Ore algebras
[
4
,
9
]
, the order is typically at most
(d+1)r
in the worst case, though often much lower in practice. For comparison, the exponential generating function (EGF) satisfies an ODE of order at most
r
(Stanley
[
16
]
, Ch. 6). This phenomenon is a fundamental feature of the holonomic triangle (see Principle
8.1
and Figure
1
).

3.2.
Singularity analysis and asymptotic behavior

As
x\to 1^{-}
:

U_{m}(x)\sim\frac{J_{m}}{\mathrm{e}(1-x)^{2}}\quad\text{where}\quad J_{m}=\delta_{m,0}+\int_{0}^{1}mt^{m-1}(1-t)\mathrm{e}^{t}\,\mathrm{d}t.

The point
x=1
is a pole of order 2 (dominant singularity). By the transfer theorem of Flajolet and Sedgewick
[
7
]
(Theorem VI.3, p.393), if a generating function has a singular expansion
G(x)\sim C(1-x)^{-\alpha}
near
x=1
with
\alpha=2
, then

[x^{n}]G(x)\sim\frac{C}{\Gamma(\alpha)}n^{\alpha-1}=C\cdot n.

Applying this with
C=\mathrm{e}^{-1}J_{m}
yields

u_{n}(m)\sim\mathrm{e}^{-1}J_{m}\cdot n.

This establishes the asymptotic behavior claimed in Theorem
1.3
with the connection constant

(3.1)

f(m)=\mathrm{e}^{-1}J_{m}.

3.3.
Discrete recurrence and arithmetic structure

We derive the recurrence satisfied by
J_{m}=f(m)\mathrm{e}
. From the definition above, we have

(3.2)

J_{m}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }m=0,\\
\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}mt^{m-1}(1-t)\mathrm{e}^{t}\,\mathrm{d}t&\text{if }m\geq 1.\end{cases}

For
m\geq 1
, we analyze the integral expression using a key identity.

Auxiliary integrals.
Define the auxiliary integrals
I_{m}=\int_{0}^{1}t^{m}\mathrm{e}^{t}\,\mathrm{d}t
for
m\geq 0
. We have
I_{0}=\mathrm{e}-1
. Integration by parts (
u=t^{m},\mathrm{d}v=\mathrm{e}^{t}\,\mathrm{d}t
) yields the well-known recurrence:

(3.3)

I_{m}=[t^{m}\mathrm{e}^{t}]_{0}^{1}-m\int_{0}^{1}t^{m-1}\mathrm{e}^{t}\,\mathrm{d}t=\mathrm{e}-mI_{m-1},\quad m\geq 1.

Relating
J_{m}
and
I_{m}
.
We can express
J_{m}
(for
m\geq 1
) directly in terms of
I_{m}
:

\displaystyle J_{m}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}mt^{m-1}(1-t)\mathrm{e}^{t}\,\mathrm{d}t=m\int_{0}^{1}(t^{m-1}\mathrm{e}^{t}-t^{m}\mathrm{e}^{t})\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=m(I_{m-1}-I_{m}).

We now establish a crucial identity. Using the recurrence (
3.3
) for
I_{m}
:

\displaystyle J_{m}

\displaystyle=m(I_{m-1}-(\mathrm{e}-mI_{m-1}))=m((m+1)I_{m-1}-\mathrm{e}).

Now consider
I_{m+1}
. Using the recurrence (
3.3
) repeatedly:

\displaystyle I_{m+1}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{e}-(m+1)I_{m}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{e}-(m+1)(\mathrm{e}-mI_{m-1})

\displaystyle=m(m+1)I_{m-1}-m\mathrm{e}.

Thus, we have proven the identity:

(3.4)

J_{m}=I_{m+1}\quad\text{for }m\geq 1.

We verify this for
m=0
:
J_{0}=1
(by definition), and
I_{1}=\mathrm{e}-1\cdot I_{0}=\mathrm{e}-(\mathrm{e}-1)=1
. The identity holds for all
m\geq 0
.

Deriving the recurrence for
J_{m}
.
Using the identity (
3.4
) and the recurrence (
3.3
):

\displaystyle J_{m+1}

\displaystyle=I_{m+2}=\mathrm{e}-(m+2)I_{m+1}=\mathrm{e}-(m+2)J_{m}.

This establishes the first-order recurrence:

(3.5)

J_{m+1}=\mathrm{e}-(m+2)J_{m}\quad\text{for }m\geq 0.

Elimination to second order.
To obtain a homogeneous recurrence of order 2, we eliminate the inhomogeneous term
\mathrm{e}
by taking successive instances of (
3.5
):

J_{m+2}=\mathrm{e}-(m+3)J_{m+1},\quad J_{m+1}=\mathrm{e}-(m+2)J_{m}.

Subtracting these equations:

J_{m+2}=(m+2)J_{m}-(m+3)J_{m+1}+J_{m+1}=(m+2)[J_{m}-J_{m+1}].

Since
f(m)=\mathrm{e}^{-1}J_{m}
, dividing by
\mathrm{e}
gives:

(3.6)

f(m+2)=(m+2)[f(m)-f(m+1)].

Arithmetic structure.
We now derive the
\mathbb{Z}
-linear form in
\mathrm{e}
by introducing the sign-normalized quantity:

K_{m}=(-1)^{m}J_{m}.

From the first-order recurrence
J_{m+1}=\mathrm{e}-(m+2)J_{m}
, multiplying by
(-1)^{m+1}
:

(-1)^{m+1}J_{m+1}=(-1)^{m+1}\mathrm{e}-(-1)^{m+1}(m+2)J_{m}=-(-1)^{m}\mathrm{e}+(m+2)(-1)^{m}J_{m}.

In terms of
K_{m}
, this becomes:

K_{m+1}=(m+2)K_{m}-(-1)^{m}\mathrm{e}.

We seek a decomposition
K_{m}=a_{m}-\mathrm{e}b_{m}
with
a_{m},b_{m}\in\mathbb{Z}
and
a_{m},b_{m}\geq 0
. Substituting into the recurrence:

a_{m+1}-\mathrm{e}b_{m+1}=(m+2)(a_{m}-\mathrm{e}b_{m})-(-1)^{m}\mathrm{e}.

Expanding and separating terms by the
\mathbb{Q}
-linear independence of
\{1,\mathrm{e}\}
(using the transcendence of
\mathrm{e}
):

\begin{cases}a_{m+1}=(m+2)a_{m},\\
b_{m+1}=(m+2)b_{m}+(-1)^{m}.\end{cases}

With initial conditions
K_{0}=1
(giving
a_{0}=1,b_{0}=0
) and
K_{1}=2-\mathrm{e}
(giving
a_{1}=2,b_{1}=1
), we find:

a_{m}=(m+1)!,\quad b_{m}=D_{m+1},

where
D_{m}
are the derangement numbers. This confirms the
\mathbb{Z}
-linear form in (
1.3
).

3.4.
Analytic continuation and functional equation

We define the analytic continuation via the auxiliary function
I(z)=\int_{0}^{1}t^{z}\mathrm{e}^{t}\,\mathrm{d}t
, which converges and is holomorphic for
\Re z>-1
. By the identity principle for holomorphic functions, the relation
J_{m}=I_{m+1}
established in (
3.4
) extends to the complex domain:
J(z)=I(z+1)
for
\Re z>-1
. The connection constant is
f(z)=\mathrm{e}^{-1}J(z)
. This provides an analytic continuation of
f(z)
to the half-plane
\Re z>-2
.

Lemma 3.2

(Growth bounds for
f
)
.

The function
f(z)
satisfies
|f(z)|=O(1/|z|)
for
\Re z\geq 0
, ensuring the moderate growth required for Carlson’s theorem. The proof, based on the explicit formula (
3.8
) established below, is given in Section
5
.

To derive the functional equation, we apply Carlson’s theorem (Theorem
2.1
). From the discrete recurrence (
3.6
), define

h(z)=f(z+2)-(z+2)[f(z)-f(z+1)].

By (
3.6
), we have
h(m)=0
for all
m\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
. By Lemma
3.2
,
f(z)
is holomorphic for
\Re z\geq 0
and satisfies
|f(z)|=O(1/|z|)
. From this asymptotic expansion
f(z)=1/(z+2)+O(1/z^{2})
as
|z|\to\infty
, we obtain
f(z)-f(z+1)=O(1/|z|^{2})
. Therefore
(z+2)[f(z)-f(z+1)]=O(1/|z|)
, so that
h(z)=O(1/|z|)
is bounded for
\Re z\geq 0
.

Since
h(z)
is bounded, it has exponential type
0<\pi
, so Carlson’s theorem applies directly. Together with
h(n)=0
for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
, we conclude
h(z)\equiv 0
and thus:

(3.7)

f(z+2)=(z+2)f(z)-(z+2)f(z+1).

3.5.
Explicit formula

The incomplete Gamma function
\gamma(a,x)=\int_{0}^{x}t^{a-1}\mathrm{e}^{-t}\,\mathrm{d}t
(see
[
5
, Section 8.2]
,
[
17
, Section 8]
, or classically
[
1
, Section 6.5]
) is analytic in
x
on the complex plane cut along
(-\infty,0]
. Since
x=-1
lies on this branch cut, we employ the standard analytic continuation and use the principal branch throughout. We now derive an explicit formula for
f(z)
in terms of
\gamma
.

Recall that
f(z)=\mathrm{e}^{-1}J(z)=\mathrm{e}^{-1}I(z+1)
where
I(z)=\int_{0}^{1}t^{z}\mathrm{e}^{t}\,\mathrm{d}t
. The substitution
t=-u
in the integral defining
\gamma(z+2,-1)
gives:

\displaystyle\gamma(z+2,-1)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{-1}t^{z+1}\mathrm{e}^{-t}\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}(-u)^{z+1}\mathrm{e}^{u}\,(-\,\mathrm{d}u)

\displaystyle=-\mathrm{e}^{i\pi(z+1)}I(z+1),

where we use the principal branch
(-1)^{z+1}=\mathrm{e}^{i\pi(z+1)}
. Since
f(z)=\mathrm{e}^{-1}I(z+1)
and
\mathrm{e}^{i\pi}=-1
, we obtain:

I(z+1)=-\mathrm{e}^{-i\pi(z+1)}\gamma(z+2,-1)=-\mathrm{e}^{-i\pi z}\cdot\mathrm{e}^{-i\pi}\gamma(z+2,-1)=-\mathrm{e}^{-i\pi z}\cdot(-1)\gamma(z+2,-1)=\mathrm{e}^{-i\pi z}\gamma(z+2,-1).

Therefore:

(3.8)

f(z)=\mathrm{e}^{-1-i\pi z}\gamma(z+2,-1).

Alternatively, using the relation between incomplete Gamma and confluent hypergeometric functions
[
5
, Eq. 8.4.15]
:

(3.9)

f(z)=\frac{1}{z+1}\,{}_{1}F_{1}(2;z+2;-1).

The confluent hypergeometric function
{}_{1}F_{1}(a;b;x)
satisfies the identity (Kummer’s transformation):

{}_{1}F_{1}(a;b;x)=\mathrm{e}^{x}{}_{1}F_{1}(b-a;b;-x),

which can be verified to reproduce the functional equation (
3.7
).

For
z=0
:
\gamma(2,-1)=\int_{0}^{-1}u\mathrm{e}^{-u}\,\mathrm{d}u=1
. Then
\mathrm{e}^{-1-i\pi\cdot 0}\cdot(1)=\mathrm{e}^{-1}
, hence
f(0)=\mathrm{e}^{-1}
as expected.

This completes the
\mathrm{e}
-world analysis and establishes Theorem
1.3
.

4.
Asymptotic analysis: the world of
\pi

We now establish Theorem
1.4
, following the same analytical program as for the
\mathrm{e}
-world. The argument parallels Section
3
with adaptations for the
\sqrt{n}
asymptotics and
\mathbb{Q}
-linear forms characteristic of the
\pi
-world. The holonomic differential equation satisfied by
V_{m}
is recorded in Proposition
8.2
.

4.1.
Generating function and differential equation

For
(v_{n}(m))
, let
V_{m}(x)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}v_{n}(m)x^{n}
. Following analysis similar to Section
3
, the first-order ODE is:

(1-x^{2})V_{m}^{\prime}(x)+V_{m}(x)\left(\frac{m-2}{x}-mx-1\right)=mx.

Solving via integrating factor
\mu(x)=x^{m-2}(1-x)^{3/2}(1+x)^{1/2}
yields:

V_{m}(x)=\frac{x^{2-m}}{(1-x)^{3/2}(1+x)^{1/2}}\left(\delta_{m,0}+\int_{0}^{x}mt^{m-1}\sqrt{\frac{1-t}{1+t}}\,\mathrm{d}t\right).

4.2.
Singularity analysis and asymptotic behavior

As
x\to 1^{-}
:

V_{m}(x)\sim\frac{L_{m}}{\sqrt{2}(1-x)^{3/2}}\quad\text{where}\quad L_{m}=\delta_{m,0}+\int_{0}^{1}mt^{m-1}\sqrt{\frac{1-t}{1+t}}\,\mathrm{d}t.

The point
x=1
is the dominant singularity with exponent
\alpha=\frac{3}{2}
. By the transfer theorem of Flajolet and Sedgewick
[
7
]
(Theorem VI.1, p.388) with
\Gamma\left(\frac{3}{2}\right)=\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2}
:

v_{n}(m)\sim\frac{L_{m}/\sqrt{2}}{\Gamma(3/2)}n^{3/2-1}=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}L_{m}\sqrt{n}.

This establishes the asymptotic behavior claimed in Theorem
1.4
with the connection constant

(4.1)

g(m)=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}L_{m}.

4.3.
Discrete recurrence: derivation

We derive the recurrence satisfied by
L_{m}=g(m)\sqrt{\pi/2}
. From the definition above, we have

(4.2)

L_{m}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }m=0,\\
\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}mt^{m-1}\sqrt{\frac{1-t}{1+t}}\,\mathrm{d}t&\text{if }m\geq 1.\end{cases}

For
m\geq 1
, we analyze the integral expression.

Define
w(t)=\sqrt{\frac{1-t}{1+t}}
. We compute the derivative of
w(t)
by taking the logarithmic derivative:

\frac{w^{\prime}(t)}{w(t)}=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}\left(\frac{1}{2}\ln(1-t)-\frac{1}{2}\ln(1+t)\right)=-\frac{1}{2(1-t)}-\frac{1}{2(1+t)}=-\frac{1}{1-t^{2}}.

Thus,
w^{\prime}(t)=-\frac{w(t)}{1-t^{2}}
.

For
m\geq 1
, we apply integration by parts to
L_{m}=\int_{0}^{1}mt^{m-1}w(t)\,\mathrm{d}t
. Setting
u^{\prime}=mt^{m-1}
(so
u=t^{m}
) and
v=w(t)
, we obtain:

\displaystyle L_{m}

\displaystyle=[t^{m}w(t)]_{0}^{1}-\int_{0}^{1}t^{m}w^{\prime}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t.

The boundary term is
[1\cdot 0-0\cdot 1]=0
, hence:

\displaystyle L_{m}

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{1}t^{m}\left(-\frac{w(t)}{1-t^{2}}\right)\,\mathrm{d}t=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{t^{m}w(t)}{1-t^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}t.

This identity is valid for
m\geq 1
. We now relate
L_{m}
and
L_{m+2}
using this identity:

\displaystyle L_{m}-L_{m+2}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{t^{m}w(t)}{1-t^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}t-\int_{0}^{1}\frac{t^{m+2}w(t)}{1-t^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{w(t)}{1-t^{2}}(t^{m}-t^{m+2})\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}t^{m}w(t)\,\mathrm{d}t.

By definition (
4.2
), the right-hand side equals
L_{m+1}/(m+1)
:

L_{m+1}=\int_{0}^{1}(m+1)t^{m}w(t)\,\mathrm{d}t.

Combining these results yields:

L_{m}-L_{m+2}=\frac{L_{m+1}}{m+1}.

Rearranging gives the homogeneous second-order recurrence:

(4.3)

L_{m+2}=L_{m}-\frac{L_{m+1}}{m+1}.

This derivation is valid for
m\geq 1
. We verify it holds for
m=0
:
L_{2}=L_{0}-L_{1}/1
. With
L_{0}=1
,
L_{1}=\int_{0}^{1}w(t)dt=\frac{\pi}{2}-1
, we get
L_{2}=1-(\frac{\pi}{2}-1)=2-\frac{\pi}{2}
. Direct calculation confirms this value.

For
g(m)=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}L_{m}
:

(4.4)

g(m+2)=g(m)-\frac{g(m+1)}{m+1}.

4.4.
Arithmetic structure: the
\mathbb{Q}
-linear forms

We now establish the explicit
\mathbb{Q}
-linear decomposition
K_{m}=p_{m}-\pi q_{m}
with
p_{m},q_{m}\in\mathbb{Q}_{\geq 0}
.

Define the sign-normalized quantity:

K_{m}=(-1)^{m}L_{m}.

From the recurrence
L_{m+2}=L_{m}-\frac{L_{m+1}}{m+1}
, multiplying by
(-1)^{m+2}
:

(-1)^{m+2}L_{m+2}=(-1)^{m+2}L_{m}-(-1)^{m+2}\frac{L_{m+1}}{m+1}.

This gives:

K_{m+2}=(-1)^{m+2}L_{m+2}=(-1)^{m}L_{m}+(-1)^{m+1}\frac{L_{m+1}}{m+1}=K_{m}+\frac{K_{m+1}}{m+1}.

Writing
K_{m}=p_{m}-\pi q_{m}
with
p_{m},q_{m}\in\mathbb{Q}_{\geq 0}
, the recurrence separates into:

(4.5)

\displaystyle p_{m+2}

\displaystyle=p_{m}+\frac{p_{m+1}}{m+1},

(4.6)

\displaystyle q_{m+2}

\displaystyle=q_{m}+\frac{q_{m+1}}{m+1}.

Initial conditions.
From
K_{0}=L_{0}=1
:
p_{0}=1,q_{0}=0
. From
L_{1}=\frac{\pi}{2}-1
, we have
K_{1}=-L_{1}=1-\frac{\pi}{2}
:
p_{1}=1,q_{1}=\frac{1}{2}
.

Alternation patterns.
The recurrences (
4.5
)–(
4.6
) with positive signs exhibit alternation patterns:

Proposition 4.1

(Alternation and closed formulas)
.

We establish explicit formulas for the rational coefficients
p_{m}
and
q_{m}
appearing in the
\mathbb{Q}
-linear decomposition
K_{m}=p_{m}-\pi q_{m}
. These coefficients exhibit alternation patterns and admit closed formulas in terms of double factorials.

For all
k\geq 1
:

(i)

p_{2k+1}=p_{2k}
(odd
p
-values freeze)

(ii)

q_{2k}=q_{2k-1}
(even
q
-values freeze)

(iii)

For even indices:

p_{2k}=\frac{(2k)\mskip-3.0mu\mathop{!!}}{(2k-1)\mskip-3.0mu\mathop{!!}},\qquad q_{2k}=\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{(2k-1)\mskip-3.0mu\mathop{!!}}{(2k-2)\mskip-3.0mu\mathop{!!}}

(iv)

For odd indices:

p_{2k+1}=\frac{(2k)\mskip-3.0mu\mathop{!!}}{(2k-1)\mskip-3.0mu\mathop{!!}},\qquad q_{2k+1}=\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{(2k+1)\mskip-3.0mu\mathop{!!}}{(2k)\mskip-3.0mu\mathop{!!}}

Proof.

We establish the alternation patterns by induction, then derive the closed formulas via telescoping products. The key observation is that the positive sign in (
4.5
)–(
4.6
) creates a telescoping structure.

We first prove that
p_{2k+1}=p_{2k}
for all
k\geq 1
.

Base case: Direct computation gives
p_{0}=1
,
p_{1}=1
,
p_{2}=1+\frac{1}{1}=2
,
p_{3}=1+\frac{2}{2}=2
. Thus
p_{3}=p_{2}=2
.

Inductive step: Assume
p_{2k+1}=p_{2k}
. Then:

p_{2k+2}=p_{2k}+\frac{p_{2k+1}}{2k+1}=p_{2k}\left(1+\frac{1}{2k+1}\right)=p_{2k}\cdot\frac{2k+2}{2k+1}.

And:

p_{2k+3}=p_{2k+1}+\frac{p_{2k+2}}{2k+2}=p_{2k}+\frac{1}{2k+2}\cdot p_{2k}\cdot\frac{2k+2}{2k+1}=p_{2k}\left(1+\frac{1}{2k+1}\right)=p_{2k+2}.

Thus
p_{2k+3}=p_{2k+2}
, confirming the pattern.

Similarly,
q_{2k}=q_{2k-1}
follows by identical induction, starting from
q_{0}=0
,
q_{1}=\frac{1}{2}
,
q_{2}=0+\frac{1/2}{1}=\frac{1}{2}
. So
q_{2}=q_{1}=\frac{1}{2}
.

We now derive closed formulas via telescoping products.
From the relation
p_{2k+2}=p_{2k}\cdot\frac{2k+2}{2k+1}
derived above, we obtain a telescoping product for
k\geq 1
. Using
p_{2}=2
:

p_{2k}=p_{2}\prod_{j=1}^{k-1}\frac{2j+2}{2j+1}=2\cdot\frac{4\cdot 6\cdot 8\cdots(2k)}{3\cdot 5\cdot 7\cdots(2k-1)}.

We express the terms using double factorials. The denominator is
(2k-1)\mskip-3.0mu\mathop{!!}
. The numerator is the product of even numbers from 4 to
2k
. Since
(2k)\mskip-3.0mu\mathop{!!}=2\cdot 4\cdot 6\cdots(2k)
, the numerator is
(2k)\mskip-3.0mu\mathop{!!}/2
. Thus:

p_{2k}=2\cdot\frac{(2k)\mskip-3.0mu\mathop{!!}/2}{(2k-1)\mskip-3.0mu\mathop{!!}}=\frac{(2k)\mskip-3.0mu\mathop{!!}}{(2k-1)\mskip-3.0mu\mathop{!!}}.

This establishes the formula for
p_{2k}
in (iii). By the freezing property (i),
p_{2k+1}=p_{2k}
, establishing the formula in (iv).

For the
q
coefficients, we use the relation
q_{2k+1}=q_{2k-1}\cdot\frac{2k+1}{2k}
(derived similarly to
p
) with
q_{1}=\frac{1}{2}
. The telescoping product is:

q_{2k+1}=q_{1}\prod_{j=1}^{k}\frac{2j+1}{2j}=\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{3\cdot 5\cdot 7\cdots(2k+1)}{2\cdot 4\cdot 6\cdots(2k)}.

The numerator is
(2k+1)\mskip-3.0mu\mathop{!!}
and the denominator is
(2k)\mskip-3.0mu\mathop{!!}
. Thus:

q_{2k+1}=\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{(2k+1)\mskip-3.0mu\mathop{!!}}{(2k)\mskip-3.0mu\mathop{!!}}.

This establishes the formula for
q_{2k+1}
in (iv). By the freezing property (ii),
q_{2k}=q_{2k-1}
(for
k\geq 1
). Applying the formula for odd indices to
q_{2k-1}
yields the formula for
q_{2k}
in (iii).
∎

4.5.
Analytic continuation and functional equation

For
\Re z>0
, extend
L_{m}
(for
m\geq 1
) to

L(z)=\int_{0}^{1}zt^{z-1}\sqrt{\frac{1-t}{1+t}}\,\mathrm{d}t.

The connection constant is
g(z)=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}L(z)
, which is holomorphic for
\Re z>0
(taking the principal branch of
t^{z-1}
).

Lemma 4.2

(Growth bounds for
g
)
.

The function
g(z)
satisfies
|g(z)|=O(|z|^{-1/2})
in vertical strips, ensuring the moderate growth required for Carlson’s theorem. The proof, based on the explicit formula (
4.8
) established below, is given in Section
5
.

From the discrete recurrence (
4.4
), define the function

h(z)=g(z+2)-g(z)+\frac{g(z+1)}{z+1}.

By (
4.4
), we have
h(m)=0
for all
m\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
. By Lemma
4.2
,
g(z)
is holomorphic for
\Re z\geq\epsilon>0
and exhibits polynomial growth in vertical strips, hence
h(z)
also has polynomial growth. This polynomial growth is of exponential type
<\pi
. Together with
h(n)=0
for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
, Carlson’s theorem (Theorem
2.1
) applies, yielding
h(z)\equiv 0
and thus:

(4.7)

g(z+2)=g(z)-\frac{g(z+1)}{z+1}.

4.6.
Explicit formula and verification

We establish an explicit formula for
g(z)
in terms of the Gamma function and prove its validity by verifying the functional equation.

Proposition 4.3
.

The connection constant
g(z)
has simple poles at the negative integers
z\in\{-1,-2,-3,\ldots\}
and is holomorphic at
z=0
. It is given by:

(4.8)

g(z)=\sqrt{2}\left[\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{z}{2}+1\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{z+1}{2}\right)}-\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{z+1}{2}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{z}{2}\right)}\right].

Proof.

We prove this identity by showing that the proposed expression satisfies the functional equation (
4.7
) and the initial conditions. Since both the integral definition and the proposed formula satisfy the same order-2 AFE with exponential type
<\pi
, agreement at
z=0
and
z=1
(verified below) propagates to all of
\mathbb{Z}
via the recurrence. By Carlson’s theorem, the difference of two functions with exponential type
<\pi
that vanishes on
\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
must vanish identically on
\mathbb{C}
.

We first verify that the proposed formula (
4.8
) has exponential type
<\pi
in vertical strips, as required for uniqueness. The uniform version of Stirling’s formula shows that for Gamma ratios of the form
\Gamma(z/2+\alpha)/\Gamma(z/2+\beta)
, the dominant exponential factors
\exp(-\pi|\Im z|/4)
cancel exactly, leaving polynomial growth (specifically, decay
O(|z|^{-1/2})
) in
|z|
. Since polynomial growth implies exponential type
0<\pi
, the growth condition required for uniqueness is satisfied. (A detailed derivation is given in Section
5
.)

Define the auxiliary function:

A(z)=\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{z}{2}+1\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{z+1}{2}\right)}.

We have
A(z-1)=\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{z-1}{2}+1\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{z-1+1}{2}\right)}=\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{z+1}{2}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{z}{2}\right)}
.
Thus, the proposed formula is
g(z)=\sqrt{2}(A(z)-A(z-1))
.

We derive relations between shifted values of
A(z)
using
\Gamma(x+1)=x\Gamma(x)
:

\displaystyle A(z)A(z-1)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{z}{2}+1\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{z+1}{2}\right)}\cdot\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{z+1}{2}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{z}{2}\right)}=\frac{\frac{z}{2}\Gamma\left(\frac{z}{2}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{z}{2}\right)}=\frac{z}{2}.

This implies
A(z+1)A(z)=\frac{z+1}{2}
, so
A(z+1)=\frac{z+1}{2A(z)}
.
Also,
A(z+2)=\frac{z+2}{2A(z+1)}=\frac{z+2}{2}\frac{2A(z)}{z+1}=\frac{z+2}{z+1}A(z)
.

We now verify the functional equation
g(z+2)=g(z)-\frac{g(z+1)}{z+1}
. Dividing by
\sqrt{2}
, this becomes:

A(z+2)-A(z+1)=A(z)-A(z-1)-\frac{A(z+1)-A(z)}{z+1}.

Rearranging, we need to verify:

A(z+2)+A(z-1)=A(z+1)\frac{z}{z+1}+A(z)\frac{z+2}{z+1}.

We compute both sides using the relations derived above. Left-hand side:

A(z+2)+A(z-1)=\frac{z+2}{z+1}A(z)+\frac{z}{2A(z)}.

Right-hand side:

A(z+1)\frac{z}{z+1}+A(z)\frac{z+2}{z+1}=\frac{z+1}{2A(z)}\frac{z}{z+1}+\frac{z+2}{z+1}A(z)=\frac{z}{2A(z)}+\frac{z+2}{z+1}A(z).

Since the left-hand side equals the right-hand side, the functional equation is satisfied. Finally, we verify the initial values match those derived from the integral
L_{m}
. We have
L_{1}=\pi/2-1
, hence

g(1)=\sqrt{2/\pi}(\pi/2-1)=\frac{\pi-2}{\sqrt{2\pi}}.

We check the formula (
4.8
) at
z=1
.

A(1)=\frac{\Gamma(3/2)}{\Gamma(1)}=\sqrt{\pi}/2

and

A(0)=\frac{\Gamma(1)}{\Gamma(1/2)}=1/\sqrt{\pi}.

Then

\sqrt{2}(A(1)-A(0))=\sqrt{2}(\sqrt{\pi}/2-1/\sqrt{\pi})=\sqrt{2}\frac{\pi-2}{2\sqrt{\pi}}=\frac{\pi-2}{\sqrt{2\pi}}=g(1).

This matches. We also verify a second initial value to ensure complete uniqueness. For
z=0
:
L_{0}=1
, hence

g(0)=\sqrt{2/\pi}\cdot 1=\sqrt{2/\pi}.

Using the explicit formula (
4.8
), we have

g(0)=\sqrt{2}(A(0)-A(-1)).

We compute

A(0)=\frac{\Gamma(1)}{\Gamma(1/2)}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}

and

A(-1)=\lim_{z\to-1}A(z)=\lim_{z\to-1}\frac{\Gamma(z/2+1)}{\Gamma((z+1)/2)}=0.

Thus

g(0)=\sqrt{2}(1/\sqrt{\pi}-0)=\sqrt{2/\pi},

confirming the expected value. Since the expression (
4.8
) satisfies the same linear functional equation as the connection constant
g(z)
, matches the initial conditions, and exhibits the required growth properties, the identity is proven by uniqueness.
∎

This formula expresses
g(z)
as a combination of ratios of ordinary Gamma functions, confirming its regular nature and completing the proof of Theorem
1.4
.

5.
The AGF prototypes:
\Gamma
,
f
, and
g

Having established the explicit formulas for
f(z)
and
g(z)
in Sections
3
and
4
, we now reveal the unifying structure: these functions, together with Euler’s Gamma function, are instances of a single class characterized by three properties. We verify these properties systematically, completing the proofs of the growth bounds invoked earlier.

5.1.
The three defining properties

Recall from Definition
1.1
that an additive Gamma function satisfies:

(i)

An additive functional equation with rational coefficients;

(ii)

Moderate growth in vertical strips (polynomial, hence exponential type
<\pi
);

(iii)

Uniqueness via normalization on an interval of length equal to the order.

We now verify these properties for
\Gamma(z)
(order 1),
f(z)
(order 2), and
g(z)
(order 2).

5.2.
Euler’s Gamma function: the order-1 prototype

Euler’s product formula (1729), refined by Gauss, can be written:

\Gamma(z)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{n!n^{z}}{z(z+1)\cdots(z+n)}.

This classical formula is typically presented as a limit defining
\Gamma(z)
. We now show its deeper structure:
it is the connection constant of a first-order P-recursive sequence
.

Consider the sequence

(5.1)

w_{n+1}(z)=\frac{n+1}{n+z}w_{n}(z),\quad w_{1}(z)=1.

The solution is
w_{n}(z)=\frac{n!}{(z)_{n}}
where
(z)_{n}=z(z+1)\cdots(z+n-1)
is the Pochhammer symbol. Using Stirling’s approximation,
w_{n}(z)\sim\Gamma(z)n^{1-z}
, identifying
\Gamma(z)
as the connection constant.

Verification of AGF properties for
\Gamma(z)
:

(i)

AFE:

\Gamma(z+1)=z\Gamma(z)
, with rational coefficient
R_{0}(z)=z
,
R_{1}(z)=-1
.

(ii)

Growth:
By Stirling’s formula,
|\Gamma(z)|\sim\sqrt{2\pi}|z|^{\Re z-1/2}e^{-\pi|\Im z|/2}
in vertical strips. The exponential factor has exponent
\pi/2<\pi
.

(iii)

Uniqueness:

\Gamma(z)
is determined by the AFE together with
\Gamma(1)=1
.

The holonomic triangle for
\Gamma(z)
is illustrated in Figure
2
.

5.3.
Growth bounds for

6.
The structure of additive Gamma functions

6.1.
From examples to general theory

The functions
\Gamma(z)
,
f(z)
, and
g(z)
share three properties: connection constant origin, additive functional equations with rational coefficients, and controlled growth. This motivates a general definition.

We call these functions
additive Gamma functions
because, like Euler’s Gamma function (the order-1 prototype), they are uniquely determined by a linear functional equation together with a growth condition, and emerge from asymptotic analysis of holonomic sequences.

The key mechanism ensuring rational coefficients is the integer slope condition. Consider a Gamma factor
\Gamma(n+\alpha z+\beta)
in
u_{n}(z)\sim\Lambda(n,z)h(z)
. Under
z\mapsto z+1
:

\frac{\Gamma(n+\alpha(z+1)+\beta)}{\Gamma(n+\alpha z+\beta)}=\frac{\Gamma(n+\alpha z+\alpha+\beta)}{\Gamma(n+\alpha z+\beta)}.

When
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}
with
\alpha>0
, this ratio equals
(n+\alpha z+\beta)_{\alpha}
, polynomial in
z
. When
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}
with
\alpha<0
, it equals
\frac{1}{(n+\alpha z+\beta-\alpha)_{|\alpha|}}
, rational in
z
. However, if
\alpha\notin\mathbb{Z}
, the poles form two disjoint infinite arithmetic progressions, preventing rationality.

6.2.
The Integer Slope Condition

We now state and prove the central technical tool:

Lemma 6.1

(Integer Slope Condition)
.

Let
\phi(z)=\alpha z+\beta
be a linear function with
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{C}
and
\alpha\neq 0
. The ratio

R(z):=\frac{\Gamma(\phi(z+1))}{\Gamma(\phi(z))}=\frac{\Gamma(\alpha z+\alpha+\beta)}{\Gamma(\alpha z+\beta)}

is a rational function of
z
if and only if
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}
.

Proof.

We treat sufficiency and necessity separately.

Sufficiency (
\Leftarrow
).
Assume
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}
. We distinguish two cases.

If
\alpha=k>0
, using the functional equation
\Gamma(w+1)=w\Gamma(w)
iteratively
k
times yields
\Gamma(w+k)=(w)_{k}\Gamma(w)
, where
(w)_{k}=w(w+1)\cdots(w+k-1)
is the Pochhammer symbol. Setting
w=\alpha z+\beta
, we obtain

R(z)=\frac{\Gamma(w+k)}{\Gamma(w)}=(w)_{k}=\prod_{j=0}^{k-1}(\alpha z+\beta+j),

a polynomial in
z
of degree
k
.

If
\alpha=-k<0
with
k>0
, let
w=\alpha z+\beta
. The numerator is
\Gamma(w-k)
. Writing
\Gamma(w)=\Gamma((w-k)+k)
and applying the product formula, we get
\Gamma(w)=(w-k)_{k}\Gamma(w-k)
. Thus

R(z)=\frac{\Gamma(w-k)}{\Gamma(w)}=\frac{1}{(w-k)_{k}}=\frac{1}{\prod_{j=0}^{k-1}(\alpha z+\beta-k+j)},

the reciprocal of a polynomial, hence rational.

Necessity (
\Rightarrow
).
Assume
R(z)\in\mathbb{Q}(z)
. We prove
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}
by contradiction.

A rational function has finitely many poles and zeros. The function
\Gamma
has no zeros and simple poles at
\{0,-1,-2,\ldots\}
.

The poles of the denominator
\Gamma(\alpha z+\beta)
occur at
\alpha z+\beta=-k
for
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
, giving

P_{\mathrm{denom}}=\left\{z_{k}=\frac{-\beta-k}{\alpha}:k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\right\}.

The poles of the numerator
\Gamma(\alpha z+\alpha+\beta)
occur at

P_{\mathrm{numer}}=\left\{z^{\prime}_{k}=\frac{-\beta-\alpha-k}{\alpha}=z_{k}-1:k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\right\}.

Since
\Gamma
has no zeros, the numerator is finite and nonzero at every point of
P_{\mathrm{denom}}
that does not belong to
P_{\mathrm{numer}}
. At such points,
R(z)
has a zero (from the pole of the denominator).

If
\alpha\notin\mathbb{Z}
, the sets
P_{\mathrm{denom}}
and
P_{\mathrm{numer}}
are disjoint: equality
z_{j}=z^{\prime}_{k}
would imply
\alpha=k-j\in\mathbb{Z}
, contradicting our assumption. Consequently,
R(z)
vanishes at every point of the infinite set
P_{\mathrm{denom}}
.

The only rational function with infinitely many zeros is identically zero. But
R(z)\not\equiv 0
since
\Gamma
is nowhere zero. This contradiction establishes
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}
.
∎

6.3.
Characterization of additive Gamma functions

As motivated in the introduction (Definition
1.1
), the properties common to
\Gamma(z)
,
f(z)
, and
g(z)
lead to the formal definition of an additive Gamma function: a meromorphic function satisfying a homogeneous additive functional equation with rational coefficients and exhibiting polynomial growth in vertical strips. This definition captures the essential structure shared by all connection constants arising from holonomic sequences with integer-slope asymptotics.

Theorem 6.2

(Characterization of AGFs)
.

A meromorphic function
h
is an AGF of order
r
if and only if
h
arises as a connection constant from a sequence
u_{n}(z)
that is holonomic in
(n,z)
with integer-slope asymptotics:

u_{n}(z)\sim\Lambda(n,z)h(z)\quad\text{as }n\to\infty,

where

\Lambda(n,z)=\lambda^{n}n^{\rho(z)}\prod_{j}\Gamma(n+\alpha_{j}z+\beta_{j})^{m_{j}}

with constant
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}^{*}
, affine
\rho(z)
with integer slope, integer slopes
\alpha_{j}\in\mathbb{Z}
, and
m_{j}\in\mathbb{Z}
.

Proof.

Direction (
\Leftarrow
).
Suppose
h(z)
is the connection constant of a holonomic sequence
u_{n}(z)
with integer-slope asymptotics
u_{n}(z)\sim\Lambda(n,z)h(z)
. By Proposition
6.3
, the holonomicity guarantees the existence of a contiguity relation in
z
, from which
h(z)
inherits an additive functional equation with rational coefficients. The moderate growth condition follows from the structure of
\Lambda(n,z)
: Gamma factors with integer slopes have ratios (under parameter shifts in
z
) that exhibit polynomial growth via Stirling estimates, as demonstrated for
f(z)
and
g(z)
in Section
5
. Hence
h
is an AGF.

Direction (
\Rightarrow
).
Suppose
h
is an AGF of order
r
. We construct a sequence
u_{n}(z)
holonomic in
(n,z)
having
h(z)
as its connection constant.

A direct definition
u_{n}(z):=n^{z}h(z)
is not valid because
n^{z}
is not P-recursive in
n
over
\mathbb{Q}(n,z)
: the ratio
(n+1)^{z}/n^{z}=(1+1/n)^{z}
is not rational in
n
.

Instead, we construct a holonomic sequence asymptotically equivalent to
n^{z}
. The sequence

w_{n}(z):=\frac{n!}{\Gamma(n+1-z)}

satisfies recurrences in both variables:

w_{n+1}(z)=\frac{n+1}{n+1-z}w_{n}(z),\qquad w_{n}(z+1)=(n-z)w_{n}(z),

with coefficients in
\mathbb{Q}(n,z)
, so
w_{n}(z)
is holonomic in
(n,z)
. By Stirling’s formula,
w_{n}(z)\sim n^{z}
as
n\to\infty
.

More generally, the class of holonomic sequences is closed under multiplication
[
16
]
. The components of a general shell
\Lambda(n,z)
are:

(1)

The geometric term
\lambda^{n}
satisfies
a_{n+1}=\lambda\cdot a_{n}
(holonomic, trivial in
z
).

(2)

The power term
n^{\rho(z)}
with
\rho(z)=c_{1}z+c_{0}
is asymptotically equivalent to
w_{n}(z)^{c_{1}}\cdot n^{c_{0}}
, a product of holonomic sequences.

(3)

Each Gamma factor
\Gamma(n+\alpha_{j}z+\beta_{j})^{m_{j}}
with
\alpha_{j}\in\mathbb{Z}
is holonomic in
(n,z)
via the functional equations of
\Gamma
.

Let
\widetilde{\Lambda}(n,z)
be the product of these holonomic components. Then
\widetilde{\Lambda}(n,z)
is holonomic in
(n,z)
and
\widetilde{\Lambda}(n,z)\sim\Lambda(n,z)
as
n\to\infty
.

Define
u_{n}(z):=\widetilde{\Lambda}(n,z)\cdot h(z)
. Since
h(z)
is independent of
n
, the sequence
u_{n}(z)
inherits holonomicity from
\widetilde{\Lambda}(n,z)
and has the required asymptotics
u_{n}(z)\sim h(z)\Lambda(n,z)
.
∎

Proposition 6.3

(From Holonomic Sequence to AFE)
.

Let
u_{n}(z)
be holonomic in
(n,z)
, i.e., satisfy compatible linear recurrences in both
n
and
z
with coefficients in
\mathbb{Q}(n,z)
. Assume integer-slope asymptotics as in Theorem
6.2
:

u_{n}(z)\sim\Lambda(n,z)h(z)\quad\text{where}\quad\Lambda(n,z)=\lambda^{n}n^{\rho(z)}\prod_{j}\Gamma(n+\alpha_{j}z+\beta_{j})^{m_{j}}

with
\rho(z)
affine of integer slope and
\alpha_{j}\in\mathbb{Z}
.

Then the connection constant
h(z)
satisfies an AFE with rational
coefficients.

Proof.

Since
u_{n}(z)
is holonomic in
(n,z)
, elimination theory in Ore algebras
[
4
]
guarantees the existence of a contiguity relation in
z
:

(6.1)

\sum_{k=0}^{r^{\prime}}S_{k}(n,z)u_{n}(z+k)=0,

where
S_{k}(n,z)\in\mathbb{Q}(n,z)
are rational functions.

Substituting the asymptotic form
u_{n}(z+k)\sim\Lambda(n,z+k)h(z+k)
and dividing by
\Lambda(n,z)
yields

(6.2)

\sum_{k=0}^{r^{\prime}}h(z+k)\cdot T_{k}(n,z)\approx 0,\quad\text{where }T_{k}(n,z):=S_{k}(n,z)\frac{\Lambda(n,z+k)}{\Lambda(n,z)}.

We analyze the ratio
\Lambda(n,z+k)/\Lambda(n,z)
. The power part contributes
n^{c_{1}k}
since
\rho(z)=c_{1}z+c_{0}
is affine with
c_{1}\in\mathbb{Z}
; this is a polynomial in
n
. For each Gamma factor, the ratio

G_{j}(n,z,k)=\frac{\Gamma(n+\alpha_{j}(z+k)+\beta_{j})}{\Gamma(n+\alpha_{j}z+\beta_{j})}

equals
(n+\alpha_{j}z+\beta_{j})_{M}
if
M=\alpha_{j}k>0
, or
1/(n+\alpha_{j}z+\beta_{j}+M)_{|M|}
if
M<0
. By Lemma
6.1
, since
\alpha_{j}\in\mathbb{Z}
, these are rational in
(n,z)
.

Thus each
T_{k}(n,z)
admits an expansion in powers of
n
:

T_{k}(n,z)=n^{\sigma_{k}}\left(R_{k}^{(0)}(z)+R_{k}^{(1)}(z)n^{-1}+O(n^{-2})\right)

where
\sigma_{k}\in\mathbb{Z}
and
R_{k}^{(j)}(z)\in\mathbb{Q}(z)
.

Let
\sigma=\max_{k}\sigma_{k}
be the dominant exponent. Dividing (
6.2
) by
n^{\sigma}
and taking
n\to\infty
, the leading coefficient must vanish:

\sum_{k:\sigma_{k}=\sigma}h(z+k)\cdot R_{k}^{(0)}(z)=0.

If this equation is trivial (all
R_{k}^{(0)}=0
for the maximal indices), we proceed to the next power of
n
. Since (
6.1
) holds exactly for all
n
, some coefficient must be nontrivial, yielding a nondegenerate AFE

\sum_{k=0}^{r^{\prime}}R_{k}(z)h(z+k)=0,\qquad R_{k}(z)\in\mathbb{Q}(z).\qed

Example 6.4

(Derivation of AFE for
f(z)
)
.

We illustrate Proposition
6.3
with the
\mathrm{e}
-world example.

The sequence
u_{n}(z)
satisfies the P-recurrence (after clearing denominators):

(6.3)

(n+z)u_{n+2}(z)-(n+z)u_{n+1}(z)-u_{n}(z)=0.

The functional equation for
f(z)
does not arise directly from this recurrence, which relates terms at fixed
z
. Instead, one derives a contiguity relation linking
u_{n}(z)
,
u_{n}(z+1)
, and
u_{n}(z+2)
via elimination. Shifting
z\mapsto z+1
in (
6.3
) yields

(n+z+1)u_{n+2}(z+1)-(n+z+1)u_{n+1}(z+1)-u_{n}(z+1)=0,

and similarly for
z\mapsto z+2
. The three equations (at
z
,
z+1
,
z+2
) form a system from which a contiguity relation in
z
can be extracted.

With the asymptotic ansatz
u_{n}(z)\sim f(z)\cdot n
, we have
u_{n}(z+k)\sim f(z+k)\cdot n
. Since the asymptotic shell
\Lambda(n,z)=n
is independent of
z
, the ratio
\Lambda(n,z+k)/\Lambda(n,z)=1
contributes no
z
-dependence.

The leading-order substitution into the contiguity relation yields a degenerate equation (the
O(n)
terms cancel). To obtain the correct AFE, one uses the refined expansion
u_{n}(z)=f(z)\cdot n+c(z)+O(1/n)
and extracts the coefficient of the leading non-vanishing term. Matching at order
O(1)
recovers the functional equation

f(z+2)=(z+2)[f(z)-f(z+1)]

established directly in Section
3
.

This example shows that while the general mechanism of Proposition
6.3
applies, extracting the AFE may require asymptotic refinement beyond leading order when the shell
\Lambda(n,z)
has trivial
z
-dependence.

7.1.
The fundamental distinction

Formulas (

8.
The holonomic perspective

8.1.
Scope and limitations

An additive Gamma function of order
r
is a single mathematical object that manifests in three equivalent forms (Figure
1

This correspondence applies specifically to sequences with factorisable asymptotics having integer slopes, ensuring rational coefficients in the functional equations.

8.2.
The holonomic triangle of order-
r
equations

Principle 8.1

(The holonomic triangle)
.

This principle provides a conceptual framework positioning AGFs within the broader landscape of holonomic objects. It is not a formal theorem but rather an organizing perspective that guides the theory.

For P-recursive sequences with integer-slope asymptotics and moderate algebraic growth, an additive Gamma function of order
r
typically admits three related descriptions:

(1)

P-recurrence:
A sequence
u_{n}(z)
satisfying

\sum_{k=0}^{r}p_{k}(n,z)u_{n+k}(z)=0

with
p_{k}(n,z)\in\mathbb{Q}(n,z)
and integer-slope asymptotics
u_{n}(z)\sim\Lambda(n,z)h(z)
.

(2)

AFE:
The connection constant
h(z)
satisfying

\sum_{k=0}^{r}R_{k}(z)h(z+k)=0

with rational coefficients
R_{k}(z)\in\mathbb{Q}(z)
.

(3)

D-finite ODE:
The ordinary generating function
U(x,z)=\sum_{n}u_{n}(z)x^{n}
satisfying a linear ODE:

\sum_{j=0}^{m}Q_{j}(x,z)\frac{\mathrm{d}^{j}U}{\mathrm{d}x^{j}}=0,

with
Q_{j}(x,z)\in\mathbb{Q}(x,z)
. Under the integer-slope and moderate growth conditions, the order
m
depends on both the width
r
of the P-recurrence and the maximum degree
d
of its polynomial coefficients (see Remark
3.1
). Based on elimination theory in Ore algebras
[
4

9
]

(d+1)r
, though often much lower in practice.

6.3

z
for

and taking the asymptotic limit
n\to\infty

•

(1)
\to
(3): Standard generating function techniques
[
7
]
.

•

(2)
\leftrightarrow
(3): Via singularity analysis and the transfer theorem.

•

(2)
\to
(1): The connection constant
h(z)
of an AGF determines a canonical P-recursive sequence through its generating function, as shown in Theorem

.

We emphasize that AGFs are not D-finite functions in general; rather, they are organized by additive functional equations. In favorable cases (such as our examples

Proposition 8.2

(Holonomic certificate for the

For each fixed parameter

V_{z}(x):=\sum_{n\geq 1}v_{n}(z)x^{n}
satisfies a first-order linear differential equation with rational coefficients in

9.
Concluding remarks

This work establishes mirror symmetry between recurrences connecting
\mathrm{e}
and
\pi
. Through parametrization and asymptotic analysis, connection constants extend to meromorphic functions satisfying additive functional equations with rational coefficients. The arithmetic duality (
1.3
) between
\mathbb{Z}
-linear forms in
\mathrm{e}
and
\mathbb{Q}
-linear forms in
\pi
provides the motivating structure.

We constructed order-2 AGFs
f(z)
and
g(z)
, recognizing Euler’s Gamma function as the order-1 prototype. The Integer Slope Condition (Lemma
6.1
) ensures rational coefficients, and Theorem
6.2
establishes the equivalence between AGFs and connection constants from integer-slope P-recurrences. The explicit formulas (
3.8
) and (
4.8
) reveal the regular/irregular dichotomy.

9.1.
Open questions

The integer slope condition delineates where rational-coefficient AFEs emerge. For sequences with non-integer slopes or non-factorisable asymptotics, whether functional equations exist (and with what coefficients) remains open; beyond this boundary, transcendental coefficients would likely be required. Several directions merit further investigation:

(1)

Classification at order 2.
Systematic study of order-2 AGFs and their connection to Heun’s differential equations
[
15
]
could illuminate Diophantine properties of exotic constants.

(2)

Extensions.
Natural generalizations include: (a) logarithmic asymptotics when characteristic roots coincide; (b)
q
-analogues where Jacobi theta functions replace Gamma functions.

(3)

Applications.
Black hole quasi-normal modes
[
10
]
yield three-term recurrences; AGF methods might provide analytical tools if integer slopes appear. Higher-order P-recurrences (
r\geq 3
) could yield linear forms with better Diophantine control.
