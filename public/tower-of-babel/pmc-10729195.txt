Virtual reality as an adjunct to pulmonary rehabilitation of patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease: a protocol for systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Yi Li, Hongzhan Jiang, Zhi Lyu
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
IntroductionChronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is a prevalent chronic lung disease characterised by persistent and progressive airflow obstruction resulting from tracheal and/or alveolar lesions. Patients afflicted with COPD endure a poor quality of life primarily due to the symptoms of the disease. Pulmonary rehabilitation (PR) constitutes a core component of the comprehensive management of individuals dealing with COPD. Nevertheless, suboptimal adherence and completion rates are the chief impediments associated with PR. Virtual reality (VR) is emerging as a promising approach to support patients with COPD in their PR journey. Currently, no comprehensive systematic review has evaluated the impact of VR as a PR adjunct in patients with COPD. We aimed to investigate and summarise the evidence from recent studies related to the effect of VR as an adjunct to PR in COPD cases.Methods and analysisWe will conduct a comprehensive search of databases, including Web of Science, CINAHL, PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials and ClinicalTrials.gov, from their inception up to May 2023 to identify randomised controlled trials examining VR as an adjunct to PR in patients with COPD, with no restrictions on publication status or language. Our primary outcome measure will be the 6-min walk test. Two independent researchers will screen the literature for suitable articles for inclusion in this meta-analysis. Data collection and assessment of bias risk will be performed. This meta-analysis is intended to furnish data on each outcome as sufficient data become available. Heterogeneity will be assessed using the χ2 test and I2 statistics. The current review will adhere to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses guidelines.Ethics and disseminationEthical approval is waived due to the retrospective nature of this study. Furthermore, the findings will be disseminated through peer-reviewed journals.PROSPERO registration numberCRD42022374736.

Introduction

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is a chronic lung disease characterised by symptoms such as dyspnoea, cough and expectoration due to persistent and progressive airflow obstruction.1 As of 2019, COPD was the third main reason for death across the world.2 The increasing burden of COPD is attributed to factors like population ageing and the persistence of risk factors, including tobacco use and air pollution.3 The primary symptoms that disrupt the daily lives of patients with COPD comprise dyspnoea, coughing and expectoration,4 often overshadowing anxiety and depression, which are frequently overlooked symptoms in patients with COPD.5 6 Recognised as a complex, heterogeneous disease, including intrapulmonary and extrapulmonary components, COPD necessitates both pharmacological treatment and comprehensive chronic disease management to enhance patients’ quality of life.7 As per the core components of comprehensive COPD management, pulmonary rehabilitation (PR) constitutes a holistic care programme, comprising a range of interventions such as aerobic exercises, strength training, educational sessions, psychological support and nutritional counselling.8 9 Endurance-based exercise training, and disease-specific education are the main components of PR.10 According to a 2015 meta-analysis comprising 65 randomised controlled trials (RCTs) conducted by the Cochrane Collaboration, PR has been shown to alleviate dyspnoea and fatigue, enhance patients’ quality of life, and improve exercise capacity.11 However, PR faces substantial challenges regarding compliance and completion rates due to transportation, symptom severity and disruptions to daily life.12 13 An Australian study revealed that a significant proportion of individuals referred to PR did not participate in the programme, with percentages ranging from 8%–50%. Furthermore, among those who initiated treatment, 10%–32% did not complete the programme.14

With the advancement of virtual reality (VR) technology, it has emerged as a potential tool for PR in patientswith COPD.15–17 VR, a 3D imaging technology, facilitates interaction between human senses and virtual environments.18 VR technology can be categorised as immersive and non-immersive. Immersive VR technology allows patients to fully immerse themselves in visual, auditory, and kinesthetic stimuli using head-mounted devices, alleviating anxiety and depression symptoms in patients with COPD.16 Non-immersive VR technology involves patients using their bodies during gameplay, providing exercise benefits.19 Common game platforms in VR technology include ‘Wii Fit’, ‘Xbox Kinect,’ and ‘Wii Sports software’.15 19

A recent meta-analysis examined the impact of active video games (AVGs) as an adjunct to PR in patients with COPD.20 However, this study did not include immersive VR technology and featured limited, low-quality studies, including non-RCTs, with no subgroup or sensitivity analyses conducted. Currently, a comprehensive meta-analysis assessing the effect of VR as a supplementary tool in PR for patients with COPD has not been reported. After we submitted our protocol, a study conducted a meta-analysis on the effect of VR as an auxiliary tool for PR in patients with COPD.21 We note that this study had a limited number of databases included and a relatively short time period for study inclusion, which raises concerns about its quality. Thus, we devised an extensive systematic review and meta-analysis to evaluate the influence of VR when used in conjunction with PR for patients with COPD.

Methods and analysis

This protocol has been developed following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses Protocols (PRISMA-P) 2015 guidelines22 and registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) under Registration ID: CRD42022374736.

Study eligibility

Study type

Eligible studies will include RCTs as they offer unbiased information compared with other study designs. We will include patients who received VR rehabilitation in one or more arms of the RCTs. Quasirandomised RCTs, such as those based on alphabetical order, birthdate, or days of the week, will be excluded. Only peer-reviewed articles will be considered.

Participant type

We will include adults (aged≥18 years) with objective COPD diagnoses based on the latest Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease (GOLD) guidelines: FEV1/FVC<0.7, that is, a ratio<0.7 1 s after bronchodilator inhalation.1

Intervention type

RCTs use VR as an adjunct to PR in patients with COPD. In this context, the intervention refers to rehabilitation using VR technology (including immersive and non-immersive VR), possibly supervised by rehabilitation professionals like occupational therapists and physiotherapists or individuals trained by rehabilitation experts, such as paid or unpaid caregivers for patients with COPD.

Comparison type

The control group should be given an alternative rehabilitation treatment modality or receive standard medical care. Here, ‘standard medical care’ refers to the conventional PR typically administered to patients with COPD.9

Outcome measurements

Primary outcomes

The primary outcome measure of exercise tolerance will be assessed using the 6-min walk test, a recommendation by the American Thoracic Society.23

Secondary outcomes

Dyspnoea will be primarily assessed using the Medical Research Council scale and the Borg scale.24 25 Quality of life will be evaluated through various instruments, including the capacity index (BODE), St. George’s Respiratory Questionnaire, COPD Assessment Test, or other validated tools.26–28 Physical function in patients with COPD will be assessed using the Senior Fitness Test (formerly referred to as the Functional Fitness Test or Fullerton test).29 Anxiety and depression status will be evaluated using instruments such as the Beck Depression Inventory, Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale and other validated scales.30 31

Search strategy

Electronic searches

We will conduct electronic searches on various search engine databases, including Web of Science, CINAHL, PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials and ClinicalTrials.gov (https://www.clinicaltrials.gov/), covering the period from inception to May 2023. The PubMed search strategy is included in the online supplemental material.

For each database, we will combine free-text search terms with controlled vocabulary terms (eg, Medical Subject Headings in Medline and Emtree in Embase). There will be no restrictions on the year of publication. We will record search results in a predefined Excel sheet and manage records using EndNote (V.X9.1).

Retrieval of additional resources

We will employ the snowball method to identify additional related studies by checking the references of included articles. We will also reach out to experts in the fields of PR and COPD for the potential identification of additional RCTs. Additionally, we will use Google Scholar to uncover grey literature studies.

Data extraction and processing

Study screening

We will initially use EndNote V.X9.1 to identify duplicate records in the retrieved data, followed by a manual screening of duplicate records. Two researchers (YL and HJ) will independently assess the titles and abstracts of publications retrieved from the literature search. Articles meeting the predefined inclusion criteria will be selected for full-text evaluation. The full texts of these articles will be thoroughly reviewed by two reviewers for the final selection of eligible studies. Any disagreements will be resolved through consultation with a third researcher or group discussion. We will document all excluded studies along with specific reasons for exclusion. Data collection and processing

Two independent researchers (YL and HJ) will extract data from the selected RCTs. Data will include general information (titles, authors, study country, publication year, single-centre or multicentre, funding), study methodology details (objectives, design, eligibility criteria, randomization, allocation method, blinding and total study duration), participant characteristics (age, gender, sample size, number included in the analysis, diagnostic criteria and body mass index), intervention details (VR regimen specifics, duration, compliance and follow-up duration) and outcome measures (primary/secondary outcomes, time points, adverse events and methods of outcome assessment). For dichotomous data, we will record the number of subjects and the number who experienced the event in each group. For continuous data, we will record the number of subjects along with means and SD in each group. We will also attempt to collect additional information from the original study authors when necessary. Any discrepancies will be resolved through consultation with a third researcher or group discussion.

Evaluation of the risk of bias

We will use the Cochrane Risk of Bias-2 tool33 to evaluate the methodological quality of the included studies. The evaluation will consider five aspects: bias arising from the randomisation process, bias due to deviations from intended interventions, bias due to missing outcome data, bias in outcome measurement and bias in the selection of reported results. This assessment will be performed by two researchers (YL and HJ).

Grading the quality of evidence

We will apply the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) guidelines to assess the quality of the generated evidence.

Data synthesis

We will conduct meta-analyses to calculate the summarised estimated therapeutic efficacy for each outcome, along with 95% CIs. Dichotomous values will be presented as ORs with 95% CIs, while MDs will be calculated for outcomes measured on the same scale with continuous data. Standardised MDs will be used when different scales will be employed in the included RCTs. The analysis will be performed using Review Manager (V.5.4). In cases where a meta-analysis is not feasible, a narrative summary of findings from various RCTs will be provided.

Assessment of heterogeneity

Statistical heterogeneity among the included studies will be assessed using the I2 statistic and χ2 test.34 Significance in statistical heterogeneity will be defined as p<0.10 and/or an I2 statistic>50%.35 36 In the absence of heterogeneity, we will use a fixed-effect model for meta-analyses; otherwise, a random-effect model will be employed.37

Dealing with missing data

We will make efforts to contact study authors to obtain missing information or clarification whenever possible. In cases where no responses will be received from the authors, we will use available data and consider potential impacts on bias determination. Additional network studies or prior meta-analyses will be consulted for data confirmation when necessary.38

Assessment of reporting biases

For meta-analyses containing 10 or more RCTs, we will assess publication bias using a funnel plot. Additionally, we will use Egger’s test to evaluate publication bias.39

Subgroup and sensitivity analyses

Subgroup analysis will be conducted when sufficient data are available. Specifically, we will perform subgroup analyses stratified by age, gender, illness severity, intervention duration and the type of control group (placebo, non-treatment, usual treatment, or other active treatment). Additionally, we will conduct a subgroup analysis considering different VR equipment platforms, as different equipment may have varying therapeutic effects. Sensitivity analysis will be conducted to assess the obtained outcomes’ reliability. This will involve excluding non-published literature, low-quality RCTs and comparing outcomes between fixed-effect and random-effect models.

Patient and public involvement

No patients or members of the public were involved in the development of the study protocol.

Discussion

VR-mediated PR can overcome issues such as cumbersome transit, limited space and time limits associated with traditional PR.14 Moreover, numerous studies have indicated that VR-based rehabilitation environments are perceived as more engaging and enjoyable, motivating patients to participate actively compared with conventional methods.40 41 VR-assisted MR can enable healthcare professionals to remotely monitor patients with COPD on a broader scale, especially the elderly, and provide supervision.17 Furthermore, global healthcare trends indicate the effectiveness of emerging technologies in the rehabilitation field. Hence, integrating novel technologies as adjunct tools alongside traditional rehabilitation methods can be beneficial for patients.42 Currently, there is a lack of comprehensive assessments regarding the impact of VR as an adjunct to PR in patients with COPD, emphasising the need for comprehensive systematic reviews and meta-analyses. Unlike the meta-analysis conducted by Wang et al,20 our study will not restrict its search to studies involving AVGs.

We firmly believe that our meta-analysis holds significant importance for future research and policy development. We anticipate that our findings will provide valuable insights into the use of VR as an adjunct to PR in patients with COPD.
