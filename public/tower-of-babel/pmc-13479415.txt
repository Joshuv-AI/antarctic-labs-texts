Nutritional knowledge, sustainable food practices and food safety, in child and youth care facilities in KwaZulu-Natal, South Africa
Authors: Mumsy Evidence Chibe, Carin Napier, Heleen Grobbelaar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChild and Youth Care Centres (CYCCs) play an important role in providing nutritional support to disadvantaged children and youth. However, little is known about how food handlers apply national nutrition and food hygiene standards in resource-constrained CYCC settings in South Africa.AimThe aim of the study was to examine food, nutrition, and hygiene practices among food handlers at CYCCs in KwaZulu-Natal Province of South Africa.MethodologyA qualitative exploratory research design was used in the study. Three online focus group discussions (FGDs) were conducted in August and September 2020, with 18 food handlers from two CYCCs participating. COVID-19 Alert Level 5 restrictions necessitated online FGDs. The discussions were audio-recorded, transcribed verbatim, and analysed thematically. Co-researchers’ independent coding, peer debriefing, member checking, and safeguarding of an audit trail all assisted to assure trustworthiness.ResultsThe study revealed different levels of awareness about nutrition guidelines and food-based dietary guidelines, with a significant gap between knowledge and practical application. While participants exhibited a high level of awareness of food safety, hygiene, and storage practices, implementation was hindered by limited financial resources, inadequate nutrition instruction, a lack of standardised recipes, and insufficient access to hygiene supplies and personal protective equipment. Budget constraints significantly affected meal planning and dietary diversification.ConclusionWhile food handlers in CYCCs have a basic understanding of food safety and cleanliness, they face systematic and structural impediments to effectively apply nutrition requirements. Strengthening practical nutrition training, improving resource allocation, and increasing institutional and policy support are critical for improving food, nutrition, and hygiene practices and promoting improved health outcomes for children in CYCCs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s41043-026-01343-5.Keywords: Institutional child care, Food handlers, Nutrition and food safety practices, Hygiene practices, Qualitative study, South Africa

Introduction

Globally, child and youth care centres (CYCCs) and community-based childcare facilities play a critical yet diverse role in providing alternative care for orphans, vulnerable children, and young people, particularly in low- and middle-income countries (Better Care Network and [1, 2]. In many African countries, institutionalisation remains a common option (for example, in the Maghreb region, high rates of child abandonment and limited foster-care capacity have resulted in widespread reliance on institutions), with hundreds of thousands of children estimated to be in institutional care in countries such as Algeria, Morocco, and Tunisia [3]. In South Africa (SA), these centres are critical environments for the care and development of vulnerable children. Many children enter care with pre-existing health conditions such as low birth weight, HIV exposure, or disabilities, which significantly affect their nutritional status and overall development [4]. In addition to health challenges, children often face poverty, neglect, orphanhood, abuse, and malnutrition all of which compound their vulnerability [5, 6]. Child and Youth Care Workers, particularly those involved in food handling, play a pivotal role in shaping the nutritional outcomes of children in residential care. Despite their frontline responsibilities, their nutritional knowledge remains underexplored. Understanding their familiarity with the South African Food-Based Dietary Guidelines (SAFBDGs) is essential, as these guidelines provide a framework for promoting healthy eating habits among children [7]. Previous studies indicated that while many food handlers possess some level of nutritional awareness, disparities in knowledge and practice persist, potentially undermining the health and development of children in care [8, 9]. Moreover, food safety and hygiene practices are critical in preventing foodborne illnesses, which are often linked to inadequate food preparation, poor sanitation, and low literacy levels among food handlers [10, 11]. The first 1000 days of life are a crucial window for nutritional intervention, with long-term implications for physical and cognitive development [12]. Therefore, they must be equipped with nutritional expertise and a thorough understanding of the socioeconomic factors that influence children’s dietary needs [13, 14]. This research aligns with four key Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) as outlined by the United Nations [15].

It primarily supports SDG 2: Zero Hunger by promoting the provision of safe and nutritious food through improved food handling and production practices. It contributes to SDG 3: Good Health and Well-being by addressing the reduction of foodborne illnesses caused by poor hygiene and unsafe food preparation. The study relates to SDG 6: Clean Water and Sanitation, as effective hygiene practices among food handlers are dependent on access to clean water and adequate sanitation infrastructure. Finally, it supports SDG 4: Quality Education, recognising that training and educating food handlers essential for fostering sustainable and safe food systems [16].

This study followed a qualitative approach to investigate food handlers’ nutritional knowledge, food safety awareness, and handling practices in CYCCs across KwaZulu-Natal, South Africa. By examining these factors, the research aimed to identify gaps and include looking at the impact of nutritional knowledge, sustainable food practices, and food-safety habits in South African CYCCs. Most existing research focuses on schools or general food-service settings, with CYCCs frequently disregarded despite their role in feeding vulnerable children. There is little evidence of food handlers’ real knowledge, behaviours, and limitations in these establishments. This study therefore fills a gap by investigating the interrelated elements within sector in KwaZulu-Natal [8]. The study is underpinned by a combination of the Knowledge, Attitude, and Practices model and the Social Ecological Model. These frameworks together provide a multi-level understanding of the factors that influence food handlers’ behaviours [17].

Methodology

Study design

This qualitative study employed an exploratory research methodology and online FGDs to examine food, nutrition, and hygiene behaviours among food handlers. The study was conducted in August and September 2020, a period during which South Africa was under Alert Level 5 restrictions due to the COVID-19 epidemic [18]. Consequently, data collection was shifted from face-to-face interactions to an online format. The researchers aimed to understand food handlers’ experiences and practices, with a focus on data depth and richness above volume. The interactive aspect of the FGDs provided an opportunity for the participants to share practical challenges and insights around nutrition and hygiene. The findings were systematically through a structured thematic process to ensure credibility and contextual relevance.

Setting

The study was conducted in eThekwini Municipality, which is a large metropolitan area on the east coast of South Africa in KwaZulu-Natal, covering approximately 2,555 square kilometres and home to a diverse population of over 3.6 million people as of 2016 Community [19]. The municipality faces complex social, economic, and governance challenges, making it a relevant context for studying interventions for vulnerable populations [20]. Specifically, the research was carried out at two registered and licensed CYCCs providingcare and personal development services to children and youth who had been abandoned, orphaned, neglected, or deemed at risk by the Children’s Court. The sector is regulated by the Department of Social Development and provides critical support structures for at-risk children, offering a focused and ethically grounded setting for examining the implementation of child welfare interventions. The sector in provides care and protection for vulnerable children under the Children’s Act. In eThekwini, high poverty and HIV/AIDS rates increase the demand for these services. CYCCs face challenges such as limited funding and staff shortages. Despite this, they play a vital role in child safety and development [21].

Sample selection

A purposive sampling strategy was used to select two centres that were registered with the Department of Social Development. These centres were chosen based on their willingness to participate, geographic diversity, and capacity to accommodate more than 30 children and youth. Additionally, they provided daily meal services, including breakfast, lunch, and dinner. Within the sector, participants were eligible if they had at least one or more years of experience being in contact with food at a certain stage of procurement, production, consumption and post consumption. Both centres were formally registered in the province, providing full-time residential care to children and youth in accordance with national norms and standards. They were typical of the region’s medium-sized (accommodating between 20 and 60 residents) (Children’s Act, 2005 (Act No. 38 of 2005), notably in terms of staffing organisation, food-service arrangements, and the age range of the children they served (5 to 18 years). Because of these commonalities, they were deemed appropriate to provide insights into food handling, nutrition practices, and hygiene behaviours often observed in comparable facilities.

Transferability

Purposive selection, restricts transferability by definition. Because the CYCCs were not selected at random or to represent the province’s full variety, the findings may not accurately reflect practices in smaller, larger, rural, faith-based, or newly developed centres. CYCCs with diverse funding structures, management systems, or levels of resource constraints may face unique food safety and nutrition challenges. As a result, the insights gained from these two institutions should be viewed as context-specific rather than widely generalisable. Transferability is thus determined by the degree to which other centres share similar characteristics, which readers must ascertain using the study’s thorough contextual explanations.

Sample size

A total of 18 participants were engaged, comprising both child and youth care workers and managers. The sample size was not predetermined but was instead guided by the principle of data saturation, achieved when no new themes or insights emerged during analysis. Ahmed [22] cautioned that saturation is a complex concept and should not be treated as a universal indicator of rigour in qualitative research. Accordingly, additional considerations such as sample size, were taken into account, ensuring alignment with the specific research questions, theoretical frameworks, and methodological coherence [22].

Information sheets and consent forms were distributed to the participants through managers before the study commenced. During an initial briefing meeting, the objectives, scope, and procedures of the study were explained in detail, and the participants were invited to ask questions for clarification. At this stage, the participants were informed that participation was voluntary, that no financial incentives would be offered, and that they could withdraw from the study at any time without negative consequences.

On the day of each focus group session, informed consent was reconfirmed. The participants provided verbal agreement to participate, accompanied by a visible gesture (raising their hands), before the discussion began. This ensured compliance with ethical requirements for online qualitative research.

Three online FGDs were conducted, each consisting of six participants to foster interaction and facilitate rich dialogue. The online format reduced logistical constraints and enabled broader participation across different staff roles while maintaining safety and accessibility.

The discussions were guided by a semi-structured question schedule and focused on three key areas:

Food and nutrition challenges;

Food and nutrition challenges;

Food preparation processes;

Food preparation processes;

Food handling and hygiene practices.

Food handling and hygiene practices.

Each session was verified by the lead researcher, who ensured that the discussions remained focused while allowing the participants to share their experiences openly.

Data collection

The data were gathered through three online FGDs, with an interview schedule based on the nutrition and food safety guidelines for child and youth care workers in South Africa. Each FGD included six participants and was guided by the primary researcher through Zoom [23]. FGDs are a valuable method for gathering diverse perspectives through interactive group dialogue. They help to uncover shared experiences and deeper insights. FGDs are especially useful for exploring complex social issues and informing programme or policy development [24]. This method offered flexibility and extended geographic access, allowing the participants to join even during the strict COVID-19 lockdown period. The administration of the meetings was managed well and ensured good attendance through the timely scheduling of 40 to 60-min sessions, clear communication with the participants, and effective facilitation during the sessions. Invitations and reminders were sent in advance, ensuring that the participants were informed and prepared. Technical support was available to assist with connectivity issues, and ground rules were communicated at the beginning of each session to guide respectful and focused discussion. The facilitator maintained engagement, managed time efficiently, and ensured that each participant had the opportunity to contribute. These efforts collectively supported active participation and good attendance across all the sessions. Each session promoted interactive and reflective discussions on the participants’ nutritional knowledge, food safety, and preparation practices, as well as the challenges they faced in their roles as food handlers and caregivers. All the sessions were conducted in English; however, the participants were encouraged to respond in the language with which they felt most comfortable with, primarily IsiZulu. The sessions were audio-recorded with participants consent. The responses given in IsiZulu were translated into English and transcribed verbatim for analysis.

Analysis

All the discussions were audio-recorded (with the participants’ consent) and transcribed verbatim. The transcripts were reviewed and coded thematically using an inductive approach. The codes were grouped into broader categories and themes, reflecting recurring patterns and unique insights across the dataset. The analysis continued iteratively until consensus was reached that data saturation had been achieved, ensuring comprehensive coverage of the study’s objective. A thematic analysis by Braun and Clarke [25] was conducted manually following the six-step framework. This involved familiarisation with the data, generation of initial codes, searching for themes, reviewing themes, defining and naming themes, and producing the final report. This structured approach facilitated the identification of key patterns and meaningful themes within the participants’ narratives. The primary researcher conducted the data analysis, ensuring consistency and depth of interpretation. To enhance the credibility and authenticity of the findings, co-researchers verified the audit trails, and member checking was used as an additional validation strategy. Audit trails provided a transparent account of the data collection and analysis processes, supporting the dependability of the study [26]. Member checking, which involved presenting selected transcripts to participants for feedback, helped to ensure that their views, experiences, and intentions were accurately captured and interpreted [27]. In the first instance, the two co-researchers confirmed that the data collection and analysis procedures, including the coding process, had been followed rigorously. In the second instance, participant feedback confirmed that their responses had been recorded accurately and adequately.

Reflectivity

The primary researcher is a lecturer at a higher education institution with a background in public health nutrition; her master’s thesis focused on child development initiatives using corn soya. This expertise positioned her as a knowledgeable outsider, which helped her connect with participants but also risked moulding conversations via a nutrition-focused lens. Despite the fact that she had no prior relationships with the participating CYCCs, her professional identification, occasionally prompted participants to respond in ways they assumed were expected. To control this influence, she kept a reflective notebook throughout the data collection and analysis process, noting how her assumptions, emotional reactions to perceived resource constraints, and disciplinary training might affect interpretation. These reflective notes helped her to stay aware of power dynamics, avoid overemphasising technical nutrition issues, bracket pre-existing notions, and ensure that developing themes represented the participants’ experiences rather than her own professional framing.

Coding

The primary researcher oversaw the coding procedure, while one co-researcher independently coded a portion of the transcripts to increase trustworthiness. Additionally, another co-researcher verified the codes to confirm that they were accurate. Any discrepancies in coding were explored in brief peer-debriefing sessions until agreement was established. These talks helped to refine the coding methodology, and the co-researchers checked the audit trail to ensure that the analysis was transparent and consistent.

An inductive thematic approach was used for the analysis, starting with open coding to capture the participants’ experiences, which were then grouped into broader categories and refined into final themes with co-researcher input. The codebook, including code names, definitions, and example quotes, is provided as supplementary material to illustrate how themes were derived from the data.

Member checking and trustworthiness

Member checking was conducted by two individuals, except the researchers, to ensure the accuracy and resonance of the findings. A total of two participants from both CYCCs assessed selected excerpts and preliminary themes. The participants were asked to confirm whether the summaries represented their experiences accurately and to make clarifications or edits. Feedback resulted in minor language modifications to better capture the participants’ intentions, notably regarding food preparation routines and cleanliness standards, but no major changes to the themes were required. This technique increased the reliability and trustworthiness of the findings by ensuring that they represented the participants’ opinions appropriately.

Ethical consideration

Ethical clearance for the study was granted by the Durban University of Technology (DUT) Institutional Research Ethics Committee (IREC No: 076/15). Permission to conduct the research was obtained from the Department of Social Development provincial office, following a formal application supported by a detailed study summary. The request was endorsed by senior departmental officials, after which the researcher was provided with a list, from which two CYCCs were purposively selected based on existing critical gaps identified within these facilities. Studies have highlighted limitations in food safety and hygiene practices among the food handlers, with many lacking formal training in food handling and preparation. This lack of expertise poses risks to the health and well-being of children in their care [23, 28]. Furthermore, gatekeeper permission was secured from the respective managers, and meetings were held with the food handlers to present the purpose and procedures of the study. The information sheets were provided in both English and isiZulu, and informed consent was obtained from all the participants. Participation was entirely voluntary, with no financial compensation or personal costs involved. The participants were informed of their right to withdraw at any time without consequence. Anonymity and confidentiality were strictly maintained through the use of identification codes and the exclusion of children from the study. Additional ethical safeguards, including data triangulation, member checking, and reflexive journaling, were used to ensure the credibility and trustworthiness of the research process. The researcher maintained a reflexive journal to monitor potential biases and ensure transparency in interpretation.

Findings and discussion

The analysis of the focus group data yielded three major themes with related sub-themes that shed light on the knowledge, practices, and challenges surrounding nutrition, food preparation, and food safety. Direct participant quotations are included to illustrate key insights and support the credibility of the findings.

Profiles of the participants

The participants’ biographical information (included in Table 1) provides significant context for interpreting the study findings. The majority of participants were female (n = 14; 78%), mostly Black African (n = 12; 67%), and mostly isiZulu-speaking (n = 10; 56%). Participants ranged in age from 30 to 70 years old, with the largest proportions in the 36–40 (n = 4; 22%) and 51–55 (n = 4; 22%) age categories. Food handling roles at many CYCCs in South Africa are dominated by women, mirroring broader gendered trends in work distribution, caregiving, and household responsibilities [29, 30]. This demographic composition closely aligns with the findings as shared cultural and linguistic backgrounds likely facilitated open communication during the focus group discussions, enabling participants to express their experiences more freely and in depth [31]. At the same time, these characteristics reflect the broader socio-economic pattern, where women, particularly Black African women, are overrepresented in caregiving roles, often with limited access to formal training opportunities. This context helps explain some of the observed gaps in nutrition knowledge, understanding of Food-Based Dietary Guidelines and food safety practices, as well as the reliance on experiential and culturally embedded knowledge systems. [13] argue that education influences people’s understanding of nutrition, hygiene, child development, and food safety. Care workers with higher levels of education typically demonstrate a greater knowledge because their training provides structured learning that explains why certain activities are necessary, rather than just how to conduct them.

Sociodemographics of the participants

The participants’ length of service, which ranged from 1 to 14 years, is another important factor in the study’s results, as it influences both competence and trust in food-handling techniques. The majority of participants had approximately four (4) years of work experience (n = 5; 28%). Overall, longer tenure was associated with better practical abilities in food safety and cleanliness, most likely due to cumulative experience and ongoing exposure to workplace practices. However, experience alone did not always translate into accurate or up-to-date knowledge, particularly in the absence of continuous formal training. Conversely, less experienced participants showed greater uncertainty and dependence on informal learning from colleagues. This interplay between experience and education helps explain the inconsistencies observed in the application of nutrition and hygiene practices across participants [8]. Overall, social demographic characteristics collectively contribute to the diversity in knowledge levels, behaviors, and challenges identified in the study, reinforcing the need for targeted, context-specific training interventions that consider education background, language, and years of experience [32–35] .

Themes and sub-themes

Theme 1: nutrition-related knowledge of the participants

This theme examined the participants’ level of nutrition knowledge, focusing on their understanding of the nutritional guidelines for children, food dietary guidelines, and any training on nutrition they might have acquired.

Sub-theme 1.1: knowledge of following nutritional guidelines

The participants demonstrated various levels of understanding of nutritional standards, indicating a gap between knowledge and practical application. While several food handlers were aware of national dietary requirements, many noted that limited financial resources and ingredient availability frequently hampered adherence. Participant 2 (P2) in Focus Group 2 (FG2) remarked: "There are some guidelines that we do not follow because we lack the resources…" Another participant further explained, “As much as we try to implement the right nutritional guidelines, sometimes it is not possible to get or buy fresh meat or vegetables, for example. We use what we have”(P2, FG2). One participant expressed inadequate understanding of nutritional guidelines, saying: “We have little knowledge about nutrition guidelines for children” (P1, FG1).

This disconnect highlights a common challenge in under-resourced care settings: policy and guideline awareness alone insufficient without the necessary infrastructure and support to implement them. The findings align with the work of Mukunya et al. [36], who assert that structural challenges, such as resource scarcity, often undermine even well-intentioned efforts to adhere to nutritional standards in institutional care. The problem is not a lack of willingness, but a system-level inadequacy in support. Research on nutrition initiatives in low-resource settings suggests that awareness of guidelines may not always translate into practice due to contextual limitations such as funding and lack of organisational support. Implementation constraints, such as limited resources and conflicting priorities, hinder adherence to nutrition-related policies in low- and middle-income countries, despite stakeholder support and understanding of their importance [37]. The present findings are consistent with previous research indicating that the knowledge of Child Care Workers (CCWs) regarding recommended fruit and vegetable intake, appropriate serving sizes, and the importance of dietary variety is often inadequate. Studies by [8] identified significant gaps in CCWs’ understanding of basic nutrition principles within child residential care settings. Similarly, more recent work by Chibe, Napier and Grobbelaar [38] further highlights the limited nutritional knowledge among caregivers responsible for food preparation and feeding practices in such facilities. These studies collectively emphasize that insufficient knowledge among CCWs may negatively influence the quality and diversity of meals provided to children in residential care. Consequently, the current findings reinforce the need for structured nutrition education and support the development of a comprehensive food preparation and nutrition manual specifically designed for child residential care facilities. Such a resource could help improve CCWs’ understanding of balanced diets, recommended portion sizes, and the inclusion of a variety of fruits and vegetables, thereby contributing to improved nutritional outcomes for children in institutional care.

Sub-theme 1.2: awareness of South African food-based dietary guidelines

Many of the participants expressed unfamiliarity with the SAFBDGs, indicating that their training lacked depth and practical relevance. For instance, P3 in FG3 stated: "We are not very familiar with the nutrition guidelines, even though the module is not strong enough" (P3, FG3). Despite this gap, food handlers showed eagerness to learn and apply such knowledge, provided they received appropriate training and resources. There was broad agreement among participants that (FBDGs) were very useful and contained a balance diet. One interviewee in FG3 put it this way, “We are given a menu which is already planned, our job is to follow that.” (P1, FG3). Frontline staff in low resource settings often lack awareness of national nutrition guidelines due to limited training and support mechanisms. The discussion on how useful the nutritional recipes included in the guidelines were indicated that participants demonstrated insufficient nutrition knowledge. As one of the participants (P3) in the FG3 explained, “…when we implement, we must implement something that we know, not information which we are not sure of. Right now we do not have information and we are so hopeful that the guidelines will be able to teach us about nutrition and menu planning”(P3, FG3).

For example, studies among school food handlers revealed that many were unaware of the FBDGs and balanced meal planning, but believed that further education and training would improve their ability to follow healthy dietary practices [39]. In other institutional and community contexts, knowledge and implementation of the SAFBDGs have been limited because training did not adequately contextualise the information for practical use. Studies on integrating the SAFBDGs into educational settings identify difficulties such as educators’ lack of understanding about the recommendations and insufficient nutrition education in training programmes, which limit successful dissemination and use [40]. Evaluations of the SAFBDG field-testing in African contexts show that even with acceptable guideline messages, comprehension and practical uptake may require specific communication strategies and reinforcement through nutrition education interventions. This aligns with previous studies suggesting that staff can effectively adopt evidence-based practices when training is contextualised and practical [41]. The findings emphasise the need for targeted capacity-building programmes that address both knowledge and the ability to act on that knowledge. The results of this study are also consistent with the findings of Leme et al. [42], who reported that adherence to dietary guidelines is influenced by several socio-demographic factors, including age, sex, and income. These factors shape individuals’ access to nutrition information, their food choices, and their ability to apply dietary recommendations in daily life. The convergence of these findings with those of the current study reinforces the importance of considering socio-demographic contexts when designing and implementing nutrition education interventions. While previous studies have highlighted low nutrition knowledge and the influence of socio-demographic characteristics on adherence to dietary guidelines, the present study contributes additional insight by demonstrating how these factors interact within the specific context of the study population. In doing so, it provides further evidence that improving nutrition education requires not only strengthening CYCWs knowledge but also addressing structural and contextual factors such as CYCWs training and socio-economic conditions. This new evidence can inform the development of targeted health policies, behaviour-change strategies, and the refinement of food-based dietary guidelines to better support effective nutrition education and promote healthier dietary practices.

Sub-theme 1.3: nutrition training

Participants noted that their formal nutrition training was limited and insufficient for effective meal planning. One noted, "We have learned something about nutrition, but the module did not give us sufficient information" (P1, FG2). This led to reliance on informal experience over structured, evidence-based planning. P3 in FG3 expressed that, “We are not very familiar with the nutrition training. We have a module on nutrition, but it is not strong to give you that much knowledge.” Participant 5 of FG3 echoed similar sentiments, saying “We do not know anything about the training, but if we are given the information, we are willing to incorporate it into our work” (P5, FG3).

As a result of a lack of systematic, evidence-based planning, many food handlers rely on informal experience instead. Such gaps in formal training are not unique to this context; research in other institutional and caregiving settings found that inadequate nutrition education among food service staff or health workers impaired their ability to apply dietary guidelines and plan nutritious meals effectively. For example, a systematic review discovered that nutrition training for health workers enhanced their ability to counsel carers and improve child feeding practices, implying that structured training can improve practitioners’ nutrition knowledge and competence [43]. Furthermore, nutrition education interventions with frontline staff and nutrition professionals have been shown to boost confidence, behavioural intention towards healthy practices, and practical competencies such as meal planning, which are essential for implementing dietary standards in institutional settings [44]. However, this gap may have a negative effect on children’s nutritional outcomes, especially when combined with economic constraints. Professional development that is hands-on and tailored to the context of CYCCs could bridge this gap, enhancing staff competence and improving child health outcomes [45].

Theme 2: food production

This theme focuses on how food is served, planned, prepared, and managed within the centres. It has four sub-themes: meal planning and preparation, availability of nutritious recipes, food handling and preparation practices, and food storage practices.

Sub-theme 2.1: meal planning and preparation

Effective meal planning was identified as a core responsibility, but one severely constrained by financial limitations. P2 in FG2 shared, "You can plan a menu, but if you don’t have funds, it’s not sustainable"(P2, FG2). This statement is found in all three focus groups discussions with (P7) in FG3 supporting the views of others by stating that “…money is our limitation when it comes to planning and successfully executing the menu”(P7, FG2). This is a recurring theme: care workers are knowledgeable and committed, but they lack the resources to implement even basic nutritional planning. Research in resource-limited settings indicates that financial restrictions have a direct impact on the quality and diversity of meals in care and early development settings. For example, studies of early children development facilities in South Africa have found that irregular funding and limited budgets lead staff to replace cheaper foods or restrict meal variety, often affecting dietary quality despite their best intentions [46, 47]. Research in Australian childcare facilities shows that food budgets and cost demands impact menu planning and hinder adherence to nutrition requirements. Staff say that even well-designed meal planning tools can exceed budgets [48]. Similarly, systematic reviews of nutrition programme implementation in low- and middle-income contexts identify cost and inadequate resources as common barriers to providing nutritionally appropriate meals in institutional settings. Financial constraints are among the most frequently cited obstacles to effective meal planning and guideline adherence [37].

Insufficient financial and operational assistance can make even well-intentioned nutrition strategies impossible to implement. As a result, the study’s findings underscore the need for more sustainable and equitable funding models from relevant agencies, such as the Department of Social Development, to assist care facilities in meeting nutritional criteria and improving child health outcomes.

Sub-theme 2.2: availability of nutritious recipes

Participants unanimously highlighted the absence of diverse and cost-effective recipes. P5 in FG2 observed: "We do not have any recipes" (P5, FG2). In support of this claim, three participants from FG3 admitted that they did not have recipes. Participant 4 from FG3 echoed the statement:

“We do not follow recipes, we apply our skills”(P4, FG3). Many staff members relied on personal cooking skills, which while practical may not align with adherence to nutritional standards and potentially limit dietary diversity and nutrient adequacy for children in care. Similar challenges have been documented in institutional and childcare settings where a lack of standardised, nutritionally balanced recipes limits staff’s capacity to regularly provide healthy meals. The findings of the present study are consistent with existing literature on the nutritional adequacy of meals provided in childcare settings. Similar to the observations reported by Nzama and Napier [49] in registered childcare facilities in Inanda, KwaZulu-Natal, this study found that none of the childcare centres met at least 60% of the recommended daily requirements for energy, fibre, and calcium for children aged 2–5 years. These parallels reinforce the persistent concern regarding the nutritional insufficiency of menus offered in such settings. However, the current study extends this body of knowledge by providing additional empirical evidence from a different context, thereby underscoring the broader applicability of these findings and highlighting the ongoing gap between dietary guidelines and actual practice in early childhood nutrition. According to studies conducted in early childhood and institutional care settings, having access to easy, affordable recipes guides increasing menu variety, promotes adherence to nutritional standards, and boosts staff confidence in meal preparation [50], [51]. Furthermore, evidence from low-resource settings suggests that recipe guides adapted to local food availability and budgetary constraints are more likely to be embraced and followed than generic dietary guidelines [52]. The World Health Organization [53] acknowledges that practical tools, such as culturally relevant cookbooks and meal planning guidelines, are critical for implementing nutrition guidelines into practice. Providing standardised, inexpensive, and simple-to-prepare recipes matched to local food systems could thus improve meal preparation consistency, increase dietary diversity, and support nutrient adequacy in child care centres.

Sub-theme 2.3: food handling and preparation practices

Participants demonstrated a strong understanding of safe food handling practices, including handwashing and avoiding cross-contamination. P3 in FG3 emphasised: "Vegetables and meat need to be washed properly before preparation" (P3, FG3). The participants also raised the importance of running water in food handling and preparation by presenting the following narrative: “The kitchen requires clean running water”. On food preparation another participant (P4, FG1) expressed that: “Before cooking, meat should be checked; if it has an odour, it means that it is off and it needs to be thrown away” (P4, FG1). Despite this, the inability to source varied and nutritious ingredients limited the ability to translate food safety into balanced nutrition. These results suggest that while hygiene training may be adequate, additional support is needed to align food safety with improved dietary quality. Similar results have been found in institutional and childcare settings, where food handlers frequently demonstrate good awareness of hygiene and safety protocols, particularly hand hygiene and raw/cooked food separation [54], [55]. However, evidence reveals that strict food safety measures alone do not ensure nutritionally appropriate meals. In resource-constrained settings, inadequate access to water, diverse and nutritious ingredients can impair the ability to transform safe food handling into balanced and health-promoting meals [56]. According to research in low- and middle-income countries, food safety training is commonly prioritised above nutrition-sensitive meal preparation, resulting in hygienic but nutritionally monotonous meals [57]. This disparity highlights the need for integrated training methods that combine food safety, nutrition education, and menu planning. These findings further clearly support the need for outreach programming and training for staff on food safety handling and preparation practices [58]. The findings of this study reinforce and extend existing evidence that proper food handling practices play a critical role in reducing the risk of foodborne illness among high-risk populations. Consistent with the work of Reynolds and Rajagopal [59], the results confirm that adherence to safe food handling protocols remains a key determinant of food safety outcomes. However, this study further contributes new knowledge by demonstrating that the effectiveness of these practices is closely linked not only to awareness but also to the mode and consistency of food safety communication within the workplace. Specifically, the results highlight that while knowledge of food safety principles is necessary, it is insufficient on its own to ensure compliance. Instead, practical, context-based training, reinforced by clear written policies and visible food safety signage, significantly enhances understanding and consistent application of safe practices. This finding strengthens the argument made by Reynolds and Rajagopal [59] by providing empirical evidence that situational and continuous communication strategies are essential for translating knowledge into behavior. Strengthening support mechanisms, such as enhancing food procurement, budgeting, and access to different ingredients, will help to guarantee that appropriate hygienic practices are accompanied by increased dietary quality, thereby better addressing children’s dietary needs.

Sub-theme 2.4: food storage practices

Participants demonstrated a solid understanding of correct food storage practices. Statements such as “Food that requires a refrigerator must be stored in the refrigerator and canned food must be stored in a cool, dry place” (P6, FG1) indicate that basic safety protocols are followed. P4 in FG1 raised the issue of proper food storage and stock rotation during the conversation by saying, “Food should be consumed before the expiry date.” In addition, P11 in FG3 gave her perspective on other suitable food storage techniques articulating: “Food that needs to be vacuumed-packed must be stored in an airtight container. For a sugar bag, it is important to empty it into an airtight container once it is opened” (P4, FG1).

However, these efforts could be enhanced further by ongoing training and the provision of adequate storage infrastructure. This ensures not only safety but also preservation of nutrient value in stored foods. Previous research has shown that proper food storage is crucial not only for preventing foodborne illness but also for preserving the nutritional value of foods, notably fruits, vegetables, and animal-sourced products [60]. Inadequate storage conditions, such as inconsistent cooling or overcrowded storage areas, can hasten nutrient loss and jeopardise food quality, even if food handling techniques are otherwise acceptable. Research in childcare and institutional food service contexts has shown that knowledge alone is insufficient without adequate infrastructure, since limited refrigeration capacity and unpredictable electrical supply can undermine proper storage practices [61]. These findings imply that, while participants’ food storage knowledge is rather strong, continual refresher training and investment in proper storage facilities are required. Strengthening both human capability and physical resources will assure food safety while promoting nutrient retention, resulting in better dietary quality for children in CYCCs.

Theme 3: food safety and hygiene practices

This theme addresses the measures taken to ensure cleanliness and safety in food preparation. It has two sub-themes: Use of Personal and Kitchen Hygiene, and Personal Protective Equipment, highlighting daily practices that reduce the risk of contamination and promote safe food handling.

Sub-theme 3.1: personal and kitchen hygiene

Participants were mindful of hygiene, particularly handwashing. However, economic constraints again surfaced as a barrier. P1 in FG1 admitted, “.

Sometimes there is no money to buy soap, which reflects the ongoing resource constraints at the centre. The lack of basic supplies limits our ability to carry out everyday hygiene practices, despite knowing their importance for children’s health and wellbeing”(P1, FG1). Participants shared their opinions, emphasising personal hygiene by encouraging children to wash their hands before eating. P2 in FG1 expressed this viewpoint declaring: “We even encourage children and youths to wash their hands before collecting and eating food, but sometimes they wash without using handwashing soap” (P2, FG2).. Such findings reveal a systemic problem where even fundamental hygiene practices are vulnerable to budget shortfalls.

Evidence from institutional and childcare settings suggests that appropriate hygiene practices are critical for reducing infectious illnesses, especially among children with developing immune systems [1, 2]. However, studies conducted in low- and middle-income settings consistently show that insufficient funding and irregular supply of hygiene materials, such as soap, disinfectants, and protective equipment, undermine compliance with recommended hygiene standards, even when staff knowledge is adequate [62, 63]. In such situations, hygiene breaches are less a reflection of poor attitudes or neglect and more a result of structural and economical constraints. According to research, persistent changes in sanitation practices within institutional settings necessitate both behaviour-focused instruction and consistent availability of hygiene infrastructure and consumables [64] [1, 2]. Without regular provision of basic supplies, CYCCs may fail to fulfil minimum cleanliness standards, increasing children’s risk of preventable diseases. These findings highlight the need for increased institutional support and specific budget allocations to enable continuous access to key hygiene supplies, thereby protecting child health and well-being. The findings support earlier research by Lee [65] and Lee and Seo [66], which showed that training improves hygiene practices, as reflected in higher post-training checklist scores. This aligns with established evidence emphasizing the importance of structured sanitary management in food safety. However, the current study adds nuance by showing that not all areas improve equally,specifically, personal hygiene, ingredient control, and food storage did not show significant improvements after training. Furthermore, consistent with prior literature, demographic and facility-related factors such as regulatory registration, workers’ age and experience remain key determinants of hygiene practices, highlighting that training alone may be insufficient without considering these contextual influences.

Sub-theme 3.2: use of personal protective equipment

Awareness of the importance of Personal Protective Equipment (PPE) was evident, but actual usage was inconsistent due to financial constraints. P1 in FG3 commented, “A food handler must make sure that they are wearing a safety uniform in the kitchen, like safety shoes but we do not have these uniforms” (P1 in FG3), commented: “You must wear protective clothing such as an apron, safety clothes and shoes as part of the food safety protective equipment.” (P9 in FG2), While the theoretical understanding of PPE was high, its practical application was limited. This gap was observed as inadequate knowledge and practice, potentially exposing both food handlers and children to health risks such as food contamination and workplace injuries. Previous research showed that proper use of PPE, such as gloves, aprons, hairnets, and protective footwear, is critical in institutional kitchens to prevent physical injuries, reduce exposure to heat and sharp equipment, and reduce the risk of food contamination [67, 68], [69]. However, research in low-resource institutional and food service contexts repeatedly shows that PPE compliance is more strongly related to the availability of resources and institutional assistance than to individual willingness [70, 71]. Where PPE is not frequently provided, food workers may emphasise fundamental tasks over safety precautions, even being aware of the risks involved. Furthermore, evidence suggests that consistent PPE use necessitates both training and organisational commitment, including budgetary allocation and safety standard enforcement [72]. Without institutional investment in protective equipment as part of core operations resources, food handlers will be unable to transform knowledge into safe practice. These findings highlight the importance of CYCC management and relevant authorities incorporating PPE into everyday food service operations to protect personnel’s well-being and maintain food hygiene requirements.

Conclusion and recommendations

This study examined food, nutrition, and hygiene practices amongst food handlers in Children and Youth Care Centres (CYCCs) and revealed a persistent gap between knowledge and practice. While participants demonstrated general awareness of dietary guidelines, food safety principles, and hygiene requirements, the findings show that this knowledge is not consistently translated into practice. Key barriers included limited financial resources, inadequate and irregular training, and insufficient infrastructure, particularly in relation to food storage, kitchen facilities, and access to personal protective equipment (PPE). The study further found that challenges related to meal planning, the use of nutritious recipes, and adherence to hygiene protocols are largely systematic rather than individual, highlighting the constrained environments within which food handlers operate. Variations in educational levels and years of experience also contributed to inconsistencies in the application of practices, with some participants relying on experiential knowledge rather than standardised guidelines.

From a policy perspective, the study underscores the need for stronger institutional support and regulatory oversight to ensure that CYCCs meet minimum nutrition and hygiene standards. Policymakers, particularly within the Department of Social Development, should prioritise developing and enforcing clear guidelines that integrate nutrition, food safety, and child care standards. There is also a need for sustainable and equitable funding mechanisms to support the procurement of nutritious foods, hygiene supplies, and essential kitchen equipment. Investment in infrastructure, such as adequate storage facilities and refrigeration, is critical to safeguarding food quality and safety. At a programme level, organisations managing CYCCs should implement continuous, context-specific training programmes for food handlers, focusing on practical skills in meal planning, safe food handling, and hygiene practices, rather than one-off training interventions.

For practice and academic, the study highlights the importance of developing culturally appropriate, affordable, and easy-to-use resources, such as standardised recipe guides aligned with the South African Food-Based Dietary Guidelines (SAFBDGs) and including locally available foods. These tools can support more consistent and nutritionally balanced meal preparation in resource-constrained settings. In addition, there is a need for stronger collaboration between academic institutions, government, and CYCCs to support capacity building, knowledge transfer, and evidence based intervations. Future research should expand to include a wider range of CYCCs across different geographical contexts to enhance the generalisatbility of findings, and should also assess the long-term impact of targeted nutrition and hygiene interventions on child health outcomes. Overall, the study contributes to the growing body of knowledge on institutional food environments and reinforces the need for integrated, multi-level strategies that address both individual competencies and structural constraints.
