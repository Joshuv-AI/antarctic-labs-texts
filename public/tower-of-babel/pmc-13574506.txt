Ventricular pulsed field ablation from bench to bedside: preclinical and clinical evidence from a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Paolo Compagnucci, Luca Finori, Michela Casella, Alessio Gasperetti, Henry D Huang, Yari Valeri, Leonardo D’Angelo, Francesca Campanelli, Giorgio Giacomini, Giovanni Volpato, Laura Cipolletta, Quintino Parisi, Luigi Di Biase, Andrea Natale, Pasquale Santangeli, Antonio Dello Russo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsEvidence supporting the use of pulsed field ablation (PFA) for ventricular arrhythmia (VA) ablation remains limited and heterogeneous, and the relationship between preclinical lesion characteristics and clinical outcomes is incompletely defined. We sought to systematically summarize preclinical lesion metrics and clinical outcomes of ventricular PFA.Methods and resultsWe performed a systematic review and meta-analysis of preclinical and clinical studies of ventricular PFA. Preclinical studies reporting lesion depth or transmurality and clinical studies detailing procedural efficacy and safety were included. Random-effects models were used to pool lesion characteristics and clinical outcomes. Seventy-five studies were included (33 preclinical, 43 clinical; 355 patients). In preclinical models, mean lesion depth was 5.96 mm(95%CI, 5.07–6.84) in healthy endocardium and 5.19 mm (95%CI, 2.58–7.79) in healthy epicardium. Notably, lesion depth was quantitatively similar in models of scarred myocardium (5.96 mm; 95%CI, 5.39–6.53). Lesion transmurality was variable in healthy tissues (32%; 95%CI, 15–52%) but higher within scar (53%; 95%CI, 36–70%). Consistently, lesion depth was similar between PFA and RF in healthy myocardium, but greater with PFA in scarred models (within-study mean depth difference, 2.09 mm; 95%CI, 0.84–3.34). Greater contact force, repeated applications, and higher energy/duration settings were associated with increased lesion depth. Clinically, acute success was high for both premature ventricular complexes (PVCs,90%; 95%CI, 83–96%) and ventricular tachycardia (VT;92%; 95%CI, 87–96%) in studies with ≥3patients. Over a mean follow-up of approximately 5months, recurrence rates were 19% (95%CI, 12–28%) for PVCs and 21% (95%CI, 10–35%) for VT. The overall complication rate was 10% (95%CI, 5–17%) in studies with ≥3patients, with coronary artery spasm and conduction disturbances representing the most frequent adverse events.ConclusionVentricular PFA demonstrates high acute efficacy and encouraging mid-term outcomes, supported by preclinical evidence of favourable lesion penetration within scarred myocardium. Safety considerations-particularly coronary and conduction system effects-and the heterogeneity of preclinical evidence highlight the need for further investigation.Keywords: Pulsed field ablation, Ventricular tachycardia, Premature ventricular complexes, Catheter ablation, Meta-analysis, Safety, Recurrence, Electroporation

Introduction

Catheter ablation is increasingly recommended to improve symptoms and reduce implantable cardioverter-defibrillator shocks and arrhythmia recurrence in patients with ventricular arrhythmias (VAs).1–3

Conventional catheter ablation relies on radiofrequency energy (RF), with effective lesion formation critically depending on adequate catheter-tissue contact, appropriate power and irrigation settings, and tissue impedance.2,4,5 In thick ventricular myocardial regions and in substrates characterized by extensive scarring or intramural location, achieving durable and deep lesions remains challenging.2,5,6 These limitations may translate into an increased risk of post-ablation arrhythmia recurrence, which persists even in the contemporary era.5,7

Pulsed field ablation (PFA) is an emerging energy source characterized by a minimally thermal profile and relative cardiomyocyte selectivity, potentially expanding safety margins near non-target tissues and mitigating heat-related complications, such as steam pops.8–10

Rapid adoption of PFA in atrial fibrillation ablation has stimulated interest in ventricular applications, particularly given emerging data suggesting greater lesion depth compared to thermal energy sources.11,12

However, despite the potential utility of PFA for the treatment of VAs, its real-world use remains limited and at an early stage of adoption, being largely confined to expert centres, as highlighted by a 2024 survey in which PFA was reported to be rarely used for ventricular tachycardia (VT) ablation, accounting for only 1.7% of respondents.13 Furthermore, the current evidence base for ventricular PFA remains fragmented, consisting largely of first-in-human experiences, small case series, and registries employing heterogeneous systems and energy delivery protocols.11 Moreover, the relationship between preclinical lesion characteristics, particularly lesion depth and transmurality, and clinical outcomes also remains incompletely defined.

In this systematic review and meta-analysis, we aimed to summarize the available evidence from preclinical studies of PFA in ventricular settings and to synthesize clinical data on acute safety, procedural effectiveness, and mid-term arrhythmia recurrence following ventricular PFA.

Methods

Study design and registration

We performed a systematic review and meta-analysis of preclinical and clinical studies evaluating ventricular PFA. Study protocols were registered as follows: the clinical component in PROSPERO (ID: CRD420251138923) and the preclinical component in the Open Science Framework (DOI: 10.17605/OSF.IO/TZ28H). The present manuscript was reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) statement.14

Eligibility criteria

Preclinical studies included animal models of ventricular PFA reporting lesion depth or transmurality assessed by pathology. In the absence of randomized controlled trials evaluating ventricular PFA, eligible clinical studies included non-randomized comparative studies, single-arm registries, and case reports or series detailing at least one outcome of interest in patients with premature ventricular complexes (PVCs) and/or VT undergoing ventricular PFA. Conference abstracts without full data were excluded.

Search strategy and study selection

MEDLINE/PubMed, Scopus, medRxiv, and ClinicalTrials.gov were searched from inception to 10 October 2025. The full search strategy is provided in the Supplementary Methods. Reference lists of eligible studies were manually screened. Duplicate records were removed using Rayyan and Zotero. Two reviewers (L.F. and P.C.) independently screened titles and abstracts and subsequently assessed full texts for eligibility; disagreements were resolved by consensus or third-party adjudication (M.C.). Data extraction

For preclinical studies, extracted variables included animal species, ventricular chamber, procedural approach (endocardial or epicardial), dosing parameters, lesion depth, and transmurality. When studies reported medians with interquartile ranges or ranges, means and standard deviations were derived using established methods (Luo et al.15; Wan et al.16)

For pooling of lesion depth and transmurality, when multiple contact force settings were reported, we prioritized adequate-contact conditions, defined as contact force ≥10 g, in accordance with the HRS expert consensus on catheter ablation of VAs.2 The effects of contact, number of applications, and amplitude/duration settings were further explored using within study comparisons between high and low settings, when feasible.

For clinical studies, extracted data included study design, sample size, patient demographics, arrhythmia type, procedural approach, ablation system, acute procedural success, intraprocedural or ≤30-day adverse events, follow-up duration, and arrhythmia recurrence. When available, individual patient-level data were averaged; otherwise, aggregated data were extracted.

Outcomes

For the preclinical component, the primary outcomes were lesion depth and transmurality assessed on pathology. For the clinical component, primary outcomes were: (i) acute success (PVC elimination and any-clinical and non-clinical-VT non-inducibility), (ii) procedure-related complications occurring intra-procedurally or within 30 days, and (iii) arrhythmia recurrence during follow-up.

Risk of bias assessment

Clinical studies were appraised using Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) critical appraisal tools (Prevalence, Case Series, and Case Reports). Preclinical studies were assessed using the SYRCLE risk-of-bias tool and ARRIVE 2.0 guidelines. Formal assessment of publication bias (e.g. funnel plots or Egger's test) was not performed. For meta-analyses of proportions, these methods have recognized methodological limitations because they were developed primarily for comparative effect measures.17,18 In addition, the small number of studies available for the comparative analyses precluded meaningful assessment of publication bias.17,18

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables (lesion depth) were pooled using inverse variance weighted random-effects models with restricted maximum likelihood (REML) estimation. As a sensitivity analysis, lesion depth was additionally analysed on the logarithmic scale to assess the robustness of the findings and the impact of the skewed distribution of a strictly positive variable.

Proportions were pooled using inverse-variance weighted random-effects models after variance stabilization with the Freeman–Tukey double arcsine transformation. Pooled estimates were back-transformed for presentation. Between-study heterogeneity was quantified using the I2 statistic.

Pre-specified subgroup analyses were conducted for both preclinical and clinical datasets. Given the early-stage nature of ventricular PFA, all available clinical evidence was included in the main analysis; however, sensitivity analyses restricted to studies with ≥3 patients were performed to enhance robustness. Additionally, leave-one-out analyses were performed in the presence of substantial heterogeneity (I2 > 50%).

Within-study mean differences in lesion depth were calculated for: (i) high vs. low catheter–tissue contact, (ii) high vs. low number of applications, (iii) high vs. low energy settings, and (iv) PFA vs. RF, when both modalities were compared within the same study. Mean differences were defined as high minus low for parameter-based comparisons and as PFA minus RF for energy source comparisons. Study-specific mean differences and corresponding standard errors were calculated and pooled using random-effects models. For transmurality, proportions were extracted for each comparison group, and pooled analyses were performed using random-effects models. Between-group differences were summarized as pooled risk differences with 95% confidence intervals.

All tests were two-sided, with statistical significance defined as P < 0.05. Analyses were performed using the meta package in R (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Clinical population

Among 355 patients, 122(34.4%) underwent PFA for PVCs and 233 (65.6%) for VT (n = 225) or VF (n = 8). The weighted mean age was 58 years (range 27–78), and 86 patients (24%) were women. Among VT/VF cases, the underlying substrate was ischaemic cardiomyopathy in 115 patients (49%), non-ischaemic cardiomyopathy in 82(35%), mixed (ischaemic and valvular) in 1(0.4%), and unspecified structural heart disease in 22(9%), while the remaining 13 patients(6%) had idiopathic VT/VF; the procedural approach was epi-endocardial in 38(16%) patients, and epicardial in 3(1%) cases.4,48,52–57,59–71,76–86,92 Among PVC cases, idiopathic PVCs accounted for most cases (n = 9 679%), followed by non-ischaemic (n = 2 016%) and ischaemic cardiomyopathy(n = 6,5%); the procedural approach was epi-endocardial in 2(2%) cases and involved ablation within the coronary venous system in 16(13%).58,72–75,80–82,84,85,87–92 PFA was used in redo procedures in 42 PVC cases (34%) and 142 VT cases (61%). Hybrid PFA/RF workflows were common (n = 142 overall; n = 97 for VT/VF; n = 45 for PVC).

A detailed description of epi-endocardial/epicardial procedures and of cases involving ablation within the coronary venous system is provided in the Supplementary Results.

Safety

Procedure-related complications were reported in 63 patients overall (see Supplementary material online, Table S5). Of these, 59 occurred within 1 day of the procedure, whereas 9 were reported between Days 1 and 30. The most common complication was ST-segment changes, occurring in 16 patients (5%), followed by transient (n = 11, 3%)/persistent (n = 3, 1%) conduction system disorders, vascular access complications (n = 8, 2%), stroke/thromboembolic events (n = 5, 1%), and heart failure exacerbation/cardiogenic shock (n = 5, 1%). ST-segment changes occurred during epicardial PFA application in nine cases, after ablation within the middle cardiac vein in one patient (see Supplementary Results), and during endocardial ablation at the left/right ventricular outflow tract (n = 3), LV anterior wall (n = 2), and RV moderator band (n = 1), suggesting that proximity-related coronary artery spasm may have contributed to most cases.

The proportion meta-analysis of all clinical studies showed a 4% (95%CI, 0–10%; I2 = 0%) rate of any procedure-related complications (see Supplementary material online, Figure S28). Restricting the analysis to studies with ≥3 patients yielded a complication rate of 10% (95%CI, 5–17%; I2 = 39%; Figure 6). Notably, the complication rate appeared lower with the pentaspline catheter (0%; 95%CI, 0–3%; I2 = 0%) and with the PFA generator coupled with irrigated catheters (3%; 95%CI, 0–19%; I2 = 21%) compared to the lattice-tip catheter (14%; 95%CI, 6–24%; I2 = 0%; Supplementary material online, Figure S29).

Risk of bias

Preclinical

Using SYRCLE and ARRIVE 2.0 tools (see Supplementary material online, Table S9), the overall risk of bias was moderate, primarily driven by frequent ‘unclear’ ratings for randomization, allocation concealment, and blinding, as well as incomplete reporting (sample-size justification, exclusions). Additional concerns included heterogeneity in catheter/waveform parameters, tissue conditions, and survival-to-histology intervals.

Clinical

The first-in-human VCAS study and the AVAAR registry were judged to have low-to-moderate risk of bias (see Supplementary material online, Table S10), with concerns related to small sample size and early-feasibility design (VCAS),48 as well as non-consecutive enrolment and short follow-up (AVAAR).85 Among case series/reports, quality was variable, ranging from moderate to high (see Supplementary material online, Tables S11 and S12). The main limitations included unclear inclusion criteria, heterogeneous endpoint definitions, and limited handling of missing data/confounding.

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis provide a comprehensive evaluation of ventricular PFA, integrating preclinical lesion metrics with emerging clinical outcomes across a range of PFA systems and dosing strategies.11 The main results can be summarized as follows.

First, preclinical data demonstrate that lesion depth associated with ventricular PFA is generally consistent regardless of the presence of scar.26,27 Furthermore, PFA produces lesion depths comparable to RF in healthy myocardium, whereas in scarred ventricular models PFA achieves greater lesion depth than RF. Nonetheless, transmural lesion formation is not universally achieved with endocardial-only or epicardial-only PFA applications. Transmurality appears more likely in scarred myocardium and within the RV endocardium compared with the LV.23 These observations suggest that, based on current evidence, endocardial-only PFA is unlikely to eliminate the need for endo-epicardial procedures when targeting complex, intramural or subepicardial substrates.2,67,93

Second, early clinical experience demonstrates high acute success rates in complex and selected patient populations, frequently including a substantial proportion of redo procedures.85 Mid-term outcomes are encouraging, particularly considering that most available data derive from technologies originally designed for atrial ablation.8,12,48,85 However, complication rates are not negligible, particularly when PFA is compared with contemporary RF ablation technologies,5 with additional PFA-specific considerations.85

Preclinical data

Across experimental models, mean lesion depth with PFA is approximately 6 mm, translating into variable rates of lesion transmurality (Figures 2–4). Our exploratory direct comparative analysis demonstrated similar lesion depth between PFA and RF in healthy myocardium,20,22,40,41,44,50,94 whereas PFA produced significantly deeper lesions in scarred ventricular models.22,25,40 Although based on a limited number of heterogeneous studies, these findings suggest a potential advantage of PFA for targeting deep intramural substrates in scar-related VT.22,48

Mechanistically, while fibrotic tissue may limit conductive heating during RF delivery, it may conversely facilitate the distribution of PFA electric fields towards surviving myocyte bundles within heterogeneous scar.22,40,95 This property may enhance lesion effectiveness in regions that are traditionally challenging to treat with thermal energy.22,40,95

Importantly, the high heterogeneity in preclinical lesion thickness, resulting in wide prediction intervals, suggests that differences among ablation systems have a major influence on ventricular lesion formation and penetration. These differences involve not only catheter design (e.g. circumferential regional catheters developed for pulmonary vein isolation vs. large-tip focal or focal devices that may be better suited for ventricular navigation), but also PFA waveform characteristics, pulse parameters, and energy dosing strategies.8,10,11 Accordingly, the reported lesion metrics should not be generalized to PFA as a technology per se, but are instead platform-, system-, and dose-specific, representing an important caveat for clinical application (Table 1, Supplementary material online, Table S2).8,10,11 Whereas some systems use catheters originally designed for RF ablation and subsequently adapted for PFA delivery,75,92 others have been specifically developed for PFA applications, such as the high-voltage system, which was optimized for ventricular use.48 Substantial variability also exists in catheter features. Some systems retain saline irrigation, such as dual-energy devices and the high-voltage system, whereas others do not require irrigation.22,26 While irrigation is essential for lesion formation and safety during RF ablation and may mitigate residual thermal effects associated with some PFA systems,96 its impact on PFA lesion formation remains incompletely understood.

As with RF ablation, several ablation parameters can be adjusted to increase lesion depth. Some evidence suggests that catheter-tissue proximity is sufficient for effective lesion formation,21 whereas other studies have reported an association between increasing contact force and greater lesion depth.32–34,37,39,42,46 The number of PFA applications20,31,36,47,49 and voltage/duration settings29,38,49 also seem to represent important determinants of lesion thickness. These variables may be combined into composite lesion indices,32 although their utility in the ventricular setting remains to be established. Additional strategies to augment lesion depth include bipolar PFA, in which energy is delivered between two catheters positioned across the myocardial wall,24,39 and RF/PFA stacking, which involves the sequential, colocalized application of RF and PFA using dual-energy devices.50 This approach may enhance lesion depth through an RF-mediated increase in local tissue conductivity, a phenomenon that appears to be driven by the rise in tissue temperature following RF application.50,97 While promising, these approaches require further validation, particularly in view of the potential risks associated with deeper or more extensive myocardial injury.24

Clinical data

Despite advances in mapping and ablation strategies, recurrence after VA ablation remains substantial, with one-year freedom from recurrence approximating 60–80% for VT2,5 and 70–80% for PVCs.2,98 This suboptimal long-term efficacy has been attributed to several factors, including incomplete mapping, incomplete substrate/trigger elimination, and substrate progression.2,6 In this context, PFA may offer a complementary approach for improving substrate modification, particularly in deep myocardial substrates or within scar. Nonetheless, insufficient lesion depth in healthy myocardium, inadequate target identification, and lesion regression due to reversible electroporation may represent significant issues with current technologies adopted for ventricular PFA.

Lesion reversibility appears particularly relevant, as both preclinical studies and a case report describing the histologic lesion analysis of an explanted heart after ventricular pentaspline PFA have shown that ablation lesions are characterized by a central irreversibly electroporated core surrounded by a zone of reversible injury, which may recover conduction over time.29,36,37,52 This phenomenon may have clinical implications for acute procedural endpoints, particularly the elimination of abnormal local electrograms and acute suppression of PVCs or non-inducibility of VT. Accordingly, preclinical studies have shown that the immediate post-ablation reduction in local electrogram amplitude does not correlate with lesion depth,27,35 while studies employing serial post-ablation electroanatomical mapping43 or magnetic resonance imaging have demonstrated a reduction in ablation lesion volume beyond the first 24 h.35,49 A similar pattern has also been reported in a clinical study of PVC ablation using a focal PFA system.90 Our meta-analysis confirms and expands these findings by reporting a discrepancy between the almost universal acute procedural success and the non-negligible risk of VA recurrence during mid-term follow-up, a finding that was particularly pronounced for the PFA generator coupled with irrigated catheters.92 Therefore, the optimal post-ablation waiting time for assessing acute procedural success with individual technologies should be better delineated as ventricular PFA enters the clinical arena. Whether conventional acute endpoints for VT ablation remain equally informative after PFA, or whether complementary strategies—for instance, more widespread use of post-ablation non-invasive programmed ventricular stimulation—may improve assessment of procedural efficacy, should be further investigated in future studies.2

Notwithstanding this issue, the high acute success and favourable follow-up outcomes observed in this meta-analysis should be interpreted in light of the complexity of the treated population, with 34% of PVC cases and 61% of VT cases representing redo procedures after prior RF ablation. The continuous development of dedicated PFA or dual-energy tools specifically for targeting ventricular tissue may further enhance procedural efficacy.48,89,90

From a safety perspective, the overall complication rate of approximately 10% in larger studies is consistent with available data on RF ablation.2 However, coronary events emerge as a specific concern during ventricular PFA procedures, particularly during epicardial ablation.99 While nitrate pre-treatment may mitigate acute spasm, the long-term consequences of PFA applications in proximity to coronary arteries remain incompletely defined and warrant further investigation.71,100

In addition, although the effects of PFA on the specialized cardiac conduction system are incompletely understood, the observed rates of transient/persistent conduction disturbances suggest that caution should be applied when delivering PFA near the atrioventricular node.24,75 A single study reported that the risk of atrioventricular block may be particularly high when using a linear catheter(ThermoCool-SmartTouch) coupled with a PFA generator via a retrograde-aortic approach due to current leakage from the ring electrode, highlighting the importance of technical factors.92 In most other reported cases, however, conduction disturbances were attributed to PFA delivery in close proximity to the cardiac conduction system rather than to energy shunting, suggesting that anatomical location may represent the predominant mechanism.

Finally, although preclinical evidence supporting colocalized, sequential application of RF and PFA remains limited,50 hybrid workflows with RF/PFA stacking are increasingly reported in the literature, largely driven by the availability of dual-energy platforms.85 The relative safety and efficacy of RF-only, PFA-only, and combined approaches for ventricular arrhythmia ablation should be systematically evaluated in future studies.

Limitations

These findings should be interpreted in light of several limitations. Preclinical evidence is characterized by substantial heterogeneity in ablation platforms, energy delivery protocols, and the timing and methods used for lesion assessment. However, rigorous sensitivity analyses, including leave-one-out analyses and log-transformed meta-analyses, demonstrated that the findings were robust to both the exclusion of individual studies and the statistical approach used. Clinical data are limited by small sample sizes and relatively short-follow-up durations. Accordingly, differences in safety and efficacy outcomes across individual ablation systems should be considered hypothesis-generating. Notably, the systematic differences in efficacy and safety outcomes observed between overall estimates and those restricted to cohorts with ≥3 patients suggest the possibility of a small-study effect. This may reflect a combination of selection bias, publication bias (preferential reporting of technical successes), and the involvement of high-volume, pioneering centres. Standardization of procedural parameters, lesion assessment methodologies, and clinical endpoints will be essential to better define the role of PFA in VA ablation. Future studies will need to further address several open questions for ventricular PFA, including the impact of catheter irrigation and catheter design-especially regarding the comparison between large-focal and conventional focal catheters-PFA dosing, PFA/PFA or RF/PFA stacking, and the value of conventional acute procedural endpoints.

Conclusions

Ventricular PFA demonstrates high acute efficacy, acceptable near-term safety, and consistent mid-term durability across both PVC and VT ablation. Preclinical lesion metrics provide a mechanistic rationale for these findings, suggesting favourable penetration of PFA within scarred myocardium, where it appears to achieve greater lesion depth than RF, and emphasizing the role of PFA dosing. Future studies should systematically validate strategies to mitigate PFA-specific complications-such as coronary artery spasm-in VA ablation procedures, and to further define the safety and efficacy of combined RF/PFA approaches, including lesion stacking.
