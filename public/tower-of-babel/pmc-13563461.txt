Sound Exposure in Total Joint Arthroplasty: Are All Operating Room Personnel at Risk?
Authors: Matthew T. Hoffa, Justin M. Lapow, Rachael Tolsma, Abbey E. DeBruin, David M. Rossi, Brian T. Nickel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundA hearing conservation program is recommended by the Occupational Safety and Health Administration for prolonged exposure exceeding 85 dB. Arthroplasty surgeons often experience levels above this threshold. There is a paucity of literature regarding sound exposure for all arthroplasty operating room (OR) personnel. This study directly assesses and compares sound exposure levels for all OR personnel in total joint arthroplasty.MethodsSound data were recorded for 20 total hip arthroplasty (THA) and 20 total knee arthroplasty (TKA) cases. Recording devices collected sound data for the 5 primary OR roles—primary surgeon, first assistant, surgical technologist, anesthesia staff, and circulating nurse. T-test and analysis of variance were completed to compare between case type and OR personnel, respectively.ResultsAverage sound level exceeded 85 dB for the primary physician in hip and knee arthroplasty. Sound exposure in THA commonly exceeded that of TKA. The primary surgeon experienced significantly higher average sound level than all other OR personnel in TKA and THA (P < .01). The primary surgeon also experienced a significantly higher percentage of total time with sound exceeding 90 dB relative to other OR personnel (P < .0001).ConclusionsAll total joint arthroplasty OR personnel, including anesthesia and nursing, may be exposed to some degree of potentially dangerous sound. This risk is most notable for direct operative staff, and significantly higher for the primary surgeon. Our data suggests hearing protection is indicated by the Occupational Safety and Health Administration standards for the primary surgeon; however, all OR personnel would likely benefit based on our measured exposures.

Introduction

Orthopaedic surgeons and operating room (OR) personnel have many occupational hazards, one of which is noise-induced hearing loss (NIHL). NIHL occurs when one is exposed to repeated or prolonged high noise levels, resulting in permanent, untreatable damage to the nerve endings in the ear [1]. The Occupation Safety and Health Administration (OSHA) requires employers to develop a hearing conservation program when employees are exposed to sound levels ˃85 dB for an average of 8 h per day [1]. Interestingly, some states recommend that hearing conservation programs are initiated for average sound levels ˃80 dB [2]. Hearing protection programs are not currently implemented routinely for orthopaedic surgeons.

Orthopaedic surgeons have been found to have 3.7 increased odds NIHL when compared to nonsurgical colleagues, with another study showing about 50% of orthopaedic staff suffered from hearing loss [3,4]. During total joint arthroplasty (TJA), intraoperative sound recordings can reach over 100 dB, and arthroplasty surgeons have been shown to exceed the recommended time-weighted average noise levels set by OSHA [5,6]. Studies have shown that the sound exposure also exceeds recommended levels in robot-assisted TJA, with some studies suggesting the robotic-assisted cases, or those with automated tools, have higher levels of sound exposure [[7], [8], [9], [10]]. Given this mounting evidence, several studies have recommended the implementation of a noise protection plan for orthopaedic surgeons [5,11,12]. Although noise protection is recommended to prevent NIHL, there is a fine balance between sufficient hearing protection and the need for effective verbal communication among staff in the OR. Unsurprisingly, previous literature has demonstrated inconsistent hearing protection recommendations for this reason [13,14].

Current studies investigating sound exposure in TJA have generally focused on sound exposure of the primary surgeon, with few also including the assistant. Given that there are many vital members of the surgical team who could potentially be at risk for dangerous levels of sound exposure, we sought to investigate the sound exposure levels for all OR personnel. The objective of our study was to determine the level of sound exposure for all OR personnel in TJA ORs in order to inform hearing protection recommendations at our institution. As such, we assessed sound exposure levels in 20 THA and 20 total knee arthroplasty (TKA) cases for the surgeon, first assist, surgical technologist, nursing staff, and anesthesia staff. We hypothesized that all OR personnel would be exposed to potentially dangerous levels of sound during TJA.

Material and methods

The study was conducted at a single tertiary referral center, and included cases from 1 fellowship-trained adult reconstruction orthopaedic surgeon. Sound data were collected from a total of 20 THA and 20 TKA cases. Of the cases included, 18/20 TKA cases were completed manually with 2 completed utilizing an image-guided burr robotic system. All THA cases were completed manually without use of automated impaction devices. An OSHA-compliant sound recording device was utilized to obtain sound exposure data from each case. A total of 5 recording devices were used for each case, spread between the primary surgeon (attending surgeon), the first assistant (APP or resident), the surgical technologist, the anesthesia work area, and the nursing station. The primary (attending) surgeon was responsible for final implant placement, broaching, reaming, and saw use in the majority of cases. The recording device microphones were attached to the inside of the surgical hood of the operative team and on the workstation of the anesthesia personnel and circulating nurse in their typical locations (Fig. 1). All cases were completed in ORs of equal dimension, measuring 25′× 25′ with 11′ ceilings. OR walls are metal, though covered with drywall and a vinyl type covering. Bed location, as well as anesthesia and nursing stations were consistent throughout cases. Recordings began at the time of surgical site sterile preparation and were completed after bandages were applied for all but the attending surgeon who occasionally left at time of superficial closure. Recording data were configured automatically by the sound recording device software. Nursing staff periodically monitored the nursing station sound recorder during key portions of the cases to identify peak sound generators.Figure 1Sound recording locations. Sound recording locations. (a) In the surgical hood of the primary surgeon, assistant, and surgical technologist; (b) nursing station; (c) anesthesia station.

Data analysis was completed utilizing both Microsoft Excel and GraphPad Prism 10.6.1 digital statistics software. Basic statistics were completed to analyze trends in sound data for each type of surgery among the different OR personnel. The GraphPad Prism software was utilized to complete t-test comparisons of sound data between type of surgery while analysis of variance was utilized to compare among OR personnel.

The study was reviewed by the University’s Institutional Review Board and was deemed to be Institutional Review Board exempt as part of an internal Quality Improvement project, a type of minimal risk research. No informed consent was required for this study.

Results

The primary surgeon (attending) was exposed to an average sound level of 89.1 dB during TKA and 89.3 dB during THA. The average sound level experienced by all OR personnel exceeded 80 dB for both THA and TKA cases (Tables 1 and 2). Average sound was higher for THA than TKA for all OR personnel except for the surgical technologist, in which case sound levels were nearly identical for THA and TKA. The higher average sound level in THA relative to TKA was only significant for the surgical assistant (P < .0001) and anesthesia work station (P < .007). The average amount of time which sound exceeded 90 dB was significantly higher in THA than TKA for the surgical assistant (P < .0001). Sound exposure level notably exceeded 100 dB for all OR personnel. The primary surgeon experienced sound exposure levels exceeding 120 dB during THA and TKA cases. The direct operative staff including the assistant surgeon and surgical technologist both experienced sound exposure level >115 dB for THA and >110 dB for TKA (Tables 1 and 2).

The primary surgeon was exposed to the highest degree of sound on average. Relative to all other OR personnel the primary surgeon had significantly higher average sound level in both THA and TKA (P < .0001) (Fig. 2). The primary/attending surgeon also experienced significantly higher average sound exposure level than all other OR personnel in both TKA (P < .0001) and THA (P < .003). In addition, the primary surgeon spent a higher amount of time exposed to >90 dB of sound relative to all other OR personnel during TKA and THA cases (P < .0001).Figure 2Average sound exposure based on surgical role. Overall, sound levels demonstrated a vast range with sound levels ˂70 dB, as well as >100 dB. The average case duration from draping to skin closure for TKA was 63 min, and 60 min for THA, while the primary surgeon was present for 38 min for TKA and 30 min for THA on average. As such, the primary/attending surgeon experienced, on average, sounds ˃90 dB for more than 4 min per TKA and more than 3.5 min per THA.

Discussion

Unsafe levels of sound exposure are common in the TJA OR. Orthopaedic surgeons have an increased risk of hearing loss relative to their nonsurgical colleagues. We set out to assess the level of sound exposure for all OR personnel in TJA cases to inform hearing protection recommendations at our institution. Our study provides novel data regarding the sound exposure of nonoperative OR personnel in TJA. We found that while all OR personnel experience some degree of potentially dangerous sound, the primary (attending) surgeon experienced significantly higher average sound exposure than all other OR personnel. An OSHA hearing conservation program should be considered for TJA ORs with the primary surgeon being the most clearly indicated team member; however, all personnel are likely to benefit given peak sound levels.

The risk of sound exposure to the primary (attending) surgeon in TJA is well established. Our findings align well with the literature demonstrating risk to the attending surgeon in TJA cases. Unique to our study is the inclusion of all OR personnel. Previous studies rarely include risk profile of nonoperative personnel such as surgical technologists, nursing staff, and anesthesia staff [13,15]. We quantified sound exposure using standard audiometry measurements. We included average sound level, the mean dB level of an event; as well as sound exposure level, a standardized measure of total sound energy of an event expressed as a single dB value [16]. As expected, the primary surgeon in our cases experienced the greatest average level of sound exposure. With that being said, all OR personnel experienced average sound ˃80 dB with overall sound exposure levels exceeding 100 dB for each. The surgical technologist is a previously unstudied participant that experienced average sound >85 dB during THA. Anesthesia and nursing staff do experience average sound exceeding 80 dB, comparable to a garbage disposal or street traffic, which in states such as California qualifies for initiation of a hearing conservation program [2]. With that being said, their exposure does fall below the average sound level OSHA indicates for a hearing conservation program (85 dB). Regardless, our data demonstrate that all OR personnel in TJA cases are exposed to potentially dangerous sound levels. This information is important for counseling of OR personnel and for the development of appropriate hearing conservation programs.

Average sound exposure was almost always higher in THA than TKA. This is likely due to the large force needed to seat acetabular components and the repetitive, significant sound from femoral broaching. Our data showed that the surgical technologist had higher average levels of sound exposure than the primary assistant during THA and TKA. This finding may be due to positioning of the assistant during the case relative to the surgical site and tools. For example, in THA the assistant is typically across the table from the surgeon, while the surgical technologist is more commonly closely positioned to the primary surgeon. Although our sound data was not linear, we did have nursing staff monitoring our nursing station sound recorders and it was noted that the noisiest parts of THA were cup impaction and broaching, while for TKA it was the femoral and tibial resections and final implant impaction. If stratified by type of arthroplasty, our data suggest that a hearing conservation should be more strongly considered for cases of THA given the average sound exposure of all operative personnel. Further, for the OR support staff, such as nursing and anesthesia staff, selective use of hearing protection during cup impaction and broaching for THA, and bony resections and implant impaction for TKA is most advisable. Based upon the results of this study, hearing protection is now worn by the majority of arthroplasty surgeons and assistants at our institution.

Overall time spent experiencing >90 dB sound was highest for the primary/attending surgeon, with exposure for 11.1% of the case for TKA and 11.8% for THA. Although the percentage of time is relatively high, these cases are completed quickly, and as such, this equates to <5 min per case in our sample. With that being said, TJA can be optimized in ambulatory and outpatient surgery centers to allow for 8+ cases per day in some settings. As such, the total daily exposure to potentially dangerous levels of sound is quite significant. More importantly, surgeons must consider the cumulative impact of this sound exposure over the course of years and how it pertains to their longevity and long-term hearing health. This is not only the case for the primary surgeon, but also for all OR personnel who are regularly present for TJA cases.

Important to note, 90% (18/20) of the TKA, and all of the THA cases included in this study were completed manually. Automated impactors were not used for any of the included cases. There is growing evidence that the use of robotic arthroplasty technique, particularly in TKA, is associated with significantly higher average levels of sound exposure [5,10]. The difference is especially notable with the use of the Mako system, a widely commercially available robotic platform used frequently in TJA [17]. In our case, our average sound exposure reached potentially dangerous levels utilizing almost exclusively manual techniques, and as such, it may be extrapolated that the risk of noise induced hearing loss and the time spent above dangerous sound thresholds would be even higher if robotic techniques were used. This is a particularly meaningful consideration for surgeons utilizing robotic technique and may influence their hearing protection behaviors.

Our study provides a meaningful new perspective on sound exposure in TJA by considering all OR personnel, though it is not without limitations. The sample size is relatively large; however, there are some cases in which the average sound level was unable to be calculated by the recording software, almost exclusively at the nursing station. Average sound level was unable to be calculated for 1 THA at the anesthesia station and 11 TKA and 6 THA cases at the nursing station, though all other sound data were available for these stations in each of those cases. Sound data were unable to be obtained for 2 TKA cases and 1 THA case for the surgical technologist. Fortunately, our data demonstrates low intrasubject variability across cases for average sound level, and as such, it is unlikely this has a meaningful impact on the interpretation of our results. An additional limitation if our lack of linear data marking specific timepoints in each case. With that being said, we have information from our nurses monitoring the nursing station sound recorders during cases to help identify the noisier portions of each case, with the expected steps being responsible for peak sounds. Also, average duration of time above 85 dB specifically, the value relevant to OSHA recommendations, could not be calculated given the ranges provided by the recording software. While the study demonstrates level of sound exposure, it does not provide a means of quantifying the clinical consequences of this exposure on OR personnel, information that would reinforce the significance of our data.

Other considerations regarding the generalizability of our data must be discussed. All cases were completed in ORs of the same size (25′ × 25′ × 11′ height), with the same setup/positioning of personnel and equipment. OR walls were metal base with dry wall and vinyl type coating. The dimensions of the room and wall materials may influence the acoustics of the room; however, in our case with large rooms with tall ceilings and resurfaced walls, it seems less likely these factors amplified sound. The layout of the OR also follows a standard design, and while outliers exist, it is felt that the ORs in our study represent an average layout. Lastly, all cases were completed by a single primary/attending surgeon. With that being said, the significant amount of data from a range of institutions and surgeons investigating sound levels in TJA supports that sound exposure in TJA is not unique to the surgeon.

Further research investigating different hearing protection modalities in TJA is needed. It is important to better understand the efficacy of different hearing protection modalities, as well as their impact on clinical workflow and communication. Longitudinal studies further investigating the long-term impact of sound exposure in TJA, as well as the potentially positive impact of hearing conservation efforts would be of benefit.

Conclusions

TJA is a common and effective surgery for both hip and knee arthritis. While surgical outcomes are largely positive for patients, these cases can have a significant adverse impact on surgeon’s hearing. To this point, there has been limited data describing the levels of sound exposure of other OR personnel in TJA cases. Our study demonstrates that all OR personnel are exposed to some degree of potentially dangerous sound during TJA, often exceeding safe levels outlined by OSHA. The primary surgeon is exposed to a significantly higher level of sound during these cases which may predispose them to hearing loss. Based on the findings of this study, a hearing conservation program, as outlined by OSHA, is indicated for the primary surgeon in THA and TKA ORs, though all OR personnel would likely benefit.
