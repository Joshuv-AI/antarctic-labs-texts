Evaluation of biomarkers sTREM-1, procalcitonin and C reactive protein in the diagnosis of pleural infection
Authors: Vanessa Adelia Alvarenga, Lisete Ribeiro Teixeira, Roberta Karla Barbosa Sales, Philippe Figueiredo Braga Colares, Carlos Sergio Rocha Silva, Evaldo Marchi, Milena Marques Pagliarelli Acencio
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractDiagnosis of complicated parapneumonic pleural effusion (CPPE), empyema and uncomplicated parapneumonic pleural effusion (UPPE) is vital in therapeutic management.ObjectiveTo measure the pleural fluid concentrations of soluble triggering receptor expressed on myeloid cell-1 (sTREM-1), procalcitonin (PCT) and C reactive protein (CRP) to provide information for diagnosing and discriminating between CPPE and UPPE.MethodsRetrospective data analysis from February 2016 to December 2017, focused on pleural fluid analysis of patients with infectious (PPE, empyema and tuberculosis) and non-infectious (malignant and transudates) effusions.ResultsOver 224 pleural fluid samples collected, 29 were empyema, 48 CPPE, 31 UPPE, 35 tuberculous, 47 malignant and 34 transudates. The levels of sTREM-1 and PCT were significantly higher in empyema (3658±1474 and 1704±1027) and CPPE (2651±1058 and 841±667) and lower in transudates (p<0.001). CRP levels were significantly higher in empyema (88.8±61.5) and CPPE (83.8±42.6) than in other groups (p<0.001), with no difference between them.ConclusionThe study demonstrated higher sTREM-1, PCT and CRP levels in empyema and CPPE and lower levels in transudates. Both sTREM-1 and CRP showed a higher than 70% diagnostic accuracy between infectious and non-infectious pleural effusions and between CPPE and UPPE; however, not superior to LDH pleural fluid levels.

Introduction

Parapneumonic effusion (PPE) is one of the most frequent causes of exudative pleural effusion observed in clinical practice. It is associated with considerable morbidity and mortality, with up to 20% of patients requiring surgery or dying, especially those with pleural infection, despite all medical advances in the diagnosis and treatment of this disease.1 2

Recent studies have shown an increase in the global incidence of PPE, especially in the extremes of age. Although its incidence is frequently underestimated in Brazil, with studies indicating an occurrence of PPE between 20% and 30% of patients hospitalised with pneumonia.1 2

While uncomplicated PPE (UPPE) can be treated with antibiotic therapy alone, the presence of complicated pleural infection or empyema determines the need for additional therapy with tube thoracostomy or even a surgical approach. Thus, rapid and accurate diagnosis remains a significant challenge in patients with suspected pleural infection.2 3

Adequate assessment of clinical, radiological and laboratory parameters is essential to assist in the therapeutic decision of antibiotic treatment or to associate the pleural drainage approach or surgery.1 3

Although detection of intrapleural pus (empyema) or non-purulent fluid with a culture or gram-positive stain for pathogens defines the presence of pleural infection, biochemical markers of inflammatory activity in the pleural fluid, such as pH, glucose and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), are validated to identify complicated PPE (CPPE) and determine appropriate decision thresholds. Beyond those, other biomarkers have been proposed and applied in clinical practice to identify patients earlier with worse outcomes.13

C reactive protein (CRP) is considered a good biomarker to detect an inflammatory state, triggered or not by an infectious process. In addition, a recent meta-analysis showed that the measurement of CRP in pleural fluid is a biomarker of moderate capacity to diagnose PPE and differentiate CPPE from UPPE, considering that CRP should be used in combination with other markers for such purpose. More recent biomarkers, such as the soluble trigger receptor expressed in myeloid cells (sTREM-1) and procalcitonin (PCT), were demonstrated to play a key role in the host’s inflammatory response to microbial infections.46 Therefore, it has been speculated that when measured in pleural fluid, they can be useful in diagnosing or ruling out pleural infections and identifying CPPE.

The present study aimed to measure the pleural fluid concentrations of sTREM-1, PCT, CRP and markers already considered as gold standards, in patients with different causes of pleural effusions, and to assess their usefulness in predicting an infectious aetiology and discriminating between CPPE and UPPE.

Material and methods

Retrospective study from February 2016 to December 2017 of consecutive pleural fluid analysis of patients with infectious (PPE and tuberculosis) and non-infectious (malignant and transudates) effusions with clinical indication for thoracentesis and/or pleural drainage. A pleural fluid volume of 60 mL was collected, and after centrifugation, the supernatant was stored in −80°C. All patients who underwent thoracentesis and/or pleural drainage signed an informed consent form that includes authorisation for the use of their samples in future research. In addition, a specific consent form was also submitted for the use of these samples in this study.

The study was carried out at the Instituto do Coracao do Hospital das Clinicas da Faculdade de Medicina da Universidade de São Paulo (InCor, HC-FMUSP) and Faculdade de Medicina de Jundiai and was approved by the ethics committee of the institutions involved (approval number 1.357.797).

Diagnostic criteria

The following criteria were used to diagnose the aetiology of pleural effusions:

Malignant pleural effusion (MPE): confirmed by the presence of malignant cells on cytological examination of the pleural fluid or biopsy specimen.

Malignant pleural effusion (MPE): confirmed by the presence of malignant cells on cytological examination of the pleural fluid or biopsy specimen.

Tuberculous pleuritis: confirmed with one of the following criteria: presence of positive rapid molecular test for tuberculosis (Xpert MTB/RIF) or culture for Mycobacterium tuberculosis (MTB) in pleural fluid or pleural biopsy tissue sample; presence of granulomas evidenced in the parietal pleura biopsy; an exudative lymphocytic effusion with high adenosine deaminase (ADA) levels (>30 IU/L) cleared in response to therapy or positive sputum culture for MTB or positive Xpert MTB/RIF and clinical and radiological response to tuberculosis treatment.

Tuberculous pleuritis: confirmed with one of the following criteria: presence of positive rapid molecular test for tuberculosis (Xpert MTB/RIF) or culture for Mycobacterium tuberculosis (MTB) in pleural fluid or pleural biopsy tissue sample; presence of granulomas evidenced in the parietal pleura biopsy; an exudative lymphocytic effusion with high adenosine deaminase (ADA) levels (>30 IU/L) cleared in response to therapy or positive sputum culture for MTB or positive Xpert MTB/RIF and clinical and radiological response to tuberculosis treatment.

PPE: confirmed by pleural effusion secondary to pneumonia (bacterial or viral) but not purulent fluid. Pneumonia was diagnosed based on the clinical presentation and radiological evidence. PPE was classified as either UPPE, characterised by anechoic pleural effusion with no or minimal septations treated with antibiotics alone, as assessed by ultrasound or chest radiography or CPPE, characterised by a non-purulent fluid requiring both antibiotic therapy and tube thoracostomy, typically associated with size and/or radiological appearance of septations and/or loculations.1 7

Empyema: defined as purulent pleural fluid, accompanied or not by gram stain or culture positivity on pleural fluid.

Transudates: defined by Light’s criteria,8 with pleural fluid/serum ratio <0.6 for LDH and <0.5 for total proteins and LDH level in the pleural fluid less than two-thirds of the maximum value of the method.

Laboratorial analysis

In the pleural fluid were evaluated: cellularity with global and differential counting, pH measurement, biochemical parameters, such as glucose, LDH, total proteins, ADA in an automated device and bacterioscopy and culture.

sTREM-1, PCT and CRP determination

sTREM-1 and PCT (R&D System, Minneapolis, USA) were measured by ELISA according to the manufacturer’s directions. Quantification of the biomarkers was done by comparison of the optical density in the ELISA reader (Power Wave; Bio-Tek). Lower-level detection value for sTREM-1 was 93.8 pg/mL and PCT was 31.2 pg/mL.

The CRP levels were obtained through immunoturbidimetry with intensification of the reaction by particles using the CRPL3 kit (Roche, Germany) and were read out using the automated Cobas 8000 device (Roche, Germany).

Statistical analysis

The results were analysed by SigmaStat Statistical Programme (San Rafael, California, USA). Analysis of variance (ANOVA) test was used to compare values between groups following Tukey test for multiple comparisons. When the data were not within the normality curve, Kruskal-Wallis and Dunn test of variance was used for multiple comparisons. Pearson or Spearman tests were done to determine correlations. A receiving operating characteristic (ROC) curve was performed for the diagnostic evaluation and subsequent evaluation of the sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, negative predictive value and diagnostic accuracy. The value of p<0.05 was considered significant.

Patient and public involvement

Patients or the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of our research.

The level of PCT showed the same trend as sTREM-1 levels, being significantly higher in empyema when compared with the other groups (p<0.001). In the evaluation of CRP, we did not observe a statistical difference between empyema and CPPE. However, these two groups had significantly higher levels compared with the other groups (p<0.001) (figure 1C).

We found weak, but significant, correlations between sTREM-1 and PCT (R=0.277; p<0.001), PCT and CRP (R=0.206; p=0.010), as well as between sTREM-1 and CRP (R=0.378; p<0.001). Likewise, the correlations of sTREM-1, PCT and CRP with the classic markers (LDH, pH and glucose) were weak. sTREM-1 and LDH (R=0.483), sTREM-1 and glucose (R=−0.470), PCT and LDH (R=0.284), CRP and LDH (R=0.491), CRP and glucose (R=−0.431) and CRP and pH (R=−0.298).

Cytological parameters were also significant, with the best correlation being between CRP and LDH levels (R=0.491; p<0.001).

We also analysed the diagnostic accuracy of the new markers in determining infectious pleural effusions and differentiating between CPPE and UPPE by determining the best cut-off point observed in the ROC curve.

Infectious versus non-infectious pleural effusions

We have obtained the area under the ROC curve for determining infectious and non-infectious pleural effusions. The figures are as follows: 0.606 (0.537–0.721) for PCT, 0.789 (0.691–0.846) for sTREM-1 and 0.800 (0693–0.854) for CRP.

To differentiate between infectious and non-infectious diseases, the cut-off points for diagnostic differentiation are as follows: PCT of >184.8 pg/mL, sTREM-1 of >804.6 pg/mL and CRP of >14 mg/dL (table 3).

In our analysis of markers to differentiate between CPPE and UPPE, we found that the area under the curve (AUC) for PCT was 0.501 (0.393–0.697), for sTREM-1 0.774 (0.621–0.882) and for CRP 0.735 (0.560–0.834).

Discussion

Infectious pleural effusion is a common clinical problem, and diagnostic differentiation between CPPE and UPPE is vital in therapeutic management.1

Classical pleural fluid biochemical parameters, including pH, LDH and glucose, are useful in distinguishing infectious diseases, particularly in differentiating UPPEs from CPPEs. However, none of these parameters has high sensitivity (55%–61%) or specificity (78%–89%).9

Among the classic markers, pleural fluid pH has demonstrated superior accuracy in identifying infectious aetiologies. A pH value below 7.20 is strongly associated with CPPEs and empyema, reflecting increased metabolic activity of bacteria. Furthermore, a low pleural fluid pH not only corroborates the diagnosis of a complicated infectious process but also constitutes a criterion for the indication of chest tube drainage.9 However, in most Brazilian centres, its quantification is carried out incorrectly, collecting the material in tubes with EDTA and quantifying it on bench top devices or strips. To correctly quantify the pH, it is necessary to collect the pleural fluid in a heparinised syringe, quantify in a blood gas analysis device, within a maximum of 4 hours after collection.10 In our study, the pH, despite presenting the lowest levels in infectious diseases, mainly in empyema (average of 6.9), showed a diagnostic accuracy of 57.1%, probably because its quantification, in most cases, was not carried out using a blood gas analysis device. The same reason may explain its weak correlation with CRP.

The development of the initial effusion is due to increased permeability of the pleural membranes in response to inflammation in the underlying lung parenchyma, which is thought to result in transfer of the interstitial fluid across the visceral pleura. During this initial exudative phase, the fluid will have a normal glucose level and pH >7.20.2

If inflammation persists, the pleural space is exposed to bacterial invasion. The presence of bacteria increases local glucose consumption, resulting in a decrease in pH and glucose. The exudative phase will then progress to the fibrinopurulent stage.2

In our study, the lowest glucose levels were observed in empyema (24.2±5.3 mg/dL), corroborating the description that the greater the severity of the pleural infection, the greater the local glucose consumption, lowering its levels.1 10 Porcel, using a 60 mg/dL as a cut-off point of glucose in the pleural fluid, described a sensitivity for glucose in differentiating complicated from uncomplicated pleural effusion of 55% and a specificity of 89%; our study observed a sensitivity of 54.5% and a specificity of 96.1%.9

LDH levels in pleural fluid indicate the degree of pleural inflammation; the higher the enzyme concentration, the more inflamed the pleural surface.9 The bacterial metabolism and neutrophil phagocytic activity lead to increased lactic acid production. As the neutrophil number in the pleural space rises, LDH is released, and this is responsible for the typically high levels seen in infected pleural fluid.2

In this study, we observed the highest LDH levels in empyema, followed by CPPE and tuberculosis effusions. We found the highest cellularity, emphasising that the cellular predominance in empyema and CPPE was neutrophils and lymphocytes in tuberculosis.

These biochemical parameters are considered standards for defining infectious pleural effusions and are currently used in clinical practice; however, the pathophysiology of inflammation in infectious effusions still needs to be completely understood. New mediators are being studied as markers of inflammation and pleural fibrosis, as well as diagnostic differentiation and therapeutic target markers.2

We analysed the biomarkers of infection or inflammation, PCT, sTREM-1 molecule and CRP, cited in the literature as promising in diagnosing infectious pleural effusions.

CRP production is induced by systemic inflammation of infectious or non-infectious origin. The output of PCT and sTREM-1, unlike CRP, has been described to be elevated only in response to bacterial infection but not to non-infectious inflammation.46

We demonstrated high levels of PCT, sTREM-1 and CRP in empyema and CPPE and lower levels in transudates, with significant but weak correlations between the new and classic markers. We observed that LDH was the only one of the traditional markers correlated with all the new markers and with the highest correlation indices.

We used two diagnostic analyses of the new markers, the differentiation between infectious diseases and complicated and UPPEs.

The sTREM-1 and CRP markers achieved good diagnostic performance in both cases, with accuracy greater than 70%. PCT had a diagnostic accuracy of around 50%.

PCT levels >0.25 ng/mL were specifically noted to have a sensitivity of 77.78% and a specificity of 74.14% for distinguishing infectious pleural effusion from non-infectious pleural effusion.11 However, in another study, no difference in pleural fluid PCT levels was observed between empyema and PPE cases. This analysis revealed that pleural fluid PCT is unsuitable as a marker for the differentiation of empyema because its AUC was < 0.5. However, pleural fluid PCT may be a useful marker in differentiating PPE from other types of effusions (excluding empyema); when using a cut-off point of 0.11 ng/mL, the sensitivity was 75.0% and the specificity was 79.0%.12

Additionally, pleural PCT at a level >0.25 ng/mL was associated with a longer total length of hospital stay (17.0 vs 8.7 days, 95% CI −12.5 to −4.0, p<0.001) and a higher rate of complication (38% vs 0%, p=0.004) in a retrospective analysis of 38 patients who received surgery for CPPE.13 A meta-analysis indicated that PCT has an overall sensitivity of 0.62 and a specificity of 0.71, with the ROC analysis demonstrating an AUC of 0.80. These findings suggest that pleural fluid PCT might have modest performance in diagnosing PPE.14

sTREM-1 is present in many biological fluids and can be used as a surrogate marker of TREM-1 activation, displaying a moderate diagnostic performance in differentiating sepsis from sterile inflammatory response, as well as a moderate prognostic significance in assessing the mortality of infection in adult patients. sTREM-1 concentration at the site of the presumed infection is more clinically useful, although its role to guide antibiotic strategy is yet to be evaluated.15

Liu et al6 found that the sTREM-1 levels are significantly higher in PPE than in tuberculous effusions. Similarly, another study16 found that pleural sTREM-1 at a cut-off value of 374 pg/mL yields a sensitivity of 93.8% and a specificity of 90.9% in differentiating bacterial pleural effusion from tuberculous pleurisy.16

We demonstrated that sTREM-1 levels were elevated in pleural exudates, with the highest levels in empyema, CPPE and tuberculosis, with a significant correlation with pleural fluid LDH and glucose. According to our results, sTREM-1 has a good diagnostic accuracy for infectious pleural effusions and in the differentiation between CCPE and UPPE (75% and 75.8%, respectively).

These data corroborate a meta-analysis that evaluated pleural sTREM-1, showing an AUC of 0.92 in diagnosing bacterial effusions, with a sensitivity of 78% and specificity of 83%.17

Our data demonstrated elevated levels of sTREM-1 also in pleural effusion due to tuberculosis, differing from data in the literature, which shows an increase of sTREM-1 only in empyema and PPE.6 16 17 sTREM-1 levels are higher in empyema than in CPPE and higher in CPPE than in UPPE. These data agree with the Chan et al study that argued that the sTREM molecule may have the potential to help with pleural drainage decision.17

CRP, known as ‘acute-phase protein’, is produced early in the inflammatory process and provides enhanced protection against micro-organisms, limits tissue damage and promotes a rapid return to a homeostatic state during infection. Circulating CRP may exude into the pleural cavity, and increased pleural CRP may present a possible biomarker for pleural infection.4

We found that CRP levels in empyema and CPPE were quite high, differentiating from UPPE, tuberculosis, cancer and transudates. CRP levels correlated with the classic pleural fluid parameters LDH, pH and glucose.

Among the new biomarkers, CRP is the easiest to implement, is less expensive and is already routinely used in clinical laboratories.

Porcel JM9 demonstrated, in a review article, that CRP has a good assessment in distinguishing CPPE and UPPE with an AUC of 0.80, similar to the parameters pH, glucose and LDH. He also reported that the association of CRP with these markers can improve diagnostic accuracy.9 In our study, we obtained a diagnostic accuracy of 72.8% for pleural fluid CRP in distinguishing CPPE and UPPE.

In a recent meta-analysis involving seven studies (942 patients), the sensitivity and specificity of pleural CRP for PPE were, respectively, 0.80 and 0.88, and the differentiation of CPPE from UPPE had a sensitivity of 0.65, a specificity of 0.85 and an AUC of 0.83.4

Among the limitations of this present study, we highlight the methodological difficulty of the available kits for quantifying PCT and sTREM-1, with the need for replicates due to interassay variability. Furthermore, pleural fluids were collected at a single point in time, and several factors could influence the inflammatory response of pleural effusion, including the type of bacteria, comorbidities and different durations of illness. We also emphasise that, ideally, the pH of the pleural fluid should be measured using a blood gas analysis device, which did not occur in most of our cases.

Another limitation of our study is its development in referral hospitals, which results in a selection bias, further complicated by its retrospective design. Future research with a prospective design, conducted in less complex centres that do not manage highly complex patients, is needed. Such studies, especially when linked to clinical outcomes, can better determine the utility of sTREM-1 and pleural fluid CRP in clinical practice.

In summary, in our study, the pleural fluid markers PCT, sTREM-1 and CRP are elevated in empyema and CPPE compared with UPPE and other aetiologies. There were correlations between the levels of PCT, sTREM-1 and CRP with the classic pleural fluid markers LDH, glucose and pH.

The sTREM-1 molecule and CRP showed good diagnostic accuracy in this sample between infectious vs non-infectious pleural effusions and CPPE vs UPPE; however, they were not superior to pleural fluid LDH. New studies correlating these markers with clinical outcomes are necessary.
