Discrete Boltzmann distributions via multisets and their coefficients


Abstract
This paper investigates the combinatorics that gives rise to the Boltzmann probability distribution. Despite being one of the most important distributions in physics and other fields of science, the mathematics of the underlying model of particles at different energy levels is underexplored. This paper gives a reconstruction, using multisets with fixed sums as mathematical representations. Counting (the coefficients of) such multisets gives a general description of binomial, trinomial, quadrinomial etc. coefficients, here called N N -nomials. These coefficients give rise to multiple discrete Boltzmann distributions that are linked to explanations in the physics literature.

Introduction

Ludwig Boltzmann introduced the distribution later named after him in
the late 19th century in his work on statistical mechanics. This
‘Boltzmann’ distribution describes the energy distribution of atoms
and moleculues. The discrete version of the Boltzmann distribution
describes the probabilities of particle configurations over various
energy levels in an equilibrium, for a fixed total energy, see

e.g.

[
2
]
for background information on the usage
in natural sciences, or
[
3
]
for usage in economics.
There are several variations of this Boltzmann distribution, in
discrete and continuous form. The continuous version is an exponential
distribution that is obtained via a limit process, using an
approximation. In general, physicists quickly move to this continuous
formulation, based on a sketch of the underlying discrete
situation. This paper, however, concentrates on this discrete stage
and reconstructs its rich mathematical structure in terms of
‘
N
-nomial coefficients’ and multisets.

These
N
-nomial coefficients are introduced in this paper as
generalisations of binomial, trinomial and quadrinomial coefficients.
This topic exists in the literature only in sketchy
form. Section
3
introduces the
N
-nomial

C_{N}(K,i)
in a new way as the number of sequences of length
K
,
containing numbers
n_{1},\ldots n_{K}\in\{0,1,\ldots,N{\kern-1.5pt}-{\kern-1.5pt}1\}
, satisfying
n_{1}+\cdots+n_{K}=i
. The standard binomial
coefficients
\binom{K}{i}
arise as special cases, for

N=2
. Trinomial and quadrinomial coefficients arise for
N=3
and

N=4
, respectively. In physical terms,
C_{N}(K,i)
is the number of
‘microstates’ of size
K
with total energy
i
, at energy levels

\{0,1,\ldots,N{\kern-1.5pt}-{\kern-1.5pt}1\}
. Several other formulations of

N
-nomials will be given, including one in algorithmic form. Also,
it will be shown how these
N
-nomials arise in certain polynomial
expressions — see Theorem
4.5
— corresponding to
the definition given by the Online Encyclopedia of Integer Sequences
(OEIS).

The paper makes systematic use of multisets. These can be seen as
‘sets’ in which elements may occur multiple times, or as ‘lists’ in
which the order of the elements does not matter. These multisets are
used for two purposes, namely for:

•

an alternative description of
N
-nomials, see
Section
4
;

•

capturing multiple particles at different energy levels.

Multisets are not explicitly used in statistical mechanics,
but they occur implicitly and can be recognised as such. For instance,
they are called compositions in
[
2
, Chapt. 2]
. There, the
probability of 4 tosses of a fair coin are discussed for sequences

HHHH
and
HTHH
, where
H
and
T
stand for heads and tails. The
probability is
\big(\frac{1}{2}\big)^{4}=\frac{1}{16}
for both
sequences. Then: “Contrast this with a question of composition. Which

composition
is more probable: 4
H
’s and 0
T
’s, or 3
H
’s
and 1
T
?” Using the notation introduced in
Section
4
we will write these compositions as two
multisets
4|{\kern 1.00006pt}H{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle
and
3|{\kern 1.00006pt}H{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle+1|{\kern 1.00006pt}T{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle
. They have
(multinomial) probabilities
\frac{1}{16}
and
\frac{1}{4}
,
respectively. Some authors also use ‘microstates’ for sequences and
‘macrostates’ for multisets, but the latter notion is not explicitly
defined. To quote from
[
14
, §1.6]
: “Thus, the correct
way of specifying a microstate of the system is through the
distribution numbers
\{n_{j}\}
, and not through the statement as to
”which particle is in which state.”” Section
4

describes an accumulation operation
\mathsl{acc}
that turns a sequence into
a multiset, basically by ignoring the order of the elements and only
counting their multiplicities (“distribution numbers”). Using the
above coins, we have
\mathsl{acc}(HHHH)=4|{\kern 1.00006pt}H{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle
and
\mathsl{acc}(HTHH)=3|{\kern 1.00006pt}H{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle+1|{\kern 1.00006pt}T{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle
. The probability
\frac{1}{4}
for the multiset

3|{\kern 1.00006pt}H{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle+1|{\kern 1.00006pt}T{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle
is obtained as the sum
\frac{1}{4}=\frac{1}{16}+\frac{1}{16}+\frac{1}{16}+\frac{1}{16}
of the four
probabilities of the four sequences
THHH
,
HTHH
,
HHTH
,
HHHT

that accumlate toe
3|{\kern 1.00006pt}H{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle+1|{\kern 1.00006pt}T{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle
.

The paper introduces multiple versions of a discrete
‘Boltzmann’ distribution.

1.

A
Boltzmann-on-multisets
distribution appears in
Section
5
as a distribution over multisets. It
captures the distribution of configurations of a fixed number of
particles over different energy levels, with a fixed total
energy. It is shown that this distribution corresponds directly to
exemplaric explations in the physics literature. The Boltzmann
distribution on multisets (macrostates) arises as image distribution
(via accumulation) from the uniform distribution on sequences
(microstates).

2.

A
Boltzmann-on-numbers
distribution is obtained from this
Boltzmann-on-multisets distribution, in
Section
6
, as a distribution over the various
energy levels, again for a fixed total energy. We use ‘frequentist
learning’ as the canonical operation that turns a multiset into a
distribution, basically by normalisation (
i.e.
by
counting). The Boltzmann-on-numbers distribution arises by applying
frequentist learning ‘in probability’ to the Boltzmann-on-multisets
distribution. Several alternative formulations are given for this
distribution, one of which is entirely in terms of
N
-nomials.

3.

Finally, a
Boltzmann-on-energy
distribution is introduced
as a special case of the Boltzmann-on-numbers distribution, namely
where the number of energy levels exceeds the total energy. This is
common in physics. It is shown that this special cases is
mathematically also very relevant, since it gives a simple
description of the
N
-nomial, in terms of a multichoose
coefficient, see Theorem
7.1
(
2
).
This leads to a simple description of this third Boltzmann-on-energy
distribution that matches formulations in the (physics) literature.

This paper contains several contributions. First, there is the
systematic development of
N
-nomial coefficients, including the
simple description in the physically relevant special case. Next, it
distinguishes the above three discrete Boltzmann distributions, with
their relationship and properties — such as their symmetry, via
closure under reversal. Thirdly, the paper establishes a close, new
connection between
N
-nomial coefficients and these three Boltzmann
distributions. Fourthly, the Boltzmann-on-multisets and
Boltzmann-on-numbers distributions are described as equilibria (fixed
points, stationary distributions) of particular Markov chains, see
Subsection
8.2
. Finally, the paper introduces two
new multivariate distributions via
N
-nomials, exploiting the
Vandermonde property that is proven for
N
-nomials, see
Subsection
8.3
. One distribution arises similarly to
the multivariate hypergeometric and Pólya distributions, but differs
from both of them. The other one is a multivariate Boltzmann
distribution that could be used to capture combinations of particles
of different kinds.

This paper builds on ideas and techniques from category theory (see

e.g.

[
12
,
11
]
), but mostly hides this
underlying approach, in order not to limit the audience. The one
place where category theory pops up is in the form of ‘functoriality’
of the multiset and distribution operations
\mathcal{M}
and
\mathcal{D}
, but
there the relevant notions are introduced concretely. Thus, no
familiarity with category theory is assumed, but readers who are
interested in this wider more abstract perspective are referred
to
[
8
]
.

Multichoose coefficients

We shall frequently use the
binomial
coefficient and

multichoose
coefficient, given in terms of factorials
respectively as:

\begin{array}[]{rclcrcl}\displaystyle\binom{n}{i}&=&\displaystyle\frac{n!}{(n-i)!\cdot i}&&\displaystyle\left(\!\binom{m}{j}\!\right)&=&\displaystyle\frac{(m+j-1)!}{(m-1)!\cdot j!}.\end{array}

The constraints are that
n\geq 0
and
0\leq i\leq n

and that
m\geq 1
. It is well-known that
\binom{n}{i}
is the number
of
subsets
of size
i
of a set of size
n
. It is less
well-known that
\big(\binom{m}{j}\big)
is the number of

multisets
of size
j
of a set of size
m
, see
Lemma
4.2
(
1
) below.

We shall need the following basic equations. The first one can be
found for instance in
[
1
, Identity 149]
. The next two
can be obtained easily from this first equation.

Lemma 2.1

Let
n\geq 1
.

1.

For
m\geq 1
,

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\displaystyle\sum_{0\leq j<m}\,\left(\!\binom{n}{j}\!\right)&=&\displaystyle\left(\!\binom{m}{n}\!\right).\end{array}

2.

For
m\geq 2
,

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\displaystyle\sum_{0\leq j<m}\,\left(\!\binom{n}{j}\!\right)\cdot j&=&\displaystyle n\cdot\left(\!\binom{m-1}{n+1}\!\right)\end{array}

3.

For
m\geq 3
,

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\displaystyle\sum_{0\leq j<m}\,\left(\!\binom{n}{j}\!\right)\cdot j^{2}&=&\displaystyle n\cdot(n+1)\cdot\left(\!\binom{m-2}{n+2}\!\right)+n\cdot\left(\!\binom{m-1}{n+1}\!\right).\end{array}

N
-nomial coefficients via sequences

This section introduces
N
-nomial coefficients, as generalisations of
binomial, trinomial, quadrinomial
etc.
coefficients. There
does not seem to be a systematic description of these coefficients in
the literature. The website
[
13
]
of the Online
Encyclopedia of Integer Sequences (OEIS) contains a description of how

N
-nomials arise in expansions of certain polynomials, together with
multiple examples for trinomial and quadrinomial coefficients. This
section introduces a new direct formula, for the
N
-ary case. It will
be related to the polynomial formulation below, in
Theorem
4.5
. The definition is first given in terms of
sequences, and then reformulated in terms of multisets, in the next
section. Several properties are included for these
N
-nomials.

We recall that the binomial coefficient
\binom{N}{i}\in\mathbb{N}
gives
the number of subsets of size
i
, of a (finite) set of size
N
. Such
a subset of size
i
, say of a set
\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}\}
of
N

elements, can be identified with sequence of binary numbers
(b_{1},\ldots,b_{N})\in\{0,1\}^{N}
of length
N
with numbers
b_{i}\in\{0,1\}
satisfying
i=\mathsl{sum}\big(b_{1},\ldots,b_{N}\big)=b_{1}+\cdots+b_{N}
. Each number
b_{i}
then tells if the element

x_{i}
is in the subset (when
b_{i}=1
) or not (when
b_{i}=0
).

This description of the binomial coefficient can be generalised to
(univariate) trinomial, quadrinomial …coefficients. We use

|{\kern 1.00006pt}X{\kern 1.00006pt}|\in\mathbb{N}
to describe the size, that is, the number of
elements, of a finite set
X
.

Definition 3.1

We fix a natural number
N\geq 1
and write
\boldsymbol{N}=\{0,1,\ldots,N{\kern-1.5pt}-{\kern-1.5pt}1\}\subseteq\mathbb{N}

first

\begin{array}[]{rcl}C_{N}(K,i)&\coloneqq&\Big|{\kern 1.00006pt}\big\{\vec{n}\in\boldsymbol{N}^{K}\;\big|\;\mathsl{sum}(\vec{n})=i\big\}{\kern 1.00006pt}\Big|.\end{array}

(1)

4
N
-nomial coefficients via multisets

This section introduces a second formulation of
N
-nomials in terms
of multisets, and also a short algorithm for computing
N
-nomials.
The multiset reformulation of
N
-nomials allows us to prove
additional basic properties about them, and in particular, to relate
our formulation of
N
-nomials to their description in terms of
polynomials (see Theorem
4.5
). Since multisets are not
so common yet, we start with a brief introduction.

Informally, multisets are ‘subsets’ in which elements may occur
multiple times. One can also view multisets as ‘sequences’ in which
the order of the elements does not matter. Multisets play a
fundamental role in combinatorics and probability, but this is not
always recognised. One reason may be that there is no commonly
accepted, good notation for multisets. We shall use ‘ket’ notation,
borrowed from quantum theory. An urn
\upsilon
containing three red
(
R
), two green (
G
) and one blue (
B
) ball will be written as:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\upsilon&=&3\big|{\kern 1.00006pt}R{\kern 1.00006pt}\big\rangle+2\big|{\kern 1.00006pt}G{\kern 1.00006pt}\big\rangle+1\big|{\kern 1.00006pt}B{\kern 1.00006pt}\big\rangle.\end{array}

(2)

Some authors, for instance in
[
5
,
1
]
,
simply repeat elements in subset notation, as in

\{R,R,R,G,G,B\}
. This does not really scale.

The numbers
3,2,1
before the three kets in (
2
) describe the
multiplicities of the elements
R,G,B
within the kets

|{\kern 1.00006pt}-{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle
. These kets have no mathematical meaning and are only used
to separate the multiplicities and their elements. The convention is
that the order of the kets does not matter, that multiplicities for
the same elements can be added, and that zero-multiplicities can be
omitted. Thus we could also write
\upsilon=1|{\kern 1.00006pt}R{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle+1|{\kern 1.00006pt}B{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle+2|{\kern 1.00006pt}G{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle+2|{\kern 1.00006pt}R{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle+0|{\kern 1.00006pt}P{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle
, where
P
stands for purple, say.

Here is another example: the polynomial
x^{3}-7x^{2}+16x-12=(x-2)(x-2)(x-3)
has a multiset of roots, namely:

2|{\kern 1.00006pt}2{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle+1|{\kern 1.00006pt}3{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle
.

In a slightly more formal way, we shall write a multiset with elements
from an arbitrary set
X
as a function
\varphi\colon X\rightarrow\mathbb{N}
with finite support: the set
\mathsl{supp}(\varphi)\coloneqq\{x\in X\;|\;\varphi(x)\neq 0\}
is required to be finite. Thus we can
write
\varphi=\sum_{x}\varphi(x)\big|{\kern 1.00006pt}x{\kern 1.00006pt}\big\rangle
. We shall freely
switch between the ket notation and this functional notation, and use
whichever is most convenient. Notice that in this context, a multiset
is always finite, but it may be defined over an arbitrary,
not-necessarily finite set
X
.

The
size
of a multiset is the total number of its elements,
including multiplicities. Thus, the urn
\upsilon
in (
2
) has
size
6
. In general, we write
\|\varphi\|\coloneqq\sum_{x}\varphi(x)
for the size of a multiset
\varphi
. We shall also write

\mathcal{M}(X)
for the set of all multisets over
X
, and
\mathcal{M}[K](X)\subseteq\mathcal{M}(X)
for the subset of multisets of size

K\in\mathbb{N}
. For
K=0
, the set
\mathcal{M}[K](X)
has precisely one
member, namely the empty multiset
\mathbf{0}
with zero elements. For the
above urn (
2
) we can now write
\upsilon\in\mathcal{M}[6]\big(\{R,G,B\}\big)
.

An easy mental picture for multisets in
\mathcal{M}(X)
is to think of
them as urns filled with multiple balls with different colours, from
the set
X
. Putting the contents of two urns together corresponds to
(pointwise) addition of multisets. This makes
\mathcal{M}(X)
a
commutative monoid, with the empty multiset
\mathbf{0}
as neutral
element. In fact,
\mathcal{M}(X)
is the
free
commutative monoid
on the set
X
. Another common, but less intuitive, model of
multisets involves ‘stars and bars’, see

e.g.

[
1
, §5.4]
.

Definition 4.1

We often consider multisets
\varphi\in\mathcal{M}(\boldsymbol{N})
on the set

\boldsymbol{N}=\{0,1,\ldots,N{\kern-1.5pt}-{\kern-1.5pt}1\}
containing the first
N

natural numbers. For such a multiset

\varphi\in\mathcal{M}[K](\boldsymbol{N})
we define
\mathsl{sum}(\varphi)\in\mathbb{N}

as:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mathsl{sum}(\varphi)&\coloneqq&\displaystyle\sum_{0\leq j<N}\,\varphi(j)\cdot j.\end{array}

(3)

It is not hard to see that if
\|\varphi\|=K
, then

\mathsl{sum}(\varphi)\in\big\{0,\ldots,(N{\kern-1.5pt}-{\kern-1.5pt}1)\cdot K\big\}
.

Accordingly, for
0\leq i\leq(N{\kern-1.5pt}-{\kern-1.5pt}1)\cdot K
we define the
subset
\mathcal{M}[K,i](\boldsymbol{N})\subseteq\mathcal{M}[K](\boldsymbol{N})
as:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mathcal{M}[K,i](\boldsymbol{N})&\coloneqq&\big\{\varphi\in\mathcal{M}[K](\boldsymbol{N})\;\big|\;\mathsl{sum}(\varphi)=i\big\}.\end{array}

We also need the property of functoriality, which can be understood in
terms of repainting. Suppose we have a function
f\colon X\rightarrow Y
, forming a transformation of
X
-colors to
Y
-colors. Then we can
turn it into a ‘repainting’ function that takes an urn with

X
-coloured balls into an urn with
Y
-coloured balls, by repainting
balls individually. The resulting function is written as
\mathcal{M}(f)\colon\mathcal{M}(X)\rightarrow\mathcal{M}(Y)
, where we thus apply

\mathcal{M}(-)
both to sets and to functions between them. Usually this
does not lead to confusion. Explicitly,

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mathcal{M}(f)\Big(\sum_{i}n_{i}\big|{\kern 1.00006pt}x_{i}{\kern 1.00006pt}\big\rangle\Big)&\coloneqq&\sum_{i}n_{i}\big|{\kern 1.00006pt}f(x_{i}){\kern 1.00006pt}\big\rangle.\end{array}

(4)

It is not hard to see that identity and compositions are
preserved:
\mathcal{M}(\mathsl{id})=\mathsl{id}
and
\mathcal{M}(g\mathrel{\circ}f)=\mathcal{M}(g)\mathrel{\circ}\mathcal{M}(f)
, and also that repainting maintains the
size of urns:
\big\|\mathcal{M}(f)(\varphi)\big\|=\big\|\varphi\big\|
.

There is an obvious operation that turns sequences (tuples, lists)
into multisets by forgetting the order. We call it

accumulation
and write it as
\mathsl{acc}
. The above
urn (
2
) can be obtained from a sequence via, for instance,

\begin{array}[]{rcccl}\mathsl{acc}\big(R,G,R,G,R,B\big)&=&3\big|{\kern 1.00006pt}R{\kern 1.00006pt}\big\rangle+2\big|{\kern 1.00006pt}G{\kern 1.00006pt}\big\rangle+1\big|{\kern 1.00006pt}B{\kern 1.00006pt}\big\rangle&=&\mathsl{acc}\big(B,G,G,R,R,R\big).\end{array}

For an arbitrary set
X
and a number
K
we can write
accumulation as a function
\mathsl{acc}\colon X^{K}\rightarrow\mathcal{M}[K](X)
, defined as
\mathsl{acc}\big(x_{1},\ldots,x_{K}\big)\coloneqq 1|{\kern 1.00006pt}x_{1}{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle+\cdots+1|{\kern 1.00006pt}x_{K}{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle
.

We include the following basic facts. Proofs may be found
in

Lemma 4.2

Let
X
be a finite set with
N\coloneqq|{\kern 1.00006pt}X{\kern 1.00006pt}|\geq 1
elements.

1.

There are
\big(\binom{N}{K}\big)
multisets
of size
K
over
X
, that is,
\Big|{\kern 1.00006pt}\mathcal{M}[K](X){\kern 1.00006pt}\Big|=\displaystyle\left(\!\binom{N}{K}\!\right)
.

2.

For an arbitrary multiset

\varphi\in\mathcal{M}[K](X)
, the number of sequences
\vec{x}\in X^{K}

with
\mathsl{acc}(\vec{x})=\varphi
is equal to the multiset coefficient

({\kern 1.0pt}\varphi{\kern 1.0pt})
, where:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}({\kern 1.0pt}\varphi{\kern 1.0pt})&\coloneqq&\displaystyle\frac{\|\varphi\|!}{\prod_{x}\varphi(x)!}.\end{array}

(5)

3.

For the sum of these multiset coefficients one
has:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\displaystyle\sum_{\varphi\in\mathcal{M}[K](X)}\,({\kern 1.0pt}\varphi{\kern 1.0pt})&=&N^{K}.\end{array}

(6)

Multinomial coefficients are often written of the form:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\displaystyle\binom{n}{n_{1},\ldots,n_{L}}&=&\displaystyle\frac{n!}{\prod_{i}n_{i}!}\qquad\mbox{where }\;n=\textstyle\sum_{i}n_{i}.\end{array}

In this notation the order of the elements
n_{i}
is irrelevant.
Hence it makes sense to define such coefficients for multisets. Using
multisets one can also give a snappy formulation of the multinomial
theorem, namely as:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\Big(x_{0}+\cdots+x_{N-1}\Big)^{K}&=&\displaystyle\sum_{\varphi\in\mathcal{M}[K](\boldsymbol{N})}\,({\kern 1.0pt}\varphi{\kern 1.0pt})\cdot\prod_{0\leq j<N}x_{j}^{\varphi(j)},\end{array}

(7)

where, recall,
\boldsymbol{N}=\{0,\ldots,N{\kern-1.5pt}-{\kern-1.5pt}1\}
.

In the sequel we shall see several forms of reversal, so we first
make this operation explicit.

Lemma 4.3

Consider the reversal self-isomorphism
\mathsl{rev}_{L}\colon\{0,\ldots,L\}\mathrel{\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\raisebox{1.50694pt}{$\scriptstyle\cong$}}}{{\raisebox{0.0pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\rightarrow$}}}}}\{0,\ldots,L\}
by
\mathsl{rev}(i)=L-i
. For a multiset
\varphi\in\mathcal{M}[K](\boldsymbol{N})
we use
functoriality to apply reversal to multisets:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mathsl{Rev}_{N-1}(\varphi)\hskip 5.0pt\coloneqq\hskip 5.0pt\mathcal{M}(\mathsl{rev}_{N-1})(\varphi)&=&\displaystyle\sum_{0\leq i<N}\,\varphi(i)\big|{\kern 1.00006pt}\mathsl{rev}_{N-1}(i){\kern 1.00006pt}\big\rangle\\[10.00002pt]
&=&\displaystyle\sum_{0\leq i<N}\,\varphi(i)\big|{\kern 1.00006pt}N{\kern-1.5pt}-{\kern-1.5pt}1-i{\kern 1.00006pt}\big\rangle.\end{array}

The following properties then hold.

1.

The multiset coefficient of a reversed multiset is the same as
the coefficient of the original:
({\kern 1.0pt}\mathsl{Rev}_{N-1}(\varphi){\kern 1.0pt})=({\kern 1.0pt}\varphi{\kern 1.0pt})
.

2.

Using the sum of multisets
\mathsl{sum}
from Definition
4.1
,
there is a commuting diagram:

\textstyle{\mathcal{M}[K](\boldsymbol{N})\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\mathsl{sum}}
\scriptstyle{\mathsl{Rev}_{N-1}}
\scriptstyle{\cong}
\textstyle{\{0,1,\ldots,(N{\kern-1.5pt}-{\kern-1.5pt}1)\cdot K\}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\mathsl{rev}_{(N-1)\cdot K}}
\scriptstyle{\cong}
\textstyle{\mathcal{M}[K](\boldsymbol{N})\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\mathsl{sum}}
\textstyle{\{0,1,\ldots,(N{\kern-1.5pt}-{\kern-1.5pt}1)\cdot K\}}

(8)

In particular, this gives:
\mathsl{sum}(\varphi)=i\Longleftrightarrow\mathsl{sum}\big(\mathsl{Rev}_{K}(\varphi)\big)=(N{\kern-1.5pt}-{\kern-1.5pt}1)\cdot K-i
.

1.

This holds because the multiset coefficient is defined in
terms of the multiplicities only — see (
5
) —
which remain unchanged.

2.

For a multiset
\varphi\in\mathcal{M}[K](\boldsymbol{N})
of size
K
,

\begin{array}[b]{rcl}\mathsl{sum}\Big(\mathsl{Rev}_{N-1}(\varphi)\Big)&=&\displaystyle\sum_{0\leq i<N}\,\varphi\big(N{\kern-1.5pt}-{\kern-1.5pt}1-i\big)\cdot i\\[11.99998pt]
&=&\displaystyle\sum_{0\leq i<N}\,\varphi(i)\cdot\big(N{\kern-1.5pt}-{\kern-1.5pt}1-i\big)\\[11.99998pt]
&=&\displaystyle\sum_{0\leq i<N}\,\varphi(i)\cdot(N{\kern-1.5pt}-{\kern-1.5pt}1)-\sum_{0\leq i<N}\,\varphi(i)\cdot i\\[6.00006pt]
&=&K\cdot(N{\kern-1.5pt}-{\kern-1.5pt}1)-\mathsl{sum}(\varphi)\hskip 5.0pt=\hskip 5.0pt\mathsl{rev}_{(N-1)\cdot K}\Big(\mathsl{sum}(\varphi)\Big).\end{array}

[formula]

The Boltzmann distribution on multisets

We have used ket notation
|{\kern 1.00006pt}-{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle
to separate multiplicities and
elements in multisets. It makes sense to use the same notation for
(finite, discrete) probability distributions, now to separate
probabilities and elements. The distribution corresponding to the urn

\upsilon
in (
2
) is then written as
\frac{1}{2}|{\kern 1.00006pt}R{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle+\frac{1}{3}|{\kern 1.00006pt}G{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle+\frac{1}{6}|{\kern 1.00006pt}B{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle
. It gives the probabilities
of randomlingly drawing a ball from
\upsilon
, per colour. We briefly
discuss (discrete) distributions in general, before concentrating
on Boltzmann distributions.

We shall write
\mathcal{D}(X)
for the set of distributions over a set
X
.
The elements of
\mathcal{D}(X)
may be written in ket form as finite formal
convex sums
\sum_{i}r_{i}|{\kern 1.00006pt}x_{i}{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle
, where
x_{i}\in X
and

r_{i}\in[0,1]
with
\sum_{i}r_{i}=1
. Equivalently, such
distributions can be written in functional form as functions

\omega\colon X\rightarrow[0,1]
with finite support

\mathsl{supp}(\omega)\coloneqq\{x\in X\;|\;\omega(x)\neq 0\}
and with

\sum_{x}\omega(x)=1
.

There is a systematic way to turn a (non-empty) multiset into
a distribution, namely by normalisation. We write this operation
as
\mathsl{Flrn}
and call it frequentist learning, since it involves learning
a distribution by counting. Explicitly, when
\varphi\neq\mathbf{0}
,
that is, when
\|\varphi\|\neq 0
,

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mathsl{Flrn}(\varphi)&\coloneqq&\displaystyle\sum_{x\in\mathsl{supp}(\varphi)}\,\frac{\varphi(x)}{\|\varphi\|}\,\big|{\kern 1.00006pt}x{\kern 1.00006pt}\big\rangle.\end{array}

(10)

For instance,
\mathsl{Flrn}\big(3|{\kern 1.00006pt}a{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle+4|{\kern 1.00006pt}b{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle+5|{\kern 1.00006pt}c{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle\big)=\frac{1}{4}|{\kern 1.00006pt}a{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle+\frac{1}{3}|{\kern 1.00006pt}b{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle+\frac{5}{12}|{\kern 1.00006pt}c{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle
.

Definition 5.1

Let
\omega=\sum_{i}r_{i}|{\kern 1.00006pt}x_{i}{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle\in\mathcal{D}(X)
be a distribution on
a set
X
, together with another set
Y
.

1.

For a function
f\colon X\rightarrow Y
there is the

image
distribution
\mathcal{D}(f)(\omega)\in\mathcal{D}(Y)
,
defined as in (
4
):

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mathcal{D}(f)\Big(\sum_{i}r_{i}\big|{\kern 1.00006pt}x_{\kern 0.81949pt}\big\rangle\Big)&\coloneqq&\sum_{i}r_{i}\big|{\kern 1.00006pt}f(x_{i}){\kern 1.00006pt}\big\rangle.\end{array}

(11)

2.

For a ‘channel’
c\colon X\rightarrow\mathcal{D}(Y)
there is the

pushforward
distribution
c_{*}(\omega)\in\mathcal{D}(Y)
given
by:

\begin{array}[]{rcccl}c_{*}\Big(\sum_{i}r_{i}\big|{\kern 1.00006pt}x_{\kern 0.81949pt}\big\rangle\Big)&\coloneqq&\sum_{i}r_{i}\cdot c(x_{i})&=&\displaystyle\sum_{y\in Y}\,\textstyle\Big(\sum_{i}r_{i}\cdot c(x_{i})(y)\Big)\,\big|{\kern 1.00006pt}y{\kern 1.00006pt}\big\rangle.\end{array}

(12)

For another channel
d\colon Y\rightarrow\mathcal{D}(Z)
we can
now define the channel composite
d\mathbin{\mathchoice{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\displaystyle\circ$\crcr$\displaystyle\cdot$\crcr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\textstyle\circ$\crcr$\textstyle\cdot$\crcr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptstyle\circ$\crcr$\scriptstyle\cdot$\crcr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\circ$\crcr$\scriptscriptstyle\cdot$\crcr}}}}c\colon X\rightarrow\mathcal{D}(Z)
as
\big(d\mathbin{\mathchoice{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\displaystyle\circ$\crcr$\displaystyle\cdot$\crcr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\textstyle\circ$\crcr$\textstyle\cdot$\crcr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptstyle\circ$\crcr$\scriptstyle\cdot$\crcr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\circ$\crcr$\scriptscriptstyle\cdot$\crcr}}}}c\big)(x)=d_{*}\big(c(x)\big)
.

A channel
c\colon X\rightarrow\mathcal{D}(Y)
gives a distribution
c(x)\in\mathcal{D}(Y)
for each
x\in X
. This corresponds to what is commonly
described as a conditional probability
\mathsl{P}(y|x)
. The pushforward
can be understood as application of the channel ‘in probability’, as
expressed by the formulation in the middle (
12
).

6
The Boltzmann distribution on numbers

We now transform the Boltzmann-on-multisets distribution

\mathsl{BoM}
into a ‘Boltzmann-on-numbers’ distribution

\mathsl{BoN}
. In physical terms, it describes the spread of
particles over the various energy levels
\{0,\ldots,N{\kern-1.5pt}-{\kern-1.5pt}1\}
. There is a systematic way to do this transformation, via
frequentist learning
\mathsl{Flrn}
from (
10
). The triangle below
defines
\mathsl{BoN}
as composition of two functions.

\textstyle{\mathcal{D}\Big(\mathcal{M}[K](\boldsymbol{N})\Big)\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\mathsl{Flrn}_{*}}
\textstyle{\big\{0,\ldots,(N{\kern-1.5pt}-{\kern-1.5pt}1)\cdot K\big\}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\mathsl{BoM}[N,K]}
\scriptstyle{\mathsl{BoN}[N,K]}
\textstyle{\mathcal{D}\big(\boldsymbol{N}\big)\hbox to0.0pt{$\;=\mathcal{D}\big(\{0,\ldots,N{\kern-1.5pt}-{\kern-1.5pt}1\}\big)$\hss}}

(16)

The diagram contains the pushforward
\mathsl{Flrn}_{*}\colon\mathcal{D}\big(\mathcal{M}[K](X)\big)\rightarrow\mathcal{D}(X)
of the frequentist
learning channel
\mathsl{Flrn}\colon\mathcal{M}[K](X)\rightarrow\mathcal{D}(X)
, as
introduced in (

\mathsl{BoN}[N,K](i)
gives the energy for a randomly chosen

Definition 6.1

For numbers
N,K\geq 1
and
0\leq i\leq(N{\kern-1.5pt}-{\kern-1.5pt}1)\cdot K
we
define the Boltzmann-on-numbers distribution as:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mathsl{BoN}[N,K](i)&\coloneqq&\mathsl{Flrn}_{*}\Big(\mathsl{BoM}[N,K](i)\Big)\\[6.00006pt]
&=&\displaystyle\sum_{0\leq j<N}\,\sum_{\varphi\in\mathcal{M}[K,i](\boldsymbol{N})}\,\frac{({\kern 1.0pt}\varphi{\kern 1.0pt})\cdot\varphi(j)}{C_{N}(K,i)\cdot K}\,\big|{\kern 1.00006pt}j{\kern 1.00006pt}\big\rangle.\end{array}

(17)

A subtle point is that if
i<N

over

\mathsl{sum}(\varphi)=i

\varphi(j)=0

special case

We double-check that the probabilities in (
17

up to one:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\displaystyle\sum_{0\leq j<N}\,\sum_{\varphi\in\mathcal{M}[K,i](\boldsymbol{N})}\,\frac{({\kern 1.0pt}\varphi{\kern 1.0pt})\cdot\varphi(j)}{C_{N}(K,i)\cdot K}&=&\displaystyle\sum_{\varphi\in\mathcal{M}[K,i](\boldsymbol{N})}\,\frac{({\kern 1.0pt}\varphi{\kern 1.0pt})\cdot\sum_{0\leq j<N}\varphi(j)}{C_{N}(K,i)\cdot K}\\[10.00002pt]
&=&\displaystyle\sum_{\varphi\in\mathcal{M}[K,i](\boldsymbol{N})}\,\frac{({\kern 1.0pt}\varphi{\kern 1.0pt})\cdot\|\varphi\|}{C_{N}(K,i)\cdot K}\\[10.00002pt]
&=&\displaystyle\sum_{\varphi\in\mathcal{M}[K,i](\boldsymbol{N})}\,\frac{({\kern 1.0pt}\varphi{\kern 1.0pt})}{C_{N}(K,i)}\hskip 5.0pt\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{NnomialMltEqn}}}{{=}}}\hskip 5.0pt1.\end{array}

&=&\displaystyle\sum_{\varphi\in\mathcal{M}[K,i](\boldsymbol{N})}\,\frac{({\kern 1.0pt}\varphi{\kern 1.0pt})\cdot\mathsl{sum}(\varphi)}{C_{N}(K,i)\cdot K}\\[10.00002pt]
&=&\displaystyle\sum_{\varphi\in\mathcal{M}[K,i](\boldsymbol{N})}\,\frac{({\kern 1.0pt}\varphi{\kern 1.0pt})\cdot i}{C_{N}(K,i)\cdot K}\\[13.99995pt]
&\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{NnomialMltEqn}}}{{=}}}&\displaystyle\frac{C_{N}(K,i)\cdot i}{C_{N}(K,i)\cdot K}\hskip 5.0pt=\hskip 5.0pt\frac{i}{K}.\end{array}

[formula]

We conclude this section with two alternative formulations of the
Boltzmann-on-numbers distributions. The second point below show that
this distribution can be obtained directly from the uniform
distribution on sequences (microstates), without considering any
multisets (macrostates).

Proposition 6.4

let
N\geq 1
and
K\geq 0
with
0\leq i\leq(N{\kern-1.5pt}-{\kern-1.5pt}1)\cdot K
.

1.

The Boltzmann-on-numbers
distribution can be expressed purely in terms of
N
-nomials:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mathsl{BoN}[N,K](i)&=&\displaystyle\sum_{0\leq j<\min(N,i+1)}\,\frac{C_{N}(K-1,i-j)}{C_{N}(K,i)}\,\big|{\kern 1.00006pt}j{\kern 1.00006pt}\big\rangle.\end{array}

(18)

2.

Recall the set
S\subseteq\boldsymbol{N}^{K}
with the uniform distribution distribution

\mathsl{uni}\in\mathcal{D}(S)
from
Lemma
5.4
(
2
). For any
0\leq n<N
let
\pi_{n}\colon\boldsymbol{N}^{K}\rightarrow\boldsymbol{N}
be the
n
-th projection from a sequence:
\pi_{n}(v)=v_{n}
. Then, for each
n
,

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mathcal{D}\big(\pi_{n}\big)(\mathsl{uni})&=&\mathsl{BoN}[N,K](i).\end{array}

1.

The result follows from the equation:

\begin{array}[b]{rcl}\displaystyle\sum_{\varphi\in\mathcal{M}[K,i](\boldsymbol{N})}\,({\kern 1.0pt}\varphi{\kern 1.0pt})\cdot\varphi(j)&\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{MltCoefEqn}}}{{=}}}&\displaystyle\sum_{\varphi\in\mathcal{M}[K,i](\boldsymbol{N}),\,\varphi(j)\neq 0}\,\frac{K!}{\varphi(j)!\cdot\prod_{m\neq j}\varphi(m)!}\cdot\varphi(j)\\[10.00002pt]
&=&\displaystyle K\cdot\sum_{\varphi\in\mathcal{M}[K,i](\boldsymbol{N}),\,\varphi(j)\neq 0}\,\frac{(K-1)!}{(\varphi(j)-1)!\cdot\prod_{m\neq j}\varphi(m)!}\\[11.99998pt]
&=&\displaystyle K\cdot\sum_{\varphi\in\mathcal{M}[K](\boldsymbol{N}),\,\mathsl{sum}(\varphi)=i,\,\varphi(j)\neq 0}\,({\kern 1.0pt}\varphi-1|{\kern 1.00006pt}j{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle{\kern 1.0pt})\\[11.99998pt]
&=&\displaystyle K\cdot\sum_{\psi\in\mathcal{M}[K-1](\boldsymbol{N}),\,\mathsl{sum}(\psi)=i-j}\,({\kern 1.0pt}\psi{\kern 1.0pt})\\[13.99995pt]
&\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{NnomialMltEqn}}}{{=}}}&K\cdot C_{N}\big(K-1,i-j\big).\end{array}

2.

We use the previous point:

\begin{array}[b]{rcl}\mathcal{D}\big(\pi_{n}\big)(\mathsl{uni})&=&\displaystyle\sum_{v\in S}\,\frac{1}{C_{N}(K,i)}\,\big|{\kern 1.00006pt}\pi_{n}(v){\kern 1.00006pt}\big\rangle\\
&=&\displaystyle\sum_{0\leq j<N}\,\sum_{v\in S,\,\pi_{n}(v)=j}\,\frac{1}{C_{N}(K,i)}\,\big|{\kern 1.00006pt}j{\kern 1.00006pt}\big\rangle\\
&=&\displaystyle\sum_{0\leq j<\min(N,i+1)}\,\sum_{w\in\boldsymbol{N}^{K-1},\,\mathsl{sum}(w)=i-j}\,\frac{1}{C_{N}(K,i)}\,\big|{\kern 1.00006pt}j{\kern 1.00006pt}\big\rangle\\[13.99995pt]
&\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{NnomialSeqEqn}}}{{=}}}&\displaystyle\sum_{0\leq j<\min(N,i+1)}\,\frac{C_{N}(K-1,i-j)}{C_{N}(K,i)}\,\big|{\kern 1.00006pt}j{\kern 1.00006pt}\big\rangle.\\
&\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{BoltzmannNumAltPropNomEqn}}}{{=}}}&\mathsl{BoN}[N,K](i).\end{array}

[formula]

7
The Boltzmann distribution on energy levels

As we briefly discussed above, physicists see the Boltzmann-on-numbers
distribution
\mathsl{BoN}[N,K](i)
as a distribution on energy
levels
\{0,\ldots,N{\kern-1.5pt}-{\kern-1.5pt}1\}=\boldsymbol{N}
, arising from
K

particles with total energy
i
. The pictures that they look at
typically have shapes as in the top row of
Figure
1
. They see the number
N
of energy
levels as a number that can go to infinity, in order to get an
approximation of a continuous distribution, see
Subsection
8.1
for an impression. Here the
focus is not such approximation, but on the precise form of the
relevant discrete probability distributions. So far we used a
constraint on the number
i
of the form
0\leq i\leq(N{\kern-1.5pt}-{\kern-1.5pt}1)\cdot K
. It turns out that the constructions that we used so far
can be simplified when we assume
i<N
. This is a crucial
observation, elaborated in the theorem below. The assumption
i<N

makes sense, when
N
is seen as a number that can become arbitrarily
big.

Theorem 7.1

Consider numbers
N,K\geq 1
.

1.

For
0\leq n\leq N
,

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\displaystyle\sum_{0\leq i<n}\,C_{N}(K,i)&=&\displaystyle\frac{n}{K}\cdot\left(\!\binom{K}{n}\!\right).\end{array}

The right-hand-side does not depend on
N
, intuitively because
if
\mathsl{sum}(\vec{m})
equals
i
, for
\vec{m}\in\boldsymbol{N}^{K}
where

i\leq n<N
, then the number
N
is big enough to so that the
requirement
m_{j}\in\boldsymbol{N}
does not impose any restriction.

2.

When
i<N
, there is a simple formula
for the
N
-nomial, namely:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}C_{N}(K,i)&=&\displaystyle\left(\!\binom{K}{i}\!\right).\end{array}

1.

By induction on
K\geq 1
. First,

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\displaystyle\sum_{0\leq i\leq n}\,C_{N}(1,i)\hskip 5.0pt\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{NnomialSeqEqn}}}{{=}}}\hskip 5.0pt\sum_{0\leq i\leq n}\,\Big|{\kern 1.00006pt}\big\{\vec{n}\in\boldsymbol{N}^{1}\;\big|\;\mathsl{sum}(\vec{n})=i\big\}{\kern 1.00006pt}\Big|&=&\displaystyle\sum_{0\leq i\leq n}\,1\\
&=&n\hskip 5.0pt=\hskip 5.0pt\displaystyle\frac{n}{1}\cdot\left(\!\binom{1}{n}\!\right).\end{array}

Next we use that we sum over
i<n\leq N
in the first
line below. When we count sequences of length
K{\kern-2.0pt}+{\kern-2.0pt}1
with sum
i<n
, then all elements of the sequence fit in the set
\boldsymbol{N}=\{0,\ldots,N{\kern-1.5pt}-{\kern-1.5pt}1\}
; hence we can express the number

C_{N}(K{\kern-2.0pt}+{\kern-2.0pt}1,i)
of sequences of length
K{\kern-2.0pt}+{\kern-2.0pt}1
as sum of
numbers of sequences of length
K
.

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\displaystyle\sum_{0\leq i<n}\,C_{N}\big(K+1,i\big)&=&\displaystyle\sum_{0\leq i<n}\,\sum_{0\leq j\leq i}C_{N}\big(K,i-j\big)\\[10.00002pt]
&=&\displaystyle\sum_{0\leq i<n}\,\sum_{0\leq j\leq i}C_{N}\big(K,j\big)\\[11.99998pt]
&\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textrm{(IH)}}}{{=}}}&\displaystyle\displaystyle\sum_{0\leq i<n}\,\frac{i+1}{K}\cdot\left(\!\binom{K}{i+1}\!\right)\\[10.00002pt]
&=&\displaystyle\displaystyle\sum_{0\leq i<n}\,\frac{i+1}{K}\cdot\frac{(K+i)!}{(K-1)!\cdot(i+1)!}\\[10.00002pt]
&=&\displaystyle\displaystyle\sum_{1\leq i<n}\,\left(\!\binom{K+1}{i}\!\right)\\[11.99998pt]
&=&\displaystyle\left(\!\binom{n}{K+1}\!\right)\qquad\mbox{by Lemma~\ref{ChooseSumLem}~\eqref{ChooseSumLemZero}}\\[8.00003pt]
&=&\displaystyle\frac{(K+n)!}{(n-1)!\cdot(K+1)!}\hskip 5.0pt=\hskip 5.0pt\frac{n}{K+1}\cdot\left(\!\binom{K+1}{n}\!\right).\end{array}

2.

By the previous item, for
i<N
, so that
i{\kern-2.0pt}+{\kern-2.0pt}1\leq N
,

\hskip-6.00006pt\begin{array}[b]{rcl}C_{N}(K,i)&=&\displaystyle\left(\sum_{0\leq j<i+1}\,C_{N}\big(K,j\big)\right)-\left(\sum_{0\leq j<i}\,C_{N}\big(K,j\big)\right)\\[13.99995pt]
&=&\displaystyle\frac{i+1}{K}\cdot\left(\!\binom{K}{i+1}\!\right)-\frac{i}{K}\cdot\left(\!\binom{K}{i}\!\right)\\[10.00002pt]
&=&\displaystyle\frac{(K+i)!}{K!\cdot i!}-\frac{(K+i-1)!}{K!\cdot(i-1)!}\\[10.00002pt]
&=&\displaystyle\frac{(K+i-1)!}{K!\cdot(i-1)!}\cdot\left(\frac{K+i}{i}-1\right)\hskip 5.0pt=\hskip 5.0pt\displaystyle\frac{(K+i-1)!}{(K-1)!\cdot i!}\hskip 5.0pt=\hskip 5.0pt\displaystyle\left(\!\binom{K}{i}\!\right)\!.\end{array}

[formula]

We finally define the Boltzmann-on-energy distribution, as a a
distribution on energy levels, via the simplifications from the
previous theorem. To emphasise that we consider the number
i
as the
total energy we shall now rename it to
e
.

Definition 7.2

Let natural numbers
E\geq 1
and
K\geq 2
be given, where
K

stands for the number of particles and
E
for the maximal / total
energy. We define the Boltzmann-on-energy function
\mathsl{BoE}[E]\colon\{K\in\mathbb{N}\;|\;K\geq 2\}\rightarrow\mathcal{D}\big(\{0,\ldots,E\}\big)
as:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mathsl{BoE}[E](K)\hskip 5.0pt\coloneqq\hskip 5.0pt\mathsl{BoN}\big[E{\kern-2.0pt}+{\kern-2.0pt}1,K\big](E)&=&\displaystyle\sum_{0\leq j\leq E}\,\frac{\big(\binom{K-1}{E-j}\big)}{\big(\binom{K}{E}\big)}\,\big|{\kern 1.00006pt}j{\kern 1.00006pt}\big\rangle.\end{array}

(19)

Notice that in the Boltzmann-on-numbers distribution in the middle
of (
19
) we use
N=E+1
and
i=E
, so that
condition
i<N
from Theorem
7.1
holds by construction.
This justifies the simple form at the right-hand-side
of (
19
), via
Proposition
6.4
(
1
)
and Theorem
7.1
(
2

\begin{array}[b]{rcl}\mathsl{BoE}[E](K)\hskip 5.0pt=\hskip 5.0pt\mathsl{BoN}\big[E{\kern-2.0pt}+{\kern-2.0pt}1,K\big](E)&\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{BoltzmannNumAltPropNomEqn}}}{{=}}}&\displaystyle\sum_{0\leq j<\min(E+1,E+1)}\,\frac{C_{N}(K-1,E-j)}{C_{N}(K,E)}\,\big|{\kern 1.00006pt}j{\kern 1.00006pt}\big\rangle.\\[11.99998pt]
&=&\displaystyle\sum_{0\leq j\leq E}\,\frac{\big(\binom{K-1}{E-j}\big)}{\big(\binom{K}{E}\big)}\,\big|{\kern 1.00006pt}j{\kern 1.00006pt}\big\rangle.\end{array}

Proposition 7.4

Let
E\geq 1
and
K\geq 2
.

1.

For
K=2
particles the
Boltzmann-on-energy distribution is uniform, of the form:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mathsl{BoE}[E](2)&=&\displaystyle\sum_{0\leq j\leq E}\,\frac{1}{E+1}\,\big|{\kern 1.00006pt}j{\kern 1.00006pt}\big\rangle.\end{array}

2.

The mean of the Boltzmann-on-energy
distribution is given by:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mathsl{Mean}\Big(\mathsl{BoE}[E](K)\Big)&=&\displaystyle\frac{E}{K}.\end{array}

3.

And its variance is:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mathsl{Var}\Big(\mathsl{BoE}[E](K)\Big)&=&\displaystyle\frac{E\cdot(E+K)\cdot(K-1)}{K^{2}\cdot(K+1)}.\end{array}

1.

For each
0\leq j\leq e
one has:

\begin{array}[]{rcccccl}\mathsl{BoE}[E](2)(j)&=&\displaystyle\frac{\big(\binom{2-1}{E-j}\big)}{\big(\binom{2}{E}\big)}&=&\displaystyle\frac{\frac{(E-j)!}{0!\cdot(E-j)!}}{\frac{(e+1)!}{1!\cdot e!}}&=&\displaystyle\frac{1}{E+1}.\end{array}

2.

The equation about the mean is obtained from
Lemma
6.3
(
2
), since:

\begin{array}[]{rcccl}\mathsl{Mean}\Big(\mathsl{BoE}[E](K)\Big)&\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{BoltzmannEneEqn}}}{{=}}}&\mathsl{Mean}\Big(\mathsl{BoN}\big[E{\kern-2.0pt}+{\kern-2.0pt}1,K\big](E)\Big)&=&\displaystyle\frac{E}{K}.\end{array}

3.

Proving the variance equation is more work. We first
show that:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\displaystyle\sum_{0\leq j\leq E}\,\frac{\big(\binom{K-1}{E-j}\big)}{\big(\binom{K}{E}\big)}\cdot j^{2}&=&\displaystyle\frac{E\cdot(K-1+2E)}{K\cdot(K+1)}.\end{array}

[formula]

Indeed:

\hskip-5.0pt\begin{array}[]{rcl}\lx@intercol\lx@add@cssclass{ltx_eqn_lefteqn}$\displaystyle\displaystyle\sum_{0\leq j\leq E}\,\frac{\big(\binom{K-1}{E-j}\big)}{\big(\binom{K}{E}\big)}\cdot j^{2}$\mbox{}\hfil\lx@intercol\\[6.00006pt]
&=&\displaystyle\frac{1}{\big(\binom{K}{E}\big)}\cdot\sum_{0\leq j\leq E}\,\left(\!\binom{K-1}{j}\!\right)\cdot(E-j)^{2}\\[11.99998pt]
&=&\displaystyle\frac{1}{\big(\binom{K}{E}\big)}\cdot\left[\,E^{2}\cdot\sum_{0\leq j\leq E}\,\left(\!\binom{K-1}{j}\!\right)-2E\cdot\sum_{0\leq j\leq E}\,\left(\!\binom{K-1}{j}\!\right)\cdot j\right.\\
&&\hskip 80.00012pt+\;\displaystyle\left.\sum_{0\leq j\leq E}\,\left(\!\binom{K-1}{j}\!\right)\cdot j^{2}\,\right]\\[11.99998pt]
&=&\displaystyle\frac{1}{\big(\binom{K}{E}\big)}\cdot\left[\,E^{2}\cdot\left(\!\binom{E+1}{K-1}\!\right)-2E\cdot(K{\kern-1.5pt}-{\kern-1.5pt}1)\cdot\left(\!\binom{E}{K}\!\right)\right.\\
&&\hskip 80.00012pt+\;\displaystyle\left.K\cdot(K{\kern-1.5pt}-{\kern-1.5pt}1)\cdot\left(\!\binom{E-1}{K+1}\!\right)+(K{\kern-1.5pt}-{\kern-1.5pt}1)\cdot\left(\!\binom{E}{K}\!\right)\,\right]\\[13.99995pt]
&=&\displaystyle\frac{(K{\kern-1.5pt}-{\kern-1.5pt}1)!\cdot E!}{(K{\kern-2.0pt}+{\kern-2.0pt}E{\kern-1.5pt}-{\kern-1.5pt}1)!}\cdot\left[\,E^{2}\cdot\frac{(K{\kern-2.0pt}+{\kern-2.0pt}E{\kern-1.5pt}-{\kern-1.5pt}1)!}{E!\cdot(K{\kern-1.5pt}-{\kern-1.5pt}1)!}-(2E{\kern-1.5pt}-{\kern-1.5pt}1)\cdot(K{\kern-1.5pt}-{\kern-1.5pt}1)\cdot\frac{(K{\kern-2.0pt}+{\kern-2.0pt}E{\kern-1.5pt}-{\kern-1.5pt}1)!}{(E{\kern-1.5pt}-{\kern-1.5pt}1)!\cdot K!}\right.\\[8.00003pt]
&&\hskip 80.00012pt\displaystyle\left.+\;K\cdot(K{\kern-1.5pt}-{\kern-1.5pt}1)\cdot\frac{(K{\kern-2.0pt}+{\kern-2.0pt}E{\kern-1.5pt}-{\kern-1.5pt}1)!}{(E{\kern-1.5pt}-{\kern-1.5pt}2)!\cdot(K{\kern-2.0pt}+{\kern-2.0pt}1)!}\,\right]\\[6.00006pt]
&=&\displaystyle E^{2}-\frac{E\cdot(2E{\kern-1.5pt}-{\kern-1.5pt}1)\cdot(K{\kern-1.5pt}-{\kern-1.5pt}1)}{K}+\frac{(K{\kern-1.5pt}-{\kern-1.5pt}1)\cdot E\cdot(E{\kern-1.5pt}-{\kern-1.5pt}1)}{K+1}\\[8.00003pt]
&=&\displaystyle\frac{E^{2}\cdot K^{2}{\kern-2.0pt}+{\kern-2.0pt}E^{2}\cdot K{\kern-1.5pt}-{\kern-1.5pt}2E\cdot K^{2}{\kern-2.0pt}+{\kern-2.0pt}2E^{2}{\kern-2.0pt}+{\kern-2.0pt}E\cdot K^{2}{\kern-1.5pt}-{\kern-1.5pt}E{\kern-2.0pt}+{\kern-2.0pt}E^{2}\cdot K^{2}{\kern-1.5pt}-{\kern-1.5pt}E\cdot K^{2}{\kern-1.5pt}-{\kern-1.5pt}E^{2}\cdot K{\kern-2.0pt}+{\kern-2.0pt}E\cdot K}{K\cdot(K+1)}\\[8.00003pt]
&=&\displaystyle\frac{2E^{2}-E+E\cdot K}{K\cdot(K+1)}\hskip 5.0pt=\hskip 5.0pt\frac{E\cdot(K-1+2E)}{K\cdot(K+1)}.\end{array}

Now we come to variance itself, using the description of the mean:

\begin{array}[b]{rcl}\mathsl{Var}\Big(\mathsl{BoE}[E](K)\Big)&=&\displaystyle\sum_{0\leq j\leq E}\,\frac{\big(\binom{K-1}{E-j}\big)}{\big(\binom{K}{E}\big)}\cdot j^{2}\;-\;\left(\sum_{0\leq j\leq E}\,\frac{\big(\binom{K-1}{E-j}\big)}{\big(\binom{K}{E}\big)}\cdot j\right)^{2}\\[11.99998pt]
&\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(*)}}{{=}}}&\displaystyle\frac{E\cdot(K-1+2E)}{K\cdot(K+1)}-\left(\frac{E}{K}\right)^{2}\\[8.00003pt]
&=&\displaystyle\frac{e\cdot(K-1+2e)\cdot K-e^{2}\cdot(K+1)}{K^{2}\cdot(K+1)}\\[8.00003pt]
&=&\displaystyle\frac{E\cdot(E+K)\cdot(K-1)}{K^{2}\cdot(K+1)}.\end{array}

[formula]

8
Final observations

Having seen precise derivations of various discrete Boltzmann
distributions we lean back for some broader observations. The style
of section is a bit less thorough and part of it may be elaborated
further in future publications.

8.1
Approximations of the discrete Boltzmann
distributions

What is called the discrete or continuous Boltzmann distribution in
statistical mechanics is an approximation of what we have described
as the Boltzmann-on-energy distribution. We briefly sketch how this
can work, in several ways.

For total energy
E
with
K
particles the Boltzmann-on-energy
distribution
\mathsl{BoE}[E](K)
has mean
\mu\coloneqq\frac{E}{K}
,
see Proposition
7.4
(
2
).
We can look at the ratio between two successive probabilities, at

j
and at
j{\kern-2.0pt}+{\kern-2.0pt}1
. This gives, when we substitute
E=K\cdot\mu
,

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\displaystyle\frac{\mathsl{BoE}[E](K)(j{\kern-2.0pt}+{\kern-2.0pt}1)}{\mathsl{BoE}[E](K)(j)}\hskip 5.0pt\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{BoltzmannEneEqn}}}{{=}}}\hskip 5.0pt\displaystyle\frac{\big(\binom{K-1}{E-(j+1)}\big)}{\big(\binom{K-1}{E-j}\big)}&=&\displaystyle\frac{(K+E-j-3)!\cdot(E-j)!}{(K+E-j-2)!\cdot(E-j-1)!}\\[10.00002pt]
&=&\displaystyle\frac{E-j}{K+E-j-2}\\[8.00003pt]
&=&\displaystyle\frac{K\cdot\mu-j}{K+K\cdot\mu-j-2}\;\xrightarrow{\hskip 8.19447ptK\rightarrow\infty\hskip 8.19447pt}\frac{\mu}{\mu+1}.\end{array}

This suggests that we can use as approximation of the
Boltzmann-on-energy distribution, in presence of many particles, the
normalisation of:

\sum_{0\leq j\leq E}\,\left(\frac{\mu}{\mu+1}\right)^{j}\,\big|{\kern 1.00006pt}j{\kern 1.00006pt}\big\rangle

(20)

Distributions with a constant ratio between successive
probabilities typically occur for (normalisations of) distributions
of the form
\sum_{j}e^{-b\cdot j}|{\kern 1.00006pt}j{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle
, for a suitable constant

b
. Indeed, the associated ratio is:

\begin{array}[]{rcccl}\displaystyle\frac{e^{-b\cdot(j+1)}}{e^{-b\cdot j}}&=&e^{-b\cdot(j+1)+b\cdot j}&=&e^{-b}.\end{array}

In our Boltzmann-on-energy situation we can solve
e^{-b}=\frac{\mu}{\mu+1}
and get:

\begin{array}[]{rcccccccl}b&=&\displaystyle-\ln\left(\frac{\mu}{\mu+1}\right)&=&\displaystyle\ln\left(\frac{\mu+1}{\mu}\right)&=&\displaystyle\ln\left(1+\frac{1}{\mu}\right)&\approx&\displaystyle\frac{1}{\mu}.\end{array}

The latter approximation is common for small

\frac{1}{\mu}
, that is, for large
\mu
. We thus get as alternative
discrete approximation of the Boltzmann-on-energy distribution, the
normalisation of:

\sum_{0\leq j\leq E}\,e^{-\frac{j}{\mu}}\,\big|{\kern 1.00006pt}j{\kern 1.00006pt}\big\rangle

(21)

The principle of maximum entropy introduced in
[
10
]
is used
in statistical mechanics to derive the equilibrium distributions. We
sketch how this works in the current situation, with
N
energy levels
and a given mean
\mu
. The aim is to find the distribution
\omega

on
\{0,\ldots,E\}
with
\mathsl{Mean}(\omega)=\mu
and maximum entropty

-\sum_{i}\omega(i)\cdot\ln\big(\omega(i)\big)
. This can be done
via Lagrange’s multiplier method, as described for a dice
in
[
2
, Example 5.3]
. We give an impression of how this
works.

Take as function:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}H\big(\vec{r},\alpha,\beta\big)&\coloneqq&\displaystyle-\sum_{0\leq j\leq E}r_{j}\cdot\ln(r_{j})-\alpha\cdot\left(1-\sum_{0\leq j\leq E}r_{j}\right)-\beta\cdot\left(\mu-\sum_{0\leq j\leq E}r_{j}\cdot j\right).\end{array}

Taking partial derivatives and setting them to zero yields,
when we abbreviate
x=e^{\beta}
.

\begin{array}[]{rcl}1&=&\displaystyle\sum_{0\leq j\leq E}\,r_{j}\hskip 5.0pt=\hskip 5.0pt\displaystyle\sum_{0\leq j\leq E}\,e^{-1+\alpha+\beta\cdot j}\hskip 5.0pt=\hskip 5.0pt\displaystyle\sum_{0\leq j\leq E}\,\frac{(e^{\beta})^{i}}{e^{1-\alpha}}\hskip 5.0pt=\hskip 5.0pt\displaystyle\sum_{0\leq j\leq E}\,\frac{x^{i}}{e^{1-\alpha}}\\[10.00002pt]
\mu&=&\displaystyle\sum_{0\leq j\leq E}\,r_{j}\cdot j\hskip 5.0pt=\hskip 5.0pt\displaystyle\sum_{0\leq j\leq E}\,e^{-1+\alpha+\beta\cdot j}\cdot j\hskip 5.0pt=\hskip 5.0pt\displaystyle\sum_{0\leq j\leq E}\,\frac{(e^{\beta})^{j}\cdot j}{e^{1-\alpha}}\hskip 5.0pt=\hskip 5.0pt\displaystyle\sum_{0\leq j\leq E}\,\frac{x^{j}\cdot j}{e^{1-\alpha}}.\end{array}

Subtracting the second line from
\mu
times the first one,
and then multiplying with
e^{1-\alpha}
yields a polynomial equation
of order
E
, namely:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}0&=&\displaystyle\sum_{0\leq j\leq E}\,x^{j}\cdot(i-\mu).\end{array}

An approximate solution
s>0
can be found, for instance via
the
nroots
function in Python’s
sympy

library. We then get a distribution on
\{0,\ldots,E\}
with mean

\mu
and with maximal entropy, as normalisation of:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\displaystyle\sum_{0\leq j\leq E}\,s^{j}\,\big|{\kern 1.00006pt}j{\kern 1.00006pt}\big\rangle&=&\displaystyle\sum_{0\leq j\leq E}\,e^{-b\cdot j}\,\big|{\kern 1.00006pt}j{\kern 1.00006pt}\big\rangle\quad\mbox{ for }b=-\ln(s).\end{array}

(22)

Finally, the formulations (
21
)
(and (
22
)) lead to a continuous approximation as
exponential with rate
\frac{1}{\mu}
, with probability densitiy
function (pdf) on
\mathbb{R}_{>0}
given by:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}j&\longmapsto&\frac{1}{\mu}\cdot e^{-\frac{j}{\mu}}\end{array}

(23)

Figure
3
gives an impression of these four
approximations (
20
) – (
23
). When
we look at the KL-divergences between the Boltzmann-on-energy
distribution and the three (discrete)
approximations (
20
) – (
22
), then the
last one performs slightly better — in this example.

and
K=5
particles occurs in all four

. On the

)

, for which
-\ln(s)=0.173

). Its

2.69
, which is bit higher than the entropy
2.67
of

\mathsl{BoE}[25](5)
.

8.2
A Markov chain with its stationary Boltzmann
distribution

In
[
9
]
several Markov chains are defined on a set

\mathcal{M}[K](X)
of multisets of size
K
. Here we describe a more
complicated chain that maintains not only the size of multisets, but
also their sum. Thus, our aim is to define a transition channel

\mathsl{shift}\colon\mathcal{M}[K,i](\boldsymbol{N})\rightarrow\mathcal{D}\big(\mathcal{M}[K,i](\boldsymbol{N})\big)
, forming a Markov chain. The
definition below is a bit technical, but the intuition is simple: we
randomly move one particle one energy level down and then randomly
move one particle one energy level up. Consider the multiset
\varphi=1|{\kern 1.00006pt}0{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle+2|{\kern 1.00006pt}1{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle+3|{\kern 1.00006pt}2{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle\in\mathcal{M}[6,8](\boldsymbol{3})
as a
configuration of
6
particles with a total energy of
8
. We can turn
it into other configurations, still with the same size
6
and
sum
8
, by carefully downgrading and upgrading single particles.

•

We can downgrade one particle from level
1
to level
0
. This
turns
\varphi
into an intermediate multiset
2|{\kern 1.00006pt}0{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle+1|{\kern 1.00006pt}1{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle+3|{\kern 1.00006pt}2{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle
via a shift from
1
to
0
. We now have to decide at
which level to upgrade. This can be at level
0
or at level
1
,
but not at level
2
since that is the highest level. Upgrading at
level
0
returns the original multiset
\varphi
, which is
possible; upgrading at level
1
turns the intermediate multiset
into
2|{\kern 1.00006pt}0{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle+4|{\kern 1.00006pt}2{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle
, via a shift from
1
to
2
. The
resulting multiset still has size
6
and sum
8
, as required.

•

We can also downgrade one particle in
\varphi
at level
2
,
resulting in an intermediate multiset
1|{\kern 1.00006pt}0{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle+3|{\kern 1.00006pt}1{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle+2|{\kern 1.00006pt}2{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle
, via a shift from
2
to
1
. Now we can decide to
upgrade at level
0
, giving
4|{\kern 1.00006pt}1{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle+2|{\kern 1.00006pt}2{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle
, or at level
1
,
giving the original multiset
1|{\kern 1.00006pt}0{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle+2|{\kern 1.00006pt}1{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle+3|{\kern 1.00006pt}2{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle
.

As an aside, this downgrading and upgrading works like the
draw-delete and draw-add for multiset partitions in
[
6
]
,
in the context of Ewens distributions in population biology.

We are now ready to define the channel
\mathsl{shift}\colon\mathcal{M}[K,i](\boldsymbol{N})\rightarrow\mathcal{D}\big(\mathcal{M}[K,i](\boldsymbol{N})\big)
in general form. The letters
‘
d
’ and ‘
u
’ are used to suggest the downgrade and the upgrade, and

\varphi_{d}
is what we called the ‘intermediate’ multiset. Notice
that the downgrade
d
cannot happen at level
0
and the upgrade
u

not at level
N{\kern-1.5pt}-{\kern-1.5pt}1
.

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mathsl{shift}(\varphi)&\coloneqq&\displaystyle\frac{\varphi(0)}{\|\varphi\|}\big|{\kern 1.00006pt}\varphi{\kern 1.00006pt}\big\rangle+\sum_{0<d<N,\,\varphi(d)>0}\;\sum_{0\leq u<N-1,\,\varphi_{d}(u)>0}\\[11.99998pt]
&&\hskip 80.00012pt\displaystyle\frac{\varphi(d)}{\|\varphi\|}\cdot\frac{\varphi_{d}(u)}{\|\varphi_{d}\|-\varphi_{d}(N{\kern-1.5pt}-{\kern-1.5pt}1)}\,\left|\vrule height=8.5pt,depth=4.5pt,width=0.0pt{\kern 1.00006pt}\right.{\kern-1.99997pt}\varphi_{d}-1|{\kern 1.00006pt}u{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle+1|{\kern 1.00006pt}u{\kern-2.0pt}+{\kern-2.0pt}1{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle{\kern-1.99997pt}\left.\vrule height=8.5pt,depth=4.5pt,width=0.0pt{\kern 0.0pt}\right>,\\[10.00002pt]
&&\quad\mbox{where }\varphi_{d}\coloneqq\varphi-1|{\kern 1.00006pt}d{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle+1|{\kern 1.00006pt}d{\kern-1.5pt}-{\kern-1.5pt}1{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle.\end{array}

This shift function forms a Markov chain on
the set of multisets
\mathcal{M}[K,i](\boldsymbol{N})
with the crucial
property that the Boltzmann-on-multisets distribution is stationary,
for this chain. This means that it is a fixed point of the
pushforward (
12
), corresponding to a statistical
equilibrium:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mathsl{shift}_{*}\Big(\mathsl{BoM}[N,K](i)\Big)&=&\mathsl{BoM}[N,K](i).\end{array}

Interestingly, we can turn the shift on multisets into a shift on
numbers, now with the Boltzmann-on-numbers distribution as fixed point.
We use the frequentist learning function as a channel
\mathsl{Flrn}\colon\mathcal{M}[K,i](\boldsymbol{N})\rightarrow\mathcal{D}(\boldsymbol{N})
and
construct its ‘Bayesian inversion’
\mathsl{Flrn}^{{\dagger}}\colon\boldsymbol{N}\rightarrow\mathcal{D}\big(\mathcal{M}[K,i](\boldsymbol{N})\big)
, with the
Boltzmann-on-multisets distribution as prior. We refer
to
[
8
]
for a general account and only provide
the relevant definitions here. We use, for
0\leq j<N
,

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mathsl{Flrn}^{{\dagger}}(j)&\coloneqq&\displaystyle\sum_{\varphi\in\mathcal{M}[K,i](\boldsymbol{N})}\,\frac{({\kern 1.0pt}\varphi{\kern 1.0pt})\cdot\varphi(j)}{\sum_{\psi\in\mathcal{M}[K,i](\boldsymbol{N})}({\kern 1.0pt}\psi{\kern 1.0pt})\cdot\psi(j)}\,\big|{\kern 1.00006pt}\varphi{\kern 1.00006pt}\big\rangle.\end{array}

The shift-on-numbers channel
\boldsymbol{N}\rightarrow\mathcal{D}(\boldsymbol{N})
is then obtained via channel composition
\mathbin{\mathchoice{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\displaystyle\circ$\crcr$\displaystyle\cdot$\crcr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\textstyle\circ$\crcr$\textstyle\cdot$\crcr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptstyle\circ$\crcr$\scriptstyle\cdot$\crcr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\circ$\crcr$\scriptscriptstyle\cdot$\crcr}}}}

from Definition
5.1
, as:

\begin{array}[]{ccrcl}\mathsl{Flrn}\mathbin{\mathchoice{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\displaystyle\circ$\crcr$\displaystyle\cdot$\crcr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\textstyle\circ$\crcr$\textstyle\cdot$\crcr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptstyle\circ$\crcr$\scriptstyle\cdot$\crcr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\circ$\crcr$\scriptscriptstyle\cdot$\crcr}}}}\mathsl{shift}\mathbin{\mathchoice{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\displaystyle\circ$\crcr$\displaystyle\cdot$\crcr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\textstyle\circ$\crcr$\textstyle\cdot$\crcr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptstyle\circ$\crcr$\scriptstyle\cdot$\crcr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\circ$\crcr$\scriptscriptstyle\cdot$\crcr}}}}\mathsl{Flrn}^{{\dagger}}&\quad\mbox{so}&\Big(\mathsl{Flrn}\mathbin{\mathchoice{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\displaystyle\circ$\crcr$\displaystyle\cdot$\crcr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\textstyle\circ$\crcr$\textstyle\cdot$\crcr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptstyle\circ$\crcr$\scriptstyle\cdot$\crcr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\circ$\crcr$\scriptscriptstyle\cdot$\crcr}}}}\mathsl{shift}\mathbin{\mathchoice{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\displaystyle\circ$\crcr$\displaystyle\cdot$\crcr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\textstyle\circ$\crcr$\textstyle\cdot$\crcr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptstyle\circ$\crcr$\scriptstyle\cdot$\crcr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\circ$\crcr$\scriptscriptstyle\cdot$\crcr}}}}\mathsl{Flrn}^{{\dagger}}\Big)_{*}&=&\mathsl{Flrn}_{*}\mathrel{\circ}\mathsl{shift}_{*}\mathrel{\circ}\big(\mathsl{Flrn}^{{\dagger}}\big)_{*}.\end{array}

It is not hard to see that it has the Boltzmann-on-numbers
distribution
\mathsl{BoN}[N,K](i)
as fixed point (equilibrium),
using that
\big(\mathsl{Flrn}^{{\dagger}}\big)_{*}\big(\mathsl{BoN}[N,K](i)\big)=\mathsl{BoM}[N,K](i)
.

In particular, the Boltzman-on-energy distribution

\mathsl{BoE}[25](5)
used in Figure
3
is a stationary
equilibrium, for a certain Markov chain. But it does not exactly match
the maximum entropy distribution (
22
). Hence, in the
discrete case, there is a mismatch between being an equilibrium (for
the above shift) and have maximal entropy (among distributions with
the same mean).

8.3
New multivariate distributions from

N
-nomials

In Lemma
3.2
(
2
) we have seen a
Vandermonde result for
N
-nomials. Such a Vandermonde result exists
for binomial (as special case) and also for multichoose coefficients,
see
[
7
]
. They can be generalised to
n
-ary form and are
then best expressed in terms of multisets. We briefly explore this
topic. This requires a bit more notation. For multisets
\varphi,\psi\in\mathcal{M}(X)
we write:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\varphi\leq\psi&\mbox{ when }&\varphi(x)\leq\psi(x)\mbox{ for each }x\in X\\
\varphi\leq_{K}\psi&\mbox{ when }&\varphi\leq\psi\mbox{ and }\|\varphi\|=K.\end{array}

Next we extend binomial and multichoose coefficients to
multisets, via pointwise definitions:

\begin{array}[]{rclcrcl}\displaystyle\binom{\psi}{\varphi}&\coloneqq&\displaystyle\prod_{x\in X}\binom{\psi(x)}{\varphi(x)}&\qquad\mbox{and}&\displaystyle\left(\!\binom{\psi}{\varphi}\!\right)&\coloneqq&\displaystyle\prod_{x\in X}\left(\!\binom{\psi(x)}{\varphi(x)}\!\right).\end{array}

The first definition requires that
\varphi\leq\psi
and
the second one that
\mathbf{1}\leq\psi
, where
\mathbf{1}=\sum_{x}1|{\kern 1.00006pt}x{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle\in\mathcal{M}(X)
is the multiset of singletons — assuming that the set

X
is finite.

These definitions are used in
[
7
,
8
]
for the
following multivariate versions of the Vandermonde properties, for

\psi\in\mathcal{M}[L](X)
.

\begin{array}[]{rclcrcl}\displaystyle\sum_{\varphi\leq_{K}\psi}\binom{\psi}{\varphi}&=&\displaystyle\binom{L}{K}&\qquad\mbox{and}&\displaystyle\sum_{\varphi\in\mathcal{M}[K](X)}\left(\!\binom{\psi}{\varphi}\!\right)&=&\displaystyle\left(\!\binom{L}{K}\!\right).\end{array}

(24)

These equations are then used to define the multivariate
hypergeometric and Pólya distributions, as distributions on
multisets of draws, in
\mathcal{D}\big(\mathcal{M}[K](X)\big)
.

\begin{array}[]{rclcrcl}\mathsl{hg}[K](\psi)&\coloneqq&\displaystyle\sum_{\varphi\leq_{K}\psi}\,\frac{\binom{\psi}{\varphi}}{\binom{L}{K}}\,\big|{\kern 1.00006pt}\varphi{\kern 1.00006pt}\big\rangle&\quad\mbox{and}&\mathsl{pl}[K](\psi)&\coloneqq&\displaystyle\sum_{\varphi\in\mathcal{M}[K](X)}\,\frac{\big(\binom{\psi}{\varphi}\big)}{\big(\binom{L}{K}\big)}\,\big|{\kern 1.00006pt}\varphi{\kern 1.00006pt}\big\rangle.\end{array}

The hypergeometric distribution captures the draw-and-remove
mode, where a drawn ball is removed from the urn. For this reason the
admissable draws
\varphi
of size
K
are required to satisfy

\varphi\leq_{K}\psi
, so that only balls that are actually in the
urn
\psi
can be drawn. The Pólya distribution uses the
draw-and-duplicate mode, where a drawn ball is returned to the urn,
together with an additional ball of the same colour,
see
[
7
]
for details. These two distributions both
interact well with the pushforward (
12
) of frequentist
learning, in the sense that:

\begin{array}[]{rclcrcl}\mathsl{Flrn}_{*}\Big(\mathsl{hg}[K](\psi)\Big)&=&\mathsl{Flrn}(\psi)&\quad\mbox{and}&\mathsl{Flrn}_{*}\Big(\mathsl{pl}[K](\psi)\Big)&=&\mathsl{Flrn}(\psi).\end{array}

(25)

These are fundamental results, see also
[
8
]
,
that express that applying frequentist learning ‘in probability’ to
the draws from urn
\psi
yields the distribution associated with the
original urn, again via frequentist learning.

These observations prepare us for a similar situation for

N
-nomials. We can generalise the Vandermonde result in
Lemma
3.2
(
2
) to multivariate
form via multisets. Let a number
N\geq 1
and a multiset

\psi\in\mathcal{M}[K](X)
of size
K
be given. We write
(N{\kern-1.5pt}-{\kern-1.5pt}1)\cdot\psi
for the
N{\kern-1.5pt}-{\kern-1.5pt}1
fold sum
\psi+\cdots+\psi

of multisets, so that
(N{\kern-1.5pt}-{\kern-1.5pt}1)\cdot\psi=\sum_{x}(N{\kern-1.5pt}-{\kern-1.5pt}1)\cdot\psi(x)\big|{\kern 1.00006pt}x{\kern 1.00006pt}\big\rangle
. For a number
0\leq i\leq(N{\kern-1.5pt}-{\kern-1.5pt}1)\cdot K
and a multiset
\varphi\in\mathcal{M}[i](X)
with
\varphi\leq(N{\kern-1.5pt}-{\kern-1.5pt}1)\cdot\psi
, we define a product of
N
-nomials:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}C_{N}\big(\psi,\varphi)&\coloneqq&\displaystyle\prod_{x\in X}C_{N}\big(\psi(x),\varphi(x)\big).\end{array}

(26)

Then one can show, analogously to (
24
),

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\displaystyle\sum_{\varphi\leq_{i}(N-1)\cdot\psi}\,C_{N}\big(\psi,\varphi\big)&=&C_{N}\big(K,i\big).\end{array}

This equation now gives rise to a new multivariate
distribution of nomials, namely:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mathsl{nom}[i](\psi)&\coloneqq&\displaystyle\sum_{\varphi\leq_{i}(N-1)\cdot\psi}\,\frac{C_{N}(\psi,\varphi)}{C_{N}(L,i)}\,\big|{\kern 1.00006pt}\varphi{\kern 1.00006pt}\big\rangle\qquad\mbox{where }N=|{\kern 1.00006pt}X{\kern 1.00006pt}|.\end{array}

(27)

This distribution is well-behaved, in the sense that
it satisfies the analogue of (
25
):

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mathsl{Flrn}_{*}\Big(\mathsl{nom}[i](\psi)\Big)&=&\mathsl{Flrn}(\psi).\end{array}

It is unclear if there is an urn model for this nomial
distribution in (
27
). It is some form of
generalisation of the hypergeometric distribution, since in the binary
case, when the underlying set
X
has two elements, one has

\mathsl{nom}[i](\psi)=\mathsl{hg}[i](\psi)
. But what it does more
generally is an open question. We conclude with an illustratation,
with urn / multiset
\psi=1|{\kern 1.00006pt}a{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle+5|{\kern 1.00006pt}b{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle+3|{\kern 1.00006pt}c{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle
; then:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\lx@intercol\lx@add@cssclass{ltx_eqn_lefteqn}$\displaystyle\mathsl{nom}[15](\psi)$\mbox{}\hfil\lx@intercol\\
&=&\frac{7}{156}\left|\vrule height=8.5pt,depth=4.5pt,width=0.0pt{\kern 1.00006pt}\right.{\kern-1.99997pt}2|{\kern 1.00006pt}a{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle+10|{\kern 1.00006pt}b{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle+3|{\kern 1.00006pt}c{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle{\kern-1.99997pt}\left.\vrule height=8.5pt,depth=4.5pt,width=0.0pt{\kern 0.0pt}\right>+\frac{5}{26}\left|\vrule height=8.5pt,depth=4.5pt,width=0.0pt{\kern 1.00006pt}\right.{\kern-1.99997pt}2|{\kern 1.00006pt}a{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle+9|{\kern 1.00006pt}b{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle+4|{\kern 1.00006pt}c{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle{\kern-1.99997pt}\left.\vrule height=8.5pt,depth=4.5pt,width=0.0pt{\kern 0.0pt}\right>+\frac{1}{26}\left|\vrule height=8.5pt,depth=4.5pt,width=0.0pt{\kern 1.00006pt}\right.{\kern-1.99997pt}1|{\kern 1.00006pt}a{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle+10|{\kern 1.00006pt}b{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle+4|{\kern 1.00006pt}c{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle{\kern-1.99997pt}\left.\vrule height=8.5pt,depth=4.5pt,width=0.0pt{\kern 0.0pt}\right>\\[1.99997pt]
&&\quad+\;\frac{15}{52}\left|\vrule height=8.5pt,depth=4.5pt,width=0.0pt{\kern 1.00006pt}\right.{\kern-1.99997pt}2|{\kern 1.00006pt}a{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle+8|{\kern 1.00006pt}b{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle+5|{\kern 1.00006pt}c{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle{\kern-1.99997pt}\left.\vrule height=8.5pt,depth=4.5pt,width=0.0pt{\kern 0.0pt}\right>+\frac{5}{52}\left|\vrule height=8.5pt,depth=4.5pt,width=0.0pt{\kern 1.00006pt}\right.{\kern-1.99997pt}1|{\kern 1.00006pt}a{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle+9|{\kern 1.00006pt}b{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle+5|{\kern 1.00006pt}c{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle{\kern-1.99997pt}\left.\vrule height=8.5pt,depth=4.5pt,width=0.0pt{\kern 0.0pt}\right>+\frac{1}{52}\left|\vrule height=8.5pt,depth=4.5pt,width=0.0pt{\kern 1.00006pt}\right.{\kern-1.99997pt}10|{\kern 1.00006pt}b{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle+5|{\kern 1.00006pt}c{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle{\kern-1.99997pt}\left.\vrule height=8.5pt,depth=4.5pt,width=0.0pt{\kern 0.0pt}\right>\\[1.99997pt]

This is neither a hypergeometric nor a Pólya distribution.

The
N
-nomials for multisets (
26
) can be used to
define a multivariate version of the Boltzmann-on-multisets
distribution, giving a ‘Boltzmann-on-multiple-multisets’ distribution.
This starts with
N
energy levels and a total energy
i
, and with a
multiset
\psi\in\mathcal{M}[K](X)
of sizes, where the finite set
X=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{L}\}
describes the different kinds
x_{j}
of
particles at hand.

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\hskip-1.99997pt\mathsl{BoMM}[N,\psi](i)&\!\coloneqq&\displaystyle\hskip-3.99994pt\sum_{\begin{array}[]{c}\\[-25.00003pt]
\scriptstyle\varphi_{1}\in\mathcal{M}[\psi(x_{1})](\boldsymbol{N}),\ldots,\varphi_{L}\in\mathcal{M}[\psi(x_{L})](\boldsymbol{N})\\[-6.99997pt]
\scriptstyle\mathsl{sum}(\varphi_{1})+\cdots+\mathsl{sum}(\varphi_{L})=E\end{array}}\!\frac{({\kern 1.0pt}\varphi_{1}{\kern 1.0pt})\cdot\ldots\cdot({\kern 1.0pt}\varphi_{L}{\kern 1.0pt})}{C_{N}(K,i)}\,\left|\vrule height=8.5pt,depth=4.5pt,width=0.0pt{\kern 1.00006pt}\right.{\kern-1.99997pt}\varphi_{1},\ldots,\varphi_{L}{\kern-1.99997pt}\left.\vrule height=8.5pt,depth=4.5pt,width=0.0pt{\kern 0.0pt}\right>.\end{array}

This is a distribution on
\mathcal{M}[\psi(x_{1})](\boldsymbol{N})\times\cdots\times\mathcal{M}[\psi(x_{L})](\boldsymbol{N}
. It can be
turned into a distribution on
L
-tuples of numbers via multiple
applications of frequentist learning. It is unclear whether such
multivariate Boltzmann distributions are useful in physics or in other
areas.

9
Conclusions

In the area of statistical mechanics one finds intuitive concrete
descriptions of particle configurations, with a fixed number of
particles and with a fixed total energey sum, see

e.g.

[
2
]
. This paper elaborates the underlying
combinatorics and statistics in a new manner, via
N
-nomials and
multisets. Physicists often skip this combinatorial part, or are even
reluctant to consider it, as in
[
15
]
. Our approach leads
to general formulas that capture the concrete descriptions in the
physics literature as instantiations. We dare to think that the
systematic use of multisets offers an enrichment not only of
