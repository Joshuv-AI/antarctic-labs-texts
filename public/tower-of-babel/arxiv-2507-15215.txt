Asymptotic Optimality in Data-Driven Decision Making
Radek Salač, Michael Kupper, Tobias Sutter

Abstract




Abstract

Given data generated by an observable stochastic process, we study how to construct statistically optimal decisions for general stochastic optimization problems. Our setting encompasses non-standard data structures, including data originating from heterogeneous sources or from randomly evolving data-generating mechanisms. We propose a decision-making approach that identifies optimal decisions for which a specific notion of risk of shifted regret decays to zero at a prescribed exponential rate. This optimal decision arises as the solution to a multi-objective optimization problem, which reflects asymptotic behavior properties of the data-generating process. Central to our framework is a rate function that characterizes this behavior via a Laplace principle, thereby generalizing standard concepts from large deviation theory. Our general formulation enables our approach to account for data from uncertain distributions and recovers classical results in data-driven decision making under uncertainty as special cases, including distributionally robust optimization. Moreover, our method enables decision-makers to systematically balance a desired rate of asymptotic risk decay against a potential loss in statistical consistency of the resulting data-driven decision. We demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed approach through illustrative examples from operations research, such as the newsvendor problem, under aleatoric uncertainty induced by heterogeneous data sources.

1
Introduction

Decision-making problems under uncertainty can be abstractly formulated as
\min_{x\in\mathcal{X}}c(x,\theta)
, where
\mathcal{X}
represents the feasible set of decisions,
\theta\in\Theta
denotes an unknown (potentially infinite-dimensional) distribution parameter, and
c(x,\theta)
stands for the risk or cost associated with the decision
x
under the stochastic model characterized by
\theta
.

Since the parameter
\theta
is unknown, the problem cannot be directly solved. Nevertheless, we assume access to training sample paths of a
\mathcal{Z}
-valued stochastic process
(Z_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, which is distributed according to a probability measure
\mathbb{P}_{\theta}
. For example,
Z_{n}
might be an empirical expectation or an empirical probability measure comprised of the first
n
elements of some underlying stochastic process. Data-driven decision-making then aims to find a mapping
X\mathbin{:}\mathcal{Z}\rightarrow\mathcal{X}
such that
X(Z_{n})
is a “good” decision when evaluated at the true unknown model
\theta
, where “good” can be expressed via possibly low
out-of-sample (or generalization) cost

c(X(Z_{n}),\theta)
or via small enough
regret

c(X(Z_{n}),\theta)-\min_{x\in\mathcal{X}}c(x,\theta)
.
A fundamental quantity to assess the asymptotic performance of a decision function
X
is the asymptotic counterpart of regret associated with the process
(Z_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
called
consistency gap of
X
, which we define as

\lim_{n\to\infty}\regret(X(Z_{n}),\theta)=\lim_{n\to\infty}c(X(Z_{n}),\theta)-\min_{x\in\mathcal{X}}c(x,\theta),

(1.1)

assuming that the limit exists.
If (
1.1
) equals to zero

\mathbb{P}_{\theta}\text{-a.s.}
, the underlying data-driven decision is (strongly) consistent.
Clearly, there are various approaches to constructing a data-driven decision that is consistent. For example, if
c
is expressed as an expected loss, the classical plug-in estimator (also known as
sample average approximation (SAA)), where the unknown expectation is replaced with its empirical counterpart, leads under relatively mild conditions to a consistent data-driven decision
X^{\mathsf{Plug}\text{-}\mathsf{in}}
defined via

X^{\mathsf{Plug}\text{-}\mathsf{in}}(Z_{n})\in\Argmin_{x\in\mathcal{X}}c(x,Z_{n}),

as established by
King & Wets 1991
and
Kleywegt et al. 2002
.
Consistency can also be established for other widely used approaches to data-driven decision making under uncertainty. For instance, in the case of
distributionally robust optimization (DRO)problems.

DROaims to make decisions that are optimal against the worst-case probability distribution within a set of plausible distributions, known as ambiguity set, defined by available information (
Kuhn et al. 2024
). In
DRO, consistency holds provided that the radius of the ambiguity set decreases at an appropriate rate (
Mohajerin Esfahani & Kuhn 2018
). In the limit, as the number of data points tends to infinity, the resulting decision then converges to that of the plug-in (or
SAA) problem.

It is well-known (
Smith & Winkler 2006
) that while the plug-in method is consistent, it exhibits poor out-of-sample performance in the finite-sample regime, a phenomenon referred to as the optimizer’s curse. Consequently, relying solely on the consistency gap to assess the quality of a data-driven decision appears less reasonable, suggesting the need for alternative evaluation criteria.
A natural criterion would be to require the regret of the evaluated data-driven decision
X(Z_{n})
under the true model
\theta
to decay at a certain, say exponential, rate. However, this is challenging for two reasons: first, since
\theta
is unknown, this decay would need to be guaranteed for any
\theta\in\Theta
; second, achieving an exponential decay rate of the regret might be inherently impossible for some models. Therefore, we propose
to search for decision functions
X
that are characterized as optimal solutions to the following criterion

\begin{array}[]{rl}\displaystyle\inf_{X,u}&\{u(z)\}_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}\\
\displaystyle\subjectto&\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\mathbb{E}_{\theta}\left[\exp(n\,\ell(\regret(X(Z_{n}),\theta)-u(Z_{n})))\right]\leq-r,\quad\forall\theta\in\Theta,\end{array}

(1.2)

where the optimization variables
X\mathbin{:}\mathcal{Z}\rightarrow\mathcal{X}
and
u\mathbin{:}\mathcal{Z}\rightarrow\real
are functions with certain regularity conditions that will be specified,
\ell\mathbin{:}[-\infty,\infty]\to[-\infty,\infty]
is a penalty function and
r>0
is a fixed parameter.
The constraint in (
1.2
) corresponds to an inequality for the
asymptotic entropic risk measure

\phi^{\mathsf{ENT}}_{\theta}
, i.e., to
\phi^{\mathsf{ENT}}_{\theta}[(f(Z_{n}))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}]\leq-r<0
with
f(z)\vcentcolon=\ell(\regret(X(z),\theta)-u(z))
for
z\in\mathcal{Z}
. That is, problem (
1.2
) admits data-driven decisions
X
and upper confidence bounds
u
such that the corresponding asymptotic entropic risk of the penalty of the shifted regret is no larger than
-r
. This behavior is enforced for all
\theta\in\Theta
, including the true (unknown) model
\theta
. Our criterion (
1.2
) shall identify a tuple of an optimal decision and the smallest upper confidence bound that can achieve the required bound on the asymptotic entropic risk, which controls the decay rate of the corresponding regret. That is also why we call
r>0
the
convergence rate parameter
.
Although (
1.2
) is just an instance of a problem class introduced below, it is helpful in developing intuition for our framework.
The constraint in (
1.2
) is quite general with respect to the choice of
\ell
. By selecting a degenerate type of a penalty function it reduces to

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\mathbb{P}_{\theta}\left[\regret(X(Z_{n}),\theta)>u(Z_{n})\right]\leq-r,\quad\forall\theta\in\Theta.

(1.3)

In this formulation, the interpretation of
u
as a high-probability upper confidence bound becomes particularly clear.

The uncertainty considered so far originates from a lack of knowledge about the true data-generating probability vector
\theta
. This type of uncertainty can typically be reduced by collecting more data. For example, one may consider a setting in which the underlying stochastic process
(Z_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
converges to
\theta
as
n\to\infty
. Such uncertainty is commonly referred to as
epistemic uncertainty
. Most of the existing literature on data-driven decision making, including stochastic programming (
Shapiro et al. 2009
) and
DRO(
Kuhn et al. 2024
), focuses on this notion of uncertainty.
In contrast,
aleatoric uncertainty
refers to randomness that is intrinsic to the data itself. Unlike epistemic uncertainty, aleatoric uncertainty cannot be eliminated, even with access to infinite data (
Wimmer et al. 2023
). This type of uncertainty naturally arises in practical scenarios, such as when data is collected from heterogeneous sources. Suppose each source
k
corresponds to a slightly different model
\theta_{k}\in B_{\theta}(R)
, where
B_{\theta}(R)\subseteq\Theta
is a ball with radius
R
centered around a nominal (but unknown) model
\theta
. We assume that the test model, i.e., the one under which the learned policy is deployed, also lies in
B_{\theta}(R)
.
In such settings, the constraint in (
1.2
), which is formulated as an asymptotic entropic risk under the model
\theta
, naturally generalizes to the asymptotic version of the so-called
asymptotic robust entropic risk measure

(
Föllmer & Knispel 2011
, Section 5)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\sup_{\theta^{\prime}\in B_{\theta}(R)}\mathbb{E}_{\theta^{\prime}}\left[\exp\left(n\,\ell\left(\regret(X(Z_{n}),\theta)-u(Z_{n})\right)\right)\right]\leq-r.

(1.4)

In this work, we show that under certain assumptions to be specified, the solution to (
1.2
), where the constraint can be expressed as a general asymptotic risk measure which satisfies an abstract Laplace principle (see
Kupper & Zapata 2021
), encompassing both the asymptotic and asymptotic robust entropic risk constraints, is remarkably simple and takes the form

\displaystyle{X}^{\star}(z)

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\argmin_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\max_{\theta\in\Theta}\left\{\regret(x,\theta)-\ell^{-1}\left(\mathcal{I}_{\theta}(z)-r\right)\right\},

(1.5)

\displaystyle{u}^{\star}(z)

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\min_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\max_{\theta\in\Theta}\left\{\regret(x,\theta)-\ell^{-1}\left(\mathcal{I}_{\theta}(z)-r\right)\right\},\quad z\in\mathcal{Z}.

The function
\mathcal{I}\mathbin{:}\Theta\times\mathcal{Z}\to[0,\infty]
stands in relationship to the constraint in (
1.2
) and reflects the asymptotic behavior of
(Z_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
. In the simple setting where the constraint is expressed by the asymptotic entropic risk measure, as in (
1.2
), the function
\mathcal{I}_{\theta}(\cdot)
corresponds to the large deviation rate function associated with the family of distributions of the sequence
(Z_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
. This implies that the structure of the optimal data-driven decision function
{X}^{\star}
and upper confidence bound
{u}^{\star}
is then induced by the concentration behavior of the underlying data generating process via the corresponding large deviation principle, which is represented by the rate function.
In the robust setting, where the constraint in (
1.2
) is replaced with (
1.4
), the standard large deviation rate function
\mathcal{I}_{\theta}(\cdot)
is replaced with by its robust counterpart
\inf_{\theta^{\prime}\in B_{\theta}(R)}\mathcal{I}_{\theta^{\prime}}(\cdot)
.

Related work.

The optimality criterion (
1.2
) is conceptually closely related to universal composite hypothesis testing problems and the underlying asymptotic optimality concept; see
(
Dembo & Zeitouni 2010
, Section 7.1)
for a comprehensive treatment. This connection is best exemplified by considering the special case where the constraint in (
1.2
) reduces to (
1.3
).
Analogous to hypothesis testing problems, we interpret the unfavorable event
\regret(X(Z_{n}),\theta)>u(Z_{n})
, where the regret of our decision exceeds the upper confidence bound, as a type I error. Clearly, one could minimize the corresponding error probability by choosing the upper confidence bound
u
to be very large. Our optimality criterion (
1.2
) then quantifies a pair of data-driven decision and corresponding upper confidence bound
({X}^{\star},{u}^{\star})
as optimal if it leads to the desired decay rate
r
of the type I error probability, while at the same time being the smallest upper confidence bound. Specifically, the pair
({X}^{\star},{u}^{\star})
must satisfy (
1.3
), and, in addition, any other pair
(X,u)
satisfying this property must be such that

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{u(Z_{n})}{{u}^{\star}(Z_{n})}\geq 1.

While the optimal composite hypothesis test
(
Dembo & Zeitouni 2010
, Theorem 7.1.11)
is defined via the level-set of an underlying large deviation rate function, a highly related structure is present in our setting. In fact, the solution (
1.5
) for the special penalty leading to problem (
1.2
) with the constraint (
1.3
) reduces to

{X}^{\star}(z)=\argmin_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\sup_{\theta\in\Theta,\ \mathcal{I}_{\theta}(z)<r}\regret(x,\theta),

(1.6)

where we also optimize over the level-sets of the large deviation rate function, but with respect to the
\theta
-variable. The optimization problem (
1.6
) can be directly interpreted as a
DROproblem, highlighting the inherent connection between the solutions to (
1.2
) and
DRO.

In this paper, we show that the optimal decision function defined in (
1.5
), for appropriately chosen asymptotic risk measures, recovers both the classical
SAAdecision rule (
Shapiro et al. 2009
) and the well-known
DROrule (
Delage & Ye 2010
;
Kuhn et al. 2024
) as special cases. More importantly, our framework provides a principled way to interpolate between these two extremes via parametrization of the penalty function, yielding a rich family of data-driven decision rules that trade off between the statistical consistency of
SAAand the robustness of
DRO, particularly in low-data regimes. This flexibility enables decision makers to tailor the decision rule to the specific characteristics and requirements of their application.

A similar perspective for data-driven decision making has been considered in
Van Parys et al. 2021
;
Sutter et al. 2024
, where the authors aim to choose a data-driven decision
X
with the goal to achieve an exponential decay rate of the so-called out-of-sample disappointment probability. Written in our notation, the authors consider a finite-dimensional problem of the form (
1.2
), where the constraint is expressed as

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\mathbb{P}_{\theta}\left[c(X(Z_{n}),\theta)>u(Z_{n})\right]\leq-r,\quad\forall\theta\in\Theta,

(1.7)

and
u(Z_{n})
is referred to as the in-sample cost. The optimal decision to this approach is given by (
1.6
) where
\regret(x,\theta)
is replaced by
c(x,\theta)
.
Moreover, existing works primarily focus on epistemic uncertainty. A notable exception is
Van Parys 2024
, which considers data-driven decision making under noisy data, i.e., data affected by aleatoric uncertainty. However, this work also relies on independence assumptions for the underlying data samples.

In the context of
DRO, the trade-off between robustness (e.g., in the sense of (
1.7
)) and the conservatism of the underlying decision (e.g., quantified via the upper-confidence bound
u
) is typically expressed by the size of the ambiguity set. The size of these ambiguity sets can also be interpreted as the strength of a certain regularization, which critically determines the resulting out-of-sample behavior (
Shafieezadeh-Abadeh et al. 2019
).
In most works (see the comprehensive textbook
Kuhn et al. 2024
), the size of the ambiguity set is chosen via cross-validation. While this approach often works well in practice, it lacks rigorous statistical guarantees.
There are a few approaches that theoretically study the statistically optimal choice of
DROambiguity set sizes.

Lam 2019
proposes ambiguity sets that offer optimal statistical guarantees in view of the central limit theorem.
Moreover, the asymptotic size of
DROambiguity sets with respect to the sample size is known both for divergence ambiguity sets (
Lam 2019
;
Duchi et al. 2021
) as well as for Wasserstein ambiguity sets (
Blanchet et al. 2019
;
Gao 2020
).
In a recent work by
Blanchet et al. 2021
, the choice of optimal radius of Wasserstein ambiguity sets, in a
DROcontext, has been analyzed via a specific projection that is defined as a hypothesis test.

Our approach is not only closely related to
DRO, but also conceptually connected to the principles of robust statistics.
Specifically, it can be shown (
Chan et al. 2024
) that the ambiguity sets of
DROproblems based on relative entropy and total variation distance correspond to confidence sets with the desired properties of the optimal confidence set proposed by
Huber 1964
. A related result is proposed by
Ganguly & Sutter 2024
, where it is demonstrated that among all confidence intervals that asymptotically guarantee a certain coverage probability (referred to as accuracy), a
DRO-based confidence interval is minimal in size.

Contribution.

We highlight the following main contributions of this paper.

0.

We propose a framework for optimal data-driven decision making in which the optimality criterion generalizes (
1.2
) by replacing the asymptotic entropic risk measure constraint with an arbitrary asymptotic risk measure which satisfies a Laplace principle. This generalized formulation enables the representation of a broad class of asymptotic risk measures under minimal assumptions on the underlying stochastic process. In particular, it includes asymptotic robust risk measures that effectively capture aleatoric uncertainty.

0.

We show that the proposed generalizations of problem (
1.2
) admit remarkably simple closed-form solutions whose structure is induced by the statistical properties of the corresponding stochastic process
(Z_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
. Specifically, assuming that the distribution of
(Z_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is such that a Laplace principle for a given asymptotic risk measure can be established, the solution is constructed from the underlying rate function, thereby incorporating information about asymptotic behavior of
(Z_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
. Moreover, we demonstrate universality of this optimal solution structure, as it can be shown to solve other modifications of the generalized problem (
1.2
) as well.

0.

We analyze how the choice of the penalty function
\ell
in the generalized formulations of (
1.2
) influences the consistency, or the resulting consistency gap, of the optimal data-driven decision. These insights offer practical design guidelines for selecting the penalty function
\ell
.
In particular, these parameters allow for a continuous interpolation between the classical and well-studied plug-in and
DROdecision rules.

0.

We derive explicit optimal data-driven decisions for various settings, including finite-state and continuous i.i.d. processes with unknown means, as well as finite-state Markov chains. As a case study, we apply our framework to classical OR problems such as the newsvendor problem, both in the standard setting with unknown demand and a more complex variant with additional aleatoric uncertainty due to random demand distributions. These examples, along with supporting numerical results, illustrate the expressiveness and broad applicability of our framework.

2
Problem Statement and Main Results

Let
(Z_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of
\mathcal{Z}
-valued random variables defined on a probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P}_{\theta})
, where
\mathcal{Z}
is some non-empty metric state space and
\theta
is a distribution parameter in some non-empty metric space
\Theta
. Further, let
\mathcal{X}
be another non-empty metric space called the
decision space
. We assume both
\Theta
and
\mathcal{X}
to be compact and convex.
Let
c\mathbin{:}\mathcal{X}\times\Theta\to\real
be a continuous
cost function
so that
x\mapsto c(x,\theta)
is strictly convex for all
\theta\in\Theta
.
The key problem of interest is then

\min_{x\in\mathcal{X}}c(x,\theta),

(2.1)

where
\theta\in\Theta
is an unknown model parameter. In other words, without the knowledge of
\theta
and based on the inference of the underlying process
(Z_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
we look for a cost function minimizing decision
x\in\mathcal{X}
, that best approximates (
2.1
).
A popular choice of cost
c
would be described by an expected loss, i.e.,
c(x,\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[h(x,\xi)]
, where
\xi
is an
\Xi
-valued random variable and
h:\mathcal{X}\times\Xi\to\real
some loss function.

We define a
data-driven decision (or decision function)
as a continuous function
X\mathbin{:}\mathcal{Z}\to\mathcal{X}
, which is to be evaluated at some step of the stochastic process
(Z_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
.
In the special case where
Z_{n}\to\theta
as
n\to\infty
, i.e.,
Z_{n}
is a consistent distribution estimator, a widely used approach in the literature is the
plug-in estimator
resulting in the
plug-in decision
defined via

X^{\mathsf{Plug}\text{-}\mathsf{in}}(Z_{n})\vcentcolon=\argmin_{x\in\mathcal{X}}c(x,Z_{n}).

(2.2)

When the cost
c
represents an expected loss, this formulation aligns with the well-known
SAAmethod.
Our goal is to find
X
systematically so that
X(Z_{n})
approximates the minimizer in (
2.1
) “effectively”, which will be formalized in the following.

To formulate a problem of the type (
1.2
), it is essential to understand how the transformed stochastic process
(\ell(\regret(X(Z_{n}),\theta)-u(Z_{n})))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, which is influenced by the decision function
X
, concentrates. This process captures the penalized shifted regret associated with the evaluated data-driven decision
X(Z_{n})
.
We employ asymptotic risk measures, such as the asymptotic entropic risk measure (see (
1.2
)), to characterize and control the concentration behavior of the transformed process.
Problem (
1.2
) defines the class of admissible decision functions
X
as those for which the asymptotic risk of the associated transformed stochastic process
(\ell(\regret(X(Z_{n}),\theta)-u(Z_{n})))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
decays exponentially at a prescribed rate
r>0
.
Motivated by the theory of large deviations, we introduce two mathematical objects that together encapsulate such information about the (transformed) stochastic process
(Z_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
.

First, let
\phi_{\theta}
be a functional called the
asymptotic risk measure of
(Z_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
under
\theta
, which maps any continuous transformation
(f(Z_{n}))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of the stochastic process
(Z_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
to the extended real line
[-\infty,\infty]
, i.e.,

\displaystyle(f(Z_{n}))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\mapsto\phi_{\theta}\left[(f(Z_{n}))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\right]\quad\text{for }f\in\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{Z}).

A widely used instance of such an asymptotic risk measure is the mentioned asymptotic entropic risk measure represented by

(f(Z_{n}))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\mapsto\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\mathbb{E}_{\theta}\left[\exp\left(nf(Z_{n})\right)\right]

for
f\in\mathcal{C}_{b}(\mathcal{Z})
and we shall see more examples as we go along.
In the generalized setting, the functional
\phi_{\theta}
then replaces the asymptotic entropic risk measure in problem (
1.2
) and thereby defines the feasibility characterizing probabilistic constraint.
Such an abstract construction might appear more intuitive, when compared to the ambivalence between the study of the expected values of arbitrary bounded continuous transformations of some random variable and the study of its distribution. Hence, this is in parallel to studying evaluations of
\phi_{\theta}
on arbitrary continuous transformations of a stochastic process in order to encapsulate some of its asymptotic behavior properties.
In other words, in our analogy
\phi_{\theta}
replaces the expectation functional
\mathbb{E}_{\theta}
as the focus shifts from distribution of a random variable to concentration properties of a stochastic process.

Second, the asymptotic behavior of the stochastic process
(Z_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
described by the asymptotic risk measure
\phi_{\theta}
shall be exactly such that there exists a so called
rate function

\mathcal{I}\mathbin{:}\Theta\times\mathcal{Z}\rightarrow[0,\infty],\quad(\theta,z)\mapsto\mathcal{I}_{\theta}(z),

which together with
\phi_{\theta}
constitutes a
Laplace principle (LP)

\phi_{\theta}\left[(f(Z_{n}))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\right]=\sup_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}\left\{f(z)-\mathcal{I}_{\theta}(z)\right\},\quad\forall f\in\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{Z}),\ \forall\theta\in\Theta.

(2.3)

Then, we say that the pair
(\phi_{\theta},\mathcal{I}_{\theta})
satisfies the
LP (
2.3
). Consequently, all the objects
\phi_{\theta}
,
\mathcal{I}_{\theta}
and
(Z_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
stand in a mutual relationship, which is encoded by (
2.3
).
Moreover, with (
2.3
) at hand, one can interpret the shape of
\mathcal{I}_{\theta}
in a direct relationship to the concentration properties of
(Z_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}

and with the optimal decision formula (
1.5
) in mind also to the consistency properties of such decision.
This also provides an insight into the inference of information about
\theta
via observations of
(Z_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
.

Before proceeding with the general setup, we consider the specific example where
\phi_{\theta}
is chosen as the asymptotic entropic risk measure, in order to develop a better understanding of (
2.3
).

Example 2.1

(LDP - asymptotic entropic risk measure)
.

In order to arrive at the
LP (
2.3
) with
\phi_{\theta}
being the asymptotic entropic risk measure,
we take a look into the theory of large deviations–a literature area, which largely inspires our nomenclature. Let
\theta\in\Theta
and denote the
\theta
-dependent family of distributions (or laws) associated with
(Z_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
by
(\mu^{\theta}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, i.e.,
\mu^{\theta}_{n}(A)\vcentcolon=\mathbb{P}_{\theta}\left[Z_{n}\in A\right]
for any
A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{Z})
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Assume that
(\mu^{\theta}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfies a so called
large deviation principle (LDP)with the rate function

2.1

\begin{array}[]{cl}\displaystyle\inf_{X\in\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{Z},\mathcal{X}),\ u\in\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{Z})}&\{u(z)\}_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}\\

2.3
Consistency

To analyze the consistency properties of the evaluated optimal data-driven decision

in context of the ultimate goal of solving

Dealing with such a notion of consistency, it is reasonable to require the process

to concentrate accordingly to the following assumption, which shall hold throughout this section.

Assumption 2.9

(Convergent process)

There exists a function

\displaystyle\begin{split}\theta\mapsto\Gamma(\theta)\vcentcolon=\regret({X}^{\star}(Z_{\infty}(\theta)),\theta)=c({X}^{\star}(Z_{\infty}(\theta)),\theta)-\min_{y\in\mathcal{X}}c(y,\theta),\end{split}

Recalling the
2.6

\ell

defined according to (

In

On the one hand, we refer to the general distributional robustness in the

On the other hand, in our case this (pseudo-)metric is given by the rate function (see (
2.15

characterized by the

In the context of the second interpretation and in the case when

is an empirical probability measure and

This dependency between the consistency gap and penalty function helps to understand the influence of the shape of the latter.

.

3
Generalized Problem Statement

In this section, we formalize the preceding results and present the most general formulation of our data-driven optimization framework. The key requirement is that the data process must satisfy an abstract Laplace principle. To that end, let
(Z_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a
\mathcal{Z}
-valued stochastic process on a probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P}_{\theta})
, where
\mathcal{Z}
is a non-empty metric space and
\theta
is an unknown parameter in a compact, convex metric space
\Theta
. On an intuitive level, as
n\in\mathbb{N}
increases, the data-generating process reveals progressively more information about the true parameter and should therefore satisfy certain concentration properties.

3.1
Asymptotic risk measures and concentration

Depending on the unknown parameter
\theta\in\Theta
, let
\phi_{\theta}
be an asymptotic risk measure mapping continuous transformations
(f(Z_{n}))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of the process
(Z_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
to the extended real line
[-\infty,\infty]
, and let
\mathcal{I}\mathbin{:}\Theta\times\mathcal{Z}\to[0,\infty]
be a rate function meeting
2.4
, such that the pair
(\phi_{\theta},\mathcal{I}_{\theta})
satisfies the Laplace principle

\phi_{\theta}\left[(f(Z_{n}))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\right]=\sup_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}\left\{f(z)-\mathcal{I}_{\theta}(z)\right\},\quad\forall f\in\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{Z}),\ \forall\theta\in\Theta.

(3.1)

When
\phi_{\theta}
represents the standard asymptotic entropic risk measure, the
LP (
3.1
) constrained to
\mathcal{C}_{b}(\mathcal{Z})
simplifies to the classical result known as Varadhan’s Lemma
(
Dembo & Zeitouni 2010
, Theorem 4.3.1)
.
However, this principle is not limited to the asymptotic entropic risk measure
\phi_{\theta}^{\mathsf{ENT}}
and there exist other functionals
\psi\mathbin{:}\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{Z})\to[-\infty,\infty]
that admit a similar representation. A key property of such functionals is
maxitivity
, meaning that
\psi(f\vee g)=\max\{\psi(f),\psi(g)\}
, where
f\vee g
denotes the pointwise maximum of the functions
f,g\in\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{Z})
. Maxitive functionals play a central role in possibility theory (
Dubois & Prade 1988
;
Zadeh 1978
) and in idempotent analysis (
Kolokoltsov & Maslov 1997
).

To better illustrate the concept, in the following, we present a list of five typical examples of asymptotic risk measures and their associated concentration properties as characterized by the Laplace principle (
3.1
), some of which have already been discussed in Section
2
. Some of the examples presented below will be revisited in the numerical experiments in Section
4
.

3.1.1
Law of large numbers

We recall Example
2.3
. Suppose that
\Theta=\mathcal{Z}
and that the sequence
(Z_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfies an
LLN, i.e.,
Z_{n}\to\theta
as
n\to\infty

\mathbb{P}_{\theta}
-a.s.. In that case, we obtain the
LP (
3.1
) as

\phi^{\mathsf{LLN}}_{\theta}\left[(f(Z_{n}))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\right]=f(\theta)=\sup_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}\{f(z)-\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{LLN}}_{\theta}(z)\},\quad f\in\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{Z}),

where the rate function is given by
\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{LLN}}_{\theta}(z)=0
if
z=\theta
, and
\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{LLN}}_{\theta}(z)=\infty
if
z\neq\theta
. It is straightforward to verify that
\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{LLN}}
satisfies Assumption
2.4
.

As a concrete example, consider the finite-state i.i.d. setting introduced in
2.2
, where the law of large numbers ensures that
Z_{n}\to\theta

\mathbb{P}_{\theta}
-a.s. as
n\to\infty
. Moreover, for an illustration of the resulting optimal decision, see
2.6
.

3.1.2
Law of large numbers under distributional uncertainty

The assumption that data at each step are drawn from a fixed distribution is often too strong in practical applications and typically subject to distributional uncertainty. For example, in the finite-state i.i.d. setting introduced in
2.2
, the probabilities
\mathbb{P}_{\theta}\left[\xi_{k}=i\right]=\theta_{i}
for
i=1,\ldots,d
are often not known precisely. Instead, they are imprecise in the sense of
Walley 1991
, meaning they are only specified within some intervals
\mathbb{P}_{\theta}\left[\xi_{k}=i\right]\in[\underline{\theta}_{i},\overline{\theta}_{i}]
.
In such cases, the corresponding expectations are not single-valued but instead lie within an interval
[\underline{\mathbb{E}}_{\theta},\overline{\mathbb{E}}_{\theta}]
that reflects the uncertainty in the underlying model.

In what follows, we focus on the
upper expectation

\overline{\mathbb{E}}_{\theta}
, which represents the worst-case expected value over a family of plausible probability distributions (the lower expectation is typically given via the relation
\underline{\mathbb{E}}_{\theta}[\xi]=-\overline{\mathbb{E}}_{\theta}[-\xi]
). The upper expectation characterizes the uncertain distribution of
Z_{n}
through
\overline{\mathbb{E}}_{\theta}[f(Z_{n})]
, where
f
ranges over
\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{Z})
or a suitable subset on which the upper expectation is well defined.
We assume that, for every
\theta\in\Theta
, the upper expectation
\overline{\mathbb{E}}_{\theta}
is defined for all random variables
\xi\mathbin{:}\Omega\to\real
belonging to a linear space
\mathcal{G}
such that
c\in\mathcal{G}
for all constants
c\in\real
and
|\xi|\in\mathcal{G}
for all
\xi\in\mathcal{G}
. Moreover, for all
\xi,\zeta\in\mathcal{G}
, we assume:

(i)

\overline{\mathbb{E}}_{\theta}[\xi]\leq\overline{\mathbb{E}}_{\theta}[\zeta]
whenever
\xi\leq\zeta
,

(ii)

\overline{\mathbb{E}}_{\theta}[\xi+\zeta]\leq\overline{\mathbb{E}}_{\theta}[\xi]+\overline{\mathbb{E}}_{\theta}[\zeta]
,

(iii)

\overline{\mathbb{E}}_{\theta}[\xi+c]=\overline{\mathbb{E}}_{\theta}[\xi]+c
for all
c\in\real
,

(iv)

\overline{\mathbb{E}}_{\theta}[\alpha\xi]=\alpha\overline{\mathbb{E}}_{\theta}[\xi]
for all
\alpha\in[0,\infty)
.

A functional satisfying these properties is called a
sublinear expectation
, and the triplet
(\Omega,\mathcal{H},\overline{\mathbb{E}}_{\theta})
is referred to as a
sublinear expectation space
(
Peng 2019
). Sublinear expectations are closely related to coherent risk measures (
Artzner et al. 1999
) and upper coherent previsions in the theory of imprecise probabilities (
Walley 1991
). It is well known that, under suitable continuity assumptions, sublinear expectations admit a robust representation of the form
\overline{\mathbb{E}}_{\theta}[\xi]=\sup_{\mathbb{Q}\in\mathcal{Q}_{\theta}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}[\xi]
,
where the ambiguity set
\mathcal{Q}_{\theta}
consists of all probability measures regarded as plausible under the parameter
\theta
. If
\mathcal{Q}_{\theta}
is a singleton, the model is fully specified; otherwise, it captures distributional uncertainty. Furthermore, we observe that Conditions
(ii)
and
(iv)
can be relaxed to a convexity condition, in which case one speaks of a
convex expectation
or a
convex risk measure
. For further details, we refer to
Föllmer & Schied 2016
.

The key assumption is that the sequence
(Z_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfies a
robust law of large numbers (RLLN), i.e.,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\overline{\mathbb{E}}_{\theta}[f(Z_{n})]=\sup_{z\in Z_{\infty}(\theta)}f(z),\quad\forall f\in\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{Z}),

for some set
Z_{\infty}(\theta)\subseteq\mathcal{Z}
. Then, we obtain the
LP (
3.1
) as

\phi^{\mathsf{RLLN}}_{\theta}\left[(f(Z_{n}))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\right]\vcentcolon=\lim_{n\to\infty}\overline{\mathbb{E}}_{\theta}[f(Z_{n})]=\sup_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}\{f(z)-\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{RLLN}}_{\theta}(z)\},\quad f\in\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{Z}),

(3.2)

where the rate function is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{RLLN}}_{\theta}(z)

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\begin{cases}{}0&\text{ if }z\in Z_{\infty}(\theta)\\
\infty&\text{ otherwise},\end{cases}\qquad(\theta,z)\in\Theta\times\mathcal{Z}.

(3.3)

Whether
\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{RLLN}}
satisfies Assumption
2.4
depends on the structure of
Z_{\infty}(\theta)
, which can be verified, for instance, in the following example. For the resulting optimal decision, see
3.3
below. For robust versions of law of large numbers, we refer to
Maccheroni & Marinacci 2005
;
de Cooman & Miranda 2008
;
Peng 2008
;
Peng 2019
;
Terán 2014
;
Blessing & Kupper 2025
.

Example 3.1

(Finite state i.i.d. process under distributional uncertainty)
.

Let
\Delta_{d}
denote the probability simplex in
d
for some
d\in\mathbb{N}
, and consider
\Theta=\Delta_{d}
.
Let
(\xi_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
be a finite-state stochastic process, i.e.,
\xi_{k}\mathbin{:}\Omega\to\{1,\ldots,d\}
. For any
\theta\in\Theta
, the law of
\xi_{k}
is assumed to lie within a ball of radius
R>0
centered at
\theta
, i.e.,

B_{\theta}(R)\vcentcolon=\{\theta^{\prime}\in\Theta\mid\mathsf{d}(\theta^{\prime},\theta)\leq R\},

where
\mathsf{d}
is a metric on
\Theta=\Delta_{d}
. This leads to the intervals
\mathbb{P}_{\theta}[\xi_{k}=i]\in\{\theta^{\prime}_{i}\mid\theta^{\prime}\in B_{\theta}(R)\}
,
i=1,\ldots,d
. In addition, the random variables
(\xi_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
are assumed to satisfy a certain independence assumption. Specifically, we require that the distribution of
\xi_{k}
is not influenced by conditioning on
\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{k-1}
. That is, we assume that the conditional distribution
\mu_{k-1,k}(\cdot\mid\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{k-1})
of
\xi_{k}
conditioned on
\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{k-1}
belongs to the set
B_{\theta}(R)
, and emphasize that
B_{\theta}(R)
does not depend on the values of
\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{k-1}
. This means that the sample
\xi_{k}
is drawn according to some distribution in
B_{\theta}(R)
, independently of
\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{k-1}
. In that case, the joint distribution of
(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{k})
modeled via the respective sublinear expectation is given by

\overline{\mathbb{E}}_{\theta}[f(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{k})]=\sup_{\mu}\sum_{(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})\in\{1,\dots,d\}^{k}}f(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})\,\mu(i_{1},\dots,i_{k}),\quad f\mathbin{:}\{1,\dots,d\}^{k}\to\real,

(3.4)

where the supremum is taken over all

\mu(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})=\mu_{0,1}(i_{1})\prod_{l=2}^{k}\mu_{l-1,l}(i_{l}\mid i_{1},\dots,i_{l-1})
for all conditional distributions
\mu_{l-1,l}\in B_{\theta}(R)
. It turns out that the sublinear expectation satisfies

\overline{\mathbb{E}}_{\theta}[f(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{k})]=\overline{\mathbb{E}}_{\theta}\left[\overline{\mathbb{E}}_{\theta}\left[f(i_{1},\dots,i_{k-1},\xi_{k})\right]\big|_{i_{1}=\xi_{1},\dots,i_{k-1}=\xi_{k-1}}\right]

4.2
Empirical expectation of i.i.d. process

Cramér’s theorem
(
Dembo & Zeitouni 2010
, Theorem 2.2.30)
allows to systematically identify large deviation rate functions corresponding to empirical means for i.i.d. random variables. Whether the state space is some finite alphabet or a finite-dimensional real vector space, Cramér’s theorem shows
the rate function to be the
Fenchel-Legendre transform
of the
logarithmic moment generating function
associated with the distribution of the underlying i.i.d. process, which becomes practically relevant, once it can be computed numerically as a function in both
z
and
\theta
.
For some distributions the Fenchel-Legendre transform is even available in closed form, which is the case in the following Gaussian example.

Let
(\xi_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
be an i.i.d. stochastic process with
\xi_{k}\sim\mathcal{N}(\theta,\Sigma)
, where
\Sigma\in\real^{d\times d}
is a known invertible covariance matrix and
\theta\in\Theta
is an unknown mean vector from some compact
\Theta\subseteq\real^{d}
.
Then, according to Cramér’s theorem the family of distributions
(\mu_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
associated with the empirical mean process
(Z_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
,
Z_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\xi_{k}
with state space
\mathcal{Z}=\real^{d}
, satisfies an
LDPwith the rate function

\mathcal{I}_{\theta}(z)=\frac{1}{2}(\theta-z)^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}(\theta-z),\quad(\theta,z)\in\Theta\times\mathcal{Z},

as justified in
(
Sutter et al. 2024
, Section 5)
. Hence, we have the
LP (
3.1
) for the asymptotic entropic risk measure
\phi^{\mathsf{ENT}}_{\theta}
with the rate function
\mathcal{I}_{\theta}
, which as a continuous function clearly satisfies
2.4
. Moreover, by the strong law of large numbers it holds
Z_{n}\to\theta=Z_{\infty}(\theta)

\mathbb{P}_{\theta}
-a.s. as
n\to\infty
and
2.9
is satisfied.
Again this is an instance of the setting of
Section

3.1.3
but the decision is different from the finite-state i.i.d. setting considered in

It is straightforward to derive a number of other than Gaussian distributions with their respective rate functions in closed form, see

, while using the fact that

for all

.

Proposition A.8

(Robust Sanov’s theorem for finite alphabets)
.

Let
(\xi_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
be a stochastic process on the finite state space
\{1,\ldots,d\}
, which is i.i.d. with respect to a non-empty set of distributions
B_{\theta}(R)\subseteq\Theta\subseteq\Delta_{d}
, parametrized by
\theta\in\Theta
. That is, under any
\theta^{\prime}\in B_{\theta}(R)
, the process
(\xi_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is i.i.d. with
\mathbb{P}_{\theta^{\prime}}\left[\xi_{k}=i\right]=\theta^{\prime}_{i}
for
i=1,\ldots,d
. Let
\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{RENT}}
be the robust relative entropy rate function given by (
3.7
). Assume that
B_{\theta}(R)
and
\Theta
are such that
\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{RENT}}_{\theta}\mathbin{:}\mathcal{Z}\to[0,\infty)
is continuous. Further,
Z_{n}\vcentcolon=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathbbm{1}_{\xi_{k}=i}\right)e_{i}
defines the empirical measure process
(Z_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
associated with
(\xi_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
.
Then, for every set
A
of probability vectors in
\Delta_{d}
, it holds

\displaystyle\begin{split}-\inf_{z\in\mathring{A}}\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{RENT}}_{\theta}(z)&\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\sup_{\theta^{\prime}\in B_{\theta}(R)}\mathbb{P}_{\theta^{\prime}}\left[Z_{n}\in A\right]\\
&\leq\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\sup_{\theta^{\prime}\in B_{\theta}(R)}\mathbb{P}_{\theta^{\prime}}\left[Z_{n}\in A\right]\leq-\inf_{z\in A}\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{RENT}}_{\theta}(z),\end{split}

(A.3)

Proof.

We adapt the proof of
(
Dembo & Zeitouni 2010
, Theorem 2.1.10)
.
Let
A\subseteq\Delta_{d}
,
\theta\in\Theta
and define

\mathcal{L}_{n}\vcentcolon=\left\{z\in\mathcal{Z}\mid\exists y\in\{1,\ldots,d\}^{n}:z=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathbbm{1}_{y_{k}=i}\right)e_{i}\right\}
.
By taking the supremum over
B_{\theta}(R)
on both sides of the inequalities (2.1.12) and (2.1.13) in the proof of
(
Dembo & Zeitouni 2010
, Theorem 2.1.10)
, we get

\displaystyle\sup_{\theta^{\prime}\in B_{\theta}(R)}\mathbb{P}_{\theta^{\prime}}\left[Z_{n}\in A\right]

\displaystyle\leq(n+1)^{d}\sup_{\theta^{\prime}\in B_{\theta}(R)}\exp\left(-n\inf_{z\in A\cap\mathcal{L}_{n}}\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{ENT}}_{\theta^{\prime}}(z)\right)

\displaystyle=(n+1)^{d}\exp\left(-n\inf_{z\in A\cap\mathcal{L}_{n}}\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{RENT}}_{\theta}(z)\right)

and

\displaystyle\sup_{\theta^{\prime}\in B_{\theta}(R)}\mathbb{P}_{\theta^{\prime}}\left[Z_{n}\in A\right]

\displaystyle\geq(n+1)^{-d}\sup_{\theta^{\prime}\in B_{\theta}(R)}\exp\left(-n\inf_{z\in A\cap\mathcal{L}_{n}}\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{ENT}}_{\theta^{\prime}}(z)\right)

\displaystyle=(n+1)^{-d}\exp\left(-n\inf_{z\in A\cap\mathcal{L}_{n}}\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{RENT}}_{\theta}(z)\right),

and thereby also the counterparts of (2.1.14) and (2.1.15), i.e.,

Then, the upper bound of (
