Lawson cones and the Allen-Cahn equation
Oscar Agudelo, Matteo Rizzi

Abstract
. In this paper we discuss nondegeneracy and stability properties of some special minimal hypersurfaces which are asymptotic to a given Lawson cone C m , n C_{m,n} , for m , n ≥ 2 m,\,n\geq 2 . Then we use such hypersurfaces to construct solutions to the Allen-Cahn equation − Δ ​ u = u − u 3 -\Delta u=u-u^{3} in ℝ N + 1 \mathbb{R}^{N+1} , N + 1 ≥ 8 N+1\geq 8 , whose zero level set has exactly k ≥ 2 k\geq 2 connected components and with infinite Morse index.



Keywords.

Allen-Cahn equation,
Lawson cones,
Minimal Surfaces,
Jacobi-Toda system.

1.
Introduction

In this work we discussed part of the recent developments related to entire and bounded solutions to
the
Allen-Cahn equation

(1.1)

\Delta u-F^{\prime}(u)=0\quad\hbox{in}\quad\mathbb{R}^{N+1},

F(s)=\frac{1}{4}(1-s^{2})^{2}
for
s\in\mathbb{R}
is the best example of a
symmetric double well potential
.

Equation (
1.1
) appears in the modeling of phase transitions phenonema (see
[
7
]
). Roughly speaking, the functions
u_{+}=1
and
u_{-}=-1
represent two different phases of a material placed in a container. When the two phases co-exist (not mixing involved), they are separated by an interface. The equilibrium configuration is the smooth function
u
, which describes the phase change, and is expected to be a solution of (
1.1
). Observe that
u_{\pm}
are trivial solutions of (
1.1
) corresponding to stable phases of the said material.

Theoretical developments related to the equation (
1.1
) are known to be strongly connected with the
Theory of Minimal Surfaces
. This statement can be briefly explained as follows. First, we remark that in dimension one, the function

(1.2)

w(z)=\tanh\left(\frac{z}{\sqrt{2}}\right),\quad z\in\mathbb{R}.

is the unique solution (up tu translation of the argument) to the problem

(1.3)

w^{\prime\prime}+w(1-w^{2})=0,\quad\hbox{in }\mathbb{R},\quad w(\pm\infty)=\pm 1,\quad w^{\prime}>0.

It is verified that

\small{w(z)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}1-2e^{-\sqrt{2}\,z}+O\left(e^{-2\sqrt{2}\,z}\right),\hskip 9.24994ptz>>0\\
-1+2e^{-\sqrt{2}\,z}+O\left(e^{-2\sqrt{2}\,z}\right),\hskip 9.24994ptz<<0.\\
\end{array}\right.}

Next, the equation (
1.1
) appears as the limit of its singularly perturbed version, namely

(1.4)

\epsilon^{2}\Delta v-F^{\prime}(v)=0\quad\hbox{in }\Omega,\qquad\ \frac{\partial v}{\partial\nu}=0\quad\hbox{on }\partial\Omega,\qquad\epsilon\to 0,

where
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N+1}
is a smooth bounded domain. As discussed in
[
19
]
, given a solution
v
of (
1.4
), the rescaling
u_{\epsilon}(x)=v(\epsilon x)
yields that

\Delta u_{\epsilon}-F^{\prime}(u_{\epsilon})=0\quad\hbox{in}\quad\Omega_{\epsilon}:=\epsilon^{-1}\Omega.

Since,
\epsilon^{-1}\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{N}
, from standard Schauder estimates, in the limit we obtain a smooth bounded function
u
that solves (
1.1
).

On the other hand, regarding the solutions of the equation (
1.4
), they are exactly the critical points in
H^{1}(\Omega)
of the energy

J_{\epsilon}(v)=\int_{\Omega}\frac{\epsilon}{2}\,|\nabla v|^{2}+\frac{1}{\epsilon}\,F(v).

Observe that
v=\pm 1
are global minimizers of
J_{\epsilon}
. The capture of configurations with coexisting phases is based on the intuition that such configurations
v_{\epsilon}
should resemble an
\epsilon-
regularization of the function

v^{*}=\mathds{1}_{\Lambda}-\mathds{1}_{\Omega\setminus\Lambda},

where
\Lambda\subset\Omega
. The characteristic functions
\mathds{1}_{\Lambda}
and
\mathds{1}_{\Omega\setminus\Lambda}
of
\Lambda
and
\Omega\setminus\Lambda
respectively might be thought as constant phases of a material. Observe also that
v^{*}
minimizes the second term in
J_{\epsilon}
, but
v^{*}\notin H^{1}(\Omega)
(see
[
19
]
).

Let
M
denote the boundary of
\Lambda
relatively to
\overline{\Omega}
. The ideas in
[
19
]
are based on the intuition that when
M
is a smooth orientable hypersurface, the transition from the phase
\mathds{1}_{\Lambda}
to the phase
\mathds{1}_{\Omega\setminus\Lambda}
takes place along the normal direction of
M
with a approximate one dimensional profile, i.e.,

v_{\epsilon}(x)\approx w\left(\frac{z}{\epsilon}\right),

where
z
corresponds to the normal direction to
M
and
w
is described in (
1.2
) and (
1.3
). The gradient term in
J_{\epsilon}(v_{\epsilon})
suggests that
M
should be critical for the area. To make this idea slightly more precise, we take
\epsilon\to 0^{+}
, so that

J_{\epsilon}(v_{\epsilon})\approx{\rm Area}(M)\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\frac{1}{2}|w^{\prime}|^{2}+F(w)\right)dz.

This intuition lead to
Modica’s result in
[
19
]
, which states that for a family of local minimizers
\{v_{\epsilon}\}_{\epsilon}
of
J_{\epsilon}
with bounded energy,

v_{\epsilon}\to v^{*}\quad\hbox{in}\quad L^{1}_{loc},

where
M=\partial\Lambda
has minimal
perimeter. We remark that in case
M
is a smooth hypersurface, this means that
M
is a minimal hypersurface. Observe also that
M
is the approximate nodal set of
v_{\epsilon}
for
\epsilon>0
small.

Kohn and Sternberg in
[
17
]
constructed a family
\{v_{\varepsilon}\}_{\varepsilon}\in(0,\varepsilon_{0})
of solutions of (
1.4
) on a bounded domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
whose zero level set is approaching some given segment as
\varepsilon\to 0
. A similar result on compact manifolds was proved by Pacard and Ritoré
[
21
]
on more general manifolds.

Modica’s developments and many other related developments during the 70th’s, lead De Giorgi to formulate its remarkable conjecture in 1978.

De Giorgi conjecture (1978):
Let
u\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
be an entire solution of the equation. Assume also that
u
is
monotone in one direction
and that
2\leq N+1\leq 8
. Then, after rotation and translation
u(x)=w(x_{1})
, where is determined by (
1.3
).

Some of the key developments in proving or disproving the De Giorgi’s conjecture are the following. This is motivated by the fact that entire minimal graphs over
\mathbb{R}^{N+1}
are affine if
N\leq 7
(
Bernstein conjecture
). The conjecture is known to be true in dimension
N+1=2,\,3
(see
[
15
,
8
]
). In dimension
4\leq N+1\leq 8
only partial results are available. For instance Savin
[
22
]
proved that in those dimensions the conjecture is true under the further assumption that
u(x_{1},x^{\prime})\to\pm 1
as
x_{1}\to\pm\infty
, for any
x^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
. Moreover, it is known that the conjecture is sharp about the dimension. In fact in
[
13
]
the authors constructed a family of monotone solutions in
\mathbb{R}^{9}
which are not one-dimensional.

At the core of the De Giorgi’s conjecture is the hypothesis of monotonicity of the solutions, which implies their stability. To be more precise, let
u\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
be a solution of (
1.1
) and consider the linearized operator of (
1.1
) around
u
, namely

L\psi:=\Delta\psi+(1-3u^{2})\psi\quad\hbox{in }\mathbb{R}^{N},\qquad\psi\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N}).

The stability of
u
is equivalent to saying that the operator
L
satisfies the maximum principle or, in turn it is equivalent to say that the associated quadratic form

B(\psi,\psi):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}|\nabla\psi|^{2}-(1-3u^{2})\psi^{2}.

is positive definite in the space
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
.

The
Morse Index of u
,
m(u)
, is the maximal dimension of a vector space of smooth functions
\psi
with
compact support
for which

B(\psi,\psi)<0.

A solution
u\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
of (
1.1
) is unstable if
m(u)>0
.

Regarding unstable solutions the following are some of the recent and relevant examples and results. Dang, Fife, Peletier in
[
12
]
exhibited saddle solutions in dimension two. Their result was later generalized to higher dimensions by Cabre and Terra, first in 2010 by showing unstable saddle solutions and then in 2011 by showing stable saddle solutions (see
[
10
,
11
]
).

In 2011, del Pino, Kowalczyk, Wei in
[
14
]
constructed solutions to (
1.1
) in three dimensions. The nodal set of such solutions is close to a large dilation of a complete embedded
non-degenerate minimal surface with finite total curvature. These solutions also have finite Morse Index. As a follow up for the three dimensional setting, in 2012 Agudelo, del Pino and Wei constructed two types of solutions to (
1.1
), one with Morse Index one and disconnected nodal set asymptotically characterized by Liouville’s equation. The other one with Morse index going to infinity and nodal set characterized by nested catenoids with logarithmically diverging ends (see
[
1
]
). These solutions correspond to limiting situations of a single one-parameter family of solutions (see
[
16
]
).

Concerned with the higher dimensional case, in
[
2
]
, the same authors constructed solutions to (
1.1
) whose nodal set resembles a
N-
catenoid with a logarithmic correction at infinity. In
[
9
]
, Cabré showed stable solutions of (
1.1
) in dimension
N+1=2m
with
m\leq 7
. The nodal set of these solutions is the
Simon’s cone

C_{m}
. In 2013, Pacard and Wei construct stable solutions to (
1.1
) in dimension
N+1
with
N\geq 7
. The nodal set of these solutions is close to a foliated
Simon’s cone
like smooth surface.

Summarizing, the classification and the construction of solutions to the Allen-Cahn equation (
1.1
) is strongly related to the theory of minimal hypersurfaces, that is hypersurfaces with zero mean curvature. In this work, we present a recent contribution that is a follow up of the results mentioned above. To be more precise, we discuss stability and nondegeneracy results for some special minimal hypersurfaces, which are asymptotic to a specific cone (see Section
2
) and we also present some recent results concerned with existence and stability of solutions to (
1.1
) (see Sect 3).

The plan of the paper is the following: in Section
2
we deal with stability and non-degeneracy properties of some special minimal hypersurfaces, in Section
3
we use these hypersurfaces to construct entire solutions to the Allen-Cahn equation with infinite Morse index
whose zero level set is the union of
k\geq 2
normal graphs over one of these hypersurfaces.

2.
minimal hypersurfaces: stability and nondegeneracy

In this part we discuss further some of the underlying geometric setting related to hysurfaces with minimal perimeter.

We define the perimeter of a measurable subset
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{N+1}
in an open set
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N+1}
as

(2.1)

{\rm Per}(E,\Omega):=\sup\left\{\int_{E}\text{div}Y\,d\xi:\,Y\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{N+1}),\,|Y|\leq 1\right\}.

If
E
has smooth boundary
\partial E
, it follows from the Divergence Theorem that
{\rm Per}(E,\Omega)
coincides with the
N
-dimensional
Hausdorff measure
of
\partial E\cap\Omega
. However, the definition in (
2.1
) allows us to treat the case of sets
E
with non-smooth boundary
\partial E
.

At this point we give precise context of the conclusion of Modica’s results stated in
[
19
]
, that is, the set
\Lambda:=\{x\in\Omega:\,v^{*}(x)=1\}
solves the minimisation problem

{\rm Per}(\Lambda,\Omega)=\min\left\{{\rm Per}(F,\Omega):|F|=\frac{m+|\Omega|}{2}\right\},

where

m=\int_{\Omega}v^{*}dx\in(-|\Omega|,|\Omega|).

Let
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{R}^{N+1}
be a hypersurface with singular set
{\rm sing}(\Sigma)
. Assume that
{\rm sing}(\Sigma)
has zero
N
-dimensional Hausdorff measure and that
\Sigma
is orientable with
\nu_{\Sigma}:\Sigma\setminus{\rm sing(\Sigma)}\to{S}^{N}
a continuous choice of its unit normal vector field. We set

{\rm Area}(\Sigma):=\int_{\Sigma}d\sigma,

where
d\sigma
is the
N
-dimensional Hausdorff measure. We note that
{\rm Area}(\Sigma)\in[0,\infty]
and it coincides with the usual notion of area when
\Sigma
is smooth. Taking a function
\phi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\Sigma\setminus{\rm sing}(\Sigma))
and considering, for
\varepsilon
small enough, the normal variation

\Sigma_{\varepsilon}:=\{y+\varepsilon\phi(y)\nu_{\Sigma}(y):\,y\in{\rm supp}(\phi)\}

and differentiating in
\varepsilon
, we have

(2.2)

\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}{\rm Area}(\Sigma_{\varepsilon})\bigg|_{\varepsilon=0}=-\int_{\Sigma}H_{\Sigma}\phi d\sigma,

where
H_{\Sigma}:=\kappa_{1}+\cdots+\kappa_{N}
is the mean curvature of
\Sigma
and
\kappa_{1},\ldots,\kappa_{N}:\Sigma\setminus{\rm sing}(\Sigma)\to\mathbb{R}
correspond to the principal curvatures of
\Sigma
. The hypersurface
\Sigma
is said to be
minimal
if
H_{\Sigma}\equiv 0
in
\Sigma\backslash{\rm sing}(\Sigma)
or equivalently, since the function
\phi
is arbitrary, if
\Sigma
is a critical point of the Area functional with respect to compactly supported normal variations. Observe that, according to our definition, minimal hypersurfaces are only
critical points
of the Area functional, not necessarily minimisers. The true minimisers of the Area functionals are called
Area minimising
hypersurfaces. More precisely, taking an open set
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{N+1}
, a bounded open set
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N+1}
and assuming, without loss of generality, that
0\in\partial E
, we introduce the following definition.

Definition 2.1
.

We say that
\Sigma:=\partial E
is an area-minimising (or minimising) hypersurface if for any
\rho>0
and for any smooth set
F\subset\mathbb{R}^{N+1}
such that
F\backslash B_{\rho}(0)=E\backslash B_{\rho}(0)
,

{\rm Per}(E,B_{2\rho}(0))\leq{\rm Per}(F,B_{2\rho}(0)).

An area minimising hypersurface is minimal, but the opposite implication is not true. Therefore, in order to give a more accurate description of minimal hypersurfaces, it is crucial to consider the second variation of the
{\rm Area}
functional. More precisely, in the above notations for the normal variation of
\Sigma
, we introduce the second variation of
{\rm Area}(\Sigma)
as follows

(2.3)

\mathcal{Q}_{\Sigma}(\phi):=\frac{d^{2}}{d\varepsilon^{2}}{\rm Area}(\Sigma_{\varepsilon})\bigg|_{\varepsilon=0}=\int_{\Sigma}\big(|\nabla_{\Sigma}\phi|^{2}-|A_{\Sigma}|^{2}\phi^{2})\,d\sigma.

We refer the reader to
[
20
]
for the detailed calculations.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{R}^{N+1}
be a minimal hypersurface of codimension one with
{\rm sing}(\Sigma)
having measure zero and such that
\Sigma\setminus{\rm sing}(\Sigma)
is an orientable hypersurface. Assume that
\Sigma
is minimal. We say that

(1)

\Sigma
is stable if for any
\phi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\Sigma\setminus{\rm sing(\Sigma)})
,
\mathcal{Q}_{\Sigma}(\phi)\geq 0
;

(2)

\Sigma
is strictly stable if

\inf\left\{\mathcal{Q}_{\Sigma}(\phi):\,\phi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\Sigma\setminus{\rm sing}(\Sigma)),\,\||A_{\Sigma}|\phi\|_{L^{2}(\Sigma)}=1\right\}>0.

Integrating by parts it is possible to see that

\mathcal{Q}_{\Sigma}(\phi)=\int_{\Sigma}-J_{\Sigma}\phi\,\phi\,d\sigma\qquad\forall\,\phi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\Sigma\setminus{\rm sing}(\Sigma)),

where
J_{\Sigma}:=\Delta_{\Sigma}+|A_{\Sigma}|^{2}
is known as the
Jacobi operator
of
\Sigma
,
\Delta_{\Sigma}
is the Laplace-Beltrami operator of
\Sigma
and
|A_{\Sigma}|^{2}:=\kappa_{1}^{2}+\dots+\kappa_{N}^{2}
is the squared norm of the second fundamental form. A distributional solution
\phi
to the Jacobi equation

(2.4)

J_{\Sigma}\phi=0

is known as a
Jacobi field
of
\Sigma
. We will be mostly interested in bounded Jacobi fields, but singular or unbounded Jacobi fields will play also a role in our discussion.

Next, let us introduce the Lawson cone

(2.5)

C_{m,n}:=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}:\,(n-1)|x|^{2}=(m-1)|y|^{2}\},\qquad m,n\geq 2,\qquad N+1=m+n.

This cone is minimal, since its mean curvature is zero everywhere, except the origin. Also,
C_{m,m}
is known as the Simon’s cone.

We are interested in some special hypersurfaces
\Sigma
, which are asymptotic to these cones. In this regard and in high dimension we have the following result.

Theorem 2.1
.

[
4
]

Let
m,n\geq 2
,
m+n\geq 8
. Then there exist exactly two smooth minimal hypersurfaces
\Sigma^{\pm}_{m,n}\subset E^{\pm}_{m,n}
satisfying that

(1)

\Sigma^{\pm}_{m,n}
are asymptotic to
C_{m,n}
at infinity.

(2)

{\rm dist}(\Sigma^{\pm}_{m,n},\{0\})=1
;

(3)

\Sigma^{\pm}_{m,n}
are
O(m)\times O(n)
-invariant.

Moreover, the Jacobi field
y\cdotp\nu_{\Sigma^{\pm}_{m,n}}(y)
never vanishes.

For these hypersurfaces we proved the following stability result (see
[
5
]
).

Theorem 2.2

(
[
5
]
)
.

The hypersurfaces constructed in Theorem
2.1
are strictly stable.

The stability of the hypersurfaces constructed in Theorem
2.1
directly follows from point
(3)
of Theroem
2.1
. The nontrivial point of Theorem
2.2
is that the stability is actually strict. This follows from an injectivity result for the the Jacobi operator
J_{\Sigma}
of
\Sigma
in some suitable function spaces (see Sections
3
and
4
of
[
5
]
for the details). In any case, strict stability does not rule out the existence of bounded and even decaying Jacobi fields, such as the Jacobi fields
\{\nu_{\Sigma}(y)\cdotp e_{i}\}_{1\leq i\leq N+1}
and the Jacobi field
y\cdotp\nu_{\Sigma}(y)
coming from dilations. Strict stability implies that the Jacobi fields do not have fast decay at infinity.

Next, we consider hypersurfaces which are asymptotic to a fixed Lawson cone in lower dimension.

Theorem 2.3

(
[
6
,
18
]
)
.

Let
m,n\geq 2
,
m+n\leq 7
. Then there exists a unique complete, embedded,
O(m)\times O(n)
invariant minimal hypersurface
\Sigma_{m,n}
such that

(1)

\Sigma_{m,n}
is asymptotic to the cone
C_{m,n}
at infinity,

(2)

\Sigma_{m,n}
intersects
C_{m,n}
infinitely many times,

(3)

\Sigma_{m,n}
meets
\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\{0\}
orthogonally,

(4)

dist(\Sigma_{m,n},C_{m,n})=1
.

For such hypersurfaces it is relevant to compute the Morse index, defined as

(2.6)

Morse(\Sigma):=\sup\{\dim(X):\,X\text{ subspace of }C^{\infty}_{c}(\Sigma\setminus{\rm sing}(\Sigma)):\,\mathcal{Q}_{\Sigma}(\phi)<0,\,\forall\,\phi\in X\backslash\{0\}\}.

It follows from the definition that
Morse(\Sigma)=0
if and only if
\Sigma
is stable. For our hypersurfaces we have the following result.

Theorem 2.4

(
[
5
]
)
.

The hypersurfaces constructed in Theorem
2.3
have infinite Morse index.

The proof of Theorem
2.4
given in Section
4
of
[
5
]
is explicit. In fact it is possible construct a infinite-dimensional subspace of compactly supported functions which make the quadratic form
\mathcal{Q}_{\Sigma}
negative. This is possible thanks to the fact that
3\leq N\leq 7
.

Minimal hypersurfaces are invariant under dilations, translations and rotations. Such geometric transformations give rise to at most
\frac{N(N+1)}{2}+N+2
linearly independent Jacobi fields, which are known as
geometric Jacobi fields
.

Definition 2.3
.

•

A minimal hypersurface
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{R}^{N+1}
is said to be nondegenerate if all its bounded Jacobi fields are geometric.

•

Let
S\subset O(N+1)
be a subgroup. We say that
\Sigma
is
S
-nondegenerate if all
S
-invariant Jacobi fields are geometric.

A similar definition was given, for instance, in
[
14
]
for the Costa-Hofmann-Meeks surfaces. In particular, for our hypersurfaces, we have the following nondegeneracy result.

Theorem 2.5
.

(1)

The hypersurfaces constructed in Theorem
2.1
are nondegenerate.

(2)

The hypersurface
\Sigma_{m,n}
constructed in Theorem
2.3
is
O(m)\times O(n)
-nondegenerate,
for any
m,n\geq 2
,
m+n\leq 7
.

The proof of this nondegeneracy result is based on a Fourier expansion of the Jacobi fields of our hypersurfaces and either the strict stability in case
N+1\geq 8
or the symmetry properties for
4\leq N+1\leq 7
, which allow us to reduce the Jacobi equation
J_{\Sigma}\phi=0
to a second order ODE.

3.
The Allen-Cahn equation

In this section we turn again up to equation (
1.1
) and we discuss the connection with the Lawson cone
C_{m,n}
introduced in (
2.5
). We observe that
C_{m,n}
is invariant under the action of the group
O(m)\times O(n)
and that
\mathbb{R}^{N+1}\backslash C_{m,n}
has
two connected components
, namely,

\displaystyle E^{+}

\displaystyle:=\bigg\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}:\,|x|^{2}<\frac{m-1}{n-1}|y|^{2}\bigg\}\quad{\hbox{inside of }C_{m,n};}

\displaystyle E^{-}

\displaystyle:=\bigg\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}:\,|x|^{2}>\frac{m-1}{n-1}|y|^{2}\bigg\}\quad{\hbox{outside of }C_{m,n}.}

Assume
N=m+n\geq 8
and let
\Sigma_{m,n}^{+}\subset E^{+}
and
\Sigma_{m,n}^{-}\subset E^{-}
be the two smooth stable minimal surfaces discussed in Theorem
2.1
. In what follows
\Sigma
denotes either
\Sigma^{+}_{m,n}
or
\Sigma^{-}_{m,n}
and
\nu:\Sigma\to S^{N}
is a fixed choice of the unit normal vector.

Next, for
\varepsilon>0
small enough, set
\Sigma_{\varepsilon}:=\varepsilon^{-1}\Sigma
and consider the associated
Fermi coordinates

{\tt x}={\tt p}+z\nu(\varepsilon{\tt p})

for
{\tt p}\in\Sigma_{\varepsilon}
and
|z|<\frac{\delta}{\varepsilon}
. The first existence result that we present is the following.

Theorem 3.1

(
[
3
]
)
.

Let
m,\,n\geq 3
,
n+m=N\geq 8
and let
\Sigma
be one of the hypersurfaces constructed in Theorem
2.1
. Then there exists
\varepsilon_{0}>0
such that, for any
\varepsilon\in(0,\varepsilon_{0})
, there exists a smooth solution
u_{\varepsilon}
to (
1.1
) in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
such that

(1)

u_{\varepsilon}
is
O(m)\times O(n)
-invariant;

(2)

the nodal set of
u_{\varepsilon}
has exactly two connected components which are normal graphs over
\Sigma_{\varepsilon}
, i.e.,

u_{\varepsilon}({\tt x})=w(z-{\tt h}_{1}(\varepsilon{\tt p}))-w(z-{\tt h}_{2}(\varepsilon{\tt p}))-1+o_{\varepsilon}(1)

where
{\tt h}_{1},{\tt h}_{2}:\Sigma\to\mathbb{R}
are
O(m)\times O(n)
-invariant functions.

(3)

m(u_{\varepsilon})=+\infty
and for any
\varepsilon>0
small and any
R>2\varepsilon^{-1}
,

(3.1)

\int_{B_{R}}\frac{1}{2}|\nabla u_{\varepsilon}|^{2}+\frac{1}{4}(1-u_{\varepsilon}^{2})^{2}\leq cR^{N}.

In low dimensions (
3.1
) suggests stability of the solution according to a conjecture raised in
[
14
]
(see also
[
23
]
). However, Theorem
3.1
provides a counterexample to such conjecture.

On the other hand, the normal graphs
{\tt h}_{1},{\tt h}_{2}
are approximate solutions to the Jacobi-Toda system

(3.2)

\begin{aligned} \varepsilon^{2}\big(\Delta_{\Sigma}{\tt h}_{1}+|A_{\Sigma}|^{2}{\tt h}_{1}\big)-a_{0}e^{-\sqrt{2}({\tt h}_{2}-{\tt h}_{1})}&=0\\
\varepsilon^{2}\big(\Delta_{\Sigma}{\tt h}_{2}+|A_{\Sigma}|^{2}{\tt h}_{2}\big)+a_{0}e^{-\sqrt{2}({\tt h}_{2}-{\tt h}_{1})}&=0\end{aligned}\quad\hbox{in}\quad\Sigma,

where
a_{0}>0
constant depending only on
w
(see (
1.2
)). By setting
{\tt v}_{1}={\tt h}_{1}+{\tt h}_{2}
,
{\tt v}_{2}={\tt h}_{1}-{\tt h}_{2}
, system (
3.2
) decouples into the system

(3.3)

\begin{aligned} \Delta_{\Sigma}{\tt v}_{1}+|A_{\Sigma}|^{2}{\tt v}_{1}&=0\\
\varepsilon^{2}\big(\Delta_{\Sigma}{\tt v}_{2}+|A_{\Sigma}|^{2}{\tt v}_{2}\big)-2a_{0}e^{-\sqrt{2}{\tt v}_{2}}&=0\end{aligned}\quad\hbox{in}\quad\Sigma.

The first equation in (
3.3
) can be easily solved by choosing
{\tt v}_{1}=0
, i.e.,
{\tt h}_{1}=-{\tt h}_{2}
. Concerned with the second one, we present the following result.

Theorem 3.2
