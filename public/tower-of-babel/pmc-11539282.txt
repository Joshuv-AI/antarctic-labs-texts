Rare posterior mediastinal hemangiomas invading the spinal cord through the intervertebral foramen and causing acute symptoms: illustrative case
Authors: Chongxi Xu, Xiang Yang, Yongliang Jiang, Yuwen Wang, Jiaxi Wang, Bin Xu, Jianguo Xu, Junpeng Ma
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUNDHemangioma is a rare vascular tumor characterized by endothelial cell proliferation that primarily occurs in soft tissues. While most cases in the paraspinal posterior mediastinum are clinically indolent, they can also exhibit aggressive growth, leading to severe clinical outcomes and necessitating urgent treatment.OBSERVATIONSA 61-year-old right-handed female presented with reduced lower-extremity mobility that had begun 4 days earlier. An examination revealed that both lower limbs had grade 4 muscle strength. Computed tomography and magnetic resonance imaging revealed a right T1–3 paravertebral dumbbell-shaped soft tissue mass invading the spinal canal, destroying the T2 vertebra, and compressing the spinal cord and nerve roots. Elective surgery was planned, but a rapid muscle strength decline prompted emergency surgery. Postoperative muscle strength improved to grade 4, and at the 3-month follow-up, muscle strength had fully recovered.LESSONSPostoperative pathological examination revealed that the mediastinal dumbbell-shaped lesion was a hemangioma. While most hemangiomas progress slowly, acute symptoms of spinal cord compression are exceedingly rare. This case demonstrates the aggressive behavior of hemangioma, which invaded through the intervertebral foramen and compressed the spinal cord, resulting in acute symptoms. Vascular anomalies include both vascular tumors and malformations. Vascular tumors are tumor masses that form due to endothelial cell hyperproliferation and can occur throughout the body. The term “hemangioma” is correctly used to describe these vascular tumors. Common histological types include cavernous, capillary, venous, and arteriovenous hemangiomas.1, 2 Hemangioma is a rare benign vascular lesion that occurs in soft tissues and can extensively cover various parts of the body.3 Simple mediastinal hemangioma (MH) is particularly rare. Although infantile hemangiomas involving the central nervous system have been reported, these patients often present with noticeable skin symptoms, facilitating early detection and treatment.4, 5 Most adult patients with MH lack specific clinical manifestations, with symptoms primarily depending on the lesion’s location, size, and extent. To our knowledge, MH rarely involves the paravertebral and epidural spaces.6–8 Cases of MH mimicking the aggressive growth of malignant tumors are extremely rare. Herein, we report the case of a posterior MH that invaded the vertebral body and extended through the intervertebral foramen into the spinal canal, resulting in acute spinal cord compression. Following pathological confirmation, we reviewed the imaging and histopathological features and treatment strategies and conducted a comprehensive literature review.

Illustrative Case

History and Examination

A 61-year-old right-handed woman with a 4-day history of unexplained reduced mobility in her lower extremities presented to our center. Neurological examination revealed muscle weakness in both lower limbs, graded as 4/5. Computed tomography (CT) showed a soft tissue density mass of approximately 4.4 × 2.4 cm in size in the right paraspinal region at the T2–3 vertebral level. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) results revealed an irregular mass on the right side of the T2 vertebra, displaying long T1 and long T2 signals. Part of the lesion extended through the intervertebral foramen into the spinal canal, compressing the corresponding spinal cord. Postcontrast MRI revealed irregular homogeneous enhancement of the mass and abnormal signal changes in the T2 vertebral body, which exhibited a striated pattern with moderate enhancement (Fig. 1A–H). Initially, we suspected the mediastinal mass was a metastatic tumor or schwannoma. However, whole-body imaging scans revealed no abnormalities in other regions, and tumor marker tests were negative. On the 3rd day after admission, the patient’s muscle strength in both lower limbs rapidly declined, with the strength of the left side decreasing to 2/5 and on the right side to 3/5. Emergency surgery was immediately performed on the patient. Surgical Procedure

The procedure was performed according to the standard neurosurgical technique for the resection of intradural space-occupying lesions.9, 10 After the patient was placed under general anesthesia and positioned prone, a 10-cm midline posterior incision was made over the T1–3 vertebrae. The skin, subcutaneous tissue, and muscles were incised in layers to expose the laminae of T1–3. The right hemilaminae of T1–3, along with the right facet and pedicle of T2 invaded by the tumor, were removed to fully expose the lesion. The tumor demonstrated marked vascularization with an extremely rich blood supply. Part of the tumor penetrated the dura mater and severely damaged the T2 vertebral body, invading the pedicle, lamina, and facet of T2 and partially affecting T1. Careful dissection was performed to release and separate the adhesions between the intradural and extradural tumor masses and the T1–3 nerve roots. The intradural component of the tumor was completely resected, and the paraspinal, pedicle, and vertebral body components were partially resected. The spinal cord and bilateral nerve roots were fully decompressed. Hemostasis was meticulously achieved within the thoracic cavity, and an artificial dura mater was used to repair the dural defect. Titanium rods and screws were fixed in the pedicle direction of the right T1 and T3 vertebrae, as well as the left T1, T2, and T3 vertebrae.

Clinical Outcomes

On the 2nd day after surgery, the weakness in both lower limbs was significantly alleviated, and the patient could independently get out of bed. Muscle strength in both lower limbs was graded as 4. A routine postoperative CT scan showed that the screws were properly fixed and that there was no obvious bleeding or further bone destruction at the lesion site. Five days after treatment, the patient recovered and was discharged. Three months postoperatively, follow-up revealed that the patient’s lower-limb activity had completely returned to normal, with muscle strength graded as 5. Postoperative pathological analysis had revealed a soft tissue tumor rich in blood vessels, including small- to medium-sized venous and capillary-like vessels, displaying invasive growth consistent with hemangioma (Fig. 1I and J). Imaging results were also reviewed at 3 months. Spinal cord compression was completely relieved, spinal cord morphology was normal, no cavity was formed, titanium rods and screws were fixed in place, and spinal stability was maintained (Fig. 2). Discussion

Observations

According to the latest classification by the International Society for the Study of Vascular Anomalies,1 cases previously referred to as “mediastinal angiomatosis” should be categorized as “mediastinal vascular tumor” or “mediastinal hemangioma.”8, 11–13 MHs are rare, accounting for approximately 0.5% of mediastinal masses. These lesions can occur at any age and exhibit no gender predominance. The anterior mediastinum is slightly more frequently affected than the posterior mediastinum. According to current literature, the vast majority (90%) of cases are either cavernous or capillary hemangiomas. The remaining cases include rare entities such as hemangioendotheliomas, hemangiofibromas, fibromas, fibrolipomas, and venous hemangiomas.14 Nearly half of the patients are asymptomatic, with the hemangioma being discovered incidentally. The remaining patients present with nonspecific symptoms such as cough, chest pain, and dyspnea, which result from compression or, less commonly, invasion of adjacent structures. For patients with MHs, current perspectives suggest that the optimal treatment is radical resection, even for asymptomatic patients. For large, diffuse hemangiomas, incomplete resection is acceptable given the low recurrence rate. Incomplete resection can reduce tumor volume and bleeding risk, as well as decrease associated clinical manifestations, but it requires careful follow-up. Currently, one case of anterior MH35 and our case report demonstrate infiltrative characteristics; however, these tumors are essentially benign, and there are no reports of metastasis or recurrence for this type of tumor. Therefore, we do not recommend postoperative radiotherapy; however, active follow-up is essential.

Based on the literature, MHs typically appear on CT as well-circumscribed, round, or oval lesions. Some might exhibit calcified thrombi, known as pathognomonic “phleboliths,” although this is not always characteristic (probability accounts for about 10% of patients with MH). McAdams et al. suggested that a characteristic feature of MHs on enhanced CT is a tumor center that shows stronger enhancement than the periphery.15 MRI usually reveals a strong signal on T2-weighted imaging with heterogeneous enhancement of the lesion mass.7 Sakurai et al. suggested that a markedly high signal on fat-suppressed T2-weighted images might be a characteristic feature.16 Overall, these tumors are often misdiagnosed as schwannomas or metastatic tumors. Therefore, the preoperative diagnosis of MH is challenging. Although these masses are rare, they should be considered a critical differential diagnosis for posterior mediastinal masses due to their unique bleeding risks compared to other tumors. Suspecting a hemangioma preoperatively can significantly influence surgical approaches and treatment strategies.

As an important differential diagnosis for mediastinal masses, diagnostic biopsy is not advised due to the increased risk of bleeding and the potential for false-negative results, making preoperative diagnosis challenging. Second, in our case of a paraspinal posterior MH, we considered that its blood supply might originate from the spinal radicular arteries or vertebral vessels and that preoperative embolization would increase the risk of ischemia. Therefore, we would recommend extending the surgical incision, carefully controlling bleeding under microscopy, and aiming for maximal tumor resection. However, if preoperative diagnosis as a hemangioma is confirmed and the responsible vessels are identified, preoperative embolization to reduce intraoperative bleeding can be considered. This approach can avoid the need for a larger incision and reduce the risk of bleeding and surgical difficulties associated with thoracoscopic resection of anterior and middle MHs.

Currently, 25 well-documented cases of hemangioma in the posterior mediastinum, in addition to our case, have been reported (Table 1).8, 10, 14, 17–34 We have summarized their age, sex, imaging findings, whether there was spinal canal invasion, and final pathology. Reports generally describe these tumors as nonaggressive masses. However, recent cases have demonstrated the potential for invasive growth.35 Posterior mediastinal paraspinal hemangiomas can invade vertebral bodies and extend into the spinal canal via the intervertebral foramina,22, 30, 33 although they have not yet been shown to cause acute spinal cord compression. In the abovementioned cases, most tumors were removed via thoracoscopic surgery. Nonetheless, taking into account the bleeding issues associated with hemangiomas, in those mediastinal masses where hemangiomas cannot be excluded, surgical intervention can be considered. Importantly, ours is the only reported case of acute progression of a posterior mediastinal paraspinal hemangioma leading to acute spinal cord compression. This confirms the potential of posterior MHs to cause central nervous system symptoms. The tumor in our case involved adjacent vertebral bodies and the spinal canal. Therefore, posterior neurosurgical access is beneficial not only for relieving spinal cord compression and repairing damaged dura mater but also for reconstructing spine stability, which is highly advantageous for such patients. Lessons

We have reported the case of a posterior mediastinal paraspinal hemangioma exhibiting invasive growth, capable of infiltrating vertebral bodies, extending into the spinal canal, invading the dura, and ultimately causing acute spinal cord compression. Therefore, masses located in the posterior mediastinum with an unclear relationship to the spinal canal should be taken seriously. Our case demonstrates that surgery performed by neurosurgeons via a posterior approach can achieve complete tumor resection while also allowing for the exploration of spinal cord conditions and the reconstruction of spine stability, which is beneficial for such patients.
