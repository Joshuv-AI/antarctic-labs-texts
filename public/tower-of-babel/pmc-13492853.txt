Mediating role of organizational justice in quality of work-life and role-based performance among hospital employees: Cross-sectional research
Authors: Adebomi Joseph Adegbenga, Akinbobola I. Olusola, Abayomi Olubanjo Olusa, Akintola A. Aderonke, Olabode John Omotosho, Jacob Ropo Jekayinoluwa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:Role-based performance in an organization’s workforce is essential to its sustainability. Hence, this study aimed to examine the mediating role of organizational justice in the quality of work-life and role-based performance among hospital employees.MATERIALS AND METHODS:A cross-sectional design was adopted, and the research data were gathered from Adventist hospitals in Southwestern Nigeria using a multistage sampling technique in 2024. A sample of 303 employees responded to the research questionnaire, which included the quality of work-life scale, role-based performance scale, and organizational justice scale. Correlation tests, simple and multiple linear regression analyses, and Sobel tests were adopted as statistical techniques, with SPSS version 23 for Windows and an online Sobel test calculator applied in the data analyses. A significance level of 0.05 was considered.RESULTS:Work-life quality significantly and positively predicted role-based performance (usβ =0.47, sβ =0.50 t = 9.88; P < .01). In addition, organizational justice significantly and positively predicted role-based performance (usβ =.47, sβ =.50, t = 9.88, P < .01). When organizational justice was joined with quality of work-life as a mediator in predicting role-based performance, there was an increase in the variance contributed to role-based performance from 32% to 38% (R2=.32; ΔR2=.06). The Sobel test result indicated that organizational justice indeed mediated the relationship between work-life quality and role-based performance (Z = 2.35, SE =.05, P < .05).CONCLUSION:The study concluded that excellent quality of work-life and organizational justice result in increased role-based performance and productivity.

Introduction

Healthcare organizations and their collaborators, including governments and policymakers, encounter significant challenges in maintaining accessibility and affordability in healthcare.[1] These challenges are further intensified by demographic changes, particularly the rapid ageing of populations. According to the Organization for Economic Cooperation and Development (OECD), by 2050, nearly 27% of the global population will be over 65, and more than 10% will be over 80.[2] This demographic shift is expected to significantly increase healthcare expenditures, with spending projected to rise from 8.8% of GDP in 2017 to 10.2% by 2030 in OECD countries.[2] Overcoming these challenges is impossible without healthcare workers’ active participation and high-level performance.[1] This is particularly so because healthcare is one of the most crucial sectors where organizational performance must be modified, enhanced, and improved.[1]

Another significant issue facing healthcare organizations due to an ageing population and the resulting rise in care demands and accessibility is a lack of healthcare workers.[34] Since healthcare is inherently a people-centred sector, addressing these challenges depends mainly on the commitment and performance of its workforce. Therefore, enhancing organizational performance in healthcare is inextricably linked to improving the job performance of healthcare workers.[567]

Job performance, particularly in the healthcare sector, has been extensively studied and conceptualized in various ways.[1] Krijgsheld et al. identify four key dimensions of performance relevant to healthcare: role-based performance, contextual performance, adaptive performance, and counterproductive work behavior.[1] Role-based performance encompasses specific job duties, technical competencies, and broader responsibilities such as citizenship behavior and customer service.[89]

Eneanya elucidated role-based performance management as the current concept in organizational performance analysis, engrained in keeping organizations focused on achieving their goals and objectives.[10] Akinbobola et al.[11] specified that the role-based performance concept relates to how successfully individuals play their prescribed role and is measured in terms of their relative success or failure in that role. The prescribed role alluded to here encompasses the tasks attached to the role, including citizenship performance and customer service.[11]

According to Shrestha et al.,[12] managing an organization, especially in the healthcare sector, has become very complex in today’s dynamic business environments. Healthcare employees play a vital role in patient care and the smooth operation of health facilities; however, they face challenges that impact their well-being and job performance, such as long shifts, disruption of sleep, work-life balance, and occupational hazards associated with exposure to various diseases. These can significantly affect their performance on the job and, in turn, determine their quality of work-life.[13]

Quality of work-life, which encompasses physical and psychological working conditions, has been shown to reduce absenteeism, accidents, stress, and job dissatisfaction while increasing productivity, institutional effectiveness, employment, and the country’s economic development.[1415] Employees in such an environment tend to be dedicated, conscientious, motivated, and successful, contributing to the organization’s overall performance. Employees are committed, principled, spirited, and successful, and perform well in a healthy, dynamic work-life quality.[16]

Similarly, organizational justice is crucial for healthcare employees’ behavior and performance[17] because healthcare employees tend to perform well at their jobs when they perceive fairness at their workplace.[1819] Organizational justice plays a vital mediating role in workplace behavior and change,[20] significantly influencing healthcare employees’ quality of work-life, performance, and well-being.[202122] Organizational justice perceptions and the effects of those perceptions on employees’ work-related attitudes and behavior are essential to role-based performance.[22] Shakerian et al.[23] found that the jigsaw technique in organizations enhances motivation and performance among healthcare workers. Whereas, according to Owan et al.,[24] mental stress is a key factor affecting job performance. Vardanjani et al.[16] reported an inverse relationship between organizational health and employee performance. In contrast, Jafarian et al.[13] observed that healthcare managers’ attention to empowerment strategies in a crisis such as COVID-19 has improved employees’ performance. Najimi et al.[25] also established a positive link between emotional intelligence, social responsibility, and job performance in health service providers.

The complexity of managing healthcare organizations in today’s dynamic environment necessitates attracting and retaining committed employees to meet increasing service demands.[12] Internal commitment from employees is crucial for adapting to societal pressures and rapid technological changes, thereby improving role-based performance.[12] Despite the recognized importance of these factors, the interplay among quality of work-life, organizational justice, and role-based performance, particularly within the context of healthcare organizations in Southwest Nigeria, remains underexplored.

Therefore, the aim of this study is to examine the mediating role of organizational justice in the relationship between quality of work-life and role-based performance among Adventist hospital employees in Southwest Nigeria.

Materials and Methods

Study design and setting

This quantitative research utilized a cross-sectional design to collect data through structured psychological scales. The study was conducted in 2024 in Adventist Hospitals in Southwest Nigeria. These hospitals are located in Ilisan-Remo, in Ogun State, Ibadan, in Oyo State, Ile-Ife, in Osun State, and Inisa, in Osun State, where the respondents were met in their offices.

Study participants and sampling

The study participants were employees of Adventist hospitals in Southwest Nigeria. The sample size for this study was determined using Krejcie and Morgan’s[26] Sample Size Determination Table. From a total of 1556 employees across the organizations in Southwest Nigeria, 303 employees were sampled utilizing a multistage sampling technique. Firstly, purposive sampling was utilized to select four Adventist hospitals. From these four hospitals, stratified sampling was used to categorize the employees into various work departments/units, and the respondents were obtained from each unit using the convenience sampling technique.

Inclusion Criteria and Exclusion Criteria: Only full-time employees in the organization (both clinical and nonclinical) who have worked in the hospital for at least a year, who are 18 years of age and above, and who show willingness to participate were considered for the study. However, those who are contract workers, trainees, interns, or part-time employees were excluded.

Data collection tool and technique

The battery of instruments comprised measures of quality of work-life, organizational justice, role-based performance, and socio-demographic characteristics of the respondents’ section.

The quality of work-life scale

The work-life scale developed by Walton[27] was used to measure the quality of life. The 30-item scale is designed to assess eight dimensions of work-life quality: adequate and fair compensation, safe and healthy environment, development of human capacities, growth and security, social integration, constitutionalism, total life space, and social relevance. All the items were rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (very low) to a very high measurement of 5 (very high). The lowest possible total score is 30, and the highest possible score is 150. Higher scores on the measure above the mean value indicate higher quality of work-life, and scores that are below the mean are indicative of low quality of life. The scale has a Cronbach’s alpha reliability coefficient of .96., and for the sub-scales, adequate and fair compensation (0.81); safe and healthy environment (0.84); development of human capacities (0.86); growth and security (0,76); social integration (0.66) constitutionalism (0.88); total life space (0.84); growth and security (0.81). The researchers conducted a pilot study and found the internal reliability of the composite measure of the quality of work-life to be α = 0.91.[28]

Role-based performance scale

Welbourne et al.[29] designed the instrument, and it was used to assess role-based performance on the job. The scale comprises 20 items with five dimensions - job, career, innovation, team, and organization. The scale is rated on a 5-point Likert scale from 1- Need much improvement to 5 - excellent. The mean score serves as the norm for interpreting the scale, such that higher scores indicate higher role-based performance. The average alpha value for each of the five factors is as follows: job holder (0.75), innovator (0.90), career person (0.90), team members (0.87), and organization members (0.84). Since each factor consists of only four items, these reliability estimates represent exceptionally strong internal consistency. For the entire scale (all 20 items), alpha values ranged from 0.86 to 0.90. The researchers conducted a pilot study and reported an internal reliability coefficient of (α = 92).[28]

Organizational justice scale

A 20-item scale developed by Colquitt[30] was adopted. It has four dimensions, namely procedural justice, distributive justice, informational justice, and interpersonal justice. The items were rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 - never to 5 - always. Higher scores indicate a higher perception of organizational justice when interpreted. The instrument showed good total reliability indices (ordinal alpha and composite reliability values greater than 0.80) and adequate discriminant and convergent validity (average variance extracted indices greater than 0.6).[31] A reliability Cronbach alpha of α =0.93 was reported for the composite measure of organizational justice in the present study.

The researchers conducted a pilot study and found an internal reliability coefficient of (α = 0.93).[28]

Socio-demographic tool

The socio-demographic factors comprise the participants’ Age, Years in Service, Gender, Educational Qualification, Marital Status, Family Size, and Hospital name. These variables are essential for understanding the characteristics of the study population.[32]

In order to conduct the study, the researchers first obtained the necessary permission from the authorities of the participating organizations (Adventist hospital administrations). Respondents were assured that the data they provided would be used for research purposes only. Participation was voluntary, and anonymity was ensured. The researcher gave the questionnaires to the participants simultaneously in their different hospital departments. After informed consent was obtained from the Adventist hospital employees, they were requested to fill out copies of the questionnaire booklet. The filled copies of the questionnaire were collected, and participants were appreciated for their participation as well as their time.

Ethical consideration

Ethical approval was granted by the Redeemer University Internal Research Ethics Committee (IREC) to conduct the study. The ethics reference number is RUN/REC/2024/113

Statistical and data analysis

SPSS software version 23 was used to analyze the data collected. Both descriptive and inferential statistics were used. Pearson Product-Moment Correlation analysis, simple and multiple linear regression, and the Sobel test were used to test the collected data. The Sobel test was conducted using an online Sobel test calculator application. A significance level of 0.05 was considered.

Mediation analysis

Adopting the Baron and Kenny mediation model,[33] regression analysis was first conducted to test the expected association for a mediation test. Afterwards, the establishment of the mediation test was confirmed with the results of the Sobel analysis, using the regression test. These are summarized in Table 4. The result in Table 4 revealed that organizational justice increases significantly with quality of work-life (B = .45, β = 0.51, t = 10.30, P < .01). This explains a significant relationship between the predictor and the mediating factor. Also, role-based performance increases with an increase in quality of work-life (B = .47, β = 0.56, t = 11.84, P < .01). This explains the significant relationship between the predictor and the criterion variable. Organizational justice significantly and positively predicted role-based performance (B = .47, β = .50, t = 9.88, P < .01). The significant relationship between the mediating factor and the criterion variable was established. When the effect of organizational justice was added to quality of work-life as a mediator in predicting role-based performance, there was an increase in the variance contributed to role-based performance from 32% to 38% (R2=.32; ΔR2=.06). This explains an indirect effect of 6% added to the role-based performance. The observed beta value for both the predictor (quality of work-life – β = from .56 to .42) and the mediator variable (organizational justice – β = from .50 to .28) decreased when the indirect effect was tested, showing a decrease in the initial prediction, and such could be attributed to the mediation effect. The outcome also confirms the four steps necessary to conduct a Sobel analysis as indicated by Baron and Kenny.[33] The Sobel test result indicated that organizational justice indeed mediated the relationship between work-life quality and role-based performance (Z = 2.35, SE =.05, P < .05). By implication, the presence of organizational justice serves as a partial mediator and enhances the influence of quality of work-life on role-based performance among employees by 6%.

Discussions

The study examined the mediating role of organizational justice in the relationship between quality of work-life and role-based performance among hospital employees. As expected, the finding revealed that the quality of work-life significantly predicted role-based performance positively. This finding agrees with the studies of Afroz[34] and Bataineh,[35] who asserted that work plays a vital part in every human’s life. The quality of work-life depicts a healthy state of employees in the workplace, thus enabling them to stay focused with less distraction and perform as expected within their job roles. Having the right state of mind and health aligns with eudaimonic well-being and fosters growth in other aspects of life and engagements. Gazi et al.[14] opined that movements to improve the quality of work-life will lead to changes in work ethics and overall societal change. On the contrary, poor work-life quality may result in the loss of essential concepts and valid approaches at the workplace.[36]

It was also discovered that organizational justice predicted role-based performance. This association is essential for the mediation test, and it aligns with the previous results of Gori et al.[37] and Obalade et al.[38] When employees’ perception of the value placed on them in their workplace is positive and high, it indicates that they are valued, and these play some form of motivational role by increasing their interest in the accomplishment of job roles. Also, the perception of equity in pay and fair distribution of needed resources at work prevents negative work orientations. Such negative work orientation triggers the sense of injustice and a behavioral response of losing interest in work activities, which could lead to poor role-based performance.

The regression analysis and the conducted Sobel test indicated that organizational justice indeed partially mediated the relationship between quality of work-life and role-based performance by significantly enhancing the impact of quality of work-life on role-based performance. This explains the relevance of a fair work environment, even when employers give good consideration to the employees’ quality of life. The effort of employees in their job roles will increase when the perception of equity is associated with their well-being (quality of life). The findings were consistent with those of previous researchers, such as Hermanto and Scrimulyani,[19] Gori et al.,[37] and Obalade et al.,[38] whose research found the mediating role of organizational justice on three work outcomes (job satisfaction, organizational commitment, and turnover intention). Their studies concluded that distributive, procedural, and interactional justice are vital in mediating the relationship between quality of work-life and organizational work outcomes. Further explaining the relevance of equity in the workplace, this study’s findings corroborated Snyman et al.’s[39] research, which revealed that organizational justice fully mediates the relationship between the quality of work-life of psychological contracts and retention policies in the higher education context. From a different perspective, Talukdar and Mishra[20] found that the relationship between quality of work-life and job performance was partially mediated by organizational justice, and fully mediated by procedural justice. The observed variance in the previous studies could be the differences in their construct, as job performance generally differs to some extent from organizational work outcomes. Also, differences in the sampled population and types of jobs could be an indicator. Even though it is expected that the outcome of the present study may be related to the previous studies due to similarities in concepts, role-based performance is still different from the overall performance, as they are specifically related to employees’ specific job activities. There is a chance that employees’ overall performance is perceived as high due to a general evaluation, while their role-based performance is not. Therefore, understanding the role of organizational justice in the relationship between quality of work-life and role-based performance is essential. With a healthy work-life balance, employees are likely to function effectively. The addition of a satisfactory perception of fairness in work-related factors would enhance employees’ satisfaction levels and give them a sense of adequate consideration by the organization. The sustenance of such positive perception is reflected in increased role-based performance.

Limitations of the Study

This study used Adventist hospital employees as respondents. This sample consists of individuals from one geographical location working in an organization where the workplace policies and structure may not represent those of other private bodies or government organizations. In addition, there is the possibility of having varying outcomes if the study captured employees in government general hospitals via other scientific sampling and was not purposive. Aside from the fact that the current study had a limited sample size, statistical techniques and research design could enable more objective and direct observations to be applied. Future research could incorporate samples from other faith-based organizations and Government organizations, utilizing mixed methods approaches. Through this, respondents could gain an in-depth understanding of their quality of life, while objective role-based performances could be measured using productivity indicators.

Conclusion

The study investigated the predictive impact of the quality of work-life on role-based performance in Adventist hospital employees while considering the mediating role of organizational justice. In accordance with the procedure for mediation analysis, the quality of work-life predicted organizational justice and role-based performance. Quality of work-life and organizational justice also predicted role-based performance. The conducted Sobel test result indicated that organizational justice indeed mediated the relationship between quality of work-life and role-based performance by significantly enhancing the impact of quality of work-life on role-based performance, with 6% additional variance attributed to it.

These findings are in tandem with the compensation theory of work-life balance, which explains how employees could switch from investing in job roles that are perceived negatively to other forms of activity. When employees are unhappy, their focus shifts towards activities that will compensate for work-life, thus compromising their job roles.[40] According to the findings, a good work-life balance is linked to great employee role-based performance. This demonstrates that when employees have a higher quality of work-life, they develop a strong commitment to both their employers and their job. Thus, the possibility of excellent work-life quality, better treatment, and healthier rewards for employees’ efforts results in increased role-based performance and productivity. For a better experience with employees’ role performance, maximizing the positive work-life, and creating an atmosphere where equity and fairness are seen as part of the organization becomes a requirement.
