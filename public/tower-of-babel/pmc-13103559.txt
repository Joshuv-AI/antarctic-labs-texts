Immunologic links between cutaneous lupus and internal organ involvement in SLE: A systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Dasarina Rizqi Amalia, RMohamad Javier, Laissa Bonita, Renny A Puspitsari, Kristian Kurniawan, Jeanette Sefanya Yefta, Brianka Yudha Nurpradika, Fauqi Amalia, Yessie Elin Santoso, Siska Suridanda Danny, BRM Ario Soeryo Kuncoro
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCutaneous lupus erythematosus (CLE) represents one of the most frequent clinical manifestations of systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), yet its relationship with internal organ involvement remains incompletely defined. Increasing evidence suggests that cutaneous inflammation may reflect systemic immune activation through dysregulated interferon signaling, immune complex deposition, and complement consumption. This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to determine the association between CLE and major organ involvement in patients with SLE.MethodsA comprehensive literature search was conducted in PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and Google Scholar for studies published between 2014 and 2024. Observational studies assessing organ involvement among SLE patients with and without cutaneous manifestations were included. Two reviewers independently performed study selection, data extraction, and quality appraisal using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale. A random-effects meta-analysis was conducted to estimate pooled odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) for renal, neuropsychiatric, hematologic, and cardiopulmonary involvement. Heterogeneity was quantified using I2, and publication bias evaluated with funnel plot and Egger's test.ResultsTwenty-three studies met inclusion criteria for qualitative synthesis, and sixteen were eligible for quantitative pooling, comprising 8412 patients. Meta-analysis demonstrated that CLE was significantly associated with increased risk of lupus nephritis (OR 2.14, 95% CI 1.62–2.85), neuropsychiatric lupus (OR 1.78, 95% CI 1.29–2.46), and hematologic abnormalities (OR 1.95, 95% CI 1.40–2.71). A modest but significant association with cardiopulmonary involvement was also observed (OR 1.52, 95% CI 1.11–2.09). Subgroup analyses revealed stronger associations in acute CLE phenotypes and in patients with high interferon signatures. Sensitivity analyses confirmed robustness of findings, with no evidence of major publication bias.ConclusionCLE is associated with significantly greater renal, neuropsychiatric, hematologic and cardiopulmonary involvement in patients with SLE compared with those without skin disease. These findings suggest that CLE is associated with increased systemic disease activity and a higher burden of internal organ involvement in SLE. CLE should therefore be considered an important clinical indicator of increased systemic risk in SLE.Keywords: Cutaneous lupus erythematosus, Systemic lupus erythematosus, Lupus nephritis, Neuropsychiatric lupus, Hematologic involvement, Meta-analysis, Autoimmune disease, Interferon pathway

Introduction

Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is a chronic, multisystem autoimmune disease characterized by a loss of immunologic self-tolerance, production of autoantibodies, formation of immune complexes, and subsequent inflammation and damage across multiple organs and tissues [1]. Skin and mucous membranes are among the most frequently affected organs in SLE, making cutaneous manifestations a significant part of the disease's clinical spectrum [2]. Indeed, dermatologic manifestations occur in about 70–85% of SLE patients at some point during the disease course [3,4].

Cutaneous lupus erythematosus (CLE) refers to skin-limited lupus (exclusive cutaneous disease) or skin involvement within systemic disease, and it is subclassified into three major subtypes based on clinical, histopathological, and immunologic features: acute CLE (ACLE), subacute CLE (SCLE), and chronic CLE (CCLE) [5,6]. Among these, chronic CLE — particularly discoid lupus erythematosus (DLE) — is the most common subtype [7,8]. The variety of cutaneous manifestations is broad, including malar (“butterfly”) rash, discoid lesions, photosensitivity, alopecia, mucosal ulcers, vasculitic lesions, and others [9,10].

Although CLE may remain restricted to the skin in many patients, for a subset it may represent an early or partial presentation of SLE [[5], [6], [7], [8], [9], [10], [11]]. Longitudinal data demonstrate that a proportion of individuals initially diagnosed with isolated CLE eventually develop full systemic disease [11,12]. According to a systematic review, the proportion of patients with CLE who later fulfill classification criteria for SLE in adult cohorts ranged from 0% to 42%, with significant variation across studies likely due to differences in follow-up duration, diagnostic criteria, and patient populations [11].

The immunopathogenesis underlying both cutaneous and systemic manifestations of lupus suggests potential mechanistic links between skin disease and internal organ involvement. Dysregulated activation of the type I interferon (IFN-I) pathway is now recognized as a central driver of autoimmunity in SLE [13]. In CLE, lesional and even non-lesional skin demonstrate upregulation of IFN-stimulated genes (ISGs), increased expression of IFN-κ by keratinocytes, and recruitment of plasmacytoid dendritic cells (pDCs), which produce IFN-α [13,14]. This “IFN-rich” environment in the skin may contribute not only to local tissue damage (rash, photosensitivity) but also to systemic immune activation, autoantibody production, and subsequent involvement of internal organs such as kidney (lupus nephritis), brain (neuropsychiatric lupus), or other target tissues [13,15].

Given this background, it is clinically and scientifically important to understand whether and how cutaneous manifestations — especially CLE subtypes — are associated with systemic organ involvement in SLE, and to delineate the immunologic pathways that may mediate this link. In this systematic review and meta-analysis, we aim to: (1) compare rates of internal organ involvement among SLE patients with and without cutaneous lupus; and (2) evaluate the association between cutaneous lupus and clinically relevant immunologic markers, such as autoantibody profiles and complement levels, reported in the included studies.

Search strategy and information sources

A systematic literature search was performed in four major electronic databases—PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Library—to identify relevant studies between 2014 and 2024. The search strategy combined Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms and free-text keywords related to CLE (e.g., “cutaneous lupus”, “CLE”, “discoid lupus”, “acute cutaneous lupus”, “subacute cutaneous lupus”), systemic lupus erythematosus, and systemic organ involvement (e.g., “lupus nephritis”, “neuropsychiatric lupus”, “cardiac involvement”, “hematologic lupus”, “visceral involvement”). Boolean operators (AND/OR) were applied to expand retrieval sensitivity, and additional filters were not applied to avoid missing potentially relevant studies. Reference lists of included articles were manually screened to identify further eligible studies. Only human studies with accessible full-text publications were considered. Two reviewers independently evaluated the relevance of each record. Any disagreement regarding eligibility was resolved through discussion until consensus was achieved. Interferon-related biomarkers were not used as primary search terms but were extracted when reported in the included studies.

Eligibility criteria

Studies were considered eligible if they investigated patients diagnosed with SLE according to the American College of Rheumatology (ACR), Systemic Lupus International Collaborating Clinics (SLICC), or EULAR classification criteria, and reported the presence or absence of cutaneous lupus involvement. Eligible studies were required to provide comparative data on internal organ manifestations between SLE patients with cutaneous involvement and those without. Internal organ involvement included renal disease (lupus nephritis), neuropsychiatric lupus, pulmonary or cardiac involvement, musculoskeletal manifestations, or hematologic abnormalities confirmed through clinical, serological, or imaging-based assessment. Observational studies, including prospective cohorts, retrospective cohorts, case-control studies, and analytical cross-sectional articles, were eligible for inclusion. Case reports, reviews, narrative papers, pediatric-only cohorts, editorials, animal studies, and studies lacking extractable data were excluded. Studies without a comparison group or without quantifiable outcome measures were also excluded.

Study selection process

All retrieved records were imported into a reference management system, and duplicates were removed electronically and manually. Titles and abstracts were screened independently by two reviewers to assess initial eligibility. Full texts were subsequently retrieved for potentially eligible studies and reviewed in detail. Reasons for exclusion were recorded systematically. A PRISMA-compliant study flowchart will be presented to illustrate the selection process, including total records identified, screened, assessed for eligibility, and included in qualitative and quantitative synthesis. Final consensus on included studies was reached through discussion with a third senior reviewer. The entire screening and selection process followed Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines to ensure methodological transparency and reproducibility.

Data extraction and management

Data extraction was performed using a standardized form pre-designed for this review. Extracted variables included study characteristics (first author, year, country, study design), patient demographics (sample size, age, sex distribution), diagnostic criteria for SLE and cutaneous lupus, disease duration, classification of CLE subtype (ACLE, SCLE, CCLE, DLE), type of internal organ involvement assessed, and corresponding clinical or serological outcomes. Numerical effect estimates including odds ratios, hazard ratios, risk ratios, and prevalence data were extracted along with confidence intervals. Laboratory markers such as anti-dsDNA titers, ANA profiles, complement levels (C3/C4), and interferon-related biomarkers were recorded when available. These variables were used to describe associations with systemic manifestations and were not intended to establish causal or mechanistic inferences regarding disease pathogenesis.

Risk-of-bias assessment

The methodological quality of included observational studies was appraised using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS), which evaluates selection of participants, comparability of exposure groups, and adequacy of outcome assessment. Each study was reviewed independently by two evaluators and assigned a score ranging from 0 to 9. Studies with scores of 7–9 were considered low risk of bias, 5–6 moderate, and below 5 high risk. Discrepancies in scoring were resolved through consensus discussion. Risk-of-bias assessment did not serve as an exclusion criterion, but scores were considered in sensitivity analyses to evaluate the robustness of pooled effect estimates.

Statistical analysis

Meta-analysis was conducted using a random-effects model (DerSimonian-Laird method), acknowledging heterogeneity in study design, patient populations, and assessment of organ involvement. Pooled odds ratios and 95% confidence intervals were calculated. When hazard ratios or risk ratios were reported, they were converted to odds ratios using established methods to maintain consistency across studies. Statistical heterogeneity was quantified using the I2 statistic, where values of 25%, 50%, and 75% indicated low, moderate, and high heterogeneity, respectively. A p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were conducted using Review Manager (RevMan 5.4) and R software (metafor package). The results are presented in forest plots to visualize the strength and direction of associations between cutaneous lupus and internal organ involvement.

Subgroup and sensitivity analyses

Pre-specified subgroup analyses were conducted based on CLE subtype (acute, subacute, chronic), type of organ involvement (renal, neuropsychiatric, hematologic, cardiopulmonary), and presence of serological biomarkers such as anti-dsDNA levels/positivity, ANA profiles, complement levels (C3/C4), and other reported immunologic markers. Sensitivity analyses were performed by sequentially removing individual studies to test the stability of pooled results. Additional analyses were conducted excluding studies with high risk of bias or those lacking multivariable adjustment. This approach enabled evaluation of whether findings remained consistent across study types and quality levels.

Publication bias

Publication bias was assessed visually using funnel plot symmetry and statistically through Egger's regression test. Potential asymmetry was interpreted with caution, particularly for outcomes synthesized from fewer than ten studies. If publication bias was suspected, trim-and-fill adjustment was planned to estimate corrected pooled effect sizes; however, reporting of adjusted models depended on outcome distribution and available sample size.

Results

Study selection

The initial search identified 2184 records across all databases, of which 1742 remained after duplicate removal. Title and abstract screening excluded 1498 studies due to irrelevance, non-comparative design, or lack of clinical organ-specific outcomes. A total of 244 full-text articles were reviewed in detail, and 23 studies met the eligibility criteria for qualitative synthesis. Among them, 13 studies provided extractable quantitative data suitable for meta-analysis. The studies included consisted of ten cohort studies, four case-control studies, and two analytical cross-sectional designs, representing a combined population of 8412 patients diagnosed with SLE. A PRISMA flow chart describing the selection process will be presented in the final manuscript.

Study characteristics

The included studies were published between 2014 and 2024, with geographic representation spanning North America, Europe, East Asia, and South America, reflecting diverse population backgrounds. Sample sizes ranged from 68 to 1240 participants. Diagnosis of SLE was based on ACR or SLICC criteria in all studies, while CLE diagnosis utilized clinical dermatologic evaluation and/or histopathological confirmation. Acute cutaneous lupus was reported in 11 studies, subacute CLE in 9 studies, chronic/discoid lupus in 14 studies, and nonspecific skin lesions in 7 studies. Internal organ involvement was assessed using renal biopsy, neuropsychiatric clinical evaluations, imaging, serological parameters, or hematologic indices depending on study design. Follow-up durations varied between six months and ten years in longitudinal studies, contributing to moderate clinical heterogeneity.

Meta-analysis of organ involvement

Association between cutaneous lupus and internal organ involvement

Quantitative synthesis demonstrated that the presence of cutaneous lupus was consistently associated with an increased likelihood of systemic organ involvement compared with SLE patients without skin disease. Across sixteen pooled studies, the direction of effect was uniformly positive, with individual study estimates clustering above the null value, supporting a robust association between cutaneous involvement and systemic disease burden (Fig. 1).

Forest plot of the association between cutaneous lupus and internal organ involvement in SLE.

LN emerged as the most frequently reported and most strongly associated outcome. Pooled analysis of twelve studies demonstrated that SLE patients with cutaneous lupus had more than two-fold increased odds of developing LN compared with those without cutaneous manifestations (pooled OR = 2.14; 95% CI 1.62–2.85; p < 0.001). Moderate heterogeneity was observed (I2 = 46%), likely reflecting variability in CLE subtype distribution and differences in LN ascertainment methods across cohorts. Notably, the majority of individual study estimates converged toward the pooled effect size, reinforcing the stability of the association.

Neuropsychiatric involvement, including seizures, psychosis, cognitive dysfunction, and cerebrovascular manifestations, was evaluated in ten studies and similarly demonstrated a significant association with cutaneous lupus. The pooled odds ratio indicated a 78% increased risk of neuropsychiatric SLE among patients with skin involvement (OR = 1.78; 95% CI 1.29–2.46; p = 0.002), with moderate heterogeneity (I2 = 39%). Hematologic abnormalities, encompassing leukopenia, anemia, and thrombocytopenia, were assessed in eight studies and were also significantly more common among patients with cutaneous disease (pooled OR = 1.95; 95% CI 1.40–2.71; p = 0.001), suggesting that cutaneous manifestations may reflect broader immune-mediated hematopoietic dysregulation.

Cardiac and pulmonary involvement was less consistently reported but remained significantly associated with cutaneous lupus. Meta-analysis of five studies demonstrated a modest but statistically significant association (pooled OR = 1.52; 95% CI 1.11–2.09; p = 0.014), with relatively low heterogeneity (I2 = 32%). Studies that specifically defined cardiopulmonary outcomes as serositis or pericardial effusion demonstrated stronger associations than those employing broader composite definitions. Collectively, these findings indicate that cutaneous lupus is not merely a localized manifestation but may serve as a clinically meaningful surrogate marker for heightened systemic organ involvement risk in SLE (Fig. 1).

Immunologic correlates of systemic organ involvement in cutaneous lupus

To elucidate potential mechanisms underlying the observed clinical associations, a complementary meta-analysis was performed examining immunologic features linked to systemic organ involvement among SLE patients with cutaneous disease. Across sixteen pooled studies, heightened immunologic activity was consistently associated with increased odds of internal organ involvement, with effect estimates demonstrating directional concordance and moderate between-study heterogeneity (Fig. 2).

Forest plot of immunologic correlates underlying systemic organ involvement in cutaneous lupus–associated SLE.

Markers reflecting active immune complex–mediated and interferon-driven inflammation demonstrated the strongest associations. High type I interferon gene-signature expression was associated with the greatest increase in systemic involvement risk, followed by low complement C3/C4 levels and elevated anti–double-stranded DNA titers. These immunologic features exhibited pooled effect sizes exceeding two-fold increased odds of organ involvement, supporting the hypothesis that interferon activation and complement consumption represent central mechanistic pathways linking cutaneous inflammation to systemic tissue injury.

Autoantibody profiles, including anti-Sm and anti-RNP positivity, were associated with more modest but consistently positive effect estimates, suggesting that broader autoantibody diversification contributes to systemic immune activation rather than serving as isolated drivers of organ damage. Additional markers of immune amplification, such as elevated circulating immune complexes and increased B-cell activating factor (BAFF) levels, further reinforced the concept that cutaneous lupus identifies an immunologically active disease phenotype predisposed to systemic involvement. Overall heterogeneity across immunologic analyses remained moderate (I2 ≈ 40%), with non-significant heterogeneity testing, indicating coherent mechanistic signals across diverse study designs (Fig. 2).

Integrated interpretation of clinical and immunologic findings

Taking together, the clinical and immunologic meta-analyses suggest that cutaneous lupus in SLE represents more than a superficial disease subset. Rather, cutaneous manifestations appear to delineate an immunologically active phenotype characterized by interferon pathway activation, complement consumption, and autoantibody-driven immune dysregulation, which collectively predispose patients to multisystem organ involvement. The concordance between clinical outcome associations (Fig. 1) and immunologic amplification patterns (Fig. 2) supports a unified skin–systemic disease axis, in which cutaneous inflammation serves as an accessible clinical marker of underlying systemic immune activity and organ damage risk.

Sensitivity analyses

Sequential omission of individual studies did not materially alter pooled effect sizes for renal, neuropsychiatric, or hematologic outcomes, confirming stability of findings. Excluding high-risk-of-bias studies led to minimal effect size attenuation, retaining statistical significance for all primary outcomes. Removing cross-sectional studies also did not change the directionality of associations, although confidence intervals widened slightly in neuropsychiatric outcomes, consistent with reduced sample volume. Model recalculations using fixed-effects methods yielded comparable results, indicating robustness independent of heterogeneity considerations.

Publication bias

Visual inspection of funnel plots showed symmetrical distribution for renal and hematologic outcomes, with mild asymmetry observed in neuropsychiatric outcomes, although Egger's test did not indicate significant publication bias (p = 0.27). For organ outcomes supported by fewer than 10 studies, funnel interpretation was inconclusive due to limited power. No trim-and-fill correction was required as pooled estimates remained stable across bias-adjusted sensitivity procedures.

Discussion

The present systematic review and meta-analysis demonstrates that cutaneous lupus is significantly associated with an increased likelihood of renal, neuropsychiatric, hematologic, and cardiopulmonary involvement in SLE. The pooled estimates across 8412 patients revealed that individuals with CLE had more than twice the risk of LN and nearly two-fold increased risk for neuropsychiatric and hematologic manifestations. These findings suggest that cutaneous disease is not merely a localized phenomenon but may reflect a higher systemic disease burden. Our conclusions are consistent with previous observational evidence suggesting that cutaneous lesions frequently coincide with serologic activity such as elevated anti-dsDNA titers, decreased complement levels, and interferon-related immune activation, which have been associated with increased risk of organ involvement in SLE [14].– [16].

LN represented the strongest association in our analysis, aligning with existing models that describe shared immunologic features between cutaneous and renal involvement in lupus. Both organs are known to be affected in states of heightened immune activity, including immune complex deposition and complement activation. In CLE, ultraviolet radiation–induced keratinocyte apoptosis has been reported to release nucleic acids that stimulate TLR7/9-dependent interferon pathways and promote autoantibody production [13]. These processes have been associated with immune complex formation and glomerular deposition observed in LN, contributing to mesangial proliferation and complement-mediated injury. The higher risk observed in ACLE subgroups further supports the concept that more inflammatory cutaneous phenotypes are associated with greater systemic disease activity, whereas CCLE (particularly discoid lupus) tends to remain more skin-limited [4], [8], [11].

Across pooled analyses, patients with CLE showed more than twice the risk of developing LN compared with those without CLE (OR 2.14). This association suggests that cutaneous inflammation may reflect underlying systemic immune activation linked to renal involvement. Features such as increased keratinocyte apoptosis, enhanced type I interferon activity, and circulating immune complexes—commonly described in CLE—have been associated with renal pathology in prior studies. The stronger association observed in acute CLE compared to chronic CLE further supports the concept that more inflammatory skin phenotypes parallel more active systemic disease [4], [8], [11].

Our findings regarding neuropsychiatric systemic lupus erythematosus (NPSLE) also merit emphasis. The observed association between CLE and NPSLE suggests that cutaneous manifestations may reflect broader systemic immune dysregulation. Previous studies have reported that interferon-related pathways are associated with endothelial activation and blood–brain barrier dysfunction, which have been implicated in neuropsychiatric manifestations of SLE [15], [17]. In this context, the higher frequency of NPSLE among patients with CLE may indicate shared inflammatory pathways rather than a direct causal relationship. Furthermore, interferon gene-signature intensity has been associated with neuropsychiatric disease activity and imaging abnormalities, providing additional context for the observed association [16].

Patients with CLE were significantly more likely to develop NPSLE compared with those without CLE (OR 1.78). This finding supports the possibility of overlapping inflammatory mechanisms, including interferon signaling, endothelial activation, and blood–brain barrier dysfunction, which have been described in prior studies. The lower frequency of neuropsychiatric involvement among patients without cutaneous manifestations further supports the role of CLE as a potential clinical indicator of increased systemic disease burden.

Hematologic abnormalities—including leukopenia, thrombocytopenia, and hemolytic anemia—were also significantly more frequent among patients with cutaneous involvement. These findings may reflect a broader pattern of immune activation, as cytopenias in SLE have consistently been associated with serologic activity and immune-mediated processes. Previous studies have linked these abnormalities to increased autoantibody production and dysregulated immune responses, which are also commonly observed in patients with active cutaneous disease [14].

Another important finding is that hematologic abnormalities occurred nearly twice as often in patients with CLE compared to those without CLE (OR 1.95). This difference suggests that patients with cutaneous involvement may exhibit a higher degree of systemic immune activation, as reflected by increased autoantibody production and immune-mediated hematologic involvement reported in prior studies.

Although CLE has traditionally been regarded as an external manifestation, our findings support a more integrated perspective in which skin involvement may serve as a clinical indicator of increased systemic disease activity. Clinically, this implies that dermatologic evaluation may provide important prognostic information and should be considered as part of comprehensive disease assessment. Recognition of cutaneous flares may facilitate earlier identification of systemic involvement, particularly in patients with concurrent serologic abnormalities such as elevated anti-dsDNA levels or reduced complement levels.

This meta-analysis has several strengths, including a comprehensive search strategy across multiple databases, adherence to PRISMA methodology, rigorous study selection and risk-of-bias assessment, and a relatively large pooled sample size. The consistency of associations across sensitivity analyses further supports the robustness of the findings. In addition, subgroup analyses based on CLE subtypes and immunologic markers provide additional clinical insight, suggesting that systemic risk may vary across different cutaneous phenotypes.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, most included studies were observational in design, which limits causal inference despite consistent associations. Second, heterogeneity in diagnostic criteria and outcome definitions may have contributed to variability across studies. Third, inconsistent reporting of CLE subtypes may have limited subgroup analyses. Fourth, limited longitudinal data restrict the ability to assess temporal relationships between cutaneous and systemic manifestations. Biomarker data—particularly interferon-related markers and complement levels—were not uniformly reported, limiting further analysis. Finally, although publication bias was not statistically significant, outcomes based on a small number of studies should be interpreted with caution.

Future research should focus on well-designed prospective studies with standardized classification of cutaneous subtypes, consistent reporting of immunologic markers, and long-term follow-up. Such approaches may help clarify the extent to which cutaneous manifestations can serve as indicators of systemic disease burden and improve risk stratification in clinical practice.

In summary, this meta-analysis demonstrates that cutaneous lupus is associated with a higher likelihood of multisystem involvement in SLE, particularly affecting renal, neuropsychiatric, and hematologic domains. These findings support the role of cutaneous manifestations as a clinically relevant indicator of increased systemic disease burden.

Conclusion

This systematic review and meta-analysis shows that SLE patients with CLE have a significantly higher likelihood of developing renal, neuropsychiatric, hematologic and cardiopulmonary involvement compared with those without skin manifestations, fulfilling the objective of comparing organ involvement between the two groups. The findings also demonstrate that cutaneous disease reflects heightened systemic immune activation characterized by increased type I interferon signaling, autoantibody production, complement consumption and immune complex formation, which together help explain the immunologic mechanisms linking skin inflammation to internal organ damage. Overall, CLE should be recognized as an important clinical marker of greater systemic risk in SLE, emphasizing the need for close monitoring of patients who present with cutaneous manifestations.
