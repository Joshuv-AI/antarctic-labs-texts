Monte Carlo-based subcellular comparison of electron energy deposition by 177Lu and 161 Tb: implications for targeted radiopharmaceutical therapy
Authors: Alexandre França Velo, Lukas M. Carter, John L. Humm
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeThis study compares the subcellular dosimetry of 161 Tb and 177Lu, focusing on β⁻ particles, conversion electrons, and Auger electrons, and their relative contributions to cellular and subcellular damage. We aim to evaluate whether the higher emission yields of 161 Tb provide a therapeutic advantage, particularly for non-internalizing targeting agents in radiopharmaceutical therapy.MethodsA stochastic radionuclide decay model was implemented in MATLAB, incorporating internal conversion and Auger cascades and validated against ICRP 107. Geant4 track code simulations modeled electron transport in single-cell and voxelized membrane geometries. Energy deposition was assessed in the membrane, cytoplasm, and nucleus for 10,000 decays of each radionuclide.Results161 Tb achieved similar nuclear energy deposition as 177Lu with about 25% of the decays, due to its higher yield of internal conversion. These conversion electrons contribute to nuclear damage playing a crucial role in cell damage. Auger electrons from 161 Tb additionally produced highly localized energy deposition at the cell membrane, that could also contribute to cell death. However, when normalizing for equivalent radiotoxicity to the bone marrow, around 75% of the 161 Tb decays provide a similar marrow absorbed dose as 177Lu, while still increasing the absorbed dose to the nucleus by approximately 18%.Conclusion161 Tb offers a more efficient subcellular energy deposition profile than 177Lu. It enables either similar therapeutic effect with fewer decays or enhanced nuclear absorbed dose under equivalent bone marrow toxicity. These results support the use of 161 Tb in targeted radiopharmaceutical therapy, particularly for isolated tumor cells and micrometastases.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40658-025-00810-y.

Introduction

Radiopharmaceutical therapies increasingly leverage the unique emission characteristics of radionuclides to improve therapeutic efficacy and minimize toxicity. A critical consideration for these therapies is that radiopharmaceutical uptake at the cellular level may be highly non-uniform, and driven by differences in radionuclide emission spectra, the energy deposition patterns in different cellular components may likewise be profoundly non-uniform – ultimately translating into variations in antitumor effect and/or toxicity. The focus of this paper is to provide a detailed description of the energy deposition properties of two radionuclides; the first lutetium-177 (177Lu), that has been widely adopted in current clinical radionuclide therapies, and terbium-161 (161 Tb), a strong potential competitor to 177Lu, to facilitate an understanding into their differential radiobiological effectiveness.

177Lu is the radionuclide in the two approved tumor targeting theranostic agents, Lutathera™ [1] and Pluvicto™ [2], used to treat neuroendocrine and prostate cancers, respectively. An attractive characteristic underlying the incorporation of 177Lu into approved radiopharmaceutical therapy agents, as well as numerous candidates in the translational pipeline, is its nearly ideal combination of properties: (1) its predominant emissions are low energy \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(E\beta_{Average}^{ - } = 134 keV)$$\end{document} beta particles that deposit their energy close to the decay site resulting in highly favorable dosimetry [3–5], (2) it emits a low 6.23% yield of 113 keV and 10.4% yield of 208 keV [3–5] gamma rays, providing a mechanism with which to quantify targeting and normal organ risk, through imaging with a gamma camera at high activities, e.g. 7.4 GBq, without being a major external exposure concern to hospital staff and patient family members, (3) the half-life of 177Lu is 6.7 days [3], allowing favorable differential kinetics between tumor targeting and normal organ clearance upon administration, but also providing a practical convenience for distribution to treatment facilities that are not close to the pharmaceutical manufacturer site, and (4) straightforward radiochemistry; lutetium can be stably linked to tumor targeting agents using the widely available chelating agent DOTA [6, 7]. Commercial production of 177Lu is well established, with both high- or low-specific activity forms produced at scale using nuclear reactors.

161 Tb has emerged as an attractive competitor to 177Lu, spurred in part by recent improvements in commercial supply and production scalability for more widespread clinical use; high specific-activity 161 Tb, reactor-produced via the 160Gd \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\to ^{n,\gamma }$$\end{document} 161Gd \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\to ^{\beta }$$\end{document} 161 Tb route, is available commercially through, e.g., Isotopia (Israel) or TerThera (The Netherlands), and cyclotron production is also possible. Compared with 177Lu, the decay kinetics and beta/photon emissions of 161 Tb are remarkably similar [8, 9]. The beta emission energy is only slightly higher than that of 177Lu (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$E\beta_{Average}^{ - }$$\end{document} = 154 keV), and the half-life 6.95 days almost the same with similar chelation chemistry. The photon emissions of 25.7 keV (I = 23.2%), 48.9 keV (I = 17.0%), and 74.6 keV (I = 10.2%) are less optimal but adequate for quantitative patient imaging [3]. Both radionuclides emit internal conversion electrons that initiate Auger electron cascades, but the yield of each of these pathways is substantially higher in the case of 161 Tb, resulting in the conjecture that the radiobiological efficacy of a 161 Tb labeled tumor targeting agent would be of greater radiobiological effectiveness than the same agent labeled with the same number of 177Lu atoms. Because the two approved 177Lu-labeled peptides (DOTATATE and PSMA-617) bind to cell surface targets (somatostatin and prostate specific membrane antigen, respectively) [10–13], we calculated the energy deposition to both the cell membrane, as a potential target of radiogenic cell inactivation, and to the cell nucleus, containing the principal target for cell inactivation, the DNA. In the comparison between the pattern of energy deposition from 177Lu and 161 Tb, we looked at and compared the relative contribution of the three major particulate emissions: beta particles, internal conversion electrons and Auger electrons.

An early biokinetic analysis using human data for somatostatin targeting peptides radiolabeled with several different radionuclides, including: 64Cu, 67 Ga, 90Y, 111In, 131I, 188Re, 177Lu and 161 Tb, based upon tumor: normal tissue absorbed dose ratios suggested that the dosimetrically most favorable radionuclide for this application is 161 Tb [14]. More recently, dosimetric studies by Spoormans et al. (2024) [15] calculated a 3.6 fold higher dose to the nucleus from 161 Tb-DOTATATE versus 177Lu-DOTATATE, reporting that the increased nucleus-absorbed dose was mainly due to internal conversion electrons and not Auger electrons by 161 Tb, and therefore postulated that this accounted for the higher radiobiological effectiveness of 161 Tb relative to 177Lu on a per-decay basis. Auger electrons possess extremely short path lengths, with most depositing energy over nanometer distances. When emitted near DNA, they induce highly localized and lethal damage [16]. Yet, the precise therapeutic implications of their emission from non-nuclear compartments, particularly the cell membrane, remain incompletely understood. Given the stochastic nature of cellular uptake and subcellular localization, understanding how energy is deposited across cellular compartments is critical for evaluating radiobiological effectiveness. However, there is some evidence that Auger electron cascades resulting from membrane targeting could produce lipid-raft-mediated cell death in contrast to the widely held hypothesis that radionuclides such as 125I are only radiobiological effective when targeting the DNA [17]. In a paper by Pouget et al. (2022) [18] Pouget hypothesizes “that the dose rate is the key determinant in the balance between the physical and DNA-centered response on one side, and the biological response that integrates all subcellular compartments and intercellular signaling pathways on the other side”. Therefore, consideration of the local energy deposition density within the cell membrane and to DNA within the cell nucleus will be important in efforts to understand the differences in radiobiological effectiveness between different radionuclides with complex emission properties.

Given that most clinical 177Lu-labeled agents (e.g., DOTATATE, PSMA-617) bind to cell surface receptors, the potential for 161 Tb-labeled analogs to exploit the effects of Auger and conversion electrons may be substantial. A phase I/II clinical trial is currently underway to evaluate the safety and efficacy of 161 Tb-PSMA-I&T in patients with metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer [19]. Whether 161 Tb will demonstrate superior therapeutic outcomes remains to be seen, but mechanistic insights from subcellular dosimetry are essential to guide its optimal use.

Materials and methods

A comparative dosimetric analysis was conducted to estimate the local energy deposition induced by particle emissions from 161 Tb and 177Lu including \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\upbeta }^{ - }$$\end{document} particles, Auger electrons, and conversion electrons. Stochastic Auger and conversion electrons for both radionuclides were generated using the stochastic decay model described in Velo et al. (2025a) [20] and in Velo et al. (2025b) [21]. MATLAB® code was created to simulate the decay stochastics employing the evaluated atomic data library (EADL) [22] for radiative and non-radiative transitions following vacancy creation. For the two radionuclides considered in this paper, Auger electrons come from vacancies induced by internal conversion [23]. The spectra were generated considering condensed phase [20]. Auger and conversion electron yields were compared against the reference data provided by ICRP Publication 107 [3]. By distinguishing Auger electrons from conversion electrons, the model enabled a detailed assessment of their respective potential contributions to cellular damage. The full \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta^{ - }$$\end{document} spectra for each radionuclide were obtained using Geant4 decay tool kit [24, 25].

The cell model and dimensions used in this study were based on the model proposed by Larouze et al. (2023) [26], consisting of a 14 µm diameter spherical cell with a 10 nm thick membrane and a concentric nucleus of 10 µm in diameter, as illustrated in Fig. 2. For the purposes of defining extracellular space, a cubic region was constructed around the cell, with side lengths set equal to the outer diameter of the cell. To enable a more detailed analysis of energy deposition within the cell membrane, a potential target for cell death from membrane binding theranostic agents, a 100 × 100 voxelized grid representing a portion of the cell membrane was designed. Each voxel measured 10 nm × 10 nm × 10 nm, resulting in a total grid dimension of 1000 × 1000 × 10 nm3.Fig. 2Schematic representation of the cell model and membrane surface geometry used in the Monte Carlo simulations

Schematic representation of the cell model and membrane surface geometry used in the Monte Carlo simulations

Three sets of simulations were conducted in this study: (1) simulations evaluating the mean energy deposition and mean absorbed dose per nuclear transformation (i.e., “S-value”) in the nucleus, cytoplasm, and membrane with the source located at the outer surface of the cell membrane, (2) simulations focused on quantifying the extent of the damage to the membrane specifically from energy deposition by Auger and conversion electrons, and (3) simulations evaluating the mean absorbed dose per nuclear transformation in the nucleus, cytoplasm, and membrane with the sources located in the extracellular space (ES).

The simulations were performed using the Geant4 radiation transport toolkit (version 11.2.0), modeling all cellular components as liquid water to comply with the assumptions of Geant4-DNA physics models [27–30]. Electron transport was handled using the G4EmDNAPhysics_option2 constructor” [28, 31, 32], which includes discrete interaction models suitable for low-energy track-structure simulations. In this physics list, ionization and electronic excitation are modeled using the Emfietzoglou dielectric formalism for energies between 9 eV and 1 MeV, while elastic scattering is described using the partial wave model for electrons from 7.4 eV to 1 MeV. Additional inelastic sub-excitation processes such as vibrational excitation (Sanche data, 2 eV–100 eV) and attachment (Melton data, 4–13 eV) are also incorporated, and Auger electron emission is modeled using the EADL database in conjunction with the Geant4 atomic relaxation interface. The default tracking cutoff for electrons is 7.4 eV, below which residual energy is assumed to be locally deposited [28]. Each simulation involved 10,000 beta decays per radionuclide, with all primary β⁻ particles, Auger electrons, and conversion electrons tracked individually down to the 7.4 eV cutoff. Event-by-event energy deposition in the membrane, cytoplasm, and nucleus was recorded to calculate the mean absorbed dose in each compartment. These absorbed doses were then normalized by the number of disintegrations to obtain cellular S-values in units of Gy/Bq·s, following the MIRD formalism [33]. For 177Lu, S-values obtained via our stochastic decay simulation were benchmarked against values from MIRDcell [34] as a validation step. Notably, at the time of writing, no 161 Tb cellular S-values were available in MIRDcell for direct comparison.

This dual mechanism is particularly advantageous for targeted radiopharmaceutical therapy and could explain the higher observed radiotoxicity of 161 Tb over 177Lu reported in the literature [4, 5, 26, 35, 36]. This is evident from the energy deposition patterns within the sub-cellular compartments depicted in Fig. 4. Figure 4b shows the energy deposit attributed to Auger electrons. For both radionuclides, the majority of deposited energy is concentrated in the cell membrane rather than the nucleus. Auger electrons generally possess extremely short ranges, on the order of nanometers, leading to highly localized energy deposition. Although their energy is relatively low, the concentration of energy deposition from multiple electrons generates a high linear energy transfer (LET) in the vicinity for the decay site making them particularly potent in inducing radiobiological damage. Figure 4c displays the relative magnitude of the energy deposition from the conversion electrons by the two radionuclides. These electrons have higher energies and longer ranges than Auger electrons that are sufficient to reach the DNA sites from emissions emanating from the cell membrane. Their ability to reach and selectively deposit dose to the nucleus, especially in the context of radiopharmaceuticals that may internalize or accumulate near the nuclear envelope, further enhances their therapeutic efficacy. Although the pattern of damage was similar for both radionuclides, the combination of high-yield Auger and conversion electron emissions from 161 Tb results in higher energy deposition within the expected targets (the nuclear DNA and the cell membrane) of radiotoxicity. This dual mechanism of radiobiological effect may be potentially advantageous for 161 Tb as a targeted radionuclide. Despite the high localized energy deposition, the biological consequences of this damage remain an area of active investigation. Across all emission types, β⁻ particles, conversion electrons, and Auger electrons, the absorbed dose to all subcellular compartments (membrane, cytoplasm, nucleus) is markedly reduced when the sources are located in the extracellular space compared to when it is bound to the cell surface. This reduction is most pronounced for Auger electrons, which contribute negligibly to absorbed dose even in the membrane, due to their extremely short-range emissions. β⁻ particles and conversion electrons from 161 Tb still deposit dose in all compartments when emitted extracellularly, though at significantly reduced levels (approximately 3–4 times lower). Importantly, the β⁻ contributions are essentially equivalent for 161 Tb and 177Lu, while Auger electron contributions are minimal in the extracellular context. Thus, the majority of the differential absorbed dose observed between the two isotopes arises from the contribution of conversion electrons, which are more abundant in 161 Tb. Discussion

The comparative analysis demonstrated strong agreement between the stochastic decay simulations and the reference conversion and Auger electron yields reported in ICRP Publication 107, validating the accuracy of our model. The implications of the higher Auger electron emission yield from 161 Tb compared to 177Lu are explored further below. This difference stems from the significantly higher internal conversion yield of 161 Tb, averaging 1.4 conversion electrons per decay, which enhances the frequency of subsequent Auger cascades. In contrast, 177Lu, despite its higher atomic number, exhibits a much lower internal conversion yield and consequently emits fewer Auger electrons per decay. An important detail regarding the conversion electrons of 161 Tb is that their emission energies overlap with the K-shell Auger electrons, because three primary nuclear transitions following β⁻ decay, at 25.65 keV (23.2%), 48.91 keV (17.0%), and 74.56 keV (10.2%) [3], fall below the K-shell binding energy. As a consequence, most of conversion electrons originate from the L-shell or beyond.

The radiation dose contribution from the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta^{ - }$$\end{document} particles is comparable for both radionuclides, as illustrated in Fig. 4a. Consequently, the differences in biological effectiveness between 161 Tb and 177Lu are attributed to their respective emissions of Auger and conversion electrons. A distinct pattern of damage is evident in Fig. 4b and 4c where the local energy deposition to the cell membrane and therefore radiobiological effects is predominantly attributed to Auger electrons, whereas damage to nuclear DNA is most likely associated with conversion electrons.

The cell nucleus, and specifically nuclear DNA, has traditionally been considered the primary target of radiation-induced cell damage. However, the extremely thin volume of cell membrane can lead to a high focal absorbed dose caused by the Auger electrons emitted by radioactive source decays on the cell membrane. The significance of high focal energy deposits in the membrane resulting from Auger electrons is not well known. That raises a question whether this highly focal energy deposition can contribute to cell death induction? On one hand, localized membrane disruption may trigger cell death especially if multiple radioligands are bound in proximity. On the other hand, if only isolated sites on the membrane are damaged without broader involvement, the therapeutic impact may be limited. In clinical scenarios, the use of radioligands that bind densely across the cell membrane could amplify these localized effects, leading to cumulative or synergistic damage. In contrast, conversion electrons demonstrated a more efficient ability to deliver damage to the cell nucleus. Their longer range and higher energy enable them to penetrate into the nuclear region. As the number of radioligands bound to the cell increases, this nuclear targeting becomes more pronounced, enhancing the probability of inducing DNA double-strand breaks, a key mechanism of radiotoxicity in targeted radiopharmaceutical therapy. This interpretation aligns with the findings of Spoormans et al. (2024) [15], who reported a 3.6-fold higher dose to the nucleus from 161 Tb-DOTATATE compared to 1⁷⁷Lu-DOTATATE. Their analysis attributed this increase primarily to internal conversion electrons rather than Auger electrons, supporting the notion that conversion electrons play a dominant role in nuclear dose delivery and radiotoxicity.

These results suggest that the therapeutic advantage of 161 Tb over 177Lu may be twofold: (i) the higher number of Auger electrons allowing for enhanced membrane damage, and (ii) the elevated conversion electron yield providing increased cell nuclear damage, and hence DNA energy deposition.

The total absorbed dose into each cellular compartment, per nuclear transformation, confirm that 161 Tb delivers substantially more cytotoxic potential than 177Lu. Importantly, the therapeutic implication of this increased absorbed dose becomes evident when comparing the efficiency of both radionuclides: to achieve the same level of total absorbed dose induced by a given number of decays of 177Lu, approximately 25% of those decays of 161 Tb are required, revealing a striking 1:4 decay efficiency ratio considering the case of a single isolated cell, eliminating the contribution of beta crossfire from neighboring cells. These results suggests that 161 Tb can deliver comparable or greater cytotoxicity with significantly fewer decay events. Figure 5e further supports this conclusion by depicting the damage patterns resulting from 2,500 decays of 161 Tb alongside 10,000 decays of 177Lu. The superior damage induced by 161 Tb, even with fewer decay events, underscores its enhanced therapeutic potential for targeted radiopharmaceutical therapy.

The simulations where decays occur in the extracellular space revealed a marked reduction in absorbed dose across all subcellular compartments compared to the case where the radioligand is bound to the cell membrane. This effect was especially pronounced for Auger electrons, which are ineffective when emitted outside the cell due to their ultra-short range. Conversion electrons still delivered measurable dose due to their greater penetration.

To illustrate the therapeutic implications of the increased electron emission yield of 161 Tb, consider a hypothetical scenario in which a 177Lu-labeled agent delivers a tumor dose of 50 Gy through specific binding to cell surfaces, with dose-limiting toxicity occurring in the bone marrow due to non-specific retention in the extracellular space, resulting in a 2.0 Gy dose to marrow cell nuclei. Based on our calculated S-values, switching to 161 Tb would increase the dose to a representative tumor cell nucleus to 80 Gy (i.e., 50 Gy × 5.22E-04 / 3.28E-04), and increase the dose to a marrow cell nucleus to 2.7 Gy (i.e., 2.0 Gy × 1.13E-04 / 8.40E-05). To keep the marrow toxicity equivalent to that of 177Lu (i.e., capped at 2.0 Gy), the administered activity of 161 Tb would need to be reduced to 74% (2.0 Gy / 2.7 Gy) of that of 177Lu. Even with this reduction, the tumor nucleus dose would still increase to approximately 59 Gy, a 18% gain in therapeutic index under the same toxicity constraint. This modeling supports the idea that 161 Tb could provide improved tumor control in scenarios where the dose-limiting organ does not express the molecular target but instead accumulates the radiopharmaceutical through non-specific mechanisms. Such cases include treatments involving monoclonal antibodies or small peptides, where bone marrow or kidneys often represent the primary dose-limiting tissues.

Conclusion

This study highlights the distinct radiobiological profiles of 161 Tb and 177Lu at the cellular level. While both radionuclides exhibit comparable \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta^{ - }$$\end{document} particle-induced damage, their divergent yields of Auger and conversion electrons result in significantly different patterns and magnitudes of subcellular energy deposition. 161 Tb, with its significantly higher emission of Auger and conversion electrons, demonstrates a dual mechanism of action: localized membrane damage driven by Auger electrons and effective nuclear targeting via conversion electrons. Although Auger electron-induced membrane damage is spatially confined, its impact may be amplified in clinical settings by the dense surface binding of radioligands, increasing the probability of cumulative biological effects. Moreover, the efficient nuclear energy delivery by conversion electrons enhances the likelihood of critical DNA damage, such as double-strand breaks.

The quantitative analysis of 161 Tb reveals that comparable total energy deposition can be achieved in the cell nucleus with just one-quarter the number of decay events relative to 177Lu, underscoring a potential fourfold increase in therapeutic efficiency, when balanced with the appropriate consideration of the unbound 161 Tb contribution to the dose limiting bone marrow.
