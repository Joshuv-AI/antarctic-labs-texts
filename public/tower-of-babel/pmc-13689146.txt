Healthcare workers’ perceptions of the potential value of whole-genome sequencing for post-exposure management of rifampicin-resistant tuberculosis: a qualitative study
Authors: Caitlin D October, Busisiwe Beko, Anja Reuter, Vincent Rennie, Nabila Ismail, Emilyn Costa Conceição, Susanne Tonsing, Rob Warren, Stephanus Malherbe, Annelies Van Rie, Lario Viljoen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRifampicin-resistant tuberculosis (RR-TB) remains a public health challenge, with suboptimal treatment outcomes and challenging management of contacts. Whole genome sequencing (WGS) generates a comprehensive drug resistance profile and transmission inference with a single assay. Limited evidence exists on how healthcare workers (HCWs) perceive the value of WGS, especially for post-exposure management.MethodsWe conducted a qualitative study with 22 purposively sampled HCWs (9 doctors, 9 nurses, 4 community health workers) at 11 public health care facilities in the Western Cape, South Africa. Following a standardized explanation of WGS and WGS-guided post-exposure management, semi-structured interviews were conducted to elicit responses on anticipated acceptability and value of WGS for post-exposure management. Interviews were audio-recorded, transcribed, and analysed using thematic analysis, combining inductive with deductive approaches informed by the Theoretical Framework of Acceptability themes of intervention coherence, self-efficacy, affective attitude, ethicality, perceived effectiveness and opportunity costs.ResultsHCWs demonstrated good anticipated intervention coherence, even though nurses and community health workers required more explanation on WGS than doctors, and pathways for WGS integration into routine care were not clear. Most HCWs indicated high confidence in anticipated application of WGS-supported recommendations. Limited familiarity with genomic tools was identified as a barrier that requires formal training. Affective attitudes were largely positive, with HCWs expressing optimism about improved clinical decision-making and reduced diagnostic uncertainty following access to WGS. Anticipated effectiveness was high, with HCWs hopeful of faster initiation of appropriate regimens for patients and contacts, reduced patient distress, and decreased medication wastage. Less applicable to post-exposure management was the anticipated benefit that WGS could reduce the burden to the health system and patients by limiting repeat visits and reducing laboratory delays. Ethicality emerged as an important advantage, with HCWs anticipating reduced stigma when contact screening is guided by the detection of hotspots. HCWs did not anticipate opportunity costs.ConclusionHCWs in high-burden settings generally anticipated that WGS would be acceptable to inform post-exposure management strategies. Successful integration of WGS into routine TB care will require tailored training and clear implementation guidance on how to interpret and apply WGS results in clinical and public health practice.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12913-026-15140-7.Keywords: Whole genome sequencing, Rifampicin-resistant tuberculosis, Tuberculosis preventive treatment, Contact tracing, Healthcare worker perspectives, Acceptability

Introduction

Multidrug-resistant and rifampicin-resistant tuberculosis (MDR/RR-TB) affects approximately 390,000 people annually and poses an important challenge to healthcare systems as it is more difficult and costly to treat compared to drug-susceptible TB (DS-TB) [1]. In 2024, the global treatment success rate for MDR/RR-TB was only 71%, compared to the 88% for DS-TB [2]. While the availability of newer all-oral short‑course regimens for MDR/RR-TB, such as the BPaLM regimen (bedaquiline, pretomanid, linezolid and moxifloxacin), reduces treatment duration, toxicity and patient costs, a diagnosis of MDR/RR‑TB still places a substantial burden on patients and their families [3, 4]. For example, the median cost to patients and their family of a course of MDR/RR-TB treatment in South Africa was estimated to be 486 US dollars [5].

Post-exposure management of TB is an essential component of TB control. It involves screening close contacts of individuals diagnosed with TB and administration of TB preventive therapy (TPT) after active disease has been ruled out [6]. Post-exposure management has been documented as difficult to implement, especially in low- and middle-income countries [7]. Key challenges include low referral rates, poor rates of initiation and completion of TPT, limited knowledge among healthcare workers (HCWs), and disparities in the health services delivery [8–13].

For 60 years, isoniazid was predominantly used for TPT. Over the past decades, several new TPT regimens using different combinations isoniazid, rifapentine, and rifampicin for a duration of 1 to 6 months duration have been endorsed by the World Health Organization (WHO) for prevention of DS-TB, using regimen. Options for TPT for MDR/RR-TB remain limited. In 2024, two randomized controlled trials (TB CHAMP and V-QUIN) demonstrated efficacy and safety of 6-months daily levofloxacin for contacts of patients with MDR/RR-TB in South Africa [14] and Vietnam [15]. To date, no trial has assessed the effectiveness of TPT regimens for contacts of index cases with (pre)-extensively drug-resistant TB (pre-XDR TB), where the Mycobacterium tuberculosis (Mtb) strain is resistant to both isoniazid and levofloxacin.

Selection of the optimal TPT regimen for contacts of patients with pre-XDR or XDR-TB would require comprehensive knowledge of the drug resistance profile of the index case. Next-generation sequencing (NGS), which includes whole genome sequencing (WGS) and targeted NGS (tNGS), is a novel technology that can accurately provide a comprehensive drug resistance profile with a single assay [16]. NGS results can be used to guide the optimal treatment regimen for a patient diagnosed with RR-TB, as well as guide HCWs in the selection of the optimal TPT regimen for contacts of persons with RR-TB [17]. In contrast to tNGS, WGS also provides information on strain relatedness, which can assist HCWs in identifying cases of active transmission and geographic hotspots of drug-resistant TB transmission. Even though tNGS was endorsed by the WHO in 2024 [18]programmatic uptake has been slow in high burden countries. In 2026, South Africa, a country with a high burden of MDR/RR-TB [19], was one of the first countries to integrate tNGS into the routine diagnostic workflows for MDR/RR-TB [20]. Because only tNGS has been rolled out in South Africa, little is known about the acceptability of WGS among HCWs.

The theoretical framework of acceptability (TFA) has been used across settings to assess the acceptability of various healthcare interventions [21–24]. The TFA consists of seven components through which acceptability can be assessed prior to, during, or post intervention delivery [25]. It includes affective attitude (how an individual feels about an intervention), burden (perceived amount of effort that is required to participate), ethicality (extent to which an intervention fits with an individual’s values), intervention coherence (extent of understanding of the intervention), opportunity costs (extent to which benefits or profits must be given up to engage with the intervention), perceived effectiveness (extent to which intervention will serve its purpose) and self-efficacy (individual’s confidence in their ability to participate in the intervention). Through these seven components, the TFA can combine individual and interpersonal perceptions to provide a structured approach to the study of acceptability of novel health care interventions.

In this qualitative study, we explore HCWs’ anticipated acceptability of WGS for post-exposure management of MDR/RR-TB in the South African context and use the TFA to improve our understanding of the acceptability of WGS in this context.

Methods

Design

We conducted an exploratory qualitative study between June and October 2025 using semi-structured interviews to examine HCW perspectives and anticipated experiences related to the integration of WGS into post-exposure management of RR-TB. This study is nested in the GenPath Precision Action for RR-TB (PARR-TB) project which aims to evaluate the routine use of WGS for the surveillance, treatment and prevention of RR-TB.

Setting

Data were collected at 11 public primary health care facilities, including a TB hospital in the Western Cape province of South Africa. These facilities predominantly serve communities characterized by poor socio-economic living conditions, high population density and overcrowding [26]. The Western Cape province has a high burden of RR-TB, with 1993 cases diagnosed and reported in 2025 [27]. RR-TB care is provided decentralized, with patients receiving care by doctors and nurses at primary care facilities, except for highly challenging cases or severely ill persons which are managed at a central TB hospital. Community health workers perform household visits during which they perform TB screening, basic health promotion and disease prevention [28].

Sampling, recruitment and data collection

At each of the participating facilities, we purposefully selected doctors, nurses, and community health workers with at least one year of experience providing RR-TB care to represent the range of HCWs that play a role in RR-TB care. We developed a semi-structured interview guide to elicit responses based on HCWs’ acceptability of WGS for post-exposure management using the TFA framework. A separate interview guide was developed specifically for community health workers with lay language and task-specific prompts. As WGS has not been introduced into routine care in South Africa, we first explained WGS and the concept of WGS for post-exposure management using visual aids and standardised language (Appendix A) before we asked questions regarding its acceptability and feasibility. Interview guides were constructed to avoid leading respondents to a positive outlook about WGS. Interviews lasted about 45 min, were conducted in private rooms at the health care facilities, and were audio recorded. Data were collected either in English or in local languages (Afrikaans or Xhosa) by trained researchers. Interviews conducted in English were transcribed verbatim using Trint’s transcription software [29]. Interviews conducted in Afrikaans or Xhosa were translated and transcribed by the researchers conducting the interviews. We invited HCWs to participate until data saturation was reached. Data saturation was monitored throughout data collection by tracking the emergence of new themes across successive interviews. Interviews continued until the proportion of new themes fell below the predefined 5% threshold, indicating that thematic saturation had been achieved [30].

Data analysis

The transcribed data were analysed using Braun and Clarke’s six steps of thematic analysis [31], combining deductive analysis to generate themes based on existing theory with an inductive approach to derive themes directly from participants’ responses [32]. The seven components of the TFA were used to summarize the themes and map responses.

The transcripts were coded by the first author using Atlas.ti (version 26.0.2.33998). Codes and emerging themes were discussed with the research team throughout the analysis to refine the coding framework and enhance analytical rigour.

Ethics

The study was approved by the Human Research Ethics Committee of the University of Stellenbosch (reference number: N24/01/002), City of Cape Town (project ID number: 12071) and the Western Cape Government Department of Health (WC_202407_003). All participants provided written informed consent.

Findings

Study participant characteristics

Of the 23 HCWs invited, 22 HCWs consented to participate. They had a mean of 11 years of experience (SD 6.5) in RR-TB care. Two were male and 20 were female, nine were doctors, nine nurses, and four community health workers.

Intervention coherence

Following a brief explanation of WGS provided to all participants, 20 out of the 22 HCWs demonstrated adequate intervention coherence, describing a clear understanding of what WGS does and how it could be applied to post-exposure management. However, two HCWs acknowledged limited familiarity with genomic technologies, particularly at the outset. This could initially undermine the confidence of doctors who are unfamiliar with genomic tools, which highlights the need for training and support during implementation.

I’m still getting to grips with the actual technicalities of how this really works. - Doctor 7

It’s still quite new. So I’m also not very, very familiar with this gene sequencing – Doctor 4

The depth of understanding differed across HCWs, with doctors demonstrating a more immediate grasp and lower cadre HCWs being more uncertain about how strain relatedness and transmission would be determined.

Whole genome sequencing gives you a much more detailed picture of the organism. And so then you can identify who has got the same organism - Doctor 9

And then how will you know that it’s not the same [strain of DR-TB], by testing? – Community health worker 1

One nurse also initially assumed WGS involved blood testing rather than sputum.

I thought maybe you were doing blood or something different – TB Nurse 8

Despite initial uncertainties, all participants were able to engage with and comprehend the underlying principles of WGS, including its use to comprehensively detect drug resistance among people diagnosed with TB, its ability to generate detailed resistance profiles to inform treatment, and its application in identifying transmission hotspots to guide targeted contact investigation. Participants were generally able to articulate how WGS could be used for TPT within existing workflows.

With regards to post-exposure management, participants recognised WGS as a valuable tool for reducing onward transmission of RR-TB, reasoning that accurate resistance profiling enables targeted regimen selection that limits further spread.

you assume that rif-resistant TB is under one umbrella and if this is showing that there are specific patterns of resistance that are spreading more and quicker and more rapidly than others, then I think it’s epidemiologically worth understanding that and knowing that if I have this type of resistance pattern I’m going to spread it faster – Doctor 1

While sometimes referring to patients rather than contacts, participants understood that WGS provides detailed resistance information to guide care.

It’s basically finding out exactly what part of the TB bacteria is set for resistance in that way, building up an effective regimen for that specific person. - Doctor 3

Affective attitude

HCWs generally expressed positive affective responses to WGS, describing excitement, reassurance, and optimism for its potential to improve clinical decision-making. Positive emotional responses were however mostly linked to reduced diagnostic uncertainty and increased confidence in treatment decisions for patients with RR-TB.

I think it’s going to work. I’ve got the feeling - Community health worker 1

I’m excited, I’m excited for a number of reasons, firstly just having the proof of the type of TB so you know that you have that reassurance that you are on the right track with the correct regimen. - Doctor 3

The culture is out [drug susceptibility results are available], patient starts with the right medication. … it is really going to help. – Community health worker 2

I believe with this one, it comes back with the whole information that will help you to treat your client – TB Nurse 3

While there was clear excitement about the ability of WGS to guide patient care, some HCWs expressed scepticism about the clinical value of WGS for guiding post-exposure management as they were uncertain around the effectiveness of certain drugs for TPT.

You could tell me the patient is sensitive to [Pyrazinamide] or whatever drug, but we don’t know how effective that (Pyrazinamide) is. So that’s why I’m not sure how the genomic sequencing is going to make a massive difference. - Doctor 2

HCWs expressed a positive attitude towards WGS for contact tracing as it could reduce stigma

The genomic testing… where you just go to a hotspot then. Because that will obviously just reduce it, like the stigma related to the index case– Doctor 2

WGS and reducing system-level burden

HCWs anticipated that WGS could support the feasibility of post-exposure management within constrained primary healthcare environments as WGS provides the information needed for TPT selection in a single assay.

The genomic sequencing … you’ll get a lot of options and then it will tell you like probably which one (medication) is the best one – Doctor 2

One doctor extended this by stating that WGS could reduce the individual burden of unnecessary side effects, particularly for children:

‘Because I mean any medication has got like side effects and you don’t want to expose someone, especially children, to something that you think, okay, let’s just do it so empirically or whatever. Not empirically but let’s just start with this and wait – Doctor 4

HCWs further anticipated that WGS could help identify cases that warrant more urgent contact tracing. This could be implemented when WGS detects strains that indicating transmission.

I think it might be a wise approach to start, at least perhaps even simultaneously, with the less resource-intensive strategy, which would be the whole genome sequencing and looking for cluster outbreaks, you know, and that can be done like tomorrow and should be done. And once you’ve uncovered the contacts, then you’re obliged to offer them TPT. – Doctor 9

HCWs spontaneously expanded on discussing the value of WGS for improved management of the index case. In the absence of WGS, identification of the drug resistance pattern often requires repeated sputum collection, prolonged laboratory turnaround times, and multiple patient visits. HCWs anticipated that WGS could reduce this clinical and system-level burden and also reduce repeated patient visits and their associated stress.

With the old one [phenotypic drug susceptibility testing (pDST)] that patient has to keep on coming back because we test, we send to lab, we wait, that patient must come back … now this patient is pre-XDR, we wait … So, it will also reduce the stress and workload from the clinicians – TB Nurse 3

Opportunity costs

Participants did not envision any opportunity costs as WGS processes to them as WGS takes place at the laboratory level.

It’s a process that takes place already, laboratory-wise. I mean, it’s not additional work that you need to do from your side - Doctor 8

Ethicality

Participants viewed WGS as ethically acceptable and even favourable to current practice, particularly given its potential to reduce the stigma, blame, and discrimination associated with an RR-TB diagnosis. HCWs emphasised that household-based contact tracing as currently implemented often exposes index cases and fuels blame within families and communities. A community health worker drew a connection between WGS and stigma reduction, reasoning that a better understanding of transmission patterns within households could address the fear-driven stigma that leads families to isolate rather than support TB patients.

It will help because the stigma of TB, most of it starts with your family and the family won’t think maybe I can also have TB and then they will say you’re not going to stay with us… but you as a family, you are there to support – Community health worker 2

WGS-guided hotspot-based screening was also perceived as more ethical and less stigmatising.

If we are screening the hotspot … people don’t know who has TB and who has not. Whereas when we go to a home, everyone might know they have TB. - TB Nurse 4

Some HCWs further suggested that framing transmission at community level could reduce individual blame and promote collective responsibility:

You take out the stigma regarding the household… you can say that this TB is running amok [rampant] in your area. - Doctor 1

HCWs also felt that WGS could clarify transmission pathways and prevent misattribution of blame.

Just to know if they have the same strain as the index case or not, they might have gotten another strain from somewhere else. - Doctor 8

There was this child, he had TB, and then he go to creche and then the parents thought he got it from the teachers. So I needed that thing [NGS] just to show it’s not – TB Nurse 5

These perspectives reinforced ethical acceptability while also supporting the social feasibility of WGS-guided post-exposure management in high-burden communities.

Anticipated perceived effectiveness

HCWs anticipated that WGS would reduce RR-TB transmission by enabling faster identification of resistance profiles, linked cases, and transmission hotspots. Participants described real-world scenarios where delayed identification contributed to ongoing transmission.

If maybe this test was done at that time maybe we were going to find the area as I’ve said before they even get sick because that was the hotspot at that time. - TB Nurse 4

The time frame is shorter to finding out… I think it can really help. - TB Nurse 9

In addition, HCWs believed WGS could be effective in ensuring that individuals receive the correct regimen from the start, which is especially relevant for index cases and to some degree for contacts:

It’s of no use giving drugs that don’t work, and then you have to change and switch all the time, so it will help the doctors also just to get the right medication for the right patient - TB Nurse 6

HCWs also explained that patients’ emotional distress may be reduced if they could receive the correct regimen from the start instead of changing one or more times during treatment:

Every now and then when you are seeing that patient, you’ve got new information… today I’m telling her this. Next week, I’m telling her another thing… like, sister last week you said it’s rifampicin, now sister it’s another thing… So I think it’s for me less stressful for the patient – TB Nurse 3

Participants also noted that prescribing drugs without precise susceptibility data often leads to medication wastage and inefficient use of limited resources:

It will help with wasting stock. I mean it will reduce a lot of expenditure on medication that we don’t really need. - TB Nurse 6

By enabling correct treatment from the outset, WGS could allow clinics to redirect financial and human resources more efficiently, further supporting perceived effectiveness.

Self-efficacy

HCWs reported that WGS could increase their confidence in clinical decision-making, particularly when managing contacts of cases with complex resistance profiles. Participants described reassurance when treatment choices were supported by WGS.

I think it’s the knowledge that what I’m choosing is the right thing and the knowledge I’ve got physical proof that it’s going to work - Doctor 1

Discussion

While raising some concerns and scepticism due to unfamiliarity with WGS, HCWs expressed predominantly positive affective attitudes toward WGS for post-exposure RR-TB management, an intervention they believed would increase their self-efficacy, reduce the burden RR-TB management poses to frontline workers, and would not pose an opportunity cost as WGS is a laboratory-based process that would integrate into existing diagnostic systems. Against a background of delays in comprehensive drug susceptibility testing, frequent regimen changes, and uncertainty regarding transmission, HCWs perceived WGS as a technology with the potential to address existing gaps in clinical decision-making and post-exposure management, underpinning its anticipated acceptability. Anticipated advantages focussed on reduced stigma and blame by better understanding of transmission and by changing contract tracing from a patient or household-centred approach to a community-centred approach when WGS identifies RR-TB transmission hotspots. HCW also extended the anticipated effectiveness of WGS from index cases to contacts, with reduced diagnostic uncertainty due to a more rapid knowledge of the comprehensive drug resistance profile. This could result in improved post-exposure management with earlier implementation of post-exposure management interventions and increased confidence in TPT decision-making, especially for complex forms of RR-TB. They anticipated that this would reduce distress for HCWs, patients and contacts.

Successful implementation of WGS-guided post-exposure management will require cadre-specific training for tailored integration into existing TB programme workflows to overcome HCWs limited exposure to the interpretation of WGS data and address the variability in diagnostics decision making responsibilities of different HCWs. Our results thus mirror the finding form Madagascar and Canada, where limited familiarity with NGS for TB diagnostics translated into scepticism and cautious adoption among clinicians and laboratory staff [33]. The need for training was also emphasized by HCWs in Khayelitsha, South Africa, who reported that additional training on interpreting WGS results would be beneficial [34]. Feasibility of WGS however extends beyond individual HCWs knowledge and competence and requires integration into the laboratory workflow. Similar to HCWs in India [34], HCWs in our study perceived WGS as a low-burden to facilities and view sequencing as a laboratory-based process that would integrate into existing diagnostic systems. Nevertheless, structural implementation challenges of WGS such as turnaround time, infrastructure limitations, and resource constraints were highlighted in another South African study [35]. This highlights that the feasibility of routine WGS is likely to depend greatly on the existing health infrastructure and the successful implementation of WGS in laboratory-level processes.

The anticipated positive effect on ethical care was an important dimension of the acceptability of WGS for post-exposure management. HCWs believed WGS could reduce stigma and diffuse household-level blame by shifting attention away from households to hotspots where multiple patients in a geographic area are infected with a genetically similar Mtb strain [36]. This is in stark contrast to findings in Belgium and Botswana, where HCWs expressed concern that the use of geospatial WGS data could stigmatise already marginalised communities by associating specific areas or groups with TB [37, 38]. These conflicting findings suggest that different contexts may influence how stigma and ethical risk are perceived in relation to genomic TB data and highlight that more research is needed to better capture the ethical implications of the use of WGS for post-exposure management.

Our study also confirms findings from another South African study, where HCWs reported that access to WGS simplified DST, enabled more evidence-based treatment decisions, reduced pill burden, and reduced the patient distress currently associated with frequent changes in treatment regimen when additional resistance is diagnosed [35]. Because acceptability is an important determinant of successful implementation, the generally positive anticipated perceptions observed in this study are encouraging for the future implementation of WGS within routine RR-TB services.

A key strength of this study was the inclusion of HCWs from multiple cadres involved in RR-TB care in a high burden setting, including doctors, nurses and community health workers. This enabled exploration of anticipated acceptability across different levels of clinical decision-making and service delivery, strengthening the depth and applicability of the findings within primary healthcare settings. Another strength is the application of the TFA framework to a complex diagnostic technology, which allowed a structured and theory-driven approach to the analysis of anticipated acceptance and feasibility.

Our study also has some limitations. First, we explored anticipated rather than experienced acceptability, as WGS is not yet routinely implemented for post-exposure management. Participants’ responses may thus not reflect actual behaviour in routine practice and participants could not fully evaluate the opportunity costs associated with routine WGS implementation. Once WGS is routinely implemented, studies should assess how HCWs perceive the actual utility of WGS for post-exposure management of RR-TB. Second, purposive sampling of diverse cadres of HCWs at public primary healthcare facilities sought to capture a range of perspectives. The findings may only be transferable to high TB-burden public primary healthcare settings with similar contextual characteristics. Third, because familiarity with genomic technologies was variable across participants, a brief standardized explanation of potential applications of WGS for drug resistance detection and transmission mapping was provided before discussing its acceptability. While most participants demonstrated adequate intervention coherence, limited prior exposure to WGS may have influenced both enthusiasm and scepticism and participants’ views may have been influenced by the information provided. Fourth, anticipated effectiveness may have been overestimated as clinicians may still face constrained therapeutic choices due to the lack of evidence-based guidelines for post-exposure management of contacts of patients with RR-TB with resistance isoniazid and fluoroquinolones.

Conclusion

This study showed the anticipated enthusiasm of HCWs for WGS for post-exposure management. Successful implementation of WGS-guided post-exposure management will likely require cadre-specific training, clear guidance on interpreting WGS results, and integration into existing TB programme workflows. To maximize the potential benefits of WGS, effective regimens for TPT in the context of resistance to current TPT regimens will be needed.
