Multi-omics analysis reveals different cholesterol metabolism subtypes in colorectal cancer
Authors: Yu Sun, Deyang Kong, Huiru Zhang, Jialiang Fan, Shuaibing Lu, Wenjing Yang, Renshen Xiang, Qi Zhang, Wei Pei, Lin Feng, Haizeng Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCholesterol metabolism (CM) plays a critical role in the progression of colorectal cancer (CRC), yet its molecular and immunological implications remain incompletely understood. Therefore, we aimed to identify CRC subtypes according to CM-related genes and reveal their distinct characteristics.MethodsBased on CM-related genes, we applied unsupervised clustering to classify CRC into two subtypes using transcriptomic data from TCGA and comprehensively compared their transcriptomic, genomic and clinical characteristics. We utilized single-cell RNA sequencing data and classified the samples into two subtypes and investigated the distinctions in the tumor microenvironment (TME) between these subtypes.ResultsTwo distinct CM subtypes were identified: Subtype A, characterized by cholesterol esterification and storage, was associated with inflammatory activation and cellular senescence. This subtype exhibited a poor prognosis and reduced predicted response to chemotherapy and immunotherapy. Tumor cells in Subtype A exhibited characteristics of epithelial-mesenchymal transition and angiogenesis. The TME in Subtype A contained higher infiltration of myeloid cells, fibroblasts, and pericytes, with dominant immunosuppressive tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), especially TAM_SPP1, which interacted closely with Fibro_IL32, promoting immune exclusion. In contrast, Subtype B was marked by enhanced cholesterol catabolism and regulation. Tumor cells in this subtype displayed features of proliferation and stem-like properties. It showed a more active immune microenvironment with increased plasma cell infiltration and fewer immunosuppressive TAMs. Finally, we constructed a prognostic signature and validated its performance across multiple datasets.ConclusionsThese findings provide comprehensive insights into CM subtypes in CRC, highlighting their clinical significance and potential therapeutic implications.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-025-03993-z.Keywords: Cholesterol metabolism, Colorectal cancer, Tumor microenvironment, Prognosis

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) ranks third in global incidence and second in mortality among all cancer types [1]. In recent years, shifts in lifestyle have contributed to a rise in both the incidence and mortality rates of CRC in China, imposing a significant burden on the healthcare system [2]. Currently, the main treatments for CRC include surgery, chemotherapy, radiotherapy and immunotherapy. Despite significant advances in diagnosis and treatment, the prognosis for CRC patients remains poor, emphasizing the need for a deeper understanding of the molecular heterogeneity of CRC to develop more effective personalized therapies.

Cholesterol plays a crucial role as a structural component of cell membranes, and is essential for maintaining their integrity and fluidity. Additionally, it serves as a precursor for bile acids and steroid hormones, emphasizing its importance in cellular functions and signaling pathways [3]. Tumor cells have an elevated demand for cholesterol to support membrane biogenesis and other essential functions. As a result, cholesterol metabolism (CM) significantly contributes to cancer progression by impacting cell proliferation, migration, and invasion [4]. In addition, cholesterol can disrupt the homeostasis of metabolism in immune cells, thus inhibits anti-cancer immunity [5]. Therefore, unveiling the role of CM in tumors will enhance our understanding of tumorigenesis, and guide therapeutic strategies to improve patient outcomes.

In this study, we identified two distinct cholesterol metabolic patterns in CRC based on CM-related genes. We performed multi-omics analysis to elucidate differences in clinical features, gene expression, genomic characteristics and tumor microenvironment (TME) between the two subtypes. We also proposed a new prognostic signature based on CM-related genes in CRC. Our findings provide new insights for stratification and precision treatment of CRC patients.

Materials and methods

Data acquisition and processing

RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) and whole-exome sequencing (WES) data, along with corresponding clinical information for CRC cases, were acquired from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database using the TCGAbiolinks package (version 2.26.0) [6]. Additionally, publicly available datasets were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/), including RNA-seq expression matrices (GSE41258 [7, 8], GSE17536 [9, 10]) and single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data (GSE132465 [11], GSE144735 [11]), along with their respective clinical information.

CM-related genes were retrieved from the GeneCards database (https://www.genecards.org/) using a relevance score greater than 20 as the selection criterion [12]. Cell senescence genes were derived from the literature as described by Saul et al. [13]. Senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) genes were sourced from the literature according to Wang et al. [14].

Subtype analysis

For the bulk RNA-seq data, CRC samples were subjected to unsupervised clustering using the ConsensusClusterPlus package (version 1.62.0) [15], based on the CM-related genes. To determine the optimal number of clusters (k) ensuring clustering stability, specimens were divided into groups ranging from k = 2 to k = 9. The most suitable k value was subsequently identified using the Proportion of Ambiguous Clustering (PAC) metric. For single-cell RNA sequencing data, “pseudobulk” RNA-seq profiles were generated from Seurat objects by aggregating single-cell expression data from the same sample. Subsequently, the nearest template prediction (NTP) algorithm provided by CMScaller package (version 2.0.1) [16] was used to assign each sample to one of the two subtypes.

Functional enrichment analysis

Gene Ontology (GO) and Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) were conducted using the clusterProfiler package (version 4.6.2) [17]. Single-sample Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (ssGSEA) was performed utilizing the GSVA package (version 1.46.0) [18].

Immune infiltration analysis

The composition of immune cells was inferred using the CIBERSORT [19] and xCell [20] algorithms, as implemented in the Immuno-Oncology Biology Research (IOBR) package (version 0.99.9) [21].

Whole exome sequencing data analysis

Somatic mutations were analyzed and visualized using the maftools package (version 2.14.0) [22]. To identify significantly recurrent regions of copy number variation (CNV), GISTIC 2.0 [23] was applied to the “Masked Copy Number Segment” data. The CNV burden was subsequently calculated as the total number of genes with copy number gains or losses within one sample.

Prediction of patient drug response and immunotherapy efficacy

The potential clinical drug response of each patient was predicted using the oncoPredict package (version 0.2) [24]. To assess potential responses to immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) therapy and evaluate the likelihood of tumor immune escape, the Tumor Immune Dysfunction and Exclusion (TIDE) algorithm was utilized [25, 26] (http://tide.dfci.harvard.edu/).

scRNA-seq data pre-processing and analysis

scRNA-seq data were processed and analyzed using the Seurat package (version 5.0) [27], following standard pipelines. The Harmony algorithm [28] with default parameters was utilized to correct the batch effects among samples. The Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) projection was used to visualize cell clusters. Cell type annotation was performed in a stepwise manner integrating prior knowledge of canonical marker genes and cluster-specific differential expression analysis. Major cell lineages were identified as follows: epithelial cells (EPCAM, KRT18), immune cells (PTPRC), CD4 + T cells (CD3E, CD4), CD8 + T cells (CD3E, CD8A), NK cells (NCAM1, KLRD1, FCGR3A), B cells (MS4A1, CD19), plasma cells (SDC1, JCHAIN, MZB1), myeloid cells (CD68, CD14), mast cells (TPSB2, KIT), fibroblasts (COL1A1, DCN), endothelial cells (PECAM1, VWF), pericytes (RGS5, ACTA2) and smooth muscle cells (TAGLN, MYL9). Subsequently, each major lineage was subclustered, and differentially expressed genes (DEGs) for individual cell clusters were identified using the FindMarkers function in the Seurat package. Cell clusters were then assigned by combing the expression patterns of DEGs with cell type-specific markers reported in the literature.

Identification of malignant cells

To distinguish malignant cells from non-malignant cells, epithelial cells were first identified based on the expression of canonical marker genes EPCAM, KRT8, and KRT13. Subsequently, CNVs within these identified epithelial cells were assessed using the copykat package (version 1.1.0) [29].

Cell stemness assessment using CytoTRACE

The differentiation state (stemness) of malignant cells was assessed using the CytoTRACE2 package (version 1.0.0) [30] to compute CytoTRACE scores. These scores range from 0 to 1, where higher values indicate greater stemness (a less differentiated state), and lower scores suggest reduced stemness (a more differentiated state).

Transcription factor (TF) activity analysis

Regulon activity was analyzed using the dorothea package (version 1.10.0) [31]. To ensure robust estimation of TF activities, regulons with confidence levels A, B, and C were selected for this analysis. The activity score for each TF within individual cells was computed using VIPER [32]. Subtype-specific TFs were subsequently identified based on the following criteria: (1) expression of the TF in >30% of cells within the subtype of interest, and (2) significantly higher TF activity in the subtype of interest compared to the other subtypes, with a log2 fold change in activity >0.5.

Cell-cell interactions using cellchat

Cell-cell communication networks were inferred using the CellChat package (version 1.6.1), leveraging the curated ligand-receptor database from CellPhoneDB. Significant signaling interactions between cell clusters were determined through permutation testing, with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Prognostic signature construction

First, DEGs between Subtype A and Subtype B were identified using the edgeR package (version 3.40.2), with a threshold of |log₂ fold change| ≥ 1 and false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05. Univariate Cox proportional hazards regression analysis was then performed on these DEGs to evaluate their individual associations with overall survival (OS). Genes with a univariate Cox p-value < 0.05 were retained for further model selection. The least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) Cox regression was applied with 10-fold cross-validation to determine the optimal penalty parameter (λ). The resulting gene set was further refined using stepwise multivariate Cox regression to select the final set of independent prognostic factors. The prognostic signature for each patient was calculated as a linear combination of the expression levels of the selected genes weighted by their respective regression coefficients from the multivariate model. Patients were stratified into high- and low-risk groups based on the median risk score in the training cohort. Survival differences between groups were assessed using Kaplan-Meier analysis and the log-rank test. The prognostic signature was further validated in two independent external cohorts obtained from the GEO database. Risk scores were calculated in each validation cohort using the same formula and coefficients derived from the training model. The predictive performance of the model was evaluated using the time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.2.3). Differences in continuous variables between two groups were evaluated using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. For categorical variables, comparisons were made using either the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate based on expected cell frequencies. Survival analyses were conducted using the Kaplan-Meier method to generate survival curves, and the Cox proportional hazards model was employed to assess prognostic factors. Statistical significance was defined as a P value less than 0.05.

Results

Identification and characterization of CM subtypes in CRC

To delineate distinct CM patterns in CRC, unsupervised clustering was performed on TCGA CRC samples using 195 CM-related genes. This analysis classified CRC samples into two robust subtypes: Subtype A (n = 161) and Subtype B (n = 332) (Fig. 1A, Fig. S1A). Heatmap visualization highlighted differential expression of CM-related genes and distinct enrichment of CM-related pathways between these subtypes (Fig. 1B, C). Specifically, Subtype A was predominantly associated with cholesterol esterification and storage pathways, suggesting a propensity for intracellular cholesterol accumulation which could alter cellular lipid profiles and metabolic functions. In contrast, Subtype B was characterized by enrichment in pathways regulating cholesterol metabolic processes and catabolism, indicating a more efficient cholesterol management system potentially maintaining cholesterol homeostasis (Fig. 1B, C).

We next investigated the clinical correlates of these CM subtypes. Patients classified as Subtype A exhibited a significantly higher T stage (P = 0.013, Fig. 1D) and a greater proportion of tumors originating in the colon versus the rectum (P = 0.006, Fig. S1B) compared to Subtype B. No significant associations were observed with N stage, M stage, or sex (Fig. 1E, F, Fig. S1C).

Furthermore, significant associations were identified between our CM subtypes and the established Consensus Molecular Subtypes (CMS). Over half of Subtype A tumors were classified as CMS4, with CMS1 also representing a notable proportion. Conversely, CMS2 was more prevalent in Subtype B (P < 0.0001, Fig. S1D). The enrichment of CMS1 in Subtype A aligns with the higher frequency of microsatellite instability (MSI) also observed in these tumors (P < 0.0001, Fig. S1E). Considering that MSI CRC has been reported to exhibit enhanced scavenging of cholesterol from its environment [33], it is plausible that Subtype A, being enriched in MSI tumors, facilitated the intracellular accumulation of externally acquired cholesterol. This accumulation could subsequently contribute to the observed cholesterol storage phenotype characteristic of Subtype A.

Interestingly, despite the enrichment of MSI tumors within Subtype A (a feature generally associated with a favorable prognosis) [34], patients classified as Subtype A demonstrated significantly poorer OS (P = 0.038) and recurrence-free survival (RFS; P = 0.038) compared to Subtype B (Fig. 1G, Fig. S1F). This adverse prognostic association was also evident within the microsatellite-stable (MSS) cohort, where Subtype A patients had significantly poorer OS (P = 0.045; Fig. S1G). Although a similar trend towards poorer outcomes for Subtype A was observed in the MSI cohort, the difference did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.14; Fig. S1H). Collectively, these findings - a significant adverse prognostic impact in MSS CRC and a consistent, albeit non-significant, trend in MSI CRC - suggested that the unfavorable prognostic impact of Subtype A was largely independent of microsatellite status.

Transcriptomic signatures differentiated the two subtypes

Transcriptomic analysis revealed distinct molecular characteristics differentiating Subtype A and Subtype B. Overall, Subtype A exhibited an elevated senescent phenotype, characterized by higher senescence scores (Fig. S2A) and the upregulation of most SASP-related genes (Fig. S2B). Consistent with this pro-inflammatory state, DEG analysis indicated that Subtype A was significantly enriched in inflammation-related pathways, including those governing myeloid cell chemotaxis and extracellular matrix (ECM) organization (Fig. 2A–D). Furthermore, immune infiltration analysis demonstrated significantly higher proportions of macrophages and neutrophils in Subtype A (Fig. S2C). The accumulation of myeloid cells, known to foster an immunosuppressive microenvironment and alter ECM dynamics [35, 36], likely contributed to the poor prognosis observed in Subtype A. In contrast, Subtype B showed significant enrichment in pathways associated with digestion and absorption, lipid and steroid metabolism, as well as peroxisome function (Fig. 2E–G). This profile demonstrated a metabolic landscape more characteristic of normal colorectal epithelium for Subtype B.

Distinct genomic instability pathways characterized the two CM subtypes

To further delineate the distinctions between the two CM subtypes, we investigated their genomic landscapes, focusing on the two major pathways of genomic instability in CRC, MSI and chromosomal instability (CIN) [37]. Subtype A was characterized by features consistent with MSI-driven genomic instability. As previously noted, it exhibited a significantly higher proportion of MSI tumors. This correlated with a markedly higher tumor mutation burden (TMB) in Subtype A compared to Subtype B (P < 0.0001, Fig. 3E). This elevated TMB in Subtype A was further reflected in a higher median mutation rate among the top 20 most frequently mutated genes (27% vs. 14%, Fig. 3A, B), increased mutation rates across the majority of driver genes (Fig. 3C), and a greater frequency of mutations within key signaling pathways implicated in cancer progression, such as Cell Cycle, Hippo, MYC, NOTCH, NRF2, PI3K and TGF-Beta pathways (Fig. 3D). To isolate the contribution of MSI to these mutational characteristics, we performed a subgroup analysis on MSS CRC cases. Within this MSS cohort, no significant difference in TMB was observed between Subtype A and Subtype B (Fig. S3A), and the mutation profiles across key signaling pathways also showed no notable distinctions (Fig. S3B). These findings suggested that the increased mutational load observed in the overall Subtype A cohort was predominantly driven by its enrichment in MSI tumors.

In contrast to the MSI-driven mutational landscape of Subtype A, Subtype B displayed characteristics indicative of CIN. CNV analysis revealed that Subtype B consistently exhibited a significantly higher CNV burden than Subtype A (P < 0.0001, Fig. 3F, Fig. S3C). Intriguingly, among major driver genes, APC was uniquely identified with a significantly higher mutation frequency in Subtype B compared to Subtype A (Fig. 3C). This observation was particularly relevant as APC mutations are well-established inducers of CIN in CRC [38], thus further supporting the characterization of Subtype B by a predominant CIN phenotype.

Differential drug sensitivity and predicted immunotherapy response between CM subtypes

Evaluating patient-specific sensitivity to various drug treatments is crucial for developing individualized therapy regimens. We therefore compared the predicted drug sensitivity to commonly used chemotherapeutic agents between the two CM subtypes. This analysis revealed that Subtype A exhibited higher predicted sensitivity to irinotecan and 5-fluorouracil, whereas Subtype B was predicted to be more sensitive to oxaliplatin (Fig. 4A–C). Consistent with the elevated senescence phenotype observed in Subtype A, this subtype also showed increased predicted sensitivity to the anti-senescence drug rapamycin [39] (Fig. 4D).

We next sought to predict the likelihood of response to immunotherapy using the TIDE algorithm. Given that microsatellite status is a well-established predictor of ICB efficacy [40], these analyses were performed separately for MSS and MSI cohorts. Within the MSS cohort, Subtype A exhibited significantly higher TIDE scores and a correspondingly lower proportion of predicted ICB responders compared to Subtype B (Fig. 4G and I). These findings suggested that MSS tumors classified as Subtype A may exhibit a poorer response to immunotherapy and possess a higher potential for immune escape [41]. In contrast, no significant difference in TIDE scores or predicted responder rates was observed between the two subtypes within the MSI cohort (Fig. 4H and J), which aligned with the generally favorable response of MSI CRC patients to immunotherapy.

Single-cell atlas revealed distinct TME compositions of CM subtypes

To further investigate the TME characteristics of the two CM subtypes at a single-cell level, we analyzed publicly available scRNA-seq data. Tumor samples were assigned as Subtype A (n = 14) and Subtype B (n = 15) using the NTP algorithm. After quality controls, a total of 12 distinct cell populations were identified across all samples (Fig. 5A). Although most cell types were present in each sample, the proportions of each cell type varied across the samples (Fig. 5B). Notably, significant differences in the abundance of cell types were observed between Subtype A and Subtype B, suggesting distinct microenvironmental compositions in the two subtypes. The results revealed that myeloid cells, fibroblasts and pericytes were more abundant in Subtype A, whereas plasma cells were enriched in Subtype B (Fig. 5C). Our findings indicated that the two subtypes possessed unique TMEs that differed from each other.

Characterizing the heterogeneity of tumor cells

By using copykat, we identified malignant cells and re-clustered them to investigate the transcriptional patterns (Fig. 6A, B). We further explored the CM-related signature scores in Subtype A and Subtype B tumor cells. The cholesterol esterification and biosynthesis scores were higher in tumor cells of Subtype A compared to those in Subtype B, whereas the cholesterol catabolic score was elevated in tumor cells of Subtype B relative to Subtype A (Fig. 6C–E). The results confirmed the distinct cholesterol metabolic profiles between Subtype A and Subtype B tumor cells.

We next investigated the signatures related to metastasis and proliferation in tumor cells of Subtype A and Subtype B to compare their malignant biological behaviors. Results showed that tumor cells of Subtype A had higher signature scores of epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) and angiogenesis, while tumor cells of Subtype B exhibited higher proliferation score (Fig. S4A-C). Tumor cells in Subtype A also had a higher score of senescence (Fig. S4D). Cell-cycle analysis demonstrated a higher proportion of cells in the S and G2M phases in Subtype B, indicating enhanced cell cycle progression (Fig. S4E). Through the CytoTRACE analysis, tumor cells of Subtype A exhibited significantly lower tumor stemness characteristics compared to Subtype B (Fig. 6F, Fig. S4F).

To uncover the specifically activated transcriptional regulons in the two subtypes, we identified TFs with differential activity compared to each other (Fig. 6G). Among the top TFs, CREB3L1 was shown to be closely related to lipid metabolism and the activation of extracellular matrix signaling [42], and both the transcriptional activity and expression levels were significantly elevated in cancer cells from Subtype A compared to those from Subtype B. HIF1A was also upregulated in Subtype A. Previous studies showed that HIF1A inhibition led to a reduction in cholesterol level [43], and cholesterol could upregulate HIF1A through hypoxia and inflammation [44]. CEBPA was the top TF in Subtype B, and it was reported that CEBPA was responsible for the homeostasis of metabolism [45], and the upregulation of CEBPA could also cause the decrease of LDLR [46]. MYC was also upregulated in Subtype B.

Differential roles of TAMs in shaping the TME of subtypes A and B

Due to the most pronounced difference in the proportion of myeloid cells between the two subtypes, we proceeded to investigate the functional disparities of myeloid cells within each group to uncover their impact on the TME. We annotated myeloid cells into 7 distinct cell clusters based on unique cell markers (Fig. 7A, Fig. S5A). Among these clusters, the TAMs population was further categorized into three subsets and named as TAM_SPP1, TAM_FCN1, and TAM_MKI67. The proportion of TAM_SPP1 was increased in the TME of Subtype A compared to Subtype B (P < 0.0001, Fig. 7B). TAM_SPP1 was distinguished by elevated expression levels of M2 macrophage markers such as MRC1, CD163, MSR1 and CCL18 [47, 48], as well as immunosuppressive markers such as SIRPA, HAVCR2, LGALS1 and LGALS3 (Fig. S5B-C). TAM_SPP1 also exhibited high expression of CM-related genes such as APOE, APOC1, ABCA1 and ABCG1, which played crucial roles in CM (Fig. S5D). The results of GSEA revealed that TAM_SPP1 was enriched in response to heat and lipid transport, indicating that their phenotypes may be closely linked to lipid metabolic processes (Fig. 7C). Additionally, senescence-related characteristic was upregulated in TAM_SPP1, which was consistent with prior research [49] (Fig. S5E).

Previous studies have shown that TAM_SPP1 could interact with fibroblasts to promote the formation of immune-excluded structure [50]. We further annotated fibroblasts into 6 cell clusters (Fig. 7D) and investigated the interactions between TAM_SPP1 and fibroblasts by utilizing CellChat to analyze their communications. TAM_SPP1 and Fibro_IL32 exhibited the strongest interaction strength (Fig. 7E). Moreover, a significant positive correlation between the proportion of TAM_SPP1 and Fibro_IL32 was observed (Fig. S5F-G). TAM_SPP1 and Fibro_IL32 exhibited heightened ligand-receptor interactions related to TGF-β and SPP1 signaling, underscoring their pivotal role of extracellular matrix remodeling (Fig. 7F). Patients exhibiting high infiltration levels of TAM_SPP1 and Fibro_IL32 demonstrated a poorer prognosis in comparison to those with low infiltration levels (P = 0.075, Fig. 7G).

In contrast to Subtype A, TAMs in Subtype B exhibited elevated expression of HLA class II molecules-related genes (Fig. S6A). Enrichment analysis revealed that these TAMs were significantly enriched in pathways associated with antigen processing and presentation, as well as immune response pathways (Fig. S6B). These findings revealed that TAMs in Subtype B may play a more prominent role in adaptive immunity, potentially functioning as antigen-presenting cells (APCs) that facilitate T-cell activation and immune surveillance within the TME.

Construction and validation of a CM-related prognostic model

To leverage the differences between the two subtypes for predicting patient outcomes more accurately, we aimed to develop a CM-related genes prognostic model. To develop a prognostic model related to CM, we initially identified the DEGs between two subtypes. Univariate Cox regression analysis was conducted using the DEGs between the two lipid metabolism subtypes, identifying 72 genes as significantly associated with prognosis. Subsequently, LASSO-Cox regression (Fig. 8A, B) and stepwise regression were applied to choose the critical prognostic factors, and 5 genes, including PCOLCE2, HOXC6, F7, EEF1A2 and CALB1 were selected to construct the CM-related prognostic signature (CMRPS) (Fig. 8C). The samples were then stratified into two groups based on the median CMRPS score. KM survival analysis demonstrated that the low CMRPS group had a significantly more favorable prognosis compared to the high CMRPS group (P < 0.0001, Fig. 8D). To validate the applicability of CMRPS, we utilized several GEO datasets, and the results showed significant prognostic differences across all datasets (GSE41258: P = 0.016, GSE17538: P = 0.029; Fig. S7A-B). Additionally, Cox regression analyses indicated that CMRPS served as an independent prognostic factor for CRC among different datasets (Fig. 8E, Fig. S7C-D). Next, we integrated CMRPS with clinical factors such as sex and stage to predict prognosis, and this achieved consistently high AUC values across multiple datasets (Fig. 8F, Fig. S7E-F). Overall, these findings underscored the accuracy and reliability of CMRPS, highlighting its potential as a robust tool for predicting patient outcomes.

Discussion

Cholesterol, which is widely distributed throughout the human body, plays a critical role in numerous physiological processes. CM is governed by a complex network that regulates various processes including biosynthesis, uptake, efflux, conversion, esterification, and trafficking [51]. Disruption in any part of the metabolic process can significantly increase the likelihood of developing disease. To date, the role of CM in CRC remains poorly understood, which inspires us to focus on CM in CRC. Based on CM-related genes, we classified CRC into two distinct subtypes that exhibit different gene expression profiles, genetic variations, immune infiltration patterns and clinical characteristics. Specifically, Subtype A’s enhanced cholesterol esterification and storage may indicate an adaptive response to the TME, where cholesterol plays a role in malignant phenotypes and immune evasion. The enrichment of myeloid cells and extracellular matrix organization in Subtype A further suggested a more aggressive phenotype. Conversely, the emphasis on cholesterol homeostasis and catabolic metabolism in Subtype B implied a different metabolic adaptation, potentially favoring tumor growth through efficient utilization of cholesterol resources. Consequently, patients in Subtype A showing a significantly poorer outcome compared to Subtype B. These distinct metabolic patterns provided valuable insights into the underlying biology of CRC and offered potential targets for personalized therapy.

Our study revealed that Subtype A was characterized by higher proportion of MSI patients, higher TMB and dysregulated cholesterol storage and biosynthesis, which were all associated with senescence [52, 53]. Persistent DNA damage, which triggers cellular senescence and promotes the secretion of SASP [54], leading to an immunosuppressive TME through metabolism, hypoxia and inflammation [55]. Senescent cells are characterized by increased cholesterol storage and intracellular cholesterol accumulation [53], which also contributes to the progression of senescence [56]. Further investigation confirmed that Subtype A exhibited higher senescence score and elevated expression of SASP genes. We also found that patients with MSI in Subtype A had poorer prognosis than Subtype B. Liao et al. found that mevalonate kinase (MVK), a critical mediator of cholesterol synthesis, acted as a critical negative regulator of tumor cell-intrinsic interferon response in MSI CRC, with reduced MVK expression correlating with improved anti-PD-1 therapy outcomes [57]. Thus, understanding the interplay between MSI CRC and CM could provide new insights into personalized therapeutic strategies.

Subtype A was enriched with MSI patients, leading to higher TMB compared to Subtype B. However, when focusing on MSS CRC cases, no significant difference in TMB was observed, indicating that the higher TMB in Subtype A was primarily driven by MSI rather than inherent mutational differences. Importantly, both in the overall cohort and in MSS cases, Subtype A consistently exhibited higher TIDE scores compared to Subtype B, which were associated with poorer responses to immunotherapy due to immune dysfunction and exclusion mechanisms. Currently, immunotherapy is predominantly applied to MSI CRC patients. In contrast, immunotherapy for MSS CRC remains largely exploratory, as these tumors often exhibit lower immunogenicity and more robust immune suppression [58]. Recent studies have explored combination therapies—such as dual PD-1/CTLA-4 blockade, anti-PD-1/PD-L1 combined with external interventions, and cytokine/chemokine inhibitors—to overcome this resistance, making immunotherapy promising for MSS CRC patients [59].

By scRNA-seq analysis, we further characterized tumor cells in the two subtypes. Tumor cells in Subtype A exhibited elevated cholesterol esterification and biosynthesis, consistent with prior findings. These cells also displayed higher scores for EMT and angiogenesis. Upregulated cholesterol esterification leads to the accumulation of cholesteryl esters in lipid droplets, forming a critical energy reservoir to support tumor cell survival and activity [60]. This process is primarily mediated by SOAT1 [61]. Notably, SOAT1 has been reported to drive EMT via regulating cholesterol esterification and contribute to tumor progression in hepatocellular carcinoma [62]. Moreover, SOAT1 activates its downstream sterol regulatory element-binding proteins (SREBPs) and promotes the synthesis of cholesterol [63], which has been shown to promote angiogenesis, cell migration and EMT [64]. In contrast, tumor cells in Subtype B exhibited a distinct metabolic profile, characterized by upregulated cholesterol catabolic pathways and enhanced proliferative capacity. Cholesterol-derived bile acids, such as deoxycholic acid and lithocholic acid, have been shown to stimulate tumor cell proliferation by increasing the expression of cyclin D1, a key regulator of G1/S phase transition [65].

The TME is a critical determinant of anti-tumor immunity and therapeutic response [66, 67]. A comprehensive understanding of the complex interactions within the TME facilitates more precise prognostic assessment and informed treatment decisions [68]. Our comprehensive analysis of scRNA-seq data revealed significant differences in the TME between Subtype A and Subtype B. Subtype A exhibited a higher abundance of myeloid cells, fibroblasts, and pericytes, whereas Subtype B was enriched in plasma cells. Further analysis on tumor cells revealed specific profiles and proposed potential TFs governing these phenotypes. A notable difference was observed in TAMs. TAM_SPP1, characterized by M2 macrophage markers and CM-related genes, was more abundant in Subtype A and interacted strongly with Fibro_IL32 through SPP1 and TGF-β signaling, contributing to an immunosuppressive TME and remodeling of the extracellular matrix. SPP1 is a phosphorylated, secreted protein generated by various cell types. It is present at low levels in circulating monocytes but can be upregulated during their differentiation into macrophages, especially those located in tumors and inflammatory sites [69]. SPP1 + TAMs are found in a variety of solid tumors, where they play critical roles in extracellular matrix remodeling and immune suppression, thus leading to an unfavorable prognosis [70]. In CRC, a previous study revealed a significant positive correlation between tumor-specific FAP + fibroblasts and SPP1 + macrophages, and this interaction promoted the formation of immune-excluded structures, thereby limiting T-cell infiltration, and patients with high expression levels of FAP or SPP1 derived less benefit from anti-PD-L1 therapy [50]. Elevated SOAT1 expression in oral squamous cell carcinoma has been shown to promote M2 polarization of TAMs [71]. Moreover, the high expression of cholesterol efflux-related genes such as ABCA1 and ABCG1 in SPP1 + TAMs, could promote cholesterol efflux and enhance M2 polarization of TAMs [72]. A previous study also showed that SPP1 + macrophages exhibited SASP features and were surrounded by senescent tumor cells [49], indicating the important role of SPP1 + macrophages in the senescence phenotype. Conversely, TAMs in Subtype B exhibited higher expression of HLA class II molecules and were enriched in antigen processing and presentation pathways, suggesting a more prominent role in anti-tumor immunity.

To experimentally validate our proposed mechanistic throughline, future research should focus on perturbing these pathways in preclinical models. For instance, SOAT1 inhibitors or SREBP inhibitors can be used to block cholesterol esterification or biosynthesis in tumor models, respectively. We hypothesize that this intervention would alter the phenotype of tumor cells, reverse M2 polarization of TAMs, diminish fibroblast activation and ECM remodeling, and finally improve T cell infiltration. Mechanistic studies, including both pathway interrogation and rescue experiments, are essential to establish causal relationships and validate the functional relevance of CM in regulating the tumor cell phenotypes and the TME.

Finally, we constructed a prognostic signature CMRPS comprising PCOLCE2, HOXC6, F7, EEF1A2 and CALB1 and validated it in independent cohorts. Integrating the CMRPS with clinical information could further enhance the predictive power for prognosis. PCOLCE2 is a glycoprotein found in the extracellular matrix. It involves in ECM receptor interactions and the Wnt signaling pathway, potentially contributing to tumor growth and metastasis [73]. Studies also showed that PCOLCE2 was connected with CM by regulating high-density lipoprotein uptake [74, 75]. HOXC6, a member of HOX gene family encoding transcription factors, was reported to be related to malignant progression and poor prognosis in CRC [76]. Previous research has indicated that HOXC6 might serve as a key regulator in ox-LDL regulation, suggesting its significant impact on lipid metabolism [77]. F7, which encoded coagulation factor VII, was associated with liver metastasis in CRC through its binding to tissue factor [78]. As a regulator of protein translation, EEF1A1 can facilitate CRC progression by signaling transduction and posttranslational modification [79]. CALB1 was a well-known calcium-binding protein, which was recognized as a marker of senescent cells [80]. It was reported that the expression of CALB1 was associated with poor prognosis in CRC, although the mechanism was unclear [81].

Despite the insights provided by our study, several limitations should be acknowledged. Methodologically, our study is limited by its retrospective design and cohort heterogeneity, including variations in sample processing, patient demographics, and disease subtypes. These factors may introduce confounding biases and affect the generalizability of our findings. Similarly, the prognostic implications of CMRPS may vary across treatment settings—particularly in the context of immunotherapy, chemotherapy, or targeted therapy—where metabolic demands and immune cell dynamics differ significantly. Furthermore, the lack of functional validation limits our ability to establish relationships between CM pathways and TME remodeling. Therefore, future studies should focus on validating these findings in independent, prospective cohorts and elucidating the precise molecular mechanisms through which CM influences CRC progression. Integrating multi-omics profiling, spatial characterization of the TME, and experimental perturbation of key metabolic nodes will be essential to dissect the functional impact of CM on tumor-immune-stromal interactions.

Conclusions

In summary, our study provided a systematic characterization of CM in CRC, identified two different CM subtypes and explored their associations with clinical characteristics, underlying biology, therapy response, and the TME. These findings enhance the understanding of CRC heterogeneity and suggest that CM-based subtyping could serve as a framework for further investigation into personalized therapeutic strategies.
