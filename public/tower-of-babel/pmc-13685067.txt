Knowledge and attitudes toward epilepsy among medical students and internship doctors: a multinational cross-sectional study in Syria, Jordan, Palestine, and Libya
Authors: Hamdah Hanifa, Amar Haj Hussin Shehadah, Feras Hamesh, Lujain Alzoubi, Carolin Maksoud, Leen Alfrra, Maya Alzoubi, Fadiah Albaroudi, Batoul Aladib, Nirmin Bachlah, Marwa Hujk, Jamal Alkhatib, Maen Alshak, Yihea Mohammad Al-Mashaqbah, Merei Assad, Tarek Farem, Ekram Hassan Hasanin, Raghad Gharaibeh, Abdelrahman Abuali, Mohammad Ghanem, Mohammad Alyahya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEpilepsy is a chronic neurological disorder characterized by sudden abnormal electrical discharges in the brain. More than three-quarters of people with epilepsy live in developing countries, where the disorder continues to pose a major public health challenge. There are also various causes of this disease, which may include tumors, infections, genetic factors, or traumas.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted on 939 medical trainees from public and private universities in four Arab countries. A convenience sampling method was used, and participants were recruited voluntarily through an online questionnaire distributed via social media platforms and paper-based questionnaires administered within medical faculties. The questionnaire consisted of several sections, including demographic characteristics, knowledge, attitudes, and practices related to epilepsy.ResultsEighty-seven point eight percent of females knew that epilepsy can occur at any age, which was significantly higher than 82.5% of males (p-value = 0.033). Furthermore, 66.9% of private university students knew about simple partial seizures while only 54.3% from public universities knew about it (p-value < 0.001). Among clinical students, 72.0% reported knowledge of simple partial seizures, compared to 31.4% among pre-clinical students (p-value < 0.001). Recognition of brain surgery as an appropriate treatment method was reported by 29.9% of students aged 21–22 years, compared to only 13.0% among those aged ≥ 25 years (p-value < 0.001).ConclusionThe findings indicate a generally favorable level of awareness and knowledge of epilepsy among medical students. However, educational gaps regarding epilepsy treatment and persistence of misconceptions emphasize the importance of revising curricula and establishing comprehensive, advanced training programs.

Background

Epilepsy is considered one of the most important chronic neurological disorders worldwide, characterized by spontaneous and recurrent epileptic seizures [1]. Different factors increase the risk of epilepsy including traumas, tumors, genetic factors, and infections of the central nervous system [2]. In 1997, the Global Campaign Against Epilepsy (GCAE) was established, which is a joint project between the World Health Organization (WHO), the International League Against Epilepsy (ILAE), and the International Bureau for Epilepsy. It aims to develop treatment services, improve patient acceptance, and prevent epilepsy worldwide [3]. Since nearly 80% of people with epilepsy live in developing countries, the disease continues to pose a significant health problem due to its psychological, health, and social consequences [4]. A study by Abbasi et al. indicated that patients with epilepsy consume large amounts of antioxidants and carbohydrates, including vitamins B12 and B6, vitamin A, calcium, folic acid, and potassium, while consuming less selenium and zinc [5]. Treatment involves anti-epileptic drugs or surgery. Epilepsy surgery is a successful treatment option for patients with focal seizures resistant to medical therapy, with an average seizure-free rate after surgery of 67.5% over two years [6]. Some studies in Saudi Arabia and Palestine showed that students know the basics, but there are still gaps in their understanding. For example, many students are not fully aware of the causes of epilepsy or the best ways to manage it, and some still believe in cultural misconceptions like linking epilepsy to mental illness or possession [7, 8]. This study aimed to assess the level of knowledge, awareness, and attitudes towards epilepsy among medical trainees in four different Arab countries, with the goal of comparing the differences between educational levels in these countries, and highlighting the most prominent misconceptions and common beliefs about epilepsy, in order to contribute to the design and implementation of educational workshops aimed at healthcare students, particularly in developing countries.

Methods

Study design

The study was conducted in several universities in four Arab countries: Syria, Jordan, Libya, and the occupied Palestinian territories, where electronic questionnaires were distributed through social media platforms to university students, specifically human medicine students, in addition to distributing paper questionnaires within the medical faculties in these universities. This organized and precise process of collecting and verifying information continued from 4 September 2025 until 18 November 2025.

Participants and data collection

Convenience sampling was used to select participants, representing both clinical and pre-clinical stages. Regarding the universities, we selected them based on the authors’ locations and their access to neighboring universities in each country. This allowed the research team to reach medical students from both public and private institutions. Of the 1100 students we were able to reach, 939 of them cooperated with us in completing the survey and were included in the final analysis, and therefore the response rate was approximately 85.4%. Exclusions included all students who declined to provide data. However, the inclusion criteria stipulated that participants must be medical students from the specified countries or internship doctors. A specialized team of medical students trained in data collection methodologies oversaw the questionnaire’s coordination and collection process. Approximately ten authors then distributed the questionnaire electronically to students via various social media platforms, including WhatsApp, Instagram, Facebook, and Telegram. In addition, some researchers conducted an average 10-minute interview in which the study was explained in terms of importance and objectives.

Validity and pilot study

The questionnaire was translated into Arabic using back-to-back translation to ensure accuracy. The content validity assessment process was overseen by a faculty member and a specialist physician, who reviewed the items to ensure clarity, relevance, and cultural context across the four countries, resulting in some minor adjustments to the section on attitudes toward epilepsy. Six stigma-related items demonstrated acceptable internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.64), and this value refers specifically to these selected items within the broader 16-item attitudes section. A pilot study was conducted on 55 students from Syria to assess the clarity, comprehensibility, and feasibility of the questionnaire. No major modifications were required based on participant feedback, and these students were excluded from the final analysis.

Ethical approval

Participation in this study was entirely voluntary and carried no academic consequences. For the online questionnaire, participants were required to provide informed consent by selecting “yes” before proceeding; those who selected “no” were automatically excluded from the analysis. For participants approached in person, written informed consent was obtained prior to data collection. Ethical approval for this study was granted by the Institutional Review Board Committee, Faculty of Medicine, University of Kalamoon, Syria (Approval ID: 19/EBR-UOK, 2025). As the study used an anonymous questionnaire without collecting identifiable or sensitive information, additional local ethical approvals from the participating countries were not required according to their institutional regulations. The study adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

To avoid the risk of duplicate responses, we limited submissions to one per IP device via Google Forms’ settings.

Statistical analysis

Before conducting the analysis, we excluded participant data with missing values. The Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS Inc.), version 25, was used during our data analysis. To summarize the demographic characteristics in our study, we used descriptive statistics. To analyze differences between groups, we used the chi-square test. The positive attitude outcome was derived from six stigma-related items. Each positive response was coded as 1, while negative or uncertain responses (“no” or “I don’t know”) were coded as 0, yielding a total score ranging from 0 to 6. Because the distribution of the score was non-normal, the median value (3) was used as the cutoff point. Students scoring 3 or above were classified as having a positive attitude, whereas those scoring below 3 were categorized as having a less positive attitude. In order to identify factors associated with positive attitude toward epilepsy, a multivariable binary logistic regression model was fitted including gender and nationality as independent variables. Adjusted odds ratios (aORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported. The level of statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 for all statistical tests, and bold values in the results tables indicate statistically significant findings.

Results

Demographic characteristics of participants:

A survey was conducted among 939 medical students. Females constituted the majority (72.6%), and most students were between 23 and 24 years old (32.2%). Furthermore, the majority of students attended public universities (60.4%). In this study, we aimed to include students from all academic years, in addition to internship students, to ensure broad representation. The number of internship students was 77 (8.2%). Finally, 71.6% of students were willing to attend an introductory lecture on epilepsy, its causes, and treatments. For more details, see Table 1.

Knowledge of epilepsy, its seizures, causes, and symptoms:

The vast majority (95.1%) of participants defined epilepsy as abnormal electrical overactivity in the brain (see Fig. 1). The most commonly identified seizure types were tonic-clonic (69.6%), followed by absence seizures (60.7%), and then simple partial seizures (59.3%). All types of seizures showed statistically significant differences between clinical and pre-clinical students (p < 0.001), while some differences were found between public and private universities. As for the causes, the most common was brain injury or head trauma (79.7%), while a very small percentage of students (4.3%) did not know any cause. Regarding symptoms, university type had no role in knowledge level, but all correct symptoms were significantly higher among clinical students (p < 0.001) (see Table 2).

Attitudes and perceptions toward epilepsy:

Ninety percent of the students have heard or read about epilepsy, and 44.4% of them have met a patient with epilepsy before. When asked whether epilepsy can occur at any age, statistically significant differences appeared in terms of gender and nationality (p-value = 0.033, 0.043), as 87.8% of female students (n = 599/682) answered 'yes', while 90.5% of Libyan students (n= 218/241) answered 'yes'. Males had a more positive attitude toward marrying a person with epilepsy compared to females (p-value = 0.020). Regarding the item “Epilepsy patients should not be accepted into medical school,” 11.4% of the students agreed, and nationality showed statistically significant differences, as 17.7% of Jordanian students supported this statement, while Libyan students were more positive, with only 4.1% agreeing (p-value < 0.001) (see Table 3).

Attitudes and perceptions toward epilepsy

Level of knowledge and awareness of emergency, medical, and surgical procedures for epilepsy:

About half of the sample (47.9%) chose that the definition of drug-resistant epilepsy is failure after using three or more drugs, compared to only 14.4% who chose the correct answer (after failure of two drugs). The effectiveness of medical treatment for epilepsy was considered “sometimes effective” by 67.4% of the students (see Table 4).

Discussion

In a systematic review that included 11 Arab countries, the median lifetime prevalence of epilepsy was estimated at 6.9 per 1,000 persons, while the median incidence rate was 89.5 per 100,000 population [12]. Although the review provided valuable regional epidemiological insights, its findings cannot be fully generalized to the entire Arab world, as it did not include all Arab countries, such as Syria and Palestine.

In this study, despite the high percentage of students who reported having knowledge about epilepsy, their responses regarding its seizure types and symptoms showed considerable variability. Only about half of the students were able to identify complex partial seizures (52.5%). In contrast, tonic-clonic seizures were the most commonly recognized type (69.6%). A similar study done on biomedical students assumed that the reason why most students recognized convulsions more than the rest of the symptoms was that they equate epilepsy with generalized tonic-clonic seizures [13]. In addition, we found that students in private universities demonstrated a higher level of knowledge compared to those in public universities. Specifically, 68.3% of private university students were aware of absence seizures, whereas 55.7% of public university students reported such knowledge. Additionally, 18.2% of students from public universities selected “I do not know” when asked about the types of epilepsy, compared to 12.1% of students from private universities. These findings suggest that private universities may place greater emphasis on education, potentially due to their educational systems, which offer more resources, increased opportunities for practical lectures, and higher student attendance rates.

Although a large number of students demonstrated a correct understanding of the causes of epilepsy, there is a small group of students who attributed epilepsy to divine punishment, with 1.4% of students. In addition, 3.8% of students assumed that the cause of epilepsy was evil spirits. In the study by Shihata et al., 9.7% of participants assumed that epilepsy is a punishment from God, and 3.7% believed that evil spirits have a role in its occurrence [10]. This may help explain the association of epilepsy with social stigma and the formation of a negative image of the condition in some Arab societies, particularly in developing countries.

Similarly, 41.3% of our participants chose psychological disorders as an etiological factor for epilepsy. In comparison, 19.7% of medical students in a study conducted in Turkey by Kartal et al. [14], 45.5% of medical students in King Abdulaziz University (by Shihata et al.) [10], and 62.7% of medical students in the University of Bahri, Sudan (by Mustafa et al.) [15] believed that psychological disorders were a cause for epilepsy. In a multi-center study conducted by Souza et al. in Brazil, United States of America, Portugal, Argentina, and South Africa, 34% of university health students believed that epilepsy was a mental illness [16]. All of the above-mentioned findings demonstrate the imperative need to increase awareness in medical students and correct popular cultural misconceptions about the etiology of epilepsy, including beliefs influenced by religion and psychological factors.

The attitudes of medical students toward patients with epilepsy varied considerably, as their responses reflected clear differences in patient acceptance and in their views on marriage, childbearing, and independent living. For example, when asked if they would agree to marry a person with epilepsy, 32.3% of males and 24.8% of females in our study said they would agree. This may reflect that emotional awareness plays a role, and marrying a patient with epilepsy may increase feelings of responsibility and fear of the future. Women may be more concerned about heredity and the risks associated with pregnancy. In contrast, a study conducted in São Paulo in Brazil by Vancini et al., where 85% of physicians said they’d maintain a relationship with someone with epilepsy and only 6% of them said they were afraid to live with someone with epilepsy [2]. On the other hand, studies indicate that epilepsy is one of the most significant neurological diseases associated with sexual dysfunction, affecting approximately two-thirds of those with the condition [17]. When addressing this concept in our survey by asking whether epilepsy patients are incapable of sexual intercourse, statistically significant differences were found between the sexes (p-value = 0.017), with 8.6% of males agreeing with this belief, compared to only 4.5% of females. Libyan students showed the lowest percentage of agreement with this statement (0.8%) compared to other nationalities, with a statistically significant difference (p < 0.001). Although sexual dysfunction is common among epilepsy patients, it cannot be definitively stated that they are completely incapable of sexual intercourse, which may explain the relatively low percentage of agreement with this belief among the sample. When comparing our findings with those of Mustafa et al. [15], who reported that 7% of medical students refused to employ a person with epilepsy in an office job, a similar pattern of professional discrimination can be observed. In our sample, 11.4% of students believed that epilepsy patients should not be accepted into medical school. Notably, Jordanian students showed the highest agreement with this statement among the four nationalities.

One of the noteworthy points not addressed in our research emerged after asking the students about the way families deal with epilepsy patients. A previous study showed that some students believed that epilepsy patients are treated in a bad and humiliating manner by their families, and the matter may reach the point of detaining them or isolating them, and they may be treated as disabled people and a burden on society [18]. In this context, it is very important to encourage people with epilepsy to engage in physical activities, as this helps them become more integrated into society [19].

According to the ILAE, drug-resistant epilepsy is defined as the persistence of seizures despite at least two syndrome-adapted antiseizure drugs (ASD) used at efficacious daily dose [20].

In our study, a large gap was found among students. 47.9% of them assumed that the correct definition is failure of treatment after three drugs (or more), while a small percentage estimated at 14.4% answered correctly that it is failure of treatment after two medications. This may indicate a gap in academic exposure to updated epilepsy guidelines. While our data cannot determine the exact cause, it is possible that some curricula do not fully reflect recent advances, which could potentially influence future clinical decision-making.

This may be even more pronounced in the educational contexts of certain countries, particularly Syria and Palestine, where the education sector faces numerous challenges, including limited research resources in recent years, directly impacted by the conflicts in these two countries [21].

The results obtained from questions assessing awareness of first aid management of epilepsy were notable. For instance, when asked to choose one or more correct first aid measures, only 76.3% of our participants chose (keep airway open), a considerably low percentage when compared to a study conducted in China by Zhao et al. [22], where 88.1% of hospital staff correctly identified it as a first aid measure. In addition, 57.8% of our participants wrongly assumed that putting something between the patient’s teeth was correct, a considerably higher percentage compared to the study conducted in Riyadh, Saudi Arabia by Abdulrahman et al. [4], where 25.5% of participants selected that answer as correct.

When students were asked about surgical treatments, significant results emerged. A result of 62.3% answered “I don’t know”. 18.3% knew about lesionectomy followed by 18.2% that knew about vagus nerve stimulation. It is notable that more advanced techniques like NeuroPace and laser ablation were known by less than 10% of students. Another study reported 51% of students that knew and chose surgical treatment for epilepsy as an effective approach [10]. However, it is important to mention that studies done on treatments of epilepsy showed that there is a risk of seizure occurrence even after the resection of the brain tumor, claiming that surrounding tissue plays a role in inducing the seizure [23].

All recent studies point to the importance of medical students developing strong reading and study habits from a young age, as this plays a crucial role in guiding them towards the most reliable sources, which help provide the best possible care for their patients [24].

Overall, this cross-sectional study sheds light on the status of epilepsy in different communities and reveals important issues that should be addressed and improved. For example, the lower adjusted odds of having positive attitudes among Jordanian students may be influenced by contextual factors not captured in our survey. Cultural or social explanations are possible, but cannot be confirmed based on our data. In contrast, the odds among Syrian and Palestinian students did not differ significantly from those of their Libyan counterparts, suggesting a general convergence of attitudes among these nationalities, which may be explained by increased societal exposure to epilepsy or by more accepting and inclusive cultural perceptions. Our study encourages further similar research in diverse cultural contexts to gain a more comprehensive understanding of the findings.

Conclusion

The results of our study demonstrated that while medical students and interns exhibited general awareness of epilepsy, remarkable gaps were noted in their knowledge of several aspects of the disease, including its etiology and management, and in their attitudes towards it. In addition, cultural and ancient beliefs seem to still play a role in influencing perceptions of epilepsy. Therefore, our study’s findings reflect the need for both enhanced medical education and better-targeted training programs for epilepsy, which would ensure optimal patient care and disease management of this highly prevalent disease.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, although the sample included a large number of medical students and interns, it was restricted to students of human medicine, which limits the generalizability of the findings to other healthcare professions that should also possess adequate awareness of epilepsy. Second, the study was conducted in only four Arab countries; therefore, the results cannot be generalized to the entire Arab region due to culturaland educational variations across countries. Finally, the use of convenience sampling and self-reported data may have introduced selection and response bias, which should be considered when interpreting the findings.
