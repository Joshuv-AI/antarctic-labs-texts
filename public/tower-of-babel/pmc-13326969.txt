Five‐Year Unified Risk Prediction Model for Gastric and Esophageal Cancer Based on Nationwide Health Check‐Up Data in Japan: J‐GESS Score
Authors: Kenta Watanabe, Sho Fukuda, Dai Kubota, Nobutake Yamamichi, Yu Takahashi, Yoshitaka Watanabe, Kyoichi Adachi, Norihisa Ishimura, Tomoyuki Koike, Hideyuki Sugawara, Kiyotaka Asanuma, Yasuhiko Abe, Takashi Kon, Eikichi Ihara, Kazuhiro Haraguchi, Yoshihiro Otsuka, Rie Yoshimura, Yugo Iwaya, Takuma Okamura, Noriaki Manabe, Akira Horiuchi, Mio Matsumoto, Kengo Onochi, So Takahashi, Tatsuki Yoshida, Yosuke Shimodaira, Katsunori Iijima
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimIn Japan, gastric cancer (GC) and esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) account for nearly all fatal upper gastrointestinal malignancies. A unified model that simultaneously predicts the incidence of these two cancers could help optimize endoscopic screening and postindex surveillance strategies.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective multicenter cohort study of 33 422 individuals who underwent screening esophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD) at 17 health check‐up institutions across Japan. Clinical and endoscopic data from the index EGD were used to develop a 5‐year risk prediction model for GC or ESCC. Significant predictors were identified using Cox regression, and a scoring system (J‐GESS score) was constructed by scaling β coefficients. Model performance was evaluated using the 5‐year inverse probability of censoring weighted (IPCW) Brier score, calibration, and c indices. Internal validation used bootstrap resampling (500 iterations) to obtain optimism‐corrected estimates. Decision curve analysis (DCA) assessed clinical utility at 5 years.ResultsAt 5 years, the IPCW‐estimated cumulative incidence of GC or ESCC was 0.84% (234 events). Independent predictors included older age, male sex, ever smoking, and endoscopic gastric mucosal atrophy. The model showed good calibration at 5 years (calibration‐in‐the‐large 0.005; slope 1.055; identical after optimism correction) and robust discrimination (Harrell's c index 0.81; Uno's 5‐year c index 0.81). DCA demonstrated positive net benefit across clinically relevant thresholds.ConclusionUsing routinely collected health check‐up data, we developed a unified, practical, and internally validated 5‐year prediction model for GC and ESCC. The J‐GESS score enables effective risk stratification and may inform postindex endoscopic surveillance and screening interval optimization in Japan.

Introduction

In Japan, where gastric cancer (GC) remains common, biennial endoscopic screening is widely conducted for individuals aged 50 and above for early detection [1]. However, the decline in GC incidence rates [2], concurrent with the decrease in Helicobacter pylori infection rates, has prompted initiatives to streamline GC screening procedures, including extending the screening intervals to 5 years for low‐risk groups [3, 4]. Meanwhile, esophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD) has been demonstrated to be a valuable diagnostic tool for the identification of additional upper gastrointestinal tract (UGIT) malignancies, including esophageal cancer (EC), in addition to GC [5]. Therefore, simplifying GC screening must consider its impact on detecting other UGIT malignancies.

EC is the second most prevalent form of cancer in the UGIT among Japanese individuals, following GC (with an incidence rate that is one fourth to one fifth that of GC) [6]. Together, EC and GC account for nearly all fatal UGIT cancers. EC is classified into esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) and esophageal adenocarcinoma (EAC) based on its histological type, each with distinct risk factors. In Japan, ESCC currently accounts for approximately 90% of ECs [7]. Consequently, within the prevailing Japanese context, the early detection of ESCC, along with GC, emerges as a pivotal objective in UGIT endoscopic screening.

Several models have been developed to predict GC [8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13] or ESCC [14, 15, 16] separately, demonstrating their utility in identifying high‐risk groups. Nevertheless, a model that can predict the incidence of both GC and ESCC simultaneously would be of significant value in identifying high‐risk groups for UGIT endoscopy screening in Japan. Most previous models relied on pre‐endoscopy factors (e.g., age, sex, smoking, and drinking), with few incorporating endoscopic findings [12, 13]. In Japan, where EGDs are presently conducted biennially, it is feasible to predict subsequent cancer risk using data that incorporate endoscopic findings at the index examination.

GC and ESCC are neoplasms arising in two adjacent organs and have many shared risk factors, including advanced age, male gender, and smoking [17]. Furthermore, mounting evidence suggests that advanced gastric mucosal atrophy (GA) functions as a risk factor not only for GC but also for ESCC [18, 19, 20, 21]. In a retrospective cohort study conducted at health screening facilities nationwide in Japan, we recently reported that a specific endoscopic finding, namely, extensive endoscopic GA, is associated with the development of ESCC, independent of other risk factors such as smoking and alcohol consumption [22]. Consequently, the development of a model capable of simultaneously predicting GC and ESCC constitutes a substantial yet feasible challenge.

This study aims to develop a 5‐year incidence prediction model (J‐GESS score: Japan Gastric cancer and Esophageal Squamous cell carcinoma Screening Score) for GC and ESCC, utilizing a retrospective cohort from 17 health screening facilities across Japan.

Methods

This retrospective, multicenter cohort study included individuals who underwent screening EGD at 17 health check‐up institutions across Japan. Details of the study protocol have been published elsewhere [23, 24]. Between January 2013 and December 2017, each institution defined an enrollment period ranging from several months to 1 year, during which all consecutive individuals who underwent screening EGD were enrolled. Eligibility required at least one follow‐up EGD by December 2022. Exclusion criteria were as follows: history of upper gastrointestinal surgery, inadequate quality of endoscopic images, absence of follow‐up EGD, or diagnosis of GC or ESCC within 1 year of the index EGD. Cases diagnosed within 1 year were excluded to minimize potential bias, as such cancers might have been suspected during the index EGD and led to early re‐examination. All cases of GC and ESCC were confirmed by histopathological examination.

Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of Akita University Hospital (Approval Number: 2946) and all participating institutions. This study adhered to the TRIPOD reporting guideline.

Data Collection

The clinical data set included information on the patient's sex, age, and drinking and smoking habits, as self‐reported by the patients at the index health check‐up. The drinking and smoking statuses were dichotomized into two categories: never and ever. Additionally, data on body mass index and waist circumference were collected based on physical measurements during the health check‐up. A comprehensive data set was also collected, encompassing the follow‐up period and the incidence of GC and ESCC.

Endoscopic Findings

At health check‐up institutions, endoscopic images are routinely and systematically recorded. Using the index endoscopic images obtained at enrollment, board‐certified endoscopists of the Japan Gastroenterological Endoscopy Society at each institution assessed and classified GA according to the Kimura‐Takemoto classification: none, mild to moderate (closed type), or extensive (open type) [25]. Images deemed inadequate quality were those that did not meet routine documentation standards sufficient to assess gastric atrophy and major upper GI pathology (e.g., insufficient anatomical coverage, motion blur, or absence of key landmarks such as angulus). Such cases were excluded.

Statistical Analysis

Missing data were handled under a missing‐at‐random assumption using multiple imputation (30 datasets) [26, 27]. Categorical and continuous variables were compared using Fisher's exact test or the chi‐square test and Mann–Whitney U test, respectively.

Time to first GC or ESCC within 5 years was modeled with Cox regression, reporting adjusted hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Within‐facility correlation was addressed using facility‐cluster robust standard errors. Predictors with p < 0.05 were retained; β coefficients were rescaled to integers to construct the J‐GESS score (0–9 points). Performance at 5 years was assessed by inverse probability of censoring weighted (IPCW) Brier score, calibration (calibration‐in‐the‐large [CITL], slope, and plots), and discrimination (Harrell's c‐index; Uno's 5‐year c index). Optimism‐corrected metrics were obtained via 500 bootstrap resamples [28]. Threshold‐wise classification indices and net benefit are assessed, and decision curve analysis (DCA) evaluated clinical utility at 5 years. Further details are provided in the Supporting Information.

Results

Study Participants

Of the 40 092 individuals who consecutively underwent screening EGD, 369 were excluded due to a history of upper gastrointestinal surgery or inadequate endoscopic image quality. An additional 6262 participants were excluded due to the absence of follow‐up EGD. Of the remaining 33 461 individuals, 39 were diagnosed with GC or ESCC within 1 year of the index EGD and were excluded to account for potential missed synchronous cancers or early re‐examinations prompted by abnormal findings. A total of 33 422 participants (median age: 54 years; 61.0% male) were ultimately included in the analysis (Figure 1). Among these, 234 events were observed; the Kaplan–Meier/IPCW‐estimated 5‐year cumulative incidence of GC or ESCC was 0.84% (95% CI, 0.74%–0.95%). Specifically, GC occurred in 193 cases (0.69%, 95% CI: 0.60%–0.79%) and ESCC in 41 cases (0.15%, 95% CI: 0.11%–0.20%).

Predictors and Scoring System Development

As shown in Table 2, multivariable Cox regression identified the following independent predictors of GC or ESCC within 5 years: age 55–64 years (adjusted HR: 2.13, 95% CI: 1.70–2.68), age ≥ 65 years (3.29, 2.56–4.22), male sex (1.85, 1.18–2.90), ever smoking (1.84, 1.11–3.04), and GA (closed‐type: 1.53, 1.13–2.06; open‐type: 5.65, 3.90–8.18). Alcohol drinking, BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2, and central obesity were not statistically significant. Based on scaled β coefficients, the 0–9 point J‐GESS score was derived (Table 2).

Internal Validation and Performance

Table 3 summarizes the J‐GESS score model performance for GC, ESCC, and the combined outcome. For the combined outcome, the IPCW Brier score at 5 years was 0.008, indicating good overall predictive accuracy. The calibration plot showed close agreement between predicted and observed risks (Figure 2A). CITL was 0.005, and the calibration slope was 1.055, with identical values after optimism correction, demonstrating near‐ideal calibration. Discrimination was robust, with a Harrell's c index of 0.81 (95% CI, 0.78–0.84). Uno's 5‐year c index was also 0.81 (0.79–0.84).

Risk Stratification and Clinical Utility

The 5‐year incidence increased monotonically with higher J‐GESS scores (Table 4). Threshold‐wise classification performance and net benefit are provided in Table S2. In practice, a score of ≥ 4 identifies a broad higher‐risk stratum with high sensitivity, whereas ≥ 6 delineates a narrower very‐high‐risk group; threshold selection should reflect screening capacity and program priorities. Decision curves showed consistently positive net benefit for the J‐GESS score across clinically relevant thresholds versus treat‐all or treat‐none strategies, supporting its use to prioritize EGD resources (the decision‐relevant range in screening corresponds to scores 1–8; Figure 3).

Discussion

We developed and internally validated a unified 5‐year risk model (J‐GESS score) for incident GC and ESCC using routinely collected health check‐up data across 17 centers in Japan. The model showed good calibration at 5 years (CITL 0.005; slope 1.055) and robust discrimination (Harrell's c index 0.81), and DCA indicated positive net benefit across clinically relevant thresholds. Given that EGD is delivered as a single screening modality in Japan and that GC and ESCC share major risk factors (age, sex, smoking, and endoscopic gastric atrophy) [17, 18, 19, 20, 21, 22], a unified prediction framework is both clinically and operationally relevant for prioritizing endoscopic resources. To our knowledge, this is the first study to develop a unified model for predicting both GC and ESCC incidence. In Japan, these two cancers account for nearly all fatal malignancies of the UGIT, underscoring the potential utility of the J‐GESS score in optimizing screening EGD strategy.

GC and ESCC share several epidemiological phenomena in common. Globally, the incidence of both cancers is declining, and similar downward trends have been observed across specific sociodemographic subgroups [29]. At the individual level, GC and ESCC often coexist, either concurrently or metachronously [30, 31, 32]. These parallel trends are likely due not only to shared demographic risk factors, such as older age and male sex, but also to shared environmental risk factors, including smoking and GA, the latter primarily induced by H. pylori infection [17, 18, 19, 20, 21, 22]. Notably, the ESCC‐specific model achieved a c statistic of 0.77, despite being derived from a cohort in which GC accounted for the majority of cancer cases (82%). This likely reflects shared risk factors, supporting a unified prediction approach.

When the performance of the J‐GESS score was evaluated separately for GC or ESCC, its predictive ability for ESCC was slightly lower than that for GC. This is plausible because key ESCC determinants, particularly heavy alcohol consumption in combination with alcohol flushing phenotype/ALDH2 genotype, were not available in our routine screening dataset [14, 33, 34]. Individuals with heavy drinking and alcohol flushing—1%–2% of the Japanese population [35]—may represent a distinct high‐risk group, identifiable via a targeted questionnaire. This approach would enable the separate identification of individuals at elevated risk for ESCC due to alcohol‐related genetic and behavioral factors, which are not captured by the present model. The present score was intentionally designed around routinely collectible variables to enhance implementability in health check‐up settings; future extensions that incorporate alcohol‐related phenotypes/genotypes via brief questionnaires may improve ESCC‐specific discrimination without sacrificing feasibility.

Several GC risk models have been proposed in East Asian populations (e.g., incorporating serologic markers such as H. pylori /pepsinogens, lifestyle factors, or detailed questionnaires) [8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13]. Direct head‐to‐head benchmarking was not feasible in our cohort because key predictors required by those tools were not uniformly available across the 17 centers and could not be reconstructed without introducing selective exclusions. Conceptually, our score differs by (i) providing a unified framework for GC and ESCC and (ii) leveraging an endoscopic marker (GA) routinely captured at screening EGD, thereby facilitating postindex surveillance planning. In lieu of model‐to‐model comparisons, we report cancer‐specific discrimination/calibration and DCA to demonstrate clinical utility within the constraints of a nationwide health check‐up dataset.

Because the degree of GA significantly contributes to the J‐GESS score and most GA in Japan is attributable to H. pylori [36], we compared their relative importance (Table S3). In univariable analysis, both H. pylori infection status (positive or eradicated) and endoscopic GA were significantly associated with the combined outcome of GC or ESCC. However, in multivariable analysis, only endoscopic GA remained a significant predictor. These findings suggest that endoscopic GA captures residual risk beyond infection status in contemporary posteradication practice. In Japan, where eradication therapy is widely performed and covered by national health insurance for all H. pylori –positive individuals [36], eradication therapy is typically initiated once infection is confirmed. Although eradication reduces GC risk by around 50% [37], cancer risk remains and depends on the extent of GA [38, 39]. Therefore, in screened populations, GA may be a more reliable GC risk predictor than H. pylori status. This pattern seems to extend to ESCC as well, as demonstrated in our previous study [22]. Operationally, GA is ascertainable at the index EGD and can directly inform postindex surveillance planning.

Although GC and ESCC differ in pathogenesis, a unified framework is justified in Japan where EGD is implemented as a single screening modality and the two cancers share major risk factors [17, 18, 19, 20, 21, 22]. In line with this rationale, calibration remained near‐ideal and cancer‐specific discrimination was acceptable, whereas DCA demonstrated positive net benefit across clinically relevant thresholds, supporting the clinical utility of a unified approach for prioritizing EGD resources. Thresholds should be selected according to screening capacity and program priorities. We therefore report threshold‐wise metrics and net benefit; although a score ≥ 4 can be used to define a broader higher‐risk stratum and ≥ 6 a narrower very‐high‐risk stratum, programs may select alternative cut‐points according to local resource constraints and priorities (Table S2).

One of the key strengths of this study is its use of a representative sample from across Japan, incorporating data from 17 endoscopic medical check‐up facilities nationwide. Furthermore, the retrospective cohort design includes all cases of GC and ESCC diagnosed at these facilities over a 5‐year period. J‐GESS score components were weighted by regression coefficients derived from the multivariable model predicting GC and ESCC incidence. However, if the incidence rates of GC and ESCC change in the future, the weighting of predictors may need to be recalibrated to maintain the model's accuracy and applicability. Methodologically, we used Cox models to accommodate variable follow‐up and censoring and applied facility‐cluster robust standard errors to account for intracenter correlation arising from shared endoscopic workflows and assessment practices. Together with bootstrap optimism correction and decision‐analytic evaluation, these choices mitigate overoptimism and enhance robustness to center‐level heterogeneity.

This study has several limitations. First, the current J‐GESS score does not account for the occurrence of EAC. Although EAC currently comprises approximately 10% of ECs in Japan, its incidence is rising and may warrant consideration in future models [40, 41]. Integrating EAC and GC into a unified model is not advisable due to their differing risk profiles—for example, GC is positively associated with GA, whereas EAC is negatively associated with it [42]. The risk of developing EAC is primarily determined by the length of Barrett's esophagus, a well‐established precursor lesion [23, 43]. Therefore, individuals with long‐segment Barrett's esophagus identified during the index endoscopy—estimated to represent 0.2% of the general Japanese population [23]—may be classified as a separate high‐risk group, outside the scope of the J‐GESS score. Second, external validation was not performed; although our cohort spans 17 nationwide health check‐up centers and approximates the intended target population [44], validation in other East Asian settings remains a priority (e.g., Korea and China, where GC and ESCC remain prevalent) [45, 46]. Third, selection bias inherent to health check‐up cohorts is possible; nevertheless, internal performance was consistent, and decision curves showed positive net benefit across relevant thresholds. Fourth, interoperator variability in assessing endoscopic gastric atrophy (GA) cannot be excluded; to enhance reproducibility, we used a three‐tier categorization (none/closed/open) and applied facility‐cluster robust standard errors to mitigate center‐level heterogeneity. Fifth, several potentially relevant predictors (e.g., family history, diet, reflux‐related findings, and alcohol flushing phenotype/ALDH2 genotype) were unavailable uniformly across centers; this was a deliberate design choice to ensure implementability based on routinely collectible screening data. In a sensitivity analysis additionally adjusting for H. pylori status, effect estimates were directionally consistent with the primary model (Table S3). Sixth, the overall event rate was low, which may limit precision at the extreme tail of predicted risk. Accordingly, we focused on 5‐year IPCW calibration and provided threshold‐wise performance and net benefit. Notably, DCA showed positive net benefit across the decision‐relevant range (scores 1–8), supporting practical usefulness (Table S2 and Figure 3). Finally, although GC and ESCC have etiologic differences, a unified framework was adopted because EGD is delivered as a single screening modality and the cancers share major risk factors; cancer‐specific Cox models retained acceptable discrimination, but the score should primarily guide risk‐based prioritization of postindex EGD surveillance, with thresholds (e.g., ≥ 4 for broader higher‐risk and ≥ 6 for narrower very‐high‐risk) chosen according to local capacity and program priorities.

In summary, the J‐GESS score is a simple, well‐calibrated, and discriminative tool derived from routinely obtainable variables, enabling risk‐based prioritization of EGD in Japan. By identifying a high‐risk stratum with substantially elevated 5‐year incidence, it offers a practical basis for postindex surveillance scheduling and resource allocation, pending external validation.
