Identifying toxicological effects of perfluoroalkyl and polyfluoroalkyl substances exposure on osteoarthritis
Authors: Zhenxing Hou, Yanzheng Gao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Perfluoroalkyl and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS) pose a significant challenge due to their persistence, bioaccumulation, and multi-system toxicity. Their impact on degenerative diseases, particularly osteoarthritis (OA), remains understudied, necessitating comprehensive toxicity assessment. Multiple databases identified targets between PFAS and OA. Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses were performed to elucidate the underlying mechanisms. Machine learning further screened targets, and molecular docking confirmed strong binding affinities between PFAS and core targets. 31 targets between PFAS and OA were identified through the multiple databases, with GO analysis revealing enrichment in inflammatory response, nuclear receptor activity, and oxidative stress. KEGG pathway enrichment analyses implicated PI3K-Akt, interleukin-17 (IL-17), and peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor (PPAR) signaling in cartilage homeostasis disruption. Machine learning further identified nine hub genes, and molecular docking confirmed strong binding affinities between PFAS and these core targets. RT-qPCR analyses indicated that PFAS may exacerbate OA via the following mechanisms: impairing ABCB1-mediated detoxification, antagonizing ESR1-mediated cartilage protection, down-regulating FGFR2 to suppress chondrocyte survival, and inhibiting DNMT1-mediated methylation to disrupt chondrocyte phenotypic stability. This study provides new insights into the potential impact of PFAS on OA and highlights the need for further investigation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-28013-6.

Introduction

PFAS are a class of man-made chemicals, including perfluorooctanesulfonic acid (PFOS), perfluorooctanoic acid (PFOA), and perfluorohexanesulfonic acid (PFHxS). Due to their heat resistance, water-repellency and stain resistance, they are widely used in everyday life, such as in waterproof and oil-repellent fabrics for clothing and sofas, and food packaging1. However, PFAS are extremely persistent in the natural environment, with characteristics such as persistence, long-range transport, and bioaccumulation2. Despite their long-term adverse effects on environmental organisms and human health, they have not yet received sufficient attention.

Recent studies3,4 have revealed that PFAS pose a wide range of potential toxicities to human health, including hepatotoxicity, neurotoxicity and pulmonary toxicity. Moreover, exposure to PFAS has been linked to endocrine disruption5, impaired fertility6, weakened immune function7, and an increased risk of certain cancers8, thyroid diseases9 and liver damage10. It may also elevate cholesterol levels, thereby indirectly heightening the risk of cardiovascular diseases11. Given the persistence and bioaccumulative nature of PFAS, these substances can remain in the environment and human body for extended periods, leading to long-term health risks. The widespread use of PFAS in industrial and consumer products, coupled with their resistance to degradation, has resulted in persistent environmental contamination. The presence of PFAS underscores the urgent need for effective strategies to mitigate their release into the environment and reduce human exposure.

OA is a degenerative joint disease that primarily affects the hip and interphalangeal joints, often leading to joint pain and stiffness12. According to the latest data, in 2019 approximately 528 million people worldwide suffered from OA, an increase of 113% since 199013. With the exacerbation of population aging and the annual increase in obesity rates, the global prevalence of OA is expected to further increase14. The consequence of OA is not only in the pain, stiffness, and mobility impairment of the affected joints, but more importantly, reduced physical activity can trigger other diseases such as cardiovascular disease, obesity, and diabetes12. Although exercise, healthy diet, drug therapy, or joint replacement surgery can alleviate clinical symptoms to some extent, there is currently no effective treatment to completely stop or reverse the progression of OA.

To fundamentally improve the quality of life for OA patients and delay disease progression, a more comprehensive understanding of the molecular mechanisms underlying OA is needed. OA has been proven to be influenced by age, gender, obesity, and joint injury15–17. However, the role of specific environmental factors has been rarely reported. Although there are few direct studies on the relationship between PFAS and OA, existing reports have suggested a potential association between PFAS and joint diseases. A Study has found that PFOS and PFOA are detected in bones, and these compounds may affect bone metabolism by influencing the function of osteoclasts18. This highlights the necessity for further research on the impact of PFAS on bone health. Zhao et al.19 also suggested that PFAS may affect the development of joint diseases by influencing inflammatory responses and immune regulation. Moreover, a study involving 49,432 participants has confirmed a significant positive linear association between serum PFOA levels and diagnosed OA, with PFOA potentially increasing OA risk by promoting inflammation and affecting metabolism20. These studies provide an important basis for future in-depth exploration of the relationship between PFAS and OA. Based on the reported research, we hypothesize that PFAS exposure-related toxicity promotes OA onset and progression through multiple pathways. Given the lack of prior studies in this area, our research represents the first attempt to explore this relationship comprehensively.

This study integrates network pharmacology, machine learning, and molecular docking to thoroughly investigate how PFAS influence the onset and progression of OA, and to elucidate their potential mechanisms in disease development. By constructing a PFAS-target-OA interaction network, we screened core pathways related to PFAS exposure and OA; further identified key feature molecules using machine learning algorithms and established a PFAS exposure-related OA prediction model; and finally, validated the binding affinity between PFAS and core targets through molecular docking. By identifying biological markers associated with PFAS exposure, this study provides potential detection indicators for environmentally induced OA. Additionally, it offers insights and methodologies that may be valuable for further research in the field of environmental health, contributing to environmental management and public health considerations.

Results

Data preprocessing and toxicity analysis

The workflow diagram is shown in Fig. 1. First, to prepare for the identification of OA-related target genes, datasets GSE51588 and GSE98918 were merged and batch-corrected. Boxplot results showed a significant reduction in expression level differences between samples of the two datasets after correction (Fig. 2A, B). Subsequently, PCA analysis was performed to reduce data dimensions and preprocess the data. The results showed homogeneity between the two datasets (Fig. 2C, D). Therefore, samples from different datasets became more comparable after batch correction and PCA. Additionally, the basic information of PFOS, PFOA, and PFHxS, including their chemical formulas and SMILES structures, is provided in (Supplementary Table 1). Toxicity predictions for the three compounds showed that they primarily cause hepatotoxicity, neurotoxicity, and eye corrosion (Supplementary Table 2).

Identification of OA target genes related to PFAS

Differential expression analysis was conducted on the merged GSE51588 and GSE98918 datasets to obtain OA target genes. A volcano plot showed 800 DEGs, including 471 upregulated genes and 329 downregulated genes (Fig. 2E). After strict filtering and quality control of the targets (retaining only those from GeneCards with a relevance score > 30), we sought a more comprehensive OA target set by integrating the differentially expressed genes with the genes catalogued in GeneCards, OMIM, and TTD, followed by removal of duplicates, resulting in a total of 1456 OA-related target genes. Additionally, after removing duplicates, 445 PFAS target genes were obtained from the ChEMBL, TargetNet, and STICH databases. Finally, the PFAS target genes were intersected with the OA-related target genes, yielding 31 target genes (Fig. 2F).

GO and KEGG analysis of target genes

GO analysis was performed on the 31 potential target genes, with the species restricted to “Homo Sapiens.” The 30 GO terms were ranked based on false discovery rate (FDR), and the top 10 terms in BP, CC, and MF with the lowest FDR were plotted as bubble chart (Fig. 3A). The BP results indicated that OA related to PFAS is closely associated with inflammatory response, cell signaling, response to microbes, and oxygen and nitric oxide metabolism. The MF results showed that OA related to PFAS may involve the regulation of various transcription factors and nuclear receptors. These regulations may affect gene expression, cell proliferation, differentiation, and metabolic processes, thereby influencing the function of articular cartilage and synovial tissue. The CC enrichment suggested that OA related to PFAS may involve structural changes in the cell membrane, remodeling of the extracellular matrix, and release of intracellular granules. These changes may be related to inflammatory response, cell signaling, and cell-to-cell interactions. In summary, PFAS exposure may induce OA development by affecting multiple aspects, including inflammatory response, cell signaling, cell proliferation, metabolic processes, and gene regulation.

Protein-protein interaction (PPI) network and hub genes

The PPI network of 29 target proteins was constructed using the STRING 12.0 database after removing isolated nodes (Fig. 3C). The more connections a node has in the network, the more interactions it has with other nodes. Additionally, the target genes were imported into Cytoscape to construct a PFAS-target-OA interaction network (Supplementary Fig. 1) and highlighted the top 5 core genes with large nodes and red color to further identify hub genes (Fig. 3D).

Machine learning to further screen hub genes

To further screen hub genes, 113 machine learning prediction models were constructed for the 29 target genes (Fig. 4A). The scores of the machine learning models were calculated using linear combination functions. Subsequently, the model scores were used to calculate the AUC to assess model performance. The algorithm with the highest average AUC, “glmBoost + XGBoost,” was selected as the best prediction model, and both the training and validation groups showed good testing performance in the ROC curves (Fig. 4B-E). Additionally, 12 hub genes corresponding to the best model were obtained, and the 9 most significantly different genes were retained (ABCB1, ACSL6, AHR, DNMT1, ESR1, ESR2, FGFR2, GRIK3, HMMR). Boxplots (Fig. 4F) showed significant differences in gene expression between the control and experimental groups, and ROC curves further verified the robustness of the model (Fig. 4G). Finally, correlation analysis was conducted on the 9 hub genes to identify regulatory relationships between genes. Among them, ABCB1 and AHR (cor 0.47), ACSL6 and AHR (cor − 0.50), AHR and ESR2 (cor − 0.47), ESR1 and ESR2 (cor 0.47), and AHR and HMMR (cor 0.46) exhibited strong significance (P < 0.001) (Fig. 4H).

Molecular docking of core target proteins with PFOS, PFOA, and PFHxS

Based on the feature gene analysis of the best machine learning model, 4 key target proteins with the most significant gene expression and the highest AUC values were carefully selected (ABCB1, DNMT1, ESR1 and FGFR2). Subsequently, molecular docking was performed between these target proteins and the three typical PFAS compounds (PFOS, PFOA, and PFHxS). During molecular docking, the binding sites between each target protein and the compounds were carefully evaluated, and ultimately, the sites with the lowest binding energy were selected as the final molecular docking results (Fig. 5A-C). The results showed that all molecular docking vina scores were less than − 7 (Supplementary Table 3), indicating high binding activity and stability between the target proteins and PFAS compounds. This finding not only underscores the pivotal roles of these target proteins in PFAS-driven OA pathogenesis but also provides a structural basis for future intervention strategies aimed at disrupting the detrimental PFAS–protein interactions or restoring normal protein function in PFAS-induced OA.

Validation of OA phenotype induction

To confirm that TNF-α successfully induced an OA-like phenotype, we measured the mRNA levels of established OA markers by RT-qPCR. C28/I2 cells were treated with 10 ng/mL TNF-α for 24 h. Compared with untreated controls, TNF-α stimulation significantly up-regulated A Disintegrin and Metalloproteinase with Thrombospondin Motifs 5 (ADAMTS5) (p < 0.0001), but Aggrecan (ACAN) expression was substantially reduced (p = 0.0002). The expression levels of Interleukin-6 (IL-6) (p < 0.0001) and Interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β) (p = 0.0002) were both markedly increased (Fig. 6A).These changes are consistent with a typical OA-like phenotype, validating the TNF-α induction model.

CCK-8 assay results

CCK-8 assay results revealed that all three PFAS compounds reduced C28/I2 cell viability in a dose-dependent manner. PFOA, PFHxS, and PFOS exhibited IC₅₀ values of 134.8 µg/mL, 204.2 µg/mL, and 305.1 µg/mL (Fig. 6B), respectively. For the IC₅₀ fitting curves of the three PFAS compounds, the coefficient of determination (R²) was greater than 0.9 in each case, indicating a high goodness-of-fit.

Validation of hub genes by RT-qPCR

To verify the predicted hub genes, OA-like C28/I2 cells were treated with the three PFAS compounds for 24 h. Expression levels of ESR1, DNMT1, FGFR2 and ABCB1 were then quantified by RT-qPCR (Fig. 6C). As shown in Fig. 6, ABCB1 mRNA in the PFHxS group was slightly—but not significantly—lower than in the vehicle control. By contrast, the remaining PFAS treatments markedly down-regulated all four genes compared with the control (p < 0.05). These results confirm that the four hub genes remain lowly expressed in the OA-like phenotype after PFAS exposure.

Discussion

This study systematically integrated network pharmacology, machine learning, and molecular docking techniques to deeply explore the molecular mechanisms by which PFAS promotes progression of OA. First, we constructed a PFAS-target-OA interaction network using network pharmacology methods to screen core pathways related to PFAS exposure and OA. Subsequently, we further identified key feature molecules using machine learning algorithms and established a PFAS exposure-related OA prediction model. Based on this, we selected 4 target proteins with the most significant gene expression and the highest AUC values (ABCB1, DNMT1, ESR1, and FGFR2) and performed molecular docking with PFOS, PFOA, and PFHxS. The results showed that all molecular docking indicated high binding activity between the target proteins and PFAS compounds. These findings revealed key targets and potential mechanisms of PFAS-induced OA, providing a scientific basis for the development of prevention and treatment strategies for PFAS-induced OA in the future.

GO and KEGG enrichment analyses indicate that PFAS exposure and OA share common enrichments in processes such as inflammatory regulation, nuclear-receptor activity, and the response to oxygen levels. Regarding inflammation, PFAS may down-regulate ABCB1-mediated efflux while simultaneously elevating IL-6 and COX-2 expression, implying amplification of inflammatory signaling24; however, direct causal links to cartilage degradation remain to be experimentally validated. At the nuclear-receptor level, ESR1’s established role in maintaining chondrocyte survival and ECM homeostasis suggests that PFAS, as a potential endocrine disruptor, merits further investigation for its interaction with ESR125. In the KEGG results, enrichment of the IL-17, PPAR, and estrogen signaling pathways aligns with existing OA research26,27. IL-17–mediated immune-cell recruitment and inflammatory amplification have been reported in OA, and PFAS may participate in these processes by modulating this pathway27; PPARγ’s anti-inflammatory and antioxidant functions in cartilage protection are well recognized; the possible impact of PFAS on PPARγ activity warrants future study to determine whether PFAS undermines PPARγ-mediated protective effects26; For estrogen signaling, the likelihood that PFAS interacts with ESR1/ESR2 implies involvement in estrogen-related regulation, though additional evidence is needed to clarify the exact mode of action. In summary, PFAS may regulate inflammation, metabolism, and immune responses in OA through a complex interaction network involving these signaling pathways. Interventions targeting these pathways may become new directions for OA treatment.

To more clearly elucidate the complex interactions among hub proteins, this study constructed a PPI network to identify genes that may be associated with the pathogenesis of OA. MMP9, a matrix metalloproteinase, may be involved in the degradation of articular cartilage, thereby promoting the development of OA28. A study29 has shown that IL10 may protect against OA by suppressing inflammatory responses and regulating immune cell functions. However, Moulin et al. found that IL10 expression levels are reduced in the synovial fluid of OA patients, which may lead to a decrease in its protective effects30. Estrogen regulates inflammatory response and cartilage metabolism through ESR1 to slow OA progression31, but PFAS may interfere with this process. In summary, these hub genes may play different roles in the pathogenesis of PFAS-induced OA by participating in processes such as inflammatory response, cartilage degradation, and immune regulation.

In this study, we further examined the intersecting genes between the three compounds and OA target genes using machine learning, identifying 9 hub genes. We also explored the potential immunoreactive relationships among these genes and observed notable correlations. The negative correlation between the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AHR) and ESR2 suggests a possible mechanism where AHR might attenuate the protective effects of estrogen on cartilage by inhibiting ESR2 signaling32. The positive correlation between ESR1 and ESR2 indicates that they may jointly modulate estrogen signaling, potentially activating pathways that inhibit chondrocyte apoptosis and promote collagen and proteoglycan synthesis for cartilage protection33. FGFR2 appears to be overactivated in processes promoting chondrocyte hypertrophy and ECM degradation. The positive correlation of FGFR2 with AHR and HMMR suggests that AHR activation might upregulate FGFR2 expression, possibly leading to abnormal proliferation of chondrocytes34. Overall, AHR, ESR1/ESR2, FGFR2, and HMMR may regulate inflammation, metabolism, and ECM homeostasis through a complex interaction network, which could represent a key aspect of the pathogenesis of OA induced by PFAS.

Ultimately, by integrating various methods to screen target genes, we identified 4 key target genes: ABCB1, DNMT1, ESR1, and FGFR2. Molecular docking results showed that these target genes play crucial roles in the progression of PFAS-induced OA. It is well-established that ABCB1 actively effluxes xenobiotics to modulate systemic drug disposition, directly influencing pharmacokinetic processes such as absorption and clearance35. Extrapolating from this mechanism, we hypothesize that PFAS may exert chondrotoxic effects in OA chondrocytes via downregulation of ABCB1 mRNA expression. This transcriptional suppression would compromise ABCB1-mediated efflux activity, leading to impaired PFAS extrusion and subsequent intracellular accumulation. Progressive elevation of intracellular PFAS concentrations may then induce sustained cellular damage, contributing to the exacerbation of OA pathophysiology. Furthermore, RT-qPCR results (Fig. 6C) from this study showed that ABCB1 was also significantly upregulated in chondrocytes with an OA phenotype after PFOS exposure, which further supports the above hypothesis. A study36 has confirmed that DNMT1 can suppress the expression of type II collagen–encoding genes in chondrocytes through methylation modification in OA. PFOA exposure has also been associated with altered DNA methylation levels in multiple placental genes37. It is hypothesized that the binding of PFAS to DNMT1 may induce low methylation, leading to chondrocyte apoptosis and the development of OA. This hypothesis was further supported by the PCR results obtained in this study (Fig. 6C).

ESR1 regulates the proliferation of chondrocytes and matrix synthesis (such as collagen and proteoglycan) to maintain cartilage homeostasis38. A study39 has shown that in the presence of estrogen, PFOA and PFOS can bind to the coactivator-binding region of ESR1, thereby inhibiting the binding of estrogen to ESR1. This antagonism of estrogen’s physiological effects may lead to abnormal subchondral bone remodeling and inflammatory responses, ultimately contributing to the development of OA. Additionally, the low expression of ESR1 in chondrocytes exposed to PFAS observed in the RT-qPCR experiment (Fig. 6C) is consistent with previous reports. A study40 has shown that FGFR2 knockout mice exhibit accelerated progression of both spontaneous and surgically induced OA, while exogenous FGF-2 supplementation can inhibit ADAMTS-5 activity and delay cartilage degradation. It is hypothesized that PFAS may interfere with FGFR2 expression and thereby promote OA progression, a finding that was also confirmed in this study (Fig. 6C).

The limitations of this study are as follows: First, the screening of target genes related to PFAS and OA relies on the predictive associations from public databases and computational simulation analyses. Although the reliability of the predicted targets has been preliminarily verified through molecular docking and PCR experiments, further mechanistic studies are still needed for confirmation. Second, when integrating associations from multiple databases, differences in gene naming and functional annotation systems across databases may lead to misalignment in target definitions. Moreover, database entries often reflect correlations rather than causal relationships. In the future, we will improve this by exploring potential mechanisms, employing causal inference methods, and combining orthogonal datasets for cross-validation. Third, the integration of heterogeneous datasets may introduce potential biases. Although the SVA method has been used to correct batch effects, differences in sample characteristics may still affect the robustness of the analysis. Meanwhile, the limitations of some datasets and the insufficient coverage of PFAS target databases restrict the universality and completeness of the results. In the future, we will incorporate larger-scale, multi-center, and standardized datasets and expand the target screening scope using updated databases. Finally, in the machine learning process, selecting models based on AUC may lead to feature selection bias, and the algorithms used are prone to bias towards high-variance features while ignoring low-abundance but functionally critical targets.

Materials and methods

Toxicity analysis of PFAS

PFOS, PFOA, and PFHxS are three common types of PFAS. First, the SMILES sequences of these three compounds were retrieved and downloaded from the PubChem database41 (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). Subsequently, the toxicity prediction results of the compounds were obtained by searching the sequences in ADMETlab 2.042 (https://admetmesh.scbdd.com/) and ProTox (https://tox.charite.de/). ADMETlab 2.0 reads SMILES to extract physicochemical descriptors and substructures, matches eight toxicophore rule sets, performs multi-task learning across 27 endpoints and outputs toxicity class, LD50 and confidence; ProTox first standardizes SMILES, extracts topological features and toxic fragments, pre-screens against a toxicity library, then applies naive Bayes, random forest and other models that incorporate Tox21 pathway data and pharmacophore models to predict molecular initiating events and organ toxicities, finally returning radar plots, network graphs and confidence scores; ChEMBL uses measured IC₅₀, Ki, EC₅₀ to reflect direct binding affinity, filters by pChEMBL ≥ 5, confidence ≥ 8 and human species; TargetNet employs deep learning on structural features to predict binding with pKi ≥ 6, confidence ≥ 0.7 and human species; STITCH integrates multi-source evidence into a composite score, keeps interactions ≥ 0.400 medium confidence and human-specific, discarding associations with unrelated compounds, with the complete PFAS target list in Supplementary Table 4 annotated by source and evidence type.

Acquisition of target genes for PFAS

The names and molecular formulas of PFOS, PFOA, and PFHxS were retrieved from the PubChem database. PFAS target genes refer to genes with potential interactions with PFAS. Target genes were then searched for in the ChEMBL database (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/chembl/), TargetNet database (http://targetnet.scbdd.com/), and the STITCH database43 (http://stitch.embl.de/), restricting the species to “Homo sapiens.” The targets obtained from ChEMBL ID were standardized utilizing the UniProt database (https://www.uniprot.org/). Finally, the targets obtained from the three databases were combined, duplicates were removed, and the potential targets for PFAS were obtained.

Acquisition of OA-related targets

OA target genes refer to genes associated with the onset and progression of osteoarthritis. First, the GeneCards database (https://www.genecards.org/), OMIM database (https://www.omim.org/), and TTD database (https://db.idrblab.net/ttd/) were searched for “osteoarthritis” and “degenerative joint disease”. Genes with a prediction score greater than 30 in the GeneCards database were considered as potential targets, and duplicates were removed from the targets in the three databases. Additionally, OA was searched for in the GEO database, and five datasets were obtained: GSE51588, GSE98918, GSE117999, GSE55235, and GSE46750. The datasets were annotated using R (version 4.4.2). Datasets GSE51588 and GSE98918 were used as the training group, while GSE117999, GSE55235, and GSE46750 were used as the validation group. The GSE51588, GSE46750, and GSE98918 datasets comprise 50, 24, and 24 samples, respectively. The GSE117999 dataset originally lists 24 samples ; however, because the raw data for four samples (GSM3316970, GSM3316972, GSM3316976 and GSM3316980) were not uploaded to the GEO database by the original authors, only 20 samples were ultimately included. The GSE55235 dataset contains 30 samples; after excluding 10 samples from rheumatoid-arthritis patients, 20 samples were ultimately retained. To improve the accuracy and reliability of data analysis, batch effects were removed using the SVA method44. The expression of sample data before and after batch correction was visualized using boxplots and principal component analysis (PCA).

Screening of OA targets related to PFAS

The integrated OA dataset was analyzed using the Limma package in R45. Additionally, the expression of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) related to OA was analyzed using the R package limma. DEGs were filtered with the conditions Adj. P < 0.05 and |logFC| > 0.585, and the results were displayed in a volcano plot. Subsequently, the DEGs were merged with the OA targets obtained from the three online databases, duplicates were removed to obtain the total OA targets. Finally, the total OA targets were intersected with the main chemical targets of PFAS to identify potential targets for PFAS-induced OA. The results were presented in a Venn diagram.

Functional and pathway enrichment analysis

To identify the biological process (BP), cellular component (CC), and molecular function (MF) involved in the potential targets, we conducted GO analysis to reveal the potential biological mechanisms of PFAS-induced OA. Additionally, to further understand the connection between the potential targets and related metabolic pathways, KEGG21–23 pathway enrichment analysis was conducted. Both GO and KEGG analyses were performed using the R package clusterProfiler46, with Adj. P < 0.05 set to identify the main gene functions and pathways involved in PFAS-induced OA. Finally, the results of the GO and KEGG analyses were visualized using bubble charts and horizontal bar charts.

PPI analysis and screening of hub targets

To identify key proteins closely related to PFAS-induced OA, the intersecting genes obtained from the Venn diagram were uploaded to the STRING database, with the species restricted to “Homo sapiens” and the “Minimum Required Interaction Score” set to “Medium Confidence > 0.4.” The PPI network corresponding to the target genes was then obtained. Additionally, to further reflect the topological properties of the PPI and other metadata information, the results from the STRING database were imported into Cytoscape software (Version 3.10.3)47. By visualizing the results, hub genes with core regulatory functions in the network were identified, which can serve as potential biomarkers for PFAS-induced OA. Finally, a PFAS-target-OA interaction network was constructed to reveal the molecular association mechanisms between PFAS and OA.

Hub gene selection through 113 machine learning algorithm models

To identify hub genes associated with PFAS-induced OA, the Venn-derived intersecting genes were used as the feature set. The training cohort combined GSE51588 and GSE98918 (74 samples: 52 OA vs. 22 controls), and three independent cohorts—GSE117999, GSE55235 and GSE46750 (64 samples in total)—served as the validation set. After batch-effect removal with the ComBat function (sva package), the intersecting expression matrices were Z-score standardized. One hundred and thirteen diagnostic models were built by pairwise combining 11 algorithms (SVM, Ridge, glmBoost, Lasso, Stepglm, plsRglm, RF, GBM, LDA, XGBoost and NaiveBayes). Machine learning algorithm parameters and validation can be found in (Supplementary Table 5). Each model underwent five-fold cross-validation-based feature selection in the training set, retaining ≥ 5 variables. Model training, probability calculation and class prediction were implemented with an in-house R script. The final model was selected according to the mean Area Under the Curve (AUC) across training and validation sets, and its signature genes were extracted as hub genes. Receiver Operating Characteristic Curves (ROC), boxplots and Spearman correlation analyses were applied to evaluate model discriminative power, hub-gene expression differences and their regulatory interplay. Additionally, the Enet, GBM, XGBoost, and glmboost models have incorporated cross-validation or early stopping mechanisms to mitigate overfitting.

Molecular docking of PFAS with core target proteins

To elucidate the impact of PFAS toxicity on OA hub genes (Hub genes are potential targets associated with PFAS-induced OA), molecular docking was conducted to predict the binding modes and affinities between PFOS, PFOA, PFHxS, and core target proteins. First, the 3D structures of PFOS, PFOA, and PFHxS were downloaded from the PubChem database, and the co-crystal structures of protein targets were obtained from the RCSB PDB database (http://www.rcsb.org/). Subsequently, the pdb files of proteins and the sdf files of the 3D structures of the three compounds were uploaded to the CB-DOCK2 website48 (https://cadd.labshare.cn/cb-dock2/index.php). Blind docking was performed and the predicted binding sites and affinities were obtained. A lower binding energy (such as the Vina score) indicates a stronger interaction between the protein and the ligand, which is more likely to correspond to the binding state observed experimentally49. Consequently, the protein-ligand binding pose with the lowest vina score was selected as the best predicted pose. These best predicted poses were then imported into Discovery Studio 2019 (Version 19.1) for visualization to obtain high-resolution 3D and 2D diagrams of molecular docking.

Cell culture

The C28/I2 human chondrocyte cell line was purchased from Shanghai YaJi Biological Technology Co., Ltd. Cells were first cultured in complete DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS. After 48 h at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂, the medium was removed, cells were gently washed twice with PBS, and OA-like conditions were induced by adding fresh medium containing 10 ng/mL TNF-α for 24 h505152. Upon reaching 85% confluence, the cells were dissociated with 0.25% trypsin (Beyotime Biotech, Inc.) and subcultured.

CCK-8 assay

CCK-8 was employed to evaluate the potential cytotoxicity of three PFAS compounds on C28/I2 chondrocytes with an OA-like phenotype. C28/I2 cells were first primed with 10 ng/mL TNF-α for 24 h to establish an OA phenotype (verified by RT-qPCR), then seeded into 96-well plates at 1 × 10⁴ cells/well and allowed to attach overnight. The next day the medium was aspirated, and cells were exposed to 8–10 serial dilutions of PFOS, PFOA or PFHxS prepared in serum-free DMEM (six replicates per concentration) for 24 h at 37 °C, 5% CO₂. After removal of the compound-containing medium, 100 µL CCK-8 working solution (CCK-8 : serum-free DMEM = 1 : 10) was added to each well, incubation was continued for 2 h, and absorbance at 450 nm was measured with a Multiskan FC microplate reader (Thermo Fisher).

RNA extraction and quantitative real-time PCR (RT-qPCR)

To confirm successful induction of the OA-like phenotype, C28/I2 cells were plated in 6-well plates at 3.5 × 10⁵ cells/well and cultured in complete DMEM for 24 h. The next day, control wells received fresh complete DMEM, whereas experimental wells received complete DMEM containing 10 ng/mL TNF-α for 24 h. Additionally, to determine the relative expression of predicted target genes, cells were seeded as above and, on the following day, control wells were treated with complete DMEM plus 10 ng/mL TNF-α, while experimental wells were treated with complete DMEM containing each PFAS compound at its respective IC₅₀ concentration. After 24 h, total RNA was extracted using Total RNA Kit I (Novoprotein). Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized using NovoStart 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis SuperMix (Novoprotein). RT-qPCR was performed on an ABI 7500 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems) with RNA18SN5 (18 S) as the endogenous reference gene. Relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2^–ΔΔCt method, and all groups were run in triplicate.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 9. Comparisons were performed using two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-tests and one-way ANOVA. For IC₅₀ determination, drug concentrations were log₁₀-transformed and fitted with nonlinear regression using the “log(inhibitor) vs. normalized response—variable slope” model. Significance is indicated as *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, or ****p < 0.0001.

Conclusion

By integrating network toxicology, machine learning, molecular docking and RT-qPCR analyses, this study delineated potential molecular links between PFAS exposure and OA, identifying ABCB1, DNMT1, ESR1 and FGFR2 as key target genes. It is hypothesized that PFAS may contribute to OA pathogenesis through the disruption of cellular signaling, alteration of gene expression, and damage to cartilage proteins. These findings provide a foundation for future investigations; however, the absence of human epidemiological evidence means the relationship must be further validated in population-based studies to establish causality and clarify the clinical and public-health relevance of PFAS exposure to OA.
