The optoelectronic, mechanical, and thermoelectric properties of novel wide band gap double perovskites: a first-principles study
Authors: Muhammad Salman Khan, Mohannad Al-Hmoud, Sarah Nabirye, Siti Maisarah Aziz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The quest for efficient and sustainable materials for energy applications is essential for managing global energy challenges. This work investigates the electronic structure, optical, mechanical, and thermoelectric nature of Ba2CaXO6 (X = Se and Te) double perovskite to identify their potential for photovoltaic applications. We calculate electronic band structures, density of state, and optical characteristics employing density functional theory (DFT) and using advanced exchange-correlation functionals. These materials possess direct wide band gaps, which make them ideal for photovoltaic and optoelectronic applications. Optical absorption investigations suggest substantial absorption in the ultraviolet-visible (UV-Vis) band, which improves their utility in solar energy harvesting. Both materials have mechanical stability, ductility, and near-isotropic elastic behavior, which makes them ideal for structural and device applications. Ba2CaTeO6 exhibits greater stiffness and shear resistance; while Ba2CaSeO6 appears more ductile and compliant. In addition, thermoelectric performance is examined employing Boltzmann transport theory, which reveals an appealing combination of high Seebeck coefficients and low thermal conductivity, indicating thermoelectric device functionality. The relationship between structural stability, electronic configuration, and thermal transport mechanisms is thoroughly examined, with an emphasis on the effect of X-site substitution on functional characteristics. Our results show that Ba2CaSeO6 and Ba2CaTeO6 have incredible potential as multifunctional materials for clean energy technologies, providing a route to enhance next-generation solar energy and thermoelectric systems.

Introduction

Perovskite materials recently attracted much consideration for an inclusive range of applications, specifically for their exceptional features1–3. They have a wide range of applications, including electrochemical sensors, fuel cells, thermoelectric generators, and solar cells4–6. This appeal stems mostly from their outstanding optical characteristics and solidity, notably in photovoltaics6,7. In addition, their distinctive structural properties make them intriguing for catalysis8. Simple perovskites (ABX3) have cubic structure within the Fm3m (225) space group9. The A-site ion coordinates with 12 surrounding ions, resulting in an octahedral configuration. Furthermore, the B-site ion engages to nearby anions. The X-site ion usually comprises a halogen element10. Double perovskites, on the other hand, offer more chemical and compositional versatility than simple perovskites. These materials, having the formula A₂BB’X6, have been properly utilized in different disciplines, including electrocatalysis, thermoelectricity, spintronics, and photovoltaics11,12. In this formula, A, B, and B’ designate cations that occupy precise positions in their unit cell structures, while X denotes anions. Replacing the halogen at the X site with oxygen extremely affects the material’s characteristics, leading to the production of perovskite oxides13. Perovskite oxides, an important subgroup of the perovskite family, have sparked considerable scientific interest because of their potential for innovation and devices. Fennie’s study found that the electromagnetic coupling method in PbTiO3/BiFeO3 produces a considerable magnetoelectric coupling effect14. The ferroelectric BaTiO3’s electrical structure, chemical bonding, and optical characteristics were analyzed, demonstrating its significant application potential. Double perovskite oxides display a wide range of electrical characteristics, from insulator and metallic to spin semi metallic15,16. Nanostructured TiO2 thin films with optimal annealing temperatures showed greater optical absorption, which renders them ideal for electron transport layers in perovskite solar cells17. Thulium doping at the B-site cation in completely inorganic perovskites improves crystallinity and defect passivation, resulting in very stable highly effective solar cells with efficiency greater than 17%18. Lead-free halide perovskites provide environmentally beneficial alternatives with excellent multifunctional capabilities, highlighting their potential as next-generation solar energy conversion devices19.

Dai et al.20 studied the mechanical, optical, electrical, and thermoelectric characteristics of Ba2MgXO6 (X = Se, Te) perovskites. Their outcomes revealed that these materials are stable, with no imaginary frequencies in the phonon spectrum and a negative binding energy and formation enthalpy. Ba2MgXO6 is brittle due to its elastic characteristics, whereas Ba2MgSeO6 and Ba2MgTeO6 have direct band gaps and semiconducting capabilities. A recent study also showed that Ba2MgXO6 was an ionic material. These materials have large absorption coefficients in the ultraviolet range, reaching the Dulong-Petit limit for a heat capacity of about 800 K. Suraja et al.21 discovered that Ba2CdTeO6, Sr2CdTeO6, and Ca2CdTeO6 had band gaps of 3.3, 3.8, and 3.9 eV, making them appropriate for temperature sensing and photoluminescence. Manoun et al.22 synthesized Sr2MgTeO6 at ambient temperature, whereas some recent studies demonstrated its excellent thermal stability as a light-emitting phosphor23–25. Sleight et al.26 synthesized Ba₂MgTeO6 and discovered a yellowish cubic structure with a lattice constant of 8.130 Å. Haddouch et al.27 observed a 2.8 eV band gap in BaSrMgTeO6. Dias et al.28 considered the microwave dielectric features of A2MgTeO6 (A = Ba, Sr, Ca) materials synthesized employing solid-state methods. Halizan et al.29 investigated the electronic structure and optical features of Sr2CaTeO6. Manoun et al.30 synthesized Sr2ZnTeO6 and confirmed its cubic structure under ambient conditions. The band gap was 4.12 eV, determined with optical spectroscopy31. Bayer et al.32 found lattice constants of 7.94 Å and 8.14 Å in Ba2MgTeO6 and Sr2MgTeO6, respectively, using solid-state processes. Ubic et al.33 confirmed a lattice constant of 7.9 Å for Sr2MgTeO6 in their studies. Using density functional theory (DFT), we first thoroughly analyze the structural properties of Ba2CaSeO6 and Ba2CaTeO6. We investigated their transport, optoelectronic, and structural features in detail. In addition to expanding our knowledge of the basic characteristics of Ba2CaSeO6 and Ba2CaTeO6, this study highlights their probable uses in cutting-edge data storage applications and optoelectronic technologies. We hope to aid in the development of novel materials for environmentally friendly technologies by examining the connections between their structural, optoelectronic, and transport properties. This study offers substantial knowledge into the design and optimization of double perovskites for energy applications.

Computational method

In this study, Ba2CaSeO6 and Ba2CaTeO6 were investigated using the Wien2k code34. This code uses the density functional theory and is notable for its accuracy in calculating the all-physical features35. Whereas the exchange-correlation potential is often taken into account using Perdew, Burke Ernzerhof generalized gradient potential36. In addition, the (mBJ) potential was taken into consideration while calculating physical properties37. The Fourier expansion coefficient (Gmax), the maximum greatness of the wave vector (Kmax), and optimal k-mesh are chosen as 11, 12 (a.u)−1, 7.0/RMT (RMT = minimum value of Mufn-Tin sphere), and 11 × 11 × 11. The BoltzTraP code38 uses a constant relaxation time (τ = 0.4 × 10− 14 s) calculation to analyze thermoelectric properties. A solid k mesh (14 × 14 × 14) was used to achieve an energy junction.

Results and discussion

Structure properties

In the cubic phase having Fm̅3m space group, Ba2CaSeO6 crystallizes (Fig. 1). To produce BaO12 cuboctahedra, Ba2⁺ is attached to identical O2⁻ atoms. These cuboctahedra share corners with other BaO12 cuboctahedra, appear with six other BaO12 cuboctahedra, appear with four CaO6 octahedra, and face four SeO6 octahedra. Every Ba-O bond has a length of 2.87 Å. There is no tilt to the octahedra that share a corner. Every Ca-O bond possesses a length of 2.26 Å. SeO6 octahedra, which share faces along eight corresponding BaO12 cuboctahedra and corners with six equivalent CaO6 octahedra, are formed when Se6⁺ is coupled to six identical O2⁻ atoms. There is no tilt to the octahedra that share a corner. Every Se-O bond has a length of 1.79 Å. Four identical Ba2⁺, one Ca2⁺, and one Se6⁺ atom are joined to O2⁻ in a deformed linear geometry. The cubic Fm̅3m space group is where Ba2CaTeO6 crystallizes and has a structure formed by perovskites. To create BaO12 cuboctahedra, Ba2⁺ is bound with twelve equivalent O2⁻ molecules. There is no tilt to the octahedra that share a corner. Four equivalent Ba2⁺, one Ca2⁺, and one Te6⁺ atom is connected to O2⁻ in a linear geometry. The calculated lattice constants values for Ba2CaXO6 (X = Se, Te) show a good consistency with previously reported data for similar double perovskites (see Table 1), confirming the reliability and accuracy of our results. Figure 2a,b depicts how the indicated behavior reflects the energy-volume relationship regulated by solid-state physics principles of equilibrium and the Birch-Murnaghan equation of state, which describes total energy (E) as a function of system volume (V). Each material has an equilibrium volume (V0), where the total energy is minimized. This is the volume that balances the attractive and repulsive forces within the substance. As the volume drops below V0, atoms are forced closer together. The system’s energy grows as a result of strong repulsive forces between overlapping electron clouds, which prevail at shorter interatomic distances. Cohesive energy is the amount of energy necessary for splitting a crystal into individual atoms, demonstrating the strength of bonding inside the crystal lattice. The calculated cohesive energies of Ba2CaSeO6 (-3.48) eV/atom and Ba2CaTeO6 (-3.95) eV/atom (see Table 1) show that Ba2CaTeO6 has greater bonding contacts and an increasingly stable crystal lattice than Ba2CaSeO6. The computed formation energies for Ba2CaSeO6 (-3.01) eV/atom and Ba2CaTeO6 (-3.17) eV/atom reveal stability, with negative values (see Table 1). Ba2CaSeO6 has a slightly lower formation energy, making it less thermally beneficial than Ba2CaTeO6. The noticed trends in cohesive and formation energies align, indicating that Ba2CaTeO6 has superior stability overall. This could be owing to variations in chemical bonding or atomic arrangements caused by replacing Selenium (Se) with Tellurium (Te). Te is larger and may generate stronger interactions inside the lattice, resulting in greater stability for Ba2CaTeO6.

Electronic band structure

Figure 3a–d compares these calculated band profiles with the PBE-GGA and TB-mBJ approximations. Thus, the band edge in Ba2CaSeO6 and Ba2CaTeO6 is a direct band gap (Γ-Γ). For Ba2CaSeO6 and Ba2CaTeO6, PBE-GGA predicts energy gaps of 2.1 and 3.0 eV, respectively. The mBJ produces energy gaps of 3.8 eV for Ba2CaSeO6 and 4.6 eV for Ba2CaTeO6, much larger than those achieved with PBE-GGA. The TB-mBJ model incorporates non-locality to better describe the electronic structure at the Γ-point. The Se atom in Ba2CaSeO6 is less polarizable and has a lower atomic radius than Te in Ba2CaTeO6. This results in stronger bonding and a smaller electronic distribution in Ba2CaSeO6, which reduces the band gap slightly when compared to Ba2CaTeO6. Furthermore, Te is a heavier element with stronger relativistic effects. The observed differences in electronic band structure contributions for Ba2CaSeO6 and Ba2CaTeO6 could be attributed to the constituent elements’ intrinsic chemical and electronic characteristics, as well as their interactions within the crystal structure. Because of their relative delocalization and weak hybridization with other states, the Ba-p and Ba-d states impacts the valence band at 3.0 eV. The Ba-d orbitals impact minimally in the CB from 4.0 to 4.54 eV because their orbitals are higher in energy and less involved in bonding interactions with other elements. Ca-p/s states impact the VB from 5.5 to – 3.0 eV by interacting with O-p and Se/Te-p states. Ca-p orbitals dominate around the Fermi level between – 3.0 eV and 0 eV due to hybridization with Se-p/Te-p states, underlining their importance in determining upper valence band characteristics. Ca-s/p states make a significant contribution at 4.5 to 5.8 eV due to their involvement in anti-bonding states generated with Se/Te-s orbitals. Selenium (Se) is more electronegative than Tellurium (Te), which means its p-orbitals have lower energy and are more closely bonded than Te. The Se-p states contributed in the valence band at lower energies in − 5.5 eV to − 4.8 eV, but Te-p states dominate at slightly higher energies in – 4.8 eV to – 4.0 eV. Se-s states dominate the lower conduction band in Ba2CaSeO6 at 3.8 to 4.8 eV due to their lower energy relative to Te-s levels. In Ba2CaTeO6, Te-s states contribute at higher energy ranges in 4.5 to 5.5 eV, reflecting Te’s lower ionization energy and greater orbital size. The O-p/s states between – 5.5 eV and – 4.8 eV result from high hybridization with Se-p and Te-p levels, which form bonding orbitals. The discrepancy in energy range contributions between 4.0 and 4.5 eV in Ba2CaSeO6 and 5.0 eV to 5.5 eV in Ba2CaTeO6 reflects a greater energy of O-p states in the presence of less electronegative Te. The O-p orbitals in the CB at 4.0 to 4.5 eV for Ba2CaSeO6 and 5.0 to 5.5 eV for Ba2CaTeO6 are affected by their interaction with the respective Se/Te states, which are shifted to higher energy by hybridization in Te-containing materials.

Density of states

The partial density of states provides detailed information about how specific atomic orbitals contribute to a material’s electronic structure. These observed density of states properties of Ba2CaSeO6 and Ba2CaTeO6 are described by investigating the electronic structure and chemical contact between the atoms. Figure 4a,b illustrates the density of states of both Ba2CaSeO6 and Ba2CaTeO6 in the valence band, with the Ba-p contributing slightly from − 5.5 to -5.0 eV in Ba2CaSeO6 and − 4.5 to -4.0 eV in Ba2CaTeO6. Tellurium with a larger atomic radius with lesser electronegativity compared to Se; therefore, the energy levels of Te-p orbitals approach the Fermi level. This shift is reflected in the Ba-p contributions, nearer the Fermi level for Ba2CaTeO6. The Ba-p and -d states hybridize with the Se- and Te-p levels, ranging from 3.0 eV to the Fermi Level. This hybridization occurs because Ba is an alkaline earth metal with low electronegativity, allowing its orbitals to interact with the chalcogen p states (Se and Te). Ba-s states contribute minimally to both materials in the same energy area. In a crystal environment, the Ba-d orbitals undergo crystal field splitting, causing the majority of the Ba-d states to shift to higher energy levels. This results in a small contribution to the conduction band region. Both materials, Ba2CaSeO6 and Ba2CaTeO6, exhibit a large contribution of Ca-p/s states to the VB at -5.5 eV to -5.0 eV for Ba2CaSeO6 and − 4.5 eV to -4.0 eV for Ba2CaTeO6. Selenium (Se) has a higher electronegativity and a smaller size than Tellurium (Te), resulting in more stable valence orbitals. This stabilization shifts the contribution of Se-based states to lower energy levels, influencing the overall distribution of Ca-p/s contributions. The dominance of Ca-p states ranging from 3.0 eV to 0 eV is due to the hybridization between Ca orbitals and the surrounding anions (Se and Te). Cs orbitals are often more energetic and confined. Their interaction with the valence band states of Ba2CaSeO6 and Ba2CaTeO6 is low, resulting in a limited contribution in the specified energy ranges. Ca-s and Ca-p states dominate in the conduction band from 4.5 to 5.8 eV because they are less firmly bound and more delocalized than valence levels. In the valence band region from both Ba2CaSeO6 and Ba2CaTeO6 materials, the maximum involvement comes due to Se-p state ranging − 5.5 to -4.8 eV, because selenium has a stronger bonding interaction due to its smaller size, higher electronegativity, and closer energy alignment of its p orbitals with the surrounding atoms while Te-p states dominate in a higher energy range, -4.8 eV to -4.0 eV, because Te with larger atomic size and lesser electronegativity, leading to weaker bonding interactions along with higher energy p states. The states of Se in -3.0 eV to -1.8 eV and Te in -2.4 eV to -1.0 eV contribute slightly because they are less involved in bonding. In the CB region, Se-s states dominate in the range 3.8 eV to 4.8 eV. Selenium, being smaller and more electronegative, has s-orbitals closer in energy to the conduction band edge, contributing more heavily. Te-s states contribute to a greater energy range, 4.5 eV to 5.5 eV, due to tellurium’s larger atomic size and weaker electronegativity, which places its s orbitals at higher energies. Both materials, Ba2CaSeO6 and Ba2CaTeO6, have a considerable contribution of O-p/s states to the valence band in -5.5 to -4.8 eV for Ba2CaSeO6, having minor impact due to the O-s states, and − 4.5 eV to -4.0 eV for Ba2CaTeO6, having a tiny influence from the O-s states. In Ba2CaSeO6, the electronegativity gap between O and Se is greater than in Ba2CaTeO6. Ba2CaSeO6 has stronger covalent bonding, resulting in greater hybridization between the O-p and Se-p states in the valence band area. These energy valence bands, O-p/s states, contribute somewhat more energy in Ba2CaSeO6 than in Ba2CaTeO6. The relative position of O-p states in the CB is determined by the cation’s energy level and bonding environment. For Ba2CaSeO6, O-p states contribute in the 4.0 eV to 4.5 eV range because Se-derived conduction band levels are closer to O-p states due to a smaller atomic size and less diffuse orbitals. Because of the bigger atomic size and weaker interaction with O in Ba2CaTeO6, the Te-derived conduction band states have a higher energy. These shift the O-p state contributions to a higher range from 5.0 to 5.5 eV.

Optical properties

The behavior dielectric function real component ε1(ω), as illustrated in Fig. 5a, can be understood based on electronic transitions and interband absorptions that occur in the materials at different energy ranges. Ba2CaSeO6 and Ba2CaTeO6 possess ε1(0) of 3.0 and 2.8, respectively (see Table 2). The obtained static dielectric constants are in fine agreement with the reported literature, representing that our optical results are accurate and consistent with established trends for double perovskite oxides. The first peak of both Ba2CaSeO6 and Ba2CaTeO6 materials occurs at 7.5 eV. This peak represents a resonance state in which the electrical structure of the materials enables efficient optical transitions. After the initial peak, ε1(ω) declines from 7.7 eV to 12.3 eV as the density of states and the likelihood of optical transitions diminish in this energy range. The second increase in ε1(ω) reaches high peaks at 17.0 eV for both Ba2CaSeO6 and Ba2CaTeO6 materials, induced by another series of interband transitions. These transitions occur between deeper valence band states or higher conduction band states, which have a greater density of states, and the electronic transition probabilities at these energies. Following the second peak, ε1(ω) declines to become negative at 18.0 and 17.5 eV for Ba2CaSeO6 and Ba2CaTeO6, respectively. The discrepancies in ε1(ω) behavior between Ba2CaSeO6 and Ba2CaTeO6 can be attributed to electronic structural differences between the chalcogens. Te possesses greater atomic size and small electronegativity compared to Se, that disturbs the electronic properties and transition probabilities in Ba2CaSeO6 and Ba2CaTeO6. Figure 5b illustrates the behavior of Ba2CaSeO6 and Ba2CaTeO6. The threshold energies for Ba2CaSeO6, at 4.0 eV, and Ba2CaTeO6, at 4.8 eV, are determined by the materials’ optical band gaps. The slightly higher number for Ba2CaTeO6 indicates a greater band gap than Ba2CaSeO6. The initial peak exists at 9.0 eV in Ba2CaSeO6 and 9.5 eV in Ba2CaTeO6, resembling transitions between deeper valence band and higher conduction band. Atomic composition differences (for example, Se vs. Te) affect the electronic structure, and as a result, the peak positions. As photon energy rises, ε2(ω) drops due to decreasing oscillator strength and fewer permitted transitions. The second set of high peaks at higher photon energies is caused by electronic transitions between deeper core levels or higher-energy conduction band states. The minor shift at 18.5 eV for Ba2CaSeO6 and 18.0 eV for Ba2CaTeO6 illustrates the variation in electronic structure due to the replacement of Se with Te, which impacts hybridization and bonding properties. Beyond the second peak, ε2(ω) drops further as photon energy exceeds the energy levels of most electrons.

The refractive index n(ω) of constituents Ba2CaSeO6 and Ba2CaTeO6 was attributed to their electronic structure, specifically the relationship of electromagnetic radiation with their interband transitions. Figure 5c shows that the static n(0) indicates the material’s reaction to low-frequency or static electric fields. Ba2CaSeO6 has a slightly higher n(0) of 1.90 compared to Ba2CaTeO6 (see Table 2), representing modest changes in electronic polarizability at low energy. The obtained static n(0) are compared with the reported literature, representing an accurate and consistent trends for our studied double perovskite oxides. The initial high peaks in n(ω) at 7.3 eV indicate strong optical transitions in the material. The decrease from 8.5 eV to 13.0 eV for n(ω) suggests that the material absorbs less electromagnetic energy in this range, resulting in fewer allowable optical transitions. At about 17.0 eV, both show a new set of significant interband transitions. These transitions involve deeper core levels or more complex transitions with higher conduction bands. The increase in n(ω) indicates increased optical activity caused by higher oscillator strengths and high-energy transitions. Following the second peak, n(ω) drops again. The absorption coefficient I(ω) measures how photons interact with a material’s electrical structure. The patterns shown in Fig. 5d for Ba2CaSeO6 and Ba2CaTeO6 can be explained using electronic transitions in the materials. Figure 5d indicates that the I(ω) was started at 4.0 eV and 4.8 eV for Ba2CaSeO6 and Ba2CaTeO6, respectively. The first peaks are at 10.0 eV and 9.5 eV for Ba2CaSeO6 and Ba2CaTeO6, respectively. These exist between levels deep in the valence band and high-energy states in the conduction band. The peak intensity indicates a significant density of states overlap between the first and last state. Beyond these peaks, the absorption coefficient I(ω) falls and then increases, peaking at 20.5 and 20.0 eV for Ba2CaSeO6 and Ba2CaTeO6 materials, respectively. These high-energy peaks frequently imply a resonance effect, for the photon energy matches the variance between tightly packed energy levels, resulting in increased absorption. The drop in I(ω) after the peaks happens because there are fewer states accessible for transitions at higher energies.

The reflectivity R(ω) varies with photon energy and is intimately related to the electronic structure and optical transitions in materials. Figure 5e illustrates the reflectivity R(ω) of Ba2CaSeO6 and Ba2CaTeO6. Ba2CaSeO6 and Ba2CaTeO6 with R(0) of 0.90 and 0.85, respectively (see Table 2). Ba2CaSeO6 and Ba2CaTeO6 have slightly distinct electrical band structures, resulting in differing static dielectric constants. The first peaks of Ba2CaSeO6 and Ba2CaTeO6 materials occur at 10.0 eV and 9.4 eV, respectively. That variation in the positions of the preliminary peaks at 10.0 eV in Ba2CaSeO6 and 9.5 eV in Ba2CaTeO6 is produced by changes in the band structures. The atoms have lower electronegativity and are larger than Se atoms, resulting in smaller band gaps and lower transition energies. After the initial peak, R(ω) drops as the density of states in the conduction band around these transition energies declines. The second, higher-energy peaks in R(ω) are 23.0 eV and 22.0 eV for Ba2CaSeO6 and Ba2CaTeO6, respectively. That somewhat lower position of the second peak in Ba2CaTeO6 when compared to Ba2CaSeO6 illustrates the influence of Te, which reduces the energy of electronic transitions due to its higher atomic mass and more diffuse electronic orbitals. Following the second peak, R(ω) declines for both Ba2CaSeO6 and Ba2CaTeO6. The decrease in the R(ω) succeeding the second peak is due to the ability of high-energy photons to excite electrons into a variety of states, resulting in fewer coherent electronic transitions overall. The Energy Loss Function L(ω) is a vital parameter in optical and electrical studies of constituents. It denotes that energy is lost by rapidly traveling electrons through a material due to interactions with its electronic structure, particularly plasmon excitations. The threshold energy values for L(ω) represent the minimal energy needed to excite plasmons or other collective electronic oscillations. Figure 5f indicates that the value of 5.5 eV for Ba2CaSeO6 and 6.0 eV for Ba2CaTeO6 match specific electronic transitions or plasma frequencies found in these materials. Ba2CaTeO6 has a slightly higher plasma frequency than Ba2CaSeO6, owing to changes in electronic density or bonding environment. The peaks of L(ω) at 26.0 eV in Ba2CaSeO6 and 25.5 eV in Ba2CaTeO6 indicate bulk plasmon resonances. This is a joint oscillation of the material’s delocalized electrons plasma caused by external electromagnetic fields. The plasmon resonance’s peak energy is examined by the material’s electron density for dielectric characteristics. These high peaks suggest intense plasmonic activity. Peak positions change between Ba2CaSeO6 and Ba2CaTeO6 because of their differing atomic weights, lattice configurations, and chemical environments. The decrease in L(ω) after the peaks at 26.0 eV and 25.5 eV is caused by the dampening of plasmon oscillations and the start of interband electronic transitions.

Mechanical properties

Elastic details can accurately determine the mechanical stability and anisotropy of quaternary chalcogenides such as Ba2CaSeO6 and Ba2CaTeO6. Both materials in the cubic phase permit basic analysis of mechanical properties using the fundamental elastic constants (C11, C12, and C44). This discussion focuses on the variations and implications of these characteristics, including their stability, ductility, rigidity, and potential mechanical applications. The computed elastic constants along with other factors for Ba2CaSeO6 and Ba2CaTeO6 exhibit close agreement with earlier reported values (see Table 3), validating the mechanical stability and anisotropic nature of these materials. Bulk modulus demonstrates resistance to change in volume under uniform pressure. Ba2CaTeO6 has a slightly greater value, B = 110.02 GPa, as compared to Ba2CaSeO6, that is 103.54 GPa (see Table 2). It indicates Ba2CaTeO6 is slightly greater incompressible and mechanically stiffer in volumetric deformation. The increase could be ascribed to a higher Te-O bonding interaction than Se-O, resulting from Te’s somewhat greater electronegativity and mass. Both results lie in the medium stiffness range, displaying balanced structural rigidity. The shear modulus indicates resistance to form change at constant volume, where Young’s modulus measures stiffness under uniaxial tension. Ba2CaTeO6 (190.96 GPa) exceeds Ba2CaSeO6 (185.61 GPa) concerning of stiffness and resistance to shear deformation. Comparably, Young’s modulus demonstrating that Ba2CaTeO6 is considerably stiffer under linear stress. This indicates that it is appropriate for structural applications that require increased mechanical strength. Poisson’s ratio provides ductility and bonding properties. Ba2CaSeO6 has v = 0.322, and Ba2CaTeO6 has ν = 0.309 (see Table 3). Both results are very close to but slightly greater than 0.3, suggesting that both compounds are ductile and have central-force bonding properties. The telluride’s slightly decreased value could suggest a little more directed bonding and lesser plasticity, which is consistent with its higher rigidity. The Pugh ratio, a ductility indication, differentiates between ductile (B/G > 1.75) and brittle (B/G < 1.75) materials. Both materials possess ductile properties: 1.87 for Ba2CaSeO6 and 1.93 for Ba2CaTeO6 (see Table 3). This shows that the Se-based compound is additionally ductile, which could be helpful in applications that need flexibility. The decline in Pugh’s ratio after Se substitution reflects a trade-off between ductility and stiffness. Cauchy pressure provides details regarding the bonding properties. Both compounds show substantial positive values: 46.30 GPa for Ba2CaSeO6 and 40.93 GPa for Ba2CaTeO6. Positive values mean a high level of metallic or ionic bonding. Ba2CaSeO6 has a somewhat greater value, showing a more ionic nature, while Ba2CaTeO6 has a smaller value, possibly indicating covalent tendencies. This bonding nature defines their deformation tendency when stressed. Ba2CaSeO6 has Cʹ = 16.63 GPa, but Ba2CaTeO6 has a considerably higher value of 23.89 GPa (see Table 3), exhibiting better resistance to shear in the Te-based material. This complements its higher shear and Young’s moduli and leads to its superior mechanical strength. Elastic anisotropy is a key characteristic for predicting fracture initiation and propagation. A value of one implies perfect isotropy. Both compounds exhibit moderate anisotropies, with A = 1.45 for Ba2CaSeO6 and A = 1.44 for Ba2CaTeO6 (see Table 3). These values suggest essentially isotropic mechanical behavior, which is helpful in decreasing internal stresses and mechanical failure in thermal cycling or fabrication operations. Overall, Ba2CaTeO6 is the mechanically strongest material. Its greater elastic constants lead to greater shear modulus, Young’s modulus, and shear resistance, implying increased stiffness and strength. Ba2CaSeO6 exhibits somewhat higher ductility and Cauchy pressure, signifying a more compliant structure with potential to enhance shock absorption. Both materials are mechanically sound (satisfying all Born stability conditions for the cubic phase: C11 > 0, C44 > 0, C11 > C12, C11 + 2C12 > 0), but their potential applications differ due to the disparity between strength and ductility. Ba2CaTeO6 is more appropriate for load-bearing or thermomechanical applications, while Ba2CaSeO6 is better for flexible coatings or less stiff electronic substrates. The practically equal anisotropies result indicates equivalent dimensional responses under multiple loading directions, supporting comparable efficiency in polycrystalline and thin-film forms. This near-isotropic distinctive may help both materials in advanced microelectronics and optoelectronics, where mechanical consistency is essential. Elastic research demonstrates that Ba2CaSeO6 and Ba2CaTeO6 are ductile, mechanically stable materials with near-isotropic properties. Ba2CaTeO6 has larger strength and stiffness, although Ba2CaSeO6 is more flexible and can absorb more energy. Their mechanical properties can be adjusted to specific applications by making use of each composition’s own features.

Thermoelectric properties

The Seebeck coefficient is a measurement of the power produced in response to a temperature gradient within a solid. Figure 6a displays that at 50 K, the highest Seebeck coefficient values for Ba2CaSeO6 and Ba2CaTeO6 are − 0.5 and − 0.6 µV/K, respectively. Carriers at the Fermi level contribute significantly to the thermoelectric voltage, resulting in larger Seebeck coefficients (S) values. From 50 K up to 650 K, the Seebeck coefficient (S) values drop. Higher thermal energy causes more carriers to pass the band gap. Because the Seebeck coefficient S and carrier density have an inverse relationship, increasing carrier concentration decreases the Seebeck coefficient. At about 650 K, the minimum S values are − 3.1 µV/K and − 3.7 µV/K for Ba2CaSeO6 and Ba2CaTeO6, respectively. The larger Te atoms in Ba2CaTeO6 induce stronger phonon interactions than the lighter Se atoms in Ba2CaSeO6, thus reducing S in Ba2CaTeO6. Figure 6b illustrates a reduction in the electrical conductivity (σ/τ) ranging from 50 to 650 K for both Ba2CaSeO6 and Ba2CaTeO6. Figure 6b indicates the highest σ/τ values at 50 K are 1.14 × 1018 and 1.15 × 1018 (Ωms)−1 for Ba2CaSeO6 and Ba2CaTeO6, respectively. At low temperatures (50 K), scattering is mostly limited by impurity scattering. These mechanisms are temperature-independent, which results in larger σ/τ values. As the temperature rises, phonon scattering becomes more prominent. Phonons are quantized vibrations of the lattice that increase in density with temperature. High thermal vibrations deform the lattice, leading to more collisions between charge carriers and phonons, resulting in reduced carrier mobility (µ). This raises resistance and decreases conductivity. At 650 K, Ba2CaSeO6 and Ba2CaTeO6 have minimum σ/τ of 0.99 × 1018 and 0.97 × 1018 (Ωms)−1, respectively. Differences in lattice thermal vibrations owing to heavier atoms, Te in Ba2CaTeO6, which improves phonon scattering somewhat more than Se in Ba2CaSeO6.

The linear increase in electronic thermal conductivity (κe) with temperature observed in materials such as Ba2CaSeO6 and Ba2CaTeO6 is mostly due to the interaction of electronic transport characteristics regulated by the behavior of charge carriers. At increasing temperatures, more electrons are transferred across the band gap between the valence band and conduction band. That raises the concentration of carriers (both electrons and holes), increasing electronic heat conductivity. According to Fig. 6c, the thermal conductivity of Ba2CaSeO6 and Ba2CaTeO6 materials is 3.8 and 4.5 (1014 Wm− 1K− 1s− 1) at 300 K, respectively, attaining an extreme value of 8.2 and 8.5 (1014 Wm− 1K− 1s− 1) at 600 K. Ba2CaSeO6 possesses a lower thermal conductivity than Ba2CaTeO6 at 300 K, but it increases dramatically at 600 K. This could be attributed to a significant increase in carrier concentration with temperature. Ba2CaTeO6 has a somewhat higher thermal conductivity at both temperatures (300 K and 600 K) than Ba2CaSeO6, owing to stronger intrinsic carrier mobility. Tellurium (Te) possesses a larger radius and lower electronegativity than selenium, resulting in wider electronic bands. This generally results in increased mobility and a stronger temperature dependency of κe. Figure 6d shows the performance of the figure of merit ZT for Ba2CaSeO6 and Ba2CaTeO6. Figure 6d indicates that the ZT at around 300 K is 0.16 and 0.23 for Ba2CaSeO6 and Ba2CaTeO6, respectively. The maximum figure of merit ZT at around 650 K is 0.70 and 0.60 for Ba2CaSeO6 and Ba2CaTeO6, respectively. Ba2CaSeO6 and Ba2CaTeO6 have distinct band profiles. A greater energy gap in Ba2CaSeO6 could reduce bipolar conduction at higher temperatures, resulting in improved performance.

Conclusions

Ba2CaSeO6 was thermally less efficient than Ba2CaTeO6 because of its slightly lower formation energy. The determined cohesive and formation energy trends coincide, implying that Ba2CaTeO6 is more stable in general. These results are due to the different atomic configurations or chemical bonds brought on by substituting tellurium for selenium. Ba2CaTeO6 can be more stable as Te is larger and could produce greater interactions inside the lattice. Both Ba2CaSeO6 and Ba2CaTeO6 display a direct band gap (Γ-Γ) in their band profiles. Band gap values of 3.8 eV for Ba2CaSeO6 and 4.6 eV for Ba2CaTeO6 can be obtained using TB-mBJ, which is significantly higher than what PBE-GGA accomplished. Compared to Te in Ba2CaTeO6, the Se atom in Ba2CaSeO6 possesses a smaller atomic radius and is therefore less polarizable. Because of the hybridization with Se/Te-p states, Ca-p states predominate at the Fermi level between − 3.0 eV and 0 eV, demonstrating their significance in determining upper valence band properties. Electronic structural differences between Se and Te account for the changes in ε1(ω) performance between Ba2CaSeO6 and Ba2CaTeO6. R(ω) decreases for both Ba2CaSeO6 and Ba2CaTeO6. Since high-energy photons may stimulate electrons into a range of states, there are typically fewer coherent electronic transitions, which is responsible for the fall in R(ω) after the second peak. The L(ω) threshold energy values reflect the lowest energy required to induce collective electronic oscillations, such as plasmons. Bulk plasmon resonances are shown by intensities in L(ω) at 26.0 eV for Ba2CaSeO6 and 25.5 eV for Ba2CaTeO6. S is reduced in Ba2CaTeO6 because the bigger Te atoms there generate stronger phonon interactions than the lighter Se atoms in Ba2CaSeO6. Both materials are ideal for structural and device applications due to their near-isotropic elastic behavior, ductility, and mechanical stability. Although Ba2CaSeO6 appears more ductile and compliant, Ba2CaTeO6 demonstrates better stiffness and shear resistance. A trade-off between stiffness and flexibility is confirmed by the observed elastic features, which permit customized use of these quaternary chalcogenides in the construction of multifunctional materials. The combination of electronic transport properties regulated by charge carrier behavior is the main cause for the linear increase in electronic thermal conductivity with temperature seen in Ba2CaSeO6 and Ba2CaTeO6. At 300 K, Ba2CaSeO6’s thermal conductivity is less than that of Ba2CaTeO6, but it rises significantly at 600 K. This could be explained by the fact that the carrier concentration rises considerably with temperature. For both materials, the ZT value rises when the temperature increases from 50 K to 650 K.
