Effect of sequential laboratory passaging on virulence factors traits and antifungal susceptibility variation in Candida albicans clinical isolates
Authors: Hasti Nouraei, Zahra Yousefi, Zahra Zareshahrabadi, Neda Amirzadeh, Seyedeh Zahra Rajabi, Keyvan Pakshir
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe virulence of Candida albicans is associated with phenotypic plasticity and hydrolytic enzyme production. However, routine laboratory subculturing represents a large and understudied variable that has the potential to diminish research reproducibility and diagnostic accuracy. In this study, we investigated the effect of serial passages on key virulence factors and antifungal susceptibility variation changing clinical isolates.MethodsThirty clinical C. albicans isolates, pre-selected for enzyme production were subjected to ten sequential in vitro passages. Enzymatic activities of phospholipase, proteinase and esterase were quantified at each passage. Phenotypic stability was assessed on each passages by the colony morphology, and fluconazole susceptibility was performed at passages 1, 5 and 10 by the standard broth microdilution method.ResultsSerial passaging induced significant variation, isolate-dependent instability. Dynamic phenotypic switching between different colony morphologies were realized. Enzyme stability was factor-specific: while proteinase activities showed significant fluctuation, phospholipase and esterase were more stable. Crucially, fluconazole susceptibility and the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) changed in both directions; two resistant isolates reverted to susceptible, and three susceptible isolates acquired resistance and majority of the isolates revealed MIC variation.ConclusionRoutine laboratory passaging can fundamentally and unpredictably alter critical pathogenic traits in C. albicans, including phenotype, enzyme production, and antifungal resistance. Such plasticity challenges the reliability of data generated from high-passage strains. These findings emphasize the need to use low-passage clinical isolates in research and diagnostics as a way of obtaining results that truly reflect the in vivo virulence potential of the pathogen.Keywords: Candida albicans, Sequential laboratory passaging, Enzyme production, Phenotype switching, Antifungal susceptibility

Background

The increasing incidence of invasive fungal infections, particularly those caused by Candida albicans, has become a major clinical concern, especially among immunocompromised patients [1–3]. The most common pathogenic species of Candida, C. albicans, utilizes many virulence mechanisms in its pathogenic process, including enzyme production, such as phospholipase, proteinase, and esterase, as well as a morphology based virulence mechanism like phenotypic switching between white and opaque colonies [4, 5]. These virulence characteristics together enhance the ability of this organism to invade host tissues and evade pressing immune responses, contributing to its clinical persistence and treatment challenges [6]. A critical but often overlooked factor in C. albicans pathogenicity research is the potential impact of laboratory subculturing, or passaging, on the organism’s virulence profile diversity. In clinical and research laboratories, clinical or other isolates routinely undergo both stock and lab passaging and still routinely undergo repeated passaging for storage, etiological agent diagnosis, and patient treatment [7]. The extent to which serial passages can affect enzymatic, phenotypic, or antifungal susceptibility is not well defined. Any impact caused by normal passaging could have significant meaning from a diagnostic, experimental reproducibility, and ultimately a patient treatment perspective [8]. Existing studies have largely focused on individual virulence traits in isolation, leaving a gap in our understanding of how sequential passaging collectively affects C. albicans pathogenicity. This study was designed to evaluate the effects of ten sequential passages on three key aspects of virulence: enzymatic activity patterns, phenotypic switching behavior, and antifungal activity against the commonly used azole, fluconazole. We aimed to determine whether repeated subculturing induces measurable changes in these virulence-associated properties. Such information is a necessary step toward standardizing fungal isolate use protocols and enhancing patient care advantages in clinical mycology. The comprehensive methodology employed herein, including intra- and inter-assay controls along with advanced statistical modeling, offers a firm foundation upon which to understand how general laboratory procedures can influence pathogenic characteristics of C. albicans.

Methods and material

In this study a total of 30 positive enzyme traits from 19 unique clinical isolates (many isolates were positive for two or all three enzymes) were selected from stock samples based on positive enzymatic activity: 10 isolates with positive phospholipase activity, 10 proteinase, and 10 esterase producer isolates. All clinical isolates used in this study were previously identified as Candida albicans using molecular methods as described in our earlier work [2, 9–11]. Briefly in our previous studies, genomic DNA was extracted from pure cultures, and species-specific PCR was performed targeting the ITS region was amplified using universal primers ITS1 and ITS4, and amplicons were sequenced. The enzymatic activity intensity of the isolates was determined after 10 repeating sequential passages. Additionally, morphological variations (colony phenotype and color) were assessed for all 30 enzyme‑positive selections, which corresponded to 19 unique clinical isolates due to overlapping enzyme production. Each of these 19 isolates was subcultured daily for 10 passages on YPDA + PB plates, and colony morphology was documented at every passage. Furthermore, to assess changes in the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) of the isolates against fluconazole on the first, fifth, and tenth passage days, a broth microdilution susceptibility test was conducted. Prior to the start of this passaging experiment, each isolate had undergone only one passage after initial sampling from the patient, specifically the primary culture on Sabouraud dextrose agar for isolation and species identification. The isolates were then preserved at -20 °C in appropriate preservation media (e.g., glycerol or Sabouraud broth with cryoprotectant) for a period of less than three months before being revived for this study.

Sample preparation

The samples were cultivated on Sabouraud dextrose agar (SDA). For initial screening of enzyme producer isolates, samples were cultured on specific media for phospholipase, proteinase, and esterase enzymatic assays, and positive isolates were selected.

Sequential passaging protocol

Each of the 19 clinical isolates was subjected to ten sequential in vitro passages. The passaging procedure was performed as follows:

At Passage 1 (P1), a single well-isolated colony from the original stock culture was inoculated onto SDA and incubated at 32 °C for 24 h. After that the colony was subcultured onto fresh SDA plates to initiate the next passage (P2). A loopful of growth was suspended in sterile saline to achieve a turbidity equivalent to 2 McFarland standard (approximately 1–5 × 108 CFU/mL). The suspension was used for enzymatic assays, phenotypic switching plates, and 0.5 McFarland standard (approximately 1–5 × 106 CFU/mL) was used for antifungal susceptibility testing (at designated passages). This procedure was repeated daily for ten consecutive days, always using 24-hour ± 1 h cultures.

Enzymatic activity assays

Proteinase activity

Proteinase activity was screened by using bovine serum albumin (BSA) agar with a modified procedure of Dagdeviren et al. [12]: BSA agar (1.5% agar, 0.1% BSA, pH 4.0) was made and autoclaved and poured into sterile plates. A 6-mm sterile filter disc loaded with standardized inoculum (10⁷ cells/mL in PBS) was placed on agar surfaces. The media were kept at 30 °C for 5 days under humidified conditions.

The clear zone diameters were measured (mm), and proteinase activity was evaluated by precipitation zone ratio (Pz) calculated as:

Pz negative (1), Pz 1+ (0.9–0.99), Pz 2+ (0.8–0.89), Pz 3+ (0.7–0.79), and Pz 4+ (< 0.7).

Each sample was subcultured each day for ten days, and the data for each passage were recorded.

Phospholipase activity

Phospholipase activity was evaluated according to the egg yolk agar method described by Price et al. [13], supplementing SDA with 5.5 g/L CaCl₂ and 58.4 g/L NaCl; crucially, 20 mL/L of sterile egg yolk supernatant (obtained by centrifuging egg yolk emulsion at 5,000 ×g for 30 min) was added to the base medium after autoclaving. Test strains were spot-inoculated onto this medium using 10 µL aliquots of 2 McFarland standard suspensions. Following inoculation, the plates were incubated at 37 °C for 5 days. After incubation, phospholipase production was quantified by measuring the diameter of the precipitation halos (in mm) formed around the colonies; these measurements were then used to calculate Pz values, which were subsequently graded according to established criteria. Each sample was subcultured daily for ten days, and the data for each passage were recorded.

Esterase activity assay

Esterase production was determined through Tween 80 hydrolysis using the following method: the medium was prepared (1% peptone, 0.5% NaCl, 0.01% CaCl₂, and 1.5% agar) adjusted to pH 7.0; after autoclaving, 0.5% sterile Tween 80 was aseptically added to the base medium once it had cooled to 50 °C. Test organisms were spot-inoculated onto the solidified medium using standardized suspensions. Inoculated plates were then incubated at 35 °C for a duration of 10 days. Following incubation, esterase activity was quantified by measuring the diameters (in mm) of the opaque hydrolysis halos formed around the colonies; these measurements were used to calculate an esterase zone ratio (Ez) value, determined analogously to the Pz value calculation method described for phospholipase activity [14]. Each sample was subcultured each day for ten days, and the data for each passage were recorded.

Antifungal susceptibility test

The minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) of fluconazole was determined according to Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) guidelines (M27-A3 for yeasts) using a broth microdilution method [15]. Serial two-fold dilutions of fluconazole (0.125–64 µg/mL) were prepared in RPMI-1640 medium buffered with MOPS to pH 7.0. Standardized yeast inoculum suspensions (0.5 McFarland turbidity) were aseptically added (100 µL per well) to sterile 96-well plates containing the drug dilutions. Plates were incubated at 32 °C for 24–48 h, wells containing only media serving as negative controls and drug-free wells as positive controls. Following incubation, MIC values were determined visually by comparing each well to the growth control well. The MIC was defined as the lowest concentration of fluconazole that produced a prominent reduction in turbidity (approximately 50% inhibition) relative to the drug-free control. Any wells with signs of contamination (atypical turbidity, sediment, or color) were excluded and the test was repeated. To determine MIC variability, the samples were evaluated for antifungal susceptibility test at the 1st, 5th, and 10th passages. All enzymatic activity assays and antifungal susceptibility tests were performed in duplicate technical replicates.

Phenotype changes on YPDA medium

Colony phenotype change evaluation was performed using yeast extract-peptone-dextrose agar supplemented with phloxine B (YPDA + PB; per liter: 10 g yeast extract, 20 g peptone, 20 g dextrose, 20 g agar, 5 mg phloxine B) [16]. The isolates were initially cultured on SDA at 35 °C for 48 h. Pure colonies were standardized to make suspensions (0.5 McFarland turbidity) in sterile saline and adjusted spectrophotometrically (530 nm). Aliquots (10 µL) of each were spot-inoculated onto YPDA + PB plates. The inoculated plates were incubated at 32 °C for 10 days and examined daily. Colony color and morphological differences were noted and documented after incubation, and phenotyping changes were evaluated during 10 passages for each sample. Different types of colony phenotypes, like ring, stipple, fuzzy shape, and combinations of shapes, were observed [17]. This assessments were performed in duplicate plates per isolate per passage, but the passaging experiment was not independently repeated for this qualitative endpoint due to resource constraints; the duplicate within‑run replication provided consistent observations.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics, Version 22.0. The changes in phospholipase, proteinase, and esterase activity grades across the ten sequential passages were analyzed using the Friedman test, a non-parametric repeated measures alternative to ANOVA. Where the Friedman test indicated a significant overall effect, post-hoc analysis was conducted using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test with a Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons, comparing the activity grade at Passage 1 to those at Passages 5 and 10. The median and interquartile range (IQR) were used to describe the central tendency and variability of the enzyme activity grades for each passage.

For all analyses, a p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Phenotypic variations

Sequential passaging ten times on YPDA + PB revealed diverse colony morphology and color (phenotypic switching) in the test isolates. Phenotypic types encountered were o-smooth (O-S), stipple (S), hat (H), fuzzy (F), ring (R), and irregular (I), usually as mixed populations. Colors ranged from white (W) to light pink (LP) and pink (P) (Fig. 1). Phenotype stability was variable across isolates. One isolate was stable and exhibited a consistent o-smooth, white phenotype from passage 1 to passage 10. The other isolates showed significant and dynamic shifts. For instance, one isolate changed from a mixed phenotype (o-smooth with fuzzy) in passage 1 to various combination of irregular, ring, stipple, and hat forms in subsequent passages, with corresponding color changes. Effect of sequential passaging on phospholipase activity

The 10 phospholipase producers had variable phospholipase activity during the 10 passages. At passage 1, the grade distribution was 30% (2+), 30% (3+), and 40% (4+). The pattern varied during the passaging, and at passage 10, one isolate (10%) had become negative, and the others turned grades to 1+ (20%), 2+ (20%), 3+ (40%), and 4+ (10%). Statistical analysis did not reveal any significant relationship between passage number and variations in phospholipase activity (p > 0.05). The median activity grade ranged from 2.5 to 3.5 with an interquartile range (IQR) of 1 to 2. One isolate had no variation in its phospholipase activity, while the other isolate had considerable intra-isolate variability, with fluctuations in the activity levels (Fig. 2).

Discussion

The virulence of C. albicans is inherently linked with its ability to switch phenotypes and produce hydrolytic enzymes such as phospholipase, proteinase, and esterase. Our study investigated how serial sequential passaging might affect these virulence factors. The most significant finding was that repeated subculturing induces deep, non-random, and often unpredictable alterations in virulence traits, underscoring the dynamic nature of this organism even in static in vitro conditions. Our data demonstrate sequential passaging causes significant and measurable changes in colony phenotype, extracellular enzyme production, and antifungal susceptibility. Phenotypic switching is a proven virulence mechanism driven by dramatic gene expression changes that facilitate immune evasion and biofilm formation [5, 19]. We observed extensive phenotypic instability in C. albicans isolates, manifesting as diverse morphologies (stipple, ring, fuzzy, hat, and irregular) and changes in colony color. This was an isolate-specific variation; while one isolate remained o‑smooth, another exhibited extreme variation across multiple phenotypes. Some isolates initially displayed a white opaque‑smooth morphology, transitioned to light pink or pink mixed forms by intermediate passages, and then reverted to white or light pink in later passages. Such bidirectional color switching within a few passages suggests that the underlying regulatory mechanisms are highly dynamic and reversible rather than reflecting stable genetic mutations [5]. The rapid reversion observed here implies that serial passaging may temporarily perturb regulatory circuit expression without fixing epigenetic or genetic changes. These results suggest that intrinsic genetic or epigenetic backgrounds predispose specific clinical strains to increased plasticity, an observation consistent with long-established principles of C. albicans biology [20–22]. The isolate-dependent propensity for switching supports the conclusion that not all C. albicans strains are equal. The potential susceptibility of laboratory conditions to induce phenotypes different from those that are host-adapted poses a significant methodological caveat: serial passage can produce an attenuated or modified pathogenic representation of a clinical isolate over time [23]. Therefore, caution is necessary when studying phenotypic switching phenomena. Our investigation of hydrolytic enzymes (proteinase, phospholipase, and esterase) revealed a clear dichotomy in their stability during serial passaging, demonstrating an enzyme-specific and isolate-dependent response to in vitro culture. Proteinase activity variation was statistically correlated with the number of passages (p < 0.05). This plasticity is consistent with the known role of SAPs as key virulence factors for tissue invasion and immune evasion [24]. The liability of SAP expression has been reported elsewhere: at different infection stages, alternative SAP genes are expressed sequentially, and their expression is highly sensitive to pH, nutrient, and cell density [22, 24, 25]. The high variability observed evidenced by a broadening distribution of activity and the transient emergence of proteinase-negative isolates points to a labile regulatory system. The observed fluctuations likely represent a rapid adaptive response to a changing in vitro environment. One potential mechanism involves quorum sensing molecules, which dynamically regulate SAP gene expression according to cell density and are intrinsically perturbed by serial passaging-induced dilution [25]. This renders proteinase production a highly labile property in vitro. Attenuation or variable expression of a major virulence factor such as proteinase upon passaging suggests that a laboratory-adapted strain may have a “falsely” decreased pathogenicity [7, 26]. This finding necessitates careful strain maintenance records and underscores the use of low-passage clinical isolates or recovery from frozen stocks to preserve the initial virulence signature before pathogenicity studies. In contrast, phospholipase and esterase were relatively stable during passaging. Their stability, given their roles in host cell membrane degradation and nutrient uptake, suggests that they are controlled by more robust or constitutively active circuits that experience less stress from repeated subculture [27]. However, some isolates consistently maintained high phospholipase or esterase activity indicates that intrinsic genotypic processes can “fix” stable virulence profiles across environmental pressures [28]. Stability of phospholipase activity has also been in other longitudinal investigations, possibly because it serves such a critical role in the initial stages of host cell invasion, a process that may be constitutively required even under culture conditions [27]. The divergent behaviors of these enzymes are consistent with, and further extend, existing knowledge of C. albicans virulence regulation. While macro-level phenotypic switching is an evolved survival mechanism, our data indicate that plasticity also operates at a micro-level, differentially regulating specific virulence pathways [26].

The other most clinically relevant finding of the current research concerns MIC variation. Our data demonstrate that serial in vitro passaging can produce isolate-dependent, bidirectional changes in susceptibility to fluconazole. This observation challenges the concept of a static antifungal resistance pattern for a clinical isolate and reveals a dynamic realignment of the cell’s physiological status to laboratory conditions [29]. Our data showed two distinct categories. First: Loss of resistance (reversion to susceptibility): the transition of an isolate from resistant (MIC > 64 µg/mL) to susceptible (MIC = 2 µg/mL) indicates that the host environment’s selective pressure maintaining the resistant phenotype was eliminated in vitro. High‑level resistance, often mediated by constitutive overexpression of efflux pumps (e.g., CDR1, MDR1) or mutations within the drug target ERG11, is energetically costly. Without fluconazole, reversion to a susceptible phenotype through downregulation of these energetically expensive mechanisms provides a fitness advantage, leading to outgrowth of susceptible subpopulations [9, 30, 31]. This “fitness cost-associated reversion” is well documented in other pathogens when antibiotic pressure is removed. Second, shifting from susceptible to resistant strains (De Novo Emergence): studies have shown that C. albicans can rapidly develop resistance in response to drug pressure, but our data crucially demonstrate that similar outcomes can arise from the non-drug-related stresses of routine culture [7, 32]. Resistance acquisition by previously susceptible isolates is a highly alarming outcome. This de novo emergence of resistance in a drug-free condition is probably the result of metabolic and oxidative stresses inherent to serial passage. One hypothesis is that Hsp90-mediated stress responses may contribute to this phenomenon, though this remains to be directly tested. Hsp90 stabilizes numerous signal transducers, and its genetic or pharmacologic perturbation can suppress azole resistance emergence [33]. Moreover, passaging stress may induce genomic instability, such as aneuploidy of chromosomes bearing resistance genes (e.g. chromosome 5 carrying ERG11), leading to their overexpression and drug resistance in the absence of direct drug pressure [7]. These findings integrate into a broader body of evidence highlighting the dynamic nature of fungal resistance. The reciprocal changes described are compatible with the concept of “adaptive evolution” in vitro with the laboratory setting providing a new selective environment. For these reasons antifungal susceptibility testing must be performed on low-passage clinical isolates, ideally from an original culture or an early-passage stock, to ensure that the profile reflects the isolate’s status at the time of infection.

The processes responsible for expressed virulence characteristics and antifungal susceptibility are complex. Serial passaging represents an experimental evolution process. Within the nutrient-limited, axenic laboratory environment, selective pressures differ from those in the host. Microevolution is caused by stochastic mutation, epigenetic alteration, and subpopulation selection, forcing the isolate away from adaptation to the host [34]. Phenotypic switching generally precedes these more stable genetic changes.

These findings have profound implications for both basic research and for clinical diagnostics. The instability of key phenotypes means that isolate characteristics are not stable. Clinically, if a subculture loses its resistance phenotype and becomes falsely susceptible before testing, this could lead to inappropriate treatment selection. Conversely, de novo resistance emergence in the laboratory implies that findings from a high-passage isolate may not reflect the original clinical pathogen biology.

Limitations of the study

One limitation of this study is that the molecular mechanisms of the described phenotypic and metabolic alterations remain unknown including mutations in crucial regulatory genes or changes in gene expression patterns. Additional studies using whole-genome sequencing and transcriptome analysis of isolates before and after passaging would reveal more about the genetic regulation of this adaptation. Furthermore, we did not assess biofilm formation, a major virulence trait of Candida albicans that may also be influenced by sequential passaging and therefore deserves separate investigation. Additionally, there is a lack of in vivo validation using animal models, meaning that the clinical relevance remains unknown. The scope of antifungal testing was also restricted to fluconazole alone, so the generalization of our susceptibility findings to other triazoles or to distinct drug classes such as echinocandins or polyenes cannot be made directly. These limitations should be considered when interpreting our conclusions.

Conclusion

In conclusion, serial passaging profoundly alters the virulence and antifungal susceptibility profiles of C. albicans clinical isolates. We observed bidirectional fluconazole MIC shifts that changed resistance categories, meaning isolates could become resistant or revert to susceptible simply through routine subculturing. Because clinical isolates often undergo multiple passages before antifungal susceptibility testing, such changes may generate inaccurate profiles and compromise treatment decisions. Therefore, we recommend minimizing subcultures before AST, reporting passage numbers alongside results, interpreting enzyme‑based typing with caution, and standardizing passage documentation across laboratories. High priority should also be given to cryopreservation of low‑passage isolates to preserve their original pathogenic integrity. Collectively, these measures are essential for improving both clinical diagnostic accuracy and research reproducibility.
