Background

Posttraumatic aortic lesions are typically located at the aortic isthmus after blunt trauma. In 1–2% of cases, initial diagnosis is missed, and patients survive long enough to develop chronic aortic expansions. These can become symptomatic due to mechanical compression, causing cough, dyspnea, or dysphagia [1]. Patients with mono- or polygenetic connective tissue disorders (CTD), in particular, exhibit an increased predisposition to developing arterial lesions - both following heavy trauma and even in the context of inadequate or minor traumatic events. Loeys-Dietz syndrome (LDS) is a genetic disorder caused by pathogenic variants in genes of the TGF-β signaling pathway - most commonly TGFBR1, TGFBR2, SMAD2, SMAD3, TGFB2, or TGFB3 - predisposing patients to arterial aneurysms, dissections, and marked vascular tortuosity. According to the 2022 ACC/AHA Guidelines, repair is recommended for descending thoracic aortic aneurysms ≥ 5.5 cm and below in patients with LDS (surgery to replace the intact aortic arch, descending aorta, or abdominal aorta at a diameter of ≥ 4.5 cm may be considered) and CTDs [2]. In patients with CTD, open, endovascular, or hybrid approaches are debated. Spinal cord ischemia (SCI) is a major risk of both open and endovascular repair, potentially leading to severe disability. To reduce this risk, preservation of spinal arteries, careful hemodynamic management, and preemptive supportive procedures (e.g., subclavian revascularization, MISACE-Concept) are discussed or advised respectively. Neuromonitoring using somatosensory evoked potentials (SSEP) and motor evoked potentials (MEP) can support intraoperative decisions.

We present a case in which an interdisciplinary staged hybrid approach was chosen for treatment of a complex post-traumatic thoracic aortic aneurysm in a patient with a variant of uncertain significance in the TGFB3 gene (MIM #615582), which can be placed within the spectrum of Loeys–Dietz syndrome type 5, but is not diagnostic. The focus of this report is on the execution of the staged treatment strategy, aiming primarily to prevent spinal cord ischemia and nerve injury.

Case report

A 41-year-old female patient was referred with progressive dyspnea and orthopnea, hoarseness, chronic cough, and recurrent pulmonary infections (3–4 times per year, with a noticeable increase in both frequency and severity over the last three years). She had sustained a ski accident 20 years before the current diagnosis. Her family history was negative for aneurysms or vascular dissections. On presentation she was hemodynamically stable and laboratory values were within normal limits. In the patient’s history, there have been unusually frequent bronchopulmonary infections in recent years, including at the time of admission. Computed tomography angiography (CTA) revealed a descending thoracic aortic aneurysm measuring 125 mm in maximal diameter without any thrombus or wall calcification, compressing the left subclavian artery (LSA), cardiac structures and the recurrent laryngeal and phrenic nerves. Given the patient’s body measures (168 cm, 59 kg), the aneurysm is disproportionately large relative to thoracic dimensions, resulting in relevant compression of the left lung. The left vertebral artery (VA) arose directly from the aortic arch between the left carotid artery and LSA as a common anatomic variation, indicated by the arrow in Fig. 1A. Additional findings included a circumferential, highly calcified stenotic lesion at the proximal neck of the aneurysm located at the aortic isthmus (at the typical location of an aortic transection) and an elongated and tortuous arch configuration. The aorta in landing zone (LZ) 3 was compressed by the aneurysm. Furthermore, aneurysms of a right renal accessory artery (10 mm) (Fig. 2A-D). Further aneurysmatic pathologies in splenic hilum (7 mm), and left internal cerebral artery (5 mm) were seen (not depicted).

Due to the aneurysm’s size as well as the associated respiratory compromise and nerve compression, urgent repair was indicated. A customized endograft (including fenestrated arch repair due to the lack of a landing zone) was deemed to be unsuitable due to the tortuous configuration, the anatomical variation of the left VA offspring and the time of manufacturing. A physician modified device (PMD) was not chosen due to the young patient’s age and the lack of long-term data for this still relatively experimental approach.

Another important reason for choosing a partially open procedure was the high clinical suspicion of a systemic CTD. In this context, a purely endovascular approach would have necessitated proximal sealing in landing zone 0, making a definitive surgical solution in this region appear more appropriate. An entirely open repair would have required a thoraco-abdominal approach and selective distal perfusion in a single-stage procedure, carrying a substantial risk of organ and spinal cord ischemia. In the region of the left VA and LSA, the combination of the aneurysm’s large size and pronounced anatomical deformities provided insufficient assurance of preserving neural structures, especially regarding the phrenic nerve. A multidisciplinary team opted for a staged hybrid approach, knowing that leaving the very large thoracic aneurysm formation may be associated with problems such as infection or relevant endoleak (EL). Surgical treatment was started 5 days after initial diagnosis of the pathology.

Stage 1: Frozen Elephant Trunk (FET)

A FET procedure was performed via median sternotomy using a Terumo Thoraflex™ 28/30/15 cm graft (Thoraflex™ Hybrid, Terumo Aortic, Inchinnan, Scotland, UK) with distal Anastomosis performed in zone II of the aortic arch and ending of the Stent-Segment of the FET in the aneurysm. The brachiocephalic trunk and left common carotid artery were reimplanted under deep hypothermic circulatory arrest (26 °C) and antegrade cerebral perfusion. The left VA and the LSA were not addressed during this stage. The patient had an uneventful recovery (1-day ICU stay, 5 days on ward).

Stage 2: Thoracic Endovascular Aortic Repair (TEVAR)

After interdisciplinary discussion and according to the patients wish, an endovascular completion was performed 3 weeks later after recovery from stage one. Spinal cord monitoring was performed using MEPs, SSEPs, and peripheral signals from the popliteal fossa to differentiate between potential spinal cord and peripheral ischemia. An individualized method to simulate the situation of spinal cord perfusion after TEVAR was performed: Two occlusion balloons were used (Reliant™ Balloon Catheter, Medtronic, Minneapolis, MN, USA), both inflated, to block blood flow in FET and intended distal landing zone, which simulated the coverage of lumbar arteries. Blocking the aortic portion equivalent to the minimal length of coverage by TEVAR led to transient loss of MEP signals, prompting debranching and transposition of the left VA to the left common carotid artery. Due to the direct origin of the left VA from the aortic arch, it could be selectively debranched. In contrast, the LSA was not revascularized because of dense adhesions and was intentionally covered. After transposition of the left VA, balloon occlusion tests were repeated, and neurophysiological monitoring showed no alterations of MEP/SSEP. Endovascular completion was performed by percutaneous transfemoral TEVAR, using a Cook – Stent graft (Zenith® Alpha Thoracic Endovascular Graft, Cook Medical, Bloomington, IN, USA, ZTA-PT-34-30-161, - ZTA-P-28-109, and - ZTA-P-28-155 devices with an overall treatment length of 18 cm distal to LSA. Angiographic result after completion is shown in Fig. 1B; the arrow indicating VA after transposition.

CTA and intraoperative Angiography confirmed exclusion of the aneurysm and patency of all reconstructions therefore, additional plug occlusion of the LSA was not performed.

A 3D video animation of the CT scans before and after treatment is available as an online supplemental video clip. A silent splenic infarction was observed. Despite nosocomial pneumonia, the further clinical course was uncomplicated, and the patient was discharged 7 days post-TEVAR to a rehabilitation clinic.

At 2-month follow-up, CTA showed a 70% stenosis in the anastomotic region of the reimplanted left carotid artery, correlating with dizziness and visual disturbances. A covered trans carotid stent graft-PTA of the stenotic lesion (2× Bentley BeGraft® Covered Stent, Bentley InnoMed GmbH, Hechingen, Germany, 9 mm and 7 mm × 37 mm) resolved symptoms. At 6 and 12 months, the patient remained neurologically intact. Hoarseness nearly completely resolved during FU, and orthopnea likewise improved; no further clinically relevant bronchial infections were observed. Imaging showed stable graft positioning and reduced aneurysm diameter (sac shrinkage: 12 mm to 113 mm after 12 month).

After completion of aortic repair, whole-genome sequencing revealed a heterozygous variant of uncertain significance in TGFB3 (MIM #615582, p.(Phe147Cys)), which may be suggestive of Loeys–Dietz syndrome type 5 but is not diagnostic.

Discussion

This case illustrates the complexity of managing large, symptomatic thoracic aortic aneurysms, especially in patients with initially undiagnosed CTD. The late diagnosis of a manifest CTD (TGFB3 gene, MIM #615582), emphasizes the importance of evaluating potential underlying CTD etiologies even in cases initially presumed to be purely posttraumatic. While open aneurysm repair remains the traditional standard in CTDs, recent data suggest that hybrid or endovascular strategies may now be viable and safe alternatives in selected cases with challenging anatomy in high-volume aortic centers [3, 4]. The absence of an adequate proximal landing zone rendered the use of a single-branched endovascular solution unsuitable in this case. Therefore, a hybrid strategy was favored. At present, the follow-up of this case amounts to 1.5 years. Nevertheless, particularly in endovascular and hybrid concepts as alternative treatment strategies to open surgery, close and structured follow-up is essential. In this case, the direct origin of the left VA from the aortic arch enabled selective debranching and critically contributed to spinal cord protection.

The left subclavian artery (LSA) was intentionally left unrevascularized despite the elective setting. Although this strategy remains controversial, particularly in younger patients, it was considered justified given the successful revascularization of the vertebral artery (VA), which plays a key role in spinal cord perfusion [5]. While current guideline recommendations generally favor LSA revascularization [6, 7] to reduce the risk of spinal cord ischemia, posterior circulation compromise, and upper extremity malperfusion, these are not directly applicable in this case due to the specific anatomical variant of the VA. Ensuring vertebrobasilar inflow and thereby perfusion of the anterior spinal artery was regarded as the decisive factor for spinal cord protection [8].

Endovascular revascularization of the LSA was deemed unfavorable due to anatomical constraints. Regarding alternative techniques such as hybrid solutions (e.g. as described in B-SAFER-Trial [9]), these were carefully evaluated but ultimately considered unsuitable in this specific case. However, due to the anatomical variant and a small vessel diameter (approximately 3.5 mm), the vertebral artery was not considered amenable to endovascular or hybrid reconstruction.

An extra-anatomic bypass was considered as a secondary option and would have been reserved for clinically relevant malperfusion of the left arm. In the present case, perfusion of the left upper extremity was not critically impaired, as verified by clinical examination, duplex ultrasonography, and oscillography. Given the restored vertebral artery perfusion and absence of ischemic signs, prophylactic bypass was not pursedu [8]. The staged approach, intraoperative neuromonitoring, blood pool management to avoid perioperative anemia and intraoperative balloon occlusion testing were instrumental in guiding these decisions and optimizing TEVAR graft positioning to minimize the risk of spinal ischemia in this case. Although balloon occlusion testing does not represent an exact simulation of spinal cord hypoperfusion, it is considered a reasonable approximation and was specifically adapted as a pragmatic simulation method in this case. This method may represent a complementary, case-dependent strategy for spinal cord protection, rather than a broadly generalizable approach. Balloon occlusion testing, as applied in the present case, should be interpreted as a surrogate assessment rather than a validated predictor of spinal cord ischemia. While it may provide adjunctive, case-specific information, it does not allow for definitive risk stratification.

Moreover, the current implementation is subject to inherent diagnostic limitations. Temporary occlusion results in cessation of perfusion to the entire lower body, which may lead to an overestimation of the impact on spinal cord perfusion and thereby reduces the specificity of the assessment.

To mitigate this limitation, a stepwise refinement of the technique may be considered. In particular, the use of an additional iliac perfusion sheath in a second diagnostic step could allow maintenance of distal perfusion and enable a more differentiated evaluation of spinal cord ischemia risk.

The three-week interval between stage 1 and stage 2 was determined by the recovery period following stage 1, which was complicated by a mild nosocomial pneumonia. In addition, the interval was prolonged due to an intermittently withdrawn consent by the patient regarding continuation of the treatment, driven by concerns about the risk of paraplegia. Ultimately, the staged hybrid approach enabled effective aneurysm exclusion with appropriate spinal protection and without neurological or relevant peripheral nerve complications.

To avoid later complications at a potential LZ 0 in a patient with suspected systemic vascular CTD, and with respect to the mentioned stenotic and anatomical challenges, an open surgical proximal anastomosis was preferred. A landing in zone 2 would not have provided a sufficiently reliable sealing zone. Although the available landing zone measured 32 mm and would have been technically feasible, it was considered too high-risk in the context of a suspected CTD. To minimize the duration of distal perfusion and cardiac arrest - and, most importantly, to prevent phrenic nerve injury - an endovascular solution was chosen for the distal segment. The potential for persistent compressive effects on adjacent neural structures due to the large aneurysmal sac was considered in the treatment strategy. However, a relevant and timely reduction in aneurysm sac size following endovascular exclusion was anticipated. In the event of persistent or progressive symptoms, a secondary surgical approach with aneurysm sac resection in the presence of the indwelling endografts would have been considered as a staged option. In addition to the reduction in aneurysm sac diameter due to shrinking after endovascular sealing, the decrease in pulsatile pressure within the aneurysm sac is a key factor explaining the improvement in clinical symptoms. Surgical sac-resection ultimately not required, as the observed reduction in sac size and pressure was sufficient to achieve symptomatic relief and thereby the intended therapeutic goal.

Accurate determination of the underlying etiology is crucial not only for diagnosis and surgical planning but also for follow-up. This applies equally to other vascular lesions, blood pressure management, and the propensity for intimal hyperplasia, which in our case necessitated early endolining. In this case the treatment strategy took the clinical suspicion of an underlying CTD into account. Although the findings were chronic, definitive genetic testing could not be awaited, as the patient’s respiratory compromise required timely intervention. Furthermore, the authors were not willing to accept the rupture risk associated with the aneurysm size alone while awaiting completion of the diagnostic work-up.

LDS itself is a rare entity, and according to a recent systematic review, only 3896 genetically confirmed cases have been reported in the literature to date. Pathologies involving TGFB3 mutations (like LDS Type 5) are among the rarest, with only a small number of reported cases [10]. While the identified TGFB3 variant is formally classified as a variant of uncertain significance, from a clinical perspective, it warrants consideration in line with a syndromic CTD. This is based on the established role of the TGF-β signaling pathway in the pathophysiology of heritable aortopathies, where disruptions are known to impair connective tissue integrity.

Conclusion

This case underlines the importance of a holistic and anticipatory approach in managing complex aortic lesions.

From an etiological point of view, it is important to understand that in patients with mono- and polygenetic CTD, minor trauma can also lead to aortic lesions.

Particular attention should be paid to the potential syndromic origin of aortic disease, especially in morphologically atypical or extensive presentations. Even before genetic confirmation, age, anatomical features - such as arterial tortuosity or multiple visceral aneurysms - or history should prompt evaluation and influence therapeutic strategy. In this context, in addition to strategies to avoid neurological complications, the solution to the proximal surgical anastomosis or endovascular solution for the proximal region is crucial. The solution must consider etiology, access trauma, and, above all, long-term options (including possible redo surgery). This case highlights the value of combining open and endovascular techniques of cardiac and vascular surgery within a center experienced in managing complex aortic pathology.
