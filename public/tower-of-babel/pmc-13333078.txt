Ventral temporal-frontal pathways in the human brain based on diffusion MRI tractography
Authors: Kep Kee Loh, Kyriaki Neophytou, Kristina Drudik, Elise B. Barbeau, Kyrana Tsapkini, Michael Petrides
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In the primate brain, two ventral fiber pathways connect the temporal cortex with the frontal cortex: the Uncinate Fasciculus (UF) and the Temporal-Frontal extreme capsule Fasciculus (TFexcF). The definition and reconstruction of these pathways in the human brain have been inconsistent between research groups and have led to conflicting findings about their functional roles. Here, we define the UF and TFexcF in the human brain based on a review of the temporal-frontal connections demonstrated in the macaque monkey via invasive tracer studies and a novel protocol for reconstructing them in the human brain using diffusion-weighted Magnetic Resonance Imaging (dMRI) tractography. A key advantage of the present protocol is the reconstruction of the tracts based on anatomically defined cortical terminations which would allow interpretation of their functional role given the cortical areas that they interconnect. The UF provides the interaction between orbitofrontal cortex and the anterior-medial temporal region involved in emotional processing and information integration in memory. The dorsal TFexcF enables the online monitoring of information within working memory and the attentional allocation of the highly processed visual, auditory and multisensory information in the anterior-to-middle temporal cortex. Finally, the ventral TFexcF enables the selective retrieval of highly processed visual, auditory and multisensory information via interaction of the ventrolateral prefrontal cortex with the anterior-to-middle temporal cortex.

Introduction

Functional communication between the frontal and temporal cortical regions of the human brain is critical for various aspects of cognitive processing associated with visual, auditory, and multisensory perception, memory storage and retrieval, and language comprehension and production. There are two ventral pathways linking the temporal cortex and the frontal cortex: the uncinate fasciculus (UF) and the temporo-frontal extreme capsule fasciculus (TFexcF). The UF consists of fibers that cross in the shape of a hook at the point where the anterior temporal lobe joins the frontal lobe. Several studies have consistently defined the UF as the hooked fibers that link the orbitofrontal cortex (OFC) with the temporal polar and antero-medial temporal region (Ebeling and v Cramon, 1992; Catani and Thiebaut de Schotten, 2008). However, these hook-shaped axons must be clearly differentiated from the non-curving ascending axons that course in the extreme capsule medial to the anterior insula and link the temporal lobe with the lateral frontal lobe. The axons that ascend in the extreme capsule medial to the anterior insula to reach the various lateral frontal areas were named the Temporo-Frontal extreme capsule Fasciculus (TFexcF) by Petrides and Pandya (1988) to differentiate them from the hook-shaped UF.

Currently, there is no clear consensus regarding the cortical origins and trajectories of the ventral temporal-frontal white matter pathways in the human brain (Ebeling and v Cramon, 1992; Catani and Thiebaut de Schotten, 2008; Barbeau et al., 2024). The existence of multiple models of the human ventral temporal-frontal pathways has made it complicated to study the cognitive roles of these pathways. For instance, there is currently an ongoing debate about which temporal-frontal pathway(s) are involved in semantic processing. Some researchers have argued for a role of the inferior occipito-frontal fasciculus in a ventral semantic stream (e.g., Duffau, 2008), but others have argued that it is instead the uncinate fasciculus (e.g., de Zubicaray et al., 2011), or the temporal-frontal extreme capsule fasciculus (e.g., Barbeau et al., 2024) that support semantic processing. This debate is hard to resolve since the different groups have each defined the pathways differently and reached different conclusions about their behavioral correlates. Because of its invasive nature, the injection of anatomical tracers, which is the gold-standard method for elucidating the precise origins, courses and terminations of connections in macaque monkeys, cannot be performed in human subjects. Post-mortem brain dissection has been the available method for assessing the white matter pathways in the human brain. This method allows the visualization of the main white matter fiber bundles and their main trajectories between various parts of the brain, but it cannot establish the precise cortical origins and terminations of the fiber bundles. Also, the gross dissection in cadaver brains for the demonstration of one fiber system in the brain can often damage other surrounding fiber systems. Thus, gross white matter dissection cannot disentangle the fiber systems that pass close to each other or intersect with one another within the same region.

In the present investigation, we use diffusion Magnetic Resonance Imaging (dMRI) tractography to examine the ventral temporo-frontal pathways in terms of their cortical terminations. We reviewed macaque monkey tracer injection studies to define the temporal-frontal connections that make up the UF and the TFexcF and their terminations in the frontal and temporal cortex. Next, we determined the corresponding cortical terminations of the UF and TFexcF in the human brain based on cytoarchitectonic homologies and constructed regions-of-interest (ROIs) in the Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) standard stereotaxic space (ICBM152 2009 nonlinear asymmetric) using the Human Connectome Project Multimodal Parcellation atlas (Glasser et al., 2016). These ROIs were subsequently used as termination ROIs for the extraction of the UF and the TFexcF streamlines from dMRI tractograms. To the best of our knowledge, there are no existing protocols that allow the reconstruction of the ventral temporo-frontal pathways based on their specific cortical terminations. A strong advantage of reconstructing tracts using cortical terminations is that the cortical areas interconnected by the tracts are clearly known and consistent across subjects. By contrast, reconstruction protocols that are based on seeding ROIs within the white matter might yield tracts that interconnect different cortical areas across subjects. This poses a critical problem when trying to understand the functional role of a tract which depends on the specific areas it connects. In the present work, we further divided the TFexcF into a ventral part (vTFexcF), which connects the ventrolateral prefrontal cortex (VLPFC, i.e., inferior frontal gyrus) with the temporal cortex, and a dorsal part (dTFexcF), which connects the dorsolateral frontal cortex (DLPFC, i.e., the middle and superior frontal gyri) with the temporal cortex. This subdivision was motivated by the fact that the DLPFC and VLPFC play distinct roles in cognition (e.g., Petrides, 2005).

We applied our protocol to extract the UF, dTFexcF and vTFexcF in 19 healthy adult subjects. Spatial probability maps of the tracts were constructed to define their average spatial location and trajectories in MNI space. Specific connectivity profiles of the UF, dTFexcF and vTFexcF were computed and the results provided the first insights regarding the connections that make up each tract. Finally, we discuss the functional roles of the UF, dTFexcF and vTFexcF based on studies with macaque and human subjects.

Method

Data

T1w structural and diffusion-weighted MRI brain scans were obtained from 19 healthy adult subjects (8 male and 11 female, mean age = 24.5, SD = 3.06) as part of a separate functional MRI study (Drudik et al., 2025). This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) McGill University Faculty of Medicine and Health Sciences (Protocol number: 21–11–043, date of approval: 10 January 2022). Informed written consent was obtained from the subjects to participate in the study according to the institutional guidelines established by the Research Ethics Committee of the Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI). The subjects were undergraduate and postgraduate students and postdoctoral personnel. The inclusion criteria were: right-handedness, age between 18 and 40 years, no previous history of neurological or psychiatric disease, and no serious medical conditions. Whole-brain tractograms were generated using anatomically constrained particle-filtering probabilistic tractography using Tractoflow version 2.4.2 (Theaud et al., 2020).

MRI acquisition

T1w structural and diffusion-weighted MRI brain scans were acquired on a Siemens 3 Tesla MAGNETOM Prisma scanner at the McConnell Brain Imaging Centre at the Montreal Neurological Institute, McGill University. The high-resolution T1w images were acquired using an MPRAGE sequence (TR = 2300 ms; TE = 2.96 ms; flip angle = 9°; 192 slices; isometric voxel size = 1 mm3). Diffusion-weighted MRI data were acquired using a multiband echoplanar imaging sequence (TR = 3200 ms; TE = 71.0 ms; 78 slices; b-values(directions) = 0(9), 400(7), 1250 (29), 2500(64) s/mm2; 109 gradient directions; isotropic voxel size = 2 mm3).

MRI processing

The T1w MRI structural data and diffusion-weighted MRI data were processed using the Tractoflow pipeline (version 2.4.2; (Theaud et al., 2020). For the T1w MRI data, we first performed a manual alignment of the images to the Anterior Commissure – Posterior Commissure (AC-PC) plane using ITK-SNAP. The AC-PC aligned images were then entered into the Tractoflow pipeline. T1w processing in the Tractoflow pipeline includes denoising (Garyfallidis et al., 2014), N4 bias correction, brain mask extraction, registration to the DWI space using antsRegistration (Avants et al., 2008) that involves a rigid and affine registration followed by a nonlinear Symmetric Normalization (SyN) registration, and tissue segmentation (Jenkinson et al., 2012) to compute the anatomical maps for diffusion tractography.

The processing of the diffusion-weighted MRI data in Tractoflow mainly includes denoising (MRtrix3 dwidenoise: Tournier et al., 2019), correction of deformation induced by the magnetic field susceptibility artifacts and eddy-currents (Topup and Eddy from FSL: Andersson et al., 2003; Smith et al., 2004; Andersson and Sotiropoulos, 2016), brain extraction (Bet from FSL: Smith, 2002), N4 bias correction, cropping, normalization, resampling, and finally the estimation of the fiber orientation distribution function (fODF) (Garyfallidis et al., 2014). The shells and b-values specified to compute the fODF metrics were “0 1250 2500″ following Tractoflow recommendations (i.e., b-values > 700 mm2/s).

Whole-brain tractograms were generated using anatomically constrained particle-filtering probabilistic tractography (Girard et al., 2014; Theaud et al., 2020) and seeding from the white matter with 20 seeds per voxel and other parameters left as default. Finally, streamlines that were found to make a 300-degree loop onto themselves were removed (Barbeau et al., 2020).

Definition of UF and TFexcF cortical termination ROIs

To define the cortical origins of the connections that constitute the UF and the TFexcF (i.e., dTFexcF and vTFexcF), we examined the available information based on comparative cytoarchitectonic investigations of the frontal cortex in the macaque monkey and human brains and the connectivity of these frontal areas with the temporal cortex in the macaque monkey using retrograde and anterograde tracers (Petrides and Pandya, 1988, 1999, 2002, 2006, 2007, 2009; Gerbella et al., 2010; Yeterian et al., 2012; Frey et al., 2014). We then classified each frontal and temporal area as being connected via the UF when the white matter made a sharp hook around the limen insula to connect the orbitofrontal cortex (OFC) with the temporal pole and antero-medial temporal lobe. The axons constituting the TFexcF exhibit an ascending (i.e., not hooked) trajectory between the temporal and frontal areas and course medial to the anterior insula through the extreme capsule. Based on the macaque UF and TFexcF frontal and temporal cortical terminations (Fig. 1, left panel; Table 1), we determined the corresponding cortical areas in the human brain based on cytoarchitectonic homologies, localized them in the MNI average brain (Petrides, 2019), and selected the corresponding parcels from the HCP-MMP atlas to construct the cortical termination ROIs for UF and TFexcF tract extraction (Fig. 1, middle panel; Table 1).

Uncinate fasciculus cortical termination ROIs

The frontal terminations of the macaque UF include orbitofrontal areas 11, 13 and 14 and the orbital aspect of area 47/12 and area 10 (Fig. 1; Table 1). Corresponding to these areas, we included the following HCP-MMP parcel areas to construct the human Orbitofrontal Cortical (OFC) ROI (Fig. 1): the orbital parts of area 47 – 47s and 47m; middle orbitofrontal areas – 11l and 13l; and the medial orbitofrontal areas – OFC and pOFC. The HCP-MMP areas 10v and 10pp were not included as part of the OFC ROI because they extended largely onto the medial and polar aspects of the frontal cortex and were connected via the TFexcF.

The temporal terminations of the macaque UF include the temporal pole and the adjacent entorhinal and perirhinal cortex (Fig. 1; Table 1). We included the following parcels for the human temporal polar/medial temporal (TP/MTC) cortical ROI (Fig. 1): temporal polar areas TGd and TGv, and medial temporal cortical areas EC and PeEC.

Temporal-frontal extreme capsule fasciculus cortical termination ROIs

The frontal terminations of the macaque TFexcF consist of the ventrolateral frontal areas 45 and 47/12 (lateral part) and the dorsolateral frontal areas 8A, 8B, 9, 9/46, 46 and 10 (lateral part) (Fig. 1; Table 1). Based on the location of these areas in the frontal lobe of the human brain, we constructed a dorsolateral prefrontal termination ROI (DLPFC) and a ventrolateral prefrontal termination ROI (VLPFC) (Fig. 1). This separation was motivated by the fact that the DLPFC and VLPFC are associated with different executive control processes (Petrides, 2005). The DLPFC ROI included superior frontal areas 9a, 9p and 9m; middle frontal areas a9–46v, p9–46v, 9–46d and 46; rostral frontal areas 10d, a10p, p10p, 10pp, 10v and 10r; and caudal frontal areas 8BL, 8BM, 8Ad, 8Av, 8C and s6–8. Note that we included the dorsomedial areas 8BM, 9m, 10r and ventromedial areas 10d, 10r and 10v in the DLPFC ROI because these areas also receive connections from the temporal cortex through the extreme capsule. The VLPFC ROI included the anterior inferior frontal areas a47r, p47r and 47l; middle inferior frontal areas 45, IFSa, and FOP5; and caudal inferior frontal areas 44, IFSp, IFJa and FOP4. The distinction of the prefrontal terminations into DLPFC and VLPFC, allowed us to reconstruct a dorsal and a ventral TFexcF, i.e., dTFexcF and vTFexcF, respectively.

The temporal connections of the macaque TFexcF, from both the DLPFC and VLPFC, reach the anterior-to-middle parts of the superior temporal gyrus (areas TS1, TS2, TS3), superior temporal sulcus (TAa, TPO, IPa, PGa, TPO, TEm, TEa), inferior temporal cortex (TE1, TE2, TE3), and the ventromedial temporal areas TH, TF and TL (Fig. 1). We constructed two separate ROIs in the human temporal cortex: a main mTC region that included the main part of all temporal gyri, except for the temporal pole and entorhinal and perirhinal medial temporal lobe (TP/MTC) and the posteriormost part of the temporal lobe at the border with the parietal and occipital lobes (pTC) (Fig. 1). The mTC consisted of cortical areas in the anterior-and-middle part of the superior temporal gyrus (STGa, TA2, A4 and A5), superior temporal sulcus (STSda, STSva, STSdp and STSvp), middle temporal gyrus (TE1a, TE1m and TE1p), inferior temporal gyrus (TE2a, TE2p) and ventromedial temporal areas (TF, PHA1, PHA2 and PHA3). The pTC consisted of cortical areas in the most posterior part of the superior temporal gyrus (STV, TPOJ1), inferior and middle temporal gyri (PHT, PH) and ventral temporal cortex (VVC, FFC). For the extraction of the dorsal and ventral TFexcF, a combined ROI including the mTC and the pTC were used. We separated the mTC and the pTC so that we could investigate the extent to which the temporal-frontal connections via the TFexcF reached the posterior parts of the temporal cortex in the human brain.

The three frontal termination ROIs (i.e., OFC, DLPFC and VLPFC ROIs) and the three temporal termination ROIs (i.e., the TP/MTC, mTC and pTC ROIs) are defined in the MNI standard stereotaxic space. The termination ROIs are presented in Fig. 1. The individual HCP-MMP areas that make up each termination ROI were derived from a volumetric version of the HCP-MMP atlas in the MNI ICBM152 2009 nonlinear asymmetric standard space available from the DSI Studio package (http://dsi-studio.labsolver.org).

Tract extraction

The extraction of the UF and TFexcF were performed on the whole-brain tractograms using the Trackvis software (Wang et al., 2007). The tractograms were first transformed into MNI space using SyN ANTS (Avants et al., 2008) between the T1w image and the MNI 2009c asymmetric template and applying the transformation to the tractogram (Greene et al., 2018). To ensure that only ventral temporal-frontal streamlines were selected (and not the temporal-frontal streamlines that course dorsally and posteriorly from the temporal cortex in the white matter under the parietal cortex, such as the arcuate fasciculus), two disc-shaped ROIs (one for each hemisphere) with a radius of 15 mm were manually drawn at the point where the temporal and frontal lobes joined and angled to capture the white matter streamlines of the UF and the TFexcF. This disc-shaped ROI was an inclusion ROI (“Any part” option) for both the extraction of the UF and the TFexcF. For the UF, the OFC ROI and the TP/MTC ROI were additionally set as inclusion ROIs using the “Either end” option to ensure that only streamlines that terminated in these ROIs were included. In the present study, we separated the TFexcF into a dorsal and a ventral part. For the dorsal TFexcF (dTFexcF), the DLPFC ROI and a combination of the mTC and pTC ROIs were included as ROIs. For the ventral TFexcF (vTFexcF), the VLPFC ROI and a combination of the mTC and pTC ROIs were included as ROIs. The termination ROIs (OFC, TP/MTC, DLPFC, mTC, pTC, VLPFC) are shown in the right panel in Fig. 1.

Tract quantitative analyses

The number of streamlines for each of the three tracts, in each hemisphere, was computed using the scil_count_streamlines.py from the Sherbooke Connectivity Imaging Lab Python dMRI processing toolbox (SCILPY; https://github.com/scilus/scilpy). The number of streamlines of each tract per subject hemisphere were then imported into RStudio (version 2023.06.2+561 running R version 4.3.1) for further analyses. For subsequent analyses, we included only successfully reconstructed tracts which are defined as tracts that contained >10 streamlines in both hemispheres. The mean, maximum and minimum number of streamlines of the successfully reconstructed tracts were computed separately for each hemisphere across subjects and presented in Table 1. To assess the potential asymmetries in the pathways, the laterality index (LI) for the number of streamlines in each white matter tract were calculated as follows: LI = (L − R)/(L + R). LI values ranged from −1 to 1, and positive and negative LI values correspond to left- and right-ward asymmetry, respectively (Thiebaut de Schotten et al., 2011). The statistical significance of the asymmetry (LI values) was assessed via a one-sample t-test implemented via the t.test function in R and reported in Table 1.

Tract spatial probability maps

Tract spatial probability maps for the UF, dTFexcF and vTFexcF were computed across subjects as follows: First, each subject’s tract was converted into a tract density map using scil_compute_streamlines_density_map.py (SCILPY; https://github.com/scilus/scilpy) that depicted the number of streamlines that pass through each voxel; second, each subject’s tract density map was binarized (i.e., all non-zero voxels set to 1) to obtain a map that depicted the spatial extent of the tract; finally, the binarized tract density maps were averaged across subjects in MNI ICBM152 2009 nonlinear asymmetric space to obtain a tract spatial probability map that depicted the probability of the tract at each voxel in the brain. Note that for computing the tract spatial probability maps, we included only subjects that demonstrated >10 streamlines on both hemispheres for a particular tract. As a result, the tract spatial probability maps for the UF, vTFexcF and dTFexcF were computed from 13, 15 and 16 subjects respectively. In Fig. 2, we display the spatial probability maps of the UF, dTFexcF and vTFexcF as a series of coronal sections in MNI space to elucidate the trajectories of the three tracts.

Tract intrinsic connectivity profiles

By virtue of our cortical-based tract reconstruction protocol, we were able to investigate the specific connectivity of the three ventral temporal to frontal cortex tracts, namely the UF, dTFexcF and vTFexcF. For each tract, we divided the frontal and temporal cortical ROIs into subregions to explore the extent to which the various frontal and temporal subregions interconnect through the tract (Fig. 3):

For the UF, we divided the OFC ROI into three subregions: Medial OFC (HCP-MMP areas OFC and pOFC), Middle OFC (HCP-MMP areas 11l and 13l) and Lateral OFC (HCP-MMP areas 47m and 47s), and the TP/MTC ROI into three subregions: Dorsal Temporal Pole (HCP-MMP area TGd), Ventral Temporal Pole (HCP-MMP area TGv) and Medial Temporal Cortex (HCP-MMP areas PEec and EC).

For the dTFexcF, we divided the DLPFC ROI into four subregions: Rostral DLPFC (HCP-MMP areas 10d, a10p, p10p, 10pp, 10v, and 10r), Superior DLPFC (HCP-MMP areas 9a, 9p and 9m), Middle DLPFC (HCP-MMP areas a9–46v, p9–46v, 9–46d, and 46) and Caudal DLPFC (HCP-MMP areas 8BL, 8Ad, 8Av, 8C, s6–8, and 8BM), and the temporal ROI into 13 subregions: Anterior STG (HCP-MMP areas STGa and TA2), Middle STG (HCP-MMP areas A4 and A5), Posterior STG (HCP-MMP areas STV and TPOJ1), Anterior STS (HCP-MMP areas STSda and STSva), Middle STS (HCP-MMP areas STSdp and STSvp), Anterior MTG (HCP-MMP area TE1a), Middle MTG (HCP-MMP areas TE1m and TE1p), Posterior MTG (HCP-MMP area PHT), Anterior ITG (HCP-MMP area TE2a), Middle ITG (HCP-MMP area TE2p), Posterior ITG (HCP-MMP area PH), Anterior VTC (HCP-MMP area TF) and Posterior VTC (HCP-MMP areas PHA 1–3, VVC and FFC).

For the vTFexcF, we divided the VLPFC ROI into three subregions: Rostral VLPFC (HCP-MMP areas a47r, 47l, and p47r), Middle VLPFC (HCP-MMP areas 45, IFSa, and FOP5) and Caudal VLPFC (HCP-MMP IFSp, IFJa, 44 and FOP4). The 13 subregions of the temporal ROI are identical to those of the dTFexcF described above.

For each tract, we computed the number of streamlines between each pair of frontal-temporal subregions in each subject hemisphere using the scil_filter_tractogram.py (SCILPY; https://github.com/scilus/scilpy). We then normalized the streamline counts between each frontal-temporal subregion pair by dividing them by the total number of streamlines in the tract in each subject hemisphere and expressing the result as a percentage. The intrinsic frontal-temporal connectivity profile of each tract was then obtained by averaging the percentages of streamline counts for each frontal-temporal subregion pair in the tract across subjects and hemispheres. The average intrinsic connectivity profile of each tract is presented as boxplots in Fig. 3.

Results

This research defined the human ventral temporal-frontal white matter pathways, namely the Uncinate Fasciculus (UF) and the Temporal-Frontal extreme capsule Fasciculus (TFexcF), based on existing gold-standard invasive tracing studies of the temporal-frontal pathways in the macaque brain, and provided a novel protocol for reconstructing the UF, and the dorsal (dTFexcF) and ventral TFexcF (vTFexcF) based on their cortical terminations. The protocol was applied to extract the UF, and the dTFexcF and vTFexcF from the preprocessed whole-brain dMRI tractograms of the 19 healthy subjects to examine the anatomical properties of these pathways. The characteristics of the extracted tracts are reported in Table 2.

Out of the 19 subjects, the number of subjects with successful reconstructions (number of streamlines > 10 on both hemispheres) of the UF, dTFexcF and vTFexcF were 13, 16 and 15, respectively. For the UF, the mean (standard deviation) streamline counts for left and right hemispheres were 110.6 (104.4) and 229.2 (185.7) respectively. The mean laterality index (LI) was −0.33 and significantly differed from 0 (t (12) = −3.43, p = 0.005) indicating a significant rightward asymmetry. For the dTFexcF, the mean (SD) streamline counts for left and right hemispheres were 727.7 (764.0) and 511.5 (782.3) respectively. The mean LI was +0.27 and significantly differed from 0 (t (15) = 2.40, p = 0.03) indicating a significant leftward asymmetry. For the vTFexcF, the mean (SD) streamline counts for left and right hemispheres were 209.9 (166.9) and 197.3 (222.8) respectively. The mean LI was −0.07 and did not differ significantly from 0 (t (14) = −0.64, p = 0.54) indicating no significant asymmetry for the vTFexcF.

Tract spatial probability maps

For the UF, the tract probability maps in the left and right hemispheres were constructed with data from 13 subjects (Fig. 2). The UF extended from the temporal pole and adjacent anteromedial temporal lobe to the OFC. The highest degree of overlap occurred between y = −5 to y = 30 (Fig. 2). Starting from the rostral part of the OFC (y ~ +55), fibers from various orbitofrontal regions traverse caudally in the white matter and stay below the putamen and the globus pallidus (around y ~ +35 to +5) until the point where the temporal lobe joins the frontal lobe (y ~ 0). At this point, the UF fibers traverse through the temporal stem, forming the characteristic hook, and extend ventrally and anteriorly to reach the cortex in the temporal pole (y ~ +15 to +20) and the anterior medial temporal cortex (y ~ −5 to +5). In this anterior medial temporal region lies the rhinal sulcus that defines the entorhinal and perirhinal cortex, below which are found the amygdala and the anterior part of the hippocampus. Thus, the fibers of the hook-shaped UF link the orbitofrontal cortex with the temporal polar cortex, the entorhinal/perirhinal cortex and the amygdala and anterior hippocampus.

For the dTFexcF, the tract probability maps were constructed with data from 16 subjects (Fig. 2). The dTFexcF extended from the anterior-to-middle and posterior parts of the temporal cortex to the DLPFC. The highest degree of overlap occurred between y = −10 to y = +20 (Fig. 2). Starting from the rostral part of the DLPFC (y ~ +65), fibers from various dorsolateral frontal regions traverse caudally and ventrally in the white matter in the extreme capsule (around y ~ +20 to +5) until the point where the temporal cortex joins the frontal cortex (y ~ 0). At this point, dTFexcF fibers traverse down the temporal stem and extend ventrally into the temporal cortex. Some of the fibers reach the anterior parts of the temporal cortex (y ~ +0 to +20), while others extend posteriorly to reach the middle and posterior parts of the temporal cortex (y ~ −5 to −60).

For the vTFexcF, the tract probability maps were constructed with data from 15 subjects (Fig. 2). The vTFexcF extended from the anterior-to-middle and posterior parts of the temporal cortex to the ventrolateral frontal cortex. The highest degree of overlap occurred between y = −5 to y = +20 (Fig. 2). Starting from the rostral part of the VLPFC (y ~ +60), fibers from various ventrolateral frontal regions traverse caudally and ventrally in the white matter in the extreme capsule (around y ~ +30 to +5) until the point where the temporal cortex joins the frontal cortex (y ~ 0). At this point, the vTFexcF fibers traverse down the temporal stem and extend ventrally into the temporal cortex. Some of the fibers extend anteriorly to reach the anterior parts of the temporal cortex (y ~ +0 to +20), while others extend posteriorly to reach the middle and posterior parts of the temporal cortex (y ~ −5 to −60).

Tract intrinsic connectivity profiles

To demonstrate the specific temporal-frontal connectivity of the UF, dTFexcF and vTFexcF, we computed for each tract, the average percentage of streamlines that connect each pair of temporal and frontal subregions across hemispheres and subjects. The temporal to frontal connectivity profile of the UF, dTFexcF, and vTFexcF are presented as boxplots in Fig. 3.

For the UF, the strongest connectivity was observed between the dorsal temporal pole (HCP-MMP area TGd) and the middle part of the orbitofrontal cortex (HCP-MMP areas 11l and 13l) (mean percentage of streamlines = 62.7%). Of the three temporal subregions, the temporal pole showed the strongest connectivity across the medial (HCP-MMP areas OFC and pOFC) (mean percentage of streamlines = 7.5%), lateral (HCP-MMP areas 47m and 47s) (mean percentage of streamlines = 5.5%) and middle orbitofrontal cortex (mean percentage of streamlines = 62.7%). The ventral temporal pole showed moderate connectivity only to the middle orbitofrontal cortex (mean percentage of streamlines = 9.9%) but not with the medial (mean percentage of streamlines = 0.9%) and lateral orbitofrontal cortex (mean percentage of streamlines = 0.9%). The medial temporal cortex (HCP-MMP areas PeEC and EC) showed almost no connectivity (mean percentage of streamlines < 0.3%) with the orbitofrontal cortex (but see, Discussion section).

For the dTFexcF, the largest percentage of streamlines (mean percentage of streamlines = 9.8%) was observed between the rostral DLPFC (HCP-MMP areas 10d, a10p, p10p, 10pp, 10v, and 10r) and the anterior inferior temporal gyrus (HCP-MMP area TE2a). Strong connectivity was also observed between the rostral DLPFC and the anterior STS (HCP-MMP areas STSda and STSva) (mean percentage of streamlines = 4.5%), middle STS (HCP-MMP areas STSdp and STSvp) (mean percentage of streamlines = 4.3%), anterior MTG (HCP-MMP area TE1a) (mean percentage of streamlines = 4.0%), middle MTG (HCP-MMP areas TE1m and TE1p) (mean percentage of streamlines = 5.1%), and the anterior ventral temporal cortex (HCP-MMP area TF) (mean percentage of streamlines = 5.8%). In the superior temporal gyrus, only the anterior part (HCP-MMP area STGa and TA2) showed some weak connectivity (mean percentage of streamlines = 2.3%) with the rostral DLPFC.

Compared with the rostral DLPFC, the superior DLPFC (HCP-MMP areas 9a, 9p and 9m), middle DLPFC (HCP-MMP areas a9–46v, p9–46v, 9–46d, and 46) and caudal DLPFC (HCP-MMP areas 8BL, 8Ad, 8Av, 8C, s6–8, and 8BM) showed weaker temporal connectivity overall but the same pattern of temporal connectivity with the anterior ITG showing the strongest connectivity (mean percentage of streamlines = 1.8% – 4.9%) followed by the anterior-to-middle STS (mean percentage of streamlines = 1.3% – 3.6%), anterior-to-middle MTG (mean percentage of streamlines = 1.7% – 3.4%), anterior ventral temporal cortex (mean percentage of streamlines = 1.8% – 4.9%) and the anterior STG (mean percentage of streamlines = 0.6% – 1.7%).

Across the four DLPFC subregions, there was very weak connectivity (mean percentage of streamlines = 0 – 1.4%) from the middle STG (HCP-MMP areas A4 and A5), posterior STG (HCP-MMP areas STV and TPOJ1), posterior MTG (HCP-MMP area PHT), middle ITG (HCP-MMP area TE2p), posterior ITG (HCP-MMP area PH) and the posterior ventral temporal cortex (HCP-MMP areas PHA1–3, VVC and FFC).

For the vTFexcF, the strongest connectivity (mean percentage of streamlines = 18.8%) was observed between the rostral VLPFC (HCP-MMP areas a47r, 47l and p47r) and the anterior ITG. Strong connectivity was also observed between the rostral VLPFC and the anterior STS (mean percentage of streamlines = 6.1%), middle STS (mean percentage of streamlines = 6.6%), anterior MTG (mean percentage of streamlines = 6.1%), middle MTG (mean percentage of streamlines = 6.2%) and the anterior ventral temporal cortex (mean percentage of streamlines = 7.8%). In the superior temporal gyrus, only the anterior STG showed some weak connectivity (mean percentage of streamlines = 2.6%) with the rostral VLPFC. For the rostral VLPFC, connectivity with the middle-posterior STG, posterior MTG, middle-posterior ITG and the posterior VTC was weak (mean percentage of streamlines = 0.3 – 2.0%).

The middle VLPFC (HCP-MMP areas 45, IFSa, and FOP5) showed the same pattern of temporal connectivity as the rostral VLPFC but the connectivity was overall weaker: there was stronger connectivity with the anterior-middle STS (mean percentage of streamlines = 3.2% – 4.7%), anterior-middle MTG (mean percentage of streamlines = 3.1% – 4.3%), anterior ITG (mean percentage of streamlines = 9.1%), anterior VTC (mean percentage of streamlines = 8%) and anterior STG (mean percentage of streamlines = 1.7%) and very weak connectivity with the middle-posterior STG (mean percentage of streamlines = 0.3%), posterior MTG (mean percentage of streamlines = 0.9%), middle-posterior ITG (mean percentage of streamlines = 0.5%) and posterior VTC (mean percentage of streamlines = 0.2%). Interestingly, the most posterior part of the VLPFC (HCP-MMP areas 44, IFSp, IFJa, FOP4) showed very weak connectivity (mean percentage of streamlines = 0 – 1.0%) to all temporal subregions.

Discussion

The distinct connectivity profiles of the ventral temporal-frontal pathways were previously examined in the macaque monkey based on gold-standard invasive tract-tracing methods. A focused review of macaque tract-tracing studies was performed to determine the cortical terminations of the connections that make up the uncinate fasciculus (UF) and the dorsal (dTFexcF) and ventral (vTFexcF) temporal-frontal extreme capsule fasciculi. Based on these macaque cortical terminations, we defined the cytoarchitectonic homologs in the human brain and constructed ROIs using the publicly available HCP-MMP atlas (Glasser et al., 2016) for extracting the UF, the dTFexcF and the vTFexcF in a dMRI protocol we developed.

This protocol allowed us to extract tracts that interconnect the same set of cortical areas across subjects. There are several key advantages of the present protocol. First, the extracted tracts will be more functionally homogeneous (since they interconnect the same areas across subjects) and thus potential behavioral correlations should be more detectable with these tracts versus tracts with varying cortical terminations across subjects. Second, tract-behavioral correlations should be more interpretable since the cortical terminations of the extracted tracts are known. Note that we have performed the tractogram processing using an automated pipeline with minimal changes from its default parameters (Tractoflow version 2.4.2) and the tract extraction using the identical set of ROIs across subjects in MNI space. The functional role of the three pathways based on currently available information is briefly discussed below.

Uncinate fasciculus

The UF is the white matter pathway that interconnects the orbitofrontal cortex with the temporal polar and anterior-medial parts of the temporal cortex, such as the entorhinal and perirhinal cortex, which provide the major input to the hippocampal memory system and the amygdala-based emotional system. The UF showed significant rightward asymmetry with more streamlines in the right compared to the left hemisphere (Table 2), an observation that is congruent with Highley et al. (2002) who reported that, based on a study of post-mortem brains, the UF was 27% larger and contained 33% more fibers in the right than the left hemisphere.

Based on the average spatial probability map of the UF (Fig. 2), we can discern the general trajectory of the UF streamlines originating from various orbitofrontal cortical regions and the temporal polar and adjacent anterior-medial temporal region. The UF converges as a bundle of white matter fibers (from y = −5 to y = −10) forming a hook that curves around the limen insula. The point where the UF fibers first connect the temporal cortex with the frontal cortex lies between y = −5 and y = 0 in MNI space. This location is comparable to the center-of-gravity of the UF stem (i.e., the point where all fibers pass through) at y ~ −2 reported by Hau and colleagues (2017).

We further investigated the intrinsic connectivity profile of the UF by computing the mean proportion of streamlines that interconnected each pair of sub-regions that constituted the temporal and frontal ROIs (Fig. 3). This analysis revealed that the largest proportion of UF streamlines interconnected the dorsal temporal pole (area TGd) with the middle part of the orbitofrontal cortex (11l and 13l). This was in line with Hau and colleagues (2016) who reported that the highest termination density of the UF was found in the temporal pole and the medial frontal-orbital gyri (where 11l and 13l lie). Notably, the UF connectivity to the ventral part of the temporal pole (area TGv) was less pronounced, indicating a dissociation between dorsal and ventral parts of the temporal pole. UF connectivity to the more medial (areas OFC and pOFC) and lateral (areas 47m and 47s) parts of the OFC are also significantly weaker than the connectivity to the middle OFC (areas 11l and 13l). In agreement, Hau and colleagues (2016) also reported fewer UF terminations to the more lateral and medial parts of the orbitofrontal cortex and to the medial temporal region. Tract-tracing experiments in the macaque brain have revealed monosynaptic connections from the UF to the medial temporal regions (e.g., perirhinal and entorhinal cortex). However, in our results, UF connectivity to the medial temporal cortex was weak. Other studies that had performed UF reconstructions using dMRI tractography (e.g., Thiebaut de Schotten et al., 2012; Hau et al., 2016; Panesar et al., 2017) also did not observe medial temporal projections in the entorhinal and perirhinal cortex. We believe that the failure to observe UF projections to the medial temporal cortex could be related to MRI signal loss and distortion effects often associated with this region (Olman et al., 2009). In their anatomical dissections, Ebeling and Cramon (1992) reported UF projections to the anterior temporal (BA 20, 38) and medial temporal regions (BA 28, 34 and 36). Future work is needed to verify the anterior-medial temporal projections via the UF by considering the details of each subject’s individual anatomy to define specific cortical termination ROIs

In both the macaque and human brains, the UF interconnects the orbitofrontal cortex with the temporal pole and anterior-medial parts of the temporal cortex where the emotion-regulating amygdala lies (Aggleton et al., 1980; Rempel-Clower and Barbas, 2000; Ghashghaei and Barbas, 2002) and the adjacent entorhinal and perirhinal cortex that provides major input to the hippocampal memory system (Suzuki and Amaral, 2004). The orbitofrontal cortex plays an important role in the regulation of emotional information and the representation of reward values (Rolls et al., 2020). This role is achieved via its direct UF connections with the anterior-medial temporal cortex, which provides access to emotional information and past experiences via the amygdala and the hippocampal memory system. The orbitofrontal cortex is also directly linked via the UF to the temporal pole which is associated with the integration of highly processed auditory and visual inputs from the posterior temporal regions with emotions and past experiences from the adjacent medial temporal regions (Olson et al., 2007). This connectivity thus allows the regulation by the orbitofrontal cortex of the highly processed emotional-multisensory information from the temporal pole. In other words, the UF is a critical pathway allowing the interaction between reward value processing (orbitofrontal cortex), multisensory and emotional information integration (temporal pole), and memory retrieval and storage (hippocampal system). UF abnormalities are also associated with social-emotional processing problems in several clinical populations (Von Der Heide et al., 2013), such as behavioral inhibition in behavioral variant frontotemporal dementia (Hornberger et al., 2011), Capgras delusion (i.e., believing that a familiar person was replaced by an imposter) (Edelstyn et al., 2001), and reduplicative paramnesia (i.e., believing that a person, place, object, or event has been duplicated or exists in two different places at the same time) (Lee et al., 2011). Furthermore, the UF has also been associated with reward-based learning and retrieval of complex associations in both monkey (Browning and Gaffan, 2008) and human subjects (Thomas et al., 2015).

Dorsal temporal-frontal extreme capsule fasciculus

In the present study, we dissociated the TFexcF into a dorsal (dTFexcF) and a ventral (vTFexcF) component in appreciation of the functional dissociation between the dorsal and ventral parts of the lateral frontal cortex (see Petrides, 2005). The dTFexcF is a major temporal-frontal white matter pathway that interconnects the dorsolateral frontal cortex with the temporal cortex. Based on the average spatial probability map of the dTFexcF (Fig. 2), the general trajectory of the dTFexcF streamlines that originate from various cortical regions in the dorsolateral frontal cortex and the temporal cortex converge in the white matter that joins the frontal and temporal cortex. The highest overlap of dTFexcF occurred between y = −10 to y = 20 in MNI space where the streamlines travel dorsally and anteriorly from the temporal cortex, through the extreme capsule that lies medial to the anterior insula and proceeds in a dorsal direction into the dorsolateral frontal regions. We observed a convergence of these three pathways, i.e., the dTFexcF, vTFexcF and UF, at y = 0 (Fig. 2) because this is the narrow space through which the pathways must cross to link the temporal with the frontal cortex.

We observed the strongest connectivity in the dTFexcF between the anterior inferior temporal cortex and the rostral DLPFC (Fig. 3). There was moderate dTFexcF connectivity from the DLPFC regions to the anterior-to-middle superior temporal sulcus (STS), anterior-to-middle middle temporal gyrus (MTG), anterior superior temporal gyrus (STG) and the anterior part of the ventral temporal cortex (VTC). In the primate temporal cortex, the anterior-to-middle parts of the STS and MTG are involved in multisensory information processing and the anterior-to-middle parts of the ITG and VTC are involved in high-level visual processing (for review, see Petrides, 2023). As such, the strong connectivity of the dTFexcF with these regions could suggest a dominance of this pathway for processing visual and multisensory information. In contrast, we observed that dTFexcF connectivity to the STG, which is associated with auditory processing (Petrides, 2023) was relatively weaker. Within the STG, dTFexcF connectivity to the anterior STG was relatively stronger than the middle-posterior STG. Based on macaque monkey studies, the anterior and posterior parts of the superior temporal cortex are respectively specialized for object versus spatial-related auditory processing (Rauschecker and Scott, 2009). Thus, in the auditory domain, the dTFexcF could be supporting the interaction between the DLPFC and the anterior STG for object-related auditory processing. The interaction between the DLPFC and the posterior STG for spatial-related auditory processing could be supported by the arcuate fasciculus (see Barbeau et al., 2023). Finally, dTFexcF connectivity to the posterior parts of the temporal cortex was weaker compared to the anterior-middle regions. These observations agree with Barbeau and colleagues (2023, 2024) who suggested that the TFexcF mainly connects the anterior-to-intermediate part of the temporal cortex with the anterior regions of frontal cortex whereas the arcuate fasciculus connects the posterior part of the temporal cortex with the posterior lateral frontal cortical regions.

In the frontal lobe, the dTFexcF connects the DLPFC that has been shown to be critical for the monitoring of information within working memory (see Petrides, 2005). In the temporal cortex, the dTFexcF connects to the ITG and adjacent fusiform gyrus in the ventral temporal cortex that is involved in the recognition and processing of visual information, such as faces and objects and words (McCandliss et al., 2003; Grill-Spector and Weiner, 2014; Weiner and Zilles, 2016). Between the STG that processes auditory information and the inferior and ventral temporal lobe that processes visual information, multisensory information converges within the deep STS in the macaque monkey (Seltzer and Pandya, 1978) and this is connected with prefrontal cortex (Seltzer and Pandya, 1989). This middle temporal cortical region has expanded in the human brain, and it has been argued that, in the language dominant left hemisphere of the human brain, the multisensory integration leads to semantic processing of language (Petrides, 2023). Thus, the dTFexcF is key for permitting the interaction between DLPFC and the temporal cortex to monitor information within working memory. Barbeau and colleagues (2023) suggested that the connection between the caudal frontal cortex and the posterior temporal cortex via the Arcuate Fasciculus plays a critical role in the allocation of attention to stimuli.

Ventral temporal-frontal extreme capsule fasciculus

The vTFexcF is a major temporal-frontal white matter pathway that interconnects the ventrolateral frontal cortex with the temporal cortex. Based on the average spatial probability map of the vTFexcF (Fig. 2), vTFexcF streamlines that link cortical regions in the ventrolateral frontal cortex with the temporal cortex converge in the white matter below the anterior insula. The highest overlap of vTFexcF occurred between y = −5 to y = +20 in MNI space where the streamlines travel dorsally and anteriorly from the temporal cortex, through the extreme capsule, to terminate into the ventrolateral frontal regions.

Within the vTFexcF, the strongest connectivity is observed between anterior inferior temporal cortex with the anterior VLPFC (Fig. 3). There is moderate vTFexcF connectivity to the anterior-middle parts of the middle temporal gyrus, superior temporal sulcus, and the anterior superior temporal region from the anterior and middle VLPFC regions. There is weak vTFexcF connectivity to the caudal VLPFC region, the posterior temporal cortex, and the middle-posterior parts of the superior temporal gyrus. These results agree with Barbeau and colleagues (2023) who showed that the posterior part of the superior temporal gyrus is connected with the caudal VLPFC via the Arcuate Fasciculus. As with the dTFexcF, there is stronger vTFexcF connectivity to the inferior-ventral and middle parts of the temporal cortex, which are associated with visual and multisensory information processing, and the anterior part of the superior temporal gyrus which is involved in object-related auditory processing. Connectivity with the posterior temporal cortex (spatial-related auditory processing) is weak.

In the language dominant left hemisphere, areas 44 and 45 on the inferior frontal gyrus constitute the language production region (Mohr et al., 1978; Levine et al., 2003; Petrides, 2014). The inferior frontal gyrus in the human brain that is linked with the temporal lobe via the vTFexcF is primarily the pars triangularis (area 45) and the pars orbitalis (area 47/12), as was shown above. The pars opercularis (area 44) is more strongly connected with the posterior superior temporal gyrus (Wernicke’s area) via the arcuate fasciculus that curves around the posterior end of the Sylvian fissure (Kaplan et al., 2010; Petrides, 2014). Area 44 appears to be the area organizing the speech motor act, but the granular prefrontal cortical area 45 has been shown to be involved in the selective retrieval of information from posterior cortical areas that precedes the production of speech, such as lexical retrieval (Petrides, 2014; Barbeau et al., 2023). By connecting the anterior ventrolateral frontal areas, which are involved in selective information retrieval, with the intermediate part of the inferior and middle temporal region and the anterior superior temporal gyrus, where highly processed visual, multisensory and object-related auditory information is represented, the vTFexcF is a pathway for the selective retrieval of highly processed information for cognitive operations in the prefrontal cortex. Interestingly, the vTFexcF connectivity between the caudal ventrolateral frontal region and the middle and posterior superior temporal region is weak. This finding suggests that the interaction between these regions, which are involved in the organization of speech acts (area 44) and speech processing (posterior superior temporal gyrus), could be supported by a distinct pathway – the Arcuate Fasciculus (Petrides, 2014; Barbeau et al., 2023).

Limitations

A limitation of the present study is the relatively small sample size (n = 19). Accordingly, the number of successfully reconstructed tracts included in the main analyses were limited (UF = 13, vTFexcF = 15 and dTFexcF = 16). This might limit the generalizability of our findings regarding streamline densities, tract asymmetry and connectivity profiles of the three tracts. The reconstructed tracts also show great inter-subject variability in terms of the number of streamlines (Table 2). To address this limitation, future work will involve applying our tract extraction protocol to large public neuroimaging datasets with higher quality diffusion data and adopting state-of-the-art tractography techniques to improve the tractogram estimation.

Conclusions

The present article has provided a definition of the human ventral temporal-frontal white matter pathways based on information from gold-standard invasive tracing data in the macaque monkey, and a standardized protocol for extracting these tracts using dMRI tractography based on the frontal and temporal terminations of the pathways. This protocol will pave the way for future investigations examining the functional role of the ventral temporal-frontal white matter pathways. It will also provide a framework for identifying and explaining deficits from impairments in these pathways in clinical populations.
