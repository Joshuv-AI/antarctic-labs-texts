Association between insulin resistance–related indices and the risk of recurrence after catheter ablation in patients with nonvalvular atrial fibrillation
Authors: Zhaofen Wang, Haijuan Wang, Xiaoxue Guo, Chu Zhang, Peng Chang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundInsulin resistance (IR) is closely associated with the development and recurrence of atrial fibrillation (AF). Several non-insulin-based surrogate markers of insulin resistance, including the triglyceride-glucose index (TyG), triglyceride-glucose-body mass index (TyG-BMI), metabolic score for insulin resistance (METS-IR), triglyceride-to-high-density lipoprotein cholesterol ratio (TG/HDL-C), estimated glucose disposal rate (eGDR), and C-reactive protein-triglyceride-glucose index (CTI), can be calculated from routine clinical biomarkers. However, the value of these indices in predicting recurrence after catheter ablation for AF remains unclear. In particular, CTI, a novel marker integrating inflammatory and metabolic status, has not been fully explored for its potential role in predicting post-ablation AF recurrence. This study aimed to systematically evaluate the association and predictive performance of six insulin resistance surrogate markers for AF recurrence after catheter ablation.MethodsThis retrospective two-center cohort study included 590 patients with nonvalvular AF who underwent first-time catheter ablation between January 2022 and March 2025. Six IR surrogate indices were calculated, and AF recurrence within 1 year was defined as the primary endpoint. Multivariable Cox regression, Kaplan-Meier analysis, restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis, C-statistic, net reclassification improvement (NRI), and integrated discrimination improvement (IDI) were used to assess associations and predictive value.ResultsDuring 1-year follow-up, 167 patients experienced recurrence (28.31%). Multivariable Cox analysis showed that lower eGDR and higher TyG, TyG-BMI, METS-IR, and CTI were associated with increased recurrence risk, with more stable associations observed for eGDR, TyG-BMI, METS-IR, and CTI. Kaplan-Meier and RCS analyses further supported stratified differences and exposure-response relationships for some indices. For predictive performance, eGDR showed the most favorable overall performance, while CTI, TyG-BMI, and METS-IR also provided incremental predictive value. TyG and TG/HDL-C showed relatively limited incremental value. After false discovery rate correction, subgroup analyses showed generally consistent results.Conclusion Among the six IR surrogate indices, eGDR, CTI, TyG-BMI, and METS-IR were associated with AF recurrence after catheter ablation. eGDR showed the most favorable overall predictive performance. These indices may serve as adjunctive markers for post-ablation recurrence risk stratification, although prospective validation is needed.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12933-026-03306-w.

Introduction

AF is one of the most common cardiac arrhythmias and is associated with an increased risk of stroke, heart failure, and mortality [1]. Although catheter ablation is an important rhythm-control strategy for symptomatic AF, recurrence after ablation remains common, particularly in patients with persistent AF [2, 3]. Therefore, identifying reliable and easily accessible predictors of post-ablation recurrence is clinically important for risk stratification and follow-up management.

IR has been increasingly recognized as a contributor to the development and recurrence of AF [4, 5]. Potential mechanisms include atrial electrical remodeling, structural remodeling, oxidative stress, inflammation, fibrosis, and autonomic dysfunction [6–8]. However, direct assessment of IR using the hyperinsulinemic-euglycemic clamp is complex and impractical in routine clinical settings, while Homeostasis Model Assessment of Insulin Resistance (HOMA-IR) requires insulin measurement [9]. Therefore, several non-insulin-based surrogate markers, including TyG, TyG-BMI, TG/HDL-C, METS-IR, eGDR, and CTI, have been proposed and can be calculated using routinely available clinical and laboratory parameters [10–14].

Although these surrogate indices have shown value in cardiovascular risk assessment [15], evidence regarding their role in predicting AF recurrence after catheter ablation remains limited [9]. Existing studies have mainly focused on individual markers, whereas the comparative predictive performance and incremental value of different IR-related indices remain to be further clarified. In particular, CTI, a novel marker integrating inflammatory and metabolic status, has not yet been fully explored for its potential value in predicting post-ablation AF recurrence [16].

Therefore, the present study aimed to systematically evaluate the association between six IR surrogate markers and 1-year recurrence after catheter ablation in patients with nonvalvular AF. We further compared their predictive performance and incremental value beyond conventional clinical risk factors, with the goal of providing additional evidence for post-ablation recurrence risk stratification.

Methods

Study population

This study is a retrospective cohort study. We consecutively enrolled patients with nonvalvular AF who underwent their first catheter ablation procedure at the Second Hospital of Lanzhou University and the First Hospital of Lanzhou University between January 2022 and March 2025. Inclusion criteria included: (1) age ≥ 18 years; (2) first-time AF catheter ablation. Exclusion criteria included: (1) valvular heart disease; (2) end-stage renal disease; (3) significant liver dysfunction (serum transaminase levels ≥ 3 times the upper limit of normal); (4) lack of data required to calculate IR-related indices; (5) missing follow-up data; (6) malignant tumors or severe psychiatric disorders; (7) thyroid dysfunction. This study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of Lanzhou University School of Medicine (approval No.2026 A-811). As this was a retrospective study, informed consent was waived.

Data collection and definitions

Baseline data, including demographic characteristics, medical history, medication use, laboratory parameters, and echocardiographic parameters, were extracted from the hospital’s electronic medical record system using a standardized electronic data collection form. The primary data collected for each participant are listed below (see Table 1 ). All blood samples were collected from peripheral veins after an overnight fast of at least 8 h.

Hypertension was defined as a prior clinical diagnosis, current use of antihypertensive medications, systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mmHg, and/or diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mmHg. Diabetes mellitus was defined by the presence of any of the following criteria: (1) prior clinical diagnosis; (2) current use of antidiabetic medications or insulin; (3) fasting plasma glucose (FPG) ≥ 7.0 mmol/L; (4) 2-hour plasma glucose ≥ 11.1 mmol/L during an oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT); (5) typical symptoms of diabetes plus a random plasma glucose ≥ 11.1 mmol/L; or (6) glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) ≥ 6.5% [6]. Paroxysmal AF (PAF) was defined as AF that terminates spontaneously within 7 days, most commonly within 48 h. Persistent AF (PsAF) was defined as AF lasting > 7 days that does not terminate spontaneously or requires intervention for termination [17].

Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight (kg) divided by height squared (m2). Surrogate markers of IR were calculated as follows: TyG index = ln [TG (mg/dL) × FPG (mg/dL) / 2]; TyG-BMI index = TyG × BMI (kg/m2); TG/HDL-C ratio = TG (mg/dL) / HDL-C (mg/dL); METS-IR = ln [(2 × FPG (mg/dL)) + TG (mg/dL)] × BMI (kg/m2) / ln [HDL-C (mg/dL)] [12]; CTI = 0.412 × ln (CRP [mg/L]) + ln [TG (mg/dL) × FPG (mg/dL) / 2] [13]; eGDR = 19.02 − (0.22 × BMI) − (3.26 × hypertension) − (0.61 × HbA1c), where hypertension was coded as 1 (yes) or 0 (no), and HbA1c was expressed as a percentage according to the Diabetes Control and Complications Trial (DCCT) standard [14].

Ablation protocol

Our center’s radiofrequency catheter ablation protocol has been described previously [18]. In brief, the procedure was performed using a 10-pole circular mapping catheter and a 3.5-mm irrigated-tip ablation catheter (Biosense Webster, Diamond Bar, CA, USA), guided by three-dimensional electroanatomical mapping with the CARTO 3 system (Biosense Webster, Diamond Bar, CA, USA). Radiofrequency energy was delivered at a power of 35–40 W and an irrigation rate of 17–25 mL/min, with the catheter-tip temperature limited to 43 °C and the duration of each application limited to 15 s.

All patients underwent pulmonary vein isolation (PVI) as the fundamental ablation strategy. According to the overall procedural strategies recorded in this study, patients were classified into three mutually exclusive categories: PVI alone, PVI with additional ablation, and PVI combined with left atrial appendage closure.

Some patients, particularly those with persistent AF, underwent one or more additional ablation procedures based on their clinical and electrophysiological characteristics. These procedures could include roof-line ablation, mitral isthmus-line ablation, cavotricuspid isthmus ablation, and vein of Marshall ethanol infusion. Superior vena cava (SVC) isolation was performed when an arrhythmogenic focus originating from the SVC was identified. Because more than one additional lesion set could be performed in the same patient, the individual additional procedures were not mutually exclusive.

Concomitant left atrial appendage closure was performed in selected patients according to clinical indications and the operator’s assessment. The procedural endpoint of ablation was successful electrical isolation of all pulmonary veins.

Outcomes and follow-up

The primary endpoint of this study was AF recurrence during the 1-year follow-up period after the 3-month blanking period. All patients were prescribed antiarrhythmic drugs (AADs) for 3 months after catheter ablation to reduce the risk of early atrial arrhythmia recurrence during the blanking period. Thereafter, continuation or discontinuation of AAD therapy was determined jointly by the treating physician and the patient according to the patient’s clinical condition. According to the routine institutional follow-up practice, all included patients had documented follow-up at 3, 6, and 12 months after discharge through outpatient visits or telephone interviews. Rhythm status was evaluated using 12-lead electrocardiography and/or 24-hour Holter monitoring during scheduled follow-up when available, and additional rhythm monitoring was performed when symptoms suggestive of arrhythmia occurred. AF, atrial tachycardia, or atrial flutter lasting longer than 30 s that occurred after the 3-month blanking period following ablation was defined as post-ablation recurrence of AF. Recurrence events were assessed according to the predefined rhythm-documentation criteria and were adjudicated without reference to baseline insulin resistance surrogate indices. Any atrial arrhythmia occurring during the blanking period was not considered a recurrence of AF.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.5.1) and SPSS (version 27.0). A two-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Missing data for candidate variables and variables retained in the final analyses were summarized and are reported in Supplementary Table S1. Variables with missing rates greater than 20% were excluded before the analyses. For variables retained in the final analyses, missing values were handled using multiple imputation with the mice package in R. Normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov (K–S) test. Continuous variables with a normal distribution are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and comparisons between groups were performed using the independent samples t-test. Non-normally distributed continuous variables are presented as median (interquartile range, IQR), and comparisons between groups were conducted using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables are expressed as frequencies (percentages), and comparisons between groups were performed using the chi-square (χ2) test.

To evaluate the association between insulin resistance surrogate indices and the risk of AF recurrence after catheter ablation, three sequentially adjusted Cox proportional hazards models were constructed. Model 1 was unadjusted; Model 2 was adjusted for age and sex; and Model 3 was further adjusted for AF duration ≥ 24 months, AF type, left atrial diameter, left ventricular ejection fraction, NT-proBNP, ablation strategy, antiarrhythmic drug use, SGLT2 inhibitor use, rivaroxaban use, and study center. Covariates were selected based on clinical relevance, prior evidence, and their plausible association with post-ablation recurrence, rather than solely on statistical significance in univariable analyses. To ensure comparability across different insulin resistance surrogate indices, each index was entered separately into the same fully adjusted model.

In the Cox regression analyses, each insulin resistance surrogate index was evaluated as a continuous, dichotomous, and tertile-based categorical variable. Cutoff values for dichotomous analyses were determined using ROC curves for 1-year post-ablation AF recurrence, with the optimal cutoff selected according to the maximum Youden index: 8.62 for TyG, 218.32 for TyG-BMI, 39.25 for METS-IR, 5.07 for CTI, 6.83 for eGDR, and 1.34 for TG/HDL-C. These cohort-derived cutoffs were used for exploratory risk stratification and were not considered established clinical thresholds. Their robustness was further assessed using bootstrap resampling with 1,000 repetitions, as detailed in (Supplementary Table S2).

To minimize multicollinearity and avoid overadjustment, variables that were mathematically embedded in, or directly overlapped with, the insulin resistance composite indices were not included in the primary fully adjusted model, including body mass index, triglycerides, HDL-C, fasting plasma glucose, glycated hemoglobin, hypertension, and C-reactive protein. After model construction, multicollinearity was assessed using the variance inflation factor, with a value < 5 indicating no significant multicollinearity (Supplementary Figure S1).

Kaplan–Meier curves were generated to estimate the cumulative incidence of AF recurrence after catheter ablation, and differences between groups were compared using the log-rank test. Restricted cubic splines (RCS) were used to evaluate the potential dose-response relationship between insulin resistance surrogate markers and AF recurrence. Four knots were placed at the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles, with the median value as the reference. Overall and nonlinear associations were assessed using Wald tests based on the fitted Cox models. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed to evaluate the predictive performance of the baseline model and models incorporating each surrogate marker of IR. Improvements in predictive ability were assessed by comparing the C-statistic, continuous NRI, and IDI. In addition, decision curve analysis (DCA) was conducted to evaluate the clinical net benefit of the models across a range of threshold probabilities. Finally, subgroup analyses were performed to examine the robustness of the association between surrogate markers of IR and AF recurrence across different populations. Subgroup variables included age, sex, BMI, HTN, DM, AF type, LAD, and LVEF. Interaction terms between surrogate markers of IR and each subgroup variable were incorporated into the multivariable Cox models, and corresponding P values for interaction were calculated to assess effect modification across subgroups. Given the multiple interaction tests performed, false discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied to adjust for multiple comparisons.

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 590 patients with AF who underwent first-time radiofrequency catheter ablation and completed 1-year follow-up were included; 167 patients experienced AF recurrence, corresponding to a recurrence rate of 28.31% (See Table 2).

Among the included patients, 310 (52.54%) were enrolled from the Second Hospital of Lanzhou University and 280 (47.46%) from the First Hospital of Lanzhou University. As shown in Supplementary Table S3, baseline demographic and clinical characteristics, laboratory and echocardiographic parameters, procedural features, clinical risk scores, and IR-related indices were generally balanced between centers. The distribution of ablation strategies was similar between centers (P = 0.565). The 1-year recurrence rates were 26.45% (82/310) and 30.36% (85/280), respectively, with no significant between-center difference (P = 0.293).

Compared with patients without recurrence, those with recurrence were more frequently female and had a higher prevalence of PsAF, AF duration ≥ 24 months, hypertension, diabetes, heart failure, and coronary heart disease (all P < 0.05). They also had higher BMI, SBP, CRP, FPG, HbA1c, NT-proBNP, LAD, CHA2DS2-VA score, and HAS-BLED score, but lower LVEF and LAAFv (all P < 0.05).

For IR-related indices, TyG, TyG-BMI, METS-IR, and CTI were significantly higher in the recurrence group, whereas eGDR was significantly lower (all P < 0.05). TG/HDL-C did not differ significantly between groups (P = 0.346).

Predictive value of alternative markers of IR for AF recurrence

First, Kaplan–Meier analysis was used to evaluate the associations between various surrogate markers of insulin resistance and AF recurrence following catheter ablation (See Fig. 1).

Kaplan–Meier curve analysis revealed significant differences in recurrence-free survival across tertiles of TyG-BMI, METS-IR, and CTI, with patients in the highest tertiles exhibiting the least favorable recurrence-free survival curves (all log-rank P < 0.001). The TyG index showed a similar but relatively weaker pattern, with patients in the higher tertiles showing lower recurrence-free survival, and the differences among groups were statistically significant (log-rank P = 0.007). In contrast, eGDR exhibited the opposite pattern: patients in the lowest eGDR tertile had the lowest recurrence-free survival, whereas those in the highest tertile had the most favorable survival curve (log-rank P < 0.001). No significant differences in recurrence-free survival were observed across tertiles of TG/HDL-C (log-rank P = 0.674).

We further used sequentially adjusted Cox regression models to assess the associations between six surrogate markers of insulin resistance and the risk of AF recurrence (See Table 3). In the fully adjusted model, TyG, TyG-BMI, METS-IR, and CTI were all associated with an increased risk of AF recurrence, whereas eGDR was associated with a reduced risk of recurrence. These associations were generally consistent across analyses modeling the indices as continuous variables, ROC cutoff-based dichotomous variables, and tertiles. TG/HDL-C showed a significant association only in the ROC cutoff-based dichotomous analysis, but this association was not reproduced in the continuous-variable or tertile analyses, suggesting relatively limited stability.

After Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate correction, the main results remained generally consistent (Supplementary Table S4). Although Schoenfeld residual tests suggested potential non-proportional hazards in some fully adjusted models (Supplementary Table S5), the main conclusions remained substantially unchanged in further sensitivity analyses using stratified Cox models and time-dependent Cox models (Supplementary Tables S6–S7). Overall, the associations of TyG, TyG-BMI, METS-IR, CTI, and eGDR with AF recurrence were relatively robust, whereas the association for TG/HDL-C was less stable. To further account for potential between-center heterogeneity, we performed center-stratified Cox sensitivity analyses, in which participating center was treated as a stratification variable. The results remained generally consistent with the primary Cox regression analyses, suggesting that the main findings were not materially affected by center effects (Supplementary Table S8).

Incremental predictive value of IR surrogate indices for recurrence after AF ablation

To evaluate the incremental predictive value of surrogate markers for insulin resistance, we used ROC curve analysis to compare the discriminatory ability of the baseline model with that of models incorporating each marker individually (see Fig. 3). The results showed that the model incorporating eGDR achieved the highest AUC (0.744), followed by the models incorporating CTI, TyG-BMI, and METS-IR, with AUCs of 0.704, 0.695, and 0.691, respectively. The AUCs of the models incorporating TyG and TG/HDL-C were relatively close to that of the baseline model. Overall, among the evaluated surrogate markers for insulin resistance, eGDR demonstrated the strongest incremental discriminatory ability, while CTI, TyG-BMI, and METS-IR also showed modest improvements in AUC compared with the baseline model.

After adding each of the six surrogate markers for insulin resistance to the baseline model, we further compared their incremental predictive value for AF recurrence after catheter ablation (See Table 4). Overall, the eGDR- and CTI-added models showed more favorable predictive performance than the models incorporating the other markers. The eGDR-added model had the highest C-statistic, increasing from 0.660 to 0.744, followed by the CTI-added model, with a C-statistic of 0.704. Both models showed significant improvements in IDI and NRI compared with the baseline model. The TyG-BMI- and METS-IR-added models also improved model performance to some extent, whereas the TyG- and TG/HDL-C-added models showed relatively limited incremental predictive value.

Further pairwise comparisons showed that, after FDR adjustment, the AUC of the eGDR-added model was significantly higher than those of the TyG-, TyG-BMI-, TG/HDL-C-, and METS-IR-added models. However, the difference between the eGDR- and CTI-added models did not reach statistical significance (Supplementary Table S9). Similarly, pairwise comparisons of incremental NRI and IDI did not confirm a significant difference between eGDR and CTI(Supplementary Table S10). These findings suggest that eGDR and CTI may be two promising surrogate markers for insulin resistance in improving risk prediction after AF ablation, with eGDR showing the most favorable overall numerical performance.

To further comprehensively evaluate model performance, we additionally assessed the calibration of the baseline model and the models incorporating each insulin resistance surrogate marker. The calibration plots showed generally acceptable agreement between the predicted and observed 12-month recurrence risks across models (Fig. 4). Compared with the baseline model, the 12-month IPCW-adjusted Brier score decreased after adding TyG-BMI, METS-IR, CTI, and eGDR, with the largest reduction observed in the eGDR-added model, followed by the CTI-added model (Supplementary Table S11). The calibration intercepts were close to zero across all models, suggesting no substantial systematic underestimation or overestimation of recurrence risk. However, the calibration slopes were below 1 for all models, particularly for the eGDR-added model, indicating that some degree of optimism or overfitting could not be excluded. Therefore, although eGDR and CTI showed more favorable overall predictive performance, these findings should be interpreted with caution and require external validation.

After evaluating model discrimination, reclassification, and calibration, we further performed decision curve analysis (DCA) to assess the potential clinical utility of the different models (Fig. 5). Overall, the eGDR- and CTI-added models showed relatively higher net benefit than the baseline model and most other IR index-added models across a broad range of threshold probabilities. In particular, the eGDR-added model demonstrated the most favorable overall net benefit numerically, especially within the clinically relevant threshold range. In contrast, the TyG- and TG/HDL-C-added models provided limited additional net benefit over the baseline model. These findings suggest that adding eGDR or CTI to the baseline model may improve the potential clinical usefulness of risk prediction for AF recurrence after catheter ablation. However, given the retrospective nature of this study and the need for external validation of model calibration, these results should be interpreted cautiously before clinical application.

Subgroup analysis of IR surrogate indices and the risk of recurrence after AF ablation

To further assess the robustness of the associations between surrogate markers of insulin resistance and AF recurrence after catheter ablation, we performed subgroup analyses stratified by age, sex, BMI, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, AF type, LAD, and LVEF. Given their more favorable overall predictive performance in the main analyses, subgroup analysis results for eGDR and CTI are presented in the main text (Figs. 6 and 7), whereas exploratory subgroup analyses for TyG-BMI and METS-IR are provided in the Supplementary Figures (S2-S3).

Across the examined clinical subgroups, higher eGDR was consistently associated with a lower risk of AF recurrence, whereas higher CTI was generally associated with a higher risk of recurrence. Although the effect estimates varied numerically across subgroups, no significant interactions were observed after FDR correction. Exploratory subgroup analyses of TyG-BMI and METS-IR showed similar findings. These results suggest that the associations of eGDR and CTI with post-ablation AF recurrence were generally consistent across different clinical subgroups. However, given the exploratory nature of the subgroup analyses, these findings should be interpreted with caution (Fig. 7).

Sensitivity analyses for eGDR

Because eGDR is mathematically derived from BMI, hypertension status, and HbA1c, additional eGDR-specific sensitivity analyses were performed to evaluate whether the association between eGDR and AF recurrence was mainly driven by hypertension and HbA1c. After further adjustment for hypertension and HbA1c, eGDR remained significantly associated with AF recurrence (HR = 0.36, 95% CI: 0.23–0.59, P < 0.001), with consistent findings observed in the residual eGDR and BMI-component analyses. The detailed results of the sensitivity analyses and multicollinearity diagnostics are shown in Supplementary Figures S4-S5. Overall, these findings suggest that the association between eGDR and AF recurrence was not fully explained by hypertension and HbA1c. Nevertheless, given the mathematical construction of eGDR, its predictive value should still be interpreted as reflecting an integrated cardiometabolic risk burden rather than metabolic information unique to eGDR alone.

Discussion

In this two-center retrospective cohort study, we compared the associations between six routinely available surrogate indices of IR and recurrence after first-time catheter ablation for AF, and evaluated their incremental predictive value. The main findings were as follows. First, after multivariable adjustment, lower eGDR and higher TyG-BMI, METS-IR, TyG, and CTI were associated with AF recurrence, whereas the findings for TG/HDL-C were less consistent. Second, across different modeling approaches, sensitivity analyses, and subgroup analyses, the associations of eGDR, TyG-BMI, METS-IR, and CTI with recurrence were generally directionally consistent. Third, restricted cubic spline analyses suggested heterogeneous exposure-response relationships between the evaluated indices and recurrence risk. Finally, the model incorporating eGDR showed the most favorable numerical improvement in discrimination and risk reclassification, followed by the model incorporating CTI. TyG-BMI and METS-IR also provided smaller but statistically significant improvements, whereas TyG and TG/HDL-C showed limited incremental predictive value. Overall, the utility of different IR surrogate indices for assessing the risk of AF recurrence after ablation appeared to vary.

The findings of the present study should be interpreted in the context of existing evidence. A recent systematic review and meta-analysis by Ghoshouni et al. showed that TyG, HOMA-IR, and METS-IR were associated with AF recurrence after catheter ablation, whereas TG/HDL-C showed no significant association with recurrence [19]. The present study is partly consistent with these findings: METS-IR showed a relatively stable association with recurrence, whereas the results for TG/HDL-C were inconsistent across different variable forms and its incremental predictive value was limited. Although TyG levels were higher in patients with recurrence and remained associated with recurrence in multiple adjusted models, adding TyG to the baseline model led to only a small improvement in model performance, suggesting limited incremental predictive value.

This phenomenon may be related to the different metabolic dimensions captured by these indices. TG/HDL-C mainly reflects lipid-related metabolic disturbances, and TyG mainly reflects dysregulated glucose and lipid metabolism represented by fasting triglycerides and fasting glucose. In contrast, composite indices such as TyG-BMI, METS-IR, eGDR, and CTI further integrate information on obesity, glycemic control, blood pressure status, lipid metabolism, and inflammation [10–14]. Therefore, these composite indices may more comprehensively reflect the adverse cardiometabolic phenotype of patients with AF. Previous studies have also suggested that TyG-BMI and METS-IR are associated with various cardiometabolic outcomes and AF recurrence after catheter ablation [4, 20–24], and that their predictive performance may be superior to that of TyG and TG/HDL-C [9, 12]. In addition, differences among studies in patient characteristics, baseline metabolic risk, ablation strategies, recurrence monitoring methods, follow-up duration, and covariate adjustment may also contribute to heterogeneity in the results. Overall, the present study suggests that, compared with single glucose- and lipid-related metabolic indices, composite indices integrating multidimensional cardiometabolic information may be more useful for assessing the risk of AF recurrence after catheter ablation; however, further validation is still needed. In addition, to further clarify the novelty and complementary value of the present study in relation to previously published TyG-related studies in Cardiovascular Diabetology, a comparative summary is provided in (Supplementary Table S12).

Among the various indices included in this study, eGDR showed the most stable association with AF recurrence after catheter ablation and demonstrated relatively favorable overall performance in terms of discrimination, risk reclassification, IPCW-adjusted Brier score, and decision curve analysis. eGDR is calculated using BMI, hypertension status, and HbA1c, and a lower eGDR generally indicates poorer estimated glucose disposal and a higher degree of insulin resistance. Although eGDR was originally applied mainly in diabetes-related research and has shown a certain degree of agreement with the hyperinsulinemic-euglycemic clamp technique [25], its components reflect broader cardiometabolic and glycometabolic risks rather than diabetes status alone [26, 27]. In recent years, eGDR has also been used in non-diabetic populations, and related studies have suggested that lower eGDR is associated with adverse cardiovascular outcomes, incident AF, and an increased risk of AF recurrence after catheter ablation [28–33]. In the present study, the association between eGDR and recurrence remained stable after further adjustment for hypertension and HbA1c, and consistent findings were also observed in the residual eGDR analysis, suggesting that its prognostic information may not be entirely dependent on any single component variable. Nevertheless, eGDR should still be understood as a comprehensive cardiometabolic risk marker that integrates BMI, blood pressure status, and glycometabolic information. Its predictive value may reflect a multidimensional cardiometabolic risk burden and should not be simply attributed to a single insulin resistance-related effect.

CTI is another indicator of interest in the present study. By integrating CRP into the TyG index, CTI incorporates information on both glucose-lipid metabolic abnormalities and systemic inflammation. Previous studies have suggested that higher CTI is associated with adverse cardiovascular outcomes, heart failure, mortality risk, and incident AF, and may provide additional information beyond TyG or CRP alone [34–38]. In the present study, CTI remained associated with AF recurrence after catheter ablation in multivariable analyses and showed a certain degree of incremental predictive value, suggesting that the inflammation-metabolism phenotype may be related to the risk of post-ablation recurrence. In addition, after FDR correction, pairwise comparisons between the eGDR and CTI models showed no statistically significant difference, suggesting that CTI and eGDR may provide complementary risk information from different dimensions. Specifically, CTI reflects an inflammation-metabolism phenotype, whereas eGDR more broadly integrates BMI, blood pressure status, and glycometabolic risk. Overall, CTI may serve as a potential auxiliary indicator for assessing the risk of AF recurrence after catheter ablation, although its clinical utility requires further validation.

These findings have certain biological plausibility. Previous studies have suggested that insulin resistance and its related metabolic abnormalities may contribute to the development of AF and post-ablation recurrence through multiple interrelated pathophysiological pathways [39, 40]. At the electrophysiological level, insulin resistance-related oxidative stress and calcium homeostasis abnormalities may affect NADPH oxidase activity, CaMKII activation, RyR function, gap junction protein expression, and atrial ion currents, thereby increasing conduction heterogeneity and arrhythmia susceptibility [7, 19]. At the structural remodeling level, inflammatory responses, oxidative stress, activation of profibrotic pathways, accumulation of advanced glycation end products, and lipotoxic injury may promote collagen deposition, atrial fibrosis, and impaired myocardial metabolism [19, 41–43]. In addition, insulin resistance-related metabolic abnormalities may also be associated with interatrial conduction delay, low-voltage zone formation, left atrial enlargement, and autonomic dysfunction, thereby further increasing susceptibility to AF development and post-ablation recurrence [44–47] Therefore, the associations between multiple composite metabolic indices and recurrence risk observed in this study may reflect the integrated relationship between adverse cardiometabolic status and atrial electrical remodeling, structural remodeling, and autonomic remodeling.

From a clinical perspective, AF recurrence is not determined solely by electrophysiological factors, but may also be related to obesity, dysglycemia, hypertension, systemic inflammation, and the overall burden of cardiometabolic risk. Therefore, composite indices such as eGDR, CTI, and TyG-related indices may serve as simple and low-cost markers of adverse cardiometabolic phenotypes, helping to identify patients at higher risk of recurrence. In particular, eGDR and CTI showed relatively stable predictive associations in the present study and may serve as candidate auxiliary indicators for post-ablation risk stratification in future studies. Clinically, these indices may provide reference information for postoperative rhythm monitoring, follow-up frequency, and management of metabolic risk factors, including enhanced assessment and intervention for modifiable risk factors such as body weight, blood pressure, blood glucose, blood lipids, and inflammatory status. In addition, existing risk scores for post-ablation recurrence, such as DR-FLASH and APPLE, do not directly incorporate insulin resistance-related information. Future studies may further evaluate whether integrating such indices into existing risk scores or multidimensional cardiometabolic risk frameworks can improve recurrence risk prediction and individualized management. However, these indices should not be regarded as standalone criteria for guiding anticoagulation, repeat ablation, or long-term antiarrhythmic therapy.

Nevertheless, this study has several limitations. First, because of its retrospective design, information bias may have occurred during follow-up. Although follow-up was recorded at 3, 6, and 12 months after ablation, the intensity and modality of rhythm monitoring varied across patients, which may have introduced detection bias. In particular, because continuous rhythm monitoring or implantable loop recorders were not routinely used, outcome assessment based on outpatient records, telephone follow-up, electrocardiography, Holter monitoring, or clinical symptoms may have underestimated the incidence of asymptomatic AF recurrence. In addition, the duration, clinical course, and related interventions for recurrent episodes, such as electrical cardioversion, reinitiation of antiarrhythmic drugs, or repeat ablation, were not systematically and completely recorded. Therefore, persistent AF recurrence lasting more than 7 days could not be reliably identified, and further analyses based on these clinically relevant recurrence endpoints could not be performed. These factors may have affected the reported recurrence rate and influenced the estimated associations and predictive performance of the models.

Second, although this study adjusted for available medication variables, including the use of antiarrhythmic drugs, SGLT2 inhibitors, and rivaroxaban, information on medication type, dose, duration, post-ablation changes, discontinuation, and adherence was not systematically collected. In particular, this study lacked complete information on antiarrhythmic drug classes, such as Class IC versus Class III agents, as well as metformin, statins, and other lipid-lowering drugs; therefore, medication-related residual confounding cannot be excluded. In addition, the proportion of baseline SGLT2 inhibitor use was relatively low, which may reflect real-world prescribing patterns during the enrollment period, but may also limit the generalizability of the findings to contemporary populations with broader guideline-concordant use of SGLT2 inhibitors, particularly patients with diabetes, heart failure, or high cardiometabolic risk. Further validation in such populations is warranted.

Third, detailed left atrial substrate parameters, standardized left atrial strain measurements, and operator-level procedural variables were not routinely collected during the study period; therefore, their potential effects on post-ablation AF recurrence and on the association between insulin resistance and recurrence could not be fully evaluated. Fourth, this study used multiple surrogate markers of insulin resistance calculated from metabolic parameters rather than gold-standard methods, such as the hyperinsulinemic-euglycemic clamp, which may have affected the accuracy of assessing true insulin resistance levels. Fifth, although multiple confounders were adjusted for in the multivariable models, and constituent variables were excluded when different insulin resistance indices were included to reduce the impact of multicollinearity, residual confounding cannot be completely ruled out.

Finally, the lack of an independent external validation cohort limits the generalizability of our findings. Although ROC-derived dichotomous cutoffs were used for exploratory risk stratification, these thresholds were derived from the full analytic cohort and may therefore be subject to overfitting and optimism bias. Bootstrap internal validation suggested that some cutoffs were relatively stable, whereas others, especially TG/HDL-C, showed substantial variability. Therefore, these cutoffs should not be regarded as established clinical thresholds. In addition, although several insulin resistance-related indices showed statistically significant improvements in discrimination and reclassification, the absolute gains were limited. These findings should be regarded as hypothesis-generating results and as supplementary information for risk stratification, rather than as evidence that directly changes post-ablation management. Future multicenter prospective studies are needed to validate these findings and to determine whether incorporating these indices into clinical workflows can improve follow-up strategies, rhythm monitoring, metabolic risk management, and patient outcomes after AF ablation.

Conclusion

In conclusion, among the six insulin resistance-related surrogate indices evaluated, eGDR, CTI, TyG-BMI, and METS-IR showed relatively stable associations with AF recurrence after catheter ablation, whereas TyG and TG/HDL-C had relatively limited predictive value. eGDR showed the most favorable overall numerical performance, and CTI may also provide certain inflammation–metabolism risk information, suggesting that different indices may reflect different dimensions of cardiometabolic risk. These findings support the potential value of non-insulin-based insulin resistance surrogate indices, particularly eGDR and CTI, as adjunctive markers for post-ablation recurrence risk stratification. However, these indices should not be used as standalone tools to guide clinical management. Future prospective multicenter studies are needed to validate these findings and to further determine whether interventions targeting adverse metabolic and inflammatory phenotypes can improve clinical outcomes after AF ablation.
