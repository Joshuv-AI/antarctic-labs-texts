Patients’ Attitudes on the Use of Expanded Outreach to Find a Kidney Donor: A Mixed-Methods Study
Authors: Anne Solbu, Renee B Cadzow, Maria Keller, Rhys Mendel, Barbara Breckenridge, Heather Gardiner, Liise K Kayler
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRationale & ObjectiveAccess to living donor kidney transplantation (LDKT) can be improved by strategies that expand donor outreach. Kidney transplant candidates’ attitudes on such strategies are unclear. This study explored candidates’ attitudes on the use of donor outreach strategies with wide reach.Study DesignA Quasi-mixed methods design using semi-structured interviews.Setting & ParticipantsAdult kidney transplant candidates at one transplant program in the Northeast who had completed a trial of a behavioral LDKT access intervention. Participants were informed of a variety of donor outreach approaches by viewing a short video and were asked scaled and open-ended questions about their experiences and attitudes towards such approaches, thoughts about the video, and recommendations for support.Analytical ApproachQualitative data were analyzed for themes and subthemes using conventional content analysis, supplemented by descriptive statistics of demographic and scaled data.ResultsAll 24 participants had discussed LDKT with at least one person. Over 40% had done or would do any of 12 expanded donor outreach activities. Six themes were identified: (1) reaching people, mainly with conversations; (2) de-motivators to sustain donor outreach; (3) openness to expanded outreach; (4) varied capacity for expanded outreach due to ability, health, economic, social, and technology challenges; (5) recommended human assistance and tools for outreach; and (6) the video was inspirational.LimitationsSmall sample of patients at one transplant center may limit representativeness.ConclusionsKidney transplant candidates are receptive to conducting expanded donor outreach activities. Findings highlighted individual preferences on outreach approaches and the need to address multiple individual and contextual barriers.Index Words: kidney transplant, living kidney donation, donor outreach, donor search, donor recruitment, donor solicitation

Living donor kidney transplant (LDKT) is the gold standard treatment for end-stage kidney disease.1 Most kidney transplant candidates do not receive LDKT and ∼17 Americans die each day while waiting for a kidney transplant.2 Although there are interventions available to increase LDKT, most are designed to create contexts where individuals feel comfortable having conversations about living donation with friends and family.3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11 When close contacts are unable to donate, few interventions provide strategies for expanded donor outreach that might increase the numbers of potential donors. Expanded donor outreach involves diversifying engagement strategies beyond traditional methods (eg, conversations), such as through social media posts,12 sharing of educational animated videos,13, 14, 15, 16 donor-recipient matching websites,17,18 and advertisements (eg, television, radio, car clings, and billboards newspapers).19, 20, 21 Kidney donors have indicated that they would not have thought to donate except for exposure to personal stories reported in the media19 and 53% of potential donors at a single center had been petitioned via social media22, suggesting efficacy of public solicitation. There have been calls for initiatives from within transplant centers to professionalize the process of helping patients find a donor.23 Although patients’ attitudes about discussing living kidney donation have been extensively reported,3,24, 25, 26, 27, 28 limited research has examined other types of donor outreach, resulting in an evidence gap.29

To support the development of effective LDKT access interventions, this study sought to understand patients’ attitudes about expanded donor outreach, response to a researcher-developed video overview about varied outreach approaches, and recommendations for support.

Methods

Design

This mixed methods30 study of the attitudes of kidney transplant candidates toward expanded donor outreach was conducted at a single transplant program in the northeastern United States with participants who had completed an ongoing randomized controlled trial of a digital behavioral intervention or usual care condition to improve LDKT access. The participants were more than one year post initial evaluation, provided electronic consent, and completed the interview over the telephone. For the parent study, information on socio-demographics had been collected on enrollment in the trial. The Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) guidelines were followed for reporting.31 This study was approved by the University at Buffalo Institutional Review Board (STUDY00002771).

Setting

This work emerged from ongoing research of a digital behavioral intervention for kidney transplant access (KidneyTIME) conducted at a safety-net hospital where rates of living donor kidney transplant are 6% annually among newly waitlisted patients compared to over 30% nationally.2 KidneyTIME is delivered in 2-3 minute 2D animated videos to promote receptivity and behaviors toward LDKT; donor outreach is enabled by sharing study videos with network members.13 The design of each video is theory-and stakeholder-informed using an iterative development process as previously described.14,15 A new video intended for the KidneyTIME curriculum provides an overview of various outreach strategies to generate awareness about the need for a living kidney donor, including verbal communication, sharing videos, social media, mass media, and other promotions (flyers, yard signs, car clings, t-shirts, and business cards). The video is 2.40 minutes long. Screenshots are provided in Fig 1.

Recruitment and Procedures

Semi-structured exit interviews were held with a subset of participants completing the 12-month KidneyTIME trial.32 Prior studies utilizing the exit interviews examined participant experiences of the intervention33 or participant feedback on new video content developed in response to newly discovered information needs.34,35 Based on recommendations by intervention participants33 for other outreach modalities beyond video sharing, the current study utilized the last remaining exit interviews to explore the topic of expanded donor outreach. Randomized controlled trial inclusion criteria included: referred to the kidney transplant program, ability to speak English, at least 18 years of age, and email or text access. Exclusion criteria were lack of internet access and prior exposure to a LDKT focused intervention. We invited 51 individuals to the study (42 control arm and 9 intervention arm) of whom 35 consented to participate and were emailed the video. Twenty-four (18 control arm and 6 intervention arm) completed the exit interview from April 2023 to January 2025. Interviews were held until data saturation was achieved (ie, the point where interviews no longer yielded new information).

Data Collection

Individual interviews were conducted by a White female researcher who holds a PhD in communication sciences and has 4 years of experience in kidney transplant research. The interview guide is provided in Table 1. The interview included a series of dichotomous questions about whether participants had done or would be willing to do (1-yes/0-no) any of 13 donor outreach approaches (eg, conversations, flyers, and yard signs). Open-ended questions explored approaches previously employed by candidates to find a donor, opinions of the video and the donor outreach approaches shown in the video, and recommendations for strategies to help individuals find a living kidney donor. Interviews were held via telephone and lasted 14 to 32 minutes (average 24 minutes). All interviews were audio-recorded, transcribed verbatim, and reviewed for accuracy. Participants were compensated $25 for the interview.

Data Analysis

Sociodemographic data and scaled interview questions were analyzed using summary statistics. Transcripts were imported into Dedoose software (version 9.2.22) and read several times by all six members of the research team. Two members of the team (AS and MK) coded the transcripts using a general content analysis approach.36 The initial codes were generated deductively from the interview topics and research objectives. Multiple readings and interpretations of the responses to the questions and the associated ensuing conversations were coded inductively, where codes were informed by the participants’ words or phrases. Upon completion of the first 2 transcripts, the preliminary coding scheme was reviewed and discussed by four members of the research team (AS, MK, RC, and LK) for accuracy and consistency. Disputes over code definitions and applications were resolved through discussions and then applied to all transcripts. Coding saturation was achieved when no further coding modifications were needed. The team generated reports of the transcript excerpts at each code and subcode and reviewed the codes and excerpts to identify relationships between the codes and develop themes.37 This also facilitated the identification of quotes to exemplify those themes. Finally, a kidney transplant recipient and community advocate (BB), read the transcripts and contributed important insights that helped shape final interpretation of the data.

Results

Participant Characteristics

Participants’ mean age was 56 ± 13 years old. A third reported non-Hispanic Black race (33%) and the remainder were non-Hispanic White. About half were female (46%), not receiving dialysis (46%), and reported an annual household income of less than $30,000 (58%; see Table 2). All participants had discussed living donation with at least one person before the interview. Over 40% had done or would do any of 12 expanded donor outreach activities (Fig 2). Six themes were identified. These themes are described below and summarized in Table 3 with exemplar quotes.

Theme 1: Reaching People, Mainly with Conversations

When asked about their approach to finding a donor, participants mentioned having conversations with people they know, predominantly face-to-face. All reported having at least one to 2 conversations, mostly with close friends and family. Nine participants reported having 20 to over 50 conversations, additionally reaching in-laws, church members, coworkers, acquaintances, clients, and people at events and family functions. Participants felt that talking with people was an optimal approach due to the sensitivity of the topic, the importance of maintaining relationships, and the ability to tell a more touching story. Some patients also used other communication channels, including social media, email, and sharing videos, mainly to reach people they know.

Theme 2: De-Motivators to Sustain Donor Outreach

Belief That Requesting Living Donation Is Pointless

Participants expressed that further requesting donation was pointless because they did not know any more people to ask or the people they knew were not willing or not suitable to donate. Many felt that their social network members already knew about their need for a kidney and would step forward if they so desired. A few respondents described prior unsuccessful efforts which led to reduced motivation to conduct ongoing outreach. Participants who were against public outreach didn’t believe that a stranger would choose to donate to them or didn’t consider themselves important enough, like a priest or pillar of the community (Participant 15). Some had seen advertisements of others looking for donors and felt it was not worth it to add their own story to what they perceived to be a saturated environment.

Discomfort Requesting Living Donation

Participants expressed feeling shy, insecure, nervous, and awkward having conversations about living donation. One participant explained that it was not my style to ask for any type of help (Participant 24). Others noted that the request for kidney donation is too big and feared judgement. They also expressed concerns about the donor’s health and burden of undergoing testing and surgery. Some indicated they would feel guilty if anything negative happened to the donor and were reluctant to accept donation from their children. One participant would only approach the general public on behalf of someone else in need.

Not The Right Time

Participants explained that it was not the right time to continue outreach due to not yet being approved for transplant, having a still-functioning kidney, and having a possible potential donor awaiting evaluation. Participants who were not yet approved for a transplant did not want to waste anyone’s time or get them interested in donating if they were not going to be eligible. Participants who were not yet on dialysis and felt okay or stable, preferred to focus on maintaining their current kidney function, such as through diet and exercise. Those who had a potential donor in mind did not see a need to continue their outreach until they fully explored this possibility.

Theme 3: Openness to Expanded Outreach

Most participants were open to doing expanded donor outreach activities. Some were ready and willing while others said they would do more outreach if there was help. One participant stated that they could be pushed into it (Participant 20). Some would try any activity, while others had preferences. Respondents often preferred approaches they had seen others use (business cards, car clings, or billboards) or that they felt able to undertake given their technology inexperience. Some were concerned about the loss of privacy with broadcasting too widely.

Participants also provided details on the perceived utility of each queried approach. Emails or texts were thought to be helpful for reaching family members and friends who lived out of town or people they did not talk to a lot. Several participants said that a conversation should happen before sending an electronic message to ensure it is read and not misunderstood and to avoid blindsiding them (Participant 19). Social media was considered a good way to convey their story to friends and relatives, elicit support and sympathy, prompt viewers to further share their need for a kidney, and receive offers to donate. Speaking to a large group, like church groups or family functions, was considered an option for reaching a wider social network. Posting their story on a website appealed to participants who had seen such a website before or knew how to create content. Business cards and flyers for kidney donation were considered useful conversation starters or to be used for broad dissemination by leaving under car windshield wipers or in grocery stores. Flyers could be big enough for posting a picture and the story of the participant. Yard signs could spread awareness in their neighborhood, the neighborhood of friends and family, or at events (eg golf tournaments). T-shirts could attract people’s attention and could be sold to fund their creation. Car clings could be an efficient way of widely disseminating the message by putting one on their own car or asking friends and family to put them on their cars. Billboards and ads could get a lot of people’s attention, but it was only possible if there were funds to support them. Many suggested that these approaches would invite a conversation that is otherwise difficult to initiate.

Theme 4: Varied Capacity for Expanded Outreach Due to Ability, Health, Economic, Social, and Technological Challenges

Conducting outreach was often compromised by participants’ abilities and not knowing how to make materials. One respondent expressed reluctance to engage in outreach due to being horrible at public speaking (Participant 20). Participants indicated that their physical and mental health limited opportunities for outreach, such as the need for cataract surgery to restore vision, having no energy due to dialysis, and being in a state of depression. Participants expressed financial barriers to expanded outreach, including limited income, and reliance on disability income and savings, as well as the cost of some outreach strategies like billboards and ads. Some did not have a yard or a car on which to post a sign or sticker. Others did not have people around to help them do outreach, did not go places where they could share information, or were not on the internet or social media. One person mentioned they would need to join a church to leverage additional social connections.

Theme 5: Recommended Human Assistance and Tools for Outreach

Human Assistance

Participants shared the importance of human assistance for outreach, describing help they had received from friends or family creating and posting social media content, putting up yard signs, and building a webpage. In some cases, close friends and spouses took on the role of sharing the patient’s story, mostly through word of mouth. Others mentioned ways that their family members could be helpful since they are good at crafting things like business cards, yard signs, and t-shirts. Participants recommended other forms of support they would like from the transplant center, including assistance with producing outreach materials and messages, identifying groups of people to request donation, connecting to support groups for outreach, and educating potential donors. They indicated that potential donors should be educated to build their knowledge and trust in the process and inform about financial resources that support donors’ lost wages and out-of-pocket expenses.

Tools

Participants suggested tools like templates, draft messages, and step-by-step instructions for expanded outreach approaches. They also suggested workshops to create t-shirts or stickers, providing materials to produce these items, or to have these outreach materials produced for them. One respondent suggested that the transplant center create a donor-recipient matching website. These suggestions correspond with respondents’ reported constraints related to ability, money, and social support.

Theme 6: Video was Inspirational

Provided Good Suggestions

Participants reported that the video taught them something new or served as a reminder of the strategies available to them. They said that the video inspired them to either get started, try additional outreach activities, or ask others to help them with outreach.

Was Engaging

Respondents noted that the video kept their attention, was fun, and that the animation was more engaging compared to live actors and less likely to provoke a stressful or distracting emotional response. They explained that the video was slow enough to let the information sink in and fast enough to keep their attention. The extent of information was either just about right (Participant 21) or they wanted more, such as the best strategies for social media posting.

Discussion

In this mixed-methods study, kidney transplant candidates reported that they communicated their need for a kidney donor primarily with people they know through conversations, although many also used other modes, including email, social media, and video sharing. When initial conversations did not yield donors, participants often lost motivation to sustain outreach, although some planned to resume outreach later when their kidney function deteriorates, transplant eligibility is confirmed, or if a prospective donor is not able to donate. Participants were open to engaging in different types of donor outreach activities; however, some preferred certain activities and needed motivation. They described multiple challenges that limit ongoing and broad outreach about LDKT, including ability, health and social, financial, and technological challenges. Providing human assistance and tools were suggestions to improve their donor outreach opportunities. Participants also reported being satisfied with the educational animated video overview of expanded outreach actions and found it inspirational.

Our findings echo many motivational barriers to communicating about living kidney donation described in past research, including feelings of discomfort, guilt, concern for donor safety, fear of rejection, and protecting privacy.26, 27, 28, 29,38 Our study revealed varying levels of motivation to engage in donor outreach due to timing. Timing reasons for delaying further outreach were residual kidney function and not yet placed on the transplant waitlist. Aligned with prior research, we found that asking for a kidney asynchronously is easier for some than asking face-to-face given the enormity of the request;29 however, motivation for public outreach may be tempered by public disclosure vulnerability29 and perceptions of low response efficacy (whether the outreach action really will make any difference) due to being lost in the crowd29 and perceived low social worthiness.29 To ensure engagement, future efforts to increase donor outreach should be personalized with respect to content and timing.

Our study draws attention to ability barriers to expanded donor outreach, some of which have been reported elsewhere.29,39 Participants often lacked knowledge of how to make materials and what to say. Ability barriers were compounded by varying levels of health, financial, technological, and social challenges. Some approaches were not considered affordable or not a good fit to candidates’ technological proficiency or social context. Specific social challenges were low social support to assist with outreach, small social circles to reach into, and limited opportunities to do some outreach actions (lack of car or yard). Prior research has also identified that individuals find public solicitation to be time consuming and emotionally taxing.29 These findings underscore the need for comprehensive interventions that address multiple barriers to donor outreach.

Participants had varied preferences for types of outreaches. The most widely preferred approaches appeared to be social media, sharing videos, and handing out print materials (cards and flyers). Some previous LDKT access interventions provided booklets and DVDs to distribute knowledge and generate awareness about LDKT; however, the extent of sharing is unknown and likely too low given the bulky nature of the material.3,4,10,40, 41, 42, 43, 44, 45 Providing technology to enable greater outreach may be more scalable, yet candidates’ technology proficiency may be a barrier.16 For example, in a mobile application study for sharing personal stories about the need for donation alongside links to informational websites, only ∼46% enrolled and used the offered technology.46 In our KidneyTIME trial, 61% of intervention candidates shared short animated educational videos about LDKT with their social networks during conversations and by sending video links in emails, texts, and social media. Taken together, these findings suggest the need for both digital and tangible tools to improve individuals’ opportunities to participate in expanded donor outreach.

Our findings highlight that not all patients are prepared to make outreach materials and disseminate them. Many requested assistance or guidance, a similar theme in other research on public solicitation.26,29 Interventions that incorporate the patient’s existing social support structure in outreach activities (often named donor champions) have shown evidence to facilitate LDKT; however, limited enrollment by patients and attendance by network members are known barriers.5,6 This is possibly due to distance barriers or that candidates lack energy to attend meetings.47,48 Given the positive reception to the video assessed in this study, animated video education, which allows patients to learn at home and at their own pace, may be a useful medium for training both candidates and network members in outreach.

Our study has limitations. As a single-center study with a small sample size, transferability may be constrained. Inclusion of some trial participants from the intervention arm who received shareable videos may explain patients’ tendency to report video sharing as a preferred outreach method. Interviews were conducted by research staff located at the transplant center, which may have influenced both willingness to participate and responses to the interview questions. However, we captured divergent opinions. The additional member-checking step provided by our patient-expert found that participants’ responses resonated; she identified types of interviewed people similar to those with whom she has worked for more than 20 years in her kidney transplant outreach and advocacy organization.

In conclusion, expanded donor outreach is an option when close contacts are unable to donate, yet few interventions support such approaches. This qualitative study unveils 6 themes underscoring willingness and preferences regarding engagement in donor outreach activities. The findings of socioeconomic, technological, and clinical barriers present opportunities for enhancing support. Larger studies exploring interventions to improve effective expanded donor outreach are needed to increase LDKT, thereby improving longevity and quality of life for people with kidney failure.
