Patterns of multimorbidity, their outcomes and analytical methods for their determination: a systematic review
Authors: Črt Krebs, Matic Mihevc, Marija Petek Šter
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMultimorbidity is becoming a pressing public health problem, as more than 50% of the world’s adult population over the age of 60 is multimorbid. In order to better stratify risk in patients pattern identification is a promising approach. We conducted this systematic review to inform future research on multimorbidity patterns, their related outcomes and analytical methods used to derive them.MethodsThis systematic review followed PRISMA 2020 guidelines and was registered in PROSPERO registry (CRD420261290771). Bibliographic databases PubMed, Ovid MEDLINE(R), and Web of Science were systematically searched. Original observational studies examining multimorbidity patterns and associated health outcomes were included. Data was independently extracted by two reviewers and synthesised qualitatively. Methodological quality was assessed using a modified Newcastle–Ottawa Scale.ResultsWe examined a total of 7897 articles based on title and abstract. This systematic review includes an analysis of 30 studies and provides an update on the current state of multimorbidity patterns identification, their outcomes and the analytical methods used to derive them. We found that across all included studies the most commonly identified and stable patterns are those which include a cardiovascular, metabolic, respiratory or neurological/neuropsychiatric component. The cardiometabolic pattern showed the least heterogeneity in its composition. The most commonly measured outcome between patterns and outcomes was mortality. Higher all-cause mortality was observed in the majority of studies in patterns including a cardiovascular, metabolic or neurological/neuropsychiatric component. We found that identifying multimorbidity patterns using electronic health records and questionnaires are the most prevalent methods of data gathering. The most commonly used methodology in this field was latent class analysis followed by other methods such as, fuzzy c-means, k-means clustering, and factor analysis.ConclusionAcross the included studies, the most consistently identified and stable multimorbidity patterns were those comprising cardiovascular, metabolic, respiratory, and neurological/neuropsychiatric components. Patterns that included cardiovascular, metabolic, or neuropsychiatric conditions were most frequently associated with increased all-cause mortality. These findings support the clinical relevance of pattern-based approaches to multimorbidity. Future research should prioritise methodological standardisation, transparent reporting, and systematic validation of identified patterns to improve comparability and facilitate their translation into clinical practice and public health planning.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-27784-5.Keywords: Multimorbidity, Multimorbidity patterns, Disease clustering, Functional outcomes, Electronic health records, Methodological approaches, Disease patterns

Introduction

The increasing prevalence of patients living with multiple concurrent chronic conditions has made multimorbidity a central challenge in healthcare. This condition is referred to as multimorbidity. It is defined as the simultaneous presence of at least two chronic conditions, either physical and/or mental [1]. At the global level, multimorbidity is becoming a pressing public health problem, as more than 50% of the world’s adult population over the age of 60 is multimorbid [2]. Multimorbidity is a multifaceted problem, as it is associated with lower quality of life, premature death, more frequent hospitalisations and polypharmacy [3–6]. All of the above leads to increased healthcare costs and thus represents an additional burden on the public health budget [7].

Despite its prevalence, the management of multimorbidity remains poorly aligned with existing clinical practice guidelines. Most guidelines are developed within a single-disease framework and rarely account for the coexistence of multiple conditions [8]. As a result, multimorbid patients are often exposed to complex and potentially conflicting treatment recommendations, increasing the risk of adverse drug interactions and suboptimal care [6, 8].

Importantly, multimorbidity is not a homogeneous condition. Individuals with multimorbidity differ in terms of disease combinations, trajectories, and prognoses. To capture this heterogeneity, research has increasingly focused on identifying distinct multimorbidity patterns which are non-random clusters of chronic conditions that tend to co-occur within individuals [9]. These patterns reflect underlying differences in disease prevalence across populations and are further shaped by demographic, behavioural, and socioeconomic factors such as age, sex, body mass index, physical activity, and socioeconomic status [10]. Previous studies suggest that multimorbidity is particularly prevalent among older adults, women, and individuals with obesity [11]. Among the various patterns described in the literature, the cardiometabolic pattern typically encompassing conditions such as diabetes, hypertension, cardiovascular disease, and obesity has been reported most consistently [12].

The identification of multimorbidity patterns relies on a range of analytical approaches, including latent class analysis, cluster analysis (e.g. k-means, fuzzy c-means), and factor analysis. These methods differ in their conceptual assumptions, particularly with regard to whether diseases are grouped based on latent structures, similarity measures, or shared variance, and whether a single disease can belong to multiple patterns simultaneously [13]. Consequently, the choice of method can substantially influence both the number and the composition of identified patterns, contributing to heterogeneity across studies.

Beyond their descriptive value, multimorbidity patterns are clinically meaningful because they are differentially associated with health outcomes. Evidence indicates that individuals belonging to certain patterns most notably the cardiometabolic pattern have a higher risk of all-cause and cause-specific mortality, as well as poorer functional outcomes [12]. Monitoring outcomes such as all-cause mortality, functional decline, and healthcare utilization therefore provides important prognostic information and may support more tailored approaches to care. Given the growing body of literature and the substantial methodological diversity in this field, there is a clear need for a comprehensive synthesis of existing evidence.

Previous reviews have addressed specific components of multimorbidity research, including multimorbidity patterns [9, 14], the analytical methods used to identify them [13, 15], and their associations with health outcomes across different data sources [16]. However, these domains have largely been examined in isolation. This review integrates all three dimensions to provide a comprehensive and methodologically informed synthesis of the evidence. By doing so, it enhances the interpretability and generalisability of findings and clarifies how multimorbidity patterns can be applied to risk stratification, targeted prevention strategies, and population-level public health planning.

The aim of this systematic review is to provide a comprehensive synthesis of evidence on multimorbidity patterns, the analytical methods used to identify them, and their associations with key health outcomes. This was done to critically appraise methodological heterogeneity and inform future standardisation in multimorbidity research.

Methods

Design

This systematic review was conducted and reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [17]. The review protocol was registered in the international prospective register of systematic reviews (PROSPERO) under registration number CRD420261290771.

Research questions

The research questions were formulated using the PEO framework (Population, Exposure, Outcome). Based on this framework, the research questions were as follows:

Which multimorbidity patterns were most identified across adult populations in different countries, healthcare settings, and study designs?

Which health outcomes were most frequently measured in studies of multimorbidity patterns, and which specific patterns were most consistently associated with the poorest clinical outcomes, particularly increased mortality, functional decline and healthcare utilization?

Search strategy

To identify potentially relevant documents, we searched PubMed, Ovid Medline(R) and Web of Science Core Collection bibliographic databases [18–20]. In addition, we also searched the following targeted scientific journals Journal of Multimorbidity and Comorbidity, Frontiers Public Health, The Journals of Gerontology, Series A: Biological Sciences and Medical Sciences, Aging-Clinical & Experimental Research and BMC Public Health [21–25] and the references of articles found during the search. The search strategy was developed in collaboration with a librarian from the Central Medical Library of the University of Ljubljana Faculty of Medicine. The search was conducted between December 2025 and April 2026. The search was restricted to studies published from 2018 onwards to ensure the inclusion of the most recent and methodologically relevant evidence. This cut-off was selected because a prior systematic review by Ng et al. [13] had already synthesized earlier literature on multimorbidity patterns and analytical methods. By focusing on studies published subsequently, this review avoids duplication, captures advances in analytical approaches and data sources (particularly the increasing use of large-scale electronic health records), and enhances the relevance and comparability of findings to contemporary clinical and public health contexts. For the search, we used a combination of free keywords and indexed terms (MeSH) linked with appropriate Boolean operators (OR, AND). The search strings are presented in the supplementary material (Additional_file_1; Table 1A-C).

Eligibility criteria

For a study to be included it had to meet all the inclusion criteria. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (a) studies had to be original scientific articles published in English, that were by design observational studies (cohort or cross-sectional design); (b) multimorbidity had to be defined as the co-occurrence of at least two concurrent chronic diseases; (c) studies had to identify patterns of multimorbidity and their associated outcomes or effects, and provide a clear description of the analytical methods used to identify these patterns; (d) studies had to published after and including 2018 and (e) studies had to define multimorbidity as the coexistence of two or more chronic conditions.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: (a) systematic reviews and meta-analyses; (b) studies focusing on a single multimorbidity pattern (e.g., cardiometabolic only) or investigated disease-specific cohorts (e.g., chronic kidney disease), this was done to ensure the review captured a comprehensive range of multimorbidity clusters and their effects on key outcomes, such as mortality and healthcare utilisation and (c) the study identified multimorbidity patterns based on health conditions fewer than 10 in number.

Data extraction and analysis

Data from the included studies were systematically extracted using a specifically developed data extraction tool. The data extraction was performed independently by ČK and MPŠ. Any discrepancies were discussed and resolved by consensus to ensure accuracy and consistency of the extracted information. The following data were extracted from each study: bibliographic data (authors, year of publication), country where the study was conducted, year of the study or data collection period, study design (cohort, cross-sectional), sample size and characteristics of the study population, data collection method (questionnaire, interview, electronic health records, clinical examination, laboratory measurements, physical measurements), analytical method for identifying multimorbidity patterns, number of identified multimorbidity patterns, method of disease ascertainment, observed outcomes (all-cause mortality, functional outcomes, quality of life, healthcare utilization, polypharmacy) and key research findings regarding the characteristics and prognosis of individual patterns. We analysed the extracted data using qualitative synthesis methods.

Quality assessment

We assessed the quality and reliability of the evidence from the included studies using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) [26]. The NOS is a widely used tool for the assessment of methodological quality of non-randomised studies (cohort and case–control studies). For the specific purposes of our research question, the NOS was adapted following the model of Beridze et al. [14]. The adaptations were made to better align with our research questions.

The domain for assessment of outcome was removed, as the primary purpose of our review was not to evaluate clinical interventions or compare outcomes between exposed and unexposed groups. Instead, we expanded the tool with domains that are more relevant to this type of analysis, including a more detailed assessment of the method of disease ascertainment, the look-back period, and the appropriateness and transparency of the statistical methods used to identify patterns. The quality assessment was performed by the author (ČK) and co-author (MPŠ). In case of doubt, issues were resolved through discussion with the co-author (MM) until consensus was reached.

The final adapted tool included three domains: study selection, comparability, and analytical validity, and allowed for a rating of 0 to 9 stars, with a higher number of stars reflecting higher methodological quality of the studies. Based on the total number of stars, we classified the studies into three quality categories: low quality (0–4 stars), moderate quality (5–7 stars), and high quality (8–9 stars). The modified quality assessment tool is available in the supplementary material (Additional_file_2 and Additional_file_3).

Results

Study selection

The process of identifying, reviewing and including documents in the systematic literature review was carried out in accordance with the PRISMA 2020 recommendations. Thirty studies [27–56] were included in the final analysis. The document selection process is shown graphically in the PRISMA diagram (Fig. 1).

Quality assessment of included studies

The results of the quality assessment of the 30 studies included in this review are presented in Table 2 (Additional_file_4) and the supplementary material (Additional_file_1). Using the modified Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS), overall scores ranged from 6 to 9 stars. All studies fell within the moderate (5–7 stars) to high-quality (8–9 stars) range. A total of (n = 14) studies were assigned 8 stars, while (n = 10) studies received 7 stars. The maximum score of 9 stars was awarded to (n = 2) studies, whereas the lowest score of 6 stars was assigned to (n = 4) studies. No study was classified as low quality (0–4 stars). The mean score was 7.5 stars, indicating generally robust methodological quality across the included studies.

Qualitative analysis

Our systematic review of the literature included 30 articles. Fifteen studies were conducted in Europe, of which four in Sweden, three in Spain, three in Denmark, two in the United Kingdom, one in Germany, one in Italy and one in the Netherlands. Nine studies originated from Asian countries (Japan, China, Taiwan), five from the United States, and one was a joint study between Switzerland and China. The two largest studies had a sample size of over 1 million participants followed by eight studies [33, 37, 38, 49, 51, 57], where the sample size exceeded 100,000, while the other studies had smaller samples. In terms of the population observed, 6 studies included multimorbid individuals, 22 studies included the general population whereas the remaining 2 studies included hospitalised individuals. The age groups were either specific, e.g. between 18 and 65 years, or general, with a lower limit for inclusion, e.g. 65 years. The most used method of data collection were questionnaires (n = 17), followed by electronic health data (n = 14), laboratory measurements (n = 9), interviews (n = 6), clinical examinations (n = 6), death registers (n = 5), physical measurements (n = 2) and functional measurements (n = 2). Half of the included studies (n = 15) used only one method for data collection, while the remaining studies used a combination of two or more methods. The study design was predominantly cohort (n = 22) and in a few cases cross-sectional (n = 8). The shortest follow-up period was 1 year [30] and the longest 15 years [36, 47].

Multimorbidity patterns

An analysis of the included studies revealed considerable heterogeneity in the number of identified patterns and chronic diseases included in each pattern. The highest number of multimorbid patterns was reported in the study by Littlejohns T. J. et al., [42], which identified 15 multimorbidity patterns. The smallest reported number of multimorbidity patterns was 3 patterns. A total of 176 patterns were identified across 30 studies. Frequently identified components in the patterns were cardiovascular (including heart failure, ischemic heart disease, coronary heart disease) (n = 22) and metabolic (n = 12). When the latter two co-occurred they formed the cardiometabolic pattern (n = 11). Respiratory components were identified in 20 studies, with additional respiratory-related patterns reported less frequently, including asthma (n = 8), pulmonary patterns (n = 3), and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (n = 2). Neuropsychiatric patterns were identified in (n = 6) studies, while neurological and psychiatric patterns were each reported in (n = 5) studies. Patterns that were also observed were depression (n = 6), Parkinson’s disease (n = 3), and dementia (n = 3). Additionally mental health–related patterns were observed in (n = 12) studies. Musculoskeletal or osteoarticular patterns were identified in (n = 11) studies, usually in combination with other disease groups rather than as isolated entities. Cancer-related patterns appeared in (n = 10) studies. Non-specific or low-morbidity patterns (e.g. “minimal disease”, “relatively healthy”, or “unspecified”) were reported in (n = 15) studies. These clusters often represented transitional or baseline their size decreased over time as individuals were distributed into specific patterns. Multisystem or complex high-morbidity patterns were identified in (n = 5) studies, typically reflecting advanced disease accumulation across multiple organ systems. Sex-specific patterns were reported in (n = 4) studies, indicating limited evidence of sex stratification when forming a pattern.

Observed outcomes

The most frequently reported outcome was mortality, assessed in (n = 20) studies. Within these (n = 15) examined all-cause mortality, while (n = 4) focused on 5-year mortality, (n = 1) on 4-year mortality, and (n = 1) on 8-year mortality. Cause-specific mortality was investigated in (n = 3) studies. Across most studies, multimorbidity patterns characterised by cardiovascular and metabolic conditions (e.g. hypertension, diabetes, coronary heart disease) were consistently associated with the highest risks of both all-cause and cause-specific mortality.

Healthcare utilization was the second most frequently reported outcome (n = 6). Additional related outcomes included healthcare complexity (n = 1) and healthcare costs (n = 1). Patterns combining somatic and mental health conditions, as well as more complex multisystem patterns (e.g. cardiometabolic–respiratory or physical–mental–degenerative patterns), were consistently associated with the highest levels of healthcare use. These included increased primary care visits, specialist consultations, hospital admissions, and prolonged length of stay. In contrast, low-burden or non-specific patterns were generally associated with lower utilization. Neuropsychiatric and psychiatric-dominant patterns were particularly associated with prolonged hospital stays and increased inpatient days. Functional outcomes were assessed in (n = 4) studies and activities of daily living (ADL) evaluated in (n = 2). Sensory–cognitive and musculoskeletal patterns were consistently linked to the poorest functional outcomes, whereas arthritis-related clusters were associated with more rapid physical functional decline.

Quality of life was examined in two studies, both reporting the lowest scores in mental health–dominant patterns. Finally, four studies assessed the relationship between the number of chronic diseases and outcomes. While a higher number of conditions was associated with increased mortality risk, the overall number of diseases was not the primary determinant of healthcare utilization or complexity. Instead, disease composition within specific multimorbidity patterns was more strongly associated with outcomes, particularly healthcare use and risk of falls.

Analytical methods for identifying multimorbidity patterns

The analysis of 30 included studies revealed substantial methodological heterogeneity in the identification of multimorbidity patterns (Tabel 3). The most frequently applied method was latent class analysis (n = 15), followed by fuzzy c-means clustering (n = 4), k-means clustering (n = 4), factor analysis (n = 4), and hierarchical clustering (n = 3). The number of chronic conditions included to derive a pattern varied widely, with (n = 16), studies including ≤ 25 conditions, seven studies including 26–100 conditions, and seven studies incorporating > 100 conditions. Disease ascertainment methods clustered into two main categories: self-reported data and administrative data based on ICD codes. Latent class analysis was predominantly applied to self-reported and yields probabilistic, mutually exclusive patient groups based on latent structures. In contrast, clustering approaches (fuzzy c-means, k-means, hierarchical clustering) were more commonly used with ICD-based electronic health record data and produce groups based on similarity measures, with fuzzy c-means allowing partial membership across clusters. Factor analysis, also typically applied to lower-dimensional datasets, identifies patterns as groups of co-occurring diseases based on shared variance rather than discrete patient clusters. The number of identified multimorbidity patterns ranged from 3 to 15, with most studies reporting between 4 and 6 patterns. Fuzzy c-means clustering yielded the highest average number of patterns (6.5), while latent class analysis showed the greatest variability in the number of identified patterns (Table 2).

Discussion

Summary of findings and comparison with the existing literature

This systematic review of 30 studies provides an update on the current state of multimorbidity patterns identification, their outcomes and the analytical methods used to derive them. We found that across all included studies the most commonly identified and stable patterns are those which include a cardiovascular, metabolic, respiratory or neurological/neuropsychiatric component. The cardiometabolic pattern showed the least heterogeneity in its composition. The most commonly measured outcome between patterns and outcomes was mortality. Higher all-cause mortality was observed in the majority of studies in patterns including a cardiovascular, metabolic or neurological/neuropsychiatric component. We found that identifying multimorbidity patterns using electronic health records and questionnaires are the most prevalent methods of data gathering. The most commonly used methodology in this field was latent class analysis followed by other methods such as, fuzzy c-means, k-means clustering, and factor analysis.

Using the modified Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS), no studies were classified as low quality however, differences between moderate and high-quality st udies were observed. These primarily related to sample representativeness, ascertainment of multimorbidity, and control for confounding variables. Some studies used nationally representative samples, while others were limited to regional populations, reducing generalizability. Adjustment for confounders ranged from minimal (age and sex) to more comprehensive models including socioeconomic and lifestyle factors, which is critical for accurate identification of multimorbidity patterns. Differences were also noted in outcome ascertainment studies using self-reported questionnaire data tended to have lower quality, likely due to reduced accuracy and the lack of clinical validation, compared with those based on electronic health records.

The two most frequently used methods for data gathering were electronic health data and questionnaires. Electronic health data was predominantly used in studies originating from Europe while questionnaires were more often used in Asian countries. In Spain, for example, data is collected using electronic health records in all cases. This is due to the fact that the Spanish electronic health record system is ranked among the top five electronic health record systems among OECD countries [58]. In contrast, Asian countries use questionnaires, which is related to the delayed implementation of the use and collection of electronic health data, heterogeneity between different information systems and infrastructure and financial constraints [59]. The main advantage of using electronic health data is that it enables longitudinal monitoring, monitoring of the development of multimorbidity patterns, and monitoring of large populations [14]. In our literature review, 8 out of 10 studies with a sample size greater than 100,000 used electronic health data. The largest two studies including over 1 million participants used electronic health data [28, 35]. The disadvantage of electronic health data stems from inconsistent recording (underreporting) and data entry errors [60]. A study comparing the quality, specificity, sensitivity and reliability of electronic health data with questionnaires found that there are often differences between the two data sources, with a 61.7% match between them. Questionnaires proved to be more sensitive, while the most complete data was obtained by combining both methods [61]. Across the included studies, most analyses converged on a modest number of multimorbidity clusters, typically identifying four to six distinct patterns per cohort. Among these, the most reproducible and least heterogeneous clusters were those combining cardiovascular and metabolic conditions most commonly heart failure, ischemic or coronary heart disease, alongside diabetes, hypertension, and dyslipidemia [62]. The stability of this cardiometabolic pattern likely reflects a shared pathophysiology, including insulin resistance, endothelial dysfunction, and chronic low-grade inflammation [63]. Its prominence is further supported by its high and increasing prevalence at the population level [64], driven by ageing demographics and the persistence of well-established risk factors such as obesity, sedentary behaviour, and poor dietary patterns.

A second consistently observed cluster was the respiratory pattern, encompassing chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) and asthma. The recurrence of this pattern across datasets is plausibly linked to the cumulative impact of environmental and behavioural exposures, particularly tobacco smoking and air pollution [65]. These exposures not only directly impair pulmonary function but also contribute to systemic inflammation, which may facilitate overlap with other multimorbidity clusters. The role of shared risk factors is critical here for instance, elevated body mass index (BMI) is a recognized risk factor for asthma and is simultaneously central to the development of metabolic disorders such as type 2 diabetes, hypertension, and dyslipidemia [66]. This overlap reinforces the concept that multimorbidity patterns are not discrete entities but rather intersecting constellations shaped by common etiological pathways.

Neurological conditions, in contrast, rarely formed isolated clusters. Only a single study identified a purely neurological pattern, whereas most analyses demonstrated that neurological disorders tend to co-occur with either psychiatric conditions (forming neuropsychiatric clusters) or with cardiovascular and vascular diseases. This recurrent co-clustering may indicate underlying mechanistic links, such as shared vascular pathology, neuroinflammation, or bidirectional interactions between cerebrovascular integrity and neurodegenerative processes [67, 68]. Collectively, these findings suggest that multimorbidity patterns are largely driven by a limited set of interconnected biological mechanisms and shared risk exposures, rather than by organ-specific disease processes in isolation.

The findings indicate substantial heterogeneity in both multimorbidity patterns and their associated outcomes. In particular, cardiovascular and cardiometabolic clusters were consistently associated with elevated risks of both all-cause and cause-specific mortality. This aligns with global epidemiological data showing that circulatory diseases remain the leading cause of death worldwide [69]. In addition to disease composition, the overall burden of chronic conditions exerted a cumulative effect on mortality. Evidence from Yu et al. [54] suggests that all-cause mortality risk increases by approximately 24% with each additional chronic condition, underscoring a dose–response relationship between multimorbidity and adverse outcomes.

Notably, several studies identified the neurological/neuropsychiatric pattern being associated with some of the highest all-cause mortality rates. This observation is likely multifactorial. Contributing mechanisms may include underdiagnosis or undertreatment of both psychiatric and somatic conditions [70, 71], behavioural risk factors (e.g. smoking) [72], and iatrogenic effects particularly the metabolic side effects associated with antipsychotic medications [73]. Additionally, structural determinants such as socioeconomic disadvantage and healthcare marginalization may further exacerbate risk in this group [74, 75].

Regarding healthcare utilization, expenditures increased in a nonlinear manner with the number of chronic conditions, indicating disproportionate cost escalation among patients with more complex multimorbidity [39, 76]. However, disease composition emerged as a stronger determinant of healthcare use than the absolute number of conditions alone. This suggests that specific multimorbidity patterns drive resource needs more than simple disease counts. Overall, these findings emphasize that multimorbidity is heterogeneous and that pattern-based approaches may better inform risk stratification and care planning.

Unsupervised machine learning in multimorbidity research encompasses a range of clustering and pattern-extraction techniques, each predicated on specific assumptions about the structure of the data. A critical but sometimes underappreciated issue is that multimorbidity datasets typically consist of high-dimensional, sparse, and predominantly binary indicators (presence/absence of diagnoses), which has direct implications for method selection [77].

Distance-based methods such as k-means and fuzzy c-means are frequently applied, but their suitability is questionable in this context. K-means performs hard clustering in Euclidean space, assigning individuals to clusters by minimizing within-cluster variance [78]. This implies that cluster centroids are arithmetic means, an operation that is not well-defined for binary disease indicators and may yield non-interpretable “average patients.” More fundamentally, Euclidean distance is not an optimal similarity measure for binary data, where measures such as Jaccard, Dice, or Hamming distance better capture co-occurrence structure [79]. Fuzzy c-means is not constrained by hard assignment because it allows partial membership across clusters, which is conceptually appealing for overlapping disease patterns, but it inherits the same geometric limitations [80]. Alternatives such as k-medoids or k-modes are often more appropriate, as they allow the use of non-Euclidean similarity measures and yield representative exemplars rather than synthetic centroids however, their computational cost may become prohibitive in very large cohorts [81].

Density-based approaches, particularly Hierarchical Density-Based Spatial Clustering of Applications with Noise (HDBSCAN), provide a promising alternative. These methods do not assume spherical clusters or require pre-specification of cluster number, and they can accommodate arbitrary distance metrics, making them well-suited to sparse, binary data. In addition, they explicitly model noise and outliers, which are common in heterogeneous multimorbidity populations [82].

Model-based (probabilistic) approaches, such as latent class analysis, assume that the population comprises a finite number of unobserved subgroups and that observed disease indicators are conditionally independent given class membership. These methods offer a principled statistical framework, including likelihood-based model selection and uncertainty quantification, but their assumptions particularly local independence may be violated in the presence of strong disease interdependencies [83].

Factor analysis differs conceptually from clustering in that it seeks latent dimensions underlying disease co-occurrence rather than discrete patient groupings. Classical factor analysis assumes continuous, approximately normally distributed variables, which limits its applicability to binary diagnosis data. In such settings, multiple correspondence analysis (MCA) provides a more appropriate analogue, enabling dimensionality reduction while preserving the categorical nature of the data [84].

More recently, network-based approaches have gained traction in multimorbidity research. These methods represent diseases (or patients) as nodes in a graph, with edges weighted by measures of co-occurrence or association. Community detection algorithms (e.g., Louvain or Leiden) are then applied to identify clusters of tightly connected nodes [85]. This framework naturally accommodates complex interdependencies between conditions and avoids some of the restrictive assumptions inherent in traditional clustering [86].

A persistent challenge in this field is that different methods and even different parameterizations of the same method often yield divergent multimorbidity patterns, limiting comparability and reproducibility. Addressing this requires improved methodological transparency and rigor. Studies should explicitly report preprocessing steps (e.g., disease coding schemes, prevalence thresholds, handling of sparsity), justify the choice of similarity measures, and fully specify model parameters [87, 88]. The rationale for selecting the number of clusters or classes must also be clearly articulated, whether based on statistical criteria (e.g., information criteria in model-based approaches), structural diagnostics (e.g., dendrogram inspection), or clinical interpretability.

Additionally, no strong linear association was observed between the number of included conditions and the number of identified patterns; although studies with more conditions tended to identify slightly more patterns, this relationship was weak and primarily influenced by the choice of analytical method rather than data alone. Overall, these findings indicate that methodological approach and data source are the principal determinants of variability in multimorbidity pattern identification.

Finally, identified multimorbidity patterns should be systematically validated. Common validation strategies include: (1) association with clinically relevant outcomes; (2) stability across subsamples or resampling procedures; (3) clinical plausibility; (4) robustness across different analytical methods; and (5) consistency in relation to known or hypothesized determinants [89]. Progress in multimorbidity research will depend on tighter alignment between data characteristics and analytical methods, standardized reporting practices, and integration of clinical domain knowledge to ensure that derived patterns are both statistically defensible and clinically meaningful.

Implications for research and practice

This review indicates that multimorbidity patterns are reproducible constructs with distinct prognostic profiles; however, their clinical and public health utility is constrained by methodological heterogeneity. Future research should prioritise harmonisation of disease inclusion criteria used to define patterns, alongside standardisation of analytical methods, to enhance comparability across studies. Longitudinal designs should integrate electronic health records with self-reported data and incorporate clinical examinations to better characterise pattern trajectories particularly transitions from non-specific to higher-risk constellations (e.g. cardiometabolic patterns) and to identify associated health outcomes. Patterns should be assessed using validation testing. Moreover, the validity of multimorbidity patterns ultimately depends on their predictive performance for relevant health outcomes, an aspect addressed in only a subset of existing studies.

From a clinical and public health standpoint, the consistent identification of cardiometabolic and neurological/neuropsychiatric patterns both associated with increased all-cause mortality supports their integration into risk stratification frameworks and population-level prevention strategies. Health systems, traditionally structured around single-disease models, should incorporate multimorbidity pattern recognition into primary care planning, resource allocation, and the development of integrated care pathways.

Strengths and limitations

This review has some limitations. Firstly, using our rigorous inclusion and exclusion criteria might have led to a lack of reporting of studies that used fewer diseases to perform clustering. Secondly, low and middle-income countries were underrepresented, which may limit the generalisability of findings to global populations. Thirdly differences in controlling confounding variables across studies may affect the results. Finally, the quality assessment tool was adapted to the specific research question although this allowed a more tailored evaluation, it may introduce subjectivity and limits comparability with other reviews.

This systematic review has several important strengths. It provides an integrated synthesis of multimorbidity patterns, associated outcomes, and the analytical methods used to derive them domains that are typically examined separately. By explicitly addressing methodological heterogeneity (data sources, disease ascertainment, and clustering techniques), it offers a more critical and methodologically informed interpretation of the evidence. The focus on recent studies enhances relevance in current multimorbidity research, particularly the use of large-scale electronic health records and contemporary analytical approaches. Inclusion of large population-based cohorts further supports the robustness and generalisability of consistently identified patterns, particularly cardiometabolic, cardiovascular, respiratory, and neuropsychiatric clusters were consistently identified and showed distinct associations with outcomes such as mortality and healthcare utilisation.

Conclusion

Across the included studies, the most consistently identified and stable multimorbidity patterns were those comprising cardiovascular, metabolic, respiratory, and neurological/neuropsychiatric components. Patterns that included cardiovascular, metabolic, or neuropsychiatric conditions were most frequently associated with increased all-cause mortality. These findings support the clinical relevance of pattern-based approaches to multimorbidity. Future research should prioritise methodological standardisation, transparent reporting, and systematic validation of identified patterns to improve comparability and facilitate their translation into clinical practice and public health planning.
