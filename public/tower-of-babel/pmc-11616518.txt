Presenting the framework of the whole slide image file Babel fish: An OCR-based file labeling tool
Authors: Nils Englert, Constantin Schwab, Maximilian Legnar, Cleo-Aron Weis
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionMetadata extraction from digitized slides or whole slide image files is a frequent, laborious, and tedious task. In this work, we present a tool to automatically extract all relevant slide information, such as case number, year, slide number, block number, and staining from the macro-images of the scanned slide.We named the tool Babel fish as it helps translate relevant information printed on the slide. It is written to contain certain basic assumptions regarding, for example, the location of certain information. This can be adapted to the respective location. The extracted metadata can then be used to sort digital slides into databases or to link them with associated case IDs from laboratory information systems.Material and methodsThe tool is based on optical character recognition (OCR). For most information, the easyOCR tool is used. For the block number and cases with insufficient results in the first OCR round, a second OCR with pytesseract is applied.Two datasets are used: one for tool development has 342 slides; and another for one for testing has 110 slides.ResultsFor the testing set, the overall accuracy for retrieving all relevant information per slide is 0.982. Of note, the accuracy for most information parts is 1.000, whereas the accuracy for the block number detection is 0.982.ConclusionThe Babel fish tool can be used to rename vast amounts of whole slide image files in an image analysis pipeline. Furthermore, it could be an essential part of DICOM conversion pipelines, as it extracts relevant metadata like case number, year, block ID, and staining.Keywords: DICOM, Digital pathology, Optical character recognition, Automatization

Introduction and objectives

The accurate and automated labeling of entire whole slide image (WSI) files produced by a scanner, concerning the case IDs, typically composed of year and case number, and slide ID, typically composed of block number and slide number, represents a critical phase in digital pathology workflows. Traditionally, 1- or 2-D barcodes affixed to slides are the primary means of associating information with the slide or its corresponding file. Typically, each slide barcode represents the associated slide ID, with which each slide can be uniquely identified and assigned to the related case. These codes are easily decodable by computers during the scan process and exhibit resilience to image degradation. Therefore, these codes are the way to go for routine pathology workflows.

However, a significant challenge arises when laboratory information systems (LIS) and staining machines assign different identifiers onto slides, which are only directly mappable to the slide ID with access to the respective system. Furthermore, there is no common information system in typical biobanking or scientific contexts. Usually, slides are printed and labeled independently of any clinical information database. This results in the slide ID barcode assigned by the LIS not being printed on the slide during the staining process, which makes slide metadata extraction and case assignment during the scan process not possible.

As digital pathology continues to develop, large-scale solutions to this problem will hopefully be developed. Such as by creating standardized communication interfaces between the different systems and using a uniform metadata standard, such as DICOM. Such standardized systems could also make use of slide label bar coding standards, like AUTO1420 from the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute, which is still in development. However, in our case, these problems have not yet been solved. To solve the problem of a missing or unusable imprinted slide ID barcode in a fully automated manner, we introduce a novel tool capable of extracting relevant slide information from label images through a combination of optical character recognition (OCR) and some use of defined heuristics. These heuristics contain factors such as the case number composition and the arrangement of information elements on the label.

The tool is implemented in Python following an object-oriented paradigm and leverages standard libraries such as PyTorch,1 ensuring its adaptability to diverse institutional and situational requirements. Additionally, the comprehensive codebase is openly accessible and thoroughly documented on GitHub, hopefully streamlining its utilization by the broader community.

If enough metadata is printed on a slide label (in the form of printed text), our tool can be used to automatically reconstruct the associated slide- or case-ID and to save the metadata as a separate JSON file or in a DICOM header to facilitate the WSI transfer.

Material and methods

Data type

All slides were generated as part of the routine diagnostics at the Institute of Pathology Heidelberg (IPH). The dataset consists of cases from a small subcohort of a routine pathology lab. These cases are digitized (as .ndpi files), and the institute's nomenclature adheres to specific conventions.

The relevant information is retrieved using the macro-images produced by the scanner (Hamamatsu S60 Scanner) during the scanning preparations.

In Fig. 1, two examples of such macro-images are depicted. The details of the information arrangement are visualized in Fig. 2.

Examples of two macro-images.

Here are two examples of macro images: (A) shows conventional staining and (B) shows immunohistochemical staining. The information on the slide labels is not arranged in a standardized fashion, and there are artifacts like rotation, translation, shadows, handwritten non-relevant information, and dirt.

Anatomy of a slide label.

Typically, slide labels contain sample information that is easily understandable to humans. However, implicit rules and layers exist underlying this foundation.

A: An exemplary data matrix represents the optimal slide identification method for automated computational scanning workflows. However, to get a redundant slide identification system, additional human-readable identifiers are required, according to the latest slide labeling directives defined by the College of American Pathologists (CAP).21

B: A typical case number consists of B1: the case type or laboratory, B2: the ascending case number per year, and B3: the year. Naturally, various other combinations are feasible.

C: The tissue block is denoted here by a combination of characters and numbers, allowing for multiple possible combinations.

D: Staining information with predefined places, e.g., non-IHC (D1) and IHC (D2).

E: Handwritten information or other information, while present, does not contribute to the effective identification of cases.

Typically, there are several challenges for OCR with these macro-images: There are artifacts like rotation and translation, so the position of the information parts is not fixed. Artifacts like overlapping text, dirt, and shadows are also added.

Datasets

Two datasets are used: One for tool development, consisting of 342 slides, and the other for testing, consisting of 110 slides.

Each slide has a unique printed slide ID composed of a case ID, block ID, and slide number (refer to section 3.2 - “Arrangement of the information tags on the slide label” below).

Regarding the potential prefixes, 48 ‘T' and 294 ‘E' cases are included. As for the suffixes, 19 cases were scanned in 2023 and 323 in 2024. For the block number, the default ID is ‘A 1′ (259 times in the dataset). Other IDs like ‘C 42′ are found only in 71 cases.

In the second dataset, used for testing, the only prefix included is ‘E'. Regarding the suffixes, there are only cases from 2024. For the block number, 37 cases have the default ID ‘A 1′, whereas the remaining 73 cases have other IDs like for example ‘B 2′.

The distribution of staining per dataset is highly unbalanced, as hematoxylin and eosin (HE) is the vast majority in both datasets.

Computation

The code has been developed and tested on conventional desktop computers.

Code and data availability

The code for the developed tool is available on GitHub: https://github.com/cpheidelberg/WholeSlideImageFileBabelFish-Tool.

The datasets used for development and testing are from routine cases, as described above. These contain patient-related information like the unique pathology identification number. Therefore, we have decided to not publish this, however, anonymized data.

Theory

Information tags printed on the slide label

In pathology, the clinical work routine is initially focused on the case and subsequently patient-based. Practically, this means that one is always asked for the case ID before any discussion, as every clinical colleague probably knows. Thus, paramount importance is placed on the unique case number as the most critical piece of information associated with each case (B in Fig. 2). Furthermore, every case can be comprised of several tissue specimens represented by a block ID.

How these individual IDs, prefixes, and suffixes are designated is highly variable. For instance, any combination of Arabic or Roman numerals, letters, and special characters can be used. The nomenclature of the IPH, which is also the target of the OCR tool, is explained below.

Case ID: The unique case ID is (compare Table 1), in Germany, typically composed of a prefix for the case type or lab it was produced at, a case number, and a suffix for the year. The prefix is usually a letter signifying the kind of case, such as H for histology cases and C for cytology cases (B1 in Fig. 2). A space separates the prefix from the following case number. The case number is typically carried forward continuously over the year after receipt (B2 in Fig. 2). For example, the first case that occurred in the year 0 AC might be represented as ‘00001/0000’, denoting the first case of that year. The suffix stands for the year, for example, ‘24’ for 2024, and it is separated from the case number by a ‘/’ (B3 in Fig. 2).

Concepts and their combination.

Each slide label contains various pieces of information, such as the unique case ID and block ID. The different parts are explained in the table, and if possible, the DICOM equivalent or respective term is mentioned.

The unique case number, directly related to the patient's specimen, and the unique slide IDs are significant for a digital pathology workflow. The latter is directly mapped to the corresponding whole slide image files. Only based on this slide ID can image files be uploaded to a PACS and correctly mapped to the specimen or respective case and ultimately to the patient.

Block and slide ID: Furthermore, several tissue blocks exist per case or submitted specimen. These tissue blocks have an ID, e.g., composed of Arabic, a single character, or a combination of both. For instance, A denotes the first subset. Something like ‘D 42’ would be the 42nd block in subset D (C in Fig. 2). The unique case ID (compare Table 1), in combination with the block ID, describes a unique block. As for every tissue block, several staining can be done; the aforementioned case ID with the block ID and the slide number describes a unique slide as the unique slide ID.

A submitted specimen is a study represented by a study ID in DICOM terms. The staining are in DICOM-terms the series, like different sequences in an MRI examination.

Arrangement of the information tags on the slide label

All of the aforementioned information is arranged on the slide label according to a defined pattern specific to each institution. For instance, at our institution, the case number is in the lower half of the label, with the block number beneath it. The staining information is either in the upper right corner for immunohistochemistry (IHC), as D2 in Fig. 2, or next to the case number for non-IHC as D1 in Fig. 2.

Additionally, there is reserved space for a data matrix (A in Fig. 2) and handwritten information like ‘Calcification’, ‘Metall’, or ‘Hard to cut' (E in Fig. 2). Both are neglected in this work as they are either not readable by the respective tools, not interpretable, or not readable at all due to the large variance in handwriting.

Stepwise approach to retrieve the information

As delineated previously, the label contains diverse information, such as case numbers or staining, which are distributed across its surface. Additionally, noise factors like image alterations or missing label portions may be present. Hence, a systematic approach is employed herein, comprising the following sequential steps:

Step 1: Image acquisition of the slide label.

Step 2: Augmentation of the slide label image.

Step 3: OCR.

Step 4: Extraction of pertinent information components.

Step 1 with image acquisition

The WSIs used in this work are generated by a Hamamatsu slide scanner (Hamamatsu S60) and are stored in the proprietary .ndpi file format.

These files are accessed using the openslide-based2,3 Python tool openslide-python.4

Step 2 with augmentation of the image of the slide label

As depicted in Fig. 1, shadows and other disturbances can be on the slide label image. To remove these disturbances, to correct the images, two image inpainting strategies are implemented.

Strategy 1 relies on conventional image restoration techniques like contrast stretching, thresholding, and morphological operations. Furthermore, the background is removed using the ski-image-implantation of the rolling ball algorithm, initially described by Sternberg et al.4,5

Strategy 2, which is still experimental, relies on CNN-based image segmentation for the text parts in the image. Therefore, a FastSCNN Pytorch implementation has been trained on a virtual dataset.1,6,7 This virtual dataset is a Pytorch dataset that generates per batch random potential case numbers, block numbers, staining information, and other random text against a randomly chosen background. Furthermore, this dataset also randomly generated image noise like shadows or salt-and-pepper noise.

Step 3 with optical character recognition (OCR)

For the recognition of characters and letters, the Python tool EasyOCR has been used.8 It generated a Python dictionary per fed in label image containing the position of the retrieved text pieces.

Furthermore, for block number identification, the Python library pytesseract has been used as an additional OCR, which basically relies on the tesseract OCR.9,10

In summary, this project has used the two major OCR libraries for Python, potentially benefiting from the strengths of both.

Extraction of pertinent information components

In this step, essential data such as the case number, ID prefix or laboratory ID, ID suffix or year, block number, and staining details are extracted. For every of these information pieces, a specific tool has been implemented.

For the case ID (A inFig. 3): From all the retrieved text pieces, pattern- and length-based, the case number is identified. In our setting, the pattern is that the year with two digits is at the end of the ID and separated from the case number per year by a /. The prefix, in our case, the characters ‘E', ‘R', or ‘T', are only for the non-IHC cases present. Compare the examples given in Fig. 2A and Fig. 3A.

Stepwise information extraction approach.

The information parts are arranged with a specific pattern on the slide label. After label image acquisition (section 3.3.1 - “Step 1 with image acquisition”) and image impairment (section 3.3.2 - “Step 2 with augmentation of the image of the slide label”), optical character recognition (OCR) is applied (section 3.3.3 - “Step 3 with optical character recognition”). Afterward, the relevant information is extracted stepwise (section 3.3.4 - “Extraction of pertinent information components” with extraction of pertinent information components).

A: First, the case number is retrieved based on its known pattern. In our case, there is a leading letter for the specimen type or for the lab, and there is at the end a 2-digits year identifier separated by a /.

B: Second, the block number is extracted. It can be a single character, a single letter, or a combination of both. As additional argument, its location beneath the case number (purple box) is take into account.

C: Third, the staining information is retrieved based on string comparison. Therefore, a list of known staining is provided.

For the block ID (B inFig. 3): Two strategies are implemented to retrieve the block ID. Strategy 1 is pattern-based. It is assumed that the block ID either consists, as in our setting, of a single letter, of a single digit, or a combination of both. Furthermore, there are no special characters. Compare the example given in Fig. 2B. Strategy 2 additionally relies on the known position of the block number. Given the consistent positioning directly beneath the case ID, we use a relative positioning approach. In our setting, it is every time beneath the case number, usually the closest information piece. Compare the example given in Fig. 2B and note that the depicted dashed box is also used as a region, an area where block numbers could be.

For the staining (C inFig. 3): Staining names and abbreviations are variable and do not follow a fixed pattern in our setting. Therefore, a matching approach is employed. A table is provided to the tool, containing all available staining and their name variations. This table also covers typical OCR errors like ‘CDZ0’ instead of ‘CD20’. Two strategies are combined here. In strategy 1, the matching stain from the table is searched for. If no match is found, the default stain is set to HE, because in our institute, HE-stained slides do not receive any printed staining information in most cases. In strategy 2, fuzzy logic-based string matching is used to consider writing variations and OCR errors.

Metric

The macro-images of a total of 452 whole slides (compare section 2.2 - “Datasets” above) are used to establish and evaluate the performance of the use tool. For these macro-label images, all information is manually defined as ground truth.

The tool's results are compared to this ground truth in two ways. For example, for the case ‘E 1234/42’ with the slide ‘B-3 CD20’, the resulting prefix ‘E', case number ‘1234’, suffix or year ‘42’, the block ID ‘B-3′, and the staining name ‘CD21’ the result is evaluated as follows.

First, it is measured if all information, including the final ID containing the case number, the block number, and the staining, is correctly retrieved. This would be incorrect in the example given above as there is a difference in staining with ‘CD21’ instead of ‘CD20’.

Second, for all information pieces separately the results are compared to the ground truth. In the example given above, there would be four correct predictions and one incorrect.

In analogy to classification tasks, accuracy is applied to quantify the results.

Results

The herein presented OCR-based WSI file labeling tool, which we coined as WSI file Babel fish, referring to a famous science fiction novel,11 is developed and tested on WSI files from our routine lab.

Information retrieval performance

A training set comprising 342 WSI files is used to develop the tool.

This set is used to set the parameters for retrieving the information parts: The case number (e.g., 1234), the suffix or the year (e.g., ‘/24’), the prefix or lab identifier (e.g., ‘E'), the staining (e.g., ‘CD138’), and the block ID (e.g., ‘B 42′). As in many cases, there is only an HE staining and only one block; the default staining is HE, and the default block number is A 1. It is important to mention that these default values occur frequently in our datasets. This could have a positive influence on the archived metrics, because such default value predictions may be more likely correct. However, in our institute, we are not able to avoid these specific default value assignments, because all our HE-stained slides usually do not receive any staining labeling at all, and the same applies to slides with block ID A1.

For the training set, with conventional image processing before OCR, the overall rate of correctly identified information is 339/342 (compare Table 2). The accuracy for the entire information set is 0.991, whereas for information parts like block ID, it is in the range 0.994–1.000. Regarding the training column in Table 2, it is worth mentioning that a total of four label information was incorrectly recognized, with these four errors being spread over only three cases. Specifically, in one case, both the year and case number were incorrectly recognized.

For both datasets, the block ID is the less reliable retrieval, as it is also the shortest information tag. Because of this, there is no large error margin for the block ID. In contrast, for the case number, we have defined rules for missing spaces or wrongly placed separation signs.

For the cases where the case number retrieval has not worked, there are either image distortions, dirt, or partially overlapping tags.

Effect of the image enhancement

The macro-images of the used train and test datasets do contain image disturbances like shadows, half-printed letters, dust, etc.

To compensate for this, we are employing standard image processing techniques like contrast stretching.

Furthermore, we have developed an inpainting tool to compensate for such image disturbances. This tool uses standard digital image processing techniques like morphological operations, the rolling-ball algorithm,4,5 or a FastSCNN Pytorch implementation.1,6,7 The latter is trained in reconstructing texts in a virtual dataset.

To test these three image-repairing approaches, the images from the test set are randomly disturbed by applying shadows and adding salt-and-pepper noise. These noises are also randomly added and are random in regard to parameters like shadow intensity and salt-and-pepper noise grain size. Typically, the noise is so strong that even a human would find it difficult to inspect visually.

In such a setting, with three iterations, the overall accuracies are for the morphological operations-based compensation 0.264 ± 0.132, for the rolling ball algorithm-based inpainting 0.436 ± 0.022 and the FastSCNN-based inpainting 0.124 ± 0.033. Interestingly, the individual information tags perform differently. In this setting, the case number is not the most stable one, with 0.339 ± 0.101 for the morphology-based approach, 0.480 ± 0.019 for the ball algorithm-based approach, and respectively with 0.327 ± 0.0451for the FastSCNN-based approach.

In contrast, the block ID seems to be more stable, represented by the following values 0.552 ± 0.037, 0.606 ± 0.011, and 0.406 ± 0.053, respectively.

In summary, all three approaches do not reliably work in the case of massive noise. In such a setting, the ball algorithm-based image inpainting is surprisingly better than the FastSCNN-based approach.

Discussion

To enhance the effectiveness of LIS in pathology and histology, it is crucial to link each case database entry with its associated WSI files and their metadata like staining and specimen number. This would enable users to easily and quickly access specific digital slides of a case, allowing them to digitally microscope and annotate the slides with just a few mouse clicks. The need for physical retrieval and delivery of case-related slides would then be eliminated, resulting in significant time savings. Such systems require tools that fully automate the extraction of necessary metadata from newly scanned slides to assign the digital slide to the correct case. Although most scanners already offer tools that can read out slide-metadata via QR codes which are printed on the slides, we are not aware of any tool that can also process slide labels on which the meta information is printed in the form of text.

Therefore, most of us have encountered the tedious task of renaming WSI files after scanning according to our local case identification conventions, so that all necessary metadata is assigned to each file.

In this paper, we present a tool that can be used in both research and routine settings, provided no data matrices are available, to reliably and automatically extract all relevant slide metadata based on OCR. The extracted metadata can then be used to rename the slide files meaningfully and/or link them to case databases. Therefore, whenever facing a vast set of WSI files with only textual printed labels, our WSI file naming tool can be of help.

Limitations of the tool and the study

One major limitation of our tools is that they have been developed using a small and unbalanced dataset from a single institution. So, it can be assumed that our trained OCR model most likely has poor generalizability. Users from other institutions who wish to apply it to their WSI must adapt the tools to their local slide naming conventions. For instance, at another institution, the block number might be represented by Roman letters placed to the right of the case number. However, because our code is publicly available, well-documented inline, and written in an object-oriented manner, it should be easily adaptable to other settings by defining a child class.

Another limitation is that our datasets could be more balanced concerning staining. Most cases are stained using the default HE stain. Therefore, it has yet to be tested on all 300 staining methods available at our institution. Additionally, as the staining detection relies on a table of known staining, this table must be adapted for use in another institution.

It is also important to mention that our tool is intended more as a temporary metadata extraction solution which can be used if slide identifier barcodes are not available or cannot be linked to a LIS case entry. Hopefully, in the future, more standardized cross-system solutions based, for example, on DICOM, will be developed, so that workaround solutions like ours are no longer necessary.

Finally, regarding the usage in routine digital pathology, the tool's accuracy, albeit larger than 0.92, needs to be better for routine usage. Every wrongly labeled file could lead to a misdiagnosis or patient mix-up in a routine setting. For routine use, a safety mechanism would have to be installed. For example, matching with information from the laboratory information system, which knows which slides per case exist, could be a safety mechanism. Additional image analysis tools that check, for example, whether the expected tissue is present on the section and whether the specified staining is present could also be used as a safety mechanism in this context. However, mixing up stains or tissues is not the greatest danger. Typically, a pathologist notices this relatively quickly. The greatest threat is a mix-up of similar tissues, such as two cases with prostate biopsies.

Next developments for the Babel fish tool

Another major area for improvement of our tool is that it currently only works with printed text. As depicted in Fig. 2, handwritten information is often found on the slide label. In contrast to our example in Fig. 2, this handwritten information can be relevant by for example warning that there will be many artifacts due to calcification. Writing all case information by hand on the slide is also common in a scientific setting. Therefore, our WSI Babel fish tool would be of no help. In this regard, a subsequent tool version could incorporate OCR for handwritten texts.

Interestingly, there are publications like Soumya et al. showing that the tesseract OCR, which we also have incorporated into our tools, is well suited for the recognition of handwritten letters.12 However, based on the dataset available to us, we cannot confirm that. Despite the minimal information handwritten on the sections, such as the abbreviation “HD” for Heidelberg, our experience indicates that neither easyOCR8 nor pytesseract9,10 can accurately recognize this with the given parameters. Maybe Recurrent Neural Network-based tools like those proposed by Parthiban et al. could apply to slide labels.13 However, our dataset needs more examples, and the variance is significantly higher here. Not only does everyone have different handwriting, but the font size, for example, is also subject to a high degree of variability. Thus, a vast dataset needs to be collected for handwritten slide label identification.

Another limitation is that our developed tool is based on various rules in addition to the naming conventions described above (compare Table 1). For example, the localization of the respective pieces of information is directly predefined (compare Fig. 3). In addition, the identification and processing of the case number, suffix, etc. is also based on various rules. In summary, these rules lead to reliable results in our setting. However, in a different setting, for example, with slide modifications in our lab's slide printing, the rules must be adapted again. One significant advantage of machine learning is typically that it is more robust against data changes and variations due to generalization. In the next project, image caption generation tools or image-to-text translation models could be an alternative.14, 15, 16 Of note, our limited dataset of only 442 would be too small for such an approach. Either more data would be needed, or a data generation tool would also be required.

Another limitation is that our OCR model was finetuned with a relatively small and unbalanced dataset solely from our institution. This could result in limited generalizability, making it more challenging to recognize slide labels that differ significantly from those at our institution. In order to train a more generalizable slides label OCR model, a much larger and more divers dataset could be collected from various institutions. Because our project is freely available and modifiable, other institutions can also finetune our model to fit their local datasets, enabeling them to successfully our tool within their own organization.

As mentioned before, we configured our tool to return the default value “HE” if no lettering can be detected in the staining-area of the given slide label. The same is applied if no block ID can be detected. Then, the default value “A1” is returned. It is important to note that assigning default values comes with the drawback that we are not able to determine whether the value of the corresponding attribute (here: stain and or block) could be recognized or not. It would be better to predict nothing (return None), if no characters were recognized. Because in such a case, we have no guarantee at all whether the returned default value is correct. However, in our institute, we are not able to avoid these specific default value assignments. The reason for this is that, until we recently updated our nomenclature all our HE-stained slides did not include staining labeling. And, the same applies to slides with block ID A1. One solution for this specific issue could be to implement an additional function to distinguish whether, in specific areas, is nothing printed on or whether something is printed on but cannot be assigned to a valid value. If the second situation has been detected, one could return None or issue a warning that this specific default value prediction is uncertain.

Whole slide file naming and implications for DICOM

The automated extraction of relevant information per section or WSI file presented here is an essential step for research projects and routine use. In the next step, the respective WSI file would then have to be named accordingly. However, this again leads to potentially different nomenclatures; e.g., the suffix could be separated with ‘a-‘ or ‘a_’. Or, it may be necessary to save the extracted metadata separately, e.g., in a JSON file. However, this would then again separate the data from the metadata.

This is why working with DICOM is advantageous, as the DICOM file already contains corresponding tags for the extracted metadata.17, 18, 19 This means that the data are standardized on the one hand, and data and metadata are combined in one file on the other. In such a DICOM setting, the Babel fish tool would be part of the DICOM conversion as it prepares the metadata. However, with the further development of digital pathology, it would be desirable if more scanner manufacturers would support the DICOM standard, which seems to be very rare at this time.
