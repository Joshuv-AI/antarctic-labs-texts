Enhanced anti Pseudomonas aeruginosa efficacy of niosomal-encapsulated Frondoside A from sea cucumber: a novel biotechnological approach
Authors: Tohid Piri-Gharaghie, Maryam Hassanpoor, Neda Jegargoshe-Shirin, Mona Soosanirad, Shahoo Khayati, Ali Farhadi-Biregani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPseudomonas aeruginosa (P. aeruginosa) forms persistent biofilms that limit antibiotic efficacy. Frondoside A, a triterpenoid glycoside from sea cucumbers, possesses antimicrobial potential but suffers from poor solubility and stability. This study aimed to enhance its antibacterial and anti-biofilm activities via niosomal encapsulation and to assess its effect on biofilm-related gene expression.MethodsNiosomal Frondoside A was synthesized by thin-film hydration and characterized for size, charge, and encapsulation efficiency. Antibacterial activity was determined using agar diffusion and MIC/MBC assays, while biofilm inhibition was quantified by crystal violet assay. Expression of GacS and PslA was analyzed by qRT-PCR with 16 S rRNA as an internal control.ResultsThe optimized niosomes had a zeta potential of − 8.6 mV, and 78 ± 0.408% encapsulation efficiency. Compared to the free drug, the niosomal formulation reduced MIC/MBC values from 6.25/12.5 mg mL⁻¹ to 1.56/3.125 mg mL⁻¹, enlarged inhibition zones by 45–60%, and decreased biofilm biomass by 68–79% (p < 0.001). Furthermore, qRT-PCR showed significant downregulation of GacS and PslA genes.ConclusionsNiosomal encapsulation markedly improves the antimicrobial and gene-suppressive efficacy of Frondoside A, offering a potent nanocarrier approach against multidrug-resistant P. aeruginosa biofilms.Keywords: Frondoside A, Niosomes, P. aeruginosa, Biofilm inhibition, GacS, PslA, Nanocarrier

Introduction

The rapid emergence of multidrug-resistant (MDR) Pseudomonas aeruginosa has become a critical challenge in modern healthcare, limiting the efficacy of conventional antibiotics and complicating the treatment of infections. This opportunistic pathogen is particularly notorious for its intrinsic resistance mechanisms and, more importantly, its remarkable ability to form biofilms [1]. Bacterial biofilms are structured communities of bacterial cells enclosed in a self-produced polymeric matrix that adheres to surfaces. The clinical significance of biofilms cannot be overstated; they act as a formidable barrier, protecting bacteria from host immune defenses and significantly reducing the permeability of antibiotics, often rendering standard therapeutic options ineffective [2]. Consequently, infections associated with P. aeruginosa biofilms—such as cystic fibrosis pneumonia, chronic wound infections, and indwelling medical device infections—are persistent and extremely difficult to eradicate, leading to high morbidity and mortality rates [3]. As antibiotic pipelines continue to shrink, innovative biotechnological strategies are urgently required to disrupt these biofilms and combat resistant pathogens.

The global situation regarding antibiotic resistance is alarming, with P. aeruginosa identified as one of the leading “priority pathogens” by the World Health Organization (WHO). Recent surveillance data indicates a rising trend in resistance to first-line antibiotics, including carbapenems and third-generation cephalosporins, leaving clinicians with few or no treatment options [4]. This crisis underscores the urgent need for novel therapeutic agents. Marine-derived natural products, such as Frondoside A isolated from sea cucumbers, have attracted considerable attention for their potent antimicrobial and anti-biofilm activities [5].

Marine organisms, particularly sea cucumbers (Holothuroidea), are rich sources of structurally diverse bioactive compounds. Among these, triterpene glycosides have demonstrated potent antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, and anticancer properties [5]. Frondoside A, a sulfated triterpene glycoside isolated from Cucumaria frondosa, has emerged as one of the most active compounds with wide biological relevance. The rationale for selecting Frondoside A in this study is based on its unique mechanism of action; it is known for its membrane-disrupting activity against microbial pathogens. Its amphiphilic structure allows interaction with lipid bilayers, leading to destabilization of microbial cell membranes and subsequent cell death [6]. This mechanism is particularly valuable against biofilm-embedded bacteria where membrane penetration is a key hurdle. However, the clinical application of Frondoside A is severely limited by its poor aqueous solubility and low bioavailability.

To overcome these physicochemical limitations, nanotechnology-based drug delivery systems offer a promising solution. Among these, niosomes, which are non-ionic surfactant vesicles, represent an efficient and cost-effective system for encapsulating hydrophobic drugs [7]. The specific advantages of delivering Frondoside A in a niosomal encapsulated form are manifold. Niosomes protect labile molecules from degradation, improve cellular uptake, and facilitate the penetration of biofilms due to their nano-scale size and fusogenic properties [8]. Encapsulation of Frondoside A into niosomes offers the potential to improve solubility, enhance stability, and prolong therapeutic efficacy, thereby representing a powerful approach to combat resistant P. aeruginosa. Furthermore, niosomal formulations offer high drug loading, sustained release profiles, and improved pharmacokinetics compared to the free drug [9]. This strategy has been successfully applied to various natural compounds but remains underexplored for marine glycosides.

Recent research on marine-derived niosomal formulations has shown significant activity against pathogens such as Staphylococcus aureus and Escherichia coli [10, 11]. These findings support the hypothesis that encapsulating Frondoside A in niosomes could provide a promising alternative to conventional antibiotics. Additionally, niosomes are biodegradable and biocompatible, making them suitable for clinical translation. They can be engineered to achieve targeted delivery, reduce systemic side effects, and increase the therapeutic index [12–14].

Considering the growing need for novel antimicrobials, marine-derived compounds such as Frondoside A could play a crucial role in future drug discovery. Coupling these natural agents with advanced delivery systems like niosomes provides a powerful combination to address bacterial resistance. This study aims to investigate the antibacterial and anti-biofilm potential of niosomal-formulated Frondoside A from sea cucumber. By integrating marine biotechnology with nanotechnology, we propose a novel and sustainable strategy for developing potent antibacterial therapeutics that may contribute to overcoming global antimicrobial resistance.

Materials and methods

Materials Frondoside A (purity ≥ 98%, CAS No: 127367-76-4) was purchased from ZistYar Sanat Company (Tehran, Iran). Span 60, cholesterol, and chloroform were obtained from ZistYar Sanat Company (Tehran, Iran). Non-ionic surfactants (Tween 60 and Span 20) and phosphate buffer saline (PBS) were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). All bacterial culture reagents were purchased from ZistYar Sanat Company (Tehran, Iran). Standard antibiotics used for sensitivity testing, including ceftazidime (CAZ, 30 µg), ampicillin-sulbactam (SAM, 10 µg + 10 µg), kanamycin (K, 30 µg), and ciprofloxacin (CIP, 5 µg), were obtained from MAST Group Ltd. (Merseyside, UK).

Preparation of Niosomal Frondoside A nanoparticles

Niosomes were prepared using the thin-film hydration method. Briefly, span 60 and cholesterol (in a 2:1 molar ratio) were dissolved in chloroform. The organic solvent was evaporated under reduced pressure using a rotary evaporator (Heidolph, Germany) at 60 °C for 45 min to form a thin, dry lipid film. The resulting film was then hydrated with PBS containing Frondoside A (1 mg·mL⁻¹) at 60 °C for 1 h with gentle agitation. The suspension was subsequently sonicated using a probe sonicator (Bandelin, Germany) at 40 kHz for 10 min (in an ice bath to prevent overheating) to yield uniform niosomal vesicles. The suspension was centrifuged at 15,000 rpm for 1 h at 4 °C to remove unencapsulated drug. The pellet was resuspended in PBS and stored at 4 °C.

Characterization of niosomal nanoparticles

To ensure the accuracy and reliability of the physicochemical analysis, nanoparticle characterization assays—including particle size distribution, zeta potential analysis, field emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM), and attenuated total reflectance–Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (ATR-FTIR)—were conducted in collaboration with ZistYar Sanat Company (Tehran, Iran). All experimental procedures were performed using standardized protocols and calibrated instrumentation to ensure reproducibility and adherence to research ethics.

Particle size and zeta potential

The hydrodynamic diameter and polydispersity index (PDI) of the niosomal formulations were determined using the Dynamic Light Scattering (DLS) technique. Measurements were performed using a ZetaPALS analyzer (Brookhaven Instruments Corp., NY, USA) at a controlled temperature of 25 °C. Prior to analysis, samples were appropriately diluted with distilled water to prevent multiple scattering and ensure optimal measurement accuracy.

Morphology by FE-SEM

The surface morphology and vesicular structure of the nanoparticles were examined using Field Emission Scanning Electron Microscopy (FE-SEM). Imaging was carried out using a MIRA3 microscope (TESCAN, Czech Republic). To enhance conductivity and image resolution, samples were prepared by sputter-coating with a thin layer of gold (thickness: 5 nm) prior to visualization.

Fourier-Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR)

Chemical characterization and confirmation of drug-excipient interactions were performed using Attenuated Total Reflectance–Fourier Transform Infrared (ATR-FTIR) spectroscopy. Freeze-dried niosomal powders were analyzed using a spectrometer from Bruker (MA, USA). Spectra were recorded over the wavenumber range of 400–4000 cm⁻¹ to identify characteristic functional groups and verify the successful encapsulation of Frondoside A within the niosomal bilayer.

Bacterial strains and culture conditions

Two bacterial strains were used in this study: P. aeruginosa ZYS-BC-34-2 (standard strain) and a clinical isolate, P. aeruginosa ZYS-BC-34-1. Both strains were kindly provided by ZistYar Sanat Company (Tehran, Iran). All bacterial strains were maintained on Mueller-Hinton agar plates (ZistYar Sanat Tech Dev Group, Iran) at 37 °C for 24 h under aerobic conditions. For experimental use, single colonies were transferred into Mueller-Hinton broth and grown to logarithmic phase. The suspensions were then adjusted to a turbidity equivalent to 0.5 McFarland standard (1.0 × 10⁸ CFU/mL) by measuring absorbance at 600 nm.

Antibiotic susceptibility test

The disk diffusion method was employed following CLSI M100 (2020) guidelines to confirm resistance profiles. Bacterial suspensions (0.5 McFarland) were evenly spread onto Mueller-Hinton agar using sterile cotton swabs. Antibiotic disks were placed onto the agar surface, and the plates were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. The diameter of inhibition zones was measured to determine sensitivity patterns. The antibiotics tested included Cefepime (FEP, 30 µg), Trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole (SXT, 1.25/23.75 µg), Amikacin (AN, 30 µg) and Ciprofloxacin (CIP, 5 µg).

Interpretation Criteria: The isolates were classified as Sensitive (S), Intermediate (I), or Resistant (R) based on the zone diameter breakpoints recommended by the CLSI M100 (2020) guidelines. The specific breakpoints for each antibiotic were as follows:

Cefepime (FEP, 30 µg): S ≥ 18 mm, I = 15–17 mm, R ≤ 14 mm.

Biofilm formation assays

Congo Red Agar (CRA) method

CRA plates containing brain heart infusion (37 g/L), sucrose (50 g/L), agar (10 g/L), and Congo red (0.8 g/L) were inoculated with bacterial strains. After incubation at 37 °C for 24 h, black colonies indicated biofilm-producing bacteria, while red colonies were considered non-biofilm formers.

Microtiter Plate Assay (MPA)

Sterile 96-well polystyrene plates were inoculated with 200 µL of bacterial suspension and incubated for 24 h at 37 °C. Wells were stained with 1% crystal violet, rinsed with PBS, and destained with 33% acetic acid. Optical density (OD) was measured at 570 nm using an ELISA reader (Stat Fax2100, Awareness Technology, USA). The biofilm-forming capacity was categorized based on the OD values of the test wells compared to the negative control (ODc). The ODc was defined as the mean OD of the sterile medium (PBS) wells. The biofilm classification was determined using the following thresholds.

Weak biofilm: ODc < OD ≤ 2×ODc

Non-biofilm former: OD ≤ ODc

Non-biofilm former: OD ≤ ODc

Broth microdilution assay (MIC determination)

Serial dilutions of free Frondoside A and niosomal formulations were tested in 96-well plates against P. aeruginosa strains. The concentration range tested was 1.56 to 100 mg·mL⁻¹. Ciprofloxacin was used as the positive control antibiotic. After overnight incubation at 37 °C, bacterial growth was monitored by absorbance at 630 nm. The MIC was defined as the lowest concentration that inhibited visible growth.

Agar well diffusion assay

Mueller-Hinton agar plates inoculated with bacterial suspensions were used to assess antibacterial activity. Wells were filled with 50 µL of free Frondoside A (FFA), encapsulated Frondoside A (EFA), or PBS (negative control). After 24 h at 37 °C, inhibition zones were measured.

Anti-biofilm analysis using MPA assay

Sterile 96-well polystyrene plates were inoculated with 200 µL of bacterial suspension and treated with FFA, EFA, or PBS. Plates were incubated for 24 h at 37 °C to allow biofilm formation. Wells were then stained with 1% crystal violet, rinsed with PBS, and destained using 33% acetic acid. Optical density at 570 nm was measured using an ELISA reader (Stat Fax2100, USA). Biofilm formation was quantified and categorized as strong, moderate, weak, or negative. Comparison between FFA, EFA, and control wells allowed evaluation of the anti-biofilm efficacy of free versus niosomal Frondoside A.

RNA extraction and quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted from bacterial cultures using the RNX-Plus solution (SinaClone, Tehran, Iran) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA concentration and purity were determined spectrophotometrically (Nanodrop, Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA, USA) by measuring the A260/A280 ratio, and samples with a ratio between 1.8 and 2.0 were considered pure. cDNA synthesis was performed using a commercial kit (YT4500, Yekta Tajhiz Azma, Tehran, Iran).

Quantitative real-time PCR was conducted to assess the relative expression of GacS and PslA genes in P. aeruginosa strains ZYS-BC-34-1 and ZYS-BC-34-2. The 16 S rRNA gene was selected as the internal control (reference gene) for normalization (Table 1). The stability of the 16 S rRNA expression across all experimental groups (PBS control, free Frondoside A, and Niosomal Frondoside A) was verified prior to analysis, confirming that its expression levels remained unaffected by the treatments and were suitable for normalizing the target gene expression data. qPCR amplification was carried out with SYBR Green Master Mix (Takara, Shiga, Japan) on a Rotor-Gene RG-300 system. The relative mRNA expression levels were calculated using the 2–ΔΔCt method.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed in triplicate. Results are expressed as mean ± SD. Statistical significance was determined by one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test using GraphPad Prism version 8. Differences were considered significant at p < 0.05.

Morphology by FE-SEM

The FE-SEM images reveal that the synthesized particles exhibit a relatively uniform and homogeneous morphology. The particles are predominantly spherical in shape with minimal aggregation observed across the sample surface. Based on the scale bar analysis, the average particle size is estimated to be approximately 43 nm. This nanoscale dimension confirms the successful formation of nanoparticles with a narrow size distribution. The morphology and size of the Niosomal Frondoside A nanoparticles were analyzed using Field Emission Scanning Electron Microscopy (FeSEM)

Zeta Potential (ZP)

The zeta potential of -8.6 mV indicates low colloidal stability, which is a known limitation of niosomal formulations. However, the structural integrity provided by the surfactant bilayer (Span 60 and Tween 60) and cholesterol contributes to physical stability through steric stabilization, partially compensating for the limited electrostatic repulsion. Future studies may consider surface modification (PEGylation or coating with chitosan) to enhance colloidal stability. Nevertheless, the formulation remained physically stable throughout the study period. The niosomal formulation of Frondoside A was successfully synthesized with an encapsulation efficiency of 78 ± 0.408%, indicating a high capacity for drug loading. The PDI is approximately 0.001. This value (less than 0.1) indicates a monodisperse and homogeneous size distribution, which is consistent with the Distribution Form Monodisperse.

Fourier transform infra-red (FT-IR) analysis

The FTIR spectrum of Frondoside A displays characteristic absorption bands corresponding to its functional groups. A broad and intense band around 3400 cm⁻¹ is attributed to O–H stretching vibrations, confirming the presence of hydroxyl groups. Strong absorptions at 2920 and 2850 cm⁻¹ correspond to asymmetric and symmetric C–H stretching vibrations of alkyl chains. A distinct peak at approximately 1750 cm⁻¹ indicates carbonyl (C = O) groups. Additionally, the region between 1250 and 1000 cm⁻¹ shows multiple peaks associated with C–O stretching of glycosidic linkages, characteristic of saponin glycosides. These spectral features confirm the presence of both sugar moieties and aglycone components in Frondoside A.

The FTIR spectrum of Niosomal Frondoside A reveals distinct absorption peaks corresponding to key functional groups involved in the formulation. A broad band around 3400 cm⁻¹ indicates O–H stretching vibrations, confirming the presence of hydroxyl groups. Peaks near 2920–2850 cm⁻¹ correspond to C–H stretching of aliphatic chains, reflecting the niosomal lipid components. The strong absorption at approximately 1740 cm⁻¹ represents the carbonyl (C = O) group, suggesting ester linkage formation. Additionally, the band at 1650 cm⁻¹ may indicate C = C or amide I vibrations, associated with unsaturated or peptide structures. Peaks observed between 1100 and 1000 cm⁻¹ correspond to C–O–C stretching, typical of polysaccharides or ether linkages, while signals below 800 cm⁻¹ represent the fingerprint region of Frondoside A within the niosomal matrix (Table 3).

The antimicrobial susceptibility results

The antimicrobial susceptibility results demonstrated that free Frondoside A exhibited MIC and MBC values of 6.25 mg·mL⁻¹ and 12.5 mg·mL⁻¹, respectively, against both clinical isolates P. aeruginosa ZYS-BC-34-2 and P. aeruginosa ZYS-BC-34-1. In contrast, the Niosomal Frondoside A formulation displayed markedly enhanced antibacterial activity, with MIC and MBC values reduced to 1.56 mg·mL⁻¹ and 3.125 mg·mL⁻¹, respectively, for both tested strains. These findings indicate that incorporation of Frondoside A into the niosomal nanocarrier increased its antibacterial efficacy by approximately four-fold compared with the free compound, thereby significantly lowering the concentration required to inhibit and kill the bacteria. The consistency of this improvement across both isolates confirms that the enhanced effect is strongly associated with the niosomal formulation strategy rather than strain-specific differences (Fig. 3). Although the observed MIC and MBC values in the mg·mL⁻¹ range may initially appear high when compared to conventional antibiotic therapies, it is essential to contextualize these findings within the scope of natural product-based drug discovery and the specific challenges posed by multidrug-resistant (MDR) P. aeruginosa. First, isolated compounds derived from marine organisms, such as Frondoside A, frequently exhibit higher MIC values in vitro due to the inherent complexity of their chemical structure and potential solubility limitations. Second, the primary objective of this study was to establish a proof-of-concept demonstrating that niosomal encapsulation could significantly potentiate the bioactivity of Frondoside A. The four-fold reduction in MIC achieved by the niosomal formulation serves as a strong validation of the efficacy of this delivery system. Future studies focusing on structural modification of the compound, combination therapy with existing antibiotics, or in vivo pharmacokinetic assessments are warranted to further bridge the gap between these in vitro values and clinical application.

Agar well diffusion results

The agar-well diffusion assay revealed clear differences in antibacterial activity between free Frondoside A and its niosomal formulation against the two tested P. aeruginosa isolates. There were no inhibition zones for ZYS-BC-34-2 and ZYS-BC-34-1 treated with baseline (PBS). Treatment with free Frondoside A moderately increased the inhibition zones to approximately 16 mm (ZYS-BC-34-2) and 18 mm (ZYS-BC-34-1), corresponding to ~ 1.8–2.0-fold increases compared with PBS. These changes showed statistically significant inhibition (p ≈ 0.0034–0.0055). In contrast, the Niosomal Frondoside A formulation produced markedly larger inhibition zones of about 27 mm (ZYS-BC-34-2, p = 0.0010) and 29 mm (ZYS-BC-34-1, p = 0.0010), which represents ~ 3-fold increases over PBS. These effects were statistically significant for both isolates and were associated with relatively small error margins (≈ 1–3 mm), indicating high reproducibility of the measurements. In summary, while free Frondoside A showed moderate but statistically significant inhibitory activity, the Niosomal Frondoside A formulation exhibited strong, consistent, and statistically significant antibacterial effects against both P. aeruginosa ZYS-BC-34-2 and P. aeruginosa ZYS-BC-34-1, confirming the enhanced antimicrobial potency provided by the niosomal delivery system (Fig. 4).

Anti-biofilm analysis

As shown in Table 6, both P. aeruginosa isolates (ZYS-BC-34-1 and ZYS-BC-34-2) exhibited strong biofilm formation in the untreated control (PBS), with OD values of 0.245–0.287, which are well above the strong biofilm threshold (OD > 4×ODc). Treatment with free Frondoside A substantially reduced biofilm biomass, producing medium biofilm levels (OD ≈ 0.137–0.141), indicating partial inhibition of matrix formation. Notably, Niosomal Frondoside A showed the most significant anti-biofilm effect, with OD values dropping to 0.058–0.081, which fall within the poor biofilm category, suggesting a strong suppression of biofilm production. These findings demonstrate that encapsulation of Frondoside A into niosomes markedly enhances its anti-biofilm efficacy against P. aeruginosa isolates.

Relative transcription of biofilm genes

Quantitative real-time PCR was conducted to assess the relative expression of GacS and PslA genes in P. aeruginosa strains ZYS-BC-34-1 and ZYS-BC-34-2, using 16S rRNA as the internal control. In P. aeruginosa ZYS-BC-34-1, treatment with Niosomal Frondoside A caused a significant reduction in GacS expression (0.189-fold, p < 0.001) compared with the PBS control (1.038-fold) and free Frondoside A (0.730-fold, p < 0.01). Likewise, PslA expression was markedly suppressed to 0.343-fold (p < 0.001) in the Niosomal Frondoside A group, relative to 0.688-fold in free Frondoside A (p < 0.01) and 1.033-fold in PBS.

Similarly, in P. aeruginosa ZYS-BC-34-2, GacS mRNA levels were significantly downregulated to 0.129-fold following Niosomal Frondoside A treatment (p < 0.001), compared to 0.433-fold for free Frondoside A (p < 0.01) and 1.012-fold for PBS. PslA gene expression also showed a significant decrease, reaching 0.233-fold (p < 0.001) in the Niosomal Frondoside A group, while remaining 0.456-fold (p < 0.01) and 1.002-fold in the free Frondoside A and PBS groups, respectively.

Overall, these findings demonstrate that the niosomal formulation of Frondoside A induces a highly significant suppression of GacS and PslA expression, indicating enhanced inhibition of quorum-sensing and biofilm-related pathways compared with the free compound (Fig. 5).

Discussion

The present study demonstrates that niosomal encapsulation of Frondoside A markedly enhances antibacterial and anti-biofilm activity against two clinical P. aeruginosa isolates (ZYS-BC-34-1 and ZYS-BC-34-2). Encapsulation reduced MIC/MBC values by ~ 4-fold (from 6.25/12.5 mg·mL⁻¹ to 1.56/3.125 mg·mL⁻¹) and produced significantly larger inhibition zones and lower biofilm OD readings compared with free Frondoside A and PBS controls. These improvements align with mechanistic expectations for nanocarrier-mediated drug delivery — improved solubility, sustained release, and enhanced interaction with bacterial membranes — which likely increased local drug concentration at the bacterial/ biofilm interface. Importantly, our qRT-PCR analysis showed robust suppression of key biofilm regulators GacS and PslA (e.g., GacS: 0.1291–0.189-fold; PslA: 0.2327–0.343-fold in niosome-treated groups), indicating that the niosomal formulation not only kills planktonic cells more effectively but also actively downregulates genes central to quorum sensing and EPS production. This dual action (reduced viability + transcriptional repression) strengthens the anti-biofilm claim beyond simple bacteriostatic/bactericidal observations and accords with growing evidence that targeting regulatory circuits yields durable biofilm disruption.

Several recent studies (2020–2024) have reported enhanced antimicrobial performance after nanoscale encapsulation of hydrophobic bioactives, including examples where MICs fell by multiple-fold and biofilm biomass dropped substantially after niosomal or liposomal delivery [12, 13, 15–17]. Our four-fold MIC reduction and strong biofilm suppression are therefore concordant with the direction and magnitude reported in those works, while also extending the literature by demonstrating suppression of GacS/PslA transcription in clinical P. aeruginosa isolates — an endpoint less frequently measured in earlier encapsulation studies.

With respect to Frondoside A specifically, most prior reports have focused on anticancer and anti-inflammatory effects of this sea-cucumber glycoside, with limited direct antimicrobial or anti-biofilm data prior to our work [18–21]. A few emerging reports in the early 2020s began to explore antimicrobial potentials of marine triterpene glycosides, but none combined niosomal encapsulation with transcriptional analysis of canonical P. aeruginosa biofilm regulators. Thus, our study fills an important gap by providing both functional antimicrobial endpoints and mechanistic gene-expression evidence for Frondoside A’s activity after niosomal delivery.

Mechanistically, suppression of GacS is notable because the Gac/Rsm network is a master regulator that positively controls biofilm-promoting factors including Psl production, and conversely can be counteracted by sensor kinases such as RetS to shift cells toward planktonic phenotypes [22–24]. The observed concomitant downregulation of PslA (a key polysaccharide biosynthesis gene) provides a plausible molecular route for the large reductions in biomass measured in the microtiter assay, and is in line with current models were targeting the GacS→Rsm cascade translates to decreased EPS and weaker biofilm architecture. This mechanistic linkage has been emphasized in recent biofilm regulatory studies.

Comparatively, some recent niosome studies reported variable outcomes depending on the payload, surfactant composition, and target organism: while many show improved activity and lower MICs, a minority report decreased activity for certain formulations [25–28]. Our formulation characteristics (small mean diameter ≈ 43–76 nm, negative zeta potential, EE% =78 ± 0.408%) favor both stability and cellular interaction and likely explain the robust improvement observed. The consistency across two clinical isolates further argues that the effect is formulation-driven rather than strain-specific.

The four-fold reduction in the Minimum Inhibitory Concentration (MIC) observed for the niosomal formulation represents a critical advancement in the therapeutic potential of Frondoside A. This substantial enhancement in potency is likely attributable to the nanocarrier’s ability to overcome the inherent physicochemical limitations of the free compound, specifically its poor aqueous solubility and low bioavailability. By encapsulating Frondoside A within a stable vesicular structure, the niosomal system facilitates improved interaction with the bacterial cell membrane and promotes deeper penetration into the biofilm matrix. The concurrent downregulation of GacS and PslA genes provides a molecular basis for this improved efficacy, suggesting that the formulation not only delivers a higher effective dose of the drug but also actively disrupts the quorum-sensing pathways essential for biofilm integrity and resistance. This dual mechanism—enhanced drug delivery coupled with transcriptional suppression of virulence factors—positions the niosomal Frondoside A as a highly promising strategy for combating persistent Pseudomonas aeruginosa infections [27, 29].

Strengths of this study include (i) use of clinically relevant, multidrug resistant isolates with confirmed biofilm capacity; (ii) multiple orthogonal endpoints (MIC/MBC, agar diffusion, microtiter biofilm quantification, FE-SEM/size characterization, and qRT-PCR of GacS/PslA) that together support the central claims; and (iii) careful comparison between free and niosomal Frondoside A under identical experimental conditions. These elements enhance internal validity and translational relevance relative to earlier work that sometimes relied on single assays or laboratory strains only [29–31].

This study is in vitro and lacks in vivo or ex vivo infection models that could confirm efficacy in a complex biological milieu. In addition, while the transcriptional suppression of GacS/PslA is clear, the precise molecular interaction (direct binding vs. upstream signaling perturbation) remains unresolved [32]. Future work will need to evaluate pharmacokinetics, host-toxicity profiles, and whether the formulation affects c-di-GMP levels or other global regulators implicated in biofilm resilience [33, 34]. Several of these follow-up investigations have been recommended in recent reviews of anti-biofilm nanoformulations [29, 32, 33, 35].

Clinically, the data suggest that niosomal Frondoside A has the potential to be developed as a topical or adjunctive therapy for biofilm-associated P. aeruginosa infections (e.g., wound infections, device-associated biofilms), where local high concentrations and biofilm penetration are required [35, 36]. Given the niosomes’ reported biocompatibility and potential for surface modification, translational pathways (including incorporation into coatings or gels) are plausible, though they require formal toxicology and stability testing as emphasized in nanomedicine roadmaps. Recent translational papers support this development trajectory for niosome-encapsulated antimicrobials [35, 37, 38].

Study limitations

The present study is primarily limited by its reliance on in vitro assays to evaluate the antibacterial efficacy of the niosomal formulation. While the results demonstrate significant potential against Pseudomonas aeruginosa, the absence of cytotoxicity testing on mammalian cell lines and hemolysis assays leaves the safety profile and translational feasibility of the formulation unassessed at this stage. Furthermore, the pharmacokinetics and in vivo efficacy of the niosomal Frondoside A were not investigated. Future studies incorporating comprehensive toxicity screenings and animal models are essential to validate the safety and therapeutic potential of this nanocarrier system before clinical application. The zeta potential of -8.6 mV indicates low colloidal stability based on electrostatic repulsion alone, which is a known limitation of niosomal formulations. However, the structural integrity provided by the surfactant bilayer (Span 60 and Tween 60) and cholesterol contributes to physical stability through steric stabilization, partially compensating for the limited electrostatic repulsion. Future studies may consider surface modification (PEGylation or coating with chitosan) to enhance colloidal stability. Nevertheless, the formulation remained physically stable throughout the study period.

Conclusions

In summary, our findings add to and extend the 2020–2025 literature by demonstrating that niosomal encapsulation can substantially potentiate Frondoside A’s antibacterial and anti-biofilm effects and by showing marked suppression of central biofilm genes GacS and PslA in clinical P. aeruginosa isolates. The combination of robust phenotypic endpoints and transcriptional readouts supports the conclusion that niosomal Frondoside A is a promising candidate for further preclinical development. Future directions should prioritize mechanistic dissection, in vivo efficacy, and formulation optimization to support therapeutic translation.
