Failed cochlear implant followed by auditory brainstem implant in a patient with Mondini malformation and uncontrolled otomastoiditis: a case report

Background

Cochlear implant (CI) implantation is the standard intervention for children with severe-to-profound sensorineural hearing loss who do not benefit from hearing aids, including those with congenital inner ear malformations, such as Mondini malformation. Early implantation facilitates speech and language development; however, infection remains a significant complication. Otitis media (OM) is among the most common pediatric diseases worldwide and is classified into acute otitis media (AOM) and chronic otitis media (COM). The global incidence of AOM has been estimated to be 10.8 new episodes per 100 individuals annually, whereas COM—though less frequent at 4.8 new cases per 1000 individuals annually—is associated with greater clinical concern due to its chronicity and sequelae [1]. Preventive strategies, such as administration of the 11-valent pneumococcal conjugate vaccine (PCV) during infancy, have been shown to reduce the risk of moderate-to-severe ear disease in adolescence by 1.2% [2].

Although relatively uncommon, AOM and mastoiditis in CI recipients may progress to chronic otomastoiditis, which can lead to serious outcomes, including meningitis, cholesteatoma, facial nerve palsy, and threatened implant survival [3, 4]. Persistent or uncontrolled infection may necessitate explantation, and in some cases, reimplantation is precluded by labyrinthine ossification (LO). Mondini’s original description highlighted incomplete apical turn partitioning, an enlarged vestibular aqueduct, and a bulbous medial internal auditory canal [5]. While CI implantation can generally be performed safely in these patients—with intraoperative cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) leak as a recognized, but manageable risk—long-term surgical, auditory, and speech outcomes are typically comparable to those observed in children with normal inner ear anatomy. Here, we report a case of Mondini malformation complicated by otomastoiditis following CI implantation that ultimately required CI explantation and transition to auditory brainstem implant (ABI) implantation.

Case presentation

A 17-year-old Thai girl was diagnosed with bilateral profound hearing loss at 5 months of age. There was no reported family history of genetic disorders related to hearing impairment. Imaging revealed bilateral Mondini malformation type II with cochlear nerve hypoplasia in the right ear, and aplasia in the left. Otoscopic examination revealed normal bilateral ear pinnae and external auditory canal with intact tympanic membranes bilaterally. She was initially fitted with hearing aids at 6 months, but subsequently underwent right-sided CI implantation at age 15 months due to poor speech and language development. Postoperatively, her speech and language improved significantly, which facilitated effective verbal communication and good academic performance in mainstream school. At the final postoperative CI evaluation prior to loss to follow-up, the patient’s Categories of Auditory Performance (CAP) score was 8 (Appendix).

The patient was then lost to follow-up for a period of about 5 years. At age 11, her parents observed an absence of auditory responses from their daughter, and bloody otorrhea discharge from her right ear. She returned to the hospital at age 12. Evaluation revealed granulation tissue in the external ear canal with purulent discharge. Temporal bone computed tomography (CT) imaging showed otomastoiditis with cholesterol granuloma. Despite multiple antibiotic regimens and repeated mastoidectomy (Fig. 1) her infection persisted, which necessitated CI explantation. Initially, the electrode array was left in situ in an attempt to preserve cochlear duct patency and prevent LO; however, her infection remained uncontrolled after 4 months of treatment (Fig. 2).

During the early postoperative period following ABI activation, the patient demonstrated partial auditory improvement. She was able to discriminate one-, two-, and three-syllable words; however, sentence comprehension remained dependent on lip reading.

Psychosocially, the patient initially exhibited reduced verbal interaction and a preference for social withdrawal, likely reflecting frustration with limited auditory performance. Her parents also experienced increased stress due to expectations of outcomes comparable to cochlear implantation. Following device adjustment and counseling, both the patient and her family gradually adapted to the new communication limitations, with improved emotional adjustment over time.

Following CI explantation, the patient’s CAP score improved from 0 to 1 after activation of the ABI at 1 month and increased to 2 at the second follow-up. After 1 year of consistent monitoring following ABI implantation, the CAP score reached 4. Audiological assessments revealed aided thresholds of 45–55 decibels (dB) (Fig. 6). However, device mapping was limited by the need to maximize current levels across all channels, which prevented the achievement of optimal listening comfort.

Owing to the use of maximum current levels, the patient has been followed up annually to monitor her long-term progress. At 3-year follow-up after ABI, the patient demonstrated stable functional use of auditory input in combination with visual cues, and was able to pursue higher education, indicating sustained real-world benefit despite limited speech perception. However, detailed longitudinal quantitative audiological data beyond the third year were not available.

Discussion

CI implantation is generally regarded as a safe and effective intervention, though complications that are broadly categorized as minor or major may occur. Minor complications, which are typically managed conservatively, include otitis media, wound infection, vertigo, tinnitus, facial nerve stimulation, hematoma, and transient chorda tympani injury. Major complications, which often require surgical intervention or may result in long-term sequelae, include flap dehiscence or infection, device failure or migration, mastoiditis, and electrode-related problems, such as damage or misplacement [6].

Otitis media (OM) is one of the most frequent complications following CI, particularly in young children. The reported incidence of acute OM (AOM) and mastoiditis in pediatric CI recipients is 5.5% and 1–2%, respectively, with lower rates in adults [6]. Infection can spread through the round or oval window, vascular channels, or surgical defects. A history of AOM prior to CI implantation may increase the risk of post-CI infection, although many affected children have no such history. Interestingly, some studies reported a reduced incidence of AOM after CI implantation, possibly reflecting preoperative infection control, age-related reduction, intraoperative prophylactic antibiotics, and the protective effects of mastoidectomy [7, 8].

If inadequately treated, AOM alone and mastoiditis alone can both progress to LO. This pathological process progresses from inflammation to fibrosis to abnormal bone formation, and most commonly affects the basal turn of the cochlea and the lateral semicircular canal adjacent to the mastoid cavity [9, 10]. Uncontrolled infection may necessitate CI explantation, due either to device failure or medical complications. Reported CI implantation failure rates range from 1.5 to 15%, with failures classified as hard (defined as confirmed functional loss) or soft (defined as the presence of nonspecific symptoms, such as tinnitus or vertigo) [11].

Alsalhi et al. [9] reported CI implantation failure due to LO in Mondini malformation, with potential mechanisms ranging from traumatic electrode insertion to post-implantation meningitis or autoimmune processes. However and in contrast, chronic and uncontrolled otomastoiditis appears to be the precipitating factor for LO in a malformed cochlea in the present case.

Management of mastoiditis in CI recipients is particularly challenging since no standardized protocols currently exist. Although most cases arise within the first 2 years after CI implantation, onset has been documented as late as 58 months [10]. Prompt recognition and aggressive management are essential since delayed intervention may result in meningitis or CI failure. Approximately half of patients respond to a prolonged course of intravenous antibiotics, which is recommended to be at least 7 days followed by extended 3-week oral therapy, while others ultimately require surgical intervention [10]. In our patient—despite intensive medical and surgical management, her infection persisted and CI explantation was indicated. Moreover, the prolonged infection-free interval before reimplantation likely contributed to the development of LO, which has been reported to develop as early as 3 weeks following infection onset [12].

Radiologically, the progressive obliteration of the cochlear lumen observed on CT imaging is consistent with labyrinthine ossification secondary to chronic inflammation. This process likely impeded electrode reinsertion by physically obstructing the scala tympani, highlighting the importance of early infection control and timely surgical decision-making to preserve cochlear patency.

When CI reimplantation is no longer feasible, auditory brainstem implant (ABI) implantation serves as a salvage treatment option. Compared with previously reported pediatric ABI outcomes, particularly in children with inner ear malformations such as those described by Sennaroglu et al., many patients can achieve CAP scores of 5 or higher, corresponding to understanding of common phrases without lip reading [13]. In contrast, our patient achieved a CAP score of 4 and remained dependent on visual cues for sentence comprehension, indicating a relatively lower level of auditory performance. This discrepancy is likely attributable to delayed ABI implantation in the present case, which was performed as a salvage procedure following prolonged infection, cochlear implant failure, and labyrinthine ossification, rather than as a primary auditory rehabilitation strategy. Nevertheless, our result remains consistent with the literature in demonstrating that ABI provides a limited but clinically acceptable auditory benefit, enabling detection of syllabic differences, although speech perception remained limited. Device mapping was constrained by the need for high current levels across all channels without achieving optimal listening comfort, resulting in aided thresholds ranging from 40 to 65 dB. Consequently, the patient’s auditory perception remains suboptimal, and she continues to rely heavily on visual cues for communication.

In this case, psychosocial impact was assessed only through clinical observation and family report, without the use of standardized questionnaires. Validated instruments could provide a more objective evaluation of emotional and social outcomes, and should be considered in future studies. This is because CI and ABI implantation both present significant psychosocial challenges for patients and their families. Children with hearing loss are at substantially increased risk of psychological disorders compared to their normal-hearing peers, with prevalence rates up to five times higher [10]. Device adaptation requires sustained family support, ongoing rehabilitation, and patient and family acceptance of potential complications and limitations.

Conclusion

This case illustrates the severe consequences of post-CI infections, particularly in children with inner ear malformations. Chronic otomastoiditis may cause CI failure and irreversible cochlear ossification, leaving ABI implantation as the only viable treatment option. While ABI yields meaningful auditory awareness, the speech outcomes are inferior to those conferred by CI. Early infection recognition, aggressive infection management, and close follow-up are essential for preserving CI function and for optimizing outcomes in this vulnerable pediatric population.
