KLAP: KYP lemma based low-rank approximation for ℋ 2 \mathcal{H}_{2} -optimal passivation
Jonas Nicodemus ⋆ and Matthias Voigt ‡ and Serkan Gugercin † and Benjamin Unger §

Abstract
. We present a novel passivity enforcement (passivation) method, called KLAP, for linear time-invariant systems based on the Kalman-Yakubovich-Popov (KYP) lemma and the closely related Lur’e equations. The passivation problem in our framework corresponds to finding a perturbation to a given non-passive system that renders the system passive while minimizing the ℋ 2 \mathcal{H}_{2} or frequency-weighted ℋ 2 \mathcal{H}_{2} distance between the original non-passive and the resulting passive system. We show that this problem can be formulated as an unconstrained optimization problem whose objective function can be differentiated efficiently even in large-scale settings. We show that any minimizer of the unconstrained problem yields the same passive system. Furthermore, we prove that, in the absence of a feedthrough term, every local minimizer is also a global minimizer. For cases involving a non-trivial feedthrough term, we analyze global minimizers in relation to the extremal solutions of the Lur’e equations, which can serve as tools for identifying local minima. To solve the resulting numerical optimization problem efficiently, we propose an initialization strategy based on modifying the feedthrough term and a restart strategy when it is likely that the optimization has converged to a non-global local minimum. Numerical examples illustrate the effectiveness of the proposed method.

LTI systems, passivity, passivation, KYP lemma,

-optimization, gradient-based optimization

1.1.
Literature review and state-of-the-art

Key foundational work on dissipative systems can be traced back to Willems
[
56
,
57
]
, who introduced a formal framework for passive systems and their relation to Lyapunov stability. Willems defined dissipative systems in terms of a supply rate and a storage function.
A comprehensive treatment of passivity and
L_{2}
gain methods is given in
[
52
]
, establishing links between dissipativity and control design. A recent overview of dissipativity theory is given in
[
11
]
, including a detailed chapter about the
KYP
lemma and the Lur’e equations
[
11
, Cha. 3]
.
A survey of passivation methods is given in
[
31
,
30
]
; see also
[
32
]
for a comparative study of the methods. Passivation methods can be divided into three categories:
Methods in the first category such as
[
30
, Sec. 10.7]
or
[
22
]
are based on the
KYP
lemma and result in a constrained optimization problem, where, usually, the goal is to find the smallest perturbation on the output matrix in a weighted norm such that a solution to the
KYP
inequality exists. The problem can be formulated as a standard
LMI
and is known to be convex. Unfortunately, the computational cost scales poorly with the system size due to the presence of a Lyapunov matrix (in our manuscript
X
in (
2.2
)), which is instrumental for the
KYP
lemma. The trace parameterization methods
[
21
,
17
]
try to overcome this issue by eliminating the Lyapunov matrix from the optimization problem.
However, this approach first requires solving many Lyapunov equations and storing those solutions.
KLAP
is similar to the trace parametrization methods but bypasses the need for this initial stage of solving many Lyapunov equations.
Instead, only two Lyapunov equations are solved in each iteration of the optimization procedure.
Methods of the second category are based on the characterization of passivity in terms of the spectrum of a Hamiltonian matrix, see
[
29
,
28
]
. The idea consists of iteratively perturbing the Hamiltonian matrix until the perturbed Hamiltonian matrix has no imaginary eigenvalues, indicating passivity of the perturbed model.
However, this method has no convergence guarantees. Moreover, imaginary eigenvalues may be reintroduced during the iteration and thus, it may take many iterations to remove all imaginary eigenvalues, making the method potentially inefficient. Various improvements of this approach were considered, e.g., the use of structure-preserving methods for the Hamiltonian eigenvalue problem
[
48
]
or the extension to wider problem classes such a descriptor systems with general supply rates
[
13
]
.
A related approach to finding the closest passive system is devised in
[
23
]
. Therein, a low-rank
ODE
is proposed to find an optimal low-rank perturbation in the sense of a weighted Frobenius norm of the difference of the output matrices.
Finally, the methods of the third category are based on passivity enforcement at discrete frequencies via linear or quadratic programming
[
46
,
47
,
35
]
.
Consequently, methods from this category only guarantee passivity in a certain frequency range.
Instead of finding the nearest passive system, one may also look for the nearest port-Hamiltonian system. This problem is studied in
[
27
]
. There, the authors consider the Frobenius norm of the difference of the system matrices as a perturbation measure. However, this measure is not invariant under state-space transformations and depends on the particular realization under consideration.

Recall the passivation problem defined in (

For an asymptotically stable dynamical system (

\left\|G\right\|_{\mathcal{H}_{2}}^{2}\vcentcolon=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left\|G(\mathrm{i}\omega)\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\,\,\mathrm{d}\omega.

Note that

(3.1)

\displaystyle\left\|G-\widehat{G}\big(\cdot;\widehat{C}\big)\right\|_{\mathcal{H}_{2}}^{2}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left\|G(\mathrm{i}\omega)-\widehat{G}\big(\mathrm{i}\omega;\widehat{C}\big)\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\,\,\mathrm{d}\omega.

This distance measure implies that the passivated model
\widehat{\Sigma}(\widehat{C})
has the same feedthrough term

Therefore, given the asymptotically stable system

in (
1.1

in (

(3.2)

\min_{\widehat{C}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}}\big\|G-\widehat{G}\big(\cdot,\widehat{C}\big)\big\|_{\mathcal{H}_{2}}\qquad\text{such that}\qquad\widehat{\Sigma}(\widehat{C})\text{ is passive.}

For the forthcoming analysis, we define the feasible set as

(3.3)

\widehat{\mathcal{C}}\vcentcolon=\left\{\widehat{C}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}\;\Big|\;\widehat{\Sigma}\big(\widehat{C}\big)\text{ is passive}\right\}.

In the passivation approach we will develop, it is in principle possible to consider the case that the matrix

y(t)=\widehat{C}(L)x(t)+Du(t),\end{cases}

where

(3.5)

\widehat{C}(L)\vcentcolon=B^{\mathsf{T}}\mathcal{L}^{-1}\big(-LL^{\mathsf{T}}\big)+ML^{\mathsf{T}}

and
\mathcal{L}
is the bijective Lyapunov operator defined in (
2.5
). By construction, (
3.4
) is guaranteed to be passive for any
L\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
; see
Proposition

2.4
. The associated transfer function is given by

(3.6)

\widehat{G}(s;L)\vcentcolon=\widehat{C}(L)(sI_{n}-A)^{-1}B+D.

Note that we employ a slight abuse of notation here, as we denote the passivated system, the passivated transfer function, and the passivating output matrix depending on
L
using the same symbols as the quantities depending on
\widehat{C}
in (
1.2
) and (
1.4
). However, we will explicitly write the dependence in the following to prevent confusion.

The constrained optimization problem (
3.2
) can be equivalently formulated as the unconstrained minimization problem

(3.7)

\min_{L\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}}\mathcal{J}(L)\qquad\text{with}\qquad\mathcal{J}(L)\vcentcolon=\big\|G-\widehat{G}(\cdot;L)\big\|_{\mathcal{H}_{2}}^{2}.

Before establishing existence and uniqueness of the solution of (
3.7
), we first present an alternative description of the objective functional and its gradient, which we will later use for our numerical algorithm.

Theorem 3.2
.

Assume that
\Sigma
in (
1.1
) is asymptotically stable and let
\mathcal{P}\in\mathcal{S}^{n}_{\succeq}
denote the controllability Gramian of (
1.1
). Then
\mathcal{J}
defined in (
3.7
) is
differentiable, and

(3.8a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}(L)

\displaystyle=\tr\left(\big(C-\widehat{C}(L)\big)\mathcal{P}\big(C^{\mathsf{T}}-\widehat{C}(L)^{\mathsf{T}}\big)\right),

(3.8b)

\displaystyle\nabla\mathcal{J}(L)

\displaystyle=2\mathcal{X}L-2\mathcal{P}\big(C^{\mathsf{T}}-\widehat{C}(L)^{\mathsf{T}}\big)M,

where
\mathcal{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is the unique solution of the Lyapunov equation

(3.9)

A\mathcal{X}+\mathcal{X}A^{\mathsf{T}}-\mathcal{P}\big(C^{\mathsf{T}}-\widehat{C}(L)^{\mathsf{T}}\big)B^{\mathsf{T}}-B\big(C-\widehat{C}(L)\big)\mathcal{P}=0.

Before proving
Theorem

3.2
, we first recall the following auxiliary result to relate the inner products of solutions of Lyapunov equations.

Lemma 3.3

(​​
[
58
, Lem. A.1]
)
.

Let
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be Hurwitz and
D,\,F\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be such that the matrices
Y,\,Z\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
solve the Lyapunov equations

\displaystyle AY+YA^{\mathsf{T}}+D=0\qquad\text{and}\qquad A^{\mathsf{T}}Z+ZA+F=0.

Then,
\tr\big(D^{\mathsf{T}}Z\big)=\tr\big(F^{\mathsf{T}}Y\big)
.

Proof of
Theorem

3.2
.

Through direct algebraic manipulation, it can be verified that the controllability Gramian
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{e}}
of the error system
\Sigma_{\mathrm{e}}(L)=(A_{\mathrm{e}},B_{\mathrm{e}},C_{\mathrm{e}}(L),0)
defined via

\displaystyle A_{\mathrm{e}}

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\begin{bmatrix}A&0\\
0&A\end{bmatrix},

\displaystyle B_{\mathrm{e}}

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\begin{bmatrix}B\\
B\end{bmatrix},

\displaystyle C_{\mathrm{e}}(L)

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\begin{bmatrix}C&-\widehat{C}(L)\end{bmatrix}

is given as
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{e}}=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}\mathcal{P}&\mathcal{P}\\
\mathcal{P}&\mathcal{P}\end{smallmatrix}\right]
. Hence, using standard results on the
\mathcal{H}_{2}
-norm, see, e.g.,
[
3
,
53
]
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}(L)

\displaystyle=\tr\left(C_{\mathrm{e}}(L)\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{e}}C_{\mathrm{e}}(L)^{\mathsf{T}}\right)=\tr\left(\left(C-\widehat{C}(L)\right)\mathcal{P}\left(C^{\mathsf{T}}-\widehat{C}(L)^{\mathsf{T}}\right)\right),

which is the first statement.

Inserting (
2.6a
) and setting
X\vcentcolon=\mathcal{L}^{-1}\big(-LL^{\mathsf{T}}\big)
yields

\mathcal{J}(L)=\tr\big(C\mathcal{P}C^{\mathsf{T}}\big)-2\tr\big(B^{\mathsf{T}}X\mathcal{P}C^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}+ML^{\mathsf{T}}\mathcal{P}C^{\mathsf{T}}\big)\\
+\tr\big(B^{\mathsf{T}}X\mathcal{P}XB+2B^{\mathsf{T}}X\mathcal{P}LM^{\mathsf{T}}+ML^{\mathsf{T}}\mathcal{P}LM^{\mathsf{T}}\big).

We calculate the Fréchet derivative of
\mathcal{J}
, which is the usual derivative for finite-dimensional spaces. To this end we introduce a perturbation
\Delta_{L}
for
L
, which invokes the symmetric perturbation
\Delta_{X}\vcentcolon=\mathcal{L}^{-1}(-(L+\Delta_{L})(L+\Delta_{L})^{\mathsf{T}})-\mathcal{L}^{-1}(-LL^{\mathsf{T}})
for
X
. Using the cyclic property of the trace, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}(L+\Delta_{L})-\mathcal{J}(L)

\displaystyle=-2\tr\big(\mathcal{P}C^{\mathsf{T}}B^{\mathsf{T}}\Delta_{X}\big)-2\tr\big(M^{\mathsf{T}}C\mathcal{P}\Delta_{L}\big)

\displaystyle+2\tr\big(BB^{\mathsf{T}}X\mathcal{P}\Delta_{X}\big)+\mathcal{O}\big({\|\Delta_{X}\|}_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\big)

\displaystyle+2\tr\big(M^{\mathsf{T}}B^{\mathsf{T}}X\mathcal{P}\Delta_{L}\big)+2\tr\big(\mathcal{P}LM^{\mathsf{T}}B^{\mathsf{T}}\Delta_{X}\big)+\mathcal{O}\big({\|\Delta_{X}\|}_{\mathrm{F}}{\|\Delta_{L}\|}_{\mathrm{F}}\big)

\displaystyle+2\tr\big(M^{\mathsf{T}}ML^{\mathsf{T}}\mathcal{P}\Delta_{L}\big)+\mathcal{O}\big({\|\Delta_{L}\|}_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\big).

The definition of
\Delta_{X}
is equivalent to

\displaystyle A^{\mathsf{T}}\Delta_{X}+\Delta_{X}A+\big(\Delta_{L}L^{\mathsf{T}}+L\Delta_{L}^{\mathsf{T}}+\Delta_{L}\Delta_{L}^{\mathsf{T}}\big)=0.

Hence, applying
Lemma

3.3
together with the additional Lyapunov equations

\displaystyle AY+YA^{\mathsf{T}}+BC\mathcal{P}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle AZ+ZA^{\mathsf{T}}+\mathcal{P}XBB^{\mathsf{T}}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle AW+WA^{\mathsf{T}}+BML^{\mathsf{T}}\mathcal{P}

\displaystyle=0

yields

\displaystyle\tr\big(\mathcal{P}C^{\mathsf{T}}B^{\mathsf{T}}\Delta_{X}\big)

\displaystyle=\tr\big(\big(\Delta_{L}L^{\mathsf{T}}+L\Delta_{L}^{\mathsf{T}}+\Delta_{L}\Delta_{L}^{\mathsf{T}}\big)Y\big)=\tr\big(L^{\mathsf{T}}\big(Y+Y^{\mathsf{T}}\big)\Delta_{L}\big)+\mathcal{O}\big({\|\Delta_{L}\|}_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\big),

\displaystyle\tr\big(BB^{\mathsf{T}}X\mathcal{P}\Delta_{X}\big)

\displaystyle=\tr\big(\big(\Delta_{L}L^{\mathsf{T}}+L\Delta_{L}^{\mathsf{T}}+\Delta_{L}\Delta_{L}^{\mathsf{T}}\big)Z\big)=\tr\big(L^{\mathsf{T}}\big(Z+Z^{\mathsf{T}}\big)\Delta_{L}\big)+\mathcal{O}\big({\|\Delta_{L}\|}_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\big),

\displaystyle\tr\big(\mathcal{P}LM^{\mathsf{T}}B^{\mathsf{T}}\Delta_{X}\big)

\displaystyle=\tr\big(\big(\Delta_{L}L^{\mathsf{T}}+L\Delta_{L}^{\mathsf{T}}+\Delta_{L}\Delta_{L}^{\mathsf{T}}\big)W\big)=\tr\big(L^{\mathsf{T}}\big(W+W^{\mathsf{T}}\big)\Delta_{L}\big)+\mathcal{O}\big({\|\Delta_{L}\|}_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\big).

Setting
\mathcal{X}\vcentcolon=-\big(Y+Y^{\mathsf{T}}\big)+\big(Z+Z^{\mathsf{T}}\big)+\big(W+W^{\mathsf{T}}\big)
and using (
2.6a
), we obtain

A\mathcal{X}+\mathcal{X}A^{\mathsf{T}}=\big(BC\mathcal{P}-\mathcal{P}XBB^{\mathsf{T}}-BML^{\mathsf{T}}\mathcal{P}\big)+\big(BC\mathcal{P}-\mathcal{P}XBB^{\mathsf{T}}-BML^{\mathsf{T}}\mathcal{P}\big)^{\mathsf{T}}\\
=\mathcal{P}\big(C^{\mathsf{T}}-\widehat{C}(L)^{\mathsf{T}}\big)B^{\mathsf{T}}+B\big(C-\widehat{C}(L)\big)\mathcal{P}.

This shows that
\mathcal{X}
solves the Lyapunov equation (
3.9
).
Plugging these identities back into the expression for
\mathcal{J}(L+\Delta_{L})-\mathcal{J}(L)
above yields

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}(L+\Delta_{L})-\mathcal{J}(L)

\displaystyle=-2\tr\big(L^{\mathsf{T}}\big(Y+Y^{\mathsf{T}}\big)\Delta_{L}\big)-2\tr\big(M^{\mathsf{T}}C\mathcal{P}\Delta_{L}\big)+2\tr\big(L^{\mathsf{T}}\big(Z+Z^{\mathsf{T}}\big)\Delta_{L}\big)

\displaystyle\qquad+2\tr\big(M^{\mathsf{T}}B^{\mathsf{T}}X\mathcal{P}\Delta_{L}\big)+2\tr\big(L^{\mathsf{T}}\big(W+W^{\mathsf{T}}\big)\Delta_{L}\big)

\displaystyle\qquad+2\tr\big(M^{\mathsf{T}}ML^{\mathsf{T}}\mathcal{P}\Delta_{L}\big)+\mathcal{O}\left(\big({\|\Delta_{X}\|}_{\mathrm{F}}+{\|\Delta_{L}\|}_{\mathrm{F}}\big)^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=2\tr\big(\big(L^{\mathsf{T}}\mathcal{X}-M^{\mathsf{T}}C\mathcal{P}+M^{\mathsf{T}}B^{\mathsf{T}}X\mathcal{P}+M^{\mathsf{T}}ML^{\mathsf{T}}\mathcal{P}\big)\Delta_{L}\big)

\displaystyle\qquad+\mathcal{O}\left(\big({\|\Delta_{X}\|}_{\mathrm{F}}+{\|\Delta_{L}\|}_{\mathrm{F}}\big)^{2}\right).

Since
\Delta_{L}\to 0
implies
\Delta_{X}\to 0
, and

\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}\to\mathbb{R},\qquad\Delta_{L}\mapsto\tr\big(\big(L^{\mathsf{T}}\mathcal{X}-M^{\mathsf{T}}C\mathcal{P}+M^{\mathsf{T}}B^{\mathsf{T}}X\mathcal{P}+M^{\mathsf{T}}ML^{\mathsf{T}}\mathcal{P}\big)\Delta_{L}\big)

is a bounded linear operator, we can conclude Fréchet differentiability of
\mathcal{J}
.
Moreover, using again (
2.6a
), we conclude that the gradient of
\mathcal{J}
is given by (
3.8b
).
∎

Theorem 3.4
.

Consider system (
1.1
) and assume it is asymptotically stable and controllable. Furthermore, let
D+D^{\mathsf{T}}\in\mathcal{S}^{m}_{\succeq}
. Then, the minimization problem (
3.7
) is solvable and for any minimizers
L_{1},L_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
, we have
\widehat{C}(L_{1})=\widehat{C}(L_{2})
, i.e., the minimizing passive system is unique.

Proof.

To prove the claim, we consider the constrained optimization problem (
3.2
) and use (
3.8a
), (
1.5
), and (
3.3
) to rewrite it as

(3.10)

\min_{\widehat{C}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}}{\|C-\widehat{C}\|}_{\mathcal{P}}\qquad\text{such that }\widehat{C}\in\widehat{\mathcal{C}}.

Due to
Theorem

2.3
we observe that
\widehat{C}\in\widehat{\mathcal{C}}
if and only if there exists
X\in\mathcal{S}^{r}_{\succeq}
such that the
KYP
inequality (
2.2
) is satisfied. As the mapping

\displaystyle(\widehat{C},X)\mapsto\begin{bmatrix}-A^{\mathsf{T}}X-XA&\widehat{C}^{\mathsf{T}}-XB\\
\widehat{C}-B^{\mathsf{T}}X&D+D^{\mathsf{T}}\end{bmatrix}

is continuous, we note that
\widehat{\mathcal{C}}
is closed. Moreover, direct verification demonstrates that
\widehat{\mathcal{C}}
is convex and non-empty (since
0\in\widehat{\mathcal{C}}
). Hence, the optimization problem (
3.10
) is uniquely solvable due to the Hilbert projection theorem
[
45
, Thm. 4.10]
.
The result now follows from
Proposition

2.4
.
∎

Remark 3.5
.

The constrained optimization problem (
3.10
) is considered in the literature, albeit in a slightly reformulated way; see for instance
[
31
, Sec. 5.4.1]
. Specifically, the matrix
\widehat{C}
is typically considered as a perturbed variant of the original matrix
C
, i.e.,
\widehat{C}=C+\Delta P^{-1}
for some
\Delta\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
, where
P
is a Cholesky factor of the controllability Gramian, i.e.,
\mathcal{P}=PP^{\mathsf{T}}
. With this change of coordinates, we obtain
\|C-\widehat{C}\|_{\mathcal{P}}=\|\Delta\|_{\mathrm{F}}

and hence simply minimize the (weighted) perturbation in the Frobenius norm.

Remark 3.6
.

We can define a new objective functional and its associated gradient by replacing the controllability Gramian with any symmetric positive semi-definite matrix
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{w}}
, for instance, given by the frequency-limited or time-limited controllability Gramian; cf.
[
26
,
7
]
. We could also consider another dynamical system

W(s)=C_{\mathrm{w}}(sI-A_{\mathrm{w}})^{-1}B_{\mathrm{w}}+D_{\mathrm{w}}
as a weight, where
A_{\mathrm{w}}
is Hurwitz. Then we can define the weighted
\mathcal{H}_{2}
error

\displaystyle\big\|G-\widehat{G}(\cdot;L)\big\|_{W}^{2}\vcentcolon=\big\|\big(G-\widehat{G}(\cdot;L)\big)W\big\|_{\mathcal{H}_{2}}^{2}=\big\|C-\widehat{C}(L)\big\|_{\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{w}}}^{2}

with weighted controllability Gramian

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}A&BC_{\mathrm{w}}\\
0&A_{\mathrm{w}}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{w}}&\mathcal{P}_{1}\\
\mathcal{P}_{1}^{\mathsf{T}}&\mathcal{P}_{2}\end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{w}}&\mathcal{P}_{1}\\
\mathcal{P}_{1}^{\mathsf{T}}&\mathcal{P}_{2}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}A^{\mathsf{T}}&0\\
C_{\mathrm{w}}^{\mathsf{T}}B^{\mathsf{T}}&A_{\mathrm{w}}^{\mathsf{T}}\end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}BD_{\mathrm{w}}\\
B_{\mathrm{w}}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}BD_{\mathrm{w}}\\

We refer to
[

for further details.

3.2.
Numerical considerations

Suppose we use a gradient-based optimization strategy to solve the minimization problem (
3.7
). In that case, we need to solve two Lyapunov equations in each optimization step: the first one defined by (
2.4a
) related to (
3.5
), and the second by (
3.9
). Solving these Lyapunov equations is thus the main computational cost. We refer the reader to
[
50
,
8
]
for an overview of state-of-the-art methods for solving Lyapunov equations. Here, for the Lyapunov equation
A^{\mathsf{H}}X+XA=W
, we distinguish three scenarios
4
4

4

Here, we write the Lyapunov equation for complex matrices, since the diagonalizing transformation we consider may result in a complex matrix
A
.
.
First, the matrix
A
is diagonal, say
A=\diag(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n})
. This can be achieved, e.g., if the model is obtained from data
and such a diagonal form is enforced during the data-driven modeling stage,
or if the model is diagonalizable and the transformation to diagonal form is done before the passivation. In this case, a closed-form solution is given by

x_{ij}=\frac{w_{ij}}{\overline{\lambda_{i}}+\lambda_{j}},

where
w_{ij}
corresponds to the
(i,j)
entry of the right-hand side
W
, giving a computational cost of
\mathcal{O}\big(n^{2}\big)
for solving the Lyapunov equation. If
A
is not in diagonal form or the transformation to diagonal form is not possible, numerically not stable, or computationally infeasible, then we can either use a Krylov subspace method or an ADI method if
n
is large and
A
is sparse, or the Bartels-Stewart algorithm or Hammarling’s method if
n
is small or moderate and
A
is dense. The latter two methods require
\mathcal{O}(n^{3})
floating point operations, while the former two are iterative methods that are typically much more efficient in the large and sparse setting. A detailed complexity analysis is available, e.g., in
[
38
]
.
Notably, the Bartels-Stewart algorithm first performs a Schur decomposition of
A^{\mathsf{H}}
. To avoid recomputing the Schur decomposition, we only compute it once and then transform the system accordingly. Whenever numerically reasonable, we recommend transforming the system to diagonal form, as this reduces the overall computational cost significantly; see
Section

4.3
. If we want to retain real-valued matrices throughout the optimization, it is advisable to transform them into real-diagonal form.

3.3.
Local minimizers and uniqueness

In the proof of
Theorem

3.4
, we have seen that the constrained passivation problem (
3.10
) is convex, and hence any local minimizer will be a global minimizer. Unfortunately, the transformation from the constrained optimization problem (
3.10
) to the unconstrained optimization problem (
3.7
) results in a non-convex minimization problem. Hence, if we apply gradient-based algorithms to solve the unconstrained minimization problem (
3.7
), we must investigate whether we have found the global minimum or are stuck in a non-global local minimum.
Recall from (
2.6b
) the definition of
M
:
D+D^{\mathsf{T}}=MM^{\mathsf{T}}
. As we will see, the situation is quite different for the cases
M=0
and
M\neq 0
, so we analyze the two scenarios separately.

3.3.1.
Case
M=0

Albeit the resulting passive system is unique, the minimizer of (
3.7
) may not be unique. This can be easily seen in the case
M=0
. In this case, if
L^{\star}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
is a minimizer, so is
L^{\star}U
for any orthogonal matrix
U\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
.

Proposition 3.7
.

Assume
D+D^{\mathsf{T}}=0
. Then any local minimizer of (

) is a global minimizer.

Proof.

We note that for
D+D^{\mathsf{T}}=0
, the objective functional (
3.8a
) and
\widehat{C}
from (
3.5
) only depend on
LL^{\mathsf{T}}
, not on
L
.
Let
\check{L}
be a local minimizer of (
3.7
), i.e., there exists
\delta>0
such that

\mathcal{J}\big(\check{L}\big)\leq\mathcal{J}\big(L\big)\qquad\text{for all $LL^{\mathsf{T}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}$ with $\big\|LL^{\mathsf{T}}-\check{L}\check{L}^{\mathsf{T}}\big\|\leq\delta$}.

Further, assume that
\check{L}
is not globally optimal and let
L^{\star}
be a global minimizer. We define

(3.11)

L_{\theta}L_{\theta}^{\mathsf{T}}\vcentcolon=(1-\theta)\check{L}\check{L}^{\mathsf{T}}+\theta L^{\star}(L^{\star})^{\mathsf{T}},\qquad\theta\in(0,1).

Let
\theta
be such that
\big\|L_{\theta}L_{\theta}^{\mathsf{T}}-\check{L}\check{L}^{\mathsf{T}}\big\|\leq\delta
.
Then, using

\widehat{C}\big(L_{\theta}\big)=(1-\theta)\widehat{C}\big(\check{L}\big)+\theta\widehat{C}\big(L^{\star}\big)
,
we obtain

\displaystyle\big\|C-\widehat{C}(L_{\theta})\big\|_{\mathcal{P}}

\displaystyle\leq(1-\theta)\big\|C-\widehat{C}\big(\check{L}\big)\big\|_{\mathcal{P}}+\theta\big\|C-\widehat{C}\big(L^{\star}\big)\big\|_{\mathcal{P}}

\displaystyle<\big\|C-\widehat{C}\big(\check{L}\big)\big\|_{\mathcal{P}},

has to be a global optimizer.

Theorem 3.10
.

Assume

. Let

\widehat{C}

\widehat{\mathcal{C}}
defined in (

).

Proof.

To simplify the notation in the proof, we introduce the matrix function

(3.12)

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}(C,X)\vcentcolon=\begin{bmatrix}-A^{\mathsf{T}}X-XA&C^{\mathsf{T}}-XB\\
C-B^{\mathsf{T}}X&D+D^{\mathsf{T}}\end{bmatrix}.

We assume that
\widehat{C}
does not lie on the boundary. Then there exist
\Delta\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}\setminus\{0\}
and
\varepsilon>0

\widehat{\Sigma}\big(\widehat{C}+\alpha\Delta\big)
is passive for all
\alpha\in(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)
. We will use this fact to prove the result via contradiction.
Let
\Delta_{\widehat{C}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}\setminus\{0\}

\widehat{\Sigma}\big(\widehat{C}+\Delta_{\widehat{C}}\big)

In particular, for

there exists symmetric
\Delta_{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
such that

(3.13)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\preceq\mathcal{W}\big(\widehat{C}+\Delta_{\widehat{C}},X+\Delta_{X}\big)

(3.14)

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}-A^{\mathsf{T}}(X+\Delta_{X})-(X+\Delta_{X})A&\big(\widehat{C}+\Delta_{\widehat{C}}\big)^{\mathsf{T}}-(X+\Delta_{X})B\\
\big(\widehat{C}+\Delta_{\widehat{C}}\big)-B^{\mathsf{T}}(X+\Delta_{X})&D+D^{\mathsf{T}}\end{bmatrix}.

We define
\widehat{C}_{\alpha}\vcentcolon=\widehat{C}+\alpha\Delta_{\widehat{C}}
and
X_{\alpha}\vcentcolon=X+\alpha\Delta_{X}
.
Due to convexity of (
3.12
), we immediately conclude
0\preceq\mathcal{W}\big(\widehat{C}_{\alpha},X_{\alpha}\big)
for all
\alpha\in[0,1]
.
We now prove that

\displaystyle 0\not\preceq\mathcal{W}\big(\widehat{C}_{\alpha},X_{\alpha}\big)\qquad\text{for any $\alpha<0$}.

Since we do not rely on a specific choice of
\Delta_{X}
,
we conclude that
\widehat{\Sigma}\big(\widehat{C}_{\alpha}\big)
cannot be passive for
\alpha<0
.
We investigate two cases:

Case 1:

Assume that
\Delta_{\widehat{C}}-B^{\mathsf{T}}\Delta_{X}=0
. Using the Schur complement and the fact that
X
solves the Riccati equation (
2.3
), we conclude that
0\preceq\mathcal{W}(\widehat{C}_{\alpha},X_{\alpha})
is satisfied if and only if
0\preceq-A^{\mathsf{T}}X_{\alpha}-X_{\alpha}A
and

\displaystyle 0\preceq-\alpha(A^{\mathsf{T}}\Delta_{X}+\Delta_{X}A).

Since this holds for
\alpha\in[0,1]
, we immediately conclude that this inequality cannot hold for
\alpha<0
.

Case 2:

Assume
\Delta_{\widehat{C}}-B^{\mathsf{T}}\Delta_{X}\neq 0
. Then there exists
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that

\displaystyle y^{\mathsf{T}}(\Delta_{\widehat{C}}^{\mathsf{T}}-\Delta_{X}B)(D+D^{\mathsf{T}})^{-1}(\Delta_{\widehat{C}}-B^{\mathsf{T}}\Delta_{X})y=1.

Set
\beta\vcentcolon=y^{\mathsf{T}}\left(A^{\mathsf{T}}\Delta_{X}+\Delta_{X}A+2\big(\Delta_{\widehat{C}}^{\mathsf{T}}-\Delta_{X}B)(D+D^{\mathsf{T}})^{-1}(\widehat{C}-B^{\mathsf{T}}X)\right)y
. Using once again the Schur complement and the fact that
X
solves the Riccati equation (
2.3
), we estimate

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\leq y^{\mathsf{T}}\left(-A^{\mathsf{T}}X_{\alpha}-X_{\alpha}A-\big(C_{\alpha}^{\mathsf{T}}-X_{\alpha}B\big)\big(D+D^{\mathsf{T}}\big)^{-1}\big(C_{\alpha}-B^{\mathsf{T}}X_{\alpha}\big)\right)y

\displaystyle=y^{\mathsf{T}}\left(-\alpha A^{\mathsf{T}}\Delta_{X}-\alpha\Delta_{X}A-2\alpha\big(\Delta_{\widehat{C}}^{\mathsf{T}}-\Delta_{X}B)(D+D^{\mathsf{T}})^{-1}(\widehat{C}-B^{\mathsf{T}}X)\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\left.-\alpha^{2}\big(\Delta_{\widehat{C}}^{\mathsf{T}}-\Delta_{X}B\big)\big(D+D^{\mathsf{T}}\big)^{-1}\big(\Delta_{\widehat{C}}-B^{\mathsf{T}}\Delta_{X}\big)\right)y

\displaystyle=-\alpha\beta-\alpha^{2}.

We conclude that

(3.15)

\displaystyle 0\leq-\alpha(\beta+\alpha)

is a necessary condition for
0\preceq\mathcal{W}(\widehat{C}_{\alpha},X_{\alpha})
.
By assumption,
0\preceq\mathcal{W}(\widehat{C}_{\alpha},X_{\alpha})
for all
\alpha\in[0,1]
, and therefore (
3.15
) must hold. In particular, evaluating (
3.15
) at
\alpha=1
, we obtain
0\leq-\beta-1
, which implies
\beta\leq-1
.
Hence, we conclude that (
3.15
) cannot hold for
\alpha<0
.∎

Remark 3.11
.

The previous result provides a sufficient condition for a local minimizer being a global one, namely if the extremal solutions of the
ARE
(
2.3
) coincide. Unfortunately, this characterization is not true if
D+D^{\mathsf{T}}\not\succ 0
.
For instance, in the scalar case, i.e.,
n=m=1
and
D=0
, the Lur’e equations (
2.4
) reduce to

2AX=-L^{2},\qquad BX-C=0.

If we assume
A<0
and
B\neq 0
, then (
2.4
) is only solvable if
\sign(B)=\sign(C)
. Hence, the feasible set equals
\widehat{\mathcal{C}}=\bigl\{\widehat{C}\in\mathbb{R}\;\big|\;\widehat{C}/B\geq 0\bigr\}
. For all
\widehat{C}\in\widehat{\mathcal{C}}
, including
\widehat{C}\neq 0
, the unique solution is
X=-{\widehat{C}}/{B}
.
Consequently, in the view of the passivation problem,
X_{\min}=X_{\max}
does not imply that we are on the boundary of the feasible set and, therefore, does not imply that we have found the nearest passive system.

Theorem

3.10
provides a simple way to detect global minimizers. If our optimization algorithm converges to a minimizer
L^{\star}
, we can check whether the condition of
Theorem

3.10
is satisfied.
In particular, we can compute the eigenvalues of the matrix

\displaystyle Y^{\star}\vcentcolon=

\displaystyle\,A-B\big(D+D^{\mathsf{T}}\big)^{-1}\left(\widehat{C}(L^{\star})-B^{\mathsf{T}}\mathcal{L}^{-1}\left(-L^{\star}\big(L^{\star}\big)^{\mathsf{T}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,A-B(D+D^{\mathsf{T}})^{-1}M\big(L^{\star}\big)^{\mathsf{T}}.

If, up to a prescribed tolerance, the eigenvalues are not on the imaginary axis, then we conclude
X_{\min}\neq X_{\max}
and we are potentially stuck in a non-global local minimum. In this case, we suggest performing a small gradient step on
\widehat{C}^{\star}\vcentcolon=\widehat{C}\big(L^{\star}\big)
in the direction of
\nabla_{\widehat{C}}\mathcal{J}(L^{\star})
, i.e.,

(3.16)

\widehat{C}^{\star}\leftarrow\widehat{C}^{\star}-\alpha\nabla_{\widehat{C}}\mathcal{J}(L^{\star}),

with
\nabla_{\widehat{C}}\mathcal{J}(L^{\star})=2(\widehat{C}^{\star}-C)\mathcal{P}
and
\alpha\ll 1
. We then check whether the resulting
\widehat{C}^{\star}
still yields a passive realization. If this is the case we simply restart the optimization from this point on, by obtaining the new initial
L_{0}
via (
2.7
). If not, we apply our initialization strategy, which is described in the forthcoming
Section

3.4
or try a smaller step size
\alpha
.

3.4.
Initialization via solutions of the perturbed ARE

The
ARE
(
2.3
) associated with the
KYP
inequality or the Lur’e equations does not have a solution for a non-passive system.
If we have a solution of the

at hand, we can use it as an initialization for the optimization problem via (
2.7
).
Now the idea is the following: Instead of perturbing
C
to find a passive realization, we perturb the feedthrough term
D
. Then we solve the
ARE
for the perturbed system and use the solution as an initialization for the optimization problem.
The perturbation can be chosen as
\Delta_{D}=-\frac{\lambda_{\min}}{2}I_{m}
, with

\lambda_{\min}<0
being the smallest eigenvalue of the modified Popov function along the imaginary axis
\Phi(\imath\omega)
for all
\omega\in\mathbb{R}
.
Since we may not obtain the exact value of
\lambda_{\min}
, we can set
\Delta_{D}=-\left(\frac{\lambda_{\min}}{2}-\varepsilon\right)I_{m}
for some small
\varepsilon>0
to ensure that the perturbed system is passive.
In our algorithms we use a sampling technique over a sufficiently large frequency domain. The pseudocode for this initialization strategy is given in

5.
Conclusions

We presented a novel approach, called
KLAP
, to address the
\mathcal{H}_{2}
-optimal passivation problem for linear dynamical systems. The core of
KLAP
combines the
KYP
or positive real lemma with the existence of rank-minimizing solutions. This combination allows us to explicitly parametrize the output matrix, ensuring that the system is passive by design. Using this parametrization, we formulated the optimization problem and examined its well-posedness. Additionally, we proposed an initialization strategy based on the
ARE
related to the
KYP
inequality. Since our parametrization may lead to the presence of non-global local minima, we established a criterion for identifying the global minima and introduced a restart strategy in case the algorithm is potentially stuck in a non-global local minimum.
We validated the effectiveness of our approach on three benchmark examples, demonstrating significant speedups over traditional methods based on the positive real lemma while achieving comparable accuracy in
\mathcal{H}_{2}
-error. Although not covered in this work, we expect that the same approach can be readily applied to the bounded real lemma to find the nearest contractive system. Furthermore, we foresee the method being extendable to parametric systems and differential-algebraic equations. Also, the application of
KLAP
to the
\mathcal{H}_{\infty}
-optimal passivation problem is a promising direction for future research.
