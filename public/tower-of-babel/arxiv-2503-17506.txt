Optimization over Trained Neural Networks: Difference-of-Convex Algorithm and Application to Data Center Scheduling
Xinwei Liu, Vladimir Dvorkin

Abstract
When solving decision-making problems with mathematical optimization, some constraints or objectives may lack analytic expressions but can be approximated from data. When an approximation is made by neural networks, the underlying problem becomes optimization over trained neural networks. Despite recent improvements with cutting planes, relaxations, and heuristics, the problem remains difficult to solve in practice. We propose a new solution based on a bilinear problem reformulation that penalizes ReLU constraints in the objective function. This reformulation makes the problem amenable to efficient difference-of-convex algorithms (DCA), for which we propose a principled approach to penalty selection that facilitates convergence to stationary points of the original problem. We apply the DCA to the problem of the least-cost allocation of data center electricity demand in a power grid, reporting significant savings in congested cases.



I
Introduction

Optimization over trained neural networks has gained momentum thanks to their remarkable expressivity in representing complex objectives and constraints in decision-making. Rectified Linear Unit (ReLU) neural networks can be trained to learn complex laws from data and then integrated into mathematical optimization as mixed-integer constraints
[
1
,
2
]
. For example, ReLU networks efficiently approximate natural gas and power flows from data, enabling optimal dispatch of gas systems and power grids
[
3
,
4
]
—the application domain of interest to this work.

For practically sized neural networks, such optimization becomes computationally expensive. The standard mixed-integer programming (MIP) approach enjoys Big-M reformulation of ReLU constraints but suffers from numerical issues when M is large
[
1
,
2
]
. The SOS1 approach resolves numerical issues
[
5
]
but requires computationally expensive branch-and-cut procedures. At the rate of one binary variable (or SOS1 constraint) per ReLU, such methods quickly become intractable. To improve tractability, the literature suggests identifying stable neurons
[
6
]
and patterns in neural activations
[
7
]
, as well as pruning
[
8
]
. Considerable progress has been made in the space of neural network verification, offering improvements via semidefinite programming relaxation of ReLU neurons
[
9
,
10
]
and bound propagation
[
11
]
, although their applications outside the verification space have yet to be explored. In addition, a principled heuristic proposed in
[
12
]
exploring global and local linear relaxations of neural networks has shown to outperform MIP solvers.

Our interest in optimization over trained neural networks is motivated by the growing electricity demand of AI. Large networks of data centers can help grid operators reduce the cost of grid operations by spatially reallocating electricity demand among electrically remote locations. This problem can be solved using different types of coordination (e.g., see
[
13
]
for a brief overview), but more naturally via strategic market participation of data centers. By seeking the least-cost demand allocation in the grid, they implicitly help the system achieve the minimum operating cost. Strategic participation models either feature restrictive assumptions (e.g., linear price model
[
14
]
) or require grid data
[
15
]
which is not available to regular participants. The use of neural networks provides a more practical solution: They are trained on historical market outcomes to approximate the price model, which can then be used to optimize demand allocations.

Contributions:
Inspired by their potential impact on grid operations, we develop a new efficient algorithm for optimization over trained neural networks. Our algorithm relaxes the ReLU constraints by penalizing them in the objective function. Such a formulation makes the problem amenable to difference-of-convex programming
[
16
]
, which iteratively solves simple convex sub-problems, thereby avoiding the computational complexity of the standard MIP formulations. However, the challenge is to compute the penalty parameter, which sufficiently penalizes the ReLU constraints without impacting optimality. We establish the principled approach to computing such a penalty. Our approach is inspired by solution techniques for complementarity problems whose structure is similar to the problem at hand
[
17
]
. We provide an application to strategic participation of data centers in electricity markets, where trained neural networks are used to approximate the market price as a function of data center demands. Our simulations on small and large neural networks reveal significant cost savings for data center owners.

Optimization over Trained Neural Networks

Consider a
trained
neural network (NN) with the following mathematical representation:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\lambda}=\mathbf{W}^{N+1}\text{ReLU}(\dots\text{ReLU}(\mathbf{W}^{1}\mathbf{d}+\mathbf{b}^{1})\dots)+\mathbf{b}^{N+1},

i.e., feedforward network with fully-connected layers of ReLU neurons. Consider the input
\mathbf{d}=[d_{1},\dots,d_{n}]^{\top}

\mathcal{D}

\mathbf{y}^{i}=[y_{1}^{i},\dots,y_{p}^{i}]^{\top}.
Let

be the

and

and
\mathbf{b}^{i}
be the bias vector. The output

is given by the ReLU activation function
\text{ReLU}(\mathbf{W}^{i}\mathbf{y}^{i-1}+\mathbf{b}^{i})=\text{max}\{\mathbf{0},\mathbf{W}^{i}\mathbf{y}^{i-1}+\mathbf{b}^{i}\},
understood element-wise.

In optimization over trained neutral networks, the parameters
\mathbf{W}^{i}
and
\mathbf{b}^{i}
for each layer

are fixed, and the decision variables include the input
\mathbf{d}
, and the outputs
\mathbf{y}^{1},\dots,\mathbf{y}^{N}
and

of the hidden and final linear layers, respectively. The optimization problem takes the form:

\displaystyle\underset{{\mathbf{d}\in\mathcal{D},\mathbf{y},\boldsymbol{\lambda}}}{\text{minimize}}\;\;\!

\displaystyle c(\boldsymbol{\lambda})

(1a)

subject to

\displaystyle\mathbf{y}^{1}=\text{ReLU}(\mathbf{W}^{1}\mathbf{d}+\mathbf{b}^{1}),

(1b)

\displaystyle\mathbf{y}^{i}=\text{ReLU}(\mathbf{W}^{i}\mathbf{y}^{i-1}+\mathbf{b}^{i}),\forall i=2,\dots,N

(1c)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\lambda}=\mathbf{W}^{N+1}\mathbf{y}^{N}+\mathbf{b}^{N+1},

(1d)

where the input
\mathbf{d}
belongs to its bounded domain
\mathcal{D}
,
c:\mathbb{R}^{m}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
is the convex cost function acting on NN’
s outputs, and variable
\mathbf{y}=[\mathbf{y}^{1\top},\dots,\mathbf{y}^{N\top}]^{\top}
stacks the outputs of hidden layers. The goal of this optimization is to find the optimal NN input
\mathbf{d}^{\star}
that minimizes the cost function.

We relate the optimization over trained NNs to mixed-complementarity problems. The neuron activations
y=\text{ReLU}(a)
are represented by complementarity constraints:

\displaystyle y=a+v,

(2a)

\displaystyle 0\leqslant y\perp v\geqslant 0,

(2b)

where
v
is an auxiliary variable, and expression

means that
y
and
v
are orthogonal, i.e., their inner product is zero. We can verify this ReLU formulation by examining two scenarios,
a\geqslant 0
and
a\leqslant 0
. If
a\geqslant 0
, then
y=a
, and
v=0
and all conditions are satisfied. If
a\leqslant 0
, then
y=0
,
v=-a\geqslant 0

2b

[
5
]
, or address it through Big-M reformulation
[
1
]
; in this paper, however, we will take a different path to address this constraint. We rewrite problem (
1
) as follows:

\displaystyle\underset{{\mathbf{d}\in\mathcal{D},\mathbf{y},\mathbf{v},\boldsymbol{\lambda}}}{\text{minimize}}\;\;\!

\displaystyle c(\boldsymbol{\lambda})

(3a)

subject to

\displaystyle\mathbf{y}^{1}=\mathbf{W}^{1}\mathbf{d}+\mathbf{b}^{1}+\mathbf{v}^{1},

(3b)

\displaystyle\mathbf{y}^{i}=\mathbf{W}^{i}\mathbf{y}^{i-1}+\mathbf{b}^{i}+\mathbf{v}^{i},\forall i=2,\dots,N

(3c)

\displaystyle\mathbf{0}\leqslant\mathbf{y}^{i}\perp\mathbf{v}^{i}\geqslant\mathbf{0},\quad\quad\quad\!\;\forall i=1,\dots,N

(3d)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\lambda}=\mathbf{W}^{N+1}\mathbf{y}^{N}+\mathbf{b}^{N+1},

(3e)

The number of complementarity constraints is equal to the number of hidden neurons. At the rate of one constraint per ReLU, the problem quickly becomes intractable. In the next section, we introduce an algorithm to address this challenge.

III
Difference-of-Convex Algorithm for Optimization Over Trained Neural Networks

In this section, we first introduce a bilinear reformulation of problem (
3
) that penalizes complementarity constraint violations in the objective function. The difference–of–convex algorithm (DCA) is then introduced to solve the bilinear formulation. Leveraging the specifics of trained NNs, we then present a principled method to optimize the DCA parameters to converge to the solution of the original problem.

III-A

Bilinear Formulation and DCA Algorithm

The bilinear formulation relaxes the complementarity constraints (
3d
) and penalizes them in the objective function:

\displaystyle\underset{{(\mathbf{d},\mathbf{y},\mathbf{v},\boldsymbol{\lambda})\in\mathcal{O}}}{\text{minimize}}\;\;\!

\displaystyle c(\boldsymbol{\lambda})+\rho\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{y}^{i\top}\mathbf{v}^{i}

(4a)

subject to

\displaystyle\mathbf{y}^{1}=\mathbf{W}^{1}\mathbf{d}+\mathbf{b}^{1}+\mathbf{v}^{1},

(4b)

\displaystyle\mathbf{y}^{i}=\mathbf{W}^{i}\mathbf{y}^{i-1}+\mathbf{b}^{i}+\mathbf{v}^{i},\;\forall i=2,\dots,N

(4c)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\lambda}=\mathbf{W}^{N+1}\mathbf{y}^{N}+\mathbf{b}^{N+1},

(4d)

where
\rho>0
is a penalty parameter and set
\mathcal{O}
contains the input domain
\mathcal{D}
and non-negativity of variables
\mathbf{y}
and
\mathbf{v}
. Under such constraints, the penalty function
\rho\mathbf{y}^{i\top}\mathbf{v}^{i}
for each layer
i
is zero if and only if
\mathbf{y}^{i}\perp\mathbf{v}^{i}
, as required by (
3d
). By solving this penalized problem, we aim to obtain the solution to the original non-convex problem (
3
). Finding the global optimum solution of (
3
) is NP-hard, so we seek
strongly stationary solutions
–to be formally defined later–which satisfy the Karush-Kuhn-Tucker conditions (KKTs) of (
3
). We observe that the penalty functions for each layer
i
can equivalently be written as
[
17
]
:

\displaystyle\rho\mathbf{y}^{i\top}\mathbf{v}^{i}=\frac{\rho}{4}\lVert\mathbf{y}^{i}+\mathbf{v}^{i}\rVert_{2}^{2}-\frac{\rho}{4}\lVert\mathbf{y}^{i}-\mathbf{v}^{i}\rVert_{2}^{2}

(5)

Substituting (
5
) into the objective function (
4a
), we represent the latter as the difference–of–convex function

\displaystyle f(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{v})=\underbrace{c(\boldsymbol{\lambda})+\frac{\rho}{4}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lVert\mathbf{y}^{i}+\mathbf{v}^{i}\rVert_{2}^{2}}_{f_{1}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{v})}-\underbrace{\frac{\rho}{4}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lVert\mathbf{y}^{i}-\mathbf{v}^{i}\rVert_{2}^{2}}_{f_{2}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{v})}

where the non-convex objective function
f
is given by the difference of two convex functions
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
. We do not include variable
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
into the arguments of
f
and
f_{1}
, as it can be represented by the affine transformation of the output of the last hidden layer
\mathbf{y}^{N}
. The basic DCA minimizes (
III-A
) over iterations subject to constraints (
4b
)–(
4d
).
It overestimates the concave part of (
III-A
) by a linear function using a gradient evaluated at a current iterate and solves a resultant auxiliary (convex) sub-problem to obtain a new iterate
[
16
]
. In terms of function (

, the corresponding sub-problem at iteration

\displaystyle\underset{{(\mathbf{d},\mathbf{y},\mathbf{v},\boldsymbol{\lambda})\in\mathcal{O}}}{\text{minimize}}\;\;

\displaystyle c(\boldsymbol{\lambda})+\frac{\rho}{4}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lVert\mathbf{y}^{i}+\mathbf{v}^{i}\rVert_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\quad\quad-\frac{\rho}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(\mathbf{y}^{i(k)}-\mathbf{v}^{i(k)})^{\top}(\mathbf{y}^{i}-\mathbf{v}^{i})

(7a)

subject to

\displaystyle\mathbf{y}^{1}=\mathbf{W}^{1}\mathbf{d}+\mathbf{b}^{1}+\mathbf{v}^{1},

(7b)

\displaystyle\mathbf{y}^{i}=\mathbf{W}^{i}\mathbf{y}^{i-1}+\mathbf{b}^{i}+\mathbf{v}^{i},\;\forall i=2,\dots,N

(7c)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\lambda}=\mathbf{W}^{N+1}\mathbf{y}^{N}+\mathbf{b}^{N+1},

(7d)

where the variables
\mathbf{y}^{i(k)},\mathbf{v}^{i(k)}
are fixed to the corresponding values from iteration

f_{2}
evaluated at
(\mathbf{y}^{i(k)},\mathbf{v}^{i(k)})

IV
Application to Data Centers Scheduling

Our motivating problem is the optimal scheduling of electricity demand from large data centers in power systems. In this section, we first describe a popular yet difficult-to-implement approach to solving this problem as a bilevel optimization problem. We then detail an arguably more practical NN-based approach.

IV-A

Conventional Bilevel Formulation

Consider an operator of spatially distributed data centers who allocates the workloads to minimize the cost of electricity consumption. This problem is solved in advance, when the operator receives a forecast of the total electricity demand, denoted by
\Delta
, to be allocated among grid nodes hosting data centers. The optimal allocation is driven by locational marginal prices (LMPs), defining the cost of electricity at specific nodes. The prices are computed via dual variables of the optimal power flow (OPF) problem
[
19
]
:

\displaystyle\underset{{\underline{\mathbf{p}}\leqslant\mathbf{p}\leqslant\overline{\mathbf{p}}}}{\text{minimize}}\quad

\displaystyle\mathbf{p}^{\top}\mathbf{C}\mathbf{p}+\mathbf{c}^{\top}\mathbf{p}

(14a)

subject to

\displaystyle\mathbf{1}^{\top}(\mathbf{p}-\boldsymbol{\ell}-\mathbf{d})=0\quad\colon\lambda

(14b)

\displaystyle|\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{p}-\boldsymbol{\ell}-\mathbf{d})|\leqslant\overline{\mathbf{f}}\quad\colon\underline{\boldsymbol{\mu}},\overline{\boldsymbol{\mu}}

(14c)

which seeks generator dispatch
\mathbf{p}
that minimizes quadratic cost (
14a
). The dispatch must remain within a feasible range
[\underline{\mathbf{p}},\overline{\mathbf{p}}]
and satisfy a set of grid constraints. Equality constraint (
14b
) enforces a system-wide power balance between dispatched generation and loads composed of conventional loads
\boldsymbol{\ell}
and data center loads
\mathbf{d}
. The power flows in transmission lines are computed using the matrix of power transfer distribution factors
\mathbf{F}
, which maps net power injections
(\mathbf{p}-\boldsymbol{\ell}-\mathbf{d})
to power flows as
\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{p}-\boldsymbol{\ell}-\mathbf{d})
. They are capped by line capacity
\overline{\mathbf{f}}
via (
14c
). The optimal dual variables (stated after the colon signs) are used to compute LMPs as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\pi}=\mathbf{1}\lambda^{\star}-\mathbf{F}^{\top}\overline{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{\star}+\mathbf{F}^{\top}\underline{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{\star}

(15)

where the uniform price
\lambda^{\star}
is corrected by line congestion terms, justifying the locational nature of electricity prices.

To map a specific demand allocation
\mathbf{d}
to LMPs, we need to solve the OPF sub-problem (
14
). The optimal allocation becomes a bilevel optimization problem, which optimizes
\mathbf{d}
using feedback from the embedded OPF sub-problem, i.e.,

\displaystyle\underset{{\mathbf{d}}}{\text{minimize}}\quad

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\pi}^{\top}\mathbf{d}

(16a)

subject to

\displaystyle\underline{\mathbf{d}}\leqslant\mathbf{d}\leqslant\overline{\mathbf{d}}

(16b)

\displaystyle\mathbf{1}^{\top}\mathbf{d}=\Delta

(16c)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\pi}\in\text{dual sol. of \eqref{problem:opf} formulated on $\mathbf{d}$}

(16d)

where the objective function (
16a
) minimizes the total charge for electricity consumption of data centers. Constraints in (
16b
) ensure that the allocation respects the minimum and maximum capacity of data centers. The equality constraint (
16c
) enforces the conservation of electricity demand.

IV-B

Solution Based on the Optimization over Trained NN

There are several practical challenges that prevent solving problem (
16
). The first is the lack of data necessary to formulate OPF problem (
14
), including actual network matrix
\mathbf{F}
, cost coefficients
\mathbf{C}
and
\mathbf{c}
, and dispatch limits
\underline{\mathbf{p}}
and
\overline{\mathbf{p}}
. Second, solving (
16
) means optimizing over its KKTs that itself is an NP-hard problem
[
15
]
. Finally, the actual market engine can be more complicated than a well-posed convex formulation (
16
), e.g., include unit commitment binary variables preventing its standard KKT reformulation.

The optimization over trained NNs overcomes these challenges. NNs can approximate the mapping from data center loads
\mathbf{d}
to resulting charges from historical data. Consider a labeled dataset
\{(\mathbf{d}_{1},\lambda_{1}),\dots,(\mathbf{d}_{n},\lambda_{n})\}
, where for each load instance
\mathbf{d}_{i}
we have the corresponding charge
\lambda_{i}
, obtained as the inner product of LMPs and data center loads from past outcomes. A trained NN, with fixed weight and bias parameters, takes input
\mathbf{d}
and outputs
\lambda
. The optimization problem with the embedded trained NN takes the form:

\displaystyle\underset{{\mathbf{d},\mathbf{y},\mathbf{v},\lambda}}{\text{minimize}}\;\;

\displaystyle\lambda

(17a)

subject to

\displaystyle\underline{\mathbf{d}}\leqslant\mathbf{d}\leqslant\overline{\mathbf{d}}

(17b)

\displaystyle\mathbf{1}^{\top}\mathbf{d}=\Delta

(17c)

\displaystyle\mathbf{y}^{1}=\mathbf{W}^{1}\mathbf{d}+\mathbf{b}^{1}+\mathbf{v}^{1},

(17d)

\displaystyle\mathbf{y}^{i}=\mathbf{W}^{i}\mathbf{y}^{i-1}+\mathbf{b}^{i}+\mathbf{v}^{i},\forall i=2,\dots,N

(17e)

\displaystyle\mathbf{0}\leqslant\mathbf{y}^{i}\perp\mathbf{v}^{i}\geqslant\mathbf{0},\quad\quad\quad\!\;\forall i=1,\dots,N

(17f)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\lambda}=\mathbf{W}^{N+1}\mathbf{y}^{N}+\mathbf{b}^{N+1},

(17g)

where the NN input takes the forecast
\Delta
and computes the least-cost allocation of electricity demands
\mathbf{d}
.

VI
Conclusions

We develop a difference-of-convex algorithm to solve decision-making problems with objectives or constraints represented by trained neural networks. The algorithm avoids the computational bottleneck of ReLU logical constraints through informed penalization of such constraints in the objective function, hence avoiding computationally expensive mixed-integer techniques. The application to the problem of the optimal data center demand allocation in power grids revealed a large reduction in the total demand charge relative to the baseline demand allocation. Our results justify applications in the settings with a clear baseline solution upon which the algorithm can improve. As future research, we will expand the scope of modeling constraints, e.g., to include both spatial and temporal demand allocation, and provide more theoretical guarantees on the convergence behavior.
