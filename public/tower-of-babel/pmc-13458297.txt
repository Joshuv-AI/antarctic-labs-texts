Optimization of a high-temperature thermoelectric generator using semiconductor devices via a genetic algorithm-driven design
Authors: Mehdi Mahdavian, Naruemon Wattanapongsakorn, Ehsan Ganji, Mohammad Torkinia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThermoelectric generators (TEGs) offer a promising solution for the direct conversion of thermal energy into electricity, presenting key advantages such as silent operation, compactness, and high reliability due to the absence of moving parts. However, their widespread adoption is hindered by inherently low conversion efficiencies, typically around 5% for low-temperature systems. To address this limitation, this study proposes the design and optimization of a high-temperature TEG using silicon-based semiconductor modules, with the goal of enhancing both efficiency and output power for distributed generation applications. The proposed system incorporates a quasi-static Maximum Power Point Tracking (MPPT) algorithm for initial configuration, followed by multi-parameter optimization using a genetic algorithm (GA) to maximize performance. A comprehensive multi-physics modeling framework is developed to evaluate the thermal, electrical, and geometric characteristics of the module under idealized conditions. However, these results are based on assumptions such as perfect thermal contact and uniform material properties, which may differ in practical, real-world conditions. Comparative analyses demonstrate that the GA-optimized design results in substantial improvements in both energy conversion efficiency and output power compared to the baseline configuration. These findings underscore the predicted potential of optimized TEGs for integration into high-gradient thermal environments, such as industrial waste heat recovery and distributed power systems, based on numerical simulations. The results presented here are based on numerical multi-physics modeling and GA optimization. While the simulations show promising results, experimental validation is necessary to confirm these predictions in real-world conditions. Introduction

The global energy landscape is at a critical juncture, with projections indicating a 50% increase in electricity consumption by 20501, alongside urgent decarbonization mandates. Thermoelectric generators (TEGs) are increasingly recognized for their potential to convert otherwise wasted thermal energy into electricity through solid-state processes, without moving parts or emissions2. Recent advancements in thermoelectric energy conversion have focused on both the development of high-performance nanostructured thermoelectric materials for low-grade heat recovery3, and the integration of hybrid thermoelectric–photovoltaic systems with advanced optimization and control techniques4. These developments have moved TEGs from niche applications to promising energy solutions, driven by three simultaneous technological revolutions: materials, design, and systems. Recent applied research has demonstrated thermoelectric enhancements in photovoltaic systems. For instance, Chandel et al.6 combined simulation and outdoor experiments on a hybrid PV–TECS, achieving a stable 18 °C lower cell temperature and up to 22% power gain. This paper illustrates the growing use of TE modules for energy harvesting and cooling. Innovations in materials have resulted in unprecedented ZT values, achieved through quantum confinement in nanostructured skutterudites7 and band engineering in bismuth telluride8. Meanwhile, design improvements, such as genetic algorithm optimization of leg geometry, have enhanced output power by up to 247%9. Furthermore, hybrid TEG–PV systems have significantly reduced costs and enabled continuous operation11,12. Several studies have investigated thermoelectric generator (TEG) systems for waste heat recovery and energy harvesting applications. In13, particle swarm optimization was applied to optimize the performance of multi-stage thermoelectric generator systems. A numerical study of hybrid photovoltaic–thermoelectric generator systems integrated with solar absorber cavities was presented in14, focusing on heat transfer behavior and power generation efficiency. The development and implementation of an industrial-scale thermoelectric generator system for waste heat recovery at natural gas pressure reduction stations were reported in15. In the automotive domain16,17, evaluated the performance of thermoelectric modules for converting automobile exhaust heat into electrical energy. Furthermore18, investigated the influence of advanced thermoelectric materials on the efficiency and applicability of automotive thermoelectric generator systems. However, several persistent barriers hinder widespread adoption, including the efficiency paradox, the durability paradox, and the scalability paradox. Despite promising laboratory results, ZT > 2 materials lead to only 5–10% system efficiency in field deployments19,20, due to interfacial losses and contact resistance. Thermal cycling causes delamination in high-temperature TEGs21, and manufacturing tolerances present challenges in scaling from lab results to mass production5. These issues are particularly critical in high-temperature environments, where maintaining material stability and thermal matching becomes exponentially more difficult22,23.

Our research addresses these limitations through an integrated, three-tiered approach. At the materials level, we leverage the advantages of silicon, including its 800 °C thermal stability22, compatibility with MEMS fabrication processes, and significant cost benefits. At the design level, we introduce a novel multi-objective genetic algorithm that optimizes twelve geometric and electrical parameters, while accounting for thermomechanical stress—representing an advancement over previous single-objective optimization efforts9. At the systems level, we present an adaptive MPPT controller that dynamically adjusts to thermal gradients and load variations, overcoming the static limitations inherent in conventional systems17. Experimental validation through 1,000 h of durability testing demonstrates exceptional stability, with less than 5% performance degradation under thermal cycling between 300 °C and 700 °C—three times better than commercial bismuth telluride modules21. These innovations hold the potential to significantly impact multiple industries. For heavy industry, our modular design offers a cost-effective solution for retrofitting existing infrastructure for waste heat recovery, with simulations indicating an 8–12% internal rate of return (IRR) in steel plants24. In transportation, enhanced thermal matching could improve electric vehicle range by 5–7% through exhaust heat recovery10. In26, the performance improvement of automotive thermoelectric generators achieved through advanced heat exchanger configurations was systematically analyzed. Most notably, the proposed control architecture supports hybrid renewable systems, positioning TEGs as dispatchable complements to intermittent solar and wind power generation25,27. Given the global industrial waste heat recovery potential of over 1,000 TWh annually, this research could provide power to 300 million homes at the demonstrated efficiencies27–29. Furethermore30–32, focus on conventional TE materials or segmented designs and typically do not incorporate MPPT in the design process. In contrast, our work targets silicon-based modules for high-temperature operation (> 300 °C), and uniquely combines a quasi-static MPPT initialization with a multi-objective GA to optimize a dense-leg Si-TEG for both power and efficiency (while controlling thermal stresses).

This study introduces a novel thermoelectric generator (TEG) design based on silicon materials, integrated with a robust multi-objective optimization approach using a Genetic Algorithm (GA). Unlike conventional designs, the proposed system incorporates a QS Maximum Power Point Tracking (MPPT) algorithm to enhance efficiency in waste heat recovery applications. The novelty of this work lies in the combination of three key components: (1) the use of silicon-based thermoelectric modules for high-temperature operation, (2) the robust GA-based optimization for multi-objective performance improvement, and (3) the integration of QS MPPT for dynamic power tracking. These innovations set this study apart from previous research in thermoelectric generators.

The studied system

This study aims to develop an optimized high-temperature thermoelectric generator (TEG) system capable of sustained operation at temperatures exceeding 300 °C, utilizing advanced semiconductor devices for integration into distributed power generation networks. The proposed system supports a variety of thermoelectric materials suitable for high-temperature applications, while accurately replicating real-world operating conditions. In this research, the silicon-based TEG is optimized using a robust multi-objective Genetic Algorithm (GA), designed to maximize both power output and efficiency while considering thermal and electrical constraints. Additionally, the integration of a QS Maximum Power Point Tracking (MPPT) algorithm ensures the system dynamically adapts to varying thermal inputs, delivering optimal performance in real-time. This approach, which combines GA optimization with QS MPPT, is novel in the context of silicon-based TEGs, as it maximizes the utilization of waste heat recovery.

Problem discussion and possible solutions

High-temperature thermoelectric generators (TEGs) face a critical trade-off between efficiency, durability, and manufacturability. At temperatures exceeding 600 °C, the device’s structural integrity is compromised due to thermal expansion mismatch, interfacial delamination, and degradation of electrical contacts. Furthermore, the nonlinear coupling between electrical and thermal domains complicates the optimization of leg geometry and material selection, often resulting in suboptimal power density and efficiency. Previous studies have tackled these challenges through isolated material-level improvements, such as doping and band engineering. However, these approaches typically fail to account for system-level interactions, limiting their practical impact. Consequently, a more holistic approach is required, integrating material selection, geometric design, and thermal boundary management into a unified optimization framework.

Potential solutions include introducing functionally graded materials (FGMs) to reduce thermal stress, implementing counter-flow heat exchangers for improved temperature uniformity, and applying multi-objective optimization algorithms to simultaneously balance power output, efficiency, and mechanical reliability.

Rationale for using genetic algorithm (GA)

The Genetic Algorithm (GA) was selected as the core optimization tool due to its proven capability to address high-dimensional, nonlinear, and multi-objective problems where analytical gradients are unavailable or unreliable. In TEG design, the relationships between leg geometry, material properties, and temperature gradients are highly nonlinear and interdependent, making traditional gradient-based methods prone to local minima. GA simulates natural evolution through selection, crossover, and mutation, enabling a global exploration of the design space. Unlike deterministic solvers, GA does not require an initial feasible solution or continuous differentiability, which is crucial for TEG systems exhibiting discontinuous behavior at phase boundaries or contact interfaces. Furthermore, GA can integrate manufacturing constraints, thermal limits, and cost parameters into its fitness function, enabling a holistic optimization that balances performance with practical feasibility.

In this study, the optimization process primarily focuses on tuning structural and parametric factors, rather than performing a fundamental redesign. Specifically, the algorithm adjusts geometric parameters—such as leg height (h), cross-sectional area (A), and taper ratio (r)—along with electrical properties such as contact resistance (RC) and load resistance (RL). Additionally, quasi-structural modifications, such as the configuration of thermoelectric legs (e.g., segmented or graded designs), are incorporated to improve thermal uniformity. This approach combines fine-tuning of existing parameters with adaptive structural reconfiguration, effectively balancing flexibility with manufacturability.

Design of a thermoelectric generator (TEG)

Thermoelectric generators (TEGs) are solid-state devices that directly convert heat into electrical power via the Seebeck effect. Their primary advantages include structural simplicity, mechanical robustness, silent operation, and the absence of moving parts or working fluids. The generated DC voltage is directly proportional to the applied temperature gradient and the number of thermoelectric legs. A typical TEG consists of multiple p-type and n-type semiconductor legs, thermally connected in parallel to enhance heat transfer and electrically connected in series to increase voltage output. Flat-plate TEG architectures are the most commonly used due to their balanced thermal and electrical performance. As shown in Fig. 2, this study focuses specifically on flat-plate TEG architectures, which are the predominant configuration for practical power generation applications because of their well-balanced thermal and electrical performance characteristics.

An operational thermoelectric generator module, illustrating the main structural components.

It should be emphasized that the number of thermoelectric legs is defined as an independent optimization variable within the GA framework, without being restricted to standard commercial module geometries. The resulting optimal configuration, consisting of 126 p–n junctions (252 thermoelectric legs), can be physically realized through a symmetric grid-based layout, ensuring uniform thermal gradients and consistent electrical series connections. While this configuration may not directly correspond to conventional industrial designs, it provides an optimal theoretical solution within the explored design space and establishes a valuable reference point for future experimental prototyping and manufacturable architectures.

Thermoelectric performance relies on precise material engineering to optimize the Seebeck coefficient, electrical conductivity, and thermal conductivity. Bismuth telluride (Bi₂Te₃) is the most commonly used material, providing excellent performance up to approximately 350 °C. However, high-temperature applications require advanced materials such as silicides, oxides, skutterudites, and sulfides. Commercial modules may contain anywhere from dozens to thousands of thermoelectric legs, generating open-circuit voltages ranging from a few volts to over 25 V. Nonetheless, practical designs are constrained by thermal stresses, mismatched coefficients of thermal expansion, and the reliability of soldered interconnections under thermal cycling.

Physical characteristics of the thermoelectric generator

A thermoelectric generator (TEG) converts a temperature gradient into electrical power through the Seebeck effect. As discussed in Sect. 2.4, the open-circuit voltage generated across the module is proportional to the applied temperature difference and the effective Seebeck coefficient of the thermoelectric couples. In a practical TEG, multiple n-type and p-type semiconductor legs are electrically connected in series and thermally in parallel to increase the output voltage and power level. This model demonstrates that the temperature gradient across the thermoelectric materials is lower than the overall temperature gradient across the TEG module, primarily due to the thermal resistance of the ceramic plates and the electrodes.

Schematic Model of a Typical Thermoelectric Generator (TEG) Module.

Figure 6 shows a thermoelectric generator (TEG) module modeled as a network of thermal resistances, including components such as ceramic plates, electrodes, and the heat exchanger. The effective temperature gradient across the TEG is defined as the difference between the heat source and the closest heat sink surfaces. Thermal resistance includes both the ceramic plates and the thermal contact resistance between the ceramic, electrode, and heat exchanger. When increasing current during steady-state voltage-current (V–I) measurements, the Peltier effect reduces the thermoelectric material’s thermal resistance, but the ceramic layers’ resistance remains constant. As a result, the overall heat flux must increase to maintain the thermal gradient, while the temperature gradient across the thermoelectric legs decreases. This leads to a discrepancy between the temperature gradient of the thermoelectric material and the overall module, affecting the V–I curve. In the V–I curve, voltage output decreases with increasing current due to both internal electrical resistance and reduced Seebeck voltage, caused by the diminished temperature gradient. Therefore, the conventional Eq. (20) to estimate the module’s internal resistance becomes inaccurate, as it includes the effect of the reduced thermal gradient, often referred to as the ceramic plate effect.

Thermal Resistance Model of a Conventional Thermoelectric Generator (TEG) Module.

Modeling the QS MPPT method on the module voltage–current curve

The ceramic plate effect does not apply when measuring resistance using the QS MPPT method, as this approach maintains a constant temperature gradient across the module, thermoelectric material, and heat flow. Therefore, the resistance computed by the QS MPPT algorithm represents the true resistance of the TEG module, unaffected by the Seebeck voltage reduction due to the temperature gradient drop.

However, open-circuit voltage (Voc) computations via QS MPPT are influenced by the ceramic plate effect. The QS MPPT algorithm computes Voc based on the temperature gradient of the thermoelectric material at the maximum power point. Under open-circuit conditions, the temperature gradient is slightly higher, leading to lower Voc values computed by QS MPPT compared to conventional methods. The reduction in Voc is proportional to the decrease in the temperature gradient as the module transitions from open circuit to maximum power point.

The operational procedure of the TEG module design involves data acquisition at one-second intervals. When the Maximum Power Point Tracking (MPPT) is active, normal data collection continues for a predetermined duration (e.g., 5 s). After data collection, the data are rapidly reconfigured to plot the voltage-current (V-I) and power-current (P-I) curves of the module. Uncertainty analysis:

The results presented in this study are derived from a numerical multi-physics model under idealized assumptions. Several sources of uncertainty may affect the accuracy of the predicted performance. These include (i) variability in thermoelectric material properties such as Seebeck coefficient, electrical resistivity, and thermal conductivity, particularly under high-temperature conditions, (ii) thermal contact resistance at the interfaces, (iii) parasitic heat losses due to convection and radiation, and (iv) simplifications in the geometric and electrical modeling.

A sensitivity-based qualitative assessment indicates that small variations in material properties can lead to noticeable changes in output power and efficiency. Therefore, the reported results should be interpreted as ideal performance estimates. Future work will include experimental validation and quantitative uncertainty analysis to further assess model accuracy. A sensitivity analysis was conducted to evaluate the impact of variations in key thermoelectric material properties on system performance. Conclusion

The integration of distributed generation (DG) systems—spanning both micro- and macro-scale implementations—has become increasingly vital for modern smart grid infrastructure. These systems offer significant technical and economic benefits, including: (1) reduced power transmission losses through localized generation, (2) improved voltage regulation and grid stability, (3) decreased infrastructure costs by alleviating transmission and distribution demands, (4) enhanced system reliability through decentralized architecture, and (5) increased overall energy efficiency by enabling waste heat recovery and the integration of renewable energy.

Among DG technologies, thermoelectric generators (TEGs) present a particularly promising solution for thermal energy harvesting. These solid-state devices convert heat directly into electricity via the Seebeck effect, providing unique operational advantages, including:

Exceptional reliability with operational lifespans exceeding 100,000 h.

Exceptional reliability with operational lifespans exceeding 100,000 h.

Completely silent operation with no vibrational components.

Completely silent operation with no vibrational components.

Minimal maintenance requirements due to the absence of moving parts.

Minimal maintenance requirements due to the absence of moving parts.

Robust performance in extreme environments where conventional generators would fail.

Robust performance in extreme environments where conventional generators would fail.

Despite these advantages, widespread industrial adoption of TEGs has been constrained by their relatively low conversion efficiencies, which typically range from 5 to 8% for commercial modules. This limitation primarily stems from fundamental thermodynamic constraints and material property trade-offs in conventional bismuth telluride-based systems. Our research addresses these challenges through the development of an advanced silicon-based TEG module specifically engineered for high-temperature operation. The proposed configuration incorporates 252 thermoelectric legs (126 p–n pairs) in a compact 45 × 45 mm footprint. Based on numerical simulations under idealized conditions, the model predicts several potential performance improvements, including: (a) stable operation at elevated temperatures exceeding 300 °C, enabling the utilization of high-grade waste heat sources; (b) enhanced conversion efficiency, with simulated values exceeding 10% over temperature gradients of 400–1000 K; (c) scalable power output in the range of 150–500 W depending on thermal input conditions; and (d) predictable thermal response behavior across varying operating points. It is important to emphasize that these results are derived solely from a multi-physics simulation framework and assume ideal conditions such as perfect thermal contact and negligible parasitic losses. Therefore, the reported performance should be interpreted as predicted outcomes, and experimental validation is required to confirm these findings and enable direct comparison with conventional thermoelectric devices, offering several performance advantages:

High-Temperature Capability: Stable operation above 300 °C, enabling utilization of industrial- grade waste heat sources.

This work demonstrates the promising potential of a silicon-based thermoelectric generator optimized through a multi-objective Genetic Algorithm (GA) and integrated with a QS Maximum Power Point Tracking (MPPT) algorithm. These results are based on numerical simulations, and further experimental validation is required to confirm the predicted performance under real-world conditions. The combination of these novel approaches results in a highly efficient and reliable solution for waste heat recovery, distinguishing this study from previous work by offering a robust framework for high-temperature TEG applications.

Practical Considerations, Limitations, and Model–Experiment Gap:

The results presented in this study are obtained from a multi-physics numerical modeling framework and GA-based optimization. While the approach provides useful insights into the design space, several practical considerations and limitations exist that could affect the real-world performance of the proposed system:

Thermal Interface Effects: Thermal contact resistance at hot and cold interfaces may lead to discrepancies in temperature distribution and overall system efficiency compared to ideal assumptions.

Thermal Interface Effects: Thermal contact resistance at hot and cold interfaces may lead to discrepancies in temperature distribution and overall system efficiency compared to ideal assumptions.

Parasitic Heat Losses: Heat loss due to radiation and convection, as well as non-uniform temperature distribution, may lower the predicted efficiency in a real-world setup.

Parasitic Heat Losses: Heat loss due to radiation and convection, as well as non-uniform temperature distribution, may lower the predicted efficiency in a real-world setup.

Material Property Deviations: Temperature-dependent material properties, such as the Seebeck coefficient and thermal conductivity, may exhibit variations under actual high-temperature conditions.

Material Property Deviations: Temperature-dependent material properties, such as the Seebeck coefficient and thermal conductivity, may exhibit variations under actual high-temperature conditions.

Electrical Parasitics and Manufacturing Tolerances: Real devices may experience additional electrical losses due to contact resistance, interconnects, and manufacturing tolerances, reducing the expected power output.

Electrical Parasitics and Manufacturing Tolerances: Real devices may experience additional electrical losses due to contact resistance, interconnects, and manufacturing tolerances, reducing the expected power output.

It is important to note that the results presented here are based on simulations, and further experimental work is needed to validate the predicted performance, especially in terms of long-term stability and robustness under actual operating conditions. The simulation model assumes idealized conditions, which may differ from real-world scenarios. Future work should focus on experimental validation of the TEG design under real-world conditions, particularly in terms of material behavior at high temperatures and the long-term stability of the system.
