Finding equilibria: simpler for pessimists, simplest for optimists
Léonard Brice, Thomas A. Henzinger, K. S. Thejaswini

Abstract
We consider simple stochastic games with terminal-node rewards and multiple players, who have differing perceptions of risk. Specifically, we study risk-sensitive equilibria (RSEs), where no player can improve their perceived reward—based on their risk parameter—by deviating from their strategy. We start with the entropic risk (ER) measure, which is widely studied in finance. ER characterises the players on a quantitative spectrum, with positive risk parameters representing optimists and negative parameters representing pessimists. Building on known results for Nash equilibira, we show that RSEs exist under ER for all games with non-negative terminal rewards. However, using similar techniques, we also show that the corresponding constrained existence problem—to determine whether an RSE exists under ER with the payoffs in given intervals—is undecidable. To address this, we introduce a new, qualitative risk measure—called extreme risk (XR)—which coincides with the limit cases of positively infinite and negatively infinite ER parameters. Under XR, every player is an extremist: an extreme optimist perceives their reward as the maximum payoff that can be achieved with positive probability, while an extreme pessimist expects the minimum payoff achievable with positive probability. Our first main result proves the existence of RSEs also under XR for non-negative terminal rewards. Our second main result shows that under XR the constrained existence problem is not only decidable, but 𝖭𝖯 {\mathsf{NP}} -complete. Moreover, when all players are extreme optimists, the problem becomes 𝖯𝖳𝖨𝖬𝖤 {\mathsf{PTIME}} -complete. Our algorithmic results apply to all rewards, positive or negative, establishing the first decidable fragment for equilibria in simple stochastic games with terminal objectives without restrictions on strategy types or number of players.

Introduction

Stochastic systems have been used extensively in several areas including verification
[
14
]
, learning theory
[
1
]
, epidemic processes
[
21
]
to name a few. Several real-world systems however do not work with a centralised control. Therefore, modelling using stochastic systems with multiple agents makes for more faithful abstractions of such systems without a centralised control. Some examples of fields in which multi-agents stochastic modelling include cyber physical systems
[
31
]
, distributed and probabilistic computer programs
[
11
]
, probabilistic planning
[
32
]
. In such cases, the problem of reasoning about multiple agents with several, often times orthogonal objectives, becomes important. For multi-agent systems modelled with stochasticity on the underlying arena, a fundamental question to ask is the existence or finding of an equilibrium.
The most popular equilibria in literature are Nash equilibria
[
25
]
. However, those come with their own downsides. The computational complexity for studying Nash equilibria over multi-agent systems is prohibitively expensive, and even undecidable in the general case, where systems have
10
or more players
[
34
]
.
Further, even if Nash equilibria could be computed efficiently, they do not faithfully model the agents in real world settings
since they do not consider their tolerance or averseness to risk.

Let us consider a
1
-player game where a protagonist is proposed two options: (a) earning $1; (b) playing a lottery in which, with probability
\frac{1}{40}
, she gets $40, and with probability
\frac{39}{40}
, she does not earn anything.
Classically, rational strategies would be maximising the expected payoff. From this perspective, both options yield an expected payoff of $1, making them equivalent.
This approach is particularly justified when the game represents a scenario that can be repeated many times: the law of large numbers ensures that, in the long run, the average payoff will converge to the expected payoff. However, when the game is played only once, the protagonist may prioritise immediate needs. If she urgently requires $1, the guaranteed option (a) becomes preferable.

Conversely, if she is a risk-taker or finds herself in a situation where only the $40 can make a significant difference, she may prefer the high-risk option (b).
Although this choice might appear irrational, it mirrors the behaviour of millions of people who participate everyday in games with a negative expected payoff, driven by the allure of a potentially life-changing win, and generating an annual turnover of USD 536 billions
[
9
]
for the gambling industry.
That industry, on the other hand, operates on a large scale where expected payoff becomes the key metric.
This contrast underscores the importance of alternative measures to expected payoff that account for each agent’s risk tolerance.

Risk Measures.

A
risk measure
captures the perception that a player has of what their payoff will be. In that sense, they generalise the notion of expected payoff.
Various risk measures exist in the literature, and have been used extensively in the field of economics and finance.
Some of these risk measures include expected shortfall (ES), value at risk (VaR)
[
4
]
, variance
[
6
]
, entropic risk measure (ER)
[
13
]
.

A lot of work has been done in considering these risk measures over MDPs which use variance (along with mean) as a risk-measure
[
12
,
29
,
23
]
, ES
[
30
,
19
,
24
]
(also referred to as conditional value at risk (CVaR), average value at risk (AVaR), expected tail loss (ETL), and superquantile in literature) and ER
[
16
,
7
,
5
]
. Studying the entropic risk measure in MDPs appears more practical compared to expected shortfall or using variance-penalised risk-measures. This impracticability of ES and variance-penalised measure in particular is due to the intractable exponential memory
[
15
]
and time required to compute optimal strategies
[
29
]
, even for the one agent system of Markov decision processes (MDPs). On the other hand, when the risk measure used is ER, players have optimal positional strategies in MDPs
[
17
]
, which makes it a prime candidate for consideration in multi-agent settings.

Entropic Risk Measure.

The entropic risk measure is computed by assigning to each agent a risk parameter, i.e., a value
\rho\in\mathbb{R}
.
The entropic risk measure of a random variable

If the risk parameter
\rho
is positive, then more weight will be given to the bad payoffs: the corresponding player can then be considered as risk-averse.
Conversely, players with a negative
\rho
are more risk-loving.
When
\rho
tends to
0
, the entropic risk measure converges to the classical expectation
\mathbb{E}[X]
.

Unfortunately, even for two player zero-sum stochastic games with total-reward objectives (payoff is the sum of the rewards seen along the way), computing optimal strategies can only be done in
{\mathsf{PSPACE}}
, when the base
e
is replaced by an algebraic number; and if

is the base of the exponent, then it is decidable only subject to Shanuel’s conjecture
[
5

. Solving the two-player zero-sum case is a specific case of finding equilibria in two-agent systems where the payoffs of the two agents are exactly the negation of each others and so are the risk parameters of each of the agents.
Therefore, reasoning about multi-agent systems with ER also has potential to be computationally intractable.

Extreme Risk Measure.

We introduce a new risk measure called extreme risk measure (XR) to identify tractable risk parameters. Consider an agent who wishes to maximise the lowest payoff received with positive probability.
In our example,
by choosing option (a) her only payoff is
\$1
, whereas by choosing option (b), the payoffs that she receives with positive probability are
\$40
and
\$0
.
This agent would choose the option (a) since, then, the lowest reward she gets is
\$1
, instead of
\$0
. This would be her choice regardless of the probabilities or if the lottery amount in option (b) is increased.
Such agents can be considered “extreme pessimists” because
their perceived payoff can be thought of as the minimum among all the possible payoffs.
Similarly, one can define “extreme optimists” whose perceived reward is the best payoff that can be obtained with positive probability.
In the above scenario, an extreme optimist posed with the same options would choose option (b), no matter how small the probability is of receiving that payoff.

Extreme pessimists can be used to model safety-critical agents, where any positive probability of low reward or failure is unacceptable.
On the other hand, extreme optimists model naturally the opponents of such agents.
In a multiplayer setting, they can be an accurate modelling of agents like hackers in a system, who are happy with a small probability of success, or agents that have the possibility to restart their interactions with the same system, so that as long as there is a non-zero probability of achieving a high reward, they are guaranteed to receive that high reward.

Our results.

We consider the problem of finding equilibria in a multiplayer stochastic game, that is, a game in which the payoffs that the players receive depend on the
terminal vertex
that is reached, and in which an infinite play is associated to the zero payoff vector.

Our contributions are four fold.
Firstly, we consider the problem of finding equilibria where entropic risk measure is used to determine the perceived reward of each player. Each player has their own risk-sensitivity parameter, and we wish to find an equilibrium where no player has the incentive to deviate and increase their risk measure. We show that, when the rewards are all non-negative, such an equilibrium always exists.
We conjecture that this remains true when rewards can be negative.
Although some equilibria exist, not all equilibria are made the same, with some equilibria being more desirable than the others. One might want to find an equilibrium that maximises the overall social welfare, or want to minimise it for certain agents. A reasonably general setting is providing an interval for the risk measure for each agent and to check if there is an equilibrium satisfying these constraints. We call this problem
constrained existence problem of risk-sensitive equilibria
(RSEs).
We show (in
Section

3
) that this problem is undecidable when the risk parameters of the players are rational values, with undecidability results extending from the constrained existence problem for Nash equilibria in the work of Ummels and Wojtczak
[
34
]
. However, we find restrictions on strategies to recover decidability. If we restrict the memory requirements of each player, then for (small) finite memory strategies, we can solve the problem by encoding it using the existential theory of reals with exponentiation, giving us decidability subject to Shanuel’s conjecture, and
{\mathsf{PSPACE}}
algorithms when the base of the exponents are encoded as small algebraic instances, reminiscent of the two-player zero-sum case by Baier et al.
[
5
]
.

Secondly, since the general problem is undecidable, and even in restricted cases, we obtain complexities that are
{\mathsf{PSPACE}}
or higher, we pivot to searching for a more tractable risk measure that can be used to find equilibria in multi-agent systems. We define extreme risk measure (XR) as a novel risk measure to consider in multi-agent stochastic systems. We show (in
Section

4
) that our new definition is robust, since it exactly captures the well-studied entropic risk measure when the risk parameters tend to
\pm\infty
.
We further show the existence of
such equilibria for games with non-negative rewards. Moreover, there exists a stationary strategy profile that can be algorithmically constructed in polynomial time. We conjecture, again, that this remains true when negative rewards are involved.
One further advantage of XR as a risk measure is that it is indifferent to the exact probabilities of the underlying stochastic model, since it only deals with events that occur with a positive probability and, therefore, can also be used in systems where the underlying probabilities are unknown.

Thirdly, we show that the constrained existence problem of RSEs is decidable and also
{\mathsf{NP}}
-complete when the perceived payoff is calculated using XR, where each agent is either an extreme optimist or pessimist. The
{\mathsf{NP}}
membership is nontrivial and follows several steps. First, we show that if there is a strategy that satisfies the constraints, then there is a finite abstraction of this strategy. Later, we show that this finite abstraction of the strategy has a polynomial representation.
With this polynomial representation of the strategy, we show that verifying whether a given polynomially represented strategy is a risk-sensitive equilibrium that satisfies the constraints can also be done in polynomial time.
Finally, we show that if all players are extreme optimists, this problem is
{\mathsf{PTIME}}
-complete.

Preliminaries

We assume that the reader is familiar with the basics of probability and graph theory. However, we define some concepts for establishing notation.

Probabilities.

Given a (finite or infinite) set of outcomes
\Omega
and a probability measure
\mathbb{P}
over
\Omega
, let
X
be a random variable over
\Omega
, that is, a mapping
X:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
. We then write
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}[\mathbb{X}]
, or simply
\mathbb{E}[X]
, for the expectation of
X
, when it is defined.
Given a finite set
S
, a
probability distribution
over
S
is a mapping
d:S\rightarrow[0,1]
that satisfies the equality
\sum_{x\in S}d(x)=1
.
We write
\mathsf{Supp}(d)
for the
support
of the distribution
d
, that is, the set of elements
x\in S
such that
d(x)>0
.

Risk measures.

Given a set
\Omega
of outcomes, a
risk measure
over
\Omega
is a mapping
M
which maps a probability measure
\mathbb{P}
over
\Omega
and a random variable
X
to a real value
M^{\mathbb{P}}[X]
.

Sometimes, in the literature, risk measures are expected to have the following three properties: (1) they are
normalised
, i.e., we have
M^{\mathbb{P}}[0]=0
; (2) they are
monotone
, i.e., the pointwise inequality
X\leq Y
implies
M^{\mathbb{P}}[X]\leq M^{\mathbb{P}}[Y]
; and (3) they are
translative
, i.e.,
M^{\mathbb{P}}[X+c]=M^{\mathbb{P}}[X]+c
for every constant
c
.
In particular, the expectation of a random variable
\mathbb{E}
is a risk measure.
We do not refer to the above properties again and only state them here to remark that all the risk measures we will consider satisfy the above properties.
We remark that the definition of translative sometimes instead refers to satisfying the opposite of the property we define as translative,
M^{\mathbb{P}}[X+c]=M^{\mathbb{P}}[X]-c
for every constant
c
. This is a matter of whether we use our risk measure or its negation.

Graph, paths, games.

A directed graph
(V,E)
consists of a set of
vertices

V
and a set of ordered pair of vertices, called
edges
,
E
.
In a directed graph
(V,E)
, for each vertex
u
, we write
E(u)
to represent the set
E\cap(\{u\}\times V)
.
For simplicity, we often write
uv
for an edge
(u,v)\in E
.
A
path
in the directed graph
(V,E)
is a (finite or infinite) word
\pi=\pi_{0}\pi_{1}\dots
over the alphabet
V
such that
\pi_{n}\pi_{n+1}\in E
for every
n
such that
\pi_{n}
and
\pi_{n+1}
exist.
We write
\mathsf{Occ}(\pi)
for the set of vertices occurring along
\pi
, and
\mathsf{Inf}(\pi)
for those that occur infinitely often, if there are any.
The prefix
\pi_{0}\dots\pi_{n}
is written as
\pi_{\leq n}
or
\pi_{<n+1}
, and the suffix
\pi_{n}\pi_{n+1}\dots
is written as
\pi_{\geq n}
or
\pi_{>n-1}
.
A finite path
\pi=\pi_{0}\dots\pi_{n}
is
simple
if every vertex occurs at most once along
\pi
.
It is a
cycle
if its last vertex
\pi_{n}
is such that
\pi_{n}\pi_{0}\in E
.

Definition 1

(Game)

A

is a tuple
\mathcal{G}=(V,E,\Pi,(V_{i})_{i\in\Pi},\mathsf{p},\mu)
, where we have:

•

(V,E)
, called the

;

•

a finite set
\Pi

;

•

a partition
(V_{i})_{i\in\Pi\cup\{?\}}

, where

denotes the set of vertices

by player

, and the vertices in

are called

;

•

a

\mathsf{p}:E(V_{?})\rightarrow[0,1]
, such that for each stochastic vertex

, the restriction of
\mathsf{p}
to

is a probability distribution;

•

a mapping
\mu:T\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{\Pi}
called

, where

is the set of

, i.e. vertices of the graph

that have no outgoing edges.
We also write
\mu_{i}
, for each player

.

In a more general framework, payoffs can be assigned to all infinite paths.

We thus extend the mapping
\mu
to the set

A game is

.

An

is a tuple
(\mathcal{G},v_{0})

\mathcal{G}_{\upharpoonright v_{0}}
, where

is an

.
In what follows, when the context is clear, we use the word

We often assume that we are given a game

Histories and plays.

We call

a path in the underlying graph that is infinite, or whose last vertex is terminal.
Other paths are called

.
We will then use the notations
\mathsf{Hist}(\mathcal{G})
to denote finite paths in the graph of the game, and
\mathsf{Plays}(\mathcal{G})
to denote both finite and infinite paths. For a history

, we write
\mathsf{last}(h)=h_{n}
.
We will also write
\mathsf{Hist}_{i}(\mathcal{G})

.
A history or play in an initialised game

is a history or play in

whose first vertex is

.

.

A (initialised or not)

We sometimes write
{\bar{\sigma}}(hv)

.

Pure, stationary, and positional strategies.

We say that a strategy

Risk-sensitive equilibria, constrained existence problem.

In multiplayer stochastic games, we wish to study generalisations of the classical Nash equilibria where the expectation is replaced by other risk measures.
Such generalisations are called
risk-sensitive equilibria

[
26
]
. We define this for games played over graphs

When
M
is a risk measure and
{\bar{\sigma}}
is a strategy profile, we write
M({\bar{\sigma}})
for
M^{\mathbb{P}_{\bar{\sigma}}}
.

Definition 3

(Risk-sensitive equilibrium)
.

Let
\mathcal{G}_{\upharpoonright v_{0}}
be a game, and let
\bar{M}=(M_{i})_{i\in\Pi}
be a tuple of risk measures.
Let
{\bar{\sigma}}
be a strategy profile in
\mathcal{G}_{\upharpoonright v_{0}}
, let
i
be a player, and let
\sigma^{\prime}_{i}
be a strategy for player
i
, called
deviation
of player
i
from
{\bar{\sigma}}
.
The deviation
\sigma^{\prime}_{i}
is
profitable
with regards to the risk measure
M_{i}
if we have
M_{i}({\bar{\sigma}}_{-i},\sigma^{\prime}_{i})[\mu_{i}]>M_{i}({\bar{\sigma}})[\mu_{i}]

The strategy profile
{\bar{\sigma}}
is a
\bar{M}
-
risk-sensitive equilibrium
, or
\bar{M}
-RSE, if no player
i
has a profitable deviation from
{\bar{\sigma}}
with regards to
M_{i}
.

The following problem is the main focus throughout our paper.

Question

(Constrained existence of risk-sensitive equilibria)
.

Given a game
\mathcal{G}_{\upharpoonright v_{0}}
, a tuple of risk measures
\bar{M}
, and two payoff vectors
{\bar{x}},{\bar{y}}\in\mathbb{Q}^{\Pi}
, does there exist a
\bar{M}
-RSE
{\bar{\sigma}}
in
\mathcal{G}_{\upharpoonright v_{0}}
such that for each
i\in\Pi
, we have
x_{i}\leq M_{i}({\bar{\sigma}})[\mu_{i}]\leq y_{i}
?

To turn this problem into an algorithmic decision problem, we need to restrict it to some specific sets of risk measures that can be finitely encoded.
That is what we do in the sequel of this paper, with the
entropic risk measure
, and later with the
extreme risk measure
.

3
Entropic risk measure

The entropic risk measure is a measure of the perceived payoff, which depends on the aversion or inclination of the player toward risk through the exponential utility function.
It is defined using a
risk parameter
, i.e. a real value
\rho\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
: large positive values indicate risk-averseness, large negative values risk-inclination. To see a visual representation of the entropic risk measure, see
Figure

1
in the introduction.

Definition 4

(Entropic risk measure)
.

Given a risk parameter
\rho
, the
entropic risk measure
is defined for every probability measure
\mathbb{P}
and random variable
X
as

\mathbb{M}_{\rho}^{\mathbb{P}}[X]=-\frac{1}{\rho}\log_{e}\left(\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}\left[e^{-\rho X}\right]\right).

For computational reasons, this definition is generalised by allowing every base
\beta>1
instead of Euler’s constant. The
entropic risk measure with base
\beta
is then defined by:

\mathbb{M}^{\mathbb{P}}_{\beta\rho}[X]=-\frac{1}{\rho}\log_{\beta}\left(\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}\left[\beta^{-\rho X}\right]\right).

The three parameters
\mathbb{P}
,
\rho
and
\beta
can be omitted when they are clear from the context.

Remark 5
.

•

For every
\beta
and
\rho
, the entropic risk measure
\mathbb{M}_{\beta\rho}
is a risk measure.

•

By enabling any base
\beta
, we obtain a definition that is more general only on a computational level, since handling Euler’s constant may not be equivalent to handling rational values.
Baring computational concerns, these definitions with different bases are equivalent, since for every
\beta
we have
\mathbb{M}_{\beta\rho}=\mathbb{M}_{e\rho^{\prime}}
, where
\rho^{\prime}=\rho\log_{e}(\beta)
.

•

The above definition implies that for
\rho=0
, the function is not defined.
However, it is known that for all
\mathbb{P}
,
\beta
and
X
, the quantity
\mathbb{M}_{\rho}
converges to
\mathbb{E}[X]
when
\rho
tends to
0
(see e.g.
[
28
]
).
Therefore, we henceforth assume that
\mathbb{M}_{0}[X]=\mathbb{E}[X]
to make risk entropy defined for all finite risk parameters
\rho
.

When we are given a profile
{\bar{\rho}}=(\rho_{i})_{i\in\Pi}
of risk parameters, we will sometimes write
\mathbb{M}_{\beta{\bar{\rho}}}[\mu]
for the tuple
\left(\mathbb{M}_{\beta\rho_{i}}[\mu_{i}]\right)_{i\in\Pi}
.
Risk entropy defines a family of RSEs, namely the
(\mathbb{M}_{\beta\rho_{i}})_{i}
-RSEs, that we also call
(\beta,{\bar{\rho}})
-entropic risk-sensitive equilibria
, or
(\beta,{\bar{\rho}})
-ERSEs.
The following theorem states the existence of such an RSE that uses no randomness in its strategy profile, in cases where all the payoffs are non-negative.

Theorem 6

(Existence of ERSE)
.

Let
\mathcal{G}_{\upharpoonright v_{0}}
be a simple stochastic game with only non-negative payoffs.
Then, there exists a (pure)
(\beta,\rho)
-ERSE over
\mathcal{G}_{\upharpoonright v_{0}}
.

Proof.

Pure Nash equilibria always exists in a stochastic multi-player games with prefix-closed Boolean objectives
[
33
, Theorem 3.10]
(a correction of an existing proof
[
10
]
). It is known that simple stochastic games where rewards are all positive (or all negative) can be converted into a game with reachability objectives such that if there is an NE in one, there is an NE in the converted game with the reachability objective. Indeed, if all the rewards are positive, we can always scale the rewards for each player of a stochastic game to ensure they are in the unit interval
[0,1]
. If the rewards are within the unit interval, then for terminals with reward
p
, we can instead add a probabilistic node that reaches this terminal vertex with probability
p
.
Therefore, with the same result, Nash equilibria always exist in simple stochastic games with non-negative rewards on the terminals.

Then, we can conclude our theorem using the following lemma.

Lemma 7

(App.
7
)
.

Given a game
\mathcal{G}_{\upharpoonright v_{0}}
and a tuple
{\bar{\rho}}\in\mathbb{R}^{\Pi}
, there exists a game
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}_{\upharpoonright v_{0}}
with the same underlying graph, player set, and probability function (but possibly different payoff function), such that the
(\beta,\rho)
-ERSEs in
\mathcal{G}_{\upharpoonright v_{0}}
are exactly the Nash equilibria in
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}_{\upharpoonright v_{0}}
. ∎

We conjecture that this result remains true when we remove the guarantee that rewards are non-negative.
We now turn to the constrained existence problem of
\left(\beta,{\bar{\rho}}\right)
-ERSEs.
Unfortunately, it is undecidable in the general case.

Proposition 8
.

The constrained existence problem of
\left(\beta,{\bar{\rho}}\right)
-ERSEs with
{\bar{\rho}}\in\mathbb{Q}^{\Pi}
is undecidable, even for any fixed value of
\beta
, for
{\bar{\rho}}=(0)_{i}
, and with only nonnegative payoffs.

Proof.

The undecidability of the constrained existence problem follows from the work of Ummels and Wojtczak
[
34
, Theorem 4.9]
where they show the undecidability of the constrained existence problem for Nash equilibria in the setting with 10 or more players. Since Nash equilibria is a specific instance of the setting of ERSEs where the risk parameters
{\bar{\rho}}
is
0
for each player, the undecidability of our setting follows.
∎

We therefore turn our attention to the constrained existence problem when the class of strategies considered is restricted.

Theorem 9

(App.
A.2
)
.

The constrained existence problem of
(\beta,{\bar{\rho}})
-ERSEs, in quantitative simple stochastic games:

1.

remains undecidable when players are restricted to pure strategies;

2.

is decidable when players are restricted to stationary strategies

(a)

subject to Shanuel’s conjecture if

are algebraic;

(b)

in

{\mathsf{SqrtSum}}

The

Proof Sketch.

The undecidability of the case where pure strategies are considered is inherited from Nash equilibria
[

, since the reduction uses only pure strategies.
The decidability of this stationary case is reminiscent of similar results for the two-player zero-sum case, which was recently studied in the work of Baier et al.
[

.
However, the techniques used are quite different and also require inspiration from the work of Ummels and Wojtczak
[

We write formulas in the existential theory of reals (

) which puts them in

For the case

∎

4
Extreme risk measure

This section introduces a new risk measure that provides a tractable alternative to existing risk measures available in the literature. We provide a simpler, yet robust, alternative that allows us later to tackle the constrained existence of equilibria in multiplayer settings.

Let us consider a random variable
X
that ranges over
\mathbb{R}
.
The
pessimistic risk measure
of
X
is the highest value
x
such that
X
almost-surely takes a value above
x
.
When
X
takes finitely many values, that corresponds to the least value that it takes with positive probability.
In probability theory, that measure is sometimes referred to as
essential infimum
, written
\essinf
.
The definition of
optimistic risk measure
is symmetric.

Definition 10

(Optimistic, pessimistic risk measure)
.

The pessimistic risk measure of a random variable
X
is defined by

\mathbb{PM}[X]=\essinf(X)=\sup\{x\in X~|~\mathbb{P}(X\geq x)=1\}
.
Analogously, the
optimistic risk measure
of
X
is
\mathbb{OM}[X]=\esssup(X)=\inf\{x\in X~|~\mathbb{P}(X\leq x)=1\}
.

When we are given a game
\mathcal{G}_{\upharpoonright v_{0}}
, we can assign an risk measure for each player by defining a partition
(P,O)
of
\Pi
, where the set
P
represents the set of players that are
pessimists
, whose perceived payoffs are defined by the pessimistic risk measure, while
O
represents the
optimists
, who intend to maximise their optimistic risk measure.
For convenience, we group both measures under the umbrella term
extreme risk measure (XR)
, and often assume that
(P,O)
is given; then, we write
\mathbb{X}_{i}
for
\mathbb{PM}
when
i\in P
, and for
\mathbb{OM}
when
i\in O
.
Since each player
i
is usually interested only in the risk measure of their own payoff, we will also write
\mathbb{X}_{i}({\bar{\sigma}})
for the quantity
\mathbb{X}_{i}({\bar{\sigma}})[\mu_{i}]
.
We define
extreme risk-sensitive equilibria
, or XRSEs for short, as
(\mathbb{X}_{i})_{i}
-RSEs.

We show that our definition of extreme risk measure corresponds to the limit cases of entropic risk measure.
Observe that in

Theorem 11

(App.
C.1
)
.

Let

\mathbb{R}

•

The limit risk entropy of

We now answer a fundamental question about equilibria, which is if one always exists.
We show that (stationary) XRSEs are guaranteed to exist in games with only non-negative rewards, similarly as ERSEs.
But our proof does not rely on the same arguments, and we instead give a constructive proof.

5
Constrained existence of extreme risk-sensitive equilibria

We now study the computational complexity of the constrained existence problem of XRSEs.
The main result of this section is the following theorem, which proves that, contrary to the same problem with ERSEs, it is a decidable fragment of the constrained existence of RSEs.

Theorem 13
.

The constrained existence problem for XRSEs is
{\mathsf{NP}}
-complete and is
{\mathsf{NP}}
-hard even when all players are pessimistic and all rewards are non-negative.

First, we prove
Theorem

16
, which shows that if there is an XRSE then there is one that uses finite memory. We show that this finite-memory strategy profile can be described only using polynomial size, which in turn proves
{\mathsf{NP}}
membership (
Lemma

18
).

Later, we consider the problem of XRSEs when the players are restricted to pure, stationary or positional strategies. We show that in all the above cases, the problem remains
{\mathsf{NP}}
-complete. The upperbound is similar to the general case, but the lower bound is shown in
Lemma

20
by showing a reduction from
{\mathsf{3SAT}}
to the constrained existence problem.

Theorem 14
.

The constrained existence problem of XRSEs is also
{\mathsf{NP}}
-complete when the players are restricted to positional, stationary, or pure strategies.

Finally, we show in the following theorem that when all players are optimistic, the problem becomes
{\mathsf{PTIME}}
-complete.

Theorem 15
.

The constrained existence problem of XRSE is
{\mathsf{PTIME}}
-complete when all players are optimists, that is, when
P=\emptyset
.

We dedicate the rest of the section to proving these three results.

5.1
Membership in
{\mathsf{NP}}

{\mathsf{NP}}
-membership is a consequence of the fact that when an XRSE exists, there also exists one with the same extreme risk measures that uses finite memory, with a number of states that is polynomial in the size of the game.
Let us therefore illustrate, with examples, how and why memory is required in such XRSEs.
We consider the following constrained existence question and analyse the same question on three example graphs.

(*)

Is there an XRSE in the game in which both players have a risk measure
1
?

Game in
Figure

2(a)
.

Let us consider the game in
Figure

2(a)
again.
The answer to Question
(*)
here is
no
.
Intuitively, such an XRSE would require at least two plays of positive probability: one that ends in
t_{1}
, and one that ends in
t_{2}
.
For those two plays to occur with positive probability, the strategy profile must proceed to a randomised action at vertex
a
or
b
: i.e., one of the players, at some point of time, must toss a coin to give the payoff
1
to player
\Circle
in one case and to
\Square
in the other.
But then, since that player is the only one that can see that coin, they have a profitable deviation by lying about the outcome, and always choose the option that gives them the best payoff.
More randomisation will not help: as long as one of the players randomises, be it once, several times, or infinitely often, they have an incentive to deviate and stay in the cycle and wait until the other player leaves.

Game in
Figure

2(b)
.

Consider now a slight modification, as shown in
Figure

2(b)
.
There, the first player that plays is determined at random by the edge that is taken from an initial stochastic vertex.
The answer to Question
(*)
for this game is
yes
.
The random choice on which player gets payoff

is decided by the stochastic vertex. Since both players can see which edge is taken from there, this serves as a source of unbiased randomness based on which they act. For example, it can be decided that if the play visits the vertex
a
immediately after

, then player
\Circle
must visit the terminal
t_{1}
, and similarly, if it visits the vertex
b
, then player
\Square
must visit the terminal
t_{2}
. If the edge

is taken, player
\Square
punishes player
\Circle
by always going back to

In other words, the stochastic vertex provides the players with a common coin.

Game in

Here, both players
\Circle

The stationary strategy profile in which from vertex

t_{1}

\Square

If, from vertex

\Circle
goes from vertex

to

leaves to

.
This is because a player has a profitable deviation only if they can play in a way that guarantees them a risk measure better than

If there remains a play that occurs with nonzero probability and offers a lower reward, then the player does not increase their risk measure.
Therefore, an XRSE where player

We see in this last example that memory is required to remember either the subset of players that are being anchored, or if a player has deviated from the strategy and must be punished.
Given one or more players that are being anchored, the memory state of any of the players does not change unless either a player deviates or, more importantly, randomisation occurs. When randomisation occurs, the set of players that are anchored in each of the plays is a subset of the set of players anchored before this play
split
.
In our examples, the set of players that are anchored at

After the splits, when we have only one player to anchor, the players can follow a positional strategy profile; and similarly when one player deviates and must be punished the players can follow a positional strategy profile.

(App.

6
Discussion

Our definition opens up several promising directions for future research.
One immediate extension of our work would be to study games with more sophisticated objectives, such as mean payoff or discounted sum.
Another extension of our work is to study the concurrent version of such games, where players choose actions concurrently rather than in a turn-based setting. Concurrent stochastic multi-player games on stochastic graphs have traditionally been viewed as intractable, often requiring infinite memory to achieve optimal strategies, which has limited exploration of multi-player versions of the same problem.

Finally, our definition of risk-sensitive equilibria is modelled after Nash equilibria and suffers from several of their limitations.
Like Nash equilibria, RSEs allow irrational behaviours when one player deviates and must be punished, as exemplified in our game
Figure

2(c)
.
Exploring alternative definitions of risk-sensitive equilibria that are modelled after other equilibria concepts more suited for games on graphs
[
27
, Section 7.1]
, such as subgame-perfect equilibria, could provide a more rational framework for player decision-making.

is known, strategies that realise the infimum and the supremum can be seen as optimal strategies in the Boolean zero-sum game in which player

wants with positive probability (if they are optimist) or with probability

(if they are pessimist) to reach the set of terminals yielding them at least payoff
x
(if
x>0
) or to avoid the set of terminals yielding them less than payoff
x
(if
x\leq 0
).
This is then a reachability game (seen either from player

j
’s perspective), and it is well-known

AG011

that in such a game, for both players, positional strategies suffice to maximise the probability of winning.
In particular, if one has a strategy to win that game with positive probability, or with probability

, there is also such a strategy that is positional.

are the smallest.
This suffices to guarantee Property

Construction of the strategy profile
{\bar{\sigma}}^{\star}

Based on
\Lambda
as in
Lemma

17
, we construct a strategy profile that finite memory states.
Formally, the definition of the finite-memory strategy
{\bar{\sigma}}^{\star}
will be done by defining its memory structure.

The memory states are the following:

•

for each player

, the state
\mathsf{punish}_{i}
;

•

for each vertex
v
and each subset
A\subseteq\Pi
of players such that there exists
h
with
A=\Lambda(h)
, the state
\mathsf{anchor}_{Av}
;

•

the state
\mathsf{anchor}_{\Pi\bot}
.

Observe that there are at most
2^{p}+\mathcal{O}(p)
such memory states.
We now define the transitions from each of those states from each vertex. Observe that long as the memory state does not change, strategy profile corresponds to a positional strategy profile. So, we describe such memoryless strategy profiles and also describe when the memory state changes.
For each set
A\subseteq\Pi
, we write
W_{A}
for the set of vertices that
{\bar{\sigma}}
may visit while anchoring the set
A
, i.e., the set of vertices
v
such that there exists a history
hv
with
\Lambda(hv)=A
.

Punishing memory states
\mathsf{punish}_{i}
.

First, let us define what
{\bar{\sigma}}^{\star}
does when in state
\mathsf{punish}_{i}
, for some player
i

punishing strategies
, followed when player
i
deviates from the assigned strategy with the other memory states.
By
Lemma

26
, there is a positional strategy profile
{\bar{\tau}}^{{\dagger}i}_{-i}
that minimises, from every vertex of the game, the payoff that player
i
can enforce.
In addition, we pick an arbitrary positional strategy
\sigma^{{\dagger}i}_{i}
.
Then, when the strategy profile
{\bar{\sigma}}^{\star}
is in the memory state
\mathsf{punish}_{i}
on reads a vertex

, the memory update function outputs the vertex
{\bar{\tau}}^{{\dagger}i}(v)
and the same memory state
\mathsf{punish}_{i}
.

Anchoring states with no player to anchor.

Let us now define what happens in memory state
\mathsf{anchor}_{\emptyset v}
.
Consider the objective of achieving a payoff vector that has positive probability to be achieved in
{\bar{\sigma}}
, while visiting only vertices of
W_{\emptyset}
.
Using classical attractor-based proofs (or
Lemma

26
), there exists a positional strategy profile that achieves that objective with probability
1
from every vertex from which that is possible: let us call it
{\bar{\tau}}^{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{\faAnchor}}\emptyset}
.
Then, when in memory state
\mathsf{anchor}_{\emptyset v}
and reading the vertex
w
, we distinguish two cases.

•

If
vw
is an edge that is compatible with the strategy profile
{\bar{\tau}}^{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{\faAnchor}}\emptyset}
, then the strategy profile
{\bar{\sigma}}^{\star}
outputs the vertex
{\bar{\tau}}^{{\dagger}i}(w)
, where
i
is the player controlling

, and shifts to the memory state
\mathsf{punish}_{i}
.

•

Otherwise, it outputs the vertex
{\bar{\tau}}^{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{\faAnchor}}\emptyset}(w)
and moves to the memory state
\mathsf{anchor}_{\emptyset w}
.

Anchoring state with one player to anchor.

We can now move to singletons, and define what happens in the states of the form
\mathsf{anchor}_{\{i\}v}
.
In such a state, we define the strategy based on the that gives player
i
exactly the extreme risk measure
z_{i}
.
More precisely, we want player
i
to receive payoff
z_{i}
with positive probability, and never leave the set of vertices
W_{\{i\}}
with probability
1
.
Using
Lemma

26
, there exists a positional strategy profile
{\bar{\tau}}^{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{\faAnchor}}i}
that satisfies that property from every vertex from which it is possible.
Note that that objective is in particular satisfiable, and therefore satisfied by
{\bar{\tau}}^{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{\faAnchor}}i}
, from every vertex
v\in W_{\{i\}}
.
Similar to the previous step, we define the strategy profile
{\bar{\sigma}}^{\star}
in the states of the form
\mathsf{anchor}_{\{i\}v}
so that it follows the strategy profile
{\bar{\tau}}^{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{\faAnchor}}i}
, remembers the last vertex that was visited and uses that memory to switch to the corresponding punishing state when some player
j
deviates.

Anchoring states with two or more players to anchor.

Now, let us consider the states of the form
\mathsf{anchor}_{Av}
, where
A
has cardinality at least
2
.
The existence of
\mathsf{anchor}_{Av}
implies that there is a history
h
such that
\Lambda(h)=A
.
Moreover, since each randomisation splits the label of histories in sets that have at most one element in common (Properties
1
and
2
), there is only one side of each split that can contain
A
, which implies that among such histories
h
, we can choose one that is a prefix of all others.
After history
h
, the histories labelled by
A
form a sequence
h,hv_{1},hv_{1}v_{2},\dots
which may be infinite, end in a terminal vertex, or end with a new split.

If that sequence end with a split,
then there is a longest history
hv_{1}\dots v_{q}
with
\Lambda(hv_{1}\dots v_{q})=A
and
k\geq 2
vertices
w_{1},\dots,w_{k}\in\mathsf{Supp}({\bar{\sigma}}(hv_{1}\dots v_{q}))
such that we have
\Lambda(hv_{1}\dots v_{q}w_{\ell})\neq\emptyset,A
for each
\ell
.
We can then define a simple history
h^{\prime}v_{q}
that also goes from the vertex
\mathsf{last}(h)
to the vertex
v_{q}
, with
\mathsf{Occ}(h^{\prime}v_{q})\subseteq\mathsf{Occ}(\mathsf{last}(h)v_{1}\dots v_{q})
.
We then define the strategy profile
{\bar{\sigma}}^{\star}
in each state
\mathsf{anchor}_{Av}
so that it follows the history
h^{\prime}v_{q}
and remembers the last vertex visited, and switches to the state
\mathsf{punish}_{i}
and follows the strategy profile
{\bar{\tau}}^{{\dagger}i}
when a given player
i
deviates and takes an edge that they are not supposed to take.
Moreover, when an edge is taken that does belong to the history
h^{\prime}v_{q}
, but not because a player deviated (it is then necessarily because of a stochastic vertex), the memory switches to the state
\mathsf{anchor}_{\emptyset v}
(where
v
is the last vertex seen) and immediately follows the corresponding strategy.
Finally, when the vertex
v_{q}
is reached and the memory is in state
\mathsf{anchor}_{A\mathsf{last}(h^{\prime})}
, the strategy profile
{\bar{\sigma}}^{\star}
chooses randomly between the edges
v_{q}w_{1}
, …, and
v_{q}w_{k}
, all with positive probability.
Such action will often be referred to as a

.

If that sequence is infinite or end in a terminal vertex,
then
\pi^{A}=v_{1}v_{2}\dots
is a play, and satisfies
\Lambda(h\pi^{A}_{<k})=A
for each
k
.
We can then consider a play
\pi^{A\star}
with
\mathsf{Occ}(\pi^{A\star})\subseteq\mathsf{Occ}(\pi^{A})
and
\mathsf{Inf}(\pi^{A\star})\subseteq\mathsf{Inf}(\pi^{A})
that is either a simple path from
\pi^{A}_{0}
to the terminal reached by
\pi^{A}
, or, if
\pi^{A}
is infinite, a simple lasso (i.e., a play of the form
h^{\prime}c^{\omega}
, where the history

is simple).
We can moreover choose
\pi^{A\star}
so that the set of vertices visited infinitely often (if there are any) in
\pi^{A\star}
is included in the set of vertices visited infinitely often in
\pi^{A}
.
Then, we can define
{\bar{\sigma}}^{\star}
in the states of the form
\mathsf{anchor}_{Av}
as following the play
\pi^{A\star}
, and remembering the last vertex seen.
When a player
i
deviates and takes an edge that should not be taken, the memory switches to the state
\mathsf{punish}_{i}
and follows the strategy profile
{\bar{\tau}}^{{\dagger}i}
.
Finally, when an edge is taken that does not belong to
\pi^{A\star}
but does not correspond to a deviation either, we switch to the state
\mathsf{anchor}_{\emptyset w}
where
w
is the last vertex seen, and to the corresponding strategy profile.

Initialisation.

The strategy profile
{\bar{\sigma}}^{\star}
has the state
\mathsf{anchor}_{\Pi\bot}
as the initial memory state.
In this state, it behaves exactly as in any state of the form
\mathsf{anchor}_{\Pi v}
, but without having memorised a last visited vertex
v
, since there is no such vertex.
From that memory state therefore, it necessarily reads the vertex
v_{0}
, and starts acting as described in the previous case.

The pure case.

In this construction, the vertices on which the strategy profile
{\bar{\sigma}}^{\star}
proceeds to an actual randomisation (i.e., the vertices
v
such that there exists a history
hv
such that the support of the distribution
{\bar{\sigma}}^{\star}(hv)
contains more than one element) are vertices on which
{\bar{\sigma}}
also proceeds to such a randomisation.
Therefore, if
{\bar{\sigma}}
is pure (i.e., if randomisations occur only on stochastic vertices), so is
{\bar{\sigma}}^{\star}
.

A combinatorial break: counting states

Now that the strategy
{\bar{\sigma}}^{\star}
is defined, let us bound the memory it uses.
There are, obviously, exactly
p
states of the form
\mathsf{punish}_{i}
, and one state
\mathsf{anchor}_{\Pi\bot}
.
To prove that there are at most
3np-2n
states of the form
\mathsf{anchor}_{Av}
, we need to prove that there are at most
3p-2
sets
A
such that there is a history
h
with
\Lambda(h)=A
.

Let us call
\Lambda
-anchored
all such sets
A
.
By analogy with strategies, we write
\Lambda_{\upharpoonright hv}
for the labelling that maps each history
h^{\prime}\in\mathsf{Hist}\mathcal{G}_{\upharpoonright v}
compatible with
{\bar{\sigma}}_{\upharpoonright hv}
to the set
\Lambda(hh^{\prime})
, and we will also use the notion of anchoredness for each of those labellings
\Lambda_{\upharpoonright hv}
.
We proceed by proving the following stronger result.

Proposition 34
.

For every history
h
compatible with
{\bar{\sigma}}
, if
\Lambda(h)
contains at least two elements, then there are at most
3|\Lambda(h)|-2
sets that are
\Lambda_{\upharpoonright h}
-anchored.

{claimproof}

For each history
h
, we write
f(h)
for the number of
\Lambda_{\upharpoonright h}
-anchored sets that have cardinal at least
2
.
There are
|\Lambda(h)|+1
subsets of
\Lambda(h)
that have cardinality
0
or
1
: the result will therefore be proved if we prove
f(h)\leq 2|\Lambda(h)|-3
.
The proof goes by induction on
m=\Lambda(h)\geq 2
.

Base case.

If
m=2
, the set
\Lambda(h)
is a pair
\Lambda(h)=\{i,j\}
.
Then, since the
\Lambda_{\upharpoonright h}
-anchored sets are all subsets of
\Lambda(h)
, the only set of cardinality at most
2
that is
\Lambda_{\upharpoonright h}
-anchored is the pair
\{i,j\}
itself, hence we have
f(h)=2\times 2-3=1
, as desired.

Inductive case.

If
m>2
, and if we assume that the result is true for every history
h^{\prime}
with
2\leq|\Lambda(h^{\prime})|\leq m-1
, then let
\{v_{1},\dots,v_{k}\}\subseteq\mathsf{Supp}({\bar{\sigma}}(h))
be the set of possible next vertices
v
such that
|\Lambda(hv)|\geq 2
.

If
k=1
, i.e., if
\Lambda(hv_{1})=\Lambda(h)
, then we have
f(h)=f(hv_{\ell})
and the result for
h
will be proved if we prove it for
hv_{1}
.
Following that reasoning, we can extend the history
h
until we are not in that case: if we always are, then the only
\Lambda_{\upharpoonright h}
-anchored sets are
\Lambda(h)
itself, and possibly the empty sets and some singletons, hence
f(h)=1
and the result is immediate.
We can therefore assume that
k>1
.

Let
i
be the player controlling the vertex
\mathsf{last}(h)
; we set
i=\bot
if
\mathsf{last}(h)
is a stochastic node.
Then, by Properties
1
and
2
of
Lemma

17
guaranteed during the construction of
\Lambda
, the sets
\Lambda(hv_{1})\setminus\{i\},\dots,\Lambda(hv_{k})\setminus\{i\}
form a partition of
\Lambda(h)\setminus\{i\}
.
Therefore, no set of cardinality at least
2
can be simultaneously
\Lambda(hv_{\ell})
-anchored and
\Lambda(hv_{\ell^{\prime}})
-anchored for
\ell\neq\ell^{\prime}
, hence the equality
f(h)=1+\sum_{\ell}f(hv_{\ell})
.
Now, since each set
\Lambda(hv_{\ell})
has at least
2
and less than
m
elements, we can apply the induction hypothesis to deduce:

f(h)\leq 1+\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}(2|\Lambda(hv_{\ell})|-3).

Moreover, we have
\sum_{\ell}|\Lambda(hv_{\ell})|\leq m+k-1
(each element of
\Lambda(h)
occurs in one of the sets
\Lambda(hv_{\ell})
, except possibly one that would occur in all of them), hence the inequality above becomes:

f(h)\leq 1+2(m+k-1)-3k

=2m-1-k

and since we have assumed
k\geq 2
, we obtain
f(h)\leq 2m-3
.

Let us recall that
\Lambda(v_{0})=\Pi
.
As a particular case of this claim, we obtain, if
p\geq 2
, that there are at most
3p-2
sets that are
\Lambda
-anchored, as desired.
In the case
p=1
, the game
\mathcal{G}_{\upharpoonright v_{0}}
is an MDP, and using
Lemma

26
, we can immediately construct
{\bar{\sigma}}^{\star}
as a positional strategy (which has therefore
1\leq 3n\times 1-2n+1+1
memory states) with the same risk measure as
{\bar{\sigma}}
.

The strategy profile
{\bar{\sigma}}^{\star}
has the desired extreme risk measures.

We now show that
\mathbb{X}({\bar{\sigma}}^{\star})=\mathbb{X}({\bar{\sigma}})
.
Let us recall that we defined
\bar{z}=\mathbb{X}({\bar{\sigma}})
.

Proposition 35
.

The strategy profile
{\bar{\sigma}}^{\star}
satisfies the equality
\mathbb{X}({\bar{\sigma}}^{\star})=\bar{z}
.

{claimproof}

Let
i
be a player: we want to prove that
\mathbb{X}({\bar{\sigma}}^{\star})=z_{i}
.
Let us first see how
z_{i}
has positive probability of being obtained in the strategy profile
{\bar{\sigma}}
, and we will then show that no larger (respectively smaller) if
i
is optimistic (respectively pessimistic) has a positive probability by the same strategy.

Player
i
gets payoff
z_{i}
with positive probability.

Let
A\subseteq\Pi
be one of the smallest sets (for the inclusion relation) containing
i
such that there exists a history
hu
with
\Lambda(hu)=A
.
Then, by construction of
\Lambda
, there exists a finite sequence of sets
\Pi=A_{0},A_{1},\dots,A_{m}=A
and of histories
h_{1}v_{1}w_{1},\dots,h_{m}v_{m}w_{m}
where for each
k
, the history
h_{k+1}
starts from
w_{k}
, the history
h_{1}v_{1}\dots h_{k}v_{k}w_{k}
is compatible with
{\bar{\sigma}}
, and we have
\Lambda(h_{1}v_{1}\dots h_{k}v_{k})=A_{k-1}
and
\Lambda(h_{1}v_{1}\dots h_{k}v_{k}w_{k})=A_{k}
.
We can then write
hu=h_{1}v_{1}\dots h_{m}v_{m}w_{m}
.

Consider the strategy profile
{\bar{\sigma}}^{\star}
, which initially follows the positional strategy profile
{\bar{\tau}}^{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{\faAnchor}}\Pi}
. This strategy profile generates, with nonzero probability, a history
h^{\prime}_{1}v_{1}
starting from vertex
v_{0}
to vertex
v_{1}
, based on our construction.
From that vertex
v_{1}
, it proceeds to a randomised action and, with positive probability, moves to the vertex
w_{1}
and switches to the positional strategy profile
{\bar{\tau}}^{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{\faAnchor}}A_{1}}
, and so on: there is, therefore, a history
h^{\prime}_{1}v_{1}h^{\prime}_{2}v_{2}\dots h^{\prime}_{m}v_{m}w_{m}
that is compatible with the strategy profile
{\bar{\sigma}}^{\star}
and after which the collective memory is in the state
\mathsf{anchor}_{Av_{m}}
, and plays accordingly.

Since
A_{m}=A
is the subset of
\Pi
where
i\in A=\Lambda(hu)
, we are in the case where the set
A
is no longer split further by our labelling. That is, there is a play
\pi
from
w_{m}
such that
\Lambda(h\pi_{\leq k})=A
for every
k
such that that is defined.
Then, in the construction of the strategy profile
{\bar{\sigma}}^{\star}
we have distinguished two cases: the one where
A
was a singleton, and the one where it had at least two elements (the empty case is excluded, since
A
contains the player
i
).

If
A
is a singleton,
then after the history
hu
, without any player deviating, all players are following the strategy profile
{\bar{\tau}}^{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{\faAnchor}}i}
.
By its definition, that strategy profile achieves the payoff
z_{i}
for player
i
with positive probability.

If
A
has at least two elements,
then after that same history, all players are following the play
\pi^{A\star}
, which yields the same payoffs as
\pi^{A}
.
However, we must still prove that player
i
actually gets the payoff
z_{i}
in
\pi^{A}
, and that the play
\pi^{A\star}
is generated with positive probability (i.e. that it does not cross infinitely many stochastic vertices—which we must first show for
\pi^{A}
).
We do so in the following claim, which we will use again later.

Claim 36
.

The play
\pi^{A}
is (eventually) generated with positive probability when the players follow the strategy profile
{\bar{\sigma}}
.
Similarly, the play
\pi^{A\star}
is generated with positive probability when they follow
{\bar{\sigma}}^{\star}
.
Both plays yield to each player
j\in A
the payoff
z_{j}
.

{claimproof}

Let
j\in A
.
Let us proceed by case disjunction according to the risk measure used by player
j
.

If player
j
is an optimist,
then, by Property
3
of
Lemma

17
, we have
\mathbb{X}_{j}({\bar{\sigma}}_{\upharpoonright h})=z_{j}
, and therefore
\mathbb{P}_{{\bar{\sigma}}_{\upharpoonright h}}(\mu_{j}=z_{j})>0
, i.e., by the law of total probability:

\mathbb{P}_{{\bar{\sigma}}_{\upharpoonright hu}}(\pi^{A})\mathbb{P}_{{\bar{\sigma}}_{\upharpoonright h}}(\mu_{j}=z_{j}\mid\pi^{A})+\sum_{k}\sum_{w\in E(\pi_{k})\setminus\{\pi_{k+1}\}}\mathbb{P}_{{\bar{\sigma}}_{\upharpoonright hu}}(\pi^{A}_{\leq k}w)\mathbb{P}_{{\bar{\sigma}}_{\upharpoonright hu}}(\mu_{j}=z_{j}\mid\pi^{A}_{\leq k}w)>0.

But using Property
5
, all the terms of the summation on the right are zero, hence the product
\mathbb{P}_{{\bar{\sigma}}_{\upharpoonright h}}(\pi^{A})\mathbb{P}_{{\bar{\sigma}}_{\upharpoonright h}}(\mu_{j}=z_{j}\mid\pi^{A})>0
is positive, i.e. the play
\pi^{A}
has a positive probability of being generated and
\mu_{j}(\pi^{A})=z_{j}
.

If player
j
is a pessimist,
then because of Property
5
again, for every
k\geq 0
and each
w\in\mathsf{Supp}\left({\bar{\sigma}}\left(h\pi^{A}_{\leq k}\right)\right)
, there exists a strategy
\tau_{j}^{kw}
such that
\mathbb{X}_{j}({\bar{\sigma}}_{-j\upharpoonright h\pi^{A}_{\leq k}w},\tau_{j}^{kw})>z_{j}
.
By composing all those strategies, we obtain a deviation
\tau_{j}
of the strategy
\sigma_{j\upharpoonright h}
; which, by Property
4
, satisfies the inequality
\mathbb{X}_{j}({\bar{\sigma}}_{-j\upharpoonright h},\tau_{j})\leq z_{j}
.
Therefore, either:

•

we have:

\min_{k}\min_{w\in\mathsf{Supp}\left({\bar{\sigma}}\left(h\pi^{A}_{\leq k}\right)\right)\setminus\{\pi^{A}_{k+1}\}}\mathbb{X}_{j}({\bar{\sigma}}_{-j\upharpoonright h\pi_{\leq k}w},\tau^{kw}_{j})\leq z_{j},

which is impossible by definition of the strategies
\tau_{j}^{kw}
;

•

or we have
\mathbb{P}_{{\bar{\sigma}}_{-j\upharpoonright h},\tau_{j}}(\pi^{A})=\mathbb{P}_{{\bar{\sigma}}_{\upharpoonright h}}(\pi^{A})\neq 0
and
\mu_{j}(\pi^{A})\leq z_{j}
, and then actually
\mu_{j}(\pi^{A})=z_{j}
.

We have thus proven that player
j
gets the payoff
z_{j}
in
\pi^{A}
, and that the play
\pi^{A}
is generated with positive probability in
{\bar{\sigma}}
.
The analogous results about
\pi^{A\star}
follow using the equalities
\mathsf{Occ}(\pi^{A\star})=\mathsf{Occ}(\pi^{A})
and
\mathsf{Inf}(\pi^{A\star})=\mathsf{Inf}(\pi^{A})
.

In those two cases (if
A
is a singleton or has several elements), we obtain that the strategy profile
{\bar{\sigma}}^{\star}
is such that, with some positive probability, player
i
gets the payoff
z_{i}
.

Player
i
gets risk measure
z_{i}
.

We still have to prove that player
i
has zero probability of getting a lower payoff (if they are a pessimist) or a higher payoff (if they are an optimist).
To show both cases, we prove the following claim:

Claim 37
.

Every payoff vector that has a positive probability of being achieved in the strategy profile
{\bar{\sigma}}^{\star}
also has a positive probability of being achieved in the strategy profile
{\bar{\sigma}}
.

{claimproof}

Let
\bar{z}^{\prime}
be such a payoff vector.
Then, there is a history
hw
compatible with
{\bar{\sigma}}^{\star}
and a set
A\subseteq\Pi
such that, after the history

, the strategy profile
{\bar{\sigma}}^{\star}
is in state
\mathsf{anchor}_{A\mathsf{last}(h)}
, and from that point it has a nonzero probability of achieving the payoff vector
\bar{z}^{\prime}
while staying in states of the form
\mathsf{anchor}_{Av}
.

If
A
is empty
, then the strategy profile
{\bar{\tau}}^{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{\faAnchor}}\emptyset}
has been defined as a strategy profile that almost surely generates a payoff vector that is generated with positive probability by
{\bar{\sigma}}
, from every vertex from which that is possible.
That requirement is satisfiable, and therefore satisfied by
{\bar{\tau}}^{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{\faAnchor}}\emptyset}
, from the vertex
w
, since that vertex is itself reached with positive probability in the strategy profile
{\bar{\sigma}}
.
Therefore, the payoff vector
\bar{z}^{\prime}
is also achieved with positive probability in
{\bar{\sigma}}
.

If
A
is a singleton,
say
A=\{j\}
, then the strategy profile
{\bar{\tau}}^{\scalebox{0.5}{\text{\faAnchor}}j}
has been defined so that from every vertex from which that is possible, on the one hand, it generates the payoff

with positive probability, and on the other hand, it is almost sure that the payoff vector that will be generated has also positive probability to be generated in
{\bar{\sigma}}
.
Similarly as above, that requirement is satisfiable from the vertex

, since
{\bar{\sigma}}_{\upharpoonright hw}
satisfies it.
Therefore, again, the payoff vector
\bar{z}^{\prime}
is also achieved with positive probability in
{\bar{\sigma}}
.

If
A
has at least two elements
, then the strategy profile
{\bar{\sigma}}^{\star}
stays in states of the form
\mathsf{anchor}_{Av}
only along one play, namely

, and that play generates a payoff vector that was also associated with the play
\pi^{A}
that by
Claim

, has positive probability to be generated in
{\bar{\sigma}}
, hence the same conclusion.

This proves the equality
\mathbb{X}_{i}({\bar{\sigma}}^{\star})=z_{i}
.

The strategy profile
{\bar{\sigma}}^{\star}
is an XRSE.

We have now constructed the finite-memory strategy profile
{\bar{\sigma}}^{\star}
, showed that it had the expected number of memory states, and that it generates the expected risk measures.
We must now give the final argument for our construction: that strategy profile is also an extreme risk-sensitive equilibrium.
We will prove that result by showing separately that optimists have no profitable deviations, and then that neither do pessimists.

Proposition 38
.

No optimist has a profitable deviation in
{\bar{\sigma}}^{\star}
.

{claimproof}

Let
i
be an optimist, and let us consider a deviation
\sigma^{\prime}_{i}
of that player from
{\bar{\sigma}}^{\star}
. Let us write
z^{\prime}
for the risk measure
z^{\prime}=\mathbb{X}_{i}({\bar{\sigma}}^{\star}_{-i},\sigma^{\prime}_{i})
.

Let us notice that along every play compatible with
{\bar{\sigma}}^{\star}_{-i}
, the transitions that are possible in the memory structure of the strategy profile
{\bar{\sigma}}^{\star}
can be classified as follows:

•

transitions among states of the form
\mathsf{anchor}_{Av}
for a fixed
A
;

•

Indeed, consider some payoff vector

h_{q_{t}}^{t}=t
in the graph
(V,F_{\ell})
.
Those paths define a directed acyclic graph (DAG)
D=(V_{D},E_{D})
rooted at
w
, where all non-terminal vertices have at least one outgoing edge, with
V_{D}\subseteq V
and
E_{D}\subseteq F_{\ell}
.
Now, since
\sigma^{\prime}_{i}
guarantees that no terminal vertex will be reached, each branch
h^{t}
of that DAG is such that there exists a (smallest) index
j
with
h^{t}_{j}\in W\cap V_{i}
, and
\sigma^{\prime}_{i}(h^{t}_{j})\neq h^{t}_{j+1}
.
It may be the case that
h^{t}_{j}\sigma^{\prime}_{i}(h^{t}_{j})\in E_{D}
, i.e., that from
h^{t}_{j}
, player
i
proceeds to an undetectable deviation and takes another branch of the DAG.
But that cannot be the case for all
t
, otherwise, there would be a branch
h^{t}
that would be followed with positive probability when following
({\bar{\sigma}}^{F_{\ell}}_{-i},\sigma^{\prime}_{i})
from
w
, and therefore a terminal vertex
t
that would be reached with positive probability, which contradicts the definition of
w
.

There must therefore exist an edge
uv\in F_{\ell}\setminus E_{D}
, with
u\in V_{D}\cap V_{i}\cap W

We will show that such an edge should have been removed during the final refinements loop.
First, it immediately satisfies Condition
1
.
Moreover, the vertex
u
is necessarily on a branch
h^{t}
of
D
that leads to a terminal vertex
t
, hence it satisfies Condition
3
.
Finally, since
v
is accessible from
w
in
(V,F_{\ell})
, the terminal vertices that are accessible from
v
in
(V,F_{\ell})
are all accessible from
w
in that same graph, and therefore are accessible from
w
in the DAG
D
.
Since
w
is accessible from
v_{0}
without visiting any vertex of
W
, and it particular without visiting
u
, it means that the terminal vertices accessible from
v
are also accessible from
v_{0}
without using the edge
uv
.
In other words, the edge
uv
satisfies Condition
2
, and should have been removed during the final refinements.

This case is therefore impossible: when the players follow the strategy profile
({\bar{\sigma}}^{F_{\ell}},\sigma^{\prime}_{i})
, it is almost sure that some terminal vertex will be reached, and that concludes the proof.

But now, if the strategy
\sigma^{\prime}_{i}
does use edges that do not belong to
F_{\ell}
, let us show that it cannot be a profitable deviation either.

Claim 50
.

If there is a history
hv
compatible with
{\bar{\sigma}}^{F_{\ell}}
such that
v\sigma^{\prime}_{i}(hv)\not\in F_{\ell}
, then we have
\mathbb{X}_{i}({\bar{\sigma}}^{F_{\ell}}_{\upharpoonright hv},\sigma^{\prime}_{i\upharpoonright hv})\leq z_{i}^{k}
.

{claimproof}

After such a history, the strategy profile
{\bar{\sigma}}^{F_{\ell}}_{-i}
follows the positional strategy profile
{\bar{\tau}}^{{\dagger}i}_{-i}
.
By definition of that strategy profile, we have
\mathbb{X}_{i}({\bar{\sigma}}^{F_{\ell}}_{\upharpoonright hv},\sigma^{\prime}_{i\upharpoonright hv})\leq\mathsf{val}(v)
.
On the other hand, the vertex
v
is accessible from
v_{0}
in
(V,F_{\ell})
, since it is visited with positive probability in
{\bar{\sigma}}^{F_{\ell}}
.
Therefore, it does not belong to the set
A_{k}
(if
k
is even) or
A_{k-1}
(if
k
is odd), and in particular not to the set
V_{\frownie}^{k}
or
V_{\frownie}^{k-1}
, which means that we have
\mathsf{val}(v)\leq z_{i}^{k}
.
Hence the conclusion.

In the general case, the payoffs that player
i
obtains with positive probability in the strategy profile
({\bar{\sigma}}^{F_{\ell}}_{-i},\sigma^{\prime}_{i})
are either obtained using only edges that belong to
F_{\ell}
, or by using an edge that does not: in both cases, we have shown that player

cannot get a payoff greater than
z_{i}^{k}
, which proves that the strategy profile
{\bar{\sigma}}^{F_{\ell}}
is an XRSE.

Algorithm

2
answers
\mathsf{Yes}
only on positive instances, and outputs in that case a succinct representation of an XRSE matching the constraints.

We will now prove the converse.

Proposition 51
.

Algorithm

3
recognises all positive instances.

{claimproof}

Let us assume that we have a positive instance, i.e., that there exists an XRSE
{\bar{\sigma}}
with
{\bar{x}}\leq\mathbb{X}({\bar{\sigma}})\leq{\bar{y}}
.
Let us show that the algorithm will answer
\mathsf{Yes}
.
To do so, we first prove the following invariant: if an edge is removed at some step
before the final refinements
, then it is never taken by the XRSE
{\bar{\sigma}}
.

Invariant 6
.

For each
k\geq 0
, every edge that has positive probability to be eventually taken in
{\bar{\sigma}}
belongs to
E_{k}
.

{claimproof}

We prove the invariant by induction.

Base case.

The case
k=0
is immediate, since we have
E_{0}=E
.

Further, at step
k=1
, if the strategy profile
{\bar{\sigma}}
uses eventually, with positive probability, an edge that does not belong to
E_{1}
, then it goes with positive probability to a vertex
v\in\mathsf{Attr}(V^{0}_{\frownie},E_{0})
.
Then, with positive probability, a terminal vertex will be reached that gives to some player
i
a payoff greater than
y_{i}
, which is impossible.
Therefore, such an edge cannot be taken in
{\bar{\sigma}}
.

Induction step.

Let us assume that the invariant is true until step
k\geq 1
, and let us show that it holds at step
k+1
.
Let
uv
be an edge that is used with positive probability when following
{\bar{\sigma}}
, and let us assume toward contradiction that it does not belong to
E_{k+1}
.
Since the invariant is true at each step until
k
, we can assume that
uv
has been removed at step
k
, i.e., that we have
uv\in E_{k}\setminus E_{k+1}
.
Then, we have
u\not\in A_{k}
and
v\in A_{k}
.
The strategy profile
{\bar{\sigma}}
has therefore positive probability of visiting the set
A_{k}
.

We must now distinguish the cases where
k
is even or odd.
If
k
is even, then the strategy profile
{\bar{\sigma}}
has therefore positive probability of visiting a vertex
v\in V_{\frownie}^{k}
.
If player
i
is the player controlling
v
, then that player has a deviation in which they get risk measure at least
\mathsf{val}(v)>z_{i}^{k}
.
Let us now note that when the players follow the strategy profile
{\bar{\sigma}}
, it is almost sure that a terminal vertex will eventually be reached, and that all the terminal vertices that have positive probability of being reached also have positive probability of being reached in
{\bar{\sigma}}^{E_{k}}
, since the invariant is true at step
k
: therefore, we have
z_{i}^{k}\geq\mathbb{X}_{i}({\bar{\sigma}})
, and player
i
has a profitable deviation after
v
, which contradicts the fact that
{\bar{\sigma}}
is an XRSE.

If
k
is odd, then, by definition of
A_{k}
, when the strategy profile
{\bar{\sigma}}
is followed, there is a positive probability of reaching no terminal.
But that is impossible in the cycle-averse case.

The invariant is therefore necessarily still true at step
k+1
.

We are now able to conclude.
The answer
\mathsf{No}
can be given in the two following cases:

•

If at step
k
, we have
v_{0}\in\mathsf{Attr}(V^{k}_{\frownie},E_{k})
.

Then, the strategy profile
{\bar{\sigma}}
visits the set
V^{k}_{\frownie}
with positive probability.
With the same arguments that were used in the proof of
Invariant

6
, that is not possible.

•

If during steps
k-1
and
k
, no edge is removed, but we have
z^{k}_{i}<x_{i}
for some player
i
.

Since
{\bar{\sigma}}
uses only edges of
E_{k}
by
Invariant

6
and reaches almost surely a terminal (since we are in the cycle-averse case), we have
\mathbb{X}_{i}({\bar{\sigma}})\leq z^{k}_{i}
, and therefore
\mathbb{X}_{i}({\bar{\sigma}})<x_{i}
: that case is therefore also impossible by definition of
{\bar{\sigma}}
.

None of those cases is possible, hence our algorithm will eventually answer
\mathsf{Yes}
.

Complexities.

We consider here the complexity of the two algorithms.
Since at least one edge is removed every two steps, there are
\mathcal{O}(m)

In each of them, we need

.
Hence, the complexity

In the cycle-averse case, when an output is asked, we need to add the final refinements loop: which consist of

2
, and
3

Hence the complexity
