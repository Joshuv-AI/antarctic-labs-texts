Case Report: Suspected Rupture of a Papillary Thyroid Tumor in a Patient With Graves’ Disease Who Experienced Airway Narrowing Without Respiratory Distress
Authors: Daniel Goodman, Samuel Bosco, Bradford Davison Smith
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis case report describes a 55-year-old woman presenting with acute dysphagia and neck swelling after a “popping” sensation in her neck. CT and MRI revealed a large retropharyngeal cystic mass and a left thyroid lobe mass, causing airway displacement. Despite significant effacement of the oropharyngeal and supraglottic airway on flexible nasopharyngolaryngoscopy, the patient remained asymptomatic regarding respiratory distress. Subsequent ultrasound-guided fine needle aspiration biopsy diagnosed malignant papillary thyroid carcinoma. Concurrently, previously undiagnosed Graves’ disease was identified. The clinical presentation was attributed to suspected rupture or hemorrhage of the malignant thyroid tumor, leading to a serosanguineous fluid collection and inflammatory edema. The patient was managed with high-dose steroids and intravenous antibiotics, resulting in improved airway edema. She never developed stridor or dyspnea throughout her hospital course and later underwent a total thyroidectomy with neck dissection. This case highlights the potential for a thyroid malignancy to present with suspected spontaneous rupture or hemorrhage causing airway effacement and raises the question of whether Graves’ disease, due to increased vascularity, put patients at additional risk of this presentation.Keywords: thyroid cancer, papillary, graves disease, airway obstruction, thyroidectomy

Introduction

Spontaneous rupture or hemorrhage of a thyroid nodule or malignancy is a rare but potentially life-threatening event. While the precise epidemiological incidence has not been established, a retrospective study of over 630,000 emergency department presentations identified only 59 cases of intrathyroidal hemorrhage over a ten-year period, with the majority managed conservatively and surgical intervention required in fewer than 15% of cases. 1 When hemorrhage does produce significant mass effect, airway compromise is the most feared consequence. A review of reported cases of thyroid hemorrhage causing airway narrowing found that the majority of patients required endotracheal intubation for worsening respiratory distress, with several requiring urgent surgical intervention.2-11 Spontaneous rupture of a thyroid malignancy is even less commonly described, and its co-occurrence with Graves’ disease — an autoimmune condition associated with increased intraglandular vascularity — has not been previously characterized in the literature.12,13 We present a case of suspected spontaneous rupture of a papillary thyroid carcinoma in a patient with newly diagnosed Graves’ disease, which produced marked radiographic and endoscopic airway effacement in the absence of any respiratory symptoms, and discuss the diagnostic and management considerations this atypical presentation raises.

Case

A 55-year-old woman presented to the emergency department with a chief complaint of dysphagia after she moved her neck in an unusual way while putting on a t-shirt and felt a popping sensation. She reported mild neck swelling and dysphonia, as well as a brief neck pain which quickly resolved on its own. At no point in her clinical course did she have any difficulty breathing. She admitted occasional palpitations and tremors over the past several months. She had not had a medical evaluation in four years. She had no notable medical history and was not taking any medication. She had no known allergies. Social history was unremarkable; she never smoked. She was a social drinker at most. She had no notable family history.

The patient was afebrile, hypertensive, and mildly tachycardic, but other vital signs were within normal limits (temperature 36.6oC, pulse 108, respiratory rate 20, blood pressure 175/86 mmHg, SpO2 99%). Physical examination showed a woman, appearing stated age, in no apparent distress, with minor symmetric edema of the anterior neck and coarseness of the voice. There was no stridor, dyspnea, or tenderness to palpation. On presentation to the emergency department, a complete blood count and comprehensive metabolic panel were drawn. The only abnormal lab value at that time was an elevated white blood cell count of 12.27 x 103/µL. Cytology and Surgical Pathology Summary

Because of her neck pain and associated dysphagia, dysphonia, and swelling, computed tomography with IV contrast of the neck was obtained. This demonstrated a large multiloculated cystic mass in the left retropharyngeal space extending from the level of the epiglottis to the thyroid with associated edema, measuring 15 x 6.5 x 5.5 cm. This collection may or may not have been contiguous with a 6 cm partially calcified left thyroid lobe mass with surrounding edema encasing the carotid arteries, displacing the airway toward the right. The overall impression was possible ruptured thyroid malignancy with surrounding inflammation versus possible superinfected mass with abscess. The thyroid mass and inflammation extended into the mediastinum (Figure 1).

After approximately 7 hours in the emergency department on the day of presentation, the patient was admitted to the general medical/surgical unit. The decision to admit was largely driven by the airway narrowing seen on CT. Otolaryngology – head and neck surgery consultation occurred on hospital day 1. Flexible nasopharyngolaryngoscopy was performed, which showed edema and ecchymosis of the left lateral and posterior oropharyngeal wall, and near complete effacement of the supraglottic airway by an edematous and ecchymotic left arytenoid and aryepiglottic fold, obscuring view of more distal structures. The airway was, however, patent (Figure 2). Based on the above findings, intubation was considered by the multi-disciplinary treatment team, but ultimately decided against, as the patient had no increase in respiratory effort, and no stridor. The patient was moved to the step-down unit for airway monitoring and continuous pulse-oximetry.

Magnetic resonance imaging was performed on hospital day 1, which identified an 11.2 cm mixed solid-cystic left neck mass with marked retropharyngeal and hypopharyngeal edema and a rim enhancing loculated 4 cm fluid collection in the left hypopharyngeal space partially surrounding the left superior thyroid cartilage. High-dose steroids (dexamethasone 8 mg IV every 8 hours) and intravenous antibiotics (ampicillin-sulbactam 3 g IV every 6 hours) were started, as well as reflux precautions, pantoprazole 40 mg IV daily, and famotidine 20 mg PO twice daily. She was made nil-per-os.

Given the presenting symptoms of palpitations, tachycardia, and hypertension, thyrotoxicosis was suspected. Endocrinology was consulted. A thyroid-stimulating hormone level was drawn and was undetectable (less than 0.01 mIU/L). Free T4 was 2.34 ng/dL, and free T3 was 3.8 pg/mL. Of note, this testing occurred within approximately 24 to 48 hours of iodinated contrast administration, and after initiation of high-dose glucocorticoids. Thyroid stimulating antibody levels were 333% of reference value, conferring a diagnosis of autoimmune thyrotoxicosis. The patient was started on methimazole 20 mg PO daily as well as propranolol 20 mg PO q12h due to persistent hypertension.

To further narrow the etiology of airway narrowing, and to potentially offer symptomatic relief via decompression, bedside ultrasound-guided fine needle aspiration was proposed. The benefit of diagnostic information and possible decompression was weighed against the potential risk of bleeding, particularly given the possibility of increased thyroid vascularity in the setting of Graves’ disease, and ultimately the care team and patient elected to proceed. Ultrasound-guided fine needle aspiration was performed on hospital day 2. This resulted in Bethesda VI papillary thyroid carcinoma.

From presentation until hospital day five, she continued without increased work of breathing or stridor. Nasopharyngolaryngoscopy was performed daily. By hospital day four, her airway edema had improved to the point that both vocal cords could be visualized. The left vocal cord was noted to be hemorrhagic but not edematous, and both vocal cords had full abduction and adduction. Throughout her hospital course, she never had stridor or dyspnea. Her diet was slowly advanced to regular. Laboratory workup showed primary hyperthyroidism and positive thyroid-stimulating antibodies. Outpatient follow up with endocrinology and head and neck surgical oncology was arranged.

On hospital day 5, she was discharged on amoxicillin-clavulanate 875-125 mg PO twice daily for 6 days, famotidine 20 mg PO twice daily, losartan 25 mg PO daily, methimazole 20 mg PO daily, methylprednisolone 4 mg PO dose pack, pantoprazole 40 mg PO daily, and propranolol 10 mg PO twice daily. Two months later, she underwent total thyroidectomy with central neck dissection and left level 2-4 neck dissection without incident.

Intraoperatively, the tumor appeared to be invading the overlying strap muscle. On the pathology report, the diagnosis was papillary carcinoma, classic subtype, with cystic degeneration, sclerosis, and inflammatory reaction. There was lymphatic invasion, but no evidence of extrathyroidal extension. The tumor was 10.5 x 6 x 4.5 cm and margins were clear of tumor. There were 9 lymph nodes out of 31 that contained tumor: 1 of 9 in the central compartment (largest deposit 1.0 cm, no extranodal extension), 7 of 8 in left level IIA (up to 0.5 cm, with microscopic extranodal extension), 1 of 6 in left level III (0.5 cm, no extranodal extension), and 0 of 8 in left level IV. Final pathologic staging was pT3aN1bM0 stage II papillary thyroid carcinoma.

At the time of her post-operative follow up 1 week later, her nasopharyngolaryngoscopy findings showed complete resolution of the previously noted edema of the arytenoid and aryepiglottic fold. Methimazole was discontinued at the time of thyroidectomy, as surgical removal of the thyroid gland eliminated the source of hyperthyroidism. She was then started on levothyroxine 137 mcg PO daily, with the intention of suppressing TSH to 0.1-0.5. Her post-operative thyroglobulin level was 2.0 ng/mL. Three months later she underwent radioactive iodine (RAI) treatment with 74.8 mCi of I-131 PO capsule. Her follow-up thyroglobulin one month later was less than 1 ng/mL; she has a repeat thyroglobulin scheduled for 6 months post-RAI.

Discussion

This case can be summarized as a 55-year-old woman with previously unknown Graves’ disease who likely experienced suspected spontaneous rupture or hemorrhage of a malignant thyroid tumor resulting in airway narrowing. The exact etiology of her airway narrowing cannot be determined from the available diagnostics. Based on CT and MRI, blood or serosanguineous fluid presumably exerted a mass effect in the paralaryngeal and parapharyngeal spaces possibly along with inflammation and edema of the surrounding tissues. Treatment with high-dose intravenous glucocorticoids was expected to decrease any inflammatory edema, especially after five days. The fact that there was still significant airway effacement after 5 days of treatment suggested that there was at least some element of hematoma or seroma. The presence of serosanguinous fluid in the fine needle aspiration supports a mixed etiology.

The exact incidence of spontaneous thyroid hemorrhage has not been documented in an epidemiological study, but it is widely agreed to be a rare occurrence. The number of case reports and retrospective studies detailing thyroid nodule hemorrhage has sharply increased due to the widespread adoption of radiofrequency ablation of thyroid nodules, for which rupture is a rare but well-known adverse outcome. 14 Fine needle aspiration may also rarely result in clinically significant hemorrhage. 4 In one retrospective study of patients at an Italian tertiary medical center emergency department, 59 cases of intrathyroidal hemorrhage were observed among the 631,129 total patients treated over a 10 year period. The most common symptoms were neck pain, dyspnea, and dysphagia. Only 6 of these patients were admitted and only one needed urgent surgery due to active bleeding. 7 patients had surgery within 6 months, with malignancy identified in only 3 cases. 1 Another retrospective review of ultrasonographic findings of 101 thyroid nodules identified 59 cases of intranodular hemorrhage. Ultrasonographic findings that correlated with intranodular hemorrhage included increased vascularity, spongiform content, and non-smooth margin of internal solid portion. 15

Patients with Graves’ disease are known to have increased vascularity within the thyroid gland. Histologic research has linked this finding to increased expression of vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF).12,16 This raises the question of whether increased intraglandular vascularity associated with Graves’ disease might predispose patients to intrathyroidal hemorrhage. While no direct correlation has been shown between incidence of intrathyroidal hemorrhage and Graves’ disease, numerous studies have characterized higher degrees of internal vascularity, and higher internal vascularity has been linked to increased likelihood of hemorrhage.13,15,17 Graves’ disease is a known risk factor for post-operative hematoma in patients who undergo thyroidectomy.11,18 Future retrospective or registry-based studies examining rates of spontaneous thyroid hemorrhage in Graves’ disease patients compared to euthyroid controls, as well as studies evaluating papillary thyroid carcinoma as an independent risk factor for spontaneous rupture, would be valuable in substantiating the hypothesis raised by this case.

Due to the close anatomic relationship of the thyroid to the larynx, intrathyroidal hemorrhage can lead to airway compression and edema from a combination of mass effect and inflammatory mediators. Numerous individual case studies detail airway narrowing from various causes of thyroid hemorrhage, including spontaneous bleeding, physical injury, or iatrogenic trauma. In 11 identified cases: 9 patients were intubated for worsening respiratory distress;2-10 6 were treated with partial or complete thyroidectomy;2-4,6,7,9 3 required surgical exploration with clot evacuation;8,10,19 1 was decompressed with a needle; while 1 resolved with conservative therapy.5,20

The approach for any patient with imminent airway compromise is to secure the airway by any means possible, whether by transoral/transnasal endotracheal intubation or surgical airway if necessary. Once the airway is secured and the patient is stable, imaging can be used to identify etiology. If thyroid hemorrhage is suspected, it is important to determine whether there is active bleeding. Neck expansion, worsening respiratory symptoms, and imaging findings can hint at active bleeding. Bleeding can be controlled with pressure, by addressing underlying coagulopathy, or by surgical exploration with cautery, ligation of vessels, or partial or complete thyroidectomy. In a patient without respiratory compromise, nasopharyngolaryngoscopy can be performed to assess for edema or effacement. If imaging shows thyromegaly or signs of a neoplastic process, thyroid function serology should be obtained. In cases of primary hyperthyroidism, follow up serology for thyroid-stimulating antibodies is appropriate. Neoplastic-appearing nodules should be biopsied when the patient is stable, according to Ti-RADS guidelines. 21 Malignancies warrant partial or complete thyroidectomy with or without neck dissection, radioactive iodine, and/or TSH-suppression as outlined by NCCN guidelines. 22

This case has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting its findings. As a single-patient case report, it cannot establish a causal relationship between Graves’ disease and spontaneous thyroid tumor rupture. It also cannot provide any estimate of incidence or generalizability. The thyroid function panel was obtained one to two days after intravenous iodinated contrast administration and initiation of high-dose glucocorticoids, both of which may transiently influence thyroid biochemistries. In particular, iodine loading in a patient with underlying autoimmune thyroid disease may have contributed to or unmasked the thyrotoxicosis via the Jod-Basedow phenomenon, and we cannot exclude this as a confounder. Finally, the mechanism of airway compromise, whether predominantly hemorrhagic, serosanguineous, or inflammatory, was inferred from imaging characteristics and FNA cytology rather than confirmed histologically, as no formal surgical drainage or clot evacuation was performed during the acute presentation.

Conclusion

This case details a woman with previously unknown Graves’ disease and papillary thyroid cancer who experienced suspected thyroid tumor rupture or hemorrhage with resultant airway narrowing, but with no respiratory symptoms. It highlights the potential for a patient with thyroid malignancy to present with airway effacement from suspected spontaneous rupture or hemorrhage. More research is needed to determine specific risk factors for spontaneous rupture of thyroid tumors, including whether Graves’ disease and papillary thyroid cancer are independent risk factors.
