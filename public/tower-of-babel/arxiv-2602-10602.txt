Learning Mixture Density via Natural Gradient Expectation Maximization
Yutao Chen, Jasmine Bayrooti, Steven Morad

Abstract
Mixture density networks are neural networks that produce Gaussian mixtures to represent continuous multimodal conditional densities. Standard training procedures involve maximum likelihood estimation using the negative log-likelihood ( nll ) objective, which suffers from slow convergence and mode collapse. In this work, we improve the optimization of mixture density networks by integrating their information geometry. Specifically, we interpret mixture density networks as deep latent-variable models and analyze them through an expectation maximization framework, which reveals surprising theoretical connections to natural gradient descent. We exploit such connections to derive the natural gradient expectation maximization (n gem ) objective. We empirically show that n gem achieves up to 10 × \times faster convergence while adding almost zero computational overhead, and scales well to high-dimensional data where nll otherwise fails.

Introduction

Predictive modeling is a central task in probabilistic machine learning, where one posits a conditional distribution
p(\boldsymbol{y}|\boldsymbol{x})
over the features
\boldsymbol{x}
and targets
\boldsymbol{y}
. For cases where
\boldsymbol{y}
is continuous, it is usually analytically convenient to assume that
p(\boldsymbol{y}|\boldsymbol{x})
is Gaussian. However, Gaussian distributions are unimodal and hence incapable of fitting
multimodal
distributions. An alternative is the Gaussian mixture model, which approximates arbitrary continuous smooth densities via a finite convex combination of Gaussian components.

\mathbb{E}[x]=0
while the Gaussian mixture is more flexible.

Building upon Gaussian mixtures, mixture density networks
(
3
)
constitute a family of flexible probabilistic models designed for fitting complex and multimodal conditional distributions. Specifically, mixture density networks leverage neural networks to map features
\boldsymbol{x}
to the distributional parameters of Gaussian mixtures that approximate the conditional density
p(\boldsymbol{y}|\boldsymbol{x})
. Mixture density networks are conventionally optimized via stochastic gradient descent by minimizing the negative log-likelihood loss
(
3
)
.

Mixture density networks are widely used for uncertainty-aware prediction and multi-valued regression, where an input
\boldsymbol{x}
may correspond to multiple valid outputs
\boldsymbol{y}
, e.g., speech synthesis
(
6
)
, inverse kinematics or forward dynamics
(
10
)
in robotics. However, despite being accessible in principle, the optimization of mixture density networks is often challenging, impeding practical adoption. Particularly, the loss landscape of Gaussian mixture negative log-likelihood is non-convex and contains many poor local minima
(
7
)
. Empirical studies
(
16
;
19
)
also reveal that mixture density networks are susceptible to training inefficiency and mode collapse, where Gaussian mixtures effectively degenerate into one singular Gaussian.

Our Contributions
We revisit mixture density network optimization through the lens of latent-variable models and expectation maximization (EM). We identify theoretical connections between EM and natural gradient descent for Gaussian mixtures, and exploit such connections to construct a tractable natural gradient EM (n
gem
) objective that yields fast and stable convergence by leveraging the information geometry of the underlying probabilistic models.

Empirically, we demonstrate that n
gem
effectively stabilizes and accelerates mixture density network optimization, achieving up to 10
\times
faster convergence with negligible extra computational overhead. We further validate the scalability of n
gem
with high-dimensional and practical datasets.

3.3
Natural Gradient EM

Drawing upon previous theoretical analyses (

To motivate the factorization perspective, we first rewrite the s

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\textnormal{n{gem}}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=

\displaystyle H\left(\left\lfloor\rho^{(\boldsymbol{x})}\right\rfloor,\boldsymbol{\pi}^{(\boldsymbol{x})}\right)

3.4
Interpretation

In this section, we provide further justifications to intuit how n
gem
exploits the information geometry to improve learning mixture density networks.

Recall that NGD penalizes the KL divergence between the distributions induced by a parameter update. As an example, consider two univariate Gaussians
p
and
q
with the same variance
\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{2}
. Their KL divergence between is given by

\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\|q)=(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{2})^{2}/(2\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{2}),

(23)

which implies that Gaussians with lower variances are more sensitive to parameter updates
7
7

7

A small change
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{2}
results in a large KL divergence.
, and vice versa. Our n
gem
partially works as an automatic learning rate scheduler that scales the gradients of Gaussian components proportionally to their variances
\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{2}
(
21
), allowing the model to quickly jump through high-variance regions while taking conservative steps in low-variance regions, leading to faster and more stable convergence.

Furthermore,
7
show that
all
local minima of the Gaussian mixture
nll
is a combination of
(a)
one component covering multiple ground-truth Gaussians and
(b)
multiples components converging to one ground-truth Gaussian. For the former case, the covering Gaussian must have high variance
\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{2}
to cover multiple modes, and n
gem
allows escaping such mode collapsing local minima by again amplifying the gradients proportionally to
\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{2}
.

Though orthogonal to our work, Gaussian mixtures have also been used as surrogate posteriors in variational Bayesian inference

6
Conclusion

In this work, we introduce natural gradient expectation maximization (n
gem
) for mixture density network
optimization
. We identify theoretical connections between EM and NGD to derive tractable natural gradient EM updates for MDN. Our proposed method n
gem
can stabilize and accelerate learning mixture density networks, incurs negligible computational overheads, while remaining easy to implement in practice (see
Appendix

C
).

Our empirical evaluations across a range of tasks – from synthetic examples to high-dimensional inverse problems – suggest that n
gem
is both effective and robust. We expect n
gem
to become a
drop-in
alternative to the standard
nll
objective for learning deep mixture density networks.

Limitations and Future Work
One of the fundamental assumption we rely on is the diagonal Gaussian covariance matrix, which is, although common in deep learning, still restrictive. An interesting future research direction would be deriving tractable natural gradient EM updates for Gaussian components with low-rank covariance approximations. Another future direction would be extending n
gem
to non-Gaussian mixture models within the exponential family.

Appendix A
Natural Gradient Descent

In machine learning, the gradient descent algorithm for minimizing some objective function
J(\boldsymbol{\theta}):\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is

\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t+1}\leftarrow\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t}-\beta\nabla J(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t}),

(24)

where
\beta
is commonly known as the
learning rate
.

One can show that the above gradient descent update can be derived from minimizing the first-order Taylor expansion of
J(\boldsymbol{\theta})
at
\boldsymbol{\theta}=\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t}
with an additional Euclidean distance penalty

\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t+1}=\operatornamewithlimits{\arg\min}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\underbrace{J(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t})+\nabla J(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t})^{\top}(\boldsymbol{\theta}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t})}_{\text{Taylor expansion}}+(2\beta)^{-1}\underbrace{\lVert\boldsymbol{\theta}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t}\rVert_{2}^{2}}_{\text{Penalty}}.

(25)

Therefore, we say that gradient descent implicitly assumes that the model parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}
lives in a
Euclidean space
by using a Euclidean distance penalty to constrain large updates. However, for probabilistic models
p(\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{\theta})
, the parameter space is generally not Euclidean. Instead, a more natural penalty term would be the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence between the induced distributions, resulting in the following optimization objective

\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t+1}=\operatornamewithlimits{\arg\min}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}J(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t})+\nabla J(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t})^{\top}(\boldsymbol{\theta}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t})+\beta^{-1}\underbrace{\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(p(\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t}\|p(\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{\theta}))}_{\text{Penalty}}.

(26)

Since
\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\|q)
does not have an analytical form generally, we instead consider its second-order Taylor expansion at
\boldsymbol{\theta}=\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t}

\displaystyle\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(p(\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t}\|p(\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{\theta}))

\displaystyle\approx\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(p(\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t}\|p(\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t}))

\displaystyle+(\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(p(\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t}\|p(\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{\theta}))\big|_{\boldsymbol{\theta}=\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t}})^{\top}(\boldsymbol{\theta}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t})

\displaystyle+\dfrac{1}{2}(\boldsymbol{\theta}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t})^{\top}(\nabla^{2}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(p(\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t}\|p(\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{\theta}))\big|_{\boldsymbol{\theta}=\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t}})(\boldsymbol{\theta}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t}),

(27)

where

1.

The first term
\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(p(\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t}\|p(\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t}))
is trivially
zero
.

2.

The second term is also
zero
because the expectation of the score function
\nabla\log p(\boldsymbol{x})
is zero.

\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(p(\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t}\|p(\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{\theta}))\big|_{\boldsymbol{\theta}=\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t}}=-\mathbb{E}_{p(\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t})}\left[\underbrace{\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\log p(\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t})}_{\text{Score}}\right]=0.

(28)

3.

The third term is
\dfrac{1}{2}(\boldsymbol{\theta}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t})^{\top}F(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t})(\boldsymbol{\theta}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t})
, where
F(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t})
is the Fisher information matrix (
6
) at
\boldsymbol{\theta}=\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t}
.

\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{2}\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(p(\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t}\|p(\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{\theta}))\big|_{\boldsymbol{\theta}=\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t}}=-\mathbb{E}_{p(\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t})}\left[\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{2}\log p(\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t})\right]=F(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t}).

(29)

Therefore, we have
\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(p(\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t}\|p(\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{\theta}))\approx\dfrac{1}{2}(\boldsymbol{\theta}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t})^{\top}F(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t})(\boldsymbol{\theta}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t})
. Plugging back the approximation and solving the new optimization objective

\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t+1}=\operatornamewithlimits{\arg\min}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}J(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t})+\nabla J(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t})^{\top}(\boldsymbol{\theta}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t})+(2\beta)^{-1}\underbrace{(\boldsymbol{\theta}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t})^{\top}F(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t})(\boldsymbol{\theta}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t})}_{\text{Penalty}}.

(30)

lead to the following natural gradient descent (NGD) update

\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t+1}\leftarrow\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t}-\beta F^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t})\nabla J(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t}).

(31)

Natural gradient descent yields the steepest descent on a Riemannian manifold with the Fisher information metric, which subsumes the standard gradient descent on the Euclidean space as a special case when
F(\boldsymbol{\theta})=I
.

Appendix B
Proofs and Derivations

B.1
Proof of
Proposition

3.1

We assume a probabilistic model
p(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{\theta})
with observed variables
\boldsymbol{x}
, latent variables
\boldsymbol{z}
, and parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}
.

We begin by rewriting the gradient of the observed-data log likelihood as follows:

\displaystyle\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\log p(\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{\theta})

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{p(\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{\theta})}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}p(\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{\theta})

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{p(\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{\theta})}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\int p(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{\theta})\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{z}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{p(\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{\theta})}\int\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}p(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{\theta})\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{z}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{p(\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{\theta})}\int p(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{\theta})\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\log p(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{\theta})\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{z}

\displaystyle=\int p(\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta})\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\log p(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{\theta})\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{z}.

(32)

Note that by definition
Q(\boldsymbol{\theta}\mid\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t})=\int p(\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t})\log p(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{\theta})\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{z}
. It is then clear that

\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\log p(\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{\theta})\big|_{\boldsymbol{\theta}=\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t}}=\int p(\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t})\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\log p(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t})\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{z}=\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}Q(\boldsymbol{\theta}\mid\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t})\big|_{\boldsymbol{\theta}=\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t}}.

(33)

B.2
Proof of
Corollary

3.2

We view the mixture density network
f(\boldsymbol{x};\boldsymbol{\theta})
as a latent variable model
p(\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{x};\boldsymbol{\theta})
with observed features
\boldsymbol{x}
and targets
\boldsymbol{y}
, latent variables
\boldsymbol{z}
, and network parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}
.

Following
Proposition

3.1
, with a trivial substitution of
p(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{\theta})
with
p(\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{x};\boldsymbol{\theta})
, we can similarly derive that

\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\log p(\boldsymbol{y}|\boldsymbol{x};\boldsymbol{\theta})=\int p(\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y};\boldsymbol{\theta})\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\log p(\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{x};\boldsymbol{\theta})\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{z}.

(34)

Recall that by definition we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\textnormal{{nll}}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=-\log p(\boldsymbol{y}|\boldsymbol{x};\boldsymbol{\theta}),

(35)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\textnormal{s{gem}}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=-\int p(\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t})\log p(\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{x};\boldsymbol{\theta})\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{z},

(36)

where
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t}
is the parameter used for evaluating the responsibilities
\rho^{(\boldsymbol{x})}
(
15
).

Therefore, it is clear that

\displaystyle\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{L}_{\textnormal{{nll}}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\big|_{\boldsymbol{\theta}=\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t}}=-\int p(\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t})\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\log p(\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{x};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t})\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{z}=\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathcal{L}_{\textnormal{s{gem}}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\big|_{\boldsymbol{\theta}=\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t}}.

(37)

B.3
Proof of
Proposition

3.3

Assuming the E-step (
9
) is exact, we consider the second-order Taylor expansion of the M-step objective
Q(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t})
(
10
)

\displaystyle Q(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t})

\displaystyle\approx Q(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t}|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t})

\displaystyle+(\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}Q(\boldsymbol{\theta}\mid\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t})\big|_{\boldsymbol{\theta}=\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t}})^{\top}(\boldsymbol{\theta}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t})

\displaystyle+\dfrac{1}{2}(\boldsymbol{\theta}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t})^{\top}(\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{2}Q(\boldsymbol{\theta}\mid\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t})\big|_{\boldsymbol{\theta}=\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t}})(\boldsymbol{\theta}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t}).

(38)

Assuming the M-step (
10
) is also exact, the gradient of
Q(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t})
at the maximum
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t+1}
must be zero

\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}Q(\boldsymbol{\theta}\mid\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t})\big|_{\boldsymbol{\theta}=\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t+1}}=0.

(39)

Replacing
Q(\boldsymbol{\theta}\mid\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t})
with its the second-order Taylor approximation, we have

(\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}Q(\boldsymbol{\theta}\mid\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t})\big|_{\boldsymbol{\theta}=\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t}})+(\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{2}Q(\boldsymbol{\theta}\mid\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t})\big|_{\boldsymbol{\theta}=\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t}})(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t+1}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t})\approx 0.

(40)

Note that because

•

\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\log p(\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{\theta})\big|_{\boldsymbol{\theta}=\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t}}=\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}Q(\boldsymbol{\theta}\mid\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t})\big|_{\boldsymbol{\theta}=\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t}}
as shown in
Proposition

3.1
, and

•

\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{2}Q(\boldsymbol{\theta}\mid\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t})\big|_{\boldsymbol{\theta}=\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t}}=\mathbb{E}_{p(\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{x};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t})}\left[\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{2}\log p(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t})\right]
by definition,

we can equivalently write as follows

\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\log p(\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t})+\mathbb{E}_{p(\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{x};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t})}\left[\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{2}\log p(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t})\right](\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t+1}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t})=0.

(41)

The term
\mathbb{E}_{p(\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{x};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t})}\left[\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{2}\log p(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t})\right]
is sometimes known as the
local
complete-data FIM. Simply taking the expectation on both sides w.r.t. a point mass
p(\boldsymbol{x})
over the observed data point
\boldsymbol{x}
yields

\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\log p(\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t})+\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{p(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t})}\left[\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{2}\log p(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t})\right]}_{-\hat{F}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t})}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t+1}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t})=0,

(42)

which can be rearranged as follows

\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t+1}=\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t}-\hat{F}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t})^{-1}(-\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\log p(\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t})).

(43)

B.4
Proof of
Proposition

3.4

Assuming that the complete-data log-likelihood
\log p(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{\theta})
is twice differentiable,
17
shows that generally the complete-data FIM
\hat{F}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
is block-diagonal.

In this section, we show that as a special case, a Gaussian mixture model
p(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{\theta})
with parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}=\{\boldsymbol{\pi}_{k},\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
has a block-diagonal
\hat{F}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
with exactly
K+1
blocks. We begin by writing down the complete-data log-likelihood

\log p(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{\theta})=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbbm{1}_{\{\boldsymbol{z}=k\}}[\log\boldsymbol{\pi}_{k}+\log\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{x};\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k})],

(44)

where
\mathbbm{1}_{\{\boldsymbol{z}=k\}}
is the characteristic function that returns 1 when
\boldsymbol{z}=k
and 0 otherwise.

Note that
\log p(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{\theta})
can be equivalently rewritten as

\log p(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{\theta})=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbbm{1}_{\{\boldsymbol{z}=k\}}\log\boldsymbol{\pi}_{k}+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbbm{1}_{\{\boldsymbol{z}=k\}}\log\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{x};\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k})=\ell_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\ell_{k},

(45)

where
\ell_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbbm{1}_{\{\boldsymbol{z}=k\}}\log\boldsymbol{\pi}_{k}
and
\ell_{k}=\mathbbm{1}_{\{\boldsymbol{z}=k\}}\log\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{x};\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k})
.

It is then clear that for any
1\leq i,j\leq K
and
i\neq j
, we have

\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\mu}_{i}}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\mu}_{j}}\log p(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{\theta})=\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\mu}_{i}}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\mu}_{j}}\ell_{i}=\boldsymbol{0},

(46)

because
\ell_{i}
is a constant w.r.t.
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{j}
. Similarly, we can show that

\displaystyle\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\mu}_{i}}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\mu}_{j}}\log p(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{\theta})

\displaystyle=\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\mu}_{i}}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{j}}\log p(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{\theta})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{i}}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\mu}_{j}}\log p(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{\theta})

\displaystyle=\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{i}}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{j}}\log p(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{\theta})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\mu}_{i}}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}\log p(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{\theta})

\displaystyle=\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{i}}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}\log p(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{\theta})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\mu}_{j}}\log p(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{\theta})

\displaystyle=\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{j}}\log p(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{\theta})

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{0}.

(47)

Therefore, we can conclude that the Hessian of the complete-data log-likelihood is block-diagonal as all the off-diagonal blocks are zero matrices as we show above.

\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{2}\log p(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{\theta})=\begin{bmatrix}\nabla^{2}_{\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{1}}\ell_{1}&\cdots&0&0\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&\cdots&\nabla^{2}_{\boldsymbol{\mu}_{K},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{K}}\ell_{K}&\vdots\\
0&\cdots&\cdots&\nabla^{2}_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}\ell_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}\end{bmatrix}.

(48)

Finally, note that by definition the complete-data FIM
\hat{F}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=-\mathbb{E}_{p(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{\theta})}[\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{2}\log p(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{\theta})]
, where

•

The expectation of
\nabla^{2}_{\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k}}\ell_{k}
for
k\in\{1,\dots,K\}
is

\mathbb{E}_{p(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{\theta})}[\nabla^{2}_{\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{1}}\ell_{1}]=\mathbb{E}_{p(\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{\theta})}[\mathbbm{1}_{\{\boldsymbol{z}=k\}}]\cdot\mathbb{E}_{p(\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{z};\boldsymbol{\theta})}[\nabla^{2}\log\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{x};\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k})]=\boldsymbol{\pi}_{k}\cdot-F_{k},

(49)

•

The expectation of
\nabla^{2}_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}\ell_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}
is

\mathbb{E}_{p(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{\theta})}[\nabla^{2}_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}\ell_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}]=\mathbb{E}_{p(\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{z};\boldsymbol{\theta})}\mathbb{E}_{p(\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{\theta})}[\nabla^{2}\log\mathrm{Cat}(\boldsymbol{z};\boldsymbol{\pi})]=-F_{\boldsymbol{\pi}},

(50)

with
F_{k}
for
k\in\{1,\dots,K\}
being the FIM of the
k
-th Gaussian and
F_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}
being the FIM of the categorical distribution.

Therefore, the complete-data FIM
\hat{F}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
of a Gaussian mixture is given as follows

\hat{F}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=-\mathbb{E}_{p(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{\theta})}[\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{2}\log p(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z}|\boldsymbol{\theta})]=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\pi}_{1}F_{1}&\cdots&0&0\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&\cdots&\boldsymbol{\pi}_{K}F_{K}&\vdots\\
0&\cdots&\cdots&F_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}\end{bmatrix}.

(51)

B.5
Fisher Information Matrix of Gaussian Mixtures

Consider a Gaussian mixture model with parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}=\{\boldsymbol{\pi}_{k},\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
. The log density is

\log p(\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{\theta})=\log\sum_{k=1}^{K}\boldsymbol{\pi}_{k}\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{x};\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k}).

(52)

The gradient of the log density is

\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\log p(\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{\theta})=\dfrac{\sum_{k=1}^{K}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}[\boldsymbol{\pi}_{k}\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{x};\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k})]}{\sum_{k=1}^{K}\boldsymbol{\pi}_{k}\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{x};\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k})}.

(53)

We can see that the gradient w.r.t. any parameter will also depend on all other parameters through the denominator term
\sum_{k=1}^{K}\boldsymbol{\pi}_{k}\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{x};\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k})
, which is not factorizable due to the log-sum term in
\log p(\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{\theta})
.

The interdependence structure of the log density gradient results in highly complex Hessian matrices, which is often undesirable and practically intractable to evaluate. Furthermore, one needs to compute the FIM (negative expected Hessian) via numerical integration, which exacerbates the intractability.

B.6
Fisher Information Matrix of Gaussian Distributions

Consider a Gaussian distribution with parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}=\{\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\sigma}\}
. The log density is

\log p(\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{\theta})=-\log\det(\mathrm{diag}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}))-\dfrac{1}{2}(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu})^{\top}\mathrm{diag}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{-2})(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu}).

(54)

The gradient of the log density is

\displaystyle\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\log p(\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{\theta})=\dfrac{\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu}}{\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{2}},

(55)

\displaystyle\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\log p(\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{\theta})=\dfrac{(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu})^{2}}{\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{3}}-\dfrac{1}{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}.

(56)

The Hessian of the log density is

\displaystyle\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\log p(\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{\theta})=-\dfrac{1}{\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{2}},

(57)

\displaystyle\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\log p(\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{\theta})=-\dfrac{2(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu})}{\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{3}},

(58)

\displaystyle\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\log p(\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{\theta})=-\dfrac{2(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu})}{\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{3}},

(59)

\displaystyle\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\log p(\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{\theta})=-\dfrac{3(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu})^{2}}{\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{4}}+\dfrac{1}{\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{2}}.

(60)

Note that because
\mathbb{E}_{p(\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{\theta})}[\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu}]=0
and
\mathbb{E}_{p(\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{\theta})}[(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu})^{2}]=\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{2}
, we can derive that

F(\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\mu}&\boldsymbol{\sigma}\end{bmatrix})=-\mathbb{E}_{p(\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{\theta})}\begin{bmatrix}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\log p(\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{\theta})&\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\log p(\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{\theta})\\
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\log p(\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{\theta})&\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\log p(\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{\theta})\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\dfrac{1}{\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{2}}&0\\
0&\dfrac{2}{\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{2}}\end{bmatrix}.

(61)

B.7
Fisher Information Matrix of Categorical Distributions

Consider a categorical distribution parametrized by logits
\boldsymbol{\psi}
such that the probabilities
\boldsymbol{\pi}=\mathrm{softmax}(\boldsymbol{\psi})
. Let
\boldsymbol{x}
be a one-hot vector describing the event outcome. The log density is

\log p(\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{\psi})=\boldsymbol{x}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\psi}-\log\sum_{k}\exp(\boldsymbol{\psi}_{k}).

(62)

The gradient of the log-density is

\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\psi}}\log p(\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{\theta})=\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{\pi}.

(63)

The Hessian of the log-density (for
i\neq j
) is

\displaystyle\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\psi}_{i}}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\psi}_{j}}\log p(\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{\theta})=-\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\psi}_{j}}\boldsymbol{\pi}_{i}=\boldsymbol{\pi}_{i}\boldsymbol{\pi}_{j},

(64)

\displaystyle\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\psi}_{i}}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\psi}_{i}}\log p(\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{\theta})=-\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\psi}_{i}}\boldsymbol{\pi}_{i}=\boldsymbol{\pi}_{i}^{2}-\boldsymbol{\pi}_{i}.

(65)

Putting together in matrix form, the Hessian

\nabla^{2}\log p(\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{\psi})=\boldsymbol{\pi}\boldsymbol{\pi}^{\top}-\mathrm{diag}(\boldsymbol{\pi})
. Therefore, we can derive the FIM

F(\boldsymbol{\psi})=-\mathbb{E}_{p(\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{\psi})}[\nabla^{2}\log p(\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{\psi})]=\mathrm{diag}(\boldsymbol{\pi})-\boldsymbol{\pi}\boldsymbol{\pi}^{\top}.

(66)

However, note that under this parametrization, the FIM
F(\boldsymbol{\psi})
is not invertible (one can verify that
F(\boldsymbol{\psi})\mathbf{1}=0
). Instead, we replace the inverse of
F(\boldsymbol{\psi})
with the Moore-Penrose pseudo-inverse in practice

F(\boldsymbol{\psi})^{+}=\mathrm{diag}(\boldsymbol{\pi})^{-1}-\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\top}.

(67)

Therefore, the complete-data natural gradient
\hat{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{\psi}}
w.r.t. the logits
\boldsymbol{\psi}
can be simplified to

\displaystyle\hat{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{\psi}}

\displaystyle=F(\boldsymbol{\psi})^{+}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\psi}}\log p(\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{\theta})

\displaystyle=(\mathrm{diag}(\boldsymbol{\pi})^{-1}-\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\top})(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{\pi})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{diag}(\boldsymbol{\pi})^{-1}\boldsymbol{x}-\mathbf{1}.

(68)

Appendix C
Implementation

We provide a reference implementation of our approach using the JAX
(
5
)
framework.

import jax
from jax.numpy import jnp

Dist = tuple["weight", "mu", "sigma"]

def mdn(x) -> Dist:
...

@jax.custom_jvp
def precond(dist: Dist) -> Dist:
return dist

@precond.defjvp
def _(primals, tangents):
dist, grads = *primals, *tangents
d_w, d_mu, d_sigma = dist
g_w, g_mu, g_sigma = grads
return dist, (g_w, g_mu * d_std ** 2, g_std / 2.0 * d_std ** 2)

def ngem_loss(x, y):
d_w, d_mu, d_sigma = jax.vmap(mdn)(x)
# Apply natural gradient preconditioning
d_w, d_mu, d_sigma = precond((d_w, d_mu, d_sigma))
# [E-step] compute responsibilities rho
log_weights = jax.nn.log_softmax(d_w, axis=-1)
# Standard Gaussian log probability for each component
log_normal = jax.vmap(gaussian_log_prob)(d_mu, d_sigma, y[:, jnp.newaxis])
log_rho = jax.nn.log_softmax(log_weights + log_normal, axis=-1)
rho = jax.lax.stop_gradient(jnp.exp(log_rho))
# [M-step] natural gradient descent
pi = jax.lax.stop_gradient(jnp.exp(log_weights))
return -(rho / pi * (log_weights + log_normal)).sum(axis=-1).mean()
