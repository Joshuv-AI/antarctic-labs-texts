Understanding Estrogen Circuit–Level Effects Important for Targeted Interventions in Psychiatric Disorders
Authors: Teresa A Milner
Published: 2026

In a recent article published in Biological Psychiatry: Global Open Science, LaChapelle-Sproat et al. (1) provide a timely review of the nonclassical role of 17β-estradiol (E2) as a rapid, membrane-associated neuromodulator of reward circuitry, showing that bidirectional effects create state-dependent windows of vulnerability for either major depressive disorder (MDD) or substance use disorders (SUDs). Drawing on anatomical, molecular, and behavioral studies, the authors review evidence that synapse-level E2 signaling can both dampen and amplify reward-related plasticity depending on hormonal state, thereby linking cyclical and life-stage fluctuations in E2 to opposing risks for mood and addiction. Characterizing E2 bidirectionally reveals opportunities for sex-specific mechanism-based interventions and reframes E2 as a dynamic circuit-specific gate for susceptibility and resilience rather than a static endocrine background factor.

Female sex remains a major risk factor for MDD, a point underscored by the recent special issue of Biological Psychiatry (2), “Women’s reproductive mood disorders across the lifespan” (volume 99, issue 9, 2026). Human imaging and transcriptomics studies further define sex-specific substrates of depression. Talishinsky et al. (3) reported sex-dependent gene expression patterns associated with abnormal functional connectivity in the default mode network (DMN), with enrichment for genes involved in synaptic function, immune signaling, and neurodevelopment. Notably, the prefrontal cortex and nucleus accumbens show pronounced sex-specific transcriptional changes in patients with depression. These findings are consistent with LaChapelle-Sproat et al.’s (1) emphasis on the fact that E2 fluctuations influence synaptic and circuit-level functions in these regions and suggest a mechanistic pathway by which hormonal state interacts with sex-specific molecular programs to shape depression vulnerability.

LaChapelle-Sproat et al. (1) indicate that although SUD prevalence is higher in men, women exhibit accelerated progression from initial drug exposure to drug dependence (“telescoping”). Moreover, recent issues of Biological Psychiatry, including those entitled “Mechanisms of food and drug reward” (volume 99, issue 6, 2026) and “Mechanisms and consequences of compulsive alcohol use” (volume 99, issue 1, 2026), highlight sex differences in reward and compulsive use (4,5). The importance of sex and sex hormones in shaping vulnerability was recently reported in a study of substance-naïve youth showing sex-specific network alterations associated with having a family history (FH) of SUD (FH+) (6). In particular, FH+ females showed heightened transition energy in DMN brain regions, including the medial prefrontal cortex and hippocampus, whereas FH+ males exhibited reduced transition energy in attention networks such as frontoparietal and temporoparietal regions, which may amplify reward salience. Conceptually, these findings suggest that family risk may bias females toward impaired braking of internal, context-driven states and males toward excessive acceleration in salience networks, distinct alterations that could both increase susceptibility to SUD (6). By examining hormonal receptor localization and rapid E2 actions in adult brain reward systems, the current review (1) provides important groundwork for testing how these hormonal mechanisms interact with sex-specific network vulnerabilities across adolescence and adulthood.

LaChapelle-Sproat et al. highlight that E2 actions in the hippocampus shape mood regulation and create windows of cognitive and emotional susceptibility to depression-like behaviors. Studies from my laboratory have also demonstrated that E2 fluctuations and chronic stress create similar windows that facilitate opioid-associative learning in the rat hippocampus [reviewed in (7)]. Briefly, the opioid peptide enkephalin and the mu opioid receptor (MOR) and delta opioid receptor (DOR) expression in female hippocampal circuits are organized under elevated estrogen states in a manner that enhances sensitivity to both endogenous and exogenous opioids, and chronic stress further alters enkephalin levels and MOR and DOR distributions in females in a manner that would promote opioid-associative learning processes. Conversely, stress-driven changes in the hippocampal opioid system are limited in males. These sex-specific changes in the hippocampal opioid system are accompanied by parallel changes in synaptic plasticity, stress- and kinase-related gene expression, glutamate receptor trafficking (8), and glial density (9), all of which may contribute to greater susceptibility of females to opioid use disorder relapse, especially after chronic stress (10). Finally, the review would be strengthened by more explicit consideration of hippocampal dopamine signaling. Our recent ultrastructural data show that dopamine D1 and D2 receptors localize to distinct presynaptic and postsynaptic compartments in hippocampal subregions (11), thereby providing a substrate for bidirectional dopaminergic modulation of excitation and inhibition that likely interacts with E2- and opioid-dependent plasticity to shape circuit-level susceptibility.

Menopause can differentially influence prevalence, symptomology, and treatment efficacy in psychiatric conditions including MDD and SUD. Historically, progress in menopause research has been limited by a lack of robust animal models, but the accelerated ovarian failure (AOF) model induced by 4-vinylcyclohexene diepoxide (VCD) [reviewed in (12,13)] now helps to fill key knowledge gaps in the understanding of menopausal-related hormonal changes in health and disease. The AOF rodent reproduces the irregular estrous cycles and hormonal fluctuations (especially E2) of perimenopause (peri-AOF) and the subsequent undetectable levels of E2, decreased progesterone, and elevated luteinizing hormone and follicle-stimulating hormone and androstenedione levels seen in postmenopause (post-AOF). The model’s predictable, reproducible time course permits rigorous, stage-specific comparisons that are difficult to achieve with other rodent models. Using the AOF model, our group has shown that peri-AOF and post-AOF hypertension are mediated by distinct hypothalamic mechanisms (12,13). Notably, hypothalamic modulation of peri-AOF hypertension involves primary estrogen receptor β and NMDA receptors, whereas post-AOF hypertension depends on primary G protein–coupled estrogen receptor signaling and AMPA receptors. Thus, AOF can be used to delineate menopause-specific mechanisms of depression and SUD. The circuit-level synthesis of E2 actions provided by LaChapelle-Sproat et al. (1) across hormonal states provides important guidance for such studies in all life cycle stages including menopause.
