SR-FSL: Sample reconstruction enhanced few-shot learning for real-time motor unit identification from surface electromyogram
Authors: Yunfei Liu, Zhaoyang Sheng, Dongfang Li, Xiang Chen, Ping Zhou, Xu Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDeep learning (DL) methods have demonstrated promising performance in online motor unit (MU) identification from high-density surface electromyogram (HD-sEMG). However, its dependency on larger amounts of data limits practicability.MethodsTo address this issue, a novel few-shot learning method enhanced by sample reconstruction strategy is presented for online MU identification. In this method, a spatio-temporal neural network was first pre-trained based on the simulated HD-sEMG data and MU spike trains, endowing it with initial representational capabilities for MU features. Then, an innovative sample reconstruction strategy was employed to generate physiologically interpretable synthetic samples for model fine-tuning with minimal experimental data. These samples were intended to enhance the model’s ability to characterize the spatiotemporal features of MUAP waveforms, thereby improving the online MU identification performance. Experimental HD-sEMG signals were collected from the abductor pollicis brevis muscles of ten subjects using an 8 × 8 electrode array.ResultsThe results demonstrated that the proposed method can achieve a matching rate of approximately 93% in online MU identification stage, with only 6 s of experimental data for offline model fine-tuning, significantly outperforming the comparison methods.ConclusionsThis work provides a novel solution for efficient real-time MU identification, and the findings are expected to advance the widespread application of DL-based real-time HD-sEMG decomposition in developing advanced neural-machine interfaces towards robotic motor control and rehabilitation medicine.

Introduction

Motor unit (MU), as the basic functional unit of the neuromuscular control system, plays a key role in transmitting movement intentions and controlling muscle contraction [1]. The contraction of muscle fibers is accompanied by the generation of MU action potential (MUAP) and the MUAP train (MUAPT). These MU activities reflect the real motor neural commands and can be effectively measured through electromyogram (EMG), which is the superimposition of multiple MUAPTs and acquisition noise [2]. Due to its non-invasive nature, surface EMG (sEMG) has been widely used in fields such as motor control [3], neuromuscular disease diagnosis [4] and rehabilitation engineering [5] etc. In recent years, the focus of sEMG technology has shifted from macroscopic to microscopic perspectives [6]. Compared to the coarse and uncertain nature of macroscopic sEMG signals, microscopic MU activity contains more detailed and precise motor neural commands [7], which are crucial for a deeper understanding of the mechanisms of muscle force generation and the neural control of human movement.

Owing to the development of high-density sEMG (HD-sEMG), blind source separation (BSS) techniques [8] have made it possible to decompose certain MU activities from HD-sEMG signals. Over the past several decades, numerous effective HD-sEMG decomposition methods have emerged [9], with notable examples including convolutional kernel compensation (CKC) [10], progressive FastICA peel-off (PFP) [11] etc. These methods offer a non-invasive approach to observing the behavior of human spinal motor neurons by revealing the spatiotemporal characteristics of MUs, such as firing pattern and spatial distribution. As the growing demand of real-time systems [12, 13], many researchers have developed online versions of the HD-sEMG decomposition methods. The online methods typically follow a ‘two-stage’ decomposition strategy [14], where the MU separation vectors (SVs) or MU filters are first initialized during the offline stage [15], and then directly applied to the HD-sEMG data stream during the online stage, enabling real-time extraction of MU spike trains (MUSTs) [16, 17]. Building on these technologies, an increasing number of studies have demonstrated that, compared to using macroscopic sEMG features, utilizing the decomposed MU activities significantly enhances the accuracy of muscle force estimation [18, 19], joint angle estimation [20], and gesture recognition [21] in motor control, while also providing new insights for the diagnosis [22] and rehabilitation [23] of neuromuscular diseases [24].

With the advancement of artificial intelligence technology, deep learning (DL) has shown significant potential for online HD-sEMG decomposition [25]. In contrast to the sensitivity of SVs to noise, DL model is capable of learning more generalized features from HD-sEMG features, thereby improving its robustness in online decomposition. In 2021, Clarke et al. [26] proved this point through applying data augmentation to bidirectional recurrent neural network (Bi-GRU), which was the first demonstration of the feasibility and potential of DL in online HD-sEMG decomposition. Another issue within SV-based decomposition methods is the high latency introduced by multiple necessary processing steps, such as signal whitening & extension and the extraction of MUSTs [26]. In contrast, the end-to-end DL models can bypass these steps and enable real-time MU identification with lower latency. For instance, Wen et al. [27] utilized a multi-output convolutional neural network (CNN) model to directly identify MU spikes from raw HD-sEMG signals, significantly reducing the online latency by roughly estimating the spike positions of MUs. This is a feasible strategy for practical applications as many methods focus on the firing rate (FR) of MU activities rather than the exact spike positions [28]. Furthermore, another major advantage of DL lies in its flexible pipeline design, such as data augmentation, advanced model architectures, and specific loss functions etc., which can further enhance the performance of HD-sEMG decomposition [29]. Recent studies have demonstrated improved performance in real-time MU identification using networks such as U-Net [30] and DRS-Net [31] etc. The aforementioned studies collectively highlight the immense potential of DL techniques in real-time HD-sEMG decomposition, which may further expand the application boundaries of microscopic MU activities [32].

Despite the good performance achieved by DL models, they often exhibit strong data dependency. The conventional SVs can be calibrated with only 5 s of HD-sEMG data [33]. However, the offline training of DL models requires 60 s of HD-sEMG data and its corresponding MUST labels [26, 27, 29–31], which is unacceptable in practical applications. This is because the offline signal collection, decomposition, and DL model training impose a significant burden on time. The losses incurred during the offline stage may outweigh the performance improvements during the online stage. The inherent data dependency becomes a significant limitation of DL methods [34] and hinders their further application in HD-sEMG decomposition. Currently, no research has focused on addressing this issue, which represents a critical research gap that requires urgent attention.

With above considerations, this study proposes a novel few-shot learning approach for real-time MU identification. The proposed approach fully leverages prior knowledge of MU activity to facilitate the training process of the DL model. The simulated HD-sEMG signals are first utilized to pre-train the DL model for promoting its ability to learn global knowledge related to MU identification. Then, the feature extractor of the pre-trained DL model is transferred and further fine-tuned with limited experimental data. In this process, an innovative sample reconstruction strategy is introduced to promote the DL model to learn generalizable spatiotemporal features of MUAPs. This study emphasizes the importance of utilizing physiological prior knowledge of MU activities to guide the training process of DL model, contributing to the potential application of DL-based online decomposition methods in fields such as robotic motor control and rehabilitation medicine etc.

Methods

The overall methodological principle of this study is illustrated in Fig. 1. All details are described as follows.

Experimental data collection

Subjects

This study recruited ten adult subjects (age: 25.10 ± 4.10, mean ± standard deviation; eight males and two females, all right-handed), all of whom were free from neuromuscular disorders. Before participating, all subjects were provided with detailed information about the experiment and gave their written informed consent. The experimental protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the University of Science and Technology of China (Hefei, Anhui, China), under Application No. 2022 N(H)−163, in February 2022.

Experiments

Fig. 2(a) Illustration of the experimental setup. (b) Representative example of experimental HD-sEMG and force signals, as well as the partial MUSTs obtained using the offline PFP method

(a) Illustration of the experimental setup. (b) Representative example of experimental HD-sEMG and force signals, as well as the partial MUSTs obtained using the offline PFP method

A thumb abduction task was employed to capture HD-sEMG signals from the abductor pollicis brevis (APB) muscle of the dominant hand in all subjects, given its common application in sEMG research [18, 35]. The experimental setup for simultaneous force and HD-sEMG signal recording during thumb abduction is shown in Fig. 2a. HD-sEMG signals were acquired using a flexible electrode array composed of 64 monopolar electrodes (2 mm in diameter, 4 mm inter-electrode spacing), arranged in an 8 × 8 grid, and a custom-built multi-channel sEMG recording system. The system featured a two-stage amplifier with a total gain of 60 dB (AD8200, Analog Devices; OPA349, Texas Instruments), a band-pass filter ranging from 20 to 500 Hz, and a 16-bit analog-to-digital converter sampling at 2 kHz (ADS1198, Texas Instruments). A reference electrode was placed on the dorsum of the hand.

The hand was stabilized using custom-designed 3D-printed components to isolate the thumb and minimize interference from other muscles. Force measurement was achieved with a load cell (LDST-V-HY, Luckly Inc., Beijing, China) attached to a thumb positioning ring. Before initiating the force task, subjects were instructed to perform thumb abduction at their maximal force, with three repetitions to determine the maximum voluntary contraction (MVC). The force task was designed to gradually increase from 0 to a target level over 2 s, maintain that force for 3 s, and then return to baseline within 1 s. This sequence was repeated five times for each force level, with target force levels set at 20% and 30% MVC. A rest period of approximately 5 min was provided between both force levels to mitigate muscle fatigue. In total, 60 s of HD-sEMG data were collected per subject (2 force levels × 5 repetitions × 6 s), which were then stored on a computer (CPU: 12th Gen Intel(R) Core(TM) i5-12400, RAM: 16 GB, GPU: NVIDIA RTX 3090) and analyzed using MATLAB (version R2022b, MathWorks, Natick, USA) and PyTorch platform.

Data pre-processing

Fig. 3(a) Illustration of the data slicing, label transformation and network structures of the MU classifier and feature extractor. (b) Illustration of the process of DL model pre-training, model transfer and fine-tuning

(a) Illustration of the data slicing, label transformation and network structures of the MU classifier and feature extractor. (b) Illustration of the process of DL model pre-training, model transfer and fine-tuning

First, some necessary digital filters including a Butterworth bandpass filter set between 20 and 500 Hz and a notch filter centered at 50 Hz (49–51 Hz) were applied on experimental HD-sEMG signals to eliminate unwanted frequency components. Next, channels with excessive noise were discarded and were then restored using linear interpolation based on the sEMG signals in neighboring channels. Subsequently, the well-established offline PFP method [11] was applied to decompose the HD-sEMG data into MUSTs, as shown in Fig. 2b. The reliability of MUSTs was evaluated through several metrics such as coefficient of variation of spike amplitudes (CoVamp) and inter-spike intervals (CoVisi), and the mean discharge rate (MDR) [36]. In this study, only those MUSTs that met the criteria of CoVamp < 0.3, CoVisi < 0.4, and MDR < 35 Hz were retained for further analysis. The reliable MUSTs were utilized both as labels for training the DL model and as references for evaluating the online decomposition performance produced by the DL model on the testing data.

Deep learning model

The DL model was employed to identify the firing state of individual MUs within a given HD-sEMG input frame. As shown in Fig. 3a, the HD-sEMG data and its corresponding MUSTs were sliced into frames with a window length of L and a step size of S. In this study, the L and S were set to 40 and 5 data points, respectively. Some pretests revealed that such choices represent the optimal trade-off between accuracy and real-time performance [37]. The MUST frames were then converted into firing labels to supervise or evaluate the DL [27] model’s outputs, as shown in Fig. 3a. The firing label for the i- th MU in the j- th HD-sEMG frame was set to positive only if one spike from that MU was present in that frame. Please note that the occurrence of multiple spikes of one MU within a single MUST frame can be minimized when the frame length L was set to 40 (about 20 ms). In this case, the model learns to transform an input matrix of dimension \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:M\times\:L$$\end{document} into a binary output of size \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:N\times\:1$$\end{document}, where M denotes the number of HD-sEMG channels and N corresponds to the number of MUs to be identified. This is a typical multi-label classification task [29], where an input HD-sEMG frame may contain multiple positive MU labels, indicating the concurrent firing of multiple MUs within that HD-sEMG frame. In addition, the output size N corresponds to the number of MUs decomposed by the offline decomposition using the PFP method at each force level for each subject. In other words, all MUs obtained during the offline decomposition stage were used as prediction targets in both DL model training and testing on unseen real-time data streams, without any additional selection or exclusion.

The model structure consists of a feature extractor and a MU classifier, as shown in Fig. 3a. The feature extractor is composed of a hybrid network, which includes a CNN network for spatial feature extraction and a Bi-GRU network for temporal feature characterizing, both of which have been proven effective for HD-sEMG decomposition in previous studies. The CNN network consists of 16 convolutional kernels with a size of 3 × 3, a stride of 1, and zero padding of (1) It extracts spatial feature map of size \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:L\times\:16\times\:8\times\:8$$\end{document} from the reshaped HD-sEMG frame of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:L\times\:1\times\:8\times\:8$$\end{document}, which are then flattened and batch normalized. The normalized feature map of size \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:L\times\:1024$$\end{document} is further input into the Bi-GRU network for temporal feature extraction, which has a hidden size of 128 and layer number of 2. The obtained feature map is then passed through a ReLU activation function, a dropout layer with a rate of 0.5 and a flatten layer to serve as the output of the feature extractor. The MU classifier consists of two linear layers, which are utilized to identify the firing of individual MUs from the output of feature extractor. Each linear layer reduces its dimensionality to 128 and N, respectively. Finally, the outputs of the DL model are obtained by applying a sigmoid activation function, which indicates the probability of each MU firing within the HD-sEMG frame.

Simulated data generation and model pre-training

The mechanism from MU activities to the formation of HD-sEMG has been well described by existing neurophysiology models [38]. This forward modeling approach provides a deeper understanding of how the neuromuscular control system functions. More importantly, it can also aid in training DL models to learn how to reverse-engineer MU activities from HD-sEMG signals. Thus, a transfer learning approach was adopted in this study, which utilized simulated HD-sEMG data for DL model pre-training and experimental data for model fine-tuning, as shown in Fig. 3b. A previously established MU to HD-sEMG simulation model [16] was utilized to generate training dataset for pre-training the DL model.

Figure 4a illustrates the detailed process of the simulation. It begins with neural excitation from the central nervous system, which triggers the recruitment and firing of MUs in the MU pool. A total of 120 MUs were simulated in the MU pool according to previous studies [16, 18, 38]. The size and recruitment threshold (RT) of MUs follow an exponential distribution, where many small MUs have lower RTs, while a few large MUs have higher RTs. Each MU is recruited and discharged once the excitation exceeded its RT. The FR encoding method assigns the same minimum FR of 8 Hz to all MUs, with the first MU (lowest RT) and the last MU (highest RT) receiving peak FR of 25 Hz and 35 Hz, respectively. The MUAP model includes a three-dimensional coordinate system to generate two-dimensional (2D) MUAPs for each MU. The depths of MUs were simulated to be a random number that had a positive relationship with the fiber numbers. To maintain consistency with the experimental setup, the simulated HD-sEMG data was also configured to be recorded using the same surface electrode array as in the experiment. Similarly, the neural excitation curve is consistent with the experimental force curve, with the maximum excitation level set to 30%. As shown in Fig. 4b, these settings maximize the consistency between the simulated and the experimental HD-sEMG signals, thereby facilitating the model’s knowlege transfer to experimental scenarios. A total of 60 s of HD-sEMG data with 30 dB signal-to-noise ratio was simulated (10 repetitions).

The simulated HD-sEMG data and the MUSTs obtained using PFP were sliced into frames (length L, step size S) and were then used to form input-label pairs for pre-training the DL model. The commonly adopted binary cross-entropy (BCE) loss and Adam optimizer with an initial learning rate of 0.001 and a weight decay of 0.001 were utilized. The batch size was set to 256 and the maximum number of training epochs was set to 200. Early stopping was applied if the training loss failed to improve over 30 consecutive epochs. These hyper-parameters utilized in this study were optimized and selected through the grid search approach. The pre-trained DL model parameters were saved for further analysis.

Experimental sample reconstruction and model fine-tuning

Due to the differences in the MUAP 2D waveforms and the number of MUs to be identified, it is not feasible to directly transfer the above model to experimental data. Thus, model fine-tuning base on experimental data is necessary to improve the performance. In this case, it is desirable to achieve rapid model transfer based on a small amount of experimental data, as this is crucial for reducing the burden of offline decomposition and model training. However, the limited amount of data used for fine-tuning inevitably leads to issues with weak generalization. To address this problem, a novel sample reconstruction strategy is employed to generate synthetic HD-sEMG and MUST pairs for mode fine-tuning in this study.

Figure 5 illustrates the detailed process of the proposed sample reconstruction strategy. The limited experimental HD-sEMG data and its corresponding MUSTs are sliced into multiple frames/samples using a sliding window with the length of L and step size S. The above reconstruction process is applied to all HD-sEMG frames with spike labels to generate the corresponding synthetic samples. It can be observed in Fig. 5 that the synthetic HD-sEMG exhibits different morphology compared to the raw HD-sEMG, primarily reflected in the position of the MUAP waveforms. Nevertheless, they represent similar MU activities, i.e., both MU1 and MU2 fire simultaneously within the given sample. It also means the raw HD-sEMG sample and synthetic HD-sEMG sample have the identical labels. When both samples are used for model fine-tuning, the differences in morphology assist the model in extracting universally applicable MU features. Besides, the influence of synthetic sample size on model performance is explored in subsequent sections.

To address the issue of variable output dimension (the difference in the number of MUs), the parameters of MU classifier in pre-trained DL model were re-initialized (Fig. 4b), and only the parameters of feature extractor were saved as the initial parameters to preserve the initial expressive capability of MU features. The DL model was fine-tuned utilizing the small amount of experimental samples and the synthetic samples generated through the proposed sample reconstruction strategy. The batch size was reduced to 64 to enhance the model’s generalization ability. The remaining training settings were identical to those of the pre-training process.

Performance evaluation

The fine-tuned DL model is capable of real-time identification of MU activities from the experimental data stream. To evaluate the performance of the proposed method, only one repetition (6 s) at each force level from each subject was used for model fine-tuning, while the remaining four repetitions (24 s) were used for online testing. That is, the DL model was fine-tuned and tested at each force level for one subject. During the online testing stage, the HD-sEMG data stream was standardized and segmented into a sequence of temporally overlapping windows (length L, step size S). These windows were sequentially input into the DL model for MU identification. Due to the lack of ground truth labels in the experiments, the references were obtained from the offline decomposed MUSTs [16]. It is worth mentioning that the MR metric is essentially an F1-score metric, which measures the level of agreement between the identification results and the references.

The effect of pre-training with different lengths of simulated data on the experimental performance of DL model was first evaluated. This helps in selecting the optimal data length for model pre-training, thus achieving a balance between performance and training workload. A total of 6 data lengths were tested, ranging from 10 s to 60 s, with intervals of 10 s. The pre-trained model was fine-tuned using one repetition of experimental data and tested using the remaining repetitions. The optimal data length for pre-training was selected for further analysis.

In terms of the proposed sample reconstruction strategy, the effect of different shift range D on the performance of DL model was evaluated. In this process, the number of synthetic samples was equal to that of the experimental samples, which means the ratio of experimental samples to synthetic samples in the fine-tuning dataset was 1:1. A total of 8 shift ranges were compared, ranging from 5 data points to 40 data points, with an interval of 5 data points. It is worth noting that if an inappropriate shift \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\epsilon\:}_{i,j}$$\end{document} is generated, for example, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\triangle{t}_{i,j}+{\epsilon\:}_{i,j}$$\end{document} exceeds the sample length (40 data points), it is deleted and re-generated. After the selection of optimal shift range D, the effect of synthetic sample size on the performance of DL model was further evaluated, where the ratio of experimental samples to synthetic samples in the fine-tuning dataset was set to 1:1, 1:2, 1:3 and 1:4, respectively.

After the parameter optimization, three methods were selected for performance comparison. The first method (termed CNN-BiGRU method) does not use any pre-training or sample reconstruction strategies, representing the conventional approach for MU identification based on temporal [26] or spatial [27] networks. The second method (termed Transfer method) solely utilizes the simulated data for model pre-training, representing a previously mentioned digital twin method [34]. The third method (termed Reconstruction method) solely uses the sample reconstruction strategy for generating synthetic samples, representing a method in ablation study. The DL model structure utilized in the comparison methods is identical to that of the proposed method. This is because the focus of this study is not on exploring the optimal model structure. It should be noted that the first two comparison methods can be regarded as ablations of the proposed method, and therefore, no additional ablation experiments are necessary to implement.

To evaluate the influence of different parameters (pre-training data length, shift range and synthetic sample size) and different methods on real-time MU identification performance, four separate one-way repeated-measure ANOVA was conducted on the MR metric, with the parameter values (6 levels for pre-training data length, 8 levels for shift range and 4 levels for synthetic sample size) or methods (four levels: CNN-BiGRU, Transfer, Reconstruction and the proposed method) considered as within-subject factors. Results

Parameter optimization

A total of 8 reliable MUs were obtained from the simulated HD-sEMG data through the offline PFP method. These MUs served as labels to pre-train the DL model, providing it with the initial capability to identify MUs. In the experimental dataset, an average of 10.2 ± 1.93 reliable MUs were identified per subject across both force levels, with averages of 4.9 ± 1.45 and 5.3 ± 0.95 at 20% MVC and 30% MVC, respectively.

Figure 6a explores the impact of different pre-training data lengths on the experimental MU identification performance, when using 6 s experimental data for DL model fine-tuning. It can be observed that as the data length increases, the identification performance gradually improves. After the pre-training data length exceeds 30 s, the improvement in the MR metric diminishes. A similar trend can be observed across the force level of 20%MVC, 30%MVC. When considering all the force levels, the one-way repeated-measure ANOVA on MR metric reported the main effect of the pre-training data length (F = 26.813, p < 0.001). Pairwise comparisons further indicated that the optimal performance was achieved when the pre-training data length was set to 50 s. Further increasing the data length no longer leads to performance improvements. Figure 6b illustrates the effect of different pre-training data lengths on the model’s training time. As the data length increased, the training time rose significantly. Nevertheless, this increase in time consumption did not substantially add to the time cost in DL model’s application in experimental signals. Therefore, a pre-training data length of 50 s was selected for further analysis.

Online MU identification

After the parameter optimization, the optimal settings were 50 s for pre-training data length, 30 data points for shift range D, and 1:3 for the ratio of raw HD-sEMG samples to synthetic samples. In this case, Fig. 7a presents representative examples of synthetic samples and their corresponding raw sample. It can be observed that, in channels with lower MUAP amplitudes, the waveform of the synthetic samples closely resembles that of the raw sample. However, in channels with higher MUAP amplitudes, the synthetic samples exhibit different waveform patterns compared to the raw sample. Additionally, the synthetic samples still retain the amplitude distribution patterns of the raw sample, which can be observed from the peak-to-peak spatial distribution. Figure 7b presents the feature distribution of a representative MU’s positive samples after principal component analysis (PCA), including the training/fine-tuning samples, synthetic samples, and testing samples. It is observed that the number of training samples is much smaller compared to the testing samples. Moreover, the testing samples exhibit diverse feature distribution, encompassing many samples with features distinct from those of the training samples. After applying the proposed sample reconstruction strategy, the synthetic samples enrich the feature distribution of the training dataset, while remaining well within the distribution range of both the training and testing samples, without generating any outlier samples that would disrupt the original feature distribution. The closely aligned centers and similar shapes of the covariance ellipses suggest that the synthetic samples exhibit a distribution in the PCA-reduced feature space that is broadly consistent with those of the training and testing data. Moreover, the covariance ellipse of the synthetic samples appears closer to that of the testing samples than to that of the training samples, which is consistent with the hypothesis that the synthetic samples may help reduce the distribution mismatch between the training and testing data.

Discussions

The online identification of MU activities based on DL methods is a promising approach [25]. It should be acknowledged that the proposed method still requires 50 s of simulated data for DL model pre-training. Therefore, the overall data requirement of the complete workflow, including both pre-training and fine-tuning, remains comparable to that of previously reported methods. Nevertheless, the pre-training stage in the proposed framework is performed only once and the resulting pre-trained model can be saved and then applied to all new subjects and experimental scenarios, which substantially reduces the practical burden of DL-based decomposition methods in real-world applications. Compared with previous studies that utilize 60 s of experimental HD-sEMG data for model training [26, 27, 29–31], the proposed method achieves good real-time MU identification performance (approximately 93% revealed by MR) with only 6 s of experimental data for model fine-tuning. From the perspective of real-world deployment, the proposed method therefore substantially alleviates the dependence of existing DL-based real-time decomposition methods on experimentally collected data, thereby facilitating their broader application in robotic motor control and rehabilitation medicine.

Using simulated HD-sEMG data for model pre-training is a feasible and valuable approach [34]. When employing the pre-training strategy, a positive effect on online MU identification performance was consistently observed. This is because the simulation model accurately captures the electrophysiological properties of MUs as well as the generation process of HD-sEMG signals. The core characteristics of MUs in the simulation model, such as recruitment and firing, are derived from actual experimental observations [38]. Consequently, the generated simulation data authentically reflects the spatiotemporal features of MU activities, including MUAP waveforms and spatial distribution patterns in HD-sEMG signals. This enables the model to learn physiologically meaningful signal representations during the pre-training phase, thereby enhancing its initial feature characterization capabilities when transferred to real-world experimental data. Among the extensive studies on HD-sEMG decomposition, simulated HD-sEMG data is widely employed for method validation [16, 26, 39]. This research underscores its significance in DL-based method development. Furthermore, the consistency between simulation strategies (such as neural excitation curves, electrode counts, and arrangements) and experimental settings further enhances the morphological similarity between simulated and experimental HD-sEMG signals. This also maximizes the assurance that DL model pre-trained with simulated data can be directly applied to experimental data (consistency in input dimension). It is worth noting that the acquisition of simulated data is highly convenient, but this study did not observe a sustained positive effect of increasing data volume on identification performance. This may be due to the lack of diverse simulations and pre-training (e.g., varying force levels).

The proposed sample reconstruction strategy can generate realistic synthetic samples, thereby facilitating the training of DL models with small datasets. This is because such a strategy aligns with the underlying principles of MU activities. In terms of waveform principle, the 2D MUAPs extracted for each MU via the STA method exhibit relative stability both temporally and spatially. This quasi-stationary property is critical to the effectiveness of existing online decomposition methods [14]. The sample reconstruction strategy leverages this waveform stability property, regarding the MUAP 2D waveforms as specific representation of the MU activities, thereby generate a large number of new samples. It is noteworthy that the peel-off approach has been proven effective in conventional BSS methods such as PFP [11]. This work emphasizes its successful application in DL methods. In terms of firing principle, the spike positions of individual MUs exhibit random offset property, which has early been observed by researchers and modeled through ISI parameters in simulation models [38]. This property indicates that the spike position of individual MUs undergoes random shift within a certain time range, even though it maintains a stable FR. Such shift results in the spikes of each MU exhibiting a wide positional distribution within the EMG signals. When experimental training data is scarce, the limited samples can easily cause DL model to learn erroneous spike position features. The proposed approach effectively addresses this issue by generating a rich set of new HD-sEMG samples with varying spike positions, thereby enhancing the generalization capability of DL model. The alignment of the proposed strategy with both physiological principles collectively ensures the physiological rationality and validity of the generated synthetic samples.

Another reason for the effectiveness of the synthetic samples is their generation based on real experimental signals. The proposed strategy does not introduce any additional artificial noise or signal components to experimental samples, only modifying their MUAP waveform structures. This means that, despite the waveform differences between the synthetic and experimental samples, their statistical distribution and the embedded MU structural information remain similar (Fig. 7). This approach avoids distribution shifts caused by inconsistencies in sample characteristics, thereby preventing the synthetic samples from negatively impacting the DL model training. Furthermore, it is noteworthy that deep generative models, such as diffusion model [40], are also feasible methods for data generation and have been applied in the EMG field. However, these models require large amounts of data for training and operate as black-box models, which leads to poor interpretability of the generated samples. In contrast, our strategy is more efficient and physiologically interpretable, making it suitable for practical applications.

It is worth mentioning that the proposed method offers outstanding real-time performance. Owing to the high processing speed of GPUs, testing a single sample in the DL model takes much less time (< 0.1ms) than the step size (approximately 2.5ms), ensuring stable operation even with very short window lengths and step sizes. Furthermore, the results demonstrate that real-time MU identification based on DL is much faster than methods that rely on separation vectors [15, 16, 39], which require window length and step size of several hundred milliseconds. It can be found that other DL-based studies report similar online performance [27, 29, 31]. However, their DL models appear to take a long time to test a single HD-EMG sample, even exceeding the chosen step size, which could pose issues in real-time applications. Therefore, employing a lightweight model such as the proposed method is crucial for real-time MU identification.

This study has some limitations that warrant further exploration. Firstly, the proposed method has only been tested on a single muscle. Future research should therefore focus on evaluating its effectiveness on a broader range of muscles, in order to test its generalization performance. Secondly, although the proposed method reduces the data dependency of DL models, it still increases the model’s training burden. Further research should investigate more efficient few-shot learning methods. Thirdly, this study did not investigate the influence of simulation model parameters on the effectiveness of pre-training. Developing more physiologically realistic simulation models may further improve model performance. Finally, the proposed method only identifies the firing states within the HD-sEMG samples. Future work should explore how to estimate more accurate spike positions while maintaining real-time performance.

Conclusion

An innovative sample reconstruction strategy-enhanced few-shot learning method for real-time MU identification is proposed in this study. The results revealed that using simulated data to pretrain the DL model is effective in improving the baseline performance of MU identification in experimental data. Furthermore, the proposed sample reconstruction strategy exhibits good physiological interpretability as it is designed based on spatiotemporal characteristics of MU activities, generating a substantial quantity of synthetic samples that possess physiologically meaningful characteristics. Based on the transfer learning framework, the proposed method requires only a small amount of experimentally collected training dataset to achieve satisfactory real-time MU identification accuracy in testing dataset. This research provides an innovative solution to the issue of data dependency in current DL methods for MU identification, and is expected to promote the development and application of more efficient, practical real-time MU decomposition methods.
