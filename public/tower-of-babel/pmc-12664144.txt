Regulatory role of sirtuin-1 by targeting MDM2 to mitochondria-associated membranes formation in the treatment of NAFLD
Authors: Rui Zhang, Quanwei Zhang, ZiYi Cui, BenZeng Huang, Manman Li, Haitian Ma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) is a common liver disorder characterized by excessive fat accumulation in the liver. Aberrant enrichment of mitochondria-associated membranes (MAMs) plays a significant role in promoting the overproduction of reactive oxygen species (ROS). However, the precise role of MAMs in NAFLD and the potential targets regulating MAMs formation remain unclear.This study demonstrated that SIRT1 inhibits abnormal MAM enrichment in the livers of NAFLD mice and PA-incubated hepatocytes. Moreover, the protective effects of SIRT1 against NAFLD caused by excessive caloric intake are closely associated with its ability to block MAMs-induced mitochondrial Ca2⁺ overload. This regulation alleviates Ca2⁺ dysregulation and subsequently inhibits ROS overproduction-mediated mitochondrial dysfunction. Transcriptomic analysis confirmed that mouse double minute 2 homolog (MDM2) may acts as a downstream target of activated SIRT1 in regulating MAMs formation and thereby protecting against the development of NAFLD. Taken together, this reseach revealed that SIRT1 activation inhibits the aberrant formation of MAMs caused by excessive calorie intake. This effect is linked to the suppression of MDM2 expression and disruption of its interaction of MDM2 with resident MAMs Ca2 + channels by SIRT1, ultimately alleviating mitochondrial Ca2⁺ overload induced mitochondria oxidative stress.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12964-025-02251-7.

Introduction

Non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) is a multifaceted liver disorder characterized by the excessive accumulation of fat in the liver, independent of alcohol consumption. NAFLD encompasses a spectrum of conditions ranging from simple steatosis to non-alcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH) and can progress to advanced liver fibrosis and cirrhosis [1, 2]. The prevalence of NAFLD has been rising globally, posing a significant public health challenge due to its association with obesity, type 2 diabetes, and metabolic syndrome [3]. Mitochondrial dysfunction plays a crucial role in the development of NAFLD [4]. When mitochondria are impaired by factors such as oxidative stress and mitochondrial DNA damage, their ability to metabolize fatty acids becomes compromised, leading to the excessive accumulation of fat in the liver [5]. Additionally, mitochondrial dysfunction can also increases the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS), which can further damage cells and exacerbate lipid accumulation [4, 6]. Ca2⁺ signaling pathways are pivotal in maintaining mitochondrial function, cellular energy balance, and metabolic regulation, making them potential targets for therapeutic interventions in metabolism-related diseases [7, 8]. Numerous studies have revealed that dysregulation of Ca2⁺ within cells may lead to excessive ROS generation. High Ca2⁺ levels can enhance the activity of the electron transport chain, increasing the leakage of electrons that react with oxygen to form ROS [9]. Importantly, previous research has shown that Ca2⁺ dysregulation is implicated in promoting hepatic steatosis [10, 11]. Disruptions in Ca2⁺ homeostasis, including impaired Ca2⁺ signaling and altered intracellular Ca2⁺ levels, can trigger lipid metabolism dysregulation, mitochondrial dysfunction, and activation of inflammatory pathways. These changes exacerbate liver damage and contribute to the progression of NAFLD [8]. However, the mechanisms by which Ca2⁺ dys-homeostasis induces mitochondrial dysfunction during the development of NAFLD remain elusive. Therefore, understanding the role and underlying mechanisms of Ca2⁺ homeostasis imbalance in NAFLD pathogenesis is essential. This knowledge could pave the way for novel therapeutic strategies targeting the occurrence and progression of NAFLD.

Mitochondria-associated membranes (MAMs) are specialized regions of the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) that form close connections with mitochondria, acting as critical hubs for cellular signaling and function [12]. These contact sites are essential for regulating Ca2⁺ signaling between the ER and mitochondria. MAMs are enriched with key components such as inositol 1,4,5-trisphosphate receptors (IP3Rs) on the ER, voltage-dependent anion channels (VDACs) on the outer mitochondrial membrane, and their chaperone GRP75, which work together to efficiently transfer Ca2⁺ from the ER to the mitochondrial matrix [13, 14]. MAMs serve as platforms for numerous cellular processes, including lipid metabolism and Ca2⁺ transfer. Recent research suggested that abnormal MAMs function is implicated in metabolic diseases, as the Ca2⁺ transfer plays a crucial role in activating mitochondrial enzymes that drive the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle [13, 15]. Although disruptions in MAM-regulated Ca2⁺ signaling have been linked to a range of pathological conditions, ongoing debates underscore the complex role of MAMs in metabolic diseases [16]. Importantly, either excessive or inadequate MAMs contact between the ER and mitochondria can lead to Ca2⁺ dysregulation. This may be associated with altered expression of Ca2⁺ channels and abnormal interactions of MAMs-resident proteins, which in turn cause mitochondrial dysfunction and contribute to the development of metabolic disease [17]. Given the intricate relationship among MAM formation, Ca2⁺ transfer, mitochondrial dysfunction, and lipid metabolism disorders, it is crucial to elucidate the role of MAMs in the development of NAFLD. These observations highlight the urgent need to investigate whether MAMs formation inhibits or facilitates lipid accumulation in the progression of NAFLD, as well as to identify key potential factors that regulate MAMs formation.

Sirtuin 1 (SIRT1), a member of the Sirtuin family of NAD⁺-dependent deacetylases, is involved in various cellular processes, including aging, transcription, apoptosis, and inflammation [18]. Numerous studies have highlighted the beneficial effects of activated SIRT1 in the treatment of metabolism-related diseases, and the aberrant down-regulation of SIRT1 expression is consistently observed both in the liver of NAFLD patients and in lipid metabolism disorder models [19]. Recently, SRT2104, a selective SIRT1 agonist, has entered clinical trials in individuals with metabolic diseases, and its therapeutic effects were correlated with the improvement of mitochondrial function by regulating SIRT1—marking a significant step forward for SIRT1 as a targeted therapeutic strategy for metabolism-related diseases. Unfortunately, it remains unknown whether SIRT1 is involved in regulating MAMs formation and thereby protecting against the occurrence of NAFLD [20]. Our previous research revealed that the SIRT1 signaling pathway mitigated Ca2⁺ dysregulation in palmitic acid-induced hepatocellular steatosis [21]. Accordingly, we speculated that SIRT1 may play a potential role in regulating MAMs formation, thereby improving hepatic steatosis caused by excessive calorie intake. Importantly, elucidating the regulatory mechanisms of SIRT1 on MAMs formation and function can provide a brand-new perspective on therapeutic approaches for NAFLD.

Herein, we confirmed both in vivo and in vitro that exposure to high-energy conditions boosts MAMs formation and triggers the occurrence of NAFLD. Mechanistically, activated SIRT1 mitigates mitochondrial dysfunction induced by excessive calorie intake by inhibiting MAMs’ over-enrichment-mediated mitochondrial Ca2⁺ overload, thereby inhibiting hepatic steatosis. Furthermore, the activation of SIRT1 on inhibiting the aberrant formation of MAMs is related to inhibiting the high expression of MDM2, which disrupts its interaction with resident Ca2+ channels at MAMs. Cumulatively, our results revealed a novel mechanism whereby activated SIRT1 protects against the occurrence of NAFLD by blocking MAMs over-formation through targeting and disrupting MDM2 interaction with MAMs resident proteins. This relieves mitochondrial dysfunction caused by MAMs-mediated Ca2⁺ dysregulation, suggesting that SIRT1 serves as a crucial target for the treatment of NAFLD. Notably, these findings provide dependable guidance to help researchers develop new strategies for preventing the occurrence of NAFLD.

Material and methods

Reagents

SRT1720 (T5096), a selective SIRT1 activator, was obtained from TargetMol (Boston, MA, USA). BODIPY™ 493/503 (D3922), MitoTracker™ Green FM (M7514), Rhodamine-123 (R302), and ER-Tracker™ Blue-White DPX (E12353) were sourced from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Waltham, MA, USA). Palmitic acid (P9767), dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, D8418), and Ru360 (557440) were procured from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). BasalMedia supplied DMEM/F12 medium (L310KJ), 0.25% Trypsin (S310KJ), and phosphate buffer solution (PBS, B310KJ) from Shanghai, China. Biochemical assay kits for triglyceride (TG) (A110-1–1), total cholesterol (TC) (A111-1–1), low-density lipoprotein (LDL) (A113-1–1), high-density lipoprotein (HDL) (A112-1–1), H2O2 (A064-1–1), malondialdehyde (MDA) (A003-1–2), aspartate aminotransferase (AST) (C010-2–1), and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) (C009-2–1) were acquired from the Nanjing Jiancheng Institute of Biotechnology (Nanjing, Jiangsu, China). The BCA protein assay kit was purchased from the Beyotime Institute of Biotechnology (Shanghai, China). Inositol 1,4,5-trisphosphate receptor type 1 (IP3R1) and Glucose-regulated protein 75 (GRP75) were purchased from Cell Signaling Technology (Danvers, MA, USA); Voltage-dependent anion channel 1 (VDAC1), Phosphorylated Protein Kinase RNA-like Endoplasmic Reticulum Kinase (p-PERK), Protein Kinase RNA-like Endoplasmic Reticulum Kinase (PERK), Glucose-regulated protein 78 (GRP78), Mitochondrial Calcium Uniporter (MCU1R), ATP synthase subunit alpha (ATP5A), Ubiquinol-Cytochrome C Reductase Core Protein 2 (UQCRC2), Ubiquinone Oxidoreductase Subunit B8 (NDUFB8) and β-Tubulin were acquired from Proteintech (Wuhan, China).

Animal experiment

All experiments were conducted following the guidelines set forth by the Centre for National Laboratory Animal Welfare Ethics and were approved by the Animal Welfare & Ethical Committee of Nanjing Agricultural University (Permit Number: NJAU.No20220413076).

Male C57BL/6 J mice, aged 8 weeks and weighing 20—25 g, were obtained from Vital River Laboratories (Beijing, China). The mice were housed under controlled temperature conditions with a 12-h light/dark cycle and were given ad libitum access to water and food. Initially, the mice were randomly allocated into two groups based on their body weight: a normal control (NC) group and a high-fat/high-cholesterol (HFHC) group. The NC group received a standard chow diet consisting of 10% fat, 70% carbohydrate, and 20% protein (Jiangsu Xietong Pharmaceutical Bioengineering Co., Ltd, Nanjing, China), while the HFHC group was fed a diet containing 40% fat, 40% carbohydrate, 20% protein, and 1.8% cholesterol (Jiangsu Xietong Pharmaceutical Bioengineering Co., Ltd, Nanjing, China). Both body weight and food consumption were monitored weekly.

After 8 weeks on the HFHC diet, the mice were divided into two groups: one continued on the HFHC diet, while the other received SRT1720 (50 mg/kg) via intragastric administration, dissolved in a 0.5% carboxymethylcellulose (CMC) solution, for an additional 8 weeks. The control and NAFLD groups were administered an equivalent volume of the vehicle solution. At the end of the experiment, the mice were fasted overnight, euthanized via cervical dislocation, and subjected to cardiac puncture for blood collection. The serum was then isolated by centrifugation. Liver tissues were harvested, weighed, and either fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde or immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen for subsequent analysis.

Cell culture

The human hepatocellular carcinoma cell line HepG2 and mouse hepatocyte cell line AML12 were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) and cultured in Dulbecco’s modified eagle medium (DMEM) and DMEM/F12 medium, respectively. The media were supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (P/S); additionally, the AML12 cells were supplemented with insulin-transferrin-selenium (ITS-X, 41400–045; Gibco) and 40 ng/ml dexamethasone. The cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2; the cells were seeded in 6-well plates or 96-well plates and treated with a vehicle or 400 μM palmitic acid (PA) for 24 h.

Histopathology analysis

Liver tissues were harvested and immediately fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde. For Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) staining, the fixed liver tissues were embedded in paraffin and sectioned. The sections were then deparaffinized, rehydrated, and stained with H&E to assess general liver pathology. For the assessment of hepatic steatosis, liver tissues were embedded in Tissue-Tek O.C.T. Compound (Servicebio) and snap-frozen. Frozen sections were cut and stained with Red Oil O to detect lipid accumulations. The stained sections were then rinsed, counterstained with hematoxylin, and imaged.

Biochemical index assays

The levels of triglyceride (TG), total cholesterol (TC), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) in plasma or liver, along with H2O2 and malondialdehyde (MDA) in liver homogenates, were quantified using biochemical assay kits (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, China).

Establishment of AML12 cells overexpressing SIRT1

To construct AML12 cells with stable overexpression of SIRT1, a lentiviral vector (pCDH-CMV-MCS-EF1-puro) containing the mouse SIRT1 gene and a puromycin resistance gene was used. This vector leverages the CMV promoter for SIRT1 expression and the EF1α promoter to drive puromycin resistance. The pCDH-CMV-MCS-EF1-puro plasmid, along with the packaging plasmids pMD2.G and psPAX2, were co-transfected [22] into HEK293T cells using Lipofectamine 3000 to produce lentiviruses. After 48 h, the supernatant containing the lentiviruses was collected and filtered. Then, the AML12 cells were seeded in 6-well plates and infected with the lentivirus at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 10 in the presence of 8 µg/mL polybrene. 48 h post-infection, the cells were selected with 2 µg/mL puromycin for another 48 h. After that, the overexpression of SIRT1 was verified by western blot analysis, ensuring the successful establishment of the modified AML12 cells.

Construction of Mito-ER linker

The sequence of the Mito-ER linker is based on a previous report [22]. Briefly, a synthetic linker encodes a monomeric red fluorescent protein (mRFP) between the N-terminal outer mitochondrial membrane (OMM) targeting sequence from mAKAP1 and the C-terminal ER targeting sequence from yUBC6. This construct is designed to decrease the ER-mitochondria distance, thereby increasing the contact sites between mitochondria and the ER.

D-siRNA of MDM2 and IP3R1 transfection

The D-siRNA vector of MDM2 and IP3R1 were provided by Integrated DNA Technologies, Inc. The sequence of shRNA used in this study is as follows: MDM2 dsiRNA (Sense strand: rArArA rGrArC rUrGrA rArUrC rArGrA rCrCrA rUrGrG rArUA A; Antisense strand: rUrUrA rCrCrA rUrGrC rUrUrC rUrCrA rUrGrU rCrCrU) and IP3R1(Sense strand: rGrUrU rUrCrA rUrCrU rGrCrA rGrCrC rUrArA rUrArA rArAC A; Antisense strand: rUrGrU rCrUrU rUrArC rArGrC rCrArG rArArA rCrUrU). For transfection, 2 nmol of each dsiRNA was diluted in Opti-MEM medium (31985070, Gibco,USA) and mixed with Lipofectamine 3000 reagent (L3000015, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The mixture was incubated for 10–15 min at room temperature to allow complex formation. Subsequently, the dsiRNA-lipid complexes were added dropwise to the AML12 cells; then, the cells were then incubated in an incubator for 48 h before downstream analysis.

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) and immunoblotting

Hepatic tissue and cellular samples were lysed using RIPA lysis buffer (P0013B, Beyotime, China) containing protease (P1005, Beyotime, China) and phosphatase inhibitors (P1045, Beyotime, China). Protein concentration was analyzed using the BCA protein assay kit (P0009, Beyotime, China). The 5 μg/μL liver and 2 μg/μL cell protein samples were processed through SDS-PAGE gels and then transferred to PVDF membranes. After blocking with skim milk, the membranes were incubated with primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight and HRP-linked secondary antibodies for 2 h at room temperature, respectively. Protein abundance was detected using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) substrate, and signals were visualized with a CCD-based imaging system (Tanon 4600SF, Shanghai, China). Densitometric quantification of protein bands was performed using ImageJ software (NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA).

For the Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) test, the samples were processed using the protein A + G immunoprecipitation kit with protein A + G agarose gel (P2197S, Beyotime, China). Briefly, the lysis buffer was used to lyse cells or liver tissues to prepare protein samples. Subsequently, specific antibodies were added to the protein A + G agarose gel to form antibody-gel complexes. The protein sample was then added to facilitate the binding of the target protein with the specific antibody. After washing steps to remove unbound proteins and impurities, the target protein-antibody complexes were eluted from the gel using elution buffer and analyzed by Western blot.

Mitochondria-associated membranes (MAMs) separation

Mitochondria-associated membranes (MAMs) were isolated from mouse liver tissues and AML12 cells using a modified protocol based on previous reported [23]. Briefly, the liver tissues or hepatocytes were homogenized in ice-cold buffer containing 250 mM sucrose, 10 mM HEPES, 1 mM EDTA, and protease inhibitors. The homogenate was centrifuged at 600 g for 10 min to obtain the post-nuclear supernatant (PNS), which was then centrifuged at 10,000 g for 10 min to pellet the crude mitochondria. Then, the crude mitochondrial pellet was resuspended in buffer containing 0.5 M sucrose, 10 mM HEPES, and 1 mM EDTA, and loaded on top of a discontinuous sucrose gradient (2.0 M, 1.5 M, and 1.0 M sucrose). After ultracentrifugation at 100,000 g for 1 h, the MAMs fraction was collected at the 1.5 M/2.0 M sucrose interface and diluted with buffer containing 0.25 M sucrose, 10 mM HEPES, and 1 mM EDTA. The diluted MAMs fraction was then centrifuged at 7,000 g for 10 min to obtain the final MAMs pellet, which was resuspended in the same buffer for further analysis. The purity and integrity of the MAMs fraction were verified by immunoblotting with specific markers for the endoplasmic reticulum (Calreticulin), mitochondria (Cytochrome-C), and MAMs (phosphatidylserine synthase 1).

ER and mitochondria contact analysis by transmission electron microscopy and laser confocal microscopy

The liver tissues were examined by transmission electron microscopy (TEM) using a HITACHI HT-7700 microscope (Hitachi High-Tech Corporation, Tokyo, Japan). Liver samples were obtained from mice and fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde and 1% osmium tetroxide. The samples were then dehydrated in a graded series of ethanol and embedded in epoxy resin. Ultrathin Sects. (60 nm) were cut with a diamond knife and stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate. The sections were observed and photographed with the TEM at 80 kV and 33,000 × magnification. The perimeter of the MAMs was measured using Image J (NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA) according to the previous research [24].

The AML12 and HepG2 cells were transfected with a plasmid encoding ER-targeted DsRed2 (DsRed2-ER-5, Addgene, USA)1, incubated for 48 h, and stained with MitoTracker Green FM (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) for 30 min at 37 °C, or the AML12 cells tranfected with Mito-ER linker co-stained with MitoTracker Green FM and ER-Tracker Blue-White for 30 min at 37 °C. The morphology of mitochondria and ER localization were visualized by a confocal microscope (LSM 980, ZEISS, Germany). The images for analyzing colocalization of mitochondria and ER were analyzed by Image J Fiji plugin. The Pearson correlation coefficient, a lucid and standard methods to quantify the overlap between two fluorescent channels, was applied to analyze the colocalization of the fluorophores representing DsRed2-ER and MitoTracker Green or MitoTracker Green and ER-Tracker Blue-White [25].

Subcellular Ca2+ measurement

Subcellular Ca2⁺ levels in AML12 cells were measured using specific fluorescent indicators for mitochondrial and ER Ca2⁺. Mitochondrial Ca2⁺ was monitored with the genetically encoded Ca2⁺ sensor pCAG mito-GCaMP5G (Plasmid #105009, Addgene, USA), and ER Ca2⁺ was measured using the Ca2⁺ sensor pcDNA-D1ER (Plasmid #36325, Addgene, USA). AML12 cells were transfected with these Ca2⁺ indicators 48 h prior to imaging. To accurately assess Ca2⁺ dynamics and eliminate the influence of capacitative Ca2⁺ entry (CCE), all experiments were conducted in Ca2⁺-free Hank’s Balanced Salt Solution (HBSS, ThermoFisher Scientific, USA) supplemented with 1 mM EGTA to chelate residual extracellular Ca2⁺, ensuring no external Ca2⁺ sources. The cells were excited at 488 nm, and fluorescence emissions were recorded at 490 nm (CFP) and 540 nm (YFP) using the EVOS Cell Imaging System (ThermoFisher Scientific, USA). Ratiometric analysis of YFP/CFP fluorescence (F490/F540) was conducted using MetaMorph software (Molecular Devices) to evaluate changes in intracellular Ca2⁺ levels. The fluorescent probes were calibrated according to the manufacturer’s protocol to ensure accurate Ca2⁺ concentration measurements. Fluorescence intensity and ratiometric data were further analyzed using MetaFluor 6.1 software.

Reactive oxygen species (ROS) measurement

Mitochondria ROS levels were measured by using DCFH-DA (HY-D0940, MedChemExpress, USA) and MitoSOX Red (HY-D1055, MedChemExpress, USA) Respectively. In brief, AML12 cells were co-incubated with DCFH-DA, MitoTracker™ Red (M46752, ThermoFisher SCIENTIFIC, USA) and DAPI (C1005, Beyotime, China) for 30 min at 37 ℃. After rinse twice with PBS, the fluorescence intensity was observed using confocal microscope. Following with incubation of MitoSOX Red, AML12 cells were washed with PBS twice and then collected the cells with trypsin. The level of MitoSOX Red was measured by flow cytometer (BD bioscience, USA); and at least 10,000 events were selected for each sample. The quantitively measurement was performed by Flowjo V10 software.

Mitochondria membrane potential measurement

To assess the level of MMP in AML12 cells, dual staining with Rhodamine 123 and DAPI was performed. AML12 cells were seeded in confocal dishes and incubated with a culture medium containing 5 μM Rhodamine-123 (MedChemExpress, USA) for 30 min, followed by two washes with PBS. Images were then captured using an inverted fluorescence microscope (Invitrogen, USA).

Lipid droplets staining

A dual-staining involving BIODIPY 493/503 and DAPI was employed to detect the lipid accumulation on AML12 cells. The AML12 cells were placed in dishes and exposed to a culture medium with 5 μM BIODIPY 493/503 (MedChemExpress, USA) for 30 min. Following incubation, the cells underwent two PBS washes. Imaging was performed using an inverted fluorescence microscope (Invitrogen, USA).

Statistical analysis

All data analyses were conducted using Prism 9 (GraphPad, USA) and SPSS 20.0 (IBM, USA), with results presented as means ± SD. For comparisons between two treatment groups, the Student’s T-Test was employed, while One-way ANOVA was used for comparisons across multiple treatment groups. Statistical significance was determined for P values less than 0.05.

Results

High-energy condition boosts hepatic MAMs formation and inhibits hepatic SIRT1 expression.

Current research on mitochondria-associated membranes (MAMs) in metabolism-related diseases presents significant contradictions. Some studies suggested that MAMs formation promotes fatty liver, while others reports the opposite effect [16, 26]. To clarify the impact of high-caloric diets on MAMs formation, we first assessed the regions of close contact between mitochondria and the ER using transmission electron microscopy. As shown in Fig. 1A, the proximity between mitochondria and the ER was significantly increased in the livers of mice fed a high-fat, high-carbohydrate (HFHC) diet compared to those fed a normal diet. Quantitative analysis further indicated that the proportion of mitochondria adjacent to the ER was elevated in the livers of NAFLD mice. Additionally, in vitro analysis used Pearson’s coefficient based on the colocalization of mitochondria and ER fluorescence to evaluate MAMs formation. Results from confocal imaging and Pearson’s coefficient revealed that high-energy stimulation led to an increased association between mitochondria and the ER in hepatocytes (Fig. 1B). IP3R1 forms a complex with other proteins, including GRP75 and VDAC1, at MAMs, which facilitates the transfer of Ca2⁺ from the ER to mitochondria, thereby influencing mitochondrial function. In this study, both in vivo and in vitro results demonstrated that excessive calorie intake significantly increased the protein expression levels of IP3R1, GRP75, and VDAC1 (Fig. 1C and D, Fig. S1B). On the other hand, we also examined the expression of these Ca2⁺ channel proteins in the MAMs fraction, and the purity of the MAMs components was confirmed, as shown in Fig. S1A. As expected, increased levels of IP3R1, GRP75, and VDAC1 were observed in the MAMs fraction isolated from liver tissues of NAFLD mice and palmitic acid (PA)-stimulated hepatocytes (Fig. 1C and D, Furthermore, SIRT1 protein expression was downregulated in both liver tissues and AML12 cells following exposure to high-fat, high-cholesterol (HFHC) diets and PA treatment (Fig. S1C).

Activation of SIRT1 blocks the aberrant MAMs formation and relieves hepatic steatosis

Given the positive role of SIRT1 in relieving oxidative stress, endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress, and maintaining Ca2⁺ homeostasis, we hypothesized that SIRT1 may be involved in regulating MAMs formation [21, 27, 28]. Accordingly, we employed SRT1720, a selective SIRT1 activator, to investigate the regulatory effect of SIRT1 signaling on MAMs formation in NAFLD mice. Thus, we evaluated the effects of SRT1720 on the phenotypic characteristics of NAFLD mice, and the results demonstrated that body weight, liver weight, and abdominal fat weight were significantly reduced in HFHC diet-fed mice treated with SRT1720 compared to those fed the HFHC diet alone, without any changes in food intake (Fig. S2A–S2C). Furthermore, SRT1720 treatment ameliorated chronic liver injury induced by the HFHC diet in mice (Fig. S2D). These results implied that high-calorie-overtake obviously inhibits the hepatic SIRT1 expression level, and activated SIRT1 prevent the occurrence of NAFLD.

As illustrated in Fig. 2A, the morphological results from transmission electron microscopy (TEM) demonstrated that SRT1720 treatment significantly reduced the aberrant formation of MAMs induced by the high-fat, high-carbohydrate (HFHC) diet. Western blot analysis further revealed that SRT1720 restored the elevated expression levels of IP3R, GRP75, and VDAC1 proteins induced by the HFHC diet in the liver of mice (Fig. 2B). Additionally, SRT1720 treatment mitigated excessive fat accumulation and hepatic steatosis caused by the HFHC diet (Fig. 2C and D). Biochemical analyses confirmed that SRT1720 improved systemic lipid metabolism disorders induced by the HFHC diet in mice (Fig. 2E). However, SIRT1 overexpression counteracted these negative effects, reducing both MAMs formation and the aberrant expression of Ca2⁺ channel proteins. One of the key biological functions of MAMs is the regulation of ER-mitochondrial Ca2⁺ transport, and some studies have shown that MAMs hyperactivity can lead to intracellular Ca2⁺ overload [29, 30]. Our results demonstrated that SIRT1 overexpression inhibited the high-energy-stimulation-induced impairment of ER Ca2⁺ storage while reducing the elevated mitochondrial Ca2⁺ levels ([Ca2⁺] Mito) at baseline. Furthermore, in SIRT1-overexpressing hepatocytes, the lower trough of ER Ca2⁺ levels ([Ca2⁺]ER) and higher peak of mitochondrial Ca2⁺ levels induced by ATP under high-energy conditions were improved (Fig. 3C). Ca2⁺ overload can disrupt the electron transport chain, leading to increased electron leakage and reactive oxygen species (ROS) production. Given that DCFH-AM may disperse to non-mitochondrial regions despite mitochondria being the primary source of ROS, we co-stained Mito-Tracker Red with DCFH-AM to assess mitochondrial oxidative stress using confocal imaging. As shown in Fig. 3D and Fig. S3A, SIRT1 overexpression inhibited the mitochondria oxidative stress caused by high energy conditions after incubation of Mito-SOX Red dye, a specific indicator of mitochondrial superoxide production. This result futher was further comfirmed by dual-staining of DCFH-DA and Mito-tracker Red (Fig. 3E and Fig. S3B). These results demonstrated that activated SIRT1 reduces the mitochondria oxidative stress caused by high-calorie-overtake through relieving MAMs over-enrichment mediated Ca2+ dys-homeostasis.

Mitochondrial oxidative stress is a key driver of mitochondrial dysfunction, leading to the loss of mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP), impaired energy metabolism, and increased susceptibility to cell damage [31]. In this study, we identified a protective role of activated SIRT1 against mitochondrial dysfunction and steatosis induced by high-energy stimulation in hepatocytes. Western blot analysis revealed that high-energy stimulation significantly reduced the expression levels of mitochondrial respiratory chain proteins ATP5A and NDUFB8 in hepatocytes, indicating compromised mitochondrial function. However, SIRT1 overexpression restored mitochondrial function, as it effectively blocked the reduction in mitochondrial respiratory protein expression levels (Fig. S3D). Further analysis using fluorescence microscopy showed that high-energy stimulation reduced MMP levels, as indicated by Rhodamine 123 staining, and increased lipid accumulation, as shown by BODIPY staining. Notably, SIRT1 overexpression mitigated both the reduction in MMP and the increase in lipid accumulation caused by high-energy stimulation (Fig. 3F and Fig. S3C). Taken together, these findings suggested that SIRT1 overexpression alleviates Ca2⁺ dysregulation, oxidative stress, and mitochondrial dysfunction induced by excessive calorie intake. These beneficial effects of activated SIRT1 are associated with its ability to inhibit the abnormal enrichment of MAMs, further underscoring the critical role of SIRT1 in protecting against NAFLD progression.

Discussion

MAMs are specialized regions where the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) and mitochondria are in close contact, playing critical roles in various cellular processes including lipid metabolism, Ca2⁺ transfer, mitochondrial function, and cell death [13, 14]. Recently, MAMs have emerged as a significant area of research in liver health, particularly concerning metabolic dysfunction-associated liver disease [36]. In the context of prolonged high caloric intake, dysfunctional MAMs are increasingly recognized as contributing to the pathogenesis of lifestyle-related diseases; however, considerable controversy remains regarding MAMs formation and its function in metabolism-related diseases, especially in the development of NAFLD. Some studies have reported miscommunication between mitochondria and the ER in both hepatic steatosis and NASH mice, suggesting that a reduction in Ca2⁺ transfer from the ER to mitochondria may lead to significant mitochondrial dysfunction, and that enhancing MAMs formation can inhibit lipid accumulation in the liver [26]. Conversely, other research has proposed that increased MAMs formation plays a crucial role in inducing mitochondrial dysfunction due to excessive Ca2⁺ channel interactions [16]. Therefore, substantial research is still needed to further elucidate MAMs formation and its role in the development of metabolism-related diseases. In this study, we first explored whether MAMs formation tends to be enriched in the liver of NAFLD mice and in high-energy-stimulated hepatocytes. Intriguingly, morphological and colocalization imaging clearly showed an enhancement of MAMs formation in the livers of NAFLD mice and in high-energy-triggered hepatocytes, suggesting that long-term exposure to high-energy conditions can increase the physical connections between mitochondria and the ER. The expression levels of MAMs-resident proteins typically represent the degree of MAMs enrichment, and we found that the expression of IP3R1, VDAC1, and GRP75 was upregulated in hepatic homogenates and isolated MAMs fractions from the livers of NAFLD mice and high-energy-stimulated hepatocytes. These findings are consistent with previous reports indicating that high-energy or high-fat diets are sufficient to increase hepatic MAMs formation in the liver and even in muscle tissue [16]. Therefore, our data indicate that excessive calorie intake promotes aberrant MAMs formation, which may be a crucial factor in the development of NAFLD.

Cellular regulation of MAMs formation and the potential factors involved in MAMs-mediated internal homeostasis, especially under conditions of excessive calorie intake, remain critical areas of investigation. Our previous research demonstrated that dimethyl fumarate mitigates excessive lipid accumulation caused by high-energy stimulation through a close relationship with SIRT1-regulated Ca2⁺ homeostasis, thereby alleviating hepatocellular steatosis [21]. Additionally, studies have reported that SIRT1 exerts beneficial effects on NAFLD by modulating lipid metabolism and mitochondrial function [37]. However, it remains unclear whether SIRT1 regulates MAMs formation under conditions of excessive calorie intake and whether SIRT1 protects against the development of NAFLD by modulating the abnormal enrichment of MAMs. Moreover, previous research has revealed that SIRT1 plays multiple roles in regulating mitochondrial functions, including mitochondrial dynamics and mitophagy, and it modulates ER stress responses by deacetylating key proteins involved in the unfolded protein response, such as X-box binding protein 1 and activating transcription factor 6. This deacetylation activity helps restore ER homeostasis and maintain proper ER function under stress conditions [38–40]. Based on these findings, we hypothesized that SIRT1 may be one of the most promising targets for regulating MAMs function due to its multifaceted roles in improving lipid metabolism disorders, mitochondrial dysfunction, and the unfolded protein response under stress conditions. In this study, we found that, in addition to alleviating oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and excessive lipid accumulation in NAFLD mice and high-energy-stimulated hepatocytes, activation of SIRT1 significantly inhibited hepatic MAMs over-enrichment and the increased expression of MAMs-related Ca2⁺ channels. These data suggested that activated SIRT1 may downregulate MAMs hyperfunction, thereby protecting against the development of NAFLD. Importantly, we employed mitochondria- and ER-targeted Ca2⁺-sensitive fluorescent biosensors to detect dynamic changes in Ca2⁺ signaling within hepatocytes, and the results suggested that SIRT1 overexpression inhibits high-energy stimulation-induced disruption of ER Ca2⁺ regulation and mitochondrial Ca2⁺ overload. One of the main biological functions of MAMs is to regulate Ca2⁺ homeostasis, and the MAMs-resident protein complex IP3R1-GRP75-VDAC1 facilitates Ca2⁺ transport from the ER to mitochondria [41]. Therefore, these data suggested that the positive therapeutic effects of activated SIRT1 on NAFLD caused by excessive calorie intake are mainly achieved by regulating the abnormal enrichment of MAMs and the imbalance of MAMs-mediated Ca2⁺ homeostasis.

In the context of long-term high-calorie intake, mitochondrial Ca2⁺ overload acts as a key driver of oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and impaired energy metabolism [17, 42]. Elevated mitochondrial Ca2⁺ levels can disrupt normal mitochondrial respiration, leading to increased ROS production; conversely, this ROS production can damage components of the mitochondrial respiratory chains [43, 44]. Meanwhile, oxidative stress further exacerbates mitochondrial dysfunction, resulting in impaired ATP synthesis and the accumulation of toxic lipid intermediates within hepatocytes [45]. Research has shown that mitochondrial Ca2⁺ homeostasis is crucial for maintaining normal liver function and lipid metabolism. Disruptions in this homeostasis, as observed with higher expression of MCU, can result in enhanced mitochondrial Ca2⁺ uptake, leading to mitochondrial Ca2⁺ overload, ROS accumulation, and mitochondrial swelling [33, 46]. Therefore, the interplay between elevated mitochondrial Ca2⁺, oxidative stress, and lipid accumulation creates a vicious cycle that further exacerbates liver damage. In this study, we found that the inhibitory effects of activated SIRT1 on MAMs over-enrichment caused by high-energy stimulation are accompanied by a reduction in the aberrant expression of MAMs-related Ca2⁺ channel proteins, mitochondrial Ca2⁺ overload, oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and hepatic steatosis. Mitochondrial dysfunction not only triggers hepatocyte apoptosis but also promotes the progression of hepatic steatosis [47]. To further clarify whether the mechanism by which activated SIRT1 inhibits lipid accumulation is related to mitigating MAMs over-enrichment, we constructed mitochondria-ER linkers to facilitate the physical tethering between mitochondria and the ER directly. The function and sequence of this synthetic linker have been described in previous research [22]. Similarly to high-energy-stimulated hepatocytes, SIRT1 overexpression has been shown to counteract Mito-ER linker-induced aberrant MAMs formation and mitochondrial Ca2⁺ dysregulation [22]. Additionally, we found that activated SIRT1 inhibited the increased expression of MCUR1 induced by the Mito-ER linker. MCUR1, a key regulatory subunit of MCU located in the inner membrane of mitochondria, is responsible for the uptake of Ca2⁺ into the mitochondria [32, 48]. These findings deeply support our hypothesis that the effects of activated SIRT1 in reducing lipid accumulation caused by excessive calorie intake are achieved by regulating MAMs over-enrichment-induced mitochondrial Ca2⁺ overload. Subsequently, we used the mitochondrial Ca2⁺ uptake inhibitor Ru360 to confirm this conclusion, demonstrating that SIRT1 improves steatosis by relieving MAMs over-enrichment-induced mitochondrial Ca2⁺ overload. Similarly to the protective effects of activated SIRT1 on NAFLD caused by excessive calorie intake, treatment with Ru360 in hepatocytes inhibited Mito-ER linker-induced oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and excessive lipid accumulation. Taken together, our results demonstrated that activated SIRT1 inhibits MAMs over-enrichment-mediated high expression of MCUR1 to alleviate mitochondrial dysfunction induced by mitochondrial Ca2⁺ overload, thereby protecting against the occurrence of NAFLD caused by excessive calorie intake.

Given the critical role of SIRT1 in regulating MAMs formation and thereby protecting against the occurrence of NAFLD, it is urgent to investigate the potential target factors involved in SIRT1-mediated regulation of MAMs formation. Thus, we sought to identify downstream target factors of SIRT1 that may be involved in the regulatory effects on MAMs enrichment. Among the numerous identified factors, MDM2 emerged as the most promising candidate, exhibiting the highest expression levels induced by high-energy stimulation in our RNA-seq data, and activated SIRT1 reduced the elevated expression of MDM2 triggered by high-energy stimulation. MDM2 is a well-known regulator of the p53 tumor suppressor and is increasingly recognized for its role in cellular stress responses [49]. However, the subcellular localization of MDM2 remains controversial, with some studies suggesting its biological activity occurs in the nucleus, while others point to mitochondrial localization. We analyzed the expression of MDM2 in MAM fractions and liver homogenates. Interestingly, our results revealed that overexpression or activation of SIRT1 suppressed the high expression of MDM2 induced by high-energy stimulation in liver tissues or hepatocytes. Futhermore, CoIP results showed that SIRT1 inhibits the interaction between MDM2 and key MAM-resident proteins in the IP3R1-GRP75-VDAC complex. This suggested that SIRT1 may reduce high-energy-stimulation-induced MAMs over-enrichment by suppressing abnormal interactions within the MAM calcium channel complex. Next, we used Dsi-RNA vector to knockdown the expression of MDM2 for further reveal the role of MDM2 on regulating MAMs formation. Our results suggested that This knockdown significantly suppressed the increase in mitochondrial-ER colocalization coefficient and the expression of MAMs-associated Ca + channels induced by high-energy conditions and Mito-ER linkers. Previous studies have reported that downregulation of IP3R1 improves mitochondrial function and alleviates ER stress by reducing calcium flux in obese mice [16]. Following a similar approach, we aimed to uncover the potential biological function of MDM2 especially under metabolic disorder condition. Interestingly, knockdown of IP3R1 did not affect mitochondrial calcium levels under basal conditions. However, silencing both IP3R1 and MDM2 significantly suppressed PA induced Ca2 + overload in hepatocytes. Additionally, fluorescence staining revealed that silencing MDM2 and IP3R1 reversed PA-induced reductions in mitochondrial membrane potential and lipid accumulation in hepatocytes.

Our findings suggested that SIRT1 activation alleviate NAFLD by inhibiting the aberrant enrichment of MAMs, potentially through the regulation of MDM2. Specifically, activated SIRT1 appears to block the over-formation of MAMs caused by excessive calorie intake, thereby alleviating mitochondrial Ca2⁺ overload and subsequent mitochondrial dysfunction. Mechanistically, SIRT1 may inhibit MDM2 expression and disrupt its interactions with resident proteins at MAMs, preventing abnormal MAMs formation. These insights highlight the potential of SIRT1 as a therapeutic target for NAFLD and suggest that MDM2 could also serve as a prospective target for future drug development.
