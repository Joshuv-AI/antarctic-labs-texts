Multi-omics integration and machine learning define robust molecular subtypes and prognostic signatures in hepatocellular carcinoma
Authors: Xiangyu Wang, Biaobiao Yan, Jianhua Yang, Zhen Cheng, Wenchao Song, Yinfeng Yang, Jinghui Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) exhibits high aggressiveness and substantial molecular heterogeneity, yet precise and individualized therapeutic strategies remain limited. Comprehensive multi-omics integration and machine learning–driven modeling hold promise for refining molecular subtyping and improving prognostic prediction in HCC.MethodsWe developed a computational framework integrating multi-omics datasets from HCC patients. Ten clustering algorithms were combined to perform integrative multi-omics clustering and identify molecular subtypes. Ten machine learning algorithms were subsequently applied to construct a consensus prognostic signature. The clinical relevance of subtypes and risk groups was assessed through survival analysis, immunotherapy response prediction, and tumor immune microenvironment profiling. Single-cell RNA sequencing and spatial transcriptomics data were incorporated to determine the cellular origins and spatial expression patterns of key genes.ResultsIntegrative multi-omics analysis identified four prognostically distinct cancer subtypes (CS1–CS4), with CS4 exhibiting the most favorable clinical outcomes. Five key genes were selected to build a robust prognostic model. Patients in the low-risk group demonstrated significantly better survival, an enhanced response to immunotherapy, and a higher probability of exhibiting a “hot tumor” phenotype. Conversely, the high-risk group showed poorer prognosis and reduced immunotherapy benefit. Single-cell and spatial transcriptomics analyses revealed that the key genes are predominantly enriched in malignant hepatocytes and display spatial patterns suggestive of tumor regional heterogeneity.ConclusionsThis study provides a refined molecular classification of HCC through integrative multi-omics analysis and establishes a machine learning–driven prognostic model with potential utility in early prognosis prediction and immunotherapy stratification. The dual-dimensional single-cell and spatial transcriptomic analyses further illuminate the cellular and spatial features associated with key prognostic genes. Prospective clinical validation is required to confirm the model’s predictive performance. Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), the predominant form of primary liver cancer, accounts for more than 80% of liver cancer cases and remains one of the leading causes of cancer-related deaths worldwide [1, 2] . Despite advances in surveillance, diagnosis and therapeutic interventions, the prognosis of HCC is still poor, with five-year survival rates remaining low and global incidence continuing to rise [3]. The biological complexity of HCC is underscored by its diverse etiologies, including viral hepatitis, alcohol-related liver disease and metabolic disorders [4], as well as by its highly heterogeneous molecular and histopathological features. Over the past decade, molecular subtyping has emerged as a promising approach for understanding HCC heterogeneity [5], identifying clinically relevant subgroups and informing targeted therapies. Previous studies based primarily on transcriptomic or single-omics data have proposed subtypes associated with metabolic, inflammatory and stromal features [6], highlighting the potential of molecular classification to improve clinical decision-making [7, 8].

However, several critical challenges remain unresolved. Many existing classifications rely on a single omics layer, such as mRNA expression, thereby capturing only a fraction of the molecular complexity of HCC [9]. This limitation often leads to unstable subtypes with poor reproducibility across cohorts [10]. In addition, most studies have employed a single clustering method, leaving results vulnerable to methodological bias [11]. Importantly, these earlier approaches rarely integrate spatial or single-cell data, which are essential for understanding tumor heterogeneity within its microenvironmental context. Consequently, despite progress in molecular stratification, the lack of robust, multi-dimensional subtyping frameworks continues to hinder the translation of molecular insights into clinical practice [12].

To address these gaps, we conducted a comprehensive integrative analysis of HCC by combining multi-omics integration, machine learning–based prognostic modeling and spatial transcriptomic profiling [13]. Using datasets from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and multiple independent validation cohorts, we applied ten advanced clustering algorithms to establish a consensus classification system that captures the molecular diversity of HCC with greater robustness. We further constructed and validated a prognostic model using an ensemble learning framework, systematically identifying key prognostic biomarkers [14]. Finally, we incorporated single-cell and spatial transcriptomic data to dissect the cellular origins, functional states and spatial localization of critical genes associated with tumor progression and patient outcomes [15].

Our findings reveal four reproducible molecular subtypes of HCC with distinct genomic, transcriptomic, immunological and clinical features. We demonstrate that these subtypes are characterized by specific patterns of immune activity, genomic instability, mutational landscapes and therapeutic sensitivities. In addition, we identify a panel of five core prognostic genes—CD24, DTYMK, PTTG1, STIP1 and UBE2S—that exhibit subtype-specific expression, prognostic relevance and spatial enrichment in malignant tumor regions. The remainder of this paper details (i) the integrative multi-omics approach used to define HCC subtypes, (ii) the development and validation of a machine learning–based prognostic model, (iii) the biological and clinical characterization of the identified subtypes and (iv) the single-cell and spatial transcriptomic analyses that elucidate the functional and spatial basis of tumor heterogeneity. Together, these results provide a novel framework for improving prognostic evaluation and advancing precision medicine in HCC (Fig. 1).

Material and methods

Preprocessing and integration of multi-omics and multicenter cohort data in HCC

To ensure that downstream multi-omics integration accurately reflects biological variability rather than technical artifacts, preprocessing steps were selected based on their widespread validation in large-scale cancer genomics studies. In particular, RNA-seq, microarray, methylation and mutation platforms exhibit substantial systematic differences; therefore, standard normalization, probe filtering and batch-effect correction were applied to improve cross-cohort comparability and robustness.

Multi-omics data of liver hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) were collected from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA, https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov), including patients with complete transcriptome profiles (mRNA and lncRNA), DNA methylation data, somatic mutation data and corresponding clinical information. Transcriptome data were obtained using the TCGAbiolinks R package [16] and mature miRNA identifiers were annotated with the miRBaseVersions.db package. Somatic mutation data were downloaded via TCGAbiolinks and processed using maftools [17]. DNA methylation data and matched clinical annotations were retrieved from the UCSC Xena browser (https://xenabrowser.net/).

To improve the generalizability and robustness of the analyses, additional validation cohorts were incorporated. Specifically, the GSE14520 dataset was retrieved from GEO and HCC-related data were downloaded from the International Cancer Genome Consortium (ICGC, https://dcc.icgc.org/).

High-throughput transcriptome sequencing data were normalized to TPM. For microarray datasets, preprocessing was performed using limma [18] including background correction, log₂ transformation and quantile normalization. When multiple probes corresponded to the same gene symbol, the probe with the highest average expression value was selected. Samples with OS < 1 month were excluded to minimize potential confounding. Batch effects across cohorts were corrected using ComBat from the sva package [19].

Single-cell RNA-seq data were obtained from GSE166635 [20] and spatial transcriptomics data (HCC1R and HCC4R) from GSE238264 [21].

Robust multi-omics consensus integration for HCC subtype identification

The selection of feature-filtering and clustering strategies was driven by the need to balance biological signal extraction with noise reduction in highly heterogeneous HCC datasets. Using variability-based filtering (MAD), prognostic relevance (Cox) and mutation frequency ensures that retained features reflect both molecular diversity and clinical significance. Moreover, multiple clustering algorithms were adopted because single methods are often sensitive to data structure, whereas consensus integration markedly improves reproducibility.

Sample identifiers were first matched across five omics layers (n = 333). TPM expression data were log₂-transformed prior to analysis. Promoter-associated CpG island probes were retained for methylation data. Non-synonymous mutations were defined and processed using maftools.

Feature selection used the getElites function in MOVICS [22]. For mRNA, lncRNA, miRNA and DNA methylation, the MAD method selected the top 1,500 variable features, followed by Cox regression (p < 0.05). For binary mutation data, the 5,000 most frequently mutated genes were identified and the top 5% were retained.

The optimal number of clusters was determined using getClustNum, integrating CPI, Gap statistics and Silhouette scores [23, 24]. Based on these metrics and prior HCC knowledge, four molecular subtypes were selected. Clustering was performed using ten algorithms (CIMLR, ConsensusClustering, SNF, iClusterBayes, PINSPlus, moCluster, NEMO, IntNMF, COCA, LRA) via getMOIC and consensus integration was conducted with getConsensusMOIC (distance = “euclidean”, linkage = “average”) [22, 25]. Survival differences were tested using Kaplan–Meier and log-rank tests.

Functional characterization of molecular subtypes via GSEA and GSVA

GSEA and GSVA were selected because they provide complementary perspectives on pathway activity: GSEA identifies coordinated gene set shifts at the group level, while GSVA quantifies continuous pathway activity per sample. The combination enhances biological interpretability of subtype differences.

Differential expression analysis was performed using runDEA (edgeR) [26] and subtype-specific markers were identified using runMarker. DEGs were filtered based on adjusted p-value < 0.05. Multiple testing correction used the Benjamini–Hochberg method.

GSEA was conducted using runGSEA with GO Biological Process gene sets, while GSVA [27] used Hallmark pathways to quantify sample-level pathway activation.

Immune cell infiltration profiling in LIHC

Six deconvolution algorithms were jointly applied because each computational method has different strengths in detecting specific immune populations. Using multiple tools provides a more comprehensive and cross-validated estimation of the tumor immune microenvironment.

Immune cell infiltration was quantified using TIMER [28], CIBERSORT [29], xCell [30], MCP-counter [31], quanTIseq [23] and EPIC [32]. FDR < 0.05 was considered significant.

Nearest template prediction (NTP) analysis

NTP was chosen because it allows single-sample classification without model retraining, making it ideal for cross-cohort validation where platform differences may cause model drift. This improves portability and clinical translational potential.

Subtype-specific signatures from the training cohort were used to build NTP templates. External validation datasets were standardized before prediction. Prediction confidence was evaluated using FDR < 0.05. Concordance with unsupervised clustering was assessed using Cohen’s kappa.

Construction of a consensus machine learning-based prognostic signature

A consensus machine-learning (ML)strategy was adopted because different survival models capture distinct patterns (linear, nonlinear, high-dimensional, interaction-based). Constructing 99 model combinations reduces the risk of algorithm-specific bias, ensuring stable and generalizable prognostic performance.

Gene expression matrices were Z-score normalized. TCGA-HCC served as the training set; GEO and ICGC cohorts were used for external validation.

Ten algorithms were integrated: CoxBoost, stepwise Cox, Lasso, Ridge, Enet, survival-SVM, GBM, SuperPC, plsRcox and RSF. Ninety-nine models were generated and evaluated using C-index across validation cohorts. The model with the highest mean C-index was selected.

Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA)

WGCNA was selected because it identifies coordinated gene modules rather than isolated DEGs, enabling discovery of biologically coherent pathways and potential regulatory hubs relevant to HCC phenotypes.

The top 25% most variable genes (MAD) were used. A scale-free network was constructed with pickSoftThreshold (R2 > 0.9). Adjacency and TOM matrices were computed and modules were detected using dynamic tree cutting (min size = 100). Module eigengenes were correlated with clinical traits. Genes with |GS| > 0.5 and |MM| > 0.5 were defined as hub candidates.

Drug sensitivity prediction

pRRophetic was chosen as it is the most extensively validated algorithm for transcriptome-based drug response inference and allows systematic exploration of subtype-specific therapeutic vulnerabilities when experimental drug-sensitivity data are unavailable.

IC50 values were estimated for each sample and compared among subtypes. Predicted sensitivities were integrated with pathway activity and immune characteristics to enhance biological interpretability.

Functional enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes

GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were employed to systematically characterize biological functions and pathways represented in DEGs, providing mechanistic insight into molecular differences among subtypes or phenotypes.

Analyses were performed using clusterProfiler [33], with FDR < 0.05 defining significance.

Single-cell and spatial transcriptomic atlas of hepatocellular carcinoma

Single-cell and spatial transcriptomics (scRNA-seq and ST, respectively) were included to complement bulk multi-omics by offering fine-resolution cellular composition and spatial context. Using both improves interpretation of subtype-specific microenvironment features.

scRNA-seq data were processed using Seurat [33], with mitochondrial filtering, normalization, scaling, Harmony batch correction, clustering and t-SNE visualization. Marker-based cell-type annotation was performed.

Spatial transcriptomics from GSE238264 were analyzed to assess spatial expression patterns and microenvironmental organization.

Cell-cell communication analysis using CellChat

CellChat was selected because ligand–receptor signaling analysis provides functional interpretation of microenvironment interactions that cannot be inferred from bulk data alone.

CellChat workflows included identifying overexpressed ligands/receptors, computing communication probabilities, pathway aggregation, network visualization and signaling role quantification.

Spatial transcriptomic analysis and trajectory mapping

Spatial trajectory inference was added to capture dynamic gene-expression gradients associated with malignant progression, complementing static cellular composition analysis.

ST data were processed with Seurat, deconvolved using SpaCET [34], normalized with SCTransform, clustered using UMAP and visualized with SpatialFeaturePlot. SPATA2 was used for trajectory mapping to characterize spatial changes linked to tumor evolution.

Cell lines

The liver cancer cell line HepG2 and the normal hepatic stellate cell line LX-2 were obtained from Wuhan Pricella Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China. Cells were cultured and maintained under the supplier’s recommended conditions and all experiments were performed when the cells were in the logarithmic growth phase.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted from liver cancer cells (HepG2) and normal hepatic stellate cells (LX-2) using TRIzol Reagent (Invitrogen, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA purity and concentration were determined using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized from 1 μg of total RNA using the PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit (Takara, Japan) following the manufacturer’s protocol.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) was performed on a QuantStudio 6 Flex Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, USA) using SYBR Green Master Mix (Applied Biosystems, USA). The reaction mixture (20 μL) contained 10 μL SYBR Green Master Mix, 0.5 μL each of forward and reverse primers (10 μM), 2 μL cDNA template and nuclease-free water. The thermal cycling conditions were as follows: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s and 60 °C for 60 s.

Each sample was run in triplicate. The relative expression levels of target genes (CD24, STIP1, DTYMK, PTTG1, UBE2S) were normalized to GAPDH as an internal control and calculated using the 2^–ΔΔCt method.

Results

Multi-omics consensus prognostic molecular subtypes of HCC

The workflow of this research is shown in Fig. 1. To systematically characterize the molecular heterogeneity of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), we performed integrative clustering across multiple omics layers, including mRNA, lncRNA, miRNA, DNA CpG methylation and somatic mutation data. Ten state-of-the-art multi-omics clustering algorithms were initially applied independently to the dataset and the optimal number of clusters was determined based on multiple criteria, including the Clustering Prediction Index (CPI), Gap statistics, silhouette coefficient and prior knowledge from HCC-related studies.

Subsequently, a consensus ensemble approach was employed to integrate the clustering results (Figs. S1 A-B), yielding four robust molecular subtypes (CS1–CS4, Consensus Subtypes 1–4) with distinct multi-omics profiles (Figs. 2A–C). These subtypes exhibited characteristic patterns across transcriptomic, epigenetic and genomic layers: for example, specific mRNAs, lncRNAs, miRNAs, CpG methylation sites and frequently mutated genes were differentially distributed among the subtypes, reflecting their unique molecular landscapes.

Survival analysis demonstrated that these subtypes were significantly associated with overall survival (OS) (p < 0.05; Fig. 2D). Notably, patients in CS3 and CS4 exhibited markedly better survival outcomes compared to those in CS1 and CS2, indicating that this multi-omics consensus subtyping framework effectively stratifies HCC patients with prognostic relevance.

Biological and genomic characteristics of HCC molecular subtypes

To elucidate the molecular features of the identified hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) subtypes, differentially expressed genes (DEGs) among the four molecular subtypes (CS1–CS4) were identified using the limma package. The top 100 DEGs (adjusted p < 0.05 and |log₂FC| > 1) were selected for visualization, revealing distinct expression patterns across subtypes (Fig. 3A).

Biological and Genomic Characteristics of HCC Molecular Subtypes. (a) Heatmap Displaying Differentially Expressed Genes (DEGs) Across the four Molecular Subtypes. (b) gene set variation analysis (GSVA) showing distinct pathway enrichment patterns among the subtypes. C) gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) of DEGs using gene ontology biological processes (GOBP) gene sets. d) comparison of tumor mutational burden (TMB) across the four molecular subtypes. e) proportions of genomic alterations within each subtype. F) comparison of mutation frequencies for key genes among the four subtypes. (G–J) predicted drug sensitivity (IC50 values) for gemcitabine (g), lapatinib (h), Sunitinib (i) and sorafenib (j) across the molecular subtypes

Subsequently, Gene Set Variation Analysis (GSVA) was employed to quantify pathway activity and assess 21 biologically relevant pathways related to immune regulation and cellular functions, including antigen processing, B-cell and T-cell functions, cytotoxicity, cytokine signaling, chemokine activity, complement activation, Toll-like receptor signaling, TNF superfamily signaling and cell cycle regulation. The results demonstrated that CS1 and CS3 exhibited higher enrichment scores in immune-related pathways, indicative of an immune-activated phenotype, whereas CS2 and CS4 generally showed lower enrichment, suggesting an immune-suppressive or immune-silent state (Fig. 3B).

To further delineate subtype-specific biological processes, Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis was performed on upregulated genes, highlighting notable differences among subtypes (Fig. 3C). CS1 and CS3 were predominantly enriched in RNA processing and nuclear export pathways (e.g., RNA splicing, mRNA export, ribonucleoprotein complex biogenesis), mitotic processes (e.g., sister chromatid segregation) and energy metabolism pathways (e.g., cellular respiration, mitochondrial respiratory chain assembly). In contrast, CS2 and CS4 exhibited lower or negative enrichment in these pathways, indicating reduced activity in transcriptional, metabolic and mitotic functions. Furthermore, processes associated with coagulation, wound healing and amino acid and fatty acid metabolism were preferentially enriched in CS1 and CS3, whereas CS2 and CS4 displayed repressed activity, suggesting biologically inert characteristics (Fig. 3C). Collectively, these findings indicate that CS1 and CS3 are metabolically and transcriptionally active subtypes with heightened immune signaling, while CS2 and CS4 may represent biologically quiescent or immune-suppressed subtypes.

Tumor mutational burden (TMB) analysis revealed no significant differences among the four subtypes, suggesting that TMB is not a primary determinant of subtype stratification (Fig. 3D). In contrast, analyses of genomic instability demonstrated marked differences in the fraction of genome altered (FGA), with CS1 displaying the highest FGA ( > 0.3) and CS4 the lowest, indicating a gradient from genomic instability to genomic stability across subtypes. Further examination of the fraction of genome gained (FGG) and lost (FGL) corroborated these findings, with CS1 showing the highest values for both FGG and FGL and CS4 exhibiting the lowest (Kruskal-Wallis test, p < 0.01) (Fig. 3E).

Mutation frequency analysis of key driver genes revealed that TP53 and CTNNB1 were the most frequently altered, with frequencies of 27% and 26%, respectively. Notably, TP53 mutations were enriched in CS1 and CS3, whereas CTNNB1 mutations were predominantly observed in CS2 and CS4, suggesting subtype-specific oncogenic mechanisms (Fig. 3F).

Finally, predicted drug sensitivity was evaluated for four clinically relevant anticancer agents: Gemcitabine, Lapatinib, Sunitinib and Sorafenib. CS1 exhibited the lowest estimated IC50 values across all drugs, indicating the highest chemosensitivity, whereas CS4 showed the highest IC50 values, consistent with a more resistant phenotype (Figs. 3G–J). These findings provide a mechanistic and pharmacogenomic rationale for tailoring therapeutic strategies according to molecular subtype.

Immune landscape and stability validation of HCC consensus subtypes

To investigate the immune contexture associated with the four consensus molecular subtypes of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), we first performed a comprehensive analysis of immune cell infiltration using six independent computational frameworks (TIMER, CIBERSORT, CIBERSORT-ABS, quanTIseq, MCP-counter, xCell and EPIC). This integrative approach revealed pronounced subtype-specific immune signatures (Fig. 4A). Among them, CS1 was characterized by markedly elevated infiltration of B cells, CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells, neutrophils, macrophages and myeloid dendritic cells, consistent with an immune-inflamed phenotype. In contrast, other subtypes exhibited comparatively reduced immune cell infiltration, indicative of heterogeneous tumor–immune microenvironmental states.

To evaluate the reproducibility and generalizability of the proposed classification, we constructed subtype-specific classifiers based on the top 20 upregulated genes within each subtype. These classifiers were then applied to external validation cohorts using the Nearest Template Prediction (NTP) algorithm, which enables single-sample subtype assignment without prior model training. Application of NTP to the GSE14520 cohort successfully reproduced the four consensus subtypes, with CS2 consistently associated with superior overall survival, thus recapitulating the prognostic patterns observed in the discovery cohort (Fig. 4B–C).

To further assess classification stability, we compared the concordance between the consensus molecular subtypes and those predicted using two independent methods, NTP and Prediction Analysis of Microarrays (PAM). Cross-method consistency was high, with statistically significant agreement observed between the consensus ensemble, NTP and PAM across both TCGA-HCC and GSE14520 cohorts (κ > 0.68, all p < 0.001; Fig. 4D–F). These results underscore the robustness and reproducibility of the proposed subtyping framework across independent datasets and computational strategies.

Collectively, these analyses demonstrate that the consensus molecular subtypes of HCC capture distinct immune microenvironmental features and retain stable prognostic value across diverse cohorts and classification algorithms, highlighting their potential clinical utility for patient stratification and therapeutic decision-making.

Systemic identification of hub genes and pathways in HCC via WGCNA reveals splicing machinery dysregulation

To elucidate the landscape of gene networks dysregulated in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), we implemented weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) on transcriptomic data from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA). We selected the top 50% of genes (n = 9718) by median absolute deviation (MAD) from 333 HCC tumor samples, rigorously excluding outlier samples from an initial cohort of 423 (Fig. 5A). This stringent approach ensured the robustness of downstream analysis.

(b) hierarchical clustering dendrogram of genes, with modules identified via dynamic tree cut. (c) correlation between the module eigengene of the blue module (MEblue) and gene significance (GS). (d) correlation between the module eigengene of the turquoise module (MEturquoise) and gene significance. (e) GO and KEGG functional enrichment analyses of genes within the MEblue and MEturquoise modules. (f) volcano plot displaying differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in the TCGA-HCC cohort. Construction of a scale-free co-expression network, achieved with a soft-thresholding power of 9 (scale-free R2 = 0.9; Fig. 5A), identified four distinct gene modules (Fig. 5B). Notably, the MEblue and MEturquoise modules exhibited strong positive correlations (r = 0.6, p < 1e-33 and p < 2e-33, respectively) with the CS1 HCC subtype (Fig. 5B). This pointed to their critical involvement in defining the molecular characteristics of this clinically relevant HCC subtype. We then focused on identifying hub genes, using module membership (MM > 0.5) and gene significance (GS > 0.5) as dual selection criteria. This yielded a set of 272 high-confidence hub genes within the MEblue and MEturquoise modules.

Functional enrichment analysis of these hub genes, utilizing Gene Ontology (GO) and KEGG pathways, illuminated key biological processes (Fig. 5E). Strikingly, we observed a significant enrichment of RNA splicing-related processes (Biological Process; BP), including RNA splicing, mRNA splicing via spliceosome and transesterification reactions, indicating a central role in post-transcriptional regulation. Concordantly, Cellular Component (CC) analysis revealed that these genes were predominantly localized to spliceosome complexes. Molecular Function (MF) analysis further demonstrated enrichment in various RNA-binding activities, such as pre-mRNA, snRNA and single-stranded RNA binding, implicating these genes in splice site recognition and snRNA-mediated regulation. KEGG pathway analysis reinforced these findings, revealing significant enrichment in pathways related to the spliceosome, mRNA surveillance and nucleocytoplasmic transport. Furthermore, enrichment in immune-related pathways like Fc gamma R-mediated phagocytosis and Shigellosis suggested potential roles in inflammatory responses or host-pathogen interactions (Fig. 5E).

To pinpoint potential therapeutic targets, we conducted differential expression analysis on the TCGA-HCC cohort (373 tumor and 50 adjacent normal samples). We identified 1,608 differentially expressed genes (DEGs; |log₂ fold change| > 1, FDR < 0.05), visualized in a volcano plot (Fig. 5F). Intersection of these DEGs with the 272 WGCNA-derived hub genes revealed a crucial set of 46 overlapping genes (Fig. 5G), including GITI, MYL6B, TSEN54 and others. These 46 genes, poised to be pivotal regulators in HCC progression, warrant further investigation to elucidate their precise roles and regulatory mechanisms in liver cancer.

A consensus machine learning-based prognostic signature reveals high-risk stratification in HCC

To develop a robust prognostic classifier for hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), we implemented an integrated learning strategy using a multi-model consensus prediction framework (Fig. 6A). We trained 101 combinations of machine learning algorithms on the TCGA-HCC cohort and quantitatively evaluated their performance based on the average concordance index (C-index) across independent validation cohorts (GSE14520 and ICGC). The ensemble model combining CoxBoost and survival support vector machine (survival-SVM) demonstrated superior predictive performance, exhibiting the highest average C-index across the validation datasets. This model was therefore selected as the final prognostic signature.

Construction and prognostic evaluation of the consensus machine learning-based predictive model. (a) workflow for developing predictive models using 99 combinations of machine learning algorithms. The concordance index (C-index) for each model was calculated across the TCGA-HCC, GSE14520 and ICGC cohorts, with models ranked based on the average C-index in the validation sets. (b) key prognostic genes identified using the CoxBoost algorithm. (C–E) Kaplan–Meier survival analyses comparing high- and low-risk groups in the TCGA-HCC, GSE14520 and ICGC cohorts, respectively, showing significant differences in overall survival. (F-H) visualization of risk scores, risk stratification, survival status and expression profiles of prognostic genes across the TCGA-HCC, GSE14520 and ICGC cohorts

Within this ensemble, the CoxBoost–survivalSVM framework enabled efficient feature selection, ultimately identifying a 5-gene signature comprising UBE2S, CD24, PTTG1, DTYMK and STIP1 (Fig. 6B). Risk scores derived from this 5-gene signature robustly stratified patients across multiple independent validation cohorts (TCGA, GSE14520 and ICGC), with Kaplan–Meier survival analysis demonstrating significantly poorer overall survival in the high-risk groups (Figs. 6C–E; log-rank p < 0.001, p = 0.00085 and p = 0.00011, respectively).

Importantly, the predictive performance of the model was further supported by its C-index values, which were 0.650 (95% CI: 0.588–0.713) in the TCGA training cohort, 0.706 (95% CI: 0.619–0.793) in the ICGC validation cohort and 0.616 (95% CI: 0.560–0.673) in the GSE14520 cohort, indicating consistent and reliable prognostic discrimination across datasets.

Analysis of risk score distributions revealed significant differences between high- and low-risk groups across the TCGA, GSE14520 and ICGC cohorts, confirming the signature’s strong discriminative ability (Fig. 6. 6F–H). Within these risk-stratified groups, deceased patients (red) were predominantly clustered in the high-risk group, whereas survivors (green) were mainly distributed in the low-risk group, further validating the model’s reliability in prognostic prediction. Moreover, expression profiles of the five key prognostic genes (UBE2S, CD24, PTTG1, DTYMK and STIP1) were consistently and positively correlated with the calculated risk scores across different cohorts (Fig. 6. 6F–H), suggesting their functional relevance in the risk prediction model and their potential as therapeutic targets.

Performance evaluation of the prognostic model across multiple cohorts

To comprehensively evaluate the robustness and generalizability of the prognostic model, we validated its performance across the TCGA, GSE14520, ICGC and integrated Meta-cohort datasets (Fig. 7A). The results demonstrated that the model consistently exhibited reliable prognostic discrimination across all four independent cohorts.

Dots indicate hazard ratios (HRs) and horizontal lines represent the 95% confidence intervals. The dashed line denotes hr = 0.6. (B–D) time-dependent ROC curves showing the 1-, 3- and 5-year overall survival (os) predictive performance of the model in the TCGA, ICGC and GSE14520 cohorts. (e) differences in restricted mean survival time (RMS) at 1 year and 2 years after treatment between high- and low-risk groups. In the forest plot comparison (Fig. 7A), the model showed the best performance in the TCGA cohort, presenting the highest hazard ratio (HR) and the narrowest 95% confidence interval, indicating the strongest predictive ability in the training dataset. The ICGC and GSE14520 cohorts displayed similarly strong and stable predictive performance, with HRs remaining significant and all confidence intervals located to the right of the reference dashed line (HR = 0.6), suggesting good generalizability in external validation cohorts. In contrast, many previously published models exhibited substantial variability across cohorts, with some showing HR values close to the reference line, indicating weaker stability. The integrated Meta-cohort further confirmed the robustness of our model across multi-cohort combined analyses.

To further assess the time-dependent predictive performance of the model, we generated time-dependent ROC curves for 1- year, 3- year and 5-year overall survival (OS) in the TCGA, ICGC and GSE14520 cohorts (Fig. 7B–D). In the TCGA cohort, the model achieved AUCs of 0.676, 0.641 and 0.640 at 1, 3 and 5 years, respectively, indicating stable predictive capability. In the ICGC cohort, the AUCs reached 0.733, 0.731 and 0.831, showing superior performance in external validation and suggesting strong transferability. In the GSE14520 cohort, the AUCs at the three time points were 0.644, 0.653 and 0.615, reflecting consistently moderate-to-high predictive accuracy. Overall, the model demonstrated stable and reliable prognostic performance across multiple cohorts and time points.

To comprehensively evaluate the applicability of our model in hepatocellular carcinoma (LIHC) immunotherapy and its cross-cancer predictive capability, we applied the model to the IMvigor-MUC cohort to validate its performance in the immunotherapy setting. Unlike previous studies, when assessing long-term survival differences after 3 months of treatment, we compared the restricted mean survival time (RMS) at 12 and 24 months between the low- and high-risk groups to account for the potential delayed clinical effects of immunotherapy (p < 0.05; Fig. 7E–F). The results showed that patients in the low-risk group had significantly better prognoses than those in the high-risk group, suggesting that they may derive greater clinical benefit from immunotherapy. Further analysis of risk score distributions across different immunotherapy response categories revealed significant differences between PR and PD, as well as between PD and CR (p < 0.05; Fig. 7G), further supporting the potential value of the model in predicting immunotherapeutic efficacy.

Risk score stratification unveils distinct immune microenvironment and therapeutic sensitivity profiles in HCC

To delineate the biological implications of the risk score within the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME), we performed a comprehensive analysis of immune cell infiltration and therapeutic sensitivity in high- and low-risk HCC subgroups. Our findings revealed marked differences in immune cell composition (Fig. 8A), with the low-risk group exhibiting significant enrichment of CD8+ T cells, CD4+ T cells, M1 macrophages, activated dendritic cells and NK cells. Conversely, the high-risk group displayed elevated levels of myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) and enhanced T cell co-inhibitory signaling, indicative of a profoundly immunosuppressive phenotype (Fig. 8A).

(b) predicted immunotherapy response differences between high-risk and low-risk patients, as assessed by the tide algorithm. (c) distribution of myeloid-derived suppressor cell (MDSC) characteristics between risk groups. (d) comparison of TIDE-related features across high-risk and low-risk patients. Assessment of tumor signature pathways demonstrated significant activation of pro-proliferative and DNA repair pathways in the high-risk group (Fig. S1F), highlighting enhanced proliferative capacity and genome stability maintenance. Notably, pathways related to exosome activity and ferroptosis were also significantly upregulated in the high-risk group, suggesting a greater capacity for immune evasion and treatment resistance.

Analysis of immuno-oncology biomarkers revealed distinct patterns of distribution across risk groups (Fig. S1G). The high-risk group exhibited significantly elevated scores for TMEscore_CIR, TMEscoreA_CIR, T cell inflamed GEP, Immune_Checkpoint and Mismatch_Repair, suggesting heightened immune-related signaling activity, potentially associated with complex immune regulation or increased genomic instability. Conversely, TMEscoreB_CIR and Pan_F_TBRs were significantly higher in the low-risk group, indicative of a more favorable immune microenvironment with increased immunogenicity, potentially leading to improved responses to immunotherapy.

Computational prediction of immunotherapy responses using the TIDE algorithm indicated significantly higher predicted response rates in the low-risk group compared to the high-risk group (Fig. 8B; Fisher’s exact test p < 0.001), supporting the notion of greater immunotherapy sensitivity in the low-risk subgroup. Concordantly, the high-risk group exhibited significantly elevated MDSC levels, overall TIDE scores and T cell exclusion scores (Figs. 8C-D, F), further suggesting enhanced immune evasion and poorer immunotherapy responsiveness. Interestingly, T cell dysfunction scores were significantly higher in the low-risk group (Fig. 8E), indicating potential T cell exhaustion due to prolonged immune responses.

Finally, drug sensitivity predictions based on pRRophetic suggested that the low-risk group may exhibit relatively higher sensitivity to multiple chemotherapeutic and targeted agents, including Cisplatin, 5-Fluorouracil, Axitinib, Cediranib, Wee1 inhibitors, PD173074 and Alpelisib (Fig. 8G).

Single-cell transcriptomic analysis reveals CNV-driven heterogeneity and differential expression of prognostic hub genes in HCC epithelial subpopulations

To dissect the cellular heterogeneity of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) and its impact on gene expression, we performed a single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) analysis of HCC tissues using the GSE166635 dataset. After rigorous quality control, we retained 22,631 high-quality cells from two HCC patients for downstream analysis. Unsupervised clustering, dimensionality reduction and t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE) visualization identified 10 major cell types, including B cells, CD8+ T cells, dendritic cells (DCs), endothelial cells, epithelial cells, fibroblasts, mast cells, monocytes/macrophages, proliferating T cells (Tprolif) and regulatory T cells (Tregs) (Fig. S1C).

To specifically investigate epithelial cell heterogeneity, we re-clustered the epithelial cell population, revealing five distinct subpopulations (Fig. S1D). Copy number variation (CNV) analysis using inferCNV, with immune cells as a normal reference, demonstrated prominent CNV events specific to these subpopulations (Fig. 9B). Subpopulations 0, 2 and 3 exhibited significant copy number gains on chromosome 8 (chr8), while subpopulation 1 displayed copy number losses on chromosome 3 (chr3). Subpopulation 4, in contrast, showed minimal CNV alterations (Fig. 8B), suggesting a relatively normal epithelial cell phenotype. CNV scores confirmed these observations, with subpopulation 4 exhibiting the lowest and subpopulation 3 the highest CNV score (Fig. S1E). An integrated cellular atlas, visualizing the spatial distribution of the 11 major cell types in high-dimensional space using t-SNE, is shown in Fig. 9A.

CNV-Driven heterogeneity and functional characterization of key genes in hepatocellular carcinoma epithelial cell subpopulations. (a) t-SNE visualization of single-cell transcriptomic clustering of epithelial cells. (b) heatmap of inferred copy number variations (inferCNV) across epithelial subpopulations. (c) identification of subpopulation-specific marker genes. (d) volcano plot showing differentially expressed genes among epithelial cell subpopulations. (E–I) t-SNE plots illustrating the expression patterns of five hub genes across epithelial subpopulations

We validated the identified cell types using a panel of 53 established cell-type-specific marker genes (Fig. 9C), confirming the accuracy of cell classification. Differential gene expression analysis between epithelial subpopulations revealed distinct functional characteristics, with representative differentially expressed genes (DEGs) visualized using volcano plots (Fig. 9D). For example, KRT19, CLDN4 and EPCAM expression are the highest in the malignant epithelail clusters (Fig. 9C).

Finally, we examined the expression patterns of five key hub genes identified in our previous analysis—CD24, DTYMK, PTTG1, STIP1 and UBE2S—across the epithelial subpopulations (Fig. 9E–I). CD24 expression was specifically enriched in malignant epithelial cells (Fig. 9E), suggesting a critical role in tumorigenesis and progression. While DTYMK, PTTG1, STIP1 and UBE2S exhibited broader expression across multiple subpopulations, they demonstrated varying degrees of cell-type specificity (Fig. 9F–I), highlighting their potential roles in mediating heterogeneity within the tumor microenvironment.

Dissecting malignant epithelial heterogeneity in HCC reveals lineage hierarchy and distinct functional states

To characterize the molecular diversity within malignant epithelial cells in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), we performed subclustering, revealing four functionally distinct subpopulations: Malignant-1, Malignant-2, Malignant-3 and Malignant-4 (Fig. 10A). Differential gene expression analysis identified specific marker genes for each subpopulation, highlighting significant transcriptional heterogeneity (Fig. 10B) that underlies their distinct functional states.

Functional heterogeneity, lineage hierarchy and regulatory features of malignant epithelial cell subpopulations in HCC. (a) t-SNE visualization showing the clustering of malignant epithelial cells into distinct subpopulations. (b) volcano plot of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) among malignant epithelial subpopulations. (c) heatmap of pathway enrichment analyses highlighting functional distinctions across malignant subpopulations. (D–F) CytoTRACE analysis revealing differentiation states and lineage trajectories within malignant epithelial subpopulations. (G–J) KEGG pathway enrichment analyses of malignant subpopulations, illustrating signaling pathways associated with tumor progression and cellular heterogeneity

To comprehensively delineate the functional and regulatory landscape of these malignant epithelial subpopulations, we generated an integrated heatmap incorporating key genes, pathways and regulatory factors (Fig. 10C). This analysis included functional genes (e.g., USP5, SYK, CHEK2 and BGN), transcription factors (TFs) and TF cofactors, as well as surface protein markers. Functional annotations from GO Biological Processes (GO_BP), KEGG and WikiPathways related to immune regulation, cell cycle, DNA repair and metabolism were also integrated. The resulting heatmap provides a systems-level view of the molecular features defining each malignant subpopulation, confirming clear differences in activity for pathways across GO, KEGG and WikiPathways.

Functional enrichment analysis revealed striking biological distinctions among the subpopulations (Fig. 10C). Malignant-1 was significantly enriched in cell cycle, DNA replication and repair pathways, including mismatch repair, homologous recombination and the Fanconi anemia pathway, indicating high proliferative activity. Malignant-2 exhibited enrichment in steroid metabolism, bile secretion and cholesterol metabolism pathways, suggesting pronounced metabolic reprogramming. Malignant-3 was associated with immune activation and inflammatory pathways, such as TNF signaling, Toll-like receptor signaling and positive regulation of leukocyte activation, suggesting a highly immune-active state. Malignant-4 showed enrichment in complement activation, costimulatory signaling and endocytosis pathways, implicating it in immune regulation and antigen uptake. For example, PPP1R1A expression is greatest in Malignant-1, HSD17B6, GDF7 and CHEK2 expression is greatest in Malignant-3 and CETP expression is greatest in Malignant-2. Furthermore, multiple surface markers and transcription factors (e.g., SYK, PXN, PPP1R1A, SNHG15) displayed subpopulation-specific expression patterns, potentially governing functional states within each cluster.

CytoTRACE analysis predicted a differentiation gradient within the malignant subpopulations, with Malignant-3 exhibiting the highest stemness potential, followed by Malignant-4, Malignant-1 and Malignant-2, which appeared more differentiated (Figs. 10D–F). This suggests that these malignant subpopulations represent distinct stages in tumor progression, with unique lineage relationships and functional characteristics.

KEGG pathway enrichment analysis further characterized the subpopulations (Fig. 10. 10G–J). Malignant-1 was enriched for coenzyme biosynthesis, branched-chain amino acid metabolism, steroid hormone biosynthesis, cholesterol metabolism, complement and coagulation cascades, ribosome and amino acid metabolism pathways, highlighting metabolic reprogramming and immune modulation. Malignant-2 showed positive enrichment in pathways related to breast cancer, basal cell carcinoma, various O-glycosylation processes and glycosphingolipid biosynthesis, indicating oncogenic signaling and metabolic alterations. Malignant-3 was enriched in nucleotide excision repair, base excision repair, DNA replication, homologous recombination and mismatch repair pathways, reflecting genomic instability, while immune-related TNF signaling and cell adhesion molecule pathways were negatively enriched. Malignant-4 was significantly enriched in antigen processing and presentation, neutrophil extracellular trap formation and phagosome pathways, implicating its involvement in tumor immune surveillance and inflammation.

Collectively, these results reveal pronounced molecular and functional heterogeneity among malignant epithelial subpopulations in HCC, providing crucial insights into tumor biology and potential therapeutic targets.

In contrast, significant differences in signaling activity were observed within the malignant epithelial compartment: Malignant-1 exhibited the most extensive interaction profile, while Malignant-4 displayed the fewest (Fig. 11A, B). Deconstruction of outgoing signaling patterns from the malignant epithelial subpopulations (Malignant-1 to Malignant-4) demonstrated preferential communication with CD8 Tcm and cDC1 (type I conventional dendritic) cells (Figs. 11D–G). This highlights the importance of signals emanating from malignant cells in modulating the activity of these key immune populations. Our findings reveal a complex network of intercellular communication within the HCC TME, with the ANNEXIN pathway playing a particularly prominent role in mediating interactions between malignant cells and key immune components.

Joint validation of differentially expressed genes at the protein and transcript levels in liver cancer tissues

Integrated analysis of immunohistochemistry (IHC) and qPCR further delineated the differential expression patterns of five candidate genes between liver cancer and normal liver tissues, revealing highly consistent directional changes.

CD24, STIP1 and DTYMK exhibited markedly elevated protein expression in tumor tissues. IHC analysis showed moderate-to-strong positive staining for all three genes in tumor samples, with both staining intensity and area substantially higher than those observed in normal tissues, which generally displayed low-to-moderate staining. Corresponding quantitative analysis confirmed a significant increase in the proportion of IHC-positive areas in tumor samples. qPCR results were fully consistent with these findings: transcriptional levels of CD24, STIP1 and DTYMK were significantly higher in HepG2 liver cancer cells compared to normal hepatic stellate LX-2 cells (CD24, p = 0.0139; STIP1, p = 0.0103; DTYMK, p = 0.00162), further validating their upregulation in liver cancer.

In contrast, PTTG1 and UBE2S were more highly expressed in normal tissues. IHC demonstrated moderate staining of both genes in normal liver, whereas staining was markedly reduced in tumor tissues, accompanied by a significant decrease in positive area proportion. qPCR results similarly showed higher expression of PTTG1 and UBE2S in LX-2 cells compared with HepG2 cells (PTTG1, p = 0.000341; UBE2S, p = 5.92 × 10− 5), consistent with the observed protein expression trends (Fig. 14 A–T).

Discussion

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) remains the third leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide [35]. Accurate molecular subtyping is essential for understanding disease progression, identifying therapeutic vulnerabilities and guiding precision medicine. Gene expression is tightly regulated by genetic and epigenetic mechanisms, such as DNA methylation, gene mutations and histone modifications, suggesting that single-omics approaches may overlook important regulatory interactions. In contrast, multi-omics integration provides a more comprehensive view of tumor heterogeneity. Nevertheless, many previous studies have been constrained using a single omics layer and reliance on one clustering method, raising concerns about methodological bias and reproducibility. Our study aimed to overcome these limitations through multi-omics integration, algorithmic consensus clustering and multi-cohort validation.

In this work, we comprehensively analyzed the TCGA-HCC cohort and identified four reproducible molecular subtypes (MOCS subtypes). These subtypes displayed distinct clinical outcomes, immune infiltration patterns, genomic alterations, pathway enrichment profiles and drug sensitivities. Among them, CS1 emerged as the most aggressive subtype, with poor prognosis, high genomic instability and strong enrichment of proliferative and metabolic pathways, while CS4 was characterized by genomic stability, immune suppression and drug resistance. The robustness of this classification was confirmed across independent external datasets. Previous studies, such as those by Hoshida et al. and Boyault et al., have also proposed molecular subtypes of HCC based on transcriptomics. However, our study differs by integrating five omics layers and leveraging 10 advanced clustering algorithms, thereby minimizing methodological bias and achieving higher reproducibility.

Functionally, differential gene expression and pathway enrichment analyses revealed that CS1 and CS3 were enriched in immune-active pathways, including T cell–mediated cytotoxicity, cytokine signaling and NK cell responses, suggesting an inflamed microenvironment [36]. By contrast, CS2 and CS4 showed enrichment of immune suppression and escape mechanisms [37]. This dichotomy aligns with earlier observations that HCC tumors can be classified into immune-active and immune-silent phenotypes, but our study expands this framework by linking immune activity with genomic instability and drug sensitivity. Importantly, GO enrichment showed that CS1 and CS3 exhibited higher activity in nucleic acid metabolism, mitosis and mitochondrial respiration, consistent with highly proliferative tumors [38, 39].

At the genomic level, tumor mutational burden (TMB) did not differ significantly across subtypes, indicating that TMB alone is insufficient for molecular stratification in HCC [40]. Instead, measures of genomic instability—fraction of genome altered (FGA), gained (FGG) and lost (FGL)—proved more discriminatory, with CS1 showing the highest instability and CS4 the lowest [41]. Subtype-specific mutation patterns were also observed: TP53 mutations predominated in CS1 and CS3, consistent with their aggressive phenotype, while CTNNB1 mutations were enriched in CS2 and CS4, in line with previous reports linking CTNNB1 to immune exclusion [41, 42]. These findings underscore the biological relevance of our classification and suggest that distinct oncogenic pathways underlie different HCC subtypes.

Drug sensitivity predictions further highlighted clinically relevant differences. CS1 was predicted to be more responsive to gemcitabine, lapatinib, sunitinib and sorafenib, while CS4 was resistant to these agents. Such results support a precision oncology approach in which molecular subtypes guide therapy selection [43, 44]. Beyond subtyping, we developed a robust prognostic model based on 46 candidate genes identified from multi-omics integration. Using an ensemble learning strategy across 101 algorithmic combinations, the CoxBoost–survivalSVM framework yielded the best predictive performance. Validation in TCGA, GSE14520 and ICGC cohorts confirmed that the risk model reliably stratified patients into high- and low-risk groups with distinct survival outcomes [45–49].

Notably, five core genes—UBE2S, CD24, PTTG1, DTYMK and STIP1—emerged as consistent predictors of prognosis. Prior studies have implicated UBE2S and PTTG1 in cell cycle regulation [50, 51], while STIP1 has been linked to immune evasion and therapeutic resistance through chaperone-mediated pathways [52]. Our findings extend these observations by demonstrating their spatial enrichment in malignant tumor regions and specific expression in malignant epithelial subpopulations. Single-cell RNA sequencing further revealed that these genes contribute to tumor heterogeneity, immune regulation and cell–cell communication, with CD24 marking malignant epithelial cells and ANXA1–FPR3 signaling emerging as a critical immune interaction axis [53–55]. Spatial transcriptomics corroborated their spatial localization in high-malignancy niches [56, 57]. Together, these results suggest that these genes are not only prognostic markers but also potential therapeutic targets.

Beyond the molecular and immunological differences observed across subtypes, the distinct immune microenvironmental states identified in this study also have potential implications for therapeutic decision-making. In particular, the high-risk group, which exhibited an immune-cold phenotype characterized by reduced effector immune infiltration and enrichment of immunosuppressive pathways, may respond poorly to conventional immune checkpoint blockade. Emerging evidence suggests that alternative or combinatorial strategies may be more suitable for such immune-excluded tumors. For example, inhibition of the TGF-β pathway has been shown to remodel stromal barriers and restore intratumoral T-cell infiltration, thereby enhancing immunotherapy responsiveness in immune-cold HCC. Oncolytic virotherapy represents another promising strategy, as it can induce immunogenic cell death, promote chemokine production, recruit cytotoxic immune cells and convert noninflamed tumors into inflamed ones. Moreover, metabolic reprogramming—such as targeting lactate production, mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation, or lipid metabolism—may alleviate metabolic competition within the tumor microenvironment and improve antitumor immune activity. Although these considerations go beyond the scope of the current study, they provide potential therapeutic directions for future mechanistic investigations and clinical exploration, especially for patients classified into the high-risk, immune-cold subgroup.

Despite several strengths, this study has several limitations. First, although rigorous normalization and batch-effect correction were applied, residual heterogeneity across different platforms may still influence the results. Second, while the prognostic model and subtype framework were validated across multiple cohorts, the mechanistic roles of the five core genes still require further experimental validation. Third, drug sensitivity predictions were based on pRRophetic computational analyses and require confirmation in preclinical models and clinical trials. Fourth, single-cell and spatial transcriptomic analyses provided a modern dimension to the study, mainly for validating the enrichment of core prognostic genes (such as CD24, UBE2S, PTTG1, DTYMK and STIP1) in malignant epithelial cells and revealing their spatial heterogeneity. Although the sample size is limited, these observations are primarily descriptive and should be interpreted with caution, yet they still provide valuable information for supporting bulk analysis findings and guiding future experimental design. Finally, large-scale, prospective, multi-center studies are essential to establish the translational applicability of our model in clinical practice.

Overall, our study presents a comprehensive molecular classification of HCC based on multi-omics integration, robust consensus clustering and machine learning–driven prognostic modeling. We highlight the biological and clinical heterogeneity of HCC subtypes, identify five core prognostic genes with potential therapeutic value and provide novel insights into their spatial and cellular context. Future research should focus on mechanistic validation of these genes, exploration of subtype-specific therapeutic strategies and integration of prospective clinical data to advance precision medicine for HCC.
