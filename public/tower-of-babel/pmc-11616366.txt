Partial vitreous occlusion of Ahmed glaucoma valve implant with controlled intraocular pressure: a case report

Background

The Ahmed glaucoma valve (AGV) implant, a glaucoma drainage device that evacuates aqueous humor to an external reservoir, has been shown to be effective in lowering intraocular pressure (IOP) [1, 2]. However, postoperative complications also have been reported, including hypotony, excessive capsule fibrosis and clinical failure, vitreous occlusion of the tube, erosion of the tube or plate edge, strabismus, and infection [3–5].

Among the complications, vitreous occlusion of an AGV implant tube is rare but potentially devastating, as it could lead to elevated IOP due to obstruction of aqueous outflow or retinal issues such as retinal detachment caused by vitreous traction. Previous studies have documented cases wherein occlusion was effectively managed through laser treatment or surgery [6, 7]. However, there have been no reports of partial vitreous occlusion of the tube, nor of any instances where the IOP was stabilized with medical treatment.

In this case report, I present a unique instance of partial vitreous occlusion of an AGV implant wherein IOP could be controlled through utilization of topical antiglaucoma medications.

Case presentation

An 86-year-old Korean male with primary open-angle glaucoma in the left eye visited our ophthalmology clinic with uncontrolled IOP despite maximal medical treatment. He had undergone cataract surgery at 45 and 72 years of age in the right and left eye, respectively, as well as penetrating keratoplasty twice in the right eye at 67 and 74 years of age, the latter two cases due to bullous keratopathy. He had no family history of ocular disease. At the same visit, he had no light perception in the right eye due to corneal graft rejection, and the best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) in the left eye was 20/25. His IOP was 12 mmHg and 30 mmHg in the right and left eyes, respectively, as measured by Goldmann applanation tonometry, despite using topical antiglaucoma medications—latanoprost once daily and brimonidine and dorzolamide-timolol twice daily—in the left eye. Anterior-segment examination of the left eye showed vitreous prolapse, which was confined adjacent to the pupillary margin, and gonioscopy revealed an open angle. Further examination of the left optic nerve revealed glaucomatous optic nerve head cupping with a vertical cup-to-disc ratio of 0.7 and diffuse thinning of the retinal nerve fiber layer (RNFL) on both RNFL photography (Vx-10; Kowa Optimed, Tokyo, Japan) and optical coherence tomography (Cirrus HD-OCT; Carl Zeiss Meditec, Dublin, CA, USA) with corresponding superior and inferior visual field defects measured by standard automated perimetry (Humphrey Field Analyzer II; Carl Zeiss Meditec). Given the uncontrolled IOP despite maximal medical treatment, AGV implantation was performed in the left eye.

On the first postoperative day, his IOP had decreased to 8 mmHg in the left eye, and the AGV tube was patent. Therefore, we recommended that he discontinue using topical antiglaucoma medications and start on postoperatively prescribed eyedrops including topical antibiotics and a steroid. At postoperative 1-week, his BCVA was 20/28 while the IOP remained stable, which was 9 mmHg in the left eye.

At postoperative 1-month, the IOP in the left eye was elevated to 25 mmHg, and partial vitreous occlusion of the AGV tube was identified. Fundus photography and optical coherence tomography (OCT) revealed a flat retina without cystoid macular edema. To release the occlusion, neodymium:yttrium–aluminum-garnet (Nd:YAG) laser vitreolysis (32 shots, 6.0–8.0 mJ) directed at a vitreous strand was performed. Also, we prescribed for the left eye, additionally to the postoperative eyed rops, the topical medications latanoprost (once daily) and dorzolamide-timolol (twice daily). Two days post-laser treatment, the IOP was normalized to 9 mmHg with persistent partial vitreous occlusion of the AGV tube (Fig. 1). Anterior-segment OCT also confirmed the vitreous strand occluding the tube (Fig. 2). We planned either to repeat Nd:YAG laser vitreolysis or perform an anterior vitrectomy. However, as the patient declined any additional laser treatment or surgery, we revised our plan to close monitoring for potential elevation in IOP resulting from further vitreous occlusion or retinal issues such as detachment caused by vitreous traction.

Thereafter, the IOP remained stable throughout the entire follow-up period: 10 mmHg, 8 mmHg, and 9 mmHg at 2 months, 3 months, and 6 months postoperatively, respectively, without any retinal complications.

Discussion and conclusions

Prompt intervention may be necessary to mechanically release vitreous occlusion of an AGV implant tube in instances where complete occlusion results in uncontrolled elevation of IOP despite medical treatment. Fortunately, our patient experienced only partial occlusion of the tube, and his IOP could be controlled using anti-glaucoma medications. There are two possible explanations for the partial occlusion: first, the amount of vitreous might have been too small to completely occlude the tube inlet; second, as our preoperative examination revealed, the vitreous was confined around the pupillary margin, which might have made for insufficient proximity between the vitreous and the tube inlet.

Regarding IOP, there was an unexpected rise noted in our patient at postoperative 1-month, which contrasted with the earlier postoperative periods. Two mechanisms may have contributed to this: first, partial vitreous occlusion of the tube could have resulted in decreased aqueous outflow; second, the patient might have been going through a hypertensive phase, which is characterized by an IOP rise secondary to bleb encapsulation that peaks at postoperative 1-month in patients undergoing AGV implantation [8, 9]. It is possible that the combined actions of the two mechanisms synergistically led to elevation of IOP in our patient.

In the literature, a few other cases of postoperative ocular hypertension resulting from vitreous occlusion of an AGV tube have also been reported [6, 7], although the treatment strategies differed from those in the current study. Salvador et al. [6] related that two patients (11.76%) with refractory glaucoma experienced blockage of the AGV tube by vitreous after surgery and were treated with Nd:YAG laser (one case) and vitrectomy (one case). Similarily, a case series by Gu et al. [7] showed that three patients with secondary glaucoma had elevated IOP due to tube blockage by vitreous, all of which were resolved using Nd:YAG laser, resulting in stabilized IOP.

With regard to the types of occlusion, as classified by its location, the previous cases [6, 7] and the present one are similar in that they all involved distal tube occlusion, which occurs in the anterior chamber, ciliary sulcus, or pars plana [10]. The other type is proximal tube obstruction, which presents at the tube–plate junction due to fibrous ingrowth [10, 11]. However, as classified by the extent of occlusion, the current case was partial tube occlusion, in contrast to the complete occlusion observed in the earlier cases [6, 7].

To sum up, in contrast to the relevant earlier studies, in which laser or surgical treatment was performed to control IOP, it is noteworthy that we are reporting, for the first time, a case of a partially vitreous-occluded AGV implant tube, in which IOP could be medically controlled. Although complete removal of vitreous occlusion is desirable, it is suggested that when this is not feasible, close monitoring for possible complications such as progression of vitreous occlusion or retinal diseases arising from vitreous traction is needed.

In conclusion, I present herein a special case of partial vitreous occlusion of an AGV implant tube, wherein IOP could be controlled using topical antiglaucoma medications. In cases where removal of vitreous is impracticable, close follow-up for potential complications including IOP elevation and retinal detachment is advisable.
