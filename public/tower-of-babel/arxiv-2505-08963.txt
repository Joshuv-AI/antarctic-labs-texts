Bandlimited signal reconstruction from orthogonally-projected data
Nguyen T. Thao, Marek Mi skowicz

Abstract
We show that a broad class of signal acquisition schemes can be interpreted as recording data from a signal x x in a space 𝒰 {\mathscr{U}} (typically, though not exclusively, a space of bandlimited functions) via an orthogonal projection w = P 𝒱 ​ x w=P_{{\mathscr{V}}}x onto another space 𝒱 {\mathscr{V}} . A basic reconstruction method in this case consists in alternating projections between the input space 𝒰 {\mathscr{U}} and the affine space 𝒲 {\mathscr{W}} of signals u u satisfying P 𝒱 ​ u = w P_{{\mathscr{V}}}u=w (POCS method). Although this method is classically known to be slow, our work reveals new insights and contributions: (i) it applies to new complex encoders emerging from event-based sampling, for which no faster reconstruction method is currently available; (ii) beyond perfect reconstruction, it converges robustly under insufficient (e.g., sub-Nyquist) or inconsistent data (due to noise or errors); (iii) the limit of convergence achieves optimal least-squares approximations under such conditions; (iv) semi-convergence inherently results in regularized reconstructions under ill-posed data acquisition conditions; (v) when w w is produced by discrete sampling, the iterative method can be rigorously discretized for DSP implementation — even with non-separable input spaces 𝒰 {\mathscr{U}} . While moving beyond the traditional focus on perfect reconstruction in harmonic analysis, our analysis preserves the deterministic framework of infinite-dimensional Hilbert spaces, consistent with Shannon’s sampling theory. For illustration, we apply our proposed theory to two contrasting sampling situations: multi-channel time encoding and nonuniform point sampling in a Sobolev space.

Introduction

The growing interest in event-based sampling
[
1
]
has renewed attention to signal reconstruction from
nonuniform samples. By tradition, the theoretical research on this subject had been mostly focused on perfect reconstruction of bandlimited signals from point samples

[

[

. The proposed reconstruction algorithm, which we will call the LT method, was an adaptation of an iterative algorithm originally designed in

for point sampling. This growing research is however limited in two aspects: (a) a difficulty to generalize the LT algorithm for sampling schemes beyond the pure integration of a bandlimited signal; (b) a lack of theoretical framework for the analysis of the LT method under noisy or insufficient sampling. On the first aspect, numerical results from
[
12
]
showed that the LT algorithm is no longer guaranteed to converge under leaky integration even with oversampling. A proposed extension of this algorithm to multi-channel signal encoding in
[
11
]
did yield numerical convergence, but as an empirical observation without existing theoretical justifications. On the second aspect, the LT algorithm remains the result of research in harmonic analysis specifically geared to situations of perfect reconstruction, as mentioned above. The analysis of noisy and insufficient sampling is however of major importance in practice. Note the basic fact that the finite sampling of a bandlimited signal is automatically insufficient in the traditional
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
space of Shannon’s sampling theory.

In this article, we propose a theoretical framework of sampling schemes that have potential applications in event-based sampling and benefit from an available iterative algorithm of bandlimited input reconstruction, with the following property: it systematically converges to the best input approximation from the available samples whenever the sampling problem is theoretically well-posed. This implies
a fortiori
that the algorithm achieves perfect reconstruction whenever the sampling data is exact and uniquely characteristic of the input. Meanwhile, within the same theoretical framework, it addresses the practical concerns of noisy and insufficient sampling.

Our framework is originated from
[
13
]
where an alternative iterative algorithm to the LT method was proposed for bandlimited input reconstruction from integrals. While the algorithm yielded similar complexity and rate of convergence to the LT method in the situation where the latter achieves perfect reconstruction, the convergence to optimal input approximations was obtained under any other stable sampling situations. The algorithm was based on the method of projections onto convex sets (POCS)
[
14
,
15
,
16
]

[

]

In this section, we give examples of data acquisition schemes that consist in recording from an input

II-A

Bandlimited extrapolation

We first connect (
1

is only known by its values

in a certain time interval

II-B

Nonuniform sampling with orthogonal kernels

Assume that samples of the type (
4

\displaystyle w:={\textstyle\sum\limits_{k\in{\sf Z}}}\mathrm{w}_{k}\hskip 1.0ptv_{k}/\|v_{k}\|^{2}\quad\mbox{and}\quad d:={\textstyle\sum\limits_{k\in{\sf Z}}}\mathrm{d}_{k}\hskip 1.0ptv_{k}/\|v_{k}\|^{2}.

III
Reconstruction by POCS

In this section, we review in theoretical depth the properties of iteration (

III-A

Properties of convergence from POCS literature

Iterations of the type (
2
) were first studied in
[
32
]
in the general case where
{\mathscr{U}}
and
{\mathscr{W}}
are closed convex sets. For any such set

,

is the element of
{\mathscr{S}}
that is closest to

in norm. With the additional assumption that

and

are affine subspaces, which is the case of this paper, it was shown that

converges in norm to

x^{\scriptscriptstyle(\infty)}=P_{{\mathscr{U}}\cap{{\mathscr{W}}}}\hskip 1.0ptx^{\scriptscriptstyle(0)}.

(9)

In other words,
x^{\scriptscriptstyle(\infty)}
is the point of
{\mathscr{U}}\cap{\mathscr{W}}
that is closest to the initial guess
x^{\scriptscriptstyle(0)}
in norm.
The difficult situation of interest in this paper is when
{\mathscr{U}}\cap{{\mathscr{W}}}=\emptyset
. Theorem 4.1 of
[
19
]
states that
(x^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)})_{n\geq 0}
remains convergent as long as the distance of

to
{\mathscr{W}}
, defined by

d(u,{{\mathscr{W}}}):=\inf_{v\in{\mathscr{W}}}\|u{-}v\|=\|u-P_{\mathscr{W}}u\|,

(10)

can be minimized within

. In this case, the limit of

is a point of

{\mathscr{U}}_{\mathscr{W}}:=\Big\{u^{*}\in{\mathscr{U}}:d(u^{*},{\mathscr{W}})=\inf_{u\in{\mathscr{U}}}d(u,{\mathscr{W}})\Big\}.

(11)

The complete behavior of

is given by the following theorem.

Theorem III.1
(
[
19
]
)

Let

be recursively obtained by the POCS iteration of (
2

If
{\mathscr{U}}_{\mathscr{W}}\neq\emptyset

converges of limit

(12)

If

tends to

When

.

III-B

Dependence of
{\mathscr{U}}_{\mathscr{W}}
with data

As mentioned in the introduction, the space
{\mathscr{W}}
of interest is specifically defined by

{\mathscr{W}}:=\big\{u\in{\mathscr{H}}:P_{\mathscr{V}}\hskip 1.0ptu=w\big\}.

(13)

When

is given by (
1

as seen above. When

uniquely characterizes a signal in

is singleton. Then, (
9

12

is corrupted by noise as in (
3

1.

When

12

2.

What is the condition for iteration (

Motivated by the result of Section

For any of these questions, what needs to be determined is the dependence of

with

Given that

, we have the following equivalences:
u\in{\mathscr{W}}

\Leftrightarrow

P_{\mathscr{V}}u=w=P_{\mathscr{V}}w

\Leftrightarrow

P_{\mathscr{V}}(u{-}w)=0

\Leftrightarrow

u{-}w\in{\mathscr{V}}^{\perp}

\Leftrightarrow

u\in{\mathscr{V}}^{\perp}\!+w
. Thus,

{\mathscr{W}}={\mathscr{V}}^{\perp}\!+w.

This gives us access to a more explicit expression of

Proposition III.2

\displaystyle\forall u\in{\mathscr{H}},\qquad P_{\mathscr{W}}u=u+(w-P_{\mathscr{V}}\hskip 1.0ptu).

(15)

Proof:

By space translation,
v=P_{\mathscr{W}}u

\Leftrightarrow

v{-}w=P_{{\mathscr{V}}^{\perp}}(u{-}w)

\Leftrightarrow

v=P_{{\mathscr{V}}^{\perp}}(u{-}w)+w=u-P_{\mathscr{V}}u+w
since
w\in{\mathscr{V}}
.
∎

We thus find that

{\mathscr{U}}_{\mathscr{W}}=\Big\{u^{*}\in{\mathscr{U}}:\|w-P_{\mathscr{V}}u^{*}\|^{2}=\displaystyle\inf_{u\in{\mathscr{U}}}\|w-P_{\mathscr{V}}u\|^{2}\Big\}.

(16)

Thus,

is the set of least-squares solutions of the equation

. This leads to the following qualitative interpretation of Theorem

Proposition III.3

When

12

III-C

General result on least-squares solutions

The second question amounts to finding the condition for

It will be convenient to study this question in a more abstract setting. For any bounded linear operator

Lemma III.4

{{\mathscr{V}}}

Let

{\overline{{\mathscr{R}}}}{\times}{\overline{{\mathscr{R}}}}^{\perp}
. For any
v\in{\mathscr{R}}
,
\|w{-}v\|^{2}=\|w_{\scriptscriptstyle\|}{-}v\|^{2}+\|w_{\scriptscriptstyle\perp}\|^{2}
. The infimum of
\|w_{\scriptscriptstyle\|}{-}v\|
for
v\in{\mathscr{R}}
is 0, but can be attained in
{\mathscr{R}}
when and only when
w_{\scriptscriptstyle\|}\in{\mathscr{R}}
. Thus,
\|w-v\|
has a minimizer in
{\mathscr{R}}
if and only if
w\in{\mathscr{R}}\oplus{\overline{{\mathscr{R}}}}^{\perp}
. This completes the proof since
{\overline{{\mathscr{R}}}}^{\perp}={\mathscr{R}}^{\perp}
.
∎

We then have the following equivalences.

Theorem III.5

The following statements are equivalent:

(i)

{\mathscr{U}}_{Q,w}\neq\emptyset
for all
w\in{\mathscr{V}}
,

(ii)

{\cal R}(Q)\oplus{\cal R}(Q)^{\perp}={\mathscr{V}}
,

(iii)

{\cal R}(Q)
is closed in
{\mathscr{V}}
,

(iv)

\gamma(Q)>0
, where

\gamma(Q):=\inf\Big\{\|Qu\|_{\mathscr{V}}:u\in{\cal N}(Q)^{\perp},\|u\|_{\mathscr{U}}=1\Big\}

(19)

and
{\cal N}(Q)
is the null space of
Q
.

Proof:

(i)
\Leftrightarrow
(ii): From (

),
{\mathscr{U}}_{Q,w}
is non-empty if and only if
\|w-v\|^{2}
has a minimizer
v^{*}
in the range
{\cal R}(Q)
of
Q
. By Lemma
III.4
with
{\mathscr{R}}:={\cal R}(Q)
, this is equivalent to

. This proves that (i)
\Leftrightarrow
(ii).

(ii)
\Leftrightarrow
(iii): Since
\overline{{\cal R}(Q)}=({\cal R}(Q)^{\perp})^{\perp}
in

, then

. Therefore, (ii)
\Leftrightarrow

{{\cal R}(Q)}=\overline{{\cal R}(Q)}

(iii)
\Leftrightarrow
(iv): This is shown in Theorem 2.5 of

.
∎

The coefficient
\gamma(Q)
is called the reduced minimum modulus of

[

, chap.IV]
.

III-D

Stability of POCS iteration (
2
)

Returning to our original space setting where
{\mathscr{U}}
and
{\mathscr{V}}
are closed subspaces of
({\mathscr{H}},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle)
, the set
{\mathscr{U}}_{\mathscr{W}}
from (
16
) is equal to
{\mathscr{U}}_{Q,w}
in (
18
) with the operator
Q
defined by

\begin{array}[t]{rcl}Q:\quad{\mathscr{U}}&\rightarrow&{\mathscr{V}}\\
u&\mapsto&P_{{\mathscr{V}}}u\end{array}.

(20)

As an answer to our second question, it follows from Theorem
III.5
that iteration (
2
) is stable if and only if
{\cal R}(Q)=P_{\mathscr{V}}({\mathscr{U}})
is closed. Note that this is automatically satisfied when either
{\mathscr{U}}
or
{\mathscr{V}}
is of finite dimension. This is because
P_{\mathscr{V}}({\mathscr{U}})
is of finite dimension and hence closed. Therefore, the stability of iteration (
2
) is never an issue in practical sampling which consists in acquiring a finite number of samples of the type (
4
) and hence implies a finite-dimensional space
{\mathscr{V}}
.

However, stability becomes an issue when dealing with the theoretical question of perfect reconstruction of signals in a space
{\mathscr{U}}
of infinite dimension (such as bandlimited signals in
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
), since this is possible only when
{\mathscr{V}}
is also infinite-dimensional. In this case, we derive the condition of stability using the criterion that
\gamma(Q)>0
from Theorem
III.5
. Let us give a more explicit expression of
\gamma(Q)
in (
19
) given our assumption of (
20
). A vector
u
is in
{\cal N}(Q)
if and only if
u\in{\mathscr{U}}
and
P_{\mathscr{V}}u=0
, which is equivalent to
u\in{\mathscr{I}}:={\mathscr{U}}\cap{\mathscr{V}}^{\perp}.

As
{\cal N}(Q)^{\perp}\subset{\mathscr{U}}
, then
{\cal N}(Q)^{\perp}={\mathscr{U}}\cap{\mathscr{I}}^{\perp}
. Thus,
\gamma(Q)
yields the explicit description

\gamma(Q)=\inf\Big\{\|P_{{\mathscr{V}}}u\|:u\in{\mathscr{U}}\cap{\mathscr{I}}^{\perp},\|u\|=1\Big\}.

(21)

It can be verified that
\gamma(Q)
is the principal angle between
{\mathscr{U}}
and
{\mathscr{V}}^{\perp}
(see
[
33
]
for the definition of space angle). Now, the angle between subspaces in infinite dimension is a difficult topic that requires advanced research on a case-by-case basis. This question is however of pure theoretical interest. We address in the next subsections an issue of higher priority to practitioners on the numerical side.

III-E

Landweber iteration interpretation

More properties on the numerical behavior of iteration (
2
) can be obtained by deriving equivalent forms of it. It is clear from (
2
) that
x^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}\in{\mathscr{U}}
for all
n\geq 1
. Let us assume that
x^{\scriptscriptstyle(0)}
is chosen in
{\mathscr{U}}
as well, so that
P_{\mathscr{U}}x^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}=x^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}
for all
n\geq 0
. By injecting (
15
) into (
2
), we obtain

x^{\scriptscriptstyle(n+1)}=x^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}+\hskip 1.0ptP_{\mathscr{U}}(w-P_{\mathscr{V}}x^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}),\qquad n\geq 0.

(22)

One can easily see that the adjoint of
Q
is the operator

\begin{array}[t]{rcl}Q^{*}:\quad{\mathscr{V}}&\rightarrow&{\mathscr{U}}\\
v&\mapsto&P_{\mathscr{U}}v\end{array}.

(23)

Indeed, since orthogonal projections are self-adjoint, we have for all
u\in{\mathscr{U}}
and
v\in{\mathscr{V}}
that
\langle Qu,v\rangle=\langle P_{{\mathscr{V}}}u,v\rangle=\langle u,P_{{\mathscr{V}}}v\rangle=\langle u,v\rangle=\langle P_{\mathscr{U}}u,v\rangle=\langle u,P_{\mathscr{U}}v\rangle
.
Then, iteration (
22
) yields the form of

x^{\scriptscriptstyle(n+1)}=x^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}+\hskip 1.0ptQ^{*}(w-Qx^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}),\qquad n\geq 0.

(24)

This is a Landweber iteration
[
34
]
. An outstanding consequence is addressed in the next subsection.

III-F

Regularization by semi-convergence

Given our definition of stability, we saw that iteration (
2
) is theoretically convergent regardless of data errors when
\gamma(Q)>0
with the operator
Q
of (
20
). In practice however, this condition is insufficient for the iteration to be numerically well-behaved. One encounters problems as soon as
\gamma(Q)/||Q\|
is of the order of the machine precision or less, where
\|Q\|
is the operator norm of
Q
. The term of “ill-posed problem” is often used in practice to imply this situation
[
35
]
. Remarkably, the Landweber iteration possesses an inherent mechanism to mitigate this issue through semi-convergence: it naturally regularizes the reconstruction estimate when the iteration is stopped early enough. While extensive theories have been developed on this type of regularization
[
21
]
, we will give here only a few elements of it for qualitative understanding.

Assume that
\|Q\|\leq 1
(as is the case in (
20
) since orthogonal projections are non-expansive) and
Q
is compact, which is automatically realized when
{\mathscr{V}}
is of finite dimension. Then
Q
yields a singular system
(\varphi_{i},\psi_{i},\sigma_{i})_{i\geq 1}
, which satisfies the following properties:
(\varphi_{i})_{i\geq 1}
and
(\psi_{i})_{i\geq 1}
are orthonormal families in
{\mathscr{U}}
and
{\mathscr{V}}
, respectively,
(\sigma_{i})_{i\geq 1}
is a non-increasing sequence of non-negative numbers,
Q\varphi_{i}=\sigma_{i}\psi_{i}
and
Q\psi_{i}=\sigma_{i}\varphi_{i}
for all
i\geq 1
. Using the notation
u_{i}:=\langle u,\varphi_{i}\rangle_{\mathscr{U}}
and
v_{i}:=\langle v,\psi_{i}\rangle_{\mathscr{V}}
for any
u\in{\mathscr{U}}
,
v\in{\mathscr{V}}
and each
i\geq 1
, one finds from (
24
) that

x_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle(n+1)}=x_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}+\sigma_{i}(w_{i}-\sigma_{i}x_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}),\qquad n\geq 0.

Let us write
w=Qx+d
where
d
is the data error, and let
e^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}:=x^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}-x
be the resulting error of the
n
th iterate. Since
w_{i}=\sigma_{i}x_{i}+d_{i}
, subtracting
x_{i}
from the above equation yields

e_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle(n+1)}=\mu_{i}\hskip 1.0pte_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}+\sigma_{i}\hskip 1.0ptd_{i}\qquad\mbox{where}\qquad\mu_{i}:=1\!-\!\sigma_{i}^{2}.

Limiting ourselves to the singular directions in
{\cal N}(Q)^{\perp}
, we have
\sigma_{i}>0
, and hence,
\mu_{i}\in[0,1)
since
\sigma_{i}\in(0,\|Q\|]\subset(0,1]
. It can then be found by induction that

e_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}=\mu_{i}^{n}\hskip 1.0pte_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle(0)}+(1\!-\!\mu_{i}^{n})\hskip 1.0pt\frac{d_{i}}{\sigma_{i}},\qquad n\geq 0.

The first term, which is the noise-free iteration error, monotonically tends to 0. Meanwhile, the second term, which is the noise contribution, on the contrary increases in magnitude with iteration. Roughly speaking, regularisation by semi-convergence consists in stopping the iteration when the two terms are globally of similar order. The critical singular directions are those along which
\sigma_{i}
is small, as can be seen in the second term. However, note that the amplified error limit
d_{i}/\sigma_{i}
also takes more iterations to be reached, as
\mu_{i}^{n}
remains close to 1 for larger values of
n
. A complete theory is presented in
[
21
]
, showing that stopping the Landweber iteration at the right time leads to a regularized reconstruction that is optimal in a theoretical sense called order-optimality. A study of practical stopping rules is proposed in
[
36
]
.

IV
Sampling with orthogonal kernels

In this section, we focus on the important case where the space
{\mathscr{V}}
has a known orthogonal basis
(v_{k})_{k\in{\sf Z}}
. We saw in Section
II-B
that this situation is indirectly realized when the acquired data consists of samples
(\mathrm{w}_{k})_{k\in{\sf Z}}
of the form

\mathrm{w}_{k}:=\langle x,v_{k}\rangle+\mathrm{d}_{k},\qquad k\in{\sf Z}

as a reminder of (
4
), for any given orthogonal family
(v_{k})_{k\in{\sf Z}}
of the ambient space
{\mathscr{H}}
. In this case,
{\mathscr{V}}
and
w
are defined by (
5
). The task of this section is to express iteration (
2
) and its properties in terms of the sequence of samples

{\mathbf{w}}=(\mathrm{w}_{k})_{k\in{\sf Z}}

instead of
w
. An outstanding outcome will be the possibility to rigorously implement the iteration by means of discrete-time operations, regardless of the input space
{\mathscr{U}}
.

IV-A

POCS algorithm

Let us express iteration (
2
) in terms of
(\mathrm{w}_{k})_{k\in{\sf Z}}
.
As
(v_{k})_{k\in{\sf Z}}
are independent vectors (since they are orthogonal), it can be seen from (
5
) and (
6
) that
P_{\mathscr{V}}u=w
if and only if
\langle u,v_{k}\rangle=\mathrm{w}_{k}
for all
k\in{\sf Z}
. The space
{\mathscr{W}}
then becomes explicitly

{\mathscr{W}}=\Big\{u\in{\mathscr{H}}:~\forall k\in{\sf Z},\langle u,v_{k}\rangle=\mathrm{w}_{k}\Big\}.

The explicit implementation of iteration (
2
) in terms of
(\mathrm{w}_{k})_{k\in{\sf Z}}
can be obtained by injecting (
5b
) and (
6
) into the equivalent iteration of (
22
). After distribution of the projection
P_{\mathscr{U}}
, this yields

x^{\scriptscriptstyle(n+1)}=x^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}+P_{\mathscr{U}}\Big({\textstyle\sum\limits_{k\in{\sf Z}}}\big(\mathrm{w}_{k}-\langle x^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}\!,v_{k}\rangle\big)\hskip 1.0ptv_{k}/\|v_{k}\|^{2}\Big).

(25)

Note that
P_{\mathscr{U}}
need not be computed on any element of
{\mathscr{H}}
, but only on the vectors
(v_{k})_{k\in{\sf Z}}
.

IV-B

Formulation in terms of sampling operator

Iteration (
25
) was established as an early expression of (
2
) in terms of
{\mathbf{w}}
. It however cumulates the shortcomings of preventing signal processing insight and of being impractical in implementation, as will be seen later on.
For a conceptual understanding of this iteration, it is better to perform a more formal transformation of (
2
).
By orthogonality of
(v_{k})_{k\in{\sf Z}}
, it follows from (
5b
) that
\mathrm{w}_{k}=\langle w,v_{k}\rangle=\|v_{k}\|\hskip 1.0pt\langle w,\hat{v}_{k}\rangle
where

\hat{v}_{k}:=v_{k}/\|v_{k}\|,\qquad k\in{\sf Z}.

We can then write that

\displaystyle{\mathbf{w}}=DCw

(26)

where

\displaystyle C:\begin{array}[t]{rcl}{\mathscr{V}}&\rightarrow&\ell^{2}({\sf Z})\\
v&\mapsto&\big(\langle v,\hat{v}_{k}\rangle\big)_{k\in{\sf Z}}\end{array},

\displaystyle D:\begin{array}[t]{rcl}\ell^{2}({\sf Z})&\rightarrow&{\mathscr{D}}\\
(\mathrm{c}_{k})_{k\in{\sf Z}}&\mapsto&(\|v_{k}\|\mathrm{c}_{k})_{k\in{\sf Z}}\end{array},

and

\displaystyle{\mathscr{D}}:=\Big\{(\mathrm{c}_{k})_{k\in{\sf Z}}:{\textstyle\sum\limits_{k\in{\sf Z}}}\mathrm{c}_{k}^{2}/\|v_{k}\|^{2}<\infty\Big\}.

The space
{\mathscr{D}}
is a Hilbert space equipped with the weighted norm
\|\cdot\|_{\mathscr{D}}
such that

\|{\mathbf{c}}\|_{{\mathscr{D}}}^{2}={\textstyle\sum\limits_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}}\mathrm{c}_{k}^{2}/\|v_{k}\|^{2},\qquad{\mathbf{c}}\in{\mathscr{D}}.

(33)

The operator
C
is unitary since
(\hat{v}_{k})_{k\in{\sf Z}}
is an orthonormal basis of
{\mathscr{V}}
. The operator
D
is also unitary, this time because it is invertible and norm preserving, since
\|D{\mathbf{c}}\|_{\mathscr{D}}=\|{\mathbf{c}}\|
for all
{\mathbf{c}}\in\ell^{2}({\sf Z})
. As
DC
is unitary,
C^{*}D^{*}DC
is the identity on
{\mathscr{V}}
.
Iteration (
22
) can then be rewritten as

x^{\scriptscriptstyle(n+1)}=x^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}+Q^{*}C^{*}D^{*}DC(w-Qx^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}),\qquad n\geq 0.

This leads to

x^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}=x^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}+V^{*}({\mathbf{w}}-Vx^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}),\qquad n\geq 0

(34)

where
V:=DCQ
. The next proposition gives the explicit descriptions of
V
and
V^{*}
.

Proposition IV.1

V:\begin{array}[t]{rcl}{\mathscr{U}}&\!\!\rightarrow&{\mathscr{D}}\\
u&\!\!\mapsto&\big(\langle u,v_{k}\rangle\mbox{\raisebox{-2.58334pt}{$\scriptstyle{{\mathscr{V}}}$}}\big)_{k\in{\sf Z}}\end{array}\hskip-5.69054pt,\quad V^{*}:\begin{array}[t]{rcl}{\mathscr{D}}&\!\!\rightarrow&{\mathscr{U}}\\
{\mathbf{c}}&\!\!\mapsto&\sum\limits_{k\in{\sf Z}}\mathrm{c}_{k}\hskip 1.0ptu_{k}\end{array}

(35)

where
u_{k}:=P_{\mathscr{U}}v_{k}/\|v_{k}\|^{2}
for all
k\in{\sf Z}
.

Proof:

For all
u\in{\mathscr{U}}
,
Vu=DCP_{\mathscr{V}}u=(\langle P_{\mathscr{V}}u,v_{k}\rangle)_{k\in{\sf Z}}=(\langle u,v_{k}\rangle)_{k\in{\sf Z}}
. Let
{\mathbf{c}}=(\mathrm{c}_{k})_{k\in{\sf Z}}\in{\mathscr{D}}
. We have
V^{*}{\mathbf{c}}=Q^{*}D^{*}C^{*}{\mathbf{c}}=P_{\mathscr{U}}D^{-1}C^{-1}{\mathbf{c}}
.
Since
C^{-1}{\mathbf{c}}=\sum_{k\in{\sf Z}}\mathrm{c}_{k}\hat{v}_{k}
and
\hat{v}_{k}=v_{k}/\|v_{k}\|
, then
D^{-1}C^{-1}{\mathbf{c}}=\sum_{k\in{\sf Z}}\mathrm{c}_{k}v_{k}/\|v_{k}\|^{2}
. Finally,
V^{*}{\mathbf{c}}=\sum_{k\in{\sf Z}}\mathrm{c}_{k}u_{k}
where
u_{k}=P_{\mathscr{U}}v_{k}/\|v_{k}\|
.
∎

We now have the direct relation
{\mathbf{w}}=Vx+(\mathrm{d}_{k})_{k\in{\sf Z}}
. We call
V
the
sampling operator
. One can verify that iteration (
25
) is retrieved by injecting (
35
) into (
34
) and factoring out
P_{\mathscr{U}}
. But the interesting contribution of (
34
) is its simple form as a Landweber iteration in terms of
{\mathbf{w}}
.

IV-C

Convergence interpretation

Theorem
III.1
still gives the convergence behavior of iteration (
2
), and hence of iteration (
34
). One however wishes to have a description of the set
{\mathscr{U}}_{\mathscr{W}}
of iteration limits in terms of
{\mathbf{w}}
. Since
DC
is unitary,
\|DCv\|_{\mathscr{D}}=\|v\|
for all
v\in{\mathscr{V}}
. Then,

\displaystyle\forall u\in{\mathscr{U}},\qquad\|w-P_{\mathscr{V}}u\|=\|w-Qu\|=\|{\mathbf{w}}-Vu\|_{\mathscr{D}}

so that
{\mathscr{U}}_{\mathscr{W}}
in (
16
) becomes

{\mathscr{U}}_{\mathscr{W}}=\Big\{u^{*}\in{\mathscr{U}}:\|{\mathbf{w}}-Vu^{*}\|_{\mathscr{D}}^{2}=\displaystyle\inf_{u\in{\mathscr{U}}}\|{\mathbf{w}}-Vu\|_{\mathscr{D}}^{2}\Big\}.

Thus,
{\mathscr{U}}_{\mathscr{W}}
is the set of least-squares solutions of the equation
Vu={\mathbf{w}}
, where the least-squares evaluation is in the sense of the weighted norm
\|\cdot\|_{\mathscr{D}}
.
By simple application of Theorem
III.1
, we can then state the following result.

Proposition IV.2

Let
(x^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)})_{n\geq 0}
be recursively defined by (
34
) for some initial iterate
x^{\scriptscriptstyle(0)}\in{\mathscr{U}}
and some sequence
{\mathbf{w}}=(\mathrm{w}_{k})_{k\in{\sf Z}}\in{\mathscr{D}}
.

If
{\mathscr{U}}_{\mathscr{W}}\neq\emptyset
,
x^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}
converges of limit

x^{\scriptscriptstyle(\infty)}=P_{{\mathscr{U}}_{\mathscr{W}}}x^{\scriptscriptstyle(0)}
.

If
{\mathscr{U}}_{\mathscr{W}}=\emptyset
,
\|x^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}\|
tends to
\infty
.

When convergent, iteration (
34
) tends to the least-squares solution of the equation
Vu={\mathbf{w}}
in
{\mathscr{U}}
which is closest to the initial iterate
x^{\scriptscriptstyle(0)}
.

IV-D

Discrete-time implementation of iteration

It appears from (
34
) that
x^{\scriptscriptstyle(n+1)}\!-x^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}
remains in the range of
V^{*}
. So does
x^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}\!-x^{\scriptscriptstyle(0)}
for all
n\geq 0
. Therefore, there must exist for each
n\geq 0
a discrete-time vector
{\mathbf{c}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}\in{\mathscr{D}}
such that

x^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}=x^{\scriptscriptstyle(0)}+V^{*}{\mathbf{c}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)},\qquad n\geq 0.

(36)

As
V^{*}
may not be injective, the sequence
({\mathbf{c}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)})_{\geq 0}
is not necessarily unique. Let us show that (
36
) is at least achieved by the discrete-time sequence recursively defined by

\displaystyle{\mathbf{c}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(n+1)}:={\mathbf{c}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}+({\mathbf{w}}_{0}-VV^{*}{\mathbf{c}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}),\qquad n\geq 0

(37)

where
{\mathbf{w}}_{0}:={\mathbf{w}}-Vx^{\scriptscriptstyle(0)}
, starting from
{\mathbf{c}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(0)}=0
. As (
36
) is trivially true at
n=0
, let us assume that it is true at some
n\geq 0
. By applying
V^{*}
to the members of (
37
) and adding
x^{\scriptscriptstyle(0)}
, we obtain

\displaystyle x^{\scriptscriptstyle(0)}+V^{*}{\mathbf{c}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(n+1)}

\displaystyle=x^{\scriptscriptstyle(0)}+V^{*}{\mathbf{c}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}+V^{*}({\mathbf{w}}_{0}-VV^{*}{\mathbf{c}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)})

\displaystyle=x^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}+V^{*}\big({\mathbf{w}}_{0}-V(x^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}\!-x^{\scriptscriptstyle(0)})\big)

\displaystyle=x^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}+V^{*}({\mathbf{w}}-Vx^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)})=x^{\scriptscriptstyle(n+1)}.

Thus, (
36
) is true for all
n\geq 0
. We have thus established the following result.

Proposition IV.3

For a given initial guess
x^{\scriptscriptstyle(0)}\in{\mathscr{U}}
, the sequence
(x^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)})_{n\geq 0}
recursively defined by Landweber iteration (
34
) is equivalently obtained by the system

\displaystyle{\mathbf{c}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(n+1)}

\displaystyle={\mathbf{c}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}+({\mathbf{w}}_{0}-VV^{*}{\mathbf{c}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)})

(38a)

\displaystyle x^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}

\displaystyle=x^{\scriptscriptstyle(0)}+V^{*}{\mathbf{c}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}

(38b)

starting from
{\mathbf{c}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(0)}=0
, where
{\mathbf{w}}_{0}:={\mathbf{w}}-Vx^{\scriptscriptstyle(0)}
.

If the targeted estimate of
x
is the
m
th iterate
x^{\scriptscriptstyle(m)}
, then one only needs to iterate
m
times the discrete-time operation of (
38a
), then get
x^{\scriptscriptstyle(m)}
by executing (
38b
) once at
n=m
. For storage and transmission,
x^{\scriptscriptstyle(m)}
may not even need to be computed. One may wait until the signal
x^{\scriptscriptstyle(m)}
needs to be output physically to perform (
38b
) from
{\mathbf{c}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(m)}
by means of analog circuits. In (
38a
), the whole complexity lies in the matrix-vector multiplication
(VV^{*}){\mathbf{c}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}
.

Proposition IV.4

VV^{*}
is the matrix of coefficients

VV^{*}=\Big[\langle P_{\mathscr{U}}v_{\ell},v_{k}\rangle/\|v_{\ell}\|^{2}\Big]_{(k,\ell)\in{\sf Z}\times{\sf Z}}.

(39)

Proof:

For any given
\ell\in{\sf Z}
, let
{\mathbf{e}}_{\ell}
be the sequence in
{\mathscr{D}}
with 1 as the
\ell
th coordinate and 0 elsewhere. Then,
VV^{*}{\mathbf{e}}_{\ell}=Vu_{\ell}=\big(\langle u_{\ell},v_{k}\rangle\big)_{k\in{\sf Z}}=\big(\langle P_{\mathscr{U}}v_{\ell},v_{k}\rangle/\|v_{\ell}\|^{2}\big)_{k\in{\sf Z}}
.
∎

IV-E

Discussion

Signal processing algorithms often refer to continuous-time signals but are typically thought of as discrete-time algorithms via the Shannon sampling theorem under bandlimitation. This is for example the context of
[
8
,
23
,
24
]
. This could be applied to iteration (
25
).
This would however create a double summation in (

), as
x^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}
in the inner product

would need to be expanded in terms of its components in this basis. Meanwhile, the discretization technique of

solely results from the inherent discreteness of the acquired data

. In this context, the space

need not even be separable (except when one aims at the perfect reconstruction of

from

).

V-A

Encoding system description and problem

Inspired by the modeling of spiking neural networks, the works of

{\bm{x}}=(x_{1}(t),{\cdots},x_{M}(t))

\mathrm{w}_{ij}:=\int_{t^{i}_{j-1}}^{t^{i}_{j}}x_{i}(t)\,{\mathrm{d}}t+\mathrm{d}_{ij},\qquad j\in{\sf Z}_{i}

(41)

where
{\sf Z}_{i}
is some set of consecutive integers,
(t^{i}_{j})_{j\in{\sf Z}_{i}}
is some increasing sequence of instants, and
(\mathrm{d}_{ij})_{j\in{\sf Z}_{i}}
is some sequence of acquisition errors. Meanwhile, it is assumed for each

that

(x_{1}(t),{\cdots},x_{M}(t))={\mathbf{A}}\,(\check{x}_{1}(t),{\cdots},\check{x}_{N}(t))

(42)

where
{\mathbf{A}}
is a full rank
M{\times}N
matrix with
N\leq M
, the
M
-tuple and
N
-tuple are seen as column vectors, and
\check{x}_{1},\cdots,\check{x}_{N}
are source signals in

V-G

Comparison with prior work

The prior work of
[
11
,
23
,
24
]
introduced the encoding system considered in this section for the main objective of perfect input reconstruction. The signal reconstruction methods proposed in
[
11
,
23
]
were inspired by the POCS algorithm. The simple case where
{\mathbf{A}}
is the
M{\times}1
matrix
\big[1\cdots 1\big]^{\top}
was studied in
[
23
]
. Although the adopted reconstruction algorithm was a non-standard version of POCS iteration (which can only be rigorously justified as alternating a projection with an average of projections
[
37
]
), it can be verified to be equivalent to the POCS iteration of (
25
) after re-indexation of
(\mathrm{w}_{k},{\bm{v}}_{k})
as
(\mathrm{w}_{ij},{\bm{v}}_{ij})
. As a result, the theory of Section
III
uncovers all the convergence properties of their algorithm, including in the cases of inconsistent or insufficient data. Moreover, we give in (
52
) the time-discretized form of the iteration for practical implementation, whose complexity all lies in a matrix-vector multiplication. The work of
[
11
]
considered the general case of a full-rank tall matrix
{\mathbf{A}}
. While perfect reconstruction was numerically demonstrated, the reconstruction method used was a deviation of the POCS algorithm. As a result, it lacked a comprehensive theoretical framework for convergence analysis and the potential to converge beyond the conditions required for perfect reconstruction. Meanwhile, our POCS algorithm and analytical results hold with such a general matrix
{\mathbf{A}}
.

The more complete and concise analysis of the multichannel time-encoding system has also been an opportunity to outline a generalized construction of orthogonal sampling kernel by integration. The principle of integrating a signal over disjoint parts of its domain can be generalized from one-dimensional signals (which is realized with ASDM or integrate-and-fire) to multi-dimensional functions defined on any measurable space.

VI
Bandlimited interpolation by iterative piecewise-linear corrections

We now wish to apply our approach to the traditional point sampling problem of bandlimited signals. Assume that samples of the type

\mathrm{x}_{k}:=x(t_{k})+\mathrm{e}_{k},\qquad k\in{\sf Z}

are acquired from an input
x\in{\mathscr{B}}
, where
{\sf Z}
is some index set of consecutive integers,

It appears that an iteration previously designed by Gröchenig in

I

be the Sobolev space

in
\dot{H}^{1}({\mathbb{R}})

VI-C

Impact of POCS approach

By equivalence to (

VII
Conclusion

In this paper, we have developed a whole theory of signal reconstruction when, in a Hilbert space
{\mathscr{H}}
, data is extracted from an input
x\in{\mathscr{U}}
in the form
w=P_{\mathscr{V}}x
with some possible acquisition deviation
d
. In this case, there is a POCS-based algorithm for the reconstruction of
x
, which systematically tends to the least-squares solution of the equation
P_{\mathscr{V}}u=w
in
{\mathscr{U}}
that is closest to any chosen initial guess
x^{\scriptscriptstyle(0)}
, provided that the least-squares solutions theoretically exist. As a result, it achieves best input approximations under noisy or insufficient sampling, while guaranteeing perfect reconstructions whenever
d=0
and
P_{\mathscr{V}}u=w
has a unique solution in
{\mathscr{U}}
. Thus, the POCS algorithm addresses simultaneously the practical concerns of non-ideal sampling and the theoretical objectives of perfect reconstruction. When the acquired data is in the form of samples
(\mathrm{w}_{k})_{k\in{\sf Z}}
, the knowledge of
w:=P_{\mathscr{V}}x
is inherently obtained when
{\mathscr{V}}
has an orthogonal basis
(v_{k})_{k\in{\sf Z}}
and
\mathrm{w}_{k}=\langle x,v_{k}\rangle
for all
k\in{\sf Z}
. In this case, the iterative part of the POCS algorithm has an intrinsic and rigorous discrete-time implementation, which does not require a Nyquist-rate type of sampling of the iterates. This situation of data acquisition appears in a number of emerging event-based sampling schemes based on input integration (ASDM, integrate-and-fire, multi-channel time encoding). Through a more fundamental analysis, our framework either leads to improved reconstruction methods compared to those previously proposed, or reveals convergence properties of the existing methods under noisy or insufficient sampling — properties that have remained unknown until now. We have also applied our framework to an existing algorithm for the bandlimited interpolation of nonuniform point samples, and in this process, have uncovered convergence properties of this method beyond the ideal situation of perfect reconstruction. With these applications, we have not only demonstrated the effectiveness of our framework on existing encoding schemes, but we have also illustrated its potential for generating new event-based encoders under the constraint of a robust and readily implementable signal reconstruction method.
Our approach to multi-channel time encoding fundamentally consisted in extending the principle of integrating the input over disjoint parts found in ASDM and integrate-and-fire, from one-dimensional integration to the multi-dimensional integration of functions on any measurable space. In the second application of point sampling, we have shown the effectiveness of our framework in dealing with ambient Hilbert spaces other than the traditional
L^{2}
-spaces.
