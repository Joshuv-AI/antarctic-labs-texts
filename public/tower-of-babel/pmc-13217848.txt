Active host control of the internal O2 microenvironment in reef-building corals
Authors: Qingfeng Zhang, Elena Bollati, Cesar O Pacherres, David J Hughes, Walter Dellisanti, Christine Ferrier-Pages, Michael Kühl
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryThe gastrovascular cavity of corals plays a central role in internal circulation, digestion, reproduction, and symbiont acquisition. However, despite its importance, our understanding of the physic-chemical characteristics and dynamic properties of this internal microenvironment remains limited. Here, we employ high-resolution microsensor measurements to investigate the vertical distribution of O2 within the coral gastrovascular cavity. By combining microsensor analysis with time-lapse imaging, we show that O2 levels inside corals are strongly affected by slow, synchronized tissue movements, suggesting a mechanism of active ventilation through modulation of cavity volume and exchange with the surrounding seawater. Together with cilia beating, these movements reduce O2 accumulation under light conditions and alleviate O2 depletion in darkness, thereby stabilizing internal O2 availability. These findings highlight a hitherto overlooked role of behavior in actively regulating the internal microenvironment and O2 status of the coral holobiont and provide critical insight into the organism-environment interactions of reef-building corals.Subject areas: environmental science, ecology, biological sciences

Introduction

Reef-building corals, like other basal Metazoa, rely on diffusion to exchange substrates with the surrounding seawater through a diffusive boundary layer,1 and this exchange process is essential for coral physiology and metabolism.2,3,4 The coral tissue and the boundary layer exhibit pronounced O2 dynamics varying from hyperoxia to hypoxia or even anoxia on a daily basis, driven by daytime photosynthesis of endosymbiotic algae (Symbiodiniaceae) and respiratory processes in the animal, algal symbionts, and associated microbiomes.5 These steep O2 gradients impair both O2 exchange between the coral and seawater and internal O2 transport within the coral holobiont, which can induce oxidative stress and hypoxia, potentially triggering coral bleaching and mortality.6,7,8,9,10

Despite lacking specialized gas exchange structures, corals can alleviate strong O2 concentration gradients across the diffusive boundary layer via movement of cilia covering their ectoderm, inducing vortical flows that locally enhance advective O2 transport.11,12,13 Furthermore, by actively expanding and contracting their polyps, corals can modulate their surface area to tissue volume ratio, which in turn affect their exposure to ambient flow and gas exchange capacity.14,15 Tentacle movements further contribute to these processes by influencing local flow regimes and improving mass transfer.16 In soft corals and sea anemones, regular tissue contractions have been shown to facilitate O2 and nutrient exchange.17,18 In contrast, in reef-building corals, tissue contraction/expansion cycles are slower19 and often less conspicuous than in other anthozoans and have so far been documented mainly on diel timescales.20,21 Thus, the functional role of these subtle tissue movements in shaping coral microenvironments remains poorly understood.

While the external surface of corals is crucial for gas exchange, the coral gastrovascular cavity (GVC) plays a key role in the circulation of gases, nutrients, and metabolites in the internal environment.22 The GVC is nutrient rich and harbors a unique microbiome.23,24,25 Consequently, the conditions within the GVC not only directly affect coral physiological health but also influence digestive process and the activity of associated microbes. Within this semi-enclosed microenvironment, O2 dynamics are determined by respiration, photosynthesis, and transport. Studies have shown steep O2 gradients under light conditions, ranging from hyperoxia in the upper regions of the GVC to hypoxia in the deeper regions, indicating insufficient mixing within the cavity.23,24,25

Despite growing recognition of the physiological importance of the GVC, the circulation in the GVC and exchange process between the internal microenvironment and the external seawater remain largely unknown. In particular, potential active ventilation mechanisms operating within the GVC have received little attention, leaving a significant gap in our understanding of how corals actively regulate internal mass exchange processes. Recent studies have shown that coral tissue movements are affected by environmental stressors such as iron enrichment and heat stress and, in turn, lead to O2 depletion within the internal microenvironment, but the underlying mechanisms and ecological significance of these processes are still unclear.26,27,28

In this study, we investigate the O2 dynamics within the coral GVC and examine how corals actively regulate O2 level in this internal microenvironment. Using high-resolution microsensors, we characterized the spatial and temporal dynamics of O2 in the GVC in different coral species. In parallel, we employed time-lapse imaging to document coral tissue movements and analyzed the relationship between tissue contraction/expansion and O2 fluctuations. By manipulating ciliary beating and tissue motility, we further explored how these behavioral mechanisms contribute to the regulation of O2 homeostasis within the GVC.

Results and discussion

Distribution of O2 in the coral GVC under dark and light

We used a 50 μm tip-size microsensor to investigate O2 distribution in the GVC from the coral mouth to the base of the GVC (see STAR Methods for further details), in 10 coral species under dark and light conditions (Figure 1). Overall O2 dynamics in the GVC were driven by respiration and photosynthesis, where the GVC microenvironment was largely hypoxic or anoxic in the dark and became partially hyperoxic under light conditions due to photosynthetic activity of endosymbiotic microalgae. While these findings agree with a few earlier studies of the O2 microenvironment in the GVC,23,24,25 the large variation between technical replicates on the same polyp (dashed lines in Figure 1) indicated that the O2 concentration in the GVC fluctuated over time.

In the dark, O2 concentration profiles showed a decreasing gradient from the coral mouth to the upper part of the GVC over a depth interval of ∼0.5 mm. Deeper regions of the GVC were anoxic in most coral species, except for Eunicella singularis and Oculina patagonica. Under illumination, O2 concentrations near the coral mouth and at the bottom of the GVC were often higher than the region in between. The shape of measured profiles reflects the complex internal geometry of the GVC, where upper and lower measuring positions of the microsensor tip were relatively close to the pharynx or the endoderm tissue lining the GVC, harboring O2 producing/respiring symbionts and respiring host tissue, as compared to when the microsensor tip was positioned in the bulk fluid compartment of the GVC.

Besides diffusive O2 exchange along concentration gradients between tissues and the aqueous content of the GVC, the O2 status of the GVC can also be affected by more active transport mechanisms such as cilia beating-driven advective transport, which has been shown to enhance fluid mixing and gas exchange at the external coral tissue layer over mm scales.11,12,13,29 However, while there are indications of a similar role of cilia-covered endoderm tissue for fluid exchange with the external seawater and internal mass transfer in corals (e.g., Bouderlique et al.30), the mechanisms and role of active GVC ventilation for coral holobiont ecophysiology remain unexplored.

In Stylophora pistillata, Pocillopora damicornis, and E. singularis, the GVC was shallower (approximately 1 mm deep) and exhibited a relatively constant O2 concentration with depth. Although internal fluid movement was not directly visualized, our measurements of homogeneous O2 distributions provide indirect but compelling evidence that cilia beating-induced vortical flows efficiently mixed the gastrovascular fluid in these coral species. In contrast, O2 gradients in the GVC were steeper in corals with larger polyp sizes (e.g., Lobophyllia hemprichii with a 5–8 mm deep GVC and Caulastrea curvata with a 3–5 mm GVC). In light, the O2 concentration in L. hemprichii decreased from 480 μmol O2 L-1 at the coral mouth to 0 μmol O2 L-1 at a depth of 2.5–3.5 mm into the GVC and then increased to 150 μmol O2 L-1 at the base of the GVC (Figure 1). Such steep gradients reflect the heterogeneity of O2 distribution and indicate that the GVC environment is incompletely mixed, especially over larger distances and in corals with deeper polyps.

Correlation between coral tissue movement and O2 dynamic in the coral GVC

We positioned the microsensor tip at the center of the GVC and recorded O2 concentrations over longer time periods (hours) after a transition from light to darkness. These measurements showed pronounced fluctuations of the O2 concentration over time in L. hemprichii, Cycloseris sp., and Blastomussa wellsi (Figure 2). However, such temporal changes varied among coral species, where e.g., Cycloseris sp. exhibited more frequent small-scale O2 fluctuations and maintained a relatively well-oxygenated GVC in darkness. In contrast, L. hemprichii and B. wellsi exhibited slower but larger O2 dynamics occasionally exceeding 100 μmol O2 L-1 within 10 minutes, resulting in a more strongly deoxygenated GVC. These pronounced O2 fluctuations were often accompanied by slow coral tissue movements. To further explore the relationship between coral tissue movement and O2 dynamics in the GVC, we recorded the vertical movement of the coral mouth as an indicator of coral tissue movement, while simultaneously measuring the O2 concentration in the GVC. Measurements in L. hemprichii under light revealed a strong correlation between O2 fluctuations and tissue movement, with O2 concentration increasing when the coral mouth moved upward during polyp expansion (Figure S1, Video S1).

The electrochemical O2 microsensors used in these initial experiments measured O2 concentrations at the 50 μm wide measuring tip, which limits representation of overall O2 dynamics in the GVC and can make readings sensitive to changes in distance from surrounding coral tissue. To overcome such constraints, we employed a special fiber-optic O2 microsensor (see STAR Methods), which integrates O2 measurements over a 3 mm long fiber tip without causing O2 consumption during measurements.31 We used this sensor type for more detailed investigation of O2 dynamics in polyps of Caulastrea curvata, where the GVC depth fits with the length of the sensing region of the optical microsensor, allowing us to measure the average O2 concentration across the entire GVC microenvironment.

The coral mouth moved over a total vertical range of 3–4 mm with occasionally pronounced upward and downward motions. The plotted coral tissue movement distances from the contraction-expansion cycles exhibited a bimodal pattern. We speculate that this pattern arises because the contraction-expansion cycles are not always uniform, and some polyps pause before reaching the fully contracted or fully expanded states. Analyzing these vertical motions revealed that the amplitude ranged from 1 to 2.5 mm, and lasted 8.3 ± 3.3 min, over which the O2 concentration in the GVC exhibited fluctuations of 40–60 μmol O2 L-1 in light and 20–40 μmol O2 L-1 in darkness (Figure 3 and S2). In light, O2 concentrations in the GVC increased when the coral mouth moved upward during tissue contraction and decreased when it moved downward during tissue relaxation. In darkness, this relationship was reversed, with O2 concentrations in the GVC decreasing when the coral mouth moved upward (Figures 3A, 3B, and S2) and increasing during tissue relaxation. Cross-correlation analysis revealed a significant coupling between O2 concentration and tissue movement distance under light conditions (Table S1). In darkness, coral tissue expansion increased the distance between the sensor tip and the polyp mouth, resulting in weaker correlations. In contrast, when cilia beating was inhibited by Na3VO4, tissue movements became more pronounced, leading to stronger correlations. Importantly, similar tissue movements occurred even without a sensor placed in the GVC indicating they were not artifacts of the microsensor measurements (Video S1).

We interpret the relationship between tissue movement and GVC O2 dynamic as follows (Figure S3): in light, tissue contraction reduces GVC volume facilitating more rapid accumulation of O2 produced in surrounding tissues containing endosymbionts, while tissue expansion increases GVC volume leading to an intake of seawater and a decrease in GVC O2 concentration. Under darkness, these trends were reversed, i.e., tissue contraction led to a decrease in the O2 concentration in the GVC due to more rapid O2 depletion of the smaller volume by respiration in the surrounding tissues, while tissue relaxation increased the volume and counteracted O2 depletion by intake of more oxygenated water from the surroundings. However, we acknowledge that we cannot account for the internal volume of the GVC from our measurements of tissue expansion and contraction, and a quantitative testing of this mechanism will require direct measurements of the internal tissue surface area and volume in the GVC under expansion and contraction, e.g., using non-invasive imaging modalities such as optical coherence tomography.14,15

We propose that the slow tissue contraction-expansion cycles observed across different coral species in this study are distinct from previously described cnidarian movement behaviors. Unlike the overall diel expansion and contraction of reef-building coral polyps, which primarily modulate external surface area,10,11 or the rapid pulsation behavior observed in soft corals and sea anemones that generates strong advective flows for bulk mixing,17,18,32 the movements described here are slow and occur over longer timescales (on the order of ∼10 min) and influence O2 dynamics within the GVC, thereby suggesting a functional role in internal fluid circulation and exchange processes. Furthermore, these slow movements appear to be weakly rhythmic and persist under both light and dark conditions, suggesting that they may be linked to internal physiological cues such as O2 availability or metabolic demand. Taken together, these observations suggest a distinct and previously underappreciated mode of internal regulation in reef-building corals, complementing cilia-driven transport and diel polyp expansion-contraction cycles.

Tissue movement and cilia-driven flows modulate the O2 level in the coral GVC

To further investigate the role of coral tissue movement and cilia-induced advective flow in modulating the O2 level in the GVC, we used 92 mM MgCl2 to inhibit tissue motility33 and 1 mM Na3VO4 to arrest cilia beating11,12 in C. curvata. We measured the temporal dynamics of O2 concentration under each treatment (Figure 4), as well as the spatial distribution of O2 within the GVC (Figure 5). The anesthetic effects of MgCl2 and Na3VO4 may also suppress the animals’ metabolic rate and reduce oxygen consumption. Although this possibility remains to be investigated, our measurements under dark conditions showed that MgCl2 or Na3VO4 treatments led to a decrease in oxygen concentration within the GVC (Figures 4B and 5B). This suggests that the potential reduction in oxygen consumption due to metabolic suppression was relatively minor and did not offset the decrease in oxygen supply caused by inhibited tissue movement and ciliary beating.

Inhibition of tissue motility reduces O2 fluctuation amplitude: when corals were sedated with MgCl2, O2 fluctuations in the GVC became less frequent and the amplitude decreased from ∼50 μmol O2 L-1 without MgCl2 treatment to <20 μmol O2 L-1 under both light and dark conditions (Figures 4C–4F). However, the internal O2 level remained at the same level as the control group under light (Figure 4A; Table S2), suggesting that even though tissue movement was inhibited, cilia beating-induced advective flows could mix the GVC microenvironment and alleviate accumulation of O2 under experimental light conditions (100 μmol photons m-2 s-1). Under dark conditions, the average O2 concentration in the GVC of MgCl2 treated corals was lower (50–70 μmol O2 L-(1) than in the controls (130–150 μmol O2 L-1, Figure 4B; Table S2), and we did not observe expansion of the coral polyp. Therefore, GVC depths were greater in the control group under darkness, and the sensor was positioned in the deeper part of the GVC during the time series measurement. In the expanded coral polyp in the dark, the spatial limitation may further constrain the water exchange driven solely by ciliary beating in the basal region, potentially resulting in even lower O2 concentrations.

Enhanced tissue movement following ciliary arrest: with Na3VO4 treatment, the O2 level in the GVC was similar to controls under light, while GVC O2 concentrations were lower in darkness (Figures 4A and 4B; Table S2). Time lapse recordings showed that coral tissue movement was more pronounced when cilia were arrested, under both light and dark conditions. The range of coral tissue movement increased from 4 mm without Na3VO4 addition to 7 mm with Na3VO4 addition (Figure 3F), and Levene’s test indicated significantly different variances between treatments (p < 0.01). Additionally, the duration of each up-and-down movement of the coral mouth decreased from 8.3 ± 2.9 min to 5.5 ± 2.0 min. We speculate that the observed, enhanced tissue movement could be a compensatory behavior by the coral to offset the loss of cilia-driven mixing.

Extreme GVC O2 conditions under simultaneous inhibition of tissue and ciliary movement: with both MgCl2 and Na3VO4 treatment, the integrated O2 concentration increased to 400 μmol O2 L-1 under light, occasionally peaking at 500 μmol O2 L-1, and decreased to <50 μmol O2 L-1 in the dark (Figures 4A and 4B; Table S2). Kruskal-Wallis tests confirmed significant differences among the four treatments (p < 0.001), with large effect sizes observed under both dark (η2 = 0.502) and light (η2 = 0.667) conditions; Dunn’s post-hoc tests further indicated that all pairwise comparisons were statistically significant (p < 0.001). Measured O2 concentration profiles also showed that the O2 concentration was highest under light and lowest in darkness when both MgCl2 and Na3VO4 were administered, i.e., when active transport via tissue movement or cilia beating was inhibited (Figures 5A and 5B). Local O2 concentration in the GVC reached up to 600 μmol O2 L-1 in light, which was two-to 3-fold higher than in controls (Figures 5C–5E). Such high internal O2 levels can generate severe oxidative stress in corals.6,7,8 In contrast, the lack of water exchange under dark conditions promoted enhanced O2 depletion, leading to anoxia in the middle and lower regions of the GVC (Figure 5B). These findings highlight the physiological importance of active transport processes in modulating the coral internal oxygen environment, where tissue movement and ciliary beating can alleviate excessive oxygen buildup during the day and enhance oxygen supply during the night, thereby contributing to a higher level of internal O2 homeostasis. At the coral holobiont scale, these mechanisms can explain recent observations showing that many species are capable of oxyregulation across a wide range of environmental O2 conditions.34

Beyond O2 dynamics, such active transport may also contribute to the acquisition of organic substrates and inorganic carbon sources, the distribution of nutrients within and between the GVC of interconnected individual polyps, and the excretion of metabolic waste. The internal transport of O2, nutrients, and waste products could thus be tightly coupled to water movement driven by tissue plasticity and ciliary beating. However, fluid dynamics in the GVC remain understudied. To date, only a few studies have examined the internal flow patterns in reef-building corals,30,35 and key processes—such as water movement within the cavity, the capacity for internal-external exchange, and the plasticity of these mechanisms under environmental stress—are still largely unexplored. Characterizing the integrated dynamics of these processes will provide a more comprehensive view of coral physiology and homeostasis. In this context, novel analytical tools, such as sensor nanoparticle-based imaging of internal O2 conditions across several polyps,36 may further enhance the quantification of spatiotemporal dynamics of the chemical microenvironment, helping us understand more broadly the process of substrate exchange in diffusion-limited multicellular organisms.

Taken together, while the GVC of reef-building corals exhibits strong light-driven dynamics, corals can also actively control their internal O2 microenvironment via a combination of cilia-induced advective transport and active, albeit slow, tissue contraction/expansion behavior. While inhibiting one or both behavioral mechanisms, O2 conditions in the GVC of corals became more extreme, i.e., more hypoxic in the dark and more hyperoxic in the light. These results indicate that coral behavior plays a critical role in maintaining O2 homeostasis and may represent key adaptive traits enabling corals to persist in a dynamic O2 environment fluctuating between nighttime hypoxia/anoxia and daytime hyperoxia.2 Our study thus reveals a previously underappreciated role of behavior in regulating internal microenvironments, expanding current views of how sessile, diffusion-limited organisms maintain physiological stability.

Limitations of the study

In this study, we used O2 concentration as a proxy for changes in the GVC microenvironment. However, the drivers of tissue movements and ciliary beating in corals are likely multifactorial and cannot be fully explained by O2 alone. In addition, tissue movement was quantified based on the vertical movement of the coral mouth and surrounding tissues, providing a simplified representation of coral morphology that does not fully capture the three-dimensional dynamics of internal cavity deformation. Experiments were conducted on corals with relatively large, individual polyps to facilitate measurement. However, the contributions of tissue movements and ciliary beating to circulation and exchange processes in the GVC may vary among coral species with different polyp sizes and morphologies. Especially in corals with interconnected polyps and GVCs, potential exchange and coordination behavior between polyps may introduce additional complexity.

Experimental model and study participant details

Colonies of Caulastrea curvata and Galaxea fascicularis were obtained from Dejong Marine Life (Netherlands) and maintained in the laboratory at the University of Copenhagen, Marine Biology Section in Helsingør, Denmark. Colonies of Cladocora caespitosa, Oculina patagonica and Eunicella singularis were sampled by SCUBA diving from the northern Mediterranean Sea,26 and colonies of Stylophora pistillata were grown in indoor aquaria at the Center Scientifique de Monaco. All corals were kept in constantly aerated, thermostated aquaria (salinity of 35 PSU), under a photon irradiance (400–700 nm) of 150 μmol photons m-2 s-1 with a 12:12h light-dark cycle. Tropical corals (C. curvata, G. fascicularis and S. pistillata) were kept at 25°C, while corals from the Mediterranean Sea (C. caespitosa, O. patagonica and E. singularis) were kept at 18°C. Partial colonies of Lobophyllia hemprichii, Goniastrea sp., Pocillopora damicornis and Platygyra lamellina were collected from the reef flat of Heron Island (Great Barrier Reef, Australia; permit n. G24/49877.(1) and kept in an outdoor aquarium at Heron Island Research station that was continuously flushed with aerated seawater from the reef flat. Colonies of Cycloseris sp., Lobophyllia hemprichii, and Blastomussa wellis. used for temporal measurements in this study, were acquired from Majestic Aquariums (Taren Point, Sydney, Australia) and maintained in a research aquarium at the University of Technology Sydney. Corals were kept at 26°C under a predominantly blue spectrum light (180 μmol photons m-2 s-(1) across a 10:14 h light:dark cycle. Prior to measurements in the flow chamber, coral colonies were fragmented into nubbins (n = 3–(6) and acclimated for 1–2 weeks before measurements.

Method details

Microscale O2 measurements in the coral gastrovascular cavity

The spatial O2 distribution in the GVC was measured using electrochemical O2 microsensors with a tip diameter of 50 μm and a custom-made slender sensor shaft to enable insertion into deep gastrovascular cavities without tissue disturbance (OX-50; Unisense A/S, Denmark; accuracy of ∼0.5–1% air saturation, and a detection limit and resolution of 0.1–0.2% air saturation). The O2 microsensor was connected to a multichannel microsensor meter (fx-6 UniAmp, Unisense A/S, Denmark), and the sensor was mounted on a motorized micromanipulator (Unisense A/S, Denmark) to enable precise control of the sensor position. Data acquisition and positioning of the microsensor were done via a PC running dedicated software (SensorSuite Profiler v3.4; Unisense A/S, Denmark).

The average O2 concentration dynamic in the GVC over time was measured using a special fiber-optic O2 microsensor (OXIMP250; Pyroscience GmbH, Germany); with a detection limit of 0.2% air saturation, an accuracy of ±0.2–2% air saturation, and a resolution of 0.1–0.5% air saturation (https://www.pyroscience.com/en/products/all-sensors/oximp250?file=files/website_data/Downloads/Specifications/Sensors/Specification_InsertableMini%20Probe.pdf&cid=22620). The sensor is optimized for integrative measurements in samples with oxygen micro-heterogeneities like animal tissue, organs or soils. It consists of an optical fiber (diameter of 250 μm), where the sensing area covers a length of 3 mm at the sensor tip and measures integrated O2 levels over the entire sensor area. The fiber-optic microsensor was mounted on the motorized micromanipulator (Unisense A/S, Denmark) and connected to a PC-interfaced optical O2 meter (FSO2-C4, Pyroscience, Germany). Data were recorded via the software Pyro Workbench (v1.5.4, Pyroscience, Germany). Both types of O2 microsensors were calibrated from signal readings in 100% air-saturated seawater and anoxic water (using an alkaline ascorbate solution) at experimental temperature and salinity.

Microsensor measurements were performed in a flow chamber under a steady, laminar flow (1 cm s-1), which is within the range of flow velocities observed in situ on reef flats.37 The flow chamber was connected to a submersed water pump in a supporting aquarium tank beneath the flow chamber that provided a flow of constantly aerated temperature-controlled seawater. The positioning of the microsensor tip relative to the coral tissue was observed via a digital microscopy camera (Dino-Lite Edge AM7515MZT, AnMo Electronics, Taiwan). The O2 sensor tip was positioned at the center of the coral mouth by manually handling the micromanipulator; the sensor was then lowered vertically by 50–100 μm steps using the motor until a contraction of the coral polyp was observed, and this position was therefore identified as the base of the gastrovascular cavity. During depth profiling, O2 concentration was measured by moving the sensor tip stepwise from the gastrovascular cavity’s base upward toward the mouth (n = 3–6). For temporal O2 dynamic measurements, the sensor tip of the electrochemical O2 microsensor was positioned at 50% depth of the gastrovascular cavity (midway between mouth and GVC base), and the sensor tip of the fiber-optic O2 microsensor was kept just above the base of the gastrovascular cavity. O2 dynamics were measured in darkness or under illumination by a fiber optic, white LED lamp (KL 2500 LED, Schott, Germany). Corals kept in the indoor aquarium were measured under a photon irradiance (400–700 nm) of 100–200 μmol photons m-2 s-1, i.e., matching long-term growth conditions in our husbandry aquaria, while corals sampled from the shallow reef flat were measured under a higher photon irradiance of 300–500 μmol photons m-2 s-1, representative of reef flat conditions.38,39 Photon irradiance was quantified in the experimental setup with a photon irradiance meter (ULM-500, Heinz Walz, Germany) equipped with a spherical micro quantum sensor (US-SQS/L, Heinz Walz, Germany).

We used magnesium chloride (MgCl2) to immobilize coral tissue movement and sodium orthovanadate (Na3VO4) to arrest cilia beating.11,12,33,40 We used 92 mM MgCl2 because published studies have demonstrated that this concentration effectively and rapidly immobilizes cnidarians, while allowing for quick recovery.33 The effect of cilia beating arrestment was tested by imaging coral fragments on a microscope (BX50WI, Olympus, Japan) equipped with a 40× water dipping objective (LUMPLFLN 40XW, 0.8 NA, Olympus, Japan) and a high-speed camera (Chronos 1.4, Kron Technology, Canada). A polyp of Caulastrea curvata was placed in a custom-made flow chamber with a transparent glass bottom to observe the cilia beating frequency at edges of the fragment. In a Na3VO4 concentration of 1 mM, cilia beating was fully arrested after 2 h, and the arrest persisted for at least 6 h.

Microsensor measurements were performed on corals in seawater without MgCl2 or Na3VO4 addition as control and in seawater with (1) 92 mM MgCl2, (2) 1 mM Na3VO4, and (3) 92 mM MgCl2 and 1 mM Na3VO4 (demineralized water was added to adjust the osmotic pressure). Measurements commenced 30 min after placing the coral polyp in seawater with MgCl2, and 2 h after placing it in seawater with Na3VO4.

Time-lapse imaging of corals

During time-series measurements of O2 concentration in the gastrovascular cavity, we used a digital microscope (Dino-Lite Edge AM7515MZT, AnMo Electronics, Taiwan) to capture time-lapse images of coral tissue movement. The video recordings for C. curvata were captured at 1 frame every 30 seconds under light and 1 frame every 2 min during dark measurement; for L. hemprichii, the video recordings were captured at 1 frame every 10 s under light. The imaging involved a brief 2–3 s exposure of the coral to low light levels (<10 μmol photons m-2 s-(1) from a white LED light. To accurately measure the range of tissue movement, we made markings on the microsensor at distances of 5, 7, 9 and 11 mm from the sensor tip and used these markings as references for distance quantification of coral tissue contraction/expansion.

Quantification and statistical analysis

We used kymograph analysis of the time-lapse images to track the trajectory of coral tissue movement. The image sequences were first converted to binary images. In the converted image, the sensor was white, while the coral mouth and the shadow in the mouth were black (Figure S3B). We drew a straight line (1 pixel wide) along the sensor and used the kymograph function in ImageJ to track the trajectory of the black pixels on the white line (Figure S3C), which corresponds to the trajectory of the coral mouth moving along the sensor. From the kymograph results, we extracted the position of the coral mouth over time and used the markings on the sensor to calibrate the distance. Some time-lapse recordings in the dark had lower contrast, and we marked the position of the coral mouth over time manually.

Cross-correlation analysis was performed to evaluate the synchronization between O2 concentration and tissue movement distance. Both time series were detrended and standardized prior to analysis, and the cross-correlation function (CCF) was computed in R, with the optimal lag defined as the time shift yielding the maximum absolute correlation coefficient. Homogeneity of variances in tissue movement distance between Na3VO4-treated and untreated corals was assessed using Levene’s test from the car package. Kruskal–Wallis tests were used to assess differences in O2 concentration across four conditions, followed by Dunn’s post-hoc tests with Benjamini–Hochberg adjustment for multiple pairwise comparisons. Given the relatively large sample size, η2 was calculated to estimate effect size. Kruskal–Wallis tests, Dunn’s post-hoc tests, and η2 effect size calculations were performed in R using the stats, FSA, and rstatix packages.41
