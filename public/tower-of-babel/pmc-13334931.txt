Smartphone-based lightweight AI system for real-time multiple anterior segment disease screening: development and real-world validation

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAnterior segment diseases are a major global cause of preventable blindness, especially in regions with limited access to specialized ophthalmic care. Diagnosis typically requires slit-lamp biomicroscopy, creating a significant access bottleneck in primary care and rural settings. Existing AI solutions often lack the efficiency and generalizability necessary for widespread mobile deployment and primarily focus on individual conditions, failing to meet the demand for scalable, multi-disease screening platforms.MethodsWe developed the Intelligent Detection System (IDS), a smartphone-compatible AI platform for real-time, automated, multi-disease screening of anterior segment diseases. The core model, Eye-YOLO, is a novel, lightweight deep learning model optimized for standard smartphone images. IDS integrates image quality assessment and urgency classification. The model was rigorously trained on a large, heterogeneous multi-center dataset of 24,671 images (comprising 17,853 slit-lamp and 6,818 smartphone images) collected from three tertiary hospitals in China. IDS was prospectively deployed and validated via a widely accessible WeChat Mini Program for community screening.ResultsEye-YOLO achieved a mean average precision of 0.816 with an exceptionally compact architecture (2.77 million parameters and 7.3G FLOPs), enabling real-time inference at 131 FPS on mobile devices. In controlled testing, IDS demonstrated high diagnostic performance (93.50% accuracy; AUC = 0.9837) with consistent performance across multiple smartphone brands (AUC > 0.95). Crucially, with AI assistance, junior ophthalmologists achieved an accuracy of 93.79% (AUC 0.8984), compared with 95.79% for senior experts. In real-world external validation via the WeChat Mini Program, IDS achieved 98.25% accuracy.ConclusionsIDS provides a highly efficient, robust, and globally scalable framework for multi-disease anterior segment screening using ubiquitous smartphone technology. Background

The anterior segment of the eye—including the cornea, conjunctiva, lens, eyelid, and iris—constitutes the front third of the ocular structure and is essential for maintaining optical clarity and visual function. Due to its anatomical exposure, high metabolic demand, and susceptibility to environmental, mechanical, and inflammatory insults, it is vulnerable to a broad range of disorders, such as keratitis, conjunctivitis, pterygium, and cataracts [1–4]. Among these, cataracts and corneal opacity are leading global causes of blindness, particularly in low- and middle-income countries where access to specialized care remains limited [5, 6]. With aging populations and increasing environmental risk exposure, the burden of anterior segment diseases continues to rise, underscoring the urgent need for accessible and scalable screening solutions [7–9].

Accurate diagnosis necessitates a comprehensive slit-lamp examination, the gold standard in ophthalmic evaluation. However, the requirement for specialized equipment and skilled clinicians creates a significant barrier to entry in primary or rural settings, contributing to delayed diagnoses and missed opportunities for early treatment [10, 11]. This infrastructure and personnel shortage highlights the need for non-invasive, cost-effective, and scalable technologies capable of detecting multiple anterior segment conditions simultaneously.

Artificial intelligence (AI), particularly deep learning, has demonstrated transformative potential in ophthalmology, largely driven by success in posterior segment disease detection using large retinal image datasets [12–16]. However, AI implementation for the anterior segment has lagged due to challenges associated with image variability, lack of standardized protocols, and the scarcity of large, diverse datasets incorporating multiple diseases [17]. These constraints impede algorithmic generalizability and clinical deployment.

Smartphones, with their widespread adoption, portability, and growing imaging and computational capabilities, provide an ideal platform for mobile health (mHealth) applications. With over 4.3 billion users worldwide, smartphone-based AI tools could democratize access to ophthalmic screening, particularly in underserved regions [18–20]. However, most current AI research focuses on single-disease detection, which limits clinical utility. For example, deep learning models have achieved high accuracy in grading cataracts [21–25], diagnosing keratitis [26–28], and detecting pterygium [29, 30] via smartphone images. Despite these successes, such systems operate independently and often require disease-specific imaging protocols. This fragmented approach is inefficient in primary care settings, where patients often present with multiple or overlapping anterior segment conditions. Consequently, there is a clear need for an integrated, multi-disease platform capable of comprehensive screening [31, 32].

To address these challenges, we present the Intelligent Detection System (IDS), a lightweight, deep learning–based platform for real-time, multi-disease screening of the anterior segment using smartphone-acquired images. Trained on a large and heterogeneous image dataset, the Eye-YOLO (the core of the IDS) recognizes multiple anterior segment diseases across varied imaging sources. Deployed as a WeChat Mini Program, IDS was prospectively validated in real-world, community-based screening. This work establishes a clinically deployable, scalable framework for AI-assisted anterior segment diseases screening, bridging the gap between algorithmic innovation and primary eye care implementation.

Methods

Study Design and Overview

This retrospective and prospective observational study aimed to develop and validate a smartphone-compatible platform for real-time screening of multiple anterior segment diseases. The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Detailed ethical approval and informed consent procedures are provided in the Declarations section. This study was reported in accordance with the STARD-AI guidelines.

Data Collection

Data were collected from Xiamen Eye Center, Xiang’an Hospital of Xiamen University, and the First Affiliated Hospital of the University of South China between March 2019 and August 2025. Retrospective slit-lamp images were obtained between March 2019 and December 2022, while prospective smartphone images were collected between March 2021 and January 2025 using a standardized smartphone imaging protocol (Additional File 1: Methods S1).

To evaluate the real-world applicability of the model, a dedicated WeChat Mini Program was developed for the real-world external validation. Between March and August 2025, users accessed the Mini Program through pilot deployment in outpatient clinics, community-based eye health educational seminars, and digital dissemination via ophthalmic health education articles on WeChat Official Accounts. A total of 310 users from multiple regions voluntarily uploaded 406 anterior segment images via the Mini Program, following standardized guidelines, which constituted the MiniProgram External Test Dataset (MED).

To align the dataset with the model’s predefined classification scope, inclusion and exclusion criteria were applied. The inclusion criteria were: (1) images of patients whose ground-truth diagnoses fell within the eight pre-defined target common anterior segment diseases, and (2) eye images from normal subjects without target diseases. Conversely, the exclusion criteria were: (1) images of patients with other rare ocular diseases or conditions outside the eight target categories, and (2) low-quality images. Specifically, all images were pre-screened using the Automated Anterior Segment Image Quality Control System (ACS) to ensure inclusion of only high-quality, clear images. Images with blurred focus, inadequate illumination, overexposure, abnormal eye positioning, or incomplete corneal exposure were excluded [33]. Only one image per eye per patient was retained in the final dataset.

In total, 17,853 slit-lamp images and 6,818 smartphone images (captured using HUAWEI smartphones) were collected. Of these, 300 images were annotated by ophthalmic experts to form the Expert Smartphone Dataset (ESD). Additionally, 1,551 images captured using Xiaomi and iPhone smartphones were collected to assess model generalizability, forming the Xiaomi Smartphone Dataset (XSD) and iPhone Smartphone Dataset (ISD), respectively. Image annotation

After quality control (Additional File 1: Figure S1), all eligible images were annotated with one or more of eight predefined disease labels: cataract, keratitis, pterygium, conjunctival injection, pinguecula, corneal degeneration, eyelid masses, and conjunctival masses (Additional File 1: Table S1). These labels were chosen to represent the most common and visually identifiable anterior segment diseases, based on their clinical prevalence [34, 35].

Slit-lamp image annotations followed a multi-tiered process. Three junior ophthalmologists (2–3 years of experience) independently annotated each image, followed by a review from three intermediate-level ophthalmologists (5–8 years of experience) who resolved discrepancies by consensus. Ambiguous cases were adjudicated by a panel of three senior experts (> 10 years of experience) to determine the final labels. Images with inconclusive diagnoses were excluded. For smartphone-acquired images, annotations were based on the clinical diagnoses made by ophthalmologists at the time of image capture. No time restriction was imposed. To evaluate the clinical utility of AI assistance, the same group of junior ophthalmologists independently assessed the ESD dataset without AI support. After a 2-week washout period to minimize recall bias, the same images were re-evaluated with AI assistance. In the real-world external validation of the WeChat Mini Program, smartphone-acquired images collected through the program underwent the same multi-tier annotation protocol.

Deep learning model architecture

We developed a novel anterior segment disease detection network based on a feature fusion strategy, termed Eye-YOLO, which is an optimized multi-label object detection model YOLOv11 [36] (Additional File 1: Method S2, Figure S2). The model directly outputs bounding boxes with corresponding disease categories and confidence scores. Eye-YOLO introduces an innovative layered feature fusion strategy. Based on this strategy, the Layered Feature Fusion (LFF) module is designed, thereby more efficiently capturing long-range dependencies. Eye-YOLO introduces three Adaptive Spatial Feature Fusion (ASFF) modules after the original PAFPN neck [37], with each ASFF module processing one output from the PAFPN neck. Subsequently, the outputs are integrated with the base residual layers to achieve feature fusion and enhancement (Additional File 1: Table S2).

Layered feature fusion module

Multi-scale detection remains a major challenge in object detection. To address this, we proposed a layered feature fusion strategy to enhance the network’s ability to integrate contextual information at different scales. This strategy enhances the network width by introducing multi-level residual paths in each building block. These paths enable the network to learn and extract features at different scales, thereby enhancing its ability to capture key information from complex images. In each module, the input features are divided into multiple groups, which are processed in parallel through convolutional operations to extract diverse information. Based on the layered feature fusion strategy, we introduce a novel module called the Layered Feature Fusion (LFF) module (Additional File 1: Figure S3). This module enhances the object detector’s ability to extract multi-scale features while maintaining fast inference speed. The C3k2 block is the basic building block of YOLOv11 [36]. The LFF module is designed based on the C3k2 block and relies on the layered feature fusion strategy to expand the network width by introducing multi-level residual paths. Model training and optimization

To prevent overfitting and improve generalization, we applied multiple image augmentation techniques [38] (Additional File 1: Methods S3–4, Figure S4). The SPD dataset was split into training and test sets in an 8:2 ratio. Five-fold cross-validation was used on the training set to identify the optimal model. Additionally, the SLD dataset was included to enrich feature diversity. Model performance was evaluated on the test set (Additional File 1: Methods S5-6, Figure S5). During the training phase, the Eye-YOLO was trained for 300 epochs with a batch size of 8. The SGD optimizer was used, with a weight decay rate of 1 × 10⁻⁴, an initial learning rate (lr) of 0.01, and a momentum of 0.937. In the testing phase, the non-maximum suppression (NMS) algorithm was employed with an IoU threshold set at 0.7 to eliminate redundant detection results. Detailed methodological descriptions and associated references are provided in the Supplementary Materials and are cited here for completeness [39–44]

Smartphone system implementation

To facilitate early screening of various anterior segment diseases for users, an anterior segment disease automated Intelligent Detection System (IDS) was developed based on a previously established image quality control algorithm and a lightweight disease detection model, Eye-YOLO. The overall workflow of the IDS is shown in Fig. 1. The IDS was implemented as a WeChat Mini Program (Tencent, Shenzhen, China), enabling real-time inference and accessibility in community-based settings. The system workflow involves four critical stages: (1) Smartphone image capture following standardized protocols; (2) Automated quality control via ACS; (3) Disease detection using the Eye-YOLO model; and (4) Output generation, including multi-label detection results with bounding boxes and model-derived confidence scores, accompanied by target-specific heatmaps and screening suggestion. The development and deployment environment is detailed in Method S7 (Additional File 1: Methods S7).

Statistical analysis

Model performance was evaluated using standard metrics, including the number of parameters (Params), floating-point operations (FLOPs), frames per second (FPS), and mean average precision (mAP) for object detection. For clinical diagnostic evaluation, image-level disease labels were derived directly from the detection outputs. An image was considered positive for a given disease if at least one predicted bounding box exceeded the predefined confidence threshold after non-maximum suppression; otherwise, it was considered negative. Based on these image-level labels, we calculated sensitivity, specificity, F1 score, accuracy, and the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). ROC curves and confusion matrices were plotted to visualize model performance. Model interpretability was visualized using Gradient-weighted Class Activation Mapping (Grad-CAM) [45]. Data are presented as mean ± SD for normally distributed variables and as median (IQR) for non-normally distributed variables. All statistical analyses and visualizations were performed using Python (v3.8.18) with packages including Grad-CAM (v1.5.0), pandas (v2.0.3), scikit-learn (v1.3.2), numpy (v1.24.4), Pillow (v10.2.0), and matplotlib (v3.7.5).

Results

Comparative performance and efficiency of Eye-YOLO

To identify a model suitable for smartphone-based deployment in anterior segment disease detection, we compared several object detection models to evaluate its suitability for resource-constrained mobile environments (Table 2). Eye-YOLO achieved the highest mAP (0.816) while maintaining exceptional computational efficiency (2.77 M parameters, 7.3G FLOPs) and high inference speed (131 FPS). This performance profile—achieving superior detection quality with a model size nearly 15 to 20 times smaller than heavy transformer-based competitors like DINO or Dynamic RCNN—validates its lightweight design and suitability for real-time mobile deployment. Ablation experiments confirmed the effectiveness of the proposed LFF and ASFF modules in improving detection performance (Additional File 1: Table S2).

Eye-YOLO performance in individual anterior segment diseases

To further assess the disease-specific diagnostic performance of Eye-YOLO, we evaluated its metrics across eight anterior segment disease categories in the SPD dataset (Table 3). The model achieved a mean accuracy of 93.50%, with consistently high mean sensitivity (0.9255), specificity (0.9358), F1 score (0.9073), and AUC (0.9837; 95% CI: 0.9717–0.9924), indicating strong overall diagnostic capability. Eye-YOLO performed particularly well in common, well-represented diseases such as cataract (sensitivity 0.9775; AUC 0.9787) and corneal degeneration (sensitivity 0.9750; AUC 0.9902). In contrast, conditions with limited case representation in the training dataset—such as keratitis, conjunctival masses, and eyelid masses—exhibited relatively lower sensitivities, likely reflecting dataset imbalance rather than intrinsic model limitations. Nevertheless, the model maintained high specificity across all disease categories, indicating a low false-positive rate and suggesting its potential for reliable deployment in real-world screening scenarios. Comparative clinical performance of junior ophthalmologists, AI-assisted junior ophthalmologists, senior experts, and Eye-YOLO

The diagnostic performance of unaided junior ophthalmologists, AI-assisted junior ophthalmologists, senior experts, and the Eye-YOLO model was evaluated on the ESD dataset (Table 5; Fig. 4).

Unaided junior ophthalmologists demonstrated high specificity (0.9834) but relatively limited sensitivity (0.6701), resulting in a mean AUC of 0.8268. With AI assistance, sensitivity increased to 0.8265 and mean AUC improved to 0.8984 (95% CI: 0.8641–0.9307), while specificity remained high at 0.9703. Overall accuracy improved from 90.33% to 93.79%. Senior experts achieved a mean AUC of 0.9291, sensitivity of 0.8667, specificity of 0.9896, and the highest overall accuracy (95.79%). The Eye-YOLO model achieved the highest AUC (0.9789; 95% CI: 0.9650–0.9892) and sensitivity (0.9336), with a specificity of 0.8869.

Overall, AI assistance markedly improved junior ophthalmologists’ diagnostic performance and narrowed the gap with senior experts, whereas the Eye-YOLO model achieved the highest sensitivity with lower specificity than human experts. Discussion

Undetected anterior segment diseases remain a major contributor to preventable vision loss, particularly in regions with limited access to specialized ophthalmic care. These conditions typically require slit-lamp examinations performed by experienced clinicians, yet such equipment and expertise are often unavailable in primary care settings. The growing accessibility of smartphones, combined with recent advances in AI, offers a promising solution to bridge this diagnostic gap and facilitate earlier disease detection at the community level.

In this study, we developed a deep learning–based Intelligent Detection System (IDS) for real-time screening of multiple anterior segment diseases using smartphone-acquired images. At its core, the Eye-YOLO model was specifically designed for lightweight deployment, enabling real-time inference on mobile devices without the need for high-end hardware. With only 2.77 million parameters and 7.3G FLOPs, Eye-YOLO achieved high diagnostic accuracy and consistent performance across datasets from various smartphone brands (Huawei, Xiaomi, iPhone), with all AUCs exceeding 0.95 and sensitivities above 0.89. This robust cross-device generalizability demonstrates the potential for scalable and accessible ophthalmic screening.

Importantly, integration of IDS into the WeChat Mini Program enabled real-world external validation beyond retrospective datasets. In this deployment setting, IDS achieved a high overall accuracy of 98.25%. Within the Mini Program workflow, image adequacy is assessed prior to model inference, and images that do not meet minimal interpretability criteria prompt users to retake images. This structured screening pathway reflects the practical design of community-based digital triage systems [46, 47].

The IDS also demonstrated dual utility within hierarchical healthcare systems. By significantly improving the diagnostic performance of junior ophthalmologists, it may serve as a clinical decision support tool in primary settings. Simultaneously, deployment through a widely used social platform enables large-scale community screening, facilitating early risk identification and triage beyond traditional clinical infrastructure. As a first-pass screening tool, IDS was designed to identify eight common and clinically significant anterior segment diseases rather than provide fine-grained subtyping, aligning its role with population-level risk stratification rather than comprehensive clinical diagnosis.

However, this study has several limitations. First, performance in certain early-stage or underrepresented disease categories was comparatively lower, likely reflecting limited positive sample sizes and the inherent difficulty of detecting subtle lesions on smartphone-acquired images. Larger, multi-center prospective studies are warranted to further validate performance stability and generalizability in real-world populations. Second, as a screening tool, IDS focuses on risk stratification rather than clinical subtyping, and its reliability for atypical cases requires evaluation. Additionally, the limited number of physicians in the human-AI comparison may affect generalizability. Finally, IDS is a triage aid rather than a clinical replacement; professional follow-up remains essential for symptomatic individuals.

Conclusions

This study presents a practical and scalable solution for anterior segment disease screening using a lightweight deep learning model integrated into a smartphone-based platform. By combining lightweight architecture, cross-device robustness, and structured workflow integration, IDS demonstrates promising potential to enhance community-level triage and expand access to ophthalmic care.
