Evaluating wildlife community composition around villages with varying Guinea worm (Dracunculus medinensis) transmission in Chad, Africa
Authors: Avery L Korns, Karmen Unterwegner, Adam J Weiss, Philip Tchindebet Oaukou, Hubert Zirimwabagabo, Richard B Chandler, Dana J Morin, Michael J Yabsley, Christopher A Cleveland
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSince 2012, Guinea worm (GW – Dracunculus medinensis) infections in animals have complicated the Guinea worm Eradication Program's (GWEP) goal to eradicate this parasite. Increased infections in domestic dogs (Canis lupus familiaris) and cats (Felis catus) have been a concern. Detections of infections in wild felids from Chad and olive baboons (Papio anubis) in Ethiopia highlight a significant knowledge gap concerning the role of wildlife in GW transmission. Furthermore, little is known about the diversity of wildlife surrounding villages in Chad and what role these animals may play in GW transmission. We aimed to provide data on the community composition of wildlife surrounding Chadian villages by deploying game cameras to monitor terrestrial wildlife at study sites in the Sarh region of southern Chad. Seven villages were chosen for game camera deployment based on accessibility, spatial independence, and GW disease (GWD) endemicity. Villages were classified by GW transmission levels as either endemic, sporadic, or negative based on reports during a previous surveillance study. Eight game cameras were deployed per village from February–November 2019. A total of 32 mammalian species were recorded from 9280 trap nights. Our results indicate higher wildlife diversity surrounding villages with GWD. Specifically, we found a higher detection of primates and meso-mammals surrounding villages with consistent reports of Guinea worm infections. Single-season occupancy models were fit for the following species: tantalus monkeys (Chlorocebus tantalus), patas monkeys (Erythrocebus patas), Egyptian mongoose (Herpestes ichneumon), and pale fox (Vulpes pallida). Occupancy of tantalus monkeys, patas monkeys, and Egyptian mongoose was greater in endemic and sporadic villages than villages with no reported cases of GWD. While wildlife host competency remains unknown, our results provide documentation on the wildlife species present around villages in Sarh, Chad, a step towards increasing the understanding wildlife may potentially play in the transmission of GW.Keywords: (max 6), Eradication, Game cameras, Guinea worm, Occupancy models, Reservoirs, Wildlife

Introduction

Dracunculiasis is a painful and debilitating disease caused by the parasitic nematode Guinea worm (GW - Dracunculus medinensis) that has afflicted people in parts of Africa, south Asia and the Middle East for much of recorded history (Adamson, 1988; Grove, 1990; Muller, 1971, 1991). Typical transmission to the definitive host occurs from ingestion of cyclopoid copepods containing infective third-stage larvae (Fedchenko, 1870). The Guinea Worm Eradication Program (GWEP), led by the Carter Center in partnership with Ministries of Health in endemic countries, has been successful at reducing the number of human cases from 3.5 million across 21 countries in 1986 to only 14 cases in 5 countries (Cameroon, Central African Republic, Chad, Mali, and South Sudan) in 2023 (Watts, 1987; World Health Organization, 1996; WHO Collaborating Center for Research, Training and Eradication of Dracunculiasis, CDC, 2022a; Hopkins et al., 2024). Between January and June 2024, while there were only three human cases documented, there were 297 animal infections reported in five countries (Angola, Chad, Ethiopia, Mali, and South Sudan) (Hopkins et al., 2024). In 2010, Chad reported its first human GWD case after a 10-year absence (Djidina et al., 2011), followed soon thereafter by reports of GW infections in domestic dogs and domestic cats (Eberhard et al., 2014; Hopkins et al., 2018). Chadian animal infections, mostly from domestic dogs, have consistently outnumbered Chadian human cases since their first detection in 2012, and peaked in 2019 with 1982 infections (Hopkins et al., 2020). Since 2019, dog cases have decreased in Chad due to numerous intervention efforts, and there were 407 dog cases reported in 2023 (Hopkins et al., 2024). Interestingly, GW infections in dogs were first observed to not follow the classical epidemiological patterns seen in humans (Eberhard et al., 2014). This different epidemiology led to the hypothesis that an aquatic paratenic or transport host (such as fish or amphibians) was now involved in the lifecycle, which has been supported by experimental and field studies (Eberhard et al., 2014, 2016b, 2016a; Cleveland et al., 2017, 2019; Box et al., 2021). Increased prevention measures, such as tethering dogs, has reduced global animal infections to 886 in 2023, of which 496 (56 %) originated from Chad (Hopkins et al., 2024).

In addition to the increase in domestic dog infections, GW infections confirmed by molecular methods have been documented in several wildlife species in Chad, Ethiopia, and South Sudan (Hopkins et al., 2018, 2024; WHO Collaborating Center for Research, Training and Eradication of Dracunculiasis, CDC, 2019; 2022b). Chad has reported multiple infections of un-emerged (subcutaneous) GW in African wildcats (Felis lybica), a serval (Leptailurus serval), and African civets (Hopkins et al., 2018, 2021, 2023). Ethiopia has reported at least one olive baboon (Papio anubis) infection annually since 2013 except for 2021 and 2023 (Hopkins et al., 2023). Additionally, in Ethiopia, one leopard (Panthera pardus) and one serval were reported with un-emerged GW in 2019 and 2023, respectively (Hopkins et al., 2020, 2023). From 2023 to 2024, South Sudan reported 14 individual wild animals with un-emerged worms, including servals, African wild cats, a genet, and African civets, and two genets with emerged worms (Hopkins et al., 2024). Despite some issues with sample quality (samples may be desiccated), or the challenge of removing worms from wildlife, recent molecular studies have confirmed that GW from humans and animals (both domestic and wild) are the same species (Thiele et al., 2018; Durrant et al., 2020). The presence of GW infections in wildlife highlights a significant knowledge gap in GW epidemiology regarding the role of wildlife in the transmission and maintenance of Guinea worm.

The specific role of wildlife in GW transmission is unknown, but wildlife infections have the potential to contaminate water sources and may act as a source of transmission to people or domestic animals. This issue is complicated by a lack of data on wildlife species present in endemic areas and how wildlife may interact with humans and domestic animals. Therefore, the primary objective of this study was to document the wildlife community composition among villages in Chad, Africa with varying GWD prevalence. We set multiple camera traps surrounding villages with different GWD endemicity levels and fit single-season occupancy models with covariates expected to influence a species habitat selection and activity levels. We were particularly interested in how species occupancy may vary in relation to water bodies and village centers. Increased occupancy near permanent water sources may increase a species risk of exposure to GW (Eneanya et al., 2024). Increased occupancy near village centers may increase the risk of contaminating water sources frequented by villagers if that species is able to act as a host for GW. However, this effect may vary with an individual species tolerance to anthropomorphic change (Easter et al., 2020). Our objective of this study is to contribute to a better understanding of wildlife's potential role in GW transmission.

Materials and methods

Site selection and study area

This study was conducted in seven villages in the Sarh region of southeastern Chad (Fig. 1). Villages were chosen for game camera placement based on several factors: surveillance intensity, accessibility, spatial independence, and GWD endemicity (Supplemental Fig. 1, Supplemental Table 1). Only the 432 villages with the highest level of surveillance activity (Level 1) were considered for game camera placement to increase confidence in accurate GWD endemicity designations. Villages were classified as Level 1 if they met the following conditions: reported consistent transmission of GWD since 2010, trained village volunteers conducted household searches 3–4 times per week in search for signs of infection, offered cash rewards for reporting of suspected cases, suspected cases were investigated by a supervisor within 24 h, provided health education to villagers, and conducted periodic evaluations of cash reward awareness and dracunculiasis education (Guagliardo et al., 2020). Village accessibility was a critical factor, as some villages are not easily accessible during the rainy season. Occupancy models (described below) assume statistical independence among sampling locations, which is violated if individual animals can be detected at multiple villages. To meet the spatial independence assumption, we only included villages that were at least 20 km away from each other. The distance of 20 km was chosen to ensure non-overlapping home ranges for most small mammals and increase likelihood that reported domestic dog and human GW infections were not from genetically identical worms (Ribado et al., 2021). To classify GWD endemicity, GW infection data during 2014–2018 (provided by The Carter Center) were summarized, and villages were classified as having endemic transmission (categorized by reporting greater than 10 infections in any host during the four-year study), sporadic transmission (categorized by reporting 1–10 infections in any host during the four-year study), or no reported cases (no infections in any host during the four-year study).

Map of villages where game cameras were deployed. Buffers show the non-overlapping 10 km radial buffer zone around each village. Three villages (Tarakoh, Ngangoli Tounia, and Gentil) were classified as endemic transmission, three villages (Dilbodo, Maicomb, and Gongo) were classified as sporadic transmission, and Waltama was the only negative village in the region that met all criteria for inclusion.

All villages included in this study were along the Chari River or its tributaries and southeast of the Salamat floodplain (Fig. 1). This area is in the Sudan-Savanna biotic zone, where abundant rainfall supports larger human populations and increased cultivation. Agriculture, alongside livestock grazing and browsing, have converted the area to broadleaf shrubby savanna (Keith and Plowes, 1997). Cattle and goats are the most commonly raised livestock. Peri-domestic dogs traditionally roam freely in villages, although there are some villages without dogs. From WorldClim data collected from 1970 to 2000, average annual minimum and maximum temperature of the area ranges from 15 °C to 39 °C. Average annual precipitation is around 990 mm, with most of the rain falling from May–September (Fick and Hijmans, 2017).

Game camera deployment

Eight Browning Dark Ops HD cameras (Forestry Suppliers, Inc., Jackson, MS) were deployed at each village for a total of 56 cameras (Supplemental Fig. 2). The deployment plan included four game cameras set at the cardinal positions (north, south, east, and west) 500 m away from the village center and another four game cameras set up at the offset cardinal positions (northeast, southeast, southwest, and northwest) 1000 m away from the village center. However, in some cases, adjustments in placement were necessary because of landowner requests or geographic factors. Cameras were placed in a security box on a tree 0.6 m above the ground and secured with a nylon strap and locking cable. Orientation of cameras avoided pointing directly due East or due West and often were strategically pointed at various game trails in the immediate area. Cameras were triggered by motion with a 60 s delay. Cameras were deployed from February 27, 2019–March 5, 2019, and picked up November 4, 2019–November 9, 2019. Cameras were checked at the end of May and August 2019 to replace batteries and SD cards.

Data analysis

All animals detected by the cameras were identified to the most specific taxonomy possible and cataloged. Analysis was performed using single-season occupancy models to estimate site occupancy (Image 1 - the probability that a species occurs at a site) and detection probability (p – the probability that the species is detected at the site during a single survey occasion, conditional on the site being occupied) (MacKenzie et al., 2006). Accounting for variation in detection probability is important, because wildlife can be present in a region but go undetected if they are cryptic or rare. Individual cameras within each village are not independent of one another. However, this nested study design allows for more interpretations of species’ activity and space use within villages. Analysis was done in R version 4.1.1 using package ‘unmarked’ and ArcGIS Pro version 2.9.0 (R Core Team, 2021; Kellner et al., 2023). All covariates were standardized to have a mean of zero and unit variance.

Occupancy was modeled as a function of endemicity level, Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI), landcover type, and the distance from the camera to the village center (m), the nearest water source (m), and the nearest path (m). Species-specific home range size estimates were used to calculate statistics for NDVI and percent landcover for each village using a dissolved buffer around each camera (Table 1). Landsat 8 images (Path 181 and 182, Row 54) processed using Level 1 Terrain Precision Correction (L1TP) were downloaded from the U.S. Geological Survey. Mean, maximum, minimum, and range NDVI were calculated for each buffered village area using as many cloud-free images as possible during the study period. Landcover data for 2019 were downloaded from Esri at the 10 m scale (Karra et al., 2021). Landcover types in this area included water, trees, flooded vegetation, crops, built area, bare ground, and rangeland. Paths surrounding villages and cameras were digitized using the satellite base map of ArcGIS Pro.

Detection probability was modeled as a function of climate, NDVI, and total trapping days. Climate variables included average monthly temperature, maximum monthly temperature, minimum monthly temperature, monthly temperature range, and average monthly precipitation. Climate variables were calculated for all villages using WorldClim 1970–2000 data in ArcGIS Pro using a 30 km overlapping dissolved buffer, as differences in temperature and precipitation between villages were minimal (Fick and Hijmans, 2017). Temporal changes in precipitation and temperature across the rainy and dry season may alter activity and movement, and thus detection probability (p). Maximum and minimum NDVI were included as detection covariates to help account for taller grasses concealing smaller species. An individual camera's total trapping days was included to help account for days lost due to camera failure or human disturbance.

We fit multiple models with a combination of occupancy and detection covariates and compared the models using Akaike's Information Criterion (AIC) and relative Akaike model weights (wi) (Burnham and Anderson, 2002). Correlation coefficients of covariates within the best-fit models were checked to ensure no moderate or strong correlations. Models were tested for goodness-of-fit using parametric bootstrapping of the sum of squared residuals with 1000 simulations. The best fit models were used to estimate village-level occupancy. Posterior distributions of latent occurrence were computed using empirical Bayes method (Kéry and Royle, 2016).

Results

Detections

Four cameras across three villages were lost over the study due to damage or theft. Daily survey occasions were excluded from analyses if cameras were deemed inoperable due to the following reasons: cameras died before battery replacement, human interaction moved camera to point at the ground, or dense vegetation obstructed camera view (generally occurred in August after high rainfall) (total of 1396 days). In total, 52 cameras were fully operable for 9280 trapping days. The cameras acquired 1,070,891 pictures, comprising 5584 animal detections (Table 2).

Mammalian species comprised 4106 of the total detections. There were 32 mammalian species identified, encompassing 17 families and 8 orders (Fig. 2, Supplemental Fig. 2). The most frequently observed orders were Artiodactyla (n = 1368 detections), Carnivora (n = 985), and Primates (n = 944). The 15 wild Carnivora species detected were: side-striped jackal (Lupulella adustus), pale fox, serval, caracal (Caracal caracal), African wildcat (Felis lybica lybica), spotted hyaena (Crocuta crocuta), striped hyaena (Hyaena hyaena), honey badger, African civet (Civettictis civetta), common genet (Genetta genetta), rusty-spotted genet (Genetta maculata), marsh mongoose (Atilax paludinosus), Egyptian mongoose, white-tailed mongoose (Ichneumia albicauda), and banded mongoose (Mungos mungo). The common genet and rusty-spotted genet are the two genet species whose known range falls in southern Chad. They can be differentiated by the pelage, shape and size of dorsal spots, and coloration of the tail tip (Angelici and Gaubert, 2013; Delibes and Gaubert, 2013). However, both species are nocturnal and were typically photographed too far from the camera to determine pelage characteristics and make a specific species identification. Both species were observed in the few close-up pictures captured but were included together for further analysis and are hereafter referred to as genets.

For the purposes of comparing across villages and endemicities, detections of domestic animals and wild mammal detections that were unable to be identified to a specific species (excluding the genets) were removed. The most widespread species detected in every village were side-striped jackal, white-tailed mongoose, African civets, and genets. The highest number of carnivore species a single village detected was 10, which was found in both Ngangoli Tounia (endemic) and Gentil (endemic). Gongo (sporadic) and Maicomb (sporadic) both recorded the least amount of unique carnivore species (five and six, respectively) and the least amount of total carnivore detections (29 and 12, respectively).

The negative village (Waltama) recorded the highest number of carnivore detections (n = 200). Endemic villages (Ngangoli Tounia, Tarakoh, and Gentil) recorded an average of 80.0 carnivore detections per village, while sporadic villages (Dilbodo, Maicomb, and Gongo) recorded an average of 81.7 carnivore detections per village. Most wild carnivore detections at the negative village were comprised of pale fox (49.0 %, 98/200) and spotted hyena (20.0 %, 40/200). In endemic villages, the carnivore species with the greatest number of detections were side-striped jackals (35.0 %, 84/240) and genets (17.1 %, 41/240). In sporadic villages, the carnivore species with the greatest number of detections were genets (35.1 %, 86/245) and pale fox (29.0 %, 71/245).

The three non-human primate species detected were: Tantalus monkeys, Patas monkeys, and Northern lesser galagos (Galago senegalensis). Waltama (negative) recorded no primate species. Tantalus monkeys were detected in all villages except for Waltama (negative), and most detections (96.6 %, 884/915) came from endemic villages. Additionally, there were over 150 detections of tantalus monkeys in every endemic village, with the majority of all tantalus detections occurring in Gentil (54.9 %, 502/915). Patas monkeys were only detected at one village, Gongo (sporadic). Northern lesser galagos had low detections (n = 15) but were seen in both endemic and sporadic villages.

Occupancy models

We were unable to fit occupancy models to all species detected. Side-striped jackals, white-tailed mongooses, genets, and civets were detected in all villages during the study period, so no occupancy model was needed. Additionally, occupancy models were not created for those species with either too limited detections during the study period (African wildcat, marsh mongoose, and banded mongoose), home ranges that were too large to establish spatial independence among villages (spotted hyena), or both (caracal, serval, honey badger, and striped hyena).

Occupancy models were fit for the following species: tantalus monkey, patas monkey, Egyptian mongoose, and pale fox (Table 3) and yielded variable results (Table 4). Patas monkey was the only species whose best-fit model did not include distance or landcover covariates. For the tantalus monkey, all distance covariates were included in the best-fit model. The probability of occupancy increased with increasing distance from the village center and increasing distance to path, while occupancy decreased with increasing distance to water. For the Egyptian mongoose, probability of occupancy increased with increasing distance from paths and decreased with increasing percent of water. For the pale fox, the probability of occupancy increased with increasing percent rangeland and increasing distance from water.

Climate, NDVI, and camera trapping effort (i.e., the number of daily survey occasions) were important detection covariates for all species. Increasing average monthly temperature (patas monkey and pale fox) and increasing maximum monthly temperature (tantalus monkey and Egyptian mongoose) had a positive effect on detection. Precipitation was only an important detection covariate for the pale fox and had a positive effect on detection. Increasing maximum NDVI had a positive effect on the detection of tantalus monkeys, while increasing minimum NDVI had a positive effect on the detection of patas monkeys and Egyptian mongooses. Increasing camera trapping effort had a negative effect on the detection of all species except for the patas monkey.

Discussion

The objective of this study was to determine wildlife occupancy surrounding villages in Chad with varying levels of GW transmission. While several wild primate and carnivore species are susceptible to GW infection and can act as definitive hosts, the importance of these species in the transmission pathway is unknown (Hopkins et al., 2018, 2021, 2023, 2024). Limited investigation into wildlife infections, as well as limited data on wildlife communities near GWD-endemic areas, complicate a full understanding of the transmission dynamics of this disease. Overall, endemic GWD villages recorded higher levels of mammalian diversity than the negative control village (Waltama). There were fewer species of mesopredators, specifically mongoose species, and no primate detections in Waltama. However, only a single control village was included in this study. While occupancy models could only be built for four species detected during this study, the potential importance of other widespread species should not be overlooked. Other species detected that could be exposed to GW infections due to their diet include genets, civets, marsh mongooses, side-striped jackals, African wildcats, servals, caracals, and honey badgers. Of these, African wildcats, African civets, and a serval are reported with emerged or un-emerged Guinea worms in Chad (Hopkins et al., 2018, 2021). Most of these species are dietary generalists with the ability to adapt to changes in resources. However, the marsh mongoose is more of a specialist species, feeding primarily on crustaceans, with the addition of amphibians, fish, mollusks, terrestrial arthropods, and rodents (Baker and Ray, 2013). The marsh mongooses’ aquatic dietary preference and riparian habitat needs may increase their risk of exposure.

Multiple experimental infection trials of primates (Leiper, 1907; Brug, 1930; Muller, 1972; Crichton and Beverley-Burton, 1976), along with natural infections in Ethiopian olive baboons, provide support that some non-human primate species are susceptible to Dracunculus infections. A grivet monkey (Chlorocebus aethiops), a relative of the tantalus monkey, and a patas monkey have both been successfully experimentally infected (Muller, 1972), but whether natural infections are possible remains unknown. It is likely Ethiopian olive baboons are infected via the traditional route of consumption of contaminated water instead of through paratenic hosts, as the diet of olive baboons is primarily vegetation and insects (Palombit, 2013) and the importance of the paratenic transmission pathway in Ethiopia compared to Chad remains debated (McDonald et al., 2020; Wilson-Aggarwal et al., 2020). Similarly, tantalus and patas monkeys are both generalist omnivores consuming primarily plant gums, flowers, and arthropods, supplemented by fruits, seeds, leaves, stems, reptiles, bird eggs, and young birds (Isbell, 2013; Nakagawa, 2013). If natural infections of tantalus or patas monkeys do occur in Chad, it is likely also through the traditional route of GW transmission, ingesting water containing copepods infected with third-stage GW larvae.

Each species chosen for occupancy modeling is described in detail in the following paragraphs. Within the scope of GW transmission risk, potentially significant covariates include increased occupancy closer to the nearest permanent water source (as species could be exposed more to contaminated water sources) and closer to the village center (as this could risk contamination of water sources more frequently used by villagers). Similarly, Eneanya et al. (2024), reported that proximity to permanent and inland water bodies was an important correlate for Guinea worm infection of dogs in Chad.

Tantalus monkey occupancy probability increased farther away from village centers and farther away from paths, which could indicate some avoidance to human activity and landscape disturbance. Occupancy probability increased closer to a permanent water source, which could place tantalus monkeys at an increased risk for GW exposure. Detection probability increased with increasing maximum monthly temperature and maximum NDVI, likely due to resource availability encouraging increased activity and movement. For example, tantalus monkeys were frequently observed foraging for fruit, a preferred energy source, in April which is the month with the hottest maximum temperature (Nakagawa, 2000). This can also help explain why tantalus monkey detection probability decreased as trapping length increased, as tantalus were more active during the beginning of the study period.

Patas monkey occupancy was not associated with any environmental variables included in our analysis. However, detection probability increased as average monthly temperature and minimum NDVI increased, like tantalus monkeys. This may also be due to increased resource availability causing an increase in activity and movement, as tantalus and patas monkeys have similar dietary preferences (Kavanagh, 1978; Nakagawa, 1989, 2000, 2003).

Egyptian mongooses were also only detected in endemic and sporadic villages, and it is unlikely they were present in the negative control. Occupancy probability was negatively affected by the percentage of water but positively affected by the distance to the nearest path. It is possible that increased occupancy nearest paths is inflated by the majority (80.6 %, 25/31) of Egyptian mongoose detections coming from one camera. Egyptian mongooses have a penchant to create and follow permanent paths (Ben-Yaacov and Yom-Tov, 1983), supported by personal observations of an Egyptian mongoose believed to be the same individual due to loss of part of the tail seen following the same trail in front of a camera multiple times. Detection probability increased as maximum monthly temperature and minimum NDVI increased, which may be reflective of their diet and increasing resource availability providing more prey options. Egyptian mongooses are opportunistic and omnivorous, with their diet changing based on season and location (Palomares, 1993). Typically, much of their diet is comprised of small mammals, amphibians, reptiles, and birds and supplemented by eggs, fish, crabs, crayfish, insects, carrion, fungi, and fruit (Stuart, 1983; Delibes et al., 1984; Palomares and Delibes, 1991a, 1991b; Palomares, 1993; Bandeira et al., 2018; Angelici, 2000). The suitability of Egyptian mongoose as a host for D. medinensis is unknown, but their carnivorous diet and presence in endemic villages suggests that they could be exposed to infected copepods.

Pale foxes were detected mostly in Waltama (negative) and Dilbodo (sporadic) with two detections in Gentil (endemic). Pale foxes are one of the least studied canids in the world (Sillero-Zubiri et al., 2004; Sillero-Zubiri, 2013). Most pale fox research comes from populations in the Termit and Tin Toumma National Nature and Cultural Reserve in eastern Niger, an environment of hot summers, mild winters and low annual precipitation described as being dominated by sand dunes with patches of grasses, shrubs, and trees (Sillero-Zubiri et al., 2016). While most of the pale fox distribution is in the semi-arid Sahel, they are known to occur into the moister parts of Guinean savannahs (Sillero-Zubiri et al., 2004; Sillero-Zubiri and Wacher, 2012; Sillero-Zubiri, 2013). The previous southernmost records in Chad come from Zakouma National Park, which is located approximately 240 km northeast of our study. Occupancy probability increased with increasing percentage of rangeland and with increasing distance from the nearest permanent water source, which may be indicative of the pale foxes’ adaptation to a more arid environment. Additionally, this could result in less risk of exposure to GWD. The limited data on pale foxes, combined with the fact that previous research comes from their primary arid habitat, makes it difficult to determine the niche of pale foxes in our study area. Previous research indicates they are likely primarily insectivorous, but avians, rodents, and squamates may also be important dietary components (Sillero-Zubiri, 2013; Burruss et al., 2017). Of important consideration is that many detections of pale foxes in Waltama came from a camera located above their burrows (55.1 %, 54/98), which may have biased their relative activity. Detection probability increased with increasing average monthly temperature and increased precipitation. Pale foxes may be more active with increased precipitation if they are taking advantage of increased insect activity and food resources.

There are several important limitations of this study. Firstly, only one GW negative village was included. This negative village also reported lower domestic dog populations during the 2018 census (Supplemental Table 1). However, there was only one village with no GW reports during the surveillance period that also met the other selection criteria (i.e., one negative village that was accessible and spatially independent from the other villages). Additionally, while village accessibility was an important criterion for inclusion in this study due to the logistics of accessing the village during the rainy season for camera maintenance, it is possible other factors associated with more accessible villages, such as increased human populations, increased farming and livestock, and increased habitat fragmentation, may result in less wildlife surrounding these villages. An important limitation within the scope of GW transmission is that it is possible that distance to the nearest water source may have a more significant effect on occupancy but may not have been able to be accounted for accurately due to seasonal flooding creating temporary water sources. Future research could look more into measuring seasonal flooding in relation to wildlife occupancy. Another limitation is that home range sizes used for landcover and NDVI analyses were based on the best available data from published literature, but there may be differences unique to the Chadian landscape and habitats for each species in this region. Home range estimates for both patas and tantalus monkeys were taken from Nakagawa (1999), as their study site (a seasonally flooded Sahelo-Sudanian prairie beside the Chari River in Northern Cameroon) seemed to resemble ours most similarly. However, patas monkey home range size has been recorded to be large in Laikipia, Kenya, ranging from 23.4 km2 to 32 km2 and increasing with group size (Chism and Rowell, 1988). If patas monkeys in our area do have this large of a home range, it would violate the spatial independence assumption, though patas monkeys were only detected in one village (Gongo). Egyptian mongooses are understudied in Africa, so the home range estimate used in this study is of males (slightly larger than females) from populations in SW Spain (Palomares, 1994). As stated previously, pale foxes are understudied and the only home range estimate comes from populations in Termit and Tin Toumma National Nature and Cultural Reserve, which may or may not be similar in our study area. Furthermore, home ranges around each camera were combined to create village-level landcover statistics. However, future work could investigate camera-level statistics to potentially provide more insight into species-specific habitat utilization and activity.

Conclusions

By working within local communities, providing health education and resources, ensuring safe drinking water, and establishing numerous other interventions (e.g., proactive tethering of dogs, burning/burying aquatic animal waste, filtering water) the global Guinea Worm Eradication Program has been highly successful in reducing human dracunculiasis cases globally. Infections in domestic dogs and cats in Chad, combined with sporadic detections in wildlife, pose a new challenge for the Chadian Guinea worm eradication program. Recent epidemiological modeling has highlighted several important environmental and anthropogenic variables associated with infections (Richards et al., 2020). Strengthening health education for villagers on human and animal infections, including prevention and containment strategies, alongside the implementation of novel diagnostic tools and treatment options, is essential. Additionally, incorporating knowledge of geographic, topographic, and environmental predictors of infections further enhances disease management efforts.

This study aimed to provide baseline information on wildlife species present near villages around Sarh, Chad, a hotspot for GW transmission. A higher biodiversity of wildlife was detected in GWD endemic villages, specifically for primate and mesopredator species. While it is unknown if tantalus and patas monkeys can serve as hosts for GW in the wild, similar species have shown susceptibility to infection in experimental trials (Leiper, 1907; Muller, 1972). Similarly, little is known on whether herpestids can become infected with GW, but dietary preferences and confirmed presence around endemic villages suggest the Egyptian mongoose could be exposed to infection. However, the role of wildlife in sustaining transmission of GW once human, domestic dog, and cat infections are eliminated remains unknown. Ultimately, increasing knowledge of wildlife as potential alternative hosts will provide a broader perspective into the management of D. medinensis.
