The 2D Lorentz-violating fermionic Casimir effect under thermal conditions
1 K. E. L. de Farias, 1,3 M. A. Anacleto, 2 Iver Brevik, 1,3 F. A. Brito, 1,3 E. Passos, 1 Amilcar Queiroz, 1,3 João R. L. Santos

Abstract
In the present work, we explore the dimensional projection method applied to a fermionic Lorentz invariance violation (LIV) theory with the CPT-even dimension-six extension. Due to the dimensional reduction of a fermionic system from 4 ​ D 4D to 2 ​ D 2D , it is possible to study the influence of LIV on the Casimir effect under the MIT bag boundary condition model in a low-dimensional setting, where results are obtained without any approximations for a null-temperature system. Moreover, the Matsubara formalism is applied to derive closed expressions for the influence of temperature on the physical observables: Casimir energy, Casimir force, and entropy associated with the system in a LIV context. For each thermal observable, the influence of the LIV correction term is considered in the analysis of both low- and high-temperature regimes.

II
The 4D to 2D LIV-Fermion thories

Let us now consider the dimensional projection approach in the Dirac sector extended with the CPT-even dimension-six LIV term. According to this, the associated fermionic Lagrangian density is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{4D}_{ferm}=i\bar{\psi}\partial_{\mu}\Gamma^{\mu}\psi-i\bar{\alpha}\bar{\psi}(u\cdot\partial)^{3}u_{\mu}\Gamma^{\mu}\psi,

(1)

where
\bar{\alpha}=\alpha/M^{2}_{\rm Pl}
, with
M^{2}_{\rm Pl}
being the Planck mass of quantum gravity and
\alpha
is a dimensionless LIV parameter responsible for introducing the high-derivative term. When the LIV parameter
\alpha
is submitted to the limit
\alpha\to 0
, any LIV effect vanishes and returns to the usual theory.

II.1
The 4D-2D Projection Approach

We consider the following representation of the gamma matrices in the four-dimension:

\displaystyle\Gamma^{0}=\begin{pmatrix}0\,&\mathbb{I}\\
\mathbb{I}\,&0\end{pmatrix},\;\;\Gamma^{\mu}=\begin{pmatrix}0\,&\sigma^{\mu}\\
\bar{\sigma}^{\mu}&0\end{pmatrix},\;\;\sigma^{\mu}=(1,\vec{\sigma}),\;\;\bar{\sigma}^{\mu}=(1,-\vec{\sigma})\,,

(2)

with the spinors

\displaystyle\psi=\begin{pmatrix}\psi_{L}\\
\psi_{R}\end{pmatrix},\;\;\bar{\psi}=\psi^{\dagger}\Gamma^{0}.

(3)

Therefore, the Lagrangian density, Eq.(
1
), is rewritten in the following matrix representation

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{4D}_{ferm}=\begin{pmatrix}\psi^{\dagger}_{L}&\psi^{\dagger}_{R}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}0&i\partial_{\mu}{\sigma}^{\mu}-i\bar{\alpha}(u\cdot\partial)^{3}n_{\mu}{\sigma}^{\mu}\\
i\partial_{\mu}\bar{\sigma}^{\mu}-i\bar{\alpha}(u\cdot\partial)^{3}n_{\mu}\bar{\sigma}^{\mu}&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\psi_{L}\\
\psi_{R}\end{pmatrix}.

(4)

Note that the sectors
\psi_{L}
and
\psi_{R}
are naturally uncoupled (in the massless regime). Namely, the sector
\psi_{L}
can be written as

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{ferm}^{4D}=i\psi^{\dagger}_{L}\partial_{\mu}{\sigma}^{\mu}\psi_{L}-i\bar{\alpha}\psi^{\dagger}_{L}(u\cdot\partial)^{3}n_{\mu}{\sigma}^{\mu}\psi_{L}.

(5)

Now we proceed with the dimensional projection which we reduce the terms of the 4D fermionic Lagrangian density to plane
x_{0}-x_{3}
. Firstly, the gamma matrices in 2D are defined as:

\displaystyle\sigma^{3}=\begin{pmatrix}1\,&\,0\\
0\,&-1\end{pmatrix},\;\gamma^{0}=\sigma^{1}=\begin{pmatrix}0\,&1\\
1\,&0\end{pmatrix},\;\gamma^{1}=-i\sigma^{2}=\begin{pmatrix}0\,&-1\\
1\,&\;\;0\end{pmatrix}.

(6)

Let us note that
\gamma^{1}

is just the matrix associated with the spatial direction in two dimensions, as we chose this direction as
x_{3}
, it is convenient to rename the matrix as
\gamma^{3}
. The reason for this choice of gamma representation can be better understood by imposing the conditions
\partial_{1}=\partial_{2}=0
, yielding to

\displaystyle\sigma^{\mu}\partial_{\mu}=\gamma^{0}\gamma^{0}\partial_{0}+\gamma^{0}\gamma^{3}\partial_{3},

\displaystyle u^{\mu}\partial_{\mu}=u^{0}\partial_{0}+u^{3}\partial_{3}\,,

(7)

which has the necessary structure to build the kinetic fermions terms in 2D. Therefore, We obtain that the 4D fermionic Lagrangian density, Eq.(
5
), when projected to plane
x_{0}-x_{3}
, reduces to

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{2D}_{ferm}=i\psi^{\dagger}_{R}\partial_{i}{\gamma}^{i}\psi_{R}-i\bar{\alpha}\psi^{\dagger}_{R}(u^{i}\partial_{i})^{3}n_{i}{\gamma}^{i}\psi_{R}\,,

(8)

corresponding to a 2D fermionic theory extended from a CPT-even dimension-four LIV operator.

II.2
The 2D massive-fermionic model

In order to study some applications in the next section, for example, the Casimir effect, we shall consider the massive version of the massless 2D fermionic higher-derivative sector whose Lagrangian density is

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{2D}_{ferm}=\bar{\psi}\big(i\partial\!\!\!/-i\bar{\alpha}(u\cdot\partial)^{3}u\!\!\!/-m_{f}\big)\psi,

(9)

where
m_{f}
is the mass of a simple fermion. Let us now derive the dispersion relation associated with the above Lagrangian density. The equation of motion takes the form

\displaystyle\big(i\partial\!\!\!/-i\bar{\alpha}(u\cdot\partial)^{3}u\!\!\!/-m_{f}\big)\psi=0.

(10)

After a straightforward algebra, we find that the free continuous spectrum is governed by the following modified dispersion relation:

\displaystyle k^{2}+2\bar{\alpha}(u\cdot k)^{4}+\bar{\alpha}^{2}(u\cdot k)^{6}u^{2}-m^{2}_{f}=0.

(11)

The set of equations associated with the time-like and the space-like
configurations are respectively given by

\displaystyle\omega^{2}-k_{z}^{2}+2\bar{\alpha}\omega^{4}+\bar{\alpha}^{2}\omega^{6}-m^{2}_{f}=0,\;\;\Leftrightarrow\;\;u_{\mu}=(1,\vec{0})

(12a)

\displaystyle\omega^{2}-k_{z}^{2}+2\bar{\alpha}k_{z}^{4}-\bar{\alpha}^{2}k_{z}^{6}-m^{2}_{f}=0.\;\;\Leftrightarrow\;\;u_{\mu}=(0,\vec{1})

(12b)

Considering first the time-like case. Note that the dispersion relation associated with this configuration is not trivial, and gives us a set of solutions:

\displaystyle\omega=

\displaystyle-\left(\frac{2}{3}\right)^{1/3}\frac{1}{\left(9u\bar{\alpha}^{2}+\sqrt{3}\sqrt{4\bar{\alpha}^{3}+27u^{2}\bar{\alpha}^{4}}\right)^{1/3}}+\frac{\left(9u\bar{\alpha}^{2}+\sqrt{3}\sqrt{4\bar{\alpha}^{3}+27u^{2}\bar{\alpha}^{4}}\right)^{1/3}}{2^{1/3}3^{2/3}\bar{\alpha}}

(13)

\displaystyle\omega=

\displaystyle\frac{1\pm i\sqrt{3}}{2^{2/3}3^{1/3}\left(9u\bar{\alpha}^{2}+\sqrt{3}\sqrt{4\bar{\alpha}^{3}+27u^{2}\bar{\alpha}^{4}}\right)^{1/3}}-\frac{(1\mp i\sqrt{3})\left(9u\bar{\alpha}^{2}+\sqrt{3}\sqrt{4\bar{\alpha}^{3}+27u^{2}\bar{\alpha}^{4}}\right)^{1/3}}{12^{2/3}\bar{\alpha}}

(14)

where
u=\sqrt{k_{z}^{2}+m_{f}^{2}}
. The previous expressions unveil that only Eq. (
13
) is a real solution. However, even this real solution presents some problems since it diverges as

, and we do not recover the usual result as expected. As our aim needs a positive dispersion relation, we must discard this option and analyze the regime where the dispersion relation is positive. Hence, let us consider redefine

y=\left(9u\bar{\alpha}^{2}+\sqrt{3}\sqrt{4\bar{\alpha}^{3}+27u^{2}\bar{\alpha}^{4}}\right)^{1/3}\,,

(15)

and to set the constraint

-\left(\frac{2}{3}\right)^{1/3}\frac{1}{y}+\frac{y}{2^{1/3}3^{2/3}\bar{\alpha}}>0.

(16)

The above constraint leads us to a transcendental inequality. The consequence of this is that the momentum is dependent on
\bar{\alpha}
, then the momentum
k_{z}
has an upper limit, limiting its allowed values. In fact, the factor
\bar{\alpha}
should be small due to the LIV effect. Namely, the behavior of the dispersion relation, Eq. (

\omega
when the coupling parameter is small, we can expand Eq. (

y=\sqrt{\alpha}(2\sqrt{3})^{1/3}\left(1+\frac{3u}{2\sqrt{3}}\sqrt{\alpha}\right).

(17)

In this case, according to the (

), we can rewrite the expression in Eq. (

\omega=\frac{1}{\sqrt{3\alpha}}\left[\left(-1+\frac{\sqrt{3}u}{2}\sqrt{\alpha}\right)+\left(1+\frac{\sqrt{3}u}{2}\sqrt{\alpha}\right)\right]\,,

(18)

such as

\omega(k_{z})=u=\sqrt{k_{z}^{2}+m_{f}^{2}}.

(19)

Therefore, it is a behaviour that the LIV does not exert any influence on. From Eq. (

\displaystyle\omega(k_{z})=\pm\sqrt{k_{z}^{2}\left(1-\bar{\alpha}k_{z}^{2}\right)^{2}+m_{f}^{2}}.

Notice that the above solutions correctly reproduce the usual dispersion relations in the limit

III
The Casimir energy

The vacuum energy fluctuations arise from the instantaneous creation and annihilation of particles making a quick change in the energy. This energy may be manifested as the Casimir energy, which can be obtained by the modified dispersion found early and submitted to a boundary condition. To determine this energy in the proposed system, we have two alternatives based on the dispersion relations previously obtained. The first is the real solution of the timelike dispersion relation shown in (
13
) which is the real result from the dispersion relation. However, it seems impossible to work out for two reasons. The first problem is the complexity of the form found, and the second point is the problem of the negative dispersion relation, as shown before. Moreover, it presents a divergence in the limit
\bar{\alpha}\to 0
. Another alternative is the space-like approach, see Eq. (
20
), which gives us a good behaviour function with non-negative values.

Hence, by taking the MIT bag boundary condition in the
z
-direction, the momentum component becomes discrete, with
k_{n}=\left(n+\frac{1}{2}\right)\frac{\pi}{a}
,
n=0,1,2,\ldots
. Consequently, the vacuum energy may be derived by using:

\displaystyle E_{0}(a)=\hslash c\sum_{n}\omega_{n},

(22)

which means the sum of all normal modes of a wave oscillation. The fluctuation energy under a MIT bag boundary condition is written as

\displaystyle E_{0}(a)

\displaystyle=\hslash c\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sqrt{\left(n+\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2}\left(\frac{\pi}{a}\right)^{2}\left[1-\bar{\alpha}\left(n+\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2}\left(\frac{\pi}{a}\right)^{2}\right]^{2}+m_{f}^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\hslash c}{\bar{\alpha}^{1/2}}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sqrt{\bar{g}^{2}\left(n+\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2}\left(1-\bar{g}^{2}\left(n+\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2}\right)^{2}+M^{2}},

(23)

where
\bar{g}^{2}=\bar{\alpha}\frac{\pi^{2}}{a^{2}}
and
M^{2}=\bar{\alpha}m^{2}_{f}
.

We can verify that the sum present in the above expression is divergent. To avoid such a divergence, we have to use a regularization method to separate the divergent part from the finite energy term originating from the Casimir energy. Here we use the Abel-Plana formula, but first, the previous sum should be rewritten as

f(n)=\sqrt{\bar{g}^{2}n^{2}\left[1-\bar{g}n^{2}\right]^{2}+m_{f}^{2}}\,.

(24)

Some consideration should be taken before the calculus. Although the fermion mass appears in Eq. (
24
), we are going to work with the non-massive case,
m_{f}\to 0
. Then, the Abel-Plana formula to a integer sum is given by

\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}f(k+1/2)=\int_{0}^{\infty}dxf(x)-i\int_{0}^{\infty}dx\frac{f(ix)-f(-ix)}{e^{2\pi x}+1}\,.

(25)

The advantage of using this method is that the divergent part is presented in the first integral in the r.h.s., which is discarded by the renormalization process. Therefore, the normalized Casimir energy is found by performing the last integral of the r.h.s, which gives us the following result

\displaystyle E_{0}^{\rm ren}(a)

\displaystyle=-\frac{i\hslash c}{\bar{\alpha}^{1/2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{dx}{e^{2\pi x}+1}\left\{\sqrt{\bar{g}^{2}(ix)^{2}\left[1-\bar{g}^{2}(ix)^{2}\right]^{2}}-\sqrt{\bar{g}^{2}(-ix)^{2}\left[1-\bar{g}^{2}(-ix)^{2}\right]^{2}}\right\}

\displaystyle=-\frac{2\hslash c}{\bar{\alpha}^{1/2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{dx}{e^{2\pi x}+1}\sqrt{\bar{g}^{2}x^{2}\left[1+\bar{g}^{2}x^{2}\right]^{2}}=-\frac{\pi\hbar c}{48a}-\frac{7\pi^{3}\bar{\alpha}\hbar c}{1920a^{3}}.

(26)

It is straightforward to note that in the limit
\bar{\alpha}\to 0
, the LIV contribution vanishes, and the first term is the usual result of a unidimensional system. Notice also that the LIV contribution is proportional to
\mathcal{O}(3)
in the distance, while the usual term has
\mathcal{O}(1)
. This result unveils that at a large distance, the usual parts of the Casimir energy dominate, while in the short distance, the LIV term dominates, allowing for finding a signal of the LIV presence in an experiment. Moreover, the energy diverges as
a\to 0
as expected.

The presence of a boundary condition in the system leads us to a Casimir energy density, which is a finite energy present between the plates of the system. However, the energy cannot be measured in the laboratory. Nevertheless, this energy acts on the plates causing a force, which may be attractive or repulsive depending on the boundary condition to which the system is exposed. This force is found by taking the negative derivative of the energy in terms of the distance, i.e.:

F_{c}=-\frac{dE}{da}=-\frac{\pi\hslash c}{48a^{2}}-\frac{7\pi^{3}\bar{\alpha}\hslash c}{640a^{4}}.

(27)

Note that the force is negative, which leads us to an attractive force between the points. It is worth highlighting that both Casimir’s energy density and Casimir’s force present a linear behaviour in
\bar{\alpha}
, and the expression obtained does not need any approximation, which is usually necessary in theories with higher order derivative terms. Furthermore, the behaviour of the force is similar to the energy density, where the force dissipates when
a\to\infty
and diverges to
a\to 0
, but with greater intensity, once the order of the factors is increased in one. For both the energy and Casimir force cases, the LIV correction term contributes to the increase in intensity.

IV
Finite temperature

In this section, we analyze the effect of the temperature on the system. There are many ways to introduce temperature into a model, and here, we used the Matsubara formalism. In this formalism, we should use a Euclidean field theory, which is obtained by a Wick rotation in the time coordinate,
t\rightarrow-i\tau
, consequently, the Euclidean time
\tau
is confined to the interval
\tau\in[0,\beta]
, where
\beta=(k_{B}T)^{-1}
, being
k_{B}
the Boltzmann constant. The partition function in the path integral representation becomes:

Z=\quad\int\quad D\bar{\psi}D\psi\exp\left(-\int_{0}^{\beta}d\tau\int d^{3}x\mathcal{L}_{E}\right).

(28)

As we consider a free fermion field, the partition function is simplified as

Z=\operatorname{det}\left(\gamma_{E}^{\mu}\partial_{\mu E}+m\right)\,.

(29)

Hence, by using the partition function above, we may find the free energy given by the following expression:

\mathcal{F}=-k_{B}T\ln\left[\operatorname{det}\left(\gamma_{E}^{\mu}\partial_{\mu E}+m\right)\right]=-k_{B}T\operatorname{Tr}\left[\ln\left(P_{E}^{2}+m^{2}\right)\right],

(30)

where
P_{E}^{2}
is the Dirac operator.

The following expression gives the finite temperature Casimir effect:

E_{\rm cas}(a,T)=\mathcal{F}(a,T)-\mathcal{F}(T),

(31)

where
\mathcal{F}(a,T)
is the free energy of the system, and

\mathcal{F}(T)=E_{0}+f(T),

(32)

the first term of r.h.s. is the Minkowski divergent term at null temperature and the second one is the radiation term. The free energy is given by

\mathcal{F}(T,a)=-\frac{1}{\beta}\sum_{l=-\infty}^{\infty\prime}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\ln\left(\xi_{l}^{2}+\omega_{n}^{2}\right)\text{, }

(33)

where the prime sign means the odd values of
\ell
, i.e.,

\xi_{l}=\frac{\pi}{\beta}\ell,\quad\ell=\pm 1,\pm 3,\pm 5,\ldots\,.

(34)

We can see that
\xi_{\ell}
are the Matsubara frequencies for a fermion system. The discrete values come from the anti-periodic boundary condition applied to the
\tau
. Therefore, the free energy may be rewritten as

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(T,a)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\beta}\sum_{l=-\infty}^{\infty\prime}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\ln\left(\xi_{l}^{2}+\omega_{n}^{2}\right)=\frac{1}{\beta}\lim_{s\rightarrow 0}\frac{\partial}{\partial s}\frac{1}{\Gamma(s)}\left\{\sum_{l=-\infty}^{\infty\prime}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}dte^{-t\left(\xi_{l}^{2}+\omega_{n}^{2}\right)}t^{s-1}\right\}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\beta}\lim_{s\rightarrow 0}\frac{\partial}{\partial s}\frac{1}{\Gamma(s)}\sum_{l=-\infty}^{\infty\prime}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}dt\ t^{s-1}e^{-t\left[\frac{\pi^{2}}{\beta^{2}}(2l+1)^{2}+\omega_{n}^{2}\right]}.

(35)

Observe that the sum over
\ell
is not trivial. Before any calculus, we should rearrange the sum by considering the recurrence relation:

\sum_{l=-\infty}^{\infty\prime}e^{-t\left(\frac{\ell\pi}{\beta}\right)^{2}}=\frac{\beta}{2\sqrt{\pi t}}+\frac{\beta}{\sqrt{\pi t}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{\ell}e^{-\frac{\ell^{2}\beta^{2}}{4t}}.

(36)

Consequently, Eq. (
35
) becomes

\mathcal{F}(T,a)=\frac{1}{\beta}\lim_{s\rightarrow 0}\frac{\partial}{\partial s}\frac{1}{\Gamma(s)}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}dtt^{s-1}\left\{\frac{\beta}{2\sqrt{\pi t}}+\frac{\beta}{\sqrt{\pi t}}\sum_{l=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{\ell}e^{-\ell^{2}\beta^{2}/4t}\right\}e^{-t\omega_{n}^{2}}

(37)

It may be rewritten, separating the terms in the following form:

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(T,a)=\lim_{s\rightarrow 0}\frac{\partial}{\partial s}\frac{1}{\Gamma(s)}\int_{0}^{\infty}dt\frac{t^{s-1}}{\sqrt{\pi t}}\left\{\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}e^{-t\omega_{n}^{2}}+\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{\ell}e^{-\frac{\ell^{2}\beta^{2}}{4t}}+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{\ell}e^{-\frac{\ell^{2}\beta^{2}}{4t}-t\omega_{n}^{2}}\right\}

(38)

The first term in parentheses represents the result that is obtained when we consider the Minkowski spacetime, which gives us a divergent result, which will be removed at the given moment. Note that we can compact it as contribution
n=0
in the sum present in the second term in parentheses, and then we perform the integral, where we get

\lim_{s\rightarrow 0}\frac{\partial}{\partial s}\frac{1}{\Gamma(s)}\int_{0}^{\infty}dt\frac{t^{s-1}}{\sqrt{\pi t}}\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}e^{-t\omega_{n}^{2}}=-\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\omega_{n}.

(39)

It is clear that this sum is the negative part of Eq. (
26
) where it has already been performed in the last section to get the renormalized part, i.e.,
E^{\rm ren}_{0}(a)=E_{0}(a)-E_{0}
. The next one is given by:

\lim_{s\rightarrow 0}\frac{\partial}{\partial s}\frac{1}{\Gamma(s)}\int_{0}^{\infty}dt\frac{t^{s-1}}{\sqrt{\pi t}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{\ell}e^{-\ell^{2}\beta^{2}/4t}\,,

(40)

it is obtained when
n=0
from the last terms in the bracket, and leads us to the black body radiation term
f(T)
.

The last one is the contribution of the temperature and the boundary condition at the same time. It is expressed by

\displaystyle\lim_{s\rightarrow 0}\frac{1}{\Gamma(s)}\int_{0}^{\infty}dt\frac{t^{s-1}}{\sqrt{\pi t}}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{\ell}e^{-t\omega_{n}^{2}-\ell^{2}\beta^{2}/4t}

\displaystyle=\lim_{s\rightarrow 0}\frac{\partial}{\partial s}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{\ell}2^{\frac{3}{2}-s}}{\sqrt{\pi}}\left(\frac{\omega_{n}^{2}}{\ell^{2}\beta^{2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{4}-\frac{s}{2}}\frac{K_{\frac{1}{2}-s}\left(2\ell\beta\sqrt{\omega_{n}^{2}}\right)}{\Gamma(s)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\beta}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{\ell}\frac{e^{-2\ell\beta\omega_{n}}}{\ell}\,.

(41)

Finally, substituting Eq. (
38
) and its respective results given by Eqs. (
39
)-(
41
) in Eq. (
31
), we obtained the renormalized Casimir energy associated to the

\displaystyle E_{\rm cas}(T,a)=\frac{\pi\hslash c}{48a}+\frac{7\pi^{3}\bar{\alpha}\hslash c}{1920a^{3}}+\frac{1}{2\beta}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{\ell}\frac{e^{-2\ell\beta\omega_{n}}}{\ell}.

(42)

Note that the first terms are independent of the temperature parameter, then in the analysis of temperature behaviour, these terms play the role of a constant

IV.1
Low temperature limit

As mentioned before, the order in which the sums are performed shows us an expression that may be used to find the low-temperature behaviour,
T\to 0(\beta\to\infty)
. Therefore, if the sum in
n
is first performed, it does not give an analytic result. Then, we must consider some approximation since the LIV parameter
\bar{\alpha}
is very small. It is worth highlighting that the null temperature term may be removed since it is a constant in this limit. Then by expanding Eq. (
42
) in respect to
\bar{\alpha}
, we get

\displaystyle E_{\rm cas}^{\rm low}(T,a)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\beta}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{\ell}\frac{e^{-2\ell\beta\omega_{n}}}{\ell}

\displaystyle\approx\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{\ell}}{\beta}\left[\frac{\pi e^{-2\hslash c\beta l\left(n+\frac{1}{2}\right)\frac{\pi}{a}}}{l}+\frac{2\pi^{3}\bar{\alpha}\hslash c\beta\left(n+\frac{1}{2}\right)^{3}e^{-2\hslash c\beta l\left(n+\frac{1}{2}\right)\frac{\pi}{a}}}{a^{3}}\right].

(43)

Performing the sum in
n
, we obtain

\displaystyle E_{\rm cas}^{\rm low}(T,a)=\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\frac{\pi(-1)^{l}e^{-l\pi\hslash c\beta/a}}{\beta}\left[\frac{1}{l\left(e^{2l\pi\hslash c\beta/a}-1\right)}+\frac{\pi^{2}\bar{\alpha}\hslash c\beta\left(5e^{2l\pi\hslash c\beta/a}+17e^{4l\pi\hslash c\beta/a}+27e^{6l\pi\hslash c\beta/a}-1\right)}{4a^{3}\left(e^{2l\pi\hslash c\beta/a}-1\right)^{4}}\right].

(44)

In the low-temperature limit, we consider
T\to 0\ (\beta\to\infty)
, then, the above expression becomes:

\displaystyle E_{\rm cas}^{\rm low}(T,a)\approx\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\frac{\pi(-1)^{l}e^{-l\pi\hslash c\beta/a}}{\beta}\left[\frac{1}{le^{2l\pi\hslash c\beta/a}}+\frac{27\pi^{2}\bar{\alpha}\hslash c\beta e^{-2l\pi\hslash c\beta/a}}{4a^{3}}\right].

(45)

Finally, the sum in
\ell
may be performed and provides us with the following expression

\displaystyle E_{\rm cas}^{\rm low}(T,a)=-\frac{\pi\log\left(e^{-3\pi\hslash c\beta/a}+1\right)}{\beta}-\frac{27\hslash c\pi^{3}\bar{\alpha}}{4a^{3}\left(e^{3\pi\hslash c\beta/a}+1\right)}.

(46)

By taking the limit
T\to 0
, the thermal contribution vanishes as expected. This behaviour may be observed clearly in Fig.
1
.

It is straightforward to note from Fig.

that the Casimir energy at low temperatures is extremely sensitive to the increase in the LIV parameter
\alpha
. In addition, the behaviour of the Casimir energy with LIV presence and without LIV remains similar, but the LIV parameter modifies the intensities of each energy. It is worth highlighting that the energy from the vacuum fluctuation cannot increase infinitely as
T\to\infty
. This behaviour is a consequence of the approximation used in the low-temperature regime. Consequently, it should present an upper limit after a determined temperature.

The force may be obtained from the energy equation. The derivative in terms of
a
from the expression without approximation gives us a long equation with many terms of each order in the exponential function. Therefore, we are going to focus on a simplified version of a low-temperature approximation, whose equation is

F(T,a)=-\frac{3\hbar c\pi^{2}}{a^{2}\left(1+e^{\frac{3\hbar c\pi\beta}{a}}\right)^{2}}\left[1-\frac{27\pi\bar{\alpha}}{4a^{2}}+e^{\frac{3\hbar c\pi\beta}{a}}\left(1-\frac{27\pi\bar{\alpha}}{4a^{2}}+\frac{27\hbar c\pi^{2}\alpha\beta}{4a^{3}}\right)\right].

(47)

As expected, in the limit
T\to 0
, this expression vanishes completely. Moreover, the expression obtained is inadequate to obtain a high-temperature limit since it diverges as
T\to\infty
. The behaviour of the Casimir force is shown in Fig.

\bar{\alpha}/a^{2}
. Furthermore, the increase in the LIV parameter causes the temperature required for a transition from attractive to repulsive behavior to be lower.

IV.2
High Temperature Limit

The high-temperature limit is obtained by taking the sum over
\ell
in a regime where
T\to\infty
, which corresponds to
\beta\to 0
. By following the previous procedures, we should remove the null temperature term since it does not contribute to the thermal behaviour. Hence, from Eq. (
42
), the sum over
\ell
results in

\displaystyle E_{\rm cas}(T,a)=-\frac{1}{\beta}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\ln(1+e^{-2\beta\omega_{n}})\,.

(48)

This result is well-known in the literature as the closed form of fermion in finite temperature. However, the sum over
n
of the above equation is divergent, consequently, we must use the approximation
\ln(1+z)=\approx z
since
e^{-2\beta\omega_{n}}<1
, then

\displaystyle E_{\rm cas}^{\rm high}(T,a)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\beta}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\ln\left(1+e^{-2\beta\omega_{n}}\right)

\displaystyle\approx-\frac{1}{\beta}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}e^{-2\beta\omega_{n}}\,.

(49)

The renormalized part of the energy given by the above sum may be obtained by taking the Abel-Plana formula (
25
), consequently we get

E_{\rm ren}^{\rm high}(T,a)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{i}{\beta}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{dt}{e^{2\pi t+1}}\left\{e^{-2\beta\omega_{n+}}-e^{-2\beta\omega_{n-}}\right\},

(50)

where

\omega_{i\pm}=\pm i\frac{t\hslash c\pi}{a}\left[1+\bar{\alpha}t^{2}\left(\frac{\pi}{a}\right)^{2}\right].

(51)

Therefore, the Casimir energy density takes the following form:

\displaystyle E_{\rm ren}^{\rm high}(T,a)=

\displaystyle-\frac{i}{\beta}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{dt}{e^{2\pi t}+1}2i\sin\left\{\frac{2\hslash c\beta t\pi}{a}\left[1+\bar{\alpha}t^{2}\left(\frac{\pi}{a}\right)^{2}\right]\right\}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\beta}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{dt}{e^{2\pi t}+1}\left\{\sin\left(\frac{2\hslash c\beta t\pi}{a}\right)\cos\left[2\hslash c\beta\bar{\alpha}\left(\frac{t\pi}{a}\right)^{3}\right]+\cos\left(\frac{2\hslash c\beta t\pi}{a}\right)\sin\left[2\hslash c\beta\bar{\alpha}\left(\frac{t\pi}{a}\right)^{3}\right]\right\}.

(52)

As the LIV constant
\bar{\alpha}
is much smaller than the unit, i.e.,
\bar{\alpha}\ll 1
, we may consider an approximation and Eq. (
52
) is rewritten as

E_{\rm ren}^{\rm high}(T,a)=\frac{2}{\beta}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{dt}{e^{2\pi t}+1}\left\{\sin\left(\frac{2\hslash c\beta t\pi}{a}\right)+2\hslash c\beta\bar{\alpha}\left(\frac{t\pi}{a}\right)^{3}\cos\left(\frac{2\hslash c\beta t\pi}{a}\right)\right\}.

(53)

Finally, by performing the integrals, we find an analytic expression which describes the renormalized Casimir energy in a high-temperature regime:

\displaystyle E_{\rm cas}^{\rm high}(T,a)=\frac{a}{2\pi\hslash c\beta^{2}}+\frac{3a\bar{\alpha}}{4\pi(\hslash c)^{3}\beta^{4}}-\frac{\text{csch}\left(\frac{\pi\hslash c\beta}{a}\right)}{2\beta}-\frac{\pi^{3}\hslash c\bar{\alpha}\left(23\cosh\left(\frac{\pi\hslash c\beta}{a}\right)+\cosh\left(\frac{3\pi\hslash c\beta}{a}\right)\right)\text{csch}^{4}\left(\frac{\pi\hslash c\beta}{a}\right)}{32a^{3}}\,.

(54)

In a high-temperature regime, we should use the expansion to small arguments in the hyperbolic function, which gives us

E_{\rm cas}^{\rm high}(T,a)=\frac{\pi\hslash c}{12a}+\frac{7\pi^{3}\hslash c\bar{\alpha}}{480a^{3}}-\frac{(\hslash c)^{3}\beta^{2}}{a^{3}}\left(\frac{7\pi^{3}}{720}+\frac{31\pi^{5}\bar{\alpha}}{2016a^{2}}\right)\,.

(55)

V
Entropy

Since we have the thermal Casimir energy, obtaining the Casimir entropy of the system is possible by taking the derivative in terms of the temperature as follows

S=-\frac{\partial E}{\partial T}.

(57)

Note that any null temperature contribution vanishes due to the derivative; then, we only consider terms which are temperature dependent. However, from Eq. (
42
), we will get a divergent value when applying the sums after the derivations. An alternative is getting approximations to the temperature limit those made in the determination of the energy. Although it works, the calculus may be exhaustive and repetitive, consequently, we can apply the derivative in the energy at low- and high-temperature limits. Hence, we start first with the low-temperature limit, which is expressed as

S_{\rm low}=\pi k_{\rm B}\ln\left(e^{-\frac{3\pi\beta\hslash c}{a}}+1\right)+\frac{3\pi^{2}\beta\hslash ck_{\rm B}}{a\left(e^{\frac{3\pi\beta\hslash c}{a}}+1\right)^{2}}\left[\left(e^{\frac{3\pi\beta\hslash c}{a}}+1\right)+\frac{27\pi^{2}\beta\bar{\alpha}\hslash c}{4a^{3}}e^{\frac{3\pi\beta\hslash c}{a}}\right]\,,

(58)

where in the limit
T\to 0
, the entropy goes to zero, respecting the Nernst heat theorem as shown in Fig.
5
. It is worth highlighting that in the regime where
\bar{\alpha}\to 0
the Casimir entropy diverges as expected due to the nature of the phenomenon.

VI
Conclusion

In the present work, we first studied the dimensional projection method applied to a
4D
LIV fermionic system, reducing its dimensionality to
2D
. As a result, we obtain a modified dispersion relation (
11
), which may be separated into the timelike and spacelike approaches given, respectively, by (
12a
) and (
12b
). From these modified equations, we analyzed each case in which we conclude that only the space-like approach is physically adequate since the limit
\bar{\alpha}\to 0
may be taken without problem as pointed out in (
20
). Then, we used the dispersion relation (
21
) to find the quantum fluctuation under an MIT boundary condition represented by Eq. (
26
). The expression of the Casimir energy obtained does not have any approximation, in contrast with the results found in the literature. Moreover, the force was found from the energy given by (
27
).

In the second part of this work, the temperature was introduced using the Matsubara formalism, allowing us to derive an expression to the Casimir energy with finite temperature (
42
). However, this expression cannot satisfy the entire temperature regime since, by performing both sums, the energy diverges; consequently, two temperature regimes were adopted. The low-temperature regime, which describes the behaviour of the energy with a temperature near zero given by Eq. (
46
), is illustrated in Fig.
1
. It is straightforward that the energy goes to zero when the temperature
T\to 0
, as expected. In addition, the Casimir force is represented in a low temperature by Eq. (
47
) and its behaviour is shown in Fig.
2
where it is evident that the Casimir force vanishes as
T\to 0
.

The high-temperature regime is shown in Fig.
3
which is expressed by Eq. (
55
), and it presents a correct behaviour when
T\to\infty
converging to a constant value. Moreover, the LIV parameter modifies the intensity of the force and increases as the value of
\bar{\alpha}
increases. The expression of the Casimir force was found
in Eq.(
56
) and its behaviour was depicted in Fig.
4
.

The last point analyzed was the entropy associated with the vacuum fluctuation. Expressions for both the low- and high-temperature regimes were derived and given by (
58
) and (
59
), respectively. The behaviour at low temperature was plotted in Fig.
5
, where the asymptotic limit to
T\to 0
, respects the third law of thermodynamics, leading us to a null Casimir entropy. In the high-temperature limit, shown in Fig.
6
, we can see that the Casimir entropy goes to zero since the energy remains constant after reaching critical values for the temperature, as described in Fig.
3
. In both cases, the LIV parameter
\bar{\alpha}
modifies not only the behaviour of the entropy but also its intensity.

Finally, the application of the dimensional projection method to a fermion system to study the Casimir energy in an LIV scenario and its thermal properties was first developed in the current work.
