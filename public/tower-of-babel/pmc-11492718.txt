A case report of mediastinal extramedullary hematopoiesis: imaging, pathological and thoracoscopic biopsy correlation
Authors: Fengbo Yao, Dingbiao Li, Ying Wang, Yanfei Wang, Yongchang Lv
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo summarize the clinical features, imaging characteristics, and surgical diagnosis and treatment experience of mediastinal extramedullary hematopoiesis.MethodsBy summarizing and analyzing the case data and reviewing the literature, one case of posterior mediastinal extramedullary hematopoiesis on thoracoscopic biopsy in our department was summarized.ConclusionPosterior mediastinal extramedullary hematopoiesis is a response to failure of bone marrow erythropoiesis, which is relatively rare clinically, and diagnosis requires a combination of patient history and imaging, puncture biopsy or thoracoscopic excisional biopsy can provide a pathological diagnosis, and surgical complete resection is the main treatment modality.Keywords: Posterior mediastinal extramedullary hematopoiesis, History of anemia, Imaging, Puncture biopsy, Thoracoscopic resection biopsy

Case information

The patient was a 42-year-old man who was admitted to the hospital with a posterior mediastinal mass that had been detected on examination for one month. A CT scan of the chest revealed a bilateral posterior mediastinal paravertebral mass. During the course of the disease, the patient had no accompanying symptoms such as fever, chest pain, cough, etc. He was in good general condition on physical examination, with no chest or back pain, no anemia or bleeding, and had normal mental health, diet, sleep, and urination and defecation. He underwent splenectomy for congenital anemia at the age of 12 years, and his anemia improved after the surgery. Bone marrow aspiration examination was unremarkable, and he denied the history of thalassemia, sickle cell anemia, leukemia, and other hematologic and tumor-related diseases. She denied history of blood transfusion, trauma, and drug and food allergy.

Laboratory examination

Blood routine: white blood cell count (WBC) 6.5 × 109/L, red blood cell count (RBC) 5.34 × 1012/L, hemoglobin (Hb) 169 g/L, hematocrit (HCT) 0.48 L/L, mean corpuscular volume of red blood cells (MCV) 89.9fL, mean corpuscular hemoglobin volume of red blood cells (MCH) 31.6pg, platelet count (PLT) 499 × 109/L. Coagulation function was normal, liver and kidney function electrolytes were normal, and tumor markers were normal.

Imaging examination

Electrocardiogram, echocardiogram, and abdominal ultrasonography were normal. Contrast-enhanced chest CT showed: bilateral paravertebral mildly enhanced multiple soft tissue masses, the size of the larger one was about 2.9 × 3.8 × 3.5 cm, and the posterior mediastinum was mostly considered to be occupying the posterior mediastinum (see Fig. 1). Thoracic enhancement MRI showed: posterior mediastinum about T8 ∼ 10 vertebrae on both sides of the T1, short T2 signal shadow, clear boundaries, strengthened after enhancement scanning, DWI/ADC did not see water molecule diffusion restriction, the larger one is about 36 mm × 29 mm × 37 mm, and the boundary with the neighboring vertebrae is clear, the neighboring bone does not see obvious abnormal signals, and it did not invade into the vertebral canal (see Fig. 2).

Chest CT enhancement scan: multiple mass-like moderate-slightly low density foci were seen in the mediastinum on both sides of the vertebral body at about the T8-10 level, the large one measuring about 2.9 × 3.8 × 3.5 cm, with mild enhancement after enhancement

Chest MRI enhanced scan: multiple clustered long T1 and short T2 signal shadows are seen on both sides of the posterior mediastinum at about T8-10 vertebrae, with clear borders, enhanced after enhanced scanning, and no obvious abnormal signals are seen in the neighboring bones

Surgical treatment and pathologic results

The left posterior mediastinal mass was resected under thoracoscopic examination, and the contraindications for surgery were excluded. During the operation, a purple-red mass was seen in the left posterior mediastinum, which was soft and closely attached to the posterior mediastinal pleura next to the thoracic vertebrae (see Fig. 3), and a lot of tiny blood vessels were seen to be growing into the mass when it was separated, and the boundaries between the posterior wall of the mass and the thoracic vertebrae were not clear, so the mass would ooze and bleed easily when it was separated and needed to be hemostatized by electrocoagulation and titanium clips to stop hemorrhage. Finally, a thoracoscopic partial resection of the left posterior mediastinal mass was performed, and the specimen was sent for pathologic examination. Intraoperative rapid pathology result: benign lesion. Postoperative pathological findings: immunohistochemistry: CD15 (partially +); CD20 (partially +); CD3 (partially +); CD56 (-); CgA (-); CK (-); Ki-67 (+,30%); LCA (+); MPO (partially +); SYN (-); TdT (focally +); TTF-1 (-). The posterior mediastinal mass was diagnosed as extramedullary hematopoiesis (see Fig. 4). Regular imaging and blood tests were followed up after discharge, and no mass enlargement or hematologic abnormalities were seen.

Thoracoscopic view: posterior mediastinal purplish-red mass, soft in texture, tightly adhering to the posterior mediastinal pleura next to the thoracic vertebrae, with unclear boundaries with the thoracic vertebrae

Pathological findings: posterior mediastinal mass diagnosed as extramedullary hematopoiesis

Discussion

Physiologically, in the embryonic and fetal period, hematopoietic cells are mainly concentrated in the yolk sac, liver, spleen, lymph nodes, bone marrow and other tissues and organs until about 2 months after birth, when bone marrow hematopoiesis completely replaces other hematopoiesis [1]. In pathological cases, the bone marrow hematopoiesis is usually insufficient to meet the circulatory requirements due to hematological disorders, malignant neoplastic disorders, and systemic systemic pathologies, and the hematopoietic stem cells are differentiated from extramedullary hematopoietic tissues such as liver, spleen, and lymph nodes, which restores hematopoiesis and stimulates the hematopoietic cells to proliferate and form lumps, and the hematopoietic phenomenon that occurs outside of the bone marrow is called EMH. common extramedullary hematopoietic organs are liver, spleen and lymph nodes, and less commonly in pleura, lungs, gastrointestinal tract, mammary glands, skin, brain, kidneys and adrenal glands [2–4].

Posterior mediastinal EMH needs to be differentiated from neurogenic tumors, cystic masses, pleural tumors, and pyothorax, etc. Chest CT images of posterior mediastinal EMH often show multiple soft tissue masses in the posterior mediastinal paravertebral region, which are connected to the wide base of the spine, with a smooth margin and clear borders, and due to compensatory hyperplasia of hematopoietic tissues, the foci have a rich blood supply, most of them are relatively homogeneous in density, and cystic degeneration, necrosis, and calcification are rare, and the enhancement scans show either mild enhancement or uniform enhancement, reflecting the feature of extramedullary hematopoiesis as rich hematopoietic tissue [5–8]. MRI images showed single or multiple, unilateral or bilateral soft tissue masses in the paraspinal sulcus of the posterior mediastinum, and unilateral and bilateral posterior mediastinum EMH have been reported [7–10]. Some scholars divided the intrathoracic extramedullary hematopoietic tissue into four stages. Stage I: active hematopoietic process, showing isotropic T1 and isotropic T2 signals, with no obvious enhancement on enhanced scanning; Stage II: old hematopoiesis due to deposition of ferritin-containing hemosiderin, showing long T1 and short T2, with no enhancement on enhancement; and Stage III: almost complete development of steatosis, showing long T1, long T2, with mild enhancement on enhancement; Stage IV: both hematopoietically active tissues and fatty degeneration components with uneven signal length [11]. In addition to CT and MRI examinations, radionuclide imaging using 99mTc sulfur colloid has been reported for the diagnosis of posterior mediastinal masses due to intrathoracic extramedullary hematopoiesis [12, 13]. However, fine needle biopsy may be required to provide cytologic or histologic confirmation when imaging still fails to confirm the diagnosis. A CT-guided aspiration biopsy of the posterior mediastinal mass can be performed to clarify the nature of the mass, and the diagnosis of posterior mediastinal EMH can be clarified if a reddish jelly-like tissue sample is obtained by aspiration and confirmed to be hematopoietic tissue by pathological examination. EMH can be seen as a dark red or purplish-red lesion on gross pathological histological section, and a nodular tissue can be observed microscopically, which contains adipose tissue and juvenile red blood cells and megakaryocytes at various developmental stages [12, 13]. These cells show active hematopoietic function, and a large number of nucleated erythrocytes can be observed, the nucleus is located in the center, round and large, the chromatin is densely stained, the nucleolus is unclear, and the cytoplasm is wide and reddish stained [14].

EMH is a benign lesion, mostly associated with hematopoietic dysfunction and chronic anemia, often secondary to hematologic disorders such as thalassemia, hereditary spherocytosis, and primary thrombocythemia, and in a few cases inspired by malignant tumors, such as breast, kidney, and lung cancer [15]. Most of them are treated by complete surgical resection and diagnosis, but some authors believe that extramedullary hematopoiesis is a benign lesion, and its presence has a compensatory function for the long-term chronic anemia and bone marrow hematopoietic insufficiency, and surgical resection may lead to reanemia or worsening of anemia, and surgical intervention is only necessary when EMH is accompanied by hemorrhage or compression of neighboring tissues [16, 17]. In this case, EMH was admitted to the hospital because of a “posterior mediastinal mass found on examination for 1 month”, and although the previous history of anemia 30 years ago was considered, there were no manifestations of anemia or hematological related diseases, and it was difficult to make a clear diagnosis by CT and MRI imaging alone, and a sample of the mass was needed for pathological examination to make a clear diagnosis, which was clarified by some scholars after CT-guided puncture and biopsy. Some scholars use CT-guided puncture biopsy to obtain samples for clear diagnosis, which is less invasive and easy to operate, and the author believes that it is worth advocating, especially for the bilateral posterior mediastinal masses similar to this case, and the surgical evaluation can not be complete resection of the mass, puncture biopsy may be more suitable. It is worth noting that EMH is highly vascularized and the mass is brittle, so both puncture and thoracoscopic excisional biopsy are very likely to lead to bleeding of the mass, and the operation should be performed carefully and cautiously.

In conclusion, posterior mediastinal extramedullary hematopoiesis (EMH) is a response to insufficient bone marrow erythropoiesis, which is relatively rare in clinical practice, and many clinicians have never encountered this type of patients. It is relatively difficult to diagnose, and in addition to careful questioning of the patient’s past history of anemia and hematology, and a series of imaging tests, CT-guided puncture biopsy or thoracoscopic excisional biopsy may be required to make a definitive diagnosis. In terms of treatment, for asymptomatic EMH, regular follow-up and active treatment of the primary disease can be performed. In the absence of anemia or bone marrow hematopoietic insufficiency, most scholars still recommend the surgical treatment of complete thoracoscopic mass resection. In clinical work, if we encounter asymptomatic posterior mediastinal mass combined with chronic anemia and history of hepatosplenomegaly, we should be highly alert to the possibility of intrathoracic extramedullary hematopoiesis [16].
