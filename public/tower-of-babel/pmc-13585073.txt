Effect of Prenatal Intervention on Dental Caries and Risk Factors in Children with Unilateral Cleft Lip and/or Palate in Medellín, Colombia: A Nonrandomized Controlled Clinical Trial
Authors: Ana María Cerón-Zapata, Bibiana Andrea Castro-Montoya, Paola Valdivieso-Gaviria
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and aimCleft lip and palate prenatal diagnosis has increased, seemingly allowing better preparation of the mother regarding psychosocial aspects, feeding, growth, and development. The purpose of the study was to compare the proportion of early childhood caries (ECC) using the ICDAS visual criteria and caries risk assessment by CAMBRA® in individuals with unilateral cleft lip and palate (UCLP) diagnosed prenatally and postnatally, treated with nasoalveolar molding (NAM) and whose mothers received a personalized motivational intervention approach (PMIA).Materials and methodsA nonrandomized controlled experimental study was conducted on children with UCL/P treated with NAM and diagnosed prenatally and postnatally. Both groups had PMIA. Dental caries was assessed using the ICDAS criteria during the first 3 years of life, and caries risk was assessed using the CAMBRA® system in the third year of life. Results: A higher proportion of cavitated caries lesions was observed in the prenatal group at 2 years of age. At 3 years old, a higher proportion of noncavitated caries lesions was observed in the postnatal group according to the ICDAS criteria, without statistical significance.ConclusionBoth groups presented early childhood caries despite receiving a personalized motivational intervention approach. There were no significant differences in the CAMBRA® risk index between the groups.Clinical significanceDespite the interventions carried out, both groups showed the presence of ECC. This highlights a significant public health issue that demands broader and more systematic intervention by institutions, with a particular emphasis on educational programs on oral health with rigorous follow-up.How to cite this articleCerón-Zapata AM, Castro-Montoya BA, Valdivieso-Gaviria P. Introduction

Cleft lip and/or palate (CL/P) is a craniofacial difference that occurs prenatally.1 In Colombia, a prevalence of 0.327 per 1000 inhabitants2 and 1.54 per 1,000 live births has been reported.3

Cleft lip and/or palate (CL/P) can be diagnosed pre- and postnatally. Prenatal diagnosis often generates feelings of shock, guilt, despair, and fear in parents.4 The purpose of prenatal diagnosis of cleft palate is to educate and prepare the family to face the interventions required for the comprehensive rehabilitation of the person with CL/P. An interdisciplinary team should treat the person with CL/P and their family. The team seeks to guide the parents through this process.5 The emotions that emerge with the diagnosis can influence the ability to understand the information, contribute to the assimilation of the craniofacial difference, and encourage psychological preparation for when the child is born.4,6 This diagnosis has been increasing over the years with routine ultrasound performed between 13 and 20 weeks of pregnancy. Prenatal diagnosis with counseling can improve the psychosocial aspects of early family care as it provides parents with complete and timely information about the interdisciplinary treatment before birth.7

Children with CL/P have a higher prevalence of early childhood caries (ECC),8 considered a rapidly developing carious lesion in the primary dentition, and can destroy the dentition. It is widespread, with its highest prevalence in socially disadvantaged groups.9,10 When complementary feeding begins at 6 months, there is a risk of developing early childhood caries associated with nighttime bottle-feeding with liquids other than water or milk.11,12

Disease prevention and early caries diagnosis should be an aspect of the interdisciplinary management of people with CL/P. Patients with CLP visit the pediatric dentist early in life, and this is an opportunity to deliver anticipatory guidance for the prevention of ECC. Oral health education alone was reported to be inefficient because persuasiveness by a dentist is realized outside observance of the parents' knowledge to modify their behaviors.13 Some studies have reported an approach called the personalized motivational interviewing approach (PMIA)14 with inconsistent results.

Dental caries can be measured using diagnostic indices, which numerically express the description of defined diagnostic criteria. The most well-known is the dmft/dmfs index.15 Another system used is the “International Caries Detection and Assessment System (ICDAS),” which was developed to standardize the visual detection of carious lesions based on their severity and treatment needs.16 The published literature has reported a high prevalence of dental caries in children with CL/P.17 This diagnosis has been associated with the anatomy of the cleft area,18 hypoplastic defects,18 prolonged bottle-feeding, especially during the night,19 increased consumption of sugary foods,20 and the use of presurgical orthopedic appliances.21

Caries risk assessment is an essential component in the prevention and treatment of dental caries. Risk factors for dental caries can be analyzed through various risk assessment systems.22 The CAMBRA® (Caries Management by Risk Assessment) system allows for understanding the causes of dental caries, preventing disease progression, and ultimately preventing it altogether. It evaluates disease indicators, risk factors, and protective factors.23

After a child with CL/P is born, treatment begins under the care of an interdisciplinary team. During these early months of life, some interdisciplinary groups include presurgical nasoalveolar molding (NAM) in their care guidelines. This treatment aims to reposition the nasal cartilage, lengthen the columella, and approximate the alveolar processes.24 In scientific literature, the use of orthopedic and orthodontic appliances has been reported as a risk factor associated with dental caries.25,26

In this context, given the gap in the literature regarding caries risk assessment in children with CL/P treated with NAM, this study aimed to compare the proportion of ECC using the ICDAS visual criteria and caries risk assessment by CAMBRA® in individuals with unilateral (CL/P) diagnosed prenatally and postnatally, treated with NAM and whose mothers received a PMIA.

Materials and Methods

A prospective, nonrandomized, controlled experimental study was conducted on children with unilateral CL/P who attended the Clínica Noel Foundation for the first time in Medellin, Colombia, in 2014. The population consisted of newborns up to 2 months of age diagnosed pre- and postnatally with CL/P, who were treated with NAM and agreed to participate in the study.

The PICO question was P: children with unilateral cleft lip and palate (CL/P) treated with nasoalveolar molding (NAM), I: personalized motivational interviews giving guidance and training on feeding and oral health promotion to mothers who received the diagnosis of their children prenatally C: personalized motivational interviews giving guidance and training on feeding and oral health, promotion to mothers who received the diagnosis of their children postnatally, O: proportion of dental caries.

According to the Public Health Surveillance System of Colombia (SIVIGILA), the registry of live births of patients with cleft lip and palate (CL/P) in Medellín during the year 2014 recorded 45 children.27 The sample was nonprobabilistic based on the availability of patients with the studied craniofacial anomaly. The following inclusion criteria were considered: patients between 0 and 2 months of age with a prenatal or postnatal diagnosis of nonsyndromic unilateral cleft lip and palate (UCL/P), living in Medellín, Colombia, and treated with NAM. Patients with UCL/P associated with syndromes were excluded.

A total of 28 children were included and divided into two groups according to the timing of their diagnosis: one group of children with UCL/P treated with NAM diagnosed prenatally, and the other group of children with UCL/P treated with NAM diagnosed postnatally. Both groups underwent the following interventions: during the first consultation, a one-on-one personalized motivational interview was offered to the primary caregiver of the study participant. Infant oral health education was provided through audiovisual aids and demonstrations on oral health promotion, including oral hygiene, anticariogenic diet, oral habits, early management of malocclusion, and accident prevention.28 Education was conducted by a pediatric dentistry student, an internationally certified lactation consultant nutritionist, and a pediatric dentistry professor. It was delivered using a PowerPoint® presentation with images related to each topic. First, a lecture covering the five issues was presented. Caregivers were encouraged to practice oral hygiene measures in front of the researcher, and it was verified that they understood the instructions. After the first tooth eruption, confirmed during follow-up visits, a small toothbrush with a thick handle and a small amount (rice-grain size) of fluoride toothpaste (1000 ppm) was used.

The follow-up of the children with UCL/P was conducted as follows: initially, during the first 6 months, appointments were scheduled weekly. Starting from the sixth month, appointments were held monthly. After the first year, appointments were conducted every 6 months; however, the ICDAS evaluation was performed annually, and in the final year, the CAMBRA caries risk was measured.

In the prenatal group, the first education session was conducted before birth. In contrast, the postnatal group was performed after birth, and during the same week, the person with UCL/P joined the interdisciplinary CL/P group. For the next 3 years, the caregiver's children with UCL/P continued receiving information about oral health promotion and feeding during consultations from the pediatric dentist resident who attended them clinically.

After enrolling in the study, individuals were seen weekly during the first semester while undergoing NAM treatment. During the second semester, after the primary rhino–cheiloplasty, they were seen monthly and treated with the Hotz-type obturator until the primary palatoplasty surgery was performed at around 12 months of age. In the second and third years of life, they had semiannual dental checkups, during which dental prophylaxis, clinical examinations, and fluoride varnish applications were performed. At each appointment, the pediatric dentistry student and professor provided feedback on oral hygiene, an anticariogenic diet, oral habits, early management of malocclusion, and accident prevention. During the appointment, the child with UCL/P was examined in the dental chair, where a clinical examination was performed to evaluate their dentition.

Each child with UCL/P was clinically evaluated at ages 1, 2, and 3 by the student and the pediatric dentistry teacher, who were previously calibrated in ICDAS and conducted the clinical examinations. Prophylaxis was performed using pumice/water and dental floss. Subsequently, the teeth were dried using an air jet for 5 seconds, and a WHO probe was used to evaluate the surfaces.

During the follow-up visits, children with carious lesions received additional treatment. Children with noncavitated lesions were treated with topical fluoride varnish (Duraphat®), while those with cavitated lesions were treated with light-cured resin restorations (Z100 by 3M®).

The surfaces of the anterior teeth in the cleft area and the posterior teeth were examined, starting from the upper right posterior quadrant and moving to the next quadrant in a clockwise direction. When there was more than one carious lesion on the occlusal surface, the most severe lesion was classified and recorded. The difference in proportions of dental caries was calculated using the following formula: the number of surfaces for each code was divided by the total number of surfaces in the group and multiplied by 100.

To code carious lesions in this study, the authors used the definition of early childhood caries, which consists of having a cavitated lesion (ICDAS codes 3–6) or a noncavitated lesion (ICDAS codes 1 and 2).16,29 The ICDAS criteria manual was consulted to differentiate between initial lesions and opacities due to poor mineralization.16 The ICDAS code 0 was assigned to developmental defects on the tooth surface without any signs of caries. ICDAS codes 1 or 2 were used for incipient lesions, such as white spots or discolorations that did not correspond to healthy enamel. Missing or unerupted teeth were coded as ICDAS 99, considering the age of the children participating in the study.

Every child with UCL/P underwent two examinations by two previously trained and calibrated examiners using ICDAS-II criteria simultaneously, one after the other. To prevent bias and allow agreement between examiners, one examiner was unaware of the evaluation codes of the other. Three time points were considered: T1 ICDAS at 1 year, T2 ICDAS at 2 years, and T3 ICDAS at 3 years of age.

The agreement between examiners was obtained using Cohen's kappa test, with a value of 0.966. Intraclass correlation coefficients were evaluated to assess intraoperator agreement, with values ranging between 0.966 and 0.996. With these coefficients, reliable information can be obtained.

The CAMBRA® risk assessment system was measured at 3 years of age, when the deciduous dentition was complete. The specific age dental caries risk assessment form was conducted following the CAMBRA® protocol with data provided by the parents of the children with UCL/P. The three components of the forms (disease indicators, risk factors, and protective factors) were determined through examination and interview. Patient stratification was based on the dental caries risk assessment form responses regarding the presence or absence of disease. Caries risk was categorized as high, moderate, or low. Disease indicators for ages 0–5 years, according to CAMBRA®, included visible white spots, visible decalcifications, or evident deterioration present on the child's teeth; restorations placed in the last 2 years; visible plaque on teeth and gums that bleed easily. Risk factors included visible heavy plaque on teeth, frequent small snack meals between main meals, and bottomless pits and fissures. Protective factors included living in a fluoridated community, using fluoride toothpaste at least once or twice daily, and receiving fluoride varnish application in the last 6 months.

All parents signed informed consent forms before entering the study. This study was approved by the ethics committee of CES University, Medellin, Colombia, with protocol number #74, and by the Research Committee of the Noel Clinic Foundation. CES University provided funding for this research project. CES University did not have a role in the design, interpretations, or views expressed in this publication. The authors declare that the research was conducted without any commercial or financial relationships that could be construed as a potential conflict of interest. This nonrandomized controlled clinical trial was not registered.

Statistic Analysis

The chi-square and Fisher's exact tests were used to compare qualitative variables between the prenatal and postnatal groups. For quantitative variables, the normality of the data was tested, and in the presence of non-normality, the Mann–Whitney U test was applied. Differences in proportions of ICDAS scores between the prenatal and postnatal groups were estimated. Finally, the chi-square test was used to compare CAMBRA variables between the prenatal and postnatal groups.

For the ICDAS index, differences in proportions of occurrence in the different ICDAS codes were evaluated. These were calculated and compared between T2 and T3. The codes were divided into ICDAS 1 and 2 (noncavitated lesions), ICDAS 3 and 4 (moderate lesions), and ICDAS 5 and 6 (severe lesions).

For the CAMBRA® risk level, indicator variables, risk factors, environmental and biological risk factors, and protective factors were compared between prenatal and postnatal diagnosis groups using the chi-square test, considering p-values less than 0.05 as statistically significant.

The association between severe CAMBRA risk and ICDAS codes at T3 was calculated by dividing the number of surfaces for each code at T3 by the total surface area of the group and multiplying by 100. A proportion difference between the prenatal and postnatal groups was then calculated to find the p-value.

Data were recorded in Excel and subsequently analyzed statistically. STATA 16, licensed by CES University, was used for statistical analysis.

Results

During the 3-year (36 months) follow-up period, the study began with 28 children with UCL/P, of whom ten children did not continue in the study: two did not return to the Noel Clinic Foundation, five stopped responding, one changed health insurance provider, one moved to another city, and one died (Fig. 1). Overall, 61.11% of the children with UCL/P were in the postnatal diagnosis group.

Discussion

This research compared the differences in caries proportions using the ICDAS visual criteria and caries risk assessment by CAMBRA® in individuals diagnosed with UCL/P prenatally and postnatally. The mothers of these individuals received PMIA on nutrition and oral health promotion.

The selection bias was controlled by two researchers, verifying that all patients met the inclusion criteria. Information bias control was performed by conducting a prospective longitudinal study, where records were obtained following the proposed project time points. As this was an exploratory study, no estimates of measures of association or effect were performed; therefore, no statistical regressions were conducted to control for potential confounding variables. The summary of findings was done descriptively and as hypothesis testing using p-values. During the 3-year follow-up of patients, a loss rate of 12.5% was observed in the prenatal and 45% in the postnatal groups.

This is the first study to conduct a prenatal intervention in children with UCL/P through one-on-one motivational interviews about feeding and oral health promotion in Colombia.

González et al. conducted an observational study on dental caries in children with CL/P using the ICDAS system in Bogotá.30 They found a high prevalence of dental caries in the deciduous dentition, similar to this study.

Lesions with greater severity were found in the group that received the first postnatal motivational interview. At T2, a patient with many surfaces affected by dental caries was observed, which could have influenced the research results. At the end of the study, both groups presented a high level of experience with dental caries.

Socioeconomic status is a factor that can contribute to the oral health status of children. Chaffee et al. reported that patients with low socioeconomic status, maternal education, and social stratum exhibited a higher prevalence of dental caries.31 These variables could have influenced the results of our research, as all children with UCL/P were from low socioeconomic status, and most parents had education levels up to high school.

Abirami et al. conducted an umbrella review to assess caries experience in children with CL/P comprehensively. They found a high prevalence of caries during deciduous, mixed, and permanent dentition.8 Vitor et al. conducted a cross-sectional study measuring caries lesions in children aged 6–10 years with CL/P using the ICDAS criteria, reporting that most tooth surfaces were coded as 0.32 Mutarai et al. compared a group of patients with CL/P to a control group without clefts. They found a higher prevalence of caries in children with CL/P, associated with risk factors such as bottle use at night.33 In contrast, in this research, by the third year of age, most children with UCL/P presented some sign of dental caries lesion, similar to what was reported by Antonarakis et al., who found that nonsyndromic CL/P patients had a higher prevalence of dental caries, both in permanent and deciduous dentition, compared to control groups without CL/P.34

It is worth noting that most patients consumed more than three snacks per day and drank sugary beverages, showing a high frequency of sugar exposure that altered saliva pH and increased the risk of demineralization and, consequently, the risk of developing dental caries. Chaudhari et al. in their study found low buffering capacity in patients with CL/P in unstimulated saliva and a high prevalence of extensive dental caries lesions.15

Another risk factor to consider in this population, although not studied microbiologically, was using the NAM obturator. It has been shown that the use of orthopedic appliances decreases pH levels, increasing colonies of Streptococcus mutans.21

In this study, despite the educational intervention, there was an increase in dental caries in both groups year after year. All individuals who participated in the survey began consuming unrefined whole-cane sugar (Panela in Spanish) water at age 1. When assessing the risk, parents reported that, although they were informed of the risk of dental caries associated with its consumption, they considered this food to provide energy for their child. That unrefined whole cane sugar cubes were part of their family's dietary tradition. Jaffé reported that the Colombian beverage unrefined whole cane sugar infusion (agua de panela in Spanish) is used as a source of energy, as an infusion to warm up in cold climate regions, and as a refreshing drink in warm climate regions.35 The results of this study align with Villegas, who found in a study on sugar consumption and ECC in Cali, Colombia, high usage of unrefined whole cane sugar infusion frequently in individuals with ECC.36

Regarding the intervention conducted with PMIA, Murthy et al.37 compared the effectiveness of educating mothers in two ways in children with CL/P, using an audiovisual method and a brochure. They found better results when using the audiovisual method. The intervention conducted in this research is not directly comparable since it utilized the personalized motivational interviewing approach, permanent telephone availability, and in-person support from the lactation consultant if needed.

In the systematic review conducted by Soares et al., they concluded that methods to prevent dental caries included preventive dental programs for pregnant women; counseling on diet and nutrition; prenatal oral health care; promotion of oral health in nursing practice; maternal oral health programs conducted by nondental health professionals; education on dental health combined with the use of fluoridated varnishes for children; early preventive dental visits; and the use of toothpaste with more than 1000 ppm of fluoride.38 In this study, all these strategies were used to prevent early childhood caries; however, it was found that parents were not willing to avoid consuming unrefined whole cane sugar infusion at night.

In 2014, in Medellín, the birth of 45 new patients with cleft lip and palate was recorded. However, it is essential to highlight that only those patients who chose to participate in the study could meet all the established inclusion criteria. This underscores the inherent difficulty in finding mothers with UCL/P prenatal diagnosis who would attend the Clinic to receive interdisciplinary treatment for children with UCL/P.

The CAMBRA® risk assessment was exclusively performed during the T3 period; at that time, patients had fully developed their deciduous dentition.

Study Limitations

It is suggested that a cohort study with a larger sample size be conducted due to the prevalence of UCL/P. The high proportion of dental caries in the studied children could be addressed by implementing a different oral health communication strategy, utilizing other technologies, and suggesting further research in this field.

Conclusion

After conducting personalized motivational interviewing interventions on oral health education for mothers of children with UCL/P diagnosed prenatally and postnatally treated with NAM, it was found that both groups exhibited ECC. In the postnatal diagnosis group, codes 5 and 6, according to the ICDAS criteria, were observed at 2 years, and codes 1 and 2 at 3 years. The two groups had no significant differences in the CAMBRA® risk index.

Clinical Significance

Despite the interventions carried out, both groups showed the presence of ECC. This finding highlights a significant public health issue that demands broader and more systematic intervention by institutions, with a particular emphasis on educational programs on oral health with rigorous follow-up.
