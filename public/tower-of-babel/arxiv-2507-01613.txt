When Less Is More: Binary Feedback Can Outperform Ordinal Comparisons in Ranking Recovery
Shirong Xu, Jingnan Zhang, Junhui Wang

Abstract
Paired comparison data, where users evaluate items in pairs, play a central role in ranking and preference learning tasks. While ordinal comparison data intuitively offer richer information than binary comparisons, this paper challenges that conventional wisdom. We propose a general parametric framework for modeling ordinal paired comparisons without ties. The model adopts a generalized additive structure, featuring a link function that quantifies the preference difference between two items and a pattern function that governs the distribution over ordinal response levels. This framework encompasses classical binary comparison models as special cases, by treating binary responses as binarized versions of ordinal data. Within this framework, we show that binarizing ordinal data can significantly improve the accuracy of ranking recovery. Specifically, we prove that under the counting algorithm, the ranking error associated with binary comparisons exhibits a faster exponential convergence rate than that of ordinal data. Furthermore, we characterize a substantial performance gap between binary and ordinal data in terms of a signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) determined by the pattern function. We identify the pattern function that minimizes the SNR and maximizes the benefit of binarization. Extensive simulations and a real application on the MovieLens dataset further corroborate our theoretical findings.

1  Introduction

Paired comparison data arises from evaluating items in pairs and is commonly encountered across various scenarios, including college comparisons
(
Caron et al. 2014
)
, product evaluations
(
Böckenholt and Dillon 1997
;
Duineveld et al. 2000
)
, sports tournaments
(
Buhlmann and Huber 1963
;
Li et al. 2022
;
Jadbabaie et al. 2020
)
, and human feedback in large language models
(
Zhu et al. 2023
;
Poddar et al. 2024
)
. Usually, it is commonly assumed that a true parameter vector
\bm{\theta}^{\star}=(\theta_{1}^{\star},\ldots,\theta_{n}^{\star})
exists for
n
items with
\theta_{i}^{\star}
representing the preference of
i
-th item. A central problem resolving around paired comparison data is to estimate the ordering of
\bm{\theta}^{\star}
, and then a complete ranking of
n
items can be obtained
(
Chen et al. 2019
;
Chen et al. 2022a
;
Chen et al. 2022b
;
Wauthier et al. 2013
)
.

Throughout the past century, the literature has seen extensive research dedicated to the development of parametric paired comparison models. One prominent line of work focuses on modeling comparisons as
binary
outcomes. Specifically, the probability that item
i
is preferred over item
j
under a given criterion is modeled as

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(i\succ j\right)=F(\theta_{i}^{\star}-\theta_{j}^{\star}),

(1)

where
\succ
denotes a ranking relationship under a specific criterion, such as preference. For instance, in the context of large language models, users may be asked to express a binary preference when comparing two textual responses. Similarly, in sports tournaments, two teams compete, and the outcome of the match reflects which team is stronger.

The function
F(\cdot)
can take various forms, such as the logistic function,
F(x)=(1+\exp(-x))^{-1}
, or the normal cumulative distribution function. These correspond to the Bradley-Terry-Luce (BTL) model
(
Bradley and Terry 1952
)
and the Thurstone-Mosteller (TM) model
(
Thurstone 1994
)
, respectively. Furthermore,
Stern 1990
introduced a model in which binary comparisons arise from the comparison of two gamma-distributed random variables with different scale parameters. This framework includes the BTL and Thurstone-Mosteller models as special cases for different values of the gamma shape parameter.

Another line of research aims at modeling non-binary ordinal paired comparisons. This line of research is motivated by scenarios in which items are evaluated with varying degrees of preference. For example, consumers may express a strong preference for one product over another when comparing alternatives. One of the earliest contributions in this area extends the Bradley-Terry-Luce (BTL) model to account for ties in paired comparisons, effectively incorporating three distinct levels of preference
(
Glenn and David 1960
;
Rao and Kupper 1967
;
Davidson 1970
)
. Building on this,
Agresti 1992
introduces an adjacent-categories logit model that accommodates comparisons with more than three options, while naturally reducing to the BTL model when only two categories are present. Further extensions to continuous paired comparison data are presented in
Stern 2011
and
Han et al. 2022
. Notably,
Han et al. 2022
proposes a general paired comparison framework capable of modeling both continuous and ordinal observations.

To clarify the distinction between binary and ordinal comparisons, we present an example in Figure
1
. In this example, the same two users are asked to compare the same pair of items under two differently structured systems. In the first system (Left), users provide binary responses to their comparisons, while in the second system (Right), they offer more detailed, ordinal feedback. In the first case, the preference ordering between the items is ambiguous because the two users give conflicting responses. In contrast, under the second system, item 1 receives more favorable feedback: user 1 expresses a strong preference for item 1, whereas user 2 only slightly prefers item 2. This example illustrates that when a system restricts users to binary choices, valuable information about the strength of preferences may be lost. In other words, the responses in Scenario I are binarized versions of those in Scenario II. Specifically,
strongly agree
and
somewhat disagree
are reduced to
1\succ 2
and
2\succ 1
, respectively, resulting in significant information loss.

Figure 1
:
Two users submit pairwise comparison responses using two distinct sets of system-defined options. In Scenario I (Left), a preference ranking tie occurs. In Scenario II (Right), object 1 receives more favorable feedback.

The example in Figure
1
naturally gives rise to
the intuition
that ordinal comparison data conveys more information about the underlying ground-truth ranking. Consequently, one might expect that using ordinal comparisons would lead to more accurate ranking estimates of items. However, in this paper, we theoretically demonstrate that this intuition does not hold within a broad class of ordinal comparison models. In fact, we show that binarizing ordinal comparison data into binary comparisons can significantly improve the estimation of the ground-truth ranking.

To address the question of which type of comparison data is more effective for recovering item rankings, it is crucial to understand the underlying relationship between ordinal and binary comparison data. Specifically, as illustrated in Example
1
, when two different systems elicit responses on the same items from the same group of users, what is the fundamental connection between their binary and ordinal comparison responses? To some extent, binary responses can be viewed as the result of binarizing the corresponding ordinal responses. This raises a natural question: Is there a modeling framework that explicitly captures the binarization process?

To address the above questions, we begin by introducing a general class of parametric models designed to capture ordinal outcomes in pairwise comparisons without ties. This focus is motivated by the fact that ties are often absent in many real-world comparison settings, such as sports competitions and consumer preference surveys. The proposed model takes the form of a generalized additive model, consisting of a link function that captures the preference difference between two items and a pattern function that characterizes the distribution over ordinal response levels. The contributions of this paper within the proposed framework can be summarized as follows:

(1)

A key advantage of this framework is that it subsumes a broad family of binary comparison models—including those in (
1
)—as special cases. In particular, when ordinal comparison data are binarized, the resulting binary responses follow the model specified in (
1
). This connection enables a principled comparison of the ranking recovery performance between binary and ordinal comparison data.

(2)

Within this framework, we theoretically show that binarizing ordinal comparison data can lead to a faster convergence rate in recovering the ground-truth ranking when using the counting algorithm. Specifically, we demonstrate that the ranking error associated with binary comparison data exhibits a faster exponential convergence rate. This result implies that, provided a sufficiently large number of users contribute comparison data, binary comparisons consistently outperform ordinal ones in terms of ranking accuracy. This result offers valuable insight into the importance of binary comparison data, particularly given its widespread use in preference learning to enhance the performance of large language models
(
Zhu et al. 2023
;
Slocum et al. 2025
)
.

(3)

We also establish the existence of a nontrivial gap in ranking error between binary and ordinal comparison data. This performance gap is governed by the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) associated with the pattern function: the smaller the SNR, the greater the benefit of binarizing ordinal data. Furthermore, we characterize the pattern function that minimizes the SNR, thereby identifying the setting in which binarization yields the greatest improvement. This theoretical finding is further supported by extensive simulation studies, as presented in Section
5.1
.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section
1.1
introduces the necessary notations. In Section
2
, we develop the proposed ordinal comparison model and provides background on the ordinal comparison graph under the proposed model. Section
3
presents a theoretical analysis showing that binarized comparison data lead to improved performance in ranking recovery for both two-item and
n
-item ranking problems. In Section
4
, we identify the pattern function that minimizes the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR), thereby yielding the greatest benefit from binarizing ordinal comparison data. Section
5
presents extensive simulations and a real-data application to validate our theoretical findings. All proofs of theorems and supporting lemmas are provided in the Appendix.

2  Proposed Method

In this section, we introduce a general ordinal comparison model for analyzing paired comparisons represented by symmetric, non-zero discrete values. Such comparisons frequently occur in contexts such as sports tournaments and consumer surveys. We further investigate the properties of the proposed model and highlight its connections to binary comparison models, including both the Bradley–Terry–Luce (BTL) model
(
Bradley and Terry 1952
)
and the Thurstone–Mosteller (TM) model
(
Thurstone 1994
)
, under specific choices of link functions.

2.1
Strength Link Function

We begin with presenting the definition of the strength link function, which serves as a fundamental building block, and allows for a range of adaptations within the framework.

Definition 1

(Strength Link Function)
.

A function
\phi
is a strength link function if
\phi
satisfies the following properties:

(1)

Increasing Monotonicity:
\phi(x)>\phi(y)
if
x>y
;

(2)

Origin Symmetry:
\phi(x)=-\phi(-x)
for any
x\in\mathbb{R}
.

According to the definition above, the conditions for
\phi
to be qualified as a strength link function encompass both increasing monotonicity and symmetry about zero. Under the constraints imposed on
\phi
, it is evident that
\phi(0)=0
and
\phi(x)>0
for all
x>0
. The selection of
\phi
is notably flexible, necessitating only symmetry with respect to the origin and increasing monotonicity. Similar conditions has also been considered in
Han et al. 2022
. Figure
2
illustrates several examples of possible choices for
\phi
.

Figure 2
:
Four examples for
\phi(x)
: (1)
\phi(x)=x^{3}
; (2)
\phi(x)=x
; (3)
\phi(x)=\frac{1-e^{-x}}{1+e^{-x}}
; (4)
\phi(x)=\mathop{\mathrm{sign}}(x)|x|^{1/3}
.

In addition to the examples depicted in Figure
2
, various strength link functions can be devised by leveraging the cumulative distribution functions of various continuous random variables as demonstrated in Lemma
1
.

Lemma 1
.

Let
X
be a symmetric continuous random variable centered around zero with support on
\mathbb{R}
, and let
F(X)
denote its associated cumulative distribution function (CDF) without point masses. Then the function
\phi(x)=C\log\left(\frac{F(x)}{1-F(x)}\right)
is a strength link function, where
C
is any positive constant.

Lemma
1
shows that for a symmetric continuous random variable
X
, a corresponding strength link function can be expressed as
\phi(x)=\log\left(\frac{F(x)}{1-F(x)}\right)
, where
F(x)
is the cumulative distribution function (CDF) of
X
. This result offers a flexible approach for constructing strength link functions based on commonly used distributions, such as the logistic and standard normal distributions. Furthermore, it serves as a key element in establishing connections between the proposed comparison model and classical binary paired comparison models, including the BTL and TM models. These connections will be further explored in Section
2.4
.

2.2
Probabilities over Preference Strength Levels

In this section, we aim to examine the probabilities associated with different levels of preference strength using three real-world datasets from the domains of sports tournaments, recommender systems, and large language models. Understanding whether more extreme comparison outcomes are more likely to occur is crucial for developing a practical ordinal comparison model.

The first dataset we analyze the absolute point differences of the NBA 2023-2024 season game results. Here, the absolute point differences between competing teams were used to define multiple discrete strength levels for paired comparison modeling. Specifically, the absolute score differences were grouped into intervals representing varying degrees of margin of victory. These intervals—ranging from close games (e.g., 1 to 7 points) to large blowouts (e.g., 42 or more points)—serve as ordinal strength levels that quantify the extent of dominance by the winning team.

The second dataset we utilize is the MovieLens 100K dataset
(
Harper and Konstan 2015
)
, in which user ratings for movies are recorded on a 1–5 scale. For each individual user, we compute the pairwise differences between the ratings of all movies they have rated, treating these non-zero differences as indicators of relative preference strength between items. Aggregating over all users, we regard these differences as instances of ordinal pairwise comparisons. To analyze the structure of such comparisons, we further take the absolute values of the rating differences and construct an empirical distribution, which reflects the frequency of different levels of preference intensity observed in the dataset.

The third dataset we consider is the UltraFeedback dataset
(
Cui et al. 2023
)
. In this dataset, the authors employed GPT-4 to assign 5-point ratings across multiple aspects to different answers generated by various LLMs for the same prompt. For each prompt, we compute the rating difference between two answers and treat the result as ordinal comparison data. The rating differences range from 0.25 to 4 and are categorized into five equally spaced intervals of width 0.75, each representing a distinct degree of preference strength.

This empirical pattern suggests that a well-founded ordinal pairwise comparison model should incorporate the property that the probability of an outcome decreases as its magnitude increases. This observation serves as a key motivation for the ordinal comparison modeling framework developed in the subsequent sections.

2.3
Ordinal Comparison Model

In this section, we propose the general discrete comparison model on top of the proposed strength link function in Section
2.1
and observation in Section
2.2
.

Let
\gamma
denote the preference difference between two items under comparison, and let
k\in\Upsilon(K)
represent a possible ordinal outcome of the comparison. We define the propensity function
g_{\phi,\psi}(\gamma,k)
in the form of a generalized additive model:

\displaystyle\textbf{Propensity Function:}\quad g_{\phi,\psi}(\gamma,k)=\phi(\operatorname{sign}(k)\gamma)+\psi(k),

where
\psi
is an even function that controls the effect of the magnitude of the ordinal outcome. For a positive
k
, the function
g_{\phi,\psi}(\gamma,k)
increases with
\gamma
, indicating that the tendency to observe outcome
k
increases as the preference difference between the two items grows. Furthermore, the function
\psi(k)
is introduced to capture the phenomenon that more extreme comparison outcomes are less likely to occur.

Let
G(\phi,\psi,\gamma,K)
denote the general discrete comparison model parametrized by
\phi
,
\psi
,
\gamma
, and a positive integer
K\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
specifying the range of outputs. Given that
Y\sim G(\phi,\psi,\gamma,K)
, the probability of
Y=k
is given as

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(Y=k\right)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\Psi_{\phi,\psi}(\gamma)}\exp\left(g_{\phi,\psi}(\gamma,k)\right),

(2)

for any
k\in\Upsilon(K)
, where
\Psi_{\phi,\psi}(\gamma)=\sum_{k\in\Upsilon(K)}\exp(g_{\phi,\psi}(\gamma,k))
is a normalizing constant.

The model in (
2
) is designed to capture preference differences in ordinal comparison settings, such as sports games and consumer surveys. In sports, outcomes often take symmetric, discrete values excluding zero—commonly seen in games like badminton, tennis, and football. In this framework, the parameter
\gamma
denotes the strength difference between two teams or players, with larger values indicating greater disparity. The strength link function
\phi
governs how
\gamma
influences the distribution of outcomes. The value of
K
is context-dependent. For instance, in badminton, a game is won by reaching 21 points with at least a two-point margin, implying a maximum score difference of 21; thus,
K
is set to 21. In consumer surveys, as shown in Figure
1
, respondents may choose from four options: strongly agree, somewhat agree, somewhat disagree, and strongly disagree. This corresponds to the case where
K=2
.

In the following theorem, we highlight several noteworthy properties inherent in the proposed discrete comparison model.

Theorem 1
.

If
Y\sim G(\phi,\psi,\gamma,K)
, then
Y
possesses the following properties:

(1)

The probability of
Y
being positive is

\mathbb{P}(Y>0)=\frac{\exp(\phi(\gamma))}{\exp(\phi(-\gamma))+\exp(\phi(\gamma))}=\begin{cases}\frac{e^{\gamma}}{1+e^{\gamma}},\text{ if }\phi(\gamma)=\frac{\gamma}{2},\\
\Phi(\gamma),\text{ if }\phi(\gamma)=\frac{1}{2}\log\left(\frac{\Phi(\gamma)}{1-\Phi(\gamma)}\right).\end{cases}

Particularly, if
\gamma=0
, then
\mathbb{P}(Y>0)=\mathbb{P}(Y<0)
for any
\phi
and
\psi
.

(2)

The random variable
-Y
follows
G(\phi,\psi,-\gamma,K)
.

(3)

The mean and variance of
Y
are given as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(Y)=\tanh(\phi(\gamma))\cdot\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{K}ke^{\psi(k)}}{\sum_{k=1}^{K}e^{\psi(k)}},

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}(Y)=\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{K}k^{2}e^{\psi(k)}}{\sum_{k=1}^{K}e^{\psi(k)}}-\left(\tanh\big(\phi(\gamma)\big)\cdot\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{K}ke^{\psi(k)}}{\sum_{k=1}^{K}e^{\psi(k)}}\right)^{2}.

The corresponding signal-to-noise ratio is given as

\displaystyle\textnormal{SNR}(Y)=\frac{[\mathbb{E}(Y)]^{2}}{\mathrm{Var}(Y)}=\frac{\tanh^{2}(\phi(\gamma))}{\frac{1}{\textnormal{SNR}(X)}+1-\tanh^{2}(\phi(\gamma))},

where
X\sim\textnormal{Geo}(\psi,K)
. In particular, when
K=1
, we have
\mathrm{SNR}(X)=\infty
, which implies that
\mathrm{SNR}(Y)=\sinh^{2}(\phi(\gamma))
.

In Theorem
1
, property (1) characterizes the probability that
Y
is positive under the proposed model. This property is particularly important in scenarios where
Y
represents the outcome of a comparison between two items. For example, if
Y
denotes the score difference between teams
i
and
j
, then
\mathbb{P}(Y>0)
corresponds to the probability that team
i
defeats team
j
. Moreover, property (1) serves as a crucial bridge between the proposed model and existing models that consider only binary comparisons. Property (2) establishes the symmetry of
Y
with respect to
\gamma
. This is especially relevant when
\gamma
reflects the notational worth disparity between two items, indicating that reversing the sign of
\gamma
should invert the likelihood of the comparison outcome. Property (3) provides explicit expressions for the expectation and variance of
Y
, offering insight into the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) of the model. Notably, when
K=1
, the SNR of
Y
reaches its maximum, given by
\sinh^{2}(\phi(\gamma))
.

2.4
Ordinal Comparison Graph

In the context of comparison data, it is commonly assumed that there exists a true preference vector, denoted by
\bm{\theta}^{\star}=(\theta_{1}^{\star},\ldots,\theta_{n}^{\star})^{T}
. A higher value of
\theta_{i}^{\star}
relative to
\theta_{j}^{\star}
(i.e.,
\theta_{i}^{\star}>\theta_{j}^{\star}
) indicates that item
i
is ranked higher than item
j
in the ground-truth ordering. Let
y_{ij}^{(l)}
denote the observed preference difference between items
i
and
j
in the
l
-th comparison. We assume that this observation follows the distribution

y_{ij}^{(l)}\sim G(\phi,\psi,\theta_{i}^{\star}-\theta_{j}^{\star},K),

where the distribution
G
depends on the strength link function
\phi
, the pattern function
\psi
, the preference difference
\gamma_{ij}^{\star}=\theta_{i}^{\star}-\theta_{j}^{\star}
, and the number of ordinal levels
K
. Specifically, the probability mass function takes the form

\mathbb{P}\left(y_{ij}^{(l)}=k\right)=\Psi_{\phi,\psi}^{-1}(\gamma_{ij}^{\star})\exp\left(g_{\phi,\psi}(\gamma_{ij}^{\star},k)\right),\quad\text{for }k\in\Upsilon(K),

where
y_{ij}^{(l)}=k
indicates that item
i
is preferred to item
j
by
k
ordinal levels.

Similar to the BTL model, the optimality of the parameter vector
\bm{\theta}^{\star}
in the proposed model is not unique, given that the values of
\gamma_{ij}^{\star}
remain unchanged with any translation of
\bm{\theta}^{\star}
. To ensure the uniqueness of
\bm{\theta}^{\star}
, we require
\bm{1}_{n}^{T}\bm{\theta}^{\star}=0
as existing literature
(
Liu et al. 2023
;
Fan et al. 2025
)
.

Theorem 2
.

Suppose that
Y_{ij}\sim G(\phi,\psi,\theta_{i}^{\star}-\theta_{j}^{\star},1)
for some even functions
\psi
. The proposed model reduces to the following binary pairwise comparison models:

(1)

(BTL model) If
\phi(x)=\frac{1}{2}\log\left(\frac{\sigma(x)}{1-\sigma(x)}\right)
with
\sigma(x)=\frac{e^{x}}{1+e^{x}}
being the CDF of logistic distribution, then we have
\mathbb{P}(Y_{ij}=1)=\frac{e^{\theta_{i}^{\star}}}{e^{\theta_{i}^{\star}}+e^{\theta_{j}^{\star}}}
.

(2)

(Thurstone-Mosteller model) If
\phi(x)=\frac{1}{2}\log\left(\frac{\Phi(x)}{1-\Phi(x)}\right)
with
\Phi(x)=\int_{-\infty}^{x}(2\pi)^{-1/2}e^{-x^{2}/2}dx
being the CDF of standard normal distribution, then we have
\mathbb{P}(Y_{ij}=1)=\Phi(\theta_{i}^{\star}-\theta_{j}^{\star})
.

In Theorem
2
, we present the connections of the proposed model to the BTL model and TM model when
K=1
under specific choices of
\phi
, which are two particular cases of the class of strength link functions specified according to Lemma
1
.

3  Is Ordinal Comparison Data Always Better?

In this section, we investigate whether ordinal comparison data or binary comparison data is more effective for inferring the true ranking of items. Recall that
\gamma_{ij}^{\star}=\theta_{i}^{\star}-\theta_{j}^{\star}
, we consider the setting where
y_{ij}^{(l)}\sim G(\phi,\psi,\gamma_{ij}^{\star},K)
represents an ordinal comparison, and its binarized counterpart
\mathop{\mathrm{sign}}(y_{ij}^{(l)})\sim G(\phi,0,\gamma_{ij}^{\star},1)
, as described in Theorem
1
. In other words, for any
\phi
, we have

\displaystyle\underbrace{y_{ij}^{(l)}\sim G(\phi,\psi,\gamma_{ij}^{\star},K)}_{\text{Ordinal Comparison}}\,\xRightarrow{\text{Binarization}}\,\underbrace{\mathop{\mathrm{sign}}(y_{ij}^{(l)})\sim G(\phi,0,\gamma_{ij}^{\star},1)}_{\text{Binary Comparison}}.

A specific example of the above process is when
\phi=\frac{1}{2}\log\frac{\sigma(x)}{1-\sigma(x)}
, in which case
\mathop{\mathrm{sign}}(y_{12}^{(l)})
essentially follows the BTL model. While binarization intuitively results in information loss—making binary comparison data seemingly less informative for recovering the true ranking—we will demonstrate that this intuition does not always hold. To illustrate this point, we begin with a warm-up analysis of the two-item ranking problem using the counting method
(
Shah and Wainwright 2018
)
, and then extend the discussion to the setting of full ranking recovery.

3.1
Two-item Ranking Problem

As a warm-up, we consider the two-item ranking problem. Let there be two items with preference parameters
\bm{\theta}^{\star}=(\theta_{1}^{\star},\theta_{2}^{\star})
. Without loss of generality, assume
\theta_{1}^{\star}>\theta_{2}^{\star}
, implying that item 1 is more preferred than item 2. The central goal is to recover the ranking implied by
\bm{\theta}^{\star}
. One of the simplest approaches to this task is the counting algorithm
(
Shah and Wainwright 2018
)
, a count-based method shown to be optimal under minimal assumptions on the pairwise comparison process. Suppose we observe a full comparison dataset
\{y_{12}^{(l)}\}_{l=1}^{L}
. We also consider its binarized counterpart
\{z_{12}^{(l)}\}_{l=1}^{L}
, where
z_{12}^{(l)}=\mathop{\mathrm{sign}}(y_{12}^{(l)})
indicates whether item 1 is preferred to item 2 in the
l
-th comparison.

The count-based method then derive the ordering in preference between items 1 and 2 based on the following metrics:

Count Score using Raw Data:

\displaystyle A=\frac{1}{L}\sum_{l=1}^{L}y_{12}^{(l)},

Count Score using Binarized Data:

\displaystyle B=\frac{1}{L}\sum_{l=1}^{L}z_{12}^{(l)},

where
A
and
B
represent the accumulated pairwise rewards of item
1
based on the original discrete dataset and its binarized counterpart, respectively. Here,
A>0
indicates that item 1 has a higher accumulated score out of the
L
comparisons, implying that item 1 is preferred. Similarly,
B>0
means that more than half of the individuals choose item 1 over item 2, also indicating a preference for item 1. We are interested in which metric is more likely to provide correct preference ranking of items. This problem reduces to analyzing
\mathbb{P}(A>0)
and
\mathbb{P}(B>0)
.

Theorem 3
.

Suppose that
y_{12}^{(l)}\sim G\big(\phi,\psi,\theta_{1}^{\star}-\theta_{2}^{\star},K\big)
with
\theta_{1}^{\star}>\theta_{2}^{\star}
, and let
X\sim\mathrm{Geo}(\psi,K)
for any
K\geq 2
. Then, as
L\to\infty
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(B>0)

\displaystyle\to\Phi\left(\sqrt{L}\sinh\big(\phi(\theta_{1}^{\star}-\theta_{2}^{\star})\big)\right),

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(A>0)

\displaystyle\to\Phi\left(\frac{\sqrt{L}\tanh\big(\phi(\theta_{1}^{\star}-\theta_{2}^{\star})\big)}{\sqrt{\frac{1}{\mathrm{SNR}(X)}+1-\tanh^{2}\big(\phi(\theta_{1}^{\star}-\theta_{2}^{\star})\big)}}\right),

where
\Phi
is the standard normal cumulative distribution function. Moreover, it holds that

\frac{\tanh\big(\phi(\theta_{1}^{\star}-\theta_{2}^{\star})\big)}{\sqrt{\frac{1}{\mathrm{SNR}(X)}+1-\tanh^{2}\big(\phi(\theta_{1}^{\star}-\theta_{2}^{\star})\big)}}=\frac{\sinh\big(\phi(\theta_{1}^{\star}-\theta_{2}^{\star})\big)}{\sqrt{1+\frac{1}{\mathrm{SNR}(X)\left[1-\tanh^{2}(\phi(\theta_{1}^{\star}-\theta_{2}^{\star}))\right]}}}\leq\sinh\big(\phi(\theta_{1}^{\star}-\theta_{2}^{\star})\big),

with equality holding if and only if
\mathrm{SNR}(X)=\infty
. Here,
\mathrm{SNR}(X)=\infty
indicates that
X
is a degenerate distribution concentrated at a single point.

Theorem
3
characterizes the limiting behavior of the probabilities
\mathbb{P}(A>0)
and
\mathbb{P}(B>0)
. Notably, the limit of
\mathbb{P}(B>0)
exceeds that of
\mathbb{P}(A>0)
, and the gap between them depends on the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) of
X
, which is governed by the ordinal value pattern
\psi
. This result implies that, in the asymptotic regime, binarizing ordinal comparison data leads to a faster convergence rate in recovering the true ranking. Although this may appear counterintuitive, the underlying intuition is clear: binarization discards magnitude information but significantly reduces the noise inherent in ordinal responses. In other words, while ordinal comparisons provide more detailed information, they also introduce greater uncertainty—an effect that binarization effectively mitigates. Additionally, there are two cases in which binarization yields significant improvement.

Case 1
:

When
\text{SNR}(X)
is small, the gap between the limiting values of
\mathbb{P}(A>0)
and
\mathbb{P}(B>0)
increases. This suggests that binarization can be particularly beneficial when the distribution of ordinal values exhibits high variance. This conclusion is further supported by our simulation results in
Scenario II
.

Case 2
:

When
\theta_{1}^{\star}\gg\theta_{2}^{\star}
, the quantity
\tanh^{2}\big(\phi(\theta_{1}^{\star}-\theta_{2}^{\star})\big)
approaches 1, thereby amplifying the gap between the limiting values of
\mathbb{P}(A>0)
and
\mathbb{P}(B>0)
. This indicates that binarization can be particularly effective for relatively easy ranking recovery tasks.

Theorem
3
establishes that the limiting value of
\mathbb{P}(B>0)
is greater than that of
\mathbb{P}(A>0)
. However, this result does not provide a direct comparison of the actual magnitudes of
\mathbb{P}(B>0)
and
\mathbb{P}(A>0)
in finite-sample settings, as approximation errors persist and it remains unclear which probability is more affected. To address this, we theoretically analyze their convergence rates and establish Theorem
4
.

Theorem 4
.

For
K\geq 2
, suppose that
y_{12}^{(l)}\sim G\big(\phi,\psi,\theta_{1}^{\star}-\theta_{2}^{\star},K\big)
with
\theta_{1}^{\star}>\theta_{2}^{\star}
, and let
X\sim\mathrm{Geo}(\psi,K)
be non-degenerate. Then,

\displaystyle\lim_{L\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1-\mathbb{P}(B>0)}{1-\mathbb{P}(A>0)}=\lim_{L\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\mathbb{P}(B\leq 0)}{\mathbb{P}(A\leq 0)}=0.

This result implies that there exists a positive integer
L_{0}
such that,

\mathbb{P}(B>0)>\mathbb{P}(A>0)\,\text{ for all }\,L\geq L_{0}.

This result indicates that the counting algorithm based on binarized comparison data outperforms its ordinal counterpart in recovering the correct ranking between the two items.

In Theorem
4
, we show that although both
\mathbb{P}(A>0)
and
\mathbb{P}(B>0)
converge to one as
L
increases, the probability
\mathbb{P}(B\leq 0)
becomes negligible compared to
\mathbb{P}(A\leq 0)
for sufficiently large
L
. This result has a practical implication: when enough comparison data is collected, the probability of incorrectly ranking two items using binary comparison data is significantly smaller than when using ordinal data. Moreover, the threshold
L_{0}
beyond which binarization becomes advantageous depends on the value of
\text{SNR}(X)
. Specifically, when
\text{SNR}(X)
is large, the benefit of binarization only emerges at larger sample sizes. In contrast, when
\text{SNR}(X)
is small, the advantage of using
B
over
A
is more pronounced, and hence a smaller
L
is sufficient for this improvement to manifest. This claim is further supported by the empirical results presented later.

3.2
Multiple-Item Ranking Problem

In this section, we extend the result from Section
3.1
to the full ranking recovery problem for
n
items using the count method. In particular, we show that binarizing the comparison data can also improve full ranking recovery performance compared to using the original ordinal comparisons.

Consider
n
items with preference parameters
\bm{\theta}^{\star}=(\theta_{1}^{\star},\theta_{2}^{\star},\ldots,\theta_{n}^{\star})
. Without loss of generality, we assume
\theta_{1}^{\star}>\theta_{2}^{\star}>\cdots>\theta_{n}^{\star}
, indicating that item 1 is the most preferred. The goal is to recover the full ranking encoded by
\bm{\theta}^{\star}
. A key distinction between the
n
-item ranking problem and the two-item ranking problem is the availability of indirect comparisons. Suppose a full comparison dataset is observed as
\{\bm{y}^{(l)}\}_{l=1}^{L}
with
\bm{y}^{(l)}=(y_{ij}^{(l)})_{i,j\in[n]}
. In the two-item case, we only observe direct comparisons between items 1 and 2. In contrast, in the
n
-item setting, we also observe comparisons such as
y_{13}^{(l)}
and
y_{23}^{(l)}
, which provide indirect evidence about the relative preference between items 1 and 2.

For
\{\bm{y}^{(l)}\}_{l=1}^{L}
, where
\bm{y}^{(l)}=(y_{ij}^{(l)})_{i,j\in[n]}
, we calculate the score of the
i
-th item using the original comparison data and binarized comparison data as follows:

Count Score using Raw Data:

\displaystyle S_{i}=\frac{1}{L}\sum_{j\in[n]\setminus\{i\}}\sum_{l=1}^{L}y_{ij}^{(l)},

Count Score using Binarized Data:

\displaystyle\widetilde{S}_{i}=\frac{1}{L}\sum_{j\in[n]\setminus\{i\}}\sum_{l=1}^{L}\operatorname{sign}(y_{ij}^{(l)}),

where
S_{i}
represents the total pairwise rewards accumulated by item
i
, and
\widetilde{S}_{i}
represents the scaled total number of comparisons won by item
i
.

Let
\bm{S}=(S_{1},\ldots,S_{n})
and
\widetilde{\bm{S}}=(\widetilde{S}_{1},\ldots,\widetilde{S}_{n})
denote the score vectors of
n
items. The expected ranking score vectors are
\bm{S}^{\star}=(S_{1}^{\star},\ldots,S_{n}^{\star})
and
\widetilde{\bm{S}}^{\star}=(\widetilde{S}_{1}^{\star},\ldots,\widetilde{S}_{n}^{\star})
, where
S_{i}^{\star}
and
\widetilde{S}_{i}^{\star}
given as

\displaystyle S_{i}^{\star}=\frac{1}{L}\sum_{j\in[n]\setminus\{i\}}\sum_{l=1}^{L}\mathbb{E}\left(y_{ij}^{(l)}\right)\,\mbox{ and }\,\widetilde{S}_{i}^{\star}=\frac{1}{L}\sum_{j\in[n]\setminus\{i\}}\sum_{l=1}^{L}\mathbb{E}\left(\mathop{\mathrm{sign}}(y_{ij}^{(l)})\right).

(3)

In Theorem
5
, we establish the validity of using either
\widetilde{\bm{S}}
or
\bm{S}
to recover the true item ranking. This is because the expected scores
\bm{S}^{\star}
and
\widetilde{\bm{S}}^{\star}
are both consistent with
\bm{\theta}^{\star}
in terms of ranking. As the sample size
L
increases,
\bm{S}
and
\widetilde{\bm{S}}
converge to their respective expectations, thereby ensuring consistent ranking recovery.

Theorem 5

(
Ranking Consistency
)
.

Both
\bm{S}^{\star}
and
\widetilde{\bm{S}}^{\star}
are consistent with
\bm{\theta}^{\star}
in terms of ranking, meaning that for any
i\neq j
,

(S_{i}^{\star}-S_{j}^{\star})(\theta_{i}^{\star}-\theta_{j}^{\star})>0\quad\text{and}\quad(\widetilde{S}_{i}^{\star}-\widetilde{S}_{j}^{\star})(\theta_{i}^{\star}-\theta_{j}^{\star})>0.

In what follows, we investigate whether
\bm{S}
or
\widetilde{\bm{S}}
yields more accurate rankings. To this end, we assess ranking performance using the Kendall tau distance
(
Kendall 1938
)
, following standard practice in the literature
(
Xu et al. 2025
;
Chen et al. 2022a
)
. Specifically, we measure the Kendall tau distance between the rankings induced by
\bm{S}
and
\widetilde{\bm{S}}
and the true ranking
\bm{\theta}^{\star}
by calculating

\displaystyle\tau(\bm{S},\bm{\theta}^{\star})=\frac{2}{m(m-1)}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq m}\mathbb{I}\left[\big(S_{i}-S_{j}\big)\big(\theta_{i}^{\star}-\theta_{j}^{\star}\big)\leq 0\right],

\displaystyle\tau(\widetilde{\bm{S}},\bm{\theta}^{\star})=\frac{2}{m(m-1)}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq m}\mathbb{I}\left[\big(\widetilde{S}_{i}-\widetilde{S}_{j}\big)\big(\theta_{i}^{\star}-\theta_{j}^{\star}\big)\leq 0\right],

where
\mathbb{I}(\cdot)
represents the indicator function. Here,
\tau(\bm{S},\bm{\theta}^{\star})
denotes the proportion of item pairs that are misranked by
\bm{S}
relative to the ground truth ranking
\bm{\theta}^{\star}
. The case of
\tau(\bm{S},\bm{\theta}^{\star})=0
indicates perfect ranking agreement, meaning that
\big(S_{i}-S_{j}\big)\big(\theta_{i}^{\star}-\theta_{j}^{\star}\big)>0
for all
i\neq j
.

To assess whether
\widetilde{\bm{S}}
yields more accurate rankings than
\bm{S}
, we first analyze the asymptotic limits of the expected Kendall tau distances
\mathbb{E}[\tau(\bm{S},\bm{\theta}^{\star})]
and
\mathbb{E}[\tau(\widetilde{\bm{S}},\bm{\theta}^{\star})]
in Theorem
6
.

Theorem 6
.

Suppose that
y_{ij}^{(l)}\sim G\big(\phi,\psi,\theta_{i}^{\star}-\theta_{j}^{\star},K\big)
with
\theta_{i}^{\star}>\theta_{j}^{\star}
for
i<j
, and let
X\sim\mathrm{Geo}(\psi,K)
for
K\geq 2
. Then, as
L\to\infty
, the expected Kendall tau distance satisfies

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\tau(\bm{S},\bm{\theta}^{\star})\right]

\displaystyle\to\frac{2}{m(m-1)}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq m}\Phi\left(-\frac{\sqrt{2nL}\,\bar{D}_{ij}}{\sqrt{\,\frac{1}{\textnormal{SNR}(X)}+1-\bar{V}_{ij}}}\right),

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\tau(\widetilde{\bm{S}},\bm{\theta}^{\star})\right]

\displaystyle\to\frac{2}{m(m-1)}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq m}\Phi\left(-\frac{\sqrt{2nL}\,\bar{D}_{ij}}{\sqrt{\,1-\bar{V}_{ij}}}\right),

where, for
i<j
,

\displaystyle\bar{D}_{ij}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2n}\left[2\tanh\big(\phi(\gamma_{ij}^{\star})\big)+\sum_{k\neq i,j}\left(\tanh\big(\phi(\gamma_{ik}^{\star})\big)-\tanh\big(\phi(\gamma_{jk}^{\star})\big)\right)\right],

\displaystyle\bar{V}_{ij}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2n}\left[4\tanh^{2}\big(\phi(\gamma_{ij}^{\star})\big)+\sum_{k\neq i,j}\left(\tanh^{2}\big(\phi(\gamma_{ik}^{\star})\big)+\tanh^{2}\big(\phi(\gamma_{jk}^{\star})\big)\right)\right].

In Theorem
6
, we characterize the limiting ranking errors of
\mathbb{E}\left[\tau(\bm{S},\bm{\theta}^{\star})\right]
and
\mathbb{E}\left[\tau(\widetilde{\bm{S}},\bm{\theta}^{\star})\right]
. Notably, similar to the two-item ranking case, there exists a performance gap between these two limits. As long as
X
is non-degenerate (indicating
\text{SNR}(X)>0
), the limiting value of
\mathbb{E}\big[\tau(\widetilde{\bm{S}},\bm{\theta}^{\star})\big]
is strictly smaller than that of
\mathbb{E}\big[\tau(\bm{S},\bm{\theta}^{\star})\big]
, indicating that binary comparisons offer superior asymptotic efficiency for ranking recovery. Moreover, this performance gap is influenced by
\text{SNR}(X)
in the sense that a smaller
\text{SNR}(X)
leads to a larger advantage for binarized comparisons.

Nevertheless, Theorem
6
only establishes the existence of a gap between the asymptotic limits of the two ranking errors; it does not ensure that
\mathbb{E}\big[\tau(\widetilde{\bm{S}},\bm{\theta}^{\star})\big]
is smaller than
\mathbb{E}\big[\tau(\bm{S},\bm{\theta}^{\star})\big]
in finite-sample settings. To address this limitation, we analyze the convergence rates of both quantities in Theorem
7
, showing that
\mathbb{E}\big[\tau(\widetilde{\bm{S}},\bm{\theta}^{\star})\big]
converges faster than
\mathbb{E}\big[\tau(\bm{S},\bm{\theta}^{\star})\big]
. A direct implication of this result is that, for sufficiently large
L
, ranking recovery based on binarized comparison data consistently outperforms that based on ordinal comparison data.

Theorem 7
.

Define
R(\widetilde{\bm{S}},\bm{S})\triangleq\frac{\mathbb{E}[\tau(\widetilde{\bm{S}},\bm{\theta}^{\star})]}{\mathbb{E}[\tau(\bm{S},\bm{\theta}^{\star})]}
. Suppose all assumptions in Theorem
6
hold, it then holds that

\displaystyle\lim_{L\rightarrow\infty}R(\widetilde{\bm{S}},\bm{S})=\lim_{L\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\mathbb{E}\big[\tau(\widetilde{\bm{S}},\bm{\theta}^{\star})\big]}{\mathbb{E}\big[\tau(\bm{S},\bm{\theta}^{\star})\big]}=0.

(4)

Furthermore, there exists a positive integer
L_{1}
such that

\mathbb{E}\big[\tau(\widetilde{\bm{S}},\bm{\theta}^{\star})\big]<\mathbb{E}\big[\tau(\bm{S},\bm{\theta}^{\star})\big],\text{ for all }L\geq L_{1},

implying that the counting algorithm based on binarized comparison data outperforms its ordinal counterpart in recovering the ranking of
n
items.

4  Sigal-to-Noise Ratio Analysis

Based on the analysis presented in Section
3
, the SNR of
X\sim\mathrm{Geo}(\psi,K)
emerges as a critical factor in determining the extent to which binarizing ordinal comparison data enhances ranking performance. As established in Theorems
3
and
6
, the asymptotic performance gap between the count-based ranking methods applied to binarized versus full ordinal data increases as
\mathrm{SNR}(X)
decreases. That is, the benefit of binarization is most pronounced when
\text{SNR}(X)
is minimized. This observation naturally motivates the following question:

\displaystyle\text{What choice of }\psi(k)\text{ minimizes }\mathrm{SNR}(X)?

The minimal value of
\mathrm{SNR}(X)
corresponds to the maximal relative gain achieved by employing binarized comparison data instead of full ordinal comparison data.

To address the above question, we consider two distinct scenarios:
(1) minimizing
\mathrm{SNR}(X)
without any constraints on
\psi(k)
, and
(2) minimizing
\mathrm{SNR}(X)
under the assumption that
\psi(k)
is non-increasing in
k
. The second scenario is motivated by the empirical observation in Section
2.2
, where more extreme ordinal comparisons are found to be less frequent in real datasets. Accordingly, we impose the constraint that
\mathbb{P}(X=k)\geq\mathbb{P}(X=k+1)
for all
k\in[K-1]
and investigate the minimal
\mathrm{SNR}(X)
under this monotonicity condition.

Theorem 8

(
Minimal
\mathrm{SNR}(X)
without Constraints
)
.

Suppose
X\sim\mathrm{Geo}(\psi,K)
. Then the signal-to-noise ratio of
X
satisfies

\mathrm{SNR}(X)\geq\frac{4K}{(K-1)^{2}},

with the equality holding if and only if

\psi(k)=\begin{cases}C+\log\left(\dfrac{K}{K+1}\right),&\text{if }k=1,\\
C+\log\left(\dfrac{1}{K+1}\right),&\text{if }k=K,\\
-\infty,&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

for any constant
C\in\mathbb{R}
. This choice of
\psi
corresponds to a two-point distribution of
X
supported on
\{1,K\}
.

For the first scenario, we establish Theorem
8
, which characterizes the minimal SNR achievable by
X
without imposing structural constraints on
\psi
. Interestingly, the minimal SNR is closely tied to the maximum category
K
of the ordinal comparison: as
K
increases, the minimal SNR decreases. This result suggests that in practice, when the number of categories in ordinal comparison data is large, binarizing the ordinal responses may lead to greater improvements in the asymptotic regime.

However, the minimal SNR is attained when
\psi(k)=-\infty
for all
k\notin\{1,K\}
. Plugging this choice of
\psi
into the proposed model
g_{\phi,\psi}
yields an ordinal comparison model that generates responses only from the set
\{-K,-1,1,K\}
. This implies that users provide only extreme responses (
\pm K
) or mild responses (
\pm 1
), omitting intermediate levels. Such behavior may not align with the patterns typically observed in real-world ordinal datasets (Section
2.2
).

A more practical approach to ordinal comparison should preserve the non-increasing pattern of probabilities. To this end, we impose an additional constraint on
\psi
, requiring that
\psi(1)\geq\psi(2)\geq\cdots\geq\psi(K)
. This ensures that
\mathbb{P}(X=k)\geq\mathbb{P}(X=k+1)
if
X\sim\text{Geo}(\psi,K)
. Under this monotonicity constraint, the minimal SNR is characterized in Theorem
9
.

Theorem 9

(
Minimal
\mathrm{SNR}(X)
with non-increasing

\psi
)
.

Suppose
X\sim\mathrm{Geo}(\psi,K)
, where
\psi(i)\geq\psi(j)
for
i<j
. Then the signal-to-noise ratio satisfies

\mathrm{SNR}(X)\geq\frac{24(K+1)}{4K^{2}-4K+1},

with the equality holding if and only if

\psi(k)=\begin{cases}C+\log\left(\frac{(2K^{2}+K+2)(K-1)}{2(2K-1)}\right),&\text{if }k=1,\\
C,&\text{if }k\in\{2,\ldots,K\},\end{cases}

for any constant
C\in\mathbb{R}
. This choice of
\psi
corresponds to the distribution of
X
uniformly supported on
\{2,\ldots,K\}
.

In Theorem
9
, we show that under the non-increasing constraint, the minimal SNR achievable by
X\sim\text{Geo}(\psi,K)
is
\frac{24(K+1)}{4K^{2}-4K+1}
. This minimal SNR is attained when
X
is uniform over
{2,\ldots,K}
with additional mass placed at
X=1
. It is worth noting that when
K=2
, Theorems
8
and
9
yield the same distribution and minimal SNR.

To highlight the distinction between the two cases, we present several illustrative examples in Figure
6
. Specifically, for each
K\in\{2,4,8\}
, the figure displays the distributions of
X
that achieve the minimal SNR, both without constraints and under the non-increasing pattern constraint on
\psi
.

5.2
Real Application

In this section, we conducted our analysis using the MovieLens dataset
(
Harper and Konstan 2015
)
, which comprises user–movie ratings collected from a large-scale movie recommendation platform. Each record in the dataset includes a user identifier, a movie identifier, a discrete rating score, and a timestamp indicating when the rating was provided. To ensure statistical reliability in subsequent analyses, we focused on movies that attracted substantial user engagement. Specifically, we filtered the dataset to retain only those movies rated at least 200 times. This threshold helps exclude movies with insufficient rating data, which could introduce noise and undermine robustness. For each user in the filtered dataset, we constructed pairwise rating differences between all pairs of popular movies they had rated. Formally, for user
l
and movies
i
and
j
, if both movies were rated by user
l
, we define the pairwise rating difference as ordinal comparison. We retained only pairs with non-zero differences to capture meaningful preferences that distinguish between the two movies. This procedure yields a dataset of pairwise preference observations, encoding the relative strength of user preferences between pairs of popular movies.

We randomly partition the observed pairwise comparisons into a training set (70%) and a test set (30%). 6  Summary

This paper investigates the performance gap between ordinal and binary comparison data in ranking recovery using the counting method. To this end, we propose a general parametric framework for modeling ordinal paired comparisons without ties. When binary responses are interpreted as binarized versions of ordinal data, the framework naturally reduces to classical binary comparison models. A central finding of our study is that binarizing ordinal data can significantly improve the accuracy of ranking recovery, challenging the common intuition that ordinal comparisons carry more information than binary ones. Specifically, we show that under the counting algorithm, the ranking error associated with binary comparisons converges exponentially faster than that of ordinal data. Moreover, we demonstrate that the performance gap is determined by the pattern of ordinal levels. We identify the pattern that maximizes the benefit of binarization. Our theoretical results are further supported by extensive numerical experiments.

A.1.1
Proof of Theorem

Proof of Property (1)
: From the definition of
G(\phi,\gamma,K)
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(Y=k)=\frac{1}{\Psi_{\phi,\psi}(\gamma)}\exp\left(g_{\phi,\psi}(\gamma,k)\right),\quad k\in\Upsilon(K)\triangleq\{-K,\ldots,-1,1,\ldots,K\},

where
g_{\phi,\psi}(\gamma,k)=\phi(\operatorname{sign}(k)\gamma)+\psi(k)
.

To compute
\mathbb{P}(Y>0)
, we sum over all positive values of
k
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(Y>0)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbb{P}(Y=k)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{1}{\Psi_{\phi,\psi}(\gamma)}\exp\left(\phi(\gamma)+\psi(k)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\Psi_{\phi,\psi}(\gamma)}\exp(\phi(\gamma))\sum_{k=1}^{K}\exp(\psi(k)).

The normalizing constant is

\displaystyle\Psi_{\phi,\psi}(\gamma)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=-K}^{-1}\exp\left(\phi(-\gamma)+\psi(k)\right)+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\exp\left(\phi(\gamma)+\psi(k)\right)

\displaystyle=\exp(\phi(-\gamma))\sum_{k=1}^{K}\exp(\psi(k))+\exp(\phi(\gamma))\sum_{k=1}^{K}\exp(\psi(k))

\displaystyle=\left[\exp(\phi(-\gamma))+\exp(\phi(\gamma))\right]\sum_{k=1}^{K}\exp(\psi(k)).

Therefore, we conclude that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(Y>0)=\frac{\exp(\phi(\gamma))}{\exp(\phi(\gamma))+\exp(\phi(-\gamma))}=\frac{\exp(2\phi(\gamma))}{\exp(2\phi(\gamma))+1)}

According to the definition of
G(\phi,\psi,\gamma,K)
,
\mathop{\mathrm{sign}}(Y)
follows the following distribution

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\mathop{\mathrm{sign}}(Y)=1)=\frac{\exp(2\phi(\gamma))}{\exp(2\phi(\gamma))+1}.

Therefore,
\mathop{\mathrm{sign}}(Y)
can be viewed as following
G(\phi,0,\gamma,1)
. This then implies the desired result.

Proof of Property (2)
: Next, by the definition of
G(\phi,\gamma,K)
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(-Y=k)=\mathbb{P}(Y=-k)=\left[\Psi_{\phi,\psi}(\gamma)\right]^{-1}\exp(g_{\phi,\psi}(\gamma,-k))=\left[\Psi_{\phi,\psi}(\gamma)\right]^{-1}\exp(g_{\phi,\psi}(-\gamma,k)),

where the last equality follows from the property that
g_{\phi,\psi}(\gamma,-k)=g_{\phi,\psi}(-\gamma,k)
.

Proof of Property (3)
: We first derive the expectation of
Y
.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(Y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in\Upsilon(K)}k\cdot\mathbb{P}(Y=k)=\frac{1}{\Psi_{\phi,\psi}(\gamma)}\sum_{k\in\Upsilon(K)}k\cdot\exp\left(g_{\phi,\psi}(\gamma,k)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\Psi_{\phi,\psi}(\gamma)}\left[\sum_{k=1}^{K}k\cdot\exp\left(\phi(\gamma)-\beta k\right)+\sum_{k=1}^{K}(-k)\cdot\exp\left(\phi(-\gamma)-\beta k\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\Psi_{\phi,\psi}(\gamma)}\sum_{k=1}^{K}k\left[\exp\left(\phi(\gamma)-\beta k\right)-\exp\left(\phi(-\gamma)-\beta k\right)\right].

We factor out
\exp(\phi(\gamma))
and
\exp(\phi(-\gamma))
and obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(Y)=\frac{1}{\Psi_{\phi,\psi}(\gamma)}\left[\exp(\phi(\gamma))-\exp(\phi(-\gamma))\right]\sum_{k=1}^{K}k\exp(\psi(k)).

Using the expression for the normalizing constant

\displaystyle\Psi_{\phi,\psi}(\gamma)=\left[\exp(\phi(\gamma))+\exp(\phi(-\gamma))\right]\sum_{k=1}^{K}\exp(\psi(k)),

we conclude that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(Y)=\frac{\left[\exp(\phi(\gamma))-\exp(\phi(-\gamma))\right]\cdot\sum_{k=1}^{K}k\exp(\psi(k))}{\left[\exp(\phi(\gamma))+\exp(\phi(-\gamma))\right]\cdot\sum_{k=1}^{K}\exp(\psi(k))}=\tanh(\phi(\gamma))\cdot\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{K}k\exp(\psi(k))}{\sum_{k=1}^{K}\exp(\psi(k))}.

Next, we turn to calculate
\text{Var}(Y)
. We first compute
\mathbb{E}(Y^{2})
.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(Y^{2})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in\Upsilon(K)}k^{2}\cdot\mathbb{P}(Y=k)=\frac{1}{\Psi_{\phi,\psi}(\gamma)}\sum_{k\in\Upsilon(K)}k^{2}\cdot\exp\left(g_{\phi,\psi}(\gamma,k)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\Psi_{\phi,\psi}(\gamma)}\left[\sum_{k=1}^{K}k^{2}\exp\left(\phi(\gamma)+\psi(k)\right)+\sum_{k=1}^{K}k^{2}\exp\left(\phi(-\gamma)+\psi(k)\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{\left[\exp(\phi(\gamma))+\exp(\phi(-\gamma))\right]\cdot\sum_{k=1}^{K}k^{2}\exp(\psi(k))}{\left[\exp(\phi(\gamma))+\exp(\phi(-\gamma))\right]\cdot\sum_{k=1}^{K}\exp(\psi(k))}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{K}k^{2}\exp(\psi(k))}{\sum_{k=1}^{K}\exp(\psi(k))}.

The expression for
\mathbb{E}(Y^{2})
is symmetric in
\gamma
, because
k^{2}
is even and cancels the asymmetry of
\phi(\gamma)
vs
\phi(-\gamma)
. Recall that

\mathbb{E}(Y)=\frac{\left[\exp(\phi(\gamma))-\exp(\phi(-\gamma))\right]\cdot\sum_{k=1}^{K}k\exp(\psi(k))}{\left[\exp(\phi(\gamma))+\exp(\phi(-\gamma))\right]\cdot\sum_{k=1}^{K}\exp(\psi(k))}.

Then, the variance is given as

\displaystyle\operatorname{Var}(Y)=

\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{K}k^{2}\exp(\psi(k))}{\sum_{k=1}^{K}\exp(\psi(k))}-\left(\frac{\left[\exp(\phi(\gamma))-\exp(\phi(-\gamma))\right]\cdot\sum_{k=1}^{K}k\exp(\psi(k))}{\left[\exp(\phi(\gamma))+\exp(\phi(-\gamma))\right]\cdot\sum_{k=1}^{K}\exp(\psi(k))}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{K}k^{2}\exp(\psi(k))}{\sum_{k=1}^{K}\exp(\psi(k))}-\left(\tanh(\phi(\gamma))\cdot\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{K}k\exp(\psi(k))}{\sum_{k=1}^{K}\exp(\psi(k))}\right)^{2}.

Derivation of SNR
(Y)
.
We define

\mu\triangleq\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{K}ke^{\psi(k)}}{\sum_{k=1}^{K}e^{\psi(k)}},\quad\sigma^{2}\triangleq\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{K}k^{2}e^{\psi(k)}}{\sum_{k=1}^{K}e^{\psi(k)}}-\mu^{2}.

Then the expectation and variance of
Y
are given by

\mathbb{E}(Y)=\tanh(\phi(\gamma))\cdot\mu,\quad\mathrm{Var}(Y)=\sigma^{2}+\mu^{2}(1-\tanh^{2}(\phi(\gamma))).

Hence, the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) of
Y
is

\mathrm{SNR}(Y)=\frac{\mathbb{E}(Y)^{2}}{\mathrm{Var}(Y)}=\frac{\mu^{2}\tanh^{2}(\phi(\gamma))}{\sigma^{2}+\mu^{2}(1-\tanh^{2}(\phi(\gamma)))}.

Using the identity
\mathrm{SNR}(X)\triangleq\mu^{2}/\sigma^{2}
, we can rewrite the above as

\mathrm{SNR}(Y)=\frac{\mathrm{SNR}(X)\cdot\tanh^{2}(\phi(\gamma))}{1+\mathrm{SNR}(X)(1-\tanh^{2}(\phi(\gamma)))}.

Equivalently,

\mathrm{SNR}(Y)=\frac{\tanh^{2}(\phi(\gamma))}{\frac{1}{\mathrm{SNR}(X)}+1-\tanh^{2}(\phi(\gamma))}.

When
K=1
, then
\mathbb{E}(Y)
and
\text{Var}(Y)
become

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(Y)=\tanh(\phi(\gamma))\,\mbox{ and }\,\text{Var}(Y)=1-\tanh^{2}(\phi(\gamma)).

The signal-to-noise ratio is then given as

\displaystyle\frac{[\mathbb{E}(Y)]^{2}}{\text{Var}(Y)}=\frac{\tanh^{2}(\phi(\gamma))}{1-\tanh^{2}(\phi(\gamma))}=\sinh^{2}(\phi(\gamma)).

Since
\text{SNR}(X)
is always non-negative,
\sinh^{2}(\phi(\gamma))
is the maximal value. This completes the whole proof.
\blacksquare

Proof of Theorem
8
.
For the function
\psi
, we denote the corresponding probability mass vector by

\displaystyle\bm{p}(\psi)=(p_{1}(\psi),\ldots,p_{K}(\psi))=\left(\frac{e^{\psi(1)}}{\sum_{k=1}^{K}e^{\psi(k)}},\ldots,\frac{e^{\psi(K)}}{\sum_{k=1}^{K}e^{\psi(k)}}\right),

where each
p_{k}(\psi)
represents the probability mass assigned to category
k
under
\psi
.

To make the dependence of the random variable
X
on
\psi
explicit, we introduce the notation
X(\psi)\sim\mathrm{Geo}(\psi,K)
. By construction,
X(\psi)
takes values in the finite set
\{1,\ldots,K\}
for any choice of
\psi
, and hence its expectation
\mathbb{E}[X(\psi)]
lies in the interval
[1,K]
.

Step 1. Maximum Variance with Mean Constraint.
For a fixed value
s\in[1,K]
, we define the associated class of probability mass vectors
\bm{p}=(p_{1},\ldots,p_{K})
as

\mathcal{H}(s)=\left\{\bm{p}:\sum_{k=1}^{K}p_{k}=1\text{ and }\sum_{k=1}^{K}k\cdot p_{k}=s\right\}.

It is important to note that vectors in
\mathcal{H}(s)
are general probability mass functions, without being restricted to those induced by
\psi
. In contrast,
\bm{p}(\psi)
refers to the subclass of distributions that satisfy the structural constraint imposed by the softmax transformation of
\psi
.

We first consider the problem of determining the maximum variance of a random variable
X_{0}
whose distribution satisfies
\mathbb{P}(X_{0}=k)=p_{k}
for
k\in[K]
, where the probability vector
\bm{p}
belongs to the set
\mathcal{H}(s)
. We aim to maximize the variance of
X_{0}
supported on
\{1,2,\dots,K\}
, under the constraint that its mean is fixed at
s\in[1,K]
. The variance of
X_{0}
is given by

\operatorname{Var}(X_{0})=\mathbb{E}[X_{0}^{2}]-s^{2}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}k^{2}p_{k}-s^{2}.

We thus aim to maximize the second moment
\sum_{k=1}^{K}k^{2}p_{k}
under the constraints

\sum_{k=1}^{K}p_{k}=1,\quad\sum_{k=1}^{K}kp_{k}=\mu,\quad p_{k}\geq 0.

Since this is a convex optimization task with linear constraints, we introduce Lagrange multipliers
\lambda_{1}
and
\lambda_{2}
, and define the Lagrangian as

\mathcal{L}(\bm{p},\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})=\sum_{k=1}^{K}k^{2}p_{k}-\lambda_{1}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K}kp_{k}-\mu\right)-\lambda_{2}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K}p_{k}-1\right).

Taking the partial derivative with respect to
p_{k}
yields that

\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial p_{k}}=k^{2}-\lambda_{1}k-\lambda_{2}=0.

This quadratic equation implies that
p_{k}>0
only if
k
satisfies

k^{2}-\lambda_{1}k-\lambda_{2}=0.

Since this equation has at most two real roots, the optimal distribution must be supported on at most two points. Hence, the maximum is attained by a
two-point distribution
. Therefore, for any
s>0
, the maximum variance
\sum_{k=1}^{k}k^{2}p_{k}
is achieved at a two-point distribution.

Step 2. Closed Form of Variance for Two-point Distribution.
Let the two support points be
a<b\in\{1,2,\dots,K\}
, and suppose
\mathbb{P}(X_{0}=a)=p
,
\mathbb{P}(X_{0}=b)=1-p
. The mean constraint gives:

pa+(1-p)b=s\quad\Rightarrow\quad p=\frac{b-s}{b-a}.

Then,

\mathbb{E}[X_{0}^{2}]=pa^{2}+(1-p)b^{2}=\frac{b-s}{b-a}a^{2}+\frac{s-a}{b-a}b^{2}.

This expression is maximized when
a=1
and
b=K
, giving the maximal second moment:

\mathbb{E}[X_{0}^{2}]=\frac{K-s}{K-1}\cdot 1^{2}+\frac{s-1}{K-1}\cdot K^{2}.

Hence, the maximum variance under the mean constraint is:

\displaystyle\operatorname{Var}(X_{0})=\left(\frac{K-s}{K-1}\cdot 1+\frac{s-1}{K-1}\cdot K^{2}\right)-s^{2}.

(A1)

Step 3. Minimum SNR.
From Step 1 and Step 2, we conclude that
the maximum variance given a fixed mean is achieved by a two-point distribution supported on

\{1,K\}
. Therefore, the
minimum signal-to-noise ratio (SNR)
is also attained by a distribution supported on
\{1,K\}
. Let
K\geq 2
be fixed, and suppose
X_{0}
takes values
1
and
K
with probabilities
\mathbb{P}(X_{0}=1)=p
and
\mathbb{P}(X_{0}=K)=1-p
, respectively. Then the ratio of the second moment and the first moment is

\frac{\mathbb{E}(X_{0}^{2})}{[\mathbb{E}(X_{0})]^{2}}=f(p)=\frac{p+K^{2}(1-p)}{(p+K(1-p))^{2}},\quad p\in(0,1).

Let
q=1-p
, so
p=1-q
and
q\in(0,1)
. Then we obtain a function
g(q)
as

g(q)=\frac{1+(K^{2}-1)q}{(1+(K-1)q)^{2}}.

Set
a=K^{2}-1
and
b=K-1
, so

g(q)=\frac{aq+1}{(bq+1)^{2}}.

To find the maximizer, take the derivative:

g^{\prime}(q)=\frac{a(bq+1)^{2}-2b(aq+1)(bq+1)}{(bq+1)^{4}}.

Set the numerator equal to zero, we have

\displaystyle a(bq+1)^{2}-2b(aq+1)(bq+1)=0\Longrightarrow a(bq+1)-2b(aq+1)=0

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow

\displaystyle abq+a-2abq-2b=0\Longrightarrow-abq+a-2b=0\Longrightarrow abq=a-2b\Longrightarrow q^{\star}=\frac{a-2b}{ab}.

Thus,

p^{\star}=1-q^{\star}=1-\frac{a-2b}{ab}=\frac{ab-a+2b}{ab}.

Recall
a=K^{2}-1
,
b=K-1
, so

\displaystyle ab

\displaystyle=(K^{2}-1)(K-1),

\displaystyle ab-a+2b

\displaystyle=(K^{2}-1)(K-2)+2(K-1),

and hence

\displaystyle p^{\star}

\displaystyle=\frac{(K^{2}-1)(K-2)+2(K-1)}{(K^{2}-1)(K-1)}=\frac{K^{3}-2K^{2}+K}{K^{3}-K^{2}-K+1}=\frac{K}{K+1}.

Substitute
p=\frac{K}{K+1}
into the original function:

\displaystyle f\left(\frac{K}{K+1}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\frac{K}{K+1}+K^{2}\cdot\frac{1}{K+1}}{\left(\frac{K}{K+1}+K\cdot\frac{1}{K+1}\right)^{2}}=\frac{K+K^{2}}{(K+K)^{2}}\cdot(K+1)=\frac{(K+1)^{2}}{4K}.

Therefore, the function

f(p)=\frac{p+K^{2}(1-p)}{(p+K(1-p))^{2}}

achieves its maximum at

p^{\star}=\frac{K}{K+1},\quad\text{with maximum value}\quad f(p^{\star})=\frac{(K+1)^{2}}{4K}.

Hence, the minimum SNR is

\text{SNR}_{min}=\frac{1}{\frac{(K+1)^{2}}{4K}-1}=\frac{4K}{(K-1)^{2}},

ttained uniquely when
X_{0}
is supported on
\{1,K\}
with probability
\frac{K}{K+1}
on
1
and
\frac{1}{K+1}
on
K
.

Step 4. Construction of
\psi
.
Define the function
\psi
as

\displaystyle\psi(k)=\begin{cases}\log\left(\dfrac{K}{K+1}\right),&\text{if }k=1,\\
\log\left(\dfrac{1}{K+1}\right),&\text{if }k=K,\\
-\infty,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Then the distribution induced by
\psi
satisfies

\mathbb{P}(X(\psi)=1)=\frac{e^{\psi(1)}}{e^{\psi(1)}+e^{\psi(K)}}=\frac{K}{K+1},\quad\mathbb{P}(X(\psi)=K)=\frac{e^{\psi(K)}}{e^{\psi(1)}+e^{\psi(K)}}=\frac{1}{K+1}.

This completes the proof.
\blacksquare

Proof of Theorem
9
.
To begin with, for a probability mass vector
\bm{p}=(p_{1},\ldots,p_{K})
, let
X(\bm{p})
denote the discrete random variable such that
\mathbb{P}(X(\bm{p})=k)=p_{k}
. It is worth noting that under the constraint
p_{1}\geq p_{2}\geq\cdots\geq p_{K}
, the expectation of
X(\bm{p})
cannot exceed
\frac{K+1}{2}
, with equality if and only if the distribution is uniform. We further define
\bm{p}(\psi)=(p_{1}(\psi),\ldots,p_{K}(\psi))
as the probability mass vector induced by a real-valued function
\psi
, where
p_{k}(\psi)=\frac{e^{\psi(k)}}{\sum_{j=1}^{K}e^{\psi(j)}}\quad\text{for }k=1,\ldots,K
.

Let
\mathcal{H}(s)
denote the set of all non-increasing probability mass vectors with fixed expectation
s
, defined as

\mathcal{H}(s)=\left\{\bm{p}:\sum_{k=1}^{K}p_{k}=1,\;\sum_{k=1}^{K}k\cdot p_{k}=s,\;\text{and }p_{1}\geq p_{2}\geq\cdots\geq p_{K}\right\}.

For any
s\in[1,\frac{K+1}{2}]
, we define
\bm{p}_{s}^{\star}
as the minimizer of the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) among all distributions in
\mathcal{H}(s)
:

\bm{p}_{s}^{\star}=\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}_{\bm{p}\in\mathcal{H}(s)}\text{SNR}(X(\bm{p})).

Next, we aim to show that
\bm{p}_{s}^{\star}
must satisfy
p^{\star}_{s,2}=p^{\star}_{s,3}=\cdots=p^{\star}_{s,K}
, indicating that
X(\bm{p}_{s}^{\star})
places equal probability mass on the values
\{2,\ldots,K\}
.

Proof by Contradiction.
We assume that
\bm{p}_{s}^{\star}
does not satisfy
p^{\star}_{s,2}=p^{\star}_{s,3}=\cdots=p^{\star}_{s,K}
. This implies that there exists
k\in\{2,\ldots,K\}
such that
p_{s,k}^{\star}>p_{s,k+1}^{\star}
. Then, we define a new mass vector
\bm{p}^{\varepsilon}=(p_{1}^{\varepsilon},\ldots,p_{K}^{\varepsilon})
by transferring mass
\varepsilon
from
p_{s,k}^{\star}
to
p_{s,1}^{\star}
and
p_{s,k+1}^{\star}
as follows:

\displaystyle\text{Construction of $\bm{p}^{\varepsilon}$: }\begin{cases}p_{k}^{\varepsilon}=p_{s,k}^{\star}-\varepsilon,\\
p_{1}^{\varepsilon}=p_{s,1}^{\star}+\dfrac{\varepsilon}{k},\\
p_{k+1}^{\varepsilon}=p_{s,k+1}^{\star}+\left(1-\dfrac{1}{k}\right)\varepsilon,\\
p_{i}^{\varepsilon}=p_{s,i}^{\star}\quad\text{for }i\notin\{1,k,k+1\}.\end{cases}

Step 1: Total Probability is Preserved

\sum_{i=1}^{K}p_{i}^{\varepsilon}=\sum_{i=1}^{K}p_{s,i}^{\star}-\varepsilon+\frac{\varepsilon}{k}+\left(1-\frac{1}{k}\right)\varepsilon=\sum_{i=1}^{K}p_{i}=1.

Here, at the new probability mass vector, we can choose
\varepsilon
sufficiently small so that the following inequality is preserved.

\displaystyle p_{1}^{\varepsilon}\geq p_{2}^{\varepsilon}\geq\cdots\geq p_{K}^{\varepsilon}.

Step 2: Expectation is Preserved

Note that
\mathbb{E}[X(\bm{p}_{s}^{\star})]=s
. Then:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X(\bm{p}^{\varepsilon})]

\displaystyle=s-\varepsilon\cdot k+\dfrac{\varepsilon}{k}\cdot 1+\left(1-\dfrac{1}{k}\right)\varepsilon\cdot(k+1)

\displaystyle=s+\varepsilon\left(\dfrac{1}{k}-k+\left(1-\dfrac{1}{k}\right)(k+1)\right)=s.

This shows that
\bm{p}^{\varepsilon}\in\mathcal{H}(s)
.

Step 3: Variance Increases

We consider the change in second moment:

\displaystyle\delta_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left\{[X(\bm{p}^{\varepsilon})]^{2}\right\}-\mathbb{E}\left\{[X(\bm{p}_{s}^{\star})]^{2}\right\}

\displaystyle=-\varepsilon\cdot k^{2}+\frac{\varepsilon}{k}\cdot 1^{2}+\left(1-\frac{1}{k}\right)\varepsilon\cdot(k+1)^{2}

\displaystyle=\varepsilon\left[-k^{2}+\frac{1}{k}+\left(1-\frac{1}{k}\right)(k+1)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{\varepsilon}{k}\left[-k^{3}+1+(k-1)(k^{2}+2k+1)\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{\varepsilon}{k}\left(k^{2}-k\right)=\epsilon(k-1)>0,

for any
k\geq 2
. One can verify

\displaystyle\text{SNR}(X(\bm{p}^{\varepsilon}))-\text{SNR}(X(\bm{p}_{s}^{\star}))=\frac{s^{2}}{\mathbb{E}\left\{[X(\bm{p}^{\varepsilon})]^{2}\right\}-s^{2}}-\frac{s^{2}}{\mathbb{E}\left\{[X(\bm{p}_{s}^{\star})]^{2}\right\}-s^{2}}<0

Because the perturbation
\varepsilon
only changes three coordinates slightly, strict monotonicity is preserved for sufficiently small
\varepsilon
. This contradicts with the assumption that
\bm{p}_{s}^{\star}
minimizes
\text{SNR}(X(\bm{p}))
within
\mathcal{H}(s)
. Therefore, we conclude that for any
s\in[1,\frac{K+1}{2}]
, the minimal SNR achievable over
\mathcal{H}(s)
must satisfy

p_{s,2}^{\star}=p_{s,3}^{\star}=\cdots=p_{s,K}^{\star}.

Therefore, the optimal
\bm{p}
that minimizes
\text{SNR}(X(\bm{p}))
must also satisfy this condition.

Next, we intend to investigate which
\bm{p}=(p_{1},\ldots,p_{k})
with
p_{2}=\ldots=p_{K}
gives the minimum
\text{SNR}(X(\bm{p}))
. For a
\bm{p}
, let
X(\bm{p})
be a discrete random variable supported on
\{1,2,\ldots,K\}
, with probability mass function

\mathbb{P}(X(\bm{p})=1)=p_{1},\quad\mathbb{P}(X(\bm{p})=i)=p\quad\text{for }i=2,\ldots,K,

where
p_{1}\geq p>0
and
p_{1}+(K-1)p=1
. The goal is to minimize the SNR with respect to
\bm{p}
defined by

\mathrm{SNR}(X(\bm{p}))=\frac{(\mathbb{E}[X(\bm{p})])^{2}}{\operatorname{Var}(X(\bm{p}))}.

Using the constraint
p_{1}=1-(K-1)p
, we write

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X(\bm{p})]

\displaystyle=1\cdot(1-(K-1)p)+\sum_{i=2}^{K}i\cdot p=1-(K-1)p+p\sum_{i=2}^{K}i

\displaystyle=1-(K-1)p+p\left(\frac{K(K+1)}{2}-1\right)=\mu(p).

Similarly, for the second moment:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left\{[X(\bm{p})]^{2}\right\}

\displaystyle=1^{2}\cdot(1-(K-1)p)+\sum_{i=2}^{K}i^{2}\cdot p

\displaystyle=1-(K-1)p+p\left(\frac{K(K+1)(2K+1)}{6}-1\right)=M_{2}(p).

Thus, the variance is
\operatorname{Var}(X(\bm{p}))=\sigma^{2}(p)=M_{2}(p)-\mu(p)^{2}
. We define the function

f(p):=\frac{\mu(p)^{2}}{\sigma^{2}(p)}=\frac{(a_{1}p+1)^{2}}{b_{1}p+1-(a_{1}p+1)^{2}},

where

a_{1}=\frac{K(K-1)}{2},\quad b_{1}=\frac{K(K+1)(2K+1)}{6}-K.

To simplify notation, define

u=a_{1}p+1,

which implies

p=\frac{u-1}{a_{1}},\quad u\in\left[1,\,a_{1}\cdot\frac{1}{K-1}+1\right].

Rewriting
f
as a function of
u
, we have

f(u)=\frac{u^{2}}{b_{1}\cdot\frac{u-1}{a_{1}}+1-u^{2}}=\frac{u^{2}}{\frac{b_{1}}{a_{1}}(u-1)+1-u^{2}}.

Set the constant

c=\frac{b_{1}}{a_{1}}=\frac{\frac{K(K+1)(2K+1)}{6}-K}{\frac{K(K-1)}{2}}=\frac{(K+1)(2K+1)-6}{3(K-1)}.

Thus,
f(u)
can be written as

f(u)=\frac{u^{2}}{c(u-1)+1-u^{2}}=\frac{u^{2}}{D(u)},

where
D(u)=c(u-1)+1-u^{2}
. Differentiating
f(u)
with respect to
u
, we have

\displaystyle f^{\prime}(u)=\frac{2uD(u)-u^{2}(c-2u)}{D(u)^{2}}=\frac{u(cu-2c+2)}{D(u)^{2}}.

Setting
f^{\prime}(u)=0
to find critical points yields

u=0\quad\text{or}\quad cu-2c+2=0.

Since
u=a_{1}p+1\geq 1
, we discard
u=0
. Solving for
u
gives

u=\frac{2c-2}{c}=2-\frac{2}{c}.

Returning to variable
p
,

p^{\star}=\frac{u-1}{a_{1}}=\frac{2-\frac{2}{c}-1}{a_{1}}=\frac{1-\frac{2}{c}}{a_{1}}.

Substituting the expressions for
a_{1}
and
c
,

a_{1}=\frac{K(K-1)}{2},\quad c=\frac{2K^{2}+3K-5}{3(K-1)}.

Calculate the numerator,

1-\frac{2}{c}=\frac{2K^{2}+3K-5-6(K-1)}{2K^{2}+3K-5}=\frac{2K^{2}-3K+1}{2K^{2}+3K-5}.

Therefore,

p^{\star}=\frac{1-\frac{2}{c}}{a_{1}}=\frac{2(2K^{2}-3K+1)}{K(K-1)(2K^{2}+3K-5)}=\frac{2(2K-1)}{K(K-1)(2K+5)}.

This value
p^{\star}
lies within the allowed domain and corresponds to the minimum of
f(p)
. With this,

\displaystyle p_{1}=1-p^{\star}(K-1)=1-\frac{4K-2}{2K^{2}+5K}=\frac{2K^{2}+K+2}{2K^{2}+5K}.

To make
X(\bm{p}(\psi))
achieve this minimum SNR, we can choose

\displaystyle\psi(k)=\begin{cases}\log\left(\frac{(2K^{2}+K+2)(K-1)}{2(2K-1)}\right),&\text{ if }k=1,\\
0,&\text{ if }2\leq k\leq K.\end{cases}

Plugging this
\psi
to
\text{SNR}(\bm{p}(\psi))
yields that

\displaystyle\text{SNR}_{min}=\frac{24(K+1)}{4K^{2}-4K+1}.

This completes the proof.
\blacksquare

A.2  Proof of Lemmas

Lemma A1
.

Suppose that
y_{ij}^{(l)}\sim G(\phi,\psi,\gamma_{ij}^{\star},K)
, and let the corresponding rate function be defined as

I(z)=\sup_{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}}\left\{z\lambda-\log\mathbb{E}[e^{\lambda y_{ij}^{(l)}}]\right\}.

Then, the rate function evaluated at zero satisfies

I(0)=\log\left(\cosh\left(\phi(\gamma_{ij}^{\star})\right)\right)-\inf_{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}}\log\left(\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{K}e^{\psi(k)}\cosh\left(\phi(\gamma_{ij}^{\star})+\lambda k\right)}{\sum_{k=1}^{K}e^{\psi(k)}}\right).

In particular, when
K=1
, we have

I(0)=\log\left(\cosh\left(\phi(\gamma_{ij}^{\star})\right)\right).

Proof of Lemma
A1
.
Note that the probability mass function
\mathbb{P}(y_{ij}^{(l)}=k)
is given by

\mathbb{P}(y_{ij}^{(l)}=k)\propto\exp\left(\phi(\operatorname{sign}(k)\cdot\gamma_{ij}^{\star})+\psi(k)\right),\quad k\in\Upsilon(K).

The moment generating function (MGF) of
X
is:

M(\lambda)=\mathbb{E}[e^{\lambda y_{ij}^{(l)}}]=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left(\mathbb{P}(y_{ij}^{(l)}=k)e^{\lambda k}+\mathbb{P}(y_{ij}^{(l)}=-k)e^{-\lambda k}\right).

Substituting the expressions yields that

\displaystyle M(\lambda)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\Psi_{\phi,\psi}(\gamma_{ij}^{\star})}\sum_{k=1}^{K}e^{\psi(k)}\left(e^{\phi(\gamma_{ij}^{\star})+\lambda k}+e^{-\phi(\gamma_{ij}^{\star})-\lambda k}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2}{\Psi_{\phi,\psi}(\gamma_{ij}^{\star})}\sum_{k=1}^{K}e^{\psi(k)}\cosh(\phi(\gamma_{ij}^{\star})+\lambda k).

By the definition of the Cramér rate function,

I(0)=-\inf_{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}}\log M(\lambda).

Substituting the MGF, we have

I(0)=-\inf_{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}}\log\left(\frac{2}{\Psi_{\phi,\psi}(\gamma_{ij}^{\star})}\sum_{k=1}^{K}e^{\psi(k)}\cosh(\phi(\gamma_{ij}^{\star})+\lambda k)\right).

Using the expression for
y_{ij}^{(l)}

\Psi_{\phi,\psi}(\gamma_{ij}^{\star})=2\cosh(\phi(\gamma_{ij}^{\star}))\sum_{k=1}^{K}e^{\psi(k)},

we obtain

\displaystyle I(0)

\displaystyle=-\inf_{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}}\log\left(\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{K}e^{\psi(k)}\cosh(\phi(\gamma_{ij}^{\star})+\lambda k)}{\cosh(\phi(\gamma_{ij}^{\star}))\sum_{k=1}^{K}e^{\psi(k)}}\right)

\displaystyle=\log\left(\cosh(\phi(\gamma_{ij}^{\star}))\sum_{k=1}^{K}e^{\psi(k)}\right)-\inf_{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}}\log\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K}e^{\psi(k)}\cosh(\phi(\gamma_{ij}^{\star})+\lambda k)\right)

\displaystyle=\log\left(\cosh(\phi(\gamma_{ij}^{\star}))\right)-\inf_{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}}\log\left(\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{K}e^{\psi(k)}\cosh(\phi(\gamma_{ij}^{\star})+\lambda k)}{\sum_{k=1}^{K}e^{\psi(k)}}\right).

Here, it is worth noting that since
\cosh(x)\geq 1
for any
x\in\mathbb{R}
. Therefore, if
X\sim\text{Geo}(\psi,K)
is not a degenerate distribution (mass on a single point), we always have

\displaystyle\log\left(\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{K}e^{\psi(k)}\cosh(\phi(\gamma_{ij}^{\star})+\lambda k)}{\sum_{k=1}^{K}e^{\psi(k)}}\right)>0

for any
K\geq 2
and any
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
.

Particularly, when
K=1
, the rate function
I(0)
becomes

\displaystyle I(0)=

\displaystyle\log\left(\cosh(\phi(\gamma_{ij}^{\star}))\right)-\inf_{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}}\log\left(\cosh(\phi(\gamma_{ij}^{\star})+\lambda)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\log\left(\cosh(\phi(\gamma_{ij}^{\star}))\right),

where the last equality follows by taking
\lambda=-\phi(\gamma_{ij}^{\star})
. This completes the proof.
\blacksquare
.

Lemma A2
.

For
i,j\in[n]
and
l\in[L]
, suppose that
y_{ij}^{(l)}\sim G\big(\phi,\psi,\theta_{i}^{\star}-\theta_{j}^{\star},K\big)
with
\theta_{i}^{\star}>\theta_{j}^{\star}
for
i<j
, and let
X\sim\mathrm{Geo}(\psi,K)
for
K\geq 2
. Define
Z_{ij}^{(l)}
and
\widetilde{Z}_{ij}^{(l)}
as

\displaystyle Z_{ij}^{(l)}

\displaystyle=2y_{ij}^{(l)}+\sum_{k\in[n]\setminus\{i,j\}}\left(y_{ik}^{(l)}+y_{kj}^{(l)}\right),

\displaystyle\widetilde{Z}_{ij}^{(l)}

\displaystyle=2\mathop{\mathrm{sign}}(y_{ij}^{(l)})+\sum_{k\in[n]\setminus\{i,j\}}\left(\mathop{\mathrm{sign}}(y_{ik}^{(l)})+\mathop{\mathrm{sign}}(y_{kj}^{(l)})\right).

If
X
is non-degenerate, then we have

\displaystyle\sup_{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}}-\log\mathbb{E}(e^{\lambda Z_{ij}^{(l)}})<\sup_{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}}-\log\mathbb{E}(e^{\lambda\widetilde{Z}_{ij}^{(l)}}).

for any
i\neq j
.

Proof of Lemma
A2
.
Note that
y_{ij}^{(l)}
are independent for all
i\neq j
and
l\in[L]
. Therefore, the moment generating functions (MGFs) of
Z_{ij}^{(l)}
and
\widetilde{Z}_{ij}^{(l)}
are given by

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(e^{\lambda Z_{ij}^{(l)}})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[e^{\lambda\left(2y_{ij}^{(l)}+\sum_{k\in[n]\setminus\{i,j\}}\left(y_{ik}^{(l)}+y_{kj}^{(l)}\right)\right)}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}(e^{2\lambda y_{ij}^{(l)}})\cdot\prod_{k\in[n]\setminus\{i,j\}}\left\{\mathbb{E}(e^{\lambda y_{ik}^{(l)}})\cdot\mathbb{E}(e^{\lambda y_{kj}^{(l)}})\right\},

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(e^{\lambda\widetilde{Z}_{ij}^{(l)}})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[e^{\lambda\left(2\operatorname{sign}(y_{ij}^{(l)})+\sum_{k\in[n]\setminus\{i,j\}}\left(\operatorname{sign}(y_{ik}^{(l)})+\operatorname{sign}(y_{kj}^{(l)})\right)\right)}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}(e^{2\lambda\operatorname{sign}(y_{ij}^{(l)})})\cdot\prod_{k\in[n]\setminus\{i,j\}}\left\{\mathbb{E}(e^{\lambda\operatorname{sign}(y_{ik}^{(l)})})\cdot\mathbb{E}(e^{\lambda\operatorname{sign}(y_{kj}^{(l)})})\right\}.

Taking logarithms, we obtain

\displaystyle\log\mathbb{E}(e^{\lambda Z_{ij}^{(l)}})

\displaystyle=\log\mathbb{E}(e^{2\lambda y_{ij}^{(l)}})+\sum_{k\in[n]\setminus\{i,j\}}\left\{\log\mathbb{E}(e^{\lambda y_{ik}^{(l)}})+\log\mathbb{E}(e^{\lambda y_{kj}^{(l)}})\right\},

\displaystyle\log\mathbb{E}(e^{\lambda\widetilde{Z}_{ij}^{(l)}})

\displaystyle=\log\mathbb{E}(e^{2\lambda\operatorname{sign}(y_{ij}^{(l)})})+\sum_{k\in[n]\setminus\{i,j\}}\left\{\log\mathbb{E}(e^{\lambda\operatorname{sign}(y_{ik}^{(l)})})+\log\mathbb{E}(e^{\lambda\operatorname{sign}(y_{kj}^{(l)})})\right\}.

For each
i\neq j
and
l\in[L]
, it holds that

\displaystyle\log\mathbb{E}(e^{\lambda y_{ij}^{(l)}})

\displaystyle=\log\left(\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{K}e^{\psi(k)}\cosh(\phi(\gamma_{ij}^{\star})+\lambda k)}{\cosh(\phi(\gamma_{ij}^{\star}))\sum_{k=1}^{K}e^{\psi(k)}}\right)

\displaystyle=-\log(\cosh(\phi(\gamma_{ij}^{\star})))+\underbrace{\log\left(\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{K}e^{\psi(k)}\cosh(\phi(\gamma_{ij}^{\star})+\lambda k)}{\sum_{k=1}^{K}e^{\psi(k)}}\right)}_{\triangleq C_{ij}(\lambda)},

\displaystyle\log\mathbb{E}(e^{\lambda\operatorname{sign}(y_{ij}^{(l)})})

\displaystyle=-\log(\cosh(\phi(\gamma_{ij}^{\star})))+\underbrace{\log\left(\cosh(\phi(\gamma_{ij}^{\star})+\lambda)\right)}_{\triangleq D_{ij}(\lambda)}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle-\log\mathbb{E}(e^{\lambda Z_{ij}^{(l)}})

\displaystyle=R_{ij}-\underbrace{\left\{C_{ij}(2\lambda)+\sum_{k\in[n]\setminus\{i,j\}}\left[C_{ik}(\lambda)+C_{jk}(\lambda)\right]\right\}}_{P_{ij}(\lambda)},

\displaystyle-\log\mathbb{E}(e^{\lambda\widetilde{Z}_{ij}^{(l)}})

\displaystyle=R_{ij}-\underbrace{\left\{D_{ij}(2\lambda)+\sum_{k\in[n]\setminus\{i,j\}}\left[D_{ik}(\lambda)+D_{jk}(\lambda)\right]\right\}}_{Q_{ij}(\lambda)},

where

\displaystyle R_{ij}=\log(\cosh(\phi(\gamma_{ij}^{\star})))+\sum_{k\in[n]\setminus\{i,j\}}\left\{\log(\cosh(\phi(\gamma_{ik}^{\star})))+\log(\cosh(\phi(\gamma_{jk}^{\star})))\right\}.

Next, we aim to show that for any
i\neq j
,

\inf_{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}}P_{ij}(\lambda)>\inf_{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}}Q_{ij}(\lambda).

Note that
\cosh(x)
is convex. By Jensen’s inequality, for any
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle\log\left(\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{K}e^{\psi(k)}\cosh(\phi(\gamma_{ij}^{\star})+\lambda k)}{\sum_{k=1}^{K}e^{\psi(k)}}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\log\left[\cosh\left(\phi(\gamma_{ij}^{\star})+\lambda\cdot\mathbb{E}(X)\right)\right],

where
X
is the random variable with
\mathbb{P}(X=k)\propto e^{\psi(k)}
on
k\in\{1,\ldots,K\}
. Since
X
is non-degenerate, the inequality is strict:

\log\left(\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{K}e^{\psi(k)}\cosh(\phi(\gamma_{ij}^{\star})+\lambda k)}{\sum_{k=1}^{K}e^{\psi(k)}}\right)>\log\left[\cosh\left(\phi(\gamma_{ij}^{\star})+\lambda\cdot\mathbb{E}(X)\right)\right].

Hence,

\displaystyle P_{ij}(\lambda)

\displaystyle>\log\left[\cosh\left(\phi(\gamma_{ij}^{\star})+2\lambda\mathbb{E}(X)\right)\right]

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{k\in[n]\setminus\{i,j\}}\left\{\log\left[\cosh\left(\phi(\gamma_{ik}^{\star})+\lambda\mathbb{E}(X)\right)\right]+\log\left[\cosh\left(\phi(\gamma_{jk}^{\star})+\lambda\mathbb{E}(X)\right)\right]\right\}

\displaystyle=Q_{ij}(\widetilde{\lambda}),

where
\widetilde{\lambda}=\lambda\mathbb{E}(X)
. Moreover, since

\displaystyle\sup_{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}}-\log\mathbb{E}(e^{\lambda Z_{ij}^{(l)}})=\sup_{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}}\left\{R_{ij}-P_{ij}(\lambda)\right\}=R_{ij}-\inf_{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}}P_{ij}(\lambda),

\displaystyle\sup_{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}}-\log\mathbb{E}(e^{\lambda\widetilde{Z}_{ij}^{(l)}})=\sup_{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}}\left\{R_{ij}-Q_{ij}(\lambda)\right\}=R_{ij}-\inf_{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}}Q_{ij}(\lambda).

It then follows that

\inf_{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}}P_{ij}(\lambda)>\inf_{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}}Q_{ij}(\lambda)\Longleftrightarrow\sup_{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}}-\log\mathbb{E}(e^{\lambda Z_{ij}^{(l)}})<\sup_{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}}-\log\mathbb{E}(e^{\lambda\widetilde{Z}_{ij}^{(l)}}).

This completes the proof.
\blacksquare

Theorem A10

(Cramér’s Theorem;
Klenke 2013

.

Let
(X_{i})_{i\geq 1}

\mathbb{E}[e^{tX_{1}}]<\infty

S_{n}=\sum_{i\in[n]}X_{i}

a>\mathbb{E}[X_{1}]

I(a)=\sup_{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}}\left[a\lambda-\log\mathbb{E}[e^{\lambda X_{1}}]\right]
. Particularly, when
a=0
, we have

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\mathbb{P}(S_{n}\geq 0)=-I(0)=-\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\left\{-\log\mathbb{E}[e^{tX_{1}}]\right\}.

Theorem
A10
, known as Cramér’s Theorem, is a well-established result whose detailed proof can be found in Theorem 23.3 of

.
