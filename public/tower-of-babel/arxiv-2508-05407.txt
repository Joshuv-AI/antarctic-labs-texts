A unified framework for the analysis, numerical approximation and model reduction of linear operator equations, Part I: Well-posedness in space and time
Moritz Feuerle, Richard Löscher, Olaf Steinbach, Karsten Urban

Abstract
We present a unified framework to construct well-posed formulations for large classes of linear operator equations including elliptic, parabolic and hyperbolic partial differential equations. This general approach incorporates known weak variational formulations as well as novel space-time variational forms of the hyperbolic wave equation. The main concept is completion and extension of operators starting from the strong form of the problem. This paper lays the theoretical foundation for a unified approach towards numerical approximation methods and also model reduction of parameterized linear operator equations which will be the subject of the following parts.



1
Introduction

Linear operator equations can, e.g., be derived from partial differential and integral equations. Typical examples include elliptic, parabolic and hyperbolic second order partial differential equations (PDEs) as well as boundary integral equations. The analysis of well-posedness (existence, uniqueness and stability of solutions) as well as the construction and investigation of numerical approximation methods are usually done problem-specific and different for the above mentioned classes of problems.

The aim of this paper is to provide a unified framework for the analysis, numerical approximation and model reduction of linear operator equations. In particular we aim at deriving a general approach which allows us to construct well-posed and optimally stable

The forthcoming part will then concentrate on deriving corresponding discretizations and numerical approximations which will benefit from the general framework constructed in this first part. Finally, the close relation between the approximation error and the residual will then also allow us to construct a quite general approach towards model reduction of
parameterized
linear operator equations.

In such a general framework we are able to cover stationary elliptic as well as time-dependent parabolic and hyperbolic problems. Elliptic problems are formulated in Sobolev and the time-dependent problems in Lebesgue-Bochner spaces using space-time variational formulations. The latter ones are well established for parabolic problems, but less is known for transport, wave and Schrödinger-type problems. The framework presented in this paper allows for well-posed variational formulations for all those equations. We shall detail the general approach to several examples, which will also include known weak and ultra-weak formulations. To the very best of our knowledge, this is a novel framework. It is, however, related to

Our approach also extends existing results in a unified manner, recovering also well-known formulations for elliptic and parabolic problems, while also allowing for well-posed formulations for hyperbolic problems.

2.1
Operator equations

We are now going to formulate the class of linear operator equations that we will consider in the sequel.

The classical form of an operator equation can only be expected to be well-posed in exceptional cases (depending on

(5)

2.2

Our goal is to
construct a well-posed and optimally stable extension

is injective on

is an isometric isomorphism, i.e., the operator equation (
9
) is well-posed and optimally stable.

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
(B1)
and
(B2)
hold. The operator
{\mathmakebox[0pt]{\phantom{B}\mkern 2.0mu\overline{\mkern-2.0mu\phantom{B}}}B}
from (
9
) is

(a)

an isomorphism, i.e.,
{\mathmakebox[0pt]{\phantom{B}\mkern 2.0mu\overline{\mkern-2.0mu\phantom{B}}}B}\in\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{is}}({\mathmakebox[0pt]{\phantom{U}\mkern 2.0mu\overline{\mkern-2.0mu\phantom{U}}}U},{V}^{\prime})
(well-posedness) and

(b)

isometric, i.e.,
\lVert{\mathmakebox[0pt]{\phantom{B}\mkern 2.0mu\overline{\mkern-2.0mu\phantom{B}}}B}\rVert_{\mathcal{L}({\mathmakebox[0pt]{\phantom{U}\mkern 2.0mu\overline{\mkern-2.0mu\phantom{U}}}U},{V}^{\prime})}=\lVert{\mathmakebox[0pt]{\phantom{B}\mkern 2.0mu\overline{\mkern-2.0mu\phantom{B}}}B}^{-1}\rVert_{\mathcal{L}({V}^{\prime},{\mathmakebox[0pt]{\phantom{U}\mkern 2.0mu\overline{\mkern-2.0mu\phantom{U}}}U})}=1

(optimal stability).

Proof.

First, note that
{\mathmakebox[0pt]{\phantom{U}\mkern 2.0mu\overline{\mkern-2.0mu\phantom{U}}}U}
and
{\mathmakebox[0pt]{\phantom{B}\mkern 2.0mu\overline{\mkern-2.0mu\phantom{B}}}B}
are well-defined thanks to
(B1)
.
By (
8
) we have that the operator
{\mathmakebox[0pt]{\phantom{B}\mkern 2.0mu\overline{\mkern-2.0mu\phantom{B}}}B}:{\mathmakebox[0pt]{\phantom{U}\mkern 2.0mu\overline{\mkern-2.0mu\phantom{U}}}U}\to R({\mathmakebox[0pt]{\phantom{B}\mkern 2.0mu\overline{\mkern-2.0mu\phantom{B}}}B})\subseteq{V}^{\prime}
is an isometry, thus injective and
\lVert{\mathmakebox[0pt]{\phantom{B}\mkern 2.0mu\overline{\mkern-2.0mu\phantom{B}}}B}\rVert_{\mathcal{L}({\mathmakebox[0pt]{\phantom{U}\mkern 2.0mu\overline{\mkern-2.0mu\phantom{U}}}U},{V}^{\prime})}=1
. It remains to shown, that (i)
{\mathmakebox[0pt]{\phantom{B}\mkern 2.0mu\overline{\mkern-2.0mu\phantom{B}}}B}
is surjective, i.e.,
R({\mathmakebox[0pt]{\phantom{B}\mkern 2.0mu\overline{\mkern-2.0mu\phantom{B}}}B})={V}^{\prime}
, and (ii)
\lVert{\mathmakebox[0pt]{\phantom{B}\mkern 2.0mu\overline{\mkern-2.0mu\phantom{B}}}B}^{-1}\rVert_{\mathcal{L}({V}^{\prime},{\mathmakebox[0pt]{\phantom{U}\mkern 2.0mu\overline{\mkern-2.0mu\phantom{U}}}U})}=1.
To show the surjectivity, let
f\in{V}^{\prime}
be arbitrary but fixed.
By
(B2)
, the range of
{B}
is dense in
{V}^{\prime}
. Hence, there exists a sequence
\lparen u_{n}\rparen_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset D({B})
, such that
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\lVert f-{B}u_{n}\rVert_{{V}^{\prime}}=0
.
Thus, for all

\lVert u_{n}-u_{m}\rVert_{{\mathmakebox[0pt]{\phantom{U}\mkern 2.0mu\overline{\mkern-2.0mu\phantom{U}}}U}}\overset{\eqref{eq: norm X = Bu}}{=}\lVert u_{n}-u_{m}\rVert_{D({B})}=\lVert{B}(u_{n}-u_{m})\rVert_{{V}^{\prime}}\leq\lVert{B}u_{n}-f\rVert_{{V}^{\prime}}+\lVert{B}u_{m}-f\rVert_{{V}^{\prime}}\ \to\ 0

as
n,m\to\infty
, i.e.,
\lparen u_{n}\rparen_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a Cauchy sequence in
{\mathmakebox[0pt]{\phantom{U}\mkern 2.0mu\overline{\mkern-2.0mu\phantom{U}}}U}
.
Hence, by the completeness of
{\mathmakebox[0pt]{\phantom{U}\mkern 2.0mu\overline{\mkern-2.0mu\phantom{U}}}U}
, there exists
\bar{u}\in{\mathmakebox[0pt]{\phantom{U}\mkern 2.0mu\overline{\mkern-2.0mu\phantom{U}}}U}

\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\lVert\bar{u}-u_{n}\rVert_{{\mathmakebox[0pt]{\phantom{U}\mkern 2.0mu\overline{\mkern-2.0mu\phantom{U}}}U}}=0

\lVert f-{\mathmakebox[0pt]{\phantom{B}\mkern 2.0mu\overline{\mkern-2.0mu\phantom{B}}}B}\bar{u}\rVert_{{V}^{\prime}}\leq\lVert f-{B}u_{n}\rVert_{{V}^{\prime}}+\lVert{B}u_{n}-{\mathmakebox[0pt]{\phantom{B}\mkern 2.0mu\overline{\mkern-2.0mu\phantom{B}}}B}\bar{u}\rVert_{{V}^{\prime}}=\lVert f-{B}u_{n}\rVert_{{V}^{\prime}}+\lVert u_{n}-\bar{u}\rVert_{{\mathmakebox[0pt]{\phantom{U}\mkern 2.0mu\overline{\mkern-2.0mu\phantom{U}}}U}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n\to\infty}}{{\xrightarrow{\hskip 22.76219pt}}}0,

i.e.,

so that

, where the second inclusion follows from
(B2)
. Hence we conclude
{V}^{\prime}=R({\mathmakebox[0pt]{\phantom{B}\mkern 2.0mu\overline{\mkern-2.0mu\phantom{B}}}B})
, i.e., (i). In order to show (ii), we have

\lVert{\mathmakebox[0pt]{\phantom{B}\mkern 2.0mu\overline{\mkern-2.0mu\phantom{B}}}B}^{-1}\rVert_{\mathcal{L}({V}^{\prime},{\mathmakebox[0pt]{\phantom{U}\mkern 2.0mu\overline{\mkern-2.0mu\phantom{U}}}U})}=\sup_{f\in{V}^{\prime}\setminus\{0\}}\frac{\lVert{\mathmakebox[0pt]{\phantom{B}\mkern 2.0mu\overline{\mkern-2.0mu\phantom{B}}}B}^{-1}f\rVert_{{\mathmakebox[0pt]{\phantom{U}\mkern 2.0mu\overline{\mkern-2.0mu\phantom{U}}}U}}}{\lVert f\rVert_{{V}^{\prime}}}=\sup_{f\in{V}^{\prime}\setminus\{0\}}\frac{\lVert{\mathmakebox[0pt]{\phantom{B}\mkern 2.0mu\overline{\mkern-2.0mu\phantom{B}}}B}{\mathmakebox[0pt]{\phantom{B}\mkern 2.0mu\overline{\mkern-2.0mu\phantom{B}}}B}^{-1}f\rVert_{{V}^{\prime}}}{\lVert f\rVert_{{V}^{\prime}}}=\sup_{f\in{V}^{\prime}\setminus\{0\}}\frac{\lVert f\rVert_{{V}^{\prime}}}{\lVert f\rVert_{{V}^{\prime}}}=1.

In particular, the inverse

is bounded (even isometric) and thus

, which concludes the proof.
∎

Theorem 2.4
.

Let
(B1)
,

and (

be a subset and

Proof.

First, note that

{\mathmakebox[0pt]{\phantom{U}\mkern 2.0mu\overline{\mkern-2.0mu\phantom{U}}}U}

{\mathmakebox[0pt]{\phantom{U}\mkern 2.0mu\overline{\mkern-2.0mu\phantom{U}}}U}={U}=L^{2}(I;H^{1}_{0}(\Omega))\cap H^{1}_{0,}(I;H^{-1}(\Omega))
with norm
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{{\mathmakebox[0pt]{\phantom{U}\mkern 2.0mu\overline{\mkern-2.0mu\phantom{U}}}U}}=\sqrt{\lVert\cdot\rVert_{{U}}^{2}+2\langle\partial_{t}\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{{V}^{\prime}\times{V}}}
.

Remark 3.3

and therefore the well-posed formulation (

) of the heat equation corresponds exactly to the well-posed and optimally stable formulation introduced in
[
23
]
.

Remark 3.4

Note, that the supremizer of (

v_{u}=R_{V}^{-1}{B}u=R_{V}^{-1}\partial_{t}u+u
, which was already used in

,

,

,

,

to show the stability of the formulation.

Remark 3.5
.

The above results hold true when replacing

by an arbitrary bounded self-adjoint coercive operator

as we can replace the norm on

3.2.2
Weak in time

3.3.1
Strong in time

We start with the strong in time variational formulation of the wave equation as given in
example

2.5
. We denote by
\mathbb{F}
the 2D flip operator, i.e. for two Banach spaces
{X}_{1}
and
{X}_{2}
, their vector product
{X}\coloneqq{X}_{1}\times{X}_{2}
and
\begin{pmatrix}x_{1}\\
x_{2}\end{pmatrix}\in{X}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{F}\begin{pmatrix}x_{1}\\
x_{2}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}x_{2}\\
x_{1}\end{pmatrix},\qquad\mathbb{F}({X}_{1}\times{X}_{2})={X}_{2}\times{X}_{1},\qquad\lVert\cdot\rVert_{\mathbb{F}{X}}=\lVert\mathbb{F}\cdot\rVert_{{X}},

\displaystyle(\mathbb{F}{X})^{\prime}=\mathbb{F}{X}^{\prime},\qquad\mathbb{F}L^{2}(I;{X})=L^{2}(I;\mathbb{F}{X}).

Thus, with
W\coloneqq L^{2}(\Omega)\times H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
, we get
{U}=H^{1}_{0,}(I;W^{\prime})\cap L^{2}(I;\mathbb{F}W)
and

and their norms are given by
\lVert\vec{w}\rVert_{W}^{2}\coloneqq\lVert w_{1}\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\lVert w_{2}\rVert_{H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)}^{2}
,
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{{U}}^{2}\coloneqq\lVert\partial_{t}\cdot\rVert_{{V}^{\prime}}^{2}+\lVert\cdot\rVert_{\mathbb{F}{V}}^{2}
and
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{{V}}\coloneqq\lVert\cdot\rVert_{L^{2}(I;W)}
.
Now, for
\vec{f}\in{V}^{\prime}
, the variational formulation in
example

2.5
amounts to find
\vec{u}^{*}\in{U}
such that

{B}\vec{u}^{*}=\vec{f}\text{ in }{V}^{\prime}\qquad\text{with}\qquad\langle{B}\vec{u},\vec{v}\rangle_{{V}^{\prime}\times{V}}\coloneqq\langle\partial_{t}\vec{u}+A\vec{u},\vec{v}\rangle_{{V}^{\prime}\times{V}},

(20)

for all
\vec{u}\in{U}
and all
\vec{v}\in{V}
and
A\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}0&-I\!d\\
-\Delta_{x}^{\mathrm{we}}&0\end{pmatrix}:\mathbb{F}W\rightarrow W^{\prime}
.
Further, by
W^{\prime}=L^{2}(\Omega)\times H^{-1}(\Omega)
, the dual space and the dual norm of
{V}^{\prime}
read

{V}^{\prime}=L^{2}(I;W^{\prime}),\qquad\lVert\cdot\rVert_{{V}^{\prime}}^{2}=\lVert\cdot\rVert_{L^{2}(Q)}^{2}+\lVert\cdot\rVert_{L^{2}(I;H^{-1}(\Omega))}^{2}.

(21)

Remark 3.6
.

The operator
{B}
in (
20
) is bounded, i.e.
{B}\in\mathcal{L}({U},{V}^{\prime})
, as it holds for
\vec{u}\in{U}
, using
R_{H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)}\equiv-\Delta_{x}^{\mathrm{we}}
for the Riesz operator (
1
) on

, that

\displaystyle\lVert{B}\vec{u}\rVert_{{V}^{\prime}}^{2}

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{eq:Applications:strong wave:dual Ts}}{=}\lVert\partial_{t}u_{1}-u_{2}\rVert_{L^{2}(Q)}^{2}+\lVert(\Delta_{x}^{\mathrm{we}})^{-1}\lparen\partial_{t}u_{2}-\Delta_{x}^{\mathrm{we}}u_{1}\rparen\rVert_{L^{2}(I;H^{1}_{0}(\Omega))}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\big\lparen\lVert\partial_{t}u_{1}\rVert_{L^{2}(Q)}^{2}+\lVert u_{2}\rVert_{L^{2}(Q)}^{2}+\lVert\partial_{t}u_{2}\rVert_{L^{2}(I;H^{-1}(\Omega))}^{2}+\lVert u_{1}\rVert_{L^{2}(I;H^{1}_{0}(\Omega))}^{2}\big\rparen

\displaystyle=2\lparen\lVert\vec{u}\rVert_{\mathbb{F}{V}}^{2}+\lVert\partial_{t}\vec{u}\rVert_{{V}^{\prime}}^{2}\rparen=2\lVert\vec{u}\rVert_{{U}}^{2}.

Theorem 3.1

(
[
16
, Ch. 3 Thm. 8.1 & eq. (8.15)]
)
.

Let
f\in L^{2}(Q)
, then there exists a unique
u^{*}\in H^{2}(I;H^{-1}(\Omega))\cap H^{1}(I;L^{2}(\Omega))\cap L^{2}(I;H^{1}_{0}(\Omega))
such that

\partial_{tt}u^{*}-\Delta^{\mathrm{we}}_{x}u^{*}=f\text{ in }L^{2}(I;H^{-1}(\Omega)),\quad u^{*}(0)=0,\quad\partial_{t}u^{*}(0)=0.

Further, there exists
\hat{C}<\infty
independent of
u^{*}
and
f
such that
\lVert\partial_{t}u^{*}\rVert_{L^{2}(Q)}^{2}+\lVert u^{*}\rVert_{L^{2}(I;H^{1}_{0}(\Omega))}^{2}\leq\hat{C}\lVert f\rVert_{L^{2}(Q)}^{2}
.

Remark 3.7
.

Using the techniques of
[
19
, Theorem 5.1, Remark 4.6]
, we can show that the constant in the preceding theorem equals
\hat{C}=\frac{T^{2}}{2}
and the estimate is in fact sharp in the powers of
T
.

Corollary 3.2
.

The variational formulation (
20
) possesses a solution
\vec{u}^{*}\in{U}
for each
\vec{f}\in{Y}\coloneqq H^{1}_{0,}(I;L^{2}(\Omega))\times L^{2}(Q)
. Furthermore, there exists
C_{Q}<\infty
independent of
\vec{u}^{*}
and
\vec{f}
such that
\lVert\vec{u}^{*}\rVert_{{U}}\leq C_{Q}\lVert\vec{f}\rVert_{L^{2}(Q;\mathbb{R}^{2})}
. In particular
theorem

2.2
holds by
lemmas

2.1
and
2.3
.

Proof.

For
\vec{f}=(f_{1},f_{2})\in{Y}
define
f\coloneqq\partial_{t}f_{1}+f_{2}\in L^{2}(Q)
and denote by
u^{*}
and
C
the solution and the constant given by
theorem

3.1
with respect to the right-hand side
f
. Now, defining
\vec{u}^{*}\equiv(u^{*}_{1},u^{*}_{2})\coloneqq(u^{*},\partial_{t}u^{*}-f_{1})
, it holds
\vec{u}^{*}\in{U}
,
\partial_{t}u^{*}_{1}-u^{*}_{2}=f_{1}
in
L^{2}(Q)
and
\partial_{t}u^{*}_{2}-\Delta_{x}^{\mathrm{we}}u^{*}_{1}=\partial_{tt}u^{*}-\partial_{t}f_{1}-\Delta_{x}^{\mathrm{we}}u^{*}=f_{2}
in
L^{2}(I;H^{-1}(\Omega))
.
Hence, we conclude, that
\vec{u}^{*}
solves (
20
) as it holds for all
\vec{v}\in{V}
, that

\displaystyle\langle{B}\vec{u}^{*},\vec{v}\rangle_{{V}^{\prime}\times{V}}

\displaystyle=\lparen\partial_{t}u^{*}_{1}-u^{*}_{2},v_{1}\rparen_{L^{2}(Q)}+\langle\partial_{t}u^{*}_{2}-\Delta_{x}^{\mathrm{we}}u^{*}_{1},v_{2}\rangle_{L^{2}(I;H^{-1}(\Omega))\times L^{2}(I;H^{1}_{0}(\Omega))}

\displaystyle=\lparen f_{1},v_{1}\rparen_{L^{2}(Q)}+\langle f_{2},v_{2}\rangle_{L^{2}(I;H^{-1}(\Omega))\times L^{2}(I;H^{1}_{0}(\Omega))}=\langle\vec{f},\vec{v}\rangle_{{V}^{\prime}\times{V}}.

Now, regarding the stability estimate, it first holds by
theorem

3.1
, together with the 1D Poincaré inequality
\lVert\partial_{t}f\rVert_{L^{2}(Q)}\leq\frac{T}{\sqrt{2}}\lVert f\rVert_{L^{2}(Q)}
for all
f\in H^{1}_{0,}(I;L^{2}(\Omega))
, that

\displaystyle\lVert\partial_{t}u^{*}_{1}\rVert_{L^{2}(Q)}^{2}+\lVert u^{*}_{1}\rVert_{L^{2}(I;H^{1}_{0}(\Omega))}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\hat{C}\lVert\partial_{t}f_{1}+f_{2}\rVert_{L^{2}(Q)}^{2}\leq 2\hat{C}(\lVert\partial_{t}f_{1}\rVert_{L^{2}(Q)}^{2}+\lVert f_{2}\rVert_{L^{2}(Q)}^{2})

\displaystyle\leq 2\hat{C}\tilde{C}_{T}\lVert\vec{f}\rVert_{L^{2}(Q;\mathbb{R}^{2})}^{2},

with
\tilde{C}_{T}\coloneqq\max\{1,\frac{T^{2}}{2}\}
.
Next, using Poincaré’s inequality in
\Omega
, it holds
H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow_{d}L^{2}(\Omega)
for some
C_{\Omega}<\infty
, in particular
\lVert f\rVert_{L^{2}(I;H^{-1}(\Omega))}\leq C_{\Omega}\lVert f\rVert_{L^{2}(Q)}
for all
f\in L^{2}(Q)
by
remark

1.2
, and thus

\displaystyle\lVert\partial_{t}u^{*}_{2}\rVert_{L^{2}(I;H^{-1}(\Omega))}^{2}+\lVert u^{*}_{2}\rVert_{L^{2}(Q)}^{2}=\lVert f_{2}+\Delta_{x}^{\mathrm{we}}u^{*}_{1}\rVert_{L^{2}(I;H^{-1}(\Omega))}^{2}+\lVert\partial_{t}u^{*}_{1}-f_{1}\rVert_{L^{2}(Q)}^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad\leq 2\big\lparen\lVert f_{2}\rVert_{L^{2}(I;H^{-1}(\Omega))}^{2}+\lVert u^{*}_{1}\rVert_{L^{2}(I;H^{1}_{0}(\Omega))}^{2}+\lVert\partial_{t}u^{*}_{1}\rVert_{L^{2}(Q)}^{2}+\lVert f_{1}\rVert_{L^{2}(Q)}^{2}\big\rparen

\displaystyle\qquad\leq(2\tilde{C}_{\Omega}+4\hat{C}\tilde{C}_{T})\lVert\vec{f}\rVert_{L^{2}(Q;\mathbb{R}^{2})}^{2},

with
\tilde{C}_{\Omega}\coloneqq\max\{1,C_{\Omega}^{2}\}
. Now, defining
C_{Q}^{2}\coloneqq 2\tilde{C}_{\Omega}+6\hat{C}\tilde{C}_{T}
, we get

\displaystyle\lVert\vec{u}^{*}\rVert_{{U}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\lVert\partial_{t}u^{*}_{2}\rVert_{L^{2}(I;H^{-1}(\Omega))}^{2}+\lVert\partial_{t}u^{*}_{1}\rVert_{L^{2}(Q)}^{2}+\lVert u^{*}_{1}\rVert_{L^{2}(I;H^{1}_{0}(\Omega))}^{2}+\lVert u^{*}_{2}\rVert_{L^{2}(Q)}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C_{Q}\lVert\vec{f}\rVert_{L^{2}(Q;\mathbb{R}^{2})}^{2}.

∎

Although, (
20
) admits a unique solution
\vec{u}^{*}\in{U}
for all
\vec{f}\in H^{1}_{0,}(I;L^{2}(\Omega))\times L^{2}(Q)\subset{V}^{\prime}
we will now show that this can not be extended to all
\vec{f}\in{V}^{\prime}
. Thus, for the wave equation, unlike the previous examples, considering
{\mathmakebox[0pt]{\phantom{U}\mkern 2.0mu\overline{\mkern-2.0mu\phantom{U}}}U}
and
{\mathmakebox[0pt]{\phantom{B}\mkern 2.0mu\overline{\mkern-2.0mu\phantom{B}}}B}
instead of
{U}
and
{B}
, respectively, is in fact necessary for a well-posed formulation (instead of just the optimal stability) as
{B}:{U}\rightarrow{V}^{\prime}
isn’t already an isomorphism.

Theorem 3.3
.

The operator
{B}
in (
20
) does not define an isomorphism as there does not exists an inf-sup constant
\beta>0
such that
\beta\lVert\vec{u}\rVert_{{U}}\leq\lVert{B}\vec{u}\rVert_{{V}^{\prime}}
for all
\vec{u}\in{U}
, i.e. the inverse of
{B}
is not bounded.

Proof.

We construct a counterexample, following the ideas presented in
[
24
, Theorem 4.2.24]
. To this end, let
\phi_{k}\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
denote the normalized eigenfunctions and
0<\lambda_{1}\leq\lambda_{2}\leq\ldots\to\infty
the eigenvalues of the spatial Laplacian, given as
-\Delta_{x}\phi_{k}=\lambda_{k}\phi_{k}
in
\Omega
with
\lVert\phi_{k}\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=1
, and consider the function

\vec{u}^{k}(t,x)\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}\phi_{k}(x)\int_{0}^{t}s\sin(\sqrt{\lambda_{k}}s)\,ds\\
\phi_{k}(x)t\sin(\sqrt{\lambda_{k}}t)\end{pmatrix},

which solves (
20
) for
\vec{f}^{k}\coloneqq(0,f^{k})
,
f^{k}(t,x)\coloneqq 2\phi_{k}(x)\sin(\sqrt{\lambda_{k}}t)
. Elementary computations give
\lVert\vec{u}^{k}\rVert_{{U}}^{2}=\lambda_{k}^{-3/2}\big(T\,\sqrt{\lambda_{k}}+\frac{2}{3}T^{3}\lambda_{k}^{3/2}-\frac{1}{2}T\,\sin(\sqrt{\lambda_{k}}T)\big)

and

\lVert\vec{f}^{k}\rVert_{{V}^{\prime}}^{2}=\lambda_{k}^{-3/2}\big({2T\sqrt{\lambda_{k}}-\sin(2\sqrt{\lambda_{k}}T)}\big)
.
Therefore, taking the limit we get
\frac{\lVert\vec{f}^{k}\rVert_{{V}^{\prime}}}{\lVert\vec{u}^{k}\rVert_{{U}}}\to 0
for
k\to\infty

which completes the proof.
∎

Thus, in contrast to elliptic and parabolic PDEs, in this case the norm induced by the operator is not equivalent to the norm on
{U}
. In order to determine
\lVert{B}\cdot\rVert_{{V}^{\prime}}
, using
R_{H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)}\equiv-\Delta_{x}^{\mathrm{we}}
, it holds for all
\vec{u}\in{U}
, that

\displaystyle\lVert{B}\vec{u}\rVert_{{V}^{\prime}}^{2}\overset{\eqref{eq:Applications:strong wave:dual Ts}}{=}\lVert\partial_{t}u_{1}-u_{2}\rVert_{L^{2}(Q)}^{2}+\lVert\partial_{t}u_{2}-\Delta_{x}^{\mathrm{we}}u_{1}\rVert_{L^{2}(I;H^{-1}(\Omega))}^{2}

\displaystyle\quad=\lVert\partial_{t}u_{1}\rVert_{L^{2}(Q)}^{2}+\lVert u_{2}\rVert_{L^{2}(Q)}^{2}-2\lparen\partial_{t}u_{1},u_{2}\rparen_{L^{2}(Q)}

\displaystyle\qquad+\lVert\partial_{t}u_{2}\rVert_{L^{2}(I;H^{-1}(\Omega))}^{2}+\lVert u_{1}\rVert_{L^{2}(I;H^{1}_{0}(\Omega))}^{2}+2\langle\partial_{t}u_{2},u_{1}\rangle_{L^{2}(I;H^{-1}(\Omega))\times L^{2}(I;H^{1}_{0}(\Omega))}

\displaystyle\quad=\lVert\vec{u}\rVert_{{U}}^{2}+2\langle\partial_{t}\vec{u},J\vec{u}\rangle_{{V}^{\prime}\times{V}},

with
J\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}0&-I\!d\\
I\!d&0\end{pmatrix}
.

Remark 3.8
.

Alternatively, using the Riesz operator (
1
) on
{V}
, given by
R_{{V}}=\begin{pmatrix}I\!d&0\\
0&-\Delta_{x}^{\mathrm{we}}\end{pmatrix}
, together with (
11
), we get

\displaystyle\lVert{B}\vec{u}\rVert_{{V}^{\prime}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\langle{B}\vec{u},R_{{V}}^{-1}{B}\vec{u}\rangle_{{V}^{\prime}\times{V}}=\langle\partial_{t}\vec{u}+A\vec{u},R_{{V}}^{-1}(\partial_{t}\vec{u}+A\vec{u})\rangle_{{V}^{\prime}\times{V}}

\displaystyle=\langle\partial_{t}\vec{u},R_{{V}}^{-1}\partial_{t}\vec{u}\rangle_{{V}^{\prime}\times{V}}+2\langle\partial_{t}\vec{u},R_{{V}}^{-1}A\vec{u}\rangle_{{V}^{\prime}\times{V}}+\langle A^{*}R_{{V}}^{-1}A\vec{u},\vec{u}\rangle_{\mathbb{F}{V}^{\prime}\times\mathbb{F}{V}},

where
A^{*}=\begin{pmatrix}0&-\Delta_{x}^{\mathrm{we}}\\
-I\!d&0\end{pmatrix}:W\rightarrow\mathbb{F}W^{\prime}
denotes the adjoint of
A
.
To further simplify this expression, note that
R_{{V}}=AJ^{*}
and
J^{-1}=J^{*}
, hence
R_{{V}}^{-1}A=J
as well as
A^{*}R_{{V}}^{-1}A=\begin{pmatrix}-\Delta_{x}^{\mathrm{we}}&0\\
0&I\!d\end{pmatrix}=R_{\mathbb{F}{V}},
with
R_{\mathbb{F}{V}}
denoting the Riesz operator (
1
) on
\mathbb{F}{V}
. Thus, we get the same result, namely

\displaystyle\lVert{B}\vec{u}\rVert_{{V}^{\prime}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\langle\partial_{t}\vec{u},R_{{V}}^{-1}\partial_{t}\vec{u}\rangle_{{V}^{\prime}\times{V}}+2\langle\partial_{t}\vec{u},J\vec{u}\rangle_{{V}^{\prime}\times{V}}+\langle R_{\mathbb{F}{V}}\vec{u},\vec{u}\rangle_{\mathbb{F}{V}^{\prime}\times\mathbb{F}{V}}

(22)

\displaystyle=\lVert\partial_{t}\vec{u}\rVert_{{V}^{\prime}}^{2}+2\langle\partial_{t}\vec{u},J\vec{u}\rangle_{{V}^{\prime}\times{V}}+\lVert\vec{u}\rVert_{\mathbb{F}{V}}^{2}=\lVert\vec{u}\rVert_{{U}}^{2}+2\langle\partial_{t}\vec{u},J\vec{u}\rangle_{{V}^{\prime}\times{V}}.

As we can see, the representation of the norm
\lVert{B}\cdot\rVert_{{V}^{\prime}}
is quite similar to the one derived for the heat equation in (
19
). However, the additional term

2\langle\partial_{t}\vec{u},J\vec{u}\rangle_{{V}^{\prime}\times{V}}=2\langle\partial_{t}u_{2},u_{1}\rangle_{L^{2}(I;H^{-1}(\Omega))\times L^{2}(I;H^{1}_{0}(\Omega))}-2\lparen\partial_{t}u_{1},u_{2}\rparen_{L^{2}(Q)}

can not be bounded from below by zero. In fact, for
\vec{u}^{k}
as defined in the proof of
theorem

3.3
, we compute
\langle\partial_{t}\vec{u}^{k},J\vec{u}^{k}\rangle_{{V}^{\prime}\times{V}}=-\frac{T^{3}}{3}-\frac{\sin(2\sqrt{\lambda_{k}}T)}{4\lambda_{k}^{3/2}}+\frac{T}{2\lambda_{k}}
,
which goes to
-\frac{T^{3}}{3}<0
as
k\to\infty
. Moreover, as the function
\vec{u}^{k}
solves (
20
) for the right-hand side
\vec{f}^{k}
, it holds
{B}\vec{u}^{k}=\vec{f}^{k}
and we get

\frac{\lVert\vec{f}^{k}\rVert_{{V}^{\prime}}}{\lVert{B}\vec{u}^{k}\rVert_{{V}^{\prime}}}=\frac{\lVert\vec{f}^{k}\rVert_{{V}^{\prime}}}{\lVert\vec{f}^{k}\rVert_{{V}^{\prime}}}=1\qquad\text{but}\qquad\frac{\lVert\vec{f}^{k}\rVert_{{V}^{\prime}}}{\lVert\vec{u}^{k}\rVert_{{U}}}\to 0,\quad k\to\infty.

Hence, the norms
\lVert{B}\cdot\rVert_{{V}^{\prime}}
and
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{{U}}
are not equivalent. By (
8
) and (
22
), we have a characterization of
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{{\mathmakebox[0pt]{\phantom{U}\mkern 2.0mu\overline{\mkern-2.0mu\phantom{U}}}U}}
on
{U}\subseteq_{d}{\mathmakebox[0pt]{\phantom{U}\mkern 2.0mu\overline{\mkern-2.0mu\phantom{U}}}U}
.
Now, with the norm
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{{\mathmakebox[0pt]{\phantom{U}\mkern 2.0mu\overline{\mkern-2.0mu\phantom{U}}}U}}
characterized (at least on
{U}
), let us consider the space
{\mathmakebox[0pt]{\phantom{U}\mkern 2.0mu\overline{\mkern-2.0mu\phantom{U}}}U}
. Unlike to the elliptic and parabolic equations, we cannot give
{\mathmakebox[0pt]{\phantom{U}\mkern 2.0mu\overline{\mkern-2.0mu\phantom{U}}}U}
explicitly, but we can characterize it to some extend by
theorem

2.4
.

Corollary 3.4
.

The adjoint problem of (
20
), namely to find
\vec{v}^{*}\in{V}
such that

\langle{B}^{*}\vec{v}^{*},\vec{u}\rangle_{{U}^{\prime}\times{U}}=\langle\vec{g},\vec{u}\rangle_{{U}^{\prime}\times{U}}\quad\forall\vec{u}\in{U},

possess a solution for each
\vec{g}\in{X}\coloneqq L^{2}(Q)\times H^{1}_{,0}(I;L^{2}(\Omega))\subset_{d}{G}
. Further, there exists
C^{*}_{Q}<\infty
independent of
\vec{v}^{*}
and
\vec{g}
such that
\lVert\vec{v}^{*}\rVert_{{V}}\leq C^{*}_{Q}\lVert\vec{g}\rVert_{L^{2}(Q;\mathbb{R}^{2})}
. In particular
theorem

2.4
holds by
remark

2.6
.

Proof.

In addition to the flip operator
\mathbb{F}
, we define the time reversal operator by
\mathbb{T}f(t)\coloneqq f(T-t)
for almost all
t\in I
. It is easy to see, that
\mathbb{F}^{2}=I\!d
,
\mathbb{T}^{2}=I\!d
,
\mathbb{F}^{*}=\mathbb{F}
,
\mathbb{T}^{*}=\mathbb{T}
,
\mathbb{T}\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{F}\mathbb{T}
,
\partial_{t}\mathbb{T}\mathbb{F}=-\mathbb{T}\mathbb{F}\partial_{t}
and
\mathbb{T}\mathbb{F}A=A^{*}\mathbb{T}\mathbb{F}
. Further, it holds
\mathbb{T}\mathbb{F}\vec{u}\in{V}
,
(\mathbb{T}\mathbb{F}\vec{u})(T)=0
and
\mathbb{T}\mathbb{F}A\vec{u}\in\mathbb{F}{V}^{\prime}
for all
\vec{u}\in{U}
.
Now, let
\vec{g}\in{X}
be arbitrary but fixed, define
\vec{f}\coloneqq\mathbb{T}\mathbb{F}\vec{g}\in{Y}
and denote by
\vec{u}^{*}\in{U}
the solution of
{B}\vec{u}^{*}=\vec{f}
in
{V}^{\prime}
given by
corollary

3.2
. Defining
\vec{v}^{*}\coloneqq\mathbb{T}\mathbb{F}\vec{u}^{*}\in{V}
, it holds for all
\vec{u}\in{U}

4
Conclusions and Outlook

In this paper, we presented a general abstract framework towards well-posed and (optimally) stable formulations of linear operator equations. The starting point is always a classical formulation, which does not need to be well-posed. This requires a Gelfand triple structure for the test space. The second step is to restrict the space for the right-hand sides in such a way that the operator equation admits a solution for such right-hand side data (think of smooth functions). Finally, we form a completion for the trial space and a unique continuous extension of the operator. This procedure can be made more explicit if also the trial space allows for a Gelfand triple structure.

This general framework is applied to the Poisson, heat and wave equation, the latter two in a variational space-time setting. Our findings are summarized in Table
1
. We reproduce well-known results concerning strong, weak and ultra-weak formulations of elliptic and parabolic problems. However, the presented setting also applies for the hyperbolic wave equation, where we derive well-posed formulations, which are (to the very best of our knowledge) new. We can characterize trial spaces and induced norms in such a way that the formulation of the wave equation in these spaces is well-posed and optimally stable. It turns out that these are non-standard Sobolev-type spaces.

As already indicated above, this paper is meant to lay the theoretical foundation for a subsequent numerical discretization (in terms of Galerkin and Petrov-Galerkin schemes) and also for model reduction of parameterized linear operator equations. This will be the topic of subsequent parts of this work. It is clear that the numerical realization of the involved norms will be a challenge. Concerning model reduction, we will investigate to which extend the combination of parameter-dependent trial spaces (i.e., leaving the realm of linear model reduction and the known barrier of the Kolmogorov
n
-width for transport- and wave-type problems) and parameter-independent test spaces (allowing for an efficient computation of the norm of the residual as an a posteriori error estimator) might be beneficial.

Moreover, we are aiming to apply the presented framework also to first order transport problems, singular integral and Schrödinger-type operators.
