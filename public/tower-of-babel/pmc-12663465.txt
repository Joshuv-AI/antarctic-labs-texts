Segmentation as a bottleneck in numerical cognition
Authors: Lauren S. Aulet, Jessica F. Cantlon
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Despite frequent appeals to the ecological relevance of number perception, number perception is typically studied using stimuli that differ greatly from real-world scenes. Consequently, it remains unclear whether numerical discrimination observed in controlled experimental settings operates under the same principles as it does ‘in the wild.’ In the present study, adult participants completed a number discrimination task with four stimulus conditions: (1) dot arrays, (2) dot arrays with visual noise, (3) naturalistic images, and (4) pseudo-naturalistic images designed to preserve object boundaries while minimizing featural complexity. Consistent with previous work, we found that accuracy for numerical discrimination of naturalistic and pseudo-naturalistic arrays was significantly worse than for dot arrays. Moreover, for the naturalistic conditions, but much less so for dot arrays, accuracy declined as the total number of objects increased. Crucially, this effect was driven by the presence of occluded objects, suggesting that numerical discrimination is constrained by the ability to segment individual items in cluttered visual scenes. Furthermore, in naturalistic and pseudo-naturalistic conditions, there was a significant interaction between total number of objects and object occlusion, such that the number of objects only impacted performance when stimuli contained occluded objects. Our findings extend previous work on connectedness and grouping effects in numerical perception by demonstrating that in naturalistic contexts, the demands of object segmentation, particularly when occlusion is present, create a measurable bottleneck that systematically affects numerical discrimination in ways not observed with traditional stimuli. This suggests that models of numerical cognition must account for the computational costs of resolving complex visual scenes into countable units, costs that increase with set size and visual complexity.

Introduction

Research on numerical cognition frequently highlights the role of numerical discrimination in naturalistic behavior, such as foraging, to emphasize its evolutionary significance1,2. However, the experimental stimuli typically employed in research on number perception – arrays of fully individuated, homogeneous elements - bear little resemblance to naturalistic scenes of objects. While this discrepancy is not inherently problematic, as much of cognitive psychology relies on the investigation of isolated processes, it rests on the critical assumption that researchers have accurately identified and isolated the essential components of numerical perception. Consequently, an empirical question arises: do the numerical discrimination tasks utilizing dot array stimuli encompass all the crucial numerical components intrinsic to real-world number discrimination, or are they lacking in some respects? This inquiry is fundamental to establishing the external validity of the commonly used dot array stimuli and their broader applicability to ecological contexts.

Recent work examining naturalistic number discrimination has demonstrated that naturalistic number discrimination exhibits key features of number perception (i.e., ratio effects;3 and is instantiated by similar neural regions (i.e., intraparietal sulcus;4. One key difference, however, is that human participants are significantly worse at discriminating numerosity as depicted in real world scenes, as compared to artificial dot array stimuli3.

What could account for this decrease in accuracy? One possibility is that this decrease is due to the additional semantic and/or featural information in real world scenes. In other words, if semantic or featural information is processed during numerical discrimination, even when irrelevant for the task, then this information, which is far more variable in naturalistic images than in standard experimental dot arrays, may slow and/or impair task-relevant numerical processes.

Another possibility is that the decrease in accuracy observed in numerical discrimination of natural scenes is due to more complex object processing demands and configural information in real world scenes. In other words, the arrangement of items in naturalistic scenes may be more difficult to individuate and enumerate. For example, previous work using dot array stimuli has demonstrated that item connectedness significantly reduces number estimates5–8, such that when items were connected or overlapping, participants reported those arrays as being significantly less numerous compared to when the items were not connected or overlapping.

The nature of numerical perception and its relationship to object individuation remains a subject of ongoing debate. Several theories propose that numerical information is extracted automatically during early visual processing9–11, with some suggesting this occurs at the level of the retina or primary visual cortex. While these researchers acknowledge that perceptual factors like item connectedness can modulate numerical perception10, they maintain that the core process of numerosity extraction operates largely independently from other visual processes.

This creates an interesting tension: if numerosity can be extracted as a primary visual attribute, yet is simultaneously affected by factors like connectedness that influence object individuation, what happens in naturalistic visual scenes where object segmentation becomes challenging? The present study explores this question by examining how performance in numerical discrimination varies across stimulus types that place different demands on object segmentation processes. If numerical discrimination is significantly constrained by the challenges of segmenting individual objects in cluttered or occluded scenes, this would have important implications for understanding how numerosity perception operates in real-world contexts.

In the present work, we compared participant performance at discriminating different kinds of stimuli, varying from artificial dot arrays, like those commonly used in experimental research, to naturalistic images of objects. By systematically varying stimulus properties across conditions, we sought to identify whether decreased accuracy with naturalistic stimuli stems from differences in semantic/featural complexity, spatial arrangement, or object segmentation. This approach allows us to evaluate both the ecological validity of traditional numerical cognition paradigms and develop a more complete understanding of real-world numerical processing.

Methods

All experimental procedures adhered to university standards, as approved by the Research Subjects Review Board.

Participants

37 undergraduates (Mean age = 19.51 years; 23 male) participated for course credit. All participants had normal or corrected-to-normal vision. Procedures were approved by the local Institutional Review Board (IRB). All participants provided informed consent in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations of the IRB.

Two subjects’ data were removed from the Noise condition for accuracy significantly lower than 2.5 SD below the mean accuracy for that condition (i.e., near-chance accuracy).

Stimuli

Four different stimulus types were used: artificial dot arrays (‘Artificial’), dot arrays with noise (‘Artificial with noise’), naturalistic images (Naturalistic), and ‘parsed’ images (Naturalistic ‘Parsed’). In each stimulus set, arrays ranged from 4 to 24 items. For dot arrays and dot arrays with noise conditions, one third of trials were controlled for density, for cumulative area, or for cumulative perimeter (i.e., contour length). Artificial dot array stimuli were generated using the ggplot2 package in R12. The stimuli for the Artificial with noise condition were identical to the stimuli in the Artificial condition except stimuli were masked using the ‘noise stim’ component in Psychopy213, with ‘filtered’ noise set to an opacity of 0.5. Naturalistic images were a curated set of images obtained from the internet. Parsed images were then custom generated in Adobe Photoshop to best match the item boundaries, spatial arrangement of items, as well as the featural information, such as color, in each Naturalistic image (see Fig. 1). No Artificial or Artificial with Noise stimuli contained occluded elements. 202 of 377 parsed/naturalistic stimuli contained occluded elements (53.5%) demonstrating that occlusion was balanced across trials.

Task

For all conditions, participants completed a magnitude comparison task where they indicated via keyboard press (left or right arrow) which of the two stimuli contained more items. Two stimuli were presented simultaneously on the left and right side of the screen. The task was administered using Psychopy13. Stimuli were displayed for 1 s, followed by a 600ms mask. Participants were encouraged to respond as quickly as possible following the disappearance of the mask (and stimulus). Participants completed 142 trials of each condition (blocked by condition; random order across participants), for a total of 568 trials per participant. The distribution of ratio values and total number of items across all trials of the experiment are depicted in Fig. 2.

Results

First, we evaluated the within-subjects effect of condition on accuracy. A one-way repeated measures ANOVA found a significant effect of condition on mean accuracy, F(3, 90) = 22.82, p < .01. Post-hoc t-tests found that there was a significant difference between all conditions (ps < 0.002; Bonferroni-corrected) except Artificial with Noise and Parsed, t(31) = 0.166, p = .87. Specifically, mean accuracy was significantly greater for the Artificial condition than the other three conditions (see Fig. 3), replicating previous work finding better accuracy for traditionally used dot arrays than for naturalistic images3. Notably, however, mean accuracy in the Naturalistic condition was significantly worse than mean accuracy in the Artificial with Noise condition, suggesting that the poorer performance observed in the Naturalistic condition cannot be attributed to a purely additive effect of visual noise alone.

To further understand the relations between stimulus conditions, we correlated mean accuracy across conditions. We found only two significant correlations, between dots and dots with noise conditions (r(31) = 0.44, p < .001), and between parsed and natural conditions (r(31) = 0.45, p < .001). In other words, participant performance on artificial stimulus dot array conditions were correlated, and performance on naturalistic stimuli were correlated, but not across artificial and naturalistic stimuli (see Fig. 4).

Subsequent analyses used a binomial generalized linear mixed effect models (LME) approach in order to simultaneously account for the effects of several variables at the individual trial level. Models were implemented using the lme4 package in R14. Models contained fixed effects of ratio, condition, and total number, and a random effect (i.e., intercept) of participant. The dependent variable was trial accuracy (i.e., correct [1] or incorrect [0]). All continuous predictors (ratio, total number) were standardized (z-scored) prior to analysis using the scale() function in R to improve model convergence and interpretability.

We found a significant effect of numerical ratio (between the two stimulus arrays), χ2(8) = 1502, p < .001, such that accuracy decreased as numerical ratio (smaller number / larger number) increased (see Fig. 5A; Table 1). Follow-up within-condition analyses found that there was a significant effect of ratio in each condition (Artificial: χ2(2) = 402.19, p < .001; Artificial with Noise: χ2(2) = 235.80, p < .001; Naturalistic: χ2(2) = 495.55, p < .001; Parsed: χ2(2) = 376.25, p < .001; see Table 1). The ubiquitous presence of a ratio effect, consistent with Weber’s law, suggests similar deployment of the approximate number system across all conditions15.

In addition to, and independently of, the effect of ratio, we found a significant effect of the total number of items on each trial (summed across both comparison arrays), χ2(8) = 609.55, p < .001, such that accuracy decreased as total number of items on each trial increased. Moreover, we found a significant condition by total number interaction, χ2(7) = 145.22, p < .001, suggesting that the effect of total number on accuracy differed across conditions. Follow-up within-condition analyses demonstrated that although there was a significant effect of total number across all four conditions, there was a much larger effect of total number parsed and natural conditions (Parsed: χ2(2) = 286.26, p < .001; Naturalistic: χ2(2) = 259.94, p < .001), than in the Artificial and Artificial with Noise conditions (Artificial: χ2(2) = 32.00, p < .001; Artificial with Noise: χ2(2) = 30.87, p < .001) (see Fig. 5B; Table 1). This difference across conditions suggests that accuracy is more dramatically impacted by total number of items in both parsed and natural stimuli than artificial stimuli.

What explains the outsized effect of total number in Naturalistic and Parsed, compared to Artificial and Artificial with Noise, conditions? To evaluate the possibility that differences in occlusion may drive the effect of total number on mean accuracy, we split trials into two categories: those in which objects were occluded (to any extent) and those in which they were not occluded (Fig. 6A). For the Naturalistic and Naturalistic (Parsed) conditions separately, we used a binomial generalized mixed effect model with fixed effects of ratio, total number, and occlusion, with a random effect of participant. The dependent variable was trial accuracy (i.e., correct [1] or incorrect [0]). For both conditions, we found a significant interaction between the total number of items and occlusion (Naturalistic: χ2(4) = 69.87, p < .001 ; Parsed: χ2(4) = 62.98, p < .001), such that there was a greater effect of total number on mean accuracy when the stimuli contained occlusion than when they did not (Fig. 6B). These results suggest that poorer discrimination for naturalistic arrays is, in part, a result of the occlusion present.

Discussion

In the present study, we demonstrated that numerical discrimination of naturalistic scenes was significantly less accurate than discrimination of artificial dot arrays commonly used in numerical cognition research. Furthermore, we found that accuracy for all arrays decreased as the total number of items increased, and that, crucially, this effect was much greater for naturalistic and parsed arrays than for dot arrays and dot arrays with visual noise. Importantly, this effect of total number of elements was on top of, and independent from, the ratio difference between trial arrays, as it was calculated by summing across both arrays to index the total number of elements requiring perception.

Our results revealed that numerical discrimination of natural stimuli, compared to artificial stimuli, was significantly more impacted by the total number of items per trial, suggesting an item-level processing cost specific to natural arrays. This cost was particularly pronounced when arrays contained partially occluded items, compared to fully visible items, indicating that the observed item-wise cost in natural scenes likely relates to object segmentation processes. This finding aligns with research on object perception in natural scenes, where segmentation and individuation processes have been shown to be computationally demanding and attention-dependent17–19. Future work, however, should parametrically vary presentation times and response windows to better evaluate whether numerical discrimination in general, and of real-world scenes, in particular, constitutes a parallel or serial process.

Our results reveal an important interplay between object segmentation and numerical discrimination in complex visual scenes. This relationship directly challenges interpretations of numerical representation in early visual and subcortical areas20. Previous work has demonstrated that perceptual factors like connectedness and grouping can affect numerical judgments5,6, findings that some theories of automatic numerical extraction have incorporated10. However, our results suggest a stronger constraint: the interaction between total number and occlusion indicates that numerical extraction may not be “automatic” at all, but rather contingent on the visual system’s ability to parse object boundaries. This interdependence implies that studies using simplified, clearly segmented stimuli may overestimate the numerical capacities of early visual processing stages, as such stimuli bypass the segmentation challenges inherent in naturalistic viewing conditions.

These findings present challenges for theoretical perspectives that underemphasize the role of object segmentation in numerical perception. “Purely numerical” accounts21 often characterize numerosity as a primary visual attribute extracted relatively independently of other visual processes, whereas “purely non-numerical” accounts22–24 suggest that numerical judgments arise from statistical properties of visual scenes without necessarily requiring object individuation. Both perspectives struggle to explain why occlusion specifically interacts with set size to affect performance in the manner we observed.

While our results emphasize the impact of individuation and segmentation on numerical performance in the presence of occlusion, other cognitive factors, such as inhibitory control, may also contribute to performance differences. In the present task, participants may have needed to employ inhibitory control to avoid double-counting visual fragments of the same object or to suppress the misperception of several disconnected segments as multiple items. Such demands on executive function go beyond basic perceptual segmentation, introducing the possibility that individual differences in inhibitory control may influence accuracy. This aligns with previous research demonstrating that inhibitory control plays a crucial role in various numerical tasks, including magnitude comparison25 and arithmetic problem solving26. Considering the potential role of inhibitory control, alongside perceptual segmentation, could provide a richer and more comprehensive understanding of the mechanisms underlying numerical cognition in complex scenes. Future research incorporating direct manipulations or measurements of inhibitory control would be valuable in disentangling these processes.

The significant differences we found between artificial and natural stimuli raise important concerns about the generalizability of the numerical cognition literature to real-world scenarios. The absence of significant correlations in mean accuracy across natural and artificial conditions suggests fundamental differences in how participants process numerical information in naturalistic versus artificial stimuli. These findings have important methodological implications for the study of numerical cognition. The widespread use of artificial dot arrays in numerical cognition research has provided valuable insights into basic numerical processing mechanisms. However, the significant differences we observed between artificial and naturalistic stimuli suggest that complementary approaches using real-world stimuli are necessary. This is particularly important given that most everyday numerical judgments involve complex scenes with partially occluded objects, varying depths, and complex textures.

Though we only observed significant correlations between performance on parsed and naturalistic, and artificial and artificial with noise stimuli, we observed moderate, though non-significant, correlations between the naturalistic and noise stimuli (r = .26) and parsed and artificial stimuli (r = .30). Interestingly, these pairings might suggest that there are commonalities to discriminating stimuli with featural noise (as in naturalistic and noise stimuli) and without featural noise (as in parsed and artificial stimuli). Future work with larger sample sizes should examine these relations across stimulus types, to evaluate potential important stimulus features in naturalistic stimuli besides occlusion.

A key limitation of our study is that naturalistic and parsed stimuli were not generated with parametric control over low-level features. While this preserved ecological validity, as real word stimuli vary systematically from the typical dot arrays used in lab-based studies, it prevents us from fully disentangling effects of different visual properties such as size, spacing, and spatial frequency. Future work could use generative methods to create naturalistic stimuli with controlled variations in specific features. Importantly, though we cannot rule out all contributions of low-level features, the specificity of the effect of total number to occluded items strongly suggests that object segmentation demands, rather than other visual differences, drive the observed performance costs observed in naturalistic and parsed stimuli.

Finally, the study’s findings suggest that early naturalistic number discrimination abilities in infants might be influenced by their ability to segment objects, rather than purely by their numerical sensitivity. Indeed, young infants have more difficulty individuating objects based on featural information, such as color or pattern, than spatial information, suggesting difficulty dealing with occlusion27,28. If there are improvements in object individuation over early infant and childhood development29–31, and numerical sensitivity depends on veridical object segmentation (at least under conditions of occlusion), then developmental changes in numerical acuity and/or salience could be driven by changes in object segmentation accuracy32,33. Collectively, these results suggest that numerical cognition research needs to better integrate findings from object individuation and visual attention to fully understand how numerosity is processed and develops.
