Establishment of a Specialized Pectus Clinic: Referral Patterns, Diagnostic Characteristics, and Early Management Experience at a University Hospital
Authors: Zeead M. AlGhamdi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryObjectives:To describe the establishment and early impact of a dedicated pectus clinic at a university hospital, focusing on patient characteristics, referral pathways, clinical presentation, and initial management strategies.Methods:A retrospective observational study was conducted at a university hospital between January 2020 and April 2025, with 153 patients included in the final analysis. Data collected included demographic characteristics, referral sources, clinical presentation, deformity type, and treatment options. Descriptive statistical analysis was performed.Results:The mean patient age was 18.3 (range 1–44) years, with males accounting for 83.9% of cases. Pectus carinatum was the most common diagnosis (48.1%), followed by pectus excavatum (42.3%). Patient-initiated referrals, including those influenced by digital and social media platforms, represented 63.2% of referrals. Psychosocial concerns were the main presenting complaint in 60.6% of patients, whereas isolated functional symptoms were reported in 9%. Annual visits increased progressively throughout the study period. Non-operative treatment was commonly used for pectus carinatum, while surgical management was more frequent for pectus excavatum.Conclusions:A dedicated pectus clinic improved access to specialized care and supported structured management. The high proportion of patient-initiated referrals highlights the growing role of non-traditional referral pathways. The specialized clinic improved awareness, standardized the care, and addressed unmet needs in patients with pectus deformities.

Introduction

Pectus deformities, including pectus excavatum (PE) and pectus carinatum (PC), are among the most common congenital anomalies of the anterior chest wall, affecting approximately 1% of the population [1]. PE, characterized by posterior depression of the sternum and adjacent costal cartilages, accounts for nearly 90% of cases and occurs in 1 in 300–400 live births, PC, defined by anterior protrusion of the sternum and costal cartilages, is the second most common deformity, with an incidence of approximately 1 in 1,000 adolescents [2,3].

In addition to cosmetic concerns, pectus deformities can have significant psychosocial and functional implications, with affected individuals often reporting body image dissatisfaction, low self-esteem, and avoidance of social or physical activities, particularly during adolescence. Psychosocial distress, rather than objective cardiopulmonary impairment, frequently provides the impetus to seek medical consultation and treatment. Consequently, along with anatomical severity, contemporary management integrates psychological and patient-reported outcomes [4,5].

Some argue that intervention is unnecessary in many cases, as objective physiological impairment may be limited and evolving cultural perceptions have led to a greater acceptance of chest wall differences [6]. Nevertheless, treatment decisions should ideally be made on an individual basis, with reference to physical findings, symptom burden, psychosocial impact, patient preferences, and the risks and benefits of the available options [7]. Management includes non-operative and surgical approaches, with orthotic bracing and vacuum devices being effective in selected patients with PC and mild to moderate PE, respectively [8,9]. Surgical correction, using minimally invasive or open techniques, is generally reserved for severe deformities, when conservative therapy proves ineffective, or in cases of significant functional or psychosocial impairment [10].

However, despite the availability of effective non-operative and operative options, access to specialized pectus care remains limited in Saudi Arabia and the region. The relatively small number of healthcare professionals with a focused interest in chest wall deformity and the absence of a specialized pectus clinic may delay referrals, evaluations, and appropriate management. As a result, patients with pectus deformities often encounter barriers to specialized care. The delivery of care remains fragmented, with limited management options, variable referral pathways, patient selection, and follow-up practices, which may compromise continuity and consistency. By providing centralized, protocol-driven, multidisciplinary care tailored to this population, dedicated pectus deformity clinics have emerged to address these gaps and improve the provided care. These clinics focus on standardized assessment, individualized treatment planning, longitudinal follow-up, and systematic data collection [11]. However, published data describing their structure, organization, and experience remain limited, thereby highlighting the need to report specialized models of care.

This study describes the establishment and early experience of a dedicated pectus deformity clinic established at a university hospital, including patient characteristics and management pathways, and highlights the role of such specialized clinics in delivering standardized, patient-centered care. In subsequent deformity-specific studies, detailed outcome analyses and comprehensive descriptions of specific management strategies will be presented.

Methods

Between January 2020 and April 2025, we retrospectively reviewed the medical records of patients referred to the dedicated pectus deformity clinic at King Fahd Hospital of the University, Alkhobar, Eastern Province, Kingdom of Saudi Arabia. Institutional review board approval was obtained prior to retrospective data extraction and analysis, with authorization to review medical records covering the specified study period. Of the initially screened 174 presentations for suspected pectus deformity reported during this period, 18 were excluded as duplicate records, leaving details for 156 patients. Although a further three patients, who had no objective anterior chest wall deformity on clinical or radiological assessment were excluded from the final analysis, their details were retained in the screened cohort for the purposes of characterizing referral patterns. The final analysis cohort accordingly comprised 153 patients with confirmed chest wall deformities. Given the retrospective nature of the study, the Institutional review board waived the requirement for individual informed consent.

Although to ensure uniform assessment, treatment selection, and follow-up, the clinic follows predefined clinical pathways, this still provides scope for individualized decision-making based on the severity of deformities and patient preferences.

Referrals were established to have been made through outpatient clinics, primary care physicians, and self-referrals, including those based on information gained from digital and social media platforms. All patients underwent an initial clinical evaluation that included detailed history taking, physical examination, and documentation of deformity type and symmetry. Functional symptoms and psychosocial concerns related to the chest wall deformity were assessed as part of the routine clinical evaluation to identify the primary reasons for presentation to the clinic, whether functional, psychosocial, or both, rather than through standardized quality-of-life questionnaires or validated patient-reported outcome measures. Assessments of pulmonary function, echocardiography, and chest computed tomography were requested selectively according to the clinical indication.

Where appropriate, management prioritized non-operative treatment. For PE, conservative options, including vacuum bell therapy, were offered in selected cases based on age, deformity severity, symptom burden, and suitability. During the study period, 5 (7.5%) patients initiated vacuum bell therapy. Surgical correction was considered for adult patients, as well as for individuals of any age presenting with moderate to severe deformity, previous unsuccessful conservative treatment, or significant functional or psychosocial impact. A total of 37 (56.6%) patients underwent the minimally invasive repair of pectus excavatum (MIRPE) procedure. To assess severity, preoperative evaluation included clinical examination, pulmonary function assessment, echocardiography, and chest computed tomography.

For PC, dynamic compression bracing was the first-line approach in pediatric and adolescent age groups, and 50 (66%) patients were involved, whereas surgical correction, including the Abramson technique or modified Ravitch procedure, was reserved for 3 (4%) patients who failed to respond to or were unsuitable for non-operative management. The relatively limited use of vacuum bell therapy in patients with pectus excavatum reflects to some extent the developmental progression of the specialized clinic and the gradual introduction of non-operative management options over time. Initially, the clinic primarily provided operative management, followed later by the introduction of non-operative treatment options such as dynamic compression bracing for PC. Vacuum bell therapy for pectus excavatum was incorporated subsequently as part of the continued expansion of available treatment pathways. In addition, logistical and financial considerations may have influenced treatment selection, particularly given the limited availability of dynamic compression braces and vacuum bell devices within the governmental healthcare system, as well as the potential financial burden on families when self-funded. These findings highlight some of the practical challenges associated with implementing newer, non-operative modalities within specialized pectus services.

All patients presenting with PE or PC during the study period were included for analysis. with no exclusions made based on treatment modality, as both conservative and surgical options were offered according to clinical indication. Although some patients indicated a preference for non-operative management, surgery was recommended when conservative measures were deemed inappropriate by the treating surgeon. Each patient ultimately received an individualized treatment plan tailored to clinical characteristics and suitability.

At baseline and throughout care in the pectus deformity clinic, all the assessed patients underwent standardized evaluation. The initial assessment documented referral source, detailed history, physical examination, and deformity characteristics, including type, symmetry, severity, and perceived impact on function and quality of life.

Patients with PC treated with orthotic bracing were reviewed 4 weeks after brace fitting to assess compliance, skin tolerance, and early response, and then at 3-month intervals during the period of active correction. After satisfactory correction, follow-up continued at 3- to 6-month intervals during the maintenance phase.

Patients with PE who underwent minimally invasive repair using the Nuss technique were followed by performing clinical examinations and chest radiography to assess bar position, wound healing, and complications. The initial postoperative visit occurred 2 weeks after discharge, followed by visits at 1, 3, and 6 months, and thereafter annually until bar removal. To confirm bar position and procedural safety, chest computed tomography was performed prior to bar removal, particularly in adults. In addition, pain control, activity tolerance, and cosmetic satisfaction were assessed at each visit.

Outcomes were documented descriptively, including the degree of clinical correction, patient or parental satisfaction, and complications. Satisfaction was assessed at treatment completion, at the end of bracing therapy for patients with PC and after bar removal for patients with PE. Additional imaging was performed selectively when recurrence or complications were suspected.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as the means or medians with ranges, as appropriate. Categorical variables are reported as counts and percentages. All analyses were performed using SPSS version 24 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA).

Results

The final analysis cohort included 153 patients, with annual presentations being found to have undergone a progressive increase throughout the study period (Fig. 1). Mean age at presentation was 18.3 (median 16; range 1–44) years, with most patients being adolescents or young adults. Notably, there was marked male predominance (83.9% male, 16.1% female). Most patients were Saudi nationals (94.2%), among whom, 75.5% were referred from the Eastern Province of Saudi Arabia, reflecting the role of the clinic as a regional referral center. The remaining 24.5% were referred from other regions of Saudi Arabia and Gulf countries.

Annual number of patient presentations to the pectus deformity clinic during the study period (2020–2024).

Pectus carinatum was the most common diagnosis (48.1%), with PE accounting for 42.3% and acquired chest wall deformities, rib flare, and other congenital anomalies accounting for the remaining cases. Referral sources were diverse. Clinic-based referrals accounted for 36.8% of cases, whereas patient-initiated referrals, including self-referral and those motivated through digital and social media platforms, represented 63.2%, thereby highlighting the importance of non-traditional pathways in accessing specialized care.

Psychosocial distress was the most common presenting concern (60.6%), with Isolated functional and cardiopulmonary symptoms being less frequent at 9.0%, and 18.6% of patients reporting both psychosocial and functional impacts. A further 11.6% reported no symptoms at presentation. Most patients had no comorbidities (86.5%), whereas among those affected, scoliosis was the most commonly reported condition (7.1%), followed by connective tissue disorders such as Marfan syndrome (1.9%). Discussion

Pectus deformities, including PC, PE, and pectus arcuatum, comprise a heterogeneous group of chest wall conditions with variable anatomical severity, psychosocial impact, and treatment requirements. However, despite advances in non-operative and surgical techniques, care delivery remains fragmented, with inconsistent referral pathways, patient management, and follow-up practices. Historically, patients with PE were often advised that the deformity would resolve with age, that surgery carried substantial risks with limited benefits, and that the condition was primarily cosmetic with minimal functional impact. Such perceptions have accordingly contributed to delayed referral, under-recognition of the symptom burden, and limited access to specialized evaluation [12]. In the cohort described herein, the mean age of patients at presentation was 18.3 years (20.32 for PE and 16.09 for PC), thus indicating delayed referral and that many patients are evaluated late adolescence or early adulthood rather than earlier, when counseling or intervention could begin (Fig. 2).

Age distribution of patients with pectus excavatum and pectus carinatum.

A notable finding was the diversity of referral sources. Although clinic-based referrals comprised a substantial proportion, patient-initiated referrals, including self-referral and those based on exposure to digital and social media content, represented the majority. This pattern highlights the complementary roles of institutional pathways and non-traditional mechanisms in facilitating access to specialized care for conditions that remain under-recognized or misunderstood. Evidence from among different disciplines indicates the growing influence of digitally driven self-referrals, particularly for specialized services such as pectus care, for which non-traditional pathways may become the predominant route to evaluation [13,14].

Despite the higher reported prevalence of PE in the general population, at our clinic, PC accounted for 48.1% of consultations, compared with the 42.3% for PE (Table 1). Pectus deformities is typically characterized by a marked male predominance, with an estimated male-to-female ratio of 4:1, and in approximately 15% of cases is associated with connective tissue disorders such as Marfan syndrome and scoliosis. Although given the small sample size assessed in this study, we are unable to draw definitive conclusions regarding broader epidemiological trends, this distribution is consistent with observations reported by Marcelo et al. and may reflect the influence of a dedicated chest wall clinic. Greater awareness, accessibility, and targeted referral may contribute to a more widespread recognition of PC, resulting in a distribution that is more comparable to, or even exceeds, PE in clinical practice [15].

It has been established that there is a clinically significant association between pectus deformities and adolescent idiopathic scoliosis. In healthy schoolchildren, scoliosis is reported in 2% to 3% of the general population, whereas pectus deformity-associated scoliosis occurs in approximately 5% to 10% of cases [16,17]. In the cohort assessed in this study, 7.1% of patients with pectus deformities also had concomitant scoliosis at the initial evaluation, and the dedicated clinic facilitated systematic recognition of this association, which has important clinical implications. In cases of adolescent idiopathic scoliosis that co-occur with a pectus deformity, referral to a spine surgeon is routinely arranged, as scoliosis in this context may be more severe and have a greater necessity of surgical correction [18]. Consequently, this highlights the need consider the interaction between the two deformities when planning management, as correction of one may influence the status of the other [19].

The establishment of specialized clinics provides a structured framework for standardized care, follow-up, and systematic data collection, enabling the identification of knowledge gaps and continuous quality improvement. Such clinics also represent an important proportion of the academic hospital workload and contribute substantially to medical education. By delivering condition-specific, patient-centered care, they provide a focused learning environment that encourages residents and medical students to pursue clinical excellence. The skills developed in these settings will enhance the quality of care they go on to provide throughout their careers [20].

Study limitation

The limitations of this study include its retrospective single-center design. In addition, the study primarily describes the establishment and early clinical experience of a specialized pectus clinic and was not designed to evaluate long-term outcomes or to compare treatment modalities, as its primary objective was to present the impact of establishing a dedicated pectus service.

In conclusion, specialized clinics in Saudi Arabia for patients with pectus deformities, including PE and PC, address the substantial physical, psychological, and functional burdens associated with these conditions. Dedicated pectus clinics improve access to specialized care and facilitate multidisciplinary management, thereby contributing to more consistent and patient-centered outcomes. Such clinics provide comprehensive assessment to ensure accurate diagnosis and tailored treatment planning.
