Integrating BERT-XL with multi-dimensional knowledge graphs for knowledge completion and relation reasoning in archival fragmented texts
Authors: Zhenghan Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Archival fragmented texts pose considerable challenges for knowledge extraction owing to semantic deficiency, contextual discontinuity, and entity recognition ambiguity, all arising from document deterioration and incomplete digitization. This paper proposes an integrated framework that combines BERT-XL with multi-dimensional knowledge graphs to tackle these challenges in archival text processing. The framework employs a synergistic fusion mechanism incorporating cross-modal attention and gated integration to enable bidirectional information flow between neural text representations and structured graph knowledge. We design knowledge completion algorithms that draw on multi-dimensional graph structures encompassing temporal, hierarchical, and spatial dimensions to infer missing entities and attributes. Additionally, we develop multi-hop relation reasoning methods that traverse heterogeneous graph dimensions to discover implicit connections across document boundaries. Experiments conducted on historical archival corpora from three provincial archives demonstrate that the proposed approach achieves substantial improvements over baseline methods, with Hits@1 of 0.578 and MRR of 0.648 for knowledge completion, and F1 score of 0.769 for relation reasoning. The results confirm that integrating contextual language models with multi-dimensional knowledge representations effectively compensates for information scarcity in fragmented archival contexts.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-52038-0.

Introduction

Digital archives now hold unprecedented volumes of historical documents, yet physical decay, incomplete digitization, and fragmentation continue to obstruct meaningful computational analysis1. When manuscripts deteriorate or transcription fails, the resulting text segments become semantically impoverished and contextually isolated2. We refer to this pervasive condition as archival text fragmentation, a phenomenon that disrupts knowledge extraction across administrative records, official correspondence, and historical manuscripts alike3.

Early computational treatments of fragmented texts relied on rule-based pattern matching and statistical language models4. These methods rarely survived contact with severe semantic gaps. Deep learning brought new possibilities, but mainstream frameworks still assume continuous, well-formed input rather than the irregular fragments that archival work routinely produces5. Knowledge completion—originally a knowledge graph technique for inferring missing entities and relations—offers one avenue forward6, while relation reasoning extends inference across disparate textual pieces7.

We wish to be precise about why this work matters today. One might suppose that OCR and digitization have rendered archival text extraction a settled problem, but that impression holds only for modern, cleanly typeset documents. Historical archives tell a different story. Physical decay, ink fading, and non-standard typefaces push standard OCR pipelines well beyond their comfort zone, producing outputs riddled with garbled characters, truncated entities, and missing clauses. These corrupted outputs constitute what information scientists call “dark data”—material that has been digitized in some nominal sense yet remains computationally inaccessible because its content cannot be reliably parsed or queried59. Heidorn59 first drew attention to the pervasive problem of dark data in scientific repositories; we argue that historical archives represent an even more acute instance of this phenomenon, where physical degradation compounds the usual challenges of poor indexing and metadata. Our framework is designed precisely for this dark-data recovery scenario: it does not replace OCR but rather operates downstream of OCR, compensating for the residual errors and information losses that conventional pipelines leave behind. In this capacity, it serves as a computational tool for illuminating historical records that would otherwise remain hidden in plain sight—digitized but effectively unreachable by researchers.

Transformer-based pre-trained language models have redefined contextual representation8. BERT and related architectures excel at named entity recognition, semantic similarity, and question answering9. BERT-XL, with its segment recurrence and relative position encoding, captures longer-range dependencies than standard BERT10. Yet even BERT-XL stumbles when applied directly to archival materials: its pre-training corpora consist of modern, coherent text whose vocabulary, syntax, and discourse differ markedly from historical records11.

Knowledge graphs structure factual knowledge as entity–relation networks, enabling explicit relational reasoning12. Multi-dimensional knowledge graphs add temporal validity, hierarchical organization, and spatial attributes to this foundation13. Archival collections naturally exhibit such multi-dimensional structure—institutional hierarchies, chronological sequences, and cross-referential document clusters14. Combining language models with structured graphs has attracted growing interest, but existing integration strategies seldom address archival fragmentation head-on.

We identify three core obstacles. Semantic deficiency arises when isolated phrases admit multiple interpretations without surrounding context15. Contextual discontinuity appears when physical damage or partial digitization severs logical links between segments. Entity recognition falters when names surface in abbreviated, archaic, or partially legible forms that diverge from pre-training patterns16. Together, these obstacles throttle scholarly access to digitized heritage.

Prior BERT–knowledge-graph integrations fall short of our needs for specific reasons. KG-BERT17 treats triples as sentences for scoring but ignores multi-dimensional attributes. ERNIE and KEPLER inject entity embeddings yet assume coherent input text. Graph-text fusion transformers such as GreaseLM blend modalities without mechanisms for missing-information compensation. None of these methods was designed for discontinuous, semantically sparse archival fragments. Table 1 summarizes the gaps.

Our framework addresses these gaps through three distinct contributions. First, we design a bidirectional fusion architecture that allows neural text representations and multi-dimensional graph embeddings to mutually reinforce one another via cross-modal attention and gated integration. Second, we construct a multi-dimensional knowledge graph encoding temporal, hierarchical, and spatial relationships specific to archival organization. Third, we develop multi-hop reasoning algorithms that traverse heterogeneous dimensions to infer implicit connections even when direct evidence is sparse. Experimental evaluation on corpora from three provincial archives demonstrates substantial gains over strong baselines in both knowledge completion (Hits@1 0.578, MRR 0.648) and relation reasoning (F1 0.769).

Illustrative Example. Consider a fragmented administrative letter: “Director Zhang… Provincial Bureau… approved the transfer… 1Mo. 1935.” Standard models struggle because the month is partially illegible and the recipient organization is truncated. Our framework retrieves candidate entities from the knowledge graph (e.g., “Provincial Education Bureau,” “Provincial Finance Bureau”), aligns temporal constraints (early 1935), and applies hierarchical reasoning (Director-level authority) to resolve ambiguities that purely textual inference cannot settle.

Theoretical foundations and technical background

BERT-XL model principles and architecture

The Transformer architecture19 computes representations through parallel self-attention, sidestepping the vanishing-gradient issues that plagued recurrent networks and enabling efficient large-scale pre-training20. BERT21 builds on this foundation with bidirectional masked language modeling. Its attention mechanism—scaled dot-product attention followed by multi-head concatenation22—captures contextual dependencies between arbitrary token positions. We omit the standard formulations here, as they are well documented in the original papers; interested readers may consult Appendix A for the full mathematical details including Equations A1–A3.

What matters for our purposes is a specific limitation: standard Transformers truncate context at a fixed window, which hinders processing of related archival fragments dispersed across separate segments23. BERT-XL addresses this through segment recurrence, caching hidden states from earlier segments for reuse when encoding subsequent ones24. It further replaces absolute position indices with relative position encoding25, a design choice that preserves positional coherence even when fragments arrive in uncertain order. These two properties—cross-segment memory and order-robust encoding—make BERT-XL a natural backbone for archival text processing. The detailed equations for relative position attention appear in Appendix A.

Archival-specific adaptation. We modify BERT-XL in two ways for our domain. First, we continue pre-training on a 2.3-million-token archival corpus containing Republican-era administrative documents (1912–1949) to adjust vocabulary distributions. Second, we introduce a fragment boundary token [FRAG] that signals discontinuity, enabling the model to reset positional expectations at fragment junctions. These adaptations reduce perplexity on held-out archival text from 38.7 (vanilla BERT-XL) to 24.2 (adapted). Section 4.1 reports full statistics.

These dimensions allow downstream reasoning to filter candidates by date range, verify hierarchical plausibility, and restrict spatial scope.

Ontology design. Our ontology follows archival science principles of provenance and original order29. Four layers organize the schema: (1) Entity layer: concrete instances—persons, organizations, documents, events. (2) Concept layer: abstract categories (e.g., “Official Correspondence,” “Personnel File”) that mirror archival arrangement30. (3) Temporal layer: time-stamped relations and period attributes encoding dynasties, regimes, or calendar years. (4) Spatial layer: geographic entities and location-based relations common in administrative records31.

Knowledge completion and relation reasoning methods

Relationship between knowledge completion and relation reasoning. These two tasks address complementary facets of information recovery. Knowledge completion fills gaps within individual triples—predicting a missing head or tail entity given the relation. Relation reasoning, by contrast, infers entirely new relational facts by chaining existing triples across multiple hops. In our framework the two tasks interact bidirectionally: successful entity completion supplies additional nodes for reasoning paths, while successful path-based reasoning provides contextual evidence that disambiguates entity candidates. We limit reasoning depth to four hops, a practical ceiling beyond which accuracy degrades sharply (see Sect. 4.3).

Knowledge Completion. Given a partial triple with a missing tail, the task seeks the most plausible completion:4\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\\hat :{t}=\mathrm{a}\mathrm{r}\mathrm{g}{\mathrm{m}\mathrm{a}\mathrm{x}}_{{t}^{{\prime\:}}\in\:E}P\left({t}^{{\prime\:}}\right|h,r)$$\end{document}

Here E is the candidate entity set and the probability reflects semantic plausibility34.

Translation-based models interpret relations as vector translations. TransE scores triples via \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\parallel\:h+r-t\parallel\:$$\end{document}, an elegant scheme that falters on one-to-many mappings35. TransH and TransR introduce relation-specific hyperplanes and projection spaces36. Semantic matching models—DistMult, ComplEx—use bilinear or complex-valued scoring to capture symmetric and asymmetric patterns37.

Relation Reasoning. Reasoning derives new facts by chaining existing ones. Rule-based reasoning applies symbolic Horn clauses:5\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{r}_{new}(h,t)\leftarrow\:{r}_{1}(h,{e}_{1})\wedge\:{r}_{2}({e}_{1},{e}_{2})\wedge\:...\wedge\:{r}_{n}({e}_{n-1},t)$$\end{document}

This approach offers interpretability but scales poorly when rule spaces explode38. Embedding-based reasoning performs inference implicitly via vector operations39. Path-based methods strike a middle ground, explicitly enumerating multi-hop paths and scoring them:6\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:P\left(t|h,r\right)=\sum\:_{p\in\:{\mathcal{P}}_{h\to\:t}}P\left(p|h,r\right)\cdot\:P\left(t|p,h\right)$$\end{document}

where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\mathcal{P}}_{h\to\:t}$$\end{document} collects paths from h to t40.

Illustrative Example. Suppose fragments mention “Director Li” and “Provincial Archive” separately, with no direct relation observed. Path-based reasoning might discover: Director Li → supervised → Document Section → subordinate_to → Provincial Archive, yielding an inferred relation (Director Li, affiliated_with, Provincial Archive). This inference, in turn, helps resolve an ambiguous entity mention “the Director” in a third fragment.

Challenges in Archival Contexts. Translation models demand abundant training examples per relation—rarely available when fragmentation limits observable instances41. Semantic matching requires rich entity contexts that fragments seldom provide. Rule-based reasoning needs explicit relation statements, yet archival text often leaves relations implicit. Path-based methods can aggregate weak evidence, but path sparsity in fragmented graphs hampers connectivity42. Our integrated approach compensates by fusing contextual neural encoding with multi-dimensional graph structure, allowing textual cues and graph topology to reinforce one another.

Archival fragmented text preprocessing and feature extraction

Archival fragments differ qualitatively from modern digital text. Physical decay, OCR misreadings, and historical orthography inject noise patterns absent from contemporary corpora43.

OCR and Image Processing Integration. For severely damaged documents, we combine Tesseract OCR with image preprocessing: adaptive thresholding handles ink bleed-through, morphological operations remove noise, and contrast enhancement recovers faded text. When OCR confidence drops below 0.6, the system queues the region for human review. This hybrid approach reduces character error rate from 8.3% (raw OCR) to 2.1% (post-processing) on our test set.

Tokenization must respect domain-specific terminology. We combine a statistical segmenter with a 15,000-entry archival lexicon so that institutional names (”Provincial Education Bureau”), official titles (“Director-General”), and specialized terms remain intact44. Named entity recognition then labels persons, organizations, locations, dates, and document types using a fine-tuned BERT-CRF tagger achieving 89.2% F1 on our annotated subset.

Figure 3 presents the end-to-end data flow of our integrated framework from text input to final predictions. The pipeline begins with preprocessed archival fragments entering the BERT-XL encoder, which produces contextualized token embeddings for each input sequence. These embeddings then pass through a named entity recognition module that identifies mentions of persons, organizations, dates, and other relevant entity types. For each detected mention, the system queries the multi-dimensional knowledge graph to retrieve candidate entities based on string similarity and type constraints. The cross-modal attention and gated fusion module subsequently combines textual representations with graph-derived entity embeddings, producing knowledge-enhanced representations that capture both contextual semantics and structured relational information. Finally, the enriched representations feed into two parallel output branches: the knowledge completion scorer ranks candidate entities to fill missing slots in incomplete triples, while the relation reasoning path ranker evaluates multi-hop traversal paths to infer implicit relations between linked entity pairs.

Synergistic fusion mechanism of multi-dimensional knowledge graph and BERT-XL

Bridging the representational gap between knowledge graph embeddings and text semantic embeddings presents a fundamental challenge for integration. These two modalities originate from distinct learning paradigms—graph embeddings encode structural relational patterns while text embeddings capture sequential contextual semantics. Our alignment strategy projects both representation types into a shared semantic space through learnable transformation matrices. Several patterns stand out. Word-level damage proves easiest to repair, largely because the temporal dimension of the knowledge graph effectively constrains candidate interpretations for garbled dates and names, and local context around the corrupted token often remains intact. Phrase-level truncation is moderately harder: the model must reconstruct entity boundaries from partial strings, a task where hierarchical constraints prove critical. Sentence-level gaps present a steeper challenge, since the model must infer entire missing propositions rather than individual tokens, pushing the system toward heavier reliance on multi-hop reasoning paths. Cross-document fragmentation, unsurprisingly, poses the greatest difficulty. When relevant evidence is scattered across physically separate files, the model depends on graph connectivity that may itself be sparse. We regard the cross-document performance (Hits@1 of 0.463) as an honest reflection of the current framework’s ceiling and a priority for future improvement.

Dimensional contribution analysis reveals that temporal and hierarchical dimensions contribute most significantly to archival knowledge completion. Removing the temporal dimension alone causes 8.3% performance degradation, while eliminating hierarchical organization information results in 6.7% decline. Spatial dimension removal produces more modest 3.2% reduction, reflecting the administrative rather than geographical nature of our test collections. The entity layer naturally contributes most fundamentally, with its removal rendering the model essentially equivalent to text-only baselines.

Ablation experiments systematically validated each architectural component. Removing cross-modal attention reduced Hits@1 by 5.4% points (from 0.578 to 0.524), confirming its critical role in bridging textual and graph representations. Eliminating the gated fusion mechanism caused a 3.4-point drop (to 0.544), while replacing iterative interaction layers with single-pass processing resulted in 4.2-point degradation (to 0.536). These results, detailed alongside computational cost tradeoffs in Table 9, collectively demonstrate that each proposed component contributes meaningfully to the overall system effectiveness. Cross-Document Reasoning Case Study. Two documents from separate archives mention “Deputy Director Liu” in different contexts: an approval letter (Archive A, File 1934-A-027) and a personnel record (Archive B, File 1935-B-103). No direct textual overlap connects them. Our system identifies the entity mention in both fragments, retrieves candidates from the knowledge graph, and applies temporal (1934–1935 activity period) and hierarchical (deputy-director rank at a bureau with transfer authority) constraints to resolve the ambiguity. The multi-hop path Liu Wenhua -> appointed_at -> Provincial Finance Bureau -> administers -> Southern Branch confirms the identity and establishes the cross-document relation, raising linking confidence from 0.41 (text-only) to 0.89 (full model).

The hierarchical paths from both candidates terminated at the same target institution, and the temporal overlap left the model without a decisive signal. Discussion

Our experimental outcomes point to a pattern that merits closer scrutiny. What stands out most is that the integration of BERT-XL with multi-dimensional knowledge graphs produces gains exceeding what simple additive combination would predict. This super-additive effect suggests a genuine synergy between the two representational paradigms. When textual context alone fails to disambiguate, structured graph knowledge injects decisive constraints; when graph connectivity proves thin, rich contextual embeddings compensate by capturing implicit semantic relationships. Neither modality, operating in isolation, matches what their combined deployment achieves.

BERT-XL segment recurrence proves well-suited to archival fragmented texts, though perhaps not for the most obvious reason. The chief benefit is not processing longer continuous sequences, since fragmented texts tend to be short, but rather maintaining coherent representations when encoding related fragments sequentially. In practice, the model accumulates context across fragment boundaries, treating a set of related fragments almost as a single extended document. This property aligns naturally with archival organization, where documents within a file or series share contextual ties despite their physical separation.

Table 14 summarizes the systematic error analysis conducted on our model’s predictions, categorizing failure cases into four distinct modes. Cross-institutional reasoning accounts for 12.3% of errors, where accuracy drops to 54.2% when inference requires traversing boundaries between separate bureaucratic hierarchies due to sparse inter-institutional edges in the knowledge graph. Temporal ambiguity affects 8.7% of instances, with performance declining to 48.1% when fragments lack explicit date references and multiple plausible time periods exist. Rare entities appearing fewer than five times in training constitute the largest failure category at 23.1%, achieving only 38.0% Hits@1 compared to the overall average of 57.8%, reflecting insufficient training signal for long-tail entities. Highly fragmented inputs with over 40% missing content represent 6.2% of cases, where performance drops to 41.2% due to severe information scarcity. These findings highlight specific areas for future improvement, including enriching cross-institutional graph connectivity, developing temporal disambiguation modules, and exploring few-shot learning techniques for rare entity linking.

Adaptability across different archival text types varies. Administrative correspondence and institutional reports benefit most, as these document types exhibit regular structural patterns and reference extensive organizational networks that the multi-dimensional graph captures well. Personal papers and informal communications prove more challenging, with idiosyncratic expression patterns and social networks less completely represented in formal archival metadata. Technical and scientific documents occupy a middle ground, benefiting from domain terminology consistency while sometimes lacking the relational density that enables effective graph-based reasoning.

We must acknowledge that all three provincial archives share a common genre: Republican-era Chinese administrative records (1912–1949). This homogeneity reinforces internal validity but constrains external generalizability. We cannot confidently predict how our framework would perform on medieval European manuscripts, Arabic diplomatic correspondence, or Japanese colonial records, where scripts, organizational hierarchies, and temporal conventions differ fundamentally. A preliminary transfer experiment on 2,000 private manuscript fragments (personal letters, 1920–1940 s) yielded Hits@1 of 0.471, below our main result (0.578) but still exceeding the best baseline (GreaseLM at 0.389 on the same set). This suggests moderate transferability even without extensive domain-specific tuning, though broader cross-cultural and cross-linguistic validation remains a necessary next step before any strong claims of universal applicability can be supported.

Several practical strategies could accelerate domain transfer. The continued pre-training stage (Sect. 2.1) is deliberately modular: substituting the 2.3-million-token archival corpus for a comparable corpus from a new domain should adapt BERT-XL vocabulary and syntax distributions without altering the fusion architecture. The multi-dimensional knowledge graph ontology could be restructured for different archival traditions, such as replacing administrative hierarchies with ecclesiastical or feudal structures, while preserving the dimensional embedding framework. Incremental knowledge graph updating mechanisms would enable continuous expansion as new materials are digitized, rather than requiring full retraining. We further note that knowledge distillation into smaller student models and quantization-aware training represent viable paths toward reducing the computational barrier for institutions with limited GPU resources. Exploring few-shot learning and data augmentation strategies for rare entity types60 may also address the long-tail problem identified in our error analysis (Table 14).

Knowledge completion and relation reasoning exhibit bidirectional enhancement that warrants explicit acknowledgment. Improved entity prediction directly enables longer reasoning paths by reducing error propagation across multi-hop inference chains. Simultaneously, successful relation reasoning provides feedback that refines entity prediction confidence, particularly for ambiguous mentions where relational context disambiguates between candidate entities. We observed this effect most clearly in iterative processing scenarios, where alternating between completion and reasoning tasks progressively improved both outcomes compared to single-pass processing.

Computational complexity raises practical concerns. Our full model requires approximately 203ms per reasoning instance on GPU hardware, which suits batch processing but may constrain real-time interactive use. Memory demands scale linearly with knowledge graph size, reaching 12GB for our experimental graph. The modular architecture, however, permits selective component activation: routine queries can bypass full multi-hop reasoning, reserving intensive processing for genuinely complex cases. For institutions operating under tight computational budgets, the lite configuration offers a reasonable compromise between accuracy and speed.

What sets our approach apart from existing methods is fundamentally a question of how knowledge is represented and accessed. Traditional knowledge graph methods treat text as raw material for triple extraction, discarding contextual richness once structured facts are obtained. Pure neural approaches encode context implicitly but forgo explicit relational structure for systematic reasoning. Our integration retains both: contextual nuance remains accessible through BERT-XL embeddings, while structured relationships support explicit path-based inference. This dual representation accounts for the performance advantages most visible in scenarios that demand both semantic understanding and logical reasoning over incomplete information.

Conclusion

This paper has investigated knowledge completion and relation reasoning for archival fragmented texts, a persistent challenge at the intersection of digital humanities and archival science. We developed an integrated framework that combines BERT-XL contextual encoding with multi-dimensional knowledge graph structures tailored to archival domain requirements. The framework confronts three fundamental difficulties: semantic deficiency, contextual discontinuity, and entity recognition ambiguity, all of which have long impeded effective processing of fragmentary historical documents.

Our contributions span several interconnected dimensions. The fusion architecture establishes bidirectional information flow between neural text representations and structured graph knowledge through cross-modal attention and gated integration. This design treats text and graph as complementary modalities rather than competing ones. The knowledge-enhanced text representation enriches fragmentary textual evidence with structured relational context, enabling robust understanding even when local information proves insufficient. The multi-hop relation reasoning algorithm navigates complex paths across dimensional boundaries, revealing implicit connections that neither direct pattern matching nor simple graph traversal could uncover on its own.

The practical implications extend across multiple application domains. Archival digital management benefits from automated gap-filling in incomplete records and intelligent linking across document collections. Historical document restoration gains computational assistance for reconstructing damaged or partially preserved texts. Intelligent retrieval systems can surface semantically related materials even when explicit keyword overlap is absent, broadening discovery possibilities for researchers working with fragmentary historical sources.

Candid acknowledgment of limitations guides future directions. Our experiments drew from three provincial archives with predominantly Chinese administrative content from 1912 to 1949, meaning our effectiveness claims hold firmly within that genre and period but should be extended cautiously to other archival traditions, languages, and historical eras. A pilot transfer experiment confirmed that in-domain performance (Hits@1 0.578) exceeds cross-domain performance (0.471 on personal letters), though even the transferred model outperformed baselines. Domain transfer remains challenging when target archives differ substantially in vocabulary, organizational structure, or temporal scope. Real-time processing constraints currently preclude interactive applications requiring sub-second response times, although our lite configuration (58ms on A100) approaches that threshold. Computational demands remain substantial: the full model requires 31.2GB GPU memory and 28.7 h of training, restricting deployment to server-class infrastructure. Knowledge distillation and model compression techniques could mitigate this barrier in future work.

Several future research directions hold particular promise. Large-scale domain-adaptive pretraining on diverse archival corpora spanning multiple languages, scripts, and centuries could strengthen the model foundational language understanding for historical texts. Cross-cultural collaborations with European national archives and Middle Eastern manuscript repositories would test whether our multi-dimensional ontology design generalizes or demands fundamental restructuring.

The most pressing direction involves multimodal processing. Our current framework operates exclusively on text and structured knowledge graphs, leaving unaddressed the many archival materials that resist machine-readable text extraction: handwritten manuscripts with non-standard calligraphy, documents where physical layout carries semantic meaning, and severely damaged pages where visual pattern recognition might succeed where OCR fails. Future work should investigate integrating document image features, perhaps through a visual encoder such as LayoutLM or a vision Transformer fine-tuned on historical document images61, with our existing text-graph fusion architecture. Handwriting analysis models could provide character-level confidence signals informing the gated fusion mechanism. Physical artifact metadata (paper type, ink composition, binding structure) could be encoded as additional knowledge graph dimensions. We view this multimodal extension not as optional but as a necessary evolution if the framework is to serve archives in their full material complexity.

Incremental knowledge updating mechanisms represent a third priority, enabling continuous graph expansion as new archival materials are digitized without requiring complete retraining. Together, these directions point toward increasingly capable tools for illuminating the dark data within our documentary heritage, tools that respect the material specificity of historical records while bringing modern computational methods to bear on their recovery.
