The educational effectiveness in flexible bronchoscopic intubation training using three-dimensional printed difficult airway models for anaesthesia residents: a single centre randomised controlled study
Authors: Chen-Tse Lee, Kuang-Fang Lin, Chia-Wei Lee, Wei-Hsuan Tai, Chun-Yu Wu, Yen-Yuan Chen, Wei-Zen Sun, Kuang-Cheng Chan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFlexible bronchoscopic intubation (FBI) is a vital skill for anaesthesiologists managing difficult airways. Traditional training models often fail to replicate real difficult airway scenarios, limiting their educational value. This study aimed to assess the effectiveness of three-dimensional (3D) printed difficult airway models, developed from patient CT/MRI images, in improving FBI skills among anaesthesia residents compared to conventional manikin models.MethodsTwenty-eight anaesthesia residents were randomised equally into the 3D-printed model group and the AirSim manikin group. Each resident received a standardised lecture, followed by FBI training using their assigned model and then a crossover with the alternate model. Educational effectiveness was assessed using a global rating scale, performance on the ORSIM simulator scenarios, and a trainee-reported questionnaire measuring suitability, realism, and educational effectiveness.ResultsThe global rating scales of operative performance and FBI time improved significantly after training in both groups. The post-training performance on the ORSIM simulator did not differ significantly between both groups. Compared with the AirSim manikin model, the 3D-printed model had a higher trainee-reported educational effectiveness (8.6 ± 1.0 vs. 7.9 ± 1.0; p = 0.050). Exploratory post-hoc analyses suggested that prior FBI experience modified the training response.ConclusionsBoth 3D-printed difficult airway models and conventional manikins were associated with improvement in FBI performance, with no statistically significant between-group differences in objective skill-acquisition outcomes. Trainees perceived the 3D-printed model as more educationally effective.Trial registrationNot applicable. Introduction

Flexible bronchoscopic intubation (FBI) is a crucial skill for anaesthesiologists to master and considered a cornerstone in the management of difficult airways [1, 2]. Airway management for patients with difficult airways remains highly challenging, requiring considerable learning time before these techniques can be safely applied in real clinical practice [3]. The difficulty in learning to manoeuvre the flexible bronchoscope may be the hardest part of learning FBI, including complex motor skills and hand-eye coordination, and lack of experience and training has been found to be the main reason for insufficient competence [4].

Three-dimensional (3D) technique could be a promising solution to the scarce real clinical exposure to difficult airway condition [5, 6]. Current literature has reported that 3D-printed models may enhance the visualisation and characterisation of patient-specific anatomy, and in this way help in pre-surgical planning [7–11]. Efforts to utilise 3D printing for the purposes of medical education, specifically skill building, are few yet emerging [12–14]. Considering the reported advantages in visualisation and characterisation, 3D-printed difficult airway models mimicking real scenarios faced in clinical practice may help anaesthesia residents to learn more efficiently for how to deal with difficult airways and to become familiar with various FBI techniques. However, this hypothesis has yet to be investigated.

Studies evaluating FBI training have demonstrated that proficiency in manoeuvring a flexible bronchoscope can be acquired through repeated practice [3, 15–17]. However, they were only evaluated by low and median fidelity models and even anaesthetised and paralysed patients with normal airway [4, 15]. Recently, the ORSIM bronchoscopy simulator (Airway Simulation Limited, Auckland, New Zealand) which incorporates some virtual difficult airway scenarios has been developed and may be useful as an assessment tool to evaluate FBI skills [18].

In this prospective randomised controlled study, we aimed to develop difficult airway models from the CT/MRI images of real difficult airway cases by using 3D printing techniques and evaluate the educational effectiveness of these 3D-printed models compared to conventional manikin models in FBI training for anaesthesia residents.

Methods

Study design

The protocol for this prospective, randomised controlled study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of National Taiwan University Hospital (201908009RIN). The manuscript was prepared following the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) 2010 checklist.

Our study population consisted of anaesthesia residents from first year to four year of training in our hospital. The participating anaesthesia residents enrolled from National Taiwan University Hospital were randomly assigned to either the 3D-printed model group or the AirSim manikin (TruCorp AirSim Bronchi, GERIN Technology Co., Ltd.) group at a 1:1 ratio using the sealed envelope method. Before the practical sessions, all participants attended a lecture on airway anatomy and FBI. This session also included a review of imaging from patients with various difficult airway anatomies and an introduction to the training models used in the study. Following these lectures, residents in both study groups practised FBI three times for each two different clinical scenarios using their assigned models, either the 3D-printed models (a simulated airway of laryngeal cancer approached through the nose, and a simulated airway of laryngeal cancer approached through the oral cavity) (Fig. 1) or the AirSim manikin models (tongue oedema, and pharyngeal obstruction with cervical fixation) [4]. After completing the designated FBI training, residents will be assessed using five different ORSIM bronchoscope simulator scenarios.

In a cross-over design, each resident in both study groups repeated FBI practice following the aforementioned steps with the alternate model. The crossover design was undertaken to give all trainees exposure to both modalities and was not used for primary outcome ascertainment.

Devices

The two clinical scenarios for each arm reflected the available scenarios for that technology: the AirSim manikin provides a fixed set of interchangeable inserts (tongue oedema; pharyngeal obstruction with cervical fixation), whereas each 3D-printed model reproduced the airway anatomy of the selected scenarios from source patients (laryngeal cancer).

The development of 3D-printed difficult airway models followed standard process.

We reviewed the medical records from January 2010 to August 2019 in National Taiwan University Hospital and searched for cases documented with a potentially difficult intubation during pre-anaesthesia assessment or with an unanticipated difficult intubation during anaesthesia induction. The CT/MRI image files of interest were applied for acquisition from the Department of Medical Imaging.

We used the AirSim manikin as the main body of our 3D-printed difficult airway model in a common simulation supine position. In this way the teaching aid could be switched to different styles of 3D-printed difficult airways as needed. Assessment by ORSIM simulator after the assigned model FBI training

ORSIM bronchoscope simulator was provided for testing the trainees’ post-training performance. Performance on the ORSIM simulator was evaluated using three primary metrics: the ORSIM score (a rating scale from 0 to 100), intubation time, and the collision avoidance percentage. The ORSIM score provides a comprehensive measure of economy of movement, stability of image, maintenance of ideal flight path, avoidance of collisions, purposeful movement, and speed. [18] Intubation time was recorded as the duration from the insertion of the bronchoscope to the successful visualization of the carina. The avoidance percentage was calculated to assess the trainee’s ability to navigate the airway while minimizing contact with the bronchial walls. Post-training evaluation

A previously validated global rating scale of operative performance was applied in the present study before and after the assigned model FBI training to assess skills acquisition which contains eight subsets: respect for tissue, time and motion, instrument handling, knowledge of instruments, flow of the procedure, use of assistants, knowledge of the procedure, and overall performance [17].

The satisfaction on suitability, realism, and educational effectiveness for each training protocol were assessed using a trainee-reported questionnaires with a rating scale from 0 to 10 [19, 20].

Outcome measures

The primary outcome was the global rating scale of operative performance in FBI. The secondary outcomes (objective) were the FBI time with the assigned model and performance on the ORSIM simulator (intubation time, collision avoidance, and ORSIM performance score). The secondary outcomes (perception-based) were the trainee-reported satisfaction outcomes on suitability, realism, and educational effectiveness.

Statistical analysis

This was a single-centre study designed to evaluate the initial effectiveness of the models; no formal a priori sample-size calculation was performed, and the sample (n = 14 per arm) reflects the maximum number of anaesthesia residents available for full participation during the study period.

The normality of distribution was tested using the Shapiro–Wilk test and visually assessed using histograms. Continuous variables are presented as mean (standard deviation) or median (interquartile range) as appropriate. The primary outcome (global rating scale) and performance on the ORSIM simulator were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test or Wilcoxon signed-rank test as appropriate; trainee-reported outcomes were compared using Student’s t test.

Post-hoc exploratory analyses were performed to compare the performance between novice (≤ 5 FBI experiences within 1 year) and non-novice (> 5 FBI experiences within 1 year) residents [18]. In addition, multivariable linear regression analysis was performed to identify the predictive value of the training group and the FBI experience within 1 year on the outcome variables in ORSIM simulation and trainee-reported outcomes including suitability, realism, and educational effectiveness. The variance inflation factor (VIF) was checked before regression analysis to avoid the multicollinearity, and the goodness of fit for the regression model was examined using the adjusted R-squared. All statistical analyses were performed on the SPSS Statistics software package V.20 (IBM, Chicago, USA) with a two-tailed P value of < 0.05 indicating a significant difference.

Performance in ORSIM simulation

The difference in the performance on the ORSIM simulator of anaesthesia residents between the two study groups was not statistically significant in the five scenarios (Supplementary Table 1). In the post-hoc analysis, the performance on the ORSIM simulator in novice and non-novice residents were summarised in Supplementary Table 2. In the test model with complex airway, the difference in performance between novice and non-novice residents was statistically significant in ORSIM scenario 4 (retropharyngeal mass) and were not statistically significant in ORSIM scenario 3 (tongue tumor) and ORSIM scenario 5 (vestibular mass).

Trajectory of FBI time during assigned models training

The FBI time decreased significantly after the assigned model FBI training in both groups (median intubation time decreased by 14 (8–39) seconds [p = 0.001] in the 3D-printed model group and decreased by 8 (3–19) seconds [p = 0.003] in the AirSim manikin group, Fig. 3). In the Post-hoc analysis, among non-novice residents the FBI time improved significantly only in the 3D-printed model group (median intubation time decreased by 14 (8–24) seconds [p = 0.003] in the 3D-printed model group and decreased by 4 (8–9) seconds [p = 0.683] in the AirSim manikin group).

Post-training trainee-reported outcomes

Regarding the trainee-reported outcome variables: suitability were 8.4 ± 0.9 in the 3D-printed model group and 7.7 ± 0.8 (p = 0.064) in the AirSim manikin group; realism were 7.1 ± 0.0 in the 3D-printed model group and 6.6 ± 1.5 (p = 0.262) in the AirSim manikin group; effectiveness were 8.6 ± 1.0 in the 3D-printed model group and 7.9 ± 1.0 (p = 0.050) in the AirSim manikin group (Fig. 4).

Multivariable linear regression

The VIF was tested to be 1 (less than 5 is generally considered a low level of multicollinearity). After controlling for the FBI experience within 1 year, the trainee-reported educational effectiveness was significantly higher in the 3D-printed model group than the one in the AirSim manikin model group by a difference of 0.79 (95% confidence interval [0.01–1.57]; p = 0.050). Nevertheless, none of the other outcome variables, e.g. the intubation time and the ORSIM score in all five ORSIM scenarios, and so on, had a significant difference between the two groups. Additionally, almost all the outcome variables regarding operative skills were significantly associated with the FBI experience within 1 year (Table 2).

Discussion

The primary finding of this study was that training with 3D-printed difficult airway modelswas associated with similar improvements in visual motor coordination and micro-adjustment skills compared to low-fidelity manikins among anaesthesia residents. In addition, the utilization of 3D-printed models for FBI training hashigher trainee-perceived educational effectiveness, of borderline statistical significance (p= 0.050).

3D-printed models may provide comparable educational effectiveness when compared with other commercially available options [21]. In our study, each participant had better self-rated after-training operative performance either with 3D-printed difficult airway models or low-fidelity manikins models; the improvement of the self-rated operative performance and the post-training performance on the ORSIM simulator were also all comparable between the two groups. Considering its relatively low cost, 3D-printed models may be a compelling alternative to commercially available options for FBI training [21]. Additionally, the use of patient-specific models for practising procedures on a 3D-printed model may offer a highly realistic training environment and help learners to develop their skills and build confidence prior to carrying out procedures in real-world settings for difficult airways [22, 23].

3D-printed models may be a valuable adjunct to commercially available options for difficult airway FBI training especially for non-novice trainees due to its ability to reproduce the complex situation when managing real difficult airways. Several studies emphasised comparison the learning effectiveness between trainees and expert in intubation [3, 4, 16]. From the post-hoc exploratory analysis in the present study, FBI time improved significantly only in the 3D-printed model group but not in the AirSim manikin group among non-novice residents. A flattened performance curve in FBI time among non-novice residents in the AirSim manikin group indicates that the low-fidelity teaching aid may have a lack of ability to reproduce the complex situation in managing real difficult airways and may provide limited chance for non-novice trainees to refine its visual motor coordination and micro-adjustment skills when managing real difficult airways.

The post-hoc multivariable linear regression analysis results from the present study confirmed the importance of hands-on experience for FBI operative skills training. Almost all the outcome variables regarding operative skills were significantly associated with the prior experience of FBI within 1 year. In addition, after the assigned models training in the present study, novice residents had comparable performance to those of non-novice residents in some complex airway scenarios under the ORSIM simulator, such as ORSIM scenario 3 (tongue tumor) and ORSIM scenario 5 (vestibular mass). This finding from the post-hoc exploratory analysis was consistent with the results of a previous randomised controlled trial by Nilsson and colleagues which demonstrated that with proper training, first-year novice residents were capable of improving their FBI performance to a level comparable with anaesthesia consultants [4]. Considering the safety concerns from FBI practice in real patients especially for novice residents, 3D-printed models may be an effective alternative to acquire hands-on difficult airways FBI experience.

Despite the possible benefits of 3D-printed models for training FBI, they are not without limitations [5, 24]. One limitation is that the models are created from CT or MRI images in the supine position and while the patient is conscious, which may not fully replicate the challenging condition caused by the loss of pharyngeal muscle tone induced by anaesthesia or sedation. Additionally, the models are static and not dynamic, which may limit their ability to replicate the challenging process during awake FBI. Furthermore, the material of our 3D-printed models is not elastic, and this fact may limit their use only in FBI training but not suitable for other intubation tools.

The present study has some limitations. First, despite the maximum number of anaesthesia residents available for full participation during the study period was achieved, small sample size (n = 14 per arm) may have reduced the power to detect modest between-group differences. Second, the study was conducted at a single tertiary academic centre; residents at other centres may differ in baseline FBI exposure, available teaching protocols, and training culture, all of which could modify the response to either modality. Third, the airway pathologies practised in the two arms were not matched. Although this partly reflects the intrinsic constraints of each training modality, it limits the ability to isolate the effect of model fidelity from scenario type. The downstream ORSIM evaluation, which used identical scenarios for both groups, provides a more direct between-group comparison and showed no statistically significant difference. Fourth, the FBI experience prior to enrollment may have different effect on skills acquisition after training protocol. We performed post-hoc analysis to adjust the possible confounding effect from prior FBI experience and the main results were consistent. Fifth, the trainee-reported outcomes, including suitability, realism, and educational effectiveness, should be interpreted cautiously. Although participants were anaesthesia residents with prior exposure to airway management, some were novices in FBI, and their evaluation of these may differ from that of experienced airway practitioners.

Conclusions

Both 3D-printed difficult airway models and conventional manikins were associated with improvement in FBI performance, with no statistically significant differences in objective skill acquisition. Trainees perceived the 3D-printed model as more educationally effective. These preliminary findings suggest that patient-derived 3D-printed models may serve as a promising adjunct to FBI training, especially in settings where clinical exposure to complex airways is limited.
