Parallelisation of partial differential equations via representation theory Thanks: Submitted to the editors 11 November 2024.
Sheehan Olver

Abstract
A little utilised but fundamental fact is that if one discretises a partial differential equation using a symmetry-adapted basis corresponding to so-called irreducible representations , the basic building blocks in representational theory, then the resulting linear system can be completely decoupled into smaller independent linear systems. That is, representation theory can be used to trivially parallelise the numerical solution of partial differential equations. This beautiful theory is introduced via a crash course in representation theory aimed at its practical utilisation, its connection with decomposing expansions in polynomials into different symmetry classes, and give examples of solving Schrödinger’s equation on simple symmetric geometries like squares and cubes where there is as much as four-fold increase in the number of independent linear systems, each of a significantly smaller dimension than results from standard bases.

1
intro

Introduction.

A function in a symmetric geometry like a square or cube can be decomposed into different
symmetry classes
by understanding the ways in which symmetry groups may present themselves as linear operators, which is precisely the topic of representation theory. A simple example of this fact is that univariate functions can be decomposed into even and odd parts, which falls out naturally from a Taylor series expansion provided the expansion converges:

{\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}f_{k}x^{k}}\qquad=\qquad\underbrace{\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}f_{2k}x^{2k}}_{\hbox{Even}}\qquad+\qquad\underbrace{\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}f_{2k+1}x^{2k+1}}_{\hbox{Odd}}

An even-odd decomposition can be viewed as a specific example of a
symmetric decomposition
corresponding to the symmetry action of reflection
x\mapsto-x
. More precisely, we view reflection as the cyclic group of order 2, which for concreteness we write as a multiplicative
group
{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}:=\left\{{-1,1}\right\}
. The basis of monomials
x^{k}
are
symmetry-adapted
with respect to
{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}
: they translate applying the symmetry action of reflection to multiplication by a
representation
, the basic object of study in the field of representation theory which correspond to maps from the group to linear operators. Even and odd monomials translate reflection into multiplication by the trivial representation
\rho_{\rm t}(g)=1
and sign representation
\rho_{\rm s}(g)=g
, respectively: for all
g\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}
we have

(gx)^{2k}=\rho_{\rm t}(g)x^{2k},\qquad(gx)^{2k+1}=\rho_{\rm s}(g)x^{2k+1}.

Splitting a function into even-odd parts can be reinterpreted as splitting the expansion according to bases associated with these two
irreducible representations
of
{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}
.

Even-odd decompositions generalise naturally to squares and cubes.
On a square, this can be viewed is a consequence of the basis
x^{k}y^{j}
respecting the symmetry action of reflection in each coordinate, i.e., reflection corresponds to representations of the group

{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}^{2}={\mathbb{Z}}_{2}\times{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}:=\left\{{\begin{pmatrix}1\\
&1\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}1\\
&-1\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}-1\\
&1\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}-1\\
&-1\end{pmatrix}}\right\},

which has four irreducible representations:

\displaystyle\rho_{\rm t}(g)\;

\displaystyle=1,\quad\rho_{\rm h}\begin{pmatrix}g_{1}\\
&g_{2}\end{pmatrix}=g_{1},\quad\rho_{\rm v}\begin{pmatrix}g_{1}\\
&g_{2}\end{pmatrix}=g_{2},\quad\rho_{\rm s}(g)=\det g.

We can therefore decompose a function into four different types of symmetry classes: trivial (even-even), horizontal (odd-even), vertical (even-odd), and sign (odd-odd) representations, as depicted in Figure
1
.

Figure 1:

A function on a square can be decomposed into four different symmetry classes according to their even-odd symmetry, which can be viewed in terms of the irreducible representations of
{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}^{2}
. On the left we plot an arbitrary function
f(x,y)=\cos(6y(y-1)(x-1/5)^{2}+\sin(4y-1/10){\rm e}^{x})
and on the right we plot the four components of its symmetric decomposition corresponding to
{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}^{2}
. Applying a reflection across the
x
- or
y
-axis leaves each term either invariant or swaps the sign. Certain PDEs can be parallelised across four independent solves according to this decomposition.

Symmetric decompositions generalise to other symmetry groups such as the dihedral group
D_{4}
associated with a square or the octohedral group
O_{h}
associated with a cube. For example, if we utilise the full
D_{4}
symmetry group of a square instead of only reflection along the
x
- and
y
-axes we can decompose a function into six components, see Figure
2
. Such a decomposition falls out of the construction of symmetry-adapted bases where applying the symmetry action is equivalent to multiplication by a representation.
The irreducible representations enumerate the basic ways symmetry may present itself and hence we wish to find bases which correspond to irreducible representations.
A major difference is that in general the irreducible representations are matrix-valued, which makes the construction more involved than the scalar representations associated with
{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}
and
{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}^{2}
. Therefore, we need to consider the symmetry group acting on a basis with more than one element in tandem. Moreover, while in the case of the square we can write a basis corresponding to irreducible representations explicitly, in the case of the cube we can only compute such a basis numerically. But the approach to decomposition does generalise and we outline a systematic procedure to do so.

Figure 2:

A symmetric decomposition associated with the full symmetry group of the square (the dihedral group
D_{4}
) into six symmetry classes for the same function from Figure
1
. The first two columns correspond to scalar irreducible representations where the functions are either invariant or flip sign whenever we apply a symmetry action. The last column corresponds to a 2-dimensional irreducible representation, thus whilst they remain in an invariant subspace the symmetry is not as visually obvious, but the numerical solution of certain classes of PDEs still decouples across these terms.

Our motivation for utilising symmetry-adapted bases is that they naturally decouple the discretisation of certain important classes of partial differential equations (PDEs) into smaller independent systems, the number equal to the total of the dimension of all irreducible representations, which can in principle be solved in parallel.
For simplicity we focus on the Schrödinger equation

\Delta u+a({\mbox{\boldmath$x$\unboldmath}})u=\lambda u

where
a
is invariant under the corresponding symmetry action with natural (Neumann) boundary conditions, that is, where the normal derivative of
u
vanishes at the boundary. For example, on a square where the potential is invariant under
D_{4}
we have four scalar irreducible representations and one 2-dimensional representation and a discretisation built using an associated symmetry-adapted basis will decouple the Schrödinger equation into 6 independent eigenvalue problems which can be solved in parallel. On the cube where the potential is invariant under
O_{h}
this increases substantially to 20 independent eigenvalue problems. Note this feature is not limited to scalar-valued PDEs, and extends naturally to vector-valued equations as discussed briefly in the conclusion.

In the discussion we focus on monomial bases for pedagogical reasons but essentially everything we discuss is applicable to multivariate orthogonal polynomials, which have a natural link to symmetry groups in that the degree-
n
orthogonal polynomials are closed under symmetry actions
[
13
]
and hence generate representations. It should be emphasised for the reader inexperienced in numerical methods:
do not use monomials
in numerical methods! They are prone to ill-conditioning and orthogonal polynomials have many beautiful properties such as sparse discretisations (as discussed in detail in our recent review and references therein
[
27
]
). In fact many of the figures in this paper are generated using multivariate orthogonal polynomials for numerical stability.

The paper is structured as follows:

Section
: We discuss prior work in incorporating discrete symmetries into the discretisation of partial differential equations via symmetry-adapted bases and the practical computation of decompositions of representations.

Section
: We give a quick overview of the essentials of representation theory with an emphasis on practical applied mathematics: we state the standard results in terms of concrete matrices and vectors as opposed to abstract vector spaces. This includes
Schur’s lemma
(Lemma
8
) which is a fundamental result in representation theory that states, essentially, that any linear map that
intertwines
irreducible representations is by necessity either zero or a a scalar multiple of the identity. It also includes a discussion on how any representation can be decomposed into irreducible representations via an orthogonal matrix that can be computed numerically.

Section
: Symmetry-adapted bases are constructed on a square where this is accomplished in closed form and on a cube where we utilise the numerical algorithm for decomposing representations.

Section
: Symmetry-adapted bases can decouple PDEs into distinct systems, whose number is the sum of the dimensions of the irreducible representations. The workhorse for proving this result is Schur’s lemma: by showing that portions of discretisations of PDEs are either zero or a scalar multiple of the identity implies that they are sparse. We demonstrate examples on the square using the dihedral group
D_{4}
(which decouples across 6 systems) and a cube using the octohedral group
O_{h}
(which decouples across 20 systems). We conclude with an example of 3 one-dimensional particles (so also living in a cube) using only permutation and negation symmetry (which decouples across 8 systems, a four-fold increase over existing bases).

Section
: We conclude by discussing extensions to vector-valued PDEs and other groups, including the high-dimensional setting of the Schrödinger equation with multiple particles.

Background.

The idea of using symmetry and representation theory to decouple PDEs goes back a long ways and in a sense underlies much of quantum mechanics, as discussed in
[
31
,
11

[
32
]

[
14
]
(see also
[
30
]
), which investigated the numerical solution of PDEs on regular polygons. Further investigation of tackling subproblems associated with such a construction was investigated by Bossavit
[
8
]
. Allgower, Georg and collaborators pursued using symmetry-adapted bases for boundary element methods
[
1
,
2
,
3
,
4
]
including on the surface of a cube
[
5
]
. The
D_{4}
symmetry group has also been used in the construction of spectral methods
[
20
]
. Munthe–Kaas constructed Fourier-like symmetry-adapted bases associated with different discrete groups including the
D_{3}
symmetry group associated with an equilateral triangle
[
22
,
9
]
. Outside of PDEs, symmetry-adapted bases have been used effectively in computational algebraic geometry
[
16
]
, computing cubature rules
[
10
]
, and optimisation
[
21
]
.

To extend these tools beyond simple symmetries like
D_{4}
requires the algorithmic decomposition of representations. Dixon
[
12
]
introduced an iterative approach to decompose a representation but the algorithm does not split apart multiple copies of the same irreducible representation. Serre
[
29
]
gives an explicit construction of projectors including the case of multiple copies of the same irreducible representation.
This involved summing over every element of the group but the cost can be massively reduced
[
18
]
, a technique which has been implemented in a GAP package
[
19
]
. An important special case that arises below are monomial representations, which have exactly one nonzero entry in each row and column, and an algorithm specific to monomial matrices was constructed in
[
28
]
. The method for incorporating symmetries of the cube in
[
5
]
utilised software developed in
[
33
]
, which is unfortunately no longer available. More recently, I introduced an algorithm based purely on numerical linear algebra (and implementable with standard floating point arithmetic) for block-diagonalising representations of the symmetric group
[

to compute symmetry-adapted bases for the cube.

3
crash

A crash course in representation theory.

A representation is a map from a group to invertible linear operators in a way that the group structure is preserved:

Definition 1
.

For a group
G
and a vector space
V
, a representation is a map
\rho:G\rightarrow\hbox{\rm GL}(V)
that is a homomorphism, that is, for any
a,b\in G
we have

\rho(ab)=\rho(a)\rho(b).

In the special case where
V={\mathbb{R}}^{n}
(i.e.,
\rho
is in matrix form) and
\rho:G\rightarrow O(n)
for the orthogonal group
O(n):=\{Q\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times n}:Q^{\top}Q=I\}
we refer to
\rho
as an orthogonal representation.

As a very basic example consider the symmetric group
S_{3}
corresponding to permutations of 3 elements. We identify this with permutation matrices so that the group is generated from

\tau_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\\
&&1\end{pmatrix}\qquad\hbox{and}\qquad\tau_{2}=\begin{pmatrix}1\\
&0&1\\
&1&0\end{pmatrix},

that is we take
S_{3}:=\left\langle{\tau_{1},\tau_{2}}\right\rangle\subset O(3)
. These are the
Coxeter generators
of simple transpositions which can be written in cyclic notation as
\tau_{1}\cong(12)(3)
and
\tau_{2}\cong(1)(23)
. This can be interpreted as an example of an orthogonal representation since
\tau_{1},\tau_{2}\in O(3)
.
But this is not the only representation corresponding to
S_{3}
. Another is the trivial representation
\rho_{\rm t}:S_{3}\rightarrow O(1)
defined by
\rho_{\rm t}(g)=1
. And there is the sign representation
\rho_{\rm s}:S_{3}\rightarrow O(1)
defined by the sign of the permutation
\rho_{\rm s}(g)=\det g
, or in terms of the Coxeter generators it is generated from

\rho_{\rm s}(\tau_{1})=\rho_{\rm s}(\tau_{2})=-1.

All groups have a trivial representation and all matrix groups have a sign representation. But there are many other representations, and representation theory can broadly be viewed as the area looking to classify all possible representations of a group.

3.1
R

educible and irreducible representations.

A representation often contains
subrepresentations
: a subspace
W\subset V
may be invariant under
\rho
in which case
\rho:G\rightarrow\hbox{\rm GL}(W)
is also a representation.

Definition 2
.

A representation is reducible if it contains a nontrivial subrepresentation of smaller dimension. A representation acting on a nontrivial vector space is irreducible if it is not reducible.

For example, permutation matrices leave the space of vectors with all entries equal (
\{{{\left({c,\ldots,c}\right)}^{\top}}:c\in{\mathbb{R}}\}
) invariant. Thus permutation matrices are in fact a
reducible representation
, containing a copy of the trivial representation as a subrepresentation.

Different irreducible representations can be
isomorphic
, eg., by conjugation by a fixed orthogonal matrix. However there are only a finite number of irreducible representations up to isomorphism, in fact, it is equal to the number of conjugacy classes (i.e., the number of equivalence classes under the relationship
a\sim b
if there exists
g\in G
such that
a=gbg^{-1}
):

Theorem 3
.

[
29
, Theorem 7]

The number of irreducible representations of a group
G
(up to isomorphism) is equal to the number of conjugacy classes of
G
.

In other words given any finite group we can enumerate the irreducible representations, choosing a specific form for each isomorphism class. We refer to irreducible representations in such an enumeration as
irreducible representations in canonical form
.

As a very simple example, we consider the cyclic group of order 2,
{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}
. Being a commutative group the conjugacy classes are equal to the order, hence we know there are two irreducible representations. These are in fact the trivial (
\rho_{\rm t}
) and sign representations (
\rho_{\rm s}
). Similarly
{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}^{2}
is of order 4 hence has four irreducible representations and these are exactly the products of the representations of
{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}
, that is:
\rho_{\rm t}
,
\rho_{x}
,
\rho_{y}
, and
\rho_{\rm s}
. A more complicated example is
S_{3}
which has three conjugacy classes associated with the partitions
3=3
,
3=2+1
and
3=1+1+1
according to the size of their cycles:

\underbrace{\{(123),(132)\}}_{3=3},\underbrace{\{(12)(3),(23)(1),(13)(2)\}}_{3=2+1},\underbrace{\{(1)(2)(3)\}}_{3=1+1+1}

Thus we need to find 3 irreducible representations which we can associate with the same partitions. We know two scalar representations already:

\displaystyle\rho_{3}(g)\;

\displaystyle=\rho_{\rm t}(g),\qquad\rho_{1+1+1}(g)=\rho_{\rm s}(g).

The missing irreducible representation is 2-dimensional and can be defined according to its action on the generators:

\rho_{2+1}(\tau_{1})=\begin{pmatrix}-1\\
&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad\rho_{2+1}(\tau_{2})={1\over 2}\begin{pmatrix}1&\sqrt{3}\\
\sqrt{3}&-1\end{pmatrix}.

We invite the reader to confirm that this is in fact a representation of
S_{3}
by checking it satisfies the rules of its presentation, that is:

\rho_{2+1}(\tau_{1})^{2}=\rho_{2+1}(\tau_{2})^{2}=(\rho_{2+1}(\tau_{1})\rho_{2+1}(\tau_{2}))^{3}=I.

Remark 4
.

Note whilst the irreducible representations and the conjugacy classes are both one-to-one with partitions it is a mistake to view them as some how connected to each other. The proof of Theorem
3
is based on showing they are both bases of class functions (functions that depend only on conjugacy class) and hence have the same number. This is like saying that monomials and Lagrange polynomials are bases for the same set of polynomials: this does not imply that we can associate a given Lagrange polynomial with a given monomial.

We now go through the enumeration of the irreducible representations for the square (
D_{4}
) and cube (
O_{h}
).

3.2
I

D_{4}
.

The symmetry group of an
n
-sided regular polygon is the
dihedral group

D_{n}
, and the general classification of irreducible representations are known
[
29

. Whilst the general construction has a simple explicit form, for concreteness we specialise the results to the square
D_{4}

which is generated by a rotation
r

s

D_{4}=\left\langle{r,s:r^{4}=s^{2}=(sr)^{2}=1}\right\rangle=\{1,\underbrace{r,r^{2},r^{3}}_{\hbox{rotations}},\underbrace{s,sr,sr^{2},sr^{3}}_{\hbox{reflections}}\}.

There are five conjugacy classes:

\left\{{1}\right\},\left\{{r,r^{3}}\right\},\left\{{r^{2}}\right\},\left\{{s,sr^{2}}\right\},\left\{{sr,sr^{3}}\right\}.

Thus we are aiming to construct a total of 5 irreducible representations.

Taking the unit square
\square:=[-1,1]^{2}
we can view
D_{4}\subset O(2)
as a symmetry group generated by a single reflection and a
90^{\circ}
counter-clockwise rotation:

s=\begin{pmatrix}1\\
&-1\end{pmatrix}\quad\hbox{and}\quad r=\begin{pmatrix}&-1\\
1&\end{pmatrix}.

This definition itself forms a two-dimensional irreducible representation,
\rho_{\rm f}(g)=g
, which is known as the faithful representation. There are four other (scalar) irreducible representations generated by

\displaystyle\rho_{\rm t}(s)\;

\displaystyle=1,

\displaystyle\rho_{\rm t}(r)

\displaystyle=1,

\displaystyle\rho_{\rm ref}(s)\;

\displaystyle=-1,

\displaystyle\rho_{\rm ref}(r)

\displaystyle=1,

\displaystyle\rho_{\rm rot}(s)\;

\displaystyle=1,

\displaystyle\rho_{\rm rot}(r)

\displaystyle=-1,

\displaystyle\rho_{\rm s}(s)\;

\displaystyle=-1,

\displaystyle\rho_{\rm s}(r)

\displaystyle=-1

which we refer to as trivial, reflect, rotate, and sign representations. It is easy to verify that these all satisfy the rules of the presentation and hence we have enumerated all five irreducible representations.

The symmetry group of the cube is the octohedral group

\tau_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}&&1\\

where we view these as equivalent to the Coxeter generators for

3.4
D

ecomposing into irreducible representations.

An intuitive result is that a representation can be decomposed into subrepresentations until all we are left with are irreducible representations, which can be expressed rigorously as follows:

Proposition 5
.

[
15
, Proposition 1.8]

For any representation
\rho:G\rightarrow\hbox{\rm GL}(V)
of a finite group
G
there is a decomposition

V\cong V_{1}^{\oplus a_{1}}\oplus\cdots\oplus V_{r}^{\oplus a_{r}}

where
\rho_{k}:G\rightarrow\hbox{\rm GL}(V_{k})
are distinct irreducible representations. The decompositions of
V
into a direct sum of the
r
summands is unique, as are the
V_{k}
that occur (up to isomorphism) and their multiplicities
a_{k}
.

This formulation is a slightly abstract and so for our purposes it is more convenient to work with concrete matrices. In this setting decomposing a representation is best thought of as a block-diagonalisation process akin to diagonalising a matrix. We express this as follows:

Theorem 6
.

[
26
, Theorem 1]
Given an orthogonal representation
\rho:G\rightarrow O(n)
of a finite group
G
, there exists an orthogonal matrix
Q
such that

Q^{\top}\rho(g)Q=\begin{pmatrix}\rho_{1}(g)^{\oplus a_{1}}\\
&\ddots\\
&&\rho_{r}(g)^{\oplus a_{r}}\end{pmatrix}

where
\rho_{1},\ldots,\rho_{r}
are all irreducible representations in canonical form. Here we use the notation

A^{\oplus k}:=\diag(\underbrace{A,\ldots,A}_{\hbox{$k$ \rm times}}).

As an example we consider block-diagonalising the permutation matrices associated with
S_{3}
. The matrix

Q=\left({\begin{array}[]{cc|c}\sqrt{2}&1/\sqrt{6}&-1/\sqrt{3}\\
-\sqrt{2}&1/\sqrt{6}&-1/\sqrt{3}\\
&-\sqrt{2/3}&-1/\sqrt{3}\end{array}}\right)

satisfies

\displaystyle Q^{\top}\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\\
&&1\end{pmatrix}Q\;

\displaystyle=\left({\begin{array}[]{cc|c}-1&&\\
&1&\\
\hline\cr&&1\end{array}}\right),

\displaystyle Q^{\top}\begin{pmatrix}1\\
&0&1\\
&1&0\end{pmatrix}Q\;

\displaystyle=\left({\begin{array}[]{cc|c}1/2&\sqrt{3}/2\\
\sqrt{3}/2&-1/2\\
\hline\cr&&1\end{array}}\right).

In other words, we have block-diagonalised the representation associated with permutation matrices to two irreducible representations of smaller dimension:

Q^{\top}gQ=\diag(\rho_{2+1}(g),\rho_{3}(g)).

A number of algorithms based using group theoretical considerations (character tables, cosets, etc.)
[

,

. However, an algorithm built using only numerical linear algebra techniques was introduced in

that can compute the matrix

directly from generators of the representation. This was shown only for the symmetric group though the result
[

translates to other groups which we outline now. Essentially the idea mimics an approach for computing eigenvectors of a matrix

via a nullspace computation. That is, if a (known) eigenvalue

Similarly, for a representation
\rho:G\rightarrow O(n)

has (unkown) multiplicity

a basis

corresponding to the associated columns of
Q
that correspond to
\rho_{k}
can be computed by finding a basis for solutions to

\displaystyle\rho(g_{1})U_{k}^{(j)}-U_{k}^{(j)}\rho_{k}(g_{1})\;

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\rho(g_{m})U_{k}^{(j)}-U_{k}^{(j)}\rho_{k}(g_{m})\;

\displaystyle=0,

where
g_{1},\ldots,g_{m}
are the generators of

Vectorising the columns of

reduces this to a standard nullspace problem, and proving that the columns of the resulting

are orthogonal follows from Schur’s lemma as

3.5
S

chur’s lemma.

Whilst representation theory is a rich and beautiful theory for our primary purpose of showing that a PDE splits across different symmetry classes we only need one foundational result:
Schur’s lemma
. This is a powerful tool that tells us something about so-called
intertwining maps
:

Definition 7
.

Let
\rho_{U}:G\rightarrow\hbox{\rm GL}(U)
and
\rho_{V}:G\rightarrow\hbox{\rm GL}(V)
be two representations. An
intertwining map
(or
G
-module homomorphism) is a linear operator
\varphi:U\rightarrow V
such that for all
\mbox{\boldmath$c$\unboldmath}\in U
we have

\rho_{V}(g)\varphi(\mbox{\boldmath$c$\unboldmath})=\varphi(\rho_{U}(g)\mbox{\boldmath$c$\unboldmath}).

If a matrix
A\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m\times n}
encodes an intertwining map, that is for all
g\in G

A\rho_{U}(g)=\rho_{V}(g)A

where
U={\mathbb{R}}^{n}
and
V={\mathbb{R}}^{m}
, we refer to it as an
intertwining matrix with respect to
\rho_{U}
and
\rho_{V}
.

Schur’s lemma states that intertwining maps are highly structured:

Lemma 8
.

[
15
, Schur’s Lemma 1.7]
If
\rho_{U}:G\rightarrow\hbox{\rm GL}(U)
and
\rho_{V}:G\rightarrow\hbox{\rm GL}(V)
are irreducible representations of
G
and
\varphi:U\rightarrow V
is an intertwining map, then

1.

Either
\varphi
is an isomorphism, or
\varphi=0
.

2.

If
\rho_{U}=\rho_{V}
, then
\varphi=cI
for some
c\in{\mathbb{C}}
.

Hidden inside this statement is an elegant tool for proving that specific matrices are either zero or a constant times the identity, which will prove invaluable for block-diagonalising discretisations of PDEs. We can rewrite this lemma in a way to emphasise what it is saying on the level of matrices, that any intertwining matrix with respect to canonical irreducible representations is either a multiple of the identity matrix or zero:

Corollary 9
.

Suppose
\rho_{U}:G\rightarrow O(n)
and
\rho_{V}:G\rightarrow O(m)
are irreducible representations in canonical form. Suppose that
A\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m\times n}
is an intertwining matrix with respect to
\rho_{U}
and
\rho_{V}
. For some
c\in{\mathbb{R}}
we have

A=\begin{cases}0&\rho_{U}\neq\rho_{V},\\
cI&\rho_{U}=\rho_{V}.\end{cases}

Computing symmetric decompositions.

We now turn to the task of computing symmetric decompositions such as those associated with

used to produce Figure

\left\{{p_{1},\ldots,p_{n}}\right\}
where a symmetry action is equivalent to multiplying the basis by a representation.
We write a basis as a column-vector:

{\mathbf{p}}({\mbox{\boldmath$x$\unboldmath}}):={{\left({p_{1}({\mbox{\boldmath$x$\unboldmath}}),\ldots,p_{N}({\mbox{\boldmath$x$\unboldmath}})}\right)}^{\top}}.

Then we can express the notion of a symmetry-respecting and symmetry-adapted basis as follows:

Definition 10
.

A
symmetry-respecting basis
with respect to an orthogonal representation
\rho:G\rightarrow O(N)
is a vector of functions
{\mathbf{p}}:\Omega\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
satisfying

{\mathbf{p}}(g{\mbox{\boldmath$x$\unboldmath}})=\rho(g){\mathbf{p}}({\mbox{\boldmath$x$\unboldmath}}).

If
\rho
is a canonical irreducible representation we refer to it as a
irreducible symmetry-adapted basis
and if
\rho
is block-diagonal where each block is irreducible (a la Theorem
6
) then we refer to it as a
symmetry-adapted basis
.

Our main example of a symmetry-respecting basis are monomials of precisely degree
n
which span homogeneous polynomials. For example, in 2D we have the basis

{\mathbf{p}}_{n}(x,y)={{\left({x^{n},x^{n-1}y,\ldots,xy^{n-1},y^{n}}\right)}^{\top}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}.

We know (via the binomial theorem) that homogeneous polynomials are closed under any orthogonal change of variables, that is, for any
g\in G\subset O(2)
there must exist
\rho(g)\in O(n+1)
such that

{\mathbf{p}}(g{\mbox{\boldmath$x$\unboldmath}})=\rho(g){\mathbf{p}}({\mbox{\boldmath$x$\unboldmath}})
. It follows that
\rho
must itself be a representation as we have

\rho(ab){\mathbf{p}}({\mbox{\boldmath$x$\unboldmath}})={\mathbf{p}}(ab{\mbox{\boldmath$x$\unboldmath}})=\rho(a){\mathbf{p}}(b{\mbox{\boldmath$x$\unboldmath}})=\rho(a)\rho(b){\mathbf{p}}({\mbox{\boldmath$x$\unboldmath}})

hence
\rho(ab)=\rho(a)\rho(b)
.

Remark 11
.

As mentioned in the introduction everything we discuss translates to multivariate orthogonal polynomials, where the span of the degree
n
polynomials is invariant under orthogonal coordinate changes
[
13
]
and hence generate representations. And for our squares and cubes a tensor product basis built from univariate functions that capture even-odd symmetry (are symmetry-adapted with respect to
{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}
), e.g., tensor products of Legendre polynomials, generate the exact same representations as monomial bases.

In the special case of
{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}^{2}
we know that such a representation arising from applying reflection along the
x
- and
y
-axis to the monomial basis is already decomposed, eg. the generators satisfy

\displaystyle{\mathbf{p}}_{n}(\diag(-1,1){\mbox{\boldmath$x$\unboldmath}})\;

\displaystyle=\diag((-1)^{n},(-1)^{n-1},\ldots,1){\mathbf{p}}_{n}({\mbox{\boldmath$x$\unboldmath}})

\displaystyle{\mathbf{p}}_{n}(\diag(1,-1){\mbox{\boldmath$x$\unboldmath}})\;

\displaystyle=\diag(1,-1,\ldots,(-1)^{n}){\mathbf{p}}_{n}({\mbox{\boldmath$x$\unboldmath}})

which are both diagonal. Thus monomials are already symmetry-adapted with respect to
{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}^{2}
, but for other groups like the dihedral group the representation
\rho
will not be automatically decomposed.

When
\rho
is not decomposed we can employ Theorem
6
to deduce a matrix

that block-diagonalises it. We can thereby deduce a symmetry-adapted basis:

Lemma 12
.

Suppose
{\mathbf{p}}
is a symmetry-respecting basis with respect to
\rho:G\rightarrow O(n)
and

block-diagonalises
\rho

\mbox{\boldmath$q$\unboldmath}({\mbox{\boldmath$x$\unboldmath}}):=Q^{\top}{\mathbf{p}}({\mbox{\boldmath$x$\unboldmath}})

is a symmetry-adapted basis.

Proof.

This falls out immediately from the definitions:

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$q$\unboldmath}(g{\mbox{\boldmath$x$\unboldmath}})\;

\displaystyle=Q^{\top}{\mathbf{p}}(g{\mbox{\boldmath$x$\unboldmath}})=Q^{\top}\rho(g){\mathbf{p}}({\mbox{\boldmath$x$\unboldmath}})=\underbrace{Q^{\top}\rho(g)Q}_{\hbox{block-diagonalised}}\mbox{\boldmath$q$\unboldmath}({\mbox{\boldmath$x$\unboldmath}}).

5
PDEs

Decoupling PDEs via symmetry-adapted bases.

We now return to the main theme of this paper: a symmetry-adapted basis has no communication between bases corresponding to different irreducible representations when used to discretise PDEs. Moreover, because of Schur’s lemma, there is no communication between different basis elements of each canonical irreducible representation. Thus we can split the numerical solution of a PDE in a square across 6 different independent linear systems (4 for each each scalar irreducible representation and another 2 for the 2-dimensional faithful representation) and in a cube across 20 different systems.

Consider the a Schrödinger equation

\Delta u+a({\mbox{\boldmath$x$\unboldmath}})u=\lambda u

with natural (Neumann) boundary conditions on a geometry
\Omega
which is invariant under a symmetry action.
We focus on a weak-formulation of the equation though everything we shall discuss extends to strong-formulation and more complicated PDEs provided some symmetry is present as outlined in the conclusions. More precisely:
given finite polynomial vector spaces
U
(the
trial space
) and
V
(the
test space
), find
u\in U
such that

\left\langle{\nabla v,\nabla u}\right\rangle+\left\langle{v,au}\right\rangle=\lambda\left\langle{v,u}\right\rangle

for all
v\in V
, where we assume the standard inner product

\left\langle{f,g}\right\rangle:=\int_{\Omega}f({\mbox{\boldmath$x$\unboldmath}})g({\mbox{\boldmath$x$\unboldmath}}){\rm d}{\mbox{\boldmath$x$\unboldmath}}
.

Given bases for
U
and
V
we arrive at a discretisation of the PDE. Monomials may be the immediate choice of basis though for reliable and stable computations it is preferable to use orthogonal polynomials, and in fact these can lead to sparse discretisations
[
27
]
, but the discussion that follows is independent of the choice of basis.
In particular, if
U={\rm span}(p_{1},…,p_{N})
and
V={\rm span}(q_{1},…,q_{N})
,
we write these as a vector of functions
{\mathbf{p}}({\mbox{\boldmath$x$\unboldmath}})={{\left({p_{1}({\mbox{\boldmath$x$\unboldmath}}),\ldots,p_{N}({\mbox{\boldmath$x$\unboldmath}})}\right)}^{\top}}

so that for
\mbox{\boldmath$c$\unboldmath},\mbox{\boldmath$d$\unboldmath}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
we have

u({\mbox{\boldmath$x$\unboldmath}})=\sum_{k=1}^{N}c_{k}p_{k}({\mbox{\boldmath$x$\unboldmath}})={\mathbf{p}}({\mbox{\boldmath$x$\unboldmath}})^{\top}\mbox{\boldmath$c$\unboldmath}\qquad\hbox{and}\qquad v({\mbox{\boldmath$x$\unboldmath}})=\sum_{k=1}^{N}d_{k}q_{k}({\mbox{\boldmath$x$\unboldmath}})=\mbox{\boldmath$q$\unboldmath}({\mbox{\boldmath$x$\unboldmath}})^{\top}\mbox{\boldmath$d$\unboldmath}.

Plugging this into the weak-formulation reduces the problem to finding
\mbox{\boldmath$c$\unboldmath}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
such that

\mbox{\boldmath$d$\unboldmath}^{\top}\left({\left\langle{\nabla\mbox{\boldmath$q$\unboldmath},\nabla{\mathbf{p}}\!{}^{\top}}\right\rangle+\left\langle{\mbox{\boldmath$q$\unboldmath},a{\mathbf{p}}\!{}^{\top}}\right\rangle}\right)\mbox{\boldmath$c$\unboldmath}=\lambda\mbox{\boldmath$d$\unboldmath}^{\top}\left\langle{\mbox{\boldmath$q$\unboldmath},{\mathbf{p}}\!{}^{\top}}\right\rangle\mbox{\boldmath$c$\unboldmath}

for all
\mbox{\boldmath$d$\unboldmath}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
, where here the inner products act entrywise, e.g.

\displaystyle\left\langle{\mbox{\boldmath$q$\unboldmath},a{\mathbf{p}}\!{}^{\top}}\right\rangle\;

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\left\langle{q_{1},ap_{1}}\right\rangle&\cdots&\left\langle{q_{1},ap_{N}}\right\rangle\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\left\langle{q_{N},ap_{1}}\right\rangle&\cdots&\left\langle{q_{N},ap_{N}}\right\rangle\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle\left\langle{\nabla\mbox{\boldmath$q$\unboldmath},\nabla{\mathbf{p}}\!{}^{\top}}\right\rangle\;

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\left\langle{\nabla q_{1},\nabla p_{1}}\right\rangle&\cdots&\left\langle{\nabla q_{1},\nabla p_{N}}\right\rangle\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\left\langle{\nabla q_{N},\nabla p_{1}}\right\rangle&\cdots&\left\langle{\nabla q_{N},\nabla p_{N}}\right\rangle\end{pmatrix}.

Since this holds true for all
d
, including
\mbox{\boldmath$e$\unboldmath}_{k}
for
k=1,\ldots,N
, we have arrived at a generalised eigenvalue problem:

(\underbrace{\left\langle{\nabla\mbox{\boldmath$q$\unboldmath},\nabla{\mathbf{p}}\!{}^{\top}}\right\rangle}_{\hbox{stiffness matrix}}+\underbrace{\left\langle{\mbox{\boldmath$q$\unboldmath}^{\top},a{\mathbf{p}}\!{}^{\top}}\right\rangle}_{\hbox{mass matrix}})\mbox{\boldmath$c$\unboldmath}=\lambda\left\langle{\mbox{\boldmath$q$\unboldmath},{\mathbf{p}}\!{}^{\top}}\right\rangle\mbox{\boldmath$c$\unboldmath}.

This discretisation will in general be dense (though it is sparse if we use a standard Finite Element Method basis or more generally a
p
-Finite Element Method basis built from orthogonal polynomials
[
6
]
).
However, if we build the bases using representation theory we can ensure sparsity and in fact decouple completely between different irreducible representations.
We now prove this for the stiffness and mass matrices.

Lemma 13
.

Suppose
{\mathbf{p}}
and
q
are symmetry-adapted bases with respect to representations
\rho_{U}
and
\rho_{V}
, respectively, and
a:\Omega\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
is invariant under the symmetry group
G\subset O(d)
. Then
\left\langle{\mbox{\boldmath$q$\unboldmath},a{\mathbf{p}}\!{}^{\top}}\right\rangle
is an intertwining matrix with respect to
\rho_{U}
and
\rho_{V}
.

Proof.

This result falls out of a couple observations. First note that for any orthogonal representation
\rho
we have
\rho(g)^{\top}=\rho(g)^{-1}=\rho(g^{-1})
.
And for any orthogonal matrix
g\in O(d)
that leaves
\Omega
invariant integration is left unchanged under an associated change-of-variables:

\int_{\Omega}f(g{\mbox{\boldmath$x$\unboldmath}}){\rm d}{\mbox{\boldmath$x$\unboldmath}}=\int_{\Omega}f({\mbox{\boldmath$x$\unboldmath}}){\rm d}{\mbox{\boldmath$x$\unboldmath}},

which can be seen since the Jacobian of the change-of-variables is given by
|\det Q|=1
.
We introduce the notation of applying a symmetry action
(g\star f)({\mbox{\boldmath$x$\unboldmath}}):=f(g{\mbox{\boldmath$x$\unboldmath}})
, so that a symmetry-adapted basis has the property, for
g\in G
,

g\star{\mathbf{p}}=\rho_{U}(g){\mathbf{p}}\quad\hbox{and}\quad g\star\mbox{\boldmath$q$\unboldmath}=\rho_{V}(g)\mbox{\boldmath$q$\unboldmath}

and note that the invariance of integration for orthogonal change-of-variables implies that

\left\langle{g\star v,u}\right\rangle=\int_{\Omega}v(g{\mbox{\boldmath$x$\unboldmath}})u({\mbox{\boldmath$x$\unboldmath}}){\rm d}{\mbox{\boldmath$x$\unboldmath}}=\int_{\Omega}v({\mbox{\boldmath$x$\unboldmath}})u(g^{\top}{\mbox{\boldmath$x$\unboldmath}}){\rm d}{\mbox{\boldmath$x$\unboldmath}}=\left\langle{v,g^{\top}\!\star u}\right\rangle.

Finally, if
a
is invariant under
G
we have for any other function
f
and for
g\in G

(g\star[af])({\mbox{\boldmath$x$\unboldmath}})=a(g{\mbox{\boldmath$x$\unboldmath}})f(g{\mbox{\boldmath$x$\unboldmath}})=a({\mbox{\boldmath$x$\unboldmath}})f(g{\mbox{\boldmath$x$\unboldmath}})=a({\mbox{\boldmath$x$\unboldmath}})(g\star f)({\mbox{\boldmath$x$\unboldmath}}).

Putting everything together we have:

\displaystyle\rho_{V}(g)\left\langle{\mbox{\boldmath$q$\unboldmath},a{\mathbf{p}}\!{}^{\top}}\right\rangle\;

\displaystyle=\left\langle{g\star\mbox{\boldmath$q$\unboldmath},a{\mathbf{p}}\!{}^{\top}}\right\rangle=\left\langle{\mbox{\boldmath$q$\unboldmath},g^{\top}\!\star[a{\mathbf{p}}\!{}^{\top}]}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle{\mbox{\boldmath$q$\unboldmath},ag^{\top}\!\star{\mathbf{p}}\!{}^{\top}}\right\rangle=\left\langle{\mbox{\boldmath$q$\unboldmath},a(\rho_{U}(g^{-1}){\mathbf{p}})^{\top}}\right\rangle=\left\langle{\mbox{\boldmath$q$\unboldmath},a{\mathbf{p}}\!{}^{\top}}\right\rangle\rho_{U}(g).

The argument adapts to the stiffness matrix:

Lemma 14
.

Suppose
{\mathbf{p}}
and
q
are symmetry-respecting bases with respect to
\rho_{U}
and
\rho_{V}
, respectively. Then
\left\langle{\nabla\mbox{\boldmath$q$\unboldmath},\nabla{\mathbf{p}}\!{}^{\top}}\right\rangle
is an intertwining matrix with respect to
\rho_{U}
and
\rho_{V}
.

Proof.

In this proof it is convenient to use the convention
\nabla{\mathbf{p}}^{\top}:\Omega\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times n}
arising from applying the gradient to each column of
{\mathbf{p}}^{\top}
and we write
\nabla\mbox{\boldmath$q$\unboldmath}\equiv(\nabla\mbox{\boldmath$q$\unboldmath}^{\top})^{\top}:\Omega\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times d}
.
Note that the chain rule for gradients can be written as
\nabla[g\star u]({\mbox{\boldmath$x$\unboldmath}})=g^{\top}[g\star\nabla u]({\mbox{\boldmath$x$\unboldmath}})
. or in particular

\nabla[g\star{\mathbf{p}}^{\top}]=g^{\top}(g\star\nabla{\mathbf{p}}^{\top})
and
\nabla[g\star\mbox{\boldmath$q$\unboldmath}]=(g\star\nabla\mbox{\boldmath$q$\unboldmath})g^{\top}
.
Finally since
\rho_{V}(g)\mbox{\boldmath$q$\unboldmath}=g\star\mbox{\boldmath$q$\unboldmath}
one can deduce that

\rho_{V}(g)\nabla\mbox{\boldmath$q$\unboldmath}=\nabla[g\star\mbox{\boldmath$q$\unboldmath}]=g^{\top}(g\star[\nabla\mbox{\boldmath$q$\unboldmath}]),

with a similar formula relating
\rho_{U}
and
\nabla{\mathbf{p}}
.
Hence the result follows very similarly to the previous lemma:

\displaystyle\rho_{V}(g)\left\langle{\nabla\mbox{\boldmath$q$\unboldmath},\nabla{\mathbf{p}}\!{}^{\top}}\right\rangle\;

\displaystyle=\left\langle{g^{\top}(g\star\nabla\mbox{\boldmath$q$\unboldmath}),\nabla{\mathbf{p}}\!{}^{\top}}\right\rangle=\left\langle{\nabla\mbox{\boldmath$q$\unboldmath},g(g^{\top}\!\star\nabla{\mathbf{p}}\!{}^{\top})}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle{\nabla\mbox{\boldmath$q$\unboldmath},\nabla[g^{\top}\!\star{\mathbf{p}}\!{}^{\top}]}\right\rangle=\left\langle{\nabla\mbox{\boldmath$q$\unboldmath},\nabla{\mathbf{p}}\!{}^{\top}}\right\rangle\rho_{U}(g).

Thus for any symmetry-respecting bases our discretisation of Schrödinger’s equation gives us intertwining matrices. But then Schur’s lemma guarantees these decouple provided our basis is decomposed!

Theorem 15
.

Support
q
and
{\mathbf{p}}
are symmetry-adapted bases. Then the mass and stiffness matrices are block-diagonalised after permutation, where the number of blocks is at most the sum of the dimensions of all irreducible representations.

Proof.

Enumerating the irreducible representations in canonical form as
\rho_{1},\ldots,\rho_{r}
we can write the symmetry-adapted bases as

q

\displaystyle=\left({\begin{array}[]{c:c:c|c|c:c:c}(\mbox{\boldmath$q$\unboldmath}_{1}^{(1)})^{\top}\hfil\lx@intercol\vrule\lx@add@cssclass&\ldots\hfil\lx@intercol\vrule\lx@add@cssclass&(\mbox{\boldmath$q$\unboldmath}_{1}^{(a_{1})})^{\top}&\ldots&(\mbox{\boldmath$q$\unboldmath}_{r}^{(1)})^{\top}\hfil\lx@intercol\vrule\lx@add@cssclass&\ldots\hfil\lx@intercol\vrule\lx@add@cssclass&(\mbox{\boldmath$q$\unboldmath}_{r}^{(a_{r})})^{\top}\end{array}}\right)^{\top}

\displaystyle{\mathbf{p}}\;

\displaystyle=\left({\begin{array}[]{c:c:c|c|c:c:c}({\mathbf{p}}_{1}^{(1)})^{\top}\hfil\lx@intercol\vrule\lx@add@cssclass&\ldots\hfil\lx@intercol\vrule\lx@add@cssclass&({\mathbf{p}}_{1}^{(b_{1})})^{\top}&\ldots&({\mathbf{p}}_{r}^{(1)})^{\top}\hfil\lx@intercol\vrule\lx@add@cssclass&\ldots\hfil\lx@intercol\vrule\lx@add@cssclass&({\mathbf{p}}_{r}^{(b_{r})})^{\top}\end{array}}\right)^{\top}

where

\mbox{\boldmath$q$\unboldmath}_{\kappa}^{(k)}
and
{\mathbf{p}}_{\ell}^{(j)}
are irreducible symmetry-adapted bases with respect to
\rho_{\kappa}
and
\rho_{\ell}
, respectively. Schur’s Lemma
8
guarantees that if
\kappa\neq\ell
then the associated mass matrix
\left\langle{\mbox{\boldmath$q$\unboldmath}_{\kappa}^{(k)},a{\mathbf{p}}_{\ell}^{(j)}}\right\rangle
is zero, as is the associated stiffness matrix. Thus the mass and stiffness matrix are block-diagonal with
r
different blocks. But we also have
\left\langle{\mbox{\boldmath$q$\unboldmath}_{\kappa}^{(k)},a{\mathbf{p}}_{\kappa}^{(j)}}\right\rangle=c_{\kappa,k,j}I_{d_{\kappa}}
where
d_{\kappa}
is the dimension of
\rho_{\kappa}
hence each of these blocks can be viewed as a block matrix where the blocks are constant multiples of the identity. Permuting the rows/columns to group together different basis elements results in a block-diagonal matrix with
d_{\kappa}
blocks. Thus the total number of blocks is
d_{1}+\cdots+d_{r}
.

There is another angle we can consider; rather than utilising more symmetry than even-odd symmetry we can consider potentials with less symmetry, e.g., consider the Schrödinger equation in a cube with potential
a(x,y,z)=(x-y)^{2}+(y-z)^{2}+(x-z)^{2}

and also invariant under negation

Monomials capture the negation symmetry and enable decoupling into two linear systems. On the other hand, we can combine negation and permutation in the group

and

conc

Conclusions.

Symmetry can be used effectively to decouple the numerical solutions of partial differential equations into independent solves. This is standard practice for even-odd symmetry and rotational symmetry but we have seen how more sophisticated symmetry groups like those associated with the square and cube can be used to drastically increase parallelisation. This is accomplished by combining knowledge of irreducible representations with either an explicit or numerical block-diagonalisation procedure. In the case of the cube there is a drastic increase in parallelisation, where in the case of permutation symmetry we go from 2 to 8 independent numerical problems, each with much matrices of significantly smaller dimension. While we focussed on monomial bases for simplicity everything translates to multivariate orthogonal polynomials, which on squares and cubes have the exact same representation structure as the monomial basis.

Everything discussed extends to other groups, including the symmetric group, which is relevant to Schrödinger equations with many particles, other dihedral groups which apply to equations posed on regular polygons, and the hyperoctohedral group, which applies to hyper-cubes. Note that the potential for parallelisation grows rapidly as the dimension increases: for example the number of independent numerical solutions associated with the symmetric group is equal to the total number of Young Tableau associated with all partitions, which can be computed using the hook length formula. In 20-dimensions this would parallelise a PDE over an astronomically high 10 billion cores! Though it would be rather challenging to find a machine with more cores than the current human population, and the setup cost of the current approach of forming the full system and decomposing numerically would be prohibitively expensive, to say the least. Nevertheless, the potential for scaling the numerical solution of PDEs to higher dimensions is clear.

A more realistic goal is constructing effective numerics directly for polynomial bases associated with special classes of irreducible representations, such as the trivial and sign representations of
S_{n}
corresponding to computing Bosons and Fermions. Note that approximating potentials invariant with respect to permutations by symmetric polynomials is an active research results with effective schemes recently introduced
[
7
]
. Such techniques have the potential to overcome the curse of dimensionality: the number of symmetric polynomials of degree-
p
in dimension
d
correspond to partitions of
p
with
d
rows, which grows only algebraically as
p
or
d
become large.

Finally, a straightforward extension is to vector-valued PDEs like Maxwell’s equation. While we focussed on scalar bases and partial differential equations everything translates to the vector case if we modify the notation of symmetry-adapted according to the principle of
equivariance
, that is, a
vector symmetry-respecting basis

{\mathbf{p}}:\Omega\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times d}
would satisfy

{\mathbf{p}}(g{\mbox{\boldmath$x$\unboldmath}})g=\rho(g){\mathbf{p}}({\mbox{\boldmath$x$\unboldmath}})

and decomposing
\rho
leads to a vector symmetry-adapted basis. Weak formulations involving gradients, divergence, and curl operators are then intertwining matrices by very similar arguments to Lemma
14
. Another example are Sturm–Liouville operators: if we have a matrix-valued function
A:\Omega\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times d}
that is equivariant in the sense that, for all
g\in G
,

A(g{\mbox{\boldmath$x$\unboldmath}})g=gA({\mbox{\boldmath$x$\unboldmath}})

then an operator such as
\left\langle{\nabla v,A\nabla u}\right\rangle
will also be an intertwining operator.
In all these cases Schur’s lemma will guarantee that a discretisation automatically decouples according to the sum of the dimensions of the irreducible representations.
