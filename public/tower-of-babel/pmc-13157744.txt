Foreign body-type giant cell reaction post intracranial endovascular clot retrieval: a postmortem case
Authors: Kanila Perera, Nadya Lazareva, Catriona McLean, Geoffrey Cloud
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and aimsEndovascular clot retrieval (ECR) has revolutionised acute ischaemic stroke management. While procedural safety is well established, little attention has been paid to vascular responses to hydrophilic polymer coatings used on retrieval devices. This report presents postmortem histopathological findings implicating a foreign body giant cell reaction secondary to hydrophilic polymer exposure as a cause of recurrent stroke following ECR.MethodsA detailed clinical and neuropathological review was conducted on a 72-year-old patient who experienced three sequential ischaemic strokes within 4 months following ECR and angioplasty of a right middle cerebral artery (M2) occlusion. Limited postmortem examination of the heart and brain was undertaken with histopathological and immunoperoxidase staining to assess potential vascular inflammatory mechanisms.ResultsPostmortem examination demonstrated multiple infarcts of differing ages, diffuse atherosclerotic change and a florid multinucleated giant cell reaction containing non-polarisable basophilic material along the luminal surface of the right M2 segment and adjacent vessels. The internal elastic lamina remained intact, with only focal fibrinoid necrosis and sparse medial inflammation. No amyloid angiopathy, vasculitis or cardiac embolic source was identified. The morphological features were consistent with a chronic inflammatory response to embolised hydrophilic polymer coating material from prior endovascular instrumentation.ConclusionsThis case highlights an under-recognised mechanism of post-ECR recurrent stroke linked to hydrophilic polymer-induced vascular inflammation. If recognised antemortem, immunosuppression may be more effective than usual secondary stroke prevention treatments.

Introduction

Endovascular clot retrieval (ECR) or mechanical thrombectomy is a highly efficacious treatment that since 2015 has become part of the standard of care of hyperacute stroke secondary to large vessel occlusion. ECR reduces disability and improves functional outcomes compared with medical management alone.13 Parallelling this, ECR techniques and devices have also evolved, with stent retriever and contact aspiration approaches (often augmented with balloon-guided catheters), largely replacing earlier mechanical extraction devices of the early 2000s.4 5 Contemporary devices have improved first pass recanalisation and safety outcomes; however, polymer coatings on these devices may damage the vessel lumen during traversal. We present postmortem histopathological findings from a case of recurrent multi-territory ischaemic strokes in which a giant cell reaction secondary to hydrophilic polymer material was identified within the intimal surface of a vessel previously treated with ECR, providing a plausible explanation for recurrent events in the treated territory.6 7

Case presentation

A patient in his early 70s with treated hypertension, dyslipidaemia and type 2 diabetes mellitus presented within 4 hours of symptom onset in February 2025 with acute right middle cerebral artery (MCA) territory symptoms of left upper limb weakness and facial droop, sensory inattention and left visual field deficits. The National Institute of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) score was 9.

This was on a background of prior presentation to a separate health service for a transient ischaemic attack in October 2023, with 5 min of right facial droop and arm weakness with associated less than 50% internal carotid artery stenosis. He was discharged on 3 weeks of dual antiplatelet therapy with aspirin and clopidogrel and stepped down to clopidogrel monotherapy thereafter. Eight months later, he re-presented in May 2024 with subacute left leg weakness; CT showed right frontal convexity subarachnoid haemorrhage. There was no history of trauma. MRI demonstrated additional left frontal and left marginal sulcal superficial siderosis suggestive of underlying probable cerebral amyloid angiopathy (CAA). His clopidogrel therapy was then stopped and at the time of his presentation in February 2025 he had not been taking antithrombotic treatment for 9 months.

Acute CT showed no established right MCA infarction, CT angiography demonstrated a right M2 occlusion and CT Perfusion demonstrated a perfusion mismatch of 71 mL using Rapid software (figure 1a). Intravenous thrombolysis was withheld owing to concerns of underlying CAA, and the patient went direct to ECR. The procedure was technically challenging, requiring five passes, repeated aspiration attempts and two stent retriever deployments followed by balloon angioplasty for presumed intracranial atherosclerotic disease (ICAD) of the right MCA, with a final reperfusion modified Treatment in Cerebral Infarction score of 2C. The aspiration catheters used in the procedure were SOFIA 5F and Penumbra RED 43 devices, composed of stainless-steel and nitinol (nickel-titanium alloy) coils, with an inner polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) lining with proprietary hydrophilic coatings.8 9 Balloon angioplasty of the identified focal right MCA stenosis was undertaken with a NeuroSpeed 1.5×8 mm device, which itself has a double-layer hydrophilic coating.10 The two Medtronic Solitaire X 4×20 mm stents used in the procedure were composed of nitinol with platinum markings, and the manufacturer does not stipulate the use of hydrophilic coatings on this device.11

Postprocedure recovery was favourable, with NIHSS score improving to 1. MRI showed expected infarction in the right lateral frontal, anterior parietal and temporal lobes without new haemorrhage or susceptibility changes meeting Boston 2.0 criteria for probable CAA (figure 1b). Brief atrial flutter with 4:1 block was observed on inpatient telemetry; however, given its lack of reproducibility and angiographic findings, stroke aetiology was favoured to be ICAD (figure 2a–c). The patient was discharged on dual antiplatelet therapy of aspirin and clopidogrel.

On review in the stroke outpatient service 6 weeks later, in April 2025, an NIHSS score of 0 was achieved. Community rehabilitation was ongoing but the modified Rankin Scale score was 2, predominantly due to fatigue. An outpatient transthoracic echocardiogram showed a structurally normal heart with normal biventricular systolic function and no major valvular abnormalities. Although a bubble study was not performed, normal-sized atria with no resting interatrial shunt were observed. 19 days of outpatient telemetry demonstrated predominantly sinus rhythm and rare atrial and ventricular ectopy. No further episodes of atrial fibrillation/flutter were identified.

On 25 May 2025, the patient re-presented to the hospital with a delayed presentation of left homonymous hemianopia (NIHSS score of 4). Imaging demonstrated an established right posterior cerebral artery infarct with P2 occlusion (figure 1c). No accelerated progression of right MCA intracranial atherosclerosis was identified on CT angiography. CT of chest, abdomen and pelvis showed an incidental splenic infarct. Despite no confirmed cardioembolic source, on discussion with the patient’s medical treatment decision makers, a consensus decision was made to commence therapeutic anticoagulation given evidence of recurrent embolic events. A repeat MRI brain undertaken on 30 May demonstrated persisting right frontal Susceptibility Weighted Imaging (SWI) changes reflective of the old known intraparenchymal haemorrhage (not meeting Boston 2.0 criteria for probable CAA) and an established right Posterior Cerebral Artery (PCA) territory infarct (figure 1d). There was no radiographic evidence of CAA-related inflammation or amyloid-beta-related angiitis seen on MR angiographic sequences.

Within 72 hours of anticoagulation commencement, the patient developed further neurological deterioration with a new right MCA infarct confirmed on CT (figure 1e). Repeated cardiac evaluation remained negative. Neurological decline continued, and care was transitioned to palliation. The patient died in June 2025 from recurrent multi-territory ischaemic stroke.

Postmortem histopathological examination

A limited autopsy with restricted organ examination of the heart and brain was undertaken following permission from the next of kin. Examination of the heart revealed no significant structural or microscopic abnormality, and no amyloid was detected. Fibromyxoid changes and calcification of the aortic valve were observed and no vegetations were identified. There was mild coronary artery atherosclerosis. Examination of the brain revealed multiple infarcts of varying age in both left and right hemispheres, ranging from months to hours old. No amyloid plaques or tangles were seen with Abeta and tau immunoperoxidase. Diffuse atherosclerotic changes of the right MCA, left MCA, left Anterior Cerebral Artery (ACA) and basilar artery were observed, as evidenced by fibrointimal hyperplasia and luminal narrowing. Multinucleate giant cells containing non-polarisable and non-birefringent basophilic material (online supplemental figure 1.1) were present throughout the right parietal and occipital cortices and right basal ganglia, occluding capillary lumina (online supplemental figure 1.3). The right M2 segment of the MCA showed numerous luminal surface multinucleate giant cells (online supplemental figure 1.2) with focal underlying fibrinoid necrosis and an associated light chronic inflammatory infiltrate throughout the wall with no destruction of the internal elastic lamina. Similar changes were also seen in a distal small branch of the right MCA (post-M2 subdivision), in an area that was not accessed by guidewires, aspiration catheters or stent retriever devices during the digital subtraction angiogram. Giant cells were also seen adjacent to the wall of the right ACA. Importantly, no giant cells were observed destroying the internal elastic lamina and the tunica media and adventitia showed a sparse inflammatory cell. The luminal giant cells, the florid localised reaction in the M2 MCA segment, the lack of giant cells destroying the internal elastica and medial inflammation point away from a diagnosis of a large vessel arteritis process and suggest a giant cell response to hydrophilic coating from the previous endovascular procedure.

Discussion

Foreign body embolisation and extravasation of hydrophilic polymer material following endovascular procedures using coated devices were first described in various end-organ vascular beds in 2009.12 Hydrophilic polymer coatings are widely employed to reduce friction between devices and vessel walls and to minimise arterial spasm, and, as evidenced in this case, are frequently used in various proprietary balloon angioplasty and aspiration catheters that are commonplace in ECR procedures.6 7 12 Mehta et al and others have described foreign body emboli in patients following vascular procedures using hydrophilic-coated devices, including cases involving endovascular cerebral aneurysm coil embolisation.12 13 Histopathological examination demonstrated basophilic intravascular material with associated histiocytic and giant cell reactions across multiple end-organ vascular beds, often remote from the procedural site.12

Our case adds to this literature by demonstrating intracranial foreign body giant cell reaction following ECR and highlights hydrophilic polymer embolisation as a potentially under-recognised entity in stroke medicine. To the best of our knowledge, this has been described twice before by Ishikawa et al14 and more recently by Wang et al.15

Although traditional vascular risk factors and diffuse intracranial atherosclerosis were present, the geographical predominance of histopathological changes within the right hemisphere—corresponding to the territory previously treated with ECR—suggests an additive, procedure-related contribution to recurrent ischaemic events. Potential mechanisms include microvascular occlusion at the time of polymer embolisation or progressive endothelial injury driven by chronic local inflammatory response to retained foreign material. Further data are required to consider if procedure duration, degree of difficulty in passage of catheters and whether individual stent retriever devices with differing proprietary polymer coatings will result in variable clinical and histopathological outcomes.

Clot analysis studies provide indirect evidence supporting the frequency of foreign material deposition during thrombectomy. Abbasi et al identified foreign particles in approximately 50% of retrieved thrombi, with morphological features consistent with neurovascular catheter materials.7 However, inflammatory responses were not observed within clot specimens, suggesting that tissue-level reactions may evolve over time and remain undetected without histopathological examination. Notably, PTFE catheter lining material was more frequently identified than hydrophilic polymers, underscoring ongoing uncertainty regarding the relative contribution of different proprietary coatings.

Conclusion

In an era where postmortem examination is infrequently performed, foreign body embolisation following ECR is likely underappreciated. This mechanism should be considered in patients with unexplained vasculopathy or recurrent ischaemic events after endovascular intervention. Further clinicopathological correlation is required to define prevalence, procedural risk factors and potential mitigation strategies.
