CDK4/6 Inhibitors and Curcumin Synergistically Suppress HER2− Breast Cancer via Lethal ROS and Inhibition of Metastasis
Authors: Chenyu Wang, Yiqin Zou, Chunyu Chang, Siyue Liu, Xiankun Ke, Liang Hu, Di Wang, Yukang Lu, Shiwen Luo, Sijian Huang, Yilan Hu, Wenqi Yu, Aiping Deng, Ming Yue, Jue Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCDK4/6 inhibitors represent a key therapy for HR+/HER2− breast cancer, yet resistance and limited efficacy in triple‐negative breast cancer (TNBC) remain significant challenges. Using a self‐established drug combination prediction platform, we identified a strong synergy between the CDK4/6 inhibitor palbociclib and the natural compound curcumin across both HR+/HER2− and TNBC models, an effect that was also observed with ribociclib. Mechanistically, the combination led to a collapse of redox homeostasis. Despite upregulating transcriptional antioxidant responses, the treatment downregulated critical ROS‐detoxifying enzymes (e.g., GPX4) and depleted glutathione. This imbalance induced lethal oxidative stress, lipid peroxidation, and extensive DNA damage. Furthermore, excessive ROS accumulation contributed to suppression of epithelial‐mesenchymal transition (EMT) and reduced migratory and invasive phenotypes. The antioxidant N‐acetylcysteine (NAC) attenuated both cytotoxicity and migration inhibition, supporting a central role for ROS in these effects. Together, these findings identify a redox‐based mechanism underlying the combination and provide a rationale for further preclinical evaluation in HR+/HER2− breast cancer and TNBC.

Introduction

Breast cancer is the most frequently diagnosed malignancy and the leading cause of cancer‐related deaths among women worldwide, including in China [1, 2, 3]. The HER2‐negative (HER2−) population represents the majority of cases [4] and primarily comprises two distinct molecular subtypes: hormone receptor‐positive (HR+/HER2−) and triple‐negative breast cancer (TNBC) [5, 6]. HR+/HER2− breast cancer is the most prevalent subtype, typically treated with endocrine therapy [7, 8]. In contrast, TNBC is an aggressive subtype characterized by high heterogeneity, early metastatic propensity, and intrinsic resistance to conventional endocrine and HER2‐targeted therapies [9]. Although cytotoxic chemotherapy remains the cornerstone of TNBC treatment, high recurrence rates highlight the urgent need for novel therapeutic strategies.

The advent of CDK4/6 inhibitors has revolutionized the treatment pattern for advanced HR+/HER2− breast cancer [10]. Mechanistically, cyclin D‐CDK4/6 complexes phosphorylate the retinoblastoma (RB) protein, leading to its inactivation and subsequent release of E2F transcription factors that drive G1/S phase progression [11]. CDK4/6 inhibitors block this phosphorylation event, thereby maintaining RB in its active hypophosphorylated state and preventing G1/S transition. Combining these agents with endocrine therapy significantly improves progression‐free or overall survival, as demonstrated in the pivotal Phase III trials PALOMA‐2 [12], MONALEESA‐2 [13], and MONARCH‐3 [14], establishing this regimen as the standard first‐line treatment for HR+/HER2− breast cancer. However, acquired resistance to CDK4/6 inhibitors eventually develops in a considerable number of patients [15]. Furthermore, the application of CDK4/6 inhibitors in TNBC remains challenging and controversial. Despite the presence of functional retinoblastoma (RB) protein in a subset of TNBC tumors, monotherapy efficacy is limited [16], partly due to compensatory mechanisms such as lysosomal drug sequestration [17] and c‐Myc overexpression [18]. Consequently, research focus has shifted toward exploring rational combination strategies to overcome resistance and extend the benefits of CDK4/6 inhibition to TNBC patients. Currently, combination regimens with PARP inhibitors [19], CDK7 inhibitors [20, 21], or BRD4 inhibitors [22] have shown promise in preclinical studies, though their clinical translation requires further validation.

Curcumin, a natural polyphenol derived from turmeric, has garnered significant research interest due to its multi‐targeted anticancer properties [23, 24]. Curcumin and its major metabolite tetrahydrocurcumin (THC) have emerged as promising candidates for the treatment of TNBC [25, 26]. THC has been shown to suppress TNBC progression by targeting TRIP13 and disrupting its interactions with key signaling molecules, thereby unveiling a novel mechanistic basis for its anti‐tumor activity [26]. Nevertheless, the clinical application of curcumin and its analogs (curcuminoids) is limited by their poor bioavailability and a lack of robust validation of pharmacokinetics and efficacy in human studies. To address these challenges, ongoing research is exploring novel delivery systems, such as nanoparticles, to enhance stability and therapeutic outcomes [27, 28, 29]. Additionally, only a limited number of studies have investigated the combined use of CDK4/6 inhibitors with curcumin in the context of TNBC, suggesting an area ripe for further exploration.

Our recent work identified a potent synergistic interaction between palbociclib and curcumin in both HR+/HER2− and TNBC cell lines using a network‐based prediction platform [30]; however, the underlying mechanisms remained elusive. Building on this finding, we performed integrated transcriptomic and functional analyses to elucidate the mechanistic framework. Our results demonstrate that this synergy is primarily driven by ROS‐mediated DNA damage and reversal of EMT, leading to enhanced apoptosis and suppression of invasion and metastasis both in vitro and in vivo. This study provides mechanistic evidence for the synergistic anti‐tumor effects of palbociclib and curcumin and supports further preclinical investigation of this combination in breast cancer.

Materials and Methods

Cell Culture

The human breast cancer cell lines MCF7, MDA‐MB‐231, and MDA‐MB‐468 were obtained from Wuhan Pronosei Biotechnology Co. Ltd., while the mouse breast cancer cell line 4 T1 was purchased from Shanghai Jihe Biotechnology Co. Ltd. MCF7, MDA‐MB‐231, and MDA‐MB‐468 were cultured in high‐glucose DMEM medium (Gibco), whereas 4 T1 cells were maintained in RPMI 1640 medium (Gibco). All media were supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Gibco, 15 140‐122). Additionally, the medium for MCF7 cells was supplemented with 0.01 mg/mL insulin. The cells were cultured at 37°C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2.

Cytotoxicity Assay

Cell viability was assessed using the CCK‐8 assay (Biosharp, BS350C). Cells were seeded in 96‐well plates (5000 cells/well) and treated as indicated. After adding 10 μL CCK‐8 reagent and incubating for 2 h at 37°C, absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA).

Colony‐Formation Assay

Cells were trypsinized, seeded in 6‐well plates, and treated with indicated drugs for 24 h after adhesion. The medium was replaced, and cells were cultured for 2–3 weeks with renewal every 3 days. Colonies were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Biosharp, BL539A), stained with crystal violet, and colonies containing > 50 cells were counted.

Cell Cycle and Apoptosis Analysis

Cell cycle and apoptosis were analyzed using the Cell Cycle and Apoptosis Analysis Kit (Biosharp, BL114A) and Annexin V‐FITC/PI Apoptosis Detection Kit (ZOMANBIO, ZP327‐1). For cell cycle analysis, cells were fixed in 70% ethanol, stained with PI/RNase A, and examined by flow cytometry. For apoptosis analysis, cells were stained with Annexin V‐FITC/PI and analyzed by flow cytometry (BD FACSCalibur; Beckman Cytoflex LX).

RNA‐Sequencing

Cells were collected 24 h post‐treatment for RNA‐seq. Sequencing was performed by BGI Genomics (Shenzhen, China) on the DNBSEQ platform. Total RNA was extracted, and mRNA libraries were constructed, amplified into DNA Nanoballs, and sequenced (PE150) on a DNBSEQ‐G400 system. Raw reads were filtered to obtain clean reads for differential gene expression analysis.

Comet Assay

Following 24 h of drug treatment, cells were resuspended in cold PBS (1 × 106 cells/mL) and analyzed using a Comet Assay Kit (Beyotime, C2041S). Cells were mixed with low‐melting‐point agarose, layered on slides, lysed, and subjected to electrophoresis at 4°C in the dark. After neutralization and staining, images were obtained by fluorescence microscopy. DNA damage was quantified using the formula: Tail DNA (%) = (Tail DNA Intensity/Total Comet DNA Intensity) × 100.

Immunofluorescence

Cells on coverslips were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X‐100 (Biosharp, BS084), and blocked with 1% BSA. After incubation with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C, cells were treated with fluorescent secondary antibodies, stained with DAPI (Biosharp, BL739A), and imaged using an inverted fluorescence microscope (Olympus IX83, Japan).

Western Blot

Cells were lysed with RIPA buffer (Beyotime, P0013B), and protein concentrations were measured using the BCA Protein Assay Kit (YEASEN, 20201ES76). Proteins were separated by SDS‐PAGE, transferred to PVDF membranes (Bio‐Rad, 1 620 177), incubated with primary and HRP‐conjugated secondary antibodies, and visualized using the Ultrasensitive ECL Kit (PUMOKE, PMK0448) with a chemiluminescence imaging system (Bio‐Rad, USA). All antibodies used in this study are provided in Table S1.

RNA Isolation and Quantitative RT‐PCR Analysis

Total RNA was extracted using the RNA Extraction Kit (Novizan, RC112‐01) and reverse‐transcribed into cDNA with the PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit (TaKaRa, RR037A). qPCR was performed using iTaq Universal SYBR Green Supermix (Bio‐Rad, 1 725 121) on a CFX Touch Real‐Time PCR System (Bio‐Rad). Relative mRNA levels were calculated by the 2−ΔΔCt method, normalized to ACTIN. Primer sequences are listed in Table S2.

ROS Detection

Intracellular ROS levels were measured using the Reactive Oxygen Species Assay Kit (Beyotime, S0033S). Cells (1 × 106) were incubated with 5 μM DCFH‐DA at 37°C for 30 min in the dark, then washed, resuspended in PBS, and analyzed by flow cytometry (BD FACSCalibur, USA).

MDA Assay

Malondialdehyde (MDA) levels were measured using the Lipid Peroxidation MDA Assay Kit (Beyotime, S0131S). Cell lysates were mixed with TBA working solution, heated at 100°C, cooled, and centrifuged. Supernatants were transferred to a 96‐well plate, and absorbance at 532 nm was measured using a microplate reader (Thermo Fisher, USA).

GSH Measurement

Intracellular GSH levels were measured using the GSH and GSSG Assay Kit (Beyotime, S0053). Samples were lysed with Protein Removal Reagent M, subjected to freeze–thaw cycles, and centrifuged. For GSSG detection, samples were treated with GSH Scavenger. Absorbance was measured using a microplate reader (Thermo Fisher, USA), and GSH levels were calculated as: GSH = Total Glutathione–GSSG × 2.

Transwell Experiment

Matrigel (Corning, 356234) was diluted (1:8) and added to Transwell chambers. Cells in serum‐free medium were seeded in the upper chambers, with 10% FBS medium in the lower chambers. After 48 h, invaded cells were fixed, stained with crystal violet, and counted in five random fields under a microscope.

Wound Healing

Cells were seeded in 6‐well plates to 90%–100% confluence, and scratches were made with a 200 μL pipette tip. After washing, cells were cultured in medium containing 1% FBS and the indicated drugs. Images were taken at 0, 24, and 48 h, and wound closure was quantified using ImageJ.

Preparation and Characterization of Curcumin Liposome (Cur@Lipo)

Curcumin‐loaded liposomes (cur@Lipo) were prepared using the thin‐film hydration method. Briefly, 50 mg of soy lecithin, 2.5 mg of DSPE‐PEG2000, and 3 mg of curcumin were dissolved in 6 mL of anhydrous ethanol. The organic solvent was subsequently removed by rotary evaporation to form a thin lipid film. Then, 5 mL of saline was added to hydrate the film under ultrasonication for 30 min. Free curcumin was removed through centrifugation and dialysis (MWCO = 3500 Da). The obtained cur@Lipo was characterized by dynamic light scattering (DLS) (Brookhaven Instruments, USA), and the concentration of encapsulated curcumin was quantified using a SpectraMax iD5 microplate reader (Molecular Devices, USA).

Animal Experiments

Female BALB/c mice (7–8 weeks old) were obtained from Wuhan Zikeheng Biotechnology Co. Ltd. and maintained under SPF conditions. All procedures were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of the Department of Medicine, Wuhan University of Science and Technology. Mice were randomized into four groups (n = 5) comprising control, palbociclib, cur@Lipo, and combination. Over 21 days, treatments were administered every other day. Palbociclib was given by oral gavage at 2.5 mg/kg, and cur@Lipo was injected intravenously at 1 mg/kg. Mice were then euthanized, and lungs were collected to assess metastatic nodules and for further analysis. Carcasses were disposed of according to institutional guidelines.

ALT Measurement

ALT activity was detected using an ALT/GPT Assay Kit (Solarbio, BC1555). Blood samples were collected from the retro‐orbital plexus of anesthetized mice. The blood was allowed to clot at room temperature for 15–20 min and then centrifuged at 1000 × g for 10 min at 4°C to obtain the serum. The supernatants were incubated with the reaction reagents. Absorbance was measured at 505 nm using a microplate reader, and ALT activity was calculated from the pyruvate standard curve.

Immunohistochemistry

Processing of tumor tissue sections was performed by Wuhan Saiwei Biotechnology Co. Ltd. Sections were dewaxed, rehydrated, treated with 3% hydrogen peroxide, blocked, and incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C. After incubation with secondary antibodies, signals were visualized using DAB and counterstained with hematoxylin. Slides were scanned using a Pannoramic MIDI scanner and analyzed with CasViewer 2.4.

Statistical Analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software (version 9). Differences between two groups were analyzed using an unpaired two‐tailed Student's t‐test, while comparisons among three or more groups were evaluated using one‐way or two‐way ANOVA.

Results

CDK4/6 Inhibitors and Curcumin Exhibit Synergy in Inhibiting HR+/HER2− Breast Cancer and TNBC Cell Lines

Our previous studies indicated a promising synergistic effect between the CDK4/6 inhibitor palbociclib and curcumin [30]. We first evaluated their individual effects on a panel of breast cancer cell lines, including HR+/HER2− (MCF‐7) and TNBC (MDA‐MB‐231 and MDA‐MB‐468) cells. The results confirmed that both compounds suppressed cell proliferation in a dose‐dependent manner, with more pronounced effects observed at 48 h compared to 24 h (Figure 1A; Figure S1A). To quantify the drug interaction, we calculated the combination index (CI) using the Chou‐Talalay method. This method provides a quantitative measure of synergistic (CI < 1), additive (CI = 1), and antagonistic (CI > 1) effects [31]. Our results showed that the combination of palbociclib and curcumin yielded CI values of 0.532, 0.647, and 0.612 in MCF‐7, MDA‐MB‐231, and MDA‐MB‐468 cells, respectively (Figure 1B; Figure S1B,C).

(A) Cell viability after palbociclib or curcumin treatment for 24/48 h. (B) Synergistic effect of combined treatment (Combo). (C, D) Colony formation in cells treated with DMSO, palbociclib (2.5 μM), curcumin (2.5 μM), or combination. (E, F) Apoptosis analysis by flow cytometry after treatment with DMSO, palbociclib (10 μM), curcumin (20 μM), or combination. (G, H) Cell cycle distribution analysis. Data are presented as means (± SD) of technical triplicates. *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001; ns, not significant.

We then expanded our assessment to include ribociclib, another FDA‐approved CDK4/6 inhibitor. Consistent with the findings for palbociclib, CCK‐8 assays demonstrated that ribociclib also exhibits a similar inhibitory effect on breast cancer cell proliferation (Figure S1D). Similarly, the combination of ribociclib and curcumin resulted in CI values of 0.717 and 0.389 in MCF‐7 and MDA‐MB‐231 cells (Figure S1E,F). This synergy was further corroborated by colony formation assays, where low‐dose combinations significantly abrogated clonogenic survival compared to monotherapies (Figure 1C,D; Figure S1G,H).

Flow cytometric analysis revealed that the combination of palbociclib/ribociclib and curcumin significantly induced apoptosis in all tested cell lines (Figure 1E,F; Figure S2A–C). Intriguingly, cell cycle analysis indicated a distinct change in phase distribution. While CDK4/6 inhibitors typically induce G1 arrest, the combination with curcumin resulted in a pronounced shift toward G2/M phase arrest (Figure 1G,H; Figure S2D). Collectively, these findings confirm strong synergistic anti‐tumor activity between CDK4/6 inhibitors and curcumin in both HR+/HER2− and TNBC models, manifesting as enhanced anti‐proliferative effects, induction of apoptosis, and a marked G2/M phase arrest.

Given that CDK4/6 inhibitors are clinically administered alongside endocrine therapy for HR+/HER2− disease, we benchmarked our curcumin‐palbociclib doublet against the standard‐of‐care (letrozole plus palbociclib) in MCF‐7 cells (Figure S3A–D). The doublet achieved a degree of synergy (CI = 0.532) comparable to that of the standard‐of‐care (CI = 0.719). Unexpectedly, the triple combination of curcumin, letrozole, and palbociclib provided no additional benefit and displayed mild antagonism (CI = 1.072). This aligns with reports that curcumin independently suppresses estrogen receptors (ERs) [32], rendering concurrent aromatase inhibition redundant. These findings suggest that the palbociclib‐curcumin combination warrants further investigation as a potential therapeutic strategy, particularly in settings where endocrine therapy is ineffective or poorly tolerated.

Considering the established role of RB1 aberrations in resistance to CDK4/6 inhibitors, we investigated whether this synergy required canonical suppression of the CDK4/6‐RB1 axis. Western blotting revealed that while curcumin alone had no effect on RB1, palbociclib alone and the combination significantly reduced p‐RB1 (Ser807/811) in both MCF‐7 and MDA‐MB‐231 cells (Figure S4A). However, total RB1 protein responses varied. The combination reduced total RB1 in MCF‐7 cells but not in MDA‐MB‐231 cells. Notably, in MCF‐7 and MDA‐MB‐231 cells, phosphorylated RB1 reduction was comparable between palbociclib alone and the combination, yet robust synergy was only achieved with the combined treatment. This indicates that the enhanced antitumor activity is not primarily mediated through the RB1 pathway but rather through alternative pathways.

RNA Sequencing Profiling Reveals That the Combination of Palbociclib With Curcumin Alters the Genes Expression in ROS and EMT Pathways

To dissect the molecular mechanisms underlying the observed synergy, we performed RNA sequencing (RNA‐seq) on MDA‐MB‐231 cells treated with palbociclib, curcumin, or their combination. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using thresholds of FDR < 0.05 and |log2FC| > 1. While curcumin treatment elicited very few DEGs, palbociclib induced substantial transcriptional changes, characterized by 916 upregulated and 704 downregulated genes. Notably, although the total number of DEGs in the combination group was slightly higher than in the palbociclib group, the expression profile shifted significantly toward downregulation, resulting in 727 upregulated and 967 downregulated genes. This shift suggests that the combination induces a transcriptional response distinct from that of palbociclib monotherapy (Figure 2A).

(A) Volcano plot displaying differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in MDA‐MB‐231 cells treated with palbociclib (10 μM), curcumin (20 μM), or the combination. (B) Over‐representation analysis (ORA) showing the top 10 upregulated and downregulated pathways. (C) GSEA enrichment plots. (D) Heatmap showing expression profiles of genes in the indicated pathways. Data are presented as log2‐normalized values.

Over‐representation analysis (ORA) of the DEGs was performed using the Hallmark gene sets from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB) as the pathway reference. The top 10 significantly enriched Hallmark pathways for upregulated genes in the combination treatment group were associated with mTORC1 signaling, the p53 pathway, endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress, bile acid metabolism, reactive oxygen species (ROS), apoptosis, adipogenesis, inflammatory signaling (NF‐κB), glycolysis, and gluconeogenesis. Conversely, the top 10 enriched pathways for downregulated genes were related to cell cycle progression (mitotic spindle, G2/M checkpoint), estrogen response, hypoxia, epithelial‐mesenchymal transition (EMT), DNA repair, KRAS signaling, MYC targets, and TGF‐β signaling (Figure 2B).

To identify signatures specific to the combination, we compared these profiles against those of the monotherapies. Palbociclib alone triggered compensatory pathways such as cholesterol homeostasis and mTORC1 signaling but failed to significantly activate the ROS pathway (Figure S5A). While it effectively suppressed cell cycle and DNA repair signatures, it did not notably inhibit EMT (Figure S5B). Similarly, curcumin monotherapy yielded fewer DEGs and lacked prominent ROS activation, with downregulated genes primarily related to inflammation rather than EMT (Figure S5C,D).

Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) corroborated the ORA findings and defined a transcriptional signature unique to the combination. This regimen exhibited significant positive enrichment for ROS‐associated pathways alongside concurrent negative enrichment for DNA repair, G2/M checkpoint, and EMT (Figure 2C). Heatmap visualization further confirmed the coordinated upregulation of ROS‐related genes and the suppression of EMT markers specifically in the combination group. Detailed inspection suggested that the suppression of cell cycle and DNA repair genes was largely driven by palbociclib, whereas the robust modulation of ROS and EMT was a distinct feature of the dual treatment (Figure 2D).

Collectively, these data support a model wherein the two agents exert complementary effects. Palbociclib primarily drives an anti‐proliferative phenotype by suppressing CDK4/6‐dependent transcriptional programs and impairing DNA repair capacity. Curcumin, while not significantly activating ROS as a single agent, contributes by modulating metabolic and inflammatory pathways. Critically, their combination uniquely induces a pronounced ROS response, which may interact with and enhance the effects of both agents. A notable finding was that palbociclib monotherapy activates mTORC1 signaling and lipid/cholesterol biosynthetic programs, which may represent compensatory pro‐survival or adaptive responses. This compensatory activation appeared to be partially modulated by curcumin in the combination setting. These transcriptional changes provided a testable mechanistic framework for the observed enhanced anti‐tumor activity of the palbociclib‐curcumin combination in TNBC.

Synergistic Disruption of Redox Homeostasis by Palbociclib and Curcumin Triggers Oxidative Stress in Cancer Cells

Our initial RNA‐seq and GSEA analyses pointed to a robust activation of ROS‐related pathways following combined treatment with palbociclib and curcumin. To decipher the transcriptional pattern driving this activation, we examined individual ROS‐related genes and uncovered a paradoxical response. While a broad set of core antioxidant and stress‐response genes, such as GCLC, GCLM, and SRXN1, was significantly upregulated, critical antioxidant enzymes including GPX4 and SOD1, as well as the metabolic regulator PFKP, were notably downregulated in the combination group (Figure 2D). To validate these transcriptomic findings, we selected five representative genes (GCLC, SRXN1, GPX4, SOD1, and PFKP) for quantitative PCR analysis. Consistent with the RNA‐seq data, the drug combination significantly upregulated GCLC and SRXN1 (Figure 3A) while downregulating GPX4, SOD1, and PFKP (Figure 3B,C), compared to either single agent. These results confirm a dichotomous transcriptional response within ROS‐related pathways.

We next investigated whether this unique transcriptional signature translated into functional redox imbalance. Flow cytometric analysis revealed a sharp surge in intracellular ROS levels following the combination treatment (Figure S6A,B). Importantly, this effect was fully abrogated by the ROS scavenger NAC, confirming the specificity of the oxidative stress (Figure 3D,E; Figure S6C,D). The observed phenotype suggests that the selective downregulation of primary ROS scavengers like GPX4 and SOD1 likely compromised cellular antioxidant capacity, directly promoting ROS accumulation. Additionally, the suppression of PFKP implies a potential reduction in glycolytic flux, which could further exacerbate oxidative stress by limiting NADPH production. Thus, the data suggest a scenario where a global compensatory upregulation of antioxidant programs is ultimately overwhelmed by the targeted loss of key defense components.

Biochemical assays further corroborated this state of redox collapse. The combination treatment caused a significant reduction in the GSH/GSSG ratio (Figure 3F) and a concomitant increase in lipid peroxidation, as evidenced by elevated MDA levels (Figure 3G). These markers indicate severe oxidative damage and a critical failure of antioxidant defenses. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that palbociclib‐curcumin co‐treatment induces a lethal disruption of redox homeostasis. Despite mounting a transcriptional stress response, cancer cells fail to neutralize the oxidative burden due to the specific suppression of essential detoxifying enzymes, resulting in irreparable cellular damage.

Palbociclib and Curcumin Synergistically Induce ROS‐Dependent DNA Damage and Cell Death

In addition to inducing lipid peroxidation, excessive ROS can inflict direct damage on genomic DNA. To determine whether the combination treatment triggers such genotoxicity, we first employed the alkaline comet assay. As shown in Figure 4A, Figure S7A, cells treated with the palbociclib‐curcumin combination exhibited significantly elongated comet tails compared to those in the monotherapy or control groups. Quantitative analysis of the tail DNA% confirmed a dramatic increase in DNA strand breaks induced by the dual treatment (Figure 4B, Figure S7B). These results indicated that the drug synergy potently triggered substantial DNA damage.

To further characterize the nature of this DNA damage and the associated cellular response, we examined the formation of γH2AX foci, a recognized hallmark of DNA double‐strand breaks (DSBs) [33]. Immunofluorescence staining revealed a pronounced accumulation of distinct nuclear γH2AX foci in combination‐treated cells (Figure S7C–E), a finding consistent with the induction of DSBs. Consistently, Western blot analysis corroborated these findings, showing a marked upregulation of γH2AX protein levels following the combination treatment (Figure S7F–H). Crucially, pre‐treatment with NAC substantially reduced both γH2AX foci formation and protein levels, confirming that the observed genotoxicity is predominantly ROS‐dependent (Figure 4C–F, Figure S7I,J).

Parallel flow cytometric apoptosis assays (Annexin V/PI) demonstrated a synergistic increase in the apoptotic cell fraction following the combination treatment. This lethal effect was largely rescued by NAC, thereby establishing a causal link between ROS accumulation, subsequent DNA damage, and the execution of the cell death program (Figure 4G,H, Figure S7K). Collectively, these results delineated a coherent mechanistic cascade wherein the combination of palbociclib and curcumin impairs antioxidant defenses and triggers excessive ROS accumulation. This oxidative stress promotes lipid peroxidation and causes irreparable ROS‐mediated DNA damage, evidenced by both comet and γH2AX assays, which ultimately culminate in apoptotic cell death.

Combination of Palbociclib and Curcumin Suppresses EMT and Inhibits Metastatic Phenotypes in Breast Cancer Cells

Transcriptome profiling indicated that the concurrent treatment with palbociclib and curcumin elicited a pronounced suppression of the EMT gene program, an effect not significantly observed with either agent alone. To validate these transcriptomic findings, we performed targeted molecular and functional assays. Quantitative PCR confirmed significant downregulation of canonical EMT‐associated transcripts, including CDH2, VIM, and SNAI2, following the combination treatment (Figure 5A–C). Concordantly, Western blot analysis demonstrated a marked reduction in mesenchymal markers and EMT regulators in the combination group relative to monotherapies (Figure 5D,E, Figure S8A). Specifically, we observed substantial decreases in Snail, Vimentin, and Slug in MCF‐7 cells; N‐cadherin, Vimentin, and Slug in MDA‐MB‐231 cells; and N‐cadherin, Vimentin, and Snail in MDA‐MB‐468 cells. Notably, among these, the EMT‐driving transcription factors Snail and Slug displayed the most consistent and pronounced reduction across all tested models.

We next assessed functional consequences of this molecular suppression on cell motility and invasiveness. In wound‐healing assays, cells exposed to the combination regimen displayed significantly impaired directional migration, evidenced by a slower closure rate compared to vehicle or single‐agent treatments (Figure 5F,G; Figure S8B,C). Similarly, transwell invasion assays showed a robust decrease in invasive cell numbers after the combined treatment, consistent with reduced metastatic potential (Figure 5H,I; Figure S8D,E).

Although previous studies suggest that ROS typically promote EMT and metastasis [34, 35], we sought to clarify the relationship between redox changes and EMT regulation in our specific context. Given that the combination treatment severely disrupted redox homeostasis and led to pronounced ROS accumulation, we investigated whether this oxidative stress was causative or inhibitory. Intriguingly, pre‐treatment with the antioxidant NAC effectively rescued the invasive phenotype in transwell assays and restored both the mRNA and protein expression of Slug, a key EMT regulator. These data indicate that ROS accumulation suppresses EMT, at least in part by downregulating Slug at the mRNA level (Figure 5J–M; Figure S8F,G). These findings suggest that ROS accumulation contributes to the downregulation of Slug and the suppression of invasive phenotypes, although the contribution of treatmentinduced cytotoxicity cannot be fully excluded.

Collectively, our results demonstrate that the combination of palbociclib and curcumin suppresses EMT‐associated molecular features and reduces migratory and invasive phenotypes in vitro. Diverging from the canonical paradigm where mild oxidative stress promotes invasion, we establish that the induction of severe oxidative stress functions as a critical suppressor of core EMT regulators, such as Slug. This ROS‐dependent downregulation suppresses the mesenchymal phenotype and impedes tumor cell dissemination.

Co‐Administration of Palbociclib and Cur@Lipo Triggers DNA Damage and Suppresses Metastasis In Vivo

To evaluate the therapeutic efficacy of the combination in a physiological context, we utilized a 4 T1 syngeneic murine model of lung metastasis. 4 T1 cells were injected via the tail vein, and 1 week later, mice were randomized into vehicle, palbociclib, cur@Lipo, and combination groups. The treatment regimen consisted of palbociclib administered at 2.5 mg/kg by oral gavage every 48 h and curcumin liposome (cur@Lipo, Figure S9A–C) at 1 mg/kg by intravenous injection every 48 h, while the combination group received both agents at the same doses and schedule (Figure 6A).

Body weights were monitored throughout the study to evaluate in vivo tolerability. Vehicle‐treated mice exhibited a progressive decline in body weight by day 15, likely attributable to the increasing metastatic burden. While monotherapies caused a mild weight reduction, the combination group maintained relatively stable body weights without significant difference compared to the single‐agent treatments, indicating no obvious additional body weight loss under the tested conditions (Figure 6B).

After 21 days of treatment, macroscopic examination demonstrated that lungs from the vehicle group were markedly enlarged with a heavy burden of metastatic nodules. Although both monotherapies effectively attenuated pathological lung enlargement and nodule formation, the combination treatment led to a near‐complete eradication of visible metastases, restoring lung size to a grossly normal appearance (Figure 6C). Histopathological assessment via H&E staining corroborated these findings: vehicle‐treated lungs exhibited extensive metastatic foci that severely disrupted normal alveolar architecture, whereas combination‐treated lungs showed minimal residual tumor burden, closely resembling healthy, non‐tumor‐bearing tissue (Figure 6D).

Consistent with our in vitro data, immunohistochemical analyses provided further mechanistic insights. The combination treatment significantly suppressed the expression of the proliferation marker Ki‐67 while simultaneously upregulating the DNA damage marker γH2AX and the apoptosis marker cleaved caspase‐3. Furthermore, the expression of Slug was substantially downregulated in the combination group compared to the monotherapy groups (Figure 6E,F).

This enhanced anti‐metastatic efficacy was achieved without exacerbating systemic toxicity. Serum ALT levels in the vehicle group were elevated well beyond the normal range for healthy BALB/c mice (15.1–53.4 U/L) [36], likely reflecting systemic inflammation or secondary hepatic stress caused by extensive metastasis. Following drug treatments, ALT levels were significantly reduced. Notably, the combination group restored ALT values to the normal range, showing no statistically significant difference compared to the cur@Lipo monotherapy, which indicates an absence of added hepatotoxicity (Figure 6G). This functional safety was further confirmed by liver H&E staining, which revealed no evident necrosis, inflammatory infiltration, or macroscopic structural abnormalities in combination‐treated mice (Figure 6H). Collectively, these in vivo data confirm that the concurrent administration of palbociclib and cur@Lipo effectively reduces pulmonary metastatic burden by halting proliferation, triggering DNA damage, and reversing EMT‐driven invasive phenotypes, all while maintaining a highly favorable safety profile.

Discussion

In this study, we demonstrate that combining CDK4/6 inhibitors with curcumin exerts potent synergistic anti‐tumor effects in both HR+/HER2− and TNBC models, primarily driven by the induction of lethal oxidative stress. While elevated reactive oxygen species (ROS) can promote tumorigenesis by activating pro‐survival pathways, they also act as a double‐edged sword [37]; excessive ROS accumulation triggers oxidative cell death, a principle exploited by several anticancer therapies [38, 39]. Our RNA‐seq and functional analyses revealed a paradoxical redox imbalance: the combination broadly upregulates antioxidant demand while critically suppressing essential ROS‐detoxifying enzymes like GPX4 and SOD1. In the TNBC models examined, the combination increased ROS, lipid peroxidation, DNA damage, and apoptosis. Given the reported dependence of some TNBC cells on antioxidant systems [40, 41], these findings suggest that redox vulnerability may contribute to the observed response, although the generality of this mechanism across TNBC requires evaluation in a broader panel of models.

Beyond cytotoxicity, this severe oxidative stress also disrupts the EMT program. The combination therapy significantly downregulated key EMT regulators, including Snail, N‐cadherin, vimentin, and notably, Slug. Since Slug is a major driver of invasion and metastasis [42], its suppression likely contributes to the antimetastatic activity observed in our models. Pre‐treating cells with the antioxidant NAC rescued both the cell viability and the expression of EMT markers, supporting the conclusion that EMT suppression is, at least in part, a consequence of oxidative stress. While moderate ROS is often considered a trigger for EMT, our data suggest that an extreme oxidative burden may create an intracellular environment incompatible with the maintenance of a mesenchymal state (Figure 7).

Mechanism of combined CDK4/6 inhibitors and curcumin against HR+/HER2− and TNBC. Co‐treatment disrupts cellular redox homeostasis via GPX4 suppression and GSH depletion. This leads to severe ROS accumulation, which kills tumor cells by inducing DNA damage and lipid peroxidation. Parallel to this cytotoxicity, oxidative stress also suppresses EMT‐associated migration. Phenotypic rescue by NAC validation highlights that these antitumor and anti‐metastatic effects are fundamentally ROS‐dependent. These findings provide a biological rationale for targeting redox vulnerabilities to overcome resistance in breast cancer therapies.

It is also important to view these findings in the context of the different clinically approved CDK4/6 inhibitors. These agents differ in target selectivity, toxicity‐driven dosing schedules, and central nervous system penetration [43, 44]. We mainly used palbociclib for our mechanistic assays since it was a direct hit in our initial screening, and we found comparable synergy with ribociclib. Both agents inhibit the RB pathway and share similar, intermittent dosing profiles due to neutropenia [45, 46]. Our results therefore indicate that the synergy is reproducible across at least two CDK4/6 inhibitors; however, whether it extends to the broader drug class remains an open question. In particular, abemaciclib was not evaluated in this study, and its higher CDK4 selectivity, concurrent CDK9 inhibition [43, 47], and continuous dosing regimen could alter its effects on oxidative stress dynamics, warranting dedicated investigation before any class‐wide conclusion can be drawn.

The potential clinical implications of these findings should be considered hypothesis‐generating. For HR+/HER2− breast cancer, our transcriptomic data indicate that curcumin attenuates adaptive survival responses to CDK4/6 inhibitor monotherapy, including the mTORC1 activation and lipid biosynthetic programs observed in our sequencing data. Whether this effect translates into a delayed emergence of resistance remains to be determined. Moreover, because ROS‐induced cell death may not strictly depend on an intact RB pathway, it is tempting to speculate that this combination could retain activity in tumors with attenuated CDK4/6‐RB signaling. However, the present study did not directly evaluate bona fide CDK4/6 inhibitor‐resistant models or RB1‐deficient models, and our data therefore do not demonstrate that the combination can overcome established CDK4/6 inhibitor resistance. This possibility represents a testable hypothesis that requires validation in appropriate resistant models before any such inference can be made.

Similarly, although the combination showed robust activity in TNBC cell lines and markedly reduced lung metastatic burden in the 4 T1 model, these findings should be interpreted with caution. CDK4/6 inhibitors are not currently established therapies for TNBC, and the tail‐vein 4 T1 model primarily evaluates pulmonary colonization rather than the entire metastatic cascade. Accordingly, our results provide a rationale for further preclinical investigation of this combination in TNBC, preferably using models that recapitulate the full metastatic process.

There are a few limitations to our study. First, we relied primarily on NAC to rescue the redox balance. While effective, future studies using targeted genetic tools such as GPX4 or SOD1 overexpression will be needed to definitively prove the causal role of these specific enzymes. Second, our anti‐metastatic data comes largely from the 4 T1 model; these results should be validated in a broader range of orthotopic or PDX systems that capture the complete metastatic cascade. Third, only two of the three approved CDK4/6 inhibitors were evaluated, and the absence of bona fide resistant and RB1‐deficient models limits the generalizability of our conclusions. Finally, overcoming curcumin's poor oral bioavailability through advanced nanoparticle delivery systems remains an essential prerequisite for clinical translation. Overall, we identified a highly synergistic combination that targets HER2− breast cancer by inducing lethal oxidative stress and repressing EMT. This highlights the therapeutic potential of manipulating ROS dynamics and provides a rationale for improving CDK4/6 inhibitor‐based therapies.
