Decoupling the “attract-and-kill” strategy: Independent zoospore attraction and ROS-executed killing synergistically drive disease-suppressive intercropping
Authors: Yixiang Liu, Jiaqing Wu, Yuxin Yang, Yiyi He, Rong Zhou, Ying Li, Junwei Sun, Mingchun Gong, Xinyue Mei, Yingbin Li, Huichuan Huang, Fei Du, Weiping Deng, Chen Ye, Xiahong He, Long Li, Jianjun Hao, Min Yang, Youyong Zhu, Shusheng Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Soilborne Phytophthora diseases pose a major threat to agricultural sustainability. However, how nonhost roots disrupt the transmission of soilborne Phytophthora pathogens without relying solely on classical antimicrobial exudates remains poorly understood. Through a decade-long field study, we demonstrate that strip intercropping can sustainably suppress disease incidence by up to 46.85% by leveraging nonhost roots as ecological barriers that intercept zoospore transmission. Moving beyond the conventional focus on antimicrobial exudates, we resolve the “attract-and-kill” strategy into two discrete functions: a broad-spectrum attraction function widespread among nonhost plants (13 of 15 genera), which alone reduces disease incidence by 9.2%–24.4%, and a specialized killing function restricted to a few species, such as garlic, in which elevated concentrations of sulfur compounds at the root interface induce cystospore rupture and inhibit germination, delivering 42.9%–49.3% field suppression. The synergy between universal attraction and targeted killing enhances disease suppression at the rhizosphere interface. Mechanistically, killing is executed through a conserved reactive oxygen species–programmed cell death (ROS–PCD) pathway, with pathogen sensitivity determined by intrinsic redox-buffering capacity. Metagenomic profiling further revealed that garlic roots and sulfur compounds are associated with the enrichment of genes involved in microbial motility and apoptosis-related pathways, adding a complementary mechanistic layer to the attract-and-kill framework. We thus propose this ecology-based, two-component strategy for sustainable Phytophthora management in diversified cropping systems.

Introduction

Soilborne diseases caused by a wide range of pathogenic fungi and oomycetes represent persistent, devastating threats to global agricultural productivity (Moura et al., 2022). Among these, diseases caused by Phytophthora species are particularly destructive. Their rapid spread through waterborne zoospores and broad host range can cause significant economic losses, fostering an unsustainable reliance on chemical pesticides (Sims et al., 2019; Jacquet et al., 2022). While conventional approaches, such as deploying resistant cultivars, applying fungicides, and implementing cultural practices, can provide partial control, they frequently fall short of ensuring long-term agricultural sustainability (Jacquet et al., 2022).

Enhancing biodiversity within agroecosystems has emerged as a promising strategy for mitigating disease outbreaks and stabilizing crop production (Zhu et al., 2000; Renard et al., 2019). Intercropping, a core practice in diversified cropping systems, is widely used in both tropical and temperate regions due to its benefits for nutrient acquisition, improved plant health, and optimized land-use efficiency (Zhu and Morel et al., 2019). Critically, intercropping systems can effectively suppress disease incidence through multiple, often synergistic mechanisms. These include dilution of the pathogen population through increased crop diversity, creation of physical barriers that hinder pathogen spread, induction of host resistance, and modification of the field microclimate to make it less conducive to pathogens (Boudreau, 2013; Novak and Cremer., 2015; Costanzo and Bàrberi, 2016).

Substantial evidence now supports the efficacy of intercropping in combating soilborne diseases, thereby addressing key challenges to agricultural sustainability (Zhang et al., 2013, 2015; Dong et al., 2014; Gao et al., 2014; Yang et al., 2014; Pretty et al., 2018). Root-mediated rhizochemical interactions are increasingly recognized as pivotal drivers of this suppression. A notable mechanism involves root exudates from nonhost plants directly disrupting pathogen life cycles. Recent research has documented an “attract-and-kill” phenomenon in specific systems. For instance, root exudates from plants such as fennel and maize have been shown to simultaneously attract Phytophthora zoospores and significantly impair their viability or induce mortality (Zhang et al., 2020; Yang et al., 2022). However, an intriguing paradox arises: many empirically successful disease-suppressive intercropping systems rely on nonhost plants that lack known potent antimicrobial properties (Medhasi et al., 2025). This observation strongly suggests that chemotactic attraction of zoospores itself may be a widespread and effective defense mechanism capable of interfering with pathogen motility and infection, even in the absence of direct killing activity. This perspective leads directly to two pivotal and interconnected scientific questions: (i) to what extent does zoospore attraction alone contribute to disease control within the complex matrix of field environments? and (ii) across diverse nonhost plants, what is the fundamental relationship between the attraction and killing functions observed in the attract-and-kill paradigm, and what underlying mechanisms govern this relationship?

To address these critical questions, our study systematically dissects the attract-and-kill strategy through three interconnected objectives: (i) to quantify the individual contributions of zoospore attraction and killing to the prevention of pathogen transmission across crop rows in an intercropping context; (ii) to experimentally decouple the attraction and killing functions mediated by root exudates and assess their synergistic effects on disease suppression using the highly effective garlic–Phytophthora capsici system as a model; and (iii) to mechanistically elucidate the biochemical pathway responsible for pathogen killing by the identified compounds, with a specific focus on the role of sulfur-induced reactive oxygen species (ROS) and programmed cell death (PCD) pathways.

Results

Long-term sustainable management of soilborne diseases through intercropping

We examined disease control and sustainability within diversified cropping systems using a field survey, which revealed that intercropping maize with pepper consistently reduced the occurrence of P. capsici infection in pepper plants from 2011 to 2020, with an average reduction of 3.62% in disease incidence (Figures 1A and 1B). On average, the year-to-year temporal stability of the proportion of healthy plants among intercropped peppers was significantly greater than that among monocultured peppers (Figure 1C). Compared with monoculture, maize–pepper intercropping, whether arranged in strips, rings, or patches (Figure 1D), significantly inhibited the spread of Phytophthora blight in pepper, with disease incidence reduced by 18.76%, 32.33%, and 10.73%, respectively (Figure 1E). Among the three patterns, ring intercropping, with the inoculation point at the center, showed the lowest disease incidence (Figure 1E). Patch intercropping may have allowed greater root-contact-mediated disease transmission at the corners, where pepper plants were in close proximity, resulting in less effective control (Figure 1E). Although strip intercropping had higher disease incidence in the inoculated rows, it provided the best control in the indicator rows (Strip-I) due to complete separation by the maize rows, with a 46.85% reduction in disease incidence (Figure 1E). Moreover, the various intercropping systems significantly reduced the peak rate of disease progression (Figure 1F). Specifically, strip intercropping not only lowered the peak rate of disease progression but also significantly reduced the duration of disease occurrence in the indicator rows (Strip-I), which may account for the lowest observed level of disease transmission (Figure 1F). In practical applications, achieving optimal disease control with ring intercropping might be challenging, as disease may originate in the outer rings, leading to more severe outbreaks. In contrast, strip intercropping may help confine disease to the inoculated strip, potentially providing a more reliable and consistent barrier against disease spread.

We also investigated the distribution of P. capsici zoospores across the substrate profile by tracking their movement across the system in the presence of nonhost plants. Our results revealed substantial aggregation of zoospores in the maize root zone (Figure 1G). Compared with the control, maize roots significantly impeded the horizontal movement of zoospores within the substrate, thereby reducing the number of zoospores reaching the distal end (Figure 1G). These findings indicate that nonhost plant root systems can effectively act as biological barriers, limiting the spread of P. capsici zoospores in the substrate.

The attract-and-kill strategy is broadly implemented by nonhost roots to control disease in intercropping systems

We measured the infection-related behaviors of P. capsici and Phytophthora nicotianae in vitro using roots of host and nonhost plants representing 16 genera across 9 families to understand how roots of nonhost plants block the infection process of Phytophthora. The results revealed that, except for hairy vetch (Vicia villosa), all examined plant species attracted P. capsici or P. nicotianae zoospores (Figures 2A and 2B; Supplemental Figure 1). Specifically, nonhost species such as crown daisy chrysanthemum and Arabidopsis, as well as the host species pumpkin, attracted P. capsici zoospores but not P. nicotianae zoospores (Figures 2A and 2B). Almost all plant roots, except those of hairy vetch, significantly promoted zoospore encystment in P. capsici (Figures 2A and 2B). Only a subset of nonhost plants, including maize, garlic, crown daisy chrysanthemum, and sunflower, inhibited cystospore germination (Figures 2A and 2C). Moreover, maize and garlic roots induced zoospore rupture in both P. capsici and P. nicotianae, whereas balsam pear, shallot, and fennel induced rupture in only one species (Figures 2A and 2D). We then evaluated the efficacy of intercropping pepper plants with eight neighboring plant species for managing Phytophthora blight in the field (Figure 2E). The results indicated that nonhost plants, such as maize, crown daisy, and balsam pear, which attract zoospores and also inhibit germination or induce rupture, significantly restricted the dissemination of P. capsici in the field (Figure 2F). Hairy vetch, which had no detectable effect on any examined infection-related process, showed the highest disease incidence when intercropped with pepper (Figure 2F). Compared with pepper monoculture, intercropping pepper with these three plants reduced the incidence of pepper blight by 49.34%, 43.74%, and 42.85%, respectively. A multiple linear regression (MLR) model was established to determine the relationship between disease incidence and pathogen infection-related variables, with the model (disease incidence = 90.112–5.037 × chemotactic ratio (CR) − 0.253 × inhibition of motility + 0.06 × inhibition of germination − 1.48 × rupture ratio) explaining 71.8% of the variation (Supplemental Table 1). The analysis revealed a significant negative correlation between disease incidence and the capacity of nonhost plant roots to attract zoospores and cause zoospore rupture. Garlic and maize emerged as representative nonhost plants that mediate attract-and-kill interactions with both P. capsici and P. nicotianae, demonstrating significant efficacy in controlling the spread of Phytophthora blight. Together, these findings support a broadly applicable attract-and-kill framework in which nonhost roots restrict pathogen transmission by combining zoospore attraction with germination inhibition and/or cystospore rupture (Figures 2A–2D; Supplemental Figure 1; Supplemental Table 3).

Chemical interactions in the rhizosphere underlie the attract-and-kill strategy

Because garlic roots showed the strongest and broadest capacity to interfere with the infection process among the tested nonhost plants, the garlic–P. capsici attract-and-kill interaction model was selected to investigate rhizosphere processes. In greenhouse experiments, we found that intercropping one or two rows of garlic (P1//G1 and P1//G2) with one row of pepper significantly reduced the incidence of pepper Phytophthora blight in the indicator rows by 52.52% compared with monoculture (Figures 3A and 3B). When one garlic plant was removed from the garlic row (P1//G1–), disease incidence in the indicator rows increased significantly by 40.51% compared with that in the P1//G1 treatment (Figures 3C and 3D). Furthermore, even when pepper plants in monoculture were planted with the same row spacing as that used in the intercropping systems, disease incidence in the intercropped indicator rows was significantly lower than that in monoculture (Figures 3E and 3F). Disease incidence in the intercropped indicator rows of P1//G1 and P1//G2 increased significantly when the soil beneath the garlic plants was amended with activated carbon, whereas no such increase was observed in the monoculture system (Figure 3F). Moreover, garlic root exudates consistently and strongly attracted zoospores and significantly interfered with P. capsici infection in a dose-dependent manner (Supplemental Figure 2), similar to the inhibitory effect of garlic roots. To further pinpoint the active compounds, we analyzed garlic root-associated chemical compounds by gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC–MS). Three sulfur compounds, diallyl disulfide (DADS), allyl propyl disulfide (APDS), and dipropyl disulfide (DPDS), were consistently identified in garlic root exudates, root volatiles, and rhizosphere soil by GC–MS (Supplemental Table 2). Their concentrations in the rhizosphere soil were 10.09, 11.52, and 1.35 mg·kg−1, respectively (Supplemental Table 3). Five other sulfur compounds, diallyl sulfide (DAS), methyl propyl disulfide, diallyl trisulfide (DATS), dimethyl trisulfide, and methyl propyl trisulfide, were detected only in the root volatiles (Supplemental Table 2). All eight sulfur compounds, which shared sulfur-containing structures flanked by alkyl or alkenyl groups, were able to disrupt P. capsici infection-related processes (Figure 3G). Structurally, increased numbers of sulfur atoms or alkenyl groups enhanced the antimicrobial activity of the sulfur compounds against P. capsici at all infection stages (Figure 3G). Interestingly, sulfur compounds in which alkenyl groups were replaced by propyl or methyl groups displayed stage-specific antimicrobial activity (Figure 3G). Importantly, when formulated according to their concentrations in the rhizosphere soil, a cocktail of these sulfur compounds showed synergistic antimicrobial activity against the infection processes of P. capsici in a dose-dependent manner (Supplemental Table 5). However, none of the sulfur compounds attracted P. capsici zoospores (Supplemental Table 4). Analysis of DADS distribution revealed a concentration gradient around the root system, with the highest levels occurring near the rhizosphere (Figure 3H).

Root-secreted compounds exert killing activity by inducing ROS accumulation, apoptosis, and autophagy

The attraction of zoospores to substances such as alcohols and peptides in plant root exudates is well documented (Hua et al., 2008). The present study investigates how nonhost plant roots kill Phytophthora pathogens. RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) was performed to profile the gene expression patterns of P. capsici in response to sulfur compounds in garlic root exudates and to elucidate the underlying antimicrobial mechanisms. The Gene Ontology (GO) (Supplemental Figure 3) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) (Supplemental Figure 4) enrichment analyses revealed that the expression of genes involved in antioxidant pathways was significantly altered by sulfur compounds, such as DADS, APDS, DPDS, and DATS. Heatmap analysis revealed that genes in the ROS and PCD pathways, including genes associated with apoptosis and autophagy, were significantly affected by sulfur compounds, especially DATS (Figure 4A; Supplemental Figure 5; Supplemental Tables 7 and 8). Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) confirmed the trends observed in RNA-seq for the selected genes (Supplemental Figure 6). ROS staining with DCFH-DA (2′,7′-Dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate) confirmed the induction of oxidative stress by the sulfur compounds, with intense fluorescence observed in hyphae treated with sulfur compounds, especially those treated with DATS and DADS (Supplemental Figure 7). Staining with Annexin V-FITC/propidium iodide (PI) confirmed apoptosis in zoospores treated for 5 min with DADS and DATS, with subsequent PI staining indicating cell death (Supplemental Figure 8). After 15 min, the number of zoospores stained with PI in the presence of sulfur compounds increased significantly, indicating late-stage apoptosis or cell death in encysted spores (Supplemental Figure 8A). Hoechst 33342/PI double labeling revealed altered membrane permeability in P. capsici zoospores and indicated the occurrence of apoptosis (Supplemental Figure 8B). We further verified that sulfur compounds induce PCD in P. capsici by adding exogenous inhibitors. In mycelial growth assays, the addition of exogenous glutathione (GSH; a reactive oxygen species scavenger), 1,4-bis[2-(3,5-dichloropyridyloxy)]benzene (TCPOBOP; an apoptosis inhibitor), and MRT68921 HCl (an autophagy inhibitor) alleviated the inhibitory effects of DADS and DATS on P. capsici mycelial growth (Figure 4B). Specifically, TCPOBOP and MRT68921 HCl significantly alleviated DADS-mediated inhibition of hyphal growth, whereas GSH had a more pronounced effect on alleviating DATS-mediated inhibition (Figure 4B). After pretreatment with reduced GSH, the inhibitory effects of sulfur compounds, including DADS, APDS, DPDS, and DATS, on P. capsici cystospore germination were significantly weakened (Figure 4C). Our metagenomic profiling of garlic rhizosphere microbiomes also indicated that garlic planting significantly suppressed amino acid metabolism pathways but enhanced cell motility and other functional pathways compared with control soil (Figure 4D). In addition, the apoptosis–fly pathway was significantly upregulated after DADS treatment (Figure 4E).

Based on these results, we further confirmed the presence of ROS and PCD in P. capsici near the garlic rhizosphere, with garlic roots inducing ROS production and autophagy in hyphae (Figure 5A). Consistently, apoptotic death in P. capsici zoospores near the garlic rhizosphere was also observed (Figure 5A). We further confirmed that the garlic-derived sulfur compound DATS significantly altered the expression of genes associated with antioxidant defense and detoxification pathways in Phytophthora species (Figure 5B), triggering a ROS burst consistent with its inhibitory effects on mycelial growth (Figure 5C; Supplemental Table 11). Among the tested species, P. capsici, P. nicotianae, and Phytophthora cactorum showed high sensitivity to DATS (Supplemental Table 11), with a marked increase in ROS fluorescence observed in their mycelia and significant enrichment of genes related to oxidative stress response pathways (Figures 5B and 5C). In contrast, Phytophthora melonis exhibited low sensitivity to DATS, with a high half-maximal effective concentration (EC50) of 99.53 μg ml−1 (Supplemental Table 11). Discussion

Soilborne pathogen accumulation poses a critical threat to stable global crop production, a challenge exacerbated by intensive monoculture systems (Zhu et al., 2000; Longwe et al., 2023). Intercropping, as a nature-based solution, enhances biodiversity and suppresses soilborne diseases (Lai et al., 2011). Critically, our decade-long field study demonstrates that pepper–maize intercropping not only consistently reduces Phytophthora incidence but also enhances the temporal stability of plant health. This finding extends the established principle that plant diversity stabilizes crop yield (Li et al., 2021) to a more direct measure of crop health. It highlights that the functional benefits of biodiversity in agricultural systems operate by maintaining the underlying health of crops, which is essential for sustainable productivity. To decipher the mechanism behind this sustained suppression, we moved beyond the traditional focus on species richness and dilution effects (Zhu et al., 2000). Our comparison of intercropping configurations reveals that spatial design is a decisive yet often overlooked factor in reliable disease suppression. While biodiversity provides a foundational backdrop, the efficacy of intercropping hinges on the formation of a continuous root-zone barrier, as exemplified by strip intercropping. In contrast, patchy or ring layouts introduce spatial discontinuities or configuration-dependent vulnerabilities that compromise this barrier function (Boudreau, 2013). Our in vitro spatial profiling of zoospore distribution provided direct visualization showing that nonhost roots function not as passive physical blocks but as active biological interceptors, aggregating zoospores. This finding identifies the pivotal role of nonhost root systems in blocking cross-row transmission and infection by Phytophthora. Therefore, maximizing barrier continuity is key to resilient disease suppression. This visual evidence establishes the nonhost root zone as a critical active interface for pathogen interception.

Root-mediated rhizochemical interactions, which define the active interception interface of the nonhost root zone, are increasingly recognized as pivotal drivers of disease suppression in diversified cropping systems (Homulle et al., 2022). Extending beyond the attract-and-kill model initially described by our group in specific systems (Fang et al., 2016; Zhang et al., 2020; Yang et al., 2022), this study systematically dissects its prevalence and functional modularity (Figure 6). The attraction of Phytophthora zoospores emerged as a remarkably common trait among nonhost plants, occurring in 13 of 15 genera, and was potentially mediated by root electrochemical gradients (Savory et al., 2014; Zhang et al., 2019). Crucially, this widespread trait functioned as an independent defense mechanism in the field: nonhosts with attraction capacity alone (e.g., fennel) achieved significant disease suppression, with a 38.24% reduction, whereas the absence of attraction (e.g., hairy vetch) led to markedly higher disease incidence, with a 2.1-fold increase. Thus, the prevalence of this independent interception function provides a mechanistic basis for the disease-suppressive effect common to many intercropping patterns. The broad-spectrum nature of this attraction may reflect the generalist foraging strategy of pathogens such as P. capsici (Iribarren et al., 2019). Intercropping systems can exploit this conserved pathogen behavior, turning a fundamental host-finding mechanism against the pathogen itself. Notably, this pathogen chemotaxis can also be directly disrupted by blocking specific chemoreceptors, highlighting a complementary strategy for intervention (Ji et al., 2025). However, the capacity to directly inhibit cystospore germination or induce cystospore rupture, termed the kill function, proved to be a specialized trait restricted to a minority of nonhost plants, such as garlic and maize. The synergistic integration of targeted killing with universal attraction achieves the most robust control, with plants combining both functions, such as garlic, suppressing distal disease transmission by up to 77.27%. Our MLR model, explaining 71.8% of the variation, provides quantitative validation that field efficacy is predominantly governed by two dissociable processes: zoospore attraction and pathogen inactivation. This supports the attract-and-kill strategy not merely as a phenomenon but as a composite strategy, highlighting a shift from passive resistance to active interception and offering a novel ecological pathway for disease control.

The specialized kill function, although detected only in a subset of nonhost plants, is critical for disease suppression, as demonstrated in garlic, for which both intact roots and root exudates proved indispensable in controlled experiments. This root–exudate synergy is consistent with the widespread “cocktail effect” in rhizosphere chemical defense (Yang et al., 2023) and mirrors the cooperative action of phenolic compounds, flavones, and antioxidants in establishing rhizosphere defense networks (Wang et al., 2022; Wu et al., 2025a), indicating that garlic’s strategy exemplifies a broader plant defense principle. Crucially, these sulfur compounds exhibited no attractant activity, providing direct chemical evidence for the functional dissociation between attraction and killing. Spatial assessment revealed that spore rupture and germination inhibition were significantly amplified near the root interface. This finding extends the traditional concept of a rhizosphere chemical shield by illustrating how its efficacy is dynamically shaped by root-facilitated pathogen interception. Beyond direct toxicity, our metagenomic evidence suggests that garlic roots enrich microbial functional traits associated with motility, pointing to a complementary microbiome-mediated suppressive mechanism. This observation is consistent with documented roles of Allium species in shaping beneficial microbial communities (Zhou et al., 2024; Wu et al., 2025b), yet provides new functional insights by linking microbial motility to the interception capacity of the rhizosphere. Thus, the kill function emerges as a sophisticated, multi-layered strategy integrating direct chemical action with a reconfigured soil microbiome, thereby providing an ecological basis for sustainable cropping systems that minimize chemical inputs.

Antimicrobial substances secreted by nonhost plants disrupt the infection process of Phytophthora spp. by inducing ROS accumulation and PCD. Our findings show that ROS accumulation occurs in both spores and hyphae after exposure to garlic roots and sulfur compounds. These sulfur compounds also triggered PCD-associated processes, including apoptosis and autophagy, in P. capsici, thereby playing a pivotal role in disrupting its infection process. Comparable oxidative-stress-induced cell death has been reported for several naturally active compounds, such as coumarin, benzothiazole, and allyl isothiocyanate, further supporting its broad applicability in pathogen suppression (Mei et al., 2019; Li et al., 2020; Song et al., 2021). Moreover, the enrichment of apoptosis-related pathways in the garlic rhizosphere microbiome provides in situ evidence that sulfur compounds exert their antimicrobial effects by promoting PCD-related processes, a mechanism that impacts both the target Phytophthora species and the surrounding microbial community. Critically, this ROS-driven inhibitory mechanism is conserved across Phytophthora species, forming a core axis of sulfur-compound toxicity. Within this framework, species sensitivity to the garlic-derived compound DATS is directly governed by intrinsic redox homeostasis, manifesting as a clear divergence: susceptible species exhibit robust ROS bursts and stress gene induction, whereas tolerant lineages show minimal oxidative responses. Thus, sulfur-triggered ROS escalation serves as the core biochemical mechanism of the kill function, with pathogen-specific sensitivity thresholds directly dictating suppression efficacy. This establishes intrinsic ROS buffering capacity as a key predictor of vulnerability to root-exuded defenses, providing a mechanistic basis for the targeted application of this function in sustainable disease management.

In summary, this study establishes a decoupled, modular attract-and-kill strategy as an ecological mechanism for sustainable Phytophthora control. We demonstrate that disease suppression arises from two discrete root-mediated functions: a broadly conserved attraction function that intercepts zoospores and a specialized killing function mediated by a conserved ROS–PCD pathway. Field validation in strip intercropping confirmed that the synergy between these two functions enables robust, sustainable suppression. Crucially, the efficacy of the kill function is determined by the pathogen’s intrinsic redox-buffering capacity, providing a trait-based predictor of vulnerability. By shifting from passive resistance to active rhizosphere interception, this work provides a mechanistic framework for designing biodiversity-based agroecosystems that enhance resilience while reducing pesticide reliance.

Methods

Pathogen

P. capsici strain 501 and P. nicotianae strain YXCJ were provided by the Key Laboratory of Agro-biodiversity and Pest Management, Yunnan Agricultural University. P. capsici was grown on V8 medium at 25°C in an incubator under a 12-h light–dark cycle for 7 days to induce sporangial production. Sterile water was added to the culture plate, which was then chilled at 4°C for 30 min. Afterward, the plates were incubated at room temperature for another 30 min to facilitate zoospore release. A zoospore suspension (106 spores ml−1) was prepared after the zoospores were filtered through sterile gauze and counted using a hemocytometer.

Field trials

From 2010 to 2021, a field study was conducted at Wenshan Experimental Station (24°28′N, 104°45′E), Yunnan, China, to assess the effectiveness of pepper‒maize intercropping in suppressing Phytophthora blight in pepper. Ten fields were intensively sampled, with five random sites per field and 50 plants per site evaluated for P. capsici infection based on disease symptoms. Temporal stability was assessed by calculating the reciprocal of the coefficient of variation using 10 years of disease survey data (Li et al., 2021). We implemented three distinct planting arrangements, namely strip, patch, and ring intercropping patterns, with pepper monoculture serving as the control, to investigate how varying maize–pepper intercropping patterns influence the spread of pepper diseases (Figures 1D–1G). In the strip and patch intercropping systems, the within-row plant spacing and between-row spacing for pepper were both set at 25 cm, whereas maize was planted with a within-row plant spacing of 25 cm and a between-row spacing of 37.5 cm. The row spacing between pepper and maize strips was maintained at 25 cm to ensure a consistent inter-row distance across systems. In the ring intercropping pattern, the planting density of maize strips was adjusted to match that of the strip and patch intercropping systems. Similarly, the planting density of pepper strips was kept identical to that in the other two intercropping patterns to ensure comparability in plant populations across treatments. The incidence of Phytophthora blight in pepper was investigated from 7 days post-inoculation until all pepper plants had died in the inoculated rows. Field disease incidence was analyzed using a logistic model, and the instantaneous disease growth rate was calculated using the method described previously (Wei et al., 2020).

Distribution of Phytophthora zoospores in plant root systems

A spore distribution apparatus measuring 20 × 16 × 3 cm was established to investigate the distribution of zoospores in the root systems of neighboring plants. Zoospore collection devices were positioned at grid intervals of 2.5 × 2.5 cm within this apparatus, with quartz sand of 20–22 mesh used as the medium. Two treatments were used to simulate pepper monoculture and pepper–maize intercropping. A maize plant was placed at the center of each intercropped group, whereas no plant was placed in the corresponding position in the control group. At a horizontal position of 2 cm from the designated zoospore release site, P. capsici zoospore suspension (106 spores ml−1) was slowly released. After 20 min, spore concentrations at different positions and depths were recorded. Zoospore counts were determined using a cell counter after the samples were stained with a 1:1 mixture of trypan blue and spore suspension. Surfer software (v15.0, Golden Software, Golden, CO, USA) was used to generate a spatial distribution map of spore concentration.

Assessing the broad applicability of the attract-and-kill strategy across host and nonhost interactions with Phytophthora zoospores

Fifteen crops from nine families, namely Poaceae, Brassicaceae, Asteraceae, Fabaceae, Cucurbitaceae, Apiaceae, Amaranthaceae, Solanaceae, and Amaryllidaceae, were assayed for their interactions with two wide-host-range pathogens, P. capsici and P. nicotianae, to verify the broad applicability of the attract-and-kill strategy. A special apparatus was used to monitor the interaction between Phytophthora spores and the roots of host and nonhost plants (Yang et al., 2014). Briefly, a square chamber was constructed by fixing capillary tubes onto a glass slide and sealing the chamber with a coverslip. The root-cap region of host and nonhost roots was immersed in a suspension of sporangia, zoospores, or cystospores (106 spores ml⁻¹) within the chamber. The behavior of zoospores in the rhizosphere was recorded every 5 min for 30 min using a Leica DM2000 microscope (Wetzlar, Germany). Additionally, cystospore germination in the rhizosphere was observed at 1 h intervals for up to 6 h using the same setup. A capillary tube was used as the control in place of a root. The numbers of zoospores, cystospores, germinated cystospores, and ruptured cystospores in the rhizosphere were counted. The cystospore germination inhibition rate and cystospore rupture rate were subsequently calculated as indicators of the kill effect. The CR and zoospore motility inhibition rate were used to evaluate the attraction of plant roots to zoospores. The CR was calculated as the number of zoospores and cystospores on the test root divided by the number of zoospores and cystospores in the control (Halsall, 1976), where CR > 1 indicates positive chemotaxis. Each assay was conducted three times. Then, eight selected nonhost and host plants that exhibited varying levels of Phytophthora infection inhibition, including garlic, maize, balsam pear, crown daisy chrysanthemum, fennel, hairy vetch, pumpkin, and tomato, were intercropped with pepper in the field (Figure 2E) using identical planting specifications to evaluate their effectiveness in controlling Phytophthora blight in pepper.

Greenhouse assays

Three independent greenhouse experiments were conducted to determine the effects of intercropping pepper (cultivar “8891”) with garlic (cultivar “Bai Qi Xing”) on the suppression of Phytophthora blight in pepper. In the first experiment, the treatments included pepper monoculture and pepper//garlic intercropping (P1//G1 and P1//G2) (Figure 3A). The second experiment included the P1//G1– treatment, which mirrored P1//G1 but involved removing one garlic plant from each row to determine the contribution of garlic to suppressing Phytophthora disease (Figure 3C). In the third experiment, uniform row spacing was maintained for all pepper plants to eliminate the influence of physical distance, and activated carbon was applied to explore the role of garlic root exudates in disease suppression (Figure 3E). The activated carbon was mixed with the soil at a 3:100 (w/w) ratio and applied to the soil under the garlic plants in the intercropped plots or between the pepper rows in the monoculture plots 24 h before P. capsici inoculation (Rivera-Utrilla et al., 2001). Each treatment was replicated three times. Approximately 10 days after garlic transplantation, corresponding to 2 weeks after pepper planting, P. capsici zoospore suspension (1.5 ml of 106 spores ml⁻¹) was injected at the base of the stem of a central pepper plant. Disease incidence was recorded from 3 days post-inoculation until all inoculated plants died.

Antimicrobial activity of garlic root exudates against P. capsici

Garlic root exudates were collected using a hydroponic method and extracted with ethyl acetate. The concentrated extract was dissolved in methanol to prepare stock solutions, which were then diluted for subsequent assays. Chemotaxis of P. capsici zoospores toward garlic root exudates was assessed using a capillary-based method. The antimicrobial effects of the exudates on sporangial release, zoospore motility, and cystospore germination were evaluated by mixing diluted exudates with P. capsici sporangia, zoospores, or cystospores on glass slides. Distilled water with methanol served as the control. All treatments were performed in triplicate, and quantification was performed as described by Yang et al. (2014). Detailed procedures are provided in the supplemental information.

Identification of sulfur compounds in garlic root exudates, root volatiles, and rhizosphere soil

Root exudates, root volatiles, and soil volatiles were collected from garlic (Allium sativum) plants and analyzed using GC–MS (Shimadzu QP2010, Kyoto, Japan) (Ye et al., 2021). Volatile compounds were collected from fresh roots and rhizosphere soil using dynamic headspace analysis, and the collected volatiles were eluted and concentrated before GC–MS analysis (Pino, 1992). Compound identification was performed using the NIST14 mass spectral library. Headspace solid-phase microextraction was employed to quantify volatile compounds in rhizosphere soil (Yang et al., 2022). Relative and final concentrations were calculated using standard curves and normalization formulas based on collection time and sample mass. Additional details of the experimental procedures, including sample preparation, GC–MS settings, and standard curve construction, are provided in the supplemental information.

Antimicrobial activity of sulfur compounds against P. capsici

The antimicrobial activity of eight sulfur compounds detected in the garlic rhizosphere against P. capsici infection-related processes—including chemotaxis, hyphal growth, zoospore release from sporangia, zoospore motility, and cystospore germination or rupture—was determined at different concentrations (0, 10, 50, 100, 500, and 1000 μg ml−1). The method used to assess the antimicrobial activity of these sulfur compounds was the same as that described above for garlic root exudates. In addition, the effects of two cocktails, cocktail A containing three main compounds and cocktail B containing all eight compounds, were further tested at concentrations corresponding to 0.5-, 1-, 5-, and 10-fold their actual concentrations in the rhizosphere. These concentrations were calculated based on the final concentration in soil (Cfs) of DADS and the relative proportions of all sulfur compounds. The proportions of the three common sulfur compounds, namely DADS, APDS, and DPDS, in the cocktails were based on their final concentrations in the garlic rhizosphere soil (Cfs), whereas the other five sulfur compounds were included according to their average proportions in root volatiles (Supplemental Table 2). Each treatment had three replicates.

Six mycelial plugs, each 7 mm in diameter, were placed in 60 ml of carrot juice medium (CJM) for shake-flask culture at 140 rpm and 25°C for 24 h. Afterward, different concentrations of sulfur compounds (0, 10, 50, 100, 500, and 1000 μg ml⁻¹) were added to the CJM. After an additional 12-h incubation, the liquid medium was removed by filtration. The mycelia were then wrapped in filter paper, dried, and weighed to calculate the mycelial growth inhibition rate. Each treatment had three replicates.

Transcriptome analysis of P. capsici after exposure to sulfur compounds

P. capsici mycelia were cultured in CJM and treated with sulfur compounds at 100 μg ml−1 or with a methanol-containing control solution. After treatment, the mycelia were harvested, washed, and stored at −80°C for total RNA extraction (Mei et al., 2019). RNA was isolated and reverse-transcribed into cDNA before RNA-seq library preparation and sequencing (Mei et al., 2019). Differentially expressed genes were identified by comparing transcript levels between the treatment and control samples, using a fold-change threshold of ≥ 2 and P ≤ 0.01. Gene expression levels were normalized using the RPKM (reads per kilobase per million mapped reads) method (Kraushaar et al., 2013). To assess the reliability of the RNA-seq data, the relative fold changes of 12 selected genes were validated by real-time qPCR and compared with the RNA-seq results. Detailed experimental procedures are described in the supplemental information.

Effects of exogenous inhibitors on mycelial growth and cystospore viability of P. capsici under sulfur compound stress

Previous reports have suggested that GSH has antioxidant properties and can scavenge intracellular ROS. TCPOBOP is an apoptosis inhibitor that can influence apoptotic pathways, thereby preventing PCD (Baskin-Bey et al., 2007). MRT68921 HCl is an autophagy inhibitor that effectively inhibits the autophagy-related protein kinases ULK1 and ULK2 (Petherick et al., 2015). Here, we added GSH, TCPOBOP, and MRT68921 HCl to CJM, followed by the addition of 50 μg ml−1 DADS or DATS. Mycelial growth inhibition was assessed as described above for sulfur compound treatments, with three independent biological replicates for each treatment.

Zoospore suspensions (106 spores ml−1) were treated with 1.5 mM GSH or sterile water for 20 min (Kumar et al., 2014). Each sample was centrifuged at 4,000 × g for 2 min (Eppendorf, Germany), washed with sterile water to remove residual GSH, and used to generate a cystospore suspension. Different sulfur compounds, namely DADS, APDS, DPDS, DAS, and DATS, at the same concentrations as those used for ROS staining, were mixed with the GSH-treated cystospore suspensions. Similarly, sulfur compounds were mixed with cystospore suspensions treated with sterile water as positive controls. Sterile water was mixed with cystospore suspensions treated with sterile water as negative controls. Each treatment was repeated four times. After 6 h, cystospore germination rates were assessed.

Detection of ROS, autophagy, and apoptosis by fluorescence microscopy

P. capsici mycelia were cultured and treated with various concentrations of sulfur compounds, including DAS, DPDS, DADS, APDS, and DATS. ROS accumulation was assessed using DCFH-DA staining and visualized under a fluorescence microscope. Autophagy was detected using monodansylcadaverine staining. To evaluate plant–pathogen interactions, garlic or pepper roots were co-incubated with hyphae under the same treatment conditions. Apoptosis in zoospores was assessed using annexin V-FITC/PI staining after interaction with garlic roots or short-term exposure to sulfur compounds and was further validated by Hoechst 33342/PI double staining. Additionally, to evaluate the broad applicability of sulfur-compound-induced ROS accumulation, four Phytophthora species, namely P. capsici, P. nicotianae, P. cactorum, and P. melonis, were treated with 30 μg ml⁻¹ DATS, and DCFH-DA staining was used to detect ROS accumulation.

Metagenomic analysis

Total genomic DNA was extracted using the Mag-Bind Soil DNA Kit (Omega Bio-tek, Norcross, GA, USA) following the manufacturer’s protocol. DNA concentration and purity were assessed using a TBS-380 fluorometer and a NanoDrop2000 spectrophotometer, respectively. DNA quality was evaluated by electrophoresis on a 1% agarose gel. Data analysis was conducted on the Majorbio Cloud Platform (https://www.majorbio.com). After adapter trimming using fastp (v0.20.0), paired-end Illumina reads were assembled. Contigs ≥ 300 bp were retained in the final assembly and used for subsequent gene prediction and annotation. Open reading frames (ORFs) within the assembled contigs were predicted using Prodigal/MetaGene (http://metagene.cb.k.u-tokyo.ac.jp/). Predicted ORFs ≥ 100 bp were retained and translated into amino acid sequences according to the NCBI translation table (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Taxonomy/taxonomyhome.html/index.cgi?chapter=tgencodes). A non-redundant gene catalog was constructed with CD-HIT (v4.6.1) using thresholds of 90% sequence identity and 90% coverage. High-quality reads were then aligned to this catalog using SOAPaligner (v2.21) to calculate gene abundance, with ≥ 95% identity required. KEGG annotation was performed using Diamond (v0.8.35) for BLASTP alignment against the KEGG database (http://www.genome.jp/kegg/), applying an e-value cutoff of 10⁻⁵.

Statistical analysis

The experiments were conducted using a completely randomized design. Statistical analyses were performed using SAS software v9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA), and figures were generated using SigmaPlot (Systat Software, San Jose, CA, USA). Differences between treatments were assessed using either one-way ANOVA or Student’s t-tests, as appropriate. The least significant difference test was applied for post hoc multiple comparisons. MLR was used to analyze the relationships between the pathogen infection process and disease incidence. MLR was used to quantify the combined effects of multiple infection-related variables on disease incidence and to control for potential confounding factors. The direction and strength of these relationships were evaluated based on regression coefficients and their statistical significance. The real-time qPCR data are presented as the means ± standard errors of the means. The correlations between the RNA-seq and real-time qPCR results were analyzed using linear regression and Pearson’s correlation analyses in Microsoft Excel 2022 (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA, USA). The significance of the Pearson correlation coefficient was tested using SPSS Statistics 26 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA).
