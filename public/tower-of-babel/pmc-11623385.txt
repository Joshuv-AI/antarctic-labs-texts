The Tailpipe’s Tale: Traffic-Related Air Pollutants and Ovarian Cancer Risk
Authors: Silke Schmidt
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Two young adult women chat while crossing an intersection where vehicles wait in the background.

Associations between inhaled air pollution and the risk of lung cancer are well established.1 Growing evidence supports an association of two air pollutants with the risk of breast cancer: nitrogen dioxide (NO2) and fine particulate matter [PM with aerodynamic diameter of ≤2.5μm (PM2.5)].2–4 Because uterine, ovarian, and other hormone-sensitive cancers are far less common than breast cancer,5 much larger studies are required to evaluate their relationships with air pollution and other environmental risk factors.

Analyzing data for more than 40,000 US women enrolled in the Sister Study6 (https://sisterstudy.niehs.nih.gov/), researchers recently reported in Environmental Health Perspectives7 that each increase of 5 ppb in average annual exposure to NO2—a proxy for traffic-related air pollution8,9—was associated with a 17%–21% increase in risk of ovarian cancer. This result is similar to recent findings10 for uterine cancer. Overall, the other two air pollutants examined in this prospective cohort study—PM2.5 and ozone—were not associated with changes to ovarian cancer risk.

The researchers found associations between NO2 exposure and increased risk for ovarian cancer. For ozone and PM2.5, the results did not show increased risk overall, but they did suggest that the timing of exposures—before or after menopause—might play a role in disease development. The new study is among the first analyses of individual-level air pollution exposures and the incidence of one of the deadliest cancers5 a woman can have. All Sister Study participants have at least one sister diagnosed with breast cancer, so they are at higher risk of breast and ovarian cancer than are women without that family history.6

The researchers based their exposure estimates of annual average pollutant concentrations on all reported addresses for each participant, from the time of enrollment (2003–2009) to June 2017. This method accounted for exposure changes due to participants moving to a new home during the study period. The mean follow-up time was 9.8 years.

“This is a big step forward since not all cohort studies routinely track residential addresses over time or use those data for exposure assessment,” says Alexandra White, the study’s senior author and a cancer epidemiologist at the National Institute of Environmental Health Sciences (NIEHS). “The newer air pollution model we used11 combines both satellite and ground-level measurements to estimate pollutant levels at higher spatiotemporal resolution than previous models.”

Among the 40,308 participants eligible for this analysis (35–74 years of age at enrollment), only 249 women were diagnosed with ovarian cancer during the follow-up period, underscoring the rarity12 of this disease. The researchers adjusted for confounding variables, such as race, ethnicity, educational attainment, and neighborhood socioeconomic status score. The results varied little when including additional risk factors for ovarian cancer, such as smoking status, physical activity, parity (the number of times a woman has given birth), and age at birth of their first child.

A recent analysis of more than 544,000 participants in a different study, the All of Us Research Program (https://allofus.nih.gov/), analyzed approximately 1,500 ovarian cancer cases. The researchers found a 20% increase in ovarian cancer risk for those in the highest quartile of PM2.5 exposure, compared with those in the lowest quartile.4 Exposure estimates were based on the residential ZIP code at enrollment, and the analysis included cases that existed at the time of enrollment, as well as new cases.

In the current study,7 a 5-unit increase in PM2.5 exposure was associated with a 2.9-fold increase in the risk of ovarian cancer before, but not after, menopause. (The increased risk from NO2 exposure did not exhibit such heterogeneity.) Although this association was not statistically significant, the idea that higher PM2.5 exposure in the premenopausal years may translate to higher ovarian cancer risk is intriguing, says Shelley Tworoger, who was not involved in the study. Tworoger is a professor of oncological sciences at Oregon Health and Science University’s Knight Cancer Institute.

“We think of the time before menopause as a period of susceptibility, when changes in ovarian and fallopian tube tissues may increase cancer risk later in life,” she says. Other risk factors, such as parity, tubal ligation, oral contraceptive use, and the number of ovulatory cycles,13 also occur prior to menopause. Tworoger adds that the ability to account for residential mobility over time helped identify the potential heterogeneity in the PM2.5-associated risk with respect to menopause.

Jiajun Luo, an instructor at the University of Chicago who was not involved in the study, agrees that the exposure assessment method is one of its strengths. He also notes that the Sister Study’s recruitment strategy—women with a family history of breast cancer—allows for the analysis of specific genetic data in future studies. “Follow-up studies of great interest [would] include testing whether specific mutations in the BRCA1 [Breast Cancer 1] or BRCA2 genes modify the effects of air pollutants,” says Luo, “as well as comparing results for Sister Study participants with those for women at average genetic risk.”

The air pollutants that participants were exposed to and the incidence of ovarian cancer both varied across different parts of the country, White notes. For example, in an earlier analysis from the Sister Study, regional differences in the chemical composition of PM2.5 helped explain a higher associated risk of breast cancer in the western United States compared with other parts of the country.3 As for NO2, the traffic-related air pollution that it reflects is a variable mixture of diesel exhaust, benzene, polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons, and other chemicals, some of which have known carcinogenic or endocrine-disrupting effects.14,15

Ovarian cancer has several subtypes that are etiologically distinct and may also differ in their respective risk factors.16–18 It is plausible, says Tworoger, that endocrine-disrupting chemicals may be more relevant for hormone-dominated subtypes, whereas PM2.5-related pathways involving inflammation and oxidative stress may play a greater role in subtypes that have also been associated with cigarette smoking.16 “The study provides a strong rationale for future pooled analyses of … cohort studies to tease apart which air pollutant is associated with which subtype of ovarian cancer,” says Tworoger. “For a deadly disease with fewer known environmental risk factors than other cancers, I believe such pooled analyses will ultimately enhance our ability to predict high-risk subgroups of women for whom more intense screening is warranted.”
