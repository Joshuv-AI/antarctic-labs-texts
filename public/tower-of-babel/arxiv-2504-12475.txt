Best practices in Quantum Monte Carlo for metal catalysis: CO hydrolysis on Pt(111) Journal: Advances in Quantum Chemistry
Ali Bağcı a, Philip E. Hoggan b∗

Abstract
Many scientists are now researching hydrogen production since it affords a clean, sustainable alternative to fossil fuel [ 4 ] . Research work and prototyping various aspects of hydrogen power continues to advance. Over 90 % of all chemical manufacture uses a solid catalyst. This work describes catalytic selective hydrogen production (via water-gas shift, hydration of pre-adsorbed CO) optimising reactant structure on a model catalyst. Focus is on partial O-H bond dissociation after carbon-monoxide is co-adsorbed with water at Pt(111). Finally, hydrogen gas is given off. Many chemical reactions involve bond-dissociation. This process is often the key to rate-limiting reaction steps at solid surfaces. Bond-breaking is poorly described by Hartree-Fock and DFT methods, our embedded active site approach is used. We showcase Quantum Monte Carlo (QMC) methodology using the ground-state Slater Determinant of a simple four primitive-cell layer model, oriented to expose Pt (111), to initialise the QMC. This stochastic approach solves the Schrödinger equation. It recently came of age for heterogeneous systems involving solids. During hydrolysis of carbon monoxide, initial O-H bond stretch is rate-limiting. Its dissociation energy is offset by surface Pt-H bond formation. The reactive formate (H-O-C=O) species formed by initial hydrolysis of CO, also interacting with a vicinal Pt. The products are carbon dioxide and hydrogen, with a H-atom dissociated from the formate species and another desorbed from Pt(111). This yields 100% hydrogen gas. This single-determinant work is compared to a high-level configuration interaction (CI) wave-function. Activation barriers are given to 2.2kJ/mol (c.f. 0.7 of the CI benchmark). Active sites are embedded in the metal lattice exposing its close-packed face. These trial wave-functions start the QMC.

Initial water attack of CO at Pt(111) is the rate-limiting step, studied in what follows. Of course, it is not perfectly ’green’ since the catalysts are often based on heavy metals that must be mined and purified. Nevertheless, a catalyst can function without poisoning for an enormous number of cycles (infinite re-use limit). Some can be cleaned and the hydrogen synthesis is a ’grey’ but non-polluting scheme.

Theoretical Method:

Chemical reactions modify electronic correlation energy, at metal catalyst surfaces. Our simple model truncates fcc metals, with a close-packed (111) planar interface. The model compares physisorbed reactants to a transition-state (TS) geometry, located using a Quantum Monte Carlo (QMC) force constant calculation (below). The Transition-State geometry can be optimized using these force constants, as programmed during our earlier work developing the CASINO software

The TS is compact, however, when the water is 7 Å  from the surface (physisorbed), there is a small residual interaction (generally missing from DFT, but accessible to QMC). The TS involves chemisorption that stabilises its product-like form (as a precursor to actual overlap-driven bond formation). A reference active site is defined with a high-level wave-function to start QMC. It caters for reacting molecules and surface atoms describe the metal. This is embedded using periodic potentials. Quantum Monte Carlo proceeds in two stages. First, Variational Monte Carlo (VMC) then, the Diffusion step, Diffusion Monte Carlo (DMC). The final result is almost fully correlated. DMC (and also VMC) scale well with system size (at worst cubically and, in some circumstances linear scaling can be obtained), to efficiently use modern computer architecture that is massively parallel and, above all, produce quantitative, accurate physical properties that are difficult to obtain otherwise. QMC benchmarks of activation barriers for reactions adsorbed on solid catalyst surfaces have such pre-requisites. Some comparisons of gas-phase reactions to their adsorbed counterpart demonstrate a lowering of the barrier, for a given mechanism.

The QMC approach uses statistical physics over a large population, comprising sets of instantaneous particle positions.
They are often called ’walkers’ (c.f. the one-dimensional random-walk).

2.1
Input Slater Determinant: Solid-state k-point grids and twist-averaging.

A DFT Generalised Gradient (PBE) calculation over a modest 2x2 k-point grid in the first Brillouin zone can be unfolded in co-ordinate space as QMC input.
The product of a Jastrow factor with a Slater determinant gives the trial wave-function (in Slater-Jastrow form). Random numbers actually serve to initialise the ’walkers’ from the trial wave-function giving initial electron density/population). They start a Variational Monte Carlo (VMC) stage. VMC permits trial wave-function optimisation.
Its converged output starts the highly accurate Diffusion Monte Carlo (DMC) runs.

Several offset grids (twists) are used for each DMC run, which are then averaged. The poor input is compensated in by relatively long Diffusion Monte Carlo (DMC) runs for 16 twists.

2.2
Variation Monte Carlo:

A preliminary VMC calculation is carried out in order to generate several thousand configurations (instantaneous positions i.e. electron co-ordinates). VMC is driven by minimisation. The so called
local energy
is minimised:

\frac{H\psi}{\psi}

(2)

The kinetic energy terms involved are smoother and have lower variance in exponentially decaying bases, as shown in our work on wave-function quality
[
30
]
. A Jastrow factor including electron pair, electron-nucleus and three-body (two-electron and nucleus) is defined. This Jastrow factor
[
28
,
29
]
is carefully optimised with respect to its polynomial coefficients (up to 5 % of the total time). This factor uses a polynomial expansion in the variables of explicit correlation.

2.2.1
Generic Jastrow factor

A Slater-Jastrow wave-function is defined as the product of
\Psi_{S}

\Psi{\bf(R)}=e^{J(R)}\Psi_{S}{\bf(R)}=e^{J{\bf(R)}}D_{u}{\bf(R_{u})}\,D_{d}{\bf(R_{d})}

(3)

The determinants
D_{u}(R_{u})
and
D_{d}(R_{d})
treat ’up’ and ’down’ spin separately if necessary. This new approach
[

which generates a huge parameter set was tested for our platinum slab model, introduced above and detailed below. The set was converged in its full extent, but can be reduced somewhat by symmetry (grouping atoms). Here, the DMC steps are rather time-consuming.

The generic Jastrow factor used (in Common Algebraic Specification Language (CASL)), had polynomials in e-e, e-n (to order 9) and e-e-n (to order 4), with cut off radius 10 au.

Truncation to a finite range involves a factor:

t(R)=(r-L)^{C}\Theta_{H}(r-L)

(4)

Take C=3, since the wave-function and at least two derivatives are continuous.
\Theta_{H}
is the Heaviside step-function (Wigner Seitz cell diameter L).

J({\bf R})=\sum_{i<j}^{N}u_{P_{ij}}(r_{ij})\,+\,\sum_{i}^{N}\,\sum_{I}^{N_{n}}\chi_{S_{iI}}(r_{iI})\,+\,\sum_{i<j}^{N}\sum_{I}^{N_{n}}f_{T_{ijI}}(r_{iI},r_{jI},r_{ij})

(5)

u,\chi
and

are polynomials (with variational coefficients) in the inter-particle distances. The symbols P, S, T denote the channel indices. The

This 3-body term involves ’double-counting’, some contributions already included in electron-electron and electron-nucleus terms. It is avoided when cutoff functions are switched on

In the present work, DMC runs are short, because the Jastrow factor is fully optimised, and we have obtained convergence for variance followed by energy Variation Monte Carlo (VMC).
High-order expansions in the instantaneous inter-particle distance (9 for 2 particle an 4 for 3 particle) lead to large parameter sets (550 here), since individual atoms are treated. When all unbound Pt-atoms are grouped, and also with 3

Occasional large DMC populations and sub-variational data over portions of DMC runs are due to pseudo-potential non-locality. To remove this defect, non-local grid energy is evaluated. For 3d electrons it is greater than for higher d-shells (Ni is a 3d transition metal, see

2.3
Diffusion Monte Carlo:

During DMC runs, the ground-state component of the trial wave function is projected out by solving the Schrödinger equation (SWE) in imaginary time. DMC is carried out in the fixed-phase approximation, which uses the nodes from the input trial wave-function. An updated overview of the VMC and DMC methods is given in
[
19
]
. These trial wave-functions can be optimised with a complex Jastrow factor,
[
26
]
because they potentially provide the input with exact nodes. This improves the single Slater determinant describing a ground-state from the DFT orbitals for heterogeneous systems. DFT ground-state orbitals decay exactly, but Jastrows are needed to improve its nodes. They are also improved by embedding the MRCI active site
[
10
]
.

The imaginary-time SWE is transformed into a Diffusion equation in the 3N-dimensional space of electron coordinates (with the potential energy acting as a source/sink term) by replacing
t
by the pure-imaginary time
it
:

The time-dependent Schrödinger equation may be written as:

i\,\frac{d}{dt}\Psi(r,t)=-\frac{1}{2}\nabla^{2}\Psi({\bf{r}},t)\,+\,V({\bf{r}})\Psi({\bf{r}},t)

(6)

where
\,\nabla^{2}
is the scalar Laplacian of an instantaneous electron-position vector:
\,{\bf{r}}
.
Defining the Hamiltonian (for stationary states):

\mathcal{H}=-\frac{1}{2}\nabla^{2}\,+\,V({\bf{r}})

(7)

The time-dependent SWE (replacing
\it it
with
\tau
), becomes the diffusion equation (8) below, where excited state contributions contain exponentially decreasing factors, fading out as the ’time’ variable
\tau=it
is increased in many small steps. The best-estimate of the ground-state energy eigenvalue (E
0
) is adjusted. Note that the exact value gives a zero derivative.

-\,\frac{d}{d\tau}\Psi({\bf{r}},t)=(\mathcal{H}-E_{0})\,\Psi({\bf{r}},t)

(8)

The imaginary-time SWE can therefore be solved by combining diffusion and branching or dying processes. Introducing importance sampling, using the trial wave function transforms the problem into one involving drift as well as diffusion but greatly reduces the population fluctuations due to the branching/dying process. The Fermionic wave function antisymmetry is maintained by constraining the nodal surface to equal that of the trial wave function. To resolve energy values, QMC statistical error in total values must be kept small. Initially, a 0.005 au time-step is used, extrapolated to the limit 0 to account for possible time-step bias.
Assuming the equilibration of a QMC calculation is of negligible duration,
\sigma
falls off as 1/
\sqrt{T}
, where T is the cpu-time (h). Accuracy for a given time also depends on the quality (nodes) of the trial wave-function. Lower fixed-phase error reduces equilibration time.

Fixed-node error (fixed phase, of complex wave-functions) is systematic and steps must be taken to make it small.
Single-determinant DFT ground-states are used for heteorgeneous systems here. Their influence is limited, however, by evaluating energy differences between structures with the same atoms. The imaginary-time SWE is transformed into a Diffusion equation in the 3N-dimensional space of electron coordinates. Convergence of the DMC properties is quadratic, therefore, energy eigen-values may converge from above or below the exact value. The DMC algorithm can be made variational in the Casula T-move scheme
[
27
]
, which was used in this work.
