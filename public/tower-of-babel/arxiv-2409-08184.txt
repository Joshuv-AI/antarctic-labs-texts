Outgoing monotone geodesics of standard subspaces
Jonas Schober

Abstract
We prove a real version of the Lax–Phillips Theorem and classify outgoing reflection positive orthogonal one-parameter groups. Using these results, we provide a normal form for outgoing monotone geodesics in the set Stand ⁡ ( ℋ ) \mathrm{Stand}(\mathcal{H}) of standard subspaces on some complex Hilbert space ℋ \mathcal{H} . As the modular operators of a standard subspace are closely related to positive Hankel operators, our results are obtained by constructing some explicit symbols for positive Hankel operators. We also describe which of the monotone geodesics in Stand ⁡ ( ℋ ) \mathrm{Stand}(\mathcal{H}) arise from the unitary one-parameter groups described in Borchers’ Theorem and provide explicit examples of monotone geodesics that are not of this type.



Introduction

Standard subspaces are much-studied objects in algebraic quantum field theory (AQFT). Here, given a complex Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
, a
standard subspace
is a real subspace
{\tt V}\subseteq\mathcal{H}
with

{\tt V}\cap i{\tt V}=\{0\}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\overline{{\tt V}+i{\tt V}}=\mathcal{H}

(
[
Lo08
]
,
[
Ne21
]
,
[
MN21
]
,
[
NÓ21
]
,
[
NØÓ21
]
). Given a standard subspace
{\tt V}\subseteq\mathcal{H}
, one has the densely defined
Tomita operator

T_{\tt V}:{\tt V}+i{\tt V}\to\mathcal{H},\quad x+iy\mapsto x-iy.

Then, by polar decomposition

T_{\tt V}=J_{\tt V}\Delta_{\tt V}^{\frac{1}{2}}

one obtains an anti-unitary involution
J_{\tt V}
and the positive densely defined operator
\Delta_{\tt V}
. This pair
(\Delta_{\tt V},J_{\tt V})
is referred to as the
pair of modular objects
of
{\tt V}
.

Now, given a complex Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
, one is interested in the geometric structure of the set
\mathrm{Stand}(\mathcal{H})
of standard subspaces of
\mathcal{H}
(
[
Ne18
]
).
\mathrm{Stand}(\mathcal{H})
is a so-called reflection space (see
[
Ne18
, Def. 1.1]
), which brings up the natural question of classifying
geodesics
, i.e. morphisms of reflection spaces, from
{\mathbb{R}}
to
\mathrm{Stand}(\mathcal{H})
. Concretely, one would like to understand geodesics
\gamma:{\mathbb{R}}\to\mathrm{Stand}(\mathcal{H})
that are
monotone
in the sense that, for
t_{1}\leq t_{2}
, one has
\gamma(t_{1})\subseteq\gamma(t_{2})
. By
[
Ne18
, Prop. 2.9]
, under an additional strong continuity assumption, such monotone geodesics are of the form
\gamma(t)=U_{t}{\tt V}
, where
{\tt V}\subseteq\mathcal{H}
is a standard subspace and
(U_{t})_{t\in{\mathbb{R}}}
is a strongly continuous unitary one-parameter group satisfying

J_{\tt V}U_{t}=U_{-t}J_{\tt V}\quad\forall t\in{\mathbb{R}}

(1)

and

\qquad U_{t}{\tt V}\subseteq{\tt V}\quad\forall t\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}.

In general, a description of all monotone geodesics in
\mathrm{Stand}(\mathcal{H})
is an open problem. In the present paper, we will give a normal form for these monotone geodesics under the additional assumption that

\bigcap_{t\in{\mathbb{R}}}U_{t}{\tt V}=\{0\}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\overline{\bigcup_{t\in{\mathbb{R}}}U_{t}{\tt V}}=\mathcal{H}.

Triples
(\mathcal{H},{\tt V},U)
satisfying these conditions are called
outgoing
(
[
RS79
, Sec. XI.11]
). Outgoing subspaces are well-studied objects in the area of optical and acoustical scattering theory (
[
RS79
, Chap. XI.11]
) and are usually considered in the context of complex Hilbert spaces, where a normal form is provided by the Lax–Phillips Theorem (
[
RS79
, Thm. XI.82]
). The Lax–Phillips Theorem states that, given a complex Hilbert space
\mathcal{E}
, a subspace
\mathcal{E}_{+}\subseteq\mathcal{E}
and a unitary one-parameter group
(U_{t})_{t\in{\mathbb{R}}}
, one has

\bigcap_{t\in{\mathbb{R}}}U_{t}\mathcal{E}_{+}=\{0\}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\overline{\bigcup_{t\in{\mathbb{R}}}U_{t}\mathcal{E}_{+}}=\mathcal{E},

if and only if there exists a complex Hilbert space
\mathcal{K}
such that

\mathcal{E}\cong L^{2}({\mathbb{R}},\mathcal{K})\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathcal{E}_{+}\cong L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}_{+},\mathcal{K})

with the operators
(U_{t})_{t\in{\mathbb{R}}}
being realized by

(U_{t}f)(x)=f(x-t).

We, on the other hand, are interested in triples where
\mathcal{E}_{+}
is a standard subspace and therefore, in particular, a real Hilbert space. A real version of the Lax–Phillips Theorem, as we need it, was proven in the unpublished Bachelor thesis
[
Fr20
]
, written in German. To make this result accessible to a broader audience, in the present paper, we provide an alternative proof for this real version of the Lax–Phillips Theorem.

Considering outgoing triples of the form
(\mathcal{H},{\tt V},U)
, the real version of the Lax–Phillips Theorem together with condition (
1
) can be used to relate
J_{\tt V}
to a Hankel operator, which can be shown to be positive. Here, given a complex Hilbert space
\mathcal{K}
, Hankel operators are bounded operators
H
on the
\mathcal{K}
-valued Hardy space
H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{+},\mathcal{K})
satisfying

HS_{t}=S_{t}^{*}H\qquad\forall t\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+},

where the operators
S_{t}\in B(H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{+},\mathcal{K}))
are given by

(S_{t}f)(x)=e^{itx}f(x).

This relation between
J_{\tt V}
and positive Hankel operators has been explored in
[
Sc23
, Sec. 4, Sec. 5]
and
[
ANS22
]
. In the scalar-valued case
\mathcal{K}={\mathbb{C}}
, in
[
ANS22
, Sec. 4]
, given a positive Hankel operator
H\in B(H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{+}))
, explicit symbols, i.e. functions
h\in L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}},{\mathbb{C}})
with

\left\langle f\middle|Hg\right\rangle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\overline{f(x)}h(x)g(-x)\,dx,

are constructed. We will adapt the ideas given there to the vector-valued case and construct a much broader class of concrete symbols (Theorem
2.32
).

Using this real Lax–Phillips Theorem and these symbols for positive Hankel operators, we will prove the main result of this paper (Theorem
3.15
), which provides a normal form for outgoing monotone geodesics of standard subspaces realized on some
L^{2}
-space over the real line.

A class of examples of such outgoing monotone geodesics is given by Borchers’ Theorem (
[
Bo92
, Thm. II.9]
,
[
Lo08
, Thm. 2.2.1]
). Borchers’ Theorem states that if the self-adjoint infinitesimal generator

\partial U\coloneqq\lim_{t\to 0}\frac{1}{it}(U_{t}-\textbf{1})

is either strictly positive or negative, then one has the commutation relations

\Delta_{\tt V}^{is}U_{t}\Delta_{\tt V}^{-is}=U_{e^{\mp 2\pi s}t},\qquad J_{\tt V}U_{t}J_{\tt V}=U_{-t}\qquad\forall s,t\in{\mathbb{R}}.

This yields an anti-unitary representation of the affine group
\mathrm{Aff}({\mathbb{R}})
on the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
. By the representation theory of
\mathrm{Aff}({\mathbb{R}})
(Appendix
C.1
), one then knows that there exists a real Hilbert space
\mathcal{M}
such that

\mathcal{H}\cong L^{2}({\mathbb{R}},\mathcal{M})\qquad\text{and}\qquad{\tt V}\cong L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}_{+},\mathcal{M})

with the operators
(U_{t})_{t\in{\mathbb{R}}}
being realized by

(U_{t}f)(x)=f(x-t).

It then is easy to see that
(\mathcal{H},{\tt V},U)
is outgoing. A central result of this paper (Theorem
3.24
) is to show how one can identify monotone geodesics that arise from Borchers’ Theorem in the normal form for general outgoing monotone geodesics of standard subspaces provided before.

This paper is structured as follows:

In Subsection
1.1
, we provide a real version of the Lax–Phillips Theorem both in the picture of position and momentum space. The momentum space version of the real Lax–Phillips Theorem (Theorem
1.16
) then states that, if
(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{E}_{+},U)
is outgoing, then there exists a real Hilbert space
\mathcal{M}
such that

\mathcal{E}\cong L^{2}({\mathbb{R}},\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}})^{\sharp},\qquad\mathcal{E}_{+}\cong H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{+},\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}})^{\sharp}\qquad\text{and}\qquad U_{t}\cong S_{t},\quad t\in{\mathbb{R}}

with

(S_{t}f)(x)=e^{itx}f(x).

Here, defining

\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{M}}:\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}}\cong{\mathbb{C}}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathcal{M}\to\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}},\quad z\otimes v\mapsto\overline{z}\otimes v,

we denote

L^{2}({\mathbb{R}},\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}})^{\sharp}\coloneqq\{f\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}},\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}}):(\forall x\in{\mathbb{R}})\,f(-x)=\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{M}}f(x)\}

H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{+},\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}})

\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}}
.

In Subsection
1.2
, we will extend this framework by an involution
\theta
, representing the modular operator

. We will show that these involutions are of the form
\theta=\theta_{h}

In Section

. We use the fact that, for every positive Hankel operator

1
Outgoing one-parameter groups

In this section, we will provide a normal form for outgoing subspaces in complex and real Hilbert spaces. In the complex case, this is provided by the classical Lax–Phillips Theorem, whereas in the real case, we will prove a real version of the Lax–Phillips Theorem. Later, we will include orthogonal involutions into the picture and provide a normal form for outgoing reflection positive orthogonal one-parameter groups (cf. Definition
1.21
).

1.1
The Lax–Phillips Theorem

We start with the definition of an outgoing subspace:

Definition 1.11
.

Let
\mathcal{E}
be a real or complex Hilbert space,
\mathcal{E}_{+}\subseteq\mathcal{E}
be a closed subspace and
(U_{t})_{t\in{\mathbb{R}}}\subseteq\mathop{\rm U{}}\nolimits(\mathcal{E})
be a orthogonal/unitary one-parameter group. We call
(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{E}_{+},U)

outgoing
if

U_{t}\mathcal{E}_{+}\subseteq\mathcal{E}_{+}\qquad\forall t\geq 0

and

\bigcap_{t\in{\mathbb{R}}}U_{t}\mathcal{E}_{+}=\{0\}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\overline{\bigcup_{t\in{\mathbb{R}}}U_{t}\mathcal{E}_{+}}=\mathcal{E}.

A classical result providing a normal form for outgoing subspaces on a complex Hilbert space is the Lax–Phillips Theorem:

Theorem 1.12
.

(Lax–Phillips)
(
[
RS79
, Thm. XI.82]
)

Let
\mathcal{E}
be a complex Hilbert space,
\mathcal{E}_{+}\subseteq\mathcal{E}
be a closed subspace and
(U_{t})_{t\in{\mathbb{R}}}\subseteq\mathop{\rm U{}}\nolimits(\mathcal{E})
be a unitary one-parameter group. Then
(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{E}_{+},U)
is outgoing, if and only if there exists a complex Hilbert space
\mathcal{M}
and a unitary map

\psi:\mathcal{E}\to L^{2}({\mathbb{R}},\mathcal{M})

such that

\psi(\mathcal{E}_{+})=L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}_{+},\mathcal{M})\qquad\text{and}\qquad\psi\circ U_{t}=\widehat{S}_{t}\circ\psi\quad\forall t\in{\mathbb{R}},

where the orthogonal shift operators
{\widehat{S}_{t}:L^{2}({\mathbb{R}},\mathcal{M})\to L^{2}({\mathbb{R}},\mathcal{M})}
are given by

(\widehat{S}_{t}f)(x)=f(x-t),\qquad t\in{\mathbb{R}},f\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}},\mathcal{M}).

We now want to prove a real version of the Lax-Phillips Theorem. For this, we need the following definition:

Definition 1.13
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a real Hilbert space. We set
\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}}\coloneqq{\mathbb{C}}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathcal{M}
and define the complex conjugation

\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{M}}:\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}}\to\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}},\quad z\otimes v\mapsto\overline{z}\otimes v.

Further, given an operator
A\in B(\mathcal{M})
, we define an operator
A_{\mathbb{C}}\in B(\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}})
by

A_{\mathbb{C}}(z\otimes v)=z\otimes(Av),\qquad z\in{\mathbb{C}},v\in\mathcal{M}.

Now, the real Lax–Phillips Theorem states the following:

Theorem 1.14
.

(Lax–Phillips, real version)
(
[
Fr20
, Thm. 3.3]
)

Let
\mathcal{E}
be a real Hilbert space,
\mathcal{E}_{+}\subseteq\mathcal{E}
be a closed subspace and
(U_{t})_{t\in{\mathbb{R}}}\subseteq\mathop{\rm U{}}\nolimits(\mathcal{E})
be an orthogonal one-parameter group. Then
(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{E}_{+},U)
is outgoing, if and only if there exists a real Hilbert space
\mathcal{M}
and an orthogonal map

\psi:\mathcal{E}\to L^{2}({\mathbb{R}},\mathcal{M})

such that

\psi(\mathcal{E}_{+})=L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}_{+},\mathcal{M})\qquad\text{and}\qquad\psi\circ U_{t}=\widehat{S}_{t}\circ\psi\quad\forall t\in{\mathbb{R}},

where the orthogonal shift operators
{\widehat{S}_{t}:L^{2}({\mathbb{R}},\mathcal{M})\to L^{2}({\mathbb{R}},\mathcal{M})}
are given by

(\widehat{S}_{t}f)(x)=f(x-t),\qquad t\in{\mathbb{R}},f\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}},\mathcal{M}).

Proof.

It is easy to see that triples of the form
(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{E}_{+},U)\cong(L^{2}({\mathbb{R}},\mathcal{M}),L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}_{+},\mathcal{M}),\widehat{S}_{t})
are outgoing, so we now assume that the triple
(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{E}_{+},U)
is outgoing and show that it is of this form. First, we notice that
(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{E}_{+},U)
is outgoing, if and only if
(\mathcal{E}_{\mathbb{C}},(\mathcal{E}_{+})_{\mathbb{C}},U_{\mathbb{C}})
is outgoing. Therefore, by the Lax–Phillips Theorem (Theorem
1.12
), there exists a complex Hilbert space
\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}
and a unitary map

\psi:\mathcal{E}_{\mathbb{C}}\to L^{2}({\mathbb{R}},\widetilde{\mathcal{M}})

such that

\psi((\mathcal{E}_{+})_{\mathbb{C}})=L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}_{+},\widetilde{\mathcal{M}})\qquad\text{and}\qquad\psi\circ(U_{t})_{\mathbb{C}}=\widehat{S}_{t}\circ\psi\quad\forall t\in{\mathbb{R}}.

Identifying
\mathcal{E}
with
{\mathbb{R}}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathcal{E}\subseteq\mathcal{E}_{\mathbb{C}}
and the subspace
\mathcal{E}_{+}
with
{\mathbb{R}}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathcal{E}_{+}
it just remains to show that there exists a real subspace
\mathcal{M}\subseteq\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}
such that

\psi(\mathcal{E})=L^{2}({\mathbb{R}},\mathcal{M})\qquad\text{and}\qquad\psi(\mathcal{E}_{+})=L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}_{+},\mathcal{M}).

For this, we notice that
\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{E}}
commutes with the unitary one-parameter group
((U_{t})_{\mathbb{C}})_{t\in{\mathbb{R}}}
, which implies that the operator
\theta\coloneqq\psi\circ\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{E}}\circ\psi^{-1}
commutes with the unitary one-parameter group
(\widehat{S}_{t})_{t\in{\mathbb{R}}}
. On the other hand
\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{E}}
commutes with the orthogonal projection onto
(\mathcal{E}_{+})_{\mathbb{C}}
, so
\theta
commutes with the orthogonal projection onto
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}_{+},\widetilde{\mathcal{M}})
. This, by Corollary
B.33
, implies that there exists an anti-linear operator
\sigma
on
\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}
such that

(\theta f)(x)=\sigma f(x),\qquad x\in{\mathbb{R}}.

Since
\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{E}}
and therefore
\theta
is an anti-linear involution, also
\sigma
must be an anti-linear involution, which implies that, setting

\mathcal{M}\coloneqq\ker(\sigma-\textbf{1}),

we have
\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}\cong{\mathbb{C}}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathcal{M}
. Now we notice that

\ker(\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{E}}-\textbf{1})={\mathbb{R}}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathcal{E}\cong\mathcal{E}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\ker(\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{E}}-\textbf{1})\cap(\mathcal{E}_{+})_{\mathbb{C}}={\mathbb{R}}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathcal{E}_{+}\cong\mathcal{E}_{+}.

Then, the statement follows from

\psi(\mathcal{E})=\psi\ker(\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{E}}-\textbf{1})=\ker(\theta-\textbf{1})=L^{2}({\mathbb{R}},\ker(\sigma-\textbf{1}))=L^{2}({\mathbb{R}},\mathcal{M})

and

\psi(\mathcal{E}_{+})=\psi\left(\ker(\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{E}}-\textbf{1})\cap(\mathcal{E}_{+})_{\mathbb{C}}\right)=L^{2}({\mathbb{R}},\mathcal{M})\cap L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}_{+},\widetilde{\mathcal{M}})=L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}_{+},\mathcal{M}).\qed

Instead of using the Lax–Phillips theorem as stated above, we want to use the Fourier transform to pass to momentum space:

Definition 1.15
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a real Hilbert space. On the Hilbert space
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}},\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}})
we define an involution
\sharp
by

f^{\sharp}(x)=\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{M}}f(-x),\qquad x\in{\mathbb{R}}

and for any subspace
H\subseteq L^{2}({\mathbb{R}},\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}})
, we set

H^{\sharp}\coloneqq\{f\in H:f^{\sharp}=f\}.

Denoting by
H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{+},\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}})\cong H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{+})\otimes\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}}
the Hardy space on the upper half-plane with values in
\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}}
(Definition
B.11
, Definition
B.17
), we can now formulate the momentum space version of the Lax–Phillips Theorem:

Theorem 1.16
.

(Lax–Phillips, momentum space version)

Let
\mathcal{E}
be a real Hilbert space,
\mathcal{E}_{+}\subseteq\mathcal{E}
be a closed subspace and
(U_{t})_{t\in{\mathbb{R}}}\subseteq\mathop{\rm U{}}\nolimits(\mathcal{E})
be an orthogonal one-parameter group. Then
(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{E}_{+},U)
is outgoing, if and only if there exists a real Hilbert space
\mathcal{M}
and an orthogonal map

\psi:\mathcal{E}\to L^{2}({\mathbb{R}},\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}})^{\sharp}

such that

\psi(\mathcal{E}_{+})=H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{+},\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}})^{\sharp}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\psi\circ U_{t}=S_{t}\circ\psi\quad\forall t\in{\mathbb{R}},

where

(S_{t}f)(x)=e^{itx}f(x),\qquad t,x\in{\mathbb{R}},f\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}},\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}})^{\sharp}.

Proof.

We consider the unitary Fourier transform

\mathcal{F}:L^{2}({\mathbb{R}},\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}})\to L^{2}({\mathbb{R}},\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}}),\quad(\mathcal{F}f)(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-ixp}f(p)\,dp.

Then one has

\mathcal{F}L^{2}({\mathbb{R}},\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}})^{\sharp}=L^{2}({\mathbb{R}},\mathcal{M})\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathcal{F}H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{+},\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}})^{\sharp}=L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}_{+},\mathcal{M})

and

\mathcal{F}\circ S_{t}=\widehat{S}_{t}\circ\mathcal{F}.

The statement then follows immediately from Theorem
1.14
.
∎

1.2
Reflection positivity

In this subsection, we consider reflection positive orthogonal/unitary one-parameter groups. They are defined as follows:

Definition 1.21
.

(
[
NÓ18
, Def. 2.1.1]
)
Let
\mathcal{E}
be a real or complex Hilbert space,
\mathcal{E}_{+}\subseteq\mathcal{E}
be a closed subspace and
\theta\in\mathop{\rm U{}}\nolimits(\mathcal{E})
. Then the triple
(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{E}_{+},\theta)
is called
reflection positive
, if

\left\langle\xi\middle|\theta\xi\right\rangle\geq 0\qquad\forall\xi\in\mathcal{E}_{+}.

Now let
(U_{t})_{t\in{\mathbb{R}}}
be a strongly continuous orthogonal/unitary one-parameter group on
\mathcal{E}
. We say that the quadruple
(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{E}_{+},U,\theta)
is a
reflection positive orthogonal/unitary one-parameter group
, if
(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{E}_{+},\theta)
is a reflection positive Hilbert space and

\theta U_{t}=U_{-t}\theta\quad\forall t\in{\mathbb{R}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad U_{t}\mathcal{E}_{+}\subseteq\mathcal{E}_{+}\quad\forall t\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}.

The goal of this subsection is to provide a normal form for outgoing reflection positive orthogonal one-parameter groups, i.e. reflection positive orthogonal one-parameter groups
(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{E}_{+},U,\theta)
for which the triple
(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{E}_{+},U)
is outgoing. For this, we need the following definitions:

Definition 1.22
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a real Hilbert space. We define involutions
\sharp
and
\flat
on the space
L^{\infty}\left({\mathbb{R}},B(\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}})\right)
by

f^{\sharp}\left(x\right)\coloneqq\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{M}}f\left(-x\right)\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{M}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad f^{\flat}\left(x\right)\coloneqq f\left(-x\right)^{*}

and set

L^{\infty}\left({\mathbb{R}},\mathop{\rm U{}}\nolimits(\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}})\right)\coloneqq\left\{f\in L^{\infty}\left({\mathbb{R}},B(\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}})\right):f\cdot f^{*}=f^{*}\cdot f=\textbf{1}\right\}.

and

L^{\infty}\left({\mathbb{R}},\mathop{\rm U{}}\nolimits(\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}})\right)^{\sharp}\coloneqq\left\{f\in L^{\infty}\left({\mathbb{R}},\mathop{\rm U{}}\nolimits(\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}})\right):f^{\sharp}=f\right\}.

and

L^{\infty}\left({\mathbb{R}},\mathop{\rm U{}}\nolimits(\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}})\right)^{\sharp,\flat}\coloneqq\left\{f\in L^{\infty}\left({\mathbb{R}},\mathop{\rm U{}}\nolimits(\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}})\right):f^{\sharp}=f^{\flat}=f\right\}.

Definition 1.23
.

Let
\mathcal{K}
be a complex Hilbert space. We define

R:L^{2}({\mathbb{R}},\mathcal{K})\to L^{2}({\mathbb{R}},\mathcal{K}),\quad(Rf)(x)=f(-x).

Further, for a function
h\in L^{\infty}\left({\mathbb{R}},B(\mathcal{K})\right)
, we denote the corresponding multiplication operator on
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}},\mathcal{K})
by
M_{h}
and define

\theta_{h}\coloneqq M_{h}R\in B(L^{2}({\mathbb{R}},\mathcal{K}))

and

H_{h}\coloneqq P_{+}\theta_{h}P_{+}\in B(H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{+},\mathcal{K})),

where
P_{+}
denotes the orthogonal projection onto
H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{+},\mathcal{K})
.

Given these definitions, we have the following theorem:

Theorem 1.24
.

(
[
Sc23
, Prop. 4.1.7, Thm. 4.1.11, Lem. 4.3.25]
)

Let
\mathcal{E}
be a real Hilbert space,
\mathcal{E}_{+}\subseteq\mathcal{E}
be a closed subspace,
(U_{t})_{t\in{\mathbb{R}}}\subseteq\mathop{\rm U{}}\nolimits(\mathcal{E})
be an orthogonal one-parameter group and
\theta\in\mathop{\rm U{}}\nolimits(\mathcal{E})
be an involution. Then
(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{E}_{+},U,\theta)
is an outgoing reflection positive orthogonal one-parameter group, if and only if there exists a real Hilbert space
\mathcal{M}
, a function
h\in L^{\infty}\left({\mathbb{R}},\mathop{\rm U{}}\nolimits(\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}})\right)^{\sharp,\flat}
and an orthogonal map

\psi:\mathcal{E}\to L^{2}({\mathbb{R}},\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}})^{\sharp}

such that

\psi(\mathcal{E}_{+})=H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{+},\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}})^{\sharp}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\psi\circ U_{t}=S_{t}\circ\psi\quad\forall t\in{\mathbb{R}}

and

\psi\circ\theta=\theta_{h}\circ\psi\qquad\text{and}\qquad H_{h}\geq 0.

Proof.

In view of the Lax–Phillips Theorem (Theorem
1.16
), it suffices to classify reflection positive orthogonal one-parameter groups of the form

(L^{2}({\mathbb{R}},\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}})^{\sharp},H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{+},\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}})^{\sharp},S,\theta).

Now, using that
R
is an involution satisfying

RS_{t}=S_{-t}R\qquad\forall t\in{\mathbb{R}},

the condition

\theta S_{t}=S_{-t}\theta\qquad\forall t\in{\mathbb{R}}

is equivalent to

(\theta R)S_{t}=S_{t}(\theta R)\qquad\forall t\in{\mathbb{R}},

which, by Proposition
B.31
, is equivalent to
\theta R=M_{h}
for some function
h\in L^{\infty}\left({\mathbb{R}},B(\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}})\right)
, i.e. to

\theta=M_{h}R=\theta_{h}.

Since
\theta=\theta_{h}
defines an orthogonal operator on
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}},\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}})^{\sharp}
, the function
h
must have unitary values, i.e.
h\in L^{\infty}\left({\mathbb{R}},\mathop{\rm U{}}\nolimits(\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}})\right)
and satisfy
h=h^{\sharp}
. Further, one has

(\theta_{h}\theta_{h}f)(x)=h(x)h(-x)f(x),

which shows that
\theta_{h}
is a unitary involution, if and only if
h(-x)=h(x)^{*}
for almost every
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
, i.e.
h=h^{\flat}
. This shows that

\theta S_{t}=S_{-t}\theta\qquad\forall t\in{\mathbb{R}}

is satisfied, if and only if there exists a function
h\in L^{\infty}\left({\mathbb{R}},\mathop{\rm U{}}\nolimits(\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}})\right)^{\sharp,\flat}
such that
\theta=\theta_{h}
.

Finally, using that

L^{2}\left({\mathbb{R}},\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}}\right)={\mathbb{C}}\cdot L^{2}\left({\mathbb{R}},\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}}\right)^{\sharp}\qquad\text{and}\qquad H^{2}\left({\mathbb{C}}_{+},\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}}\right)={\mathbb{C}}\cdot H^{2}\left({\mathbb{C}}_{+},\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}}\right)^{\sharp},

the reflection positivity condition

\left\langle f\middle|\theta_{h}f\right\rangle\geq 0\qquad\forall f\in H^{2}\left({\mathbb{C}}_{+},\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}}\right)^{\sharp}

is equivalent to

\left\langle f\middle|\theta_{h}f\right\rangle\geq 0\qquad\forall f\in H^{2}\left({\mathbb{C}}_{+},\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}}\right),

which, in turn, is equivalent to

H_{h}=P_{+}\theta_{h}P_{+}\geq 0.\qed

Remark 1.25
.

The operators
H_{h}
appearing in this theorem are so-called Hankel operators. This motivates our construction of symbols for positive Hankel operators in the following section.

2
Constructing symbols for positive Hankel operators

In this section, we will consider positive Hankel operators and their symbols as introduced in the following:

Definition 21
.

(
[
ANS22
, Def. 1.3, Thm. 3.5]
)
Let
\mathcal{K}
be a complex Hilbert space. An operator
H\in B(H^{2}\left({\mathbb{C}}_{+},\mathcal{K}\right))
is called
\mathcal{K}
-Hankel operator
, if

H\widetilde{S}_{t}=\widetilde{S}_{t}^{*}H\qquad\forall t\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+},

where

\widetilde{S}_{t}\coloneqq P_{+}S_{t}P_{+}\in B(H^{2}\left({\mathbb{C}}_{+},\mathcal{K}\right)).

Examples of such
\mathcal{K}
-Hankel operators are given by the operators
H_{h}
introduced in Definition
1.23
:

Lemma 22
.

(
[
ANS22
, Lem. 1.4]
)

Let
\mathcal{K}
be a complex Hilbert space and
h\in L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}},B(\mathcal{K}))
. Then the operator
H_{h}
is a
\mathcal{K}
-Hankel operator.

Proof.

For
t\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
one has

S_{t}H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{+},\mathcal{K})\subseteq H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{+},\mathcal{K}),

so, for every
f\in H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{+},\mathcal{K})
, one has

P_{+}S_{t}P_{+}=S_{t}P_{+}.

This yields

P_{+}S_{-t}=(S_{t}P_{+})^{*}=(P_{+}S_{t}P_{+})^{*}=(P_{+}S_{t}P_{+})^{*}P_{+}=\widetilde{S}_{t}^{*}P_{+},

so

H\widetilde{S}_{t}=P_{+}\theta_{h}P_{+}S_{t}P_{+}=P_{+}\theta_{h}S_{t}P_{+}=P_{+}S_{-t}\theta_{h}P_{+}=\widetilde{S}_{t}^{*}P_{+}\theta_{h}P_{+}=\widetilde{S}_{t}^{*}H.\qed

This lemma motivates the following definition:

Definition 23
.

Let
\mathcal{K}
be a complex Hilbert space and
H
be a
\mathcal{K}
-Hankel operator. A function
h\in L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}},B(\mathcal{K}))
is called a
symbol
for the Hankel operator
H
, if
H=H_{h}
.

In this section, we will explicitly construct some symbols for Hankel operators under the assumption that the Hankel operator is positive. Many of the results and proofs in this section are adaptations of those in
[
ANS22
]
, where the scalar-valued case
H^{2}\left({\mathbb{C}}_{+}\right)
was considered. In order to extend their results to the vector-valued case, it is necessary to partially redo some of their arguments. We do so replacing scalars by vectors and operators, while carefully handling the additional difficulties that arise in this setting.

2.1
Positive Hankel operators and Carleson measures

In this subsection, we will consider positive Hankel operators and see how they are connected to a certain class of measures. Given a complex Hilbert space
\mathcal{K}
, in the following, we will identify
L^{2}\left({\mathbb{R}},\mathcal{K}\right)
with the Hilbert space completation of
L^{2}\left({\mathbb{R}},{\mathbb{C}}\right)\otimes_{\mathbb{C}}\mathcal{K}
and for
f\in L^{2}\left({\mathbb{R}},{\mathbb{C}}\right)
and
v\in\mathcal{K}
we shortly write
fv
for
f\otimes v\in L^{2}\left({\mathbb{R}},\mathcal{K}\right)
. The measures we will consider are the following:

Definition 2.11
.

Let
\mathcal{K}
be a complex Hilbert space and let

\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{K})_{+}\coloneqq\{A\in B(\mathcal{K}):A=A^{*},A\geq 0\}.

An
\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{K})_{+}
-valued measure
\mu
on
{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
is called a
\mathcal{K}
-Carleson measure
, if

H^{2}\left({\mathbb{C}}_{+},\mathcal{K}\right)^{2}\ni(f,g)\mapsto\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\left\langle f(i\lambda)\middle|d\mu(\lambda)g(i\lambda)\right\rangle

defines a continuous sesquilinear form on
H^{2}\left({\mathbb{C}}_{+},\mathcal{K}\right)
. In this case we write
H_{\mu}
for the operator in
B(H^{2}\left({\mathbb{C}}_{+},\mathcal{K}\right))
with

\left\langle f\middle|H_{\mu}g\right\rangle=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\left\langle f(i\lambda)\middle|d\mu(\lambda)g(i\lambda)\right\rangle\qquad\forall f,g\in H^{2}\left({\mathbb{C}}_{+},\mathcal{K}\right).

The following proposition relates Carleson measures to positive Hankel operators. It is an operator-valued version of the results provided in
[
ANS22
, Sec. 3.2]
.

Proposition 2.12
.

Let
\mathcal{K}
be a complex Hilbert space and
\mu
be a
\mathcal{K}
-Carleson measure. Then, the map
\mu\mapsto H_{\mu}
defines a bijection between the set of Carleson measures and the set of positive Hankel operators.

Proof.

We first show that, in fact, the operators
H_{\mu}
are positive Hankel operators. For every
\mathcal{K}
-Carleson measure
\mu
and every
t\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
one has

\displaystyle\left\langle f\middle|H_{\mu}\widetilde{S}_{t}g\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\left\langle f(i\lambda)\middle|d\mu(\lambda)e^{-t\lambda}g(i\lambda)\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\left\langle e^{-t\lambda}f(i\lambda)\middle|d\mu(\lambda)g(i\lambda)\right\rangle=\left\langle\widetilde{S}_{t}f\middle|H_{\mu}g\right\rangle\qquad\forall f,g\in H^{2}\left({\mathbb{C}}_{+},\mathcal{K}\right),

which implies
H_{\mu}\widetilde{S}_{t}=\widetilde{S}_{t}^{*}H_{\mu}
and therefore
H_{\mu}
is a
\mathcal{K}
-Hankel operator. The positivity follows immediately from the fact that the measure
\mu
has values in the positive operators.

We now show that the map
\mu\mapsto H_{\mu}
is surjective. So let
H\in B\left(H^{2}\left({\mathbb{C}}_{+},\mathcal{K}\right)\right)
be a positive
\mathcal{K}
-Hankel operator. For every
f,g\in H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{+})
we have

|\left\langle fv\middle|Hgw\right\rangle|\leq\left\lVert H\right\rVert\cdot\left\lVert f\right\rVert\cdot\left\lVert g\right\rVert\cdot\left\lVert v\right\rVert\cdot\left\lVert w\right\rVert,

so there exists a bounded operator
H^{(f,g)}\in B(\mathcal{K})
with

\left\lVert H^{(f,g)}\right\rVert\leq\left\lVert H\right\rVert\cdot\left\lVert f\right\rVert\cdot\left\lVert g\right\rVert

(2)

such that

\left\langle fv\middle|Hgw\right\rangle=\left\langle v\middle|H^{(f,g)}w\right\rangle\qquad\forall v,w\in\mathcal{K}.

This equation, together with the Hankel condition
H\widetilde{S}_{t}=\widetilde{S}_{t}^{*}H
, implies that

H^{(f,\widetilde{S}_{t}g)}=H^{(\widetilde{S}_{t}f,g)}\qquad\forall t\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}.

(3)

Now, for every trace class operator
A\in B_{1}(\mathcal{H})
, by (
2
), we have

\left|\mathop{{\rm tr}}\nolimits\left(AH^{(f,g)}\right)\right|\leq\left\lVert A\right\rVert_{1}\cdot\left\lVert H\right\rVert\cdot\left\lVert f\right\rVert\cdot\left\lVert g\right\rVert,

so there exists a bounded operator
H^{A}\in B(H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{+}))
such that

\mathop{{\rm tr}}\nolimits\left(AH^{(f,g)}\right)=\left\langle f\middle|H^{A}g\right\rangle\qquad\forall f,g\in H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{+}).

This, together with (
3
), implies that
H^{A}
is a
{\mathbb{C}}
-Hankel operator. If further
A\in B_{1}(\mathcal{H})\cap\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{K})_{+}
, by construction, we have
H^{A}\geq 0
. Then, by
[
ANS22
, Sec. 3.2]
, there exists a measure
\mu^{A}
such that

\left\langle f\middle|H^{A}g\right\rangle=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\overline{f(i\lambda)}g(i\lambda)d\mu^{A}(\lambda)\qquad\forall f,g\in H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{+}).

Further, by construction, the map

B_{1}(\mathcal{H})\cap\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{K})_{+}\ni A\mapsto\mu^{A}

is a monoid homomorphism, which, by
[
Ne98
, Prop. I.7, Thm. I.10]
, implies that there exists a
\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{K})_{+}
-valued measure
\mu
on
{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
such that, for every
f,g\in H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{+})
, one has

\mathop{{\rm tr}}\nolimits\left(AH^{(f,g)}\right)=\left\langle f\middle|H^{A}g\right\rangle=\mathop{{\rm tr}}\nolimits\left(A\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\overline{f(i\lambda)}g(i\lambda)d\mu(\lambda)\right)\qquad\forall A\in B_{1}(\mathcal{H})\cap\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{K})_{+},

which implies

\left\langle fv\middle|Hgw\right\rangle=\left\langle v\middle|H^{(f,g)}w\right\rangle=\left\langle v\middle|\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\overline{f(i\lambda)}g(i\lambda)d\mu(\lambda)w\right\rangle\qquad\forall v,w\in\mathcal{K}.

Therefore, we have

\left\langle f\middle|Hg\right\rangle=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\left\langle f(i\lambda)\middle|d\mu(\lambda)g(i\lambda)\right\rangle\qquad\forall f,g\in H^{2}\left({\mathbb{C}}_{+},\mathcal{K}\right).

For the injectivity, we notice that, given two
\mathcal{K}
-Carleson measures
\mu,\nu
such that
H_{\mu}=H_{\nu}
, we have

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\overline{f(i\lambda)}g(i\lambda)\left\langle v\middle|d\mu(\lambda)v\right\rangle=\left\langle fv\middle|H_{\mu}gv\right\rangle=\left\langle fv\middle|H_{\nu}gv\right\rangle=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\overline{f(i\lambda)}g(i\lambda)\left\langle v\middle|d\nu(\lambda)v\right\rangle

for every
f,g\in H^{2}\left({\mathbb{C}}_{+}\right)
. This, by
[
ANS22
, Sec. 3.2]
, implies that

\left\langle v\middle|\mu v\right\rangle=\left\langle v\middle|\nu v\right\rangle

for every
v\in\mathcal{K}
and therefore
\mu=\nu
.
∎

This proposition allows us to define:

Definition 2.13
.

Let
\mathcal{K}
be a complex Hilbert space. Given a positive
\mathcal{K}
-Hankel operator
H\in B(H^{2}\left({\mathbb{C}}_{+},\mathcal{K}\right))
, we write
\mu_{H}
for the unique
\mathcal{K}
-Carleson measure with
H=H_{\mu_{H}}
, i.e. the map
H\mapsto\mu_{H}
is the inverse of the map
\mu\mapsto H_{\mu}
.

2.2
Constructing symbols using Carleson measures

In this subsection, we want to construct symbols for positive Hankel operators from the perspective of Carleson measures. So, given a Carleson measure
\mu
, we want to construct a symbol for the Hankel operator
H_{\mu}
. In this construction, two types of functions will play a central role. The first one are the functions
Q_{\xi}
defined as follows:

Definition 2.21
.

Let
\xi\in{\mathbb{C}}_{+}
. Then, we define the function

Q_{\xi}:{\mathbb{C}}_{+}\to{\mathbb{C}},\quad z\mapsto\frac{1}{2\pi}\cdot\frac{i}{z-\overline{\xi}}.

Remark 2.22
.

By Proposition
B.16
, for every
\xi\in{\mathbb{C}}_{+}
, one has
Q_{\xi}\in H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{+})
satisfying

\left\langle Q_{\xi}\middle|f\right\rangle=f(\xi)\qquad\forall f\in H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{+}).

The second type of functions that play a crucial role in the construction of symbols for the Hankel operator
H_{\mu}
are the functions
\mathcal{N}_{\mu}
defined like this:

Definition 2.23
.

(cf.
[
ANS22
, Def. 3.9]
)
Let
\mathcal{K}
be a complex Hilbert space and let
\mu
be a
\mathcal{K}
-Carleson measure. We define the function
\mathcal{N}_{\mu}:{\mathbb{C}}\setminus i(-\infty,0]\to B(\mathcal{K})
by

\mathcal{N}_{\mu}(z)=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\frac{1}{\lambda-iz}-\frac{\lambda}{1+\lambda^{2}}\,d\mu(\lambda).

(For the existence of the integral, see Remark
2.24
(a).) Further, we set

\mathcal{R}_{\mu}\coloneqq\mathop{{\rm Re}}\nolimits\,\mathcal{N}_{\mu}\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathcal{N}_{\mu}+\mathcal{N}_{\mu}^{*}\right)\qquad\text{and}\qquad{\mathcal{I}}_{\mu}\coloneqq\mathop{{\rm Im}}\nolimits\,\mathcal{N}_{\mu}\coloneqq\frac{1}{2i}\left(\mathcal{N}_{\mu}-\mathcal{N}_{\mu}^{*}\right).

Remark 2.24
.

(a)

The integral used for the definition of the function
\mathcal{N}_{\mu}
does in fact exist. For this, we notice that, for every
v\in\mathcal{K}
, one has
Q_{i}v\in H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{+},\mathcal{K})
. This implies that, for every
v,w\in\mathcal{K}
, one has

\displaystyle\left\langle v\middle|\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{2}}\cdot\frac{1}{(1+\lambda)^{2}}\,d\mu(\lambda)w\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle v\middle|\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}|Q_{i}(i\lambda)|^{2}\,d\mu(\lambda)w\right\rangle=\left\langle Q_{i}v\middle|H_{\mu}Q_{i}w\right\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert H_{\mu}\right\rVert\cdot\left\lVert Q_{i}v\right\rVert\cdot\left\lVert Q_{i}w\right\rVert=\left\lVert H_{\mu}\right\rVert\cdot\left\lVert Q_{i}\right\rVert^{2}\cdot\left\lVert v\right\rVert\cdot\left\lVert w\right\rVert,

so the integral

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\frac{1}{(1+\lambda)^{2}}\,d\mu(\lambda)

exists and defines a bounded operator in
B(\mathcal{K})
. Now, for
z\in{\mathbb{C}}\setminus i(-\infty,0]
the function

{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\ni\lambda\mapsto\frac{(1+\lambda iz)(1+\lambda)^{2}}{(\lambda-iz)(1+\lambda^{2})}

is bounded since the denominator has no zeroes on
{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
and the limits for
\lambda\downarrow 0
and
\lambda\to\infty
exist and are
\frac{1}{-iz}
and
iz
respectively. This implies the existence of the integral

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\frac{(1+\lambda iz)(1+\lambda)^{2}}{(\lambda-iz)(1+\lambda^{2})}\cdot\frac{1}{(1+\lambda)^{2}}\,d\mu(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\frac{1+\lambda iz}{(\lambda-iz)(1+\lambda^{2})}\,d\mu(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\frac{1}{\lambda-iz}-\frac{\lambda}{1+\lambda^{2}}\,d\mu(\lambda)

and therefore the definition of the function
\mathcal{N}_{\mu}
is valid.

(b)

For
x+iy\in{\mathbb{C}}\setminus i(-\infty,0]
one has

\mathcal{N}_{\mu}(x+iy)=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\frac{1}{\lambda+y-ix}-\frac{\lambda}{1+\lambda^{2}}\,d\mu(\lambda)=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\frac{\lambda+y+ix}{(\lambda+y)^{2}+x^{2}}-\frac{\lambda}{1+\lambda^{2}}\,d\mu(\lambda).

This implies

\mathcal{R}_{\mu}(x+iy)=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\frac{\lambda+y}{(\lambda+y)^{2}+x^{2}}-\frac{\lambda}{1+\lambda^{2}}\,d\mu(\lambda)

(4)

and

{\mathcal{I}}_{\mu}(x+iy)=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\frac{x}{(\lambda+y)^{2}+x^{2}}\,d\mu(\lambda).

(5)

(c)

The function

{\mathbb{C}}_{+}\ni z\mapsto\mathcal{N}(-iz)

is an operator-valued version of a so-called Pick-function (cf.
[
Si19
, Thm. 3.4]
).

Before we can use the functions
\mathcal{R}_{\mu}
and
{\mathcal{I}}_{\mu}
to construct symbols for Hankel operators, we need to investigate some of their basic properties:

Lemma 2.25
.

Let
\mathcal{K}
be a complex Hilbert space and let
\mu
be a
\mathcal{K}
-Carleson measure. Then, for every
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\times}
, one has

\mathcal{R}_{\mu}(-x)=\mathcal{R}_{\mu}(x)\qquad\text{and}\qquad{\mathcal{I}}_{\mu}(-x)=-{\mathcal{I}}_{\mu}(x)

and

0\leq\mathop{{\rm sgn}}\nolimits(x){\mathcal{I}}_{\mu}(x).

Proof.

For
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\times}
, by equation (
4
), we get

\mathcal{R}_{\mu}(-x)=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\frac{\lambda}{\lambda^{2}+x^{2}}-\frac{\lambda}{1+\lambda^{2}}\,d\mu(\lambda)=\mathcal{R}_{\mu}(x)

and by equation (
5
), we get

{\mathcal{I}}_{\mu}(-x)=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\frac{-x}{\lambda^{2}+x^{2}}\,d\mu(\lambda)=-{\mathcal{I}}_{\mu}(x).

Further, by equation (
5
), one has

\mathop{{\rm sgn}}\nolimits(x){\mathcal{I}}_{\mu}(x)=\mathop{{\rm sgn}}\nolimits(x)\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\frac{x}{\lambda^{2}+x^{2}}\,d\mu(\lambda)=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\frac{|x|}{\lambda^{2}+x^{2}}\,d\mu(\lambda)\geq 0.\qed

The idea now is to use some variation of the function
\mathcal{N}_{\mu}
as a symbol for the positive Hankel operator
H_{\mu}
. Unfortunately, in general, the function
\mathcal{N}_{\mu}
is not bounded, but the following lemma shows that at least its imaginary part is bounded and its real part does not grow faster than logarithmically:

Lemma 2.26
.

(cf.
[
ANS22
, Thm. 4.1]
)

Let
\mathcal{K}
be a complex Hilbert space and let
\mu
be a
\mathcal{K}
-Carleson measure. Further, let
\alpha\coloneqq\left\lVert H_{\mu}\right\rVert
. Then, for every
z\in{\mathbb{C}}_{+}\cup{\mathbb{R}}^{\times}
, one has

\left\lVert{\mathcal{I}}_{\mu}(z)\right\rVert\leq 2\alpha\qquad\text{and}\qquad\left\lVert\mathcal{R}_{\mu}(z)\right\rVert\leq\frac{8}{\pi}\alpha|\log|z||+\alpha.

Proof.

For every function
f\in H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{+})
and
v\in\mathcal{K}
we have

\left\langle v\middle|\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\left|f(i\lambda)\right|^{2}\,d\mu(\lambda)v\right\rangle=\left\langle fv\middle|H_{\mu}fv\right\rangle\leq\alpha\left\lVert f\right\rVert^{2}\cdot\left\lVert v\right\rVert^{2}=\alpha\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left|f(t)\right|^{2}\,dt\cdot\left\langle v\middle|\textbf{1}v\right\rangle,

so

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\left|f(i\lambda)\right|^{2}\,d\mu(\lambda)\leq\alpha\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left|f(t)\right|^{2}\,dt\cdot\textbf{1}.

(6)

Therefore, for
\rho\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
, one has

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\frac{\rho}{(\lambda+\rho)^{2}}\,d\mu(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\rho\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\left|\frac{i}{i\lambda+i\rho}\right|^{2}\,d\mu(\lambda)=\rho(2\pi)^{2}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\left|Q_{i\rho}(i\lambda)\right|^{2}\,d\mu(\lambda)

\displaystyle\overset{(\ref{eq:muLineEstimate})}{\leq}\rho(2\pi)^{2}\alpha\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left|Q_{i\rho}(t)\right|^{2}\,dt\cdot\textbf{1}=\alpha\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{\rho}{\rho^{2}+t^{2}}\,dt\cdot\textbf{1}=\pi\alpha\cdot\textbf{1},

(7)

using Lemma
D1
(a) in the last step. Further, considering the holomorphic square root

\sqrt{\cdot}:{\mathbb{C}}\setminus(-\infty,0]\to{\mathbb{C}}

with
\sqrt{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}={\mathbb{R}}_{+}
, the function

f:{\mathbb{C}}_{+}\to{\mathbb{C}},\quad z\mapsto 2\pi\frac{\sqrt{-iz}}{z+i}

is bounded and holomorphic, so
f\in H^{\infty}({\mathbb{C}}_{+})
(Definition
B.11
), which, by Lemma
B.18
(d), implies that

f\cdot Q_{i\rho}\in H^{\infty}({\mathbb{C}}_{+})\cdot H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{+})=H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{+}).

Therefore

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\frac{\lambda|1-\rho^{2}|}{(\lambda^{2}+\rho^{2})(1+\lambda^{2})}\,d\mu(\lambda)\leq 4|1-\rho^{2}|\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\frac{\lambda}{(\lambda+\rho)^{2}(\lambda+1)^{2}}\,d\mu(\lambda)

\displaystyle\qquad=4|1-\rho^{2}|\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\left|\frac{\sqrt{-i(i\lambda)}}{(i\lambda+i\rho)(i\lambda+i)}\right|^{2}\,d\mu(\lambda)=4|1-\rho^{2}|(2\pi)^{2}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\left|f(i\lambda)Q_{i\rho}(i\lambda)\right|^{2}\,d\mu(\lambda)

\displaystyle\qquad\leq 4\alpha|1-\rho^{2}|(2\pi)^{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left|f(t)Q_{i\rho}(t)\right|^{2}\,dt\cdot\textbf{1}=4\alpha|1-\rho^{2}|\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left|\frac{\sqrt{-it}}{(t+i\rho)(t+i)}\right|^{2}\,dt\cdot\textbf{1}

\displaystyle\qquad=4\alpha|1-\rho^{2}|\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{|t|}{(t^{2}+\rho^{2})(t^{2}+1)}\,dt\cdot\textbf{1}=8\alpha\left|\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\frac{t(\rho^{2}-1)}{(t^{2}+\rho^{2})(t^{2}+1)}\,dt\right|\cdot\textbf{1}

\displaystyle\qquad=8\alpha|\log(\rho)|,

(8)

using Lemma
D1
(h) in the last step. For
x+iy\in{\mathbb{C}}_{+}\cup{\mathbb{R}}^{\times}
, using equation (
5
) and writing
|A|=\sqrt{AA^{*}}
for
A\in B(\mathcal{K})
, this yields

\displaystyle|{\mathcal{I}}_{\mu}(x+iy)|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\frac{|x|}{(\lambda+y)^{2}+x^{2}}\,d\mu(\lambda)\leq\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\frac{|x|}{\lambda^{2}+x^{2}}\,d\mu(\lambda)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{\pi}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\frac{|x|}{(\lambda+|x|)^{2}}\,d\mu(\lambda)\overset{(\ref{eq:rhoPiAlphaEstimate})}{\leq}2\alpha\cdot\textbf{1},

so

\left\lVert{\mathcal{I}}_{\mu}(x+iy)\right\rVert\leq 2\alpha.

Further, by equation (
4
), we have

\displaystyle|\mathcal{R}_{\mu}(x+iy)|

\displaystyle=\left|\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\frac{\lambda+y}{(\lambda+y)^{2}+x^{2}}-\frac{\lambda}{1+\lambda^{2}}\,d\mu(\lambda)\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\frac{-y\lambda^{2}+\lambda(1-x^{2}-y^{2})+y}{((\lambda+y)^{2}+x^{2})(1+\lambda^{2})}\,d\mu(\lambda)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\frac{y(1+\lambda^{2})+\lambda|1-x^{2}-y^{2}|}{((\lambda+y)^{2}+x^{2})(1+\lambda^{2})}\,d\mu(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\frac{\lambda|1-x^{2}-y^{2}|}{((\lambda+y)^{2}+x^{2})(1+\lambda^{2})}\,d\mu(\lambda)+\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\frac{y}{(\lambda+y)^{2}+x^{2}}\,d\mu(\lambda)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\frac{\lambda|1-x^{2}-y^{2}|}{(\lambda^{2}+y^{2}+x^{2})(1+\lambda^{2})}\,d\mu(\lambda)+\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\frac{y}{(\lambda+y)^{2}}\,d\mu(\lambda)

\displaystyle\overset{(\ref{eq:rhoPiAlphaEstimate})}{\leq}\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\frac{\lambda|1-|x+iy|^{2}|}{(\lambda^{2}+|x+iy|^{2})(1+\lambda^{2})}\,d\mu(\lambda)+\alpha\cdot\textbf{1}

\displaystyle\overset{(\ref{eq:alphaLogEstimate})}{\leq}\frac{8}{\pi}\alpha\left|\log|x+iy|\right|\cdot\textbf{1}+\alpha\cdot\textbf{1}

so

\left\lVert\mathcal{R}_{\mu}(x+iy)\right\rVert\leq\frac{8}{\pi}\alpha|\log|x+iy||+\alpha.\qed

Proposition 2.27
.

Let
\mathcal{K}
be a complex Hilbert space and let
\mu
be a
\mathcal{K}
-Carleson measure. Then, for every
\xi\in{\mathbb{C}}_{+}
and
v,w\in\mathcal{K}
, one has

\left\langle v\middle|\mathcal{N}_{\mu}w\right\rangle\cdot Q_{\xi}\in H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{+}).

Proof.

By Lemma
2.26
, setting
\alpha\coloneqq\left\lVert H_{\mu}\right\rVert
, for every
z\in{\mathbb{C}}_{+}
, we have

\left\lVert\mathcal{N}_{\mu}(z)\right\rVert\leq\left\lVert\mathcal{R}_{\mu}(z)\right\rVert+\left\lVert{\mathcal{I}}_{\mu}(z)\right\rVert\leq\frac{8}{\pi}\alpha|\log|z||+\alpha+2\alpha=3\alpha+\frac{4}{\pi}\alpha|\log(|z|^{2})|.

Therefore, for
x+iy\in{\mathbb{C}}_{+}
, we get

\displaystyle\left\lVert\mathcal{N}_{\mu}(x+iy)\right\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left(3\alpha+\frac{4}{\pi}\alpha\left|\log\left(x^{2}+y^{2}\right)\right|\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=(3\alpha)^{2}+\frac{24}{\pi}\alpha^{2}\left|\log\left(x^{2}+y^{2}\right)\right|+\left(\frac{4\alpha}{\pi}\right)^{2}\left|\log\left(x^{2}+y^{2}\right)\right|^{2}.

(9)

Now, for
x+iy\in{\mathbb{C}}_{+}
, if
x^{2}+y^{2}\leq 1
, we have

\left|\log\left(x^{2}+y^{2}\right)\right|=-\log(x^{2}+y^{2})\leq-\log(x^{2})=-2\log|x|

and if
x^{2}+y^{2}\geq 1
with
x^{2}\geq y^{2}
, we have

\left|\log\left(x^{2}+y^{2}\right)\right|=\log(x^{2}+y^{2})\leq\log(2x^{2})=\log(2)+2\log|x|

and if
x^{2}+y^{2}\geq 1
with
x^{2}\leq y^{2}
, we have

\displaystyle\left|\log\left(x^{2}+y^{2}\right)\right|

\displaystyle=\log(x^{2}+y^{2})\leq\log(2y^{2})\leq\log\left(\frac{81}{24}y^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\log\left(\frac{\left(3\sqrt{y}\right)^{4}}{4!}\right)\leq\log\left(\exp\left(3\sqrt{y}\right)\right)=3\sqrt{y}.

In any case, we get

\left|\log\left(x^{2}+y^{2}\right)\right|\leq\max\left\{\log(2)+2|\log|x||,3\sqrt{y}\right\}\leq\log(2)+2|\log|x||+\frac{3}{2}(y+1)

and

\left|\log\left(x^{2}+y^{2}\right)\right|^{2}\leq\max\left\{\left(\log(2)+2|\log|x||\right)^{2},(3\sqrt{y})^{2}\right\}\leq(\log(2)+2|\log|x||)^{2}+9y.

Together with (
9
) this implies that there are constants
A,B,C,D\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
such that

\displaystyle\left\lVert\mathcal{N}_{\mu}(x+iy)\right\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle\leq A+B|\log|x||+C|\log|x||^{2}+Dy.

This implies that, for every
\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
, one has

\displaystyle\sup_{y>0}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left\lVert\mathcal{N}_{\mu}(x+iy)\right\rVert^{2}|Q_{i\lambda}(x+iy)|^{2}\,dx

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{2}}\sup_{y>0}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{A+B|\log|x||+C|\log|x||^{2}+Dy}{(\lambda+y)^{2}+x^{2}}\,dx

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{2}}\left[\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{A+B|\log|x||+C|\log|x||^{2}}{\lambda^{2}+x^{2}}\,dx+D\sup_{y>0}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{y}{y^{2}+x^{2}}\,dx\right]

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{2}}\left[\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{A+B|\log|x||+C|\log|x||^{2}}{\lambda^{2}+x^{2}}\,dx+D\pi\right]<\infty

and therefore, for
v,w\in\mathcal{K}
, one has

\displaystyle\sup_{y>0}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left|\left\langle v\middle|\mathcal{N}_{\mu}(x+iy)w\right\rangle\cdot Q_{i\lambda}(x+iy)\right|^{2}\,dx

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\left\lVert v\right\rVert^{2}\cdot\left\lVert w\right\rVert^{2}\cdot\sup_{y>0}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left\lVert\mathcal{N}_{\mu}(x+iy)\right\rVert^{2}|Q_{i\lambda}(x+iy)|^{2}\,dx<\infty,

so

\left\langle v\middle|\mathcal{N}_{\mu}w\right\rangle\cdot Q_{i\lambda}\in H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{+}).

Now, for any
\xi\in{\mathbb{C}}_{+}
, we have

\frac{Q_{\xi}(z)}{Q_{i\lambda}(z)}=\frac{z+i\lambda}{z-\overline{\xi}},\qquad\text{so}\qquad\frac{Q_{\xi}}{Q_{i\lambda}}\in H^{\infty}({\mathbb{C}}_{+}).

This, by Lemma
B.18
(d), implies that

\left\langle v\middle|\mathcal{N}_{\mu}w\right\rangle\cdot Q_{\xi}=\frac{Q_{\xi}}{Q_{i\lambda}}\cdot\left\langle v\middle|\mathcal{N}_{\mu}w\right\rangle\cdot Q_{i\lambda}\in H^{\infty}({\mathbb{C}}_{+})\cdot H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{+})=H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{+}).\qed

Corollary 2.28
.

(cf.
[
ANS22
, Eq. (30)]
)

Let
\mathcal{K}
be a complex Hilbert space and let
\mu
be a
\mathcal{K}
-Carleson measure. Then, for every
z,\xi\in{\mathbb{C}}_{+}
and
v,w\in\mathcal{K}
, one has
\left\langle Q_{z}v\middle|\mathcal{N}_{\mu}^{*}RQ_{\xi}w\right\rangle=0
.

Proof.

By Lemma
2.25
one has
\mathcal{N}_{\mu}^{*}R=R\mathcal{N}_{\mu}
, so

\left\langle Q_{z}v\middle|\mathcal{N}_{\mu}^{*}RQ_{\xi}w\right\rangle=\left\langle Q_{z}v\middle|R\mathcal{N}_{\mu}Q_{\xi}w\right\rangle=\left\langle RQ_{z}\middle|\left\langle v\middle|\mathcal{N}_{\mu}w\right\rangle\cdot Q_{\xi}\right\rangle=0,

using in the last step that
RQ_{z}\in H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{-})
and
\left\langle v\middle|\mathcal{N}_{\mu}w\right\rangle\cdot Q_{\xi}\in H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{+})
by Proposition
2.27
.
∎

With this preparation, we can prove the following generalization of
[
ANS22
, Thm. 4.1]
:

Theorem 2.29
.

Let
\mathcal{K}
be a complex Hilbert space and let
\mu
be a
\mathcal{K}
-Carleson measure. Then
i\cdot{\mathcal{I}}_{\mu}
is a symbol for the Hankel operator
H_{\mu}
.

Proof.

For
z,\xi\in{\mathbb{C}}_{+}
we have

Q_{\xi}(-\overline{z})=\frac{1}{2\pi}\frac{i}{-\overline{z}-\overline{\xi}}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\frac{-i}{\overline{z}-(-\overline{\xi})}=\overline{\frac{1}{2\pi}\frac{i}{z-(-\xi)}}=\overline{Q_{-\overline{\xi}}(z)}.

(10)

Further, since
\overline{Q_{-\overline{\xi}}}\in H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{-})
, we have

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\overline{Q_{z}(x)}\overline{Q_{-\overline{\xi}}(x)}\,dx=\left\langle Q_{z}\middle|\overline{Q_{-\overline{\xi}}}\right\rangle=0.

(11)

We therefore get

\displaystyle\left\langle Q_{z}\middle|\mathcal{N}_{\mu}RQ_{\xi}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\overline{Q_{z}(x)}Q_{\xi}(-x)\cdot\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\frac{1}{\lambda-ix}-\frac{\lambda}{1+\lambda^{2}}\,d\mu(\lambda)\,dx

\displaystyle\overset{(\ref{eq:QReflectionIdentity})}{=}\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\overline{Q_{z}(x)}\overline{Q_{-\overline{\xi}}(x)}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}2\pi Q_{i\lambda}(x)-\frac{\lambda}{1+\lambda^{2}}\,d\mu(\lambda)\,dx

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\overline{Q_{z}(x)}\overline{Q_{-\overline{\xi}}(x)}\left[2\pi Q_{i\lambda}(x)-\frac{\lambda}{1+\lambda^{2}}\right]\,dx\,d\mu(\lambda)

\displaystyle\overset{(\ref{eq:Q0Identity})}{=}\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\overline{Q_{z}(x)}\overline{Q_{-\overline{\xi}}(x)}\cdot 2\pi Q_{i\lambda}(x)\,dx\,d\mu(\lambda)

\displaystyle=2\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\left\langle Q_{z}Q_{-\overline{\xi}}\middle|Q_{i\lambda}\right\rangle\,d\mu(\lambda)=2\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\overline{Q_{z}(i\lambda)Q_{-\overline{\xi}}(i\lambda)}\,d\mu(\lambda)

\displaystyle\overset{(\ref{eq:QReflectionIdentity})}{=}2\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\overline{Q_{z}(i\lambda)}Q_{\xi}(i\lambda)\,d\mu(\lambda)=2\left\langle Q_{z}\middle|H_{\mu}Q_{\xi}\right\rangle.

This implies that for
z,\xi\in{\mathbb{C}}_{+}
and
v,w\in\mathcal{K}
, one has

\displaystyle\left\langle Q_{z}v\middle|(i\cdot{\mathcal{I}}_{\mu})RQ_{\xi}w\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left[\left\langle Q_{z}v\middle|\mathcal{N}_{\mu}RQ_{\xi}w\right\rangle-\left\langle Q_{z}v\middle|\mathcal{N}_{\mu}^{*}RQ_{\xi}w\right\rangle\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left[2\left\langle Q_{z}v\middle|H_{\mu}Q_{\xi}w\right\rangle-0\right]=\left\langle Q_{z}v\middle|H_{\mu}Q_{\xi}w\right\rangle,

using Corollary
2.28
in the penultimate step. Since

\overline{{\rm span}\left\{Q_{z}v:z\in{\mathbb{C}}_{+},v\in\mathcal{K}\right\}}=H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{+},\mathcal{K})

by Lemma
B.18
(a) and both operators
H_{\mu}
and
P_{+}(i\cdot{\mathcal{I}}_{\mu})RP_{+}
are bounded by Lemma
2.26
, we get that

H_{\mu}=P_{+}(i\cdot{\mathcal{I}}_{\mu})RP_{+}=H_{i\cdot{\mathcal{I}}_{\mu}}.\qed

Example 2.210
.

(

)
Given a complex Hilbert space

-valued

2.3
Constructing more symbols using projections

We now want to construct a more general class of symbols than the ones in Theorem
2.29
. For this, we need the following definition:

Definition 2.31
.

Let
\mathcal{K}
be a complex Hilbert space and let
\mu
be a
\mathcal{K}
-Carleson measure. Given a projection
p\in B(\mathcal{K})
and an operator
C\in B((\textbf{1}-p)\mathcal{K},p\mathcal{K})
, we define

\beta(\mu,p,C):{\mathbb{R}}^{\times}\to B(\mathcal{K}),\quad x\mapsto\begin{pmatrix}i\,p{\mathcal{I}}_{\mu}(x)p&C+p\mathcal{R}_{\mu}(x)(\textbf{1}-p)\\
C^{*}+(\textbf{1}-p)\mathcal{R}_{\mu}(x)p&i\,(\textbf{1}-p){\mathcal{I}}_{\mu}(x)(\textbf{1}-p)\end{pmatrix}.

The following theorem shows that as long as the so-defined functions are bounded, they define symbols of the positive Hankel operator
H_{\mu}
:

Theorem 2.32
.

Let
\mathcal{K}
be a complex Hilbert space and let
\mu
be a
\mathcal{K}
-Carleson measure. Further let
p\in B(\mathcal{K})
be a projection and let
C\in B((\textbf{1}-p)\mathcal{K},p\mathcal{K})
.
If
p\mathcal{R}_{\mu}(\textbf{1}-p)
is bounded, then
\beta(\mu,p,C)
is a symbol for the Hankel operator
H_{\mu}
.

Proof.

One has

\beta(\mu,p,C)-i\cdot{\mathcal{I}}_{\mu}=\begin{pmatrix}0&C+p\mathcal{N}_{\mu}^{*}(\textbf{1}-p)\\
C^{*}+(\textbf{1}-p)\mathcal{N}_{\mu}^{*}p&0\end{pmatrix}.

For
z,\xi\in{\mathbb{C}}_{+}
and
v,w\in\mathcal{K}
this yields

\displaystyle\left\langle Q_{z}v\middle|\left[\beta(\mu,p,C)-i\cdot{\mathcal{I}}_{\mu}\right]RQ_{\xi}w\right\rangle

\displaystyle\qquad=\left\langle Q_{z}pv\middle|\mathcal{N}_{\mu}^{*}RQ_{\xi}(\textbf{1}-p)w\right\rangle+\left\langle Q_{z}(\textbf{1}-p)v\middle|\mathcal{N}_{\mu}^{*}RQ_{\xi}pw\right\rangle+\left\langle Q_{z}v\middle|\begin{pmatrix}0&C\\
C^{*}&0\end{pmatrix}RQ_{\xi}w\right\rangle

\displaystyle\qquad=0+0+0=0,

where the first two terms vanish by Corollary
2.28
and the last term since

Q_{z}v\in H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{+},\mathcal{K})\qquad\text{and}\qquad\begin{pmatrix}0&C\\
C^{*}&0\end{pmatrix}RQ_{\xi}w\in H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{-},\mathcal{K}).

Since

\overline{{\rm span}\left\{Q_{z}v:z\in{\mathbb{C}}_{+},v\in\mathcal{K}\right\}}=H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{+},\mathcal{K})

by Lemma
B.18
(a), this implies that

P_{+}\left[\beta(\mu,p,C)-i\cdot{\mathcal{I}}_{\mu}\right]RP_{+}=0,

which is equivalent to

P_{+}\beta(\mu,p,C)RP_{+}=P_{+}(i\cdot{\mathcal{I}}_{\mu})RP_{+}=H_{\mu},

using Theorem
2.29
in the last step.
∎

Remark 2.33
.

Choosing
p=\textbf{1}
or
p=0
, we get

\beta(\mu,\textbf{1},0)=\beta(\mu,0,0)=i\cdot{\mathcal{I}}_{\mu},

so Theorem
2.32
is a generalization of Theorem
2.29
.

Example 2.34
.

For the
\mathcal{K}

Theorem 2.35
.

Let
\mathcal{K}
be a complex Hilbert space, let
\mu
be a
\mathcal{K}
-Carleson measure and let
h\in L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}},B(\mathcal{K}))
be a symbol for the Hankel operator
H_{\mu}
. Then, for any projection
p\in B(\mathcal{K})
, the following are equivalent:

(a)

h(-x)=h(x)^{*}=-(2p-\textbf{1})h(x)(2p-\textbf{1})
for almost all
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
.

(b)

There exists
C\in B((\textbf{1}-p)\mathcal{K},p\mathcal{K})
such that
h=\beta(\mu,p,C)
.

Proof.

(a)
\Rightarrow
(b):

Writing

h=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}\qquad\text{and}\qquad 2p-\textbf{1}=\begin{pmatrix}\textbf{1}&0\\
0&-\textbf{1}\end{pmatrix}

for the decomposition
\mathcal{K}=p\mathcal{K}\oplus(\textbf{1}-p)\mathcal{K}
, we have

(2p-\textbf{1})h(2p-\textbf{1})=\begin{pmatrix}\textbf{1}&0\\
0&-\textbf{1}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\textbf{1}&0\\
0&-\textbf{1}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}a&-b\\
-c&d\end{pmatrix},

so

\begin{pmatrix}a^{*}&c^{*}\\
b^{*}&d^{*}\end{pmatrix}=h^{*}=-(2p-\textbf{1})h(2p-\textbf{1})=\begin{pmatrix}-a&b\\
c&-d\end{pmatrix}

and therefore
a=-a^{*}
,
c=b^{*}
and
d=-d^{*}
. This yields that

h=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
b^{*}&d\end{pmatrix}

with

a=-a^{*}\qquad\text{and}\qquad d=-d^{*}.

(13)

Now, by Theorem
2.29
, both functions
a=php
and
i\cdot p{\mathcal{I}}_{\mu}p
are symbols for the positive Hankel operator
pH_{\mu}p
, so, by
[

, Cor. 4.8]
, we have

(see Definition
B.17
). This yields

a-i\cdot p{\mathcal{I}}_{\mu}p\overset{(\ref{eq:adAntiSymmetry})}{=}-\left(a-i\cdot p{\mathcal{I}}_{\mu}p\right)^{*}\in H^{\infty}({\mathbb{C}}_{+},B(p\mathcal{K})).

By Lemma
B.18
(c), this implies that there exists an operator
K\in B(p\mathcal{K})
with
{K=-K^{*}}
such that

a-i\cdot p{\mathcal{I}}_{\mu}p\equiv K.

Further, for almost every
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
, one has

a(-x)=ph(-x)p=ph^{*}(x)p=a^{*}(x)\overset{(\ref{eq:adAntiSymmetry})}{=}-a(x)

and

{\mathcal{I}}_{\mu}(-x)=-{\mathcal{I}}_{\mu}(x)

by Lemma
2.25
. This yields

K=a(-x)-i\cdot p{\mathcal{I}}_{\mu}(-x)p=-\left(a(x)-i\cdot p{\mathcal{I}}_{\mu}(x)p\right)=-K,

so
K=0
and therefore

a=i\cdot p{\mathcal{I}}_{\mu}p.

By the same argument, one sees that

d=i\cdot(\textbf{1}-p){\mathcal{I}}_{\mu}(\textbf{1}-p).

Now, by Theorem
2.29
, both functions

h=\begin{pmatrix}i\cdot p{\mathcal{I}}_{\mu}p&b\\
b^{*}&i\cdot(\textbf{1}-p){\mathcal{I}}_{\mu}(\textbf{1}-p)\end{pmatrix}

and

i\cdot{\mathcal{I}}_{\mu}=\begin{pmatrix}i\cdot p{\mathcal{I}}_{\mu}p&i\cdot p{\mathcal{I}}_{\mu}(\textbf{1}-p)\\
i\cdot(\textbf{1}-p){\mathcal{I}}_{\mu}p&i\cdot(\textbf{1}-p){\mathcal{I}}_{\mu}(\textbf{1}-p)\end{pmatrix}

are symbols for the positive Hankel operator
H_{h}
. So, by
[
Pa88
, Cor. 4.8]
, we have

H^{\infty}({\mathbb{C}}_{-},B(\mathcal{K}))\ni h-i\cdot{\mathcal{I}}_{\mu}=\begin{pmatrix}0&b-i\cdot p{\mathcal{I}}_{\mu}(\textbf{1}-p)\\
b^{*}-i\cdot(\textbf{1}-p){\mathcal{I}}_{\mu}p&0\end{pmatrix}

and therefore

(h-i\cdot{\mathcal{I}}_{\mu})^{*}\in H^{\infty}({\mathbb{C}}_{+},B(\mathcal{K})).

This implies that the function

\displaystyle f

\displaystyle\coloneqq(h-i\cdot{\mathcal{I}}_{\mu})^{*}-p\mathcal{N}_{\mu}(\textbf{1}-p)-(\textbf{1}-p)\mathcal{N}_{\mu}p

\displaystyle\,=\begin{pmatrix}0&b-p\mathcal{R}_{\mu}(\textbf{1}-p)\\
b^{*}-(\textbf{1}-p)\mathcal{R}_{\mu}p&0\end{pmatrix}

is holomorphic on
{\mathbb{C}}_{+}
. Now, by

b(-x)=ph(-x)(\textbf{1}-p)=ph(x)^{*}(\textbf{1}-p)=b(x)

and Lemma
2.25
, we have

f(-x)=f(x)

for almost every
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
. Further, for every
v,w\in\mathcal{K}
and every
\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
, we have

\displaystyle\left\langle v\middle|fw\right\rangle\cdot Q_{i\lambda}

\displaystyle=\left\langle v\middle|(h-i\cdot{\mathcal{I}}_{\mu})^{*}w\right\rangle\cdot Q_{i\lambda}-\left\langle pv\middle|\mathcal{N}_{\mu}(\textbf{1}-p)w\right\rangle\cdot Q_{i\lambda}-\left\langle(\textbf{1}-p)v\middle|\mathcal{N}_{\mu}pw\right\rangle\cdot Q_{i\lambda}

\displaystyle\in H^{\infty}({\mathbb{C}}_{+})\cdot H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{+})+H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{+})+H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{+})=H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{+}),

using Proposition
2.27
for the last two terms.
Therefore, by Corollary
B.22
, the function
f
is constant, so there exists an operator
C\in B((\textbf{1}-p)\mathcal{K},p\mathcal{K})
such that

b-p\mathcal{R}_{\mu}(\textbf{1}-p)=C,

which is equivalent to

b=C+p\mathcal{R}_{\mu}(\textbf{1}-p).

We then have

h=\begin{pmatrix}i\cdot p{\mathcal{I}}_{\mu}p&C+p\mathcal{R}_{\mu}(\textbf{1}-p)\\
C^{*}+(\textbf{1}-p)\mathcal{R}_{\mu}p&i\cdot(\textbf{1}-p){\mathcal{I}}_{\mu}(\textbf{1}-p)\end{pmatrix}=\beta(\mu,p,C).

(b)
\Rightarrow
(a):

One has

\displaystyle-(2p-\textbf{1})\beta(\mu,p,C)(x)(2p-\textbf{1})

\displaystyle\qquad=-\begin{pmatrix}\textbf{1}&0\\
0&-\textbf{1}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}i\cdot p{\mathcal{I}}_{\mu}(x)p&C+p\mathcal{R}_{\mu}(x)(\textbf{1}-p)\\
C^{*}+(\textbf{1}-p)\mathcal{R}_{\mu}(x)p&i\cdot(\textbf{1}-p){\mathcal{I}}_{\mu}(x)(\textbf{1}-p)\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\textbf{1}&0\\
0&-\textbf{1}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle\qquad=\begin{pmatrix}-i\cdot p{\mathcal{I}}_{\mu}(x)p&C+p\mathcal{R}_{\mu}(x)(\textbf{1}-p)\\
C^{*}+(\textbf{1}-p)\mathcal{R}_{\mu}(x)p&-i\cdot(\textbf{1}-p){\mathcal{I}}_{\mu}(x)(\textbf{1}-p)\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle\qquad=\beta(\mu,p,C)(x)^{*}=R\beta(\mu,p,C)(-x)

using Lemma
2.25
for the last equality. ∎

3
Classifying standard and Borchers-type quadruples

In this section, we will link the results of the previous sections to standard subspaces. Throughout this section, given a complex Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
, by
\mathcal{H}^{\mathbb{R}}
we will denote the set
\mathcal{H}
interpreted as a real Hilbert space endowed with the real scalar product

\left\langle\cdot\middle|\cdot\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}^{\mathbb{R}}}\coloneqq\mathop{{\rm Re}}\nolimits\,\left\langle\cdot\middle|\cdot\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}.

We will then identify quadruples of the form
(\mathcal{H}^{\mathbb{R}},{\tt V},U,J_{\tt V})
among reflection positive orthogonal one-parameter groups under the additional assumption that
(\mathcal{H}^{\mathbb{R}},{\tt V},U)
is outgoing. We will see that these quadruples are the ones equivalent to

\left(L^{2}({\mathbb{R}},\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}})^{\sharp},H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{+},\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}})^{\sharp},S,\theta_{\beta(\mu,p,C)}\right)

for some real Hilbert space
\mathcal{M}
, some
\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}}
-Carleson measure
\mu
, some projection
p\in B(\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}})^{\sharp}
and some operator
C\in B((\textbf{1}-p)\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}},p\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}})
(cf. Definition
2.31
, Theorem
3.15
). We will further see that the quadruples arising from Borchers’ Theorem (cf. Theorem
3.21
, Definition
3.23
) are the ones corresponding to the case
d\mu(\lambda)=2\cdot d\lambda\cdot\textbf{1}
and
C=0
(cf. Theorem
3.24
, Example
2.34
).

3.1
Standard quadruples

To link standard subspaces to the results of Section
1
, we start with the following lemma:

Lemma 3.11
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a complex Hilbert space and let
{\tt V}\subseteq\mathcal{H}
be a standard subspace. Further let
(U_{t})_{t\in{\mathbb{R}}}
be a strongly continuous unitary one-parameter group satisfying

J_{\tt V}U_{t}=U_{-t}J_{\tt V}\quad\forall t\in{\mathbb{R}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad U_{t}{\tt V}\subseteq{\tt V}\quad\forall t\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}.

(14)

Then the quadruple
(\mathcal{H}^{\mathbb{R}},{\tt V},U,J_{\tt V})
is a reflection positive orthogonal one-parameter group.

Proof.

For every
v\in{\tt V}
one has

J_{\tt V}v=J_{\tt V}T_{\tt V}v=\Delta_{\tt V}^{\frac{1}{2}}v

and therefore

\left\langle v\middle|J_{\tt V}v\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=\langle v|\Delta_{\tt V}^{\frac{1}{2}}v\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\geq 0,

which implies that also

\left\langle v\middle|J_{\tt V}v\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}^{\mathbb{R}}}=\mathop{{\rm Re}}\nolimits\,\left\langle v\middle|J_{\tt V}v\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=\left\langle v\middle|J_{\tt V}v\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\geq 0.

This implies that
(\mathcal{H}^{\mathbb{R}},{\tt V},J_{\tt V})
is a real reflection positive Hilbert space. That
(\mathcal{H}^{\mathbb{R}},{\tt V},U,J_{\tt V})
is a reflection positive orthogonal one-parameter group then follows immediately from (
14
).
∎

This lemma shows that every quadruple of the form
(\mathcal{H}^{\mathbb{R}},{\tt V},U,J_{\tt V})
satisfying (
14
) is a reflection positive orthogonal one-parameter group. The question we want to answer in this subsection is which reflection positive orthogonal one-parameter groups are of this type. For this, we define:

Definition 3.12
.

Let
(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{E}_{+},U,\theta)
be a reflection positive orthogonal one-parameter group. We say that
(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{E}_{+},U,\theta)
is
standard
, if there exists a complex Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
, a standard subspace
{\tt V}\subseteq\mathcal{H}
and a unitary one-parameter group
(\widetilde{U}_{t})_{t\in{\mathbb{R}}}\subseteq\mathop{\rm U{}}\nolimits(\mathcal{H})
such that

(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{E}_{+},U,\theta)\cong(\mathcal{H}^{\mathbb{R}},{\tt V},\widetilde{U},J_{\tt V}),

i.e. if there exists an orthogonal map
\psi:\mathcal{E}\to\mathcal{H}^{\mathbb{R}}
satisfying

\psi(\mathcal{E}_{+})={\tt V},\qquad\psi\circ\theta=J_{\tt V}\circ\psi\qquad\text{and}\qquad\psi\circ U_{t}=\widetilde{U}_{t}\circ\psi\quad\forall t\in{\mathbb{R}}.

We will answer the question which reflection positive orthogonal one-parameter groups are standard under the additional assumption of them being outgoing. By Theorem
1.24
, we know that all outgoing reflection positive orthogonal one-parameter groups are – up to orthogonal equivalence – precisely the ones of the form

\left(L^{2}({\mathbb{R}},\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}})^{\sharp},H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{+},\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}})^{\sharp},S,\theta_{h}\right)

for some real Hilbert space
\mathcal{M}
and some
h\in L^{\infty}\left({\mathbb{R}},\mathop{\rm U{}}\nolimits(\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}})\right)^{\sharp,\flat}
with
H_{h}\geq 0
(Definition
1.23
). Therefore our question boils down to classifying those functions
h\in L^{\infty}\left({\mathbb{R}},\mathop{\rm U{}}\nolimits(\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}})\right)^{\sharp,\flat}
for which the above quadruple is standard. For this, we need the following definition:

Definition 3.13
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a real Hilbert space. We set

B(\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}})^{\sharp}\coloneqq\{A\in B(\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}}):\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{M}}A\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{M}}=A\}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathop{\rm U{}}\nolimits(\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}})^{\sharp}\coloneqq\mathop{\rm U{}}\nolimits(\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}})\cap B(\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}})^{\sharp}

(cf. Definition
1.13
).

One difficulty in classifying outgoing standard quadruples is that, by passing from
\mathcal{H}
to
\mathcal{H}^{\mathbb{R}}
, the complex structure on
\mathcal{H}
gets lost. If

(\mathcal{H}^{\mathbb{R}},{\tt V},\widetilde{U},J_{\tt V})\cong\left(L^{2}({\mathbb{R}},\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}})^{\sharp},H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{+},\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}})^{\sharp},S,\theta_{h}\right),

there exists a complex structure
{\mathcal{I}}
on
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}},\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}})^{\sharp}
such that
H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{+},\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}})^{\sharp}
is a standard subspace in the complex Hilbert space
(L^{2}({\mathbb{R}},\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}})^{\sharp},{\mathcal{I}})
and such that the operators
S
are complex linear with respect to
{\mathcal{I}}
. The following proposition deals with such complex structures:

Proposition 3.14
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a real Hilbert space and let
h\in L^{\infty}\left({\mathbb{R}},\mathop{\rm U{}}\nolimits(\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}})\right)^{\sharp,\flat}
. Further let
{\mathcal{I}}
be a complex structure on
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}},\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}})^{\sharp}
such that the following assertions hold:

(i)

The real Hilbert space
{\tt V}\coloneqq H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{+},\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}})^{\sharp}
is a standard subspace in the complex Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}\coloneqq(L^{2}({\mathbb{R}},\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}})^{\sharp},{\mathcal{I}})
with
J_{\tt V}=\theta_{h}
.

(ii)

For every
t\in{\mathbb{R}}
one has
S_{t}{\mathcal{I}}={\mathcal{I}}S_{t}
.

Then there exists a function
I\in L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}},\mathop{\rm U{}}\nolimits(\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}}))^{\sharp}
and a projection
p\in B(\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}})^{\sharp}
such that:

(a)

{\mathcal{I}}=M_{I}
.

(b)

H_{h}>0

(see Definition
1.23
)
.

(c)

I=h(2p-\textbf{1})
.

Proof.

(a)

By assumption, we have

S_{t}{\mathcal{I}}={\mathcal{I}}S_{t}\qquad\forall t\in{\mathbb{R}},

which, by Proposition
B.31
, is equivalent to
{\mathcal{I}}=M_{I}
for some
I\in L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}},B(\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}}))
. Since
{\mathcal{I}}
is a complex structure on
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}},\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}})^{\sharp}
, we even have
I\in L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}},\mathop{\rm U{}}\nolimits(\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}}))^{\sharp}
.

(b)

For every
f\in{\tt V}=H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{+},\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}})^{\sharp}
one has

\left\langle f\middle|H_{h}f\right\rangle_{H^{2}}=\left\langle f\middle|\theta_{h}f\right\rangle_{L^{2}}=\left\langle f\middle|J_{\tt V}f\right\rangle_{L^{2}}=\langle f|\Delta_{\tt V}^{\frac{1}{2}}f\rangle_{L^{2}}=\mathop{{\rm Re}}\nolimits\,\langle f|\Delta_{\tt V}^{\frac{1}{2}}f\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=\langle f|\Delta_{\tt V}^{\frac{1}{2}}f\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\geq 0.

If

0=\langle f|\Delta_{\tt V}^{\frac{1}{2}}f\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=\langle\Delta_{\tt V}^{\frac{1}{4}}f|\Delta_{\tt V}^{\frac{1}{4}}f\rangle_{\mathcal{H}},

then
\Delta_{\tt V}^{\frac{1}{4}}f=0
and therefore

f=T_{\tt V}f=J_{\tt V}\Delta_{\tt V}^{\frac{1}{2}}f=J_{\tt V}\Delta_{\tt V}^{\frac{1}{4}}\big(\Delta_{\tt V}^{\frac{1}{4}}f\big)=0.

We therefore have
\left\langle f\middle|H_{h}f\right\rangle_{H^{2}}>0
for every
f\in H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{+},\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}})^{\sharp}
. Since

\left(H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{+},\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}})^{\sharp}\right)_{\mathbb{C}}\cong H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{+},\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}})

this implies that
H_{h}>0
.

(c)

By Proposition
C.22
, for any standard subspace
{\tt V}\subseteq\mathcal{H}
, we have

J_{{\tt V}}{\tt V}={\tt V}^{\prime}=i{\tt V}^{\perp_{\mathbb{R}}},

where

{\tt V}^{\prime}\coloneqq\{v\in\mathcal{H}:\left(\forall w\in{\tt V}\right)\,\mathop{{\rm Im}}\nolimits\left\langle v\middle|w\right\rangle=0\}.

Applying this to the standard subspace
{\tt V}=H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{+},\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}})^{\sharp}
in the complex Hilbert space
\left(L^{2}({\mathbb{R}},\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}})^{\sharp},{\mathcal{I}}\right)
, we get

M_{I}H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{-},\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}})^{\sharp}={\mathcal{I}}{\tt V}^{\perp_{\mathbb{R}}}=J_{\tt V}{\tt V}=\theta_{h}H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{+},\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}})^{\sharp}=M_{h}H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{-},\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}})^{\sharp}.

This, by Lemma
B.18
(e), implies that
I=hu
for some
u\in\mathop{\rm U{}}\nolimits(\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}})
. The fact that
I,h\in L^{\infty}\left({\mathbb{R}},\mathop{\rm U{}}\nolimits(\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}})\right)^{\sharp}
then implies that

\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{M}}u\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{M}}=u,

so
u\in\mathop{\rm U{}}\nolimits(\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}})^{\sharp}
. Now, for
x\in{\mathbb{R}}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathcal{M}
, one has
\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{M}}x=x
and therefore

\displaystyle\left\langle x\middle|ux\right\rangle=\left\langle\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{M}}x\middle|u\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{M}}x\right\rangle=\overline{\left\langle x\middle|\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{M}}u\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{M}}x\right\rangle}=\overline{\left\langle x\middle|ux\right\rangle}=\left\langle ux\middle|x\right\rangle=\left\langle x\middle|u^{*}x\right\rangle.

Therefore, for
z\in{\mathbb{C}}
and
v\in\mathcal{M}
, we have

\displaystyle\left\langle z\otimes v\middle|u(z\otimes v)\right\rangle=|z|^{2}\left\langle 1\otimes v\middle|u(1\otimes v)\right\rangle=|z|^{2}\left\langle 1\otimes v\middle|u^{*}(1\otimes v)\right\rangle=\left\langle z\otimes v\middle|u^{*}(z\otimes v)\right\rangle,

so

\left\langle\xi\middle|u\xi\right\rangle=\left\langle\xi\middle|u^{*}\xi\right\rangle

for every
\xi\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}}
. Since, by the polarization identity, an operator is uniquely determined by its values on the diagonal, this yields
u=u^{*}
. This, together with the unitarity of
u
implies that the operator

p\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}(\textbf{1}+u)\in B(\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}})^{\sharp}

is a projection and we have
u=(2p-\textbf{1})
, so

I=hu=h(2p-\textbf{1}).\qed

With this preparation, we can prove our theorem classifying standard quadruples:

Theorem 3.15
.

(Classification of outgoing standard quadruples)

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a real Hilbert space and let
h\in L^{\infty}\left({\mathbb{R}},\mathop{\rm U{}}\nolimits(\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}})\right)^{\sharp,\flat}
. Then the following are equivalent:

(a)

The quadruple
\left(L^{2}({\mathbb{R}},\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}})^{\sharp},H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{+},\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}})^{\sharp},S,\theta_{h}\right)
is standard.

(b)

There exists a function
I\in L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}},\mathop{\rm U{}}\nolimits(\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}}))^{\sharp}
with
I^{2}=-\textbf{1}
such that
{{\tt V}\coloneqq H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{+},\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}})^{\sharp}}
is a standard subspace in the complex Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}\coloneqq(L^{2}({\mathbb{R}},\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}})^{\sharp},M_{I})
with
J_{\tt V}=\theta_{h}
.

(c)

H_{h}>0
and there exists a projection
p\in B(\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}})^{\sharp}
such that
h^{*}=-(2p-\textbf{1})h(2p-\textbf{1})
.

(d)

H_{h}>0
and there exists a projection
p\in B(\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}})^{\sharp}
and an operator
C\in B((\textbf{1}-p)\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}},p\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}})
such that
h=\beta(\mu_{H_{h}},p,C)
.

Proof.

(a)
\Leftrightarrow
(b):

By definition,
\left(L^{2}({\mathbb{R}},\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}})^{\sharp},H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{+},\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}})^{\sharp},S,\theta_{h}\right)
is standard, if and only if there exists a complex structure
{\mathcal{I}}
on
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}},\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}})^{\sharp}
such that
{{\tt V}\coloneqq H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{+},\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}})^{\sharp}}
is a standard subspace in the complex Hilbert space
(L^{2}({\mathbb{R}},\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}})^{\sharp},{\mathcal{I}})
with
J_{\tt V}=\theta_{h}
and such that, for every
t\in{\mathbb{R}}
, the operator
S_{t}
is unitary. In this case, by Proposition
3.14
(a), there exists a function
I\in L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}},\mathop{\rm U{}}\nolimits(\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}}))^{\sharp}
such that
{\mathcal{I}}=M_{I}
.

(b)
\Rightarrow
(c):

By Proposition
3.14
(b), we have

H_{h}>0
. By Proposition
3.14
(c) there exists a projection
p\in B(\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}})^{\sharp}
such that
I=h(2p-\textbf{1})
. This yields

-(2p-\textbf{1})h(2p-\textbf{1})=-h^{*}h(2p-\textbf{1})h(2p-\textbf{1})=-h^{*}II=h^{*}.

(c)
\Rightarrow
(b):

We set

u\coloneqq 2p-\textbf{1}\in\mathop{\rm U{}}\nolimits(\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}})^{\sharp}\qquad\text{and}\qquad I\coloneqq hu\in L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}},\mathop{\rm U{}}\nolimits(\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}}))^{\sharp}.

Then

I^{2}=huhu=h(2p-\textbf{1})h(2p-\textbf{1})=-hh^{*}=-\textbf{1},

so
M_{I}
is a complex structure on
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}},\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}})^{\sharp}
.

In the following
\left\langle\cdot\middle|\cdot\right\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}
denotes the real scalar product on the real Hilbert space
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}},\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}})^{\sharp}
and
\left\langle\cdot\middle|\cdot\right\rangle_{\mathbb{C}}
the complex scalar product on the complex Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}=(L^{2}({\mathbb{R}},\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}})^{\sharp},M_{I})
defined by

\left\langle f\middle|g\right\rangle_{\mathbb{C}}\coloneqq\left\langle f\middle|g\right\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}-i\left\langle f\middle|M_{I}g\right\rangle_{\mathbb{R}},\qquad f,g\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}},\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}})^{\sharp}.

We want to show that
{{\tt V}=H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{+},\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}})^{\sharp}}
is a standard subspace in the complex Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
. So let
f\in{\tt V}\cap M_{I}{\tt V}
. Then
f=Ig
for some
g\in{\tt V}
. This yields

\left\langle f\middle|H_{h}f\right\rangle_{H^{2}}=\left\langle f\middle|\theta_{h}f\right\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}=\left\langle Ig\middle|IuRf\right\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}=\left\langle g\middle|uRf\right\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}=0,

where the last equality follows from
g\in{\tt V}=H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{+},\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}})^{\sharp}
and

uRf\in H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{-},\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}})^{\sharp}=\left(H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{+},\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}})^{\sharp}\right)^{\perp_{\mathbb{R}}}.

Since
H_{h}>0
, this implies
f=0
, so

{\tt V}\cap I{\tt V}=\{0\}.

(15)

Now let
f\in({\tt V}+M_{I}{\tt V})^{\perp_{\mathbb{C}}}\subseteq({\tt V}+M_{I}{\tt V})^{\perp_{\mathbb{R}}}
. Using that

{\tt V}^{\perp_{\mathbb{R}}}=\left(H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{+},\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}})^{\sharp}\right)^{\perp_{\mathbb{R}}}=H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{-},\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}})^{\sharp}=RH^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{+},\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}})^{\sharp}=R{\tt V},

this yields

f\in({\tt V}+M_{I}{\tt V})^{\perp_{\mathbb{R}}}={\tt V}^{\perp_{\mathbb{R}}}\cap M_{I}{\tt V}^{\perp_{\mathbb{R}}}=R{\tt V}\cap M_{I}R{\tt V}.

Now
h=h^{\flat}
implies
M_{h}R=RM_{h^{*}}
, so

M_{I}R=M_{h}uR=RM_{h^{*}}u=RM_{(Iu)^{*}}u=RuM_{I^{*}}u=-RuM_{I}u.

Therefore, using that
u{\tt V}={\tt V}
, we have

f\in R{\tt V}\cap M_{I}R{\tt V}=R{\tt V}\cap RuM_{I}u{\tt V}=Ru(u{\tt V}\cap M_{I}u{\tt V})=Ru({\tt V}\cap M_{I}{\tt V})\overset{(\ref{eq:TrivialIntersection})}{=}Ru\{0\}=\{0\},

i.e.
({\tt V}+M_{I}{\tt V})^{\perp_{\mathbb{C}}}=\{0\}
, which implies
\overline{{\tt V}+I{\tt V}}=\mathcal{H}
. Therefore
{\tt V}
is a standard subspace in the complex Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
.

Finally, we show that
J_{\tt V}=\theta_{h}
. We first notice that
\theta_{h}
is an anti-unitary involution on
\mathcal{H}
since
\theta_{h}
is real linear and

\theta_{h}M_{I}=\theta_{h}M_{h}u=\theta_{h}\theta_{h}Ru=Ru=uR=-M_{I}M_{I}uR=-M_{I}\theta_{h}.

Now, for the subspace
{\tt V}^{\prime}=M_{I}{\tt V}^{\perp_{\mathbb{R}}}
(cf. Proposition
C.22
), we have

\displaystyle{\tt V}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=M_{I}{\tt V}^{\perp_{\mathbb{R}}}=M_{I}\left(H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{+},\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}})^{\sharp}\right)^{\perp_{\mathbb{R}}}

\displaystyle=M_{I}RH^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{+},\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}})^{\sharp}=M_{h}uR{\tt V}=(M_{h}R)(u{\tt V})=\theta_{h}{\tt V}.

(16)

Further, for
f\in{\tt V}=H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{+},\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}})^{\sharp}
, we have

3.2
Borchers-type quadruples

In this subsection, we will consider a class of examples of outgoing standard quadruples and see how they fit into the context of Theorem
3.15
. The class we will consider are the unitary one-parameter groups appearing in Borchers’ Theorem, which states the following:

Theorem 3.21
.

(Borchers’ Theorem, one-particle)
(cf.
[
Bo92
, Thm. II.9]
and
[
Lo08
, Thm. 2.2.1]
)

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a complex Hilbert space and
{\tt V}\subseteq\mathcal{H}
be a standard subspace. Further let
{(U_{t})_{t\in{\mathbb{R}}}\subseteq\mathop{\rm U{}}\nolimits(\mathcal{H})}
be a strongly continuous unitary one-parameter group such that

U_{t}{\tt V}\subseteq{\tt V}\qquad\forall t\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}.

If
\pm\partial U>0
then the following commutation relations hold:

\Delta_{\tt V}^{is}U_{t}\Delta_{\tt V}^{-is}=U_{e^{\mp 2\pi s}t},\qquad J_{\tt V}U_{t}J_{\tt V}=U_{-t}\qquad\forall s,t\in{\mathbb{R}}.

We start by showing that the one-parameter groups described in Borchers’ Theorem give rise to outgoing standard quadruples:

Proposition 3.22
.

(
[
Sc23
, Cor. 6.3.8]
)

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a complex Hilbert space and
{\tt V}\subseteq\mathcal{H}
be a standard subspace. Further let
{(U_{t})_{t\in{\mathbb{R}}}\subseteq\mathop{\rm U{}}\nolimits(\mathcal{H})}
be a strongly continuous unitary one-parameter group such that

U_{t}{\tt V}\subseteq{\tt V}\qquad\forall t\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}.

If
\pm\partial U>0
, then
(\mathcal{H}^{\mathbb{R}},{\tt V},U,J_{\tt V})
is an outgoing standard quadruple.

Proof.

By Lemma
3.11
, the quadruple
(\mathcal{H}^{\mathbb{R}},{\tt V},U,J_{\tt V})
is a reflection positive orthogonal one-parameter group, which by definition is standard. To show that
(\mathcal{H}^{\mathbb{R}},{\tt V},U)
is outgoing, we first assume that
\partial U>0
. Then, by Proposition
C.24
, there exists a real Hilbert space
\mathcal{M}
and a unitary operator

\psi:\mathcal{H}\to(L^{2}({\mathbb{R}},\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}})^{\sharp},M_{i\cdot\mathop{{\rm sgn}}\nolimits\cdot\textbf{1}})

such that

\psi({\tt V})=H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{+},\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}})^{\sharp}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\psi\circ U_{t}=S_{t}\circ\psi\quad\forall t\in{\mathbb{R}}.

That
(\mathcal{H},{\tt V},U,J_{\tt V})
is outgoing then follows by the Lax–Phillips Theorem (Theorem
1.16
).

If
-\partial U>0
, we notice that

\widetilde{U}_{t}\coloneqq J_{\tt V}U_{t}J_{\tt V}

defines a unitary one-parameter group with

\widetilde{U}_{t}{\tt V}^{\prime}=J_{\tt V}U_{t}{\tt V}\subseteq J_{\tt V}{\tt V}={\tt V}^{\prime}

(cf. Proposition
C.22
) and

\partial\widetilde{U}=\lim_{t\to 0}\frac{1}{it}\left(J_{\tt V}U_{t}J_{\tt V}-\textbf{1}\right)=J_{\tt V}\lim_{t\to 0}\frac{1}{-it}\left(U_{t}-\textbf{1}\right)J_{\tt V}=-J_{\tt V}\partial UJ_{\tt V}>0.

By the above discussion, we then know that
(\mathcal{H}^{\mathbb{R}},{\tt V}^{\prime},\widetilde{U})
is outgoing. This implies

\bigcap_{t\in{\mathbb{R}}}U_{t}{\tt V}=\bigcap_{t\in{\mathbb{R}}}J_{\tt V}\widetilde{U}_{t}J_{\tt V}{\tt V}=J_{\tt V}\bigcap_{t\in{\mathbb{R}}}\widetilde{U}_{t}{\tt V}^{\prime}=\{0\}

and

\overline{\bigcup_{t\in{\mathbb{R}}}U_{t}{\tt V}}=\overline{\bigcup_{t\in{\mathbb{R}}}J_{\tt V}\widetilde{U}_{t}J_{\tt V}{\tt V}}=J_{\tt V}\overline{\bigcup_{t\in{\mathbb{R}}}\widetilde{U}_{t}{\tt V}^{\prime}}=\mathcal{H},

i.e.
(\mathcal{H},{\tt V},U,J_{\tt V})
is outgoing
∎

We want to answer the question if there are outgoing standard quadruples that do not arise in this way from Borchers’ Theorem, i.e. for which the infinitesimal generator of the unitary one-parameter group
(U_{t})_{t\in{\mathbb{R}}}
does not satisfy
\pm\partial U>0
. Phrased like this, the question is obviously answered positively, since one can just take direct sums of unitary one-parameter groups with strictly positive and strictly negative generators: We consider complex Hilbert spaces
\mathcal{H}_{1},\mathcal{H}_{2}
, standard subspaces
{\tt V}_{1}\subseteq\mathcal{H}_{1}
and
{\tt V}_{2}\subseteq\mathcal{H}_{2}
and unitary one-parameter groups

that satisfy

U^{(1)}_{t}{\tt V}_{1}\subseteq{\tt V}_{1}\quad\text{and}\quad U^{(2)}_{t}{\tt V}_{2}\subseteq{\tt V}_{2}\qquad\forall t\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}

and

\partial U^{(1)}>0\qquad\text{and}\qquad-\partial U^{(2)}>0.

Then
{\tt V}\coloneqq{\tt V}_{1}\oplus{\tt V}_{2}
is a standard subspace in the complex Hilbert space

and defining the unitary one-parameter group

, the quadruple
(\mathcal{H}^{\mathbb{R}},{\tt V},U,J_{\tt V})
is outgoing standard by Proposition
3.22
. This motivates the following definition:

Definition 3.23
.

Let
\mathcal{E}
be a real Hilbert space,
\mathcal{E}_{+}\subseteq\mathcal{E}
be a closed subspace,

For all

Proof.

One has

so

t^{2}-\left(\frac{1-t}{2}\right)^{2}>0.

We therefore have

\frac{2}{1+\lambda^{2}}\cdot(t+\lambda^{2})>\left\lVert B_{t}(\lambda)\right\rVert,

which implies that

\rho_{t}(\lambda)=\frac{2}{1+\lambda^{2}}\cdot(t+\lambda^{2})\textbf{1}+\begin{pmatrix}0&B_{t}(\lambda)^{*}\\
B_{t}(\lambda)&0\end{pmatrix}

is strictly positive.
∎

Corollary 3.34
.

For all
t\in\left(\frac{1}{3},1\right]

\mu_{t}
is a
{\mathbb{C}}^{4}
-Carleson measure with
H_{\mu_{t}}>0
.

Proof.

By Proposition
3.33
the signed measure
\mu_{t}
is in fact a (positive) measure. To show that it is a
{\mathbb{C}}^{4}
-Carleson measure, we notice that the function
\rho_{t}
is bounded since it is continuous and the limits for
\lambda\downarrow 0
and
\lambda\to\infty
exist and are

\lim_{\lambda\downarrow 0}\rho_{t}(\lambda)=2t\cdot\textbf{1}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\lim_{\lambda\downarrow 0}\rho_{t}(\lambda)=2\cdot\textbf{1}.

This implies that there exists
K\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
such that

\rho_{t}(\lambda)\leq K\cdot 2\cdot\textbf{1}=K\cdot\rho_{1}(\lambda)\qquad\forall\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}.

Then, for every
f\in H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{+},{\mathbb{C}}^{4})
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\left\langle f(i\lambda)\middle|d\mu_{t}(\lambda)f(i\lambda)\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\left\langle f(i\lambda)\middle|\rho_{t}(\lambda)f(i\lambda)\right\rangle\,d\lambda\leq K\cdot\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\left\langle f(i\lambda)\middle|\rho_{1}(\lambda)f(i\lambda)\right\rangle\,d\lambda

\displaystyle=K\cdot\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\left\langle f(i\lambda)\middle|d\mu_{1}(\lambda)f(i\lambda)\right\rangle\leq K\cdot\left\lVert H_{\mu_{1}}\right\rVert\cdot\left\lVert f\right\rVert^{2},

using that
\mu_{1}
is a
{\mathbb{C}}^{4}
-Carleson measure by Example
2.210
. This, by polarization, implies that

H^{2}\left({\mathbb{C}}_{+},{\mathbb{C}}^{4}\right)^{2}\ni(f,g)\mapsto\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\left\langle f(i\lambda)\middle|d\mu_{t}(\lambda)g(i\lambda)\right\rangle

defines a continuous sesquilinear form on
H^{2}\left({\mathbb{C}}_{+},{\mathbb{C}}^{4}\right)
, i.e.
\mu_{t}
is a
{\mathbb{C}}^{4}
-Carleson measure. That
H_{\mu_{t}}>0
then follows from Proposition
A1
(a).
∎

Now, we want to show that, for
t\in\left(\frac{1}{3},1\right]
, choosing the projection

p=\begin{pmatrix}\textbf{1}_{{\mathbb{C}}^{2}}&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}\in B({\mathbb{C}}^{4}),

the function
\beta(\mu_{t},p,C_{t})
is a symbol for the Hankel operator
H_{\mu_{t}}
and satisfies

\beta(\mu_{t},p,C_{t})\in L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}},\mathop{\rm U{}}\nolimits({\mathbb{C}}^{4}))^{\sharp,\flat}.

Proposition 3.35
.

Let

t\in\left(\frac{1}{3},1\right]\qquad\text{and}\qquad p=\begin{pmatrix}\textbf{1}_{{\mathbb{C}}^{2}}&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}\in B({\mathbb{C}}^{4}).

Then

\beta(\mu_{t},p,C_{t})\in L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}},\mathop{\rm U{}}\nolimits({\mathbb{C}}^{4}))^{\sharp,\flat}

and
\beta(\mu_{t},p,C_{t})
is a symbol for the Hankel operator
H_{\mu_{t}}
.

Proof.

By Lemma
D1
(b),(c), for
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\times}
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{i}{\pi}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\frac{x}{x^{2}+\lambda^{2}}\cdot A_{t}(\lambda)\,d\lambda

\displaystyle=\mathop{{\rm sgn}}\nolimits(x)\cdot\frac{i}{\pi}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\frac{|x|}{x^{2}+\lambda^{2}}\cdot A_{t}(\lambda)\,d\lambda

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1+|x|}\begin{pmatrix}i\mathop{{\rm sgn}}\nolimits(x)\left(t+|x|\right)&0\\
0&i\mathop{{\rm sgn}}\nolimits(x)\left(t+|x|\right)\end{pmatrix}

and by Lemma
D1
(d),(g) we get

C_{t}+\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\left[\frac{\lambda}{x^{2}+\lambda^{2}}-\frac{\lambda}{1+\lambda^{2}}\right]\cdot B_{t}(\lambda)\,d\lambda=\frac{1}{1+|x|}\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{2(1-t)|x|}&-\sqrt{1-t^{2}}\\
\sqrt{1-t^{2}}&\sqrt{2(1-t)|x|}\end{pmatrix}.

We therefore have

\displaystyle\beta(\mu_{t},p,C_{t})(x)=\frac{1}{1+|x|}\cdot\begin{pmatrix}i\mathop{{\rm sgn}}\nolimits(x)\left(t+|x|\right)&0&\sqrt{2(1-t)|x|}&\sqrt{1-t^{2}}\\
0&i\mathop{{\rm sgn}}\nolimits(x)\left(t+|x|\right)&-\sqrt{1-t^{2}}&\sqrt{2(1-t)|x|}\\
\sqrt{2(1-t)|x|}&-\sqrt{1-t^{2}}&i\mathop{{\rm sgn}}\nolimits(x)\left(t+|x|\right)&0\\
\sqrt{1-t^{2}}&\sqrt{2(1-t)|x|}&0&i\mathop{{\rm sgn}}\nolimits(x)\left(t+|x|\right)\end{pmatrix}.

(18)

This immediately yields

\beta(\mu_{t},p,C_{t})(-x)=\beta(\mu_{t},p,C_{t})(x)^{*}=\mathcal{C}_{{\mathbb{R}}^{4}}\beta(\mu_{t},p,C_{t})(x)\mathcal{C}_{{\mathbb{R}}^{4}}\qquad\forall x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\times}.

Also, by direct calculation, using that

\displaystyle\left(t+|x|\right)^{2}+\sqrt{2(1-t)|x|}^{2}+\sqrt{1-t^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\left(|x|^{2}+2t|x|+t^{2}\right)+2(1-t)|x|+(1-t^{2})

\displaystyle=|x|^{2}+2|x|+1=(|x|+1)^{2},

one sees that
\beta(\mu_{t},p,C_{t})(x)
is unitary for every
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\times}
. We therefore have

\beta(\mu_{t},p,C_{t})\in L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}},\mathop{\rm U{}}\nolimits({\mathbb{C}}^{4}))^{\sharp,\flat}.

Finally, by Theorem
2.32
,
\beta(\mu_{t},p,C_{t})
is a symbol for the Hankel operator
H_{\mu_{t}}
.
∎

With this preparation, we can finally provide a class of examples of quadruples that are outgoing standard but not of Borchers-type:

Theorem 3.36
.

Let

t\in\left(\frac{1}{3},1\right)\qquad\text{and}\qquad p=\begin{pmatrix}\textbf{1}_{{\mathbb{C}}^{2}}&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}\in B({\mathbb{C}}^{4}).

Then the quadruple

\left(L^{2}({\mathbb{R}},{\mathbb{C}}^{4})^{\sharp},H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{+},{\mathbb{C}}^{4})^{\sharp},S,\theta_{\beta(\mu_{t},p,C_{t})}\right)

is outgoing standard but not of Borchers-type.

Proof.

By Corollary
3.34
we have

H_{\mu_{t}}>0

and by Proposition
3.35
we have

\beta(\mu_{t},p,C_{t})\in L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}},\mathop{\rm U{}}\nolimits({\mathbb{C}}^{4}))^{\sharp,\flat}

and that
\beta(\mu_{t},p,C_{t})
is a symbol for the Hankel operator
H_{\mu_{t}}
. By Theorem
3.15
this implies that the quadruple

\left(L^{2}({\mathbb{R}},{\mathbb{C}}^{4})^{\sharp},H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{+},{\mathbb{C}}^{4})^{\sharp},S,\theta_{\beta(\mu_{t},p,C_{t})}\right)

is outgoing standard.

On the other hand, for
t\neq 1
, by equation (
18
), one has

\beta(\mu_{t},p,C_{t})\neq i\cdot\mathop{{\rm sgn}}\nolimits\cdot\textbf{1},

so, by Theorem
3.24
, the quadruple

\left(L^{2}({\mathbb{R}},{\mathbb{C}}^{4})^{\sharp},H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{+},{\mathbb{C}}^{4})^{\sharp},S,\theta_{\beta(\mu_{t},p,C_{t})}\right)

is not of Borchers-type.
∎

Appendix A
Strict positivity of Hankel operators

In this section, given a Carleson measure
\mu
, we want to know under which conditions the corresponding Hankel operator
H_{\mu}
is strictly positive, i.e.
H_{\mu}>0
. Throughout this section, given a measure
\mu
, let

\mu=\mu_{ac}+\mu_{sc}+\mu_{pp}

by the decomposition of
\mu
into its absolutely continuous part, its singular continuous part and its pure point part. We start with some sufficient conditions for
H_{\mu}
to be strictly positive:

Proposition A1
.

Let
\mathcal{K}
be a complex Hilbert space and let
\mu
be a
\mathcal{K}
-Carleson measure. Further, assume that one of the following conditions is fulfilled:

(a)

There exists a subset
E\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
with positive Lebesgue-measure such that the Radon-Nikodym derivative

\frac{d\mu_{ac}}{d\lambda}(\lambda)\in B(\mathcal{K})

is strictly positive for almost every
\lambda\in E
.

(b)

There exists a countable set
N\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
with

\sum_{\lambda\in N}\frac{\lambda}{(\lambda+1)^{2}}=\infty

such that

\mu_{pp}(\{\lambda\})\in B(\mathcal{K})

is strictly positive for every
\lambda\in N
.

Then
H_{\mu}>0
.

Proof.

(a)

Assume that (a) holds and let
f\in H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{+},\mathcal{K})
such that
\left\langle f\middle|H_{\mu}f\right\rangle=0
. Then

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\left\langle f\middle|H_{\mu}f\right\rangle=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\left\langle f(i\lambda)\middle|d\mu(\lambda)f(i\lambda)\right\rangle\geq\int_{E}\left\langle f(i\lambda)\middle|d\mu_{ac}(\lambda)f(i\lambda)\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\int_{E}\left\langle f(i\lambda)\middle|\frac{d\mu_{ac}}{d\lambda}(\lambda)f(i\lambda)\right\rangle\,d\lambda\geq 0,

so

\int_{E}\left\langle f(i\lambda)\middle|\frac{d\mu_{ac}}{d\lambda}(\lambda)f(i\lambda)\right\rangle\,d\lambda=0.

This implies that

\left\langle f(i\lambda)\middle|\frac{d\mu_{ac}}{d\lambda}(\lambda)f(i\lambda)\right\rangle=0

for almost every
\lambda\in E
. Since
\frac{d\mu_{ac}}{d\lambda}(\lambda)
is strictly positive for every
\lambda\in E
, this implies that

f(i\lambda)=0

for almost every
\lambda\in E
. Since the zeros of non-zero holomorphic functions are discrete and therefore a Lebesgue-null set, this implies that
f=0
. We therefore have

\left\langle f\middle|H_{\mu}f\right\rangle>0

for every
f\in H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{+},\mathcal{K})\setminus\{0\}
and therefore
H_{\mu}>0
.

(b)

Assume that (b) holds and let
f\in H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{+},\mathcal{K})
such that
\left\langle f\middle|H_{\mu}f\right\rangle=0
. Then

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\left\langle f\middle|H_{\mu}f\right\rangle=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\left\langle f(i\lambda)\middle|d\mu(\lambda)f(i\lambda)\right\rangle\geq\int_{N}\left\langle f(i\lambda)\middle|d\mu_{pp}(\lambda)f(i\lambda)\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lambda\in N}\left\langle f(i\lambda)\middle|\mu_{pp}(\{\lambda\})f(i\lambda)\right\rangle\geq 0,

so

\sum_{\lambda\in N}\left\langle f(i\lambda)\middle|\mu_{pp}(\{\lambda\})f(i\lambda)\right\rangle=0.

This implies that

\left\langle f(i\lambda)\middle|\mu_{pp}(\{\lambda\})f(i\lambda)\right\rangle=0\qquad\forall\lambda\in N.

Since
\mu_{pp}(\{\lambda\})
is strictly positive for every
\lambda\in N
, this implies that

f(i\lambda)=0\qquad\forall\lambda\in N.

By Szegő’s Theorem, for every
F\in H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{+},\mathcal{K})\setminus\{0\}
, we have

\sum_{z\in F^{-1}(\{0\})}\frac{\mathop{{\rm Im}}\nolimits(z)}{\mathop{{\rm Re}}\nolimits(z)^{2}+(\mathop{{\rm Im}}\nolimits(z)+1)^{2}}<\infty

(cf.
[
RR94
, Thm. 5.13(ii)]
). But for the function
f
, since
iN\subseteq f^{-1}(\{0\})
, we get

\sum_{z\in f^{-1}(\{0\})}\frac{\mathop{{\rm Im}}\nolimits(z)}{\mathop{{\rm Re}}\nolimits(z)^{2}+(\mathop{{\rm Im}}\nolimits(z)+1)^{2}}\geq\sum_{\lambda\in N}\frac{\mathop{{\rm Im}}\nolimits(i\lambda)}{\mathop{{\rm Re}}\nolimits(i\lambda)^{2}+(\mathop{{\rm Im}}\nolimits(i\lambda)+1)^{2}}=\sum_{\lambda\in N}\frac{\lambda}{(\lambda+1)^{2}}=\infty,

so
f=0
. We therefore have

\left\langle f\middle|H_{\mu}f\right\rangle>0

for every
f\in H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{+},\mathcal{K})\setminus\{0\}
and therefore
H_{\mu}>0
.

∎

Example A2
.

(a)

We consider the
B({\mathbb{C}}^{2})
-valued measure
\mu
defined by

d\mu(\lambda)=\left[\chi_{(0,1)}(\lambda)\cdot\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}+\chi_{[1,\infty)}(\lambda)\cdot\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\right]\,d\lambda.

Then
\mu_{pp}=0
and clearly, for every
\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
, the operator

\frac{d\mu_{ac}}{d\lambda}(\lambda)

is not strictly positive, so neither (a) nor (b) are fulfilled. Now let

f=\begin{pmatrix}f_{1}\\
f_{2}\end{pmatrix}\in H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{+},{\mathbb{C}}^{2})

with
\left\langle f\middle|H_{\mu}f\right\rangle=0
. Then

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\left\langle f\middle|H_{\mu}f\right\rangle=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\left\langle f(i\lambda)\middle|d\mu(\lambda)f(i\lambda)\right\rangle=\int_{(0,1)}|f_{1}(i\lambda)|^{2}\,d\lambda+\int_{[1,\infty)}|f_{2}(i\lambda)|^{2}\,d\lambda.

This implies that

f_{1}(i\lambda)=0

for almost every
\lambda\in(0,1)

and

f_{2}(i\lambda)=0

for almost every
\lambda\in[1,\infty)
.
This yields
f_{1}=0
and
f_{2}=0
, so
f=0
. We therefore have

\left\langle f\middle|H_{\mu}f\right\rangle>0

for every
f\in H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{+},{\mathbb{C}}^{2})\setminus\{0\}
and therefore
H_{\mu}>0
.

This example shows that, for the Hankel operator
H_{\mu}
to be strictly positive, it is not necessary that either condition (a) or (b) of Proposition
A1
are fulfilled, they are just sufficient.

(b)

The last example suggests that, instead of the existence of a subset
E\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
with positive Lebesgue measure such that the Radon-Nikodym derivative of
d\mu_{ac}
is strictly positive on
E
, one should rather assume that, for every
v\in\mathcal{K}\setminus\{0\}
, there exists a subset
E_{v}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
with positive Lebesgue-measure such that the Radon-Nikodym derivative of
\left\langle v\middle|d\mu_{ac}v\right\rangle
is strictly positive on
E_{v}
. The following example though shows that this is not sufficient:

We consider the
B({\mathbb{C}}^{2})
-valued measure defined by

d\mu(\lambda)=\left|\begin{pmatrix}1\\
-\frac{1}{1+\lambda}\end{pmatrix}\right\rangle\left\langle\begin{pmatrix}1\\
-\frac{1}{1+\lambda}\end{pmatrix}\right|\,d\lambda.

Then, for every

v=\begin{pmatrix}v_{1}\\
v_{2}\end{pmatrix}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{2},

we have

\left\langle v\middle|\frac{d\mu}{d\lambda}(\lambda)v\right\rangle=\left|\left\langle\begin{pmatrix}1\\
-\frac{1}{1+\lambda}\end{pmatrix}\middle|v\right\rangle\right|^{2}=\left|v_{1}-\frac{v_{2}}{1+\lambda}\right|^{2}.

Therefore, for
v\neq 0
, we have

\left\langle v\middle|\frac{d\mu}{d\lambda}(\lambda)v\right\rangle>0

for almost every
\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
.

On the other hand we consider the function
f\in H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{+},{\mathbb{C}}^{2})
given by

f(z)=\begin{pmatrix}\left(\frac{i}{i+z}\right)^{2}\\
\frac{i}{i+z}\end{pmatrix}.

Then

f(i\lambda)=\begin{pmatrix}\left(\frac{i}{i+i\lambda}\right)^{2}\\
\frac{i}{i+i\lambda}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\left(\frac{1}{1+\lambda}\right)^{2}\\
\frac{1}{1+\lambda}\end{pmatrix}=\left(\frac{1}{1+\lambda}\right)^{2}\cdot\begin{pmatrix}1\\
1+\lambda\end{pmatrix}.

Therefore

\displaystyle\left\langle f\middle|H_{\mu}f\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\left\langle f(i\lambda)\middle|\frac{d\mu}{d\lambda}(\lambda)f(i\lambda)\right\rangle\,d\lambda

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\left|\left(\frac{1}{1+\lambda}\right)^{2}\cdot\left\langle\begin{pmatrix}1\\
-\frac{1}{1+\lambda}\end{pmatrix}\middle|\begin{pmatrix}1\\
1+\lambda\end{pmatrix}\right\rangle\right|^{2}\,d\lambda

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\left|\left(\frac{1}{1+\lambda}\right)^{2}\cdot 0\right|^{2}\,d\lambda=0,

which implies that
H_{\mu}
is not strictly positive.

After seeing some sufficient conditions for
H_{\mu}
to be strictly positive, we provide a condition for
H_{\mu}
to be not strictly positive:

Proposition A3
.

Let
\mathcal{K}
be a complex Hilbert space and let
\mu
be a
\mathcal{K}
-Carleson measure. If there exists a countable set
N\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
with

\sum_{\lambda\in N}\frac{\lambda}{(\lambda+1)^{2}}<\infty

such that the operator
\mu({\mathbb{R}}_{+}\setminus N)
is not strictly positive, then
H_{\mu}\ngtr 0
.

Proof.

Let
N\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
be a countable set with

\sum_{\lambda\in N}\frac{\lambda}{(\lambda+1)^{2}}<\infty

such that

\left\langle v\middle|\mu({\mathbb{R}}_{+}\setminus N)v\right\rangle=0

(19)

for some vector
v\in\mathcal{K}\setminus\{0\}
. Then, for the subset
iN\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
, we have

\sum_{z\in iN}\frac{\mathop{{\rm Im}}\nolimits(z)}{\mathop{{\rm Re}}\nolimits(z)^{2}+(\mathop{{\rm Im}}\nolimits(z)+1)^{2}}=\sum_{\lambda\in N}\frac{\mathop{{\rm Im}}\nolimits(i\lambda)}{\mathop{{\rm Re}}\nolimits(i\lambda)^{2}+(\mathop{{\rm Im}}\nolimits(i\lambda)+1)^{2}}=\sum_{\lambda\in N}\frac{\lambda}{(\lambda+1)^{2}}<\infty,

so, by
[
RR94
, Thm. 5.13(ii)]
, we can form the Blaschke product

B(z)\coloneqq\left(\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda\in N\\
\lambda\leq 1\end{subarray}}\frac{z-i\lambda}{z+i\lambda}\right)\cdot\left(\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda\in N\\
\lambda>1\end{subarray}}-\frac{z-i\lambda}{z+i\lambda}\right),

for which one has
B\in H^{\infty}({\mathbb{C}}_{+})\setminus\{0\}
and

B(i\lambda)=0\qquad\forall\lambda\in N.

Now, for every function
f\in H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{+})
, one has
B\cdot f\otimes v\in H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{+},\mathcal{K})
and

\displaystyle\left\langle(B\cdot f\otimes v)\middle|H_{\mu}(B\cdot f\otimes v)\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}|B(i\lambda)|^{2}\cdot|f(i\lambda)|^{2}\cdot\left\langle v\middle|d\mu(\lambda)v\right\rangle

\displaystyle\overset{(\ref{eq:NZeroSet})}{=}\int_{N}|B(i\lambda)|^{2}\cdot|f(i\lambda)|^{2}\cdot\left\langle v\middle|d\mu(\lambda)v\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lambda\in N}\mu(\{\lambda\})\cdot|B(i\lambda)|^{2}\cdot|f(i\lambda)|^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lambda\in N}\mu(\{\lambda\})\cdot 0\cdot|f(i\lambda)|^{2}=0.

This shows that
H_{\mu}
is not strictly positive.
∎

Even though, for general Hilbert spaces
\mathcal{K}
, the converse implication in the above proposition does not hold (see Example
A2
(b)), equivalence holds in the one-dimensional case
\mathcal{K}={\mathbb{C}}
:

Corollary A4
.

Let
\mu
be a
{\mathbb{C}}
-Carleson measure. Then
H_{\mu}\ngtr 0
, if and only if there exists a countable set
N\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
with

\sum_{\lambda\in N}\frac{\lambda}{(\lambda+1)^{2}}<\infty

such that

\mu({\mathbb{R}}_{+}\setminus N)=0.

Proof.

“
\Leftarrow
”:

This follows immediately from Proposition
A3
.

“
\Rightarrow
”:

Assume that
H_{\mu}
is not strictly positive, so there exists
f\in H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{+})\setminus\{0\}
such that

\left\langle f\middle|H_{\mu}f\right\rangle=0.

We now assume that
\widetilde{\mu}\coloneqq\mu_{ac}+\mu_{sc}\neq 0
. Then there exists a compact subset
K\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
such that

\widetilde{\mu}(K)>0.

Since
iK\subseteq{\mathbb{C}}_{+}
is compact and
f\neq 0
is holomorphic, the set

iK\cap f^{-1}(\{0\})

is finite. Since
\widetilde{\mu}
has no pure point part, for every
\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
, we have

\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\widetilde{\mu}(U_{\varepsilon}(\lambda))=0

and therefore also

\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\widetilde{\mu}\left(\bigcup_{\lambda\in iK\cap f^{-1}(\{0\})}U_{\varepsilon}(\lambda)\right)=0

We therefore can choose
\varepsilon>0
sufficiently small such that, for the compact set

C\coloneqq K\setminus\left(\bigcup_{\lambda\in iK\cap f^{-1}(\{0\})}U_{\varepsilon}(\lambda)\right),

one has

\widetilde{\mu}(C)>0.

Since
iC
is compact and
|f|^{2}
is continuous, the minimum

c\coloneqq\min_{\lambda\in C}|f(i\lambda)|^{2}

exists, but, by construction, the function
f
has no zeros on
iC
, so
c>0
. This implies that

\displaystyle\left\langle f\middle|H_{\mu}f\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\left|f(i\lambda)\right|^{2}\,d\mu(\lambda)\geq\int_{C}\left|f(i\lambda)\right|^{2}\,d\widetilde{\mu}(\lambda)\geq\int_{C}c\,d\widetilde{\mu}(\lambda)=c\cdot\widetilde{\mu}(C)>0,

in contradiction to our assumption
\left\langle f\middle|H_{\mu}f\right\rangle=0
. Therefore

0=\widetilde{\mu}=\mu_{ac}+\mu_{sc},

so

\mu=\mu_{pp}.

Since

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\frac{1}{1+\lambda}\,d\mu_{pp}(\lambda)=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\frac{1}{1+\lambda}\,d\mu(\lambda)<\infty,

we know that
\mu=\mu_{pp}
is supported on a countable set, so there exists a countable set
N\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
such that

\mu({\mathbb{R}}_{+}\setminus N)=0

and

\mu(\{n\})>0\qquad\forall n\in N.

If

\sum_{n\in N}\frac{n}{(n+1)^{2}}=\infty,

then, by Proposition
A1
(b), we would have that
H_{\mu}
is strictly positive in contradiction to our assumption. This implies that

\sum_{n\in N}\frac{n}{(n+1)^{2}}<\infty.\qed

Appendix B
Hardy spaces and multiplication operators

B.1
Hardy spaces on half-planes

In this subsection, we will introduce the concept of Hardy spaces and collect some basic facts about them. We will restrict ourselves mostly to Hardy spaces on the upper half-plane
{\mathbb{C}}_{+}
, but everything can be done analogously for the lower half-plane
{\mathbb{C}}_{-}
. We start with the definition of a Hardy space:

Definition B.11
.

For a holomorphic function
f:{\mathbb{C}}_{+}\to{\mathbb{C}}
, we set

\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{H^{p}}\coloneqq\sup_{y>0}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left|f(x+iy)\right|^{p}\,dx\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}\quad\text{for }1\leq p<\infty

and

\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{H^{\infty}}\coloneqq\sup_{z\in{\mathbb{C}}_{+}}\left|f(z)\right|.

Now, for
1\leq p\leq\infty
, we define the
Hardy space

H^{p}({\mathbb{C}}_{+})
by

H^{p}({\mathbb{C}}_{+})\coloneqq\{f:{\mathbb{C}}_{+}\to{\mathbb{C}}\text{\,holomorphic}:\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{H^{p}}<\infty\}.

Theorem B.12
.

(
[
RR94
, Cor. 5.24, Sec. 5.29]
)

For
1\leq p\leq\infty
, the Hardy space
H^{p}({\mathbb{C}}_{+})
is a Banach space. Further,
H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{+})
becomes a Hilbert space when endowed with the scalar product

\left\langle f\middle|g\right\rangle_{H^{2}}\coloneqq\lim_{y\downarrow 0}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\overline{f(x+iy)}g(x+iy)dx,\quad f,g\in H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{+},\mathcal{K}).

Theorem B.13
.

(
[
RR94
, Cor. 5.17, Thm. 5.19, Thm. 5.23, Sec. 5.29]
)

For
1\leq p\leq\infty
, for every
f\in H^{p}({\mathbb{C}}_{+})
, the non-tangential limit

f_{*}(x)\coloneqq\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}f(x+i\varepsilon)

exists for almost every
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
and defines a function
f_{*}\in L^{p}({\mathbb{R}},{\mathbb{C}})
with
\left\lVert f_{*}\right\rVert_{p}=\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{H^{p}}
. In particular, for the scalar product on
H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{+})
, one has

\left\langle f\middle|g\right\rangle_{H^{2}}=\left\langle f_{*}\middle|g_{*}\right\rangle_{L^{2}}\quad\forall f,g\in H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{+}).

This theorem shows that we have an isometric embedding

\iota_{p}:H^{p}\left({\mathbb{C}}_{+}\right)\to L^{p}\left({\mathbb{R}},{\mathbb{C}}\right),\quad f\mapsto f_{*}.

From now on, we will identify
H^{p}\left({\mathbb{C}}_{+}\right)
with
\iota_{p}\left(H^{p}\left({\mathbb{C}}_{+}\right)\right)\subseteq L^{p}\left({\mathbb{R}},{\mathbb{C}}\right)
and we will write
f(x)
instead of
f_{*}(x)
for
f\in H^{p}\left({\mathbb{C}}_{+}\right)
and
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
.

After considering Hardy spaces in general, we will now focus on the Hardy space
H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{+})
.
H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{+})
as a Hilbert space is an example of a so-called reproducing kernel Hilbert space, defined as follows:

Definition B.14
.

Let
X
be an arbitrary set and
\mathcal{H}
be a Hilbert space that is a subset of the complex-valued functions on
X
. We say that
\mathcal{H}
is a
reproducing kernel Hilbert space
if the linear functionals

\mathop{{\rm ev}}\nolimits_{x}:\mathcal{H}\to{\mathbb{C}},\quad f\mapsto f\left(x\right)

are continuous for every
x\in X
. Then, by the Riesz Representation Theorem, for every
x\in X
, there exists a function
Q_{x}\in\mathcal{H}
such that

f\left(x\right)=\mathop{{\rm ev}}\nolimits_{x}\left(f\right)=\left\langle Q_{x}\middle|f\right\rangle.

Lemma B.15
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a reproducing kernel Hilbert space on a set
X
. Then

\overline{\mathrm{span}\left\{Q_{x}:x\in X\right\}}=\mathcal{H}.

Proof.

Let
f\in\left\{Q_{x}:x\in X\right\}^{\perp}
. Then for every
x\in X
we have

0=\left\langle Q_{x}\middle|f\right\rangle=f\left(x\right)

and therefore
f=0
.
∎

Proposition B.16
.

(
[
ANS22
, Ex. C.2]
,
[
Sc23
, Prop. A.1.7]
)

The Hilbert space
H^{2}\left({\mathbb{C}}_{+}\right)
is a reproducing kernel Hilbert space and its reproducing kernel functions are given by the Szegö kernel

Q_{w}:{\mathbb{C}}_{+}\to{\mathbb{C}},\quad z\mapsto\frac{1}{2\pi}\cdot\frac{i}{z-\overline{w}},\qquad w\in{\mathbb{C}}_{+}.

After considering Hardy spaces of scalar-valued functions, we now want to introduce Hardy spaces with values in a Hilbert space or in the bounded operators on some Hilbert space respectively:

Definition B.17
.

(
[
RR94
, Def. 5.15]
,
[
Sc23
, Def. A.1.9]
)
Let
\mathcal{K}
be a complex Hilbert space.

(a)

For a holomorphic function
f:{\mathbb{C}}_{+}\to\mathcal{K}
, we set

\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{H^{2}}\coloneqq\sup_{y>0}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left\lVert f(x+iy)\right\rVert^{2}\,dx\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

and define

H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{+},\mathcal{K})\coloneqq\{f:{\mathbb{C}}_{+}\to\mathcal{K}\text{\,holomorphic}:\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{H^{2}}<\infty\}.

(b)

For a holomorphic function
f:{\mathbb{C}}_{+}\to B(\mathcal{K})
, we set

\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{H^{\infty}}\coloneqq\sup_{z\in{\mathbb{C}}_{+}}\left\lVert f(z)\right\rVert

and define

H^{\infty}({\mathbb{C}}_{+},B(\mathcal{K}))\coloneqq\{f:{\mathbb{C}}_{+}\to B(\mathcal{K})\text{\,holomorphic}:\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{H^{\infty}}<\infty\}.

We summarize some basic properties of vector/operator-valued Hardy spaces in the following lemma:

Lemma B.18
.

(
[
Sc23
, Lem. A.1.6, Prop. A.1.7, Cor. A.1.12, Prop. A.1.13, Cor. A.1.14]
)
Let
\mathcal{K}
be complex Hilbert space. Then the following hold:

(a)

\displaystyle\overline{{\rm span}\left\{Q_{z}\cdot v:z\in{\mathbb{C}}_{+},v\in\mathcal{K}\right\}}=H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{+},\mathcal{K}).

(b)

Let
f\in H^{\infty}({\mathbb{C}}_{+},B(\mathcal{K}))
have self-adjoint boundary values, i.e.
f(x)=f(x)^{*}
for almost every
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
. Then
f
is constant.

(c)

\displaystyle H^{\infty}({\mathbb{C}}_{+},B(\mathcal{K}))\cap H^{\infty}({\mathbb{C}}_{-},B(\mathcal{K}))=B(\mathcal{K})\cdot\textbf{1}.

(d)

Let
f\in L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}},B(\mathcal{K}))
. Then, the inclusion
M_{f}H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{+},\mathcal{K})\subseteq H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{+},\mathcal{K})
holds, if and only if
{f\in H^{\infty}({\mathbb{C}}_{+},B(\mathcal{K}))}
.

(e)

Let
f,g\in L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}},B(\mathcal{K}))
such that
f(x),g(x)\in\mathop{\rm U{}}\nolimits(\mathcal{K})
for almost all
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
. Then, one has
M_{f}H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{+},\mathcal{K})=M_{g}H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{+},\mathcal{K})
, if and only if there exists
u\in\mathop{\rm U{}}\nolimits(\mathcal{K})
such that
f=g\cdot u
.

Proof.

(a)

By Lemma
B.15
and Proposition
B.16
, we have

\overline{{\rm span}\left\{Q_{z}:z\in{\mathbb{C}}_{+}\right\}}=H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{+}).

This yields

\overline{{\rm span}\left\{Q_{z}\cdot v:z\in{\mathbb{C}}_{+},v\in\mathcal{K}\right\}}=\overline{{\rm span}\left\{Q_{z}:z\in{\mathbb{C}}_{+}\right\}}\cdot\mathcal{K}=H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{+})\cdot\mathcal{K}=H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{+},\mathcal{K}).

(b)

We start with the scalar-valued case. So let
f\in H^{\infty}({\mathbb{C}}_{+})
with
f(x)\in{\mathbb{R}}
for almost every
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
. Since
\exp:{\mathbb{C}}\to{\mathbb{C}}
maps bounded sets to bounded sets, we have

g_{\pm}\coloneqq e^{\pm if}\in H^{\infty}({\mathbb{C}}_{+})

with
|g_{\pm}(x)|=1
for almost every
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
. This implies that

|e^{\pm if(z)}|=|g_{\pm}(z)|\leq\left\lVert g_{\pm}\right\rVert_{\infty}=1\qquad\forall z\in{\mathbb{C}}_{+}.

This yields

\mathop{{\rm Im}}\nolimits(f(z))\geq 0\quad\text{and}\quad\mathop{{\rm Im}}\nolimits(f(z))\leq 0\qquad\forall z\in{\mathbb{C}}_{+}

and therefore
f(z)\in{\mathbb{R}}
for every
z\in{\mathbb{R}}
. This, by the Cauchy–Riemann equations, implies that
f
is constant.

We now consider the operator-valued case. So let
f\in H^{\infty}({\mathbb{C}}_{+},B(\mathcal{K}))
with
f(x)=f(x)^{*}
for almost every
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
. Then, for
v\in\mathcal{K}
, we define
f_{v}\in H^{\infty}({\mathbb{C}}_{+})
by

f_{v}(z)=\left\langle v\middle|f(z)v\right\rangle,\qquad z\in{\mathbb{C}}_{+}.

Then
f_{v}(x)\in{\mathbb{R}}
for almost every
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
, so by the above discussion
f_{v}
is constant. Then, by polarization, for every
v,w\in\mathcal{H}
, the map

{\mathbb{C}}_{+}\ni z\mapsto\left\langle v\middle|f(z)w\right\rangle

is constant, i.e.
f
is constant.

(c)

Let
f\in H^{\infty}({\mathbb{C}}_{+},B(\mathcal{K}))\cap H^{\infty}({\mathbb{C}}_{-},B(\mathcal{K}))
. Then
f^{*}\in H^{\infty}({\mathbb{C}}_{+},B(\mathcal{K}))
, so

\mathop{{\rm Re}}\nolimits(f)\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}(f+f^{*})\in H^{\infty}({\mathbb{C}}_{+},B(\mathcal{K}))

and

\mathop{{\rm Im}}\nolimits(f)\coloneqq\frac{1}{2i}(f-f^{*})\in H^{\infty}({\mathbb{C}}_{+},B(\mathcal{K})).

Since both
\mathop{{\rm Re}}\nolimits(f)
and
\mathop{{\rm Im}}\nolimits(f)
have self-adjoint boundary values, by (b), they are constant. This implies that also
f=\mathop{{\rm Re}}\nolimits(f)+i\mathop{{\rm Im}}\nolimits(f)
is constant.

(d)

Let
f\in H^{\infty}({\mathbb{C}}_{+},B(\mathcal{K}))
and
g\in H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{+},\mathcal{K})
. Then obviously
M_{f}g=f\cdot g
is holomorphic. Further

\displaystyle\left\lVert M_{f}g\right\rVert_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\left\lVert f\cdot g\right\rVert_{2}^{2}=\sup_{y>0}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left\lVert f(x+iy)g(x+iy)\right\rVert^{2}\,dx

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{y>0}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\infty}^{2}\cdot\left\lVert g(x+iy)\right\rVert^{2}\,dx=\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\infty}^{2}\cdot\left\lVert g\right\rVert_{2}^{2},

so
M_{f}g\in H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{+},\mathcal{K})
.

Now, for the converse, we first consider the scalar valued case. So let
f\in L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}},{\mathbb{C}})
with

M_{f}H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{+})\subseteq H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{+}).

This, in particular, implies
f\cdot Q_{i}\in H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{+})
. Therefore, by
[
RR94
, Thm. 5.19(i)]
, we have

0=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{f(x)\cdot Q_{i}(x)}{x-\overline{z}}\,dx=\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{f(x)}{(x-\overline{z})(x+i)}\,dx\quad\forall z\in{\mathbb{C}}_{+},

which, by
[
Ma09
, Sec. 3.1]
, implies that
f\in H^{\infty}({\mathbb{C}}_{+})
.

We now consider the operator-valued case, so let
f\in L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}},B(\mathcal{K}))
with

M_{f}H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{+},\mathcal{K})\subseteq H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{+},\mathcal{K}).

Then, for every
v,w\in\mathcal{K}
, defining

f_{v,w}\coloneqq\left\langle v\middle|f(\cdot)w\right\rangle\in L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}},{\mathbb{C}}),

we get

M_{f_{v,w}}H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{+})=\left\langle v\middle|M_{f}(H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{+})\otimes w)\right\rangle\subseteq\left\langle v\middle|M_{f}H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{+},\mathcal{K})\right\rangle\subseteq\left\langle v\middle|H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{+},\mathcal{K})\right\rangle\subseteq H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{+}),

so
f_{v,w}\in H^{\infty}({\mathbb{C}}_{+})
. This means that, for
z\in{\mathbb{C}}_{+}
, we can define the map

F_{z}:\mathcal{K}\times\mathcal{K}\to{\mathbb{C}},\quad(v,w)\mapsto f_{v,w}(z),

which is sesquilinear by construction. Further, for every
z\in{\mathbb{C}}_{+}
, we have

|F_{z}(v,w)|=|f_{v,w}(z)|=\left|\left\langle v\middle|f(z)w\right\rangle\right|\leq\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\infty}\cdot\left\lVert v\right\rVert\cdot\left\lVert w\right\rVert\qquad\forall v,w\in\mathcal{K},

so there exists
A(z)\in B(\mathcal{K})
with
\left\lVert A(z)\right\rVert\leq\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\infty}
such that

F_{z}(v,w)=\left\langle v\middle|A(z)w\right\rangle,\qquad\forall v,w\in\mathcal{K}.

Then

A:{\mathbb{C}}_{+}\to B(\mathcal{K}),\quad z\mapsto A(z)

is a bounded function, for which all the functions

\left\langle v\middle|A(\cdot)w\right\rangle=f_{v,w},\quad v,w\in\mathcal{K}

are holomorphic. This, by
[
Ne00
, Cor. A.III.5]
, implies that
A
is holomorphic and therefore
A\in H^{\infty}({\mathbb{C}}_{+},B(\mathcal{K}))
. Now, by construction, for every
v,w\in\mathcal{K}
and almost every
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
, we have

\left\langle v\middle|A(x)w\right\rangle=f_{v,w}(x)=\left\langle v\middle|f(x)w\right\rangle,

which implies that
f=A\in H^{\infty}({\mathbb{C}}_{+},B(\mathcal{K}))
.

(e)

By multiplying with
M_{g^{-1}}
and
M_{f^{-1}}
respectively from the left, we get

M_{g^{-1}f}H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{+},\mathcal{K})=H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{+},\mathcal{K})\quad\text{and}\quad M_{f^{-1}g}H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{+},\mathcal{K})=H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{+},\mathcal{K}).

By (d) this implies
g^{-1}f,f^{-1}g\in H^{\infty}({\mathbb{C}}_{+},B(\mathcal{K}))
. On the other hand

g^{-1}f=(f^{-1}g)^{-1}=(f^{-1}g)^{*}\in H^{\infty}({\mathbb{C}}_{-},B(\mathcal{K})),

so, by (c), we get

g^{-1}f\in H^{\infty}({\mathbb{C}}_{+},B(\mathcal{K}))\cap H^{\infty}({\mathbb{C}}_{-},B(\mathcal{K}))=B(\mathcal{K})\cdot\textbf{1}

and therefore, there exists
u\in B(\mathcal{K})
such that
f=g\cdot u
. Finally,
u\in\mathop{\rm U{}}\nolimits(\mathcal{K})
since
f
and
g
are unitary. ∎

B.2
Holomorphic functions with symmetric boundary values

In this subsection, we will see that holomorphic functions with symmetric boundary values that satisfy certain integrability conditions are constant:

Proposition B.21
.

Let
f:{\mathbb{C}}_{+}\to{\mathbb{C}}
be a holomorphic function such that, for almost every
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
, the limit

f(x)\coloneqq\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}f(x+i\varepsilon)

exists and such that
f(-x)=f(x)
holds. If further
f\cdot Q_{i\lambda}\in H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{+})
for every
\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
, then
f
is constant.

Proof.

Let
\lambda,\mu\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
. Then

(f\cdot Q_{i\lambda})\cdot Q_{i\mu}\in H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{+})\cdot H^{\infty}({\mathbb{C}}_{+})=H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{+})

by Lemma
B.18
(d) and we have

\displaystyle f(i\lambda)Q_{i\lambda}(i\lambda)Q_{i\mu}(i\lambda)

\displaystyle=\left\langle Q_{i\lambda}\middle|f\cdot Q_{i\lambda}\cdot Q_{i\mu}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(x)\cdot Q_{i\lambda}(x)\cdot Q_{i\mu}(x)\cdot\overline{Q_{i\lambda}(x)}\,dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(x)\cdot\frac{i}{2\pi(x+i\lambda)}\cdot\frac{i}{2\pi(x+i\mu)}\cdot\frac{-i}{2\pi(x-i\lambda)}\,dx

\displaystyle=\frac{i}{(2\pi)^{3}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{f(x)(x-i\mu)}{(x^{2}+\lambda^{2})(x^{2}+\mu^{2})}\,dx

\displaystyle=\frac{i}{(2\pi)^{3}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}x\cdot\frac{f(x)}{(x^{2}+\lambda^{2})(x^{2}+\mu^{2})}\,dx+\frac{\mu}{(2\pi)^{3}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{f(x)}{(x^{2}+\lambda^{2})(x^{2}+\mu^{2})}\,dx

\displaystyle=\frac{\mu}{(2\pi)^{3}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{f(x)}{(x^{2}+\lambda^{2})(x^{2}+\mu^{2})}\,dx,

(20)

where, for the last equality, we use that
f(-x)=f(x)
for almost every
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
, so the integrand of the first integral is anti-symmetric and therefore the integral vanishes. Then using that

Q_{i\mu}(i\lambda)=\frac{i}{2\pi(i\lambda+i\mu)}=\frac{1}{2\pi(\lambda+\mu)}

(21)

and therefore

Q_{i\lambda}(i\lambda)=\frac{1}{2\pi(\lambda+\lambda)}=\frac{1}{4\pi\lambda},

(22)

for
\mu\neq\lambda
, we get

\displaystyle f(i\lambda)

\displaystyle\overset{(\ref{eq:BigfiLCalc})}{=}\frac{1}{Q_{i\lambda}(i\lambda)Q_{i\mu}(i\lambda)}\cdot\frac{\mu}{(2\pi)^{3}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{f(x)}{(x^{2}+\lambda^{2})(x^{2}+\mu^{2})}\,dx

\displaystyle\overset{(\ref{eq:QiMuValue}),(\ref{eq:QiLValue})}{=}2\pi(\lambda+\mu)\cdot 4\pi\lambda\cdot\frac{\mu}{(2\pi)^{3}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{f(x)}{\mu^{2}-\lambda^{2}}\cdot\left[\frac{1}{x^{2}+\lambda^{2}}-\frac{1}{x^{2}+\mu^{2}}\right]\,dx

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda\mu(\lambda+\mu)}{\pi(\mu^{2}-\lambda^{2})}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{f(x)}{x^{2}+\lambda^{2}}-\frac{f(x)}{x^{2}+\mu^{2}}\,dx

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda\mu}{\pi(\mu-\lambda)}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{f(x)}{(x+i\lambda)(x-i\lambda)}-\frac{f(x)}{(x+i\mu)(x-i\mu)}\,dx

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda\mu(2\pi)^{2}}{\pi(\mu-\lambda)}\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(x)Q_{i\lambda}(x)\overline{Q_{i\lambda}(x)}-f(x)Q_{i\mu}(x)\overline{Q_{i\mu}(x)}\,dx

\displaystyle=\frac{4\pi\lambda\mu}{\mu-\lambda}\left[\left\langle Q_{i\lambda}\middle|f\cdot Q_{i\lambda}\right\rangle-\left\langle Q_{i\mu}\middle|f\cdot Q_{i\mu}\right\rangle\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{4\pi\lambda\mu}{\mu-\lambda}\left[f(i\lambda)\cdot Q_{i\lambda}(i\lambda)-f(i\mu)\cdot Q_{i\mu}(i\mu)\right]

\displaystyle\overset{(\ref{eq:QiLValue})}{=}\frac{4\pi\lambda\mu}{\mu-\lambda}\left[f(i\lambda)\cdot\frac{1}{4\pi\lambda}-f(i\mu)\cdot\frac{1}{4\pi\mu}\right]

\displaystyle=f(i\lambda)\cdot\frac{\mu}{\mu-\lambda}-f(i\mu)\cdot\frac{\lambda}{\mu-\lambda}.

Rearranging this equation, we get

f(i\mu)\cdot\frac{\lambda}{\mu-\lambda}=f(i\lambda)\cdot\frac{\mu}{\mu-\lambda}-f(i\lambda)=f(i\lambda)\cdot\frac{\lambda}{\mu-\lambda},

which is equivalent to
f(i\mu)=f(i\lambda)
. This shows that
f\big|_{i{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}
is constant and therefore
f
is constant.
∎

Corollary B.22
.

Let
\mathcal{K}
be a complex Hilbert space and let
f:{\mathbb{C}}_{+}\to B(\mathcal{K})
be a holomorphic function such that, for almost every
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
, the limit

f(x)\coloneqq\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}f(x+i\varepsilon)

exists in the weak operator topology and such that
f(-x)=f(x)
holds. If further

\left\langle v\middle|fw\right\rangle\cdot Q_{i\lambda}\in H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{+})\qquad\forall\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+},v,w\in\mathcal{K},

then
f
is constant.

Proof.

By Proposition
B.21
, for every
v,w\in{\mathbb{C}}
, the holomorphic function

\left\langle v\middle|fw\right\rangle:{\mathbb{C}}_{+}\to{\mathbb{C}}

is constant. This implies that also
f
is constant.
∎

B.3
Operators commuting with the shift

In this subsection, we will investigate operators on
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}},\mathcal{K})
commuting with the shift-operators. We start with the following proposition:

Proposition B.31
.

(
[
Sc23
, Prop. 2.1.9]
)

Let
\mathcal{K}
be a complex Hilbert space and consider the unitary one-parameter group
{(S_{t})_{t\in{\mathbb{R}}}\subseteq\mathop{\rm U{}}\nolimits(L^{2}({\mathbb{R}},\mathcal{K}))}
defined by

(S_{t}f)(x)=e^{itx}f(x),\qquad x\in{\mathbb{R}}.

Then

(S_{\mathbb{R}})^{\prime}\coloneqq\{A\in B(L^{2}({\mathbb{R}},\mathcal{K})):(\forall t\in{\mathbb{R}})\,AS_{t}=S_{t}A\}=\{M_{f}:f\in L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}},B(\mathcal{K}))\}.

Proof.

Considering the functions
e_{t}\in L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}},{\mathbb{C}})
with
e_{t}(x)=e^{itx}
and identifying

L^{2}({\mathbb{R}},\mathcal{K})\cong L^{2}({\mathbb{R}},{\mathbb{C}})\,\widehat{\otimes}\,\mathcal{K},

we have that

S_{t}=M_{e_{t}}\otimes\textbf{1}_{\mathcal{K}},\quad t\in{\mathbb{R}}.

If
f\in L^{1}({\mathbb{R}},{\mathbb{C}})
with

0=\int_{\mathbb{R}}e_{t}(x)\cdot f(x)\,dx=\widehat{f}(-t)\quad\forall t\in{\mathbb{R}},

then
\widehat{f}=0
and therefore
f=0
, since the Fourier transform is injective. This implies that the functions
(e_{t})_{t\in{\mathbb{R}}}
span a weakly dense subspace in
L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}},{\mathbb{C}})
. Therefore, the operators
(S_{t})_{t\in{\mathbb{R}}}
span a weakly dense subspace in

\{M_{f}:f\in L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}},{\mathbb{C}})\}\otimes{\mathbb{C}}\textbf{1}_{\mathcal{K}}\subseteq B(L^{2}({\mathbb{R}},{\mathbb{C}}))\otimes B(\mathcal{K}).

This, by
[
RD75
, Thm. 1]
, yields

\displaystyle(S_{\mathbb{R}})^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\left(\{M_{f}:f\in L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}},{\mathbb{C}})\}\otimes{\mathbb{C}}\textbf{1}_{\mathcal{K}}\right)^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\left(\{M_{f}:f\in L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}},{\mathbb{C}})\}^{\prime}\otimes({\mathbb{C}}\textbf{1}_{\mathcal{K}})^{\prime}\right)^{\prime\prime}=\left(\{M_{f}:f\in L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}},{\mathbb{C}})\}\otimes B(\mathcal{K})\right)^{\prime\prime}

using
[
Sa71
, Cor. 2.9.3]
in the last step. This implies the statement, since, identifying

L^{2}({\mathbb{R}},\mathcal{K})\cong L^{2}({\mathbb{R}},{\mathbb{C}})\,\widehat{\otimes}\,\mathcal{K},

by
[
Sa71
, Thm. 1.22.13]
, we have

\left(\{M_{f}:f\in L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}},{\mathbb{C}})\}\otimes B(\mathcal{K})\right)^{\prime\prime}=\{M_{f}:f\in L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}},B(\mathcal{K}))\}.\qed

Corollary B.32
.

Let
\mathcal{K}
be a complex Hilbert space and let
A\in B(L^{2}({\mathbb{R}},\mathcal{K}))
. Further let
P_{+}
be the orthogonal projection onto
H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{+},\mathcal{K})
and let

(S_{t}f)(x)=e^{itx}f(x),\qquad t,x\in{\mathbb{R}},f\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}},\mathcal{K}).

Then
A
commutes both with
P_{+}
and
(S_{t})_{t\in{\mathbb{R}}}
, if and only if there exists
B\in B(\mathcal{K})
such that

(Af)(x)=Bf(x),\qquad x\in{\mathbb{R}},f\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}},\mathcal{K}).

Proof.

By Proposition
B.31
, the operator
A
commutes with
(S_{t})_{t\in{\mathbb{R}}}
, if and only if
A=M_{f}
for some function
f\in L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}},B(\mathcal{K}))
. Now,
M_{f}
commutes with
P_{+}
, if and only if

M_{f}H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{+},\mathcal{K})\subseteq M_{f}H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{+},\mathcal{K})\qquad\text{and}\qquad M_{f}H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{-},\mathcal{K})\subseteq M_{f}H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{-},\mathcal{K}),

which, by Lemma
B.18
(d) is equivalent to

f\in H^{\infty}({\mathbb{C}}_{+},B(\mathcal{K}))\cap H^{\infty}({\mathbb{C}}_{-},B(\mathcal{K}))=B(\mathcal{K})\cdot\textbf{1},

using Lemma
B.18
(c) for the last equality.
∎

Corollary B.33
.

Let
\mathcal{K}
be a complex Hilbert space and let
A
be a bounded (anti-)linear operator on
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}},\mathcal{K})
. Further let
P
be the orthogonal projection onto
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}_{+},\mathcal{K})
and let

(\widehat{S}_{t}f)(x)\coloneqq f(x-t),\qquad t,x\in{\mathbb{R}},f\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}},\mathcal{K}).

Then
A
commutes both with
P
and
(\widehat{S}_{t})_{t\in{\mathbb{R}}}
, if and only if there exists a bounded (anti-)linear operator
B
on
\mathcal{K}
such that

(Af)(x)=Bf(x),\qquad x\in{\mathbb{R}},f\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}},\mathcal{K}).

Proof.

We first assume that
A
is linear and consider the Fourier transform

\mathcal{F}:L^{2}({\mathbb{R}},\mathcal{K})\to L^{2}({\mathbb{R}},\mathcal{K}),\quad(\mathcal{F}f)(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-ixp}f(p)\,dp.

Then one has

\mathcal{F}H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{+},\mathcal{K})=L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}_{+},\mathcal{K})\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathcal{F}\circ S_{t}=\widehat{S}_{t}\circ\mathcal{F}.

This implies that the operator
\widehat{A}\coloneqq\mathcal{F}^{-1}\circ A\circ\mathcal{F}
commutes with
(S_{t})_{t\in{\mathbb{R}}}
and the projection onto
H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{+},\mathcal{K})
and therefore, by Corollary
B.32
, there exists
B\in B(\mathcal{K})
such that

(\widehat{A}f)(x)=Bf(x),\qquad x\in{\mathbb{R}},f\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}},\mathcal{K}).

This immediately implies that

(Af)(x)=Bf(x),\qquad x\in{\mathbb{R}},f\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}},\mathcal{K}).

We now assume that
A
is anti-linear. Let
\sigma
be any anti-unitary involution on
\mathcal{K}
. We then define
\widetilde{A}\in B(L^{2}({\mathbb{R}},\mathcal{K}))
by

(\widetilde{A}f)(x)=\sigma(Af)(x),\qquad x\in{\mathbb{R}},f\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}},\mathcal{K}).

Then
\widetilde{A}
is linear and commutes both with
P
and
(\widehat{S}_{t})_{t\in{\mathbb{R}}}
, so there exists
\widetilde{B}\in B(\mathcal{K})
such that

(\widetilde{A}f)(x)=\widetilde{B}f(x),\qquad x\in{\mathbb{R}},f\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}},\mathcal{K}).

Defining the anti-linear operator
B\coloneqq\sigma\widetilde{B}
, this implies

(Af)(x)=Bf(x),\qquad x\in{\mathbb{R}},f\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}},\mathcal{K}).\qed

Appendix C
Standard subspaces and the affine group

In this section, we collect some basic facts about standard subspaces and representations of the affine group. For a more detailed exposition of these two topics as well as their relation to each other, we refer to
[
Lo08
]
. In the following, we present some of the results relevant to this paper.

C.1
Positive energy representations of the affine group

In this subsection, we provide some basic results of the representation theory of the affine group
\mathrm{Aff}({\mathbb{R}})
. The affine group is an example of a so-called graded group, defined as follows:

Definition C.11
.

A
graded group
is a pair
\left(G,\varepsilon_{G}\right)
of a group
G
and a group homomorphism
\varepsilon_{G}:G\to\left\{\pm 1\right\}
. We set
G_{\pm}\coloneqq\varepsilon_{G}^{-1}\left(\left\{\pm 1\right\}\right)
.

Example C.12
.

(a)

The affine group
\mathrm{Aff}({\mathbb{R}})={\mathbb{R}}\rtimes{\mathbb{R}}^{\times}
becomes a graded group with the grading

\mathrm{Aff}({\mathbb{R}})_{+}={\mathbb{R}}\rtimes{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathrm{Aff}({\mathbb{R}})_{-}={\mathbb{R}}\rtimes{\mathbb{R}}_{-}.

(b)

Given a complex Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
we consider the group
\mathrm{AU}\left(\mathcal{H}\right)
of unitary and anti-unitary operators on
\mathcal{H}
. This becomes a graded group with the grading

\mathrm{AU}\left(\mathcal{H}\right)_{+}=\mathop{\rm U{}}\nolimits(\mathcal{H})\quad\text{and}\quad\mathrm{AU}\left(\mathcal{H}\right)_{-}=\mathrm{AU}\left(\mathcal{H}\right)\setminus\mathop{\rm U{}}\nolimits(\mathcal{H}).

Now that we have the concept of a graded group, we want to define what a representation of a graded group is:

Definition C.13
.

An
anti-unitary representation
of a graded group
(G,\varepsilon_{G})
on a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
is a morphism of graded groups
{\rho:G\to\mathrm{AU}\left(\mathcal{H}\right)}
.

We are especially interested in so-called positive energy representations of the affine group, which are defined as follows:

Definition C.14
.

(a)

Given a unitary representation
{\rho:\mathrm{Aff}({\mathbb{R}})_{+}\to\mathrm{U}\left(\mathcal{H}\right)}
of the
ax+b
-group
\mathrm{Aff}({\mathbb{R}})_{+}
, we say that
\rho
is a
positive energy representation
, if, for the one-parameter group

U:{\mathbb{R}}\to\mathop{\rm U{}}\nolimits(\mathcal{H}),\quad t\mapsto\rho(t,1),

one has

0\leq\partial U\coloneqq\lim_{t\to 0}\frac{1}{it}(U_{t}-\textbf{1}).

We call
\rho
a
strictly positive energy representation
if additionally
{\ker(\partial U)=\{0\}}
.

(b)

An anti-unitary representation
{\rho:\mathrm{Aff}({\mathbb{R}})\to\mathrm{AU}\left(\mathcal{H}\right)}
of the affine group is a (strictly) positive energy representation if the restriction
{\rho\big|_{\mathrm{Aff}({\mathbb{R}})_{+}}:\mathrm{Aff}({\mathbb{R}})_{+}\to\mathrm{AU}\left(\mathcal{H}\right)}
is a (strictly) positive energy representation.

Example C.15
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a real Hilbert space. We set

\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{M}}\coloneqq L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}_{+},\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}}).

Then the map

\rho_{\mathcal{M}}:\mathrm{Aff}({\mathbb{R}})\to\mathrm{AU}(\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{M}})

with

(\rho_{\mathcal{M}}(t,1)f)(x)\coloneqq e^{itx}f(x),\quad(\rho_{\mathcal{M}}(0,e^{s})f)(x)\coloneqq e^{\frac{s}{2}}f(e^{s}x)\qquad s,t\in{\mathbb{R}}

and

(\rho_{\mathcal{M}}(0,-1)f)(x)\coloneqq i\cdot\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{M}}f(x)

defines a strictly positive energy representation of the affine group
\mathrm{Aff}({\mathbb{R}})
.

The following proposition shows that, in fact, every strictly positive energy representation of
\mathrm{Aff}({\mathbb{R}})
is of this form:

Proposition C.16
.

Let
(\rho,\mathcal{H})
be a strictly positive energy representation of the affine group
\mathrm{Aff}({\mathbb{R}})
. Then there exists a real Hilbert space
\mathcal{M}
such that
(\rho,\mathcal{H})
is equivalent to the anti-unitary representation
(\rho_{\mathcal{M}},\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{M}})
. Further, the representation
(\rho_{\mathcal{M}},\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{M}})
is irreducible, if and only if
\mathcal{M}={\mathbb{R}}
.

Proof.

By
[
Lo08
, Thm. 1.6.1]
there exists – up to equivalence – exactly one irreducible unitary representation of
\mathrm{Aff}({\mathbb{R}})_{+}
with strictly positive energy. By
[
NÓ17
, Sec. 2.4.1]
this representation is given by the restriction of
(\rho_{\mathbb{R}},\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{R}})
to
\mathrm{Aff}({\mathbb{R}})_{+}
. Further, by
[
Lo08
, Thm. 1.6.1]
, every unitary representation of
\mathrm{Aff}({\mathbb{R}})_{+}
with strictly positive energy is a multiple of this unique irreducible representation and therefore equivalent to the restriction of
(\rho_{\mathcal{M}},\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{M}})
to
\mathrm{Aff}({\mathbb{R}})_{+}
for some real Hilbert space
\mathcal{M}
.

Now, by
[
Lo08
, Thm. 1.6.3]
, for every unitary representation of
\mathrm{Aff}({\mathbb{R}})_{+}
, there exists – up to equivalence – just one unique extension to an anti-unitary representation of
\mathrm{Aff}({\mathbb{R}})
on the same Hilbert space. Therefore, every anti-unitary representation of
\mathrm{Aff}({\mathbb{R}})
is equivalent to
(\rho_{\mathcal{M}},\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{M}})
for some real Hilbert space
\mathcal{M}
. Finally, by
[
Lo08
, Thm. 1.6.3]
the representation
\rho_{\mathcal{M}}
is irreducible, if and only if its restriction to
\mathrm{Aff}({\mathbb{R}})_{+}
is irreducible, which is the case, if and only if
\mathcal{M}={\mathbb{R}}
.
∎

C.2
Standard subspaces

In this subsection, we introduce standard subspaces and their modular objects and show how Borchers’ Theorem links to positive energy representations of the affine group. We start with the definition of a standard subspace:

Definition C.21
.

(
[
Lo08
, Sec. 2.1]
)
Let
\mathcal{H}
be a complex Hilbert space. A real subspace
{\tt V}\subseteq\mathcal{H}
is called a
standard subspace
, if

{\tt V}\cap i{\tt V}=\{0\}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\overline{{\tt V}+i{\tt V}}=\mathcal{H}.

Given a standard subspace
{\tt V}\subseteq\mathcal{H}
, we consider the densely defined
Tomita operator

T_{\tt V}:{\tt V}+i{\tt V}\to\mathcal{H},\quad x+iy\mapsto x-iy.

Then, by polar decomposition

T_{\tt V}=J_{\tt V}\Delta_{\tt V}^{\frac{1}{2}}

one obtains an anti-unitary involution
J_{\tt V}
and the positive densely defined operator
\Delta_{\tt V}
. We refer to this pair
(\Delta_{\tt V},J_{\tt V})
as the
pair of modular objects
of
{\tt V}
.

The following proposition shows that standard subspaces always come in pairs:

Proposition C.22
.

(
[
Lo08
, Prop. 2.1.3, Thm. 2.1.4]
)

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a complex Hilbert space and let
{\tt V}\subseteq\mathcal{H}
be a standard subspace. Then its symplectic complement

{\tt V}^{\prime}\coloneqq i{\tt V}^{\perp_{\mathbb{R}}}=\{w\in\mathcal{H}:(\forall v\in{\tt V})\mathop{{\rm Im}}\nolimits\left\langle v\middle|w\right\rangle=0\}

is also a standard subspace satisfying:

(a)

{\tt V}^{\prime\prime}={\tt V}
.

(b)

J_{{\tt V}^{\prime}}=J_{\tt V}
.

(c)

\Delta_{{\tt V}^{\prime}}=\Delta_{\tt V}^{-1}
.

(d)

J_{\tt V}{\tt V}={\tt V}^{\prime}
.

A classical result in the context of standard subspaces is Borchers’ Theorem, which states the following:

Theorem C.23
.

(Borchers’ Theorem, one-particle)
(cf.
[
Bo92
, Thm. II.9]
and
[
Lo08
, Thm. 2.2.1]
)

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a complex Hilbert space and
{\tt V}\subseteq\mathcal{H}
be a standard subspace. Further let
{(U_{t})_{t\in{\mathbb{R}}}\subseteq\mathop{\rm U{}}\nolimits(\mathcal{H})}
be a strongly continuous unitary one-parameter group such that

U_{t}{\tt V}\subseteq{\tt V}\qquad\forall t\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}.

If
\pm\partial U>0
then the following commutation relations hold:

\Delta_{\tt V}^{is}U_{t}\Delta_{\tt V}^{-is}=U_{e^{\mp 2\pi s}t},\qquad J_{\tt V}U_{t}J_{\tt V}=U_{-t}\qquad\forall s,t\in{\mathbb{R}}.

In the following proposition, we show that the one-parameter groups described in Borchers’ Theorem can be realized on the
L^{2}
-space on the real line by using our results about the representation theory of the affine group obtained in Appendix
C.1
:

Proposition C.24
.

(
[
Sc23
, Cor. 6.3.4]
)

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a complex Hilbert space and
{\tt V}\subseteq\mathcal{H}
be a standard subspace. Further let
{(U_{t})_{t\in{\mathbb{R}}}\subseteq\mathop{\rm U{}}\nolimits(\mathcal{H})}
be a strongly continuous unitary one-parameter group such that

U_{t}{\tt V}\subseteq{\tt V}\qquad\forall t\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}.

If
\partial U>0
, then there exists a real Hilbert space
\mathcal{M}
and a unitary operator

\psi:\mathcal{H}\to(L^{2}({\mathbb{R}},\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}})^{\sharp},M_{i\cdot\mathop{{\rm sgn}}\nolimits\cdot\textbf{1}})

such that

\psi({\tt V})=H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{+},\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}})^{\sharp},\qquad\psi\circ J_{\tt V}=\theta_{i\cdot\mathop{{\rm sgn}}\nolimits\cdot\textbf{1}}\circ\psi,\qquad\psi\circ U_{t}=S_{t}\circ\psi\quad\forall t\in{\mathbb{R}}

and

\big(\psi\Delta_{\tt V}^{-\frac{is}{2\pi}}\psi^{-1}f\big)(x)=e^{\frac{s}{2}}f(e^{s}x)\quad\forall s\in{\mathbb{R}}.

Proof.

The commutation relations in Borchers’ Theorem imply that the map

\rho:\mathrm{Aff}({\mathbb{R}})\to\mathrm{AU}(\mathcal{H})

with

\rho(t,1)=U_{t},\quad\rho(0,e^{s})=\Delta_{\tt V}^{-\frac{is}{2\pi}}\quad\text{and}\quad\rho(0,-1)=J_{\tt V},\qquad s,t\in{\mathbb{R}},

defines a strictly positive energy representation of the affine group
\mathrm{Aff}({\mathbb{R}})
(cf. Appendix
C.1
). Then, by Proposition
C.16
, without loss of generality, we can assume that, for some real Hilbert space
\mathcal{M}
, we have

\mathcal{H}=L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}_{+},\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}}),\qquad U_{t}=S_{t}\quad\forall t\in{\mathbb{R}},\qquad(J_{\tt V}f)(x)=i\cdot\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{M}}f(x)

and

\big(\Delta_{\tt V}^{-\frac{is}{2\pi}}f\big)(x)=e^{\frac{s}{2}}f(e^{s}x)\quad\forall s\in{\mathbb{R}}.

(23)

Now, we consider the set

\Omega\coloneqq\left\{z\in{\mathbb{C}}:-\frac{\pi}{2}<\mathop{{\rm Im}}\nolimits(z)<\frac{3\pi}{2}\right\}

and the functions
Q_{z}\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}_{+},{\mathbb{C}})
,
z\in{\mathbb{C}}\setminus{\mathbb{R}}
, defined by

Q_{z}(x)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\cdot\frac{i}{x-\overline{z}},\qquad x\in{\mathbb{R}}

(cf. Proposition
B.16
).

Then, for
v\in\mathcal{M}\cong{\mathbb{R}}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathcal{M}
and
\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
, we define the maps

F_{\lambda,v}^{\pm}:\Omega\to\mathcal{H},\quad s\mapsto\exp\left(\mp\frac{is}{2\pi}\log(\Delta_{\tt V})\right)Q_{\pm i\lambda}v

and

G_{\lambda,v}^{\pm}:\Omega\to\mathcal{H},\quad s\mapsto e^{\pm\frac{s}{2}}Q_{\pm i\lambda}(e^{\pm s}\cdot)v.

The functions
F_{\lambda,v}^{\pm}
and
G_{\lambda,v}^{\pm}
are holomorphic and coincide on
{\mathbb{R}}
by Equation (
23
), which implies that they are equal. This especially implies that

\Delta_{\tt V}^{\pm\frac{1}{2}}Q_{\pm i\lambda}v=F_{\lambda,v}^{\pm}(\pm i\pi)=G_{\lambda,v}^{\pm}(\pm i\pi)=\pm iRQ_{\pm i\lambda}v=\pm i\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{M}}Q_{\pm i\lambda}v=\pm J_{\tt V}Q_{\pm i\lambda}v

and therefore

J_{\tt V}\Delta_{\tt V}^{\pm\frac{1}{2}}Q_{\pm i\lambda}v=\pm Q_{\pm i\lambda}v,

so

H_{\pm}\coloneqq\overline{{\rm span}\{Q_{\pm i\lambda}v:\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+},v\in\mathcal{M}\}}\subseteq\ker\big(J_{\tt V}\Delta_{\tt V}^{\pm\frac{1}{2}}\mp\textbf{1}\big).

Then, using that

\ker\big(J_{\tt V}\Delta_{\tt V}^{\frac{1}{2}}-\textbf{1}\big)={\tt V}

and

\ker\big(J_{\tt V}\Delta_{\tt V}^{-\frac{1}{2}}+\textbf{1}\big)=i\ker\big(J_{\tt V}\Delta_{\tt V}^{-\frac{1}{2}}-\textbf{1}\big)=i\ker\big(J_{{\tt V}^{\prime}}\Delta_{{\tt V}^{\prime}}^{\frac{1}{2}}-\textbf{1}\big)=i{\tt V}^{\prime}={\tt V}^{\perp_{\mathbb{R}}},

we get

H_{+}\subseteq{\tt V}\qquad\text{and}\qquad H_{-}\subseteq{\tt V}^{\perp_{\mathbb{R}}}.

(24)

We now consider the unitary map

\Psi:L^{2}\left({\mathbb{R}}_{+},\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}}\right)\to(L^{2}({\mathbb{R}},\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}})^{\sharp},M_{i\cdot\mathop{{\rm sgn}}\nolimits\cdot\textbf{1}}),\quad(\Psi f)(x)\coloneqq\begin{cases}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}f(x)&\text{if }x>0\\[3.22916pt]
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{M}}f(-x)&\text{if }x<0.\end{cases}

Then,

\Psi(H_{\pm})=\overline{{\rm span}\{Q_{\pm i\lambda}v:\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+},v\in\mathcal{M}\}}=H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{\pm},\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}})^{\sharp},

where the inclusion
\overline{{\rm span}\{Q_{\pm i\lambda}v:\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+},v\in\mathcal{M}\}}\subseteq H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{\pm},\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}})^{\sharp}

follows by

(Q_{\pm i\lambda}v)^{\sharp}(z)=\overline{\frac{1}{2\pi}\cdot\frac{i}{-z-\overline{\pm i\lambda}}}\cdot\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{M}}v=\frac{1}{2\pi}\cdot\frac{i}{z-\overline{\pm i\lambda}}\cdot v=(Q_{\pm i\lambda}v)(z)

and the equality, since, for
f\in H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{\pm},\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}})^{\sharp}\cap\overline{{\rm span}\{Q_{\pm i\lambda}v:\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+},v\in\mathcal{M}\}}^{\perp}
, one has

0=\left\langle\pm Q_{\pm i\lambda}v\middle|f\right\rangle=\left\langle v\middle|f(\pm i\lambda)\right\rangle\qquad\forall\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+},v\in\mathcal{M}

and therefore
f\big|_{\pm{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\equiv 0
, which implies
f=0
(cf. Lemma
B.15
, Lemma
B.18
(a)). Then, by (
24
), we have

\displaystyle H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{+},\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}})^{\sharp}

\displaystyle=\Psi(H_{+})\subseteq\Psi({\tt V})=\left(\Psi({\tt V}^{\perp_{\mathbb{R}}})\right)^{\perp_{\mathbb{R}}}

\displaystyle\subseteq\left(\Psi(H_{-})\right)^{\perp_{\mathbb{R}}}=\left(H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{+},\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}})^{\sharp}\right)^{\perp_{\mathbb{R}}}=H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{+},\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}})^{\sharp}

and therefore

{\tt V}=H^{2}({\mathbb{C}}_{+},\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{C}})^{\sharp}.

Finally, we have

(\Psi J_{\tt V}\Psi^{-1}f)(x)=i\cdot\mathop{{\rm sgn}}\nolimits(x)\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{M}}f(x)=i\cdot\mathop{{\rm sgn}}\nolimits(x)Rf^{\sharp}(x)=\theta_{i\cdot\mathop{{\rm sgn}}\nolimits\cdot\textbf{1}}f(x).\qed

Appendix D
Evaluating some integrals

In this section, we will evaluate some concrete integrals appearing in the proof of Lemma
2.26
and Proposition
3.35
:

Lemma D1
.

The following equations hold for all
x\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
:

(a)

\displaystyle\frac{2}{\pi}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\frac{x}{x^{2}+\lambda^{2}}\,d\lambda=1
.

(b)

\displaystyle\frac{2}{\pi}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\frac{x}{x^{2}+\lambda^{2}}\cdot\frac{1}{1+\lambda^{2}}\,d\lambda=\frac{1}{1+x}
.

(c)

\displaystyle\frac{2}{\pi}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\frac{x}{x^{2}+\lambda^{2}}\cdot\frac{\lambda^{2}}{1+\lambda^{2}}\,d\lambda=\frac{x}{1+x}
.

(d)

\displaystyle\frac{2}{\pi}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\left[\frac{\lambda}{x^{2}+\lambda^{2}}-\frac{\lambda}{1+\lambda^{2}}\right]\cdot\frac{\lambda}{1+\lambda^{2}}\,d\lambda=\frac{1}{1+x}-\frac{1}{2}
.

(e)

\displaystyle\frac{2}{\pi}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\frac{(\lambda-1)\sqrt{\lambda}}{\lambda\left(x^{2}+\lambda^{2}\right)}\,d\lambda=\frac{\sqrt{2x}(x-1)}{x^{2}}
.

(f)

\displaystyle\frac{2}{\pi}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\frac{\lambda}{x^{2}+\lambda^{2}}\cdot\frac{\sqrt{\lambda}(\lambda-1)}{1+\lambda^{2}}\,d\lambda=\sqrt{2}\cdot\frac{\sqrt{x}}{1+x}
.

(g)

\displaystyle\frac{2}{\pi}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\left[\frac{\lambda}{x^{2}+\lambda^{2}}-\frac{\lambda}{1+\lambda^{2}}\right]\cdot\frac{\sqrt{\lambda}(\lambda-1)}{1+\lambda^{2}}\,d\lambda=\sqrt{2}\cdot\left[\frac{\sqrt{x}}{1+x}-\frac{1}{2}\right]
.

(h)

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\frac{\lambda(x^{2}-1)}{(\lambda^{2}+x^{2})(\lambda^{2}+1)}\,d\lambda=\log(x).

Proof.

(a)

For every
x\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
one has

\displaystyle\frac{2}{\pi}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\frac{x}{x^{2}+\lambda^{2}}\,d\lambda

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\pi}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\frac{x}{x^{2}+(xy)^{2}}x\,dy=\frac{2}{\pi}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\frac{1}{1+y^{2}}\,dy

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\pi}\left[\arctan(y)\right]_{0}^{\infty}=\frac{2}{\pi}\cdot\frac{\pi}{2}=1.

(b)

For every
x\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\setminus\{1\}
one has

\displaystyle\frac{2}{\pi}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\frac{x}{x^{2}+\lambda^{2}}\cdot\frac{1}{1+\lambda^{2}}\,d\lambda

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\pi}\frac{x}{1-x^{2}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\frac{1}{x^{2}+\lambda^{2}}-\frac{1}{1+\lambda^{2}}\,d\lambda

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1-x^{2}}\left[\frac{2}{\pi}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\frac{x}{x^{2}+\lambda^{2}}\,d\lambda-x\cdot\frac{2}{\pi}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\frac{1}{1+\lambda^{2}}\,d\lambda\right]

\displaystyle\overset{\mathrm{(a)}}{=}\frac{1}{1-x^{2}}\left[1-x\right]=\frac{1}{1+x}.

By the continuity of both sides in
x
, the equality also holds for
x=1
.

(c)

For every
x\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
one has

\displaystyle\frac{2}{\pi}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\frac{x}{x^{2}+\lambda^{2}}\cdot\frac{\lambda^{2}}{1+\lambda^{2}}\,d\lambda

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\pi}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\frac{x}{x^{2}+\lambda^{2}}\cdot\left[1-\frac{1}{1+\lambda^{2}}\right]\,d\lambda

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\pi}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\frac{x}{x^{2}+\lambda^{2}}\,d\lambda-\frac{2}{\pi}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\frac{x}{x^{2}+\lambda^{2}}\cdot\frac{1}{1+\lambda^{2}}\,d\lambda

\displaystyle\overset{\mathrm{(a)},\mathrm{(b)}}{=}1-\frac{1}{1+x}=\frac{x}{1+x}.

(d)

For every
x\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
one has

\displaystyle\frac{2}{\pi}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\left[\frac{\lambda}{x^{2}+\lambda^{2}}-\frac{\lambda}{1+\lambda^{2}}\right]\cdot\frac{\lambda}{1+\lambda^{2}}\,d\lambda

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad=\frac{1}{x}\cdot\frac{2}{\pi}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\frac{x}{x^{2}+\lambda^{2}}\cdot\frac{\lambda^{2}}{1+\lambda^{2}}\,d\lambda-\frac{2}{\pi}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\frac{1}{1+\lambda^{2}}\cdot\frac{\lambda^{2}}{1+\lambda^{2}}\,d\lambda

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\overset{\mathrm{(c)}}{=}\frac{1}{x}\cdot\frac{x}{1+x}-\frac{1}{1+1}=\frac{1}{1+x}-\frac{1}{2}.

(e)

One has

\displaystyle 1+t^{2}\pm\sqrt{2}t=\frac{1}{2}\left[2+2t^{2}\pm 2\sqrt{2}t\right]=\frac{1}{2}\left[1+\left(\sqrt{2}t\pm 1\right)^{2}\right].

This yields

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\frac{1}{1+t^{2}\pm\sqrt{2}t}\,dt

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\frac{1}{1+\left(\sqrt{2}t\pm 1\right)^{2}}\,dt

\displaystyle=\left[\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\arctan\left(\sqrt{2}t\pm 1\right)\right]_{0}^{\infty}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left[\frac{\pi}{2}-\arctan(\pm 1)\right].

(25)

We then have

\displaystyle 1+t^{4}

\displaystyle=\left(1+t^{2}\right)^{2}-2t^{2}=\left(1+t^{2}+\sqrt{2}t\right)\left(1+t^{2}-\sqrt{2}t\right),

so

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\frac{1+t^{2}}{1+t^{4}}\,dt

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\frac{1}{1+t^{2}+\sqrt{2}t}+\frac{1}{1+t^{2}-\sqrt{2}t}\,dt

\displaystyle\overset{(\ref{eq:firstBigIntegral})}{=}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left[\frac{\pi}{2}-\arctan(1)\right]+\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left[\frac{\pi}{2}-\arctan(-1)\right]=\frac{\pi}{\sqrt{2}}.

(26)

For
x\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
one has

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\frac{xt^{2}-1}{1+t^{4}}\,dt

\displaystyle\overset{t=\frac{1}{y}}{=}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\frac{x\frac{1}{y^{2}}-1}{1+\frac{1}{y^{4}}}\frac{1}{y^{2}}\,dy=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\frac{x-y^{2}}{y^{4}+1}\,dy,

so

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\frac{xt^{2}-1}{1+t^{4}}\,dt

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left[\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\frac{xt^{2}-1}{1+t^{4}}\,dt+\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\frac{x-y^{2}}{y^{4}+1}\,dy\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\frac{(x-1)\left(1+t^{2}\right)}{1+t^{4}}\,dt

\displaystyle=\frac{x-1}{2}\cdot\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\frac{1+t^{2}}{1+t^{4}}\,dt\overset{(\ref{eq:secondBigIntegral})}{=}\frac{x-1}{2}\cdot\frac{\pi}{\sqrt{2}}.

(27)

This yields

\displaystyle\frac{2}{\pi}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\frac{(\lambda-1)\sqrt{\lambda}}{\lambda\left(x^{2}+\lambda^{2}\right)}\,d\lambda

\displaystyle\overset{\lambda=xt^{2}}{=}\frac{2}{\pi}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\frac{(xt^{2}-1)\sqrt{x}t}{xt^{2}\left(x^{2}+x^{2}t^{4}\right)}2xt\,dt

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\pi}\cdot\frac{2\sqrt{x}}{x^{2}}\cdot\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\frac{xt^{2}-1}{1+t^{4}}\,dt

\displaystyle\overset{(\ref{eq:thirdBigIntegral})}{=}\frac{2}{\pi}\cdot\frac{2\sqrt{x}}{x^{2}}\cdot\frac{x-1}{2}\cdot\frac{\pi}{\sqrt{2}}=\frac{\sqrt{2x}(x-1)}{x^{2}}.

(f)

For every
x\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\setminus\{1\}
one has

\displaystyle\frac{2}{\pi}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\frac{\lambda}{x^{2}+\lambda^{2}}\cdot\frac{\sqrt{\lambda}(\lambda-1)}{1+\lambda^{2}}\,d\lambda

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad=\frac{2}{\pi}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\frac{\lambda^{2}\left(x^{2}-1\right)}{\left(x^{2}+\lambda^{2}\right)\left(1+\lambda^{2}\right)}\cdot\frac{\sqrt{\lambda}(\lambda-1)}{\lambda\left(x^{2}-1\right)}\,d\lambda

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad=\frac{2}{\pi}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\left[\frac{x^{2}}{x^{2}+\lambda^{2}}-\frac{1}{1+\lambda^{2}}\right]\cdot\frac{\sqrt{\lambda}(\lambda-1)}{\lambda\left(x^{2}-1\right)}\,d\lambda

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad=\frac{x^{2}}{x^{2}-1}\cdot\frac{2}{\pi}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\frac{(\lambda-1)\sqrt{\lambda}}{\lambda\left(x^{2}+\lambda^{2}\right)}\,d\lambda-\frac{1}{x^{2}-1}\cdot\frac{2}{\pi}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\frac{(\lambda-1)\sqrt{\lambda}}{\lambda\left(1+\lambda^{2}\right)}\,d\lambda

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\overset{\mathrm{(e)}}{=}\frac{x^{2}}{x^{2}-1}\cdot\frac{\sqrt{2x}(x-1)}{x^{2}}-\frac{1}{x^{2}-1}\cdot\frac{\sqrt{2\cdot 1}(1-1)}{1^{2}}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad=\frac{\sqrt{2x}(x-1)}{x^{2}-1}-0=\sqrt{2}\cdot\frac{\sqrt{x}}{1+x}.

By the continuity of both sides in
x
, the equality also holds for
x=1
.

(g)

For every
x\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
, one has

\displaystyle\frac{2}{\pi}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\left[\frac{\lambda}{x^{2}+\lambda^{2}}-\frac{\lambda}{1+\lambda^{2}}\right]\cdot\frac{\sqrt{\lambda}(\lambda-1)}{1+\lambda^{2}}\,d\lambda

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad=\frac{2}{\pi}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\frac{\lambda}{x^{2}+\lambda^{2}}\cdot\frac{\sqrt{\lambda}(\lambda-1)}{1+\lambda^{2}}\,d\lambda-\frac{2}{\pi}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\frac{\lambda}{1+\lambda^{2}}\cdot\frac{\sqrt{\lambda}(\lambda-1)}{1+\lambda^{2}}\,d\lambda

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\overset{\mathrm{(f)}}{=}\sqrt{2}\cdot\frac{\sqrt{x}}{1+x}-\sqrt{2}\cdot\frac{\sqrt{1}}{1+1}=\sqrt{2}\cdot\left[\frac{\sqrt{x}}{1+x}-\frac{1}{2}\right].

(h)

For every
x\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
, one has

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\frac{\lambda(x^{2}-1)}{(\lambda^{2}+x^{2})(\lambda^{2}+1)}\,d\lambda

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{2\lambda}{\lambda^{2}+x^{2}}-\frac{2\lambda}{\lambda^{2}+1}\,d\lambda

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\left[\log(\lambda^{2}+x^{2})-\log(\lambda^{2}+1)\right]_{0}^{\infty}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\left[\log\left(\frac{\lambda^{2}+x^{2}}{\lambda^{2}+1}\right)\right]_{0}^{\infty}=\frac{1}{2}\log(x^{2})=\log(x).\qed
