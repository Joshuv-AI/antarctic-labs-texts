Fast Tensor Completion via Approximate Richardson Iteration
Mehrdad Ghadiri, Matthew Fahrbach, Yunbum Kook, Ali Jadbabaie

Abstract
We study tensor completion (TC) through the lens of low-rank tensor decomposition (TD). Many TD algorithms use fast alternating minimization methods, which solve highly structured linear regression problems at each step (e.g., for CP, Tucker, and tensor-train decompositions). However, such algebraic structure is lost in TC regression problems, making direct extensions unclear. To address this, we propose a lifting approach that approximately solves TC regression problems using structured TD regression algorithms as blackbox subroutines, enabling sublinear-time methods. We theoretically analyze the convergence rate of our approximate Richardson iteration-based algorithm, and we demonstrate on real-world tensors that its running time can be 100x faster than direct methods for CP completion.

Introduction

Tensor completion
(TC) is the higher-order generalization of matrix completion.
It has many applications across data mining, machine learning, signal processing, and statistics
(see
Song et al. 2019
for a detailed survey of applications).
In this problem, we are given a partially observed tensor as input
(i.e., only a subset of its entries is known),
and the goal is to impute the missing values.
Under low-rank and other statistical assumptions,
we can hope to recover the missing values by minimizing a loss function over only the observed entries.

Consider a tensor
\bm{\mathscr{X}}\in\mathbb{R}^{I_{1}\times\cdots\times I_{N}}

with a subset of observed indices
\Omega\subseteq[I_{1}]\times\cdots\times[I_{N}]
.
The general TC problem is

\min_{\bm{\theta}}\,\mathcal{L}_{\Omega}(\widehat{\bm{\mathscr{X}}}(\bm{\theta}),\bm{\mathscr{X}})+\mathcal{R}(\bm{\theta})\,,

(1)

where
\bm{\theta}
are the learnable parameters,
\widehat{\bm{\mathscr{X}}}(\bm{\theta})\in\mathbb{R}^{I_{1}\times\cdots\times I_{N}}
is the reconstructed tensor,
\mathcal{L}_{\Omega}
is the loss defining the error between
\widehat{\bm{\mathscr{X}}}(\bm{\theta})
and
\bm{\mathscr{X}}
on entries in
\Omega
,
and
\mathcal{R}(\bm{\theta})
is the regularization term. For brevity, we write
\widehat{\bm{\mathscr{X}}}
without explicit dependence on
\bm{\theta}
.
Rank constraints can be incorporated into (
1
) by including appropriate penalty terms in
\mathcal{R}(\bm{\theta})
.

This work focuses on the sum of squared errors

\mathcal{L}_{\Omega}(\widehat{\bm{\mathscr{X}}},\bm{\mathscr{X}})=\sum_{(i_{1},\ldots,i_{N})\in\Omega}(\widehat{x}_{i_{1}\ldots i_{N}}-x_{i_{1}\ldots i_{N}})^{2}\,.

(2)

To introduce our approach, we use a running example where

\widehat{\bm{\mathscr{X}}}=\bm{\mathscr{G}}\times_{1}\mathbf{A}^{(1)}\times_{2}\mathbf{A}^{(2)}\cdots\times_{N}\mathbf{A}^{(N)}

is a
low-rank Tucker decomposition

with core tensor
\bm{\mathscr{G}}\in\mathbb{R}^{R_{1}\times\dots\times R_{N}}
and
factor matrices
\mathbf{A}^{(n)}\in\mathbb{R}^{I_{n}\times R_{n}}
, and
\times_{i}
is the mode-
i
product.

In general, TC is a non-convex and NP-hard problem
(
Hillar and Lim 2013
)
.
The special case where
\Omega=[I_{1}]\times\cdots\times[I_{N}]

is the widely studied
tensor decomposition
(TD) problem,
for which
alternating least squares
(ALS) methods are regularly used to compute
\bm{\mathscr{G}},\mathbf{A}^{(1)},\ldots,\mathbf{A}^{(N)}
.

Each ALS step fixes all but one of
\bm{\mathscr{G}},\mathbf{A}^{(1)},\dots,\mathbf{A}^{(N)}
, and optimizes the unfixed component.
In the case of TD, each ALS step is a
highly structured
linear regression problem.
For example, in the core tensor update for
\bm{\mathscr{G}}
, ALS solves

\min_{\bm{\mathscr{G}}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{R_{1}\times\cdots\times R_{N}}}\left\lVert\left(\bigotimes_{n=1}^{N}\mathbf{A}^{(n)}\right)\vvec(\bm{\mathscr{G}}^{\prime})-\vvec(\bm{\mathscr{X}})\right\rVert_{2}\,,

(3)

where
\otimes
is the
Kronecker product
and

\vvec(\bm{\mathscr{G}}^{\prime})\in\mathbb{R}^{R}
is the flattened version of
\bm{\mathscr{G}}^{\prime}

with
R:=\prod_{n=1}^{N}R_{n}
.

Diao et al. 2019
and
Fahrbach et al. 2022

recently exploited the Kronecker product structure in (
3
) to design algorithms with running times that are sublinear in the size of the full tensor
I:=\prod_{n=1}^{N}I_{n}

by using
leverage score sampling
to approximately solve (
3
).

In TC, however, only a subset of observations appear in the loss function.
Letting
\mathbf{A}=\bigotimes_{n=1}^{N}\mathbf{A}^{(n)}
and
\mathbf{b}=\vvec(\bm{\mathscr{X}})
,
the core tensor update becomes

\vvec(\bm{\mathscr{G}})\leftarrow\argmin_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{R}}\,\lVert\mathbf{A}_{\Omega}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{b}_{\Omega}\rVert_{2}\,,

(4)

where
\mathbf{A}_{\Omega}
is a submatrix of
\mathbf{A}

whose rows correspond to the indices in
\Omega
.
Observe that the Kronecker product structure in (
3
)
no longer exists
for
\mathbf{A}_{\Omega}
in (
4
),
hence fast TD methods do not immediately extend to
\Omega
-masked TC versions.

A natural idea to overcome the lack of structure in the
\Omega
-masked updates is to lift (
4
) to a higher-dimensional problem by introducing variables
\mathbf{b}_{\overline{\Omega}}
, where
\overline{\Omega}
is the complement of
\Omega
,
i.e., letting the unobserved entries in
\bm{\mathscr{X}}
be
free variables
.
This is a convex problem with much of the same structure as the design matrix in the full TD problem, and gives the same solution as the TC update in (
4
):

(\mathbf{x}^{*},\mathbf{b}_{\overline{\Omega}}^{*})=\argmin_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{b}_{\overline{\Omega}}}\,\lVert\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{b}\rVert_{2}\,.

(5)

To our knowledge, such ideas date back to
Healy and Westmacott 1956
in the experimental design and causal inference literature.
However, it is not clear a priori that lifting is helpful for
computational efficiency
—it restores the structure of the design matrix,
but the new problem (
5
) is larger and higher dimensional.
Our work proposes to solve (
5
) with the following two-step procedure,
called
mini-ALS
.
Given
\mathbf{x}^{(k)}
, it repeats the following:

1.

Set
\mathbf{b}^{(k)}_{\overline{\Omega}}\leftarrow\mathbf{A}_{\overline{\Omega}}\mathbf{x}^{(k)}
and
\mathbf{b}^{(k)}_{\Omega}\leftarrow\mathbf{b}_{\Omega}
.

2.

Set
\mathbf{x}^{(k+1)}\leftarrow\argmin_{\mathbf{x}}\lVert\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{b}^{(k)}\rVert_{2}
.

Mini-ALS iterations can still be expensive since
{\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{I\times R}}
is a tall-and-skinny matrix (i.e.,
I\gg R
).
However, step two is a structured ALS for TD,
so we propose solving it
approximately
with row sampling,
allowing us to tap into a rich line of work on leverage score sampling
for CP decomposition
(
Cheng et al. 2016
;
Larsen and Kolda 2022
;
Bharadwaj et al. 2023
)
,
Tucker decomposition
(
Diao et al. 2019
;
Fahrbach et al. 2022
)
,
and tensor-train decomposition
(
Bharadwaj et al. 2024
)
.
Returning to the running Tucker completion example,
updating the core tensor in step two of mini-ALS via (
3
)
only requires sampling
\tilde{O}(R)
rows of
\mathbf{A}
,
which leads to a substantially
smaller
problem.
Further, we can compute
\mathbf{b}^{(k)}

lazily
, i.e.,
only the entries corresponding to sampled rows.
We call our lifted iterative method
approximate-mini-ALS
.

1.1
Our Contributions

We summarize our main contributions as follows:

•

In
Section

3
, we propose using the mini-ALS algorithm
for each step of ALS for TC, and show that it simulates the
preconditioned Richardson iteration
(
Lemma

3.5
).
Our main theoretical contribution is proving that the second step of a mini-ALS iteration can be performed
approximately
.
We quantify how small this approximation error must be for approximate-mini-ALS to converge
at the same rate as the Richardson iteration (
Theorem

3.7
).
This lets us to extend a recent line of work
on leverage score sampling-based TD ALS algorithms to the TC setting.

•

In
Section

4
,
we use state-of-the-art TD ALS algorithms
for CP,
Tucker,
and tensor-train decompositions
as
blackbox subroutines
in Algorithm
1

to obtain novel sampling-based TC algorithms.
Hence, our lifting approach for TC also benefits from future TD algorithmic improvements.

•

In
Section

5
,
we compare the empirical performance of
our lifted algorithm to direct methods for a
low-rank CP completion
task on synthetic and real-world tensors.
We observe that mini-ALS can be orders of magnitude faster than direct ALS methods,
while achieving comparable reconstruction errors.
Finally, we propose an
accelerated
version with adaptive step sizes that extrapolates the trajectory of
\mathbf{x}^{(k)}
and converges in fewer total iterations.

1.2
Related Work

CP completion.

Tomasi and Bro 2005
proposed an ALS algorithm for CP completion that repeats the following two-step process:
(1) fill in the missing values using the current CP decomposition, and
(2) update one factor matrix.
Their algorithm is equivalent to running
one iteration
of mini-ALS in each step of ALS.
As
Tomasi and Bro 2005
discuss, this can lead to slower convergence and an increased likelihood of converging to suboptimal local minima because of errors introduced by the imputed missing values.

In contrast, approximate-mini-ALS runs
until convergence
in each step of ALS.
By doing this, we establish a connection to the Richardson iteration
and build on its convergence guarantees.
Further,
Tomasi and Bro 2005
explicitly fill in
all missing values
using the current decomposition, whereas we only impute missing values
required by row sampling
,
allowing us to achieve sublinear-time updates in the size of the tensor.
In general, iteratively fitting to imputed missing values falls under the umbrella of
expectation-maximization
(EM) algorithms
(
Little and Rubin 2019
, Chapter 8)
.

Statistical assumptions.

Similar to minimizing the nuclear norm for matrix completion
(
Fazel 2002
;
Candes and Recht 2012
)
, a line of research in noisy TC suggests minimizing a convex relaxation of rank and identifies statistical assumptions under which the problem is recoverable
(
Barak and Moitra 2016
)
.
Two standard assumptions are
incoherence
and the
missing-at-random
assumption. In
Section

3
, we discuss how these assumptions provide a bound on the number of steps required for mini-ALS.
Alternating minimization approaches have also been applied
in this line of noisy TC research
(
Jain and Oh 2014
;
Liu and Moitra 2020
)
.
It is also consistently observed that methods based on TD and tensor unfoldings are more practical and computationally efficient
(
Acar et al. 2011
;
Montanari and Sun 2018
;
Filipović and Jukić 2015
;
Shah and Yu 2019
;
Shah and Yu 2023
)
.

2
Preliminaries

Notation.

The
order
of a tensor is the number of its dimensions
N
.
We denote scalars by normal lowercase letters
x\in\mathbb{R}
,
vectors by boldface lowercase letters
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
matrices by boldface uppercase letters
\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
,
and higher-order tensors by boldface script letters
\bm{\mathscr{X}}\in\mathbb{R}^{I_{1}\times\dots\times I_{N}}
.
We use normal uppercase letters for the size of an index set,
e.g.,
[N]=\{1,2,\dots,N\}
.
We define
I_{\neq k}:=I/I_{k}
for
k\in[N]
and similarly
R_{\neq k:=R/R_{k}}
.
We denote the
i
-th entry of
\mathbf{x}
by
x_{i}
,
the
(i,j)
-th entry of
\mathbf{X}
by
x_{ij}
, and the

(i,j,k)
-th entry of a third-order tensor
\bm{\mathscr{X}}
by
x_{ijk}
.

Linear algebra.

A symmetric matrix
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is
positive semi-definite
(PSD) if
\mathbf{v}^{\top}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v}\geq 0
for any
\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
For two symmetric matrices
\mathbf{A}
,
\mathbf{B}
, we use
\mathbf{A}\preccurlyeq\mathbf{B}
to indicate that
\mathbf{B}-\mathbf{A}
is PSD.
For a PSD matrix
\mathbf{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
and vector
\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we define
\lVert\mathbf{v}\rVert_{\mathbf{M}}:=(\mathbf{v}^{\top}\mathbf{M}\mathbf{v})^{1/2}
.
For
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
,
\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, and
\Omega\subseteq[m]
, we use
\mathbf{A}_{\Omega}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\Omega|\times n}
and
\mathbf{b}_{\Omega}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\Omega|}
to denote the submatrix and subvector with rows indexed by
\Omega
.
We let
\otimes
denote the Kronecker product and
\odot
denote the Khatri–Rao product.

Tensor algebra.

The fibers of a tensor are the vectors obtained by fixing all but one index,
e.g., if
\bm{\mathscr{X}}\in\mathbb{R}^{I\times J\times K}
,
the column, row and tube fibers are

\mathbf{x}_{:jk}
,
\mathbf{x}_{i:k}
, and
\mathbf{x}_{ij:}
, respectively.
The
mode-
n
unfolding
of a
tensor
\bm{\mathscr{X}}\in\mathbb{R}^{I_{1}\times\dots\times I_{N}}
is the matrix

\mathbf{X}_{(n)}\in\mathbb{R}^{I_{n}\times(I_{1}\cdots I_{n-1}I_{n+1}\cdots I_{N})}

that arranges the mode-
n
fibers of
\bm{\mathscr{X}}
as rows of
\mathbf{X}_{(n)}
sorted lexicographically by index. The
vectorization

\vvec{(\bm{\mathscr{X}})}\in\mathbb{R}^{I_{1}\cdots I_{N}}
of
\bm{\mathscr{X}}
stacks the elements of
\bm{\mathscr{X}}
lexicographically by index.

For
n\in[N]
, we denote the
mode-
n
product
of a tensor

\bm{\mathscr{X}}\in\mathbb{R}^{I_{1}\times\dots\times I_{N}}
and matrix

\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{J\times I_{n}}
by

\bm{\mathscr{Y}}=\bm{\mathscr{X}}\times_{n}\mathbf{A}
, where

\bm{\mathscr{Y}}\in\mathbb{R}^{I_{1}\times\dots\times I_{n-1}\times J\times I_{n+1}\times\dots\times I_{N}}
.
This operation multiplies each mode-
n
fiber of
\bm{\mathscr{X}}
by
\mathbf{A}
, and is expressed elementwise as

(\bm{\mathscr{X}}\times_{n}\mathbf{A})_{i_{1}\dots i_{n-1}ji_{n+1}\dots i_{N}}=\sum_{i_{n}=1}^{I_{n}}x_{i_{1}i_{2}\dots i_{N}}a_{ji_{n}}.

The inner product of two tensors
\bm{\mathscr{X}},\bm{\mathscr{Y}}\in\mathbb{R}^{I_{1}\times\dots\times I_{N}}
is

\langle\bm{\mathscr{X}},\bm{\mathscr{Y}}\rangle=\sum_{i_{1}=1}^{I_{1}}\sum_{i_{2}=1}^{I_{2}}\dots\sum_{i_{N}=1}^{I_{N}}x_{i_{1}i_{2}\dots i_{N}}y_{i_{1}i_{2}\dots i_{N}}.

The Frobenius norm of a tensor
\bm{\mathscr{X}}
is

\lVert\bm{\mathscr{X}}\rVert_{\textnormal{F}}=\sqrt{\langle\bm{\mathscr{X}},\bm{\mathscr{X}}\rangle}
.

2.1
Tensor Decompositions

The tensor decompositions of
\bm{\mathscr{X}}\in\mathbb{R}^{I_{1}\times\dots\times I_{N}}
below can be viewed as higher-order analogs of low-rank matrix factorization.
See
Kolda and Bader 2009
for a comprehensive survey on this topic.

CP decomposition.

A rank-
R

CP decomposition
represents
\bm{\mathscr{X}}

with
\bm{\lambda}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{R}
and
N
factors
\mathbf{A}^{(n)}\in\mathbb{R}^{I_{n}\times R}
, for
n\in[N]
,
where each column of
\mathbf{A}^{(n)}
has unit norm.
The reconstructed tensor
\widehat{\bm{\mathscr{X}}}
is defined elementwise as:

\widehat{x}_{i_{1}\ldots i_{N}}=\sum_{r=1}^{R}\lambda_{r}\,a^{(1)}_{i_{1}r}\cdots a^{(N)}_{i_{N}r}\,.

Tucker decomposition.

A rank-
\mathbf{r}

Tucker decomposition
represents
\bm{\mathscr{X}}
as a
core tensor

\bm{\mathscr{G}}\in\mathbb{R}^{R_{1}\times\dots\times R_{N}}
and
N

factor matrices

\mathbf{A}^{(n)}\in\mathbb{R}^{I_{n}\times R_{n}}
, for
n\in[N]
,
where
\mathbf{r}=(R_{1},\dots,R_{N})
is the multilinear rank
(
Ghadiri et al. 2023a
)
.
The reconstructed tensor

\widehat{\bm{\mathscr{X}}}=\bm{\mathscr{G}}\times_{1}\mathbf{A}^{(1)}\times_{2}\dots\times_{N}\mathbf{A}^{(N)}

is defined elementwise as:

\widehat{x}_{i_{1}\ldots i_{N}}=\sum_{r_{1}=1}^{R_{1}}\cdots\sum_{r_{N}=1}^{R_{N}}g_{r_{1}\ldots r_{N}}a_{i_{1}r_{1}}^{(1)}\cdots a_{i_{N}r_{N}}^{(N)}\,.

TT decomposition.

A rank-
\mathbf{r}

tensor train (TT) decomposition

(
Oseledets 2011
)
represents
\bm{\mathscr{X}}

using
N
third-order
TT-cores

\bm{\mathscr{A}}^{(n)}\in\mathbb{R}^{R_{n-1}\times I_{n}\times R_{n}}
, for
n\in[N]
, with the convention
R_{0}=R_{N}=1
.
The reconstructed tensor
\widehat{\bm{\mathscr{X}}}
is defined elementwise as:

\displaystyle\widehat{x}_{i_{1}\ldots i_{N}}

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\mathbf{A}^{(1)}_{:i_{1}:}}_{1\times R_{1}}\underbrace{\mathbf{A}^{(2)}_{:i_{2}:}}_{R_{1}\times R_{2}}\cdots\underbrace{\mathbf{A}^{(N-1)}_{:i_{N-1}:}}_{R_{N-2}\times R_{N-1}}\underbrace{\mathbf{A}^{(N)}_{:i_{N}:}}_{R_{N-1}\times 1}\,.

Remark 2.1
.

All three of these TDs are instances of the more general
tensor network
framework (see
Section

A.4
).

2.2
ALS Formulations

Alternating least squares
(ALS) methods are the gold standard for low-rank tensor decomposition, e.g., they are the first techniques mentioned in the MATLAB Tensor Toolbox
(
Bader and Kolda 2023
)
.
ALS cyclically minimizes the original least-squares problem (
1
) with respect to one block of factor variables while keeping all others fixed.
Repeating this process converges to a nontrivial local optimum
and reduces the original nonconvex problem to a series of (convex) linear regression problems in each step (see
Section

A.1
).

Updating a block of variables in an ALS step for a TD problem
is typically a
highly structured
regression problem
that can be solved very fast with problem-specific algorithms.
We describe the induced structure of each ALS update for the tensor decomposition types above.

CP factor matrix update
\equiv
Khatri–Rao regression.

In each ALS step for CP decomposition, all factor matrices are fixed except for one, say
\mathbf{A}^{(n)}
.
ALS solves the following linear least-squares problem:

\mathbf{A}^{(n)}\leftarrow\argmin_{\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{I_{n}\times R}}\,\left\lVert\left(\bigodot_{i=1,i\neq n}^{N}\mathbf{A}^{(i)}\right)\,\mathbf{A}^{\top}-\mathbf{X}_{(n)}^{\top}\right\rVert_{\textnormal{F}}\,.

(6)

Then, we set
\lambda_{r}=\lVert\mathbf{a}^{(n)}_{:r}\rVert_{2}
for each
r\in[R]
and normalize the columns of
\mathbf{A}^{(n)}
.
Each row of
\mathbf{A}^{(n)}
can be optimized independently, so (
6
)
solves
I_{n}
linear regression problems where the design matrix is a
Khatri–Rao product
.

Tucker core update
\equiv
Kronecker regression.

The core-tensor ALS update solves: with all factors
\mathbf{A}^{(n)}
fixed,

\bm{\mathscr{G}}\leftarrow\argmin_{\bm{\mathscr{G}}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{R_{1}\times\cdots\times R_{N}}}\,\left\lVert\left(\bigotimes_{n=1}^{N}\mathbf{A}^{(n)}\right)\vvec(\bm{\mathscr{G}}^{\prime})-\vvec(\bm{\mathscr{X}})\right\rVert_{2}\,,

where the design matrix is a
Kronecker product
of the factors.

Tucker factor update
\equiv
Kronecker-matrix regression.

When ALS updates
\mathbf{A}^{(n)}
with all the other factor matrices
and core tensor fixed, it solves:

\mathbf{A}^{(n)}\leftarrow\argmin_{\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{I_{n}\times R_{n}}}\,\left\lVert\left(\bigotimes_{i=1,i\neq n}^{N}\mathbf{A}^{(i)}\right)\mathbf{G}_{(n)}^{\top}\mathbf{A}^{\top}-\mathbf{X}_{(n)}^{\top}\right\rVert_{\textnormal{F}}\,,

where
\mathbf{G}_{(n)}
,
\mathbf{X}_{(n)}
are the mode-
n
unfoldings of
\bm{\mathscr{G}}
and
\bm{\mathscr{X}}
.
This is equivalent to solving
I_{n}
independent linear regression problems, where the design matrix is the product of a Kronecker product and another matrix.
It can also be viewed as solving
I_{n}
instances of structured but
constrained linear regression

(
Fahrbach et al. 2022
)
.

TT-core update
\equiv
Kronecker regression.

Given a TT decomposition
\{\bm{\mathscr{A}}^{(n)}\}_{n=1}^{N}
and
n\in[N]
,
the
left chain

\mathbf{A}_{<n}\in\mathbb{R}^{(I_{1}\cdots I_{n-1})\times R_{n-1}}

and

right chain

\mathbf{A}_{>n}\in\mathbb{R}^{R_{n}\times(I_{n+1}\cdots I_{N})}

are matrices depending on the cores
\bm{\mathscr{A}}^{(n^{\prime})}
for
n^{\prime}<n
and
n^{\prime}>n
, respectively (see
Section

A.3
for details).
When ALS updates
\bm{\mathscr{A}}^{(n)}
with all other TT-cores fixed, it solves

\bm{\mathscr{A}}^{(n)}\!\!\leftarrow\!\!\argmin_{\bm{\mathscr{B}}\in\mathbb{R}^{R_{n-1}\times I_{n}\times R_{n}}}\bigl\lVert(\mathbf{A}_{<n}\otimes\mathbf{A}^{\top}_{>n})\,\mathbf{B}_{(2)}^{\top}-\mathbf{X}_{(n)}^{\top}\bigr\rVert_{\textnormal{F}}\,,

which is equivalent to solving
I_{n}
Kronecker regression problems in
\mathbb{R}^{I_{\neq n}}
.

3
Approximate Richardson Iteration

We now present our main techniques for reducing tensor completion to tensor decomposition.
When we use ALS to solve a TC problem,
we must efficiently solve least-squares problems
\min_{\mathbf{x}}\,\lVert\mathbf{Px}-\mathbf{q}\rVert_{2}
.
The rows of the design matrix
\mathbf{P}
correspond to the
subset of observations
in the TC problem,
so
\mathbf{P}
does not necessarily have the structure of the design matrix in the full TD problem.

A direct approach is to compute the closed-form solution
\mathbf{x}^{*}=(\mathbf{P}^{\top}\mathbf{P})^{-1}\mathbf{P}^{\top}\mathbf{q}
, but computing
(\mathbf{P}^{\top}\mathbf{P})^{-1}
is often impractical.
Two techniques are commonly used to overcome this:
(1) iterative methods and (2) row sampling.
Iterative methods repeat the same relatively cheap per-step computation

many times
to approximate the original expensive computation.
Row sampling methods (e.g., leverage score sampling) randomly pick rows of
\mathbf{P}

and solve a least-squares problem on the sampled rows to obtain an approximate solution to the original problem with high probability.
Directly computing leverage scores for a general
\mathbf{P}
, however,
is
also prohibitively expensive
since it requires computing
the same matrix
(\mathbf{P}^{\top}\mathbf{P})^{-1}
(see
Section

A.2
for details).

We show that our novel
approximate-mini-ALS
method
is a principled approach for tensor completion.
In
Section

3.1
, we show how lifting
restores the structure
of the full TD ALS update step,
enabling fast least-squares methods for a larger (but equivalent) problem.
In
Section

3.2
, we show that iteratively solving this lifted problem (i.e., mini-ALS) is connected to an iterative method called the
Richardson iteration

(
Richardson 1911
)
,
which we can also view as a matrix-splitting method.
In other words, mini-ALS and the Richardson iteration with a certain preconditioner give the same sequence of iterates
\{\mathbf{x}^{(k)}\}_{k\geq 0}
.
Lastly in
Section

3.3
,
we prove novel convergence guarantees for
approximately
solving the lifted problem
(i.e., for approximate-mini-ALS).
This allows us to directly use fast leverage-score sampling algorithms for
CP decomposition
(
Cheng et al. 2016
;
Larsen and Kolda 2022
;
Bharadwaj et al. 2023
)
,
Tucker decomposition
(
Diao et al. 2019
;
Fahrbach et al. 2022
)
,
and TT decomposition
(
Bharadwaj et al. 2024
)

as blackbox subroutines.

3.1
Lifting to a Structured Problem

Consider the linear regression problem with
\mathbf{P}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\Omega|\times R}
and
\mathbf{q}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\Omega|}
given by

\mathbf{x}^{*}=\argmin_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{R}}\,\lVert\mathbf{P}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{q}\rVert_{2}^{2}\,.

(7)

If there exists a tall structured matrix
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{I\times R}

with a subset of rows
\Omega\subseteq[I]
such that
\mathbf{A}_{\Omega}=\mathbf{P}

(permutations of the rows allowed),
then we can lift (
7
) to a higher-dimensional problem while preserving the optimal solution.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{I}
be the lifted response such that
\mathbf{b}_{\Omega}=\mathbf{q}

and
\mathbf{b}_{\overline{\Omega}}
is a free variable. If

(\mathbf{x}^{*},\mathbf{b}^{*}_{\overline{\Omega}})=\argmin_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{R},\mathbf{b}_{\overline{\Omega}}\in\mathbb{R}^{I-|\Omega|}}\,\left\lVert\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{b}\right\rVert_{2}\,,

(8)

then
\mathbf{x}^{*}
also minimizes (
7
),
i.e., the original linear regression problem
\min_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{R}}\,\lVert\mathbf{P}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{q}\rVert_{2}^{2}
.

Proof.

For any
\mathbf{x}
, we have

\lVert\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{b}\rVert_{2}^{2}=\lVert\mathbf{A}_{\Omega}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{b}_{\Omega}\rVert_{2}^{2}+\lVert\mathbf{A}_{\overline{\Omega}}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{b}_{\overline{\Omega}}\rVert_{2}^{2}\,,

so

\min_{\mathbf{x}}\lVert\mathbf{P}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{q}\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq\min_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{b}_{\overline{\Omega}}}\lVert\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{b}\rVert_{2}^{2}\,.

Moreover, for any
\mathbf{x}
, taking
\mathbf{b}_{\overline{\Omega}}=\mathbf{A}_{\overline{\Omega}}\mathbf{x}
gives us
\lVert\mathbf{A}_{\overline{\Omega}}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{b}_{\overline{\Omega}}\rVert_{2}^{2}=0
, which implies that

\min_{\mathbf{x}}\lVert\mathbf{P}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{q}\rVert_{2}^{2}\geq\min_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{b}_{\overline{\Omega}}}\lVert\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{b}\rVert_{2}^{2}\,.

Therefore,

\min_{\mathbf{x}}\lVert\mathbf{P}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{q}\rVert_{2}^{2}=\min_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{b}_{\overline{\Omega}}}\lVert\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{b}\rVert_{2}^{2}\,,

and
\mathbf{x}^{*}
also minimizes (
7
).
∎

For the rest of this section,
let
\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}\in\mathbb{R}^{I\times R}
,
\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}\in\mathbb{R}^{I}

be the zero-masked lifted matrix and vector such that

(\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}_{\Omega},\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}_{\overline{\Omega}})=(\mathbf{A}_{\Omega},\bm{0})\quad\text{and}\quad(\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}_{\Omega},\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}_{\overline{\Omega}})=(\mathbf{b}_{\Omega},\bm{0}).

Lemma 3.2
.

Problem

is a convex quadratic program.

Proof.

Since
\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}\in\mathbb{R}^{I}

and
\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}_{\overline{\Omega}}=\bm{0}

we can write (

\mathbf{b}_{\overline{\Omega}}\end{bmatrix}-\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\,,

where
\mathbf{I}
is the
I\times I

∎

3.2
Iterative Methods for the Lifted Problem

Iterative methods for solving linear systems and regression problems have a long history and have been used to expedite several algorithms in theory and practice.
The algorithms we consider use the exact arithmetic model, but all of these methods can be carried out with numbers with
\log\nicefrac{{\kappa}}{{\varepsilon}}
bits, where
\kappa
is the condition number of the matrix (see, e.g.,
Ghadiri et al. 2023b
;
Ghadiri et al. 2024
).
There is a literature on
inexact
Richardson iteration for solving linear systems,
but they require the error
\widehat{\varepsilon}
to be smaller than than
1/\kappa
,
which is not achievable with leverage-score sampling
Golub and Overton 1988
;
Golub and van der Vorst 1997
.

Lemma 3.4

(Preconditioned Richardson iteration,
(
Lee and Vempala 2024
, Lemma 6.1)
)
.

Consider the least-squares problem
\mathbf{x}^{*}=\argmin_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{R}}\,\lVert\mathbf{P}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{q}\rVert
.
Let
\mathbf{M}
be a matrix such that
\mathbf{P}^{\top}\mathbf{P}\preccurlyeq\mathbf{M}\preccurlyeq\beta\cdot\mathbf{P}^{\top}\mathbf{P}

for some
\beta\geq 1
,
and consider the Richardson iteration:

\mathbf{x}^{(k+1)}=\mathbf{x}^{(k)}-\mathbf{M}^{-1}(\mathbf{P}^{\top}\mathbf{P}\mathbf{x}^{(k)}-\mathbf{P}^{\top}\mathbf{q})\,.

Then, we have that

\lVert\mathbf{x}^{(k+1)}-\mathbf{x}^{*}\rVert_{\mathbf{M}}\leq\Bigl(1-\frac{1}{\beta}\Bigr)\lVert\mathbf{x}^{(k)}-\mathbf{x}^{*}\rVert_{\mathbf{M}}\,.

We now present a key lemma showing that alternating minimization between
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{b}_{\overline{\Omega}}
corresponds to preconditioned Richardson iterations
on the original least-squares problem.
Below, one can easily check that
\mathbf{A}
,
\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}
, and
\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}

in our lifted approach satisfy this condition.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
\mathbf{A},\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}\in\mathbb{R}^{I\times R}
,
\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}\in\mathbb{R}^{I}
such that
\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}-\mathbf{A}
and

\bigl[\begin{matrix}\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}&\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}\end{matrix}\bigr]

are orthogonal, i.e.,
(\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}-\mathbf{A})^{\top}\bigl[\begin{matrix}\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}&\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}\end{matrix}\bigr]=\mathbf{0}
.
Then, the iterative method

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}^{(k)}

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}+(\mathbf{A}-\widetilde{\mathbf{P}})\,\mathbf{x}^{(k)}\,,

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}^{(k+1)}

\displaystyle=\argmin_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{R}}\,\lVert\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}-\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}^{(k)}\rVert_{2}^{2}\,,

simulates Richardson iterations with preconditioner
\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}

for the regression problem
\min_{\mathbf{x}}\,\lVert\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}\mathbf{x}-\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}\rVert_{2}^{2}
,
i.e.,

\mathbf{x}^{(k+1)}=\mathbf{x}^{(k)}-(\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A})^{-1}(\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}\mathbf{x}^{(k)}-\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{q}})\,.

(9)

Proof.

Assume that
\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}
is full rank.
Solving the normal equation for
\mathbf{x}^{(k+1)}
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}^{(k+1)}

\displaystyle=(\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A})^{-1}\mathbf{A}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}^{(k)}

\displaystyle=(\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A})^{-1}\mathbf{A}^{\top}(\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}+(\mathbf{A}-\widetilde{\mathbf{P}})\,\mathbf{x}^{(k)})

\displaystyle=\mathbf{x}^{(k)}-(\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A})^{-1}\mathbf{A}^{\top}(\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}\mathbf{x}^{(k)}-\widetilde{\mathbf{q}})\,.

Since
\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}-\mathbf{A}
and
\bigl[\begin{matrix}\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}&\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}\end{matrix}\bigr]
are orthogonal,

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}^{\top}(\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}\mathbf{x}^{(k)}-\widetilde{\mathbf{q}})

\displaystyle=\bigl(\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}-(\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}-\mathbf{A})\bigr)^{\top}\bigl[\begin{matrix}\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}&\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}\end{matrix}\bigr]\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{x}^{(k)}\\
-1\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}^{\top}(\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}\mathbf{x}^{(k)}-\widetilde{\mathbf{q}})\,.

Therefore,

\mathbf{x}^{(k+1)}=\mathbf{x}^{(k)}-(\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A})^{-1}(\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}\mathbf{x}^{(k)}-\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{q}})\,,

which completes the proof.
∎

Remark 3.6
.

In the tensor completion setting,
\mathbf{A}-\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}
vanishes over
\Omega
,
so
\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}^{(k)}
only updates entries in
\overline{\Omega}
while maintaining
\mathbf{q}
on
\Omega
.
Thus, computing
\mathbf{x}^{(k+1)}
corresponds to

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}^{(k+1)}

\displaystyle=\argmin_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{R}}\,\lVert\mathbf{Ax}-\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}^{(k)}\rVert^{2}_{2}=\argmin_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{R}}\,\bigl\{\lVert\mathbf{Px}-\mathbf{q}\rVert^{2}_{2}+\lVert\mathbf{A}_{\overline{\Omega}}\,(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{(k)})\rVert_{2}^{2}\bigr\}\,.

3.3
Approximately Solving the Lifted Problem

We have shown that alternating minimization for the lifted problem (
8
)
has strong connections to preconditioned Richardson iteration
and inherits its convergence guarantees.
However, for this observation to be useful,
we need to use fast regression algorithms for the
\mathbf{x}^{(k+1)}
updates that
exploit the structure
of
\mathbf{A}
,
i.e.,
when solving
\min_{\mathbf{x}}\,\lVert\mathbf{Ax}-\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}^{(k)}\rVert_{2}
.

This is where leverage score sampling comes in to play.
We exploit the structure of
\mathbf{A}
to efficiently compute its leverage scores,
and then we solve the regression problem efficiently but
approximately
.
By using a sketching method, our work deviates from the standard (exact) Richardson iteration.

Our next result shows how using approximate least-squares solutions

in each step of block minimization

affects the convergence guarantee of our lifted iterative method.

Data:

\mathbf{A},\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}\in\mathbb{R}^{I\times R}
,
\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}\in\mathbb{R}^{I}
,
\beta\geq 1
,
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
,
\widehat{\varepsilon}\in[0,1/\beta^{2})
with
\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}\preceq\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}\preceq\beta\cdot\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}

Result:

\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{R}

Initialize
\mathbf{x}^{(0)}=\bm{0}

1

for

k=0,1,\dots,\left\lceil\frac{\log(\nicefrac{{2\beta}}{{\varepsilon}})}{2\,(\nicefrac{{1}}{{\beta}}-\sqrt{\widehat{\varepsilon}})}\right\rceil

do

2

Set
\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}^{(k)}\leftarrow\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}+(\mathbf{A}-\widetilde{\mathbf{P}})\,\mathbf{x}^{(k)}

//
Implicit

Set
\mathbf{x}^{(k+1)}
to a vector such that
\lVert\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}^{(k+1)}-\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}^{(k)}\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq(1+\widehat{\varepsilon})\,\min_{\mathbf{x}}\,\lVert\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}-\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}^{(k)}\rVert_{2}^{2}

return

\mathbf{x}^{(k)}

Algorithm 1

approx-mini-als

Theorem 3.7
.

Let
\mathbf{A},\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}\in\mathbb{R}^{I\times R}
,
\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}\in\mathbb{R}^{I}
, and
\beta\geq 1

such that

\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}-\mathbf{A}
and
\bigl[\begin{matrix}\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}&\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}\end{matrix}\bigr]
are orthogonal, and

\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}\preceq\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}\preceq\beta\cdot\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}\,.

Let
\varepsilon\in(0,1),\widehat{\varepsilon}\in[0,1/\beta^{2})
and

approx-least-squares
be an algorithm that for any

\widehat{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{R}
and
\mathbf{f}=\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}+(\mathbf{A}-\widetilde{\mathbf{P}})\,\widehat{\mathbf{x}}
,
computes
\overline{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{R}
in time
O(T)
such that

\lVert\mathbf{A}\overline{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{f}\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq(1+\widehat{\varepsilon})\,\min_{\mathbf{x}}\,\lVert\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{f}\rVert_{2}^{2}\,.

Then, Algorithm
1
returns an approximate solution

\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{R}
, using
approx-least-squares
as a subroutine, such that

\displaystyle\lVert\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}-\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}\rVert_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left(1+\frac{2\widehat{\varepsilon}}{(\nicefrac{{1}}{{\beta}}-\sqrt{\widehat{\varepsilon}})^{2}}\right)\,\min_{\mathbf{x}}\,\lVert\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}\mathbf{x}-\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}\rVert_{2}^{2}+\varepsilon\,\lVert\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}\,(\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{P}})^{-1}\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}\rVert_{2}^{2}\,,

in
O\left(\frac{\beta}{1-\sqrt{\widehat{\varepsilon}}\beta}\cdot T\log\nicefrac{{\beta}}{{\varepsilon}}\right)
time.

Proof.

We show that Algorithm
1
gives the desired output.
Suppose
approx-least-squares
yields
\mathbf{x}^{(k+1)}
for given inputs
\mathbf{A},\widetilde{\mathbf{P}},\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}
, and
\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}^{(k)}
(i.e.,
\widehat{\mathbf{x}}\leftarrow\mathbf{x}^{(k)}
,
\mathbf{f}\leftarrow\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}^{(k)}
, and
\overline{\mathbf{x}}\leftarrow\mathbf{x}^{(k+1)}
), which satisfies

\lVert\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}^{(k+1)}-\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}^{(k)}\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq(1+\widehat{\varepsilon})\,\min_{\mathbf{x}}\lVert\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}-\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}^{(k)}\rVert_{2}^{2}=(1+\widehat{\varepsilon})\,\lVert\pi_{\mathbf{A}^{\perp}}\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}^{(k)}\rVert_{2}^{2}\,.

We can also decompose the LHS using
\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}^{(k)}=\pi_{\mathbf{A}}\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}^{(k)}+\pi_{\mathbf{A}^{\perp}}\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}^{(k)}
as follows:

\lVert\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}^{(k+1)}-\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}^{(k)}\rVert_{2}^{2}=\lVert\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}^{(k+1)}-\pi_{\mathbf{A}}\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}^{(k)}\rVert_{2}^{2}+\lVert\pi_{\mathbf{A}^{\perp}}\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}^{(k)}\rVert_{2}^{2}\,.

Combining the above, we get

\lVert\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}^{(k+1)}-\pi_{\mathbf{A}}\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}^{(k)}\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq\widehat{\varepsilon}\,\lVert\pi_{\mathbf{A}^{\perp}}\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}^{(k)}\rVert_{2}^{2}\,.

Denoting
\mathbf{x}^{*}=(\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{P}})^{-1}\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}=\argmin_{\mathbf{x}}\,\lVert\mathbf{Bx}-\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}\rVert_{2}
and using the triangle inequality,

\displaystyle\lVert\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}^{(k+1)}-\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}^{*}\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\lVert\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}^{(k+1)}-\pi_{\mathbf{A}}\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}^{(k)}\rVert_{2}+\lVert\pi_{\mathbf{A}}\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}^{(k)}-\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}^{*}\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\widehat{\varepsilon}}\,\lVert\pi_{\mathbf{A}^{\perp}}\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}^{(k)}\rVert_{2}+\lVert\pi_{\mathbf{A}}\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}^{(k)}-\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}^{*}\rVert_{2}\,.

(10)

We now bound each term in the RHS. As for the second term, since
\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}^{(k)}=\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}+(\mathbf{A}-\widetilde{\mathbf{P}})\,\mathbf{x}^{(k)}
and
(\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}-\mathbf{A})^{\top}\begin{bmatrix}\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}&\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}\end{bmatrix}=0
, by
Lemma

3.5
,

\displaystyle(\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A})^{-1}\mathbf{A}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}^{(k)}

\displaystyle=\argmin_{\mathbf{x}}\,\lVert\mathbf{Ax}-\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}^{(k)}\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{x}^{(k)}-(\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A})^{-1}(\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}\mathbf{x}^{(k)}-\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{q}})\,,

which is exactly a Richardson iteration with preconditioner
\mathbf{M}\leftarrow\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}
in
Lemma

3.4
(satisfying
\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}\preceq\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}\preceq\beta\,\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}
).
Thus,
\lVert(\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A})^{-1}\mathbf{A}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}^{(k)}-\mathbf{x}^{*}\rVert_{\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}}\leq(1-\beta^{-1})\,\lVert\mathbf{x}^{(k)}-\mathbf{x}^{*}\rVert_{\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}}
, and

\lVert\pi_{\mathbf{A}}\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}^{(k)}-\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}^{*}\rVert_{2}\leq\left(1-\frac{1}{\beta}\right)\,\lVert\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}^{(k)}-\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}^{*}\rVert_{2}\,.

(11)

Regarding the first term in (
10
), since
\mathbf{Ax}^{(k)}
is in the column space of
\mathbf{A}
,

\pi_{\mathbf{A}^{\perp}}\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}^{(k)}=\pi_{\mathbf{A}^{\perp}}\bigl(\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}+(\mathbf{A-B})\,\mathbf{x}^{(k)}\bigr)=\pi_{\mathbf{A}^{\perp}}(\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}-\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}\mathbf{x}^{(k)})\,.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\lVert\pi_{\mathbf{A}^{\perp}}\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}^{(k)}\rVert_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\lVert\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}-\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}\mathbf{x}^{(k)}\rVert_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\lVert\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}\mathbf{x}^{*}-\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}\mathbf{x}^{(k)}\rVert_{2}^{2}+\min_{\mathbf{x}}\,\lVert\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}\mathbf{x}-\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}\rVert_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\lVert\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}^{*}-\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}^{(k)}\rVert_{2}^{2}+\min_{\mathbf{x}}\,\lVert\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}\mathbf{x}-\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}\rVert_{2}^{2}\,,

where the last inequality follows from
\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}\preceq\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}
.
Thus,

\lVert\pi_{\mathbf{A}^{\perp}}\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}^{(k)}\rVert_{2}\leq\lVert\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}^{*}-\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}^{(k)}\rVert_{2}+\min_{\mathbf{x}}\,\lVert\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}\mathbf{x}-\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}\rVert_{2}\,.

(12)

Combining (
10
), (
11
), and (
12
), we have

\lVert\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}^{(k+1)}-\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}^{*}\rVert_{2}\leq\left(1-\frac{1}{\beta}+\sqrt{\widehat{\varepsilon}}\right)\,\lVert\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}^{*}-\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}^{(k)}\rVert_{2}+\sqrt{\widehat{\varepsilon}}\,\min_{\mathbf{x}}\,\lVert\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}\mathbf{x}-\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}\rVert_{2}\,.

Denoting
\alpha=1-\frac{1}{\beta}+\sqrt{\widehat{\varepsilon}}\,
, by induction, we have

\displaystyle\lVert\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}^{(k)}-\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}^{*}\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\alpha^{k}\,\lVert\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}^{*}-\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}^{(0)}\rVert_{2}+(1+\alpha+\alpha^{2}+\cdots+\alpha^{k-1})\times\sqrt{\widehat{\varepsilon}}\,\min_{\mathbf{x}}\,\lVert\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}\mathbf{x}-\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle=\alpha^{k}\,\lVert\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}^{*}-\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}^{(0)}\rVert_{2}+\frac{1-\alpha^{k}}{1-\alpha}\times\sqrt{\widehat{\varepsilon}}\,\min_{\mathbf{x}}\,\lVert\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}\mathbf{x}-\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}\rVert_{2}\,.

(13)

We also have

\displaystyle\lVert\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}\mathbf{x}^{(k)}-\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}\rVert_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\lVert\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}\mathbf{x}^{(k)}-\pi_{\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}}\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}\rVert_{2}^{2}+\lVert\pi_{\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}^{\perp}}\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}\rVert_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\lVert\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}\mathbf{x}^{(k)}-\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}\mathbf{x}^{*}\rVert_{2}^{2}+\min_{\mathbf{x}}\,\lVert\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}\mathbf{x}-\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}\rVert_{2}^{2}\,.

(14)

We then bound the first term by using
\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}\preceq\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}\preceq\beta\,\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}
and (
13
) as follows:

\displaystyle\lVert\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}\mathbf{x}^{(k)}-\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}\mathbf{x}^{*}\rVert_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\lVert\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}^{(k)}-\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}^{*}\rVert_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\alpha^{2k}\,\lVert\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}^{*}-\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}^{(0)}\rVert_{2}^{2}+2\,\left(\frac{1-\alpha^{k}}{1-\alpha}\right)^{2}\times\widehat{\varepsilon}\,\min_{\mathbf{x}}\,\lVert\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}\mathbf{x}-\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}\rVert_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\beta\alpha^{2k}\,\lVert\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}\mathbf{x}^{*}-\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}\mathbf{x}^{(0)}\rVert_{2}^{2}+2\,\left(\frac{1-\alpha^{k}}{1-\alpha}\right)^{2}\times\widehat{\varepsilon}\,\min_{\mathbf{x}}\,\lVert\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}\mathbf{x}-\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}\rVert_{2}^{2}\,.

Putting this bound back into (
14
),

\lVert\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}\mathbf{x}^{(k)}-\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq 2\beta\alpha^{2k}\,\lVert\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}\mathbf{x}^{*}-\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}\mathbf{x}^{(0)}\rVert_{2}^{2}+\left(1+2\widehat{\varepsilon}\,\left(\frac{1-\alpha^{k}}{1-\alpha}\right)^{2}\right)\,\min_{\mathbf{x}}\,\lVert\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}\mathbf{x}-\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}\rVert_{2}^{2}\,.

Setting

k=\left\lceil\frac{\log(\nicefrac{{2\beta}}{{\varepsilon}})}{2\,(\nicefrac{{1}}{{\beta}}-\sqrt{\widehat{\varepsilon}})}\right\rceil\,,

we have

\lVert\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}\mathbf{x}^{(k)}-\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq\varepsilon\,\lVert\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}\mathbf{x}^{*}-\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}\mathbf{x}^{(0)}\rVert_{2}^{2}+\left(1+\frac{2\widehat{\varepsilon}}{(\nicefrac{{1}}{{\beta}}-\sqrt{\widehat{\varepsilon}})^{2}}\right)\,\min_{\mathbf{x}}\,\lVert\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}\mathbf{x}-\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}\rVert_{2}^{2}\,,

which completes the proof with
\mathbf{x}^{(0)}=\bm{0}
.
∎

Remark 3.8
.

To better understand the theorem, observe that

\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}=\mathbf{P}^{\top}\mathbf{P}
is a
\beta
-spectral approximation of
\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}
,

\varepsilon
controls the reducible error
\varepsilon\,\lVert\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}\mathbf{x}^{*}\rVert_{2}^{2}
,
and
(1+\widehat{\varepsilon})
is the error in the approximate least-square update for each
\mathbf{x}^{(k)}
.

Bounding
\beta
.

First, observe that in the case of TD,
we have
\mathbf{P}=\mathbf{A}
, so
\beta=1
.
More generally, if
\textnormal{rank}(\mathbf{A})=s\leq\min\{I,R\}

and
\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{U}\mathbf{\Sigma}\mathbf{V}^{\top}
is a compressed SVD, then
\mathbf{A}
is said to satisfy the
standard incoherence condition
with parameter
\mu

(
Chen 2015
)
if

\max_{i\in[I]}\left\lVert\mathbf{e}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{U}\right\rVert_{2}\leq\sqrt{\frac{\mu s}{I}}\,,\quad\max_{r\in[R]}\left\lVert\mathbf{V}^{\top}\mathbf{e}_{r}\right\rVert_{2}\leq\sqrt{\frac{\mu s}{R}}\,.

The
\lVert\mathbf{e}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{U}\rVert_{2}^{2}
and
\lVert\mathbf{V}^{\top}\mathbf{e}_{r}\rVert_{2}^{2}
values are the leverage scores of the rows and columns of
\mathbf{A}
.
Applying
Cohen et al. 2015
,
if each row of
\mathbf{A}
is observed with probability
p
such that
p\geq\frac{c\mu s\log s}{I}
for some absolute constant
c
, then

\frac{1}{2}\,\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}\preceq\frac{1}{p}\,\mathbf{A}_{\Omega}^{\top}\mathbf{A}_{\Omega}\preceq\frac{3}{2}\,\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}\,,

which gives
\beta=2/p
.
Let
\zeta=\max_{i\in[I]}\lVert\mathbf{a}_{i}\rVert_{2}
, where
\mathbf{a}_{i}
is row
i
of
\mathbf{A}
.
Then, the
\alpha\zeta^{2}
-ridge leverage scores of
\mathbf{A}
(i.e.,
\mathbf{a}_{i}^{\top}(\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}+\alpha\zeta^{2}\cdot\mathbf{I}_{R})^{-1}\mathbf{a}_{i}
), for
\alpha\geq 1
, are at most
1/\alpha
. If
p
is the observation rate, taking
\alpha=\frac{c\log s}{p}
gives the required incoherence condition. This can be done by introducing an
\ell_{2}
-regularization term to the TC optimization problem (i.e., solving a ridge regression problem in each ALS step).
Note that usually
\alpha
can be chosen to be much smaller in practice.

4
Sampling Methods for Tensor Completion

We are now ready to efficiently solve the
unstructured
least-squares problem (
7
) induced by ALS for tensor completion, i.e., for
\mathbf{P}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\Omega|\times R}
and observations
\mathbf{q}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\Omega|}
, find

\mathbf{x}^{*}=\argmin_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{R}}\,\lVert\mathbf{P}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{q}\rVert_{2}^{2}\,.

As in Algorithm
1
,
we lift this problem to higher dimension to get a structured design matrix
\mathbf{A}
,
and use a known fast algorithm for approximately solving the structured least-squares problem in each step of approximate-mini-ALS.
For a given
\widehat{\varepsilon}\in(0,1/\beta^{2})
, the approximate solver computes a solution
\overline{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{R}
in time
O(T_{\widehat{\varepsilon}})
such that

\lVert\mathbf{A}\overline{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{b}\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq(1+\widehat{\varepsilon})\,\min_{\mathbf{x}}\lVert\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{b}\rVert_{2}^{2}\,.

Therefore, for a desired
\varepsilon_{1}\in(0,1)
, we set
\widehat{\varepsilon}=\Theta(\varepsilon_{1}/\beta^{2})
and use a sufficiently small
\varepsilon\leftarrow\varepsilon_{2}
in
Theorem

3.7
.
Putting everything together, Algorithm
1
finds an approximate solution
\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{R}
in time
O(\beta T_{\varepsilon_{1}\beta^{-2}}\log\frac{\beta}{\varepsilon_{2}})
that satisfies

\displaystyle\lVert\mathbf{P}\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{q}\rVert_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq(1+\varepsilon_{1})\,\lVert\pi_{\mathbf{P}^{\perp}}\mathbf{q}\rVert_{2}^{2}+\varepsilon_{2}\,\lVert\mathbf{\pi}_{\mathbf{P}}\mathbf{q}\rVert_{2}^{2}\,,

(15)

where
\mathbf{\pi}_{\mathbf{P}}
and
\mathbf{\pi}_{\mathbf{P}^{\perp}}
are the orthogonal projection matrices into the column space and null space of
\mathbf{P}
, respectively (see
Section

A.1
).
With this in hand, we now present the running times of our lifted iterative method for TC problems
by combining
Theorem

3.7
with state-of-the-art tensor decomposition results based on leverage score sampling.

4.1
CP Completion

Each ALS update step for CP completion solves a regression problem where the design matrix is the Khatri–Rao product:
for
\mathbf{A}^{(k)}\in\mathbb{R}^{I_{k}\times R}
,

\mathbf{A}^{\neq k}:=\bigodot_{n=1,n\neq k}^{N}\mathbf{A}^{(n)}\in\mathbb{R}^{I_{\neq k}\times R}
,
and
\mathbf{Q}=(\mathbf{X}^{\top}_{(k)})_{\Omega}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\Omega|\times I_{k}}
,

\mathbf{A}^{(k)}\leftarrow\argmin_{\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{I_{k}\times R}}\,\bigl\lVert(\mathbf{A}^{\neq k})_{\Omega}\,\mathbf{A}^{\top}-\mathbf{Q}\bigr\rVert_{\textnormal{F}}\,.

The design matrix
(\mathbf{A}^{\neq k})_{\Omega}
does not necessarily have any structure,
so a direct method relies on solving the normal equation, which takes
O(R^{\omega}+R|\Omega|(R+I_{k}))
time.
Thus, the running time of
one round
of ALS, i.e., updating all
N
factors,
is

O\left(N\left(R^{\omega}+R^{2}|\Omega|\right)+R|\Omega|\sum_{n=1}^{N}I_{n}\right).

Previous work on CP tensor decomposition
(
Cheng et al. 2016
;
Larsen and Kolda 2022
;
Bharadwaj et al. 2023
)

developed fast methods for efficiently computing the leverage scores of a Khatri–Rao product matrix.
In particular,
Bharadwaj et al. 2023
designed a data structure for computing and maintaining the
leverage scores of
\mathbf{A}^{\neq k}
during ALS updates.
This approach requires sampling
\tilde{O}(R/\varepsilon)
rows of
\mathbf{A}^{\neq k}
.
Due to the Khatri–Rao product structure, each row of
\mathbf{A}^{\neq k}
can be mapped to a sequence of one choice from the rows of each
\mathbf{A}^{(n)}
for
n\in[N]\backslash\{k\}
.
Hence, sampling a row from
\mathbf{A}^{\neq k}
is equivalent to the following:
for each
n\in[N]\backslash\{k\}
, sample a row from
\mathbf{A}^{(n)}
according to some conditional distribution given sampled rows from
\mathbf{A}^{(1)},\dots,\mathbf{A}^{(n-1)}
, and then compute the Hadamard product of
N-1
sampled rows.
Maintaining the full
I_{n}
-dimensional vector for a conditional probability for each
n
is costly,
so
Bharadwaj et al. 2023
developed a binary tree-based data structure to speed up leverage-score sampling for
\mathbf{A}^{\neq k}
.

Applying
Bharadwaj et al. 2023
, one round of ALS runs in time

\tilde{O}(\varepsilon^{-1}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\left(I_{n}R^{2}+NR^{3}\right)).

Using their CP TD algorithm as the approximate solver in Algorithm
1
, and combining its guarantee with
Theorem

3.7
,
we can extend their approach to CP completion.

Corollary 4.1
.

There is an ALS CP completion algorithm such that
(i) after a factor matrix update, each row of
\mathbf{A}^{(n)}
satisfies (
15
),
and (ii) the total running time of one round is

\tilde{O}\left(\frac{\beta^{2}}{\varepsilon_{1}}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\left(I_{n}R^{2}+NR^{3}\right)\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{2}}\right)\,.

There is
no dependence
on
|\Omega|
in the running time due to leverage score sampling, i.e., it runs in sublinear time.

4.2
Tucker Completion

Fahrbach et al. 2022
designed block-sketching techniques and fast Kronecker product-matrix multiplication algorithms
to exploit the ALS structure for Tucker decomposition.

4.2.1
Core Tensor Update

Recall that for a Tucker decomposition we use the notation
I=\prod_{n\in[N]}I_{n}
and
R=\prod_{n\in[N]}R_{n}
.
The ALS core tensor update in the Tucker completion problem is

\bm{\mathscr{G}}\leftarrow\argmin_{\bm{\mathscr{G}}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{R_{1}\times\cdots\times R_{N}}}\left\lVert\left(\bigotimes_{n=1}^{N}\mathbf{A}^{(n)}\right)_{\Omega}\hskip-5.69046pt\vvec(\bm{\mathscr{G}}^{\prime})-\vvec(\bm{\mathscr{X}})_{\Omega}\right\rVert_{2}.

The design matrix above restricted to
\Omega
is exactly
\mathbf{P}
in our general setup.

We compare the running times of the direct method and our lifting approach.
In the former, we can compute an exact solution to a least-squares problem
\mathbf{x}^{*}=(\mathbf{P}^{\top}\mathbf{P})^{-1}\mathbf{P}^{\top}\mathbf{q}
in time
O(|\Omega|R^{2}+R^{\omega})
.

To achieve a fast lifted method, we solve the second step of Algorithm
1

using the leverage score sampling-based
core tensor update algorithm in
(
Fahrbach et al. 2022
, Theorem 1.2)

with running time

\tilde{O}\left(\sum_{n=1}^{N}\left(I_{n}R_{n}+\frac{R_{n}^{\omega}N^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right)+\frac{R^{2-\theta^{*}}}{\varepsilon}\right)\,,

where
\theta^{*}>0
is an optimizable constant depending on
\{R_{n}\}_{n\in[N]}
.
Using this as the
approx-least-squares
subroutine in
Theorem

3.7
, we achieve the following.

Corollary 4.2
.

There is an algorithm that computes an ALS Tucker completion core tensor update satisfying (
15
)
in time

\tilde{O}\left(\left(\sum_{n=1}^{N}\left(I_{n}R_{n}+\beta^{4}R_{n}^{\omega}N^{2}\varepsilon_{1}^{-2}\right)+\frac{\beta^{2}R^{2-\theta^{*}}}{\varepsilon_{1}}\right)\beta\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{2}}\right)\,.

(16)

4.2.2
Factor Matrix Update

The ALS factor matrix update for
\mathbf{A}^{(k)}
in the Tucker completion problem is

\mathbf{A}^{(k)}\leftarrow\hskip-2.84544pt\argmin_{\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{I_{k}\times R_{k}}}\left\lVert\left(\left(\bigotimes_{n=1,n\neq k}^{N}\hskip-2.84544pt\mathbf{A}^{(n)}\right)\mathbf{G}_{(k)}^{\top}\right)_{\Omega}\hskip-2.84544pt\mathbf{A}^{\top}\hskip-2.84544pt-\mathbf{Q}\right\rVert_{\textnormal{F}},

where
\mathbf{Q}=(\mathbf{X}^{\top}_{(k)})_{\Omega}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\Omega|\times I_{k}}
is a sparse matrix of observations.
The running time of a direct method that solves the normal equation is

O(R_{k}^{\omega}+R_{k}|\Omega|(R_{\neq k}+R_{k}+I_{k}))
, where
R_{\neq k}=R/R_{k}
.

The running time of the sampling-based factor-matrix update for
\mathbf{A}^{(k)}
in
Fahrbach et al. 2022

for the full decomposition problem is

\tilde{O}\left(\sum_{n=1}^{N}\left(I_{n}R_{n}+\frac{R_{n}^{\omega}N^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}+I_{k}RR_{n}\right)+\frac{I_{k}R_{\neq k}^{2-\theta^{*}}}{\varepsilon}\right)\,.

Combining this result with
Theorem

3.7
,
Algorithm
1
has the following running time for a factor matrix update.

Corollary 4.3
.

There is an algorithm that computes an ALS Tucker completion factor matrix update for
\mathbf{A}^{(k)}
,
with each row of
\mathbf{A}^{(k)}
satisfying (
15
),
in time

\textstyle\tilde{O}\left(\left(\sum_{n=1}^{N}\left(I_{n}R_{n}+\beta^{4}R_{n}^{\omega}N^{2}\varepsilon_{1}^{-2}\right)+\frac{\beta^{2}I_{k}R_{\neq k}^{2-\theta^{*}}}{\varepsilon_{1}}+I_{k}R\sum_{n=1}^{N}R_{n}\right)\beta\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{2}}\right)\,.

4.3
TT Completion

Each ALS step for TT decomposition solves the following least-squares problem
with a Kronecker product-type design matrix:
for
\mathbf{A}^{\neq k}:=\mathbf{A}_{<k}\otimes\mathbf{A}^{\top}_{>k}\in\mathbb{R}^{I_{\neq k}\times(R_{k-1}R_{k})}
and
\mathbf{Q}=(\mathbf{X}_{(k)}^{\top})_{\Omega}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\Omega|\times I_{k}}
,

\bm{\mathscr{A}}^{(k)}\leftarrow\argmin_{\bm{\mathscr{B}}\in\mathbb{R}^{R_{k-1}\times I_{k}\times R_{k}}}\left\lVert\left(\mathbf{A}^{\neq k}\right)_{\Omega}\,(\mathbf{B}_{(2)})^{\top}-\mathbf{Q}\right\rVert_{\textnormal{F}}\,.

Solving this directly with the normal equation takes

O(\bar{R}_{k}^{\omega}+\bar{R}_{k}|\Omega|(\bar{R}_{k}+I_{k}))
time for
\bar{R}_{k}:=R_{k-1}R_{k}
.
Thus, the time for one round of ALS is

O\left(\sum_{n=1}^{N}\left(\bar{R}_{n}^{\omega}+\bar{R}_{n}|\Omega|\left(\bar{R}_{n}+I_{n}\right)\right)\right).

Bharadwaj et al. 2024
proposed a sampling-based ALS algorithm that crucially relies a
canonical form
of the TT decomposition with respect to the index
k
. Any TT decomposition can be converted to this form through a QR decomposition, and this form ensures that
(\mathbf{A}^{\neq k})^{\top}\mathbf{A}^{\neq k}=I_{R_{k-1}R_{k}}
.
It follows that the leverage scores of
\mathbf{A}^{\neq k}
are simply the diagonal entries of
\mathbf{A}^{\neq k}(\mathbf{A}^{\neq k})^{\top}=(\mathbf{A}_{<k}\mathbf{A}_{<k}^{\top})\otimes(\mathbf{A}_{>k}^{\top}\mathbf{A}_{>k})
.

It follows from properties of the Kronecker product
(
Diao et al. 2019
)
that

\ell_{i^{\neq k}}(\mathbf{A}^{\neq k})=\ell_{i_{<k}}(\mathbf{A}_{<k})\cdot\ell_{i_{>k}}(\mathbf{A}^{\top}_{>k})\,,

where
i^{\neq k}=\underline{i_{1}\cdots i_{k-1}i_{k+1}\cdots i_{N}}
,
i_{<k}=\underline{i_{1}\cdots i_{k-1}}
, and
i_{>k}=\underline{i_{k+1}\cdots i_{N}}
(see
Section

A.3
for notation).
Therefore, efficient leverage score sampling for
\mathbf{A}^{\neq k}
reduces to that for
\mathbf{A}_{<k}
and
\mathbf{A}_{>k}
.
To this end,
Bharadwaj et al. 2024
adopt an approach similar to
Bharadwaj et al. 2023
for leverage score-based CP decomposition.
Each row of
\mathbf{A}_{<k}
corresponds to a series of one slice for each third-order tensor
\mathbf{A}^{(n)}
for
n<k
, which results in a series of conditional sampling steps using a data structure adapted from the one used for CP decomposition.
In contrast,
Bharadwaj et al. 2024
show that one round of approximate TT-core updates,
if
R_{n}=R
for all
n\in[N-1]
, can run in time

\tilde{O}(R^{4}\varepsilon^{-1}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\left(N+I_{n}\right))
,
which leads to the following result.

Corollary 4.4
.

There is an ALS TT completion algorithm such that
(i) after a TT-core update, each row fiber of
\bm{\mathscr{A}}^{(n)}

satisfies (
15
),
and (ii) the total running time of one round is

\tilde{O}\left(\frac{\beta^{2}R^{4}}{\varepsilon_{1}}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\left(N+I_{n}\right)\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{2}}\right)\,.

6
Conclusion

This work shows how to lift TC problems to a larger structured problem
and use fast sketching-based TD algorithms as ALS subroutines,
extending their guarantees to the TC setting and establishing novel connections to iterative methods.
We prove guarantees for the convergence rate of an approximate version of the Richardson iteration,
and we study how these algorithms perform in practice on real-world tensors.
One interesting future direction is to analyze the speedup that adaptive step sizes give in
accelerated-mini-als
.

A.1
Least-Squares Linear Regression

For a design matrix
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
and response
\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, consider the least-squares problem

\mathbf{x}^{*}=\argmin_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\,\lVert\mathbf{Ax}-\mathbf{b}\rVert_{2}\,.

The solution
\mathbf{x}^{*}
can be obtained by solving the
normal equation

\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}^{*}=\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{b}
.
Therefore,
\mathbf{x}^{*}=(\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A})^{-1}\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{b}
.
If
\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}
is singular, then we can use the
pseudoinverse

\mathbf{A}^{+}
.

The
orthogonal projection matrix

\pi_{\mathbf{A}}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
onto the image space of
\mathbf{A}
is defined by

\pi_{\mathbf{A}}=\mathbf{A}\,(\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A})^{-1}\mathbf{A}^{\top}
, and satisfies
\pi_{\mathbf{A}}^{2}=\pi_{\mathbf{A}}
and
\lVert\pi_{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{v}\rVert_{2}\leq\lVert\mathbf{v}\rVert_{2}
for any
\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Recall that any
\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
can be uniquely decomposed as
\mathbf{v}=\pi_{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{v}+\pi_{\mathbf{A}^{\perp}}\mathbf{v}
, where
\pi_{\mathbf{A}^{\perp}}=\mathbf{I}_{n}-\pi_{\mathbf{A}}
is the orthogonal projection to the orthogonal subspace of
\textnormal{colsp}(\mathbf{A})
.

Given
\mathbf{b}=\pi_{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{b}+\pi_{\mathbf{A}^{\perp}}\mathbf{b}
, the first term is the
reducible error
by regressing
\mathbf{b}
on
\mathbf{x}
,
i.e., taking the optimum
\mathbf{x}^{*}
so that
\mathbf{Ax}^{*}=\pi_{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{b}
.
The second term
\pi_{\mathbf{A}^{\perp}}\mathbf{b}
is the
irreducible error
, i.e.,
\min\,\lVert\mathbf{Ax}-\mathbf{b}\rVert_{2}=\lVert\pi_{\mathbf{A}^{\perp}}\mathbf{b}\rVert_{2}
.

A.2
Leverage Score Sampling for Tensor Decomposition

ALS formulations show how each tensor decomposition step reduces to solving a least-squares problem of the form

\min_{\mathbf{x}}\,\lVert\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{b}\rVert_{2}
with a highly structured
\mathbf{A}
.
While we can find the optimum in closed form via
(\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A})^{+}\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{b}
, matrix
\mathbf{A}
has
I=I_{1}\cdots I_{N}
rows corresponding to each entry of the tensor (i.e., it is a tall skinny matrix), which can make using the normal equation challenging in practice.

Randomized sketching methods are a popular approach to approximately solving this problem with faster running times with high probability.
In general, these approach sample rows of
\mathbf{A}
according to the probability distribution defined by the
leverage scores
of rows, resulting in a random sketching matrix
\mathbf{S}
whose height is much smaller than that of
\mathbf{A}
. For a matrix
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{I\times R}
with (
I\gg R
), the leverage scores of
\mathbf{A}
is the vector
\ell\in[0,1]^{I}
defined by

\ell_{i}\defeq\bigl(\mathbf{A}\,(\mathbf{A^{\top}A})^{+}\mathbf{A}^{\top}\bigr)_{ii}\,.

Then, for a given
\varepsilon,\delta\in(0,1)
, the sketching algorithm samples
\tilde{O}(\nicefrac{{R}}{{\varepsilon\delta}})
many rows, where the
i
-th row is drawn with probability
\ell_{i}/\sum_{i}\ell_{i}=\ell_{i}/\text{rank}\,(\mathbf{A})
.
With probability at least
1-\delta
, we can guarantee that

\min_{\mathbf{x}}\,\lVert\mathbf{S}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{S}\mathbf{b}\rVert_{2}\leq(1+\varepsilon)\,\min_{\mathbf{x}}\,\lVert\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{b}\rVert_{2}\,.

The reduced number of rows in
\mathbf{SA}
leads to better running times for the least-squares solves.
However, naïvely computing leverage scores takes as long as computing the closed-form optimum since we need to compute
(\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A})^{+}
.
This is where the
structure
of the design matrix
\mathbf{A}
comes in to play, i.e., to speed up the leverage score computations.

A.3
Tensor-Train Decomposition

Given a tensor-train (TT) decomposition
\{\bm{\mathscr{A}}^{(n)}\}_{n=1}^{N}
and index
n\in[N]
, define the

left chain

\mathbf{A}_{<n}\in\mathbb{R}^{(I_{1}\cdots I_{n-1})\times R_{n-1}}

and
the
right chain

\mathbf{A}_{>n}\in\mathbb{R}^{R_{n}\times(I_{n+1}\cdots I_{N})}
as:

\displaystyle a_{<n}(\underline{i_{1}\dots i_{n-1}},r_{n-1})

\displaystyle=\sum_{r_{0},\dots,r_{n-1}}\prod_{k=1}^{n-1}a^{(k)}_{r_{k-1}i_{k}r_{k}}

\displaystyle a_{>n}(r_{n},\underline{i_{n+1}\dots i_{N}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{r_{n+1},\dots,r_{N}}\prod_{k=n+1}^{N}a^{(k)}_{r_{k-1}i_{k}r_{k}}\,,

where for any
i_{s}\in[I_{s}]
with
s(\neq n)\in[N]
,
\underline{i_{1}\dots i_{n-1}}:=1+\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}(i_{k}-1)\prod_{j=1}^{k-1}I_{j}
and
\underline{i_{n+1}\dots i_{N}}:=1+\sum_{k=n+1}^{N}(i_{k}-1)\prod_{j=n+1}^{k-1}I_{j}
.
When ALS optimizes
\bm{\mathscr{A}}^{(n)}
with all other TT-cores fixed, it solves the regression problem:

\bm{\mathscr{A}}^{(n)}\!\!\leftarrow\!\!\argmin_{\bm{\mathscr{B}}\in\mathbb{R}^{R_{n-1}\times I_{n}\times R_{n}}}\bigl\lVert(\mathbf{A}_{<n}\otimes\mathbf{A}^{\top}_{>n})\,\mathbf{B}_{(2)}^{\top}-\mathbf{X}_{(n)}^{\top}\bigr\rVert_{\textnormal{F}}\,,

which is equivalent to solving
I_{n}
Kronecker regression problems in
\mathbb{R}^{\prod_{k\neq n}I_{k}}
.

A.4
Tensor Networks

A
tensor network
(TN) is a powerful framework that can represent any factorization of a tensor,
so it can recover the three decompositions above as special cases.
A TN decomposition
\text{TN}(\bm{\mathscr{A}}^{(1)},\dots,\bm{\mathscr{A}}^{(N)})
represents a given tensor
\bm{\mathscr{X}}
with
N
tensors
\bm{\mathscr{A}}^{(1)},\dots,\bm{\mathscr{A}}^{(N)}
and a
tensor diagram
. As in the above decompositions, the goal is to compute

\argmin_{\bm{\mathscr{A}}^{(1)},\dots,\bm{\mathscr{A}}^{(N)}}\,\lVert\bm{\mathscr{X}}-\text{TN}(\bm{\mathscr{A}}^{(1)},\dots,\bm{\mathscr{A}}^{(N)})\rVert_{\textnormal{F}}^{2}\,.

A tensor diagram
1
1

1

We refer readers to
https://tensornetwork.org/diagrams/
for more details.
consists of nodes with dangling edges, where a node indicates a tensor, and its dangling edge represents a mode, so that the number of dangling edges is the order of the tensor. For example, a node without an edge indicates a scalar, one with one dangling edge is a vector, and one with two dangling edges is a matrix.

When two dangling edges of two nodes are connected, we say that the two tensors are
contracted
along that mode (i.e., a mode product of those two tensors). For example, when a node with two dangling edges shares one edge with another node with one dangling edge, it indicates a matrix-vector multiplication. Hence, the number of unmatched dangling edges in a tensor diagram corresponds to the order of its representing tensor.

ALS for TN decomposition.

Given a TN decomposition
\{\bm{\mathscr{A}}^{(n)}\}_{n\in[N]}
, when ALS optimizes a tensor
\bm{\mathscr{A}}^{(n)}
with all others fixed, it solves a linear regression problem of the form

\bm{\mathscr{A}}^{(n)}\leftarrow\argmin_{\bm{\mathscr{B}}}\,\lVert\mathbf{A}_{\neq n}\mathbf{B}-\mathbf{X}\rVert_{\textnormal{F}}\,,

where
\mathbf{A}_{\neq n}
is an appropriate matricization depending on
\bm{\mathscr{A}}^{(1)},\dots,\bm{\mathscr{A}}^{(n-1)},\bm{\mathscr{A}}^{(n+1)},\dots,\bm{\mathscr{A}}^{(N)}
, and
\mathbf{B}
and
\mathbf{X}
are suitable matricizations of
\bm{\mathscr{B}}
and
\bm{\mathscr{X}}
, respectively. Structure of
\mathbf{A}_{\neq n}
can be specified through a new tensor diagram obtained by removing the node of
\bm{\mathscr{A}}^{(n)}
from the original tensor network diagram.

Just as the ALS approaches for other decomposition algorithms,
Malik et al. 2022
proposed a sampling-based approach via leverage scores. First of all, they pointed out that
\mathbf{A}_{\neq n}^{\top}\mathbf{A}_{\neq n}
can be efficiently computed by exploiting inherent structure of
\mathbf{A}_{\neq n}
(i.e., contract a series of matched edges in a tensor diagram in an appropriate order). They then presented a leverage-score sampling method that draws rows of
\mathbf{A}_{\neq n}
without materializing a full probability vector, and in spirit this approach is similar to one used for the CP decomposition in
Section

4.1
.

Warm-Up: Coupled Matrix Problem

We consider the coupled matrix problem of the following form
(
Baksalary and Kala 1980
)

\mathbf{A}\mathbf{X}\mathbf{B}^{\top}+\mathbf{C}\mathbf{Y}\mathbf{D}^{\top}=\mathbf{E}\,,

d\times d

is an
n\times n

For a fixed
\mathbf{Y}

\argmin_{\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}}\lVert(\mathbf{B}\otimes\mathbf{A})\vvec(\mathbf{X})-\vvec(\mathbf{E}-\mathbf{C}\mathbf{Y}\mathbf{D}^{\top})\rVert_{2}\,.

(17)

Matrix
\mathbf{Y}

Thus, we can apply an alternating minimization algorithm for computing
\mathbf{X}
and
\mathbf{Y}

For our experiments, we initialize

B.2.2
Accelerated Methods

We explain how to accelerate the Richardson iteration.

1.

For odd iterations (e.g., the first iteration), run mini-ALS normally.

2.

For even iterations, compute
\widehat{\mathbf{x}}^{(k+1)}
using normal mini-ALS, but then set

\mathbf{x}^{(k+1)}=\mathbf{x}^{(k)}+\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\left(\widehat{\mathbf{x}}^{(k+1)}-\mathbf{x}^{(k)}\right),

where
\alpha=\frac{\lVert\widehat{\mathbf{x}}^{(k+1)}-\mathbf{x}^{(k)}\rVert_{2}}{\lVert\mathbf{x}^{(k)}-\mathbf{x}^{(k-1)}\rVert_{2}}
.

Note that setting
\alpha=0
is equivalent to running mini-ALS normally. We explain the intuition behind this accelration with the example in
Figure

4
, which illustrates the case where
x
and
b_{\overline{\Omega}}
both have only one variable. As mentioned previously, the lifted problem is a convex quadratic problem (see
Lemma

3.2
), and the iterations of mini-ALS alternate between optimizing
x
and optimizing
b_{\overline{\Omega}}
.
In
Figure

4
, the star (
\star
) denotes the optimal point, and the ellipses denote the level sets of the quadratic function. In the steps where we optimize
x
, we search for the point on the line that is parallel to the
x
axis that crosses our current point and touches the smallest ellipse among the points on the line. Similarly, when we update
b_{\overline{\Omega}}
we search on the line that is parallel to the
b_{\overline{\Omega}}
axis.

Following the points in
Figure

4
, one can see that the points obtained through our iterations in the one-variable case form similar triangles, where the ratio of corresponding sides for every two consecutive triangles are the same.
Therefore, if we denote the side length of the first triangle with
1
, then the side length for the next triangles are
\alpha,\alpha^{2},\alpha^{3},\ldots
.
Using the notation in
Figure

4
,
\alpha=\frac{\ell^{(2)}}{\ell^{(1)}}
. The sum over this geometric series is equal to
\frac{1}{1-\alpha}
, which inspires our
adaptive step size
in even iterations.

Note that in the one-variable case, we can recover the optimal solution with only two iterations as the lines connecting
(x^{(k)},b_{\overline{\Omega}}^{(k-1)})
go through the optimal solution. While this does not necessarily hold in higher-dimensional settings, our experiments demonstrate that acceleration improves the number of iterations and the running time of our approach significantly, especially when the number of observed entries are small (i.e., when
\beta
is large).
