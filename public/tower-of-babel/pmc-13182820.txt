Peripheral nerve sheath tumors-on-a-chip: Next-generation platforms for mechanistic and therapeutic studies
Authors: William H Delaney, Sara A Faulkner, Wilbur A Lam, Nicholas M Boulis, Yongzhi Qiu, Kecheng Lei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRecapitulation of the complex nerve–tumor–immune microenvironment is critical for understanding Peripheral Nerve Sheath Tumors (PNSTs). Conventional in vitro models and animal systems often struggle to model crucial intercellular and inter-organ communication with full physiological relevance, creating a bottleneck in discovering effective therapeutics. Organ-on-Chip (OoC) technologies offer a paradigm-shifting solution. These microengineered platforms precisely integrate human cell biology with controllable fluidic and mechanical cues to replicate tissue- and organ-level physiology, enabling real-time, quantitative monitoring of tissue dynamics. This review posits that OoC technology is poised to revolutionize PNST research. In this review, we summarize the development of advanced Tumor-Nerve-Immune-on-Chip systems that integrate patient-derived Schwann cells, immune components, and functional microvasculature. These systems will accurately model PNSTs. Adoption of OoC will enable a predictive and individualized experimental framework, accelerating therapeutic discovery and allowing for personalized drug prediction for these challenging diseases.Keywords: Peripheral nerve sheath tumor, Organ-on-Chip, Microfluidics, Disease modeling, Translational research

Background: peripheral nerve sheath tumors

Peripheral nerve sheath tumors (PNST) are tumors that arise from cells that form the protective covering of peripheral nerves [1,2]. These tumors range from benign to malignant. Common benign types include neurofibromas which are associated with Neurofibromatosis type 1 (NF1), and schwannomas which are associated with Neurofibromatosis type 2 (NF2), and Schwannoma (SWN) [1–3]. The main malignant type is the malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumor (MPNST) [2]. Benign neurofibromas can be cutaneous or plexiform. Cutaneous neurofibromas are soft, benign tumors that grow under the skin and appear as skin-colored bumps or lumps, whereas plexiform neurofibromas are benign tumors that develop within the tissue covering nerves [3,4]. Plexiform neurofibromas can cause significant pain and dysfunction, and in a subset of NF1 patients, can undergo malignant transformation into MPNST [3]. MPNSTs are soft-tissue sarcomas with a poor prognosis. They are often associated with complex genetic alterations [2]. Across all forms, the hallmark features are progressive tumor growth, chronic pain, and neurological dysfunction [5]. Because PNSTs frequently arise from genetic disorders (like NF1, NF2, and SWN) and involve unique nerve microenvironments, specialized research models are needed to study their biology and develop therapies [2].

PNSTs are nerve-associated lesions driven by neoplastic Schwann lineage cells within a multicellular tumor microenvironment [6,7]. In NF1, neurofibromas arise from Schwann cells carrying biallelic NF1 mutations, but these lesions contain a mixture of other cell types, including fibroblasts, endothelial cells, and immune cells [8]. Studies in both patients and NF1-mutant mouse models show that neurofibroma formation requires not only NF1 loss in Schwann cells but also NF1-haploinsufficient stromal cells [8]. In NF2 and SWN, the primary tumors formed are schwannomas as well as meningiomas in some cases of NF2 [9]. Schwannomas are composed of proliferating Schwann cells, perineurial and glial cells, axons, blood vessels, and stromal cells [10]. This complex tumor microenvironment makes schwannomas highly heterogeneous [11]. Key cellular components of PNSTs include non-neoplastic fibroblasts and vascular endothelial cells, as well as infiltrating mast cells and macrophages, with smaller clusters of T lymphocytes reported in neurofibroma tissue [6]. Tumor Schwann cells secrete chemoattractant signals, such as SCF and CSF1 that recruit mast cells and macrophages, sustaining inflammatory signaling linked to tumor growth and stromal remodeling [12]. The 3D architecture is rich in collagen type XVIII and fibrotic, with tumor and nerve-resident cell types mingled in a highly disorganized extracellular matrix (ECM) that constitutes more than 50% of tumor dry weight [13,14]. Mast cell-derived profibrotic signaling, such as TGF-β, promotes fibroblast proliferation and collagen deposition, reinforcing an ECM-dominated microenvironment that shapes spatial organization and transport within the lesion [6,12]. Biomechanical cues from this remodeled ECM, including increased matrix stiffness and cell spreading, activate mechanotransduction programs in Schwann cells through RhoA and YAP/TAZ signaling, linking mechanics to cell state and disease behavior [15,16]. Nuclear YAP/TAZ links mechanical tension to cell cycle gene programs, and, in stiff environments, mechanotransduction favors proliferation programs over repair, aligning with maladaptive growth seen in PNST biology [15]. The interaction between neoplastic Schwann cells and their surrounding stromal cells is a critical determinant of tumor progression, recurrence risk, and therapeutic outcomes for PNSTs (Fig. 1).

Despite the substantial morbidity associated with these conditions, effective therapies remain limited. Surgery is the primary treatment method, but it is often insufficient due to tumor infiltration and recurrence [4,27]. Currently, two drug treatments are FDA-approved for NF1, and one drug is in trial for NF2. Selumetinib and Mirdametinib were approved in 2020 and 2025, respectively. Both drugs are selective MEK1/2 inhibitors that target an overactive RAS-MAPK pathway caused by NF1 gene loss [28]. They can reduce the size of plexiform neurofibroma and relieve symptoms, but responses are partial; therefore, tumor regrowth is common [28]. Bevacizumab is not FDA-approved and is in phase 2 of its clinical trial to treat NF2. Bevacizumab is a VEGF-neutralizing antibody being studied to treat vestibular schwannomas by shrinking tumors and improving hearing through angiogenesis inhibition [29–31]. Together, these drugs represent advances in neurofibromatosis therapy but are not curative. Therefore, most patients are left dependent on ongoing surveillance and repeated intervention.

Across NF1, NF2, and SWN, the unifying reality is that patients endure a lifelong burden of tumor growth, pain, neurological dysfunction, and disfigurement [32]. The visible nature of cutaneous tumors in NF1 can cause stigma and social isolation, while vestibular schwannomas in NF2 often lead to deafness and balance problems that erode independence [3,19,29]. In SWN, relentless pain becomes the defining feature of the disease, frequently resistant to conventional analgesics [10]. These clinical realities extend beyond physical health and affect quality of life for both patients and their families.

It is challenging for current PNST models to reproduce the multicellular dependency of tumor growth observed in neurofibromatosis, in which neoplastic Schwann cells require stromal fibroblasts, endothelial cells, and immune cells to initiate and sustain tumor growth [8]. Existing 2D and spheroid systems also fail to reproduce the aligned three-dimensional architecture of peripheral nerves, including axon fasciculation and perineurial barriers, which shape tumor expansion and invasion [33]. 2D cell models lack stable cell-cell junction topology and apicobasal polarity, native ECM architecture and stiffness, and spatial gradients of oxygen, nutrients, cytokines, and drugs. Biomechanical features of nerve tissue, including ECM viscoelasticity, tissue stiffness, and interstitial flow, are poorly represented in conventional models despite evidence that these cues are critical for regulating Schwann cell phenotype and therapeutic responses [34]. Immune-driven remodeling, including macrophage and mast cell mediated fibrosis and angiogenesis, remains difficult to sustain in long term culture, limiting mechanistic studies of tumor immune crosstalk [35]. Furthermore, prolonged cell culture can induce genetic drift, which alters gene expression in the studied cells [36]. Thus, 2D cell cultures often fail to capture tumor architecture and cellular interactions. 3D organoid and co-culture models better mimic tumor architecture and have also been used to study PNST [37]. In these models, multiple tumor cell types are co-cultured in a 3D matrix to form tumor-like spheroids or tissues. One study demonstrated that combining NF1−/− Schwann cells with other supporting cells in a matrix can give rise to neurofibroma-like tissue in vitro [23]. However, these systems are harder to scale and lack key components of real tumors, such as blood vessels and immune cells. These biological limitations directly translate into therapeutic blind spots. Together, these gaps define the core rationale for developing PNST-specific Organ-on-Chip (OoC) systems that integrate a multicellular architecture, nerve-aligned structure, biomechanical context, and controlled drug delivery.

Introduction to OoC

An OoC is a microengineered device that integrates living human cells into a controlled microfluidic system that mimics the architecture, biomechanical cues, and functional outputs of human tissues [38]. These devices move far beyond the simplicity of static 2D cell cultures that lack physiological flow interactions. OoCs enable the integration of spheroids from multiple cell types to recreate the organ microenvironment. OoC platforms aim to reduce specific translational gaps of animal models by incorporating human cells, tissue-relevant mechanics, and perfused microenvironments. However, their predictive value remains context-dependent and requires further clinical validation [39,40]. Another advantage of OoCs is their ability to recapitulate tissue–tissue interfaces within a microenvironment that incorporates fluid flow and nutrient exchange [41]. This capability provides a closer approximation to human tissue-scale physiology and cell-specific responses than static in vitro models, while still lacking systemic integration, endocrine signaling, immune crosstalk, and whole body pharmacokinetics present in living organisms. Some microfluidic chips incorporate aligned nanofibers to model peripheral nerve regeneration, enabling analysis of topographical cue effects on axonal growth cones and supporting drug screening for peripheral nerve injury [42]. This class of microfluidic systems, which integrates aligned nanofibers for controlled axonal guidance and drug screening, demonstrates the transformative potential of OoC technology to move beyond simple 2D culture and recapitulate the complex structural and functional microenvironment of human peripheral nerves in vitro.

OoC systems differ from static 3D models such as spheroids or organoids by incorporating controlled microfluidic perfusion and defined shear stress, which recreate key aspects of tissue-level transport and mechanical signaling [41,43,44]. In static 3D cultures, diffusion alone governs nutrient delivery, waste removal, and drug exposure, leading to nonphysiological gradients and limited control over mechanical cues [37,39]. In contrast, OoC platforms can apply continuous or pulsatile flow to regulate metabolite transport while exposing cells to tunable shear forces known to shape endothelial function and tumor cell behavior [38,43,45,46]. These dynamic transport and biomechanical features enable OoC models to reproduce vascular delivery and interstitial flow that are more physiologically relevant than static 3D cultures [47] (Fig. 2).

Development of OoC

Early 21st-century pioneers such as Michael Shuler at Cornell recognized that microfluidic control could sustain mammalian cells in more physiologically relevant environments [49,50]. Shuler envisioned multi-organ chips for toxicity testing. Early efforts revealed new challenges: shear stress could damage sensitive cells; flow had to balance nutrient delivery with protection from differentiation [48]. Also, a reliable long-term culture required precise control of oxygen and pH [48]. Solutions to this challenge included optimized flow channels and eventually the integration of external life-support systems (e.g., syringe pumps, peristaltic pumps) [51,52]. This period laid the groundwork for OoC by demonstrating that 3D structures, multiple cell types, and controlled perfusion were essential for creating predictive biological models.

The appeal of OoCs lies in their ability to reproduce healthy organ function and versatile disease modeling [53]. By combining patient-derived cells with engineered microenvironments, OoCs can recapitulate pathological states such as chronic inflammation, tumor growth, or neurovascular dysfunction [53–55]. For instance, Joint-on-a-chip (JoC) technology has emerged to model osteoarthritis by simulating the complex activities of cartilage, subchondral bone, and synovium [56,57]. This approach enables the study of mechanisms that cannot be adequately captured in animals or static cultures, such as the role of mechanical strain in amplifying inflammatory responses or the interaction between tumor cells and perfused vasculature. Furthermore, OoCs can integrate multiple tissue types into “multi-organ” or “human-on-a-chip” systems, which allow researchers to study drug absorption, distribution, metabolism, and toxicity in a physiologically relevant manner [39,58].

Lung-on-chip

Lung-on-chip was one of the earliest landmarks, introduced in 2010 [44]. This OoC was created using polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS), a material commonly used in microfluidics to fabricate a two-channel device separated by a thin, porous membrane [59]. Human alveolar epithelial cells and capillary endothelial cells were cultured on opposite sides of the membrane, while vacuum chambers along the sides of the lung-on-chip applied cyclic stretching to mimic breathing motions. This engineering innovation was critical for reproducing lung-specific biomechanics [60]. Biologically, the model revealed that cyclic strain amplified inflammatory and toxic responses to nanoparticles, proving that physical forces are indispensable to modeling lung physiology and disease [44].

Gut-on-chip

Shortly thereafter, the gut-on-a-chip expanded the concept of dynamic organ modeling [61]. The engineering advance here was the integration of microfluidic flow with flexible membranes, which generated peristalsis-like motions [62]. This setup created luminal and basal channels, which enabled both nutrient perfusion and waste removal [63]. Under these conditions, intestinal epithelial cells spontaneously organized into three-dimensional villus-like structures, displaying crypt–villus differentiation gradients, mucus secretion, and CYP3A4-mediated drug metabolism [62]. The ability to co-culture with commensal bacteria further highlighted the importance of combining microengineering with biological complexity [63]. Without the mechanical cues and fluid flow provided by the chip, these features could not be replicated in traditional 2D cultures.

Blood-brain barrier-on-chip

The blood–brain barrier (BBB)-on-a-chip addressed one of the most challenging physiological interfaces [64–66]. Engineers introduced microchannels lined with brain microvascular endothelial cells, combined with pericytes and astrocytes cultured under conditions of shear stress [66,67]. Advances in fabrication allowed for tighter control over microchannels and surface coatings, thereby facilitating the formation of tight junctions [66]. These chips integrated Transepithelial Electrical Resistance (TEER) electrodes, which allow real-time monitoring of barrier integrity and permeability [66,68]. This represented a technical leap in quantitative assessment of drug transport and inflammatory responses. This quantitative ability provides a far more predictive platform than conventional 2D assays.

From there, the field advanced toward brain-on-chip platforms that captured multicellular complexity. A notable innovation was the development of tetra-culture systems that brought together endothelial cells, astrocytes, neurons, and microglia within a perfused microfluidic network. These devices often relied on multilayer fabrication techniques and membrane-free designs to allow direct cell–cell interactions across compartments. Continuous perfusion sustained long-term viability and functional crosstalk among cell types. Engineering innovations in membrane-free designs and precise flow control were key to capturing these neurovascular interactions [69].

Tumor-on-a-chip

While OoC systems were first developed for physiological organs such as the lung or gut, some of the most transformative applications have emerged in oncology. Here, the major engineering advance was the incorporation of pre-formed perfused vascular networks within the OoC to deliver nutrients, drugs, and signaling molecules [55]. In 2010, Moll, C. et al. used soft lithography and hydrogel patterning to create vascular channels adjacent to tumor compartments seeded with either cell lines or patient-derived tumor organoids [70]. These systems allowed visualization of angiogenesis, invasion, and intravasation events in real time [47,55,70]. Furthermore, integration with imaging and biosensors enabled quantitative analysis of tumor growth and drug response [47, 55]. Parallel advances in metastasis-on-chip devices added further layers of complexity by modeling extravasation and organ-specific colonization [71].

Tumor-on-chip systems have also been adapted to incorporate patient-derived tumor organoids (PDTOs), preserving intratumoral heterogeneity and clinical relevance [72,73]. When tested with chemotherapeutic drugs such as paclitaxel and targeted agents such as bevacizumab, these platforms demonstrated variable responses that closely aligned with clinical outcomes [55]. This ability to predict patient-specific drug responses in vitro underscores the potential of tumor-on-chip technology to serve as a precision oncology tool.

Beyond drug testing, patient-derived models offer opportunities for studying mechanisms of resistance [74]. For instance, the co-culture of PDTOs with perfused vasculature has been used to assess how hypoxia and stromal interactions contribute to therapeutic failure [55]. Also, the ability to integrate fibroblasts, immune cells, and ECM components into OoC platforms enables researchers to dissect these interactions with unprecedented resolution [45,55,75]. Such systems provide quantitative data on invasion speed, vessel permeability, and drug penetration, bridging a critical gap between cell culture and in vivo biology [76].

Human on chip

Human-on-chip systems use microfluidic links to connect multiple OoC units, so they behave more like an integrated human body than isolated tissues [77]. Advances in microfluidics, sensor integration, and vascularization push OoC technology toward full human-on-chip systems [78]. In 2017, Bauer et al. showed functional coupling of human pancreatic islets and liver spheroids in a dual OoC, capturing insulin–glucose cross talk over 15 days in culture [79]. Such multi-organ OoCs more faithfully recapitulate metabolism, toxicity, and inter-organ signaling than single-organ models [80]. Researchers still face challenges in media compatibility, scaling, and long-term viability as they strive to build predictive human organ systems on chips [81]. These systems mark a step toward fully integrated human-on-chip platforms that model whole-body physiology and guide personalized therapeutic testing (Fig. 3).

Engineering the OoC

The OoC system design begins with material selection and fabrication methods and culminates in quality management, including sterility and scalability considerations. The ability of an OoC to replicate organ-specific physiology depends not only on the biological components seeded into the device but also on the physical and chemical properties of the materials that shape the microenvironment [83]. Over the past decade, significant progress has been made in evaluating biomaterial options, refining microfabrication methods, and addressing challenges of reproducibility and scalability [84].

Fabrication techniques

OoC systems can be fabricated using various microfabrication approaches. The first approach is photolithography and etching techniques, borrowed from semiconductor manufacturing, which provide precise control over microchannel geometry but can be costly and technically demanding [85,86]. Another technique is injection molding and hot embossing [87,88]. These methods offer scalability and consistency, making them attractive for industrial-scale OoC production [89]. Laser cutting provides rapid prototyping with moderate resolution and is often used for early-stage design testing [90,91]. Recently, 3D printing has emerged as a fabrication method for constructing complex geometries and integrated multi-material devices [92,93]. The use of 3D printing in OoC technologies has allowed researchers to leverage high-precision, layer-by-layer fabrication to construct complex microfluidic architectures and optimize cellular microenvironments [94]. It holds promise for high-throughput, customizable OoC systems but still faces challenges in resolution, reproducibility, and material biocompatibility [93,95] (Fig. 4).

Materials overview

OoC platforms rely on materials that are biocompatible, optically transparent, and mechanically suitable for reproducing tissue microenvironments [59,84]. These materials fall broadly into two categories: natural biomaterials and synthetic polymers [97]. Natural biomaterials such as collagen, gelatin, fibrin, hyaluronic acid, alginate, and chitosan are widely used due to their similarity to components of the ECM [84]. They provide excellent biocompatibility and support for cell adhesion and differentiation. Furthermore, the integration of hydrogel microspheres (HMs) into these platforms has advanced the ability to mimic the ECM, enabling the construction of complex 3D tissue networks and the study of inter-organ crosstalk [98]. However, their limitations include batch-to-batch variability, relatively weak mechanical strength, and challenges in long-term stability [84]. Synthetic polymers such as PDMS offer tunable mechanical properties and flexibility in fabrication [84]. PDMS has been widely adopted due to its transparency, gas permeability, and ease of molding via soft lithography [59]. However, it can absorb small hydrophobic molecules, which can distort drug testing results [99]. PDMS absorption of hydrophobic small molecules can reduce effective drug exposure in tumor-on-chip assays, which is particularly relevant for PNST screening of agents such as anti-angiogenic antibodies and small-molecule pathway inhibitors used in NF-related tumors. This limitation has spurred efforts to identify and validate alternative polymers with reduced absorption [100]. In practice, many OoC platforms combine natural and synthetic materials to create biological fidelity with structural integrity. Having established the materials and manufacturing methods that make OoC systems possible, we next consider how these devices are brought to life through perfusion and functional monitoring. In the context of PNST, collagen- and laminin-rich gels are necessary to support neurite extension and nerve regeneration signals [101]. Collagen and laminin alignment improves axon guidance in nerve repair and collagen scaffolds guide neurite outgrowth and Schwann cell alignment [102,103]. However, collagen hydrogel structure loses integrity during long-term cultures and should be combined with synthetic polymers and thermoplastics to maintain robustness [104,105]. Synthetic polymer fibers combined with collagen have been shown to promote Schwann cell alignment and provide structural stability [106]. PNST can also be used in a Cyclic Olefin Copolymer (COC) based platform to improve long-term robustness. COC-based hybrid microfluidic platforms integrate thermoplastics which allow for manufacturability and strong OoC integrity [107]. PDMS sorbs small lipophilic molecules, which reduce free drug concentration and can shift dose response curves in perfused OoCs [99,108, 109]. This issue is relevant for PNST drug screening with MEK inhibitors such as selumetinib and mirdametinib, where small errors in exposure can mask partial responses or resistance. Validated low-sorption alternatives for drug screening include COC, polystyrene, polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA), and glass-based builds, which show lower adsorption than PDMS in comparative studies [100,110].

Pump, flow, and sensors

Once the cells are seeded, microfluidic pumps continuously perfuse the OoCs with nutrients and biochemical factors, thereby maintaining the function of the reconstructed tissue and cell-cell interactions [51, 111]. Perfusion also provides physiologically relevant flow and shear stress stimuli to cultured cells [51]. Embedded sensors enable real-time monitoring of cellular responses and microenvironmental conditions [111–113]. Below, we review the types of pumps used in OoC platforms as well as the various sensors and analytical tools integrated into different OoC designs.

Microfluidic pump systems

Maintaining controlled perfusion is critical for supplying nutrients, removing waste, and recreating physiological fluid forces in OoCs. A variety of pump systems exist, ranging from passive gravity-driven flows to active precision pumps, each suitable for different flow rates and experimental needs [114]. Gravity-driven flow is the simplest approach and uses hydrostatic pressure differences [115]. For example, a raised inlet reservoir can drive the fluid to flow into a lower outlet reservoir. At the same time, the channel length is adjustable to regulate the resistance and the flow rate [114,115]. This passive method is power-free and ideal for low flow rates. It is mainly used for short-term or low-shear experiments. A rocking platform can be incorporated to periodically tilt the OoC, thereby sustaining a continuous back-and-forth flow [116]. These systems maintain indefinite perfusion with minimal equipment [115]. Another method used is syringe pumps, which can generate relatively steady and non-pulsatile flow [117]. They can deliver stable flow rates that are adjustable from nanoliters to milliliters per minute [47]. However, syringe pumps are usually unidirectional and do not recirculate fluid. Thus, large media volumes and media replacement may be required for long-term experiments [43]. Peristaltic micropumps are another commonly used pump type [118,119]. These pumps allow circulating and programmable flow without large external equipment [119]. Peristaltic methods, however, exhibit flow pulsatility and low flow rates, which can lead to bubble formation [114,118]. Another common pump method is pressure-driven pumps. These systems use compressed gas pressure controllers to push fluid through microchannels [114]. Pressure-driven flow is smooth and can support recirculation [120]. Pressure-based pumps offer precise control and can cover a broad flow range (0.1 μL/min up to hundreds of uL/min) while minimizing mechanical vibration [114,120] (Table 1).

Integrated sensors and analytical readouts

A major advantage of OoC platforms is the ability to monitor tissue responses in real time via integrated microsensors. TEER sensors are widely used to measure barrier tissue integrity and can assess the electrical resistance across a cell layer [121]. Real-time TEER allows researchers to record events such as damage to an epithelial/endothelial barrier induced by toxins and its recovery upon treatment [68]. TEER is non-destructive, so it can be used to monitor how tissues mature and respond to stimuli over weeks [68]. TEER integration is common across many OoC platforms as a built-in metric for barrier tissues [68]. Electrical Activity Sensors, such as Multi-Electrode Arrays (MEAs), monitor the electrophysiological function of electrically excitable cells, such as neurons in a brain-on-chip or cardiomyocytes in a heart-on-chip [122]. MEAs are grids of microscale electrodes that can detect field potentials or action potentials from cells [123]. This can be used to measure the responses of electrically excitable cells in various conditions [124]. In one example, platinum microelectrodes were embedded into a heart-on-chip device in conjunction with TEER electrodes to have simultaneous readouts of barrier integrity and spontaneous electrical signals from cardiac tissue [68,123,124].

Another category of OoC sensors is microenvironment sensors. Cells in OoCs reside in a tightly controlled microenvironment, and measuring local conditions such as oxygen and pH is key for understanding tissue functions [46,125,126]. Many OoC devices embed optical or electrochemical sensors for O2 and pH levels [125,126]. Izadifar et al. demonstrated a two-channel Gut-Liver-on-chip with integrated oxygen and pH sensors along with TEER electrodes [46]. The integrated sensors enabled continuous, real-time measurement of oxygen levels and pH of the culture medium. Also, the OoC was constructed with gas-impermeable materials in certain regions to establish an oxygen gradient between two channels that mimic physiological conditions [46]. Sensors that measure biochemical outputs can also be embedded into OoCs. One approach is integrating electrochemical biosensors into the microfluidic circuit [127,128]. For example, microfabricated electrodes coated with enzymes or receptors that can detect target metabolites by generating an electrical signal when the target is present [112]. Another innovation is the use of Field-Effect Transistors (FETs) as sensitive biochemical sensors. Aydogmus et al. presented a silicon-polymer hybrid OoC with a floating-gate FET sensor to detect changes in local ion concentration [122]. Mechanical sensors can be incorporated into certain OoC platforms to measure mechanical forces or stresses generated by cells. For example, flexible micropillar arrays can be built into an OoC’s cell culture surface, and as cells tug on these small cantilevers, their deflection can be tracked to quantify traction forces [113]. These sensors can be used to measure contractile forces in cardiac muscle and other types of tissue contraction [129]. It is also worth noting that live microscopy is a primary analytical tool for OoCs [46,130]. Most OoCs are made of optically clear materials and designed to fit on microscope stages, enabling time-lapse imaging of cell morphology. While low-throughput microscopy is currently typical for the exploratory phase of OoC development and mechanistic studies, its reliance on manual imaging restricts assay scale and standardization [131]. Conversely, high-throughput imaging is essential for integrating these models into early-stage drug discovery, enabling large-scale target discovery and primary screening [131]. Optical analyses are often done in parallel with integrated sensors [132]. For example, in the multi-sensor Gut-Liver-on-chip, researchers monitored cellular morphology and used an ATP-based luminescence assay to gauge metabolic activity [46]. The combination of sensors with high-resolution imaging provides a more comprehensive picture of the tissue’s physiological conditions.

OoC technology offers a fundamentally new paradigm in biomedical research. By merging the precision of microengineering with the complexity of human biology, these devices create physiologically relevant experimental systems and set the stage for their eventual application to rare and understudied diseases, such as neurofibromatosis, where traditional models have significant therapeutic gaps [71].

Future directions for OoC modeling of PNSTs

The biological and therapeutic challenges presented by PNSTs highlight a fundamental gap between traditional disease models and the complex pathophysiology of these tumors. Many studies use two-dimensional cultures of Schwann cells derived from patients with PNSTs [133]. In one study, immortalized NF1-mutant Schwann cell lines were used to study altered proliferation rates and RAS signaling of Schwann cells [134]. However, conventional two-dimensional cultures fail to capture the multicellular interactions and dynamic signaling that drive tumor initiation and progression [135]. Consequently, preclinical testing has struggled to predict clinical outcomes, which has limited the development of effective therapies [5]. To bridge this gap, emerging microengineered OoC platforms offer a powerful alternative. 2D cultures lack structural cues such as an organized ECM, cell polarity, and biochemical gradients [135,136]. Prior OoC work highlights why these features are essential. In lung models, cyclic mechanical stretch is critical in regulating inflammatory and toxic responses, which can be overlooked when this tissue-level mechanics is missing [41,60]. In gut models, perfusion and deformation drive villus-like architecture and crypt villus differentiation gradients, which do not form under static 2D conditions [54,62,63]. In BBB models, shear and multicellular coculture support tight junction formation and improve barrier function, which increases physiological fidelity relative to conventional 2D assays [65, 121]. These same architectural needs apply to PNST studies where gradients, mechanics, and multicellular interfaces determine drug penetration and regulate stromal immune signaling. By enabling precise control of the tumor microenvironment, OoC models can more accurately mimic the biology of PNSTs and accelerate the discovery of patient-specific treatments.

Regulatory support for OoC technologies is increasing. The U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) now accepts OoCs and other microphysiological systems (MPS) as “new approach methodologies” (NAMs) that may reduce or replace animal testing for safety and efficacy assessments [137]. The NIH’s recent shift toward prioritizing human-based research technologies, marked by the establishment of the Office of Research Innovation, Validation, and Application (ORIVA), underscores a major federal commitment to moving away from animal models to improve clinical translatability [138]. This initiative provides a compelling argument for adopting OoC solutions, which can utilize human stem cells and pathological specimens to replicate patient-specific biological responses that traditional animal studies often fail to predict accurately. In September 2024, the FDA accepted a preliminary submission under its pilot program Innovative Science and Technology Approaches for New Drugs (ISTAND) Pilot Program for a human “liver-chip” tool designed to predict drug-induced liver injury, signaling that regulators are willing to consider OoC-derived data in preclinical evaluation [139]. With regulatory interest growing in OoCs as alternatives to animal testing [58], tumor-on-chip systems may become central tools for oncology drug development. Tumor-on-chip platforms offer a unique opportunity to model PNSTs by incorporating human neurons, Schwann cells, fibroblasts, and immune components, thereby enabling systematic study of nerve-tumor–microenvironment interactions and preclinical evaluation of targeted therapies.

Given the cellular and genetic heterogeneity inherent to PNSTs, where tumor development involves multiple cell and tissue types, conventional in vitro systems cannot capture the complex paracrine and biochemical signaling underlying these interactions. OoCs address these limitations by enabling a compartmentalized co-culture of multiple cell types. For example, tumor-on-chip and nerve-on-chip systems can replicate vascular drug delivery and cytokine gradients that modulate merlin or neurofibromin-deficient Schwann-cell proliferation [20,21]. Such OoC platforms can be tuned to recapitulate microdeletion subtypes or mosaic loss-of-function mutations observed in NF1 and NF2, offering a scalable platform for investigating genotype-to-phenotype correlations.

To translate PNST-on-Chip platforms toward clinical utility, it is key to develop a patient-derived PNST-on-Chip system that can be sustained for at least six weeks with longitudinal readouts of tumor growth, vascular permeability, and cytokine secretion. In parallel, a vascularized tumor–nerve-on-chip should be benchmarked against matched patient outcomes with a predefined target of at least 75% concordance between on-chip drug responses and clinical response to MEK or anti-angiogenic therapies. Meeting these measurable objectives would establish technical readiness for prospective testing of personalized treatment strategies and provide quantitative criteria for regulatory and translational evaluation.

Integration of multi-organ, immune, and neurovascular systems

One of the promising directions for OoC applications in neurofibromatosis is the integration of multi-organ, immune, and neurovascular systems. Because neurofibromatosis affects several tissue types (e.g., peripheral nerves, vasculature, skin, and the central nervous system), interconnected OoCs could simulate systemic drug distribution. Coupling a nerve-on-chip and a vascularized tumor-on-chip platform would allow modeling of perineural invasion, ischemia, and immune cell trafficking under continuous perfusion. This coupled system would provide a physiologically relevant environment for testing therapies that target both local and systemic disease. Current studies indicate that the immune microenvironment, particularly macrophages and T-cell infiltration, plays a critical role in neurofibroma growth and malignant transformation [1,10,35]. Future OoC systems could incorporate patient-derived immune cells to model tumor-immune crosstalk and cytokine signaling. These systems could help explain why some NF tumors are resistant to targeted therapies despite molecular inhibition of RAS/MAPK or PI3K/mTOR signaling. Understanding this phenomenon could point toward personalized therapies.

Multi-organ and immune-integrated PNST-on-chip models face practical limitations in sourcing matched autologous cells from a single patient [140]. Surgical resections yield tumor cells and limited adjacent nerve tissue, whereas procuring sufficient numbers of healthy autologous Schwann cells, fibroblasts, endothelial cells, and circulating immune cells at a sufficient scale often proves unfeasible within clinical timelines [141–143]. Peripheral blood provides immune cells, yet ex vivo expansion alters their phenotype and effector function, potentially distorting tumor-immune readouts [141,142,144,145]. Tumor specimens also show variable viability and stromal content, with dissociation bias against fragile cell types and loss of native cell ratios [146]. These constraints complicate the construction of fully personalized platforms.

Predictive OoC models for therapeutic testing

OoC platforms provide a bridge between molecular profiling and therapeutic screening. When integrated with transcriptomics or single-cell sequencing, these systems can generate multi-dimensional datasets that describe how patient-derived neurofibromas or schwannomas respond to MEK, mTOR, or anti-angiogenic inhibitors. The resulting drug-response profiles can reveal adaptive resistance mechanisms or tumor-stroma crosstalk. Vascularized tumor-on-chip systems allow for the controlled delivery of pharmacologic agents through endothelial channels, enabling quantitative assessment of permeability, distribution, and cytotoxicity, important for understanding the efficacy of these agents [55].

As these models evolve, the convergence of OoC data with computational analytics may enable predictive frameworks for personalized treatment in neurofibromatosis. Paracrine signaling between immune, stromal, and tumor compartments is a key driver of PNST progression and can be modeled in a Tumor-Nerve-Immune-on-Chip platform. In NF1-associated neurofibromas, infiltrating mast cells release inflammatory cytokines, such as TGF-β, that promote ECM deposition, including large amounts of collagen, which, in turn, stiffens the nerve microenvironment and supports Schwann cell proliferation and survival [8,134,147,148]. A compartmentalized microfluidic design with nerve-tumor, stromal, and perfused vascular-immune channels would enable controlled deliveries of immune cells and cytokines and the formation of biochemical gradients. Such a design provides a framework for investigating how immune-derived paracrine signals propagate through fibroblasts to alter Schwann cell behavior and therapeutic response to PNST.

PNST-on-chip components

PNST-on-chip platforms require design features that differ from those of generic tumor or OoC systems, as PNST biology depends on nerve-specific structure and function. Aligned nanofiber scaffolds are needed to reproduce axonal guidance and Schwann cell alignment, which shape perineural invasion and tumor cell migration along nerve tracts. Mechanical cues must be tuned to peripheral nerve physiology, with low shear stress and compliant matrices that preserve neurite extension and Schwann cell differentiation rather than the higher shear environments used in vascular or epithelial OoCs. Functional readouts should also extend beyond morphology and viability to include neural activity, with integrated MEAs enabling longitudinal monitoring of electrophysiological changes and neurotoxicity during drug exposure. Together, these PNST-specific requirements define a distinct design space for PNST-on-chip systems and underscore why direct translation of generic tumor-on-chip architectures is insufficient for modeling nerve–tumor interactions (Table 3).

Clinical translation

For OoC models to reach their full translational potential in neurofibromatosis, challenges such as scalability, reproducibility, and regulatory validation must be addressed. Establishing standardized OoCs will be crucial for laboratory consistency and eventual FDA qualification. Moreover, collaborations between academic centers, patient registries, and biotechnology companies could facilitate the creation of biobanks of patient-derived neurofibroma and schwannoma organoids optimized for OoC seeding. The long-term vision is to develop a patient-on-chip platform that can recapitulate each individual’s unique genetic, immune, and physiological landscape of disease to guide precision therapy.

Limitations

Chronic disease timescales

PNST-on-chip platforms face limitations associated with slow tumor natural history and recapitulation of peripheral nerve biology. Plexiform neurofibromas often persist for years and show heterogeneous volumetric growth across patients and lesions [154]. Longitudinal MRI studies report modest median annual growth in many plexiform neurofibromas, with a subset showing faster growth linked to new clinical morbidities [155]. NF2-associated vestibular schwannomas often grow slowly over multi-year imaging follow-up, with reported average growth near 1 mm per year in some cohorts [156]. Most OoC cancer studies operate on week-scale experimental windows, which limit the study of tumor evolution, cumulative microenvironment remodeling, therapy adaptation, and late transformation events [58]. Peripheral nerve microphysiological systems extend culture duration into the multi-week range, e.g., 40-day neuron–Schwann cell co-culture with myelination dynamics, yet this duration still mismatches PNST clinical timelines [149].

Nerve architecture and functional innervation

PNSTs form within peripheral nerve sheaths, so model fidelity depends on recreating endoneurial, perineurial, and vascular barrier compartments surrounding axons [157]. Perineurial tight junctions and endoneurial microvascular endothelium regulate solute and leukocyte flux, defining the intrafascicular microenvironment required for axonal function [158]. Blood-nerve barrier studies report limited molecular characterization and slow progress over decades, which complicates the construction of validated barrier modules within PNST-on-chip [158].

PNST modeling also faces challenges posed by bidirectional nerve-cancer signaling and tumor-associated neurite outgrowth, which require functional neurons and neuroactive mediators in vitro [152].

Tumor microenvironment and immune crosstalk

Plexiform neurofibromas contain Schwann cells, fibroblasts, perineurial cells, immune cells, ECM, and blood vessels in a complex microenvironment [24]. Human immunoprofiling links malignant progression in NF1-associated PNST with increased immune infiltration and predominance of myeloid populations, including tumor-associated macrophages implicated in immune evasion [1]. Neurofibroma model studies identify macrophages as contributors to tumor growth and to response variation under targeted pharmacologic inhibition [159]. Immune-competent tumor-on-chip experiments show that environmental stress and chronic exposure drive immune dysfunction, such as NK cell exhaustion, which complicates sustained immune-tumor co-culture [45]. Quality guidance for microphysiological systems highlights reproducibility risks from cell sourcing, operating conditions, and readout pipelines, which become more acute as PNST-on-chip systems add immune and stromal complexity [160].

Genetic heterogeneity and malignant transformation

MPNST pathogenesis involves stepwise genetic change, including NF1 alterations and early CDKN2A loss, with recurrent PRC2 inactivation through EED or SUZ12 loss in many tumors [161]. OoC models seeded with cells from a limited panel of widely used MPNST cell lines risk underrepresenting interpatient heterogeneity and tumor evolution trajectories [162]. Recent efforts have expanded PNST resources by establishing new patient-derived MPNST lines and orthotopic xenografts. Yet, overall model availability remains limited compared with that of common cancers [163]. Patient-derived neurofibroma organoids preserve aspects of cellular and molecular heterogeneity. However, organoid systems still omit key nerve barrier and innervation compartments without additional engineering [164]. Modeling malignant transformation from plexiform neurofibroma to MPNST requires long-term longitudinal sampling and control of the microenvironment, which current in vitro systems rarely provide [165].

Readouts and clinical benchmarking

Clinical PNST studies track outcomes such as tumor volume, neurologic morbidity, hearing loss, and pain, often using serial imaging and functional measures [4]. Most PNST-on-chip assays emphasize proliferation, viability, and pathway markers, so alignment with clinical endpoints, such as volumetric MRI response or symptom change, remains limited [166]. Electrophysiology and sensory neuron assays offer routes to functional readouts relevant to PNST-associated neuropathic pain, yet few PNST platforms integrate standardized stimulation and recording protocols aligned with pain-linked phenotypes [167]. Fit-for-purpose validation frameworks for microphysiological systems call for a defined context of use, performance metrics, and inter-lab reproducibility, which PNST-on-chip efforts will need for translational credibility [160].

Conclusion

OoC technology represents a major advancement in how diseases are modeled and how therapies are developed. It merges microengineering with living human cells to recreate physiological conditions. Applied to PNSTs, these systems can mimic the nerve-tumor-immune microenvironment that drives neurofibromatosis and related disorders. Through controlled perfusion, biomechanical cues, and real-time monitoring, they can reveal how Schwann cells, fibroblasts, immune cells, and vasculature interact to sustain tumor growth or respond to treatment. Beyond neurofibromatosis, the implications are broader. The same design principles that enable nerve-tumor modeling can be applied to cancers, degenerative disorders, and systemic diseases where understanding multicellular signaling is critical. Tumor-on-chip systems already provide patient-specific drug testing, and multi-organ-on-chips are advancing toward human-on-chip frameworks that capture metabolism, toxicity, and immune function in an interconnected model. As fabrication methods mature and sensors improve, these devices will transition from research prototypes to standardized platforms for preclinical testing and clinical translation. OoC systems signal the next generation of tissue culture. They bridge the gap between static cell assays and animal models, delivering human-relevant data that can accelerate discovery and reduce clinical trial failure rates. For PNSTs and beyond, they offer not only a new experimental tool but a new paradigm for understanding and treating complex human disease.
