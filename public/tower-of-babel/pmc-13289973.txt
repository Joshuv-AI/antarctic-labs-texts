Endoscopic therapy may be more effective in long-term control of upper gastrointestinal angioectasias when compared with somatostatin analogues
Authors: Muhammad S. Shabbir, Mary Ayad, Christian S. Jackson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and study aimsBleeding secondary to upper gastrointestinal angioectasias requires medical intervention, hospitalization, and transfusions. Endoscopic and medical therapies have been used to treat upper gastrointestinal angioectasias. Therapeutic efficacy of treatment modalities used in management of upper gastrointestinal angioectasias, specifically short- and long-term outcomes, remains an area of interest.Patients and methodsThis was a retrospective cohort study utilizing TriNetX. ICD codes were used to identify patients with angioectasias of the upper gastrointestinal tract with concomitant iron deficiency anemia, while excluding patients with other vascular lesions of the upper gastrointestinal tract. Patients were subsequently divided into two groups, endoscopic and medical therapy group.Results At 6 months, patients receiving endoscopic therapy demonstrated lower rates of hospitalization (70% vs 88%, P < 0.001), need for esophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD) (30% vs 48%, P <0.001), and all-cause mortality (12% vs 22%, P = 0.002). At 3 years, endoscopic therapy was still superior in terms of EGD burden (42% vs 56%, P = 0.001), hospitalization (83% vs 91%, P = 0.008), all-cause mortality (26% vs 38%, P = 0.003), and blood transfusion (35% vs 45%, P = 0.013). At the 5-year interval, endoscopic therapy continued to demonstrate a reduced need for EGDs, hospitalizations, blood transfusions, and all-cause mortality. Mean hemoglobin was higher in the endoscopic cohort compared to the medical therapy at all intervals. ConclusionsEndoscopic therapy alone may provide better short and long-term clinical outcomes for patients with upper gastrointestinal angioectasias as compared with medical therapy with somatostatin analogues alone. Multicenter randomized clinical trials will need to be performed to further validate these findings.

Introduction

Gastrointestinal angioectasias are the most common vascular lesions found during endoscopic evaluation, especially in older adults 1 . They are a leading cause of gastrointestinal bleeding, particularly from the small bowel, and are associated with high recurrence rates and significant healthcare costs 2 3 . Angioectasias are most frequently found in individuals older than age 60 years, with prevalence increasing as the population ages. They account for about 5% to 10% of all gastrointestinal bleeding 1 4 .

Upper gastrointestinal angioectasias (angioectasias diagnosed in the stomach and duodenum) can cause both overt bleeding (e.g., melena, hematemesis) and occult bleeding leading to acute blood loss anemias as well as iron deficiency anemias 5 . Managing such cases requires not only requires identification of the bleeding source but also long-term strategies to reduce transfusion dependence, improve hemoglobin (Hb) levels, and minimize hospitalizations.

Endoscopic measures, surgical options, and pharmacotherapy are all possible treatment options for gastrointestinal angioectasias 6 7 . Endoscopic management of upper gastrointestinal angioectasias primarily involves ablative technologies, which include but are not limited to argon plasma coagulation (APC) 8 , endoscopic band ligation 9 , clipping, polidocanol injection 10 11 , and sclerotherapy 12 . Pharmacologic therapy, particularly somatostatin analogues, has been proven to be a safe and effective treatment option for gastrointestinal angioectasias, especially those diagnosed in the small bowel 13 . Other pharmacologic treatment options that have been investigated include thalidomide, hormonal therapy, and angiogenesis inhibitors like bevacizumab 7 .

Although both endoscopic and pharmacologic interventions have been studied in small bowel bleeding, limited data are available about how these treatment modalities impact Hb improvement, transfusion requirements, need for repeat esophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGDs), and all-cause mortality over extended follow-up periods in patients diagnosed with angioectasias in the upper gastrointestinal tract.

We conducted a retrospective cohort study utilizing a large, real-world clinical database to compare outcomes in patients with gastric or duodenal angioectasias who were treated with either endoscopic therapy or somatostatin analogues. We assessed both short- and long-term outcomes, including changes in Hb levels, transfusion requirements, utilization of additional diagnostic procedures, hospitalization rates, and all-cause mortality over a follow-up period of up to 10 years. The primary aim of the study was to determine improvement in Hb levels at 6 months and 3 and 5 years following initiation of therapy. Secondary outcomes included need for blood transfusion, need for intravenous (IV) iron, need for EGDs, rates of hospitalization, and all-cause mortality at the same time intervals.

Patients and methods

TriNetX, a global health research network, provided access to electronic medical records. This study was performed on December 28, 2025, on the Global Collaborative Network. All data collection, processing, and transmission was done in compliance with all Data Protection laws applicable to the contributing health care organizations, including the EU Data Protection Law Regulation 2016/679, the General Data Protection Regulation on the protection of natural persons with regard to the processing of personal data and the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act, the US federal law that protects the privacy and security of healthcare data. TriNetX is ISO 27001:2013 certified and maintains a robust information technology security program that protects both personal data and health care data. This was a retrospective cohort study. ICD codes were used to identify individuals with angioectasias of the stomach and duodenum. Patients were required to have a diagnostic endoscopy or capsule endoscopy within 3 months before or after diagnosis of angioectasias ( Supplementary Table 1 ). Patients were also required to have a concomitant diagnosis of iron deficiency or iron deficiency anemia with Hb < 14 g/dL. Patients with a diagnosis of duodenal ulcer, gastric ulcer, Dieulafoy's lesion of the stomach and small intestine, malignancy of the stomach and small intestine, and angioectasias of the colon were excluded from the cohort. Patients were also required to have received at least one blood transfusion or IV iron before diagnosis of angioectasias. Patients who received bevacizumab and thalidomide were also excluded from the study. In addition, patients with a diagnosis of acute pancreatitis within 6 months before or after initiation of somatostatin analogues, patients who underwent ERCP during the same time interval, and patients with a diagnosis of acromegaly and gigantism were excluded from the medical therapy group. To ensure that somatostatin analogues were used specifically for angioectasias, patients with a diagnosis of variceal bleeding (primary and secondary) were excluded from the study. The cohort meeting the above criteria was further divided into two groups: patients who received endoscopic therapy only and patients who received medical therapy only. Patients in the endoscopic therapy group were required to have received therapeutic endoscopy within 6 months, while excluding somatostatin analogues, whereas patients in the medical therapy group were required to have received somatostatin analogues within 6 months, while excluding therapeutic endoscopy. A flowchart diagram showing the methodology is shown in Fig. 1 . This study was exempt from informed consent. The data reviewed are a secondary analysis of existing data, do not involve intervention or interaction with human subjects, and were de-identified per the de-identification standard defined in Section §164.514(a) of the HIPAA Privacy Rule. The process by which the data were de-identified is attested to through a formal determination by a qualified expert as defined in Section §164.514(b)(1) of the HIPAA Privacy Rule .

Flow diagram.

All statistical analyses were performed using the TriNetX software through its browser-based real-time analytics platform, TriNetX Live (TriNetX LLC). Baseline characteristics of both cohorts were defined using mean and standard deviation. One-to-one (1:1) propensity score matching (PSM) was performed for age, gender, race, ethnicity, comorbidities (variables of comorbidity index), antiplatelets, anticoagulants, and lab values for coagulation (prothrombin time, activated partial thromboplastin time, international normalized ratio. These variables were selected because they have been found to impact outcomes in patients with angioectasias. One-to-one PSM was performed using the TriNetX platform, which applies logistic regression to estimate propensity scores based on user-defined covariates. Matching was conducted using a greedy nearest-neighbor algorithm with a caliper width of 0.1 pooled standard deviations. To minimize selection bias, the platform randomizes the order of rows prior to matching. Post-matching P values assess the balance of covariates between cohorts. Continuous variables were compared using independent sample T-test and categorical variables were compared using the Pearson chi-square test.

Following matching, outcomes were analyzed using adjusted odds ratios (aORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) ( Supplementary Table 2 ). Two-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The platform uses the R survival package v3.2–3 to calculate hazard ratio (HR). Outcomes were assessed assuming the proportionality hazard assumption holding true.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first study to compare endoscopic vs. medical therapy for treatment of upper gastrointestinal angioectasias over multiple years. Our study found that over a 5-year period, endoscopic therapy is most likely superior regarding improved clinical outcomes, which include but are not limited to reduced rates of hospitalization, repeat endoscopic procedures, blood transfusions, and all-cause mortality when compared with medical therapy alone. Patients receiving endoscopic therapy consistently demonstrated higher levels of Hb at different time intervals over a 5-year period.

Use of endoscopic therapy for treatment was described in a case series by Gunnlaugsson 14 , who noted that the upper gastrointestinal angioectasias tract could be treated successfully with electrocoagulation. One of the first long-term outcome studies on angioectasias was performed by Clouse et al. 15 . The rebleeding rate was 50%, although most of these lesions were either not recognized on initial endoscopy or inaccessible during endoscopy. Interestingly, the mortality percentage observed in this case series (31%) was similar to our study (37%). Kwan et al. evaluated patients diagnosed with vascular lesions of the upper gastrointestinal tract 16 . Gastrointestinal angioectasias and gastric antral vascular malformations were included. For transfusion- and non-transfusion-dependent angioectasias, endoscopic therapy showed a statistically significant increase in Hb levels over a median follow-up interval of 16 months with use of argon APC. Unlike other ulcerative lesions in the gastrointestinal system, such as peptic ulcer bleeding, which has a 30-day recurrence rate of 10% to16% (and can be reduced to below 1% with eradication of H. pylori and nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory use), upper gastrointestinal angioectasias have been reported to have a higher recurrence rate of approximately 30% after hospital discharge at mean follow-up of 33 months 17 18 . Rebleeding and need for transfusion are well-described clinical outcomes of gastrointestinal angioectasias. Even though these are significant clinical outcomes, a higher percentage of patients diagnosed with small bowel angioectasias present with rebleeding and need for transfusion when compared with angioectasias of the upper gastrointestinal tract or colon 19 . Because small bowel angioectasias have a higher percentage of rebleeding and transfusion, this has led to use of medical therapy. There is emerging evidence to suggest that medical therapy may be used alone or as adjuvant therapy in conjunction with endoscopy 20 . Medical therapy alone or used as adjuvant therapy with endoscopy may not be needed in treatment of angioectasias of the upper gastrointestinal tract once blood and iron transfusions have been given. Endoscopic therapy may be successful secondary to the ability to direct ablative therapy for a longer time due to thickness of the stomach and duodenum and lack of persistent peristalsis, which the endoscopist can encounter in the small bowel. However, in cases of rebleeding, it has been recommended that patients be referred to percutaneous embolization or surgery 21 . Based on our findings, a second-look endoscopy for rebleeding may be beneficial in management of these patients.

Even though upper gastrointestinal angioectasias have not been extensively studied, prevalence of upper gastrointestinal angioectasias may be higher than once thought. For example, angioectasias of the upper gastrointestinal tract comprise approximately 4% to 7% of all cases of non-variceal gastrointestinal bleeding 22 . Wuerth et al. observed a 32% increase in presence of upper gastrointestinal bleeding secondary to angioectasias of the upper gastrointestinal tract 23 . Moreover, in a study that evaluated over 5000 individuals undergoing EGD, upper gastrointestinal angioectasias were diagnosed in 10% of cases 24 . Because these angioectasias are more prevalent and are associated with upper gastrointestinal bleeding, more attention needs to be placed on their recognition and treatment.

We feel that one of the significant strengths of our study is that the TriNetX database is derived from a large multicenter cohort. This is in comparison to previous studies, which were single-center, lacked population diversity, and controlled a limited number of variables with short-term follow-up. Our study has robust statistical methodology, including 1:1 PSM that minimizes risk of selection bias. A comprehensive set of confounders was matched for this purpose, which includes demographics, comorbidities, medications, and laboratory variables associated with bleeding risk.

Our study has several limitations. First, we were unable to ascertain cause of death. Second, given lack of actual endoscopic data, endoscopic characteristics of the angioectasias and the number of angioectasias could not be determined. For example, in peptic ulcer bleeding, there have been well-studied means to recognize stigmata of recent hemorrhage as well as means to treat peptic ulcer bleeding. To date, this does not exist for management of bleeding of upper gastrointestinal angioectasias. Another key limitation is inability to perform stratified analysis with octreotide and lanreotide due to the small number of patients in the cohorts. Certain limitations stem from the nature of electronic health records, including differences in coding practices, events occurring outside the participating health care organizations not being captured, and an actual reduction in number of transfusions between the two groups. The primary/secondary outcome structure reflects clinical prioritization, and all findings should be interpreted as associative and exploratory, without claims of definitive causal inference. Finally, we were unable to calculate rebleeding rates between the groups because there was no specific ICD-10 code for rebleeding.

Conclusions

Endoscopic therapy appears to provide better outcomes compared with medical therapy alone in treatment of upper gastrointestinal angioectasias, and its benefits extend to an increase in survival rate and long-term morbidity and mortality. Considering these results, early implementation of endoscopic intervention as a first-line therapeutic approach in appropriate patients appears to be a reasonable option. Multicenter, prospective, randomized studies on this topic are required to confirm the findings from our study and to explore other advanced strategies that can be used in this regard.
