Prehospital thrombolysis with tenecteplase in out-of-hospital cardiac arrest: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Alan Cowley, Dan Cody, Eleanor Jaquet, Shani Corb, Magnus Nelson
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe role of thrombolysis in out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA) remains uncertain, with limited evidence of benefit. This study evaluated whether prehospital administration of Tenecteplase improves outcomes in patients with presumed thromboembolic OHCA.MethodsA retrospective cohort study was conducted using a regional cardiac arrest registry comprising 2,829 adult OHCA cases. Of these, 171 patients received Tenecteplase in the prehospital setting. Logistic regression was used to assess the association between thrombolysis and two outcomes: 30-day survival (primary) and return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC) at hospital arrival (secondary), adjusting for relevant confounders.ResultsThirty-day survival was 4% in the thrombolysis group versus 10% in controls. Adjusted odds of survival were significantly lower with thrombolysis (OR 0.08, 95% CI 0.04–0.19), as were odds of achieving ROSC (OR 0.21, 95% CI 0.14–0.33). Shockable rhythm (OR 15.89) and witnessed arrest (OR 1.99) were strong independent predictors of survival. Model fit was moderate (McFadden’s R² = 0.27).ConclusionPrehospital thrombolysis with Tenecteplase was not associated with improved survival or ROSC in OHCA and may be linked to worse outcomes. These findings highlight a lack of sustained benefit associated with empiric thrombolysis based on suspected thromboembolic cause in OHCA, and raise questions about its routine prehospital use without definitive diagnosis.Keywords: Thrombolysis, Out-of-hospital cardiac arrest, Tenecteplase, Return of spontaneous circulation, Survival outcomes, Ambulance, Paramedic

Background

Thrombolytic therapy is an established intervention for conditions such as ST-elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) and massive pulmonary embolism (PE), where early reperfusion improves outcomes [1, 2]. However, its utility in cardiac arrest, particularly when administered in the ‘out of hospital’ setting, remains controversial. While case reports and limited series suggest potential benefit [3–5], large-scale studies have not demonstrated consistent survival advantages, and some suggest possible harm [6].

In the United Kingdom, Tenecteplase is used by select prehospital teams due to a few pragmatic advantages, principally bolus administration and legal exemption from prescription requirements for registered paramedics under Schedule 17 of the Human Medicines Regulations 2012. Within South East Coast Ambulance Service NHS Foundation Trust (SECAmb), Tenecteplase is administered by Critical Care Paramedics (CCPs) when a thromboembolic cause of arrest is suspected, such as PE or STEMI, based on limited clinical indicators such as clinical history, rhythm type, pre-arrest ECG changes, or known risk factors.

This context introduces diagnostic uncertainty and the potential for misclassification, particularly given the absence of definitive tools (e.g. imaging, biomarkers) in the prehospital phase. Consequently, thrombolysis may be administered inappropriately, or too late to be effective. Furthermore, international guidelines (e.g. ILCOR, Resuscitation Council UK) recommend thrombolysis only in suspected PE, not undifferentiated or presumed STEMI-related arrests.

This study investigates the real-world outcomes of Tenecteplase administration in out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA) using a single-agent protocol across a large regional cohort. The aim is to assess whether thrombolysis improves survival or ROSC rates, or whether its use in this setting should be reconsidered. Given the scarcity of observational data on this topic, especially from paramedic-led systems, our findings may inform future protocols and patient selection strategies.

This study adds to existing literature by focusing on the real-world use of Tenecteplase in OHCA where a thromboembolic cause was suspected by experienced prehospital clinicians. Unlike the TROICA [5] trial, which studied unselected OHCA, or prior observational cohorts with mixed inclusion criteria, this dataset reflects current UK clinical practice within a regional critical care paramedic system. It highlights both the frequency of thrombolysis use and the challenges of accurate prehospital identification of PE or STEMI in cardiac arrest.

Methods

Patient population

Adult patients (≥ 18 years) who experienced OHCA and received advanced life support from SECAmb were included. The thrombolysis group comprised patients treated with Tenecteplase by a CCP for presumed thromboembolic causes of arrest. These cases were identified from the registry over a five-year period to ensure adequate sample size. The control group included non-thrombolysed OHCA cases collected over a single year (April 2022–March 2023), during which CCP coverage was consistent.

Exclusion criteria included traumatic cardiac arrest or known pregnancy. Patients were grouped based on whether thrombolysis was administered in the prehospital setting. Tenecteplase was typically given when CCPs suspected pulmonary embolism or STEMI as the underlying cause, based on available clinical information. In the control group, reasons for non-thrombolysis included absence of a CCP or a clinical decision not to administer it.

Although the dataset recorded whether a critical care paramedic (CCP) was present at the scene, it did not capture whether thrombolysis was considered and withheld, or simply not indicated. As such, we were unable to separate the non-thrombolysis group into those who had access to thrombolysis but did not receive it, and those for whom thrombolysis was unavailable.

To preserve anonymity, time-based trends and annual breakdowns of thrombolysis use were not reported.

Primary and secondary outcomes

The primary outcome was 30-day survival following out-of-hospital cardiac arrest. This endpoint was chosen to assess whether prehospital thrombolysis provides any long-term survival benefit in OHCA.

The secondary outcome was return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC) sustained to hospital arrival, as documented by ambulance clinicians. Transient ROSC episodes, defined as any brief return of circulation that did not persist to hospital arrival and was not associated with sustained perfusion or transfer of care to hospital teams, were not included. Data were sourced from prehospital care records; defibrillator logs were not systematically reviewed.

The study followed STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) guidelines for transparent reporting of observational data [7].

Statistical analysis

Patient population and sample size considerations

A total of 170 cases of thrombolysis were identified during the study period. Due to the low frequency of this intervention, a five-year data collection window was required to achieve a sufficient sample for analysis. All statistical analyses were performed in Stata version 17 (StataCorp, College Station, TX). A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

A preliminary power calculation suggested that detecting a statistically significant difference in 30-day survival would require an effect size of approximately 72%, assuming a baseline survival rate of 8.3%. For ROSC, a smaller effect size (~ 33%) would suffice, based on a typical 30% ROSC rate in OHCA [8].

No a priori sample size calculation was performed prior to data collection, as this was a retrospective observational study. However, based on an estimated survival rate of 10% in the control group and a clinically significant absolute difference of 5%, a minimum of approximately 1,150 patients per group would be required to detect such a difference (α = 0.05, β = 0.20). Our final sample size of 2,882 patients exceeds this threshold.

A post-hoc power analysis was additionally conducted to evaluate the risk of type II error in non-significant comparisons. However, we acknowledge that post-hoc analysis cannot replace pre-specified power planning and should be interpreted cautiously. The results are included for completeness and context rather than inferential strength.

Descriptive and comparative analysis

Baseline characteristics were summarised using standard descriptive statistics. Group comparisons were performed using Fisher’s exact or chi-square tests for categorical variables and t-tests or nonparametric equivalents for continuous variables, as appropriate. Data distributions were assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test prior to applying parametric tests.

Regression modelling

Logistic regression was used to assess the association between thrombolysis and both 30-day survival and ROSC. Multivariable models adjusted for known predictors of OHCA outcomes, including age, gender, witnessed status, initial rhythm, and bystander CPR. These were selected based on both clinical relevance and availability within the dataset. Arrest location was also available but excluded due to limited granularity and overlap with other variables. Other variables such as response time, transport interval, and time of arrest were not available. Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported. Model fit was assessed using McFadden’s pseudo-R² [9].

A time-restricted sensitivity analysis was also performed, comparing outcomes between thrombolysed and non-thrombolysed patients from the 2022–2023 calendar year only.

Power analysis

Given the small number of survivors in the thrombolysis group, a post-hoc power analysis was conducted to evaluate the likelihood of detecting significant effects based on observed outcomes.

Missing data

Analyses were conducted using a complete case approach. Cases with missing data for key variables were excluded from the regression models. Overall, missing data were minimal and not systematically associated with outcome or exposure.

Results

Patients and interventions

A total of 2,829 OHCA patients were included. Of these, 171 (6%) received prehospital thrombolysis with Tenecteplase administered by a Critical Care Paramedic (CCP). The remaining 2,658 patients received standard advanced life support (ALS) without thrombolysis (Fig. 1).

Descriptive characteristics

Thrombolysis was administered in line with SECAmb clinical guidelines when a thromboembolic cause (e.g. PE or STEMI) was suspected. The control group either lacked CCP attendance or the attending CCP did not administer thrombolysis based on clinical judgment.

Post-resuscitation care varied between hospitals, potentially influencing longer-term outcomes. However, all patients received Level 1 emergency care, and prehospital ALS delivery followed consistent protocols.

Primary and secondary outcomes

Primary outcome

Thirty-day survival was significantly lower in the thrombolysis group (4%) compared to the non-thrombolysis group (10%) (Table 2). The unadjusted odds ratio (OR) for survival with thrombolysis was 0.36 (95% CI: 0.16–0.81) (Table 3; Fig. 2), indicating a reduced likelihood of survival. After adjusting for confounders, the adjusted OR decreased further to 0.08 (95% CI: 0.04–0.19), suggesting a strong association between thrombolysis and reduced survival.

Despite these statistically significant findings, the logistic regression model demonstrated moderate fit (McFadden’s R² = 0.27), suggesting that unmeasured variables may also influence survival outcomes.

Secondary outcome

The secondary outcome, return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC) at hospital arrival, occurred in 39% of patients in the thrombolysis group compared to 27% in the non-thrombolysis group (Table 2). Although ROSC was more frequent in the thrombolysis group in unadjusted analysis, this association was no longer present after multivariable adjustment and was instead associated with a significantly lower likelihood of ROSC.

In multivariable logistic regression, thrombolysis was independently associated with a significantly lower likelihood of ROSC (adjusted OR 0.21; 95% CI: 0.14–0.33), suggesting that the apparent unadjusted benefit may reflect confounding by indication, particularly the higher rate of shockable rhythms in the thrombolysis group. Notably, this association persisted despite a higher incidence of shockable rhythms in the thrombolysis group, which are typically linked to improved outcomes.

Full model estimates, including odds ratios and confidence intervals for all covariates, are presented in Tables 3 and 4 and visualised in Figs. 2 and 3. These confirm the dominant impact of rhythm type, age, and witnessed status on ROSC and survival, and further question any independent benefit of thrombolysis in this context. The overall explanatory power of the adjusted models was modest (Nagelkerke pseudo-R² = 0.13 for survival), suggesting that a substantial proportion of outcome variability remains unexplained by the included variables.

Sensitivity analysis

As a sensitivity analysis to mitigate the risk of time-related bias, we repeated the primary and secondary outcome comparisons using only data from the same calendar year (2022–2023) for both groups. In this subgroup (n = 1198), survival to 30 days was 0.0% in the thrombolysis group (n = 44) compared to 9.2% in the non-thrombolysis group (n = 1154). ROSC at hospital arrival occurred in 15.9% of the thrombolysis group and 27.5% of the non-thrombolysis group. Although the thrombolysis cohort was small, these results were consistent with the main analysis, suggesting the absence of a substantial time-related confounding effect.

Subgroup analysis

In patients presenting with a shockable rhythm (VF/pVT), survival remained lower in the thrombolysis group compared to controls, despite shockable rhythms being a strong predictor of survival overall. To assess whether the effect of thrombolysis varied by initial rhythm, a logistic regression model was fitted with an interaction term between thrombolysis and rhythm type.

The interaction term was not statistically significant, indicating that the negative association between thrombolysis and survival was consistent across both shockable and non-shockable rhythms.

Post-hoc power analysis

A post-hoc power analysis was conducted to assess the likelihood of detecting meaningful differences given the observed event rates. For the primary outcome (30-day survival), the study had 81% power to detect a 72% relative difference in survival, assuming a baseline rate of 8.3% and α = 0.05. For ROSC, the sample size allowed detection of a 33% difference with similar power.

These findings support the statistical validity of the observed associations, despite the limited number of survivors in the thrombolysis group [10].

Discussion

In this large retrospective cohort study, prehospital thrombolysis with Tenecteplase was not associated with improved outcomes in out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA). On the contrary, both 30-day survival and ROSC rates were significantly lower in the thrombolysis group, even after adjustment for confounders such as rhythm type and witnessed status.

These findings raise important questions about the utility of thrombolysis in the prehospital phase of OHCA. Although thromboembolic causes such as pulmonary embolism (PE) and myocardial infarction (MI) are plausible triggers of cardiac arrest, they are difficult to reliably identify in the field. In our cohort, thrombolysis was administered based on a clinical suspicion often guided by rhythm type, history, or known risk factors, but this approach may lack diagnostic precision.

The results align closely with recent cohort study findings [6], which reported poor positive predictive value (PPV = 36.7%) for thrombolysis decisions in prehospital OHCA, despite high sensitivity for detecting thromboembolic causes. Like our study, Forbes’ cohort showed very low survival in patients who received thrombolysis, even among those later confirmed to have PE or STEMI. This suggests that delays in drug delivery, or the underlying severity of these conditions when presenting as cardiac arrest, may negate the potential benefits of reperfusion therapy.

Notably, in our dataset, the thrombolysis group had a much higher proportion of patients with shockable rhythms - typically associated with improved prognosis [11]. That outcomes were worse despite this advantage underscores the need to question whether Tenecteplase offers benefit in this context. It also suggests the possibility of selection bias or residual confounding, though the adjusted odds ratios remained significant. The inverse association between thrombolysis and both ROSC and 30-day survival in adjusted models may reflect confounding by indication. Patients selected to receive Tenecteplase may have appeared to have a suspected embolic cause or more severe arrest features not fully captured in the dataset. Notably, this association persisted despite a higher incidence of shockable rhythms in the thrombolysis group, which are typically linked to improved outcomes.

Interestingly, bystander CPR was associated with reduced survival in adjusted models. While this appears counterintuitive, it may reflect residual confounding. Bystander CPR is more likely in witnessed arrests with longer downtimes, where prolonged resuscitation may occur before CCP arrival or definitive care.

One explanation may relate to timing: drug administration often occurs late in the resuscitation timeline, when systemic perfusion is poor and cellular viability may already be compromised. Furthermore, without imaging or confirmatory diagnostics, thrombolysis may be given to patients without a true thromboembolic pathology. While no safety data (e.g. bleeding) were captured in this study, other observational studies have not identified a high risk of major haemorrhage in this setting, implying that the issue is not safety, but lack of benefit.

Our models demonstrated only modest explanatory power (pseudo-R² = 0.13 for survival), consistent with other prehospital cardiac arrest studies. This likely reflects the inherent complexity and variability of OHCA outcomes and the limitations of observational data. Important factors such as arrest duration, CPR quality, time to thrombolysis, and post-ROSC care were not captured and may contribute to residual confounding. Furthermore, no comorbidity data were available, preventing formal adjustment using tools such as the Charlson Comorbidity Index.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. It was retrospective and observational, and the number of thrombolysis cases was small, with very few survivors, limiting statistical power. The groups were not matched, and although regression analysis adjusted for major confounders, unmeasured variables likely influenced outcomes. Clinical judgement guided thrombolysis administration, introducing potential selection bias. Additionally, variations in hospital-based post-resuscitation care were not controlled for and may have influenced long-term survival.

Our thrombolysis and control groups were drawn from different time periods, with the thrombolysis group spanning five years and the control group one year. While both came from the same region and EMS system, this introduces a potential for time-related bias - particularly given the inclusion of the COVID-19 pandemic period. However, although the main analysis pooled data from multiple years to improve statistical power, we conducted a time-matched sensitivity analysis using only 2022–2023 data to address potential time-related bias.

Although the SECAmb registry follows Utstein recommendations, certain variables such as time of arrest, EMS response times, and comorbidities were not available within the registry itself and could not be included in the analysis. Arrest location was partially available and is included in a subgroup sensitivity analysis, but was not sufficiently complete across the full time period for multivariable modelling. This limits the completeness of covariate adjustment and introduces the possibility of residual confounding.

We explored stratifying the non-thrombolysis group based on CCP attendance, however the available data could not reliably distinguish cases where thrombolysis was considered but not administered from those in whom it was not possible due to CCP absence. This limits our ability to interpret the role of thrombolysis eligibility versus availability.

Although this study used a large real-world dataset, the retrospective design and absence of randomisation limit causal inference. The difference in inclusion periods between the thrombolysis and control groups may also introduce temporal bias, although both were drawn from the same regional system with consistent prehospital protocols. Future prospective studies may benefit from explicitly exploring interaction effects between thrombolysis and covariates such as initial rhythm or witnessed status, which may influence treatment responsiveness. Stratified analyses based on factors like bystander CPR provision or EMS-witnessed arrests may also provide further insight into differential outcomes. Additionally, alternative modelling approaches - such as mixed-effects regression or machine learning-based classifiers - may enhance predictive accuracy and allow for deeper exploration of treatment heterogeneity in OHCA populations.

Finally, while registry data were detailed, definitive post-mortem diagnoses were unavailable for most patients, limiting our ability to confirm thromboembolic aetiologies.

Conclusion

In this real-world cohort, prehospital thrombolysis with Tenecteplase was not associated with improved outcomes in OHCA and may be associated with worse survival. These findings, consistent with recent evidence, suggest that the use of thrombolysis in undifferentiated cardiac arrest should be re-evaluated. Future research should focus on improving diagnostic accuracy for thromboembolic causes of OHCA and identifying subgroups who may benefit from targeted intervention.
