Physics informed neural network can retrieve rate and state friction parameters from acoustic monitoring of laboratory stick-slip experiments
Authors: Prabhav Borate, Jacques Rivière, Samson Marty, Chris Marone, Daniel Kifer, Parisa Shokouhi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractVarious machine learning (ML) and deep learning (DL) techniques have been recently applied to the forecasting of laboratory earthquakes from friction experiments. The magnitude and timing of shear failures in stick-slip cycles are predicted using features extracted from the recorded ultrasonic or acoustic emission (AE) signals. In addition, the Rate and State Friction (RSF) constitutive laws are extensively used to model the frictional behavior of faults. In this work, we use data from shear experiments coupled with passive acoustic (variance, kurtosis, and AE rate) interleaved with active source ultrasonic monitoring (transmitted wave amplitude) to develop physics-informed neural network (PINN) models incorporating the RSF law and AE rate generation equation with wave amplitude serving as a proxy for friction state variable. This PINN framework allows learning RSF parameters from stick-slip experiments rather than measuring them through a series of velocity step experiments. We observe that when the stick-slip cycles are irregular, the PINN models outperform the data-driven DL models. Transfer learning (TL) PINN models are also developed by pre-training on data collected at one normal stress level followed by forecasting shear failures and retrieving RSF parameters at other stress levels (i.e., with different recurrence intervals) after retraining on a limited amount of new data. Our findings suggest that TL models perform better compared to standalone models. Both standalone and TL PINN-estimated RSF parameters and their ground truth values show excellent agreements thus demonstrating that RSF parameters can be retrieved from laboratory stick-slip experiments using the corresponding acoustic data and that the transmitted wave amplitude provides a good representation of the evolving frictional state during stick-slips.Subject terms: Geophysics, Seismology

Introduction

Earthquakes arise due to frictional instabilities along tectonic faults1. Laboratory experiments, such as those utilizing a double direct shear apparatus2, have been widely used to study the frictional properties of Earth materials3–10. In general, frictionally weak gouges exhibit a velocity-strengthening behavior, where friction increases with slip velocity11–17. On the other hand, fault gouges with higher frictional strength generally exhibit a velocity-weakening frictional behavior, where friction decreases with slip velocity11,18–22. A velocity weakening behavior is a necessary, but not sufficient, condition for frictional instability23, and such stick-slip instabilities occur when the fault weakening rate with slip surpasses the rate of elastic unloading24,25. The second-order variations in friction coefficient are typically modeled with the RSF framework23, and the related frictional parameters for a given fault/material are typically measured through a series of velocity steps. In such experiments, the load point velocity of the moving block is increased/decreased in a step-wise fashion, and subsequent changes in friction coefficients are measured. To facilitate the retrieval of frictional parameters, these experiments are typically conducted under stable-sliding conditions, that is, under a range of conditions (normal stress, sliding velocity, machine stiffness, etc) that do not lead to frictional instabilities. Because of that, friction experiments often include two stages, the first one where the frictional parameters are estimated with velocity steps under a stable sliding regime, and the second one where laboratory earthquakes (stick-slips) are produced and studied7,26–28. The two stages are often conducted consecutively, to ensure that the fault properties have not dramatically changed, but it would be highly beneficial if the frictional parameters could be retrieved directly from the stick-slip data. In this study, we propose a physics-informed machine learning that includes RSF laws, such that the RSF parameters can be directly learned from the stick-slip data.

Laboratory-scale friction experiments combined with active-source ultrasonic monitoring have shown that systematic changes in ultrasonic features along the seismic cycle, such as changes in wave velocity and spectral amplitude, can be used to foretell oncoming labquakes29–33. Machine Learning approaches have been used to predict labquakes from both active ultrasonic monitoring34–36 and passive monitoring (AE)37–39. Extreme gradient boosting approach can accurately estimate the fault strength throughout the stick-slip cycle40–42 based just on the AE data originating from the faults as an input. In these studies, approximately 100 features are fed into the ML models, and the variance and kurtosis of the AE data are found to be the most predictive features40,41,43,44. Standard AE analysis often involves creating a catalog record of the events that include location, magnitude, and timing. A Random Forest regression model implemented with such catalog parameters as inputs45 performs equally well compared to the statistical features-based models41,43 in predicting the shear stress and time to failure. A deep learning framework built with convolutional neural network and recurrent neural network modules performs satisfactorily in forecasting the timing of lab earthquakes using raw AE signals as input46,47. A transfer learning-based convolutional encoder-decoder model developed utilizing numerical simulations of a slipping fault learns a mapping between the AE histories and fault friction, leading to accurate lab quake friction predictions48,49. The autoregressive method developed using the long short-term memory, temporal convolution network, and transformer network allows for predicting shear stress at future instances via iterative predictions using previous data points50. The computational studies carried out for simulating and estimating frictional parameters of a spring-slider model demonstrate that PINNs51–54 accurately reproduce slow slip events and effectively estimate frictional parameters, showing potential for fault slip monitoring55,56.

In this work, PINN frameworks are developed to estimate RSF parameters during stick-slip experiments using active source ultrasonics-passive AE data, while also simultaneously forecasting shear stress, time to failure, and/or slip rate history. Two physics-based penalty models are incorporated into the PINN framework. The first one is the rate and state friction laws23, and the second one is a model that connects the AE release rate to the fault slip rate57. Data-driven DL and PINN models are developed to investigate labquake predictions in different training data sizes and locations, transfer learning, and future instances scenarios.

Results & discussion

We compare results for models based on XGBoost, data-driven DL, PINN, and transfer learning. In each case, the task is to predict the RSF parameters, as well as the shear stress, time to failure, and slip rate using the spectral amplitude from active data, and the variance, kurtosis, and AE rate from passive data. To estimate the variance of each model, we run the model entirely from scratch using five random seed instances. The final output is taken as the mean of these instances, with error bars corresponding to one standard deviation.

Friction experiment & passive-active monitoring

Our data are from frictional sliding of Westerly granite surfaces that were dusted with a light coating of quartz powder. We used the double direct shear (DDS) configuration in which two faults are sheared simultaneously (Figure 1a). The granite surfaces are dust-coated with min-u-sil (ϕ 40 μm) (US Silica Co.) to hasten the attainment of steady-state conditions associated with frictional wear. Shear (τ) and normal (σ) stresses are applied via servo-control with hydraulic pistons. Feedback to the servo-control is provided by DCDTs (direct current differential transformers), which measure the displacement of the load point with the resolution of ± 0.1 μm, and strain-gauge load cells that measure force to ±5 N. We also measure local fault shear displacement with a DCDT attached directly to the center block of the DDS arrangement. The fault slip rate is calculated from a time derivative of the local shear displacement. Experiments start by applying a fault normal stress, which is held constant throughout shear via servo-control. Shear displacement is imposed by advancing the DDS center block at a servo-controlled rate of Vl= 10 µm/s. All displacements and stresses are recorded at 10 kHz and averaged to rates from 0.1 to 1 kHz, depending on the section of the experiment, for storage.

We use data from two experiments: p5763 and p5759. Fault size and normal stress level distinguish the two experiments resulting in very different recurrence intervals and lab quake magnitudes, as shown in Figure 2. A 10×10cm2 fault area with a normal stress of σ= 9 MPa is used in p5763, while a smaller fault size of 5×5cm2 is used in p5759 to achieve a stiffer assembly (σ= 20 MPa) for a similar amount of force.

A comparison of the two datasets used in this study: shear stress, time to failure, slip rate, recurrence interval count, and amplitude are shown for (a) Experiment p5763 and (b) Experiment p5759.

The friction experiments included continuous passive-active acoustic monitoring using a local array of PZT sensors. Throughout the experiment, pairs of p-wave ultrasonic transducers are used to periodically probe the faults (Figure 1b,c). The steel platens holding the DDS assembly have blind holes in them where the piezoelectric transducers are epoxy-glued. On one side of the shearing assembly, a single transmitter T delivers a sequence of half-sine pulses at 500 kHz every 20 ms as part of active ultrasonic monitoring. On the other side, the receivers (eight and six receivers in p5759 and p5763 experiments respectively) are recording continuously at a sampling rate of 10 MHz (Figure 1d). The resulting active-source data for experiments p5763 and p5759 are made up of 38016 and 10236 waveforms (per channel) that are captured across 100 and 40 stick-slip cycles, respectively. Attenuation in the assembly is such that the recorded active pulses are fully attenuated after 1 ms, and the remaining 19 ms of data are treated as AE recordings.

Ultrasonic & acoustic feature extraction

We extract one feature from active-source ultrasonic data: spectral amplitude. At a frequency of 400 kHz, near the center frequency of transmitted waves, the spectral amplitude θi, a physics-based ultrasonic feature34, is calculated as the amplitude of the Fourier transform of each signal Si (the ith recorded ultrasonic signal at time ti) within a finite window of size T extending from w1 to w2=w1+T as shown in Figure 3a (Here, we use a window of size T=4.5μs). The signal captured within this window corresponds to the P-wave packet. The spectral amplitude extracted from all eight and six receivers for the p5759 and p5763 experiments respectively are shown in Figure 4. For each experiment, the mean amplitude value, calculated by considering the amplitude of all receivers at each time step, is used as an input. It should be noted here that this spectral amplitude feature is chosen as a proxy for the state variable within the RSF framework, as detailed in the Methods section.

To capture the evolution of the acoustic emission energy, higher-order moments of the acoustic emission time series data are computed as acoustic emission features. A 1-sec long backward-looking moving time window with 90% overlap is used to extract variance (Var) and kurtosis (Kur) from the passive data40,58 as shown in Figure 3b. This window duration is roughly 10% of the average recurrence interval (9.4 seconds). The extracted Var and Kur from all six and eight receivers for the p5763 and p5759 experiments are shown in Figure S1 and S2 respectively. AEs are searched within the continuous waveforms using a standard STA/LTA algorithm59–62. The STA/LTA algorithm continuously calculates the ratio between the energy (i.e., the square of the amplitude) contained in two consecutive time windows, with a “short time window” preceding a “long time window”. Here, we took advantage of the fact that AEs time arrivals are almost synchronous across the receivers (the maximum difference in arrival time is typically less than 10 μs) by applying the STA/LTA algorithm to the stack of the continuous power waveforms. Applying the STA/LTA algorithm to the stacked waveform minimizes the false detections and maximizes the detection of low amplitude AEs. We set the length of the short time window to 100 samples (0.01 ms) and the length of the long time window to 1000 samples (0.1 ms). Finally, we defined the occurrence in the stacked waveform of an AE as the STA/LTA ratio greater than or equal to 2. Similar to the spectral amplitude feature, the averaged acoustic emission features are also used as input to the models.

In both datasets, the extracted averaged active (θ) and passive (Var, Kur, AE) features exhibit systematic changes throughout the seismic cycles, as illustrated in Figure 3c,d. The spectral amplitude for each individual receiver also exhibits long-term trends (Figure 4), and despite averaging over all sensors, the averaged spectral amplitude also exhibits long-term trends: it increases throughout the experiment for the p5759 dataset, while it decreases in the later part of the experiment for p5763 (Figure 3).

Performance of XGBoost, data-driven DL, PINN and transfer learning models

For various training-validation-test splits, we evaluate the efficacy of XGBoost, data-driven DL, PINN, and transfer-learned models for predicting shear stress, TTF (i.e, time to failure), and slip rate from the extracted active-passive features. The results of the XGBoost model serve as the baseline performance. The data-driven DL model refers to a Multilayer Perceptron (MLP) model trained using an Adam optimizer. The regression MLP model is developed using training and validation datasets. Features are provided with a data history of one seismic cycle prior to the current time. The physics-based penalties in the form of equations are incorporated into the loss function of the MLP model to build the PINN models, as illustrated schematically in Figure 5. We build three PINN models: PINN #1, PINN #2, and PINN #3. The rate and state friction law (Equation 1) penalizes the PINN #1 model, while the AE rate generation equation (Equation 2) penalizes the PINN #2 model. PINN #3 is penalized using both Equations 1 and 2. The XGBoost, data-driven DL, PINN, and transfer learning model framework are all explained in detail in the “Methods” section.

Schematic representation of the PINN framework developed for shear stress, time to failure, and slip rate prediction using active-passive data: We explore three PINN models (PINN #1, PINN #2, and PINN #3) penalized by rate and state law and AE rate generation equation.

First, we examine how well data-driven DL and PINN models perform for various splits of the training, validation, and test data using the passive-active dataset of experiment p5763. Given the time series nature of the data, the training-validation-test datasets are chosen sequentially. When less than the first 80% segment of the data is chosen for training, the models perform poorly. We believe that this is because of the changing long-term trends in θ, which results in very different training and test data distributions. Specifically, when the model only ‘sees’ the slightly-decreasing portion of θ, up to 5600 s (corresponds to 70% data) in Figure 3c, it performs poorly on the test set with a sharply downward trending θ, which the model is unable to learn during training. Therefore, the models are built with the training set sizes of 80% and 10% such that the training and test set have similar distributions (both sampling θ with either a slightly or sharply decreasing trend). Figure 6 compares the performance of the baseline XGBoost model (grey color) for the two training set sizes when predicting shear stress, time to failure, and slip rate. We find that for shear stress predictions, XGBoost models trained with 80% of the training data produce test R2 scores of more than 0.8. Reducing the training set size to 10% results only in a slight degradation in the model performance. The outcomes of the data-driven DL MLP models are comparable to those of the XGBoost models, as seen in Figure 6 (green color). The three PINN models forecast shear stress and time to failure either on par with or marginally better than the data-driven XGboost & DL MLP models. Notably, PINN #3 forecasts slip rate less accurately (showing higher residuals). Predictions and the residuals in Figure 6 (d)-(f) indicate that the models show a combination of under-prediction and over-prediction towards the end of the seismic cycle. The R2 scores of all the models on training and validation data are given in Figure S3. The training times and root-mean-squared error (RMSE) values for the models are listed in Table S1. It is observed that data-driven DL models converge more quickly than XGboost and PINN models. We think that sluggish convergence is the result of the XGBoost and PINN models being more sensitive to long-term trends than the data-driven DL models. In sum, we find that in the case of limited training data (10%), all the models predict shear stress and time to failure equally well whereas slip rate is predicted better by data-driven DL, PINN #1, and PINN #2 models compared to XGBoost and PINN #3 models.

When we embed physics-based penalties in PINN, we treat the frictional parameters as learnable constants36 to retrieve these parameters and analyze the learning capabilities of the three PINN models. From the fully trained model’s learning layers, these weights are first extracted and then restored to their original scale considering the scaling that was applied during data normalization. Table 1 compares the learned parameters by PINN #1, PINN #2, and PINN #3 with the expected values obtained from the literature32. We observe that the learned μ0 and Dc values fall within the acceptable range. To compare the learned value of C we do not have the expected value of it as it is not possible to retrieve all the parameters in C from the experiment and model. In the case of the parameters a and b, it is observed that the learned values are under-predicted compared to the expected values when 80% training data is used. Remarkably, the learned values are closer to the expected values when 10% training data is used. We think this is because of the long-term trend observed in the active feature (θ). Overall, the findings demonstrate that it is possible to extract the friction parameters from the stick-slip data using active-passive signals. Our accurate retrieval of these parameters further supports the assumption that ultrasonic transmission amplitude may be used as a proxy for the state variable.

Next, we aim to find out whether PINN models present an advantage over XGBoost and MLP models in estimating shear stress far into the future. To do so, a model is developed using training (80%) and validation (10%) data on the p5763 dataset. It is then evaluated using testing (10%) data at different instances in the future: (1) immediately after the validation set, (2) half a stick-slip cycle after the validation set, (3) one stick-slip cycle after the validation set, or (4) two stick-slip cycles after the validation set. Figure 7 shows that, as shear stress is forecasted further into the future, the model’s performance gradually degrades. The XGBoost models perform better than the other models while the PINN models fare equally or marginally better than the MLP model. One possible explanation is the differences in how the MLP and XGBoost models handle heterogeneous features. XGBoost makes decisions based on the values of features, treating them independently of one another. MLP, on the other hand, learns new information through the linear mixing of the features. This could make predicting several steps a challenging task for MLP.

Lastly, the sensitivity of the XGBoost, data-driven DL, and PINN models to the training data selection is investigated. To that end, we vary the starting point of the training dataset, which constitutes 20% of the data, and fix the validation and testing datasets, which are the trailing 10% and 20% of the entire p5759 dataset respectively, as indicated in Figure S4. Our findings indicate that all model performances deteriorate with the increasing separation between training datasets from validation and testing datasets. In other words, a sequential selection of training-validation-test sets yields the best-performing models. When it comes to the training data selection, shear stress exhibits greater sensitivity than the time to failure prediction. The XGBoost model performs better than the MLP data-driven DL & PINN models when predicting shear stress using training data that is far away from the validation and testing data (Location #2). However, all of the MLP models outperform the XGBoost model when it comes to time to failure prediction.

Our observations so far indicate PINN’s marginal performance gain against MLP when modeling p5763 data. Next, we compare the generalizability of our models by developing transfer learning models pre-trained on the p5763 dataset with an 80%-10%-10% split before being fine-tuned to make predictions for the p5759 dataset. To establish a baseline, standalone XGBoost, MLP, and PINN models for the p5759 experiment are developed using training and validation data, and evaluated on testing data. For both the standalone and TL p5759 models, we use respectively 10% and 20% of the data for validation and testing, While training set sizes consist of 70% and 20% of the data. When the training set size is reduced from 70% to 20%, we observe a generally decreasing R2 score trend, except for the slip rate prediction. In testing, the PINN #1 and PINN #2 models outperform the reference XGBoost and MLP models with the improvement more pronounced for the models with the smaller training dataset size. The PINN #3 models outperform the MLP models in terms of predicting shear stress and time to failure, but not slip rate. Unlike what is reported in36, the incorporation of RSF law and AE rate generation equation in MLP models does not seem to significantly improve model performance with PINN models showing no or little advantage over their MLP models. One hypothesis to explain this observation is the regularity of the stick-slip cycles in both experiments p5759 and p5763. This is unlike the p5270 dataset (with only active-source ultrasonic monitoring and other physics laws) used in our prior study34,36, where the seismic cycles are fairly irregular (see Figure 3). Given that p5270 has only active ultrasonic monitoring data, we can build a PINN1 model for this dataset embedding RSF in the loss function. The training data sizes consist of 70% and 5% while the validation and testing data are set to 10% and 20% respectively for this investigation. The R2 scores of all the models on training and validation data are given in Figure S7. Figure 10 compares the PINN1 and MLP model for this dataset. We observe that with limited training data (5%), the PINN1 model outperforms the purely data-driven DL model while the performance is on par when 70% of the data is used for training. In other words, the PINN model advantage seems to become more significant for a dataset with irregular stick-slip cycles like p5270 than for datasets p5759 and p5763, where the cycles are regular. Methods

Given the time evolution of the extracted input features θi, Vari, Kuri, and AEi, our machine learning task is forecasting the history of shear stress (τi), time to failure (TTFi), and slip rate (Vi) (model outputs) to learn the RSF parameters. To that end, multi-output ML, DL, and PINN models (penalized by RSF law and AE rate generation equation) are trained and validated. Following34, the input features are given data history of one seismic cycle before the current time to predict the shear stress, time to failure, and slip rate at the current time.

Data splits & normalization

Each experiment’s dataset is split into non-overlapping training, validation, and testing sets. The models are trained using the training dataset; the validation dataset is used for hyperparameter tweaking; and the unseen testing dataset is utilized to assess each model’s performance. Given the time series nature of the data, we do not employ random sampling or mix the data during training, validation, or testing to preserve the stick-slip cycle sequence. To investigate the predictive abilities of the p5763 models with limited training data, the models are trained using training set sizes of 80% (80 seismic cycles) and 10% (10 seismic cycles) while preserving the size of validation and test sets. The validation and testing sets constitute 10% of the data each. Similarly, the training set sizes in the p5759 models consist of 70% (about 28 seismic cycles) and 20% (about 8 seismic cycles), with the validation and testing set making up 10% and 20% of the dataset, respectively. The data are scaled using min-max normalization before being used for model training, validation, and testing. To do this, we normalize the training data first, then use the training data min-max values to normalize the validation and test datasets.

Baseline model

We use a machine-learning technique called eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost)63 as a baseline model. The XGBoost performance serves as a benchmark against which the Multilayer perception (MLP) and PINN models are compared. To prevent overfitting, an early stopping condition is in place throughout the training phase, which results in the training termination if the validation error does not change after 20 iterations. The optimized set of parameters for this model after hyperparameter tuning are 0.01, 6, and 600 for the learning rate, maximum tree depth, and the number of gradient-boosted trees, respectively.

Data-driven DL model

Comparing the MLP and Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) models’ results for predicting shear failure indicates that the performance differences between the two models are marginal, as evidenced by the similar R2 scores and retrieved frictional parameters reported in Table S4 & Table S5, respectively. Therefore we have opted to use MLP, a fully connected neural network architecture, as the reference DL model due to its simplicity and faster training times. All our MLP models use features within one seismic cycle to account for the history and the sequential nature of the data. Using the MLP model, a learning bias-based PINN framework can be effectively implemented52,64,65. Hyperparameter tuning is achieved through grid search, varying the number of learning rates, layers, batch sizes, and nodes to determine the optimal parameters based on the performance of the validation set. The top-performing model has five hidden layers with 128, 64, 32, 16, and 8 nodes, in order from the first to the last. After hyperparameter tuning, a batch size of 32 and a learning rate of 0.001 are used. We employ a rectifier linear unit (ReLU) activation for every hidden layer while using linear activation for the output layer. The mean squared error (MSE) loss function is used. To avoid overfitting, early stopping (patience = 20) is enabled and the number of epochs is set to 100. Lastly, we assess the model performance using the R2 score metric.

The PINN models are built by incorporating one or two physics-based penalties in the loss function as shown schematically in Figure 5. The first model is the rate-and-state friction (RSF) law23,66,67 derived from laboratory fault friction experiments. This relation connects the time and velocity dependence of friction.

where μ0 is the friction coefficient at a baseline sliding velocity V0. The variable μ is the friction coefficient, V is the sliding velocity, and Dc is the critical slip distance needed to renew the microscale contacts25,66–68. Parameters a and b are material constants having second-order dependencies69–74. Previous studies employing optical observations show that real contact area increases with increasing normal stress and that at a constant normal stress, contact area increases over time75–78. At steady state, θ=DcV, and hence the frictional state variable (θ), with units of time, is often interpreted as a measure of contact lifetime23,79,80. Therefore, the state variable (θ) may be viewed as a representative of micro-contact quantity (real contact area) or quality (contact shear strength)69. Laboratory shear experiments on bare westerly granite and other materials81 have shown that P-wave transmitted amplitude may serves as a proxy for the real contact area at steady state69,75,76. Following these observations, we have used spectral amplitude of transmitted active-source ultrasonic signals as a proxy for θ. As we are not solving for θ in this work, neither aging nor slip law is considered.

Velocity step experiments are often used to parameterize the RSF friction law. In general, RSF parameters a, b, and Dc are inverted by minimizing an L2-norm between the measured and simulated friction data82–86. The simulated frictional data are generated by using RSF law with aging and/or slip law solved using numerical integration techniques with an elastic coupling equation as a constraint. The least-squares method is typically used iteratively to find the values of the parameters that fit the frictional data with an average R2 > 0.9. In this work, we consider RSF parameters listed in the literature32 as ground-truth values for our models.

Predicting shear stress in the future

We explore whether PINN models improve the model performance when used for prediction further in the future as opposed to the next time step. For this investigation, we develop the XGBoost, MLP, PINN #1, PINN #2, and PINN #3 models using the p5763 experimental dataset with a training-validation-testing split of 80%-10%-10% respectively. Model predictions for the next time step, half, one, and two failure cycles (Half, one, and two cycles correspond to 50, 100, and 200 timesteps.) are compared. The optimized set of parameters for the XGBoost model for each time step case after hyperparameter tuning are 0.1, 6, and 100 for the learning rate, maximum tree depth, and the number of gradient-boosted trees, respectively. In the MLP models, after hyperparameter tuning on all the models for each time step case, a batch size of 32 and a learning rate of 0.001 are used. The best-performing model in each time step case has five hidden layers with 2048, 1024, 512, 256, and 128 nodes, in order from the first to the last.

Sensitivity of the model to the training data selection

In unfavorable scenarios, systems may encounter malfunctions and result in a gap in the recorded data. Understanding how machine learning models behave with discontinuous data is crucial for ensuring their robustness. By studying sensitivity to training data selection, we can assess how well our models generalize when faced with such anomalies. To investigate this, we use the p5759 dataset with a training-validation-test data split of 20%-10%-20%, respectively. While the validation and testing sets remain fixed and sequential constituting the tail of the dataset, we vary the starting point of the training dataset as shown in Figure S4. In the first scenario, the training, validation, and testing datasets are in order while in the second scenario, the training data sequence is chosen from the beginning of the data, far from the validation and testing data. In each scenario, the performance of the XGBoost, MLP, PINN #1, PINN #2, and PINN #3 models are compared with the R2 score as a metric.

Transfer learning model

Taking a transfer learning approach, the generalizability of the models is examined using datasets from different experiments. To that end, MLP and each of the three PINN models trained and validated on the p5763 dataset (reference model) are used to initialize MLP and PINN models for the p5759 dataset. XGBoost method is not used in developing the transfer learning model since, unlike neural networks, it does not contain learnable weights. Using the same neural network architecture, transfer learning is accomplished by fine-tuning the learning rate, batch size, and all of the pre-trained p5763 reference model weights using only a limited training set (20% of the entire dataset) from experiment p5759. After hyperparameter tuning, a batch size of 32 and a learning rate of 0.001 are used for transfer learning MLP and PINN models. The models are trained using the Adam optimizer for 100 epochs, with an early stopping point of 20 to prevent overfitting. We use R2 scores, RMSE, and training time for each model to compare the performance of the models after transfer learning with that of the standalone models (Data-driven DL, PINN #1, PINN #2, PINN #3) trained exclusively on the p5759 dataset, with the training dataset sizes of 70% and 20% of the entire dataset.

PINN1 model: performance evaluation in the presence of irregular stick-slip cycles

Stick-slip cycles in both p5763 and p5759 experiments are very regular. Therefore, we modify PINN #1 (penalized by RSF friction law) to PINN1 and use it to make predictions on the p5270 friction experiment with irregular stick-slip cycles, where only active source ultrasonic monitoring is available34. We report the results pertaining to the PINN1 and MLP models trained using 70% and 5%, validated, and tested on 10%, and 20% of the entire dataset, respectively. Although, in general, the training data size should be greater than or equal to the testing data size in this study we investigated the training data size of 5% for two reasons. Firstly, by doing so we can compare the performance of the models developed using 70% and 5% training data. Secondly, as reported in36 for this dataset the performance improvement by implementing PINN is most noticeable when the training data are scarce. The R2 score is used as a metric to examine how well these models predict the shear stress, TTF, and slip rate variables. From first to last, the five hidden layers in the best-performing model include 128, 64, 32, 16, and 8 nodes. Following hyperparameter adjustment, a learning rate of 0.001 and a batch size of 32 are applied.
