Can grip strength capture what chair standing does? methodological considerations on functional measures and successful aging in three international cohorts
Authors: Zebo Kudratova, Azamat Karabaev, Vazira Raxmanova, Arofat Sadikova, Shahlo Axmadjonova, Farzan Vira Bagheri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Population aging has accelerated globally, making successful aging a key public health priority. Zhuang and Gao address the prospective association between cumulative average grip strength and successful aging using three international cohorts. While their multi-cohort design offers valuable cross-population insights, several methodological choices warrant consideration. Grip strength may not fully capture functional determinants of successful aging. Self-reported chronic disease introduces misclassification bias varying across socioeconomic groups. The two-stage temporal design does not fully address selection bias and cross-cohort comparability from heterogeneous exposure windows. Additionally, the cohort-relative cognitive threshold undermines cross-population comparability. These considerations highlight the need for methodological reflection and suggest directions for strengthening future multi-cohort studies.

To the Editor

We read with interest the article by Zhuang and Gao on the prospective association between cumulative average grip strength and successful aging across three international cohorts [1]. While the study addresses an important public health issue, several methodological considerations warrant attention regarding exposure selection, outcome measurement, and cross-cohort comparability.

1- The choice of grip strength as a proxy for lower-extremity function

The authors employed cumulative average grip strength as the primary exposure measure, yet successful aging, the outcome of interest, is a multidimensional concept that heavily depends on lower-extremity function. Grip strength primarily reflects upper-extremity muscle function, while mobility, activities of daily living, and social participation, core components of successful aging, are more directly influenced by lower-extremity strength [2]. This raises a fundamental question: if the goal is to assess the association between cumulative muscle function and successful aging, why was grip strength chosen over more functionally relevant measures such as the Five-Times Sit-to-Stand Test (CST-5) or gait speed? A comparative study of 3,099 community-dwelling older adults demonstrated that lower-extremity performance tests, including 4-meter gait speed, the Short Physical Performance Battery (SPPB), and the Six-Minute Walking Test, were significant predictors of mortality, whereas handgrip strength was not independently associated with survival in the fully adjusted model [3]. This evidence suggests that lower-extremity functional measures may capture clinically relevant health information that grip strength does not. Furthermore, biomechanical studies demonstrate that CST-5 performance is more closely tied to metabolic health, including glucose regulation and insulin sensitivity, than grip strength, because the lower-extremity muscles constitute the largest glycogen reservoir in the body [4]. Therefore, the observed association between grip strength and successful aging may not fully capture the biological pathways linking muscle function to chronic disease prevention.

2- Self-reported chronic disease status

Another concern relates to the reliance on self-reported physician-diagnosed chronic diseases as a component of the successful aging definition. This approach carries a well-documented risk of misclassification bias, particularly where diagnostic confirmation is limited. A systematic review found substantial variability in item-level reliability versus medical records, with kappa coefficients ranging from 0.66 to 0.86 across conditions [5]. A meta-analysis demonstrated that only diabetes showed good-to-excellent reliability (Cohen’s Kappa = 0.81), while most other comorbidities exhibited poor-to-moderate reliability [6]. Self-reported stroke is particularly concerning, with positive predictive values ranging from 22% to 87% depending on population and stroke prevalence [7]. In low-prevalence populations, a substantial proportion of self-reported cases may be false positives. Hypertension accuracy also varies considerably; a meta-analysis of 22 studies estimated pooled sensitivity at 42.1%, indicating that less than half of individuals with hypertension would be identified by self-report [8]. This is especially relevant given hypertension is among the conditions considered in the successful aging definition. Additionally, differential misclassification across socioeconomic groups is a concern. A study using English Longitudinal Study of Ageing data demonstrated that underreporting of chronic conditions was more prevalent among respondents with lower education levels [9]. Individuals with lower educational attainment, who may also have lower grip strength, might be incorrectly classified as disease-free and therefore successfully aging, potentially biasing the observed association in either direction.

3- The two-stage temporal design

The authors employed a two-stage temporal design to address reverse causality. However, several aspects warrant consideration. First, excluding individuals who already met successful aging criteria at baseline reduces reverse causality risk but creates a selection bias that limits generalizability. Factors predicting transition to successful aging may differ from those predicting its maintenance [10]. A longitudinal study demonstrated that selection bias can substantially inflate associations; the relationship between retirement age and age at onset was significant in the full sample (β = 0.31) but became non-significant after accounting for selection (β = 0.06) [11]. Second, the exposure window varied considerably across cohorts: 4 years in CHARLS, 8 years in ELSA, and 6 years in SHARE. A 4-year cumulative average in CHARLS may not be directly comparable to an 8-year cumulative average in ELSA. The trapezoidal weighting method accounts for time intervals between measurements but does not address differences in the etiologically relevant exposure window across cohorts. Third, the Rowe-Kahn model underlying the definition of successful aging has been critiqued for its static nature. Critics argue that successful aging should be viewed as a dynamic lifelong process rather than a static endpoint achieved at age ≥ 60 [10]. The authors’ temporal design, which treats successful aging as a dichotomous outcome at a single follow-up wave, does not fully capture this dynamic nature.

4- Arbitrary thresholds for cognitive function and outcome definitions

The authors defined high cognitive function using the median of the concurrent cohort sample. This creates a cohort-relative thresholding problem: in cohorts with overall poorer cognitive performance, the median is lower, and more individuals are classified as high cognitive function. This undermines cross-cohort comparability and may bias pooled meta-analysis estimates. A more robust approach would involve harmonizing cognitive scores across cohorts using item response theory (IRT) or absolute population-based norms [12]. Furthermore, the choice of the median is not the only option; a systematic review documented a wide range of thresholds each yielding substantially different classifications [10]. This variability reflects the absence of consensus and can materially affect both the incidence of successful aging and its association with exposure variables. Additionally, within-cohort Z-standardization used in sensitivity analyses does not address cross-cohort comparability. A more valid approach would involve demographically adjusted equivalent scores that standardize cognitive performance on a common metric across populations [13].

Conclusion

While this study provides valuable insights, several methodological concerns warrant attention. Grip strength may not fully capture functional determinants of successful aging, as lower-extremity measures have shown stronger predictive validity. Self-reported chronic disease introduces misclassification bias varying across socioeconomic groups. The two-stage design does not fully address selection bias and cross-cohort comparability issues from heterogeneous exposure windows. Additionally, the cohort-relative cognitive threshold undermines cross-population comparability. Addressing these considerations would strengthen future research.
