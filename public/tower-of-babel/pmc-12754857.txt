Diabetes prevention in the Caribbean using lifestyle intervention and Metformin escalation (LIME): results from a quasi-experimental study
Authors: Saria Hassan, Natasha P Sobers, Joanne Paul-Charles, Joseph Herbert, Kavita Dharamraj, Deron Galusha, Elsie Cruz, Enid Garcia-Rivera, Lyna Fredericks, Guedy Arniella, Adithya Cattamanchi, Oswald P Adams, Rohan G Maharaj, Cruz M Nazario, Maxine Nunez, Marcella Nunez-Smith
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe Caribbean region suffers from a high burden of diabetes and diabetes-associated mortality. Despite the evidence for diabetes prevention programs, there have been very few such initiatives in the region. To address this gap, we describe the effectiveness and implementation of the Lifestyle Intervention and Metformin Escalation (LIME) Clinical Trial in reducing the risk of diabetes among individuals with pre-diabetes in the Caribbean.MethodsLIME is a quasi-experimental pre-post study that took place between 2018 and 2021. LIME recruited individuals with high-risk pre-diabetes (hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) between 6.0 and 6.4%) across clinical sites in Barbados, Trinidad and Tobago, Puerto Rico, and the US Virgin Islands. Eligible participants were enrolled in a 6-week lifestyle modification workshop. Six months later, individuals who did not lose at least 5% of their bodyweight or had an HbA1c of 6% or higher were prescribed metformin medication. The primary outcome was a change in HbA1c at 6- and 12-months. Paired t-test and McNemar’s test were used to examine change in primary outcome. Implementation outcomes of reach, adoption, and implementation fidelity were assessed using programmatic data.ResultsOf 914 individuals screened, 211 (23%) had high-risk pre-diabetes. Of those, 114 enrolled in the study (54% Reach). At the 6-month follow-up, there was a significant reduction in average HbA1c from 6.19% to 5.95% (p < 0.001), average weight from 86.7 kg to 85 kg (p < 0.001), with a reduction in added sugar intake from 7.3 tsp/day to 3.8 tsp/day (p < 0.0001) but no significant change in physical activity level or fruit and vegetable intake. Reduction in HbA1c (6.19% to 5.67%, p < 0.0001) and weight (85.1 kg to 83.7 kg, p = 0.0149) was maintained at 12 months. Trained facilitators variably engaged in leading workshops (adoption 25%-100%), and were 100% adherent to the protocol (fidelity).ConclusionLIME is an effective diabetes prevention intervention for individuals with pre-diabetes in the Caribbean. Future work should investigate approaches to improve reach (eligible participant engagement) and adoption (participation of trained facilitators), and report on cost-effectiveness to inform scale-up of the intervention.Trial registrationclinicaltrials.gov NCT03258723 (23/08/2017).Keywords: Diabetes prevention, Implementation science, Hybrid-I, Metformin, Caribbean, Adaptation, Lifestyle intervention

Background

The North American and Caribbean region has the highest age-adjusted prevalence of diabetes in the world at 10.8% [1]. The prevalence of diabetes across the Caribbean is 9%, this is significantly higher than the prevalence worldwide and in South and Central America, which is about 7.5% [1, 2]. Some countries and states in the Caribbean have much higher prevalence rates (above 15%) including Barbados, Puerto Rico, and the USVI [3]. Trinidad and Tobago has the highest age-adjusted prevalence of diabetes at 12.12% [3]. This high prevalence comes with high diabetes-associated mortality: diabetes accounts for 13.8% of adult deaths in the Caribbean, with higher rates in some countries, such as Trinidad and Tobago, at 15% of deaths [2, 4].

Diabetes prevention is needed to address this rising burden of diabetes and diabetes-associated morbidity and mortality in the Caribbean. Since the Diabetes Prevention Program (DPP) results were published in 2002, there has been growing evidence that lifestyle intervention with and without metformin medication is highly effective in preventing, or at least delaying, the onset of diabetes [5–10]. More recent studies have used an escalation model that starts with lifestyle change workshops modeled on the DPP followed by metformin medication if lifestyle change is ineffective in achieving weight loss and/or a change in hemoglobin A1c [11]. The approaches of lifestyle change and metformin medication have extensive evidence from different parts of the world to show their effectiveness and cost-effectiveness in reducing the incidence of diabetes [12–14]. Despite this, the Caribbean region has had minimal evidence of the implementation of effective and scalable diabetes prevention programs [1, 15, 16].

We seek to address this gap with the Lifestyle Intervention with Metformin Escalation (LIME) study. LIME combines lifestyle modification and appropriate metformin prescribing to reduce the incidence of diabetes in the Caribbean. LIME assesses the effectiveness of a tailored lifestyle modification program combined with guideline-compliant metformin medication on reducing hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) in high-risk individuals with pre-diabetes in the Caribbean.

Methods

Lifestyle Intervention with Metformin Escalation (LIME) study overview

LIME is a hybrid implementation-effectiveness trial with a quasi-experimental pre-post study design. It aims to reduce HbA1c among individuals with high-risk pre-diabetes (HbA1c 6.0–6.4.0.4%) [14, 17] using a combination of lifestyle modification and metformin medication. The trial took place between 2018 and 2020 on the islands of Barbados, Trinidad, the US Virgin Islands, and Puerto Rico. A full protocol of the LIME study has been published [18]. Since the publication of the protocol, the COVID-19 pandemic has led to the following important modifications: (1) Limitation of the follow-up period to 12 months; (2) Inability to recruit a non-equivalent control group and change from a pre-post with non-equivalent control group study design to a pre-post study design.

The site leads recruited participants at each clinic using flyers posted in waiting rooms for self-referral and given to providers who could directly refer their patients to the study team. Site leads attended team meetings to talk to providers about the study and encourage them to refer eligible patients. In Trinidad and Tobago and in Barbados, site leads reviewed charts to identify potentially eligible patients for recruitment.

Eligibility criteria

Patients with pre-diabetes were eligible for the study if they met the following criteria: age 40–60 years, body mass index (BMI) >= 23 kg/m2 or waist circumference >80/90 cm, and a patient at the respective clinic. Cut off of 23 Kg/m2 was used for BMI, given that a proportion of participants identified as Asian [19]. Exclusion criteria included: not speaking English or Spanish, planning to move off-island in the next 12 months, already on metformin for pre-diabetes, elevated creatinine with eGFR < 60 mL/min/1.73m2 and pregnancy. Eligible patients had a point-of-care HbA1c obtained using the HbA1cNow + portable machine. They were included in the study if their HbA1c was between 6% and 6.4%. This HbA1c range identifies individuals at the highest risk of developing diabetes in the next 5 years [17]. Furthermore, this HbA1c range has been shown to be where diabetes prevention is most cost-effective [14]. If HbA1c was higher than 6.4%, they were deemed ineligible and were referred to their primary care physician for follow-up care, given a possible diagnosis of diabetes.

Study enrollment

Eligible patients signed up for one of several workshop groups (held at different times for their convenience). Patients who attended at least one workshop were considered engaged in the study and therefore their data would be analyzed as having participated in the intervention group. If participants who were consented never appeared for the workshop they had not engaged in the intervention and therefore not considered for data analysis or follow up.

The lifestyle change workshops

Eligible individuals (see below) were recruited and enrolled in an evidence-based lifestyle change workshop program based on the Self-Management Resource Center’s Chronic Disease Self-Management Program [20–22]. The workshop program consisted of 6 weekly sessions, each 2.5 h long. Session topics included healthy portions and the healthy plate, simple label reading, avoiding sugar-sweetened beverages, identifying unhealthy and cooking healthy foods and drinks, strategies to deal with stress and emotions that negatively affect health (e.g., problem-solving, coping skills, and social support), and incorporating physical activity into daily routines. Participants made “action plans,” practiced exercising together, brainstormed ideas to address challenges they identified, and communicated between sessions using WhatsApp. All workshops were conducted by two trained facilitators. Each participating island site determined which persons within their group would be best positioned to be trained as a facilitator. As many individuals as desired by the site could be trained as facilitators.

Metformin escalation

Participants were brought back for follow-up six months after the start of the workshops. If their HbA1c remained above 6% at six months and they had not lost at least 5% of their weight, they were prescribed metformin medication. Their primary care provider prescribed Metformin 500 mg by mouth twice a day. Participants could call their provider’s office if they have any issues with the medication. All participants had a final follow-up at 12 months.

Data collection

At each study visit (baseline, six months, and twelve months), participants completed a survey, and clinical and laboratory assessments. Survey data included demographics, medical history, family history, diabetes self-efficacy score (using the Risk Perception Survey-Developing Diabetes, RPS-DD focusing on the personal control and worry domains) [23], physical activity (using the World Health Organization Global Physical Activity Questionnaire, GPAQ) [24], and nutrition (using the dietary screening questionnaire) [25]. Adherence to metformin medication was to be assessed using pill count at the 12-month visit. Clinical assessments included weight, height, BMI, waist circumference, weight-to-hip ratio, and blood pressure. Clinical staff undertook training to ensure clinical assessment standardization across sites. Laboratory data collected included cholesterol and serum creatinine. All survey, clinical assessment, and laboratory assessment data were collected and managed using REDCap (Research Electronic Data Capture) electronic data capture tools hosted at Emory University. REDCap is a secure, web-based software platform designed to support data capture for research studies [26]. REDCap Laboratory assessment included HbA1c (through point-of-care HbA1cNow + machine).

Sample size calculation

Given a study timeline of 12 months for implementation of the LIME strategy, we did not have sufficient follow-up time to have diabetes onset be a primary outcome. Instead, we powered the study to be based on proxy outcomes from the Diabetes Prevention Program; namely 5% weight loss and reduction of HbA1c by 0.2%. In addition, we were interested in the primary outcome of the proportion of persons who have an HbA1c that comes down below the high-risk range – less than 6%. Therefore, our target enrollment was 100 to allow us to detect a 20% reduction in the proportion of participants whose HbA1c falls below 6% over 12 months, with 80% power and a two-sided alpha of 0.05. In addition, it allows for a 30% loss to follow-up. This sample size also provides power to detect a change in average HbA1c of 0.2, and to observe a change in the average weight of at least 5% before and after the intervention.

Analysis

Reach

Programmatic data was used to determine the number of people who attended a workshop session after being recruited (engaged individuals), representing the program’s reach. Univariate analysis was used to determine if there were critical differences in sociodemographic, clinical, and laboratory characteristics between engaged and not engaged.

Effectiveness

The primary outcome was the proportion of individuals with HbA1c less than 6%. Secondary outcomes included average HbA1c, weight, blood pressure, BMI, waist circumference, waist-to-hip ratio, self-efficacy score, physical activity, added sugar intake, and fruit and vegetable intake. Paired t-test (continuous variables) and McNemar’s test (proportions) were used to examine the difference in primary and secondary outcomes between baseline and six months and baseline and twelve months.

Physical activity was measured using responses to the GPAQ; using established methods, the GPAQ responses were translated to metabolic equivalent-time units and subsequently classified as high, moderate, or low physical activity [24]. Added sugar intake and fruit and vegetable intake were calculated using responses from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) Dietary Screener Questionnaire using established methods. The responses were translated into weekly servings for fruit and vegetable intake and teaspoons daily for added sugar intake (excluding sugar from cereal).

Adoption

Each site reported how many individuals were trained in workshop facilitation. The sites then reported which trained individuals facilitated at least one workshop session. The proportion for each site was calculated.

Implementation

Fidelity was assessed to the protocol by determining whether patients were appropriately referred for metformin medication based on criteria of HbA1c and weight. In addition, fidelity to delivering the workshops was assessed using a pre-defined checklist. Each workshop series (a 6-session set) was evaluated once by a master trainer (expert facilitator) using a pre-defined checklist to assess fidelity to workshop content and delivery. The checklist was reviewed for the level of fidelity.

COVID disruption of follow-up

The COVID-19 pandemic disrupted our ability to conduct follow-up appointments for participants. This reduced the number of individuals at six and 12-month follow-ups. Only individuals who completed follow-up and had complete data for outcomes of interest were included in the pre-post analyses.

Effectiveness

Tables 4 and 5 depict the 6-month and 12-month outcomes. At six months follow-up, 52% of participants had lowered their HbA1c to less than 6%, and 18% had lost more than 5% of their weight. Sixty-three percent of participants (47 out of 75; 5 missing data) did not require metformin at the 6-month follow-up (they had lost at least 5% of their weight or their HbA1c was less than 6%). 75% (38 out of 51, 1 missing) of participants at 12 months had an HbA1c of less than 6%. Eighty-three percent (40 out of 48, 4 missing) of participants at 12 months had either reduced their HbA1c by less than 6% or lost at least 5% of their weight. At both the 6-month and 12-month follow-up periods, there was a statistically significant decline in average HbA1c (p = 0.0007 and p < 0.0001 at 6- and 12-months, respectively). There was a statistically significant reduction in weight at six months (p = 0.0005) that was sustained at 12 months (p = 0.0149). There was no significant change in the level of physical activity but a significant reduction in added sugar intake at six months (p < 0.0001) that was sustained at 12 months (p = 0.0028). There was no statistically significant change in self-efficacy at 6- and 12-months.

Implementation fidelity

There was 100% fidelity to the referral for metformin medication. At six months, 18.4% of patients had lost at least 5% of their weight, 52% of patients had reduced their HbA1c below 6%, and 37.3% of patients across sites did not drop their HbA1c at 6months or lose 5% of their weight and were therefore referred for metformin medication per protocol.

Using checklists, fidelity to workshop content and delivery as intended was 100%.

Discussion

The LIME trial successfully and sustainably reduced HbA1c among individuals with high-risk pre-diabetes in the Caribbean. A sustained reduction in weight accompanied the decrease in HbA1c. A relatively small proportion of patients (37%) required metformin medication per protocol compared to a similar escalation study in India, where 72% required metformin medication [11]. These differences may be due to differences in protocol (in the India-based study, eligibility is not only based on HbA1c, included all pre-diabetes not just high-risk pre-diabetes, and assessment for metformin eligibility was done at four months instead of 6 months) or differences in the curriculum that was used for lifestyle change (the DPP in the India study versus the Self-Management Resource Center curriculum in the LIME study).

Given the high burden of diabetes in the Caribbean, the findings from the LIME study can potentially transform prediabetes care and weight management in the region if scale-up of the program can be achieved. The small island developing states of the Caribbean have high levels of obesity led by Barbados (36%), and Trinidad and Tobago, Antigua and Barbuda, all at around 31%; the sustained weight loss found in this program can be offered as an option to persons struggling with the comorbidities associated with obesity including diabetes [28]. This is particularly important given that studies in primary care settings in the Caribbean have shown that HbA1c control may not be achieved with routine care [29]. The successful implementation of the program in four different islands speaks to the generalizability of the work to other Caribbean countries. All sites implemented the program in a primary healthcare setting. The continuity of care and the relationship between patients and staff and providers at the health center is likely integral to the motivation to engage in the program. It is unclear at this time the degree to which the program can be implemented in other healthcare settings.

While there was no significant change in the level of physical activity or self-efficacy score, there was a significant reduction in added sugar intake. We believe this to have been driving the change in weight and HbA1c observed. Reducing excess sugar intake has the potential to improve not only diabetes control but it has also been shown to improve blood pressure control, cardiovascular disease risk, and oral health [30, 31]. Qualitative interview data may provide insight into the challenges to increasing physical activity, such as access to well-lit parks, free public gyms, or exercise programs. For the self-efficacy score, we focused on personal control and worry. Worry is likely a great concern in this context where most individuals are surrounded by friends and family members with the disease; this is something the program should look to address in the future. The personal control score was relatively high at baseline (3.55/4) leading to a minimal change over time. There is also the possibility of social desirability bias that can be addressed in the future by using a social desirability scale. Limitations in sample size may have also driven the lack of effect in both self-efficacy score and physical activity.

Despite the success of the LIME trial in reducing the risk of developing diabetes among individuals with high-risk pre-diabetes, its reach was only 50%. This means that, of those identified as eligible to engage in the study, only 50% did (i.e. only 50% of those who consented to participate attended one workshop session and engaged in the study). This indicates the urgent need to identify ways of encouraging eligible individuals to engage in the program. In comparing the characteristics of participants who were engaged versus not engaged, the only statistically significant difference can be seen in the level of education. It is important for us to understand reasons behind those with lower levels of education not engaging in future iterations of the program and to improve reach. Once they attended the workshop, participants successfully achieved weight loss and reduced their HbA1c. Ongoing analysis of in-depth interviews with participants and implementers to understand reasons for and against engagement in the program will provide helpful information to improve reach and ensure future program sustainability. Increased engagement of health providers and organizations trusted by patients may help assist with reach.

Adoption looking at the proportion of trained staff who engaged in conducting the lifestyle workshops – this number was variable across sites. This reflects the challenge of engaging existing clinic staff and providers. The site with the lowest rate of adoption had engaged nurses within the clinics to conduct the workshops. The busy schedule and multiple demands on nurses makes it difficult for them to find time to facilitate the workshops. This was a similar challenge for the clinicians in Barbados who were engaged to be facilitators. Indeed, with multiple competing demands, existing clinic staff/providers experienced challenges finding time outside of work hours to deliver the workshops. The highest adoption was among staff where nutrition education was part of their existing scope of work (Puerto Rico) and among recent graduates from medical schools who were in between positions (Trinidad). With the scale-up of the program, future models of care should provide dedicated time/effort that is compensated for workshop delivery to ensure sustainability.

The success of LIME is promising and there is a need to address the scalability of the program given the challenges outlined above. Qualitative work as we have noted can help better understand reasons for low reach and adoption. As we entertain different strategies to improve reach (e.g. partnering with faith-based organizations to encourage engagement in lifestyle change) and adoption (e.g. having dedicated, compensated staff to facilitate workshops) it will be imperative to assess their associated cost. Cost-effectiveness analysis for the LIME study will be important to understand not only the most cost-effective implementation strategy but also to convince policy-makers to include programs like LIME routinely within the primary care healthcare infrastructure.

There are some limitations of the study that should be noted. The COVID-19 pandemic limited our ability to use a non-equivalent control group which would have strengthened the study design. The COVID-19 pandemic also limited the follow-up period for the study to 12-months and meant that several participants could not be brought back for a 12-month visit because of the pandemic; this reduced the number of individuals at follow-up, which may have impacted the significance of our results. At the time of follow-up, health clinics where the study was conducted sought to reduce the number of persons attending clinics by canceling non-essential activities such as research projects. This introduces a degree of bias given that the sites that started recruitment earlier had more patients attend follow-ups at 6- and 12-months. This led to more participants in the follow-up data sample from Trinidad and Tobago and Barbados (started in 2018) than in USVI and PR (started in 2019). The variable start time of the sites was largely due to differences in time to approval of the study by the ethical review board of the respective institutions. The variable start time also contributed to the unequal distribution of participants across the four clinical sites, with those starting earlier (Trinidad and Tobago and Barbados) recruiting more participants than those starting later (USVI and PR). Another contributor to the variability across sites is the challenge of recruiting participants given stringent eligibility criteria. The two sites that used chart review (Trinidad and Tobago and Barbados) to identify potentially eligible patients were more successful in the timely recruitment of a larger number of patients meeting eligibility criteria. While the objective of the study was to see improvement among the highest-risk patients with pre-diabetes, future work can explore the impact on all patients with pre-diabetes increasing the number of persons eligible for participation and reducing the burden of recruitment. The variability in the start time of the study sites and the challenges of identifying eligible participants contributed to the overall study period being quite prolonged with the first participant being recruited in 2018 and the study ending in 2020. This prolonged window introduces biases due to changes in the environment that may be impacting the outcomes under consideration independent of the intervention. Future studies using a control arm will be able to discern changes due to the environment versus the intervention.

Another limitation is that fruit and vegetable intake and added sugar intake were assessed using the DSQ which has not been validated specifically for persons with pre-diabetes. Future work should consider validating this easy-to-administer tool in this population for improved validity of the study. Another limitation relates to missing data; this was minimal for our primary outcome of HbA1c at both 6-months (3 missing) and 12-month (1 missing) follow-ups. Given the small number in the missing data pool even for secondary outcomes we were not able to conduct any comparison analyses and no imputation methods were applied. Another limitation is the use of point-of-care HbA1c testing that is not widely available in all settings in the Caribbean, which could affect the scale-up of LIME. Future work should also assess workshop attendance rates and their association with study outcomes. There may be a minimum number of sessions that must be attended for effect, or it may be particular sessions that contribute to effectiveness. This should be explored to inform future scale up. Lastly, metformin adherence data was inconsistently collected and could not accurately be reported. However, in sensitivity analyses, no difference was noted in the primary outcome of HbA1c less than 6% or greater than 5% weight loss when comparing the participants who received metformin versus those who did not. Future work should assess metformin adherence to determine the extent to which the medication does (or does not) have an impact on study results. Despite these limitations, the success of this program is extremely promising. Future work will investigate the scale-up of the program, sustainability of effect at 24 months, and cost-effectiveness.

Conclusion

The LIME study proved effective in reducing HbA1c and weight among individuals with high-risk prediabetes in 4 Caribbean-based primary care clinics. 63% and 81% of participants at 6- and 12-months, respectively, lost at least 5% of their weight or dropped their HbA1c below the high-risk range of 6%. By carefully studying the implementation of LIME, barriers to scale-up were identified, including limited reach and adoption. Understanding how to overcome these barriers will be critical to scale-up and scale-out of this program with significant potential in addressing pre-diabetes and obesity in the Caribbean. LIME has the potential to increase access to effective lifestyle change programs for persons at high risk of developing diabetes, and the consideration of medication such as metformin to assist with diabetes prevention.
