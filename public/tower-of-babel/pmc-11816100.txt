Municipal breast and colorectal–anal cancer mortality in Sardinia, Italy from 2012 to 2020: application of a hierarchical Bayesian method
Authors: Antonello Russo, Laura Maria Beatrice Belotti, Emilio AL Gianicolo
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundExcess mortality rates for breast cancer and colorectal–anal cancer in Sardinia are reported in the literature. The aim of the study is to analyse mortality for both cancer entities in Sardinian at municipality level.MethodsSex-specific mortality rates by region and municipality of residence were computed using data from the Italian National Institute of Statistics for the years 2012–2020. Causes of death for malignant breast cancer in women and colorectal–anal cancer in both genders were analysed. The epidemiological analysis was based on the direct and indirect standardisation method. To obtain better interpretable maps of the distribution of relative risk (RR) of mortality, the hierarchical Bayesian Poisson-Gamma method was used.ResultsWith regard to breast cancer mortality in women, RRs showed an excess of mortality at the municipal level in four municipalities. In relation to colorectal–anal cancer, RRs showed an excess mortality in four municipalities for men and in three municipalities when considering the total population. At the health-district level, an excess mortality was observed in 1 out 24 districts for colorectal–anal cancer, in both men and the total population.ConclusionThis study provides an analysis of mortality at the municipal level for breast cancer and colorectal–anal cancer in Sardinia. It is recommended that the Sardinian regional authorities act on modifiable risk factors, like increasing the screening coverages for both neoplasms, in order to reduce the burden of both diseases.

Introduction

Colorectal and breast cancers are among the most frequent causes of death in Italy.1 According to the Italian National Institute of Statistics (ISTAT), age-standardised mortality rates for colorectal–anal cancer in both sexes and breast cancer in 2020 are more than 10% higher in Sardinia than in Italy.2

Among the determinants of these two cancer entities, deficiencies in screening activities36 and the influence of environmental factors have to be mentioned.7 8 In addition, risk factors for breast cancer include genetic mutations, a long fertile period, a high breast density, a family history of breast or ovarian neoplasia, exposure to radiation, diethylstilbestrol, lack of physical activity, overweight, postmenopausal obesity, taking hormone replacement therapy at menopause, first pregnancy after the age of 30, not breast feeding and alcohol consumption.9

With regard to colorectal cancer (CRC), the following risk factors have been reported: alcohol, smoking, obesity, the consumption of red and processed meats, genetic predisposition, a family or personal history of colorectal neoplasia and particular medical conditions such as diabetes, inflammatory bowel disease, exposure to abdominal radiation, cystic fibrosis, cholecystectomy and androgen deprivation therapy in patients with prostate cancer. In contrast, dietary fibre consumption and physical activity are mentioned as protective factors.10

With reference to screening activities, the PASSY study (Progressi delle Aziende Sanitarie per la Salute in Italia) study reported in Sardinia a lower coverage than on average in Italy for both mammography screening and colorectal screening.11 12

With regard to the influence of environmental factors, the sixth Sentieri Report analysed the association between sources of environmental exposure and health outcomes. It reported limited evidence for an association between CRC and proximity to chemical plants and coalmines. Limited evidence is also reported for the association between breast cancer and proximity to petrochemical plants and oil refineries, steel plants, hazardous and/or mixed waste dumps and first-generation incinerators.13

An analysis conducted with two Bayesian methods on breast cancer mortality using the 1981 Italian rates as a reference did not show any excess mortality within the Sardinian health districts for the period 1983–1985.14

To cope with the fact that cancer registries cover only the provinces of Nuoro and Sassari and therefore less than half of the Sardinian population, a study was also conducted with the aim of estimating the incidence, survival and mortality due to cancer in Sardinia for 2012–2015. Mathematical models were used to estimate the cancer burden, using historical data on regional causes of death (1970–2007) and survival for the southern macro-area from the cancer registries included in the EUROCARE-4 study (EUROpean CAncer REgistry-based study on survival and CARE of cancer patients, 1985–2002). For men, the results showed a steady upward trend in the incidence of CRC over the period 1970–2015. In fact, in this period CRC became the most frequent cancer diagnosis, followed by lung cancer.15

Palomba et al conducted a study on the prevalence in Sardinia of BRCA1-2 mutations in patients with familial recurrence of invasive breast and ovarian carcinoma and found a higher frequency in the middle of Sardinia. However, the overall prevalence in the region was lower than other Italian and European regions.16

Pes et al conducted a study for the period 1981–1988 on the association among male military personnel between CRC and glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency. They found both a cluster in the central area of Sardinia and a deficiency of G6PD in the villages of the north-eastern and southern-western flatlands, that is, in areas infested with malaria since pre-Roman times. This study not only confirmed a moderate inverse relationship between CRC mortality and G6PD but also that most of the variability in CRC mortality is unexplained.17

Finally, an analysis of mortality conducted in Italy at the province level on persons between 30 and 89 years of age for the period 2012–2014 shows higher standardised mortality ratios (SMRs) adjusted for age and education level in all Sardinian provinces both for breast cancer and for colorectal and anal cancer.18

Thus, even if both cancer entities have been analysed in the last years, so far there is a lack of epidemiological evidence at health district and municipal level. Therefore, the aim of the study is to analyse the age-standardised rates of Italy and Sardinia in three periods from 2012 to 2020 and to address this gap by analysing mortality for breast cancer and colorectal–anal cancer in Sardinian health districts and municipalities in comparison to the regional reference.

Methods

The Sardinian healthcare system is organised into eight local health trusts (LHTs) and 24 health districts (online supplemental figure S1)19

All analyses were conducted at district and municipality level (n=377) and according to the degree of urbanisation. The degree of urbanisation, produced by EUROSTAT, is based on a combination of geographical contiguity and population density and allows classifying municipalities into the following categories: cities; towns and suburbs; rural areas (https://www.istat.it/it/archivio/156224)

Through the National Institute of Statistics (ISTAT), the number of deaths by initial cause of death, by gender and municipality of residence, for the years 2012–2020 was acquired. Causes of death were coded according to the 10th revision of the International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems (ICD-10). Data on initial causes of death, detailed by age group (0 years, 1–4 years, 5–9 years, 10–14 years, …, 95 years and older), were only available at a regional level.

In addition, annual mortality data by regional area of residence from 2012 to 2020 by gender and initial cause of death according to the Eurostat classification called European Shortlist for Causes of Death (COD SL-2012) were acquired.2

Finally, the annual average population numbers for the years 2012–2020 were obtained by processing the residential population data at the beginning of the year (2012 to 2021) at the municipal and regional level, by gender and year of age, as detailed in the online supplemental material.2

Causes of death for malignant breast cancer in women (ICD-10 code: C50) and colorectal–anal cancer in both genders and in total (ICD-10 codes: C18-C21) occurring in the period 2012–2020 in the population residing in Sardinia were analysed. We treated colorectal–anal cancers together, because it was only at this level of grouping, as set out in the European Shortlist for Causes of Death, that details by age group were available to us. However, the prevalance of anal cancers is low, that is, less than 0.9% of the total number of colorectal–anal cancers observed at municipal level. For data protection purposes, ISTAT is not allowed to disclose cause-specific number of deaths occurred in 1 year at municipal level if less than three. We compared the number of deaths provided by ISTAT at the regional level, for which data protection constrains are easily meet, with the sum of deaths by cause provided by ISTAT at the municipal level: the total number of deaths not provided at municipal level was 951 out of 147 216 (0.7%). Similar percentages are observed for the specific causes of death analysed in our study.

In fact, the cause of death is missing for 25 out of 3440 total deaths for breast cancer (<0.8%) and for 25 out of 5218 total deaths for colorectal–anal cancer (<0.5%).

Therefore, given the very low number of municipal deaths without an initial cause of death, no missing imputation was carried out.

The epidemiological analysis for the causes of death under study was based on the direct and indirect standardisation method.

The direct method consists of calculating standardised death rates by multiplying the specific mortality rates per 5-year age group observed in the study population (by gender, cause of death and region/municipality) to the size of the age groups of a reference population. The population used as a reference for standardisation is the ‘European Standard Population—2013 edition’.20 In particular, the average annual Sardinian and Italian populations were used for the cause and gender specific rates. The analysis was conducted for the 3-year periods of 2012–2014, 2015–2017 and 2018–2020. Direct standardised rates allow comparisons both temporally and spatially, after accounting for the confounding effect of different demographic structures. The standardised rates for the three 3 year periods in the Sardinia region were compared with the Italian ones to obtain standardised mortality rate ratios (comparative mortality figures—CMFs).21

The indirect method involves estimating the relative risks (RRs) of mortality by cause through the calculation of SMRs. The number of ‘observed’ cases was divided by the number of ‘expected’ cases, obtained by applying the mortality rates of a reference population (Sardinia region) to the population under study (municipalities and health districts), specific by sex and age class (5-year classes: 0–4 years, 5–9 years, …, 95 years and over). SMR values greater than 1 represented an excess mortality in the study population over the reference population, after accounting for the confounding effect of age. Analyses were stratified by the degree of urbanisation (rural, small towns, urban) to evaluate the ‘urban effect’ as a potential confounder or effect modifier.22 For the assessment of the uncertainty, 95% CI were calculated, applying the Poisson formula if the number of observed deaths was less than 100 and the Byar approximation otherwise.

For small municipalities and very low numbers of observed cases, the estimates provided by the SMRs are not stable and the geographical distribution of the SMRs is difficult to interpret.23 To overcome this issue and obtain better interpretable maps of the mortality RR distribution (with 95% credibility intervals), the hierarchical Bayesian Poisson-Gamma method was used, both at the municipal and health district levels.24 This method combines the Poisson distribution with an a priori Gamma distribution, whereby the events observed in each municipality and the variability of the SMRs in the entire study area are taken into account. The result is a smoothing of the SMR estimates’ variability, which tends towards the overall mean. The adjustment is greater the more unstable the estimates are. It follows that estimates based on a large number of observed cases are preserved; in contrast, estimates based on few observations are ‘smoothed’ towards the overall mean. Since there were no a priori assumptions about the distribution of risks, uninformative a priori distributions were chosen.25

Cartograms were drawn up using the administrative boundaries of Italian municipalities as of 1 January 2023 from ISTAT sources.26

The Geographic Information System (QGIS.org, 2022), Stata release V.17 software (StataCorp, 2021. College Station, Texas: StataCorp LLC) and WINBUGS software were used for the analyses.

Discussion

Our study provides an epidemiological analysis of mortality at the municipal level for breast cancer and colorectal–anal cancer in Sardinia using recent data. The analysis showed an increased risk among Sardinian residents compared with the Italian population for breast cancer in women and for colorectal–anal cancer in men and in the total population. Our study also showed that this excess risk appears to be persistent when comparing regional and national standardised rates over the three periods analysed.

The Bayesian RR indicator showed how excess mortality for the causes under study affects small and rural municipalities. In fact, Oristano, the most populous municipality where an excess for breast cancer mortality was observed, had an average female population in the 9- year period of about 16,000 residents. The largest municipality in which an excess of mortality in the total population for colorectal–anal cancer was observed had an average population in the 9-year period of less than 15,000 residents (Guspini).

The analysis by degree of urbanisation made it possible to highlight that excess mortality rates for breast cancer were observed in small towns. With respect to the 24 health districts that make up the Sardinian health system in the territory, an excess mortality was observed in the Ales Terralba district alone for colorectal–anal cancer in men and in the total population.

Strengths and limitations

One of the strengths of our study is the use of different spatial dimensions of the territory under analysis and different mortality indicators. In fact, the region under study was analysed both according to health districts and to the current municipal administrative boundaries. Mortality rates were standardised in order to account for age. Finally, through Bayesian analyses, we accounted for the instability of estimates that characterises small municipalities, resulting in robust estimates of relative mortality risks.

Some limitations have to be considered while interpreting the results of our study. The main limitation of the study is the lack of adjustment for socioeconomic deprivation and degree of urbanisation. Detailed data on both confounders were unfortunately unavailable to us. Furthermore, we used an ecological study design. Thus, inference at an individual level of the results obtained at health district or municipality level is not allowed. Another limitation is the use of mortality data, on which several factors, not least the quality of hospital care, are known to play a role.

Unfortunately, in Sardinia, only two out eight LHTs (Sassari and Nuoro) have a cancer registry. However, they do not publish municipal or health district mortality results with which to compare our results.27

Budroni et al filled the gap of epidemiological analysis for the Sardinian region by using mathematical models that estimated mortality and incidence for the main cancer sites for the recent period 2012–2015.15 However, their estimates were not precise. In fact they estimated in 2012 295 deaths for breast cancer in Sardinian women while ISTAT provided in the same year a number of 374 cases.15

The Sixth Report of the Sentieri Study analysed causes of death and hospitalisation potentially associated with the sources of environmental exposures of the various Sites of National Interest for Remediation (SIN) over the period 2013–2017.13 In relation to breast and colon cancer, the authors observed for the Porto Torres SIN, composed of the municipalities of Porto Torres and Sassari, an excess of hospitalisation for CRC and breast cancer. For the Sulcis-Iglesiente-Guspinese SIN, composed of the 39 municipalities, the Report showed an excess mortality for both breast cancer and CRC among women.13 In our study, we observed among the 39 municipalities of the Sulcis-Iglesiente-Guspinese SIN a higher mortality for breast cancer in Gonnosfanadiga and for CRC in the men and in the total population of Guspini. It should also be noted that Gonnosfanadiga and Guspini are also part of the historic Arbus mining area.28

For Portotorres, a municipality in the north of Sardinia close to an important industrial area, our results showed a higher mortality breast cancer and colorectal–anal cancer, but with 95% credibility intervals that do not exclude the null hypothesis. In addition, we highlighted that for the municipality of Portoscuso, located in the vicinity of an industrial area in south-eastern Sardinia named Portovesme, the results showed a higher mortality only for colorectal-anal cancer in men but with wide statistical uncertainty.

In relation to breast cancer, our analysis confirmed the analysis conducted by Bernardinelli et al, which did not observe excess mortality at the level of health districts but showed how the rates for the entire Sardinian territory had excess mortality compared with Italian rates and made it possible to grasp in which specific municipalities excess mortality was found compared with the regional reference.

In Sardinia, on the other hand, there appears to have been less colorectal and mammography screening coverage. In fact, total colorectal screening coverage in Sardinia was 36% (CI 95% 33.8% to 38.3%) in the period 2012–2015 and 44.1% (41.5% to 46.8%) in the period 2016–2019, while in Italy, the corresponding coverage percentages were 42.4% (41.9% to 42.9%) and 47.4% (46.9% to 47.9%).11

Moreover, the coverage of total mammography screening in Sardinia was 67% (64% to 69.9%) in the period 2012–2015 and 66.7% (62.8%to 70.4%) in the period 2016–2019, where in Italy the corresponding coverage percentages were 71.9% (71.2% to 72.6%) and 74.7% (74.1% to 75.4%).12

Screening coverage data are unfortunately not published at the level of health districts. It was, therefore, not possible to associate the excess mortality calculated in our study with the levels of screening campaigns. We would also point out that, in some territories, screening started 6 years late. In fact, local health authorities started screening programmes for the prevention of breast cancer between 2006 and 2012 and for the prevention of CRC between 2008 and 2014.29 30

According to an ad hoc survey conducted in 2014, a lack of invitations to screening and of adherence to screening recommendations was attributed, at least in part, both to insufficient regional governance in the planning, monitoring and evaluation phase of the screening programmes and to the failure to identify centres of reference for surgical services and to insufficient governance of the LHTs. Within the LHTs, in fact, the use of temporary employee, poor coordination of the professionals and structures involved, and poor involvement of general practitioners have emerged.31 In fact, this situation has led to the adoption of corrective measures to be implemented by 2019 when the Regional Council defined the programme ‘P-1.4, Early identification of screened cancers and systemic care’.31

Conclusions

This study has highlighted critical health issues in Sardinia as a whole and in some municipalities and districts of the region.

It is recommended that the Sardinian regional authorities act on modifiable risk factors, among them screening coverage, in order to reduce excess breast and CRC mortality through early diagnosis.

Finally, it is recommended to continue epidemiological surveillance throughout the region.
