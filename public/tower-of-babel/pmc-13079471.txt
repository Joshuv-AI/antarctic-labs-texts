Effect of head injury on blood transfusion requirements in severe trauma: a post-hoc analysis of the RESTRIC trial
Authors: Hiroki Nagasawa, Kazuhiko Omori, Noriko Tanaka, Chihiro Maekawa, Michika Hamada, Soichirou Ota, Hiromichi Ohsaka, Youichi Yanagawa, Yutaka Kondo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo evaluate whether head-region injury, as defined by Abbreviated Injury Scale (AIS) coding, is associated with transfusion requirements in patients with severe trauma.MethodsThis study is a post-hoc analysis of the RESTRIC trial, a multicentre, cluster-randomised, crossover, non-inferiority trial comparing restrictive (7–9 g/dL) and liberal (10–12 g/dL) blood transfusion strategies for patients with severe haemorrhagic trauma. Adult patients with severe trauma anticipated to require transfusion were included. Head injury was defined as an AIS score ≥ 2 in the head region. The primary outcome was cumulative red blood cell (RBC) transfusion volume within 28 days. Key secondary outcomes included RBC transfusion volume within 24 and 48 h. Propensity score matching (PSM) was used to balance baseline injury severity using Injury Severity Score (ISS) and, separately, AIS scores for body regions excluding the head. Sensitivity analyses were conducted using generalised linear mixed models (GLMM) with random intercepts for study centre.ResultsAmong the 411 patients, 157 (38.2%) had head AIS ≥ 2. In unadjusted analyses, 28-day RBC transfusion volumes were similar between patients with and without head injury (median 10 vs. 10 units). After adjustment using PSM and GLMM, no evidence of an independent association between head-region injury and RBC transfusion volume was observed at 24 h, 48 h, or cumulatively at 28 days (IRR 0.97, 95% CI 0.78–1.21). In contrast, transfusion strategy, abdominal or limb injury, and early interventions were consistently associated with increased transfusion requirements.ConclusionIn this post-hoc analysis of a randomised transfusion trial, we found no evidence of an independent association between head-region injury and transfusion requirements within the limits of statistical power and exposure definition. These findings should be interpreted as an absence of evidence rather than evidence of no effect and should not be extrapolated to isolated intracranial traumatic brain injury. Further studies incorporating precise identification of intracranial injury and coagulation phenotypes are warranted.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00068-026-03141-4.Keywords: Blood transfusion, Head injury, Haemorrhage, Injury

Introduction

Effective massive haemorrhage control and appropriate blood transfusion management are essential components of severe trauma care [1]. Surgical and interventional radiology procedures are essential for controlling active bleeding; however, transfusion requirements are influenced by systemic trauma-associated coagulopathy, which may persist even after haemorrhage control has been achieved [1]. These systemic responses may persist even after haemostasis has been achieved and contribute substantially to transfusion burden and outcomes.

Traumatic brain injury (TBI) has been associated with distinct coagulation abnormalities and poor outcomes in critically injured patients. Experimental and clinical studies have suggested that brain tissue injury and blood–brain barrier disruption may trigger systemic coagulation disturbances that could theoretically exacerbate bleeding from extracranial injuries [2–4]. However, in patients with multiple injuries, it remains unclear whether the presence of head-region injury independently increases transfusion requirements or whether observed differences reflect greater overall injury severity and treatment complexity.

Head injury is common among patients with severe trauma and is frequently accompanied by injuries to other body regions that are direct sources of haemorrhage. Previous studies evaluating transfusion requirements in trauma populations have yielded inconsistent results [5], and few have rigorously adjusted for regional injury patterns or centre-level practice variation. Moreover, many trauma registries rely on AIS coding, which does not reliably distinguish intracranial TBI from extracranial head injuries, limiting mechanistic interpretation [6, 7].

Therefore, we conducted a post-hoc analysis of the Restrictive Transfusion Strategy for Critically Injured Patients (RESTRIC) trial [8] to evaluate whether head-region injury, defined using AIS coding, is independently associated with blood transfusion requirements in patients with severe trauma. We hypothesised that, after adjustment for overall and regional injury severity, head injury would be independently associated with transfusion volume in a mixed trauma cohort. Clarifying this relationship may help contextualise transfusion decision-making in patients with multiple injuries by distinguishing the contribution of head-region injury from that of overall injury burden and active extracranial bleeding.

Methods

Statistical overview

Because transfusion volume is influenced by both injury severity and institutional practice, we used complementary analytical approaches to minimise confounding and improve robustness. Propensity score matching (PSM) was applied to balance baseline injury severity and regional injury patterns between the head injury and no head injury groups. Generalised linear mixed models (GLMM) were then used to estimate associations while accounting for clustering by study centre. Finally, doubly robust estimation combining propensity score adjustment with outcome regression was performed as a sensitivity analysis to assess the consistency of results under different model specifications.

Study design and setting

This study is a post-hoc analysis of the RESTRIC trial, a multicentre, cluster-randomised, crossover, non-inferiority trial involving 22 Japanese emergency and critical care centres, including university-affiliated and tertiary referral hospitals, and 411 patients [8]. The study protocol was registered in the UMIN Clinical Trials Registry (UMIN000034405) in October 2018 and published in July 2020 [9]. The trial included patients with severe trauma who were anticipated to require blood transfusion upon hospital arrival. Participating clinicians managed patients according to a predefined target haemoglobin level based on cluster randomisation: restrictive (7–9 g/dL) and liberal (10–12 g/dL) groups; transfusion practices in the RESTRIC trial were not standardised according to head injury or TBI status, reflecting real-world clinical decision-making. All participating centres adhered to these assigned thresholds during each period, although other aspects of resuscitation (e.g., MTP triggers, availability of viscoelastic testing) followed local practice.

Transfusion volumes were expressed in units based on the Japanese blood product unit system. In Japan, 1 unit of blood contains approximately 200 mL of donated blood, comprising approximately 140, 80, and 20 mL of red blood cells (RBCs), fresh frozen plasma (FFP), and platelet concentrate (PC), respectively.

Transfusion volumes were recorded cumulatively from admission. Patients who died before 28 days were included, and their transfusion totals up to the time of death were used in the 28-day analysis. Thus, no transfusion data were missing due to death.

Ethical approval and consent procedures for the RESTRIC trial and this post-hoc analysis are described in the Statements and Declarations section.

Participants

The RESTRIC trial enrolled patients with trauma aged ≥ 20 years who met at least one of the following criteria: severe bleeding with possible shock, suspected life-threatening bleeding upon arrival at the emergency department, and potential for severe postoperative bleeding in the acute phase. Exclusion criteria included cardiac arrest before or upon arrival at the emergency department, transfer from other institutions, consent withdrawal at initial assessment, severe burns (≥ 15% total body surface area), pregnancy, chronic anaemia, and blood transfusion refusal. This post-hoc analysis followed the intention-to-treat principle of the RESTRIC trial, and no additional patients were excluded beyond those excluded in the original trial. All enrolled patients were included regardless of protocol adherence or survival status, and no exclusions were made based on early mortality.

Exposure definition

Head injury was defined as an AIS score ≥ 2 in the head region. This definition reflects anatomical injury severity but does not distinguish intracranial TBI from extracranial head injuries, such as scalp or skull trauma. This limitation arises because the RESTRIC registry recorded only region-level AIS scores (Head, Head and Neck, Face, Chest, Abdomen, Limbs, and Surface), and full AIS diagnostic codes or neuroimaging data were unavailable. Consequently, the exposure evaluated in this study represents head-region injury rather than CT-confirmed intracranial TBI, and the analyses address transfusion requirements associated with head-region injury per se. The Head and neck AIS variable represents a broader regional category in the registry and was used for descriptive purposes only; it was not used to define exposure.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the total number of RBC units transfused within 28 days. Patients who died before day 28 were not excluded; their transfusion data were included up to the time of death, and mortality was analysed as a secondary outcome. Key secondary outcomes included cumulative RBC transfusion volumes within 24 and 48 h, reflecting the early haemostatic phase of trauma care. Additional secondary outcomes, as confirmatory analyses, included mortality at 28 days and total number of blood product units at 7 days, and 28 days. Tertiary outcomes, as exploratory, included organ failure (respiratory, renal, and hepatic) within 7 days; complications within 28 days (including cerebral infarction, acute myocardial infarction, pulmonary embolism, deep vein thrombosis, bowel ischaemia, and sepsis); and consent withdrawal post-randomisation.

Statistical analyses

Participants were classified into two groups based on the presence or absence of head injury: no head injury and head injury groups. Data are presented as medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs), partly reported effect-size estimates with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), or as counts with percentages, as appropriate. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, and continuous variables were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test, partly with absolute differences as Hodges–Lehmann median differences for skewed transfusion outcomes.

To assess the effect of head injury while adjusting for other injury severities, analyses using Injury Severity Score (ISS) and AIS scores excluding the head were performed. A two-step propensity score matching approach was applied to balance overall injury severity and regional injury patterns that may independently affect transfusion requirements. First, we compared the two groups using PSM, adjusting for age, sex, antithrombotic drug use, prehospital time < 60 min, haemostatic and non-haemostatic interventions within 6 h, randomised group, injury type, and ISS. Second, we performed additional PSM analyses adjusting for age, sex, antithrombotic drug use, prehospital time < 60 min, haemostatic and non-haemostatic interventions within 6 h, randomised group, injury type, and AIS scores for each body region (face, chest, abdomen, limbs, and skin). Variables included in the propensity score model were selected a priori based on clinical relevance and expert consensus, focusing on factors reflecting overall injury severity and major extracranial bleeding sources that could plausibly influence transfusion requirements. For PSM, we used 1:1 nearest-neighbour matching without replacement, applying a calliper width of 0.2 of the standard deviation of the logit of the propensity score. Covariate balance after PSM was assessed using standardised mean differences (SMD), with SMD < 0.1 considered indicative of acceptable balance. SMDs were used for both continuous and categorical variables to assess covariate balance independent of sample size. Group differences in RBC transfusion volume at 28 days were then compared using the Mann–Whitney U test.

Sensitivity analyses

Additionally, we performed a GLMM with a negative binomial distribution and log link as a sensitivity analysis. To account for centre-level clustering, we included a random intercept for site (22 institutions). The outcome of interest was set on a 28-day RBC transfusion. Fixed effects were age, sex, antithrombotic drug use, prehospital time < 60 min, haemostatic and non-haemostatic interventions within 6 h, randomised group, injury type, and AIS scores ≥ 2 for each body region (head, face, chest, abdomen, limbs, and skin).

To address survivorship bias, we conducted sensitivity analyses, truncating transfusion volumes at 24 and 48 h (haemostatic phase). These outcomes were analysed using GLMM with a negative binomial distribution, log link, and random intercept for site, while adjusting for the same covariates as in the above analysis.

Because transfusion volume data contained multiple zeros, we performed a two-part sensitivity analysis to approximate a hurdle model. First, we modelled the probability of receiving any transfusion (binary outcome) using a mixed-effects logistic regression with a random site intercept. Second, we modelled transfusion volume among transfused patients only using a mixed-effects negative binomial regression with a random site intercept. Two-part models were implemented in SPSS by running a mixed-effects logistic regression (probability of any transfusion) and a mixed-effects negative binomial regression (transfusion volume among transfused patients). Platelet transfusion volume was analysed using a GLMM (negative binomial distribution, log link) with site-specific random intercepts. Given the limited number of patients who received platelet transfusions (n = 122, 29.7%), the results were considered exploratory.

Additional sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess the robustness of the primary findings and were not intended to provide independent effect estimates. As part of these robustness checks, we examined potential effect modification by transfusion strategy by including an interaction term between head injury and randomised strategy (liberal vs. restrictive) in the GLMM as another sensitivity analysis.

Missing data were handled by complete-case analysis, as missingness was < 5% for all variables except for the time from injury to hospital arrival (9.2%).

Given this low proportion, no imputation was performed. All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.4.3 (The R Project for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) and SPSS version 29.0 (IBM Corp., New York, USA). Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05 or at the 95% CI.

Results

A total of 411 patients were included in the present study; all deaths during follow-up were included in the analysis of transfusion outcomes. The cohort was divided into 2 groups: patients without head injury [no head injury group; n = 254 (61.8%)] and those with head injury [head injury group; n = 157 (38.2%)]. The baseline characteristics are presented in Table 1 and Online resource 1. Patients with head injury had higher overall injury severity (median ISS 29 vs. 19) and higher face and chest AIS scores, whereas abdominal injury severity and rates of haemostatic intervention were lower. Other baseline characteristics were similar between the groups.

In the PSM cohort balanced for AIS scores excluding the head region (110 matched patients in each group), all AIS components were well balanced. RBC transfusion at 28 days remained non-significant in these adjusted cohorts (9 [4–18] vs. 10 [4–16] units; P = 0.318) [Fig. 1].

Sensitivity analyses

Across all sensitivity analyses, including mixed-effects models, truncated time windows, and two-part models, head-region injury was not independently associated with transfusion requirements. In the GLMM, head injury was not significantly associated with transfusion requirements (IRR 0.983, 95% CI 0.790–1.224). In contrast, the randomised restrictive transfusion strategy, abdominal or limbs injury, and early haemostatic or non-haemostatic intervention remained consistently associated with transfusion volume [Online Resource 4]. In the GLMM negative binomial models with random intercept for site, head injury was again not associated with transfusion requirements within 24 h (IRR 1.084, 95% CI 0.850–1.382) or 48 h (IRR 1.028, 95% CI 0.820–1.290) [Online Resources 5 and 6]. In a two-part analysis, AIS of head ≥ 2 was not significantly associated with the probability of receiving any RBC transfusion (OR 1.202, 95% CI 0.512–2.820) or transfusion volume among those transfused (IRR 0.984, 95% CI 0.819–1.181) [Online Resources 7 and 8].

Analyses of platelet transfusion volumes yielded similarly null associations but were limited by wide confidence intervals and are therefore presented in the supplementary material only [Online Resources 9 and 10].

Sensitivity analyses: interaction analysis

We next determined whether the effect of head injury on transfusion volume differed by transfusion strategy. In the GLMM including an interaction term between head injury and transfusion strategy, the interaction was not statistically significant (interaction IRR = 0.964 [95% CI 0.644–1.442], p = 0.858). Stratified analyses showed no meaningful difference between strategies: the IRR for head injury was 0.730 (95% CI 0.570–0.935) in the restrictive group and 1.369 (95% CI 1.069–1.753) in the liberal group.

Discussion

In this post-hoc analysis of a multicentre randomised transfusion trial, we found no evidence that head-region injury defined by AIS coding was independently associated with increased blood transfusion requirements in patients with severe trauma, suggesting that head-region injury alone was not a dominant determinant of transfusion demand within this dataset. This finding was consistent across multiple analytical approaches, including propensity score matching and mixed-effects regression models, and across both early and late transfusion time windows.

Transfusion requirements in severe trauma are determined by multiple interacting factors, including the extent of active bleeding, physiological derangement, and institutional resuscitation practices [1, 10, 11]. Beyond anatomical injury severity, clinical decision-making—such as the early implementation of haemostatic interventions, goal-directed coagulation management, and adjunctive pharmacologic therapies—can substantially alter transfusion volumes independent of the primary bleeding source [8, 12–14]. System-level elements, including trauma system organisation and blood management protocols, further contribute to variability across centres [15, 16]. In this context, our findings indicate that head injury was not identified as an independent dominant of transfusion demand within this mixed trauma cohort, rather than implying an absence of clinical relevance.

Moderate or severe TBI has been associated with trauma-associated coagulopathy [2–4], and the likelihood of coagulopathy increases with higher head AIS scores [3, 17]. On this basis, we prespecified the hypothesis that head-region injury might be associated with higher transfusion volumes in severely injured patients. However, this hypothesis was not supported by our results. After adjustment for overall injury severity and extracranial injury distribution, head injury was not independently associated with transfusion volume, suggesting that extracranial bleeding sources and overall injury burden are more strongly associated with transfusion needs in this cohort. Although cumulative 28-day transfusion volume captures overall transfusion burden, it may be influenced by downstream clinical factors such as prolonged critical care or early mortality. Importantly, the absence of evidence for an association was consistent when early transfusion volumes at 24 and 48 h—more reflective of acute haemorrhage and early resuscitation—were examined.

Although experimental and clinical studies have proposed that the pathophysiology of coagulopathy in patients with TBI may differ from that of trauma-induced coagulopathy [1, 4, 18], the present analysis cannot evaluate these mechanisms. TBI could not be directly identified in the RESTRIC dataset, and no coagulation phenotyping was performed. Accordingly, any mechanistic interpretation regarding TBI-related coagulopathy remains speculative and outside the scope of this study. Our findings should therefore be interpreted as reflecting transfusion demand associated with head-region injury rather than confirmed intracranial TBI.

Our study addressed a different question from prior transfusion research in TBI. Randomised threshold trials, such as HEMOTION [19], evaluated whether liberal versus restrictive haemoglobin triggers influence neurological outcomes, whereas the present analysis examined whether the presence of head injury itself was associated with transfusion requirements in a mixed trauma cohort. Observational studies of isolated TBI [16, 20] have focused on coagulopathy and outcome associations rather than transfusion demand. A recent meta-analysis of randomised trials reported no survival benefit of liberal transfusion strategies in patients with TBI and identified increased transfusion-related complications, concluding that evidence remains insufficient to recommend a specific transfusion strategy [21]. Our findings neither contradict nor support these recommendations, as they address a distinct outcome—transfusion demand rather than transfusion thresholds or neurological outcomes.

From a clinical perspective, bleeding from head-region injuries often contributes less to overall blood loss than haemorrhage from abdominal or extremity injuries, which may partly contextualise the observed findings without implying a causal explanation. Overall, within the limits of statistical power, exposure definition, and available data, we found no evidence that head-region injury defined by AIS coding was independently associated with increased transfusion requirements. In this cohort, transfusion requirements appeared to be more strongly associated with overall injury burden, haemodynamic instability, and evidence of active bleeding than with head-region injury status alone.

This study has some limitations. First, as this post-hoc analysis was not powered to detect differences in transfusion volume, the possibility of a type II error cannot be excluded. Therefore, given the limited sample size and heterogeneity in transfusion and haemostatic practices, these findings should be interpreted with caution and viewed as hypothesis-generating. Second, owing to the lack of specific diagnostic codes, we could not distinguish between extracranial and intracranial head injuries. Although restricting the exposure definition to head AIS ≥ 2 improved specificity for clinically relevant head-region injury, extracranial head injuries may still have been included. This exposure misclassification may have attenuated potential associations between true intracranial TBI and transfusion requirements, precluded mechanistic interpretation, and limited extrapolation to isolated moderate–severe TBI. Third, unmeasured confounders, including prehospital interventions, catecholamine use, or viscoelastic testing, could not be evaluated. Fourth, transfusion volume is a time-dependent outcome that may be influenced by early mortality; although sensitivity analyses using truncated time windows were performed, residual survival bias cannot be completely excluded. Fifth, propensity score matching may introduce selection effects by restricting analyses to matched subsets of patients, potentially limiting generalisability. Sixth, transfusion practices in trauma care are rapidly evolving; therefore, data collected during the RESTRIC trial period may not fully reflect current transfusion strategies, further limiting generalisability to contemporary practice. Finally, this study was conducted within the Japanese trauma care system, and transfusion practices may differ in other settings.

Conclusion

In this post-hoc analysis of the RESTRIC trial, we found no evidence that head-region injury was associated with increased transfusion requirements within the limits of statistical power and measurement precision. As this analysis was not powered to detect modest differences in transfusion volume, the findings should be interpreted as absence of evidence rather than evidence of no association. Within the limits of exposure definition and available data, transfusion requirements in this cohort were more closely associated with overall injury burden and haemostatic management than with head-region injury status as defined by AIS coding. Because detailed diagnostic information for intracranial TBI was unavailable, no mechanistic conclusions regarding TBI-related coagulopathy can be drawn. Further studies with larger cohorts, precise characterisation of intracranial injury, and direct assessment of coagulation are warranted.
