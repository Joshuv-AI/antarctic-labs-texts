Exploring diversity and distribution patterns of chicken gut bacteriophage community
Authors: Wenbo Chen, Yu Zhang, Huanyu Gong, Ze Cao, Kairi Yang, Jiandui Mi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Chickens harbor a diverse gut virome, with bacteriophages playing a central role in shaping microbial communities, modulating gut microbiota composition, and influencing metabolic pathways. Despite significant recent advances in gut virome studies, comprehensive analyses of the bacteriophage component within the chicken gut viral genomes remain limited. A comprehensive chicken virome database (CVD) was developed by integrating diverse datasets from various continents, containing 32,792 viral contig sequences with an estimated completeness of over 50% and representing 17,268 species-level medium-quality operational taxonomic units (vOTUs) genomes inhabiting the chicken gut. Comparative analyses revealed minimal overlap between CVD and other virome databases, underscoring its distinctiveness and highlighting the need for high-quality, host-specific viral catalogs in studying chicken gut microbiota. Metagenomic analyses revealed the functional attributes of these prokaryotic viruses, including their potential roles in modulating gut microbiota composition, metabolic pathways, and antibiotic resistance. Classification results showed that the majority of known virus vOTUs (80.2%) were identified as Caudoviricetes (74.8%) and Malgrandaviricetes (3.5%) at the class level. Caudoviricetes, the most dominant phage class in all samples, varied in the relative abundance. At the genus level, Lactobacillus was considered the most common host, aligning with the bacterial composition of the chicken gut. Correlation analysis between the composition of gut bacteriome and the phage component of the virome indicated a significant relationship. Additionally, these bacteriophages carry a variety of potential auxiliary metabolic genes (AMGs) and a few potential antibiotic resistance genes (ARGs), but the relationship between potential ARGs and viral communities was not significant. This study provides a foundational resource for understanding the chicken gut bacteriophage community across regions, while recognizing current geographic limitations. Our findings contribute to the growing knowledge of host- bacteriophage interactions and offer a basis for exploring how bacteriophage communities may influence poultry health and management.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s42523-025-00484-y.

Introduction

In recent years, there has been a growing interest among scientists in studying the microbiome, which refers to the diverse community of microorganisms living within a specific environment or organism [1]. The gut microbiome has attracted significant attention due to its crucial role in various physiological processes, including nutrient absorption, enzyme synthesis, and short-chain fatty acid production [2]. While bacteria have traditionally been the focus of gut microbiome research, it is now recognized that viruses also play a significant role in shaping gut ecology and host health [3].

The gut virome, consisting of viruses that co-colonize the gastrointestinal tract (GIT) alongside bacteria, fungi, archaea, and helminths, is a pivotal component of the gut microbiome. In addition to bacteria, a substantial quantity of viruses is present, outnumbering bacteria at a ratio of 1:1 to 10:1 [4]. Bacteriophages, which dominate the virome, are particularly important for regulating bacterial populations, driving horizontal gene transfer, and influencing microbial metabolic pathways. Although some disease-associated viruses, such as Herpesviruses, Polyomaviruses, Adenoviruses, Papillomaviruses, Hepatitis B Virus, Hepatitis C Virus and Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV), are present in the intestinal viromes of certain hosts, this study specifically focuses on understanding the bacteriophage community within the chicken gut [5, 6].

Within the context of the gut virome, chickens serve as a valuable model for studying viral diversity and function. As a cornerstone of global food production, chickens are influenced by their gut microbiota, which in turn affects their health, productivity, and the safety of chicken-derived products [7, 8]. Moreover, chickens can serve as reservoir hosts for viruses with zoonotic potential, highlighting the importance of monitoring and understanding the chicken gut virome [9]. Despite its significance, our understanding of the chicken gut virome, particularly its bacteriophage component, remains limited by methodological challenges and the complexity of viral communities [10, 11].

Recent advancements in metagenomic sequencing and bioinformatics have transformed our ability to identify diverse viruses and generate numerous viral genome catalogs and databases for several ecosystems, such as ocean viruses [12, 13], soil, the human [14] and pig gut [15], and the rumen ecosystem [16]. For chickens, an integrated catalog of microbiota comprising bacteria, archaea, and viruses has been constructed. However, the number of viral genomes with over 50% completeness is only approximately 3,500, which is about 10% of total [17]. Leveraging these tools, researchers have embarked on a global effort to comprehensively characterize the gut virome, aiming to unravel its diversity, composition, and potential roles in host health and disease susceptibility [7, 8, 18].

Hence, this study aimed to understand the bacteriophage community structure, correlation with their host, their functional potential, and the status of antibiotic resistance in the bacteriophage component of chicken. Since these environments are the least explored in terms of metaviromics, our study presents an effort toward the ongoing venture of understanding the bacteriophage community. By integrating diverse datasets from various geographic locations, chicken species, and production systems, we developed a comprehensive chicken virome database with 32,792 viral contig sequences ( > 50% complete), representing 17,268 species-level vOTUs in the chicken gut. Compared to previous studies, which often relied on limited datasets or regional sampling, our study provides a broader and more representative catalog of the chicken gut bacteriophage community. Understanding the bacteriophage diversity in the chicken gut not only enhances our knowledge of enteric disease syndromes but also contributes to improving the feed conversion efficiency in chicken species. Moreover, insights into the chicken gut bacteriophage community have implications for chicken health, food safety, and public health by revealing potential links to pathogen dynamics and the transmission of antibiotic resistance. This underscores the importance of ongoing research in this field.

Materials and methods

Data collection, assembly, and identification of viral contigs from chicken gastrointestinal tract metagenomes

To perform a comprehensive search for prokaryotic viruses of the chicken GIT, published metagenomics data generated via next generation sequencing were downloaded from the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI), covering 1,057 chickens. In total, 17 NCBI BioProjects were included, covering poultry husbandry regimes from 6 countries across 3 continents, including PRJNA417359 [19], PRJEB33338 [20], PRJEB22062 [21], PRJNA408020 [22], PRJNA347599 [23], PRJNA543206 [24], PRJNA193217 [25], PRJNA291299, PRJNA375762, PRJNA415593, PRJNA293646, PRJNA338370, PRJNA340908, PRJNA383926, PRJNA586747, PRJNA704780, PRJNA598014 [26]. To ensure data reliability and consistency, we applied a systematic dataset selection procedure. Specifically, only datasets with clear descriptions of host species, sampling sites, and sequencing platforms were included, and those with a total size smaller than 500 MB were excluded to avoid potential biases associated with low sequencing depth and limited coverage. Viral sequences were identified by “metav”, a pipeline described in our previous study [15]. Briefly, the metagenomic data were subjected to quality control with fastp v0.23.2 [27], and host contamination was removed with BWA v0.7.17-r1188 [28], then the remaining data were assembled individually using Megahit (v1.2.9) with k list 39, 59, and 79 [29]. After assembly, viral contigs > 1.5 kb were identified with VirSorter2 v2.2.3 [30], which was run with the –include-groups parameter set to dsDNAphage,NCLDV,ssDNA,lavidaviridae to enrich for bacteriophages and other prokaryotic viruses. This was performed following the viral sequence identification SOP with keeping categories Keep1 and Keep2 as putative viral contigs (http://dx.doi.org/10.17504/protocols.io.bwm5pc86) and DeepVirFinder v1.0 [31] with parameter of –dvf-score 0.95. Then all individual viral contigs of single samples were combined for the next analysis procedures.

Viral contigs cluster, quality control and database comparison

The viral contigs were then clustered into “virus operational taxonomic units (vOTUs)” using a supporting code from the CheckV repository using the MIUVIG recommended parameters (95% ANI + 85% AF) [32]. The quality of viral contigs and vOTUs were assessed with CheckV (v.1.0.1) (database v.1.4) [33]. Only the viral contigs and vOTUs with Complete, High-quality ( > 90% completeness), and Medium-quality (50–90% completeness) were retained for further analysis.

To ensure consistency with quality standards, we used CheckV on the CMKMC (Chicken Gut Multi‑Kingdom Microbiome Catalog) [17], RVD (Rumen Virome Database) [16], PVD (Pig Virome Database) [15], and URPC (Unified Ruminant Phage Catalogue) [34] datasets to filter low-quality sequences, retaining vOTU genome fragments with > 50% completeness. The viral contigs were clustered into species-level vOTUs using the MIUVIG thresholds (95% ANI + 85% AF). These vOTUs were then merged with those from the CVD database and further grouped using a threshold (90% query coverage, 70% identity) to reduce redundancy.

Taxonomic classification and host and lifestyle prediction of vOTUs

First, all identified candidate viral genomes above medium quality were predicted taxonomy with the software tool geNomad (v.1.2.0) [35]. The integrated machine learning framework iPHoP (v1.3.3) [36] incorporated with 6 host prediction approaches, including Blast, CRISPR, VirHostMatcher [37, 38], WIsH [39], PHP [40], and RaFAH [41] was used to predict the probable hosts of the vOTUs of above medium-quality. Host genomes were sourced from GTDB r214 [42] and IMG-published genomes as of August 2023 [43]. BACPHLIP (v.0.9.6) [44] and PhaTYP [45] were used to predict the lifestyles of the chicken viruses with above medium-quality.

AMGs annotation and identification of ARG and MGE carried by vOTUs of the above medium quality

vOTUs of above medium quality were processed with VirSorter2 with the parameter –prep-for-dramv. Then the vOTUs and table files were used to identify the auxiliary metabolic genes (AMGs) using DRAMv with default parameters [46]. The encoded proteins were predicted using prodigal-gv with -p meta model (https://github.com/apcamargo/prodigal-gv.). We downloaded the SARG (v3.2.1-S) 47 and searched it for potential antibiotic resistance genes (ARGs) carried by the vOTUs of above medium quality using BLASTp model of DIAMOD (v2.0.15.153) [47] with a threshold of 80% identify [48] and 85% alignment coverage. The mobile genetic elements (MGEs) database was downloaded from https://github.com/KatariinaParnanen/MobileGeneticElementDatabase [49]. The MGEs were also identified using BLASTp with DIAMOND with the same parameters used for potential ARGs identification.

Virome and bacteria composition and abundance across the metagenomic samples

The coverage of vOTUs of above medium quality in the 1,057 chicken metagenomes were calculated by mapping the metagenomic sequence reads to vOTUs using CoverM of the contig model with default parameters (v0.6.1) (https://github.com/wwood/CoverM), which utilizes minimap2 as the underlying aligner [50]. vOTUs were considered present if ≥75% of their length was covered at ≥90% identity. Abundance was calculated using TPM normalization. The bacterial community in different taxonomic levels of metagenomic samples were quantified with Kraken2 (v2.1.2) [51] with the standard Kraken2 database. The classification results of Kraken2 were summarized in relative abundance by Bracken (v2.9) [52].

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses and data visualizations were conducted using R (v4.4.1) and GraphPad Prism (v10.2.3). Geographical locations of the samples were visualized using “ggplot2”, “ggrepel”, and “maps” packages. Treemap was performed using “igraph”, “tidyverse”, “viridis”, “data.tree”, “phyloseq”, and “ggtree” packages to visualize the annotated taxonomy and relative abundance, in which the size of the bubbles indicates the relative abundance of the raw read counts [53]. Principal coordinate analysis (PCoA), Adonis test and Procrustes analysis were performed using the vegan package (v2.6–6.1) [54]. Alpha diversity metrics, including Shannon index, Observed Richness, Chao1, Pielou’s Evenness and the Inverse Simpson index, were calculated based on contig-level (vOTU) abundances. Differences between virome and bacteriome were analyzed using linear regression. Procrustes analysis evaluated the correlation between virome and predicted bacterial host compositions using Hellinger-transformed genus-level abundance matrices and Bray-Curtis distances [55]. PCoA ordinations were aligned, and symmetric correlation coefficients with P-values (999 permutations) were computed using the “vegan” package. P-values were adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction. Significant bacterium-virus correlations (Spearman’s ⍴ ≥ 0.90 or ≤ −0.90, FDR < 0.05) were used to construct networks, visualized via bar plots and network diagrams. To model virus composition, we used PCoA1 as the response variable. Data were Yeo-Johnson transformed to address skewness. Linear mixed-effects models (“lme4”) [56] analyzed fixed effects (source material, breeding program, continent), with study identity as a random effect.

Results

The bacteriophage community from the chicken gut microbiome

We analyzed 1,057 metagenomic samples from 17 previously published NCBI BioProjects, covering various regions worldwide (Asia, Europe, and North America) (Fig. 1a). A total of 32,792 unique bacteriophage genomes were identified. Among them, 17,268 vOTUs were at least 50% completeness (Supplementary Table 1), including 3,687 fully complete vOTUs (Figs. 1b and 1c). Furthermore, most vOTUs with completeness exceeding 50% were larger than 10 kb (Fig. S1), meeting the criteria for medium or high sequencing quality, indicative of their potential to provide more reliable results [57]. Comparative analysis revealed that the CVD dataset is highly distinct. Of 17,232 unique species-level vOTUs, 16,963 (98.44%) did not overlap with vOTUs from other datasets (Fig. 2). Among the remaining 269 vOTUs, only 171 (approximately 1%) overlapped with sequences from the CMKMC database, another chicken-specific dataset. This highlights a low degree of overlap even with previous dataset derived from the chicken gut, underscoring the minimal overlap even within datasets derived from similar host systems. This low overlap highlights the distinctiveness of the CVD dataset, particularly when compared to datasets from other animal microbiomes, including RVD, PVD, and URPC. In total, the CMKMC, RVD, PVD, and URPC datasets contained 3,498 (94.75%), 33,796 (81.07%), 48,155 (99.71%), and 56,562 (87.72%) unique sequences, respectively. Additionally, we analyzed vOTUs exceeding 200 kilobases (kb) in length (Fig. S2) with > 50% completeness across these datasets and a Jumbo Bacteriophage Database (Jumbo DB) [58]. Notably, no overlap was observed between the CVD dataset and others. These findings underscore the importance of comprehensive, high-quality databases like CVD in advancing viral diversity analyses and studies of virus-host interactions in chicken gut microbiota. Taxonomic annotation

After classification, the majority of known vOTUs (80.2%) were identified as Caudoviricetes (74.8%) and Malgrandaviricetes (3.5%) at the class level (Fig. 3a). Caudoviricetes was the most prominent class of bacteriophages across all samples with differing relative abundance (Fig. S3). This dominance is likely influenced by the use of bulk metagenomic sequencing, which predominantly recovers double-stranded DNA (dsDNA) sequences of bacterial origin. As such, the bacteriophage composition largely reflects bacterial dsDNA viruses, especially prophages. However, only 6.9% of medium-quality vOTUs could be classified at the family level. Among these, Microviridae was the most frequently identified at the family level (3.38%), belonging to Malgrandaviricetes at the class level (Fig. 3b, c). Host and lifestyle prediction

Identifying the hosts of viruses is crucial for understanding their impact on the host dynamics and consequently, on the host community diversity and function [59]. We predicted hosts taxonomy of 17,268 vOTUs using metagenome-assembled genomes (MAGs) from public datasets with iPHoP. To reduce bias and improve ecological relevance, we prioritized MAGs derived from chicken gut environments. Only host predictions with high confidence scores (≥90%) were retained for downstream analysis. The host prediction results indicated that bacteria were the most common hosts for the bacteriophages, accounting for 62% (n = 10,705) of the total vOTUs, and archaea were also hosts, although accounting for less than 1% (n = 38) of the total. The remaining 37.8% (n = 6,525) of vOTUs could not be confidently assigned to any host (Fig. 4a). Among all the predicted virus-microbial host relationships, Firmicutes_A (n = 3,377), Firmicutes (n = 3,208) and Bacteroidetes (n = 1,723), the most abundant bacterial phyla in the gut microbiome, were found to be the common hosts for viruses at the phylum level (Fig. 4a, b). These groups include bacteria that are dominant in the GIT [60]. The ratio of virulent and temperate bacteriophages varied among the different hosts, with most phages predicted to be virulent (Temperate 7,718; Virulent 9,550). Among the predicted bacteriophage–host associations, 17,062 vOTUs were linked to bacterial genera that were also targeted by other vOTUs (generalist genera), whereas only 206 vOTUs were associated with genera that had no additional viral associations (specialist genera). This trend suggests that most predicted host genera are commonly infected by multiple phages, reflecting a high degree of interaction overlap and redundancy in the gut phage–bacteria network. Specifically, at the genus level, Lactobacillus (belonging to Firmicutes) were the most assigned hosts, followed by Bacteroides (belonging to Bacteroidetes) (Fig. 4c). Close interactions between the gut bacteriophage community and Bacteriome

Research has indicated that the gut microbiome is associated with the richness and diversity of bacteriophages in the human gut [61–63], but the relationships between the bacteriophage community and bacteriome in the chicken gut remains unclear. To address this, we aimed to associate each vOTU with bacteria in the samples. Our findings revealed that the most common bacterial phylum was Firmicutes, followed by Proteobacteria, Bacteroidota and Actinobacteriota. The majority of chicken gut bacteria were from the Lactobacillaceae family, predominantly including Lactobacillus crispatus, Ligilactobacillus salivarius and Limosilactobacillus reuteri (Fig. 5a). Additionally, over 10% of the bacteria sequences, in terms of relative abundance, were assigned to Escherichia coli. Our analysis of 1,057 individual samples revealed distinct correlations between the α-diversity of the gut bacteriophage community and bacteriome at the species level. We observed a significant positive correlation in community evenness (Pielou’s index; linear regression, adjusted R2 = 0.23, p = 2.01e-62; Fig. 5b), indicating that samples with more evenly distributed bacterial communities also harbored more evenly distributed bacteriophage communities. Similarly, diversity indices (Fig. S4) showed moderate but significant correlations (Shannon index: adjusted R2 = 0.19, p = 2.34e-49; Inverse Simpson index: adjusted R2 = 0.15, p = 8.21e-40). In contrast, species richness metrics demonstrated fundamentally different patterns: observed richness showed no linear relationship (adjusted R2 = −0.001, p = 0.986), while Chao1 estimated richness exhibited a significant but negligible correlation (adjusted R2 = 0.004, p = 0.025). These results suggest that while the structural distribution and diversity of chicken gut bacterial and viral communities are closely linked, the absolute number of species in each community may vary independently.

To further investigate specific bacteriophage-bacteria associations, we compared the relative abundance of each vOTU and gut bacteria at genus levels. For these comparisons, we utilized 1,777 genera from the 1,057 sequenced samples. Based on Procrustes analysis we compared the overall distribution of the gut bacteriophage community and bacteriome based on Bray–Curtis distances and found a significant correlation with coefficient r = 0.86 (p = 0.001) for chicken metagenomes at the genus level (Fig. 5c). Regarding correlations between the virome and bacteria at the genus level, those with a correlation greater than |0.90| (Fig. 5d), the resulting network from metagenomes included 178 nodes (17 bacterial genera and 161 vOTUs) and 249 edges, all showing significant positive correlations (Spearman correlation, FDR < 0.05) between the gut bacteriophage community and bacteriome. Notably, 9 viral genera showed significant positive correlations in relative abundance with the gut bacteria (Fig. 5d). These findings underscore the intimate interconnections and frequent co-occurrences among specific viral and bacterial taxa in chicken gut.

Functional capacity of the gut bacteriophage community

Bacteriophages can encode potential AMGs to modulate the metabolisms of the host [64]. In order to gain a better understanding of the bacteriophage AMGs host metabolism, we utilized predictive tool DRAM-v to identify bacteriophage carrying AMGs. A total of 21,178 genes were identified as potential AMGs, with the “M” flag (corresponding to metabolic function) being the most prevalent (Fig. 6a). These genes span a broad spectrum of microbial metabolic activities, encompassing categories such as carbon utilization, transporters, miscellaneous (MISC), organic nitrogen utilization, and energy metabolism. Among these, potential AMGs involved in information system (n = 4,166), amino acid (n = 2,760), central carbon (n = 1,657), peptidase (n = 1,272) and carbohydrate-active enzymes (CAZY, n = 510) were highly enriched. These findings suggest that bacteriophages may carry a diverse array of metabolic genes, which could potentially influence host metabolic pathways. An analysis of 17,268 viral genomes revealed a limited presence of putative ARGs, with only 48 predicted ARG hits and their corresponding vOTUs (Fig. 6b). Among the identified ARGs, most frequently observed was Escherichia coli mdfA (n = 9), followed by tet(Q) (n = 2), Other major facilitator superfamily transporters (n = 2), mph (B) (n = 2), macA (n = 2), and FosB (n = 2). ARGs are frequently transferred among bacteria via MGEs [65–67]. A total of 4 MGE types and 14 subtypes were detected (Fig. 6c). The prevalence of specific MGEs involved in potential ARG dissemination includes tnpA (n = 32), IS91 (n = 7), p0111 (n = 4), int2 (n = 4), and Tn916-orf19 (n = 3). To further assess the potential for transduction, we distinguished the lifestyles of these ARG-carrying vOTUs. The majority were predicted to be virulent (0.1%), while 0.5% were temperate. Similar to ARG-carriers, most MGE-carrying vOTUs were also temperate (Fig. S5).

Analyzing influencing factors on virus composition

We further traced the source of each sample in our study. Information on the continent was available for all genomes. We also collected other influence factors for all samples (Fig. S6). However, most information was unrecorded. Given that the bacteriophage community is highly personalized [68, 69], we then visualized bacteriophage composition for each sample to clearly show the difference. At the class level, the Caudoviricetes class (tailed bacteriophages) was the most abundant across all samples(Fig. S3a), same as the observations in the human gut [70]. The high proportion of the Caudoviricetes could reduce the observed differences in abundance distributions. In detail, some virus families, such as Adenoviridae, Microviridae, Autographiviridae, and Schitoviridae, appeared in all regions but with different relative abundances (Fig. S3b). In general, samples from Asia exhibited the lowest diversity, with the “Unknown” family dominating nearly all relative abundance. In both Asia and North America, the predominant viral contigs could be classified under the family Microviridae. In Asia, some samples showed predominant viral contigs classified as Autographiviridae. In North America, some samples showed predominant viral contigs classified as Schitoviridae. In Europe, the most frequently found families were Drexlerviridae, Autographiviridae, and Rountreeviridae. For source material (Fig. S3c), in cecal contents, Rountreeviridae, Schitoviridae and Microviridae were frequently classified. In chicken litter, Rountreeviridae and Herelleviridae were frequently classified. In cloacal contents, Drexlerviridae were predominant. In ileum contents, most were unclassified. Feces were very complex. We also explored the geographic difference and source material statistically (Fig. S7) and found that the differences were significant, but fit was not good (R2 = 0.085 and R2 = 0.019). Further studies need to explore the complicated influencing factors on bacteriophage composition.

Discussion

The composition of the gut bacteriophage community

Our study provides a comprehensive analysis of the vOTUs in the chicken gut microbiome, highlighting the significant interactions between bacteriophages and their bacterial hosts. Although the geographic coverage of our dataset is not exhaustive, we prioritized data quality by selecting only high-quality, publicly available metagenomic datasets. This approach ensured consistency and robustness in our analyses, albeit at the cost of broader geographic representation. Caudoviricetes emerged as the predominant class identified. Caudoviricetes comprises double-stranded DNA (dsDNA) viruses that infect a wide range of bacterial hosts. Members of Caudoviricetes are ubiquitous across diverse environments, including soil [71], water [72, 73], and within the gut microbiomes of animals [74] and humans [47, 75]. These viruses are known for their ability to infect a broad spectrum of bacterial hosts, including those from the phyla Proteobacteria and Bacteroidetes [73], which are dominant and integral components of gut bacterial communities. Within the identified viral families, Microviridae was the most represented, belonging to the class Malgrandaviricetes. Microviridae consists of small circular single-stranded DNA viruses that are prevalent and diverse across various ecosystems [76]. Their prevalence in the chicken gut virome suggests a potential role in shaping gut microbiota; however, further studies are needed to directly assess their impact on nutrient metabolism, pathogen control, and overall gut health. A previous study revealed that microviruses were found to infect hosts spanning 17 bacterial phyla, predominantly including phyla known to be susceptible to Microviridae (e.g., Proteobacteria and Bacteroidetes) [77]. Therefore, the prevalence of Caudoviricetes and Microviridae in the chicken gut virome underscores their significant roles in modulating microbial communities, as these viruses are predicted to infect the major representatives of the bacterial microbiota.

The chicken ecosystem harbors a core gut microbiome comprising predominant genera of bacteria (e.g., Lactobacillus and Bacteroides), as well as archaea (e.g., Methanobrevibacter), which play a crucial role in essential gut functions. To explore the presence of a bacteriophage community, we analyzed bacteriophage profiles across multiple chicken gut microbiomes. While certain viral families such as Caudoviricetes and Microviridae were frequently observed across individuals, we did not identify a universal bacteriophage community at finer taxonomic levels. Instead, our findings revealed that chicken gut bacteriophage community is highly individualized (Fig. S3). Previous studies have consistently demonstrated significant temporal stability and individual uniqueness in viral compositions among human individuals [69, 78, 79] and in ruminant animals [16]. Additionally, individual viral compositions are primarily dominated by a small number of viral contigs [68].

The gut bacteriophage community is influenced by a complex interplay of factors. Gut bacteriophage datasets demonstrate high specificity, underscoring the host specificity of bacteriophage communities. Notably, even when compared to another chicken gut dataset, the CVD exhibits minimal overlap, highlighting its distinctiveness as well as the complexity and potential regional diversity of the chicken gut virome. However, tracking the origin studies within the CVD reveals that geographic differences and source materials alone are insufficient to explain this distinctiveness. Further research is necessary to uncover the intricate factors shaping viral composition. These differences may be influenced by ecological environments, farming practices (such as housing conditions, hygiene standards, and biosecurity measures), antibiotic usage, regulatory frameworks, and other factors. Further research can help uncover the specific reasons for these variations. Additionally, our results revealed that the features detected alone are insufficient to explain the variability in the virus. This variability may also be partly impacted by diverse genetic backgrounds, physiological differences, species and breed variations, gender disparities, age ranges, and specific geographical locations. Further feature exploration is needed to understand these complexities.

Bacteriophage–bacterial interactions drive dynamics in chicken gut microbiota

We found that Firmicutes, Proteobacteria and Bacteroidetes were the most abundant bacterial phyla within the chicken gut, consistent with the predicted hosts for vOTUs. These groups are well-known as major constituents of the gut microbiome across various hosts, including chicken [80]. They play crucial roles in digestion, metabolism, and immune function, influencing overall gut health and host physiology [81]. At the genus level, Lactobacillus (belonging to Firmicutes) was the most frequently observed bacteria. Lactobacillus is renowned in the GIT for its role as a major producer of short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), which serve as energy sources. Moreover, it generates bacteriocins with bacteriostatic or bactericidal properties, either by pH reduction or by modifying receptors against pathogenic microbes [82, 83]. This was followed by Bacteroides (belonging to Bacteroidetes) (Fig. 3c), which secrete cellulases and hemicellulases to degrade cellulose and hemicellulose into glucose and other sugars. With a substantial number of genes dedicated to polysaccharide and monosaccharide metabolism, Bacteroides play a pivotal role as complex carbohydrate degraders in the host gut [84]. These findings highlight the potential regulatory role of bacteriophages in microbial structures and functions within the chicken GIT, though the specific mechanisms remain to be elucidated.

The presence of a higher number of virulent bacteriophages in the chicken gut microbiome suggests an active dynamic of bacterial lysis and turnover [85]. This dynamic likely influences the stability and composition of the bacterial community, thereby contributing to the maintenance of gut health. Our findings also indicate that generalist bacteriophage–host associations dominate the viral ecology of the chicken gut, with most bacterial genera targeted by multiple vOTUs. This aligns with previous studies using bipartite networks, which found that multiple bacteriophages often infect the same bacterial genera [86, 87]. These generalist interactions may enhance gut microbiome resilience by increasing phage adaptability and fostering viral coexistence within bacterial taxa.

After further exploring bacteriophage–bacteriome interactions, we found the chicken gut bacteria are closely associated with the bacteriophage community, similar to the human gut [61–63]. The predominant presence of Lactobacillaceae species and Escherichia coli highlights the potential roles these bacteria play in shaping the chicken gut virome. Escherichia coli is a well-known host for various ARGs [88] and is a common target for phages [89], which may further influence the dynamics of resistance gene transfer. Moreover, the significant correlations observed between the bacteriophage community and bacteriome α-diversities and the strong alignment in their overall distributions suggest a co-evolutionary relationship and potential interdependencies between viral and bacterial communities.

The identification of key bacteria and their interactions with phages provides valuable insights into the diverse and dynamic gut environment. Notably, prominent bacteria such as Enterobacteriaceae, Oscillospiraceae, and Bacteroidaceae were detected to have strong correlations with the virome. These findings suggest that specific bacterial families frequently co-occur with their associated bacteriophages in the chicken gut, implying a high degree of interaction and interdependence between bacteriophages and their bacteria within the chicken gut. The intimate interconnections and frequent co-occurrences underscore the complex ecological relationships within the chicken gut microbiome.

Future studies should focus on elucidating the specific mechanisms underlying bacteriophage-bacteria interactions and their implications for chicken health and productivity. Understanding these interactions could pave the way for the development of phage-based therapies or probiotics aimed at optimizing gut health and enhancing the overall well-being of chicken.

Bacteriophages influence gut microbiota through cascade effects

Bacteriophages can profoundly influence the dynamics of the gut microbiota, not only directly targeting specific hosts but also triggering cascade effects. For example, bacteriophages may influence hosts by modulating metabolites. Virus-encoded AMGs, originating from bacteria and integrated into bacteriophage genomes, are widespread among bacteriophages [90, 91]. These bacteriophage infections can alter activities of microorganisms, impacting metabolic processes within the gut by reprogramming bacterial host metabolism and encoding bacterial exotoxins [92]. Our results suggest that potential bacteriophage AMGs may influence host metabolic pathways, but direct evidence for their modulatory role requires further experimental validation. The high enrichment of genes involved in information systems, amino acid metabolism, and central carbon metabolism suggests that viruses extensively influence these critical cellular functions. Additionally, the potential presence of peptidase and CAZY genes highlights the role of viral AMGs in protein degradation and carbohydrate metabolism, respectively. This comprehensive understanding of bacteriophage AMGs enhances our knowledge of bacteriophage-host interactions. Further research is needed to explore how the chicken bacteriophage community influences feed digestibility and nutrient utilization, which could inform strategies to enhance chicken health and productivity by selectively modulating the gut virome.

In addition, we explored the role of bacteriophages in potential ARGs. The small number of potential ARGs (~0.3% of vOTUs) found in the analyzed set of 17,268 viral genomes, while not directly significant with the bacteriophage community, aligns with previous observations that ARGs are rarely encoded by bacteriophages [93]. Furthermore, lifestyle prediction revealed that the vast majority of these ARG-carrying vOTUs were temperate, which is consistent with the primary role of temperate phages in specialized transduction rather than the promiscuous generalized transduction [94]. The overall diversity and abundance of ARGs were lower in viruses compared to bacteria [95]. Antibiotic resistance is a critical global concern, with recent studies highlighting viruses, particularly bacteriophages, as significant reservoirs of ARGs in the environment [96, 97]. The presence of ARGs within the environmental bacteriophage community suggests that bacteriophages could play pivotal roles in the horizontal transfer of ARGs among bacteria [96, 97]. However, the extent of viral ARG abundance remains debated, with some studies suggesting potential overestimations [93]. Bacteriophages may not typically carry a significant number of ARGs compared to bacterial genomes, aligning with previous studies indicating that ARGs are rarely encoded by bacteriophages in pig [98] and soil [99]. Empirical data support the rarity of ARGs in phage genomes. Consistent with some previous studies, we found that only a small fraction of phage genomes contained ARGs. This suggests that, under our analytical parameters, bacteriophages may be a less prominent direct reservoir of these genes in the chicken gut than other mechanisms. Mechanistically, ARGs located on mobile elements that invade a prophage can inactivate the prophage, preventing it from replenishing the pool of ‘living’ phages [93]. This inactivation reduces the likelihood of ARGs being maintained and propagated within viral populations. Furthermore, while MGEs like transposase, plasmids and insertion element are efficient at spreading ARGs through conjugation, phage-mediated generalized transduction is much less common. Laboratory experiments have shown that ARG transfer via phage transduction is 1000-fold less common than via conjugative elements. This is because generalized transduction relies on the rare event of erroneous encapsidation of non-phage DNA [100].

The study revealed that while viral genomes infrequently encode potential ARGs, those identified often confer multidrug resistance. Specifically, when ARGs were present, those conferring multidrug resistance predominated. This suggests that viral vectors, such as phages, carrying ARGs are more likely to harbor genes enabling bacteria to resist multiple antibiotics simultaneously. In clinical contexts, bacteriophage therapy shows promise as a targeted approach for combating multidrug-resistant infections [101]. By leveraging the ability of bacteriophages to target specific bacterial strains, researchers and clinicians can potentially mitigate the impact of multidrug resistance without exacerbating the spread of ARGs. Specifically, Escherichia coli mdfA was the most common ARG, with Escherichia coli being the most abundant host. This suggests that Escherichia coli may be a prevalent multidrug-resistant strain and that these strains are frequently selected by phages as hosts.

These findings provide insights into the role of bacteriophages in the chicken gut resistome. While the limited presence of ARGs in bacteriophage genomes is a positive indication, it is essential to consider the high abundance and diversity of ARGs already present in the chicken gut microbiome. Although generalized transduction, which relies on the rare event of erroneous encapsidation of non-phage DNA, is infrequent, it remains an important mechanism for horizontal gene transfer, particularly in environments with abundant ARGs like the chicken gut [102]. Therefore, based on our current metagenomic data, the contribution of bacteriophages to the dissemination of ARGs in the chicken gut microbiome appears limited. The low abundance of ARGs within the identified phage genomes is an encouraging finding that lends preliminary support to the potential feasibility of bacteriophage therapy from the perspective of antibiotic resistance dissemination. However, the possibility of ARG transfer, even at low frequencies, highlights the need for continued monitoring.

Conclusions

In summary, our study successfully characterized the bacteriophage community structure within the chicken gut, highlighting the critical role of bacteriophages in shaping gut microbiota and modulating microbial interactions. Metagenomic analyses revealed the functional potential of these bacteriophages, particularly in influencing metabolic pathways and microbial dynamics. While AMGs and a small number of potential ARGs were identified within the bacteriophage genomes, their low prevalence suggests a limited direct role of phages in ARG dissemination within this ecosystem, based on our current dataset. The functional roles and actual mobility of these genes warrant further experimental investigation to elucidate their true ecological and clinical implications. Importantly, this study establishes the CVD as a highly unique and valuable resource for studying bacteriophage diversity and bacteriophage-host interactions. The distinctiveness of the CVD underscores its potential to advance our understanding of the chicken gut bacteriophage community and provide insights into the ecological and functional complexities of bacteriophage communities.
