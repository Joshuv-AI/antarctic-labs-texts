Seasonal climate signals driving flowering of Robinia pseudoacacia in Piedmont (Italy): implications for apiculture under climate change
Authors: Fausto Carbonari, Chiara Epifani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Flowering dynamics of Robinia pseudoacacia L. (black locust), a key honey plant in southern Europe, are highly sensitive to climate variability and represent a useful indicator of environmental change. Despite its ecological and economic importance, long-term studies on black locust flowering behaviour remain limited in Italy. This study analyses a multi-decadal time series of flowering onset dates collected in Piedmont (1981–2025) and assesses the robustness of the observed relationships by incorporating data from the Italian Phenological Network (IPHEN, 2011–2025). Seasonal thermal indices, derived from the AgERA5 dataset, were used to assess the influence of autumn, winter, and spring temperatures on flowering timing. Over the study period, flowering onset advanced by about 0.5 days per year (p-value < 0.01), in parallel with a significant warming trend. Spring temperatures emerged as the main drivers: a 1 °C increase in the spring maximum temperature index was associated with an advance of more than 4 days in flowering onset. The winter temperatures also showed a significant, though weaker, negative association, suggesting a modulatory role on interannual phenological variability. In contrast, correlations with autumn temperatures reflected shared long-term trends rather than independent climatic control. Analyses on the pooled Montaldo + IPHEN dataset, including internal k-fold cross-validation, confirmed the robustness of the spring signal and highlighted the operational value of the winter index, which can be computed by late February, and predicts flowering onset with an average error of about 5 days. These results clarify the relative role of seasonal thermal signals in regulating the black locust flowering and provide useful information to support apicultural planning under ongoing climate change.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00484-026-03184-7.

Introduction

Plant phenology represents a sensitive indicator of climate change, with important ecological and applied implications (Rosenzweig et al. 2008). Among the various stages of the life cycle, flowering is particularly responsive to rising temperatures (Fitter et al. 1995; Fitter and Fitter 2002), with potential effects on plant–pollinator interactions and associated ecosystem services (Walther et al. 2002; Parmesan 2006; Cleland et al. 2007; Memmott et al. 2007; Le Conte and Navajas 2008; Kudo and Ida 2013), including apiculture (Reddy et al. 2013; Van Espen et al. 2023). Numerous studies conducted at European and global scales have documented an advancement of phenological phases linked to spring warming, confirming the high sensitivity of temperate tree species to current climatic conditions (Menzel 2000; Menzel et al. 2006; Piao et al. 2019). These findings have guided the development of most phenological models, which are mainly based on spring thermal indices and reflect the dominant role of thermal forcing during this phase (Schwartz 2013; Chuine and Régnière 2017). In applied contexts such as beekeeping, phenological timing is also relevant at the interannual scale, because nectar flows occur within short, weather-sensitive windows.

In this context, R. pseudoacacia L. represents a case of particular interest, both for its ecological characteristics and for its socio-economic relevance. It is a species native to North America, widely naturalised in Europe and the main source of nectar for acacia honey, a high-value product that, in Italy, represents the most appreciated and profitable spring honey. Studies conducted in Hungary and Poland have shown a significant advance in flowering in relation to spring temperatures (Walkovszky 1998; Jatczak and Walawender 2009; Jabłońska et al. 2015). Most long-term records for R. pseudoacacia originate from Central and Eastern Europe (e.g. Walkovszky 1998; Jabłońska et al. 2015), while data from Western Europe remain scarce. In Italy, the available evidence is still limited and based on relatively short time series, often referring to vegetative phases rather than flowering (Orlandi et al. 2014). On the other hand, the marked interannual variability in acacia honey yields, linked to nectar production and mediated by flowering dynamics, is well documented (Osservatorio Nazionale Miele and ISMEA 2023; Osservatorio Nazionale Miele 2024). This variability motivates the need for indicators that provide timely information to anticipate key phases of the season. In addition, there is the problem of late frosts, which can interact with phenological advancement: although some studies in Central Europe have shown a reduction in the risk of damage from late frosts (Scheifinger et al. 2003), in general phenological advancement can increase vulnerability to such events, which may act as selective forces with ecological and evolutionary implications (Inouye 2000; Augspurger 2013).

Although spring forcing is recognized as the main driver of flowering, the role of thermal conditions preceding spring remains poorly explored. In temperate tree species, the transition from dormancy to flowering is regulated by the interaction between winter chilling signals and spring forcing, which determine dormancy release and promote growth resumption (Amasino 2010; Vitasse and Basler 2013; Luedeling et al. 2013). Winter cold, in addition to modulating hormonal balances, also appears crucial for ensuring the efficient mobilization of carbon reserves: in temperate fruit species, insufficient chilling can impair the use of stored reserves during vegetative recovery (Marafon et al. 2011; Pagter et al. 2015; Kaufmann and Blanke 2017). Although black locust appears to have a reduced or uncertain chilling requirement (Cook et al. 2012; Kim et al. 2022), yet further long-term analyses are needed to quantify the contribution of different seasons, particularly winter, to the species’ phenological variability. Clarifying these aspects is essential for understanding the climatic mechanisms that regulate flowering in this species. Against this backdrop, we examine whether a winter-based signal available earlier in the season might complement spring predictors for timely forecasting of flowering onset.

This study addresses this gap by using two sets of flowering start dates collected in Piedmont: a historical series spanning over thirty years (1981–2025) relating to a single site near Montaldo Scarampi (province of Asti) and a complementary dataset from the IPHEN phenological network (2011–2025), encompassing multiple sites distributed across six provinces. Through trend analysis, detrending and linear regressions with seasonal thermal indices, the study aims to (i) describe the shared climate-phenology trend and quantify the contribution of autumn, winter and spring temperatures to the interannual variability of flowering onset, with particular attention to the role of winter conditions, (ii) assess the phenological plasticity after removing the long-term trends, and (iii) assess whether the inferred relationships remain consistent across datasets (Montaldo Scarampi and IPHEN). By explicitly comparing winter and spring indices, the study contributes to clarifying the climatic mechanisms that regulate the phenology of R. pseudoacacia and provides useful elements for assessing the resilience of beekeeping production in a context of climate change.

Materials and methods

Phenological and climatic data

The phenological data used in this study are the R. pseudoacacia flowering onset dates, corresponding to BBCH stage 61 (Meier 2001), from two datasets. The first phenological dataset comprises a historical series of R. pseudoacacia flowering start dates collected annually between 1981 and 2025 for a total of 40 observations, with data missing only in 1988, 1993, 1994, 1997 and 2000. The data were collected in a black locust forest located near a beekeeping farm in Montaldo Scarampi (province of Asti, Piedmont, Italy; 44°50′00.9″N, 8°15′55.6″E; 260 m a.s.l.) and were provided by Aspromiele (Associazione Produttori Miele Piemonte), a major regional beekeepers’ association in Piedmont. The observations, recorded directly by the farm owner, report the start and end dates of flowering for the entire black locust forest rather than for individual trees. Although collected using less formalised methods than standardised protocols (e.g., IPHEN), this multi-decadal series is exceptional in scope and continuity, providing a rare opportunity to access the long-term climate influence on R. pseudoacacia.

The second dataset, extracted from the national IPHEN project (Mariani et al. 2013), includes 38 observations of flowering onset (BBCH61) collected between 2011 and 2025 from 31 sites across six Piedmont provinces (Fig. 1). The network applies a standardised BBCH protocol (Meier 2001) to monitor the full phenological cycle of R. pseudoacacia, from winter dormancy release to the end of flowering. All phenological phases are recorded weekly on ten trees per site, using the median of observations to minimise individual variability. Data are collected by trained volunteer observers, mainly beekeepers, in collaboration with their regional associations, and undergo quality control before archiving. While site availability varies over time due to the volunteer-based nature of the network, several IPHEN sites near Montaldo Scarampi increase local sampling density and enable cross-dataset comparisons beyond the single-site series. Missing years are 2012, 2014, 2015, 2020, and 2021.

To ensure consistency between the two datasets, when multiple BBCH61 records were available within the same year for a given IPHEN site, only the first occurrence was retained, making the data directly comparable with those from Montaldo Scarampi. Dates were converted from calendar format to Day of the Year (DOY, where January 1 = 1) to allow uniform temporal comparison.

Finally, daily minimum, maximum and mean temperature data for 1980–2025 were obtained from the AgERA5 dataset (Copernicus Climate Change Service, C3S; Boogaard et al. 2020) for the grid cells corresponding to the phenological sites.

Seasonal thermal indices

To evaluate the influence of climatic conditions on the onset of flowering in R. pseudoacacia, a set of seasonal thermal indices was calculated. These indices summarise temperature trends in the months preceding flowering, focusing on autumn, winter and early spring, to quantify the thermal accumulation that regulates flowering timing. Seasons were defined according to meteorological convention: autumn (September–November) and winter (December–February), while the spring window was restricted to March 1 – April 15, to ensure a realistic temporal link and avoid overlap with flowering onset, which in the Montaldo Scarampi series never occurred before April 20 and in the IPHEN dataset not before April 16.

The preceding summer was excluded because it is too distant from the following year’s flowering onset. During this period, physiological processes such as bud formation and carbohydrate storage occur and are generally completed by late summer or extend into autumn (Barbaroux and Breda 2002; Cooke et al. 2012; Delpierre et al. 2016; Silvestro et al. 2019). Consequently, any summer influence is assumed to be already incorporated into autumn temperature dynamics.

Temperature data for 1980–2025 were aggregated by the previously defined seasons. For each season, three indices were computed: the mean of daily minimum, mean, and maximum temperatures. Each index name explicitly combines the acronym mT (mean temperature) with the temperature type and season (e.g., mTmin_winter indicates the seasonal mean of daily minimum temperatures from December to February). Seasonal indices were calculated for the AgERA5 grid cell corresponding to Montaldo Scarampi for the main analyses, and for each IPHEN site for the combined-dataset analyses and internal cross-validation.

For the main analyses, two predictors were specified a priori to represent distinct seasonal components: spring forcing and winter mildness. Spring forcing was quantified as the mean of daily maximum temperature during the spring window (mTmax_spring), to represent daytime warming during the pre-flowering period. Winter conditions were quantified as the mean of daily maximum temperature during winter (mTmax_winter), used here as an index of “mild winters” (i.e., winters characterised by frequent above-average daytime temperatures), to test whether winter warmth contains explanatory information beyond spring forcing. The remaining indices were retained for descriptive comparison and sensitivity analyses, rather than for predictor screening.

Preliminary checks and detrending

A preliminary analysis of the phenological datasets (Montaldo Scarampi and IPHEN) was conducted to check data quality, and potential outliers. The interquartile range (IQR) method and visual inspection of histograms and boxplots were used to screen for potential data-entry issues and to flag extreme observations.

For the Montaldo Scarampi time series and for all the seasonal thermal indices, linear trends were assessed to distinguish long-term changes from interannual variability. When a significant trend was detected (p-value < 0.05), the series was detrended by removing the fitted linear component. Subsequent analyses were conducted both on the raw series, to characterise shared long-term co-trending, and on detrended anomalies, to quantify interannual covariation and highlight phenological plasticity (Wu et al. 2007; Iler et al. 2017).

Identification of thermal predictors and model development

Analyses in this section refer to the Montaldo Scarampi time series (1981–2025). Predictors used in the main analyses were pre-specified a priori as mTmax_spring (spring forcing) and mTmax_winter (winter mildness), as defined in the previous section. Pearson’s correlation coefficient (r; Pearson 1895) between flowering onset (DOY) and the seasonal thermal indices was computed using both raw and detrended series to separate long-term co-trending from interannual covariation. Correlation analyses were performed for descriptive purposes and sensitivity assessment.

Simple linear regression models were fitted separately for mTmax_spring and mTmax_winter to quantify their individual associations with flowering onset. To assess whether winter warmth provides information beyond spring forcing, we additionally performed a partialling-out analysis, in which mTmax_winter was regressed on mTmax_spring and the residual winter component was tested against flowering onset; this robustness check is not intended to imply a specific physiological mechanism (e.g., chilling).

Model assumptions were evaluated using standard residual diagnostics, including visual inspection of residual plots and the Shapiro–Wilk test for residual normality (Shapiro and Wilk 1965).

Combined-dataset analysis and internal cross-validation

The analysis was extended by incorporating the IPHEN dataset to assess the robustness of the relationships identified in the long-term Montaldo Scarampi series when additional observations were pooled. The IPHEN dataset covers a shorter and more recent period, but it increases the number of observations and supports cross-dataset comparisons. Simple linear regression models were developed on this combined series using the two a priori predictors mTmax_winter and mTmax_spring.

For model performance, a k-fold cross-validation procedure with k=5 was adopted. The choice of k=5 ensures that, at each iteration, approximately 80% of the data are used for training and 20% for testing, providing a balanced trade-off between model learning and unbiased performance evaluation. In each iteration, the dataset was randomly divided into five subsets, using k-1 folds for training and the remaining fold for testing. Predictive performance was evaluated using R², MAE, and RMSE, reported as averages across folds. This procedure provides an internal robustness assessment of model performance on the available data.

The entire statistical processing and analysis workflow was implemented in a reproducible and automated way to ensure consistency and traceability of results. To this end, the Python environment (v3.11.2; Van Rossum and Drake 2009) was used with the following packages: “pandas” (v2.3.2; McKinney 2010), “NumPy” (v2.3.3; Harris et al. 2020), “xarray” (v2025.9.0; Hoyer and Hamman 2017), “SciPy” (v1.16.2; Virtanen et al. 2020), “statsmodels” (v0.14.5; Seabold and Perktold 2010), “scikit-learn” (v1.7.2; Pedregosa et al. 2011), “matplotlib” (v3.10.6; Hunter 2007) and “seaborn” (v0.13.2; Waskom 2021).

Results

Descriptive analysis of flowering onset dates

The two phenological datasets, Montaldo Scarampi (1981–2025) and IPHEN (2011–2025), showed similar means and comparable interannual variability. Distributions were broadly consistent across datasets. Further details are provided in the Supplementary Materials (Table S1, Fig. S1). The IPHEN series included one unusually late observation (2019, DOY=157), which was retained and highlighted in figures (Fig. S1) and evaluated through influence diagnostics and sensitivity analyses. Overall, both datasets provided a solid basis for subsequent analyses. The analysis first focused on the longer Montaldo Scarampi series, with IPHEN incorporated in the combined-dataset analysis and internal cross-validation.

Because altitude could potentially affect phenology, its influence on the comparability between the Montaldo Scarampi and IPHEN series was assessed. The IPHEN sites were grouped into three classes, defined according to the 25th and 75th percentiles of the elevation distribution (h): low (h≤238.5 m), intermediate (238.5<h≤317.0 m), and high (h>317.0 m). Consistent with this, there was no significant difference in flowering dates (BBCH61) among the elevation classes (Kruskal–Wallis H = 1.30, p-value > 0.05) (Fig. S2, Table S2). The site-level analysis, based on the Spearman correlation between elevation and flowering onset date, indicated a weak and non-significant association (ρ = 0.23, n = 31, p-value > 0.05). These results indicate that altitude did not have a detectable influence on flowering timing within our dataset. The elevation of Montaldo Scarampi (260 m a.s.l.), which falls within the intermediate range, the most represented class in the IPHEN series (15 sites), therefore poses no limitation for comparison.

Overall, AgERA5-derived indices showed substantial overlap across altitude classes (Fig. S3), indicating limited climatic separation at this spatial resolution. Therefore, stratifying by altitude was unlikely to add explanatory power, and all sites were analysed together.

The historical series of the flowering onset in Montaldo Scarampi revealed a long-term negative trend during the period 1981–2025 (Fig. 2). The linear regression indicates a progressive advancement of flowering dates over time, with a rate of approximately 0.50 days per year (5 days/decade), with values shifting from later occurrences in the early 1980 s toward earlier dates in the most recent decades. This long-term trend is overlaid by marked interannual variability. Individual years deviate considerably from the regression line, with both delayed (up to +15 days) and early (up to −12 days) flowering events occurring throughout the period considered, as confirmed by the analysis of residuals.

To further investigate whether this overall trend was temporally uniform or characterized by periods of differing intensity, temporal heterogeneity in trend strength was explored descriptively using a moving-window approach, following Iler et al. (2013) (Fig. S4).

Seasonal thermal indices showed a progressive warming trend from 1981 to 2025 (Table 1). Autumn and winter indices increased consistently, whereas spring warming was weaker and was not detected for minimum temperatures. Decadal-scale changes in flowering trend intensity were further explored with a period-based comparison of climatic conditions to characterize the temporal evolution of background temperature. Anomalies were most pronounced in autumn and winter, with recurrent positive values over the last decade, while spring displayed a less linear pattern with alternating warm and cool years (Figs. S5, S6). This framework outlines the climatic context underlying the observed phenological variability and forms the basis for subsequent analyses of the links between seasonal temperatures and flowering onset.

Because flowering onset and several thermal indices exhibited significant long-term trends (Table 1), associations were evaluated in two complementary ways. Analyses on the raw series were used to characterise shared long-term co-trending that includes the background warming signal, whereas analyses on detrended anomalies were used to isolate interannual covariation and highlight year-to-year phenological responses (plasticity) independent of shared trends.

Relationships between thermal indices and flowering onset

Correlation analyses on raw and detrended series are reported as supporting evidence: the persistence of the association in detrended anomalies suggests that the relationship is not determined exclusively by the common long-term trend, but is consistent with interannual climate control.

Pearson correlations between flowering onset dates and thermal indices, before and after detrending, revealed distinct seasonal patterns (Table 2). In autumn, initially significant negative correlations disappeared after detrending, indicating that they reflected shared trends rather than by interannual covariation. In winter, however, the signal persisted: indices maintained significant negative correlations even after detrending (up to r = –0.51), indicating that winter temperature contains explanatory information for flowering timing beyond the shared long-term trend, although the underlying mechanism cannot be inferred from these data. The strongest relationships occurred in spring, where thermal indices based on mean and maximum temperatures showed stable negative correlations with the phenological phase (up to r = –0.72), regardless of detrending. Finally, the linear regression models presented below focused on two indices selected a priori to represent winter conditions during dormancy (mTmax_winter) and spring thermal forcing after bud break (mTmax_spring), allowing for an explicit comparison between winter and spring and the assessment of both biological interpretability and operational potential (longer lead time) for flowering prediction.

Linear regression analysis confirmed that these indices significantly explain the interannual variability of flowering onset (Fig. 3, Table S3). The relationship between the BBCH61 phase and mTmax_winter was negative and highly significant (β = –3.94 ± 0.76 days/°C, p-value < 0.001), indicating that a 1 °C increase of the index is associated with an advancement of about 4 days in flowering onset.

A similar but stronger signal emerged for the spring thermal index based on maximum temperatures (mTmax_spring, period March 1–April 15). The linear regression yielded a coefficient of β = –4.54 ± 0.72 days/°C (p-value < 0.001) and R² = 0.51 (n = 40), indicating that flowering onset is associated with an advancement of about 4.5 days per 1 °C increase in this index. Because the predictor is a seasonal mean, a 1 °C difference corresponds to an average warming across the entire window. Residual diagnostics did not indicate major violations (Shapiro–Wilk W = 0.99 for winter and 0.98 for spring; p-values > 0.05), supporting the adequacy of the linear regression.

To assess potential carry-over effects between winter and spring conditions, we quantified the covariation between seasonal thermal indices and tested whether the winter signal persists beyond the component shared with spring. The correlation between indices is moderate and weakens after detrending (Pearson r = 0.54 and 0.46 in raw and detrended series respectively), corresponding to limited collinearity (VIF = 1.41 and 1.27, respectively). As a diagnostic control, we isolated the component of the winter index not explained by the spring index by regressing mTmax_winter on mTmax_spring (R² = 0.30; p-value < 0.001) and using the residuals as a measure of winter variability independent of spring. The flowering onset dates remain significantly associated with these residuals (β = −1.77 days/°C; p-value < 0.05; R² = 0.06), indicating that the winter signal cannot be attributed solely to winter–spring covariation, although other inter-seasonal dependencies or unmodelled confounding in specific years cannot be entirely ruled out.

Combined-dataset analysis and internal cross-validation of the models

To assess the robustness of the relationships identified in the long-term Montaldo Scarampi series when additional observations were pooled, the Montaldo Scarampi and IPHEN datasets were merged into a single combined dataset (n = 78).

As a preliminary step, the restriction of the Montaldo Scarampi series to the 1991–2025 period was initially considered to ensure direct comparability with the IPHEN dataset. However, sensitivity analyses (Table S4) showed that regression parameters were highly consistent between the restricted and the full datasets. On this basis, the complete Montaldo Scarampi series (1981–2025) was retained in order to maximize statistical power and exploit the longer temporal coverage.

The simple linear regression with mTmax_winter (Table S4, Fig. S7). revealed a significant relationship between mTmax_winter and flowering onset date (β = –3.58 ± 0.61 days/°C, p-value < 0.001; R² = 0.31). As expected, the model with mTmax_spring (Table S4, Fig. S8). demonstrated a greater ability than the winter index to explain the phenomenon of flowering (β = −3.66 ± 0.45 days/°C, p-value <0.001; R2 = 0.47). For both simple linear regression models based on mTmax_winter and mTmax_spring, the distribution of residuals did not meet the normality assumption. The Shapiro–Wilk test showed W = 0.95 (p-value < 0.01) for the mTmax_winter model and W = 0.94 (p-value < 0.01) for the mTmax_spring model, indicating departures from normality. Inspection of the residuals highlighted one influential observation (DOY = 157, IPHEN). Influence diagnostics (leverage and Cook’s distance) confirmed that DOY = 157 was an influential observation; detailed results are reported in the Supplementary Material (Table S5). This observation was retained in the main analyses. We repeated the regression and cross-validation steps excluding DOY = 157 as a sensitivity analysis; the corresponding estimates and performance metrics are reported in the Supplementary Material (Table S6; Figs. S9–S10; Table S7).

For the mTmax_winter, MAE were around 5.5 days in both training and test folds, with RMSE values approximately 7.6 days. The coefficient of determination was moderate in training (R² = 0.32) and more variable in testing (R² = 0.31 ± 0.24), reflecting limited sample size and heterogeneity across sites/years. The mTmax_spring displayed a stronger and more stable performance, with lower errors (MAE ≈ 4.8–4.9 days; RMSE ≈ 6.7–6.8 days) and higher R² (0.47 in training; 0.44 in testing). These results confirm the greater explanatory power of spring temperatures, while still acknowledging their limited operational usefulness due to proximity to the flowering event.

Despite the moderate R² of the winter index, its MAE remains within ~5 days, matching the 7-day temporal resolution of the IPHEN phenological monitoring. This level of error supports a practical identification of the week in which BBCH61 occurs, which is relevant for beekeeping operations; model deviations are of the same order as the observational uncertainty associated with weekly monitoring.

Discussion

The results confirm that the advancement of flowering onset at Montaldo Scarampi reflects the high sensitivity of R. pseudoacacia to climate warming. Although based on a single site, this finding aligns with trends observed in other temperate trees (Menzel et al. 2006; Piao et al. 2019) and the climate–phenology relationships are consistent across the IPHEN observations from multiple sites in Piedmont. For black locust, this sensitivity is particularly relevant, as it highlights the species’ marked phenotypic plasticity (Granata et al. 2020), a trait that may enhance its ability to adjust phenology to a rapidly changing climate. Similar plasticity occurs in other naturalized species, supporting their success across heterogeneous European environments (Davidson et al. 2011; Bouteiller et al. 2021). However, such phenological shifts, though adaptive for the species, may lead to a mismatch with honeybee colony development, potentially limiting nectar exploitation during early springs (Le Conte and Navajas 2008).

Seasonal thermal indices show significant warming over 1981–2025, strongest in autumn and winter, consistent with warming trends reported for Italy and the Euro-Mediterranean region (Brunetti et al. 2006; Lionello and Scarascia 2018; Bilbao et al. 2019); spring indices show more moderate increases in mean and maximum temperatures, while minimum spring temperatures show no significant trend.

The correlation analysis clearly shows a seasonal hierarchy of climate signals. Spring temperatures, in particular the mTmax_spring index maintains a strong negative association with the start date of flowering in both the original series and the detrended anomalies, confirming the dominant role of spring thermal forcing in controlling vegetative recovery and progression towards flowering (Delpierre et al. 2016; Kim et al. 2022). In addition to this dominant signal, however, there is a secondary but persistent contribution from winter temperature conditions. Winter indices, particularly mTmax_winter, show significant correlations even after removing the trend, suggesting that winter temperature may modulate flowering timing on an interannual scale. However, its explanatory contribution remains smaller than that of spring forcing. This evidence should be interpreted with caution: in the absence of direct proxies for chilling and observations on dormancy recovery, the result indicates a robust statistical association, but does not allow for the unambiguous attribution of a specific physiological mechanism.

Although winter and spring indices are moderately correlated (limited collinearity), the winter association persists after partialling out the component shared with spring, although the spring-independent component explains a limited fraction of variance (R² ≈ 0.06); therefore, the winter signal cannot be attributed solely to winter–spring covariation, even if inter-seasonal carry-over effects in some years cannot be entirely ruled out. Despite these limitations, the winter signal is physiologically plausible. In R. pseudoacacia, flowering occurs at a stage when the leaves are already developed or rapidly developing, indicating an early spring flowering strategy (Farkas and Zajácz 2007), a pattern also frequently observed during IPHEN. In this context, milder winter temperatures could facilitate the transition to the reproductive phase, in accordance with recent observations on deciduous tree species in Italy that highlight a relationship between late winter temperatures and early flowering (Fornaciari et al. 2023).

Our results are consistent with the phenological mechanisms in temperate broadleaf species, where dormancy–growth transition is regulated by thermal and photoperiodic cues (Chuine et al. 2013; Silvestro et al. 2025), confirming the dominant role of spring temperatures in advancing phenological phases (Menzel et al. 2006; Piao et al. 2019).

The phenological series from Montaldo Scarampi, consistent with the IPHEN data, provides a unique reference for black locust in Italy and a basis for assessing model performance. As expected, the model based on the spring index has a higher explanatory power than the winter model, while the winter model, despite showing more moderate R² values, maintains average absolute errors of ~5.5 days. This level of accuracy is comparable to the temporal resolution of IPHEN observations (weekly) and therefore is of the same order as uncertainty of the phenological data (Alilla et al. 2022).

In practice, a moderate anomaly (+1 °C) would suggest earlier-than-normal flowering, but the associated advance (~ 4–5 days) is comparable to the prediction error (MAE ≈ 5 days). Accordingly, such signals should be interpreted as a multi-day time window rather than as a precise flowering date, and thus provide limited actionable information on their own. By contrast, a larger anomaly (+2 °C) implies a greater advance (~ 7–9 days), which is more likely to exceed the residual variability of the model and therefore to be operationally distinguishable. For his reason, predictions are reported as a weekly-scale time window, consistent with the uncertainty of the model, and are more reliable under pronounced seasonal anomalies. Because resource use by honey bees strongly depends on weather conditions during flowering, week-scale shifts of this magnitude can alter the overlap between flowering and favourable foraging windows, with potential operational implications even in the absence of direct yield effects. Finally, earlier flowering may increase exposure to unfavourable weather conditions (e.g., late frost), framing these outcomes in terms of risk management, as this study does not directly measure nectar flows or colony performance.

Some limitations, however, remain. Phenological data were not collected under fully uniform protocols, an intrinsic limitation, although harmonization criteria were applied to reduce heterogeneity. A further limitation of this study is that it focuses on seasonal mean thermal indices and does not explicitly account for temperature extremes (e.g., late winter or early spring frost events) or for the duration and persistence of warm or cold spells. Such factors may influence phenological responses even when seasonal mean temperatures are similar, because, at comparable mean values, intra-seasonal variability and the temporal structure of thermal conditions (i.e., the frequency, duration and timing of thermal events) may differ, particularly in years characterised by high intra-seasonal variability. The AgERA5 climatic data provide continuous spatial and temporal coverage throughout the study period, but the grid resolution (~9 km) may be limiting in areas with complex topography. This mismatch may reduce correlation strength and contribute to residual model variability; however, it is expected to act mainly as additional noise, attenuating rather than inflating climate–phenology relationships, and therefore does not invalidate the consistency of the seasonal signals observed.

Finally, the analysis focused exclusively on thermal variables, excluding other potentially relevant factors such as water availability and photoperiod. This choice reflects the dominant role of temperature in temperate species phenology but narrows the model’s applicability and points to future research integrating multiple climatic drivers.

This study demonstrates that the floral phenology of R. pseudoacacia at Montaldo Scarampi (Piedmont), is strongly and consistently linked to seasonal warming, with a marked and continuous advance in flowering over the last four decades.

The main signal is driven by spring temperatures, while winter thermal conditions emerge as a modulating factor of interannual variability, suggesting a broader sensitivity of the phenological cycle to temperatures prior to vegetative recovery.

From a methodological point of view, the distinction between long-term trends and interannual anomalies has made it possible to separate the dominant bioclimatic signal from the phenological plasticity-related component. The integration of the long local historical series with IPHEN data broadened the dataset’s geographic coverage and supported the robustness of the climate–phenology relationships across the available Piedmont sites.

Although the model based on spring temperatures has the greatest predictive power, the winter index retains significant operational value, as it can be calculated at the end of winter and provides early indications of the likely flowering period. In a context of increasing climate uncertainty and the high sensitivity of acacia honey production to weather conditions, such tools can provide concrete support for beekeeping management.

A promising next step is to evaluate whether integrating winter thermal information into spring-based forcing models, currently focused on spring forcing alone, can improve forecast robustness under increasing climate uncertainty. Future work should also extend the analysis to a large number of sites and climatic contexts and integrate more detailed phenological observations on dormancy and vegetative recovery phases in order to clarify the role of winter signals and other environmental drivers. In this sense, the strengthening of phenological networks such as IPHEN represents a key resource for linking local observations, climatic dynamics and operational applications.

In parallel, future work could integrate simple indices of temperature extremes and duration (e.g., number of frost days, frequency or persistence of warm spells) to complement seasonal mean indicators, potentially improving both the interpretation of phenological responses and the operational relevance of the predictions under increasingly variable climatic conditions.
