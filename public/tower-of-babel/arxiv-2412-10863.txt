The structure of rough sets defined by reflexive relations
Jouni Järvinen, Sándor Radeleczki

Abstract
. For several types of information relations, the induced rough sets system RS does not form a lattice but only a partially ordered set. However, by studying its Dedekind–MacNeille completion DM(RS), one may reveal new important properties of rough set structures. Building upon D. Umadevi’s work on describing joins and meets in DM(RS), we previously investigated pseudo-Kleene algebras defined on DM(RS) for reflexive relations. This paper delves deeper into the order-theoretic properties of DM(RS) in the context of reflexive relations. We describe the completely join-irreducible elements of DM(RS) and characterize when DM(RS) is a spatial completely distributive lattice. We show that even in the case of a non-transitive reflexive relation, DM(RS) can form a Nelson algebra, a property generally associated with quasiorders. We introduce a novel concept, the core of a relational neighborhood, and use it to provide a necessary and sufficient condition for DM(RS) to determine a Nelson algebra.

1.
Introduction

Rough set theory, initially introduced in
[
29
]
, is grounded in the concept of indistinguishability
relations. These relations are equivalence relations defined such that two objects are considered equivalent when they appear identical based on their observable properties.

Let
E
be an equivalence relation on
U
.
Every subset
X
of
U
can be approximated by two sets.
The upper approximation
X^{\blacktriangle}
consists of elements that are
related to at least one element in
X
by the equivalence relation

E
. This set represents the elements that might belong to
X
given the information provided by
E
.
The lower approximation
X^{\blacktriangledown}
consists of elements such that all elements related to them by
E
are also in
X
. This set represents the elements that definitely belong to
X
based on the information provided by
E
.

The rough set of
X
is the pair
(X^{\blacktriangledown},X^{\blacktriangle})
. This pair
captures the uncertainty related to the subset
X
due to the limited information provided by the equivalence relation
E
.
The collection of all rough sets, denoted by

\mathrm{RS}
, can be ordered coordinate-wise using the set-inclusion relation. The lattice-theoretic properties of RS have been extensively studied. For example, J. Pomykała and
J. A. Pomykała
[
30
]
proved that RS forms a Stone
algebra. This result was further refined by S. D. Comer

[
6
]
, who established that
\mathrm{RS}
forms a regular
double Stone algebra. Piero Pagliani
[
28
]
introduced a Nelson algebra structure on
\mathrm{RS}
and showed that these
Nelson algebras induced by equivalence relations are semisimple.

Rough approximations and rough sets can be extended beyond
equivalence relations by using other types of binary relations.
A quasiorder, a reflexive and transitive relation, is one such
example. Rough sets defined by quasiorders have been investigated
by several researchers (see
[
9
,
23
,
24
,
25
,
27
,
31
]
, for instance).
The rough set lattice
\mathrm{RS}
determined by a quasiorder
is known to be completely distributive and spatial. Moreover, a Nelson algebra can be defined on
\mathrm{RS}
(see
[
14
,
15
,
16
,
21
,
22
]
). A tolerance, a reflexive and symmetric relation, is another type of binary relation that can be used to define rough sets. While the rough set system
\mathrm{RS}
defined
by a tolerance may not always form a lattice
[
12
]
,
it does form a completely distributive and spatial lattice when
the tolerance is induced by an irredundant covering. Furthermore,
a pseudocomplemented regular Kleene algebra can be defined on

\mathrm{RS}
in this case (see
[
17
,
18
,
19
]
).

For arbitrary binary relations D. Umadevi presented the
Dedekind–MacNeille completion
\mathrm{DM(RS)}
of
\mathrm{RS}

in
[
34
]
. She also described the meets and joins in

\mathrm{DM(RS)}
.
In
[
20
]
, we investigated pseudo-Kleene algebras defined
on
\mathrm{DM(RS)}
for reflexive relations. We proved that these
algebras are always paraorthomodular.
Also in a recent work
[
35
]
, Umadevi studies

\mathrm{DM(RS)}
in case of a reflexive relation.

The motivation for this research is to further explore the order-theoretic and algebraic properties of relation-based rough set structures. Since some older studies have a special role, we start by recalling them.

J. Varlet
[
37
]
proved that a double pseudocomplemented distributive lattice is regular if and only if every chain of its prime filters has at most two elements. Varlet
[
36
]
also showed that a distributive double pseudocomplemented lattice is a regular double Stone algebra if and only if its prime filters form disjoint chains of one or two elements. In
[
5
]
,
R. Cignoli proved that a quasi-Nelson algebra (see page
5
of this work for the definition) is a Nelson algebra if and only if its prime filters have the so-called interpolation property.

In our previous works, we proved that in a complete, completely distributive, and spatial lattice, analogous results can be presented using completely join-irreducible elements. The advantage of this is that dealing with join-irreducible elements is simpler than dealing with prime filters.

We proved in
[
19
]
that a completely distributive and spatial pseudocomplemented Kleene algebra is regular if and only if its set of completely join-irreducible elements has at most two levels. Because the complete lattices of rough sets defined by tolerances induced by irredundant coverings are completely distributive and spatial, we applied this result to prove that
\mathrm{RS}
forms a regular pseudocomplemented Kleene algebra. Using relationships between the completely
join-irreducible elements, we also provided a representation theorem stating that each pseudocomplemented Kleene algebra defined on a completely distributive and spatial lattice is isomorphic to some
\mathrm{RS}
defined by a tolerance induced by an irredundant covering.

In
[
15
]
, we showed that a completely distributive and spatial Kleene algebra forms a Nelson algebra if and only if its set of completely join-irreducible elements has the interpolation property. Using this result, we proved that rough sets determined by a quasiorder form a Nelson algebra. Additionally, we proved that every completely distributive and spatial Nelson algebra is isomorphic to some rough set defined by a quasiorder.

Note also that M. Gehrke and E. Walker proved in
[
10
]
that for an equivalence
E
,
\mathrm{RS}
is isomorphic to
\mathbf{2}^{I}\times\mathbf{3}^{K}
, where
\mathbf{2}
and
\mathbf{3}
are the chains of 2 and 3 elements,

is the set of the
E
-classes having one element, and

is the set of

-classes having more than one element. This means that the completely join-irreducible
elements of
\mathrm{RS}
form disjoint chains of one or two elements.

The above discussion highlights the special interest in the notions of completely join-irreducible elements, spatiality, and complete distributivity. In this work, we characterize the set of the completely join-irreducible elements of
\mathrm{DM(RS)}
. Furthermore, we characterize the cases when
\mathrm{DM(RS)}
is spatial and completely distributive.

2.
Rough sets by reflexive relations

Originally rough approximation operations were defined
in terms of equivalence relations
[
29
]
.
Several studies in the literature define rough set approximations using arbitrary binary relations, with one of the earliest definitions appearing in
[
38
]
.

Let
R
be a binary relation on
U
. We denote for
any
x\in U
,
R(x):=\{y\in U\mid(x,y)\in R\}
.
The symbol
:=
denotes “equals by definition”.
For any set
X\subseteq U
, the
lower approximation
of
X
is

X^{\blacktriangledown}:=\{x\in U\mid R(x)\subseteq X\}

and the
upper approximation
of
X
is

X^{\blacktriangle}:=\{x\in U\mid R(x)\cap X\neq\emptyset\}.

We may also determine rough set approximations in terms of the inverse

\breve{R}
of
R
, that is,

X^{\triangledown}:=\{x\in U\mid\breve{R}(x)\subseteq X\}

and

X^{\vartriangle}:=\{x\in U\mid\breve{R}(x)\cap X\neq\emptyset\}.

For all
X\subseteq U
,

X^{\blacktriangle}=\bigcup_{x\in X}\breve{R}(x)\text{ \ and \ }X^{\vartriangle}=\bigcup_{x\in X}R(x).

In particular,
\{x\}^{\vartriangle}=R(x)
and
\{x\}^{\blacktriangle}=\breve{R}(x)
for all

x\in U
.

Let
\wp(U)
denote the family of all subsets of
U
. We also write

\displaystyle\wp(U)^{\blacktriangledown}:=\{X^{\blacktriangledown}\mid X\subseteq U\},\qquad\wp(U)^{\blacktriangle}:=\{X^{\blacktriangle}\mid X\subseteq U\},

\displaystyle\wp(U)^{\triangledown}:=\{X^{\triangledown}\mid X\subseteq U\},\qquad\wp(U)^{\vartriangle}:=\{X^{\vartriangle}\mid X\subseteq U\}.

Galois connections appear in diverse areas of mathematics and theoretical computer science. A Galois connection is a relationship between two ordered sets that connects them through a pair of order-preserving functions, indicating a strong structural similarity.

Let

and
Q

(f,g)
of maps

between

and

f(p)\leq q\iff p\leq g(q)
for all

and

.
The essential properties and results on Galois connections

[
7

8
]

Originally Pawlak
[
29
, p. 351]
defined a rough set as
an equivalence class of sets which look the same in view of the knowledge restricted by the given indistinguishability relation, that is, as a class of sets having the same lower approximation and the same upper approximation. This notion generalizes in a
natural way to arbitrary binary relations.

Let
R
be a binary relation on
U
. A relation
\equiv
is
defined on
\wp(U)
by

X\equiv Y\iff X^{\blacktriangledown}=Y^{\blacktriangledown}\text{\quad and \quad}X^{\blacktriangle}=Y^{\blacktriangle}.

The equivalence classes of
\equiv
are called
rough sets
. Each element in a given rough set looks the same, when observed through the knowledge given by the knowledge
R
.
Namely, if
X\equiv Y
, then exactly the same elements belong certainly or possibly to
X
and
Y
.

The order-theoretical study of rough sets was initiated by T. B. Iwiński in
[
11
]
. In his approach, rough sets are defined as pairs
(X^{\blacktriangledown},X^{\blacktriangle})
, where
X\subseteq U
. This definition aligns with Pawlak’s original definition, because if
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\wp(U)
is an equivalence class of
\equiv
, then
\mathcal{C}
is uniquely determined by the pair
(X^{\blacktriangledown},X^{\blacktriangle})
, where
X
is any member of
\mathcal{C}
; a set
Y\subseteq U
belongs to
\mathcal{C}
if and only if
(Y^{\blacktriangledown},Y^{\blacktriangle})=(X^{\blacktriangledown},X^{\blacktriangle})
.

We denote by
\mathrm{RS}
the set of all rough sets, that is,

\mathrm{RS}:=\{(X^{\blacktriangledown},X^{\blacktriangle})\mid X\subseteq U\}.

The set
\mathrm{RS}
can be ordered coordinatewise by

(X^{\blacktriangledown},X^{\blacktriangle})\leq(Y^{\blacktriangledown},Y^{\blacktriangle})\overset{\rm def}{\iff}X^{\blacktriangledown}\subseteq Y^{\blacktriangledown}\ \text{and}\ X^{\blacktriangle}\subseteq Y^{\blacktriangle}.

It is known that
\mathrm{RS}
is not always a lattice if
R
is a
reflexive and symmetric binary relation; see
[
12
]
.

The
Dedekind–MacNeille completion
of an ordered set is the smallest complete lattice containing it; see
[
7
]
,
for example.

\mathrm{RS}
by

.
D. Umadevi

\mathrm{DM(RS)}=\{(A,B)\in\wp(U)^{\blacktriangledown}\times\wp(U)^{\blacktriangle}\mid A^{\vartriangle\blacktriangle}\subseteq B\text{ and }A\cap\mathcal{S}=B\cap\mathcal{S}\}.

Here

\mathcal{S}:=\{x\in U\mid R(x)=\{z\}\text{ for some $z\in U$}\}.

The elements of
\mathcal{S}
are called

.
This means that
x\in\mathcal{S}
if and only if

|R(x)|=1
.

Next we present two examples, which motivate the study of rough
sets defined by reflexive relations, which are the main topic
of this article.

Example 2.2
.

In this work, we primarily consider rough sets defined by
reflexive relations. Reflexive relations can be viewed as
directional similarity relations
.
Reflexivity is an evident property of similarity, as each object is inherently similar to itself.

Symmetry is not an obvious property of similarity. For instance, A. Tversky states in
[
33
]
that similarity should not be treated as a symmetric relation. Statements like “
a
is like
b
” are directional, with
a
as the subject and
b
as the referent. It is not equivalent in general to the converse similarity statement “
b
is like
a
”. Tversky also provides concrete examples, like “the portrait resembles the person” rather than “the person resembles the portrait”, and “the son resembles the father” rather than “the father resembles the son”.

It is also clear that similarity relations are not necessarily transitive. For example, we may have a finite sequence of objects
x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}
such that each pair of consecutive objects
x_{i}
and
x_{i+1}
are similar, but the objects
x_{1}
and
x_{n}
have nothing in common.

In the following example, we discuss how rough approximations, rough sets, and their operations can be viewed when defined in terms of a directional similarity relation.

Example 2.3
.

Let
R
be a directional similarity relation on
U
. If
X
is a subset of
U
, then
X^{\blacktriangledown}
consists of elements
x
such that all elements to which
x
is
R
-similar are in
X
. The set
X^{\blacktriangledown}
can therefore be viewed as the set of elements that are certainly in
X
given the knowledge
R
. Similarly, the set

X^{\blacktriangle}
can be seen as the set of elements that are possibly in
X
. If
x
is in
X^{\blacktriangle}
, then there is at least one element in

X
to which
x
is
R
-similar. The rough set
(X^{\blacktriangledown},X^{\blacktriangle})

of
X
represents how the set
X
appears when observed through the knowledge restricted by
R

From usual sets, we can form new sets using the operations of union (elements that belong to at least one of the sets), intersection (elements that belong to all sets), and complement (elements that do not belong to the set). Since
\mathrm{DM(RS)}
forms a lattice with a De Morgan complementation
\sim

The join of two rough sets
(X^{\blacktriangledown},X^{\blacktriangle})
and
(Y^{\blacktriangledown},Y^{\blacktriangle})

(X^{\blacktriangledown},X^{\blacktriangle})\vee(Y^{\blacktriangledown},Y^{\blacktriangle})=((X^{\blacktriangledown}\cup Y^{\blacktriangledown})^{\vartriangle\blacktriangledown},(X^{\blacktriangle}\cup Y^{\blacktriangle}))=((X^{\blacktriangledown\vartriangle}\cup Y^{\blacktriangledown\vartriangle})^{\blacktriangledown},(X\cup Y)^{\blacktriangle}).

Similarly, their intersection can be considered as the meet
in
\mathrm{DM(RS)}
:

(X^{\blacktriangledown},X^{\blacktriangle})\wedge(Y^{\blacktriangledown},Y^{\blacktriangle})=(X^{\blacktriangledown}\cap Y^{\blacktriangledown},(X^{\blacktriangle}\cap Y^{\blacktriangle})^{\triangledown\blacktriangle})=((X\cap Y)^{\blacktriangledown},(X^{\blacktriangle\triangledown}\cup Y^{\blacktriangle\triangledown})^{\blacktriangle}).

The complement may be defined as:

{\sim}(X^{\blacktriangledown},X^{\blacktriangle})=(X^{\blacktriangle c},X^{\blacktriangledown c})=(X^{c\blacktriangledown},X^{c\blacktriangle}).

A
subdirect product

such that the canonical projections

(A_{i},B_{i})_{i\in I}
and
(C_{j},D_{j})_{j\in J}
are subsets
of
\mathrm{DM(RS)}
, then

{\bigvee}_{i\in I}(A_{i},B_{i})\vee{\bigvee}_{j\in J}(C_{j},D_{j})=\Big(\big({\bigcup}_{i\in I}A_{i}\cup{\bigcup}_{j\in J}C_{j}\big)^{\vartriangle\blacktriangledown},\bigcup_{i\in I}B_{i}\cup\bigcup_{j\in J}D_{j}\Big).

Proof.

In this proof, equalities (i) and (ii) are based on how the joins are formed and follow from (

△
distributes over joins, we have (iii) in view of (GC4). (iv) is a plain application of (GC6). Equality (v) is again

\displaystyle{\bigvee}_{i\in I}(A_{i},B_{i})\vee{\bigvee}_{j\in J}(C_{j},D_{j})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm(i)}}{{=}}\Big(\big({\bigcup}_{i\in I}A_{i}\big)^{\vartriangle\blacktriangledown},\ {\bigcup}_{i\in I}B_{i}\Big)\vee\Big(\big({\bigcup}_{j\in J}C_{j}\big)^{\vartriangle\blacktriangledown},\ {\bigcup}_{j\in J}D_{j}\Big)

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
R
be a reflexive relation. If
(A,B)\in\mathrm{DM(RS)}
, then

(2.5)

(A,B)=\bigvee\{(\{a\}^{\vartriangle\blacktriangledown},\{a\}^{\vartriangle\blacktriangle})\mid a\in A\}\vee\bigvee\{(\{b\}^{\blacktriangledown},\{b\}^{\blacktriangle})\mid b\in B^{\triangledown}\}.

Proof.

Using Lemma
2.5
, the right side of Equation (
2.5
) can be written
in the form:

\Big(\big(\bigcup\{\{a\}^{\vartriangle\blacktriangledown}\mid a\in A\}\cup\bigcup\{\{b\}^{\blacktriangledown}\mid b\in B^{\triangledown}\}\big)^{\vartriangle\blacktriangledown},\ \bigcup\{\{a\}^{\vartriangle\blacktriangle}\mid a\in A\}\cup\bigcup\{\{b\}^{\blacktriangle}\mid b\in B^{\triangledown}\}\Big).

Let us denote this pair by
(\mathsf{L},\mathsf{R})
.
We prove that
(A,B)=(\mathsf{L},\mathsf{R})
.

First, we show that
B=\mathsf{R}
.
If
a\in A
, then
\{a\}\subseteq A
and

\{a\}^{\vartriangle\blacktriangle}\subseteq A^{\vartriangle\blacktriangle}\subseteq B
.
If
b\in B^{\triangledown}
,
then
\{b\}^{\blacktriangle}\subseteq B^{\triangledown\blacktriangle}=B
. This means that

\mathsf{R}\subseteq B
.

Suppose that

c\in B
. Because
B=B^{\triangledown\blacktriangle}=\bigcup\{\{b\}^{\blacktriangle}\mid b\in B^{\triangledown}\}
,
this means that
c\in\{b\}^{\blacktriangle}
for some
b\in B^{\triangledown}
. Thus,

c\in R
. We have proved that
\mathsf{R}=B
.

To prove
A=\mathsf{L}
, we show first that

\bigcup\{\{b\}^{\blacktriangledown}\mid b\in B^{\triangledown}\}\subseteq\bigcup\{\{a\}^{\vartriangle\blacktriangledown}\mid a\in A\}.

There are two possibilities:
\{b\}^{\blacktriangledown}=\emptyset
and

\{b\}^{\blacktriangledown}\neq\emptyset
. If
\{b\}^{\blacktriangledown}=\emptyset
for some

b\in B^{\triangledown}
, then
\bigcup\{\{b\}^{\blacktriangledown}\mid b\in B^{\triangledown}\}

does not change because of this
b
. If
\{b\}^{\blacktriangledown}\neq\emptyset
,
then this means that
\{b\}^{\blacktriangledown}=\{b\}
and
R(b)=\{b\}
. We
have that
b\in\mathcal{S}
. Hence,

b\in B\cap\mathcal{S}=A\cap\mathcal{S}\subseteq A
.
Because
\{b\}=R(b)=\{b\}^{\vartriangle}
, we have that

\{b\}^{\blacktriangledown}=\{b\}^{\vartriangle\blacktriangledown}
belongs to

\{\{a\}^{\vartriangle\blacktriangledown}\mid a\in A\}
and we have again that

\bigcup\{\{b\}^{\blacktriangledown}\mid b\in B^{\triangledown}\}\subseteq\bigcup\{\{a\}^{\vartriangle\blacktriangledown}\mid a\in A\}
. Therefore,

\bigcup\{\{a\}^{\vartriangle\blacktriangledown}\mid a\in A\}\cup\bigcup\{\{b\}^{\blacktriangledown}\mid b\in B^{\triangledown}\}=\bigcup\{\{a\}^{\vartriangle\blacktriangledown}\mid a\in A\}.

Let
b\in\bigcup\{\{a\}^{\vartriangle\blacktriangledown}\mid a\in A\}
. Then

b\in\{a\}^{\vartriangle\blacktriangledown}
for some
a\in A
and

b\in\{a\}^{\vartriangle\blacktriangledown}\subseteq A^{\vartriangle\blacktriangledown}=A
. Thus,

\bigcup\{\{a\}^{\vartriangle\blacktriangledown}\mid a\in A\}\subseteq A
which gives

\big(\bigcup\{\{a\}^{\vartriangle\blacktriangledown}\mid a\in A\}\big)^{\vartriangle\blacktriangledown}\subseteq A^{\vartriangle\blacktriangledown}=A
.
This means that
\mathsf{L}\subseteq A
.

On the other hand, if
a\in A
, then
a\in\{a\}\subseteq\{a\}^{\vartriangle\blacktriangledown}
. Thus,

a\in\bigcup\{\{a\}^{\vartriangle\blacktriangledown}\mid a\in A\}\subseteq(\bigcup\{\{a\}^{\vartriangle\blacktriangledown}\mid a\in A\})^{\vartriangle\blacktriangledown}.

We have proved that
A\subseteq\mathsf{L}
and also

\mathsf{L}=A
. This means
that equation (
2.5
) holds.
∎

3.
Completely join-irreducible elements and atoms

Completely join-irreducible elements help in understanding the structure of a lattice.
They are the building blocks in the sense that they cannot be broken down further within the lattice.
An element
j
of a complete lattice
L
is called

completely join-irreducible
if
j=\bigvee S

implies
j\in S
for every subset
S
of
L

[
7
, p. 242]
. Note that the least element
0
of
L
is not completely
join-irreducible. The set of completely join-irreducible elements of
L
is denoted by

\mathcal{J}(L)
, or simply by
\mathcal{J}
if there is no danger of confusion. Note that if
L
is finite, then
\mathcal{J}(L)
consists of those elements which
cover precisely one element.

Remark 3.1
.

For all
X\subseteq U
,
X^{\vartriangle}=\bigcup\{\{x\}^{\vartriangle}\mid x\in X\}
.
Therefore, each completely join-irreducible element of
\wp(U)^{\vartriangle}
must be of the form
\{x\}^{\vartriangle}=R(x)
for some
x\in U
.
Similarly, every completely join-irreducible element of
\wp(U)^{\blacktriangle}
must
be of the form
\{x\}^{\blacktriangle}=\breve{R}(x)
, where
x\in U
.
Hence, the completely join-irreducible elements of
\wp(U)^{\vartriangle}
are elements
R(x)
such that for all
\mathcal{H}\subseteq\wp(U)
,
R(x)=\bigcup\{Y^{\vartriangle}\mid Y\in\mathcal{H}\}
implies that
R(x)=Y^{\vartriangle}
for some
Y\in\mathcal{H}
.

Our following lemma characterizes the completely join-irreducible
elements of
\wp(U)^{\vartriangle}
in terms of
R
-neighbourhoods.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
R
be a binary relation on
U
. The following are equivalent for
X\subseteq U
:

(i)

X^{\vartriangle}
is completely join-irreducible in

\wp(U)^{\vartriangle}
;

(ii)

X^{\vartriangle}=R(x)
for some
x\in U
such that
for any
A\subseteq U
,

R(x)=\bigcup\{R(a)\mid a\in A\}
implies

R(x)=R(b)
for some
b\in A
.

Proof.

(i)
\Rightarrow
(ii):
Suppose that
X^{\vartriangle}
is completely join-irreducible in

\wp(U)^{\vartriangle}
. By Remark
3.1
,

X^{\vartriangle}=R(x)
for some
x\in U
such that
for all
\mathcal{H}\subseteq\wp(U)
,

R(x)=\bigcup\{Y^{\vartriangle}\mid Y\in\mathcal{H}\}
implies that

R(x)=Y^{\vartriangle}
for some
Y\in\mathcal{H}
.
Let
R(x)=\bigcup\{R(a)\mid a\in A\}
for some

A\subseteq U
. Because each
R(a)=\{a\}^{\vartriangle}
belongs to

\wp(U)^{\vartriangle}
, we have
R(x)=R(b)
for some
b\in A
.

(ii)
\Rightarrow
(i): Let
X^{\vartriangle}=R(x)
be such that (ii) holds.
Assume that
R(x)=\bigcup\{Y^{\vartriangle}\mid Y\in\mathcal{H}\}

for some
\mathcal{H}\subseteq\wp(U)
. Since

\bigcup\mathcal{H}=\bigcup\{Y\mid Y\in\mathcal{H}\}
,
we have

\big(\bigcup\mathcal{H})^{\vartriangle}=\big(\bigcup\{Y\mid Y\in\mathcal{H}\}\big)^{\vartriangle}=\bigcup\{Y^{\vartriangle}\mid Y\in\mathcal{H}\}
. Hence, our assumption
is equivalent to
R(x)=\big(\bigcup\mathcal{H}\big)^{\vartriangle}
.

It is easy to see that any set
X\subseteq U
satisfies

X^{\vartriangle}=\bigcup\{\{a\}^{\vartriangle}\mid a\in X\}
. By this,

R(x)=\bigcup\{\{a\}^{\vartriangle}\mid a\in\bigcup\mathcal{H}\}=\bigcup\{R(a)\mid a\in\bigcup\mathcal{H}\}.

Because (ii) holds, we have that
R(x)=R(a)
for some
a\in\bigcup\mathcal{H}
.
Hence, there is
Y\in\mathcal{H}
such that
a\in Y
. We get

R(x)=R(a)=\{a\}^{\vartriangle}\subseteq Y^{\vartriangle}\subseteq\big({\bigcup}\mathcal{H}\big)^{\vartriangle}=R(x).

This means that
R(x)=Y^{\vartriangle}
and therefore
X^{\vartriangle}=R(x)
is
completely join-irreducible in
\wp(U)^{\vartriangle}
.
∎

The following corollary is obviously true, because
\breve{R}
is reflexive whenever
R
is. Therefore, this corollary follows directly from Lemma
3.2
.

Corollary 3.3
.

Let
R
be a binary relation on
U
.
The following are equivalent for any
x\in U
:

(i)

X^{\blacktriangle}
is completely join-irreducible in

\wp(U)^{\blacktriangle}
;

(ii)

X^{\blacktriangle}=\breve{R}(x)
for some
x\in U
such that
for any
A\subseteq U
,

\breve{R}(x)=\bigcup\{\breve{R}(a)\mid a\in A\}
implies

\breve{R}(x)=\breve{R}(b)
for some
b\in A
.

In the rest of this section, we assume that
R
is a reflexive
binary relation on
U
. This is equivalent
to the fact that
x\in R(x)
. Note also that this means
that
x\in\mathcal{S}
if and only if
R(x)=\{x\}
.
In addition, if
x\in\mathcal{S}
, then

\{x\}\subseteq\{x\}^{\vartriangle\blacktriangledown}=R(x)^{\blacktriangledown}=\{x\}^{\blacktriangledown}\subseteq\{x\}
. Thus,
R(x)^{\blacktriangledown}=\{x\}
and, in particular,

(R(x)^{\blacktriangledown},R(x)^{\blacktriangle})=(\{x\},\{x\}^{\blacktriangle})
.
Our following lemma is technical in nature. We will need these properties in what follows.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
R
be a reflexive relation on
U
and

s\in\mathcal{S}
.

(i)

If
s\in\{b\}^{\blacktriangle}
, then
b=s
;

(ii)

\{s\}^{\blacktriangle}\in\mathcal{J}(\wp(U)^{\blacktriangle})
;

(iii)

\{s\}=\{s\}^{\vartriangle}\in\mathcal{J}(\wp(U)^{\vartriangle})
.

Proof.

(i) As
\{b\}^{\blacktriangle}=\breve{R}(b)
,
s\in\{b\}^{\blacktriangle}
gives

b\in R(s)=\{s\}
, that is,
b=s
.

(ii) Suppose
\{s\}^{\blacktriangle}=\bigcup_{a\in A}\{a\}^{\blacktriangle}
for some

A\subseteq U
. Because
s\in\{s\}^{\blacktriangle}
,
s\in\{a\}^{\blacktriangle}
for some

a\in A
. By (i),
s=a
. Thus,
\{x\}^{\blacktriangle}=\{a\}^{\blacktriangle}
and

\{s\}^{\blacktriangle}
is completely join-irreducible in
\wp(U)^{\blacktriangle}

by Corollary
3.3
,

(iii) Let
\{s\}=R(s)=\{s\}^{\vartriangle}=\bigcup_{a\in A}\{a\}^{\vartriangle}

for some
A\subseteq U
. Because the size of each
\{a\}^{\vartriangle}
is at
least one, there exists
a\in A
such that
\{s\}^{\vartriangle}=\{a\}^{\vartriangle}
.
∎

Our next proposition shows that the completely join-irreducible
elements of
\wp(U)^{\vartriangle}
and
\wp(U)^{\blacktriangle}
determine completely
join-irreducible elements of
\mathrm{DM(RS)}
.

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
R
be a reflexive relation on
U
and
x\in U
.

(i)

If
x\notin\mathcal{S}
and
\{x\}^{\blacktriangle}
is completely join-irreducible in
\wp(U)^{\blacktriangle}
, then

(\{x\}^{\blacktriangledown},\{x\}^{\blacktriangle})
is completely join-irreducible in

\mathrm{DM(RS)}
.

(ii)

If
\{x\}^{\vartriangle}
is is completely join-irreducible in
\wp(U)^{\vartriangle}
, then
(\{x\}^{\vartriangle\blacktriangledown},\{x\}^{\vartriangle\blacktriangle})
is completely join-irreducible in
\mathrm{DM(RS)}
.

Proof.

(i) Because
x\notin\mathcal{S}
,
R(x)\nsubseteq\{x\}
and so

\{x\}^{\blacktriangledown}=\emptyset
. Thus,

(\{x\}^{\blacktriangledown},\{x\}^{\blacktriangle})=(\emptyset,\{x\}^{\blacktriangle})
. Suppose
that
(\emptyset,\{x\}^{\blacktriangle})=\bigvee_{i\in I}(A_{i},B_{i})
for some index set
I
and

(A_{i},B_{i})_{i\in I}\subseteq\mathrm{DM(RS)}
. Since

\emptyset=\bigvee_{i\in I}A_{i}=(\bigcup_{i\in I}A_{i})^{\vartriangle\blacktriangledown}\supseteq\bigcup_{i\in I}A_{i}
, we have
A_{i}=\emptyset
for all
i\in I
.

Now
\{x\}^{\blacktriangle}=\bigvee B_{i}
gives that
\{x\}^{\blacktriangle}=B_{k}
for some

k\in I
by Lemma
3.2
, because
\{x\}^{\blacktriangle}
is completely join-irreducible in
\wp(U)^{\blacktriangle}
.
Therefore,
(A_{k},B_{k})=(\emptyset,\{x\}^{\blacktriangle})
and

(\emptyset,\{x\}^{\blacktriangle})
is completely join-irreducible.

(ii) Let
\{x\}^{\vartriangle}
be completely join-irreducible in
\wp(U)^{\vartriangle}
.
Suppose that

(\{x\}^{\vartriangle\blacktriangledown},\{x\}^{\vartriangle\blacktriangle})={\bigvee}_{i\in I}(A_{i},B_{i})=\big(({\bigcup}_{i\in I}A_{i})^{\vartriangle\blacktriangledown},{\bigcup}_{i\in I}B_{i}\big).

This means that

\{x\}^{\vartriangle\blacktriangledown}=({\bigcup}_{i\in I}A_{i})^{\vartriangle\blacktriangledown}.

We obtain

\{x\}^{\vartriangle}=\{x\}^{\vartriangle\blacktriangledown\vartriangle}=({\bigcup}_{i\in I}A_{i})^{\vartriangle\blacktriangledown\vartriangle}=({\bigcup}_{i\in I}A_{i})^{\vartriangle}={\bigcup}_{i\in I}{A_{i}}^{\vartriangle}.

Because
\{x\}^{\vartriangle}
is completely join-irreducible in
\wp(U)^{\vartriangle}
,
\{x\}^{\vartriangle}={A_{k}}^{\vartriangle}
for some
k\in I
. This implies

\{x\}^{\vartriangle\blacktriangledown}={A_{k}}^{\vartriangle\blacktriangledown}=A_{k}
. Note that

A_{k}\in\wp(A)^{\blacktriangledown}
.

The fact
{A_{k}}^{\vartriangle\blacktriangle}\subseteq B_{k}
implies

\{x\}^{\vartriangle\blacktriangle}={A_{k}}^{\vartriangle\blacktriangle}\subseteq B_{k}.

On the other hand,
\{x\}^{\vartriangle\blacktriangle}={\bigcup}_{i\in I}B_{i}\supseteq B_{k}
.
Thus,
\{x\}^{\vartriangle\blacktriangle}=B_{k}
. We have proved that

(\{x\}^{\vartriangle\blacktriangledown},\{x\}^{\vartriangle\blacktriangle})=(A_{k},B_{k})
, that is,
(\{x\}^{\vartriangle\blacktriangledown},\{x\}^{\vartriangle\blacktriangle})
is completely
join-irreducible.
∎

We can now write the following characterization of complete join-irreducible
elements.

Theorem 3.6
.

Let
R
be a reflexive relation on
U
. The set of completely join-irreducible
elements of
\mathrm{DM(RS)}
is

(3.1)

\displaystyle\{(\{x\}^{\vartriangle\blacktriangledown},\{x\}^{\vartriangle\blacktriangle})\mid\text{$\{x\}^{\vartriangle}$ is completely join-irreducible in $\wp(U)^{\vartriangle}$}\}

(3.2)

\displaystyle\cup\ \{(\{x\}^{\blacktriangledown},\{x\}^{\blacktriangle})\mid\text{$\{x\}^{\blacktriangle}$ is completely join-irreducible in $\wp(U)^{\blacktriangle}$
and $x\notin\mathcal{S}$}\}.

Proof.

In view of Proposition
3.5
,
elements in sets (
3.1
) and (
3.2
) are
completely join-irreducible in
\mathrm{DM(RS)}
.

Conversely, assume now that
(A,B)
is a completely join-irreducible
element in
\mathrm{DM(RS)}
. Then, in view of Lemma
2.6
,
(i)
(A,B)=(\{a\}^{\vartriangle\blacktriangledown},\{a\}^{\vartriangle\blacktriangle})
for some
a\in A
or
(ii)
(A,B)=(\{b\}^{\blacktriangledown},\{b\}^{\blacktriangle})
, where

b\in B^{\triangledown}\subseteq B
.

(i) Clearly, in the case
(A,B)=(\{a\}^{\vartriangle\blacktriangledown},\{a\}^{\vartriangle\blacktriangle})

we have to prove only that
\{a\}^{\vartriangle}
is completely join-irreducible
in
\wp(U)^{\vartriangle}
. This can be done by using the equivalence of
Lemma
3.2
.
Assume that
R(a)=\bigcup\{R(c)\mid c\in C\}
for some
C\subseteq U
.
This gives that

\displaystyle\{a\}^{\vartriangle\blacktriangledown}=R(a)^{\blacktriangledown}

\displaystyle=\big(\bigcup\{R(c)\mid c\in C\}\big)^{\blacktriangledown}=\big(\bigcup\{\{c\}^{\vartriangle}\mid c\in C\}\big)^{\blacktriangledown}

\displaystyle=\big(\bigcup\{\{c\}^{\vartriangle\blacktriangledown\vartriangle}\mid c\in C\}\big)^{\blacktriangledown}=\big(\bigcup\{\{c\}^{\vartriangle\blacktriangledown}\mid c\in C\}\big)^{\vartriangle\blacktriangledown}.

Similarly,

\displaystyle\{a\}^{\vartriangle\blacktriangle}=R(a)^{\blacktriangle}

\displaystyle=\big(\bigcup\{R(c)\mid c\in C\}\big)^{\blacktriangle}=\bigcup\{R(c)^{\blacktriangle}\mid c\in C\}

\displaystyle=\bigcup\{\{c\}^{\vartriangle\blacktriangle}\mid c\in C\}

This means that

\displaystyle(\{a\}^{\vartriangle\blacktriangledown},\{a\}^{\vartriangle\blacktriangle})

\displaystyle=\Big(\big(\bigcup\{\{c\}^{\vartriangle\blacktriangledown}\mid c\in C\}\big)^{\vartriangle\blacktriangledown},\bigcup\{\{c\}^{\vartriangle\blacktriangle}\mid c\in C\}\Big)

\displaystyle=\bigvee\big\{(\{c\}^{\vartriangle\blacktriangledown},\{c\}^{\vartriangle\blacktriangle})\mid c\in C\big\}.

Since
(\{a\}^{\vartriangle\blacktriangledown},\{a\}^{\vartriangle\blacktriangle})
is
completely join-irreducible,

(\{a\}^{\vartriangle\blacktriangledown},\{a\}^{\vartriangle\blacktriangle})=(\{c\}^{\vartriangle\blacktriangledown},\{c\}^{\vartriangle\blacktriangle})
for some
c\in C
.
Therefore,
\{a\}^{\vartriangle\blacktriangledown}=\{c\}^{\vartriangle\blacktriangledown}
and

\{a\}^{\vartriangle}=\{a\}^{\vartriangle\blacktriangledown\vartriangle}=\{c\}^{\vartriangle\blacktriangledown\vartriangle}=\{c\}^{\vartriangle}
.
This proves that
\{a\}^{\vartriangle}
is completely join-irreducible
in
\wp(U)^{\vartriangle}
.

(ii) Suppose that
(A,B)=(\{b\}^{\blacktriangledown},\{b\}^{\blacktriangle})
, where

b\in B^{\triangledown}
. If
b\in\mathcal{S}
, then

\{b\}^{\vartriangle}=R(b)=\{b\}
gives that

(\{b\}^{\blacktriangledown},\{b\}^{\blacktriangle})=(\{b\}^{\vartriangle\blacktriangledown},\{b\}^{\vartriangle\blacktriangle})
.
So, this case is already covered by part (i).

If
b\notin\mathcal{S}
, then

(A,B)=(\{b\}^{\blacktriangledown},\{b\}^{\blacktriangle})=(\emptyset,\{b\}^{\blacktriangle})
.
We show that
\{b\}^{\blacktriangle}
is completely join-irreducible in

\wp(U)^{\blacktriangle}
. Suppose that

\{b\}^{\blacktriangle}=\breve{R}(b)=\ \bigcup\{\breve{R}(d)\mid d\in D\}

for some
D\subseteq U
. Since
\breve{R}
is reflexive, we
have that
d\in\breve{R}(d)\subseteq\breve{R}(b)
for all
d\in D
.
Thus,
b\in R(d)
for each
d\in D
. Observe that this yields

d\notin\mathcal{S}
for every
d\in D
.
Indeed,
d\in\mathcal{S}
and
b\in R(d)
would imply

b=d\in\mathcal{S}
, contradicting
b\notin\mathcal{S}
.
Therefore,
\{d\}^{\blacktriangledown}=\emptyset
for all
d\in D
.

We can now write

{\bigvee}_{d\in D}(\{d\}^{\blacktriangledown},\{d\}^{\blacktriangle})=\Big(\big(\bigcup\{d\}^{\blacktriangledown}\big)^{\vartriangle\blacktriangledown},\bigcup\{d\}^{\blacktriangle}\Big)=\Big(\emptyset^{\vartriangle\blacktriangledown},\bigcup\{d\}^{\blacktriangle}\Big)=\Big(\emptyset,\bigcup\breve{R}(d)\Big).

Since

, we have

{\bigvee}_{d\in D}(\{d\}^{\blacktriangledown},\{d\}^{\blacktriangle})=(\emptyset,\{b\}^{\blacktriangle}).

Because
(\emptyset,\{b\}^{\blacktriangle})
is completely
join-irreducible in
\mathrm{DM(RS)}
, we get

(\emptyset,\{b\}^{\blacktriangle})=(\{d\}^{\blacktriangledown},\{d\}^{\blacktriangle})=(\emptyset,\{d\}^{\blacktriangle})
for some

.
This yields
\breve{R}(b)=\{b\}^{\blacktriangle}=\{d\}^{\blacktriangle}=\breve{R}(d)
.
By Corollary
3.3
,

\{b\}^{\blacktriangle}
is completely join-irreducible in
\wp(U)^{\blacktriangle}
.
∎

We will now present two examples. It is well-known that rough set lattice

. By

Example 3.7
.

Let
R
be a reflexive relation on
U=\{1,2,3\}

such that

R(1)=\{1\}^{\vartriangle}=\{1,2,3\},\quad R(2)=\{2\}^{\vartriangle}=\{2\},\quad R(3)=\{3\}^{\vartriangle}=\{1,3\}.

Note that the relation
R
is not symmetric, because
(1,2)\in R
, but
(2,1)\notin R
. Similarly,
R
is not transitive, because
(3,1)\in R
and
(1,2)\in R
,
but
(3,2)\notin R
.

4.
On complete distributivity

The idea behind complete distributivity is that it extends the usual distributive property of lattices to arbitrary joins and meets. In a completely distributive lattice, the order of taking joins and meets can be interchanged in a very general way, which is not necessarily true in ordinary distributive lattices.
A complete lattice
L
is
completely distributive

if for any doubly indexed
subset
\{x_{i,\,j}\}_{i\in I,\,j\in J}
of
L
, we have

\bigwedge_{i\in I}\Big(\bigvee_{j\in J}x_{i,\,j}\Big)=\bigvee_{f\colon I\to J}\Big(\bigwedge_{i\in I}x_{i,\,f(i)}\Big),

that is, any meet of joins may be converted into the join of all
possible elements obtained by taking the meet over
i\in I
of
elements
x_{i,\,k}
, where
k
depends on
i

[
7
, p. 239]
.

5.

As we mentioned in the introduction,

A

(L,\vee,\wedge,{\sim},0,1)
is
an algebra such that

{\sim}(x\vee y)={\sim}x\wedge{\sim}y\text{ \ and \ }{\sim}(x\wedge y)={\sim}x\vee{\sim}y.

Note that this means that
\sim
is an order-isomorphism
between
(L,\leq)
and
(L,\geq)
.

We say that a De Morgan algebra is
completely distributive
if its underlying lattice is completely distributive.
Let
(L,\vee,\wedge,{\sim},0,1)
be a completely
distributive De Morgan algebra. We define for any

the element

(5.1)

This

A

is a De Morgan algebra

\displaystyle=\{j\in\mathcal{J}\mid j<g(j)\};

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}^{\circ}

\displaystyle=\{j\in\mathcal{J}\mid j=g(j)\};

\displaystyle=\{j\in\mathcal{J}\mid j>g(j)\}.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let

Proof.

. Then,

, that is,

(ii) If

is such that

, then

, because
g(j)

is the least element which is not below
{\sim}j
.
If

, then

.

and we must have

On the other, if

, then

.

A

is a bounded lattice such that for all

This element

is the
relative pseudocomplement
of

with
respect to
b
, and it is denoted by
a\Rightarrow b
. It is well known that
any completely distributive lattice

determines a Heyting algebra

(L,\vee,\wedge,\Rightarrow,0,1)
.

According to R. Cignoli

of

with respect to

(5.2)

exists. This means that every Kleene algebra whose underlying lattice is a Heyting algebra forms a quasi-Nelson algebra. In particular,
any Kleene algebra defined on a completely distributive lattice is a

(\emptyset,\{4\})\vee(\emptyset,\{1,2\})=(\emptyset,\{1,2,4\}),

which equals
(\{2,4\}^{\blacktriangledown},\{2,4\}^{\blacktriangle})
.

Intersection:

The meet of
(\{1,3,4\}^{\blacktriangledown},\{1,3,4\}^{\blacktriangle})=(\{3,4\},U)
and
(\{1,2,3\}^{\blacktriangledown},\{1,2,3\}^{\blacktriangle})=(\{1,2,3\},U)
is

(\{3,4\},U)\wedge(\{1,2,3\},U)=(\{3\},U),

which is the same as the rough set
(\{1,3\}^{\blacktriangledown},\{1,3\}^{\blacktriangle})
.

Complement:

The De Morgan complement of
(\{2,4\}^{\blacktriangledown},\{2,4\}^{\blacktriangle})=(\emptyset,\{1,2,4\})
is

{\sim}(\emptyset,\{1,2,4\})=(\{3\},U),

which is equal to
(\{1,3\}^{\blacktriangledown},\{1,3\}^{\blacktriangle})
.

Let us consider the map
g
. Now,

\displaystyle g(\emptyset,\{4\})=(\{3,4\},U),\quad g(\emptyset,\{1,2\})=(\{1\},\{124\}),\quad g(\{3\},\{2,3,4\})=(\{3\},\{2,3,4\}).

This means that

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}^{-}

\displaystyle=\{(\emptyset,\{4\}),(\emptyset,\{1,2\})\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}^{\circ}

\displaystyle=\{(\{3\},\{2,3,4\})\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}^{+}

\displaystyle=\{(\{3,4\},U),(\{1\},\{1,2\,4\})\}.

Because
\mathrm{DM(RS)}
does not form a Nelson algebra, the
interpolation property should not hold. This can seen by setting

p=(\emptyset,\{4\})
and
q=(\emptyset,\{1,2\})
.
Now
p,q<g(p),g(q)
, but there is no element
k\in\mathcal{J}

such that
p,q\leq k\leq g(p),g(q)
.

The following proposition can be considered
as a corollary of Theorem
5.6
.

Proposition 5.9
.

Let
R
be a reflexive relation on
U
such that

\mathfrak{core}R(x)
and
\mathfrak{core}\breve{R}(x)

are nonempty for each

, then

\mathrm{DM(RS)}
forms a Nelson algebra.

Proof.

Let
R
be a reflexive relation on
U
such that

\mathfrak{core}R(x)
and
\mathfrak{core}\breve{R}(x)

are nonempty for each

.
Then each

\breve{R}(x)

It is now clear that condition (ii) of Theorem

6.
Some concluding remarks

In this work, we have especially studied the rough set systems defined by reflexive binary relations. We have shown how reflexive relations can be interpreted as directional similarities. Even though
\mathrm{RS}
defined by a reflexive relation does not form a lattice, the completion
\mathrm{DM(RS)}
always is a lattice. Therefore, studying its lattice-theoretical properties opens a new view to rough set theory. In particular, we can perform computations on rough sets because their “union” “intersection”, and “completion” always exist, as we have shown in Examples
2.3
and
5.8
.

In conclusion, we highlight a significant property of rough sets induced by equivalences or quasi-orders: they enable the definition of Nelson algebras. These algebras, which can be viewed as enriched Heyting algebras or as a subvariety of residuated lattices, allow the application of both intuitionistic logic-based and substructural logic-based deduction methods.

Until now, such rich algebraic structures were not known in the case of general binary relations. Our results show that, under certain conditions, even in the case of reflexive binary relations, the induced rough set systems form Nelson algebras. This makes it possible to extend known deduction methods to more general rough set systems. An example of such a rough set lattice can be seen in Example
5.7
.
