Effectiveness of locomotion training in patients with rheumatoid arthritis: a prospective clinical trial
Authors: Takeshi Mochizuki, Koichiro Yano, Katsunori Ikari, Ken Okazaki
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Abstract [Purpose] The present study aimed to determine the effectiveness of locomotion training on physical function and quality of life in patients with rheumatoid arthritis (RA) and identify factors associated with its effectiveness. [Participants and Methods] Data were obtained from 103 patients with rheumatoid arthritis who underwent locomotion training for 6 months using the Health Assessment Questionnaire Disability Index, pain visual analog scale, 10-meter walking speed, timed up-and-go test, single-leg standing time, grip strength, 25-question Geriatric Locomotive Function Scale, Short Form-8, fat-free mass index, and fat mass index. [Results] A significant improvement in the Health Assessment Questionnaire Disability Index, pain visual analog scale score, maximum 10-meter walking speed, timed up-and-go test, single-leg standing time, grip strength, and physical component summary in Short Form-8 was observed at six months. [Conclusion] The current study demonstrated that locomotion training for 6 months improved physical function, certain aspects of quality of life, and sarcopenia in patients with RA. We believe that locomotion training should be recommended as a non-pharmacological therapeutic option for improving physical function in patients with RA.Keywords: Locomotion training, Physical function, Rheumatoid arthritis

INTRODUCTION

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is an inflammatory autoimmune disease that causes pain, swelling, stiffness, and loss of function in the joints throughout the body. Studies have shown that over time, patients with RA exhibit a worsening of the Health Assessment Questionnaire Disability Index (HAQ-DI), an indicator of physical function1, 2). As such, one of the treatment goals for RA is improving quality of life (QOL)3). Moreover, the European League Against Rheumatism (EULAR) recommends exercise for pain management in patients with inflammatory arthritis4). In line with this, evidence has shown that exercise therapy improves the HAQ-DI, pain, and disease activity of RA5).

The Japanese Orthopedic Association recommends locomotion training to improve and sustain standing and gait functions in middle-aged and old people. Previous studies have shown that locomotion training improves physical function, which includes mobility, strength, balance, and gait6). We believe that locomotion training is a suitable exercise therapy for patients with RA given its low intensity. According to our previous pilot study, there have been only few cases demonstrating the effectiveness of the locomotion training7). Moreover, patients with RA are more prone to sarcopenia than healthy individuals, with 20%–44% of patients with RA having sarcopenia8). In our previous study, the sarcopenia rate was 29.6% among patients with RA aged ≥65 years9). In patients with RA, there have been insufficient studies about the effectiveness of low-intensity exercise on sarcopenia.

The present study aimed to determine the effectiveness of locomotion training on physical function, QOL, and sarcopenia in patients with RA and identify factors associated with its effectiveness.

PARTICIPANTS AND METHODS

The inclusion criteria were as follows: fulfilled the 1987 American College of Rheumatology (ACR) classification criteria or the 2010 ACR/EULAR criteria10, 11), low disease activity in Disease Activity Score in 28 joints with erythrocyte sedimentation rate (DAS28-ESR) or Clinical Disease Activity Index (CDAI), and independent walking. The exclusion criteria included having exercise habits, dementia, heart disease (severe valvular disease or New York Heart Association Functional Classification ≥II), lung disease (forced vital capacity <60% or high-resolution computed tomography findings of grade 3 interstitial lung disease12)), severe cerebral disease, and/or joint surgeries. As a sample selection method, informed consent was obtained from consecutive patients who met the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Subsequently, the patients who provided consent were enrolled in this study.

Locomotion training was performed for 6 months. This study investigated changes in physical function, QOL, and body composition at 3 and 6 months.

This study was conducted in accordance with the principles stated in the Declaration of Helsinki and written informed consent was obtained from all the patients. The Ethics Committee for Clinical Research of Kamagaya General Hospital approved this study (approval number: TGE01619-064).

Locomotion training13) for patients was provided on a one-to-one basis by a physical therapist. The patients performed the locomotion training at home for 6 months. The locomotion training that was provided by the Japanese Orthopedic Association consists of single-leg standing with eyes open (1 min for each leg×3 sets per day), squatting (5 times×3 sets per day), heel raises (10 times×3 sets per day), and forward lunges (10 times×3 sets per day). The exercise should always be performed while holding something to prevent falls using catching the table.

Physical function and QOL assessments were performed using the HAQ-DI, pain visual analog scale (VAS) of joints, 10-meter (m) walking speed, timed up-and-go (TUG) test, single-leg standing time, grip strength, 25-question Geriatric Locomotive Function Scale (GLFS-25), and Short From-8 (SF-8) at baseline, and at 3 and 6 months. The grip strength was recorded as mean value of two measurements. The HAQ-DI, which scored from 0 to 3, with higher scores indicating greater disability, is a standard patient-reported assessment tool that measures physical disability14). This tool includes questions related to 20 activities of daily living, such as dressing, grooming, arising, eating, walking, maintaining hygiene, reaching objects, maintaining grip, opening things, and performing daily activities. The 10-m walking speed (m/s) and TUG test time (s) were measured at comfortable and maximum walking speeds twice, after which the longer time was recorded. The TUG test recorded the time required by the patient to stand up from a chair, walk 3 m, cross a line marked on the floor, turn around, walk back, and sit down on the chair15). The single-leg standing time was measured with the subjects’ eyes open for both right and left legs twice, after which the longer time was recorded16). The GLFS-25 was designed to identify individuals with high-risk conditions who may soon require care services owing to problems with the locomotive organs and tissues. The GLFS-25 is a self-administered measure comprising 25 items, which are scored from 0 (no impairment) to 4 points (severe impairment), with the scores being added to obtain a total score ranging from 0 to 10017). The SF-8 is an alternate form of the SF-36 that is widely used to assess health-related QOL18). The SF-8 includes eight items: general health, physical functioning, role physical, bodily pain, vitality, social functioning, mental health, and role emotional. It also includes two summaries: the physical component summary (PCS) and the mental component summary (MCS). This study used the SF-8 given that its brevity minimizes administration time and respondent burden. Norm-based scoring was used for analysis. The physical function of the patients was evaluated by physical therapists.

Body composition was measured using bioelectrical impedance analysis on a MC-780A Body Composition Analyzer (TANITA, Tokyo, Japan), which estimates segmental body composition including fat-free mass (FFM) and fat mass (FM). The fat-free mass index (FFMI) and fat mass index (FMI) were calculated by dividing the FFM and FM by the square of the patient’s height (kg/m2).

Sarcopenia was defined according to the consensus report of the Asian Working Group for Sarcopenia, which recommended cutoff values for handgrip strength of <28 kg for males and <18 kg for females, usual gait speed of <1.0 m/s, and skeletal muscle mass index calculated by dividing the skeletal muscle mass by the square of the patient’s height as muscle mass measurements of <7.0 kg/m2 for males and <5.7 kg/m2 for females using bioelectrical impedance analysis19). Sarcopenia is defined as the low handgrip strength or slow usual gait speed and low muscle mass and severe sarcopenia as low handgrip strength, slow usual gait speed, and low muscle mass in individuals aged ≥65 years.

In statistical analyses, paired t-tests were used to compare C-reactive protein, DAS28-ESR, HAQ-DI, pain VAS, 10-m walking speed, TUG test, single-leg standing time, grip strength, GLFS-25 score, PCS and MCS in SF-8, FFMI, and FMI at baseline, 3 months, and 6 months in all patients and those with physical dysfunction (HAQ-DI >0.5). In patients with physical dysfunction, factors associated with improvement in the HAQ-DI were evaluated by comparing patients with and without improvements in the HAQ-DI. Improvement in the HAQ-DI was defined using the minimal clinically important difference (MCID) of the HAQ-DI. The MCID is defined as 0.2220). The rate of improvement in the HAQ-DI was evaluated in patients with physical dysfunction. Cutoff values of the HAQ-DI at baseline associated with an improvement in the HAQ-DI at 3 and 6 months were determined using receiver operating characteristic analysis with the corresponding sensitivity and specificity and area under the curve (AUC). In older patients with RA aged ≥65 years, the changes in proportions of low muscle mass, low handgrip strength, slow usual gait speed, sarcopenia, and severe sarcopenia were analyzed. All analyses were performed using the R Statistical Package, version 3.3.2 (http://www.r-project.org/), with a p-value of <0.05 indicating statistical significance.

DISCUSSION

The present 6-month study is an intervention study, which evaluated the effects of locomotion training, a home-based training program consisting of low-intensity exercises, on physical function and QOL in patients with RA. Our findings revealed that locomotion training effectively improved the physical function and QOL, including the HAQ-DI, 10-m walking speed, TUG test, single-leg standing time, and PCS in SF-8, in patients with RA, many of whom were older (mean and median age: 68.4 and 71 years). Locomotion training is simple and easy to perform by the elderly. In fact, one study showed that locomotion training for 6 months significantly improved single-leg standing time and grip strength but not walking speed, GLFS-25, and Euro QOL among community-dwelling individuals aged ≥60 years old with sarcopenia and pre-sarcopenia (mean age: 69.2 years)21). Similarly, another study found that locomotion training for 3 months significantly improved single-leg standing time and GLFS-25 among older participants aged ≥65 years old (median age: 74 years)22). Based on our previous reports, we believe that locomotion training can contribute to the improvement in physical function and QOL among elderly patients with RA.

The present study found no improvement in the GLFS-25. Notably, our study found a median GLFS-25 of 6 at baseline, whereas a previous study23) found a median value of 10 at baseline. Another study found that locomotion training improved the GLFS-25 among middle-aged workers (mean age: 46.0 years)24). To improve the GLFS-25, locomotion training should be initiated at a younger age and among those with the lowest possible GLFS-25 scores.

Locomotion training significantly improved pain VAS. One of the factors for the decrease in disease activity is presumed to be improvement in pain VAS. Although we could not evaluate levels of inflammatory cytokines, a study on mice with collagen-induced arthritis showed that low-intensity exercise decreased inflammatory cytokines such as tumor necrosis factor-α25). We believe that exercise therapy affects the improvement of conditions in RA.

The FMI was identified as a factor associated with the improvement in the HAQ-DI. Studies have shown that the FMI23), along with the percentage of fat mass26), increases gradually with age. As such, systematic reviews and meta-analyses recommend that people with rheumatic and musculoskeletal diseases maintain a healthy body weight to avoid poor outcomes27). Patients with a low HAQ-DI (i.e.,<0.625) may not benefit from locomotion training. In order to observe improvements in the HAQ-DI with locomotion training, patients should have a low FM and a HAQ-DI of ≥0.625. Conversely, age and duration of RA were not associated with improvements in the HAQ-DI. A previous report found that concurrent strength and endurance training was effective in patients with both early (mean disease duration, 2.9 years) and chronic (mean disease duration, 14.5 years) RA28). The EULAR recommendations suggest that it is never too late to start exercise29). In this study, the older patients who performed the locomotion training had improved the proportions of low muscle mass and sarcopenia. Locomotion training has been shown to be effective in improving physical function tests such as single-leg standing, 10-m maximal gait speed, and knee extension strength in older people13). We believe that the locomotion training should proceed regardless of age or duration of RA. However, patients with RA having severe sarcopenia need to consider different exercises instead of locomotion training.

The present study has some limitations worth noting. First, given that this study did not have a control group, we could not clarify the differences between patients with RA and the general population. However, this study confirmed the effectiveness of locomotion training in patients with RA. Second, our patients showed a relatively low HAQ-DI and suppressed RA disease activity. Therefore, it remains unclear whether similar results can be obtained in patients with a high HAQ-DI and disease activity. Third, this study included only a limited number of patients with physical dysfunction, and different results might have been obtained had the number of patients with physical dysfunction been larger. However, we believe that the benefits of locomotion training and factors associated with improvements in the HAQ-DI have useful implications in daily clinical practice. Finally, this study had a follow-up period of 6 months. As such, the effects of locomotion training over a longer observation period remain unknown. Future studies are warranted to determine the effectiveness of locomotion training over a longer observation period.

In conclusion, the current study demonstrated that locomotion training for 6 months improved physical function, certain aspects of QOL, and sarcopenia in patients with RA. Therefore, we believe that locomotion training should be recommended as a non-pharmacological therapeutic option for improving physical function in patients with RA.
