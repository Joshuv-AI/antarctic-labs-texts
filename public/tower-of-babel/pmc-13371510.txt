Psychometric properties of the second version of the hospital survey on patient safety culture across a regional hospital system of Brazil
Authors: Richard Muñoz, Danilo Oliveira, Alejandro Arrieta
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionThe World Health Organization emphasizes regular patient safety culture (PSC) measurement for safer healthcare. While global interest grows, PSC awareness and measurement lags in Latin America. This study tackles the need for a validated PSC tool in Brazil. It aims to evaluate the psychometric properties of the Brazilian Portuguese version of the Hospital Survey on Patient Safety Culture v2 (HSOPSC v2) within a regional Brazilian hospital system.MethodsThe survey was distributed to 4,152 clinical and non-clinical staff across eight private non-profit hospitals in Brazil between April and May 2023. Construct validity was assessed using confirmatory factor analyses (CFA) and exploratory structural equation modeling (ESEM). Reliability was evaluated through Cronbach’s alphas for CFA models and McDonald’s Omega for ESEM models. Convergent validity was tested by regressing HSOPSC v2 patient safety outcomes on factor scores of the best-fitting model. Ethical considerations were addressed through anonymous survey administration to ensure respondent privacy and encourage truthful responses.ResultsOut of 2,434 respondents (59% response rate), nurses (39%), females (72%), and those with multiple jobs (75%) were most represented. A bifactor ESEM model best fit the data, with all HSOPSC v2 items significantly linked to the general PSC factor. “Communication about Error,” “Response to Error,” and “Staffing and Work Pace” showed the most influence on overall PSC. While reliability was generally strong, “Staffing and Work Pace” and “Organizational Learning” showed very weak reliability. Convergent validity was supported by significant links between model factors and patient safety outcomes, with the general factor explaining the most variance in patient safety grade (28.3%).ConclusionThe HMA survey, adapted from the HSOPSC v2, demonstrates strong psychometric properties, making it a valid and reliable instrument for measuring PSC in the Brazilian healthcare context. The bifactor ESEM model’s superior fit suggests the presence of a general PSC factor alongside 10 dimensions of PSC. The significant associations between model factors and patient safety outcomes highlight the survey’s practical relevance. These findings support the use of the HSOPSC v2 survey for PSC measurement in Brazil, which can inform targeted interventions to improve patient safety across various hospitals and staff roles.Clinical trialNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-026-14709-6.

Introduction

Patient safety culture (PSC) refers to the beliefs, norms, and values that a healthcare organization has around patient safety. This latent, implicit, cultural notion is hierarchical in nature and reinforced in practice by distinct levels of in-person care, organizational administration, and health system regulations and policies [2]. The World Health Assembly Global Patient Safety Action Plan 2021–2030 emphasized the need for measuring PSC regularly and use the assessment for health system operation and management purposes with the goal of eliminating avoidable harm in health care [3]. Though the interest on PSC is growing globally, there is still a lack of awareness and measurement in most LatAm countries [4].

In this study, we used the Healthcare Management Americas (HMA) survey on PSC, which is based on the second version of the Hospital Survey on Patient Safety Culture (HSOPSC v2) from the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality (AHRQ) [16]. The HMA survey seeks to be comparable across LatAm countries with a Spanish and Portuguese version culturally adapted to the region. It also extends HSOPSC v2 by adding three questions that can shed light on patient safety sub-cultures: gender, race, and multiple job status (weather the staff has additional jobs, a common phenomenon in LatAm healthcare workforce). It also includes a life satisfaction question to assess staff’s subjective wellbeing. The Brazilian version of the HMA survey builds on HSOPSC v2 translation produced by the Oswaldo Cruz Foundation, who validated the instrument using staff from one hospital in Sao Paulo, Brazil [14]. While the authors found their instrument valid and reliable, they recommended that future research test the dimensional structure of the original model across multiple hospitals in Brazil.

To put in context, Brazil is the largest healthcare market in LatAm, with more than 7,100 hospitals and 427 thousand beds distributed in 26 states. Brazil provides universal health coverage through the Unified Health System (SUS), and it is estimated that 72% of Brazilians relies exclusively on SUS to get healthcare. Private hospitals, which represent 62% of all hospitals, can serve SUS patients. It is estimated that 50.7 million Brazilians (24% of the population) have access to the private healthcare system (International Trade Administration, [8]). This study implemented the HMA survey in eight private non-for-profit hospitals located in two large states of Brazil (Parana and Minas Gerais). Of the eight hospitals, seven serve SUS patients and one exclusively serves patients with private insurance [12]. Our goal was to assess the psychometric properties of the instrument and make practical recommendations on its use within the Brazilian health system.

Materials & methods

The instrument

The HMA survey is based on the AHRQ’s HSOPSC v2, which consists of 32 questions factored into 10 factors or dimensions of PSC, as described in the Appendix (Table S1). HSOPSC v2 also includes two patient safety outcomes: the number of patient safety events, and the patient safety rating. The HMA survey extends HSOPSC v2 by adding four questions: gender, race, multiple job status, and a Likert-scaled question about life satisfaction. The Portuguese version of the HSOPSC v2 was based on the translated, culturally adapted and validated version produced by researchers from the Oswaldo Cruz Foundation [14]. Our team used a pre-test and focus group analysis with a total of 20 patient safety experts to review and adjust the Oswaldo Cruz Foundation instrument with minimal changes to guarantee comparability. The HMA survey instrument, with a note on how it compares to the Oswaldo Cruz Foundation instrument, is presented in the Appendix (Table S2). Access links to the HMA survey in Portuguese and English are provided in Tables S3 and S4, respectively.

Sample and data collection

The HMA survey was conducted at all eight private hospitals managed by ProSaude in Brazil, between April and May 2023. We chose to include all facilities managed by this organization as ProSaude enforces consistent corporate patient safety policies and quality certifications across its entire network. Based in Sao Paulo, this non-profit hospital management organization oversees all patient safety initiatives and indicators for its operations throughout Brazil. The full population of staff across these eight hospitals was invited to participate to make the staff perspective on patient safety more inclusive. Because the full population was used, formal sample size calculation was not necessary. Non-hospital services were excluded from the sample of facilities.

The HMA survey was distributed by the patient safety team of each hospital to all staff via email, WhatsApp and through printed flyers placed throughout the hospitals containing a QR code. To further facilitate completion, computers were made available in strategic locations, and employees were allowed to complete the survey during work hours. To manage and promote the survey, a PSC survey committee was formed, led by the patient safety manager with representatives from various hospital departments. This committee held weekly meetings for five weeks with area leaders to actively encourage participation. To incentivize participation, prizes were awarded to units that achieved the highest response rates. Additionally, all staff members who completed the survey were invited to participate in a free certificate-granted course on healthcare quality. To ensure data quality and streamline the collection process, all responses were gathered electronically using the Qualtrics platform, producing a real time monitoring report by hospital, large units and staff position available to the PSC survey committee.

Validity testing – construct validity

Construct validity is the degree to which a scale is measuring the construct or latent attribute it is intended to measure [1]. Both confirmatory factor analyses (CFA) and exploratory structural equation modeling (ESEM) were used to assess the construct validity of the Brazilian version of the HMA survey.

Though not used for this study, exploratory factor analysis (EFA) is a data-driven approach that assesses the dimensionality of the data, whereby specifically, confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was used to test that dimensionality, seeing if a priori hypothesized model structure fits the data well, which tests for construct validity. However, because CFA does not allow for cross-loadings (having more than one item per factor), in practice this may have forced researchers to choose more parsimonious models than what fits the data best. ESEM is a special case combination of CFA and aspects of EFA where the structure of the CFA models is tested but allowing cross-loadings [9].

Mplus version 8.5 was used to perform the analyses reported in this paper [11]. Following the analytic protocol from E. van Zyl and M. ten Klooster and using the convenient online platform to aide in Mplus coding of CFA and ESEM developed by De Beer and Van Zyl [9, 17], various hierarchical structures were tested [9, 17]. For this study, there were four models tested from each data-driven approach. For CFA methods: (1) one-underlying factor model (all items loading onto one latent trait), (2) the first-order 10-factor HSOPSC v2 model (the base USA AHRQ’s HSOPSC v2 model with all ten first-ordered, inter-correlated factors), (3) a second-order one-factor model (all 10 HSOPSC v2 factors loading onto one hierarchical overall score), and (4) a bifactor model, encompassing a general factor (G-factor) of all 32 items, along with the 10 USA HSOPSC specific factors (S-factors) all separately controlled. For ESEM methods: (5) a regular ESEM based on the 10 USA HSOPSC v2 factors, (6) a second-order ESEM model, (7) an ESEM-within-CFA model, the same model structure supported by Reis et al. [14], and (8) a bifactor ESEM model. In the bifactor models all items were allowed to load on one general G-factor and on several S-factors that were orthogonal from each other and the G-factor. It is worth noting that for comparison purposes we estimated the same ESEM-within-CFA model performed by Reis et al., with the Oswaldo Cruz Foundation instrument [14]. Our goal is to assess the construct validity of the HMA survey, considering the best of the eight model structures that better fit the data.

Reliability testing

Reliability refers to the consistency of test scores across test replications. For the factors of each CFA model, we assessed reliability based on Cronbach’s alphas with a threshold of α > 0.70. Because of the cross-loading nature of ESEM, a McDonald’s Omega coefficient [10] was calculated for the overall general reliability of the entire scale (G-factor) and for each respective factors’ reliability (S-factors). We considered a threshold of ω > 0.60 for first-order models (non-bifactor), and ω > 0.50 for the bifactor ESEM model [13].

Validity testing – convergent validity

Convergent validity is a subset of construct validity and refers to the degree which a scale correlates with external scales or constructs that were evaluated at the simultaneous cross-sectional measurement point as the original scale, which are expected to correlate and be associated on strong theoretical grounds [1].

Once the final best-fitting model was deduced and found to be valid and reliable, convergent validity testing was conducted by regressing both HSOPSC v2 outcomes onto the factor scores of the best-fitting model. Multivariate regression was used to perform both unadjusted and adjusted models, the latter adjusted by the staff- and hospital-level demographics. The two PSC outcomes of the HSOPSC v2 are the number of patient safety events and the patient safety rating, both cross-sectionally measured and self-reported survey questions. They were both Likert-scale-based, with five response options each, but treated continuous [15]. This was performed because it would help to statistically associate which validated factor scores and patient- and hospital-level patient safety subculture groups are related to either survey outcome. Knowing which factors and groups are statistically significant would help better target interventions to assess and improve PSC across and within hospitals more accurately.

Results

A total of 4,152 clinical and non-clinical hospital staff across all eight hospitals received the HMA survey. The survey was completed by 2,434 clinical and non-clinical hospital staff, with a response rate of 59%. Table 1 displays the person- and hospital-level demographic counts. Two hospitals provided 74% of all responses, and the most common respondent were nurses (39%) and supervisor/manager/clinical leader/senior leader (30%). Nearly 30% of staff spent less than 1 year working at the hospital, and 50% had 1 to 5 years. Almost 75% of responders had multiple jobs, 72% were female, 56% self-identified as white, and 72% considered themselves as very satisfied or satisfied with their life.

There were several CFAs and ESEMs performed, four each, with their respective model fit estimates, and an overall concluding criteria, as shown in Table 2. The four CFA models were as follows. Model 1: the underlying first-order factor model. Model 2: the base USA AHRQ’s HSOPSC v2 model. Model 3: a second-order, single-factor model. Model 4: a bifactor model. The best-fitting CFA model was the bifactor (model 4) [χ2/df = 3.721; CFI = 0.952; TLI = 0.938; RMSEA = 0.033; 95% CI RMSEA = (0.032–0.035); SRMR = 0.029; AIC = 186945.959; BIC = 187948.749; aBIC = 187399.087].

The four ESEM models were ESEM strategies imposed onto the CFA model structures, allowing for cross-loadings. The models were as follows. Model 5: a traditional ESEM based on the 10 USA HSOPSC v2 factors. Model 6: a second-order ESEM USA model. Model 7: an ESEM-within-CFA model. Model 8: a bifactor ESEM model.

Reliability estimates

Regarding the reliability estimates for the best fitting model (model 8), we report Cronbach’s Alphas and McDonald’s Omega for each factor and overall. While Cronbach’s Alphas are not appropriate for the cross-loading nature of ESEM, and McDonald’s Omegas should be used instead ([10]; [13]) we report both coefficients for comparison purposes. Overall, the internal consistency reliability of the instrument was acceptable (see Table 3), with coefficients above recommended thresholds (Cronbach’s Alpha = 0.92 > 0.70 and McDonald’s Omega = 0.95 > 0.50). However, two factors did not meet the traditional Cronbach’s Alpha threshold of 0.70 nor the McDonald’s Omega threshold of 0.50: Teamwork (Cronbach’s Alpha = 0.63, McDonald’s Omega = 0.47) and Staffing and Work Pace (Cronbach’s Alpha = 0.61, McDonald’s Omega = 0.09). Additionally, three factors did not meet the McDonald’s Omega threshold, Organizational Learning (McDonald’s Omega = 0.17), Response to Error (McDonald’s Omega = 0.48), and Hospital Management Support for Patient Safety (McDonald’s Omega = 0.49). See Table 3 for the full delineation of all the reliability estimates.

Discussion

This study employed factor analytic and structural equation modeling techniques to psychometrically validate the HMA survey, and extended version of the AHRQ’s HSOPSC v2 translated to Brazilian Portuguese and culturally adapted to the Brazilian healthcare system. We found that a bifactor ESEM model, which allows for item cross-loadings, provides the best fit. Our results demonstrate that the HMA survey, and consequently the HSOPSC v2 instrument, has great construct validity, great overall reliability, and good convergent validity that demonstrated associations with PSC outcomes. The overall general (G) factor accounts for the largest variation in patient safety rating, about 41.7% of the variation in the staff-rated patient safety rating before controlling for staff and hospital-level covariates, and about 28.3% after. The difference between controlled and uncontrolled results reflects the significant variation in patient safety rating by hospital site, staff position, and having multiple jobs.

Our study found a low levels of reliability in 5 dimensions of PSC. In particular, the internal reliability of Staffing and Work Pace and Organizational Learning were very weak based on McDonald’s Omegas (McDonald’s Omega = 0.09 and 0.17, respectively). The weak reliability of some dimensions of PSC has been noted in multiple psychometric studies of the HSOPSC instrument. In a literature review of 62 validation studies of the HSOPSC version 1 [18], the authors found that more than half of the studies reported Cronbach’s alpha below the acceptable threshold for 50% or more of the PSC dimensions. In particular, Staffing was the dimension with the lowest internal reliability, and Organizational Learning had the third lowest Cronbach’s alpha, below the 0.70 threshold. Our results are consistent with these findings and are also consistent with the previous reliability estimates of the HSOPSC v2 instrument in Brazil produced by the Oswaldo Cruz Foundation [14]. The authors found Staffing and Work Pace, Handoff and Information Exchange, and Organizational Learning as the PSC dimensions with the lowest Cronbach’s alpha, below the 0.70 threshold. This requires caution when interpreting and making decisions associated with weak PSC dimensions, in particular Staffing and Work Pace and Organizational Learning.

While the drivers of the low reliability of these PSC dimensions require further investigation, we think they are associated with the negatively worded items that compose those dimensions. Negatively worded items increase cognitive load that can lead to biased or misunderstood responses, limiting the reliable participation of staff with lower educational background. In the context of the HSOPSC, a psychometric analysis of the Georgian version of the survey [7] showed that negatively worded items may be prone to reversed item bias, affecting reliability. The authors suggested that this effect is inherent of HSOPSC design and so should be accounted when analyzing the psychometric properties of any language version. In a non-healthcare context, Wong and coauthors [19] show that negatively worded items can decrease internal consistency reliability, and the problem is pronounced in cross-cultural settings due to cultural and linguistic differences in response styles and cognitive processing. The authors show evidence of increased reliability coefficients when negatively worded items are reduced. It is then not surprising that in our study the PSC dimensions with the strongest reliability estimates do not have any negatively worded item, while those with the lowest reliability have 2 or more. For instance, 3 of the 4 items composing Staffing and Work Pace are negatively worded.

It is worth noticing the consistency of our results with the Oswaldo Cruz Foundation’s results [14]. The HMA survey was built on the translation of the Oswaldo Cruz Foundation, and both instruments share high similarities as shown in Table S2 in the Appendix. Both studies confirm the validation and reliability of the HSOPSC v2 in Brazil, but our study extends the results by incorporating more hospitals (eight compared to 1), more staff and a larger regional variability. Methodologically, while we both found the ESEM-within-CFA model structure provides a good fit, we found that the bifactor ESEM provided the best fit overall, indicating that the survey data represents a structure with more flexibility cross-loading and, most importantly, the existence of a general factor that is highly correlated with the patient safety rating.

The limitations of this research are those of traditional cross-sectional survey research in that causality cannot be established. Recall bias and the Hawthorne effect may have factored in when respondents were filling out this survey. Some of this was mitigated by the anonymous, voluntary and third-party administration of the survey – to protect respondents from administrative retaliation – thus yielding more truthful assessments. Generalizability remains an important concern for our study, as nearly three-quarters of the responses came from just a quarter of the participating hospitals. However, this non-uniform distribution reflects a key characteristic of the Brazilian hospital network, which is highly concentrated in small private facilities. Around 62% of hospitals in the country are private, and a significant number of these private hospitals (59%) have fewer than 50 beds [5]. While achieving full external validity would require a larger sample of both public and private facilities, our analysis of multiple hospitals across different regions of Brazil, and the consistency with a similar study performed in one hospital in Sao Paulo [14], provides a more robust evidence of the validity and reliability of the HSOPSC v2 in a Brazilian context.

Based on our results, we recommend the use of the HMA survey in Brazilian hospitals as a validate and reliable instrument to assess PSC, identify gaps, and make evidence-based decisions to strength PSC. However, caution should be put on when analyzing and making decisions associated with weak PSC dimensions, in particular Staffing and Work Pace and Organizational Learning. We also recommend considering the implications of the structural model behind our study, the bifactor ESEM model, which provided the best fit among multiple alternative model structures. Our study concludes that PSC is a multidimensional, hierarchical construct, anchored by a single common factor, and orthogonal specific factors [6]. It must be recognized that some items may belong to more than one PSC dimension (cross-loading), and a general G-factor exists that is common across all PSC dimensions (individual S-factors). In practice, hospitals should analyze and compare their general PSC factor by scoring the survey using parameter estimates of the bifactor ESEM solution, instead of traditional scoring methods (summed scored or summated average scores) because the parameter estimates will be able to consider the variance of the general and specific factors. We invite Brazilian hospitals to participate in the HMA survey to help growth the Brazilian PSC database benchmark, identify gaps compared to similar hospitals in Brazil, and get actionable reports to strength PSC.
