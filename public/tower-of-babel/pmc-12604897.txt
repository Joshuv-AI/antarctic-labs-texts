Balloon Dilatation and Cryobiopsy on an Ultra‐Peripheral Ground Glass Nodule: A Case Report
Authors: Ria Katrina Cortez, Yuji Matsumoto, Hideaki Furuse, Kenya Sato, Takaaki Tsuchida
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTGround‐glass nodules (GGNs) pose a considerable diagnostic challenge. Recently, a balloon catheter to dilate peripheral bronchi has become commercially available, offering a potential solution to this limitation. This is a case of a 73‐year‐old female nonsmoker with rheumatoid arthritis on Tacrolimus. Chest CT scan showed a 13.9‐mm pure GGN at the right upper lobe. Annual chest CT scan noted stability in size for 7 years, but recently the size increased to 17.0 mm with a positive bronchus sign in the 12th generation. An ultrathin bronchoscope could be inserted up to the 9th generation. Balloon dilatation was done at this point to pass the subsequent tortuous bifurcation, advancing the bronchoscope further up to the 10th generation. Where radial endobronchial ultrasound showed a blizzard sign, cryobiopsy was performed, revealing lung adenocarcinoma. This case highlights the value of balloon dilatation and cryobiopsy for ultra‐peripheral GGNs.

Introduction

Ground‐glass nodules (GGNs) pose a considerable diagnostic challenge due to their often‐imperceptible appearance on X‐ray fluoroscopy and subtle presentation on radial endobronchial ultrasound (R‐EBUS). Accurate localisation and safe sampling are critical, yet can be limited by the inability to position the bronchoscope sufficiently close to peripheral lesions. Although advancing the bronchoscope deeper into the bronchial tree improves stability, anatomical constraints frequently prevent adequate access. Recently, a balloon catheter designed specifically for peripheral bronchial dilation has become commercially available, offering a potential solution to this limitation. We report a case in which this balloon device facilitated the diagnosis of a peripheral GGN, highlighting its potential utility in bronchoscopic interventions.

Case Report

This is a case of a 73‐year‐old female nonsmoker. The patient has a history of hypertension and diabetes, and has had a meningioma resected. Family history includes gastric cancer on the paternal side. Eight years prior to the procedure, she was diagnosed with rheumatoid arthritis and was started on Tacrolimus. The baseline chest computed tomography scan revealed a 13.9‐mm pure GGN in the right upper lobe. Therefore, she was referred to the respiratory endoscopy division and was advised to monitor. Annual chest CT scan noted stability in size for 7 years, but recently the nodule's size increased to 17.0 mm, located approximately 1.0 cm from the costal pleura, with no enlarged lymph nodes. The 12th generation bronchus, which sharply branches off towards the head from the right B3b, showed bronchial sign positive. Then, the patient was scheduled for bronchoscopy.

Virtual navigation bronchoscopy and virtual fluoroscopy were reconstructed using a workstation (Ziostation2; Ziofsoft, Tokyo, Japan) (Figure 1A,B). Under local and intravenous conscious sedation, a thin bronchoscope (BF‐P260F; Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) was initially inserted up to the 5th generation, passing through right B3b. Since the thin bronchoscope could not be inserted beyond this point, it was replaced with an ultrathin bronchoscope (BF‐MP290F; Olympus), allowing insertion up to the 9th generation.

There were only three branches left to the target lesion, but the course was tortuous on the right, left, and head sides. We decided to dilate at that point using a bronchodilatation balloon catheter with a maximum inflated diameter of 4.5 mm and a length of 10 mm (BR‐N1; Kaneka, Osaka, Japan) (Figure 2). The balloon was inflated under bronchoscopic guidance by injecting saline until sufficient dilatation was achieved. One inflation was adequate, and no airway collapse was observed after balloon removal. Consequently, the bronchoscope was advanced further up to the 10th generation, leading to the target lesion guided under X‐ray fluoroscopy. At the location where R‐EBUS showed a blizzard sign (Figure 1C), adjacent to the lesion, cryobiopsy using a 1.1‐mm cryoprobe (Erbe Elektromedizin GmbH, Tübingen, Germany) was performed, with only mild bleeding and no other complications observed. Rapid on‐site evaluation suggested a positive result, and subsequent histopathology revealed lung adenocarcinoma (Figure 1D).

(B) During the procedure, balloon dilatation was done to gradually increase the bronchial diameter. (C) Following balloon dilatation, a significant increase in the luminal diameter of the bronchus was noted.

Discussion

The case involves a patient who presented with an ultra‐peripheral pure GGN, posing a significant diagnostic challenge due to its location and radiological characteristics. Thorough planning is essential when approaching peripheral pulmonary lesions, especially for those located far from the hilum via complex bronchi [1]. In addition, pure GGNs are inherently more difficult to diagnose compared to solid nodules. To address these challenges, a series of procedural innovations were employed, including the use of an ultrathin bronchoscope, balloon dilatation and cryobiopsy. These strategies enabled localisation and biopsy of the lesion, culminating in the diagnosis of lung adenocarcinoma.

The ultrathin bronchoscope ultimately used can typically reach around the 6th generation bronchi. In this case, insertion was possible up to the 9th generation, but it was important to pass another tortuous bifurcation to achieve stable access to the target GGN. Therefore, we applied a newly released balloon catheter to dilate the point, which facilitated deeper insertion into the 10th generation, allowing the ultrathin bronchoscope to position as close as possible to the GGN. In general, the closer the bronchoscope is to the lesion, the more targeted and effective the biopsy becomes. Balloon dilatation played a crucial role in extending bronchoscopic reach, particularly into the outer third of the lung, a region that remains challenging with conventional bronchoscopes. On average, it was reported to advance to 2.3 additional bifurcations or to a position 14.7 ± 0.8 mm away from the pleura [2]. Although current evidence on the balloon dilatation's diagnostic utility is still limited, this case demonstrates its potential benefit in overcoming anatomical barriers.

Bronchoscopically, GGNs are notoriously difficult to characterise. Pure GGNs often show the blizzard sign on R‐EBUS, which is a subtle but notable increase in intensity and radius of the whitish acoustic shadow distinct from the snowstorm appearance of normal parenchyma [3]. This finding correlates histologically with lesions ranging from adenocarcinoma in situ to well‐differentiated adenocarcinoma. These weak cellular atypia often make it difficult to make a pathological diagnosis based on cell morphology alone, and it is crucial to identify changes in tissue structure. Although forceps biopsy is commonly used, it carries the risk of tissue crush artefacts, particularly in delicate GGNs. In this case, cryobiopsy using a 1.1‐mm ultrathin cryoprobe enabled the retrieval of a larger, better‐preserved specimen, improving histological assessment. The diagnostic yield of cryobiopsy in GGNs was reported as 82.61% using the ultrathin cryoprobe in combination with various bronchoscope settings [4]. We have also demonstrated a significantly higher diagnostic yield of 88.8% in the cryobiopsy group compared to 63.8% in the conventional biopsy group [5].

This case highlights the potential added value of combining balloon dilatation and cryobiopsy into the evaluation of ultra‐peripheral pure GGNs. These techniques may significantly enhance diagnostic yield by improving access and obtaining higher‐quality tissue samples. Nevertheless, current data on the integration of balloon dilatation and cryobiopsy remain scarce, underscoring the need for further studies to validate this evolving diagnostic strategy.
