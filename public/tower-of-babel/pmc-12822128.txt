Standardized nutritional screening and assessment for nasopharyngeal cancer patients: an evidence-based implementation project
Authors: Wenwen Cai, Rufei Yang, Chao Tang, Yueyao Cao, Jing Li, Jinmei Huang, Yalan Song
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionRadiotherapy for nasopharyngeal cancer (NPC) frequently induces malnutrition, which is a serious complication that adversely affects patients’ quality of life and prognosis. Although there are currently nutritional assessment procedures for such patients, their clinical effectiveness requires further investigation.ObjectiveThis study aims to evaluate the impact of implementing an optimal nutritional assessment strategy on the nutritional management skills of nurses and patients and on the nutritional status of NPC patients undergoing radiotherapy.MethodsThe project followed a three-phase approach. In the first phase, a systematic search and critical appraisal of evidence on nutritional management for NPC radiotherapy patients was conducted, yielding 17 best-practice items and 18 audit indicators. A baseline assessment of these indicators was subsequently performed in a radiotherapy ward between November 2024 and January 2025. The findings from this audit informed the second phase, where barriers and facilitators were analyzed using the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research. This analysis led to the development of the 4 C Nutrition pathway as the core intervention strategy, which was implemented in the third phase from March to May 2025. The effectiveness was evaluated by comparing pre- and post-implementation data on audit compliance, knowledge levels of nurses, patients’ treatment adherence, and nutritional status indicators. Comparative analyses used paired or independent-sample t-tests for continuous variables and chi-square tests for categorical variables, with significance set at P < 0.05 (SPSS software, version 25.0).ResultsA total of 51 patients were included pre-implementation, compared to 52 post-implementation. Following evidence translation, the implementation rates of nurse review indicator increased overall from a range of 0% to 90.2% pre-implementation to 50.00% to 100.00% post-implementation. Nurse nutrition management knowledge questionnaire scores increased from (68.22 ± 5.90) to (85.11 ± 7.10). Patients’ nutritional management adherence scores increased from (103.43 ± 12.92) to (117.00 ± 5.97). Moreover, at the 4-week follow-up, the hemoglobin level in the post-implementation group (126.38 ± 6.19) g/L was significantly higher than the pre-implementation level (109.58 ± 17.15) g/L. All these differences were statistically significant (P < 0.05).ConclusionsThe implementation effectively enhanced the standardization of clinical nutritional assessment for nurses, significantly narrowed the gap between clinical practice and best evidence, and improved the knowledge of nutritional management among nurses and patients.Trial registrationThis study has been retrospectively registered with the China Clinical Trial Registry. Clinical trial number: ChiCTR2500107011. Registration date: 1 August 2025.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12912-025-04221-3.

Introduction

Nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC) is a malignant tumor of the nasopharyngeal epithelium [1]. It is one of the most common tumors in the head and neck region, with an estimated 133,354 new cases and 8,008 deaths per year globally, and its incidence rate shows a growing trend [2]. NPC exhibits a significant ethnic and geographic distribution; for example, the incidence rate in certain regions of southern China exceeds the global average by over 20 times [3]. The prevailing clinical treatment regimen is radiotherapy in combination with systemic drug therapy, with drugs administered as induction therapy, concurrent therapy, or adjuvant therapy [4, 5]. Despite the marked improvement in the prognosis for NPC that has been achieved through advances in treatment modalities [6], the quality of life of patients remains poor. This phenomenon can be attributed to the anatomical characteristics of the head and neck region, particularly the presence of substantial mucosal tissue [7]. As a result, this area is particularly sensitive to radiotherapy, giving rise to a spectrum of adverse effects. These complications include diminished salivary secretion, xerostomia, oral mucositis, increased susceptibility to oral infections, and various functional impairments [8, 9]. Additionally, patients may experience difficulty in chewing and swallowing, as well as loss of taste [10, 11]. Following radiotherapy, up to 95% of patients experience severe or moderate malnutrition [12, 13], which reduces tumor cell sensitivity to radiotherapy, compromises the accuracy of radiotherapy targeting, and diminishes tolerance to radiotherapy, thereby prolonging hospitalization [8]. Malnutrition also constitutes a risk factor for local tumor recurrence and reduced survival rates [14], significantly impairing patients’ quality of life. Therefore, proactive nutritional support is critical. In light of this, accumulating evidence further indicates that the primary goal of nutritional care for cancer patients should be the prevention of nutritional deficiencies [15]. Thus, the early identification and management of malnutrition are vital for improving the prognosis of NPC patients, reducing the chronic disease burden, and enhancing their quality of life [15, 16]. Concurrently, international guidelines explicitly recommend nutritional management for cancer patients [17], stressing early detection and intervention for malnutrition and metabolic disorders through standardized screening and assessment processes [18]. Among these, nutritional screening and assessment represent critical steps requiring efficient, economical, highly sensitive, and specific screening tools [19]. Accordingly, clinicians need standardized procedures to clarify assessment indicators and multidisciplinary collaboration responsibilities, with recommendations to include weight changes and body mass index in routine monitoring [19]. Despite these clear recommendations, a significant gap persists between guidelines and routine practice, translating these evidence-based recommendations into operational protocols for NPC patient management requires further exploration and validation [20]. For example, despite healthcare institutions having demonstrated positive shifts in implementing clinical nutrition programs, their sustained implementation remains inconsistent, particularly in facilities lacking dedicated dietitians [21]. While oncologists can assume this responsibility in the absence of specialist nutritionists, they may lack the professional training required to conduct comprehensive nutritional assessments. This deficiency poses a significant risk, as it may result in the undiagnosed or inadequate management of malnutrition, consequently leading to delayed interventions, poor treatment tolerance, and an escalation in complications [22]. This highlights an urgent need for structured, evidence-based approaches to nutritional care. To tackle this challenge, our study translated the highest-quality available evidence into clinical practice by leveraging Evidence-Based Practice (EBP) to develop a scientifically standardized nutritional assessment process for NPC patients. This initiative aimed to standardize nursing protocols, improve the nutritional knowledge of both nurses and patients, and ultimately lower the incidence of malnutrition.

Materials and methods

Study design

This EBP implementation project is based on the Practice Application of Clinical Evidence System (PACES) and Getting Research into Practice (GRiP) developed by the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) [23]. These tools constitute the foundational EBP implementation framework that has been widely adopted across China’s healthcare sector. The methodology employed in this study adheres to JBI’s three-phase framework, utilizing the proven cyclical process of audit, feedback and re-audit to transform clinical practice effectively, irrespective of project scale [23, 24]. The project unfolded in three distinct phases: (1) Formed a project team, identified the best available scientific evidence, and developed assessment criteria to conduct a baseline review; (2) Development of tailored strategies addressing non-compliance issues and barriers revealed by the baseline audit results; (3) Follow-up audit post-implementation of the change policy. Research location and subjects

This study was approved by the participating institution’s Medical Ethics Committee (Protocol: Medical Ethics [Initial Review] - KY-2024-41), with written informed consent obtained from all participants prior to initiation. Conducted in the Radiation Therapy Department of a tertiary specialized cancer hospital in Guangzhou, Guangdong, China, it included 103 NPC patients receiving radiotherapy and 18 department nurses, enrolled between November 2024 and May 2025.

The inclusion criteria for patients: (1) Age ≥ 18 years; (2) Newly diagnosed patients with NPC confirmed by pathological and imaging examinations; (3) Clinical stage I–IV (2017 8th edition UICC/AJCC staging criteria for NPC); (4) First-time recipients of radiotherapy; (5) Alert and able to communicate and read normally. The exclusion criteria for patients: (1) Patients with concurrent tumors or severe organ dysfunction (e.g., heart, liver, kidneys). The inclusion criteria for nurses: (1) ≥ 1 year of clinical experience in radiotherapy departments; (2) Primary-level or higher professional title; (3) Associate degree or higher education level.

Phase 1: Form a project team, identify the best available scientific evidence, and develop assessment criteria to conduct a baseline review

Form a multidisciplinary team

This study established the Clinical Practice Team for Nutritional Assessment of Radiation Therapy Patients, as a multidisciplinary team comprising two physicians, four nurses, and one nursing graduate student. The team includes an Associate Chief Physician specializing in radiation therapy with over 10 years of clinical experience leading strategic planning; a Deputy Chief Physician who is a qualified clinical nutritionist from the hospital’s Department of Clinical Nutrition, supervising project implementation; and a Nursing Department Head possessing over 15 years of oncology nursing management experience handling cross-departmental communication, coordination, and resource allocation. Additional members feature an Oncology Specialist Nurse with 20 years of radiation therapy experience, a Radiation Therapy Specialist Nurse with 15 years of departmental expertise, a Research Nurse trained in systematic evidence-based methods, and a Master’s degree student specializing in oncology nursing. Collectively representing a well-structured talent pool, the team’s educational background comprises one PhD, two master’s degrees, and four bachelor’s degrees.

Construction of review indicators

The team conducted evidence searches and evaluations, integrating findings to include 18 articles and resulting in an evidence summary of 17 items. Under the strict guidance of the nursing department head and adhering to feasibility, appropriateness, meaningfulness, and effectiveness (FAME) principles, clinical doctors, nurse managers, and radiotherapy nurses jointly assessed each item’s feasibility, applicability, clinical significance, and effectiveness [25]. Based on the 17 evidence-based criteria, the team ultimately confirmed 18 assessment indicators. These indicators were meticulously designed to systematically evaluate adherence to a standardized nutritional care pathway, encompassing key components such as nutrition risk screening, nutrition monitoring, monitoring frequency, methods for assessing malnutrition, and the assessment of patients’ self-management abilities. The review covered two subject categories: NPC patients and nursing staffs. Team members conducted the review via on-site verification of nurses’ evidence application and documentation of NPC patients’ assessment results. Among these indicators, 1, 2, and 12 were non-quantitative. They were assessed solely by the team to determine departmental compliance with standards, with no involvement of patient or nurse reviews. A complete list of all indicators, including their classification, evidence sources, and collection methods, is provided in Table 1 (which can be found at the end of this manuscript), and the evidence retrieval and inclusion process is described in Supplementary Material 1.

Assessment of indicator baseline levels and data collection methods

Basic information

This section compiled the basic demographic characteristics of patients, including ID number, age, marital status, education level, place of residence, employment status, monthly income, smoking history, and family medical history.

Process level

Investigators used a review criteria checklist to verify the completion of review indicators across departments, nursing staff, and patients. Correct implementation received a “√”; non-implementation or incorrect execution received an “×”. Compliance rate = (Number of correctly implemented items / Totally reviewed items) × 100%.

Nurse level

A self-designed questionnaire was developed based on the 17 evidence items to assess nurses’ knowledge of nutritional management for NPC radiotherapy patients (see Supplementary Material 2). This process ensured strong content validity. The questionnaire was reviewed and approved by the research team for clarity, relevance, and comprehensiveness. First, it collected demographic data on nurses, including gender, age, educational level, years of work experience and position. Furthermore, its 25 questions focusing on key areas such as complication prevention, dietary adjustments, and multidisciplinary collaboration. The instrument comprises single-choice, multiple-choice, and true/false questions. Each correct answer scores 4 points, with a maximum total of 100 points; higher scores reflect greater knowledge. In this study, the questionnaire was administered both before and after the implementation of EBP, allowing for a comparison of changes in nurses’ knowledge and skills in nutritional management over this period. This study employed Cronbach’s α coefficient to assess the internal consistency reliability of the questionnaire, which yielded a value of 0.702. The value is considered​ acceptable for a newly developed scale, particularly given the small sample size.

Patient level

At the patient level, treatment adherence was measured using the Nutrition Self-Efficacy Scale developed by Ziwen He [26]. This 31-item instrument covers four domains: nutrition therapy knowledge, treatment attitudes, coping behaviors, and social support. All items employ a 5-point Likert scale (total range: 31–155), with higher scores indicating better therapy adherence. The scale demonstrates high reliability (Cronbach’s α = 0.933; test-retest reliability = 0.959). For patients with baseline nutritional risk scores ≥ 3, BMI, hemoglobin, and albumin changes were tracked at 2- and 4-weeks post-intervention based on researcher assessments.

Between November 2024 and January 2025, the baseline audit covered 51 eligible radiotherapy patients, of whom 12 had a nutritional screening score of ≥ 3, as well as 18 nurses. Knowledge surveys utilized multi-channel data collection: (1) Patient questionnaires completed under researcher guidance with on-site collection during per-discharge quality verification; (2) Nurse questionnaires administered electronically post-standardized training. The group structured 18 review indicators into standardized record forms capturing patient demographics, indicator codes, review content, implementation methods, compliance status, and remarks. The researchers evaluated each item against the audit protocols, documented the rates of compliance with the criteria, and ensured that all the necessary data were captured. Laboratory data were retrieved from hospital information systems. Discharged patients received telephone follow-up documentation by designated department staff. All data collectors underwent standardized training, and data accuracy, validity, and completeness were ensured through double data entry and verification on the same day.

Phase 2: Development of tailored strategies addressing non-compliance issues and barriers revealed by the baseline audit results

Facilitator and barrier analysis

This study employed the updated Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR) to systematically identify facilitators and barriers [27]. Through structured focus group discussions with stakeholders targeting audit indicators, we conducted textual analysis, iterative coding, and thematic categorization aligned with CFIR domains. This process identified critical determinants of nutritional assessment implementation for NPC patients across all five CFIR domains: Innovation; Outer Setting; Inner Setting; Individual; Implementation Process. Facilitator and barrier

Development of a 4C EBP protocol for NPC radiotherapy patients

Leveraging the identified facilitators and barriers, the research team convened nutritionists, radiation oncologists, and oncology nurses to collaboratively design “4 C Nutrition”, a closed-loop management system integrating nutrition assessment, intervention, education, and reassessment. The operational framework comprises the following evidence-based components:

Convene Team: Fostering Integrated Clinical Partnerships A comprehensive care pathway was established to coordinate nutritional support with radiotherapy plans. Nursing staff conducted an initial Nutritional Risk Screening (NRS)-2002 screening for admitted patients within 24 h of admission. Upon identifying high-risk patients, they performed a comprehensive assessment, notified clinicians, and initiated electronic consultation. Subsequently, registered nutritionists were then responsible for triaging nutritional risk cases, conducting Patient-Generated Subjective Global Assessment (PG-SGA) assessments, and providing nutritional guidance. This process was fully participated in and followed up by nurses, while the team designed a “Nutritional Screening and Assessment Checklist” to assist nurses in documenting patients’ nutritional assessment status. Additionally, the system was structured to enable nursing management to monitor compliance weekly and address any delays during monthly evaluations. The objective of recording assessment results was to establish an integrated, continuous process for clinical screening, consultation, and assessment. Furthermore, a between-group comparison of outcomes​ was conducted to evaluate the effectiveness of the intervention on nutritional dynamics throughout the course of radiotherapy. Check Status: This program establishes a unified clinical assessment method The capacity-building program enhanced nurses’ professional capabilities through monthly meetings, including training on evidence-based translation processes and nutritional monitoring. These meetings included training on evidence-based clinical translation processes and application methods, as well as training in nutritional monitoring and care record writing, covering patient physical measurements, laboratory indicators, and self-nutrition management level assessments. For patient nutritional status assessment, specific criteria were established: initial monitoring was to be completed within 24 h of admission; ongoing monitoring was to be conducted progressively based on the patients’ nutritional risk; and these processes were to be integrated into routine clinical workflows during the patients’ hospital stay to ensure timely detection of changes in condition. To ensure nurses strictly adhered to the specified protocols, the team conducted reviews of relevant indicators. Customize Plan: Implementing Patient Centered Literacy Strategies In addition to providing routine nutrition and health education, nurses were tasked with regularly updating multimedia patient education resources related to radiotherapy and disseminating these resources through digital platforms. The department also displayed these electronic resources on a dedicated health education wall for these electronic resources to ensure free access for all inpatients. To ensure the quality of patient education, nurses involved in the program completed a certification process to validate their proficiency in health education. This process required them to demonstrate pedagogical competencies, ensuring they were equipped with effective methods to adapt to patients’ varying health literacy levels. Cycle Review: Embedding Data-Driven Quality Cycles To more effectively implement this EBP, the team established a quality improvement collaborative group that adhered to the plan-do-check-act (PDCA) method to monitor key indicators and ensure their sustained implementation. Moreover, the team conducted monthly data reviews, using obstacle analysis to enhance operational efficacy. In addition, patient monitoring frequency was differentiated by risk level: high-risk patients were monitored weekly, while low-risk patients were monitored monthly, to facilitate dynamic care adjustments and maintain service quality.

Phase 3: Follow-up audit post-implementation of the change policy

During the implementation phase, the 4 C model was translated into clinical practice. A three-month follow-up audit was conducted from March to May 2025, replicating the baseline methodology. The follow-up audit included 18 radiotherapy nurses and 52 radiation therapy patients, of whom 8 had a nutritional screening score of ≥ 3 points.

Data analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 25.0). Continuous variables that followed a normal distribution were presented as the mean ± standard deviation, while those that did not follow a normal distribution were presented as medians and inter-quartile ranges. Categorical variables were expressed as numbers and percentages. To evaluate the effectiveness of the implementation, comparative analyses were conducted. Differences in continuous variables between before and after the implementation groups were analyzed using independent-sample t-tests, such as hemoglobin, BMI, and albumin changes. For between-group comparisons, including analyses of knowledge scores between different nurse cohorts, paired t-tests were employed. Changes in categorical compliance rates for the audit indicators were compared using the chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test, depending on which was more appropriate. A two-sided P-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Between November 2024 and January 2025, a total of 51 patients and 18 nurses were included in the baseline audit. The follow-up audit, conducted from March to May 2025, included 52 patients and the same 18 nurses.

In terms of the general characteristics of patients, comparisons were made between the pre-intervention group and the post-intervention group in terms of marital status, educational level, place of residence, employment status, monthly income, smoking history, and family medical history. The findings indicated that there were no statistically significant differences observed between the two groups (P > 0.05, results are presented in Table 3 at the end of the manuscript).

Discussion

This study aimed to establish a standardized nutritional assessment process for NPC radiotherapy patients through multidisciplinary collaboration [28, 29]. By enhancing healthcare team compliance in nutritional status evaluation [30], it sought to ensure patient safety during radiotherapy and reduce treatment related adverse events. Using the JBI Evidence Implementation Framework [24], the research team developed a structured methodology to integrate the best evidence into clinical practice, thereby improving care quality. The implementation significantly increased comprehensive nutritional assessment compliance among 18 nurses and raised nutritionist consultation rates. This evidence translation strengthened nurse-patient nutritional management capabilities, maintained patients’ nutritional status, and demonstrated potential to accelerate recovery trajectories.

Prior to implementation, the project faced significant operational obstacles, primarily due to the lack of standardized nutritional protocols. Furthermore, inconsistent nutritional monitoring standards among nurses added to the variability [31]. The lack of standardized assessment processes and inconsistent nursing practices also complicated matters [32, 33]. To overcome these obstacles, the team used the CFIR framework to conduct a deductive analysis of the barriers and facilitators for the 18 review indicators in the study, revealing 9 unique barriers and 10 facilitators within the CFIR sub-dimensions. The team then systematically utilized the identified facilitating factors while actively addressing implementation barriers to develop the “4 C Nutrition” assessment pathway. This pathway focused on capacity building for nurses to integrate overall nutritional assessment and structured patient education for NPC patients undergoing radiotherapy (staged according to the 8th edition of the UICC/AJCC TNM classification). Building on baseline review findings, the team operationalized evidence into practice through four key strategies: (1) Established integrated pathways synchronizing nutrition support with radiotherapy. Nurses completed NRS-2002 screening and e-consultations upon admission; nutritionists then used PG-SGA to triage malnutrition cases based on the severity of nutritional risk, while leadership monitored compliance weekly [34]; (2) The pathway specified nurse screening timelines (within 24 h) and nutritionist response protocols. Additionally, step-by-step interventions were integrated into the workflow through training templates, and compliance audits were conducted [35]; (3) Digital resources were updated biannually and delivered by competency-validated nurses; (4) The PDCA cycle was used to track screening compliance and nutritional trends, with statistical process control analysis conducted monthly to provide a basis for program optimization. NPC patients undergoing radiotherapy were monitored according to risk stratification (weekly or monthly intervals).

Overall, these systematic interventions mentioned earlier collectively produced clinically meaningful results: compliance improved significantly from highly variable levels of 0–90.2% at baseline to a uniform range of 50–100% across 15 clinical indicators, with three core interventions achieving 100% universal adoption. The results demonstrated that both patients and nurses had improved their knowledge in nutritional management, validating the educational framework’s effectiveness in bridging competency gaps. Importantly, the observed improvement in patients’ hemoglobin levels following the 4-week intervention provided objective biochemical confirmation of the pathway’s tangible impact on nutritional status. Improvements in these indicators not only signified procedural compliance, but also represented a shift in oncology nutrition care practices from experience-based approaches to EBP. By optimizing processes such as screening compliance and referral response times, and combining these with clinically significant improvements in hemoglobin levels, the framework proved to be a double success in terms of enhancing system efficiency and patient health. Achieving a 100% adoption rate for key intervention measures indicated that the practice had effectively overcome implementation barriers previously identified through CFIR analysis, confirming its practicality in translating theoretical facilitators into sustained practice. The knowledge gains among nurses and patients further indicated successful cultural integration of nutritional priorities, a critical foundation for long-term sustainability [36, 37]. Crucially, the simultaneous improvement across multiple metrics demonstrated the multidimensional effectiveness of the intervention. This proved that systematically addressing specific contextual barriers through a structured pathway could simultaneously optimize workflow integration, evidence-based application, and patients’ outcomes. Thus, the replicable model provides a pathway for advancing oncology nutrition services, demonstrating how methodologically guided implementation strategies can translate audit metrics into meaningful clinical value.

These findings are consistent with those of other evidence-based programs that have used the JBI framework in clinical settings and demonstrated significant improvements in audit compliance and knowledge. For instance, Xie et al.‘s research on deep vein thrombosis prevention in patients with aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage revealed that a cyclical audit-feedback-intervention-review process significantly increased the implementation rates of key indicators, thus validating its effectiveness in driving practice change [38]. Similarly, our success in fostering multidisciplinary collaboration is consistent with Zhang’s team’s findings [24], as their study on the use of intermittent pneumatic compression during surgery for venous thrombosis prevention also confirmed that structured frameworks are essential for overcoming communication barriers between healthcare specialists.

Although comprehensive and significant improvements were achieved through the intervention measures, post-implementation review identified two quantifiable indicators with suboptimal compliance. For example, Indicator 10, which concerns the implementation of skeletal muscle mass measurement for nutritional assessment, achieved only 50% adherence. Simultaneously, Indicator 15, which requires patients’ understanding of dietary management principles including energy and protein calculations, reached just 57.69% compliance. For Indicator 10, sustainability analysis revealed a critical structural barrier: the hospital’s fee-for-service model for this diagnostic tool. Subsequent patient interviews confirmed cost sensitivity as the primary deterrent, with financial constraints leading to frequent refusals that compromised nutritional evaluation accuracy. Regarding Indicator 15, significant educational challenges emerged due to the inherent complexity of nutritional calculations combined with diverse patient factors, including varying health literacy levels, educational backgrounds, age ranges, and cognitive capacities. These intersecting variables created substantial learning barriers that limited knowledge retention. In response to these findings, the group plans to conduct a specialized feasibility study in the future to address financial accessibility barriers in skeletal muscle measurement. At the same time, specialized research on educational factors will be conducted to redesign the nutrition education framework, including optimizing teaching methods, adjusting teaching materials according to health literacy levels, and formulating caregiver engagement strategies. In addition, the stability of BMI and albumin levels observed at the two- and four-week follow-ups should be analyzed alongside tumor treatment methods and the pathophysiology of cancer. This is because cancer-related malnutrition is characterized by its ongoing nature and multiple causative factors [39]. As serum albumin has a half-life of three weeks, it acts as a lagging indicator that responds slowly to improvements in intake or symptom management [40]. Thus, given that participants were already malnourished at the outset, significant changes in their bodily indicators may have been difficult to observe during the four-week trial. Furthermore, albumin levels are influenced by patients’ nutritional status and metabolic requirements; inflammatory events and high nutritional risk are both associated with low albumin levels. Specifically, inflammation reduces serum albumin levels by increasing capillary permeability, re-prioritizing liver protein synthesis, and redistributing serum proteins, which makes albumin unsuitable as a diagnostic marker for malnutrition in inflammatory diseases [40, 41]. Therefore, the short-term stability of these standard biomarkers does not necessarily imply the ineffectiveness of nutritional care interventions. Measurable changes in BMI or albumin levels require months rather than weeks of sustained nutritional support [42, 43]. Additionally, the small sample size of the at-risk subgroup resulted in limited statistical power. This low power, combined with the inherent biological stability of these biomarkers, made it highly challenging to detect statistically significant changes over the short study duration. Consequently, these findings emphasize the need for nurses to implement systematic nutritional screening, monitoring, and support programs throughout the entire cancer treatment process, as meaningful improvements in biomarkers may occur during long-term follow-up or the rehabilitation phase.

Crucially, to ensure the long-term success of our project, we set up a quality improvement team to drive continuous improvement through the PDCA cycle. This guarantees ongoing monitoring, regular feedback and iterative process optimization, thereby institutionalizing the project. Furthermore, sustained leadership support from the nursing department, coupled with the significant benefits delivered by this pathway, is expected to incentivize its long-term application even further.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be considered. Firstly, the pre-post audit design and non-random recruitment of participants restrict the ability to definitively attribute any observed changes to the intervention itself, as the effects of secular trends or concurrent initiatives cannot be excluded. Secondly, the generalizability of the findings may be limited by the pilot nature of this single-center study and its small sample size, particularly within the nutritional risk subgroup (n = 12 at baseline; n = 8 at follow-up). This limited statistical power, increasing the risk of type II errors. Thirdly, although improvements were observed in process metrics and some clinical outcomes, important patient-centered endpoints such as quality of life were not captured by the study. Finally, the patient cohort was defined using the 8th edition of the TNM staging system, whereas the 9th edition is now available. To address these limitations, future research should take the following approaches: (1) employ more robust methodologies, such as interrupted time series analysis or multi-stage optimization strategies, to quantify the intervention’s efficacy more rigorously; (2) conduct multicenter studies with larger sample sizes to enhance statistical power and validate the findings across diverse settings; (3) incorporate validated patient-reported outcome measures, including quality-of-life scales, to enable a more holistic, patient-centered evaluation of the nutritional care pathway’s impact; and (4) validate the pathway in patient cohorts staged according to the updated TNM criteria in the 9th edition.

Conclusion

This study successfully applied the JBI EBP Framework to hospital practice, effectively integrating evidence-based medicine into the clinical setting. The results suggest that multidisciplinary collaboration, particularly involving nurses based on a structured framework, is an effective way of bridging the gap between evidence and practice in clinical nutrition. Through iterative audit feedback loops and customized implementation strategies, the standardization of nutritional assessment for NPC patients has been significantly improved. Crucially, phased reporting and stakeholder feedback strengthened nurses’ sense of ownership and maintained their engagement throughout the implementation process. This study confirms that integrating EBP into a systematic quality improvement program provides a feasible and sustainable model for transforming clinical workflows and optimizing patient outcomes.
