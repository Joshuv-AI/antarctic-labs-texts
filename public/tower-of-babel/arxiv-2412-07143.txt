Let

An operator

One of the most well-known results is Hadwiger’s characterization theorem, which classified all continuous and rigid motion invariant valuations on the space of convex bodies (compact convex sets). Hadwiger’s theorem has wide applications in integral geometry and geometric probability. Ludwig has made significant and profound contributions to the field of valuation theory. Let
\phi

In this paper we give two measurable spaces:

2.2.
Background material on Orlicz spaces.

Young functions play a crucial role in Orlicz spaces.
A Young function
\phi
is a non-negative, real-valued function defined on
[0,\infty)
, which can be expressed as

\phi(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\varphi(s)ds\,\,\mathrm{for}\,\ t\geq 0.

The function
\varphi:[0,\infty)\rightarrow[0,\infty)
is non-decreasing, right-continuous, with
\varphi(0)=0
and
\lim_{s\rightarrow\infty}\varphi(s)=\infty
.
Young function
\phi
is convex, non-negative, continuous, and increasing on
[0,\infty).
Moreover,
\phi(0)=0
and
\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\phi(t)=\infty
. The function
\phi^{*}
is the complementary function to
\phi
, defined by

\phi^{*}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\psi(s)ds,

where
\psi(t)=\mathrm{sup}\{s:\varphi(s)\leq t\}

for
t\geq 0
. The pair
(\phi,\phi^{*})
is referred to as complementary Young functions. Additionally, the complementary function
\phi^{*}
of
\phi
is also a Young function. The following lemma provides further properties of the Young function
\phi
.

Lemma 1
.

[
13
]

If
\phi
is a Young function, then

\lim\limits_{t\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\phi(t)}{t}=\infty\,\,and\,\,\lim\limits_{t\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\phi^{-1}(t)}{t}=0

for all
t\geq 0
.

The Orlicz class
L_{\dagger}^{\phi}(\mathbb{R}^{n},|x|^{2}dx)
is defined as

L_{\dagger}^{\phi}(\mathbb{R}^{n},|x|^{2}dx)=\{h:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R},\,\mathrm{measurable},\,\rho_{\phi}(h)<\infty\},

where

\rho_{\phi}(h)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\phi(|h(x)|)|x|^{2}dx.

And the Orlicz space
L^{\phi}(\mathbb{R}^{n},|x|^{2}dx)
is defined as

L^{\phi}(\mathbb{R}^{n},|x|^{2}dx)=\{h:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R},\,\mathrm{measurable},\,\rho_{\phi}(\delta h)<\infty\,\,\mathrm{for}\,\,\mathrm{any}\,\,\delta>0\}.

A Young function
\phi
satisfies the
\Delta_{2}
-condition
if there exist
T\geq 0
and a positive constant
k
such that

\phi(2t)\leq k\phi(t)

for all
t\geq T
.
If
\phi
satisfies
\Delta_{2}
-condition, then the Orlicz class is equivalent to the Orlicz space.

The Orlicz norm
\|h\|_{\phi}
of
h\in L^{\phi}(\mathbb{R}^{n},|x|^{2}dx)
is given by

\|h\|_{\phi}=\mathrm{sup}\left\{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|h(x)g(x)||x|^{2}dx:g\in L^{\phi^{*}}_{\dagger}(\mathbb{R}^{n},|x|^{2}dx),\rho_{\phi^{*}}(g)\leq 1\right\}.

The functional
\|\cdot\|_{\phi}:L^{\phi}(\mathbb{R}^{n},|x|^{2}dx)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a semi-norm. Since we identify
h
and
g
when
\|h-g\|_{\phi}=0
, this turns
\|\cdot\|_{\phi}
into a norm, making
L^{\phi}(\mathbb{R}^{n},|x|^{2}dx)
a normed linear space.
For a sequence of functions
\{h_{k}\}
in
L^{\phi}(\mathbb{R}^{n},|x|^{2}dx)
,
h_{k}
converges to
h
in
L^{\phi}(\mathbb{R}^{n},|x|^{2}dx)
if

\lim\limits_{k\rightarrow\infty}\|h_{k}-h\|_{\phi}=0.

The following result involves the convergence of a sequence of functions in Orlicz spaces.

Lemma 2
.

[
13
]

Let
\{h_{i}\}\subset L^{\phi}(\mathbb{R}^{n},|x|^{2}dx)
be a sequence and
h\in L^{\phi}(\mathbb{R}^{n},|x|^{2}dx)
such that
\|h_{i}-h\|_{\phi}\rightarrow 0
. Then, there exist a subsequence
\{h_{k}\}
of
\{h_{i}\}
and a functional
g^{*}\in L^{\phi}(\mathbb{R}^{n},|x|^{2}dx)
such that

(1)\ h_{k}\rightarrow h
a.e.;

(2)\ |h_{k}|\leq g^{*}
a.e. for all
k
.

Convergence in norm provides a foundation for analyzing continuity in normed linear spaces.
An operator
\Psi:L^{\phi}(\mathbb{R}^{n},|x|^{2}dx)\rightarrow\langle\mathbb{M}^{n},+\rangle
is said to be continuous if, for every sequence
\{h_{k}\}
converging to
h
in
L^{\phi}(\mathbb{R}^{n},|x|^{2}dx)
, we have
\Psi(h_{k})\rightarrow\Psi(h)
.

Next we introduce the subspace
L^{\phi}_{a}(\mathbb{R}^{n},|x|^{2}dx)
of
L^{\phi}(\mathbb{R}^{n},|x|^{2}dx)
.
The annulus
A[r,R)
represents the region between two concentric spheres and is given by

A[r,R)=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:r\leq|x|<R\},

where
r
and
R
are real numbers satisfying
0\leq r<R,
and
|x|

is the distance from the origin to
x
.
The subspace
L^{\phi}_{a}(\mathbb{R}^{n},|x|^{2}dx)
is defined by

L^{\phi}_{a}(\mathbb{R}^{n},|x|^{2}dx)=\{h\in L^{\phi}(\mathbb{R}^{n},|x|^{2}dx):\text{supp}(h)\subseteq A[r,R)\},

where the support of
h,
denoted by supp
(h)
, is the set of points in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
h\neq 0
.
The definition implies that
h
vanishes outside the annulus
A[r,R)
.

The indicator functions and simple functions play crucial roles in our subsequent proofs. Define
\chi_{M}
as the indicator function of
M\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, that is,
\chi_{M}(x)=1
for
x\in M
and
\chi_{M}(x)=0
otherwise. If
s
is a simple function, then there exist pairwise disjoint sets
M_{i}\in\mathscr{B}
that have finite measure and scalars
\alpha_{i}
,
i=1,\ldots,l
such that

s=\sum\limits_{i=1}\limits^{l}\alpha_{i}\chi_{M_{i}}(x).

Using the definition of indicator functions and the Orlicz norm, Kone obtained the following result.

Lemma 3
.

[
13
]

Let
M\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
. If
0<\mu_{n}(M)<\infty
, then

\|\chi_{M}\|_{\phi}=\phi^{*^{-1}}\left(\frac{1}{\mu_{n}(M)}\right)\mu_{n}(M).

Background material on convex polytopes

Let
e_{1},\ldots,e_{n}
denote the standard basis vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{n}

Let
[u_{1},\ldots,u_{k}]
represent the convex hull of points
u_{1},\ldots,u_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

\mathcal{P}^{n}

For
Q\in\mathcal{P}^{n}
, the moment matrix
\mathrm{M}(Q)
is defined as

\mathrm{M}(Q)=\int_{Q}x_{i}x_{j}dx.

An operator
\mathrm{Y}:\mathcal{P}^{n}\rightarrow\langle\mathbb{M}^{n},+\rangle
is
\mathrm{SL}(n)
covariant if

\mathrm{Y}(\vartheta Q)=\vartheta\mathrm{Y}(Q)\vartheta^{t}

for any
Q\in\mathcal{P}^{n}
and
\vartheta\in\mathrm{SL}(n)
. An operator
\mathrm{Y}:\mathcal{P}^{n}\rightarrow\langle\mathbb{M}^{n},+\rangle
is called weak simple if there is a constant matrix
\mathrm{M}_{0}\in\mathbb{M}^{n}
satisfying
\mathrm{Y}(Q)=\mathrm{M}_{0}
, where
Q\in\mathcal{P}^{n}
with dim
Q\leq n-1
. If
\mathrm{M}_{0}=\textbf{0}
, where
0
denotes the zero matrix, then the operator
\mathrm{Y}
is simple.
Note that the moment matrix
\mathrm{M}
is a simple,
\mathrm{SL}(n)
covariant, and continuous function.

A closed cube
\mathcal{C}
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is characterized by the set

\mathcal{C}=\{(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}):c_{i}\leq x_{i}\leq d_{i},x_{i},c_{i},d_{i}\in\mathbb{R}\},

where
|c_{i}-d_{i}|=|c_{j}-d_{j}|
,
1\leq i,j\leq n
. For two sets
M_{1},M_{2}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, their symmetric difference is expressed as

M_{1}\bigtriangleup M_{2}=M_{1}\backslash M_{2}\cup M_{2}\backslash M_{1}.

Lemma 4
.

[
29
]

Given that
\varepsilon>0
and
M\in\mathscr{B}
with
\lambda(M)<\infty
, there is a sequence of finite closed cubes
C_{1},C_{2},\ldots,C_{l}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
\lambda(M\bigtriangleup\bigcup\limits_{i=1}\limits^{l}C_{i})<\varepsilon

3.
Proof of main theorems

Theorem 3
.

Let
n\geq 2
. If
\xi\in C_{0}(\mathbb{R})
, and for any
\delta>0
, there exists a real number
\gamma_{\delta}\geq 0
such that
|\xi(\beta)|\leq\gamma_{\delta}\phi(\delta|\beta|)
for
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
, then the operator
\Psi:L^{\phi}(\mathbb{R}^{n},|x|^{2}dx)\rightarrow\langle\mathbb{M}^{n},+\rangle
defined by

\Psi(h)=\mathrm{K}(\xi\circ h),

is a continuous and
\mathrm{SL}(n)
covariant valuation on
L^{\phi}(\mathbb{R}^{n},|x|^{2}dx)
.

Proof.

Since
|\xi(\beta)|\leq\gamma_{\delta}\phi(\delta|\beta|)
for
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
, then

\displaystyle|\mathrm{K}_{ij}(\xi\circ h)|

\displaystyle=\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(\xi\circ h)(x)x_{i}x_{j}dx\right|\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|(\xi\circ h)(x)||x|^{2}dx

\displaystyle\leq\gamma_{\delta}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\phi(\delta|h(x)|)|x|^{2}dx<\infty.

It shows that
|\Psi(h)|<\infty
for
h\in L^{\phi}(\mathbb{R}^{n},|x|^{2}dx)
. Furthermore, from
\xi(0)=0
, it follows that
\Psi(0)=\mathrm{\textbf{0}}.

Since

\mathrm{K}(h)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}xx^{t}|h(x)|dx

for any
h_{1},h_{2}\in L^{\phi}(\mathbb{R}^{n},|x|^{2}dx)
, we have

\displaystyle\Psi(h_{1}\vee h_{2})+\Psi(h_{1}\wedge h_{2})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}xx^{t}|\xi\circ(h_{1}\vee h_{2})|dx+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}xx^{t}|\xi\circ(h_{1}\wedge h_{2})|dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\{h_{1}\geq h_{2}\}}xx^{t}|\xi\circ h_{1}|dx+\int_{\{h_{1}<h_{2}\}}xx^{t}|\xi\circ h_{2}|dx

\displaystyle\,\,\,\,\,+\int_{\{h_{1}\geq h_{2}\}}xx^{t}|\xi\circ h_{2}|dx+\int_{\{h_{1}<h_{2}\}}xx^{t}|\xi\circ h_{1}|dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}xx^{t}|\xi\circ h_{1}|dx+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}xx^{t}|\xi\circ h_{2}|dx

\displaystyle=\Psi(h_{1})+\Psi(h_{2}),

which implies that
\Psi
is a valuation on
L^{\phi}(\mathbb{R}^{n},|x|^{2}dx)
.

Due to the
\mathrm{SL}(n)
covariance of
\mathrm{K}
, then

\Psi(h\circ\vartheta^{-1})=\mathrm{K}(\xi\circ(h\circ\vartheta^{-1}))=\mathrm{K}((\xi\circ h)\circ\vartheta^{-1})=\vartheta\mathrm{K}(\xi\circ h)\vartheta^{t}=\vartheta\Psi(h)\vartheta^{t}

for all
\vartheta\in\mathrm{SL}(n)
. Thus,
\Psi
is
\mathrm{SL}(n)
covariant.

To establish the continuity of
\Psi
, we need to prove that
\Psi(h_{i})\rightarrow\Psi(h)
as
h_{i}\rightarrow h
in
L^{\phi}(\mathbb{R}^{n},|x|^{2}dx)
.
Suppose that
\{h_{i}\}
is a sequence of functions in
L^{\phi}(\mathbb{R}^{n},|x|^{2}dx),
and
h_{i}\rightarrow h
in
L^{\phi}(\mathbb{R}^{n},|x|^{2}dx)
. By Lemma
2
, there is a subsequence
\{h_{k}\}
of
\{h_{i}\}
satisfying
h_{k}\rightarrow h
a.e., and
g^{*}\in L^{\phi}(\mathbb{R}^{n},|x|^{2}dx)
such that
|h_{k}|\leq g^{*}
a.e. for all
k
. Since
\xi
is continuous, it follows that
(\xi\circ h_{k})(x)x_{i}x_{j}\rightarrow(\xi\circ h)(x)x_{i}x_{j}
a.e. as
k\rightarrow\infty
. Additionally,
since
|\xi(\beta)|\leq\gamma_{\delta}\phi(\delta|\beta|)
for
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
, we have

|(\xi\circ h_{k})(x)x_{i}x_{j}|\leq|(\xi\circ h_{k})(x)||x|^{2}\leq\gamma_{\delta}\phi(\delta|h_{k}(x)|)|x|^{2}\leq\gamma_{\delta}\phi(\delta g^{*})|x|^{2},\,a.e.

Observe that
\gamma_{\delta}\phi(\delta g^{*})|x|^{2}
is integrable, by the dominated convergence theorem, we obtain

\lim\limits_{k\rightarrow\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(\xi\circ h_{k})(x)x_{i}x_{j}dx=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(\xi\circ h)(x)x_{i}x_{j}dx.

Thus,
\Psi(h_{k})\rightarrow\Psi(h)
as
h_{k}\rightarrow h
in
L^{\phi}(\mathbb{R}^{n},|x|^{2}dx)
, which demonstrates that
\Psi
is continuous. ∎

Lemma 5
.

Suppose that
\Psi:L^{\phi}(\mathbb{R}^{2},|x|^{2}dx)\rightarrow\langle\mathbb{M}^{2},+\rangle
is an
\mathrm{SL}(2)
covariant. Then there is a real constant

such that

\Psi(0)=c\rho_{\pi/2}.

Here,
\rho_{\pi/2}

Let
\vartheta_{1},\vartheta_{2}\in\textrm{SL}(2)
, where

=diag

.
The
\textrm{SL}(2)
covariance of
\Psi
implies that

\Psi(0)=\Psi(0\circ\vartheta_{i}^{-1})=\vartheta_{i}\Psi(0)\vartheta_{i}^{t}

(3.1)

for

.
Given that
\Psi(0)=(a_{ij})_{2\times 2}
,
by substituting
\vartheta_{1}
into (

\begin{pmatrix}a_{11}&a_{12}\\
a_{21}&a_{22}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}k^{2}a_{11}&a_{12}\\

. Hence,

.
Taking

.

Lemma 6
.

Suppose that
n\geq 3
. An operator
\Psi:L^{\phi}(\mathbb{R}^{n},|x|^{2}dx)\rightarrow\langle\mathbb{M}^{n},+\rangle
is
\mathrm{SL}(n)
covariant, then

\Psi(0)=\mathrm{\textbf{0}}.

Proof.

For a real number
k\in\mathbb{R}\backslash\{0\}
, let

\vartheta_{n}=\mathrm{diag}(k^{n-1},k^{-1},\ldots,k^{-1}),

then
\vartheta_{n}\in\mathrm{SL}(n)

\Psi(0)=\left(a_{ij}\right)_{n\times n}

\Psi
has the property of
\mathrm{SL}(n)

\Psi(0)=\Psi(0\circ\vartheta_{n}^{-1})=\vartheta_{n}\Psi(0)\vartheta_{n}^{t}.
A direct calculation gives

a_{ij}=k^{-2}a_{ij},\ \ a_{i1}=k^{n-2}a_{i1},\ \ a_{1i}=k^{n-2}a_{1i},\ \ \ 2\leq i,j\leq n;

(3.2)

and

a_{11}=k^{2n-2}a_{11}.

(3.3)

By combining (
3.2
) and (
3.3
), and the arbitrariness of

k
, we obtain

a_{ij}=0,\ \ a_{i1}=a_{1i}=0,\ \ a_{11}=0,\ \ 2\leq i,j\leq n.

(3.4)

Thus,
\Psi(0)=\textbf{0}.

Lemma 7

Lemma 11
.

Let
n\geq 2
. Suppose that
\phi
is a Young function satisfying the
\Delta_{2}
-condition. If an operator
\Psi^{\prime}:L^{\phi}(\mathbb{R}^{n},|x|^{2}dx)\rightarrow\langle\mathbb{M}^{n},+\rangle
is a continuous and
\mathrm{SL}(n)
covariant valuation, then there exists
\xi\in C_{\phi}(\mathbb{R})
and

\Psi^{\prime}(h)=\mathrm{K}(\xi\circ h)

for any
h\in L^{\phi}_{a}(\mathbb{R}^{n},|x|^{2}dx).

Proof.

We divide the proof into three steps as follows. First, we apply Lemma
7
and Lemma
8
to construct the functional
\xi
. Second, we establish the continuity of
\xi
.
Finally, we present some properties of
\xi
.

Construction of
\xi
.
For
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
, define
\mathrm{Y}_{\alpha}:\mathcal{P}^{n}\rightarrow\langle\mathbb{M}^{n},+\rangle
by setting

\mathrm{Y}_{\alpha}(Q)=\Psi^{\prime}(\alpha\chi_{Q})

for
Q\in\mathcal{P}^{n}.

It is shown that
\mathrm{Y}_{\alpha}
is an
\mathrm{SL}(n)
covariant valuation in
[
34
]
.
Next, we prove that
\mathrm{Y}_{\alpha}
is continuous. Applying Lemma
4
, there exists a sequence
\{Q_{i}\}
, which is the union of a finite number of closed cubes, such that
\lambda(Q_{i}\bigtriangleup Q)<{1}/{i}
for
Q\in\mathcal{P}^{n}
. If
x\in Q_{i}\bigtriangleup Q
, then there is a positive constant
d
such that
|x|\leq d.
Observe that

\mu_{n}(Q_{i}\bigtriangleup Q)=\int_{Q_{i}\bigtriangleup Q}|x|^{2}dx\leq d^{2}\int_{Q_{i}\bigtriangleup Q}dx=d^{2}\lambda(Q_{i}\bigtriangleup Q)<d^{2}/i,

which implies that

\lim\limits_{i\rightarrow\infty}\mu_{n}(Q_{i}\bigtriangleup Q)=0.

If
\mu_{n}(Q_{i}\bigtriangleup Q)=0
, then
\|\chi_{Q_{i}\bigtriangleup Q}\|_{\phi}=0
. Hence,
\|\alpha\chi_{Q_{i}}-\alpha\chi_{Q}\|_{\phi}=0
. In the other case, if
0<\mu_{n}(Q_{i}\bigtriangleup Q)<\infty
, then

\|\alpha\chi_{Q_{i}}-\alpha\chi_{Q}\|_{\phi}=|\alpha|\|\chi_{Q_{i}\bigtriangleup Q}\|_{\phi}=|\alpha|\phi^{*^{-1}}\left(\frac{1}{\mu_{n}(Q_{i}\bigtriangleup Q)}\right)\mu_{n}(Q_{i}\bigtriangleup Q).

Let
t=1\big/\mu_{n}(Q_{i}\bigtriangleup Q)
, we obtain

\|\alpha\chi_{Q_{i}}-\alpha\chi_{Q}\|_{\phi}=|\alpha|\frac{\phi^{*^{-1}}(t)}{t}.

Applying Lemma
1
, we have
\|\alpha\chi_{Q_{i}}-\alpha\chi_{Q}\|_{\phi}\rightarrow 0
as
i\rightarrow\infty
.
Thus, due to the continuity of
\Psi^{\prime}
, it leads to that

\mathrm{Y}_{\alpha}
is continuous on
\mathcal{P}^{n}
.
For
Q\in\mathcal{P}^{n}
with
\lambda(Q)=0
, it follows from the
\|\alpha\chi_{Q}\|_{\phi}=0
that
\alpha\chi_{Q}=0
a.e. in
L^{\phi}(\mathbb{R}^{n},|x|^{2}dx)
. Thus,
\mathrm{Y}_{\alpha}(Q)=\Psi^{\prime}(0)=0
, indicating that
\mathrm{Y}_{\alpha}
is simple.
By Lemma
8
, there is a real constant
c_{\alpha}
such that

\Psi^{\prime}(\alpha\chi_{Q})=c_{\alpha}\mathrm{M}(Q)

(3.5)

for
Q\in\mathcal{P}^{n}
.
Define the functional
\xi:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
by setting

\xi(\alpha)=c_{\alpha}

for
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
.

Continuity of
\xi
.

Let
\{\alpha_{i}\}\subset\mathbb{R}
be a sequence satisfying
\alpha_{i}\rightarrow\alpha
as
i\rightarrow\infty
. Assume that
\mu_{n}(Q)\neq 0
.
Observe that

\|\alpha_{i}\chi_{Q}-\alpha\chi_{Q}\|_{\phi}=|\alpha_{i}-\alpha|\phi^{*^{-1}}\left(\frac{1}{\mu_{n}(Q)}\right)\mu_{n}(Q).

It follows that
\|\alpha_{i}\chi_{Q}-\alpha\chi_{Q}\|_{\phi}\rightarrow 0

as
\alpha_{i}\rightarrow\alpha
. By the continuity of
\Psi^{\prime}
, we have
\Psi^{\prime}(\alpha_{i}\chi_{Q})\rightarrow\Psi^{\prime}(\alpha\chi_{Q})
. Since

\xi(\alpha)=\frac{\Psi^{\prime}(\alpha\chi_{Q})}{\mathrm{M}(Q)}\,\,\mathrm{for}\,\,Q\in\mathcal{P}^{n},\,\,\mathrm{M}(Q)\neq 0,

we have
\xi(\alpha_{i})\rightarrow\xi(\alpha)
as
\alpha_{i}\rightarrow\alpha
.
Similar to the proof of Lemma 8 in
[
30
]
, we obtain

\Psi^{\prime}(s)=\mathrm{K}(\xi\circ s)
, where
s=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{l}\alpha\chi_{M_{i}}
,
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
,
M_{i}\subset A[r,R)
and
s\in L^{\phi}_{a}(\mathbb{R}^{n},|x|^{2}dx)
. The detailed proof will be omitted.

Properties of
\xi
.
Assume that
\alpha=0
and
\mathrm{M}(Q)\neq 0
for
Q\in\mathcal{P}^{n}
in (
3.5
).
By Lemma
6
, we have
\xi(0)=0
.
For any
\gamma\geq 0
, there exists some
\beta_{0}\neq 0
such that
|\xi(\beta_{0})|>\gamma\phi(|\beta_{0}|)
. Then, there is a sequence
\{\beta_{i}\}
in
\mathbb{R}\backslash\{0\}
satisfying

|\xi(\beta_{i})|>2^{i}\phi(|\beta_{i}|).

Thus, there exists a subsequence
\{\beta_{i_{j}}\}
of
\{\beta_{i}\}
satisfying either

\xi(\beta_{i_{j}})>2^{i_{j}}\phi(|\beta_{i_{j}}|)\quad\text{or}\quad\xi(\beta_{i_{j}})<-2^{i_{j}}\phi(|\beta_{i_{j}}|).

Let
B_{n}
denote the unit ball in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Tsang (
[
30
]
) constructed a collection of pairwise disjoint balls defined by
\{M_{j}:M_{j}=r_{j}B_{n}+c_{j}e_{1}\}
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, which satisfy the following conditions:
(1)\ c_{j}>r_{j}
for
j\geq 1
;
(2)\ 0<r_{j}<1
for
j\geq 1
;
(3)\

c_{j}>c_{j-1}+2
for
j\geq 2
;

(4)\
(c_{j}+r_{j})^{2}\lambda(M_{j})=\beta^{\prime}_{j}
for
j\geq 1
, where
\{\beta_{j}^{\prime}\}
is a sequence of positive numbers in
\mathbb{R}
; (5)
\lim\limits_{j\rightarrow\infty}\frac{c_{j}^{2}}{(c_{j}+r_{j})^{2}}=1
. Let
\beta^{\prime}_{j}=\frac{1}{2^{i_{j}}\phi(|\beta_{i_{j}}|)}
for all
j\in\mathbb{N}
.
We define
h:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
by

h=\sum\limits_{j=1}\limits^{\infty}\beta_{i_{j}}\chi_{M_{j}}.

Since
\phi
satisfies the
\Delta_{2}
-condition, in order to prove that
h\in L^{\phi}(\mathbb{R}^{n},|x|^{2}dx)
, it is sufficient to show that
\rho_{\phi}(h)<\infty
. In fact,

\displaystyle\rho_{\phi}(h)

\displaystyle=\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\phi\left(\left|\sum\limits_{j=1}\limits^{\infty}\beta_{i_{j}}\chi_{M_{j}}\right|\right)|x|^{2}dx

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{j=1}\limits^{\infty}\phi(|\beta_{i_{j}}|)\int_{M_{j}}|x|^{2}dx

\displaystyle\leq\sum\limits_{j=1}\limits^{\infty}\phi(|\beta_{i_{j}}|)(c_{j}+r_{j})^{2}\lambda(M_{j})

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{j=1}\limits^{\infty}\frac{1}{2^{i_{j}}}=1<\infty.

If
\xi(\beta_{i_{j}})>2^{i_{j}}\phi(|\beta_{i_{j}}|)
for
j\in\mathbb{N}
, then

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{j=1}\limits^{\infty}\xi(\beta_{i_{j}})c_{j}^{2}\lambda(M_{j})

\displaystyle>\sum\limits_{j=1}\limits^{\infty}2^{i_{j}}\phi(|\beta_{i_{j}}|)c_{j}^{2}\lambda(M_{j})

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{j=1}\limits^{\infty}\frac{c_{j}^{2}}{(c_{j}+r_{j})^{2}}=\lim\limits_{l\rightarrow\infty}\sum\limits_{j=1}\limits^{l}\frac{c_{j}^{2}}{(c_{j}+r_{j})^{2}}=\infty.

Observe that
\int_{M_{j}}x_{i}x_{j}dx=\lambda(M_{j})(c_{j}^{2},0,\ldots,0)^{t}
for
j\in\mathbb{N}
, so

\displaystyle\mathrm{K}_{ij}(\xi\circ h)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(\xi\circ h)(x)x_{i}x_{j}dx

\displaystyle=\left(\sum\limits_{j=1}\limits^{\infty}\xi(\beta_{i_{j}})c_{j}^{2}\lambda(M_{j}),0,\ldots,0\right)^{t}.

Hence,

\left|\mathrm{K}(\xi\circ h)\right|=\infty.

If
\xi(\beta_{i_{j}})<-2^{i_{j}}\phi(|\beta_{i_{j}}|)
for
j\in\mathbb{N}
, then

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{j=1}\limits^{\infty}\xi(\beta_{i_{j}})c_{j}^{2}\lambda(M_{j})

\displaystyle<-\infty.

A similar argument as in the first case yields

\left|\mathrm{K}(\xi\circ h)\right|=\infty,

which contradicts the fact that

\left|\mathrm{K}(\xi\circ h)\right|<\infty.

It follows that
|\xi(\beta)|\leq\gamma\phi(|\beta|)
for
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
. Since simple functions are dense in
L^{\phi}_{a}(\mathbb{R}^{n},|x|^{2}dx)
, then for any
h\in L^{\phi}_{a}(\mathbb{R}^{n},|x|^{2}dx),
there exists a sequence
\{s_{i}\}
such that
\|s_{i}-h\|_{\phi}\rightarrow 0
. According to Lemma
2
, consider a subsequence
\{s_{k}\}
of
\{s_{i}\}
that converges to
h(x)
a.e. Due to the continuity of
\xi
, we have

\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}(\xi\circ s_{k})(x)x_{i}x_{j}=(\xi\circ h)(x)x_{i}x_{j},\,\,a.e.

Since
s_{k}\in L^{\phi}_{a}(\mathbb{R}^{n},|x|^{2}dx)
, it follows that
|\mathrm{K}(\xi\circ s_{k})|<\infty
. From the above discussion, we have

|(\xi\circ s_{k})(x)x_{i}x_{j}|\leq|(\xi\circ s_{k})(x)||x|^{2}\leq\gamma\phi(|s_{k}(x)|)|x|^{2}.

As
\gamma\phi(|s_{k}(x)|)|x|^{2}
is integrable, the dominated convergence theorem implies that

\lim\limits_{k\rightarrow\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(\xi\circ s_{k})(x)x_{i}x_{j}dx=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(\xi\circ h)(x)x_{i}x_{j}dx.

Additionally, due to
\|s_{k}-h\|_{\phi}\rightarrow 0
and the continuity of
\Psi^{\prime}
, we obtain
\Psi^{\prime}(s_{k})\rightarrow\Psi^{\prime}(h)
as
k\rightarrow\infty
.
This yields

\lim\limits_{k\rightarrow\infty}\Psi^{\prime}(s_{k})=\lim\limits_{k\rightarrow\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(\xi\circ s_{k})(x)x_{i}x_{j}dx=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(\xi\circ h)(x)x_{i}x_{j}dx=\Psi^{\prime}(h)

for any
h\in L^{\phi}_{a}(\mathbb{R}^{n},|x|^{2}dx)
.
∎

Lemma 12
.

Let
n\geq 2
.
An operator
\Psi^{\prime}:L^{\phi}(\mathbb{R}^{n},|x|^{2}dx)\rightarrow\langle\mathbb{M}^{n},+\rangle
is a valuation and
\xi\in C_{0}(\mathbb{R})
. If for any non-negative and any non-positive
h\in L^{\phi}(\mathbb{R}^{n},|x|^{2}dx)
,
\Psi^{\prime}(h)=\mathrm{K}(\xi\circ h)
, then

\Psi^{\prime}(h)=\mathrm{K}(\xi\circ h)

for any
h\in L^{\phi}(\mathbb{R}^{n},|x|^{2}dx)
.

Proof.
For
h\in L^{\phi}(\mathbb{R}^{n},|x|^{2}dx),
note that
h=h\vee 0+h\wedge 0
, where
h\vee 0
is non-negative and
h\wedge 0
is non-positive in
L^{\phi}(\mathbb{R}^{n},|x|^{2}dx)
.
From the definition of
\mathrm{K}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{K}_{ij}(\xi\circ(h\vee 0))+\mathrm{K}_{ij}(\xi\circ(h\wedge 0))

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\xi\circ((h\vee 0)+(h\wedge 0))x_{i}x_{j}dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(\xi\circ h)(x)x_{i}x_{j}dx

\displaystyle=\mathrm{K}_{ij}(\xi\circ h)

for
h\in L^{\phi}(\mathbb{R}^{n},|x|^{2}dx)
. From
\Psi^{\prime}(0)=0
, it shows that

\Psi^{\prime}(h)=\Psi^{\prime}(h)+\Psi^{\prime}(0)=\Psi^{\prime}(h\vee 0)+\Psi^{\prime}(h\wedge 0)=\mathrm{K}(\xi\circ(h\vee 0))+\mathrm{K}(\xi\circ(h\wedge 0))=\mathrm{K}(\xi\circ h)

for
h\in L^{\phi}(\mathbb{R}^{n},|x|^{2}dx)
.
∎

.

n\geq 2
.

\phi

\Psi^{\prime}:L^{\phi}(\mathbb{R}^{n},|x|^{2}dx)\rightarrow\langle\mathbb{M}^{n},+\rangle

\mathrm{SL}(n)

\xi\in C_{\phi}(\mathbb{R}),

and

\Psi^{\prime}(h)=\mathrm{K}(\xi\circ h)

for any
h\in L^{\phi}(\mathbb{R}^{n},|x|^{2}dx).

Proof.

By Lemma
12
, it suffices to show that (
3.6
) holds for
h\geq 0
and
h\leq 0
.
Let
h\in L^{\phi}(\mathbb{R}^{n},|x|^{2}dx)
such that
h\geq 0
. For
M_{j}=A[1/2^{j+1},1/2^{j})\cup A[j+1,j+2)
with

k\in\mathbb{N}

Observe that
0\leq h_{1}\leq\cdots\leq h_{k}\leq h

, a.e. Since
\xi

\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}(\xi\circ h_{k})(x)x_{i}x_{j}=(\xi\circ h)(x)x_{i}x_{j},\,\,\mathrm{a.e.}
