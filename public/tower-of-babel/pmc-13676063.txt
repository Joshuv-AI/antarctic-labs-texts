Polygenic risk score assessment of LDL-Cholesterol levels and progression to severe hypercholesterolemia and statin treatment; a longitudinal study from a large middle eastern biobank
Authors: Umm-Kulthum Ismail Umlai, Salman M Toor, Usama Aliyu, Shaban Mohammed, Nadeem Qureshi, Moza S.H. Al Hail, Mohammed Gomaa, Amin Jayyousi, Jassim M. Al Suwaidi, Omar M.E. Albagha
Published: 2027

Introduction

Hypercholesterolemia (HC), particularly elevated low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), is a major modifiable risk factor for atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease and affects approximately 20% of the global population [1]. LDL-C is influenced by monogenic, polygenic, lifestyle and environmental factors. Over 120 LDL-C polygenic scores (PGS) are listed in the PGS Catalog [2], but most were developed in European or predominantly European datasets, and their performance can be reduced in other ancestries because of differences in linkage disequilibrium, allele frequencies and effect sizes [3]. Moreover, existing LDL-C PGS have largely been evaluated using cross-sectional lipid traits or case-control definitions rather than longitudinal progression to clinically severe hypercholesterolemia. In previous work in the Qatar Biobank (QBB), multi-ancestry-derived PGS showed the strongest performance for LDL-C prediction [4]. Here, we integrated 11 published LDL-C PGS into a composite PGS (cPGS) and evaluated its association with treatment-corrected LDL-C, progression to severe hypercholesterolemia and statin treatment in the longitudinal QBB cohort.

Materials and methods

Study cohort

The study included 13,695 native Qatari participants from QBB with whole-genome sequencing and phenotypic data. Whole-genome sequencing and serum LDL-C measurements were described previously [4]. LDL-C values were corrected for cholesterol-lowering treatment as described previously [5]. Severe hypercholesterolemia (Severe-HC) was defined as corrected LDL-C ≥ 4.9 mmol/L while the statin-treatment group (Statin-Tx) comprised participants on treatment and had corrected LDL-C < 4.9 mmol/L. Participants not on statins and with LDL-C < 4.9 mmol/L were considered without severe hypercholesterolemia. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Boards of QBB (E-2020-QF-QBB-RES-ACC-0154–0133) and Hamad Bin Khalifa University (QBRI-IRB 2021–03–081), and all participants provided written informed consent.

Polygenic scores assessment and statistical analysis

Eleven published LDL-C PGS from diverse populations were selected as previously described [2,4,6]. PGS were calculated for all participants and normalized within each cohort using PLINKv2.0. In the training cohort, LDL-C and the 11 PGS were used in an elastic net model to derive the cPGS. In the validation cohort, linear regression assessed association between PGS and treatment-corrected LDL-C and reported adjusted R2 and incremental adjusted R2 over clinical covariates. Participants were stratified into quintiles (Q1 to Q5) of cPGS genetic risk. Cox proportional hazards models in the test cohort estimated hazard ratios (HR) per SD increase in PGS and by cPGS quintile. Discrimination was assessed using the C-index, and incremental improvement was quantified as ΔC-index and Net Reclassification Improvement (NRI) using bootstrapping (2,000 resamples). Sensitivity analyses were performed after adding baseline LDL-C to the model, and in age- and BMI-matched groups using stratified Cox regression. Continuous variables are reported as mean (SD), and categorical variables as n (%). Welch's t-test and chi-square tests were used to compare groups. Analyses were conducted in R v4.1.4, with P < 0.05 considered significant.

Performance of PGS in predicting LDL-C levels and hypercholesterolemia in the validation cohort

In the validation cohort, most PGS predicted corrected LDL-C, but cPGS (which included 1,102,026 variants) had the largest incremental improvement when added to the model that included age and sex (Δ adjusted R2 = 0.1289; P < 0.001; Fig. 1C-i). Participants with severe hypercholesterolemia had substantially higher cPGS than those without hypercholesterolemia (0.58 versus −0.06; P < 0.001; Fig. 1C-ii), and the proportion with severe hypercholesterolemia increased approximately 4-fold from the lowest (Q1) to highest (Q5) cPGS quintile (Fig. 1C-iii). The statin-treatment group showed a smaller but significant increase in cPGS (0.098 versus −0.06; P < 0.01; Fig. 1C-iv), with ∼1.5-fold increase in the highest versus lowest cPGS quintile (Fig. 1C-v).

Performance of PGS in predicting progression to severe hypercholesterolemia or incident statin treatment in the test cohort

In the longitudinal test cohort, cPGS predicted progression to severe hypercholesterolemia (HR/SD = 1.86; 95%CI 1.52–2.27; P < 0.001; Fig. 1D-i) and improved discrimination beyond age, sex, and BMI (ΔC-index = 0.043; NRI = 0.325; P < 0.05; Fig. 1D-ii). Severe-hypercholesterolemia progressors had higher cPGS than non-progressors (0.47 versus −0.04; P < 0.001; Fig. 1D-iii). The highest cPGS quintile had a 3.5-fold higher proportion of severe hypercholesterolemia cases and an HR of 4.62 (95% CI 2.40–8.89; P < 0.001) compared with the lowest quintile (Fig. 1D-v).

Prediction of incident statin treatment was weaker (cPGS HR/SD = 1.30; 95%CI 1.09–1.56; P < 0.01, ΔC-index = 0.016; P = 0.10; Fig. 1Ei). After adding baseline LDL-C to prediction models, cPGS remained associated with severe hypercholesterolemia (HR/SD = 1.38; 95%CI 1.11–1.71; P < 0.01), but the incremental improvement was not significant (ΔC-index = 0.008; P = 0.33; NRI = 0.272; P = 0.12), and no PGS predicted incident statin treatment.

In age- and BMI-matched analyses, cPGS remained associated with progression to severe hypercholesterolemia and showed greater incremental discrimination (HR/SD = 1.99; 95%CI 1.48–2.68; ΔC-index = 0.230; NRI = 0.494; P < 0.005). Similar, although less pronounced, findings were observed for incident statin treatment (HR/SD = 1.53; 95%CI 1.18–2.00; ΔC-index = 0.100; NRI=0.582; P < 0.005).

Discussion

We developed a cPGS and evaluated its performance in predicting corrected LDL-C levels, progression to severe hypercholesterolemia, and incident statin treatment. Most existing LDL-C polygenic scores have been developed using cross-sectional lipid measurements or case-control studies and have rarely been evaluated against longitudinal disease progression.

In this study, cPGS predicted future progression to severe hypercholesterolemia; the highest cPGS quintile had a 3.5-fold higher proportion of severe hypercholesterolemia cases and an HR of 4.6 compared with the lowest quintile. Importantly, the cPGS provided significant incremental predictive value beyond conventional clinical variables, including age, sex, and BMI, highlighting its potential utility for early risk stratification.

The performance of cPGS was weaker for incident statin treatment, likely because statin initiation also reflects clinical decision-making, healthcare access, comorbidities and patient preferences. Interestingly, the incremental predictive value of cPGS increased considerably in age- and BMI-matched analyses which suggests that cPGS captures risk information that is largely independent of age and adiposity and may be particularly useful for identifying individuals at increased risk despite otherwise similar clinical profiles.

Notably, when baseline LDL-C levels were incorporated into the prediction model, cPGS remained associated with progression to severe hypercholesterolemia; however, the incremental improvement in discrimination was no longer significant. This observation suggests that while genetic risk contributes to long-term susceptibility, measured LDL-C remains a strong predictor once available. However, the primary objective of a polygenic score is to identify individuals at increased risk before clinically significant LDL-C elevation occurs and before severe hypercholesterolemia develops.

In this study, cPGS predicted future progression to severe hypercholesterolemia, supporting its potential role for earlier risk stratification before traditional clinical risk factors become strongly predictive. Following external validation, such approaches may complement conventional risk assessment strategies and facilitate earlier identification of individuals who could benefit from preventive intervention.

Strengths include the large population-based cohort, whole-genome sequencing, longitudinal follow-up, independent training, validation and test datasets, and treatment-corrected LDL-C. Limitations include the absence of external validation, analysis based on single follow-up visit, and potential residual effects of statin treatment. To our knowledge, this is the first longitudinal evaluations of LDL-C PGS for severe hypercholesterolemia progression in a Middle Eastern population.
