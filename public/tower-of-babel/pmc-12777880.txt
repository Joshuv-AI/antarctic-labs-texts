Integration of continuous renal replacement therapy in patients on extracorporeal life support: timing, modality, and future directions
Authors: Jian Hou, Zhuandi Lin, Jianhao Li, Dayu Wang, Bo Hu, Guangtao Xu
Published: 2025

Acute kidney injury (AKI) and the need for renal replacement therapy (RRT) remain pervasive complications among critically ill cardiovascular patients [1–3]. Current investigations illuminate several interlinked key points that should guide clinical practice and research priorities, including the following aspects: (1) cardiorenal interactions in critical cardiovascular disease and extracorporeal support are common and prognostically important; (2) optimal timing, modality, and integration of RRT with other extracorporeal technologies remain unresolved and likely require syndrome- and device-specific approaches; and (3) future progress needs pragmatic trials and mechanistic studies that account for device-organ crosstalk and patient-centered outcomes.

First, the epidemiology and prognostic implications of acute kidney injury/renal replacement therapy (AKI/RRT) in cardiovascular critical care are stark [4]. Large registry- and cohort-derived data reviewed show AKI incidence of roughly 30–85% depending on the population and definition, and RRT utilization that varies widely (a few percent to >50%) in extra-corporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) and cardiogenic shock cohorts [5,6]. Importantly, both critical cardiovascular disease and those receiving temporary mechanical circulatory support face substantially higher risks of AKI and RRT and, correspondingly, higher short-term mortality [7,8]. The Canadian review on RRT in cardiogenic shock summarized these points and emphasized that RRT in this context is associated with markedly worse outcomes—a consistent finding across the collection [9].

Second, the limitations of extrapolating general ICU RRT trial data to cardiovascular populations might exist. Randomized timing trials in heterogeneous critically ill cohorts (ELAIN, AKIKI, STARRT-AKI and related trials) have not demonstrated clear survival benefits for early universal initiation of RRT and in some cases signaled harm (infections, dialysis-dependence) [10]. These trials largely under-represent critical cardiovascular disease and ECMO patients, whose hemodynamic vulnerabilities and susceptibility to volume-related organ interactions differ fundamentally from septic or postoperative cohorts [11]. Thus, the ‘one-size-fits-all’ timing paradigm is unlikely to be optimal for cardiovascular extracorporeal settings. The review authors therefore reasonably propose tailored thresholds-for example, a lower pH threshold and explicit consideration of diuretic-resistance and venous congestion as indications for RRT in cardiogenic shock - while acknowledging that prospective data are lacking [9]. These individualized thresholds reflect the unique physiology of cardiogenic shock, in which severe venous congestion, impaired renal perfusion, and metabolic acidosis progress differently compared with sepsis or postoperative states. Lower pH thresholds, refractory diuretic resistance, and clinical evidence of venous hypertension are therefore used as early surrogates of impending organ injury in this population.

Third, papers analyzing ECMO-associated AKI reinforce the bi-directional complexity of device-kidney interactions [12,13]. Longitudinal analyses show that many patients have AKI before ECMO cannulation and that pre-ECMO AKI and the need for post-ECMO RRT both independently predict higher mortality [14]. Notably, ECMO initiation itself often precedes a marked increase in RRT requirement among patients with preexisting renal impairment [15]. These observations suggest both patient selection and periprocedural management (fluid strategy, hemolysis mitigation, anticoagulation choice, and circuit integration) materially influence renal trajectories. Such insights argue for early nephrology involvement and protocolized strategies to minimize further renal injury during extracorporeal support [16].

Fourth, modality and practical integration matter. Continuous modalities (CRRT or slow low-efficiency dialysis) are favored in hemodynamically fragile patients because they allow gentler ultrafiltration rates and avoid precipitous intravascular volume shifts that can destabilize VA-ECMO or LVAD flows [9,17,18]. Where possible, integrating CRRT access into existing ECMO circuits can avoid additional vascular access complications [19]. Anticoagulation strategies (regional citrate vs systemic heparin) should be individualized given bleeding vs circuit life tradeoffs, particularly when full systemic anticoagulation is already necessary for mechanical support [20]. Although integrating CRRT into an existing ECMO circuit may reduce the need for additional vascular access, it also introduces technical challenges, including the risk of negative-pressure zones at the CRRT inlet, microbubble entrainment, and increased circuit alarms. These issues necessitate close monitoring of inlet pressures, air-detection systems, and circuit performance to avoid unintended ECMO flow disturbances.

Fifth, the collection points to research priorities. Pragmatic, device-specific randomized trials are sorely needed to answer timing, modality, and ultrafiltration-rate questions in critical cardiovascular disease and ECMO populations [21]. Trials should prespecify clinically meaningful, patient-centered endpoints (survival free of dialysis, functional status, and quality-of-life), and should capture mechanistic intermediate outcomes (renal perfusion metrics, biomarkers of tubular injury, hemolysis measures) [22–24]. Parallel bench and translational work to dissect extracorporeal circuit–mediated inflammation, hemolysis, and microvascular dysfunction will help design safer circuits and adjunctive strategies [25]. Emerging biomarkers (e.g., NGAL, cystatin-C) and renal perfusion metrics (including renal Doppler resistive index) hold promise for refining AKI phenotypes and guiding early intervention. However, their diagnostic and prognostic value in ECMO and cardiogenic shock remains insufficiently validated, and future studies should evaluate their integration into decision-making frameworks. Beyond traditional trial designs, advanced methodological frameworks should be leveraged to accelerate progress. Adaptive platform trials in cardiogenic shock may permit simultaneous evaluation of multiple renal support strategies, while careful target-trial emulation of high-resolution observational data can yield complementary causal insights when hemodynamic and temporal granularity is available. Preventative strategies also deserve greater attention: early AKI bundles that integrate biomarker-guided assessment, hemodynamic optimization, venous congestion reduction, and circuit-protective measures may help mitigate renal injury before RRT becomes necessary.

Finally, clinical implications for today are pragmatic: (1) identify and document baseline renal risk early in the CS/ECMO trajectory, (2) adopt conservative, individualized thresholds for initiating RRT that weigh hemodynamic tolerance and the reversibility of the underlying cardiac condition, (3) prefer continuous/slow modalities where volume removal is required in hemodynamically supported patients, and (4) engage multidisciplinary teams (cardiology, cardiac intensivists, nephrology) to align goals of care and RRT strategies. Until high-quality randomized evidence in these populations is available, careful phenotyping and individualized care represent the best path forward.

In closing, this collection successfully synthesizes an evolving, high-stakes area: renal support when cardiovascular ICU care is shaped by advanced mechanical circulatory and extracorporeal technologies. The papers collectively shift the field from descriptive epidemiology toward actionable, hypothesis-generating proposals that should catalyze the next wave of trials and translational studies. Clinicians should incorporate the nuanced, device-aware perspectives advanced here, and investigators should prioritize trials and mechanistic work that explicitly address the unique cardiorenal-device interface.
