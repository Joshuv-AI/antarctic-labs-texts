Frailty and Time-To-Theatre for Elderly Hip Fracture Patients at a Regional Hospital in Australia: A Retrospective Cohort Study
Authors: Stephanie Anderson, Sanne Norden, Ben Maslen, Luke Baitch
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveElderly hip fracture patients constitute a vulnerable population in whom operative management is frequently required. Comorbidities commonly exist and are often a factor leading to delays in entry to the operating room. Our regional hospital manages many hip fractures and we sought to understand the demographic and medical details of this group, and whether frailty was a factor in time-to-theatre and length of stay (LOS).MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort study of all patients over 65 years with hip fractures managed operatively at our regional hospital in Australia from 2018-2022. Demographics, and details of medical, surgical and anaesthetic management were collected. Data to allow calculation of a frailty score (the 11-item modified frailty index (mFI-11)) were collected to allow determination of a ‘frail’ and ‘non-frail’ cohort. These cohorts were compared with respect to time-to-theatre and LOS.ResultsOur cohort consisted of 453 subjects. Median age was 83.1 years (range 65-103). Most subjects (71.5%) were living in the community pre-fracture. 324 subjects were ‘frail’. Average time-to-theatre was longer in the frail cohort (28.9 h (frail) vs 22.0 h (non-frail), P<0.0001), as was average LOS (7.4 days (frail) vs 6.3 days (non-frail), P=0.0077). However, after adjusting for ASA score, age and sex, there was no longer evidence of these relationships (time-to-theatre, P=0.054; LOS, P=0.477). Post hoc subgroup analyses revealed that frail subjects had longer LOS if time-to-theatre was >24h, and that frail subjects had higher odds of any complication than non-frail subjects, whether time-to-theatre was <24h or >24h.ConclusionsThis study confirmed a demographic profile for elderly hip fracture patients at our hospital similar to that noted in large databases. Although the mFI-11 may retain value in defining clinically complex hip fracture patients, we were unable to find evidence of its utility as an independent predictor of time-to-theatre or LOS.

Introduction

With Australia’s ageing population, there is an increasing burden of hip fracture patients. 1 Indeed, in Australia in 2022-23, femoral fractures were one of the top three reasons for emergency admissions to hospital requiring surgery. 2 This complex group of patients have been the subject of important and practical clinical guidance from Australia 3 and overseas. 4 Early surgery has been generally recommended for hip fractures, as it has been associated with lower mortality and shorter hospital stay.5,6 Pathways to surgery are often affected by patients’ comorbidities such that early surgery is not always possible. In cases of delay, it is unclear whether it is these comorbidities, or the delay itself, that leads to worse outcomes. 7

Frailty is a known risk factor for adverse outcomes during hospitalisation in adults, including postoperative mortality, postoperative complications and length of stay (LOS) in elderly patients undergoing many types of surgery. 8 Whilst increased age itself is a risk factor for adverse outcomes after surgery, 9 the accumulation of physiological and psychological deficits associated with increasing frailty has been shown to be a better predictor for mortality and morbidity in some surgical cohorts.10-12 Frailty is a predictor for a variety of adverse outcomes for patients with hip fractures, including mortality, perioperative complications, and prolonged length of hospital stay.13-15

Although both frailty, and delays to surgery for hip fracture patients, have been associated with worse outcomes, there is limited research to suggest that there is an association between these two phenomena. Moreover, frailty amongst hip fracture patients has been studied in patients admitted to tertiary hospitals, but rarely in those admitted to regional hospitals. Regional patients suffer delays to surgical care related to transfer to centres with orthopaedic units,16,17 however the impact of frailty in a regional cohort is not well known. This study aims to describe the characteristics of an Australian regional referral hospital’s hip fracture patient cohort with a focus on frailty and time-to-theatre.

Methods

This study was a retrospective cohort study of elderly hip fracture patients admitted to and operated on at our health service. Inclusion criteria were: age 65 years and older, admitted to our regional hospital with a hip fracture in the years 2018 to 2022, underwent operative management at our facility, medical record available. Our hospital is a regional referral centre in Australia comprising 337 beds and serving a population of approximately 280 000. All surgical subspecialties are provided except cardiothoracic and neurosurgery. Subjects for this study were identified for inclusion from a list provided by our Health Information department based on clinical coding data. The electronic medical records of identified patients were accessed by members of the research team and a number of demographic, medical, surgical and anaesthetic data collected and entered into a secure database. Exclusion criteria: age <65 years, high-force injuries, pathologic or periprosthetic fractures, other similar injuries not requiring surgical fixation, no operation performed, transfer to another facility for operation.

Data collected included age, sex, date and time of triage in the emergency department, date and time of operation, and date of discharge. American Society of Anesthesiologists physical status (ASA) score, operation performed, type of anaesthetic, nerve blocks performed before surgery and as part of the anaesthetic, and analgesic drugs administered before and after operation, were recorded. Specific medical issues encountered before and after operation were recorded and reasons for delays to surgery, if they could be identified, were noted. Other data including subjects’ usual place of residence and discharge destination, and mobilisation post-operatively were recorded, when available. Sample size estimation of 500 was based on 64% frail in our cohort (from pilot data) and this number of subjects allowing detection of a 26% increase in time-to-theatre. We perform approximately 100 hip fracture operations per year; this led to a five-year cohort.

Specific data to allow a determination of frailty were collected that allowed calculation of a modified frailty index-11 (mFI-11) score. 18 The 11 components of the score are 10 medical comorbidities (hypertension, diabetes, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, ischaemic heart disease (prior myocardial infarction or percutaneous intervention/coronary artery bypass graft), stroke (with or without residual neurological deficit), peripheral vascular disease, heart failure, impaired sensorium) and overall functional dependence. Frailty has been variously defined according to mFI scores and we chose to use scores greater than or equal to 2 to indicate ‘frailty’.19-21 In a large cohort study from the USA, the median mFI-11 in hip fracture patients was 0.18 (i.e. 2/11) and these authors used this median value as their cut-off for analysis. 22

Descriptive statistics were used to describe basic demographic data. Comparisons between the ‘frail’ and ‘not frail’ groups were performed for specific outcomes including time-to-theatre using time-to-event analysis and log rank test, and LOS using t tests after transformation of skewed data. To investigate whether frailty as a distinct construct is related to time-to-theatre and LOS, we also performed multivariable regression, testing for relationships with frailty after controlling for ASA score, age and sex. Model assumptions were checked with qqplots and deemed to be satisfactory. Bonferroni or Sidak correction was applied when multiple testing occurred. Statistical analyses were conducted using StataNow/BE version 19.5 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA).

Average time-to-theatre was 33 h (95% confidence interval (CI) 30.7-35.4 h). Minimum time-to-theatre was 3.6 h and maximum was 232.8 h. The proportion of the cohort in theatre before 24 h was 46.8%, before 36 h was 68.4%, and before 48 h was 84.3%. In the frail and non-frail cohorts, average times to theatre were, respectively, 35.5 h and 26.9 h. In attempting to differentiate an effect of frailty and theatre delay on postoperative outcomes like postoperative LOS and complications, we compared frail and non-frail and delayed and non-delayed subjects using a log linear regression model. Post hoc comparison of these groups using a Sidak adjustment for multiple testing found that mean postoperative LOS was 20% longer in the frail cohort if surgery occurred after 24h, compared to before 24h from admission (95% CI 1-44%); we found no evidence for a difference in the other comparisons after adjusting for multiple comparisons (Table 6). Similarly, the occurrence of any postoperative complication was modelled using a logistic regression model and we compared the same subgroups, again with Sidak correction. Frailty versus non-frailty altered the odds of any complication in both <24h and >24h cohorts, however no evidence was found for differences when comparing the theatre timing groups in frail, and non-frail, subjects (Table 7). For patients whose time-to-theatre was <24h, the odds of any complication was estimated to increase by 141% if classed as frail (95% CI 12-420%). Although we found evidence for associations of frailty with the outcomes when investigated marginally, after conditioning on ASA grade, age, and sex, there was no longer evidence of an association with time-to-theatre (P=0.054 ) or LOS (P=0.477 ) . In the multivariable model, frail patients had on average an estimated increase in 5.85 hours for time-to-theatre (95% CI -0.09-11.79) and 0.53 days (95% CI -0.93-1.99) LOS.

Discussion

This study presents characteristics of an elderly hip fracture cohort at a regional referral hospital in Australia. Many of the features of the demographics of our cohort, and their perioperative management, are similar to those reported by the Australian and New Zealand Hip Fracture Registry (ANZHFR). 23 For example, the average age of our cohort is 83.1 years, compared to 81 years in the ANZHFR. Other features of our dataset, compared to the ANZHFR data include proportion female (68.7% versus 66%), proportion residing in the community pre-fracture (71.5% versus 74%), proportion receiving a nerve block at admission (79.9% versus 83%) and proportion in theatre by 48 h after admission (84.3% versus 77%).

Frailty and its association with delayed entry to theatre for hip fracture surgery has been noted previously. 24 Xi et al, in a retrospective cohort study of 194 patients from China, found a difference in time-to-theatre for frail versus non-frail subjects of greater than 2 days. 24 In our cohort, frail subjects did take longer to reach the operating theatre, but the difference was measurable in hours rather than days. Reasons for these delays to theatre in our cohort, in similar fashion to other studies, were due to a combination of medical issues and organisational factors. 25 Although delayed surgery for hip fracture patients has not always been associated with a higher risk of complications, particularly if patients have no specific medical issues preoperatively, 7 current Australian guidelines advocate early surgery 3 and we continue to strive to achieve this for our patients.

Various definitions of ‘frailty’ have been used in the literature, with various scoring systems used. 26 We chose to use the mFI-11 due to its simplicity, our ability to gather the requisite information from patients’ medical records, and its common use in other research studies. 26 Although we used a threshold score of 2 to define frailty,19,20 others have used different cut-off values. 27 In sensitivity analyses, using a cut-off score of 3, results for time-to-theatre did not change, although results for LOS were no longer significant. Also, regression analysis controlling for other comorbidity-based descriptors such as ASA score revealed that frailty became a non-significant predictor of both time-to-theatre and LOS. These results suggest that the relationships between frailty and time-to-theatre, and frailty and LOS, can largely be explained by ASA grade, age, and sex, rather than frailty as a distinct construct.

This study is limited by its retrospective nature and relatively small sample size. Other researchers, including one group from Australia, have described retrospective application of frailty scores and the limitations associated with such an approach. 28 Nevertheless, our unadjusted findings are consistent with other work in showing that frailty, however defined, is associated with longer time-to-theatre and hospital LOS. Many other indices of frailty exist and have been used in previous work. We appreciate that various clinical measures can indicate and co-exist with frailty, such as nutritional deficiencies and sarcopenia, 29 but that these are not readily defined in retrospective work. Also, although patients in our cohort could have been geographically remote from our hospital, we were unable to include details of transfer distances and times that could have been factors in patients’ delays. We also appreciate that the timeframe of our study includes COVID-related lockdowns and although our health service continued to offer emergency surgical capacity during this time, the effect of this on outcomes like time-to-theatre are not able to be determined.

Frailty scores based on medical comorbidities may be predicted to show an association between frailty and time-to-theatre, as medical comorbidities requiring preoperative treatment may contribute to delays to theatre entry. Other frailty scales used in clinical practice and research – such as the FRAIL scale 30 and the mFI-19 31 – and that have been used to predict complications in patients after hip fracture surgery, similarly use medical comorbidities as at least a component of their calculation. Our data contribute to the discussion regarding whether complications for patients following hip fracture surgery are contributed to simply by medical comorbidity, or by delays to theatre per se. When comparing frail and non-frail cohorts in association with time-to-theatre less than or greater than 24h, our exploratory data seem to suggest that frailty is associated with postoperative complications, and delay to theatre with longer postoperative LOS. We believe that the approximately 1-day reduction in postoperative LOS in non-frail subjects has important health economic benefits and is therefore clinically relevant; reducing LOS has been identified as a potential expenditure-reducing measure in hip fracture patients. 32 In any case, patients benefit from early theatre for hip fractures from fracture fixation providing both analgesia and earlier rehabilitation.

Conclusion

This retrospective cohort study of elderly hip fracture patients from a regional referral hospital in Australia has confirmed that the demographic and management profiles of our patients are similar to those described in registry data for this country. Although the mFI-11 may have utility as a means of identifying complex and frail hip fracture patients, it was not an independent predictor of time-to-theatre or LOS in our cohort of patients.
