Structure-forward targeting of claudins with synthetic binders
Authors: Alex J. Vecchio
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Claudins regulate molecular transport between cells via the paracellular route at tight junctions in epithelial and endothelial tissues and are prime targets for biologic therapies. Claudin–claudin interactions through their extracellular segments facilitate form and function of tight junction barriers, making for defined targetable surfaces. However, developing biologic or small molecule binders against claudin extracellular segments is challenging because they are small, dynamic, and no structures of assemblies exist to guide efforts to rationally design binders. Here, I review claudin targeting strategies, recent advances using natural and synthetic molecules, and the impact these molecules have had on claudin or tight junction biology. I surmise that the therapeutic promise of any molecule is dependent on deeply considering how claudins assemble and what structures they take within and outside of tight junctions. This review thus focuses on structure-forward themes, with the hope of providing a framework for researchers to apply this knowledge in the design and development of new claudin-binding molecules with diverse properties, mechanisms, and functions.

Goal

The goal of this review was not to restate all that has been done to target claudins or tight junctions with drugs, biologics, or biologic-like molecules. Great reviews have been written on that topic [1-9]. Instead, this review intends to provide a unique perspective on claudin targeting through the lens of structural biology, which can be leveraged to strengthen design approaches of claudin-binding molecules. I will briefly describe the goals and approaches of targeting claudins, highlight the challenges that small molecule drugs or nonsynthetic biologics encounter in claudin and tight junction biology, and promote the advantages of synthetic binders, which can be more readily re-engineered based on feedback from experimentation. The review will thus focus exclusively on synthetic binders or biologic-adjacent molecules that function through direct interactions with claudins. Such molecules hold great promise for early diagnosis, facilitated drug delivery, and therapies against claudin-driven diseases.

Claudins regulate paracellular transport at tight junctions

Tight junctions are the most apical cellular junction, represent the closest contacts between two cells in nature, and are composed of macromolecular assemblies of soluble scaffolding and integral membrane proteins (Fig. 1A) [14-16]. These ‘kissing points’ function less in cell–cell adhesion and more in regulating molecular transport through paracellular spaces. Tight control of paracellular transport is critical for homeostatic physiology of endothelial and epithelial tissues, while barrier maintenance must also be preserved as cells grow, divide, and move throughout development. The paracellular pathway is an attractive target to improve delivery of drugs [14,17], but the relaxation of tight junction barriers required to achieve it remains an ever-present obstacle.

Although each tight junction protein performs a unique function, the system has implicit redundancy to ensure that tight junction barrier function is maintained if individual proteins are compromised. The best representative of this balance between uniqueness and redundancy lies in a family of integral membrane proteins called claudins. Claudins direct tight junction formation, structure, and function as claudin-less cells cannot form tight junctions [15,18-21]. Human and mammalian genomes encode for 24–26 claudin sub-types—each exhibiting tissue-specific expression patterns and possessing sequences that vary in identity between 16 and 87%. Claudins are classified into two major groups based on sequence homology—classic and nonclassic—and four to five minor groups based on deeper functional or sequence analyses (Fig. 1B) [14,19,20,22]. Despite these differences in sequence, claudins are thought to converge on nearly identical tertiary structures, which enable each to interact and function uniquely [22-25]. For claudins, conservation of structure and divergence in sequence imparts each subtype with the blueprints for claudin assembly and novel interactions and functions.

Claudin structure

Claudins share a tetra-spanning transmembrane (TM) 3D structure [26-31]. They begin with variable length N-termini leading to TM1, which is linked to TM2 by an antiparallel four beta stranded extracellular segment (ECS) 1 (Fig. 1C). Between beta strand 4 and TM2 is extracellular helix (ECH1)—this helix has only been observed in the structure of claudin-15 but is believed to be present in other claudins due to a conserved consensus sequence of SLLALP [26,31]. TM2 is followed by an intracellular domain that links TM2 to TM3, with the latter having a helical region that extends outside of the membrane (ECH2) more than any other TM. TM3 connects to TM4 through ECS2, which has another beta strand that links to beta strand 1 on ECS1. The intramolecular interactions between ECS1 and ECS2 form the tertiary structure of a complete extracellular domain, which is shaped like a cupped left hand and has two regions, the ‘back of the hand’ and the ‘palm’ (Fig. 1D). Beta strands 3 and 4 are crosslinked through a disulfide bond conserved in all claudins while diversity in sequence and length of claudin C-termini form intrinsically disordered domains that end inside cells (Fig. 1E). Claudin structure enables claudin–claudin lateral (cis) and longitudinal (trans) interactions that form tight junction strands on one cell and extracellular barriers or pores in paracellular space, respectively [24]. Claudins integrated into strands through cis/trans interactions are rendered immobile, but the strands themselves are not—they break and reform dynamically to create the greater strand meshworks visualized by electron microscopy [32,33]. Claudin structure also facilitates interactions between the intracellular C-termini of claudins and scaffolding proteins, which tether tight junctions to the cytoskeleton (Fig. 1A) [34]. For such a simple fold, claudin structural homology masks each subtype’s functional novelty. Our understanding of each subtype’s unique function, but more importantly when, where, and how one member assembles with others, remains mostly incomplete, obscuring the most effective approaches to target claudins.

Considerations for targeting claudins to modulate paracellular permeability

Despite major gaps in our basic understanding, it is logical to conclude that claudin-binding molecules could increase or decrease paracellular permeability through tissues by disruption of claudin–claudin interactions or physical blockage of paracellular channels. Increasing tight junction permeability could facilitate drug delivery through these restrictive spaces to treat tissue-specific diseases. Central nervous system (CNS) disorders, for instance, are recalcitrant to delivery of drugs due to the blood–brain barrier (BBB) [4,9,35,36]. The BBB is the diffusion barrier that limits paracellular transport of molecules from the blood to the brain and is formed by an interdependent network of endothelial cells whose properties are precisely regulated by tight junctions, specifically claudin-5 [37-41]. Binders that transiently and reversibly open BBB tight junctions would be powerful tools to enable delivery of therapeutics through the BBB to treat CNS disorders. The epidermal skin barrier formed by claudin-1 and other claudins is another example where drug delivery could be hastened by claudin targeting [42-46]. Decreasing tight junction permeability may be the goal in treating disease states caused by unrestricted paracellular leakiness. Inflammatory bowel diseases (IBD) like Crohn’s, ulcerative colitis, and celiac disease result from dysregulated gastrointestinal barrier function, often due to the overexpression of pore-forming claudins like claudin-2, and can be attenuated by blocking claudin-2 channels [47,48]. Limiting permeability could be accomplished by increasing barrier-forming claudin or decreasing pore-forming claudin expression and lifespans at the tight junction—a challenge for binders—or by stabilizing the assembly of barrier-forming claudins or destabilizing channel-forming claudin assembly. The latter may be achievable by claudin binders. If modulating paracellular permeability of a tissue is the goal, then targeting of single or multiple claudin subtypes could be a viable approach. How to develop or design synthetic binders for this purpose is dependent on the structures and dynamics of individual claudins and the oligomeric organization of claudins at large in tight junctions. Because tuning native opening or closing of tight junctions is the goal, in-depth understanding of claudin assembly and the structures of barriers and pores that direct paracellular transport becomes a necessity.

Considerations for targeting claudins to disrupt cellular events

Which tissue to target and when depends on which claudin subtype resides in that tissue and where (apical or sub-apical). To confound things, claudins are often not localized exclusively to tight junctions and instead migrate out to diffuse within apical or basal membranes [14,49,50]. Here, claudins interact with other membrane proteins, alter cell signaling cascades, or become receptors for viral or bacterial proteins [51-55]. Cancers often employ claudins to facilitate their rapid growth and migration [3,56,57]. The tight junctions of these cells are damaged or nonexistent, and so claudins in basolateral membranes instead function here to regulate cell growth, proliferation, and stemness—especially as it pertains to the epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT)—a natural process during tissue development that is hijacked by cancer cells to escape cell death and metastasize [58-60]. By tuning the expression levels and subtypes present on the surfaces of their membranes, cancer cells alter cell adhesion and promote motility—triggering EMT. Because cancers are tissue-specific, the claudin subtypes hijacked by cancer cells vary. Strategically, because the complexes that claudins form with themselves or with other proteins may be smaller and more accessible when nontight junction localized, monomeric claudin targeting may be the preferred approach to diagnose or treat these types of diseases. Nonsynthetic antibody-based binders have successfully been used to target claudins −1, −6, and −18.2 in cancer using such approaches, which will be discussed in more detail [61-67]. Therefore, the design and development of synthetic binders can be more rational and empirical compared with barrier disruption approaches and can take advantage of in silico or in vitro techniques available in many laboratories. Because altering claudin interactions with themselves or other proteins is the aim, knowing how claudins interact and where interactions occur can improve success.

Claudin targeting through a structural biology lens

Despite their overweight functional importance in and out of tight junctions, claudins are small in molecular mass at ~24 kDa. Structural biology has shown that this mass is largely overestimated because 60% of it is either unstructured, intracellular, or embedded in membranes—making for only ~10 kDa of ‘functional’ mass that is targetable or accessible [68]. This 10 kDa surface constitutes the extracellular domain (Fig. 2A). Although the 60% of claudin structure that lies outside of the extracellular domain may also be important for claudin assembly or cell signaling, it is practically impossible to target these regions with binders or even drugs, as membrane penetration is a prerequisite. Therefore, although small and dynamic, the extracellular domain represents the most promising surface to target binders against.

The claudin extracellular structure resembles a left hand with ‘back of the hand’ and ‘palm’ regions (Fig. 1D). The ‘back of the hand’ is devoid of significant grooves or pockets and is challenging to rationally target with small or large molecules (Fig. 2A). The ‘palm’ is similar but has a small yet pronounced cleft toward the center formed by the bases of the beta strands and ECH1. This cleft may only accommodate small molecules or peptides. The function of this cleft is unknown but is unlikely to be used in claudin interactions, so binders would do little to alter tight junction permeability or claudin-related cellular events. The ‘palm’, which forms the cupped portion of the hand, is clearly the most antigenic surface and thus ideal to target binders against. In vitro panning or in silico docking campaigns could take this information into account in their experimental designs to increase the likelihood of finding ‘palm’ region binders.

The structural assessment and theoretical targeting approaches outlined above, however, are limiting because they simplify the complex organization of claudins to assume that they exist in isolation. Isolated claudin monomers, which are a dominant biophysical state in vitro, have value as input models for in vitro and in silico campaigns that intend to target nonjunctional claudins. Synthetic binders that are developed to target isolated claudins could be used to block their interactions with other membrane proteins or pathogenic proteins from viruses or bacteria to alter cell signaling events and diagnose or treat diseases, such as food poisoning, hepatitis, and cancers. However, targeting claudins in intact tight junctions to modulate paracellular permeability requires more complex and less well-known structural models.

Is the extracellular domain of claudins accessible in tissues the same way it is in vitro? To appreciate the challenges that need to be overcome to disrupt claudin assembly and open or close tight junction barriers we need to visualize the structure(s) of claudin assemblies in vivo. However, structural biology has not yet resolved these organizations in even near-atomic detail. Fortunately, several models of claudin assemblies have been proposed, and some have been experimentally validated [69-74]. Because many models are variants of the antiparallel double-row model proposed by Suzuki et. al, this serves as a good starting point [69]. In this model, 12 claudins on two cells (six per cell) assemble in cis and trans, forming two adjacent hourglass-shaped channels in paracellular space (Fig. 2B). In cis, the secondary structural elements of the extracellular domain interact, with the ‘palm’ region facing the ‘back of the hand’, yielding a stack of cupped left hands on one membrane (Fig. 2C inset). This is repeated on the opposite membrane and is related by two, 180° rotations in x and y. These two rows on opposing membranes form a ‘funnel’ and join with their equivalents to form hourglass-shaped channels (Fig. 2B). Depending on whether a claudin is pore- or barrier-forming, the apertures or arrangements of funnels and hourglasses may vary to allow or restrict molecular transport, respectively. The ‘palm’ regions form the interior of the hourglass, while ‘back of the hand’ regions are shielded through claudin–claudin interactions. In this organization, both regions, which are small to begin with, are now inaccessible to a binder of standard size (say, > 20 kDa). How then, might we target these assemblies to break or fortify barriers?

Challenges and opportunities to alter tight junctions with claudin binders

Disruption of tight junction barriers is ideally accomplished through high specificity and affinity binding of a synthetic binder to claudins, where the binder’s association and dissociation rates could be tuned by altering its chemical or physical properties. For transient reversible opening of tight junction barriers for drug delivery, for instance, fast association and slow dissociation rates (on the order of minutes or hours) would allow for short-term weakening of paracellular permeability upon delivery of the binder to open a window of time to deliver drugs through these spaces without long-term barrier breakdown that could alter tissue homeostasis, possibly irreversibly (Fig. 2C). For tight junction fortification to quell leakiness, specific high-affinity interactions with claudins would benefit from slow dissociation rates as well. Here, selective interactions with barrier-forming claudins to strengthen their assemblies or specific interactions with pore-formers to disrupt them could be made tunable and time dependent. Discovering molecules with such biophysical properties or re-engineering existing claudin-binding molecules is likely to have very limited success, whereas designing such molecules iteratively via experimentation could be more tractable. To understand why success has been limited thus far, we must return to our structural models to reveal best approaches.

The antiparallel double-row model and in vivo experiments have confirmed that ECH1 and ECH2, and the extracellular loops (ECL) that connect secondary structural elements, play important roles in cis and trans interactions, respectively (Fig. 2B) [69,71-73]. The two ECH and ECL4 form the top and bottom of the hourglasses and are thus accessible to binders. However, the dimensions of the top and bottom of the hourglasses are ~16 × 28 Å and the membranes are ~40 Å apart, so it is clear that only small drugs or low molecular weight binders could access these spaces. However, such molecules may be too small to actually disrupt claudin assembly and may be more amenable as pore blockers. Although these spaces are small and the ‘palm’ region partially inaccessible due to claudin–claudin interactions, nature has developed an effective method to circumvent these challenges and disrupt tight junction assembly.

Lessons from a natural claudin binder

One ideal claudin-binding protein is the C-terminal domain of Clostridium perfringens enterotoxin (cCpE) (Fig. 2C). The mode of cCpE binding to claudins and its mechanism for disrupting their assemblies have been determined and reviewed previously [12,75-79]. In brief, cCpE binds the entire extracellular palm surface of receptor claudins and employs ECS2 and a five-residue sequence within it to bind cCpE selectively and with high affinity. Sequence and potentially structural differences in claudin ECS2 and ECLs define receptors from nonreceptors. Wild type and mutant cCpE as well as peptides derived from it have been shown to open tight junction barriers and are being used to detect claudin overexpressing cancers and deliver drugs through the BBB in model endothelia [80-95]. However, despite its direct and 1 : 1 binding mode to claudins, cCpE is limited for widespread use because it preferentially binds only a subset of claudins with low nanomolar affinity, while binding most claudins poorly [13,30,31,77]. This selectivity makes cCpE tunability difficult because no matter how or where it is altered, cCpE will likely retain higher affinity for receptor claudins versus target nonreceptor claudins. For example, the skin and BBB are composed of claudins with low to no cCpE binding capacity (claudin-1 and claudin-5) [40,42]. cCpEs engineered to bind these nonreceptor claudins would still preferentially bind receptor claudins like claudin-4 present within versus their intended targets. In tight junctions devoid of receptor claudins, this approach may be sound. However, in tissues with receptors present, cCpE-induced damage may be less controllable. Moreover, without direct delivery to their intended tissue, these cCpEs will bind receptor claudins in other tissues along the way to their target, disrupting off-target tissues, increasing off-target effects, and making oral, inhalation, transdermal or injection drug delivery systems impossible. As a diagnostic tool for detecting receptor claudin overexpressing cancers, for instance, cCpE holds promise—but as a barrier disruptor, many challenges remain. Despite cCpE’s limitations as a therapeutic binder, its natural claudin-binding ability and mechanism of disrupting claudin assembly and tight junction permeability serve as a useful prototype to base design of first-generation synthetic binders on. These binders could function like cCpE to target and bind claudins well or to open barriers but have tunable properties where modifications could alter and improve their claudin selectivity. To succeed, understanding cCpE’s mechanism of claudin binding and disassembly becomes important to inform binder design strategies.

Like a paracellular pore, cCpE is small (15 kDa) with o dimensions ~28 × 38 Å—slightly larger than the aperoture of the hourglass (~16 × 28 Å). Superpositioning the crystal structure of cCpE bound to claudin-4 onto one claudin within the antiparallel double-row model helps us to visualize how cCpE can be used as a prototype for binder design and how it might disrupt tight junction barriers (Fig. 2D) [31]. cCpE binds the complete extracellular surface of the ‘palm’ region and conforms, remarkably, around neighboring claudins present in cis/trans assemblies with minimal steric clashes. This fit is exceptional because cCpE is thought to bind individual claudins in apical membrane pools to elicit CpE’s cytotoxicity and not require disintegrating claudins from intact tight junctions [78]. Why cCpE fits so well within a tight junction pore is an open question. How cCpE disrupts claudin cis/trans assemblies is not. Multiple crystal structures of cCpE in complex with various claudin receptors reveal that cCpE binding alters the helical structure of ECH1 and ECH2 and that claudin ECLs conform to cCpE’s surface (Fig. 3A) [27-31]. The structural changes induced by cCpE binding may thus disable claudin cis interactions governed by ECHs while the mass of cCpE sterically hinders trans interactions directed by claudin ECLs. Using cCpE as a guide, targeting these regions in similar ways would make for an effective claudin binder.

If cCpE conforms so well to intact claudin pores, how is it not a universal paracellular permeability enhancer or blocker? One might speculate that the pores assembled by receptor claudins differ in size or structure from those of nonreceptor claudins, which could explain cCpE’s inability to disrupt the latter in model tissues [79,80]. However, the antiparallel double-row model is based on assembly of claudin-15 pores, a nonreceptor claudin, yet cCpE conforms well (Fig. 2D) [13,69]. It appears then that just fitting into pores or the cupped left hand of a claudin is insufficient to impart barrier disruption. This makes sense because the cupped left-hand structure is likely conserved across claudins, so sidechain substitutions at key points or the dynamics or sequence of the ECLs could be what defines claudin receptors from nonreceptors. Indeed, crystal structures and functional assays show that residues on ECL1 and ECL4 play vital roles in cCpE binding and define receptors (Fig. 3A,B) [31,68,96]. Considering the key role of claudin ECLs in trans assembly and synthesizing ideas that were previously discussed, we can hypothesize that effective binders should: (1) be small enough to fit in a pore, (2) conform to the cupped hand structure of claudins, and (3) interact with both ECL1 and ECL4. This binding mode would have the greatest chance of altering cis and trans interactions in a manner like cCpE. In this way, cCpE is a prototype to model and benchmark binders against as it has been optimized through evolution to bind claudins. Unfortunately, its perfected function limits its applicability and tunability for broad use as a tight junction permeabilizer, but provides key insights for us to exploit in novel binder development.

What magnitude of disruption should we expect if a binder with these three traits can be developed? Perhaps physical disruption of claudins in assembled pores, which is difficult, is not required to open barriers? Why can molecules developed to bind individual claudins not be carried to tight junctions to disable their integration and assembly? These are logical questions that can also be answered by understanding cCpE’s mechanism of action and how claudins integrate into tight junctions. The static structural models presented mask the dynamics and kinetics of claudin assembly, which are vital to consider (Fig. 2B).

It is known that nonpolymerized (monomeric) claudins integrate and polymerize into apical tight junctions from basolateral pools [97]. However, because access to monomeric claudins in basolateral pools often requires the disordering of apical polymeric claudins first, hijacking individual claudins to deliver binders to tight junctions may have low success rates. However, after their roles in barrier formation are complete, some claudins are degraded while others escape to apical or remain in basal pools where they function in cell signaling events described previously [24]. Once out of tight junctions, there may be no way back in through the basolateral to apical integration pathway as tight junctions also prevent apical membrane proteins from diffusing laterally to basolateral compartments (‘fence’ function) [14,98,99]. This basolateral to apical integration pathway has been validated in model epithelial monolayers where binders against monomeric claudins, like cCpE, open barriers with greater magnitude and efficiency when delivered to basolateral versus apical compartments [13,80]. Specifically, barrier disruption is 2–5× greater upon basolateral versus apical delivery of cCpE, with apical delivery yielding ~20% increased permeability within 24 h. Here, the binder, ‘sequesters’ basolateral claudins away from polymerization and subsequent integration into apical tight junctions through disabling of claudin cis/trans interactions. While this sequestering mechanism is a very effective albeit indirect means to break barriers, it is, unfortunately, a difficult one to execute for directly breaking apical claudin complexes for drug delivery. This leaves physical disruption of claudins in assembled strands as the primary path forward.

Molecules that directly bind claudins and alter their structures, assemblies, or functions

Despite the biological, physical, and logistical challenges, synthetic or biologic-adjacent direct claudin binders have been identified through standard and nonstandard in vivo, in vitro, or in silico approaches. Briefly, I will update what types of molecules exist, how they were developed, their mechanisms of action, and what they are used for. The majority of these molecules do not qualify as synthetic binders, however. Those that do will be highlighted, while others can be classified as either synthetic small molecules or peptides, or natural peptides or biologics.

Small molecules

CL5B and M01 are examples of small molecules that directly bind claudins. CL5B was discovered by screening claudin-5 proteoliposomes against a small molecule library to detect obstruction of anti-claudin-5 antibodies [100]. Affinity measurements revealed it bound claudin-5 with ~32 μm affinity and docking showed it binds the palm region between ECH2 and beta strand 5. This molecule opened the barriers of claudin-5 expressing endothelial and epithelial cells 40–50% at 100 μm concentration and enhanced the transport of various sized molecules in vitro and in vivo. CL5B may work to disrupt paracellular interactions between claudin-5, but it also alters polymerization or integration of claudin-5 into tight junction strands, which may be an indirect intracellular mechanism. M01 is another small molecule inhibitor of claudin-5 interactions discovered by expressing claudin-5 in junction-less HEK cells and screening for molecules that disrupt cell/cell contact enrichment [101]. It binds claudin-5 with ~240 nm affinity around W41 and W57 in the palm region and decreases barrier function of epithelial cells 30–40% when applied at 50–100 μm concentrations. At high concentrations, M01 enhanced drug delivery through the BBB in culture models and mouse brain; whereas at 10 μm, it decreased neuroinflammation [102]. It also showed altered claudin-5 expression, internalization, and integration into tight junctions, which explains its barrier disruption properties.

New methods for screening small molecules that attenuate BBB function have recently been described that include Fish-based assay for Non-invasive Assessment of Permeability (FishNAP) [103]. This model system used larval zebrafish and assessed BBB opening of 2320 FDA-approved small molecules in the presence of a sedative, where an increase in BBB permeability by a lead molecule induces sedation. At concentrations at or below 5 μm, 78 compounds induced sedation and this number was refined to 11 after biological replicates. Testing BBB permeability of 1 and 10 kDa tracers showed that although eight opened the BBB, only two did so directly, LDE-225 and Trametinib. Three molecules were then selected to assess BBB opening in mice, and all three increased tracer permeability by decreasing claudin-5 levels. Although Fish-NAP is a powerful system to screen small molecules, the molecular mechanisms they employ to open the BBB are challenging to unravel due to it being a whole organism readout. Although none of these small molecules likely bind directly to claudins, FishNAP could be useful to assess the effects that direct claudin-binding molecules have on the whole organism, which makes it a powerful new model system.

Peptides

Thiostrepton is a natural macrocyclic thiopeptide antibiotic produced by Streptomyces azureus that is used to treat bacterial infections in domestic animals [104]. Watari et al. discovered in a library of 32 560 compounds that thiostrepton inhibited the interaction between claudin-4 and cCpE using a time-resolved FRET screening assay [105]. The affinity of thiostrepton to claudin-4 was measured to be ~6.6 μm. Moreover, in Caco-2 cells, which express endogenous claudins, including claudin-4, the authors found a concentration-dependent and reversible opening of tight junction barriers, as assessed by transepithelial electrical resistance (TEER). TEER decreased ~50% using 100 μm thiostrepton and transport of 4 kDa fluorescein dye was increased ~25%. Moreover, they found that these alterations to tight junction permeability were not a result of changes to claudin expression or localization, indicating that thiostrepton is a direct and semi-selective claudin-4 binding natural peptide capable of opening tight junction barriers expressing claudin-4 at > 10 μm without affecting claudin levels or organization.

Claudin-based peptides have been synthesized that mimic the ECS of claudins −1 and − 5. These include CLDN-153-80, C1C2 (CLDN-153-81), CLDN-131-81, C5C2, f1-C5C2, C5EFYDP and their derivatives [44,106-111]. Many of these peptides were developed before structures of claudins were determined and contain ECS1 sequences composed of beta strands 3–4, ECL2, ECH1 and the top of TM2. C5EFYDP is a D-amino acid peptide encompassing the top of ECH2. How or whether such peptides fold or whether they form claudin-like structure is an open question, but strands and helices form under different buffer conditions [107]. These ECS1 peptides induce significant and reversible opening of tight junction barriers in concentration-dependent ways. Many delocalize claudins and other tight junction proteins from tight junctions and are mostly effective at > 100 μm concentrations. Although some have been shown to directly bind claudins with up to ~150 nm affinity, most also get internalized, occluding their mechanism of barrier disruption, which could be due partially to direct disassembly of claudin interactions but also indirect hijacking of intracellular organization of tight junction proteins. These ECS1 claudin-based peptides have been used to increase permeability of claudin-1 and claudin-5 containing epithelial and endothelial tissues, while C5EFYDP has been shown to be a barrier sealing peptide that decreases permeability in alcohol-induced barrier disruption.

Nonclaudin-based peptides have been discovered through screening phage display libraries against bronchial epithelial monolayers and monitoring tissue permeability [112]. These include PN-159, a 19-amino acid peptide that resembles a sequence found in a hypothetical protein from Burkholderia bacteria; and PN-78, a 7-amino acid peptide that is found in many proteins across bacteria. Of these, PN-159 is most effective, directly and selectively binding claudins with ~300–800 nm affinity. Modeling suggests that PN-159 binds the ECS1 region in the cupped hand of claudins. Both peptides open tight junction barriers, increasing permeability > 80% at 10–100 μm in reversible manners. Despite binding to claudin-1 and claudin-5 better than other subtypes, PN-159 increased permeability of Caco-2 cells to a much greater extent than endothelial cells, which are enriched in these two subtypes. It was later shown that PN-159 binds claudin-4 and claudin-7, which explains this result [113]. Nevertheless, in epithelial and endothelial tissues, both peptides increased the transport of fluorescein dye and albumin, hinting at the magnitude of disruption they cause. A cyclic variant of PN-78 also increased permeability of tissues 80% at 100 μm but surprisingly did not increase delivery of fluorescein [112].

In summary, the use of small molecules and peptides is extensive, and several stand out for further development or alteration. The mechanisms of barrier opening of these molecules are multi-faceted and, in some cases, unknown or only partially characterized, but generally not solely explainable through direct obstruction of claudin assembly. Most of the molecules that do in fact directly interact with claudins typically do so with low affinity and poor selectivity, which is highlighted by their effectiveness at high micromolar concentrations. Due to the small 10 kDa targeting surface of claudins, small molecules and peptides may always be limiting in their abilities and efficacies in claudin and tight junction biology.

Antibodies and antibody fragments

Anti-claudin monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) have been developed against claudin-1, claudin-2, claudin-4, and claudin-5 by the Kondoh laboratory, and against claudin-1 by the Baumert Lab [114-120]. These were discovered through injection of plasmid DNA encoding these proteins in rats and/or mice, which endogenously express their own claudin homologs; or using cell-free synthesis of claudin proteoliposomes [121]. These mAbs directly bind their intended claudin targets and close homologs with nanomolar to picomolar affinity and are more effective at lower concentrations (~700 nm) than peptides at altering tight junction permeability. Also, unlike peptides, mAbs cannot be internalized and generally do not alter claudin expression levels or trafficking, which highlights the advantages of biologics over other molecules. Surprisingly, anticlaudin-1 mAbs did not alter tight junction integrity and anti-claudin-2 mAbs fortified tight junction barriers. In this study, mAbs against claudin-1, claudin-3, and claudin-4 had none or negative effects to barrier integrity where the anti-claudin-2 mAb helped to seal barriers and protect tissues against leakage from inflammatory compounds [119]. This suggests that the regions of mAb binding to a claudin are important for determining its function and efficacy but also that targeting pore-forming claudins may be a strategy to better seal barriers through blockage of their pores or destabilization of their assemblies and composed strands to prevent channel formation. Claudin-5 binders have been used to open the BBB to enhance permeability, while claudin-1 and claudin-4 binders block hepatitis C viral (HCV) entry or suppress colorectal tumor growth, respectively. Anti-claudin-1 antibodies developed in mice prevent HCV infection and humanized forms of this mAb prevent infection in humans and treat liver fibrosis and cancer [63,64,120,122]. These results show that antibodies are powerful tools to alter claudin-based cellular events and disrupt tight junction barriers and that their synthetic analogs could improve or expand on their utility.

SAIL66 is a tri-specific antibody T-cell engager (TCE) that was designed to bind to CLDN6 with one fragment antigen binding region (Fab) and CD3/CD137 with the other, thereby activating T cells through CD3 activation and CD137 co-stimulation [123]. SAIL66 was shown to activate T cells in in vitro co-culture assays and in vivo, leading to increased T-cell infiltration of tumors. Details of the anti-claudin-6 Fab are not given so no mechanism is known, but such bi- and tri-modal TCEs are being engineered for antitumor efficacy to alter claudin signaling cascades in cellular events that influence cancer.

Antibodies against claudin-6 and claudin-18.2, two claudins involved in solid tumor cancers and gastric cancer, have progressed beyond the laboratory to the clinic and some have been used in clinical trials and/or approved by the FDA. These have been developed for varying modalities like mAbs, bispecific Abs, TCEs, antibody drug conjugates (ADCs), chimeric antigen receptor T-cell (CAR-T), and T-cell vaccine (CARVac) therapies. Summaries of this progress, as the molecules are well-established and often proprietary, can be found here [7,61,62,65,66,124].

Anti-claudin antibody fragments constitute either a two chain 50 kDa Fab comprised of a variable (V) and constant (C) domains with heavy (H) and light (L) chains, or a single chain variable fragment (scFv), which is a fusion of each V domain from the H and L chains linked together (28 kDa) [125]. I will discuss them from largest to smallest molecule.

As noted above, the antibody SAIL66 consists of an anti-claudin-6 binding Fab but details of its development and mechanism of binding are not easily discoverable [123]. The only other verified claudin-binding Fab is COP-1, which is a new and well-characterized synthetic binder. COP-1 was developed by immobilizing human claudin-4/cCpE complexes to streptavidin and panning it against a library of Fabs with biased sequences enriched in Ser and Tyr using phage display [68,126,127]. Despite being present in panning, COP-1 did not bind cCpE alone, but bound claudin-4 and claudin-4/cCpE complexes with ~50 and ~20 nm affinity, respectively [68]. These binding affinities were determined in vitro in detergents but COP-1 also bound claudin-4 in more membrane-like environments, like nanodiscs [128]. Biophysical assays showed that COP-1 binds claudin-4 and exploits the stabilizing nature of cCpE binding to facilitate its interactions. Cryo-EM structures of claudin-4/cCpE/COP-1 complexes revealed that COP-1 binds claudin-4 at three sites that constitute the top of TM1, ECL1/beta strand 2, and ECL2/beta strand 4 on claudin-4 (Fig. 3C). Of the six complementarity-determining regions (CDRs) on each chain, CDR-H3 forms an amphipathic helix and penetrates the membrane-embedded TM1 to access its binding site. Aromatic residues on CDR-H3 stabilize its helix and enable hydrophobicity. Because COP-1 binds the tertiary structure of claudin-4 in and out of membranes, it may have utility in applications beyond structural biology.

To test this, we further characterized COP-1 to show that it is a pan-claudin-binding molecule capable of increasing the paracellular permeability of model gut [13]. In this study, using recombinant claudin-1, claudin-3, claudin-4, claudin-5, claudin-6, claudin-9, claudin-15, claudin-17, claudin-18.1, claudin-18.2, and claudin-19, we found COP-1 bound all claudins with 60–92 nm affinity in the absence of cCpE. This binding affinity was similar in claudin-reconstituted proteolipomes and found to be claudin-specific, as other tight junction membrane proteins like occludin were found to not bind COP-1. COP-1 s 3D structure binding mode was validated in this study where it was found to not only bind human, mouse, and urochordate claudins, but also distant claudin relatives like MP-20, PMP-22, and the septate junction protein Kune-kune from Drosophila melanogaster, which share a tetraspanin structure [129,130]. In Caco-2 cells, which endogenously express claudin-1, claudin-2, claudin-3, claudin-4, claudin-7, and claudin-15, we found that when 500 nm COP-1 was delivered to apical compartments, tight junction barrier function decreased ~20 %, whereas basolateral compartment delivery decreased barrier function 40%. Furthermore, COP-1 tight junction permeability increased in a concentration-dependent manner and was reversible. Apical and basolateral barrier disruption was benchmarked against cCpE, and it was determined that apical delivery resulted in equivalent effectiveness between molecules, while for basolateral delivery, cCpE was more effective at increasing paracellular permeability than COP-1. These differences were a result of each binder’s unique claudin-binding mechanism, their size and affinity differences, and their differential accessibilities to claudin-binding sites when claudins are assembled (Fig. 2B). COP-1 s ability to open tight junction barriers when claudins are assembled naturally indicates that its binding epitopes are partially accessible and that its direct binding disables claudin interactions to facilitate paracellular molecular transport (Fig. 3D).

COP-1 is a first-in-class synthetic pan-claudin binder and is effective at much lower concentrations than many previously tested molecules because it binds directly to claudins with antibody-like high affinity. Its ability to open tight junction barriers specifically (targeting claudins) and nonselectively (binding all claudins) makes COP-1 a prototype to develop further improving its tailored binding and permeability properties. As a tool, it has many of the advantages of cCpE, but because it binds claudins broadly, it may have more widespread utility. It will, however, target claudins in and out of tight junctions nondiscriminately, so tissue-specific delivery is a current limitation in its application as a therapeutic.

Another antibody fragment is 6PHU3, a bispecific scFv fusion TCE that targets claudin-6 with one scFv and CD3 with the other [131]. The anti-claudin-6 scFv was developed by Ganymed Pharmaceuticals and binds ECS1 [132]. 6PHU, via its anti-claudin-6 scFv, binds human and mouse claudin-6 with unknown affinities. This molecule exhibited potent killing of cancer cells in vitro and in vivo in mice but only when both scFvs were present. This indicates bispecificity is an important component of effective claudin-binding TCEs in the treatment of cancer.

Anti-claudin nanobodies (Nbs) are the smallest (15 kDa) functional antibody fragments and constitute a single H chain V domain (VHH). These single domain nanobodies exist naturally in camelids and sharks and differ in the length or number of CDRs [133]. CLDN6-Nb-BiTE is the first TCE to use Nbs, which are more stable and easier to produce than Abs, Fabs, or scFvs [134]. Two anti-claudin-6 Nbs (6-Nbs) were discovered by injecting recombinant claudin-6 into a healthy camel and doing three rounds of immunization followed by phage display to isolate VHH genes. The two Nbs bind claudin-6 and its orthologs selectively over other subtypes with unknown affinity, but each binds to ECS2 with nonoverlapping epitopes—so the palm and back-of-the-hand regions. Both Nbs were conjugated via a linker to form a scFv, with two VHHs capable of binding one claudin-6 with each VHH, and this was further conjugated to an anti-CD3 scFv to form CLDN6-Nb-BiTE. This molecule was shown to exhibit potent antitumor activity and enhance T-cell infiltration in vivo after creating a self-amplifying mRNA version delivered by lipid nanoparticle encapsulation.

Other synthetic proteins

DARPins (designed ankyrin repeat proteins) are antibody-like in their affinity and selectivity but structurally do not resemble antibodies in any way. An anti-claudin-6 DARPin has been developed by panning a biotinylated claudin-6 peptide (IRDFYNPL-VAEAQKREL) against a phage-displayed DARPin library provided by Abiocenter (Jiangsu, China) [135]. This sequence constitutes ECS2. One DARPin called C6DP was identified to bind claudin-6 expressing cells with ~8nM affinity, and structural modeling predicted that it may bind the palm region near the ECLS and ECL4. Further tests showed it bound specifically to claudin-6 expressing cancer cells but not claudin-less cells in vitro, ex vivo, and in vivo. This DARPin platform is novel compared with ADCs in that it is two-step, with initial recognition of claudin-6 on cancer cells by the C6DP followed by SpyTag/SpyCatcher ligation and self-assembly to deliver the conjugated anticancer drug monomethyl auristatin E to tumors. This system allows for more precise cancer cell targeting with reduced side effects.

To this point, the methods used to discover the aforementioned molecules rely heavily on in vitro or in vivo screening/panning approaches, where attaining high-quality input material (i.e., stable homogenous properly folded monomeric/oligomeric claudins) is a prime challenge. Moreover, without these inputs, output molecules cannot be rapidly filtered, thoroughly characterized, or systematically validated. A modern approach to circumvent these issues is to use in silico protein design strategies [136]. In silico protein design empowers high throughput manufacturing of hundreds to thousands of novel molecules with binding potential against a target protein, using no biological or chemical reagents initially to screen binders. After initial in silico filtering, however, downstream analyses require robust experimental validation of molecular properties like proper folding and binding. These valuative experiments are amenable to high throughput platforms, too. As such, well-designed and executed in silico workflows may rapidly and reliably yield novel protein-based folds that bind target proteins at their intended sites with antibody-like precision.

Two classes of in silico synthetic binders have been designed using machine learning (ML). Class one are soluble analog mimics of claudins (synClaudins). Three synClaudins, which have hydrophobic residues that constitute their TMs substituted for hydrophilic side chains but retain their native ECS sequences, were designed from claudin-1 (CLN1_14 and 1_18) or claudin-4 (CLN4_20) (Fig. 3E) [137]. Two synClaudins self-associate forming oligomers, and all three bind cCpE with native claudin-like affinities owing to their native ECS. Moreover, CLN4_20, which forms oligomers in vitro, dissociates into 1 : 1 CLN4_20:cCpE complexes in the presence of cCpE. Similar dissociation of synClaudin oligomers is reported recently for CLN4_20 binding to Bacteroides fragilis toxin (BFT) [138]. This result shows that synClaudins oligomerize like natural claudins through their ECS, although these assemblies are yet to be experimentally visualized. Importantly, in silico designed claudin analogs that do not retain native ECS sequence or structure do not oligomerize or bind cCpE, making them nonfunctional. Although synClaudins were not tested to determine whether they bind natural claudins, they represent new tools to study claudin interactions and to screen binders or drugs against in vitro, owing to their ease of production, superior solubility and stability, and native ECS. They may also be used to attenuate paracellular permeability or obstruct claudin-mediated cellular events. In the former case, the native ECS of synClaudins may block assembly of native claudins and open tight junction barriers. In the latter case, synClaudins could sequester harmful molecules, such as CpE or HCV, preventing their binding to native receptors. Recently, synClaudin CLDN4_20 was shown to block BFT binding to native claudin-4 in colon epithelium, and to prevent BFT-mediated tissue damage in vivo and in mice—demonstrating synClaudins’ potential as therapeutics [138]. Future studies are needed to determine whether these or novel synClaudins can be used for similar translational purposes.

Class two of in silico synthetic binders are de novo designed synthetic claudin-binding molecules (synBinders). Using the de novo protein design pipeline Bind-Craft, we used CLN1_14 and _18 synClaudins as input to discover proteins that bind the native palm region of claudins [139]. With cCpE as a guide and positive control, seven de novo designs, which were predicted to be helical coiled coils, were expressed, purified, and tested for their ability to bind synClaudins in vitro. Six synBinders bound to synClaudins and all six were also found to bind natural claudin-1 and claudin-4 with 0.5–16 μm affinity (Fig. 3F). The natural claudin-binding ability of synBinders was surprising given that synClaudins were used as input. This result highlights the power of using synClaudins in claudin-binder discovery. Furthermore, in in vitro binding assays, two synBinders inhibited cCpE binding, revealing that both synBinders and cCpE compete for binding to the palm region of claudins—validating the initial design strategy. In vivo, the same two synBinders significantly inhibited CpE-induced cell death of claudin-1-expressing cells in a concentration-dependent manner. However, inhibition by synBinders was not observed in claudin-4-expressing cells, owing to the ~1000-fold higher affinity of CpE to claudin-4 compared with claudin-1 where the synBinders cannot outcompete the toxin due to lower affinities. Nevertheless, these and results from other groups show that de novo protein design has incredible potential to expedite the development of novel claudin binders or for the maturation of existing molecules for improved binding, selectivity, and efficacy [140].

In sum, numerous natural or synthetic molecules of various sizes, shapes, and chemistry have proven to alter claudin function in or out of tight junctions. Of these, only a subset has been shown to directly bind claudins, and even fewer still have been sufficiently characterized structurally or biophysically to reveal their mechanisms of action. More mechanistic characterization is critical for future success to marry our understanding of this system at sub-molecular and tissue-level scales. The promise of claudin binders is exhibited by recently approved drugs, which demonstrate the advantages that direct, high-affinity binders with widespread binding epitopes throughout the small claudin ECS have as therapeutics. Because antibodies and antibody fragments can be highly engineered, these or binders modeled from them have been very successful and thus hold future promise for increasing or tailoring binding modes to expand functionalities. The application of in silico design strategies can expedite the refinement of existing or the development of new synthetic molecules moored or unmoored from natural proteins and has recently had success in claudin targeting and binding.

Conclusions and perspectives

A structure-forward summary highlighting the binding modes and binding regions of five of the highlighted molecules to claudins reveals the primary takeaways. Of this small but well-characterized group of binders, two binding modes emerge: (1) those that bind the palm region of claudins, like cCpE, 6-Nb1, and CLN-bs; and (2) those that bind the back-of-the-hand region, like COP-1 and 6-Nb2 (Fig. 3G). These five binders are experimentally confirmed to bind directly to claudins; three are natural and two synthetic; and two have known 3D structures and elucidated binding mechanisms while other structures are predicted [31,68,134,139,141]. Through this exercise we can see that most of claudin’s 10 kDa ECS surface is covered by these two binding modes and that these molecules rely heavily on conformationally adapting to the 3D surface structure of claudins. This suggests that these two regions are the most: (1) favorable surfaces to target; and (2) capable surfaces to form interactions that result in biologically relevant poses, affinities, and timescales. It is no wonder that these five binders are effective both in vitro and in vivo in performing their intended functionalities.

In conclusion, as key regulators of cellular events, the prime fortifiers or permeabilizers of tight junction barriers, and gatekeepers of paracellular transport, claudins are premier targets for binders. At the same time, claudins are inherently challenging to develop binders against. Because much is left to be understood about how claudins impart structure and function to tight junctions and what roles they play in regulating or dysregulating critical physiological processes, they will remain at the forefront of basic and translational research. The complexity of the tight junction system, which involves claudins, scaffolding, signaling, and other membrane proteins, makes indirect or intracellular approaches at modulating claudin or tight junction function implicitly complicated and potentially dangerous. Direct approaches that target the extracellular surfaces of claudins, which we highlighted here, have demonstrated superiority in specificity and efficacy. To build on these recent successes, deep considerations into how claudins integrate into tight junctions and what structures claudins take in and out of tight junctions becomes critical knowledge. Application of this knowledge could lead to rapid and sustained development of binders with improved targeting and claudin modulating abilities. Infusing these ideas into multidisciplinary approaches and teams, holds promise to advance new claudin-binding molecules with novel functions and possibly enhanced tunability, controllability, specificity, efficacy, and safety.
