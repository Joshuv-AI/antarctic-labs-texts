Theory of Interpretations I. Foundations The research was supported in accordance with the state task of the IM SB RAS, project FWNF-2026-0033.
Evelina Danyarova, and, Alexei Myasnikov

Abstract
This is the first paper in a series in which we lay down the foundations of the theory of interpretations. We systematically study different types of interpretations and their properties. Some of these interpretations are known, while others are new. Each of them serves different purposes. In the last section, we describe applications of interpretations to Diophantine problems, first-order classification, isotypeness, definability of structures by types, elimination of imaginaries, richness, logical categories, and bi-interpretations with ℤ {\mathds{Z}} or ℕ {\mathds{N}} . Additionally, throughout the text, we pose some open questions that naturally arise in this context and provide the most typical examples, usually from algebra. The current literature is plagued by discrepancies and inconsistencies in definitions, concepts, and fundamental applications of interpretations. To address this, we thoroughly examine various principal notions, definitions, and arguments, bringing order to the existing theory. Simultaneously, we develop several key concepts, such as regular interpretations, regular bi-interpretations, and invertible interpretations, and outline their main applications.

Introduction

In this paper, we lay down the foundations of the theory of interpretations. We systematically study different types of interpretations and their properties. Some of these interpretations are known, while others are new. Each of them serves different purposes. Our main focus is twofold: on the one hand, we study particular properties of interpretations that are required for specific applications to various problems from algebra, geometry, topology, model theory, and logic; on the other hand, we introduce general notions, techniques, and methods that are useful for developing the theory of interpretations itself. In fact, we try to outline a framework of the emerging theory that encompasses its own notions and results, techniques and research methods, applications, and general viewpoints on various phenomena that occur in this area.

This is the first paper in a series; therefore, we discuss in detail various principal notions, give precise definitions, prove some basic results, and provide the most typical examples, usually from algebra.

Until recently, the prevalent viewpoint in model theory was that it deals with properties that are transferred via interpretation; hence, the interpretation itself is rarely an object of study, only the fact that it exists. However, it has become apparent that different types of interpretations convey different properties. Interpretations with parameters are particularly effective for undecidability and stability, yet they fall short when it comes to elementary equivalence, elimination of imaginaries, or richness. The distinctions between these various types of interpretations are challenging to articulate using commonly used definitions. Therefore, it is necessary to develop specific notation and terminology to formulate precise definitions, and more importantly, to facilitate the construction of proofs. Standard interpretations, whether absolute or with parameters, are well understood and require no further explanation. However, the role of regular interpretations, also known as interpretations with definable parameters, remains insufficiently clarified. The situation becomes particularly challenging with bi-interpretations. They entail stronger interactions between algebraic structures, their definitions are more involved and complex, and they provide more variety, describing different properties of structures. The best introduction to bi-interpretations is in Hodges’ book
[
19
]
, which describes absolute interpretations in detail. It also notes that by adding constants to the language, one can obtain bi-interpretations with parameters. It is important to note that absolute interpretations are very rare. Therefore, one typically deals with various types of bi-interpretations with parameters, among which regular bi-interpretations are the most advantageous. It is hard to work with such bi-interpretations keeping the parameters as constants in the language, so again, there is a need for precise terminology. To illustrate this point, the notion of bi-interpretability in Hodges’ book and the paper
[
26
]
is referred to in this paper as weak bi-interpretability, whereas the bi-interpretability used in the influential paper
[
3
]
is different; we term it here as the strong one. They describe different relations between structures and transfer different properties, with the strong bi-interpretability transferring more properties than the weak one. Sometimes, it is not even clear which type of bi-interpretability is used in the literature.

One of the main aims of this paper is to thoroughly describe various types of interpretability, particularly bi-interpretability, and to introduce the necessary terminology. While the exposition may appear pedantic at times, this seems unavoidable.

We also describe some properties that these interpretations transfer and introduce general techniques designed for applications of interpretations to particular problems. For instance, the classical Diophantine problem for a structure
{\mathbb{A}}
asks if there exists an algorithm that, given a finite system of equations with coefficients in
{\mathbb{A}}
, decides whether or not this system has a solution in
{\mathbb{A}}
. Typically, undecidability of the Diophantine problem in
{\mathbb{A}}
is obtained by providing a computable reduction of the undecidable Diophantine problem of some other structure
{\mathbb{B}}
to the Diophantine problem in
{\mathbb{A}}
. In most cases, the structure
{\mathbb{B}}
is the ring of integers
{\mathds{Z}}
, or its variations, whose Diophantine problem is undecidable — the famous solution to the Tenth Hilbert Problem. But the reductions themselves are very different; they heavily depend on the structure
{\mathbb{A}}
. Nevertheless, analysis of these reductions shows that they almost always come from a very particular type of interpretations, so-called interpretations by equations or, more generally, Diophantine interpretations. This means that there is a general uniform method to prove undecidability of the Diophantine problems, which greatly streamlines and simplifies the proofs and does not depend on the particular structure
{\mathbb{A}}
. We describe this and some other such methods in the paper, with more detailed discussions to be presented in future papers in the series.

At present, it becomes evident that interpretations serve purposes beyond merely transferring properties. We give two examples.
The first one concerns the fundamental problem of first-order classification of structures due to Tarski. This problem requires describing the algebraic structure of all models of the first-order theory
\mathrm{Th}({\mathbb{A}})
of a given structure
{\mathbb{A}}
.
It turns out that if
{\mathbb{A}}
is bi-interpretable with
{\mathds{Z}}
, then a structure
\widetilde{\mathbb{A}}
is elementarily equivalent to
{\mathbb{A}}
if and only if
\widetilde{\mathbb{A}}\simeq\Gamma(\widetilde{\mathds{Z}})
, where
\widetilde{\mathds{Z}}\equiv{\mathds{Z}}
and
\Gamma
is the interpretation arising from the bi-interpretability of
{\mathbb{A}}
in
{\mathds{Z}}
. Thus,
\Gamma
provides the algebraic structure of all models of
\mathrm{Th}({\mathbb{A}})
. For instance, the group
\mathrm{SL}_{n}({\mathds{Z}})
, where
n\geqslant 3
, is bi-interpretable with
{\mathds{Z}}

[
37
]
. Hence, a group
H
is first-order equivalent to
\mathrm{SL}_{n}({\mathds{Z}})
if and only if
H\simeq\mathrm{SL}_{n}(\widetilde{\mathds{Z}})
for some ring
\widetilde{\mathds{Z}}
such that
\widetilde{\mathds{Z}}\equiv{\mathds{Z}}
. This method works in a much more general context; one does not even need bi-interpretability here; it suffices to have an invertible interpretation of
{\mathbb{A}}
in
{\mathds{Z}}
. We discuss this in Subsections
5.3
and
5.8
.

The second one is related to classical results like fundamental theorems of geometry (projective, affine, symplectic, etc.), rigidity theorems, theorems on abstract isomorphisms in group theory and topology, and on categorical equivalence of algebraic structures. Quite often, interpretations hidden somewhere behind these results play a key role in the arguments. Furthermore, they offer a more precise description of the interactions between the structures involved, thus enhancing the results themselves.
See
[
5
]
for the fundamental theorem of projective geometry,
[
4
]
for first-order rigidity of irreducible non-uniform higher-rank lattices,
[
6
]
for abstract isomorphisms of Chevalley groups, and
[
2
]
for Mal’cev correspondence between nilpotent
k
-
groups and nilpotent Lie algebras over a field
k
of characteristic zero.

In the theory of interpretations, fundamental constructions and basic theorems sometimes require a categorical approach. This view treats interpretations as functors, particularly when examining properties like compositions of interpretations, their homotopies, invertible interpretations, etc. Following pioneering ideas of Boris Plotkin one can consider various “logical categories” related to structure
{\mathbb{A}}

[
43
]
. Plotkin viewed these logical categories as natural generalizations of the categories of algebraic sets over
{\mathbb{A}}
from algebraic geometry, where the algebraic sets are replaced by the logical sets that are definable in
{\mathbb{A}}
by sets of formulas in the language of
{\mathbb{A}}
. One can go even further and consider projective logical geometries over
{\mathbb{A}}
, where projective logical sets are the quotients of logical sets modulo equivalence relations definable in
{\mathbb{A}}
.
It turns out that these logical categories, especially the categories of projective logical geometry, provide useful instruments for studying categorical properties of interpretations. This lies outside the scope of this paper; we only briefly mention it in Subsection
5.7
in the context of bi-interpretations and will address it in detail in the next article of the series.

Now we say a few words on the principal types of interpretations and how they come about. The notion of interpretability was introduced by Tarski; initially, it was used mainly as a tool to prove the undecidability of theories. This approach is based on the following result: if a structure
{\mathbb{A}}
is interpretable with parameters in a structure
{\mathbb{B}}
then for every sentence
\psi
in the language of
{\mathbb{A}}
there is a sentence
\psi^{\ast}
of the language of
{\mathbb{B}}
with parameters from
{\mathbb{B}}
such that
{\mathbb{A}}{\models}\psi\Longleftrightarrow{\mathbb{B}}{\models}\psi^{\ast}
. Moreover, if the signature of
{\mathbb{A}}
is finite (or, more generally, the interpretation is effective, see below) then the formula
\psi^{\ast}
can be effectively constructed from
\psi
; also, if the interpretation is absolute (no parameters) then
\psi^{\ast}
does not contain parameters as well. Note that the Gödel’s first incompleteness theorem implies that the first-order theory of the arithmetic
{\mathds{N}}
is undecidable. Hence, if
{\mathds{N}}
is absolutely interpretable in
{\mathbb{B}}
then the map
\psi\to\psi^{\ast}
gives a computable reduction of
\mathrm{Th}({\mathds{N}})
to
\mathrm{Th}({\mathbb{B}})
, so
\mathrm{Th}({\mathbb{B}})
is undecidable. The only inconvenience here is that interpretations are often coming with parameters. To this end, Tarski, Robinson, and Mostowski proved in
[
49
]
that the theory of
{\mathds{N}}
not only is undecidable but it is hereditarily undecidable (see Subsection
5.1
below),
which leads to the following much stronger result: if
{\mathds{N}}
(or
{\mathds{Z}}
) is interpretable with parameters in
{\mathbb{B}}
then
\mathrm{Th}({\mathbb{B}})
is undecidable. The book
[
49
]
together with a survey
[
14
]
was very influential in the development of the theory of interpretations at the beginning, where the focus was on the decidability questions. Over the years, this research produced a lot of interesting interpretability results in algebra.

The next principal step in the application of interpretability comes from Mal’cev, who demonstrated how interpretations can describe groups that are elementarily equivalent to each other. The starting point here is clear: if
{\mathbb{A}}
is absolutely interpretable in
{\mathbb{B}}
by a set of formulas
\Gamma
, so
{\mathbb{A}}\simeq\Gamma({\mathbb{B}})
, and if
\widetilde{\mathbb{B}}\equiv{\mathbb{B}}
, then the structure
\widetilde{\mathbb{A}}\simeq\Gamma(\widetilde{\mathbb{B}})
, which is interpretable in
\widetilde{\mathbb{B}}
by
\Gamma
, satisfies
\widetilde{\mathbb{A}}\equiv{\mathbb{A}}
. However, if the interpretation
\Gamma
contains parameters then
\widetilde{\mathbb{A}}
and
{\mathbb{A}}
may not be elementarily equivalent. For example, for the field of real numbers
{\mathds{R}}
and elements
a,b\in{\mathds{R}}
with
a\neq b
one has
({\mathds{R}},a)\not\equiv({\mathds{R}},b)
(here for
c\in{\mathds{R}}

({\mathds{R}},c)
denotes the field
{\mathds{R}}
with an extra constant
c
in the language). In his seminal work
[
28
]
on the characterization of elementarily equivalent classical matrix groups, Mal’cev used interpretability with parameters, which are taken from a definable set. In this case, similar to the case of absolute interpretability, if
{\mathbb{A}}
is interpretable in
{\mathbb{B}}
with a definable set of parameters, one can still construct a map
\psi\to\psi^{\ast}
which gives a reduction of the theory
\mathrm{Th}({\mathbb{A}})
to
\mathrm{Th}({\mathbb{B}})
. Following
[
32
,
33
,
34
]
, we refer to this type of interpretability as
regular interpretability
(Hodges, in his book
[
19
]
, uses the term
interpretability with definable parameters
, but we feel that regular interpretability is shorter and avoids overuse of the term “definable”). Thus, the notion of regular interpretability is somewhat in between absolute interpretability and interpretability with parameters. The former fits extremely well for questions related to first-order equivalence but occurs rather rarely. Interpretability with parameters is more common, but the parameters are usually not in the language of
{\mathbb{B}}
, which presents an obstacle for elementary equivalence. Regular interpretability combines the useful properties of both: it can be used in first-order classification problems in a way similar to absolute interpretability and occurs almost as often as interpretability with parameters. On the downside, to get regular interpretability one needs to go deeper into model-theoretic properties of the structures.
Nowadays, regular interpretability gives a powerful tool in studying elementarily equivalent structures.

In general, there are many properties that are preserved under interpretations:
\lambda
-
stability,
\lambda
-
saturation,
\omega
-
categoricity, independence property, and finite Morley rank, to mention a few (see below). Nevertheless, even if two structures
{\mathbb{A}}
and
{\mathbb{B}}
are absolutely interpretable in each other, one can’t expect that they are “equivalent from the model-theoretic viewpoint” (or have the same model-theoretic properties). To achieve this, one needs a much stronger notion called bi-interpretation. Absolute bi-interpretations were introduced by Ahlbrandt and Ziegler in
[
1
]
. In the book
[
19
]
Hodges laid out the fundamentals on bi-interpretations and showed interesting applications. Later, Khélif and Nies linked the QFA property of a structure
{\mathbb{A}}
with bi-interpretability of
{\mathbb{A}}
and
{\mathds{N}}
or
{\mathds{Z}}
(see
[
27
,
38
,
39
]
). Recall that a finitely generated structure
{\mathbb{A}}
is QFA (quasi-finitely axiomatizable) if there is a sentence
\psi
in the language of
{\mathbb{A}}
such that for any finitely generated structure
{\mathbb{B}}
in the language of
{\mathbb{A}}
, if
{\mathbb{B}}\models\psi
then
{\mathbb{B}}
and
{\mathbb{A}}
are isomorphic. They also proved a similar result for the properties of primality and homogeneity.

For us here, an important milestone was the paper
[
26
]
by Kharlampovich, Myasnikov, and Sohrabi on rich structures, where different types of bi-interpretability, and especially regular bi-interpretability, were introduced and studied. Intuitively, a structure
{\mathbb{A}}
is
rich
if every formula of the weak second-order logic with variables for elements in
{\mathbb{A}}
is equivalent in
{\mathbb{A}}
to some first-order formula on the same variables. The initial result here is that if a structure
{\mathbb{A}}
is absolutely bi-interpretable with a rich structure
{\mathbb{B}}
then
{\mathbb{A}}
is also rich. But again, absolute bi-interpretability of structures
{\mathbb{A}}
and
{\mathbb{B}}
is very rare (for example, it implies that the automorphism groups of
{\mathbb{A}}
and
{\mathbb{B}}
have to be isomorphic). Fortunately, the regular bi-interpretability (more precisely, its variation which is called
regular half-absolute bi-interpretability
) strikes again and gives the required results. In particular, structures regularly bi-interpretable with
{\mathds{N}}
are rich
[
26
]
.

In this paper, following
[
26
]
we develop further various types of bi-interpretability. We believe that the regular strong bi-interpretations are the most useful and applicable; we study them in detail. Invertible interpretations are another new type of interpretations that we introduce here; in many cases, they are as useful as bi-interpretations, but more common.

In the last section, we discuss applications of interpretations to Diophantine problems, the first-order classification, isotypeness and definability of structures by types, elimination of imaginaries, richness, logical categories, and bi-interpretations with
{\mathds{Z}}
or
{\mathds{N}}
. To illustrate the concepts and techniques, we provide typical examples and fundamental results. Additionally, throughout the text, we pose some open questions that naturally arise in this context.

2
Interpretations and their codes

In this section we discuss various types of interpretations of structures. Some of them are well-known, others are new, and they each serve different purposes.

Interpretations provide very precise descriptions of some intrinsic relationships between algebraic structures. These descriptions are given by formulas of first-order logic in a specific manner, so they are very precise and unambiguous. When possible, we follow the Hodges’
[
19
, Section 5.3]
and Marker’s books
[
30
, Section 1.3]
.

2.1
Languages and algebraic structures

Let
L
be a language (also called a signature) with a set of functional (operational) symbols
\{f,\ldots\}
along with their arities
n_{f}\in{\mathds{N}}
, a set of constant symbols
\{c,\ldots\}
(
n_{c}=0
for every constant symbol) and a set of relation (or predicate) symbols
\{R,\ldots\}
along with their arities
n_{R}\in{\mathds{N}}
. We write
f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
or
R(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
to show that
n_{f}=n
or
n_{R}=n
. The standard language of groups
L_{\mathrm{group}}=\{\,\cdot\,,\,^{-1},e\}
includes the symbol
\cdot
for the binary operation of multiplication, the symbol
-1
for the unary operation of inversion, and the symbol
e
for the group identity; the standard language of unitary rings is
L_{\mathrm{ring}}=\{+,\,\times\,,0,1\}
. An interpretation of a constant symbol
c
in a set
A
is an element
c^{A}\in A
, of a functional symbol
f
is a function
f^{A}\colon A^{n_{f}}\to A
, and of a predicate symbol
R
is a set
R^{A}\subseteq A^{n_{R}}
.

A countable first-order language
L=\{f_{1},\ldots,R_{1},\ldots,c_{1},\ldots\}
, where the sets of function, predicate, and constant symbols may be finite or even empty, is termed as
computable
if the functions
i\to n_{f_{i}}
and
i\to n_{R_{i}}
, defining the arities of
f_{i}
and
R_{i}
respectively, are computable. For a computable language
L
, there is a computable Gödel numbering for all first-order formulas in
L
, using variables from a countable set
X
.

An algebraic structure in the language
L
(a
L
-
structure) with the underlying set
A
is denoted by
{\mathbb{A}}=\langle A;L\rangle
, or by
{\mathbb{A}}=\langle A;f,\ldots,R,\ldots,c,\ldots\rangle
, or by
{\mathbb{A}}=\langle A;f^{A},\ldots,R^{A},\ldots,c^{A},\ldots\rangle
. For a structure
{\mathbb{A}}
we denote by
L({\mathbb{A}})
the language
L
of
{\mathbb{A}}
.

We usually denote variables by small letters
x,y,z,a,b,u,v,\ldots
, while the same symbols with bars
\bar{x},\bar{y},\ldots
denote tuples of the corresponding distinct variables, say
\bar{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
, and furthermore,
\bar{\bar{x}}
is a tuple of tuples
\bar{\bar{x}}=(\bar{x}_{1},\ldots,\bar{x}_{m})
, where
|\bar{x}_{i}|=n
. Also
\forall\,\bar{x}
denotes
\forall\,x_{1}\ldots\forall\,x_{n}
and
\exists\,\bar{x}
denotes
\exists\,x_{1}\ldots\exists\,x_{n}
, provided
\bar{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
. For any map
h\colon X\to Y
and any tuple
\bar{a}=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})
from
X^{n}
(or similarly, tuple
\bar{x}
of variables) we denote by
h(\bar{a})
the tuple
(h(a_{1}),\ldots,h(a_{n}))
. For an equivalence relation
\sim
on
X^{n}
by
X^{n}/{\sim}
we denote the set of all equivalence classes of
X^{n}
(the quotient of
X^{n}
by
\sim
). If
\bar{\bar{a}}
is a tuple
(\bar{a}_{1},\ldots,\bar{a}_{m})
of tuples
\bar{a}_{i}\in X^{n}
, then
\bar{\bar{a}}/{\sim}
is the tuple of equivalence classes
(\bar{a}_{1}/{\sim},\ldots,\bar{a}_{m}/{\sim})
.

Given a structure
{\mathbb{A}}
by a complete type in free variables
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
relative to the theory
\mathrm{Th}({\mathbb{A}})
we understand a maximal set of formulas in variables
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
that is locally satisfiable in
{\mathbb{A}}
. We denote such types by
\mathrm{tp}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
. For any tuple of elements
\bar{a}=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})\in{\mathbb{A}}
by
\mathrm{tp}(\bar{a})
we denote the complete type of
\bar{a}
, i. e., the set of all formulas
\phi(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
in the language
L({\mathbb{A}})
such that
{\mathbb{A}}\models\phi(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})
. By
\mathrm{Orb}(\bar{a})
we denote the automorphic orbit of
\bar{a}
in
{\mathbb{A}}
. Clearly, for any tuples
\bar{a},\bar{b}\in A^{n}
if
\bar{b}\in\mathrm{Orb}(\bar{a})
then
\mathrm{tp}(\bar{a})=\mathrm{tp}(\bar{b})
.

2.2
Definable sets

Let
{\mathbb{B}}=\langle B;L({\mathbb{B}})\rangle
be an algebraic structure. A subset
X\subseteq B^{n}
is called
definable
in
{\mathbb{B}}
if there is a first-order formula
\psi(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},y_{1},\ldots,y_{k})
in
L({\mathbb{B}})
and a tuple
\bar{p}=(p_{1},\ldots,p_{k})
of elements from
B
such that

X=\{(b_{1},\ldots,b_{n})\in B^{n}\mid{\mathbb{B}}\models\psi(b_{1},\ldots,b_{n},p_{1},\ldots,p_{k})\}.

The set
X
is also called
P
-
definable
, where
\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{k}\}\subseteq P\subseteq B
, and denoted by
\psi({\mathbb{B}},\bar{p})
or
\psi({\mathbb{B}}_{P})
, or
\psi(B^{n},\bar{p})
. Here the algebraic structure
{\mathbb{B}}_{P}=\langle B;L({\mathbb{B}})\cup P\rangle
is obtained from
{\mathbb{B}}
by adding new constant symbols from
P
into the language. If
P=\emptyset
then
X
is called
0
-
definable
or
absolutely definable
. It is clear that a set
X\subseteq B^{n}
is definable in
{\mathbb{B}}
if and only if there exists a finite set of parameters
P\subseteq B
such that
X
is absolutely definable in
{\mathbb{B}}_{P}
.

Recall that a formula
\phi(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
in
L({\mathbb{B}})
is called
primitive positive
or
Diophantine
if

\phi(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=\exists\,y_{1}\ldots\exists\,y_{k}\;\psi(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},y_{1},\ldots,y_{k}),

where
\psi
is a conjunction of atomic formulas. Sets definable by Diophantine formulas are called
Diophantine
.

Definition 1
.

Let
X\subseteq B^{n}
,
Y\subseteq B^{s}
be sets and
\sim_{X}
,
\sim_{Y}
be equivalence relations on
X
,
Y
, respectively. A map
g\colon(X/{\sim_{X}})^{m}\to Y/{\sim_{Y}}
is termed definable in
{\mathbb{B}}
, if the preimage in
{\mathbb{B}}
of the graph of
g
is definable in
{\mathbb{B}}
, i. e., the set

\{(b^{1}_{1},\ldots,b^{1}_{n},\>\ldots,\>b^{m}_{1},\ldots,b^{m}_{n},\>c_{1},\ldots,c_{s})\in B^{nm+s}\mid\\
g((b^{1}_{1},\ldots,b^{1}_{n})/{\sim_{X}},\>\ldots,\>(b^{m}_{1},\ldots,b^{m}_{n})/{\sim_{X}})=(c_{1},\ldots,c_{s})/{\sim_{Y}},\\
(b^{1}_{1},\ldots,b^{1}_{n}),\ldots,(b^{m}_{1},\ldots,b^{m}_{n})\in X,\>(c_{1},\ldots,c_{s})\in Y\}

is definable in
{\mathbb{B}}
. Note that here
g
may also be a function of arity
0
(i. e., a constant or a point
\bar{y}/{\sim_{Y}}\in Y/{\sim_{Y}}
). Similarly, a relation
Q
of arity
m
on the quotient set
X/{\sim_{X}}
is definable in
{\mathbb{B}}
, if the preimage in
{\mathbb{B}}
of its graph, i. e., the full preimage of the set
Q
in
B^{nm}
, is definable in
{\mathbb{B}}
. Correspondingly,
g
and
Q
are
0
-
definable in
{\mathbb{B}}
, if their preimages are
0
-
definable in
{\mathbb{B}}
.

It is obvious that the preimage in
{\mathbb{B}}
of the graph of
g
coincides with the preimage in
{\mathbb{B}}
of the graph of the map
\bar{g}\colon X^{m}\to Y/{\sim_{Y}}
, where
\bar{g}(\bar{\bar{x}})=g(\bar{\bar{x}}/{\sim_{X}})
,
\bar{\bar{x}}\in X^{m}
.

Usually, when definable maps
g
or predicates
Q
occur in this paper, the relations
\sim_{X}
and
\sim_{Y}
are also definable, but we do not require this in the definitions.

By default, we consider the sets
{\mathds{N}}
,
{\mathds{Z}}
,
{\mathds{Q}}
,
{\mathds{R}}
and
{\mathbb{C}}
as algebraic structures in the language
\{+,\times,0,1\}
. Note that definable sets in arithmetic
{\mathds{N}}
, the so-called arithmetic sets, are well-studied. In particular, it is known that every computable and computably enumerable set in
{\mathds{N}}
is definable. The same holds for the ring of integers
{\mathds{Z}}
. Furthermore, every element of
{\mathds{N}}
(or
{\mathds{Z}}
, or
{\mathds{Q}}
) is absolutely definable in
{\mathds{N}}
(
{\mathds{Z}}
,
{\mathds{Q}}
), so every definable set in
{\mathds{N}}
(
{\mathds{Z}}
,
{\mathds{Q}}
) is absolutely definable.

Recall that an algebraic structure
{\mathbb{B}}
is called
rigid
if the group of automorphisms
\mathrm{Aut}({\mathbb{B}})
of
{\mathbb{B}}
is trivial. For example,
{\mathds{N}},{\mathds{Z}},{\mathds{Q}},{\mathds{R}}
are rigid, as well as,
{\mathbb{B}}_{B}
for any
{\mathbb{B}}=\langle B;L({\mathbb{B}})\rangle
.

2.3
Interpretability with parameters

Intuitively,
interpretability
is a particular way of describing by formulas an algebraic structure
{\mathbb{A}}
in an algebraic structure
{\mathbb{B}}
.

Definition 2
.

An algebraic structure
{\mathbb{A}}=\langle A;f,\ldots,R,\ldots,c,\ldots\rangle
is interpretable with parameters in an algebraic structure
{\mathbb{B}}=\langle B;L({\mathbb{B}})\rangle
if the following conditions hold:

1)

there is a finite subset
P\subseteq B
,

2)

for some integer
n
there is a subset
A^{\ast}\subseteq B^{n}

P
-
definable in
{\mathbb{B}}
,

3)

there is an equivalence relation
\sim
on
A^{\ast}

P
-
definable in
{\mathbb{B}}
,

4)

there are interpretations
f^{A},\ldots
,
R^{A},\ldots
,
c^{A},\ldots
of the symbols
f,\ldots,R,\ldots,c,\ldots
on the quotient set
A^{\ast}/{\sim}
, all
P
-
definable in
{\mathbb{B}}
(see Definition
1
),

5)

the structure
{\mathbb{A}}^{\ast}=\langle A^{\ast}/{\sim};f^{A},\ldots,R^{A},\ldots,c^{A},\ldots\rangle
is
L({\mathbb{A}})
-
isomorphic to
{\mathbb{A}}
.

In Definition
2
we allow
P=\emptyset
, in which case the interpretation of
{\mathbb{A}}
in
{\mathbb{B}}
is called
absolute
or
0
-
interpretation
, or
interpretation without parameters
. Thus, we may assume that absolute interpretations are a particular case of interpretations with parameters. In all other similar situations when defining a notion
C
that could be absolute, we may also refer to it as
0
-
C
, or simply as
C
without parameters. Note that we have already done it in Definition
1
. Furthermore, following Marker’s book
[
30
]
, we sometimes simply write “interpretation” instead of “interpretation with parameters”, so by default our interpretation is interpretation with parameters. We write
{\mathbb{A}}\rightsquigarrow{\mathbb{B}}
if
{\mathbb{A}}
is interpretable in
{\mathbb{B}}
(with or without parameters).

If the equivalence relation
\sim
in Definition
2
is the identity relation on
A^{\ast}
, we follow Hodges’ book
[
19
]
in saying that
{\mathbb{A}}
is
injectively
interpretable in
{\mathbb{B}}
(with or without parameters). Injective interpretability
{\mathbb{A}}\rightsquigarrow{\mathbb{B}}
is sometimes called
definability
or
definable interpretability
of
{\mathbb{A}}
in
{\mathbb{B}}
. Any interpretation
{\mathbb{A}}\rightsquigarrow{\mathbb{A}}
is called
self-interpretation
.

Example 1
.

If
N
is a normal
0
-
definable subgroup of a group
G
(in the language
\{\cdot,^{-1},e\}
), then the equivalence relation
x\sim y
on
G
given by
xN=yN
is
0
-
definable in
G
. It is easy to see that the preimages in
G
of the group operations
\cdot,^{-1},e
on
G/N
, are
0
-
definable in
G
. This shows that the quotient group
G/N
is
0
-
interpretable in
G
. Similarly, if
N
is a normal subgrpoup of
G
definable with parameters in
G
, then the quotient group
G/N
is interpretable with parameters in
G
.

An embedding
h\colon{\mathbb{A}}\to{\mathbb{A}}^{\prime}
of
L
-
structures gives an (absolute) injective interpretability of
{\mathbb{A}}
in
{\mathbb{A}}^{\prime}
if and only if the set
h(A)
is (absolutely) definable in
{\mathbb{A}}^{\prime}
. In particular, every isomorphism
h\colon{\mathbb{A}}\to{\mathbb{A}}^{\prime}
gives an absolute injective interpretability.

Example 2
.

The semiring of natural numbers
{\mathds{N}}
is injectively
0
-
interpretable in the ring of integers
{\mathds{Z}}
. Indeed, by Lagrange’s Four Squares Theorem, every nonnegative integer is a sum of four squares, so the formula

\exists\,x_{1}\>\exists\,x_{2}\>\exists\,x_{3}\>\exists\,x_{4}\;(y=x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}+x_{3}^{2}+x_{4}^{2})

defines
{\mathds{N}}
in
{\mathds{Z}}
. It is also easy to see that the ring
{\mathds{Z}}
is injectively
0
-
interpretable in the semiring
{\mathds{N}}
.

Example 3
.

The ring of integers
{\mathds{Z}}
is injectively
0
-
interpretable in the ring of rationals
{\mathds{Q}}
. Due to J. Robinson
[
47
]
, the formula

\varphi(x)=\forall\>y\,\forall\>z\;([\Phi(y,z,0)\>\wedge\>(\forall\>w\;(\Phi(y,z,w)\longrightarrow\Phi(y,z,w+1)))]\longrightarrow\Phi(y,z,x)),

where
\Phi(y,z,t)=\exists\>a\,\exists\>b\,\exists\>c\;(yzt^{2}+2=a^{2}+yb^{2}-zc^{2})
,
defines the set
{\mathds{Z}}
in
{\mathds{Q}}
.

Example 4
.

The following is a simple
0
-
interpretation of
{\mathds{Q}}
in
{\mathds{Z}}
. Here we associate a fraction
m/n
with a pair of numbers
(m,n)
,
n\neq 0
, and define the equivalence relation by the rule:
(m_{1},n_{1})\sim(m_{2},n_{2})
if and only if
m_{1}n_{2}=m_{2}n_{1}
. The ring operations on pairs
(m,n)
,
n\neq 0
, are defined by the standard formulas on fractions. We can also interpret
{\mathds{Q}}
in
{\mathds{Z}}
by irreducible fractions; it gives an absolute injective interpretability of
{\mathds{Q}}
in
{\mathds{Z}}
.

Example 5
.

The field of complex numbers
{\mathbb{C}}
(in the ring language) is absolutely injectively interpretable in the field of reals
{\mathds{R}}
, where a complex number
a+ib
is represented by a pair
(a,b)
. At the same time,
{\mathds{R}}
is not interpretable in
{\mathbb{C}}

[
30
, Corollary 1.3.6]
.

Example 6
.

The ring
{\mathds{Z}}
is injectively
0
-
interpretable in any ring of algebraic integers
{\mathcal{O}}
; the ring of algebraic integers
{\mathcal{O}}
is injectively
0
-
interpretable in its field of fractions; the ring
{\mathds{Z}}
is injectively
0
-
interpretable in any algebraic number field
K

[
47
]
; the ring
{\mathds{Z}}
is interpretable in any finitely generated infinite field
F
if
\mathrm{char}F\neq 2

[
12
,
48
]
; the ring
{\mathds{Z}}
is interpretable in any infinite finitely generated integral domain
[
3
]
.

Example 7
.

Any finitely generated ring of algebraic integers
{\mathcal{O}}
is injectively absolutely interpretable in the ring
{\mathds{Z}}
.

Example 8
.

G. A. Noskov in
[
40
]
proved that the ring
{\mathds{Z}}
is interpretable with parameters in any finitely generated non-virtually abelian soluble group
G
.

Example 9
.

Let
G
be a countable group with an arithmetic multiplication table. Then
G
is absolutely interpretable in
{\mathds{N}}
. Indeed, by our assumptions, the group
G
can be represented on the set
{\mathds{N}}
with operations of multiplication and inversion such that their graphs are definable in
{\mathds{N}}
by formulas of arithmetic; hence the result.

Example 10
.

The metabelian non-abelian Baumslag – Solitar group
\mathrm{BS}(1,k)
,
k>1
, is absolutely interpretable in
{\mathds{Z}}

[
8
,
27
]
. Indeed, the group
\mathrm{BS}(1,k)
is isomorphic to the semi-direct product
{\mathds{Z}}[1/k]\rtimes{\mathds{Z}}
of the abelian groups
{\mathds{Z}}[1/k]=\{zk^{i}\mid z,i\in{\mathds{Z}}\}
and an infinite cyclic
{\mathds{Z}}
; where the action of
{\mathds{Z}}
on
{\mathds{Z}}[1/k]
is defined by
(m,x)\to xk^{-m}
,
m\in{\mathds{Z}}
,
x\in{\mathds{Z}}[1/k]
. Every element from
{\mathds{Z}}[1/k]\rtimes{\mathds{Z}}
has a form
(zk^{i},m)
,
z,i,k\in{\mathds{Z}}
; and for any
z,i,m,z_{j},i_{j},m_{j}
,
j=1,2
, one has

a)

(z_{1}k^{i_{1}},m_{1})\cdot(z_{2}k^{i_{2}},m_{2})=(z_{1}k^{i_{1}}+z_{2}k^{i_{2}-m_{1}},m_{1}+m_{2})
,

b)

(zk^{i},m)^{-1}=(-zk^{i+m},-m)
,

c)

e=(0,0)
.

So an element
(zk^{i},m)
is interpretable by a triple
(z,i,m)\in{\mathds{Z}}
. Further,
(z_{1},i_{1},m_{1})\sim(z_{2},i_{2},m_{2})
if and only if
z_{1}k^{i_{1}}=z_{2}k^{i_{2}}
and
m_{1}=m_{2}
. Note that the equivalence relation
\sim
and the group operations in the interpretation are given by computable predicates, hence this interpretation can be given by Diophantine formulas
[
31
]
. It is easy to “refine” this interpretation into an injective one.

Example 11
.

The ring
{\mathds{Z}}
is injectively interpretable in the group
\mathrm{BS}(1,k)
,
k>1
, with parameter
b=(0,1)
on the set
\{b^{n},n\in{\mathds{Z}}\}=\mathrm{C}_{\mathrm{BS}(1,k)}(b)
. Here an integer
m
is interpretable by an element
b^{m}
and for any
m_{1},m_{2}\in{\mathds{Z}}
one has
b^{m_{1}+m_{2}}=b^{m_{1}}\cdot b^{m_{2}}
. Besides, there exists a formula
\otimes(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})
in
L_{\mathrm{group}}\cup\{b\}
, such that
\mathrm{BS}(1,k)\models\otimes(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})
if and only if
x_{1}=b^{m_{1}}
,
x_{2}=b^{m_{2}}
and
x_{3}=b^{m_{1}m_{2}}
for some
m_{1},m_{2}\in{\mathds{Z}}

[
8
, Lemma 5]
.

Example 12
.

For any associative unitary ring
R
the classical matrix groups
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(R)
,
\mathrm{SL}_{n}(R)
(in this case
R
is commutative),
\mathrm{T}_{n}(R)
, and
\mathrm{UT}_{n}(R)
are absolutely injectively interpretable in
R
(a matrix is interpretable by a tuple of its elements listed in a fixed order); while
R
is interpretable in these groups, for
n\geqslant 3
, with parameters (see
[
37
, Lemma 5.1]
).

Example 13
.

Let
R
be an arbitrary unitary ring (not necessarily associative). Consider the group of upper unitriangular
3\times 3
-
matrices
\mathrm{UT}_{3}(R)
over
R
(also called the Heisenberg group over
R
). As was mentioned in Example
12
, there exists the standard absolute injective interpretation
\Gamma\colon\mathrm{UT}_{3}(R)\rightsquigarrow R
, where a matrix
A=(a_{ij})\in\mathrm{UT}_{3}(R)
is represented by a triple
(a_{12},a_{23},a_{13})
from
R^{3}
, so that

i)

(a_{1},b_{1},c_{1})\cdot(a_{2},b_{2},c_{3})=(a_{1}+a_{2},\>b_{1}+b_{2},\>c_{1}+c_{2}+a_{1}b_{2})
,
a_{i},b_{i},c_{i}\in R
,
i=1,2
,

ii)

(a,b,c)^{-1}=(-a,\>-b,\>-c+ab)
,
a,b,c\in R
,

iii)

e=(0,0,0)
.

Now we describe another absolute injective interpretation
\Delta\colon\mathrm{UT}_{3}(R)\rightsquigarrow R
, which comes from nilpotent groups or groups with bounded generation. Recall that every element
g\in\mathrm{UT}_{3}(R)
can be decomposed uniquely as a product
g=t_{12}(\alpha)\cdot t_{23}(\beta)\cdot t_{13}(\gamma)
for some
\alpha,\beta,\gamma\in R
, where
t_{ij}(\alpha)=e+\alpha e_{ij}
are transvections,
\alpha\in R
. Furthermore, for every
\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\alpha_{i},\beta_{i},\gamma_{i}\in R
,
i=1,2
, one has the following equalities that define group operations on triples
(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
:

1)

(t_{12}(\alpha_{1})\cdot t_{23}(\beta_{1})\cdot t_{13}(\gamma_{1}))\cdot(t_{12}(\alpha_{2})\cdot t_{23}(\beta_{2})\cdot t_{13}(\gamma_{2}))=t_{12}(\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2})\cdot t_{23}(\beta_{1}+\beta_{2})\cdot t_{13}(\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2}-\alpha_{2}\beta_{1})
,

2)

(t_{12}(\alpha)\cdot t_{23}(\beta)\cdot t_{13}(\gamma))^{-1}=t_{12}(-\alpha)\cdot t_{23}(-\beta)\cdot t_{13}(-\gamma-\alpha\beta)
,

3)

e=t_{12}(0)\cdot t_{23}(0)\cdot t_{13}(0)
,

4)

[t_{12}(\alpha_{1})\cdot t_{23}(\beta_{1})\cdot t_{13}(\gamma_{1}),t_{12}(\alpha_{2})\cdot t_{23}(\beta_{2})\cdot t_{13}(\gamma_{2})]=t_{13}(\alpha_{1}\beta_{2}-\alpha_{2}\beta_{1})
.

This gives the interpretation
\Delta\colon\mathrm{UT}_{3}(R)\rightsquigarrow R
. Note that the last equality
4)
is not needed for this interpretation, but we will need it in the sequel.

The interpretations
\Gamma
and
\Delta
in Example
13
differ in their codes. Thus, we proceed to discuss the concept of code.

2.4
Interpretation codes

Here we discuss the main technical tool, the interpretation code, which formalizes the notion of interpretation.

The structure
{\mathbb{A}}^{\ast}
from Definition
2
is completely described by the tuple of parameters
\bar{p}
and the following set of formulas in the language
L({\mathbb{B}})
:

\Gamma=\{U_{\Gamma}(\bar{x},\bar{y}),E_{\Gamma}(\bar{x},\bar{x}^{\prime},\bar{y}),Q_{\Gamma}(\bar{x}_{1},\ldots,\bar{x}_{t_{Q}},\bar{y})\mid Q\in L({\mathbb{A}})\},

where
\bar{x}
,
\bar{x}^{\prime}
and
\bar{x}_{i}
are
n
-
tuples of variables and
\bar{y}=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{k})
is a tuple of extra variables for parameters
\bar{p}
. Namely,
U_{\Gamma}
defines in
{\mathbb{B}}
a set
A_{\Gamma}=U_{\Gamma}(B^{n},\bar{p})\subseteq B^{n}
(the set
A^{\ast}
in Definition
2
),
E_{\Gamma}
defines an equivalence relation
\sim_{\Gamma}
on
A_{\Gamma}
(the equivalence relation
\sim
in Definition
2
), sometimes we denote it by
\sim_{\Gamma,\bar{p}}
, and the formulas
Q_{\Gamma}
define preimages in
{\mathbb{B}}
of graphs of constants, functions and predicates
Q\in L({\mathbb{A}})
on the quotient set
A_{\Gamma}/{\sim_{\Gamma}}
in such a way that the structure
\Gamma({\mathbb{B}},\bar{p})=\langle A_{\Gamma}/{\sim_{\Gamma}};L({\mathbb{A}})\rangle
is isomorphic to
{\mathbb{A}}
. Here
t_{c}=1
for a constant
c\in L({\mathbb{A}})
,
t_{f}=n_{f}+1
for a function
f\in L({\mathbb{A}})
and
t_{R}=n_{R}
for a predicate
R\in L({\mathbb{A}})
. Note that we interpret a constant
c\in L({\mathbb{A}})
in the structure
\Gamma({\mathbb{B}},\bar{p})
by the
\sim_{\Gamma}
-
equivalence class of some tuple
\bar{b}_{c}\in A_{\Gamma}
defined in
{\mathbb{B}}
by the formula
c_{\Gamma}(\bar{x},\bar{p})
. Sometimes we write formulas from
\Gamma
in a generalized form
\psi(\bar{\bar{x}},\bar{y})
, where
\bar{\bar{x}}
either equals
\bar{x}
or
(\bar{x},\bar{x}^{\prime})
, or
(\bar{x}_{1},\ldots,\bar{x}_{t_{Q}})
, which is always clear from the context.

We term
\Gamma
the
interpretation code from
L({\mathbb{A}})
to
L({\mathbb{B}})
(and sometimes write as
\Gamma\colon L({\mathbb{A}})\to L({\mathbb{B}})
). The notation
U_{\Gamma}
,
E_{\Gamma}
, and
Q_{\Gamma}
is consistently used throughout the entire paper. The number
n
is the
dimension
of
\Gamma
, while
k
is the
parameter dimension
of
\Gamma
, we denote them by
n=\dim\Gamma
and
k=\dim_{\mathrm{par}}\Gamma
, respectively.

If
\Gamma
interprets
{\mathbb{A}}
in
{\mathbb{B}}
then we refer to
\Gamma
as the
code
of this interpretation of
{\mathbb{A}}
in
{\mathbb{B}}
, and the interpretation itself is completely defined by a pair
(\Gamma,\bar{p})
(assuming the structure
{\mathbb{B}}
is clear from context). Note that there may be several different interpretations of
{\mathbb{A}}
in
{\mathbb{B}}
defined by different codes and different parameters. As we explained above, the writing
{\mathbb{A}}\rightsquigarrow{\mathbb{B}}
means that
{\mathbb{A}}
is interpretable in
{\mathbb{B}}
by some code
\Gamma
and some tuple of parameters
\bar{p}
. If we want to specify the code
\Gamma
we may write

{\mathbb{A}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Gamma}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}{\mathbb{B}}
or
\Gamma\colon{\mathbb{A}}\rightsquigarrow{\mathbb{B}}
, or
{\mathbb{A}}\simeq\Gamma({\mathbb{B}})
; in the case when we want to specify the code
\Gamma
and the parameters
\bar{p}
we write
{\mathbb{A}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Gamma,\bar{p}}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}{\mathbb{B}}
or
(\Gamma,\bar{p})\colon{\mathbb{A}}\rightsquigarrow{\mathbb{B}}
, or
{\mathbb{A}}\simeq\Gamma({\mathbb{B}},\bar{p})
. The code
\Gamma
(and an interpretation
{\mathbb{A}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Gamma}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}{\mathbb{B}}
) is
absolute
if
\bar{y}=\emptyset
; in this case, we may write
{\mathbb{A}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Gamma,\emptyset}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}{\mathbb{B}}
,
(\Gamma,\emptyset)\colon{\mathbb{A}}\rightsquigarrow{\mathbb{B}}
and
{\mathbb{A}}\simeq\Gamma({\mathbb{B}},\emptyset)
.

A surjective map
\mu_{\Gamma}=\mu_{\Gamma,\bar{p}}\colon A_{\Gamma}\to A
that induces an isomorphism
\bar{\mu}_{\Gamma}=\bar{\mu}_{\Gamma,\bar{p}}\colon\Gamma({\mathbb{B}},\bar{p})\to{\mathbb{A}}
is termed the
coordinate map
of the interpretation
(\Gamma,\bar{p})\colon{\mathbb{A}}\rightsquigarrow{\mathbb{B}}
. Note that such
\mu_{\Gamma}
may not be unique; it is uniquely defined up to an automorphism of
{\mathbb{A}}
; so if
{\mathbb{A}}
is rigid then
\mu_{\Gamma}
is uniquely defined. If we want to specify a particular coordinate map
\mu_{\Gamma}
for an interpretation
(\Gamma,\bar{p})
we write
(\Gamma,\bar{p},\mu_{\Gamma})
. Sometimes, we refer to such a triple
(\Gamma,\bar{p},\mu_{\Gamma})
as
a coordinatization of the interpretation
{\mathbb{A}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Gamma,\bar{p}}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}{\mathbb{B}}
. Some authors define interpretations exclusively as triples
(\Gamma,\bar{p},\mu_{\Gamma})
(see, for instance,
[
3
]
), so in this case each interpretation
{\mathbb{A}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Gamma,\bar{p}}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}{\mathbb{B}}
inherently comes equipped with a fixed coordinate map. We want to point out that in
[
3
]
, the direction of the arrow
\rightsquigarrow
is opposite to the one used in our notation. Note that if
{\mathbb{A}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Gamma,\bar{p}}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}{\mathbb{B}}
is an injective interpretation, then the surjection
\mu_{\Gamma}
is also injective. The map
\mu_{\Gamma}\colon A_{\Gamma}\to A
gives rise to a map
\mu_{\Gamma}^{m}\colon A^{m}_{\Gamma}\to A^{m}
on the corresponding Cartesian powers, which we often denote again by
\mu_{\Gamma}
. And we write
U^{\wedge}_{\Gamma}(\bar{\bar{x}},\bar{y})
instead of
\bigwedge_{i=1}^{m}U_{\Gamma}(\bar{x}_{i},\bar{y})
, where
\bar{\bar{x}}=(\bar{x}_{1},\ldots,\bar{x}_{m})
,
|\bar{x}_{i}|=\dim\Gamma
. In these notations, for example, the entry
U^{\wedge}_{\Gamma}(\bar{x},\bar{u},\bar{v},\bar{y})
denotes
U_{\Gamma}(\bar{x},\bar{y})\wedge U_{\Gamma}(\bar{u},\bar{y})\wedge U_{\Gamma}(\bar{v},\bar{y})
.

It is important to note that there can be really different interpretations of
{\mathbb{A}}
in
{\mathbb{B}}
, as illustrated in Example
13
, while others are essentially the same, differing mainly in the way we write formulas. We will define the latter formally below.
Prior to that, note that for an
L({\mathbb{B}})
-
structure
\widetilde{\mathbb{B}}
and a tuple
\bar{q}\in\widetilde{\mathbb{B}}
, the algebraic structure
\Gamma(\widetilde{\mathbb{B}},\bar{q})
described above might not exist. The criteria for its existence are discussed in Subsection
2.6
.

Definition 3
.

Two codes
\Gamma\colon L({\mathbb{A}})\to L({\mathbb{B}})
and
\Gamma^{\prime}\colon L({\mathbb{A}})\to L({\mathbb{B}})
are called equivalent (correspondingly,
\phi
-
equivalent, for a formula
\phi(y_{1},\ldots,y_{k})
in
L({\mathbb{B}})
) if

(1)

\dim\Gamma=\dim\Gamma^{\prime}
,
\dim_{\mathrm{par}}\Gamma=\dim_{\mathrm{par}}\Gamma^{\prime}=k
,

(2)

for any
L({\mathbb{B}})
-
structure
\widetilde{\mathbb{B}}
and any tuple
\bar{q}\in\widetilde{\mathbb{B}}
(correspondingly, any tuple
\bar{q}\in\phi(\widetilde{\mathbb{B}})
) the pair
(\Gamma,\bar{q})
defines an
L({\mathbb{A}})
-
structure
\Gamma(\widetilde{\mathbb{B}},\bar{q})
in
\widetilde{\mathbb{B}}
if and only if
(\Gamma^{\prime},\bar{q})
defines an
L({\mathbb{A}})
-
structure
\Gamma^{\prime}(\widetilde{\mathbb{B}},\bar{q})
in
\widetilde{\mathbb{B}}
, moreover, in this case
\Gamma(\widetilde{\mathbb{B}},\bar{q})=\Gamma^{\prime}(\widetilde{\mathbb{B}},\bar{q})
.

We write
\Gamma\approx\Gamma^{\prime}
(
\Gamma\approx_{\phi}\Gamma^{\prime}
) if
\Gamma
and
\Gamma^{\prime}
are equivalent (
\phi
-
equivalent).

Remark 1
.

We can always assume that for any tuple
(\bar{b},\bar{b}\,^{\prime},\bar{q})\in E_{\Gamma}({\mathbb{B}})
(similarly,
(\bar{b}_{1},\ldots,\bar{b}_{t_{Q}},\bar{q})\in Q_{\Gamma}({\mathbb{B}})
) one has
\bar{b},\bar{b}\,^{\prime}\in U_{\Gamma}({\mathbb{B}},\bar{q})
(correspondingly,
\bar{b}_{1},\ldots,\bar{b}_{t_{Q}}\in U_{\Gamma}({\mathbb{B}},\bar{q})
). Otherwise, one can replace the formula
E_{\Gamma}
with a new formula
E_{\Gamma}^{\prime}(\bar{x},\bar{x}^{\prime},\bar{y})=E_{\Gamma}(\bar{x},\bar{x}^{\prime},\bar{y})\wedge U^{\wedge}_{\Gamma}(\bar{x},\bar{x}^{\prime},\bar{y})
. And the same we can do with all the formulas
Q_{\Gamma}
,
Q\in L({\mathbb{A}})
. In particular, it follows that we can also assume
U_{\Gamma}(\bar{x},\bar{y})=E_{\Gamma}(\bar{x},\bar{x},\bar{y})
. The new code
\Gamma^{\prime}
we get this way is equivalent to the old one
\Gamma
.

Remark 2
.

Sometimes it is necessary to adjust formulas from a code
\Gamma
, such that
\sim_{\Gamma}
become an equivalence relation on the whole set
B^{n}
and
U_{\Gamma}({\mathbb{B}},\bar{p})
is closed under
\sim_{\Gamma}
, i. e., if
\bar{b}_{1},\bar{b}_{2}\in B^{n}
such that
\bar{b}_{1}\sim_{\Gamma}\bar{b}_{2}
and
\bar{b}_{1}\in U_{\Gamma}({\mathbb{B}},\bar{p})
, then
\bar{b}_{2}\in U_{\Gamma}({\mathbb{B}},\bar{p})
. Indeed, it is enough to replace the formula
E_{\Gamma}
by

E^{\prime}_{\Gamma}(\bar{x}_{1},\bar{x}_{2},\bar{y})=(U^{\wedge}_{\Gamma}(\bar{x}_{1},\bar{x}_{2},\bar{y})\longrightarrow E_{\Gamma}(\bar{x}_{1},\bar{x}_{2},\bar{y}))\,\wedge\,(\neg(\bar{x}_{1}=\bar{x}_{2})\longrightarrow U^{\wedge}_{\Gamma}(\bar{x}_{1},\bar{x}_{2},\bar{y})).

In this way we will again obtain a code equivalent to the original one.

Definition 4
.

A code
\Gamma
is injective if the formula
E_{\Gamma}(\bar{x},\bar{x}^{\prime},\bar{y})
has the form
E_{\mathrm{inj}}(\bar{x},\bar{x}^{\prime},\bar{y})=\bigwedge_{i=1}^{n}(x_{i}=x_{i}^{\prime})
, where
\bar{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
,
\bar{x}^{\prime}=(x_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,x_{n}^{\prime})
.

The following remark shows that every injective interpretation can be replaced with an interpretation given by an injective code.

Remark 3
.

For a code
\Gamma
denote by
\Gamma_{\mathrm{inj}}
the code obtained from
\Gamma
by replacing the formula
E_{\Gamma}(\bar{x},\bar{x}^{\prime},\bar{y})

Definition 5

Let

Remark 4
.

In practical scenarios, when we design a code
\Gamma
, we often neglect to ensure that the formulas

represent full preimages of the graphs of

. For example, while creating an interpretation

on pairs

with

, we may define addition

using the formula:

+(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4},x_{5},x_{6})\>=\>(x_{5}=x_{1}\cdot x_{4}+x_{3}\cdot x_{2})\,\wedge\,(x_{6}=x_{2}\cdot x_{4}).

Thus, addition is defined only for certain representatives of equivalence classes. However, the definition can be refined by replacing the formulas
Q_{\Gamma}
with new ones:

Q^{\prime}_{\Gamma}(\bar{x}_{1},\ldots,\bar{x}_{t_{Q}},\bar{y})=\exists\bar{x}_{1}^{\prime}\>\ldots\exists\bar{x}_{t_{Q}}^{\prime}\;(Q_{\Gamma}(\bar{x}_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,\bar{x}_{t_{Q}}^{\prime},\bar{y})\wedge\bigwedge\limits_{i=1}^{t_{Q}}E_{\Gamma}(\bar{x}_{i},\bar{x}_{i}^{\prime},\bar{y})\,)

and obtaining the full preimages of the graphs. Besides, if here

and

are Diophantine, then

is Diophantine as well.

2.5
Regular interpretability

An absolute interpretation is a specific type of interpretation with parameters. While it is more convenient, it appears rarely. Conversely, parameters can limit the interpretation’s applicability in certain cases. This issue can be resolved by aligning the parameter choice with a specific formula-descriptor.

Definition 7
.

We say that
{\mathbb{A}}
is regularly interpretable in
{\mathbb{B}}
if there exists a code
\Gamma\colon L({\mathbb{A}})\to L({\mathbb{B}})
and an
L({\mathbb{B}})
-
formula
\phi(y_{1},\ldots,y_{k})
,
k=\dim_{\mathrm{par}}\Gamma
, (called a parameter descriptor, or just a descriptor of
\Gamma
), such that
\phi({\mathbb{B}})\neq\emptyset
and for each
\bar{p}=(p_{1},\ldots,p_{k})\in\phi({\mathbb{B}})
the pair
(\Gamma,\bar{p})
gives an interpretation
{\mathbb{A}}\simeq\Gamma({\mathbb{B}},\bar{p})
of
{\mathbb{A}}
in
{\mathbb{B}}
.

We denote by
(\Gamma,\phi)
the regular interpretation with the code
\Gamma
and the descriptor
\phi
. We write
{\mathbb{A}}\simeq\Gamma({\mathbb{B}},\phi)
or
{\mathbb{A}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Gamma,\phi}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}{\mathbb{B}}
, or
(\Gamma,\phi)\colon{\mathbb{A}}\rightsquigarrow{\mathbb{B}}
, if
(\Gamma,\phi)
regularly interprets
{\mathbb{A}}
in
{\mathbb{B}}
.

Remark 6
.

When dealing with a regular interpretation
{\mathbb{A}}\simeq\Gamma({\mathbb{B}},\phi)
it is sometimes convenient to redefine each formula
\psi(\bar{\bar{x}},\bar{y})
from the code
\Gamma
by
\psi^{\prime}(\bar{\bar{x}},\bar{y})=\phi(\bar{y})\wedge\psi(\bar{\bar{x}},\bar{y})
. The new code will be
\phi
-
equivalent to
\Gamma
.

If for all
\bar{p}\in\phi({\mathbb{B}})
the interpretation
(\Gamma,\bar{p})
is injective, then the regular interpretation
(\Gamma,\phi)
is called
injective
. Note that if
{\mathbb{A}}\simeq\Gamma({\mathbb{B}},\phi)
is a regular injective interpretation, then
{\mathbb{A}}\simeq\Gamma_{\mathrm{inj}}({\mathbb{B}},\phi)
is a regular (injective) interpretation of
{\mathbb{A}}
in
{\mathbb{B}}
.

We say that descriptor
\phi
of a regular interpretation
(\Gamma,\phi)\colon{\mathbb{A}}\rightsquigarrow{\mathbb{B}}
is
removable
, if
\Gamma({\mathbb{B}},\bar{p}_{1})=\Gamma({\mathbb{B}},\bar{p}_{2})
for any tuples
\bar{p}_{1},\bar{p}_{2}\in\phi({\mathbb{B}})
.
If
(\Gamma,\phi)\colon{\mathbb{A}}\rightsquigarrow{\mathbb{B}}
is a regular interpretation with a removable descriptor, then
(\Gamma^{\mathrm{abs}},\emptyset)\colon{\mathbb{A}}\rightsquigarrow{\mathbb{B}}
is an absolute interpretation, where the code
\Gamma^{\mathrm{abs}}
is obtained from the code
\Gamma
by replacing every formula
\psi(\bar{\bar{x}},\bar{y})
from
\Gamma
by the formula
\psi^{\mathrm{abs}}(\bar{\bar{x}})=\exists\,\bar{y}\>(\phi(\bar{y})\wedge\psi(\bar{\bar{x}},\bar{y}))
(here
|\bar{y}|=\dim_{\mathrm{par}}\Gamma
).

Thus we intend to view absolute interpretations as a particular case of regular ones. Informally, whatever is true for regular interpretations also holds for the absolute ones, without changing much of the arguments. More formally, any absolute code
\Gamma
can be converted into a pair
(\Gamma^{\mathrm{reg}},\mathrm{reg}(y))
, termed as the
simple parameter extension
of
\Gamma
, by introducing a fictitious variable
y
, for parameter, into all formulas in
\Gamma
, namely, formally denoting every formula
\psi(\bar{\bar{x}})
from
\Gamma
by
\psi^{\mathrm{reg}}(\bar{\bar{x}},y)=\psi(\bar{\bar{x}})
; and defining
\mathrm{reg}(y)=(y=y)
. If
{\mathbb{A}}\simeq\Gamma({\mathbb{B}},\emptyset)
, then
{\mathbb{A}}\simeq\Gamma^{\mathrm{reg}}({\mathbb{B}},\mathrm{reg})
is a regular interpretation with removable descriptor. Similarly,
(\Gamma,\emptyset)
is the
simple absolutization
of
(\Gamma^{\mathrm{reg}},\mathrm{reg})
.
The simple parameter extension
(\Gamma,\emptyset)\to(\Gamma^{\mathrm{reg}},\mathrm{reg})
and simple absolutization
(\Gamma^{\mathrm{reg}},\mathrm{reg})\to(\Gamma,\emptyset)
are particular cases of notions of parameter extension and absolutization; the former is introduced in this subsection, and the latter will be addressed in one of the upcoming papers in this series.

Thus, if there exists an absolute interpretation
{\mathbb{A}}\rightsquigarrow{\mathbb{B}}
, then there exists a regular interpretation
{\mathbb{A}}\rightsquigarrow{\mathbb{B}}
, and if there exists a regular interpretation
{\mathbb{A}}\rightsquigarrow{\mathbb{B}}
, then there exists an interpretation
{\mathbb{A}}\rightsquigarrow{\mathbb{B}}
with parameters. Example
15
below shows two structures
{\mathbb{A}}
and
{\mathbb{B}}
such that
{\mathbb{A}}
is regularly interpretable in
{\mathbb{B}}
, but not absolutely; while Example
30
presents two structures
{\mathbb{A}}
and
{\mathbb{B}}
such that
{\mathbb{A}}
is interpretable in
{\mathbb{B}}
with parameters, but not regularly. The general intuition is that absolute interpretations are rarer or harder to establish than those with parameters, and regular interpretations offer a compromise between them. Regular interpretations allow for obtaining a number of consequences that cannot be derived from interpretations with parameters.

By the way, in Hodges’ book
[
19
]
the settings are slightly different: there, interpretations by default are absolute, and the regular ones are called
interpretations with definable parameters
. Here, the basic interpretations are with parameters. Of course, one can always put parameters in the language, as in Remark
5
, and obtain an absolute interpretation. Sometimes this simplifies things, but sometimes not. This makes it especially difficult to deal with regular interpretations
(\Gamma,\phi)
of
{\mathbb{A}}
in
{\mathbb{B}}
, for example, if the set
\phi({\mathbb{B}})
is infinite the language
L({\mathbb{B}})\cup\phi({\mathbb{B}})
becomes infinite, even though the initial language
L({\mathbb{B}})
is finite. Also, adding constants to the language makes it difficult to study elementary equivalence of structures, in particular, the first-order classification problems; or to study decidability problems: a decidable elementary theory
\mathrm{Th}({\mathbb{B}})
might become undecidable after adding some constants, etc.

In an upcoming paper in this series, we show that, under certain favorable conditions, an interpretation with parameters can be enhanced to a regular interpretation, and occasionally to an absolute interpretation. As an illustration, in Lemma
1
below we construct a regular interpretation
R\rightsquigarrow\mathrm{UT}_{3}(R)
from an interpretation with parameters. Furthermore, from this regular interpretation one can build up an absolute one. However, this is not always the case, as the following example shows two structures
{\mathbb{A}}
and
{\mathbb{B}}
such that
{\mathbb{A}}
is regularly interpretable in
{\mathbb{B}}
, but it can’t be absolutely interpretable in
{\mathbb{B}}
.

Example 15

(P. Gvozdevsky)
.

Let
L({\mathbb{A}})
be the empty language and
A={\mathds{Z}}
, while
L({\mathbb{B}})
be the language with a unique predicate
R
of arity
2
and
B
be an uncountable set, say
B={\mathds{R}}
, where
{\mathbb{B}}\models R(x,y)
if and only if
x-y\in{\mathds{Z}}
. Then
{\mathbb{A}}
is regularly interpretable in
{\mathbb{B}}
by means of
(\Gamma,\phi)
, where
\Gamma=\{U_{\Gamma}(x,y)=R(x,y),E_{\Gamma}(x_{1},x_{2},y)=(x_{1}=x_{2})\}
and
\phi(y)=(y=y)
. At the same time, for any
0
-
definable non-empty set
X
in
{\mathbb{B}}
and any
0
-
definable equivalence relation
\sim
on
X
the quotient
X/{\sim}
is finite or uncountable; therefore,
{\mathbb{A}}
is not absolutely interpretable in
{\mathbb{B}}
. Indeed, for any tuple
\bar{b}\in B^{n}
the automorphic orbit
\mathrm{Orb}(\bar{b})
is uncountable, while the set
\{\mathrm{Orb}(\bar{b})\mid\bar{b}\in B^{n}\}
of all orbits is finite for a fixed integer
n\geqslant 1
. Since
X
is
0
-
definable, then for any tuple
\bar{b}\in X
one has
\mathrm{Orb}(\bar{b})\subseteq X
. Suppose that there exist tuples
\bar{b},\bar{c}\in X
, such that
\mathrm{Orb}(\bar{b})=\mathrm{Orb}(\bar{c})=O
, but
\bar{b}\not\sim\bar{c}
(otherwise, the set
X/{\sim}
is finite, and there is nothing to prove). Moreover, we can assume that
{\mathbb{B}}\models\psi(\bar{b},\bar{c})
, where
\psi(x_{1},\ldots x_{n},y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})=\bigwedge\limits_{i,j=1}^{n}\neg R(x_{i},y_{j})
. Otherwise, if for all
\bar{d},\bar{e}\in O
with
{\mathbb{B}}\models\psi(\bar{d},\bar{e})
one has
\bar{d}\sim\bar{e}
, then it is easy to find
\bar{a}\in O
such that
{\mathbb{B}}\models\psi(\bar{a},\bar{b})\wedge\psi(\bar{a},\bar{c})
, so that
\bar{b}\sim\bar{a}\sim\bar{c}
, but it contradicts to
\bar{b}\not\sim\bar{c}
. And now we may find an uncountable subset

, such that
\bar{b},\bar{c}\in O_{1}
and
{\mathbb{B}}\models\psi(\bar{p},\bar{q})
for all
\bar{p},\bar{q}\in O_{1}
. Hence,
\mathrm{Orb}((\bar{b},\bar{c}))=\mathrm{Orb}((\bar{p},\bar{q}))
for all
\bar{p},\bar{q}\in O_{1}
, therefore,
\bar{p}\not\sim\bar{q}
, so the set
X/{\sim}
is uncountable.

We don’t know yet of any examples of this kind that are more natural, because it can be difficult to prove the absence of an absolute interpretation.

In this notation,

\alpha_{i}\tau_{i}=\xi_{i}\beta_{i}

\Gamma_{\mathrm{par}}
any code, which is obtained from the code
\Gamma
(even if it is not absolute) after adding a tuple
\bar{w}
of new fictitious variables (maybe empty) for parameters. We say that
\Gamma_{\mathrm{par}}
is a
parameter extension
of the code
\Gamma
by the tuple
\bar{w}
. In this case
\dim\Gamma_{\mathrm{par}}=\dim\Gamma
and
\dim_{\mathrm{par}}\Gamma_{\mathrm{par}}\geqslant\dim_{\mathrm{par}}\Gamma
and an interpretation
{\mathbb{A}}\simeq\Gamma({\mathbb{B}},\bar{p})
implies that
\Gamma({\mathbb{B}})=\Gamma_{\mathrm{par}}({\mathbb{B}},\bar{p},\bar{q})
for any
\bar{q}\in{\mathbb{B}}
and therefore
{\mathbb{A}}\simeq\Gamma_{\mathrm{par}}({\mathbb{B}},\bar{p},\bar{q})
.

Definition 8
.

A regular interpretation
(\Gamma_{\mathrm{par}},\phi\wedge\delta)\colon{\mathbb{A}}\rightsquigarrow{\mathbb{B}}
is called a parameter extension of a regular interpretation
{\mathbb{A}}\simeq\Gamma({\mathbb{B}},\phi(\bar{y}))
, if
\Gamma_{\mathrm{par}}
is a parameter extension of the code
\Gamma
by a tuple
\bar{w}
, and
\delta(\bar{y},\bar{w})
is an
L({\mathbb{B}})
-
formula. In the case, when all free variables of
\delta
are in
\bar{w}
, we name the parameters extension
(\Gamma_{\mathrm{par}},\phi\wedge\delta)
split. For an absolute interpretation
{\mathbb{A}}\simeq\Gamma({\mathbb{B}},\emptyset)
parameter extension is a regular interpretation
(\Gamma_{\mathrm{par}},\delta)
; it always splits.

It is clear, that for any regular interpretation
{\mathbb{A}}\simeq\Gamma({\mathbb{B}},\phi(\bar{y}))
and any parameter extension
\Gamma_{\mathrm{par}}
of the code
\Gamma
by a tuple
\bar{w}
, and any
L({\mathbb{B}})
-
formula
\delta(\bar{y},\bar{w})
the pair
(\Gamma_{\mathrm{par}},\phi\wedge\delta)
is a parameter extension of
{\mathbb{A}}\simeq\Gamma({\mathbb{B}},\phi(\bar{y}))
if and only if
\phi\wedge\delta({\mathbb{B}})\neq\emptyset
. The simple parameter extension above
(\Gamma^{\mathrm{reg}},\mathrm{reg})
is a particular case of parameter extension.

2.6
The admissibility conditions for interpretations

The book
[
19
]
presented admissibility conditions for (absolute) interpretations as sets of formulas and discussed their significance when approaching interpretations as functors. The specific formulas weren’t detailed as they weren’t crucial for understanding. However, to understand compositions of interpretations and bi-interpretations, it’s necessary to explore admissible conditions further, which we do in this section for arbitrary interpretations (with parameters or not).

Consider a code

\Gamma=\{U_{\Gamma}(\bar{x},\bar{y}),E_{\Gamma}(\bar{x}_{1},\bar{x}_{2},\bar{y}),Q_{\Gamma}(\bar{x}_{1},\ldots,\bar{x}_{t_{Q}},\bar{y})\mid Q\in L({\mathbb{A}})\}

for a language
L({\mathbb{A}})
into a language
L({\mathbb{B}})
with parameters
\bar{y}=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{k})
,
k=\dim_{\mathrm{par}}\Gamma
.

Intuitively, the
admissibility conditions

\mathcal{AC}_{\Gamma}(\bar{y})
for
\Gamma
is a set of formulas in the language
L({\mathbb{B}})
with free variables
\bar{y}
such that for an arbitrary
L({\mathbb{B}})
-
structure
\widetilde{\mathbb{B}}
and arbitrary tuple
\bar{q}
in
\widetilde{\mathbb{B}}
there exists an
L({\mathbb{A}})
-
structure
\Gamma(\widetilde{\mathbb{B}},\bar{q})
in
\widetilde{\mathbb{B}}
if and only if
\widetilde{\mathbb{B}}\models\mathcal{AC}_{\Gamma}(\bar{q})
.
Now we describe formulas in
\mathcal{AC}_{\Gamma}
and mention what each one of them “means”. Here for brevity we use notation
U^{\wedge}_{\Gamma}
from Subsection
2.4
.

(i)

The condition “
U_{\Gamma}(\widetilde{\mathbb{B}},\bar{q})\neq\emptyset
” is described by the formula

AC_{0}(\bar{y})\>=\>\exists\,\bar{x}\;U_{\Gamma}(\bar{x},\bar{y});

(ii)

The condition “
E_{\Gamma}(\bar{x}_{1},\bar{x}_{2},\bar{q})
defines an equivalence relation
\sim_{\Gamma}
on
U_{\Gamma}(\widetilde{\mathbb{B}},\bar{q})
” is described by formulas

\displaystyle AC_{1}(\bar{y})\>=\>\forall\,\bar{x}\>(U_{\Gamma}(\bar{x},\bar{y})\,\longrightarrow\,E_{\Gamma}(\bar{x},\bar{x},\bar{y})\,),

\displaystyle AC_{2}(\bar{y})\>=\>\forall\,\bar{x}_{1}\,\forall\,\bar{x}_{2}\>(U^{\wedge}_{\Gamma}(\bar{x}_{1},\bar{x}_{2},\bar{y})\wedge E_{\Gamma}(\bar{x}_{1},\bar{x}_{2},\bar{y})\,\longrightarrow\,E_{\Gamma}(\bar{x}_{2},\bar{x}_{1},\bar{y})\,),

\displaystyle AC_{3}(\bar{y})\>=\>\forall\,\bar{x}_{1}\,\forall\,\bar{x}_{2}\,\forall\,\bar{x}_{3}\>(U^{\wedge}_{\Gamma}(\bar{x}_{1},\bar{x}_{2},\bar{x}_{3},\bar{y})\,\wedge\,E_{\Gamma}(\bar{x}_{1},\bar{x}_{2},\bar{y})\wedge E_{\Gamma}(\bar{x}_{2},\bar{x}_{3},\bar{y})\,\longrightarrow\,E_{\Gamma}(\bar{x}_{1},\bar{x}_{3},\bar{y})\,);

(iii)

“The interpretation of a symbol
Q\in L({\mathbb{A}})
on
U_{\Gamma}(\widetilde{\mathbb{B}},\bar{q})/{\sim_{\Gamma}}
is well-defined” is described by

AC_{Q}(\bar{y})\>=\>\forall\,\bar{\bar{x}}^{1}\,\forall\,\bar{\bar{x}}^{2}\>(U^{\wedge}_{\Gamma}(\bar{\bar{x}}^{1},\bar{\bar{x}}^{2},\bar{y})\wedge\,Q_{\Gamma}(\bar{\bar{x}}^{1},\bar{y})\,\wedge\bigwedge\limits_{i=1}^{t_{Q}}E_{\Gamma}(\bar{x}_{i}^{1},\bar{x}_{i}^{2},\bar{y})\,\longrightarrow\,Q_{\Gamma}(\bar{\bar{x}}^{2},\bar{y})\,),

where
\bar{\bar{x}}^{j}=(\bar{x}_{1}^{j},\ldots,\bar{x}_{t_{Q}}^{j})
,
j=1,2
;

(iv)

“Every constant symbol
c\in L({\mathbb{A}})
interprets an element from
U_{\Gamma}(\widetilde{\mathbb{B}},\bar{q})/{\sim_{\Gamma}}
”:

\displaystyle AC_{c,1}(\bar{y})\>=\>\exists\,\bar{x}\;(U_{\Gamma}(\bar{x},\bar{y})\wedge c_{\Gamma}(\bar{x},\bar{y})\,),

\displaystyle AC_{c,2}(\bar{y})\>=\>\forall\,\bar{x}_{1}\forall\,\bar{x}_{2}\;(U^{\wedge}_{\Gamma}(\bar{x}_{1},\bar{x}_{2},\bar{y})\,\wedge\,c_{\Gamma}(\bar{x}_{1},\bar{y})\,\wedge c_{\Gamma}(\bar{x}_{2},\bar{y})\longrightarrow E_{\Gamma}(\bar{x}_{1},\bar{x}_{2},\bar{y})\,);

(v)

“Every functional symbol
f\in L({\mathbb{A}})
interprets a function on
U_{\Gamma}(\widetilde{\mathbb{B}},\bar{q})/{\sim_{\Gamma}}
”:

\displaystyle AC_{f,1}(\bar{y})\>=\>\forall\,\bar{\bar{x}}\;(U^{\wedge}_{\Gamma}(\bar{\bar{x}},\bar{y})\,\longrightarrow\,\exists\,\bar{u}\;(U_{\Gamma}(\bar{u},\bar{y})\wedge f_{\Gamma}(\bar{\bar{x}},\bar{u},\bar{y})\,)\,),

\displaystyle AC_{f,2}(\bar{y})\>=\>\forall\,\bar{\bar{x}}\>\forall\,\bar{u}\>\forall\,\bar{u}^{\prime}\;(U^{\wedge}_{\Gamma}(\bar{\bar{x}},\bar{u},\bar{u}^{\prime},\bar{y})\,\wedge\,f_{\Gamma}(\bar{\bar{x}},\bar{u},\bar{y})\,\wedge\,f_{\Gamma}(\bar{\bar{x}},\bar{u}^{\prime},\bar{y})\,\longrightarrow\,E_{\Gamma}(\bar{u},\bar{u}^{\prime},\bar{y})\,),

where
\bar{\bar{x}}=(\bar{x}_{1},\ldots,\bar{x}_{n_{f}})
.

Definition 9
.

For a code
\Gamma\colon L({\mathbb{A}})\to L({\mathbb{B}})
the set of
L({\mathbb{B}})
-
formulas
\mathcal{AC}_{\Gamma}=\{AC_{0},AC_{1},AC_{2},AC_{3}\}\cup\{AC_{c},AC_{c,1},AC_{c,2},AC_{f},AC_{f,1},AC_{f,2},AC_{R}\mid c,f,R\in L({\mathbb{A}})\}
is called the admissibility conditions of the code
\Gamma
.

To deal with regular interpretations, we introduce the following set of formulas.

Definition 10
.

Let
\Gamma\colon L({\mathbb{A}})\to L({\mathbb{B}})
be an interpretation code and
\phi(\bar{y})
be an
L({\mathbb{B}})
-
formula,
|\bar{y}|=\dim_{\mathrm{par}}\Gamma
. We define the admissibility conditions
\mathcal{AC}_{\Gamma,\phi}
as follows:

\mathcal{AC}_{\Gamma,\phi}=\{AC_{\phi}\mid AC\in\mathcal{AC}_{\Gamma}\}\cup\{\exists\,\bar{y}\>\phi(\bar{y})\},\ \mbox{where}\ AC_{\phi}=\forall\,\bar{y}\>(\phi(\bar{y})\,\longrightarrow\,AC(\bar{y})).

Thus, all formulas in
\mathcal{AC}_{\Gamma,\phi}
are sentences in
L({\mathbb{B}})
.

Definition 11
.

Let
\Gamma\colon L({\mathbb{A}})\to L({\mathbb{B}})
be an interpretation code and
k=\dim_{\mathrm{par}}\Gamma
. For an
L({\mathbb{B}})
-
structure
\widetilde{\mathbb{B}}
and a
k
-
tuple
\bar{q}
in
\widetilde{\mathbb{B}}
we say that
\Gamma(\widetilde{\mathbb{B}},\bar{q})
is well-defined, if
\Gamma(\widetilde{\mathbb{B}},\bar{q})
defined in Subsection
2.4
is, indeed, some
L({\mathbb{A}})
-
structure.

From the construction of
\mathcal{AC}_{\Gamma}
we get the following fact.

Proposition 1
.

Let
\Gamma\colon L({\mathbb{A}})\to L({\mathbb{B}})
be an interpretation code and
k=\dim_{\mathrm{par}}\Gamma
. Then for an
L({\mathbb{B}})
-
structure
\widetilde{\mathbb{B}}
and a
k
-
tuple
\bar{q}
in
\widetilde{\mathbb{B}}
the structure
\Gamma(\widetilde{\mathbb{B}},\bar{q})
is well-defined if and only if
\widetilde{\mathbb{B}}\models\mathcal{AC}_{\Gamma}(\bar{q})
, in which case
(\Gamma,\bar{q})
gives an interpretation
\Gamma(\widetilde{\mathbb{B}},\bar{q})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Gamma,\bar{q}}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}\widetilde{\mathbb{B}}
with a coordinate map
\mu_{\Gamma}\colon U_{\Gamma}(\widetilde{\mathbb{B}},\bar{q})\to U_{\Gamma}(\widetilde{\mathbb{B}},\bar{q})/{\sim_{\Gamma}}
, that maps a tuple
\bar{b}\in U_{\Gamma}(\widetilde{\mathbb{B}},\bar{q})
to the equivalence class
\bar{b}/{\sim_{\Gamma}}
. In particular, if
(\Gamma,\bar{p})
interprets
{\mathbb{A}}
in
{\mathbb{B}}
then
{\mathbb{B}}\models\mathcal{AC}_{\Gamma}(\bar{p})
.

Let
{\mathbb{A}}\simeq\Gamma({\mathbb{B}},\bar{p})
. If
{\mathbb{B}}\models\mathcal{AC}_{\Gamma}(\bar{q})
for some tuple
\bar{q}
in
{\mathbb{B}}
then
\Gamma({\mathbb{B}},\bar{q})
is well-defined, but it may not be isomorphic to
{\mathbb{A}}
.

Corollary 1
.

Let
{\mathbb{A}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Gamma,\phi}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}{\mathbb{B}}
be a regular interpretation. Then
{\mathbb{B}}\models\mathcal{AC}_{\Gamma,\phi}
and if
\widetilde{\mathbb{B}}\models\mathcal{AC}_{\Gamma,\phi}
for an
L({\mathbb{B}})
-
structure
\widetilde{\mathbb{B}}
, then
\phi(\widetilde{\mathbb{B}})\neq\emptyset
and for every tuple
\bar{q}\in\phi(\widetilde{\mathbb{B}})
the
L({\mathbb{A}})
-
structure
\Gamma(\widetilde{\mathbb{B}},\bar{q})
is well-defined.

Remark 7
.

If the language
L({\mathbb{A}})
is finite then the set
\mathcal{AC}_{\Gamma}
is also finite. In this case one can take a single formula
AC_{\Gamma}(\bar{y})=\bigwedge\{AC(\bar{y})\mid AC(\bar{y})\in\mathcal{AC}_{\Gamma}\}
, such that for any
L({\mathbb{B}})
-
structure
\widetilde{\mathbb{B}}
and
\bar{q}\in\widetilde{\mathbb{B}}
one has
\widetilde{\mathbb{B}}\models AC_{\Gamma}(\bar{q})
if and only if
\Gamma(\widetilde{\mathbb{B}},\bar{q})
is well-defined. In this case, the set of sentences
\mathcal{AC}_{\Gamma,\phi}
is also finite and it can be described by a single sentence
AC_{\Gamma,\phi}
— the conjunction of the sentences in
\mathcal{AC}_{\Gamma,\phi}
.

2.7
Translation for formulas

Let
\Gamma=\{U_{\Gamma}(\bar{x},\bar{y}),E_{\Gamma}(\bar{x}_{1},\bar{x}_{2},\bar{y}),Q_{\Gamma}(\bar{x}_{1},\ldots,\bar{x}_{t_{Q}},\bar{y})\mid Q\in L({\mathbb{A}})\}
be an interpretation code from
L({\mathbb{A}})
to
L({\mathbb{B}})
. In this subsection, we describe three types of translations, or reductions, of formulas from
L({\mathbb{A}})
to
L({\mathbb{B}})
(perhaps, with extra constants in the language):
\Gamma
-
translation

\psi\to\psi_{\Gamma}
,
(\Gamma,\phi)
-
translations

\psi\to\psi_{\Gamma,\forall\phi}
,
\psi\to\psi_{\Gamma,\exists\,\phi}
, and
(\Gamma,\bar{p},\mu_{\Gamma})
-
translation

\psi\to\psi_{\Gamma,\bar{p},\mu_{\Gamma}}
. In Section
2.8
we prove the Reduction Theorem and its corollaries that reveal the meaning and purpose of these translations.

We start with the translation
\psi\to\psi_{\Gamma}
.
Take a formula
\psi(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})
in
L({\mathbb{A}})
. We may assume that
\psi
does not have variables from
\bar{y}
and that it is written in the prenex normal form where the matrix is a disjunction of conjunctions of unnested atomic formulas and their negations; otherwise, we replace
\psi
with an equivalent formula
\psi^{\mathrm{pre}}
in the required form. Here by an unnested atomic formula, we understand a formula in one of the following forms
R(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n_{R}})
,
f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n_{f}})=x_{0}
,
x_{i}=x_{j}
, or
x_{i}=c
, where
R
is a predicate symbol from
L({\mathbb{A}})
,
f
is a functional symbol from
L({\mathbb{A}})
,
c
is a constant from
L({\mathbb{A}})
, and
x_{i}
-
s are variables (see
[
19
, Section 2.6]
). If the language
L({\mathbb{A}})
is computable, then there is an algorithm that, when given
\psi
, computes such a formula
\psi^{\mathrm{pre}}
and the length of
\psi^{\mathrm{pre}}
is bounded by a linear function of the length of
\psi
.

Following
[
19
]
we define first an intermediate map
\psi\to\psi^{\ast}
as follows. We start with replacing every variable
x_{i}
by an
n
-
tuple of distinct variables
\bar{x}_{i}
, where
n=\dim\Gamma
. Then we define the map
\psi\to\psi^{\ast}
on unnested atomic formulas as

\left.\begin{array}[]{rcl}x_{i}=x_{j}&\longrightarrow&E_{\Gamma}(\bar{x}_{i},\bar{x}_{j},\bar{y}),\\
x_{i}=c&\longrightarrow&c_{\Gamma}(\bar{x}_{i},\bar{y}),\\
f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n_{f}})=x_{0}&\longrightarrow&f_{\Gamma}(\bar{x}_{1},\ldots,\bar{x}_{n_{f}},\bar{x}_{0},\bar{y}),\\
R(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n_{R}})&\longrightarrow&R_{\Gamma}(\bar{x}_{1},\ldots,\bar{x}_{n_{R}},\bar{y}),\end{array}\right.

where
|\bar{y}|=\dim_{\mathrm{par}}\Gamma
and
U_{\Gamma},E_{\Gamma},c_{\Gamma},f_{\Gamma},R_{\Gamma}
are formulas from the code
\Gamma
. Furthermore, we put

\left.\begin{array}[]{rcl}(\psi_{1}\vee\psi_{2})^{\ast}&=&\psi^{\ast}_{1}\vee\psi^{\ast}_{2},\\
(\psi_{1}\wedge\psi_{2})^{\ast}&=&\psi^{\ast}_{1}\wedge\psi^{\ast}_{2},\\
(\neg\psi_{1})^{\ast}&=&\neg\psi^{\ast}_{1},\\
(\exists\,x\>\psi_{0})^{\ast}&=&\exists\,\bar{x}\>(U_{\Gamma}(\bar{x},\bar{y})\wedge\psi^{\ast}_{0}),\\
(\forall\,x\>\psi_{0})^{\ast}&=&\forall\,\bar{x}\>(U_{\Gamma}(\bar{x},\bar{y})\longrightarrow\psi^{\ast}_{0}).\end{array}\right.

And finally, we define the
\Gamma
-
translation
\psi(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})\to\psi_{\Gamma}(\bar{x}_{1},\ldots,\bar{x}_{m},\bar{y})
by

\psi_{\Gamma}(\bar{x}_{1},\ldots,\bar{x}_{m},\bar{y})=\psi^{\ast}(\bar{x}_{1},\ldots,\bar{x}_{m},\bar{y})\wedge U^{\wedge}_{\Gamma}(\bar{x}_{1},\ldots,\bar{x}_{m},\bar{y}).

Here we use notation
U^{\wedge}_{\Gamma}
from Subsection
2.4
for brevity.

In the book
[
19
]
, the map
\psi\to\psi_{\Gamma}
is defined differently with
\psi^{*}
denoted as
\psi_{\Gamma}
. Here, we’ve slightly modified the definition of
\psi_{\Gamma}
to maintain its utility, while facilitating our proofs.

Note that if
\Gamma
is absolute, i. e.,
\bar{y}=\emptyset
, then for every formula
\psi
in
L({\mathbb{A}})
the formula
\psi_{\Gamma}
has no variables corresponding to the parameters
\bar{y}
.

Remark 8
.

If the languages
L({\mathbb{A}}),L({\mathbb{B}})
and the interpretation
\Gamma
are computable, then the map
\psi\to\psi_{\Gamma}
is computable. And if the language
L({\mathbb{A}})
is finite, then for an
L({\mathbb{A}})
-
formula
\psi
the length of the
L({\mathbb{B}})
-
formula
\psi_{\Gamma}
is bounded by a linear function of the length of
\psi
.

Remark 9
.

If a formula
\psi
and a code
\Gamma
are Diophantine, then the formula
\psi_{\Gamma}
is Diophantine too.

Remark 10
.

If
\psi
is in (prenex)
\Sigma_{n}
-
form (
\Pi_{n}
-
form) and
\Gamma
is
\Sigma_{m}
-
code or
\Pi_{m}
-
code, then
\psi_{\Gamma}
is equivalent to a formula in (prenex)
\Sigma_{n+m+1}
-
form (
\Pi_{n+m+1}
-
form).

Remark 11
.

If
\Gamma={\mathbf{Id}}_{L({\mathbb{A}})}
then
\psi
is equivalent to
\psi_{\Gamma}
in first-order logic.

Remark 12
.

For an interpretation
\Gamma
the
\Gamma
-
translation
(\mathcal{AC}_{{\mathbf{Id}}_{L({\mathbb{A}})}})_{\Gamma}=\{\psi_{\Gamma}\mid\psi\in\mathcal{AC}_{{\mathbf{Id}}_{L({\mathbb{A}})}}\}
of formulas from the admissibility conditions
\mathcal{AC}_{{\mathbf{Id}}_{L({\mathbb{A}})}}
could play the role of the admissibility condition set
\mathcal{AC}_{\Gamma}(\bar{y})
. Namely,
(\mathcal{AC}_{{\mathbf{Id}}_{L({\mathbb{A}})}})_{\Gamma}
satisfies Proposition
1
, where
\mathcal{AC}_{\Gamma}(\bar{y})
is replaced by
(\mathcal{AC}_{{\mathbf{Id}}_{L({\mathbb{A}})}})_{\Gamma}
.

Definition 12
.

Let
\Gamma\colon L({\mathbb{A}})\to L({\mathbb{B}})
be an interpretation code and
L({\mathbb{B}})
-
formula
\phi(\bar{y})
, where
|\bar{y}|=\dim_{\mathrm{par}}\Gamma
. Then for a formula
\psi
in
L({\mathbb{A}})
we define
(\Gamma,\phi)
-
translations
\psi\to\psi_{\Gamma,\forall\phi}
and
\psi\to\psi_{\Gamma,\exists\,\phi}
as

\psi_{\Gamma,\forall\phi}=\forall\,\bar{y}\>(\phi(\bar{y})\longrightarrow\psi_{\Gamma}(\bar{y})\,),\quad\psi_{\Gamma,\exists\,\phi}=\exists\,\bar{y}\>(\phi(\bar{y})\wedge\psi_{\Gamma}(\bar{y})\,).

If
\Gamma
is an absolute code, then we assume that
\psi_{\Gamma,\forall\phi}
and
\psi_{\Gamma,\exists\,\phi}
are just
\psi_{\Gamma}
.

In particular, if
\psi
is a sentence, then
\psi_{\Gamma,\forall\phi}
,
\psi_{\Gamma,\exists\,\phi}
are sentences too.

Remark 13
.

If the languages
L({\mathbb{A}}),L({\mathbb{B}})
and the code
\Gamma
are computable, then the maps
\psi\to\psi_{\Gamma,\forall\phi}
,
\psi\to\psi_{\Gamma,\exists\,\phi}
are computable too.

Remark 14
.

If the code
\Gamma
and formulas
\phi,\psi
are Diophantine, the formula
\psi_{\Gamma,\exists\,\phi}
is Diophantine too.

Let us note that the
\Gamma
-
translation and the
(\Gamma,\phi)
-
translations of formulas depend solely on a code
\Gamma
and a formula
\phi
, and they have no connection to a specific interpretation
{\mathbb{A}}\rightsquigarrow{\mathbb{B}}
. The third translation,
\psi\to\psi_{\Gamma,\bar{p},\mu_{\Gamma}}
, which we will consider, translates formulas from
L({\mathbb{A}})\cup A
into formulas in
L({\mathbb{B}})\cup B
. This translation depends on the interpretation
(\Gamma,\bar{p},\mu_{\Gamma})
of the algebraic structure
{\mathbb{A}}=\langle A;L({\mathbb{A}})\rangle
into the algebraic structure
{\mathbb{B}}=\langle B;L({\mathbb{B}})\rangle
. As before, for every element
a\in A
we denote by
\bar{\mu}^{-1}_{\Gamma}(a)
some fixed tuple
\bar{b}
from
\mu_{\Gamma}^{-1}(a)
.

Definition 13
.

For any formula
\psi(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m},a_{1},\ldots,a_{s})
in
L({\mathbb{A}})\cup A
with free variables
x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}
and parameters
a_{1},\ldots,a_{s}\in A
we put

\psi_{\Gamma,\bar{p},\mu_{\Gamma}}(\bar{x}_{1},\ldots,\bar{x}_{m})=\psi_{\Gamma}(\bar{x}_{1},\ldots,\bar{x}_{m},\bar{\mu}_{\Gamma}^{-1}(a_{1}),\ldots,\bar{\mu}_{\Gamma}^{-1}(a_{s}),\bar{p}),

so,
\psi_{\Gamma,\bar{p},\mu_{\Gamma}}
is a formula in
L({\mathbb{B}})\cup B
.

Note that the formula
\psi_{\Gamma,\bar{p},\mu_{\Gamma}}
depends on the choice of fixed tuples
\bar{b}
from preimages
\mu_{\Gamma}^{-1}(a)
,
a\in A
, but, as we will see later, the set
\psi_{\Gamma,\bar{p},\mu_{\Gamma}}({\mathbb{B}}_{B})\subseteq B^{nm}
does not depend on this choice (see Lemma
3
), and this is enough for us to work with the
(\Gamma,\bar{p},\mu_{\Gamma})
-
translation.

If the code
\Gamma
is absolute, then for any formula
\psi
in
L({\mathbb{A}})
one has
\psi_{\Gamma,\emptyset,\mu_{\Gamma}}=\psi_{\Gamma}
.

2.8
Reduction Theorem

In Section
2.7
, translations of formulas via a code
\Gamma\colon L({\mathbb{A}})\to L({\mathbb{B}})
were defined. In this section, we prove the Reduction Theorem, which together with its implications reveals the power of these translations. This theorem is a fundamental result in interpretability, appearing in various forms in the literature, such as in
[
19
, Theorem 5.3.2]
.

We start with the following notations.

Notation 1
.

Assume that
\Gamma\colon L({\mathbb{A}})\to L({\mathbb{B}})
is a code,
\psi(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})
is a formula in
L({\mathbb{A}})
and
\psi^{+}(\bar{x}_{1},\ldots,\bar{x}_{m},\bar{y})
is a formula in
L({\mathbb{B}})
, where
|\bar{y}|=\dim_{\mathrm{par}}\Gamma
,
|\bar{x}_{i}|=\dim\Gamma
,
i=1,\ldots,m
. Then we write

\psi\Longleftrightarrow_{\Gamma}\psi^{+}

if and only if for any interpretation
(\Gamma,\bar{q},\mu_{\Gamma})
of an
L({\mathbb{A}})
-
structure
\widetilde{\mathbb{A}}
in an
L({\mathbb{B}})
-
structure
\widetilde{\mathbb{B}}
one has

\mu_{\Gamma}^{-1}(\psi(\widetilde{\mathbb{A}}))=\psi^{+}(\widetilde{\mathbb{B}},\bar{q}),

or, equivalently, if and only if for any
L({\mathbb{A}})
-
structure
\widetilde{\mathbb{A}}
, any
L({\mathbb{B}})
-
structure
\widetilde{\mathbb{B}}
and any tuple
\bar{q}\in\widetilde{\mathbb{B}}
with interpretation
\widetilde{\mathbb{A}}\simeq\Gamma(\widetilde{\mathbb{B}},\bar{q})

1)

if
{\mathbb{A}}\models\psi(\bar{a})
,
\bar{a}=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{m})\in\widetilde{\mathbb{A}}
, then for any coordinate map
\mu_{\Gamma}
of the interpretation
\widetilde{\mathbb{A}}\simeq\Gamma(\widetilde{\mathbb{B}},\bar{q})
one has
\widetilde{\mathbb{B}}\models\psi^{+}(\mu_{\Gamma}^{-1}(a_{1}),\ldots,\mu_{\Gamma}^{-1}(a_{m}),\bar{q})
, i. e.,
{\mathbb{B}}\models\psi^{+}(\bar{b}_{1},\ldots,\bar{b}_{m},\bar{q})
for any elements
\bar{b}_{i}\in\mu_{\Gamma}^{-1}(a_{i})
,

2)

and conversely, if
{\mathbb{B}}\models\psi^{+}(\bar{b}_{1},\ldots,\bar{b}_{m},\bar{q})
,
\bar{b}_{1},\ldots,\bar{b}_{m}\in\widetilde{\mathbb{B}}
, then
\bar{b}_{1},\ldots,\bar{b}_{m}\in U_{\Gamma}(\widetilde{\mathbb{B}},\bar{q})
and for any coordinate map
\mu_{\Gamma}
of the interpretation
\widetilde{\mathbb{A}}\simeq\Gamma(\widetilde{\mathbb{B}},\bar{q})
one has
\widetilde{\mathbb{A}}\models\psi(\mu_{\Gamma}(\bar{b}_{1}),\ldots,\mu_{\Gamma}(\bar{b}_{m}))
.

Theorem 1

(The Reduction Theorem)
.

For a code
\Gamma\colon L({\mathbb{A}})\to L({\mathbb{B}})
and a formula

3
Homotopic interpretations

In this and the next sections, we will focus on interactions between several interpretations. Homotopic interpretations form “parallel connections”, while invertible and bi-interpretations form “sequential connections” of interpretations.

3.1
Homotopy with parameters

Let

\displaystyle\Gamma_{1}=\{U_{\Gamma_{1}}(\bar{x}_{0},\bar{y}),E_{\Gamma_{1}}(\bar{x}_{1},\bar{x}_{2},\bar{y}),Q_{\Gamma_{1}}(\bar{x}_{1},\ldots,\bar{x}_{t_{Q}},\bar{y})\mid Q\in L({\mathbb{A}})\},

\displaystyle\Gamma_{2}=\{U_{\Gamma_{2}}(\bar{v}_{0},\bar{z}),E_{\Gamma_{2}}(\bar{v}_{1},\bar{v}_{2},\bar{z}),Q_{\Gamma_{2}}(\bar{v}_{1},\ldots,\bar{v}_{t_{Q}},\bar{z})\mid Q\in L({\mathbb{A}})\}

be codes, consisting of
L({\mathbb{B}})
-
formulas, where
|\bar{x}_{i}|=\dim\Gamma_{1}
,
|\bar{v}_{i}|=\dim\Gamma_{2}
,
|\bar{y}|=\dim_{\mathrm{par}}\Gamma_{1}
,
|\bar{z}|=\dim_{\mathrm{par}}\Gamma_{2}
.

Definition 14
.

Two interpretations
(\Gamma_{1},\bar{p}_{1})

-

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Gamma_{1}({\mathbb{B}},\bar{p}_{1})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathbb{A}}}

is finite, then the inverse statement also holds. However, if a homotopy

Lemma 6

Let

Straightforward.

3.2
Regular homotopy

A regular analog of homotopy is the following.

Definition 16
.

Two regular interpretations
(\Gamma_{1},\phi_{1})
and
(\Gamma_{2},\phi_{2})
of an algebraic structure
{\mathbb{A}}
into an algebraic structure
{\mathbb{B}}
are called regularly homotopic, if there is an
L({\mathbb{B}})
-
formula
\theta(\bar{x}_{1},\bar{x}_{2},\bar{y}_{1},\bar{y}_{2},\bar{w})
(a connector or a connecting formula) and an
L({\mathbb{B}})
-
formula
\delta(\bar{y}_{1},\bar{y}_{2},\bar{w})
(a descriptor),
|\bar{x}_{i}|=\dim\Gamma_{i}
,
|\bar{y}_{i}|=\dim_{\mathrm{par}}\Gamma_{i}
,
i=1,2
, such that for any tuples of parameters
\bar{p}_{1}\in\phi_{1}({\mathbb{B}})
,
\bar{p}_{2}\in\phi_{2}({\mathbb{B}})
there exists a tuple
\bar{r}\in\delta(\bar{p}_{1},\bar{p}_{2},{\mathbb{B}})
(i. e.,
{\mathbb{B}}\models\delta(\bar{p}_{1},\bar{p}_{2},\bar{r})
), moreover, for any such
\bar{r}
the formula
\theta(\bar{x}_{1},\bar{x}_{2},\bar{p}_{1},\bar{p}_{2},\bar{r})
defines an

L({\mathbb{A}})
-
isomorphism
\lambda_{\bar{p}_{1},\bar{p}_{2},\bar{r}}\colon\Gamma_{1}({\mathbb{B}},\bar{p}_{1})\to\Gamma_{2}({\mathbb{B}},\bar{p}_{2})
. In this case, we call the pair
(\theta,\delta)
regular homotopy between regular interpretations
(\Gamma_{1},\phi_{1})
and
(\Gamma_{2},\phi_{2})
and write
(\theta,\delta)\colon(\Gamma_{1},\phi_{1})\to(\Gamma_{2},\phi_{2})
, or
(\theta,\delta)\colon\Gamma_{1}({\mathbb{B}},\phi_{1})\to\Gamma_{2}({\mathbb{B}},\phi_{2})
.

When discussing a regular homotopy between regular interpretations, the prefix “regular” is often omitted when the context makes it clear.

There are several particular but important cases of Definition
16
, that we would like to discuss separately.

Definition 17

(particular cases of Definition
16
)
.

1)

Regular homotopy between absolute interpretations.
If one (or both) of
\Gamma_{1}
and
\Gamma_{2}
is absolute, then we define a regular homotopy between them by replacing each absolute interpretation

2)

Regular homotopy without a descriptor.
We say that

Remark 23
.

Let

Remark 24
.

Two regular interpretations

We refer to

of

2.5

Remark 25

Let

Definition 18

We name an algebraic structure

The symmetry of regular homotopy is easy. Suppose that regular interpretations
(\Gamma_{1},\phi_{1})

4
Invertibility and bi-interpretability

If a structure
{\mathbb{A}}
is interpreted in a structure
{\mathbb{B}}
then
{\mathbb{A}}
inherits some properties from
{\mathbb{B}}
. If they are interpreted in each other (
mutually interpreted
) then they have many properties in common, in particular their elementary theories are reducible to each other via the translation maps (see Section
5.1
), provided these interpretations are absolute or regular. Thus, mutual interpretability is a useful equivalence relation on structures.
However, we can’t say that mutually interpretable structures are “really the same” from model theory view-point. Fortunately, there is a natural relation between algebraic structures, called bi-interpretability, which is much stronger than the mutual interpretability and in a big picture can be viewed as really similar in the model-theoretic framework.

Invertible and bi-interpretations are constructed based on the successive application of interpretations, their compositions. By “invertible interpretation”, we refer to an interpretation
{\mathbb{A}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Gamma}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}{\mathbb{B}}
that has an inverse interpretation
{\mathbb{B}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Delta}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}{\mathbb{A}}
, such that the composition of interpretations
\Gamma\circ\Delta
is homotopic to the identity interpretation. More precisely, we obtain two concepts of invertibility (right and left) depending on which composition the additional conditions are imposed on:
\Gamma\circ\Delta
or
\Delta\circ\Gamma
.
If the conditions are fulfilled simultaneously for different inverses,
\Delta_{1}
and
\Delta_{2}
, this is known as two-sided invertibility. If they are fulfilled for the same inverse,
\Delta
, then it is called bi-interpretability. These definitions typically have three levels: absolute, regular, and involving parameters.

Bi-interpretability, particularly regular bi-interpretability, poses certain challenges. Invertible interpretations are simpler to handle and can sometimes suffice for the problem at hand. In a forthcoming paper in our series, we’ll show that a regular right-invertible interpretation of
{\mathbb{A}}
in
{\mathbb{B}}
suffices to algebraically characterize all structures
\widetilde{\mathbb{A}}
with
\widetilde{\mathbb{A}}\equiv{\mathbb{A}}
, based on the models of the theory
\mathrm{Th}({\mathbb{B}})
. However, regular bi-interpretability helps in offering a clearer understanding of the overall picture.

We begin with compositions of interpretations.

4.1
Compositions of interpretations

It is known that the relation
{\mathbb{A}}\rightsquigarrow{\mathbb{B}}
is transitive on algebraic structures (see, for example,
[
19
]
). The proof of this fact is based on the notion of
\Gamma
-

Definition 20

Let
\Gamma\colon L({\mathbb{A}})\to L({\mathbb{B}})

Remark 27

Example 19

Suppose that
A=B={\mathds{Q}}
,
L({\mathbb{A}})=\{<,0\}
and
L({\mathbb{B}})=\{<\}
. So that
{\mathbb{A}}
is the rational numbers with linear order and constant

, while
{\mathbb{B}}
is rational numbers with linear order only. We define codes of interpretations
{\mathbb{A}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Gamma}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}{\mathbb{B}}
and

{\mathbb{B}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Delta}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}{\mathbb{A}}

\Gamma=\{U_{\Gamma}(x,y)=(x=x),E_{\Gamma}(x_{1},x_{2},y)=(x_{1}=x_{2}),<_{\Gamma}(x_{1},x_{2},y)=(x_{1}<x_{2}),0_{\Gamma}(x,y)=(x=y)\}
and

\Delta=\{U_{\Delta}(x)=(x=x),E_{\Delta}(x_{1},x_{2})=(x_{1}=x_{2}),<_{\Delta}(x_{1},x_{2})=(x_{1}<x_{2})\}

\Gamma
has

as a parameter and
\Delta
is absolute. We fix two coordinate maps
\mu_{\Delta 1},\mu_{\Delta 2}
of the interpretation
{\mathbb{B}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Delta}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}{\mathbb{A}}

\mu_{\Delta 1}\colon x\to x
and
\mu_{\Delta 2}\colon x\to x-1
. Then the interpretations
\Gamma\circ\Delta({\mathbb{A}},0)
and
\Gamma\circ\Delta({\mathbb{A}},1)
are not homotopic, since there is no
L({\mathbb{A}})
-
definable order preserving bijection

and an
L({\mathbb{A}})
-
formula
\theta(x_{1},x_{2},c_{1},\ldots,c_{s})
that defines the graph of

and so on, that is, there exists an infinite set

. Since order on

\theta

x_{i}=c_{j}

x_{i}<c_{j}

30

The following lemma says, how the serial translation of formulas works if one has a series of interpretations
{\mathbb{A}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Gamma}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}{\mathbb{B}}
and

{\mathbb{B}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Delta}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}{\mathbb{C}}

Lemma 11
.

Let
(\Gamma,\bar{p},\mu_{\Gamma})
be an interpretation of
{\mathbb{A}}=\langle A;L({\mathbb{A}})\rangle
in
{\mathbb{B}}
, and
(\Delta,\bar{q},\mu_{\Delta})
be an interpretation of
{\mathbb{B}}
in
{\mathbb{C}}
. Then for any formula
\varphi(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})
in
L({\mathbb{A}})\cup A
one has

\varphi_{\Gamma\circ\Delta,\,(\bar{\bar{p}},\bar{q}),\,\mu_{\Gamma\circ\Delta}}({\mathbb{C}})=(\varphi_{\Gamma,\bar{p},\mu_{\Gamma}})_{\Delta,\bar{q},\mu_{\Delta}}({\mathbb{C}}),

where
\mu_{\Gamma\circ\Delta}=\mu_{\Gamma}\circ\mu_{\Delta}
,
\bar{\bar{p}}\in\mu_{\Delta}^{-1}(\bar{p})
.

Proof.

We use the Reduction Theorem
1
all the time. Take any elements
\bar{\bar{c}}_{1},\ldots,\bar{\bar{c}}_{m}
in
{\mathbb{C}}
. If
{\mathbb{C}}\models\varphi_{\Gamma\circ\Delta,(\bar{\bar{p}},\bar{q}),\mu_{\Gamma\circ\Delta}}(\bar{\bar{c}}_{1},\ldots,\bar{\bar{c}}_{m})
, then
\bar{\bar{c}}_{1},\ldots,\bar{\bar{c}}_{m}\in U_{\Gamma\circ\Delta}({\mathbb{C}},\bar{\bar{p}},\bar{q})
. Let
\bar{b}_{i}=\mu_{\Delta}(\bar{\bar{c}}_{i})
and
a_{i}=\mu_{\Gamma}(\bar{b}_{i})
, i. e.,
a_{i}=\mu_{\Gamma\circ\Delta}(\bar{\bar{c}}_{i})
,
i=1,\ldots,m
.
Since
{\mathbb{A}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Gamma\circ\Delta}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}{\mathbb{C}}
one has
{\mathbb{A}}\models\varphi(a_{1},\ldots,a_{m})
. Therefore,
{\mathbb{A}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Gamma}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}{\mathbb{B}}
gives
{\mathbb{B}}\models\varphi_{\Gamma,\bar{p},\mu_{\Gamma}}(\bar{b}_{1},\ldots,\bar{b}_{m})
, and
{\mathbb{B}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Delta}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}{\mathbb{C}}
gives
{\mathbb{C}}\models(\varphi_{\Gamma,\bar{p},\mu_{\Gamma}})_{\Delta,\bar{q},\mu_{\Delta}}(\bar{\bar{c}}_{1},\ldots,\bar{\bar{c}}_{m})

Conversely, if
{\mathbb{C}}\models(\varphi_{\Gamma,\bar{p},\mu_{\Gamma}})_{\Delta,\bar{q},\mu_{\Delta}}(\bar{\bar{c}}_{1},\ldots,\bar{\bar{c}}_{m})

\bar{\bar{c}}_{1},\ldots,\bar{\bar{c}}_{m}
are in
U_{\Delta}({\mathbb{C}},\bar{q})
and
{\mathbb{B}}\models\varphi_{\Gamma,\bar{p},\mu_{\Gamma}}(\bar{b}_{1},\ldots,\bar{b}_{m})
for
\bar{b}_{i}=\mu_{\Delta}(\bar{\bar{c}}_{i})
,

. Therefore
\bar{b}_{1},\ldots,\bar{b}_{m}\in U_{\Gamma}({\mathbb{B}},\bar{p})
and
{\mathbb{A}}\models\varphi(a_{1},\ldots,a_{m})
for
a_{i}=\mu_{\Gamma}(\bar{b}_{i})
,
i=1,\ldots,m
. And since
\bar{\bar{c}}_{i}\in\mu^{-1}_{\Gamma\circ\Delta}(a_{i})
,

, one has
{\mathbb{C}}\models\varphi_{\Gamma\circ\Delta,(\bar{\bar{p}},\bar{q}),\mu_{\Gamma\circ\Delta}}(\bar{\bar{c}}_{1},\ldots,\bar{\bar{c}}_{m})
.

4.2
Invertible interpretations with parameters

The following definition uses the notion of composition of interpretations and notations from Lemma
10
.

Definition 21
.

We say that an interpretation
(\Gamma,\bar{p},\mu_{\Gamma})
of
{\mathbb{A}}
in
{\mathbb{B}}
has a right inverse if there exists an interpretation
(\Delta,\bar{q},\mu_{\Delta})
of
{\mathbb{B}}
in
{\mathbb{A}}
such that the following conditions hold:

1)

the algebraic structure
\Gamma\circ\Delta({\mathbb{A}},\bar{\bar{p}},\bar{q})
is uniquely defined (see Remark
27
) and isomorphic to
{\mathbb{A}}
;

2)

the interpretation
(\Gamma\circ\Delta,\bar{\bar{p}},\bar{q})
is homotopic to the identical interpretation
{\mathbf{Id}}_{L({\mathbb{A}})}
, i. e., there exists a definable coordinate map
\mu_{\mathbb{A}}\colon U_{\Gamma\circ\Delta}({\mathbb{A}},\bar{\bar{p}},\bar{q})\to A
.

In this case we refer to
(\Delta,\bar{q},\mu_{\Delta})
as a right inverse for
(\Gamma,\bar{p},\mu_{\Gamma})
and to
(\Gamma,\bar{p},\mu_{\Gamma})
as a left inverse for
(\Delta,\bar{q},\mu_{\Delta})
.

Remark 29
.

Note that if a right inverse
(\Delta,\bar{q},\mu_{\Delta})
of
(\Gamma,\bar{p},\mu_{\Gamma})
exists, it is not necessarily unique. Furthermore, if an interpretation
(\Delta^{\ast},\bar{q}^{\ast},\mu_{\Delta^{\ast}})
of
{\mathbb{B}}
in
{\mathbb{A}}
is strongly homotopic to
(\Delta,\bar{q},\mu_{\Delta})
, then
(\Delta^{\ast},\bar{q}^{\ast},\mu_{\Delta^{\ast}})
is also a right inverse of
(\Gamma,\bar{p},\mu_{\Gamma})
. We will prove this in one of the subsequent papers of this series.

Example 20
.

For a finitely generated ring
{\mathcal{O}}
of algebraic integers, interpretations
\Gamma\colon{\mathds{Z}}\rightsquigarrow{\mathcal{O}}
and
\Delta\colon{\mathcal{O}}\rightsquigarrow{\mathds{Z}}
from Examples
6
and
7
are both right and left inverses to each other. Here
\Gamma
and
\Delta
are absolute, but the connector of the homotopy between
\Delta\circ\Gamma
and
{\mathbf{Id}}_{L_{\mathrm{ring}}}
has parameters.

Definition 22
.

An interpretation
(\Gamma,\bar{p},\mu_{\Gamma})
of
{\mathbb{A}}
in
{\mathbb{B}}
is called right-invertible (correspondingly, left-invertible; two-sided invertible), if it has a right inverse
(\Delta,\bar{q},\mu_{\Delta})
(correspondingly, a left inverse; a right inverse
(\Delta,\bar{q},\mu_{\Delta})
and a left inverse
(\Delta^{\ast},\bar{q}^{\ast},\mu_{\Delta^{\ast}})
).
If
\Gamma,\Delta
are absolute and
\mu_{\mathbb{A}}\colon U_{\Gamma\circ\Delta}({\mathbb{A}},\bar{\bar{p}},\bar{q})\to A
is
0

-
definable, then we term
\Gamma
absolutely right-invertible. We define similarly absolutely left-invertible andabsolutely two-sided invertible interpretations.

Usually, by
invertible interpretation
we mean a right-invertible one.

Remark 30
.

Suppose that
(\Gamma,\bar{p},\mu_{\Gamma})
is a right-invertible interpretation of
{\mathbb{A}}
into
{\mathbb{B}}
with a right inverse
(\Delta,\bar{q},\mu_{\Delta})
and definable coordinate map
\mu_{\mathbb{A}}\colon U_{\Gamma\circ\Delta}({\mathbb{A}},\bar{\bar{p}},\bar{q})\to A
. Recall that the location of
\Gamma\circ\Delta({\mathbb{A}},\bar{\bar{p}},\bar{q})
in
{\mathbb{A}}
depends on
\mu_{\Delta}
and does not depend on
\mu_{\Gamma}
(see Remark
27
). If we change
\mu_{\Delta}
, the coordinate map
\mu_{\mathbb{A}}
may no longer be definable in
{\mathbb{A}}
. At the same time, we may always replace
\mu_{\Gamma}
with
\mu_{\Gamma 0}=\bar{\mu}_{\mathbb{A}}\circ\bar{\mu}^{-1}_{\Gamma\circ\Delta}\circ\mu_{\Gamma}
, where
\mu_{\Gamma\circ\Delta}=\mu_{\Gamma}\circ\mu_{\Delta}
, and get that
\mu_{\Gamma 0}\circ\mu_{\Delta}=\mu_{\mathbb{A}}
\mu_{\Gamma}\circ\mu_{\Delta}
is definable in
{\mathbb{A}}
.

Definition 23
.

An interpretation
(\Gamma,\bar{p},\mu_{\Gamma})
of
{\mathbb{A}}
in
{\mathbb{B}}
is called strongly right-invertible, if it has a strong right inverse, i. e., a right inverse
(\Delta,\bar{q},\mu_{\Delta})
, such that the composition
\mu_{\Gamma}\circ\mu_{\Delta}
is definable in
{\mathbb{A}}
.

Remark 31
.

So, if
(\Gamma,\bar{p},\mu_{\Gamma})
is a right-invertible interpretation, then we always can redefine the coordinate map
\mu_{\Gamma}
by new one
\mu_{\Gamma 0}
and obtain a strongly right-invertible interpretation
(\Gamma,\bar{p},\mu_{\Gamma 0})
. And if
{\mathbb{A}}
is rigid, then an interpretation
(\Gamma,\bar{p},\mu_{\Gamma})
of
{\mathbb{A}}
in
{\mathbb{B}}
is right-invertible if and only if it is strongly right-invertible.

Further, one has the following continuation of the results of Lemma

Suppose there exists a strongly right-invertible interpretation

be a right inverse of
(\Gamma,\bar{p},\mu_{\Gamma})

\mu_{\Gamma}\circ\mu_{\Delta}
is definable in
{\mathbb{A}}
by a formula
\theta(\bar{\bar{x}},x,\bar{\bar{p}},\bar{q},\bar{r})
,
\bar{r}\in{\mathbb{A}}
. By Lemma

X
is definable in
{\mathbb{A}}

is definable in
{\mathbb{B}}

is definable in
{\mathbb{B}}
by a formula

in

\bar{b}\in{\mathbb{B}}
, then the set

is definable in
{\mathbb{A}}
by formula
\chi_{\Delta}(\bar{\bar{x}}_{1},\ldots,\bar{\bar{x}}_{m},\bar{\bar{b}},\bar{q})
,
\bar{\bar{b}}\in\mu_{\Delta}^{-1}(\bar{b})
. Therefore,

4.3
Regularly invertible interpretations

A regular analog of invertible interpretation is the following.

Definition 24
.

We say that a regular interpretation
(\Gamma,\phi)\colon{\mathbb{A}}\rightsquigarrow{\mathbb{B}}
has a regular right inverse if

(1)

there exists a regular interpretation
(\Delta,\psi)\colon{\mathbb{B}}\rightsquigarrow{\mathbb{A}}
(a regular right inverse for
(\Gamma,\phi)
), such that

(2)

the regular interpretations
(\Gamma\circ\Delta,\phi_{\Delta}\wedge\psi)
and
{\mathbf{Id}}_{L({\mathbb{A}})}
are regularly homotopic, i. e., there exist a connecting formula
\theta(\bar{u},x,\bar{t},\bar{w})
and a formula-descriptor
\delta(\bar{t},\bar{w})
in
L({\mathbb{A}})
, where
|\bar{u}|=\dim\Gamma\cdot\dim\Delta
,
|\bar{t}|=\dim_{\mathrm{par}}\Gamma\cdot\dim\Delta+\dim_{\mathrm{par}}\Delta
, such that for every tuples of parameters
\bar{a}\in\phi_{\Delta}\wedge\psi({\mathbb{A}})
and
\bar{r}\in\delta(\bar{a},{\mathbb{A}})
(there exists such
\bar{r}
for each
\bar{a}
) the formula
\theta(\bar{u},x,\bar{a},\bar{r})
defines an isomorphism
\Gamma\circ\Delta({\mathbb{A}},\bar{a})\simeq{\mathbb{A}}
.

In this case we refer to
(\Gamma,\phi)
as a regular left inverse for
(\Delta,\psi)
.

Definition 25
.

A regular interpretation
(\Gamma,\phi)\colon{\mathbb{A}}\rightsquigarrow{\mathbb{B}}
is called regularly right-invertible (correspondingly, regularly left -invertible; regularly two-sided invertible), if it has a regular right inverse (correspondingly, a regular left inverse; a regular right inverse and a regular left inverse).

Remark 33
.

As before, in the case of regularly right-invertible interpretation
(\Gamma,\phi)
with right inverse
(\Delta,\psi)
, a connector
\theta
and a descriptor
\delta
, for any tuples of parameters
\bar{a}=(\bar{\bar{p}},\bar{q})\in\phi_{\Delta}\wedge\psi({\mathbb{A}})
,
\bar{r}\in\delta(\bar{a},{\mathbb{A}})
and any coordinate map
\mu_{\Delta}\colon U_{\Delta}({\mathbb{A}},\bar{q})\to B
there exists a coordinate map
\mu_{\Gamma}\colon U_{\Gamma}({\mathbb{B}},\mu_{\Delta}(\bar{\bar{p}}))\to A
such that the composition
\mu_{\Gamma}\circ\mu_{\Delta}
is definable by the formula
\theta(\bar{u},x,\bar{a},\bar{r})
.

The following result is an analog of Lemma
13
for regularly invertible interpretations. Its premise, namely restrictions on sets
X
and
Y
, is not exotic (see Remark
15
).

Lemma 15
.

Suppose there exists a regularly right-invertible interpretation
(\Gamma,\phi)
of
{\mathbb{A}}=\langle A;L({\mathbb{A}})\rangle
into
{\mathbb{B}}=\langle B;L({\mathbb{B}})\rangle
,
n=\dim\Gamma
; and
X\subseteq A^{m}
,

are subsets, such that for any tuple
\bar{p}\in\phi({\mathbb{B}})
and any coordinate map
\mu_{\Gamma}
of interpretation
(\Gamma,\bar{p})\colon{\mathbb{A}}\rightsquigarrow{\mathbb{B}}
one has

Y=\mu_{\Gamma}^{-1}(X)
. Then
X
is

0
-
definable in
{\mathbb{A}}
if and only if
Y
is
0
-
definable in
{\mathbb{B}}
.

Proof.

Let
(\Delta,\psi)
be a regular right inverse for
(\Gamma,\phi)
and
(\theta,\delta)\colon(\Gamma\circ\Delta,\phi_{\Delta}\wedge\psi)\to{\mathbf{Id}}_{L({\mathbb{A}})}
be a regular homotopy. We use the Reduction Theorem
1
and Lemma
2
all the time.
If
X
is
0
-
definable in
{\mathbb{A}}
by a formula
\pi(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})
in

then
Y
is
0
-
definable in
{\mathbb{B}}
by the formula
\pi_{\Gamma,\exists\,\phi}(\bar{x}_{1},\ldots,\bar{x}_{m})
in
L({\mathbb{B}})
. Conversely, let
Y
be absolutely definable by a formula
\chi(\bar{x}_{1},\ldots,\bar{x}_{m})
in
L({\mathbb{B}})
. Then
X
is absolutely definable by the formula

\pi(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})=\exists\,\bar{\bar{y}}\>\exists\,\bar{z}\>\exists\,\bar{w}\>\exists\,\bar{\bar{x}}_{1}\>\ldots\exists\,\bar{\bar{x}}_{m}\>(\phi_{\Delta}(\bar{\bar{y}},\bar{z})\wedge\psi(\bar{z})\wedge\delta(\bar{\bar{y}},\bar{z},\bar{w})\wedge\\
\wedge\chi_{\Delta}(\bar{\bar{x}}_{1},\ldots,\bar{\bar{x}}_{m},\bar{z})\wedge\bigwedge\limits_{i=1}^{m}\theta(\bar{\bar{x}}_{i},x_{i},\bar{\bar{y}},\bar{z},\bar{w})\,).

Indeed, let
\bar{a}=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{m})\in X
. Take any tuples
\bar{p}\in\phi({\mathbb{B}})
and
\bar{q}\in\psi({\mathbb{A}})
. Fix a coordinate map
\mu_{\Delta}
of the interpretation
(\Delta,\bar{q})\colon{\mathbb{B}}\rightsquigarrow{\mathbb{A}}
and a tuple
\bar{\bar{p}}\in\mu_{\Delta}^{-1}(\bar{p})
. Then
\bar{\bar{p}}\in\phi_{\Delta}({\mathbb{A}},\bar{q})
. Let
\bar{r}\in{\mathbb{A}}
be a tuple, such that
{\mathbb{A}}\models\delta(\bar{\bar{p}},\bar{q},\bar{r})
, and
\mu_{\Gamma}
a coordinate map of the interpretation
(\Gamma,\bar{p})\colon{\mathbb{A}}\rightsquigarrow{\mathbb{B}}
, such that the composition
\mu_{\Gamma}\circ\mu_{\Delta}
is defined by the formula
\theta(\bar{\bar{x}},x,\bar{\bar{p}},\bar{q},\bar{r})
. Then any tuple
\bar{\bar{a}}=(\bar{a}_{1},\ldots,\bar{a}_{m})\in\mu_{\Gamma}^{-1}(\bar{a})
is in
Y=\chi({\mathbb{B}})
, therefore, any tuple
(\bar{\bar{a}}_{1},\ldots,\bar{\bar{a}}_{m})\in\mu_{\Delta}^{-1}(\bar{\bar{a}})
is in

. And since

, i. e.,

Conversely, if
\bar{a}\in\pi({\mathbb{A}})
, then there exist tuples
\bar{q}\in\psi({\mathbb{A}})
,
\bar{\bar{p}}\in\phi_{\Delta}({\mathbb{A}},\bar{q})
,
\bar{r}\in\delta({\mathbb{A}},\bar{\bar{p}},\bar{q})
,
(\bar{\bar{a}}_{1},\ldots,\bar{\bar{a}}_{m})\in\chi_{\Delta}({\mathbb{A}},\bar{q})
, such that
{\mathbb{A}}\models\theta(\bar{\bar{a}}_{i},a_{i},\bar{\bar{p}},\bar{q},\bar{r})
,

. Fix again a coordinate map
\mu_{\Delta}
of the interpretation

is in
\phi({\mathbb{B}})
and
\bar{\bar{a}}=(\bar{a}_{1},\ldots,\bar{a}_{m})=\mu_{\Delta}(\bar{\bar{a}}_{1},\ldots,\bar{\bar{a}}_{m})

. Let
\mu_{\Gamma}
be a coordinate map of the interpretation

\mu_{\Gamma}\circ\mu_{\Delta}
defines by the formula

\mu_{\Gamma}\circ\mu_{\Delta}(\bar{\bar{a}}_{1},\ldots,\bar{\bar{a}}_{m})=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{m})=\bar{a}

∎

4.4
Bi-interpretability with parameters

Let us fix two algebraic structures
{\mathbb{A}}=\langle A;L({\mathbb{A}})\rangle
and
{\mathbb{B}}=\langle B;L({\mathbb{B}})\rangle
.

As we have already mentioned, a
bi-interpretation
between
{\mathbb{A}}
and
{\mathbb{B}}
is a pair of mutual interpretations
{\mathbb{A}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Gamma}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}{\mathbb{B}}
and
{\mathbb{B}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Delta}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}{\mathbb{A}}
, such that
\Delta
is simultaneously a right and left inverse for
\Gamma
. But below we will give also a more strong version of this concept. As before, we will use the notion of composition of interpretations and notations from Lemma
10
. Let us remind again that when we have interpretations
{\mathbb{A}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Gamma,\bar{p}}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}{\mathbb{B}}
and
{\mathbb{B}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Delta,\bar{q}}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}{\mathbb{A}}
the location of
\Gamma\circ\Delta({\mathbb{A}},\bar{\bar{p}},\bar{q})
in
{\mathbb{A}}
depends on a coordinate map
\mu_{\Delta}
of interpretation
\Delta
and does not depend on a coordinate map
\mu_{\Gamma}
of interpretation
\Gamma
as well as the location of
\Delta\circ\Gamma({\mathbb{B}},\bar{\bar{q}},\bar{p})
in
{\mathbb{B}}
depends on
\mu_{\Gamma}
and does not depend on
\mu_{\Delta}
(see Remark
27
).

Definition 26
.

Algebraic structures
{\mathbb{A}}
and
{\mathbb{B}}
are called bi-interpretable (with parameters) in each other if the conditions
(1)
and
(2)
below hold:

(1)

there exists an interpretation
(\Gamma,\bar{p})
of
{\mathbb{A}}
into
{\mathbb{B}}
and an interpretation
(\Delta,\bar{q})
of
{\mathbb{B}}
into
{\mathbb{A}}
, so the algebraic structures
\Gamma\circ\Delta({\mathbb{A}},\bar{\bar{p}},\bar{q})
and
\Delta\circ\Gamma({\mathbb{B}},\bar{\bar{q}},\bar{p})
are well-defined and
\Gamma\circ\Delta({\mathbb{A}},\bar{\bar{p}},\bar{q})
is isomorphic to
{\mathbb{A}}
, while
\Delta\circ\Gamma({\mathbb{B}},\bar{\bar{q}},\bar{p})
is isomorphic to
{\mathbb{B}}
;

(2)

any of the following two equivalent conditions (see Lemma
16
below) holds:

(i)

(\Gamma\circ\Delta,\bar{\bar{p}},\bar{q})
is homotopic to
{\mathbf{Id}}_{L({\mathbb{A}})}
, and
(\Delta\circ\Gamma,\bar{\bar{q}},\bar{p})
is homotopic to
{\mathbf{Id}}_{L({\mathbb{B}})}
for some coordinate maps
\mu_{\Gamma}
and
\mu_{\Delta}
of interpretations
\Gamma
and
\Delta
correspondingly, i. e., there exist definable coordinate maps
\mu_{\mathbb{A}}\colon U_{\Gamma\circ\Delta}({\mathbb{A}},\bar{\bar{p}},\bar{q})\to A
and
\mu_{\mathbb{B}}\colon U_{\Delta\circ\Gamma}({\mathbb{B}},\bar{\bar{q}},\bar{p})\to B
;

(ii)

there exist coordinate maps
\mu_{\Gamma 0}
and
\mu_{\Delta}
of interpretations
\Gamma
and
\Delta
correspondingly such that the composition
\mu_{\Gamma 0}\circ\mu_{\Delta}\colon U_{\Gamma\circ\Delta}({\mathbb{A}},\bar{\bar{p}},\bar{q})\to A
is definable in
{\mathbb{A}}
and there exist coordinate maps
\mu_{\Gamma}
and
\mu_{\Delta 0}
of interpretations
\Gamma
and
\Delta
correspondingly such that the composition
\mu_{\Delta 0}\circ\mu_{\Gamma}\colon U_{\Delta\circ\Gamma}({\mathbb{B}},\bar{\bar{q}},\bar{p})\to B
is definable in
{\mathbb{B}}
.

We say that
{\mathbb{A}}
and
{\mathbb{B}}
are strongly bi-interpretable if they are bi-interpretable and the following strengthening of item
(2)(ii)
holds:

(iii)

there exist coordinate maps
\mu_{\Gamma}
and
\mu_{\Delta}
of interpretations
\Gamma
and
\Delta
correspondingly such that the composition
\mu_{\Gamma}\circ\mu_{\Delta}\colon U_{\Gamma\circ\Delta}({\mathbb{A}},\bar{\bar{p}},\bar{q})\to A
is definable in
{\mathbb{A}}
and the composition
\mu_{\Delta}\circ\mu_{\Gamma}\colon U_{\Delta\circ\Gamma}({\mathbb{B}},\bar{\bar{q}},\bar{p})\to B
is definable in
{\mathbb{B}}
.

Additionally, we say that

•

{\mathbb{A}}
is (strongly) half-absolutely bi-interpretable with
{\mathbb{B}}
if the interpretation
\Gamma
is absolute;

•

{\mathbb{A}}
and
{\mathbb{B}}
are quasi-absolutely (strongly) bi-interpretable, if both the interpretations
\Gamma
,
\Delta
are absolute;

•

{\mathbb{A}}
and
{\mathbb{B}}
are absolutely (strongly) bi-interpretable, if the interpretations
\Gamma
,
\Delta
are absolute and
\mu_{\mathbb{A}},\mu_{\mathbb{B}}
(
\mu_{\Gamma 0}\circ\mu_{\Delta}
,
\mu_{\Delta 0}\circ\mu_{\Gamma}
,
\mu_{\Gamma}\circ\mu_{\Delta}
,
\mu_{\Delta}\circ\mu_{\Gamma}
) are
0
-
definable;

•

{\mathbb{A}}
is (strongly) half-injectively bi-interpretable with
{\mathbb{B}}
if the interpretation
\Gamma
is injective;

•

{\mathbb{A}}
and
{\mathbb{B}}
are (strongly) injectively bi-interpretable, if both the interpretations
\Gamma
and
\Delta
are injective. (Strong) injective bi-interpretability is also called (strong) bi-definability.

Lemma 16
.

Suppose that
{\mathbb{A}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Gamma,\bar{p}}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}{\mathbb{B}}
and
{\mathbb{B}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Delta,\bar{q}}}{{\rightsquigarrow}}{\mathbb{A}}
are interpretations. Then the items
(2)(i)
and
(2)(ii)
in Definition
26
are equivalent.

Proof.

Indeed, if the item
(2)(i)
holds, then we use the argument from Remark
30
to construct
\mu_{\Gamma 0}
and
\mu_{\Delta 0}
as needed. And vice versa, if the item
(2)(ii)
holds, then for the pair of coordinate maps
\mu_{\Gamma},\mu_{\Delta}
the interpretations
(\Gamma\circ\Delta,\bar{\bar{p}},\bar{q})
and
{\mathbf{Id}}_{L({\mathbb{A}})}
, as well as
(\Delta\circ\Gamma,\bar{\bar{q}},\bar{p})
and
{\mathbf{Id}}_{L({\mathbb{B}})}
, are homotopic.
∎

We denote bi-interpretation by
(\Gamma;\Delta)
, or by
(\Gamma,\bar{p};\Delta,\bar{q})
, or by
(\Gamma,\bar{p},\mu_{\Gamma};\Delta,\bar{q},\mu_{\Delta})
, sometimes by
(\Gamma,\bar{p},\theta_{\mathbb{B}};\Delta,\bar{q},\theta_{\mathbb{A}})
or by
(\Gamma,\bar{p},\mu_{\Gamma},\theta_{\mathbb{B}};\Delta,\bar{q},\mu_{\Delta},\theta_{\mathbb{A}})
, where
\theta_{\mathbb{A}}
is a formula that defines
\mu_{\mathbb{A}}
and
\theta_{\mathbb{B}}
is a formula that defines
\mu_{\mathbb{B}}
. Here notion
(\Gamma;\Delta)
does not mean that interpretations
\Gamma,\Delta
are absolute, but simply in this case there is no need to focus on the parameters. If
\Gamma,\Delta
are both absolute, we write also
(\Gamma,\emptyset;\Delta,\emptyset)
.

The following results show that sometimes invertible interpretations are in fact bi-interpretations.

Lemma 17
.

Let
{\mathbb{B}}
be an i
-
rigid algebraic structure and
(\Gamma,\bar{p},\mu_{\Gamma})\colon{\mathbb{A}}\rightsquigarrow{\mathbb{B}}
be a right-invertible interpretation with a right inverse
(\Delta,\bar{q},\mu_{\Delta})
. Then
(\Gamma,\bar{p},\mu_{\Gamma};\Delta,\bar{q},\mu_{\Delta})
is a bi-interpretation between
{\mathbb{A}}
and
{\mathbb{B}}
.

Proof.

From the conditions of the lemma
(\Gamma\circ\Delta,\bar{\bar{p}},\bar{q})
is homotopic to
{\mathbf{Id}}_{L({\mathbb{A}})}
. And since
{\mathbb{B}}
is i
-
rigid,
(\Delta\circ\Gamma,\bar{\bar{q}},\bar{p})
is homotopic to
{\mathbf{Id}}_{L({\mathbb{B}})}
.
∎

Lemma 18
.

Let
{\mathbb{B}}
be a rigid and i
-
rigid algebraic structure and
(\Gamma,\bar{p},\mu_{\Gamma})\colon{\mathbb{A}}\rightsquigarrow{\mathbb{B}}
be a strongly right-invertible interpretation with strong right inverse

. Then
(\Gamma,\bar{p},\mu_{\Gamma};\Delta,\bar{q},\mu_{\Delta})

{\mathbb{A}}
and
{\mathbb{B}}
.

Proof.

Since
{\mathbb{B}}
is i
-
rigid, interpretations
\Delta\circ\Gamma({\mathbb{B}},\bar{\bar{q}},\bar{p})
and
{\mathbf{Id}}_{L({\mathbb{B}})}
are homotopic. And since,
{\mathbb{B}}
is rigid, there exits a unique coordinate map
\mu_{\Delta\circ\Gamma}\colon U_{\Delta\circ\Gamma}({\mathbb{B}},\bar{\bar{q}},\bar{p})\to B
, so it is
\mu_{\Delta}\circ\mu_{\Gamma}
and it is definable in
{\mathbb{B}}
, as required.
∎

.

Let
{\mathbb{B}}
be a rigid and i
-

(\Gamma,\bar{p})\colon{\mathbb{A}}\rightsquigarrow{\mathbb{B}}

(\Delta,\bar{q})

is a strong bi-interpretation between
{\mathbb{A}}
and

\mu_{\Gamma}
and
\mu_{\Delta}

Remark 34
.

Assume that
{\mathbb{A}}
is half-absolute bi-interpretable with

Lemma 19
.

Suppose that
{\mathbb{A}}
is a rigid algebraic structure and

We will sometimes refer to an ordinary bi-interpretation as a

for contrast. Observe that the notion of a bi-interpretation defined in the books

]
and

]

At the same time, all the consequences of the weak bi-interpretation, known to us, are in fact consequences of weaker conditions, namely, of right or two-sided invertible interpretations. However, it is important to mention that right now we do not have examples of weak bi-interpretations of algebraic structures that are not strong, so we leave this as an open problem.

{\mathbb{A}}

and

Algebraic structures
{\mathds{N}}
,

Example 23

In

4.6
Regular bi-interpretability

Now we introduce a regular analog of bi-interpretation.

Definition 27
.

Algebraic structures
{\mathbb{A}}
and
{\mathbb{B}}
are called regularly bi-interpretable, if

(I)

there exist a regular interpretation
(\Gamma,\phi)
of
{\mathbb{A}}
in
{\mathbb{B}}
and a regular interpretation
(\Delta,\psi)
of
{\mathbb{B}}
in
{\mathbb{A}}
, such that

(II)

(\Gamma\circ\Delta,\phi_{\Delta}\wedge\psi)
is regularly homotopic to
{\mathbf{Id}}_{L({\mathbb{A}})}
, and
(\Delta\circ\Gamma,\psi_{\Gamma}\wedge\phi)
is regularly homotopic to
{\mathbf{Id}}_{L({\mathbb{B}})}
, i. e.,

(a)

there exist a connecting formula
\theta_{\mathbb{A}}(\bar{u},x,\bar{\bar{y}},\bar{z},\bar{w})
and a formula-descriptor
\delta_{\mathbb{A}}(\bar{\bar{y}},\bar{z},\bar{w})
in
L({\mathbb{A}})
, where
|\bar{u}|={\dim\Gamma\cdot\dim\Delta}
,
|\bar{\bar{y}}|=\dim_{\mathrm{par}}\Gamma\cdot\dim\Delta
,
|\bar{z}|=\dim_{\mathrm{par}}\Delta
, such that
{\mathbb{A}}\models\forall\,\bar{\bar{y}}\>\forall\,\bar{z}\>(\phi_{\Delta}(\bar{\bar{y}},\bar{z})\wedge\psi(\bar{z})\longrightarrow\exists\,\bar{w}\>\delta_{\mathbb{A}}(\bar{\bar{y}},\bar{z},\bar{w}))
and for any tuples
\bar{q}\in\psi({\mathbb{A}})
,
\bar{\bar{p}}\in\phi_{\Delta}({\mathbb{A}},\bar{q})
,
\bar{r}\in\delta_{\mathbb{A}}(\bar{\bar{p}},\bar{q},{\mathbb{A}})
the formula
\theta_{\mathbb{A}}(\bar{u},x,\bar{\bar{p}},\bar{q},\bar{r})
defines some coordinate map
U_{\Gamma\circ\Delta}({\mathbb{A}},\bar{\bar{p}},\bar{q})\to A
;

(b)

there exist connecting formula
\theta_{\mathbb{B}}(\bar{u},x,\bar{\bar{z}},\bar{y},\bar{v})
and a formula-descriptor
\delta_{\mathbb{B}}(\bar{\bar{z}},\bar{y},\bar{v})
in
L({\mathbb{B}})
, where
|\bar{u}|={\dim\Gamma\cdot\dim\Delta}
,
|\bar{\bar{z}}|=\dim_{\mathrm{par}}\Delta\cdot\dim\Gamma
,
|\bar{y}|=\dim_{\mathrm{par}}\Gamma
, such that
{\mathbb{B}}\models\forall\,\bar{\bar{z}}\>\forall\,\bar{y}\>(\psi_{\Gamma}(\bar{\bar{z}},\bar{y})\wedge\phi(\bar{y})\longrightarrow\exists\,\bar{v}\>\delta_{\mathbb{B}}(\bar{\bar{z}},\bar{y},\bar{v}))
and for any tuples
\bar{p}\in\phi({\mathbb{B}})
,
\bar{\bar{q}}\in\psi_{\Gamma}({\mathbb{B}},\bar{p})
,
\bar{s}\in\delta_{\mathbb{B}}(\bar{\bar{q}},\bar{p},{\mathbb{B}})

the formula
\theta_{\mathbb{B}}(\bar{u},x,\bar{\bar{q}},\bar{p},\bar{s})
defines some coordinate map
U_{\Delta\circ\Gamma}({\mathbb{B}},\bar{\bar{q}},\bar{p})\to B
.

Regular bi-interpretation as above between
{\mathbb{A}}
and
{\mathbb{B}}
are denoted by
(\Gamma;\Delta)
, or by
(\Gamma,\phi;\Delta,\psi)
, or by
(\Gamma,\phi,\theta_{\mathbb{B}},\delta_{\mathbb{B}};\Delta,\psi,\theta_{\mathbb{A}},\delta_{\mathbb{A}})
.

As we’ve already mentioned, a regularly right-invertible interpretation of
{\mathbb{A}}
into
{\mathbb{B}}
allows us to describe models of elementary
\mathrm{Th}({\mathbb{A}})
by means of models of elementary theory of
\mathrm{Th}({\mathbb{B}})
. It turns out that such a reduction is especially informative if there is a regular bi-interpretation between
{\mathbb{A}}
and
{\mathbb{B}}
.

Definition 28
.

We say that
{\mathbb{A}}
and
{\mathbb{B}}
are strongly regularly bi-interpretable, if they are regularly bi-interpretable, i. e., items
(I)
–
(II)
from Definition
27
hold, and additionally

(III)

for any
tuples of parameters
\bar{p}\in\phi({\mathbb{B}})
,
\bar{q}\in\psi({\mathbb{A}})
,
\bar{r}\in{\mathbb{A}}
,
\bar{s}\in{\mathbb{B}}
there exists a pair of coordinate maps
(\mu_{\Gamma},\mu_{\Delta})
for interpretations
(\Gamma,\bar{p})
and
(\Delta,\bar{q})
, such that for any
\bar{\bar{p}}\in\mu^{-1}_{\Delta}(\bar{p})
and
\bar{\bar{q}}\in\mu^{-1}_{\Gamma}(\bar{q})
if
{\mathbb{A}}\models\delta_{\mathbb{A}}(\bar{\bar{p}},\bar{q},\bar{r})
and
{\mathbb{B}}\models\delta_{\mathbb{B}}(\bar{\bar{q}},\bar{p},\bar{s})
, then the coordinate maps
{\mu_{\Gamma}\circ\mu_{\Delta}\colon}U_{\Gamma\circ\Delta}({\mathbb{A}},\bar{\bar{p}},\bar{q})\to A
and
{\mu_{\Delta}\circ\mu_{\Gamma}\colon}U_{\Delta\circ\Gamma}({\mathbb{B}},\bar{\bar{q}},\bar{p})\to B
are defined in
{\mathbb{A}}
and
{\mathbb{B}}
correspondingly by the formulas
\theta_{\mathbb{A}}(\bar{u},x,\bar{\bar{p}},\bar{q},\bar{r})
and
\theta_{\mathbb{B}}(\bar{u},x,\bar{\bar{q}},\bar{p},\bar{s})
.

Remark 35
.

Note that item
(III)
in Definition
28
does not imply item
(II)
in Definition
27
even if both homotopies have no descriptors. The first statement in item
(II)
must be true for all tuples of parameters from the set
X=\phi_{\Delta}\wedge\psi({\mathbb{A}})
. From Lemma
10
and
Remark
28
we know that

X=\{(\bar{\bar{p}},\bar{q})\>\mid\>\bar{q}\in\psi({\mathbb{A}}),\>\bar{p}\in\phi({\mathbb{B}}),\>\bar{\bar{p}}\in\mu_{\Delta}^{-1}(\bar{p})\},

where
\mu_{\Delta}
runs over all coordinate maps of the interpretation
{\mathbb{B}}\simeq\Delta({\mathbb{A}},\bar{q})
. Denote by
\mu_{\Delta}^{\bar{p},\bar{q}}
the coordinate map
\mu_{\Delta}
of the interpretation
{\mathbb{B}}\simeq\Delta({\mathbb{A}},\bar{q})
from item
(III)

must be true for all tuples of parameters from the set

Y=\{(\bar{\bar{p}},\bar{q})\>\mid\>\bar{q}\in\psi({\mathbb{A}}),\>\bar{p}\in\phi({\mathbb{B}}),\>\bar{\bar{p}}\in(\mu_{\Delta}^{\bar{p},\bar{q}})^{-1}(\bar{p})\}.

So

, but it may be that

.

5
Applications of interpretations

The different interpretability options described above allow different conclusions to be drawn. Some of them are known, and others we are going to prove in subsequent papers in this series.

5.1
Undecidability of elementary theory

In this section we assume that all the theories
T
in a language
L
that we consider are deductively closed, i. e., for any
L
-
sentence
\psi
if
T\vdash\psi
then
\psi\in T
. Recall that a theory
T
in a language
L
is
decidable
if there is an algorithm that for a given sentence
\psi
of the language
L
decides whether or not
\psi\in T
; otherwise
T
is
undecidable
. And
T
is
hereditarily undecidable
if every subtheory
T_{0}\subseteq T
is undecidable.

A. Tarski proved that the elementary theory
\mathrm{Th}({\mathds{Z}})
of the ring of integers
{\mathds{Z}}
is hereditarily undecidable (see
[
49
]
).
Yu. Ershov et al. later demonstrated that every finitely axiomatizable undecidable theory is hereditary undecidable
[
14
, Corollary 3.4.1]
.

The main result of this section is that interpretations with parameters transfer the property of hereditary undecidability.
Originally, these results were central to studying interpretations of algebraic structures. Nowadays, there are several variations, and we mention here the one that is closest to our exposition.

Theorem 3

(
[
13
, Ershov, Theorem 2, p. 272]
)
.

Suppose that the language
L({\mathbb{A}})
is finite and the first-order theory
\mathrm{Th}({\mathbb{A}})
is hereditarily undecidable. If
{\mathbb{A}}
is interpretable (with parameters) in
{\mathbb{B}}
, then the first-order theory
\mathrm{Th}({\mathbb{B}})
is hereditarily undecidable too.

Corollary 13
.

In Section
2.3
, we have mentioned that the ring
{\mathds{Z}}
can be interpreted in several mathematical structures: a ring of algebraic integers
{\mathcal{O}}
, a number field
K
, any finitely generated infinite integral domain, any finitely generated infinite field
F
with
\mathrm{char}\,F\neq 2
, and a finitely generated non-virtually abelian solvable group
G
. As a result, each of these structures has a hereditarily undecidable elementary theory.

Sometimes, interpretations transfer undecidability itself, but for this, regular interpretability is required.

Theorem 4
.

Suppose that
{\mathbb{A}}
and
{\mathbb{B}}
are algebraic structures in computable languages and
{\mathbb{A}}
is regularly interpretable in
{\mathbb{B}}
by means of a computable code
\Gamma
. If the first-order theory
\mathrm{Th}({\mathbb{B}})
is decidable, then
\mathrm{Th}({\mathbb{A}})
is also decidable. Correspondingly, if
\mathrm{Th}({\mathbb{A}})
is undecidable, then
\mathrm{Th}({\mathbb{B}})
is undecidable as well.

Proof.

The result is directly deduced from Lemma
2
.
∎

Example 29
.

For any field
F
and any linear space
V
of dimension
\geqslant 3
over field
F
there exists a regular interpretation
F\rightsquigarrow\mathrm{P}(V)
of field
F
into projective space
\mathrm{P}(V)
over
V

[
5
]
. Therefore, if elementary theory
\mathrm{Th}(F)
is undecidable (for instance, if
F={\mathds{Q}}
), then elementary theory
\mathrm{Th}(\mathrm{P}(V))
is undecidable too.

It is time to consider an example of interpretability with parameters which excludes regularity.

Example 30
.

Suppose that
c
is a real number, whose decimal notation is not computable. The algebraic structure
\langle{\mathds{R}};+,\cdot,c\rangle
is interpretable in
\langle{\mathds{R}};+,\cdot\rangle
with parameter

, but not regularly interpretable in it, because the elementary theory of the second structure is decidable, but the first is not. It is obvious that algebraic structures
\langle{\mathds{R}};+,\cdot,c\rangle
and
\langle{\mathds{R}};+,\cdot\rangle
are also strongly bi-interpretable with parameters in each other, but not regularly bi-interpretable.

5.2
Diophantine problem and e
-
interpretations

Diophantine interpretations are one of the main tools to prove decidability/undecidability of the Diophantine problem in algebraic structures.

Recall that the
Diophantine problem
for a given algebraic structure
{\mathbb{A}}=\langle A;L({\mathbb{A}})\rangle
asks if there exists an algorithm that decides whether or not a given finite system of Diophantine equations, i. e., of atomic formulas in
L({\mathbb{A}})\cup A
, has a solution in
{\mathbb{A}}
; in other words, if there is an algorithm that for a given Diophantine sentence
\psi
of the language
L({\mathbb{A}})\cup A
decides whether or not
{\mathbb{A}}\models\psi
. Yu. Matiyasevich, following the work of M. Davis, H. Putnam and J. Robinson, proved that the ring
{\mathds{Z}}
has an undecidable Diophantine problem
[
31
]
, thus solving the Tenth Hilbert Problem.

Lemma 23
.

Suppose that
{\mathbb{A}}
and
{\mathbb{B}}
are algebraic structures in computable languages and
{\mathbb{A}}
is e
-
interpretable (with parameters) in
{\mathbb{B}}
by means of a computable Diophantine code
\Gamma
.
Then if the Diophantine problem in
{\mathbb{B}}
is decidable then the Diophantine problem in
{\mathbb{A}}
is also decidable. Correspondingly, if the Diophantine problem in
{\mathbb{A}}
is undecidable then the Diophantine problem in
{\mathbb{B}}
is undecidable as well. Moreover, if the languages of
{\mathbb{A}}
and
{\mathbb{B}}
are finite then there is a polynomial time reduction of the Diophantine problem in
{\mathbb{A}}
to the Diophantine problem in
{\mathbb{B}}
.

Proof.

It follows from the Reduction Theorem
1
and Remarks
8
,
9
.
∎

Using e
-
interpretation J. Denef proved that the ring of polynomials in one variable
K[x]
over an integral domain
K
has undecidable Diophantine problem
[
10
,
11
]
; O. Kharlampovich and A. Myasnikov proved that the Diophantine problem is undecidable over

(1)

free associative algebras
A
over a field
K
,

(2)

free Lie algebras
L
of rank
r\geqslant 3
a field
K
,

(3)

group algebra
K(G)
of a torsion-free hyperbolic or a toral relatively hyperbolic
group
G
over a field
K

[
20
,
21
]
.

The Diophantine problem in commutative rings (not necessarily unitary) was studied in detail in
[
15
]
, and for finite dimensional and similar algebras/rings in
[
16
]
. In
[
37
]
it was shown that the Diophantine problem in the classical matrix groups
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(R),\mathrm{SL}_{n}(R),\mathrm{T}_{n}(R),\mathrm{UT}_{n}(R)
,
n\geqslant 3
, over associative unitary rings
R
is polynomial time equivalent (more precisely, Karp equivalent) to the Diophantine problem in
R
. In the case of
\mathrm{SL}_{n}(R)
the ring
R
is assumed to be commutative. Similar results hold for
\mathrm{PGL}_{n}(R)
and
\mathrm{PGL}_{n}(R)
, provided

has no zero divisors (for
\mathrm{PGL}_{n}(R)
the ring
R
is not assumed to be commutative). These results were generalized for Chevalley groups in
[
7
]
. The Diophantine problem in solvable groups was intensively studied in algorithmic group theory
[
17
]
; we refer to the paper
[
37
]
for the latest results and a survey on the known ones.

It is important to note that all the aforementioned results were obtained through Diophantine interpretations of
{\mathds{Z}}
within the structures being examined.

5.3
Non-standard models and the first-order classification problem

Recall that the
first-order classification problem for an algebraic structure
{\mathbb{A}}
is to find an “algebraic description” of all models of elementary theory
\mathrm{Th}({\mathbb{A}})
, i. e., of all algebraic structures
\widetilde{\mathbb{A}}
in the language
L({\mathbb{A}})
first-order
equivalent to
{\mathbb{A}}
,
\widetilde{\mathbb{A}}\equiv{\mathbb{A}}
.

Theorem 5

(expected in the next paper)
.

Let an infinite algebraic structure
{\mathbb{A}}
is interpretable with parameters in an algebraic structure
{\mathbb{B}}
,
{\mathbb{A}}\simeq\Gamma({\mathbb{B}},\bar{p})
. Then every algebraic structure
\widetilde{\mathbb{A}}\equiv{\mathbb{A}}
is elementarily embeddable in an algebraic structure
\Gamma(\widetilde{\mathbb{B}},\bar{q})
for some
\widetilde{\mathbb{B}}\equiv{\mathbb{B}}
and
\bar{q}\in\widetilde{\mathbb{B}}
, such that
|\widetilde{\mathbb{A}}|\leqslant|\widetilde{\mathbb{B}}|\leqslant|\widetilde{\mathbb{A}}|+|L({\mathbb{B}})|+\aleph_{0}
.

If the interpretation
{\mathbb{A}}\rightsquigarrow{\mathbb{B}}
is regular, then the statement of Theorem
5
can be strengthened.

Theorem 6

(expected in the next paper)
.

Let
(\Gamma,\phi)\colon{\mathbb{A}}\rightsquigarrow{\mathbb{B}}
be a regular interpretation and
\widetilde{\mathbb{B}}
be an
L({\mathbb{B}})
-
structure such that
\widetilde{\mathbb{B}}\equiv{\mathbb{B}}
. Then

(1)

for every tuple
\bar{q}\in\phi(\widetilde{\mathbb{B}})
the algebraic structure
\Gamma(\widetilde{\mathbb{B}},\bar{q})
is well-defined and one has
\Gamma(\widetilde{\mathbb{B}},\bar{q})\equiv{\mathbb{A}}
;

(2)

moreover, if interpretation
(\Gamma,\phi)
is self-homotopic then for all
\bar{q}_{1},\bar{q}_{2}\in\phi(\widetilde{\mathbb{B}})
one has
\Gamma(\widetilde{\mathbb{B}},\bar{q}_{1})\simeq\Gamma(\widetilde{\mathbb{B}},\bar{q}_{2})
, i. e., there exists an
L({\mathbb{A}})
-
structure
\widetilde{\mathbb{A}}\equiv{\mathbb{A}}
, such that
\widetilde{\mathbb{A}}\simeq\Gamma(\widetilde{\mathbb{B}},\phi)
.

In the assumptions of Theorems
5
and
6
, algebraic structures of the kind
\Gamma(\widetilde{\mathbb{B}},\bar{q})
are called
non-standard models of
{\mathbb{A}}
(relative to
{\mathbb{B}}

5.4
Stability and saturation

Let
{\mathbb{A}}=\langle A;L({\mathbb{A}})\rangle
be an algebraic structure and
X\subseteq A
be a subset. The
Stone space

S^{{\mathbb{A}}}_{m}(X)
consists of all complete
m
-
types over
X

\mathrm{tp}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})
of formulas in
L({\mathbb{A}})\cup X

\mathrm{Th}_{X}({\mathbb{A}})
(in other words,
\mathrm{tp}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})
is finitely satisfiable in
{\mathbb{A}}_{X}
)
[
30
]
. Let
\kappa
be an infinite cardinal. Algebraic structure
{\mathbb{A}}
is called
\kappa
-
saturated
if for any
X\subseteq A
,
|X|<\kappa
, any integer
m
every complete type
\mathrm{tp}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})\in S_{m}^{{\mathbb{A}}}(X)
is realized in
{\mathbb{A}}
. An algebraic structure
{\mathbb{A}}
in a countable language
L({\mathbb{A}})
is called
\kappa
-
stable
if for any algebraic structure
\widetilde{\mathbb{A}}\equiv{\mathbb{A}}
,
\widetilde{\mathbb{A}}=\langle\widetilde{A};L({\mathbb{A}})\rangle
, and any subset
X\subseteq\widetilde{A}
if
|X|=\kappa
then
|S^{\widetilde{\mathbb{A}}}_{m}(X)|=\kappa
for all integers
m
. If
{\mathbb{A}}
is
\omega
-
stable, then it is
\kappa
-
stable for any infinite cardinal
\kappa

[
30
, Theorem 4.2.18]
.

Note that for any interpretation
(\Gamma,\bar{p},\mu_{\Gamma})\colon{\mathbb{A}}\rightsquigarrow{\mathbb{B}}
, a subset
X\subseteq A
and a type
\mathrm{tp}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})\in S_{m}^{{\mathbb{A}}}(X)
, by the Reduction Theorem
1
, there exists a type
\mathrm{tp}_{\Gamma}(\bar{x}_{1},\ldots,\bar{x}_{m})\in S_{m\cdot\dim\Gamma}^{{\mathbb{B}}}(\bar{\mu}^{-1}_{\Gamma}(X)\cup\bar{p})
, such that
\{\psi_{\Gamma,\bar{p},\mu_{\Gamma}}\mid\psi\in\mathrm{tp}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})\}\subseteq\mathrm{tp}_{\Gamma}(\bar{x}_{1},\ldots,\bar{x}_{m})
; and for every non-equal types
\mathrm{tp}_{1}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}),\mathrm{tp}_{2}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})\in S_{m}^{{\mathbb{A}}}(X)
any such
\mathrm{tp}_{1,\Gamma}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})
and
\mathrm{tp}_{2,\Gamma}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})
are also non-equal; in particularly,
|S_{m\cdot\dim\Gamma}^{{\mathbb{B}}}(\bar{\mu}^{-1}_{\Gamma}(X)\cup\bar{p})|\geqslant|S_{m}^{{\mathbb{A}}}(X)|
.

(
[

, Exercise 4.5.23]
)

Let

(with parameters). For any infinite cardinal

\kappa
-

is

Proof.

It is directly follows from the Reduction Theorem

.

(

Let

Suppose that

5.5

In 2012 B. Plotkin introduced the notion of isotypic algebraic structures
[
44
]
, that plays an important part in logical geometries and logical equivalence of algebraic structures.

Definition 29
.

Algebraic structures
{\mathbb{A}}
and
\widetilde{\mathbb{A}}
in a language

\mathrm{tp}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
in

and vice versa.

Note that, if

and

. Thus Theorem

gets the following upgrade at the level of isotypeness.

(expected in the next paper)
.

Let
{\mathbb{A}}
and
{\mathbb{B}}
be regularly bi-interpretable in each other, so
{\mathbb{A}}\simeq\Gamma({\mathbb{B}},\phi)
and

. Then if an
L({\mathbb{B}})
-
structure

is isotypic to
{\mathbb{B}}

\widetilde{\mathbb{A}}\simeq\Gamma(\widetilde{\mathbb{B}},\phi)
is isotypic to
{\mathbb{A}}
. And conversely, if an
L({\mathbb{A}})
-
structure
\widetilde{\mathbb{A}}
is isotypic to
{\mathbb{A}}
, then
\widetilde{\mathbb{B}}\simeq\Delta(\widetilde{\mathbb{A}},\psi)
is isotypic to
{\mathbb{B}}
.

Actually, the regular bi-interpretability of
{\mathbb{A}}
and
{\mathbb{B}}
is not necessary for transferring the property of isotypeness between

and
{\mathbb{B}}
. This can be achieved through a weaker property as described in the result below.

Theorem 12

(expected in the next paper)
.

Let
{\mathbb{A}}\simeq\Gamma({\mathbb{B}},\phi)
be a regular self-homotopic interpretation. Then if a

-
structure

is isotypic to

\widetilde{\mathbb{A}}\simeq\Gamma(\widetilde{\mathbb{B}},\phi)
is isotypic to

In 2018 A. Myasnikov and N. Romanovskii further developed the idea of isotypness
[

]
. They introduced the following definition.

Definition 30
.

An algebraic structure
{\mathbb{A}}=\langle A;L({\mathbb{A}})\rangle
is definable by types, if every algebraic structure

in the language

that is isotypic to

is isomorphic to

.

(expected in the next paper)
.

Let
{\mathbb{A}}
and

is definable by types.

Recall that an algebraic structure
{\mathbb{A}}=\langle A;L({\mathbb{A}})\rangle
is
prime
if it elementarily embeds in any model of
\mathrm{Th}({\mathbb{A}})
(any algebraic structure
\widetilde{\mathbb{A}}\equiv{\mathbb{A}}
);
{\mathbb{A}}
is
atomic
, if every complete type
\mathrm{tp}(\bar{a})
,
\bar{a}\in{\mathbb{A}}
, is
principal
(or

), i. e., there exists a formula

, such that
\mathrm{Th}({\mathbb{A}})\cup\{\phi\}\vdash\mathrm{tp}(\bar{a})

{\mathbb{A}}
is

if for any integer

and every two tuples

if
\mathrm{tp}(\bar{a})=\mathrm{tp}(\bar{b})
then there is an automorphism

, such that

(the latter is an equivalent form of the definition of homogeneity for countable structures

30
, Proposition 4.2.13]
). It is known that an algebraic structure
{\mathbb{A}}
in a countable language

is prime if and only if it is countable and atomic, provided that

has infinite models

is countable and atomic, then it is homogeneous

30
, Lemma 4.2.14]

and

Theorem 14

(expected in the next paper)
.

Let
{\mathbb{A}}\simeq\Gamma({\mathbb{B}},\phi)
be a regular right invertible interpretation of

in
{\mathbb{B}}

is prime too.

It was claimed in
[

that a similar result holds for bi-interpretations with parameters, though it was proven only for regular bi-interpretations, so whether this is true for bi-interpretations with parameters is, in fact, an open question.

As we have seen, regular bi-interpretations offer several advantages. Next, we will explore the implications of bi-interpretation with parameters.
