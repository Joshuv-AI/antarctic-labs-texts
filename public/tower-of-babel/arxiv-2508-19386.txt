Numerical simulations of oscillations for axisymmetric solar backgrounds with differential rotation and gravity
Ha Pham, Florian Faucher, Damien Fournier, Hélène Barucq, Laurent Gizon

Abstract
Local helioseismology comprises of imaging and inversion techniques employed to reconstruct the dynamic and interior of the Sun from correlations of oscillations observed on the surface, all of which require modeling solar oscillations and computing Green’s kernels. In this context, we implement and investigate the robustness of the Hybridizable Discontinuous Galerkin (HDG) method in solving the equation modeling stellar oscillations for realistic solar backgrounds containing gravity and differential rotation. While a common choice for modeling stellar oscillations is the Galbrun’s equation, our working equations are derived from an equivalent variant, involving less regularity in its coefficients, working with Lagrangian displacement and pressure perturbation as unknowns. Under differential rotation and axisymmetric assumption, the system is solved in azimuthal decomposition with the HDG method. Compared to no-gravity approximations, the mathematical nature of the wave operator is now linked to the profile of the solar buoyancy frequency N N which encodes gravity, and leads to distinction into regions of elliptic or hyperbolic behavior of the wave operator at zero attenuation. While small attenuation is systematically included to guarantee theoretical well-posedness, the above phenomenon affects the numerical solutions in terms of amplitude and oscillation pattern, and requires a judicious choice of stabilization. We investigate the stabilization of the HDG discretization scheme, and demonstrate its importance to ensure the accuracy of numerical results, which is shown to depend on frequencies relative to N N , and on the position of the Dirac source. As validations, the numerical power spectra reproduce accurately the observed effects of the solar rotation on acoustic waves.

Proof.

\displaystyle\nabla w

5
HDG formulation of the cylindrical modal equations

In
Section

4
, we have derived the modal boundary value
problem
Eqs.

4.16
and
4.17

for each azimuthal mode
m
on the meridional half-disk
D
,
Fig.

1
.
For the remaining of the section, the subscript
m
is dropped
from the unknowns and sources, such that we consider the system,

\begin{cases}\boldsymbol{A}_{m}\boldsymbol{u}+\boldsymbol{\beta}_{1}w+[\nabla]_{m}w=\mathbf{g},\\
[\nabla\cdot]_{m}\boldsymbol{u}+\boldsymbol{\beta}_{2}\cdot\boldsymbol{u}+\varrho\,w=h.\end{cases}\text{ on }D,\hskip 2.84544pt\text{ with boundary conditions }\begin{cases}\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}=0,\hskip 38.99998pt\text{on }\partial D^{a},\\
w+\alpha_{\mathrm{bc}}\,\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}=0,\,\,\text{on }\partial D^{b}.\end{cases}

(5.1)

Recall that
\boldsymbol{u}(\eta,z)=(u_{\eta}(\eta,z),u_{\phi}(\eta,z),u_{z}(\eta,z))^{t}

and
\boldsymbol{n}
is the normal vector defined from
\tilde{\boldsymbol{n}}

of
\partial D=\partial D^{a}\cup\partial D^{b}
, cf.
Eq.

4.5
.

We solve this problem with the Hybridizable Discontinuous Galerkin
method (HDG,
[
13
]
), following the perspective of

[
44
]
for notations.
In the HDG formulation, one distinguishes between the value of
an unknown on the union of the interior of mesh cells and its
trace on the skeleton of the mesh, here corresponding to a set
of edges.
Specifically, in addition to the so-called first-order volume unknowns

(\boldsymbol{u},w)
approximating the value (of the corresponding quantities)
in the interior of the cells, another unknown that we referred to as the
HDG numerical trace
\lambda
is introduced and defined on the set of
edges, to represent the Dirichlet trace of
w
there.
With this variable, the global system
Eq.

4.16

on
D
is written as a union of boundary value problems (local BVP)
each defined on a mesh cell with boundary data
\lambda
.

These local BVPs are decoupled from one another, i.e., independent per cell,
forming block-diagonal structures in the global linear system
(in terms of unknowns
(\boldsymbol{u},w,\lambda)
).
The link between cells is carried out by the constraints on the
boundary data
\lambda
, and imposes a certain degree of regularity
of the solution across the interfaces.
The aforementioned block-diagonal structure allows to employ
static condensation to yield a linear system solely in terms
of
\lambda
, which forms the essence of the HDG method in
the DG family.
Another specificity of a HDG method is the choice of stabilization
of the error in the Neumann trace, which in implementation provides
an expression for the numerical trace
\yhwidehat{\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}}

(which approximates the trace of
\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}=\tilde{\boldsymbol{u}}\cdot\tilde{\boldsymbol{n}}
,
see below), in terms of
\lambda
and the volume unknowns.

5.4.3
Summary

We can now put the discretized local and global problems together,

The description follows notation of

(where the HDG method is used for elastic anisotropic waves),

\displaystyle\hskip 19.91684pt\mathbb{A}^{e}\,\mathsf{U}^{e}\,\,+\,\,\mathbb{C}^{e}\,\mathbb{R}_{e}\,\Lambda=\,\mathsf{G}^{e}\,\,,\hskip 28.45274pt\forall\,\,e=1\,\ldots,n_{\mathrm{cell}}\,,

(5.48a)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sum_{e=1}^{n_{\mathrm{cell}}}\,\,\mathbb{R}^{t}_{e}\,\Big(\,\mathbb{B}^{e}\,\mathsf{U}^{e}\,\,+\,\,\mathbb{L}^{e}\,\mathbb{R}_{e}\,\Lambda\,\Big)\,=\,\boldsymbol{0}_{N_{\mathrm{dof}}\times 1}.

(Interpretation of HDG stabilization in radially symmetric backgrounds)
.

Here, we provide some interpretation for stabilization

5.59

We first recall that without rotation and

We introduce some working notions in the hyperbolic region. Compared to a classical wave operator
\partial_{t}^{2}-\Delta_{x}

\eta_{\boldsymbol{n}}
at zero attenuation, i.e. with

,

\mathfrak{f}=\begin{cases}\text{ is space-like},&\quad\eta_{\boldsymbol{n}}>0,\\
\text{ is time-like},&\quad\eta_{\boldsymbol{n}}<0\end{cases},\quad\text{for }\mathfrak{f}\in\mathbb{B}_{\mathrm{hyp}}.

In particular when

We can write

\eta_{\boldsymbol{n}}=\dfrac{c_{0}^{2}}{N^{2}-\omega^{2}}\left(n_{r}^{2}-\mathfrak{c}^{2}n_{\parallel}^{2}\right),\quad\text{ on }\mathbb{B}_{\mathrm{hyp}}.

(5.64)

We next observe the different nature of

(diffusion-type stabilization) and

(convection-type stabilization) at zero attenuation. The definition of

–

the stabilization

\tau_{d}=\mathrm{i}\omega\,\alpha_{\text{tune}}\,\eta_{\boldsymbol{n}}\times\begin{cases}-1,&\quad\text{ elliptic or hyperbolic time-like face},\\

(5.66)

–

6.3
Empirical study of the HDG stabilization

The HDG discretization involves a stabilization parameter

\tau
,
Section

5.5
, which is critical for
the accuracy of the method (e.g.,
[
45
]
).
Here we use the Liouville-c formulation of the equation
and solves for unknowns
(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{c},w_{c})
, such that the
relation for the numerical traces is written as

\widehat{\boldsymbol{\xi}_{c\,m}}\,=\,\boldsymbol{\xi}_{c\,m}\,-\,\tau\big(w_{c\,m}\,-\,\lambda)\,\boldsymbol{n}\,,\qquad\quad\text{HDG Numerical trace relation.}

(6.3)

Following the derivation carried out in

Section

5.5
, the
stabilization term
\tau
is of the form

\tau\,=\,|\boldsymbol{A}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{1}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}|\,+\,\,\alpha_{\text{tune}}\,|\boldsymbol{n}^{\mathrm{t}}\,\boldsymbol{A}^{-1}\,\boldsymbol{n}|\,,

(6.4)

with
\alpha_{\text{tune}}
a scaling factor.
For the heterogeneous solar backgrounds considered, analytical solutions
do not exist, therefore two criteria are set up to decide of an acceptable
stabilization:
(1) The absence of artifact in the solution,
and (2) Comparison with a reference solution based on a refined mesh.

We have performed a comparative study for values of
\alpha_{\text{tune}}

in the complex plane, with amplitude
\pm\omega${10}^{6}$
.
For the sake of conciseness, we do not provide all the choices
of stabilization that have been experimented, and only work
with the following illustrative choices:

\displaystyle\tau_{\mathrm{a}}

\displaystyle\,=\,1\,,

(6.5a)

\displaystyle\tau_{\mathrm{b}}

\displaystyle\,=\,|\boldsymbol{A}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{1}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}|\,+\,${10}^{6}$\omega\,|\boldsymbol{n}^{\mathrm{t}}\,\boldsymbol{A}^{-1}\,\boldsymbol{n}|\,,

(6.5b)

\displaystyle\tau_{\mathrm{c}}

\displaystyle\,=\,|\boldsymbol{A}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{1}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}|\,-\,${10}^{6}$\mathrm{i}\omega\,|\boldsymbol{n}^{\mathrm{t}}\,\boldsymbol{A}^{-1}\,\boldsymbol{n}|\,.

(6.5c)

In particular, we will see that the stabilization is critical mostly when
the source is in or close to
\mathbb{B}_{\mathrm{hyp}}
, i.e., when there are parts of the
domain in which
N^{2}>\omega^{2}
.

6.4
Ignoring gravity effects

To ignore the effects of gravity in the problem, one can
take the gradient of the gravity potential to zero (
\nabla\Phi_{0}=0
)
in the equations.
This assumption is reasonable when one
focuses on the solar acoustic p-modes, cf.
[
3
]
.
In this case, the buoyancy frequency
N^{2}
defined in
Eq.

3.27

is zero, hence

6.5
Summary

In this section, we have shown the behaviour of the wavefield
with solar background for three representative frequencies.
The difficulty of working with solar background is the non-zero
buoyancy frequency, which possibly leads to the existence of
the region
\mathbb{B}_{\mathrm{hyp}}
.

–

In the case where the frequency is above cut-off frequency,

\mathbb{B}_{\mathrm{hyp}}
is empty and the solutions are stable with respect
to the choice of the HDG stabilization, as depicted in
Fig.

5
.

–

For frequencies below cut-off,
\mathbb{B}_{\mathrm{hyp}}
is not empty.
The behaviour of the solution is affected
by the position of the source:
if the source is in
\mathbb{B}_{\mathrm{ell}}
, the solutions are evanescent
in
\mathbb{B}_{\mathrm{hyp}}
, and the HDG stabilizations behave well
(left of
Fig.

6
).
If the source is in
\mathbb{B}_{\mathrm{hyp}}
(or even close to),
propagation of singularity arises, with back and forth
reflections in the region
\mathbb{B}_{\mathrm{hyp}}
in a zigzag pattern.
In this case the choice of stabilization plays a role
in avoiding artifacts near the source position
(right of
6
).

–

Ignoring the gravity effects, the buoyancy frequency
is zero in the entire domain, hence
\mathbb{B}_{\mathrm{hyp}}
is empty.
This avoids the propagation of singularity for frequencies
below cut-off. However, ignoring gravity drastically
changes the solutions, which are not comparable in terms
of phase and amplitude,
Fig.

11
.

In the context of helioseismology, the sources and receivers
are taken near the surface and frequencies below cut-off are
studied. This corresponds precisely to the zone of
\mathbb{B}_{\mathrm{hyp}}
.
Note that here we have only shown the results for the azimuthal
mode
m=0
, but the behaviour is the same for non-zero azimuthal
modes, which are not included for the sake of conciseness.

Remark 17

(Including rotation and differential flow)
.

Due to the small values of the solar rotation, below
\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro Hz}
,
there are little visual differences in the solutions when incorporating
the rotation for the frequencies used above. However, these differences
are important as they allow to infer the rotation,
cf.
Sections

7
and
14
.
Differences may be expected when studying extremely
low frequencies with magnitude comparable to the solar
rotation, which is the case for inertial modes
[
25
]
.
Nonetheless, this is out of the scope of the current paper
and it will be the focus of future works.

The objective of this section is to validate our

helioseismic products, such as power spectra

7.16

and expected values of cross-covariance
Eq.

7.22

We illustrate with the synthetic power spectrum associated
with the standard solar model in

and compare different models of solar rotation in

We will show that the synthetic power spectrum for standard

contains peaks of energy that are in agreement with observations, and
with the eigenfrequencies computed with software

The power spectrum computed from the solar background containing rotation

Since the axisymmetric solver constructed in this work takes into account gravity

7.1
Derivation of synthetic helioseismic data

Helioseismic data correspond to line-of-sight Doppler
velocities and are obtained from instruments on board of
satellites (such as the Helioseismic and Magnetic Imager, HMI,
of the Solar Dynamics Observatory mission), or from
ground-based telescopes.
Here we show how to model helioseismic data with the azimuthal
Green’s kernel
G_{p}^{m}
given
Definition

3
, solved
with the HDG discretization method in
Section

5
.

Synthetic Doppler signal

In this work, in assuming single-height
contribution
15
15

15

The observation height
r_{\mathrm{obs}}
is commonly
set
\geq 1
, we use
r_{\mathrm{obs}}=$1.000\,21$
in our experiments.
,
we model observed signal
\psi(\hat{\mathbf{x}},\omega)

at position
\hat{\mathbf{x}}=(\theta,\phi)
on the solar disk
as the Eulerian pressure
\delta_{p}^{\mathrm{E}}(r_{\mathrm{obs}},\hat{\mathbf{x}},\omega)
;
secondly we assume
that each realization of the observation is
caused by a scalar stochastic source
\mathfrak{s}
.
The wave operator employed is from
Eq.

2.26
with only scalar source,

\psi(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{\rm obs};\omega):=\delta_{p}^{\mathrm{E}}(r_{\mathrm{obs}},\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{\rm obs},\omega),\quad\text{where }\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{odiv}}\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{\xi}\\[1.99997pt]
\delta_{p}^{\mathrm{E}}\end{pmatrix}\,=\,\begin{pmatrix}0\\[1.99997pt]
\mathfrak{s}\end{pmatrix}.

(7.1)

The synthetic signal, denoted by
\psi(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{\rm obs};\omega)
is related to the scalar Green’s
function
G_{p}

Eq.

2.28
as,

\psi(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{\rm obs};\omega):=\delta_{p}^{\mathrm{E}}(r_{\mathrm{obs}},\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{\rm obs},\omega)=\int_{\mathbb{B}_{\odot}}G_{p}(r_{\rm obs},\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{\rm obs},\mathbf{s};\omega)\,\mathfrak{s}(\mathbf{s})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{s}.

(7.2)

The observables are then projected onto spherical harmonic
functions
Y^{m}_{\ell}
, yielding coefficients denoted by

\psi^{lm}(\omega)
,

\psi^{lm}(\omega):=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}\psi(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{\rm obs};\omega)\,\overline{Y_{l}^{m}(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{\rm obs})}\,\mathrm{d}\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{\rm obs}=\int_{\mathbb{B}_{\odot}}\mathcal{K}_{p}^{lm}(r_{\rm obs},\mathbf{s};\omega)\,\mathfrak{s}(\mathbf{s})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{s}.

(7.3)

In the last expression, we have also introduced the kernel
\mathcal{K}_{p}^{lm}
defined as,

\mathcal{K}_{p}^{lm}(r_{\rm obs},\mathbf{s};\omega):=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}G_{p}(r_{\rm obs},\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{\rm obs},\mathbf{s};\omega)\,\overline{Y_{l}^{m}(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{\rm obs})}\,\mathrm{d}\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{\rm obs}.

(7.4)

Its expression is obtained by substituting the last expression
of
Eq.

7.2
and interchanging the integration order between

\int_{\mathbb{B}_{\odot}}d\mathbf{r}
and
\int_{\mathbb{S}}\mathrm{d}\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{\rm obs}
.

Azimuthal Green’s kernel

Recall
5
which states the cylindrical expansion of
G_{p}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{s};\omega)

with coefficients given by azimuthal Green’s kernel
G^{m}_{p}
,

G_{p}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{s};\omega)=\dfrac{1}{2\pi}\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}G^{m}_{p}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}},\tilde{\mathbf{s}}^{\prime};\omega)\,e^{\mathrm{i}m(\phi-\phi^{\prime})},\quad\mathbf{s}\notin\text{ z-axis}.

(7.5)

When the position of the Dirac source is on the axis, there is only the term associated with
m=0
in the above summand,

G_{p}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{s};\omega)=\dfrac{1}{2\pi}G^{0}_{p}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}},0,z^{\prime};\omega),\hskip 28.45274pt\mathbf{s}\in\text{ z-axis}.

(7.6)

In numerical resolution, our first choice is the Liouville-c
variant
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Liouville-c}}
, which yields
G_{c}^{m}
related to
G^{m}_{p}
by

G_{p}^{m}(\eta,z,\eta^{\prime},z^{\prime})\,=\,G_{c}^{m}(\eta,z,\eta^{\prime},z^{\prime})\,\,c_{0}(\eta,z)\sqrt{\rho_{0}(\eta,z)}\,\,c_{0}(\eta_{\mathrm{src}},z_{\mathrm{src}})\sqrt{\rho_{0}(\eta_{\mathrm{src}},z_{\mathrm{src}})}.

(7.7)

The value of
G^{m}_{c}
is obtained, in each resolution, for a frequency
\omega
, a point source position

(\eta_{\mathrm{src}},z_{\mathrm{src}})
, and mode
m
, as,

\displaystyle G^{m}_{c}(\eta,z;\eta_{\mathrm{src}},z_{\mathrm{src}};\omega):=w_{m}(\eta,z),

(7.8)

\displaystyle\text{ where }\quad\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Liouville-c}}^{m}\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{u}^{m}(\eta,z))\\
\ w_{m}(\eta,z)\end{pmatrix}=\dfrac{1}{\eta}\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{0}\\
\delta(\eta-\eta_{\mathrm{src}},z-z_{\mathrm{src}})\end{pmatrix}.

We next relate
\mathcal{K}_{p}^{lm}
to the numerically computed 2.5D kernel
G^{m}_{c}
.
We denote the representation of the source
\mathbf{s}
in cylindrical and spherical coordinates by

\mathbf{s}\leftrightarrow(\eta^{\prime},z^{\prime},\phi^{\prime})\leftrightarrow(s,\theta^{\prime},\phi^{\prime})\quad\text{ where }\eta^{\prime}=s\cos(\theta^{\prime}),\quad z^{\prime}=s\sin(\theta^{\prime})\,.

(7.9)

From the definitions of
\mathcal{K}_{p}^{lm}

Eq.

7.4
and spherical harmonics

Y^{m}_{\ell}(\theta,\phi)=c(\ell,m)P^{\lvert m\rvert}_{\ell}(\cos\theta)e^{\mathrm{i}m\phi}
,
we have,

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{p}^{lm}(r_{\rm obs},\mathbf{s};\omega)=\mathcal{K}_{p}^{lm}(r_{\rm obs},;\omega)=\mathcal{K}_{p}^{lm}(r_{\rm obs},s,\theta^{\prime},\phi^{\prime};\omega)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}G_{p}(r_{\rm obs},\theta_{\rm obs},\phi_{\rm obs},\mathbf{s};\omega)\,\overline{Y_{l}^{m}(\theta_{\rm obs},\phi_{\rm obs)}}\,\sin\theta_{\rm obs}\mathrm{d}\theta_{\rm obs}\mathrm{d}\phi_{\rm obs}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\hskip-5.69046pt\int_{0}^{\pi}\hskip-2.84544pt\left(\sum_{m^{\prime}}G^{m^{\prime}}_{p}(r_{\rm obs},\theta_{\rm obs},\eta^{\prime},z^{\prime};\omega)e^{\mathrm{i}m^{\prime}(\phi_{\rm obs}-\phi^{\prime})}\right)c(\ell,m)\overline{P^{\lvert m\rvert}_{\ell}(\cos\theta_{\rm obs})}e^{-\mathrm{i}m\phi_{\rm obs}}\,\mathrm{d}\phi_{\rm obs}\sin\theta_{\rm obs}\mathrm{d}\theta_{\rm obs}

\displaystyle=c(\ell,m)e^{-\mathrm{i}m\phi^{\prime}}\int_{0}^{\pi}G^{-m}_{p}(r_{\rm obs},\theta_{\rm obs},\eta^{\prime},z^{\prime};\omega)\,\overline{P^{\lvert m\rvert}_{\ell}(\cos\theta_{\rm obs})}\sin\theta_{\rm obs}\,\mathrm{d}\theta_{\rm obs}\,.

We thus obtain the following result.

Identity 6
.

The Green’s kernel corresponding to synthetic observables
Eq.

7.4

is related to the azimuthal Green’s kernel as

\mathcal{K}_{p}^{lm}(r_{\rm obs},s,\theta^{\prime},\phi^{\prime};\omega)=e^{-\mathrm{i}m\phi}\,\tilde{\mathcal{K}}_{p}^{lm}(r_{\rm obs},s,\theta^{\prime};\omega)\,.

(7.10)

where the quantity
\tilde{\mathcal{K}}_{p}^{lm}
defined as

\tilde{\mathcal{K}}_{p}^{lm}(r_{\rm obs},s,\theta^{\prime};\omega)=c(\ell,m)\int_{0}^{\pi}G^{-m}_{p}(r_{\rm obs},\theta_{\rm obs},\eta^{\prime},z^{\prime};\omega)\,\,\,\overline{P^{\lvert m\rvert}_{\ell}(\cos\theta_{\rm obs})}\sin\theta_{\rm obs}\,\mathrm{d}\theta_{\rm obs}

(7.11)

is independent of
\phi^{\prime}
.
We also have the independence of

\lvert\mathcal{K}_{p}^{lm}\rvert^{2}

with respect to
\phi^{\prime}
:

\lvert\mathcal{K}_{p}^{lm}(r_{\rm obs},s,\theta^{\prime},\phi^{\prime};\omega)\rvert^{2}=\lvert\tilde{\mathcal{K}}_{p}^{lm}(r_{\rm obs},s,\theta^{\prime};\omega)\rvert^{2}.

(7.12)

7.1.1
Synthetic power spectrum

The power spectrum is defined with the expected
value of the components
\psi^{lm}
such that:

\mathcal{P}_{l}^{m}(\omega):=\mathbb{E}[|\psi^{lm}(\omega)|^{2}].

(7.13)

We further assume that the random (realization) sources
are spatially uncorrelated and from a single depth
r_{\rm src}

such that

\mathbb{E}[s(\mathbf{r},\omega)\overline{s(\mathbf{r}^{\prime},\omega)}]=S(\omega)\delta(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\delta(r-r_{\mathrm{src}}),

(7.14)

where
S
represents the power distribution of the sources with frequencies.
In this case, in terms of the quantities
\tilde{\mathcal{K}}_{p}^{lm}
from
Eq.

7.11
, the power spectrum becomes

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{l}^{m}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{B}_{\odot}}\int_{\mathbb{B}_{\odot}}\mathcal{K}_{p}^{lm}(r_{\mathrm{obs}},\mathbf{r},\omega)\overline{\mathcal{K}_{p}^{lm}(r_{\mathrm{obs}},\mathbf{r}^{\prime},\omega)}\mathbb{E}[s(\mathbf{r})\overline{s(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})}]\mathrm{d}\mathbf{r}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{r}^{\prime}

(7.15)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{B}_{\odot}}|\mathcal{K}_{p}^{lm}(r_{\mathrm{obs}},\mathbf{r},\omega)|^{2}\mathbb{E}[s(\mathbf{r})\overline{s(\mathbf{r})}]\mathrm{d}\mathbf{r}

\displaystyle=S(\omega)\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}|\mathcal{K}_{p}^{lm}(r_{\mathrm{obs}},r_{\mathrm{src}},\hat{\mathbf{r}},\omega)|^{2}\mathrm{d}\hat{\mathbf{r}}

\displaystyle=S(\omega)\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}|\mathcal{K}_{p}^{lm}(r_{\mathrm{obs}},r_{\mathrm{src}},\theta,\phi,\omega)|^{2}\sin\theta\,\mathrm{d}\theta\mathrm{d}\phi.

By applying
Eq.

7.12
, we obtain

\mathcal{P}_{l}^{m}(\omega)=2\pi S(\omega)\int_{0}^{\pi}|\tilde{\mathcal{K}}_{p}^{lm}(r_{\mathrm{obs}},r_{\mathrm{src}},\theta,\omega)|^{2}\sin\theta\mathrm{d}\theta.

(7.16)

Therefore, for every azimuthal mode
m
, the power spectrum
can be computed from the simulations for sources at fixed
r_{\mathrm{src}}
and at many
\theta
locations to perform the integration. We will see in
Section

7.3
that the dependency of the power spectrum on the azimuthal order
m
is directly related to the rotation.

7.1.2
Synthetic cross-covariance

In addition to the power spectrum
Eq.

7.16
,
another useful helioseismic product is the cross-covariance
between the wavefields at two positions
\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{1}
and

\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{2}
on the visible disk.
It is typically computed in the frequency domain as

C(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{1},\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{2},\omega)=\psi(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{1},\omega)\overline{\psi(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{2},\omega)}.

(7.17)

The expected value
\mathcal{C}
of the cross-covariance is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{1},\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{2},\omega)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\psi(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{1},\omega)\overline{\psi(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{2},\omega)}]

(7.18)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{B}_{\odot}}\int_{\mathbb{B}_{\odot}}G_{p}(r_{\mathrm{obs}},\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{1},\mathbf{r},\omega)\overline{G_{p}(r_{\mathrm{obs}},\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{2},\mathbf{r}^{\prime},\omega)}\mathbb{E}[s(\mathbf{r})\overline{s(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})}]\mathrm{d}\mathbf{r}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{r}^{\prime}

(7.19)

\displaystyle=\int_{V}G_{p}(r_{\mathrm{obs}},\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{1},\mathbf{r},\omega)\overline{G_{p}(r_{\mathrm{obs}},\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{2},\mathbf{r},\omega)}\mathbb{E}[s(\mathbf{r})\overline{s(\mathbf{r})}]\mathrm{d}\mathbf{r}.

(7.20)

Writing the decomposition of
G_{p}
from
5
,

G_{p}(r_{\mathrm{obs}},\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{1},\mathbf{r},\omega)=\dfrac{1}{2\pi}\sum_{m}G_{p}^{m}(r_{\mathrm{obs}},\theta_{1},r,\theta,\omega)\textrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}m(\phi_{1}-\phi)},

(7.21)

we obtain

\mathcal{C}(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{1},\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{2},\omega)=\dfrac{S(\omega)}{2\pi}\sum_{m}\textrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}m(\phi_{1}-\phi_{2})}\int_{0}^{\pi}G_{p}^{m}(r_{\mathrm{obs}},\theta_{1},r_{\mathrm{src}},\theta,\omega)\overline{G_{p}^{m}(r_{\mathrm{obs}},\theta_{2},r_{\mathrm{src}},\theta,\omega)}\sin\theta\mathrm{d}\theta.

(7.22)

7.1.3
Simplification for the case without rotation

Without rotation, it is convenient to work in the spherical harmonic basis. In terms of the quantity introduced in
Eq.

7.4

(7.23)

(7.27)

\displaystyle=S(\omega)\sum_{l}\frac{4\pi}{2l+1}|\mathcal{K}_{p}^{l0}(r_{\rm obs},r_{\rm src},\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{0},\omega)|^{2}\sum_{m=-l}^{l}Y_{\ell}^{m}(\theta_{1},\phi_{1})\overline{Y_{l}^{m}(\theta_{2},\phi_{2})}.

Remark 19
.

The derivatives of

As

We obtain the derivatives

\displaystyle\frac{d\theta}{dz}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\dfrac{z^{2}}{\eta^{2}+z^{2}}}}\frac{\sqrt{\eta^{2}+z^{2}}-\dfrac{2z^{2}}{2\sqrt{\eta^{2}+z^{2}}}}{\eta^{2}+z^{2}}=-\frac{\eta}{\eta^{2}+z^{2}}=-\frac{\cos\theta}{r},

(7.39)

(7.40)

In particular, the derivatives of the simplified rotation

7.3.2
Effect of rotation on the power spectrum

By incorporating rotation in the equations,
the power spectrum depends on the azimuthal
order
m
,
Eq.

7.16
, and so
do the eigenvalues, that we label
\omega_{n\ell m}

with their radial
n
, latitudinal
\ell
and
longitudinal
m
wavenumbers.
These eigenvalues
represent position of peaks in magnitude of the power spectrum. The extraction
of their value from the power spectrum is discussed below.

Definition 4
.

For a fixed
n
and
\ell
,
we define the frequency shift
\Delta\omega_{n\ell m}

caused by rotation as the difference with
the eigenfrequency for
m=0
, such that,

\Delta\omega_{n\ell m}:=\omega_{n\ell m}-\omega_{n\ell 0},\quad-\ell\leq m\leq\ell.

(7.42)

Extraction of eigenfrequency from spectrum

To compute the shift
Eq.

7.42
, the eigenfrequency
\omega_{n\ell m}

is extracted from the power spectrum
\mathcal{P}_{l}^{m}(\omega)

by fitting a Lorentzian function, as shown in
Fig.

15
. This approach is more accurate than using directly the maximum value of the power spectrum whose accuracy is limited by the frequency resolution

Shift quantification by a-coefficients

To quantify the effect of rotation, the shift
\Delta\omega_{n\ell m}

Eq.

7.42
is next represented as,

\Delta\omega_{n\ell m}\,\,{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0}\simeq}\,\,\sum_{j=1}^{j_{\rm max}}a_{j}(n,\ell)\,\Pi_{j}^{(\ell)}(m),\quad-\ell\leq m\leq\ell.

(7.43)

At a fixed
\ell
, this fit is performed for all values of
m
with
\lvert m\rvert\leq\ell
. Here
a_{j}(n,\ell)
are the so-called a-coefficients and
\Pi_{j}^{(\ell)}
is a polynomial of degree
j
. The maximum degree
j_{\rm max}
depends on the complexity of the rotation profile and of the noise level in the case of observations. Standard helioseismic inversions use
j_{\rm max}=6,18,
or 36
[
34
]
.
Here, we use
j_{\rm max}=6
.

For the solar rotation
Fig.

14b

8
Conclusion

We have developed a forward solver for the equations of
stellar oscillations with background flow and gravity,
and have validated it in the context of helioseismology.
This is realized with the Hybridizable Discontinuous Galerkin
method, and used to solve the equations for realistic solar backgrounds.
These backgrounds are challenging due to their drastic
variations and the non-zero buoyancy frequency in the case
with gravity, which leads to
a change in the nature of the problem from ellipticity
to hyperbolicity, at zero attenuation and for frequencies below acoustic cut-off.
Despite encoding attenuation, appropriate choices of HDG stabilization and meshing
are required in numerical implementations to maintain accuracy.
It is not only due to the existence of these regions,
but also to the fact that
the formation and observation heights employed in helioseimology
are positioned in the transition zone between the two behaviors.

From the Green’s kernels, we derive helioseismic products such as
the power spectrum. The positions of high power match the
eigenfrequencies obtained independently with the GYRE eigensolver.
Including rotation, the power spectrum depends on the azimuthal
order
m
and the change in the eigenfrequency can be determined
analytically for simple rotation profiles.
The validation presented in this paper has been conducted in the solar context,
but our assumption of azimuthal symmetry allows for the extension of our solver
to other objects such as gas giants and stars, including fast rotators like
Altair, which has recently been found to exhibit non-radial pulsations
[
49
]
.

The developed solver is a valuable tool for generating synthetic observables,
enabling the study of differential rotation using various helioseismic techniques,
such as time-distance
[
15
]
and holography
[
37
]
.
Moreover, we readily get access to the 3D displacement field as the HDG
method solves the first-order system.
This will allow for more accurate modeling of observables, taking into
account projection and radiative transfer effects
[
20
]
.
These experiments allow us
to validate our code without and with rotation.
This efficient forward solver also provides a crucial step towards nonlinear
inversions (see, e.g.,
[
41
]
for iterative holography).
In addition to the acoustic modes that are traditionally used in
helioseismology, the inclusion of rotation further gives access
to inertial modes such as Rossby modes that have recently been
observed on the Sun
[
38
,
25
]
. These modes have
sensitivity deep inside the Sun and have a potential for better
constraining the base of the convection zone
[
23
]
.

Appendix A
Cylindrical expansion of
\boldsymbol{A}^{0}_{\Omega}
and its coefficient
\boldsymbol{A}_{m}

In this appendix
we compute the coefficients
\boldsymbol{A}_{m}
appearing in the system of boundary
value problems
Eq.

4.16

defined on mode
m
.
These are also the coefficients in the cylindrical expansion
Eq.

4.12
of
matrix-valued differential operator
\boldsymbol{A}^{0}_{\Omega}

Eq.

2.24
,

[\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{u}](\eta,\phi,z)=\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}\boldsymbol{A}_{m}(\eta,z)\boldsymbol{u}_{m}(\eta,z)\,e^{\mathrm{i}\,m\,\phi}\,.

(A.1)

Recall from
Section

4
that the components of a tensor in basis
\{\mathbf{e}_{\eta},\mathbf{e}_{\phi},\mathbf{e}_{z}\}
are denoted by
[\cdot]
, e.g. for a vector
\boldsymbol{u}
, and its vector product with matrix
M
,

\boldsymbol{u}=u_{\eta}\mathbf{e}_{\eta}+u_{\phi}\mathbf{e}_{\phi}+u_{z}\mathbf{e}_{z}\hskip 2.84544pt\Rightarrow\hskip 2.84544pt[\boldsymbol{u}]=(u_{\eta},u_{\phi},u_{z})^{t};\hskip 14.22636pt\left[M\boldsymbol{u}\right]=\left[M\right]\left[\boldsymbol{u}\right].

The cylindrical expansion of a vector
\boldsymbol{u}
is given by,

[\boldsymbol{u}](\eta,\phi,z)\,=\,\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}\boldsymbol{u}_{m}(\eta,z)\,e^{\mathrm{i}m\phi}\,,\quad\text{where}\,\,\boldsymbol{u}_{m}(\eta,z)=(u_{\eta m},u_{\phi m},u_{zm})^{t}\,.

(A.2)

We recall the definition of
\boldsymbol{A}_{0}^{\Omega}
from

Eq.

2.24

\boldsymbol{A}_{0}^{\Omega}:=\boldsymbol{B}_{0}^{\Omega}\,-\,2\mathrm{i}\omega\gamma_{\mathrm{att}}\,+\,\mathbf{Y}_{\Omega}\otimes\Big(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\rho}\,-\,\dfrac{\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{p}}{\Gamma_{1}}\Big),

(A.3)

where the matrix-valued differential operator
\boldsymbol{B}_{0}^{\Omega}
is,
Eq.

2.25
,

\boldsymbol{B}_{0}^{\Omega}:=\big(-\mathrm{i}\omega+\mathbf{v}_{0}\cdot\nabla\big)^{2}\,+\,2\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{c}\times(-\mathrm{i}\omega+\mathbf{v}_{0}\cdot\nabla)\,-\,2\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{c}\times\nabla\mathbf{v}_{0}\,-\,\nabla\left((\mathbf{v}_{0}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{v}_{0}\right).

(A.4)

The zero-th order term containing
\mathbf{Y}_{\Omega}
, and the inverse scale height vectors are,

Eq.

2.13
,

\mathbf{Y}_{\Omega}=\nabla\phi_{0}+\ \boldsymbol{\Omega}\times\boldsymbol{\Omega}\times\mathbf{x}\overset{\lx@cref{creftype\lx@tilde refnum}{phifullexp}}{=}\nabla\phi_{0}-\Upomega^{2}\eta\mathbf{e}_{\eta},\quad\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{p}=-\dfrac{\nabla p}{p},\quad\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\rho}=-\dfrac{\nabla\rho}{\rho}\,.

(A.5)

The computation is carried out for differential rotation flow background, cf.
1
,

\mathbf{v}_{0}=(\boldsymbol{\Omega}-\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{c})\times\mathbf{x}=\eta\,(\Omega-\Omega_{c})\mathbf{e}_{\phi},

(A.6)

with the observation frame rotation rate
\Omega_{c}
, full rotation rate
\Omega
and the difference between the two rates
\tilde{\Omega}
,

\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{c}=\Omega_{c}(\eta,z)\,\mathbf{e}_{z},\,\,\text{ with }\Omega_{c}\text{ constant},\hskip 14.22636pt\boldsymbol{\Omega}=\Omega(\eta,z)\,\mathbf{e}_{z},\hskip 14.22636pt\tilde{\Omega}(\eta,z):=\Omega(\eta,z)-\Omega_{c}.
