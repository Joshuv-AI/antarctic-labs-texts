Sensitivity of Marine Pelagic Fish Catch Potential to Changes in Primary Production in a Global Fisheries Model
Authors: Isabella Morgante, William W L Cheung, Laurent Bopp
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTClimate‐driven changes in ocean primary production are expected to have cascading effects across marine food webs, yet the sensitivity of marine fish catch potential to primary production variability is rarely incorporated into vulnerability frameworks relative to physical stressors such as warming and deoxygenation. Life‐history traits can mediate species sensitivity to environmental change, reflecting evolutionary adaptations to the environmental conditions of their habitats. Using a global fisheries dynamic bioclimatic envelope model (DBEM) forced by alternative primary production projections under identical physical ocean conditions, we assess how the variability in primary production propagates to estimates of maximum catch potential (MCP) for 120 pelagic fish species with diverse life history traits. Sensitivity is quantified using an index defined as the ratio of inter‐simulation variability in MCP to variability in primary production. Mixed‐effects models are used to evaluate how biological traits and sea surface temperature (SST) influence the sensitivity. SST is the main predictor of the primary production sensitivity index across species within the DBEM modelling framework. Sensitivity index values are highest in cold waters (< 15°C) and decline more than 10‐fold across the observed SST gradient, with the strongest attenuation above 27°C. These patterns are consistent with physiological constraints encoded in the DBEM's temperature‐dependent formulations. Maximum body size is a significant but modest predictor of sensitivity, for every 10 cm increase in body length, the sensitivity index decreased by 1.5% (95% CI: 0.9%–2.2%). However, biological traits explain only a small proportion of species‐level variation in the sensitivity index. Our findings suggest that trait‐only climate vulnerability assessments may overlook important temperature‐dependent constraints on species sensitivity that emerge from bottom‐up energy pathways. Integrating bottom‐up pathways and structural uncertainty into species climate risk assessments can improve climate‐ready management in systems where production variability is a major driver of catch potential.Keywords: climate change, fisheries vulnerability, global fisheries model, pelagic fish, primary production, sensitivity index

Introduction

Climate change is driving rapid shifts in ocean conditions that threaten marine ecosystems and the fisheries that depend on them. Rising temperatures, ocean acidification and changes in oxygen, salinity and primary production affect marine organisms at multiple levels, influencing individual physiological and life history processes, shifting species distributions and phenology and altering food web dynamics (Bindoff et al. 2019; Bopp et al. 2013; Cooley et al. 2023). These changes are expected to continue in the 21st century, resulting in projected global declines in fish biomass and disruptions to fisheries, livelihoods and food security (Barange et al. 2014; Bryndum‐Buchholz et al. 2019; Cheung et al. 2016; Lam et al. 2016; Tittensor et al. 2021). However, assessments of how these changes translate into species‐level vulnerability have focused predominantly on physical climate drivers, leaving bottom‐up energy constraints comparatively underexplored.

The vulnerability and associated risk of marine fish to climate change varies depending on the inherent sensitivity of a species, their ability to adapt to changing environmental conditions and their exposure to the climate hazard (Field et al. 2014; Jones and Cheung 2017). Most vulnerability assessments emphasise physical climate drivers, particularly ocean warming, acidification and deoxygenation, while bottom‐up energy constraints remain less explored (Cheung et al. 2018, 2022; Ding et al. 2017; Jones and Cheung 2017; Sora et al. 2025). Ocean warming and temperature extremes are considered major threats for marine fisheries as changes in temperature affect fish populations through a combination of physiological and ecological mechanisms (Cheung et al. 2016; Dahlke et al. 2020; Perry et al. 2005; Pörtner and Farrell 2008; Smale et al. 2019; Smith et al. 2023). Outside a species' thermal window, aerobic scope is reduced with resulting constraints on higher level functions such as growth and reproduction (Pinsky et al. 2013; Pörtner and Farrell 2008; Pörtner and Knust 2007). Temperature stress interacts synergistically with acidification and deoxygenation, compounding physiological constraints such as acid–base regulation and reduced aerobic capacity (Breitburg et al. 2018; Pörtner et al. 2014; Steckbauer et al. 2020). These interacting stressors can result in decreased fish biomass and catch potential (Cheung et al. 2010; Dahlke et al. 2020; Lotze et al. 2019; Przeslawski et al. 2015).

Life history and ecological traits are widely used as proxies to assess the intrinsic vulnerability of marine fish to climate stressors in place of detailed population‐level data which is often limited (Cheung et al. 2005; Foden et al. 2013). Among these traits, maximum body size, temperature preference and distributional range arise as key predictors (Bryndum‐Buchholz et al. 2019; Cheung et al. 2018; Jones and Cheung 2017; Pinsky et al. 2019). Large‐bodied species, with high metabolic demands, are generally more susceptible to warming and deoxygenation due to constraints on aerobic performance and increased hypoxia risk (Pinsky et al. 2013; Pörtner and Knust 2007). In contrast, small‐bodied species typically experience lower metabolic stress and their opportunistic life history strategies such as rapid growth, short lifespans and high turnover rates confer greater resilience to acute physiological stress (Muhling et al. 2017; Pecuchet et al. 2017; Petrik 2019; Winemiller 2005; Winemiller and Rose 1992). Beyond body size, thermal tolerance and biogeographic range further affect species' responses to climate change. Species with broader thermal tolerance windows are assumed to be more capable of withstanding environmental variability, whereas those with narrower windows such as polar species may be more constrained (Dahlke et al. 2020; Pörtner and Farrell 2008; Sunday et al. 2019). Similarly, species with broader latitudinal ranges may be less vulnerable because they can shift distributions towards suitable habitats.

These trait‐based frameworks have been instrumental in characterising climate vulnerability to physical drivers but have been slower to incorporate the bottom‐up pathways through which changes in primary production propagate to consumers. This omission likely reflects the difficulty of quantifying the trophic and physiological pathways that link primary producers to consumers across the diversity of pelagic life histories (Kadin et al. 2019). Changes in primary production can propagate and amplify through the food web, influencing the abundance and productivity of higher trophic levels (Chust et al. 2014; Guibourd de Luzinais et al. 2023; Heneghan et al. 2021; Lotze et al. 2019; Marshak and Link 2021; Mcowen et al. 2015; Stock et al. 2017). Small‐bodied species, whose opportunistic life histories tightly couple them to prey availability, may be particularly responsive to such fluctuations (Muhling et al. 2017; Pecuchet et al. 2017; Winemiller 2005). Ocean warming further modifies these dynamics by restructuring plankton communities (Helaouët and Beaugrand 2007; Kwiatkowski et al. 2019), altering both the quantity and quality of energy available to higher trophic levels (Blanchard et al. 2012; Cheung et al. 2011). Physiological constraints at high temperatures may also reduce the capacity of fish to convert available energy into biomass, as energy is increasingly diverted to maintenance and stress responses rather than growth when species approach their thermal limits (Dahlke et al. 2020; Pörtner and Knust 2007). Consequently, sensitivity to changes in primary production may itself be moderated by the thermal environment. Incorporating metrics of species' sensitivity to primary production variability could therefore provide a more mechanistic understanding of climate risk, capturing bottom‐up pathways of change that are otherwise overlooked in current assessments.

Given the vulnerability of fish and fisheries to climate change and the crucial role of primary production in supporting fish biomass, this study investigates how climate‐driven changes in primary production influence maximum catch potential, and how these effects interact with ocean temperature. Specifically, using outputs from a global fisheries model, we develop a sensitivity index and use a statistical framework to test two hypotheses: (1) that biological and ecological traits can predict the sensitivity of fish catch potential to primary production variability, with smaller‐bodied fish being more sensitive, and (2) that sensitivity to primary production decreases at high ocean temperatures as species approach their thermal limits.

Materials and Methods

The Dynamic Bioclimate Envelope Model (DBEM) (Cheung et al. 2011) was used to project changes in fish catch potential for 120 commercially important pelagic fish species between 1950 and 2100 under a high‐emissions scenario (Shared Socioeconomic Pathway (SSP) 5–8.5). Four DBEM simulations were run using identical physical oceanographic forcings but different primary production inputs derived from two versions of a biogeochemical model and two primary production formulations.

This simulation design, in which physical forcing is fixed across simulations and only the primary production input varies, allows inter‐simulation variation in the catch potential to be attributed exclusively to the primary production input forcing rather than to co‐varying physical drivers. As the differences between primary production forcings stem from structural differences between versions of an established mechanistic biogeochemical model, rather than arbitrarily imposed perturbations, this design has the added benefit of allowing us to quantify how existing structural uncertainty in primary production projections propagates into catch potential within the DBEM.

To reduce the effect of interannual variability, maximum catch potential and primary production values were aggregated into five 20‐year averaged intervals (2001–2020, 2021–2040, 2041–2060, 2061–2080, 2081–2100). The period 1971–2000 served as the baseline for computing relative change and 1950–2000 provided model spin‐up. A sensitivity index (SI) was then calculated for each species, grid cell and time interval to quantify the variability in maximum catch potential relative to primary production variability across simulations. Species' biological and ecological traits were subsequently used in linear mixed models and generalised additive mixed models to explain variation in the SI.

DBEM Simulations

The DBEM is a spatially explicit bioclimatic envelope model that projects species‐level abundance and catch by adjusting a species' habitat suitability and carrying capacity over time based on physiology, life history traits, depth and latitudinal ranges and environmental preferences across a global 0.5° latitude x 0.5° longitude ocean grid. Maximum catch potential was estimated by applying a fishing mortality corresponding to the species' maximum sustainable yield (MSY). The analysis included 120 pelagic species spanning three functional groups: small (< 30 cm; n = 38), medium (30–89 cm; n = 45) and large (≥ 90 cm; n = 37) pelagic fishes. Collectively, these species accounted for more than 90% of global pelagic species catch between 2015 and 2019 (Pauly et al. 2020).

Ocean and Biogeochemical Forcings

Physical Forcings

Physical oceanographic input variables required for the DBEM were obtained from the Institut Pierre‐Simon Laplace Climate Model (IPSL‐CM6A‐LR; Boucher et al. 2020) run under a high emissions, no mitigation scenario (SSP5‐8.5). Monthly model output was used to compute annual means prior to input into the DBEM. Projections for each ocean variable were re‐gridded from a global 1° longitude × 1° latitude grid to a 0.5° latitude × 0.5° longitude grid using bilinear interpolation to match the spatial resolution required by the DBEM. For near‐coastal grid cells with missing values after interpolation, a distance‐weighted extrapolation method was applied using inverse‐distance weighting between valid ocean cell centroids with distances calculated using the haversine formula.

Primary Production Forcings

Two offline configurations of the NEMO‐PISCES biogeochemical model (v3.6 and v4.2; Bopp et al. 2022) were forced with identical IPSL‐CM6A‐LR physical conditions to produce independent primary production offline simulation time series. ‘Offline’ refers to simulations in which the biogeochemical model is driven by externally supplied physical fields rather than being fully coupled to the ocean circulation model. This experimental design differs fundamentally from standard Earth System Model ensemble approaches in which all variables, both physical and biogeochemical, co‐vary across ensemble members. Here, physical ocean conditions are held constant across all model simulations and only the primary production input to the fisheries model varies. This controlled isolation of primary production allows the variability in maximum catch potential to be specifically attributed to primary production rather than to co‐varying physical drivers.

The two PISCES model versions represent meaningfully distinct biogeochemical structures (Bopp et al. 2022). Using two model generations was therefore intended to capture structural uncertainty arising from differences in biogeochemical model architecture. Although both versions are forced by the same physical fields and share a common model lineage, their different underlying biogeochemical formulations generate independent primary production trajectories, particularly under projected climate change conditions where structural differences become more pronounced (Bopp et al. 2022; Kwiatkowski et al. 2020).

For each configuration, two primary production formulations were used to reflect alternative trophic assumptions. The first was total depth‐integrated primary production (vintPP). The second was a trophic level‐adjusted primary production value (taPP) which accounts for size‐structured energy transfer, assuming that small phytoplankton contribute to higher trophic levels through an additional trophic step (Cheung et al. 2011; Pauly and Christensen 1995). Thus, taPP = NPP[large] + NPP[small] × TTE where a generic trophic transfer efficiency (TTE) of 10% was applied (Pauly and Christensen 1995). This yielded a total of four DBEM simulations for each fish species: PISCES v3.6 + vintPP, PISCESv3.6 + taPP, PISCESv4.2 + vintPP and PISCESv4.2 + taPP.

Sensitivity Analysis

Sensitivity Index

A sensitivity index (SI) was calculated for each grid cell, i and 20‐year time interval to quantify the relative variability in projected change in maximum catch potential (MCP) compared to variability in projected change in primary production across the four DBEM simulations. Change in both MCP and primary production was expressed as the proportional change relative to the 1971–2000 baseline for each grid cell. Expressing change relative to the baseline reduces bias arising from differences in absolute primary production among regions, allowing the SI to compare variability in projected relative responses rather than absolute production differences. where sd denotes the standard deviation across the four simulations. SI values greater than 1 indicate that variability in maximum catch potential exceeds variability in primary production across the simulation ensemble and values less than 1 indicate attenuated sensitivity.

As the standard deviation computed across only four simulation values is inherently noisy at the individual grid‐cell level, a hierarchical median aggregation strategy was applied to derive robust species‐level SI estimates. Grid‐level SIs were first aggregated to the Large Marine Ecosystem (LME) level by taking the median across all contributing grid cells. Species‐level SIs were then calculated as the median across all LMEs in which the species had catch. Medians were used throughout to reduce the influence of extreme values and to guard against unstable standard deviation estimates at the grid level propagating to higher‐level summaries. Grid cells without catch in any of the four simulations were excluded to ensure the SI was computed only where the species was consistently present.

This aggregation approach means that SI values used in subsequent statistical models represent the central tendency of the sensitivity relationship across a species' occupied range, rather than any single grid‐cell estimate. The stability of this approach was confirmed by the high conditional R 2 values in mixed models, indicating that the hierarchically aggregated SI captures a coherent biological signal across species and regions.

Species Traits

Five biological and ecological traits hypothesised to influence species' sensitivity to primary production variability were compiled for each species from publicly available datasets. Maximum body length, trophic level and the von Bertalanffy growth rate coefficient were extracted from FishBase (Froese and Pauly 2025). Latitudinal breadth was based on the difference between the northern and southern range limits of a species. These traits were selected because they capture key aspects of a species' life history, ecological niche and distribution that influence its dependence on and response to variations in primary production. Species‐specific trait values are provided in Table S1.

Statistical Analyses

Two complementary statistical approaches were used to examine the relationship between species traits and sensitivity index. Linear mixed models (LMMs) were used to test the independent effects of biological and ecological traits, and generalised additive mixed models (GAMMs) assessed the potentially non‐linear influence of sea surface temperature. All predictors were mean‐centred and standardised prior to statistical modelling to facilitate interpretation of effect sizes. The sensitivity index was log10‐transformed to normalise its distribution. All analyses were conducted in R v4.4.1 using the lme4 LMMs and mgcv for GAMMs.

Trait—Sensitivity Index Relationships

Linear mixed models (LMMs) were fitted with globally aggregated species‐level SI values as the response variable and species traits as fixed‐effect predictors to test the relationship between biological and ecological traits and the sensitivity of maximum catch potential to primary production variability, independent of location‐specific environmental conditions. Random intercepts were included for time interval to account for repeated observations.

To account for model selection uncertainty, a bootstrapped model‐averaging approach was applied (Burnham and Anderson 2002). Species were resampled with replacement to generate 1000 bootstrap replicates. For each replicate, a full LMM including all traits as fixed effects was fitted, and all‐subsets model selection was performed. Models within ΔAICc ≤ 4 of the best‐performing model were retained; this threshold was chosen to allow inclusion of models with substantial support while remaining conservative relative to the commonly used ΔAICc ≤ 2 criterion (Burnham and Anderson 2002). Parameter estimates were averaged across retained models using Akaike weights applied to full model averaging (i.e., parameters absent from a model were assigned a coefficient of zero). The relative importance of each predictor was quantified as the sum of Akaike weights across all top models containing that variable. To test whether the effect of latitudinal breadth depended on the geographic position of a species' distribution, a sensitivity analysis was conducted in which a species latitudinal centroid and its interaction with latitudinal breadth were included in the full model. A second sensitivity test was also conducted that excluded maximum body size from the candidate predictor set to test if the functional group categorical variable importance in the full model reflected its correlation with body size or if it showed a distinct ecological effect detectable independently of body size.

Effect of Sea Surface Temperature on the Sensitivity Index

To assess whether the relationship between maximum catch potential sensitivity and species traits was mediated by temperature, a GAMM was used to evaluate the influence of SST and its interaction with body size on the SI. The model included smooth terms for SST and body size, and a tensor product interaction between them to capture potential non‐linear joint effects. Random intercepts were specified for taxon, LME and time interval to account for repeated measures across these grouping levels. LMEs encompass distinct oceanographic and productivity regimes (e.g., eastern boundary upwelling systems, temperate shelf seas with pronounced seasonal blooms and tropical shelves). Thus, including LME as a random effect allows regional differences in production systems to be incorporated in the statistical framework without explicitly classifying each observation into a separate production system category. Observations were weighted by the square root of the number of grid cells contributing to each species‐LME‐time interval combination to down‐weight regions with greater spatial coverage.

The model was fitted using fast restricted maximum likelihood (fREML) in mgcv, with penalisation of non‐informative smooth terms to guard against overfitting. Residuals were assumed to follow a scaled Student's t distribution to account for heavy tails and improve robustness to outliers. To evaluate sensitivity to smoothing parameter selection, basis dimensions (k) for SST, body size and their tensor product interaction were varied systematically across a range of k = 5–20, with the final values selected based on AIC and the k‐index diagnostic. Final model selection was also informed by residual checks and concurvity diagnostics.

Parametric bootstrapping was used to derive 95% confidence intervals for parameter estimates. For each of 1000 bootstrap replicates, new response vectors were simulated from the marginal fitted distribution (i.e., with random effects set to zero) using the estimated Student's t parameters, and the model was refitted to each simulated dataset. The interaction between SST and body size was visualised separately for small (< 30 cm), medium (30–89 cm) and large (≥ 90 cm) pelagic fishes across the observed SST range, with random effects set to zero to isolate fixed‐effect predictions. Effect sizes were calculated for each bootstrap iteration as the difference between the maximum and minimum predicted SI within each size class across the SST range. Variance explained by the model was partitioned into contributions from fixed effects alone (marginal R2) and fixed plus random effects combined (conditional R 2).

Results

Global Projections of Maximum Catch Potential for the Study Species

The different formulations of primary production used in the fisheries simulations project globally stable or increasing primary production (Figure 1a; Bopp et al. 2022). In the context of this study's simulation framework, global maximum catch potential for large and medium pelagic fish is projected to decline over the next century with a mean decline of −45.2% ± 3.7% and −55.9% ± 4.8% by 2080–2100, respectively (Figure 2b,c). These uncertainty ranges (±1 standard deviation across simulations) reflect the component of projected variability attributable exclusively to differences in primary production forcing, since all four simulations share identical physical ocean conditions. All four simulations for large and medium pelagic species showed good agreement in the direction and magnitude of global change, indicating that the sign of the projected decline is robust to primary production variability.

Trait‐Based Predictors of Species Sensitivity Index

Body size and latitudinal breadth were the most important and statistically significant trait predictors of species‐level sensitivity index (mean relative importance 0.83 and 0.73 respectively) (Figure 3a,b). In general, species with smaller body size and narrower latitudinal ranges were more sensitive to primary production variation. For every 10 cm increase in body length, the sensitivity index decreased by 1.5% (95% CI: −2.2% to −0.9%) and for every 10° expansion in latitudinal range the sensitivity index increased by 4.5% (95% CI: +2.1% to +7.6%) (Table 1). Trophic level also exhibited a positive effect (+2.8% SI per trophic level; 95% CI: +0.2% to +7.4%; Table 1), although the small magnitude of the effect and smaller relative parameter importance indicate that differences in trophic position contribute only modestly to variation in the sensitivity index. Growth rate and functional group had low relative importance (< 0.3) and non‐significant coefficients. When body size was excluded from the candidate set, relative importance of functional group increased (from 0.066 to 0.238) and both coefficients became statistically significant (Table S2; Figure S1), suggesting that the statistical signal captured by the functional group is tied to the body size gradient it partly encodes rather than reflecting a distinct ecological effect independent of size within the DBEM.

Effect of SST on the Sensitivity Index

A strong non‐linear relationship was identified between sea surface temperature (SST) and the sensitivity index using bootstrapped GAMMs. The smooth term for SST was highly significant across bootstrap replicates (median edf = 5.36; proportion with p < 0.05 = 0.93; Table 3). In contrast, body size (proportion with p < 0.05 = 0.005) and the body size‐SST interaction (proportion with p < 0.05 = 0.26) were not consistently significant, indicating that SST exerts a dominant and largely size‐independent effect on the sensitivity index within the model.

Predicted sensitivity index declined with increasing SST across all body size classes. The sensitivity index is highest in cooler waters (< 15°C), remains relatively constant at intermediate temperatures (15°C–27°C) and declines markedly above 27°C. The magnitude of this SST‐related change in sensitivity index was comparable among size classes, with median differences between maximum and minimum predicted log10 sensitivity index values of 1.07 for small and medium species and 1.13 for large species (Table 4). These effect sizes correspond to roughly an 11–13‐fold difference in sensitivity index across the full temperature range, implying that within the DBEM, species inhabiting cold waters are about an order of magnitude more sensitive to primary production variation than those in warm waters. Differences among size classes were not statistically meaningful, with substantial overlaps in the 95% confidence intervals for these estimates. The GAMM explained 42% of the deviance (adjusted R 2 = 0.55). Random effects for LME, taxon and time interval were significant in 87%–100% of bootstrap replicates (Table 3) which underscore a strong species‐ and region‐specific structuring of sensitivity index. The consistently strong LME effect suggests that regional ecosystem characteristics, including differences in broad production regimes represented by different LMEs, contribute to variation in sensitivity index beyond SST.

Discussion

Our analysis reveals that the sensitivity of pelagic maximum catch potential to primary production variability is determined by both environmental conditions and species traits, as projected by the DBEM. Conventional trait‐based frameworks typically treat sensitivity as an intrinsic biological property, yet SST emerges as an important environmental mediator in the model, while maximum body size acts as a key predictor independent of environmental conditions. Given the central role of primary production in fisheries productivity, understanding how temperature and biological traits affect sensitivity in a modelling context is an important step towards building more mechanistically complete vulnerability assessments.

Trait‐Based Determinants of Sensitivity Index to Primary Production

Maximum body size is identified as an important biological trait shaping the sensitivity index, with smaller‐bodied species exhibiting larger sensitivity indices (Figure 3a,b; Table 1). Small pelagic species are tightly coupled to primary production and often consume a large portion of total plankton biomass through direct feeding (Pikitch et al. 2014; Ruzicka et al. 2024; A. D. M. Smith et al. 2011), making them highly responsive to fluctuations of lower trophic level production. Empirical studies have attributed rapid population changes of small pelagic fish to variability in plankton productivity and other ocean drivers (Alheit and Hagen 1997; Checkley et al. 2017; Lindegren et al. 2013). For example, the Peruvian anchoveta population was found to vary consistently with zooplankton biovolume from 1961 to 2005 (Ayón et al. 2004, 2008) and in the North Pacific and Humboldt Current, changes in anchovy and sardine populations were driven largely by ocean warming (Chavez et al. 2003; Lindegren et al. 2013; Takasuka et al. 2007).

In contrast, large‐bodied pelagic species, such as tunas and billfishes, were found to have lower sensitivity indices, consistent with their slower life histories and broad foraging ranges that can buffer against short‐term changes in food availability (Pecuchet et al. 2017; Winemiller 2005). As the DBEM applies primary production constraints identically across species (by scaling grid cell level carrying capacity proportionally to primary production change when it falls below a species‐specific historic threshold), the lower sensitivity of larger‐bodied species reflects the greater role of life history constraints in catch dynamics for these species within the model. This is supported by the supplementary analysis in which excluding body size from the candidate predictor set increased the relative importance of functional group and coefficient significance (Table S2; Figure S1). The explanatory power of the functional group is largely due to the size structure it inherently encodes rather than additional ecological roles that a functional group may play within an ecosystem. This does not imply that the ecological differences among functional groups are unimportant but, more broadly, functional roles that operate independently of size, such as differences in prey selectivity, habitat use, or trophic pathway that are not well proxied by body length, are not well resolved in the DBEM.

The projected increase in global MCP for small pelagic fishes differs from previous multi‐model global fisheries projections that generally report stable or declining forage fish production under climate change. This difference primarily reflects the experimental design rather than conflicting model behaviour. Our simulations were deliberately forced using a single Earth System Model framework (IPSL‐CM6A‐LR coupled with NEMO‐PISCES) that projects a globally neutral or increasing primary production trajectory under SSP5‐8.5 (Figure 1a; Bopp et al. 2022), in contrast to the declines in primary production projected by many CMIP6 models. Small pelagic fishes in the DBEM are tightly coupled to primary production through bottom‐up effects on carrying capacity and, as a result, the increased primary production translates into higher catch potential for these species. Larger pelagic fishes are more strongly constrained by warming‐related physiological limitations embedded within the DBEM, including temperature‐dependent habitat suitability, growth and carrying capacity (Cheung et al. 2011, 2016; Pörtner and Farrell 2008), resulting in declining catch potential despite favourable bottom‐up conditions. Thus, the global projections (Figure 2) should be interpreted primarily as demonstrating the differing sensitivity of pelagic functional groups to alternative primary production trajectories rather than representing a revised projection of future pelagic catch.

Beyond the DBEM, empirical and multi‐model evidence suggests that sustained declines in primary production are further amplified through food web pathways as the energy losses at each trophic level accumulate, disproportionately affecting higher‐trophic‐level predators (Guibourd de Luzinais et al. 2023; Kirby and Beaugrand 2009; Kwiatkowski et al. 2019; Lotze et al. 2019; Petrik et al. 2020). This may also explain why trophic level was only a weak predictor of the sensitivity index in this study. Although trophic level describes a species' position within the food web, it does not capture ecological flexibility through prey switching or dietary breadth and these processes are not explicitly represented within the DBEM. Instead, ecological flexibility is expressed in the DBEM primarily through distributional shifts as populations can redistribute towards grid cells where primary production remains favourable rather than through shifts in diet or trophic pathway. The absence of food web dynamics and changes in transfer efficiency at higher trophic levels in the DBEM suggests that projected catch declines for large pelagics may be conservative relative to what fully integrated trophic and food web dynamics would produce. In models that explicitly resolve trophic interactions, trophic plasticity may prove to be a more informative predictor of species sensitivity than trophic level alone. Thus, small and large pelagic species may face different climate risks. Small pelagics are directly sensitive to changes in primary production and catch outcomes depend on the direction of change, whereas larger pelagics face risks from sustained energy declines, slow recovery rates and trophic amplification pathways not fully captured by the current modelling framework.

The positive association between latitudinal breadth and sensitivity index (Table 1) runs counter to the assumption that broader ranges confer greater resilience and adaptive capacity to change. This result likely reflects an increased likelihood for wide‐ranging species to be exposed to high sensitivity environments, such as cold or temperate regions, rather than an inherent biological response. This implies that latitudinal breadth may have limited value as an independent predictor of a species' sensitivity and has broader implications for how distributional range is used in trait‐based climate vulnerability frameworks. Latitudinal breadth is commonly interpreted as a resilience proxy, on the assumption that wide‐ranging species can track suitable habitat as conditions change (Cheung et al. 2018; Jones and Cheung 2017). Our results suggest this interpretation may be incomplete when sensitivity to bottom‐up drivers is considered. A wide‐ranging species concentrated in cold, high‐sensitivity waters may face greater exposure to production variability than a narrow‐ranging species confined to lower sensitivity environments. Conditioning range‐based vulnerability metrics on the temperature and productivity characteristics of the occupied habitat, rather than treating range extent alone as a proxy for resilience, warrants attention in future trait‐based assessments.

Temperature as a Driver of Sensitivity Index

The strong, non‐linear relationship between SST and the sensitivity index indicates that sea surface temperature constrains how variability in primary production propagates to projections of maximum catch potential within the DBEM (Figure 4). The DBEM embeds physiological constraints through temperature‐dependent carrying capacity and growth formulations (Cheung et al. 2011), so the observed relationship here reflects both model structure and the ecological mechanisms the model is designed to represent.

Predicted species‐level sensitivity index (log10SI) across sea surface temperature (SST) gradients for small (< 30 cm), medium (30–89 cm) and large (≥ 90 cm) pelagic species, based on bootstrapped GAMM predictions. Shaded areas represent 95% confidence intervals.

As the sensitivity index is calculated from the ratio of the variability in projected change in maximum catch potential and the variability in projected change in primary production (Equation 1), part of the observed SST relationship could theoretically arise from temperature‐related variation in the denominator where smaller primary production variability would tend to increase the sensitivity index holding MCP variability constant, rather than reflecting differences in catch potential responses. Although expressing primary production as proportional change reduces the influence of differing baseline productivity, we found a consistent negative relationship between SST and the variability of projected relative primary production change across the five projection periods (r = −0.41 to −0.22), suggesting that warmer regions generally exhibited lower variability in projected change in primary production among simulations. Consequently, the described denominator effect would be expected to elevate SI in warmer waters, whereas the opposite pattern is observed (Figure 4). The decline in SI with increasing SST therefore cannot be explained solely by temperature‐related differences in projected primary production variability and instead indicates that variability in projected catch potential responds more strongly to temperature beyond what would be expected from denominator effects alone.

The reduced sensitivity index at elevated ocean temperatures is consistent with species operating near the upper bounds of the thermal performance curves encoded in the DBEM, where temperature‐driven changes in aerobic capacity and energy transfer efficiency can limit how effectively primary production is converted into growth and biomass (Brett 1971; Clark et al. 2011; Fry 1947; Pörtner and Farrell 2008). In this model context, when temperature conditions are already constraining performance, additional primary production cannot readily be converted into increased catch potential. In contrast, the disproportionate increase in sensitivity index at low temperatures suggests that in cold, productive waters, surplus energy beyond maintenance can be more readily channelled into somatic growth, such that even modest differences in primary production translate into pronounced variation in catch potential. In colder‐water ecosystems, primary production is highly seasonal and biomass production is more strongly dependent on primary production availability (Blanchard et al. 2012). Furthermore, trophic transfer efficiency is relatively higher in colder environments resulting in higher trophic amplification of productivity signals (Atkinson et al. 2024; Barneche et al. 2021; Lotze et al. 2019; Stock et al. 2017). Species operating at intermediate temperatures show a moderate and relatively stable sensitivity index, consistent with efficient aerobic performance and energy transfer under these conditions.

Empirical validation of these model‐derived temperature‐sensitivity patterns is an essential next step to extend these findings beyond the modelling context. The DBEM simplifies the complex and species‐specific relationships between temperature, aerobic scope and production efficiency. The patterns identified here generate testable predictions, specifically that the biomass responsiveness of pelagic fish populations to primary production variability should be highest in cold, high‐latitude systems and lowest in warm tropical systems that should be evaluated against observed time series of fish biomass and catch across contrasting thermal regimes. Such analyses would clarify the extent to which the modelled sensitivity index signal reflects real ecological dynamics rather than model structure alone.

The GAMM explained approximately 42% of the deviance, with substantial remaining variance attributed to the LME random effect (Table 3), indicating ocean temperature is not the only environmental factor shaping the sensitivity index. Aerobic scope is also restricted, both in reality and within the DBEM, by factors such as deoxygenation and acidification (Deutsch et al. 2015; Pörtner and Farrell 2008; Pörtner and Knust 2007), whether these factors contribute to the residual inter‐LME variance identified here remains to be explored.

Regional Differences and Implications for Fisheries

The heterogeneous spatial patterns of the sensitivity index across Large Marine Ecosystems create distinct regional risk profiles that carry practical implications for fisheries management analogous to frameworks that organise management responses according to the degree of uncertainty and the controllability of the system (Cheung 2018; Peterson et al. 2003). Interpreting these spatial patterns requires jointly considering three dimensions: the sensitivity index (the strength of coupling between primary production and catch potential), projection uncertainty (confidence in the direction and magnitude of future change) and the magnitude of the projected change.

Where sensitivity is high and projection uncertainty is low, the signal for risk is both strong and credible. In these systems, primary production variability is likely to amplify into maximum catch potential variability and the direction of that change is reasonably consistent across projections. The North Sea and Mediterranean Sea illustrate this case as both regions show relatively high sensitivity indices alongside comparatively low inter‐model variance in projected primary production change (Figure 5a,b,g,h). These regions are well documented in terms of climate‐driven fisheries impacts, with observed and projected declines in commercially important stocks partly attributed to warming and primary production shifts (Ben Lamine et al. 2023; Hidalgo et al. 2022). Proactive monitoring, dynamic harvest control rules and revision of reference points to reflect shifting productivity regimes are justified in these systems as the hazard is both amplifying and there is confidence in the direction of change (Boyce et al. 2023; Drexler et al. 2025; Woods et al. 2022).

In regions where sensitivity is high but projection uncertainty is also high, the nature of the risk is different. Species and regions in this category face large potential amplification of any primary production change, but the direction and magnitude of that change is not confident. The modelled responses in sub‐equatorial regions including the East Brazil Shelf, Caribbean Sea and Northern Australia fall into this category. Here the management challenge is one of preparedness rather than immediate adaptation. The high sensitivity implies that if the hazard materialises, the biological response will be pronounced, but precautionary commitment to a specific trajectory is premature. Scenario‐based planning and flexible frameworks that can accommodate a wide range of futures may be more appropriate than fixed reference point adjustment (Boyce et al. 2023; Tittensor et al. 2021).

Where sensitivity is low, the direct amplification pathway from primary production variability to catch potential variability is dampened regardless of projection uncertainty. However, as previously noted, low sensitivity to variability does not imply low vulnerability. Eastern Boundary Upwelling Systems, particularly the Humboldt and Benguela Currents, showed notably low sensitivity indices despite relatively cool waters (Figure 5a–d), likely because high baseline productivity buffers against variability as long as changes remain above the threshold required to sustain biomass production. Despite covering less than 1% of the global ocean, upwelling systems support up to 20% of the world's capture fisheries (Du et al. 2024; Pauly and Christensen 1995) and the history of dramatic anchovy and sardine boom‐bust cycles demonstrates that apparent buffering can give way to rapid, large‐magnitude state changes when productivity thresholds are crossed or unfavourable environmental conditions coincide with fishing pressure (Chavez et al. 2003).

These contrasting regional patterns provide indirect evidence for the role of the underlying production regime as LMEs are defined in part by characteristic productivity and oceanographic conditions such as upwelling, spring blooms and tropical systems. The strong and consistent LME random effect (Table 3) provides some evidence that the production regime contributes to the spatial variation in sensitivity, although the LME identity also captures a broader range of environmental and ecological differences. The DBEM relies on annually averaged primary production forcing which precludes explicit representation of seasonal dynamics. Disentangling seasonal production effects would require a finer temporal resolution of production forcing than the current framework provides.

These considerations underscore that the regional risk profiles identified here should be interpreted within the constraints of the global modelling framework. The DBEM is appropriate for identifying broad‐scale patterns and cross‐system comparisons but cannot resolve local drivers that may dominate catch potential variability within a specific fishery or management unit. The sensitivity index and regional risk signals presented here are therefore most useful as a screening tool for identifying where the relationship between primary production and fish catch potential warrants closer attention, rather than as direct inputs to operational management. Translating these global patterns into locally actionable guidance will require region‐specific analyses that integrate finer‐scale oceanographic forcing, local ecological dynamics and the socioeconomic context of the fisheries in question.

Limitations and Future Directions

The results presented here should be interpreted as simulation‐based indicators of sensitivity to primary production change within a single modelling framework and thus warrant consideration of several study limitations.

The most significant structural constraint is reliance on a single global fisheries model (the DBEM). While the controlled forcing design of holding physical conditions constant and varying only primary production is a deliberate methodological strength that isolates the bottom‐up pathway, all sensitivity patterns identified reflect how the DBEM mechanistically represents primary production constraints, temperature‐dependent physiology and life history dynamics. Similarly, the DBEM's annual forcing resolution limits its ability to resolve seasonal production dynamics. The DBEM has been observed to show stronger non‐linear cumulative impacts of climate change in comparison to other global models (Heneghan et al. 2021), so extending this analysis across the ensemble of fisheries and marine ecosystem models participating in the Fisheries and Marine Ecosystem Model Intercomparison Project (Blanchard et al. 2024; Tittensor et al. 2018) would test whether the trait‐based and temperature‐mediated sensitivity signals identified here are robust across alternative model architectures.

While the experimental design used here was intentionally selected to isolate the propagation of variability in projected primary production through the DBEM, alternative experimental designs that are beyond the scope of this study could address complementary questions. For example, a fully factorial experiment that independently manipulates temperature and primary production could provide a more controlled test of temperature's modifying response to a given magnitude of primary production change. Such experiments could allow direct temperature effects to be separated from primary production effects and provide a useful extension for testing temperature‐primary production interactions. Work related to this has been conducted by Heneghan et al. (2021). Similarly, because species are not randomly distributed across thermal environments, disentangling species‐specific from region‐specific contributions to the SST‐sensitivity relationship remains an important target for future work. This could be done through a dedicated single‐species simulation experiment or a formal within versus between species decomposition of the temperature effect.

The two PISCES configurations used here differ in their underlying biogeochemical parameterisations, providing meaningful structural differences in the primary production projections (Bopp et al. 2022). Nevertheless, both versions share a common model lineage and are forced by a single Earth system model (IPSL‐CM6A‐LR). Expanding to a broader multi‐model ensemble of ESM outputs would build greater confidence in the sensitivity estimates and their regional patterns, particularly for regions where IPSL‐CM6A‐LR may have known biases in primary production projections.

Ecologically, the DBEM captures physiological and bioenergetic constraints but omits higher‐trophic interactions, predator–prey dynamics and community‐level responses that could substantially modify sensitivity signals. The absence of trophic amplification pathways in the model means that projected sensitivity for large‐bodied, high‐trophic‐level species is likely conservative. Incorporating additional environmental stressors known to constrain aerobic scope, particularly deoxygenation and ocean acidification, would allow assessment of whether these drivers produce sensitivity patterns analogous to, or distinct from, the SST relationship identified here and could account for a portion of the residual inter‐LME variance identified in the GAMM. An additional consideration is trophic plasticity. Species capable of exploiting a broader range of prey resources or range of trophic pathways may be more resilient to variability in primary production than species occupying similar trophic positions but exhibiting greater dietary specialisation. The DBEM does not represent species flexibility in feeding behaviour or diet and so trophic plasticity was not expected to meaningfully explain additional variation in the sensitivity index. However, the role of trophic plasticity in sensitivity ought to be explored in fisheries and marine ecosystem models that explicitly simulate trophic interactions and food‐web dynamics.

Finally, the mechanistic relationships identified here generate testable predictions that should be evaluated against observed time series of fish biomass and catch across contrasting thermal regimes. Such validation would strengthen the ecological credibility of model‐derived sensitivity indices and clarify the extent to which simulation‐based vulnerability signals translate to real‐world fisheries outcomes.

Conclusion

These findings demonstrate that pelagic fish sensitivity to primary production changes, as projected by the DBEM under a controlled forcing design, is not a fixed biological property but emerges from species traits and SST. Body size provides a biologically meaningful predictor of sensitivity index, with smaller‐bodied species more tightly coupled to primary production variability through direct trophic linkages and faster life histories. However, biological traits alone explain only a small fraction of variance in the sensitivity index, and SST explains substantially more, acting as a dominant environmental control on how primary production signals are amplified or dampened before reaching fish catch potential within the model.

This has direct consequences for how vulnerability is assessed and communicated. Trait‐only frameworks may risk systematically misclassifying species in thermally extreme environments, and in particular may underestimate the disproportionate exposure of high‐latitude fisheries to future production change. The heterogeneous regional risk profiles identified here that combine sensitivity, projection uncertainty and magnitude of long‐term change further highlight that climate risk in pelagic fisheries cannot be captured by a single dimension of vulnerability. Moving towards integrated frameworks that couple biological traits with environmental context, account for modelling uncertainty and distinguish between short‐term responsiveness and long‐term exposure will be essential for building climate‐resilient fisheries management strategies. Empirical validation of these model‐derived sensitivity patterns across contrasting thermal regimes remains a key priority.
