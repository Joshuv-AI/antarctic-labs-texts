Isolated bilateral breast myeloid sarcoma: A rare case report
Authors: Abdellah Seghiri, Samia Sabri, Zahida Aqodad, Ikram Sadki, Houda Bachir, Siham Hamaz, Habiba Alaoui, Khalid Serraj
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMyeloid sarcoma (MS) is a rare extramedullary tumor composed of immature myeloid cells that is most associated with acute myeloid leukemia (AML), whereas isolated bilateral breast involvement without bone marrow disease is exceptionally uncommon. We report the case of a 38-year-old woman who presented with bilateral breast masses, both classified as BI-RADS 5 on imaging. Ultrasound-guided core needle biopsies of both breast lesions established the diagnosis of myeloid sarcoma, with immunohistochemical positivity for MPO, CD34, CD56, and CD117. Peripheral blood and bone marrow examinations showed no evidence of AML, and FDG PET/CT confirmed isolated extramedullary disease, providing accurate staging and monitoring treatment response. The patient received AML-type induction chemotherapy followed by salvage FLAG-IDA chemotherapy for residual disease and consolidative radiotherapy, achieving complete metabolic remission that was maintained at 6-month follow-up. This case highlights the diagnostic challenges of isolated bilateral breast myeloid sarcoma and emphasizes that histopathological confirmation with immunohistochemistry, complemented by FDG PET/CT, is important for accurate diagnosis, staging, treatment assessment, and multidisciplinary management.Keywords: Myeloid sarcoma, Bilateral breast involvement, FDG PET/CT, AML-type chemotherapy

Introduction

Myeloid sarcoma (MS) is a rare malignant tumor composed of immature myeloid cells that results in extramedullary tissue involvement [1]. It represents an extramedullary manifestation of acute myeloid leukemia (AML) or, less frequently, other myelodysplastic or myeloproliferative disorders [1]. According to the World Health Organization (WHO) classification of hematologic malignancies, MS is recognized as a distinct clinicopathologic entity characterized by infiltration of tissues by myeloid blasts, leading to disruption of normal architecture and frequently mimicking nonhematologic malignancies [2]. It occurs in approximately 2.5%-9% of AML patients with concomitant bone marrow involvement [3]. In contrast, isolated myeloid sarcoma, defined as an extramedullary myeloid tumor without detectable bone marrow disease, is extremely rare, accounting for fewer than 1% of AML cases or approximately 2 cases per million adults [4].

MS can involve virtually any organ, with the most frequently affected sites including the skin, lymph nodes, bone, soft tissues, central nervous system, and genital tract [5]. Breast involvement is particularly uncommon and accounts for approximately 8% of reported cases of myeloid sarcoma [6]. The clinical presentation of breast myeloid sarcoma is usually nonspecific, particularly in patients without a previous history of hematologic malignancy. Although breast imaging plays an important role in the initial evaluation, mammographic and ultrasonographic findings may overlap with those of primary breast carcinoma, lymphoma, or other malignant lesions, making radiological differentiation challenging. Therefore, histopathological examination combined with immunohistochemical analysis is important for establishing an accurate diagnosis [6].

We report a rare case of isolated bilateral breast myeloid sarcoma in a 38-year-old woman without bone marrow involvement. This case highlights the clinical, radiological, and pathological features of this unusual entity, with particular emphasis on the role of immunohistochemistry and FDG PET/CT in diagnosis, disease staging, and treatment response assessment.

Case presentation

A 38-year-old woman with no significant medical history was referred for evaluation of a progressively enlarging right breast mass that had developed over a 3-month period.

Physical examination revealed a patient in good general condition with an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status of 0. A firm, nontender mass measuring approximately 4 cm was palpated in the lower outer quadrant of the right breast, associated with ipsilateral axillary lymphadenopathy. No skin ulceration or nipple retraction was observed. Examination of the left breast revealed a painful retroareolar palpable nodule (Fig. 1).

Clinical photograph showing a palpable mass in the lower outer quadrant of the right breast at initial presentation.

Mammography demonstrated a dense irregular mass in the right breast with indistinct margins and associated skin thickening, classified as BI-RADS 5, indicating a highly suspicious lesion. The left breast showed 3 additional dense irregular masses, also classified as BI-RADS 5. Breast ultrasound confirmed a large heterogeneous hypoechoic mass in the right breast, measuring 4.8 × 4.7 cm, with irregular margins, posterior acoustic attenuation, and internal vascularity on Doppler imaging. Enlarged right axillary lymph nodes were identified, the largest measuring 3.1 × 1.5 cm. In the left breast, ultrasound revealed 3 irregular heterogeneous hypoechoic nodules with posterior attenuation and internal vascularity, the largest measuring 1.5 × 1.3 cm, associated with ipsilateral axillary lymphadenopathy (Fig. 2). These imaging findings were considered highly suspicious for malignant breast lesions and prompted bilateral core needle biopsies.

Mammographic findings at initial presentation. (A) Right mediolateral oblique (RMLO) view showing a dense irregular mass with indistinct margins and associated skin thickening in the right breast, classified as BI-RADS 5. (B) Left mediolateral oblique (LMLO) view demonstrating multiple dense irregular masses in the left breast, also classified as BI-RADS 5.

Histopathological examination of both breast lesions demonstrated diffuse infiltration by atypical mononuclear cells arranged in sheets, with complete effacement of the normal mammary architecture. Immunohistochemical analysis revealed positivity for myeloperoxidase (MPO), CD34, CD56, and CD117, with negativity for cytokeratin and CD3. The Ki-67 proliferation index was approximately 30%. Fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) identified a KMT2A::MLLT10 fusion. These findings confirmed the diagnosis of bilateral breast myeloid sarcoma (Fig. 3).

Histopathology of the right breast mass: Hematoxylin and eosin staining (HES × 10; IHC × 20) shows diffuse infiltration of atypical mononuclear cells (A). Immunohistochemical analysis demonstrates positivity for myeloperoxidase (MPO), with black arrows indicating MPO-positive blasts (B), negativity for cytokeratin (CK) (C), and a Ki-67 proliferation index of approximately 30%, with white arrows highlighting Ki-67–positive cells (D).

Complete blood count was within normal limits, and peripheral blood smear examination showed no circulating blasts. Bone marrow aspiration revealed normocellular marrow with preserved trilineage hematopoiesis and no evidence of leukemic infiltration. Cytogenetic analysis showed a normal karyotype. Whole-body CT demonstrated no additional sites of disease, confirming isolated extramedullary myeloid sarcoma.

FDG PET/CT was performed for whole-body staging and baseline assessment of disease extent. The patient received induction chemotherapy according to the standard AML-type “7+3” regimen, consisting of continuous infusion cytarabine (200 mg/m2 for 7 days) combined with daunorubicin (60 mg/m2 for 3 days). Postinduction FDG PET/CT demonstrated persistent metabolically active disease, including a right breast soft-tissue mass measuring 40 × 43 × 44 mm (SUVmax 6.87) extending to the overlying skin, a left breast nodule measuring 17 × 16 mm (SUVmax 3.62), and hypermetabolic right axillary and subpectoral lymph nodes measuring up to 15 × 11 mm (SUVmax 4.43), indicating an incomplete response to induction therapy (Fig. 4).

FDG PET/CT performed after induction chemotherapy (7 + 3 regimen) for treatment response assessment. Coronal (A), sagittal (B), axial (C), and coronal (D) images demonstrate persistent hypermetabolic activity in the right breast lesion (white arrows) and right axillary lymph nodes (blue arrows), consistent with residual active disease.

Given the persistence of active disease, salvage chemotherapy with the FLAG-IDA regimen was administered, including fludarabine (48 mg/m2 for 5 days), cytarabine (2 g/m2 for 5 days), and idarubicin (10 mg/m2 for 3 days). Follow-up FDG PET/CT showed a significant morphologic and metabolic response, with regression of the right breast mass to 25 × 14 × 21 mm and decreased FDG uptake (SUVmax 3.78). The left breast lesion achieved complete morpho-metabolic remission, and right axillary and subpectoral lymph nodes demonstrated complete metabolic response with only small residual inactive nodes (19 × 14 mm) (Fig. 5).

FDG PET/CT performed after salvage chemotherapy with FLAG-IDA. Images demonstrate marked regression of the right breast lesion with decreased metabolic activity and complete metabolic response of the left breast lesion and right axillary lymph nodes, indicating a favorable treatment response.

Allogeneic hematopoietic stem-cell transplantation was considered; however, it was not performed due to the absence of an HLA-matched donor. Consolidative radiotherapy was subsequently delivered to the right breast and axillary region (24 Gy in 12 fractions).

At 9-month follow-up, the patient remained in complete metabolic remission, with no clinical or radiological evidence of disease relapse (Fig. 6).

Clinical photograph obtained at 6-month follow-up showing complete clinical remission of the breast masses and resolution of the previous axillary findings, with a stable clinical status maintained up to 9 months.

Discussion

Myeloid sarcoma (MS) is an extramedullary neoplasm composed of immature myeloid cells and represents an uncommon manifestation within the spectrum of acute myeloid leukemia (AML) [1]. MS occurs in approximately 2.5%-9% of AML patients and is more frequently associated with FAB subtypes M4 (acute myelomonocytic leukemia) and M5 (acute monoblastic/monocytic leukemia) [4,5,7]. In contrast, isolated MS without bone marrow involvement is rare, accounting for fewer than 1% of AML cases at diagnosis, with an estimated incidence of approximately 2 cases per million adults [4,5]. The most frequently affected sites include the skin, soft tissues, lymph nodes, and reticuloendothelial system, whereas gastrointestinal and central nervous system involvement are less common [4]. Breast involvement is exceptionally rare and represents a diagnostic challenge because its clinical and imaging features are often nonspecific and may mimic primary breast carcinoma or lymphoma [8]. In our patient, the absence of bone marrow involvement combined with bilateral breast localization represents an unusual presentation of isolated extramedullary MS.

The molecular mechanisms underlying extramedullary involvement in AML remain incompletely understood. Several genetic alterations have been associated with increased leukemic cell migration and tissue infiltration; however, their precise contribution to the development of isolated myeloid sarcoma remains unclear [9,10]. In our patient, FISH analysis identified a KMT2A::MLLT10 fusion, an uncommon molecular abnormality reported in myeloid neoplasms and associated with an increased risk of relapse and aggressive disease behavior [11]. This finding supports the need for careful long-term clinical and radiological surveillance, particularly given the absence of bone marrow involvement at diagnosis.

The diagnosis of breast myeloid sarcoma remains challenging because its clinical and radiological features may overlap with other malignant breast lesions, particularly primary carcinoma and lymphoma. Histopathological examination combined with immunohistochemistry is therefore important for establishing the diagnosis. Myeloid sarcoma typically expresses myeloid markers such as myeloperoxidase (MPO), CD34, CD117, and other hematopoietic markers, although the immunophenotypic profile may vary among cases [12,13]. Fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography-computed tomography (FDG PET/CT) plays an important role in the evaluation of myeloid sarcoma by providing whole-body staging, assessing the extent of extramedullary involvement, and monitoring treatment response [13,14]. In our patient, FDG PET/CT confirmed the absence of additional disease sites, demonstrated residual metabolic activity after induction chemotherapy, and subsequently documented a favorable response after salvage therapy, supporting treatment evaluation and follow-up.

First-line management of MS generally follows AML-type treatment protocols, most commonly consisting of cytarabine combined with an anthracycline, whereas fludarabine-based regimens may be considered in selected cases [13]. Radiotherapy may be used as consolidation therapy or for persistent localized disease after systemic treatment [13]. Allogeneic hematopoietic stem-cell transplantation may be considered in selected high-risk patients, particularly those achieving remission [13,15]. In our patient, AML-type induction chemotherapy using the 7+3 regimen was initially administered; however, FDG PET/CT demonstrated persistent metabolic activity, indicating residual disease. Salvage therapy with the FLAG-IDA regimen resulted in a significant metabolic and morphological response, followed by consolidative radiotherapy to the right breast and axillary region. Allogeneic hematopoietic stem-cell transplantation was considered but could not be performed because of the absence of an HLA-matched donor.

The prognosis of isolated myeloid sarcoma remains variable, and long-term follow-up is required because of the risk of local recurrence, systemic AML progression, or relapse despite initial response to therapy [13]. Molecular abnormalities may contribute to risk stratification; however, their prognostic impact in isolated breast myeloid sarcoma remains incompletely defined. In our patient, FISH analysis identified a KMT2A::MLLT10 fusion, an uncommon genetic alteration reported in myeloid neoplasms and associated with aggressive disease behavior and an increased risk of relapse [11]. This finding supports the need for close clinical and radiological surveillance. The patient remained in complete metabolic remission at 9 months of follow-up, although longer observation is required to better assess long-term outcomes.

Conclusion

Isolated bilateral breast myeloid sarcoma is an extremely rare manifestation of extramedullary AML and represents a diagnostic challenge due to its nonspecific clinical and imaging features. Accurate diagnosis relies on histopathological examination combined with immunohistochemistry, as breast imaging findings may overlap with primary breast carcinoma or lymphoma. FDG PET/CT provides valuable information for disease staging and treatment response assessment. This case highlights the importance of considering myeloid sarcoma in the differential diagnosis of suspicious breast lesions and emphasizes the contribution of a multidisciplinary approach, including systemic therapy and consolidative radiotherapy, in the management of this rare entity.
