Human-AI Teaming Using Large Language Models: Boosting Brain-Computer Interfacing (BCI) and Brain Research
Maryna Kapitonova, Tonio Ball

Abstract
Recently, there is an increasing interest in using artificial intelligence (AI) to automate aspects of the research process, or even autonomously conduct the full research cycle from idea generation, over data analysis, to composing and evaluation of scientific manuscripts. Examples of working “AI scientist” systems have been demonstrated for computer science tasks and running molecular biology labs. While some approaches aim for full autonomy of the scientific AI, others rather aim for leveraging human-AI teaming. Here, we address how to adapt such approaches for boosting Brain-Computer Interface (BCI) development, as well as brain research resp. neuroscience at large. We argue that at this time, a strong emphasis on human-AI teaming, in contrast to fully autonomous “AI BCI researcher,” will be the most promising way forward. We introduce the collaborative workspaces concept for human-AI teaming based on a set of Janusian design principles, looking both ways, to the human as well as to the AI side. Based on these principles, we present ChatBCI, a Python-based toolbox for enabling human-AI collaboration based on interaction with Large Language Models (LLMs), designed for BCI research and development projects. We show how ChatBCI was successfully used in a concrete BCI project on advancing motor imagery decoding from EEG signals. Our approach can be straightforwardly extended to broad neurotechnological and neuroscientific topics, and may by design facilitate human expert knowledge transfer to scientific AI systems in general.

Introduction

Human–Artificial Intelligence (AI) collaborations may play an important role in promoting scientific discovery
Wang et al. 2023
. Efforts to automate research and development tasks have recently led to the creation of
“AI scientists”—AI agents specialized to autonomously carry out various roles in the scientific process.
AI scientists may run virtual or even physical labs (self-driving labs, SDLs
Rapp et al. 2024
) and thus help to
“super-charge”
fields including biomedical research, material science, or computer science.

For example, in an effort to leverage AI for biomedical research, Swanson and colleagues have recently created a virtual laboratory that brings together multiple AI scientists—implemented as large language models (LLMs) with specialized roles—to collaborate autonomously on objectives defined by human investigators. This system rapidly proposed nearly 100 designs for antibody fragments, known as nanobodies, capable of binding to the virus responsible for COVID-19—accomplishing in a short span what might otherwise take a purely human team much longer
Swanson et al. 2024
. In the field of computer science, Lu and colleagues introduced an AI scientist system that aims to automate the complete research cycle. They also use a LLM to survey scientific literature, formulate hypotheses, test them by running experiments, and then compile the results into a written paper. The system goes a step further by performing its own
‘peer review,’
evaluating and refining the outputs it generates
Lu et al. 2024
. However, despite these impressive first demonstrations, substantial challenges remain for AI-driven scientific discovery in general, as well as for the adaptation of AI-scientists to the unique requirements that the scientific domains of research and development may impose on such a system.

For AI-scientist systems (AISS) in general, for example, one major challenge is how to integrate the vast amount of implicit, unstructured, and often context-dependent knowledge and expertise that human scientists rely on for experimental design, interpretation, and innovation—which may not be sufficiently represented in the available training data. For example, Rihm and colleagues argue that current AI-driven labs are only able to incorporate explicit knowledge such as recorded data, scientific documentation, and publications and hence only see the
“tip of the iceberg”
of the relevant knowledge
Rihm et al. 2024
.

However, even explicit knowledge such as that expressed in scientific publications may hold problems of its own when used as training data for scientific AI systems. Geoffrey Boulton and Moumita Koley observed in a contribution for the International Science Council:
“Many scientific journals and papers maintain high standards, too many lack proper editorial oversight, many lack rigour and integrity, some engage in fraudulent practices, […], and agreed standards for overall governance of the process are lacking.”

International Science Council 2023
. All of these point might compromise training data quality. This situation, together with the recent
“publishing explosion”
(47% growth between 2016 and 2022 in the global number of published papers
Hanson et al. 2024
; nearly 250% increase in the number of Google Scholar hits for the term
“Brain-Computer Interface”
in the same time interval) which may reflect a overemphasis of quantity compared to quality, may result in quality issues with respect to the available training data for scientific AI systems. Such problems may be more or less serious in some research fields compared to others
Bakker et al. 2012
.

Thus, in addition to general challenges such as those related to training data availability and quality, adaptation of scientific AI to a specific research field may hold its own challenges—not only due to differences in research standards. AI systems may face vastly different requirements depending, for example, on the role of experimental work, the complexity of the relevant mathematical tools and concepts, or the degree of adoption of open science practices with resulting differences in public data and scientific software access. For example, adoption of scientific AI agents in subfields of machine learning such as diffusion modeling, transformer-based language modeling, and learning dynamics
Lu et al. 2024
benefits from publicly accessible and well-documented datasets of, for many relevant questions, adequate size and quality, as well as from well-established benchmarks. In contrast, fields such as EEG-based BCI research face much larger problems of small and diverse datasets of variable data quality. Additional domain-specific challenges arise due to the complexities of the foundation of EEG signals in multiscale brain dynamics and their relation to human cognition, emotion, and behavior
Friston 2005
.

Against this background, our objective in the present work is to adopt AI scientific agents to the field of human brain research and Brain-Computer Interfacing (BCI) Figure
1
. To address the general as well as the domain-specific challenges of this task, we introduce a concept for human-AI workspaces designed to foster scientific human-AI co-learning and collaboration, summarized in our
Janusian Design Principles.
As an instance of this design concept, we present the LLM-based ChatBCI toolbox.

We show how ChatBCI enabled a human-AI team to perform a prototypical scientific BCI project based on a public EEG dataset in a very time-efficient and productive manner, addressing the full cycle of project phases from data import, exploration, and validation, over machine learning model selection and implementation (a deep convolutional neural network as the LLM-generated brain signal decoder), setting up a training loop with integrated data augmentation, to the joint interpretation of the obtained results. We illustrate how our structured interaction framework flexibly supports different levels of agent autonomy and demonstrate how it allows transfer of expert knowledge about non-obvious, non-trivial aspects of the EEG dataset used, from the human expert to the ChatBCI AI agent. This, in turn, proved a valuable teaching and training partner for the human side. Thus, we achieved meaningful human-AI co-learning together with substantially faster collaborative completion of our BCI research task than would have been possible with a purely human approach.
Our approach holds great potential for teaching and training in the field of human brain research and BCI development. LLM-generated brain signal decoders also present a novel class of AutoML for brain signal analysis. The
“AI brain researcher”
or
“AI BCI researcher”
presented here is an instance of an
“AI worker”

It will be interesting to study how abilities learned to handle the complexities of the human brain, and of the signals that can be measured from it, may also be helpful for AI workers in other domains. We anticipate that collaborative human-AI research and development in the field of brain research and BCI, as enabled by our design concept and ChatBCI toolbox, will pave the way toward AI systems with a deep and holistic understanding of human brain function - a
"brain-grokking AI"
- with a far-reaching application potential.

Working version of the seven Principles of Janusian Design

1.

Speaking the Same Language:

Janusian workspaces foster the development of a joint collaboration language, minimizing misunderstanding and confusion. This enables AI systems to act as intuitive partners, aligning with human intentions and promoting seamless interactions. To achieve this, the workspace leverages intuitive interfaces, contextual feedback, and iterative refinement, ensuring that communication between humans and AI becomes progressively more natural and effective.

2.

Transparency and Trust:

Trust requires mutual clarity and predictability. Explainability is a cornerstone, allowing users to progressively build confidence in AI systems by understanding their decision-making processes. The framework incorporates
bidirectional transparency
, encouraging not only explainable AI (XAI) methods, but also mechanisms for AI systems to ask clarifying questions when encountering ambiguous inputs or tasks. This fosters a collaborative dynamic where both human and AI agents seek to represent/understand each other’s intentions, enhancing mutual trust and reducing the likelihood of errors.

3.

Building a Shared Knowledge Base:

The workspace amplifies human and AI collaboration by creating and maintaining a shared knowledge base. This repository encodes specialized expert knowledge and evolves iteratively. The
Janusian Wiki (Jiki)
serves as the foundation for this knowledge base, providing a structured, centralized system for documenting workflows, best practices, and domain-specific knowledge. By linking tasks, tools, and insights, the Jiki ensures that both human collaborators and AI systems have access to the collective intelligence of the workspace, facilitating informed decision-making and continuous improvement.

4.

Joint Integration of Priorities:

The Janusian framework acknowledges that human-AI teams must balance and integrate priorities jointly, addressing trade-offs such as the need for immediate actions versus long-term objectives, or reconciling priorities driven by strategic considerations or overarching research questions ("top-down") with reactive, task-specific responses ("bottom-up").

5.

Adaptive Autonomy:

AI systems adjust their level of autonomy based on task demands, enabling granular human supervision where needed while offering higher degrees of independence for routine or low-stakes tasks. For example, in the ChatBCI toolbox, the adaptive autonomy principle is exemplified through
Collaborative Test-Driven Development (TDD)
, where the AI autonomously generates test cases and mock data for routine validations, while human oversight is applied to critical or ambiguous scenarios. This flexibility ensures that the level of autonomy dynamically aligns with the complexity and importance of the task.

6.

Accessibility from Novice to Expert:

The workspace aims to balance accessibility for novice users, such as those in teaching and training scenarios, with maintaining advanced functionality tailored for expert users. It also differentiates how information from these scenarios is processed and utilized on the AI side, ensuring that insights from both novice interactions and expert workflows are effectively integrated. This inclusive design promotes efficiency and reduces the supervision workload, enabling seamless collaboration across varying levels of expertise.

7.

Continuous Evolution:

A shared Janusian workspace is not static, but designed to foster mutual co-learning between humans and AI. This principle ensures that the ChatBCI toolbox or other workspaces based by-design on this concept not only supports effective and scalable data analysis but also fosters a dynamic workspace where both human and AI partners can co-evolve to achieve aligned goals.

In summary, the Janusian framework establishes a synergistic foundation for human-AI collaboration by aligning intuitive interfaces, transparent processes, and adaptive autonomy with evolving user needs. In the following, we illustrate how the ChatBCI toolbox based on these design ideas can be used in a concrete project example.

3.1
Idea generation

The research ideas presented in Table
1
were generated using GPT-4o, as our underlying LLM. The model was provided with a detailed prompt to explore advancements in EEG-based motor imagery classification within the context of Brain-Computer Interfaces (BCIs) using the BCI Competition IV 2a dataset
Brunner et al. 2008

"MNIST of EEG"

The prompt included instructions to identify key challenges, research gaps, motivations, and potential approaches across a range of topics, including domain adaptation, lightweight architectures, temporal dynamics, and explainability. GPT-4o synthesized the list of ideas by leveraging its training on a wide range of scientific and technical texts; notably here we did not fine-tune on more specific literature. Overall, this demonstrates the utility of LLMs in assisting researchers by rapidly generating diverse, structured, and actionable ideas that may have merit in guiding future investigations. However, it was also observable that many of the proposed topics were under-specified and lacked exact knowledge of what has already been done and reported in the relevant literature. For example, the suggestion was to explore lightweight neural networks that achieve state-of-the-art accuracy while being optimized for low-power devices, which is a promising topic, but one that is already well-trodden in the field. Studies like the widely known EEGNet paper
Lawhern et al. 2018

Therefore, while this idea aligns with key goals in the field, it lacks specificity in identifying novel directions. For instance, future advancements might focus on extending lightweight models to handle more diverse datasets, integrating adaptive techniques that dynamically scale complexity based on resource availability, or innovating in model interpretability for constrained devices. Without domain-specific fine-tuning, the model had also difficulties in retrieving the published SoA in decoding accuracies on the BCI Competition IV 2a dataset. While a re-implementation of the FBCSP winning model of the original BCI Competition IV yielded 67.8% accuracy across the 4 motor imagery classes, and convnets allowed for a gain of approx. 4%

On this background, we decided for the following, human-specified topic for the first ChatBCI project:
What do the close to 100% decoding accuracies reported on the BCI Competition IV 2a dataset mean?
To which extent do they reflect sophistication of the brain signal decoding methodology, or possibly also over-fitting to this (relatively small) dataset, and/or contributions from non-brain signals (artifacts which may contain class-specific information and might be easier to decode than brain signals)? The BCI Competition IV 2a data set is particularly interesting in this context, as it was part of the original competition instructions that since three EOG channels were provided, it was
"required to remove EOG

Perform an exploratory data analysis of the BCI Competition IV 2a data set

3.2.1
Exploratory data analysis and data validation

Analysis for data exploration and validation let to several interesting observations. ChatBCI suggested initially three starting steps for data validation and exploration.
First
, examine the basic signal statistics to understand the distribution and variability of the EEG data across classes. This involves calculating the mean, standard deviation, and variance for each channel and class, identifying systematic differences in signal variance, and detecting outliers, which may indicate artefacts.
Second
, analyze event-related potentials (ERPs) to investigate time-locked neural activity by segmenting the data around key events, calculating average ERP waveforms for each class, and identifying distinctive features such as P300 peaks while ensuring the observed differences are neural rather than artefact-related.
Third
, explore class-specific differences in the frequency domain by computing power spectral density (PSD) for each class and channel, identifying frequency bands where power varies, and detecting potential artefacts such as line noise or muscle activity.

3.2.2
LLM-generated deep neural network decoder and training loop

Not surprisingly, given the well-documented abilities of LLMs in code generation (
Rozière et al. 2024
), ChatBCI was able to design and implement both deep networks for EEG decoding and working training loops, including plausibly motivated data-augmentation strategies. Figure
5
shows results obtained with a simple network consisting of a sequence of spatial and temporal convolutions, using batch normalization, dropout, and a SWISH activation function, with within-subject training using the original competition data split.

Results showed clear learning on the validation set across all subjects, reaching accuracies, on the one hand, clearly above chance, and on the other hand, also substantially below previous reports
Zhang et al. 2021
. We consider our simple models as a good starting point for, in a next step, implementing interpretability tools, and gradually moving to more complex decoding models. Setting up the network as well as the training loop used in this example, in total required less than 10 prompts.

Another next logical step would be the implementation of hyperparameter optimization and neural architecture search functionality in ChatBCI, for advancing to models with SoA accuracy or beyond. Note that this would potentially enable novel approaches for AutoML leveraging LLMs, without the necessity for expert knowledge of underlying AutoML libraries and toolboxes. We foresee that ChatBCI would also be particularly useful in BCI decoding benchmarking tasks, which require structured large-scale approaches which may especially align with the specific strengths of artificial compared to human intelligence.

Conclusions

This paper introduced the ChatBCI toolbox as a practical and innovative solution to foster human-AI collaboration in the fields of Brain-Computer Interface (BCI) research and brain signal analysis. By adopting our
Janusian Design Principles
, we demonstrated how human-AI teaming can address challenges in BCI development, such as small datasets, complex signal dynamics, and knowledge transfer. Specifically:

1.

Collaborative Human-AI Workspaces:

The ChatBCI framework was built to integrate the strengths of both human expertise and AI capabilities, providing an adaptable, interactive workspace that supports all phases of scientific research, from idea generation to experimental analysis and result interpretation.

2.

Application to Motor Imagery Decoding:

Using the ChatBCI toolbox, we explored the BCI Competition IV 2a dataset and implemented a deep learning decoder in a collaborative, time-efficient manner. This process highlighted the utility of ChatBCI for accelerating workflows, identifying domain-specific challenges, and fostering human-AI co-learning.

3.

Transfer of Expertise and Knowledge:

The structured interaction framework enabled effective transfer of non-obvious, domain-specific knowledge from human experts to the AI, ensuring meaningful co-learning. The AI, in turn, proved to be a valuable partner, suggesting analyses, generating code, and adapting to user inputs.

4.

Scalable Design for Broader Neurotechnological Applications:

While demonstrated for a specific EEG dataset, the principles and methods behind ChatBCI are extensible to a wide range of neuroscience and neurotechnology challenges, laying a foundation for future advancements in brain research and AI-driven discovery.

By merging the adaptability of AI systems with the nuanced understanding of human researchers, ChatBCI represents a step forward in achieving efficient and collaborative research processes. Our findings indicate that human-AI teaming, as enabled by ChatBCI, holds significant promise for accelerating scientific discovery while maintaining the interpretability and trust essential for impactful advancements in neuroscience and BCI.

We envision a future where in some scenarios brain-computer interfaces (BCIs) transcend traditional keyboard inputs, replacing them with direct interaction through physiological signals—a transformative goal of BCI research and development. For instance, advances like Meta’s silent speech decoding demonstrate the potential of such technologies to enable seamless communication between humans and machines
Défossez et al. 2023
. This innovation aligns perfectly with the evolving paradigm of human-AI collaboration, where neural interfacing could ensure seamless interaction and integration of human-AI teams. By integrating robust privacy and security measures (
Kapitonova et al. 2022
), such systems could enable richer societal interactions and more immersive experiences in scientific discovery, healthcare, and beyond. The convergence of neurotechnology and AI promises not only to amplify human cognitive capabilities but also to unlock new paradigms for how humans and machines collaborate to solve complex problems.

Through the type of collaborative teamwork on brain data enabled by ChatBCI, AI systems may progressively develop a deep and holistic understanding of human brain function. By engaging with the complexities of brain signals, their multiscale dynamics, and their connections to cognition, behavior, and emotion, such AI systems may transcend conventional analytical approaches. This emerging paradigm has potential not only in neuroscience and neurotechnology but also fields such as medicine, psychology, and education. Furthermore, a
"brain-grokking AI"
could unlock significant performance advantages in areas that rely on human brain function, including optimized mental health interventions and tailored cognitive training, underscoring the importance of human-AI collaboration as a cornerstone for advancing knowledge and unlocking new possibilities in science and technology.
