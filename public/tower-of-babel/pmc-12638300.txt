Design and evaluation of octopromycin-derived peptides as multifunctional antimicrobial agents against multidrug-resistant pathogens
Authors: Byambasuren Ganbaatar, Sukumar Dinesh Kumar, Eun Young Kim, Naveen Kumar Radhakrishnan, Sungtae Yang, Song Yub Shin, Chul Won Lee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Octopromycin (OPM), an antimicrobial peptide from Octopus minor, shows potent activity against multidrug-resistant (MDR) Acinetobacter baumannii. Guided by the structure–activity relationship of the N-terminal domain OPM23, we designed short α-helical peptides with enhanced antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, and antibiofilm properties while maintaining low toxicity. Antimicrobial activity was assessed by broth microdilution; protease and serum stability by HPLC. Mechanism of action was examined using membrane depolarization, NPN uptake, β-galactosidase (ONPG) hydrolysis, flow cytometry, and scanning electron microscopy. Anti-inflammatory effects were evaluated by ELISA, RT-PCR, and lipopolysaccharide (LPS) interaction assays, and biofilm inhibition/eradication was tested against MDR Pseudomonas aeruginosa (MDRPA). Four peptides—OPM23-a7, OPM23-a8, and their D-enantiomers OPM23-a7-d and OPM23-a8-d—exhibited strong antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, and antibiofilm activities with minimal hemolytic and cytotoxic effects. The D-enantiomers showed pronounced stability under physiologically challenging conditions, including high salt, proteolytic environments, and human serum. Mechanistic studies indicated disruption of bacterial membrane integrity with leakage of intracellular contents. In LPS-stimulated RAW264.7 macrophages, the peptides suppressed pro-inflammatory cytokine release via dual actions—direct LPS binding and inhibition of LPS–receptor interactions. Beyond preventing MDRPA biofilm formation, the peptides substantially reduced biomass and viability within mature biofilms. Collectively, the OPM-derived peptides showed multifunctional activity in vitro with favorable stability and low toxicity. These findings support their potential as promising peptide candidates, while in vivo efficacy and pharmacokinetic studies will be required to assess translational feasibility.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-25213-y.

Introduction

The growing prevalence of antibiotic-resistant bacteria is a major global health concern1. Pathogens once treatable with conventional antibiotics are rapidly acquiring resistance to multiple drug classes, while the discovery pipeline for new antibiotics remains sluggish. Recent reports warn of an impending post-antibiotic era in which standard treatments may fail2,3. Thus, there is an urgent need to develop viable therapeutic alternatives.

Antimicrobial peptides (AMPs), widely distributed in nature, are key components of innate immunity and display broad biological activities, including antibacterial, antifungal, antibiofilm, and anti-inflammatory effects4–6. Although their precise bactericidal mechanisms remain debated, most AMPs are thought to kill by compromising bacterial membrane integrity7. This nonspecific, membrane-disruptive action can hinder resistance development8, positioning AMPs as promising alternatives or complements to conventional antibiotics9.

AMPs can also interfere with biofilm development by altering membrane potential and permeability, disrupting quorum sensing or motility, degrading extracellular polymeric substances (EPS), suppressing the stringent response, and down-regulating target genes10. Accordingly, AMPs are being recognized as potential therapeutics for managing biofilms and biofilm-associated infections11.

A novel AMP, Octopromycin (OPM), was identified from the proline-rich protein 5 gene of Octopus minor and exhibits antibacterial and antibiofilm activities against multidrug-resistant (MDR) Acinetobacter baumannii12. OPM comprises 38 amino acids, carries a + 5 net charge, has a hydrophobic residue ratio of 36%, and contains two α-helical segments. It showed potent activity with a minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) of 50 µg/mL and a minimum bactericidal concentration (MBC) of 200 µg/mL against A. baumannii, and it both inhibited biofilm formation and eradicated established biofilms12. OPM further suppressed key processes in A. baumannii biofilms, including metabolic pathways, two-component systems, quorum sensing, and antibiotic biosynthesis13, underscoring its potential as a therapeutic option against MDR A. baumannii biofilms.

Structurally, OPM adopts two helices—helix 1 (¹⁰PIIYDYFKDQLLK²²) and helix 2 (²⁶VILRESMKN³⁴)12. Here, we focus on the N-terminal fragment OPM23 (¹RRLIRTDTGPIIYDYFKDQLLKK²³), which encompasses helix 1 and is enriched in basic and hydrophobic residues (arginine, lysine, leucine) believed to contribute to antimicrobial activity.

For next-generation antimicrobial candidates, multifunctionality is crucial: direct pathogen killing, biofilm inhibition, and mitigation of infection-associated inflammation. Accordingly, we investigated the structure–function relationship of OPM23 and designed α-helical analogs with enhanced antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, and antibiofilm properties while maintaining low cytotoxicity. Using helical-wheel insights, we adjusted positive charge, hydrophobicity, and amphipathicity through targeted residue substitutions; among the synthesized peptides, OPM23-a7 and OPM23-a8 showed superior selectivity toward bacterial cells.

To reduce proteolytic susceptibility, we prepared D-enantiomeric forms (OPM23-a7-d, OPM23-a8-d) by substituting L- with D-amino acids. Because OPM23-a7 and OPM23-a8 contain two and one isoleucine (Ile) residues, respectively, and Ile has two chiral centers, yielding four stereoisomers (L-Ile, D-Ile, L-allo-Ile, D-allo-Ile)—we replaced D-Ile with hydrophobic D-leucine (D-Leu) of identical molecular weight due to the prohibitive cost of Fmoc-D-Ile and Fmoc-D-allo-Ile for solid-phase synthesis. Given that activity is mediated by membrane disruption rather than receptor-specific binding, this substitution was expected to minimally affect bioactivity.

We then comprehensively evaluated OPM23-a7, OPM23-a8, and their D-enantiomers for resistance to proteolytic enzymes, stability in physiological salts and human serum, inhibition and reduction of biofilms, and antimicrobial mechanism by fluorescence-based assays. We additionally assessed anti-inflammatory potential with emphasis on Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) signaling in response to lipopolysaccharides (LPS), including flow cytometry to examine suppression of LPS-induced inflammation. Collectively, these data support the development of potent peptide antibiotics that combat bacterial infection and provide broad therapeutic advantages with minimal cytotoxicity to mammalian cells.

Results

Peptide design and characterization

OPM23 contains three negatively charged Asp residues that may reduce its antimicrobial activity by repelling bacterial membranes. To enhance electrostatic interactions, OPM23-a1 replaced these Asp residues with positively charged Lys. OPM23-a2 increased hydrophobicity and amphipathicity by substituting Arg5 and Lys23 with Leu, while OPM23-a3 improved amphipathicity and positive charge by replacing Leu20 with Arg, reducing hydrophobicity. OPM23-a4 enhanced amphipathicity, charge, and hydrophobicity by replacing the three Asp residues with Leu. OPM23-a5 added more positive charge by substituting Thr6 and Thr8 with Lys. To further boost charge, OPM23-a6 and OPM23-a7 replaced Ile11 and Pro10/Ile11 with Lys, respectively. Finally, OPM23-a8 optimized amphipathicity by replacing Pro10 and Ile12 with Lys.

Structural properties and stability (CD, salts, proteases, serum)

Circular dichroism (CD) spectroscopy supported amphipathic α-helical propensities predicted by helical wheels. All peptides adopted random coils in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), while OPM23-a7 and OPM23-a8 displayed α-helical spectra (double minima at 208/222 nm) in 30 mM sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) micelles and 50% trifluoroethanol (TFE). Their D-enantiomers (OPM23-a7-d, OPM23-a8-d) exhibited mirror-image spectra, indicating left-handed α-helices (Supplementary Fig. S5).

High salt concentrations can attenuate AMP efficacy by weakening electrostatic interactions with bacterial membranes16. Against E. coli (KCTC 1682) and S. aureus (KCTC 1621), MICs of OPM23-a7, OPM23-a8, OPM23-a7-d, and OPM23-a8-d in 150 mM NaCl, 4.5 mM KCl, 6 µM NH4Cl, 1 mM MgCl2, 2.5 mM CaCl2, or 4 µM FeCl3 were unchanged or increased only two- to fourfold relative to controls (Table 3).

Serum biomolecules can modulate AMP behavior18. In 25% (v/v) human serum over 60 h, OPM23-a7 maintained > 90% integrity at 12 h, 83 ± 4% at 24 h, and 74 ± 3% at 60 h; OPM23-a8 retained 40 ± 5% at 60 h (Fig. 2c; Supplementary Fig. S8). D-enantiomers showed a rapid initial signal decline that plateaued around 45% (a7-d) and 40% (a8-d). No new fragment peaks appeared (Supplementary Fig. S8), and LC-MS confirmed intact molecular ions, suggesting non-covalent adsorption to serum macromolecules based on the absence of fragment peaks and intact molecular ions. We acknowledge that direct binding assays would be required to confirm this interpretation. L-forms yielded cleavage fragments (F1, F2) at Arg/Lys residues, whereas D-forms did not.

Cytotoxicity to mammalian cells

MTT assays in HCT116, RAW264.7, and HEK293 cells revealed varying levels of cytotoxicity. In HEK293 cells, all peptides maintained cell viability above 90% up to 64 µM. In HCT116 cells, viability began to decrease at 32 µM for OPM23-a8 and OPM23-a8-d (approximately 85% and 70%, respectively), with further reductions at 64 µM (approximately 70% and 55%). In RAW264.7 cells, similar trends were observed, with viability reductions at 32 µM for OPM23-a8-d (approximately 70%) and at 64 µM ranging from approximately 80% for OPM23-a7 to 35% for OPM23-a8-d (Supplementary Fig. S10).

Antibacterial mechanism (depolarization, permeabilization, FCM, SEM)

Membrane-disrupting peptides can induce bacterial membrane depolarization19. Using diSC3-5, fluorescence increased rapidly at 1×MIC for all selected peptides against S. aureus (KCTC 1621), similar to melittin; buforin-2 had no effect. The D-enantiomers (OPM23-a7-d, OPM23-a8-d) induced greater depolarization than their L-forms, in line with their higher antibacterial activity (Fig. 3a). The dye integrates into the phospholipid bilayer and reports potential-dependent changes20.

Outer membrane permeabilization of E. coli (KCTC 1682) increased in a concentration-dependent manner in NPN assays, and inner membrane permeabilization was confirmed by β-galactosidase (ONPG) release in E. coli ML-35, comparable to melittin (Fig. 3b–c).

Flow cytometry quantified propidium iodide (PI) uptake: controls showed minimal PI fluorescence (2.18% in E. coli; 3.69% in S. aureus), buforin-2 < 5% at 1×MIC, melittin 88.86% (E. coli) and 94.47% (S. aureus), and the selected peptides > 90% in both species (Fig. 3d–e and Supplementary Fig. S11). Scanning electron microscope (SEM) of E. coli revealed surface irregularities, membrane dissolution, clustering, and rupture after peptide exposure, contrasting smooth untreated cells (Fig. 3f and Supplementary Fig. S9).

Anti-inflammatory activity and mechanism

Lipopolysaccharide (LPS) is a potent inducer of sepsis and triggers Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4)-mediated cytokine release21–23. In LPS-stimulated RAW264.7 macrophages, peptides (4 µM) significantly reduced tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), interleukin-6 (IL-6), and macrophage chemoattractant protein-1 (MCP-1) secretion (Fig. 4a–c) and downregulated their mRNA expression (Fig. 4d). LL-37 served as a positive control.

In serum, FITC-conjugated LPS binds to macrophage cell surfaces through the facilitation of LPS-binding protein (LBP), a key mediator of LPS recognition and cellular uptake27. To investigate the inhibitory effects of the selected peptides on the binding of E. coli O111:B4 FITC-LPS to RAW264.7 macrophages, flow cytometric analysis was performed. As shown in Fig. 6a, pre-incubation of FITC-LPS (1 µg/mL) with each peptide (4 µM) significantly reduced its association with RAW264.7 cells, in comparison to PMB, a well-characterized LPS antagonist. The fluorescence intensity of cells treated with FITC-LPS/peptide complexes was substantially lower than that of cells exposed to FITC-LPS alone, indicating effective inhibition of LPS–macrophage binding. These results demonstrate that the selected peptides can effectively block a critical pro-inflammatory event by preventing LPS engagement with macrophage receptors. This inhibitory mechanism is analogous to that of PMB, which neutralizes LPS via direct interaction with its lipid A domain24. The observed reduction in LPS binding likely reflects interference with LBP-mediated delivery, thereby attenuating macrophage activation and downstream cytokine production27.

The ability to displace receptor-bound LPS represents a therapeutically valuable property, as LPS engagement with pattern recognition receptors such as CD14 and TLR-4 initiates sustained pro-inflammatory signaling that is difficult to reverse using agents that target only free LPS28. LL-37, a human cathelicidin-derived AMP, is known to neutralize LPS through both direct binding and inhibition of its interaction with CD1429,30. To investigate whether the selected peptides possess similar receptor-targeting capabilities, RAW264.7 macrophages were pre-incubated with FITC-labeled LPS (1 µg/mL) for 1 h to allow receptor engagement. After removal of unbound LPS, cells were treated with each peptide (4 µM) for an additional hour. As shown in Fig. 6b, treatment with the selected peptides significantly reduced cellular fluorescence intensity, indicating effective displacement of receptor-bound FITC-LPS. This effect was comparable to that induced by LL-37. In contrast, PMB failed to displace receptor-bound LPS, consistent with its limited neutralizing activity restricted to free LPS31,32. In summary, and as illustrated in Fig. 6c, we suggest a proposed mechanism underlying the LPS-neutralizing activity of the peptides, which may involve two complementary actions: (i) direct sequestration of free LPS and (ii) competitive inhibition of receptor-bound LPS at the macrophage surface, likely mediated through CD14 and/or TLR4.

Antibiofilm activity

Biofilms shield bacteria from antibiotics and can increase tolerance by up to 1000-fold compared to planktonic cells33,34. Against MDR P. aeruginosa (MDRPA), OPM23-a7 and OPM23-a8 inhibited biofilm formation 4–8-fold more effectively than LL-37 (MBIC50/90) and markedly disrupted mature biofilms and substantially reduced biomass/viability (MBEC50), as corroborated by confocal laser scanning microscope (CLSM). (Fig. 7a–b; Supplementary Table S1). D-enantiomers were twice as effective as L-forms in both inhibition and eradication. Importantly, for some lead peptides—particularly OPM23-a7-d and OPM23-a8-d—the concentrations required to reach MBIC90 or MBEC90 (≈ 16–32 µM; Table S1) are at or near the range where an initial decline in mammalian cell viability is observed in HCT116 and RAW264.7 (~ 32 µM; Supplementary Fig. S10). Thus, in these cell lines, the 90% biofilm inhibition/eradication levels begin to fall within the cytotoxic region, whereas HEK293 cells remain more tolerant in this range. CLSM confirmed reduced biomass and increased dead-cell staining at 32 µM; the selected peptides outperformed LL-37 in disrupting mature MDRPA biofilms (Fig. 7c).

Discussion

Rational redesign of the OPM23 N-terminal region generated short α-helical analogs with markedly improved antimicrobial potency and selectivity. Progressive substitutions that increased cationic charge and tuned hydrophobicity/amphipathicity (a1→a8) translated into enhanced activity, with OPM23-a7 and OPM23-a8 ranking highest by GM MICs while maintaining low hemolysis, yielding TIs up to 55.7-fold over OPM2314,15. These data indicate that mitigating negative charges and optimizing amphipathicity strengthened electrostatic attraction and membrane insertion without proportionally increasing damage to mammalian erythrocytes.

Multiple orthogonal assays support a membrane-targeting mode of action: rapid depolarization reported by diSC3−519,20, concentration-dependent outer membrane permeabilization (NPN) and inner membrane access (ONPG), high PI uptake by flow cytometry, and SEM-visible rupture collectively demonstrate disruption of both Gram-negative and Gram-positive membranes. D-enantiomers produced stronger depolarization signals and ~ twofold higher antibacterial activity than L-forms, consistent with more persistent peptide–membrane engagement in bacterial envelopes.

Stability profiles address common clinical barriers for AMPs. Antimicrobial activity persisted under physiologic salts with ≤ 4-fold MIC shifts16. D-enantiomerization conferred resistance to chymotrypsin/trypsin degradation6,17. In serum, L-forms—especially OPM23-a7—retained substantial integrity over 60 h, whereas D-forms showed early signal decline without fragment peaks; LC-MS and chromatography indicate non-covalent adsorption to serum macromolecules rather than enzymatic cleavage18. Such adsorption may affect recovery in analytical workflows and will be relevant to downstream pharmacokinetic/formulation studies, but it does not contradict the observed protease resistance of D-forms. Future work will employ native binding assays (e.g., ultrafiltration-LC/MS, native SEC, and SPR/BLI with albumin, α₂-macroglobulin, and LBP) to directly test and quantify peptide–protein association in serum.

At non-cytotoxic concentrations, the peptides exerted potential anti-inflammatory effects in RAW264.7 cells by suppressing TNF-α, IL-6, and MCP-1 at protein and mRNA levels (Fig. 4). Mechanistically, they directly bound LPS (BC displacement)24 with potencies comparable to LL-3725, disaggregated FITC-LPS assemblies26, prevented LBP-facilitated binding of FITC-LPS to macrophages27, and displaced receptor-bound LPS—similar to LL-37 and in contrast to PMB (which primarily targets free LPS via lipid A)24,28–32. The data are consistent with a dual mechanism to neutralize both free and receptor-engaged LPS, which provides breadth for attenuating TLR4-driven cytokine cascades implicated in endotoxin responses21–23.

Finally, the peptides displayed strong antibiofilm activity against MDR P. aeruginosa, with 4–8-fold better inhibition and a fourfold lower eradication threshold than LL-37, and with D-forms approximately twice as effective as L-forms. CLSM corroborated decreased biomass and increased dead cells within mature biofilms (Fig. 7). Given the protective extracellular matrix and the up to 1000-fold increase in tolerance within biofilms33,34, these findings indicate meaningful penetration and disruption of established biofilms by the designed peptides.

Collectively, OPM23-a7, OPM23-a8, and their D-enantiomers integrate: (i) broad-spectrum, membrane-disruptive antibacterial activity with high TI14,15,19,20; (ii) stability to salts and proteases, with distinct serum interaction profiles16–18; (iii) dual-mode LPS neutralization that suppresses pro-inflammatory cytokines21–32; and (iv) potent antibiofilm inhibition and mature MDRPA biofilms33,34. These attributes, demonstrated across complementary assays, support their potential as multifunctional peptide candidates for infections where multidrug resistance, endotoxin-driven inflammation, and biofilms co-occur. Outlook: future studies will focus on pharmacokinetics, in vivo infection models (including MDRPA), and formulation strategies to mitigate serum interactions.

Conclusion

Effective AMPs must couple broad antibacterial activity with anti-inflammatory and antibiofilm functions while maintaining low toxicity. Guided by structure–activity relationship (SAR) analyses of OPM23, we designed and optimized OPM23-a7 and OPM23-a8 and their D-enantiomers (OPM23-a7-d, OPM23-a8-d). These peptides demonstrated multifunctionality—potent antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, and antibiofilm activities—together with minimal hemolytic and cytotoxic effects. The D-enantiomers showed enhanced stability under physiologically challenging conditions, including elevated salt, proteolytic environments, and human serum. Mechanistic studies revealed a membrane-disruptive bactericidal mode of action, characterized by loss of membrane integrity and leakage of intracellular contents. In LPS-stimulated RAW264.7 macrophages, the peptides significantly reduced pro-inflammatory cytokine production via a dual mechanism: direct binding to free LPS and inhibition of LPS–receptor interactions. Beyond preventing biofilm formation by MDR P. aeruginosa, the peptides caused a substantial reduction in mature biofilms produced by the same pathogen. In summary, OPM23-a7, OPM23-a8, and their D-enantiomers emerge as strong candidates due to their multifunctional activity, stability, and low toxicity in vitro. Future development must now transition to in vivo models to definitively assess their therapeutic efficacy and confirm their safety profiles. Investigating pharmacokinetics, including absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion, will be critical to determine bioavailability and optimal dosing regimens. Additionally, exploring large-scale synthesis and formulation strategies will facilitate clinical translation, addressing stability and delivery challenges. Long-term prospects include integrating these peptides into combination therapies with existing antibiotics, potentially overcoming resistance mechanisms and enhancing therapeutic outcomes. Such efforts could position these multifunctional agents as viable candidates for addressing the growing global health threat posed by MDR pathogens.

Methods

Materials

Rink amide-methylbenzhydrylamine (MBHA) resin and 9-fluorenylmethoxycarbonyl (Fmoc) amino acids were purchased from Novabiochem (La Jolla, CA, USA). Lipopolysaccharide (LPS) from E. coli O111:B4, 2,2,2-trifluoroethanol (TFE), propidium iodide (PI), Triton X-100, 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)−2,5-diphenyl-2 H-tetrazolium bromide (MTT), N-phenyl-1-naphthylamine (NPN), 3,3′-dipropylthiadicarbocyanine (diSC3-5), o-nitrophenyl-β-D-galactopyranoside (ONPG), trypsin (EC 3.4.21.4), α-chymotrypsin (EC 3.4.21.1), FITC-conjugated LPS (E. coli O111:B4), BODIPY-TR-cadaverine (BC) and human serum were from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). ELISA kits for TNF-α IL-6, and MCP-1 were from R&D Systems (Minneapolis, MN, USA).

Bacterial strains and mammalian cell lines

E. coli (KCTC 1682), P. aeruginosa (KCTC 1637), and S. aureus (KCTC 1621) were obtained from the Korean Collection for Type Cultures (KCTC, KRIBB). Methicillin-resistant S. aureus (MRSA; CCARM 3089, 3090, 3095) and multidrug-resistant P. aeruginosa (MDRPA; CCARM 2095, 2109) were from the Culture Collection of Antibiotic-Resistant Microbes (CCARM, Seoul Women’s University). Vancomycin-resistant Enterococcus faecium (VREF; ATCC 51559), A. baumannii (ATCC BAA-1605), and E. coli ML-35 (ATCC 43837) were from ATCC. A. baumannii (KCCM 40203) was from the Korean Culture Center of Microorganisms (KCCM). The bacteria used in this study were collected and maintained in our laboratory. All culturing and handling procedures strictly followed safety guidelines for proper preservation and operation. RAW264.7 (mouse macrophage), NIH-3T3 (mouse fibroblast), HaCaT (human keratinocyte), HEK293 (human embryonic kidney), and HCT116 (human colorectal carcinoma) cells were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum (Sigma-Aldrich), 100 U/mL penicillin, and 0.1 mg/mL streptomycin at 37 °C in 5% CO₂.

Peptide synthesis

Peptides were synthesized by solid-phase peptide synthesis (SPPS) using standard Fmoc chemistry. Crude products were purified by preparative RP-HPLC on an X-Bridge C18 column (20 mm × 250 mm, 15 μm) using an acetonitrile gradient in 0.05% (v/v) TFA. Purity was verified by analytical RP-HPLC (X-Bridge C18, 2.1 mm × 100 mm, 3.5 μm); 5 µL was injected, mobile phase water/acetonitrile with 0.05% TFA, 0.2 mL/min, UV 230 nm, 60-min run. All peptides showed > 95% purity. Peptide stock solutions were prepared in autoclaved water and filter-sterilized unless stated otherwise.

CD spectroscopy

CD spectra were recorded on a JASCO-715 spectropolarimeter (JASCO, Tokyo, Japan) from 190 to 250 nm at 0.1-nm intervals, 25 °C, scan speed 20 nm/min, using a 1-mm pathlength quartz cuvette. Peptides (150 µM) were prepared in water, 30 mM SDS, or 50% (v/v) TFE.

Antimicrobial activity assay

Mid-log cultures were diluted in Mueller–Hinton broth (MHB; Difco). Suspensions (2 × 105 CFU/well) were dispensed into 96-well plates; peptides were added by two-fold serial dilution. Plates were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h, and OD600 was measured. MIC was the lowest concentration with no visible growth. Experiments were performed in three independent biological replicates.

Salt sensitivity assay

MICs were determined in MHB supplemented with physiological salts (150 mM NaCl, 4.5 mM KCl, 2 mM CaCl2, 6 µM NH4Cl, 8 µM ZnCl2, 1 mM MgCl2, 4 µM FeCl3) as above. Data represent three independent assays.

Hemolysis assay

Sheep red blood cells (sRBCs) were suspended in PBS to 4% (v/v). Peptide solutions (1–512 µM; 100 µL) were mixed with sRBCs (100 µL) in 96-well plates, incubated 1 h at 37 °C, then centrifuged (1000 g, 10 min). Supernatant (100 µL) was transferred to a new plate, and absorbance at 450 nm was measured. % Hemolysis = [(Abs of peptide − Abs of PBS)/(Abs of 0.1% Triton X-100 − Abs of PBS)]. Sheep RBCs were purchased from SynergyInno (Sungnam, South Korea) and used according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The experiment was repeated three times with duplicates each time. HC10 values are means of three independent experiments.

Cytotoxicity against mammalian cells

RAW264.7, HCT116, and HEK293 cells (1 × 10⁴ cells/well, 100 µL) were seeded in 96-well plates and incubated overnight. Cells were treated with peptides (2–64 µM) for 48 h, then MTT (1.0 mg/mL, 100 µL/well) for 4 h. The supernatant was removed, and DMSO (100 µL/well) was added to dissolve formazan crystals for 15 min with gentle shaking. Absorbance was measured at 550 nm. A blank (medium + MTT + DMSO without cells) was subtracted from all readings. Percent cell viability was calculated as:

% Viability = 100 × (Abs of sample − Abs of blank)/(Abs of untreated − Abs of blank),

where untreated cells were defined as 100% viability. A positive cytotoxicity control (0.1% Triton X-100, 30 min) was included to verify assay performance but was not used for normalization. Experiments were performed in three independent biological replicates, each with technical triplicates.

Proteolytic stability

Peptide stocks (5 mM, water) were diluted in 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.4) to 150 µM (1 mL). α-Chymotrypsin (0.1 µg/mL) and trypsin (0.06 µg/mL; Sigma-Aldrich) were freshly prepared in the same buffer. Reactions were incubated at 37 °C with gentle agitation. Aliquots (50 µL) were taken at 0, 2, 4, 6, 16, 18, 20, 22, and 24 h and quenched with water containing 1% TFA (100 µL). Degradation was monitored by HPLC (UV 230 nm), and intact peptide (%) was normalized to 0 h. Selected samples were confirmed by ESI-MS. Experiments were in triplicate (mean ± SD).

Human serum stability assay

Pooled human serum (25% v/v in sterile 1×PBS) was mixed with peptides (final 200 µM) in 1.5 mL tubes (total 1.2 mL), pre-warmed to 37 °C. After gentle mixing, samples were incubated at 37 °C. Aliquots (100 µL) were collected at 0, 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, 24, 30, 36, and 60 h, combined 1:1 with ice-cold acetonitrile + 1% TFA, vortexed 30 s, and stored at 4 °C overnight. After centrifugation (15,000 rpm, 5 min, 4 °C), supernatants were 0.22 μm-filtered and analyzed by LC-ESI-MS (C18, 2.1 × 100 mm, 3.5 μm; 5–45% acetonitrile in 0.05% TFA over 20 min; 0.2 mL/min; positive-ion mode, m/z 300–1700). Extracted-ion chromatograms (2 + and 3 + ions) were integrated and normalized to 0 h. Triplicate experiments (mean ± SD).

Cytoplasmic membrane depolarization

S. aureus (KCTC 1621) mid-log cells were washed 3× with HEPES buffer (5 mM HEPES, 20 mM glucose, 0.1 M KCl) and adjusted to OD₆₀₀ = 0.1. Cells were incubated with diSC3-5 (0.4 µM, 37 °C, 60 min) and then treated with peptides. Fluorescence was recorded on an RF-5301 PC spectrofluorometer (Shimadzu, Japan) at 622 nm (excitation)/670 nm (emission).

Outer membrane permeabilization

E. coli (KCTC 1682) mid-log cells were resuspended in HEPES buffer (5 mM HEPES, 20 mM glucose, 5 mM KCN, pH 7.4) to OD₆₀₀ = 0.05. NPN (10 µM) was added, baseline fluorescence was recorded (Ex 350 nm/Em 420 nm), and changes upon peptide addition were monitored.

Inner membrane permeabilization

E. coli ML-35 mid-log cells were diluted to OD₆₀₀ = 0.5 in 10 mM sodium phosphate, 100 mM NaCl (pH 7.4) containing 1.5 mM ONPG. Hydrolysis to o-nitrophenol was monitored at 420 nm after peptide treatment.

Flow cytometry

E. coli (KCTC 1682) and S. aureus (KCTC 1621) were grown to mid-log in MHB, washed 3× with PBS, and diluted to 10⁵ CFU/mL. Suspensions were incubated with peptides at 1×MIC in the presence of PI (10 µg/mL) for 30 min at 4 °C, washed with PBS, and analyzed on a flow cytometer (Agilent, San Diego, CA; Ex 488 nm).

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM)

E. coli (KCTC 1682) at OD₆₀₀ = 0.1 were treated with peptides (2×MIC, 4 h). Cells were fixed in 2.5% (w/v) glutaraldehyde at 4 °C overnight, dehydrated through ethanol (50, 70, 90, 100%; 10 min each), followed by ethanol: tert-butanol (1:1, 15 min) and tert-butanol (15 min). Samples were dried with liquid CO₂ (critical-point dryer), coated with Au–Pd, and imaged by SEM (Gemini 500 + EDS, Oxford).

Quantification of cytokines in LPS-stimulated RAW264.7 cells

RAW264.7 cells (1 × 10⁵ cells/well) were seeded in 96-well plates. After 6 h, peptides (final 4 µM) were added; 1 h later, E. coli O111:B4 LPS (20 ng/mL) was added and cells were incubated 16 h. Supernatants were collected, and TNF-α, IL-6, and MCP-1 were quantified by ELISA (R&D Systems) per the manufacturer’s protocol.

Reverse-transcription PCR (RT-PCR)

RAW264.7 cells (5 × 10⁵ cells/well) were plated in six-well plates and incubated overnight. Peptides (4 µM) were added, with or without LPS (20 ng/mL), for 6 h in DMEM + 10% serum. Cells were washed with PBS, and total RNA was extracted (TRIzol, Ambion). cDNA was synthesized from 1 µg RNA (PrimeScript, Takara). PCR was performed with AccuPower® PCR PreMix (Bioneer) using primers for TNF-α (F 5′-CCTGTAGCCCACGTCGTAGC-3′; R 5′-TTGACCTCAGCGCTGAGTTG-3′), IL-6 (F 5′-ACAACCACGGCCTTCCCTACTT-3′; R 5′-CACGATTTCCCAGAGAACATGTG-3′), MCP-1 (F 5′-ATCCCAATGAGTAGGCTGGAGAGC-3′; R 5′-CAGAAGTGCTTGAGGTGGTTGTG-3′), and GAPDH (F 5′-GACATCAAGAAGGTGGTGAA-3′; R 5′-TGTCATACCAGGAAATGAGC-3′). Cycling: 94 °C 5 min; 30 cycles of 94 °C 1 min, 55 °C 1.5 min, 72 °C 1 min; final extension 72 °C 5 min.

LPS binding assay (BC displacement)

Cell-free E. coli O111:B4 LPS (25 µg/mL) was incubated with BC (2.5 µg/mL) in 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.4) for 15 min (equilibration), then peptides (1–16 µM) were added and incubated 30 min in the dark at room temperature. Fluorescence was measured (RF-5301 PC, Shimadzu; Ex 580 nm/Em 620 nm). %ΔF = [(F_obs − F₀)/(F₁₀₀ − F₀)] × 100, where F₀ is BC + LPS (no peptide) and F₁₀₀ is BC + LPS with polymyxin B (50 µM).

Dissociation of LPS–FITC aggregates

FITC-LPS (E. coli O111:B4, 1 µg/mL) in 1×PBS was placed in a quartz cuvette. Background fluorescence was recorded (Em 515 nm). After sequential peptide additions, fluorescence was monitored (Ex 488 nm/Em 512–515 nm) until equilibrium; dequenching indicated aggregate dissociation.

Effect of peptides on LPS binding to macrophages

FITC-LPS (1 µg/mL) was pre-incubated with peptides (4 µM, 1 h, 37 °C). RAW264.7 cells (5 × 10⁵ cells/mL) were exposed to the mixtures for 30 min at 37 °C, washed with ice-cold PBS, and analyzed by flow cytometry (Agilent). PMB and LL-37 served as positive controls; background was cells without FITC-LPS or peptides.

Effect of peptides on receptor-bound LPS

RAW264.7 cells (5 × 10⁵ cells/mL) were incubated with FITC-LPS (1 µg/mL, 1 h), washed 3× with PBS, treated with peptides (1 h), washed again, and analyzed by flow cytometry.

Inhibition of biofilm formation

Peptides were serially diluted in sterile water (100 µL/well) in 96-well polypropylene plates (Costar). MDRPA CCARM 2095 in MHB (1 × 106 CFU/mL) was added (100 µL) to yield 5 × 105 CFU/mL, 200 µL total. After overnight incubation at 37 °C, OD₅₉₅ was measured. Planktonic cells were removed; wells were washed 3 × with water, blotted, stained with 0.1% crystal violet (5 min), rinsed, destained with 95% ethanol, and OD₅₉₅ was read. Controls contained untreated bacteria. Experiments were in triplicate. MBIC was the lowest concentration preventing residual crystal violet staining. The experiments were reproduced at least three times in duplicate.

Disruption of mature biofilms

MBEC was determined using the Calgary Biofilm Device (CBD; Innovotech). MDRPA CCARM 2095 (1 × 10⁶ CFU/mL; 150 µL/well) in LB was added to 96-well plates with a peg lid (Innovotech, 19111) and incubated (37 °C, 110 rpm, 24 h). Pegs were rinsed with 0.01 M PBS and transferred to plates containing serially diluted peptides (200 µL/well) for 24 h (37 °C, 110 rpm). Pegs were rinsed and moved to recovery plates with fresh LB (200 µL/well), sonicated 10–15 min, then incubated 24 h to assess turbidity. MBEC was the lowest peptide concentration with no visible turbidity vs. sterile controls.

Confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM)

MDRPA CCARM 2095 (1 × 10⁶ CFU/mL) was cultured on discs in 24-well plates with MHB-glucose for 24 h. Discs were washed (1×PBS), transferred to fresh plates with peptides (32 µM) for 12 h, washed twice, and stained with SYTO 9 (6.7 µM) and PI (40 µM). After 30 min at 37 °C in the dark, planar images were acquired by CLSM (Zeiss LSM 710 Meta; ZEISS Microscopy, Jena, Germany) and analyzed with ZEN 2009 Light Edition. For each condition, biofilms were prepared in triplicate, and for every replicate at least three randomly selected fields were imaged. All images were acquired under identical laser power, detector gain, and analysis settings.
