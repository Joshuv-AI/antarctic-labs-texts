Surgical Reconstructive Peri‐Implantitis Treatment for 3‐ and 4‐Wall Bone Defects: A Single‐Blind Randomised Controlled Trial With a 1‐Year Follow‐Up
Authors: Jarno Hakkers, Yvonne C M de Waal, Barzi Gareb, Henny J A Meijer, Gerry M Raghoebar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimTo evaluate the effect of a reconstructive procedure during surgical treatment of peri‐implantitis in 3‐ or 4‐wall bone defects on clinical and radiographic outcomes after 1 year follow‐up.Materials and MethodsFifty‐two patients with peri‐implantitis were randomly assigned to surgical treatment with (n = 27) or without (n = 25) a reconstructive procedure using autologous bone, xenograft and a collagen membrane. Primary outcomes were the mean peri‐implant probing depth (PPD) and radiographic marginal bone level (MBL). Bleeding on probing (BoP) was the prespecified primary outcome in the original protocol; the present report emphasises PPD and MBL outcomes relevant to reconstructive treatment. Secondary outcomes included BoP, disease resolution (composite treatment outcome), suppuration (SoP), mid‐buccal recession (Midbuc REC) and keratinised mucosa (Midbuc KM). Data were analysed using multilevel mixed‐effects models.ResultsMixed‐effects models showed that patients in the test group showed enhanced marginal bone levels at all follow‐ups (β = −0.85 to −1.65 mm; p ≤ 0.04) and less mid‐buccal recession (β = −1.06 to −1.68 mm; p ≤ 0.01) compared to the control group. No significant differences were observed for PPD, BoP, SoP, Midbuc KM or disease resolution.ConclusionWhile the adjunctive reconstructive procedure did not significantly influence peri‐implant PPD, BoP, SoP or disease resolution, it was associated with improved radiographic marginal bone levels and reduced mid‐buccal soft‐tissue recession.

Introduction

Peri‐implantitis is defined as a plaque‐associated pathological condition affecting the tissues surrounding dental implants, and characterised by an increase in peri‐implant probing depth, bleeding and/or suppuration on probing (BoP/SoP) and subsequent progressive loss of the supporting bone (Berglundh et al. 2018). Over the years, numerous non‐surgical and surgical treatment protocols have been proposed, but their capacity to consistently achieve durable and successful outcomes remains limited and often unsatisfactory (Garaicoa‐Pazmino et al. 2025). Depending on the configuration of the bone defect, surgical reconstructive peri‐implantitis treatment may sometimes be indicated to potentially obtain re‐osseointegration. Although such treatment protocols have been widely studied, the number of randomised controlled trials (RCTs) rigorously evaluating the benefits of bone reconstruction in peri‐implantitis bone defects remains limited (Tomasi et al. 2019). Moreover, the decontamination protocols executed prior to defect reconstruction vary significantly, where no clear gold standard has been established so far (Herrera et al. 2023).

The available evidence supporting reconstructive surgical peri‐implantitis treatment over non‐reconstructive techniques is limited and inconclusive (Ramanauskaite et al. 2019). There are a number of RCTs comparing reconstructive surgical peri‐implantitis treatment with open‐flap debridement. Some studies found that radiographic defect fill (Renvert et al. 2018) and clinical attachment levels improved at the reconstructed sites (Renvert et al. 2021; Emanuel et al. 2020) or resulted in enhanced disease resolution (Renvert et al. 2024). On the contrary, others have reported less pronounced benefits of bone reconstruction when compared to access flap surgery alone (Heitz‐Mayfield et al. 2023), including limited effects on buccal recession (Derks et al. 2022). To date, no uniform consensus has been reached regarding the most effective surgical approach for peri‐implantitis, as the available evidence indicates that outcomes differ depending on the parameter evaluated; while some procedures have demonstrated advantages in hard‐tissue regeneration (Shahbazi et al. 2024; Li et al. 2023), clinical indicators of inflammation such as BoP and SoP appear to show minimal differences across techniques (Barootchi et al. 2025). Among the available grafting materials, deproteinised bovine bone mineral remains the most extensively documented, whereas autogenous bone, although less frequently studied, has shown promising potential for vertical bone regeneration in surgical peri‐implantitis treatment (Noelken and Al‐Nawas 2023).

Given the heterogeneity in reconstructive procedures and the inconsistent clinical outcomes reported, further investigation of this procedure within a controlled, randomised study design is warranted. Therefore, this present study aimed to assess the effect of a reconstructive procedure applied during surgical treatment of peri‐implantitis in cases with 3‐ and 4‐wall peri‐implant bone defects on clinical and radiographic outcomes after 1‐year follow‐up.

Materials and Methods

Trial Design

This study was a two‐arm, parallel‐design, investigator‐blinded RCT (1:1) comparing surgical reconstructive peri‐implantitis treatment to open‐flap debridement in 3‐ and 4‐wall peri‐implant bone defects.

Participants

Between January 2020 and August 2023, patients who were referred to the Department of Oral and Maxillofacial Surgery of the University Medical Center Groningen were screened for eligibility by one researcher (J.H.). The last follow‐up visit took place in January 2025. Peri‐implantitis was diagnosed in accordance with the criteria as proposed by the 2017 World Workshop on the Classification of Periodontal and Peri‐Implant Diseases and Conditions: presence of bleeding and/or suppuration on gentle probing; increased probing depth compared to previous examinations; and the presence of bone loss beyond crestal bone level changes resulting from initial bone remodelling. In the absence of previous examination data, peri‐implantitis was defined as the presence of bleeding and/or suppuration on gentle probing, probing depths of ≥ 6 mm and bone levels ≥ 3 mm apical of the most coronal portion of the intraosseous part of the implant.

Eligibility Criteria

The surgical intervention was conducted in cases where non‐surgical submarginal instrumentation (NSI) failed to restore peri‐implant health. The exclusion criteria prior to NSI were as follows: medical and general contra‐indications for the procedure; a history of local radiotherapy to the head and neck region; pregnancy and lactation; uncontrolled diabetes mellitus (HbA1c < 7% or < 53 mmol/mol); use of intravenous bisphosphonates; known allergy to chlorhexidine, amoxicillin and/or metronidazole; significant contra‐indications to the use of antibiotics due to other medicines; long‐term use of anti‐inflammatory drugs; patients incapable of performing basic oral hygiene measures as a result of physical or mental disorders; implants with bone loss exceeding two‐thirds of the implant length; previous surgical treatment of the peri‐implantitis lesions; and chronic bronchitis or asthma. Additional exclusion criteria after NSI were as follows: no longer fulfilling peri‐implantitis diagnosis criteria (according to the criteria outlined by Herrera et al. (2023) for the non‐surgical phase of peri‐implantitis management, i.e., residual probing depths of ≤ 5 mm, absence of SoP and no BoP at more than one site) and/or active periodontal disease. The study was approved by the UMCG Medical Ethical Committee (METc no. 2019/625, approved on 20 February 2020) and registered in the Overview of medical research in the Netherlands (NL‐OMON27505). It was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and followed the CONSORT 2025 guidelines (Hopewell et al. 2025).

Interventions

Prior to surgery, all patients received a single session of non‐surgical treatment including oral hygiene instructions, periodontal cleaning and mechanical peri‐implant debridement using an air‐polishing device (Airflow Master Piezon, EMS, Nyon, Switzerland) with erythritol‐based powder containing 0.3% chlorhexidine (PLUS Powder, EMS). Screw‐retained suprastructures were removed before surgery. Surgical procedures were performed under local anaesthesia (Ultracain D‐S Forte, Sanofi S.A., Paris, France) by two experienced clinicians (G.M.R. and Y.C.M.d.W.). Following an intra‐sulcular incision, full‐thickness flaps were elevated, and granulation tissue was removed using titanium curettes (Hu‐Friedy, Chicago, IL, USA). Implant surfaces were cleaned using the air‐polishing device until macroscopically clean. Decontamination was performed with 35% phosphoric acid gel (Temrex, Freeport, NY, USA) for 1 min, followed by thorough rinsing. Defect configuration was assessed intraoperatively to confirm eligibility. Randomisation was performed intraoperatively by opening a sealed envelope by the assistant, revealing the assigned treatment to the surgical team. In the test group, reconstructive therapy was carried out using a 1:1 mixture of autogenous bone (harvested with a SafeScraper TWIST, Geistlich Pharma AG, Wolhusen, Switzerland) and deproteinised bovine bone mineral (Bio‐Oss, Geistlich Pharma AG), which was covered with a resorbable collagen membrane (Bio‐Gide, Geistlich Pharma AG). In deviation from the registered protocol, open‐flap debridement was applied as the control treatment instead of a resective procedure to ensure methodological consistency and comparability with the reconstructive approach. Flaps were repositioned and sutured using single interrupted sutures, and suprastructures were reconnected (H.J.A.M.). Postoperatively, all patients used a 0.12% chlorhexidine and 0.05 cetylpiridiniumchloride mouthwash (Perio‐Aid, Dentaid S.L., Barcelona, Spain) for 2 weeks and received systemic amoxicillin (500 mg, three times daily) for 1 week. Sutures were removed after 2 weeks, and patients were instructed in proper peri‐implant hygiene using electric toothbrushes and interdental brushes.

Outcomes

Primary outcomes were mean peri‐implant probing pocket depth (PPD) (mm) and marginal bone level (MBL) (mm). Although mean BoP was originally designated as the primary outcome, it was revised after study initiation to align with updated clinical guidelines (Herrera et al. 2023). This modification is transparently reported here. Secondary outcome variables included mean peri‐implant BoP (%), a composite outcome defining disease resolution, based on the end points of surgical peri‐implantitis treatment as suggested by Herrera et al. (2023) (residual probing depths ≤ 5 mm; no BoP in ≥ 1 probing site; no suppuration on probing), mean peri‐implant SoP (%), mean peri‐implant plaque score (Plq) (%), mid‐buccal recession (Midbuc REC) (mm), mid‐buccal width of the keratinised mucosa (Midbuc KM) (mm), full‐mouth mean periodontal PPD (mm), full‐mouth periodontal BoP (%) and full‐mouth periodontal Plq (%).

Sample Size

Sample size estimation was conducted in accordance with the methodology described by Hentenaar et al. (2022). Calculations were based on a multilevel linear regression framework, accounting for the hierarchical nature of the data, where implants are nested within individual patients. Scherbaum and Ferreter have emphasised the importance of appropriately balancing different hierarchical levels when determining an adequate sample size and statistical power (Scherbaum and Ferreter 2009). Assuming an average of two affected implants per participant, a minimum of 51 patients were required to achieve 80% statistical power to detect a moderate effect size (0.5) at a 5% significance level, based on existing research on sample size requirements for multilevel analysis (Maas and Hox 2005). To accommodate the expected clinical course, it was anticipated that approximately 10% of patients would respond favourably to non‐surgical therapy, thereby not progressing to the surgical phase. Additionally, a 10% attrition rate was factored into the calculation. Taking these considerations into account, the final recruitment target was set at 61 patients to ensure sufficient statistical power and continuity throughout the study phases.

Randomisation

Patients were randomly assigned to receive either reconstructive (test) or access flap (control) peri‐implantitis surgery by means of randomisation with a fixed block size of four. Sealed, coded envelopes (AA0, AB1, etc.) containing notes stating either ‘reconstruction’ or ‘no reconstruction’ were individually opened during the procedure by an operator assistant. The investigator/assessor of outcomes (J.H.) was blinded to group allocation. The number of envelopes was equally divided between the two groups. A list containing the decoding procedure was kept sealed during the study period.

Statistical Methods

The distribution of continuous variables was evaluated through both visual inspection of Q–Q plots and the Shapiro–Wilk test. For variables following a normal distribution, results were expressed as means with standard deviations (SD), while non‐normally‐distributed variables were summarised using medians and interquartile ranges (25th–75th percentiles). Between‐group comparisons for non‐parametric data were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test. To assess the longitudinal effects of the interventions, both linear and logistic mixed‐effects models were applied. These models incorporated fixed effects for treatment allocation and time (in months), with interaction terms included only when they significantly improved model fit, as determined by likelihood ratio testing. Random intercepts were specified for participants, with implants nested within individuals to account for clustering. Model outputs are presented as regression coefficients (β) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for continuous outcomes, and as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% CIs for binary outcomes. All model assumptions were verified and met. Statistical significance was defined as a two‐sided p‐value ≤ 0.05. Analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.4.3; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), using the lme4 package (Bates et al. 2015). Furthermore, post hoc comparisons between 3‐ and 4‐wall defects were performed using stratified, time‐point‐specific tests (independent samples t‐tests, Fisher's exact tests and Mann–Whitney U tests); these subgroup analyses were considered exploratory. Missing follow‐up data were managed using the last observation carried forward (LOCF) method, consistent with the intention‐to‐treat (ITT) principle. Additional post hoc between‐group analyses were performed at T12 using clinically relevant threshold outcomes. These included residual PPD > 5 mm and peri‐implant BoP at more than one‐sixth of the sites per implant. Between‐group differences in proportions were assessed using Fisher's exact tests.

Outcomes

Linear and logistic mixed‐effects models estimated marginal between‐group differences at 3, 6, 9 and 12 months (Table 4). Test group patients reported higher mean MBL at every follow‐up: −0.85 mm (−1.66 to −0.05) at 3 months (p = 0.04); −1.12 mm (−1.93 to −0.31) at 6 months (p = 0.01); −1.39 mm (−2.23 to −0.55) at 9 months (p < 0.001); and −1.65 mm (−2.54 to −0.77) at 12 months (p < 0.001). Test group patients also showed less mid‐buccal recession compared to the control group at all time points: −1.06 mm (−1.87 to −0.24) at 3 months (p = 0.01); −1.26 mm (−2.08 to −0.45) at 6 months (p < 0.001); −1.47 mm (−2.31 to −0.64) at 9 months (p < 0.001); and −1.68 mm (−2.57 to −0.80) at 12 months (p < 0.001). No significant between‐group differences were detected for PPD, BoP, SoP, mid‐buccal keratinised mucosa or disease resolution. Table 5 represents the post hoc analyses within treatment arms and by defect configuration. Within the test group, BoP was lower in 3‐walled than 4‐walled defects [31.9% (31.3) vs. 44.4% (41.6); p = 0.03] at 6 months. In the control group, MBL differed between 3‐ and 4‐walled defects at 6 months (p < 0.001) and 12 months (p = 0.01). Within 3‐walled defects, test group patients showed higher MBL at 3 and 6 months: 3 months, mean (SD) 2.2 (1.2) versus 4.6 (2.2) mm, p = 0.03; 6 months, 2.4 (1.4) versus 4.3 (2.3) mm; p = 0.01. Furthermore, treatment success at 6 months favoured the test group (58.3% vs. 16.7%; p = 0.045). Additionally, Midbuc KM differed between 3‐ and 4‐walled defects at 9 months (p = 0.02) and 12 months (p = 0.048). Within 4‐walled defects, MBL was significantly higher in the test group at 12 months [2.5 (1.8) vs. 3.0 (1.0) mm; p = 0.04]. At 12 months (T12), 19.2% of implants in the test group had residual PPD > 5 mm, compared to 26.1% in the control group (p = 0.73). Additionally, 61.5% of implants in the test group exhibited BoP in more than one‐sixth of the sites, versus 65.2% in the control group (p > 0.99).

Discussion

This study compared reconstructive peri‐implantitis surgery with access‐flap surgical therapy in 3‐ and 4‐wall peri‐implantitis bone defects. Reconstructive therapy produced consistently superior radiographic bone levels and less mid‐buccal recession through 12 months, whereas clinical inflammatory parameters (PPD, BoP, SoP), keratinised mucosa and overall treatment success did not differ between the groups. Subgroup findings suggest that the MBL benefit was most pronounced early in 3‐walled defects and evident by 12 months in 4‐walled defects, with an isolated 6‐month success advantage for reconstruction in 3‐walled sites.

Several comparable studies have been conducted. Heitz‐Mayfield et al. (2023) compared guided bone regeneration using a xenograft and collagen membrane with access‐flap surgery, both supplemented with systemic antimicrobials, reporting no significant differences between the test and control groups (Heitz‐Mayfield et al. 2023). Renvert et al. (2021) conducted an RCT evaluating surgical debridement versus reconstructive therapy with deproteinised bovine bone and a native bilayer collagen membrane. Their 1‐year results showed significantly greater radiographic defect fill in the reconstructive group, despite no differences in clinical outcomes. After a 3‐year follow‐up, the reconstructive group showed further radiographic defect fill and a higher rate of disease resolution (Renvert et al. 2024). These outcomes are in line with the present study, where reconstructive surgical therapy appeared to offer a sustained benefit on marginal bone levels over time compared to the control group. Importantly, this observation was made based solely on radiographic evaluation; the presence of true peri‐implant bone can be confirmed only through clinical and histological analysis. Moreover, several authors have recommended a minimum follow‐up period of 3 years to reliably assess long‐term efficacy and stability of peri‐implantitis treatment (Carcuac et al. 2020; Lee et al. 2021; Serino et al. 2021). Disease resolution rates reported by Renvert and colleagues after 1 year differed from our observations: 35% in the treatment group and 30% in the control group. This could be attributed to a different composite treatment outcome, where Renvert and colleagues assessed BoP at four sites per implant. The present study adhered to the EFP Clinical practice guideline (Herrera et al. 2023) by not incorporating bone‐level changes in the composite outcome defining disease resolution and measuring clinical parameters at six sites per implant. Positive results favouring bone augmentation were also reported in a systematic review performed by Tomasi et al. (2019), where a weighted mean difference of 0.7 mm recession was presented when analysing differences between test and control groups in studies comparing reconstructive techniques to control procedures (Tomasi et al. 2019). Therefore, bone reconstruction during surgical peri‐implantitis treatment in 3‐ and 4‐wall defects seems to benefit mid‐buccal recession. The present study showed that 43.8% of the implants in the test group 44.4% in the control group met the endpoints of the surgical treatment at T12. In a multi‐centre RCT, Derks et al. (2022) performed either access flap surgery (control group) or reconstructive surgery (test group) using bone substitute material in 138 patients (147 implants) (Derks et al. 2022). They reported that 16.4% of the implants in the test group and 13.5% of the implants in the control group met the prerequisite outcome variables for disease resolution. Monje et al. (2023) reported 75.1% disease resolution in the test group (peri‐implantitis site augmentation with a membrane) and 79.2% in the control group (peri‐implantitis site augmentation without a membrane) (Monje et al. 2023). Studies still employ different disease resolution definitions, which makes proper comparison of the results challenging. Nevertheless, the variability in outcomes underscores that an optimal, universally acceptable treatment protocol has yet to be established.

The apparent paradox between improved radiographic outcomes and unchanged disease resolution invites reflection on what constitutes therapeutic success in peri‐implantitis treatment. Radiographic defect fill, particularly in the presence of a radiopaque xenograft, does not necessarily confirm true biological regeneration and may therefore overestimate structural recovery. Thus, while reconstructive therapy may improve radiographic appearance and soft‐tissue contour, radiographic findings alone do not allow definitive conclusions about the extent of true biological regeneration. Conversely, if genuine re‐osseointegration is achieved, reconstructive treatment may confer structural resilience with potential long‐term benefits, especially when etiological risk factors are effectively controlled. It should be noted that the absence of improved disease resolution at T12, combined with the higher frequency of adverse events in the test group, underscores the need to carefully balance potential radiographic and soft‐tissue benefits against increased treatment burden.

This study offers several methodological strengths. It included a substantial sample size and employed a rigorous, randomised and single‐blind design, thereby minimising selection and detection bias and enhancing internal validity. The protocol aligned with contemporary diagnostic standards and therapeutic frameworks, enhancing the clinical relevance and generalisability of the results. High participant retention further minimised attrition‐related bias. There are also limitations that should be addressed. First, the study population included both dentate and edentulous patients. Other than the variability in dentate and edentulous prosthetic rehabilitation, a difference in oral microbiota can be expected (de Waal et al. 2014). Second, the 1‐year follow‐up period may be considered insufficient to fully assess the long‐term stability of clinical and radiographic outcomes, as a minimum follow‐up of 3 years is often recommended to better evaluate recurrence and long‐term efficacy (Carcuac et al. 2020; Lee et al. 2021; Serino et al. 2021). Third, the heterogeneity of reconstructive materials, techniques and decontamination protocols reported in the literature limits comparability and precludes definitive conclusions regarding the superiority of any specific approach (Serino et al. 2017). Furthermore, the first clinical re‐evaluation was performed after 3 months of healing. This relatively early assessment may have captured transient tissue conditions before complete maturation, potentially underestimating the eventual stability of the surgical outcomes. Also, the investigator could potentially recognise the presence of the bone substitute material on the radiographic images owing to its inherent radiopacity, which may have introduced detection bias despite standardised and calibrated assessment procedures. No validated patient‐reported outcome (PRO) instruments (e.g., OHIP‐14) were administered, which limits insight into patient‐centred outcomes, particularly in relation to potential aesthetic changes.

Lastly, the implant surface decontamination protocol in this study included the use of an air‐polisher, which is not fully aligned with current clinical recommendations, potentially affecting both the reproducibility and contemporary clinical relevance of the treatment protocol (Herrera et al. 2023).

In summary, the surgical treatment of peri‐implantitis performed in this study resulted in significant improvements in clinical and radiographic outcomes over a 1‐year period. In addition, reconstructive therapy was associated with improved radiographic MBLs and reduced mid‐buccal soft‐tissue recession. These findings indicate that while both surgical approaches are effective in managing peri‐implantitis, the adjunctive use of bone reconstruction may provide additional benefits in maintaining peri‐implant tissue architecture.

Materials and Methods

Peri‐implant evaluations were performed prior to non‐surgical intervention (Tpre), at baseline (T0, 3 months post NSI), and at 3, 6, 9 and 12 months post‐surgery (T3–T12). Full‐mouth periodontal charts were recorded at Tpre, T0 and T12. A single experienced examiner (J.H.), blinded to allocation, conducted all assessments. Measurements at six sites per tooth and implant included BoP, plaque and SoP (binary) using a Hu‐Friedy PCPUNC156 probe; PPD was measured in millimetres. Mid‐buccal recession was assessed using a partial vinyl polysiloxane (VPS) impression as a fixed reference, with vertical measurements from mucosa to the VPS margin. For overdentures, top of the suprastructure served as reference. During surgery, peri‐implant bone defects were classified at four sites using the implant‐abutment platform based on the classification by Monje et al. (2019). Standardised radiographs were taken at T0, T3, T6 and T12 with individualised holders and the paralleling technique (Planmeca Intra X‐ray unit, Planmeca, Helsinki, Finland). Panoramic imaging was used when periapical views were not possible. Bone‐level changes were measured mesially and distally using the DICOM software (DicomWorks 1.5) using a three‐point reference scale based on implant dimensions. For bone‐level implants, the outer edges of the connection plateau were reference points; tissue‐level implants required correction for the smooth transgingival segment. Changes in MBL were calculated from measurements at the deepest defect site per implant.
