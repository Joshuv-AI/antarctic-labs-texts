Population dynamics under random switching
Alexandru Hening, Siddharth Sabharwal

Abstract
. Populations interact non-linearly and are influenced by environmental fluctuations. In order to have realistic mathematical models, one needs to take into account that the environmental fluctuations are inherently stochastic. Often, environmental stochasticity is modeled by systems of stochastic differential equations. However, this type of stochasticity is not always the best suited for ecological modeling. Instead, biological systems can be modeled using piecewise deterministic Markov processes (PDMP). For a PDMP the process follows the flow of a system of ordinary differential equations for a random time, after which the environment switches to a different state, where the dynamics is given by a different system of differential equations. Then this is repeated. The current paper is devoted to the study of the dynamics of n n populations described by n n -dimensional Kolmogorov PDMP. We provide sharp conditions for persistence and extinction, based on the invasion rates (Lyapunov exponents) of the ergodic probability measures supported on the boundary of the positive orthant ∂ ℝ + n , ∘ \partial\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n,\circ} . In order to showcase the applicability of our results, we apply the theory in some interesting ecological examples.

1.
Introduction

It is almost never the case that a species is isolated and does not get to interact with other species in its ecosystem. As such, one of the fundamental concerns of ecology is trying to see when interacting species coexist or, if coexistence of all species is not possible, determining exactly which species persist and which go extinct.

One cannot ignore the effects of the environment on species interactions. Given that the environmental conditions fluctuate, usually in a random and unpredictable fashion, it is key to have robust mathematical models that can include both random environmental fluctuations and the nonlinear intraspecies and interspecies interactions.

There are examples when biotic effects can result in species going extinct and the effects of random environmental fluctuations lead to a reversal of extinction into coexistence. In other situations, deterministic systems in which all species persist lead to extinctions when one takes into account environmental stochasticity. Recently, there have been important results and breakthroughs by modeling the dynamics using discrete or continuous time Markov processes and analyzing their asymptotic behavior (
Chesson 2000
;
Evans et al. 2013
;
Evans et al. 2015
;
Lande et al. 2003
;
Schreiber and Lloyd-Smith 2009
;
Schreiber et al. 2011
;
Blath et al. 2007
;
Benaïm and Schreiber 2009
;
Benaïm et al. 2008
;
Cattiaux and Méléard 2010
;
Cattiaux et al. 2009
;
Foldes and Stacy 2024
;
Benaïm 2023
).

One way of analyzing the coexistence of species is by computing the average per-capita growth rate of a population when rare. If this growth rate is positive the respective population tends to increase when rare, while if it is negative the population tends to decrease and goes extinct. If the ecosystem consists of only two populations then coexistence is ensured if each population can invade when it is rare and the other population is stationary (
Turelli 1977
;
Chesson and Ellner 1989
;
Evans et al. 2015
).

There is a well established general theory for coexistence for deterministic models (
Hofbauer 1981
;
Hutson 1984
;
Hofbauer and So 1989
). Starting with the important work
Hofbauer 1981
it has been shown that a sufficient condition for persistence is the existence of a fixed set of weights associated with the interacting populations such that this weighted combination of the population’s invasion rates is positive for any invariant measure supported by the boundary.

Recently there has been a flurry of papers studying the effects of environmental stochasticity on continuous-time SDE models. In
Benaïm et al. 2008
the authors found that if a deterministic continuous-time model satisfies the above persistence criterion then under some weak assumptions the corresponding stochastic differential equation with a small diffusion term has a positive stationary distribution concentrated on the positive global attractor of the deterministic system. For general stochastic differential equations with arbitrary levels of noise sufficient conditions for persistence and extinction can be found in
Schreiber et al. 2011
;
Hening and Nguyen 2018
;
Hening et al. 2021
;
Hening et al. 2022
;
Benaïm 2023
;
Foldes and Stacy 2024
.

The aim of this paper is to prove similar results for piecewise deterministic Markov processes (PDMP). Some partial results have been obtained for simple models with two or three species (
Benaïm and Lobry 2016
;
Hening and Strickler 2019
;
Hening and Nguyen 2020
;
Hening et al. 2023
). However, in these cases there are only two or three ergodic invariant probability measures on the boundary and as such the analysis is simpler. General persistence results for compact state spaces can be found in
Benaïm 2023
. In this paper we prove persistence and extinction results for compact and non-compact state spaces and therefore provide a significant advancement in the field of stochastic population dynamics.

Piecewise deterministic Markov processes have been used recently to prove some very interesting facts about biological populations. For example in
Benaïm and Lobry 2016
;
Hening and Nguyen 2020
the authors look at a two dimensional Lotka-Volterra system in a fluctuating environment. They show that the random switching between two environments that are both favorable to the same species can lead to the extinction of this favored species or to the coexistence of the two competing species. Our analysis generalizes the framework of
Benaïm and Lobry 2016
to higher-dimensional nonlinear systems that do not necessarily live in a compact set.

The paper is organized as follows. In section
2
we present our framework, the problems we study, the different assumptions and the main results. In Section
3
we showcase some interesting examples.
In Section
4
we prove some preliminary results regarding the process. For example, we show that the process is Markov-Feller, that, that there is a well-defined strong solution
(\mathbf{X}(t),r(t))
for all
t>0
, that this solution is pathwise unique and that there is tightness for certain families of (random) measures. Section
5
is devoted to the study of conditions under which
(\mathbf{X}(t),r(t))
exhibits persistence. In particular, we prove when the process converges to its unique invariant probability measure on
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n,\circ}\times\mathcal{N}
where
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n,\circ}:=(0,\infty)^{n}
and
\mathcal{N}=\{1,\dots,n_{0}\}
. In Theorem
5.1
we show that, under certain assumptions, the transition probability of
(\mathbf{X}(t),r(t))
converges in total variation to the a unique invariant probability measure of
(\mathbf{X}(t),r(t))
on
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n,\circ}\times\mathcal{N}
exponentially fast. In Section
6
we analyze when one or multiple species go extinct. First, we show in Theorem
6.1
that if there exists an invariant probability measure living on the boundary that is an
attractor
, and the boundary is accessible, then the process converges almost surely to the boundary in a weak sense. If we do not know that the boundary is accessible, we prove in Theorem
6.2
that the probability of converging exponentially fast to an attracting subspace of the boundary can be made arbitrarily close to one by starting the process close enough to that subspace. Under a few extra assumptions we show in Theorem
6.3
that for every accessible attractor
\mu
on the boundary the process converges with strictly positive probability to
\mu
.
Proofs of certain lemmas from Section
4
and Section
6
appear in Appendix
A
and Appendix
B
.

We want to mention that this work is indebted to
Benaïm 2023
which has general results for stochastic persistence, without treating the extinction cases, and looks in depth at PDMP in compact state spaces. During the writing of this paper we also became aware of the nice extinction results from
Foldes and Stacy 2024
, which make use of a different approach.

2.
Piecewise Deterministic Markov Processes

In order to take into account environmental fluctuations and their effect on the persistence or extinction of species, one approach is to study the uniform persistence for non-autonomous differential equations
(
Thieme 2000
;
Smith and Thieme 2011
;
Mierczyński et al. 2004
)
. A different approach is to consider systems that have random environmental perturbations. One way to do this is by studying stochastic differential equations
(
Schreiber et al. 2011
;
Hening and Nguyen 2018
;
Hening et al. 2021
;
Hening et al. 2022
)
. Another possibility is to look at stochastic equations driven by a Markov chain. These are piecewise deterministic Markov processes of the form

(2.1)

\frac{dX_{i}}{dt}(t)=X_{i}(t)f_{i}(\mathbf{X}(t),r(t)),i=1,\dots,n

where
r(t)
is a cadlag process taking values in a finite state space

\mathcal{N}=\{1,\dots,n_{0}\}.
Here
\mathbf{X}(t):=(X_{1}(t),\dots,X_{n}(t))
are the species densities at time
t\geq 0
and
f_{i}(\mathbf{x},k)
quantifies the fitness of species
i
when the densities are
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
and the environmental state is
k
.

The switching intensity of
r(t)
can depend on the state of
\mathbf{X}(t)
and is given by

(2.2)

\begin{array}[]{ll}&\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\{r(t+\Delta)=j~|~r(t)=i,\mathbf{X}(s),r(s),s\leq t\}=q_{ij}(\mathbf{X}(t))\Delta+o(\Delta)\text{ if }i\neq j\ \hbox{ and }\\
&\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\{r(t+\Delta)=i~|~r(t)=i,\mathbf{X}(s),r(s),s\leq t\}=(1+q_{ii}(\mathbf{X}(t))\Delta+o(\Delta).\end{array}

The above equation says that the probability that the process
r(t)
jumps from
r(t)=i
to the state
j\neq i
in the small time
\Delta
is approximately equal to
q_{ij}(\mathbf{X}(t))\Delta
.

Here
q_{ii}(\mathbf{x}):=-\sum_{j\neq i}q_{ij}(\mathbf{x})
quantifies the probability of staying in the same state
i
. We will assume that the function
q_{ij}(\mathbf{x},k)
is a bounded continuous function for each
i,j\in\mathcal{N}

and
Q(\mathbf{x})=(q_{ij}(\mathbf{x}))_{n_{0}\times n_{0}}
is irreducible for all
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}
.

It is well-known
(
Davis 1984
;
Benaïm et al. 2015
;
Benaïm 2023
)
that a process
(\mathbf{X}(t),r(t))
satisfying (
2.1
) and (
2.2
)
is a Markov process with generator acting on functions
G:\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}\times\mathcal{N}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
that are continuously differentiable in
\mathbf{x}
for each
k\in\mathcal{N}
as

(2.3)

\mathcal{L}G(\mathbf{x},k)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}f_{i}(\mathbf{x},k)\frac{\partial G}{\partial x_{i}}(\mathbf{x},k)+\sum_{l\in\mathcal{N}}q_{kl}(\mathbf{x})G(\mathbf{x},l).

Important theoretical work analyzing PDMP has been done recently. Under various irreducibility and Hörmander type conditions
Bakhtin and Hurth 2012
;
Benaïm et al. 2015
have found principles under which there exists a unique invariant probability measure for certain PDMP. The convergence in total variation, at an exponential rate, to this unique invariant measure has been shown when the state space is compact
(
Benaïm et al. 2015
)
and in the one-dimensional case there are results about the smoothness of the density of the invariant measure
(
Bakhtin et al. 2018
)
. If there is no irreducibility, one can use an associated deterministic control system to find the support of invariant probability measures
(
Benaïm et al. 2017
;
Benaïm 2023
)
. The asymptotic behavior of a PDMP his not determined just by the vector fields
f_{i}(\mathbf{x},k)
, but can drastically change according to the switching rates
q_{ij}(\mathbf{x})
. There are many interesting examples of how switching leads to rich, and sometimes counterintuitive, dynamical behavior
(
Benaïm et al. 2015
;
Benaïm and Lobry 2016
;
Hening and Strickler 2019
;
Hening and Nguyen 2020
;
Benaïm and Strickler 2019
)
.

We are interested in proving persistence and extinction results for the system (
2.1
). In order to accomplish this we modify and extend the methods from
Hening and Nguyen 2018
;
Hening et al. 2021
, which treated stochastic differential equations, while also making use of results about PDMP
Benaïm et al. 2015
and stochastic persistence
Benaïm 2023
. We have not used the methods from
Foldes and Stacy 2024
, an approach that could also yield extinction results for PDMP.

Let

\mathcal{S}_{0}:=\{(\mathbf{x},k)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}\times\mathcal{N}~:~\min_{i}x_{i}=0\}

be the extinction set and for
\eta>0
define the
\eta
-neighborhood of the extinction set

\mathcal{S}_{\eta}:=\{(\mathbf{x},k)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}\times\mathcal{N}~:~\min_{i}x_{i}\leq\eta\}.

We will denote by
\mathcal{S}_{\eta}^{c}
the complement of
\mathcal{S}_{\eta}
in
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}
. We also define
\partial\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}:=\{~\min_{i}x_{i}=0\}
so that
\mathcal{S}_{0}=\partial\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}\times\mathcal{N}
.

We next explain what we mean by persistence and extinction in our setting. The first definition of persistence, due to
Chesson 1982
, says that the probability that species are uniformly bounded away from extinction,
X_{i}\geq\delta
, as time increases, can be arbitrarily close to 1 in the sense that no matter how small one picks
\varepsilon>0
, the probability can be made greater or equal to
1-\varepsilon
. Moreover, the bound
\delta=\delta(\varepsilon)>0
does not depend on the initial conditions.

Definition 2.1
.

The process
\mathbf{X}
is stochastically persistent in probability if for any
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
\delta>0
such that

(2.4)

\liminf\limits_{t\to\infty}\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{x},k}\left\{X_{i}(t)\geq\delta,i=1,\dots,n\right\}>1-\varepsilon,\;\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n,\circ}_{+}.

For the next definition of persistence we will use the randomized occupation measures

\widetilde{\Pi}_{t}(\cdot,k):=\dfrac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{t}\boldsymbol{1}_{\{\mathbf{X}(s)\in\cdot,r(s)=k\}}ds,\,\,k\in\mathcal{N},t>0.

If
A
is a measurable set then
\widetilde{\Pi}_{t}(A,k)
measures the fraction of the time
\mathbf{X}(t)
spends in the set
A
and in environment
k
up to time
t
.

Definition 2.2
.

The process
(\mathbf{X}(t),r(t))
is almost surely stochastically persistent if for all
\varepsilon>0
there exists
\delta>0
such that with probability one

\liminf_{t\to\infty}\widetilde{\Pi}_{t}(\mathcal{S}_{\delta}^{c})>1-\varepsilon.

This definition says that the fraction of time spent by all species densities above the threshold
\delta
converges to
1
as
\delta\to 0
. We next look at extinction. First, note that extinction can only happen asymptotically as
t\to\infty
in our setting as for any
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
with
x_{i}>0,i=1,\dots,n
we will have

\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{x},k}\left\{X_{i}(t)>0,t\geq 0\right\}=1.

See Lemma
4.1
for a proof of this fact.

Definition 2.3
.

If
(\mathbf{X}(0),r(0))=(\mathbf{x},k)\in\mathbb{R}^{n,\circ}_{+}\times\mathcal{N}
we say the population
X_{i}
goes extinct with probability
p_{\mathbf{x},k}>0
if

\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{x},k}\left\{\lim_{t\to\infty}X_{i}(t)=0\right\}=p_{\mathbf{x},k}.

We say the population
X_{i}
goes extinct if for all
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n,\circ}_{+},k\in\mathcal{N}

\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{x},k}\left\{\lim_{t\to\infty}X_{i}(t)=0\right\}=1.

We will make the following assumptions throughout the paper.

Assumption 2.1
.

The transition rate
q_{ij}(\mathbf{x})
is a bounded continuous function for each
i,j\in\mathcal{N}
and
Q(\mathbf{x})=(q_{ij}(\mathbf{x}))_{n_{0}\times n_{0}}
is irreducible for all
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}
.
The functions
f_{i}(\cdot):\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}\to\mathbb{R},i=1,\dots,n
are locally Lipschitz and there is a function
F(\mathbf{x},k):\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathcal{N}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
that is continuously differentiable in
\mathbf{x}
for each
k\in\mathcal{N}
. Moreover, there exist constants
c,\delta_{0},\gamma_{b}>0
such that for every
k\in\mathcal{N}

(2.5)

F(\mathbf{x},k)\geq c(1+\|\mathbf{x}\|),~\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n,\circ},

and

(2.6)

\limsup_{\|\mathbf{x}\|\to\infty}\left[\frac{\mathcal{L}F(\mathbf{x},k)}{F(\mathbf{x},k)}+\gamma_{b}+\gamma_{b}\left(\max_{i}\{|f_{i}(\mathbf{x},k)|\}\right)\right]<0.

Remark 2.1
.

Note that (
2.6
) is equivalent to the weaker condition

(2.7)

\limsup_{\|\mathbf{x}\|\to\infty}\left[\frac{\mathcal{L}F(\mathbf{x},k)}{F(\mathbf{x},k)}+\delta_{0}\left(\max_{i}\{|f_{i}(\mathbf{x},k)|\}\right)\right]<0.

Remark 2.2
.

We note that by the continuity of
F
we can extend (
2.5
) to hold in any subspace. If we take, for example,
\mathbf{x}=(\overline{\mathbf{x}},\mathbf{x}_{n})
, let
x_{n}>0
and
\overline{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1,\circ}_{+}

then since we have for all
\mathbf{x}_{n}>0

\frac{F(\overline{\mathbf{x}},\mathbf{x}_{n},k)}{(1+\|(\overline{\mathbf{x}},\mathbf{x}_{n})\|)}\geq c

by letting
\mathbf{x}_{n}\to 0
and using the continuity of the left-hand side we will get that (
2.5
) holds on
\mathbb{R}^{n-1,\circ}\times\{0\}
.

Remark 2.3
.

The growth assumption (
2.5
) can be relaxed to being able to find some
0<\overline{\delta}<1
such that

F(\mathbf{x},k)\geq c(1+\|\mathbf{x}\|)^{\overline{\delta}},~\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n,\circ}.

Remark 2.4
.

If we assume that there is
a vector
\mathbf{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{n,\circ}_{+}
such that
\mathcal{L}(1+\mathbf{c}^{T}\mathbf{x})=\sum_{i}c_{i}x_{i}f(x_{i},k)<0
when
\mathbf{x}
is sufficiently large for any
k\in\mathcal{N}
,
then the solution will eventually enter a compact set and never leave it.
In that case we would only work on a compact set and things would be easier. We note that this situation is satisfied in many biologically relevant examples.

Assumption
2.1
is such that for some states, the solution may go to infinity - the solution
\mathbf{X}
is tight without requiring
\mathbf{X}
to live in a compact set. See the examples from Subsections
3.3
and
3.4
.

We also need to enforce the following assumption.

Assumption 2.2
.

Assume that there exists
M_{F}>0
such that for all
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}
and all
k,l\in\mathcal{N}

\frac{F(\mathbf{x},k)}{F(\mathbf{x},l)}\leq M_{F}.

Remark 2.5
.

Note that the above also implies there is a strictly positive lower bound, as clearly we get that
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}
and all
k,l\in\mathcal{N}

0<\frac{1}{M_{F}}\leq\frac{F(\mathbf{x},k)}{F(\mathbf{x},l)}\leq M_{F}.

Remark 2.6
.

Assumption
2.2
ensures that we can use the strong law of large numbers for certain martingales in order to show that almost surely

\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{M(t)}{t}=0.

In specific problems and models weaker assumptions could be found. It is useful to keep in mind the results of
Liptser 1980
when trying to prove strong laws of large numbers for local martingales.

Since piecewise deterministic Markov processes can be quite degenerate, it is not always clear that every point from the state space or from the boundary is accessible nor that invariant probability measures always exist. We define certain concepts related to which states are accessible or reachable by the process, as well as to the existence of unique invariant probability measures in certain subspaces.

For any fixed
k\in\mathcal{N}
let
\Phi_{t}^{k}(\cdot)
be the flow associated with
the ODE

(2.8)

\frac{dX_{i}}{dt}(t)=X_{i}(t)f(X_{i}(t),k),i=1,\dots,n.

That is,
\Phi_{t}^{k}(\mathbf{x},k)

is the solution at time
t

of the ODE (
2.8
)
with initial value
\mathbf{X}(0)=\mathbf{x}
. Define the orbit set

\gamma_{+}(\mathbf{x},k)=\left\{\phi_{t_{n}}^{k_{m}}\circ\cdots\circ\phi_{t_{1}}^{k_{1}}(\mathbf{x},k):,m\in\mathbb{N},t_{l}\geq 0,k_{l}\in\mathcal{N}:l=1,\dots,m\right\}

and its closure by
\overline{\gamma}_{+}(\mathbf{x},k)
. The points from
\gamma^{+}(\mathbf{x},k)
are those which are reachable from a concatenation of paths that are solutions to ODEs of the form (
2.8
).
We denote the intersection of the closures of all the orbit sets by

(2.9)

\Gamma=\bigcap_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n,\circ}_{+}}\overline{\gamma_{+}(\mathbf{x},k)}.

These are the points which are reachable, or infinitely close to being reachable, using concatenations of paths from fixed environments, from all starting points
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n,\circ}
.
Let
\mathcal{M}
denote the set of ergodic invariant probability measures of
(\mathbf{X}(t),r(t))
whose support is contained in

\partial\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}\times\mathcal{N}
. The convex combinations of the ergodic measures will be invariant probability measures living on
\partial\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}\times\mathcal{N}
- this set will be denoted by
\Conv(\mathcal{M})
. Note that by the results from
Benaïm 2023
the number of ergodic measures,
|\mathcal{M}|
, is always finite.

For any species
i
define
\mathcal{S}^{i}:=\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}~:~x_{i}>0\}
to be the subset of the state space for which species
i
has a strictly positive density. Let
\mu
be an ergodic measure. Since
\mathcal{S}^{i}
is an invariant set, that is, if
\mathbf{X}(0)\in\mathcal{S}^{i}
then almost surely
\mathbf{X}(t)\in\mathcal{S}^{i},t\geq 0
, it follows that
\mu(\mathcal{S}^{i})\in\{0,1\}.
As a result one can define the species support of
\mu
by

(2.10)

I_{\mu}:=\{\{i_{1},\dots,i_{u}\}~:~\mu(\mathcal{S}^{i_{l}})=1,l=1,\dots,u\}.

Definition 2.4
.

For any invariant probability measure
\mu
we define the invasion rate of the
i
th species, also called the external Lyapunov exponent, by

(2.11)

\lambda_{i}(\mu):=\sum_{k\in\mathcal{N}}\int_{\partial\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}}f_{i}(\mathbf{x},k)\mu(d\mathbf{x},k)

Remark 2.7
.

The intuition behind an invasion rate is the following. By Itô’s formula we have

\frac{\ln X_{i}(t)}{t}=\frac{\ln X_{i}(0)}{t}+\frac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{t}f_{i}(\mathbf{X}(s),r(s))\,ds.

If
\mathbf{X}(t)
is close to the support of an ergodic invariant measure
\mu
for a long time, then by ergodicity and the Feller property the quantity

\frac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{t}f_{i}(\mathbf{X}(s),r(s))\,ds

can be approximated with the average with respect to
\mu

\lambda_{i}(\mu):=\sum_{k\in\mathcal{N}}\int_{\partial\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}}f_{i}(\mathbf{x},k)\mu(d\mathbf{x},k)

while for
t\to\infty

\frac{\ln X_{i}(0)}{t}\to 0.

One can also show that
\lambda_{i}(\mu)
will give us the long-term log-growth rate of
X_{i}(t)
if
\mathbf{X}(t)
is close to the support of
\mu
. This shows why
\lambda_{i}(\mu)
can be called the Lyapunov exponents of
\mu
.

We note in particular that by Lemma
4.3
below, the invasion rates are well-defined. The following assumption guarantees the persistence of all the species.

Assumption 2.3
.

For any
\mu\in\Conv(\mathcal{M})
,

\max_{i=1,\dots,n}\left\{\sum_{k\in\mathcal{N}}\int_{\partial\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}}f_{i}(\mathbf{x},k)\mu(d\mathbf{x},k)\right\}>0.

Remark 2.8
.

Loosely speaking this condition ensures that the boundary
\partial\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}
will repel the process, or that all the invariant probability measures living on the boundary will be repelers. Biologically this means that for any invariant measure living on the boundary there is at least one species
i
which has a positive invasion rate.

The minmax principle shows
(
Schreiber et al. 2011
;
Benaïm 2023
)
that Assumption
2.3
is equivalent to the existence of positive weights
\mathbf{p}=(p_{1},\dots,p_{n})>0
such that

(2.12)

\min\limits_{\mu\in\mathcal{M}}\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}\lambda_{i}(\mu)\right\}>0.

This is very useful because Assumption
2.3
has to be checked for infinitely many measures, since usually
|\Conv(\mathcal{M})|=\infty
while (
2.12
) only has to be checked for finitely many ergodic measures since
|\mathcal{M}|<\infty.

Definition 2.5
.

We say that
\mathbf{x}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n,\circ}
is a Doeblin point if there exist a neighborhood
U
of
\mathbf{x}^{*}
, a non zero measure
\xi
on
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n,\circ}\times\mathcal{N}
, and
T>0
such that for all
(\mathbf{x},k)\in U
we have

(2.13)

P_{T}(\mathbf{x},k,\cdot)\geq\xi(\cdot),

where
P_{T}(\mathbf{x},k,\cdot)
is the transition probability function of the process
(\mathbf{X}(t),r(t))
.

If a Doeblin point exists one can show
(
Benaïm et al. 2015
;
Benaïm 2023
)
that there can be at most one invariant probability measure on
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n,\circ}\times\mathcal{N}
.

Let
G^{i}(\mathbf{x}):=(x_{1}f_{1}(\mathbf{x},i),\dots,x_{n}f_{n}(\mathbf{x},i))
and
\mathcal{F}_{0}(\mathbf{x})=\{G^{i}(\mathbf{x})-G^{j}(\mathbf{x}),i,j\in\mathcal{N}\}
and define recursively
\mathcal{F}_{k}(\mathbf{x})=\mathcal{F}_{k-1}(\mathbf{x})\cup\{[G^{i},V](\mathbf{x}),V\in\mathcal{F}_{k-1}(\mathbf{x}),j\in\mathcal{N}\}
where
[X,Y]
stands for the Lie bracket of two smooth vector vector fields

.

We say that (

If we want to make sure there is a stronger type of coexistence, in the sense that the system converges to a unique invariant probability measure where all species are present, we need the following technical assumption.

Assumption 2.4
.

The set
\Gamma
from (

there exists an

We can now state our main persistence result.

Theorem 2.1
.

Suppose that Assumptions
2.1
,
2.2
and

,

As a result,

is stochastically persistent in probability and almost surely stochastically persistent.

In addition, if Assumption

on
\mathbb{R}^{n,\circ}_{+}\times\mathcal{N}

For any initial value

and any

\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{x},k}\left\{\lim\limits_{T\to\infty}\dfrac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}f\left(\mathbf{X}(t),r(t)\right)dt=\sum_{k\in\mathcal{N}}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n,\circ}}f(\mathbf{u},k)\pi^{*}(d\mathbf{u},k)\right\}=1.

We next give some more definitions which are required for the extinction results.
For a subset

Note that for the case

,

Denote by

\mathcal{M}^{I},\mathcal{M}^{I,\circ},\partial M^{I}

\mathbb{R}_{+}^{I},\mathbb{R}_{+}^{I,\circ}

and

Consider

Next, we look at some conditions that are needed for the extinction results.

Assumption 2.5

There exists a subset

\max_{i\in I_{\pi}^{c},\;\pi\in\mathcal{M}^{I,\circ}}\{\lambda_{i}(\pi)\}<0.

Theorem 2.2
.

Suppose Assumptions
2.1
,
2.2

\mathbb{R}_{+}^{I}

where

Sometimes it is hard to prove that the boundary is accessible. The following theorem shows that even in that case, if we start close enough to the boundary we will converge exponentially fast to it with high probability.

Theorem 2.3
.

Suppose Assumptions

and

.

Let

We want to give more exact extinction results, so that we can say exactly which species persist and which go extinct. The following result says that if a subspace
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{I}
has only ergodic attractors

.

Suppose that Assumptions

,
2.2
,
2.5
and

\sum_{I\in S}P_{\mathbf{x},k}^{I}=1

is accessible from

.

If one ensures that subspaces have at most one interior attractor, we get the following extinction result as an immediate corollary.

.

,
2.2
,
2.5
and

3.
Applications

In this section we will showcase our general results by applying them in some interesting ecological models.

3.1.
Competitive Lotka-Volterra models

A common ecological two-species model is one in which two species compete for resources according to Lotka-Volterra functional responses
(
Wangersky 1978
)
. In our setting, this model is given by

(3.1)

\begin{split}\frac{dX_{1}}{dt}(t)&=X_{1}(t)[a_{1}(r(t))-b_{1}(r(t))X_{1}(t)-c_{1}(r(t))X_{2}(t)]\\
\frac{dX_{2}}{dt}(t)&=X_{2}(t)[a_{2}(r(t))-b_{2}(r(t))X_{2}(t)-c_{2}(r(t))X_{1}(t)].\end{split}

For simplicity we assume that the switching process
r(t)
takes values in
\mathcal{N}=\{1,2\}
, the switching rates are constant, and
r(t)
is irreducible. Here
a_{i}(k)
is the per-capita growth rate of species
i
in environment
k
,
b_{i}(k)
the intraspecific competition rate of species
i
in environment
k
and
c_{i}(k)
the interspecific competition rate of species
j
on species
i
in environment
k
.
Suppose that
a_{i}(k)>0,b_{i}(k)\geq 0,c_{i}(k)\geq 0
for
i,k=1,2
and that the switching intensities of
r(t)
, satisfy
q_{12}(\mathbf{x})>0
and
q_{21}(\mathbf{x})>0
for all

\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
.

One can easily check that
F(x,k)=\alpha(k)(1+x_{1}+x_{2})
, for
\alpha(k)>0
for
k\in\mathcal{N}
will satisfy Assumptions
2.1
and
2.2
so that our results can be applied.

Since at
\mathbf{x}=(0,0)
, we have

\lambda_{1}(\delta_{0})=\sum_{k=1}^{2}\int_{(0,0)}\left(a_{1}(k)-b_{1}(k)x_{1}-c_{1}(k)x_{2}\right)\,\delta_{0}(d\mathbf{x})=\sum_{k=1}^{2}a_{1}(k)>0

and

\lambda_{2}(\delta_{0})=\sum_{k=1}^{2}\int_{(0,0)}\left(a_{2}(k)-b_{2}(k)x_{1}-c_{2}(k)x_{2}\right)\,\delta_{0}(d\mathbf{x})=\sum_{k=1}^{2}a_{2}(k)>0

one can show by
Benaïm and Lobry 2016
, or by Theorem
2.1
, that there are invariant probability measures
\mu_{i},i=1,2
whose supports are contained in
\mathbb{R}^{\circ}_{i+}\times\mathcal{N}
,
i=1,2
where
\mathbb{R}^{\circ}_{i+}=\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}~|~x_{i}>0,x_{j}=0,j\neq i\}
.
Let

\lambda_{2}(\mu_{1})=\sum_{k=1}^{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{\circ}_{1+}}[a_{2}(k)-c_{2}(k)x_{1}]\mu_{1}(d\mathbf{x},k)

and

\lambda_{1}(\mu_{2})=\sum_{k=1}^{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{\circ}_{2+}}[a_{1}(k)-c_{1}(k)x_{2}]\mu_{2}(d\mathbf{x},k).

Note that these invasion rates can be computed explicitly but involve special functions
(
Benaïm and Lobry 2016
)
. We get the following classification of the long-term behavior of the process.

•

Suppose
\lambda_{1}(\mu_{2})<0,\lambda_{2}(\mu_{1})<0
. Then, using Theorem
2.5
, we have
p^{\mathbf{x},k}_{1}+p^{\mathbf{x},k}_{2}=1
where

p^{\mathbf{x},k}_{i}=\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{x},k}\left\{X_{i}\to\mu_{i},\lim_{t\to\infty}\dfrac{\ln X_{j}(t)}{t}=\lambda_{j}(\mu_{i}),j\in\{1,2\}\setminus\{i\}\right\}.

If
\overline{\gamma}_{+}(\mathbf{x})\cap\mathbb{R}^{\circ}_{i+}\neq\emptyset
then
p^{\mathbf{x},k}_{i}>0
.
Note that in some specific cases we can know when

\overline{\gamma}_{+}(\mathbf{x})\cap\mathbb{R}^{\circ}_{i+}\neq\emptyset

(see
Benaïm and Lobry 2016
;
Dang et al. 2014
).

•

Suppose
\lambda_{j}(\mu_{i})<0
for one
i\in\{1,2\}
and
\lambda_{i}(\mu_{j})>0:j\in\{1,2\}\setminus\{i\}
. Then, using Theorem
2.5
, we see that
X_{j}(t)
converges to
0
almost surely with the exponential rate
\lambda_{j}(\mu_{i})
for any initial condition
(\mathbf{x},k)\in\mathbb{R}^{2,\circ}_{+}\times\{1,2\}
. The occupation measure converges almost surely for all initial conditions
(\mathbf{x},k)\in\mathbb{R}^{2,\circ}_{+}\times\{1,2\}
to
\mu_{i}
.

•

Suppose
\lambda_{1}(\mu_{2})>0,\lambda_{2}(\mu_{1})>0
. Assume that at least one of the following two conditions holds,

i)

\dfrac{b_{1}(1)}{b_{2}(1)}\neq\dfrac{b_{1}(2)}{b_{2}(2)}

ii)

\dfrac{c_{1}(1)}{c_{2}(1)}\neq\dfrac{c_{1}(2)}{c_{2}(2)}.

It is shown in
(
Benaïm and Lobry 2016
, Theorem 4.1)
that
\Gamma\neq\emptyset
and there is a point
m_{0}\in\Gamma
satisfying the strong bracket condition.
By Theorem
2.2
, the transition probability of the process
(\mathbf{X}(t),r(t))
on
\mathbb{R}^{2,\circ}_{+}\times\mathcal{N}

converges exponentially fast to a unique invariant probability measure on
\mathbb{R}^{2,\circ}_{+}\times\mathcal{N}
in total variation.

The above conclusions coincide with the main results of
Benaïm and Lobry 2016
.

Remark 3.1
.

We can also recover and generalize the results from the more general setting of
Dang et al. 2014
where the authors consider

(3.2)

\begin{split}\frac{dX_{1}}{dt}(t)&=X_{1}(t)f_{1}(X_{1}(t),X_{2}(t),r(t))\\
\frac{dX_{2}}{dt}(t)&=X_{2}(t)g(X_{1}(t),X_{2}(t),r(t))\end{split}

under the assumptions that the process remains in a compact set and that for all
x_{1},x_{2}>0,k\in\mathcal{N}
one has
\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{1}}(x_{1},0,k)<0,\frac{\partial g}{\partial x_{1}}(0,x_{2},k)<0,f(0,0,k)>0,g(0,0,k)>0,\limsup_{x_{1}\to\infty}f(x_{1},0,k)<0,\limsup_{x_{2}\to\infty}g(0,x_{2},k)<0
.

3.2.
Predator-prey models

If instead we look at predator-prey systems of Lotka Volterra type
(
Lotka 1910
;
Volterra 1928
;
Wangersky 1978
)
, we get the system of the form

(3.3)

\begin{split}\frac{dX_{1}}{dt}(t)&=X_{1}(t)[a_{1}(r(t))-b_{1}(r(t))X_{1}(t)-c_{1}(r(t))X_{2}(t)]\\
\frac{dX_{2}}{dt}(t)&=X_{2}(t)[-a_{2}(r(t))-b_{2}(r(t))X_{2}(t)+c_{2}(r(t))X_{1}(t)]\end{split}

Here we assume that
r(t)\in\mathcal{N}=\{1,2\}
, and
a_{i}(k),b_{i}(k),c_{i}(k)>0
for
i=1,2
and
k\in\mathcal{N}
. The meaning of the coefficients is as follows:
a_{1}(k)
is the per-capita growth rate of the prey,
a_{2}(k)
is the per-capita death rate of the predator,
b_{1}(k),b_{2}(k)
are the intraspecific competition rates of prey and predators,
c_{1}(k)
is the attack rate of the predator on the prey and
c_{2}(k)
is the rate at which the predator makes use of the prey to sustain itself.

Remark 3.2
.

If we have
\frac{a_{1}(k)}{b_{1}(k)}\leq\frac{a_{2}(k)}{c_{2}(k)}
for at least one
k\in\mathcal{N}
, then accessibility of
(0,\infty)\times\{0\}
follows trivially, since at least one of the vector fields can access the set
(0,\infty)\times\mathcal{N}
.

Remark 3.3
.

Denote the vector fields for
k\in\{1,2\}
by

V(k)=\begin{bmatrix}x_{1}A_{1}^{k}\\
x_{2}A_{2}^{k}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}x_{1}\left(a_{1}(k)-b_{1}(k)x_{1}-c_{1}(k)x_{2}\right)\\
x_{2}\left(-a_{2}(k)-b_{2}(k)x_{2}+c_{2}(k)x_{1}\right).\end{bmatrix}

We have

V(1)-V(2)=\begin{bmatrix}x_{1}(A_{1}^{1}-A_{1}^{2})\\
x_{2}(A_{2}^{1}-A_{2}^{2}).\end{bmatrix}

Now computing the Lie Bracket we get

[V(1),V(1)-V(2)]=\begin{bmatrix}x_{1}^{2}\left(A_{1}^{1}b_{1}(2)-A_{1}^{2}b_{1}(1)\right)+x_{1}x_{2}\left(A_{2}^{1}c_{1}(2)-A_{2}^{2}c_{1}(1)\right)\\
x_{2}^{2}\left(A_{2}^{1}b_{2}(2)-A_{2}^{2}b_{2}(1)\right)+x_{1}x_{2}\left(A_{1}^{2}c_{1}(2)-A_{1}(1)c_{2}(2)\right).\end{bmatrix}

To verify that the vector fields
V(1)-V(2)
and
[V(1),V(1)-V(2)]
are linearly independent it is enough to verify that the determinant of the matrix

M=\begin{bmatrix}V(1)-V(2)&[V(1),V(1)-V(2)]\\
\end{bmatrix}

is nonzero. Note that

det(M)=Dx_{1}^{2}x_{2}+Ex_{1}x_{2}^{2}

where

(3.4)

\begin{split}D=&-c_{2}(2)\left[\left(A_{1}^{1}-\frac{A_{1}^{2}}{2}\right)^{2}+\left(\left(\frac{b_{1}(1)}{c_{2}(2)}\right)(A_{2}^{2}-A_{2}^{1})A_{1}^{2}-\left(\frac{A_{1}^{2}}{2}\right)^{2}\right)\right]\\
&-c_{2}(1)\left[\left(A^{2}_{1}-\frac{A_{1}^{1}}{2}\right)^{2}+\left(\left(\frac{b_{1}(2)}{c_{2}(1)}\right)(A_{2}^{1}-A_{2}^{2})A_{1}^{1}-\left(\frac{A_{1}^{1}}{2}\right)^{2}\right)\right]\\
E=&-c_{1}(2)\left[\left(A_{2}^{1}-\frac{A_{2}^{2}}{2}\right)^{2}+\left(\left(\frac{b_{2}(1)}{c_{1}(2)}\right)(A_{1}^{1}-A_{1}^{2})A_{2}^{2}-\left(\frac{A_{2}^{2}}{2}\right)^{2}\right)\right]\\
&-c_{1}(1)\left[\left(A_{2}^{2}-\frac{A_{2}^{1}}{2}\right)^{2}+\left(\left(\frac{b_{2}(2)}{c_{1}(1)}\right)(A_{1}^{2}-A_{1}^{1})A_{2}^{1}-\left(\frac{A_{2}^{1}}{2}\right)^{2}\right)\right].\end{split}

So unless
D=E=0
, we have
det(M)\neq 0
, at all points except possibly the straight line
Dx_{1}+Ex_{2}=0
.

Remark 3.4
.

If the fixed points of vector fields
V_{1}
, and
V_{2}
are
\mathbf{x}_{1}=(x_{11},x_{12})
and
\mathbf{x}_{2}=(x_{21},x_{22})
. Then if we have for some
i\in\{1,2\}

Dx_{i1}+Ex_{i2}\neq 0

and
(x_{i1},x_{i2})
is a globally attracting fixed point. Then there exists a fixed point which is globally attracting and the strong bracket condition holds.

Remark 3.5
.

Since the model describes predator prey dynamics, if there exists an invariant probability measure
\mu^{\prime}
on
\partial\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
, then
supp(\mu^{\prime})\subset(0,\infty)\times\{0\}
. Then Proposition 2.1 from
Benaïm and Lobry 2016
applies, so if
\frac{a_{1}(1)}{b_{1}(1)}\neq\frac{a_{1}(2)}{b_{1}(2)}
, then

(3.5)

\mu^{\prime}(dx,1)=h_{1}(x)dx=C(p_{1},p_{2},\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})\frac{p_{1}|x-p_{2}|^{\gamma_{2}}|p_{1}-x|^{\gamma_{1}-1}}{x^{1+\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2}}}dx

(3.6)

\mu^{\prime}(dx,2)=h_{2}(x)dx=C(p_{1},p_{2},\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})\frac{p_{2}|x-p_{2}|^{\gamma_{2}-1}|p_{1}-x|^{\gamma_{1}}}{x^{1+\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2}}}dx

where
p_{1}=\frac{a_{1}(1)}{b_{1}(1)}
,
p_{2}=\frac{a_{1}(2)}{b_{1}(2)}
,
\gamma_{1}=\frac{q_{12}}{a_{1}(1)}
and
\gamma_{2}=\frac{q_{21}}{a_{1}(2)}
, and
supp(\mu^{\prime})\in[p_{1},p_{2}]
if
p_{1}<p_{2}
.

Since the switching process
r(t)
is irreducible and aperiodic, there is a unique invariant distribution
\nu=(\nu_{1},\nu_{2})=\left(\frac{q_{21}}{q_{12}+q_{21}},\frac{q_{12}}{q_{12}+q_{21}}\right)

\mathcal{N}
. Hence we have an invariant measure supported on
\{(0,0)\}\times\mathcal{N}

\mu_{0}=\delta_{0}\times\nu
.

\lambda_{1}(\mu_{0})=\sum_{k=1}^{2}\int\left(a_{1}(k)-b_{1}(k)x_{1}-c_{1}(k)x_{2}\right)\mu_{0}(dx,k)=a_{1}(1)\left(\frac{q_{21}}{q_{12}+q_{21}}\right)+a_{1}(2)\left(\frac{q_{12}}{q_{12}+q_{21}}\right)>0

\lambda_{2}(\mu_{0})=\sum_{k=1}^{2}\int\left(-a_{2}(k)-b_{2}(k)x_{1}-c_{2}(k)x_{2}\right)\mu_{0}(dx,k)=-a_{2}(1)\left(\frac{q_{21}}{q_{12}+q_{21}}\right)-a_{2}(2)\left(\frac{q_{12}}{q_{12}+q_{21}}\right)<0

If we restrict our model to
\{0\}\times\mathbb{R}^{\circ}_{+}
. Then
\lambda_{2}(\mu_{0})<0
, then by Theorem
2.4
,
X_{2}(t)\to 0
a.s, as we expect since the predator dies out without the prey. There is no invariant measure supported on
\{0\}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\circ}
.

Now if we restrict our model to
\mathbb{R}^{\circ}_{+}\times\{0\}\times\mathcal{N}
. Since
\lambda_{1}(\mu_{0})>0
, then by Theorem

\mu_{1}

\lambda_{2}(\mu_{1})=(c_{2}(1)p-a_{2}(1))\left(\frac{q_{21}}{q_{12}+q_{21}}\right)+(c_{2}(2)p-a_{2}(2))\left(\frac{q_{12}}{q_{21}+q_{12}}\right).

If
\frac{a_{1}(1)}{b_{1}(1)}<\frac{a_{1}(2)}{b_{1}(2)}
, then from

Bakhtin et al. 2015
,
\mu_{1}
is absolutely continuous and given by a smooth density supported in
\left(\frac{a_{1}(1)}{b_{1}(1)},\frac{a_{1}(2)}{b_{1}(2)}\right)
, and vice-versa when
\frac{a_{1}(1)}{b_{1}(1)}>\frac{a_{1}(2)}{b_{1}(2)}
. We can find the density of
\mu_{1}
with respect to Lebesgue measure using
Benaïm and Lobry 2016
as in Remark
3.5
, and use it to compute
\lambda_{2}(\mu_{1})
as

(3.7)

\begin{split}\lambda_{2}(\mu_{1})=\int_{\frac{a_{1}(1)}{b_{1}(1)}}^{\frac{a_{1}(2)}{b_{1}(2)}}(c_{2}(1)x-a_{2}(1))h_{1}(x)dx+\int_{\frac{a_{1}(1)}{b_{1}(1)}}^{\frac{a_{1}(2)}{b_{1}(2)}}(c_{2}(2)x-a_{2}(2))h_{2}(x)dx\end{split}

Based on the sign of
\lambda_{2}(\mu_{1})
we have the following classification of the dynamics.

•

If
\lambda_{2}(\mu_{1})>0
then Assumption
2.3
is satisfied, and if in addition the conditions of Remark
3.3
and Remark
3.4
are satisfied then Assumption
2.4
also holds. This allows us to apply Theorem
2.1
and get that there is a unique invariant probability measure
\pi^{*}
in
\mathbb{R}^{2,\circ}_{+}
such that for any initial conditions
X_{1}(0),X_{2}(0),r(0))=(x_{1},x_{2},k)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2,\circ}\times\mathcal{N}
the transition probability of
(X_{1}(t),X_{2}(t),r(t))
, converges to
\pi^{*}
in total variation exponentially fast.

•

If we have instead
\lambda_{2}(\mu_{1})<0
then Assumptions
2.5
and
2.6
are satisfied. Hence by Theorem
2.2
, we see that for any initial conditions
X_{1}(0),X_{2}(0),r(0))=(x_{1},x_{2},k)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2,\circ}\times\mathcal{N}
one has
X_{2}(t)\to 0
and
X_{1}(t)\to\mu_{1}
almost surely.

Remark 3.6
.

The paper of
Du et al. 2014
studies two-species predator-prey models under switching dynamics in greater generality. They are able to show that the predator goes extinct if
\lambda_{2}(\mu_{1})<0
, without directly considering the accessiblity of the set
(0,\infty)\times\{0\}
.

3.3.
Single Species Dynamics- stability versus explosion.

In the following example, we have an isolated species that does not experience any competition in the environment
k=1
and grows (or decays) linearly while it can possibly experience intra-competition in the environment
k=2
.
Suppose the dynamics is given by

(3.8)

\frac{dX}{dt}(t)=X(t)f(X(t),r(t))

with

f(x,k)=\begin{cases}\displaystyle a_{1}(1)&\text{if }k=1\\[7.22743pt]
\displaystyle a_{1}(2)-b_{1}(2)x&\text{if }k=2.\end{cases}

We define the switching rate matrix
Q(x)=(q_{ij}(x))_{2\times 2}
, where
q_{12}(x),q_{21}(x)>0
,
a_{1}(1),b_{1}(2)>0
, and
a_{1}(2)\neq 0
.
Additionally, assume that there are constants
C,c>0
such that for all
x>0
we have

0<c\leq\min\{q_{12}(x),q_{21}(x)\},

and

\max\{q_{12}(x),q_{21}(x)\}\leq C<\infty.

Remark 3.7
.

The dynamics of this model is not restricted to any compact subset of
\mathbb{R}_{+}
. Since the process
r(t)\in\mathcal{N}
is an irreducible Markov chain then if
r(0)\neq 1
, then
r(T)=1
for some
T>0
, and since the waiting time in the state
r(t)=1
is exponentially distributed with mean at least
\frac{1}{C}
, and since
X(t)
grows exponentially when
r(t)=1
, the process escapes any compact set with positive probability.

Define the function

F(x,k)=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\alpha(1+\sqrt{x})&\text{if }k=1\\[7.22743pt]
\displaystyle\alpha\beta(1+\sqrt{x})&\text{if }k=2\end{cases}

for constants
\alpha>0
, and
\beta\in(0,1)
. If we have the condition
\displaystyle c-\frac{a_{1}(1)}{2}>0
, then we have for
k=1
that

(3.9)

\begin{split}\limsup_{x\to\infty}&\left[\frac{\mathcal{L}F(x,1)}{F(x,1)}+\delta_{0}\left(\max_{i}\{|f_{i}(\mathbf{x},1)|\}\right)\right]\\
=&\limsup_{x\to\infty}\frac{\left(xa_{1}(1)\left(\frac{\alpha}{2\sqrt{x}}\right)+q_{12}(x)\alpha(\beta-1)(1+\sqrt{x})+\delta_{0}a_{1}(1)\alpha(1+\sqrt{x})\right)}{\alpha(1+\sqrt{x})}\\
\leq&\limsup\frac{\left(\left(\frac{a_{1}(1)}{2}+\delta_{0}a_{1}(1)+c(\beta-1)\right)\alpha\sqrt{x}-c\alpha(1-\beta)+\delta_{0}a_{1}(1)\alpha\right)}{\alpha(1+\sqrt{x})}.\end{split}

Since
\displaystyle c-\frac{a_{1}(1)}{2}>0
, for
\beta\in(0,1)
, and
\delta_{0}>0
, small enough, we get that

(3.10)

\begin{split}\left[\frac{\mathcal{L}F(x,1)}{F(x,1)}+\delta_{0}\left(\max_{i}\{|f_{i}(\mathbf{x},1)|\}\right)\right]<\left(\frac{a_{1}(1)}{2(1-\beta)}-c+2\delta_{0}\left(\frac{a_{1}(1)}{1-\beta}\right)\right)<0.\end{split}

For
k=2
, we have in similar fashion

(3.11)

\begin{split}&\limsup_{x\to\infty}\left[\frac{\mathcal{L}F(x,2)}{F(x,2)}+\delta_{0}\left(\max_{i}\{|f_{i}(x,2)|\}\right)\right]\\
&\leq\limsup_{x\to\infty}\frac{\left(\frac{\alpha a_{1}(2)\beta}{2}\sqrt{x}-\frac{\alpha\beta b_{1}(2)}{2}x^{3/2}+C(1-\beta)\alpha(1+\sqrt{x})+\delta_{0}(b_{1}(2)x-1)\alpha\beta(1+\sqrt{x})\right)}{\alpha\beta(1+\sqrt{x})}\\
&\leq\limsup_{x\to\infty}\frac{\left(\alpha\beta b_{1}(2)\left(\delta_{0}-\frac{1}{2}\right)x^{3/2}+\alpha\beta\frac{a_{1}(2)}{2}\sqrt{x}+C\alpha(1-\beta)(1+\sqrt{x})+\delta_{0}b_{1}(2)\alpha\beta x-\delta_{0}\alpha\beta(1+\sqrt{x})\right)}{\alpha\beta(1+\sqrt{x})}\end{split}

As a result, if
\delta_{0}
is small enough, it follows that

(3.12)

\limsup_{x\to\infty}\left[\frac{\mathcal{L}F(x,2)}{F(x,2)}+\delta_{0}\left(\max_{i}\{|f_{i}(x,2)|\}\right)\right]<0.

As a result we can see that Assumption
2.1
is satisfied, since (
2.7
) holds for
\delta_{0}
small enough. The function
F
is defined in such a way that Assumption
2.2
is satisfied trivially.

Remark 3.8
.

We can also consider the fully linear system with

f(x,k)=\begin{cases}\displaystyle a_{1}(1)&\text{if }k=1\\[7.22743pt]
\displaystyle a_{1}(2)&\text{if }k=2.\end{cases}

where
a_{1}(1)>0,a_{1}(2)<0
. This means that the species grows linearly in environment
k=1
and dies out (also linearly) in environment
k=1
. One can show that Assumption
2.1
follows if we have
\displaystyle c-\frac{a_{1}(1)}{2}>0
.

Remark 3.9
.

For this model it is easy to see that there are only two possible scenarios for the accessibility of
\{0\}
, one when
a_{1}(2)>0
, and the other when
a_{1}(2)<0
. If
a_{1}(2)>0
, then
\{0\}
is not accessible and
\Gamma=\left[\frac{a_{1}(2)}{b_{1}(2)},\infty\right)
. The accessibility of
\{0\}
follows trivially if
a_{1}(2)<0
, since then for any initial condition
X_{2}(0)>0
we have
X_{2}(t)\to 0
as
t\to\infty
in the fixed environment
k=2
and in this case
\Gamma=[0,\infty)
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Assume the switching rates are constant. If
\lambda(\delta_{0}\times\nu)>0
, then there exists a unique invariant measure
\mu
supported on
\Gamma^{\circ}\subset(0,\infty)
, such that
\mu
is absolutely continuous, with densities given by

(3.13)

\begin{split}\mu(dx,1)=&h_{1}(x)dx\\
\mu(dx,2)=&h_{2}(x)dx.\end{split}

Here
h_{1}(x),h_{2}(x)
are smooth functions supported on
\Gamma
. Moreover:

(1)

If
a_{1}(2)>0
then

(3.14)

\begin{split}h_{1}(x)=&C_{1}\frac{(b_{1}(2)x-a_{1}(2))^{\frac{q_{21}}{a_{1}(2)}}}{x^{1+\frac{q_{12}}{a_{1}(1)}+\frac{q_{21}}{a_{1}(2)}}}\\
h_{2}(x)=&C_{1}\frac{a_{1}(1)(b_{1}(2)x-a_{1}(2))^{\frac{q_{21}}{a_{1}(2)}-1}}{x^{1+\frac{q_{12}}{a_{1}(1)}+\frac{q_{21}}{a_{1}(2)}}}\end{split}

for
x>\frac{a_{1}(2)}{b_{1}(2)}
and zero otherwise. The constant
C_{1}
is determined by

\int_{\frac{a_{1}(2)}{b_{1}(2)}}^{\infty}(h_{1}(x)+h_{2}(x))\,dx=1

(2)

If
a_{1}(2)<0
then

(3.15)

\begin{split}h_{1}(x)=&C_{1}\frac{1}{x^{1+\frac{q_{21}}{|a_{1}(2)|}-\frac{q_{12}}{a_{1}(1)}}(b_{1}(2)x+a_{1}(2))^{\frac{q_{21}}{a_{1}(2)}}}\\
h_{2}(x)=&C_{1}\frac{a_{1}(1)}{x^{1+\frac{q_{21}}{|a_{1}(2)|}-\frac{q_{12}}{a_{1}(1)}}(b_{1}(2)x+a_{1}(2))^{1+\frac{q_{21}}{a_{1}(2)}}}\end{split}

for
x>0
and zero otherwise. The constant
C_{1}
is determined by

\int_{0}^{\infty}(h_{1}(x)+h_{2}(x))\,dx=1.

Remark 3.10
.

Note that if
a_{1}(2)>0
then it is always true that
\lambda(\delta_{0}\times\nu)>0
.

Proof.

The strong bracket condition holds trivially, so Assumption
2.4
holds, and we have by Theorem
2.1
that there exists a unique invariant measure
\mu\in(0,\infty)\times\mathcal{N}
Then by Proposition 3.17 from
Benaïm et al. 2015
the support of the measure should be
\Gamma^{\circ}
and by Theorem 1 from
Bakhtin and Hurth 2012

\mu
has a density with respect to Lebesgue measure. The result from
Bakhtin et al. 2015
shows the smoothness of the density.

Since
\mu
is an invariant distribution, we know that for any
\phi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Gamma^{\circ})
the following holds

\sum\int P_{t}\phi(x,k)\mu(dx,k)=\sum\int\phi(x,k)\mu(dx,k)

Hence
\displaystyle\sum\int\mathcal{L}\phi(x,k)\mu(dx,k)=0
for any
\phi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Gamma^{\circ})
. As a result, we have

(3.16)

\begin{split}0=&\int\left(xf(x,2)\frac{d\Psi(x,2)}{dx}+q_{21}\left(\Psi(x,1)-\Psi(x,2)\right)\right)h_{2}(x)dx\\
+&\int\left(xf(x,1)\frac{d\Psi(x,1)}{dx}+q_{12}\left(\Psi(x,2)-\Psi(x,1)\right)\right)h_{1}(x)dx.\end{split}

Next, taking
\Psi(x,2)=g(x)+c
for
g\in C_{c}^{\infty}(0,\infty)
,
c\in\mathbb{R}
and
\Psi(x,1)=0
, etc., we get the pair of equations

(3.17)

\begin{split}0=&\left((a_{1}(2)x-b_{1}(2)x^{2})h_{2}(x)\right)^{\prime}+q_{21}h_{2}(x)-q_{12}h_{1}(x)\\
0=&(a_{1}(1)xh_{1}(x))^{\prime}+q_{12}h_{1}(x)-q_{21}h_{2}(x)\end{split}

along with the condition

(3.18)

\int_{supp(\mu)}q_{12}h_{1}(x)-q_{21}h_{2}(x)dx=0.

Next, we differentiate two cases, according to the sign of
a_{1}(2).

Case I:

a_{1}(2)>0
.

From (
3.17
), we can see that

\left(b_{1}(2)x^{2}-a_{1}(2)x)h_{2}(x)\right)^{\prime}=(a_{1}(1)xh_{1}(x))^{\prime}

Then there exists
C^{*}\in\mathbb{R}
such that

(3.19)

\left(b_{1}(2)x^{2}-a_{1}(2)x\right)h_{2}(x)-a_{1}(1)xh_{1}(x)=C^{*}

Combining (
3.19
) and (
3.17
) we get a pair of linear equations.

(3.20)

\begin{split}&h_{1}^{\prime}(x)+\left(\frac{q_{12}+a_{1}(1)}{a_{1}(1)x}-\frac{q_{21}}{b_{1}(2)x^{2}-a_{1}(2)x}\right)h_{1}(x)=\frac{q_{21}C^{*}}{a_{1}(1)x^{2}(b_{1}(2)x-a_{1}(2))}\\
&h_{2}^{\prime}(x)+\left(\frac{2b_{1}(2)x-(q_{21}+a_{1}(2))}{b_{1}(2)x^{2}-a_{1}(2)x}+\frac{q_{12}}{a_{1}(1)x}\right)h_{2}(x)=\frac{q_{12}C^{*}}{a_{1}(1)x^{2}(b_{1}(2)x-a_{1}(2))}\end{split}

To get the solutions, we have the following integrating factors

(3.21)

\begin{split}I_{1}(x)=&x^{\frac{q_{12}+a_{1}(1)}{a_{1}(1)}+\frac{q_{21}}{a_{1}(2)}}\cdot(b_{1}(2)x-a_{1}(2))^{-\frac{q_{21}}{a_{1}(2)}}\\
I_{2}(x)=&x^{\frac{q_{12}}{a_{1}(1)}+\frac{q_{21}+a_{1}(2)}{a_{1}(2)}}\cdot(b_{1}(2)x-a_{1}(2))^{1-\frac{q_{21}}{a_{1}(2)}}.\end{split}

The general solutions will be given by

(3.22)

\begin{split}h_{1}(x)=&\frac{1}{I_{1}(x)}\left(\int\frac{q_{21}C^{*}I_{1}(x)}{a_{1}(1)x^{2}(b_{1}(2)x-a_{1}(2))}dx+C_{1}\right)\\
h_{2}(x)=&\frac{1}{I_{2}(x)}\left(\int\frac{q_{12}C^{*}I_{2}(x)}{a_{1}(1)x^{2}(b_{1}(2)x-a_{1}(2))}dx+C_{2}\right).\end{split}

Now taking
C^{*}=0
, we can see that,

h_{1}(x)=\frac{C_{1}}{x^{\gamma}(b_{1}(2)x-a_{1}(2))^{-\frac{q_{21}}{a_{1}(2)}}}

h_{2}(x)=\frac{C_{2}}{x^{\gamma}(b_{1}(2)x-a_{1}(2))^{1-\frac{q_{21}}{a_{1}(2)}}}

where
\gamma=1+\frac{q_{12}}{a_{1}(1)}+\frac{q_{21}}{a_{1}(2)}=1+\frac{\lambda(\delta_{0}\times\nu)(q_{12}+q_{21})}{a_{1}(1)a_{1}(2)}
. Since
\lambda(\delta_{0}\times\nu)>0
, we know that the solutions are integrable functions.

Now observe that for
C_{2}=C_{1}a_{1}(1)
equation (
3.19
) is satisfied. Now, we fix
C_{1}>0
, such that we have

\int_{\frac{a_{1}(2)}{b_{1}(2)}}^{\infty}h_{1}(x)dx+\int_{\frac{a_{1}(2)}{b_{1}(2)}}^{\infty}h_{2}(x)dx=1.

To verify this is a valid solution, we check that (
3.18
) holds.
The integrals become

(3.23)

\begin{split}\int_{\frac{a_{1}(2)}{b_{1}(2)}}^{\infty}h_{1}(x)dx=&C_{1}\frac{(b_{1}(2))^{\beta}}{(a_{1}(2))^{\beta-\alpha}}B(\alpha+1,\beta-\alpha)\\
\int_{\frac{a_{1}(2)}{b_{1}(2)}}^{\infty}h_{2}(x)dx=&C_{1}\frac{(b_{1}(2))^{\beta}a_{1}(1)}{(a_{1}(2))^{\beta-\alpha+1}}B(\alpha,\beta-\alpha+1)\end{split}

showing that (
3.18
) is verified. The density functions are therefore given by

(3.24)

\begin{split}h_{1}(x)=&C_{1}\frac{(b_{1}(2)x-a_{1}(2))^{\frac{q_{21}}{a_{1}(2)}}}{x^{1+\frac{q_{12}}{a_{1}(1)}+\frac{q_{21}}{a_{1}(2)}}}\\
h_{2}(x)=&C_{1}\frac{a_{1}(1)(b_{1}(2)x-a_{1}(2))^{\frac{q_{21}}{a_{1}(2)}-1}}{x^{1+\frac{q_{12}}{a_{1}(1)}+\frac{q_{21}}{a_{1}(2)}}}.\end{split}

Case II:

a_{1}(2)<0

For this case we assume that

(3.25)

\lambda(\delta_{0}\times\nu)(q_{12}+q_{21})=a_{1}(1)q_{21}-|a_{1}(2)|q_{12}>0.

The differential equations we get in this case, following the same ideas as in Case 1, are given by

(3.26)

\begin{split}&h_{1}^{\prime}(x)+\left(\frac{q_{12}+a_{1}(1)}{a_{1}(1)x}-\frac{q_{21}}{b_{1}(2)x^{2}+|a_{1}(2)|x}\right)h_{1}(x)=\frac{q_{21}C^{*}}{a_{1}(1)x^{2}(b_{1}(2)x+|a_{1}(2)|)}\\
&h_{2}^{\prime}(x)+\left(\frac{2b_{1}(2)x-(q_{21}-|a_{1}(2)|)}{b_{1}(2)x^{2}+|a_{1}(2)|x}+\frac{q_{12}}{a_{1}(1)x}\right)h_{2}(x)=\frac{q_{12}C^{*}}{a_{1}(1)x^{2}(b_{1}(2)x+|a_{1}(2))|}.\end{split}

To get the solutions, we have the following integrating factors

(3.27)

\begin{split}I_{1}(x)=&x^{\frac{q_{12}+a_{1}(1)}{a_{1}(1)}-\frac{q_{21}}{|a_{1}(2)|}}\cdot(b_{1}(2)x+|a_{1}(2)|)^{\frac{q_{21}}{|a_{1}(2)|}}\\
I_{2}(x)=&x^{\frac{q_{12}}{|a_{1}(1)|}+\frac{-q_{21}+|a_{1}(2)|}{|a_{1}(2)|}}\cdot(b_{1}(2)x+|a_{1}(2)|)^{1+\frac{q_{21}}{|a_{1}(2)|}}.\end{split}

The general solutions become

(3.28)

\begin{split}h_{1}(x)=&\frac{1}{I_{1}(x)}\left(\int\frac{q_{21}C^{*}I_{1}(x)}{a_{1}(1)x^{2}(b_{1}(2)x+|a_{1}(2)|)}dx+C_{1}\right)\\
h_{2}(x)=&\frac{1}{I_{2}(x)}\left(\int\frac{q_{12}C^{*}I_{2}(x)}{a_{1}(1)x^{2}(b_{1}(2)x+|a_{1}(2)|)}dx+C_{2}\right).\end{split}

Taking
C^{*}=0
, we have that

h_{1}(x)=\frac{C_{1}}{x^{\tau}(b_{1}(2)x+|a_{1}(2)|)^{\frac{q_{21}}{|a_{1}(2)|}}}

h_{2}(x)=\frac{C_{2}}{x^{\tau}(b_{1}(2)x+|a_{1}(2)|)^{1+\frac{q_{21}}{|a_{1}(2)|}}}

where
\tau=1-\left(\frac{q_{21}}{|a_{1}(2)|}-\frac{q{12}}{a_{1}(1)}\right)=1-\frac{\lambda(\delta_{0})(q_{12}+q_{21})}{a_{1}(1)|a_{1}(2)|}
.
We see that (
3.19
) holds if we take
C_{2}=a_{1}(1)C_{1}
.
Also since we have
\lambda(\delta_{0}\times\nu)>0
, we get that
\displaystyle\frac{\lambda(\delta_{0}\times\nu)(q_{12}+q_{21})}{a_{1}(1)q_{12}}=q_{21}-|a_{1}(2)|>0
, which implies

. As a result, due to (

), we get that

are integrable on
(0,\infty)
.

We find the constant
C_{1}>0
by setting

\int_{0}^{\infty}h_{1}(x)+\int_{0}^{\infty}h_{2}(x)dx=1.

Now since the functions
h_{1},h_{2}
have been defined precisely, we need to check (

) to verify that they are indeed the desired density functions.

(3.29)

\begin{split}\int_{0}^{\infty}h_{1}(x)dx=&C_{1}\frac{(b_{1}(2))^{\tau-1}}{(|a_{1}(2)|)^{\frac{q_{21}}{|a_{1}(2)|}+\tau-1}}B\left(\frac{q_{21}}{|a_{1}(2)|}-\frac{q_{12}}{a_{1}(1)},\frac{q_{12}}{a_{1}(1)}\right)\\

(3.30)

\begin{split}h_{1}(x)=&C_{1}\frac{1}{x^{1+\frac{q_{21}}{|a_{1}(2)|}-\frac{q_{12}}{a_{1}(1)}}(b_{1}(2)x+|a_{1}(2)|)^{\frac{q_{21}}{|a_{1}(2)|}}},x>0\\

∎

In this section we will prove some preliminary things about the process such as well-posedness, uniqueness, and tightness. The proof of the lemmas will be given in Appendix

5.
Persistence

This section is devoted to finding conditions under which the process
(\mathbf{X}(t),r(t))
exhibits persistence. In particular we show when the process converges to a unique invariant probability measure supported on
\mathbb{R}^{n,\circ}_{+}
.

We begin the section by giving an overview of the lemmas and propositions and by sketching the proof of the main results.

It is shown in
(
Schreiber et al. 2011
, Lemma 4)
by the minmax principle that Assumption
2.3
is equivalent to the existence of
\mathbf{p}>0
such that

(5.1)

\min\limits_{\mu\in\mathcal{M}}\left\{\sum_{i}p_{i}\lambda_{i}(\mu)\right\}:=2\rho^{*}>0.

By rescaling if necessary, we can assume that
\|\mathbf{p}\|=\delta_{0}
.

Idea of proof of Theorem
5.1
.

The main ingredient in proving persistence is showing that there are constants
\theta\in(0,1),\kappa\in(0,1),\tilde{K}>0,\overline{T}>0
such that there is a ‘contraction’ of the semigroup, in the sense that for all
(\mathbf{x},k)\in\mathbb{R}^{n,\circ}_{+}\times\mathcal{N}
one has

(5.2)

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x},k}V^{\theta}(\mathbf{X}(\overline{T}),r(\overline{T}))\leq\kappa V^{\theta}(\mathbf{x},k)+\tilde{K}

where

V^{\theta}(\mathbf{x},k)=(V(\mathbf{x},k))^{\theta}=\left(\dfrac{F(\mathbf{x},k)}{\prod_{i}x_{i}^{p_{i}}}\right)^{\theta}.

This is done by first proving an estimate of the form (
5.2
) in Proposition
5.1
when
\|\mathbf{x}\|\leq M
. To achieve this we look at

(5.3)

\ln V(\mathbf{X}(T),r(T))=\ln V(\mathbf{X}(0),r(0))+G(T)

where

(5.4)

\displaystyle G(T)=\int_{0}^{T}\Phi(\mathbf{X}(t),r(t))dt+M_{G}(T).

Note that it is key here to use the persistence Assumption
2.3
which tells us intuitively that the boundary
\partial\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}
, i.e. the invariant measures living on the boundary, is ‘repelling’.
Rewriting sumption
2.3
as

\min\limits_{\mu\in\mathcal{M}}\left\{\sum_{i}p_{i}\lambda_{i}(\mu)\right\}:=2\rho^{*}>0.

allows us to prove in Lemma
5.1
that for for any
T
large enough, and
\mathbf{x}\in\partial\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+},\|\mathbf{x}\|\leq M
one has

(5.5)

\dfrac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x},k}\Phi(\mathbf{X}(t),r(t))dt\leq-\rho^{*}.

This estimate ensures that the constant
\kappa
from (
5.2
) is sub-unitary.

Using Lemma
5.1
, and the fact that
M_{G}(t)
is an
L^{2}
martingale, together with Lemma
4.5
and the Feller property allows one to control
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x},k}G(T)
uniformly for
\|\mathbf{x}\|\leq M
close enough to the extinction boundary
\partial\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}
and for
T
in a compact interval. This will allow one to finish the proof of Proposition
5.1
.

Next, one can show that for large values of
\|\mathbf{x}\}
, i.e., for
\|\mathbf{x}\|>M
one has

\mathcal{L}V^{\theta}(\mathbf{x},k)\leq-\theta\gamma_{b}V^{\theta}(\mathbf{x},k).

This implies that there is a strong enough drift making the process return into compact sets. The strong Markov property allows us to show that (
5.2
) holds for
\|\mathbf{x}\|>M
. As a result of this and Proposition
5.1
we get that (
5.2
) for all
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n,\circ}
. Once this is established, one can prove the various persistence results. Note that as we approach
\partial\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}
or
\mathbf{x}\to\infty
we have

(5.6)

V^{\theta}(\mathbf{x},k)\to\infty,

which together with (
5.2
) allows the control at the boundary and at infinity. For example, by (
5.2
) one can show

\limsup_{t\to\infty}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x},k}V^{\theta}(\mathbf{X}(t),r(t))\leq\frac{\exp(\theta H\overline{T})}{1-\kappa}.

This with (
5.6
) will imply that for any
\varepsilon>0
there exists
\delta>0
such that for all
(\mathbf{x},k)\in\mathbb{R}^{n,\circ}_{+}\times\mathcal{N}

\liminf_{t\to\infty}\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{x},k}\{\min_{i}X_{i}(t)\geq\delta\}\geq 1-\varepsilon,

which shows persistence in probability.

If Assumption
2.4
holds one can show that the Markov chain
((\mathbf{X}(\ell\overline{T}),r(\ell\overline{T}))
is irreducible and aperiodic and that every subset of
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n,\circ}\times\mathcal{N}
is petite. This then allows to use the theory from

in order to show that
((\mathbf{X}(\ell\overline{T}),r(\ell\overline{T}))
converges to a unique invariant probability measure on
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n,\circ}\times\mathcal{N}

∎

Lemma 5.1
.

Suppose that Assumption
2.3
holds. Let
\mathbf{p}
and

be as in (

).
There exists a

such that, for any

,
\mathbf{x}\in\partial\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+},\|\mathbf{x}\|\leq M
one has

(5.7)

\dfrac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x},k}\Phi(\mathbf{X}(t),r(t))dt\leq-\rho^{*}

where

(5.8)

\displaystyle\Phi(\mathbf{x},k):=\dfrac{\mathcal{L}F(\mathbf{x},k)}{F(\mathbf{x},k)}-\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{N}}q_{k\ell}(\mathbf{x})\left(\frac{F(\mathbf{x},\ell)}{F(\mathbf{x},k)}-1-\ln\frac{F(\mathbf{x},\ell)}{F(\mathbf{x},k)}\right)-\sum_{i}p_{i}f_{i}(\mathbf{x},k).

Proof.

We argue by contradiction. Suppose that the conclusion of this lemma is not true.
Then, we can find
\mathbf{x}_{m}\in\partial\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+},\|\mathbf{x}_{m}\|\leq M

.

This section is devoted to the study of conditions under which some of the species will go extinct with positive probability.

We first give the intuition behind the proofs of some of the main results.

Intuition behind the proofs of Theorems

\langle M_{U},M_{U}\rangle_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}\sum_{j\neq r(s)}q_{r(s)j}(\mathbf{X}(s),r(s))\left|U_{i}^{\theta}(\mathbf{X}(s),j)-U_{i}^{\theta}(\mathbf{X}(s),r(s))\right|^{2}ds.

(6.15)

\begin{split}\langle M_{U},M_{U}\rangle_{t}\leq&\int_{0}^{t}\sum_{j\neq r(s)}q_{r(s)j}(\mathbf{X}(s),r(s))U_{i}^{2\theta}(\mathbf{X}(s),j)ds\\
+&\int_{0}^{t}\sum_{j\neq r(s)}q_{r(s)j}(\mathbf{X}(s),r(s))U_{i}^{2\theta}(\mathbf{X}(s),r(s))ds\end{split}

Then using
2.2
and the fact that

we get

\langle M_{U},M_{U}\rangle_{t}\leq\int_{0}^{t}\sum_{j\neq r(s)}q_{r(s)j}(\mathbf{X}(s),r(s))(1+M_{F})U_{i}^{2\theta}(\mathbf{X}(s),r(s)ds.

Since we know that

\max_{ij}\sup_{(\mathbf{x},k)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}\times\mathcal{N}}|q_{ij}(\mathbf{x})|\leq C_{0}

we get

(6.17)

\langle M_{U},M_{U}\rangle_{t}\leq C_{0}|\mathcal{N}|(1+M_{F})\int_{0}^{t}U_{i}^{2\theta}(\mathbf{X}(s),r(s))ds.

In view of the Burkholder-Davis-Gundy Inequality and (
6.17
), and denoting

\tilde{c}=\tilde{C}C_{0}|\mathcal{N}|(1+M_{F})

(6.18)

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x},k}\sup_{s\in[0,t]}|M_{U}(s)|^{2}\leq\tilde{C}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x},k}(\langle M_{U},M_{U}\rangle_{t})\leq\tilde{c}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x},k}\left(\int_{0}^{t}U_{i}^{2\theta}(\mathbf{X}(t),r(t))dt\right)

Since we know that

U_{i}^{\theta}(\mathbf{x},k)=(F(\mathbf{x},k))^{\theta}\left(\frac{\mathbf{X}_{i}^{\check{p}_{i}}}{\prod_{j\in I}\mathbf{X}_{j}^{\widehat{p}_{j}}}\right)

(6.19)

\begin{split}\mathcal{L}U_{i}^{\theta}(\mathbf{X}(t),r(t))=&\theta U_{i}^{\theta-1}(\mathbf{X}(t),r(t))\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathbf{X}_{k}(t)f_{k}(\mathbf{X}(t),r(t))\frac{\partial U_{i}}{\partial\mathbf{x}_{k}}(\mathbf{X}(t),r(t))\\
+&\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}}q_{r(s)j}(\mathbf{X}(t))U_{i}^{\theta}(\mathbf{X}(t),j)\end{split}

We simplify the above to

(6.20)

\begin{split}\mathcal{L}U_{i}^{\theta}(\mathbf{X}(t),r(t))=&\theta U_{i}^{\theta}(\mathbf{X}(t),r(t))\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{\mathbf{X}_{k}(t)f_{k}(\mathbf{X}(t),r(t))}{F(\mathbf{X}(t),r(t))}\frac{\partial F}{\partial x_{k}}(\mathbf{X}(t),r(t))\\
+&\theta U_{i}^{\theta}(\mathbf{X}(t),r(t))\left(\check{p}_{i}f_{i}(\mathbf{X}(t),r(t))-\sum_{j\in I}\widehat{p}_{j}f_{j}(\mathbf{X}(t),r(t))\right)\\
+&\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}}q_{r(s)j}(\mathbf{X}(t))U_{i}^{\theta}(\mathbf{X}(t),j)\end{split}

(6.21)

\begin{split}\mathcal{L}U_{i}^{\theta}(\mathbf{X}(t),r(t))=&\left(\frac{\mathbf{X}_{i}(t)^{\check{p}_{i}}}{\prod_{j\in I}\mathbf{X}_{j}(t)^{\widehat{p}_{j}}}\right)\left(\theta F^{\theta}(\mathbf{X}(t),r(t))\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{\mathbf{X}_{k}(t)f_{k}(\mathbf{X}(t),r(t))}{F(\mathbf{X}(t),r(t))}\frac{\partial F}{\partial x_{k}}(\mathbf{X}(t),r(t))\right)\\
+&\left(\frac{\mathbf{X}_{i}(t)^{\check{p}_{i}}}{\prod_{j\in I}\mathbf{X}_{j}(t)^{\widehat{p}_{j}}}\right)\left(\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}}q_{r(s)j}(\mathbf{X}(t))F^{\theta}(\mathbf{X}(t),j)\right)\\
+&\left(\frac{\mathbf{X}_{i}(t)^{\check{p}_{i}}}{\prod_{j\in I}\mathbf{X}_{j}(t)^{\widehat{p}_{j}}}\right)\theta F^{\theta}(\mathbf{X}(t),r(t))\left(\check{p}_{i}f_{i}(\mathbf{X}(t),r(t))-\sum_{j\in I}\widehat{p}_{j}f_{j}(\mathbf{X}(t),r(t))\right)\end{split}

We can simplify the above equation to

(6.22)

\begin{split}\mathcal{L}U_{i}^{\theta}(\mathbf{X}(t),r(t))=&\left(\frac{\mathbf{X}_{i}(t)^{\check{p}_{i}}}{\prod_{j\in I}\mathbf{X}_{j}(t)^{\widehat{p}_{j}}}\right)\mathcal{L}F^{\theta}(\mathbf{X}(t),r(t))\\
+&\theta U_{i}^{\theta}(\mathbf{X}(t),r(t)))\left(\check{p}_{i}f_{i}(\mathbf{X}(t),r(t))-\sum_{j\in I}\widehat{p}_{j}f_{j}(\mathbf{X}(t),r(t))\right)\end{split}

Using Dynkin’s Formula for
U_{i}^{\theta}(\mathbf{X}(t),r(t))
, we get that

(6.23)

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x},k}(U_{i}^{\theta}(\mathbf{X}(t),r(t)))=&U_{i}^{\theta}(\mathbf{x},k)+\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x},k}\left(\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{L}(U_{i}^{\theta}(\mathbf{X}(s).r(s))ds\right)\\
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x},k}(U_{i}^{\theta}(\mathbf{X}(t),r(t)))\leq&U_{i}^{\theta}(\mathbf{x},k)+H\theta\cdot\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x},k}\left(\int_{0}^{t}U^{\theta}_{i}(\mathbf{X}(s),r(s))ds\right)\\
\end{split}

Using Gronwall’s inequality, we get the first desired inequality

(6.24)

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x},k}(U_{i}^{\theta}(\mathbf{X}(t),r(t)))\leq&e^{H\theta t}U_{i}^{\theta}(\mathbf{x},k)\\
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x},k}\left(U_{\theta}(\mathbf{X}(t),r(t))\right)=&\sum_{i\in I^{c}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x},k}\left(U_{i}^{\theta}(\mathbf{X}(t),r(t))\right)\leq\sum_{i\in I^{c}}e^{H\theta t}U_{i}^{\theta}(\mathbf{x},k)=e^{H\theta t}U_{\theta}(\mathbf{x},k)\\
\end{split}

We write Ito’s Formula for
U_{i}^{2\theta}(\mathbf{X}(s),r(s))
,

(6.25)

\begin{split}U^{2\theta}_{i}(\mathbf{X}(t),r(t))=&U^{2\theta}_{i}(\mathbf{x},k)+\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{L}U_{i}^{2\theta}(\mathbf{X}(s),r(s))ds+M_{2U}(t)\\
\end{split}

Where

denotes the martingale

(6.26)

M_{2U}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\left(U_{i}^{2\theta}(\mathbf{X}(s),r(s)+h(\mathbf{X}(s),r(s),y))-U_{i}^{2\theta}(\mathbf{X}(s),r(s))\right)\tilde{N}(dy,ds)

Observe that we have the following identity

(6.27)

\begin{split}&\mathcal{L}(U_{i}^{2\theta}(\mathbf{X}(s),r(s)))-2U^{\theta}_{i}(\mathbf{X}(s),r(s))\mathcal{L}(U_{i}^{\theta}(\mathbf{X}(s),r(s)))\\
=&\sum_{j\neq r(s)}q_{r(s)j}(\mathbf{X}(s))|U_{i}^{\theta}(\mathbf{X}(s),j)-U_{i}^{\theta}(\mathbf{X}(s),r(s))|^{2}\end{split}

Hence as a result this gives us

(6.28)

\begin{split}&\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{L}(U_{i}^{2\theta}(\mathbf{X}(s),r(s)))ds=\int_{0}^{t}2U^{\theta}_{i}(\mathbf{X}(s),r(s))\mathcal{L}(U_{i}^{\theta}(\mathbf{X}(s),r(s)))ds+\langle M_{U},M_{U}\rangle_{t}\end{split}

Combining (
6.25
) and (
6.28
) we have

(6.29)

\begin{split}U^{2\theta}_{i}(\mathbf{X}(t),r(t))\leq&U^{2\theta}_{i}(\mathbf{x},k)+\int_{0}^{t}2U^{\theta}_{i}(\mathbf{X}(s),r(s))\mathcal{L}(U_{i}^{\theta}(\mathbf{X}(s),r(s)))ds+\langle M_{U},M_{U}\rangle_{t}\\
+&M_{2U}(t)\end{split}

We take supremum on both sides to get

(6.30)

\begin{split}\sup_{s\in[0,t]}U^{2\theta}_{i}(\mathbf{X}(s),r(s))\leq&U^{2\theta}_{i}(\mathbf{x},k)+2H\theta\cdot\int_{0}^{t}U^{2\theta}_{i}(\mathbf{X}(s),r(s))ds+\langle M_{U},M_{U}\rangle_{t}\\
+&\sup_{s\in[0,t]}M_{2U}(s)\end{split}

Using Burkholder-Davis-Gundy Inequality, we get

(6.31)

(6.32)

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x},k}\left(\sup_{s\in[0,t]}U^{2\theta}_{i}(\mathbf{X}(s),r(s))\right)\leq&U^{2\theta}_{i}(\mathbf{x},k)+2H\theta\cdot\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x},k}\left(\int_{0}^{t}U^{2\theta}_{i}(\mathbf{X}(s),r(s))ds\right)+\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x},k}\left(\langle M_{U},M_{U}\rangle_{t}\right)\\
+&\tilde{C}_{1}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x},k}\left(\left(\langle M_{2U},M_{2U}\rangle_{t}\right)^{1/2}\right)\end{split}

(6.33)

\langle M_{2U},M_{2U}\rangle_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\left|U_{i}^{2\theta}(\mathbf{X}(s),r(s)+h(\mathbf{X}(s),r(s),y))-U_{i}^{2\theta}(\mathbf{X}(s),r(s))\right|^{2}dyds

Now putting the above in (

)

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x},k}\left(\sup_{s\in[0,t]}U^{2\theta}_{i}(\mathbf{X}(s),r(s))\right)\leq&U^{2\theta}_{i}(\mathbf{x},k)+2H\theta\cdot\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x},k}\left(\int_{0}^{t}U^{2\theta}_{i}(\mathbf{X}(s),r(s))ds\right)+\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x},k}\left(\langle M_{U},M_{U}\rangle_{t}\right)\\

From (

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x},k}\left(\sup_{s\in[0,t]}U^{2\theta}_{i}(\mathbf{X}(s),r(s))\right)\leq 2U^{2\theta}_{i}(\mathbf{x},k)+H_{0}(\theta)\cdot\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x},k}\left(\int_{0}^{t}\left(\sup_{s\in[0,t]}U^{2\theta}_{i}(\mathbf{X}(s),r(s))\right)ds\right).

then by Gronwall’s ineqaulity,

\end{split}

Now take

This proves the desired inequalities.
