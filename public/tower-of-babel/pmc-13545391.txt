Functionalized bio-patches for intra-abdominal applications
Authors: Shangrui Rao, Hongzheng Li, Jianhua Lu, Panpan Wei, Qingfei Zhang, Yu Wang, Weijian Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryFunctionalized bio-patches, as a pivotal innovation in biomedical engineering, hold significant promise for tissue repair and regenerative medicine. Advances in emerging biomaterials and fabrication technologies have transformed these patches into multifunctional platforms. This review highlights recent advancements in the development of functional biomaterials for bio-patch fabrication. Natural polymers, synthetic polymers, and composite polymer systems are widely employed in bio-patch construction. The review then outlines the translational applications of bio-patches in abdominal wall reconstruction, gastrointestinal repair, urinary tract restoration, and vascular regeneration. Finally, the review discusses the future directions and technical challenges associated with next-generation smart bio-patches, emphasizing functional modification, mechanical optimization, and degradation control. Collectively, this review aims to provide a comprehensive yet practical reference for biomaterials researchers and clinicians to guide material selection, patch engineering, and translational decision-making.

Introduction

Intra-abdominal organ injuries, typically resulting from blunt trauma, penetrating wounds, intraoperative accidents, or infections, are characterized by rapid onset, complex complications, and high mortality rates.1,2,3,4 Common injury types include rupture and hemorrhage of solid organs, perforation of hollow organs, and structural damage to the urinary system.5 For large or non-closable tissue defects, anatomical reconstruction and functional support often require autologous tissue transplantation (e.g., ileal neobladder replacement) or implantation of synthetic material.6,7,8 Among current biomaterials used in soft tissue repair, biomedical patches such as hydrogel sponges, porous scaffolds, and microneedle systems have become essential tools in clinical practices.9,10,11,12 However, traditional patch materials still face critical challenges in the complex intra-abdominal environment related to mechanical stability, degradation matching, and adhesion under dynamic wet conditions.13,14,15 These limitations collectively compromise their long-term structural integrity, functional performance, and ultimately their effectiveness in supporting tissue repair.16,17,18 Thus, developing multifunctional bio-patches that can address these shortcomings represents an urgent direction for broadening their clinical applicability.

The advancement of materials science and engineering technologies promotes the development of more and more functionalized bio-patches, which have potential for the repair of intra-abdominal organ defects.19,20,21 Currently, biomedical patches used in the peritoneal cavity include natural materials such as gelatin, alginate, collagen, and synthetic polymers such as polypropylene (PP), polylactic acid (PLA), and poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA).22,23,24 These patches can provide a degree of structural support and functional modification to help abdominal injuries repair.2,25,26 However, the intra-abdominal environment has unique physiological and pathological characteristics, including persistent moisture, high enzyme activity, frequent organ peristalsis, and a complex inflammatory microenvironment, which places higher demands on the comprehensive performance of the patch.27,28,29

Unlike surface wound repair, peritoneal repair occurs in a closed, moist, and dynamically moving cavity, where implanted patches are continuously exposed to peritoneal fluid, visceral organ movement, digestive enzymes, inflammatory exudates, and fluctuating intra-abdominal pressure.30 These features create several key challenges for bio-patch design. First, the wet and lubricated peritoneal surface greatly weakens interfacial adhesion, making it difficult for patches to achieve stable fixation without suture.31 Second, frequent gastrointestinal peristalsis, respiratory movement, and abdominal wall deformation impose repeated mechanical stress, requiring materials to possess sufficient elasticity, fatigue resistance, and compliance matching.32 Third, the peritoneal cavity is highly susceptible to postoperative inflammation, bacterial contamination, and fibrin deposition, which may lead to infection, fibrosis, and abnormal adhesion between visceral organ.33 Fourth, the enzymatic and fluid-rich environment may accelerate material swelling, degradation, or drug diffusion, making it difficult to maintain synchronized degradation and sustained therapeutic efficacy.34 Finally, different intra-abdominal organs, including the abdominal wall, gastrointestinal tract, urinary system, and abdominal vasculature, present distinct biomechanical and pathological microenvironments, requiring organ-specific patch designs rather than universal wound-dressing strategies.35 Therefore, functionalized bio-patches for peritoneal repair should integrate wet adhesion, mechanical adaptability, anti-adhesion and anti-inflammatory properties, infection control, and controllable degradation to meet the complex requirements of intra-abdominal tissue reconstruction.

To achieve more efficient tissue repair, the patch needs to integrate multiple functional components.36,37,38 This includes the introduction of antimicrobial factors to prevent postoperative infection; anti-inflammatory drugs, growth factors, or stem cell-derived exosomes to alleviate local inflammatory responses and promote tissue regeneration; as well as wet-adhesive or stretchable interfacial materials to ensure strong tissue bonding and compliance with dynamic mechanical load.39,40,41,42,43 Importantly, the functional requirements of bio-patches vary substantially across intra-abdominal applications. For example, abdominal wall repair requires high tensile strength, anisotropic mechanics, and anti-adhesion surfaces to tolerate repetitive abdominal loading, whereas gastrointestinal patches require wet adhesion, leakage sealing, and resistance to acidic or enzyme-rich environments.44 Urinary tract patches must withstand urine exposure and prevent fibrosis, while vascular patches demand compliance matching, rapid endothelialization, and anti-thrombogenic properties.45,46,47 Therefore, rational patch design should be guided by disease-specific biomechanical and pathological cues rather than by material selection alone, making functional bio-patches promising platforms for the precise and rapid repair of intra-abdominal organ injuries.

This review first summarizes the advantages and limitations of classical biomaterials used in the fabrication of bio-patches, including natural polymers, synthetic polymers, and hybrid composites. We then delve into the translational applications of bio-patches in various clinical contexts, such as abdominal wall defect repair, gastrointestinal perforation closure, bladder injury reconstruction, and abdominal aortic repair (Figure 1). Composition of bio-patches

The composition of bio-patches directly influences their mechanical properties, degradation behavior, and biological functionality.48,49 In response to persistent clinical challenges such as postoperative adhesions, infections, and mechanical mismatch with host tissues, the development of functional polymers and their modified derivatives has become a central focus of current research.50,51 Rather than serving merely as structural substrates, biomaterials are increasingly selected and engineered according to specific functional requirements, including mechanical support, interface regulation, biological activity, and programmable degradation. This section introduces the major categories of polymeric materials used in biomedical patches, including natural polymers, synthetic polymers, and composite systems. By comparing key performance indicators such as biocompatibility, mechanical strength, and cell affinity, we summarize the advantages and limitations of each polymer type.

Natural polymers

Natural polymers provide intrinsic biocompatibility and biological cues for cell adhesion and tissue regeneration.52,53 However, their limited mechanical strength and rapid degradation often require structural or chemical modification for intra-abdominal applications.

Collagen

Collagen, the most abundant structural protein in animals, is a major natural extracellular matrix (ECM) component found in skin, bones, and tendon.54,55 Collagen is a major ECM component whose triple-helical structure and intrinsic cell-binding motifs support tissue adhesion and integration.56,57 Type I and III collagen are therefore widely used in abdominal wall patches, although processing-induced disruption of their native architecture often compromises mechanical stability.58,59,60 It is widely used in bio-patches because it provides inherent cell-adhesive motifs and regenerative bioactivity, making it an ideal platform for biological functionalization.61

However, during extraction, purification, and post-processing, collagen often loses its native supramolecular assembly and crosslinking patterns, resulting in reduced mechanical strength and structural stability compared to native tissues. This limitation restricts its application in mechanically demanding regions such as the abdominal wall.62,63,64 To address this challenge, various strategies have been developed to reinforce and optimize collagen-derived material.65 Chemical, physical, and enzymatic crosslinking have been used to reinforce collagen patches. Chemical crosslinkers such as EDC/NHS provide substantial mechanical strengthening, whereas physical methods, including UV irradiation and dehydrothermal treatment, avoid exogenous reagents. Enzymatic crosslinking offers milder and more selective conditions while preserving collagen bioactivity (Figures 2A–2C).66,67,68 These methods not only improve the structural stability of collagen patches but also help preserve collagen’s intrinsic cell adhesion and tissue integration properties, making them especially suitable for functionalized abdominal wall bio-patches requiring enhanced regenerative capacity and biological activity.Figure 2Representative modification strategies for collagen-based patches(A) Chemical crosslinking. On the basis of optimizing structural stability, researchers are also committed to expanding the application potential of collagen in complex abdominal tissue repair through functional integration strategies. For instance, blending collagen with natural polysaccharides improves its antibacterial properties, wet adhesion capacity, and cell proliferation (Figure 2D).69 The incorporation of synthetic polymers like PLA and polyglycolic acid (PGA) significantly strengthens mechanical performance and stabilizes degradation behavior, thereby satisfying dual requirements for structural support and regenerative duration in abdominal wall patches.70 In addition, collagen serves as an excellent carrier for bioactive agents. By loading antimicrobial metal nanoparticles, plant-derived compounds, or growth factors, collagen-based patches can be endowed with anti-inflammatory, antibacterial, and pro-angiogenic functionalities. For example, covalent immobilization of VEGF via DOPA-mediated binding to collagen patches promoted cell adhesion and neovascularization, and significantly enhanced postoperative healing in the abdominal cavity.71,72 Building upon this foundation, recent innovations have focused on further improving the performance of collagen patches to meet the multifaceted requirements of intra-abdominal tissue repair. The gelatin-calcium peroxide cryogel patch developed by Razavi’s team exhibited low degradation rates and sustained oxygen release, making it suitable for repairing metabolically sensitive tissues under hypoxic intra-abdominal conditions.73 In another study, endothelial cell membranes were incorporated into crosslinked collagen scaffolds to induce in situ regeneration of xenogeneic splenic tissue within the peritoneal cavity, effectively reconstructing vascular and immune structures and enhancing patch adaptability.74 Furthermore, self-assembling collagen modified via macromolecular crowding (MMC) enables the rapid formation of porous architectures, facilitating endothelial cell organization and vascular network formation. These systems demonstrate superior tissue compatibility in enzyme-rich, highly humid intra-abdominal environments.75 Collectively, these collagen-based composite engineering strategies have significantly broadened the scope of functionalized bio-patch applications and offer a practical materials foundation for the intelligent repair of diverse intra-abdominal tissue defects.

Gelatin

Gelatin, a hydrolyzed form of collagen, retains the Arg-Gly-Asp (RGD) sequence, offering strong cell adhesion and biocompatibility.76,77 Owing to its thermo-reversible gelation behavior, enzyme-responsive degradability, and facile chemical modification, it is particularly suitable for adhesive and drug-delivery functionalization.78 It can form a gel at low temperatures and liquefy at physiological temperature, making it suitable for injectable formulations and conformal adhesion to irregular soft tissue surfaces.79 Moreover, its enzyme-responsive degradation is particularly well-suited for remodeling in postoperative intra-abdominal environments characterized by elevated proteolytic activity. However, gelatin’s inherently low mechanical strength limits its long-term stability under the dynamic mechanical stresses present in the abdominal cavity.80 To overcome this limitation, researchers have widely adopted methacrylation modification (methacrylated gelatin (GelMA)), which imparts photocrosslinking capability under mild conditions, thereby enhancing its mechanical stability, structural fidelity, and cytocompatibility.81,82 GelMA has now become a mainstream material platform for fabricating functional hydrogel patches tailored for abdominal tissue repair. In recent years, the research efforts have increasingly focused on thiol-ene click crosslinking systems, particularly those based on norbornene-functionalized gelatin hydrogels. Owing to their higher crosslinking efficiency and lower photo-initiator requirements, these systems markedly improve cell viability and represent a promising direction for advancing the functionalization of GelMA-based materials.83

Beyond structural reinforcement, gelatin has been endowed with a range of functional properties to expand its applicability in intra-abdominal repair. For example, GelMA hydrogels incorporating either persistent-radical coupling (PTPC)-induced microphase-separated architectures or dynamic bridging via 3,4-dihydroxybenzaldehyde (DB) exhibited enhanced interfacial bonding and tunable viscoelasticity (Figure 3A).84,87 Incorporation of self-assembling hemostatic peptide motifs (e.g., CFLIVIGSIIVPGDGVPGDG) enabled rapid formation of β-sheet nanofiber networks, conferring hemostatic activity and facilitating effective closure of intestinal or peritoneal defects (Figure 3B).85 Additionally, covalent introduction of dopamine methacrylamide (DMA) into GelMA matrices improved both bioadhesion and transparency, offering practical advantages for endoscopic visualization and sutureless sealing in minimally invasive abdominal procedures (Figure 3C).86 As a versatile carrier, gelatin can also be co-loaded with various bioactives such as anti-inflammatory drugs, metal nanoparticles, and adipose-derived stem cells (ADSCs), enabling the construction of hydrogel patches with functions including oxidative stress mitigation, immune regulation, and promotion of vascular and muscular regeneration.88,89,90 Collectively, these engineering strategies transform gelatin from a passive scaffold into a dynamic, multifunctional platform well-suited for next-generation bio-patches tailored to intra-abdominal surgical environments.Figure 3Functionalization strategies for gelatin-based hydrogelsFunctionalization of gelatin-based hydrogels through (A) PTPC-induced phase separation (copyright 2024, Wiley),84 (B) β-sheet nanofiber networks in gelatin-based hydrogel (copyright 2024, Wiley),85 (C) Enhanced adhesion by incorporating DMA (copyright 2023, Wiley).86

Chitosan

Chitosan is a versatile natural polysaccharide derived from the deacetylation of chitin and widely utilized in bio-patches.91,92 The free amino group (-NH2) gives it a unique positive surface charge, resulting in a variety of biomedical advantages. Its protonated amino group (-NH3+) disrupts bacterial membranes, offers broad-spectrum antibacterial activity, and resists inducing drug resistance.93,94 Its degradation product is glucosamine, which has good biological safety and is an important precursor for the synthesis of glycosaminoglycans.95,96 In addition, its cationic properties facilitate electrostatic interaction with negatively charged tissue surfaces, thereby enhancing adhesion, stabilizing the microenvironment, and promoting tissue regeneration.97,98

In terms of material processing, chitosan can be prepared into various forms such as membranes, gels, electrospun nanofibers, etc. It has certain mechanical adjustability and pore structure, and is often used to construct tissue-like three-dimensional scaffolds. However, unmodified chitosan has certain limitations, such as poor tensile properties, easy softening and rupture in a wet environment,99 and rapid dissolution under acidic conditions, which limits its stability in specific abdominal.100 To enhance its adaptability in complex physiological environments, researchers have developed a series of functional modification strategies. For instance, the photo-crosslinked chitosan hydrogel coating exhibited enhanced mechanical stability. After freeze-drying, it presented a honeycomb porous three-dimensional structure with uniform pore size (50–100 μm) and a porosity of 43.47% (Figure 4A), which was beneficial for cell migration, angiogenesis, and material exchange and diffusion.101 Furthermore, it effectively reduced postoperative abdominal adhesions by promoting the expression of TGF-β1, while maintaining good degradability to adapt to surgical applications.101 In addition, by combining chitosan with polymers such as alginate or polyacrylamide, multifunctional chitosan-based hydrogel patches were developed (Figure 4B), demonstrating strong adhesion, compressive resistance, and bioactivity. These properties enabled the patch to effectively repair tissue defects without sutures, while also suppressing inflammation and accelerating tissue regeneration.102 Moreover, by integrating highly adhesive gallic acid-modified chitosan with crosslinked hyaluronic acid (HA), a Janus structural collagen material could be prepared (Figure 4C).103 It not only achieved efficient sealing of the anastomosis and significantly reduced the risk of anastomotic leakage, but also effectively inhibited postoperative abdominal adhesions, combining high efficiency, safety, and abdominal adaptability. These advances collectively enhance the potential of chitosan-based materials in abdominal applications, particularly in scenarios requiring integrated antibacterial, anti-inflammatory, anti-adhesive, and pro-regenerative functionalities.104 Through structural tunability and interfacial adaptability, chitosan is emerging as a promising platform transitioning from a basic antimicrobial material to a multifunctional smart repair system.Figure 4Representative chitosan-based patch designs(A) Porous chitosan hydrogel. Hyaluronic acid

HA is particularly attractive for intra-abdominal bio-patches because of its excellent hydration, lubricity, and anti-adhesion properties, making it an important material for interface functionalization.105 In abdominal tissue repair, HA, owing to its loose and flexible polymer chain structure, can rapidly absorb water to form a dynamic hydrated barrier, effectively preventing abnormal peritoneal adhesions and reducing the risk of postoperative intestinal obstruction and other complications.106,107,108 This inherent anti-adhesive property has led to its widespread use in anti-adhesion gels, sprayable barriers, and biodegradable membranes, particularly for peritoneal regeneration and intestinal protection following abdominal surgery.109,110

However, native HA suffers from rapid degradation, insufficient mechanical strength, and poor tissue adhesiveness, making it difficult to maintain stable performance in the high-enzymatic activity, high-moisture, and dynamic stress environment of the abdominal cavity.111,112 To overcome these limitations, chemical modifications like methacrylation, sulfation, thiolation, and catechol functionalization have been applied to enhance crosslinking, enzymatic stability, and reversible adhesion113,114 physical blending of HA with collagen, gelatin, chitosan, or synthetic polymers (such as PLA, polycaprolactone (PCL)) to balance flexibility, mechanical support, and controlled degradation115,116,117 and network structure design through double-network hydrogels, nanofiller incorporation, or self-healing systems to improve mechanical stability and prolong in vivo residence time (Figure 5A).118,121,122 In practical applications, methacrylated HA combined with superhydrophilic N-(2-hydroxypropyl) methacrylamide (HPMA) has been used to form injectable hydrogels that undergo in situ gelation (Figure 5B). This created a stable antifouling layer, significantly reducing protein and cell adhesion, promoting peritoneal regeneration, and minimizing adhesion recurrence.119 Besides, sprayable double-network hydrogels composed of sulfated HA, chitosan, and Pluronic® F127 were presented, which could rapidly cover the wound surface, induce macrophage M2 polarization, and suppress inflammatory responses (Figure 5C).120 HA-based degradable membranes reinforced with inorganic nanoparticles such as montmorillonite exhibited both enhanced enzymatic stability and mechanical robustness. These structural and functional optimizations highlight HA as a promising candidate material for soft, anti-adhesive, and functionalized abdominal bio-patches.Figure 5Representative HA-based hydrogel systems(A) Double-network HA hydrogel. Alginate

Alginate is frequently used in bio-patches owing to its rapid ionic gelation, injectability, and tunable network structure, which are advantageous for minimally invasive delivery and controlled-release functionalization. The M/G ratio directly influences the hydrogel’s stiffness, swelling behavior, and degradation kinetics.123,124 Alginate rapidly forms an “egg-box” structure via ionic crosslinking with Ca2+ or Ba2+, offering shear-thinning and injectability ideal for in situ gelation in minimally invasive procedures.125,126 However, native alginate lacks RGD sequences, resulting in poor cell adhesion. Furthermore, its low storage modulus, susceptibility to swelling-induced rupture, and degradation rates that are often asynchronous with tissue regeneration limit its practical application.

To overcome these limitations, diverse strategies have been employed to improve both bioactivity and mechanical performance. Chemical modifications, including RGD peptide grafting and covalent crosslinking via photo-initiated reactions, have been used to improve cellular interactions and increase compressive modulus. Blending alginate with natural biomacromolecules such as gelatin or chitosan further enhances cell adhesion and structural integrity under physiological stress. Mechanical reinforcement has been achieved through composite approaches, in which sodium alginate (SA) is combined with high-strength polymers such as polyacrylamide, PLA, or hydroxyapatite, followed by chelation to form Ca-alginate hydrogels with improved structural support and controlled degradation profiles.127,128 In addition, mechanically trained alginate systems have been developed to further augment stability without introducing additional chemical components. Zijian Li et al. developed a mechanically trained Ca-alginate hydrogel (PDMT-CAIH) via cyclic biaxial stretching, significantly enhancing stability and fatigue resistance without introducing additional chemical components (Figure 6A).44 Functionalized alginate systems have been employed in various biomedical applications, including anti-adhesion barriers, cell delivery, and growth factor release. For example, alginate-assisted nanofiber membranes were integrated into PP-based composite patches. (Figure 6B),128 which can reduce postoperative peritoneal adhesions and promote tissue integration.Figure 6Preparation strategies for alginate-based biomaterialsPreparation of (A) a mechanically trained Ca-alginate hydrogel (copyright 2025, Wiley)44 and (B) an alginate-assisted nanofiber membrane (copyright 2023, Elsevier).128

Synthetic polymers

Synthetic polymers are particularly valuable for mechanical and degradation functionalization, enabling precise control over strength, elasticity, long-term stability, and programmable resorption.129 Compared with natural polymers, synthetic polymers enable deliberate molecular design through strategies including controlled copolymerization and tailored crosslinking, which allow systematic adjustment of tensile strength, degradation behavior, and porosity for specific biomedical applications. These attributes make them particularly well-suited for intra-abdominal applications such as abdominal wall reconstruction and vascular grafting.130,131 However, synthetic polymers are generally biologically inert, which may lead to chronic inflammatory responses such as foreign body giant cell accumulation or inadequate tissue integration characterized by fibrotic encapsulation. Therefore, surface functionalization or composite modification is often required to achieve a balance between mechanical performance and bioactivity. This section discusses the properties of commonly used synthetic polymers, the clinical challenges they face, and current strategies for performance optimization.

PLA

PLA is commonly employed in bio-patches when mechanical reinforcement, structural stability, and biodegradable support are required, making it valuable for load-bearing functionalization. PLA is a biodegradable polyester made from lactic acid (LA), mainly available as poly(L-lactic acid) (PLLA) and poly(DL-lactic acid) (PDLLA).132 PLA degrades via hydrolysis or enzymatic action, ultimately producing carbon dioxide and water. Its degradation period varies from six months to several years, depending on the crystallinity and molecular weight.9 Meanwhile, PLA has excellent mechanical properties. The tensile strength of PLLA is close to that of conventional PP, and the elastic modulus is also close to the mechanical requirements of bone tissue. Therefore, it is regarded as a potential alternative material for abdominal wall defect repair.133 Moreover, PLA is suitable for a variety of molding processes, including melt spinning, electrospinning, 3D printing, and so forth, providing a broad process basis for its clinical transformation.134,135 However, PLA still has obvious limitations in peritoneal applications. On the one hand, its hydrophobic surface lacks cell adhesion motifs such as RGD, resulting in poor cell invasiveness. On the other hand, the acidic products produced by its degradation may induce sterile inflammation and increase the risk of postoperative abdominal adhesions.136 These problems limit the applicability of PLA as a single patch material in complex tissue repair.

Functional material design is essential to enhance the applicability of PLA in abdominal repair. For example, the electrospun PLA-silk fibroin (PLA-SF) nanofiber mesh combines PLA with natural SF and has excellent hydrophilicity and morphological reversibility, maintaining stable structure and quality in a humid environment (Figure 7A).137 The results of animal experiments showed that this composite reduced postoperative inflammation and adhesion formation, offering a new patch solution with both high mechanical strength and anti-adhesion functionality for hernia repair.137 Another study utilized supercritical CO2 foaming to fabricate 3D porous PLA/PCL scaffolds. The resulting interpenetrating nanofiber network enhanced hydrophilicity and toughness, promoting vascular smooth muscle cell adhesion and migration (Figure 7B).138 In the future, PLA-based composites can be further optimized via biomimetic surface modifications (e.g., peptide incorporation) or degradation control strategies (e.g., blending with alkaline buffering agents) to balance degradation rates with tissue regeneration while mitigating inflammatory responses. PLGA

PLGA is widely used as a multifunctional patch material because its degradation kinetics and drug-release behavior can be precisely tailored, making it suitable for controlled therapeutic functionalization. PLGA is a degradable synthetic copolymer formed from LA and glycolic acid (GA).139 Its degradation products are metabolized through the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle and are non-toxic in the long term.140 Tuning the molecular weight and LA/GA ratio allows flexible control over PLGA’s degradation rate and mechanical strength. Based on this, it can meet the needs of different abdominal tissue repair, from short-term support (such as postoperative hemostatic membranes) to long-term structural reconstruction (such as inguinal hernia repair or pelvic organ prolapse stents).141,142,143 In addition, PLGA’s excellent drug release characteristics-capable of sustained release over periods up to six months-grant it a unique advantage in the prevention of abdominal infections or inflammation control.143 However, its hydrophobic surface limits cell adhesion, and the local pH drop during degradation may trigger aseptic inflammation, which restricts its direct use in complex intra-abdominal defect repairs.

To address these limitations, composite modification strategies have significantly improved the clinical applicability of PLGA. For instance, the prepared PLGA-chitosan microparticles have moderate particle size and stable surface charge. After cross-linking with PLGA-SA emulsion at a ratio of 5: 5 (Figure 8A), they formed a uniformly distributed loose microporous structure (pore size 100-400 nm). This structure had both good structural stability and pore advantages, and was suitable as a candidate material for the fabrication of the resultant patch. By further introducing natural polysaccharides into the PLGA composite patch, a biomimetic three-dimensional microstructure could be constructed, which not only helped to promote cell adhesion and soft tissue regeneration, but also alleviated the inflammatory response caused by acidic degradation byproducts.144 In inguinal hernia repair, the Janus electrospun patch was constructed, composed of PCL and emodin by grafting lysine onto 4arm-PLGA.145 It had multiple functions such as excellent biocompatibility, controllable degradation, antibacterial and anti-adhesion properties, and angiogenesis promotion, which effectively overcame the limitations of traditional PLGA such as high rigidity and strong degradation acidity (Figure 8B). Looking forward, PLGA shows strong potential for the creation of smart, responsive patches through the integration of multiple functional components, including the incorporation of growth factors or conductive polymers to enable dynamic adaptation to variations in the abdominal microenvironment (for example, changes in pH or enzymatic activity). When used in conjunction with 3D printing technology, PLGA patches that can be tailored to specific geometric and mechanical requirements are highly promising for the repair of complex abdominal defects. This includes applications in traumatic abdominal wall injuries and tumor resection reconstruction, where precisely matching the material’s degradation rates to the pace of tissue regeneration is essential. Polyethylene glycol (PEG)

PEG is an important synthetic polymer for bio-patches due to its hydrophilicity, protein-repellent properties, and facile end-group modification, enabling anti-fouling and smart hydrogel functionalization. PEG has a broad range of molecular weights, and its terminal hydroxyl groups can be chemically modified into functional moieties such as amines or carboxyls, facilitating conjugation with drugs, peptides, or targeting ligands.146,147,148 Additionally, PEG’s anti-protein adsorption properties contribute to reducing immune rejection.149 However, the application of pure PEG in the dynamic abdominal environment remains constrained by several limitations. PEG hydrogels generally exhibit a low storage modulus, which renders them insufficiently resistant to fluctuations in intra-abdominal pressure. Although their strong resistance to protein adsorption can help reduce inflammatory responses, this property also hinders cellular adhesion, requiring subsequent surface modification with bioactive motifs such as RGD peptides or collagen coatings. Moreover, high-molecular-weight PEG is poorly degradable, and long-term retention may lead to granuloma formation. In addition, PEG lacks inherent antibacterial activity and anti-adhesion functionality, which further limits its effectiveness in abdominal tissue repair.150

To address these challenges, composite modifications have been applied to improve the performance of PEG-based materials. For example, the PEG/Fe3O4 nanocomposite patch developed by Yang Luo’s group (Figure 9A) utilized micro/nanoscale topological surface cues to guide the aligned arrangement and migration of smooth muscle cells, achieving a 90% repair efficiency. This offered a precise platform for regenerating abdominal muscular layers such as the rectus abdominis.152 In another study, an injectable PEG patch was loaded with the mitochondria-targeted antioxidant MitoQ (Figure 9B), which suppressed reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation through controlled release, thereby mitigating postoperative oxidative damage (e.g., intestinal ischemia-reperfusion injury) and enabling a synergistic approach that combined mechanical support with bioactivity.151 Moving forward, PEG materials can be further optimized by blending with natural polymers to create smart patches with enhanced hydrophilicity, tunable degradation, and cell-inductive properties. Combined with 3D bioprinting, customizable PEG-based hydrogels hold great potential for applications in complex abdominal repairs, such as pelvic floor reconstruction or anti-adhesion barriers. Their “on-demand” degradation behavior and dynamic mechanical adaptability will be central to next-generation innovations, promoting the evolution of abdominal repair materials toward minimally invasive and functionally integrated solutions.Figure 9PEG-based systems for tissue repair(A) Microstructured PEG scaffold (copyright 2023, Elsevier) and (B) MitoQ-loaded PEG hydrogel (copyright 2024, RSC).151,152

Polyurethane (PU)

PU is highly suitable for abdominal bio-patches because of its elasticity, fatigue resistance, and tunable mechanics, making it an effective platform for dynamic mechanical functionalization. Its distinctive microphase-separated structure endows PU with high elasticity, fatigue resistance, and tunable degradability.153 PU-based patches are available in various forms. For instance, electrospun PU nanofiber membranes create high-porosity scaffolds composed of nano/microscale fibers, which mimic the natural ECM and are well-suited for soft tissue repair in the abdominal wall.1,154,155 PU sponges with interconnected pore structures can be used to deliver stem cells or growth factors, facilitating wall defect repair or vascularized tissue regeneration within the abdominal cavity.156,157 Additionally, PU solution-cast membranes coated with anti-adhesion layers have shown efficacy in reducing postoperative peritoneal adhesions.158 However, degradation products of polyester-based PU, such as urethane derivatives, may induce chronic inflammation or fibrosis, thus limiting the long-term safety of this material in intra-abdominal applications.

To tackle these issues, diverse modifications have been used to enhance the biocompatibility of PU and functional versatility. For example, Rui Guo’s team developed a solvent-free PU elastomer (Figure 10A), offering a high-strength and biocompatible patch candidate for hernia repair.160 In another study, functional patches made of PU modified with the antioxidant gallic acid (PUGA) were successfully loaded with ADSC exosomes (Figure 10B), showing angiogenic effects which have potential translational application value in ischemic abdominal tissue repair (e.g., intestinal wall regeneration).159 Looking ahead, PU materials can be further optimized by incorporating anti-inflammatory agents (e.g., IL-10-loaded microspheres) or enzymatically responsive degradable segments to precisely synchronize degradation with tissue regeneration. With the integration of 3D printing technologies, customized PU patches can be tailored to fit complex intra-abdominal defect geometries (e.g., traumatic abdominal wall injuries or post-tumor resection reconstruction). Polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS)

PDMS is an organosilicon polymer composed of a siloxane backbone and methyl side groups, which provides the material with excellent flexibility and high elasticity.161 It is mainly applied in specialized bio-patch systems requiring flexibility, chemical stability, and microfabrication compatibility, particularly for long-term barrier and smart-device functionalization. Its pronounced chemical inertness affords strong resistance to high temperatures, acids, and alkalis, allowing PDMS to maintain structural and functional stability even after long-term implantation.162 PDMS also exhibits good biocompatibility and has a lower tendency to adsorb proteins than most synthetic materials, thereby significantly reducing inflammation after implantation.163 PDMS can be fabricated into various forms including elastic membranes, porous scaffolds, microneedle patches, and hydrogels. Microneedle arrays, in particular, are often produced via photolithography or micromolding techniques as either dissolvable or non-dissolvable platforms for precise drug or vaccine delivery.10,164,165 Due to its elastic modulus being close to that of soft tissues, PDMS is well suited for soft tissue engineering applications requiring mechanical compliance.166 Furthermore, it is highly compatible with micro/nanofabrication techniques such as photolithography and soft lithography, enabling the creation of complex structures with feature resolutions below 1 μm.167 In abdominal applications, PDMS’s inert surface, mechanical flexibility, and long-term stability make it a promising candidate for postoperative anti-adhesion barriers, flexible wound sealing systems, and smart drug delivery platforms. Particularly in scenarios requiring non-degradable materials to maintain long-term tissue separation or barrier functions, PDMS offers a viable strategy to prevent abnormal adhesions between abdominal organs. Its conformability and low immunogenicity minimize foreign body responses, supporting its role as a “low-reactivity, high-stability, functionally extendable” engineered solution for intra-abdominal implants.

Natural-synthetic hybrid systems

Natural-synthetic hybrid systems represent the most common form of hybrid bio-patch design. In these systems, natural polymers typically provide cell-interactive motifs, hydration, and biodegradability, whereas synthetic polymers contribute mechanical reinforcement, structural stability, and tunable functionalization. Their interfacial synergy enables bio-patches to achieve both biological responsiveness and physicochemical robustness, making them highly promising for complex abdominal tissue repair. In recent years, they have become a promising solution to meet the challenges of complex abdominal tissue repair.

For example, collagen/PLGA composites use collagen’s RGD motifs to support cell adhesion and tissue regeneration, while PLGA reinforces strength and enables tunable anti-inflammatory drug release.168 Such features render these membranes particularly suitable for precise management of postoperative abdominal inflammation. Composite designs combining chitosan and PU, exemplified by dialdehyde PU/cellulose nanofibril anisotropic membranes, have been engineered with surface-aligned microgrooves that guide the axonal extension of neural stem cells.169 This capability can potentially be adapted to fulfill the requirements for aligned cellular arrangement during abdominal wall injury repair. Furthermore, HA/PEG double-network hydrogel(s) combine the scar-inhibiting properties of HA with the low immunogenicity of PEG, exhibiting significantly enhanced clinical safety (immunogenicity below 5%) compared to traditional crosslinked materials.170 This indicates substantial potential for application in postoperative adhesion prevention or soft tissue augmentation in the abdominal cavity.

These hybrid systems achieve functional complementarity through interfacial synergy by combining natural components that support cell adhesion and tissue regeneration with synthetic materials that provide mechanical support and controlled drug delivery. This combined approach is particularly suitable for the abdominal environment, where materials must accommodate dynamic mechanical demands arising from intra-abdominal pressure changes while simultaneously supporting essential biological activities. Looking ahead, advances in three-dimensional printing and stimulus-responsive designs, including enzyme-triggered and mechanically activated degradation and drug release, will further expand the customization of hybrid biomaterials. These innovations are expected to enable the fabrication of structures with tailored features such as graded porosity and biomimetic vascular networks, along with integrated functional performance. Together, these developments are poised to significantly advance the design of abdominal repair materials toward greater precision and dynamic adaptability.

Nanocomposite materials

Nanocomposite materials enhance traditional bio-patches’ mechanical properties and multifunctionality by incorporating nanoscale components such as metallic nanoparticles, carbon-based materials, or inorganic nanocrystals, thus enabling effective application in complex abdominal defect repairs. For instance, Khanbaba et al. developed an electrospun PU/chitosan/gold nanoparticle (PU/Cs/AuNPs) nanofibrous patch, which demonstrated mechanical properties and electrical conductivity resembling native tissue microenvironments and supported cellular viability, suggesting its potential application in regenerating electroactive abdominal tissues, such as intestinal smooth muscle or abdominal wall nerves.172 Similarly, Zhao et al. designed a substrate biomimetic corneal patch (SRCP) composed of a decellularized scaffold nanotubular framework integrated with recombinant collagen/GelMA composites.171 This material exhibited high biomechanical strength and resistance to enzymatic degradation, withstanding deformation under pressures up to 100 mmHg, potentially meeting the mechanical stability requirements for intra-abdominal high-pressure environments, such as abdominal wall defect repair. Although primarily studied in cardiac and corneal repair, the core design principles of reinforcing mechanical properties with nanocomposite strategies and using biomimetic architectures to guide tissue regeneration offer valuable insights for the development of materials for abdominal repair. To meet the specific requirements of the abdominal cavity, including the need for resistance to tissue adhesion and the ability to withstand dynamic changes in intra-abdominal pressure, functional nanocomponents can be introduced in a targeted manner. Examples include nanoparticles that release anti-inflammatory agents over a sustained period and conductive nanomaterials that support electrical signaling in neural and muscular tissues. When these approaches are combined with three-dimensional printing technologies that can fabricate gradient and biomimetic structures, they are expected to advance the intelligent and precise use of nanocomposite-enhanced patches for abdominal tissue reconstruction.

Functionalization strategies for bio-patches

Although the preceding sections categorize bio-patch materials according to their chemical origins and structural compositions, their practical value in intra-abdominal repair ultimately depends on how these material properties are translated into organ-specific functional performance.173,174 Therefore, material selection should follow a property-function-application logic. Natural polymers such as collagen, gelatin, chitosan, HA, and alginate provide biological cues, hydration, degradability, and interfacial bioactivity, making them suitable for applications requiring tissue integration, anti-adhesion, or regenerative signaling.175,176 In contrast, synthetic polymers such as PLA, PLGA, PEG, PU, and PDMS offer more controllable mechanical strength, elasticity, processability, and degradation behavior, which are essential for load-bearing support, long-term barrier function, or programmable drug delivery.177,178 Hybrid and nanocomposite systems further integrate these advantages by combining biological responsiveness with mechanical reinforcement, interfacial regulation, and multifunctional therapeutic delivery.

Different intra-abdominal application scenarios impose distinct requirements on bio-patch design. For abdominal wall repair, materials should provide tensile strength, fatigue resistance, anisotropic mechanics, and anti-adhesion surfaces to withstand cyclic intra-abdominal pressure while preventing visceral adhesion.179,180 For gastrointestinal perforation repair, wet adhesion, leakage sealing, self-healing capacity, and resistance to acidic, enzymatic, and peristaltic stresses are more critical, making adhesive hydrogels and Janus barrier systems particularly relevant.181,182 For urinary tract reconstruction, bio-patches must tolerate continuous urine exposure, maintain elasticity and luminal patency, and reduce fibrosis, calcification, and stricture formation.183 Therefore, decellularized matrices, anti-fibrotic hydrogels, and controlled-release systems are especially valuable. For vascular repair, compliance matching, burst strength, hemocompatibility, anti-thrombogenicity, and rapid endothelialization are central design priorities, favoring bioactive or nanocomposite patches that combine mechanical stability with vascular remodeling cues.184,185 The materials discussed above should be regarded as modular design platforms whose functional characteristics can be tailored to meet the pathological and biomechanical requirements of each organ-specific repair scenario discussed in Section 3.

Applications of the functionalized bio-patches

Biomedical patches, characterized by their tunable mechanical properties and bioactivity, are evolving into sophisticated platforms for abdominal surgery with clinical translation potential. Importantly, beyond static functionality, an increasing number of bio-patches are being engineered with stimuli-responsive capabilities, in which external or endogenous triggers (e.g., ultrasound, ROS, pH) induce material transformation or controlled drug release for dynamic tissue repair. This section details their application in abdominal wall defect repair, gastrointestinal perforation closure, bladder injury reconstruction, and abdominal aortic repair, providing guidance for clinical decision-making and material optimization.

Application in abdominal wall defect repair

Abdominal wall defects encompass discontinuities in the muscle-fascia layer that frequently result from trauma, surgical dehiscence, or infection, and are commonly associated with compromised peritoneal integrity.186 Disruption of this anatomical barrier can lead to serious clinical consequences, including visceral herniation with incarceration or strangulation and an elevated risk of intra-abdominal infection in open defects.187 Given the significant morbidity associated with these outcomes, timely and effective repair of abdominal wall defects remains an urgent clinical priority.188 Conventional synthetic meshes composed of materials such as PP, polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE), and polyester have long been used to restore anatomical continuity. While these materials provide baseline mechanical support, their inert nature and mechanical mismatch with surrounding tissues have been linked to postoperative complications including bowel adhesion, chronic pain, fibrotic encapsulation, and impaired tissue integration.189 These limitations have prompted extensive research efforts toward the development of advanced biomaterials that can offer both mechanical reinforcement and biological functionality.

Because the abdominal wall undergoes continuous multidirectional tension and cyclic intra-abdominal pressure, ideal patches should provide high tensile strength, fatigue resistance, anisotropic mechanics, and anti-adhesion performance. One promising direction involves the design of hydrogel patches with enhanced mechanical properties achieved through molecular-level engineering. For example, Zhang and colleagues leveraged the Hofmeister effect to incorporate sodium polyacrylate into polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) networks. The resulting double-network hydrogel exhibited a substantial increase in tensile strength and, in a rat model of abdominal wall defect, suppressed herniation while promoting tissue regeneration via sustained cytokine release (Figure 11A).190 Inspired by the hierarchical structure of silk, alternative strategies have been proposed to achieve multiscale stress buffering through synergistic interactions between nanocrystalline domains and molecular interfaces (Figure 11B).191 Additional research has introduced Janus porous hydrogel architectures that integrate enhanced mechanical strength with anti-adhesion functionality, exhibiting greater tolerance to defect environments than conventional materials in preclinical studies.7,194,195 Beyond static mechanical optimization, recent advances have introduced stimuli-responsive designs to dynamically regulate tissue repair processes. For instance, an ultrasound-responsive Janus bio-patch (JPLCL/ZnO) enables externally triggered enhancement of directional cell growth and tissue regeneration, while maintaining anti-adhesion properties via a PMPC-coated surface.32 This system demonstrates a stimulus-driven regulatory mechanism in which ultrasound exposure induces enhanced cellular alignment and proliferation within the anisotropic matrix, thereby promoting directional tissue regeneration while maintaining anti-adhesion performance at the interface.Figure 11Representative bio-patches for abdominal wall repair(A) PVA hydrogel for defect repair and adhesion prevention (copyright 2023, Wiley).190(B) Silk-inspired stress-dispersing patch (copyright 2025, ACS).191(C) Hydrogel/PP composite patch (copyright 2024, Wiley).192(D) Bioinspired Janus patch with antibacterial, regenerative, and anti-adhesive interfaces.193

Despite the mechanical advancements, hydrogel systems often require further enhancement of biological activity to support regenerative processes. To address this need, composite patch designs that integrate cellular and ECM cues have been explored. A notable example involves the seeding of activated myogenic progenitor cells onto homologous decellularized muscle scaffolds, which maintained structural integrity after implantation and significantly enhanced neovascularization and myoblast migration, yielding functional abdominal wall regeneration with improved repair efficiency compared with conventional materials.196 Building upon this concept, Akbaba and co-workers developed a tri-layer composite patch comprising an electrospun SF/chitosan oligosaccharide layer that mimics ECM topography, a PP mesh providing dynamic mechanical support, and immune-modulating components that encourage pro-remodeling macrophage phenotypes. In vitro experiments confirmed high cell viability and enhanced myogenic differentiation, illustrating the value of integrating mechanical anchoring with immune regulation and tissue compliance.

Postoperative infection and adhesion remain persistent challenges in abdominal wall reconstruction, particularly when using nonbioactive meshes. To mitigate these complications, multifunctional and responsive biomaterials have been introduced. A dual-crosslinked hydrogel patch, based on chitosan methacrylate and Pluronic F127, possessed injectable, self-healing properties capable of localized tissue regeneration through the incorporation of rosmarinic acid-loaded microspheres (Figure 11C).192 In parallel, biomimetic Janus patches were developed through surface engineering approaches in which a parietal layer with dragonfly wing-inspired microstructures enhanced bactericidal efficacy and cell attachment, while a highly hydrated zwitterionic visceral interface reduced protein adsorption and suppressed adhesion formation (Figure 11D).193 These multifunctional platforms exemplify the integration of mechanical compliance, immune modulation, antimicrobial protection, and regenerative support, offering comprehensive strategies for complex abdominal wall repair.

For abdominal wall repair, coating strategies and bulk material design should be selected based on the balance between mechanical anisotropy and anti-adhesion performance. Hydrophilic surface coatings can effectively reduce tissue adhesion through hydration layer formation, but their long-term stability under cyclic mechanical loading remains limited. In contrast, structurally anisotropic scaffolds provide superior mechanical matching and promote directional tissue regeneration, although they may require additional surface modification to prevent adhesion. Therefore, integrated designs that combine anisotropic mechanical frameworks with stable anti-adhesion interfaces are more suitable for long-term abdominal wall reconstruction.

Application in gastrointestinal perforation repair

Gastrointestinal perforation refers to a full-thickness defect of the gastrointestinal wall caused by trauma, ulcers, or surgical complications, and is often associated with life-threatening consequences such as peritonitis and sepsis due to leakage of digestive fluids.197,198 Traditional surgical interventions, including direct suturing or omental patching, face significant limitations in cases involving fragile tissues, contaminated environments, or large defects. In such scenarios, the risks of leakage and impaired tissue regeneration complicate clinical outcomes. Biomedical patches offer a promising alternative for gastrointestinal perforation repair by combining mechanical sealing, tissue protection, and bioactive regeneration.

Gastrointestinal patches must rapidly seal leakage under wet conditions while resisting acidic, enzymatic, and peristaltic stresses. Therefore, wet adhesion, self-healing, and environment-responsive degradation are key design priorities. Traditional endoscopic clipping devices often fail to adapt to inflamed or irregular tissue surfaces and may require secondary removal, while existing hydrogel materials are prone to swelling, dissolution, or mechanical instability in strongly acidic environments. Inspired by the compressible architecture of mushroom stalks, Shuang Liu et al. developed an acid-resistant double-network hydrogel patch.199 This system combined dimethylacrylamide (DMA) and SA in a dual-crosslinked framework, reinforced via Ca2+-mediated ionic interactions to form a biomimetic onion-like multilayer structure. The outer layer was coated with silica nanoparticles to enhance gastric wall adhesion, while the inner layer was loaded with acidic fibroblast growth factor (aFGF) to synergistically regulate acid secretion and promote wound healing. In a rabbit gastric perforation model, the patch demonstrated excellent compressive elasticity and achieved autonomous deployment and tight defect sealing via endoscopic delivery, effectively reducing the risk of digestive fluid leakage. Capsule endoscopy confirmed its ability to sustain drug release, stimulate mucosal regeneration, and suppress excess gastric acid secretion, thereby addressing both sealing durability and tissue regeneration (Figure 12A). Similarly, Jing Yu et al. introduced a multi-hydrogen-bond crosslinking strategy based on dicarboxylic amino acid derivatives.200 The hydrogel exhibited strong interfacial adhesion and structural integrity under acidic conditions, maintaining resilience against gastrointestinal peristalsis. In a separate approach, Cui et al. developed a Janus hydrogel with asymmetric adhesion via a gradient polyelectrolyte complexation strategy.201 Using a single-sided dipping method, negatively charged carboxyl hydrogels were coupled with cationic oligosaccharides to produce a dual-faced structure with distinct interfacial properties. The adhesive surface formed an immediate and robust bond with moist gastric tissue, while the non-adhesive side, obtained by complete neutralization of carboxyl groups, served as an effective barrier against fibrin deposition. In a rabbit gastric perforation model, the Janus hydrogel achieved tight defect sealing on the adhesive face while the opposite side prevented adhesion formation (Figure 12C). Molecular simulations further revealed how different cationic polymer species regulated interfacial charge distribution. This gradient polyelectrolyte design offers a promising strategy for developing hydrogel-based adhesives with both regenerative and anti-adhesive functionalities, particularly suited for minimally invasive repair of moist hollow organs such as the gastrointestinal tract.

In gastrointestinal repair, different sealing strategies exhibit distinct advantages and limitations. Hydrogel-based adhesives provide rapid wet adhesion and conformal sealing, making them suitable for acute leakage prevention, but their mechanical strength and long-term stability are often insufficient.203 In contrast, patch-based systems offer better mechanical support and durability, although achieving reliable adhesion under dynamic peristalsis remains challenging. Therefore, hybrid systems that integrate strong interfacial adhesion with sufficient mechanical reinforcement are more appropriate for complex gastrointestinal defects.

Application in urinary tract defect repair

Because this section covers not only bladder reconstruction but also urethral and ureteral repair, the term “urinary tract defect repair” is used here to more accurately reflect the anatomical scope of these applications. Urinary tract injuries are commonly caused by iatrogenic surgical trauma, ureteral obstruction due to calculi, tumor invasion, or external injury, and present clinical challenges such as urinary leakage, urethral or ureteral stricture, impaired urodynamics, infection, and secondary hydronephrosis. Traditional end-to-end anastomosis procedures often lead to lumen obstruction or disrupted urodynamics, primarily due to the intrinsic peristaltic behavior of the ureter and the limited compliance of conventional graft materials. Urinary patches require urine resistance, elasticity, antiencrustation properties, and the ability to prevent fibrosis and stricture formation.

Urinary tract injuries are commonly caused by iatrogenic surgical trauma, ureteral obstruction due to calculi, or tumor invasion, and present clinical challenges such as urinary leakage, ureteral stricture, and secondary hydronephrosis.204,205 Traditional end-to-end anastomosis procedures often lead to lumen obstruction or disrupted urodynamics, primarily due to the intrinsic peristaltic behavior of the ureter and the limited compliance of conventional graft materials. Urinary patches require urine resistance, elasticity, anti-encrustation properties, and the ability to prevent fibrosis and stricture formation.

The bladder, as a low-pressure organ responsible for urine storage and controlled voiding, often requires surgical reconstruction in cases of congenital malformations, neurogenic injury, or tumor-related defects. Although gastrointestinal grafts have been used to expand bladder capacity, they often lead to complications such as donor site injury, metabolic imbalance, and stone formation.206 As an alternative, Zhang et al. proposed small intestinal submucosa (SIS), a decellularized matrix rich in growth factors and low in immunogenicity, for bladder repair.207 However, rapid degradation and inadequate mechanical integrity of SIS limit smooth muscle regeneration and can lead to patch contraction. Despite significant progress, biomedical patches for urinary tract reconstruction continue to face critical challenges. For bladder reconstruction, different functionalization strategies should be selected based on the risk of calcification and the requirement for smooth muscle regeneration. Surface modification approaches that enhance biocompatibility can improve epithelialization but are often insufficient to prevent stone formation. In contrast, drug-delivery-based systems can actively regulate the urinary microenvironment and inhibit crystallization, although their effectiveness depends on release kinetics and long-term stability. Consequently, combining structural biomimicry with controlled therapeutic delivery represents a more effective approach for achieving both functional regeneration and complication prevention.

Application in abdominal aortic injury repair

Abdominal aortic injury, often resulting from aneurysmal rupture, trauma, or iatrogenic interventions, poses life-threatening risks such as massive hemorrhage, pseudoaneurysm formation, and graft-associated infection.211 Although conventional vascular grafts composed of PTFE or polyester (Dacron) can restore anatomical continuity, they have several limitations including mechanical mismatch with native tissue, delayed endothelialization, and persistent inflammatory responses. These issues contribute to a higher incidence of long-term complications such as graft stenosis or thrombosis.212

Vascular patches must tolerate pulsatile hemodynamics and therefore require compliance matching, burst strength, hemocompatibility, anti-thrombogenicity, and rapid endothelialization. Moreover, different vascular pathologies demand distinct functional strategies. For instance, aortic dissection repair emphasizes burst resistance and acute sealing ability, whereas atherosclerotic or aneurysmal lesions may additionally require anti-inflammatory, anti-calcification, and pro-remodeling functions. In a porcine abdominal aortic laceration model, a decellularized vascular patch functionalized with gold nanoparticles (AuNPs) was investigated for enhanced vascular integration.213 Over a 6-month implantation period, the AuNP-modified graft demonstrated excellent mechanical durability and biocompatibility. Post-repair, the vascular graft exhibited complete endothelial coverage with no evidence of calcification or intimal hyperplasia, highlighting its potential to overcome the poor biointegration and long-term complications typically associated with conventional vascular prostheses (Figure 14A). To address the clinical challenges associated with postoperative aneurysmal progression and complications following abdominal aortic aneurysm (AAA) repair, Chen et al. developed a dual-drug-loaded hydrogel-based perivascular patch incorporating doxycycline hydrochloride (DOX) and cycloastragenol (CAG).214 In this design, the hydrophobic compound CAG was encapsulated within nanostructured lipid carriers (NLCs), while the ROS-responsive polydopamine hydrogel matrix enabled localized and temporally controlled co-delivery of DOX and CAG at the aneurysm site. In such systems, elevated oxidative stress in the pathological microenvironment induces cleavage of oxidation-sensitive linkers, leading to localized drug release and subsequent modulation of vascular remodeling processes (Figure 14B). Despite encouraging advances in functionalized patch design for abdominal aortic repair, several key barriers to clinical translation remain. For example, the drug release efficiency of ROS-responsive dual-loaded hydrogels may fluctuate with local oxidative stress levels, making it difficult to adapt precisely to the dynamic pathological microenvironment. Similarly, while gold nanoparticle (AuNP) modification improves biofunctionality, concerns remain regarding the long-term biosafety of AuNPs, particularly their potential to accumulate and burden the lymphatic system. In addition, different anti-thrombosis strategies present distinct trade-offs. Passive approaches, such as endothelial-mimicking or hydrophilic coatings, can reduce protein adsorption and platelet adhesion, but their efficacy is limited in highly thrombogenic environments. Active strategies based on drug release or bioactive signaling can more effectively regulate coagulation and vascular remodeling, although they may introduce risks related to dose control and long-term safety. Therefore, the integration of passive surface properties with controlled bioactive modulation is critical for achieving stable and durable vascular repair.

Conclusion and outlook

Functionalized bio-patches have emerged as critical platforms for the repair of complex abdominal tissue injuries, progressively evolving from traditional mechanical fillers to multifunctional therapeutic systems with bioactivity and smart responsiveness. This review summarizes recent advances in natural polymers, synthetic polymers, and composite systems, and discusses strategies for structural design, functional integration, and regenerative modulation. Advances in materials and fabrication have transformed bio-patch design from passive coverage to multifunctional systems integrating hemostatic, anti-infective, anti-adhesive, immunomodulatory, and regenerative features, significantly enhancing tissue integration and repair. Attributed to these achievements, these bio-patches have shown great promise across key clinical scenarios, including abdominal wall defects, gastrointestinal perforation, urinary tract reconstruction, and abdominal aortic repair.

Despite encouraging preclinical results, several challenges hinder clinical translation. First, intrinsic trade-offs remain unresolved. Natural polymers often lack mechanical robustness, while synthetic materials may cause local acidification and poor immunocompatibility; composite systems, although promising, face difficulties in interface stability and scalable fabrication. Second, most current studies are limited to short-term assessments, lacking comprehensive validation under chronic inflammatory conditions, dynamic mechanical loading, and long-term regenerative settings. Third, the prevailing design and evaluation strategies often rely on standard models and passive responses, without sufficient adaptation to diverse pathological states and individualized clinical demands.

However, current studies on functionalized bio-patches still present several important shortcomings that should be critically considered. First, many reported systems demonstrate excellent performance under simplified experimental conditions, but their effectiveness may be overestimated because most in vitro models cannot fully reproduce the wet, enzyme-rich, mechanically dynamic, and inflammatory peritoneal microenvironment. Second, although multifunctional integration is frequently emphasized, combining adhesion, anti-adhesion, antibacterial activity, mechanical reinforcement, controlled degradation, and regenerative signaling within a single patch may introduce design trade-offs. For example, strong tissue adhesion may increase the risk of undesired visceral adhesion, while highly hydrated anti-adhesive surfaces may weaken fixation strength. Similarly, prolonged mechanical support may conflict with timely biodegradation and tissue remodeling. Third, the long-term biosafety of some functional components, including metallic nanoparticles, conductive nanomaterials, reactive chemical crosslinkers, and sustained drug-release systems, remains insufficiently validated. Potential concerns include chronic inflammation, local toxicity, immune activation, off-target drug effects, and accumulation or delayed clearance of nondegradable components. Fourth, many studies rely on small-animal models and short observation periods, whereas clinically relevant abdominal repair often involves larger defects, contaminated surgical fields, complex comorbidities, and long-term mechanical loading. These differences limit the predictive value of current preclinical evidence. Finally, scalable manufacturing, sterilization, storage stability, reproducibility, regulatory classification, and cost-effectiveness remain major translational barriers. Therefore, future studies should move beyond proof-of-concept demonstrations and establish standardized evaluation systems, including long-term safety assessment, large-animal validation, clinically relevant defect models, and comparative studies against existing surgical materials.

Future bio-patch development should move from material-centered fabrication toward pathology-oriented functional design, where disease-specific microenvironmental cues dictate structural and biological engineering strategies. This involves integrating dynamic covalent bonds, self-healing properties, and multi-stimuli-responsive networks to adapt to the local microenvironment; the convergence of electrospinning, 3D bioprinting, and microfluidics to construct bio-patches with structural gradients and compartmentalized functionality; and the establishment of organ-on-chip platforms and large-animal models to bridge fundamental research with translational validation. Furthermore, the integration of artificial intelligence and clinical big data is expected to drive the transition from universal materials to programmable, personalized therapeutic systems. With continued interdisciplinary collaboration, functionalized bio-patches are poised to become foundational technologies in the next generation of abdominal tissue reconstruction and regenerative medicine.
